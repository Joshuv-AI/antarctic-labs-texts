Effect of a short virtual reality training on first-attempt hand-disinfection performance in an OSCE: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Benjamin Roszipal, Martin Ernst, Alexander Hoffelner, Matthias Kalmring, Michael Wagner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReliable hygienic hand-disinfection is essential for infection prevention and control. It remains unclear whether a short VR training module improves objectively assessed procedural performance compared with conventional instruction. This single-center, parallel-group randomized controlled trial examined first-opportunity hand-disinfection performance in an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) after VR training versus a video control.MethodsIn 2025, first-year nursing students at a single Austrian University of Applied Sciences were randomized (1:1) to a 20-minute VR training module with contamination visualization feedback or a time- and headset-matched instructional video control without interactivity. Randomization used block allocation (blocks of six); instructions were standardized via audio playback. Three days later, students completed a routine five-station OSCE. Hygienic hand-disinfection was required at the beginning of each of five OSCE stations and was documented within routine station checklists. Hand-disinfection ratings from the five stations were extracted from routine OSCE checklists and coded (0 = incorrect, 1 = partially correct, 2 = correct). The preregistered primary endpoint was station 1 performance. The primary hypothesis was tested with adjusted proportional-odds regression, adjusting for age, gender, and prior hygiene-course grade as a baseline indicator of hygiene-related academic performance; all other analyses were exploratory.ResultsOf 128 randomized students (VR n = 65; control n = 63), all had available station 1 hand-disinfection scores, which constituted the primary outcome. One value was missing for the prior hygiene-course grade covariate, yielding N = 127 for adjusted models. At station 1, fully correct performance occurred in 52/65 (80.0%) in VR versus 15/63 (23.8%) in control. VR training was associated with markedly better first-opportunity performance (adjusted OR = 11.16, 95% CI [4.95, 25.14], p = 5.9 × 10− 9), supported by a binary pass/fail sensitivity analysis (adjusted OR = 6.06, 95% CI [1.84, 19.92]). Exploratory analyses suggested higher performance across stations, with group differences varying by station. No harms or adverse events were observed during the trial.ConclusionA short VR training module for hand-disinfection with contamination visualization was associated with better first-opportunity OSCE hand-disinfection performance compared with a headset-delivered instructional video control. This suggests that short, feedback-rich VR training may support immediate procedural readiness under standardized assessment conditions.Trial registrationThis study was preregistered within a larger longitudinal project and later registered as a stand-alone study in the Open Science Framework (OSF) (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/3B6SC).Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04856-w.

Introduction

Healthcare simulation is increasingly expected to demonstrate impact on outcomes relevant to healthcare quality and patient safety [1–3]. From a translational perspective, training should target high-frequency, safety-critical behaviors that must be performed reliably under routine workflow conditions. These behaviors should also be assessable with function-aligned behavioral metrics rather than satisfaction or knowledge measures alone.

Healthcare-associated infections remain a persistent patient-safety challenge, and infection prevention and control (IPC) continues to be a high-impact domain in which avoidable harm and implementation gaps persist across settings [4, 5]. Hygienic hand-disinfection is a cornerstone IPC behavior that is protocolized, observable, and directly linked to prevention efforts. Healthcare education therefore offers an opportunity to establish reliable hand-disinfection habits before extensive clinical exposure. However, correct hand-disinfection is not only a matter of procedural knowledge. Technique quality is vulnerable to contextual pressures and may be limited by insufficient opportunities for feedback-rich deliberate practice, contributing to heterogeneous practical outcomes [6–8].

Because hygienic hand-disinfection is procedural and observable, it can be assessed in Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs), which provide structured performance observation under standardized assessment conditions [9, 10]. In multi-station OSCEs, learners rotate through several standardized clinical tasks, allowing safety-relevant behaviors such as hand-disinfection to be embedded across different stations and task contexts. The use of multiple stations, modalities, and assessors can broaden performance sampling and reduce dependence on a single task, assessment format, or examiner judgement. However, OSCE scores reflect performance in an assessment context rather than a direct measure of underlying competence, requiring careful endpoint definition and interpretation [11, 12].

When the same behavior is assessed repeatedly within an OSCE circuit, sequence and warm-up effects may occur during the examination itself. Performance may improve across early stations as examinees acclimatize to the format and task demands [12, 13]. Format unfamiliarity may also impair OSCE performance, whereas short, active familiarization can improve outcomes [14]. First-opportunity performance may therefore provide a stringent indicator of immediate transfer because it captures performance before within-OSCE adaptation can influence the observed result. In this study, “first opportunity” refers to the first required hand-disinfection performance within the OSCE circuit, namely the hand-disinfection task at the beginning of station 1.

Immersive virtual reality (VR) may complement procedural training by providing standardized rehearsal and immediate feedback in a controlled environment. Evidence across nursing education suggests that VR can improve learning outcomes relative to conventional approaches, although effects vary depending on implementation and outcome definitions [15]. For hand hygiene specifically, recent evidence remains heterogeneous across technologies, comparator conditions, and outcomes [6]. Controlled studies suggest that immersive VR can improve hand-hygiene-related outcomes under certain design conditions, particularly when interactive systems provide feedback or support situated practice [16, 17].

VR can provide standardized, repeatable, and immediate feedback loops that are difficult to operationalize in traditional formats, especially when behavioral outcomes such as compliance proxies or technique components are targeted [6, 17]. This is relevant because hand-disinfection performance depends not only on declarative knowledge but also on execution quality under realistic constraints. Feedback that makes omissions, missed opportunities, or contamination consequences visible may therefore support procedural readiness [6, 16, 18, 19].

Despite increasing uptake of VR in health professions education, its added value remains unclear when outcomes are defined as observable performance in structured assessments. A key methodological limitation in the existing evidence base is the wide variation in control conditions and endpoint definitions, which complicates causal interpretation [6]. Randomized studies combining (i) a time- and headset-matched video control, (ii) blinded OSCE assessment, and (iii) a predefined first-opportunity OSCE endpoint remain scarce, particularly in early-stage nursing cohorts [6, 14]. This study addresses this gap through a preregistered RCT comparing a VR training module with contamination-visualization feedback against a headset-delivered video control. The primary confirmatory endpoint was first-station OSCE hand-disinfection performance in first-year nursing students.

Methods

Study design and registration

This single-center, parallel-group randomized controlled trial used a 1:1 between-subjects design with participant and OSCE-examiner blinding, comparing a VR training module for hand-disinfection with a headset-delivered video control. The primary endpoint, hypotheses, and analysis plan were preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF): https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/3B6SC. The trial was embedded in a broader longitudinal teaching evaluation project in the same cohort. The hand-disinfection outcomes reported here were derived from routine OSCE documentation collected three days after the intervention within the same student cohort. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee (approval number: GS3-EK-12/942–2025).

Participants and setting

Participants were first-year undergraduate nursing students enrolled in the second semester of the Bachelor of Science in Healthcare and Nursing program at the University of Applied Sciences St. Pöölten. Eligible students were informed about the study during scheduled second semester skills sessions. Inclusion criteria were enrolment in the second semester of the nursing program, age ≥ 18 years, sufficient German language proficiency to understand study procedures, attendance in the relevant skills training session, and provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria comprised conditions incompatible with safe headset use (e.g., severe motion sickness) and withdrawal of consent before or during the intervention. A total of 128 students (N = 128) consented and were randomized (female, n = 74; male, n = 54; mean age M = 23.5 years, SD = 5.66). Of these, 65 (n = 65) were allocated to the VR training group and 63 (n = 63) to the control group. Participation was voluntary and integrated into the regular course context; non-participation had no negative consequences for course assessment or progression. No monetary compensation was provided.

Randomization and blinding

Randomization was performed at the individual level using blocked allocation (1:1; block size = 6) [20]. Blocked allocation was chosen because it is an established randomization approach for maintaining approximately balanced group sizes across trial arms, which was important because the intervention sessions were conducted in small groups within scheduled teaching activities. The specific card-drawing procedure was pragmatically adapted for the classroom setting and was not adopted as a previously validated standalone randomization tool. Rather, it operationalized the blocked allocation procedure while preserving allocation concealment, because the color-to-condition mapping was generated only after all cards in each block had been selected and revealed. For each block, participants drew face-down, color-coded allocation cards and revealed them simultaneously. The cards were prepared in advance by a study team member not involved in recruitment or OSCE assessment. The block-specific color-to-condition mapping was documented immediately and used to implement the assigned intervention. Participants were not informed which format was considered the experimental condition, and OSCE examiners remained blinded to group allocation throughout outcome assessment. Because the interventions differed in interactivity, study personnel supervising the intervention sessions were not blinded.

Interventions

Participants in the intervention group completed a single 20-minute VR training module on hygienic hand-disinfection. The VR training module was custom-developed as part of a separate research project and was not a commercially available product. The present randomized trial was independent of the development project but used the completed module as the intervention condition. Development of the module involved an interdisciplinary project team, including technical VR developers and clinical/simulation experts. The virtual scenario was set in an emergency-care environment resembling a patient-care room with a patient area and a clean working area, allowing learners to experience contamination transfer between patient contact and subsequent contact with clean surfaces. The procedure was practiced within the scenario and guided through standardized step-by-step task prompts. Visual feedback was provided through contamination visualization: contacts within the virtual environment were highlighted using color markings resembling fluorescent traces. When learners touched the patient and subsequently touched a clean working area without performing hand-disinfection in between, the resulting contamination was made visible in the environment. The color coding ranged from green to orange depending on contamination duration. Feedback could be displayed in real time by pressing a button. The module did not use verbal or written reminder prompts, and the headset did not track detailed hand-disinfection technique or hand gestures. Instead, the module focused on the procedural timing of hand-disinfection opportunities within the scenario. Clinical accuracy was reviewed by hospital infection-prevention specialists, who assessed the plausibility of the scenario, the timing of hand-disinfection opportunities, and the contamination-visualization logic. Feedback from this review was incorporated before the completed module was used in the present trial.

Participants allocated to the control condition viewed a time-matched instructional video on hygienic hand-disinfection while wearing a VR headset. The video was an existing curricular resource and was not newly developed for the present study. It provided non-interactive instruction on hygienic hand-disinfection without contamination visualization or interactive feedback. Both conditions were delivered in a single session three days prior to the OSCE assessment. This timing was determined by the existing curriculum structure, as the intervention was integrated into a scheduled training day for the upcoming OSCE that took place three days before the examination.

Outcome assessment

All participants completed a routine OSCE under examination conditions three days after the assigned training. During the OSCE, students rotated through five consecutive stations, and hygienic hand-disinfection was mandatory at the beginning of all five stations. Thus, each participant had five separate hand-disinfection opportunities, one at the start of each OSCE station. The clinical tasks differed across stations; however, the hand-disinfection requirement was standardized across the OSCE circuit and was expected before students engaged with the respective station-specific task. The first opportunity therefore refers specifically to the hand-disinfection performance at station 1, before any further within-OSCE repetition or adaptation could occur. The OSCE assessed multiple station-specific skills as part of routine examination procedures; however, the present analysis did not examine performance on these clinical tasks and focused only on the hand-disinfection item documented at each station. No separate study-specific hand-hygiene assessment instrument was used. Hand-disinfection performance was documented within the standard OSCE software used for routine examinations. Each station checklist included one dedicated hand-hygiene item: “performs hygienic hand-disinfection.” This item was rated on the routine OSCE scale from 0 to 2 points and was extracted for the present analysis. Because the assessment consisted of this single checklist item within the routine OSCE documentation, no separate hand-hygiene assessment tool is available.

Variables

The primary aim of the study was to test whether VR training improved hand-disinfection performance at the first OSCE opportunity compared with the control condition. The primary outcome was hand-disinfection performance at station 1, operationalized as an ordinal variable (HW1_score; 0–2; 0 = incorrect, 1 = partially correct, 2 = correct). This endpoint was selected a priori because it captured the first required hand-disinfection performance before repeated exposure to the OSCE hand-disinfection requirement could influence performance. Ratings from stations 2–5 captured subsequent hand-disinfection opportunities and were used only for exploratory post hoc analyses. Because station 1 was also included in the station-level sequence analyses, these exploratory analyses were not independent of the primary endpoint and were interpreted only as descriptive extensions of the primary analysis. Exploratory outcomes included station-level performance across stations 1–5, mean performance across stations, the number of fully correct stations, and an exploratory learning index (station 5 minus station 1). The primary predictor was group allocation (VR training vs. control). Covariates included age in years, gender, and prior hygiene-course grade as a baseline indicator of hygiene-related academic performance. This grade was obtained from the prerequisite hygiene course completed before the intervention and was recorded on the Austrian 1–5 grading scale, with lower values indicating better performance (1 = best, 5 = worst). OSCE station number [1–5] was treated as a within-subject factor in trajectory analyses.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R (via RStudio; Posit Team, 2025). The primary confirmatory analysis tested the hypothesis that the VR training module improves hand-disinfection performance at the first OSCE station compared with the control intervention. This hypothesis was evaluated using an ordinal logistic regression model with proportional odds, with the hand-disinfection score (HW1_score) as the outcome variable, group as the main predictor, and age, gender, and prior hygiene-course grade as covariates. The proportional-odds assumption was evaluated by comparing proportional-odds and non-parallel slopes specifications. Confirmatory inference was based on an ordinal model, and results are reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals and p-values. The significance threshold was set at α = 0.05 (two-sided).

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess robustness of the primary finding while preserving the ordinal nature of the outcome as the main inferential framework. The primary sensitivity analysis recoded first-station hand-disinfection performance as fail (0) versus pass (1 or 2) and tested the group effect using binary logistic regression with the same predictors and covariates as the primary model. A linear model using the 0–2 station score was also examined as a descriptive robustness check. However, this specification is less appropriate for inference because the underlying rating scale is ordinal with only three categories. Therefore, the ordinal proportional-odds model remains the primary analysis, and the binary pass/fail model is reported as the main sensitivity analysis. The linear model is no longer used to support the main conclusion and is reported, if at all, only as an ancillary descriptive check in the supplement.

All additional analyses beyond the primary station-1 endpoint were exploratory and post hoc. These analyses were conducted to describe whether the observed first-opportunity effect was also reflected in broader OSCE performance patterns, but they were not treated as independent confirmatory tests because station 1 was included in the station-level sequence analyses. Performance trajectories across OSCE stations were examined using cumulative link mixed models with an ordinal outcome, including fixed effects for group, station, and their interaction, as well as age, gender, and prior hygiene-course grade. A participant-level random intercept was specified to account for repeated measures within individuals. Additional exploratory analyses examined mean performance across stations using linear regression, the proportion of fully correct performances using binomial count models, the learning index using linear regression, and moderation of training effects by baseline hygiene competence using ordinal regression models with interaction terms. Where appropriate, nonparametric bootstrap procedures were used to obtain robust confidence intervals for key effects. These bootstrap analyses were conducted for robustness assessment and did not alter the confirmatory inference.

Missing data and exclusions

Participants were excluded from the primary analysis only if the primary endpoint (station 1 hand-disinfection score) was unavailable. No exclusions were based on performance or outliers. Complete-case analysis was used for models including covariates, consistent with the preregistered plan.

To provide robustness-oriented uncertainty estimates for the main effect, a nonparametric bootstrap with 5,000 resamples was conducted for the VR group coefficient and transformed to odds ratios. The bootstrap median OR was 11.9520 with a 95% percentile CI [5.1023, 33.8578]. A BCa interval on the log-odds scale yielded a 95% BCa CI of [1.489, 3.291], corresponding to an OR interval of [4.4311, 26.8619]. Together, these results indicate a large and robust immediate performance advantage for VR training at the first OSCE hand-disinfection opportunity (Script Step 2).

Sensitivity analyses for the primary endpoint

Binary logistic regression (pass vs fail)

First-station performance was additionally recoded as fail (0) versus pass (1 or 2) to provide a binary sensitivity analysis of the primary endpoint. Pass rates were higher in the VR group than in the control group (60/64 vs. 46/63; complete-case N = 127). In the adjusted logistic regression, VR training was associated with higher odds of passing the first station, OR = 6.06, 95% CI [1.84, 19.92], p = 0.003. The covariates age, gender, and prior hygiene-course grade did not show clear associations with pass/fail performance and are therefore shown in Table 2. A 5,000-resample bootstrap supported the robustness of the group effect, median OR = 6.51, 95% percentile CI [2.10, 60.01] (Script Step 3.1).

Linear model / ANCOVA treating HW1 as quasi-continuous

As an ancillary robustness check, first-station performance was also analysed using a linear model on the original 0–2 score. This analysis was not used for primary inference, because the primary outcome was ordinal and the proportional-odds model was therefore considered the appropriate confirmatory analysis. The adjusted linear model showed the same direction of effect, with higher first-station performance in the VR group, b = 0.7715, SE = 0.1166, t(122) = 6.619, p = 1.02 × 10−9. Covariates were not significant and are therefore reported only briefly: age, b = −0.00435, p = 0.6817; gender [M vs W], b = −0.0385, p = 0.7526; prior hygiene-course grade, b = −0.00167, p = 0.9862. However, the model explained only a modest proportion of variance, R2 = 0.264, adjusted R2 = 0.240, despite a significant overall model test, F(4, 122) = 10.96, p = 1.261 × 10−7. This comparatively lower model fit is unsurprising because the model uses a single first-station rating with only three ordinal response categories and is retained only as an ancillary descriptive check. The residual normality test was significant, Shapiro-Wilk p = 6.68 × 10−9; therefore, a 5,000-resample bootstrap was used as an additional descriptive robustness check. The bootstrap for the group coefficient produced a median estimate of 0.7719 with a 95% percentile CI [0.5351, 0.9923]. This ancillary analysis was consistent with the ordinal and binary models but was interpreted only descriptively. (Script Step 3.2)

Exploratory trajectories across OSCE stations 1–5

An ordinal cumulative link mixed model (CLMM) with a participant-level random intercept was estimated, including fixed effects of group, station (Attempt_f), and the group × station interaction, adjusting for age, gender, and prior hygiene-course grade. Short-term learning index (station 5 − station 1)

A short-term learning index was computed as the difference between station 5 and station 1 scores (range −2 to 2; M = −0.0236; median = 0). In the adjusted linear regression (N = 127), there was no evidence for differential change from station 1 to station 5 between groups, b = 0.0434, SE = 0.1559, t(122) = 0.278, p = 0.781. The bootstrap estimate for the group coefficient was consistent with a negligible effect (median b = 0.0465, 95% percentile CI [−0.2532, 0.3525]). Covariate effects were also non-significant (Age: p = 0.413; Gender: p = 0.784; prior hygiene-course grade: p = 0.315; Script Step 5.2).

Moderation by baseline competence: group × prior hygiene-course grade

To test whether baseline competence moderated the VR effect on first-station performance, an ordinal logistic regression with a Group × Prior hygiene-course grade interaction was estimated (complete-case N = 127). The interaction was not significant, b = 0.3101, SE = 0.6353, z = 0.488, p = 0.626. The proportional-odds assumption remained supported, LR χ2 (5) = 4.23, p = 0.5168. Bootstrap inference for the interaction coefficient (5,000 resamples) yielded a median log-odds of 0.2702 with a 95% percentile CI [−1.3175, 1.8239], corresponding to a median interaction OR of 1.3102 per one-unit increase in prior hygiene-course grade, 95% CI [0.2678, 6.1960]. These findings do not support moderation of the VR effect by prior hygiene-course grade in this sample (Script Step 5.2).

Summary of findings

Across confirmatory and sensitivity analyses, first-station performance was higher in the VR group than in the control group. Exploratory analyses suggested higher average performance across stations and more fully correct executions in the VR group, whereas there was no clear evidence of a station 5–1 change difference or for moderation by prior hygiene-course grade.

Discussion

In this randomized controlled trial embedded in routine OSCE assessment, a short VR training module was associated with higher hand-disinfection performance than a time- and headset-matched passive video control. The VR training module combined guided practice, scenario-based engagement, and contamination-visualization feedback. Therefore, the findings indicate an advantage of this interactive, feedback-rich VR training module over passive instructional exposure. However, this should not be interpreted as evidence that VR alone is inherently superior.

At station 1, fully correct performance occurred more often in the VR group than in the control group. This finding is consistent with evidence suggesting that VR-based hand-hygiene interventions are more likely to show favorable outcomes when they include interactivity and feedback [6, 16, 21]. However, the comparator in the present study was passive video exposure rather than an active skills-practice condition. The observed difference may therefore reflect the combined effects of guided enactment, feedback, and scenario-based practice, rather than immersion alone.

The primary endpoint captured the earliest required hand-disinfection performance under OSCE conditions, before repeated exposure within the OSCE circuit could influence performance. This supports interpretation of the primary result as an indicator of immediate procedural readiness rather than within-assessment adaptation. In the present study, this was particularly relevant, as hand-disinfection was required repeatedly across the five-station OSCE. Later stations may therefore already have reflected acclimatization to the repeated requirement. However, OSCE outcomes remain assessment-context measures and should not be equated with sustained clinical compliance or performance under real clinical workflow conditions.

The observed effect aligns with broader evidence that VR can improve skills-related outcomes in nursing education, while also emphasizing that effects depend on instructional design and outcome choice [15, 22, 23]. For a brief and structured task such as hand-disinfection, the educational benefit may depend less on immersion itself and more on repeated enactment, attentional focus, and salient corrective feedback. The present trial strengthens interpretability by combining preregistration, a time-matched headset control, and a predefined first-opportunity OSCE endpoint. However, because the comparator was not an active practice-based condition, the study cannot determine whether similar effects could be achieved through other active, feedback-rich formats.

The headset-delivered video control is important for interpretation because it controlled for training duration and headset exposure, but not for interactivity, active practice, or visual feedback. The findings therefore support the comparative effectiveness of the VR training module over passive video instruction, while not isolating the specific contribution of immersion, feedback, or interactivity. One plausible mechanism is the module’s consequence-focused contamination visualization, which made otherwise invisible contamination transfer visible during the scenario. This may have supported learners in recognizing missed hand-disinfection opportunities and contamination transfer between patient contact and clean working areas. Similar feedback logic has been shown to improve hygiene-related outcomes when visual feedback reveals missed areas or errors [22]. However, this mechanism was not directly tested, and the present study cannot separate the effects of contamination visualization from guided practice, scenario context, or headset-based immersion.

Exploratory analyses indicated that the VR advantage extended beyond the first instance, with higher mean performance and greater consistency of fully correct executions across stations. This pattern is meaningful for IPC because infection prevention is fundamentally a reliability problem: sporadic lapses remain consequential even when average performance improves [4, 5]. At the same time, group differences varied across OSCE instances, and the learning index did not differ between groups. This suggests that the intervention primarily shifted initial performance upward, while within-circuit repetition and station-to-station variability influenced both groups once the OSCE sequence was underway [9, 24]. Station-to-station variation may reflect differences in concurrent task demands, time pressure, or contextual cues at each station that modulate attention to hand-disinfection. However, these patterns were exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously.

From a translational simulation perspective, the first-opportunity endpoint provides a concrete example of a short simulation-based preparation format influencing an observable procedural performance indicator under standardized OSCE conditions [2, 25]. Such behavioral outcomes are more interpretable than satisfaction or self-report measures and align with recommendations to strengthen simulation evaluation through explicit objectives and function-aligned metrics [3, 26]. In this sense, the study’s main contribution lies less in establishing a uniquely VR-mediated effect and more in demonstrating that a short, interactive, feedback-rich VR training module can influence immediate procedural readiness at the first assessed performance opportunity.

For health professions education, these findings suggest that short VR training modules may be feasible for supporting immediate procedural readiness when they are integrated into existing curriculum structures. The 20-minute format appears compatible with routine teaching and OSCE preparation activities [27]. However, this implication should remain bounded. The present findings do not show that immersive delivery is superior to other active, feedback-rich training formats, nor do OSCE performance gains necessarily translate into clinical compliance. Integration with workplace-based feedback and monitoring remains essential for sustained practice change.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This single-center study in first-year nursing students may not generalize to other contexts or learner groups. OSCE-derived outcomes reflect standardized assessment conditions and do not reproduce clinical interruptions, workflow pressures, or behavioral norms [4, 9]. Additionally, first-attempt OSCE validity appears stronger for cognitive/interpersonal domains than for procedural skills in parts of the OSCE validity literature, reinforcing the need to test clinical transfer for procedural reliability outcomes [28, 29]. The ordinal 0–2 scoring supports feasibility but is less granular than fine-grained observational measures and may contribute to ceiling effects. Because ratings were derived from routine OSCE checklist documentation rather than a study-specific observational instrument, measurement granularity was limited, and inter-rater variability could not be formally quantified.

The most important limitation concerns the comparator and, therefore, the interpretation of the intervention effect. Although the control condition was time-matched and headset-delivered, it consisted of passive video exposure rather than an active practice-based training condition. By contrast, the VR training module combined guided enactment, interactivity, scenario context, and consequence-focused visual feedback. Accordingly, the observed effect cannot be attributed specifically to VR as an isolated modality or affordance, but rather to an interactive, feedback-rich VR training module relative to passive instruction [6, 21, 30–32]. Thus, the study supports a pragmatic conclusion about comparative effectiveness against passive exposure, but it does not determine what VR uniquely contributes beyond other active, feedback-rich formats [6, 21, 22, 30].

In-module behavior was not instrumented, limiting insight into which interactions drove performance gains. Relatedly, no component-level conclusions can be drawn regarding the relative contribution of contamination visualization, repeated enactment, attentional guidance, or immersion. In addition, the VR training module primarily focused on recognizing and responding to hand-disinfection opportunities within the scenario rather than on measuring the detailed motor technique of hand rubbing. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of improved procedural timing and OSCE hand-disinfection performance, not as evidence that the intervention improved all technical components of hand-disinfection technique. The study also did not systematically assess user experience, usability, tolerability, motion sickness, sensory discomfort, or other challenges experienced by participants during the VR training module. This limits conclusions about feasibility and acceptability from the learner perspective.

The study assessed short-term transfer only; retention and transfer to clinical practice remain to be tested. This is consistent with the current hand-hygiene VR evidence base, where durability beyond approximately one month remains uncertain due to limited follow-up data [6, 33]. Thus, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of improved immediate procedural readiness under OSCE conditions, not of sustained behavioral change or improved clinical compliance. Participants were not informed about which format was considered experimental, however, differential expectations between interactive and non-interactive formats cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

In a andomized controlled trial embedded in routine OSCE assessment, a short VR training module for hand-disinfection was associated with higher first-opportunity hand-disinfection performance in first-year nursing students compared with a time- and headset-matched video control. Exploratory analyses also suggested higher mean performance and a greater frequency of fully correct executions across OSCE stations. Together, these findings indicate that a short, interactive, feedback-rich VR training module may enhance immediate procedural readiness under standardized assessment conditions. At the same time, the study does not establish which specific intervention components were responsible for this effect or what immersive delivery uniquely contributes rbeyond other active, feedback-rich instructional approaches. Future research should therefore examine retention, transfer to clinical practice, user experience, and the comparative value of specific training components across immersive and non-immersive formats.
