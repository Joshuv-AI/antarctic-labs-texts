Genome-wide identification of the oligopeptide transporter gene family in poplar and their responses to iron and cadmium
Authors: Ran Fu, Jun Zou, Yong Lai, Mingwan Li, Yuanyuan Chen, Chaochen Yang, Dangquan Zhang, Chaofeng Ma, Yinghao Chen, Shen Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundsThe oligopeptide transporter (OPT) family belongs to a type of proton-coupled symporter and functions in the transport of metal ions, glutathione, phytochelatins (PCs), and metal-PC complexes, as well as in the regulation of metal homeostasis. Polplar, a fast-growing woody model species which has been resequenced multiple times, shows great potential in phytoremediation. However, the identification of OPT family genes and their responses to cadmium (Cd) and iron (Fe) remain poorly understood.ResultsThis study identified 30 OPT genes (17 yellow stripe-like (YSL) subfamily members and 13 OPT subfamily members) in Populus trichocarpa, and most PtOPT proteins were localized to the plasma membrane and tonoplast. Twelve gene duplication events were detected among the members of the PtOPT gene family, with one duplication pair being a tandem duplication and the remaining 11 pairs being segmental duplications. Numerous cis-acting elements related to light responses, hormone responses, stress responses, and plant development have been identified. The expression profiles of the PtOPT genes varied significantly among different tissues. Coexpression networks centered on PtOPT4.2 were constructed to reveal its potential roles in cellular nitrogen compound metabolism, protein-containing complex metabolism, small molecule metabolism, and carboxylic acid metabolism. The expression levels of PtYSL6, PtYSL7.4, PtOPT2, PtOPT5.2, and PtYSL3.3 were highly induced by Cd under normal Fe conditions, whereas the expression levels of these five genes were reduced by Cd under high Fe conditions, indicating that these five genes may play roles in the transport and distribution of Fe and Cd, as well as in Cd detoxification.ConclusionsThese findings enhance our understanding of the critical role of PtOPT genes in response to Fe and Cd, and provide valuable molecular targets for improving Cd phytoremediation potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07931-7.

Background

Peptide transport is an energy-dependent process of peptide transmembrane movement that occurs widely in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes [1]. The peptides transported into the cell are promptly decomposed by peptidases and serve as essential sources of amino acids, nitrogen, and carbon for cellular metabolism [2]. Peptide transporters can be classified into two categories on the basis of their energy source: ATP-binding cassette (ABC-type) transporters, which utilize ATP hydrolysis for peptide transport, and proton motive force (PMF)-driven peptide transporters (PTRs) [1]. The members of the ABC transporter family can transport a broad spectrum of substrates across membranes, including amino acids, sugars, peptides, proteins, and various hydrophobic compounds and metabolites [3]. These membrane transport proteins are critically involved in fundamental physiological processes, including ion homeostasis, heavy metal transport and tolerance, hormone regulation, nutrient acquisition and growth responses, and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses [3, 4]. Unlike members of the ABC family, those of the PTR family utilize the PMF for the transport of substrates such as amino acids, peptides, and nitrates [2]. Consequently, the PTR family has also been referred to as the proton-dependent oligopeptide transporter (POT) family [1]. Like PTR transporters, members of the oligopeptide transporter (OPT) family also harness the PMF to drive substrate translocation [5]. However, unlike PTR transporters, which exclusively transport di- and tripeptides, OPT transporters accommodate longer peptide chains (2–10 amino acids linked by peptide bonds) with molecular weights typically less than 1,000 daltons across the cell membrane [6]. Recent studies have demonstrated that members of the OPT family are involved in multiple biological processes, including glutathione (GSH) transport, nitrogen uptake and distribution, long-distance transport of metal ions, and heavy metal sequestration [7–9]. Therefore, OPTs may serve as crucial transporter proteins that contribute to increasing plant tolerance to heavy metals.

According to phylogenetic analysis, the OPT family can be classified into two distinct subfamilies: the OPT subfamily and the yellow stripe-like (YSL) subfamily [7, 10]. The OPT subfamily members have been determined exclusively in plants and fungi, whereas members of the YSL subfamily have been detected in archaea, eubacteria, fungi, and plants [11]. Genes in the OPT subfamily possess two highly conserved motifs, the Asn-Pro-Gly (NPG) and Lys-Ile-Pro-Pro-Arg (KIPPR) motifs, whereas genes in the YSL subfamily lack these conserved motifs [12]. OPT subfamily members primarily mediate long-distance transport of metals, nitrogen mobilization, GSH translocation, and heavy metal sequestration [10, 13]. In contrast, YSL subfamily members function as metal chelate transporters, predominantly facilitating the uptake and distribution of mugineic acid (MA) or nicotianamine (NA) [14, 15]. In rice, OPT7 functions in the unloading of Fe from the xylem, contributing to both long-distance distribution to developing organs and local distribution within the leaf [16]. AtOPT6, a phloem-specific member of the OPT family, facilitates the loading of GSH, phytochelatins (PCs), and PC-metal complexes from the phloem into the siliques of Arabidopsis thaliana [13, 17]. In maize, ZmYSL2, ZmYSL5, ZmYSL7, ZmYSL9, and ZmYSL12 are involved in the transport of Fe-NA and Zn-NA complexes from the endosperm to the embryo during grain development [18, 19]. In poplar, PcYSL3 regulates the unloading of the Cd-NA complex from xylem vessels into neighboring ray parenchyma cells, as well as its radial movement within stems [20]. Nevertheless, information regarding OPTs in woody plant species is still limited.

Cadmium (Cd), a nonessential heavy metal with strong biological toxicity, primarily accumulates in the kidneys and bones, thereby inducing severe renal impairment and skeletal system dysfunction [21, 22]. The absorption and translocation of Cd in plants have been extensively investigated [23–25]. Plenty of evidence has shown that the OPT family is essential for the absorption and translocation of Cd [26–28]. The overexpression of the OPT family member VcYSL6 reduces Cd translocation to the aboveground parts and decreases Cd accumulation in blueberries [27]. The overexpression of BjYSL7 promotes the translocation of Cd to shoots in tobacco [29]. In A. thaliana, OPT3 is involved in the redistribution of Cd, leading to increased Cd accumulation in the opt3 mutant [30]. Iron (Fe) is an essential element for cell functions and is especially involved in chlorophyll synthesis, photosynthesis, and growth and development [31, 32]. Fe acquisition mechanisms in plants can be divided into two types: Strategy I in nongraminaceous plants and Strategy II in graminaceous plants [33]. Strategy II plants utilize YSL transporters to facilitate the uptake of Fe(III)-MAs from the rhizosphere into cells [34]. Currently, OPT family members are extensively involved in the translocation of Fe and other metals in both Strategy I and Strategy II plants [30, 34–37].

Poplar, a fast-growing woody plant, is extensively used in timber production, carbon storage, ecological conservation, and remediation of heavy metal-contaminated soils [38, 39]. With the completion of the whole genomes of poplars and the advancement of genetic transformation techniques, poplar species have emerged as model organisms for molecular breeding in woody plants [40–42]. Although the OPT gene family has been identified in Populus trichocarpa [43], the expression patterns of OPT members in response to Cd and Fe remain poorly understood. In addition, as sequencing technologies have advanced, the genome of P. trichocarpa has been resequenced multiple times, resulting in substantial enhancements to genomic databases [44, 45]. Therefore, investigating the tissue-specific expression profiles of P. trichocarpa OPT genes and elucidating their responses to Cd and Fe for efficient phytoremediation via poplars is crucial.

In this research, the OPT family members were identified by utilizing the latest genomic databases of P. trichocarpa, followed by an analysis of their phylogenetic relationships, gene duplication events, and structural characteristics. In addition, we investigated the expression profiles of OPT family members in different tissues, as well as their responses to Cd and Fe. Thus, this research offers a theoretical foundation for understanding the regulatory roles of PtOPT genes in poplar growth, as well as their involvement in Cd and Fe responses.

Materials and methods

Identification of the PtOPT genes in P. trichocarpa

The OPT genes in P. trichocarpa were detected using the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) and local Basic Local Alignment Search Tool for Proteins (BLASTP) following previously reported methods [46]. Briefly, the genomic and protein sequences of P. trichocarpa were downloaded from the JGI Phytozome database (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/info/Ptrichocarpa_v4_1, accessed on 15 April 2025), while the OPT protein sequences of A. thaliana were acquired from The Arabidopsis Information Resource (https://www.arabidopsis.org/, accessed on 15 April 2025). Next, a local BLASTP analysis was performed to identify homologous proteins in P. trichocarpa on the basis of the amino acid sequences of AtOPTs. All sequences exhibiting an E values less than 10–5 were selected. The HMM encompassing the OPT domain (PF03169) was subsequently utilized to screen the P. trichocarpa protein database, and all sequences with an E value below 10–5 were preserved. The amino acid sequences retrieved from BLASTP and Hidden Markov Model for Ergodic Regions (HMMER) were first deduplicated. The resulting candidate sequences were then analyzed via National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and HMMER to confirm the presence of the OPT domain. Ultimately, a total of 30 PtOPT sequences were identified and retained.

Chromosomal localization and bioinformatics analysis

Amino acid sequence analysis was conducted via the PhytoMine tool available on the Phytozome platform (https://jgi.doe.gov, accessed on 16 April 2025) by inputting 30 OPT protein IDs to determine the length of amino acid sequences, gene loci, and chromosomal positions of the OPT family. The molecular weight, theoretical isoelectric point (pI), and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) were calculated via the ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/, accessed on 18 April 2025). The subcellular localization of OPT proteins was determined using WOLF-PSORT (https://www.genscript.com/wolf-psort.html, accessed on 20 April 2025). The chromosomal localization of the PtOPTs was visualized via TBtools (version 2.210) [47].

Phylogenetic and sequence analyses

The 30 PtOPT sequences were aligned via ClustalX (version 2.1), and the evolutionary relationships among the PtOPT proteins were analyzed via the maximum likelihood approach implemented in MEGA software (version 11.0.13) [48]. The phylogenetic tree was generated via the iTOL website (https://itol.embl.de, accessed on 20 April 2025) [49]. Additionally, the gene and protein structures of the PtOPT family were illustrated via TBtools (version 2.210) [49, 50].

Analysis of gene replication events

MCScanX software (v11.0.13) was used to detect gene pairs both within the genome of P. trichocarpa and between A. thaliana genomes on the basis of protein sequences. The Ka/Ks ratios for duplicated gene pairs were computed via TBtools. The collinearity relationships among members of the PtOPT gene family in P. trichocarpa and A. thaliana were subsequently visualized via the Circos tool in TBtools (version 2.210) [50].

Analysis of cis-acting elements

To investigate the cis-acting regulatory elements, approximately 2000 base pairs upstream of the coding regions of PtOPT gene family members were extracted via the Gtf/Gff3 Sequences Extractor tool within TBtools software (version 2.210). Next, the identification and prediction of potential cis-acting elements were carried out via the PlantCARE database (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/, accessed on 22 April 2025) [51].

Analysis of gene expression

The expression data of OPT genes in different tissues of P. trichocarpa were sourced from the Phytozome database (https://data.jgi.doe.gov/refine-download/phytozome?q=Populus+trichocarpa, accessed on 25 April 2025) [52]. The expression data of the PtOPT genes in response to 0 or 120 μM Cd in combination with 50 or 200 μM Fe were obtained from our previous study [53]. The heatmaps were generated via TBtools (version 2.210) [54].

Analysis of the coexpression network

The coexpressed pairs of OPT family genes were obtained from the Phytozome database. Gene pairs exhibiting a correlation coefficient exceeding 0.95 were selected to construct a coexpression network, which was subsequently visualized via Cytoscape (version 3.8.2). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were carried out on the coexpressed genes of the OPT members via R (version 2023.12.1) following previously described methods [55].

Determination of the transcript levels of PtOPTs

The plantlets of P. × canescens were cultivated in a climatic chamber and supplied with Hoagland nutrient solution. After four weeks, the plantlets with similar height and growth performance were exposed to 0 or 100 μM Cd in combination with normal (50 μM) or high (200 μM) Fe for 21 days. The roots and leaves were collected for analysis. Total RNA was isolated from the roots and leaves according to previous methods [56]. Tubulin4.1 was used as a reference gene. Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR was performed using a CFX96 Real Time System (CFX96, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The specific primers were designed for each gene (Table S3). The relative expression of PtOPTs was calculated by 2−ΔΔCT [57]. Statistical analyses were performed with Statgraphics (STN, St Louis, MO, USA).

Results

Identification and physicochemical property analysis of the PtOPTs

A total of 30 PtOPT proteins were identified, comprising 17 members of the YSL subfamily and 13 members of the OPT subfamily (Table S1). The chromosome distribution results indicated that the 30 PtOPT members were unevenly distributed across 12 out of the 19 chromosomes of P. trichocarpa, with relatively high concentrations observed on Chr12 (20%), Chr17 (20%), and Chr1 (16.7%) (Fig. 1). Members of the OPT subfamily are distributed across 9 chromosomes, namely, Chr1, Chr2, Chr3, Chr5, Chr6, Chr7, Chr9, Chr12, and Chr16 (Fig. 1). However, the distribution of YSL subfamily members was more concentrated, mainly on Chr1, Chr4, Chr5, Chr11, Chr12, and Chr17 (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Distribution of the OPT genes in Populus trichocarpa. Genes in different colors represent different subfamilies. Red: OPT subfamily; blue: YSL subfamily

Distribution of the OPT genes in Populus trichocarpa. Genes in different colors represent different subfamilies. Red: OPT subfamily; blue: YSL subfamily

The OPT gene family in P. trichocarpa was named on the basis of their phylogenies and sequence homologies with the corresponding AtOPT genes (Table S1). The amino acid lengths of the 30 PtOPT members ranged from 102 (PtYSL8.3) to 780 (PtOPT2), and the molecular weights ranged from 11.23 kDa (PtYSL8.3) to 87.61 kDa (PtOPT2) (Table S1). Among the 30 members of the PtOPT family, the pI values of all the proteins except PtYSL3.5, PtYSL6, and PtOPT7.1 were above 7, indicating that the majority of the family members (27 in total) are basic proteins (Table S1). In addition, the GRAVY values of the 30 PtOPT family proteins were greater than 0, indicating that these proteins are all hydrophobic (Table S1). The subcellular localization analysis of the PtOPTs revealed that all the members of the PtOPT subfamily were localized in the plasma membrane (Table S1). Among the YSL subfamily members, 11 PtYSL proteins were localized in the plasma membrane, 5 PtYSL subfamily members (PtYSL3.3, PtYSL3.4, PtYSL3.5, PtYSL8.1, and PtYSL8.2) were localized in the tonoplast, and 1 member (PtYSL8.3) was localized in the extracellular space (Table S1).

The 30 PtOPT proteins were classified into two distinct subfamilies: the OPT subfamily and the YSL subfamily (Fig. 2). Nine AtOPT subfamily members and 13 PtOPT subfamily members were clustered together, whereas 8 AtYSL subfamily members and 17 PtYSL subfamily members formed a distinct cluster (Fig. 2). This comparative analysis further confirmed the classification of the PtOPT gene family. Collinearity analysis of the PtOPT gene family

To further elucidate the evolutionary relationships among OPT family members within and between species, we identified duplicated gene pairs and conducted collinearity analysis in P. trichocarpa and A. thaliana (Fig. 4). A total of 12 duplicated gene pairs were identified among the 17 PtOPT genes (Fig. 4A). The PtYSL3.3/PtYSL3.4 duplicated pair was a tandem duplication, whereas the remaining 11 pairs were classified as segmental duplications (Fig. 4A). Moreover, repetitive occurrences were confined to the same subfamily, and no repetition was observed between distinct subfamilies (Fig. 4A). The Ka/Ks values of the 12 PtOPT repeat pairs ranged from 0.08 to 0.55 (Table S2), suggesting that the majority of the PtOPTs experienced strong purifying selection throughout their evolutionary history. Analysis of cis-acting elements of the PtOPT gene family

To explore the potential regulatory mechanisms of the PtOPT genes, we analyzed the cis-acting elements within their promoter regions (Fig. 5). Numerous cis-acting elements are involved in growth and development, light responses, plant hormone responses, and stress responses (Fig. 5). For light-responsive elements, most PtOPT genes contain the G-Box and GT1-motifs, which are involved in photosynthesis and chlorophyll synthesis, as well as the I-box, which is involved in chloroplast development (Fig. 5). For stress response elements, most PtOPT genes contain the ARE cis-acting element involved in anaerobic induction, the MBS element involved in drought induction, and the TC-rich repeats involved in defense and stress responsiveness (Fig. 5). With respect to plant growth and development elements, most PtOPT genes contain O2-sites involved in the anaerobic response process, CAT boxes involved in tissue differentiation and organ formation, and circadian elements involved in the biological clock rhythm (Fig. 5). For phytohormone response elements, most PtOPT genes contain ABREs involved in abscisic acid (ABA) signal transduction, CGTCA motifs involved in the jasmonic acid (JA) response, and TGA elements involved in the auxin response (Fig. 5). Among the four types of cis-acting elements identified, the light-response elements in the PtOPT promoter had the highest proportion, ranging from 20 to 47% (Table S4). Expression analysis of PtOPT genes in different tissues and under Cd stress

The expression levels of the PtOPT genes across various tissue development stages were obtained from the Phytozome database (Fig. 6). All the PtOPT genes were detected across various stages in the roots, leaves, buds, and stems (Fig. 6). Nine PtOPT genes (PtYSL7.3, PtYSL3.5, PtYSL6, PtYSL7.1, PtYSL7.2, PtOPT3.2, PtOPT4.1, PtOPT4.2, and PtOPT7.1) presented high expression levels across various growth stages of roots, leaves, buds, and stems (Fig. 6). PtYSL3.2 was highly expressed in all tissues except for the swollen buds (Fig. 6). PtYSL5.2 was predominantly expressed in fully open buds, whereas its expression remained relatively low in other stages of bud development (Fig. 6). The remaining PtOPT genes exhibited no tissue-specific expression patterns (Fig. 6). To further verify the roles of PtOPT genes in response to Cd and Fe, the expression levels of nine PtOPT genes were analyzed (Figs. 9 and 10). The nine PtOPT genes exhibited differential expression patterns under Cd and Fe treatments, with significant variations between roots and leaves. Interestingly, Cd decreased the expression of PtYSL6, PtYSL7.4, PtOPT2, PtOPT5.2, and PtYSL3.3 in leaves under high Fe conditions (Fig. 10). Discussion

Members of the OPT gene family play critical roles in various physiological processes in plants, including growth and development, nutrient uptake, and stress responses, particularly in the transport and homeostasis of metal ions [16, 17]. In this study, a total of 30 PtOPT gene family members were identified in the P. trichocarpa genome, which is comparable to that in other plant species, such as Arachis hypogaea (40 OPT genes) [12], Solanum tuberosum (21 OPT genes) [58], Panax ginseng (37 OPT genes) [10], and Brassica rapa (28 OPT genes) [7], but considerably smaller than that in wheat (107 OPT genes) [59]. The members of the OPT gene family in P. trichocarpa were identified on the basis of genome data version 1.0, which led to the identification of only 20 OPT family members (9 YSL subfamily members and 11 OPT subfamily members) [43]. As the first poplar species to be sequenced, the genome data of P. trichocarpa have undergone multiple rounds of resequencing and refinement. The high-quality genome data may have altered the number of members in the OPT gene family. For example, 200 ERF genes were identified in the P. trichocarpa genome data version 1.1 [60], whereas 210 ERF genes were identified in the P. trichocarpa genome data version 4.1 [45]. On the basis of the latest P. trichocarpa genome database version 4.1, a total of 17 YSL subfamily members and 13 OPT subfamily members were identified in this study, which refined the information on the OPT family members in P. trichocarpa. Physicochemical analysis revealed that all the PtOPT proteins were hydrophobic and mostly basic (Table S1). Most of the PtOPT proteins were located in the plasma membrane, which is consistent with observations in rice, turnip, and maize [7, 9, 61], indicating the high conservation of OPT proteins across species. The 30 PtOPT genes were unevenly distributed across the 19 chromosomes, with 7 chromosomes lacking PtOPT genes. The YSL subfamily members presented a more clustered chromosomal distribution than did the OPT subfamily (Fig. 1). The members of the PtOPT gene family were divided into two major branches according to phylogenetic analysis: the OPT subfamily and the YSL subfamily, which is consistent with previous reports in other species [8, 12, 59]. Genes exhibiting analogous gene and protein structures were primarily observed within the same subfamily and phylogenetic cluster, thereby supporting the classification of PtOPTs.

Gene duplication events facilitate the generation of novel genes and contribute to the acquisition of new functions by these genes [62]. Analysis of gene duplication events suggested that the majority of PtOPT genes experienced gene duplication events. Specifically, 11 duplicated gene pairs were segmental duplications, whereas one duplicated gene pair was a tandem duplication. Thus, segmental duplication may serve as a primary driving force for the expansion of the PtOPT gene family. Similarly, the expansion of the OPT gene family in B. rapa is attributed primarily to segmental duplications [7]. The PtOPT genes may have originated from two distinct ancestors, as the gene duplication events occurred exclusively within subfamilies. The Ka/Ks values of the duplicated gene pairs suggested that the PtOPTs had undergone purifying selection throughout their evolutionary history. Duplicated genes are typically functionally redundant before functional divergence [63]. Consequently, the majority of duplicated genes rapidly become pseudogenes and are lost from the genome [64]. To mitigate the risk of gene loss during evolution, the expression levels of duplicated genes tend to be relatively lower than those of their ancestral counterparts [63]. In this study, the duplicated genes PtOPT6.1, PtOPT2, and PtOPT5.1 presented low expression levels in all tissues, which is consistent with previous studies indicating that low expression levels may facilitate the retention of duplicated genes and their functional redundancy [65]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that members of the OPT family function in the long-distance transport of sulfur, nitrogen translocation, maintenance of metal homeostasis, and heavy metal chelation [14, 16, 34, 36, 66, 67]. For example, ZmYSL2 regulates kernel development and iron distribution in maize kernels [19]. AtOPT6 functions in the translocation of GSH and PCs, as well as in the distribution of Cd in A. thaliana [17]. In addition, the expression of OPT genes is regulated by various transcription factors. For example, PcWRKY1 represses PcYSL3 to negatively regulate the transport of the Cd-NA complex [20, 68]. In this study, most PtOPT promoters contained ABREs, CGTCA motifs, and TGA elements, which are associated with phytohormone responsiveness. Additionally, cis-acting elements such as AREs, MBSs, and TC-rich repeats, which are linked to stress responses, have been identified. These results indicate that the PtOPT genes may be regulated by hormones and play crucial roles in stress responses. The GO and KEGG analyses of the coexpressed genes of PtOPTs revealed that these coexpressed genes were predominantly enriched in pathways related to cellular nitrogen compound metabolism, small molecule metabolism, and protein-containing complexes, suggesting that PtOPTs are involved in the metabolism of small molecule nitrogen-containing compounds. A gene coexpression network revealed that PtOPT4.2 regulated 575 related genes, suggesting its potential involvement in the synthesis and metabolism of nitrogen-containing compounds.

Members of the OPT family play crucial roles in the uptake and transport of Fe in both graminaceous and nongraminaceous plants [12, 33]. Previous studies have investigated the expression patterns of OPT genes under iron-deficient conditions [12]. However, the expression profiles of OPT genes under iron-sufficient conditions remain poorly understood. In maize, ZmYSLs are induced by iron deficiency, thereby increasing iron absorption, transport, distribution, and accumulation [18]. In Arabidopsis, AtYSLs are involved in the transport of metal ions or metal chelates. For example, AtYSL1, AtYSL2, and AtYSL3 are involved in the translocation of Fe-phytosiderophores (PSs) [69]. The expression levels of PtOPTs remained relatively stable under an excessive iron supply, possibly because an appropriately excessive concentration of Fe was insufficient for poplars (Fig. 7). Cd exposure can disturb the uptake and translocation of Fe in most plants, causing Fe deficiency-like symptoms in the leaves of plants [53]. Several OPT genes, including AtYSL3 [68], AtYSL1 [70], OsYSL16 [71], OsYSL9 [72], ZmYSL2 [36], and MsYSL6 [28], have been identified as key transporters of Fe and the Fe-NA complex to leaves in response to environmental stimuli, such as Fe deficiency and Cd stress. Additionally, some OPT genes, such as PcYSL3 [20], BoOPT3 [26], and AtOPT6 [13, 17], have been demonstrated to be involved in the transport of Cd, Cd-GSH, and Cd-PC for long-distance transport and detoxification of Cd. In our previous studies, a high Cd supply increased Cd accumulation and alleviated Cd toxicity [53, 73]. In the present study, PtYSL3.5 and PtOPT6.1 were induced by Cd, suggesting that these genes may play important roles in the translocation of Cd and Fe from roots to shoots. The expression patterns of PtYSL6, PtYSL7.4, PtOPT2, PtOPT5.2, and PtYSL3.3 in leaves were completely opposite in response to Cd under different Fe supply levels, indicating that these five genes play important roles in the absorption and translocation of Cd and Fe.

Conclusions

A total of 30 PtOPT genes (comprising 17 YSL subfamily members and 13 OPT subfamily members), which were unevenly distributed across 12 of the 19 chromosomes, were identified in the genome of P. trichocarpa. Most PtOPTs are located in the plasma membrane and tonoplast. A total of 12 gene duplication events were identified among the PtOPT gene family members, with one duplication pair being a tandem duplication and the remaining 11 pairs being segmental duplications. All the Ka/Ks values of the duplicated pairs were less than 1, indicating that purifying selection occurred during the evolutionary process. Numerous cis-acting elements related to light responses, hormone responses, stress responses, and plant development have been identified. Tissue-specific expression analysis revealed that the expression patterns of PtOPTs varied significantly across different tissues. Coexpression networks centered on PtOPT4.2 were constructed to reveal its potential roles in multiple biological processes, including cellular nitrogen compound metabolism, protein-containing complex metabolism, small molecule metabolism, and carboxylic acid metabolism. The expression patterns of PtYSL6, PtYSL7.4, PtOPT2, PtOPT5.2, and PtYSL3.3 in leaves indicated that these five genes may play important roles in the transport and distribution of both Fe and Cd, as well as in the detoxification of Cd. Overall, these findings enhance our understanding of the critical role of the PtOPT genes in response to Fe supply and Cd stress and provide valuable molecular targets for improving Cd phytoremediation potential.
