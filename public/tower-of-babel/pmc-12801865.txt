The ratio of GGT to PLT indices is a predictive factor for the progression of liver fibrosis in NA-treated chronic hepatitis B patients with advanced fibrosis plus PEG-IFN α-2a therapy: a case–control study
Authors: Ling Li, Huili Li, Yiru Zhao, Lisha Shi, Mireyi Suwanbai, Liying Ren, Guojun Li, Xiucheng Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/objectiveCombination therapy with pegylated interferon α-2a (PEG-IFN α-2a) and nucleoside analogue (NA) has been shown to improve liver histology in patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and hepatic fibrosis. PEG-IFN α-2a effectiveness was assessed in NA-treated CHB patients with advanced fibrosis/compensated cirrhosis.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter study included NA-treated CHB patients with advanced fibrosis/compensated cirrhosis. Patients received 48-week PEG-IFN α-2a plus 24-week follow-up. The primary endpoint was liver fibrosis status 24 weeks post-treatment.ResultsA total of 102 patients were enrolled in the study, including 35 and 67 baseline HBeAg-positive and HBeAg-negative CHB patients, respectively. At the end of PEG-IFN α-2a treatment, a total of 18 (17.6%) patients achieved HBsAg loss, and 23 (65.7%) achieved HBeAg loss. Sixty-nine patients completed the comparison of liver fibrosis and cirrhosis before and after treatment, of whom 16 (23.2%) experienced progression of liver fibrosis or cirrhosis, 32 (46.4%) experienced liver fibrosis reversal, and 21 (30.4%) remained stable. Penalized logistic regression analysis demonstrated that GPR (Intercept = -1.200; Coefficients = 0.102) and GGT levels (Intercept = -1.198; Coefficients = 0.048) at 24 weeks of PEG-IFN α-2a treatment were independent predictors of fibrosis progression. Among these, GPR at 24 weeks exhibited the highest predictive value (Corrected AUC = 0.805).ConclusionApproximately one-fifth of the NA-treated CHB patients with advanced liver fibrosis and compensated cirrhosis experienced progression of liver fibrosis after PEG-IFN α-2a treatment. The GPR at 24 weeks of treatment was an independent predictive factor for the progression of liver fibrosis in this population.Keywords: Chronic hepatitis B, Advanced liver fibrosis, Compensated cirrhosis, Pegylated interferon α-2a

Introduction

Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a major global health burden, causing more than 780,000 deaths annually and affecting over 250 million chronic carriers worldwide [1]. Up to 40% of untreated chronic hepatitis B (CHB) patients may develop liver fibrosis or cirrhosis [2]. Hepatic fibrosis, a common complication of CHB, results from an imbalance in the production, deposition, and degradation of extracellular matrix components. This process is exacerbated by persistent viral replication and hepatic inflammation, and may progress to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. Evidence indicates that effective anti-HBV therapy can significantly slow or even reverse the progression of liver fibrosis [3].

Currently, two main classes of anti-HBV drugs are available: nucleoside analogues (NAs) and pegylated interferon α (PEG-IFN α). Long-term NA therapy can mitigate hepatic inflammation and improve or reverse liver fibrosis [4, 5]. Studies report that more than 60% of CHB patients exhibit regression of cirrhosis after 3–6 years of NA treatment, although nearly 40% show no significant histological improvement [6]. Some studies further indicate no marked differences in liver histology at 1 and 3 years after initiating NA treatment [7]. These findings suggest that although long-term NA treatment can yield histological benefits in patients with advanced fibrosis, a subset still fails to improve despite prolonged antiviral therapy.

PEG-IFN α−2a exhibits both immunomodulatory and antiviral effects, including epigenetic suppression of HBV covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA). Numerous studies have shown that CHB patients with low HBsAg levels following NA therapy achieve higher clinical cure rates when switched to or combined with PEG-IFN α−2a. Approximately 30%–50% of these patients achieve HBsAg clearance, allowing treatment discontinuation [8, 9], which further reduces hepatocellular carcinoma risk [10]. Compared with NA monotherapy, PEG-IFN α−2a combined with NAs leads to more significant histological improvement in CHB patients with liver fibrosis [11]. However, some studies report that despite improvement in most patients (68%), a subset (28%) still shows no change or even progression of fibrosis after PEG-IFN α−2a treatment [12]. Thus, the efficacy of PEG-IFN α−2a—particularly in promoting fibrosis regression—in patients with advanced fibrosis or compensated cirrhosis due to CHB requires further clarification. Moreover, current studies lack analysis of the characteristics and predictors of patients who experience fibrosis progression after interferon-based therapy.

Therefore, this study enrolled NA-experienced CHB patients with advanced fibrosis or compensated cirrhosis who were treated with PEG-IFN α−2a. We aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of PEG-IFN α−2a in this population and to identify clinical characteristics and predictive factors associated with liver fibrosis progression.

Materials and methods

Study design

This real-world, two-center, retrospective observational study enrolled CHB patients from Xuzhou Medical University Affiliated Hospital and Shenzhen Third People’s Hospital between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2022. Inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 18–60 years; (2) prior NA treatment for ≥ 1 year with HBV DNA < 20 IU/mL;(3) combined PEG-IFN α−2a treatment for 48 weeks (180 µg/week, subcutaneously; Xiamen TEBIO Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) with 24 weeks of follow-up; (4) evaluation of liver fibrosis before PEG-IFN α−2a treatment and six months after completion. The initial assessment of liver fibrosis for advanced liver fibrosis or compensated cirrhosis requires: (a) METAVIR stage ≥ F3 on histopathology;; (b) Liver stiffness measurement (LSM) ≥ 9.4 kPa by transient elastography, with normal total bilirubin and ALT < 5 × upper limit of normal (ULN), valid measurements ≥ 10, success rate > 60%, and IQR/median < 0.3;; (c) ultrasound or MRI showing coarse echotexture, hypoechoic/hypodense nodules, with or without enlarged portal vein (≥ 1.3 cm) or splenomegaly [13, 14]. (5) Baseline HBV DNA < 20 IU/mL and normal or mildly elevated ALT. When multiple fibrosis assessment methods were performed during follow-up for the same patient and yielded inconsistent results, the following hierarchical decision-making process was used to determine the final fibrosis status classification: First Priority: Histological results. If a liver biopsy F-score was available, it served as the gold standard for determination. Second Priority: Transient elastography. In the absence of histological data, LSM values were prioritized due to their high accuracy and reproducibility for quantitative assessment of liver fibrosis/cirrhosis. Third Priority: Comprehensive imaging evaluation. When only ultrasound or MRI results were available, a comprehensive judgment was made based on morphological features and signs related to portal hypertension. Exclusion criteria included decompensated cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, liver transplantation, cholestasis/biliary insufficiency (total bilirubin > 1.5 × ULN, direct/total bilirubin ratio > 0.5, and alkaline phosphatase or GGT > 2 × ULN) [15], pregnancy, immunocompromised status, autoimmune diseases, coinfection with other hepatitis viruses, other liver disease etiologies, severe psychiatric disorders, or severe comorbidities.

Clinical and laboratory data

Demographic and treatment data were retrieved from electronic medical records. Patients were followed every 12 weeks from treatment initiation until 24 weeks post-treatment. Assessments included HBV DNA, HBV serology, liver and kidney function, and complete blood count. Liver ultrasound, MRI, FibroScan, or Metavir scoring was performed before and 24 weeks after PEG-IFN α−2a therapy. MRI parameters included: Axial T2-weighted fast spin-echo: TR/TE 3000/85 ms, slice thickness 5 mm; Axial DWI: single-shot echo-planar imaging with b-values 0, 50, 500, 800 s/mm2; Axial T1-weighted 3D gradient-echo (e.g., VIBE) for dynamic contrast-enhanced scans: TR/TE 3.6/1.3 ms, flip angle 10°, slice thickness 3 mm. Liver nodules were diagnosed using LI-RADS v2018. Fibrosis assessment included ADC values among other parameters. All personnel assessing fibrosis were blinded to clinical data and treatment time points. Liver biopsy slides were evaluated independently by two hepatopathologists, with disagreements resolved by consensus. LSM quality (IQR/median) was assessed per standard protocol. Key imaging findings were evaluated by two radiologists with good inter-observer agreement (kappa = 0.725).

Outcome assessment

The primary outcome was fibrotic prognosis 6 months after PEG-IFN α−2a treatment. Secondary outcomes included HBsAg and HBeAg seroconversion or loss. The criteria for classifying fibrosis status (progression, regression, or stability) were defined as follows:(1) Histology: Progression was defined as an increase in the Metavir F stage by ≥ 1. Regression was defined as a decrease in the Metavir F stage by ≥ 1. (2) Transient Elastography: Progression was defined as an increase in the liver stiffness measurement (LSM) value by ≥ 25%. Regression was defined as a decrease in LSM by ≥ 25%. (3) Imaging Assessment (Ultrasound/MRI): Progression was defined as the emergence of new or definitive signs of cirrhosis (e.g., nodular liver surface, progression of splenomegaly, new-onset ascites, or portal collateral circulation). Regression was defined as the clear improvement or disappearance of these signs. Cases with only mild, nonspecific echotexture changes or minor fluctuations in spleen size were excluded. When multiple assessment methods were performed for the same patient during follow-up and yielded inconsistent results (for instance, LSM suggested improvement while imaging indicated stability), the following hierarchical decision-making process was used to determine the final fibrosis status classification: First Priority: Histological results. Second Priority: Transient elastography findings. Third Priority: Comprehensive imaging assessment [16, 17].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized data characteristics. Categorical data are presented as frequencies (percentages). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (normally distributed) or median (range) (non-normally distributed). Group comparisons used Student’s *t*-test, Mann–Whitney U test, chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Predictive models were developed using penalized logistic regression with Firth’s correction. Missing data were handled by multiple imputation (R mice package). All tests were two-sided; P < 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0, R 4.5.1, and GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of 164 screened patients, 102 were included (median age 42 years; 80.4% male). At baseline, 35 (34.3%) were HBeAg-positive and 67 (65.7%) HBeAg-negative. NAs included entecavir (ETV, 66.67%), tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF, 18.62%), tenofovir alafenamide (TAF), and tenofovir mofenufen (TMF). All patients had received NA for ≥ 1 year. Advanced fibrosis or compensated cirrhosis was confirmed by histology, imaging, or elastography (Fig. 1).

Clinical characteristics of the completed liver fibrosis population and the unfinished group

Clinical characteristics of patients with liver fibrosis progression

The clinical indicators of 16 patients with liver fibrosis progression were compared with those of 53 patients who showed no progression, as shown in Table 3. There were no significant differences in the baseline characteristics between the two groups. However, patients with liver fibrosis progression exhibited significantly higher levels of ALT (70.0 [45.3–100.5] vs 45.0 [32.0–64.0]; P = 0.033), AST (61.0 [40.3–83.5] vs 43.0 [31.5–53.5]; P = 0.045), and GGT (80.0 [38.5–194.3] vs 51 [33.0–66.5]; P = 0.044) at 12 weeks of PEG-IFN α−2a treatment than those without progression. At 24 weeks of PEG-IFN α−2a treatment, the levels of AST (56.0 [41.3–65.8] vs 38.0 [34.5–46.0]; P = 0.006) and GGT (99.5 [66.0–129.0] vs 50.0 [35.5–69.5]; P = 0.001) in the progression group remained significantly greater than those in the nonprogression group, and the PLT (85.0 [71.3–93.8] vs 112.0 [76.5–137.0]; P = 0.009) was significantly lower than those in the nonprogression group (Fig. 2). The white blood cell (WBC) and neutrophilic granulocyte (NE) counts were also lower in the nonprogressive group than in the nonprogressive group at 24 and 48 weeks. Notably, there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of surface antigen levels or the proportion of HBeAg-negative patients at different stages of PEG-IFN α−2a treatment. Additionally, there were no significant differences in the rates of HBsAg or HBeAg loss at the treatment endpoint between the two groups.

Predictive model

To identify early predictors of fibrosis progression, we developed a penalized logistic regression model with bootstrap internal validation. Due to multicollinearity, predictors were grouped:Set 1: GGT, PLT, WBC;Set 2: GPR, ALT, WBC. Assessments were at baseline, 12, and 24 weeks. The model retained GGT at 12 and 24 weeks (Set 1) and GPR at 12 and 24 weeks (Set 2) as independent predictors (Table 4). The 24-week GPR model showed the best performance (optimism-corrected AUC = 0.805) (Figs. 3, 4). Furthermore, as shown in Table 4, the timepoint significantly influenced model performance. The mean corrected AUC for the 24-week models was 0.785, whereas all three models at the 12-week timepoint had AUCs < 0.7, indicating suboptimal predictive performance. At baseline, no significant differences were observed in clinical indicators between the progression and non-progression groups, and the penalized logistic regression analysis did not identify any independent predictors of fibrosis progression. Additionally, we included the Aspartate Aminotransferase to Platelet Ratio Index (APRI) and the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index for comparison with GPR. Similarly, predictors were divided into two sets: Set 1 included APRI, GGT, and WBC; Set 2 included FIB-4, GGT, and WBC, again covering baseline, 12-week, and 24-week timepoints. Penalized logistic regression analysis showed that only APRI at the 12-week timepoint of interferon therapy was retained alongside GGT (Intercept = 1.203; Coefficients: GGT = 0.139, APRI = 0.021) as predictors of fibrosis progression. However, GGT exhibited a stronger predictive capacity (coefficient 0.139 vs. APRI's 0.021), with APRI being nearly excluded under stringent penalization (coefficient approaching zero). Moreover, adding APRI provided only a minimal performance improvement compared to using GGT alone (AUC: 0.628 vs. 0.635).

The optimal GPR cut-off was 0.234 (Youden index), with sensitivity 75% and specificity 83%. Bootstrap 95% CI: [0.143, 0.333]. Decision curve analysis showed a net benefit of 0.1191 near this threshold (Fig. 5). The model correctly identified 81.25% of progressors and avoided intervention in 69.81% of non-progressors. Compared to the null model (which included only the intercept term, assigning all patients the same baseline risk of 23.2%), the 24-week GPR model demonstrated highly significant reclassification improvement.: continuous NRI = 0.972 (95% CI: 0.000–1.160, P = 0.008), categorical NRI = 0.486 (95% CI: 0.259–0.711, P < 0.001), IDI = 0.024 (95% CI: 0.010–0.040, P = 0.002). The model reclassified 75.0% of progressors to higher risk and 73.6% of non-progressors to lower risk (Table 5).

Performance of 24-week GPR model

To further validate the reliability of our findings, we performed a Firth's penalized maximum likelihood regression analysis within the liver stiffness measurement-based subgroup (n = 22). The results (Table 6) demonstrated that the GPR at 24 weeks of interferon therapy was an independent predictor of liver fibrosis progression in this subpopulation (P < 0.05). While the GGT level at 24 weeks of interferon therapy demonstrated a relatively high AUC, it did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.055).

Discussion

Approximately 80–90% of hepatocellular carcinoma cases develop from a background of liver fibrosis or cirrhosis [18]. Thus, halting or reversing the progression of fibrosis is crucial for reducing liver cancer risk. Our study demonstrates that nearly half of nucleoside analogue (NA)-experienced chronic hepatitis B (CHB) patients with advanced fibrosis or compensated cirrhosis showed regression of liver fibrosis 24 weeks after completing PEG-IFN α−2a therapy. These results are consistent with previous reports [11, 12, 19], confirming that PEG-IFN α−2a therapy can can ameliorate liver fibrosis in this population. However, a subset of patients still exhibited no significant histological improvement, and 23.2% experienced fibrosis progression. The proportion of patients with progressive fibrosis in our cohort was higher than that reported in some prior studies [12], which may be attributed to the higher prevalence of cirrhosis in our population or the use of different fibrosis assessment criteria—incorporating not only histology but also elastography and imaging. These observations underscore that although PEG-IFN α−2a-based therapy may lead to functional cure in some patients with advanced fibrosis or compensated cirrhosis, the risk of fibrosis progression persists and warrants continued vigilance. It should be noted that our study population was predominantly male, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to female patients. Further validation in larger, more gender-balanced cohorts is needed to confirm the broader applicability of these results.

To our knowledge, no previous studies have identified factors predictive of liver fibrosis progression during PEG-IFN α−2a therapy. Elevated GGT levels have been previously linked to fibrosis progression. With advancing fibrosis, injury to hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells worsens, leading to impaired clearance of gamma-glutamyl transferase [20]. In our study, GGT levels at both 12 and 24 weeks of interferon therapy were significantly higher in the fibrosis progression group compared to the non-progression group. Penalized logistic regression identified GGT at 12 and 24 weeks as independent predictors of fibrosis progression, with GGT at 24 weeks showing good predictive performance (AUC = 0.765). Platelet count is also recognized as a simple biomarker for liver fibrosis across various etiologies, including non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [21], hepatitis B [22], and hepatitis C [23]. In our cohort, platelet counts at 24 weeks were significantly lower in patients with fibrosis progression, although they did not retain independent predictive value in multivariate analysis. Furthermore, while elevated aminotransferase levels during PEG-IFN α−2a therapy are often regarded as favorable for HBsAg loss [24], they might also promote fibrosis progression, particularly in patients with cirrhosis [25, 26]. In this study, both ALT and AST levels at 12 and 24 weeks were higher in the progression group; however, ALT did not independently predict progression in the penalized regression model. Interestingly, we observed no association between fibrosis progression and HBsAg or HBeAg loss.

Previous studies have proposed the gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase to platelet ratio (GPR) as a non-invasive indicator for assessing severe fibrosis and cirrhosis [27, 28]. In the present study, we found that GPR at 12 and 24 weeks of interferon therapy could predict fibrosis progression in NA-experienced patients with advanced fibrosis or compensated cirrhosis receiving PEG-IFN α−2a. The 24-week GPR model exhibited strong performance across multiple metrics: excellent discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.805), clear clinical utility (net benefit = 0.119 on decision curve analysis), significant reclassification improvement (continuous NRI = 0.972, categorical NRI = 0.486), and enhanced integrated discrimination (IDI = 0.024). These findings collectively support the value and reliability of this model for assessing the risk of liver fibrosis progression. When the predicted probability based on this model exceeds the threshold of 0.234, it may help identify a patient subgroup in whom discontinuation of interferon therapy could be considered. This finding suggests that the model could potentially serve as a decision-support tool to guide individualized treatment in future prospective studies. However, the model has only been internally validated; external validation in independent, prospective cohorts from diverse regions and populations is essential before clinical application can be recommended. Although GPR has been studied as a non-invasive marker in untreated CHB patients [28], its predictive value in NA-experienced patients with advanced fibrosis/compensated cirrhosis receiving combination NA and PEG-IFN α−2a therapy was previously unclear. This study therefore addresses a gap in the literature specific to this high-risk clinical scenario.

The APRI and FIB-4 scores are also well-recognized non-invasive markers of liver fibrosis and cirrhosis in CHB patients [29]. Moreover, studies have shown that both FIB-4 [30, 31] and APRI [32, 33] can effectively predict liver fibrosis progression and clinical events. In our analysis, only the combination of APRI and GGT at 12 weeks showed predictive value for fibrosis progression, with GGT being the dominant predictor. The addition of APRI provided only minimal improvement over the univariate GGT model at 12 weeks (corrected AUC = 0.635). FIB-4 did not demonstrate predictive value at any time point. This may be due to differences in our patient cohort regarding baseline fibrosis severity and etiology. Furthermore, in multivariate models containing stronger predictors such as GGT, the predictive contributions of APRI and FIB-4 may have been masked or diminished. Within the specific context of our study, GPR appeared to outperform APRI and FIB-4 for predicting fibrosis progression, though this does not undermine the established value of APRI and FIB-4 in other clinical settings.

This study has several limitations. First, the dual-center, retrospective design, while providing real-world evidence, carries inherent risks of unmeasured confounding and selection bias. Although we applied stringent inclusion criteria, uniform data cleaning, and multivariate regression to control for known variables, residual confounding by unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Second, due to the challenges in obtaining serial liver biopsy data, the sample size was limited, and we utilized multiple fibrosis assessment methods reflective of real-world practice. This heterogeneity may introduce measurement error and reduce comparability across subgroups, potentially leading to underestimation or overestimation of certain risk factors. Third, although preliminary analysis showed no significant difference in NA distribution across fibrosis outcome groups, we did not perform a granular analysis accounting for NA type, treatment duration, or virological response. The varying pharmacological properties of different NAs remain a potential unmeasured confounder in interpreting liver fibrosis outcomes. Finally, the 24-week post-treatment endpoint is suitable for assessing short-term interferon efficacy but is inadequate for capturing the long-term dynamics of liver fibrosis, a chronic and evolving process. Thus, the predictive model and associated factors should be interpreted as short-term outcome predictors, and their long-term clinical relevance requires further validation. Future studies should involve prospective, large-scale cohorts with uniform, sensitive fibrosis assessment tools and longer follow-up. The impact of detailed antiviral treatment histories should also be thoroughly evaluated to confirm our findings.

Conclusion

This study confirms that NA-experienced CHB patients with advanced fibrosis or compensated cirrhosis can achieve a functional cure of chronic HBV infection through PEG-IFN α−2a-based therapy, although the risk of fibrosis progression requires careful consideration. The application of GPR in managing patients with advanced fibrosis or compensated cirrhosis receiving combined NA and PEG-IFN α−2a therapy helps identify individuals who remain at risk of disease progression despite active PEG-IFN α−2a treatment, thereby providing a rationale for implementing more refined, individualized treatment and monitoring strategies.
