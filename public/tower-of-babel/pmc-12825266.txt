Structured reflective sessions enhance knowledge and clinical skills in operating room technology students: a Solomon four-group study
Authors: Nasser Hassani Nia, Sahar Karami, Farimah Shahraki, Mahmoud Kohan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and aimClinical education plays a critical role in preparing operating room technology students for high-stakes, procedure-intensive environments. However, traditional training methods often fail to promote deep, transferable learning and structured reflective habits. Importantly, the effectiveness of structured reflective sessions in operating room technology education remains underexplored. This study therefore aimed to evaluate whether structured reflective sessions improve theoretical knowledge, practical skills, and educational satisfaction among operating room technology students.Materials and methodsWe conducted a Solomon four-group quasi-experimental study to separate the effects of the reflection-based intervention, pre-testing, and their interaction. Sixty undergraduate students in the 3rd and 5th semesters were assigned to four groups (intervention with pre-test, control with pre-test, intervention without pre-test, control without pre-test). The intervention used Gibbs’ six-stage model and comprised a 2-hour introductory workshop, weekly scenario-based analyses and guided small-group discussions over six weeks, and individual reflective journaling during clinical placements. Data were collected with a validated 20-item MCQ, Direct Observation of Procedural Skills (DOPS) checklists, and a standardized satisfaction questionnaire. Statistical analyses included paired and independent t-tests, ANCOVA (to adjust for baseline scores), and two-way ANOVA (to test pre-test × treatment interactions).ResultsParticipants in the intervention groups showed significantly higher post-test MCQ scores (mean = 17.46 vs. 12.86; p < 0.001) and higher DOPS scores (mean = 88.8 vs. 53.0; p < 0.001) compared with controls. Educational satisfaction was also greater in the intervention arm (mean = 78.35 vs. 70.97; p < 0.001). Solomon-design analyses indicated that these gains were attributable to the reflective intervention and not to pre-test sensitization. The intervention produced a large effect on clinical experience scores (partial η² = 0.41).ConclusionsStructured reflective sessions, delivered via Gibbs’ reflective cycle, significantly enhance theoretical understanding, psychomotor performance, and learner satisfaction among operating room technology students. We recommend integrating structured reflective practice into clinical curricula to strengthen the depth and sustainability of professional learning in high-complexity clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08431-w.

Introduction

One of the fundamental goals of medical education is the formation of students’ professional identity (PIF) [1]. Professional identity formation is closely linked to the development of clinical competence and, ultimately, to patient safety in clinical settings such as the operating room [2].

Despite its critical importance, traditional teaching methods still dominate medical and allied health education, often leading to routine and ineffective learning experiences [3]. Clinical learning, however, plays a vital role in developing practical skills and preparing students for their professional responsibilities [4]. For operating room technology students—future surgical technologists and essential members of the surgical team—acquiring adequate knowledge and skills is crucial to ensuring patient safety [5, 6]. Because professional identity shapes what students consider “acceptable clinical practice,” connecting PIF explicitly to competence helps justify educational strategies that aim to change behaviour as well as cognition [7].

Among various innovative approaches to enhance PIF, reflective practice stands out as particularly effective [8–10]. Reflection involves revisiting, analyzing, and interpreting past experiences, which fosters deeper understanding as well as the development of critical thinking and decision-making skills [11, 12]. Numerous studies have shown that active learning strategies, including reflection, significantly improve the effectiveness and retention of learning [13, 14]. Gibbs’ reflective cycle provides a structured, stepwise framework for guided reflection, comprising six stages: description, feelings, evaluation, analysis, conclusion, and action plan, which collectively support systematic learning from clinical experiences [15, 16].

Furthermore, Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory (ELT) offers a complementary framework, emphasizing a four-stage learning cycle: Concrete Experience, Reflective Observation, Abstract Conceptualization, and Active Experimentation, which underpins the transformation of clinical experiences into meaningful knowledge and skills [17]. Integrating Kolb’s ELT with Gibbs’ reflective cycle strengthens the theoretical foundation of this study and provides a clear rationale for structured reflective sessions to enhance knowledge, practical skills, and educational satisfaction [16, 17].

In operating room technology programs, clinical training remains the primary setting for acquiring practical skills and ensuring high-quality care and patient safety [18–20]. Nevertheless, evidence points to a significant gap in the quality of current clinical education, which often inadequately prepares students [21, 22]. This issue is exacerbated by the high-stress environment of operating rooms, increasing the likelihood of clinical errors and underscoring the need for updated training and ongoing evaluation [23, 24]. However, despite growing evidence for reflection in clinical settings, systematic, rigorously controlled evaluations of structured reflective interventions specifically targeting operating room technology students are scarce — creating an explicit research gap that this study addresses [11].

Research also indicates that insufficient clinical training and lack of timely, specific feedback can reduce educational effectiveness and negatively affect graduates’ professional performance [25–27] In Iran, feedback is often general and provided only at the end of terms, which fails to address individual students’ learning needs adequately [28]. Reflective practice, grounded in Gibbs’ cycle and supported by Kolb’s experiential framework, as an active learning strategy, can bridge students’ prior theoretical knowledge with their current clinical experiences, promoting deeper learning, self-regulation, and professional growth [29]. Direct observation tools such as DOPS, combined with structured reflection, are particularly well suited to measuring and reinforcing procedural competence in workplace-based education [30].

Recent studies demonstrate that reflection in clinical education can enhance critical thinking, lifelong learning, and professional competence [31, 32]. Despite a growing body of evidence highlighting the benefits of reflective practice in clinical education, its systematic integration into training programs remains limited and inconsistent—particularly in the context of operating room technology students, where its application and effectiveness have been largely overlooked, leaving a notable gap in the literature [11, 33]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of structured reflective sessions, based on Gibbs’ model and guided by Kolb’s experiential learning framework, on the theoretical knowledge, practical skills, and educational satisfaction of operating room technology students, using a Solomon four-group design to ensure robust control of pretest sensitization and other confounders [16, 17, 34].

Methods

Study design and setting

This quasi-experimental interventional study employed the Solomon four-group design, in which participants were assigned to four groups: two groups received a pre-test (one intervention, one control), while the other two did not (one intervention, one control). This design allows for independent evaluation of the intervention effect, the pre-test effect, and their interaction (Fig. 1).

Overview of the Solomon four-group study design

The study was conducted at Alborz University of Medical Sciences during the 2024–2025 academic year. Both classroom and clinical components were delivered across affiliated teaching hospitals (Imam Ali, Madani, and Alborz hospitals). Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Alborz University of Medical Sciences, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Participants and sampling

The study population comprised all operating room technology students enrolled in the “Principles of Circulating Nurse” (third semester) and “Surgical Techniques I” (fifth semester) courses. All eligible students were invited via census sampling (total N = 60; 30 per semester). Following recruitment, participants were randomly allocated to one of the four Solomon groups, ensuring equal representation from each semester. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated sequence, and allocation concealment was maintained through sealed, opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes prepared by a researcher not involved in enrollment or outcome assessment.

Coordination with the Department of Operating Room Technology and the Office of Clinical Education ensured integration with existing course schedules without disrupting formal training.

Intervention overview and dosage

The educational intervention consisted of structured reflective sessions based on Gibbs’ Reflective Cycle [16] and guided by Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory [17], delivered in three phases.

Assessment instruments included a 20-item multiple-choice questionnaire (MCQ), Direct Observation of Procedural Skills (DOPS), and a validated educational satisfaction questionnaire.

The reflective intervention totaled 9.5 h of facilitated instruction per cohort, comprising:

A 120-minute interactive workshop in week 5.

Five weekly 90-minute small-group reflective sessions (weeks 6–10).

Additionally, each student completed at least two individual written reflections (~ 30–45 min each) during clinical placement, monitored for completion.

Instructional materials included slides, structured reflection templates, clinical scenarios, and facilitator guides to ensure reproducibility.

Study phases and timeline

Weeks 1–4 (Pre-test phase)

MCQ and DOPS baseline assessments were administered to Groups A and B only. DOPS stations lasted 15–20 min per student, followed by 5–10 min of structured feedback.

Weeks 5–10 (Intervention phase)

Intervention groups (A and C) completed the structured reflective program. Week 5 included the introductory workshop, while weeks 6–10 involved scenario analyses and small-group discussions.

Weeks 9–10 incorporated at least two independent written reflections on real clinical encounters, verified by facilitators.

Weeks 11–14 (Post-test phase)

Post-intervention MCQ and DOPS assessments were administered to all groups. The satisfaction questionnaire was distributed to intervention groups at week 14.

Instruments, validity, and reliability

MCQ

20-item tests tailored to each semester, aligned with Bloom’s taxonomy; peer-reviewed for content validity.

DOPS

Five core procedural skills per semester assessed via standardized checklists in clinical settings; observations lasted 15–20 min with structured feedback.

Educational satisfaction questionnaire

20-item Likert-scale instrument (scoring 20–100) covering five domains; adapted from a validated tool.

All instruments underwent content validation by subject matter experts. Inter-rater reliability was high for both MCQ scoring and DOPS assessments (correlation coefficients > 0.90). Internal consistency for the satisfaction questionnaire was good (Cronbach’s α = 0.87). Full English versions of MCQ and DOPS instruments are provided as supplementary files.

Implementation fidelity and facilitator training

All instructors completed a standardized 3-hour training workshop prior to the intervention, including guidance on supervising guided and independent reflective sessions Facilitator guides, scenario scripts, and feedback rubrics were used to ensure consistency across sites.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: successful completion of prerequisite theoretical courses for each semester, no prior participation in structured reflective practice training, and voluntary participation with signed informed consent.

Exclusion criteria were: absence from more than one intervention session, failure to submit required reflective tasks, or voluntary withdrawal.

Data collection procedures

Baseline demographic and academic data were recorded before allocation. Trained assessors conducted MCQ and DOPS evaluations according to standardized procedures. To minimize assessor bias, DOPS raters were blinded to group allocation whenever feasible (i.e., raters were not informed whether the student belonged to an intervention or control group). All assessment data were securely stored and anonymized for analysis.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS (version X); statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The statistical plan was prespecified as follows:

Discussion

In the intervention group without reflective sessions, the mean multiple-choice question (MCQ) score declined significantly from 13.23 in the pre-test to 11.32 in the post-test (paired t-test, t = 9.33, p < 0.001). This finding indicates that clinical training alone, when not coupled with structured reflection, not only failed to improve theoretical knowledge but may lead to a decrease in assessed theoretical knowledge. Moreover, this result highlights that structured reflection must be considered a critical component in clinical education.

The decline in MCQ performance suggests that, in the absence of guided reflection, students may engage in superficial processing of clinical experiences, which can result in fragmentation of knowledge rather than consolidation. This interpretation aligns with the Gibbs’ Reflective Cycle, according to which deep learning requires the learner to critically evaluate cognitive, emotional, and behavioural aspects of their experience in order to plan for future practice. Previous research supports this view: Ahmadpour et al. demonstrated that guided reflective writing based on this model significantly improved nursing students’ cognitive abilities, empathy, and communication skills [15]. El Atmani’s qualitative study emphasised that the absence of structured analysis weakens cognitive self-awareness and theoretical understanding in clinical contexts [35]. Similarly, Bulman et al. found that the Gibbs cycle promotes critical thinking, theoretical integration and cognitive coherence, thereby improving students’ ability to analyse and consolidate knowledge [36]. From a theoretical perspective, the Cognitive Load Theory posits that learners’ working memory capacity is limited, thus instructional interventions should reduce extraneous cognitive load and manage intrinsic load to facilitate schema building and automation [29]. In addition, the Dreyfus Model of Skill Acquisition offers a framework through which the superior performance of more advanced students (fifth semester) can be explained: as learners progress from novice to competence and beyond, they rely increasingly on experience-based intuitive responses rather than rule-based processing [37].

According to Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory, the improvements observed in the reflection group indicate that students actively progressed through all four stages of the learning cycle. Their clinical experiences served as the ‘Concrete Experience,’ while the guided reflective prompts facilitated ‘Reflective Observation’ by allowing students to question, analyse, and emotionally process their actions [17].

Furthermore, the analytical discussions and structured reflective templates used in this study supported the ‘Abstract Conceptualization’ phase, enabling students to link their clinical encounters with underlying theoretical concepts and evidence-based reasoning [17].

Our findings from the Direct Observation of Procedural Skills (DOPS) test revealed significant improvements across all assessed competencies (p < 0.005), indicating that standard clinical training had a positive effect on practical abilities. Nevertheless, in the absence of structured reflective processes, those practical skills may remain procedural and lack deeper cognitive integration, thereby limiting their transferability to unfamiliar or complex clinical situations. Prior literature supports this interpretation; for instance, a systematic review by Efendi and colleagues found that combining clinical practice with virtual reality and structured reflection improves learning across cognitive, affective, and psychomotor domains [38]. From the vantage of the Dreyfus model, students at a more advanced stage (e.g., fifth semester) are better positioned to use reflection to move from mere proficiency to expert-level performance, while novices require more scaffolding and guided reflection.

The enhanced performance seen in practical skill assessments (DOPS) reflects the ‘Active Experimentation’ stage of Kolb’s model, in which learners apply newly formed concepts to improve their behaviour and performance in subsequent procedures [39].

Analysis of student satisfaction showed that the mean score in the reflection group (65.26 ± 4.23) was significantly higher than in the control group (37.46 ± 5.12) (t = 16.19, p < 0.001). The satisfaction questionnaire used included domains such as instructional quality, instructor interaction, clinical environment, perceived learning, and practical opportunities. These results suggest that structured reflection fosters a more meaningful educational experience by activating working memory, improving behavioural awareness, enhancing feedback uptake, and promoting self-assessment skills. Similar findings have been reported: Al-Salameh et al. found that nursing students participating in reflective sessions reported significantly higher satisfaction with their clinical learning experience [40]. Artioli et al. noted that the inclusion of reflection in clinical settings enabled students to move from rote imitation to behavioural analysis and self-correction [41]. Gunarto and Hariti found that reflective practices improved educational satisfaction and reduced clinical anxiety [42]. Together, our findings and prior studies affirm that structured reflection significantly enhances the overall quality of clinical education and correlates strongly with increased student satisfaction.

Together, these findings demonstrate that structured reflective sessions facilitated a complete experiential learning cycle for students, thereby transforming raw clinical exposure into integrated, applicable, and durable knowledge [43].

Importantly, comparison of students who engaged in structured reflection with those who did not reveal significantly higher theoretical knowledge scores in the intervention group, particularly in complex domains such as “Principles of the Mobile Patient.” The Solomon four-group design helped to mitigate pre-test sensitisation as a confounding factor, verifying that observed knowledge gains were attributable to the reflective intervention rather than mere exposure to the pre-test. This methodological strength aligns with prior research by Orosova et al., which also reported minimal pre-test effects [44]. As such, structured reflection appears to function as an independent educational component, rather than merely an adjunct to clinical exposure. From a cognitive theoretical perspective, embedding reflection into training may reduce extraneous cognitive load and support schema construction and automation, thereby enhancing both learning and performance.

This study is subject to several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the generalisability of its findings. Second, there is a possibility of instructor bias in DOPS ratings, despite acceptable inter-rater reliability. Third, the assessment was carried out over a short-term period, and long-term retention of knowledge and skills and their transfer to actual professional practice remain unassessed. Fourth, the Hawthorne effect cannot be ruled out; students in the intervention group may have performed better simply because they were aware of participating in a special study. Additionally, although a robust methodological design was used, factors such as individual differences in motivation or prior experience may have influenced results and were not fully controlled. Therefore, the interpretation of results should be made with caution and future research should include multi-centre studies with extended follow-up and assessment of real-world transferability.

In summary, the findings of this study indicate that combining clinical training with structured reflection can simultaneously enhance theoretical knowledge, practical skills, and student satisfaction. Accordingly, we recommend that clinical education programmes incorporate reflective components as integral elements, and that the design of these reflection sessions be tailored to students’ developmental stage. Further research should investigate long-term retention of learning, transfer to workplace performance, and comparative studies across diverse institutional settings. Overall, structured reflection has the potential to strengthen the bridge between theoretical knowledge and clinical practice and plays a pivotal role in the professional development of students.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, the study employed a robust Solomon four-group design, which strengthens internal validity. Second, multiple outcome measures were used, including MCQ for theoretical knowledge, DOPS for practical skills, and a student satisfaction questionnaire, providing a comprehensive assessment of learning outcomes. Third, structured reflective sessions were integrated into clinical training, allowing students to engage in deeper learning.

This study also has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single university, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other institutions or cultural contexts. Second, although inter-rater reliability for the DOPS was high, the assessments were not blinded, introducing a potential for performance bias. Third, the study measured immediate post-intervention outcomes; future research should investigate the long-term retention of knowledge and skills gained through reflective practice. Finally, participants in the intervention group were aware of the special training, and a Hawthorne effect cannot be entirely ruled out.

Practical Implications: The findings suggest that incorporating structured reflective sessions into clinical education can enhance theoretical knowledge, practical skills, and student satisfaction. Educators should consider integrating reflection as a core component of clinical curricula and tailor sessions to students’ developmental stage to maximize learning outcomes.

Conclusion

The results of this study demonstrate that the integration of structured reflective sessions following clinical training significantly enhances learning outcomes for operating room technology students. This positive impact was observed across increased theoretical knowledge, improved clinical skills, and higher student satisfaction with the educational process. By directly addressing the study objectives, the findings confirm that structured reflection effectively supports deeper learning and professional development in complex clinical environments. Structured reflection activates deeper cognitive engagement, fosters professional self-awareness, and improves students’ performance in real operating room settings.

Overall, the core objective of the study—to evaluate the impact of structured reflective sessions on knowledge, skills, and satisfaction—was clearly achieved. The results suggest that incorporating structured reflection into clinical training can serve as a practical and efficient educational strategy for preparing students for professional practice. Given the consistency of these outcomes, structured reflection should be considered a valuable component of modern clinical education models.
