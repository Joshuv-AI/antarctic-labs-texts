Prevalence of Clinical Vitamin A Deficiency and Associated Factors Among Children Aged 24–59 Months in Finote Selam Town, Ethiopia: A Community Based Cross‐Sectional Study, 2022
Authors: Molla Mekuria, Getachew Tesfaw Walle, Zewdie Aderaw, Mekuriaw Alemu Tenaw, Etsehiwot Debe Worku, Melese Linger Endalifer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimVitamin A deficiency is a public health problem in developing countries including Ethiopia. Thus, its prevalence and associated factors in each locality is essential for tackling the problem since the cause differs across geographical areas and community to community. The aim of this study was to assess the prevalence of clinical vitamin A deficiency and associated factors among preschool children aged 24–59 months in Finote Selam Town, Northwest Ethiopia, 2022.MethodsA Community‐based cross‐sectional study design was conducted from April 1 to April 30/2022. A total of 607 preschool children aged 24–59 months were enrolled through a simple random sampling technique. Data on clinical signs of vitamin A deficiency (xerophthalmia) were collected via detailed clinical histories and physical eye examinations conducted by trained ophthalmic personnel. No biochemical testing of serum retinol was performed. Data was collected through an interviewer‐administered structured and pre‐tested questionnaire. Bivariate (p‐Value < 0.25) and multivariable (p‐Value < 0.05) binary logistic regression analysis was carried out using SPSS version 22. An adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with the 95% confidence interval was used to measure the association between the outcome and independent variables. The Multicollinearity test was checked using VIF < 10. The Hosmer and Lemeshow test was fitted (p = 0.79).ResultsFive hundred and ninety‐one children aged 24–59 months were included in the study, giving a response rate of 97.4%. The overall prevalence of clinical vitamin A deficiency among children aged 24–59 months was 3.7% [95%CI; (2.3–5.6], comprising night blindness (2.2%), Bitot's spots (1.35%), and superficial corneal ulceration (0.15%), while conjunctival xerosis, corneal xerosis, and corneal scars exhibited zero prevalence. The odds of vitamin A deficiency were 4.89 [95% CI: 1.63–14.71] times higher among children whose mothers did not have postnatal care visits. Children aged 48–59 months were 5.43 (95% CI: 1.40, 21.0) times more likely to develop vitamin A deficiency compared with those children aged 24–35 months. Children who had been ill in the past 2 weeks were 5.85 [95%CI: 1.84, 18.59] times more likely to develop clinical vitamin A deficiency.ConclusionThe prevalence of clinical vitamin A deficiency was relatively high as compared to the national figures, indicating a moderate public health problem according to World Health Organization criteria. Targeted, practical preventative interventions to minimize clinical vitamin A deficiency should be designed, and future large‐scale biochemical studies evaluating serum retinol levels are recommended.

Background

Vitamin A serves as a critical fat‐soluble micronutrient necessary for maintaining ocular health, physical growth, immunological response, and cellular stability [1, 2, 3]. Due to elevated developmental needs and a high susceptibility to infectious diseases, preschool children represent a primary high‐risk group [4, 5, 6].

Diagnostics involve both clinical observations such as Bitot's spots and night blindness (nyctalopia and subclinical biochemical markers, specifically serum retinol levels below 0.70µmol/L. Although serum retinol concentration is the WHO‐recommended gold standard for assessing subclinical Vitamin A deficiency, clinical assessment of xerophthalmia manifestations is frequently deployed in resource‐limited field settings due to compounding logistical and financial constraints [7, 8].

Annually, between 250,000 and 500,000 children develop keratomalacia and progress to irreversible blindness, with up to half dying within a year of vision loss; this directly exacerbates child mortality from severe concurrent infections that succumb to blinding xerophthalmia, contributing to approximately 0.7 million deaths, roughly 6.5% of the global mortality rate [8].

Regional data showed the highest rates in Afar (4.8%) and Amhara (4.24%), while Tigray (1.7%) and South Nation and Nationality people's Republic of Ethiopia (SNNPR) (1.4%) recorded the lowest [6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12].

Mitigation of VAD relies on dietary variety, the fortification of staple foods, and scheduled supplementation. In Ethiopia, industrial fortification frameworks target widely consumed vehicles, specifically edible cooking oils and wheat flour, which are enriched with Vitamin A and key micronutrients to protect vulnerable populations [13]. Ethiopia integrated high‐dose Vitamin A capsule supplementation into the Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) network infrastructure in 1995. Ethiopia achieved 93% coverage by 2008, leading to a 47% reduction in night blindness and a 15% drop in xerophthalmia [8, 13, 14].

Investigating local prevalence profiles remains critical. Finote Selam Town presents a unique demographic context characterized by high urban‐rural migration density, specific localized agricultural practices focused on cash‐crop trade rather than diverse micronutrient production, and a documented regional burden of malnutrition within the Amhara region. Given the complete absence of local clinical data and the total lack of accessible biochemical diagnostic infrastructure in the area, this study uses standardized clinical assessment to bridge the public health tracking gap and guide localized intervention designs. Given the ongoing prevalence of risk factors despite routine supplementation, this study utilizes clinical assessment to investigate VAD prevalence and its contributing factors among children in Finote Selam Town, Northwest Ethiopia.

Methods and Materials

Study Design, Area and Period

A community‐based cross‐sectional study design was applied to assess CVAD. The study was conducted from April 1 to April 30, 2022, in Finote Selam Town, Northwest Ethiopia. It is far away 376 from the capital city, Addis Ababa. The town is structured with six kebeles (the smallest administrative unit in Ethiopia), with a population of 56,960; of those 4838 children were 24–59 months. There is an existing health education program that prevents disease and promotes health at the community level. Totally 12 health extension workers were recruited for the community‐based education program.

Eligibility Criteria

Those children with known fat malabsorption syndromes (such as medically diagnosed chronic diarrheal disease, celiac disease, or cystic fibrosis), known unrelated eye diseases (such as severe ocular trauma or congenital cataracts), children with severe cognitive or developmental disabilities hindering history tracking, or those who received a high‐dose Vitamin A capsule supplement within the preceding 6 months (to prevent false‐negative clinical presentations) were excluded from the study.

Sampling Techniques and Procedure

A simple random sampling technique was used to select the children aged 24–59 months from selected kebeles. Initially, 3 kebeles were selected through a simple random sampling technique. The sampling frame was developed from a community‐based health information system of the family folder in health posts. Thenafter, study subjects were selected through a systematic random sampling technique usingcomputer‐generated random numbers. Each household was visited at least three times to reduce the non‐response rate; if the child was not available in three visits, they would be replaced with another household.

Operational Definitions

Adequate dietary diversity: If the child (aged 24–59 months) consumed at least four food groups from seven food groups prior to the survey within the previous 24 h [16]. The food groups were Grains, roots, and tubers [17], Legumes and nuts [1], Dairy products [6], Flesh foods [18], Eggs [3], Vitamin‐A rich fruits and vegetables [19], Other fruits and vegetables.

Night blindness: is considered as child does not have a problem seeing during the day but does have a problem seeing in low light or at night by asking mothers/care givers [7].

Bitot's spot: ‐a slightly elevated, white foamy lesion that usually appears near the limbus at three or nine o'clock as the temporal part of the bulbar conjunctiva [20].

VAD: A child was classified as clinically deficient if they presented with a history of night blindness OR exhibited one or more physical clinical signs of xerophthalmia, such as Bitot's spots, conjunctival xerosis, corneal xerosis, corneal ulceration, or corneal scars according to WHO diagnostic standards [15].

Data Collection Tools, Procedure, and Measurement

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire composed of five themes. The questionnaire was adapted from validated diagnostic tools utilized in the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) and WHO nutrition indicators. The instrument was translated into Amharic, back‐translated by independent language experts, and pre‐tested on 5% of the total sample size in a neighboring non‐selected kebele to establish cultural suitability and technical clarity. Six data collectors (3 clinical nurses, 1 public health officer, and 2 experienced ophthalmic nurses) conducted the field survey. To standardize clinical eye examinations, the ophthalmic nurses completed a comprehensive 3‐day training program utilizing standardized WHO xerophthalmia reference slides and photographs. Inter‐observer reliability testing achieved a Cohen's kappa coefficient of 0.84. Physical field examinations were performed using a high‐intensity pen torch under bright, uniform natural daylight conditions. Any ambiguous or borderline ocular findings were immediately referred to and verified by a senior ophthalmic specialist at the Finote Selam General Hospital.

Dietary Assessment: ‐A 24‐h dietary recall approach was utilized to identify children's dietary habits. Accordingly, seven food groups including grains, legumes, vitamin A‐rich fruits and vegetables, other fruits and vegetables, egg, dairy products (milk, yogurt, and cheese), and meat were assessed to generate a dietary diversity score [21].

Data Management and Analysis

Data were checked, coded, and entered into EpiData version 4.6, then exported to SPSS version 22 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were presented using tables, figures, and text. A variable having p‐Value < 0.25 in Bivariable logistic regression was further regressed in multivariable logistic regression p‐Value < 0.05. Strength of association was determined using an AOR with 95% CI. Multicollinearity was checked (VIF < 10), and model fitness was confirmed by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (p = 0.79).

Discussion

The overall prevalence of CVAD in the current study was 3.7% (95% CI: 2.3, 5.6). In subgroup analysis the prevalence of night blindness was 2.2% and Bitot's spots were 1.35%, which officially classifies the study area as facing a moderate public health problem according to WHO 1996 guidelines.

This finding was consistent with studies conducted in different parts of Ethiopia: Lay Armachiho district, Central Gondar Zone (4.1%) [22], Addis Abeba, Ethiopia (3.5%) [11], and West Bengal in India (3.8%) [23]. This similarly relied strictly on clinical screening methodologies to detect surface eye lesions.

In contrast, this finding was higher than a similar study done in rural kebeles of Farta District within the South Gondar Zone (2.0%) [5]. This variation highlights the localized nature of nutrient vulnerabilities, potentially driven by Farta's distinct agro‐ecological focus on varied root crops compared to the commercial crop monocultures surrounding Finote Selam. Furthermore, our findings exceeded clinical rates observed in Maharashtra, India (1.9%) and broader regional pooled figures for South Asia (1.79%) [23]. It is critical to clarify that these comparative studies tracked clinical eye signs (xerophthalmia) rather than biochemical indices. Biochemical tracking of serum retinol typically reveals vastly higher prevalence rates, meaning direct comparisons must be restricted strictly to clinical cohorts to avoid misleading interpretations. A study was done in South Asia (1.79%) [23]. This variation might be due to the differences in awareness regarding maternal educational status, vitamin A supplementation, and consumption of vitamin A‐rich foods and socio‐economic status.

This study revealed non‐significant associations for several protective pillars, including high Vitamin A supplementation (VAS) coverage (89.5%) and adequate dietary diversity scores (90.2%). Scientifically, the persistence of clinical cases despite high coverage highlights a critical bottleneck: a high aggregate dietary score built primarily on grains and legumes may lack sufficient bioavailable fat content to facilitate optimal Vitamin A uptake. Furthermore, standard 6‐month capsule distribution intervals leave acute windows of vulnerability if a preschool child suffers an intense localized infection between cycles, quickly draining liver stores [24, 25].

Our study has several important limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the low total number of positive clinical outcomes (n = 22 cases) resulted in a very low Events Per Variable (EPV) ratio (~ 3.6), which explains the exceptionally wide confidence intervals observed in our multivariable model and implies a degree of statistical instability. Second, due to resource constraints, we did not apply a cluster design effect adjustment during sample calculation, meaning standard errors could be underestimated and the resulting P‐values may be overly optimistic. Third, historical night blindness tracking is vulnerable to maternal recall bias. Fourth, our reliance on a cross‐sectional framework prevents the inference of direct causal directions. Finally, the absence of senior ophthalmologists during primary screening and the lack of subclinical serum retinol tracking mean subclinical tissue deficiencies remain unquantified. These limitations are balanced by distinct study strengths, including a high response rate (97.3%), a robust community‐based randomized design, and the use of ophthalmic nurses standardized via formal WHO reference slide calibration.

Conclussions and Recommendation

In conclusion, clinical Vitamin A deficiency represents a moderate public health problem among preschool children in Finote Selam Town based on WHO epidemiological thresholds, driven primarily by the 2.2% prevalence of night blindness and 1.35% prevalence of Bitot's spots. Clinical risk is strongly concentrated among older preschool children (48–59 months of illness). Although this study is limited by a small clinical event size and lack of biochemical testing, its strengths lie in its community‐based randomized approach and strict adherence to WHO clinical standardization; those lacking maternal postnatal care access, and children suffering from recent acute. Based on these findings, we recommend the following practical, targeted actions:

Optimize Healthcare Windows: Local health extension agents should explicitly utilize routine maternal postnatal care (PNC) contacts as mandatory screening and tracking points for the child's nutritional and immunization status.

Targeted Screening for Older Preschoolers: Nutrition monitoring programs must aggressively screen children in the 48–59 month bracket, who often fall outside regular infant care checkups.

Integrated Illness Care: Local clinical protocols should systematically integrate Vitamin A tracking into the treatment of childhood illnesses, ensuring that children presenting with recent infections receive targeted nutritional support to prevent rapid depletion.

Future Research: The scientific community should design large‐scale studies integrating biochemical serum retinol analysis alongside clinical tracking to fully map out subclinical deficiencies in the Amhara Region.
