Abstract
. The M&M Game was created to help young kids explore probability by modeling a response to the question: If two people are born on the same day, will they die on the same day? Each player starts with a fixed number of M&M’s and a fair coin; a turn consists of players simultaneously tossing their coin and eating an M&M only if the toss is a head, with a person “dying” when they have eaten their stash. The probability of a tie can naturally be written as an infinite sum of binomial products, and can be reformulated into a finite calculation using memoryless processes, recursion theory, or graph-theoretic techniques, highlighting its value as an educational game. We analyze several extensions, such as tossing multiple coins with varying probabilities and evolving probability distributions for coin flips. We derive formulas for the expected length of the game and the probability of a tie by modeling the number of rounds as a sum of geometric waiting times.

Introduction

The M&M Game arose from attempts to model life expectancy in response to the following question asked by Cameron Miller and Kayla Miller (4 and 2 at the time) and answered that afternoon with their father, Steven Miller, who happened to have a bag of M&M’s on him:
If two people are born on the same day, do they die on the same day?
Later, they and Badinski, Huffaker, McCue, and Stone

\displaystyle\ =\ \ \sum_{n\ =\ k}^{\infty}\binom{n-1}{k-1}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2n}.

We give the unsimplified expression first, as it highlights the combinatorics behind the proof. On each turn, one of four equally likely events can happen: they both eat, just the first player eats, just the second eats, or neither eats. Because each player starts with
k

n

While the above formula gives the probability of a tie, it requires an infinite sum of products of binomial coefficients. Elementary calculations show the series converges exponentially fast, however, it is desirable to find a finite calculation, which also has the advantage of yielding an exact answer. In
[
BHM+17
]

Thus, we may ignore all turns with both coins landing on tails and treat the game as having three equally likely outcomes in each round, as follows:

•

Both players eat an M&M.

•

Only Player 1 eats an M&M.

•

Only Player 2 eats an M&M.

Each of these events occurs with a probability of 1/3, yielding the following result from

The probability the M

Expected Number of Turns of the M&M Game

We first consider the standard M&M Game and determine the expected number of turns based on various starting positions. To compute the expected number of turns we represent the M&M counts of each player as a state
(\text{Player 1's M{\&}M's},\text{Player 2's M{\&}M's})
and consider the expected number of turns and the respective probabilities of each of the paths from
(I_{1},I_{2})
to one of the axes, where
I_{i}
is Player
i
’s initial count. By symmetry, we may also consider this as a path from the origin to one of the lines
x\ =\ I_{1}
or
y\ =\ I_{2}
, and this is the convention we use for the remainder of this section.

Note that the only possible moves are
(1,1),(1,0),
or
(0,1)
, and each move is equally likely to occur before game terminates. During each turn, there is a
1/4
chance that both players keep the same number of M&M’s, and this is independent between turns. Hence, the number of turns before a state change follows a
\text{Geom}(1/4)
distribution with an expected value of
4/3
.

Furthermore, observe that if we fix the number of
(1,1)
(diagonal) moves, we may consider the remaining moves as either a north (or up) or an east (or right) move on a lattice. To account for each path, we can consider all of the penultimate states and then consider cases for which move is made at the end. Suppose one such penultimate state of interest is
(x,y)
, assuming without loss of generality that
x\leq y
. The number of diagonal (D) moves
k
up to
(x,y)
must be between
0
and
x
, inclusive. Fixing
k
, we have
x-k

(1,0)
(right, R) moves and
y-k

(0,1)
(up, U) moves. The total number of paths of this form is the same as the number of distinct arrangements of
k
“D”s,
x-k
“R”s, and
y-k
“U”s. This may be computed as

(2.1)

\dfrac{(x+y-k)!}{k!(x-k)!(y-k)!}\ =\ \binom{x+y-k}{k,x-k,y-k}.

Each such path has a length of
x+y-k
, and so the probability that
(x,y)
is the penultimate point and that there have been
k
diagonal moves up to that point is the number of such paths times
1/3^{x+y-k}
. Since we know the game ends in
x+y-k+1
state changes, the expected number of turns is then
\frac{4}{3}\cdot(x+y-k+1)
.

For each penultimate point, there are two possible state changes that can terminate the game, with the exception of
(I_{1}-1,I_{2}-1)
, which may be terminated with any state change. Since each of the terminating state changes are equally likely, it suffices to multiply
\mathbb{P}(\text{Path to (x, y) with k diagonal moves})
by
\text{\lx@text@lbrace Number of terminating state changes\lx@text@rbrace}/3
to obtain the probability of a full path containing
(x,y)
as the penultimate point and
k
diagonal moves up to
(x,y)
.

The penultimate points are
(I_{1}-1,y)
for
y\ =\ 0,1,\dots,I_{2}-1
and

(x,I_{2}-1)
for
x\ =\ 0,1,\dots,I_{1}-2
. Putting all of this together, we obtain our result on the game length.

Multiple Coins with Different Values

We now study our generalization of the M&M Game to multiple coins, where each coin landing on heads adds or removes a fixed number of M&M’s from a player’s stack. We consider only the case where the probability of heads for each coin is still
1/2
and there are exactly two players. To be precise, let
m
be the number of coins, and let
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
represent how each player’s stack changes if the
i
th
coin lands on heads (with positive values adding M&M’s to a stack and negative removing M&M’s). For shorthand, we denote this game by the tuple
(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
. We are mainly interested in the game represented by the tuple
(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
in the case that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}=0
, and we define its
dual game
to be the game represented by the tuple
(-a_{1},\dots,-a_{m})
.

For example, we consider the games represented by
(5,-3,-2)
and
(-5,3,2)
. We let

X_{r,t}:\text{ The sum of values of the three coins player }t\text{ tossed in round }r.

Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{r,1}\ =\ 5)\ =\ \mathbb{P}(X_{r,1}\ =\ 3)\ =\ \mathbb{P}(X_{r,1}\ =\ 2)\ =\ \mathbb{P}(X_{r,1}\ =\ -2)\ =\ \mathbb{P}(X_{r,1}\ =\ -3)=

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{r,1}\ =\ -5)\ =\ \frac{1}{8},

and

(3.2)

\mathbb{P}(X_{r,1}\ =\ 0)\ =\ \frac{1}{4}.

We note that although the values on each coin are different,
X_{r,1}
and
X_{r,2}
have the same distribution, and we observe that after flipping the sign of each coin, the two games – the original and its dual – still have the same transition and the same transition probability because we have fair coins and the sums are zero. For example, the probability of only coins
1
,
2
, and
3
landing on heads and the rest tails, giving a value of
a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}
, is the same as only the corresponding three in the dual game landing on tails and the rest heads with a resulting sum of
-(-a_{1}-a_{2}-a_{3})
. This observation is the basis for the following theorem.

\ReversingValuesDual

Proof.

We switch the analysis and start at
(0,0)
and find the probability of ending at
(k,k)
. The set of values of coins to toss in the game is
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{m})
and
\sum_{i\ =\ 1}^{m}a_{i}\ =\ 0
.

Let
N_{t}
be the number of turns for the
t
th
player in one game and
X_{r,t}
to be the sum of values of the coins player
t
tosses in round
r
in one game. We see that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{Tie})\ =\ \mathbb{P}(N_{1}\ =\ N_{2})\ =\ \sum_{n\ =\ 1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(N_{1}\ =\ n)\mathbb{P}(N_{2}=n).

By the law of total probability conditioning on the last toss,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N_{t}\ =\ n)\ =\ \sum_{\ell=1}^{\text{max}[S_{\mathcal{A}}:\mathcal{A}\subset\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}]}\mathbb{P}(k-\ell\leq X_{1,t}+\dots+X_{n-1,t}<k)\mathbb{P}(X_{n,t}\ =\ l),

where

S_{\mathcal{A}}:\text{ The sum of all elements in }\mathcal{A},\quad\mathcal{A}\subset\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}.

Here we only consider the case where
{S_{\mathcal{A}}}
is positive for the last round, otherwise one of the players would have already finished the game if a negative number were used to move to having
k
M&M’s.

We fix similar notation for the dual game. Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
be a subset of
\{-a_{1},-a_{2},\dots,-a_{m}\}
, and set
\widetilde{X_{r,t}}
to be the increment in the game with inverse coin values. Letting
\widetilde{N_{t}}
to be the number of turns for the other game with opposite coin values, the probability of a tie is

(3.5)

\mathbb{P}(\text{tie, opposite coin value})\ =\ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{N_{1}}=n)\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{N_{2}}=n),

and we see that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{N_{t}}=n)\ =\ \sum_{\ell=0}^{\text{max}[S_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}}:\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\subset\{-a_{1},\dots,-a_{m}\}]}\mathbb{P}(k-l\leq\widetilde{X_{1,t}}+\dots\widetilde{X_{n-1,t}}\leq k)\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{X_{n,t}}=\ell).

To show the game and its dual have the same probability of a tie, we need to prove

X_{r,t}\text{ and }\widetilde{X_{r,t}}\text{ have the same distribution}.

The idea is simple. For one game, by summing over all subsets
\mathcal{A}_{r}
of
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}
such that
S_{\mathcal{A}_{r}}=\ell
, we find

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{r,t}=\ell)\ =\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{A}_{r}:S_{\mathcal{A}_{r}}=\ell\\

\mathcal{A}_{r}\subset\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{m}.

For the dual game with opposite coin values,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{X_{r,t}}=\ell)\ =\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}}:S_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}}}=l\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}}\subset\{-a_{1},\dots,-a_{m}\}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}})\ =\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widetilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}}:S_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}}}=\ell\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}}\subset\{-a_{1},\dots,-a_{m}\}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{m}.

Note that
\mathcal{A}_{r}
’s and
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}}
’s are independently and identically distributed and determined by tossing
m
many fair coins. Therefore, we only need to ensure that the number of terms in the summation is the same for both games.

For all
\mathcal{A}_{r}\subset\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{m}\}
, say
\mathcal{A}_{r}\ =\ \{a^{\prime}_{1},\dots,a^{\prime}_{u}\}
(meaning for one player in one game, the coins with values
a^{\prime}_{1},\dots,a^{\prime}_{u}
land head and the rest land tail, and
a^{\prime}_{1}+\dots+a^{\prime}_{u}=\ell

\widetilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}}\ =\ \{-a_{1},\dots,-a_{m}\}\setminus\{-a^{\prime}_{1},\dots,-a^{\prime}_{u}\}\ =\ \{\widetilde{a^{\prime}_{1}},\dots,\widetilde{a^{\prime}_{v}}\}
, meaning in the game with opposite coin values, coins with value
\widetilde{a^{\prime}_{1}},\dots\widetilde{a^{\prime}_{v}}
land heads and the rest land tail. We observe that

(3.9)

\sum_{i\ =\ 1}^{m}a_{i}\ =\ 0\ =\ \sum_{i\ =\ 1}^{v}\widetilde{a^{\prime}_{i}}+\sum_{i\ =\ 1}^{u}(-a^{\prime}_{i}),

and therefore

(3.10)

\sum_{i\ =\ 1}^{v}\widetilde{a^{\prime}_{i}}\ =\ \sum_{i\ =\ 1}^{u}a^{\prime}_{i}=\ell.

Therefore, for all
\mathcal{A}_{r}\subset\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}

\widetilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}}\subset\{-a_{1},\dots,-a_{m}\}

S_{\mathcal{A}}=S_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}}
. Similarly, for all
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}}\subset\{-a_{1},\dots,-a_{m}\}

\mathcal{A}_{r}\subset\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}
such that
S_{\mathcal{A}}=S_{\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}}

\mathbb{P}(X_{r,t}=\ell)
and
\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{X_{r,t}}=\ell)

\mathbb{P}(X_{r,t}=\ell)\ =\ \mathbb{P}(\widetilde{X_{r,t}}=\ell)

An interesting outcome of the game is that with a negative denomination, the game might never end since the player can get so lucky that he always receives M&M’s to his stack. 4.
Evolving Coin Probabilities: an Exponential Distribution

In many real-world processes, probabilities are not static but evolve over time. For instance, in survival models, the likelihood of an event (such as death or system failure) increases as time progresses. To model such processes, we consider a variation of the original M&M Game. We still have two players starting with the same number of M&M’s, but now the probability of getting a head depends on how many M&M’s one has. Thus the probability of flipping heads changes dynamically as the game progresses. Specifically, the probability increases as the number of M&M’s decreases, capturing the idea that the closer a player is to “depletion” (death), the higher the probability of consuming another M&M. This variation allows us to analyze how dynamic changes in probability affect the likelihood of a tie; see for example
[
Mil17
]
for basic results in probability.

This behavior can be modeled using an exponential distribution, a common choice for describing time-dependent processes with a memoryless property. Let the probability of flipping heads,
\mathbb{P}(\text{heads})
, evolve based on the current number of M&M’s remaining. For each player, this probability is given by the following equation, which depends on the initial number of M&M’s
n
, the remaining M&M’s
m
, and the rate parameter
\lambda
:

(4.1)

\mathbb{P}(\text{heads})=1-e^{-\lambda(n-m+1)}

where

•

n
is the initial number of M&M’s,

•

m
is the current number of M&M’s remaining,

•

\lambda
is the rate parameter of the exponential distribution.

This setup ensures that as
m
decreases (i.e., as the player runs out of M&M’s), the probability of flipping heads increases. We aim to understand how this evolving probability affects the likelihood of a tie between the two players.

4.1.
Simulation Results

Simulations were conducted for various values of
n
(e.g.,
n=10
,
n=100
,
n=200
), and the results suggest that the initial number of M&M’s has little effect on the probability of a tie (see Figure
4
). Instead, the rate parameter
\lambda
is the dominant factor.

•

A higher
\lambda
leads to a rapid increase in the probability of flipping heads, which accelerates the depletion of M&M’s for both players and increases the likelihood of a tie.

•

A lower
\lambda
results in a slower increase in the probability of flipping heads, prolonging the game and reducing the probability of both players running out of M&M’s at the same time, thereby lowering the chance of a tie.

The results suggest that for
\lambda>1
, the probability of a tie increases significantly. As
n\to\infty
, the probability of a tie converges, with larger

\lambda
on the probability of a tie.

4.2.
Proposed Model: Gompertz Curve Fit

The probability of a tie as a function of
\lambda
can be modeled by a Gompertz distribution, which is commonly used to describe growth processes with an initial slow phase, followed by rapid acceleration and eventual saturation. This distribution has been employed in various contexts to model growth under constraints, as discussed in
[
DB18
]
. The general form of the Gompertz function is given by

(4.2)

P_{\text{tie}}(\lambda)\ =\ L\cdot\exp\left(-k\cdot\exp(-\lambda_{0}\cdot\lambda)\right),

where

•

P_{\text{tie}}(\lambda)
is the probability of a tie as a function of
\lambda
,

•

L
is the upper asymptote (the maximum probability of a tie),

•

k
controls the rate of growth,

•

\lambda_{0}
is the scale parameter that determines how quickly the probability changes with respect to
\lambda
.

The inflection point of the Gompertz curve occurs when
\lambda=1
, after which the probability of a tie increases rapidly. Based on the simulation results, the fitted parameters for the Gompertz curve are approximately

L\approx 1,\quad k\approx 1.22,\quad\lambda_{0}\approx 1.

Thus, we can express the probability of a tie as

(4.3)

P_{\text{tie}}(\lambda)\ \approx\ \exp\left(-1.22\cdot\exp(-\lambda)\right).

This empirical formula closely fits the simulated data, as shown in Figure
5
.

4.3.
Impact of
\lambda

From the simulation results, we conclude that the initial number of M&M’s has a negligible effect on the outcome. Instead, the rate parameter
\lambda
plays a crucial role in determining the probability of a tie. This motivates the following conjecture.

Conjecture 4.1
.

The probability of a tie,
P_{\text{tie}}
, in the M
&
M Game with an evolving probability of flipping heads that follows an exponential distribution is primarily governed by the rate parameter
\lambda
. For large
n
, the probability of a tie is given by the following approximation:

P_{\text{tie}}(\lambda)\ \approx\ \exp\left(-1.22\cdot\exp(-\lambda)\right),

where
\lambda
determines the speed at which the probability of flipping heads increases as M
&
M’s are depleted.

In essence, the probability of a tie is asymptotically independent of the initial number of M&M’s
n
and is primarily influenced by the rate parameter
\lambda
. To rigorously validate this claim, we conducted extensive Monte Carlo simulations, performing 10,000 runs for each configuration of
\lambda
and
n
. Using Pearson correlation analysis, we quantified the dependence of the tie probability on
\lambda
and
n
. The results show a strong positive correlation between
\lambda
and the tie probability, with
r=0.9881
, while the correlation with
n
is negligible,
r=-0.0027
.

These findings confirm that
\lambda
, which governs the rate of depletion, dominates the behavior of the game by synchronizing the consumption of M&M’s between players. As
\lambda
increases, the probability of a tie rises rapidly and saturates, irrespective of the initial number of M&M’s. In particular, for larger values of
\lambda
, the tie probability converges across different starting values of
n
, demonstrating its asymptotic independence. This behavior is clearly reflected in the results of our numerical simulations.

Future investigations may consider the effect of dynamically varying
\lambda
or introducing time-dependent changes to further analyze the synchronization dynamics of M&M consumption. Such extensions could provide deeper insights into the role of depletion rates in similar probabilistic models.

5.
Conclusion and Future Work

We have explored several extensions to the M&M Game, focusing on how changes in game mechanics and probability distributions impact the outcomes. We examined the effect of flipping the sign of coin values, introduced exponentially evolving probabilities, and calculated the expected number of turns for the standard M&M Game. There are numerous other directions for future work; we record a few below.

One could consider that the probability of flipping heads is not fixed, but for each player are independently drawn from a truncated normal distribution centered at
1/2
(or any other nice distribution supported on
[0,1]
). In this game, what is the probability of a tie, and how does it depend on
\sigma
and
k
?

In §
3
, we considered the generalized M&M Game where we flip multiple coins at once. However, depending on the mean value of the coins, it’s not immediately clear that the game will terminate in finite time, as we may encounter infinitely many loops in the game. For example, the number of M&M’s remaining could decrease first but recover to a previous state. When tossing a single coin, this question can be easily addressed by considering the memoryless property. For multiple coins, with positive and negative values, a rough equivalent would be erasing any loops that occur in the game; hence, further study of different types of random walks such as loop-erased random walks, see Lawler
[
Law99
]
, may be necessary for understanding this version of the game.

Finally, we briefly list several other promising directions for future work.

(1)

Investigating how different probability distributions (e.g., Poisson) influence the game’s outcomes.

(2)

Extending the analysis to multiplayer scenarios to understand how the probability of a tie changes with additional players.

(3)

Exploring asymmetric starting conditions, where players begin with different numbers of M&M’s.

(4)

Incorporating time-dependent probabilities to simulate more complex real-world processes.
