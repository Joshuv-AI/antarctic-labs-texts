The dimension of sparse and co-sparse random graph orders
Pu Gao, Arnav Kumar

Abstract
A random graph order is a partial order obtained from a random graph on [ n ] [n] by taking the transitive closure of the adjacency relation. The dimension of the random graph orders from random bipartite graphs ℬ ⁡ ( n , n , p ) \mathcal{B}(n,n,p) and from 𝒢 ⁡ ( n , p ) \mathcal{G}(n,p) were previously studied when p = Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / n ) p=\Omega(\log n/n) and when p p is not too close to 1. There is a conjectured phase transition in the sparse range at p = 1 / n p=1/n . In this paper, we investigate this conjectured phase transition and estimate the dimension of the partial orders arising from ℬ ⁡ ( n , n , p ) \mathcal{B}(n,n,p) and 𝒢 ⁡ ( n , p ) \mathcal{G}(n,p) when p = O ⁡ ( 1 / n ) p=O(1/n) . For the random bipartite order, we additionally estimate its dimension in the co-sparse regime, thereby closing all previously open ranges of p p . Finally, we establish a general upper bound on the dimension of partial orders based on their decompositions into suborders, a result that is of independent interest.

Introduction

Partially ordered sets are a classical subject of study in combinatorics and algebra, offering foundational tools and concepts that extend further to scheduling, data mining, optimization and other sciences. The study of random partial orders dates back to Kleitman and Rothschild
[
13
,
15
]
around 1970 who estimated the number of partially ordered sets on
n
elements. Their enumeration result immediately implies a number of properties for a uniformly random partially ordered set (poset) on
n
element. For instance, asymptotically almost surely (a.a.s.) such a random poset has exactly three layers, and the number of comparable pairs is approximately
\frac{3}{16}n^{2}
.
A more general model of random poset restricted to a fixed proportion
\rho
of comparable pairs was proposed and studied (for a certain range of
\rho
) by Deepak Dhar
[
9
]
, and this model is motivated by a statistical physics model of lattice gas with long-range three-body interactions. This model was further studied by Kleitman and Rothschild
[
14
]
and by Prömel, Steger and Taraz
[
17
]
for different ranges of
\rho
. Winkler
[
21
,
20
]
proposed a different model of random poset that comes from the intersection of
k
uniformly random permutations on
[n]
, and Winkler proved that for fixed
k
, the resulting random poset is a.a.s. connected and has diameter equal to three.
Finally, random poset arising from random graphs were introduced and studied in
[
4
]
and
[
10
]
. In this model we consider a random graph
\mathcal{G}_{n}
on vertex set
[n]
. The partial order
P=P([n],<_{P})
generated by
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is obtained by first including all comparable pairs
i<_{P}j
if
i<j
(order in natural numbers) and
ij\in E(\mathcal{G}_{n})
, and then taking the transitive closure. We denote this partial order by
P_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}
. In other words,
P_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}
consists of elements in
[n]
, where two elements
i
and
j
are comparable with
i<_{P}j
if there exists a sequence of vertices
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
such that
u_{1}=i
,
u_{k}=j
,
u_{1}<u_{2}<\cdots<u_{k}
and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
is a path in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
. Barak and Erdős
[
4
]
introduced the model
P_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}
for
\mathcal{G}_{n}=\mathcal{G}(n,p)
, the Erdős-Rényi random graph, and they estimated the width of
P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}
when
p
is a fixed constant between 0 and 1. Then, Albert and Frieze
[
2
]
studied the height, width, dimension and the first-order logic of
P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}
for fixed
p
. Alon, Bollobás and Janson
[
3
]

studied the number of linear extensions of
P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p=1/2)}
. The structures of sparse
P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}
when
p=o(1)
are studied by Bollob’as and Brightwell
[
7
,
6
,
5
]
including the anti-chains, the posts, and the dimension. The model
P_{\mathcal{G}_{n}}
for
\mathcal{G}_{n}=\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)
was introduced by Erdös, Kierstad and Trotter
[
10
]
, where
\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)
denotes the random bipartite graph on parts
A=[n]
and
B=\{n+1,\ldots,2n\}
where
ij
is an edge with probability
p
independently for every
(i,j)\in A\times B
. The poset
P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
has a simpler structure than
P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}
: two elements
i
and
j
are related by
i\leq_{P}j
if and only if
i\in A
,
j\in B
and
ij
is an edge in
\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)
. In other words, adjacency of vertices in
\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)
corresponds precisely to the order relations in
P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
.
The authors of
[
10
]
studied the dimension of
P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
, and proved the following.

Theorem 1

(Erdös, Kierstad and Trotter)
.

For every
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that for all
p
satisfying

\frac{\log^{1+\epsilon}n}{n}<p<1-n^{-1+\epsilon},

a.a.s.
\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}>(\delta pn\log pn)/(1+\delta p\log pn)
.

Their lower bound is tight up to a logarithmic factor, by the upper bound given by Fürhedi and Kahn
[
12
]
; see Theorem
5
below.
In the same paper
[
10
]
, they also asked many questions regarding
\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
, such as whether
\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
is unimodal as
p
increases? When does
\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
become at least
k
for constant
k
? What happens to
\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
when
p
is very close to 1? These questions remained open. As a comparison, Bollobás and Brightwell
[
6
]
proved the following regarding
\dim P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}
, improving earlier bounds given by Albert and Frieze
[
2
]
, and extending the range of
p
under study.

Theorem 2

(Bollobás and Brightwell)
.

The following hold a.a.s. for
\dim P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}
:

1.

If
1/\log\log n\ll p\leq 1-1/\sqrt{\log n}
then for every
\epsilon>0

(1-\epsilon)\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\log(1/(1-p))}}\leq\dim P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}\leq(1+\epsilon)\sqrt{\frac{4\log n}{3\log(1/(1-p))}}.

2.

If
\log n/n\leq p\ll 1/\log n
then
\dim P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}=\Theta(p^{-1})
.

3.

If
\tfrac{4}{5}\log n/n\leq p\leq(\log n-\log\log n)/n
then there exist fixed
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

c_{1}e^{pn}\leq\dim P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}\leq c_{2}e^{pn}\log^{3}n.

In view of Theorem
1
, there is a phase transition around
p=1/\log n
. Afterwards, for
1/\log n\ll p<1-n^{-1+\epsilon}
,
\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}\sim n
. There must be at least another phase transition at some
p>1-n^{-1+\epsilon}
since
\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
eventually drops back to two. In this paper, we capture this phase transition by determining the asymptotic order of the dimension of
P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
for all
p\geq 1-n^{-1+1/7}
, addressing a question (see
[
10
, Open problem (5.8)]
) of Erdős, Kierstead and Trotter: see Theorem
9
below. Theorem
2
shows that the behaviour of

\dim P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}
is quite different from
\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
: there are phase transitions around
p=\log n/n
and then around
p=1/\log n
. Bollobás and Brightwell predicted “a third transition phase, as
p
decreases through
c/n
, since the underlying random graph changes from being almost all in one component, to being a collection of small trees”. It is known that
\dim P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p=c/n)}\leq 4
when
c<1
(see Theorems
3
and
4
below), since
\mathcal{G}(n,p=c/n)
is a collection of small trees and unicyclic components. When
c>1
,
\mathcal{G}(n,p=c/n)
contains a giant component whose order is linear in
n
, and has many cycles and other complicated structures. For instance, for every fixed
k\geq 1
,
\mathcal{G}(n,p=c/n)
has a subgraph with minimum degree at least
k
, provided that
c
is sufficiently large (depending on
k
). Does “a third transition phase” appears in the way that right after
c
passes 1,
\dim P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}
immediately blows up to
\omega(1)
? Indeed, Bollobás and Brightwell specifically asked in
[
6
]
what happens to
\dim P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}
if
p=c/n
with fixed
c>1
.

In this paper we address the question of this predicted “third transition phase”, and in particular, whether the phase transition of random graph structure from a collection of small tree or unicyclic components to the appearance of a giant component is associated with a sudden jump of the dimension of the random graph order from at most four to
\omega(1)
. Rather surprisingly,
our result is negative: see Theorems
10
–
12
in Section
1.2
.

1.1
Preliminary and notations

Let
P
be a poset on a finite set of elements
V
. Without loss of generality we denote
V
by
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
where
n
is the number of elements in
V
. We always use
<_{P}
to denote the order in
P
, and use
<
to denote the order in natural numbers. For
x,y\in V
, we use
x||y
to denote that
x
and
y
are incomparable in
P
.

A realiser of a partial order
P
on a finite set of elements
V
is a set of total orders
\mathcal{R}=\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{k}\}
on
V
satisfying the following conditions.

1.

if
i<_{P}j
in
P
then
i<_{L}j
for every
L\in\mathcal{R}
; and

2.

if
i||j
in
P
then
i<_{L}j
and
j<_{L^{\prime}}i
for some
L,L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
.

The dimension of
P
is defined by the size of a minimum realiser of
P
.

We say a poset
P
is bipartite if the set of elements can be partitioned into two parts
A
and
B
such that if
x<_{P}y
then
x\in A
and
y\in B
, and we use
P(A,B)
to denote a bipartite poset, where
A,B
is a bipartition of the elements. Given a poset
P
and a subset
S
of the elements in
P
, let
P[S]
be the sub-poset of
P
induced by
S
, i.e. two elements
u,v
in
S
have the same relation in
P[S]
as they are in
P
.

Given a poset
P
, the dual of
P
is defined on the same set of elements of
P
, such that for every
x\neq y
,
x\parallel y
in
P^{\prime}
if and only if
x\parallel y
in
P
, and
x<y
in
P^{\prime}
if and only if
y<x
in
P
. Obviously,
\dim P^{\prime}=\dim P
.

Given a poset
P
, the comparable graph of
P
is the graph defined on
V(P)
, the set of elements in
P
, where two elements are adjacent if they are comparable in
P
. On the other hand, the cover graph (also called the Hasse diagram) of
P
is the graph defined on
V(P)
such that
xy
are adjacent in the cover graph if
x\neq y
,
x<_{P}y
and there is no
z
distinct from
x
and
y
such that
x<_{P}z<_{P}y
. Trotter and Moore proved that if
P
has a cover graph that is isomorphic to a tree then the dimension of
P
is at most three.

Theorem 3

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
P
be a poset whose cover graph is a tree. Then,
\dim P\leq 3
.

How about poset whose cover graph has a unique cycle? Bollobás and Brightwell
[
6
]
conjectured that such posets have dimension at most three. Abram and Segovia recently reported solving this conjecture
[
1
]
. The best known upper bound before
[
1
]
is four, which follows from the upper bound on the dimension of a poset whose cover graph is outerplanar
[
11
]
. There is also a short proof provided by Brightwell (from personal communications). Since his proof is short and self-contained, we include it below for completeness. Given a total ordering
L
on a set of elements
V
and
S\subseteq V
, let
L|_{S}
denote the total ordering on
S
obtained by restricting
L
on
S
. Given a subset of elements
S
of
P
, let
U_{P}[S]
and
D_{P}[S]
denote the up-set and the down-set of
S
, i.e.

U_{P}[S]=\{x:\ y\leq_{P}x\ \text{for some $y\in S$}\},\quad D_{P}[S]=\{x:\ x\leq_{P}y\ \text{for some $y\in S$}\}.

(1)

We may drop the subscript
P
when it is clear from the context. If
S=\{x\}
we write
U[x]
for
U[\{x\}]
, and we write
U(x)
for
U[x]\setminus\{x\}
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
P
be a poset with unicyclic cover graph. Then,
\dim P\leq 4
.

Proof.

This is a proof provided by Brightwell (via personal communications). Let
G
be the unicycle cover graph, and let
C
be the cycle in
G
.
Let
x
be a maximal element in
C
. Let
A
be the set of neighours of
x
in
G
that are greater than
x
in
P
, and let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
be an enumeration of the vertices in
A
. Let
W_{i}
be the component of
G-x
containing
u_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
. Let
W[x]=\{x\}\cup(\cup_{i=1}^{k}W_{i})
, and let
R=V(G)\setminus W[x]
. It is readily seen that both the cover graphs of the subposets of
P[W[x]]
and
P[R]
are acyclic, and hence each of them has dimension at most three. Moreover,
U(x)\subseteq W[x]
and
D(x)\subseteq R
. Let
\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}\}
be a realiser for
P[W[x]]
and
\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}\}
be a realiser for
P[R]
. Let
\hat{L}
be an arbitrary total ordering on the elements in
P
. Construct four linear orderigs on the set of elements in

as follows.

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}|_{R}<_{\pi_{i}}\tau_{i}|_{W[x]}\quad\text{for $1\leq i\leq 3$},

\displaystyle\hat{L}|_{W[x]\setminus{U[x]}}<_{\pi_{4}}\hat{L}|_{D(x)}<_{\pi_{4}}\hat{L}|_{U[x]}<_{\pi_{4}}\hat{L}|_{R\setminus D(x)}.

Note that the notation in the first inequality above means that first ordering elements in
R
according to
\sigma_{i}
, followed by elements in
W[x]
ordered according to
\tau_{i}
.
It is straightforward to verify that
\{\pi_{i}:\ 1\leq i\leq 4\}
is a realiser for
P
.

For a general poset, the following upper bound on its dimension given by Fürhedi and Kahn
[

]
is the best known, and has been proved to be close to being optimal

]

Theorem 5

(Fürhedi-Kahn
[

]
)
.

Suppose that
P
is a poset in which every element is comparable to at most

\dim P=O(k\log^{2}k)

The following lemma was used in the proof in
[

, which is useful in this paper as well. Note that

in the lemma below is not necessarily a realiser. The idea is that some operation called the “left-shift” can be repeatedly applied to turn

for more details.

Lemma 6

(
[

]
)
.

Let
P
be a poset on
V
, and let
\mathcal{R}
be a set of total orderings of

. Suppose that for every
x,y\in V
such that
x\nleq_{P}y
there exists
L\in\mathcal{R}

1.2
Main results

There are two well known upper bounds for the dimension of a poset
P
. The first is the width of
P
, which is the size of a largest anti-chain. The second upper bound is given by
Fürhedi and Kahn
[
12
]
in terms of the maximum number of elements comparable to a given element in the set (Theorem
5
). As mentioned earlier, this bound is almost optimal in view of Theorem
1
, since it matches, up to a logarithmic factor, the lower bound on the dimension of
P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
, when
p\geq\log^{1+\epsilon}n/n
(and
p
is not too close to 1). Bollobás and Brightwell proved a similar phenomenon in
P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}
for
p=\Omega(\log n/n)
: the dimension is close to the minimum of the width, and the Fürhedi-Kahn bound (Theorem
2
). This is no longer the case for sparser random graph orders. Consider
P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p=c/n)}
for an example. The minimum of the above two bounds would be around
\log n\log^{2}\log n
. However, although the maximum number of comparable elements to a given element can be as large as
\log n
, most elements are only comparable to
O(e^{c})
other elements, and the number of “problematic” elements who are comparable to more than
ce^{Kc}
other elements are bounded by
e^{-Kc}n
(see Corollary
35
). It is then reasonable to have a careful study of the sub-posets induced by these “problematic” elements, and study how they impact the dimension of the original poset. We prove the following theorems which upper bounds the dimension of a poset by the sum of certain sub-posets. Although these bounds are specifically tailored to help to bound the dimension of sparse random graph orders, we believe that they are of independent interest, as we are not aware of bounds of this nature in the literature.

Our first result is specifically for bipartite poset
P(A,B)
where the elements in
B
are greater than the elements in
A
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
P=P(A,B)
be a bipartite poset where
U[A]\setminus A\subseteq B
. Let
S_{A}\subseteq A
and
S_{B}\subseteq B
. Then,

\dim P\leq\dim P[U[S_{A}]]+\dim P[A\cup B\setminus S_{A}];

\dim P\leq\dim P[D[S_{B}]]+\dim P[A\cup B\setminus S_{B}].

For a general poset we have the following upper bound for its dimension.

Theorem 8
.

Let
P
be a poset on
V
and suppose that
S\subseteq V
. Then,

\displaystyle\dim P\leq 2(\dim P[D[S]\cup U[S]]+\dim P[V\setminus S]);

\displaystyle\dim P[D[S]\cup U[S]]\leq\dim P[D[S]]+\dim P[U[S]]+\dim P[D\cup U],

where
U=U[S]\setminus S
and
D=D[S]\setminus S
.

Note that
\dim P(A,B)\geq\max\{\dim P[U[S_{A}]],\dim P[A\cup B\setminus S_{A}]\}
in the bipartite case, and
\dim P\geq\max\{\dim P[D[S]\cup U[S]],\dim P[V\setminus S]\}
in the general case, the upper bounds in the above two theorems are tight up to a constant factor.

Now we turn to random graph orders. We start from addressing a question of Erdős, Kierstead and Trotter regarding the dimension of
P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
when
p
is very close to 1.

Theorem 9
.

Let
q:=1-p
.

1.

If
q=O(1/n)
and
q=\omega(1/n^{2})
then a.a.s.
\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}=\Theta(n^{2}q)
, and
n-\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}=\Omega(n)
.

2.

If
q=\omega(1/n)
and
q\leq n^{-1+1/7}
then a.a.s.
\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}=(1-o(1))n
.

Remark.
The vanishing error term
o(1)
in part (b) (cf. the
O(1/\log n)
error in Theorem
1
) can in fact be made explicit from our proof, although we did not pursue such a quantification. Our main message here is that there is a phase transition from
q=\omega(1/n)
to
q=O(1/n)
, at which point the dimension of the bipartite partial order ceases to be asymptotic to
n
. We excluded the uninteresting case
q=O(1/n^{2})
from part (a) since in that regime
\bar{B}
contains
O_{p}(1)
isolated edges and immediately
\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}=O_{p}(1)
.

Next we turn to the sparse regime. Using Theorem
7

we prove the following bounds on the dimension of
P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
where
p=c_{n}/n
. We assume that
c_{n}\geq 1
and
c_{n}\leq\log^{2}n
, since the dimension of
P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
for
p<1/n
is well understood by Theorem
3
, and the case
p=\Omega(\log^{2}n/n)
has already been studied by Erdös, Kierstad and Trotter
[
10
]
.

Given
c>0
, let
x=\alpha_{c}
be the unique positive solution to

\frac{e^{2}}{x^{2}}e^{-cx}=1.

(2)

Theorem 10
.

Let
p=c_{n}/n
where
c_{n}\geq 2
and
c_{n}\leq\log^{2}n
. Then,

1.

a.a.s.
\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}\geq(1-o(1))\frac{1}{2\alpha_{c_{n}}}
;

2.

there exist absolute constants
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, both independent of
n
and
c_{n}
such that a.a.s.
\gamma_{1}c_{n}/\log c_{n}\leq\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}\leq\gamma_{2}c_{n}\log^{2}c_{n}
.

Remark
.
\alpha_{c_{n}}\sim 2\log c_{n}/c_{n}
as
c_{n}\to\infty
(see Lemma
17
), and thus
\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p=c/n)}\geq(1-o_{c}(1))c/4\log c
as

.
On the other hand, for small values of
c_{n}
,
\alpha_{c_{n}}
can be numerically computed. The figure below on the left is a plot of

for
2<c<30
. From the plot the reader can see that the lower bounds obtained for small values of

(below 20) are smaller than the trivial lower bound two. We stress that the merit of Theorem
10
, together with the upper and lower bounds for
\dim P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}
in Theorems
11
and
12
, is about the asymptotic behaviour of
\dim P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
when
c
goes large. In fact, the implicit constant coefficients in the upper bounds (in both Theorems
10
and
12
) are inherited from the implicit constant in the Füredi-Kahn bound, and thus these bound do not yield useful information when
c
is small. Obtaining nontrivial lower or upper bounds on
P_{\mathcal{B}(n,n,p)}
for small values of
c
remains open. See Open Problem
14
below.

Next we consider the dimension of
P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}
. Let
Li_{2}(z):=-\int_{0}^{z}y^{-1}\log(1-y)dy
denote the dilogarithm function, also known as the Spencer’s function.

Theorem 11
.

Let
c>0
be fixed and let
p=c/n
.
Suppose that
\xi,\beta
are fixed positive real numbers such that
\xi+\alpha_{c}<1/2
and
\sup_{t\leq\alpha_{c}}f(c,\xi,t,\beta)<0
, where

\displaystyle f(c,\xi,\beta,t)=

\displaystyle\beta\log(e\xi/\beta)-c\beta(1/2-\xi-t)+\frac{1}{c}\left(Li_{2}(1)-Li_{2}(e^{-ct})\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{c}\left(Li_{2}(e^{-c\beta-ct})-Li_{2}(e^{-c\beta})+Li_{2}(e^{-c(1/2-\xi-t)-ct})-Li_{2}(e^{-c(1/2-\xi-t)}))\right).

(3)

Then,

1.3
Connections to causal set theory

Causal set theory is a theoretical framework that models spacetime as a fundamentally discrete, combinatorial structure. In contrast to general relativity, which treats spacetime as a smooth manifold, this approach—motivated by ideas from quantum gravity and quantum mechanics—posits that spacetime consists of indivisible “atoms” of events, organized into a locally finite poset; see
[
18
]
. The partial order in this poset encodes the causal relationships between events.
A central challenge in causal set theory is to generate such posets in a way that is both physically meaningful and mathematically rigorous. One of the most influential approaches to this problem is the classical sequential growth (CSG) model. Among the models studied, the random graph model

P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}
serves as a primary example of a CSG model within this framework; see
[
8
]
for work connecting the studies of
P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}
with developments in causal set theory.

A key concept in the study of causal sets is the causal representation of a poset in Minkowski space. Roughly speaking, the goal is to embed a poset

P
into the
d
-dimensional Minkowski space such that the order relation corresponds to the lightcone containment—that is,
x<y
in
P
if and only if the event
x

lies in the causal past of
y
in the embedding. The Minkowski dimension of
P
is the smallest dimension
d
for which such a representation exists. We refer interested readers to
[
16
]
for more details.
While both the (order-theoretic) dimension and the Minkowski dimension measure the complexity of a poset, they do so in fundamentally different ways. Studying the Minkowski dimension of the random poset
P_{\mathcal{G}(n,p)}
remains an intriguing and open problem, linking combinatorics, geometry, and the foundations of spacetime.
