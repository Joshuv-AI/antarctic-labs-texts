Abstract.

Identifying low-dimensional latent structures within high-dimensional data has long been a central topic in the machine learning community, driven by the need for data compression, storage, transmission, and deeper data understanding. Traditional methods, such as principal component analysis (PCA) and autoencoders (AE), operate in an unsupervised manner, ignoring label information even when it is available. In this work, we introduce a unified method capable of learning latent spaces in both unsupervised and supervised settings. We formulate the problem as a nonlinear multiple-response regression within an index model context. By applying the generalized Stein’s lemma, the latent space can be estimated without knowing the nonlinear link functions.
Our method can be viewed as a nonlinear generalization of PCA. Moreover, unlike AE and other neural network methods that operate as “black boxes”, our approach not only offers better interpretability but also reduces computational complexity while providing strong theoretical guarantees. Comprehensive numerical experiments and real data analyses demonstrate the superior performance of our method.

Key words and phrases.

PCA, autoencoder, index model, latent space, Stein’s Lemma, SVD.

Introduction

Figure 1
:
The data we consider could be unlabeled features only, or feature-label pairs, for example, CT and the corresponding diagnoses, or images of suspects and their positions on the surveillance screen, etc. The feature can be linearly embedded in a low-dimensional latent space, and the feature itself and possible labels can be generated from the embeddings through link functions. The goal of the work is to learn the latent space without knowing link functions.

Identifying low-dimensional latent spaces in high-dimensional data has been a longstanding focus in the statistics and machine learning community. This interest is driven not only by the goal of better understanding the data but also by practical applications such as data compression, storage, and transmission.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA,
Pearson, 1901
) is arguably the simplest and most popular dimension reduction method. PCA and its extensions, such as sparse PCA
(
Hui Zou and Tibshirani, 2006
)
and robust PCA
(
Candes et al., 2009
)
, have found widespread applications in various fields, including computer vision, biomedical analysis, and more.
Autoencoders (AE,
Vincent et al. 2008
) can be viewed as a nonlinear generalization of PCA by replacing simple linear projections with complex nonlinear projections defined by neural networks. With the increasing prevalence of deep learning in recent years, AE and its extensions, such as contractive AE
(
Rifai et al., 2011
)
and sparse AE
(
Makhzani and Frey, 2013
)
, have proven effective in dimension reduction and representation learning.

Despite their significant success, PCA and AE still encounter considerable challenges in learning the latent spaces for downstream tasks.
Firstly, both PCA and AE learn the latent space in a purely unsupervised manner. Although the low-dimensional manifold that the high-dimensional features align with reflects their inherent properties, relying solely on this information can be suboptimal when labels are available. Leveraging label information can enhance the quality of the learned latent space, making it more suitable for corresponding downstream tasks. Additionally, PCA is limited to learning the latent space in a linear setting. While autoencoders extend PCA to a nonlinear context, their “black-box” nature not only lacks necessary interpretability but also imposes a significant computational burden.

Rethinking the PCA, if we treat the input as the pseudo output, the data reconstruction problem can be reframed as a multiple response regression with reduced rank coefficients.
Multiple response regression in a reduced rank setting has been extensively studied in the literature, although the majority of the work has focused on linear models. Related works include
Yuan et al. (2007)
;
Mukherjee and Zhu (2011)
;
Chen et al. (2012)
;
Lu et al. (2012)
. With a reduced rank setup, dimension reduction can be achieved in multiple response regression, and the latent coefficient space can be learned. Beyond reduced rank regression, multiple response regression has also been considered under other settings, such as sparse matrix coefficients estimation
(
Lee and Liu, 2012
;
Simon et al., 2013
;
Wang et al., 2013
;
Changliang Zou and Zhang, 2022
)
. However, as these approaches are limited to linear models, complex nonlinear relationships between predictors and responses can hardly be captured and their applicability in real-world data analyses can be significantly restricted.

In the realm of nonlinear regression, the index model is a prominent statistical framework that extends linear regression to a nonlinear context. Specifically, consider the multi-index model (MIM), where the outcomes are modeled as
\mathbb{E}(y|\boldsymbol{x})=f(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})
. In this formulation,
f
represents an unknown nonlinear link function, and the matrix
\boldsymbol{B}
has a rank significantly smaller than the input dimension. Unlike other statistical methods that introduce nonlinearity, such as generalized linear models or spline regression, MIM inherently produces a latent embedding of the features through the introduction of
\boldsymbol{B}
. This characteristic is particularly aligned with our objective of estimating the latent space.

Extensive research has been conducted on MIM, particularly on the estimation of the low-rank matrix
\boldsymbol{B}
. The primary challenge lies in the fact that, without prior knowledge of the link functions, traditional methods such as least squares or maximum likelihood estimators are not applicable for estimating
\boldsymbol{B}
. Nonetheless, the statistics community has introduced a plethora of innovative solutions over the years. For instance, in low-dimensional settings with fixed rank of
\boldsymbol{B}
,
Friedman and Stuetzle (1981)
introduced the projection pursuit regression, under which the link functions and low-rank matrix can be estimated through an alternating optimization process. A series of breakthroughs related to index models are proposed by Ker-Chau Li, including regression under link violation
(
Li and Duan, 1989
)
, sliced inverse regression
(
Li, 1991
)
, and
principal Hessian directions
(
Li, 1992
)
. In the high-dimensional setting where the rank of
\boldsymbol{B}
is allowed to grow with the sample size, the estimation of
\boldsymbol{B}
is considered under the sparse subspace assumption
(
Chen et al., 2010
;
Tan et al., 2018
)
. More recently, a line of research focused on using Stein’s method in index models when the distribution of input is known. For example,
Yang et al. (2017b)
proposed to learn
\boldsymbol{B}
in a non-Gaussian MIM through a second-order Stein’s method. Unlike previous approaches, the Gaussian or elliptically symmetric assumption on the feature is not required in
Yang et al. (2017b)
, thus the application of Stein’s method in index models can be significantly broadened. Despite the success of these approaches, we shall note that most of the work concentrates on the scenario with univariate responses. To the best of our knowledge, the application of Stein’s method to nonlinear settings with multiple responses has not been thoroughly explored.

In this work, we present a unified method for learning latent spaces in both unsupervised and supervised settings. We address the problem as a nonlinear multiple-response regression within the context of an index model. By leveraging the generalized Stein’s lemma, our method estimates the latent space without requiring knowledge of the nonlinear link functions.
Our approach can be seen as a nonlinear extension of PCA. Furthermore, unlike neural network methods such as AEs that operate as “black boxes”, our framework effectively manages nonlinear models without depending on gradient-based techniques. Consequently, our approach not only offers improved interpretability, but also reduces computational complexity, and provides strong theoretical guarantees.
Comprehensive numerical experiments and real data analyses demonstrate the superior performance of our method.

2
Multiple Response Model and PCA

2.1
Nonlinear Multiple Response Model

Given an input
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and an output
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, we consider a nonlinear multiple response model:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}=\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

(2.1)

where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
is a noise vector with zero-mean i.i.d. components
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{q}
,
\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
is an unknown matrix, and
\mathcal{F}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{q})^{\top}
is a collection of unknown nonlinear functions with
f_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We note that the nonlinear function
f_{j}
can vary across different outcomes
j
. Thus, the component-wise formulation of the model (
2.1
) is given by

y_{j}=f_{j}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})+\epsilon_{j},\quad j\in[q].

(2.2)

Without loss of generality, we assume that the matrix
\boldsymbol{B}
has full column rank, meaning
\text{rank}(\boldsymbol{B})=r
. We consider a reduced rank setting where the rank
r
is significantly smaller than
p
.

For arbitrary nonlinear functions
f_{j}
, the coefficient matrix
\boldsymbol{B}
is unidentifiable in model (
2.1
). For instance, if
(\mathcal{F},\boldsymbol{B})
is a solution to model (
2.1
), then
(\mathcal{F}\circ\boldsymbol{O}^{-\top},\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{O})
is also a solution for any invertible matrix
\boldsymbol{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
. Nevertheless, the column space of
\boldsymbol{B}
remains identifiable. Therefore, our goal is to learn the column space of the matrix
\boldsymbol{B}
without knowing the nonlinear functions
\mathcal{F}
.

We shall emphasize that the feature-response pair
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
in model (
2.1
) is general, meaning it can include both real labels
\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}
and pseudo labels
\boldsymbol{x}
, which are the actual input features. When the response vector
\boldsymbol{y}
corresponds solely to the input features, model (
2.1
) transitions to the classical unsupervised setup

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}=\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}.

(2.3)

In this setup, the low-rank matrix
\boldsymbol{B}
serves a role similar to that of the leading principal components in PCA. Specifically, when
\mathcal{F}
is linear, the empirical least squares estimator of
\boldsymbol{B}
in model (
2.3
) shares the same column space as the leading principal components obtained through PCA.

By allowing the response vector to be general, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{y}=(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\top})^{\top}
, the column space of
\boldsymbol{B}
can be learned by leveraging information from both the features and the labels. Given that many real-world applications have limited labeled data, learning
\boldsymbol{B}
in this semi-supervised manner can be especially advantageous.
On the other hand, it is important to recognize that this model essentially assumes that the pseudo output and real output share the same latent space of coefficients. While this assumption might be stringent for certain scenarios, extensive analysis of real data suggests that latent spaces estimated using this semi-supervised approach often outperform those estimated solely from features or limited labels in downstream tasks. For more details, please refer to Section
5.2
.

2.2
The Stein’s Method for Latent Space Estimation

In this subsection, we present two novel approaches for estimating the column space of
\boldsymbol{B}
, utilizing both first-order and second-order Stein’s methods. We begin by introducing Stein’s score functions.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a random vector with density
P(\boldsymbol{x})
. If
P(\boldsymbol{x})
is differentiable, the first-order score function, denoted by
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x}):\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
, is defined as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\ln\{P(\boldsymbol{x})\}]=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}P(\boldsymbol{x})/P(\boldsymbol{x}).

(2.4)

Moreover, let
\boldsymbol{s}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})
denote the
i
-th element of
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
for
i=1,\ldots,p
. If
P(\boldsymbol{x})
is further twice differentiable, the second-order score function
\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{x}):\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is defined as

\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{x})=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{2}P(\boldsymbol{x})/P(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(2.5)

For
\forall k,l\in[p]
, let
\boldsymbol{T}_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})
denote the
k,l
-th entry of
\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

Under Model (
2.1
), we have the following first-order Stein’s lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

(First-order Stein’s Lemma)
Suppose model (
2.1
) holds. Assume that the expectations
\mathbb{E}\{y_{j}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\}
as well as
\mathbb{E}\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}f_{j}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\}
both exist and well-defined for
j\in[q]
. Further assume that
\lim_{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\rightarrow\infty}f_{j}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})P(\boldsymbol{x})\rightarrow 0
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{y_{j}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\}=\boldsymbol{B}\mathbb{E}\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}f_{j}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\}.

(2.6)

Collectively, equation (
2.6
) suggests

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{y}^{\top}\}=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{M}_{1},

(2.7)

where
\boldsymbol{M}_{1}=\mathbb{E}\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}f_{1}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}),\ldots,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}f_{q}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\}
.

Lemma
2.2
serves as the cornerstone of our first-order method of learning
\boldsymbol{B}
. The key point is that by the Stein’s lemma,
\boldsymbol{B}
can be completely split from derivatives of link functions so that we can learn
\boldsymbol{B}
without knowing their exact formulas.
Given
n
samples
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\}^{n}_{i=1}
, we propose the following first-order estimator of
\boldsymbol{B}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}=\text{SVD}_{l,r}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{\top}\right\}.

(2.8)

We summarize our approach in Algorithm
1
below. Note that the score function is required in Algorithm
1
. When
\boldsymbol{s}(\cdot)
is unknown, a plug-in approach can be implemented by replacing the score function with its estimates
\widehat{\boldsymbol{s}}(\cdot)
.

Beyond the first-order approach, a second-order Stein’s method takes the following form.

Lemma 2.3
.

(Second-order Stein’s Lemma)
Suppose model (
2.1
) holds. Assume that the expectations
\mathbb{E}\{y_{j}\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{x})\}
as well as
\mathbb{E}\{\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{z}}f_{j}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\}
both exist and well-defined for
j\in[q]
. Further assume that
\lim_{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\rightarrow\infty}f_{j}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})P(\boldsymbol{x})\rightarrow 0
and
\lim_{\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\rightarrow\infty}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}f_{j}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})P(\boldsymbol{x})\rightarrow 0
.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{y_{j}\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{x})\}=\boldsymbol{B}\mathbb{E}\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}f_{j}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\}\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}.

(2.9)

Collectively, equation (
2.9
) suggests

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{1}{q}\sum_{j=1}^{q}y_{j}\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\}=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{M}_{2}\boldsymbol{B}^{\top},

(2.10)

where
\boldsymbol{M}_{2}=\mathbb{E}\left\{(1/q)\sum_{j=1}^{q}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}f_{j}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\right\}
.

Based on Lemma
2.3
, we propose the following second-order estimator:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}=\text{Eigen}_{r}\left\{\frac{1}{nq}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{y}_{ij}\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\right\}.

(2.11)

We note that both first-order and second-order approaches are applicable for arbitrary labels
\boldsymbol{y}
. This implies that our methods can be utilized in both supervised and unsupervised settings. Additionally, in scenarios with limited labeled data, we can estimate
\boldsymbol{B}
in a semi-supervised manner by making slight adjustments.
For instance, consider a situation where there are additional
N-n

\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i=n+1}^{N}
, following the same marginal distribution. In such setups,
\boldsymbol{B}
can be estimated in a semi-supervised manner using the first-order method:

\displaystyle\text{SVD}_{l,r}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{i}^{\top}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\right\},

or the second-order method:

Beyond first and second-order methods, higher-order Stein’s methods can also be applied. Related work includes
Janzamin et al. (2014)
;
Balasubramanian et al. (2018)
, although they do not consider multiple response settings. However, it is important to note that higher-order approaches may face additional challenges, such as increased sample size requirements and greater computational complexities. Extensive simulation studies and real data analyses presented in Section
4
and Section
5
indicate that first and second-order methods would be sufficient in practice under general circumstances.

2.3
Relationships to Classical Latent Space Learning Methods

We recall the reconstruction task in model (
2.3
):

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}=\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}.

Under this model setup, when
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
follows a Gaussian distribution, the following theorem indicates that the column space of
\boldsymbol{B}
learned using the first-order method is equivalent to that of PCA, even if
\mathcal{F}
is nonlinear.

Theorem 2.4
.

Assume that model (
2.3
) holds with
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
following an i.i.d. Gaussian distribution with zero mean. For any arbitrary link function
\mathcal{F}
, the column space learned from (
2.8
) matches the column space of the first
r
leading principal components of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}=(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}
.

Theorem
2.4
suggests that PCA can be viewed as a special case of the first-order approach with Gaussian design. For other distributions with nonlinear
\mathcal{F}
, our approach offers a general framework for learning the latent space of
\boldsymbol{B}
.

Furthermore, model (
2.3
) can be seen as a special case of an AutoEncoder, where the encoder is a single linear map and the decoder is a general nonlinear function. With this design, the encoder can be learned directly without needing to know the decoder. By avoiding gradient-descent-based methods for learning
\boldsymbol{B}
, our approach is not only more computationally efficient but also comes with strong theoretical guarantees, as demonstrated in Section
3
.

3
Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we provide theoretical analyses of both the first-order and second-order approaches. Since only the column space of
\boldsymbol{B}
can be identified as discussed earlier, we use the following distance measure for two matrices
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
:

\displaystyle\text{dist}\left(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{2}\right)=\inf_{\boldsymbol{V}\in\mathcal{V}_{r}}\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{2}\boldsymbol{V}\|_{F}.

(3.1)

It should be noted that when
r=1
, this column space distance reduces to the vector distance
\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{2}\|_{2}
when
\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{1}\|_{2}=\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{2}\|_{2}
. We prove that the learned column space is consistent with the true column space with the distance (
3.1
) converging to 0. In the following discussion, when the context is clear, we denote
\boldsymbol{z}
as
\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}
as
\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
.

3.1
Theoretical Guarantees for the First-order Method

To establish the convergence properties, we impose the following assumptions for the first-order method.

Assumption 3.1
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are i.i.d. observations. For any
i\in[n]
,
r\in[p]
, assume that the score
\boldsymbol{s}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
is a sub-Gaussian random variable, i.e.,
\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}\ell^{-1/2}[\mathbb{E}\{|\boldsymbol{s}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})|^{\ell}\}]^{1/\ell}\leq C
, where
C
is a positive constant.

Assumption 3.2
.

Assume that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}[\mathbb{E}\{f_{j}(\boldsymbol{z})^{2\ell}\}]^{1/2\ell}\leq C
for all
j\in[q]
.

Assumption 3.3
.

For
j\in[q]
and
\ell\in[r]
, assume that
\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}_{\ell}}f_{j}(\boldsymbol{z})]=\Omega(1/\sqrt{r})
.

Assumption 3.4
.

Assume that there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that
\sigma_{r}(\boldsymbol{M}_{1})\geq C\sqrt{q/r}
.

Assumption
3.1
is a relatively mild condition and can be satisfied by a wide range of distributions, especially when
\boldsymbol{x}
consists of i.i.d. entries, which is widely adopted in the literature
(
Yang et al., 2017a
;
Balasubramanian et al., 2018
)
. It can be shown that even if
\boldsymbol{x}
are independent
t
, exponential such heavier-tailed distribution,
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
can still be element-wise sub-Gaussian. Moreover, it is easy to show that, if
\boldsymbol{x}
is multivariate normal,
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
would also be multivariate normal, therefore, each
\boldsymbol{s}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
,
k\in[p]
, would be sub-Gaussian. It is important to note that we focus on the case of sub-Gaussian scores. Theoretical results for other types of scores, such as heavy-tailed scores, can be derived using a truncation approach. We omit the details due to space constraints.

Assumption
3.2
requires that
f_{j}(\boldsymbol{z})
are essentially bounded, which would be satisfied when, for instance, the link functions are bounded,
\boldsymbol{x}
is bounded or link functions are Lipschitz continuous and
\boldsymbol{x}
is sub-Gaussian.

Assumption
3.3
requires that link functions are sensitive enough at the true parameter
\boldsymbol{B}
under the distribution
\boldsymbol{x}
. Assumption
3.4
further requires that the non-zero singular values of
\boldsymbol{M}_{1}
are of the same order.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose model (
2.1
) holds. Under Assumptions
3.1
-
3.4
, the following holds with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{V}\in\mathcal{V}_{r}}\|\boldsymbol{B}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}\boldsymbol{V}\|_{F}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{rp}{n}}\sqrt{\ln\frac{pq}{\delta}}\right).

Remark 3.6
.

We apply the proposed methods on two real-world datasets. On the MNIST dataset, we assess the finite sample performance of our methods in learning the latent space in model (

In this part, we consider the reconstruction task

Since there is no universal metric for evaluating the quality of the estimated latent space when the truth is unknown, we provide the following three metrics. We first consider normalized root squared error (NRSE) and structural similarity index measure (SSIM), which measure the performances of latent embeddings in the reconstruction task. Since these measures incorporate the influence of nonlinear decoders, to eliminate such effects, we also introduce the classification accuracy of the embedding on a multi-class classification task to measure the qualities of the estimated spaces. We compare our methods with baseline methods PCA and AE. For details of definitions of the measures and applications of the methods, please refer to Section
I.6
and Section

Figure

As seen in the figure, our methods consistently outperform PCA on NRSE and SSIM for embedding dimensions from 10 to 18, and on classification accuracy from 12 to 18. This is because our methods can capture nonlinear relationships among pixel values, which allows our methods to outperform the best linear approximation.

The AE performs slightly worse than our methods on NRSE and SSIM. Even though it is specifically designed for the reconstruction task, it has more parameters to estimate. Therefore, our methods perform better in cases with smaller sample sizes. For the classification task, the AE ranks last, which is also due to limited samples. Moreover, the latent space learned by the AE is specifically designed for the reconstruction task, while our estimators are task-free.

Comparing our first-order and second-order methods, they perform very close, although the second-order method is slightly inferior. One possible reason is that the multivariate normal assumption used for the second-order scores makes the second-order model mis-specified. Additionally, the relatively small sample size could also affect the performance of the second-order method.

6
Discussion and Future Work

In this paper, we introduced a general framework for learning the latent space within a nonlinear multiple response model setting. Our approach encompasses PCA as a special case when applied to Gaussian design. By eschewing gradient-descent-based methods for latent space learning, our approach is not only computationally more efficient but also offers strong theoretical guarantees. As a result, downstream tasks such as data compression and data transmission can greatly benefit from this framework.

It is crucial to recognize that Stein’s lemmas necessitate knowledge of the score function. When the distribution family is known, we can estimate the unknown parameters, such as the mean and covariances, and employ a plug-in approach. However, in more general settings, accurately estimating the score function for both first-order and second-order methods remains a significant area of research. Additionally, this paper focuses on continuous inputs and outputs. To handle discrete labels, one possible solution is to use approximation methods to convert discrete labels into continuous ones. For instance, in the context of one-hot labels in a multi-class classification problem, the Gumbel-softmax trick can be employed for this conversion. Exploring how Stein’s method can be adapted for general discrete scenarios will also be a major focus of our future investigations.

I.3
Score Models

There are two main streams of methods of estimating the first-order score function: one, we call direct methods is to estimate
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
directly by minimizing certain measures of difference between
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
and its estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{x})
; the other, we call noisy methods, is to estimate some noisy version of
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
and hopefully, as noise levels get smaller, we can obtain a good estimator of
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
The direct methods can be divided into two categories: parametric methods and non-parametric kernel methods. We first introduce parametric methods. Let
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
denote a parametric model of
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
parametrized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, the score macthing method
(
Hyvärinen and Dayan, 2005
)
estimates
\boldsymbol{\theta}
by minimizing the loss function

\displaystyle\ell_{\text{sm}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\|^{2}_{2}\right\},

(S1a)

by applying integral by part,
Hyvärinen and Dayan (2005)
show that minimizing (
S1a
) is equivalent to minimize

\displaystyle J_{\text{sm}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}\left[\text{tr}\left\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\|^{2}_{2}\right].

(S1b)

Suppose we have observations
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{x})
, the score matching estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{sm}}
can be obtained by minimizing the empirical version of (
S1b
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{sm}}=\text{argmin}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\text{tr}\left\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\|^{2}_{2}\right].

Instead of measuring the squared
L_{2}
–norm between
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
and its estimator, sliced-score matching
(
Song et al., 2020
)
measures the difference by their projections on random directions from certain distributions. Specifically, they consider the loss function

\displaystyle\ell_{\text{ssm}}\{\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{v})\}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\{\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\}^{2}],

(S2a)

by applying integral by part,
Song and Ermon (2019)
show that minimizing (
S2a
) is equivalent to minimize

\displaystyle J_{\text{ssm}}\{\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{v})\}=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left[\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\}\boldsymbol{v}+\frac{1}{2}\{\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\}^{2}\right]

(S2b)

Suppose for each
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
, we sample
\boldsymbol{v}_{i1}\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}_{im}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{v})
, the score matching estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{ssm}}
can be obtained by minimizing the empirical version of (
S2b
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{ssm}}=\text{argmin}\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum^{m}_{j=1}\left[\boldsymbol{v}_{ij}^{\top}\{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i};\boldsymbol{\theta})\}\boldsymbol{v}_{ij}+\frac{1}{2}\{\boldsymbol{v}_{ij}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i};\boldsymbol{\theta})\}^{2}\right].

In general,
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
is chosen be some deep neural network
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}):\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Except for parametric models, there is another line of work considering the kernel-based score estimator, and many of these methods can be unified by the following framework
(
Zhou et al., 2020
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
denote the support of
\boldsymbol{x}
,
\mathcal{K}
denote a matrix-valued kernel
\mathcal{K}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
satisfying for any
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x})
and for any
m\in N_{+}
,
\{\boldsymbol{x}\}^{m}_{i=1}\subset\mathcal{X}
and
\{\boldsymbol{c}_{i}\}^{m}_{i=1}
,
\sum^{m}_{i,j=1}\boldsymbol{c}^{\top}_{i}\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\boldsymbol{c}_{j}\geq 0
. We denote a vector-valued reproducing kernel
Hilbert space (RKHS) associated to
\mathcal{K}
by
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{K}}
. Then, the kernel estimator of
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
can be obtained by solving the regularized vector-valued regression problem

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{s}_{r}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{K}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-\boldsymbol{s}_{r}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\|^{2}_{2}+R_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}_{r}),

(S3)

where
R_{\lambda}(\cdot)
represents a regularizer belonging to the spectral class of regularization
(
Bauer et al., 2007
)
with tuning parameter
\lambda
corresponding to the estimator of
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
defined as
g_{\lambda}(\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{K}})\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{K}}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
, where
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{K}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\cdot)
and
g_{\lambda}(\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{K}})
approximate the inverse of operator
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{K}}
.
Note that
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{K}}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{K}}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{K}}
is positive semidefinite, suppose it has the eigen decomposition
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{K}}=\sum_{i=1}^{np}\sigma_{i}\langle\cdot,U_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{K}}}U_{i}
, then
g_{\lambda}(\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{K}})
can be represented by
g_{\lambda}(\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{K}})=\sum_{i=1}^{np}g^{\sigma}_{\lambda}(\sigma_{i})\langle\cdot,U_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{K}}}U_{i}
, where
g^{\sigma}_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a scalar function. For example, the Tikhonov regularization corresponds to
g^{\sigma}_{\lambda}(\sigma)=1/(\lambda+\sigma)
. In reality,
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
is unknown, so the solution is infeasible, but by integral by part,
Zhou et al. (2020)
prove that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{K}}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})=-\mathbb{E}\{\text{div}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{x},\cdot)^{\top}\}
, therefore, empirically, we can estimate
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
by

\widehat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{r}(\boldsymbol{x})=-g_{\lambda}(\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{K}})\widehat{\xi}
,
where
\widehat{\xi}=(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{div}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\cdot)^{\top}
.
Many kernel score estimators like kernel exponential family estimator
(
Strathmann et al., 2015
)
, Stein gradient estimator
(
Li and Turner, 2017
)
, spectral Stein gradient estimator
(
Shi et al., 2018
)
, etc., can be included in the framework with different choices of the function
g^{\sigma}_{\lambda}
and hypothesis space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{K}}
. For more details please refer to
Zhou et al. (2020)
and references therein.

All those direct methods mentioned above use integral by parts to avoid the unknown function
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

In contrast, noisy methods circumvent the unknown
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
by estimating a noisy version of
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
. Specifically, denoising score matching
(
Vincent, 2011
)
considers the estimator of
\boldsymbol{\theta}

minimizing the following loss function

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}\left\|\boldsymbol{s}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{\partial\ln{P_{\sigma}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|\boldsymbol{x})}}{\partial\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}\right\|^{2}_{2},

(S4a)

where
P_{\sigma}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|\boldsymbol{x})
is some smooth density estimator with bandwidth
\sigma
,
Vincent (2011)
prove that under mild conditions minimizing (
S4a
) is equivalent to minimizing

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}\left\|\boldsymbol{s}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{\partial\ln{P_{\sigma}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}}{\partial\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}\right\|^{2}_{2},

where
P_{\sigma}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})=(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln{P_{\sigma}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i})}
. Hopefully, as
\sigma
gets close to
0
, the noisy model
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x};\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{dsm}})
would be a good approximation of
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})
. Empirically, suppose for each
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
, we generate
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i,1},\ldots\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i,m}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P_{\sigma}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
, then we can get the estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{dsm}}
by minimizing the following empirical loss function:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\|\boldsymbol{s}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i,j};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\frac{\partial\ln{P_{\sigma}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i,j}|\boldsymbol{x}_{i})}}{\partial\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i,j}}\right\|^{2}_{2}.

\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
is also generally chosen to be a deep neural network
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}):\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Some modifications and extensions were proposed. For example,
Song and Ermon (2019)
proposed to train the score model using a geometric series of levels of
\sigma
.

Compared with the rapid development of first-order score models due to the prevalence of diffusion models, there is much limited work on second-order score models. For kernel methods, there’s no clear function space corresponding to second order gradients; for neural networks, directly taking gradient of first-order score models with respect to predictors not only leads to error accumulation but also in general, cannot generate symmetric output. Moreover, building a neural network for the second-order score also faces up constrains like symmetry, which makes the problem difficult. To the best of our knowledge,
Meng et al. (2021)
is the only work that propose a deep neural network model for the second-order score function. However, their method needs to estimate a first-order score model at the same time, which leads to error accumulation.

I.4
Reduced Rank Estimator, Neural Network Estimator and Ours

The reduced rank regression addresses the following multiple-response linear regression model:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}+\varepsilon,

(S5)

where
\boldsymbol{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
, and
\text{rank}(\boldsymbol{C})\leq r
for some
r\leq\min(p,q)
.
Let
\boldsymbol{X}
denote the data matrix
(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n})^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
denote the response matrix
(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{n})^{\top}
, reduced rank regression estimates the coefficient matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
by solving the constrained optimization problem:
\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}=\text{argmin}_{\text{rank}(\boldsymbol{C})\leq r}\|\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{C}\|^{2}_{F}
.

Under low-dimensional setting, i.e.,
\text{rank}(\boldsymbol{C})
is fixed, it is well known that if
r
is given,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}
has the closed form
(
Mukherjee and Zhu, 2011
)
:
\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{ols}\boldsymbol{V}_{r}\boldsymbol{V}_{r}^{\top}
.
\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{ols}
is the ordinary least squares estimator, i.e.,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{ols}=\text{argmin}\|\boldsymbol{Y}=\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{C}\|^{2}_{F}
.
\boldsymbol{V}_{r}=\text{SVD}_{l,r}\{(\boldsymbol{X}\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{ols})^{\top}\}
, i.e., the matrix consisting of the first
r
leading right singular vectors of
\boldsymbol{X}\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{ols}
.

Notably, in our model (
2.2
), if we additionally require that
r<q
, and
f_{j}(\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{a}^{\top}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}
for certain
\boldsymbol{a}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{r},j\in[q]
, it reduces to model (
S5
) with
\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{A}
, where
\boldsymbol{A}=(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{q})
. Therefore, model (
S5
) is a special case of our model (
2.2
), and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}
can be used for estimating
\boldsymbol{B}
. The reduced rank regression estimator of
\boldsymbol{B}
is defined as
\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}_{R}=\text{SVD}_{l,r}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}\right)
.

Remark I.1
.

Under the assumption that
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{N}})
, and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{N}}
is non-degenerate, by Lemma
IV.1
,
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\mathcal{N}}\boldsymbol{x}
, if we let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{x})=(1/n)(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X})^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}
, then
\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}=\text{SVD}_{l,r}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{ols}\right)
. The difference between
\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}_{R}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}
lies in the projection matrix
\boldsymbol{V}_{r}\boldsymbol{V}_{r}^{\top}
, which can be seen as the benefit of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}_{R}
taking advantage of the extra linear information of link functions in model (
S5
). Results of simulations in Section
4
demonstrate that performances of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}_{R}
are almost the same when
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{N}})
, the difference above is almost negligible.

The multi-index model has a close relationship with NNs, and many works try to show that NNs can be used to estimate MIM and construct the representation space in the low-dimensional setting
(
Bauer and Kohler, 2019
;
Damian et al., 2022
;
Mousavi-Hosseini et al., 2022
)
. Similarly, for our multi-response extension, the matrix

\boldsymbol{B}
can also be estimated by NNs. Let
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}
be a neural network parametrized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, the neural network estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}_{N}
can be obtained by

\displaystyle(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{N},\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}_{N})=\text{argmin}_{(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{B})}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\|^{2}_{2}.

(S6)

We solve the optimization problem by mini-batch gradient descent. For details of the neural network structure and training procedures, please see Section
II.3
of the Supplement.

To apply our method, by densities of
\boldsymbol{x}
, first-order and second-order score functions can be derived in closed forms. Please refer to Section
II.4
of Supplement for details. Then, for
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{N}})
, parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood estimation. For
\boldsymbol{x}\sim t_{\nu}(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t})
and
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{H}_{\chi,\psi}(0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{H}})
, parameters are estimated by a multi-cycle, expectation, conditional estimation algorithm
(
Breymann and Lüthi, 2013
)
. Then we use the plug-in estimators
\widehat{\boldsymbol{s}}(\cdot)
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{T}}(\cdot)
to calculate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}}
defined by equation (
2.8
) and equation (
2.11
), respectively.

Suppose image data
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{c\times c}\sim\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{X})

1.

We consider the normalized root squared error (NRSE), i.e.,

2.

III.1.1
of the Supplement for details.

3.

Specifically, we consider the classification accuracy defined by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{I}\{\text{argmax}\circ\;\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}}\circ\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{X})=y\}],

III.1.2
of the Supplement.

II.2
Multivariate Hyperbolic Distribution

The random vector
\boldsymbol{x}
is said to have a multivariate generalized hyperbolic distribution if

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}\overset{\text{d}}{=}\boldsymbol{u}+w\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\sqrt{w}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z},

where
\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\boldsymbol{I}_{o})
,
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times o}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
w\geq 0
is a random variable, independent of
\boldsymbol{z}
and has a Generalized Inverse Gaussian distribution, written as
\textbf{GIG}(\lambda,\chi,\psi)
. The density is

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{(\sqrt{\psi/\chi})^{\lambda}(\psi+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\gamma})^{p/2-\lambda}}{(2\pi)^{p/2}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{1/2}K_{\lambda}(\sqrt{\chi\psi})}\times\frac{K_{\lambda-p/2}(\sqrt{\{\chi+Q(\boldsymbol{x})\}(\psi+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\gamma})})\exp\{(\boldsymbol{c}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\gamma}\}}{\{\sqrt{\{\chi+Q(\boldsymbol{x})\}(\psi+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\gamma})}\}^{p/2-\lambda}},

(S1)

where
Q(\boldsymbol{x})=(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})
,
K_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is the modified Bessel function of the third kind. In the experiment, we choose
\lambda=\frac{p+1}{2}
, and fix

\boldsymbol{\gamma}=0
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}=0
, which is named multivariate hyperbolic
distribution.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\mathcal{N}}(\boldsymbol{x}-\mu_{\mathcal{N}})(\boldsymbol{x}-\mu_{\mathcal{N}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\mathcal{N}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\mathcal{N}}.

\boldsymbol{x}\sim t_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t})

\nu\geq 2

P(\boldsymbol{x})=(\nu-2)^{\nu/2}\Gamma\{(\nu+p)/2\}/\pi^{p/2}/|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}|^{1/2}/\Gamma(\nu/2)/\{\nu-2+Q_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\}^{(\nu+p)/2}
.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})=

We first prove a lemma that establishes a bound on the moment of a Gaussian random variable.

Lemma IV.2
.

If random variable

[\mathbb{E}|z|^{\ell}]^{\frac{1}{\ell}}\leq\sigma\sqrt{\ell}

Proof.

The

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|z|^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|z|^{\ell}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)dz

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{\ell}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)dz

\displaystyle\overset{(i)}{=}2\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}(\sqrt{2t}\sigma)^{\ell}e^{-t}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma}{2\sqrt{t}}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sqrt{2}\sigma)^{\ell}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\frac{\ell-1}{2}}e^{-t}dt

\displaystyle\overset{(ii)}{=}\frac{(\sqrt{2}\sigma)^{\ell}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cdot\Gamma\left(\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right),

where
(i)
is by the transformation
z=\sqrt{2t}\sigma
,
(ii)
is by
\Gamma(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{x-1}e^{-t}dt
. If
\ell
is even, then we have,

\displaystyle\frac{(\sqrt{2}\sigma)^{\ell}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cdot\Gamma\left(\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sqrt{2}\sigma)^{\ell}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cdot\frac{\ell-1}{2}\cdot\frac{\ell-3}{2},\ldots,\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\leq\frac{(\sqrt{2}\sigma)^{\ell}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\ell!}}{2^{\ell/2}}\cdot\sqrt{\pi}=\sigma^{\ell}\sqrt{\ell!}.

On the other hand, if

is odd, then we get

\displaystyle\frac{(\sqrt{2}\sigma)^{\ell}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cdot\Gamma\left(\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sqrt{2}\sigma)^{\ell}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cdot\frac{\ell-1}{2}\cdot\frac{\ell-3}{2},\ldots,\cdot 1\cdot\Gamma(1)\leq\frac{(\sqrt{2}\sigma)^{\ell}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\ell!}}{2^{(\ell-1)/2}}\leq\sigma^{\ell}\sqrt{\ell!}.

In summary, we arrive at

\displaystyle[\mathbb{E}|z|^{\ell}]^{\frac{1}{\ell}}\leq(\sigma^{\ell}\sqrt{\ell!})^{\frac{1}{\ell}}\leq\sigma\sqrt{\ell}.

Proof of Theorem

.

According to Taylor’s theorem,
f_{j}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})
can be expanded at the point
\boldsymbol{a}=\boldsymbol{0}_{r}
as follows with a function
h_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle f_{j}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla f_{j}(\boldsymbol{0}_{r}),\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\rangle+h_{j}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\|\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\nabla f_{j}(\boldsymbol{0}_{r})^{\top}+h_{j}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})\|\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2},

with
\lim_{\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{0}_{r}}h_{j}(\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x})=0
for all
j\in[q]
. Hence, we arrive at

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{0}_{r})^{\top}+\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\|\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2},

where
\nabla\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{0}_{r}):=\left\{\nabla f_{1}(\boldsymbol{0}_{r}),\ldots,\nabla f_{q}(\boldsymbol{0}_{r})\right\}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
and
\boldsymbol{H}\{\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}):=\left(h_{1}(\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x}),\ldots,h_{q}(\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x})\right\}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{\top}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{0}_{r})^{\top}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{\top}\|\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|_{2}\right).

For Gaussian distribution with zero mean and covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, we have the score of
\boldsymbol{x}
is
\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{x}
. Denote
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}:=(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}
. Thus, if the covariance matrix is unknown, i.e., the
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
replaced by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
,
we then have

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{\top}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{\top}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\dagger}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\dagger})\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{0}_{r})^{\top}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\dagger}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\dagger})\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{\top}\|\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|_{2}\right)\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq{\|(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-\boldsymbol{I}_{p})\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{0}_{r})^{\top}\|_{F}}+{\frac{1}{n}\|(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\dagger}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\dagger})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\epsilon_{i}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{\top}\|\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|_{2}\right)\|_{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\|(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-\boldsymbol{I}_{p})\|_{F}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{0}_{r})^{\top}\|_{F}}_{T_{1}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\|(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\dagger}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\dagger})\|_{F}\cdot\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\epsilon_{i}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{\top}\|\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|_{2}\right)\|_{F}}_{T_{2}}

(S1)

For term
T_{1}
of (
S1
), we have

\displaystyle\|(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-\boldsymbol{I}_{p})\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\dagger}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\|_{F}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{r}}{\sigma_{r}}\cdot\sqrt{r}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{\sigma_{r}}\cdot\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|_{2}

(S2)

Then, by Lemma
IV.4
,

\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|_{2}\leq\max{(\delta,\delta^{2})}

with probability at least

, where

,

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\|_{2}\geq CL\sqrt{\frac{\ln(2/\alpha)}{pd}}\right)\leq\alpha,

it follows that

.

3.3

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{0}_{r})^{\top}\|_{F}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{pq/r}).

of (

) is
\sqrt{rpq/n}
. For the second term
T_{2}
of (
S1

, we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\dagger}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\dagger}\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\|_{2}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\dagger}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\dagger}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\dagger}\|_{2}.

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\dagger}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\dagger}\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(1/\sqrt{n}).

(S3)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\epsilon_{i}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{\top}\|\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|_{2}\right)\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{1}{n}\left(\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}^{\top}\|_{F}+\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{\top}\|\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|_{2}\|_{F}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}^{\top}\|_{F}\leq\max_{i}\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}_{p}(\sqrt{pq})

Lemma IV.8
.

Suppose model (
2.1
) holds, under Assumptions
3.9
to
3.11
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{nq}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum^{q}_{j=1}[\boldsymbol{y}_{i,j}\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\boldsymbol{T}(x)\}]\right\|_{F}=\mathcal{O}\left\{\frac{p}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\ln\left(\frac{p}{\delta}\right)}\right\}.

Proof.

We first consider
(1/q)\sum_{j=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{y}_{j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
For notational convenience, let
f_{j}
denote
f_{j}(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x})
.

\displaystyle\left\{\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{1}{q}\sum_{j=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{y}_{j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{j=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{y}_{j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\right|^{\ell}\right\}^{1/\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{q}(f_{j}+\epsilon_{j})T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})-\sum_{j=1}^{q}\mathbb{E}\left\{(f_{j}+\epsilon_{j})T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\}\right|^{\ell}\right\}^{1/\ell}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\left\{\left(\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{q}f_{j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})\right|^{\ell}\right)^{1/\ell}+\left(\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{q}\epsilon_{j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})\right|^{\ell}\right)^{1/\ell}\right\}+\frac{1}{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\sum^{q}_{j=1}f_{j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\left[\left\{\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\sum_{j=1}^{q}f_{j}\right|^{2\ell}\right)\mathbb{E}\left(|T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2\ell}\right)\right\}^{1/2\ell}+\left\{\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\sum_{j=1}^{q}\epsilon_{j}\right|^{2\ell}\right)\mathbb{E}\left(|T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2\ell}\right)\right\}^{1/2\ell}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\sum^{q}_{j=1}f_{j}\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}[\mathbb{E}\{|T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2}\}]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\left\{2\left\|\sum^{q}_{j=1}f_{j}\right\|_{\infty}C_{5}\ell+2\left\|\sum^{q}_{j=1}\epsilon_{j}\right\|_{\infty}C_{5}\ell+2\left\|\sum^{q}_{j=1}f_{j}\right\|_{2}C_{5}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4+2\frac{C_{7}}{C_{8}}}{q}\|\sum^{q}_{j=1}f_{j}\|_{\infty}C_{5}\ell

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(4+2\frac{C_{7}}{C_{8}})C_{5}C_{6}\ell.

Therefore, for
\forall k,l\in[p]
,
1/q\sum_{j=1}^{q}[\boldsymbol{y}_{j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})\}]
follows a centered sub-exponential distribution, and
\|1/q\sum_{j=1}^{q}[\boldsymbol{y}_{j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})\}]\|_{\psi_{1}}=\sup_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{1}{\ell}\mathbb{E}(|1/q\sum_{j=1}^{q}[\boldsymbol{y}_{j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})\}]|^{\ell})^{1/\ell}\leq(4+2C_{7}/C_{8})C_{5}C_{6}
. By Bernstein’s inequality (Corollary 2.8.3 in
Vershynin (2018b)
), for
\forall t>0,k,l\in[p]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{j=1}^{q}[\boldsymbol{y}_{i,j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})\}]\right)\right|\geq t\right\}\leq 2\cdot\exp\left[-cn\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{K^{2}},\frac{t}{K}\right)\right],

where
K\leq(4+2C_{7}/C_{8})C_{5}C_{6}
and
c>0
is an absolute constant. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{nq}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{q}[\boldsymbol{y}_{i,j}\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{x})\}]\right\|_{F}\geq pt\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq K\leq l\leq p}\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{j=1}^{q}[\boldsymbol{y}_{i,j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}T_{k,l}(\boldsymbol{x})\}]\right)\right|\geq t\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle p(1+p)\cdot\exp\left[-cn\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{K^{2}},\frac{t}{K}\right)\right]

For
\forall\delta>0
,
\exists n
large enough so that
[\ln\{p(1+p)/\delta\}]/c/n<1
. Let
t=K\sqrt{[\ln\{p(1+p)/\delta\}]/c/n}
, with probability at least
1-\delta

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{nq}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{q}[\boldsymbol{y}_{i,j}\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{x})\}]\right\|_{F}\leq pt\leq pK\sqrt{\frac{\ln\left\{\frac{p(p+1)}{\delta}\right\}}{cn}},

i.e., with probability at least
1-\delta

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{nq}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{q}[\boldsymbol{y}_{i,j}\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{x})\}]\right\|_{F}=\mathcal{O}\left(p\sqrt{\frac{\ln\left(\frac{p}{\delta}\right)}{n}}\right).
