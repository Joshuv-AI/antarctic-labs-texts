A fixed-point theorem for face maps, or deleting entries in random finite sets
Tom Hutchcroft, Nicolas Monod, Omer Tamuz

Abstract
. We establish a fixed-point theorem for the face maps that consist in deleting the i i th entry of an ordered set. Furthermore, we show that there exists random finite sets of integers that are almost invariant under such deletions.



1.
Introduction

Results

Consider a finite set
E
of integers. If
E
has at least
k
elements, then we can study the operation
\alpha_{1}
of removing its smallest element,
\alpha_{2}
of removing the second smallest, and so on up to
\alpha_{k}
. More formally, if
E=\{y_{1}<\cdots<y_{n}\}
then
\alpha_{i}(E)=E\smallsetminus\{y_{i}\}
.

Our first result is that there exist random sets which are almost invariant under all these operations.

Theorem A
.

Fix
k\geq 2
and
\epsilon>0
.

There exists a finitely supported probability distribution
\mu
on the collection of finite sets
E\subseteq\mathbf{N}
with at least
k
elements such that
\alpha_{i}(\mu)
is
\epsilon
-close to
\mu
in total variation distance for all
1\leq i\leq k
.

Working with integers is merely a matter of convenience since
\alpha_{i}
is defined in terms of the ordering only. Thus, the above result holds unchanged when
\mathbf{N}
is replaced by any partially ordered set containing chains of arbitrary length.

Our next result is a fixed-point principle for continuous affine transformations of compact convex sets (in any Hausdorff topological vector space). Recall that this is the context of the Markov–Kakutani Theorem, which states that any
commuting
family of such transformations has a common fixed point. This fails of course in general for non-commuting transformations.

Theorem B
.

Let
s_{i}
be a family of continuous affine transformations of a compact convex set
K\neq\varnothing
, where
i\in\mathbf{N}
.

If
s_{j}s_{i}=s_{i}s_{j+1}
holds for all
1\leq i\leq j
, then
K
contains a point fixed by all
s_{i}
.

It is imperative in
B
that the family
s_{i}
be infinite: if for some
n\geq 2
we had only
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}
, then the relations
s_{j}s_{i}=s_{i}s_{j+1}
would only make sense for
j\leq n-1
. Any
K
that is not reduced to a point admits such transformations without a common fixed point. Indeed, choose
x\neq y
in
K
and define
s_{i}
to be the constant map to
x
if
1\leq i\leq n-1
, whilst
s_{n}
is the constant map to
y
.

Discussion of the results

The relations
s_{j}s_{i}=s_{i}s_{j+1}
are very familiar in algebraic topology, where they appear as face relations, as formalised notably by Eilenberg–Zilber
[
6
]
. Face operations are usually defined separately in every dimension; for instance, with
n
fixed, the operations
\alpha_{i}
above satisfy the facial relations for
i\leq j\leq n-1
and this is a standard model for faces. In that context,
A
states that any infinite-dimensional simplical complex carries random simplices almost-invariant under passing to faces.

Instead, we propose to define a single monoid
S
given by generators
s_{i}
subject to the above relations. Then
B
states that this monoid is
amenable
. This definition of amenability in terms of fixed points is equivalent to the existence of a left-invariant mean, as studied extensively since the foundational articles by von Neumann
[
14
]
and Day
[
3
]
. We refer to
Section

5
below for terminological clarifications.

Observe that
S
is the increasing union of the sub-monoids
S_{n}<S
generated by the first
n
elements
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}
. We shall prove that
S_{n}
coincides with the monoid defined by the same presentation as
S
but truncated on these
n
generators. It then follows from the above remark on fixed-point-free actions that none of the
S_{n}
is amenable when
n\geq 2
.

That an amenable monoid
S
is the nested union of non-amenable sub-monoids
S_{n}
cannot happen for groups. Is is not especially remarkable in the world of monoids and semigroups; nonetheless, it illustrates that the monoid
S
is not too elementary and that its amenability “hides at infinity”.

The monoid
S
might deserve further interest. It acts naturally on many sequence spaces; in
Section

6
, we investigate some of the associated ergodic theory, showing notably that the
S
-action on shift spaces exhibits a De Finetti type rigidity of invariant measures.

Our approach to the above two theorems rests on the observation that they are two visages of the same phenomenon. In particular, an explicit construction of random finite sets would lead to a proof of the fixed-point statement. However, it turns out that our argument ends up instead using a fixed-point principle to establish the existence of the random sets.

In fact it seems non-trivial to control random finite sets directly; we could only verify that explicit constructions will satisfy
A
for
k=2
. In the Appendix, we exhibit a concrete model of random sets for which one could conjecture almost-invariance.

In closing, we cannot evade a mention of Thompson’s group, which admits the group presentation

F=\big\langle g_{i}:g_{j}g_{i}=g_{i}g_{j+1}\ \forall\,1\leq i<j\big\rangle.

Consider the monoiod
F_{+}
given by that same presentation, but as monoid presentation. Then
S
is, by definition, a monoid quotient of
F_{+}
. It is well-known that
F
is amenable if and only
F_{+}
is so; this follows immediately from a normal form decomposition for
F
. On the other hand, the (non-)amenability of
F
remains a notorious open problem.

A step of our proof of
B
can evoke echoes of the work of Moore for
F
, specifically
[
11
,
12
]
. It is not clear, however, that there exists a formal connection there.

Thus, one of the appeals of
S
is that it is a complicated quotient of
F_{+}
; by contrast, we recall that the group
F
admits no proper quotients at all, except via its abelianization
\mathbf{Z}^{2}
.

In that context, an indication of the richness of
S
and a further motivation for
B
is that
F_{+}
embeds into a semi-direct product of
S
by an abelian monoid (or group), which is amenable. See the end of
Section

5
.

2.
A law of very large numbers

A
mean
on a set
X
is a positive normalized linear functional on
\ell^{\infty}(X)
, or equivalently a finitely additive probability measure on
X
. For instance, probability distributions on
X
are means, and are in fact dense in the space of means for the weak-* topology of duality with
\ell^{\infty}(X)
by Goldstine’s theorem
[
7
]
. We can also consider means as Radon probability measures on the Stone–Čech compactification of
X
.

Remark 2.1
.

Occasionally, we will use implicitly the fact that means on a subset
Y\subseteq X
correspond canonically and uniquely to means
\nu
on
X
satisfying
\nu(Y)=1
.

A mean
\nu
is called
diffuse
if
\nu(E)=0
for every finite set
E
. Informally, we think of a diffuse mean on
\mathbf{N}
as representing random very large numbers.

We write
\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k}
for the set of strictly increasing sequences of
k
positive integers. Given
k\geq 1
and
1\leq j\leq k+1
, we consider the operation
\omega_{j}\colon\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k+1}\to\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k}
which deletes the
j
th largest element of the sequence, or equivalently the
j
th element when counting backwards from the end. For instance, given
y_{1}<\cdots<y_{k+1}
, we have

\omega_{1}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k+1})=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}).

Given a mean
\nu
on
\mathbf{N}
, we define a mean
\nu_{k}
on
\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k}
recursively. Start with
\nu_{1}=\nu
on
\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{1}=\mathbf{N}
. Then, for
k\geq 1
and
f\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k+1})
we set

\nu_{k+1}(f)=\nu\left(x\mapsto\nu_{k}\left(f^{x}\right)\right)

wherein
f^{x}\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k})
is defined by

f^{x}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})=f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k},y_{k}+x).

Remark 2.2
.

We can loosely think of
\nu_{k}
as recording successive sums of IID copies of
\nu
, or the trajectory of a
\nu
-random walk on
\mathbf{N}
. Specifically, if
A_{j}\subseteq\mathbf{N}
are any sets and
A\subseteq\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k+1}
denotes the collection of those
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k+1})
for which
y_{1}\in A_{1}
and
y_{j+1}-y_{j}\in A_{j+1}
, then we have
\nu_{k+1}(A)=\nu(A_{1})\nu(A_{2})\cdots\nu(A_{k+1})
.

However we caution the reader that this is in no way a formal definition of
\nu_{k}
because means on products are not determined by their values on product sets, and any form of Fubini fails. This is related to the fact that the Stone–Čech compactification of a product is much larger than the product of the Stone–Čech compactifications.

From now on we specialise to a mean
\nu
satisfying the
idempotence
condition

(2.i)

\forall\,f\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbf{N}):\nu(f)=\nu\left(x\mapsto\nu\left(x^{\prime}\mapsto f(x+x^{\prime})\right)\right).

This condition holds notably whenever
\nu
is an invariant mean, since invariance implies
\nu\left(x^{\prime}\mapsto f(x+x^{\prime})\right)=\nu(f)
. The classical source of invariant means on
\mathbf{N}
is to take any accumulation point, in the compact space of means, of the sequence of uniform distributions on
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Seeing means as functionals on
\ell^{\infty}(X)
, this coincides with the sequence of Cesàro averages.

We note that the only finitely supported convolution idempotent on
\mathbf{Z}
is the point mass at zero; since
\nu
is supported on
\mathbf{N}
, one readily checks that it must be diffuse.

Theorem 2.3
.

For all
k\geq 1
and
1\leq j\leq k+1
we have
\omega_{j}\nu_{k+1}=\nu_{k}
.

Remark 2.4
.

In
Proposition

A.1
we show that if
\nu
is a
countably additive
probability measure on
\mathbf{N}
and we define the random walk measures
\nu_{k}
as above then
\omega_{k+1}\nu_{k+1}
has total variation distance at least
1/4
from
\nu_{k}
. As such,
Theorem

2.3
establishes a property of certain “finitely additive random walks” that cannot hold
even approximately
for true random walks. This apparent paradox is not contradictory since the convolution
(\mu,\nu)\mapsto\mu*\nu
and the operation sending
\nu
to
\nu_{k}
defined above are not continuous when extended to the space of finitely additive probability measures.

Proof of
Theorem

2.3
.

We shall show by induction on
j\geq 1
that the claim holds for all
k\geq 1
with
k+1\geq j
. For the basis of the induction, both
j=1
and
j=2
require individual consideration.

For
j=1
, let
k\geq 1
and let
f\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k})
. We have

(\omega_{1}\nu_{k+1})(f)=\nu_{k+1}(f\circ\omega_{1})=\nu\left(x\mapsto\nu_{k}\left((f\circ\omega_{1})^{x}\right)\right).

Observe that
(f\circ\omega_{1})^{x}=f
holds by definition; therefore,

(\omega_{1}\nu_{k+1})(f)=\nu\left(x\mapsto\nu_{k}(f)\right)=\nu_{k}(f)

as desired.

Consider now
j=2
and again let
k\geq 1
,
f\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k})
. If
k\geq 2
, we expand twice as above:

(\omega_{2}\nu_{k+1})(f)=\nu\left(x\mapsto\nu_{k}\left((f\circ\omega_{2})^{x}\right)\right)=\\
=\nu\left(x\mapsto\nu\left(x^{\prime}\mapsto\nu_{k-1}\left(((f\circ\omega_{2})^{x})^{x^{\prime}}\right)\right)\right).

Now we claim
((f\circ\omega_{2})^{x})^{x^{\prime}}=f^{x+x^{\prime}}
. Indeed, given
y_{1}<\cdots<y_{k-1}
,

((f\circ\omega_{2})^{x})^{x^{\prime}}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k-1})=(f\circ\omega_{2})^{x}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k-1},y_{k-1}+x^{\prime})=\\
=(f\circ\omega_{2})(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k-1},y_{k-1}+x^{\prime},y_{k-1}+x^{\prime}+x)=f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k-1},y_{k-1}+x^{\prime}+x).

Therefore

(\omega_{2}\nu_{k+1})(f)=\nu\left(x\mapsto\nu\left(x^{\prime}\mapsto\nu_{k-1}\left(f^{x+x^{\prime}}\right)\right)\right)

which, by the idempotence condition (
2.i
), is

\nu\left(x\mapsto\nu_{k-1}\left(f^{x}\right)\right)=\nu_{k}(f).

For
j=2
we still need to check the special case
k=1
. Given
f\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{1})
, we note that
(f\circ\omega_{2})^{x}(y_{1})=f(y_{1}+x)
. Therefore, using again idempotence,

(\omega_{2}\nu_{2})(f)=\nu\left(x\mapsto\nu\left(y_{1}\mapsto f(y_{1}+x)\right)\right)=\nu(f)=\nu_{1}(f).

We now consider
j\geq 3
and we assume that the claim has been established for
j-1
and for all
k
with
j-1\leq k+1
. We need to prove it for
j
and therefore we only consider
k\geq j-1
. Given
f\in\ell^{\infty}(\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k})
, the condition
j\geq 3
implies
(f\circ\omega_{j})^{x}=f^{x}\circ\omega_{j-1}
. Therefore,

(\omega_{j}\nu_{k+1})(f)=\nu\left(x\mapsto\nu_{k}\left(f^{x}\circ\omega_{j-1}\right)\right)=\nu\left(x\mapsto(\omega_{j-1}\nu_{k})\left(f^{x})\right)\right)

which by induction is

\nu\left(x\mapsto\nu_{k-1}\left(f^{x}\right)\right)=\nu_{k}(f).

∎

There is a natural identification of
\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k}
with subsets of
\mathbf{N}
of size
k
, and so we can take all
\nu_{k}
to be finitely additive probability measures on the finite subsets of
\mathbf{N}
. Theorem
2.3
then implies that the finitely additive probability measure
\mu_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=k+1}^{2k}\nu_{i}
is
1/k
-close to
\alpha_{i}(\mu)
for all
i\leq k
. In
section

5
we explain how to attain countably additive approximations and thus prove
A
.

Deleting vs filling, up vs down

Through the Looking-Glass
[
2

We write
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathbf{N})
for the set of finite subsets of
\mathbf{N}
. An element
E\in\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathbf{N})

if it is non-empty and
\max(E)-1\notin E

\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{pin}}(\mathbf{N})
for the collection of these elements and
[a,b]
for intervals of integers.

In this section we establish a bijection between
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathbf{N})
and
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{pin}}(\mathbf{N})

The following is a straightforward verification based on the definition of pin-headed.

Lemma 3.1
.

The map

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{pin}}(\mathbf{N})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathbf{N})

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\longmapsto[1,\max(E)]\smallsetminus E

is a bijection with inverse

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathbf{N})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{pin}}(\mathbf{N})

\displaystyle\varnothing

\displaystyle\longmapsto\{1\}

\displaystyle\varnothing\neq F

\displaystyle\longmapsto[1,\max(F)+1]\smallsetminus F.

∎

\sigma_{i}

Next, the operations

and

will be defined only on sets with at least

4.
The facial monoid

Consider the monoid presentation

S=\big\langle s_{i}:s_{j}s_{i}=s_{i}s_{j+1}\ \forall\,1\leq i\leq j\big\rangle.

Thus
S
is the monoid of all possible words (=finite sequences) on the letters
s_{i}
(with
i\in\mathbf{N}
), quotiented by the equivalence relation generated by the relations
s_{j}s_{i}=s_{i}s_{j+1}
applied to any substring of such words. There would be no substantial difference whatsoever working with semigroups rather than monoids; the only difference is that we allow the empty word
e
, which provides an identity element for
S
.

In order to manipulate elements of
S
it is crucial that this presentation admits normal forms. We shall say that a word is a
flat normal form
if it contains no substring
s_{j}s_{i}
with
j\geq i
. The terminology reflects the fact that in particular no proper power
s_{i}^{p>1}
can occur; thus, any flat normal form is either empty, or a single letter, or of the form
s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{n}}
with
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{n}
. In conclusion, flat normal forms can be identified with
\mathscr{P}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathbf{N})
.

The following is an elementary instance of the theory of string rewriting systems.

Lemma 4.1
.

Every element of the monoid
S
admits a unique representative in flat normal form.

Proof.

We consider the string rewriting system defined by the following rules:

s_{j}s_{i}\to s_{i}s_{j+1}\kern 14.22636pt\forall\,1\leq i\leq j.

The statement is now a very simple case of Newman’s Lemma
[
13
]
(for which a more streamlined reference is
[
8
, Lem. 2.4]
). Specifically, according to Newman’s Lemma it suffices to show that the system is both terminating and locally confluent.

Local confluence
, also called the weak Church–Rosser property, means that for all
w,w^{\prime},w^{\prime\prime}
with
w\to w^{\prime}
and
w\to w^{\prime\prime}
there exists
v
with
w^{\prime}\to^{*}v
and
w^{\prime\prime}\to^{*}v
. Here the symbol
\to
denotes an application of a rewriting rule to any substring of the given string and
\to^{*}
is the reflexive transitive closure of
\to
. Moreover, it suffices to check the case where
w\to w^{\prime}
and
w\to w^{\prime\prime}
are applications of rewriting rules to substrings of
w
that overlap. The only such overlaps are of the form
s_{k}s_{j}s_{i}
when
i\leq j\leq k
. We then check

5.
Amenability and random finite sets

Let
S
be any monoid, or more generally any semi-group. A
convex compact
S
-space
refers to a convex compact set
K
in some ambient Hausdorff topological vector space
V
, together with an
S
-action on
V
by continuous affine transformations preserving
K
.

In most of the literature,
V
is supposed locally convex. In that case it is equivalent, and perhaps more natural, to only require the
S
-action to be defined on
K
. Indeed,
K
then is naturally embedded in the dual of the Banach space of continuous affine maps
K\to\mathbf{R}
, endowed with the weak-* topology. This dual then provides a natural replacement for

which is endowed with a linear representation of
S
preserving
K
.

Theorem 5.1

(Day
[
3
,
4
]
)
.

The following are equivalent.

(1)

Every non-empty convex compact
S
-space has a fixed point.

(2)

There exists a left-invariant mean on
S
.

(3)

For every finite set
A\subseteq S
and every
\epsilon>0
there is a finitely supported probability distribution
\mu
on
S
such that
\|s\mu-\mu\|_{1}<\epsilon
holds for all
s\in A
.

When these equivalent conditions are satisfied, we say that

is

.

We caution the reader that Day called this property

, reserving the term amenability for the case where

admits a mean invariant under both left and right translations. Furthermore, he proved that the latter condition is equivalent to admitting both a left invariant mean and a right invariant one
[
3
, §6]
. We shall simply refer to his “right amenability” as the amenability of the opposite semi-group
S^{\mathrm{op}}
.

Remark 5.2
.

In the case of the facial monoid

that we study, the amenability of

is trivial: any point

fixed by

is fixed by

. Indeed, using the right action notation we have

ps_{i}=(ps_{1}^{i-1})s_{i}=(ps_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{i-1})s_{i}=ps_{1}^{i}=p.

Thus

establishes at once amenability in every sense of the word.

Notice furthermore that the above computation holds for any
S^{\mathrm{op}}
-action on any sort of set. This implies, for instance, that

satisfies the Schauder–Tychonoff fixed-point principle.

About the proof of

6.
Ergodic theory of the face relations:

a De Finetti type Theorem

Theorem
A
shows that there are probability measures on the set of finite subsets of the natural numbers that are almost-invariant to deleting the
i
th
smallest element. More generally, one could consider the set of nonempty, strictly increasing sequences of integers, with the operation of deleting the
i
th
element of the sequence. Clearly, there cannot be a completely invariant probability measure on this space. However, if one considered instead the space
\mathbf{N}^{\mathbf{N}}
of
all
sequences of integers, there is a probability measure with that property. Indeed, one could take the point mass on a constant sequence. Beyond that, any product measure is invariant to deletions. This section is motivated by the question of what other deletion-invariant measures exist.

We consider more generally a measurable space
(X,\Sigma)
, and let
\Omega=X^{\mathbf{N}}
be the set of sequences taking value in
X
. For
i\in\mathbf{N}
, let
s_{i}\colon\Omega\to\Omega
be given by

(6.i)

\displaystyle[s_{i}\omega]_{n}=\begin{cases}\omega_{n}&\text{if }n<i\\
\omega_{n+1}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that these transformation satisfy the face relations. The transformation
s_{1}
is the usual one-sided shift: it deletes the first coordinate and shifts the rest to the left. The transformation
s_{i}
leaves coordinates
1
through
i-1
unchanged, deletes the
i
th
coordinate, and shifts the rest to the left.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\Omega
. The following definitions are standard: We say that
\mu
is
invariant
if for every measurable
E\subseteq\Omega
and every
s_{i}
it holds that
\mu(s_{i}^{-1}E)=\mu(E)
. We say that
\mu
is an
IID
measure if there exists a probability measure
\eta
on
(X,\Sigma)
such that
\mu=\eta^{\mathbf{N}}
; clearly, IID measures are invariant. We say that
\mu
is
ergodic
if for a measurable
E\subseteq\Omega
such that
s_{i}^{-1}E=E
for all
i
it holds that
\mu(E)\in\{0,1\}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Every invariant ergodic probability measure on
X
is IID.

Proof.

Let
\mu
be invariant and ergodic. Let
\pi_{1}\colon\Omega\to X
be the projection
\pi_{1}(\omega)=\omega_{1}
, and let
\eta=\pi_{1}\mu
be the law of
\omega_{1}
. That is, for measurable
Y\subseteq X
,
\eta(Y)=\mu(\{\omega\,:\,\omega_{1}\in Y\})
.

We will show that if
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots
are subsets of
X
then

(6.ii)

\displaystyle\mu\left(\prod_{i}Y_{i}\right)=\prod_{i}\eta(Y_{i}).

Fix any measurable
Y\subseteq X
. For
i\in\mathbf{N}
, let

\displaystyle F_{i}=\{\omega\,:\,\omega_{i}\in Y\}.

Since
F_{i}=s_{1}^{1-i}F_{1}
and since
\mu
is
s_{1}
-invariant,
\mu(F_{i})=\mu(F_{1})=\eta(Y)
.

Fix any measurable

\displaystyle G=\{\omega\,:\,(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{M-1})\in W\}.

We claim that
\mu(F_{M}\cap G)=\mu(F_{M})\mu(G)
. This will then imply (
6.ii
) by a standard argument. Indeed, by the sigma-additivity of probability measures it suffices to show that if
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{M}
are subsets of

, and if
A_{i}=\{\omega\,:\,\omega_{i}\in Y_{i}\}
, then
\mu(A_{i}\cap\cdots\cap A_{M})=\eta(Y_{1})\cdots\eta(Y_{M})
. To this end, it suffices to show that
\mu(A_{i}\cap\cdots\cap A_{M})=\mu(A_{i}\cap\cdots\cap A_{M-1})\eta(Y_{M})
, as the proof then follows by induction.

Turning to the claim, let

be given by

\displaystyle f_{i}(\omega)=1_{F_{i}}(\omega)-\mu(F_{i})=1_{F_{i}}(\omega)-\mu(F_{1}).

Note that

and
\|f_{i}\|^{2}=\mu(F_{1})(1-\mu(F_{1}))\leq 1
. For

, let

be the isometric operator on

. It follows that

\displaystyle U_{i}f_{n}=\begin{cases}f_{n}&\text{if }n<i\\
f_{n+1}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

By the von Neumann ergodic theorem the limit

\displaystyle f=\lim_{N}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}U_{M}^{k}f_{M}

on the space of

\displaystyle f=\lim_{N}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}f_{M+k}=\lim_{N}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}f_{k},

where the second equality follows from the fact that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}f_{M+k}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}f_{k}\right\|\leq\frac{2M}{N}.

By the same argument, it holds for any

\displaystyle f=\lim_{N}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}f_{i+k}=\lim_{N}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}U_{i}^{k}f_{i+k}.

Thus

, and so

. Thus

.

Let
g(\omega)=1_{G}(\omega)-\mu(G)
, and note that

(g,f_{M})=\int(1_{G}(\omega)-\mu(G))(1_{F_{M}}(\omega)-\mu(F_{M}))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\omega)\\

is a (countably additive) probability measure on
\mathbf{N}
, and let
\nu_{k}
be the probability measure on
\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k}
defined as in the preamble to Theorem
2.3
. Then
\nu_{k}
is again countably additive, and is indeed the law of
k
steps of the
\nu
-random walk, compare
Remark

2.2
.

Proposition A.1
.

Suppose
\nu
is a countably additive probability measure, and that
\omega_{k+1}\nu_{k+1}
is
\epsilon
-close to
\nu_{k}
in total variation for some
k\geq 2
. Then
\epsilon>1/4
.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle M=\min\{y\in\mathbf{N}\,:\,\nu(\{1,\ldots,y\})>1/2\}.

This is well-defined since
\nu
is countably additive. Let
A\subseteq\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k}
be the event

\displaystyle A=\{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\,:\,y_{1}\leq M\},

and let
B\subseteq\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k+1}
be the event

\displaystyle B=\{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k+1})\,:\,y_{2}\leq M\}.

Then
B=(\omega_{k+1})^{-1}A
, and so
\epsilon\geq|\nu_{k+1}(B)-\nu_{k}(A)|
.

By the definition of
M
,
\nu_{k}(A)=\nu(\{1,\ldots,M\})>1/2
. As for
B
, it is contained in the intersection of the two independent events

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle=\{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k+1})\,:\,y_{1}\leq M-1\}

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle=\{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k+1})\,:\,y_{2}-y_{1}\leq M-1\}.

In analogy to
Remark

2.2
, the definition of
M
thus implies

\displaystyle\nu_{k+1}(B_{1})=\nu_{k+1}(B_{2})=\nu(\{1,\ldots,M-1\})\leq 1/2.

Therefore it follows
\nu_{k+1}(B)\leq\nu(\{1,\ldots,M-1\})^{2}\leq 1/4
and we conclude
\epsilon>1/4
.
∎

We note that this proof shows more generally that
\epsilon
admits a positive lower bound as soon as the mean
\nu
is not diffuse.

In probabilistic terms, this proof shows that if
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}
are independent and identically distributed random variables taking values in
\mathbf{N}
, then the joint distribution of their partial sums
y_{i}=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{i}
cannot be almost invariant to deletions.

In fact, the same proof implies a more general result: if
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}
are independent (but not necessarily identically distributed) random variables taking values in
\mathbf{N}
, and if
*
is a binary operation on
\mathbf{N}
satisfying (i)
x*y>x
and (ii)
x\geq x^{\prime}
,
y>y^{\prime}
implies
x*y>x^{\prime}*y^{\prime}
, then the joint distribution of the partial products
x_{1}*\cdots*x_{i}
cannot be almost invariant to deletions. This excludes a large range of Markov chains from constituting possible explicit constructions for Theorem
A
.

In the remainder of this section we outline an explicit construction that we conjecture satisfies Theorem
A
.

The measure
\mu
will be given by
\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=n+1}^{2n}\nu_{k}
, where each probability measure
\nu_{k}
is finitely supported on subsets of
\mathbf{N}
of size
k
. We define
\nu_{k}
as the image of the Lebesgue measure on the cube
[0,1]^{k}
under the composition of three maps:

[0,1]^{k}\xrightarrow{\ \Phi\ }\mathbf{R}^{k}\xrightarrow{\ E\ }\mathbf{R}_{\nearrow}^{k}\xrightarrow{\ \lfloor\cdot\rfloor\ }\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k}.

As above,
\mathbf{N}_{\nearrow}^{k}
is the set of strictly increasing sequences of length
k
natural numbers, which we naturally identify with the finite subsets of
\mathbf{N}
of size
k
. We denote by
\mathbf{R}_{\nearrow}^{k}
the sequences of non-negative reals that increase by at least one. We abusively suppress the index
k
in our notation for
\Phi,E
and
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
to emphasize that these maps are all truncations of the maps for
k=2n
, as follows. The first map is a product map

\Phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\bigl(\exp\exp(r_{1}x_{1}),\ldots,\exp\exp(r_{k}x_{k})\bigr)

for some constants
r_{1},\ldots,r_{2n}>1
.

The map
E
is the exponential tower map given by

E(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})=\left(y_{1},y_{1}^{y_{2}},y_{1}^{y_{2}^{y_{3}}},\ldots,y_{1}^{y_{2}^{\iddots^{y_{k}}}}\right).

The map
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
is simply the component-wise floor map that rounds each coordinate down.

In probabilistic terms, we let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
be IID random variables distributed uniformly on
[0,1]
, let
y_{i}=\exp\exp(r_{i}x_{i})
, and let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
be given by

\displaystyle z_{i}=y_{1}^{y_{2}^{\iddots^{y_{i}}}}.

Finally,
\nu_{k}
is the distribution of
(\lfloor z_{1}\rfloor,\cdots,\lfloor z_{k}\rfloor)
. Note that the rounding-down operation commutes with the deletion maps
\alpha_{i}
, since it is order preserving.

To choose the constants
r_{i}
we start by choosing
r_{2n}
to be large, and then, proceeding backwards, choose each
r_{i-1}
to be much larger than
\exp(r_{i})
. Then
\exp(r_{i-1})
is much larger than
\exp\exp(r_{i})
, and so

\displaystyle z_{2}=y_{1}^{y_{2}}=\exp\left(\exp(r_{1}x_{1})+\exp\exp(r_{2}x_{2})\right)

has approximately the same distribution as
z_{1}=y_{1}=\exp\exp(r_{1}x_{1})
. Indeed, the total variation distance between the distributions of
z_{i}
and
z_{j}
can be all made uniformly arbitrarily small by choosing
r_{1},\ldots,r_{2n}
appropriately.

The recursive structure of this construction means that proving that it is
\alpha_{1}
-almost invariant would imply that it is almost invariant to
\alpha_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
. That is, it suffices to show that the joint distribution of
(z_{1},\ldots_{,}z_{k-1})
is approximately equal to that of
(z_{2},\ldots,z_{k})
. By the comment above, the marginal distributions are indeed close. It is furthermore easy to show that the joint distribution of
(z_{1},z_{2})
is close to that of
(z_{2},z_{3})
, and likewise that the joint distribution of
(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})
is close to that of
(z_{2},z_{3},z_{4})
. However, the general proof seems elusive.
