Journey Mapping the Unmet Needs in Pediatric Adenoid Hypertrophy: A Caregiver’s Perspective
Authors: Shidi Xu, Xiaoyan Feng, Minjuan Yu, Tianhong Xu, Zhenzhen Pan, Ling Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo systematically map the patient journey and identify unmet needs in children with adenoid hypertrophy and their caregivers.MethodsA qualitative study using in-depth interviews with 25 caregivers of children aged 3–9 years was conducted. Adenoid hypertrophy was confirmed by nasoendoscopy (≥70% obstruction) and/or polysomnography (OAHI >1 event/hour). Data were analyzed through reflexive thematic analysis to construct a comprehensive journey map. The emotional curve was derived qualitatively from caregiver narratives as a heuristic visualization of emotional trajectories across care stages, not as a quantitative measure.ResultsAnalysis revealed two distinct emotional trajectories: surgical path caregivers experienced a post-discharge crisis due to support vacuum, while conservative path caregivers endured persistent anxiety from efficacy uncertainty. We identified both pathway-specific needs (post-discharge support, efficacy feedback) and cross-cutting needs (proactive information, holistic habit guidance).ConclusionThis journey mapping provides an evidence-based framework for developing targeted interventions to inform caregiver education, discharge planning, follow-up strategies, and multidisciplinary care pathways, addressing critical system failures and enhancing patient-centered care in pediatric adenoid hypertrophy.Keywords: adenoid hypertrophy, sleep-disordered breathing, patient journey mapping, qualitative research, unmet needs, caregiver experience

Introduction

Childhood sleep-disordered breathing (SDB),1,2 with an estimated prevalence of 7–15% in the pediatric population, represents a significant clinical and public health challenge.3 Adenoid hypertrophy stands as the predominant underlying cause of pediatric SDB, directly linking structural upper airway obstruction to the pathophysiological continuum ranging from primary snoring to obstructive sleep apnea (OSA).4 As a modifiable and anatomically defined condition at the mild end of the SDB spectrum, it represents a critical intervention point for preventing progression to more severe disease and mitigating long-term neurocognitive and cardiovascular sequelae.5–8 The implications of this condition extend far beyond nocturnal noise, as it is robustly associated with detrimental outcomes including neurocognitive deficits, behavioral disturbances such as inattention and hyperactivity, impaired growth, and potential cardiovascular sequelae. Consequently, pediatric SDB related to adenoid hypertrophy critically impacts a child’s developmental trajectory, academic performance, and overall quality of life, establishing its management as a priority in pediatric respiratory medicine.

The contemporary clinical paradigm for managing pediatric SDB secondary to adenoid hypertrophy is well-established, focusing on objective diagnostic measures—such as polysomnography—and evidence-based treatments aimed at restoring upper airway patency and normalizing sleep architecture.6 Diagnosis typically relies on a combination of clinical assessment, nasoendoscopy, and polysomnography (PSG), the gold standard for quantifying SDB severity.9 Treatment options range from conservative medical management with intranasal corticosteroids to first-line surgical intervention via adenoidectomy or adenotonsillectomy.10,11 Reflecting this paradigm, the vast majority of scientific literature has concentrated on refining surgical techniques, comparing the efficacy of various medical interventions, and evaluating success through objective biomarkers—primarily PSG parameters such as the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI). While these studies are indispensable for establishing treatment efficacy and safety, this predominant focus on biomedical endpoints has largely overshadowed the holistic, lived experience of children and their families navigating the full SDB care continuum from symptom onset to long-term management.

This gap underscores the critical need to embrace the principles of patient-centered care,12 which mandates a deep understanding of the complete “patient journey”—from the initial recognition of symptoms and the diagnostic odyssey, through treatment decisions and recovery, to long-term adaptation and monitoring. This journey is inherently multifaceted, fraught with physical discomfort, emotional turmoil, logistical hurdles, and informational gaps. These multifaceted challenges, however, remain largely invisible and unquantified within the framework of traditional clinical studies.13 A core component of this experience is the constellation of “unmet needs,” defined as the gaps between the services provided by the healthcare system and the actual needs of patients and their caregivers. Identifying these needs is the crucial first step towards improving care quality. However, a systematic, qualitative exploration that visually maps this journey and its associated unmet needs in the context of pediatric SDB remains conspicuously absent from the scientific discourse.

To effectively address this gap,14,15 the methodology of “patient journey mapping” offers a powerful and innovative approach. Originating from user-experience and service design, journey mapping provides a structured, visual framework to chronicle the multifaceted interactions, actions, perceptions, and emotional states of end-users—in this case, patients and caregivers—across all touchpoints with the healthcare system. This method transcends mere description; it functions as a diagnostic tool to pinpoint critical failures, inefficiencies, and moments of distress, thereby transforming subjective experiences into an evidence-based blueprint for targeted service improvements. Its application in clinical settings represents a cutting-edge, cross-disciplinary strategy for enhancing the quality and humanity of care.

Therefore, the primary aim of this qualitative study is to systematically map the patient journey and identify the key unmet needs of children with adenoid hypertrophy—the most common and readily modifiable cause of pediatric SDB—and their caregivers. By focusing on this prototypical SDB condition, we seek to generate insights that are not only condition-specific but also transferable to the broader field of pediatric sleep medicine, where understanding the lived experience of sleep-disordered breathing is essential for advancing holistic, patient-centered care.

Using in-depth, semi-structured interviews and rigorous thematic analysis, we constructed a comprehensive journey map that visualizes the key stages, touchpoints, emotional trajectories, and critical pain points that families traverse. We anticipate that the findings will not only provide a rich, empathetic narrative of the family experience but also illuminate specific, actionable targets for healthcare providers, nurses, and administrators. Ultimately, this work is intended to inform the co-design of more supportive, efficient, and truly patient-centered care pathways for this prevalent pediatric condition.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a qualitative study utilizing the patient journey mapping methodology to explore and visualize the experiences of caregivers and children with adenoid hypertrophy and associated sleep-disordered breathing (SDB). This study was situated within a descriptive qualitative design, aiming to provide a comprehensive account of the journey from the participants’ perspective. The study was guided by an interpretivist paradigm, which emphasizes understanding the subjective meanings and lived experiences of individuals in their specific contexts.

Participant Recruitment and Sampling

Primary caregivers (parents or legal guardians) of children aged 3–9 years with a confirmed diagnosis of adenoid hypertrophy and SDB by a pediatric respiratory specialist were eligible for participation. The diagnosis was objectively confirmed through nasoendoscopy (documenting adenoid size occupying ≥70% of the nasopharyngeal airway) and/or overnight polysomnography (obstructive apnea-hypopnea index, OAHI >1 event/hour). Participants were purposively sampled from the pediatric respiratory medicine outpatient clinic of a tertiary children’s hospital in Wuxi, China. Recruitment for this study has been completed during the period of January to April 2025. Purposive sampling ensured diversity in children’s age, disease severity (based on clinical assessment and/or polysomnography results), and treatment experience (including both families who opted for adenoidectomy and those pursuing conservative management). Recruitment continued until data saturation was achieved, which we defined as three consecutive interviews that yielded no new codes or thematic insights, resulting in a final sample of 25 caregiver participants. Exclusion criteria included caregivers of children with significant comorbid conditions (eg, syndromic disorders, severe cognitive impairment) or those who could not communicate in Mandarin Chinese.

Data Collection

Data were primarily collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews using a pre-developed interview guide (see Supplementary File S1). The guide was developed based on a literature review and consultations with clinical experts (including pediatric otorhinolaryngologists, pediatric respiratory specialists, and nurses), and it was piloted with two caregivers (not included in the final sample). The guide was designed to elicit rich narratives covering all stages of the patient journey, including initial symptom recognition, help-seeking behaviors, diagnostic processes, treatment decision-making, postoperative recovery, and long-term management.

All interviews were conducted by the first author (Xu Shidi), a registered nurse who holds a Master of Science in Nursing and has received specialized training in qualitative methodologies, including in-depth interview techniques. The interviews were held in a private, quiet room at the outpatient clinic. Each interview lasted between 40 to 75 minutes. With participants’ permission, all interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription service. All transcripts were cross-checked against the audio recordings by the research team to ensure accuracy. Field notes were taken during and after each interview to capture contextual observations. Brief reviews of medical records were conducted post-interview to corroborate timelines and treatment details mentioned by participants.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the interview. For children aged 3–9 years, age-appropriate verbal assent was also sought.

Data Analysis and Journey Mapping Construction

The data analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis approach and proceeded concurrently with data collection in an iterative cycle.16

Coding: Two researchers independently coded the first five transcripts line-by-line to generate initial codes. They then met to compare, discuss, and refine the codes, developing a preliminary coding framework. Any discrepancies in coding were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Deriving the Emotional Curve and Unmet Needs: The “Emotional Curve” for caregivers was constructed through a collaborative, iterative process. The team first identified and extracted all emotion-laden statements (eg, “anxious,” “relieved,” “frustrated,” “hopeless”) from the interview transcripts for each journey stage. The emotional valence assignment followed a rigorous consensus process: (1) two researchers independently identified and categorized emotion-laden statements for each journey stage; (2) they then independently assigned preliminary valence scores; (3) discrepancies were discussed in research team meetings with reference to the original transcripts; (4) final scores were determined through iterative review until full consensus was achieved. This process ensured that the emotional curve accurately reflected the frequency and intensity of emotional expressions in the data.

The “Emotional Curve” is a visual synthesis of the predominant emotional themes. “Unmet Needs” were explicitly defined as recurring, systemic failures or gaps in care that caused significant distress or burden to families. These were directly derived from the synthesized “pain points” in the journey map.

To enhance the trustworthiness and credibility of the findings,17 a process of member checking was employed. A simplified, visual version of the journey map and a summary of the unmet needs were shared with five participating caregivers. Their feedback confirmed the accuracy and resonance of our findings, and minor adjustments to the phrasing of some themes were made accordingly.

Overview of the Patient Journey and Divergent Emotional Trajectories

Analysis of caregiver narratives revealed that the patient journey follows a common pathway until a critical juncture—the treatment decision—after which it diverges into two distinct paths (Figure 2). The overarching emotional trajectories for these two paths illustrate the profound impact of this treatment decision on caregiver experience.

The patient journey map for children with adenoid hypertrophy and their caregivers. The map illustrates the sequential stages of the care pathway, beginning with common stages for all families and diverging after the treatment decision into two distinct trajectories: Path A (Surgical Treatment) and Path B (Conservative Treatment). The final long-term management stage represents a convergence of both paths. Key decision points and clinical touchpoints are highlighted to visualize the structure of the patient experience from symptom onset to long-term follow-up.

The common initial stages (Stages 1–3) were characterized by uncertainty and anxiety. Stage 4, the first stage after the decision point, represented the point of greatest divergence. For the surgical path, the postoperative acute recovery phase (Stage 4A3) constituted the emotional nadir of the entire journey, primarily driven by the “post-discharge support vacuum” (N8) and “vague recovery guidance” (N9). In contrast, the conservative management path exhibited a persistent “baseline anxiety” throughout its medication management phase (Stage 4B1), with a notable dip during the observation and doubt phase (Stage 4B2), largely due to the “unclear efficacy feedback loop” (N11) and frustrations about treatment efficacy and decision uncertainty, compounded by the “lack of support at decision turning point” (N13) (Figure 1).

After discharge, they felt abandoned. “The first night home was a nightmare. He was in pain, refused his medicine. We called the hospital, but they just said it was normal. Stage 5: Cautious Optimism in Long-Term Management

Following the acute recovery, a sense of relief emerged as symptoms improved, yet it was tempered by vigilance and ongoing struggles, particularly due to the lack of post-procedural habit reversal support (N10). “The surgery stopped the snoring, thank God. But we are still watching him like a hawk, and we do not have professional methods to correct his mouth-breathing habit.” (P14, P22).

Stage 4B1: Vigilant Management and Watchful Waiting – “Is This Even Working?”This path was defined by a constant, low-grade anxiety (“persistent baseline anxiety”). Families diligently administered medication but were plagued by doubt stemming from an “unclear efficacy feedback loop” (N11) and burdens associated with long-term medication (N12). “Every day it’s the same routine: the nasal spray, the pills. Transcending the treatment choice, two fundamental gaps were identified as cross-pathway challenges. The first was the consistent lack of “Proactive and Accessible Information and Expectation Management” (Need 3), which manifested from diagnosis through long-term management, leaving families feeling unprepared and anxious at multiple journey stages. The second shared need was for “Holistic, Habit-Centered Guidance” (Need 4), highlighting a critical gap in multidisciplinary support to address compensatory behaviors like chronic mouth-breathing, whether it persisted after surgery or despite ongoing medical management. This synthesis underscores that improving care requires addressing both the distinct stressors of each treatment path and the foundational system-wide failures in information delivery and holistic support.

Discussion

Principal Findings

This study provides the first comprehensive mapping of the patient journey in pediatric adenoid hypertrophy, translating subjective caregiver experiences into an evidence-based model of care. Our principal contribution is twofold. First, we precisely quantify the divergent emotional costs of treatment choices: the surgical path is a rollercoaster culminating in a severe post-discharge crisis, whereas the conservative path is a protracted struggle characterized by debilitating uncertainty. Second, by synthesizing these experiences, we move beyond mere description to identify a critical typology of unmet needs. This typology clearly differentiates between challenges inherent to a specific treatment trajectory (eg, the post-discharge support vacuum) and those that represent fundamental, system-wide gaps in care delivery (eg, the failure in proactive information management). Collectively, these findings provide a granular and actionable framework for quality improvement that is directly informed by the patient and family voice.

Interpretation and Comparison with Existing Literature

Our study demonstrates the utility of patient journey mapping as a powerful diagnostic tool for identifying critical failures in complex care pathways,18,19 moving beyond traditional qualitative descriptions to provide a temporally-grounded and visual model of patient experience. Unlike traditional qualitative approaches, this methodology provided both temporal granularity and a visual synthesis of emotional trajectories, which enabled the precise identification of critical intervention points within the care continuum.20 This approach directly aligns with recent calls for more dynamic, experience-based frameworks to drive healthcare quality improvement. The subsequent discussion of our findings, structured around the specific unmet needs identified through reflexive thematic analysis (as detailed in Table 2), provides a nuanced extension to the existing literature on pediatric adenoid hypertrophy.

The Surgical Pathway: A Cascade of Perioperative System Failures

The identified emotional nadir during the post-discharge period extends the established understanding of discharge vulnerability in pediatric SDB.21,22 While prior research confirms this period is one of high stress, our journey mapping delineates its precise mechanisms. The profound distress was a direct consequence of a critical “post-discharge support vacuum” (N8) and “vague recovery guidance” (N9), creating a perfect storm where managing complications, interpreting symptoms like resumed snoring, and enforcing dietary restrictions converged. This finding directly challenges a perioperative care model that peaks in support within the hospital walls and drops precipitously upon discharge. Furthermore, we identified that the foundation for this crisis is laid preoperatively, with a “lack of preoperative psychological preparation” (N6) and poor “intraoperative communication” (N7) significantly heightening anxiety before the first incision. This indicates that23,24 supportive interventions must begin early in the surgical pathway to mitigate the subsequent post-discharge crisis.

The Conservative Pathway: Active Uncertainty and the Limits of Shared Decision-Making

This study reframes the experience of conservative management from passive “watchful waiting” to an25 active psychological burden, a distinction critically fueled by the profound “uncertainty in treatment efficacy and trajectory” (N11). Our findings not only align with but extend26 the illness uncertainty theory by identifying the specific clinical determinants of uncertainty in pediatric SDB—namely, the absence of objective biomarkers for treatment response and the lack of clear decision-point criteria. This suggests that illness uncertainty in this context is not merely a psychological construct but is actively constructed by specific gaps in clinical monitoring and communication protocols. The significant dip in morale during the “observation and doubt phase (Stage 4B2)” is critically linked to the “lack of support at decision turning point” (N13). This suggests a potential shortcoming in the shared decision-making (SDM) process that precedes treatment choice. While SDM aims to align treatment with family values, our findings indicate the process is incomplete if it does not equip families with the tools—such as structured symptom trackers and clear criteria for re-evaluation—to navigate the experiential and emotional realities of the chosen path after the decision is made.27 The burden of this uncertainty is substantial, correlating with the persistent “baseline anxiety” that characterizes this path and echoing its negative impact on quality of life seen in other pediatric populations.

Cross-Cutting System Failures: Information and Holistic Care

Beyond path-specific challenges, our typology of unmet needs exposes fundamental, system-wide gaps. The universal cry for “proactive and accessible information and expectation management” (N3) underscores a systemic failure in current patient education paradigms.28 Families did not simply need more information; they needed anticipatory guidance about the emotional and symptomatic recovery curve before they encountered crises.29 This calls for a paradigm shift from one-time, pre-procedural education to a continuous, stage-specific information delivery system.30 The transition from one-time education to continuous information delivery could be operationalized through structured digital health pathways that provide: (1) stage-specific expectation-setting messages, (2) symptom tracking functionality with automated feedback, and (3) just-in-time access to clinical support based on the specific recovery phase, an approach whose potential is increasingly recognized in pediatric care.

These unmet needs have direct implications for sleep-related outcomes: inadequate post-discharge support may lead to suboptimal recovery and residual sleep-disordered breathing symptoms; uncertainty regarding conservative treatment efficacy may compromise medication adherence and timely follow-up; and the absence of habit reversal guidance perpetuates mouth-breathing, which can reinforce sleep-disordered breathing even after anatomical obstruction is resolved.

Finally, the shared theme of needing “holistic, habit-centered guidance” reveals a critical gap in the multidisciplinary management of pediatric SDB. However, our analysis crucially demonstrates that this common theme manifests with path-dependent urgency. It presents as a need for “post-procedural habit reversal support” (N10) after surgery, and as “concurrent habit management guidance” (N14) during medical treatment. This distinction highlights that while adenotonsillectomy may resolve anatomical obstruction, and medical management controls inflammation, both often fail to address the resultant functional and behavioral sequelae. The integration of allied health professionals, such as31 myofunctional therapists, into standard care pathways is therefore not an ancillary service but a crucial component for ensuring comprehensive, biopsychosocial recovery.

Clinical Implications and Recommendations for Practice

The following recommendations, while grounded in our findings, represent forward-looking implications that warrant further evaluation through implementation research. Our findings translate into a set of actionable, evidence-based recommendations, precisely targeted at the identified unmet needs. These recommendations target different levels of care delivery: from clinician-patient interactions to institutional protocols and system-wide reforms.

For the Surgical Pathway: A Proactive, Perioperative Support Model

For the Surgical Pathway: A Proactive, Perioperative Support Model

To address the cascade of failures, interventions must span the entire pathway. To mitigate the identified preoperative psychological preparation deficit (N6) and intraoperative communication gap (N7), we recommend implementing structured pre-admission counseling sessions and standardized protocols for providing real-time updates to families during surgery. Most critically, to bridge the post-discharge support vacuum (N8) and provide concrete recovery guidance (N9), the implementation of a structured, nurse-led tele-follow-up program within 24–48 hours of discharge is essential. Evidence from32 pediatric post-discharge care and33 chronic disease management supports the cost-effectiveness and clinical efficacy of such nurse-led interventions in improving outcomes and preventing complications. Finally, to address persistent mouth-breathing post-surgery (N10), clear referral pathways to allied health professionals specializing in orofacial function,34 such as orofacial myofunctional therapists or speech-language pathologists with relevant expertise, should be established.

2.For the Conservative Pathway: Structuring the “Watchful Waiting” Period

For the Conservative Pathway: Structuring the “Watchful Waiting” Period

The core goal is to objectify the invisible and reduce decisional paralysis. To combat the uncertainty in treatment efficacy (N11), clinicians should develop and utilize simple, visual symptom and side-effect tracking tools (eg, diaries or mobile apps). These tools make the “waiting” process tangible, facilitate productive clinic discussions, and empower families. For instance, we recommend developing simple visual tracking tools (eg, colour-coded symptom diaries or mobile applications) that enable families to systematically document daily symptoms, medication adherence, and potential side effects.20 The integration of such patient-generated health data into clinical decision-making is a key strategy in modern chronic disease management. Furthermore, to address the lack of support at decision turning points (N13), these tools should be integrated with clear, pre-defined criteria for evaluating conservative treatment success and triggering a re-evaluation for surgery. These tools could be embedded with predefined clinical decision benchmarks (eg, a prompt to “contact the clinic if snoring severity persists without improvement for 4 consecutive weeks”) to facilitate proactive re-evaluation and mitigate decisional conflict.

3.For All Pathways: Addressing Systemic Failures

For All Pathways: Addressing Systemic Failures

To meet the universal need for proactive information (N3), healthcare institutions should co-design standardized, visual patient education packages with families. These materials must be stage-specific and explicitly outline the typical emotional and physical trajectory for both paths, providing crucial anticipatory guidance.30 This calls for a shift from one-time information delivery to a continuous, stage-specific system, potentially leveraging digital health tools. Additionally, the need for holistic, habit-centered guidance requires integrated multidisciplinary support. This manifests as the need for concurrent habit management (N14) during conservative care and should be part of a comprehensive, biopsychosocial care model from the outset.

Strengths and Limitations

It is important to reiterate that the emotional curve and journey map presented in this study are interpretive visualizations derived from qualitative caregiver narratives. They serve as heuristic tools to synthesize and communicate the lived experience across the care continuum, rather than as validated psychometric or clinical outcome measures.

This study has several notable strengths. Its primary contribution is the development of a novel, evidence-based patient journey map that integrates qualitative themes with a visual emotional curve. This mixed-methods approach not only identifies challenges but also diagnoses their systemic origins and predicts their timing within the care continuum, providing an actionable typology of unmet needs (N1-N14). The rigorous methodology, including member checking and purposive sampling, enhances the credibility and diversity of the data.

Several limitations should be considered. First, as a qualitative study conducted at a single tertiary center in China, the generalizability of the specific care pathways and healthcare touchpoints may be limited in other systems with different resources and clinical protocols. The findings should be interpreted as contextually grounded insights that may require adaptation when applied to healthcare systems with differing models of care delivery, reimbursement structures, or cultural expectations regarding family involvement in pediatric care. Second, the predominance of mothers in our sample (84%), while reflective of the primary caregiver demographic, potentially underrepresents perspectives from fathers or other caregivers, which may influence the portrayal of decision-making dynamics and coping strategies. Third, recall bias regarding earlier journey stages is possible, though mitigated by focusing on recent experiences and corroborating timelines through medical record review. Finally, the construction of the emotional curve, while based on consensus and representative quotes, involves a degree of interpretive synthesis. Future research should include multi-center studies to validate this model across settings and, most critically, focus on implementing and evaluating the proposed interventions to measure their impact on patient experience, clinical outcomes, and healthcare utilization.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study moves decisively “beyond the snore” by systematically mapping the patient journey in pediatric adenoid hypertrophy, revealing distinct emotional trajectories and a typology of unmet needs that challenge current care paradigms. We have demonstrated that the surgical and conservative management paths entail fundamentally different caregiver experiences—characterized by a post-discharge crisis and a protracted struggle with uncertainty, respectively—each with its own set of intervention points. By transforming these subjective experiences into a structured, evidence-based model, we provide not merely a description of challenges, but a diagnostic tool and a practical blueprint for targeted quality improvement. Placing the lived experiences and specific unmet needs of families at the center of care redesign is the essential next step toward achieving truly patient-centered care in pediatric respiratory medicine. Future work should focus on implementing and evaluating the interventions proposed herein, ultimately measuring their impact on both the human experience and clinical outcomes in pediatric SDB care.
