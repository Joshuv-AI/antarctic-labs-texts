Impact of depression on medication non-adherence in coronary artery disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Dione Lorraine Jones, Jason Yue, Tu Ng Nguyen, Fahed Ahmad, Clara K Chow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression is a risk factor and complication of coronary artery disease (CAD) that is associated with poor cardiovascular outcomes. This may be related to medication non-adherence; a topic on which considerable new evidence has emerged. We aim to assess the relationship between depression and medication adherence in patients with CAD.MethodsThe Cochrane Library, Medline and Embase databases were searched for relevant studies published until January 2023. Studies were included if they reported the prevalence of adherence to cardiovascular medications in depressed versus non-depressed participants aged >18 years with CAD, including acute and chronic coronary syndromes and those undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention. The primary outcome was the OR of non-adherence to cardiovascular medications in depressed versus non-depressed patients.ResultsIncluded studies (n=30) comprised mainly of cohort or cross-sectional studies, most of which involved US participants. Nine tools were used to measure medication adherence, the majority of which relied on participant self-report. Nine depression diagnostic tools were used, including the Patient Health Questionnaire, Beck Depression Inventory and International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9/10) diagnostic codes. The prevalence of depression ranged widely (1.7–76.6%), as did medication non-adherence (5.9–72.9%). A meta-analysis of 17 studies and 82 059 patients showed that depression was associated with increased medication non-adherence (OR 1.48, 95% CI 1.23 to 1.78, p<0.001) using a random effects model. However, there was considerable heterogeneity between studies (I2=91%, τ2=0.17, p<0.001), and the association was absent under a fixed effects model (OR 0.99 95% CL 0.98 to 1.01, p=0.58).ConclusionPatients with CAD and depression may have increased odds of medication non-adherence; however, there was considerable clinical and statistical heterogeneity, underscoring the need for further research to better understand this relationship.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) and coronary artery disease (CAD) frequently coexist yet independently contribute significantly to the global burden of disease.1 One in three patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) has depression compared with 1 in 20 in the general population.2 3 This may be related to reduced quality of life from poorly controlled angina, or the significant emotional and physical distress associated with myocardial infarction (MI).4 5 Recognising the relationship between CAD and depression is important as worsening depression is associated with worse cardiovascular outcomes, including recurrent cardiac events and higher mortality.469 Potential contributing factors include medication non-adherence, smoking/substance use, unhealthy lifestyle,10 11 suicide12 and/or abnormal regulation of neurochemical pathways.13

Management of CAD aims to reduce cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, reduce symptoms and improve quality of life.14 Pharmacotherapy involves antianginal agents as well as cardioprotective medications to reduce the incidence of acute thrombotic events and ventricular dysfunction.14 Maintaining medication adherence remains an important clinical challenge as non-adherence is associated with increased mortality, morbidity and rehospitalisation.15 16 In stable CAD, non-adherence has been associated with more than twofold increased rate of subsequent cardiovascular events,17 and 1 in 10 cardiovascular events has been attributed to non-adherence.18 Identifying strategies to improve adherence is a major public health concern. Individuals with depression are three times more likely to be non-adherent to medical recommendations than non-depressed patients.19 A previous meta-analysis of seven studies (n=5058) by Crawshaw et al revealed that medication non-adherence was twice as likely in depressed patients following ACS, compared with those without depression.20

This study aims to determine the extent to which concomitant depression affects medication adherence in patients with CAD. The results of which will expand on previous studies and improve our understanding of why depressed patients with CAD have poorer outcomes.

Methods

This systematic review was performed in alignment with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.21

Search strategy

A systematic literature search of three electronic databases (Cochrane, Medline and Embase) was conducted between February and March 2023 to identify relevant papers published in English language that described a relationship between depression and medication adherence in a population with CAD. All titles and abstracts were independently screened by two individuals (DLJ and FA) to determine whether inclusion criteria were met, with any discrepancies resolved by a third individual. The search strategy used the PICO (Population, Intervention/Exposure, Comparison, Outcome) format, whereby patients with CAD were the population of interest, the exposure was the presence of depression, the comparison group comprised non-depressed patients and the outcome was non-adherence to prescribed cardiovascular medications.

Study selection

Included studies reported a quantitative relationship between medication adherence in depressed versus non-depressed patients aged >18 years with a documented history of CAD, including either acute or chronic coronary syndrome or percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). There were no restrictions to study design. ‘Depressed’ participants had a documented history of depression or symptoms suggestive of a diagnosis based on questionnaires or medical interview. Studies involving participants with similar psychiatric conditions in which depressed mood was a core feature (eg, dysthymia) were included. Included studies also measured adherence to cardiovascular medications based on participant self-report or objective measures such as pill counts and prescription refills. The adherence threshold was determined based on the assessment tool used in each individual study. For pill counting methods, non-adherence was defined as pills removed <80% of days. For prescription refills, non-adherence was defined as <80% of days covered using the medication possession ratio (MPR) or proportion of days covered (PDC). Studies without objective adherence measures were also included, whereby non-adherence was defined subjectively by participant self-report or by validated questionnaires such as the Morisky Medical Adherence Scale, or other scale. Cardiovascular medications included beta-blockers, antiplatelets (aspirin, P2Y12 receptor antagonists), antihypertensives (eg, ACE inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers) and lipid lowering medications (eg, statins). No restrictions were applied to time or place of publication, participant ethnicity or socioeconomic demographics. Individual bibliographies were screened to identify additional relevant resources.

Studies were excluded if they lacked full-text manuscript, original data, English translation or were not specific to CAD (eg, other cardiovascular diseases such as stroke, arrhythmia, peripheral artery disease or isolated risk factors such as hypertension). Conference abstracts, letters and commentaries were excluded due to insufficient data for inclusion in the meta-analysis, as were studies assessing adherence to antidepressants or non-pharmacological treatment (eg, cardiac rehabilitation). Studies specific to patients undergoing cardiac surgery were excluded due to the potential for confounding variables (eg, postoperative cognitive dysfunction or surgical complications).22 Studies investigating non-atherosclerotic ACS (eg, drug-induced, spontaneous coronary artery dissection) were also excluded due to the demographic differences from atherosclerotic CAD.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data were first collected from the included studies by one researcher (DLJ) then independently confirmed by a second researcher (JY). The following information was extracted: first author, publication year, country, study population, study design, sample size, age, sex (%male), depression tool, medication type, adherence measure, depression prevalence, non-adherence prevalence and study results. Individual study results are presented in online supplemental table 1. Risk of bias was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies.23 Studies were independently adjudicated by two individuals (DLJ and JY) against 14 items and assigned an overall classification of ‘good’, ‘fair’ or ‘poor’.

Statistical analysis

The primary exposure variable was expressed as a dichotomous categorical variable (depressed vs non-depressed) which was determined by individual study definitions.24 25 For studies reporting categorical ranges of depression severity (none, minimal, mild, moderate, severe), results were dichotomised whereby ‘mild, moderate, severe’ depressive symptoms were considered ‘depressed’, and ‘minimal symptoms’ were considered ‘nondepressed’. Studies that did not report an OR were included in the systematic review but excluded from the meta-analysis. For the primary outcome (medication non-adherence), descriptive statistics in the form of frequencies and percentages were retrieved from individual study results. Direction of effect was manipulated to ensure consistent reporting; whereby inverse calculation was used to transform adherence to non-adherence where possible.26 The meta-analysis was conducted in R V.4.3.2.27 The primary outcome was reported as an OR and 95% CI, with adjusted estimates of effect size included where possible. The SE for individual studies was calculated as SE(log(OR)) = (log CIupper − log CIlower)/3.92) where CIupper(lower) denotes the reported 95% upper(lower) CIs. The pooled estimate CI was obtained using the ‘meta’ package in R,27 28 where weighting of the included studies was scaled by the inverse variance of effect size.29 With between-study heterogeneity anticipated from non-uniform depression screening tools and different medication classes, pooled statistics with both a fixed-effects (FEM) and random-effects model (REM) were reported. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and confirmed with Egger’s regression test. Statistical heterogeneity was obtained by modelling the variance τ2 via DerSimonian-Laird estimator30 and summarised in the Higgins and Thompson’s I2 statistic.31 We performed subgroup analysis according to type of CAD presentation, medication, depression measure, adherence measure, study type and whether ORs were adjusted.

Results

Of 3185 studies retrieved by the initial search, 30 were included in the final analysis (figure 1). 19 studies reported ORs or published data enabling calculation of ORs between depression and non-adherence. Two studies had discrepancies in their data and were excluded from the meta-analysis,32 33 leaving a total of 17 studies. Of the 30 included studies, 29 were observational (14 prospective, 7 retrospective, 8 cross-sectional), and there was one randomised control trial (online supplemental table 1). The included studies represented data from multiple continents, the majority of which originated from the USA (n=15, 50%), followed by Asia (n=9, 30%), Europe (n=5, 17%) or a combination (n=1, 3%).

Most prospective and cross-sectional studies collected data from interviews and questionnaires (n=21), while six of the seven retrospective studies used database collection. There was significant sample size variation, largely dependent on the data collection method, ranging from 55 to 124 443 participants. Smaller sample sizes were more common in older prospective cohort studies (>20 years post publication),3436 whereas retrospective studies analysing insurance claims or registries had larger populations.333743 There was a higher proportion of males in all studies, averaging 72.9%.

Risk of bias assessment

On quality assessment, six studies were considered ‘good’, while 11 studies were ‘fair’ (online supplemental file 4 table A1). Common reasons for deductions included lack of participation rate transparency, lack of blinding or sample size justification and insufficient time frame for development of exposure-outcome associations (cross-sectional studies). Overall study quality was limited due to the observational nature of most included studies.

Depression

The most common depression assessment tool was the Patient Health Questionnaire 9-item (PHQ-9) (n=6), followed by the International Statistical Classification of Diseases (ICD) (n=6), the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) (n=6) and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (n=5) (online supplemental table 1). Other methods included medical interview according to the Diagnostic Interview Schedule or Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (III), PHQ (2-item), BDI-FS (Fast Screen), European Quality of Life Five Dimensions Questionnaire, Symptom Checklist-90-Revised (SCL-90-R), Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale and other non-validated questionnaires (online supplemental table 1). Five studies combined tools and one study used self-reported depressed mood alone (online supplemental table 1).44 The prevalence of depression varied widely from 1.7% to 76.6%, which narrowed slightly when stratified to ACS only (1.7–45.9%),42 45 PCI only (8.1–76.6%)41 46 and other coronary heart disease (CHD) subgroups (5.4–24.4%).44 47 Studies with a lower proportion of depression used database codes for diagnosis, whereas those with a higher proportion used screening questionnaires with divisions for disease severity.

Medication adherence

Medication adherence was most frequently measured subjectively by participant self-report (n=17) with tools such the 4-item or 8-item Morisky Medical Adherence Scale (MMAS, n=5), Medical Outcomes Study Specific Adherence Scale (n=1), Morisky Green Medication Adherence Questionnaire (n=1), Adherence to Refills and Medications Scale (n=1) or other questionnaire (n=10) (online supplemental table 1). 11 studies measured adherence objectively through either an electronic pill monitoring system (n=3)34 35 45 or prescription data (n=8) with the MPR or PDC.333742 47 Two studies combined subjective and objective measures,48 one of which assessed platelet thromboxane production as a surrogate for aspirin adherence.36 Most studies reported a dichotomous outcome (‘adherent’ vs ‘non-adherent’). The prevalence of medication non-adherence varied widely from 5.9% to 72.9%.49 50 This narrowed to 11.8–67% when limited to studies analysing prescription data.37 41 Seven studies reported high overall adherence (>80% of participants).3336 37 49 5153

Depression and medication adherence

21 studies found a significant relationship between depression and medication non-adherence. Among studies that reported a significant relationship, depression was associated with 1.07-fold to 3.7-fold odds of non-adherence.32 45 In two studies, this association was present with certain medication classes but not others.43 54 Two studies found that worsening depressive symptoms over time was associated with worsening adherence.45 55 Nine studies did not find a significant association.3436 38 44 46 50 5658

Meta-analysis

17 studies (n=82 059 patients) were included in the meta-analysis. The pooled estimates showed a significant association between depression and medication non-adherence (OR 1.48, 95% CI 1.23 to 1.78, p<0.001) using a random effects model. The corresponding odds resulting from a FEM was, however, insignificant (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.01, p=0.58). Interpretation of this association is limited by the considerable heterogeneity between studies (I2=91%, τ2=0.17, p<0.001) (figure 2). The overall effect was slightly lower when only adjusted data were considered (OR 1.39, 95% 1.15 to 1.67; online supplemental file 6, figure A6). Subgroup analysis showed a reduction in heterogeneity in subgroups stratified by CAD population, medication type, study type, depression measure and adherence measure. Subgroups with FEM and REM convergence and OR >1 included CHD subtype, antiplatelet and lipid lowering medications, prospective cohort studies, adherence measure with MPR or MMAS and depression measure with PHQ or unspecified (online supplemental file 5, figures A1–5).

Forest plot of studies reporting likelihood of medication non-adherence in depressed versus non-depressed individuals with coronary artery disease. ACEI, ACE inhibitor; APT, antiplatelet; ARB, angiotensin receptor blocker; BB, beta-blocker; BP, antihypertensive; LLD, lipid lowering drug.

Asymmetric funnel plot and Egger’s test with non-zero intercept indicated the presence of publication bias, with positive findings potentially driven by smaller studies40 45 51 and outliers59 60 (online supplemental file 6, figures A7–9). Post hoc sensitivity analysis with removal of the above studies did not yield convergence between FEM and REM. In alternate sensitivity testing which excluded the two most precise studies,38 50 convergence between FEM and REM was observed; however, publication bias remained (online supplemental file 6, figures A10 and A11).

Discussion

In this meta-analysis of 17 studies, individuals with concomitant depression and CAD had 1.48-fold odds of medication non-adherence compared with those without depression (95% CI 1.23 to 1.78) under a REM; however, this effect was not observed under the FEM. The median non-adherence rate was 28% (range 5.9–72.9%), and the prevalence of depression was 18% (1.7–76.6%). However, the interpretation of these results is limited due to considerable clinical and statistical heterogeneity and presence of publication bias. The heterogeneity is likely contributed to by the observational nature of the included studies, many of which relied on questionnaires and interviews and used a variety of assessment tools for depression and adherence.

These results expand on a previous meta-analysis by Crawshaw et al, which focused on the ACS population. The present study further includes chronic stable CAD and PCI populations, incorporating 23 additional relevant publications with a larger population (n=82 059 compared with n=5058).20 Four studies were included in both meta-analyses.45 54 56 60 Reasons for exclusion of the remaining three studies included inaccessible raw data for OR generation,61 data inconsistencies32 and population sample duplication.45 62 Whilst Crawshaw et al similarly found depression to increase odds of medication non-adherence (OR 2.00, 95% CI 1.57 to 3.33, p=0.015),20 the association is less strong in the present study.

The relationship between depression and medication non-adherence is likely multifactorial. Psychosomatic disturbances associated with depression (eg, reduced energy, concentration and impaired sleep)63 may lead to forgetfulness or diminish motivation to take medications or refill prescriptions.17 These factors may be exacerbated by social isolation and lack of self-efficacy.32 64 Depressed individuals may intentionally discontinue their medications due to lack of perceived benefit. Parallels could be drawn from cancer therapy, where belief in treatment efficacy has been shown to be an important determinant for adherence.65 Depressed individuals can experience a hopeless outlook63 which may impede motivation to prioritise behaviours that promote long-term health. Medication adherence post-ACS also seems to decline as time increases following the index event.66 Interestingly, subgroup analysis showed that depression was not associated with non-adherence to antihypertensives. The reason for this is unclear but may relate to falsely attributing somatic symptoms to elevated blood pressure,43 or avoiding other medications (eg, statins) due to the presence or fear of side effects.

Nine studies did not find a significant association between depression and medication non-adherence.3436 38 44 46 50 5658 Five of which were not included in the meta-analysis due to lack of accessible raw data, which could be a source of bias. Differing definitions of depression or adherence in these studies may contribute to a lack of association. Yeh et al used isolated ‘depressed mood’ rather than cumulative criteria,44 whereas Shemesh et al defined a SCL-90 score above the median as depressed, which likely led to over-representation.36 McGee et al and Park et al did not report validated adherence questionnaires, which may have led to measurement bias.50 56 Interestingly, Lissaker et al reported increased statin non-adherence in emotionally distressed participants but not in those with ICD-10 depression diagnoses.37 This discrepancy could reflect under-representation using ICD-10 criteria, as some symptomatically depressed patients may not have yet received a diagnosis. Alternatively, perhaps those with clinically diagnosed MDD are more likely to be receiving treatment, which may increase compliance with cardiovascular therapies.

These results highlight the importance of psychosocial considerations in CAD management. Cognitive–behavioural therapy (CBT) and/or selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors have been shown to be safe67 and effective at reducing depressive symptoms in patients with CAD.6870 Escitalopram has been shown to reduce risk of major adverse cardiovascular events and MI but not mortality.69 International guidelines recommend assessing for psychosocial risk factors as part of secondary prevention and commencing appropriate pharmacological or non-pharmacological therapy if present.71 72 While the availability of effective pharmacotherapy for CAD has increased over recent decades, the real-world benefits are limited if psychosocial barriers preventing adherence are not addressed. Studies directly evaluating whether treating depression improves adherence to cardiac medications are currently lacking. One randomised trial found an improvement in adherence to cardiovascular disease treatment and lifestyle advice after 12 weeks of internet-delivered CBT in depressed patients with high cardiovascular risk.73 Another study of hospitalised cardiac patients found better adherence following improvement in depressive symptoms at 6 months.74 It is recognised that other barriers exist to medical adherence such as cost and access, which may be more common in patients with depression and thus be a potential mediator of non-adherence. Further research is needed into implementing effective strategies to reduce depression in patients with CAD to optimise adherence and health outcomes. Systems level interventions could be further explored, such as the use of a standardised screening tool for depression symptoms post-ACS or as part of a cardiac risk factor assessment in the primary care setting, which may increase identification of high-risk individuals and allow for tailored intervention and/or follow-up.

Strengths and limitations

This meta-analysis is the most updated estimate of the impact of depression on medication non-adherence in CAD. While all studies received at least a ‘fair’ risk rating and included a large sample size from 28 countries, several limitations were identified and are discussed subsequently. The difference between FEM and REM observed here pertains to the underlying model assumptions and interpretation.75 While there exists confusion in the literature, FEM does not assume a common or homogenous effect if it is to be interpreted as a weighted average conditional on the included studies.76 While a REM interpretation is favoured from a heterogeneity point of view, as it is not conditioned on true underlying effect,77 generalisation beyond the included studies is hampered by the presence of publication bias observed here. While there is strong evidence for correlation between depression and medication adherence in a small number of large studies, the evidence remains inconclusive.

Study cohort, design, as well as class of medication, depression and adherence measures were found to be potential contributors to heterogeneity. The definition of depression was non-uniform across studies. The use of questionnaires could have been subjected to response bias, whereas ICD-9/10 codes did not assess depression symptoms and had potential for coding errors. Interparticipant variation in depression severity may have also contributed to heterogeneity, though it was not evaluated in this study. Adherence measures were inconsistent, with most studies using patient self-report which is subject to recall bias and may have underestimated non-adherence. Electronic pill monitoring and prescription-based methods were more objective but cannot confirm pill ingestion. Given the considerable variation, we would advocate for a consensus adherence definition to aid comparability in future research.

While the impact of statistical heterogeneity due to the above factors may be scrutinised via sensitivity analyses, the biases due to the quality and availability of data, as well as those inherent to the methodology of systematic reviews, may not be eliminated. Despite several studies attributing a ‘good’ risk rating, the cross-sectional and observational nature of most included studies limits causal inferences. Only published studies in English were included, which may introduce language bias, and raw data was not available for several studies which precluded their inclusion in the meta-analysis. While adjusted effect estimates were used where possible, variables such as symptom burden, dose complexity, social support, income and healthcare access were not adjusted consistently across studies. The inability to control for these potential confounders or coexistent psychiatric disorders such as anxiety or cognitive impairment is a limitation and potential source of bias. Finally, rounding errors could have occurred with inverse calculations of ORs but should not change the broad conclusions of this study. Given the above limitations, further research is needed to understand the causal relationship between depression and non-adherence, considering also variations due to age, gender, socioeconomic status and symptom burden.

Conclusion

Patients with CAD and depression may have increased odds of medication non-adherence, which may contribute to poorer outcomes. However, study quality was limited due to the observational nature of most studies and variations in measurement of depression and adherence. Further research with standardised depression and adherence measures may contribute to a better understanding of this relationship. Ongoing research into strategies that improve the detection and management of depression, and support adherence to cardioprotective drugs, is needed.
