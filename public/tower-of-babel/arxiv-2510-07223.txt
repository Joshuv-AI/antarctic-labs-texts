Multi-qubit Toffoli with exponentially fewer T T gates
David Gosset, Robin Kothari, Chenyi Zhang

Abstract
Prior work of Beverland et al. [ 2 ] has shown that any exact Clifford+ T T implementation of the n n -qubit Toffoli gate must use at least n n T T gates. Here we show how to get away with exponentially fewer T T gates, at the cost of incurring a tiny 1 / poly ⁡ ( n ) 1/\mathrm{poly}(n) error that can be neglected in most practical situations. More precisely, the n n -qubit Toffoli gate can be implemented to within error ϵ \epsilon in the diamond distance by a randomly chosen Clifford+ T T circuit with at most O ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) ) O(\log(1/\epsilon)) T T gates. We also give a matching Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) ) \Omega(\log(1/\epsilon)) lower bound that establishes optimality, and we show that any purely unitary implementation achieving even constant error must use Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Omega(n) T T gates. We also extend our sampling technique to implement other Boolean functions. Finally, we describe upper and lower bounds on the T T -count of Boolean functions in terms of non-adaptive parity decision tree complexity and its randomized analogue.

Introduction

Clifford circuits—that is, quantum computations that can be expressed as a sequence of single-qubit Hadamard, phase, and CNOT gates applied to a computational basis state—are efficiently classically simulable via the Gottesman-Knill theorem. They define an extraordinary classical limit of many-body quantum mechanics. In order to perform universal quantum computation, one requires non-Clifford resources, or
magic
. This can be in the form of a non-Clifford unitary or initial state. A natural choice is to augment the Cliffords with the single-qubit
T=\mathrm{diag}(1,e^{-i\pi/4})
gate. The resulting Clifford+
T
gate set is the canonical instruction set for fault-tolerant quantum computation in architectures based on the surface code, where Clifford gates can be performed fault-tolerantly directly while
T
gates are performed via magic state injection
[
6
]
or other more complex methods
[
17
]
.

Developing and optimizing techniques for compiling circuits over the Clifford+
T
gate set is a fundamental task that has the potential to reduce the resource costs of implementing quantum algorithms in fault tolerant architectures. For example, asymptotically optimal and ancilla-free single-qubit compilation techniques for Clifford+
T
due to Ross and Selinger
[
27
]
represent a significant practical improvement over the general methods provided by the Solovay-Kitaev theorem.

Here we consider the task of implementing a given target unitary using as few
T
gates as possible. The number of
T
gates required—its
T
-count
—is a measure of the magic possessed by the unitary. It determines the hardness of classically simulating the unitary via the so-called stabilizer rank based methods
[
8
,
4
]
. For few-qubit unitaries, where the size of the Clifford group is a reasonably small constant, it is also a proxy for the total gate count of an implementation. The
T
-count dominates the total cost of fault-tolerant implementations based on magic state distillation.

Implementing a

An even stronger model, which we do not use in any of our algorithms, is the model we call adaptive Clifford+

Multi-qubit Toffoli.

We use the power of mixed Clifford+
T
circuits to obtain dramatic improvements in
T

We first consider the
n
-qubit Toffoli gate:

\mathrm{Toff}_{n}|x\rangle|b\rangle=|x\rangle|b\oplus(x_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge x_{n-1})\rangle,\quad\textrm{for all}~x\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}~\textrm{and}~b\in\{0,1\},

(1)

which reversibly computes the
\and
of the first
n-1
bits into the last register. This gate, and gates that are Clifford-equivalent to it, is a central building block in quantum algorithms. Note that if we conjugate the last qubit by Hadamard, we obtain the
n
-qubit controlled
Z
gate, which acts as

C^{n-1}Z|x\rangle=(-1)^{x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{n}}|x\rangle.

(2)

By conjugating by a full layer of Hadamards, we can also get the diffusion operator in Grover’s algorithm, which reflects about the uniform superposition state.

It is well known that
\mathrm{Toff}_{n}
(or equivalently
C^{n-1}Z
) can be implemented exactly using only
O(n)

\mathrm{Toff}_{3}

[
1
]
, and each
\mathrm{Toff}_{3}

T

T

[
26
]

\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{unitary}}_{0}(\mathrm{Toff}_{n})=O(n).

(3)

On the other hand, Beverland, Campbell, Howard, and Kliuchnikov
[
2
, Proposition 4.1]
give a matching lower bound, even in the stronger adaptive model:

\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{adaptive}}_{0}(\mathrm{Toff}_{n})\geq n.

(4)

This seemingly closes the question (up to constant factors), since the upper and lower bounds match asymptotically.

The starting point of our work is the observation that the above lower bound only applies to the zero-error setting, whereas for practical applications, some error is always acceptable. In many cases, inverse polynomial error
\epsilon=1/\mathrm{poly}(n)
is more than enough. Surprisingly, we find that approximately implementing
\mathrm{Toff}_{n}
to within such an error budget is vastly cheaper than implementing it exactly. In particular,
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{mixed}}_{1/\poly(n)}(\mathrm{Toff}_{n})\leq\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{unitary}}_{0}(\mathrm{Toff}_{O(\log n)})=O(\log n)
. More generally we show the following.

Theorem 1
.

For any positive integer
n
and
\epsilon>0
, we have

\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{mixed}}_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{Toff}_{n})\leq\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{unitary}}_{0}(\mathrm{Toff}_{\lceil\log(1/\epsilon)\rceil+3})=O(\log(1/\epsilon)).

(5)

Thus the cost of
\epsilon
-approximating an
n
-qubit Toffoli with a mixed Clifford+
T
circuit is at most the cost of exactly implementing one small Toffoli on
\lceil\log(1/\epsilon)\rceil+3
qubits, which is independent of
n
!
Thus we get to replace a large Toffoli with a small Toffoli, and this is advantageous whenever
n\geq\lceil\log(1/\epsilon)\rceil+3
, which fails to hold only if
\epsilon
is exponentially small in

.

Theorem

The method we use to approximate
\mathrm{Toff}_{n}
is quite simple. As noted above,
\mathrm{Toff}_{n}
reversibly computes the
n-1
-bit

\and_{n-1}
. It will be slightly more convenient to consider the Clifford-equivalent gate

X^{\otimes n-1}\mathrm{Toff}_{n}X^{\otimes n}
which reversibly computes the
\mathsf{OR}
function on
n-1

\mathsf{OR}_{n-1}

We now show how to approximate the
\mathsf{OR}_{n}
gate using parity functions of the form
\mathsf{XOR}_{S}(x)=\bigoplus_{i\in S}x_{i}
, which are Clifford gates, and one small
\mathsf{OR}

First, we observe two facts:

•

If
\mathsf{OR}_{n}(x)=0
then
\mathsf{XOR}_{S}(x)=0
for all
S\subseteq[n]
.

•

If
\mathsf{OR}_{n}(x)=1
then
\mathsf{XOR}_{S}(x)=0
for exactly half the subsets
S\subseteq[n]
.

So if we pick a random
S\subseteq[n]
,

In fact, this is a

Now we only need to boost the success probability to

To make this an approximation with error

, we sample
k

S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\subseteq[n]
. Now if
\mathsf{OR}_{n}(x)=0
, then
\mathsf{XOR}_{S_{i}}(x)=0
for all of these subsets. On the other hand, if
\mathsf{OR}_{n}(x)=1
, then the probability that
all

k

is
1/2^{k}
.
So if we define
g_{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}}(x)=\mathsf{OR}_{k}(\mathsf{XOR}_{S_{1}}(x),\mathsf{XOR}_{S_{2}}(x),\ldots,\mathsf{XOR}_{S_{k}}(x))
, then for any
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}

\Pr_{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}}[\mathsf{OR}_{n}(x)\neq g_{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}}(x)]\leq 1/2^{k}.

(6)

Choosing

ensures that the overall error is at most
\epsilon
.

To move from approximating a Boolean function to approximating a unitary (
\mathrm{Toff}_{n}
), we need some notation. Throughout this paper, for any Boolean function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
, let
U_{f}
be the unitary that reversibly computes
f
as follows:

U_{f}|x\rangle|b\rangle=|x\rangle|b\oplus f(x)\rangle\quad\text{for all}~~x\in\{0,1\}^{n}~~\text{and}~~b\in\{0,1\}.

(7)

Now
\mathrm{Toff}_{n}=U_{\and_{n-1}}
and
(X^{\otimes n}\otimes I)\mathrm{Toff}_{n+1}X^{\otimes n+1}=U_{\mathsf{OR}_{n}}
.
U_{\mathsf{OR}_{n}}
is now
\epsilon
-approximated by the mixed unitary circuit that first picks
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
uniformly at random and then applies the unitary
U_{g}
corresponding to
g_{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}}(x)
. Note that the only non-Clifford gate here is the
\mathsf{OR}_{k}
gate, which is implemented using one
\mathrm{Toff}_{k+1}
gate.

We highlight a few interesting features of this approximation: Observe that the Toffoli gate is approximated by a distribution over gates of the form
U_{g}
, but each of these gates individually is perfectly distinguishable from the Toffoli gate (i.e., they are distance
1
from the Toffoli gate). So although none of the gates in the distribution is close to the Toffoli gate, the mixture is
\epsilon
-close to it. This phenomenon is unique to implementing unitaries and does not occur with state preparation. If a distribution over states is
\epsilon
-close (in trace distance) to a pure state, then at least one of the states in the support of the distribution is also
O(\sqrt{\epsilon})
-close (in trace distance) to the pure state.

Another consequence of this approximation is that the Clifford hierarchy is very non-robust to error. The
\mathrm{Toff}_{n}
gate is in level
n+1
of the Clifford hierarchy
[
10
]
, but we show that it can be
\epsilon
-approximated by gates in level
O(\log(1/\eps))
of the Clifford hierarchy.

Lastly, our result may have applications to learning and classical simulation algorithms that work when the
T
-count is low, since we now show that even circuits with large Toffoli gates do effectively have low
T
-count.

Optimality.

Some natural questions arise about the optimality of our upper bound. First, one might ask if the mixed Clifford+
T
model is necessary at all to achieve this result. Could it be possible that even
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{unitary}}_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{Toff}_{n})
is small? The lower bound of
[
2
]
only says that achieving
\epsilon=0
requires large
T
count. Our first lower bound establishes that unitary circuits approximating
\mathrm{Toff}_{n}
must use
\Omega(n)
gates:

Theorem 2
.

For any
\epsilon\in[0,1/2)
and large enough
n
, we have

\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{unitary}}_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{Toff}_{n})\geq n-2.

(8)

Another natural question is whether one can do better than the upper bound in
Theorem

1
. We provide a matching lower bound, using a generalization of the stabilizer nullity technique of Ref.
[
2
]
, showing this is impossible even in the more powerful adaptive Clifford+
T
model.

Theorem 3
.

For large enough
n
and
1/\epsilon
, we have

\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{mixed}}_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{Toff}_{n})\geq\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{adaptive}}_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{Toff}_{n})=\Omega(\min\{n,\log(1/\epsilon)\}).

(9)

Generalization.

Theorem

1
shows how to implement
\mathrm{Toff}_{n}=U_{\and_{n-1}}
or its Clifford-equivalent
U_{\mathsf{OR}_{n-1}}
efficiently with low
T
-count. We give an explanation for this in terms of Fourier expansion of the associated Boolean function. Recall the Boolean Fourier expansion of
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
as a linear combination of parities:

f(x)=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(S)\chi_{S}(x),

(10)

where
\chi_{S}(x)=(-1)^{\mathsf{XOR}_{S}(x)}
. Let us define the Fourier 1-norm of a function
f
as
\|\widehat{f}\|_{1}\equiv\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}|\widehat{f}(S)|
.
The reason we were able to approximate
\mathrm{Toff}_{n}
has to do with the
\mathsf{OR}
function having small Fourier 1-norm since

\mathsf{OR}_{n}(x)=1-\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\chi_{S}(x),

(11)

and therefore
\|\widehat{\mathsf{OR}}_{n}\|_{1}\leq 2
. We generalize
Theorem

1
to all functions with small Fourier 1-norm.

Theorem 4
.

Let
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
and
\epsilon>0
be given. Then

\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{mixed}}_{\epsilon}(U_{f})=O(\|\widehat{f}\|_{1}^{2}\log(1/\epsilon)).

(12)

Theorem

4
has the advantage that the Fourier 1-norm is relatively easy to work with—in particular, we can analytically understand its scaling with
n
for most functions of interest. However we do not expect that it tightly characterizes the
T
-count of mixed Clifford+
T
circuits.

The algorithm to establish
Theorem

4
is also simple.
We can see that
Equation

10
is proportional to a (signed) average of parity functions with respect to a probability distribution
p(S)\equiv|\widehat{f}(S)|/\|\widehat{f}\|_{1}
. In order to approximate
U_{f}
, we sample
k
sets
S
from this distribution:
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{k}
. Next we compute the sample mean

\tilde{g}(x)=\frac{\|\widehat{f}\|_{1}}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{sign}(\widehat{f}(S))\chi_{S_{i}}(x),

(13)

and we define a Boolean function
g(x)
which is
1
iff
\tilde{g}(x)\geq 1/2
. We then reversibly compute
g
. In
Section

4
we show that
k=O(\|\widehat{f}\|_{1}^{2}\log(1/\epsilon))
suffices to approximate
U_{f}
to within error
\epsilon
, and we show how to implement this procedure with
O(k)

T
gates.

Parity decision trees.

As our final structural result, we show how to upper and lower bound the
T
-count of Boolean functions computed by unitary and mixed circuits using non-adaptive parity decision tree complexity and its randomized analogue.

A function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
has non-adaptive parity decision tree complexity at most
k

\mathrm{PDT^{na}}(f)\leq k

k

S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\subseteq[n]

f(x)=g(\mathsf{XOR}_{S_{1}}(x),\ldots,\mathsf{XOR}_{S_{k}}(x))
for an arbitrary fixed function
g:\{0,1\}^{k}\to\{0,1\}
. The non-adaptive randomized parity decision tree complexity of
f

\mathrm{RPDT}^{\mathrm{na}}_{\epsilon}(f)
, is defined analogously by taking a probability distribution over non-adaptive parity decision trees, such that for any input
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
the output is correct with probability at least
1-\epsilon
.
5

We also define the (non-standard) measure

To define our models precisely, we need to discuss some distance measures on quantum states, unitaries, and channels.

For any two mixed states
\rho
and
\sigma
, let the trace distance between them be denoted by
D(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}
, where
\|A\|_{1}=\Tr(\sqrt{A^\dagger A})
. For ease of notation, we also use
D(\ket{\psi},\ket{\phi})
to mean
D(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi},\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}).

The trace distance has an operational interpretation: By the Holevo–Helstrom theorem
[
29
, Theorem 3.4]
, the maximum success probability of distinguishing the two states given one copy is
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}D(\rho,\sigma)
.
In particular, two states have trace distance
0
if they are identical and trace distance
1
if they are orthogonal (and hence perfectly distinguishable).

For any two mixed states
\rho
and
\sigma
, let the fidelity between them be denoted by
F(\rho,\sigma)=\big(\|\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}\|_{1}\big)^{2}
.
When one of the states is pure, we get the simpler formula
F(\rho,\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})=\braket{\psi|\rho|\psi}
.
When the trace distance between two states is close to
0
the fidelity is close to
1
, and vice versa. This is quantified by the Fuchs-van de Graaf inequalities
[
12
]
:

1-\sqrt{F(\rho,\sigma)}\leq D(\rho,\sigma)\leq\sqrt{1-F(\rho,\sigma)}.

(17)

For quantum channels or unitaries, the distance measure analogous to trace distance is the diamond distance.

Definition 7

(Diamond distance)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
be quantum channels which map
n
-qubit states to
n
-qubit states. Let
I_{\ell}
denote the identity channel on a Hilbert space of
\ell
qubits. Then

D_{\diamond}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})=\mathrm{sup}\left\{D\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes I_{\ell}(\rho),\mathcal{E}_{2}\otimes I_{\ell}(\rho)\bigr):\ell<\infty\right\}

(18)

where the supremum is over
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and density matrices
\rho
on
n+\ell
qubits.

A consequence of this definition is that if two channels are
\epsilon
-close in diamond distance and both act on the same state
\rho
, then their output states are also
\epsilon
-close in trace distance.

We often encounter the diamond distance between a quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
and a unitary channel
\Phi_{U}(\rho)=U\rho U^{\dagger}
, where
U
is an
n
-qubit unitary. In this situation for ease of notation we write

D_{\diamond}(\mathcal{E},U)\equiv D_{\diamond}(\mathcal{E},\Phi_{U}).

(19)

We will also use the fact that the supremum in
Equation

18
is achieved by a pure state.

Fact 8

(
[
28
]
, Lemma 2.4)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}
be quantum channels which map
n
-qubit states to
n
-qubit states. Then

D_{\diamond}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})=\mathrm{sup}\left\{D\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes I_{\ell}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|),\mathcal{E}_{2}\otimes I_{\ell}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)\bigr):\ell<\infty\right\}

(20)

where the supremum is over
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
n+\ell
-qubit pure states
|\psi\rangle
.

It is also known that the supremum in
Equations

18
and
20
is achieved by a finite value of
\ell
but we will not need this fact.

We now define the three models of Clifford+
T
circuits discussed in the introduction. A unitary Clifford+
T
circuit is the standard circuit that comes to mind when thinking of a Clifford+
T
circuit.

Definition 9

(Unitary Clifford+
T
circuit)
.

Let
V
be a quantum circuit composed of Clifford gates and
T
gates that acts on an
n
-qubit input state
|\psi\rangle
along with some ancilla qubits initialized in the all-zeros state. The
\epsilon
-approximate unitary
T
-count of
U
, denoted

\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{unitary}}_{\epsilon}(U)

is the minimum number of
T
gates in any such circuit
V
that satisfies
D_{\diamond}(\Tr_{\mathrm{anc}}[\Phi_{V}],U)\leq\epsilon
, where
\mathrm{anc}
denotes the ancilla register.

As discussed, if two channels are
\epsilon
-close in diamond distance, then replacing one by the other in a quantum circuit at most changes the output state by at most
\epsilon
in trace distance.
In particular, this means if a unitary
U
is used
k
times in a quantum circuit and is replaced by a channel

that is
\epsilon/k
-close in diamond distance, then the output state of the resulting quantum state is at most
\epsilon
close to the original output state in trace distance.

We now define mixed Clifford+
T
circuits, which is the model in which we establish all our algorithmic results.

Definition 10

(Mixed Clifford+
T
circuit)
.

Consider a probability distribution
\{p_{i}\}_{i}
over unitary Clifford+
T
circuits
V_{i}
each of which act on an
n
-qubit input state along with an ancilla register consisting of
a
qubits initialized in the all-zeros state. Let

T

n
-qubit quantum channel

. The
\epsilon
-approximate mixed unitary
T
-count of
U
, denoted

\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{mixed}}_{\epsilon}(U)

is the minimum
k
of any such channel satisfying

.

As discussed, implementing a mixed Clifford+
T
circuit on a quantum computer requires no additional quantum hardware from the quantum computer since the probabilistic sampling can be done by the classical compiler.

The last and most powerful model is the adaptive Clifford+
T
circuit. These circuits are also sometimes called “circuits with measurement and classical feed-forward” in the literature.

Definition 11

(Adaptive Clifford+
T
circuit)
.

Consider a circuit that begins with an input state
|\psi\rangle
as well as some ancilla qubits initialized in the all-zeros state, then applies a sequence of gates and single-qubit measurements in the computational basis. Each of the gates is either a
T

T

that maps
n
-qubit states to
n
-qubit states. Let

denote the expected number of
T

T

|\psi\rangle
, and let

. The
\epsilon
-approximate adaptive
T
-count of
U
, denoted

\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{adaptive}}_{\epsilon}(U)

is the minimum
k
of any such channel satisfying
D_{\diamond}(\mathcal{E},U)\leq\epsilon
.

We do not use the power of this adaptive Clifford+
T

[
15

for a more detailed discussion of this model.

3.2
Lower bound

We now prove this algorithm is optimal (up to constants). As in Beverland et al.
[
2
]
, we establish this using the stabilizer nullity proof technique.
The stabilizer nullity is a function
\nu(\cdot)
defined on all
n
-qubit quantum states as follows:

\nu(\sigma)=n-\log\left(\left|\{P\in\{\pm 1\}\cdot\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{n}:P\sigma=\sigma\}\right|\right).

(35)

The stabilizer nullity is one way to quantify magic for quantum states; it has the following properties:

1.

\nu(|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|)\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
, with
\nu(|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|)=0
if and only if
|\phi\rangle
is a stabilizer state.

2.

\nu(C\rho C^{\dagger})=\nu(\rho)
whenever
C
is a Clifford unitary.

3.

\nu(T_{j}\rho T_{j}^{\dagger})\leq\nu(\rho)+1
where
T_{j}
is the single-qubit
T
gate acting on qubit
j\in[n]
.

4.

\nu(\rho^{\prime})\leq\nu(\rho)
, where

\rho^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Tr}(\rho(I+P)/2)}\left(\frac{I+P}{2}\right)\rho\left(\frac{I+P}{2}\right),\qquad\textbf{(Pauli postselection)}

(36)

is the state obtained by measuring a Pauli
P
and postselecting on the
+1
outcome (assuming this state is well defined, i.e.,
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho(I+P)/2)\neq 0
).

5.

\nu(\rho\otimes\sigma)=\nu(\rho)+\nu(\sigma)

Properties 1, 2, and 5 follow straightforwardly from the definition, see
[
2
, Proposition 2.3]
for a proof of property 4.
6
6

6

Although Beverland et al. only state this Proposition for pure states (as they only define stabilizer nullity for pure states), the proof of Prop 2.3 given in Ref.
[
2
]
extends straightforwardly to mixed states.
For property 3, note that the single qubit magic state
|T\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle+e^{-i\pi/4}|1\rangle)
has nullity

\nu(|T\rangle\langle T|)=1,

(37)

and that we can implement a
T
gate by adjoining a magic state (increasing nullity by
1
) and then performing a sequence of nullity non-increasing operations:

2|0\rangle\langle 0|_{B}\mathrm{CNOT}_{jB}\left(\rho\otimes|T\rangle\langle T|_{B}\right)\mathrm{CNOT}_{jB}|0\rangle\langle 0|_{B}=T_{j}\rho T_{j}^{\dagger}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|_{B}.

(38)

Using property 5, we see that the nullity of the RHS, which is at most
\nu(\rho)+1
is equal to that of
\nu(T_{j}\rho T_{j}^{\dagger})
. We note that this argument generalizes (replacing
\pi/4\leftarrow\theta
everywhere) to show that stabilizer nullity can only increase by at most one if we apply any single-qubit diagonal unitary
D=\mathrm{diag}(1,e^{i\theta})
.

We first establish that all states in a ball of radius

Proof.

By directly computing all Pauli expected values in the state

\max_{P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}:P\neq I}|\langle\Phi|P|\Phi\rangle|=1-\frac{4}{2^{n}}.

Toward a contradiction, assume
\nu(\omega)<n

\omega

Randomized parity decision trees

In this section, we present lower bounds on the

Parity decision trees were first introduced by Kushilevitz and Mansour
[

, generalizing standard decision trees.
Given access to an

Definition 18

(Non-adaptive parity decision tree)
.

A non-adaptive (deterministic) parity decision tree with depth

is a fixed collection of subsets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\subseteq[n]
together with a deterministic function

.

The non-adaptive parity decision tree complexity of a Boolean function
f
, denoted
\mathrm{PDT}^{\mathrm{na}}(f)
, is the minimum depth among all parity decision trees that compute
f
correctly on every input.

Definition

18
extends to the randomized setting in the standard way, by allowing a probability distribution over parity decision trees.

Definition 19

(Non-adaptive randomized parity decision tree)

A non-adaptive randomized parity decision tree with depth
k
is a probability distribution over non-adaptive parity decision trees of depth at most
k
, and its output on an input

is the distribution on
\{0,1\}
obtained by sampling a deterministic parity decision tree from this distribution and computing its output on

For any Boolean function

and
\epsilon\geq 0
, the non-adaptive randomized parity decision tree complexity
\mathrm{RPDT}^{\mathrm{na}}_{\epsilon}(f)
is defined as the minimum depth of a randomized parity decision tree that outputs

with probability at least

over Boolean functions

such that for all

\Pr[f(x)\neq g_{i}(x)]\leq\epsilon~~\text{for all}~~x\in\{0,1\}^{n}.

(52)

\mathrm{gate}\mathrm{RPDT}^{\mathrm{na}}_{\epsilon}(f)
, is the minimum gate complexity of a randomized parity decision tree that outputs
f(x)
with probability at least
1-\epsilon
for all
x
.

We now show that
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{unitary}}_{0}(U_{f})
and
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{mixed}}_{\epsilon}(U_{f})
are upper bounded by
O(\mathrm{gate}\mathrm{PDT^{na}}(f))
and
O(\mathrm{gate}\mathrm{RPDT}^{\mathrm{na}}(f))
, respectively.

Theorem 25

(Part 3 of
Theorem

5
)
.

For any Boolean function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{unitary}}_{0}(U_{f})=O(\mathrm{gate}\mathrm{PDT^{na}}(f)),\qquad\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{mixed}}_{\epsilon}(U_{f})=O(\mathrm{gate}\mathrm{RPDT}^{\mathrm{na}}_{\epsilon}(f)).

(73)

Proof.

Note that any non-adaptive parity decision tree
g(\mathsf{XOR}_{S_{1}}(x),\ldots,\mathsf{XOR}_{S_{k}}(x))
can be implemented by a unitary Clifford+
T
circuit using
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{unitary}}_{0}(g)\leq O(\mathrm{gate}\mathrm{PDT^{na}}(f))

T
gates by
Definition

24
, since each 2-input
\and
,
\mathsf{OR}
, and
\mathsf{NOT}
gate can be implemented using
O(1)

T
gates exactly. Similarly, any randomized non-adaptive parity decision tree can be simulated by a mixed Clifford+
T
circuit using
O(\mathrm{gate}\mathrm{RPDT}^{\mathrm{na}}_{\epsilon}(f))

T
gates, whose output probability distribution is the same as the original RPDT, and thus is correct on any input with probability at least
1-\epsilon
.
∎

5.4
The adaptive case

In this subsection, we prove
Theorem

6
, restated below:
See
6

The proof is based on the connection between adaptive Clifford+
T
circuits and Clifford circuits with Pauli postselections which was used in
[
2
]
and extended in
[
15
]
.

Recall the notion of Pauli postselection from
Equation

36
. Note that Pauli postselection is a nonlinear operation due to the normalizing factor in
Equation

36
. Below we shall consider Clifford circuits which may include Pauli postselection and we write
C(|\psi\rangle)
for the output state of such an operation
C
acting on input state
|\psi\rangle
.

The following result gives a canonical form for Clifford circuit with Pauli postselections.

Lemma 26

(Theorem A.2 of
[
15
]
)
.

Let
C^{\post}
be a Clifford circuit with
m
Pauli postselections,
n
input qubits, and
a
ancillas.
Let
\{\ket{\phi_{\lambda}}\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}}
and
\{\ket{\psi_{\lambda}}\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}}
be two sets of
n
-qubit states indexed by
\mathcal{S}
.
Assume

\ket{\psi_{\lambda}}\ket{0^{t+a}}=C^{\post}\bigl(\ket{\phi_{\lambda}}\ket{T}^{\otimes t}\ket{0^{a}}\bigr)\quad\text{holds for all }\lambda\in\mathcal{S},

(74)

Then there exists an
(n+t)
-qubit Clifford unitary
C
and matrices
M_{1},M_{2},\ldots,M_{c}
such that

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{\lambda}}\ket{0^{t}}\propto CM_{c}\cdots M_{2}M_{1}\bigl(\ket{\phi_{\lambda}}\ket{T}^{\otimes t}\bigr)\quad\text{holds for all }\lambda\in\mathcal{S},

(75)

where each
M_{j}
is
I+P_{j}
for some
(n+t)
-qubit Hermitian Pauli
P_{j}
, and

\displaystyle c=t-\log\bigl(\lvert\mathsf{Stab}(\{\ket{\phi_{\lambda}}\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}})\rvert\bigr)+\log\bigl(\lvert\mathsf{Stab}(\{\ket{\psi_{\lambda}}\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}})\rvert\bigr),

(76)

where
\mathsf{Stab}(\{\ket{\phi_{\lambda}}\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}})
and
\mathsf{Stab}(\{\ket{\psi_{\lambda}}\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}})
are the stabilizer groups of
\{\ket{\phi_{\lambda}}\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}}
and
\ket{\psi_{\lambda}}\}_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}}
, respectively.

In the above, the stabilizer group of a
set
of states
\{|v_{i}\rangle\}_{i}
consists of all Pauli operators
P
such that
P|v_{i}\rangle=|v_{i}\rangle
for all
i
.

Proof of
Theorem

6
.

Let
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be given. Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is an adaptive Clifford+
T
circuit that (exactly) implements the unitary
U_{f}
using
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{adaptive}}_{0}(U_{f})

T
gates in expectation (on the worst-case input state). Let
|\Phi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)
. If we use the
2n+1
qubit input state
|\Phi\rangle^{\otimes n}|0\rangle
then we get an adaptive Clifford+
T
circuit that prepares the state

|F\rangle\equiv(U_{f}\otimes I)|\Phi\rangle^{\otimes n}|0\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|y\rangle|y\rangle|f(y)\rangle,

(77)

where on the RHS we have grouped the qubits so that the first two
n
-qubit registers each contain one qubit from each Bell pair
|\Phi\rangle^{\otimes n}
. Moreover, this adaptive Clifford+
T
circuit uses an expected number of
T
gates
t\leq\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{adaptive}}_{0}(U_{f})
. Since
|\Phi\rangle
is a stabilizer state we can prepend a Clifford to the circuit so that it acts on the all-zeros input state
|0^{2n+1}\rangle
.

Since the adaptive Clifford circuit prepares
|F\rangle
with zero error starting from
|0^{2n+1}\rangle
, any fixed sequence of measurement outcomes, Clifford gates, and
T
gates that occurs with nonzero probability must give rise to a final state equal to
|F\rangle
. Let us choose a sequence of measurement outcomes and unitary gates that occurs with nonzero probability and uses the minimum number of
T
gates. This minimum number is at most the expected number
t
used by the adaptive Clifford+
T
circuit. Note that in order to postselect on measuring qubit
i
in the state
|z\rangle
(for
z\in\{0,1\}
) we can use Pauli postselection with
P=(-1)^{z}Z
. Moreover, we can implement each
T
gate by adjoining a magic state
|T\rangle
and applying Clifford gates and Pauli postselection (see, e.g.,
Equation

38
). From this we infer a circuit
C^{\mathrm{post}}
composed of Pauli postselections and Clifford gates such that

C^{\mathrm{post}}(|0^{2n+1}\rangle|T\rangle^{\otimes t}|0^{a}\rangle)=|F\rangle|0^{t+a}\rangle.

(78)

Here
a
is the number of ancillas used, which is some positive integer. Note that for any
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
we have

(\langle\Phi|^{\otimes n}\otimes I)|x\rangle|F\rangle=2^{-n}|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle,

(79)

where the Bell pairs act on qubits
i
and
n+i
(for each
1\leq i\leq n
). Using
Equations

78
and
79
we infer that there is a circuit
D^{\mathrm{post}}
composed of Pauli postselections and Clifford gates such that

D^{\mathrm{post}}(|x\rangle|0^{2n+1}\rangle|T\rangle^{\otimes t}|0^{a}\rangle)=|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle|0^{t+a+2n}\rangle.

(80)

Here we used Pauli postselection onto the
+1
eigenspace of
X\otimes X
followed by Pauli postselection onto the
+1
eigenspace of
Z\otimes Z
to implement the projector onto each two-qubit state
|\Phi\rangle
appearing in
Equation

79
. Then we apply a Clifford which maps
n
copies of this state to
|0^{2n}\rangle
.

Now let us partition the input and output registers of
Equation

80
so that we can use
Lemma

26
. In particular we consider the set of
n+1
-qubit input states

|\phi_{x}\rangle=|x\rangle|0\rangle\quad x\in\{0,1\}^{n}

(81)

and corresponding output states

|\psi_{x}\rangle=|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle\quad x\in\{0,1\}^{n}.

(82)

The stabilizer group of
\{|\phi_{x}\rangle\}_{x}
has two elements consisting of
I^{\otimes n}\otimes Z
and the identity. The stabilizer group of
\{|\psi_{x}\rangle\}_{x}
depends on the function
f
but (a) contains only
Z
-type Pauli operators and (b) other than
I^{\otimes n+1}
, does not contain any operators that act as the identity on the last qubit. From these two properties we infer that the stabilizer group of
\{|\psi_{x}\rangle\}_{x}
contains at most two elements. Therefore

-\log\bigl(\lvert\mathsf{Stab}(\{\ket{\phi_{x}}\}_{x})\rvert\bigr)+\log\bigl(\lvert\mathsf{Stab}(\{\ket{\psi_{x}}\}_{x})\rvert\bigr)\leq 0.

(83)

Applying
Lemma

26
we infer that there is a an
(n+1+t)
-qubit Clifford unitary
C
and
n+1
-qubit Paulis
P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{c}
such that

|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle|0^{t}\rangle\propto C\prod_{j=1}^{c}\left(I+P_{j}\right)|x\rangle|0\rangle|T\rangle^{\otimes t},

(84)

where
c\leq t
due to
Equation

83
. Now consider the function

g(x)\equiv\langle x|\langle 0|\langle T|^{\otimes t}\prod_{j=1}^{c}(I+P_{j})C^{\dagger}\left(I-Z_{n+1}\right)C\prod_{j=1}^{c}(I+P_{j})|x\rangle|0\rangle|T\rangle^{\otimes t}.

(85)

From
Equation

84
we see that

\displaystyle g(x)\propto\langle x|x\rangle\cdot\langle f(x)|(I-Z)|f(x)\rangle\cdot\langle 0^{t}|0^{t}\rangle

(86)

and therefore
g(x)>0
if and only if
f(x)=1
. To complete the proof we show that there is a non-adaptive PDT which on input
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
outputs
1
if and only if
g(x)>0
. First write
P_{0}\equiv C^{\dagger}Z_{n+1}C
and let

\mathcal{W}=\langle P_{0},P_{1},\ldots,P_{c}\rangle,

(87)

and
|\phi_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle\equiv|0\rangle|T\rangle^{\otimes t}
.
Then

g(x)=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{W}}\gamma_{P}\langle x|\langle\phi_{\mathrm{in}}|P|x\rangle|\phi_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle.

(88)

for some coefficients
\gamma_{P}\in\mathbb{C}
that can be inferred from
Equation

85
. Comparing
Equations

87
and
88
with
Equations

60
and
64
we see that we have arrived at an expression for
g(x)
which is identical to
Equation

64
but with
k
replaced by
c
. We then follow the proof of
Lemma

22
to conclude that there exist sets
S_{0},S_{1},\ldots,S_{c}\subseteq[n]
and a polynomial
h:\{0,1\}^{c+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle g(x)=h(\mathsf{XOR}_{S_{0}}(x),\mathsf{XOR}_{S_{1}}(x),\ldots,\mathsf{XOR}_{S_{c}}(x)),

(89)

and therefore there is a non-adaptive PDT of size
c+1\leq t+1\leq\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{adaptive}}_{0}(U_{f})+1
that decides if
g(x)>0
(equivalently,
f(x)=1
).
∎

Examples

We now justify the bounds in
Table

1
, starting with the upper bounds. We establish upper bounds of
O(1)
for constant
\epsilon
, which can be boosted to
O(\log(1/\eps))
for any
\epsilon>0
as in
Theorem

4
. Our upper bounds are either direct reductions to
\mathsf{OR}
or use the upper bound of
\mathrm{gate}\mathrm{RPDT}^{\mathrm{na}}
from
Theorem

5
.

\mathsf{OR}_{n}(x)
: This is
Theorem

1
, since
U_{\mathsf{OR}_{n}}
is Clifford-equivalent to
\mathrm{Toff}_{n+1}
.

\mathsf{HW}_{n}^{d}(x)
: We divide the input into
4d^{2}
sets of equal size, and use the fact that
\leq d+1
balls thrown into
4d^{2}
buckets will most likely not have 2 balls in the same bucket
[
19
, Fact 1]
. Thus we can simply count how many sets have any
1
s in them, which is an
\mathsf{OR}
, and accept if this is larger than
d
. For constant
d
, this has constant success probability. For better
d
-dependence, see the protocols of
[
30
, Theorem 2]
and
[
19
, Theorem 1.5]
.

\mathsf{HW}_{n}^{k,2k}(x)
: Pick a random subset of the input bits with each bit chosen with probability
1/(2k)
and compute its
\mathsf{OR}
. This is a constant success probability protocol for
\mathsf{HW}_{n}^{k,2k}
. Alternatively, the protocol in
[
19
, Theorem 1.5]
also works for non-adaptive RPDTs.

\mathsf{CW}_{n}^{C}(x)
: Using the parity check matrix definition of a linear code, checking membership in a code
C
is a single
\mathsf{OR}
of many parities.

\mathsf{MEQ}_{n,m}(M)
: This is equivalent to checking if the bitwise
\mathsf{XOR}
of row
i
and row
i+1
is all zeros for all
i\in[n-1]
. This is a single
\mathsf{OR}
of
m(n-1)
two-bit
\mathsf{XOR}
s.

\mathsf{RankOne}_{n,m}(M)
: A non-adaptive RPDT upper bound of
4
is given in
[
13
, Theorem 3]
, which is easily seen to have gate complexity
O(1)
, since it is a computation on
4
bits.

For the lower bounds, we use the
\mathrm{RPDT}^{\mathrm{na}}
lower bound from
Theorem

5
. Since
\mathrm{RPDT}^{\mathrm{na}}
complexity is not as well studied, we use lower bounds from communication complexity. For any Boolean function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
,
the one-way communication complexity with shared randomness
R_{\epsilon}^{\to}(f^{\mathsf{XOR}})
of its associated XOR function
f^{\mathsf{XOR}}(x,y)=f(x\oplus y)
is at most
the non-adaptive randomized parity decision tree complexity
\mathrm{RPDT}^{\mathrm{na}}_{\epsilon}(f)
for every
\epsilon
(see e.g.,
[

, Page 2]
). The following lower bounds hold even for constant

\mathsf{GT}_{n}(x,y)
:
R_{1/3}^{\to}\big(\mathsf{GT}^{\mathsf{XOR}}\big)=\Omega(n)
as shown in
[
24

\mathsf{ADD}_{n}(x,y)
: This is even harder than
\mathsf{GT}_{n}(x,y)
, because if we add (using
\mathsf{ADD}_{n+1}

n+1

and

n+1

\mathsf{MAJ}_{n}(x)

\mathsf{XOR}
function is to decide if the Hamming distance between Alice and Bob’s strings is greater than
n/2
. This famously needs
\Omega(n)
randomized communication, even with two-way communication, even if promised that the Hamming weight is either
<n/2-\sqrt{n}
or
>n/2+\sqrt{n}

[
11
]
.

Our final lower bound is slightly more involved than the ones above.

Theorem 27
.

For large enough
n
, we have
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{mixed}}_{1/3}(\mathsf{INC}_{n})=\Omega(n)
.

Proof.

We prove the lower bound via a reduction from the two-party communication problem
Augmented Index
. In this problem, Alice gets an
n
-bit string
x
, and Bob gets an index
i\in[n]
as well as the partial string
x_{i+1}x_{i+2}....x_{n}
. Bob’s goal is to output
x_{i}
, where the communication is restricted to be one-way from Alice to Bob.

We design a one-way randomized protocol for
\mathsf{AugIndex}_{n}
whose communication cost is at most
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{mixed}}_{1/3}(\mathsf{INC}_{2n})+1
. Alice takes
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and forms a new string
X\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
by replacing each
0
with
01
and each
1
with
10
. Similarly, Bob forms a new string
Y\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
by setting the last
2(n-i)
bits in the same way as Alice and setting the first
2i
bits to be 0. Let
Z=X\oplus Y
. Then the last
2(n-i)
bits of
Z
are
0
since Alice and Bob agree on
x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n}
, and the first
2i
bits of
Z
coincide with those of
X
. We use
k=\max\{i:Z_{i}=1\}
to denote the index of this right-most 1. Then,
k
is even iff
x_{i}=0
and is odd iff
x_{i}=1
. Next, consider
\neg Z
, the bitwise complement of
Z
. The first
k-1
bits of
\neg Z
and
\mathsf{INC}_{2n}(\neg Z)
coincide, while their last
2n-k+1
bits are
01^{2n-k}
and
10^{2n-k}
, respectively. Hence

\displaystyle\neg Z\oplus\mathsf{INC}_{2n}(\neg Z)=0^{k-1}1^{2n-k+1},

(90)

where we use
\neg y
denotes the bitwise complement of any bit string
y
. Consequently,

\displaystyle x_{i}=\neg\mathsf{XOR}(\neg Z\oplus\mathsf{INC}_{2n}(\neg Z)).

(91)

Thus, there exists a mixed Clifford+
T
circuit with
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{mixed}}_{1/3}(\mathsf{INC}_{2n})

T
gates that, acting on Alice’s and Bob’s inputs, more specifically the
2n
qubit register that encodes
\ket{x_{1}\ldots x_{n}}\ket{0^{i}x_{i+1}\ldots x_{n}}
, outputs
x_{i}
with success probability at least
2/3
. By
Theorem

23
, this implies

\displaystyle\mathrm{RPDT}^{\mathrm{na}}_{1/3}(\mathsf{AugIndex}_{n})\leq\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{mixed}}_{1/3}(\mathsf{INC}_{2n})-1.

(92)

Hence,
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{mixed}}_{1/3}(\mathsf{INC}_{2n})-1
bits of communication suffice for Bob to compute
x_{i}
with error at most
1/3
. However,
[
5
, Theorem 5.1]
shows that
R_{1/3}^{\to}(\mathsf{AugIndex}_{n})=\Omega(n)
. We therefore conclude that
\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{mixed}}_{1/3}(\mathsf{INC}_{n})=\Omega(n)
.
∎
