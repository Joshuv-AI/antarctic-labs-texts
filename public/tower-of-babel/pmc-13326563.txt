Modified subxiphoid uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery with percutaneous suspension technique via balance hook sternal elevation device in anterior mediastinal masses
Authors: Yibo Shan, Yafei Bao, Hong Guo, Kang Feng, Jian Wu, Gangqiang Wang, Tao Wu, Yi Lu, Qingtong Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore the clinical value of the modified subxiphoid uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) with percutaneous suspension technique via “balance hook sternal elevation device” in anterior mediastinal masses.MethodsPatients who underwent balance hook-assisted subxiphoid uniportal VATS for anterior mediastinal masses resection in the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Affiliated Hospital of Yangzhou University, between September 2025 and January 2026 were included. Clinical data and perioperative indicators were analyzed.ResultsA total of 16 patients were enrolled, comprising 6 males and 10 females, with ages ranging from 34 to 75 years. Tumor size ranged from 1.3 to 5.2 cm in the longest diameter. The operative time was 75–265 min, with an estimated blood loss of 10–100 mL. The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) pain score was 4–7 on postoperative day 1, which decreased to 1–3 on postoperative day 3. Postoperative thoracic drainage duration was 2–7 days, and the postoperative hospital stay ranged from 3 to 9 days. Complete and safe resection of the tumor and thymus was successfully achieved in all patients. All patients recovered well postoperatively without significant complications.ConclusionThe modified “balance hook sternal elevation device” assisted technique effectively enlarges the retrosternal space, providing a satisfactory surgical field and adequate operating space for subxiphoid uniportal VATS. It ensures clear visualization of anterior mediastinal structures and facilitates the necessary extent of resection. The percutaneous suspension technique obviates the need for xiphoid resection, reduces sternal trauma, and further minimizes surgical injury. The combination of these two approaches holds potential clinical significance and application value in optimizing the efficacy and safety of minimally invasive anterior mediastinal surgery, minimizing surgical trauma and postoperative pain, and enhancing postoperative recovery for patients.

Background

The anterior mediastinum refers to the region located posterior to the sternum, anterior to the pericardium, and between the bilateral mediastinal pleurae. This anatomical compartment contains the thymus, lymph nodes, adipose tissue, nerves, blood vessels, and occasionally the thyroid gland extending inferiorly from the neck. It represents a common site for mediastinal masses [1]. Anterior mediastinal masses (AMMs) encompass a diverse spectrum of neoplastic and non-neoplastic conditions, predominantly originating from the thymus, accounting for over 50% of all mediastinal masses [2]. These include thymic epithelial tumors, thymic neuroendocrine tumors, malignant lymphoma, germ cell tumors, thymic hyperplasia, and thymolipoma, among others [3].

Surgical resection represents the most important treatment modality for anterior mediastinal masses, typically employed either alone or in combination with adjuvant therapies as part of the treatment strategy. Complete surgical resection serves as an independent prognostic factor for outcomes in anterior mediastinal masses [4, 5]. The distinctive anatomical location imposes stringent requirements on the selection of surgical approaches. Median sternotomy has traditionally been considered the standard procedure for achieving complete resection of AMMs; however, it is associated with substantial trauma, significant risks, pronounced postoperative pain, and compromised sternal stability [6]. With the rapid advancement of thoracoscopic techniques, VATS for AMMs resection has gradually supplanted median sternotomy as a widely adopted clinical approach, offering notable advantages including reduced trauma, decreased blood loss, lower complication rates, and diminished postoperative pain. Nevertheless, VATS presents challenges in visualization for larger tumors or those encasing major vessels [7]. While the development of three-dimensional thoracoscopy and robotic-assisted systems has partially improved the operative field, their widespread implementation remains constrained by equipment costs and learning curve considerations [8].

In recent years, with the evolution of minimally invasive concepts and innovations in surgical instrumentation, the minimally invasive treatment strategies for AMMs have exhibited a trend toward diversification. Among these advancements, the application of sternal elevation devices is progressively transforming the traditional surgical paradigm. By employing mechanical traction, the sternal elevation device increases the retrosternal space, elevating the upper portion by 3–5 cm and the lower portion by 7–10 cm. Its principal advantages include enhanced visualization of the surgical field, expanded operative workspace, and reduced compression of the great cardiac vessels by surgical instruments [9, 10]. Our department has pioneered the adoption and modification of an innovative technique-“balance hook sternal elevation device” assisted technique. This approach employs a percutaneous “soft suspension” technique to indirectly elevate the sternum. The retractor assembly is designated the “balance hook” due to its overall configuration resembling a balance scale. Compared to conventional “osseous” traction utilizing double-hook retraction, this “soft” lifting technique further mitigates postoperative pain, ensures adequate visualization of the surgical field during AMMs resection, reduces the risk of iatrogenic injury. This article presents a series of 16 cases of subxiphoid uniport video-assisted thoracoscopic resection of AMMs using the modified balance hook technique at our institution, with a focus on elucidating the standardized operational protocol and evaluating its clinical efficacy in minimally invasive AMMs resection.

Methods

Clinical data

Patients who underwent balance hook-assisted subxiphoid uniport video-assisted thoracoscopic resection of AMMs at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Affiliated Hospital of Yangzhou University, between September 2025 and January 2026 were enrolled in this study. Baseline characteristics, perioperative data, pain scores, and follow-up information were collected.

Inclusion criteria: (1) definitive diagnosis of AMMs confirmed by chest computed tomography (CT); (2) intact chest skin without ulceration or infection; (3) underwent balance hook-assisted subxiphoid uniportal VATS for AMMs resection during hospitalization.

Exclusion criteria: (1) history of previous thoracic surgery or neoadjuvant therapy; (2) history of chronic pain or long-term use of analgesic medications; (3) active infection or concomitant primary malignancies; (4) mental or cognitive disorders precluding cooperation with surgical treatment; (5) conversion to thoracotomy during the procedure.

Surgical procedures

All balance hook-assisted subxiphoid uniportal VATS for AMMs resection in this study were performed by the same experienced surgical team to ensure procedural standardization and consistency. Prior to skin disinfection and draping, the primary surgeon marks incision lines at the suprasternal notch and xiphoid process based on surface anatomical landmarks. The midpoint between these two marked points is generally selected as the installation positioning site for the Base Fixation Unit. Specific positioning markings are illustrated in Fig. 1B.

Installation of the balance hook and establishment of the subxiphoid incision

Following skin disinfection and draping, the circulating nurse collaborates with the surgical team to complete the installation of the “balance hook sternal elevation device” (Fig. 2A). The balance hook Base Fixation Unit (Fig. 1A ①) is first secured to the rails on both sides of the operating table, followed by sequential attachment of the Vertical Support Rod (②), Transverse Connecting Bar (③), Balancer Stabilizer (④), and Articulated Balance Arm (⑤). Sternal lifting devices, specifically the Ratchet Lifting Mechanism (⑥), are positioned above the suprasternal notch and the xiphoid process, respectively. Based on preoperative markings, the surgeon created two 0.5 cm puncture incisions, one at the suprasternal notch and the other at the lower end of the sternal body. A Stainless Steel Tunneling Needle (⑨) is inserted via a subcutaneous longitudinal puncture in the anterior sternal region, directed from the xiphoid process toward the suprasternal notch, traversing a distance approximately equivalent to the combined length of the manubrium and sternal body. Following puncture completion, a protective cap is affixed to the tip of the tunneling needle. The Tunneling Needle Fixator (⑧) is then installed at both ends of the tunneling needle and connected via Hooks (⑦) to the Ratchet Lifting Mechanism (⑥), ensuring stability and adjustability of sternal elevation throughout the procedure. Subsequently, a 3.0–5.0 cm longitudinal incision is made inferior to the xiphoid process (Fig. 2B). By rotating the Ratchet Lifting Mechanism, both ends of the tunneling needle are lifted upward. This maneuver indirectly elevates the sternum through tension on the skin suspension-termed “soft suspension”-thereby achieving enlargement of the retrosternal space. During sternal elevation, the circulating nurse places a cotton pad beneath the patient’s head and neck to prevent the neck from becoming suspended and hyperextended following sternal elevation, which could otherwise result in cervical spine injury or airway distortion. Additionally, because the muscles surrounding the sternum may resist traction, potentially complicate the procedure or necessitate greater force–thereby increasing the risk of injury to the sternum, skin, and muscles–close coordination with the anesthesiologist is essential to ensure adequate muscle relaxation prior to performing sternal elevation. The suspension procedure requires adherence to the principle of gradual traction for stepwise elevation, avoiding excessive traction while ensuring the basic requirements of the surgical procedure are met.

Surgical resection scope [11]

For thymic tumors without concurrent autoimmune diseases, total thymectomy including the tumor and mediastinal fat is recommended. For thymic tumors associated with autoimmune diseases, extended thymectomy encompassing the tumor, mediastinal fat, mediastinal pleura, and cardiophrenic angle fat is advised. To ensure radical resection, the surgical area should encompass the following boundaries: bilateral phrenic nerves, anteriorly to the posterior aspect of the sternum, posteriorly to the pericardium, great vessels, and trachea, superiorly to the lower pole of the thyroid gland, and inferiorly to the xiphoid process and cardiophrenic angle.

Surgical method

After satisfactory suspension, the surgeon utilized electrocautery to dissect the retrosternal space in close proximity to the sternum, taking care to avoid thermal injury to adjacent critical vessels and tissues. Following the opening of the bilateral mediastinal pleura, a wound protector was inserted (indicated by the white arrow in Fig. 2C). Dissection continued along the anterior mediastinal space posterior to the sternum, progressing cranially to the level of the left innominate vein. Subsequently, the anesthesiologist was instructed to switch to single-lung ventilation, allowing for collapse of the right lung to optimize the surgical field.

Using the right phrenic nerve as a boundary, and adhering to the principle of dissection “from inferior to superior and from right to left”, the thymus and surrounding adipose tissue anterior to the pericardium were fully exposed and systematically dissected. Upon completion of the right mediastinal dissection, the anesthesiologist was instructed to re-inflate the right lung and deflate the left lung to expand the operative view. Dissection of the left mediastinum was then carried out following the mirror-image principle of “from inferior to superior and from left to right”, until the distal portion of the left innominate vein was exposed. At this point, the dissections from both sides converged, allowing for further resection of the surrounding adipose tissue. Throughout the dissection, the surgeon meticulously identified the phrenic nerves and performed maneuvers close to the pericardial surface to avoid injury to these vital structures.

Following tumor resection, the completely excised specimen was placed into a dedicated retrieval bag and removed intact through the subxiphoid incision, strictly adhering to the no-touch isolation technique to prevent tumor fragmentation and potential seeding of the thoracic cavity or incision site. Hemostasis was meticulously achieved, and a fine-threaded drainage tube was placed through the subxiphoid incision. After confirming satisfactory lung re-expansion under thoracoscopic visualization, the “balance hook sternal elevation device” was sequentially removed, and the incisions were closed in layers, concluding the procedure (Fig. 2D).

Technical modification

In response to challenges such as limited surgical exposure and instrument interference caused by efforts to minimize incision size, our surgical team has continuously refined the incision design and adopted adjunctive measures during operative practice. The modifications are as follows: (1) A glove cuff is mounted onto the balance device (indicated by the red arrow in Fig. 2C) to secure the thoracoscope. The elastic tension of the glove cuff partially restricts the vertical mobility of the scope, thereby reducing interference with the surgeon’s instruments. (2) A metal trocar (indicated by the yellow arrow in Fig. 2C) is inserted longitudinally along the axis of the incision, positioned between the lower edge of the incision and the wound protector. This trocar serves as a dedicated access channel for energy devices; further alleviating issues related to limited visualization and instrument crowding.

Postoperative data collection

Collection of surgical indicators

Relevant surgical parameters were recorded, including operative time, surgical incision length, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative thoracic drainage volume, chest tube removal time, postoperative hospital stay, and postoperative complications.

Pain assessment

Vital signs of patients were closely monitored postoperatively, and early mobilization was encouraged. Pain levels were evaluated on postoperative days 1 and 3 using the VAS, which rates pain on a scale from 0 to 10, with 0 indicating no pain and 10 representing the most severe and intolerable pain. Higher scores indicate greater pain intensity. As part of routine postoperative care, a chest digital radiography (DR) was performed on day 2 to assess the surgical site and thoracic cavity.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Yangzhou University (Approval No. 2025-YKL06-K16). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to surgery.

Results

Baseline characteristics and clinical characteristics

A total of 16 patients diagnosed with AMMs who underwent surgical treatment at our hospital were enrolled in this study. The cohort comprised 6 males and 10 females, with ages ranging from 34 to 75 years. The maximum diameter of the masses varied from 1.3 to 5.2 cm. Regarding tumor location, 4 masses were situated in the central anterior mediastinum, 5 were in the left anterior mediastinum, and 7 were in the right. Postoperative pathological examination revealed thymic carcinoma in 1 patient, thymic hyperplasia in 1 patient, thymic cyst in 6 patients, thymoma in 6 patients, and bronchogenic cyst in 2 patients. Surgical outcomes and pain scores

All patients underwent balance hook-assisted subxiphoid uniportal VATS surgery for AMMs resection. Complete resection of the mass and total thymectomy were achieved in all cases, with no intraoperative complications. Although some patients presented with severe adhesions resulting in prolonged operative time, no conversions to thoracotomy were required. The sternal elevation device was successfully deployed in all patients, and the establishment of the percutaneous tunnel and suspension procedure were accomplished smoothly, with a mean setup time of 7.44 ± 2.48 min. Following activation of the lifting system, significant elevation of the anterior chest wall was observed macroscopically, and endoscopic visualization confirmed substantial widening of the retrosternal space. Discussion

With the advancement of thoracoscopic techniques, minimally invasive surgery has gradually become the preferred approach for the resection of AMMs due to its advantages such as smaller incisions, shorter hospital stays, and reduced postoperative pain [12]. Minimally invasive resection of AMMs refers to any surgical approach that avoids sternotomy (or partial sternotomy) and rib spreading, primarily including transcervical, transthoracic, subxiphoid, and robotic approaches, as well as combinations of the above [13–16]. Over the past few decades, the minimally invasive surgical approaches for AMMs have been continuously refined. Among these, the transthoracic intercostal and subxiphoid approaches are the most widely utilized.

Compared with conventional thoracotomy, although the unilateral thoracic approach offers the benefits of minimal invasiveness and faster recovery, the intercostal approach is associated with several limitations, including a narrow operative field, difficulty in visualizing contralateral vital structures, incomplete resection of the contralateral thymus and anterior pericardial fatty tissue, a heightened risk of injury to critical nerves and vessels, and challenges in managing unexpected massive hemorrhage. Moreover, due to intercostal nerve injury caused by the transthoracic approach, patients often experience unavoidable intercostal neuralgia postoperatively [17]. In contrast, the subxiphoid approach introduced by Kido [18] avoids bone and intercostal nerve damage while providing a surgical view comparable to that of thoracotomy, thereby reducing the risk of injury to critical structures. However, owing to the narrow retrosternal space, surgeons may encounter practical difficulties such as a limited surgical view and restricted working space during the surgery. To address these issues, Suda [19] proposed the use of artificial pneumothorax, which, although capable of laterally expanding the visual field, is limited by insufficient expansion of the retrosternal space, potential gas leakage, and hemodynamic instability. To address the challenge of inadequate retrosternal space, various mechanical sternal elevation devices have been developed [20–22]. However, clinical practice has revealed that despite multiple modifications, sternal retractors, including those utilizing dual-hook-assisted techniques, still exhibit certain limitations. These include insufficient structural stability of the frame, limited sternal elevation, and cumbersome operation and installation procedures.

Our department has also attempted lateral thoracic intercostal approach resection and compared the application of the subxiphoid three-port approach versus the subxiphoid uniportal approach in AMMs resection. Consistent with the findings of most teams, the subxiphoid three-port approach requires the intraoperative maintenance of carbon dioxide insufflation at a certain pressure to avoid interference from bilateral lung ventilation with the surgical field. However, this may exert potential adverse effects on patients’ hemodynamic parameters, particularly posing a risk of air embolism in the event of major venous injury. Moreover, when this approach is applied in obese patients or those with pectus excavatum, visualization of the anterior mediastinum is often compromised, thereby increasing surgical risk [23]. Regarding the sternal retractors used in the subxiphoid uniportal approach, whether single or double hooks, they tend to obscure the surgical field and impede manipulation. Additionally, the limited traction angle at the subxiphoid process results in inadequate sternal elevation, thereby restricting exposure of the operative field. Furthermore, the “rigid suspension” device directly contacts and retracts the sternum, posing a risk of sternal injury, which is particularly pronounced in elderly patients and those with osteoporosis [10, 22].

In general, the subxiphoid approach offers significant anatomical advantages as the primary incision, enabling access to bilateral surgical targets [24]. Sternal elevation via the subxiphoid route improves surgical visualization and working space, facilitating exposure and manipulation in this region. Compared with conventional minimally invasive techniques, sternal elevation device assisted approaches allow for more effective fat dissection, ensure clearer surgical margins, reduce the risk of organ compression and air embolism, and thereby enhance overall surgical safety [25, 26]. However, existing devices primarily rely on direct rigid mechanical retraction of the sternum and ribs to expand the retrosternal space, which may predispose patients to sternal injury. Moreover, clinical experience has revealed suboptimal stability of the retractor system, particularly in patients with higher body weight, in whom satisfactory sternal elevation is often difficult to achieve. To address these two technical challenges, the percutaneous suspension technique using a “balance hook sternal elevation device” has been developed.

To address the issue of retractor stability, the “balance hook sternal elevation device” system features the Base Fixation Units to both sides of the operating table, ensuring even force distribution across the Transverse Connecting Bar. Furthermore, through the synergistic action of the Transverse Connecting Bar, the Balancer Stabilizer, and the Articulated Balance Arm, the lifting apparatus achieves enhanced stability. By virtue of its “gantry-type” structure, the balance retractor enables symmetrical and balanced force distribution, providing greater supporting force than unilateral retractors, significantly improving stability, reducing the risk of intraoperative chest wall tilting, and avoiding uneven stress distribution. This results in a more stable surgical field and improved operative efficiency. Following modifications by our team, a single-port trocar equipped with a dedicated channel for the ultrasonic scalpel was incorporated, to reduce the need for unnecessary intraoperative adjustments and avoiding tissue traction injuries caused by repeated angle corrections, thereby shortening operative time. The dedicated channel ensures that energy is precisely delivered to the target tissue, minimizing peripheral thermal injury, preventing instrument conflicts that could damage the ultrasonic scalpel, and reducing thermal conduction injury to surrounding tissues. The elastic fixation of the glove cuff minimizes lens instability, thereby improving visualization for the surgeon and reducing the time and physical effort that the camera assistant must spend on frequent lens cleaning.

To address the issue of iatrogenic sternal injury caused by direct sternal retraction, the device employs an “indirect sternal lifting” method. This involves the use of steel needles for percutaneous puncture of the presternal skin and lifting fixators that anchor the needles to the balance retractor system. Given the close adherence of the presternal skin to the sternum in the absence of intervening loose tissue, lifting the presternal skin indirectly results in elevation of the sternum. In practice, we have found that this percutaneous suspension technique achieves satisfactory sternal elevation, provides excellent exposure of the retrosternal space, and effectively avoids direct sternal trauma. As there are no major vessels or nerves beneath the presternal skin, the puncture leaves only two small 5 mm incisions, resulting in improved cosmetic outcomes and reduced trauma. Postoperative observation and follow-up revealed significant pain reduction and accelerated recovery in patients undergoing this procedure. These benefits are likely attributable to the preservation of the xiphoid process, the non‑traumatic nature of the percutaneous suspension technique, and the effective protection of intercostal nerves with minimal damage to surrounding tissues afforded by the subxiphoid approach. Thus, the “balance hook percutaneous suspension sternal elevation device” further enhances the advantages of the subxiphoid approach: complete resection and specimen retrieval can be achieved through a single small 3–5 cm subxiphoid incision without additional incisions, while avoiding the risk of intercostal nerve injury. In accordance with surgical principles, all patients in this study underwent tumor and thymus resection, and no thymus-related complications were observed during short-term follow-up. Certainly, this may be related to the limited number of cases in the present study. A total of 16 patients were reported, and the small sample size introduces a certain degree of conditionality. In fact, according to previous reports, various postoperative complications may occur, including bleeding or sternal injury at the retraction site due to inappropriate mechanical traction, as the single/double hook sternal elevator comes into direct contact with the patient’s sternum [21]. Given the unique anatomical location of the thymus and the potential for local tumor invasion, there is a risk of phrenic nerve and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury during thymectomy, as well as possible diaphragmatic injury [27, 28]. Myasthenic crisis is the most common complication following thymectomy in patients with myasthenia gravis [29]. Additionally, other postoperative complications may include hemothorax, surgical site infection, pneumonia, pneumothorax, and pericardial effusion [24, 30]. During the learning and application process, we have also found the device to be intuitive and straightforward to operate. Compared with conventional minimally invasive surgery [31], this technique has a shorter learning curve, enabling less experienced surgical teams to achieve proficiency quickly. This enhances surgical efficiency and demonstrates significant potential for clinical adoption. By optimizing the surgical field and expanding the working space through sternal elevation, this approach effectively overcomes the technical limitations of conventional minimally invasive techniques in anatomically complex regions. Its expanded applications include partial lung resection, phrenic nerve dissection and resection, pericardial resection with biomaterial reconstruction, radical resection of tumors involving the innominate vein system with angioplasty, vena caval plasty, and diaphragmatic plication, among other complex procedures [32].

Limits

Although we have preliminarily validated the feasibility of the modified percutaneous suspension subxiphoid uniportal VATS technique using the “balance hook sternal elevation device” for the resection of AMMs, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study included only 16 patients from a single center, and the small sample size necessitates future multicenter, large-sample randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up data to further validate the efficacy and safety of this technique. Second, this study did not directly measure the tensile force applied to the skin tissue or the extent of sternal elevation. Subsequent research should quantitatively assess such biomechanical parameters to enhance technical safety and promote standardization. Furthermore, although no complications related to skin ischemia or necrosis were observed during postoperative follow-up, the theoretical risk of ischemic injury due to prolonged skin traction warrants attention. Future studies will systematically monitor intraoperative skin retraction time and evaluate postoperative skin perfusion and tissue viability to quantify the safety profile of the percutaneous suspension technique. Additionally, this study represents a unilateral retrospective analysis and did not include comparisons with other surgical approaches, such as the unmodified percutaneous suspension subxiphoid uniportal VATS technique using the “balance hook sternal elevation device” or conventional sternal retractor-assisted subxiphoid uniportal VATS. Finally, the learning curve advantages mentioned are based on subjective observations during our team’s learning and implementation process and have not been substantiated by long-term data collection and statistical analysis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the modified percutaneous suspension technique utilizing the “balance hook sternal elevation device” for sternal elevation represents a novel approach for minimally invasive resection of AMMs, successfully achieving dual optimization of surgical visualization and operative working space. This technique effectively expands the retrosternal space, providing adequate surgical exposure and maneuverability while reducing procedural risks and minimizing sternal trauma. It facilitates complete resection of the mass and surrounding adipose tissue, thereby yielding satisfactory surgical outcomes. Furthermore, this approach broadens the applicability of the subxiphoid uniportal access, enhancing its inherent advantages of reduced postoperative pain and minimized surgical trauma.
