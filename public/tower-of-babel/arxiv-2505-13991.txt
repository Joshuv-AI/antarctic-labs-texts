The a ​ b ​ c abc conjecture is true almost always
Jared Duker Lichtman

Abstract
. Let rad ⁡ ( n ) \rad(n) denote the product of distinct prime factors of an integer n ⩾ 1 n\geqslant 1 . The celebrated a ​ b ​ c abc conjecture asks whether every solution to the equation a + b = c a+b=c in triples of coprime integers ( a , b , c ) (a,b,c) must satisfy rad ⁡ ( a ​ b ​ c ) > K ε ​ c 1 − ε \rad(abc)>K_{\varepsilon}\,c^{1-\varepsilon} , for some constant K ε > 0 K_{\varepsilon}>0 . In this expository note, we present a classical estimate of de Bruijn that implies almost all such triples satisfy the a ​ b ​ c abc conjecture, in a precise quantitative sense. Namely, there are at most O ⁡ ( N 2 / 3 ) O(N^{2/3}) many triples of coprime integers in a cube ( a , b , c ) ∈ { 1 , … , N } 3 (a,b,c)\in\{1,\ldots,N\}^{3} satisfying a + b = c a+b=c and rad ⁡ ( a ​ b ​ c ) < c 1 − ε \rad(abc)<c^{1-\varepsilon} . The proof is elementary and essentially self-contained. Beyond revisiting a classical argument for its own sake, this exposition is aimed to contextualize a new result of Browning, Lichtman, and Teräväinen, who prove a refined estimate O ⁡ ( N 33 / 50 ) O(N^{33/50}) , giving the first power-savings since 1962.

1.
Introduction

In this note, we are concerned with solutions to the simple equation
a+b=c
in triples of integers
(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
. First, if
a
and
b
are both divisible by a prime
p
, then
c=a+b
is also divisible by
p
. So we may divide by
p
to give
\frac{a}{p}+\frac{b}{p}=\frac{c}{p}
. We may continue to divide by shared primes until
a
and
b
are coprime integers. Then
c=a+b
is also coprime to both
a
and
b
, which we assume hereafter.

For any integer
n\geqslant 1
, we may factor
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{1}}
for a unique collection of primes
p_{1}<\cdots<p_{k}
and exponents
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\geqslant 1
. Then let
\rad(n)=p_{1}\cdots p_{k}
denote the product of distinct prime factors of
n
. That is, we let

\displaystyle\rad(n)=\prod_{p\mid n}p.

The celebrated
abc
conjecture of Masser and Oesterlé asserts that for any
\varepsilon>0
there is a constant
K_{\varepsilon}>0
such that every triple
(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
of coprime integers solving the equation
a+b=c
must also satisfy
\rad(abc)\ >\ K_{\varepsilon}\,c^{1-\varepsilon}
. Equivalently, only finitely many such solutions to
a+b=c
may satisfy

(1.1)

\displaystyle\gcd(a,b)=1\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\rad(abc)<c^{1-\varepsilon}.

The
abc
conjecture is intrinsically attractive for suggesting that basic addition (from the simple equation
a+b=c
) has a subtle connection to multiplication (from the prime factors of
abc
). Moreover, the
abc
conjecture is highly coveted for its potential to generalize many celebrated results in number theory, including Fermat’s last theorem. See
[
4
]
for a nice exposition on these connections.

The best unconditional progress towards (
1.1
) is due to Stewart and Yu
[
6
]
, who showed that only finitely many coprime solutions to
a+b=c
may satisfy
\rad(abc)<(\log c)^{3-\varepsilon}
. This condition is exponentially stricter than (
1.1
). Recently, Pasten
[
5
]
obtained a new, subexponentially strict condition of
\rad(abc)<\exp((\log\log c)^{2-\varepsilon})
, in the special case when
a<c^{1-\varepsilon}
.

If we count solutions to
a+b=c
in a cube
(a,b,c)\in\{1,\ldots,N\}^{3}
, there are
O(N^{2})
many such triples. In fact there are about
\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}N^{2}
such coprime triples.
We may then consider the set of exceptional triples,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(N)\;=\;\bigg\{(a,b,c)\in\{1,\ldots,N\}^{3}\;:\;\gcd(a,b)=1,\begin{array}[]{c}a+b=c\\
\rad(abc)\,<\,c^{1-\varepsilon}\end{array}\bigg\}.

The
abc
conjecture is equivalent to the bound
|\mathcal{E}(N)|\leqslant O_{\varepsilon}(1)
for all
N\geqslant 1
.

In this note, we show that among the
O(N^{2})
many coprime solutions to
a+b=c
in a cube of length
N
, at most
O(N^{2/3})
of those solutions also satisfy (
1.1
). This makes precise the statement that the
abc
conjecture is true almost always.

Theorem 1.1
.

We have
|\mathcal{E}(N)|\leqslant O(N^{2/3})
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is elementary and essentially self-contained. The main argument is a half-page (see p. 4), assuming a classical estimate on radicals, which itself has a one page. Namely, Lemma
2.2
(see p. 3) shows that very few integers
n\leqslant N
can share a common radical
r=\rad(n)
. To the author’s knowledge, Theorem
1.1
has not appeared in the literature (prior to
[
2
]
), though it is folklore to experts.

Beyond presenting this elegant argument for its own sake, our exposition is aimed to motivate a 2024 result of Browning, Lichtman, and Teräväinen
[
2
]
.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
2
]
)
.

We have
|\mathcal{E}(N)|\leqslant O(N^{33/50})
.

Theorem
1.2
refines Theorem
1.1
, reducing the exponent
\frac{2}{3}=0.666\cdots
down to
\frac{33}{50}=0.66
.
However, unlike the half-page elementary proof of Theorem
1.1
, the proof of Theorem
1.2
is over 15 pages, and itself relies on a collection of more sophisticated techniques across the literature—namely, Fourier analysis, Thue equations, geometry of numbers, and determinant methods (see
[
1
]
for an accessible introduction). These techniques are used to count integer points on varieties. We hope this note serves as a friendly entry-point, and motivation for further reading on these subjects.

f(n)=O(g(n))
means that
|f(n)|\leqslant C\,g(n)
for all
n>0
where
C>0
is an absolute constant; while
f(n)=O_{\varepsilon}(g(n))
means
|f(n)|\leqslant C\,g(n)

C=C_{\varepsilon}>0

2.
Divisor bound and radical estimate

We begin with the classical divisor bound, which is one of the most useful and versatile estimates in number theory. We provide its proof for completeness in Lemma
2.1
below. Moreover, its proof will foreshadow that of Lemma
2.2
, for the main radical estimate.

Lemma 2.1

(Divisor bound)
.

We have
\tau(n)\leqslant n^{o(1)}
for any integer
n\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

We factor
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}}
into primes, so that
\tau(n)=(a_{1}+1)\cdots(a_{k}+1)
. So for any
0<\varepsilon<1
, we have

(2.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\tau(n)}{n^{\varepsilon}}=\prod_{i\leqslant k}\frac{a_{i}+1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}\varepsilon}}.

If
p_{i}^{\varepsilon}\geqslant 2
then
\frac{a_{i}+1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}\varepsilon}}\leqslant\frac{a_{i}+1}{2^{a_{i}}}\leqslant 1
. Otherwise
p_{i}<2^{1/\varepsilon}
, in which case

\displaystyle\frac{a_{i}+1}{p_{i}^{a_{i}\varepsilon}}\leqslant\frac{a_{i}+1}{2^{a_{i}\varepsilon}}\leqslant\frac{a_{i}+1}{a_{i}\varepsilon/2+1}\leqslant\frac{2}{\varepsilon},

using
p_{i}\geqslant 2
and
2^{x}\geqslant x/2+1
for all
x\geqslant 0
. Thus (
2.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\tau(n)}{n^{\varepsilon}}\leqslant\prod_{p<2^{1/\varepsilon}}\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\leqslant(2/\varepsilon)^{2^{1/\varepsilon}}.

In particular
\tau(n)\leqslant O_{\varepsilon}(n^{\varepsilon})
. Taking
\varepsilon\to 0
implies
\tau(n)\leqslant n^{o(1)}
as desired.
∎

The following radical estimate shows that there can be at most
N^{o(1)}
many integers
n\leqslant N
with a common radical
r=\rad(n)
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
r\leqslant N
and let
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}(r,N)=\{n\leqslant N:\rad(n)=r\}
. Then we have

|\mathcal{R}|\leqslant N^{o(1)}.

Proof.

We factor
r=p_{1}\cdots p_{k}

into distinct primes
p_{1}<\cdots<p_{k}
. Then the integers
n
with
\rad(n)=r

are of the form
n=p_{1}^{a_{1}}p_{2}^{a_{2}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}}
for any exponents
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\geqslant 1
. That is,

\mathcal{R}:=\Bigl\{n\leqslant N:\rad(n)=r\Bigr\}\subset\Bigl\{\,p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}}:a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\geqslant 1\,\Bigr\}.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, we have
|\mathcal{R}|\leqslant\sum_{n\in\mathcal{R}}(N/n)^{\varepsilon}
. Then we may factor the Dirichlet series as an Euler product,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{\varepsilon}}\leqslant\sum_{a_{1}\geqslant 1}\cdots\sum_{a_{k}\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{(p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{a_{k}})^{\varepsilon}}=\prod_{i\leqslant k}\Bigl(\sum_{a_{i}\geqslant 1}\frac{1}{p_{i}^{\varepsilon a_{i}}}\Bigr)=\prod_{i\leqslant k}\frac{1}{p_{i}^{\varepsilon}-1}.

If
p_{i}^{\varepsilon}\geqslant 2
then
\frac{1}{p_{i}^{\varepsilon}-1}\leqslant 1
. Otherwise
p_{i}<2^{1/\varepsilon}
, in which case

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{i}^{\varepsilon}-1}\leqslant\frac{1}{2^{\varepsilon}-1}\leqslant\frac{2}{\varepsilon},

using
p_{i}\geqslant 2
and
2^{x}\geqslant x/2+1
for all
x\geqslant 0
. Thus (
2.2
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{R}|}{N^{\varepsilon}}\leqslant\sum_{n\in\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{n^{\varepsilon}}\leqslant\prod_{p<2^{1/\varepsilon}}\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\leqslant(2/\varepsilon)^{2^{1/\varepsilon}}.

In particular
|\mathcal{R}|\leqslant O_{\varepsilon}(N^{\varepsilon})
. Taking
\varepsilon\to 0
implies
|\mathcal{R}|\leqslant N^{o(1)}
as desired.
∎

As a direct consequence of Lemma
2.2
we deduce:

Corollary 2.3
.

For any
0\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 1
, we have

\displaystyle\big|\big\{n\leqslant N:\rad(n)\leqslant N^{\lambda}\big\}\big|\ =\ N^{\lambda+o(1)}.

Proof.

We split into all possible radicals
r\leqslant N^{\lambda}
, giving

\displaystyle\big|\big\{n\leqslant N:\rad(n)\leqslant N^{\lambda}\big\}\big|

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\leqslant N^{\lambda}}|\mathcal{R}(r,N)|\leqslant\sum_{r\leqslant N^{\lambda}}N^{o(1)}=N^{\lambda+o(1)},

by Lemma
2.2
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

We may optimize Lemma
2.2
to show
|\mathcal{R}(r,N)|\leqslant N^{O(1/\log\log N)}
, taking
\varepsilon=O(1/\log\log N)
.
Similarly optimizing Lemma
2.1
gives
\tau(n)\leqslant n^{O(1/\log\log n)}
.

In 1962, de Bruijn
[
3
]
obtained the above bound
N^{\lambda+o(1)}
in the sharper quantitative form
N^{\lambda+O(1/\sqrt{\log N\log\log N})}
. Interestingly, Robert, Stewart and Tenenbaum
[
7
]
have proposed a refined
abc
conjecture, expressing the inequality
\rad(abc)<c^{1-\varepsilon}
from (
1.1
) in the quantitative form
\rad(abc)<c^{1-O(1/\sqrt{\log c\log\log c})}
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Take an exceptional triple
(a,b,c)\in\mathcal{E}(N)
. That is,
a,b,c
are coprime integers satisfying
a+b=c
and
\rad(abc)<c^{1-\varepsilon}
.
Observe that

\displaystyle\rad(abc)=\prod_{p\mid abc}p=\Big(\prod_{p\mid a}p\Big)\Big(\prod_{p\mid b}p\Big)\Big(\prod_{p\mid c}p\Big)=\rad(a)\rad(b)\rad(c),

since
\rad(n)
is a multiplicative function. In particular,

\displaystyle\rad(ab)\rad(ac)\rad(bc)=\rad(abc)^{2}<c^{2-2\varepsilon}.

Thus there is some choice of
xy\in\{ab,ac,bc\}
that satisfies

\displaystyle\rad(xy)\leqslant c^{\frac{2}{3}-\varepsilon}.

So we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}(N)|=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a,b,c\leqslant N\\
\gcd(a,b)=1\\
\rad(abc)<c^{1-\varepsilon}\\
a+b=c\end{subarray}}{\sum\sum\sum}1\ \leqslant\ 3\sum_{r\leqslant N^{2/3-\varepsilon}}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant x,y\leqslant N\\
\gcd(x,y)=1\\
r=\rad(xy)\end{subarray}}{\sum\sum}1\ \leqslant\ 3\sum_{r\leqslant N^{2/3-\varepsilon}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant n\leqslant N^{2}\\
r=\rad(n)\end{subarray}}\tau(n).

The divisor bound in Lemma
2.1
gives
\tau(n)\leqslant N^{o(1)}
, and so

(3.1)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}(N)|\ \leqslant\ \sum_{r\leqslant N^{2/3-\varepsilon}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant N^{2}\\
\rad(n)=r\end{subarray}}N^{o(1)}\leqslant\sum_{r\leqslant N^{2/3-\varepsilon}}N^{o(1)}\leqslant N^{\frac{2}{3}-\varepsilon+o(1)}

by Corollary
2.3
. Hence
|\mathcal{E}(N)|\leqslant O(N^{\frac{2}{3}})
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
