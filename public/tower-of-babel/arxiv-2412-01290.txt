Learning Smooth Distance Functions via Queries
Akash Kumar akk002@ucsd.edu, Sanjoy Dasgupta sadasgupta@ucsd.edu

Abstract
In this work, we investigate the problem of learning distance functions within the query-based learning framework, where a learner is able to pose triplet queries of the form: “Is x i x_{i} closer to x j x_{j} or x k x_{k} ?” We establish formal guarantees on the query complexity required to learn smooth, but otherwise general, distance functions under two notions of approximation: ω \omega -additive approximation and ( 1 + ω ) (1+\omega) -multiplicative approximation. For the additive approximation, we propose a global method whose query complexity is quadratic in the size of a finite cover of the sample space. For the (stronger) multiplicative approximation, we introduce a method that combines global and local approaches, utilizing multiple Mahalanobis distance functions to capture local geometry. This method has a query complexity that scales quadratically with both the size of the cover and the ambient space dimension of the sample space.



1
Introduction

In
personalized retrieval
or, more generally,
personalized prediction
, a machine learning system needs to adapt to a specific user’s perceptions of similarity and dissimilarity over a space
{\mathcal{X}}
of objects (products, documents, movies, etc). Suppose the relevant structure is captured by a distance function
d:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
. How can this be learned through interaction with the user?

This is one of the basic questions underlying the field of
metric learning

(
Kulis, 2013
)
. A key design choice in the interactive learning of distance functions is the nature of user feedback. One line of work assumes that the user is able to provide
numerical dissimilarity values

d(x,x^{\prime})
for pairs of objects
x,x^{\prime}

(
Cox and Cox, 2008
;
Stewart et al., 2005
)
; the goal is then to generalize from the provided examples to an entire distance function. Another option, arguably more natural from a human standpoint, is for the user to provide
comparative
information about distances
(
Schultz and Joachims, 2003
;
Jamieson and Nowak, 2011
)
. For instance, given a triplet of objects,
x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}
, the user can specify which of
d(x,x^{\prime})
and
d(x,x^{\prime\prime})
is larger. This is the model we consider.

Earlier work in metric learning has focused primarily on learning Mahalanobis (linear transformations) distance functions
(
Weinberger and Saul, 2009
;
Xing et al., 2002
;
Davis et al., 2007
;
Goldberger et al., 2004
)
. We look at the more general case of smooth but otherwise arbitrary distance functions
d
over
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We show a variety of results about the learnability of such distances using triplet queries.

The learning protocol operates as follows: on each round of interaction,

•

the learner selects a triplet
(x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\in{\mathcal{X}}^{3}
, and

•

the user answers with

\mbox{sign}(d(x,x^{\prime})-d(x,x^{\prime\prime}))\in\{-1,0,1\}
.

That is, the user either says that
d(x,x^{\prime})
and
d(x,x^{\prime\prime})
are equal, or if not, declares which is larger. After several such rounds, the learner halts and announces a distance function
d^{\prime}:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
.

The best we could hope is for
d^{\prime}
and
d
to agree on all triplets, that is, to be
triplet-equivalent
. But this is unrealistic if the space of distance functions is infinite, since each query only returns a constant number of bits of information. We therefore consider two notions of
approximate triplet-equivalence
.

The first, weaker, notion is that for any
x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
,

\displaystyle|d(x,x^{\prime})-d(x,x^{\prime\prime})|>\omega\implies\mbox{sign}(d(x,x^{\prime})-d(x,x^{\prime\prime}))=\mbox{sign}(d^{\prime}(x,x^{\prime})-d^{\prime}(x,x^{\prime\prime})).

Here
\omega
is a small constant that is pre-specified to the learner. In other words,
d
and
d^{\prime}
agree on all triplets
(x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
for which there is a non-negligible gap between
d(x,x^{\prime})
and
d(x,x^{\prime\prime})
. We show that this is achieved quite easily, by taking a finite cover of the space
{\mathcal{X}}
and selecting a distance
d^{\prime}
that agrees with
d
on all triplets drawn from the cover.

Informal theorem 1.

Suppose
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Let
N
be the size of a
c\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{p^{1.5}}}
-cover of
{\mathcal{X}}
with respect to
\ell_{2}
distance, where
c
is a constant that depends upon the second-order smoothness of
d
. Then
O(N^{2}\log N)
queries are sufficient for learning a distance function that is
\omega
-approximately equivalent to
d
in the (additive) sense above.

The problem with this
additive
notion of approximation is that it provides no guarantees for triplets
(x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
in which there is a significant gap between
d(x,x^{\prime})
and
d(x,x^{\prime\prime})
in that the ratio over the distances
\max\{d(x,x^{\prime})/d(x,x^{\prime\prime}),d(x,x^{\prime\prime})/d(x,x^{\prime})\}
is large, even if the distances themselves are small. For this reason, a better and stronger notion of approximation is to require that
d
and
d^{\prime}
agree on all triplets for which either
d(x,x^{\prime})>(1+\omega)d(x,x^{\prime\prime})
or
d(x,x^{\prime\prime})>(1+\omega)d(x,x^{\prime})
.

Our main result is to realize this kind of multiplicative approximation. We use a hierarchical model consisting of a finite cover of the space
{\mathcal{X}}
, and for each element of the cover, a Mahalanobis approximation to the distance function in the vicinity of that point. We show the following result.

Informal theorem 2.

Suppose
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Let
N
be the size of a
c(\frac{\omega}{p})^{1.5}
-cover of
{\mathcal{X}}
with respect to
\ell_{2}
distance, where
c
is a smoothness parameter for
d
. Then
O(N^{2}\log N+Np^{2}\log\frac{p}{\omega})
queries suffice for learning a distance function that is
(1+\omega)
-approximately equivalent to
d
in the multiplicative sense above.

A key component in this result is an efficient algorithm for learning a Mahalanobis distance function from triplet queries.

2
Related Work

There is a rich literature, spanning many decades, on methods for inferring the geometry of an unknown space, such as the internal semantic or preference space of a user. Early work dating back to the 1960s includes the psychometric literature on multidimensional scaling, which showed how to fit Euclidean distance functions to user-supplied dissimilarity values. More recently, it has become standard practice to train neural net embeddings using pairs of dissimilar and similar data points.

The geometry that is learned can be of three forms:

•

A distance function, typically Mahalanobis distance.

•

A similarity function, typically a kernel function.

•

An embedding, nowadays typically a neural net.

The information used to learn these can consist of exact distance or similarity values, as in classical multidimensional scaling, or comparisons such as triplets and quadruples.

Learning a distance function

Distance learning has been extensively studied in two main categories: (1) exact measurement information, where precise pairwise distances are provided (e.g., in metric multidimensional scaling)
(
Cox and Cox, 2008
;
Stewart et al., 2005
)
, and (2) pairwise constraints informed by class labels, where the goal is to learn a distance function under the assumption that similar objects share the same class or cluster
(
Weinberger and Saul, 2009
;
Xing et al., 2002
;
Davis et al., 2007
;
Goldberger et al., 2004
)
. Further work has explored learning with ordinal or triplet constraints, where only relative comparisons between samples are provided
(
Schultz and Joachims, 2003
;
Kleindessner and von Luxburg, 2016
)
, and queries involving ordinal constraints
(
Jamieson and Nowak, 2011
)
.

One specific class of distance functions, the
Mahalanobis distance
, defined through a linear transformation, has received considerable attention in metric learning. In supervised contexts, studies such as
Weinberger and Saul (2009)
,
Xing et al. (2002)
,
Davis et al. (2007)
, and
Goldberger et al. (2004)
have focused on designing Mahalanobis distance functions that minimize intra-class distances while maximizing inter-class distances. This has been shown to improve prediction performance when integrated with hypothesis classes
(
Shaw et al., 2011
;
McFee and Lanckriet, 2010
)
. However, unlike these works, which rely on class supervision, our approach seeks to learn a target Mahalanobis or general distance function without any side information. In
unsupervised metric learning
, classical methods like linear discriminant analysis (LDA)
(
Fisher, 1936
)
and principal component analysis (PCA)
(
Jolliffe, 1986
)
solve the problem by approximating a low-dimensional structure without requiring labeled data. Our work diverges from these methods, as we assume the learner queries triplets to obtain qualitative feedback, rather than having direct access to linearly transformed data.

Generalization bounds for metric learning in i.i.d. settings have also been explored for various loss functions
(
Verma and Branson, 2015
;
Mason et al., 2017
;
Ye et al., 2019
;
Lei et al., 2020
;
Cao et al., 2016
)
. Of particular relevance to our work are the studies by
Schultz and Joachims (2003)
and
Mason et al. (2017)
, which use triplet comparisons to learn Mahalanobis distance functions. Our approach differs from
Mason et al. (2017)
, as we allow the learner to select triplets and receive exact labels, while they use i.i.d. triplets where the oracle may provide noisy labels. Additionally,
Shalev-Shwartz et al. (2004)
addressed learning Mahalanobis distances in an online setting. For comprehensive surveys on Mahalanobis and general distance functions, refer to
Bellet et al. (2015)
and
Kulis (2013)
.

Learning an embedding

Learning appropriate representations or embeddings for a given dataset has been a central problem in machine learning, studied under various frameworks such as metric and non-metric multidimensional scaling, isometric embedding, and dimensionality reduction, among others. Classical approaches have addressed this problem in both linear
(
Jolliffe, 1986
;
Cox and Cox, 2008
)
and nonlinear settings
(
Belkin and Niyogi, 2003
;
Tenenbaum et al., 2000
;
Roweis and Saul, 2000
)
, with the primary objective of uncovering a suitable low-dimensional representation of the data’s inherent structure. In contrast, our work focuses on using an embedding of the data space in the form of a cover that globally approximates the underlying latent distance function.

Previous research has extensively explored the concept of learning embeddings for objects that satisfy specific triplet or quadruplet constraints, broadly categorized into two main areas: ordinal embeddings
(
Agarwal et al., 2007
;
Arias-Castro, 2015
;
Kleindessner and von Luxburg, 2014
;
Terada and von Luxburg, 2014
)
and triplet embeddings
(
Jamieson and Nowak, 2011
;
Tamuz et al., 2011
;
van der Maaten and Weinberger, 2012
;
Amid and Ukkonen, 2015
;
Heim et al., 2015
;
Jain et al., 2016
)
. These studies primarily aim to develop methods for discovering embeddings that can be applied to various downstream tasks such as kernel learning, nearest neighbor search, and density estimation. In our work, we adopt a different framework, where the learner utilizes feedback from a user on triplet queries, leveraging this information to approximate a distance function that is accurate both locally and globally, up to a triplet similarity transformation.

Preliminaries

We denote by
{\mathcal{X}}
a space of objects/inputs. We consider a general notion of distance on this space.

Definition 1

(distance function)
.

A bivariate function
d:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
is a distance function if

d(x,x^{\prime})\geq 0
and
d(x,x)=0
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
.

In this work, we are interested in various families of distance functions: distances on finite spaces; Mahalanobis distance functions; and smooth distances. For the latter two cases we will assume
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Mahalanobis distance function

Assume that
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
. A
Mahalanobis
distance function is characterized by a symmetric, positive semidefinite matrix (written
M\succeq 0
, or alternatively
M\in\text{Sym}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{p\times p})
), and is denoted by
d_{M}
, where for all
x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
,

\displaystyle d_{M}(x,x^{\prime})=\sqrt{(x-x^{\prime})^{\top}M(x-x^{\prime})}.

(3.1)

We denote the family of Mahalanobis distance functions by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathsf{maha}}=\left\{d_{M}:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\,|\,M\in\text{Sym}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{p\times p})\right\}

Comparisons of distances

In this work, we obtain information about a target distance function on
{\mathcal{X}}
via triplet comparisons. For any triplet
x,y,z\in{\mathcal{X}}
and distance function
d
, we define the label
\ell((x,y,z);d)\in\left\{-1,0,1\right\}
to be the sign of
d(x,y)-d(x,z)
.

The label of a triplet corresponds to the three possibilities
d(x,y)<d(x,z)
,
d(x,y)=d(x,z)
, and
d(x,y)>d(x,z)
. From a human feedback standpoint, it would also be reasonable to allow the labels to be either
\leq
or
\geq
. However, these relaxed labels would not be sufficiently informative for our purposes; for instance, they are always satisfied by the trivial distance function
d(x,x^{\prime})=1(x\neq x^{\prime})
.

Query learning a distance function:

Given a family of distance functions
{\mathcal{M}}
, the learner seeks to learn a target distance function
d
within this space by querying the user/oracle. The learner is allowed rounds of interaction in which it adaptively pick triplets
(x,y,z)\in{\mathcal{X}}^{3}
and receives their labels. We use the notation
(x,y,z)_{\ell}
to denote a triplet
(x,y,z)
along with its label
\ell((x,y,z);d)
. We call this setup
learning a distance function via queries
.

Given the family
{\mathcal{M}}
we are interested in understanding the minimal number of triplet queries that the learner needs so that it can learn
d
either exactly or approximately. To make this precise, we start by defining
triplet equivalence
between two distance functions.

Definition 2

(triplet equivalence)
.

On a given sample space
{\mathcal{X}}
, we say distance functions
d
and
d^{\prime}
are triplet-equivalent if for any triplet
(t,u,v)\in{\mathcal{X}}^{3}

\ell((t,u,v);d)=\ell((t,u,v);d^{\prime})

Triplet equivalence is achievable in finite instance spaces, but for more general spaces we need to allow approximations. We consider two approximate notions of triplet equivalence—additive (see Section
4
) and multiplicative (see Section
5
). Formally, we define these approximations as

Definition 3

(additive approximation)
.

Given a target distance function
d
and threshold
\omega>0
, we say distance function
d^{\prime}
is triplet equivalent to
d
up to additive factor
\omega
if

\displaystyle(\forall x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}),\,|d(x,x^{\prime})-d(x,x^{\prime\prime})|>\omega\implies\mbox{sign}(d(x,x^{\prime})-d(x,x^{\prime\prime}))=\mbox{sign}(d^{\prime}(x,x^{\prime})-d^{\prime}(x,x^{\prime\prime}))

Definition 4

(multiplicative approximation)
.

Given a target distance function
d
and a threshold
\omega>0
,
we say distance function
d^{\prime}
is triplet equivalent to
d
up to multiplicative factor
1+\omega
if

\displaystyle(\forall x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}),\,d(x,x^{\prime})>(1+\omega)d(x,x^{\prime\prime})\implies\mbox{sign}(d(x,x^{\prime})-d(x,x^{\prime\prime}))=\mbox{sign}(d^{\prime}(x,x^{\prime})-d^{\prime}(x,x^{\prime\prime}))

In this paper, we consider various families of distance functions: distances on finite spaces, Mahalanobis distances, and smooth distances. In each case, we study the query complexity of learning a target distance function upto approximate triplet equivalence, in both the additive and multiplicative sense.

4
Additive approximation of a smooth distance function

In this section, we show how to learn a smooth distance function
d
up to approximate triplet equivalence in the
\omega
-additive sense.

The learner’s strategy is to take an
\epsilon
-cover
\mathcal{C}
of the instance space
{\mathcal{X}}
(with respect to
\ell_{2}
distance) and to learn a distance function that is exactly triplet-equivalent to
d
on
\mathcal{C}
. This distance function is then extended to all of
{\mathcal{X}}
using
\ell_{2}
nearest neighbor.

Learning a distance function on a finite space, upto triplet equivalence

In Theorem

Theorem 5
.

Given any distance function
d:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
on a sample space
{\mathcal{X}}
, and a finite subset
{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\subset{\mathcal{X}}
, a learner can find a distance function
\hat{d}:{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\times{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that is triplet-equivalent to

on
{\mathcal{X}}_{o}

|{\mathcal{X}}_{o}|^{2}\log|{\mathcal{X}}_{o}|

Proof.

outline
:
There are only
O(|{\mathcal{X}}_{o}|^{3})
triplets involving points in
{\mathcal{X}}_{o}
, so at most this many queries are needed. To get a better query complexity, we use a reduction to comparison-based sorting.

Fix any
x_{o}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{o}
. In order to correctly answer all triplet queries of the form
(x_{o},x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
for
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{o}
, it is both necessary and sufficient to know the
ordering
of the distances
\{d(x_{o},x):x\in{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\}
. In other words, we need to sort
{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\setminus\{x_{o}\}
by increasing distance from
x_{o}
. We can do this by simulating a comparison-based sorting algorithm like mergesort. To compare which of
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}
comes first in the ordering, we make the query
(x_{o},x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
. Mergesort asks for at most
|{\mathcal{X}}_{o}|\log_{2}|{\mathcal{X}}_{o}|
such comparisons, and thus we make at most this many triplet queries for
x_{o}
.

Repeating this for all
x_{o}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{o}
, we get a total query complexity of
|{\mathcal{X}}_{o}|^{2}\log_{2}|{\mathcal{X}}_{o}|
.

It is easy to devise a distance function
\hat{d}
that is consistent with the resulting orderings, e.g. set
\hat{d}(x,x^{\prime})=j
if
x^{\prime}
is the
j
-th furthest point (in
{\mathcal{X}}_{o}
) from
x
.
We provide a formal algorithm based on this strategy in Appendix
B
with a detailed proof of Theorem
5
.

4.2
A notion of smooth distance function

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
\epsilon
-cover of the instance space
{\mathcal{X}}
, with respect to
\ell_{2}
distance. Theorem
5
shows how to learn a distance function
\hat{d}
on
\mathcal{C}
that is exactly triplet-equivalent to the target distance function
d
on
\mathcal{C}
. To extend
\hat{d}
to all of
{\mathcal{X}}
, we use
\ell_{2}
nearest neighbor. For any
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
, let
c(x)
be its nearest neighbor in
\mathcal{C}
, that is,
\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\|x-c\|
. We will treat
c(x)
as a stand-in for
x
.

This can be problematic if
d(x,c(x))
is large even though
\|x-c(x)\|\leq\epsilon
. We now introduce a smoothness condition that precludes this possibility.

Definition 6

(
(\alpha,L,\delta)
-smooth)
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We say a distance function
d:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is
(\alpha,L,\delta)
-smooth for
\alpha,L,\delta>0
if for any
x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
such that
||x-x^{\prime}||_{2}\leq\delta
we have

\displaystyle d(x,x^{\prime})\leq L\cdot||x-x^{\prime}||_{2}^{\alpha}

We’ll see that this smoothness condition holds for distance functions with bounded third derivatives.

Assumption 1

(local smoothness)
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
. For
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
, define
f_{x}:{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
by
f_{x}(x^{\prime})=d(x,x^{\prime})
.
Assume that for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
, the function
f_{x}
is
C^{3}
in an open ball around
x
and that its third partial derivatives are bounded in absolute value by a constant
M>0
. Let
\textsf{H}^{*}_{x}
be the Hessian of
f_{x}
at
x
.

The following result follows from the Taylor expansion of
f_{x}
. We will make heavy use of it.

Lemma 7

(Taylor’s theorem)
.

Fix
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
. If distance function
d:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
satisfies Assumption
1
,
then for any
x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
, we have

\displaystyle\bigg\lvert d(x,x^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}(x^{\prime}-x)^{\top}\textsf{H}^{*}_{x}(x^{\prime}-x)\bigg\rvert\leq\frac{Mp^{\frac{3}{2}}}{6}||x-x^{\prime}||_{2}^{3},

where
\textsf{H}^{*}_{x}
denotes the Hessian of the function
d(x,\cdot)
at
x
. Furthermore,
\textsf{H}^{*}_{x}
is a symmetric, positive semidefinite matrix, i.e.
\textsf{H}^{*}_{x}\succeq 0
.

This lemma suggests that for a point
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
, the distance function in the vicinity of
x
is well-approximated by the Mahalanobis distance

d(x,x^{\prime})\approx\frac{1}{2}(x^{\prime}-x)^{T}\textsf{H}^{*}_{x}(x^{\prime}-x).

Let’s take this a step further, by considering

\displaystyle(x^{\prime}-x)^{\top}\textsf{H}^{*}_{x}(x^{\prime}-x)\leq\lambda_{\max}\left({\textsf{H}^{*}_{x}}\right)\cdot||x-x^{\prime}||_{2}^{2}

where
\lambda_{\max}(\textsf{H}^{*}_{x})
denotes the largest eigenvalue of
\textsf{H}^{*}_{x}
. If these eigenvalues are uniformly upper-bounded by a constant
\gamma>0
, then it follows from Lemma
7
that the distance function
d
is
(\alpha,L,\delta)
-smooth for
\alpha=2
,
\delta=1
, and
L=(\gamma/2)+(Mp^{3/2}/6)
.

Learning a smooth distance upto additive approximation

Distance Model:

Assumption 2

Remark 8

Theorem 9
.

Consider a compact subset

5
Multiplicative approximation of a smooth distance function

Here, we aim to learn a target distance
d
up to multiplicative approximation. This is a stronger notion than additive approximation, where the learner only needs to correctly distinguish distances between points
x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
such that the distances differ by at least
\omega
; in particular, small distances can be ignored.
In contrast, in the multiplicative approximation case, the learner must learn to distinguish distances that are arbitrarily small, if they differ by a multiplicative factor of
(1+\omega)
, for any arbitrary but fixed choice of
\omega>0
.

An intuition from the previous section is that if the distances are indeed large, then a global approximation based on a cover with a suitable choice of
\ell_{2}
norm radius should suffice. However, the challenge remains in how to
approximate small local distances
for arbitrary distance functions.
A natural approach is to approximate using local Mahalanobis distance functions.
Indeed, Lemma
7
indicates that under mild conditions, locally,
d
behaves as a Mahalanobis distance function up to some additive error. We show that this local linear approximation can be used to obtain
\omega
-multiplicative approximation.

In this pursuit, we first demonstrate how to query learn local Mahalanobis distances with quadratic dependence on the ambient space dimension.

5.1
Learning a Mahalanobis distance function via queries

Although prior work has studied the problem of learning Mahalanobis distances with triplet and pairwise comparisons in the i.i.d setting
(
Wang et al., 2024
;
Mason et al., 2017
;
Schultz and Joachims, 2003
)
and adaptive embedding learning with pairwise comparisons
(
Jamieson and Nowak, 2011
)
, the query complexity in the query framework is underexplored.
We show that a binary search-based method, along a predefined set of directions in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, can statistically efficiently approximates a target matrix in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\textsf{maha}}
up to a linear scaling, discussed concretely in Algorithm
2
.

First note that positive semidefinite matrices
M
and
cM
, for
c>0
, yield Mahalanobis distance functions
d_{M}
and
d_{cM}
that are triplet-equivalent. Thus we can only hope to recover a target matrix
M^{*}
upto a scaling factor. Moreover, since each query yields a constant number of bits of information, the recovery will necessarily be approximate. We achieve approximation in Frobenius norm: for any desired
\epsilon>0
, we use triplet queries to find a matrix
M\succeq 0
such that
\|M-\tau M^{*}\|\leq\epsilon
, where the constant
\tau
is chosen so that the largest diagonal element of
\tau M^{*}
is 1.

In particular, we first identify the coordinate vector
y\in\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{p}\}
for which
y^{T}M^{*}y
is maximized; this requires
p
triplet queries of the form
(0,e_{i},e_{j})
. We then use queries to obtain
p(p+1)/2-1
other (approximate) linear constraints to return a matrix
M
such that
y^{\top}My=1
and solve the resulting system to get the distance matrix.

We state the claim of the result in Theorem
11
with a detailed proof deferred to Appendix
D
. In what follows,
\kappa(M^{*})
is the condition number of matrix
M^{*}
and we assume it has been normalized so that its largest diagonal entry is 1.

Theorem 11
.

Fix an input space
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, an error threshold
\epsilon>0
, and a target matrix
M^{*}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\textsf{maha}}
. Then, Algorithm
2
run with
\epsilon_{\textsf{alg}}=\frac{\epsilon}{2p^{2}}
outputs a distance function
d_{M}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\textsf{maha}}
such that

\displaystyle||\tau M^{*}-M||_{F}\leq\epsilon

using
\frac{p(p+1)}{2}\log\left({\frac{2p^{2}\kappa(M^{*})^{2}}{\epsilon}}\right)+p
triplet queries, where
\tau
is a scaling factor so that
\max_{i}(\tau M^{*})_{ii}=1
.

Proof.

outline
: There are
\frac{p(p+1)}{2}
degrees of freedom for a given symmetric matrix. If these degrees of freedom are correctly approximated, it becomes possible to learn the matrix within a controlled error. This is the central idea of the proof of the theorem, where we construct a basis
\mathcal{B}
of rank-1 matrices
\{u_{i}u_{i}^{T}\}
for the space
\text{Sym}(\mathbb{R}^{p\times p})
. One of these
u_{i}
’s is the coordinate vector
y
described above.
For each remaining
u_{i}
, we use binary search and triplet queries to approximately find the value
c_{i}^{*}=(u_{i}^{T}M^{*}u_{i})/(y^{T}M^{*}y)
; in particular, we find
\hat{c}_{i}=c_{i}^{*}\pm O(\epsilon)
using
\log\kappa(M^{*})^{2}/\epsilon
queries. We then solve the linear system
u_{i}^{T}Mu_{i}=\hat{c}_{i}y^{T}My
to get
M
and project the result to the semidefinite cone
2
2

2

PSD cone is at most distance
\epsilon
(in Frobenius norm) from
M
since
M^{*}
lies in it.
. To bound the Frobenius norm between
M^{*}
and
M
, we analyze the condition number of the linear system of equations.
∎

Distance Model:

Consider a distance function model defined for a threshold
\theta

\widehat{\mathcal{M}}(\theta)=\{(d,(\mathcal{C},d_{\mathcal{C}}),(\mathcal{U}_{c},d_{M_{c}})_{c\in\mathcal{C}},\ell_{2})\}

d(x,y)=\begin{cases}d_{\mathcal{C}}(c(x),c(y))+\theta,&\text{if }d_{M_{c(x)}}(x,y)>\theta,\\

where

(ties are broken with smallest

(1+\omega)
-multiplicative factor within
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}(\theta)
.

Before stating the main result, we discuss two regularity conditions on the curvature and arbitrarily small distances of a distance function.

Assumption 4

(Hessian continuity)
.

Consider a distance function
d:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq}
that is
C^{2}
in its second argument. We say
d
is Hessian continuous if there exists a scalar
L>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}},\quad||\textsf{H}^{*}_{x}-\textsf{H}^{*}_{x^{\prime}}||_{F}\leq L\cdot||x-x^{\prime}||_{2}.

Note that the assumption above stipulates the smoothness of the Hessians around a sample. More concretely, it asserts smoothness in the first argument of a distance function which does not follow from the Assumption
1
which only asserts smoothness in the first argument with bounded partial derivatives.

An immediate consequence of this result is that if
x
and
x^{\prime}
are close to each other then the distances wrt sample
x^{\prime}
in the neighborhood of
x
can be computed using the Hessian information at
x
(see Appendix
E
for further discussion).

For a general distance function
d
, it is possible that samples far apart in
\ell_{2}
distance are infinitesimally close (or even 0) under
d
, which renders both global and local approximation schemes ineffective. For example, consider a distance function
d(x,y)=||x-y||_{2}^{2}\cdot e^{-\tan||x-y||_{2}}
, where
{\mathcal{X}}=\pi/2\cdot\mathbb{S}_{2}
. In this case, as
||x-y||_{2}\to\pi/2
we get
d(x,y)\to 0
. Now, no bounded choice of
\epsilon>0
for an
\epsilon
-cover can even yield an
\omega
-additive approximation for small enough
\omega>0
.

To avoid such scenarios, we impose the following condition on the distance function.

Assumption 5

(non-zero distances)
.

For any
\delta>0
,
\Delta_{\delta}:=\liminf_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\{d(x,y):\|x-y\|_{2}>\delta\}
is strictly positive, i.e.,
\Delta_{\delta}>0
.

Now, we state the main claim of the section with the proof detailed in Appendix
E
.

Theorem 14
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a compact subset. Consider a distance function
d:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that satisfies Assumption
1
(locally smooth), Assumption
2
(triangle inequality), Assumption
3
(bounded eigenvalues), Assumption
4
(Hessian continuity), and Assumption
5
(non-zero distances) for constants
\mathchorus{e},\mathchorus{E},M,L>0
.

Then, Algorithm
3
outputs a distance function
d^{\prime}\in\widehat{\mathcal{M}}(\theta)
, triplet-equivalent to
d
up to
\omega
-multiplicative approximation, with a query complexity of at most
N^{2}\log N+Np^{2}\log\frac{1}{\vartheta}
, where
N=\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{X},\epsilon,\ell_{2})
,
\epsilon\leq\frac{C\cdot w_{1}}{p^{\frac{3}{2}}}
is the covering radius, and
\xi\leq{D\cdot w_{2}}
is the error threshold for local Mahalanobis distance functions. The parameters
w_{1},w_{2},\theta,\vartheta
are bounded as:

•

w_{1}:=\omega^{\frac{3}{2}}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega<1\}}+\frac{C_{1}}{\sqrt{\omega}}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega\geq 1\}}

•

w_{2}:=\omega\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega<1\}}+C_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega\geq 1\}}

•

\theta\leq\frac{4C^{\prime}\cdot w_{3}}{p^{3}},\quad w_{3}:=\omega^{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega<1\}}+\frac{C_{3}}{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega\geq 1\}}

•

\vartheta\leq\frac{C^{\prime\prime}\cdot w_{4}}{p^{2}},\quad w_{4}:=\omega\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega<1\}}+C_{4}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega\geq 1\}}

with constants
D,C,C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime},C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}
dependent on
\mathchorus{e},\mathchorus{E},\Delta_{\delta},M,L
, with
\delta\leq\frac{3\mathchorus{e}}{2Mp^{\frac{3}{2}}}
.

Proof.

outline
:
The key to the proof is to carefully describe the local behavior of the distance function
d
via local Mahalanobis distance functions, allowing for the computation of small distances with bounded error. Using Assumption
1
, we show that in a small neighborhood around any sample,
d
is both
strongly convex
and
smooth
, meaning that its growth rate can be measured and depends on the eigenvalue bounds
\mathchorus{e}
and
\mathchorus{E}
.
We use this information to design a threshold for switching between global distance function on a cover and local Mahalanobis distance functions.

The size of the neighborhood where local approximation with small error is feasible depends on how small
d(x,\cdot)
can be around a given sample
x\in\mathcal{X}
and the local curvature of
d
. As stated in Assumption
5
,
\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\{d(x,x^{\prime}):x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus B_{2}(x,\delta)\}
is lower-bounded by a positive constant for any fixed
\delta>0
. The key idea is to select
\delta
within the zone of strong convexity and smoothness, leading to the result that, for a sufficiently small
\hat{\beta}
and samples
x,y\in{\mathcal{X}}
, if
(y-x)^{\top}\textsf{H}_{c(x)}(y-x)>4\hat{\beta}
, then
d(x,y)
is also lower-bounded by
c^{\prime}\hat{\beta}
, where
c^{\prime}
depends on the smoothness parameters. This guides when to switch between the global distance function and local Mahalanobis distance functions. Since the global distance function is triplet-equivalent to
d
, reducing samples to their centers for large distances ensures the multiplicative approximation holds.

For distances within the
\delta
-ball, Algorithm
3
suggests using the uncentered distance wrt the Hessian at the closest center to the sample of interest. This approach is reliable because the Hessian continuity, as stated in Assumption
4
, ensures that the local distance computation remains accurate (up to small controllable error) within the neighborhood.
∎

4
, we stated a bound on the query complexity of learning a distance function on finite samples. Here, we first provide a procedure for this in Algorithm
4
, followed by the proof of Theorem
5
.

As stated in Theorem
5
, we assume that
\mathcal{X}_{o}\subset{\mathcal{X}}
is a finite set of objects or samples. A
finite sample distance function

d^{\prime}:\mathcal{X}_{o}\times\mathcal{X}_{o}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
assigns a non-negative real number to each pair of objects in
\mathcal{X}_{o}
, capturing the notion of dissimilarity or distance between them. Since

is defined on a discrete set of objects it is rather straight-forward to query learn it. Essentially, Theorem

states that on the finite sample space
{\mathcal{X}}_{o}
, using triplet queries dependent quadratically on the size of the space a learner can sufficiently find a

that is triplet equivalent to

.

Now, we restate the theorem and provide the proof.

Theorem
5
.

Given any distance function
d:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
on a sample space
{\mathcal{X}}
, for any finite subset
{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\subset{\mathcal{X}}
, a learner can query to learn a finite sample distance function
\hat{d}:{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\times{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that is triplet equivalent to
d
using at most
|{\mathcal{X}}_{o}|^{2}\log|{\mathcal{X}}_{o}|
triplet queries.

Proof.

For any fixed center
x\in{\mathcal{X}}_{o}
, the learner determines the ordering of the remaining points by increasing
distance from
x
:

\displaystyle d(x,y_{1})\leq d(x,y_{2})\leq\ldots\leq d(x,y_{|{\mathcal{X}}_{o}|-1}),\text{ where }\left\{y_{i}\right\}_{i\in[|{\mathcal{X}}_{o}|-1]}={\mathcal{X}}_{o}\setminus\{x\}

over
{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\setminus\{x\}
, yielding a permutation
\sigma_{x}:{\mathcal{X}}_{o}\setminus\{x\}\to[|{\mathcal{X}}_{0}|-1]
such that for all

\operatorname{sgn}\left({d(x,y)-d(x,y^{\prime})}\right)=\operatorname{sgn}\left({\sigma_{x}(y)-\sigma_{x}(y^{\prime})}\right)
.

Using comparison-based merge sorting, the learner can find
\sigma_{x}
with a query complexity of
(|{\mathcal{X}}_{o}|-1)\log(|{\mathcal{X}}_{o}|-1)
. Since there are
|{\mathcal{X}}_{o}|
elements and the ranking for each center is independent of the others, the learner can find

restricted to
{\mathcal{X}}_{o}
up to triplet equivalence with at most

Theorem 17

\displaystyle|R_{a,2}(\mathbf{h})|\leq\frac{Mp^{\frac{3}{2}}}{3!}\cdot||\mathbf{h}||^{3}_{2}.

E.1
Close approximation of a Hessian matrix

Using Algorithm
2
, Algorithm
5
, and Algorithm
6
we demonstrated how to learn the Mahalanobis distance function in both noiseless and noisy settings. While these approximations are generally tight, they may deviate by a factor, as stated in Theorem
12
. In the following proof, we show how this factor influences the eigenvalues of the approximated Mahalanobis distance function.

The result is stated below, followed by the proof.

Lemma 20
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a subset. Consider a distance function
d:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that satisfies Assumption
1
and Assumption
3
with scalars
\mathchorus{e},\mathchorus{E},M>0
. For a given sample
c\in{\mathcal{X}}
, assume
\textsf{H}_{c}
is an approximation of the Hessian matrix
\textsf{H}^{*}_{c}
such that
\|\tau_{c}H_{c}^{*}-H_{c}\|_{F}\leq\xi
for some scalar
\xi>0
(where
\tau_{c}
is chosen as per Algorithm
6
). Then, we have the following bounds if
\xi\leq\min(1,\mathchorus{e}/2\mathchorus{E})
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\textsf{min}}(\textsf{H}_{c})\geq\frac{\tau_{c}\mathchorus{e}}{2},\quad\lambda_{\textsf{max}}(\textsf{H}_{c})\leq 2\tau_{c}\mathchorus{E}

(E.4)

Proof.

First, we note
\tau_{c}=\frac{1}{y^{\top}\textsf{H}^{*}_{c}y}
, i.e.,
\tau_{c}
is a scalar corresponding to the Mahalanobis distance function
\textsf{H}^{*}_{c}
that scales the quadratic product
y^{\top}\textsf{H}^{*}_{c}y=1
(see proofs of Theorem
11
and Theorem
12
in Appendix
D
).

Given that
||\tau_{c}\cdot\textsf{H}^{*}_{c}-\textsf{H}_{c}||_{F}\leq\xi
using Weyl’s inequality we have the following bound on the eigenvalues of
\tau_{c}\textsf{H}^{*}_{c}
and
\textsf{H}_{c}

\displaystyle\forall(i\in\left[p\right]),\,\,\,|\tau_{c}\lambda_{i}(\textsf{H}^{*}_{c})-\lambda_{i}(\textsf{H}_{c})|\leq\xi

Thus we get the following desired bounds

\displaystyle|\lambda_{\textsf{min}}(\tau_{c}\textsf{H}^{*}_{c})-\lambda_{\textsf{min}}(\textsf{H}_{c})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tau_{c}\mathchorus{e}}{2},\quad\text{implying}\quad\lambda_{\textsf{min}}(\textsf{H}_{c})\geq\frac{\tau_{c}\mathchorus{e}}{2},

(E.5)

\displaystyle|\lambda_{\textsf{max}}(\tau_{c}\textsf{H}^{*}_{c})-\lambda_{\textsf{max}}(\textsf{H}_{c})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tau_{c}\mathchorus{e}}{2},\quad\text{implying}\quad 2\tau_{c}\mathchorus{E}\geq\lambda_{\textsf{max}}(\textsf{H}_{c}).

(E.6)

This proves the claim in
Eq.
(
E.4
) of the lemma.
∎

E.2
Well-behaved properties of a smooth distance function

In this subsection, we provide several key properties of a smooth distance function that satisfies the conditions in Theorem
14
. The first result, as stated in Lemma
23
, establishes the strong convexity (see Definition
21
) and smoothness (see Definition
22
) of

d
in a small neighborhood of a given sample, ensuring predictable behavior and enabling us to measure the rate of growth of the function. The second result, as stated in Lemma

, demonstrates how local Mahalanobis approximations, using Algorithm

, produce lower bounds on distance values within a small neighborhood. To visually understand the proofs, we illustrate them in Fig.

We begin by introducing the concepts of strong convexity and smoothness for a differentiable function. These notions allow us to study the local properties of a distance function
d
that satisfies Assumption
1
.

Definition 21

(Pointwise
\mu
-strong convexity)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function, where
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We say
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex at
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
if

f(x)-f(x_{0})\geq\nabla f(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})+\mu\cdot\|x-x_{0}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{S}.

Definition 22

(Pointwise
\gamma
-smoothness)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function, where
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We say
f
is
\gamma
-smooth at
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
if

f(x)-f(x_{0})\leq\nabla f(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})+\gamma\cdot\|x-x_{0}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{S}.

We can apply these concepts to examine the local behavior of a distance function
d
that satisfies Assumption
1
. Fixing a sample
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
, we use Taylor’s approximation from
Eq.
(
C.4
) to rewrite

where

is within a small neighborhood of

wrt
\ell_{2}
norm as

d(x,x+h)=\frac{1}{2}h^{\top}\textsf{H}^{*}_{x}h+R_{x,2}(h),

where
d(x,x)=0
and
\partial_{x}d(x,\cdot)=0
. To bound
R_{x,2}(h)
, we rely on Lemma
7
.

Next, we present Lemma
23
, with the proof outlined in the subsequent discussion.

Lemma 23
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a subset. Consider a distance function
d:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that satisfies Assumption
1
and Assumption

with scalars
\mathchorus{e},\mathchorus{E},M>0
. Then for any sample

the function

\frac{\mathchorus{e}}{4}
-strongly convex and
\mathchorus{E}
-smooth in a neighborhood of

Proof.

We proof the claim of the statement using the Taylor’s approximation as shown in Lemma
7
.

First, note that for any fixed sample
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
, and
h
in a small neighborhood of
x
we have the following

\displaystyle d(x,x+h)\geq\frac{1}{2}h^{\top}\textsf{H}^{*}_{x}h-\frac{Mp^{\frac{3}{2}}}{6}\cdot||h||^{3}_{2}

Now, if
||h||_{2}
is small enough, i.e. smaller than
\frac{3\mathchorus{e}}{2Mp^{\frac{3}{2}}}
, then we have

\displaystyle d(x,x+h)\geq\frac{\mathchorus{e}}{2}||h||^{2}_{2}-\frac{\mathchorus{e}}{4}||h||^{2}_{2}=\frac{\mathchorus{e}}{4}||h||^{2}_{2}

(E.7)

Thus,

is
\frac{\mathchorus{e}}{4}
-strongly convex at

is the smallest eigenvalue for the Hessian at any arbitrary sample

is smooth, i.e.,

\displaystyle d(x,x+h)\leq\frac{1}{2}h^{\top}\textsf{H}^{*}_{x}h+\frac{Mp^{\frac{3}{2}}}{6}\cdot||h||^{3}_{2}\leq\frac{\mathchorus{E}}{2}||h||^{2}_{2}+\frac{\mathchorus{e}}{4}||h||^{2}_{2}\leq\mathchorus{E}||h||^{2}_{2}

(E.8)

This completes the proof of the statement.
∎

Lemma 24
