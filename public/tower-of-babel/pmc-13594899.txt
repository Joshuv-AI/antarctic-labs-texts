Effects of pigeon pea-grass intercropped pastures on enteric methane emissions, carcass traits and meat quality in Nellore cattle
Authors: Suelen Cristina dos Santos Carvalho, Althieres José Furtado, Vanessa Cristina Francisco, Rolando Pasquini Neto, Gabriele Voltareli da Silva, Paulo Henrique Mazza Rodrigues, Patrícia Perondi Anchão Oliveira, Alexandre Berndt, Renata Tieko Nassu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Pigeon pea effects on Nellore methane emissions and beef quality were evaluated.•Intercropping reduced methane emission intensity per kg carcass by over 40%.•Pigeon pea intercropping increased stocking rates and final carcass weights.•The system enhanced high-value beef cuts yield without altering meat quality.•Pigeon pea-grass systems are a viable strategy for sustainable beef production.Keywords: Cajanus cajan, SF6 tracer technique, Bos indicus, Beef, Commercial cuts, Greenhouse gases

Introduction

Brazil is the world’s largest beef producer, with an estimated production of 12.35 million tonnes in 2025 (USDA, 2025). Brazilian beef production is largely based on extensive grazing systems, many of which present some degree of degradation, affecting productive efficiency and increasing enteric methane (CH4) emissions per unit of animal product (Mombach et al., 2016; Oliveira et al., 2020; Santos et al., 2022).

Nutritional strategies aimed at improving the diet quality of grazing cattle have received increasing attention, including the use of legumes intercropped with tropical grasses, a practice that increases protein intake in the diet, improves animal performance, and reduces enteric methane emissions (Boddey et al., 2020; Homem et al., 2024; Portugal et al., 2023).

Pigeon pea (Cajanus cajan L. Millsp. BRS Mandarim) is a legume that represents a significant source of crude protein, essential minerals, water-soluble vitamins, and amino acids (Olagunju et al., 2018). Its inclusion in grazing systems increases dietary protein supply (approximately above 20% on a dry matter basis) and improves the nutritional quality of the diet consumed by animals, particularly during periods of the year with lower forage availability and quality (Oliveira et al., 2017; Tupy et al., 2023).

Mixed legume–grass pastures provide greater nutritional value than grass-only pastures (Fruet et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2023). Despite these nutritional benefits, the resulting changes in cattle diet composition may influence animal performance, carcass traits, and meat quality (Santos et al., 2021).

Although previous studies have investigated the effects of legumes in animal production systems (Andrade et al., 2021; Burggraaf et al., 2020; Fruet et al., 2018; Gusha et al., 2015; Homem et al., 2024; Jacondino et al., 2022; Lim & Choi, 2023; Przywitowski et al., 2016; Santana et al., 2017; Turner et al., 2014), the novelty of this study consists of an integrated evaluation of the effects of pigeon pea intercropping, linking enteric methane emission dynamics to the processing outcomes of the beef produced. Unlike conventional research focused strictly on general animal performance, the approach of this study encompasses variables ranging from enteric methane emissions expressed per hectare, including an analysis of carcass traits, emissions per carcass equivalent, and edible portion, to a detailed assessment of commercial cuts and the quality of aged beef obtained from cattle raised on intercropped pastures.

It was hypothesized that the inclusion of this legume in intercropped pastures may influence enteric methane emissions, as well as the carcass traits, commercial cuts yield, and meat quality of cattle. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of intercropping pigeon pea in Urochloa pastures on enteric CH4 emissions expressed per hectare, carcass equivalent, and edible portion, as well as on carcass traits, commercial cuts yield, and meat quality of Nellore cattle.

Material and methods

Location, treatments and experimental design

The study was conducted at Embrapa Southeastern Livestock, located in São Carlos, São Paulo, Brazil, from July 2020 to September 2022. The experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Use and Care Ethics Committee (CEUA) under protocol number 05/2016 of Embrapa.

The experimental pastures, comprising degraded (DEG), recovered (REC), and intercropped with pigeon pea [Cajanus cajan (L.) Millsp. cv. BRS Mandarim] (MIX) systems, were originally established in 1996 with Urochloa spp. and continuously managed since then. Over time, in DEG, lack of soil nutrient correction and maintenance led to a progressive decline in forage vigor, allowing extensive encroachment by opportunistic species, primarily Paspalum notatum Flüggé. Following the specific criteria proposed by Oliveira (2007), a pasture is classified as degraded when invasive species cover contiguous canopy areas larger than 2 m2. Consequently, the DEG pasture system was purposefully maintained without any chemical fertilization or soil correction throughout the trial (from July 2020 to September 2022), effectively replicating the typical extensive grazing management found across traditional Brazilian cattle farms.

Conversely, the REC and MIX pasture systems underwent soil amendment and maintenance. The REC system received chemical nitrogen fertilization totaling 200 kg of N-urea per hectare per year, split across five separate applications during the rainy season. Both REC and MIX pastures were also amended with superphosphate and potassium fertilizations according to regional recommendations (Costa & Queiroz, 2017). The MIX system involved mechanical row-sowing of pigeon pea into the existing grass canopy after it had been grazed down to a uniform 15 cm residual height. During that experimental cycle, the initial pigeon pea population was estimated at 180.000 plants/ha, which gradually decreased to approximately 75.000 plants/ha by the conclusion of the study.

Twenty-seven (n = 27) uncastrated male Nellore steers (Bos taurus indicus), with an initial body weight of 221 ± 7 kg and aged 8 ± 1 months, were distributed across these three pasture systems: 1) DEG, managed extensively with a Urochloa decumbens Stapf cv. Basilisk and Urochloa brizantha mixture; 2) REC, established with a managed mixture of U. decumbens cv. Basilisk and U. brizantha (Hochst ex A. Rich) Stapf cv. Marandu; and 3) MIX, consisting of U. decumbens cv. Basilisk and U. brizantha cv. Marandu intercropped with pigeon pea [Cajanus cajan (L.) Millsp. cv. BRS Mandarim].

The experiment followed a completely randomised design across the three grazing systems, with each treatment replicated in three independent grazing units (1.25 ha each), totaling nine units across an experimental area of 11.25 ha. Each grazing unit contained three tester animals. The experimental unit was defined according to the level at which each response variable was obtained. The grazing unit was considered the experimental unit for stocking rate, whereas the individual tester animal was considered the experimental unit for enteric CH4 emissions, carcass traits, commercial cuts, and meat quality because these variables were measured separately in each animal.

Within each grazing unit, cattle were managed under continuous grazing using the “put-and-take” method (Mott & Lucas, 1952) to adjust stocking rates. For the REC and MIX treatments, regular entry and removal of regulator animals ensured the canopy was strictly maintained within a target intermediate height range of 15–30 cm, optimizing forage availability and structural quality (Costa & Queiroz, 2017). In contrast, the DEG system was exempted from any canopy height thresholds due to the lack of forage vigor, sparse biomass accumulation, and the dominance of low-stature Paspalum species that rarely exceeded 15 cm.

According to Furtado et al. (2023), the formulation and chemical composition of the strategic supplements varied across seasons. During the rainy season (October to March), all experimental animals remained under field conditions and received a powdered mineral mixture ad libitum, formulated with equal parts (500 g/kg as-fed) of NaCl and a commercial mineral mixture, yielding an ash content of 886.9 g/kg on a dry-matter basis. This baseline mineral supplement was also maintained ad libitum for the MIX group throughout the dry season (April to September). Conversely, during the dry season, cattle in the DEG and REC systems were transitioned to a strategic mineral-energetic-protein supplement to counteract structural and nutritional forage scarcity. This winter supplement was formulated with 480 g/kg of ground corn, 220 g/kg of urea, 150 g/kg of NaCl, and 150 g/kg of the mineral mixture (as-fed basis), providing an estimated chemical composition of 654.4 g/kg crude protein, 99.0 g/kg non-protein nitrogen, 40.2 g/kg neutral detergent fiber, and 283.5 g/kg ash on a dry-matter basis.

Stocking rate measurement and adjustment

To measure and adjust the stocking rate in the experimental areas, the number of animals and their individual body weights were recorded every 28 days using a hydraulic chute equipped with an integrated electronic scale (Parede Móvel Hidráulico/idBeck 3.0 – BechHouser®, 2009). The stocking rate was expressed as animal units (AU) per hectare, assuming that one AU corresponds to 450 kg of body weight, as commonly adopted in Brazilian pasture-based systems (Furtado et al., 2023). The stocking rate was calculated by dividing the total body weight of all animals present in each grazing unit, including both tester and regulator animals, by the standard animal unit and the grazing-unit area (ha). Regulator animals were not included among the 27 experimental animals or used for enteric CH4, carcass, or meat quality measurements; they were periodically introduced into or removed from the grazing units solely to adjust stocking rate, and their body weights were included in the calculation because they contributed to grazing pressure.

Measurement of enteric CH4

The sulphur hexafluoride (SF6) tracer gas technique was used to quantify CH4 emissions from the cattle (Johnson & Johnson, 1995; Johnson et al., 1994; Primavesi et al., 2004) as adapted by Berndt et al. (2014).

Initially, an adaptation period of 14 days was performed for animal acclimation to the sampling halters, during which no gas sampling was conducted. Following this adaptation period and 72 h prior to the first methane sampling, SF6 permeation tubes were orally inserted and positioned into the mid-reticulo-rumen. This process ensured gas equilibration within the rumen environment of the animals. Subsequently, to capture the gases exhaled through the nostrils and mouth, an external capillary sampling tubing was fitted onto the halter and connected to the vacuum tubular canisters positioned around the animals’ necks. Each tubular canister remained in place for 24 h to accumulate the exhaled gases, being replaced every 24 h. The gas sampling was conducted for five consecutive days, a period required to obtain a representative composite sample of the methane emitted by each animal (Berndt et al., 2014). This sampling protocol was carried out during the final weeks of January (representing the rainy season) and June (representing the dry season), periods that captured contrasting seasonal conditions of the production system, particularly regarding variations in forage availability and enteric methane emissions.

Background tubular canisters were suspended on the perimeter fences of each paddock at a height similar to the animals’ breathing zone, these were positioned away from the livestock to monitor ambient CH4 and SF6 concentrations representative of the pasture environment.

In the laboratory, gas samples were analysed by gas chromatography using an Agilent HP-6890 gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA) and a Shimadzu GC-2014 instrument (Shimadzu Scientific Instruments, Columbia, MD, USA). The CH4 emission rate was calculated according to the equations described by Westberg et al. (1998).

Slaughter, carcass traits and meat quality analyses

After 24 months under experimental field conditions, the animals, with an average body weight of 546 ± 8 kg and aged 35 ± 1 months, were fasted for 16 h with access to water ad libitum (Brasil, 2021). Subsequently, the animals were transported and slaughtered in a commercial abattoir following Brazilian guidelines and sanitary regulations for inspection of animal products, carcass grading and the obtaining of commercial beef cuts (Brasil, 2017).

Immediately at the end of the slaughter line, and before washing the carcasses for storage in the chilling chambers, the right and left half-carcasses were weighed individually. Their weights were summed to obtain the hot carcass weight in kilograms (kg) (Gomes, 2021). Carcass yield (CY) was calculated as the ratio between the hot carcass weight and the animal’s weight.

Subcutaneous fat thickness (SFT) was measured between the 12th and 13th ribs, at the distal third of the Longissimus muscle, from the edge adjacent to the vertebral column (Brasil, 2004). Measurements of SFT were performed using a digital calliper and classified according to fat finish (Brasil, 2004).

Rib eye area (REA) was determined at the section between the 12th and 13th ribs of the carcasses, using a transparent plastic grid overlaid on the Longissimus muscle. The area was expressed in cm2 by summing the area of the known rectangle (40–60 cm2) and the external points contained within the muscle.

Twenty-four hours post-mortem, 2.5 cm-thick steaks of the Longissimus thoracis muscle were collected between the 12th and 13th ribs from the left half-carcass of each animal.

The samples were divided into three distinct groups for the evaluation of meat quality parameters: 1) Day 0, with samples assessed immediately without ageing; 2) Day 7; and 3) Day 14, with the latter two groups subjected to ageing in a cold chamber maintained at 0–2 °C.

For the determination of muscle pH, five readings were carried out at different points on each steak using a calibrated digital pH meter (Testo®) with pH 4 and 7 buffer solutions. The final value was calculated as the mean of the readings obtained.

Instrumental colour measurement of the samples was performed using a portable Hunter Lab Miniscan XE colorimeter, with standard illuminant D65 and settings configured to X = 80.1, Y = 85.0, Z = 89.9, observer angle of 10 °, and 30 mm aperture to assess variations within the CIELab colour space, evaluating lightness (L*), red colour intensity (a*), and yellow colour intensity (b*) (King et al., 2023). The instrument was calibrated against a white reference plate (Serial no 7050). For myoglobin oxidation, the steaks were removed from vacuum packaging and exposed for thirty minutes prior to measurement. The final value was calculated as the mean of triplicate readings.

Water-holding capacity (WHC) was determined according to Hamm (1986), using 2 g meat samples cut transversely to the muscle fibres. The samples were placed between filter paper and then positioned between acrylic plates, where they were subjected to a pressure of 10 kg for 5 min. Water loss by exudation was calculated as the ratio between the sample weight after pressing and the initial sample weight, multiplied by 100 and expressed as a percentage.

Cooking loss (CL) was determined as the difference between the initial weight of the raw steaks and their weight after cooking to an internal temperature of 75 °C in a preheated Tedesco® combi oven at 180 °C, followed by cooling and draining of the exudate. Measurements were performed using a BEL Engineering semi-analytical balance, model Mark 4100, with 0.01 g precision.

After cooking, the steaks were stored under refrigeration at 5 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, 7–8 sub-samples were obtained from each steak in the form of cylinders with a diameter of 1.27 cm (half an inch), longitudinally aligned with the muscle fibres, using a coring device attached to an electric drill for shear force analysis (Wheeler et al., 1997). A calibrated Stable Micro Systems texture analyser, model TA.XT Plus, equipped with a Warner-Bratzler blade of 1.016 mm thickness and 50 kg capacity, was used with the Texture Expert software. The texture analyser was calibrated as follows: test speed 200 mm/min; post-test speed 2400 mm/min; distance 40 mm; calibration weight 10 kg. Sub-samples were placed on the texture analyser with the muscle fibers perpendicular to the Warner-Bratzler blade, and each cylinder was cut once. The maximum force was recorded for each cylinder from the curve generated by the Texture Expert software, and the mean values of the cylinders for each sample were expressed in Newtons (N).

Determination of enteric CH4 emission per carcass and edible portion

To express emission intensity across different production systems, CH4 emissions were calculated on an individual and per-hectare basis, adapting the structural framework proposed by Sakamoto et al. (2020), as follows: CH4 emission per hot carcass final weight (g/kg-1 carcass), g/CH4 emission per hot carcass weight gain (g/g-1 carcass day-1 and kg/kg-1 carcass ha-1), CH4 emission per empty body weight (g/kg-1), and CH4 emission per edible carcass portion (g/kg-1 carcass). Additionally, emission intensity was expressed in kilograms of CO2 equivalent per kilogram of carcass (CO2/CARC, kg/kg-1 carcass). For this environmental metric, cumulative daily enteric CH4 emissions were converted to CO2 equivalent by applying a Global Warming Potential (GWP100 for biogenic CH4) factor of 27, in compliance with the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) guidelines (IPCC, 2021).

Although CH4 emissions are commonly expressed as absolute emissions (g animal-1 day-1), expressing them in functional units provides a more comprehensive assessment of production system efficiency. Thus, CH4 emissions were measured using the SF6 tracer technique and integrated over the experimental period and subsequently expressed relative to production outputs by dividing total emissions by carcass gain, edible portion, or unit area, as applied by da Silva et al. (2026).

Statistical analyses

Variables measured without repeated measures

Carcass traits and meat quality variables measured only once per animal were analysed using SAS® 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). For outlier detection was performed at the replicate level based on studentized residuals obtained from PROC GLM and PROC UNIVARIATE. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data were analysed using the PROC MIXED procedure. Grazing system was included as a fixed effect, and animal was considered the experimental unit. The animal was considered the experimental unit because all carcass and meat quality measurements were obtained from individual animals at slaughter, and not from paddock-level responses. Least-squares means were compared using the PDIFF option, and differences were considered significant at P ≤ 0.05.

Variables measured with repeated measures over ageing time

Variables evaluated during the ageing process (0, 7, and 14 days) were analysed using SAS® 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA, 2018). For variables analysed in triplicate, outlier detection was performed at the laboratory replicate level based on studentized residuals obtained from PROC GLM and PROC UNIVARIATE. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data were analysed using the PROC MIXED procedure. Grazing system was included as a fixed effect, ageing time was considered a repeated measure, and animal was included as a random effect and as the subject of repeated measurements. The interaction between grazing system and ageing time was tested and, when significant, simple effects were evaluated using the SLICE option. Different covariance structures were evaluated, and the final model was selected according to the lowest corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICC) (Wang & Goonewardene, 2004). Least-squares means were compared using the PDIFF option, and differences were considered significant at P ≤ 0.05.

Edible portion and commercial cuts

The edible portion of the carcass (CEP, kg carcass-1) was estimated by linear regression using PROC REG in SAS Software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA, 2018), following an approach similar to that described by Neto et al. (2025) to obtain the three primary cut categories [hindquarter (HEP, kg carcass-1), forequarter (FEP, kg carcass-1), and ribs] (Supplementary Table).

Fat trimmings (FT, kg carcass-1), standardised to approximately 3 mm thickness in commercial cuts, and bones (kg carcass-1) were recorded separately.

Regarding enteric emissions per unit of product, a significant difference was observed for hot carcass weight gain expressed per hectare (P < 0.05). The MIX system showed reduced methane emissions per hectare, as did the REC system compared with DEG emissions. Similarly, methane related to the edible carcass portion (P < 0.05) was lower in the MIX and REC systems compared with the DEG system.

The emission intensity in kg of CO2 equivalent per kg of carcass was also affected by the pasture system (P < 0.05), being higher in DEG compared with MIX and REC. When expressed per hectare (P < 0.05), the DEG system showed substantially higher values than MIX and REC, representing increases of 87.6% and 61.4%, respectively.

Enteric methane emissions expressed as daily methane production (CH4, g d-1), methane per unit of hot carcass weight, and methane per unit of edible carcass portion were not significantly affected by the pasture systems (P > 0.05).

Discussion

The grazing systems directly affected productive performance, the stocking rate, and CH4 emissions (Table 1). The higher stocking rates observed in MIX and REC compared with DEG are associated with increased forage availability and quality, which enhance the carrying capacity and production efficiency (Boddey et al., 2020; Pezzopane et al., 2024), consequently resulting in higher carcass weight gain.

Seasonal supplementation was also an integral component of these production systems and may have contributed to the observed responses. During the dry season, the mineral-energy-protein supplement provided to DEG and REC was intended to compensate for forage scarcity and lower nutritional quality, whereas MIX continued to receive only the mineral supplement. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as responses to the integrated pasture-based production systems rather than as isolated effects of pasture composition.

Improvements in forage quality under the MIX system contributed to mitigating CH4 emissions relative to carcass gain when compared with the DEG system (Table 1). This is in agreement with Furtado et al. (2023; 2024), who demonstrated the potential of legume-based pasture systems to reduce emission intensity in beef production. Such mitigation is particularly relevant in the Brazilian context, given that in 2020, enteric methane emissions from livestock reached 54.8 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent (SEEG, 2020).

The effect of forage quality was also evident in the edible portion of the carcass, which exhibited lower methane emissions in the MIX and REC systems compared with DEG. This emphasises that both the inclusion of pigeon pea and pasture fertilisation promoted higher carcass yields (Sakamoto et al., 2020) and a reduction in relative emissions.

Emission intensity (kg of CO2 equivalent per kg of carcass) was significantly affected by the pasture system (Table 1), being lower in MIX than in DEG. This reduction highlights the mitigation potential of sustainable pasture intensification; the MIX system resulted in greater meat production per animal, which in turn led to lower methane emission per kg of hot carcass and per kg of edible portion, with reductions exceeding 40% relative to DEG.

The higher values of empty body weight and final carcass weight in MIX compared with DEG (Table 2) indicate better nutrient utilisation and greater muscle tissue deposition, suggesting that the inclusion of the legume improved the nutritional quality of the diet and animal weight gain, in agreement with results observed in sustainable intensification systems (Oliveira et al., 2018; Sakamoto et al., 2020) without compromising carcass traits.

Carcass yield values above 54% are considered indicative of good carcass yield according to Gomes (2021), although the national average for Zebu carcasses is around 51–54%. In the present study, the average carcass yield across all treatments was 51%.

Subcutaneous fat acts as a thermal insulator during chilling, preventing weight loss due to dehydration and the darkening of the cuts caused by cold exposure. Thus, carcasses with low subcutaneous fat deposition are more susceptible to cold shortening, which reduces meat tenderness. A SFT of 3 mm is commonly adopted as a technical benchmark by the Brazilian beef industry to ensure carcass protection during chilling (Gomes, 2021). In the present study, the SFT for all treatments remained below this reference value and was classified as “absent” according to national grading standards (Brasil, 2004). This characteristic is representative of pasture-based systems using Nellore cattle slaughtered at these specific weights and may be considered a limitation of the study, as it restricts the extrapolation of meat quality findings to more intensive production systems or stricter industrial standards.

The REA is related to the yield of primal cuts, carcass composition (muscle-to-bone ratio), and the degree of muscling of the animal (Luchiari Filho, 2000), and is considered optimal when ≥ 78 cm2; good, 71–77 cm2; regular, 63–70 cm2; average, 55–62 cm2; below average, 47–54 cm2; and deficient when < 46 cm2 (Gomes, 2021). Based on the classification proposed by Gomes (2021), the REA values observed in the present study indicate that animals from all pasture systems exhibited carcasses classified as having a regular and acceptable muscling level.

Animals maintained in the MIX system exhibited higher weights for the flank tip, the edible portions of the forequarter and hindquarter, total bone, and the total edible carcass compared with those in the DEG system (Table 3). These differences directly influenced economically important traits, as observed in high-value commercial cuts such as tenderloin and striploin.

The observed increase in carcass output, coupled with stable beef quality and reduced methane emission intensity, indicates that the nutritional benefits of the MIX system compensate for the traditional challenges of pasture-based intensification.

Regarding the beef quality obtained from the different production systems (Table 4), the differences observed in quality parameters across ageing times can be attributed to moisture retention and enzymatic degradation of myofibrillar proteins occurring during the wet ageing process (Ha et al., 2019; Matarneh et al., 2023).

A pH24h (pH measured 24 h after slaughter) value of 5.96 characterises the meat as having an intermediate pH (5.8–6.1), as reported by Contreras-Castillo et al. (2016) and Loudon et al. (2018). This profile is associated with the depletion of muscle glycogen reserves and the consequent reduction in lactic acid production during the post-mortem period (Mahmood et al., 2019; Silva et al., 2019). This behaviour may be associated with potential pre-slaughter stress, considering that the experimental animals were entire males, which are more sensitive to handling stress, reactive behaviour due to steroid hormones, and muscle glycogen depletion prior to slaughter (Kannan et al., 2002; Mahmood et al., 2017).

Ageing times influenced the meat pH, which increased after 7 and 14 days of ageing. These values remain within the intermediate pH range, reflecting the degradation of proteins and the release of peptides and amino acids that slightly increase pH during the ageing process (Lee et al., 2024).

Although ageing time did not affect the redness (a*) of the aged beef (Table 4), a higher yellowness (b*) was observed at 7 days followed by a decrease at 14 days. Ageing may influence meat colour due to changes in myoglobin, lipid oxidation, and pigment degradation, resulting in a more yellowish appearance (Henriott et al., 2020).

Meat lightness (L*) (Fig. 1) in the MIX and DEG treatments showed a significant increase after 7 days of ageing. However, at 14 days of ageing, this increase was maintained in the MIX, whereas a decline in L* values was observed in the DEG. The REC treatment exhibited the lowest initial lightness values, with a gradual increase over 7 and 14 days of ageing, although the increment was smaller compared with DEG and MIX. An increase in L* occurs not only when there is less myoglobin on the meat surface but also due to greater light scattering, which results from protein denaturation in the muscle structure. This phenomenon can enhance light reflectance in response to water retention during the wet ageing process (Ha et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2016; de Sousa Ferreira et al., 2023).

According to Hamm (1986), water-holding capacity (WHC) values above 70% are considered ideal or desirable; values between 60% and 70% indicate an intermediate but suboptimal retention; and values below 60% correspond to low water-holding capacity. Moisture retention in meat is closely related to the ultimate pH, as at pH levels above 5.5 there is an excess of negative charges that promote repulsion among protein filaments, increasing the available space for water molecules and reducing cooking losses (Ha et al., 2019). In the present study (Fig. 2), the REC treatment reached its peak WHC at 7 days of ageing, indicating improved water retention, which coincided with a mean pH of 6.04 (Table 4). Conversely, the MIX treatment exhibited a higher initial WHC compared with REC, remaining relatively stable up to 7 days of ageing, followed by a decline after 14 days.

A significant effect was observed on cooking loss (%), with higher water retention at day 0 and a reduction after 7 and 14 days of ageing. The progressive loss of water during ageing is associated with the gradual breakdown of structural proteins and reduced tissue integrity (Ha et al., 2019).

The shear force values (Table 4) were high at day 0, which may be attributed to the absence of SFT in the carcasses, a condition that increases susceptibility to cold shortening and consequently reduces meat tenderness (Gomes, 2021). Furthermore, the lower tenderness observed is consistent with the known characteristic of Bos taurus indicus meat, which tends to be less tender due to the higher levels of calpastatin present in the muscle. This enzyme inhibits the activity of calpains, the main proteases involved in post-mortem proteolysis during meat ageing (Guimarães et al., 2024; Haddad et al., 2022; Koohmaraie, 1994; Koohmaraie & Geesink, 2006).

A significant reduction in WBSF was observed after 7 days and 14 days of ageing, demonstrating the positive effect of ageing on meat tenderness. The progressive decrease in shear force can be explained by the degradation of myofibrillar proteins and the weakening of connective tissue bonds during the ageing process (Matarneh et al., 2023). Despite this improvement, even after 14 days the WBSF values remained above the 50 N threshold proposed as the upper limit for acceptable tenderness (Warner et al., 2021). These findings confirm that meat from Bos indicus may require longer ageing periods to achieve adequate tenderness, as previously reported by Aroeira et al. (2016).

Taken together, these data suggest that a 7-day ageing time improves meat tenderness and colour stability (b*) without the excessive loss of structural integrity observed at 14 days.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the inclusion of pigeon pea in Urochloa spp. pastures lowered enteric CH4 emission intensity expressed per hectare, per kilogram of hot carcass, and per kilogram of edible portion, achieving a reduction of >40% in CO2-equivalent emissions per kilogram of carcass compared with the degraded pasture system. Concurrently, this strategic system resulted in higher carcass production, total edible carcass weight, and yield of forequarter and hindquarter commercial cuts in Nellore cattle, directly enhancing the production of economically commercial primal cuts such as tenderloin and striploin, without compromising meat quality.
