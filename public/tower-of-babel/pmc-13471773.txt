Objective Measurement of Tooth Color Using Spectrophotometry and ICDAS: Quantifying CIELAB Color Changes Across Caries Severity
Authors: Farah Rashid, James Dudley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS) is widely used for early caries detection but relies on visual interpretation. Although spectrophotometry objectively quantifies tooth color, variation across ICDAS categories and its relationship with lesion severity remain unclear. The present in vitro study therefore characterized spectrophotometric measurements across ICDAS categories, evaluated their association with severity, and quantified differences across categories.Materials and MethodsThree hundred unrestored extracted posterior teeth were classified as ICDAS 0–4 by a calibrated examiner. CIELAB values were obtained with a Spectrophotometer at five standardized sites per tooth under controlled lighting and aggregated to tooth‐level. Differences across categories were analyzed using ANOVA/Kruskal‐Wallis with correlation and regression analysis. Color differences (ΔE00) were calculated relative to the ICDAS 0 (reference) and interpreted using published perceptibility and acceptability thresholds.ResultsColor parameters differed significantly across ICDAS categories (p < 0.001). ΔE00 increased from ICDAS 2, exceeding reported perceptibility and acceptability thresholds. With increasing severity, L* decreased while a* and b* increased with significant monotonic associations for all color parameters (ρ = −0.58 to 0.62; p < 0.001).ConclusionsStatistically significant differences in spectrophotometric color measurements were observed across caries severity categories, with progressive reductions in lightness and corresponding increases in red and yellow chromatic components. Overall color differences were greater in higher severity categories and exceeded perceptibility and acceptability thresholds from moderate lesion stages onward.Clinical SignificanceSpectrophotometric color assessment provides an objective framework for quantifying enamel changes associated with caries progression. Color differences exceeding established perceptibility and acceptability thresholds indicate clinically appreciable changes beyond early lesion stages, supporting the use of color‐based metrics as an adjunct to conventional visual assessment.

Introduction

Untreated dental caries in permanent teeth remains the most prevalent non‐communicable oral disease worldwide, affecting more than three billion people and imposing a substantial global health burden [1, 2, 3]. Early detection of carious lesions is crucial for preventing progression requiring minimally invasive interventions. Despite advances in adjunctive diagnostic technologies, visual‐tactile examination continues to form the foundation of caries detection in everyday dental practice, with emerging tools generally intended to complement rather than replace conventional assessment [4, 5]. While traditional visual and tactile inspection is inherently subjective, it may be limited in its ability to detect incipient lesions [6]. Radiographs are effective for detecting interproximal and advanced lesions due to their high specificity; however, they exhibit limited sensitivity for early enamel caries, detecting as few as 0%–29% of initial occlusal lesions [7]. Furthermore, reliance on radiographs may raise concerns regarding unnecessary radiation exposure, particularly when repeated imaging is required or in younger patients [8]. Together, these limitations highlight the potential value of objective, quantifiable methods for detecting subtle changes at the earliest stages of carious lesions.

Several optical technologies, including fiber‐optic transillumination (FOTI), quantitative light‐induced fluorescence (QLF), and optical coherence tomography (OCT), have been explored for caries detection by utilizing altered optical properties of demineralized enamel to visualize lesions at the microstructural level [9]. While these methods have demonstrated potential, their widespread clinical adoption could be limited by cost, complexity, or the need for specialized training [9]. In contrast, tooth color has been described as one of the earliest visible indicators of enamel demineralization [10, 11, 12], with incipient lesions commonly appearing clinically as white spot changes or more advanced lesions as brownish discolorations. Historically, tooth color changes have been clinically assessed through qualitative visual inspection, and relatively few optical techniques have been designed to enable their direct, standardized quantification using objective color metrics [9, 11, 13]. Objective quantification of tooth color may support improved standardization of caries assessment and reduce reliance on subjective visual interpretation, which is known to vary between examiners [14, 15]. Quantitative assessment of these color changes may enhance diagnostic reproducibility and standardization, improve clinical communication, and support more objective clinical decision making, thereby highlighting the potential value of color‐based optical methods such as spectrophotometry.

Building on this, spectrophotometry represents a promising approach for addressing the present research gap. Unlike filter‐based colorimeters, spectrophotometers capture full‐spectrum reflectance data and may offer greater accuracy, reproducibility, and sensitivity to subtle color variations while reducing the influence of ambient lighting conditions [15, 16]. These characteristics suggest that spectrophotometry may be well suited to the detection of early‐stage carious changes, where even small shifts in tooth color could be clinically meaningful [15, 17]. In dentistry, color is commonly described using the CIELAB system, in which L* denotes lightness and a* and b* represent the red‐green and yellow‐blue chromatic axes, respectively [18, 19, 20]. Color differences are commonly expressed as ΔE00 values and interpreted using established perceptibility and acceptability thresholds [20, 21]. Caries progression has been associated with decreasing L* values and positive shifts in a* and b*, reflecting mineral loss, altered enamel translucency, and greater dentin visibility [22, 23]. Several previous studies [6, 23] have explored spectrophotometric measurements in the context of dental tissues; however, these investigations have primarily focused on device validation, spectral characterization, or methodological performance rather than systematic evaluation of color changes across the International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS) categories.

Enamel demineralization has been reported to alter the optical properties of dental hard tissues, including increased light scattering, reduced translucency, and greater influence of underlying dentin on overall tooth appearance [24, 25]. These changes have been associated with reduction in lightness (L*) and shifts in chromaticity (a* and b*), reflecting both structural alterations and changes in enamel‐dentin interaction [9, 13, 23, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. Previous experimental and optical studies [15, 30] have reported that demineralized enamel may exhibit progressive darkening and chromatic variation, although these observations have primarily been described qualitatively or within controlled experimental contexts rather than systematically quantified across clinically defined severity stages. Given that spectrophotometry enables objective and standardized measurement of color using CIELAB coordinates, it may provide a suitable framework for investigating whether such optical changes are reflected in measurable differences across ICDAS categories.

ICDAS remains the most widely used visual scoring system for grading caries severity in both clinical and research settings. The detailed ICDAS criteria allow for differentiation across the caries classification, from sound enamel to distinct cavitation [31, 32]. Despite its broad adoption, ICDAS relies on visual interpretation and is therefore prone to inter‐ and intra‐examiner variability, particularly in the early stages of demineralization [14, 15, 33]. Establishing objective spectrophotometric correlates for ICDAS categories could help address these shortcomings by providing measurable, quantifiable parameters to complement visual scoring. Although spectrophotometry has been widely applied in dentistry for shade matching and color assessment, its application in caries detection remains limited. Consequently, the characterization of CIELAB color variation across early to moderate ICDAS categories (0–4) has not been extensively explored in the current literature. These considerations highlight a gap in the objective characterization of color changes associated with increasing caries severity.

The present in vitro study was designed to systematically characterize spectrophotometric CIELAB color coordinates (L*, a*, b*) and overall color differences (ΔE00) across early to moderate ICDAS categories in extracted human teeth, a clinically relevant range that has not been comprehensively examined using standardized colorimetric measures to date. In particular, the study aimed to determine whether consistent patterns and measurable magnitudes of color changes, reflected by ΔE00 values, are associated with increasing caries severity under controlled experimental conditions. Based on these considerations, the study was designed to test the hypothesis that spectrophotometric CIELAB color parameters vary systematically across ICDAS categories and demonstrate progressive associations with increasing lesion severity.

Materials and Methods

Sample Size Calculation

Based on previously published spectrophotometric studies reporting moderate differences in color parameters between carious and non‐carious enamel [34], a conservative medium effect size (Cohen's f = 0.25) was selected for power analysis. As these studies did not report formal effect size estimates, the use of a conventional medium effect size was considered appropriate for detecting between group differences. Power analysis was performed using GPower software (version 3.1.9.4) [35] with an alpha level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.80, indicating that a minimum of 200 independent teeth was required. In the present study, 300 extracted human teeth were included to increase statistical precision and ensure adequate representation across ICDAS categories. The increased sample size improves the stability of effect estimates, thereby supporting the robustness of the analysis. For each tooth, five site‐specific spectrophotometric color measurements were obtained to capture intra‐tooth spatial variability. These measurements were aggregated to generate a single representative value per tooth prior to statistical analysis, with the tooth treated as the independent experimental unit (n = 300).

Study Sample

Extracted human unrestored teeth (n = 300) were obtained from a university‐based dental simulation clinic, where they were stored in distilled water following routine clinical procedures. Specimens were obtained anonymously, and the exact interval between extraction and laboratory acquisition was not available. Upon acquisition, all teeth were cleaned of surface debris and disinfected by immersion in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 10 days. Immersion in 10% formalin has been reported as an effective method for sterilization of extracted human teeth, with exposure durations ranging from 5 days to 2 weeks in previous investigations [36, 37, 38]. Although sterilization methods may influence certain mechanical properties depending on the medium used, formalin immersion has been reported not to significantly compromise dentin bonding performance compared with alternative chemical disinfectants [38, 39, 40]. Following disinfection, specimens were rinsed and stored in distilled water at room temperature for 24 h prior to spectrophotometric measurement to allow hydration stabilization. The storage medium was refreshed weekly to minimize contamination and maintain consistent storage conditions. All teeth were maintained under consistent hydration conditions throughout the experimental period to minimize dehydration related variability in color measurements. Distilled water storage is commonly employed in in vitro dental research as a neutral medium to maintain hydration prior to laboratory testing [38].

The current study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national guidelines for research involving human biological materials. The investigation utilized fully anonymized extracted human teeth with no access to patient identifiers or clinical data. In accordance with the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (NHMRC, 2007) and institutional guidelines, research involving existing collections of de‐identified human biological specimens and posing negligible risk is exempt from Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) review. An institutional ethics self‐assessment confirmed negligible risk status; therefore, formal HREC approval and individual informed consent were not required. Standardization of Lighting and Specimen Positioning

Visual ICDAS scoring and spectrophotometric color measurements were performed under identical standardized lighting and spatial conditions in a controlled environment to ensure reproducible visual assessment and methodological consistency across procedures. All measurements were conducted according to established principles for standardized dental color evaluation, with illumination conditions designed to approximate standardized daylight viewing environments consistent with international viewing condition standards and previously reported protocol [41, 42]. Data collection was performed in a darkened room using a professional PhotoBox (XCD LED Portable Photo Studio) with a neutral gray backdrop to minimize ambient light interference. The PhotoBox was equipped with 120 diffused LEDs producing uniform illumination (approximately 6000 lm), with a measured color temperature of 5100–5500 K and illuminance between 1000 and 1500 l× [33]. These values fall within ranges recommended for standardized color evaluation to reduce chromatic bias and surface glare [43].

Illumination intensity and color temperature were monitored using a smartphone‐based lux meter application to confirm stability of lighting conditions during measurements. Smartphone light‐meter systems have been reported to provide reasonably accurate estimates of ambient illuminance when calibrated against reference devices against controlled conditions [44]. Continuous monitoring ensured stability of lighting conditions across specimens and throughout both assessment procedures and was used solely to confirm illumination consistency. Specimen positioning was standardized using fixed spatial parameters to ensure reproducible optical geometry. Each tooth was mounted with the occlusal surface oriented perpendicular to the measurement axis and stabilized using non‐reflective soft clay. Distances were maintained at 150 mm from the platform, 200 mm from the light source and 500 mm from the examiner. These parameters were determined through pilot testing to minimize shadow formation, specular reflection and angular illumination artifacts while maintaining consistent probe alignment and visual assessment conditions across all specimens. Caries Scoring Criteria Using ICDAS (Visual Assessment)

Extracted permanent teeth presenting with ICDAS codes 0 to 4 were eligible for inclusion. ICDAS 0: sound enamel with no evidence of caries following air drying, ICDAS 1: first visual change in enamel detectable only after drying, ICDAS 2: a distinct visual change in enamel visible when wet, ICDAS 3: localized enamel breakdown without visible dentin exposure and ICDAS 4: an underlying dark shadow from dentin, with or without minor enamel surface breakdown but without cavitation. Representative images of each ICDAS category (0–4) are provided in Figure S1.

Each specimen was assigned a single ICDAS code following comprehensive visual assessment of the entire occlusal surface. To ensure internal consistency, only teeth demonstrating uniform lesion severity across the occlusal surface were retained, such that all measured sites corresponded to the same ICDAS category. Both molars and premolars were included to represent posterior teeth affected by occlusal caries. As the primary objective of the study was to evaluate color variation across ICDAS severity levels rather than differences between tooth type, analysis was conducted at the tooth level without stratification by molar or premolar classification. The distribution of teeth across ICDAS categories is detailed in Tables S1 and S2. Teeth presenting with extensive cavitation (ICDAS 5–6), proximal caries, or non‐carious surface loss such as abrasion, erosion, or attrition were excluded to minimize optical confounding unrelated to caries‐associated demineralization. Advanced cavitated lesions (ICDAS 5–6) were intentionally excluded, as these stages are primarily defined by structural breakdown rather than progressive chromatic change.

ICDAS assessments were performed by a calibrated registered dentist using the official ICDAS decision tree. Examiner calibration was established through completion of the ICCMS e‐learning module, followed by scoring 40 randomly selected teeth on two occasions, 1 week apart, to minimize recall bias. Intra‐examiner reliability was calculated using Cohen's kappa in the Python programming language, yielding values of 0.90 (unweighted) and 0.96 (quadratically weighted), indicating almost perfect agreement. A single calibrated examiner performed all ICDAS assessments to ensure scoring consistency, minimize inter‐examiner variability, and maintain uniform application of diagnostic criteria across specimens. ICDAS scoring was completed prior to spectrophotometric measurements, and the examiner remained blinded to all instrumental data during visual assessment to prevent potential classification bias.

Spectrophotometric Color Values Extraction

Color measurements were obtained using a Vita Easyshade V spectrophotometer (VITA Zahnfabrik, Germany), which records color in the CIELAB color space. The device was calibrated according to the manufacturer's instructions prior to each measurement to ensure accuracy and instrument stability. Prior to the measurements, each specimen was removed from the distilled water and gently dried with cotton gauze to remove surface moisture while maintaining intrinsic hydration. No prolonged air‐drying was performed to avoid dehydration‐related optical alteration.

To capture spatial variation in enamel coloration, each tooth was assessed at five predefined sites across the occlusal surface. For molars, measurements were recorded from the central pits/fissures, mesiobuccal, distobuccal, mesiolingual, and distolingual regions. For premolars, measurements were recorded from the central groove, buccal and lingual cusp tips, and midpoints on the buccal and lingual surfaces. (Figure S2) These sites were defined using consistent anatomical landmarks and direct visual guidance to ensure standardized and reproducible probe placement. The probe tip was positioned perpendicular to the enamel surface and placed in light contact without applying additional pressure to ensure consistent measurement geometry across specimens. Each site was measured once under identical conditions to ensure consistent acquisition procedures across all teeth. The intra‐device repeatability of clinical dental spectrophotometers has been previously validated and reported to demonstrate high precision and reliability for dental color measurement [20, 45, 46].

A total of 300 teeth were evaluated, with five L*, a* and b* measurements obtained per tooth, yielding 1500 site specific color readings. To avoid pseudo‐replication and ensure independence of observations, site‐level values were aggregated to generate a single representative color value (ΔL*, Δa* and Δb*) per tooth for statistical analysis. The study approach preserves within tooth spatial information while ensuring that the statistical unit corresponded to the biological unit of interest (tooth‐level).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 31.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Teeth were treated as the independent experimental unit. Normality of tooth level color values was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene's test, and model residuals were visually inspected to confirm the adequacy of parametric assumptions. Differences in L* and b* (normally distributed) across ICDAS categories were analyzed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Games‐Howell post hoc tests. Non‐normally distributed a* values were evaluated using the Kruskal‐Wallis test with pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests and Bonferroni correction (α < 0.005).

Spearman's rank correlation (ρ) was used to assess progressive associations between ICDAS scores and spectrophotometric CIELAB color values. Linear regression models were then applied to quantify the magnitude and direction of changes in color parameters per one‐unit increase in ICDAS, reporting regression coefficients, confidence intervals, and explained variance (R 2). Given that ICDAS represents an ordinal scale, it was first entered as a numeric predictor to evaluate directional trends rather than to assume equal spacing between categories. To assess robustness to this assumption and to provide clinically interpretable comparisons, additional regression models were fitted with ICDAS specified as a categorical predictor, estimating the difference for each category relative to sound teeth (ICDAS 0). The number of significant figures was determined based on the precision of the measurement instrument and standard reporting conventions. CIELAB color values and derived descriptive statistics were reported to two decimal places, reflecting the resolution of the spectrophotometric measurements, while inferential statistical outputs (test statistics, regression coefficients, and confidence intervals) were reported to an appropriate level of precision.

Color Difference (ΔE00) Calculations

Color differences between ICDAS categories were calculated using the CIEDE2000 formula (ΔE00), which provides improved perceptual uniformity and is widely recommended in contemporary dental color research [47]. ΔE00 values were computed at the tooth level using individual CIELAB (L*, a*, and b*) measurements. To reflect within category variability and potential overlap, ΔE00 for each tooth was calculated relative to the median CIELAB coordinates of the ICDAS 0 category (reference). The median reference was selected to minimize the influence of potential outliers within the sound tooth group and to ensure consistency with the distribution‐based reporting approach. ΔE00 distributions were summarized for each ICDAS category as median (Interquartile Range, IQR). Differences in ΔE00 across ICDAS categories were assessed using the Kruskal‐Wallis test due to the non‐normal distribution of color data. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. For interpretive context, ΔE00 values of approximately 0.8 and 1.8 were considered perceptibility and acceptability thresholds, respectively, in accordance with published literature [47]. Although threshold values may vary depending on experimental design, observer characteristics, and clinical context, these values were adopted to provide clinically relevant reference points for interpretation not as universal fixed values.

Correlation and Regression Analysis of CIELAB Parameters Across ICDAS Categories

Spearman's correlation showed significant monotonic associations between ICDAS scores and all three color parameters (p < 0.001). L* exhibited a moderate negative correlation (ρ = −0.58), consistent with decreasing lightness as lesion severity increased. a* showed a strong positive correlation (ρ = 0.62), indicating progressive reddening with increasing severity, whereas b* demonstrated a weaker positive association (ρ = 0.38), reflecting a less pronounced monotonic relationship. Linear regression models further supported these findings. For each one‐unit increase in ICDAS, L* decreased by 3.563 units (95% CI = −4.120 to −3.006, p < 0.001), while a* and b* increased by 1.283 (95% CI = 1.087 to 1.480, p < 0.001) and 1.124 units (95% CI = 0.804 to 1.445, p < 0.001), respectively. The models explained 35% of the variance in L* and 36% in a*, and 14% in b*, indicating a progressive pattern of darkening, reddening, and yellowing with advancing lesion severity. (Table 5) These results were consistent with the correlation analysis, supporting the robustness of the observed associations. Discussion

The present in vitro study demonstrated that spectrophotometric color analysis detects systematic variations in enamel appearance across increasing caries severity. Lesion progression was characterized primarily by reduced lightness and increased red chromaticity, whereas changes along the yellow‐blue axis were less pronounced. These differences were statistically significant, and for several comparisons, exceeded established perceptibility thresholds, indicating that observed differences were both instrumentally measurable and potentially perceptible under controlled conditions. Collectively, the findings demonstrate clear inter‐category differences and consistent monotonic associations between ICDAS severity and CIELAB parameters. By aligning objective color measurements with clinically defined ICDAS categories, the results provide quantitative characterization of lesion‐associated color variation and support the potential role of spectrophotometry as an adjunctive tool for assessing caries lesion severity alongside visual evaluation.

Lightness (L*) Variation Across ICDAS Categories

Lightness values decreased systematically with increasing caries severity, indicating progressive darkening of enamel across ICDAS categories. The pattern is consistent with previous reports [6, 15] and experimental observations suggesting that mineral loss is associated with reduced translucency and increased dentin influence. Taken together, regression and correlation analysis indicate a consistent inverse relationship between lightness and lesion severity (average reduction of −3.56 units per ICDAS increment and 35% of variance explained), supporting the interpretation that enamel darkening progresses systematically across disease stages. The magnitude of such association was substantial (η 2 = 0.382), suggesting that L* functions as a meaningful severity associated optical parameter, although its moderate correlation (ρ = −0.58) indicates it may be better interpreted as a complementary indicator rather than a standalone marker of early lesion differentiation. The interpretation is consistent with the ΔE00 analysis, which showed that median color differences exceeded perceptibility and acceptability thresholds beyond ICDAS 1. Although such thresholds vary across studies, including those in the literature that reported [47], the observed values remained above commonly cited ranges, supporting the interpretation that cumulative optical changes become visually appreciable as lesion severity increases under controlled conditions.

Notably, a stage‐specific pattern was evident. Despite the overall decline, adjacent categories such as ICDAS 0 versus 1 and ICDAS 2 versus 3 did not differ significantly, indicating overlap in the distribution of lightness values between early and intermediate stages. In ICDAS 1 lesions, early demineralization often remains subsurface beneath an intact surface layer, limiting visible brightness changes [25]. Similarly, between ICDAS 2 and 3, further progression may manifest more prominently through chromatic alterations than through additional reductions in lightness [9]. These findings suggest that minimal optical contrast between adjacent stages may reduce discriminative capacity in early transitions. Given that lesion staging was defined using ICDAS clinical criteria rather than histological or radiographic reference standards, these transitional patterns are most appropriately interpreted as reflecting differences across clinically defined severity categories rather than absolute lesion depth. Within the current interpretive framework, the observed pattern indicates that lightness (L*) may be most informative from moderate lesion stages onward, whereas its relative stability between ICDAS 0 and 1 highlights limitations for detecting the earliest incipient lesions. Taken together, these findings indicate that L* should not be interpreted as a standalone indicator for early lesion detection but may provide greater value when used alongside complementary chromatic parameters or multimodal assessment approaches.

By contrast, the pronounced difference between ICDAS 0 and 4 illustrates the magnitude of luminance change associated with advanced lesion progression. The observed reduction (13.52 units) is consistent with substantial structural alteration of enamel, including increased scattering and absorption associated with subsurface porosity and enhanced influence of underlying dentin substrates [9, 13, 26]. These findings indicate that pronounced darkening becomes readily detectable at later stages, supporting the interpretation of L* as a severity associated optical parameter under controlled measurement conditions.

Red‐Green Axis (a*): Chromatic Shifts With Increasing Lesion Severity

In the present study, a* values shifted progressively from negative (greenish) toward positive (reddish) with increasing ICDAS severity, indicating a systematic chromatic transition accompanying lesion development. These patterns may reflect reduced enamel masking as demineralization progresses, allowing intrinsic chromatic contribution of underlying dentin to exert greater influence on overall tooth color [30, 48], as well as the accumulation of bacterial metabolites such as porphyrins that can contribute reddish discoloration in demineralized tissues [49, 50]. Across analyses, regression and correlation results indicated a strong monotonic association between a* and lesion severity (increase of +1.28 a* units per ICDAS step, and the model explained 36% of variance), indicating that chromatic changes along with the red‐green axis parallel lesion progression. The strength of such association was substantial, indicating that a* represents a meaningful severity‐associated chromatic parameter within the analytical framework of the present study. However, because these observations were obtained under standardized in vitro conditions, their interpretation should remain contextual rather than diagnostic when applied in clinical settings.

While overall group differences were large (ε 2 = 0.392), adjacent stages such as ICDAS 0 versus 1 and ICDAS 2 versus 3 did not differ significantly, indicating limited separation in a* values between early and intermediate stages. Notably, a* nevertheless showed the strongest monotonic association with ICDAS severity among the CIELAB coordinates (ρ = 0.62), suggesting that although chromatic shifts track lesion progression consistently at the overall level, transitions between neighboring stages may occur gradually or reach a temporary plateau [51, 52]. Such behavior may reflect nonlinear optical responses during demineralization, including saturation of dentin chromatic influence or stabilization of pigment accumulation at intermediate lesion depths [49, 50]. Collectively, these observations indicate that a* is more informative for characterizing overall lesion progression than for discriminating fine stage transitions. Considered alongside ΔE00 outcomes, these chromatic shifts appear to contribute meaningfully to the perceptual color changes that become visually appreciable once lesions progress beyond early stages.

These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating progressive increases in a* values with increasing lesion severity [23]. By contrast, some experimental studies of artificially induced carious lesions have reported minimal or inconsistent a* shifts [14, 52]. For example, one study [52] observed that color changes in artificial white spot lesions were driven predominantly by L* and b*, with little contribution from a*, while another study [14] found that a* changes following experimental demineralization were small and did not reach statistical significance. Methodological differences likely account for these discrepancies. Prior studies employed spectroradiometer (PR‐670) or benchtop systems (LabScan XE) requiring spectral conversion to CIELAB values, a process that may introduce variability, particularly when combined with non‐contact geometrics or large sampling apertures less suited to curved enamel surfaces [16]. In contrast, the present study used a handheld contact spectrophotometer (VITA Easyshade V) providing direct CIELAB output under controlled illumination, conditions that may enhance reproducibility and sensitivity to subtle chromatic variation [33, 53, 54]. Use of the CIELAB color space facilitates standardized quantification and comparability across studies, which may further contribute to the consistency observed. Additionally, the integrated illumination system likely reduces susceptibility to ambient light variation and may enhance measurement consistency on curved enamel surfaces, an interpretation supported by the high intra‐rater reliability observed in the current study (K > 0.90 over a two‐week interval). Collectively, these methodological distinctions suggest that variation across studies may reflect differences in instrumentation and lesion modeling rather than fundamental disagreement regarding chromatic behavior.

Yellow‐Blue Axis (b*): Secondary Chromatic Changes During Lesion Progression

In the present study, b* values increased progressively with caries severity, rising from sound enamel to moderate lesions and indicating a gradual shift toward greater yellow chromaticity as lesion severity increases. These trends are biologically plausible, as demineralization and subsurface porosity increase light scattering and reduce short wavelength reflectance, thereby enhancing the influence of dentin's inherent yellow chroma on overall tooth color [11, 25]. These optical mechanisms are consistent with the clinical impression of more advanced lesions, in which enamel translucency is reduced and underlying dentin undertones become more apparent.

Converging statistical findings indicated that b* showed a statistically significant but comparatively weaker association with ICDAS severity than the other CIELAB coordinates. Correlation and regression findings indicated a relatively weak monotonic relationship (ρ = 0.38; +1.12 units per ICDAS step), with approximately 14% variance explained, and a moderate overall effect size (η 2 = 0.147). These findings suggest that although yellowing increases systematically with lesion progression, its strength as a severity indicator is less pronounced than that of lightness or redness, indicating a comparatively smaller contribution of the yellow‐blue axis to lesion discrimination. These patterns support the interpretation that individual color coordinates contribute differently to lesion characterization.

One plausible explanation arises from prior optical studies indicating that enamel thickness and refractive index correlate only weakly with b* [27, 30], while spectrophotometric research shows that yellow‐blue chromaticity is particularly susceptible to variability related to hydration, probe positioning, and enamel translucency [28, 29]. Device comparison studies further demonstrate that inter‐instrument and inter‐protocol differences can introduce small chromatic deviations, especially along the b* axis [28]. Collectively, these biological and technical influences may increase dispersion in b* measurements and thereby reduce the strength of its association with lesion severity.

Stage‐wise comparisons revealed a non‐uniform progression pattern. Significant differences were observed between sound or early lesions and higher ICDAS categories, whereas adjacent advanced stages (ICDAS 2–3 and 3–4) did not differ significantly, indicating relative stabilization of b* values at greater lesion severity. The unequal distribution of teeth across ICDAS categories should also be considered when interpreting these comparisons (Table S1), as specimens were included consecutively based on availability rather than stratified recruitment by severity stage, thereby reflecting the neutral distribution of lesion severities within the collected sample set. The plateau‐like behavior may reflect saturation of dentin chromatic influence or increased structural heterogeneity in deeper lesions. These findings suggest that b* may be more informative for distinguishing lower from moderate severity categories than for differentiating neighboring advanced lesions. Importantly, these findings indicate that b* should not be interpreted as an independent discriminative parameter but rather as a secondary chromatic component that modulates overall color difference. When integrated with other coordinates as ΔE00, its contribution becomes perceptually meaningful, consistent with the observed increases in total color difference beyond early lesion stages. Taken together, these findings support a multidimensional interpretation of color change in caries progression, in which lightness and red chromaticity appear to provide the dominant discriminatory information, while the yellow‐blue axis contributes a complementary, context‐dependent role.

Limitations and Future Recommendations

The findings should be interpreted within the methodological context of the in vitro study. Lesion severity was classified using ICDAS criteria, a validated visual caries scoring system; histological and radiographic standards were beyond the scope of the present study design, which was structured to evaluate spectrophotometric variation across clinically defined severity categories while preserving specimen integrity for standardized optical assessment. ICDAS scoring was performed by a single calibrated examiner, and spectrophotometric measurements were obtained using a single device. Although these approaches ensured strict standardization of classification and acquisition conditions, they did not permit assessment of inter‐examiner agreement or inter‐device variability. In addition, measurements were obtained under controlled laboratory conditions which minimized environmental variability but did not fully replicate intraoral conditions, such as saliva, biofilm and variable ambient lighting. These factors may affect enamel optical properties and should be considered when extrapolating findings to clinical settings. The study focused on ICDAS 0–4 lesions to isolate progressive optical changes within early to moderate stages, while advanced cavitated lesions were intentionally excluded, as these are predominantly defined by structural breakdown rather than incremental chromatic variation. Inclusion of the full lesion spectrum in future studies would provide broader characterization of color changes across all stages of caries progression.

Future research should also extend these findings through in vivo validation, multi‐examiner designs, and incorporation of independent reference standards. Such approaches would enable evaluation of diagnostic performance and clarify the clinical applicability of spectrophotometric color assessment across diverse practice environments. Integration of objective color metrics into computational classification frameworks represents a promising direction; however, further validation is required before such applications can be considered clinically actionable.
