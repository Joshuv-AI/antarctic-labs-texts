Modeling maternal immune activation in 3D ex vivo human fetal brain cerebroids reveals IL-17A-driven disruption of cortical development
Authors: Muhammad Z. K. Assir, Mario Yanakiev, Do Hyeon Gim, Sara S. M. Valkila, Paola Muscolino, Peng Liu, Laetitia L. Lecante, Paul A. Fowler, Daniel A. Berg, Eunchai Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Maternal immune activation (MIA) disrupts brain development and increases the risk of neurodevelopmental disorders, yet the mechanisms by which MIA impacts human cortical development remain poorly understood. Here we introduce a three-dimensional ex vivo culture system, termed ‘cerebroids,’ derived from the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex of human fetal brain tissue, which preserves the key developmental processes, cellular diversity and structural integrity of the developing human cortex. Using this model, we show that IL-17A, a cytokine implicated in MIA and neurodevelopmental disorders, induces premature cortical folding, increases cortical thickness and accelerates neurogenesis and neuronal maturation. We reveal that IL-17A substantially dysregulates extracellular-matrix-related pathways, including upregulation of proteoglycans. In neural stem cells, IL-17A directly activates NF-κB signaling, leading to sustained inflammatory responses that contribute to these developmental abnormalities, which are reversed by treatment with the NF-κB pathway inhibitor parthenolide. These findings delineate how IL-17A perturbs human corticogenesis while elucidating the mechanisms underlying brain disruption during MIA.

Main

A growing body of evidence from epidemiological studies has demonstrated that maternal immune activation (MIA) significantly increases the risk of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), including autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and schizophrenia, in offspring1–5. Animal models of MIA have provided insights into its role in the pathogenesis of NDDs, revealing ASD-related behavioral deficits and structural brain abnormalities6,7, such as increased cortical thickness, elevated neuronal numbers8 and perturbed cortical layer organization7. Despite these findings, the precise cellular and molecular mechanisms by which the inflammatory environment in utero alters the developmental trajectory of the human cortex, leading to such structural and functional abnormalities, remain poorly understood.

Emerging evidence implicates IL-17A as a key mediator of MIA-associated neurodevelopmental risk. Elevated serum IL-17A levels have been shown to correlate with the severity of ASD in humans9–11. Genetic studies have also identified amplified copy number variations in the IL17A gene among ASD cohorts12. Animal studies indicate that IL-17A is a key mediator of MIA, as elevated maternal IL-17A reaches the fetal brain and is sufficient to induce abnormal cortical development, microglial activation and autism-like behaviors in offspring, while its inhibition prevents these MIA-associated abnormalities1,7,13–16.

Human pluripotent stem cell (PSC)-derived brain organoids offer a valuable platform for investigating human-specific aspects of developmental disturbances caused by genetic and environmental insults17. However, the absence of immune and vascular components in these organoid models restricts their use, particularly in modeling MIA18. Ex vivo models using primary human tissue provide a more accurate representation of brain development, but conventional slice cultures are limited by low throughput and scalability issues. To address this critical gap, we have developed a high-throughput three-dimensional (3D) ex vivo culture system, termed ‘cerebroids’, which preserves key developmental processes and structural organization.

Using cerebroids, we examined the impact of IL-17A, a cytokine mediating MIA. IL-17A disrupts corticogenesis by inducing premature folding, increasing cortical thickness and accelerating neuronal differentiation. Multi-omic analyses reveal extracellular matrix (ECM) dysregulation and sustained NF-κB activation in neural stem cells (NSCs), while parthenolide (PTL), an NF-κB inhibitor, rescues these abnormalities, providing mechanistic insight into how MIA perturbs human cortical development.

Results

Establishment and validation of a 3D ex vivo culture of human fetal brain

To establish the 3D ex vivo culture system of the human fetal brain, we dissected the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) from healthy fetal brain tissues at 11–14 gestational weeks, a critical period of active neurogenesis in the developing cortex19 and when MIA is most strongly associated with an increased risk of NDDs3. The average interval between fetal delivery and brain dissection was 65 min, allowing preservation of tissue integrity (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b; please see Supplementary Table 1 for the donor information). The tissue was cut into approximately 10-mm2 pieces, ensuring that each section retained intact architecture from the ventricular surface to the pial surface. These pieces were cultured in a free-floating manner on an orbital shaker (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 1c,d). The cytoarchitecture and apical–basal polarity of cells in the developing brain, particularly NSCs, are critical for the proper developmental trajectory of the cortex19. Cerebroids cultured for 8 days and 15 days retained the organized layers of SOX2+ NSCs, TBR2+ intermediate progenitor cells (IPCs) and CTIP2+ neurons (Fig. 1b). The proper apical–basal polarity of NSCs and the integrity of their processes were confirmed by adenovirus-mediated GFP labeling of NSCs (Fig. 1c), Nestin and GFAP immunostaining (Extended Data Fig. 1f,g), as well as scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging (Extended Data Fig. 1h–j). Notably, unlike cortical organoids derived from human induced PSCs (iPSCs), the expression pattern of GFAP in NSCs within cerebroids was comparable to that of acutely fixed fetal tissue (Extended Data Fig. 1g). The integrity of apical junctions in NSCs was verified by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 1d). Optimal culture conditions that ensure the proper proliferation rate of NSCs are crucial for maintaining the polarity between the ventricular and cortical surfaces20. In cerebroids, NSCs exhibited a proliferative capacity comparable to that of acutely fixed fetal cortex, as demonstrated by Ki67 staining (Fig. 1e,f), highlighting that the cerebroid culture media is optimal for maintaining the appropriate proliferation rate of NSCs. Furthermore, the capacity of NSCs to generate IPCs and neurons was confirmed through 5′-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) pulse–chase experiments (Fig. 1g–j). Mitotic nuclei of NSCs (pH3+) were also observed near the ventricular surface, mirroring the behavior of NSCs undergoing interkinetic nuclear migration during cell division in the developing cortex (Fig. 1h). Next, we verified the presence of different cell types in cerebroids including HOPX+ or PTPRZ1+ outer radial glial cells, CTIP2+ or SATB2+ neurons, S100β+ or Olig2+ macroglia precursor cells and IBA1+/CD68+ microglia (Extended Data Fig. 2a–g and Fig. 1k–m). We also confirmed the presence of vascular structures containing endothelial cells and pericytes (Fig. 1n,o and Extended Data Fig. 2h–l).

The ECM creates a dynamic environment that facilitates interactions among various cell types by modulating the diffusion and distribution of signaling molecules. It is also involved in the regulation of central developmental processes, such as cell proliferation, differentiation and migration20. We confirmed that cerebroids express key components of the ECM in the developing cortex, such as heparan sulfate, brevican (BCAN), chondroitin sulfate, collagens and ECM-associated protein Reelin (Extended Data Fig. 2m–r). Importantly, cerebroids, in contrast to iPSC-derived cortical organoids, showed expression patterns of heparan sulfate and chondroitin sulfate that were comparable to acutely fixed fetal tissue (Extended Data Fig. 2q,r).

Together, these findings demonstrate that cerebroids offer a robust platform that preserves key cellular architecture, along with immune, vascular and essential ECM components, enabling the study of both healthy neurodevelopment and disruptions caused by environmental factors.

Abnormal cortical development mediated by IL-17A

To further explore the impact of IL-17A during MIA in cortical development, we utilized cerebroids from gestational week 11–14 fetal brain tissues. Microglia/brain-associated macrophages (BAMs), as the resident immune cells of the developing cortex, are key responders to inflammatory signals and thus serve as a critical readout of IL-17A activity. To determine whether IL-17A treatment was sufficient to activate neuroinflammatory responses in the cerebroid model, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of microglia/BAMs isolated from cerebroids. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that IL-17A treatment in culture activated various inflammation-related pathways, such as TLR, TNF and NF-κB signaling (Fig. 2a,b). All donors used in this study exhibited comparable baseline inflammatory states, as determined by transcriptomic analysis of inflammatory markers in placental tissue (Extended Data Fig. 3a). IL-17A-treated cerebroids showed premature induction of cortical folding at day 8 ex vivo and the folding phenotype became more prominent at day 15 ex vivo (Fig. 2c and Extended Data Fig. 3b,c). SEM imaging further confirmed alterations in the cortical surface structure of IL-17A-treated cerebroids (Fig. 2d). To measure the degree of folding, we calculated the gyrification index (GI), the ratio of the fiducial surface length to the convex hull length, and it was found that IL-17A-treated cerebroids exhibited increased GI (Fig. 2e,f and Extended Data Fig. 3d). Moreover, IL-17A-treated cerebroids exhibited a significant increase in overall thickness, which was observed across cortical layers 2 and 3 (Fig. 2e,g and Extended Data Fig. 3e–g). Particularly, the intermediate zone and subplate (layer 2) showed reduction in cell density, suggesting a potential expansion of ECM in IL-17A-treated cerebroids (Fig. 2e). Next, we investigated which cell types contributed to the increased thickness. The number of CTIP2+ deep layer neurons was significantly higher in IL-17A-treated cerebroids, while no significant difference was observed in the number of SOX2+ NSCs or TBR2+ IPCs (Fig. 2h,i and Extended Data Fig. 3h). Additionally, IL-17A treatment increased the number of SATB2+ upper-layer neurons and NeuN+ mature neurons (Fig. 2j–l). IL-17A treatment also resulted in an elevated proportion of CTIP2+ and SATB2+ cells expressing higher levels of NeuN (Fig. 2m,n), suggesting accelerated neuronal maturation in IL-17A-treated cerebroids. The increased number of excitatory neurons induced by IL-17A is consistent with previous findings from studies modeling MIA using IL-6 in human dorsal forebrain organoids21. To further confirm this, we treated neurons differentiated from iPSC-derived neural progenitor cells (NPCs) with IL-17A and performed Ca2+ imaging. IL-17A-treated neurons exhibited increased spontaneous neuronal activity, further supporting enhanced maturation (Extended Data Fig. 4a–c). Enhanced neurogenesis by IL-17A

To identify the cellular mechanisms underlying abnormal cortical development, we examined the acute impact of IL-17A on NSCs. To determine whether the increased number of CTIP2+ neurons in IL-17A-treated cerebroids was due to elevated nascent neurogenesis, we conducted an EdU pulse–chase experiment. Following a 2-h pulse and 48-h chase, we observed a higher number of EdU+CTIP2+ neurons in IL-17A-treated cerebroids, suggesting that IL-17A increases nascent neurogenesis (Fig. 3a–c). This phenotype was driven by an increased generation of TBR2+ IPCs, as we observed a higher number of EdU+TBR2+ cells in IL-17A-treated cerebroids (Fig. 3d,e). The prolonged course of neurogenesis is a key factor in cortical expansion in humans26, and premature differentiation of neural progenitors into neurons can deplete the progenitor pool, resulting in reduced neuronal numbers and often leading to a microcephaly phenotype27. Although we detected more EdU-labeled CTIP2+ neurons and TBR2+ IPCs in IL-17A-treated cerebroids, the number of EdU+SOX2+ NSCs remained comparable between the two groups (Fig. 3d,f), suggesting that enhanced neurogenesis was accompanied by increased proliferation and replenishment of the NSC population. Notably, while no difference in the number of Ki67+ proliferating NSCs was found between vehicle and IL-17A-treated groups, a greater number of EdU+ cells and EdU+Ki67+ cells were observed in IL-17A-treated cerebroids, indicating that a higher number of EdU+ cells re-entered the cell cycle. (Fig. 3g–j). To further support the observation of increased neurogenesis induced by IL-17A, iPSC-derived cortical organoids were treated with IL-17A. Consistent with findings in cerebroids, the number of CTIP2+ neurons was increased in the IL-17A-treated organoids (Fig. 3k–m). However, we did not observe a cortical folding phenotype in IL-17A-treated organoids, suggesting that ECM components and cell types absent in organoids may be crucial in mediating this phenotype. Additionally, IL-17A treatment of iPSC-derived NPCs resulted in accelerated neurogenesis even in the absence of neuronal differentiation media, as shown by an increased number of MAP2+ cells in the IL-17A-treated group (Fig. 3n–p). These findings from our organoid and NPC experiments suggest that the increased neurogenesis induced by IL-17A is due to a direct effect on NSCs, rather than being mediated by microglia-driven immune responses. Remodeling of ECM induced by IL-17A

To uncover the molecular mechanisms underlying abnormal cortical development, we performed bulk RNA-seq on whole cerebroids, as well as on three isolated cell populations: microglia/BAMs, neuron-lineage-committed cells (NLCCs) and NSCs (Extended Data Fig. 5a)29. The cellular identities of these isolated populations were confirmed by expression levels of marker genes (Extended Data Fig. 5b). When comparing differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between vehicle-treated control and IL-17A-treated cerebroids, we identified five common categories of DEGs, including pathways related to epigenetic regulation/Sin-HDAC complex, TNF/NF-κB signaling, ECM and ECM-related glycosylation, energy metabolism and WNT signaling (Fig. 4a–d). While each population exhibited distinct Reactome pathway alterations, ECM-related pathways were consistently enriched across all cell types analyzed (Fig. 4a–d, Extended Data Fig. 5c and Supplementary Tables 2–9). Specifically, in NLCCs, IL-17A upregulated genes involved in ECM remodeling processes, including THBS2, THBS1, COL4A2, COL11A2, LAMA2, DSEL and COL9A2, which are associated with integrin cell-surface interactions, ECM degradation, collagen degradation and collagen chain trimerization in Reactome enrichment analysis (Fig. 4e). Mounting evidence shows that mitochondrial dynamics substantially influence cellular metabolism and shape the behavior of NSCs during brain development30. Notably, mitochondria-related metabolic pathways were uniquely enriched in the Reactome pathway analysis of NSCs (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Table 9), suggesting that altered mitochondria dynamics induced by IL-17A may contribute to the increased neurogenesis. This observation is consistent with the upregulated expression of neuronal differentiation-associated genes in NSCs from IL-17A-treated cerebroids (Extended Data Fig. 5d). Moreover, many DEGs in IL-17A-treated cerebroids were associated with neurodevelopmental and neurological disorders (Extended Data Fig. 6), suggesting that IL-17A may dysregulate shared pathways involved in these conditions. We further conducted a meta-analysis of transcriptomic data from post-mortem brains of individuals with ASD (Extended Data Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 10)31–33. To examine the ECM alterations induced by IL-17A, we performed mass spectrometry and proteomic analysis of three different protein fractions based on the solubility and complexity of proteins: the CHAPS fraction, containing cellular proteins, the soluble ECM (sECM) fraction and the insoluble ECM (iECM) fraction (Extended Data Fig. 8a,b). We observed more than 400 differentially abundant sECM and iECM proteins in IL-17A-treated cerebroids. (Fig. 4f,g, Extended Data Fig. 8c,d and Supplementary Tables 11–13). Protein–protein interaction network analysis of differentially abundant proteins in the CHAPS fraction of IL-17A-treated cerebroids revealed host–pathogen interaction of virus- and IL-17A-mediated signaling pathways, highlighting the activation of immune responses by IL-17A (Extended Data Fig. 9a). In the iECM fraction of IL-17A-treated cerebroids, two key chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans, BCAN and versican (VCAN), were upregulated (Fig. 4g). BCAN is a major proteoglycan in the brain’s ECM, playing critical roles in neural development, synaptic plasticity and neuronal stability34–37. VCAN contributes to the structural integrity of the developing ECM and is involved in cell proliferation, migration and synapse formation38–41. Both BCAN and VCAN bind to hyaluronic acid, a key ECM component that regulates cortical folding, cell migration, proliferation, axon guidance and synapse formation20,42. Hyaluronic acid binding was also highlighted in the protein–protein interaction network in the iECM fraction of IL-17A-treated cerebroids, along with intermediate filament organization and processing of DNA double-strand break ends (Fig. 4h).

The altered proteoglycan expression was visualized using wheat germ agglutinin (WGA), showing overall upregulation and increased perineuronal net immunoreactivity in IL-17A-treated cerebroids (Fig. 4i and Extended Data Fig. 9b). Additionally, chondroitin sulfate immunoreactivity was elevated at the sites of cortical folding in IL-17A-treated cerebroids (Fig. 4j), aligning with evidence implicating the ECM in cortical folding43,44. TEM further confirmed the presence of increased glycosaminoglycan-containing proteoglycans at the folding sites at the ultrastructural level (Fig. 4k,l). These findings suggest a strong association between altered ECM composition and IL-17A-induced cortical folding. We also observed that expression of BCAN was heightened in ventricular zones of IL-17A-treated cerebroids (Extended Data Fig. 9c). To identify the cell type(s) contributing to increased BCAN expression, we analyzed single-cell RNA-seq meta-data from the developing human cortex, comprising 37 donors between gestational weeks 8 and 26 across six independent studies45. While astrocytes and oligodendrocyte precursor cells show the highest BCAN expression, NSCs also express BCAN at notable levels (Extended Data Fig. 9d). Given the limited number of astrocytes and oligodendrocyte precursor cells at this developmental stage, NSCs are likely the primary source of BCAN. BCAN expression also increases progressively during development (Extended Data Fig. 9e). Accordingly, enhanced BCAN expression following IL-17A treatment is consistent with other accelerated maturation phenotypes, including increased SATB2+ and NeuN+ neurons in cerebroids (Fig. 2j–n) and enhanced spontaneous neuronal activity in iPSC-derived neurons (Extended Data Fig. 4). Elevated BCAN expression in NSCs was further confirmed in human iPSC-derived NPCs (Extended Data Fig. 9f). These alterations in ECM components are particularly noteworthy considering recent findings showing increased BCAN and VCAN expression in post-mortem brains of children with ASD46.

To assess the downstream signaling consequences of receptor engagement, we examined the expression of genes associated with IL-17A signaling in RNA-seq of NSCs isolated from IL-17A-treated cerebroids. This analysis revealed increased expression of genes in the IL-17 signaling pathway, including TRAF3IP2, which encodes ACT1, a critical adaptor protein that bridges IL-17 receptors to NF-κB activation (Fig. 5f). In parallel, we observed downregulation of NFKBIB (Fig. 5f) and TNFAIP3 (Fig. 4d), negative regulators of NF-κB, consistent with a shift toward sustained inflammatory signaling. These transcriptional changes provide strong evidence that IL-17A promotes durable NF-κB activation in NSCs rather than a transient or self-limiting response. To further substantiate this conclusion, we employed complementary proteomic analysis. Proteomic profiling of the CHAPs fraction from IL-17A-treated cerebroids revealed differential abundance of several NF-κB-associated proteins in response to IL-17A, including direct activators such as TRAF6 and TRAF247, and indirect activators such as MAP2K6, RAP1A, RAPGEF1 and CLIP348–50 (Fig. 5g). Furthermore, we confirmed the upregulated expression of ACT1 and TRAF6 upon IL-17A treatment in NPCs using immunofluorescence (Fig. 5h–k). In addition, to identify key transcriptional regulators modulating the expression of DEGs from our RNA-seq analysis, we performed transcription factor enrichment analysis using Enrichr. Among the many transcriptional regulators, five, encoded by HDAC1, NFKB1, REL, RELA and SPI1, were found to be shared across at least two different cell types and four of them are associated with NF-κB signaling, confirming a clear NF-κB activation signature in IL-17A-treated cerebroids (Fig. 5l). These orthogonal approaches converge on the same conclusion that IL-17A activates NF-κB signaling in the developing human cortex.

We next sought to functionally validate these findings using human iPSC-derived NPCs. We confirmed that IL-17A robustly induces phosphorylation of the NF-κB subunit p65, accompanied by its nuclear translocation. Unlike TNF, used as a positive control for activation of NF-κB signaling, which elicited only a transient activation, IL-17A maintained nuclear p65 localization even after 24 h, highlighting the persistent nature of the response (Fig. 5m,n). Immunofluorescence experiments further showed that increased expression of phospho-p65 after IL-17A treatment was rescued by co-treatment with PTL, a pharmacological NF-κB inhibitor through its suppression of IκB kinase activity47 (Fig. 5o,p).

Together, these results provide multimodal evidence, spanning transcriptomics, proteomics and pharmacological functional assay, that IL-17A directly engages its receptors on NSCs and activates NF-κB signaling in a sustained manner. This sustained activation distinguishes IL-17A from other inflammatory cytokines such as TNF and points to a mechanism by which MIA may drive prolonged inflammatory states in the developing human cortex.

Reversed abnormal cortical development by PTL

To confirm the involvement of NF-κB signaling pathways in the abnormal cortical development phenotype, cerebroids were treated with PTL (Fig. 6a). While PTL alone did not induce any observable phenotype, it reversed the cortical folding and thickness abnormalities across different cortical layers in IL-17A-treated cerebroids (Fig. 6b–h). Furthermore, PTL treatment mitigated the excessive neurogenesis, as evidenced by a reduction in the number of CTIP2+, SATB2+ and NeuN+ neurons in IL-17A-treated cerebroids (Fig. 6i,j). Given the upregulation of BCAN in the ventricular zone of IL-17A-treated cerebroids (Extended Data Fig. 9c), we investigated whether PTL could reverse this effect as well. Following PTL treatment, BCAN expression in the ventricular zone was decreased, reaching levels similar to those observed in the vehicle group (Fig. 6k,l). Discussion

In this study, we developed a robust high-throughput 3D ex vivo culture system, cerebroids, which faithfully preserve key components of cortical development such as cellular architecture, apical–basal polarity and essential immune, vascular and ECM elements. The preservation of cellular integrity, heterogeneity and ECM niche within cerebroids makes this model uniquely suited for investigating normal developmental processes and cell–cell interactions, as well as disruptions caused by environmental insults.

Utilizing the cerebroid, we demonstrated that IL-17A induces cortical abnormalities associated with MIA, including premature cortical folding, increased thickness and accelerated neuronal maturation, driven by enhanced neurogenesis in NSCs. Modeling MIA in cerebroids has revealed clinically relevant cellular and molecular phenotypes associated with NDDs, such as microglia-driven immune responses, structural cortical changes, an increased number of excitatory neurons and alterations in the ECM22–25. Furthermore, this model offers a unique opportunity to dissect the molecular and mechanistic basis of these abnormalities in humans during early development, a stage that is typically inaccessible. Our transcriptomic and proteomic analyses revealed that IL-17A remodels the ECM, with contributions from various cortical cell types.

Despite the essential role of the ECM in key developmental processes20,37, its involvement in various NDDs has been relatively underexplored. Our findings, which demonstrate ECM remodeling in response to MIA in ex vivo cultured human fetal brain tissues, alongside the altered expression of ECM-related genes observed in post-mortem brains of individuals with ASD, highlight the critical need for a comprehensive investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying ECM alterations and their developmental implications in the human brain.

A central advance of this study is the demonstration that IL-17A directly activates NF-κB signaling in human NSCs. We show that IL-17 receptors are expressed in NSCs, are upregulated upon IL-17A exposure and engage downstream signaling cascades involving ACT1. Transcriptomic profiling revealed upregulation of TRAF3IP2 and downregulation of the NF-κB inhibitors, TNFAIP3 and NFKBIB, indicating sustained inflammatory activation. Proteomics further identified NF-κB-associated activators such as TRAF6 and TRAF2, while transcription factor enrichment confirmed a robust NF-κB activation signature. These findings were functionally validated in human iPSC-derived NPCs, where IL-17A induced prolonged phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of p65, in contrast to the transient response triggered by TNF. Crucially, this sustained NF-κB activation, along with increased phospho-p65 expression, was rescued by the NF-κB inhibitor PTL. Additionally, PTL treatment successfully reversed IL-17A-induced cortical folding and neurogenesis abnormalities as well as aberrant upregulation of BCAN expression in cerebroids, implicating NF-κB signaling involvement. Together, these multimodal data establish NF-κB signaling as a mechanistic driver of IL-17A-induced abnormalities in human cortical development.

Our study extends prior findings from rodent models and human NPC studies by demonstrating that IL-17A directly engages its receptors on human fetal cortex-derived NSCs, induces sustained NF-κB signaling and remodels ECM composition. Whereas earlier work in human NPCs identified ERK/mTOR pathways as IL-17A targets without evidence of NF-κB activation51, our multimodal approach reveals a broader signaling repertoire with functional consequences for cortical architecture. These results advance understanding of how MIA perturbs human corticogenesis and provide a platform for dissecting molecular pathways that may underlie NDD risk.

Epidemiological, clinical and animal studies have shown that males are more vulnerable to NDDs associated with MIA, both in terms of prevalence and symptom manifestation52. Sex differences in response to MIA are likely influenced by a complex interplay of hormonal, placental and microglia-mediated inflammation53, although the precise mechanisms remain unclear. Due to limited tissue availability, sex differences were not explored in this study and further research is needed to address this gap.

In this study we cultured cerebroids for 15 d at most. A recent study demonstrated that fetal brain organoids can be maintained in long-term culture through continuous tissue fragmentation54. However, maintaining proper cytoarchitectural polarity remains a major challenge, emphasizing the need for future efforts to optimize long-term cerebroid culture protocols to preserve normal developmental trajectory, cytoarchitecture and cellular integrity. Achieving this will enable more comprehensive, long-term investigations into the impact of environmental factors on cortical development. Nonetheless, cerebroids provide a robust platform for investigating the acute effects of environmental insults.

In conclusion, we have established cerebroids as a robust, physiologically relevant model for interrogating complex neurodevelopmental processes. We have also demonstrated their application as a platform to study IL-17A-mediated disruptions in cortical development during MIA. In addition to incorporating neuronal and glial elements, cerebroids also integrate immune and vascular components, along with the ECM. This physiologically relevant complexity offers a unique platform to explore cell–cell and cell–ECM interactions across diverse cell populations, providing valuable insights into healthy brain development and NDDs.

Methods

Ethics statement

All research involving human fetal tissue was conducted in accordance with relevant ethical regulations and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Human fetal tissue was obtained through the Scottish Advanced Fetal Research (SAFeR) study (NCT04613583), which was approved by the Scottish National Health Service Research Ethics Committee (REC 15/NS/0123). Written, informed consent was obtained from all participants before tissue donation. Human iPSC and NPC lines were obtained from commercial sources and used in accordance with relevant ethical regulations. The stem cell aspects of this study are consistent with the principles outlined in the 2021 ISSCR Guidelines for Stem Cell Research and Clinical Translation.

Human fetal tissue collection

Fetal tissues from elective termination of normally progressing pregnancies were collected as part of the SAFeR study (NCT04613583)55. The collection process is approved by Scottish National Health Service Research Ethics Committee (REC 15/NS/0123) and follows the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines. Women seeking elective medical termination of pregnancy were recruited with written, informed consent by NHS Grampian research nurses, who operate independently from the research team. Participants received a Patient Information Sheet at least 24 h before consent was obtained and provided written, informed consent before enrollment in the study. The normal pregnancies were determined by ultrasound, from women over 16 years of age who spoke English and covering gestational ages from 7 to 20 weeks. Grossly abnormal fetuses were excluded, and women experiencing substantial distress were not approached. Termination was carried out using RU-486 (Mifepristone) treatment (200 mg) followed by prostaglandin-induced delivery, as reported previously56. Gestational age was confirmed by ultrasound and foot length measurement, and various maternal and fetal data were recorded. For sample collection, fetuses were immediately transported to the laboratory (Extended Data Fig. 1a), typically intact, and were weighed, measured for crown–rump length and sexed by morphology and PCR detection of sex chromosome-specific genes (ZFX and SRY) or the absence of the Y chromosome. Brain tissues from 16 donors of 11 to 14 weeks of gestation collected between 11 May 2022 and 22 April 2024 were included in this study (Supplementary Table 1). Tissue samples used in this study were consumed during experimental procedures and are no longer available for distribution.

Brain dissection and tissue culture

Whole brain tissue was transferred to cold Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS) (Gibco, cat. no. 14175095) on ice for dissection. The pia matter was carefully removed. Full-thickness, approximately 10-mm2-sized pieces of right DLPFC tissue were dissected and plated in six-well tissue culture plates (16–24 cerebroids per well). Dissection time was kept to a minimum (~30 min) and cerebroids were immediately transferred to the fresh culture medium (4 ml per well) containing a 1:1 mixture (v/v) of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12, Gibco, cat. no. 11330-032) and Neurobasal Medium (Gibco, cat. no. 21103-049), supplemented with 1 × GlutaMAX (Gibco, cat. no. 35050-038), 1 × MEM nonessential amino acids Solution (Gibco, cat. no. 11140-050), 1 × serum-free B27 (Gibco, cat. no. 17504-044), 1 × N-2 (Gibco, cat. no. 17502-048), 1 × 2-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, cat. no. 21985-023), 2.5 μg ml−1 human recombinant insulin (Gibco, 12585-014) and 1 × antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco, cat. no. 15240-096). Cerebroids were cultured in a free-floating manner on a CO2-resistent orbital shaker (Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 15351105) at a rotational rate of 120 per min in a humidified incubator (37 °C and 5% CO2). Culture medium was changed on alternate days.

Cell culture

The human iPSC line KOLF2.1J purchased from The Jackson Laboratory was used to generate cortical organoids and iPSC-derived microglia-like cells. Human iPSCs were cultured in mTeSR Plus Medium containing mTeSR Plus Basal Medium (StemCell, cat. no. 100-0274), mTeSR Plus 5X Supplement (StemCell, cat. no. 100-0275) and 1 × penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, cat. no. 15140-122).

Human iPSC line SCTi003-A-derived NPCs (StemCell, cat. no. 200-0620) were cultured in STEMdiff neural progenitor medium (StemCell, cat. no. 05833) supplemented with 1 × penicillin–streptomycin according to the supplier’s instructions.

NPCs were differentiated into forebrain neurons using STEMdiff Forebrain Neuron Differentiation Medium (StemCell, cat. no. 08600) in Laminin/Poly-L-Ornithine (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. LPLO001)-coated 12-well plates followed by maturation in STEMdiff Forebrain Neuron Maturation Medium (StemCell, cat. no. 08605).

Human iPSC-derived microglia-like cells were generated from Kolf2.1J cells using a previously published protocol57.

iPSC-derived cortical organoid culture

Human KOLF2.1J iPSC-derived cortical organoids were generated using previously published protocols58,59. iPSCs were seeded into ultra-low-attachment 96-well plates (Corning, cat. no. 7007) at 5 × 104 cells per well in mTeSR-based medium supplemented with ROCK inhibitor (1:2,000), followed by centrifugation (300g, 5 min, 4 °C) and incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 48 h to form embryoid bodies. At day 2, embryoid bodies were transferred (~24 per well) to ultra-low-attachment six-well plates (Corning, cat. no. 3471) and cultured on an orbital shaker (120 rpm) in neural induction medium consisting of DMEM/F12 (Gibco, cat. no. 11330-032), 20% KnockOut Serum Replacement (Gibco, cat. no. 10828-028), GlutaMAX (Gibco, cat. no. 35050-038), nonessential amino acids (Gibco, cat. no. 11140-050), 2-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, cat. no. 21985-023), penicillin–streptomycin, heparin (StemCell, cat. no. 7980) and dual SMAD inhibitors SB431542 (StemCell, cat. no. 72234; 5 µM) and LDN-193189 (StemCell, cat. no. 72147; 1 µM), with ROCK inhibitor included for an additional 48 h. Medium was refreshed daily for days 4–5, and from day 6 cultures were transitioned to a patterning medium containing DMEM/F12, N2 supplement (Gibco, cat. no. 17502-048), GlutaMAX, nonessential amino acids, 2-mercaptoethanol, penicillin/streptomycin and CHIR99021 (StemCell Technologies, cat. no. 72054; 3 µM). At day 7, morphologically healthy embryoid bodies were embedded in Matrigel in a 2:3 mixture with medium and plated as droplets, allowed to polymerize at 37 °C and cultured for 48 h in patterning medium without agitation, with partial medium changes every other day for days 9–13. At day 14, organoids were mechanically released from Matrigel, washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and transferred to maturation medium consisting of a 1:1 mixture of DMEM/F12 and Neurobasal medium (Gibco, cat. no. 21103-049) supplemented with N2, B27 (Gibco, cat. no. 17504-044), GlutaMAX, nonessential amino acids, penicillin–streptomycin, 2-mercaptoethanol, insulin (Gibco, cat. no. 12585-014) and CHIR99021 (1.5 µM), and maintained on an orbital shaker (120 rpm) with half-medium changes every other day.

IL-17A treatment

MIA was modeled by treating cerebroids and organoids with 100 ng ml−1 human recombinant IL-17A (PeproTech, cat. no. 200-17) every other day for a week (Figs. 2a and 3k). The dose was chosen based on the previous in vivo60–64 and two-dimensional (2D)61,65,66 and 3D62,67,68 in vitro studies. The dose of IL-17A used in earlier studies has been variable, ranging from 10 ng ml−1 to 500 ng ml−1 in 2D and 25 ng ml−1 to 500 ng ml−1 culture medium in 3D in vitro cultures. We added 100 ng ml−1 IL-17A to the cerebroid and organoid culture every 48 h. For both vehicle- and IL-17A-treated groups, two-thirds of the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing vehicle or IL-17A. 2D cultured NPCs were treated with 12.5 ng ml−1 IL-17A either daily for 5 d (Fig. 3n) or 2 d (Fig. 5h–k), or as single treatment for 30 min and 24 h (Fig. 5i–l). NPC-derived forebrain neurons were treated with 12.5 ng ml−1 IL-17A every 2 d for 1 week (Extended Data Fig. 4a).

TNF treatment

NPCs from 2D cultures were treated with a single dose of 25 ng ml−1 TNF (PeproTech, cat. no. 300-01A-50UG) for 30 min and 24 h.

PTL treatment

Cerebroids were treated with 25 µM (-)-PTL (Selleckchem, cat. no. S2341) in cerebroid culture medium on alternative days for a week with and without IL-17A treatment (Fig. 5b). We used 0.2% DMSO as vehicle control. 2D NPCs were treated with 10 µM PTL in combination with 12.5 ng ml−1 IL-17A (Fig. 5o,p).

Tissue preparation and immunofluorescent staining

Cerebroids were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 60 min at room temperature and cryoprotected with 30% sucrose overnight at 4 °C. Fetal brain tissue was fixed in 4% PFA in PBS overnight and cryoprotected sequentially with 15% and 30% sucrose at 4 °C. Cortical organoids were fixed in 4% PFA in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. Cerebroids and fetal brain tissue were cryo-sectioned at −20 °C in a cryostat (Leica Biosystems, cat. no. CM1850) perpendicular to the ventricular surface at 20-μm thickness, and stored at −20 °C. Frozen slides were gradually equilibrated to room temperature before immunostaining. For each set of staining, a minimum of 3–4 sections 160–200 μm apart were included per cerebroid. After washing and permeabilization with TBS+ (0.05 M Tris-buffered saline, 0.1% Triton-X, pH 9.0) and antigen retrieval with 1 × Target Retrieval Solution (Dako Agilent, cat. no. S169984-2) at 88–90 °C for 15 min in a plastic Coplin staining jar (Epredia, cat. no. 194), sections were blocked with the blocking solution TBS++ (containing 10% donkey serum in TBS with 0.1% Triton-X) for 1 h at room temperature. Tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 14) diluted in TBS with 0.1% Triton-X and 3% donkey serum overnight at 4 °C in an immunostaining moisture chamber (Agar Scientific, cat. no. AGL4555). A longer incubation time (48–72 h) was used for primary staining with anti-HOPX antibodies. After washing, tissues were incubated with species-specific secondary antibodies (Supplementary Table 14) and DAPI (1:5,000) diluted in TBS+ with 3% donkey serum for 2 h at room temperature.

NPCs cultured on chamber slides were fixed with 4% PFA in PBS at room temperature for 15 min.

For WGA staining of fixed tissue, cerebroid sections were washed with PBS without permeabilization agents, and incubated with Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated WGA (Invitrogen, cat. no. 11590816) in PBS at 10 µg ml−1 for 30–60 min at room temperature.

EdU pulse–chase experiments

For EdU pulse–chase experiments, 10 μM EdU (Invitrogen, cat. no. A10044) was added to culture medium for 2 h (Figs. 1g and 3a).

EdU detection was done after incubation with the secondary antibodies using the Click-iT EdU cell proliferation kit for imaging and Alexa Fluor 647 dye (Invitrogen, cat. no. C10340) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Genetic labeling of radial glia cells using adenovirus

The recombinant human Adenovirus Type 5 (dE1/E3)-expressing enhanced green fluorescent protein (Vector Biolabs, cat. no. 1060) or red fluorescent protein (Vector Biolabs, cat. no. 1660) under CMV promoter was used to selectively label apical radial glia cells in the cerebroids. We added ~15–20 µl of DMEM/F12 containing 1 × 107 plaque forming units per µl of adenovirus directly on the ventricular surface of the cerebroids after removing the culture medium. Cerebroids were incubated for 20–30 min at 37 °C and 5% CO2 without agitation. Cerebroids were then washed three times with DPBS and cultured in the fresh medium.

Calcium imaging

Neurons were incubated with 1:1 v/v 10 µM Fluor-4-AM (Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 11504786) and Pluronic acid (Fisher Scientific, cat. no. P3000MP) in the culture medium for 30 min in the incubator. Cells were washed three times with HBSS without calcium and magnesium and then incubated with HBSS containing calcium and magnesium for 20 min at room temperature. Cells were imaged on the EVOS M5000 imaging system (Thermo Fisher, cat. no. AMF5000SV) with the EVOS onstage incubator (Thermo Fisher, cat. no. AMC2000) with the following settings: 37 °C, 5% CO2 and 80% humidity.

Images were analyzed using ImageJ. Movement artifacts were removed using Linear Stack Alignment with SIFT plugin and photo bleach correction performed using the exponential bleach correction function. Circular regions of interest (ROIs) were drawn for individual neurons and fluorescence intensities were measured across the stack. ∆F/Fmin values were calculated and plotted in GraphPad Prism. Heatmaps were drawn in R using ComplexHeatmap package v2.20.0 (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/ComplexHeatmap.html).

Immunofluorescence image acquisition and image analysis

Images were acquired on a laser scanning confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, cat. no. LSM880) using ×20, ×40 (oil immersion) and ×63 (oil immersion) objectives. Uniform laser settings were used across experimental groups where intensity-based analysis was planned. For uniformity, images were obtained from the central third of the cerebroid sections. At least two to three sections were imaged for each cerebroid.

Images were analyzed using Fiji ImageJ software. To achieve consistent and reproducible results, we applied a uniform image acquisition, pre-processing and analysis pipeline to count cells across the experimental groups for a given set of staining. Rectangular ROIs were drawn with the base of the rectangle parallel to the ventricular surface and length covering the whole thickness of the cerebroid section to include all layers (Extended Data Fig. 3f,g). The width of these ROIs was kept constant for comparison across experimental groups and replicates. For quantification of nuclei positive for nuclear markers (for example, SOX2 and CTIP2), images were segmented through ImageJ interactive watershed plugin version 1.2.1 (https://imagej.net/plugins/interactive-watershed) and binary masks were generated. Uniform settings (seed dynamics, threshold intensity and peak flooding) were used for segmenting nuclei across experimental groups for each set of staining. Numbers of binarized nuclei were quantified using the analyze particle function in ImageJ. For all quantitative analysis, images were blinded before quantification.

No preprocessing was performed for intensity-based quantification analysis (Fig. 2m,n). For nuclear fluorescence intensity measurement of different markers, nuclei were segmented from DAPI images using interactive watershed plugin version 1.2.1 (Extended Data Fig. 3h), and individual masks were generated for these nuclei and applied to the 8-bit images of various markers (for example, CTIP2, SATB2 and NeuN). We measured the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) for these individual nuclei. Nuclei less than 8 or greater than 200 µm in size were filtered out. Fluorescence intensities were corrected for background noise by subtracting average background intensities.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic MFI were quantified by applying compartment-specific masks. Nuclear masks were generated using SOX2 staining, and cytoplasmic masks were generated using Nestin staining. ACT1 fluorescence intensity was measured within cytoplasmic masks, while phosphorylated p65 fluorescence intensity was measured within both nuclear and cytoplasmic masks.

Measurement of cortical folding

Cortical folding was quantified by measuring GI (Fig. 2f) on low-magnification (×4 or ×10) DAPI images of cerebroids.

GI was calculated as follows:

GI = length of fiducial (inner) surface/length of (outer) convex hull

Measurement of layer thickness

Total thickness of a cerebroid was measured as an average of three vertical lines drawn perpendicular to the ventricular surface through the central part of the DAPI-stained images of a cerebroid. Three layers of cerebroids (layers 1–3) were defined based on the cell densities (layer 1 and layer 3 being cell-dense, layer 2 being cell-sparse) and staining of specific markers (SOX2- and TBR2-enriched layer 1, CTIP2-enriched layer 3) (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 3f,g).

Electron microscopy

Tissue was fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde (TAAB, cat. no. G010) and 4% formaldehyde (Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 28909) in 0.1 M cacodylate (TAAB, cat. no. S007) buffer for 48 h at 4 °C and stored in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer at 4 °C until further processing. Samples were postfixed with 1% osmium tetroxide (TAAB, cat. no. O021) and 1.5% potassium ferrocyanide (Sigma, cat. no. P-3289) in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, washed and sequentially dehydrated with 35%, 50%, 70% and 95% ethanol followed by acetone. This was followed by Spurr’s infiltration and embedding in Spurr’s resin for 48 h at 60 °C. Sections 90-nm thick were prepared using a diamond knife (Diatome) onto formvar/carbon-coated copper grids (EMResolutions) using a Leica UC6 ultramicrotome and contrast stained with Uranyless (Labtech) and lead citrate (Leica) using a Leica AC20. Samples were viewed by TEM on the JEM-1400Plus (JEOL) and images were captured using an UltraVUE camera (AMT). SEM images were acquired on a ZEISS EVO MA10.

WGA staining for live tissue

For live staining with Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated WGA (Invitrogen, cat. no. 11590816), cerebroids were transferred to the wells of a 12-well plate containing WGA diluted in HBSS at 10 µg ml−1 for 30 min at room temperature. Cerebroids were washed three times with HBSS, imaged and transferred back to the fresh culture medium.

Assessment of the basal inflammation level of the donor fetal tissue

RNA was extracted from snap-frozen and pulverized placental tissue and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA using the GoScript Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Promega, 2790). Primers for C3, TNF, IL6, IL1B and NOS2 were designed using Integrated DNA Technologies PCR designing web tool (https://www.idtdna.com/page) (Supplementary Table 15). Transcript expression was quantified by reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT–qPCR) using the LightCycler 480 System and LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master (Roche). Samples were run in triplicate against a six-point standard curve for each transcript (Absolute Quantification analysis) using the following parameters: 95 °C for 5 min, 45 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 63 °C for 15 s and 72 °C for 10 s, followed by a melting curve.

Tissue dissociation and single-cell isolation

Cerebroids were dissociated into single cells through enzymatic digestion and mechanical dissociation using a previously published protocol69 with the following modifications: the myelin removal step with the Percoll method was not required and the duration of enzymatic digestion and mechanical dissociation was kept at 15 min.

Immunomagnetic cell sorting

Microglia were isolated through immunomagnetic positive selection of CD11b+ cells by using EasySep Human CD11b Positive Selection and Depletion Kit (StemCell, cat. no. 100-0742) and The Big Easy EasySep Magnet (StemCell, cat. no. 18001) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. CD24+THY1−CD11b− and CD24−THY1−CD11b− cells representing NLCCs and NSCs, respectively29, were isolated from a CD11b-depleted single-cell population. CD11b− cells were subjected sequentially to immunomagnetic sorting with biotinylated anti-THY-1 (CD90) antibodies (StemCell, cat. no. 60045BT) and anti-CD24 antibodies (Miltenyi Biotec, cat. no. 130-098-902) using the EasySep Release Human Biotin Positive Selection Kit (StemCell, cat. no. 17653) and EasySep Magnet according to the manufacturer’s guidelines (Extended Data Fig. 5a).

RNA extraction and RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted from whole cerebroids and isolated cells using the RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, cat. no. 74104) according to the manufacturer’s instructions in an RNase-free environment. Residual genomic DNA was removed by on-column digestion with RNase-free DNase I (Qiagen, cat. no. 79254). RNA quantity was measured on a Qubit Fluorimeter and RNA integrity number on an Agilent TapeStation. cDNA libraries were prepared with 50 ng of input at 14 cycles of amplification using Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep, Ligation kit (cat. no. 20040532) and sequenced on a NextSeq 500 machine.

FASTQ files of single-end reads were processed using FASTP70. Processed reads were mapped to human reference genome assembly GRCh38 and transcript-level quantification was obtained using Salmon v1.10.271. Transcript-level quantification data were imported in R, and gene-level quantification was obtained using tximeta72. Differential gene expression was estimated using DESeq2 package73. Volcano plots were generated using ggplot2 (version 3.5.1) and ggrepel (version 0.9.6) packages.

Heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap package. Read counts were normalized with DESeq2 and variance-stabilizing-transformed. Z-scores were calculated for each gene across samples, and mean Z-scores from three donors per group were plotted.

Protein fractionation and proteomic analysis

For proteomic analysis using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS), vehicle- and IL-17A-treated day 8 cerebroids were collected from three donors and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Protein extraction, fractionation and LC–MS/MS (Extended Data Fig. 8a) were performed using a previously published three-step decellularization and ECM extraction protocol that yields cellular (CHAPS), sECM and iECM fractions74.

Protein-level abundance quantification data were imported in R. For each fraction, proteins with nonzero abundance in at least two samples were included in the analysis. Protein abundance values were log-transformed and normalized with the median centered method of data normalization. Missing values were imputed by random draws from a Gaussian distribution centered to a minimal value using the impute.MinProb() command in the imputeLCMD package v2.1. The SummarizedExperiment package was used to generate a summarized experiment object; the limma75 package (version 3.19) was used to identify differentially abundant proteins by applying a multifactorial design assay including the treatment group and donor.

Meta-analysis of RNA-seq data

For the meta-data of post-mortem brains of individuals with ASD, we searched PubMed and Web of Science for studies published in English, from database inception to 30 May 2023, that investigated the transcriptome of human ASD brains. We used the search terms (‘ASD’ OR ‘autism’) AND (‘brain’ OR ‘cortex’) AND (‘transcriptome’ OR ‘RNAseq’ OR ‘microarray’ OR ‘gene expression’). This led to identification of three microarray76–78, six bulk31–33,79–81 and three single-nuclear RNA-seq33,82,83 studies on the post-mortem brain tissue of ASD cases and controls. Data from one study80, after applying the filters, gave a list of only six upregulated genes (two mitochondrial genes and four small nucleolar RNA genes) and were excluded from further analysis. Studies that included bulk RNA-seq data from the frontal cortex31–33 and meta-analysis of microarray data32 were selected for further analysis. To identify functionally enriched terms and pathways in an unbiased manner, we applied a protein sub-network-based functional enrichment analysis using STRING v12.084. Functional enrichment analysis was performed for each individual study, with genes ranked according to log2 fold change in ASD versus control brains. A list of all enriched pathways for four studies is provided in Supplementary Table 10. Terms enriched in at least two independent studies, with 50 or more genes and with a false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P < 0.05, were considered significant and plotted (Extended Data Fig. 7).

For analyses of BCAN and IL-17 receptor expression, we used previously published single-cell RNA-seq metadata45 from 37 donors spanning gestational weeks 8 to 26 across six independent studies85–90. Gene expression for each cell type within each donor was aggregated into a pseudobulk profile, with one cell type per donor treated as a single observation.

Functional enrichment analysis

For transcriptomic and proteomic data, we applied a protein sub-network-based functional enrichment analysis using STRING v12.0 as described above. K-means clustering was performed on the protein–protein interaction network of differentially abundant proteins using STRING.

Overrepresentation analysis was performed using Enrichr91 (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/). Enrichr was used to identify a list of enriched transcription factors having regulatory interactions with DEGs in the human TRRUST v2 (Transcriptional Regulatory Relationships Unraveled by Sentence-based Text mining) reference database92.

The list of genes implicated in neurological disorders is adopted from a recent study93.

Statistical analysis and data visualization

No statistical methods were used to pre-determine sample sizes. Sample sizes were determined by the availability of human fetal tissue and practical considerations associated with the experimental procedures. GraphPad Prism (version 8.4.3) and the R (version 4.3.1) statistical computing environment (https://www.r-project.org/) were used to analyze data and generate graphs. Samples were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Data normality was formally assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots, and homogeneity of variance was assessed before statistical analysis where appropriate. Data collection and analysis were performed blind to the experimental conditions. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was applied to compare across experimental groups (vehicle- versus IL-17A-treated) and donors. For inter-group comparison across multiple groups, Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was performed. Donor-level quantification is represented as mean ± s.e.m. while individual data points represent technical replicates. No data point was excluded from all the analyses. On average, 2–3 sections per cerebroid and 2–3 cerebroids per donor per group were analyzed for each quantification. The numbers of donors for each quantification are provided in the figure legends.

Plots were made in GraphPad Prism or R and edited in Inkscape v 1.3.2.
