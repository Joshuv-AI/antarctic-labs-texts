Additive Approximation Schemes for Low-Dimensional Embeddings
Prashanti Anderson, Ainesh Bakshi, Samuel B. Hopkins

Abstract
We consider the task of fitting low-dimensional embeddings to high-dimensional data. In particular, we study the k k -Euclidean Metric Violation problem ( k k -EMV ), where the input is D ∈ ℝ ⩾ 0 ( n 2 ) D\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}}_{\geqslant 0} and the goal is to find the closest vector X ∈ 𝕄 k X\in\mathbb{M}_{k} , where 𝕄 k ⊂ ℝ ⩾ 0 ( n 2 ) \mathbb{M}_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}}_{\geqslant 0} is the set of all k k -dimensional Euclidean metrics on n n points, and closeness is formulated as the following optimization problem, where ∥ ⋅ ∥ \|\cdot\| is the entry-wise ℓ 2 \ell_{2} norm: OPT EMV = min X ∈ 𝕄 k ⁡ ∥ D − X ∥ 2 2 . \textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{EMV}}=\min_{X\in\mathbb{M}_{k}}\lVert D-X\rVert_{2}^{2}\,. Cayton and Dasgupta [ CD06 ] showed that this problem is NP-Hard, even when k = 1 k=1 . Dhamdhere [ Dha04 ] obtained a 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( n ) ) \mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log(n)\right) -approximation for 1 1 -EMV and leaves finding a PTAS for it as an open question (reiterated recently by Lee [ Lee25 ] ). Although k k -EMV has been studied in the statistics community for over 70 years, under the name “multi-dimensional scaling,” there are no known efficient approximation algorithms for k > 1 k>1 , to the best of our knowledge. We provide the first polynomial-time additive approximation scheme for k k -EMV . In particular, we obtain an embedding with objective value OPT EMV + ε ​ ∥ D ∥ 2 2 \textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{EMV}}+\varepsilon\lVert D\rVert_{2}^{2} in ( n ⋅ B ) poly ⁡ ( k , ε − 1 ) (n\cdot B)^{\poly(k,\varepsilon^{-1})} time, where each entry in D D can be represented by B B bits. We believe our algorithm is a crucial first step towards obtaining a PTAS for k k -EMV . Our key technical contribution is a new analysis of correlation rounding for Sherali-Adams / Sum-of-Squares relaxations, tailored to low-dimensional embeddings. We also show that our techniques allow us to obtain additive approximation schemes for two related problems: a weighted variant of k k -EMV and ℓ p \ell_{p} low-rank approximation for p > 2 p>2 .

The second, more broadly, is to expand the reach of LP/SDP hierarchies to solve approximation algorithms problems beyond combinatorial optimization, by extending global correlation rounding beyond discrete optimization.

1.1
Results

k
-Euclidean Metric Violation.

We begin by formally defining the problem of finding the closest
k
-dimensional Euclidean metric under the
\ell_{2}^{2}
-norm.
We write
\E_{i\sim[n]}f(i)
for the uniform average of a function
f\,:\,[n]\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
.

Problem 1

(Euclidean Metric Violation)
.

Given a set of
\binom{n}{2}
distances
\Set{d_{ij}}_{ij\in[n]}
, the
k
-Euclidean Metric Violation
(
k
-EMV
) objective finds an embedding
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{EMV}}(d)=\min_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}}\E_{ij\sim[n]}\left\lparen d_{ij}-\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert_{2}\right\rparen^{2}

We aim to design approximation algorithms in the regime
k=O(1)
– recall, even the
k=1
case is NP-hard to solve exactly
[
CD06
]
.
Aside from being of interest in its own right, the constant
k
case is most relevant to visualization applications.
Furthermore, as we discuss below, linear dimensionality reduction techniques like random linear projections give particularly poor guarantees when
k=O(1)
.
This seems to force any algorithm with strong provable guarantees for
k
-EMV
to find non-linear dimension reductions, a goal of interest in its own right in the long-term project of obtaining a theoretical understanding of widespread methods like Isomap, t-SNE, locally linear embeddings, and autoencoders
[
HR02
,
HS06
,
TSL00
,
RS00
]
.

Despite its natural formulation, almost all algorithmic work on
k
-EMV
is heuristic – there are only a handful of approaches with provable guarantees.
Beyond the
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log n\right)
-approximation of
[
Dha04
]

, we are aware of two possible approaches for the constant-

First, one can take the optimal
n

[
BFN19
]

.
This yields a solution of objective value at most
\textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{EMV}}(d)+\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}/{k}\right)

Since
\textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{EMV}}(d)\leqslant\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}

Another approach, due to Demaine, Hesterberg, Koehler, Lynch, and Urschel
[
DHKLU21
]
, produces a non-linear embedding by adapting a polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS) for solving dense constraint satisfaction problems.

Theorem 1.1

Given input

B

Recent work by Bakshi, Cohen, Hopkins, Jayaram, and Lattanzi
[

2.1
Warmup:
n^{2^{B}}
-time Euclidean Metric Violation via Sherali Adams

We start by describing an “orthodox” use of techniques from dense constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs) to obtain an additive approximation scheme for Euclidean Metric Violation whose running time is polynomial in
n
but doubly-exponential in the input size, à la
[
DHKLU21
]
.
This guarantee can be obtained by black-box use of any PTAS for dense Max-CSPs, but it will be convenient for us to describe a Sherali-Adams linear programming approach, which we will later modify to obtain our algorithms.

Discretization and Sherali-Adams LP.

Recall,
\{d_{ij}\}_{ij\in[n]}
is an arbitrary matrix such that each
d_{i,j}\geqslant 0
.
To obtain an LP relaxation of
\min_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}}\E_{ij}(d_{ij}-|x_{i}-x_{j}|)^{2}
,
we start with a standard discretization argument.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that either
d_{i,j}=0
or
1\leqslant d_{ij}\leqslant 2^{B}
for a bit-complexity parameter
B
.
It is not hard to show that there is a solution
x
with objective value
\textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{EMV}}+\varepsilon\cdot\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}
in which each
x_{i}
assumes values in a discrete subset
S
of
\mathbbm{R}
with around
\poly\left(2^{B}/\varepsilon\right)
points, each of magnitude at most
\poly\left(2^{B}/\varepsilon\right)
.
(A slightly more sophisticated argument with a geometrically-spaced grid actually shows that
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(B/\varepsilon\right)
values per variable
x_{i}
suffices – we will return to this later).

A solution to the degree-
t
Sherali-Adams linear programming relaxation is a
degree-
t
pseudo-distribution
over
S^{n}
.
A degree-
t
pseudo-distribution
\mu
consists of
local distributions

\{\mu_{T}\}_{T\in\binom{n}{t}}
, where each
\mu_{T}
is a distribution on
S^{t}
, and the distributions are locally consistent, meaning that
\mu_{T},\mu_{U}
induce identical marginal distributions on
T\cap U
– we often write
\mu_{T\cap U}
for this marginal.
We denote the objective value associated to
\mu
by

\pE_{\mu}\E_{ij}(d_{ij}-|x_{i}-x_{j}|)^{2}:=\E_{ij}\E_{(x_{i},x_{j})\sim\mu_{ij}}(d_{ij}-|x_{i}-x_{j}|)^{2}\,

where “
\pE_{\mu}
” is the “pseudo-expectation operator” associated to
\mu
.
The degree-
t
Sherali-Adams LP has
(nS)^{\mathcal{O}(t)}
variables and constraints, and so can be solved in
(nS)^{\mathcal{O}(t)}
time.
Our goal is to round a solution
\mu
to this relaxation to a solution
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}
.
The crucial operation we can perform on a pseudo-distribution is
conditioning
on the value of a variable (see
Section

3
for details).
For any
x_{i}
and any value
z\in\supp(\mu_{i})
, we can obtain a degree
t-1
pseudo-distribution
\mu^{\prime}
by conditioning
\mu
on the event
x_{i}=z
.
This entails conditioning each of the local distributions
\mu_{T}
on this event (throwing out those local distributions which do not contain
i
).
A rounding scheme which needs to condition on
s
variables will result in an LP of size
(nS)^{\mathcal{O}(s)}
.

Global Correlation Rounding.

The key insight of
[
BRS11
]
(anticipated by earlier works in statistical physics
[
Mon08
,
IV00
]
) is (1) iterated conditioning produces approximately pairwise-independent pseudo-distributions, and (2) approximately pairwise-independent distributions are easy to round for objective functions which are sums of functions each depending on only two variables.
More precisely, a simple potential function argument shows the following:

Lemma 2.1

(Pinning Lemma, Total Variation Version)
.

Let
\mu
be a degree-
t
pseudodistribution on variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, taking values in a discrete set
S
.
For every
s\leqslant t-2
, there exists a set
T\subseteq[n]
with
|T|\leqslant s
such that if
z_{T}\sim\mu_{T}
and
\mu^{\prime}
is given by conditioning on the event
x_{T}=z_{T}
, then

\E_{z_{T}\sim\mu_{T}}\E_{ij\sim[n]}\tv(\mu^{\prime}_{ij},\mu^{\prime}_{i}\otimes\mu^{\prime}_{j})\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{\log|S|}{s}}\,.

Here
\tv
is total variation distance, and
\mu^{\prime}_{i}\otimes\mu^{\prime}_{j}
is the product of the distributions
\mu^{\prime}_{i}
and
\mu^{\prime}_{j}
.

Suppose given a degree-
t
pseudodistribution
\mu
of degree
s
with objective value at most
\textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{EMV}}+\varepsilon\cdot\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}
, for some
s
we will choose later.
By searching over all subsets
T
of size at most
t-2
and choices for values
z_{T}\in S^{T}
, we can find a conditioned pseudo-distribution
\mu^{\prime}
with objective value at most
\textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{EMV}}+\varepsilon\cdot\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}
and
\E_{ij\sim[n]}\tv(\mu_{ij}^{\prime},\mu_{i}^{\prime}\otimes\mu_{j}^{\prime})\leqslant\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log|S|)/\sqrt{t}\right)
. Then, we can obtain
y
by sampling each
y_{i}\sim\mu^{\prime}_{i}
independently. Let
\mu^{{}^{\prime}\otimes}
denote the distribution
\mu^{\prime}_{1}\otimes\mu^{\prime}_{2}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mu^{\prime}_{n}
. We can then use the pinning lemma to analyze the rounding cost as follows:

\begin{split}\E_{z_{T}\sim\mu_{T}}&\E_{ij\sim[n]}\pE_{\mu^{{}^{\prime}\otimes}}\left\lparen d_{ij}-|x_{i}-x_{j}|\right\rparen^{2}\\
&=\E_{z_{T}\sim\mu_{T}}\E_{ij\sim[n]}\pE_{\mu_{ij}}\left\lparen d_{ij}-|x_{i}-x_{j}|\right\rparen^{2}+\left\lparen\E_{z_{T}\sim\mu_{T}}\E_{ij\sim[n]}\E_{\mu^{\prime}_{i}\otimes\mu^{\prime}_{j}}(d_{ij}-|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2})-\E_{\mu^{\prime}_{ij}}(d_{ij}-|x_{i}-x_{j}|)^{2}\right\rparen\\
&\leqslant\textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{EMV}}+\varepsilon\cdot\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}+\left\lparen\E_{z_{T}\sim\mu_{T}}\E_{ij\sim[n]}\E_{\mu^{\prime}_{i}\otimes\mu^{\prime}_{j}}(d_{ij}-|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2})-\E_{\mu^{\prime}_{ij}}(d_{ij}-|x_{i}-x_{j}|)^{2}\right\rparen\\
&\leqslant\textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{EMV}}+\underbrace{\varepsilon\cdot\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}}_{\text{discretization error}}+\underbrace{\frac{2^{2B}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot O\left\lparen\E_{z_{T}\sim\mu_{T}}\E_{ij\sim[n]}\tv(\mu^{\prime}_{ij},\mu^{\prime}_{i}\otimes\mu^{\prime}_{j})\right\rparen}_{\text{rounding error}}\,,\end{split}

where we used:

•

for every
x_{i}
in the support of
\mu^{\prime}_{i}
and
x_{j}
in the support of
\mu^{\prime}_{j}
, the function
(d_{ij}-|x_{i}-x_{j}|)^{2}
is at most
O(2^{2B}/\varepsilon^{2})
, and

•

if
\alpha,\beta
are distributions and
f
is a real-valued function,
|\E_{x\sim\alpha}f(x)-\E_{x\sim\beta}f(x)|\leqslant\|f\|_{\infty}\cdot\tv(\alpha,\beta)
, by the definition of total variation.

Then we can apply the pinning lemma (Lemma
2.1
) to conclude that if we choose
T
appropriately of size
2^{O(B)}/\varepsilon^{O(1)}
, the rounded solution has expected objective value
\textsf{OPT}+\varepsilon\cdot\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}
.
Since we had to pick
|T|
exponentially large in
B
, and the size of the Sherali-Adams LP is exponential in
|T|
, we get double-exponential dependence on
B
.

Our rounding schemes always follow this plan: search over all subsets
T
of coordinates of a given fixed size, draw a sample
z_{T}\sim\mu_{T}
, condition on
x_{T}=z_{T}
, and then draw independently from each of the
1
-local conditional distributions to obtain a rounded solution.
Our technical contributions are new analyses of this canonical rounding scheme, showing that it obtains much smaller error for low-dimensional embedding problems than the aforementioned analysis suggests.

2.2
Euclidean Metric Violation in Polynomial Time

Next we describe the ideas needed to reduce the running time to
n^{\poly(B,\varepsilon^{-1})}

Our main goal is to avoid the pessimistic bound of
2^{\mathcal{O}(B)}/\varepsilon^{2}
on the maximum possible value of
(d_{ij}-|x_{i}-x_{j}|)^{2}
used in the analysis above.

7

Note that there are initial pseudo-distributions

For example, imagine that
\mu
corresponds to an actual distribution over optimal solutions
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}

Anchoring the embedding by conditioning once.

The first observation is that after conditioning on a single
x_{a}
, each local distribution
\mu_{i}^{\prime}
will place most of the probability mass of each
x_{i}
in a ball of radius
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\sqrt{\E_{i,j\sim[n]}d_{ij}^{2}}\right)
, which can be significantly smaller than
2^{B}/\varepsilon
.
8
8

8

While
[
BCHJL25
]
also utilizes an “anchoring” procedure, their approach requires that most points are very strongly anchored. In contrast, our initial anchoring procedure is much weaker and only serves as the first step in our analysis.

To see why, it is convenient to think of
\mu
as an actual probability distribution over
1

\mu
supported on
\mathbbm{R}^{n}
), and consider choosing a random

and conditioning
\mu
on the location of
x_{a}
.
This “anchors” the distribution to
x_{a}

\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\sqrt{\E_{\mu_{ia}}|x_{i}-x_{a}|^{2}}\right)

This escapes the bad example of a distribution
\mu
on solutions which are randomly shifted by

– conditioning on a single

fixes such a global random shift.
By comparing
\E_{i\sim[n]}\E_{\mu}|x_{i}-x_{a}|^{2}
to the objective function, we can also conclude that these balls are not too big on average:
\E_{i}\E_{a}\E_{\mu}|x_{i}-x_{a}|^{2}\leqslant\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}\right)
, since

\begin{split}\E_{a\sim[n]}\E_{i\sim[n]}\E_{\mu_{ia}}(|x_{i}-x_{a}|\pm d_{ij})^{2}\leqslant 2\pE_{\mu}\E_{i,a\sim[n]}\left\lparen d_{ia}-|x_{i}-x_{a}|\right\rparen^{2}+2\E_{i,a\sim[n]}d_{ia}^{2}\leqslant 4\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}\,,\end{split}

(1)

using in the last step that
\textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{EMV}}\leqslant\E_{i,a\sim[n]}d_{ia}^{2}

Furthermore, the same argument holds even if

is a Sherali-Adams pseudo-distribution.

Optimistically, if we imagine that after conditioning on
x_{a}
, each
x_{i}
is with probability

\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\sqrt{\E_{\mu}|x_{i}-x_{a}|}\right)

\E_{\mu_{ia}}|x_{i}-x_{a}|^{2}\leqslant\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}\right)

\tv

\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}\right)
in place of the

upper bound on
(d_{ij}-|x_{i}-x_{j}|)^{2}
.
This leads to rounded objective value

\textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{EMV}}+\underbrace{\varepsilon\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}}_{\text{discretization error}}+\underbrace{\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}\cdot\mathcal{O}\left\lparen\E_{z_{T}\sim\mu_{T}}\E_{ij\sim[n]}\tv(\mu^{\prime}_{ij},\mu^{\prime}_{i}\otimes\mu^{\prime}_{j})\right\rparen}_{\text{rounding error}}\,.

Then we would deploy the pinning lemma with
|T|=(B/\varepsilon)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}

Splitting into Linear and Quadratic Terms.

The sketch above makes an unrealistic assumption: that after we condition on a single
x_{a}
, there is a ball of radius
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\sqrt{\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}}\right)
containing the entire support of each
x_{i}
.
But really we only know a bound on the second moment
\E_{\mu_{ia}}|x_{i}-x_{a}|^{2}
, not a probability-1 upper bound on
|x_{i}-x_{a}|
, and even that only on average over
i\in[n]
(recall
Eq.

1
).
So, we need a version of the argument using only these second-moment bounds.

The first step is to expand each term of our objective function:

(d_{ij}-|x_{i}-x_{j}|)^{2}=d_{ij}^{2}-2d_{ij}|x_{i}-x_{j}|+x_{i}^{2}+x_{j}^{2}-2x_{i}x_{j}\,.

Since
\E_{\mu^{\prime}_{ij}}d_{ij}^{2}=\E_{\mu^{\prime}_{i}\otimes\mu^{\prime}_{j}}d_{ij}^{2}
and similarly for
x_{i}^{2}
and
x_{j}^{2}
, our rounding scheme incurs zero expected error on these terms.
The remaining rounding error can then be broken down into:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\E_{ij}d_{ij}\cdot\left\lvert\E_{\mu^{\prime}_{ij}}|x_{i}-x_{j}|-\E_{\mu_{i}^{\prime}\otimes\mu_{j}^{\prime}}|x_{i}-x_{j}|\right\rvert}_{\text{linear term}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\E_{ij}\left\lvert\E_{\mu^{\prime}_{ij}}x_{i}x_{j}-\E_{\mu^{\prime}_{i}\otimes\mu^{\prime}_{j}}x_{i}x_{j}\right\rvert=\underbrace{\E_{ij}\left\lvert\Cov_{\mu^{\prime}_{ij}}(x_{i},x_{j})\right\rvert}_{\text{quadratic term}}\,.

The Linear Term: Splitting into Tail and Bulk Contributions.

For the linear term, we will impose the probability-one upper bound on
|x_{i}-x_{j}|
“by force”, by splitting each
x_{i}
up into a bulk part and a tail part.
Let
E_{i}
be the event that
|x_{i}-x_{a}|\leqslant\varepsilon^{-\mathcal{O}(1)}\sqrt{\E_{\mu_{ia}}|x_{i}-x_{a}|^{2}}
, where
x_{a}
is the anchor point we conditioned on previously.
We can split the function
|x_{i}-x_{j}|
up as

|x_{i}-x_{j}|=|x_{i}-x_{j}|\cdot\mathbb{1}(E_{i},E_{j})+|x_{i}-x_{j}|\cdot\mathbb{1}(\overline{E_{i}}\text{ or }\overline{E_{j}})\,.

Using Chebyshev’s inequality, we can show that the tail parts can’t contribute much to the objective value of
\mu
or the objective value of the rounded solution we obtain by from each
\mu_{i}^{\prime}
independently:

\E_{ij}d_{ij}\cdot\E_{\mu^{\prime}_{ij}}|x_{i}-x_{j}|\cdot\mathbb{1}(\overline{E_{i}}\text{ or }\overline{E_{j}})\leqslant\varepsilon\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\E_{ij}d_{ij}\cdot\E_{\mu^{\prime}_{i}\otimes\mu^{\prime}_{j}}|x_{i}-x_{j}|\cdot\mathbb{1}(\overline{E_{i}}\text{ or }\overline{E_{j}})\leqslant\varepsilon\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}\,.

At the same time, for the “bulk part”
|x_{i}-x_{j}|\cdot\mathbb{1}(E_{i},E_{j})
, there is a probability-
1
upper bound of
\varepsilon^{-\mathcal{O}(1)}(\sqrt{\E_{\mu_{ia}}|x_{i}-x_{a}|^{2}}+\sqrt{\E_{\mu_{ja}}|x_{j}-x_{a}|^{2}})
, so we can bound

\begin{split}&(\text{rounding error for }|x_{i}-x_{j}|\cdot\mathbb{1}(E_{i},E_{j}))\\
&\qquad\leqslant\varepsilon^{-\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot\left\lparen\sqrt{\E_{\mu_{ia}}|x_{i}-x_{a}|^{2}}+\sqrt{\E_{\mu_{ja}}|x_{j}-x_{a}|^{2}}\right\rparen\cdot\tv(\mu_{ij}^{\prime},\mu_{i}^{\prime}\otimes\mu_{j}^{\prime})\,.\end{split}

Putting things together, to get rounding error down to
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\varepsilon\cdot\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}\right)
for the linear term
\E_{ij}d_{ij}|x_{i}-x_{j}|

\tv(\mu^{\prime}_{ij},\mu^{\prime}_{i}\otimes\mu^{\prime}_{j})

The Quadratic Term.

For the quadratic term, we can use another variant of the pinning lemma, due to
[
BRS11
]
, which shows via a distinct potential function argument for any pseudo-distribution
\mu
on variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, there is a set
T\subseteq[n]
of indices such that if
\mu^{\prime}
is the result of conditioning
\mu
on
x_{T}=z_{T}
, with
z_{T}\sim\mu_{T}
,

\E_{z_{T}\sim\mu_{T}}\E_{ij}|\Cov_{\mu^{\prime}_{ij}}(x_{i},x_{j})|\leqslant\varepsilon\cdot\E_{i\sim[n]}\Var_{\mu}(x_{i})\,.

In our case, if
\mu
is the pseudo-distribution we obtain after the anchoring step,
\E_{i\sim[n]}\Var_{\mu}(x_{i})\leqslant\E_{i\sim[n]}\E_{\mu_{ia}}|x_{i}-x_{a}|^{2}\leqslant\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}\right)
.
This means that by conditioning on
\varepsilon^{-\mathcal{O}(1)}
indices, we can push the rounding error on the quadratic term to
\varepsilon\cdot\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}
.

To make the overall argument successfully, we have to show that we can condition on a single set of
\varepsilon^{-\mathcal{O}(1)}
indices to get small rounding error simultaneously for the linear and quadratic terms.
Luckily, we can combine the potential functions from both variants of the pinning lemma into a single potential function (see
Lemma

4.4

From
n^{B}
to
\poly(n,B)
Running Time.

Now we describe how to improve the algorithm to run in truly polynomial time, for fixed
\varepsilon>0
.
There are two sources of the exponential dependence on
B
in the analysis we described so far.
First, we discretized
\mathbbm{R}
into around
2^{B}/\varepsilon
points, meaning that our LP is at least exponential-in-
B
sized.
Second, Lemma
2.1
requires taking our conditioning set
T
to have size at least
\log(\text{support size of $x_{i}$})\geqslant\log(2^{B})\geqslant B
.

To address the first issue, we show that there is actually a discretization of
\mathbbm{R}
containing only
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(B/\varepsilon\right)
points which suffices to preserve solution value
\textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{EMV}}+\varepsilon\cdot\E_{ij}d_{ij}^{2}
.
We rely again on the anchoring argument to show that a grid with geometrically increasing interval length can be used, centered at a single anchor point – this simple argument appears in Lemma
5.6
.

At this point, we could get an algorithm with running time
n^{\poly(\log B,\varepsilon^{-1})}
by using the rest of the analysis unchanged.
To get from quasi-polynomial to polynomial dependence on
B
, we use one more idea.
We apply the pinning lemma to a slightly different choice of variables – rather than
x_{i}
, we use the “truncated” versions of
x_{i}
, i.e.
\tilde{x}_{i}=(x_{i}-\E x_{i})\cdot E_{i}+\E x_{i}
.
This has the result that each
x_{i}
now assumes values in a smaller set, and is enough to show that we can use
|T|=\varepsilon^{-\mathcal{O}(1)}
, with no dependence at all on
B
, leading to our truly polynomial-time algorithm.

2.3
Weighted
k
-Euclidean Metric Violation

We turn next to Theorem
1.2
, on weighted
k
-EMV with dense weights.
The algorithmic guarantees for Max-CSPs (whose toolkit we are borrowing here) differ little between complete instances, where all possible tuples of variables participate in a constraint, and densely-weighted instances. In fact, the analysis even extends to sparse instances, as long as the underlying constraint graph enjoys some mild expansion properties.
Since our algorithm relies on tools from the CSP literature, one might expect that similarly the analysis of our algorithm could remain essentially unchanged when generalizing to the weighted
k
-EMV case, but this is not to be the case for our algorithm.
Rather, the key anchoring step in our argument breaks down.
To see why, consider two simple examples, both allowable weight matrices under our assumptions.

The first example: the weights
w_{ij}\in[0,1]
are a disjoint union of two cliques, on
1,\ldots,n/2
and
n/2+1,\ldots,n
.
Then consider the following distribution
\mu
over optimal solutions
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
which we could be required to round.
Start with a single optimal solution
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, and pick two random signs
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
.
Shift
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n/2}
by
2^{B}\sigma
and
x_{n/2+1},\ldots,x_{n}
by
2^{B}\sigma^{\prime}
.
At best we can hope to anchor half of the points by conditioning on any single
x_{a}
.
In this example, the remedy is clear: condition on two points,
x_{a},x_{b}
, to anchor both clusters.

The second example: the weights
w_{ij}\in[0,1]
form an expander graph.
In this case our algorithm can be modified so that our anchoring step works by conditioning on
x_{a}
, but we need to replace our Sherali-Adams LP with a Sum of Squares SDP.
Once we do so, we can argue as follows.
If we condition on a randomly-chosen

A Global Correlation Framework

In this section, we describe a simple framework for analyzing global correlation rounding.
This framework allows us a technically convienient way to obtain pseudoexpectations which simultaneously satisfy several distinct notions of approximate pairwise independence.

Definition 4.1

(Pseudo-distribution potentials)
.

A function
\Phi
from pseudo-distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}
is a pseudo-distribution potential if

1.

For all pseudo-distributions
\mu
we have that
\Phi(\mu)\geqslant 0
.

2.

For a fixed

, let
\mu^{\prime}
be the pseudo-distribution given by sampling a random value
\hat{x}_{i}
from the marginal of
x_{i}
and conditioning on the event

\E_{x_{i}}\left[\Phi(\mu)-\Phi(\mu^{\prime})\right]\geqslant 0\,.

The typical potential functions used in global correlation rounding by
[
RT12
,
BRS11
]
are
\E_{i\sim[n]}H(\{x_{i}\})
and
\E_{i\sim[n]}\Var(x_{i})
.
Both are pseudo-distribution potentials in the sense of Defintion
4.1
.

Definition 4.2

(Potential aligned functions)
.

A function

\Phi

\mu
such that

\E_{\{\mu\}}\mu=\mu_{0}
,

in the support of

satisfy
\pE_{\mu^{\prime}}p_{i}=0

, and

\left|\E_{\{\mu\}}\pE_{\mu}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})-\pE_{\mu^{\otimes}}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\right|\geqslant\delta\,,

such that

\E_{\{\mu\}}\E_{\hat{x_{i}}\sim\{x_{i}\}}\left[\Phi(\mu)-\Phi(\mu^{\prime})\right]\geqslant\delta^{\prime}\,,

\left|\E_{\{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}\}}\pE_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{j\in[\ell]}f_{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})-\pE_{\mu^{\otimes}_{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{j\in[\ell]}f_{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\right|\geqslant\varepsilon\,.

Then, by averaging, there exists
j\in[\ell]
such that

\left|\E_{\{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}\}}\pE_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}f_{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})-\pE_{\mu^{\otimes}_{\mathcal{T}}}f_{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\right|\geqslant\varepsilon/\ell\,.

Since
f_{j}
is
(\varepsilon/\ell,\delta_{i})
potential aligned and by definition of conditioning
\{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}\}=\mu
, by
Definition

4.2
we have that there exists
i\in[n]
such that

\E_{\{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}\}}\E_{x_{i}}\left[\Phi_{j}(\mu_{\mathcal{T}})-\Phi_{j}(\mu^{\prime}_{\mathcal{T}})\right]\geqslant\delta_{j}\,,

where
\mu^{\prime}_{\mathcal{T}}
is
\mu_{\mathcal{T}}
conditioned on
x_{i}
.
We will pick this
i
to add to the set
\mathcal{T}
which we condition on. Since all
\Phi_{j}
are psuedo-distribution potentials, conditioning on
x_{i}
does not increase any of them in expectation. Thus, at each step of conditioning the potential functions either decrease by
\delta_{j}
in expectation or are non-increasing. Therefore, each
f_{j}
can only have a large difference in expectation on
\mu
vs
\mu^{\otimes}
at most
\Phi_{j}(\mu)/\delta_{j}
many times, since after this happens mores times then the expectation of
\Phi_{j}
is negative, which contradicts the fact that pseudo-distribution potential are always positive.
∎

5.1

In this section, we will show that Lipschitz functions are potential aligned, for a carefully chosen variant of the average entropy potential. Lemma 5.1

(Lipschitz Functions are Potential Aligned)
.

Let

\left\lparen\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\varepsilon^{1/4}\right)\cdot\left(\E_{i\sim[n]}\pE_{\mu}\lVert x_{i}-\pE_{\mu}x_{i}\rVert^{2}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(\E_{i,j\sim[n]}L_{ij}^{2}\right)^{1/2},\hskip 7.22743pt\varepsilon\right\rparen-\textrm{potential aligned}

for the average entropy potential,
\Phi=\E_{#1}\,\left[\mathsf{H}(\tilde{x}_{i})\right]
, and
\mu
.

We will prove this via showing both that (a) the difference in expectation on
\mu
versus
\mu^{\otimes}
is tied to an approximate pairwise independence property, and then (b) showing that whenever this property fails the potential in question decreases in expectation when conditioning on some carefully chosen
x_{i}
.

Discretization preserves objective value.

In this section, we will show that a discretized variant of the Euclidean Metric Violation objective with alphabet size
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\varepsilon\cdot\log(\Delta k/\varepsilon)\right)
preserves the objective value up to an additive error of
\varepsilon\E_{i,j\sim[n]}d_{ij}^{2}

Lemma 5.6

(Discretization incurs small error)
.

Given a set of distances
\{d_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
such that
\Delta=\max_{i,j}d_{i,j}
and
1=\min_{i,j}d_{i,j}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
0<\varepsilon<1
, there exists a fixed set
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
depending only on
\Delta
and
\varepsilon
such that the objective

\textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{DEMV}}=\min_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\Sigma}\sum_{i,j\in[n]}\left\lparen d_{i,j}-\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert_{2}\right\rparen^{2}

satisfies

\textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{DEMV}}\leqslant\textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{EMV}}+16\varepsilon\cdot\E_{i,j\sim[n]}d_{ij}^{2}\,.

Furthermore,

|\Sigma|\leqslant\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\varepsilon\cdot\log(\Delta k/\varepsilon)\right)\,.

We first describe the set
\Sigma
. We have that:

\Sigma=\{0\}\cup\left\{\varepsilon\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{k}}(1+\varepsilon)^{t}\,|\,t\in[12\log_{1+\varepsilon}(k\Delta/\varepsilon)]\right\}\,.

In order to show that
\textsf{OPT}_{\mathrm{DEMV}}
is small, we will bound the value of the objective when taking an (approximately) optimal low diameter solution to the original
k
-EMV
problem and assigning each point
x_{i}
to its closest point in
\Sigma
.

First, we must show that such an approximately optimal low-diameter solution exists.

Fact 5.7
.

Let
\{d_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
be a set of distances such that
\Delta=\max_{i,j}d_{i,j}
and
1=\min_{i,j}d_{i,j}
. Then for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
0<\varepsilon<1
, there exists a solution
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
for the
k
-EMV
objective of value
\textsf{OPT}_{\mathrm{EMV}}+\varepsilon\E_{i,j\sim[n]}d_{ij}^{2}
where the diameter of the embedding is at most
\left(\Delta/\varepsilon\right)^{O(1)}
.

Proof.

Take some optimal solution
x_{1}^{*},\ldots,x_{n}^{*}
. We will modify the solution to get a new solution satisfying the diameter conditions with value at most
\textsf{OPT}_{\mathrm{EMV}}+\varepsilon\E_{i,j\sim[n]}d_{ij}^{2}
.

Note that we have that
\E_{i,j\sim[n]}(d_{ij}-\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert)^{2}\leqslant\textsf{OPT}_{\mathrm{EMV}}\leqslant\Delta^{2}
. Thus, by Markov’s Inequality we have that for all but an
\varepsilon^{2}/\Delta^{4}
-fraction of pairs we have that
\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert\leqslant\varepsilon^{-1}\Delta^{3}+\Delta
. Let the set of good points
x_{i}^{*}
be all points that participate in at most
\varepsilon/\Delta^{2}
pairs such that
\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert^{2}>\varepsilon^{-1}\Delta^{3}+\Delta
. Note that there are at least
1-\varepsilon/\Delta^{2}
many good points.

Furthermore, every pair of good points
x_{i}^{*},x_{j}^{*}
are not too far apart, specifically
\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert\leqslant 2\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\Delta^{3}+\Delta\right)
. Note that
i,j
both have at most
\varepsilon/\Delta^{2}
neighbors such that
\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert^{2}>\varepsilon^{-1}\Delta^{3}+\Delta
. Thus, they must have a shared neighbor
\ell
such that
\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{\ell}^{*}\rVert^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon^{-1}\Delta^{3}+\Delta
and
\lVert x_{j}^{*}-x_{\ell}^{*}\rVert^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon^{-1}\Delta^{3}+\Delta
. Therefore, by Triangle Inequality
\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert\leqslant 2\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\Delta^{3}+\Delta\right)
.

We now note that we can construct a low diameter solution by first taking the solution induced by the good points above and projecting the remaining points to the
2\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\Delta^{3}+\Delta\right)
radius ball containing the good points. The cost on all pairs that only contain good points is preserved. Furthermore, there are only
\varepsilon/\Delta^{2}n^{2}
many pairs that contain at least one bad point. Note that the procedure above only contracts distances, so the additional cost incurred on pairs after a contraction is at most
\Delta^{2}
. Thus, the cost of the solution increased by at most
\varepsilon\leqslant\varepsilon\E_{i,j\sim[n]}d_{ij}^{2}
, as desired.
∎

Furthermore, note that we must have that any optimal or approximately optimal solution must be on average contained within a not too large ball.

Lemma 5.8

(Points are contained in a ball)
.

Let
\{d_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
be a set of distances such that
\Delta=\max_{i,j}d_{i,j}
and
1=\min_{i,j}d_{i,j}
. Then for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
0<\varepsilon<1
, and any embedding

with Euclidean Metric Violation objective value at most

such that

\E_{i\sim[n]}\lVert x_{i}-c\rVert^{2}\leqslant 6\E_{i,j\sim[n]}d_{ij}^{2}\,.

Furthermore, the point

for some

Proof.

We will show that in expectation, if we pick
c
as a random

, the inequality holds, and thus there exists a fixed point

for some

such that it is true. Specifically, if we sample a uniformly random index

, then we have by Almost Triangle Inequality that

\E_{a\sim[n]}\E_{i\sim[n]}\lVert x_{i}-x_{a}\rVert^{2}=\E_{ai}\left(\lVert x_{i}-x_{a}\rVert\pm d_{ia}\right)^{2}\leqslant\E_{ai}2\left(d_{ia}-\lVert x_{i}-x_{a}\rVert\right)^{2}+2d_{ia}^{2}\,.

The first term is the Euclidean Metric Violation objective value, which is bounded by

\E_{a\sim[n]}\E_{i\sim[n]}\lVert x_{i}-x_{a}\rVert^{2}\leqslant 6\E_{i,j\sim[n]}d_{ij}^{2}\,.

We can now proceed to the proof of

Proof of

Analyzing global correlation rounding with potential functions.

We have that all the remaining parts of our objective function are potential aligned for appropriately chosen potential functions and thus we will apply

4.4

Lemma 5.10

(Average Entropy Bound)

Let
\mu

is

\E_{i}H(\widetilde{x}_{i})\leqslant O\left(k\log(Ck/\varepsilon)\right)\,.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\E_{i}H(\widetilde{x}_{i})

Finally, by our bound on the average squared norm of

Theorem 5.12

Partitioning.

In particular, we show that an
\delta\cdot n
regular graph can be partitioned into
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(1/\delta^{2}\right)
components such that the induced sub-graph on each component is a
\poly(\delta)
-expander and at most half the edges are discarded. To set up notation, for a weighted graph
G=(V,E)
, we write
E(A,B)
for the total edge weight from
A\subseteq V
to
B\subseteq V
.
Following
[
Tan11
,
GT14
]
, we use the following notion of multi-way conductance:

Definition 5.15

(Multi-Way Conductance)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and
k\in\mathbbm{N}
.
Then, the multi-way conductance is defined as

\rho_{G}(k)=\min_{A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\,\text{disjoint}}\max_{j\in[k]}\hskip 7.22743pt\phi_{G}(A_{j})\,,

where
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\subseteq V
.

Oveis Gharan and Trevisan
[
GT14
]
prove the following result relating graph partitions to multi-way conductance:

Theorem 5.16

(
[
GT14
]
, Theorem 1.7)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
.
If
\rho_{G}(k+1)>(1+\delta)\rho_{G}(k)
for some
0<\delta<1
, then there exists a partitioning of
V
into
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
such that for all
i\leqslant k
, we have (a)
\phi_{G}(P_{i})\leqslant k\cdot\rho(k)
and (b)
\phi(G[P_{i}])\geqslant\delta\cdot\rho(k+1)/(14k)
, where
G[P_{i}]
is the subgraph of
G
induced by the vertices
P_{i}
.

We will need a simple lemma about the multi-way conductance of regular dense graphs.

Lemma 5.17
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an
n
-vertex graph with edge weights
w_{ij}\in[0,1]
, where every vertex has degree
\delta n
.
Then for every
k
,
\rho_{G}(k)\geqslant 1-\tfrac{1}{\delta k}
.

Proof.

WLOG
k\geqslant 2
, since the statement is trivial when
k=1
.
Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
be any collection of disjoint subsets of
V
. By disjointness, there exists
A_{i}
with
|A_{i}|\leqslant n/k
.
The total weight of edges internal to
A
is at most
\binom{|A|}{2}\leqslant|A|^{2}
, while the volume of
A
is
|A|\delta n
.
So the conductance of
A
satisfies

\phi_{G}(A)\geqslant\frac{\delta n|A|-|A|^{2}}{\delta n|A|}=1-\frac{|A|}{\delta n}\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{\delta k}\,,

and by definition
\rho_{G}(k)\geqslant\phi_{G}(A)\geqslant(1-1/(\delta k)
, as desired. ∎

We can put these together to arrive at a partitioning-into-expanders lemma for dense graphs.

Lemma 5.18

(Expander Decomposition for regular Graphs)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an
n
-vertex regular graph with edges weights
w_{ij}\in[0,1]
, where every vertex has weighted degree
\delta\cdot n
.
There exists a partition of
V
into
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
such that each
P_{i}
has
\phi(G[P_{i}])\geqslant\Omega(\delta^{10})
,
\phi_{G}(P_{i})\leqslant 1/4
, and
\sum_{i\neq j}E(P_{i},P_{j})\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}|E|
, for some
k\leqslant O(1/\delta^{2})
.

Proof.

If
\rho(2)\geqslant\delta^{5}
then we are done, since then
G
is already an
\delta^{5}
-expander.
So assume
\rho(2)\leqslant\delta^{5}
, and let
\ell
be the greatest index such that
\rho(\ell)\leqslant\delta^{5}
. Observe, by
Lemma

5.17
,
\rho(1/(10\delta))\geqslant 0.9
and therefore
\ell<1/(10\delta)
.
We now find
\ell\leqslant k\leqslant 1/\delta^{2}+1
such that
\rho(k+1)\geqslant(1+\delta^{3})\rho(k)
and
k\rho(k)\leqslant 1/4
.

Let
k_{0}
be the first integer such that
\rho(k_{0}+1)>\tfrac{1}{4(k_{0}+1)}
.
By Fact
5.17
,
\rho(1/\delta^{2})\geqslant 1-\delta
and so
k_{0}\leqslant 1/\delta^{2}
.
If for every
\ell\leqslant k\leqslant k_{0}
we had
\rho(k+1)<(1+\delta^{3})\rho(k)
, then we would have
\rho(k_{0}+1)\leqslant(1+\delta^{3})^{k_{0}}\delta^{5}\leqslant\exp(\delta)\delta^{5}<\delta^{2}\leqslant 1/{2(k_{0}+1)}
, which goes against the definition of
k_{0}
.
So there exists
\ell\leqslant k\leqslant k_{0}
satisfying
\rho(k+1)\geqslant(1+\delta^{3})\rho(k)
and
k\rho(k)\leqslant 1/4
.

Now we apply Theorem
5.16
with this value of
k
, obtaining a partition
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
such that
\phi(G[P_{i}])\geqslant\delta^{3}\rho(k+1)/(14k)=\Omega(\delta^{10})
while
\phi_{G}(P_{i})\leqslant 1/4
.

It just remains to check that this implies that
\sum_{i\neq j}E(P_{i},P_{j})\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}|E|
.
For each
i
, we have
\sum_{j\neq i}E(P_{i},P_{j})\leqslant\phi_{G}(P_{i})\cdot\text{vol}(P_{i})\leqslant\text{vol}(P_{i})/4\leqslant|P_{i}|\cdot(\delta n/4)
.
Summing across all
i
finishes the argument.
∎

Next, we show that after conditioning on a single point, the variance is bounded for the weighted objective as well:

Lemma 5.19

(Variance after conditioning)
.

Let
\{d_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
be a set of distances and
\Set{w_{ij}}_{i,j\in[n]}
be a set of non-negative weights such that for all
i\in[n]
,
\sum_{j\in[n]}w_{i,j}=\delta\cdot n
for some
0<\delta<1
. Then for any pseudo-distribution over
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
for the Weighted
k
-EMV
objective, there exists a set of
k=\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(1/\delta^{2}\right)
points
\Set{x_{j_{\ell}}}_{\ell\in[k]}
such that

\E_{i\sim W}\tilde{\Var}(x_{i}\mid x_{j_{1}},\ldots,x_{j_{k}})\leqslant\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(1/\delta^{10}\right)\cdot\E_{i,j\sim W}d_{ij}^{2}\,,

where the distribution
i\sim W
samples indices
i\propto\sum_{j}W_{ij}
and the distribution
i,j\sim W
samples the pair
i,j\propto W_{ij}
.

Proof.

Observe, since each row has weight
\eta n
, for any fixed
c=x_{a}

\E_{#1}\,\left[\tilde{\Var}(x_{i}\mid x_{j_{1}},\ldots,x_{j_{k}})\right]=\E_{#1}\,\left[\tilde{\Var}(x_{i}\mid x_{j_{1}},\ldots,x_{j_{k}})\right]

Next, it follows from
Lemma

5.18
that there exists a partition
\Set{P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}}
such that
\phi(G[P_{i}])\geqslant\delta^{10}
. Consider the random process where
x_{j_{\ell}}
is picked uniformly from
P_{\ell}
. Then,

\begin{split}\E_{#1}\,\left[\E_{#1}\,\left[\tilde{\Var}(x_{i}\mid x_{j_{1}},\ldots,x_{j_{k}})\right]\right]&\leqslant\E_{#1}\,\left[\E_{#1}\,\left[\tilde{\Var}(x_{i}\mid x_{j(i)}\right]\right]\\
&\leqslant\E_{#1}\,\left[\E_{#1}\,\left[\pE\lVert x_{i}-x_{j(i)}\rVert^{2}\right]\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell\in[k]}\frac{\lvert P_{\ell}\rvert}{\lvert P_{\ell}\rvert^{2}}\sum_{i,a\in P_{\ell}}\pE\lVert x_{i}-x_{a}\rVert^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell\in[k]}\lvert P_{\ell}\rvert\cdot\E_{#1}\,\left[\pE\lVert x_{i}-x_{a}\rVert^{2}\right]\end{split}

(10)

where
x_{j(i)}
corresponds to the point selected in the same partition as
x_{i}
. Further, since
P_{\ell}
is an expander, by a Poincaré inequality combined with
3.10
, we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell\in[k]}\lvert P_{\ell}\rvert\cdot\E_{#1}\,\left[\pE\lVert x_{i}-x_{a}\rVert^{2}\right]\leqslant\left\lparen\frac{1}{\delta^{10}}\right\rparen\left\lparen\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell\in[k]}\left\lparen\frac{\lvert P_{\ell}\rvert}{\sum_{i,a\in P_{\ell}}w_{ia}}\right\rparen\sum_{i,a\in P_{\ell}}w_{ia}\pE\lVert x_{i}-x_{a}\rVert^{2}\right\rparen

(11)

Next, we show that
\sum_{i,a\in P_{\ell}}w_{ia}\geqslant\frac{3}{4}\cdot\delta n\cdot\lvert P_{\ell}\rvert
. Recall, from
Lemma

5.18
, we have
\phi_{G}(P_{i})\leqslant 1/4
and therefore,

\sum_{i,a\in P_{\ell}}w_{i,a}=\left\lvert E(P_{\ell},P_{\ell})\right\rvert\geqslant\delta n\cdot\left\lvert P_{\ell}\right\rvert-\frac{1}{4}\textrm{vol}(P_{\ell})\geqslant\frac{3}{4}\delta n\cdot\left\lvert P_{\ell}\right\rvert.

Substituting this back into
Eq.

11
, we have

\begin{split}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell\in[k]}\lvert P_{\ell}\rvert\cdot\E_{#1}\,\left[\pE\lVert x_{i}-x_{a}\rVert^{2}\right]&\leqslant\left\lparen\frac{4}{3\delta^{11}}\right\rparen\left\lparen\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,a\in[n]}w_{ia}\cdot\pE\lVert x_{i}-x_{a}\rVert^{2}\right\rparen\\
&\leqslant\left\lparen\frac{4}{3\delta^{10}}\right\rparen\left\lparen\E_{#1}\,\left[\pE\lVert x_{i}-x_{a}\rVert^{2}\right]\right\rparen\end{split}

Then we have by Almost Triangle Inequality that

\E_{a,i\sim W}\lVert x_{i}-x_{a}\rVert^{2}=\E_{a,i\sim W}\left(\lVert x_{i}-x_{a}\rVert\pm d_{ia}\right)^{2}\leqslant\E_{a,i\sim W}2\left(d_{ia}-\lVert x_{i}-x_{a}\rVert\right)^{2}+2d_{ia}^{2}\,.

The first term is
\textsf{OPT}_{\mathrm{WEMV}}
, which is bounded by
\E_{i,j\sim W}d_{ij}^{2}
since there exists a trivial solution achieving this value which just maps every point to
0
. Thus, plugging this back into
Eq.

10
, we can conclude that

\E_{#1}\,\left[\E_{#1}\,\left[\tilde{\Var}(x_{i}\mid x_{j_{1}},\ldots,x_{j_{k}})\right]\right]\leqslant\left\lparen\frac{8}{\delta^{10}}\right\rparen\left\lparen\E_{#1}\,\left[d_{i,j}^{2}\right]\right\rparen

∎

Lemma 5.21

(Rounding Weighted

\begin{split}&\left|\E_{\{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}\}}\pE_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\E_{i,j\sim W}(d_{ij}-\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert)^{2}-\pE_{\mu^{\otimes}_{\mathcal{T}}}\E_{i,j\sim W}(d_{ij}-\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert)^{2}\right|\\

\begin{split}\eqref{eqn:split-for-lipschitz-term-under-w}.(2),\eqref{eqn:split-for-lipschitz-term-under-w}.(3)&\leqslant\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(1/C\right)\left(\E_{i\sim W}\pE_{\mu}\lVert x_{i}-\pE_{\mu}x_{i}\rVert^{2}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(\E_{i,j\sim W}d_{ij}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\\
&\leqslant\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(1/(C\delta^{5})\right)\E_{#1}\,\left[d_{ij}^{2}\right]\end{split}

Setting
C=\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(1/(\varepsilon\delta^{5})\right)
, we can conclude that
if
\eqref{eqn:rounding-cost-on-weighted-raw-stress}.(2)>\varepsilon\E_{#1}\,\left[d_{i,j}^{2}\right]
, the decrease in potential is at least
\varepsilon^{2}\delta^{11}
. Further, since the potential starts out at at most
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(k\log(\Delta\cdot k/(\varepsilon\delta)))\right)
by
Lemma

5.20
it is bounded by
\varepsilon
after
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(k\log(\Delta\cdot k/(\varepsilon\delta))/(\varepsilon^{2}\delta^{11})\right)
rounds. Since both our potentials are now aligned, we can conclude the proof.

∎

We can now prove
Theorem

5.12
similar to how we proved
Theorem

5.4
, and note that the running time is dominated by computing a pseudo-distribution of degree

\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(k^{2}\log(\Delta\cdot k/(\varepsilon\delta))/(\varepsilon^{2}\delta^{11})\right),

since each round of conditioning requires degree
k
.

6
Entry-wise Low-Rank Approximation

In this section, we present an additive approximation scheme for entry-wise rank-
1
approximation.

Theorem 6.1

(Entry-wise LRA)
.

Given a matrix
A\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times m}
, integer
k\in N
, even
p\in N
, and
0<\varepsilon<1
, there exists an algorithm that runs in
n^{\mathcal{O}_{p}(\log^{2}(n)/\poly_{p}(\varepsilon))}
time and outputs matrices
\hat{u}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n},\hat{v}\in\mathbbm{R}^{m}
such that with probability at least
0.99
,

\lVert A-\hat{u}\hat{v}^{\top}\rVert_{p}^{p}\leqslant\textsf{OPT}_{\textrm{LRA}}+\varepsilon\cdot\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{p}^{p}\,.

The algorithm we give for this problem is based on the global correlation rounding algorithm and thus the key technical innovation is to show that the objective function can be decomposed into a sum of potential-aligned functions.

6.1
Rounding Polynomial Growth Functions

In this section, we will show that functions of the form
\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{q}\langle u_{i},v_{j}\rangle^{p-q}
are potential aligned for an appropriately chosen potential function and a certain class of pseudo-expectations, where
p,q
are integers. Note that depending on the degree
q
we will need a different potential function – we first prove this in the case where
q<p/2
and then for the case where
q\in[p/2,p-1]
.

6.1.1
Rounding High Degree Polynomial Growth Functions

Lemma 6.2

(Polynomial-growth functions are potential aligned)
.

Let
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
and let
\mu
be some pseudo-distribution. Let
p,q\in\mathbb{N}^{>0}
such that
q<p/2
and let
\{A_{ij}\}_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}\in\mathbb{R}
be a collection of real coefficients. Furthermore, let
\mu
satisfy the following inequalities:

1.

\sum_{i\in[n]}\E_{\mu}u_{i}^{p}\leqslant 2^{p}\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}}

2.

\sum_{j\in[m]}\E_{\mu}v_{j}^{p}\leqslant 2^{p}\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}}

3.

\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}\left(\E_{\mu}u_{i}^{p}\right)\cdot\left(\E_{\mu}v_{j}^{p}\right)\leqslant 2^{p+1}\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}

Further, let
E_{j}^{v}
be the event that
\lvert v_{j}\rvert\leqslant C(\pE_{\mu}v_{j}^{p})^{1/p}
Suppose
\mu^{\prime}
is a random pseudo-distribution such that
\E\{\mu^{\prime}\}=\{\mu\}
and everything in the support of
\mu^{\prime}
also satisfies the inequalities above. Furthermore,
\Phi
be a function such that the inequalities above imply that

\Phi(\mu^{\prime})\leqslant\Phi_{\max}\leqslant 2^{p-1}\cdot\log n\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{i,j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}}\,,

where
\Phi=\sum_{j\in[m]}H(\widetilde{v}_{j})\E_{\mu}v_{j}^{p}
, and
\tilde{v}_{j}=v_{j}\cdot E_{j}^{v}
. Then
\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{q}(u_{i}v_{j})^{p-q}
is

\left(1/\poly_{p,q}(\varepsilon)\left(\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}\right)^{1/2},\Omega_{p}(\varepsilon/\log n)\left(\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}\right)^{1/2}\right)

potential aligned for
\mu
and
\Phi
.

\mu^{\otimes}

Value of the LP under discretization and constraints.

Before we show that at least one of the LPs in the algorithm has good objective value, we will first need to give a characterization of optimal solutions to

Fact 6.8
.

Let
p
be an even integer. For any matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
there exists an optimal solution
\{u_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
and
\{v_{j}\}_{j\in[m]}
to
2

1.

\sum_{i\in[n]}u_{i}^{p}=\sum_{j\in[m]}v_{j}^{p}\leqslant 2^{p-1}\sqrt{\sum_{i,j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}}
.

2.

\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}u_{i}^{p}v_{j}^{p}\leqslant 2^{p}\sum_{i,j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}
.

Proof.

Consider an optimal solution
\{u_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
and
\{v_{j}\}_{j\in[m]}
. Note that we have by Almost Triangle Inequality that

\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}u_{i}^{p}v_{j}^{p}=\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}(u_{i}v_{j}\pm A_{ij})^{p}\leqslant 2^{p-1}\left(\textsf{OPT}_{\mathrm{LRA}}+\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}\right)\,.

However,
\textsf{OPT}_{\mathrm{LRA}}\leqslant\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}
since the solution which sets
u_{i}=v_{j}=0
for all
i,j
achieves this value. Thus, we have that

\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}u_{i}^{p}v_{j}^{p}\leqslant 2^{p}\sum_{i,j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}\,,

as desired. Furthermore,

\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}u_{i}^{p}v_{j}^{p}=\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}u_{i}^{p}\right)\left(\sum_{j\in[m]}v_{j}^{p}\right)\,,

and since the value of the solution is invariant to dividing all
u_{i}
by some constant
c
and multiplying
v_{j}
by the same constant we can always scale
u_{i},v_{j}
to get a new optimal solution

such that

\sum_{i\in[n]}(u_{i}^{\prime})^{p}=\sum_{j\in[m]}(v_{j}^{\prime})^{p}\,,

which completes the proof.

We now argue that our discretized LRA program has value which is comparable to the original instance in at least one iteration of the loop.

Lemma 6.9

O_{p}(\poly_{p}(1/\varepsilon)\cdot n^{3})
. We can therefore bound

\sum_{j\in[m]}H(\tilde{v}_{j})\E_{\mu}v_{j}^{p}\leqslant\left(\max_{j\in[m]}H(\tilde{v}_{j})\right)\sqrt{\sum_{i,j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}}\leqslant\log\left(n/\varepsilon\right)\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{i,j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}}\,.

Furthermore, we have by Jensens that

\sum_{j\in[m]}\Var(v_{j}^{q})^{p/(2q)}\leqslant\sum_{j\in[m]}\E_{\mu}v_{j}^{p}\leqslant\sqrt{\sum_{i,j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}}\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

We can now show that the rounding error is small after conditioning.

Lemma 6.11

(Rounding LRA)
.

Let
\varepsilon\geqslant 0
and let
\mu
be an initial pseudo-distribution which satisfies

\sum_{i\in[n]}\pE_{\mu}r\cdot u_{i}^{p}=\pE_{\mu}r\cdot s_{u}\,,

and

\sum_{j\in[m]}\pE_{\mu}r\cdot v_{j}^{p}=\pE_{\mu}r\cdot s_{v}\,

for any monomial
r
and some
s_{u},s_{v}\leqslant 2^{p}\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}}
.
Then there exists a set
\mathcal{T}
of size at most
O_{p}\left(\log^{2}(n)/\poly_{p}(\varepsilon)\right)
such that after conditioning on the values of
v_{j}\in\mathcal{T}
,

\left|\E_{\{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}\}}\pE_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{i,j\in[m]}\left(A_{ij}-u_{i}v_{j}\right)^{p}-\pE_{\mu^{\otimes}_{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{i,j\in[m]}\left(A_{ij}-u_{i}v_{j}\right)^{p}\right|\leqslant\varepsilon\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}\,.

Proof.

Note that we can rewrite our objective function as follows:

\sum_{i,j\in[m]}\left(A_{ij}-u_{i}v_{j}\right)^{p}=\sum_{0\leqslant q\leqslant p}\sum_{i\in[n],j\in[m]}\binom{p}{k}A_{ij}^{q}(u_{i}v_{j})^{p-q}\,.

When
q=0
then we have that

\sum_{i,j\in[m]}\pE_{\mu}u_{i}^{p}v_{j}^{p}=\sum_{i\in[n]}\pE_{\mu}u_{i}^{p}\left(\lVert v\rVert_{p}^{p}\right)=\sum_{i\in[n]}s_{v}\pE_{\mu}u_{i}^{p}=s_{v}\cdot s_{u}\,.

Note that this is also the pseudoexpectation of this term under independent rounding, so the contribution to the objective value of this term is always preserved in expectation under independent rounding. Furthermore, by
Lemma

6.10
,
Lemma

6.2
, and
Lemma

6.5
we have that all the remaining terms are
\left(\varepsilon\left(\sum_{i,j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}\right),\frac{\poly_{p}(\varepsilon)}{\log n}\cdot\left(\sum_{i,j\in[m]}A_{ij}^{p}\right)\right)
-potential aligned and thus by
Lemma

4.4
it suffices to take

|\mathcal{T}|\leqslant O_{p}\left(\log^{2}(n)/\poly_{p}(\varepsilon)\right)\,.

∎

We can now prove
Theorem

6.1
similar to how we proved
Theorem

5.4
, and note that the running time is dominated by computing a pseudo-distribution of degree
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log^{2}n/\poly_{p}(\varepsilon)\right)
.

We will explain how to view the rounding algorithm in

. First, we note that rather than brute forcing over all possible seed sets, it instead suffices to sample the seed set from a distribution that is independent of the LP solution. Specifically, in the analysis of the rounding algorithm, we show that a good point to condition on exists via showing that a random point suffices.

Recall that we separately analyzed the rounding error on different parts of the objective function via decomposing it into a sum. We can write the
k

\E_{i,j\sim[n]}d_{ij}^{2}-2d_{ij}\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert+x_{i}^{2}+x_{j}^{2}-2x_{i}x_{j}\,.

Note that only two terms in this sum contribute to the rounding error. To ensure rounding error is small on the

-2d_{ij}\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert

i

5.4
