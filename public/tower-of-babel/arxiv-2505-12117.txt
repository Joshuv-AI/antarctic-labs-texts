T-Rex : Fitting a Robust Factor Model via Expectation-Maximization
Daniel Cederberg

Abstract
Over the past decades, there has been a surge of interest in studying low-dimensional structures within high-dimensional data. Statistical factor models — i.e., low-rank plus diagonal covariance structures — offer a powerful framework for modeling such structures. However, traditional methods for fitting statistical factor models, such as principal component analysis (PCA) or maximum likelihood estimation assuming the data is Gaussian, are highly sensitive to heavy tails and outliers in the observed data. In this paper, we propose a novel expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm for robustly fitting statistical factor models. Our approach is based on Tyler’s M-estimator of the scatter matrix for an elliptical distribution, and consists of solving Tyler’s maximum likelihood estimation problem while imposing a structural constraint that enforces the low-rank plus diagonal covariance structure. We present numerical experiments on both synthetic and real examples, demonstrating the robustness of our method for direction-of-arrival estimation in nonuniform noise and subspace recovery.

Introduction

Traditional statistical signal processing algorithms commonly assume that
the observed data follows a Gaussian distribution. While this model is
suitable for many applications, it tends to make those algorithms highly
sensitive to deviations from Gaussianity, resulting in severe performance
degradation when the observed data contains heavy tails or outliers

[
ZKCM12
]
. The field of
robust statistical signal processing

[
ZKOM18
]
seeks to overcome these limitations by developing tools
that remain effective even when the observed data deviates significantly
from the Gaussian distribution.

In this paper, we consider the problem of robustly estimating a
covariance matrix
\Sigma\in{\mbox{\bf S}}^{n}
of the form

\Sigma=FF^{T}+D,

(1)

where
D\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n\times n}
is diagonal and
F\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n\times r}

with
r\leq n
. This covariance structure, known as a
statistical factor
model
, is closely related to factor analysis
[
Bas94
]
, a
technique used to capture underlying relationships in high-dimensional data with
a parsimonious set of parameters. Statistical factor models have widespread
applications across various fields, including econometrics, finance, and signal
processing
[
BKM11
,
GBC16
,
FWZZ21
]
. There also exist
macroeconomic and fundamental factor models
[
Con95
]
, but in this paper
we will restrict our attention to fitting
statistical
factor models.

There are two main techniques for fitting a statistical factor model. The first
technique starts by computing an unstructured covariance estimate
\hat{\Sigma}\in{\mbox{\bf S}}^{n}
, such as the sample covariance matrix, followed by a second step
that decomposes the preliminary estimate
\hat{\Sigma}
according to
(
1
). A famous method for the decomposition step is
based on principal components analysis (PCA)
[
JW07
, §9.3]
, and is
closely related to least-squares approaches that fit a statistical factor model
by minimizing the Frobenius norm difference
\|\hat{\Sigma}-(FF^{T}+D)\|^{2}_{F}
with respect to
F
and
D

[
HJ66
,
JG72
,
LCHG16
,
SB23
]
. These decomposition methods have in common that they make
no explicit assumption on the distribution of the observed data. However, if the
data contains heavy tails or outliers, using the sample covariance matrix as the
preliminary estimate leads to poor performance
[
PRFC03
]
, since the
sample covariance matrix tends to be a poor estimate of the true covariance
matrix for heavy-tailed data.

The second technique for fitting a covariance matrix of the form
(
1
) assumes that the distribution of the observed data
is known. The matrices
F
and
D
are then estimated by enforcing the structure
(
1
) into the maximum likelihood (ML) estimation problem under
the assumed distribution. It is most common to model the data as Gaussian,
resulting in the ML estimation problem
[
Jö67
]

\mbox{minimize }\log\det(FF^{T}+D)+\mathop{\bf Tr}(S(FF^{T}+D)^{-1})

(2)

with variables
F\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n\times r}
and diagonal
D\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n\times n}
. (The problem data is the sample covariance matrix
S\in{\mbox{\bf S}}^{n}_{+}.)
A
popular algorithm for solving (
2
) is the
expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm by Rubin & Thayer
[
RT82
]
,
which has a cheap iteration cost of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}r)
. Alternative
methods for solving (
2
) have also been proposed

[
LCHG16
,
SB23
,
LRW98
,
LR98
,
ZYJ08
,
KM19
]
. However, most
of these methods compute a spectral decomposition in each iteration, incurring a
higher iteration cost of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
and thus limiting their
scalability compared to Rubin & Thayer’s EM. If the diagonal matrix
D
is
constrained to have all entries equal, problem (
2
) has a
closed-form solution and is called
probabilistic PCA

[
TB99
]
.

A drawback of assuming
an underlying Gaussian distribution is that the estimation of
F
and
D
tends to become
sensitive to outliers or heavy tails in the observed data. To accomodate for more
heavy-tailed data, extensions based on the multivariate
t
-distribution

[
LR95
,
ZLL14
,
ZLKP20
]
, and other elliptical
distributions
[
HFBM+23
,
HFEBG22
]
, have
been proposed. Extensions of probabilistic
PCA to heteroscedastic data have also been studied

[
HGBF21
,
CBB+21
,
ZCW22
]
.

In this paper, we introduce a new method for robustly fitting a statistical
factor model. The derivation of our method assumes that the distribution of the
random variable
Z
describing the observed data belongs to the broad family of

elliptical probability distributions
. This family, which encompasses the
Gaussian distribution, preserves the elliptical symmetry of the Gaussian density
while allowing for distributions with much heavier tails. We then estimate
F

and
D
using a maximum likelihood approach based on the normalized random
variable
Z/\|Z\|_{2}
. Our method can be viewed as a variant of Tyler’s
M-estimator for the scatter matrix of an elliptical distribution

[
Tyl87
]
, adapted to the covariance structure
(
1
). Tyler’s estimator has garnered significant attention
in the signal processing and optimization communities
[
Tyl87
,
CWH11
,
SBP14
,
WZ15
,
ZCS16
,
FM20
,
GLN20
,
OPP21
,
DG22
]
. A
popular line of research has been to incorporate structural constraints into the
ML estimation problem associated with Tyler’s estimator
[
SW14
,
SBP16
,
STW16
,
BBG+21
]
. The existing works
[
SBP16
,
BBG+21
]
investigate Tyler’s ML estimation problem under the structural
constraint (
1
) with the additional assumption that the
diagonal entries of
D
are equal.

While Tyler’s estimator has been extensively studied, no work seems to have
investigated the impact of incorporating the covariance structure
(
1
) into the ML estimation problem associated with
Tyler’s estimator. In this paper we close this gap by introducing
T-Rex

(
T
yler’s
R
obust Factor Model via

E
xpectation-Ma
X
imization), a novel EM algorithm for robustly
fitting a statistical factor model. (As a surprising byproduct of our novel
EM-based analysis, we also uncover that Tyler’s classical fixed-point iteration
is itself an instance of the EM algorithm.) We empirically compare

T-Rex
against factor model estimators that assume the observed data
follows either a Gaussian or multivariate
t
-distribution. The numerical
results show that
T-Rex
is robust, matching the performance of
Gaussian-based methods on Gaussian data, and performing as well as methods based
on the multivariate
t
-distribution on multivariate
t
-distributed data.
Furthermore, our method outperforms the competing estimators when the data is
contaminated with outliers or deviates from the assumed data distributions of
the competing estimators. We also highlight the utility of
T-Rex
for
robust direction-of-arrival estimation
[
MGOP24
]
in non-uniform
noise
[
LCHG16
]
, and robust subspace recovery
[
LM18
]
.

A closely related work to ours is the recent
subspace constrained Tyler’s
estimator
(
STE
)
[
YZL24
]
, which, like
T-Rex
,
integrates low-rank structure into Tyler’s estimator. However, there are several
key differences between
T-Rex
and
STE
. First,
T-Rex

has a clear interpretation as a maximum likelihood estimator, whereas

STE
is derived heuristically. Specifically, at each iteration of
Tyler’s standard fixed-point procedure,
STE
applies a low-rank
approximation of the current scatter matrix estimate by computing its spectral
decomposition. This approach lacks a principled interpretation as a maximum
likelihood estimator. Second,
T-Rex
is designed for robust low-rank
scatter matrix estimation, while
STE
primarily focuses on the related task
of robust subspace recovery. Third,
T-Rex
seems to be more scalable
than
STE
since, unlike
STE
, it does not require a spectral
decomposition in each iteration. (We comment more on this in §
4.4
.) Finally, and most importantly,
T-Rex

outperforms
STE
in our experiments for both robust covariance
estimation and subspace recovery in direction-of-arrival estimation.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In §
2
we present
background on EM and Tyler’s estimator. In §
3
we introduce our
approach for robustly fitting a statistical factor model, and derive the corresponding
EM algorithm
T-Rex
. In §
4
we present extensive
numerical experiments, followed by conclusions in §
5
.

2
Preliminaries

The technical foundation of this paper relies on Tyler’s estimator and the
EM algorithm. In this section, we provide
background on both.

2.1
Background on Tyler’s estimator

Several common probability distributions, such as the Gaussian and the
multivariate
t
-distribution, are
elliptical
. The probability
density function (pdf) of an elliptical
n
-dimensional random variable
Z

with zero mean is of the form

p(z;\Sigma)=\frac{C}{\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}g(z^{T}\Sigma^{-1}z)

(3)

where
C>0
is a normalization constant,
\Sigma\in{\mbox{\bf S}}^{n}_{++}
is
the
scatter matrix
and
g:{\mbox{\bf R}}\to{\mbox{\bf R}}_{+}
is the
density
generator
determining the decay of the tails
[
ZKOM18
, §4.2]
.
(The scatter matrix is a positive multiple of the covariance matrix
whenever the latter exists.) If
Z
is elliptical with zero mean, then the
normalized random variable
X=Z/\|Z\|_{2}
has a so-called
angular
central Gaussian distribution
, with a pdf of the form

p(x;\Sigma)=\frac{\Gamma(n/2)}{2\pi^{n/2}\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}(x^{T}\Sigma^{-1}x)^{-n/2},

(4)

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the gamma function
[
ZKOM18
, §4.2]
. A
remarkable feature of the density
p(x;\Sigma)
is that it is independent
of the original elliptical distribution, since it does not depend on the
density generator
g
. The ML estimate of the scatter matrix
\Sigma
given

m
independent observations
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
of
X
can be found by
solving

\text{minimize }\log\det\Sigma+\frac{n}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log(x_{i}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}x_{i})

(5)

with variable
\Sigma\in{\mbox{\bf S}}^{n}
. This estimator of the scatter matrix is
called
Tyler’s M-estimator

[
Tyl87
]
and has garnered significant
attention in the signal processing and optimization communities (see the
references in §
1
).

2.2
Background on expectation-maximization

The EM algorithm
[
DLR77
]
is useful for solving certain ML
estimation problems where direct maximization of the likelihood is
difficult. Suppose we have
m
independent observations
\mathcal{X}\triangleq\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}
of a random variable
X
, and our goal is
to estimate a parameter vector
\theta
parameterizing its density
function
p(x;\theta)
. The ML estimate of
\theta
is the maximizer of
the log-likelihood function

L(\theta;\mathcal{X})\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log p(x_{i};\theta).

The EM algorithm can be useful when it is difficult to directly maximize
the log-likelihood with respect to
\theta
. The idea is to augment the

observed
data
\mathcal{X}
with
unobserved
data
\mathcal{R}\triangleq\{R_{1},\dots,,R_{m}\}
, where we model
R_{i},\>i=1,\dots,m

as random variables. We further assume that they are independent and that
their pdf
p(r;\theta)
depends on the parameter
\theta
. The key is to
augment the observed data in such a way that the
complete
log-likelihood function

L(\theta;\mathcal{X},\mathcal{R})\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log p(x_{i},R_{i};\theta)

(6)

would be easy to maximize if
\mathcal{R}
were known. However, since

\mathcal{R}
is not known in practice,
L(\theta;\mathcal{X},\mathcal{R})
is a random variable; thus, maximizing it with respect to

\theta
is not possible.

Given a parameter estimate
\theta_{k}
, the EM algorithm approximates the
observed data likelihood
L(\mathcal{X};\theta)
by taking the expectation
of the complete log-likelihood
L(\theta;\mathcal{X},\mathcal{R})
,
conditioned on the observed data
\mathcal{X}
, under the assumption that

\theta_{k}
is the true parameter governing the distributions. In other
words, EM is based on the approximation

\begin{split}L(\theta;\mathcal{X})&\approx\mathop{\bf E{}}_{\mathcal{R}|\mathcal{X},\theta_{k}}[L(\theta;\mathcal{X},\mathcal{R})]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathop{\bf E{}}_{R_{i}|x_{i},\theta_{k}}[\log p(x_{i},R_{i};\theta)]\end{split}

where

\mathop{\bf E{}}_{R_{i}|x_{i},\theta_{k}}[\log p(x_{i},R_{i};\theta)]=\int\log p(x_{i},r;\theta)p(r|x_{i},\theta_{k})dr.

The next parameter estimate
\theta_{k+1}
is then obtained from

\theta_{k+1}=\mathop{\rm argmax}_{\theta}\mathop{\bf E{}}_{\mathcal{R}|\mathcal{X},\theta_{k}}[L(\theta;\mathcal{X},\mathcal{R})].

3
An EM algorithm for fitting a robust factor model

In this section we describe a new method for fitting a statistical factor
model based on Tyler’s estimator. Our approach is based on incorporating
the structural constraint (
1
) into problem
(
5
), resulting in the problem

\begin{split}\text{minimize }&f(F,D)\triangleq\log\det(FF^{T}+D)+\frac{n}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log(x_{i}^{T}(FF^{T}+D)^{-1}x_{i})\end{split}

(7)

with variables
F\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n\times r}
and diagonal
D\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n\times n}
. Problem (
7
) is the ML estimation
problem for fitting a statistical factor model under the angular central
Gaussian distribution (
4
). To solve (
7
) we derive
an EM algorithm in §
3.1
and
§
3.2
, followed by a summary and implementation
details in §
3.3
and §
3.4
,
respectively.

3.1
The latent variable model

When fitting a statistical factor model, the unknown parameter to be
estimated is
\theta\triangleq(F,D)
. To design an EM algorithm for
solving (
7
), we introduce a probabilistic
model for the observed and unobserved variables,
X
and
R
, in a way that
ensures
X
is marginally distributed according to the angular central
Gaussian distribution (
4
), and also allows for easy
maximization of the complete data likelihood if
\mathcal{R}
were known.
We specify the latent variable model by assuming that the joint pdf of
(X,R)
is

p(x,r;\theta)=\frac{r^{n-1}}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\sqrt{\det\Sigma_{FD}}}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2}x^{T}\Sigma_{FD}^{-1}x\right)

(8)

where
\Sigma_{FD}\triangleq FF^{T}+D
, with support
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\times{\mbox{\bf R}}_{+}
where
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is the unit sphere in
{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}
.
(We motivate this choice of joint pdf heuristically in Appendix

A.1
; roughly speaking, it can be
interpreted as the pdf of a random variable
Z\sim N(0,\Sigma_{FD})

parameterized as
Z=RX
where
X
has unit norm.) Given this
specification for the joint density, the marginal density of
X
is

\begin{split}p(x;\theta)&=\int_{0}^{\infty}p(x,r;\theta)dr\\
&=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\sqrt{\det\Sigma_{FD}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{n-1}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2}x^{T}\Sigma_{FD}^{-1}x\right)dr\\
&=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\sqrt{\det\Sigma_{FD}}}\frac{\Gamma(n/2)2^{n/2-1}}{(x^{T}\Sigma_{FD}^{-1}x)^{n/2}}\\
&=\frac{\Gamma(n/2)}{2\pi^{n/2}\sqrt{\det\Sigma_{FD}}}(x^{T}\Sigma_{FD}^{-1}x)^{-n/2},\end{split}

where we in the third equality have used that

[
GR07
, equation (3.326)]

\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{n-1}\exp(-(a/2)r^{2})dr=\frac{\Gamma(n/2)2^{n/2-1}}{a^{n/2}}

for any
a>0
. Thus, we see that under the latent variable model
(
8
), the marginal density of
X
is the pdf of
the angular central Gaussian distribution (
4
), which
means that an EM algorithm based on this latent variable model will indeed target
the ML estimation problem (
7
).

3.2
The expectation and maximization steps

Under the latent variable model (
8
), the
complete log-likelihood function (
6
)
is

\begin{split}L(\theta;\mathcal{X},\mathcal{R})&=m(n-1)\log r-(mn/2)\log(2\pi)-\frac{m}{2}\log\det\Sigma_{FD}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}^{2}x_{i}^{T}\Sigma_{FD}^{-1}x_{i}\\
&=c-\frac{m}{2}\left(\log\det\Sigma_{FD}+\mathop{\bf Tr}(\Sigma_{FD}^{-1}S_{rx})\right)\end{split}

(9)

where
c\triangleq m(n-1)\log r-(mn/2)\log(2\pi)
is independent of

(F,D)
, and

S_{rx}\triangleq(1/m)\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}^{2}x_{i}x_{i}^{T}.

At the
k
th iteration of EM, we start with the current iterate
(F_{k},D_{k})
. To compute the next iterate
(F_{k+1},D_{k+1})
, we maximize the
complete log-likelihood (
9
), replacing
the unobserved
S_{rx}
with its conditional expectation given the observed
data
\mathcal{X}
. Specifically,
(F_{k+1},D_{k+1})
is the solution of

\text{minimize }\log\det(FF^{T}+D)+\mathop{\bf Tr}((FF^{T}+D)^{-1}\hat{S}^{(k)})

(10)

with variables
F\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n\times r}
and diagonal
D\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n\times n}
, where

\begin{split}\hat{S}^{(k)}&\triangleq\mathop{\bf E{}}_{\mathcal{R}|\mathcal{X},\theta_{k}}[S_{rx}]=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathop{\bf E{}}_{R_{i}|x_{i};F_{k},D_{k}}[r_{i}^{2}x_{i}x_{i}^{T}]=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathop{\bf E{}}_{R_{i}|x_{i};F_{k},D_{k}}[r_{i}^{2}]x_{i}x_{i}^{T}.\end{split}

(11)

To evaluate the conditional expectation in (
11
),
the following result showing that
R_{i}|x_{i}
has the distribution of a
scaled chi-distributed random variable will be useful.

Lemma 1

Assume the joint density of
(X,R)
is given by
(
8
). Then the conditional distribution of
R

given
X=x
is the same as the distribution of
(1/\sqrt{x^{T}\Sigma_{FD}^{-1}x})S
where
S\sim\chi(n)
.

Proof.

The conditional distribution of
R
given
X=x
is

\begin{split}p(r|x;\theta)&=\frac{p(x,r;\theta)}{p(x;\theta)}\\
&=\frac{\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\sqrt{\det\Sigma_{FD}}}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2}x^{T}\Sigma_{FD}^{-1}x\right)r^{n-1}}{\frac{\Gamma(n/2)}{2\pi^{n/2}\sqrt{\det\Sigma_{FD}}}(x^{T}\Sigma_{FD}^{-1}x)^{-n/2}}\\
&=\frac{(x^{T}\Sigma_{FD}^{-1}x)^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2)2^{n/2-1}}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2}x^{T}\Sigma_{FD}^{-1}x\right)r^{n-1}.\\
\end{split}

If
S\sim\chi(n)
, then the pdf of
S
is
[
Chr07
, §8.14]

p_{S}(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(n/2)2^{n/2-1}}s^{n-1}\exp\left(-\frac{s^{2}}{2}\right).

From the fact that the pdf of
aS
with
a=1/\sqrt{x^{T}\Sigma_{FD}^{-1}x}
is
(1/a)p_{S}(s/a)
, we get the desired result.

\blacksquare

By using Lemma
1
together with the fact that the
second moment of a
\chi(n)
-distributed random variable is
n

[
Chr07
, §8.14]
, we conclude that
\hat{S}^{(k)}
in
(
11
) can be computed as

\hat{S}^{(k)}=\frac{n}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{x_{i}^{T}(F_{k}F_{k}^{T}+D_{k})^{-1}x_{i}}x_{i}x_{i}^{T}.

(12)

3.3
The complete algorithm

We can now summarize the EM algorithm for solving
(
7
). The E-step consists of computing

\hat{S}^{(k)}
using (
12
), and the M-step is
given by (
10
). The complete method for solving
(
7
) is summarized in Algorithm

3.3
below. We call it
T-Rex
, standing for

T
yler’s
R
obust Factor Model via

E
xpectation-ma
X
imization. We will later discuss the
initialization, how to evaluate the M-step, and the termination criterion.

Algorithm 1

T-Rex

Initialization.
Compute
F_{0}
and
D_{0}

repeat for
k=0,1,\dots

1.
E-step.
Compute
\hat{S}^{(k)}
using (
12
).

2.
M-step.
Let
(F_{k+1},D_{k+1})
be the solution of
(
10
).

3. Check termination criterion.

Interpretation of
T-Rex
.

T-Rex
has an
insightful interpretation. The M-step can be interpreted as fitting a
low-rank plus diagonal approximation of the matrix
\hat{S}^{(k)}

[
JOP+23
]
. This matrix resembles an adaptively weighted sample
covariance matrix, where the contributions of rank-one terms
x_{i}x_{i}^{T}

are downweighted when the quantity
x_{i}^{T}(F_{k}F_{k}^{T}+D_{k})^{-1}x_{i}
is
large. The quantity
x_{i}^{T}(F_{k}F_{k}^{T}+D_{k})^{-1}x_{i}
is the

Mahalanobis distance
between
x_{i}
and the origin with respect to

F_{k}F_{k}^{T}+D_{k}
. In robust statistics, a large Mahalanobis distance is
typically interpreted as an indicator of an outlier. (This is easy to
justify for an elliptical distribution, since the density function
(
3
) for an elliptical distribution takes on a
small value when the Mahalanobis distance is large.) Hence, roughly
speaking, the EM algorithm
iteratively fits statistical factor models to weighted
sample covariance matrices, where outliers with respect to the current fit
have been downweighted
.

Interpretation of Tyler’s fixed-point iteration as EM.

Tyler’s estimator of a scatter matrix (without taking the factor model into
account) is traditionally defined as a positive definite matrix

\Sigma^{\star}\in{\mbox{\bf S}}^{n}
satisfying
[
Tyl87
]

\Sigma^{\star}=\frac{n}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{x_{i}^{T}(\Sigma^{\star})^{-1}x_{i}}x_{i}x_{i}^{T}.

(This equation is equivalent to setting the gradient of the objective
function of (
5
) to zero.) The traditional way
of computing Tyler’s estimator is through the fixed-point iteration

\Sigma_{k+1}=\frac{n}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{x_{i}^{T}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}x_{i}}x_{i}x_{i}^{T}.

(13)

If we derive an EM algorithm similar to
T-Rex
to compute Tyler’s
estimator by solving (
5
), the M-step is to
minimize the function
\Sigma\mapsto\log\det\Sigma+\mathop{\bf Tr}(\Sigma^{-1}\hat{S}^{k})

where
\hat{S}^{(k)}=(1/m)\sum_{i=1}^{m}(1/(x_{i}^{T}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}x_{k}))x_{i}x_{i}^{T}.
This M-step is solvable in closed form and gives the EM update

\Sigma_{k+1}=\hat{S}^{(k)}
, which is identical to the fixed-point
update (
13
). This reveals that
Tyler’s renowned
fixed-point iteration can be interpreted as an EM algorithm.
Since this
interpretation seems to be new in the literature, we state it as a formal
result.

Proposition 1

Consider the ML estimation problem (
5
) for
fitting an unstructured scatter matrix
\Sigma\in{\mbox{\bf S}}^{n}
for a random
variable
X
following the angular central Gaussian distribution. The EM
algorithm obtained from the latent variable model

p(x,r;\theta)=\frac{r^{n-1}}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2}x^{T}\Sigma^{-1}x\right)

where
R
is the latent variable, is equivalent to Tyler’s fixed-point
iteration (
13
).

This interpretation is useful for developing faster methods to compute
Tyler’s estimator, by incorporating acceleration techniques or general
improvements to EM algorithms
[
JJ97
,
LRW98
]
. It also
provides a framework for analyzing Tyler’s fixed-point iteration and its
extensions
[
CWH11
,
OPP21
]
through the lens of EM.

3.4
Implementation

Initialization and termination.

To initialize
T-Rex
we use PCA, and a possible termination
criterion is based on the relative change in objective value between
consecutive iterates. More details are presented in Appendix

A.2
.

Evaluating the M-step.

The M-step of
T-Rex
is similar to problem
(
2
), which is the ML estimation problem when the
observed data is assumed to be Gaussian. This problem has been extensively
studied (see the prior work in §
1
) and can be solved
with several algorithms. We prefer the EM algorithm by Rubin & Thayer

[
RT82
]
, simply because it has iteration cost
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}r)

rather than
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
and seems to work well in practice. We
summarize it in Appendix
A.2
.

Exploiting low-rank structure.

Several steps of
T-Rex
can be sped up using the inherent low-rank
structure. For example, the matrix inversion formula
[
BV04
, §C.4]
can be
used to compute
\Sigma_{k}^{-1}x_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,m
, where
\Sigma_{k}=F_{k}F_{k}^{T}+D_{k}
, at a cost of order
\mathcal{O}(nmr)
. This is
significantly lower than the cost when the low-rank structure is not taken
into account which is of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})+\mathcal{O}(n^{2}m)
.

Computing
\Sigma_{k}^{-1}x_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,m
is the bottleneck in
the fixed-point iteration (
13
) used to compute Tyler’s
estimator without the factor model structure. This bottleneck is the key
reason why computing the standard Tyler’s estimator is often considered
computationally prohibitive in high-dimensional settings. However,

T-Rex
exploits the low-rank structure and allows Tyler’s estimator
to be scaled up more gracefully to higher dimensions.

Iteration cost.

In every iteration of
T-Rex
we compute
\Sigma_{k}^{-1}x_{i},\>i=1,\dots,m
at a cost of order
\mathcal{O}(nmr)
, we then form

\hat{S}^{(k)}
at a cost of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}m)
, and finally
evaluate the M-step using the EM algorithm from
[
RT82
]
with
iteration cost
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}r)
. We have observed that
T-Rex

in practice typically converges quickly to a local minimizer of
(
7
), often within 5-10 iterations.

In the application of subspace recovery studied in the next section, we
have
n\gg m
with
n=32256
and
m=499
. In this setting it is
possible to implement
T-Rex
without forming any
n\times n

matrices, including
\hat{S}^{(k)}
, explicitly. Furthermore, in this case
we can implement the EM algorithm by Rubin & Thayer with an iteration cost
of order
\mathcal{O}(nmr)
, representing a substantial improvement over
the cost
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}r)
.

Numerical experiments

In this section we evaluate the empirical performance of

for robust direction-of-arrival estimation in

to a stylized application of

https://github.com/dance858/T-Rex-EM
.

Synthetic data simulations

Experimental setup.

First we consider an example with

1.

. Secondly, we consider

. For the contaminated

is the Frobenius norm,

,

,

, or

. The expectation in

deteriorates

,

and

remain unaffected. On Gaussian data with outliers, both

and

4.3
Direction-of-arrival estimation in nonuniform noise

In our next example we consider the problem of estimating the arrival angles of
r
sources
emitting signals that impinge on a uniform linear array of
n
sensors. The
arrival angle of source
k
relative the array normal direction is
\theta_{k}
,
and we model the array output
x_{t}\in{\mbox{\bf C}}^{n}
as

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad x_{t}=A(\theta)z_{t}+\epsilon_{t},\qquad t=1,\dots,m

where
\theta\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{r}
is the vector of arrival angles,
A(\theta)\in{\mbox{\bf C}}^{n\times r}

is a steering matrix that depends on the geometry of the array,
z_{t}\in{\mbox{\bf C}}^{r}
is the vector of source signals, and
\epsilon_{t}\in{\mbox{\bf C}}^{n}

represents noise. Under the assumption that the source signal is uncorrelated
with the noise, the covariance matrix
\Sigma\in{\mbox{\bf H}}^{n}
of the array output is

\Sigma=A(\theta)PA(\theta)^{H}+Q,

(15)

where
P\in{\mbox{\bf H}}^{n}
is the signal covariance matrix,
\sigma_{s}^{2}>0

is the signal power, and
Q\in{\mbox{\bf H}}^{n}
is the noise covariance matrix that
is further assumed to be diagonal. (Here

{\mbox{\bf H}}^{n}
is the space of Hermitian matrices of dimension
n
and the superscript
H

denotes the conjugate transpose.)
Traditional direction-of-arrival (DOA) estimators are derived under the assumption of uniform white noise,

i.e.,

Q=\sigma^{2}I
for some
\sigma^{2}>0
. This assumption implies that the
signal and noise subspaces can be correctly determined through the
spectral decomposition of the array covariance matrix
\Sigma
. However, this is no
longer true when the noise is nonuniform,
i.e.,
when
Q
is an arbitrary diagonal
matrix, since in this case the eigenvectors of
\Sigma

associated with the
r
largest eigenvalues do no longer span the signal
subspace.

Designing DOA estimators for nonuniform noise has garnered significant
attention (see, for example,
[
PG01
,
LCHG16
]
and the references therein).
One approach is to decompose an estimate
\hat{\Sigma}\in{\mbox{\bf H}}^{n}
of the array covariance matrix according to

\hat{\Sigma}=FF^{H}+D,

and then apply traditional DOA estimators such as root-MUSIC
[
SM05
]
to the matrix

FF^{H}
. This method is justified by observing that if
\hat{\Sigma}
is equal to
the true array covariance matrix, then the
column space of
F=A(\theta)P^{1/2}
is identical to the column space of the
steering matrix
A(\theta)
, which is equal to the signal subspace.

Benchmark methods.

We compare the performance of
the sample covariance matrix
(\texttt{SC})
with
GFA
,

T-Rex
and
STE
for DOA-estimation in nonuniform noise.
We apply root-MUSIC
directly to the sample covariance matrix and the scatter matrix estimate
obtained from
STE
, whereas for
GFA
and

T-Rex
, we first estimate the matrices
F
and
D
from the available
snapshots, and then apply root-MUSIC to the matrix
FF^{H}
. As performance metric we
use the mean-squared error

\text{MSE}=\mathop{\bf E{}}\|\hat{\theta}-\theta\|_{2}^{2}

(16)

where
\theta\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{r}
is the vector of true arrival angles, and
\hat{\theta}\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{r}

is the estimate obtained from MUSIC.

In this application, the data
x_{t}
is complex-valued. Although the density
function for complex-valued elliptical distributions differs slightly from that
of real-valued elliptical distributions (see
[
ZKOM18
, §4]
), the
estimation problem for Tyler’s estimator under the structural constraint
\Sigma=FF^{H}+D
remains unchanged. Furthermore, careful bookkeeping shows that our
approach
T-Rex
extends to the complex-valued setting with essentially
no changes.

We omit
TFA
from these experiments because the implementation we use
(the R-package
fitHeavyTail

[
PZW+23
]
) does not support
complex-valued data.

The purpose of this experiment is not to conduct an extensive benchmarking of
state-of-the-art methods for DOA estimation in nonuniform noise. Instead, we
simply want to highlight the differences between our approach, one based on the
Gaussian distribution (
GFA
), and another based on integrating low-rank
structure into Tyler’s estimator (
STE
). However, it is worth noting
that the
GFA
method we compare against is based on solving the same ML
estimation problem as the one considered in
[
LCHG16
,
SB23
]
, which
has been described as state-of-the-art for DOA estimation in non-uniform noise.

Experimental setup.

We consider a scenario with
n=15
sensors,
r=4
sources, true arrival
angles
\theta=(0\degree,\>5\degree,\>10\degree,\>15\degree)
, and source
covariance matrix
P=\sigma^{2}_{s}I
where
\sigma^{2}_{s}=1
is the signal
power. We will
first assume that the array output follows a complex-circular Gaussian
distribution
x\sim{\mbox{\bf C}}N(0,\Sigma)
where
\Sigma
is given by
(
15
). This is the most common assumption for DOA estimation
and corresponds to the case where the source signal and the noise are assumed
to be independent and Gaussian. We also plot the trace of the
Crámer-Rao bound (CRB) matrix that is derived in closed-form for the Gaussian setting with nonuniform
noise in
[
PG01
, equation (36)]
.

To improve robustness of DOA–estimators, researchers have proposed to consider a more heavy-tailed signal or noise model

[
MPFO13
,
ZKOM18
]
.
Following
[
OK03
,
MGO22

MGOP24
]
, we will
therefore also consider the case when the array output

follows a zero-mean complex
multivariate
t
-distribution with three degrees of freedom and covariance

\Sigma
given by (

). In this case we evaluate the CRB using

[

.

The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is defined as
[
LCHG16

\text{SNR}=\frac{\sigma_{s}^{2}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}

where
\sigma_{i}^{2}>0
is the noise variance for the
i
th sensor. Following
[

]
, we draw the

(0,1)
and then

To approximate the mean-squared
error (

) we use 300 Monte Carlo runs.

Figure

presents the MSE versus the number of

Robust subspace recovery

In our final example we consider the problem of

[
LM18
]
. In particular, we demonstrate the ability of

to robustly fit a low-dimensional linear subspace by using it to remove shadows

Experimental setup.

We consider the same setup as in

[
LMTZ15
,
Zha15
]
based on 64 images of a face from the extended Yale face
database
[
KCHK05
]
. We use the first 32 images as the training set and
reserve the remaining 32 for the out-of-sample test. To learn a low-dimensional
subspace we use a dataset consisting of the 32 train images dispersed in a
collection of 467 random images from the Background/Google folder of the Caltech
101 database
[
FFFP04
]
. The images are 192
\times
168 pixels.
By stacking the images as columns of
a matrix, we end up with a data matrix
X=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&x_{2}&\dots&x_{m}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n\times m}
with
m=499
samples, each sample
having dimension
n=32256
. As is common when fitting a linear subspace
in a robust manner, we center the images by subtracting the so-called

Euclidean median
, which is defined as the minimizer of

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|x_{i}-c\|_{2}
with respect to
c\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}
.

Under the so-called
Lambertian model
, images of convex objects taken
under changing illumination lie near a linear subspace of dimension
r=9

[
BJ03
]
. (The Lambertian model is widely used in computer vision. It
describes how certain surfaces reflect light; see
[
ON95
]
.) To remove
shadows and specularities in face images, we can estimate this nine-dimensional

T-Rex
, we
estimate the subspace with the range of
F
, where
F
is obtained from
Algorithm
3.3
with
r=9
. To benchmark our approach, we also
fit a nine-dimensional linear subspace using PCA,
spherical PCA

[
LMS+99
,
Mar05
]
(
S-PCA
) which is a version of PCA
that aims to improve the robustness to outliers,
outlier pursuit

(
OP
)
[
XCS10
,
MT11
]
which is based on convex optimization and
nuclear norm minimization, and
S-Reaper

[
LMTZ15
]
which is
another convex optimization-based approach. We also tried to apply
STE

[
YZL24
]
to this problem using the code provided by the authors
(
https://github.com/alexfengg/STE
), but
STE
ran out of memory
while computing the spectral decomposition of a matrix of size
32256\times 32256
.

Conclusions

We presented

shows impressive and
robust performance, and we therefore believe that

contributes to

A.1
The latent variable model

Here we provide a heuristic motivation for the choice of joint density
(
8
) for the latent variable model. Suppose
Z\sim N(0,\Sigma)
is an
n
-dimensional random variable and let
\mathcal{A}_{1}\subseteq{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}
. The pdf of
Z
, denoted by
p_{Z}(z)
, is defined through

\mathop{\bf Prob}(Z\in\mathcal{A}_{1})=\int_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}p_{Z}(z)dz.

Consider the change of variables
z=rx
where
r>0
and
x
is restricted to the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\times{\mbox{\bf R}}_{+}
be the set of all
(x,r)
such that
z=rx
for some
z\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
. We can express the volume element
dz
as
dz=r^{n-1}dxdr
. Hence,

\mathop{\bf Prob}(Z\in\mathcal{A}_{1})=\int_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}p_{Z}(rx)r^{n-1}dxdr.

This should be equal to
\mathop{\bf Prob}((X,R)\in\mathcal{A}_{2})
,
so the joint pdf of
(X,R)
is
p_{X,R}(x,r)=p_{Z}(rx)r^{n-1}
, which is the
same as (
8
).

A.2
Implementation details

Initialization.

We initialize
T-Rex
using the following
strategy based on PCA of the correlation matrix. (One reason for applying PCA to
the sample correlation matrix, rather than the sample covariance matrix itself,
is to ensure the initialization is scale-invariant.) Let
S\in{\mbox{\bf S}}^{n}
be the
sample covariance matrix, and define the vector of standard derivations as
s=\mathop{\bf diag}(S)^{1/2}
. The sample correlation matrix is then given by
R=\mathop{\bf diag}(s)^{-1}S\mathop{\bf diag}(s)^{-1}
. First we compute the spectral decomposition
R=Q\Lambda Q^{T}
at a cost of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
, and then form the low-rank
approximation
\hat{R}=\hat{F}\hat{F}^{T}+\hat{D}
, where

\hat{F}=Q_{1:r}\Lambda^{1/2}_{1:r},\qquad\hat{D}=\mathop{\bf diag}(\mathop{\bf diag}(R-\hat{F}\hat{F}^{T})).

The corresponding approximation of the sample covariance matrix is
\hat{S}=F_{0}F_{0}^{T}+D_{0}
, where

F_{0}=\mathop{\bf diag}(s)\hat{F},\qquad D_{0}=\mathop{\bf diag}(s)\hat{D}\mathop{\bf diag}(s).

We use this choice of
F_{0}
and
D_{0}
to initialize Algorithm
3.3
.
This initialization is not specific for Algorithm
3.3
and can
be used to initialize any iterative method for fitting a statistical factor
model. The same initialization strategy was used in
[
JOP+23
]
, and in
statistics it is closely related to what is called
principal component factor
analysis

[
JW07
, §9.3]
.

In the robust subspace recovery example in §
4.4
where
n\gg m
, we want to avoid forming an
n\times n
matrix. We
therefore initialize
T-Rex
using PCA on the data matrix. In practice,
this can be done by computing the reduced SVD of the data matrix at
a cost of order
\mathcal{O}(nm^{2})
.

Termination criterion.

A termination criterion that often works well
in practice is to terminate when the relative change in objective value between
two consecutive iterates is smaller than a threshold. Specifically,
we can terminate Algorithm
3.3
whenever

\frac{|f(F_{k+1},D_{k+1})-f(F_{k},D_{k})|}{|f(F_{k},D_{k})|}\leq\epsilon

(17)

for some prespecified
\epsilon>0
, where
f(F,D)=\log\det(FF^{T}+D)+(n/m)\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log(x_{i}^{T}(FF^{T}+D)^{-1}x_{i})
is the objective function of
the maximum likelihood estimation problem (
7
).
To evaluate the first term of
f(F,D)
efficiently, without incurring a cost that is cubic in
n
,
we use the identity

\log\det(FF^{T}+D)=\log\det(D)+\log\det(I+F^{T}D^{-1}F).

The cost of forming the
r\times r
matrix
I+F^{T}D^{-1}F
is of order
\mathcal{O}(nr^{2})
.
Evaluating
\log\det(I+F^{T}D^{-1}F)
is cheap since
the matrix
I+F^{T}D^{-1}F
is of dimension
r
. (In practice, we evaluate the
log-determinant by computing the Cholesky factorization of
I+F^{T}D^{-1}F
.)

To compute the second term of
f(F,d)
, we
must evaluate
(FF^{T}+D)^{-1}x_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,m
. This can be done
at a cost of order
\mathcal{O}(nmr)
using the matrix inversion formula
[
BV04
]
.

EM for Gaussian factor analysis.

The EM algorithm by Rubin & Thayer
[
RT82
]
is perhaps the most well-known
method for solving the Gaussian ML estimation problem
(
2
). Its iteration complexity of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}r)
sets it apart from many competing approaches. An additional advantage of the algorithm
lies in its simplicity, as it can be implemented with around 10 lines of
code in a high-level programming language.

Rubin and Thayer’s EM algorithm can be summarized as follows. Given an iterate

(F_{k},D_{k})
, the next iterate is given by

\begin{split}F_{k+1}&=SA_{k}B_{k}^{-1},\\
D_{k+1}&=\mathop{\bf diag}(\mathop{\bf diag}(S-2SA_{k}F_{k+1}^{T}+F_{k+1}B_{k}F_{k+1}^{T})),\end{split}

where

\begin{split}A_{k}&=D_{k}^{-1}F_{k}H_{k}\\
B_{k}&=H_{k}+A_{k}^{T}SA_{k}\\
H_{k}&=(I+F_{k}^{T}D_{k}^{-1}F_{k})^{-1}.\end{split}

A.3
Experimental details

For the experiments in §
4.1
, we assume that the true covariance matrix is of
the form
\Sigma_{\text{true}}=F_{\text{true}}F_{\text{true}}^{T}+D_{\text{true}}
. To construct
\Sigma_{\text{true}}
for a given value of

n
, we first compute the sample covariance matrix
S\in{\mbox{\bf S}}^{n}
from the daily returns of
n
companies on S&P 500 over the period
from January 1, 2022 to January 1, 2024. We then apply PCA on
S
and choose the
columns of the factor loading matrix
F_{\text{true}}
to be equal to the
r

first principal directions, scaled with the square root of the corresponding
eigenvalues, and then pick
D_{\text{true}}=\mathop{\bf diag}(\mathop{\bf diag}(S-F_{\text{true}}F_{\text{true}}^{T}))
.
