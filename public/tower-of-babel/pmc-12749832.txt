Remimazolam-induced anesthesia does not affect circadian rhythms significantly in mice
Authors: Yasuhito Suzuki, Ryo Imai, Yoshitaka Aoki, Shingo Kawashima, Kazuya Hokamura, Tetsuro Kimura, Tadayoshi Kurita, Takasumi Katoh, Soichiro Mimuro, Hiroshi Makino, Kazuo Umemura, Yoshiki Nakajima
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough inhalational general anesthesia affects mammalian circadian rhythms, which are generated through oscillations in expression of clock genes in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), the impact of intravenous benzodiazepine (BDZ)-induced general anesthesia is not well understood. We investigated the effects of remimazolam (RMZ), an ultra-short-acting BDZ that has recently gained widespread use in clinical anesthesia, on circadian rhythms and clock gene expression in the SCN in male C57BL/6J mice. Phase shifts in behavioral rest-activity circadian rhythms were quantitatively measured under constant dark conditions. No significant differences were detected among the interventions tested: continuous RMZ injection, bolus RMZ injection, and saline injection at different circadian times. Analyses of day/night activity distribution and the percentage of mice in inactivity bouts showed no significant differences among these groups. To assess the effects of RMZ on clock gene expression, we used continuous RMZ injection, which better reflects clinical anesthesia conditions. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction revealed that the mRNA expression levels of major clock genes Per2, Bmal1, Cry1, and Npas2 in the SCN were decreased, whereas the neuronal activity marker Egr1 was increased. The period of circadian oscillations in clock gene Per2 expression–analyzed by bioluminescence rhythms of cultured SCN tissue from PER2::LUC mice–did not change with any concentration of RMZ administration. Our findings show that RMZ has minimal effects on circadian rhythms under our experimental protocols, suggesting that RMZ has the potential to reduce complications related to circadian rhythm disruption by general anesthesia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29999-9.Keywords: Circadian rhythm, Remimazolam, Benzodiazepine, Perioperative complicationsSubject terms: Neurology, Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

Circadian rhythms are endogenous physiological cycles of approximately 24 h, observed in almost all living organisms. In mammals, the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus serves as the central circadian clock, coordinating the timing of various biological processes. These rhythms are regulated by an internal circadian oscillator, driven by a transcriptional-translational feedback loop (TTFL), where multiple clock genes regulate each other within SCN neurons. In clinical settings, circadian rhythms have recently been recognized as an important factor in perioperative patients, as rhythm disruptions are linked to postoperative delirium and poorer patient outcomes1–3.

Recent studies have highlighted the effects of certain general anesthetics on circadian rhythms, particularly desflurane4. Desflurane alters clock gene expression, producing a phase delay in rest–activity circadian rhythms that depends on the timing of anesthesia5. However, reports on the effects of intravenous anesthetics on circadian rhythms remain scarce. Benzodiazepines (BDZs), positive allosteric modulators of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors, exert sedative effects by activating GABA receptors and inhibiting postsynaptic neuronal firing6. Nearly all SCN neurons express both GABA and GABA receptors7,8, highlighting the importance of GABAergic signaling in the regulation of circadian rhythms9. GABA and GABA receptor agonists are known to have various effects on SCN circadian rhythms10,11. BDZs, such as triazolam, brotizolam, and midazolam, induce phase shifts in behavioral circadian rhythms12–14, likely through an input pathway to the SCN mediated by changes in locomotor activity15. However, no studies have quantitatively investigated the effects of BDZ-induced general anesthesia on the SCN and circadian rhythms. Whether BDZ anesthesia alters the expression of major clock genes remains largely unknown. Clarifying these findings will contribute to elucidating the GABA receptor-mediated mechanisms underlying the effects of general anesthetics on circadian rhythms. In this study, we used remimazolam (RMZ), an ultra-short-acting BDZ increasingly used for general anesthesia and sedation, and reported to exert minimal effects on postoperative delirium16–18. We analyzed the effects of RMZ on rest–activity circadian rhythms, SCN clock gene expression, and the circadian rhythm of cultured SCN tissue in mice.

Body temperature (BT) gradually decreased during continuous RMZ anesthesia, reaching a minimum temperature of 35.3 °C at 120 min (P = 0.032, see Supplementary Fig. S1 online). During continuous RMZ anesthesia, arterial pH decreased (7.31 vs. 7.43, P = 0.008), and arterial carbon dioxide partial pressure (PaCO2) increased (39.3 vs. 26.9, P = 0.008). Total locomotor activity after RMZ anesthesia (Fig. 2c)

Total running wheel rotation ratios in each group at 24, 24–48, and 48–72 h post-anesthesia are presented in Fig. 2c. Wheel rotation for the first 24 h after anesthesia decreased to 48.9–74.7% in the RMZ bolus group and to 18.9–37.9% in the RMZ continuous group. Activity in the RMZ continuous group recovered by 24–48 h across all subgroups except for CT12-16, which decreased to 63.1%. Wheel rotation recovered to pre-anesthesia levels in all groups from 48 to 72 h post-anesthesia.

Activity distribution and inactivity bout structure after RMZ anesthesia (Fig. 2d-e)

In the activity distribution analysis, total wheel-running activity during the inactive phase (CT0–12) did not differ significantly among groups (Control group: 7504 [4454–11556], RMZ bolus group: 9986 [6521–15205], RMZ continuous group: 10653 [7020–16636]; P = 0.27). In contrast, during the active phase (CT12–24), significant group differences were observed (Control group: 46682 [30551–56087], RMZ bolus group: 41673 [29506–61199], RMZ continuous group: 38085 [23557–55595]; P < 0.001, Fig. 2d).

For the percentage of mice in inactivity bouts analysis, no between-group differences were detected in either circadian phase (CT0–12: Control group 55.0 ± 29.6%, RMZ bolus group 58.4 ± 29.1%, RMZ continuous group 56.4 ± 30.4%; CT12–24: Control group 55.1 ± 35.5%, RMZ bolus group 62.7 ± 30.9%, RMZ continuous group 61.3 ± 17.5%; Fig. 2e; CT0–12 P = 0.995, CT12–24 P = 0.968). No significant differences were observed in comparisons across each circadian time bin.

After continuous RMZ anesthesia, the expression levels of clock genes Per2 (83%, P < 0.001), Bmal1 (78%, P < 0.001), Cry1 (85%, P = 0.032), and Npas2 (82%, P = 0.018) decreased (Fig. 3). However, the expression level of the clock gene Clock did not decrease (101%, P = 0.91). There was no significant difference in clock gene expression levels based on the time of day of anesthesia. Continuous RMZ anesthesia significantly upregulated the expression of Egr1 (170%, P < 0.001; Fig. 3), while c-Fos expression remained largely unchanged (P = 0.12). Expression of neuronal activity marker genes did not significantly vary by time of day of anesthesia.

Discussion

Our results showed that RMZ does not shift the rest-activity circadian rhythm phase of mice, regardless of the duration or time of day of anesthesia. RMZ suppressed the expression of four of the five clock genes expression levels (Per2 83%, Bmal1 78%, Clock 101%, Cry1 85%, and Npas2 82%) in the SCN, but no fluctuation was observed depending on the time of day of anesthesia (Fig. 3). In our previous report, the volatile anesthetic desflurane altered the expression of clock genes in the SCN in a time-dependent manner, and the largest circadian rhythm phase shifts corresponded to the time points with the greatest changes in gene expression (Per2 113%, Bmal1 187%, Clock 36%, and Cry1 218%)5. The minimal alteration in clock gene expression after RMZ anesthesia may be associated with the absence of circadian rhythm phase shifts. In contrast, both muscimol, a GABAA receptor agonist, and brotizolam, a BDZ, suppress Per1 and Per2 expression in the SCN and induce phase advances14. Per is one of the key genes forming the TTFL, which serves as the oscillator for the 24-hour circadian rhythm. BMAL with CLOCK functions as a transcriptional activator of Per and Cry. PER and CRY function as negative feedback regulators by inhibiting the transcriptional activity of the BMAL1–CLOCK complex. Npas2 functions as an alternative to Clock19. It is challenging to fully interpret the overall TTFL alterations based solely on our findings.

The increased Egr1 expression after RMZ anesthesia suggests that RMZ predominantly had an excitatory effect on SCN neurons. Although GABA is generally considered an inhibitory neurotransmitter, generating inhibitory postsynaptic currents and suppressing neuronal firing20, many studies have shown that it can also exert excitatory effects, also in the SCN21. The ratio of GABA-excitatory to GABA-inhibitory neurons in the SCN differs between dorsal and ventral regions22 and varies with light-dark conditions and subjective circadian time23,24. GABA acts as an excitatory neurotransmitter in many neurons of neonatal mice25,26. Thus, the SCN slices used in our bioluminescence assays may exhibit subtle differences in response depending on the postnatal days.

The SCN receives two major inputs that induce circadian rhythm phase shifts: the photic pathway, activated by light stimulation, and the non-photic pathway. BDZs such as triazolam and diazepam induce phase shifts in the SCN by stimulating the non-photic pathway through increased locomotor activity driven by anxiolytic effects12–15,27–29. Notably, low RMZ doses exhibit anxiolytic effects like other BDZs27. However, our results showed that RMZ did not increase but decrease locomotor activity after anesthesia (Fig. 2c). Mice have high CES1 levels in their blood, and RMZ is rapidly metabolized30. Thus, the duration of low blood concentration that elicits an anxiolytic effect was likely brief. Therefore, RMZ may not have induced phase shifts. Additionally, the activity distribution differed among groups during CT12–24 due to reduced total activity in the RMZ continuous group. In contrast, the percentage of mice in inactivity bouts showed no significant group differences in either CT0–12 or CT12–24, and no consistent bin-wise effects were detected. These findings do not determine whether RMZ influences circadian rhythm architecture, including phase and amplitude across circadian time. Moreover, no concentration of RMZ altered the circadian period of clock gene expression in the cultured SCN (Fig. 4a-b). These results suggest that high concentrations of RMZ, sufficient to lose the righting reflex, do not affect the circadian rhythm phase or period of the SCN. The direct effects of BDZs on the SCN have not been previously investigated10,11. Since GABA receptor positive allosteric modulators differ from GABA agonists in their effects on synaptic GABAergic neurotransmission31, further studies are required to clarify their distinct influences on the SCN and circadian rhythms.

The recovery time of the righting reflex after continuous RMZ anesthesia was prolonged when anesthesia was maintained at ZT4–7 (Fig. 1c), corresponding to the middle of the light phase (ZT0–12), and the rest phase for nocturnal mice. The duration of LORR after sevoflurane and isoflurane anesthesia is also known to be prolonged during the rest phase, involving mechanisms of the noradrenergic system in the locus coeruleus32. The locus coeruleus, a wakefulness-regulating region, has been shown to express BDZ receptors33, and the time-of-day dependency in RMZ anesthesia may be attributed to mechanisms involving this structure.

Clinically, these findings suggest that RMZ anesthesia could help mitigate postoperative circadian rhythm disturbances, including sleep disorders and cognitive dysfunction16–18. Unlike many general anesthetics4, our findings indicate that RMZ had little effect on mammalian circadian rhythms. Although BDZs have previously been implicated in postoperative delirium34, several clinical studies indicate that RMZ does not increase this16–18. Our results support these findings and provide fundamental data on the effects of BDZs on the central circadian clock.

This study presents some limitations. First, since a standardized RMZ anesthesia protocol for mice has not been established, we evaluated its effectiveness by measuring the duration of the LORR, vital signs, and blood concentrations. However, intraperitoneal injection itself induces mild nociceptive pain, which might have influenced the circadian rhythm. Additionally, anesthesia duration was limited to three hours, based on the dissolved RMZ concentration and the standard liquid administration volume for mice (approximately 1 mL), which is shorter than that employed in previous studies using volatile anesthetics5. Second, as this study used nocturnal mice, it remains uncertain whether the same results would be obtained in diurnal humans. Third, RMZ is rapidly metabolized in mice. The administered dose (mg/kg) was higher than that used in humans35. Although RMZ blood concentrations were comparable to those observed during human anesthesia, primary metabolites were present at high concentrations (see Supplementary Fig. S3 online). Although these metabolites are considered pharmacologically inactive36, potential off-target effects at unusually high concentrations cannot be completely excluded. Finally, circadian rhythm amplitude in cultured SCN slices was not analyzed. Amplitude varied considerably between slices, and the procedure of opening the dish lid to apply RMZ could introduce further random variability. Since this study was primarily designed to evaluate the effects of RMZ on circadian phase and period, a reliable assessment of amplitude was not feasible. Future studies using optimized experimental designs will be necessary to clarify whether RMZ influences SCN rhythm amplitude.

In conclusion, RMZ did not shift the phase of the behavioral circadian rhythm or alter the circadian period of clock gene expression in cultured SCN under our experimental conditions in mice. However, the relative expression levels of several clock genes in the SCN of mice were suppressed following RMZ anesthesia.

Methods

Ethics declaration

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Hamamatsu University School of Medicine (Approval Number: 22-005-01). All experiments were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.

Animals and environments

Experiments 1–4 were conducted using male C57BL/6J mice (8–12 weeks old, 20.0–24.0 g; SLC, Hamamatsu, Japan). Mice were housed individually in cages with minimal noise and light exposure under controlled environmental conditions (temperature, 23.0 ± 3.0℃; humidity, 50.0 ± 20%). Food and water were available ad libitum. Light–dark (LD) conditions were defined using the chronobiological term ZT, with ZT 0 as the start of the light phase and ZT12 as the start of the dark phase (light: dark = 12: 12). Constant dark (DD) condition was maintained as a continuous dark environment with no light phase (light: dark = 0: 24). Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation after completion of experimental data collection or at the time of tissue sampling.

Anesthesia

The anesthetic agent used in this study was RMZ (Mundipharma, Cambridge, UK), dissolved in normal saline solution (NSS) at a concentration of 10.0 mg/mL. For bolus RMZ anesthesia, a single dose of RMZ (2.0 mg/0.2 mL) was intraperitoneally administered using a 25-G metal needle. For continuous RMZ anesthesia, the same initial dose was administered, followed by a 180-minute continuous intraperitoneal infusion of RMZ (3.0 mg/0.3 mL per hour) using a 24-G plastic catheter connected to a syringe pump. Anesthesia was performed on a hot plate set at 38.0℃ to maintain body temperature under dim red light.

Under isoflurane (2%) anesthesia, an incision was made in the common carotid artery (n = 5). pH, BE, PaO2, PaCO2, and lactate were measured using a blood gas analyzer (ABL 90 FLEX PLUS, Radiometer, Copenhagen, Denmark). Arterial blood gas test values were compared with those obtained from control mice (n = 5) that received an equivalent intraperitoneal injection of NSS.

Plasma RMZ concentration: A total of 25 mice received continuous RMZ anesthesia. Blood samples were collected at 10, 30, 60, 180, and 360 min after the start of anesthesia with 2% isoflurane (n = 5 for each time). Collected blood samples were inserted into FC-Mix tubes (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) containing sodium fluoride, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid-2Na, and heparin sodium to inactivate carboxylesterase (see Supplementary Notes for more details). Blood samples were immediately centrifuged to collect plasma, and plasma was stored at − 30 °C until sample pretreatment. D4-RMZ (50 µL) was added to each plasma sample as an internal standard, and RMZ concentration was measured by internal standard calibration. Plasma RMZ concentration was measured by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry with a lower limit of detection of 0.4 ng/mL and a quantification range of 0.45 to 4500 ng/mL. Detailed explanations of sample pretreatment, as well as chromatographic and mass spectrometric conditions, have been previously reported37,38.

Experiment 2. Rest-activity circadian rhythm phase shift

Measurement of rest-activity circadian rhythm: For sample size calculation to detect the difference of rest-activity circadian rhythm phase shift, a power analysis was performed with effect size = 0.4, α = 0.05, and 1-β = 0.8 using G*Power (version 3.19). C57BL/6J mice with a circadian period of 23.0 to 25.0 h under DD conditions were used (n = 116). Each mouse was individually housed in its own cage equipped with a running wheel containing a magnet and a hall sensor to measure and record the count of wheel rotations. Following a minimum 10-day acclimation period under LD conditions, DD conditions were initiated. On the fifth day of DD exposure, mice were divided into three groups: the RMZ bolus group mice (n = 38), treated with bolus RMZ anesthesia; the RMZ continuous group mice (n = 39), treated with continuous RMZ anesthesia; and the Control group mice (n = 39), which were only injected with 1 mL of NSS intraperitoneally. Mice in each group were further subdivided based on the ZT at which anesthesia or control injection occurred: ZT0, ZT4, ZT8, ZT12, ZT16, and ZT20. After the anesthesia, each mouse was returned to its own cage, and wheel rotation counts were continuously recorded for more than six days until the experimental endpoint (Fig. 5a).

Analysis of rest-activity circadian rhythm: Based on recorded wheel rotation data, double-plotted actograms were generated using custom software. In these actograms, 10-minute interval data from two consecutive days were aligned side by side along the same horizontal axis to duplicate the timeline. Phase shift was calculated by measuring the difference between the predicted onset of the active phase before anesthesia and the actual onset of the active phase following anesthesia. (Fig. 5a-b) Additionally, the circadian period was measured under both LD and DD conditions before and after anesthesia. The total of wheel rotations during the 24 h before anesthesia and the subsequent 0–24 h, 24–48 h, and 48–72 h after anesthesia were compared to evaluate the effect of anesthesia on overall activity levels. Moreover, the subjective time of anesthesia was calculated using CT (CT0: start of active phase, CT12: start of rest phase). To assess the relationship between circadian timing and anesthesia effects, each group of mice was further subdivided into six CT-based subgroups: CT0–4, CT4–8, CT8–12, CT12–16, CT16–20, and CT20–24. Phase shift, circadian period, and wheel rotations were evaluated for each subgroup to determine time-of-day-dependent effects. Additionally, we quantified activity distribution and inactivity bout structure over the five days after anesthesia. Activity distribution was evaluated by binning wheel-running counts in 10-minute intervals and aligning data by CT. Inactivity bout was defined as a continuous period without wheel-running activity. For analysis, we calculated the percentage of mice in inactivity bouts per 30-minute circadian time bin across all animals in each group. These parameters were compared among the Control, RMZ bolus, and RMZ continuous groups.

Experiment 3. Clock genes and immediate-early genes

SCN sample collection: SCN samples were collected from a total of 48 mice. Following an acclimatization period of at least 10 days under LD conditions, DD conditions were initiated. On the first day of DD exposure, 24 mice were administered continuous RMZ anesthesia, and the other 24 mice were administered NSS intraperitoneally. Mice were further assigned to one of six different time points for anesthesia administration: ZT0, ZT4, ZT8, ZT12, ZT16, and ZT20. Immediately after anesthesia, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the brains were extracted into RNAlater (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). A 1.5 mm-thick coronal brain slice containing the SCN was rapidly prepared using a Brain Matrix (Muromachi Kikai, Tokyo, Japan). SCN tissue was then extracted as cubic sections under a microscope.

Experiment 4. Circadian period of SCN

SCN culture and measurement of bioluminescence: PER2::LUC mice of both sexes, aged 2–5 days, were used in this experiment (n = 40). PER2::LUC mice are C57BL/6J mice with a luciferase gene knocked in near the Period2 clock gene, allowing cellular bioluminescence to fluctuate in accordance with the endogenous expression cycle of Period2 in the presence of luciferin. Immediately after decapitation, the brain was rapidly extracted into cold Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (Sigma Aldrich, Gillingham, UK). The 300 μm-thick coronal brain slices containing SCN were prepared using a micro slicer (DTK-1000 N, DOSAKA EM, Japan). Bilateral SCN regions were dissected from the coronal brain slices under a microscope and sectioned into square tissue blocks approximately 1 mm per side. Each SCN section was placed on culture membranes (Milicell-CM, Millipore Corporations, Billerica, Massachusetts, USA) in 35 mm dishes containing 1.2 mL of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) with 0.2 mM D-luciferin K salt (FUJIFILM Wako, Tokyo, Japan) and a supplement solution (N21-MAX Media Supplement, R&D Systems Inc, Minnesota, USA). Bioluminescence intensity from the SCN was measured for 1 min at 10-minute intervals using a luminometer (Kronos Dio, Atto, Tokyo, Japan). The temperature was set at 37℃ with 5.0% CO2.

Drug treatment: After over 7 days from the start of bioluminescence measurement, 100 µl of the drug solution was gently added to each dish. The drug solutions included either RMZ alone or a combination of RMZ and FMZ, both dissolved in NSS. RMZ solution was prepared at concentrations of 0.0, 1.0, 10.0, and 100.0 µg/mL in the culture medium. The RMZ–FMZ mixture was prepared by supplementing each RMZ concentration with FMZ at a final concentration of 30.3 µg/mL (100.0 µM) in the medium. Bioluminescence measurements were continued for more than seven days following drug administration.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and mean ± standard deviation (SD), as appropriate. To determine statistical differences in LORR, RORR, physiological change, results of blood gas analysis, plasma RMZ concentration, rest-activity rhythm phase shift, circadian period, gene expression levels, and gene expression period, the Mann–Whitney U test (two groups), Kruskal–Wallis test (≥ 3 groups), with Steel–Dwass post-hoc comparisons applied only when the test was significant. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA (version 15.0), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.
