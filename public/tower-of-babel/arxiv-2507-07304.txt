Scalable ADER-DG Transport Method with Polynomial Order Independent CFL Limit
Kieran Ricardo, Kenneth Duru

Abstract
Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods are known to suffer from increasingly restrictive time step constraints as the polynomial order increases, limiting their efficiency at high orders. In this paper, we introduce a novel locally implicit , but globally explicit ADER-DG scheme designed for transport-dominated problems. The method achieves a maximum stable time step governed by an element-width based CFL condition that is independent of the polynomial degree. By solving a set of element-local implicit problems at each time step, our approach more effectively captures the domain of dependence. As a result, our method remains stable for CFL numbers up to 1 / d 1/\sqrt{d} in d d spatial dimensions. We provide a rigorous stability proof in one dimension, and extend the analysis to two and three dimensions using a semi-analytical von Neumann stability analysis. The accuracy and convergence of the method are demonstrated through numerical experiments on both linear and nonlinear test cases.

Introduction

Transport models are a fundamental component of many fluid dynamics solvers. In geophysical fluid dynamics, models may include dozens of advected or transported quantities, motivating the need for accurate, efficient, and scalable transport algorithms. Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods have recently gained popularity for modelling geophysical flows due to their scalability, high-order accuracy, and strong conservation properties
Kelly and Giraldo 2012
;
Marras et al. 2016
;
Dennis et al. 2005
;
Waruszewski et al. 2022
;
Ricardo et al. 2024
. However, a well-known limitation of high-order DG methods—common to many finite element schemes—is that the maximum stable explicit time step decreases quadratically with the polynomial degree. This constraint often renders high-order methods impractical for large-scale or real-time simulations.

One approach to circumvent this time-step restriction is to use fully implicit time integration, but such methods typically involve increased communication overhead per time step, which can limit scalability on modern high-performance computing systems. Our focus is on the development of communication-avoiding schemes that remain efficient at scale. In this work, we introduce a novel
locally implicit
, but
globally explicit
ADER-DG method that removes the dependence of the maximum stable time step on polynomial order while minimizing inter-element communication.

The DG method was originally developed for neutron transport problems
Reed and Hill 1973
and later extended to a broader class of hyperbolic conservation laws and non-conservative hyperbolic partial differential equations
Cockburn and Shu 1989
;
Cockburn et al. 1990
;
Cockburn and Shu 1991
;
Cockburn and Shu 1998
;
Cockburn et al. 1989
. A widely used strategy for solving time-dependent hyperbolic problems with DG is the method of lines, in which DG is used for spatial discretization and a strong stability preserving Runge–Kutta (SSP-RK) method is applied for time integration
Shu and Osher 1988
.

Typically, a DG method with polynomial degree
k-1
—achieving order
k
accuracy in space—is combined with an SSP-RK scheme of order
k
to obtain overall
k
th-order accuracy in both space and time. However, a fundamental limitation arises from the fact that
k
-stage,
k
th-order explicit Runge–Kutta methods do not exist for
k>4

Butcher 1964
. This limits the efficiency of the method-of-lines approach for DG methods with polynomial degree
>3
.

The ADER-DG, or locally implicit DG, method overcomes this limitation by using a DG discretisation in both space and time
Toro et al. 2001
;
Toro and Titarev 2002
;
Titarev and Toro 2002
;
Titarev and Toro 2005
;
Toro and Titarev 2006
;
Dumbser et al. 2008a
;
Dumbser et al. 2008b
;
Gassner et al. 2011
. Regardless of the polynomial order or the problem’s dimensionality, the ADER-DG method requires only one round of communication across element interfaces. Due to its arbitrary order of accuracy and low communication cost, the ADER-DG method has been successfully applied to a wide range of problems
Fambri et al. 2017
;
Gaburro et al. 2023
;
Pelties et al. 2014
;
Breuer et al. 2016
;
Breuer and Heinecke 2022
;
Rannabauer et al. 2018
;
Popov 2025
.

For all explicit schemes applied to hyperbolic problems, the maximum stable time step is constrained by the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition
Courant et al. 1928
. This condition ensures that the numerical domain of dependence includes the physical domain of dependence, thereby maintaining stability. Consider, for example, a one-dimensional Runge-Kutta DG method with
n
stages. In such a scheme, neighbouring elements exchange information via numerical fluxes
n
times per time step. For a given element size
\Delta x
, information can therefore propagate a distance of
n\Delta x
. To ensure stability, the distance travelled by the fastest waves in the system,
c\Delta t
, must not exceed this propagation distance. This requirement can be written as:

\nu:=\frac{c\Delta t}{n\Delta x}\leq 1,

where
\Delta t
is the time step,
c
is the fastest wave speed, and
\nu
is the CFL number.

In practice, both explicit RK-DG and ADER-DG methods have maximum stable time steps that are well below the CFL limit

;
Guthrey and Rossmanith 2019

A recent approach to mitigating this limitation is the regionally implicit DG method
Guthrey and Rossmanith 2019
. This method achieves a CFL number of 1, independent of the polynomial order, while requiring only two communication steps per time step. At each time step, and for each element, an implicit problem is solved over a small region consisting of all neighbouring elements—specifically,
3^{d}
elements in spatial dimension
d
—to compute a local predictor. The standard ADER-DG corrector step is then applied. Although this approach significantly increases the maximum stable time step compared to the ADER-DG method and maintains low communication overhead, the resulting implicit problem may be relatively large and computationally expensive to solve.

In this paper, we propose an alternative locally implicit ADER-DG method for transport problems, designed to improve efficiency on high-performance computing systems. Unlike the regionally implicit approach, our method solves multiple single-element implicit problems per time step. In
d
spatial dimensions, this involves solving
2^{d}
single-element implicit problems per element and requires
d
rounds of communication between face-sharing neighbors. Although the method results in a lower maximum stable CFL number —
1/{\sqrt{d}}
in
d
spatial dimensions — compared to the regionally implicit DG method, each implicit problem is element local and can be solved efficiently using standard iterative ADER-DG techniques. This localized structure may lead to better overall efficiency for certain applications on modern high-performance computing architectures.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 introduces the notation and the standard ADER-DG method. Section 3 presents our novel locally implicit DG method. In Section 4, we prove our 1D method is numerical stable for element-size CFL numbers
\leq 1
. Section 5 conducts a numerical von Neumann analysis to determine the maximum stable time step of our method in 1D, 2D, and 3D. Finally, Section 6 presents the results of our numerical experiments, where we verify the convergence of our method.

Space-time discontinuous Galerkin method

In this section, we introduce the fully implicit space-time discontinuous Galerkin method
Dumbser and Facchini 2016
and the standard locally implicit ADER-DG method
Dumbser et al. 2008a
;
Dumbser et al. 2008b
;
Gassner et al. 2011
for the linear advection equation in a periodic domain
\Omega

\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla q=0,

(1)

with the smooth and periodic initial condition
q(x,0)=f(x)
.
Here,
q
represents the advected quantity and

\bm{u}
is the advection velocity.

2.1
Implicit space-time discontinuous Galerkin method

The computational domain is discretised into spatial elements
\Omega_{k}
, which are then extended into space-time elements
\Omega_{k}\crossproduct T^{n}
, where
T^{n}=[t^{n-1},t^{n}]
denotes the
nth
time interval. Within each space-time element, the solution

q_{k}^{n}(\bm{x},t)
is approximated by a finite expansion in terms of basis functions:

q_{k}^{n}=\sum_{i}{\left(q_{k}^{n}\right)_{i}\phi_{i}(\bm{x},t)},

(2)

where
\phi_{i}(\bm{x},t)
are typically chosen as tensor-product polynomial basis functions in space and time. Note we drop the subscript
k
when it is clear from context.

The implicit space-time DG method
Dumbser and Facchini 2016
follows the standard discontinuous Galerkin framework, extended to the space-time setting. The governing partial differential equation is multiplied by each basis function
\phi_{i}
, discretely integrated over the space-time element
\Omega_{k}\crossproduct T^{n}
, and coupled to neighbouring elements via numerical fluxes. This leads to the weak form:

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial q^{n}}{\partial t}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\crossproduct T^{n}}+\left\langle\phi,\bm{u}\cdot\nabla q^{n}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\crossproduct T^{n}}+\left\langle\phi,\left(\hat{q}^{n}-q^{n}\right)\bm{u}\cdot\bm{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{k}\crossproduct T^{n}}-\left\langle\phi,\left(q^{n}-q^{n-1}\right)\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}}|_{t=t^{n-1}}=0,

(3)

where
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
denotes discrete integrals, and
\hat{q}^{n}
denotes the upwind value of
q^{n}
at the element interface. Note that upwind numerical fluxes are employed both at spatial interfaces and at the temporal boundary
t=t^{n-1}
, ensuring the discretisation is stable and consistent for arbitrarily large time-steps.

2.2
Standard ADER discontinous Galerkin method

The key idea of the ADER-DG method
Dumbser et al. 2008a
;
Dumbser et al. 2008b
;
Gassner et al. 2011
is to solve a locally implicit problem in each element to obtain a predictor
\tilde{q}
, and then use this predictor to calculate the spatial numerical fluxes and all other spatial terms in (
3
).

The element local predictor
\tilde{q}
is given by

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial\tilde{q}}{\partial t}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\times T^{n}}+\left\langle\phi,\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\tilde{q}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\times T^{n}}-\left\langle\phi,\left(q^{n-1}-\tilde{q}\right)\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}=0.

(4)

This is then used to calculate all spatial terms in (
3
), giving
q^{n}
as

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial q^{n}}{\partial t}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\crossproduct T^{n}}+\left\langle\phi,\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\tilde{q}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\crossproduct T^{n}}+\left\langle\phi,\left(\hat{\tilde{q}}-\tilde{q}\right)\bm{u}\cdot\bm{n}\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{k}\crossproduct T^{n}}-\left\langle\phi,\left(q^{n}-q^{n-1}\right)\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}}|_{t=t^{n}}=0.

(5)

The element local implicit problem
(
4
)
is solved iteratively by

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial\tilde{q}^{i+1}}{\partial t}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\times T^{n}}+\left\langle\phi,\tilde{q}^{i+1}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}=-\left\langle\phi,\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\tilde{q}^{i}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\times T}+\left\langle\phi,q^{n-1}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}},

(6)

where the superscript
i
denotes the i-th iteration. This iterative procedure also converges for non-linear problems. We use this same iterative approach for all locally implicit problems in our method.

3
Novel locally implicit discontinuous Galerkin

In this section, we introduce our locally implicit ADER-DG method for 1D, 2D, and 3D problems. Compared to the standard locally implicit DG method, our scheme allows for larger time steps, independent of the polynomial order, reducing the number of communication steps required for higher-order methods.

3.1
Notation

In two spatial dimensions, we use quadrilateral elements; in three dimensions, we use hexahedral elements. For both space and time, we employ tensor-product Gauss–Lobatto–Legendre (GLL) quadrature, along with tensor-product Lagrange polynomial basis functions collocated with the quadrature points.

We define the reference spatial element as
\tilde{\Omega}=[-1,1]^{d}
in
d
dimensions, with reference coordinates denoted by
\bm{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{d})
. Each physical element
\Omega_{k}
is the image of an invertible, element-specific mapping
\bm{r}_{k}(\bm{\xi}):\tilde{\Omega}\to\Omega_{k}
from the reference element to the physical domain.

In dimensions
d=2
and
d=3
, we must explicitly identify the portions of the element boundary over which integrals are evaluated. For this purpose, we define the element face corresponding to the boundary where
\xi_{i}=\pm 1
as:

\partial\Omega_{k}^{\xi_{i}}=\{\bm{r}_{k}(\bm{\xi}):\xi_{i}=\pm 1\}.

To include time, we define the space-time reference element as
\tilde{\Omega}\times[-1,1]
, where
\tau
denotes the reference time coordinate.

3.2
1D

In 1D, our method employs the same predictor as the standard locally implicit DG method (
4
)

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial\tilde{q}}{\partial t}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\tilde{q}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\times T^{n}}-\left\langle\phi,\left(q^{n-1}-\tilde{q}\right)\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}=0.

(7)

During the corrector phase, the predictor is used solely to compute the numerical fluxes, while all other terms are treated implicitly

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial q^{n}}{\partial t}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla q^{n}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\times T^{n}}-\left\langle\phi,\left(q^{n-1}-q^{n}\right)\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}=-\left\langle\phi,\bm{u}\cdot\bm{n}\left(\hat{\tilde{q}}-q^{n}\right)\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{k}\times T^{n}}.

(8)

This approach differs from the standard ADER-DG corrector, which uses the predictor in both the volume and flux terms. Intuitively, for time steps below the element CFL limit, the fluxes at the interface of two elements should depend only on the two elements themselves, since information from the wider domain has not yet propagated to the interface. This allows us to construct accurate and stable fluxes for an element CFL of up to 1.

3.3
2D

A straightforward extension of our 1D method to 2D is numerically unstable. Intuitively, this instability arises because the fluxes at the

x
-interface now depend on neighbouring elements in the
y
-direction. To address this issue, we adopt a two-step procedure to obtain numerical fluxes for the corrector: first, we apply our 1D method in the
x
-direction to obtain fluxes for
y
interfaces; second, we apply the same method in the
y
-direction to obtain fluxes at the
x
interfaces. These
x
-flux and
y
-flux predictors are independent and depend only on the initial predictor, therefore our 2D method has 2 communication stages and 4 element local implicit solves. Figure
1
summarises the overall 2D algorithm.

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial\tilde{q}^{0}}{\partial t}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\tilde{q}^{0}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\times T^{n}}-\left\langle\phi,\left(q^{n-1}-\tilde{q}^{0}\right)\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}=0.

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial\tilde{q}^{1}}{\partial t}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\tilde{q}^{1}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\times T^{n}}-\left\langle\phi,\left(q^{n-1}-\tilde{q}^{1}\right)\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}=-\left\langle\phi,\bm{u}\cdot\bm{n}\left(\hat{\tilde{q}}^{0}-\tilde{q}^{1}\right)\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{k}^{\xi_{1}}\times T^{n}},

Finally, the corrector step is computed as

\begin{split}\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial q^{n}}{\partial t}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla q^{n}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\times T^{n}}-\left\langle\phi,\left(q^{n-1}-q^{n}\right)\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}=&-\left\langle\phi_{h},\bm{u}\cdot\bm{n}\left(\hat{\tilde{q}}^{2}-q^{n}\right)\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{k}^{\xi_{1}}\times T}\\

(12)

3.4
3D

In 3D, the
x

y

z

y

z
-directions to obtain

y

The first four predictors follow the same form as the 2D predictors

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial\tilde{q}^{0}}{\partial t}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\tilde{q}^{0}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\times T^{n}}-\left\langle\phi,\left(q^{n-1}-\tilde{q}^{0}\right)\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}=0,

(13)

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial\tilde{q}^{i}}{\partial t}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\tilde{q}^{i}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\times T^{n}}-\left\langle\phi,\left(q^{n-1}-\tilde{q}^{i}\right)\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}=-\left\langle\phi,\bm{u}\cdot\bm{n}\left(\hat{\tilde{q}}^{0}-\tilde{q}^{i}\right)\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{k}^{\xi_{i}}\times T^{n}},\text{ for }i=1,2,3.

(14)

To obtain the final three predictors,
\tilde{q}^{i,j}

i,j=(1,2),(1,3),(2,3)

\begin{split}\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial\tilde{q}^{i,j}}{\partial t}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\tilde{q}^{i,j}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\times T^{n}}-\left\langle\phi,\left(q^{n-1}-\tilde{q}^{i,j}\right)\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}=&-\left\langle\phi,\bm{u}\cdot\bm{n}\left(\hat{\tilde{q}}^{j}-\tilde{q}^{i,j}\right)\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{k}^{\xi_{i}}\times T^{n}}\\
&-\left\langle\phi,\bm{u}\cdot\bm{n}\left(\hat{\tilde{q}}^{i}-\tilde{q}^{i,j}\right)\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{k}^{\xi_{j}}\times T^{n}}.\end{split}

With these predictors in place, we can construct stable estimates for all element interface fluxes and compute the final corrector as

\begin{split}\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial q^{n}_{k}}{\partial t}+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla q^{n}_{k}\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}\times T^{n}}-\left\langle\phi,\left(q^{n-1}-q^{n}_{k}\right)\right\rangle_{\Omega_{k}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}=&-\left\langle\phi,\bm{u}_{h}\cdot\bm{n}\left(\hat{\tilde{q}}^{2,3}-q^{n+1}_{h}\right)\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{k}^{\xi_{1}}\times T}\\
&-\left\langle\phi,\bm{u}\cdot\bm{n}\left(\hat{\tilde{q}}^{1,3}-q^{n+1}\right)\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{k}^{\xi_{2}}\times T}\\
&-\left\langle\phi_{h},\bm{u}\cdot\bm{n}\left(\hat{\tilde{q}}^{1,2}-q^{n+1}\right)\right\rangle_{\partial\Omega_{k}^{\xi_{3}}\times T^{n}}.\end{split}

(16)

4
1D Stability Analysis

In this section, we present theoretical results that demonstrate the stability of our 1D method for a given constant CFL number and polynomial order. Specifically, for order
p
we derive a
(p+1)\times(p+1)
eigenvalue problem and prove that a maximum eigenvalue of 1 implies stability. We evaluate stability for polynomial orders up to
p\leq 14
and across a range of CFL numbers, finding that our method remains stable for CFL values up to 1.

While these results are based on stability evaluations for specific CFL numbers, and thus only confirm stability for the tested values, we make the reasonable assumption that the maximum amplification factor increases with the CFL number. Based on this assumption, we conclude that our 1D method is stable for CFL values up to 1.0 for polynomial orders
\leq 14
. Although we suspect that higher-order methods are also stable, achieving conclusive results may require higher precision in the numerical linear algebra.

4.1
Notation

For the proofs we introduce additional notation. Let
x_{k}
denote the left boundary point of cell
k
, s.t.
\Omega_{k}=[x_{k},x_{k+1}]
. This allows us to distinguish between integrals along the left an right element boundaries. For example,
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}|_{x_{k}}
approximates the left time boundary integral
\int_{\tau=-1}^{\tau=1}{a(x=x_{k})b(x=x_{k})d\tau}
.

We also transform the 1D method into the reference element, and scale out a factor of
\Delta x
. Now the geometry dependence is entirely contained within the CFL number
\nu
. In reference coordinates our 1D method becomes

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial\tilde{q}_{k}}{\partial\tau}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}\times\tilde{T}}+\left\langle\phi,\nu\frac{\partial\tilde{q}_{k}}{\partial\xi}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}\times\tilde{T}}-\left\langle\phi,q^{n-1}_{k}-\tilde{q}_{k}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}=0,

(17)

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial q^{n}_{k}}{\partial\tau}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}\times\tilde{T}}+\left\langle\phi,\nu\frac{\partial q^{n}_{k}}{\partial\xi}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}\times\tilde{T}}-\left\langle\phi,q^{n-1}_{k}-q^{n}_{k}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|^{t^{n}}+\left\langle\phi,\nu\left(\hat{\tilde{q}}-q^{n}_{k}\right)\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|^{x_{k+1}}_{x_{k}}=0.

(18)

We also assume without loss of generality that
u\geq 0
. Consequently, an upqind value
\hat{\tilde{q}}|_{x_{k}}=\tilde{q}_{k-1}|_{x_{k}}
represents the value on the left side of the element boundary, therefore (
18

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial q^{n}_{k}}{\partial\tau}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}\times\tilde{T}}+\left\langle\phi,\nu\frac{\partial q^{n}_{k}}{\partial\xi}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}\times\tilde{T}}-\left\langle\phi,q^{n-1}_{k}-q^{n+1}_{k}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}+\left\langle\phi,\nu\left(\tilde{q}_{k}-q^{n}_{k}\right)\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k+1}}-\left\langle\phi,\nu\left(\tilde{q}_{k-1}-q^{n}_{k}\right)\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}=0.

(19)

4.2
Analysis

We begin by expressing the energy change after one step of the 1D method as a function of two key quantities: the difference between the corrector and predictor at time
t^{n}
, and the jump in the predictor across spatial element boundaries. This formulation enables the construction of a stability eigenvalue problem. This result is presented in Theorem
4.3
. To derive it, we first establish several preliminary lemmas.

We start by showing that the predictor recovers the solution from the previous time step at
t^{n-1}
. This result is formalised in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

At the beginning of the time step,
t^{n-1}
, the predictor
\tilde{q}_{k}
equals the previous solution
q^{n-1}
, that is
\tilde{q}_{k}(\xi,\tau=-1)=q^{n-1}_{k}(\xi,\tau=1)
.

Proof.

Let
\xi_{i}
be the GLL quadtrature points and
l_{i}
be the Lagrange polynomials which interpolate these points. The function

\tilde{q}_{k}=\sum_{i}{q^{n-1}_{k}\left(\xi=\xi_{i},\tau=1\right)l_{i}\left(\xi-\nu(\tau+1)\right)}

(20)

satisfies both

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial\tilde{q}_{k}}{\partial t}+\nu\frac{\partial\tilde{q}_{k}}{\partial x}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}\times\tilde{T}}=0

(21)

and

\tilde{q}_{k}(\xi,\tau=-1)=\sum_{i}{q^{n-1}_{k}\left(\xi=\xi_{i},\tau=1\right)l_{i}\left(\xi\right)}=q^{n-1}_{k}(\xi,\tau=1),

(22)

and therefore also satisfies

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial\tilde{q}_{k}}{\partial t}+\nu\frac{\partial\tilde{q}_{k}}{\partial x}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}\times\tilde{T}}-\left\langle\phi,q^{n-1}_{k}-\tilde{q}_{k}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}=0.

(23)

∎

Lemma 4.1 allows us to formulate the evolution of the difference between the predictor and the corrector,
\psi_{k}^{n}=q^{n}_{k}-\tilde{q}_{k}
, almost entirely in terms of
\psi_{k}^{n}
. Taking the difference between (
19
) and (
17
), and applying Lemma 4.1 yields

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial\psi_{k}^{n}}{\partial\tau}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}\times\tilde{T}}+\left\langle\phi,\nu\frac{\partial\psi_{k}^{n}}{\partial\xi}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}\times\tilde{T}}+\left\langle\phi,\psi_{k}^{n}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}-\left\langle\phi,\nu\psi_{k}^{n}\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k+1}}=\left\langle\phi,\nu\left(\tilde{q}_{k-1}-q^{n}_{k}\right)\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}.

(24)

This can also be written as

\left\langle\phi,\frac{\partial\psi_{k}^{n}}{\partial\tau}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}\times\tilde{T}}+\left\langle\phi,\nu\frac{\partial\psi_{k}^{n}}{\partial\xi}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}\times\tilde{T}}+\left\langle\phi,\psi_{k}^{n}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}-\left\langle\phi,\nu\psi_{k}^{n}\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|^{x_{k+1}}_{x_{k}}=\left\langle\phi,\nu\left(\tilde{q}_{k-1}-\tilde{q}_{k}\right)\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}.

(25)

Various boundary integrals of
\left(\psi_{k}^{n}\right)^{2}
arise in our analysis. The following lemma establishes a useful relationship between these boundary integrals.

Lemma 4.2
.

\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\left(\psi_{k}^{n}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k+1}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\psi_{k}^{n}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}=\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\psi_{k}^{n}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n}}-\left\langle\psi_{k}^{n},\nu\left(\tilde{q}_{k-1}-q^{n}_{k}\right)\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\left(\psi_{k}^{n}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}

(26)

Proof.

Substituting
\phi=\psi^{n}_{k}
in (
24
) and using SBP in both space and time yields the desired result.
∎

With these preliminary results, we are now ready to prove the following theorem, which states that the energy growth of the 1D method depends only on the difference between the corrector and the predictor,
\psi
, and the jumps in the predictor
\tilde{q}
. When combined with (
25
), this enables us to establish numerical stability for a given polynomial order and CFL number.

Theorem 4.3
.

The energy change after one step of the 1D method, (
17
)-(
18
), is

\Delta E^{n}=\sum_{k}{\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(q^{n}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(q^{n-1}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}}=\sum_{k}{\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\psi^{n}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\left(\tilde{q}_{k-1}-\tilde{q}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}}.

(27)

Proof.

Substituting
\phi=q^{n}_{k}
into (
18
) and using SBP in both space and time yields

\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(q^{n}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n}}=\left\langle\nu\left(\left(\tfrac{1}{2}q^{n}_{k}\right)^{2}-q^{n}_{k}\hat{\tilde{q}}\right)\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}^{x_{k+1}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(q^{n}_{k}\right)^{2}-q^{n}_{k}q^{n-1}_{k}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}},

(28)

therefore

\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(q^{n}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(q^{n-1}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}=\left\langle\nu\left(\left(\tfrac{1}{2}q^{n}_{k}\right)^{2}-q^{n}_{k}\hat{\tilde{q}}_{k}\right)\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}^{x_{k+1}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\left(q^{n}_{k}-q^{n-1}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}.

(29)

Noting that as
\hat{\tilde{q}}
is continuous across element boundaries, in a periodic domain
\sum_{k}{\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\hat{\tilde{q}}_{k}^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}^{x_{k+1}}}=0
, therefore

\Delta E^{n}=\Delta E^{n}+\sum_{k}{\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\hat{\tilde{q}}_{k}^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}^{x_{k+1}}}=\sum_{k}{\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\left(q^{n}_{k}-\hat{\tilde{q}}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}^{x_{k+1}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(q^{n}_{k}-q^{n-1}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}}.

(30)

We apply Lemma 4.1, substituting
q^{n-1}|_{t^{n-1}}=\tilde{q}|_{t^{n-1}}
, and also use
\hat{\tilde{q}}|_{x_{k}}=\tilde{q}_{k-1}|_{x_{k}}
to obtain

\Delta E^{n}=\sum_{k}{\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\left(q^{n}_{k}-\tilde{q}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k+1}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\left(q^{n}_{k}-\tilde{q}_{k-1}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(q^{n}_{k}-\tilde{q}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}}.

(31)

Substituting the definition of
\psi^{n}_{k}=q^{n}_{k}-\tilde{q}_{k}
gives

\Delta E^{n}=\sum_{k}{\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\left(\psi_{k}^{n}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k+1}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\left(q^{n}_{k}-\tilde{q}_{k-1}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\psi_{k}^{n}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}}.

(32)

We then apply Lemma 4.2, giving

\Delta E^{n}=\sum_{k}{\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\psi_{k}^{n}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n}}-\left\langle\nu\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\psi_{k}^{n}\right)^{2}+\psi\left(\tilde{q}_{k-1}-q^{n}_{k}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tilde{q}_{k-1}-q^{n}_{k}\right)^{2}\right)\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}},

(33)

expanding
\psi^{n}_{k}=q^{n}_{k}-\tilde{q}_{k}
in the second term yields

\Delta E^{n}=\sum_{k}{\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\psi_{k}^{n}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\left(\tilde{q}_{k}-\tilde{q}_{k-1}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}},

(34)

as required.
∎

This analysis can be easily be extended to non-periodic boundary conditions by treating the leftmost and rightmost element interfaces separately.

4.3
Stability eigenvalue problem

We now show how to use Theorem 1 to prove numerical stability for a given polynomial order and CFL number. To do this we express the nodal values of the difference between the corrector and the predictor at
t^{n}
,
\psi_{k}^{n}|_{t^{n}}
, as a linear combination of the nodal values of the jump in the predictor at the left element boundary
x_{k}
,
\left(\tilde{q}_{k-1}-\tilde{q}_{k}\right)|_{x_{k}}
. Let
\bm{\gamma}_{k}
and
\bm{\beta}_{k}
be vectors containing these nodal values, this allows us to write

\bm{\gamma}_{k}=\nu A\bm{\beta}_{k}.

(35)

See appendix
A
for an explicit construction of
\bm{\gamma}_{k}
,
\bm{\beta}_{k},
and
A
. Note that the matrix
A
only depends on the CFL number and the polynomial order of the method. The main result of this section is that
\norm{A}_{w}\leq 1
implies that our method is numerically stable, where
\norm{\boldsymbol{v}}_{w}=\sqrt{\sum_{i}{v_{i}^{2}w_{i}}}
is the
L^{2}
norm weighted with the 1D GLL quadrature weights
w_{i}
. We present this result in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.4
.

Under the assumption that
\norm{A}_{w}\leq 1
when
\nu\leq 1
, the 1D method (
17
)-(
18
) is numerically stable for all
\nu\leq 1
, that is

\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(q^{n}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(q^{n-1}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}\leq 0.

(36)

Proof.

Using theorem 4.1 we obtain

\begin{split}\sum_{k}{\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(q^{n+1}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(q^{n}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}}&=\sum_{k}{\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\psi^{n}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n}}-\left\langle\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\left(\tilde{q}_{k-1}-\tilde{q}_{k}\right)^{2}\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{x_{k}}}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{k}{\sum_{i}{\left(\left(\gamma_{k}\right)_{i}^{2}w_{i}-\nu\left(\beta_{k}\right)_{i}^{2}w_{i}\right)}}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{k}{\left(\norm{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_k}_{w}^{2}-\nu\norm{\boldsymbol{\beta}_k}_{w}^{2}\right)}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{k}{\left(\nu^{2}\norm{A\boldsymbol{\beta}_k}_{w}^{2}-\nu\norm{\boldsymbol{\beta}_k}_{w}^{2}\right)}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\sum_{k}{\left(\nu\norm{A\boldsymbol{\beta}_k}_{w}^{2}-\norm{\boldsymbol{\beta}_k}_{w}^{2}\right)}\\
&\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\nu\sum_{k}{\left(\nu\norm{\boldsymbol{\beta}_k}_{w}^{2}-\norm{\boldsymbol{\beta}_k}_{w}^{2}\right)}\\
&=\tfrac{\nu}{2}(\nu-1)\sum_{k}{\norm{\boldsymbol{\beta}_k}^{2}_{w}}\\
&\leq 0.\end{split}

(37)

∎

We have verified that
\norm{A}_{w}=1
for order
p\leq 14
and variety of CFL numbers
\nu
up to and including
\nu=1
. Interestingly, for
\nu>1\implies\norm{A}_{w}>1
.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a novel locally implicit DG method for transport problems that employs a series of element-local implicit problems, enabling a maximum stable time step that is independent of the polynomial order. This approach significantly increases the maximum stable time step compared to the standard ADER-DG method, thereby reducing the number of communication steps required for simulations and enhancing scalability. We rigorously proved stability in 1D for
\nu\leq 1
, and in 2D and 3D, we derived the maximum stable time step through a semi-analytical von Neumann analysis. The method was validated through a series of linear and nonlinear tests, demonstrating convergence at quasi-optimal order.

Future work will focus on integrating this transport method into numerical solvers for the Euler equations, as well as extending this method to more general hyperbolic problems.

Appendix A
Explicit construction of matrix A

In this section we explicitly construct the matrix
A
, which relates the nodal values of the difference between the corrector and the predictor at
t^{n}
,
\bm{\gamma}_{k}
, as a linear combination of the nodal values of jump in the predictor at the left element boundary
x_{k}
,
\bm{\beta}_{k}
. We define these vectors componentwise as

\left(\gamma_{k}\right)_{i}=\psi^{n}_{k}\left(\xi=\xi_{i},\tau=1\right),

(41)

\left(\beta_{k}\right)_{i}=\tilde{q}_{k-1}\left(\xi=1,\tau=\tau_{i}\right)-\tilde{q}_{k}\left(\xi=-1,\tau=\tau_{i}\right),

(42)

where
\xi_{i}
and
\tau_{i}
are the 1D GLL quadrature points in space and time.

To construct A we first re-write (
25
) in matrix form. Let
M
be the
(p+1)^{2}\times(p+1)^{2}
matrix such

M_{ij}=\left\langle\phi_{i},\frac{\partial\phi_{j}}{\partial\tau}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}\times\tilde{T}}+\left\langle\phi_{i},\nu\frac{\partial\phi_{j}}{\partial\xi}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}\times\tilde{T}}+\left\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\Omega}}\bigg|_{t^{n-1}}-\left\langle\phi_{i},\nu\phi_{j}\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|^{x_{k+1}}_{x_{k}},

(43)

and
L
be the
(p+1)^{2}\times(p+1)
matrix

L_{ij}=\left\langle\phi_{i},l_{j}(\tau)\right\rangle_{\tilde{T}}\bigg|_{\xi=-1},

(44)

where
l_{j}
is the Lagrange polynomial interpolating the
jth
GLL quadrature point. Let
\bm{\psi}_{k}
be the vector containing the nodal values of
\psi_{k}
, therefore (
25
) can be written

M\bm{\psi}_{k}=L\bm{\beta}_{k}.

(45)

The vector
\bm{\gamma}_{k}
contains the nodal values of
\psi_{k}^{n}
at the
\tau=1
boundary, let
S
be the
(p+1)\times(p+1)^{2}
matrix which selects these values. Explicitly,

S_{ij}=\phi_{j}\left(\xi=\xi_{i},\tau=1\right).

(46)

Therefore
\bm{\gamma}_{k}=S\bm{\psi}_{k}
and

\bm{\gamma}_{k}=SM^{-1}L\bm{\beta}_{k},

(47)

and we construct
A
as

A=\frac{1}{\nu}SM^{-1}L.

(48)

We evaluated the
L^{2}
norm of
A
for a range of polynomial orders and CFL numbers, and found when
\nu\leq 1
that
\norm{A}_{2}=1
.
