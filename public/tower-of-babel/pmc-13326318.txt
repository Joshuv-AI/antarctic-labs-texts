Relationship between talar dome osteochondral lesions and medial malleolus fractures, and their diagnostic significance for occult medial malleolus fractures
Authors: Xinzhao Zhang, Haichen Xu, ChenHui Liu, Wanheng Lan, Changling Zheng, Cong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the potential correlation between talar dome osteochondral lesions (TDOCL) and medial malleolus fractures and assess the diagnostic significance of TDOCL identified on standard anteroposterior (AP) ankle radiographs for detecting occult medial malleolus fractures.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 452 patients with ankle fractures, including 13 cases of occult medial malleolus fractures, treated at Ningde Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine between January 2015 and August 2024. For detailed analysis, the talar dome on the AP radiographic view was vertically split into three equal sections. Any definite or suspected TDOCL identified on the AP view was recorded and classified based on location. Computed tomography (CT) imaging was used as the reference standard for diagnosing TDOCL. For every patient, data including sex, age, injured side, diagnostic findings, presence or absence of TDOCL, and lesion location were independently collected. The false-negative and false-positive rates of X-ray detection of TDOCL were calculated, and the association between TDOCLs and medial malleolus fractures was analyzed. Diagnostic performance measures, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and positive likelihood ratio (LR+), were calculated for medial TDOCL in predicting occult medial malleolus fracture.ResultsMedial TDOCL and female sex were identified as independent risk factors for medial malleolus fracture. For occult medial malleolus fracture, medial TDOCL detected on AP radiographs demonstrated a sensitivity of 38.5%, specificity of 100%, PPV of 100%, and NPV of 98.2%. LR+ could not be calculated due to 100% specificity.ConclusionsMedial TDOCL is strongly associated with medial malleolus fracture and may serve as a potential warning indicator for occult injury. Clinicians should integrate radiologic findings with physical examination and consider targeted CT imaging for confirmation. These findings are hypothesis-generating and require further validation in prospective studies.

Introduction

Ankle fractures are among the most common and clinically significant musculoskeletal injuries globally [1]. Medial malleolus fractures account for approximately 32.5% of all cases [2]. According to Ebraheim et al., noncomminuted medial malleolus fractures are typically caused by rotational injuries [3]. Furthermore, talar osteochondral lesions are strongly associated with rotational ankle injuries and commonly occur alongside ankle fractures [4]. These findings suggest a potential correlation between talar osteochondral lesions and the incidence of medial malleolus fractures.

Acute ankle injuries are most commonly evaluated using X-ray imaging. Although X-ray imaging is usually sufficient to identify most medial malleolus fractures, a small proportion of occult fractures may remain undetected. Inappropriate treatment may lead to problems, including fracture displacement, malunion, nonunion, or traumatic arthritis, if these fractures go undetected. This retrospective study aims to quantitatively evaluate the relationship between talar osteochondral lesions and medial malleolus fractures and assess the diagnostic value of talar osteochondral lesions visible on the AP view for identifying occult medial malleolus fractures.

Only patients who underwent X-ray and CT examinations were included, which may introduce selection bias toward more complex injuries. A patient flowchart was used to document enrollment, exclusions, and completion of imaging evaluations.

Diagnosis of talar dome osteochondral lesion (TDOCL)

Anteroposterior (AP) ankle radiographs were used for initial screening. The talar dome was vertically divided into three equal zones: Medial, central, and lateral. TDOCL was suspected on radiography if a radiolucent subchondral defect, osteochondral detachment, or cortical irregularity was visible in the talar dome [5]. CT was used as the reference standard to confirm TDOCL and characterize fracture morphology (Fig. 1). Two independent orthopedic surgeons, blinded to clinical data and the final diagnosis, reviewed all images.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (version 23.0). Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square test. Age was categorized into three groups for clinical relevance: Young (≤ 44 years), middle-aged (45–59 years), and elderly (≥ 60 years). Binary logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent risk factors for medial malleolus fracture. The following variables were included in the regression model: Sex, age group, injured side, and TDOCL location (medial, lateral, central, both medial and lateral). Collinearity diagnostics were performed to exclude multicollinearity among variables. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Diagnostic indices, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and positive likelihood ratio (LR+), were calculated for medial TDOCL as an indicator of occult medial malleolus fracture. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and P < 0.01 was considered highly significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The study comprised 452 patients with ankle fractures. Of these, 147 had additional ankle fractures, while 305 had fractures of the medial malleolus. The cohort comprised 239 men and 213 women. On the left side, there were 233 cases; on the right, 219. Among them were 168 young (≤ 44 years old), 166 middle-aged (45–59 years old), and 118 elderly (≥ 60 years old) patients. Based on the presence and location of TDOCL, 244 cases were classified as none, 34 as medial, 157 as lateral, 2 as central, and 15 as both medial and lateral (Table 1).

Sex and TDOCL were statistically correlated with the occurrence of medial malleolus fracture, according to single-factor analysis; TDOCL was highly significantly correlated with the occurrence of medial malleolus fracture (P < 0.01). The positive detection rate of X-ray imaging for medial TDOCL was 88.64% (39/44), with a false negative rate of 20.41% (10/49). For central TDOCL, the positive detection rate was 0% (0/2), while the false negative rate was 2/2 (100%). Diagnostic performance of medial TDOCL for occult medial malleolus fracture

Using CT as the reference standard, the diagnostic performance of medial TDOCL on AP radiography for predicting occult medial malleolus fracture was calculated as follows: Sensitivity 38.5% (5/13), specificity 100% (439/439), PPV 100% (5/5), and NPV 98.2% (439/447). The positive LR+ could not be calculated due to 100% specificity.

Discussion

Female sex was independently associated with medial malleolus fractures in this study. This finding is consistent with previous epidemiologic studies demonstrating higher rates of ankle fractures in women [6]. The increased fracture risk observed in women may be related to differences in injury mechanism, ligamentous laxity, bone quality, and anatomical characteristics of the ankle joint [6–9]. These results support the importance of increased clinical vigilance for medial malleolus fractures in female patients presenting with acute ankle injuries.

This study demonstrated a significant correlation between the development of medial malleolus fractures and medial TDOCL. Bruns et al. conducted a biomechanical study on cadaveric ankle joints. They demonstrated that ankle pronation with valgus alignment produces the peak pressure at the lateral border of the talus. Concurrently, supination with varus concentrates stress on the medial aspect of the ankle joint [10]. A review study further indicates that the occurrence of medial or lateral talar osteochondral lesions depends on the injury mechanism; medial lesions are commonly associated with axial loading combined with torsional forces [4]. Based on these findings, we propose a potential impact mechanism. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, an external force leads to impaction between the talus and medial malleolus. Subsequent axial loading results in a medial malleolus fracture, with secondary medial talar osteochondral lesions. However, this mechanism remains speculative in the absence of dedicated biomechanical experiments or prospective imaging evidence. Furthermore, some talus osteochondral lesions were observed that appeared to be chronic (Fig. 3). Because the talus plays an essential role in ankle stability, osteochondral damage may alter the cartilage and subchondral bone, thereby weakening joint stability. This joint instability over time may cause repeated impingement between the talus and the medial malleolus, result in stress concentration in the medial malleolus, and increase the risk of fracture under external force (Fig. 2B).

AP and mortise radiographs of the ankle joint can reveal osteochondral lesions of the talus [11, 12]. Clinically, patients presenting with ankle injuries do not routinely undergo mortise radiographs. In this study, the diagnostic sensitivity of standard AP radiography for TDOCL was 0.35, lower than the 0.59 reported by Verhagen et al. [12, 13]. This discrepancy may be explained by differences in study populations: Verhagen’s cohort consisted primarily of patients with chronic ankle pain, whereas our study included only patients with acute ankle fractures. In acute trauma settings, overlapping osseous structures on AP radiographs may reduce visibility of lateral and central TDOCL, whereas medial TDOCL remains more readily identifiable.

According to previous epidemiological studies, the talus is the most frequent site of osteochondral lesions following ankle fractures, accounting for 42.7% of cases, and the medial zone represents the most common sublocation, at 45.1% [4]. In the present study, the sensitivity of X-ray imaging for detecting medial TDOCL (0.80) was significantly higher than that for lateral TDOCL (0.23) and central TDOCL (0). Consistent with the findings of Raiki [14], we also observed that medial TDOCLs typically have a larger surface area and that the AP view can clearly display their size and form. Furthermore, compared to the lateral side, only a few cases on AP radiographs revealed the medial edge of the talar dome overlapping the medial malleolus, which was easier to observe in TDOCL. The occurrence of osteochondral lesions in the central region of the talar dome is extremely uncommon, and we assume that they are the consequence of a vertical impact between the central region of the talar dome and the distal tibia. Furthermore, injuries in the center of the talus can present as minor lesions in the early stage, such as cartilage surface damage or small subchondral fractures. On AP and CT views, these early lesions are sometimes difficult to measure.

Only 13 cases of occult medial malleolus fractures have been reported in the past decade; one patient had a poor clinical outcome due to a missed diagnosis; the only typical indicator on first X-ray images is medial TDOCL (Fig. 4). In patients with occult medial malleolus fracture, identification of medial TDOCL on initial AP radiographs should increase clinical suspicion for associated subtle fracture. Medial TDOCL on AP radiography demonstrated 100% specificity and 100% PPV for occult medial malleolus fracture, with a sensitivity of 38.5%. In clinical practice, this radiographic sign should be integrated with careful physical examination and localized tenderness. When clinical suspicion remains high, targeted CT is recommended to confirm the presence of an occult or minimally displaced fracture. Notably, CT may still miss occult fractures when the fracture line runs parallel to the imaging plane. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrates higher sensitivity for bone marrow edema and may be used as an adjunctive modality when clinical suspicion remains high despite a negative CT scan [15, 16]. The present results are exploratory and should not be regarded as altering standard clinical practice until confirmed by large-scale prospective studies.

This study has several limitations despite its important results. First, this is a retrospective single-center study, which may be subject to inherent selection and verification bias. Only patients undergoing X-ray and CT were included, which may introduce selection bias toward more complex or suspicious injuries and potentially overestimate the diagnostic performance of medial TDOCL for occult medial malleolus fracture. Second, the number of occult medial malleolus fractures was small (n = 13), limiting statistical power for diagnostic conclusions. Third, the proposed impingement mechanism was theoretical and lacked biomechanical or prospective imaging data. Fourth, CT was used as the reference standard, but some occult fractures may be measurable only with MRI. Finally, the study did not classify TDOCL based on the ankle fracture type, which may limit understanding of injury patterns.

Conclusions

Medial TDOCL is strongly associated with medial malleolus fracture and may serve as a potential warning indicator for occult injury. Clinicians should integrate radiologic signs with physical examination and consider targeted CT for confirmation. These findings are preliminary and hypothesis-generating and require further prospective validation.
