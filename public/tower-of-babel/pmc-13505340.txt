Beyond the Nerves: Thyroid Autoimmunity as a Non‐Neural Factor for Post‐Thyroidectomy Dysphonia
Authors: Chih‐Hao Liu, Tzu‐Tang Lin, Shih‐Wei Wang, Hsin‐Yi Tseng, Wing‐Hei Viola Yu, Yi‐Chu Lin, Jia Joanna Wang, Cheng‐Hsun Chuang, Leong‐Perng Chan, Tzu‐Yen Huang, Che‐Wei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study aims to investigate the impact of elevated preoperative thyroid autoantibodies, a proposed non‐neural contributor, on postoperative voice outcomes, specifically within a cohort of patients undergoing nerve‐preserved thyroidectomy verified by intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM).MethodsA retrospective analysis included 212 patients who underwent primary nerve‐preserved thyroidectomy with IONM‐confirmed integrity of recurrent and superior laryngeal nerves. Patients were categorized into elevated antibody (EA) and normal antibody (NA) groups according to thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb) and anti‐thyroid microsomal antibody (TMAb) levels. Objective and subjective voice analyzes (VHI, VRQoL, and IVST) were performed pre‐ and postoperatively.ResultsThe EA group had a significantly higher proportion of bilateral surgeries, larger tumors, and heavier specimens. Several postoperative voice parameters differed significantly between the two groups, and after adjustment for patient characteristics, EA levels remained independently associated with abnormal shimmer (adjusted OR 2.33, p = 0.021) and abnormal IVST‐T (adjusted OR 2.72, p = 0.010). In the antibody‐grade analysis, a preliminary non‐linear pattern between antibody burden and postoperative voice outcomes was observed in shimmer, IVST‐T, and IVST‐S.ConclusionIn this retrospective exploratory study, elevated thyroid autoantibodies were associated with poorer objective voice stability and greater subjective voice impairment following IONM‐verified nerve‐preserved thyroidectomy. With standardized IONM minimizing the influence of major neural injury, these findings highlight a potential non‐neural influence of thyroiditis on postoperative dysphonia. Shimmer and IVST are useful indicators for detecting subtle functional changes, supporting thyroid antibody status as a practical serological indicator for individualized perioperative counseling and rehabilitation.Level of EvidenceLevel III.Keywords: intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM), post‐thyroidectomy dysphonia (PTD), thyroid autoimmunity, thyroidectomy fibrosis and adhesion

Introduction

Thyroidectomy is a common endocrine surgical procedure, but postoperative voice changes remain a significant concern for patients, even when major laryngeal nerves are preserved [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. While direct injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) or the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve (EBSLN) is a well‐known cause of dysphonia [6, 7, 8], many patients experience voice alterations despite anatomically and functionally intact nerves, a condition often termed post‐thyroidectomy dysphonia (PTD) [1, 3, 4]. Proposed mechanisms for these non‐neurogenic voice changes include laryngotracheal fixation due to scarring [2], impaired strap muscle function from surgical manipulation or fibrosis [9, 10, 11, 12], and subtle trauma to the laryngeal framework.

Chronic thyroiditis, particularly Hashimoto's thyroiditis, is characterized by lymphocytic infiltration and varying degrees of fibrosis within the thyroid gland [13, 14]. This underlying inflammation can lead to increased tissue friability, vascularity, and adherence of the thyroid capsule to surrounding structures, such as the trachea and strap muscles [15]. Consequently, thyroidectomy in patients with thyroiditis can be technically more challenging [15, 16], potentially increasing the risk of bleeding, operative time, and inadvertent trauma to perilaryngeal tissues, even when major nerves are meticulously preserved [15]. The presence of thyroid autoantibodies, such as thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb) and anti‐thyroid microsomal antibody (TMAb), is a key serological indicator for autoimmune thyroiditis [13, 14, 17]. Higher antibody titers have been associated with more severe thyroiditis and increased glandular fibrosis [13, 17].

Given that thyroiditis‐associated adhesions and fibrosis may increase surgical difficulty [15, 16] and contribute to mechanisms underlying PTD (like laryngotracheal fixation [2] and strap muscle involvement [9, 10, 11, 12]), it is plausible that the presence of preoperative thyroiditis, indicated by elevated antibodies [13, 17], could negatively impact postoperative voice outcomes [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. However, there is a paucity of research specifically investigating the relationship between preoperative thyroid autoantibody status and subsequent voice changes in patients undergoing nerve‐preserved thyroidectomy. Understanding this potential link is clinically relevant, as it could help identify patients at higher risk for postoperative voice disturbances. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the correlation between preoperative elevated thyroid antibodies and postoperative objective and subjective voice outcomes in patients undergoing thyroidectomy with confirmed nerve preservation.

Materials and Methods

Patients

This retrospective study enrolled 277 adult patients (age > 18 years) who underwent primary nerve‐preserved thyroid surgery between December 2020 and March 2024 at a tertiary academic and referral center. All surgeries were performed by a single surgeon using intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM) and an energy‐based device (LigaSure Exact Dissector, LED). The extent of thyroidectomy was categorized as unilateral or bilateral. Unilateral thyroidectomy consisted of lobectomy with isthmusectomy with or without pyramidal lobe resection, whereas bilateral thyroidectomy corresponded to total thyroidectomy. Surgical drains were selectively placed at the discretion of the surgeon and, when used, were removed within 48 h postoperatively.

Nerve‐preserved surgery was defined as follows: (1) a decrease of < 20% in the post‐dissection RLN electromyographic (EMG) amplitude compared with the pre‐dissection value; this stringent threshold was intentionally adopted to minimize the potential influence of mild neural impairment on postoperative voice outcomes; (2) the presence of a cricothyroid muscle (CTM) twitch response upon proximal stimulation of the EBSLN; and (3) normal postoperative vocal fold movement confirmed by fiberlaryngoscopy during the first postoperative outpatient visit, typically 1–2 weeks after surgery.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of previous thyroid or neck surgery or neck radiation (n = 10); (2) undergoing lateral neck dissection, U shape muscle flap, or transection at least unilateral strap muscle (n = 2); (3) lack of preoperative thyroid antibody data, including TgAb or TMAb (n = 18); (4) lack of pre‐ or postoperative subjective/objective voice analysis (n = 32); (5) not euthyroid preoperatively, defined as an abnormal free T4 level (n = 3). Data Collection

Demographic and surgical information, including sex, age, surgical extent (unilateral or bilateral thyroidectomy), and central neck dissection (CND, with or without), were recorded. Pathological data included thyroid tumor pathologic diagnosis (benign or malignant); main tumor volume (mL, calculated as length × width × depth); and specimen weight (g).

Preoperative thyroid function and antibody profiles were collected for all patients. Serum free thyroxine (free T4, reference range: 0.7–1.48 ng/dL), thyroid‐stimulating hormone (TSH, reference range: 0.35–4.94 μIU/mL), and thyroglobulin (Tg, reference range: < 50 ng/mL) were measured, together with TgAb (reference range: < 40 IU/mL) and TMAb (reference range: < 35 IU/mL). Patients were categorized according to their thyroid antibody status: those with either TgAb or TMAb above the reference value were assigned to the elevated antibody (EA) group, whereas patients with both values within the reference range were assigned to the normal antibody (NA) group.

To further characterize the grade of antibody elevation, each antibody titer was converted into a semi‐quantitative score. For TgAb, values < 40 IU/mL were scored as 0, 40–199 IU/mL as 1, 200–999 IU/mL as 2, and ≥ 1000 IU/mL as 3. TMAb values were scored using the same tiered structure (< 35, 35–199, 200–999, and ≥ 1000 IU/mL corresponding to scores of 0, 1, 2, and 3, respectively). The combined antibody score, defined as the sum of TgAb and TMAb scores, was subsequently used to derive a three‐level thyroid antibody grade: Grade 1 (total score = 1), Grade 2 (total score = 2), and Grade 3 (total score ≥ 3).

Objective and subjective voice analysis was standardized and performed at two time points: preoperatively (within 2 months before surgery) and 6 weeks postoperatively (median 40 days, range 33–49 days). Objective voice analysis included the multidimensional voice program (MDVP; Model 5105, Version 3.1.7, KayPENTAX, USA), which provided maximum phonation time (MPT), mean fundamental frequency (Mean F 0), jitter, shimmer, and noise‐to‐harmonic ratio (NHR). The voice range profile (VRP; Model 4326, Version 3.3.0, KayPENTAX, USA) yielded maximum pitch frequency (F max), minimum pitch frequency (F min), and pitch range (PR), defined as the number of semitones between F max and F min. Acoustic recordings were performed in a semi‐soundproof room with environmental noise less than 45 dB. Voice samples were recorded with a standardized microphone‐to‐mouth distance of approximately 10 cm during sustained phonation of /æ/ for 10 s. Up to three recordings were obtained until a stable phonation was achieved, and a stable segment of at least 3 s was selected for objective voice analysis. Subjective voice analyzes were obtained using the voice handicap index‐10 (VHI‐10; range 0–40) [18], the VHI‐30 (range 0–120) [19], the voice‐related quality of life (VRQoL; raw score, range 10–50) [20], and the index of voice and swallowing handicap of thyroidectomy (IVST) [6, 7, 21], which was specifically designed for thyroidectomy patients. The IVST includes a total score (IVST‐T; range 0–20) and two subscores: the voice domain (IVST‐V; range 0–14) and the swallowing domain (IVST‐S; range 0–6).

Changes in objective parameters were calculated as Δ = (postoperative − preoperative)/preoperative, while changes in subjective scores were expressed as Δ = postoperative − preoperative. For objective voice analyzes, a postoperative change exceeding 20% from baseline was defined as abnormal. The same threshold was applied across all objective voice parameters to provide a consistent definition of abnormal postoperative change. For subjective voice analyzes, abnormal values were determined using study‐specific cutoffs. The detailed definitions are summarized in Table 3.

Given the retrospective exploratory nature of the study, no prespecified primary or secondary endpoints were defined. Therefore, the analyzes of multiple objective and subjective voice outcomes should be interpreted as exploratory.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyzes were performed using R software (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics were expressed as counts and percentages for categorical variables and as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. Group comparisons were conducted using the independent t‐test for continuous variables and the chi‐square test for categorical variables (Fisher's exact test was applied when expected frequencies were < 5).

Given the baseline differences between NA and EA groups, multivariable logistic regression analyzes were conducted to assess the independent association between elevated thyroid autoantibodies and postoperative abnormal voice outcomes. Models were adjusted for age, sex, surgical extent, CND, thyroid pathology, main tumor volume, and specimen weight. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and corresponding p‐values were reported.

An additional analysis using a three‐tier thyroid antibody grading system was performed to evaluate the relationship between antibody burden and the severity of postoperative voice changes. Abnormal rates across grades were compared with the chi‐square test.

All statistical tests were two‐tailed, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of Patient Characteristics Between Groups

A total of 212 patients were analyzed, including 144 patients (67.9%) in the NA group and 68 patients (32.1%) in the EA group. Comparative analysis of patient characteristics between the two groups revealed prominent differences in the following parameters, listed by increasing p‐value: (1) Surgical extent: EA group had a significantly higher proportion of bilateral surgery compared to NA group (88.2% vs. 54.9%, p < 0.001). (2) Specimen weight: EA group exhibited significantly heavier main tumors than NA group (43.7 ± 38.1 g vs. 27.6 ± 19.7 g, p = 0.002). (3) Main tumor volume: Main tumor volume in the EA group was significantly larger than that in the NA group (80.8 ± 66.8 mL vs. 55.3 ± 44.9 mL, p = 0.005). (4) Sex: EA group had a higher proportion of females than NA group (80.9% vs. 67.4%, p = 0.061). These parameters with p‐values < 0.1 were subsequently selected as variables for logistic regression analysis. No significant intergroup differences were observed in age, central neck dissection, or pathology.

Regarding thyroid‐related profiles, the EA group exhibited slightly lower levels of free T4 (1.06 ± 0.36 ng/dL vs. 1.15 ± 0.25 ng/dL, p = 0.062) and higher TSH levels (4.78 ± 13.93 μIU/mL vs. 1.51 ± 1.12 μIU/mL, p = 0.057) compared to the NA group. However, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Tg levels were similar between the two groups (p = 0.652). The results were shown in Table 1.

The abnormal rates of postoperative voice parameters between the NA and EA groups are summarized in Table 3. Definitions of abnormalities for each parameter and questionnaire are also provided.

Among the objective voice parameters, the EA group demonstrated higher proportions of abnormal changes in MPT, jitter, shimmer, F max, and PR. For subjective voice parameters, the EA group exhibited significantly higher rates of abnormal postoperative worsening across all measures, including VHI‐10, VHI‐30, VRQoL, IVST‐T, IVST‐V, and IVST‐S.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the impact of elevated thyroid autoantibodies (TgAb and TMAb) on postoperative voice changes in patients undergoing primary thyroid surgery. Compared with patients with normal antibody levels (NA group), those with elevated levels (EA group) tended to undergo more bilateral surgeries and presented with larger tumor volume and heavier thyroid specimens, suggesting a wider extent of perithyroidal dissection (Table 1). Preoperatively, the groups demonstrated overall comparability in voice parameters, except for a higher mean F 0 observed in the EA group, likely attributable to its higher proportion of female patients (Table 2). Postoperatively, the EA group demonstrated higher abnormal rates across multiple objective and subjective voice parameters (Table 3). After adjustment for patient characteristics, EA status remained independently associated with abnormal shimmer and IVST‐T, identifying these parameters as the most sensitive indicators of postoperative voice changes in this cohort (Table 4). An exploratory analysis of antibody grade further revealed that patients with intermediate antibody burden (Grade 2) had the highest rates of abnormal shimmer, IVST‐T, and IVST‐S (Table 5). Taken together, these findings suggest that elevated thyroid autoimmunity is associated with less favorable postoperative voice outcomes despite preserved major neural function.

In this study, patients in the EA group presented with larger main tumor volumes, heavier thyroid specimens, and a higher proportion of bilateral surgeries. Intraoperatively, these patients often exhibited more extensive perithyroidal adhesions, resulting in a broader operative field and more meticulous dissection. Moreover, these patients frequently exhibited increased tissue friability and dense perithyroidal adhesions. Such conditions may predispose to postoperative fibrosis or adhesion formation. We hypothesize that this preexisting inflammatory state, together with the wider surgical dissection plane required in these patients, may increase the likelihood of postoperative fibrosis and adhesion formation, which could partly account for the observed voice impairment.

A difference in sex distribution was observed between the groups, resulting in a statistically higher preoperative mean F 0 in the EA group. However, our voice analysis was based on parameter changes (Δ), which helped minimize this potential bias. Other preoperative voice parameters did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting that preoperative voice assessment alone cannot distinguish patients with thyroid antibody elevation or predict the presence of thyroiditis‐related adhesions. Age and central neck dissection were also potential confounding factors, although no significant differences were observed in this cohort.

IONM in this study served not merely as a tool for neural preservation, but as a crucial methodological control to distinguish neural from non‐neural etiologies. The adopted protocol evaluated the RLN and EBSLN through laryngeal EMG signal changes and observation of CTM twitch. This approach is both practical and time‐efficient in clinical settings, causes minimal interference with surgical manipulation, and provides essential real‐time intraoperative information on neural function. Although CTM twitch primarily confirms gross EBSLN integrity and cannot completely exclude subtle or subclinical dysfunction, reliable quantitative assessment of EBSLN function remains limited in routine clinical practice. Without confirming the functional integrity of both the RLN, particularly the EBSLN, the observed deterioration in IVST‐V subscores or the tendency toward PR narrowing could easily be misattributed to subclinical EBSLN injury. Therefore, standardized IONM helped reduce the impact of major neural dysfunction, making the observed voice changes more likely to reflect non‐neural influences, within which thyroiditis may play an important role.

Additionally, all procedures were performed using the LED, which we believe helped reduce unnecessary dissection and excessive muscle retraction, an advantageous factor in cases with preoperative inflammation prone to bleeding. To better evaluate non‐neural contributors, postoperative assessments were conducted at 6 weeks, a time point chosen to exclude transient causes such as intubation‐related injury, acute laryngeal edema or inflammation, and acute wound pain [22]. At this stage, postoperative fibrosis and intermuscular adhesions are likely the primary determinants of voice alteration. To minimize potential non‐neural factors, patients who underwent procedures involving strap muscle manipulation, such as performing a U‐shaped muscle flap or transection of at least one strap muscle [23], as well as those who received lateral neck dissection, were excluded from this study due to the difficulty of isolating their potential effects on voice outcomes.

Preexisting thyroiditis may exacerbate postoperative fibrotic reactions [13, 14, 24]. Inflammation‐driven cytokine activity promotes fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition, leading to increased scar stiffness and adhesion formation. These effects may persist beyond the early postoperative period, predisposing patients to prolonged dysphonia or swallowing discomfort. The change observed in IVST‐S subscore also suggests that adhesions or fibrosis could impair laryngeal elevation and pharyngeal coordination, making it one of the abnormal indicators in this study.

Shimmer and IVST served as important evaluation tools. Shimmer quantifies cycle‐to‐cycle amplitude perturbation and is a sensitive indicator of phonatory instability, correlating with perceived roughness or breathiness [7, 25]. Previous report [7] has discussed muscular factors influencing voice stability. In the present study, the marked increase in shimmer indicates impaired vocal stability. Thyroiditis‐related fibrosis and adhesions may tether the strap muscles or restrict subtle adjustments of the laryngeal framework, thereby disrupting the precise and stable glottal closure required for clear phonation—even when the neuromuscular inputs of the RLN and EBSLN are confirmed to be intact.

The IVST, specifically designed for patients undergoing thyroidectomy, has proven to be a sensitive instrument for detecting patient‐perceived impairments, particularly those related to phonatory effort, endurance, and swallowing coordination [3, 21, 26]. It has shown strong correspondence with both neural and non‐neural forms of PTD. Compared with general voice questionnaires, the IVST provides a more targeted and suitable tool for assessing postoperative voice changes in thyroid surgery patients. In addition, the worsening of the IVST‐S subscore may reflect non‐neural influences on swallowing function. Localized fibrosis could cause adhesions between the thyroid capsule and surrounding structures, such as the trachea, thereby limiting the vertical laryngeal movement essential for normal swallowing. Such restriction may manifest as patient‐perceived swallowing difficulty, which is captured by the IVST‐S measure. Interestingly, the antibody‐grade analysis revealed that Grade 2 patients exhibited the highest rates of shimmer and IVST abnormalities, rather than showing a stepwise linear increase from Grade 1 to Grade 3. This non‐linear pattern suggests that the relationship between thyroid autoimmunity and postoperative voice outcomes may not be purely dose dependent. However, given the limited sample size within each subgroup, this observation should be interpreted cautiously and requires further validation.

Another consideration when interpreting the present findings is thyroid functional status. Although all included patients had normal free T4 levels, the EA group exhibited higher and more variable TSH levels than the NA group. This may reflect subclinical thyroid dysfunction in a subset of patients and could have contributed, at least in part, to postoperative voice changes. Future studies incorporating stricter thyroid function criteria or adjusting for thyroid functional status may help further clarify this issue.

Clinically, these findings suggest that preoperative thyroid antibody screening may provide additional information for perioperative risk assessment and individualized patient counseling, beyond its role in endocrinologic management. Elevated TgAb or TMAb levels may serve as accessible serological indicators associated with an increased risk of PTD and swallowing complaints. For such patients, enhanced perioperative strategies—including meticulous surgical dissection to minimize traction, preventive anti‐adhesion measures [24], and early postoperative rehabilitation such as voice therapy [27] and neck mobility exercises [28]—should be considered. Moreover, awareness of this risk can improve preoperative counseling, helping set realistic expectations regarding recovery of voice and swallowing functions. As surgical technologies and techniques evolve, refinement in intraoperative tissue handling and postoperative monitoring may further mitigate these outcomes [29, 30].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, a relatively large number of patients (n = 32) were excluded because complete pre‐ and postoperative voice assessments were unavailable. This exclusion may introduce selection bias, as patients with satisfactory postoperative recovery may have been less likely to complete comprehensive voice follow‐up. Consequently, the overall rate of postoperative voice abnormalities may have been overestimated.

Second, the sample size was insufficient for more detailed subgroup analyzes, particularly among patients in the EA group who underwent unilateral thyroidectomy and within the antibody‐grade subgroups. As unilateral thyroidectomy becomes increasingly favored in current practice, future studies with larger and more balanced cohorts are warranted. Nevertheless, the impact of completion thyroidectomy on postoperative voice outcomes remains difficult to control, making studies with a substantial proportion of bilateral cases, such as the present one, clinically valuable.

Third, elevated thyroid antibody levels do not necessarily represent a specific autoimmune thyroid disorder. Rather than serving as diagnostic indicators, antibody levels in this study were used as objective and routinely measurable serological indicators of underlying thyroid autoimmunity. This approach minimizes the confounding effects often encountered in studies of autoimmune thyroid disease, which typically require stringent diagnostic criteria and long‐term clinical follow‐up.

Fourth, although the antibody‐grade analysis demonstrated a higher proportion of postoperative voice abnormalities in Grade 2 patients, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited sample size within each subgroup. The relationship between antibody burden, underlying tissue changes, and postoperative voice outcomes remains incompletely understood and warrants further investigation.

Finally, several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. Although CTM twitch confirms gross EBSLN function, subtle or subclinical EBSLN dysfunction cannot be completely excluded. In addition, because multiple objective and subjective voice outcomes were evaluated without formal correction for multiple comparisons, borderline statistical significance should be interpreted as exploratory. Furthermore, the proposed roles of fibrosis, adhesions, and strap muscle traction remain hypothetical, as intraoperative adhesions, operative time, histological fibrosis severity, and postoperative laryngotracheal mobility were not directly evaluated.

Conclusion

Preoperative elevation of thyroid autoantibodies may predispose patients to postoperative voice impairment, even when laryngeal nerves are anatomically and functionally preserved. With IONM allowing meaningful minimization of potential major neural injury, the contribution of thyroiditis to non‐neural factors affecting postoperative voice changes becomes more apparent. The identification of shimmer and the IVST as indicators underscores their clinical value for detecting subtle changes in phonatory stability and patient‐perceived impairment. Recognizing thyroid antibody status as a simple and objective serological indicator enables more personalized surgical planning, perioperative counseling, and targeted rehabilitation. Further prospective investigations are warranted to validate these findings and refine preventive strategies for optimizing postoperative voice outcomes.
