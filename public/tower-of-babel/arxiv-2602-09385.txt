Separating Quantum and Classical Advice with Good Codes
John Bostanci, Andrew Huang, Vinod Vaikuntanathan

Abstract
We show an unconditional classical oracle separation between the class of languages that can be verified using a quantum proof ( 𝖰𝖬𝖠 {\mathsf{QMA}} ) and the class of languages that can be verified with a classical proof ( 𝖰𝖢𝖬𝖠 {\mathsf{QCMA}} ). Compared to the recent work of Bostanci, Haferkamp, Nirkhe, and Zhandry (STOC 2026), our proof is conceptually and technically simpler, and readily extends to other oracle separations. In particular, our techniques yield the first unconditional classical oracle separation between the class of languages that can be decided with quantum advice ( 𝖡𝖰𝖯 / qpoly {\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{qpoly} ) and the class of languages that can be decided with classical advice ( 𝖡𝖰𝖯 / poly {\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{poly} ), improving on the quantum oracle separation of Aaronson and Kuperberg (CCC 2007) and the classically-accessible classical oracle separation of Li, Liu, Pelecanos and Yamakawa (ITCS 2024). Our oracles are based on the code intersection problem introduced by Yamakawa and Zhandry (FOCS 2022), combined with codes that have extremely good list-recovery properties.

Introduction

We study the question of whether quantum proofs and advice provide more power than their classical counterparts. In the language of complexity theory, we consider whether the classes
{\mathsf{QMA}}
and
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
, and
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{qpoly}
and
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{poly}
, are distinct. These questions, first posed by
[
6
]
(for proofs) and
[
33
]
(for advice), have been long-standing open problems in the field of quantum complexity theory.

Given that unconditional separations seem out of reach today since they would imply breakthrough results like
{\mathsf{P}}\neq{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
, research attention has shifted to providing oracular evidence for their separation. The first progress towards this came from the seminal work of Aaronson and Kuperberg
[
2
]
, who introduced the weaker notion of a
unitary
, or
quantum
, oracle as a means to separate the classes of
{\mathsf{QMA}}
and
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
and asked whether their result could be strengthened to a classical oracle separation. After a long series of works which demonstrated either conditional or non-standard separations
[
19
,
27
,
31
,
7
,
40
,
28
]
, the first unconditional classical oracle separation between
{\mathsf{QMA}}
and
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
appeared in the recent work of
[
12
]
.

The oracle used in the separation of
[
12
]
(which was introduced in the paper of Zhandry
[
40
]
) leverages one of the most natural quantum properties to separate quantum from classical computers: whether two functions are related by the Hadamard transform. This idea was first exploited in the work of
[
5
]
, who presented the “Forrelation” problem, i.e., deciding whether two functions are related by the Hadamard transform, as a candidate (oracle) separation between
{\mathsf{BQP}}
and
{\mathsf{PH}}
. The works of
[
40
]
and
[

]
lift this problem to
{\mathsf{QMA}}
with a variant dubbed “spectral Forrelation”. The separation of
[
12
]
employs sophisticated techniques to analyze Forrelated oracles, including ideas inspired by mathematical physics and Hamiltonian learning. It is natural to wonder whether one can separate
{\mathsf{QMA}}
from
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
without using this heavy technical machinery.

Question 1:

Is there a conceptually simpler classical oracle separation between
{\mathsf{QMA}}
and
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
?

A closely related problem to separating
{\mathsf{QMA}}
from
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
is that of separating
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{qpoly}
, problems that can be solved with quantum advice, from
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{poly}
, problems that can be solved with classical advice. In addition to their quantum oracle separation between
{\mathsf{QMA}}
and
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
, Aaronson and Kuperberg also gave a unitary oracle separation for
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{qpoly}
and
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{poly}
and asked if this too could be lifted to a classical oracle separation. While there is no formal equivalence relating advice and proof separations, one paradigm towards advice separations follows the general outline of
[
2
]
. Their advice separation begins with a hard quantum search problem (which is also used in their proof separation), and hides the value of a random language behind an oracle that expects to receive the answer to that quantum search problem. The classical oracle separation of
[
12
]

{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{qpoly}
and
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{poly}
seems to necessitate the use of a hard

instead.

A parallel line of work starting from the paper of Yamakawa and Zhandry
[
39
]
studies the “code intersection” problem. The code intersection problem exploits another way in which quantum algorithms can exploit structure in functions: the ability to realize the convolution theorem as decoding in the Fourier basis (first noticed in
[
34
]
).
[
39
]
used this to formulate a
{\mathsf{TFNP}}
problem that can be solved by an efficient quantum computer but not by a classical one: given a random oracle
H
and code
C\subseteq\Sigma^{n}
, find a codeword that hashes to the all zeros string. This problem was later modified in the works of
[
29

27
,
7

to make some progress towards a full separation between
{\mathsf{QMA}}
and
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
by restricting/modifying the query model in question (i.e. by enforcing classical-only access or bounded adaptivity). Crucially, unlike the spectral Forrelation oracle, the code intersection problem comes with a classical search problem, which
[
27
]
uses to lift their “classically-accessible” oracle search problem to get a similar (non-standard) separation between
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{qpoly}
and
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{poly}
. This leaves open the following question:

Question 2:

Is there a (standard) classical oracle separation between
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{qpoly}
and
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{poly}
?

1.1
Our Work

We show that both these questions can be resolved using the “code intersection problem” studied in the work of Yamakawa and Zhandry
[
39
]
, combined with codes that have extremely good list-recovery properties. An important part of showing verifiable quantum advantage in
[
39
]
was ruling out randomized classical algorithms for solving their problem. At a high level, a classical algorithm might be able to find a few symbols that hash to zero, but it has no way of knowing whether they can be combined to form a good codeword until it tries almost every combination. As long as most (small) sets of symbols overlap with very few codewords, the chance that a classical algorithm stumbles upon a codeword which hashes to the right value is exceedingly unlikely.

One would expect that for a truly random code, no large collection of codewords can have large overlap with a small set of symbols. Inspired by the idea of pseudorandomness presented by
[
28
,
40
]
, one might hope to find linear codes that mimic the properties of a random code, in this case, codes with extremely good list-recoverability. We formalize this intuition by showing that by substituting folded Reed-Solomon (FRS) codes in the construction of
[
39
]
with multiplicity codes (although any code with good expansion properties would suffice), we can indeed separate
{\mathsf{QMA}}
from
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal)
.

There exists a classical oracle
\mathcal{O}
such that
{\mathsf{QMA}}^{\mathcal{O}}\not\subseteq{\mathsf{QCMA}}^{\mathcal{O}}
.

Our separation, in addition to admitting a much simpler proof, has an additional benefit over the result of
[
12
]
: our more structured oracle problem is naturally associated with a
{\mathsf{TFNP}}
problem, allowing us to lift our result to provide the
first unconditional classical oracle separation
between
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{qpoly}
from
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{poly}
in a straightforward manner.

Technical Overview

The Yamakawa-Zhandry Algorithm.

Our separation begins with the code intersection problem
[
39
]
: given a code
C\subseteq\Sigma^{n}=(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{s})^{n}
, function
H:[n]\times\Sigma\to\{0,1\}
, and hash
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, find a codeword
c\in C
such that
H(i,c_{i})=x_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
(we will use the shorthand
H(c)=x
to refer to this constraint). Yamakawa and Zhandry show that when given oracle access to
H
, this problem has an efficient quantum algorithm but no (uniform) classical ones, giving a relativized separation between
{\mathsf{FP}}
and
{\mathsf{FBQP}}
. As our result will require modifying the Yamakawa-Zhandry algorithm, we begin by briefly explaining how it works.

We begin by noting that it suffices to be able to produce the state

\ket{\psi}\propto\sum_{v:v\in C\mbox{\small\ and }H(v)=x}\ket{v}=\sum_{v\in\Sigma^{n}}\mathbb{1}_{H,x}(v)\cdot\mathbb{1}_{C}(v)\ket{v},

where
\mathbb{1}_{H,x}(\cdot)
and
\mathbb{1}_{C}(\cdot)
are indicator functions for the event
H(v)=x
and for the event
v\in C
, respectively. Taking inspiration from Regev’s reduction from SIS to LWE
[
34
]
, Yamakawa and Zhandry observe that
\ket{\psi}
is the pointwise product of the states

\ket{\phi_1}:=\ket{\phi_1(x)}\propto\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in\Sigma^{n}:H(\mathbf{v})=x}\ket{\bfv}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{v_{i}\in\Sigma:H(i,v_{i})=x_{i}}\ket{v_i}\hskip 35.0pt\text{and}\hskip 35.0pt\ket{\phi_2}\propto\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in C}\ket{\bfv},

both of which can be prepared efficiently given access to
H
. By the convolution theorem, we know that

\mathrm{QFT}_{q}\ket{\psi}\propto\mathrm{QFT}_{q}(\ket{\phi_1}\odot\ket{\phi_2})=\mathrm{QFT}_{q}\ket{\phi_1}\star\mathrm{QFT}_{q}\ket{\phi_2},

where
\odot
denotes the point-wise product of two vectors, and
\star
their convolution. Therefore, it suffices to efficiently prepare the state
\ket{\mathsf{goal}}:=\mathrm{QFT}_{q}\ket{\phi_1}\star\mathrm{QFT}_{q}\ket{\phi_2}
, as
\mathrm{QFT}_{q}^{-1}\ket{\mathsf{goal}}=\ket{\psi}
. If
C
is a
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear code, then
\mathrm{QFT}_{q}\ket{\phi_2}
is simply the uniform superposition over the dual code
C^{\perp}
, so we can produce the states

\displaystyle\mathrm{QFT}_{q}\ket{\phi_1}\otimes\mathrm{QFT}_{q}\ket{\phi_2}

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\Sigma^{n}}\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{H,x}(\mathbf{e})}\ket{\ee}\otimes\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in C^{\perp}}\ket{\bfv}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle U_{\mathsf{add}}}}{{\mapsto}}\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\Sigma^{n},\mathbf{v}\in C^{\perp}}\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{H,x}(\mathbf{e})}\ket{\ee}\ket{\bfv}\ket{\bfv+\ee}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle U_{\mathsf{sub}}}}{{\mapsto}}\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in\Sigma^{n},\mathbf{v}\in C^{\perp}}\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{H,x}(\mathbf{e})}\ket{0}\ket{\bfv}\ket{\bfv+\ee},

while our desired state is

\ket{\mathsf{goal}}=\mathrm{QFT}_{q}\ket{\phi_1}\star\mathrm{QFT}_{q}\ket{\phi_2}\propto\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in C^{\perp},\mathbf{e}\in\Sigma^{n}}\sqrt{\mathcal{D}_{H,x}(\mathbf{e})}\ket{\bfv+\ee},

where
\mathcal{D}_{H,x}(\cdot)
is the density function of
\mathrm{QFT}_{q}\ket{\phi_1}
(here, we are ignoring phases for simplicity of exposition). For a completely random function
H
and a fixed
x
, we observe that
\mathcal{D}_{H,x}(\cdot)
will have roughly half of its weight on 0 in each coordinate (since around half of all symbols in each coordinate should hash to 0 or 1), while the remaining half of its weight will be close to uniform over nonzero symbols. By taking
C
to be a folded Reed-Solomon (FRS) code with sufficiently high rate, Yamakawa and Zhandry show that one can efficiently decode
C^{\perp}
from errors over
\mathcal{D}_{H,x}(\cdot)
with high probability and consequently prepare
\ket{\mathsf{goal}}
as desired.

Lifting the Separation.

The obvious issue in using the code intersection problem to separate
{\mathsf{QMA}}
from
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
, however, is that both
{\mathsf{QMA}}
and
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
algorithms can make quantum oracle access to
H
, and our problem is already in
{\mathsf{BQP}}
! Thus, we must make some modifications to the problem at hand.

First, as observed by
[
29
]
, the first phase of this algorithm can be made non-adaptive: the state
\ket{\phi_2}
depends only on
C
, while
\ket{\phi_1}
depends only mildly on
x
, since we can simply prepare all
2n
preimage states

\ket{H^{-1}_1(0)}:=\sum_{x\in\Sigma:H(1,x)=0}\ket{x},\ldots,\ket{H^{-1}_n(1)}:=\sum_{x\in\Sigma:H(n,x)=1}\ket{x}

before selecting
\ket{\phi_1}
when given
x
. On the other hand, the second phase does not require access to
H
, and should work equally well for all
x
. Therefore, if we are given the state

\ket{\adv_H}:=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\ket{H^{-1}_i(0)}\otimes\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\ket{H^{-1}_i(1)}\otimes\ket{\phi_2}

as our quantum proof or advice, we can produce
\ket{\phi_1(x)}:=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\ket{H^{-1}_i(x_i)}
for
any

x
and use it to produce a solution to the code intersection problem for
x
without access to
H
! We can therefore replace
H
with the much weaker oracle
O_{H}(x,v)
that simply verifies if the vector
v
hashes to
x
.

Of course, this problem is still in
{\mathsf{NP}}\subseteq{\mathsf{QCMA}}
, since for a fixed
x
, the prover can always send any codeword
v
that hashes to
x
! Luckily, we claim that this is pretty much the
only
thing that the prover can do. To operationalize this intuition, we note that our quantum proof/advice is in some sense encoding many codewords along with their hashes in superposition. Thus, taking some inspiration from
[
27
,
7
]
, we define the
Code Intersection Subset Size
problem as follows:

Estimate the size of a set
E\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}\times\Sigma^{n}
, which is promised to either be the full set
\{0,1\}^{n}\times\Sigma^{n}
or a small subset
E\subseteq F\times\Sigma^{n}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}\times\Sigma^{n}
where
|F|\leq t
for some threshold
t\ll 2^{n}
, given access to the following oracle
O[H,E](x,v)
:

\displaystyle O[H,E](x,v)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if $v\in C$, $H(v)=x$, and $(x,v)\in E$,}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

This problem is naturally in
{\mathsf{QMA}}
: the prover can give as proof
\ket{\adv_H}
which depends only on
H
. Given
\ket{\adv_H}
, the verifier can sample a random
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and should be able to produce with high probability
v\in C
such that
H(v)=x
. The oracle
O
therefore allows the verifier to check if
x\in E
for any
x
, making the set estimation problem trivial. In fact, the
{\mathsf{QMA}}
verifier need not be concerned about malicious proofs, since a NO instance of
O
always outputs 0 whenever
x
(which is sampled solely by the verifier) is not in
F
.

We note that our problem differs from the oracle problems defined in
[
27
,
7
]
, which take
E=F\times\Sigma^{n}
for some small set
F
. This modification, while not impacting our
{\mathsf{QMA}}
algorithm, will be crucial in establishing a
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
lower bound.

An Entropic Viewpoint.

To rule out
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
proof systems, we first observe that the major difference between quantum and classical proofs lies in their
clonability
. In particular, an (oracle) algorithm which uses a
classical
witness can always be re-run with the same witness, even if it makes measurements. This simple and seemingly obvious fact, first formally identified in
[
40
]
, was utilized to great effect by
[
12
]
to give their classical oracle separation, and we will take advantage of it as well.

To this end, suppose there was some
Q
-query
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
verifier
V
which succeeded in the set estimation problem. We observe that this means that
V
can always distinguish between
O[H,\{0,1\}^{n}\times\Sigma^{n}]
and
O[H,E]
whenever
E
is small. But these oracles differ only at inputs
(x,v)
where
v\in C
,
H(v)=x
, and
(x,v)\notin E
! Thus, by the hybrid lemma
[
8
]
, if we measure a random query that
V
makes to
O[H,E]
, we should expect to get a new pair
(x,v)\notin E
such that
v\in C
and
H(v)=x
with good probability provided
E
is small.
2
2

2

Critically, if we have already successfully guessed some collection
E
of code words and hash values, we can always perfectly simulate
O[H,E]
. The same is
not
true for the oracles of
[
27
,
7
]
, for which even “small” sets correspond to exponentially many codewords.
This gives rise to a natural algorithm for
guessing
the hash values of codewords: starting with
E=\emptyset
, simulate a run of
V
with
O[H,E]
and measure a random query before adding the measurement outcome to
E
; rinse and repeat. By our previous argument, conditioned on having a good witness, each iteration of this algorithm should correctly produce a new codeword and hash with non-negligible probability. We can therefore turn
V
into a guesser with non-uniform advice which correctly produces many distinct codewords and their hash values without making
any
oracle queries. After guessing the classical
w
-bit witness, this gives rise to an unconditional no-query algorithm which guesses the hash values of
\ell
codewords in
C
for all
\ell\leq t
with probability

2^{-w}\cdot\bigg(\Omega(Q^{-2})\bigg)^{\ell}=2^{-\mathrm{poly}(n)}\cdot\bigg(\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)}\bigg)^{\ell}~.

We now argue that this is in fact impossible. Observing that
H
is independently random at each coordinate
i
and symbol
x\in\Sigma
, we can upper bound the success probability of any sampler which produces
\ell
points by
(\frac{1}{2})^{s(\ell)}
, where
s(\ell)
is the minimum number of symbols that appear among
\ell
distinct codewords in
C
. Taking
\ell=t=\omega(\mathrm{poly}(n))
, if we can argue that
s(\ell)=\omega(\log n\cdot\ell)
, then we see that

2^{-\mathrm{poly}(n)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)}\right)^{\ell}=\bigg(\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(n)}\bigg)^{\ell}\gg\left(\frac{1}{2^{\omega(\log n)}}\right)^{\ell}=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{s(\ell)},

which will give us our desired contradiction.

List Recovery and Code Expansion.

How might we bound
s(\ell)
? For any
\ell
distinct codewords
c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}\in C
, define the lists
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\subseteq\Sigma
such that
S_{i}
consists of all symbols in
\Sigma
that appear in the
i
’th coordinate of some codeword
c_{j}
for
j\in[\ell]
. Clearly,
s(\ell)=\min_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_{i}|
, so there must be lists
S^{*}_{1},\ldots,S^{*}_{n}
such that

|C\cap(S^{*}_{1}\times\ldots\times S^{*}_{n})|\geq\ell\hskip 36.135pt\mbox{and}\hskip 36.135pt\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S^{*}_{i}|=s(\ell)~.

We now see that the question of how small
s(\ell)
can be is precisely characterized by the
list-recoverability
of
C
. In particular, if we know that
C
is
(L,O(L))
-list-recoverable for
L\lesssim\ell
, then this would mean that
\frac{s(\ell)}{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S^{*}_{i}|=\Omega(\ell)
as desired!

Note that this is a pretty strong condition; it necessitates the use of codes that have
near-optimal list recovery
, a property that in particular is not satisfied by the FRS codes used by
[
39
]
(see
Section

3.3
for further discussion). Fortunately, there is a fix: the setting of zero-error list recovery is closely linked to the notion of unbalanced expanders, and the recent work of
[
23
]
shows that multiplicity codes exhibit precisely the sort of list recovery that we need. Moreover, the fact that multiplicity codes are
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear and that their duals have relatively good distance
[
35
]
should guarantee the success of our
{\mathsf{QMA}}
algorithm. We note that although our dual code happens to admit efficient unique decoding, our separation only needs
C^{\perp}
to be
combinatorially
uniquely decodable, since we can always provide an (inefficient) decoding oracle.

A Final Complication.

It seems that this rather simple argument completes our separation; after all, by switching to using multiplicity codes (rather than folded Reed-Solomon codes as in
[
39
,
27
,
7
]
), we have been able to rule out all possible
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
algorithms. Sadly, we have to deal with one final and rather subtle issue, which has to do with the parameters of the multiplicity codes: in the process of obtaining excellent list recovery/expansion from our multiplicity codes, we are forced to make the relative rate of our (primary) code sub-constant, which means our dual code now has sub-constant relative distance! Recalling that our error distribution should concentrate on vectors with Hamming weight roughly
n/2
, we observe that this level of noise is now likely intolerable as there may not even exist a unique decoding most of the time under this error distribution.

Our solution is relatively simple, and it uses the generous amount of flexibility that the
[
34
,
39
]
algorithm affords us. Instead of using a completely random function
H
, we will instead make our function
biased
in favor of 0 (reminiscent of a recent strategy employed by
[
21
]
). That is, for each element
\sigma\in\Sigma
,
H(i,\sigma)
will take on the value 0 with probability
p\gg\frac{1}{2}
. Thus, for
x=0^{n}
, we can expect the error vectors in
\mathrm{QFT}\ket{\phi_1}
to have Hamming weight
\approx n(1-p)
, drastically reducing the amount of noise that we are required to decode from with respect to
C^{\perp}
.

This change does not come for free, however: unlike in
[
21
]
, where the goal was to invert only
x=0^{n}
, we need to be able to invert many
x
’s, including those with large Hamming weight. By biasing
H
towards 0, on inputs like
x=1^{n}
, we create an error distribution which has expected Hamming weight
\approx np\gg n/2
, thereby
worsening
our ability to invert!

Our final idea is to observe that since our algorithm can only invert low Hamming-weight vectors
x
rather than all vectors in
\{0,1\}^{n}
, we can simply
modify the problem to enforce this condition
. Instead of trying to invert all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, we can focus on inverting vectors of the form
x\|0^{n-n^{c}}
, where
x\in\{0,1\}^{n^{c}}
and
0<c\ll 1
. That is, we will now try to differentiate
E=\{0,1\}^{n^{c}}\times 0^{n-n^{c}}\times\Sigma^{n}
from
E\subseteq F\times 0^{n-n^{c}}\times\Sigma^{n}
where
|F|\leq t\ll 2^{n^{c}}
. Since
x\|0^{n-n^{c}}
has Hamming weight at most
n^{c}
, the corresponding error distribution for
\ket{\phi_1(x \| 0^{n-n^c})}
will be concentrated on vectors with Hamming weight at most
n^{c}+(n-n^{c})(1-p)\approx n(1-p)
, guaranteeing the success of our
{\mathsf{QMA}}
algorithm provided our dual code has distance at least
O(n(1-p))
and
p\lesssim 1-1/n^{1-c}
.

A General Recipe for a Classical Oracle Separation.

Before describing the advice separation, we summarize all of the steps we have taken so far to provide a general recipe for getting a
{\mathsf{QMA}}
versus
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
oracle separation. We start with an infinite family of codes
\{C_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}
over a large alphabet
\Sigma
(so that there are many solutions to the code intersection problem) such that:

1.

C_{\lambda}^{\perp}
can be efficiently decoded up to
\Omega(\lambda^{1+c})
errors for any constant
c
,

2.

Codewords of
C_{\lambda}
consist of
n=\mathrm{poly}(\lambda)
many symbols from
\Sigma
,

3.

C_{\lambda}
has near optimal list recovery for sufficiently large
\lambda
.

Then for every
\lambda
, we can sample hash functions
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}
to be biased so that roughly a
\lambda/n(\lambda)
-fraction of symbols are pre-images of
1
. We will ask for pre-images of
x\|0^{n-\lambda}
for
x\in\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
. The bias of the
H_{i}
, together with the fact that we ask for a hash that has at most
\lambda
many
1
’s, ensures that the dual decoding problem encounters an error with
O(\lambda)
Hamming weight with high probability, which falls under our dual decoding distance of
\Omega(\lambda^{1+c})
. Thus, the Yamakawa-Zhandry algorithm works and the problem stays in
{\mathsf{QMA}}
. At the same time, a
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
verifier will imply a sampler that outputs
v
codewords of the code with probability
\mathrm{poly}(\lambda)^{-v}
, and list recovery will enforce that this corresponds to
\Omega(v\cdot n)
symbols. The bias of the
H_{i}
will mean that the probability of guessing all symbols correctly will be
\left(1-\lambda/n\right)^{\Omega(v\cdot n)}\approx\mathrm{exp}(-\lambda v)\ll\mathrm{poly}(\lambda)^{-v}
, giving us a contradiction.

By instantiating this recipe with carefully chosen multiplicity codes, we arrive at our classical oracle separation.

Moving to the Advice Setting:
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{qpoly}
vs.
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{poly}
.

Unfortunately, the set approximation problem is easy with
trusted
advice: a single classical bit suffices to indicate whether the set in question is large or small. Here, the relative simplicity of our separation (which uses almost entirely classical ideas) and our use of a
{\mathsf{TFNP}}
problem allows us to extend our separation to the question of
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{qpoly}
and
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{poly}
. In contrast, it is not at all obvious (to us) how to construct even a candidate separating language based on the (decisional) spectral Forrelation problem of
[
12
]
.

In particular, the code intersection problem gives rise to the following
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{qpoly}
language: begin by sampling some random binary language
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
. On input
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, our oracle
O
will simply return whether
x\in\mathcal{L}
or
x\notin\mathcal{L}
provided it is given a valid codeword
v
which hashes to
x
. On the one hand, our original
{\mathsf{QMA}}
algorithm still works as a
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{qpoly}
machine, since
\ket{\adv_H}
is agnostic of
x
and allows us to produce
v
for all
x
. On the other hand, as we have intuitively argued earlier, even trusted classical advice should not help a
{\mathsf{BQP}}
machine to produce many valid codewords, so for most
x
, any
{\mathsf{BQP}}
machine with classical advice will not be able to receive the output of the oracle indicating whether
x\in\mathcal{L}
. Since the advice is bounded, it cannot itself describe many elements of
\mathcal{L}
, so the
{\mathsf{BQP}}
machine will fail to decide whether
x\in\mathcal{L}
for most
x
. The problem of guessing the value of a random function
H
given bounded-size advice and without querying
H
can be made precise by appealing to results on Yao’s box problem due to Chung, Guo, Liu and Qian
[
15
]
. We show that relatively straightforward modifications of the argument used by
[
27
]
for ruling out
classical-access

{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{poly}
algorithms extend to ruling out generic
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{poly}
algorithms as well, completing the advice separation.

Some Concluding Remarks.

First, as in
[
12
]
, it is not hard to see that our oracles also separate the clonable variants of
{\mathsf{QMA}}
and
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{qpoly}
from their regular counterparts.

Secondly, we emphasize that while the code intersection problem has previously been considered in the context of proof and advice separations
[
29
,
27
,
7
]
, all existing works employed FRS codes, which (as mentioned earlier) do not enjoy strong enough list-recovery properties. In particular, running our argument with FRS codes gives an upper bound on the sampling success probability which is too weak for
any
(even weak) separation! On the other hand, multiplicity codes do not appear to have strong enough decoding properties to handle the large amounts of noise that would be incurred by perfectly random functions
H
, which necessitates our use of biased oracles and restriction to low Hamming-weight vectors (an idea which did not appear in earlier, more limited, separations).

Finally, and unrelated to the main theme of this paper, in the process of modifying the Yamakawa-Zhandry algorithm for our separation, we observe that biasing the random oracle
H
in question also has the benefit of making the quantum algorithm in question
much more efficient
. As an example, if we tweak
H
to be even
2/3
-biased, this already decreases the noise level sufficiently that we can rely on (significantly faster)
unique decoders
rather than list decoders, which currently appears to be the major bottleneck in runtime.

3
Discussion and Open Questions

3.1
Structured versus randomness in classical oracle separations

As stated in the introduction, one major barrier in lifting the oracle separation of
[
12
]
to the advice setting is that the oracle is most naturally associated with a hard quantum search problem, instead of a classical one. In their classical oracle separation, the authors identify a way of sampling instances of the spectral Forrelation problem such that the pair of functions that seems completely random, except that they are related by the Fourier transform (and, in the case of
[
12
]
, one of the two oracles being sparse). In some informal sense, our oracle separation enables an efficient quantum verifier to extract more information (namely, the solution to a hard search problem) from its witness, but doing so seems to require additional structure in the oracle.

A natural question to ask is how much information can be encoded into a quantum state before it becomes clonable, and whether our ideas are useful in encoding information into other kinds of quantum states. The cryptographic analogy of a separation between
{\mathsf{QMA}}
and
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
(or, really
\mathsf{UnclonableQMA}
) is a primitive called quantum money
[
4
]
. These are states that can not be cloned, but can be verified, similar to witnesses for
{\mathsf{QMA}}
problems that are not in
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
. An extremal form of this primitive (and this idea of encoding information in a quantum state) is known as “copy-protected software”, wherein an efficient quantum party can extract the input output behavior of an entire classical function from a quantum state. Currently, there are several candidates for quantum money constructions in the plain model
[
18
,
13
,
41
]
, but less is known about copy-protected software. We hope that ideas from our separation might be useful in finding such constructions. To make progress towards cryptographic instantiations, one concrete direction to explore is a precise characterization of which witnesses cause our
{\mathsf{QMA}}
verifier to accept with high probability.

3.2
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-completeness of a decoding problem

One interesting difference between our oracle separation and the oracle separation of
[
12
]
is that their oracle separation can be seen as an obfuscation of a
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-complete problem. To elaborate further, just as problems involving random sparse functions might model the difficulty of constructing a SAT solver which ignores the structure of the SAT instance it receives, the spectral Forrelation problem models an algorithm for solving a two-basis local Hamiltonian problem that does not look at the structure of the two local Hamiltonians it receives. Note that despite the connection to a
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-complete problem, this property is not actually needed to achieve a separation between
{\mathsf{QMA}}
and
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
, as highlighted in the actual oracle separation of
[
12
]
. In particular, the “YES” and “NO” instances can be taken to have sets
S
of a fixed size
\ell
or
\leq\ell/10
. Such
distributions
of oracles can be easily distinguished by an
{\mathsf{AM}}
protocol
[
20
]
, but it appears unlikely that solving the actual spectral Forrelation problem can be done in
{\mathsf{AM}}
, because another way to sample “NO” instances of the spectral Forrelation problem would be to re-sample sets
S^{\prime}
independent of
U
. Distinguishing such pairs from Forrelated pairs
(S,U)
seems to truly require a
{\mathsf{QMA}}
verifier.

In contrast, the problem we construct really is in
{\mathsf{AM}}
, as it directly involves distinguishing between large and small sets, and any reasonable variant of the code intersection problem would likely remain in the polynomial hierarchy. Of course, an oracle separation between
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
and
{\mathsf{AM}}
already exists (and in fact, comes from early work on separating
{\mathsf{QMA}}
from
{\mathsf{QCMA}}

[
19
]
!), but we find it intriguing that our separation relies on a problem which appears to be of only intermediate difficulty and does not need the “full” power of
{\mathsf{QMA}}
in some sense. In fact, as our problem appears on its face to be completely unrelated to quantum algorithms, we believe it remains an extremely interesting question to find a
{\mathsf{QMA}}
-complete variant of the code intersection problem.

3.3
Simplifications to the separation

Naturally, one may ask if our separations can be made
even simpler
; here, we outline a few directions to consider.

On Round-Reduction Arguments.

The work of
[
7
]
can be thought of as a round-reduction argument as follows: in the style of
[
2
]
, we will, given any
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
proof system, fix some classical witness which corresponds to the largest set of NO instances. The polynomial bound on the size of the proof means that the collection of functions
H
which are consistent with this witness remains substantial, and in particular the distribution over all consistent
H
must have large min-entropy. We conclude that the set of symbols
S
which have low entropy conditioned on this classical proof is also bounded.

We now look at the verifier’s inputs to the oracle in the first round of queries. Observe that any inputs corresponding to non-codewords, as well as ones which have low overlap with
S
will be correct with negligible probability. By the hybrid lemma, it suffices to restrict our attention to codewords which have high overlap with
S
(so called “dangerous” inputs) – but this set is bounded precisely by the list recovery of our code! At this stage, we can simply give away the values of
H
on dangerous inputs
for free
, allowing the verifier to simulate its first round of queries. As a consequence, we can peel off a round of queries at the cost of requiring more advice. Repeating this “peeling and bloating” routine with FRS codes results in a
o(\log n/\log\log n)
-round bound.

Sadly, this approach seems fairly doomed if we stick to the Yamakawa-Zhandry problem: even with nearly optimal list recovery, our advice will certainly increase by some constant factor with each round, so we would remain stuck at an
o(n)
-round bound. One could imagine a better argument that uses some special property of any collection of recovered codewords that ensures these lists do not grow by too much iteratively, but this seems quite difficult (and would definitely be much more complicated!).

Other Codes?

One might wonder why we need multiplicity codes here – after all, there are other codes which give rise to unbalanced expanders besides those of
[
23
]
, namely the constructions of
[
22
]
, but examining these constructions in closer detail presents some unexpected issues:

1.

[
22
]
construct two unbalanced expanders with near-optimal expansion, one based on Parvaresh-Vardy codes and the other based on a subcode of FRS codes. Alas, neither of these instantiations are linear, which means our quantum proof/advice-based algorithm will fail.

2.

[
22
]
also considers unbalanced expanders based on plain FRS codes, which
are
linear in some sense, but such expanders have expansion which is too weak to show classical hardness.

On the Necessity of Near-Optimal List Recovery.

Our proof relies on fairly strong expansion/list recovery properties of the underlying code
C
. It is not hard to see that we can tolerate slightly suboptimal expansion, i.e. if
s(\ell)=\Omega(\ell/\mathrm{poly}(n))
. Extending our sampling-based argument to work for polynomial or super-polynomial expansion, i.e.
s(\ell)=\ell^{O(1)}
, would open the door to a much larger class of usable codes.

What About Random Linear Codes?

It is well known that random linear codes (RLCs) and their duals have good distance with high probability, and they are by definition linear, which means our quantum proof/advice-based algorithm will succeed. However, to show classical hardness, we need near-optimal list recovery, which remains a challenging open problem to show for RLCs
[
26
]
. We believe that a proof of such a result for RLCs is the clearest way to conceptually simplify our separation.

Theorem 4.20

(
[
15
]
)
.

Let
G:[N]\to\{0,1\}
be a random function. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an unbounded-time algorithm, with
S

. For an index

Lemma 4.21
.

Let
G:\{0,1\}^{\lambda}\to\{0,1\}
be a uniformly random function. For an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that makes
Q(\lambda)=\mathrm{poly}(\lambda)
quantum queries to
G
and a family of

-bit classical advice

\Pr_{G,x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{n}}[\mathcal{A}^{G}(z_{G},x)=G(x)]>\frac{3}{5}.

\frac{1}{4000Q^{2}}
fraction of

, measuring a random query of
\mathcal{A}^{G}
(for randomly sampled

with probability at least
\frac{1}{3200Q^{2}}
.

Proof.

The proof closely follows
[
27
]
, but considers quantum queries instead of classical ones. The only way for
\mathcal{A}
to distinguish
G
from
G|^{x}
is to have nontrivial query mass at

. Denote by

the total query mass that

is placed by

when querying

and

|\Pr[\mathcal{A}^{G}(z_{G},x)=G(x)]-\Pr[\mathcal{A}^{G|^{x}}(z_{G},x)=G(x)]|\leq 4\sqrt{QM_{G,x}}.

to output

\displaystyle\Pr_{G,x}[\mathcal{A}^{G|^{x}}(z_{G},x)=G(x)]\leq\frac{1}{2}+O\left(\frac{(t+\lambda)Q}{2^{\lambda}}\right)^{1/3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\implies\Pr_{G,x}[\mathcal{A}^{G}(z_{G},x)=G(x)]-\Pr_{G,x}[\mathcal{A}^{G|^{x}}(z_{G},x)=G(x)]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{10}-\mathrm{negl}(\lambda).

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.30005pt}_{G,x}[M_{G,x}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathop{\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.30005pt}_{G,x}\left[\frac{1}{16Q}\left(\Pr[\mathcal{A}^{G}(z_{G},x)=G(x)]-\Pr[\mathcal{A}^{G|^{x}}(z_{G},x)=G(x)]\right)^{2}\right]

by Jensen’s inequality. Finally, by a Markov inequality, we see that

\Pr_{x}\left[\mathop{\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.30005pt}_{G}[M_{G,x}]\geq\frac{1}{3200Q}\right]\geq\frac{1}{3200Q^{2}}-\mathrm{negl}(\lambda).

Thus, for sufficiently large
\lambda
, for a

fraction of

, measuring a random query of
\mathcal{A}^{G}
(for randomly sampled

) will produce
x
with probability at least

.
∎

Definition 5.3

(Trace over a finite field
[
39
]
)
.

For any prime power
q=r^{m}
where
r

\Tr(x):=\sum_{i=0}^{m}x^{r^{i}}
which maps elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to
\mathbb{F}_{r}
. The trace function is
\mathbb{F}_{r}
-linear: for all
a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{r}
and
x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}

\Tr(ax+ by)=a\Tr(x)+b\Tr(y)

\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\omega_{r}^{\Tr(x \cdot y)}=0
, where
\omega_{r}:=e^{2\pi i/r}

Definition 5.4

(Quantum Fourier transform over a finite field
[
39
]
)
.

For a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
where
q=r^{m}
and
r
is prime, the quantum Fourier transform over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is the unitary denoted by
\mathrm{QFT}_{q}
such that for any

\mathrm{QFT}_{q}\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\omega_{r}^{\Tr(x \cdot z)}\ket{z}.

The QFT over an alphabet
\Sigma=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{s}
is the
s
-wise tensor product of
\mathrm{QFT}_{q}
: for
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s})\in\Sigma
,

\mathrm{QFT}_{\Sigma}\ket{\x}:=\mathrm{QFT}^{\otimes s}_{q}\ket{x_1}\ldots\ket{x_s}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\Sigma|}}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\Sigma}\omega_{r}^{\Tr(\x\cdot\z)}\ket{\z}.

Similarly, for any positive integer
n
and
\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma^{n}
, we have

\mathrm{QFT}_{\Sigma}^{\otimes n}\ket{\x}=\frac{1}{|\Sigma|^{n/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\Sigma^{n}}\omega_{r}^{\Tr(\x\cdot\z)}\ket{\z}.

The unitary
\mathrm{QFT}_{q}
can be approximated within error
\varepsilon
in operator norm in time
\mathrm{poly}(\log q,\log 1/\varepsilon)

[
16
,
37
]
.

Definition 5.5

(Fourier transform of a function
[
39
]
)
.

For functions
f,g:\Sigma^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
, we define

\widehat{f}(\mathbf{z}):=\frac{1}{|\Sigma|^{n/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma^{n}}f(\mathbf{x})\omega_{r}^{\Tr(\x\cdot\z)},\hskip 10.0pt(f\cdot g)(\mathbf{x}):=f(\mathbf{x})\cdot g(\mathbf{x}),\hskip 5.0pt\text{and}\hskip 5.0pt(f\star g)(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\Sigma^{n}}f(\mathbf{y})\cdot g(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}).

Note that

\mathrm{QFT}_{\Sigma}^{\otimes n}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma^{n}}f(\mathbf{x})\ket{\x}=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\Sigma^{n}}\widehat{f}(\mathbf{z})\ket{\z}.

Fact 5.6

(
[
39
]
)
.

The following properties hold for the Fourier transform:

1.

(Parseval’s equality) For all functions
f:\Sigma^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
,
\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma^{n}}|f(\mathbf{x})|^{2}=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\Sigma^{n}}|\widehat{f}(\mathbf{z})|^{2}
.

2.

(Pointwise transform) Suppose that we have
f_{i}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
for
i\in[n]
and

is defined by
f(\mathbf{x}):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x_{i})
. Then
\widehat{f}(\mathbf{z})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{f}_{i}(z_{i})
.

3.

(Convolution theorem) For all functions
f,g,h:\Sigma^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
,
\widehat{f\cdot g}=\frac{1}{|\Sigma|^{n/2}}(\widehat{f}\star\widehat{g})
,
\widehat{f\star g}=|\Sigma|^{n/2}(\widehat{f}\cdot\widehat{g})
, and
\widehat{f\cdot(g\star h)}=(\widehat{f}\star(\widehat{g}\cdot\widehat{h}))
.

Lemma 5.7

(Fourier transform of a linear code)
.

Let
C\subseteq\Sigma^{n}=(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{s})^{n}
be any
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear code. Then,

f(\mathbf{u})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|C|}}&\text{if $\mathbf{u}\in C$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\hskip 5.0pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 5.0pt\widehat{f}(\mathbf{u})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|C^{\perp}|}}&\text{if $\mathbf{u}\in C^{\perp}$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Since
C

\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear,

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(\mathbf{z})=\frac{1}{|\Sigma|^{n/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\Sigma^{n}}f(\mathbf{u})\omega_{r}^{\Tr(\bfu\cdot\z)}=\frac{1}{|\Sigma|^{n/2}}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in C}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|C|}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|C^{\perp}|}}.

Finally,
\widehat{f}(\mathbf{z})=0

by Parseval’s equality. The reverse direction follows by an identical argument.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\ket{\psi}

By the reverse/inverse triangle inequality,

We can thus define the normalized state

\|\ket{\psi}-\ket{\phi'}\|\leq\|\ket{\psi}-\ket{\phi}\|+\|\ket{\phi}-\ket{\phi'}\|\leq\varepsilon+|\|\ket{\phi}\|-1|\leq 2\varepsilon.\qed

Claim 5.13

\mathop{\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.30005pt}_{H_{i}\leftarrow\mathsf{Bias}_{p,\Sigma}}[|\widehat{W}_{i}^{H_{i},b_{i}}(0)|^{2}]=\begin{cases}p&\text{if $b_{i}=1$}\\
1-p&\text{if $b_{i}=0$}\\
\end{cases}\hskip 10.0pt\text{and}\hskip 10.0pt\mathop{\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.30005pt}_{H_{i}\leftarrow\mathsf{Bias}_{p,\Sigma}}[|\widehat{W}_{i}^{H_{i},b_{i}}(\sigma)|^{2}]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}\hskip 1.30005pt}_{H_{i}\leftarrow\mathsf{Bias}_{p,\Sigma}}[|\widehat{W}_{i}^{H_{i},b_{i}}(\sigma^{\prime})|^{2}].

We can directly compute the expected Fourier weight on

Appendix A
Duals of Multiplicity Codes

For a field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the multiplicity
\mathsf{mult}(f,\alpha)
of a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
at a point
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
is the largest integer
m
so that
f^{(i)}(\alpha)=0
for any non-negative integer
i<m
. The multiplicity Schwartz-Zippel Lemma from
[
17
]
says that a nonzero degree
k
univariate polynomial can vanish on at most
k
points, counting multiplicities.

Lemma A.1

(
[
17
]
)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
be a nonzero polynomial of degree at most
k
. Then
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathsf{mult}(f,\alpha)\leq k
.

Fact A.2

(Hasse derivatives, see
[
10
]
)
.

The following properties hold for the Hasse derivative:

1.

For any polynomial
f(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
, integer
i\geq 0
, and point
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
f^{(i)}(\alpha)
is the coefficient of
X^{i}
in
f(X+\alpha)
.

2.

(Linearity) For any
f,g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
,
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and
i\geq 0
,
(\lambda\cdot f+\mu\cdot g)^{(i)}=\lambda\cdot f^{(i)}+\mu\cdot g^{(i)}
.

3.

(Product rule) For any
f,g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
and
i\geq 0
, we have
(f\cdot g)^{(i)}=\sum_{k=0}^{i}f^{(k)}\cdot g^{(i-k)}
.

We first derive a natural analogue of Lagrange interpolation for the setting of Hasse derivatives.

Lemma A.3

(Hermite interpolation)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a field,
s\geq 1
be a positive integer, and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
be distinct points in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. For
i\in[n]
and
0\leq j\leq s-1
, define
\mu_{i}(X):=\prod_{i^{\prime}\neq i}(X-\alpha_{i^{\prime}})^{s}
and
\eta_{i}(X):=(\mu_{i}(X))^{-1}\bmod{(X-\alpha_{i})^{s}}
.

Then, for all
f(X)\in(\mathbb{F}_{q})_{<sn}[X]
, we can write
f(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}A_{i,j}(X)f^{(j)}(\alpha_{i})
, where

\displaystyle A_{i,j}(X)=\mu_{i}(X)(X-\alpha_{i})^{j}\sum_{t=0}^{s-1-j}\eta_{i}^{(t)}(\alpha_{i})(X-\alpha_{i})^{t}.

Proof.

We begin by showing that for any
i,i^{\prime}\in[n]
and
0\leq j,j^{\prime}\leq s-1
,
A_{i,j}^{(j^{\prime})}(\alpha_{i^{\prime}})=1
if
(i,j)=(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
and
0
otherwise. First, if
i^{\prime}\neq i
, then
A_{i,j}(X+\alpha_{i^{\prime}})=X^{s}\cdot B_{i,j}(X)
for some polynomial
B_{i,j}(X)
so
A_{i,j}^{(j^{\prime})}(\alpha_{i^{\prime}})=0
for all
j,j^{\prime}
. Similarly, since
A_{i,j}(X+\alpha_{i})=X^{j}\cdot C_{i,j}(X)
for some polynomial
C_{i,j}(X)
,
A_{i,j}^{(j^{\prime})}(\alpha_{i})=0
whenever
i=i^{\prime}
and
j^{\prime}<j
. It thus remains to consider
i=i^{\prime}
and
j\leq j^{\prime}
. By the product rule, we know that

\displaystyle A_{i,j}^{(j^{\prime})}(\alpha_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{j^{\prime}}\mu_{i}^{(j^{\prime}-k)}(\alpha_{i})\cdot\left[\sum_{t=0}^{s-1-j}\eta_{i}^{(t)}(\alpha_{i})(X-\alpha_{i})^{j+t}\right]^{(k)}(\alpha_{i})=\sum_{k=j}^{j^{\prime}}\mu_{i}^{(j^{\prime}-k)}(\alpha_{i})\cdot\eta_{i}^{(k-j)}(\alpha_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{j^{\prime}-j}\mu_{i}^{(j^{\prime}-j-\ell)}(\alpha_{i})\cdot\eta_{i}^{(\ell)}(\alpha_{i})=(\mu_{i}\cdot\eta_{i})^{(j^{\prime}-j)}(\alpha_{i}).

By construction,
(\mu_{i}\cdot\eta_{i})(X)=1+h(X)\cdot(X-\alpha_{i})^{s}
for some polynomial
h(X)
, so
\mu_{i}(X+\alpha_{i})\cdot\eta_{i}(X+\alpha_{i})=1+X^{s}\cdot h(X+\alpha_{i})
. As
0\leq j^{\prime}-j\leq s-1
, we conclude that
(\mu_{i}\cdot\eta_{i})^{(j^{\prime}-j)}(\alpha_{i})
equals 1 if
j^{\prime}=j
and 0 otherwise.

Now, let
g(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}A_{i,j}(X)f^{(j)}(\alpha_{i})
. Note that since
\deg A_{i,j},\deg f\leq sn-1
, we know that
\deg(g-f)\leq sn-1
. But for any
i^{\prime}\in[n]
and
0\leq j^{\prime}\leq s-1
, we have that

(g-f)^{(j^{\prime})}(\alpha_{i^{\prime}})=g^{(j^{\prime})}(\alpha_{i^{\prime}})-f^{(j^{\prime})}(\alpha_{i^{\prime}})=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}A_{i,j}^{(j^{\prime})}(\alpha_{i^{\prime}})f^{(j)}(\alpha_{i})\right]-f^{(j^{\prime})}(\alpha_{i^{\prime}})=f^{(j^{\prime})}(\alpha_{i^{\prime}})-f^{(j^{\prime})}(\alpha_{i^{\prime}})=0.

Thus, by the multiplicity Schwartz-Zippel Lemma (
Appendix

A
),
(g-f)(X)=0
and so
f(X)=g(X)
.
∎

Definition A.4

(Generalized multiplicity codes)
.

For invertible matrices
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{s\times s}
, define the generalized multiplicity (GM) code
\mathsf{GM}_{s,\mathbb{F}_{q}}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n};\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n};k):=\{(U_{1}\cdot c_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\cdot c_{n}):c\in\mathsf{Mult}_{s,\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n};k)\}
.

Note that GM codes have distance at least
n-\frac{k-1}{s}
by
Appendix

A
.

Theorem A.5

(Duality of GM codes)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a field, and
s\geq 1
be a positive integer, and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
be distinct points in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Then there exist invertible matrices
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{s\times s}
, so that for any positive integer
k<sn
,

\mathsf{Mult}_{s,\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n};k)=\mathsf{GM}_{s,\mathbb{F}_{q}}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n};\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n};sn-k)^{\perp}.

Proof.

Consider any pair of polynomials
f(X)\in(\mathbb{F}_{q})_{<k}[X]
and
g(X)\in(\mathbb{F}_{q})_{<sn-k}[X]
. Let
h(X):=f(X)\cdot g(X)
, and note that
h(X)
has degree at most
sn−2
. By
Appendix

A
, there exist polynomials
A_{i,j}(X)
such that

h(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}A_{i,j}(X)h^{(j)}(\alpha_{i}).

Letting
a_{i,j}
denote the coefficient of
X^{sn-1}
in
A_{i,j}(X)
, we see that the coefficient of
X^{sn-1}
in
h(X)
is

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}a_{i,j}h^{(j)}(\alpha_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}a_{i,j}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}f^{(\ell)}(\alpha_{i})g^{(j-\ell)}(\alpha_{i})=0,

by the product rule and the fact that
\deg h\leq sn-2
. Consider the following anti-triangular matrices:

U_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{i,0}&a_{i,1}&\cdots&a_{i,s-1}\\
a_{i,1}&\adots&\adots&0\\
\vdots&\adots&0&\vdots\\
a_{i,s-1}&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{s\times s}.

If we define
f_{i}:=(f^{(0)}(\alpha_{i}),\ldots,f^{(s-1)}(\alpha_{i}))\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{s}
and
g_{i}:=(g^{(0)}(\alpha_{i}),\ldots,g^{(s-1)}(\alpha_{i}))\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{s}
, we see that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle f_{i},U_{i}\cdot g_{i}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}^{T}\cdot U_{i}\cdot g_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}a_{i,j}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}f^{(\ell)}(\alpha_{i})g^{(j-\ell)}(\alpha_{i})=0.

We claim that
U_{i}
are invertible. To see this, note that by
Appendix

A
, for any
i\in[n]
,

A_{i,s-1}(X)=\mu_{i}(X)(X-\alpha_{i})^{s-1}\eta_{i}(\alpha_{i})=\eta_{i}(\alpha_{i})(X-\alpha_{i})^{s-1}\prod_{i^{\prime}\neq i}(X-\alpha_{i^{\prime}})^{s},

so
a_{i,s-1}=\eta_{i}(\alpha_{i})
. As
\mu_{i}\cdot\eta_{i}\equiv 1\bmod{(X-\alpha_{i})^{s}}
,
\mu_{i}(\alpha_{i})\cdot\eta_{i}(\alpha_{i})=1
and thus
a_{i,s-1}\neq 0
. Consequently,

\det(U_i)=a_{i,s-1}^{s}\cdot\det(J_s)=a_{i,s-1}^{s}\cdot(-1)^{s(s-1)/2}\neq 0,

where
J_{s}
is the
s\times s
reversal/exchange matrix. Thus, we have shown that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\mathtt{Enc}_{C}(f)_{i},\mathtt{Enc}_{C^{\prime}}(g)_{i}\rangle=0
for all
f
and
g
, where
C:=\mathsf{Mult}_{s,\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n};k)
and
C^{\prime}:=\mathsf{GM}_{s,\mathbb{F}_{q}}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n};\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n};sn-k)
. We conclude that
C=(C^{\prime})^{\perp}
as both
C
and
(C^{\prime})^{\perp}
are vector spaces of dimension
k
.
∎

Corollary A.6

(
Theorem

4.19
)
.

For all parameters
s
,
q
, and
k<sq
,
(\mathsf{Mult}_{s,\mathbb{F}_{q},k})^{\perp}
has distance at least
\frac{k+1}{s}
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

A.5
,
\mathrm{dist}((\mathsf{Mult}_{s,\mathbb{F}_{q},k})^{\perp})=\mathrm{dist}(\mathsf{GM}_{s,\mathbb{F}_{q}}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{q};1,\ldots,q;sq-k))\geq q-\frac{sq-k-1}{s}=\frac{k+1}{s}
.
∎

Appendix B

Proof of
Theorem

6.8
.

The proof is nearly identical to that of
[
12
]
, but we include it for completeness. Let
\mathcal{O}∶\{0,1\}^{*}\to\{0,1\}
be an oracle and let
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
be the restriction to
\lambda^{7}
-bit inputs, where the lower threshold is
\lambda_{0}
(per
Section

6
). We define the unary (promise) language
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{O}}
so that
1^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{O}}
precisely when
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
is a YES instance and
1^{\lambda}\notin\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{O}}
precisely when
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
is a NO instance.

We consider only oracles
\mathcal{O}
such that each restriction to size
\lambda^{7}
-bit inputs encodes either a YES or NO instance, and so the containment
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{O}}\in{\mathsf{QMA}}^{\mathcal{O}}
follows from
Section

6.1
(as we can hardcode the values of
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{O}}
on all inputs of length at most
\lambda_{0}^{7}
). Showing that
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{O}}\in{\mathsf{AM}}^{\mathcal{O}}
for all
\mathcal{O}
is simple: Arthur samples
x\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
and Merlin responds with any
\mathbf{v}\in\Sigma^{q}
such that
(x\|0^{q-\lambda},\mathbf{v})\in R_{C_{\lambda},H}
(which always exists as
H\in\mathsf{Good}
). Arthur accepts iff
\mathcal{O}(x,\mathbf{v})=1
. Completeness and soundness follow essentially immediately.

Now we prove the lower bound for
{\mathsf{QCMA}}
machines. Let
M_{1},M_{2},\ldots
be an enumeration of all possible Turing machines. Identify any surjective function
\iota∶\mathbb{N}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{N}^{2}
and define functions
j,a∶\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{N}
by
(j(\kappa),a(\kappa))=\iota(\kappa)
. Define
F∶\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
so that
F(a)
is the minimum value such that for all
\lambda\geq F(a)
, any
Q(\lambda)\leq a\lambda^{a}
query algorithm with
t(\lambda)\leq a\lambda^{a}
-length classical witness must misclassify some
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
. By
Section

6.2
, for every integer
a
,
F(a)
is well-defined. We identify integers
n_{1},n_{2},\ldots
where the oracles will be defined to be nonzero. Define integers
n_{1}:=1+F(a(1)),n_{\kappa}:=1+\max\{F(a(\kappa)),a(\kappa-1)(n_{\kappa-1})^{a(\kappa-1)}\}
. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots\}
, let
\mathcal{O}
equal 0 everywhere. For these input lengths,
\mathcal{O}_{n}
is trivially a NO instance.

For each
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
, run
M_{j(\kappa)}
on input
1^{n_{\kappa}}
for
a(\kappa)n_{\kappa}^{a(\kappa)}
steps and interpret its output as a quantum query circuit
\mathcal{A}_{n_{\kappa}}
which accepts a classical witness. For every query that
\mathcal{A}_{n_{\kappa}}
makes of length
<n_{\kappa}
, use the previously generated definitions of the oracle
\mathcal{O}
to hardcode these answers. For queries
\mathcal{A}_{n_{\kappa}}
makes of length
>n_{\kappa}
, replace the oracle gates with identity circuits. The resulting circuit will be
\mathcal{B}_{n_{\kappa}}
, which only makes queries of length
n_{\kappa}
. This new algorithm
\mathcal{B}_{n_{\kappa}}
can be used to derive an oracle
\mathcal{O}_{n_{\kappa}}
by applying
Section

6.2
.

It remains to prove that no
{\mathsf{QCMA}}^{\mathcal{O}}
algorithm exists. Assume, for contradiction, that there exists a
{\mathsf{P}}
-uniform family of oracle circuits
\{\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}\}
that solves the code intersection problem with witnesses of length
t(\lambda)=\mathrm{poly}(\lambda)
and
Q(\lambda)=\mathrm{poly}(\lambda)
queries. Then,
\{\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}\}
appears in the Turing machine enumeration as some
M_{j^{*}}
and there exists some
a^{*}
such that
t(\lambda),Q(\lambda)\leq a^{*}\lambda^{a^{*}}
. As
\iota
is a surjection, there exists a
\kappa^{*}
such that
\iota(\kappa^{*})=(j^{*},a^{*})
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{n_{\kappa^{*}}}
be the quantum circuit for inputs of length
n_{\kappa^{*}}
. Since the oracle is defined as being 0 for inputs
\notin\{n_{1},n_{2},\ldots\}
and
n_{\kappa^{*}+1}>a^{*}n_{\kappa^{*}}^{a^{*}}
, each query gate for inputs of length
>n_{\kappa^{*}}
is an identity gate. Thus the circuit
\mathcal{B}_{n_{\kappa^{*}}}
has the exact same output as
\mathcal{A}_{n_{\kappa^{*}}}
on inputs of size
n_{\kappa^{*}}
. However, using
Section

6.2
, we constructed an oracle
\mathcal{O}_{n_{\kappa^{*}}}
that
\mathcal{B}_{n_{\kappa^{*}}}
will misclassify. Therefore,
\mathcal{A}_{n_{\kappa^{*}}}
will answer incorrectly on input
1^{n_{\kappa^{*}}}
, completing the proof.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

7.5
.

Our proof will closely follow
[
27
]
. Suppose that for each
\lambda
we generate
(G_{\lambda},\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{\lambda})\leftarrow\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
and define a language
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}:=\bigsqcup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}}G^{-1}_{\lambda}(1)
and an oracle
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
that returns
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{|x|}(x)
on a query
x\in\{0,1\}^{*}
. It suffices to show that
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}\in{\mathsf{BQP}}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}/\mathrm{qpoly}\cap{\mathsf{NP}}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}\cap\mathsf{coNP}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}\notin{\mathsf{BQP}}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}/\mathrm{poly}
with probability 1.

To see that
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}\in{\mathsf{BQP}}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}/\mathrm{qpoly}
with probability 1, observe that Item 1 of
Section

7
implies that there is a
{\mathsf{BQP}}
machine
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}
with polynomial-size quantum advice that decides
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}
on all
x
of length
\lambda
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}
for sufficiently large
\lambda
. As
\sum_{\lambda=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}<\infty
, the Borel–Cantelli lemma (
Section

4.2
) implies that
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}
decides
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}
for all but finitely many
\lambda
with probability 1. By hard-coding all
x
’s where
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{L}
disagree,
\mathcal{A}
can be modified into a
{\mathsf{BQP}}/\mathrm{qpoly}
machine
\mathcal{A}^{\prime\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}
that decides
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}
on all
x\in\{0,1\}^{*}
with probability 1.

In addition, for any
x\in\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
, we can give any
\mathbf{v}
where
(x\|0^{q-\lambda},\mathbf{v})\in R_{C_{\lambda},H}
to certify
G(x)
. Thus, by a Chernoff/union bound, we know that with probability
1-\mathrm{negl}(\lambda)
over
\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}
, there exists an
{\mathsf{NP}}/{\mathsf{coNP}}
certificate for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{\lambda}
. We conclude via an identical argument that
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}\in{\mathsf{NP}}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}\cap{\mathsf{coNP}}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}
with probability 1.

For a
{\mathsf{BQP}}
machine
\mathcal{B}
that takes
\mathrm{poly}(\lambda)
-bit classical advice, we define
S_{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda)
to be the event over the choice of
(G,\mathcal{O}^{\prime})
that there is a
\mathrm{poly}(\lambda)
-bit classical advice family
\{z_{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}\}_{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}
such that

\Pr[\forall x\in\{0,1\}^{\lambda},\mathcal{B}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}(z_{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}},x)=G(x)]\geq\frac{2}{3}.

By Item 2 of
Section

7
, there exists
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
{\mathsf{BQP}}
machines
\mathcal{B}
,
\Pr_{\mathcal{D}_{\lambda}}[S_{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda)]\leq\frac{9}{10}
for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
.

We will consider a sequence of input lengths
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots
defined by
\lambda_{i}:=T(\lambda_{i-1})+1
, where
T(\lambda)
is the running time of
\mathcal{B}
on input of length
\lambda
. This means that when
\mathcal{B}
’s input length is
\lambda_{i-1}
, it cannot query the oracle on input lengths
\geq\lambda_{i}
, so it must be the case that

\displaystyle\Pr[S_{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda_{i})|S_{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda_{0})\land\ldots\land S_{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda_{i-1})]

\displaystyle=\Pr[S_{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda_{i})]

\displaystyle\implies\Pr[S_{\mathcal{B}}(1)\land S_{\mathcal{B}}(2)\land\ldots]\leq\Pr\left[\bigwedge_{i=0}^{\infty}S_{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{\infty}\Pr[S_{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda_{i})|S_{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda_{0})\land\ldots\land S_{\mathcal{B}}(\lambda_{i-1})]\leq\prod_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{9}{10}=0.

But there are countably many
{\mathsf{BQP}}
machines, so
\Pr[\exists\mathcal{B}:S_{\mathcal{B}}(1)\land S_{\mathcal{B}}(2)\land\ldots]=0
. We conclude that
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}\notin{\mathsf{BQP}}^{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}/\mathrm{poly}
with probability 1 over the choice of
(G,\mathcal{O}^{\prime})
, as desired.
∎
