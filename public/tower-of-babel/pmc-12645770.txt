Associations between estimated glucose disposal rate and hyperuricemia in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Dou Tang, Ping Gao, Wei Xie, Sujie Wang, Xun Wang, Leiqun Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) is a novel surrogate for insulin resistance (IR). While it has shown potential in cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes, its relationship with hyperuricemia remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to explore the association between eGDR and hyperuricemia in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).MethodsA total of 1,238 patients with T2DM were included in this cross-sectional study. eGDR was calculated using waist circumference, hypertension diagnosis, and HbA1c values measured at baseline. Logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between eGDR and hyperuricemia. Stratified and interaction analyses were performed to confirm the reliability of the results.ResultsThe prevalence of hyperuricemia was 29.7% in our study. After adjusting for potential confounders, each standard deviation (SD) increase in eGDR was associated with 17% lower odds of hyperuricemia (OR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.72–0.95, P < 0.001). This significant negative association between eGDR and hyperuricemia remained consistent when eGDR was analyzed in tertiles. Subgroup analyses showed that lower eGDR levels were associated with higher odds of hyperuricemia across all subgroups, with no significant interaction effects.ConclusionseGDR was inversely associated with hyperuricemia in Chinese patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04601-6.

Introduction

Hyperuricemia, resulting from dysregulation in purine metabolism, represents a significant and escalating global public health burden [1]. Extensive epidemiologic evidence has established that hyperuricemia is associated with serious clinical outcomes, including accelerated incidence and progression of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), metabolic syndrome, chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, and elevated all-cause mortality [2, 3]. In mainland China, the prevalence of hyperuricemia was estimated at 16.4% based on a systematic review and meta-analysis of a total population of 2,277,712 from 2000 to 2019. This significant increase is mainly due to changes in diet and lifestyle [4]. Notably, individuals with diabetes exhibit a higher incidence of hyperuricemia compared to the general population [5–7]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of African populations reported a pooled hyperuricemia prevalence of 27.28% among type 2 diabetes patients [6]. Similarly, a regional study in China documented a prevalence of 21.24% in this population [7]. Consequently, identifying modifiable risk factors for hyperuricemia is crucial for developing targeted prevention and management strategies.

Accumulating evidence strongly implicates metabolic factors, particularly insulin resistance (IR), in the development and progression of hyperuricemia [8, 9]. While the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp technique remains the gold standard for IR assessment, its clinical utility is limited by procedural complexity, invasiveness, and high cost. These limitations have driven the search for practical surrogate indices. eGDR does not require fasting blood samples or insulin measurement. It is calculated using easily obtainable clinical parameters, including waist circumference (WC), blood pressure, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), which makes it convenient, economical, and particularly suitable for large-scale epidemiological studies and risk stratification. The estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) has emerged as a validated, simple, and cost-effective surrogate marker for IR [10, 11]. Unlike traditional indices such as HOMA-IR, eGDR does not require fasting blood samples or insulin measurement. It is calculated using three routinely available clinical parameters: waist circumference as a measure of central obesity, hypertension status as an important cardiometabolic risk factor, and glycated hemoglobin reflecting chronic glycemic control. This simplicity and reliance on easily obtainable clinical data make eGDR convenient, economical, and particularly suitable for large-scale epidemiological studies and risk stratification [12, 13]. Previous studies demonstrate a strong correlation between eGDR and the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp technique, with lower eGDR values indicating more severe IR [14, 15].

Recent clinical research has linked lower eGDR to increased risks of cardiovascular disease [16], stroke [11], impaired cognitive function [17], physical frailty [18], diabetes complications [10, 19, 20], and all-cause mortality [21]. Nevertheless, the specific relationship between eGDR and hyperuricemia remains scarce. Given the established association between hyperuricemia and IR [8, 9], and the role of eGDR as a validated surrogate for IR, we hypothesize a negative association between eGDR and hyperuricemia. However, no study has directly examined this relationship, despite the higher prevalence of hyperuricemia in patients with T2DM.Therefore, this cross-sectional study aimed to investigate the association between eGDR and hyperuricemia in patients with T2DM.

Methods

Study participants

A total of 1,264 inpatients diagnosed with T2DM were retrospectively enrolled from the Department of Endocrinology at RuiJin Hospital Lu Wan Branch between March 2022 and March 2025. Diagnosis of T2DM was established using the 2020 American Diabetes Association criteria [22]. Participants were excluded based on the following criteria: missing serum uric acid measurements (n = 12), incomplete parameters for eGDR calculation (n = 22), and age under 18 years (n = 1). After applying these exclusions, 1,238 eligible T2DM patients were included in the final analysis (supplementary Fig. 1). The study protocol received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of RuiJin Hospital Lu Wan Branch, Shanghai Jiaotong University School of Medicine, in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Clinical and biochemical analysis

All participants completed structured interviews by trained research staff, including gender, age, duration of diabetes mellitus, smoking status (never, former, or current), and medical history. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or self-reported history of hypertension. Coronary Heart Disease (CHD) status was ascertained through self-reported medical records. Anthropometric measurements, such as height, weight, and waist circumference, were obtained using standardized protocols. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as body weight (kg)/height2 (m2). Resting blood pressure measurements were performed after a 5-minute seated rest using electronic sphygmomanometers. Blood samples were collected for biochemical analysis after an overnight fast of at least eight hours. Fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), uric acid (UA), liver enzymes (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST]), and renal function parameters were quantified using standard laboratory methods. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) creatinine equation [23].

Definitions of the exposure and outcome variables

The eGDR was calculated using the following formula: eGDR (mg/kg/min) = 21.158 − 0.09 × waist circumference − 3.407 × hypertension − 0.551 × HbA1c, where waist circumference is measured in centimeters, hypertension is indicated as yes (1) or no (0), and HbA1c is expressed as a percentage [24]. Hyperuricemia was defined as a serum uric acid level ≥ 420 µmol/L in males or ≥ 360 µmol/L in females [25].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with normal distributions, as assessed by Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests and Q-Q plots, were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages). Comparisons between the hyperuricemia group and the non-hyperuricemia group were performed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. The chi-square test was used for categorical variables.

Univariate logistic regression assessed each variable’s effect on the risk of hyperuricemia. Associations between eGDR and hyperuricemia were examined through multivariable logistic regression analysis. The eGDR was evaluated both as a continuous variable and in categories divided into tertiles, using the lowest tertile as the reference group. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Three regression models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age and gender. Model 3 was additionally adjusted for the duration of diabetes, ALT, AST, serum creatinine, FBG, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. Covariate selection was based on clinical relevance, existing literature, or a ≥ 10% change in the effect estimate when added to the model [26]. Stratified and interaction analyses were conducted to examine the robustness of the association between eGDR and hyperuricemia across subgroups, with adjustment for relevant covariates. An exploratory analysis was additionally performed to assess whether eGDR could provide incremental information beyond conventional risk factors, using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and DeLong’s test. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 25.0), EmpowerStats, and the R statistical package (Version 4.2.0).

Results

Participants’ characteristics by hyperuricemia

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 1,238 patients with T2DM were included in the final analysis (Table 1). Among these, 870 patients (70.3%) were classified as non-hyperuricemia and 368 patients (29.7%) as hyperuricemia. Table 1 presents the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of participants stratified by hyperuricemia status. Compared to the non-hyperuricemia group, participants with hyperuricemia were significantly younger and exhibited larger waist circumference and higher BMI (all P < 0.05). The hyperuricemia group also displayed significantly altered lipid profiles, characterized by elevated triglycerides, total cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), alongside reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (all P < 0.001). Furthermore, this group demonstrated reduced eGFR, higher serum creatinine levels, and elevated liver enzymes (all P < 0.001). The eGDR was also significantly lower in the hyperuricemia group (P < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups regarding gender distribution, FBG, HbA1c, prevalence of coronary heart disease, smoking status, or alcohol consumption (all P > 0.05).

The results of logistic regression analysis on the association between eGDR and hyperuricemia risk

Table 3 presents the association between eGDR and hyperuricemia risk in patients with T2DM. In the unadjusted model (Model 1), each standard deviation (SD) increase in eGDR was associated with a 20% reduction in the odds of developing hyperuricemia (OR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.70–0.90, P < 0.001). This negative association strengthened after adjusting for age and gender in Model 2, where each SD increase in eGDR corresponded to a 24% reduced risk (OR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.67–0.87, P < 0.001). Further adjustment for diabetes duration, ALT, AST, serum creatinine, FBG, triglycerides, HDL-C, LDL-C, smoking status, and alcohol consumption in Model 3 revealed that each SD increase in eGDR remained significantly associated with a 17% reduction in hyperuricemia risk (OR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.72–0.95, P < 0.001). This demonstrates that higher eGDR is independently associated with a lower risk of hyperuricemia.

For sensitivity analysis, eGDR was categorized into tertiles. Participants in the highest eGDR tertile (T3) exhibited a significantly lower likelihood of hyperuricemia compared to those in the lowest tertile (T1). A significant dose-response relationship was observed across tertiles in Model 1 (P for trend < 0.001). In the fully adjusted Model 3, the association persisted, with the highest tertile (T3) showing 32% lower odds of hyperuricemia compared to the lowest tertile (T1) (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.48–0.95, P = 0.025), and the dose-response trend remained significant (P for trend = 0.025).

Subgroup analyses

Stratified analyses and interaction tests were performed to examine the association between eGDR and hyperuricemia across subgroups defined by age, gender, BMI categories, and smoking status (Fig. 1). Consistently negative associations between higher eGDR and reduced hyperuricemia risk were observed across all subgroups (all adjusted ORs < 1). Notably, no significant interaction effects were detected among these subgroups (all P for interaction > 0.05). These findings support a robust and independent negative association between eGDR and the risk of hyperuricemia across diverse population strata in this cross-sectional T2DM population.

ROC curve analysis of the value of the eGDR

To explore incremental value of eGDR over traditional risk factors, we compared models with and without its inclusion (Supplementary Fig. 2). The baseline model showed an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.721. Adding eGDR slightly increased the AUC to 0.726; however, the difference was not statistically significant (DeLong test, P = 0.249).

Discussion

Our study provides the first evidence of an association between eGDR, an alternative measure of IR, and hyperuricemia in patients with T2DM. After comprehensive adjustment for confounding factors, each SD increase in eGDR was associated with 17% lower odds of hyperuricemia. The robustness of this inverse relationship was further corroborated by subgroup and interaction analyses across diverse demographic strata, including age, gender, BMI, and smoking status. These findings extend the clinical utility of eGDR into the field of purine metabolism disorders, highlighting its value beyond established areas.

The assessment of IR is commonly performed using surrogate markers. The Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), derived from fasting blood glucose and insulin levels, is widely recognized as a surrogate for the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp technique [27], buy its application can be biased in patients receiving insulin therapy or insulin secretagogues. Recently, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and its variants, calculated from fasting triglycerides and glucose, have emerged as practical alternatives due to their cost-effectiveness and simplicity [15]. The eGDR, initially developed for type 1 diabetes mellitus epidemiological research [28, 29], has attracted growing attention as another surrogate of insulin sensitivity. Unlike HOMA-IR and TyG, eGDR does not rely on fasting blood samples or insulin measurement but instead uses routine clinical parameters (waist circumference, blood pressure, and HbA1c), making it particularly convenient for large-scale studies and metabolic assessment. Importantly, eGDR has been shown to correlate strongly with the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, the gold standard for IR measurement, thereby supporting its validity [10]. eGDR provides a more comprehensive evaluation of metabolic health, extending beyond glycemic parameters alone to capture a broader metabolic profile. Given this comprehensive nature, eGDR is well suited for investigating complex metabolic conditions such as hyperuricemia, which frequently coexists with obesity, hypertension, and insulin resistance. To our knowledge, this is the first study to identify this inverse association in patients with T2DM.

The link between IR and hyperuricemia is well-documented. Mazidi et al. demonstrated significant correlations between elevated serum uric acid levels and IR parameters, including HOMA-IR and the TyG index [30]. A cross-sectional study in a general Chinese population reported a positive linear association between the TyG index and hyperuricemia risk, with each standard deviation (SD) increase in TyG corresponding to a 54.1% higher hyperuricemia risk [31]. Furthermore, a nationwide cohort study indicated that various IR surrogates, including the TyG index, TyG with body mass index (TyG-BMI), triglyceride-to-high-density-lipoprotein-cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio, and metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR), exhibit comparable predictive capabilities for incident hyperuricemia [32]. Specifically, we demonstrated a significant negative correlation between eGDR and hyperuricemia. This association remained robust after extensive adjustment for multiple potential confounders. Moreover, subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, BMI, and smoking status, along with interaction tests, confirmed the consistent inverse relationship across diverse patient subgroups, further validating the robustness of our findings. An exploratory analysis showed that adding eGDR to conventional risk factors slightly increased the AUC from 0.721 to 0.726, though the change was not significant. This suggests that the added value of eGDR is modest and should be interpreted with caution, while indicating a direction for future prospective studies to assess its clinical utility. These results reinforce the reliability and applicability of eGDR in patients with T2DM and establish it as a novel composite measure significantly associated with hyperuricemia, highlighting its potential relevance for metabolic assessment in this population. Our findings support this existing evidence by demonstrating a novel and significant inverse association between eGDR, a comprehensive IR surrogate, and hyperuricemia prevalence in patients with T2DM. While recent research has strongly linked eGDR to cardiovascular diseases [16], stroke [11], kidney disease [33], and diabetic complications [10, 19, 20], the present study is the first to document its significant association within the context of purine metabolism disorders.

Hyperuricemia exhibits a high prevalence in patients with T2DM. As IR represents a central pathophysiological feature of T2DM, it has been widely investigated as a potential link between T2DM and elevated serum uric acid levels. Although numerous studies have demonstrated a strong association between hyperuricemia and IR, the causal direction and underlying mechanisms remain controversial and incompletely understood [8]. Previous clinical studies examining the relationship between IR and hyperuricemia have reported inconsistent findings. A longitudinal cohort study with a 5.3-year follow-up showed that elevated uric acid levels preceded the development of IR, implying a potential role of hyperuricemia [34]. Conversely, a bidirectional Mendelian randomization analysis supported a causal effect of hyperinsulinemia on hyperuricemia but found no evidence for the reverse direction [35], and a cross-sectional Mendelian randomization study in newly diagnosed T2DM patients reported no evidence that plasma uric acid causally affects IR [36]. Mechanistic studies further suggest a complex, potentially bidirectional relationship: insulin resistance may enhance renal urate reabsorption via increased expression of urate transporter 1 (URAT1) and glucose transporter 9 (GLUT9) [37, 38], while hyperuricemia can induce insulin resistance through oxidative stress, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction [8, 39, 40].

The components of eGDR, including waist circumference, hypertension, and HbA1c, are closely linked to uric acid pathophysiology are closely linked to uric acid pathophysiology. WC reflects central adiposity and visceral fat accumulation, which trigger systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, ultimately leading to elevated serum uric acid levels [41]. Hypertension increases renal vascular resistance and decreases glomerular filtration rate, which directly reduce urate excretion. In addition, activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) further enhances tubular urate reabsorption [42]. Chronic hyperglycemia, reflected by elevated HbA1c, often coexists with insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia, which enhance renal urate reabsorption. Moreover, hyperglycemia can trigger oxidative stress and inflammation, further contributing to hyperuricemia [38]. Based on these mechanisms, we suggest that eGDR, as an integrative marker of insulin sensitivity, may indicate metabolic states associated with uric acid regulation, offering potential insight into hyperuricemia pathways in type 2 diabetes.

This study has several limitations that need to be acknowledged. Firstly, due to the cross-sectional nature of our study, we could not establish definitive causal inference between eGDR and hyperuricemia. Secondly, despite employing multivariable regression models and stratified analyses to account for potential confounders, there may still be residual confounding effects from unmeasured or unknown variables. In particular, information on the subjects’ medication history, such as the use of antidiabetic, lipid-lowering, antihypertensive, or uric acid–lowering drugs, was not available. These medications may have acted as potential confounders and could have influenced the associations observed. Thirdly, given that the study population consisted of Chinese patients from a single center, caution should be advised when generalizing these findings to other populations. Future prospective cohort studies are essential to establish the temporal sequence of changes in eGDR and uric acid levels and to explore potential causal pathways. Furthermore, mechanistic investigations are needed to elucidate the biological pathways between insulin sensitivity, as captured by eGDR, and uric acid metabolism.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that lower eGDR is independently associated with hyperuricemia in patients with T2DM, with this inverse association consistently observed across all subgroups. These findings reinforce the established link between insulin resistance and hyperuricemia and highlight the potential of eGDR as a novel insulin resistance surrogate to assess and manage hyperuricemia in diabetic populations. Future prospective studies could develop combined models incorporating eGDR with other factors to further evaluate its clinical applicability and inform management strategies.
