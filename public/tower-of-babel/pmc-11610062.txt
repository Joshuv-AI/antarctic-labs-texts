Prevalence of neurocognitive deficits in patients with first-episode schizophrenia in an African sample and its relationship with dimensions of psychopathology and psychosocial outcome
Authors: Onyekachi Kingsley Ugwuonye, Justus Uchenna Onu, Theclar Ogochukwu Iyidobi, Jude Uzoma Ohaeri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrent evidence supports the idea that neurocognitive deficits (NCD) constitute a core dimension of schizophrenia. Studies on longitudinal changes in neurocognition among neuroleptic-naive first-episode schizophrenia (FES) from Africa are uncommon. We aimed to highlight the prevalence of, and changes in NCD among FES on naturalistic treatment follow-up for 8 weeks, and the relationship with psychopathological and psychosocial outcomes.MethodsConsecutive FES and Healthy Control (HC) were recruited. Diagnosis of schizophrenia was based on ICD-10 criteria. The HC (n = 86) consisted of nursing students in the same facility, recruited purposely to match the cases in age and gender. After the baseline assessment, 82 FES were followed up 4-weekly for changes in NCD, psychopathological and psychosocial ratings for a period of 8 weeks, using the 3 alternate forms of the Screen for Cognitive Impairment in Psychiatry (SCIP), the Wisconsin Card Test, the Mini Mental State Examination, as well as the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale, the WHO Disability Assessment Scale, and the Global Assessment of Functioning scale. The control group was tested only once, and their scores were utilized for comparison with the patients’ scores only at week 8. The prevalence of neurocognitive deficits in the two groups was described using percentages and 95% confidence interval. Predictors of cognitive function was determined using multivariate linear regression.ResultsThe prevalence of any NCD among FES at 8 weeks and HC was 62.9% (95% CI: 51.5%, 74.2%) and 1.2% (95% CI: 0.0%, 6.6%), respectively. With treatment, there was a significant improvement in cognitive function at each interval of follow-up. At week 8, the prevalence of any NCD among patients in remission was 55.1%. Total SCIP scores at week 8 had significant inverse moderate relationship with the dimensions of psychopathology. Conversely, total SCIP score was strongly positively correlated with functioning (rhos= 0.71, p < 0.001) at week 8. At week 8, the baseline predictors of total SCIP score were, duration of untreated psychosis (β = -0.33, p = 0.01, variance = 19.8%), negative symptoms (β = -0.35, p = 0.03, variance = 4.9%) and positive symptoms (β = -0.43, p = 0.01, variance = 4.8%).ConclusionThe high prevalence of NCD when patients were in remission indicates that they are enduring and merit consideration as a domain of psychopathology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-024-06315-9.

Introduction

Neurocognitive deficits (such as attention, processing speed and working memory) are thought to be a central feature of schizophrenia, present in over 80% of patients, and are major determinants of functional disability, the indirect costs of the disease, as well as targets for psychopharmacological intervention [1–3]. These deficits are experienced across a variety of cognitive domains, and the deficits are both general and domain specific [4, 5]. Their severity ranges from moderate to severe across several domains, including attention, working memory, verbal learning and memory, and executive functions [6].

Cognitive abilities have been extensively studied in patients with schizophrenia and there is a clear consensus on the presence of cognitive deficits [7]. There is also strong evidence that cognitive impairment is detectable before illness onset and may thus represent a useful marker for early detection and intervention [8]. There is still debate, however, on the course of cognitive deficits over time, especially whether they respond to antipsychotic treatment, either directly [8–10], or indirectly, by reducing the impact of disruptive psychotic symptoms with which they are associated (especially the disorganization syndrome) [11].However, over the past two decades, the focus on these deficits increased dramatically, with treatment studies, both pharmacological and behavioral, suggesting that cognitive deficits are malleable [11, 12].

Two large datasets involving over 63 studies spanning over 21 years showed robust evidence for significant impairments in first-episode psychosis across all domains of neurocognition, with more pronounced impairment in immediate verbal memory and processing speed [7, 13]. In other words, most studies agree with the finding that neurocognitive impairments are core to schizophrenia disorder [7, 13]. However, the interpretation of these results is complicated by the wide variations in methodologies used in the different studies [14, 15]. Important methodological issues include cross-sectional designs, inconsistencies in the operational definition and categorization of cognitive domains, the use of different sample populations (incident, prevalent, neuroleptic naïve/first-episode cases, whether patients were in stable condition or actively psychotic), and different instruments for the measurement of the phenotypes (general vs. domain specific instruments). In follow-up studies, there is also the issue of not addressing practice effects, i.e., the possibility that participants learn about test instruments on subsequent intervals of testing, and, therefore, could perform better, independent of the impact of treatment on the pathology of interest [16–18].

In addition, cognitive functioning and psychopathology have been described as independent but related dimensions with many authors reporting an inverse relationship with negative and disorganization symptoms dimension [19]. Of the dimensions that emerged in factor analyses using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), the disorganization factor (which includes difficulty in abstract thinking, poor attention, disorientation, stereotyped thinking and conceptual disorganization), shows the strongest association with cognitive test scores, but still only accounts for a small proportion of the variance [11]. Hence, it is reasonable to expect that an effective treatment of these psychotic symptoms could mitigate the severity of neurocognitive impairments, without necessarily achieving recovery from the cognitive deficits.

More research needs to be done to confirm these findings in Africa, a population that has shown a large genetic variety [20]. In other to contribute an African perspective to this field, we have sought to address the above methodological issues by: (i) using a test instrument with three alternative forms (the Screen for Cognitive Impairment in Psychiatry –SCIP), in order to mitigate the impact of practice effects; (ii) following up neuroleptic - naïve first episode cases with low intensity testing over a period of 8 weeks; (iii) comparing the test scores with those of a matched control group only when the patients had received adequate antipsychotic treatment and were in stable condition; and (iv) assessing the association of cognitive test scores with dimensions of psychopathology and psychosocial outcome. The aim of the study was to highlight the prevalence of, and changes in neurocognitive deficits in patients with first-episode schizophrenia on naturalistic treatment follow-up for 8 weeks, and assess the association of the dimensions of psychopathology and psychosocial outcome with neurocognitive test scores. The authors sought to provide answers to the following issues to advance knowledge and inclusivity in research on neurocognition in schizophrenia:

Do cognitive function tests differ between persons with first-episode schizophrenia at 8 weeks of treatment (i.e., when stable) and an age and gender-matched healthy control group?Do the patients’ cognitive test results change with time; and specifically, do they improve after eight weeks of naturalistic treatment follow-up?Is there a relationship between the performance in cognitive function tests and the dimensions of psychopathology and psychosocial outcome?

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a longitudinal study which took place in a Nigerian tertiary psychiatric hospital which has a bed capacity of about 320. The hospital provides mental health care services to all age groups and in the various branches of psychiatry including psychotherapy, occupational rehabilitation, and electroconvulsive therapy (ECT). Treatment of schizophrenia at the center is usually multidisciplinary and multimodal, and divided into three phases: initiation of treatment at first-episode, acute phase treatment, and promoting recovery. According to statistics from the records department, between 2010 and 2018, an average of 188 new cases of mental disorders were seen in this institution every month, which translates to about 47 new cases weekly or 12 new cases daily. Of this number, about 5 new cases daily had diagnosis of schizophrenia, with about 3 being admitted, giving about 90 new cases of schizophrenia admitted monthly. The control group was recruited from the school of psychiatric nursing situated within the hospital. It has a staff strength of about 100, of which, 70 are in the junior cadre. The school admits 30–40 students (as regular students) each year and admits 80–90 students (for a 2- month clinical experience) every two months. This translates to 110–130 students and 70 junior staff in the school at any point in time during an academic session.

Ethical considerations

Approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics and Research Committee of the institution, with approval number of REA/Vol. 11/317. International ethical norms and standards as stipulated in the Helsinki guideline were strictly adhered to; written informed consent was obtained from all the participants. Participants were free to withdraw from the study at any time, even after having consented initially, and this did not in any way affect the patient’s medical care or their academic endeavors. The participants were also informed that if in the process of the research, any previously undiagnosed clinical condition was found, the managing team would be appropriately informed. Information gathered in the course of the research was stored in an encrypted computer accessible only to the researcher.

Sample selection

In computing the sample size, the formula for comparison of two proportions was utilized [21]. Using 2.5%, which was the estimated difference in prevalence of cognitive deficit among schizophrenia subjects from a previous study [22], at 95% confidence interval, 5% margin of error and 10% attrition rate, a minimum sample size of 80 per group was adjudged to be adequate to test the study hypothesis. However, eventually 82 cases and 86 controls were enrolled.

Patients

Consecutive in-patients admitted at the acute admission wards, were recruited until the sample size was achieved. Only in-patients were involved because of the lengthy time of interview. Patients were eligible if they were above the age 18 years, in stable clinical condition, i.e., fully conscious, not drowsy, could follow the interview process, not requiring emergency treatment, had not received antipsychotics, and could easily read and understand a simple 2-paragraph text in English or Igbo. The requirement for reading is very important in tests of neurocognition, to ensure that subjects have at least average level of intelligence and are fully conscious, to enable them to follow the rather intellectual demands of the tests. Those with comorbid chronic physical or mental health conditions or who had received ECT in the past, were excluded.

The control group

The control group subjects were selected purposively to match the cases by age and gender. They were eligible if found to be physically and mentally healthy, without a family history of mental disorders in the first-degree relatives, using clinical assessment (i.e., history taking, physical and mental status examinations), and Mini International Neuropsychiatric Inventory-screen, and able to read and understand a simple 2-paragraph passage in English or Igbo. The exclusion criteria for the cases were similarly applied to the control group.

Data collection

During the initial stages of the study, JUO, a senior team member, trained the researchers involved in data collection on how to use all the questionnaires and other instruments. Once the researchers and the senior psychiatrist with experience using the instruments were able to achieve satisfactory inter-rater reliability, the researchers began the formal interviews for the study. As an illustration, the researchers and the supervisor alternated interviewing and grading the responses of 14 respondents, whose data were not used for the main study. Since the data were continuous, analysis of inter-rater reliability was by Intra-Class Correlation coefficient (ICC) for the Screen for Cognitive Impairment in Psychiatry (SCIP). Intra-class correlation coefficient was not suitable for the two items of the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST), first, because the method requires that there be at least three items, and second, the WCST-PE and WCST – CA are coded in different directions. Kendal’s tau correlation was used to assess the reliability (i.e., repeatability) of the two variables of the WCST because that measure takes chance agreements into consideration. Kendal’s tau correlation for the WCST-Categories (WCST –CA) was 0.87, while that for the WCST-Perseverative Errors (WCST-PE) was 0.93 (p < 0.001). The ICC for the SCIP was 0.73.

Data collection for the patient group

A total of 100 patients were approached. Five declined to join the study for no particular reason even after due explanation, three had co-morbid seizure disorders, another three had co-morbid substance use disorders, two could not read a simple passage in English or Igbo, three had extrapyramidal side effects, while two absconded from hospital after a few days on admission. The diagnostic interview for the 82 consenting subjects was conducted by OKU, using the criteria for schizophrenia in the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) schizophrenia (psychotic disorders) module at the emergency unit or within two weeks in the ward when the participants were clinically stable. This was followed by the administration of socio-demographic and clinical questionnaires. Before assessing neurocognition, the authors sought to exclude potential impediments to testing neurocognition (e.g., Extrapyramidal symptoms, color blindness, and intellectual disability) using, respectively, Simpson-Angus Extrapyramidal Side Effects Scale(SAS), simple test of color vision (by showing the patient the different colors, and requesting correct identification), and a simple two-paragraph passage (either in Igbo or English) that is accepted by education experts as being adequate for first school leavers. Thereafter, the neurocognitive function tests (SCIP, MMSE and WCST), basic psychopathological symptom severity rating (BPRS) and psychosocial outcome measures (WHODAS and GAF) were applied at baseline, 4th and 8th weeks.

Data collection for the control group

The interview for the control group was done at one of the offices within the school of nursing complex. Out of a total of 116 control subjects approached, five declined joining the research, 16 had history of mental or physical illness, six had positive family history of mental illness in their first degree relatives, while three students had hearing deficits. The 86 consenting participants were given the same neurocognitive tests as the patients after obtaining their socio-demographic characteristics. However, they were only interviewed once.

Assessment Tools

The neurocognitive section of the schedules for clinical assessment in neuropsychiatry (SCAN)

The SCAN is a set of instruments/manual published by the World Health Organization aimed at assessing, measuring, and classifying the psychopathology and behavior associated with the major psychiatric syndromes of adult life [23, 24]. It is a form of interview, found acceptable to patients, which provides a detailed and accurate picture of the mental state. Section 21 of SCAN deals with cognitive impairment and/or decline, which includes the well-known mini-mental state examination (MMSE) as well as the less well-known Verbal Trail Test. This section also contains probes to assess Language, Calculation, Praxis, abstraction, fund-of-knowledge, frontal-subcortical function, and level of consciousness [24].The Cognitive Impairment Section (Sect. 21) has good validity and reliability [24]. Only the MMSE is of interest to this report.

The screen for cognitive impairment in psychiatry (SCIP)

The SCIP is a brief screening tool; a simple and easy-to-administer instrument designed with the intention of assessing cognitive impairment in psychiatric patients [25]. It was designed to facilitate a bedside or office-based assessment of cognitive deficits related to psychotic and affective disorders. It has five subtests for evaluating immediate Verbal Learning (Immediate; VLT-I) and delayed Verbal Learning (Delayed; VLT-D), Working Memory (Working Memory Test; WMT), Verbal Fluency (Verbal Fluency Test; VFT), and Processing speed/Psychomotor speed (Processing Speed Test; PST). Along with executive function, these are the cognitive deficits that have been most reliably found to be deficient in schizophrenia. It is administered with only a pencil, paper and a stopwatch, completed in 15 min. Three alternative forms of the scale are available to facilitate repeated testing without sacrificing the validity and reliability of the scale. The psychometric properties of the SCIP were studied in samples of patients with schizophrenia [26] and were shown to be adequate (Cronbach’s alpha 0.82) [26]. In addition, when comparing cognitively impaired individuals and those with adequate functioning, the proposed cut-off point of the SCIP (< 70) was associated with a sensitivity of 87.9 and specificity of 80.6 [27]. We used the English versions I, II, and III for the baseline, 4th and 8th week assessments, respectively, to reduce the possibility of practice effects [16–18].

The wisconsin card sort test (WCST)

The WCST is a neuropsychological test of “set-shifting” (the ability to display flexibility in the face of changing schedules of reinforcement) [28] or problem solving strategy (a prototype of abstraction) [29]. It allows the clinician to speculate on the following “frontal” lobe functions (with perseverative error measures): strategic planning, organized searching, utilizing environmental feedback to shift cognitive sets, directing behavior towards achieving a goal, and modulating impulsive responding. The test takes approximately 12–20 min to carry out and generates a number of psychometric scores, including numbers, percentages, and percentiles of: categories achieved (the number of ten correct matches achieved), and number of trials to achieve the first category. Other parameters are: perseverative (wrong matches that would not have been correct in the previous rule) and non-perseverative (wrong matches that would have been correct in the previous rule) errors; failure to maintain set (a measure of the loss of the correct sorting principle during the course of the test); and conceptual level achieved (the number of correct responses that occur in runs of three or more divided by the number of trials). A healthy educated adult could have up to 6–7 perseverative errors (PE) in a 64-card test, and categories achieved (CA) could range from 0 to 6 [29]. For the purpose of this study, of the six measures of the WCST, only the PE and CA are presented in this report, because they summarize the contents of the others and are considered the most useful measures.

The brief psychiatric rating scale (BPRS)

The Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale, BPRS (Overall and Gorham) [30] is a widely used instrument for assessing the positive, negative, and affective symptoms of individuals who have psychotic disorders, especially schizophrenia. The BPRS consists of 18- symptom constructs and takes 20–30 min for the interview and scoring. It is rated on a Likert scale of 1–7 (classical) (which was used in this study), or 0–6 in the new version. It takes 10–15 min to complete. A previously described dimension of psychopathology in an African population was used [31]. Remission was defined as the absence of significant symptoms of basic psychopathology [32].

The global assessment of functioning (GAF) scale

The Global Assessment of Functioning scale (GAF) (American Psychiatric Association, 1994) [33] is utilized by a clinician to gauge an individual’s overall level of functioning and his/her ability to carry out activities of daily living. The GAF is a 100- point scale that measures a patient’s overall level of psychological, social, and occupational functioning on a hypothetical continuum. It is an observer-rated single global rating of functioning.

World health organization disability assessment schedule 2.0 (36-Item adult interviewer-administered version)

The adult interviewer-administered version of the World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule 2.0 (WHODAS 2.0) [34] is a 36-item measure that assesses functional disability in adults, aged 18years and older. It assesses disability across six domains, including understanding and communicating, getting around, self-care, getting along with people, life activities (i.e.; household, work, and/or school activities), and participation in society. Each item of the WHODAS 2.0 asks the individual to rate how much difficulty he or she has had in specific areas of functioning during the past 30 days, rated on a Likert scale of 1–4 (1-none, 2-mild, 3-moderate and 4-severe).

Data analysis

Analysis of the results was done using the International Business Machine-Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS-PC), version 20. Firstly, the normality of distribution of continuous variables was done using Shapiro-Wilk. The data for the cognitive variables were found to be non-normally distributed. The changes in the median scores of cognitive tests across the intervals of the naturalistic treatment was done using Friedman Test. The independent predictors of neurocognitive performance at week 8 was done using stepwise multiple regression analyses. All tests of significance were two-tailed at the 5% level of significance.

Results

The socio-demographic characteristics of age, gender, marital status and level of education were essentially similar in the two groups (p > 0.05). However, all the students were regarded as being employed, while unemployment among the patients was 43.9% (Table 1). The prevalence of any cognitive impairment in persons with FES at baseline was 82.9% (95% CI: 73.0 − 90.3%). After receiving antipsychotic treatment for 8 weeks, the prevalence of any cognitive impairment among FES was 62.9% (95% CI: 51.5 − 74.2%), compared with 1.2% (95% CI: 0.0 − 6.6%), for the HC. At week 8, the prevalence of any cognitive deficits among patients who were remitters (i.e., had no significant psychopathological symptoms) was 54.5% (95% CI: 40.6 − 68.0%), compared with the 93.3% (95% CI: 68.1 − 99.8%) for those who still had significant psychopathological symptoms as shown in Table 2.

Table 3 shows changes in the cognitive, clinical and functional variables among the participants with FES across the intervals of naturalistic treatment. There was significant reduction in the perseverative errors and symptom severity across the intervals of naturalistic treatment (p < 0.001). In addition, there was a significant increase in the total SCIP and its domain scores and the functionality scale (p < 0.001). Table 4 shows an inverse moderate correlation between dimensions of schizophrenia symptomatology and total SCIP scores, viz.: positive symptoms (rhos = -0.52, p < 0.001), negative symptoms (rhos = -0.45, p < 0.001), disorganization symptoms (rhos = -0.45, p < 0.001), and depressive/anxiety symptoms (rhos = -0.41, p < 0.001). Furthermore, there was a positive strong correlation between total SCIP score at 8th week and the GAF-score (rhos = 0.71, p < 0.001).and inverse moderate relationship with WHODAS score (rhos = -0.52, p < 0.001). Table 5 shows the predictors of cognitive performance among persons with FES. As shown in the table, age, age at onset of illness, gender, level of education, type and dosage of medications were not significant predictors of cognitive performance. Rather, the predictors of total SCIP scores at week 8 were: duration of untreated psychosis (β = -0.33, p = 0.01, variance = 19.9%), negative symptoms dimension (β = -0.43, p = 0.01, variance = 4.8%), and positive symptoms dimension (β = -0.35, p = 0.03, variance = 5.9%).

Discussion

The aim of the study was to highlight the prevalence of, and changes in neurocognitive deficits, and their relationship with domains of basic psychopathology and psychosocial outcome measures, in patients with first-episode schizophrenia on naturalistic treatment follow-up for 8 weeks. We hypothesized that, since psychopathological symptoms are known to be significantly associated with cognitive deficits, effective antipsychotic treatment would be paralleled by improved neurocognition; such that, by the 8th week when patients would be in stable condition, their neurocognitive scores would be comparable to those of a matched healthy control group - after controlling for practice effects.

The highlights of the findings are as follows: (i) Even when the patients had achieved clinical stability following 8 weeks of treatment with antipsychotics, the prevalence of cognitive impairment among FES, using SCIP criteria, was still significantly higher when compared with the healthy control group (62.9% vs., 1.2%). Furthermore, the prevalence of cognitive impairment was also relatively significantly higher among patients with significant psychopathology when compared with those who had remission (93.3% vs., 54.9%); (ii) there was a significant improvement in cognitive function over the 8-week treatment period, most notable in the first 4 weeks, paralleling the improvement in psychopathology; (iii) there was an inverse moderate correlation between the cognitive performance scores and all the dimensions of psychopathology (p < 0.001); (iv) there was a positive strong correlation between cognitive performance scores and the score in the global assessment of functioning (p < 0.001); and (v) the significant predictors of total SCIP score at week 8 were: duration of illness, negative and positive symptoms.

The similarity of socio-demographic characteristics between the groups is not surprising in our cultural setting, for the following reasons: First, the general population in this part of Nigeria is Christian, moderately educated and rather westernized in outlook; hence, the similarity in marital status can be attributed to the emphasis on education and economic independence before marriage in the culture [35]. Second, the control group was successfully chosen to match the patients by age and gender.

While the evidence of cognitive deficits in schizophrenia is robust in the literature (range: 80 − 98%) [36, 37], having been noted by Emil Kraepelin [7, 13], our specific contribution in this regard is that we teased out for longitudinal comparison in FES, the prevalence of cognitive deficits at three levels, viz.: (a) at baseline when the disruptive symptoms of psychosis were maximal (82.9%); (b) at week 8 of antipsychotic treatment when they were in stable condition (62.9%); (iii) and also at week 8 for remitters, who had no significant psychotic symptoms (54.5%). This distinction is not usually clear in the literature, and it is important, in order to make meaning of the findings. The results of this investigation also corroborate previous reports that cognitive deficits exist in first-episode schizophrenia and persists albeit in attenuated form with treatment [7, 13].

The summary of the relationship between domains of cognitive function and psychopathology is that they were significantly correlated, cross-sectionally and longitudinally, such that, improvement in one group paralleled improvement in the other; and over time, outcome in one group was predicted by baseline scores in the other group [16]. Notwithstanding the possible contribution of practice effects to improvement in cognitive function scores [38–42] (which we tried to control by our methodology), this finding can be explained from the perspectives of both clinical observations and theoretical models of etiological relationships [8]. Clinically, it is reasonable to suggest that the disruptive symptoms of psychosis could affect performance in cognitive tasks, such that the patient’s performance would improve when they became freer from such symptoms [43]. At the theoretical level, the nature of these relationships, especially that between negative symptoms and cognitive function (described as the “hidden disabilities” of schizophrenia) [44], has been the subject of enlightening discussions [8]. Our specific contribution to this debate is the presentation of longitudinal data from a more comprehensive assessment of cognitive function (i.e., SCIP, WCST and MMSE) and psychopathology (i.e., BPRS domains as well as psychosocial outcome measures, WHODAS and GAF). Hence, we were able to show that this relationship could involve, not only basic psychopathology, as have been previously reported, but also related to psychosocial outcome measures, thus adding robustness to the findings. Harvey et al. [8] have outlined four explanatory models for the noted relationships, viz: (i) cognitive deficits and negative symptoms overlap, and have a similar etiology; (ii) the two are separate manifestations of a common etiological factor; (iii) they have separate but related etiologies; and (iv) they are independent etiologically but could be related by similarity of constituent items and relationship to distant outcomes. Our longitudinal findings support their choice of models 3 and 4 as the most likely explanation, because, in a systematic review by the same group, it was found that baseline cognition predicted long-term real world functioning up to 20 years of follow-up [45].

Meanwhile, the persistence of cognitive deficits among remitters at week 8 supports the findings of previous studies, and is said to indicate that cognitive impairment constitutes an enduring domain of psychopathology that merits consideration for inclusion in nosology; and should be explored as a trait (i.e., predating onset of frank psychosis) that has possible genetic underpinnings [ 35–37 ].

Interestingly, at week 8, the duration of untreated psychosis (DUP) was a significant predictor of total SCIP score consistent with previous reports [46–48]. This impact of DUP on neurocognitive outcome has implications for public mental health education, especially in our setting, as an affordable tool for early detection and treatment. The apparent normalization of MMSE scores from week 4 is in support of the impression that this instrument is not sensitive to assessment of cognitive deficits in schizophrenia, especially when the patients are in remission [49].

Limitations

First, it is well known that neurocognitive performance tests that are repeated at intervals are plagued by the phenomenon of practice effects, i.e., the possibility that participants learn about test instruments on subsequent intervals of testing and, therefore, could perform better, independent of the impact of treatment on the pathology of interest [50]. In order to lessen this possibility, we chose a test instrument, the SCIP, which was specifically articulated to address this issue, because it has three versions, i.e., same tests with different tasks. Second, following the finding in the literature about the usefulness of low intensity presentation (i.e., less frequent and longer intervals of presentation), we further reduced the impact of practice effects by repeating the tests once at 4-weekly intervals. Third, while the period of follow-up was short, it is comparable to the period for psychotropic drug trials [51, 52]. Fourth, while patients were lost to follow-up despite our efforts, we had a sufficient number to enable us test the hypothesis of the study. Finally, the instrument used to assess cognitive function fell short of the extensively validated MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB) used in previous studies in the region [53, 54]; and this was because we could not afford the high cost of procuring the instrument.

Conclusion

The findings of this longitudinal study support the impression that cognitive deficits are highly prevalent among neuroleptic-naive FES, even when they are in remission; hence they are enduring and merit consideration as a domain of psychopathology. The longitudinal relationship between neurocognitive performance scores, basic psychopathology domains and psychosocial outcome measures, supports the finding in the literature, that baseline cognition predicted long-term real world functioning in the long term. Our longitudinal data have added clarity to the well-replicated relationship between cognitive deficits and negative symptoms of schizophrenia, and support the impression that the MMSE is not a sensitive measure of cognitive deficits when patients are in remission. The impact of duration of untreated psychosis on neurocognitive outcome has implications for public mental health education.
