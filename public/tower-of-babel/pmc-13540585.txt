Mediating Role of Family Resilience and Avoidance Between Financial Toxicity and Quality of Life in Cancer Patients: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Yafang Liu, Lifen Wu, Kaiyang Nie, Xinlian Yang, Yongli Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFinancial toxicity has become a critical issue in cancer treatment. The substantial financial burden associated with cancer care may negatively affect patients’ physical and psychological well-being and reduce their quality of life. However, the mechanisms underlying the relationship between financial toxicity and quality of life remain underexplored in the Chinese context. This study examined the roles of family resilience and avoidance coping in this relationship among patients with cancer in China.MethodsA total of 722 patients with cancer were recruited through convenience sampling from seven tertiary hospitals in Henan, Shaanxi, Sichuan, Heilongjiang, Jilin, and Hubei Provinces. Data were collected using four standardized scales. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS and AMOS, including descriptive analyses and mediation analysis.ResultsFamily resilience and avoidance coping independently mediated the association between financial toxicity and quality of life (β family resilience = −0.061, 95% CI: −0.109, −0.019; β avoidance coping = −0.049, 95% CI: −0.091, −0.021), accounting for 21.03% and 16.90% of the total effect, respectively. The serial indirect effect through family resilience and avoidance coping was also significant.ConclusionHigher financial toxicity was associated with poorer quality of life among patients with cancer. Family resilience and avoidance coping may play important mediating roles in this association, both independently and sequentially. These findings provide a basis for developing patient- and family-centered interventions aimed at mitigating the adverse effects of financial toxicity.Keywords: financial toxicity, QoL, family resilience, avoidance coping, mediating role

Introduction

With continuous advancements in cancer treatment, an increasing number of cancer patients are able to prolong their lives.1 However, ensuring that survival is extended while maintaining and improving the quality of life (QoL) has become a major focus of research in oncology, public health, and nursing.2 At the same time, the financial burden associated with treatment costs, income losses, and long-term follow-up has become increasingly significant.3,4 Financial toxicity (FT) is a concept used to describe both the subjective and objective financial stress experienced by cancer patients as a result of treatment. It has been widely used to highlight the significant impact of this financial burden on patients’ health outcomes.5,6 The incidence of comprehensive catastrophic health expenditure (CHE) among patients with cancer is 47% in middle- and high-income countries, whereas in low-income countries, this rate increases to as high as 74.4%.7 The costs associated with cancer treatment impose a substantial financial burden on patients, their families, and society.8 As a result, financial toxicity has received increasing attention.9 Therefore, understanding how financial toxicity affects the quality of life of cancer patients has become a crucial topic in oncology research.

Existing studies provide preliminary evidence of an association between financial toxicity and quality of life among patients with cancer. Greater financial burden and fewer available resources are generally associated with poorer quality of life.10 However, financial pressure is not the only factor that affects patients during cancer treatment. Family support, patients’ coping strategies, and other social resources also play important roles.11 A more comprehensive understanding of how financial toxicity affects quality of life requires consideration of these complex mediating mechanisms. Therefore, this study examined family resilience and medical coping strategies as potential mediators of this relationship. Specifically, it investigated the serial mediating pathway through which financial toxicity may influence quality of life.

By integrating family stress theory and family resilience theory, this study advances the understanding of the mechanisms linking financial toxicity to quality of life among patients with cancer. The findings also provide a theoretical basis for clinicians to develop patient- and family-centered interventions aimed at reducing the adverse effects of financial toxicity.

Theoretical Framework

The present study was guided by the ABC-X model of family stress and Family Resilience Theory.12 In the ABC-X model, A represents the family stressor, B represents the resources available to manage the stressor, C represents the family’s appraisal of the event, and X represents the resulting crisis or adaptation.13 Families exposed to similar stressors may respond differently depending on their available resources and interpretation of the situation.14,15 In this study, financial toxicity was conceptualized as an illness-related stressor, family resilience as a family-level adaptive resource, avoidance coping as an individual response, and quality of life as an adaptation-related outcome. Because family appraisal was not directly measured, the ABC-X model was used as an organizing framework rather than tested in its entirety.

Family Resilience Theory conceptualizes resilience as a dynamic adaptive process rather than a fixed family characteristic.16 Families may strengthen resilience through shared meaning-making, flexible reorganization of roles, supportive communication, collaborative problem-solving, and mobilization of internal and external resources.17 These processes may help families maintain functioning during cancer treatment,18 recover from illness- and finance-related disruptions, and achieve positive adaptation following adversity.19 Together, these theories support the examination of family resilience and avoidance coping as potential pathways linking financial toxicity with quality of life.

Financial Toxicity and Quality of Life

Financial toxicity refers to both the objective financial burden and the subjective financial distress experienced by patients as a consequence of cancer and its treatment.20 Objective financial burden may include drug costs, related medical costs, and indirect costs, such as lost work productivity out-of-pocket.21 Subjective financial distress reflects patients’ concerns, worry, and perceived lack of financial security.22 Financial toxicity can be assessed using objective indicators, such as medical expenditure and debt, as well as patient-reported measures.23 The Comprehensive Score for Financial Toxicity (COST) is a commonly used patient-reported instrument,24 with lower original COST scores indicating greater financial toxicity.25

Quality of life is a multidimensional concept encompassing patients’ physical, emotional, social/family, and functional well-being and is an important outcome of cancer treatment and supportive care.26 The Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-General scale is widely used to assess these domains in patients with cancer.27 Previous studies have consistently associated greater financial toxicity with psychological distress,28 treatment-related difficulties,29 poorer functioning,30 and lower quality of life.31 However, the processes underlying this association remain insufficiently understood. Examining family-level resources and individual coping responses may therefore provide a more comprehensive explanation of how financial toxicity is associated with quality of life.

Family Resilience

Within this theoretical framework, family resilience refers to the capacity of the family system to adapt, reorganize, and maintain functioning when confronted with significant stress.32 1 In the context of cancer, family members frequently participate in treatment decisions, provide emotional and practical support, assume caregiving responsibilities, and share financial demands.33 As a result, the family’s ability to adapt plays a critical role in determining whether a patient can maintain a relatively good quality of life under high-stress conditions.34 Studies have shown that family resilience is positively associated with patients’ quality of life.33,34 Families with higher resilience can adapt better to the stress of the treatment process and provide emotional support and resources to help patients cope with financial difficulties.35

Avoidance Coping

Coping strategies influence how patients manage the demands associated with cancer and its treatment.36–38 Avoidance coping refers to cognitive and behavioral efforts to distance oneself from a stressor, commonly through denial, withdrawal, or distraction.39 Although it may provide temporary emotional relief, persistent avoidance may reduce patients’ engagement with illness-related information,40 symptom monitoring, treatment decisions, and communication with healthcare professionals, thereby potentially interfering with treatment adherence.41 Avoidance may also prevent patients from recognizing and processing emotions such as fear, sadness, and helplessness and may therefore be associated with prolonged anxiety or depressive symptoms.42 In addition, minimizing physical symptoms or avoiding communication about symptom progression may delay appropriate symptom management and contribute to impaired physical functioning and poorer quality of life.43 Studies among Chinese patients with cancer have also documented the use of avoidance-oriented coping under substantial illness-related and financial stress.44 Therefore, avoidance coping was included in the present study as an individual-level response that may help explain the association between financial toxicity and quality of life. Together with family resilience, it allows the proposed model to examine both family-level adaptive resources and individual-level coping processes.

Rationale of the Study

Existing studies have demonstrated an association between financial toxicity and quality of life among patients with cancer, but the mechanisms underlying this relationship remain unclear. Based on the ABC-X model of family stress, Family Resilience Theory, and previous empirical evidence, we proposed that financial toxicity may be associated with quality of life through family resilience and avoidance coping. Specifically, greater financial toxicity may be associated with lower family resilience and greater avoidance coping, which may in turn be associated with poorer quality of life.

Article Types

Study Design and Sampling

A cross-sectional study was conducted between June and December 2025. Using convenience sampling, the study was conducted at seven tertiary general hospitals in Henan, Shaanxi, Sichuan, Heilongjiang, Jilin, and Hubei provinces. Recruitment was coordinated through the oncology center at each hospital, and uniformly trained ward-based research staff were responsible for recruiting participants and administering the survey.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a clinical diagnosis of any type of cancer, (2) voluntary participation in the study, (3) physical conditions allowing participation, and (4) sufficient communication skills to understand the survey questions. Exclusion criteria were: (1) mental disorders or cognitive impairments that would prevent independent completion of the questionnaire, and (2) communication barriers or unclear expressions due to various reasons. Potentially eligible inpatients were identified through the hospital information system according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The research staff approached these patients during health education sessions or at other appropriate times and conducted recruitment individually and face to face. Following Byrne’s45 recommendations for structural equation modeling (SEM), the sample size should be at least 200 participants. A total of 746 completed questionnaires were collected. Of these, 722 were retained for the final analysis, representing 96.78% of the completed questionnaires. 24 questionnaires were excluded because the completion time was less than 5 minutes or because the responses showed a highly consistent and fixed pattern.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Huazhong University of Science and Technology ((2025) IEC (A157)). All participants provided informed consent before completing the questionnaire. For participants completing the electronic questionnaire through Wenjuanxing, electronic informed consent was obtained through an agreement statement on the first page, which participants were required to confirm before accessing the questionnaire. Participants completing the paper-based questionnaire provided written informed consent using a signed consent form. Additionally, this study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Instruments

Sociodemographic Characteristics

Demographic and clinical variables were selected a priori based on a targeted review of systematic reviews and observational studies examining financial toxicity and quality of life among adults with cancer.3,5 Previous studies have commonly examined age, educational attainment, employment and income, insurance coverage, cancer type, disease stage, and disease duration as factors potentially associated with financial toxicity and/or quality of life. Accordingly, the demographic variables collected in this study included sex, age, place of residence, educational level, marital status, change in employment status after diagnosis, monthly household income, type of basic medical insurance, and commercial insurance status. The clinical variables included cancer type, tumor–node–metastasis (TNM) stage, and disease duration. The TNM stage classifies cancer according to the extent of the primary tumor (T), regional lymph-node involvement (N), and distant metastasis (M). TNM stage was obtained from the patients’ medical records as documented by the treating clinical team and was categorized as stage I, II, III, or IV. Sex, place of residence, and marital status were additionally included as standard sociodemographic characteristics used to describe the study population.

Comprehensive Scores for Financial Toxicity Based on the Patient-Reported Outcome Measures, COST-PROM

Financial toxicity was assessed using the 11-item Comprehensive Financial Toxicity Scale (COST), developed by Souza et al25 The Chinese version was translated and validated by Yu et al,46 The Chinese version was translated by Liu and has been widely used among families of patients with cancer. It has demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.806, and retained all 11 items of the original scale. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4, with lower total scores indicating greater financial toxicity. Scores of ≥26, 14–25, 1–13, and 0 were categorized as level 0 (no impact), level 1 (mild impact), level 2 (moderate impact), and level 3 (high impact), respectively. Levels 1–3 were considered indicative of financial toxicity. The Cronbach’s α in the present study was 0.88.

Family Hardiness Index, FHI

Family resilience was assessed using the Family Hardiness Index (FHI).47 The Chinese version was translated by Liu48 and has been used among families of patients with cancer. It has demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.806.49 The FHI consists of 20 items, with responses rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 to 4), with higher scores indicating a higher level of family resilience. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.84.

Medical Coping Modes Questionnaire, MCMQ

The original 19-item Medical Coping Modes Questionnaire (MCMQ) was developed by Feifel et al50 The Chinese version was translated and revised by Shen and Jiang,51 with one resignation item added to form a 20-item questionnaire. Factor analysis supported three dimensions: confrontation (8 items), avoidance (7 items), and resignation (5 items). The seven-item avoidance subscale was retained from the original version without item addition or deletion. In the present study, only the avoidance subscale was used because the hypothesized mediation model focused on avoidance as a potentially maladaptive coping response linking financial toxicity with quality of life. Items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 to 4), with higher scores indicating greater use of avoidance coping. Cronbach’s α for the avoidance subscale was 0.89 in this study.

Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-Generic Scale, FACT-G

Quality of life was assessed using the Chinese version of the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy–General (FACT-G), Version 4, developed by Cella et al52 The scale consists of 27 items across four domains: physical well-being (7 items), social/family well-being (7 items), emotional well-being (6 items), and functional well-being (7 items). Items are rated from 0 to 4, with a total score ranging from 0 to 108, with higher scores indicating better quality of life. The Chinese version was culturally adapted under the standardized FACIT translation and linguistic validation procedures.53 It was evaluated by Wan et al54 in 552 Chinese patients with malignant tumors and demonstrated good internal consistency, test–retest reliability, construct validity, and responsiveness. Cronbach’s α in the present study for each dimension was greater than 0.80.

Data Collection

Before data collection, the nursing departments of the seven participating hospitals were contacted to obtain administrative approval and support. Each hospital designated a coordinator and an investigator, and all investigators received standardized training regarding participant recruitment, questionnaire administration, and data-quality control.

Potentially eligible patients were approached face to face at their bedside. After the purpose and procedures of the study had been explained and informed consent had been obtained, participants were invited to complete the questionnaire. The electronic questionnaire administered through Wenjuanxing was the preferred mode of completion. Participants who experienced difficulty using electronic devices or completing the electronic questionnaire, including some older adults and participants with lower educational attainment, were offered an identical paper-based version.

When assistance was required, trained investigators explained the questionnaire items using standardized, non-leading, plain language. Participants provided their responses verbally, and the investigators recorded the responses verbatim without suggesting or modifying their answers. Both formats contained the same items, item order, and response options. The electronic questionnaire was completed onsite following face-to-face recruitment and was not administered remotely.

Among the 722 questionnaires included in the final analysis, 652 (90.30%) were completed electronically and 70 (9.70%) were completed using the paper-based format. All completed questionnaires were checked for completeness and logical consistency, and the anonymity and confidentiality of participants and their data were maintained throughout the study.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0, with a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using frequencies and percentages, whereas financial toxicity, family resilience, avoidance coping, and quality of life were described using means and standard deviations. The mean scores of the four study variables within each sociodemographic and clinical subgroup were also reported. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Exploratory univariable analyses were conducted to compare the four study-variable scores across sociodemographic and clinical groups. Independent-samples t tests were used for variables with two categories, and one-way analysis of variance was used for variables with three or more categories. Welch’s tests were applied when the homogeneity-of-variance assumption was not met, followed by appropriate post hoc comparisons when the overall test was significant. Common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine associations among financial toxicity, family resilience, avoidance coping, and quality of life. Multicollinearity among the predictors was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) statistics; tolerance values below 0.10 or VIF values above 10 were considered indicative of problematic multicollinearity. AMOS version 28.0 was used to construct the serial mediation model. Indirect effects were tested using 5,000 bootstrap samples to generate bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals. Because the observed-variable serial mediation model was just-identified (saturated; df = 0), conventional global fit indices were not interpreted. Model evaluation was based on the standardized path coefficients and bootstrap confidence intervals for the indirect effects.

This study included 722 patients. The sample consisted primarily of middle-aged patients, with a mean age of 40.23 years, and the sex distribution was relatively balanced. Notably, most patients (90.03%) experienced a change in employment status after their cancer diagnosis. More than half (52.63%) reported a monthly household income of RMB 3,001–5,000. Regarding health insurance, most patients (80.61%) were covered by Urban and Rural Residents’ Medical Insurance. The most common cancer types were lung cancer (21.05%), oral cancer (17.17%), and gastric cancer (16.90%). Common Method Bias Test

Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess common method bias. The results of the unrotated principal component analysis identified six factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. The first factor accounted for 31.68% of the total variance, which was below the recommended threshold of 40%. Therefore, no serious common method bias was identified in this study.

Descriptive Statistics of Study Variables

Overall, participants reported a relatively high level of FT, with a mean score of 19.71 (SD = 9.53). In addition, 70.91% of the patients experienced at least mild financial toxicity, indicating that financial burden was common in this sample. Family resilience and QoL were both at moderate levels, with mean scores of 53.71 (SD = 14.38) and 59.64 (SD = 22.89), respectively. The score for avoidance coping was relatively high, with a mean of 18.23 (SD = 5.72). This finding suggests that patients tended to adopt avoidance strategies when facing stress related to cancer and its treatment. The details are shown in Table 2.

Mediator Model

The total association between financial toxicity and quality of life was statistically significant (β = −0.290, 95% CI: −0.382, −0.194). After family resilience and avoidance coping were included in the model, the direct association between financial toxicity and quality of life remained statistically significant (β = −0.158, 95% CI: −0.267, −0.051), indicating partial rather than full mediation. Significant specific indirect effects were observed through family resilience (β = −0.061, 95% CI: −0.109, −0.019), avoidance coping (β = −0.049, 95% CI: −0.091, −0.021), and the serial pathway through family resilience and avoidance coping (β = −0.022, 95% CI: −0.042, −0.010). These indirect pathways accounted for 21.03%, 16.90%, and 7.58% of the total association, respectively. Collectively, the indirect pathways accounted for approximately 45.5% of the total association. Thus, family resilience and avoidance coping, both separately and sequentially, partially mediated the association between financial toxicity and quality of life. Discussion

Although previous studies have explored the association between FT and QoL of cancer patients, gaps remain in understanding the underlying mechanisms of this relationship.6,10,11,55,56 The main findings of this study indicate that FT not only directly affects the QoL of cancer patients but also indirectly influences it through family resilience and avoidance coping. These results highlight the critical role of family resilience and medical coping strategies in this process.

In this study, the FT demonstrated a significant negative predictive effect on QoL. This finding is consistent with existing research,10,57,58 which indicates that the financial burden and stress experienced by cancer patients are widespread and have a significant impact on their psychological health and social functioning.59,60 Specifically, FT may lead patients to adopt inadequate or ineffective coping strategies during treatment,61 such as reducing treatment, refusing necessary medical resources, and further reducing QoL.62 Reduced income may objectively limit patients’ ability to afford and participate in social activities.10 It may also increase psychological distress and compel patients with cancer to reduce spending on social engagement, thereby contributing to social isolation.10 Social isolation has been associated with increased mortality. Although a causal relationship has not yet been established, this association may be partly explained by its effects on mental health.63 Individuals who are socially isolated may experience greater stress because they lack adequate social networks and support. They may also be more likely to withdraw from others, which can reduce their opportunities to receive advice and encouragement regarding health-promoting behaviors. Medical institutions and the government should attach importance to FT intervention of FT,64,65 such as conducting financial burden screenings, offering financial counseling services, and connecting patients with social welfare resources. Financial counseling can help patients understand anticipated out-of-pocket costs, navigate insurance reimbursement, plan treatment-related expenses, and obtain referrals to available support services. In the Chinese context, such resources may include basic medical insurance and serious-illness insurance reimbursement, government medical assistance for low-income households, charitable assistance programs, and hospital-based social work services. These resources may reduce some treatment-related expenses or improve access to necessary care.

This study found that family resilience partially mediated the relationship between FT and QoL. As a dynamic adaptation process,12,14 it encourages patients to adopt positive medical coping strategies, such as standardized treatment and psychological adjustment,33,66 thereby improving QoL. This finding addresses the limitations of previous studies that predominantly focused on the direct positive effect of family resilience on QoL.67 The family is not only a key source of emotional support but also plays a vital role in providing economic assistance and social support. FT may affect the overall well-being of patients by weakening family resilience, thereby reducing support within the family. Patients with higher family resilience are better able to mobilize family resources, adopt positive coping strategies,68 cope with the stress caused by treatment, and maintain normal life functions and emotional health. Therefore, family resilience–oriented support could be integrated into routine cancer care. During hospitalization or outpatient follow-up, these professionals could provide family communication training, guidance on redistributing caregiving and household roles, and financial planning education regarding treatment expenses and insurance reimbursement. They could also help families identify and mobilize internal and external resources, including assistance from relatives, community services, charitable programs, and government support. These interventions may improve communication and shared decision-making, reduce conflicts and uncertainty related to treatment costs and caregiving responsibilities, and strengthen the family’s capacity to adapt to both cancer treatment and financial toxicity.

Consistent with previous studies, coping strategies adopted by patients in the context of economic stress are crucial factors influencing their QoL.69 Studies conducted in China have shown that a high FT is strongly associated with patients adopting strategies such as discontinuing treatment, reducing the frequency of medical visits, and delaying surgeries or examinations, all of which fall under avoidance coping.70 Furthermore, avoidance coping is linked to poorer QoL, higher emotional distress, and lower treatment adherence in patients.44 FT affects patients’ emotions and behaviors, influencing their treatment choices.71 This effect may be particularly relevant in China,72 where high out-of-pocket medical expenses can intensify treatment-related financial pressure. In addition, some patients may experience illness-related stigma, concerns about burdening their families,73 or a preference for enduring difficulties privately.74 These attitudes may discourage disclosure of financial and emotional distress, reduce help-seeking, and contribute to delayed or avoidant treatment behaviors. Culturally appropriate support should therefore include patient–family treatment decision counseling, communication guidance that normalizes discussion of financial concerns, and individualized coping support delivered by oncology professionals. These approaches may encourage more active help-seeking and shared problem-solving rather than passive endurance or avoidance.

This study identified a significant serial indirect association of family resilience and avoidance coping in the relationship between FT and QoL. Higher FT was associated with lower family resilience, which was in turn associated with greater avoidance coping and poorer QoL. These findings are consistent with the hypothesized pathway but do not establish temporal or causal relationships because of the cross-sectional design. Moreover, family resilience and support were assessed only from the patients’ perspectives. Therefore, family members’ perceptions and the actual availability of family support were not directly examined. Longitudinal studies involving both patients and family members are needed to confirm these relationships.

Limitations and Recommendations

First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the identified mediation pathways should be interpreted as statistical associations. Future longitudinal studies should examine the temporal direction of these relationships. Because all variables were reported by patients, future dyadic studies involving both patients and family members are also needed to capture family perspectives and the actual availability of family support. Second, convenience sampling from seven tertiary hospitals in six provinces may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should recruit patients from more diverse regions, rural communities, and different levels of healthcare institutions. Third, cancer type was not included as a covariate because of heterogeneous categories and unequal subgroup sizes. Future research with larger cancer-specific samples should assess whether the proposed pathways differ across cancer types. Finally, future studies should incorporate additional mediators, such as social support and emotional regulation, to further refine the mediation model.

Conclusion

This study explored the mechanisms through which financial toxicity, family resilience, and avoidance coping styles influence the QoL of patients with cancer. The results showed that financial toxicity not only negatively impacts QoL directly but also indirectly worsens QoL by reducing family resilience and encouraging the use of avoidance coping strategies. Based on these findings, future interventions should focus on strengthening family resilience, optimizing coping strategies, and reducing reliance on avoidance coping, with the goal of improving the overall health and quality of life of patients with cancer. This study offers both theoretical and practical guidance for developing comprehensive interventions to address financial toxicity in cancer care.
