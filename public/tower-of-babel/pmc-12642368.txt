Association between preterm birth subtypes and severe maternal morbidity: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Tanko Rufai, Lucy T Greenberg, Scott A Lorch, Ciaran S Phibbs, Jeffrey S Buzas, Molly Passarella, George R Saade, Jeannette Rogowski, Nansi S Boghossian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere maternal morbidity (SMM) is a significant complication associated with preterm delivery. However, most studies have focused solely on SMM during the delivery hospitalization, without differentiating by preterm birth subtype or examining postpartum SMM and readmissions.ObjectiveTo examine the association between preterm birth subtypes and SMM during delivery hospitalization, up to 1-year postpartum, and postpartum readmissions within 365 days.Study designWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using linked birth and fetal death certificates and maternal hospital discharge data from Michigan, Oregon, and South Carolina (2008–2020). Modified Poisson regression models were used to estimate adjusted relative risks (aRR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for SMM during delivery hospitalization, postpartum SMM, and readmissions, stratified by preterm birth subtype. Models were adjusted for birth year, state, insurance type, education, maternal age, race/ethnicity, and adequacy of prenatal care.ResultsAmong 2,570,808 deliveries, 156,763 (6.1%) were spontaneous preterm births and 70,877 (2.8%) were medically indicated preterm births. For medically indicated preterm births, the aRR for SMM during delivery hospitalization were 18.0 (95% CI 16.7–19.4) at ≤ 31 weeks, 14.5 (95% CI 13.2–15.8) at 32–33 weeks, and 6.7 (95% CI 6.3–7.1) at 34–36 weeks. For spontaneous preterm births, the corresponding aRRs were 7.5 (95% CI 7.0–8.0), 6.4 (95% CI 5.9-7.0), and 3.1 (95% CI 2.9–3.3), respectively. During the postpartum period, elevated risks of SMM persisted for both subtypes. For medically indicated preterm births, aRRs for postpartum SMM ranged from 3.4 (95% CI 3.0-3.9) at ≤ 31 weeks to 2.1 (95% CI 2.0-2.3) at 34–36 weeks. For spontaneous preterm births, aRRs for postpartum SMM ranged from 2.0 (95% CI 1.8–2.2) at ≤ 31 weeks to 1.6 (95% CI 1.5–1.7) at 34–36 weeks. Postpartum readmission risk was also elevated for both subtypes.ConclusionPreterm delivery, particularly when medically indicated, is strongly associated with increased risk of SMM during delivery hospitalization, throughout the postpartum period, and with postpartum readmissions. These findings suggest that individuals experiencing preterm birth, especially medically indicated preterm deliveries, may benefit from targeted postpartum monitoring and follow-up, including earlier clinical contacts and closer surveillance for complications beyond the standard 6-week postpartum visit.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08408-z.Keywords: Severe maternal morbidity, Medically indicated preterm, Spontaneous preterm, Acute renal failure, Disseminated intravascular coagulation, Sepsis, Hysterectomy, Acute respiratory distress syndrome, Postpartum, Obstetric comorbidity index score

Background

Preterm birth (PTB; < 37 weeks gestational age) affects over 10% of newborns in the United States and is associated with mortality, significant neonatal morbidities, and long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes [1–3]. There are two main types of PTB: spontaneous and medically indicated, accounting for 65–70% and 30–35% of PTB cases, respectively [2]. Spontaneous PTB results from either preterm labor or preterm premature rupture of membranes [2]. In contrast, medically indicated PTB is intentionally planned due to maternal or fetal conditions, such as preeclampsia or non-reassuring fetal status [4, 5].

Although PTB is often conceptualized as an infant outcome, the maternal health consequences are substantial. Severe maternal morbidity (SMM), defined as a life-threatening diagnosis or a life-saving procedure during the delivery hospitalization, has been associated with PTB [6–9]. Several studies have identified an association between PTB and specific indicators of SMM, including sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, mechanical ventilation, and shock [7, 10–12]. For instance, a study in California found that PTB was significantly more common among birthing individuals with SMM than those without SMM (40.6 vs. 8.4%; P <.0001) [7]. Similarly, a study from a single hospital in Pennsylvania reported that the probability of SMM was 16% (95% CI 14–17%) at 24 weeks compared to 2.2% (95% CI 2.1–2.3%) at 38 weeks [10]. However, these studies primarily focused on SMM occurring during the delivery hospitalization without examining postpartum SMM. This is a critical gap, as the conditions leading to PTB—particularly medically indicated PTB—may reflect underlying maternal health vulnerabilities that persist or worsen after delivery. Moreover, the physiological stress and medical interventions associated with PTB may increase the risk of postpartum complications. Understanding whether PTB is associated with elevated risk of SMM beyond delivery, and whether this risk varies by PTB subtype, is essential for informing postpartum care and surveillance strategies. Additionally, existing studies were often conducted at a single institution or within a single state, employed varying definitions of SMM, and did not differentiate between subtypes of PTB [7, 10]. To address these limitations, we conducted a multi-state analysis to examine the association between PTB subtypes, categorized by gestational age groups, and SMM, during delivery hospitalization and up to 1-year postpartum, as well as postpartum readmissions occurring within 365 days.

Methods

Study sample

This retrospective cohort linked birth and fetal death certificates to maternal hospital discharge records from Michigan, Oregon, and South Carolina, covering the years 2008 to 2020. We restricted our analysis to gestational ages between 22 and 44 weeks. Inpatient hospitalizations during delivery and up to 365 days postpartum were examined for all three states. The state health departments provided the de-identified linked data. Linkages primarily use a unique patient identifier and, when needed, a second match uses a probabilistic matching algorithm based on variables such as admission and birth dates, birth hospital, patient’s zip code, and other sociodemographic information [13]. These processes achieved a match rate of over 95%. The institutional review boards of the University of South Carolina, the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia, and the health departments of the three states approved the study. The study was classified as non-human participant research due to the de-identification of records. It adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

Study variables

The main exposure was PTB, defined as delivery < 37 weeks, categorized by gestational age groups and by medically indicated or spontaneous PTB using birth certificate and hospital discharge data. Additional file 1 shows the data sources and the ICD codes to identify medically indicated and spontaneous PTB [14]. Examined outcomes included non-transfusion SMM (hereafter labelled SMM) during the delivery hospitalization and the postpartum period up to 365 days, as well as readmissions occurring within 365 days postpartum. SMM was defined using the updated Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s Ninth (ICD-09) or Tenth (ICD-10) Revision diagnosis and procedure codes, which include 16 life-threatening maternal conditions (such as amniotic fluid embolism, eclampsia, severe anesthesia complications, sepsis) and four potentially life-saving procedures (such as ventilation), excluding blood transfusion SMM events. We used the federally available data resource document from the Maternal and Child Health Bureau to aid with the transition from ICD-9 to ICD-10 codes [15].

Statistical analysis

We examined maternal characteristics by PTB subtype (spontaneous and medically indicated) and PTB status (< 37 weeks and ≥ 39 weeks). Subsequently, we assessed rates of SMM during delivery and postpartum, as well as rates of postpartum readmission (per 10,000 deliveries), across gestational age groups (≤ 31, 32–33, 34–36, 37–38, 39–40, ≥ 41 weeks) and PTB subtype. Unadjusted relative risks (RRs) were calculated for each gestational age group compared to the full-term reference group (39–40 weeks of gestation). We then used modified Poisson regression models to estimate adjusted relative risks (aRR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the association between gestational age and each outcome [16]. Deliveries were categorized into nine groups based on gestational age and PTB subtype: ≤ 31 weeks medically indicated, 32–33 weeks medically indicated, 34–36 weeks medically indicated, ≤ 31 weeks spontaneous, 32–33 weeks spontaneous, 34–36 weeks spontaneous, 37–38 weeks, 39–40 weeks (reference), and ≥ 41 weeks. Models were adjusted for maternal age, state, insurance type (private, government, self-pay, other), educational status (no high school, some high school, high school, some college, 4-year college, > 4-year college,), Adequacy of Prenatal Care Utilization Index [17] (inadequate, intermediate, adequate, adequate plus), and race and ethnicity (American Indian, Asian/Pacific Islander, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, and other). The “Other” group included birthing individuals recorded as “Other” or identified as multiracial. Race and ethnicity were included given their association with both exposure and outcomes.

Covariate data were missing for 5.3% of the sample (n = 135,179). To address this, we performed multiple imputations using the chained equations (mice package in R), generating twenty imputed datasets [18]. As a descriptive analysis, we also examined the distribution of SMM indicators during the delivery hospitalization and postpartum by PTB subtype per 10,000 deliveries. All analyses were performed using R statistical software version 4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [19].

Sensitivity analysis

In sensitivity analyses, we adjusted for an obstetric comorbidity index score, a previously validated measure comprising 26 patient-level risk factors for SMM (Additional file 2), after excluding PTB, as it was our primary exposure of interest [20]. We did not adjust for the obstetric comorbidity index in our main models due to potential overadjustment for critical causal pathways between PTB and the risk of SMM and postpartum readmission.

Results

Our final sample size was 2,570,808; 156,763 (6.1%) birthing individuals had a spontaneous PTB, while 70,877 (2.8%) had a medically indicated PTB. Additional file 3 shows study cohort exclusions. Compared to individuals who had a full-term delivery, those who experienced a medically indicated or spontaneous PTB were more likely to identify as Black, smoke, be covered by Medicaid insurance, undergo cesarean delivery, and have conditions such as pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, and diabetes mellitus. They also had higher SMM comorbidity index scores, multiple gestations, adequate plus prenatal care, and obese BMI. Older maternal age, cesarean delivery, obesity, and pregnancy complications such as preeclampsia, gestational hypertension, chronic hypertension, and diabetes mellitus were more frequent among individuals with medically indicated PTB than those with spontaneous PTB (Table 1).

Figure 1 shows the rate of SMM by gestational age group and PTB subtype during the delivery and postpartum periods, as well as postpartum readmission rates. During the delivery hospitalization, SMM rates increased as gestational age decreased—from 41 per 10,000 deliveries at 39–40 weeks to 925 for indicated and 375 for spontaneous PTB at ≤ 31 weeks. At ≥ 41 weeks, the SMM rate was slightly higher than at 39–40 weeks (44 vs. 41 per 10,000 deliveries). In the postpartum period, a similar trend was observed: SMM rates increased from 45 per 10,000 deliveries at 39–40 weeks to 288 for indicated and 134 for spontaneous PTB at ≤ 31 weeks. Notably, the postpartum SMM rate at ≥ 41 weeks was lower than at 39–40 weeks. Postpartum readmission rates also increased with decreasing gestational age, increasing from 354 per 10 000 deliveries at 39–40 weeks to 1,136 for indicated and 887 for spontaneous PTB at ≤ 31 weeks.

The unadjusted RR of SMM during delivery hospitalization and the postpartum period among individuals with medically indicated and spontaneous PTB decreased with increasing gestational age (Table 2). The RRs of SMM during delivery were elevated for medically indicated PTB at ≤ 31 weeks (RR 22.5; 95% CI 21.0–24.0), 32–33 weeks (RR 19.3; 95% CI 17.8–21.0), and 34–36 weeks (RR 8.3; 95% CI 7.9–8.8) compared to full-term deliveries. Similarly, the RRs of SMM among individuals with spontaneous PTB were elevated at ≤ 31 weeks (RR 9.1; 95% CI 8.6–9.7), 32–33 weeks (RR 7.7; 95% CI 7.1–8.3), and 34–36 weeks (RR 3.5; 95% CI 3.3–3.7) compared to full-term deliveries. After controlling for maternal sociodemographic characteristics and pre-existing medical conditions including chronic hypertension and diabetes mellitus, the aRRs of SMM during delivery among individuals with medically indicated and spontaneous PTB were slightly attenuated. For individuals with medically indicated PTB, the aRR at ≤ 31 weeks was 18.0 (95% CI 16.7–19.4), at 32–33 weeks was 14.5 (95% CI 13.2–15.8), and at 34–36 weeks was 6.7 (95% CI 6.3–7.1). For individuals with spontaneous PTB, the aRR at ≤ 31 weeks was 7.5 (95% CI 7.0–8.0), at 32–33 weeks, 6.4 (95% CI 5.9–7.0.9.0), and at 34–36 weeks, 3.1 (95% CI 2.9–3.3). The risk of SMM was also elevated at 37–38 weeks (aRR 1.7; 95% CI 1.6–1.8) (Table 2). Individuals with medically indicated PTB consistently showed higher relative risks for SMM than spontaneous PTB across all preterm gestational age categories.

Results were similar during the postpartum period, with the unadjusted RR and aRR of SMM increasing among both individuals with medically indicated and spontaneous PTB as gestational age decreased. The aRRs for SMM remained elevated for both medically indicated and spontaneous PTB, ranging from 3.4 (95% CI 3.0–3.9.0.9) at ≤ 31 weeks to 2.1 (95% CI 2.0–2.3.0.3) at 34–36 weeks for medically indicated PTB, and from 2.0 (95% CI 1.8–2.2) at ≤ 31 weeks to 1.6 (95% CI 1.5–1.7) at 34–36 weeks for spontaneous PTB (Table 2).

For postpartum readmissions, the unadjusted RRs increased among both individuals with medically indicated and spontaneous PTB as gestational age decreased (Table 3). At ≤ 31 weeks, the unadjusted RR of readmission was 3.2 (95% CI 3.0–3.4.0.4) among individuals with medically indicated PTB, while it was 2.5 (95% CI 2.4–2.6) among individuals with spontaneous PTB. At 34–36 weeks, the unadjusted RR decreased to 2.0 (95% CI 1.9–2.0.9.0) and 1.7 (95% CI 1.6–1.7) among individuals with medically indicated and spontaneous PTB, respectively.

Results were also attenuated for postpartum readmission after controlling for sociodemographic factors and pre-existing conditions. Figure 2 and Additional file 4 show the top 10 SMM indicators during delivery hospitalization and the postpartum period among individuals with no PTB, and those with medically indicated or spontaneous PTB. During the delivery hospitalization, the top 4 SMM indicators among individuals with medically indicated and spontaneous PTB included acute renal failure, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), hysterectomy, and respiratory distress syndrome. DIC was the most frequent indicator among those without PTB. Rates of certain indicators were markedly elevated among those with medically indicated PTB than those with full-term births. For example, acute renal failure and ventilation occurred at rates 19 times higher than among individuals with full-term births. Similarly, among those with spontaneous PTB, pulmonary edema/acute heart failure and ventilation were at least 7 times more common than in full-term births.

In the postpartum period, individuals with medically indicated and spontaneous PTB most commonly experienced sepsis, acute renal failure, pulmonary edema/acute heart failure, and respiratory distress syndrome. Among those without PTB, the leading indicator was sepsis.

Sensitivity analysis

Adjustment for the obstetric comorbidity index attenuated risk estimates for both medically indicated and spontaneous PTB compared to the main adjusted models. For example, among medically indicated PTBs ≤ 31 weeks, the aRR for SMM during the delivery hospitalization decreased from 18.0 (95% CI 16.7–19.4) in the main model to 3.3 (95% CI 2.9–3.8) after comorbidity adjustment (Table 2). For spontaneous PTBs ≤ 31 weeks, the aRR decreased from 7.5 (95% CI 7.0–8.0) to 3.3 (95% CI 3.0–3.6). Similar patterns were observed across later gestational age strata, indicating that maternal comorbidities contribute substantially to SMM risk.

Postpartum outcomes showed the same trend. For medically indicated PTBs ≤ 31 weeks, the aRR for postpartum SMM declined from 3.4 (95% CI 3.0–3.9) to 1.9 (95% CI 1.7–2.1), and for spontaneous PTBs, from 2.0 (95% CI 1.8–2.2) to 1.5 (95% CI 1.3–1.7) (Table 2).

Readmission risks were also attenuated after comorbidity adjustment. For medically indicated PTBs ≤ 31 weeks, the aRR decreased from 2.2 (95% CI 2.0–2.3) to 1.6 (95% CI 1.5–1.7), and for spontaneous PTBs, from 1.9 (95% CI 1.9–2.0) to 1.7 (95% CI 1.6–1.8), with similar reductions across gestational ages (Table 3).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, shorter pregnancy duration was significantly associated with a higher risk of SMM during delivery hospitalization and the postpartum period, as well as postpartum readmission. Risk increased in a dose-response pattern as gestational age decreased. Compared to individuals who delivered full-term, those with medically indicated PTB at ≤ 31 weeks had a 18.0-fold (95% CI 16.7–19.4) adjusted increased risk of SMM at delivery and a 3.4-fold (95% CI 3.0–3.9.0.9) increased risk postpartum. For spontaneous PTB at ≤ 31 weeks, the corresponding risks were 7.5-fold (95% CI 7.0–8.0) and 2.0-fold (95% CI 1.8–2.2). These elevated RRs of SMM and postpartum readmission remained elevated even after adjusting for the obstetric comorbidity index.

Our findings show that individuals with medically indicated PTB had multiple-fold higher risks of SMM during both delivery hospitalization and the postpartum period compared to those with spontaneous PTB. Prior studies have documented associations between PTB and a modified version of the CDC’s SMM definition, with limited information on the PTB subtypes [7, 10]. In one study of 67,468 births across 16 hospitals in California, the rate of PTB was five times higher among individuals with SMM compared to those without SMM (41% vs. 8%) [7]. Similarly, a Pennsylvania-based study of 153,867 deliveries between 16 and 42 weeks’ gestation reported that the probability of SMM was 16% (95% CI 14–17%) at 24 weeks’ gestation, compared to 2.2% (95% CI 2.1–2.3%) at 38 weeks [10]. Our study also corroborates previous findings that individuals who deliver preterm, compared to those who deliver full-term, are more likely to experience SMM due to conditions such as sepsis, shock, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and acute renal failure [10, 21].

Several mechanisms may explain these associations. For example, acute renal failure, often triggered by severe infections, hypertensive disorders, or preeclampsia, can necessitate medically indicated PTB to prevent severe complications [22, 23]. This condition can result in significant electrolyte imbalances, adversely affecting both maternal and fetal health [24] and can also cause systemic inflammation, potentially initiating premature labor [25]. In pregnancy, DIC can be triggered by conditions such as placental abruption and intrapartum hemorrhage, often requiring medically indicated PTB to manage bleeding and optimize outcomes [26]. While DIC does not directly cause spontaneous PTB, it often reflects a shared underlying pathology, such as placental damage or systemic infection [27]. Hysterectomy, another SMM indicator, may be required in cases of severe preeclampsia, placental abruption, or uterine rupture, conditions that often necessitate medically indicated PTB to safeguard maternal and fetal health [28]. Although less common, spontaneous PTB may also be associated with hysterectomy in the context of severe infections or uterine abnormalities [29, 30]. Importantly, these examples highlight that PTB should not be interpreted as a direct cause of SMM, but rather as a sentinel indicator of underlying maternal pathology. The temporality is complex; many conditions begin during pregnancy, contribute to preterm delivery, and persist in the postpartum period, thereby shaping both the timing of birth and subsequent maternal morbidity.

The dual burden of PTB and severe maternal complications has been well-documented across multiple studies [6, 7, 10, 31]. This dual burden may result in significant physical health challenges, as well as psychological, psychosocial, and financial repercussions for affected families [6, 32]. Individuals experiencing both conditions are at increased risk of postpartum depression and post-traumatic stress disorder, which may impair parent-infant attachment, strain partner relationships, and prolong maternal recovery [6, 32, 33]. In addition to these mental health concerns, the elevated rate of postpartum SMM observed in our study among individuals who delivered preterm further underscores the critical need for timely and comprehensive postpartum follow-up. Notably, sepsis emerged as the most frequent SMM indicator during postpartum readmissions in our cohort, highlighting the vulnerability of this period and the importance of early detection and intervention. Beyond sepsis, additional opportunities for intervention include routine postpartum blood pressure monitoring for hypertensive disorders and earlier recognition of cardiopulmonary complications such as pulmonary edema. The neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) offers a unique opportunity to deliver integrated, family-centered postpartum care to high-risk patients and should be incorporated into maternal care pathways to optimize parental health outcomes [34–36]. Finally, extending follow-up beyond the traditional 6-week postpartum visit may be particularly important for individuals delivering preterm, who often face prolonged physical recovery and elevated risk of complications.

Strengths of our study include the use of a large, population-based dataset linked to hospital discharge records from three states. In contrast to many earlier studies, we distinguished between medically indicated and spontaneous PTB, an important differentiation for understanding the risk of SMM. Furthermore, we adjusted for potential confounding variables and, in secondary analysis, incorporated a validated comorbidity index, accounting for a comprehensive array of clinical risk factors for SMM. A limitation of our study is the use of ICD codes to identify SMM, which may have limited validity; however, this concern is primarily associated with blood transfusion, which was excluded from our SMM definition [37, 38].

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that PTB, especially medically indicated, is strongly associated with an increased risk of SMM, not only during the delivery hospitalization but also during the postpartum period. Our study contributes to the growing body of literature that highlights PTB etiology as a crucial predictive factor—one that physicians may not always consider when assessing patient risk. These findings emphasize the need for integrated, patient-centered care models, such as leveraging the NICU setting, to support high-risk postpartum individuals and improve maternal outcomes.
