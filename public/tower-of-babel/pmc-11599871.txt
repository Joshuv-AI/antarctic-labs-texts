DFT study of the adsorption properties and sensitivity of a B2N monolayer toward harmful gases
Authors: Rezvan Rahimi, Mohammad Solimannejad, Ashkan Horri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we investigate the adsorption of harmful gases - CO, NO, NO2, SO2, and O3 molecules - on a B2N monolayer using periodic density functional theory. The adsorption energy values for the CO/B2N, NO/B2N, NO2/B2N, SO2/B2N, and O3/B2N complexes are determined to be -1.96, -1.39, -1.80, -0.70, and − 2.36 eV, respectively. The B2N monolayer has the ability to adsorb harmful gas molecules, even in humid air, and displays favorable adsorption energy and standard recovery time when exposed to SO2 gas. Consequently, the impact of SO2 gases on the transmission characteristics of the B2N monolayer has been assessed through current-voltage analysis. These findings are of great importance as they serve to demonstrate the remarkable sensing capabilities of a B2N monolayer in efficiently detecting SO2 gas. The desorption time for CO, NO, NO2, and O3 molecules is quite long, thereby indicating the remarkable stability of the B2N sheet for adsorption of these gases. The current study offer valuable insights for further research into the potential utilization of B2N monolayers in long-term monitoring and gas purification applications, specifically in relation to four toxic gases: CO, NO, NO2, and O3.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77659-1.Keywords: Modeling and simulation, B2N monolayer, Sensor, Electronic transport propertiesSubject terms: Condensed-matter physics; Electronics, photonics and device physics; Two-dimensional materials

Introduction

Air pollution is one of the problems that endangers human health today. With the expansion of human societies and the development of various industries, the problem of air pollution is becoming more serious day by day and its role on human health is increasing. Therefore, it is necessary to find ways for monitoring and removal of the polluting species of the environment, especially air polluting gases1–4.

Two-dimensional (2D) nanosheets with large surface/volume ratio and promising properties for the adsorption and desorption of adsorbed gases are ideal platform for monitoring target gases. As a results, 2D materials can be used to design and fabricate excellent gas sensors5. In recent years, many studies based on density functional theory (DFT) calculations have been reported in the literature that verify capability of various types of 2D materials for toxic and polluting gas sensing6–10. In fact, gas sensing by nanomaterial is quite popular file in current scientific research11–14. Recently, a variety of sensors have been developed to detect a diverse array of species15,16.

Recently, a study in the literature17,18 reported on new 2D materials with a BxNy structure. Theoretical predictions of B5N3, B7N5, B2N, B3N, and B4N suggest that among them, B2N is the most stable and has the potential for future synthesis17,18. The B2N monolayer, a thermodynamically stable B-rich two-dimensional compound, has been predicted to be a direct band gap semiconductor using first-principle calculations17. The B2N monolayer has an ultra-flat surface and consists of 8-membered B6N2 rings and pentagonal B3N2 rings. The 8-membered B6N2 rings are connected to each other through edge-sharing in the y-direction and bridging B3N2 pentagons in the x-direction. B2N is a semiconductor with a direct band gap, making it a promising example of a 2D material with wide-ranging applications19–22.

So far, the literature has reported applications of the B2N monolayer as a sensor for the O-containing VOCs in human breath19, high-capacity material in Li-ion batteries20, and as a potential material for hydrogen storage21,22. However, to the best of our knowledge, the sensing properties of the B2N monolayer towards air-polluting gases have not yet been investigated. In this study, we aim to explore the detection capabilities of the desired B2N monolayer for small molecules such as CO, NO, NO2, SO2, and O3 through electronic and transport calculations of the complexes formed by pairing the B2N monolayer with the considered gases. The study’s novelty lies in showing how a B2N monolayer can effectively identify small molecules in air pollution. This research has the potential to significantly improve the detection of harmful SO2 gas, thanks to its appropriate electronic response and quick recovery time.

Computational methods

The DFT calculations were performed using the SIESTA package23, a well-known software suite for simulating and analyzing computational solid-state physics. The Generalized Gradient Approximation (GGA) in the form of the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional was used23,24. To account for long-range dispersion interactions, Grimme’s DFT-D3 method was used to apply van der Waals (vdW) corrections25–28. This approach enables a more precise representation of the vdW forces that occur between the atoms. The atomic core electrons were modelled using norm-conserving Troullier-Martins pseudopotentials29. These pseudopotentials are commonly used in DFT calculations because they are efficient and accurate. We utilized optimized 4 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack k-point meshes30, and an energy cutoff of 600 eV to precision of the calculations. A vacuum space of 20 Å was set in the Z direction. This parameter is crucial to avoid interactions between periodic images in the calculations. Previous studies conducted by our research group have established the validity of the GGA method6–8,31–40. For the optimized B2N nanosheet, we obtained lattice parameters of a = 7.81 Å, b = 10.03 Å, and c = 20 Å, with α = β = γ = 90˚. These computational parameters, including the chosen method, K-point, energy cutoff, and lattice parameters, align with a previous study on the B2N monolayer17,21,22,41.

Results and discussion

This work focuses on the performance of adsorption and sensing of harmful gases, such as CO, NO, NO2, SO2, and O3 molecules, on the B2N nanosheet using periodic density functional theory. This was accomplished using periodic density functional theory, a quantum mechanical modeling method employed in physics and chemistry to analyze the electronic nanostructure. Before optimizing the B2N monolayer, we first optimized the k points, energy cut-off, and lattice parameters. The relaxed 22 supercell of the B2N monolayer consists of 16 B and 8 N atoms with the space group Amm2, as shown in Fig. 1(a). Figure 1(b) depicts the optimized structure of the harmful gases. The B2N monolayer contains two types of B atoms and rings, labeled as 1 and 2. The potential sites for adsorption of harmful gas molecules on the B2N sheet include the B1, B2, and N atoms, the center of the octagonal R1 and pentagon R2 rings, as well as the B-B and B-N bonds. The optimized bond lengths of the B2N nanosheet are in good agreement with previous research, confirming the reliability of our simulations17,21.

Our research primarily focused on optimizing the interaction of gas with B2N nanosheets, investigating their electronic and thermal properties, and analyzing adsorption energy. The boron atoms in the B2N monolayer, with their empty orbitals, are ideal electron acceptors for interacting with desired molecules. We predicted that these boron atoms are the most likely positions for interacting with the O and N atoms of harmful gas molecules. The octagonal cavity R1 on the B2N nanosheet showed promise as a trap for these molecules, strongly adsorbing them. However, to ensure accurate results, we evaluated all possible adsorption situations and orientations of the adsorbed molecules by examining the adsorption energy. The optimization process involved calculating the adsorption energy of the molecules on the monolayer for all probable adsorption scenarios. The configuration that yielded the best Eads was considered the most stable and is shown in Fig. 2. Among the initial configurations with negative Eads, the most stable one, with the most negative Eads, is depicted in Fig. 2. After determining the structural properties and adsorption energies in optimized complexes, we delved into examining their electronic properties and thermal stability. The results provided intriguing insights into the adsorption of harmful gases on the B2N nanosheet, especially when compared to the adsorbed humidity in the air on the nanosheet. These findings are an essential step toward understanding the interaction of harmful gases with B2N nanosheets and provide a foundation for further research and potential industrial applications. The research contributes to the scientific community’s understanding of this vital process.

In the fields of environmental research, industrial monitoring, and home safety, there is a high demand for gas-sensing technologies that are efficient and accurate. One gas in particular that requires special attention is carbon monoxide (CO). It is a colorless, odorless, and tasteless gas that poses significant health risks. The market for CO sensors is expanding rapidly because of the growing recognition of the hazards associated with CO and the implementation of stricter safety regulations. There is a rising demand for sensors that offer high accuracy and require minimal maintenance, leading to significant technological advancements in this sector. In this study, we have examined and analyzed seventeen different interaction models between the CO molecule and the B2N sheet. The most stable CO/B2N configuration, with an adsorption energy of -1.96 eV, is depicted in Fig. 2. This configuration presents an intriguing interaction model where the CO molecule is positioned diagonally above the B1_B2 bond. The C atom of the CO molecule directly interacts with the B1_B2 bond. The interaction distances between the C atom in the gas and the B1 and B2 atoms on the nanosheet are 2.09 and 1.56 Å, respectively. The C-O bond length of the CO gas increases after adsorption. It is noteworthy that the interaction distance between the B2 atom and the C atom in the CO gas is significantly less, resulting in the B2 atom being pulled up following the adsorption process. This observation is significant as it provides insights into the structural changes that occur during the adsorption process. Interestingly, the oxygen atom of the CO molecule does not directly interact with the B2N surface. This finding suggests that the interaction between the CO molecule and the B2N sheet is primarily facilitated through the C atom, with the O atom playing a fewer role in this process.

Nitrogen monoxide (NO), also known as nitric oxide, is a fascinating compound that greatly affects various aspects of our lives. NO is a colorless gas that is slightly soluble in water and is non-flammable. As a free radical with an unpaired electron, it is highly reactive and can participate in numerous chemical reactions. In the environment, nitrogen monoxide can contribute to the formation of acid rain and photochemical smog, both of which have detrimental effects on our health and the environment. For this reason, the adsorption and sensing of this gas are important. After examining fourteen different models of the interaction between NO and the sheet, the most stable NO/B2N complex is observed when the NO molecule is adsorbed perpendicularly with its N head positioned over the B2 atom. This configuration results in an adsorption energy of -1.39 eV and an interaction distance of 1.39 Å from the surface. Furthermore, in this most stable complex, the NO molecule is oriented in such a way that the O atom is facing upwards. This detail is particularly important because it could potentially affect future interactions or reactions involving the NO molecule.

Nitrogen dioxide (NO2) is a gas that holds great importance in diverse industrial, environmental, and educational settings. NO2 is recognizable by its reddish-brown color and distinct, pungent odor. It belongs to a group of gases called nitrogen oxides, which are notorious for their impact on environmental pollution. Notably, NO2 plays a significant role in the creation of smog and acid rain. Furthermore, it can contribute to global warming and climate change, making the effective management of this pollutant a paramount environmental concern. Hence, the removal of NO2 is of utmost importance. We investigated thirteen different orientations to obtain the most stable configuration between NO2 and the B2N sheet. Figure 2 shows the most stable configuration for the NO2/B2N complex. The NO2/B2N system, with an Eads of -1.8 eV, is more negative than the NO/B2N complex, indicating higher stability. This difference might be attributed to the model and position of NO2 adsorption compared to NO molecules on the B2N surface. The NO2 molecule in the middle of the R1 ring is optimized and interacts with the B2 atom through its O atom, with an interaction distance of 1.55 Å. The lengths of the N-O bonds of the NO2 gas before and after the interaction are 1.25 and 1.47 Å, respectively. The bond length of the NO2 molecule slightly increases after adsorption on the B2N sheet. Due to strong gas adsorption, the B2 atom is tilted upwards.

Sulfur dioxide (SO2) is a colorless gas with a strong, pungent odor that is released into the environment during various industrial processes. This gas poses a significant risk to both human health and the environment. Therefore, the sensing of SO2 gas is crucial in a wide range of applications, including scientific research, environmental testing, and industrial monitoring. The interaction between SO2 gas and R2 ring is diagonal, resulting in the O atom of the gas forming a complex with the B1 atom of the B2N sheet. This complex has an Eads value of -0.7 eV. The value of Eads for SO2 gas is reversible and easily recoverable. To achieve this stable configuration, we investigated eleven different directions and positions for SO2 adsorption on the B2N monolayer. The most stable structure is presented in Tables 1 and Fig. 2. In this system, the adsorbed SO2 molecule is located 1.92 Å away from the surface’ B1 atom. The weakest complex with the longest interaction distance is SO2/B2N complex. The S-O bond length stretches from 1.48 to 1.55 Å after adsorption.

Ozone (O3) is a gas molecule that plays a crucial role in our atmosphere. However, it can have substantial impacts on both our health and the environment. Additionally, it is widely utilized in various industries. While O3 gas does offer benefits, especially in industrial applications, it also presents significant risks to our health and the environment. Therefore, it is essential to effectively manage and monitor O3 gas to reduce negative impacts and maximize its advantages. Among the examination of six models and different orientations of O3 gas on the surface, Fig. 2 displays the most stable complex. The O3 molecule is optimized to be diagonally above the R1 ring on the B2N surface. The O atom of ozone gas has a highly significant interaction with the B2 atom, with an interaction distance of 1.53 Å. The strongest interaction occurs in this complex, with an Eads of -2.36 eV. The O-O bond length of the O3 gas has been extended from 1.43 Å to 1.52 Å upon adsorption. The strong adsorption and exceptional properties of this complex are due to the O3 gas radical character and its adsorption above the R1 ring. Complexes optimized in this position, such as CO, NO2, and O3, exhibit strong adsorption because this cavity is the best position for trapping molecules.

In the next step, we will examine the adsorption of air moisture over the B2N sheet and compare it with the adsorption of harmful gases. We have explored all possible scenarios of water molecule adsorption on the B2N sheet and found only one stable structure. This structure exhibits an adsorption energy of -0.12 eV, which is extremely weak. The H2O molecule is optimally positioned directly above the B1-B2 bond of the B2N sheet, with an interaction distance of 1.92 Å. This result demonstrates that harmful gas molecules can be successfully adsorbed and detect on the B2N sheet, even in humid air.

The desorption and recovery attributes of the B2N monolayer is crucial for the practical and beneficial application of sensors45,46. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate the recovery time (τ) at room temperature to assess the likelihood of harmful gas desorption. Since desorption is the opposite process of adsorption, Eadsrepresents the potential barrier to the desorption process47. By increasing Eads, the temperature required for molecule desorption also increases, resulting in a prolonged recovery time. In simpler terms, the recovery time refers to how long it takes for the sensor to reconfigure once the adsorbed molecules are released. The recovery time, which can be determined experimentally or theoretically, has been calculated using Eq. 3. The τ for desorption of CO, NO, NO2, SO2 and O3 molecules in the CO/B2N, NO/B2N, NO2/B2N, SO2/B2N, and O3/B2N complexes were calculated to be 9.8, 2.5, 1.9, 0.61, and 5.3s at 300 K. SO2molecule is detectable thanks to a suitable recovery time of 0.61 s under ambient conditions, which this recovery time required at room temperature falls within the appropriate range48. The O3/B2N complex has a long recovery time due to strong chemisorption. For the reversible adsorption of molecules, it is recommended that Eadsbe between − 0.60 eV and − 1.0 eV49. Therefore, the recovery time of SO2 gas is more suitable compared to the other adsorbed gases. The adsorption energies of the considered harmful gases on the B2N sheet range from − 0.7 to -2.36 eV, and the corresponding recovery time of the studied complexes, confirm the physisorption nature of SO2 gas and the chemisorption of the other adsorbed gases on the B2N sheet.

Based on the findings in Fig. 5, it is observed that the B2N monolayer acts as a p-type semiconductor with a direct band gap of 0.6 eV. P-type semiconductors are integral components in the electronics industry, playing a crucial role in the functionality of a diverse range of devices. The ‘P’ in p-type signifies ‘positive’, representing the positive charge carriers, or holes, which facilitate the flow of current in these materials. In a p-type semiconductor, the majority of charge carriers are in the form of holes. These empty spaces enable electrons to have transition from one hole to another, generating an electric current. This procedure, referred to as doping, culminates in an abundance of holes, thereby classifying the material as a p-type semiconductor.

The signal in sensor technology refers to the output signal generated by the sensor in response to an environmental change. Typically, this signal takes the form of an electrical voltage or current, which can be measured and analyzed to understand the nature of the change in the environment. Changes in the electronic properties of the sensor can impact the signal. These changes can have important consequences for the performance and reliability of the sensor.

In addition to achieving satisfactory adsorption energy for a harmful gas sensor system, the nanosensor must also exhibit changes in its electrical attributes upon exposure to harmful gases to function as an appropriate nanosensor. Figure 5 presents the band structure (BS), and Fig. 6 shows the total density of states (TDOS) plots for the most stable complexes, aiming to examine the effect of harmful gas adsorption on the electronic characteristics of the B2N monolayer. By analyzing the graphs, it becomes evident that the density of electronic bands expands following the adsorption of harmful gases. When CO and SO2 gases are adsorbed on the B2N sheet, the number of electrical states increases while preserving the semiconductor properties. The conduction and valence bands exhibit a slight deviation from the Fermi level. Furthermore, the direct energy gap in these complexes can be found at the X point. But after the adsorption of gases NO, NO2, and O3 on the surface, the semiconductor properties change to a metallic nature with zero energy gap, making the surface highly sensitive to these three gases.

According to Fig. 5, for adsorbed NO, NO2, and O3 gases, new energy bands intersect the Fermi level, resulting in a metallic nature for the structure. Figure 6 further illustrates that in all areas of the energy gap, new peaks emerge in the NO/B2N, NO2/B2N, and O3/B2N complexes. These peaks bring the conduction and valence band closer together, ultimately reducing the energy gap to zero eV. In the case of the CO/B2N complex, the intensity of the peaks increases in the conduction band within the energy range of 0 to 3, and 6 eV after the adsorption of CO gas. On the other hand, for NO gas, these regions are found within 1 and 6 eV, and for NO2 gas, within the energy range of 0 to 3 eV and 6 to 8 eV. After the adsorption of SO2 gas, the intensity of the peaks increases in the energy range of 1 to 3 and 6 eV, while for O3 gas, it occurs in the region of 1 to 3 and 6 eV. The increase in the number of electrical states within this energy range leads to a rise in the intensity of the desired peak.

Since our criterion for measuring changes in electrical properties is the alteration in the energy gap following the adsorption of harmful gases, this sheet exhibits substantial modifications in its electrical bands located near the Fermi level. Consequently, the Eg also undergoes significant changes. These numerical changes are documented in Table 1, while Figs. 3 and 5, and 6 provide schematic and graphical representations. These findings demonstrate that this surface can serve as both an adsorbent and a suitable sensor for molecules such as NO, NO2, SO2, and O3. It exhibits an adequate electrical response to all of these molecules. The alteration in the electronic device’s electrical properties is manifested as signals that can be observed, analyzed, and measured.

Researchers have observed that sheet work function changed transistors are widely used among measurement devices. These transistors rely on the alteration of the work function that occurs when the desired gas is adsorbed onto the surface. The work function (φ) is the energy required for an electron to move from the surface to a point just the vacuum energy level.

The and EFrepresent the electrostatic potential of a vacuum level located far from the sheet, and the energy of the Fermi level, respectively. To simplify matters, the vacuum level is set at zero. The change in the surface’s φ value before and after interaction indicates that the surface is highly sensitive to the adsorption of adsorbed gases, allowing it to exhibit sensing properties57. The work function φ values are provided in Table 1. A higher work function φ value indicates a greater difficulty in electron emission. According to Table 1, the presence of gas adsorption can greatly alter the field emission characteristics of B2N nanosheets when the difference in work function (Δφ) is larger than 0.20 eV58. The results clearly show that the B2N nanosheet functions as an φ-type sensor, exhibiting a high sensitivity towards NO2, and O3 gases with Δφ of 0.35 and 0.84 respectively.

Toward NO2 and O3 gases, the surface exhibits both electronic and “φ”-type sensor properties. This is evident from the 100% changes observed in the energy gap and changes above 0.2 in the work function after NO2 and O3 gases adsorption on the surface. However, due to their high sensitivity and prolonged recovery time, B2N sheets are better suited as disposable sensors for NO2 and O3 gases. On the other hand, the B2N sheet shows suitable Eads and standard recovery time when exposed to SO2 gas. Additionally, it exhibits acceptable changes in electronic properties. As a result, it can be regarded as a reusable sensor.

As mentioned earlier, the surface used for sensing and adsorbing SO2 gas has both Eads and appropriate recovery time, as well as acceptable changes in electronic properties. This makes it a renewable and reusable sensor. However, for other gases, such as NO2 and O3, disposable sensors can be utilized despite their excellent electronic response. Therefore, we will focus solely on analyzing the alterations in electron transmission properties within the SO2/B2N complex, as this is both more practical and applicable to sensor technology. To better understand the sensor properties of the B2N sheet compared to SO2 gases, we used the NEGF method to study the transmission and current-voltage properties of the sheet and SO2/B2N complex at 0–2 voltages. Figure 8 shows a schematic of the device and the current-voltage characteristics for the B2N sheet (black line) and the SO2/B2N complex (red line). The left and right electrodes are referred to as EL and ER, respectively, and the scattering area in the central region is called scattering. The SO2 gases are adsorbed on the B2N sheet in the scattering section, and the current-voltage properties are examined before and after gas adsorption. To begin, we focused on studying the current-voltage properties under bias voltages ranging from 0 to 2 V, as changes in electrical conductivity are crucial for gas sensors. Therefore, by observing the changes in current resulting from gas adsorption, we can experimentally estimate the ability to detect SO2 gases. Once a voltage is applied, the Fermi level of EL moves upwards relative to ER, and the current only flows through the device when the maximum VB of EL reaches the minimum CB of ER59.

From the current-voltage plot, it can be observed that, for most bias voltages, the current is reduced as a result of SO2 gas adsorption. This reduction in current can be attributed to the resistance caused by SO2 gas molecules. Specifically, the adsorption of gas increases the sheet resistance, leading to a decrease in the device current. In the final section, we will compare the results of the present study with similar studies that have used DFT simulations to sense harmful gases in Table S1 of the supplementary data. The obtained Eads values for SO2gas adsorbed on the ZnO sheet are − 0.671 eV60. However, in the present study, the Eads for SO2 gas on the B2N monolayer is -0.7 eV. The %∆Eg of SO2molecules on the χ3 borophene is nearly 23%61. In the above study, the change in the electronic properties is lower than 28% of the present work. The Eads values for CO and SO2gases adsorbed onto pristine graphene were reported − 0.08 and − 0.28 eV, respectively62. The Eads values for CO/Ti3C2O2, NO/Ti3C2O2, NO2/Ti3C2O2, and SO2/Ti3C2O2are − 0.16, -0.35, -0.18, and − 0.28 eV, respectively63. The Eads values for CO, NO, NO2, and SO2gases adsorbed onto silicane are − 0.29, -0.23, -0.21, and − 0.13 eV, respectively64. The Eads for NO and NO2gases adsorbed onto pure (Au-adsorbed) BSe monolayers are − 0.11 (-0.85) and − 0.35 (-1.12 eV), respectively65. In previous studies by our research group, the Eads values (%∆Eg) for NO and NO2radicals adsorbed on CTF are − 0.17 (85%) and − 0.18 eV (49%), respectively8. After comparing the results of our study with previous research (Table S1) on the adsorption and sensing of desired gases, it is clear that the B2N monolayer outperforms other materials in terms of adsorbing, removing, and sensing CO, NO, NO2, SO2, and O3 gases. This superiority is evident in both adsorption energy and changes in electronic properties. Furthermore, alongside the analysis of Eads and electronic properties, we also investigated current-voltage and transmission curves, as well as work functions and “φ”-type sensor properties. It is worth noting that these aspects have not been thoroughly explored in previous studies.

One notable aspect of this study is that a B2N monolayer can be used to sense harmful gases without requiring structural manipulation. In contrast, certain researchers who discuss harmful gas sensors achieve desirable properties at high costs and through expensive procedures to manipulate the nanostructure62,65–81. Finally, when comparing our results with the adsorption of harmful gases on other substrates already mentioned, we have observed that the adsorption on our studied sheet is more stable than on previously examined surfaces. As a result, the B2N monolayer may potentially provide improved performance for sensing harmful gases and it may open a new horizon for development of harmful gas sensors.

Conclusions

In this study, we employ DFT to investigate the sensor properties of a B2N monolayer in response to five toxic gases: CO, NO, NO2, SO2, and O3. The key findings can be summarized as follows:

(i)The adsorbed molecules induces a significant alteration in the band gap of the B2N sheet, thereby demonstrating a remarkable degree of sensitivity.
