A cilia-associated signature predicts resistance to anti-PD-1 immune checkpoint blockade therapy in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Fei Zhou, Gaohua Feng, Xiaoshen Zhang, Changbin Zhu, Tao Jiang, Tao Hu, Wei Li, Jun Ni, Qian Li, Zhan Huang, Chunxia Su, Shengxiang Ren, Fengying Wu, Caicun Zhou, Guanghui Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTargeting immune checkpoints, specifically programmed death 1, has significantly improved clinical outcomes in non-small cell lung cancer. However, only a proportion of patients benefited from immune checkpoint blockades (ICBs) therapy. Thus, identifying novel biomarkers to better select responders to ICBs is critically important in clinical practice.MethodsThis retrospective study included two cohorts, a discovery cohort (n = 24) and a validation cohort (n = 21) of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) patients treated with ICB at Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital between April 2016 and November 2019. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue samples were collected for RNA sequencing. Additionally, a TCGA dataset (GSE135222) and a clinical study dataset (POPLAR) were utilized as external validation and test sets, respectively.ResultsIn the discovery cohort, cilia-associated pathways were significantly enriched in LUAD patients with non-durable benefit. A three-gene cilia-associated signature (TMEM100, DNAH3, and NEFH) was established and effectively predicted resistance to ICB treatment (discovery cohort, Hazard Ratio [HR, 95% CI] = 0.21 [0.056–0.791], P = 3.64e-05; validation cohort, HR [95% CI] = 0.200 [0.049–0.830], P = 0.0003). This signature was further validated in an external cohort (HR [95% CI] = 0.461 [ 0.164–1.292], P = 0.067). Further analysis demonstrated that the cilia-associated signature was negatively associated with various types of tumor-infiltrating immune cells, including effector T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells.ConclusionOur findings suggest that cilia-associated signature may serve as a potential biomarker to identify LUAD patients with reduced likelihood of benefiting from ICB therapy, though further validation in larger cohorts is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04332-0.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases and is primarily categorized into adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large cell carcinoma subtypes1. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the most common subtype, constitutes about 40% of lung cancers. Unfortunately, LUAD is frequently diagnosed at advanced or metastatic stages, often with metastases to the central nervous system, liver, and bone2. More than 50% of LUAD patients carry driver gene mutations (such as EGFR, ALK, ROS1, RET, and ERBB2) that make them eligible for targeted therapies. However, patients lacking these mutations generally do not benefit from such treatments. The advent of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has significantly improved survival in driver gene-negative LUAD patients34. Nevertheless, responses to ICB treatment vary considerably, with response rate ranging from 26% to 46.1% [3, 5].

In LUAD, the most promising biomarkers for ICB monotherapy include programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression6 and tumor mutational burden (TMB)78. However, due to the complexity of the anti-tumor immune cycle, relying solely on a single biomarker may lead to biased predictions of treatment efficacy9. Although PD-L1 is widely used, it remains suboptimal as a biomarker10. Similarly, TMB has predictive value for ICB in NSCLC11, but the lack of a standardized computational approach complicates its interpretation and reliability12. Thus, there is an urgent clinical need to identify more effective biomarkers to distinguish patients who may resist ICB treatment.

Cilia are considered a degenerated and redundant cellular lineage expressed in nearly all cell types. Recently, the close connection between cilia and cancer growth has become an intriguing topic13. For example, in melanoma, studies have shown that the loss of primary cilia may accelerate the progression of the disease14. Interestingly, cilia can regulate various aspects of the immune system, including modulating angiogenesis and enhancing the responses of CD8+ T cells and B cell15. However, how cilia signature affects the efficacy of immunotherapy of LUAD remains scarce. This study aims to explore the correlation between ciliary features and the tumor microenvironment (TME), and how this affects the efficacy of immunotherapy, by analyzing transcriptomic data and treatment outcomes from LUAD patients.

Methods

Patient samples

Two independent cohorts, a discovery cohort (n = 24) and a validation cohort (n = 21), consisting of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue specimens from patients with LUAD were included in this retrospective study. All specimens were collected prior to ICB treatment at Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital between April 2016 and November 2019. Follow-up data was obtained from electronic medical record system. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital (K23-303). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees, as well as the principles outlined in the 2013 revision of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Tumor response was assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 [16]. Treatment responses were assessed at baseline and after every two cycles of ICB treatment using computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. Progression-free survival (PFS) was calculated from the initiation of ICB treatment until confirmation of progressive disease, death, or last follow-up. Based on the clinical outcomes, patients were categorized into two groups: those with durable clinical benefit (DCB; PFS ≥ 6 months) and those with non-durable benefit (NDB; PFS < 6 months).

RNA-seq library construction

Whole-transcriptome RNA sequencing was conducted using total RNA extracted from the FFPE specimens with a commercially available kit (AmoyDx, China). Library construction was performed by external technicians affiliated with AmoyDx and sequenced using an Illumina platform (Illumina, USA). Raw sequencing reads were trimmed, processed, and aligned against the human transcriptome using STAR. Expression counts for each sample were quantified via FeatureCounts with fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) normalization.

Gene expression and enrichment analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed using DEseq2 with standard thresholds (padj < 0.05, |log2FoldChange| > 1) to identify genes enriched in ICB-responsive groups. Subsequently, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were subjected to functional enrichment analysis through Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways using the ClusterProfiler tool, aiming to identify key biological processes and pathways associated with ICB response. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed via gene set variation analysis (GSVA) to further confirm the enrichment patterns.

Feature selection

The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) method was implemented via the glmnet package to identify essential genes for constructing a predictive model of therapeutic efficacy, using pathways significantly differentially expressed between groups as input. Features with non-zero coefficients selected by LASSO were then included in a Cox regression model to assess the statistical significance of each contributing gene. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) was used to evaluate performance of the constructed model for predicting response to immunotherapy in both discovery and validation cohorts. Association with reported TME gene sets and IFN-γ signature were further analyzed and validated in the TCGA LUAD cohort, as well as in an external cohort of ICB-treated LUAD patients (GSE135222)17. Additionally, survival, TME, and pathway enrichment characteristics were analyzed and validated using NSCLC patients from the POPLAR study cohort18.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data analysis

The scRNA-seq dataset GSE148071 [19] was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). This dataset contains baseline tissue samples from 18 LUAD patients. The downloaded data were in txt format and included patient identifiers. Quality control metrics were applied to filter out low-quality cells and potential doublets. The Seurat package (version 5.3.0) in R was used for all downstream processing and analysis of the scRNA-seq data. A total of 7,724 high-quality single cells were obtained for analysis after quality control filtering. Data normalization was performed using the normalize data function with default parameters, followed by the identification of highly variable features using FindVariableFeatures. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted for dimensionality reduction, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) was used for visualization of cell clusters. Cell type identification was performed using established marker genes, with major cell populations including epithelial cells (club cells, alveolar type I cells, alveolar type II cells, basal airway epithelial cells), macrophages, neutrophils, fibroblasts, and myeloid cells. The analysis focused on examining the proportion and expression intensity of different genes across cell subpopulations, as evidenced by the visualization of specific markers including FOXJ1, TPPP3, PIFO, TMEM100, NEFH, and DNAH3 across different cell clusters. Finally, spatial localization patterns of genes within cells were analyzed to understand their functional distribution and cellular compartmentalization.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients. Fisher’s exact test was performed to compare the distributions of categorical variables such as treatment regimens and objective response between durable clinical benefit (DCB) and non-durable benefit (NDB) groups. Survival outcomes, including PFS and overall survival (OS), were visualized using the Kaplan-Meier method, and the differences between subtypes were compared using the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (https://www.r-project.org/, v 4. 2. 0). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Broad transcriptome expression levels inadequate for predicting ICB treatment efficacy

To identify pre-treatment signatures predictive of response to ICB in advanced LUAD, we conducted transcriptome sequencing on biopsied FFPE tumor samples from 24 patients (discovery cohort) before initiating ICB treatment. In the discovery cohort, four patients received first-line immunotherapy, and 20 received second-line immunotherapy. The specific regimens, including the proportions of mono-immunotherapy and bispecific antibody immunotherapy, are summarized in Table 1. The medications used included Camrelizumab, Tislelizumab, Pembrolizumab, Sintilimab, and KN046 (an anti-PD-L1/CTLA-4 bispecific antibody). Among the patients, 17 (70.8%) received mono-immunotherapy and seven (29.2%) received dual immunotherapy. Fisher’s exact test comparing treatment regimens between DCB and NDB groups showed no significant difference (P = 0.133). Baseline demographic details are presented in Fig. 1A; Table 1. The overall response rate (ORR) was 41.7% (10/24) consistent with response rates reported in a previous study21. Among 24 patients treated with ICB, 15 demonstrated DCB and showed significantly higher PFS than NDB patients, as determined by the log-rank test (P < 0.0001, Fig. 1B), while showing no difference were observed in OS (P = 0.61, Supplementary Fig. 1). Despite distinct clinical outcomes, unsupervised clustering of pre-treatment transcriptomes did not reveal clear stratification based on clinical response (Fig. 1C). This suggests that ICB response predictions may rely on specific biological signatures rather than broad transcriptome expression.

Cilia-associated signatures are enriched in patients resistant to ICB treatment

To delineate the biological processes and pathway differences between the DCB and NDB groups in discovery cohort, GSVA was conducted based on transcriptomic data. Differential expression analysis revealed 115 significantly upregulated genes in the DCB group and 54 in the NDB group (|log2(fold change) | ≥ 1, padj < 0.05). The top DEGs indicated elevated expression of immunotherapy response-related genes, especially immunoglobulins, in the DCB group, whereas the functional roles of genes upregulated in the NDB group were less clearly defined (Fig. 2A). Overrepresentation analysis using GO annotations demonstrated that unresponsive patients were enriched for signatures associated with cytoskeletal elements, as well as cilia (Fig. 2B). Gene set enrichment analysis further confirmed these inferences extended to include variation across other genes within these pathways (Fig. 2C). While associations between cytoskeletal elements and tumor progression have been previously studied, it is less clear what role cilia-associated genes may play in influencing tumor progression and patient response to ICB. To assess the significance of this cilia-associated signature, LASSO was used to select the features for further analysis (Fig. 2D). From an initial set of six LASSO-selected features, 3 genes (TMEM100, DNAH3, and NEFH) were identified as statistically significant predictors (Fig. 2E). These were used to construct a cilia-associated risk score with the formula: Cilia risk Score = 2.4484*expr (DNAH3) + 1.8422*expr (NEFH) + 3.1607*expr (TMEM100). These results suggest that cilia-associated gene expression signature may serve as a predictor of ICB response in LUAD patient. In discovery cohort, the cilia-associated signature showed excellent performance (AUC > 0.9), surpassing previously proposed signatures related to T cell and interferon response (Fig. 3A), and corresponded to an excellent prediction of the PFS of LUAD patients following ICB Hazard Ratio [HR, 95%CI] = 0.21 [ 0.056–0.791], P = 3.64e-05, Fig. 3B-C).

Validation of cilia-associated signatures as predictor of resistance to ICB treatment

The performance of the cilia-associated risk model was further evaluated in validation cohort and an external cohort. Similar predictive trends were observed in validation cohort (Table 2), though with a slightly reduced AUC of 0.718 (Fig. 3D-E). Patients categorized as cilia-low continued to exhibit significantly better outcomes following immunotherapy (HR [95%CI] = 0.200 [0.049–0.830], P = 0.0003, Fig. 3F). To address potential confounders such as demographic and tumor stage heterogeneity, the model was applied to an external validation cohort comprising 27 LUAD patients. The three-gene cilia signature consistently identified patients with prolonged PFS (HR [95%CI] = 0.461 [ 0.164–1.292], P = 0.067) and outperformed existing immune-based signatures (Fig. 3G-I). Collectively, these findings indicate the potential robustness and generalizability of the cilia-associated signature in predicting ICB response across multiple independent LUAD cohorts, though further validation in larger cohorts is warranted.

Cilia-associated signatures are associated with TME

Given the strong link between the cilia-associated signature and ICB response, we further explored its association with TME. Interestingly, co-activation molecules were significantly enriched in the low-risk group compared to the high-risk group. Several other immune modules (class I MHC, effector T cells, and macrophages and dendritic cells) also exhibited trends toward differential expression, though these did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4A). Through gene set enrichment analysis, we could further confirm that a significant number of immune activity-related pathways were strongly down-regulated in the high-risk group (Fig. 4B). These also included pathways involving antibody-driven Fc receptor signaling and lymphoid cell interactions (Fig. 4C). Since our cohort is inherently limited in size, we then examined whether this correlation could be generalized across a broader LUAD patients. Meta-analysis of the TCGA LUAD cohort confirmed that each of the four immune modules showed significantly lower expression in the high-risk group (Fig. 4D). This was supported by a concurrent observation of the down-regulation of pathways associated with immune activity in this group, especially in terms of antibody-producing B cell activity (Fig. 4E-F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the cilia-associated signature can effectively stratify LUAD tumors based on distinct immune cell signatures.

Cilia-associated signatures demonstrate immunotherapy-specific predictive capacity

To further test the predictive performance of the cilia-associated signatures in immunotherapy efficacy, we performed additional analyses using transcriptomic and clinical data obtained from the phase II POPLAR clinical study18. In the atezolizumab monotherapy cohort, the cilia-low group exhibited a statistically significant improvement in PFS compared to the cilia-high group (P = 0.017, Fig. 5A). In contrast, no significant association was observed between cilia score and PFS in the docetaxel chemotherapy cohort (P = 0.43, Fig. 5B), indicating that the predictive capacity of the cilia signature is specific to immunotherapy rather than chemotherapy. Subsequently, we performed pathway enrichment analysis within the TME of the immunotherapy cohort. The results confirmed that the GO-Biological Process (BP) cilium organization and the ciliopathies Wiki pathways were significantly enhanced in the cilia-high group (Fig. 5C). Conversely, the KEGG T cell receptor signaling pathway and the Reactome PD-1 signaling pathways showed upregulation in cilia-low group. These results were confirmed through inspection of the running enrichment curves, where the GO-BP microtubule bundle formation process could also be seen to be upregulated in the cilia-high group (Fig. 5D). Assessment of the immune microenvironment found that effector cells, including NK cells and T cells, and checkpoint molecules were significantly enriched in the cilia-low group (Fig. 5E). These results may explain the ability of the cilia-associated signature to predict ICB treatment response and underscore its robustness and generalizability across multiple independent cohorts.

Expression of cilia-associated signatures on a single-cell level

To further investigate the expression patterns of the three cilia-associated genes within the highly heterogeneous cellular landscape of LUAD tumors, we turned to reference single-cell transcriptome data19. Using t-SNE visualization, we identified major cell clusters (Fig. 6A). The analysis revealed that established ciliary markers, including FOXJ1, TPPP3, and PIFO, were highly expressed in club cells, a predominant subtype of epithelial cells. Importantly, TMEM100, NEFH, and DNAH3 also showed high expression in the same club cell population, indicating their cilia-associated properties (Fig. 6B). The co-localization analysis demonstrated that TMEM100, NEFH, and DNAH3 exhibited consistent spatial distribution patterns with FOXJ1, TPPP3, and PIFO, providing evidence that these genes share similar biological behaviors and function as cilia-associated markers (Fig. 6C). Collectively, these results indicate that the elements of the cilia-associated signature may actually be reflecting the gene expression behavior of club cell populations with vital roles in forming the tumor immune microenvironment.

Discussion

Polygenic signatures derived from gene expression data offer a potent means to analyze complex clinical specimens and predict clinical outcome. In this study, we used a straightforward transcriptome profiling approach on FFPE samples to link gene expression with clinical ICB response in advanced LUAD. Through a rigorous multi-step discovery and validation process, a three-gene cilia-associated signature capable of effectively predicting clinical response to ICB treatment in LUAD patients. Interestingly, this signature outperforms previously proposed models centered around PD-L1 expression alone, or purely immune activity-based modules22. This research also indicates that cilia phenotype may play a role in regulating tumor-immunity crosstalk, with one gene in the signature (NEFH) showing expression in macrophages. Further validation in larger cohorts remains necessary to fully examine the clinical implications of this signature.

Although the molecules identified in our study show promise as predictive biomarkers, their precise mechanistic roles remain to be fully elucidated. Further research into these genes may uncover molecular pathways that could modulate the cilia-associated phenotype, which we have found to be negatively correlated with response to ICB, and potentially enabling more advanced LUAD patients to benefit from immunotherapy. For instance, DNAH3 is a constituent of the microtubule-associated motor protein complex, and mutations in these genes have been previously reported to be linked with chemotherapy response in the context of gastric cancer23, as well as LUAD lesions mimicking organizing pneumonia24. It is possible that perturbation with microtubule inhibitors may meaningfully restrict the functional consequences of DNAH3 overexpression, and be a useful complement to ICB blockade for these patients. TMEM100, an intracellular transmembrane protein, has been previously shown to play a role in the early differentiation of endothelial cells2526. In the current context, we speculate that TMEM100 may also potentially play a role in the development of tumor neoangiogenesis. NEFH is a more curious case, as it is traditionally understood to be expressed in neurons as a neurofilament protein27. However, in our current analysis, we find some expression of NEFH across multiple immune cell populations. The functional implications of this expression pattern remain unclear and warrant further investigation. Therefore, future studies using in vitro models and other preclinical systems will be essential to validate these hypotheses and clarify the biological functions of these genes within the TME.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size of this cohort may limit the statistical power of our analyses. Second, the absence of a statistically significant OS difference between DCB and NDB groups may be attributable to heterogeneous post-progression treatments and potential crossover effects inherent in retrospective settings. Third, although significant difference in PFS was observed in POPLAR data, indicating the potential clinical relevance of our three-gene signature, the external validation cohort also showed a non-significant trend (P = 0.067), suggesting the need for validation in larger independent cohorts. In contrast, PFS and DCB/NDB classification more accurately reflect on-treatment efficacy. Furthermore, although our results provide important clinical associations, the three-gene cilia-associated signature (TMEM100, DNAH3, and NEFH) lacks direct mechanistic evidence from wet-lab functional validation. The biological mechanisms underlying the association between cilia-associated gene expression and ICB resistance remain to be elucidated. Experiments such as cell-based functional assays and in vivo studies, which are essential to confirm the biological relevance of our observations, have not yet been performed. We acknowledge these limitations and note that these investigations are currently under active planning as part of our ongoing follow-up research.

Conclusions

Our study identifies a novel cilia-associated gene signature that provides insights into the complex interplay between PD-1/PD-L1-directed monoclonal antibodies, tumor-related genes, and immunotherapy response in LUAD. Further investigations in larger prospective cohorts and functional studies are warranted to fully establish its clinical utility.
