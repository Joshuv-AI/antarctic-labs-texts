Synthetic food pigment sunset yellow potentiates food allergy via immune dysregulation and intestinal barrier dysfunction
Authors: Dingjun Wei, Ye Ye, Qiang Huang, Mingchan Deng, Xue Feng, Philippe Madjirebaye, Yuting Fan, Yike Huang, Xuli Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmerging evidence from clinical and experimental studies has suggested potential associations between artificial food pigments like Sunset Yellow (SY) and allergic reactions, though their mechanistic contributions remain unclear. This study investigated the immunological and epithelial effects of the synthetic pigment SY using a translational approach integrating mouse model assays. In the in vivo model, SY co-administration significantly exacerbated allergic responses, manifesting as enhanced weight loss, diarrhea, and elevated levels of histamine, MCP-1, and OVA-specific immunoglobulins (IgE and IgG). SY exposure promoted pronounced Th2 polarization, characterized by increased IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 production alongside suppressed IL-2 levels, while maintaining comparably low levels of IFN-γ, TGF-β, and IL-10. Histopathological analyses revealed severe intestinal barrier damage and epithelial disruption. Complementary in vitro studies demonstrated that SY enhanced IL-1β secretion and subsequently downregulated tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Claudin-1). Mechanistically, SY was associated with activation of the MEKK1/IKK/NF-κB signaling pathway, establishing a self-amplifying inflammatory cycle that compromises epithelial integrity. These findings elucidate a multifaceted mechanism by which synthetic colorants may exacerbate allergic inflammation, underscoring the need to reconsider their safety assessment in food regulatory frameworks.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Diseases, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

In recent years, growing public concern over food safety has positioned food allergy research as a critical focus. Food allergy (FA) is a major public health problem, characterized by an abnormal immune response to specific food proteins such as ovalbumin and whey protein1. Clinical manifestations range from mild symptoms like skin itching and abdominal pain to severe, life-threatening anaphylaxis and respiratory distress2. Over the past two decades, the global prevalence of FAs has surged, now affecting 8–10% of children and 4–6% of adults, underscoring its escalating burden and its connection to industrialization-driven shifts in food processing and dietary habits2,3.

This dietary transformation, fueled by economic development and food technology breakthroughs, has increased the use of processing techniques and additives to enhance shelf life, sensory properties, and convenience4. Consequently, human dietary exposure to synthetic or “unnatural” ingredients has significantly risen, a trend hypothesized as a key driver behind the increasing FA prevalence5. Despite their widespread use, the health risks of many food additives, particularly concerning allergic sensitization, remain poorly understood6.

Among the myriad food additives, synthetic azo colorants like Sunset Yellow (SY) are of growing concern due to their pervasive presence in beverages, snacks, and confectionery products7. SY is linked to adverse effects, including oxidative stress, DNA damage, and neurobehavioral disorders8–11. Furthermore, synthetic colors may act in a “stealth mode” by binding to host proteins, potentially disrupting immune regulation, activating inflammatory cascades, and increasing intestinal permeability to large antigens, thereby promoting allergic sensitization12. Recent studies specifically on SY show it exacerbates intestinal endoplasmic reticulum stress, promotes pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and disrupts immune homeostasis13, with epidemiological evidence linking SY intake to allergic conditions like edema and urticaria14. The impact of food additives on allergy is not uniform, highlighting a complex, compound-specific nature. For instance, the artificial sweetener sucralose has been shown to exacerbate food allergy in mouse models by modulating dendritic cells and enhancing Th2 immune responses15–17. Conversely, the sweetener acesulfame potassium can alleviate food allergy via mast cell stabilization and MAPK-mediated immune regulation18. This contrast underscores the necessity for rigorous, additive-specific safety evaluations.

The gastrointestinal tract is a primary site for both food allergen exposure and the body’s defense19. A compromised intestinal mucosal barrier is strongly associated with allergic pathogenesis20. This integrated barrier, comprising tight junction proteins, a mucus layer, and immune cells, is crucial for blocking pathogen/allergen invasion and maintaining immune homeostasis21,22. When barrier integrity is impaired, allergens can penetrate the epithelium, activate surface receptors, and induce inflammatory responses that further increase permeability, creating a vicious cycle of immune dysregulation23. This process can lead to immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated hypersensitivity24. Mechanistically, the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway is a key regulator of intestinal permeability. Upon activation by stimuli like interleukin-1β, upstream kinases trigger the IκB kinase (IKK) complex, leading to IκBα degradation and the nuclear translocation of the NF-κB p50/p65 heterodimer. This subsequently upregulates genes such as myosin light chain kinase (MLCK), which disrupts tight junction stability, increases epithelial permeability, and is a pivotal event in FA development (MLCK)25.

To address the critical research gap concerning the allergenic potential of SY, we employed an integrated in vivo and in vitro strategy. Using an ovalbumin (OVA)-sensitized mouse model, we assessed allergic responses through serum-specific antibodies, histamine, cytokines, and clinical scores. Complementary in vitro studies with a Caco-2 monolayer elucidated the mechanistic role of SY in disrupting intestinal barrier integrity and activating the NF-κB p65 pathway. This comprehensive approach provides direct evidence that SY exacerbates FA by impairing the intestinal barrier and activating pro-inflammatory signaling, offering a novel perspective and a theoretical foundation for refining the risk assessment of food additives.

Results

Impact of SY treatment on physiological parameters in OVA-sensitized mice

To characterize the effects of SY on the overall physiological and allergic responses in OVA-sensitized mice, we first evaluated body weight changes and clinical allergy symptoms in all experimental groups. These assessments provide a fundamental overview of the systemic impact of SY co-exposure during allergen sensitization and challenge. Initial body weights were comparable across all experimental groups. After 28 days of intervention, mice receiving SY alone maintained body weights equivalent to the PBS control group. However, in the final body weight measurement, significantly lower weights were observed in both the OVA-sensitized group and the OVA + SY co-treatment group compared with the blank control group (Fig. 1B). Notably, the combination treatment groups (OVA + SY) demonstrated more severe allergic symptoms compared to OVA treatment alone (Fig. 1C). These results indicate that SY administration may enhance OVA-induced allergic responses, as evidenced by both physiological (weight loss) and clinical (diarrhea) parameters. Further mechanistic studies are required to elucidate these observed effects.

SY potentiates OVA-induced allergic responses in sensitized mice

Analysis of serum biomarkers revealed distinct, treatment-dependent groups. Mice sensitized with OVA + SY exhibited a significant increase in MCP-1 levels and an upward trend in histamine compared to the control group (Fig. 1D, E). Immunoglobulin profiling showed comparable IgE, IgG, and IgG1 levels between SY-only and the control group. However, OVA-challenged mice exhibited substantial increases in all three antibody classes. Compared with the OVA group, the OVA + SY group showed significantly higher levels of IgE and IgG, while no significant difference in IgG1 level was observed between the two groups (Fig. 1F–H). These findings indicate that SY exacerbates allergic immune responses under alum-assisted OVA sensitization without acting as an independent sensitizer. SY amplifies immune activation and the secretion of allergic mediators, thereby aggravating OVA-triggered allergic reactions. This observed synergy highlights the potential public health risk of SY consumption in sensitized individuals, as it may significantly amplify the severity of FA reactions.

Serum cytokine profile detection

Analysis of serum cytokine profiles revealed that SY exacerbates OVA-induced allergic inflammation (Fig. 2A–G). Co-administration of SY with OVA significantly amplified the OVA-specific Th2 response, marked by elevated levels of IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 compared to the OVA-only group. Compared with the control group, the levels of IL-2 and IFN-γ were decreased in the OVA-sensitized group. Further reduction of these two cytokines was observed in the OVA + SY group, suggesting that SY intervention further downregulated the expression of the detected Th1-associated cytokines under OVA-induced allergic conditions. These data demonstrate that SY exacerbates OVA-induced allergy by promoting the release of Th2-related cytokines, thereby potentiating allergic inflammation.

Cytokine production in splenocyte cultures

Analysis of splenocyte culture supernatants (Fig. 2H–N) depicted that SY co-treatment notably potentiates the OVA-driven immunomodulatory response. OVA + SY group exhibited a synergistic enhancement of Th2 activity, demonstrated by a significant increase in IL-4 and IL-5 production, alongside a strong upward trend in IL-13, compared to OVA stimulation alone. Conversely, IL-2 exhibited a downward trend in the OVA + SY group relative to the OVA group, whereas no notable change was found in IFN-γ levels. The levels of the regulatory cytokines IL‑10 and TGF‑β were assessed across the experimental groups. As shown in Fig. 2N, IL‑10 levels were significantly reduced in the OVA group compared with the blank control group, while the OVA + SY group showed no further significant decrease relative to the OVA group. For TGF‑β (Fig. 2M), although both the OVA and OVA + SY groups exhibited lower mean levels than the blank control and SY groups, no statistically significant differences were observed among the groups. This in vivo cytokine profile mirrors the serum findings, showing that SY exacerbates allergic sensitization by amplifying Th2 cytokines and altering Th1 and Treg-related responses.

Duodenal histopathological alterations

Histological assessment of duodenal sections (Fig. 3A) demonstrated progressive tissue damage between treatment groups. The control group maintained standard villous architecture with intact epithelial layers, while SY-treated mice showed minimal morphological changes. OVA-sensitized mice exhibited marked pathological features, including villous shortening, inflammatory cell infiltration, and epithelial disruption. The most severe histopathological changes were observed in the OVA + SY co-treatment group, which demonstrated near-complete villous atrophy, disintegration of the lamina propria, and extensive epithelial erosion. This synergistic exacerbation of intestinal damage suggests that SY co-exposure critically compromises barrier integrity, thereby facilitating enhanced antigen translocation and perpetuating a cycle of systemic immune activation that amplifies the allergic response.

Alterations in colonic tight junction protein expression

Western blot analysis of colonic tissue (Fig.3B–D) showed remarkable, synergistic damage to the intestinal barrier, as evidenced by the progressive downregulation of key tight junction proteins to tight junction proteins (Occludin, Claudin1). Expression of Occludin and Claudin1 was only slightly reduced by SY treatment alone. OVA challenge induced a more substantial loss, significantly reducing Claudin1, while Occludin merely showed a downward trend. Critically, the OVA + SY co-treatment group exhibited the most profound suppression of both proteins, indicating a severe compromise of barrier integrity. Treatment with SY combined with OVA markedly altered the expression of intestinal tight junction proteins. Such molecular changes may impair intestinal barrier function, potentially disturbing intestinal physiological integrity and favoring the passive diffusion of luminal harmful substances.

Effects of SY on Caco-2 cell viability and inflammatory factor production

The effect of SY on the viability of Caco-2 cells was determined using a CCK-8 assay (Fig. 4A). Based on this, three non-cytotoxic concentrations, low (SY-L, 0.625 μg/mL), medium (SY-M, 1.25 μg/mL), and high (SY-H, 2.5 μg/mL), were selected for subsequent evaluation. Critically, compared to the OVA-only group, SY + OVA treatment significantly elevated intestinal IL-1β levels, with a notably potent effect in the low-dose (SY-L) group compared to the medium (SY-M) and high (SY-H) doses (Fig. 4B).

SY-mediated potentiation of OVA-induced tight junction disruption in Caco-2 cells

Western blot analysis displayed that OVA exposure alone powerfully downregulated ZO-1 expression. Although the representative Western blot bands showed a slight reduction in Claudin1 protein levels in the OVA group, no statistically significant difference was observed compared with the blank group (Fig. 4C–E). The co-administration of SY with OVA markedly produced dose-dependent reductions in Claudin1 expression, with the high-dose SY group (2.5 μg/mL) showing significantly greater ZO-1 suppression compared to OVA treatment alone. This coordinated downregulation of pivotal tight junction proteins provides a mechanistic basis for SY-induced barrier dysfunction, an effect likely potentiated by the SY-amplified IL-1β secretion previously observed. These in vitro results directly corroborate our in vivo histological and molecular findings, demonstrating that SY actively disrupts epithelial integrity. By facilitating enhanced allergen penetration, SY thereby creates a permissive environment that amplifies the initiation and propagation of the allergic response cascade. In the absence of OVA challenge, treatment with SY alone (SY-L, SY-M, SY-H) did not reduce Claudin-1 expression; instead, a slight upregulation was observed, likely reflecting a cellular adaptive response or non-specific stimulation of the Caco-2 monolayer under basal conditions. In contrast, when combined with OVA, SY dose-dependently exacerbated the OVA-induced reduction in Claudin-1 expression, with the most profound suppression observed in the OVA + SY-H group (Fig. 4E).

Activation of NF-κB signaling pathway in response to OVA and SY co-exposure

Protein analysis indicated activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway in response to co-exposure, as evidenced by decreased IκB-α and increased p-IκB-α (Fig. 5). Although total MEKK1 expression remained stable between groups, levels of phosphorylated MEKK1 (P-MEKK1) were markedly elevated, indicating upstream activation of this key regulatory kinase. The most pronounced effects were observed in the high-dose OVA + SY group, which exhibited substantial IκB-α degradation, increased phosphorylated IκB-α (p-IκB-α), and elevated NF-κB p65 levels. These molecular changes suggest that SY synergizes with OVA to activate the canonical NF-κB pathway, with upstream MEKK1 phosphorylation potentially contributing to IκB phosphorylation and degradation. The subsequent liberation and translocation of NF-κB p65 provides a mechanistic explanation for the enhanced inflammatory response and exacerbation of allergic symptoms observed under combined exposure, positioning NF-κB activation as a likely signaling node in SY-aggravated FA.

Discussion

Increasing epidemiological evidence suggests that artificial pigments are linked to a higher risk of childhood allergies26. Despite this, the synthetic edible pigment SY continues to be widely used. To address this public health issue, we employed a translational research approach integrating findings from both animal models and cell culture systems, enabling a comprehensive assessment of SY’s potential impact on food allergy.

Our results demonstrated that SY treatment alone exerted negligible effects on physiological and immunological parameters, suggesting that SY by itself lacks intrinsic allergenic or inflammatory potential in the absence of a sensitizing antigen. However, when co-administered with OVA, SY markedly exacerbated hallmark features of allergic inflammation. Clinically, mice exposed to OVA + SY exhibited more severe diarrhea compared with OVA alone. These outcomes were paralleled by increases in serum histamine and MCP-1, indicating heightened effector-cell activation, a phenomenon also reported for other dietary adjuvants15,27. SY enhanced OVA-specific IgE and IgG production, suggesting promotion of B-cell activation and class switching within a Th2-skewed environment28,29. Cytokine profiling confirmed pronounced immune polarization, with SY co-treatment elevating Th2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) while significantly suppressing IL-2. In contrast, IFN-γ and TGF-β, remained comparably low in both OVA and OVA + SY groups, indicating that SY sustains rather than initiates the immunosuppressive milieu characteristic of FA. Such cytokine imbalances are well-recognized hallmarks of allergic inflammation30 and have been widely reported in murine OVA models28,31. Notably, unlike interventions such as baicalein that suppress NF-κB activation and attenuate Th2 responses and ameliorate allergic responses32, SY treatment promoted NF-κB activation, thereby amplifying Th2 profiling-driven inflammation, underscoring its role as a pro-inflammatory modulator in the context of allergic sensitization. The capacity of food additives to activate pro-inflammatory pathways and disrupt intestinal barrier function, thereby amplifying allergic responses, is not an isolated phenomenon unique to SY. A growing body of evidence implicates other common synthetic additives in similar mechanisms. For instance, the synthetic red dye Allura Red AC has been demonstrated to promote susceptibility to experimental colitis in mice33. Furthermore, beyond colorants, synthetic food colors may act in a stealth mode by binding to body proteins, which can disrupt immune regulation, activate inflammatory cascades, and increase intestinal permeability to large antigenic molecules, thereby promoting sensitization to allergens12. This proposed mechanism of impaired barrier function and immune activation provides a plausible common pathway through which diverse additives, including SY, could exacerbate allergic responses.

Histopathological analyses provided additional support for this mechanism. OVA + SY mice exhibited severe villous atrophy, epithelial erosion, and lamina propria disintegration, resembling barrier breakdown described in other dietary adjuvant models34–36. This structural damage facilitates allergen penetration and systemic immune activation23, with in vitro assays confirming SY-enhanced IL-1β secretion and tight junction protein (ZO-1, Claudin1) reduction in Caco-2 monolayers.

OVA challenge alone reduced ZO-1 and Claudin-1 expression without altering IL-1β levels, indicating the involvement of IL-1β-independent mechanisms. Several alternative pathways could mediate this OVA-induced barrier impairment. The OVA-specific IgE cross-linking on mast cells can directly release mast cell proteases (tryptase, chymase), which are known to cleave tight junction proteins and increase paracellular permeability37,38. The observed Th2 polarization with elevated IL-4 and IL-13 can directly downregulate tight junction protein expression through STAT6 signaling39. Other pro-inflammatory, particularly TNF-α, can activate NF-κB signaling and myosin light chain kinase (MLCK) to phosphorylate tight junction proteins, leading to their internalization and barrier disruption40. These pathways may collectively compromise intestinal integrity during allergic sensitization.

At the molecular level, NF-κB signaling emerged as a pivotal driver of the observed effects. Pronounced IκB-α degradation and p65 activation in OVA + SY mice align with the established role of NF-κB in regulating Th2 cytokines, IgE synthesis, and epithelial inflammatory signaling41,42. Inhibition of NF-κB has been shown to alleviate OVA-induced FA and airway inflammation28,32, whereas enhanced activation exacerbates disease, suggesting that NF-κB acts as the molecular link between SY exposure, barrier dysfunction, and Th2 immune response. Further, upstream of NF-κB, Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase Kinase Kinase 1 (MEKK1) serves as a key MAP3K that activates the IκB kinase (IKK) complex, leading to phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and degradation of IκB, followed by nuclear translocation of NF-κB dimers (p65/p50) to drive transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-643. IL-1β itself further activates the classical NF-κB pathway, contributing to intestinal barrier disruption, while the observed increase in phosphorylated MEKK1, together with NF-κB pathway changes, suggests a potential therapeutic target for allergic inflammation44,45. This self-amplifying cycle downregulates tight junction proteins, exacerbates epithelial permeability, and disrupts immune homeostasis. Furthermore, while our data establish that SY co-exposure amplifies IL-1β secretion and triggers MEKK1/IKK-dependent NF-κB activation, leading to tight junction disruption, we did not investigate downstream effectors of this pathway. As the reviewer astutely notes, seminal work by Al-Sadi et al.44 has shown that IL-1β-induced barrier defects are critically mediated by the upregulation of myosin light chain kinase (MLCK). Therefore, it remains to be determined whether SY treatment modulates MLCK expression or activity, which would provide a more complete mechanistic link between IL-1β/NF-κB signaling and the observed physical disruption of tight junctions. Investigating MLCK’s role represents a crucial next step for future research.

A key limitation of this study is its reliance on murine models and Caco-2 cell assays, which may not fully capture the complexity of human allergic responses. Moreover, our experimental design, in which SY was administered during both sensitization and challenge, prevents us from determining the specific phase (sensitization, effector, or both) in which SY exerts its exacerbating effects. While we observed significant histopathological damage, we did not quantify specific immune cell populations; thus, the influx of key effector cells like eosinophils and mast cells, while strongly suggested by the pathology, was not definitively confirmed. Furthermore, the precise molecular interactions between SY and immune or epithelial cells remain incompletely defined, and only a single dose of SY (40 mg/kg) was used, so dose-response relationships were not assessed. Future clinical and mechanistic investigations using phase-specific SY administration will be essential to confirm the translational relevance of these findings.

In summary, this study demonstrates that SY synergistically exacerbates OVA-induced food allergy by promoting effector-cell activation, skewing immune responses toward Th2 dominance, and disrupting intestinal barrier integrity. Mechanistically, SY treatment was associated with activation of the MEKK1/IKK-dependent NF-κB pathway and enhances IL-1β-mediated downregulation of tight junction proteins, creating a self-amplifying cycle of barrier dysfunction and immune dysregulation. These findings suggest that dietary pigments such as SY may function as adjuvants in FA and highlight MEKK1/IKK/NF-κB signaling as a promising therapeutic target. It is important to note that our work provides mechanistic evidence supporting the need to reassess the safety profile of SY in the context of allergic diseases, while offering a molecular mechanism to guide both regulatory considerations and the development of interventions targeting food additive-induced allergies. More broadly, the pathways identified highlight critical intersections between environmental exposures, gut barrier function, and immune regulation in FA.

Methods

Materials

Sunset Yellow (SY; S422976; purity ≥95%) was purchased from Aladdin Reagents (Shanghai, China). Ovalbumin (OVA; A5503; derived from chicken egg white; Grade V; purity ≥98%) and aluminium adjuvant (Alum; A6435; purity 95%) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (Missouri, USA). Cell culture reagents, including DMEM medium (11965-092), fetal bovine serum (10099-141), RPMI-1640 medium (11875-093), Trypsin-EDTA solution (0.25%, 25200-072), and penicillin-streptomycin (15140122), were obtained from Gibco (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Histological staining kits (G1120) were acquired from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Components for enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, including HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgE (1110-05), IgG (1071-05), and IgG1 (1036-05) antibodies, were purchased from SouthernBiotech (USA). Antibodies targeting the tight junction proteins Occludin (DF7504), Claudin-1 (AF0217), and ZO-1 (AF5145) were purchased from Affinity Biosciences, China. Antibodies for signaling pathway proteins, including IκB‑α (9242), p‑IκB‑α (2859), and NF‑κB p65 (8242), were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (USA). Antibodies against MEKK1 (19970-1-AP) and p-MEKK1 (28844-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). The loading control antibodies β‑tubulin and β‑actin were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). Histamine ELISA kit (JL45802), MCP1 ELISA kit (JL20304), IL-4 ELISA kit (JL20266), IL-5 ELISA kit (JL20267), IL-13 ELISA kit (JL20247), IL-2 ELISA kit (JL20256), IL-10 ELISA kit (JL20242), IL-4 ELISA kit (JL20266), IFN-γ ELISA kit (JL10967), TGF-β ELISA kit (JL13959) were obtained from Jianglai Biotechnology (Shanghai, China).

Female BALB/c mice (SPF grade) were obtained from Guangdong Experimental Animal Center (Guangzhou, China) and housed in controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 24 ± 2 °C; relative humidity: 50 ± 10%; 12-hour light/dark cycle) with free access to standard chow and water. All animal protocols used in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Shenzhen University, approval number AEWC-202400084. After a one-week acclimation period, animals were randomly divided into four treatment groups (7 mice per group). Strict environmental controls were implemented to minimize potential allergen contamination during the entire experimental duration.

Mice allergy experiment designs

The study employed female BALB/c mice (5-6 weeks old) adapted based on previous methods13 with some modifications. The mice were randomly assigned to four experimental groups (n = 7): The blank control group received daily oral doses of PBS (200 μL) for 28 days, while the SY group was given SY (40 mg/kg in PBS) via the same method. The dose of the SY level was chosen based on the EFSA revised maximum allowable level in food46. The OVA group was sensitized with intraperitoneal injections of ovalbumin (100 μg) plus alum (2 mg) on days 7, 14, and 21, and the OVA + SY group received both OVA-alum injections and daily oral SY. For consistency, the blank control group was injected with PBS instead of OVA, and the SY group received alum alone without OVA. On days 28, 31, and 34, mice underwent an oral challenge with a high dose (20 mg) of OVA in 200 μL PBS. After a 12 h fast, allergic reactions were assessed 30–60 min after final challenge using a standardized symptom scoring system (Table 1). Evaluations, including monitoring for diarrhea and other clinical signs, were performed by a trained researcher who was blinded to the group assignments to minimize subjective bias and ensure assessment objectivity. Blood samples were collected via retro-orbital bleeding to analyze inflammatory markers. The spleen was harvested to isolate splenocytes, which were cultured for cytokine profiling by ELISA. Intestinal tissue from the duodenum was fixed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned for histological examination via hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining. Additionally, colon tissue proteins were extracted to evaluate tight junction protein expression using Western blot analysis.

Serum biomarker analysis

Blood samples were collected via retro-orbital bleeding immediately after the 30–60 min clinical symptom assessment, which was performed 1 h following the final oral challenge. Specimens were allowed to coagulate at room temperature for 2 h, then centrifuged at 3500 × g for 15 min; the separated serum was subsequently stored at −80 °C until further analysis. OVA-specific immunoglobulins IgE, IgG and IgG1 were detected via indirect ELISA27. Briefly, microplates were coated with OVA (500 ng/well) and blocked with 5% BSA. After incubation with serially diluted serum samples, plates were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse IgG: 1:2000, anti-mouse IgG: 1:1000; anti-mouse IgG1: 1:5000), and color was developed using TMB substrate. Allergic mediators including histamine and MCP-1 were measured; and cytokine profiles encompassing Th1-type (IL-2, IFN-γ), Th2-type (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13), and regulatory cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β) were assessed. All mediator and cytokine quantifications were performed using standardized commercial ELISA kits in accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions.

Splenocyte isolation and cytokine production assay

Spleens were aseptically processed by gently pressing through a 100 μm nylon mesh into RPMI 1640 medium. The resulting cell suspension was centrifuged (300 × g, 5 min), treated with RBC lysis buffer (3 min, room temperature, RT), and washed with PBS. The isolated splenocytes were resuspended in a complete RPMI 1640 medium (supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin). For cytokine analysis, cells were seeded at 2.5 × 10⁵ cells/well in 24-well plates and stimulated with 100 μg/mL OVA for 72 h under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). After centrifugation (300 × g, 10 min), culture supernatants were collected and stored at −80 °C for subsequent quantification of Th1 (IL-2, IFN-γ), Th2 (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13), and regulatory (IL-10, TGF-β) cytokines using commercial ELISA kits.

Duodenal histopathology assessment

Duodenal tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (4 °C, 48 h) and processed through graded ethanol dehydration, xylene clearing, and paraffin embedding. Sections (5 μm thickness) were cut using a rotary microtome and mounted on slides for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining following standard protocols. After staining, sections were dehydrated and permanently mounted for microscopic evaluation. Representative H&E-stained intestinal histological sections were observed at ×100 magnification. Scale bar, 200 μm. Histopathological examination focuses on assessing tissue integrity, inflammatory cell infiltration, and structural abnormalities using light microscopy47.

Analysis of colonic tight junction proteins and inflammatory markers

Colonic protein extracts were prepared using RIPA lysis buffer, with protein concentrations determined via BCA assay. Following heat denaturation (100 °C), samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and electrotransferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA and probed overnight (4 °C) with primary antibodies targeting tight junction proteins (Occludin 1:2000, Claudin1 1:1000) and β-actin (1:1000, internal control). After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (RT, 1 h), protein expression was detected by ECL and quantified using ImageJ software (v2.1.4.7), with β-actin serving as the normalization standard.

Caco-2 cell culture

The human intestinal epithelial cell line Caco-2 was cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Cells were routinely passaged at 80% confluence using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution, with medium replacement every 48 h. All experimental procedures were performed using cells in the logarithmic growth phase to ensure optimal cellular activity and consistency.

Assessment of cell proliferation using CCK-8 assay

Caco-2 cells were plated in 96-well culture plates at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells/well and exposed to a concentration gradient of SY (40, 20, 10, 5, 2.5, 1.25, 0.625, 0.3125 μg/mL) for 24 h. Following treatment, cellular viability was determined by adding 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent to each well and measuring the absorbance at 450 nm. Each experimental condition was performed in triplicate to ensure the reproducibility of results.

Evaluation of inflammatory markers, tight junction proteins, and NF-κB pathway activation

Caco-2 cells in logarithmic growth phase were harvested and adjusted to a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells/mL for subsequent experiments. For reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection, cells were seeded in 24-well plates (1 mL/well) and treated for 24 hours (n = 3 replicates per group) with the following conditions: control (PBS), SY alone at low (0.625 μg/mL), medium (1.25 μg/mL), and high (2.5 μg/mL) concentrations, OVA alone (100 μg/mL), and OVA combined with each SY concentration. After the treatment, intracellular ROS levels were quantified using a fluorescent ROS assay kit with measurement by microplate reader.

In parallel experiments, Caco-2 cells were seeded at 2.5 × 10⁵ cells/well and exposed to the same treatment regimen for 72 hours. The concentration of IL-1β in the cell culture supernatants was then determined using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Cell culture supernatants were analyzed for IL-1β secretion using ELISA. For protein analysis, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors, followed by protein quantification via BCA assay48. Western blotting was performed to examine tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Claudin1; 1:1000 dilution), NF-κB pathway components (MEKK1, P-MEKK1, IκB-α, P-IκB-α, NF-κB P65; 1:1000 dilution), and loading controls (β-actin, β-tubulin; 1:1000 dilution). After secondary antibody incubation (HRP-conjugated), protein expression was visualized by ECL and quantified using ImageJ software (v2.1.4.7), with normalization to β-actin or β-Tubulin as appropriate.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For datasets with a normal distribution, multiple group comparisons were performed using one way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. For non-normally distributed data, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis H test was employed, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical analysis and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.
