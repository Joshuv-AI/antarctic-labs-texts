Impact of an advanced trauma life support (ATLS) simulation program on nursing students’ clinical decision-making in trauma care: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Meysam Mohebi, Soolmaz Moosavi, Fateme Hasandoost, Mohammad Mehdi Forouzanfar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and objectiveTrauma is one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide, especially in developing countries. Accurate clinical decision-making ability among nurses plays a crucial role in reducing trauma-related mortality and complications. Strengthening such skills during nursing education can better prepare students to effectively address clinical challenges they may face in real-world trauma care. This study aimed to determine the effect of an Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) simulation program on the clinical decision-making abilities of nursing students.MethodsThis quasi-experimental study with a pre-test/post-test control group design was conducted in 2025 on 66 final-year nursing students at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences. Participants were assigned to intervention (n = 31) and control (n = 35) groups. The intervention group received advanced trauma life support simulation training. Data were collected using a validated clinical decision-making questionnaire and analyzed with paired t-tests, repeated measures ANOVA, and interaction effects tests.ResultsThe mean clinical decision-making score in the intervention group increased from 40.81 ± 3.22 before the intervention to 52.24 ± 2.04 after the intervention. In the control group, the mean scores were 39.06 ± 2.27 before and 48.09 ± 1.97 after the study period. Repeated measures ANOVA indicated that time (F = 601.08, p < 0.001), group (F = 47.26, p < 0.001), and the interaction of time and group (F = 8.23, p = 0.006) had significant effects on clinical decision-making ability. The improvement in the intervention group was significantly greater than in the control group.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that ATLS-based simulation programs can significantly improve nursing students’ readiness for real-life trauma situations and should be integrated into nursing curricula.

Introduction

Trauma is one of the leading causes of mortality and morbidity worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [1]. In Iran, trauma, especially due to road traffic accidents, remains the third most common cause of death [2, 3]. Timely and effective trauma care within the first critical minutes following injury significantly increases the chances of survival and reduces long-term disability [4, 5].

Emergency nurses play a vital role as the first point of care for trauma patients [6]. However, evidence suggests that nursing students often enter the clinical environment lacking adequate preparedness to manage complex trauma cases or make timely clinical decisions [7–9]. This lack of readiness has been linked to several factors, including limited clinical experience, insufficient exposure to realistic training environments, and a lack of structured simulation-based programs during undergraduate education [10, 11].

Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) is a globally recognized standardized program designed to enhance the assessment and initial management of trauma patients [12, 13]. Although the effectiveness of ATLS in improving trauma care knowledge and skills is well documented, little is known about its direct impact on the clinical decision-making ability of nursing students.

Simulation-based education has emerged as a powerful tool in nursing curricula, enabling students to practice high-risk clinical scenarios in a safe, controlled environment [14]. These simulations not only improve technical skills but also foster critical thinking, confidence, and clinical judgment [15]. Despite its growing use, a research gap remains regarding the specific effect of ATLS-style simulations on decision-making skills in undergraduate nursing students. Recent studies have also demonstrated that high-fidelity simulation can significantly improve clinical decision-making abilities among nursing students [16].

In a qualitative phenomenological study, Hassanaloo et al. (2022) identified that while simulation-based learning faces technical, human, and support-related barriers, overcoming these challenges can lead to significant educational outcomes such as enhanced clinical skills, improved clinical judgment, increased critical thinking, and better patient safety [15].

Given the potential consequences of poor clinical decisions in trauma care, including preventable deaths and permanent disabilities, it is essential to evaluate educational strategies that enhance decision-making competence. This study aims to address this gap by investigating the impact of an Advanced Life Support Simulation Program on the clinical decision-making ability of nursing students managing trauma patients.

Methods

Study design

This study was a quasi-experimental pretest-posttest study with a control group, conducted to assess the effect of an advanced life support simulation program on the clinical decision-making ability of nursing students in trauma care.

Setting and population

The study was conducted at the Trauma Skills Center and the conference hall of the School of Nursing and Midwifery, Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran, during the academic year 2024–2025. The target population included all 8th-semester undergraduate nursing students who were in the faculty.

Sampling method

The study population consisted of all 90 eligible 8th-semester undergraduate nursing students, among whom 66 volunteered to participate and were included through census. Therefore, a priori sample size calculation was not performed. They participated after receiving full information about the study and signing informed consent forms. Based on the existing student admission system, students in Group A were considered the intervention group and those in Group B as the control group. All participants took part in the study voluntarily. Out of a total of 90 students, 66 expressed willingness to participate in the study. Participants were then assigned numerical codes using the university’s educational system and divided into two groups, intervention and control, through simple randomization using a lottery method. The allocation was performed by a secondary researcher who was blinded to the content of the intervention to minimize allocation bias.

Data collection instruments

Data were collected using a researcher-developed questionnaire consisting of two main sections:

Section 1: Demographic and Clinical Characteristics – This included age, gender, grade in the emergency nursing course, and any prior ATLS means.

Section 2: Clinical Decision-Making in Trauma Patients. A scenario-based, researcher-made instrument consisting of 15 five-option questions derived from the most recent edition of the Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) manual (American College of Surgeons) [17], and designed under the CDC’s expanded trauma classification (accidents, falls, others) [18, 19]. Each item had five response options scored as follows: optimal response = 4, helpful but not primary = 3, neutral = 2, unnecessary = 1, inappropriate = 0. The maximum possible score was 60.

Validity and reliability

Content and face validity of the instrument were assessed by five faculty members from the School of Nursing and Midwifery. Their expert feedback led to revisions in language clarity and content coverage. Scientific content was aligned with ATLS guidelines and CDC trauma classifications [20].Reliability was tested using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding a coefficient of 0.79 in a pilot study involving 20 nursing students, indicating acceptable internal consistency.

Data collection procedure

Following the acquisition of ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the School of Nursing and Midwifery at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, as well as the necessary administrative permissions from the educational deputy, the data collection process was initiated. After coordination with the relevant authorities, permission was granted to conduct an educational workshop on advanced life support in trauma patients.

The objectives and procedures of the study were first explained in detail to all eligible eighth-semester nursing students. Students were allowed to ask questions and resolve any ambiguities before voluntarily signing the informed consent form. Due to scheduling conflicts with clinical placements, it was not possible to hold the workshop across four days as originally intended. Therefore, the simulation-based training was condensed into two consecutive sessions. the intervention group attended a four-hour educational workshop in the faculty’s conference hall. At the beginning of the session, participants completed the pretest using the researcher-developed clinical decision-making questionnaire. Subsequently, the educational intervention was delivered using a blended learning approach that included lectures, instructional videos, PowerPoint presentations, and the simulation of trauma-related clinical scenarios.

Upon completion of the training, the same questionnaire was administered again as a posttest to evaluate any changes in clinical decision-making abilities; thus, both the intervention and control groups completed pretest and posttest assessments. Following the pretest, only the intervention group received the ATLS training. Immediately after this, both groups were asked to complete the posttest. For ethical considerations, the control group was subsequently offered the ATLS training, but only after they had already completed their posttest. Thus, the reported posttest data for the control group were collected before they received any training.

The simulation training for the intervention group was divided into two main parts. The first hour was dedicated to theoretical instruction, and the remaining three hours focused on practical simulation of trauma scenarios following the ATLS protocol. The scenarios were interactive and designed based on the latest guidelines from the American College of Surgeons, covering both primary assessment (XABCDE) and secondary assessment (using the SAMPLE mnemonic and head-to-toe physical examination).

Six skill stations were created, each corresponding to one component of the XABCDE algorithm. Students rotated through these stations in small groups of six students each for stations X, A, and B, and five students for stations C, D, and E, ensuring full participation of all 33 students in the intervention group. All scenarios were developed in line with the CDC’s extended trauma classification matrix, and standard moulages were used to facilitate hands-on practice. The entire simulation-based training was conducted by the primary researcher, who holds an internationally recognized certificate in advanced trauma care from the National Association of Emergency Medical Technicians (NAEMT).

Data analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS version 24. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation) were used to describe quantitative variables. To compare pre- and post-intervention scores within each group, paired t-tests were used. In addition, repeated-measures ANOVA was performed to confirm the group × time interaction effect, which provides the strongest evidence of the intervention’s impact. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the effect of the Advanced Life Support (ATLS) simulation program on the clinical decision-making ability of nursing students in trauma patients. The results showed that the implementation of this training program significantly improved the clinical decision-making ability of the students. A repeated measures analysis of variance showed that the effects of time, group, and the interaction between time and group on clinical decision-making ability were all statistically significant (p < 0.01).

The findings of the present study showed that implementing an advanced life support simulation workshop in trauma patients significantly improved clinical decision-making skills in nursing students. These results are consistent with those of Kim et al., who found that simulation-based education significantly enhanced accuracy, speed, and quality of decision-making under critical conditions such as trauma [21]. One possible explanation for this effect is the ability of simulation environments to replicate real-life clinical scenarios, allowing students to engage in repetitive, hands-on practice without fear of harming actual patients. This experiential learning process contributes to deeper understanding, improved prioritization, particularly within frameworks such as the XABCDE approach, and greater clinical readiness [22].

One of the major benefits observed in this study aligns with previous literature emphasizing the role of simulation in increasing students’ self-confidence and self-efficacy. As reported by Azizi et al. students trained via simulation reported significantly higher levels of self-efficacy and clinical performance compared to those trained using traditional methods [23]. Similarly, trauma moulage-based training has shown marked improvement in practical trauma management skills, underscoring the global relevance of simulation in clinical education [24]. Recent research by Toqan et al. (2023) and Hodrob et al. (2024) also demonstrated that high-fidelity simulation enhanced both self-satisfaction and self-confidence, reinforcing its role as a transformative teaching strategy in nursing education [25, 26].

The results of the present study, which showed significant improvement in clinical decision-making skills of nursing students after receiving advanced trauma life support (ATLS) simulation training, are consistent with the results of several studies. For example, in India, Rattan et al. (2021) reported in a descriptive study that participants had significant improvements in knowledge, skills, and confidence after completing ATLS, and this remained the case for up to two years [27]. Also, in the United States, Gutrajman et al. (2017) showed that advanced trauma simulation training significantly increased the confidence and decision-making skills of trainees [28]. Al-Shafi et al. (2016) also confirmed in a systematic review that ATLS improves practical knowledge and skills and that simulation-based training is superior to traditional methods [29].

In contrast, other approaches, such as microlearning, have shown limited effectiveness. For example, Haghihat et al. (2023) found that short-term microlearning strategies improved theoretical knowledge but had no significant impact on practical decision-making ability [30]. This contrast highlights the importance of structured, immersive training formats such as simulation, which provide the depth, realism, and repetition needed for skill mastery.

The global relevance of trauma training, especially through simulation, has become increasingly evident in light of recent mass casualty events, including conflicts and natural disasters [31]. During crises such as the recent 12-day war in Iran or the war in Ukraine, structured trauma training programs like ATLS have been shown to enhance preparedness, confidence, and clinical response capacity among healthcare professionals and students alike [32, 33]. Similarly, Lebanese medical students reported substantial growth in resilience, accountability, and technical skills following participation in advanced clinical training during the 2006 war [34]. These international examples reinforce the argument that integrating simulation-based trauma education into nursing curricula is not only beneficial but essential for health system readiness.

Beyond confirming the international evidence on the effectiveness of simulation, our findings are particularly relevant in the Iranian context. Iran faces a high burden of trauma cases due to road traffic accidents and other injuries, while access to advanced clinical training resources remains limited in many nursing schools. Simulation-based programs such as ATLS can therefore bridge the gap by providing standardized, safe, and resource-efficient training opportunities. Incorporating such programs into nursing curricula may not only improve decision-making skills but also help address the broader public health challenge of trauma care in resource-constrained settings.

Limitations

Despite the strengths of this study, such as its quasi-experimental design, use of a validated international training protocol (ATLS), and rigorous statistical analysis, some limitations must be acknowledged. The lack of long-term follow-up restricts understanding of the durability of the observed improvements. Additionally, the study was conducted in a single academic institution with a relatively small sample size, which may limit generalizability. Moreover, the use of self-assessment tools for measuring decision-making ability may introduce reporting bias. Finally, the absence of a direct comparison with other teaching methods, such as microlearning or traditional lectures, limits the assessment of the relative efficacy of the simulation approach. It is also important to note that regional geopolitical instability, including the recent conflict between Iran and Israel, hindered the ability to conduct subsequent follow-up assessments with the participating students. Another limitation of this study is the potential Hawthorne effect, whereby participants may have performed better simply because they were aware of being observed as part of a study. Although this effect cannot be ruled out, both the intervention and control groups were subject to the same testing conditions, which may have minimized its impact on the between-group comparisons.
