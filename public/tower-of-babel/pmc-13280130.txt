Radiographic comparison of lumbar vertebral morphology between cats with six and seven lumbar vertebrae
Authors: Geunah KIM, Daji NOH, Sooyoung CHOI, Miori KISHIMOTO, Sang-Kwon LEE, Kija LEE
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The lumbar vertebral body length and height are common radiographic references used to assess abdominal organ size in dogs and cats; however, variation in vertebral count may influence the accuracy of these ratio-based assessments. This study compared the vertebral morphology in cats with six or seven lumbar vertebrae. Abdominal radiographs from 674 cats were reviewed and categorized into the six (6LV, n=102) and seven lumbar vertebrae groups (7LV, n=572). The vertebral body length, height, and height to length (H/L) ratio were measured from L1–L6 in all cats and at L7 in the 7LV group. Group comparisons were performed overall and by sex, and associations with sex, body weight, breed, and transitional vertebrae were analyzed. Additional adjusted analyses were performed to account for body weight and sex. Cats in the 6LV group had significantly longer vertebral bodies and lower H/L ratios at L1 to L5, whereas L6 was longer in the 7LV group. Vertebral body height was consistently lower across lumbar levels in the 6LV group. Vertebral length and height were positively correlated with body weight in both groups, and males had larger vertebrae than females. Overall transitional vertebrae prevalence did not differ between groups, but 13th rib hypoplasia was more common in the 6LV group. In adjusted analyses, lumbar vertebral count remained independently associated with vertebral body length, height, and H/L ratio. These findings demonstrate that lumbar vertebral count is associated with distinct morphological patterns that may affect vertebra-based organ size assessment in cats.

INTRODUCTION

On radiographic examinations, vertebral body length and height are commonly used as radiographic landmarks for evaluating organ size or detecting abnormalities in small animals [23]. In clinically healthy cats, renal length is considered normal when it measures between 2.4 and 3.0 times the length of the second lumbar vertebra (L2) [3]. For the small intestine, a maximal outer wall-to-wall diameter of less than twice the L2 endplate height is regarded as normal [1]. For the colon, a colon-to-L5 vertebral body length ratio greater than 1.5 is suggestive of megacolon [25]. Although these vertebra-based parameters are widely used, their reliability may be compromised by congenital vertebral anomalies, which are common in both dogs and cats. These anomalies, including numerical variations and the presence of transitional vertebrae (TV) [26], can alter vertebral reference values and thereby affect measurement accuracy. In dogs, previous study demonstrated that German Shepherd dogs with eight lumbar vertebrae consistently exhibited a short and caudally positioned last presacral vertebra, indicating that alterations in lumbar vertebral count affect vertebral body dimensions [13]. By analogy, we hypothesized that cats with six lumbar vertebrae would have longer vertebral body length and shorter body height than those with seven lumbar vertebrae. Based on our review of the literature, however, no studies have quantitatively compared vertebral morphology between cats with six versus seven lumbar vertebrae.

The aims of this study were as follows: (1) to compare the vertebral body length, height, and height-to-length (H/L) ratio between cats with six and seven lumbar vertebrae and (2) to assess the effects of demographic and anatomical factors—including sex, body weight, breed, and the presence of TV—on vertebral morphology.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective, observational, and analytic study included domestic cats that underwent abdominal radiography at two institutions—the Veterinary Medical Teaching Hospital of Kyungpook National University and Daegu Animal Medical Center—between November 2021 and March 2025. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, institutional animal care and use committee approval was not required. Written informed consent for the use of medical data and diagnostic imaging had been obtained from all owners at the time of admission. Cats were included if they met the following criteria: (1) availability of a right lateral radiographic projection suitable for measurement and (2) complete visualization of all lumbar vertebrae without significant rotation, superimposition, or exclusion from the field of view. If multiple radiographic studies were available for the same cat, the study with optimal positioning and image quality was selected. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age less than 12 months at the time of imaging, which is associated with incomplete closure of vertebral growth plates; (2) evidence of vertebral disease, such as discospondylitis; and (3) poor-quality radiographs that could compromise measurement accuracy due to motion artifacts, improper positioning, or under-/overexposure. Cats were then divided into two groups according to the number of lumbar vertebrae: the six lumbar vertebrae (6LV) group and seven lumbar vertebrae (7LV) group.

Clinical data including breed, sex, age, and body weight were extracted from the medical records. All radiographic measurements were performed by a single observer (G.K.) with two years of experience in veterinary diagnostic imaging. Each measurement was repeated three times, and the average of the three values was used for analysis. Radiographs were reviewed using a commercially available software system (INFINITT PACS; INFINITT Healthcare, Seoul, South Korea). The presence and subtypes of TV were assessed using both lateral and ventrodorsal radiographs. For all cases in which TV were identified, both radiographic views were available and were evaluated concurrently to ensure accurate classification and prevent misidentification. To measure vertebral size, the 11th thoracic vertebra (T11) was used as a standardized anatomical landmark for enumeration. The subsequent vertebrae and ribs were counted caudally from this anchor point. The ribs associated with the second vertebra caudal to T11 were recorded as the presumed 13th ribs. The vertebral body length for each lumbar vertebra (L1–L6 or L1–L7) was measured on right lateral radiographs as the linear distance between the cranial and caudal endplates measured at the midline of the vertebral body. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 29; IBM Corp., New York, NY, USA) by a single author (G.K.), with statistical significance defined as P<0.05. Normality of body weight, vertebral length, height, and the H/L ratio was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. To assess the demographic characteristics of the study population, age and body weight distributions were analyzed according to sex. Differences in the prevalence of TV were analyzed with Pearson’s χ2 test, whereas the subtype distributions of TV were compared using the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test with Monte Carlo estimation. The association between the lumbar vertebral count and breed was analyzed using Pearson’s χ2 test, and the likelihood-ratio χ2 was additionally reported. The vertebral length, height, and H/L ratio were compared between the two groups, and associations with breed, body weight, and the presence of TV were assessed. The vertebral length and height are reported as the mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons of the vertebral length, height, and H/L ratio between the 6LV and 7LV groups were performed both in the overall study population and within subgroups stratified by sex, using the independent-samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Associations of the vertebral length, height, and H/L ratio with body weight were evaluated using Spearman correlation in each group. Sex-related differences were analyzed using independent samples t-tests for normally distributed variables and Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed variables.

To further assess whether vertebral morphology was independently associated with lumbar vertebral count after accounting for potential confounding factors, additional adjusted analyses were performed. Multiple linear regression analysis was used for vertebral body length at each lumbar level (L1–L6), with vertebral body length as the dependent variable and body weight, sex, and lumbar vertebral count as independent variables. Because vertebral body height and H/L ratio did not satisfy the assumption of normality, these variables were additionally analyzed using generalized additive models (GAMs), which included body weight as a continuous penalized spline and sex and lumbar vertebral count as fixed effects. The GAM analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

A total of 754 abdominal radiographic studies were retrospectively reviewed. All cats had radiographs suitable for vertebral measurements and were included in the comparison of lumbar vertebral body length and height. Of these, 70 cats aged ≤1 year or with radiographic evidence of open physes, 1 cat with a history of spinal surgery, 2 cats with vertebral body malformations, 2 cats with 8 lumbar vertebrae, 2 cats with insufficient imaging coverage, 2 cats with motion artifacts, and 1 cat with radiographic evidence of discospondylitis were excluded. After applying these exclusion criteria, 674 cats were included in the final analysis for this study.

The mean age of the study population (n=674) was 6.83 ± 4.03 years (range, 1–20 years), and the mean body weight was 4.8 ± 1.55 kg (range, 1.46–10.6 kg). Among the 674 cats included, 381 were male (353 neutered, 28 intact), and 293 were female (254 spayed, 39 intact). The most common breed was domestic shorthair (n=343, 50.9%), followed by Persian (n=67, 9.9%), Russian Blue (n=53, 7.9%), Scottish Fold (n=45, 6.7%), and Siamese (n=27, 4.0%). Other breeds included Abyssinian (n=17), Munchkin (n=21), Bengal (n=15), British Shorthair (n=20), Turkish Angora (n=20), and several other breeds with fewer representative cats. TLTV were identified in 87 cats (12.9%). Subtypes included L1 TLTV (n=59, 67.8%), LSTV (n=13, 14.9%), 13th rib hypoplasia (n=14, 16.1%), and 13th rib aplasia (n=1, 1.1%). Seven lumbar vertebrae were identified in 572 cats (84.8%), and 6 were identified in 102 cats (15.1%). Among cats in the 7LV group, body weight showed a moderate to strong positive correlation with vertebral body length and height across all lumbar levels (ρ=0.425 to 0.601, P<0.001). In contrast, weak but significant negative correlations were observed between body weight and the H/L ratio at L1–L6 (ρ= −0.083 to −0.147, P<0.05), whereas no significant association was found at L7. Among the sexes, males exhibited a significantly longer vertebral body length and greater vertebral body height at all lumbar levels than females (P<0.001). Males showed a significantly greater H/L ratio than females only at L1–L3 (P<0.05). Similarly, in cats in the 6LV group, body weight was positively correlated with vertebral body length and height across all lumbar levels (ρ=0.323 to 0.518, P<0.01). However, no significant correlations were found between body weight and the H/L ratio. The sex-related differences in this group were consistent with those in the 7LV group, with males having a longer vertebral body length and taller vertebral body height than females at all lumbar levels (P<0.001); however, no significant sex-related differences were found in the H/L ratio.

In the multiple linear regression models, body weight, sex, and lumbar vertebral count were significant predictors of vertebral body length at all lumbar levels (L1–L6; all P<0.001). After adjustment, the 6LV group had significantly longer vertebral bodies at L1–L5 and a significantly shorter vertebral body at L6 compared with the 7LV group. In the GAM analyses, body weight showed significant nonlinear effects on vertebral body height at all lumbar levels, and both sex and lumbar vertebral count remained significant predictors (all P<0.05). After adjustment, the 6LV group consistently had lower vertebral body height than the 7LV group. For the H/L ratio, GAM analyses showed significant effects of lumbar vertebral count at all lumbar levels (all P<0.001). After adjustment, the 6LV group retained lower H/L ratios at L1–L5 and a higher H/L ratio at L6 than the 7LV group, consistent with the unadjusted results.

DISCUSSION

In this study, the lumbar vertebral body length, height, and H/L ratio were compared between cats with six lumbar vertebrae and those with the standard seven lumbar vertebrae. The findings supported the hypothesis that cats with six lumbar vertebrae have a longer vertebral body length and shorter vertebral body height, and these associations remained significant after adjustment for body weight and sex. This represents the first quantitative comparison and reporting of these parameters in cats with six lumbar vertebrae, providing a basis for future studies evaluating vertebra-based radiographic interpretation of abdominal organ size.

The morphological changes observed in cats with six lumbar vertebrae, namely increased vertebral body length and reduced vertebral body height, are unlikely to represent simple numerical anomalies and instead suggest coordinated morphological adaptation. This compensatory association between vertebral count and segmental dimensions is consistent with findings in German Shepherd dogs, in which an increased number of lumbar vertebrae (L8) is associated with a shorter and more caudally positioned last presacral vertebra [13]. In contrast, our findings indicate that a reduced lumbar count in cats is accompanied by elongation across the lumbar column. In the present study, cats in the 6LV group had significantly longer vertebral bodies from L1 to L5 than those in the 7LV group. This finding supports a compensatory elongation that may help preserve overall lumbar functional length and flexibility, which are important for agile locomotor performance in cats [9, 15]. We speculate that this pattern reflects biomechanical adaptation, an interpretation that is consistent with developmental frameworks involving homeotic transformation and primaxial–abaxial modularity [2, 5, 6, 8, 17]. This interpretation is further supported by the significantly increased prevalence of 13th rib hypoplasia observed in cats with six lumbar vertebrae in the present study, as previously reported in association with thoracolumbar transitional variation [9, 19].

The H/L ratio represents the proportion between vertebral body height and length; lower values indicate a relatively more elongated and lower (“long-and-low”) vertebral body morphology. Accordingly, the lower H/L ratios observed in 6LV cats across multiple lumbar levels should be interpreted as a proportional shape difference rather than an isolated change in a single linear measurement. These proportional differences may affect the use of vertebra-based reference ratios, because elongation and flattening of the lumbar vertebrae alter the vertebral dimensions on which such references are based. Therefore, conventional reference values and cutoffs established in cats with seven lumbar vertebrae may not be directly applicable to cats with six lumbar vertebrae, and 6LV-specific reference ranges should be considered in future studies.

As previously reported in cats, vertebral body length increases caudally from L1, peaks at L5, and subsequently decreases at more caudal lumbar segments [4, 22]; consistent with this pattern, this characteristic distribution was preserved not only in cats with seven lumbar vertebrae but also in those with six lumbar vertebrae in the present study. This finding suggests that functional regionalization and an L5-centered length pattern are maintained even in the presence of numerical variation in lumbar vertebral count. The mid-lumbar region, particularly L4–L5, functionally represents the principal site of flexion–extension in cats, where elongated vertebral bodies facilitate substantial intervertebral disc deformation and provide favorable leverage for the epaxial and hypaxial musculature [15]. In contrast, the more caudal lumbar segments are increasingly involved in pelvic load transmission and propulsion, necessitating a comparatively shorter and more robust morphology to enhance structural stability at the lumbosacral transition [20]. Accordingly, the observed reduction in vertebral length beyond L5 more likely reflects functional regionalization of the lumbar spine rather than an effect of vertebral count, indicating that the L5 apex represents a biomechanically optimized configuration intrinsic to feline spinal mechanics.

In addition to numerical variation in lumbar vertebral count, vertebral body dimensions were found to be influenced by several factors including body weight, sex, and breed. Consistent with previous reports, the vertebral body length and height showed a positive association with body weight, which is biomechanically expected given the increased axial loading demands associated with greater body mass [4, 15]. Sex-related differences were also observed, with male cats exhibiting longer vertebrae than females, in agreement with the findings of earlier studies [4, 15, 16, 21, 22]. These findings suggest that vertebral body size is influenced by the combined effects of body weight and sex-related factors including hormonal influences. The breed distribution differed between cats with six and seven lumbar vertebrae. Previous studies have reported a prevalence of approximately 3% to 10.33% for six lumbar vertebrae in cats [12, 18]; however, a higher proportion (15.1%) was identified in the present study. This difference may, in part, reflect breed composition within the study population. Domestic shorthair cats accounted for a large proportion of the 6LV group; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given their overall predominance in the study cohort. In the present study, six lumbar vertebrae were also observed at a relatively high frequency in Turkish Angora cats (30.0%), suggesting a potential breed-associated predisposition; however, the limited sample size precludes definitive conclusions. Further studies with balanced breed representation are warranted to clarify breed-related influences and to refine prevalence estimates.

This study has several limitations. First, our analysis was limited to abdominal radiographs of the thoracolumbar region; therefore, confirmation of the total vertebral count and homeotic transformation—which require complete vertebral column imaging—was not possible. Furthermore, reliance on the 11th thoracic vertebra as a fixed landmark for enumeration may have led to misclassification in cases with anatomical variations. Second, assessment of TV was restricted to radiographic findings, and the articulation between L7 and the sacrum could not always be evaluated with confidence, making it difficult to determine the presence of sacralization. Similar challenges have been reported in human imaging, where numerical vertebral variation may be present despite apparently normal vertebral morphology, and accurate vertebral enumeration often requires whole-spine imaging and counting from a fixed cranial reference level [10, 11, 14, 24]. Third, with the exception of domestic shorthair cats, most breeds in our study were represented by relatively small sample sizes. Further studies should include whole-spine imaging to enable definitive numeration from the cervical spine and confirmation of homeotic transformation, include dedicated evaluation of the L7–sacrum articulation, and assemble larger study populations with balanced breed representation.

In conclusion, this retrospective study is the first to quantitatively compare the lumbar vertebral body length, height, and H/L ratio between cats with six and seven lumbar vertebrae. Cats with six lumbar vertebrae had longer vertebral bodies and a lower H/L ratio at L1–L5, whereas the L6 length and vertebral height across all levels were greater in cats with seven lumbar vertebrae; thus, vertebra-based organ size ratios should be applied with particular caution when interpreting radiographs of cats with six lumbar vertebrae.
