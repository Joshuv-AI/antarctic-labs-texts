Positive youth development and mental health in Latine adolescents experiencing discrimination: A mixed-methods study
Authors: Natalie Guerrero, Fangqian Ouyang, Dale D’Lima, Lizbeth Contreras, Cesar Mares, Arly Jimenez, Kelly Mosesso, Allison Stafford, Patrick Monahan, Matthew C Aalsma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The role of Positive Youth Development (PYD) and prosocial involvement in buffering the impact of racial-ethnic discrimination among Latine adolescents remains understudied.Objective:To examine whether PYD and community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement moderate the association between discrimination and depressive symptoms and suicidality in Latine adolescents through a mixed methods approach.Methods:A cross-sectional survey was conducted among Latine adolescents (N = 110), assessing racial-ethnic discrimination (Everyday Discrimination Scale [EDS]), depressive symptoms and suicidality (Mood and Feelings Questionnaire [MFQ]), PYD (PYD Short Form), and community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement (Communities That Care scales). Linear and logistic regression models examined predictors of depressive symptoms and suicidality. Moderation was tested with interaction terms. Semi-structured interviews were completed with a nested sample of adolescents and caregivers (N = 18 dyads). A thematic analysis of interviews with a focus on the role of PYD was conducted.Results:EDS scores were associated with higher MFQ scores and suicidality. PYD factors and community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement were associated with lower MFQ scores and suicidality. These trends were generally consistent in adjusted regressions. None of the PYD factors, nor community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement, moderated these relationships. Qualitative findings identified youth-described processes consistent with PYD that appeared to support coping in the context of discrimination.Conclusions:PYD factors and community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement are associated with improved mental health among Latine adolescents but may not be sufficient to buffer the harmful effects of racial-ethnic discrimination.Keywords: Latine adolescents, Racial-ethnic discrimination, Depression, Suicidality, Positive youth development, Prosocial involvement

Introduction

Latine adolescent depression and suicidality and racial-ethnic discrimination:

The prevalence of depression increases in adolescence, (Thapar et al., 2012) and adolescent depression and suicidality are associated with adverse outcomes in adulthood. (Bohman et al., 2024) Latine adolescents, a growing population in the U.S., (Bureau UC., 2025) report significantly higher levels of depressive symptoms, and suicidality among Latine adolescents is increasing. (Price and Khubchandani, 2022; Cave et al., 2020; Polanco-Roman et al., 2025) Racial-ethnic discrimination has adverse effects on the mental health of Latine adolescents, including depression and suicidality. (Cave et al., 2020; Cuevas et al., 2019; Polanco-Roman et al., 2025; Gomez-Baya et al., 2022)

Positive Youth Development:

Positive Youth Development (PYD) is a developmental framework that emphasizes youths’ strengths through nurturing supportive relationships and the settings that surround them, promoting positive outcomes in youthhood and beyond. (Catalano et al., 2004) The PYD framework suggests that nurturing youth strengths promotes the development of the 5Cs: Competence, Confidence, Character, Connection, and Compassion/Caring. PYD is associated with better mental health across populations. (Gomez-Baya et al., 2022; Catalano et al., 2004; Marín-Gutiérrez and Caqueo-Urízar, 2025; Gan et al., 2023; Marín-Gutiérrez et al., 2024; Manrique-Millones et al., 2023) Protective factors that promote Latine adolescent mental health may also buffer the negative impacts of racial-ethnic discrimination on depression and suicidality. (Ramos et al., 2022) More specifically, the 5 PYD dimensions may help adolescents respond to stressors such as racial-ethnic discrimination, weakening the association between racial-ethnic discrimination and adverse mental health outcomes. For example, connection may strengthen supportive relationships that reduce stress. However, Latine adolescents in the U.S. are underrepresented in literature evaluating the role of PYD on mental health. (Smith et al., 2017)

Although PYD is often conceptualized as a protective or buffering framework, less is known about how youth themselves describe these processes in the context of racial-ethnic discrimination. PYD has primarily been studied using quantitative survey methods. Yet adolescents’ responses to racial-ethnic discrimination may involve dynamic processes that are not adequately captured by survey measures. Quantitative moderation models may not fully capture these dynamic processes that may not function as constant characteristics. Integrating qualitative perspectives may deepen understanding of how PYD-related strengths operate in lived experience. Therefore, a mixed methods approach allows for a more comprehensive understanding of how strengths-based assets work in the context of racial-ethnic discrimination.

Community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement:

Positive community-level factors promote adolescent mental health, including decreased levels of depression, (Solmi et al., 2017) with positive impacts that may extend into adulthood. (Steiner et al., 2019) Prosocial involvement includes actions such as helping and assisting others, sharing and donating items, comforting and reassuring others, and cooperating with others to reach a shared goal. It has been suggested that prosocial involvement may be considered as a PYD construct itself (Lam, 2012) and is associated with improved adolescent mental health. (Hirani et al., 2022; Rowland B, and ET 2023) The Social Development Strategy (SDS) of the Social Development Model, validated among diverse populations, (Roosa et al., 2011) suggests that protective prosocial processes, which may include the opportunity for adolescents to participate in sports teams, service clubs, and other activities, are key to creating health-promoting communities. (Hawkins and Weis, 1985) Although adolescents’ actual participation depends on individual- and family-level factors, prosocial involvement opportunities and recognition or reward for adolescent prosocial involvement are protective community characteristics. The SDS suggests that these protective community characteristics lead to stronger bonds among adolescents, their caregivers, and their community, promoting positive self-esteem and a sense of community. (Haggerty and McCowan, 2018; Kim et al., 2015) In addition, having more opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement are community protective factors that are associated with decreased depression among early adolescents. (Monahan et al., 2014) Community opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement may increase adolescents’ sense of belonging and reinforce supportive relationships with others in the community, potentially reducing adverse psychological effects of racial-ethnic discrimination.

Current study:

To better understand how strengths-based PYD processes operate in the context of racial-ethnic discrimination, we used a sequential explanatory design.From a quantitative perspective, we aimed to test (1) the extent to which PYD and community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement are associated with decreased depressive symptoms and suicidality, and (2) whether these factors moderate the relationship between racial-ethnic discrimination and depression and suicidality among Latine adolescents. The central hypothesis is that PYD factors and community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement are associated with decreased depressive symptoms and suicidality and moderate the relationship between racial-ethnic discrimination and Latine adolescent depression and suicidality. We aimed to further interpret quantitative findings and gain additional insights on PYD in coping with racial-ethnic discrimination through engaging Latine adolescent-caregiver dyads in qualitative interviews.

Methods

This study used a sequential explanatory mixed methods design to examine associations between racial-ethnic discrimination and mental health and to explore Latine adolescents’ lived experiences of discrimination and coping. (Ivankova et al., 2006) Quantitative survey data were collected from a larger sample, and a subset of participants completed qualitative interviews. All aspects of the recruitment, compensation, and surveys were submitted to the Indiana University Institutional Review Board for approval.

Sample

We conducted a cross-sectional study among a sample of 110 Latine adolescents and a primary caregiver in a Midwest county. Adolescent participants ages 13–17 and a primary caregiver were recruited through several approaches, including recruitment in person from community-based organizations, schools, and churches that interface with adolescents. Several recruitment sites serve primarily Latine families. All participants must have identified English or Spanish as their primary language. Primary caregivers were contacted via phone, and after completing adult informed consent and adolescent assent, surveys were administered to adolescents and caregivers via REDCap and took approximately 45 min to complete. Participants each received $50 gift cards for their time.

The qualitative component was designed to provide contextual insight into Latine adolescents’ lived experiences that may not be fully captured by survey-based moderation analyses. Participants in the qualitative component were drawn from the larger quantitative survey sample using a nested, purposive sampling strategy. To ensure representation across key demographic characteristics, we sought variation by adolescent sex assigned at birth (female, male), caregiver gender (maternal, paternal), adolescent age group (13–14; 15–17 years), and adolescent preferred language (English, Spanish). Because male caregivers comprised a substantially smaller proportion of the survey sample compared to female caregivers, all eligible male caregivers were invited to participate in qualitative interviews to increase representation within this subgroup. A total of 18 Latine adolescent-caregiver dyads completed semi-structured interviews. This is consistent with research suggesting a sufficient sample size for reaching saturation. (Saunders et al., 2017) Adolescents and caregivers were interviewed separately. Interviews lasted approximately 30 min, and participants received a $50 gift card in appreciation of their time.

Measures

Racial-ethnic discrimination was measured by a modified version of the Everyday Discrimination Scale. (Williams et al., 1997) Adolescents reported the frequency of discrimination experiences attributed to their race or ethnicity on a 6-point Likert scale, where higher scores represented more frequent experiences of racial-ethnic discrimination. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.91. Depressive symptoms were measured by the Mood and Feelings Questionnaire (MFQ), a 33-item validated measure for adolescents. (Messer et al., 1995; Gómez-Ortiz et al., 2016) Suicidality was derived from four MFQ items and was dichotomized to none (0) and any endorsement (1). Cronbach’s alpha was 0.97.

Positive youth development was measured using the PYD Short Form, which assesses the 5Cs of PYD: (Geldhof et al., 2014) Competence (Cronbach’s alpha 0.79); Character (Cronbach’s alpha 0.75); Confidence (Cronbach’s alpha 0.86); Caring (Cronbach’s alpha 0.85); Connection (Cronbach’s alpha 0.85). Community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement was measured using two scales from the Communications That Care (CTC) framework: (Brady et al., 2018) the Opportunities for Prosocial Involvement (6 items) and Rewards for Prosocial Involvement (3 items) scales. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.79.

Additional details regarding measurement, scoring, and reliability are provided in the Supplemental Appendix 1.

Interview procedures

Semi-structured interviews were conducted by trained research staff using an interview guide focused on adolescents’ experiences of racial-ethnic discrimination, emotional responses, and coping strategies, aligned with the key constructs measured in the quantitative survey. Interviews were conducted before the quantitative data were analyzed and were not informed by the survey results.

Semi-structured interviews were conducted primarily by a bilingual team member who identified as Latino and was a medical student at the time. He had significant prior experience working with Latine families in the same county where this study was conducted. Interviews were conducted in locations selected by participants, including public libraries and a local community organization office, to maximize comfort and accessibility. Interviews were audio-recorded and professionally transcribed. Spanish-language interviews were transcribed in Spanish and subsequently translated into English by a professional translation service.

To protect confidentiality, identifying information was removed during transcription and prior to analysis. A bilingual member of the research team reviewed translated transcripts to ensure accuracy and cultural appropriateness of translation. The bilingual reviewer compared translated transcripts to the original Spanish transcripts to confirm fidelity of meaning and resolve discrepancies through discussion with the research team. Transcripts were not backtranslated.

Data analysis

Quantitative approach

Linear regression was used to model predictors on MFQ score outcomes, and logistic regression models were used to determine the odds of suicidality. Univariable models for each of the primary predictors of interest were completed. Then, multivariable models included two important confounders (age and sex assigned at birth), both social determinants routinely included in similar models. Finally, standard methods of moderation analysis were used to evaluate whether the relationship between racial-ethnic discrimination and MFQ scores and suicidality was moderated by PYD factors and community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement by adding an interaction term in the adjusted model. (Zhang and Halgunseth, 2025) That is, interaction terms between racial-ethnic discrimination and each PYD construct and racial-ethnic discrimination and community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement were included in separate adjusted models to test moderation. Missing data were handled using a complete-case analysis. For each analysis, only participants with complete data on all variables included in that specific model were analyzed. No imputation was performed.

Qualitative approach

For our qualitative component, we used an inductive thematic analysis approach. (Braun and Clarke, 2023) After themes were generated, we conducted a secondary analytic step to examine the extent to which themes aligned with the PYD 5Cs.

Although adolescents and caregivers participated in interviews, the qualitative analysis of the current study focused on adolescent interviews because the study centered on youth perceptions of PYD-related strengths in the context of racial-ethnic discrimination. This approach is also consistent with the quantitative analysis, which included adolescents’ self-reported PYD. Caregiver interviews were analyzed as part of the broader mixed methods study but are not the focus of the current manuscript.

For the inductive approach, two members of the research team independently reviewed transcripts to develop an initial coding framework grounded in participants’ descriptions of racial-ethnic discrimination experiences, responses, and coping strategies. Once the codebook was developed, we conducted a pilot test with several transcripts to enhance consistency in code application before dividing the remaining transcripts between the two coders. The team met regularly to refine codes, resolve discrepancies through discussion, and develop higher-order themes. To enhance analytic rigor, we maintained an audit trail of coding decisions, engaged in team-based coding, and used exemplar quotations to ground interpretations in participants’ words.

The 5Cs framework of PYD was not used as an a priori coding structure. Instead, following completion of primary thematic analysis, we conducted a secondary analytic step to examine whether and how emergent themes aligned with the 5 constructs from the 5Cs framework. Inductive themes included reacting to and coping with racial-ethnic discrimination experiences, adolescent mental health, and adolescent-caregiver relationship dynamics. After thematic analyses were complete, we examined correspondence with the PYD framework. This mapping involved a secondary analytic step in which one team member reviewed transcripts, codes, and themes for alignment with the 5Cs, followed by consensus review with a second team member, and categorization of themes. This sequential analytic strategy allowed us to preserve inductive integrity while situating findings within an established theoretical framework.

The research team includes clinicians and researchers with experience working with Latine adolescents and families. Our training in strength-based developmental frameworks may have shaped our attention to resilience-related processes. We addressed this by grounding interpretations in participants’ language and engaging in team discussion to challenge assumptions during analysis.

Integration

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings was completed during interpretation of the findings using a narrative approach. Using qualitative data to contextualize and explain the quantitative findings, a side-by-side matrix was developed. (Fetters et al., 2013; Younas and Durante, 2023)

Qualitative results

Although quantitative analyses did not support a buffering effect of PYD constructs, qualitative findings provided insight into how adolescents described strengths and coping processes that aligned with the 5Cs. Adolescents described actively mobilizing skills, relationships, and beliefs to navigate discriminatory experiences. These findings suggest that PYD-related processes may operate in ways not fully captured by cross-sectional moderation models. Additional illustrative quotations are provided in Supplemental Appendix 2. More detailed results of adolescents’ and caregivers’ narratives are the focus of separate manuscripts.

Competence as strategic coping

Several adolescents described developing skills in response to racial-ethnic discrimination, particularly improving academic or language abilities. One participant noted, “I was just eager to prove them wrong… I definitely learned English really quickly.” These narratives suggest that competence may emerge as an adaptive reaction to discrimination.

Confidence as self-belief

Adolescents described confidence and reinforcing their own future achievement despite discrimination experiences. One participant described giving themselves advice during difficult moments (“You can get through it,” and “It doesn’t matter what others say about you”). These suggest that confidence may function as an active coping strategy.

Connection as emotional support

Adolescents revealed a strong theme of connection to family, friends, and faith when coping with discrimination. One participant explained, “If I felt something bothering me, I would go to my mom.” These findings highlight that adolescents may depend greatly on connections and support systems in managing emotional distress.

Character as personal values

A few adolescents described responding to experiences of discrimination by maintaining their values and focusing on not internalizing negative comments. One participant reflected, “If someone does look down on me…that’s your personal opinion.” These observations indicate that adolescents may reframe and deal with discrimination by relying on their own character and values.

Caring as self-care and regulation

Adolescents described engaging in self-care to manage stress related to discrimination. One participant described “If you take better care of yourself, you’ll feel more confident.” These suggest that caring may be expressed through self-care habits as a coping mechanism for stress related to discrimination rather than empathy alone.

Discussion

Positive youth development and community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement: predictors of decreased depressive symptoms and suicidality

Our findings align with existing literature on strengths-based assets and adolescent mental health, highlighting the positive role of individual and community assets on adolescents, namely the development of the 5Cs and prosocial involvement. (Carlo et al., 2011) Individual constructs of PYD have been associated with positive, (Alexandrova-Karamanova, 2022) and at times negative, (Holsen et al., 2017; Kozina et al., 2021; Tomé et al., 2021; Geldhof et al., 2019) mental health among specific subgroups. This study adds to the limited data on Latine adolescents in strengths-based frameworks. Prior work using the PYD framework has rarely focused on Latine adolescents in the U.S., and this study addresses this gap. Furthermore, there has been limited investigation into the role of community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement in adolescent mental health, and this work extends this research by focusing specifically on depressive symptoms and suicidality among Latine adolescents. By using data from a community sample of Latine adolescents, this study provides much-needed additional evidence that the 5Cs, particularly Confidence and Connection, as well as community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement, may be important targets for intervention for promoting mental health, with associations of decreased depression symptoms and suicidality likelihood.

Positive youth development and community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement as potential moderators

We are unaware of studies that have evaluated each PYD dimension as a moderator in the relationship between racial-ethnic discrimination and adolescent depressive symptoms and suicidality. We found that these potentially protective individual and community factors did not moderate this relationship. There may be several explanations for this. Racial-ethnic discrimination functions as a race-based trauma requiring specific individual-level coping resources, such as self-compassion, identity development, and racial-ethnic socialization. (Anderson et al., 2019; Anderson and Stevenson, 2019; Williams et al., 2022) These mental health-promoting individual traits and community factors overall may support adolescent mental health, being associated with lower depression symptoms and suicidality, but may not be enough to buffer the racial-ethnic discrimination experiences in adolescence.

Although having opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement is associated with decreased depression among early adolescents, (Monahan et al., 2014) there are limited previous studies that have evaluated community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement as a moderator of the relationship between racial-ethnic discrimination and adolescent depressive symptoms and suicidality. However, research has shown that having opportunities to participate in activities, such as physical activity, (Zhang and Halgunseth, 2025) moderates the association between racial-ethnic discrimination and adolescent mental health. Overall, there are limited studies evaluating community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement as moderators, and this study contributes by testing this among Latine adolescents. Notably, we found that community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement did not moderate the relationship between racial-ethnic discrimination and depressive symptoms and suicidality.

Integrating the quantitative and qualitative findings provides more nuanced understanding of the role of PYD and this lack of statistical moderation. Although the quantitative analyses did not support a buffering effect, qualitative narratives demonstrated that adolescents actively drew upon PYD-related strengths in response to racial-ethnic discrimination. Adolescents described discrimination as painful and impactful. After experiencing discrimination, many adolescents talked of putting more effort into their academics, being proud of their bilingualism, turning to prayer in times of need, or speaking to their caregivers. Taken together, these stories demonstrate aspects of the 5Cs may operate as dynamic processes that adolescents draw upon after experiencing racial-ethnic discrimination, rather than stable traits that weaken the association between discrimination and mental health in statistical analyses.

Importantly, our interviews suggest that Caring might manifest differently than traditionally explored within the context of discrimination experiences. Adolescents emphasized self-care, which included relaxing, controlling their emotions, and talking to themselves positively. The types of self-care mentioned in these interviews might not be captured by the PYD Caring subscale. The quantitative measure primarily assessed empathy for others. This discrepancy and lack of statistically significant moderating effects may be partially explained by this possible mismatch between real coping techniques in response to discrimination and standardized PYD measures.

Protective factors at the community level alone may not be sufficient to buffer negative impacts of interpersonal racial-ethnic discrimination. In particular, community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement may not be sufficient in the context of systemic racism. Structural barriers, racism, and xenophobia work at multiple levels to impact adolescent mental health. (Alvarez et al., 2021; Devakumar et al., 2022) Although not measured in this study, these structures have profound impacts on communities, as well as on Latine adolescents themselves. (Murry et al., 2022; Dennis et al., 2025) These negative forces may be so greatly impactful that the community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement may not be enough for their buffering effects to be detected in our analyses.

When put together, the quantitative and qualitative results indicate that community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement and PYD characteristics support Latine adolescents’ mental health but do not function as statistical buffers against racial-ethnic discrimination. Instead, adolescents described using PYD-related strengths as context-dependent coping processes in response to racial-ethnic discrimination. These findings underscore the value of mixed methods approaches by demonstrating that qualitative data can reveal processes that are not fully captured through quantitative moderation analyses alone.

Implications for intervention

Integrating quantitative and qualitative data allowed for a deeper understanding of whether and how strengths-based resources function in the context of discrimination. Strengths-based approaches that promote the development of the 5Cs and community-level opportunities and rewards for prosocial involvement may be a promising direction for intervention development aimed at depression and suicidality prevention. Our findings suggest that PYD provides a useful strengths-based framework, but interventions that combine skill development with contextual and caregiver support may be better positioned to mitigate the mental health impacts of discrimination. Culturally responsive programming should integrate PYD with other individual and family strength-based processes, including racial-ethnic socialization, identity development, and explicit racial coping strategies. At the community level, neighborhood- and school-based supports that acknowledge and actively address discrimination are needed alongside prosocial involvement opportunities.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference. The sample of Latine adolescents is from one geographic region and is largely of Mexican descent, which may limit generalizability. We rely on self-reported measures, which may introduce bias or underreporting of key variables. Because moderation analyses typically require larger samples, the relatively small sample size may have reduced statistical power to detect interaction effects. Also, the study is not powered to test 3-way interactions and model moderation effects by gender (i.e., discrimination * protective factor * gender), and future studies with larger samples should examine gender differences in these relationships. Latine cultures are diverse, and adolescents from different cultural backgrounds may experience mental health symptoms and protective processes differently, highlighting the need for additional culturally tailored measures. The present study also does not account for generational differences (e.g., first versus later generation adolescents), which may influence the expression of cultural values such as familismo and acculturative attitudes, including motivation to learn English and navigate mainstream contexts, thereby shaping mental health outcomes and protective processes.

Additional limitations include the qualitative subsample, which was nested within the larger survey sample and may not fully represent all adolescent or caregiver perspectives, and the post hoc mapping of emergent themes to the 5Cs, which was not part of the a priori coding framework. Despite these limitations, the study’s use of interaction models and examination of protective processes at the individual and community levels, rather than focusing solely on main effects, represents an important contribution to the literature on Latine adolescent depression and suicidality.

Conclusion

PYD factors and prosocial involvement are associated with improved mental health among Latine adolescents but may not be sufficient to buffer the harmful effects of racial-ethnic discrimination on depressive symptoms and suicidality. While PYD constructs did not statistically buffer associations between discrimination and mental health, adolescents described strengths consistent with the 5Cs that shaped how they navigated discriminatory experiences. These findings underscore the importance of integrating quantitative and qualitative approaches to understand how youth strengths operate in contexts of discrimination stress. Additional work is needed to evaluate how multiple protective factors at the individual and community levels over time may buffer the negative impacts of racial-ethnic discrimination. Further exploration of the interactions between individual PYD assets and racial-ethnic identity variables with community-level protective factors is needed. With additional work in these areas, interventions that integrate individual and community factors may be developed and evaluated.
