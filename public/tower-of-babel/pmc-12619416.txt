Evaluation of diarrhea management appropriateness and its determinants among children under five years of age in regional and national referral hospitals in Eritrea: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ghebremedhin Tesfamariam, Merhawi Bahta, Nuru Abdu, Indermeet Singh Anand, Tsegereda Gebrehiwot, Ghimja Fessahaye, Eyasu H. Tesfamariam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiarrhea is a major global public health concern, accounting for 18% of deaths in children under five. Timely and appropriate management at both household and healthcare levels is crucial for reducing mortality associated with childhood diarrhea. However, adherence to recommended guidelines for managing childhood diarrhea is often suboptimal worldwide. This study aimed to assess the overall appropriateness of diarrhea management and its associated factors in children under five, with or without co-morbid illnesses.MethodsHospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted. Data were collected retrospectively from the medical records of diarrhea cases using a two-stage stratified sampling technique. The appropriateness of diarrhea management was evaluated based on WHO and national guidelines. Descriptive statistics and logistic regression were employed using IBM SPSS (V.23).ResultsOut of the 756 children under five, only 15.9% were appropriately managed. The most common factors for inappropriate management were lack of counseling (77.8%) and the failure to prescribe zinc (49.9%). Factors significantly associated with appropriate management included vomiting (AOR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.03–2.62), some dehydration (AOR = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.08–0.71), and the prescriber qualification being a medical intern (AOR = 3.86, 95% CI: 1.91–7.81) or a degree nurse (AOR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.14–0.72).ConclusionThis study highlighted a high prevalence of inappropriate diarrhea management in children under five, indicating an urgent need for interventions to reduce associated risks.

Introduction

Diarrhea is the leading cause of death among children under five, with the majority of fatalities occurring in the African and South-East Asian regions [1–6]. Deaths from diarrhea in this age group account for about 9% of all global fatalities, translating to over 1,300 young children dying each day, many of whom could be prevented with simple treatments [5, 7]. For instance, dehydration from acute diarrhea can be safely and effectively treated in over 90% of cases through oral rehydration, except in severe cases [8].

In developing countries, including Eritrea, treatment guidelines often rely on clinical presentation due to limited laboratory services and patient overload [9]. However, adherence to these guidelines for managing common childhood illnesses, such as diarrhea and respiratory infections, remains low globally [10–12]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Pocket Book of Hospital Care for Children and the Integrated Management of Newborn and Childhood Illness (IMNCI) Chart Booklet, diarrhea management requires a systematic approach to assessment, management, and prevention [9]. The essential elements in managing a child with diarrhea include providing Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT), zinc, and counseling for continued feeding. Caregivers should also receive education on feeding and hygiene practices to reduce diarrhea morbidity [3, 8, 9].

The WHO guidelines recognize Oral Rehydration Salt (ORS) therapy as the gold standard for clinically effective and cost-efficient diarrhea management. ORS is recommended for all patients, regardless of age or causative agent, unless they are comatose or severely dehydrated, as it is less expensive, equally effective, and more practical than intravenous fluids [5, 6, 13, 14]. The new ORS formulation is officially recommended by WHO and UNICEF [3]. Furthermore, antimicrobials are reserved for cases of bloody diarrhea, severe cholera, or serious non-intestinal infections [3, 15]. WHO states that antibiotic treatment is necessary in only 5% of childhood diarrhea cases [16]. Anti-diarrheal drugs and anti-emetics should be avoided in young children with acute or persistent diarrhea or dysentery, as they do not prevent dehydration or improve nutritional status, and may cause dangerous, potentially fatal side effects [9].

Reports indicate that the application of guidelines for childhood diarrhea management is suboptimal worldwide [4, 5, 9]. This often leads to inappropriate management, unnecessary costs, side effects, delayed recovery, increased care expenses, and even death. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no prior research has assessed the extent of inappropriate diarrhea management in children under five in Eritrea. Thus, this study aims to evaluate the overall appropriateness of diarrhea management, with or without co-morbid illnesses, in children under five at regional referral hospitals and Orotta National Pediatric Referral Hospital (Orotta NPRH) in Eritrea.

Materials and methods

Study design and area

A hospital-based cross-sectional study with a quantitative approach was conducted at Orotta National Pediatric Referral Hospital (NPRH) and three of the six Regional Referral Hospitals (RRHs) in Eritrea namely Mendefera RRH, Keren RRH, and Ghindae RRH. These six RRHs are geographically distributed across the eastern lowlands, central highlands, and western lowlands of the country. All referral hospitals are public and follow the same management protocols for childhood illnesses including diarrhea. Data were retrospectively collected from the medical records of diarrhea cases.

Target population

The target population for this study included inpatient and outpatient children under five years of age with diarrheal illness, with or without co-morbid conditions, who visited the six RRHs and Orotta NPRH in 2017.

Using these assumptions, the initial sample size was calculated as 532 children under five treated for diarrheal illnesses in selected regional referral hospitals and Orotta NPRH. In 2017, a total of 9,650 children under five with diarrheal illnesses were treated in the target hospitals (Ministry of Health (MOH), central HMIS office Asmara). Therefore, finite population correction and a design effect adjustment (deft = 1.5) were applied, resulting in a final sample size of 756 children.

Sampling technique

A two-stage stratified sampling technique was employed. In the first stage, one regional referral hospital was selected from each geographical area: Mendefera RRH (Central Highlands), Keren RRH (Western Lowlands), and Ghindae RRH (Eastern Lowlands). Orotta NPRH, the only national pediatric referral hospital, was also included to ensure national representation.

In the second stage, systematic random sampling was used to select medical records of children under five with diarrheal illnesses. Invalid, lost, unreadable, or incomplete medical records were replaced with complete and relevant ones.

To ensure accurate regional and national estimates, square root allocation was applied instead of equal allocation, resulting in sample sizes of 312 for Orotta NPRH, 118 for Mendefera RRH, 137 for Ghindae RRH, and 189 for Keren RRH.

Data collection tools and approach

A data collection form was adopted from a study by Arega Gashaw et al. [17] with slight modifications to suit the objective of the study. Initially, annual Health Management Information System (HMIS) reports on diarrheal cases were obtained from the statistics office of each study hospital. A systematic random sampling (SRS) technique was then employed to select medical records, stratifying them by children’s age and type of visit (outpatient or inpatient) at each selected referral hospital.

The list of selected cases was provided to the card personnel, who retrieved the medical records using their corresponding card numbers. A trained data collection team then used these medical records to gather socio-demographic data, along with information on both subjective and objective assessments made during the diagnosis of diarrhea and the details of the treatment provided. The investigator (GT) supervised, checked, sorted, and reviewed the data manually for errors and incompleteness before entering it into the checklist.

Assessment of overall diarrhea management appropriateness

Prescribers in the referral hospitals were expected to adhere to the WHO Pocket Book of Hospital Care for Children (2013) and the MOH IMNCI Chart Booklet (2015) for managing childhood illnesses. These resources served as guidelines for the senior pediatricians and the investigator (GT) to evaluate the appropriateness of diarrhea management. The approach used to judge the overall diarrhea management was very strict. It comprehensively considered the whole patients’ assessment and drug management approach. This included the entire process, from history taking to the conclusion of drug management and the counseling provided to the care giver. A child was considered appropriately managed if their condition (diarrhea and any co-morbidities) was accurately assessed, classified, and treated according to the established guidelines.

A child was deemed appropriately managed if their condition (diarrhea and any co-morbidities) was accurately assessed, classified, and treated according to established guidelines. Specifically, for diarrhea with dehydration appropriate management meant accurate assessment, classification, and prescription of the recommended rehydration therapy based on dehydration severity, along with zinc and appropriate antimicrobial therapy when necessary. Conversely, if a child diagnosed with diarrhea had some dehydration but lacked documented signs of dehydration during the physical examination, this was considered inappropriate, even if the rehydration plan was correct. According to the WHO Pocket Book of Hospital Care for Children (2013), there are three dehydration management plans (Plans A, B, and C) corresponding to the classification of severity: No Dehydration, Some Dehydration, and Severe Dehydration, respectively. Not following these plans when treating diarrhea is considered an inappropriate approach to dehydration management.

Similarly, for a child diagnosed with pneumonia as a co-morbid condition, appropriate management required documented signs and symptoms of pneumonia; failure to document these rendered the prescribed antibiotic treatment inappropriate. Consistency between the diagnosis and both subjective and objective evidence was crucial, alongside the evaluation of pharmaceutical interventions. Thus, diarrhea management was deemed appropriate only when diarrhea and any co-morbid conditions were correctly assessed, classified, and managed according to established standards. Counseling was also a critical component in assessing the appropriateness of diarrhea management. Medical records were screened for documentation of any counseling provided. Medical records with no documented counseling were considered to indicate that no counseling was provided.

Operational definition

The appropriateness of overall diarrhea management in children is determined by adherence to established guidelines, specifically the WHO Pocket Book of Hospital Care for Children (2013) and the MOH IMNCI Chart Booklet (2015). This assessment is detailed in the ‘Assessment of Diarrhea Management Appropriateness’ section. Failure to adhere to these guidelines coupled with any lack of documentation in the medical cards for the criteria used in this assessment is considered inappropriate practice.

Data quality assurance

The face and content validity of the data collection form was assessed by a panel of two senior pediatricians, who evaluated its relevance and completeness for capturing necessary data. Their feedback was incorporated before finalizing the form. Besides, data collectors were trained on the data collection process for two days.

A pre-test was then conducted using 40 medical records of diarrheal cases from 2018 at Orotta NPRH to evaluate the form’s suitability for data collectors and its effectiveness in capturing case-specific information. Following the pre-test, the data collection form was revised to address limitations and inefficiencies. An analysis of the collected data was also performed to ensure that all information could be processed and analyzed without difficulties.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using Census and Sample Processing (V.7.2) and IBM SPSS (V.23), respectively. Descriptive analysis was performed using frequencies and percentages. Tables and figures were used to present data as needed. Primarily, the factors associated with appropriate diarrheal management were assessed using binary logistic regression. Those variables that were significant (p-value < 0.2) at bivariate level were retained for multivariable analysis. Crude Odds Ratio (COR) and Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) with 95% CI are computed to assess the magnitude and direction of association. P-values less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant at the multivariable level.

Results

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants and particulars of the prescribers

A total of 756 medical records of children with diarrhea were included in this study. The median age was 16 months (Interquartile Range (IQR) = 13 months) and more than half of the children were males (57.4%). The majority of them were outpatients (78.6%) and presented with acute diarrhea (90.9%). Moreover, over half (56.2%) of all the children had watery diarrhea, while bloody diarrhea was reported in 14.8% of the diarrheal cases. About 37.2% of the enrolled cases were seen by General practitioners (GP) with 53.6% of the prescribers having more than five years of work experience. According to the Health Worker (HW) classification, 77.2% of the diarrhea cases were documented as having no dehydration and only 19.2% had some dehydration. Vomiting (56.7%), fever (39.9%), and cough (17.5%) were the most common complaints in addition to diarrhea. Besides, about 31.5% of the children had co-morbid conditions with common cold (17.5%), pneumonia (6.2%), and malnutrition (5.8%) being the most frequently documented. Prescription pattern in the management of diarrhea and co-morbidities

Pharmaceutical products were given to all children. At least one antimicrobial was prescribed to 73% of the children. Moreover, ORS, zinc and intravenous fluids were prescribed to 84.6%, 48.3% and 13.5% of the children, respectively. Paracetamol syrup (20.8%) and multi-vitamin syrup (19.3%) were among the most commonly used medicines. Notably, anti-diarrheal and anti-emetic drugs were not prescribed for the management of diarrhea or its associated symptoms in any of the diarrheal cases. Appropriateness of diarrhea management

In the assessment of diarrhea management, only 15.9% of all children were appropriately managed. Diarrhea management was found to be more appropriate in out-patients (17.3%) than in-patients (10.5%) (Fig. 1).

Only 21.2% (n = 160) of the children’s caregivers received documented counseling on diarrhea management. The main advices provided included ‘Advice when to return’ (46.9%), ‘Give extra fluid’ (45.0%) and ‘Continue feeding’ (31.9%). Follow-up appointments were for only eight caregivers, all of whom attended. Among these, two children improved, three showed no change, and three worsened. For the112 documented cases of bloody diarrhea (99 out-patient and 13 in-patient), no follow-up appointments were given to outpatient children, with one exception. ORS was prescribed to 84.7% of the children with diarrhea. Moreover, ORS was not prescribed in 12.3% of the cases, and its use was in one instance.

Moreover, although only 27 children (26: In-patient; 1: out-patient) presented with severe dehydration, 63% (n = 102/162) of all admitted children received intravenous fluids. Ringer’s lactate was the most commonly prescribed IV fluid (76.5%, n = 78/102). Furthermore, 85.2% of children with severe dehydration were initially resuscitated with IV fluids. On the other hand, IV fluids were also administered to 41.4% of 145 children with some dehydration and 3.3% of 584 children with no dehydration. Case analysis in reference to the WHO guideline (2013) showed that 55.9% (57/102) of IV fluid administrations were unjustified before considering their administration schedule. Generally, the use of IV fluid was deemed correct in 32.4% (33/102) of cases where it was administered.

Factors associated with diarrhea management appropriateness

Binary logistic regression analysis showed that sex (p = 0.113), type of diarrhea (p = 0.063), presence of vomiting (p = 0.048), presence of fever (p = 0.102), duration of diarrhea (p = 0.037), presence of some dehydration (p < 0.001), prescriber qualification (p < 0.001), and prescriber experience (p < 0.001) were significant determinants of diarrhea management appropriateness (Table 5). Besides, multivariable logistic regression indicated that children with vomiting were approximately two times more likely to receive appropriate diarrhea management compared to those without vomiting (AOR = 1.64, 95%CI: 1.03, 2.62; p = 0.038). Moreover, children with some dehydration were 76% less likely to get appropriate diarrhea management as compared to those with no dehydration (AOR = 0.24, 95%CI: 0.08, 0.71; p = 0.009). The odds of treating diarrhea cases in adherence to guidelines was approximately four times higher among medical intern than the GPs (AOR = 3.86, 95%CI: 1.91,7.81; p < 0.001). However, degree nurse prescribers were 69% less likely to adhere to the guideline compared to general practitioners (AOR = 0.31, 95%CI: 0.14, 0.72; p = 0.006) (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, only one in six of the children (15.9%) who visited the study hospitals received appropriate diarrhea management. This is a bothersome practice that underscores the critical need for prescribers to adhere to the recommended WHO protocols. This finding is lower than results from studies conducted in Ethiopia (Hawassa City (43.3%) [18], Addis Ababa (45.6%) [17], and Oromia region (79%) [19]), Bangladesh (27%) [20] and Italy (20.6%) [21]. The variation in results could be due to differences in study design, study area and the criteria used to assess the appropriateness of diarrhea management.

In a significant number of children, diarrhea was inappropriately classified (with stool characteristics not documented and the duration of diarrhea unspecified), inconsistently diagnosed, and inadequately assessed. These shortcomings in assessment, classification, and diagnosis can lead to treatments that do not adequately address underlying issues, resulting in inappropriate diarrhea management. Such deficiencies highlight challenges in adhering to established guidelines and maintaining comprehensive clinical documentation. To address these issues, it is essential to enhance healthcare professionals’ adherence to protocols through improved awareness programs and to ensure meticulous documentation practices.

One in five caregivers of the children with diarrhea received counseling on diarrhea management, which contradicts WHO guidelines. The WHO recommends counseling on providing extra fluids, continuing feeding, and scheduling follow-up appointments (particularly for children with dysentery and other danger signs) as integral component of diarrhea management [9]. This finding is inconsistent with studies from Hawassa city, Ethiopia, where most mothers (96%) were advised to provide ORS [18] and Addis Ababa where over half caretakers received advice on extra fluids and continued feeding, and immediate return if the child was unable to drink/breastfeed or if the child’s condition worsened [17]. To further improve counseling practices, targeted awareness-raising programs for prescribers should be enhanced.

ORS was prescribed to 84.7% of the children with diarrhea in this study. However, ORS was not prescribed for 12.3% of children who needed it, and its use was incorrect in one case. WHO guidelines endorse ORS therapy as the gold standard for clinically efficacious and cost-effective management of diarrhea. Unless a patient is comatose or severely dehydrated, ORS is recommended regardless of the causative agent or patient age as it is less expensive, often equally effective, and more practical than intravenous fluid [13, 14]. The prescription of ORS in the current study is higher than reported in Iraq (15%) [22], Addis Ababa, Ethiopia (66.7%) [17], Northern Tanzania (68.4%) [22], Ujjain, India (80%) [12], and a multicenter study in Indian hospitals (82%) [23]. However, it lower than studies conducted in Lebanon (92.4%) [24], Bahrain (89.3%) [13], India (86.9%) [25], and Kenya (90%) [26].

In this study, all but four children with severe dehydration received intravenous fluids, consistent with WHO recommendations and a study conducted in South Sudan [27]. The main problem identified was that the unnecessary and improperly scheduled of use of intravenous fluids in children with all forms of dehydration. While the selection of intravenous fluids for children with some dehydration was appropriate, their administration protocol did not align with WHO guidelines. Furthermore, there was an excessive and unnecessary use of intravenous fluids in children who could have been effectively managed with ORS, contributing to inappropriate diarrhea management.

The prescription rate of zinc supplementation in this study was found to be 48.3%, which contradicts WHO recommendations. According to the WHO guideline and supported by a meta-analysis of 17 randomized controlled trials of zinc supplementation and other recent systematic reviews, zinc supplementation for children under-five reduces the duration and severity of diarrheal episodes and lowers the incidence of diarrhea in the subsequent 2–3 months. Therefore, all children with diarrhea should receive zinc supplements as early as possible after diarrhea has started as part of first-line treatment [9, 28–30]. The current study’s finding is higher than studies conducted in Ethiopia (45.3%) [18] and (43.7%) [17], India (38.7%) [31], Tanzania 28% [32] Iraq (1.25%) [22] and Uganda (0%) [33].

Additionally, in the current study, the dose, route, frequency, and duration of antibiotics, zinc, and other medicines were often incorrect and/or undocumented in a substantial number of children. The low prescription rate of zinc and incorrect prescriptions in this study may indicate that prescribers’ low awareness on its importance or inadequate documentation of treatments, necessitating intervention from the policy makers and program managers.

Notably, there was no documented use of anti-diarrheal and anti-emetic medicines in this study which is in line with the WHO recommendations. These medicines are not recommended for management of acute diarrhea as they can reduce intestinal motility, prolong the disease course, extend pathogen contact with the intestinal mucosa, and potentially worsen systemic symptoms [34]. Such medicines offer no benefit in diarrhea management and may pose serious, even life-threatening side effects in children. This finding differs from a study in Iraq where anti-diarrheal and anti-emetics medicines were used in 12.8% and 33.2% of the cases, respectively [32]. Similar studies reported prescription rates for anti-diarrheal and anti-emetics in diarrhea management as 0% and 7% [17], and 0% and 3.4% [18], respectively.

The presence of vomiting, some dehydration, and prescriber qualification were identified as determinants of appropriate diarrhea management. This finding contrasts with studies conducted in Ethiopia, where diarrhea management appropriateness was significantly associated with children’s age and stool characteristics [17, 18].

The odds of treating diarrhea cases according to guidelines were about four times higher among the medical intern than among GPs. This finding, however, requires careful interpretation. One plausible explanation is that medical interns, operating under the close supervision of senior pediatric physicians, are held to a higher standard of meticulous documentation. This structured environment ensures that all aspects of guideline adherence are meticulously recorded, which may not always be the case for GPs facing high patient volumes and time constraints in a less supervised setting. Consequently, the observed difference may, in part, reflect a disparity in documentation practices rather than a fundamental difference in clinical competence. This is supported by the non-significant findings for “correct dose/route/frequency/duration of treatment” and “correct antibiotic prescription”. Conversely, degree nurse prescribers were 68% less likely to adhere to guidelines for appropriate diarrhea management compared to GPs, which could be due to differences in knowledge among the prescribers.

The study has several limitations that warrant caution when interpreting its findings. As the results were derived from a secondary data, patient medical records may have been incomplete or contained unreadable information, potentially leading to an underestimation or overestimation of certain findings. Specifically, most diagnoses and conclusions regarding signs of infection were based solely on physician documentation rather than objective interpretation, which could introduce bias. Furthermore, the lack of documentation for any criteria used in the assessment of appropriateness was considered to indicate inappropriate diarrhea management. This methodological approach may have resulted in an overestimation of inadequate diarrhea management, even in cases where treatment was performed correctly. Furthermore, data on the outcomes of diarrhea management were not collected. The findings of this study may also lack generalizability to the entire country, as it was conducted only in national and regional referral hospitals. Therefore, the authors recommend broader future studies that include lower health facilities, utilize primary sources, and employ qualitative methods, to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the subject, including outcomes in diarrheal cases.

Conclusions and recommendations

Inappropriate diarrhea management was prevalent among children under five years of age. Key determinants of management appropriateness included the presence of vomiting, dehydration status, and prescriber qualifications. Identified issues included inadequate documentation, lack of proper counseling, underutilization of zinc supplementation, and excessive use of intravenous fluids., It is crucial to strengthen awareness programs for healthcare professionals and ensure strict adherence to guidelines and protocols by relevant authorities to improve diarrhea management in this age group.
