Wiktoria Mieleszczenko-Kowszewicz, Dawid Płudowski, Filip Kołodzieczyk, Jakub Świstak, Julian Sienkiewicz, Przemysław Biecek

Abstract
. This study explores how the Large Language Models (LLMs) adjust linguistic features to create personalized persuasive outputs. While research showed that LLMs personalize outputs, a gap remains in understanding the linguistic features of their persuasive capabilities. We identified 13 linguistic features crucial for influencing personalities across different levels of the Big Five model of personality. We analyzed how prompts with personality trait information influenced the output of 19 LLMs across five model families. The findings show that models use more anxiety-related words for neuroticism, increase achievement-related words for conscientiousness, and employ fewer cognitive processes words for openness to experience. Some model families excel at adapting language for openness to experience, others for conscientiousness, while only one model adapts language for neuroticism. Our findings show how LLMs tailor responses based on personality cues in prompts, indicating their potential to create persuasive content affecting the mind and well-being of the recipients.



1.
Introduction

The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) into the public consciousness at the end of 2022 has significantly transformed the reality of completing tasks across various sectors, from business to education through administration and journalism. It is estimated that in the USA, 80% of employees will have 10% of their work affected by LLMs, with approximately 19% of occupations will have an impact on at least 50% of job duties
(
Eloundou et al. 2023
)
. The applications of LLMs in education include question-solving, error correction, study assistance, question generation, automatic grading, and material creation. LLMs also support adaptive learning through knowledge tracking and personalizing content
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
.

The vast amount of tasks where LLMs are used will inevitably raise serious challenges related to possible harms, including discrimination, exclusion, toxicity, information hazards, misinformation, malicious uses, and human-computer interaction harm
(
Weidinger et al. 2021
)
. Multiple techniques are applied to protect users from potentially harmful content, such as probing toxic content with structured patterns
(
Ousidhoum et al. 2021
)
and watermarking the suspected text fragment
(
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023
)
. LLMs are also used in red teaming activities, where they cooperate with other LLMs to safeguard models against misuse
(
Perez et al. 2022
)
.
The effectiveness of these techniques depends on the awareness of potential threats. In our work, we focus on a specific danger: the persuasion by LLMs. To our knowledge, this is the first paper aimed at understanding how LLMs adjust their responses based on a user’s personality in a persuasion task.

Given the goal we want to achieve, we pose the following research questions:

[RQ1]

Which linguistic features are crucial in personalized persuasion?

[RQ2]

How do Large Language Models adjust the linguistic features in their responses based on the user’s personality type information?

[RQ3]

Which families of Large Language Models are particularly effective in influencing specific personality traits?

Contribution

By answering the questions above, we contributed as follows:

[C1]
We identified 13 linguistic features that are crucial for persuading individuals with varying levels of Big Five personality traits.

[C2]
We conducted a comparative analysis of 19 models from 5 Large Language Models families, focusing on the language used in responses to persuasive tasks.

[C3]
We demonstrated how LLMs adapt their language in persuasive task to different personality types.

[C4]
We presented the dataset with different variances of persuasive task that can be used to validate the personalized persuasiveness of the Large Language Models.

2.
Related work

2.1.
AI and persuasion

One of the most severe threats of using LLMs is their ability to promote certain narratives persuasively
(
Shevlane et al. 2023
)
. Research confirmed that messages generated by LLMs can be as persuasive as those created by humans
(
Voelkel et al. 2023
)
. Additionally, the most common goal (27%) of GenAI misuse involves shaping or influencing public opinion with disinformation, image cultivation comprising presentation of the positive qualities of a public figure, or defamation causing damaging the reputation
(
Marchal et al. 2024
)
.

LLMs can not only create persuasive content but also personalize it at scale particularly when the personality of a human is known
(
Matz et al. 2024
)
. Since machine learning algorithms can predict a user’s personality
(
Kosinski et al. 2013
)
,
the threat of using LLMs to create personalized persuasive content poses a severe risk.
The personalized manipulation often involves the application of certain persuasion strategies in the communication, particularly the most widely recognized six Cialdini’s principles: reciprocation, consistency, social validation, liking , authority and scarcity
(
Wall et al. 2019
;
Cialdini 2001
)
. In addition, Aristotelian taxonomy identifies three core persuasive strategies: rational argumentation (logos), credibility of interlocutor (ethos) and appealing to emotion (pathos)
(
Braet 1992
)
. This exposes the diverse strategies that LLMs can employ to persuade people.

Previous research indicates that when Large Language Models (LLMs) are tasked with persuading a recipient, they predominantly employ a logos strategy
(
Wilczyński et al. 2024
)
. A research gap exists on certain persuasive-linguistic features that LLMs may use in persuasive communication.

2.2.
Language and persuasion

Our vocabulary and language affect our communicative abilities and reveal people’s priorities, intentions, and thoughts
(
Tausczik and Pennebaker 2010
)
. Research in this area focuses not only on the qualitative analysis of the content but also on the measurable (quantitative) amount of specific vocabulary within the message.
According to the research, the linguistic features that are connected with the persuasiveness of the message are word count, language emotionality, language abstraction or concreteness, analytical thinking, lexical diversity, hedges, certainty, self-references, reading difficulty, and examples
(
Ta et al. 2022
)
.
However, the study’s author’s approach does not take into account personality traits that significantly impact persuasion effectiveness.

The idea that each personality type has its characteristic way of being influenced by others is rooted in individual differences in personality traits. The Big Five personality model comprises five universal dimensions across cultures
(
Costa and McCrae 2008
;
Heine and Buchtel 2009
)
.
The first dimension captured by this model,
neuroticism
, describes a tendency to experience negative emotions such as anxiety and overall emotional instability. Individuals high in neuroticism are more prone to messages containing negative emotions such as anxiety, sadness, or anger
(
Reynaud et al. 2012
)
.
The second of the Big Five traits,
extraversion
, is characterized by a tendency to be sociable and more assertive. People high in extraversion are more engaged in social events and are more easily persuaded by interpersonal contact
(
Fishman et al. 2011
)
.

Agreeableness
is linked to tolerance, trust, and respect toward others. To persuade someone who scores high on the agreeableness trait, communication should include positive emotions and avoid conflict-saturated language
(
Kleef et al. 2010
;
Graziano et al. 1996
)
.

Openness to experience
characterizes the open-mindedness, vivid imagination, and preference for variety. People with a high level of openness are more eager to communicate
(
Piechurska-Kuciel 2018
)
and engage in intellectual activities
(
Kaufman 2013
)
.
Due to these characteristics, framing complex, emotional, and creative text can be particularly effective in persuading individuals with high levels of this trait.
Finally,
conscientiousness
, the last of the Big Five traits, is characterized by a high level of organization, responsibility, and strong ethical standards
(
Roberts et al. 2014
)
. Highly conscientious individuals are goal-oriented and meticulously complete their work
(
Dudley et al. 2006
;
MacCann et al. 2009
)
.
These characteristics are the foundation for identifying the linguistic features crucial for successfully persuading individuals based on their personality type.

Methodology

We selected linguistic features associated with personalized persuasion, as identified in the literature (see Section
2.2
). The experiment started with aquiring corpora containing LLM responses, from which we extracted the frequency of persuasive linguistic features. We identified the most significant ones using Shapley values
(
Lundberg, Scott M and Lee, Su-In 2017
)
, an explainable AI (XAI) method. This step allowed us to narrow down the linguistic features for the final stage: a regression analysis, where we examined how the interaction between the selected variables and specific personality traits influences the model’s output. Figure

3.2.
Lingustic feature extraction

After obtaining the LLMs’ responses, we analyzed them using the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) software, which measures the frequency of word usage in the text across built-in psycholinguistic categories
(
Boyd et al. 2022
)
.
Following a thorough review of the literature on the Big Five Personality Traits, we selected 13 linguistic features from the LIWC analysis relevant to personalized persuasion. Each feature was associated with a trait that could be influenced by linguistic persuasion.

We chose LIWC because it includes broader categories than those typically used in sentiment analysis. This enabled us to extend the content analysis of the models’ output to capture the presence of psychological mechanisms related to motivation, cognition, or social processes.

3.3.
Linguistic feature selection

We aim to examine how the LLM’s responses are influenced by providing information about “trait” in the prompt. While this could be obtained by statistical analysis, we decided not to impose any assumption on these “trait”-linguistic feature relations (like e.g., linearity or additional correlation with “level” element of the prompt). Thus, we evaluate this influence by calculating Shapley values
(
Lundberg, Scott M and Lee, Su-In 2017
)
on the classical machine learning model, which aims to predict the value of specific linguistic features based on the prompt variables’ values. To not impose any strict assumption on the data and also due to the categorical nature of the task, we decided to use a random forest model
(
Breiman 2001
)
.

The Shapley values measure the importance of the given variable to the machine learning model’s prediction reasoning. Thus, a high Shapley value (importance) means that the variable significantly impacts the final prediction.

We consider the LLMs capable of using specific linguistic features properly when the importance assigned to the personality trait suitable to be pursued with specific linguistic aspects is the highest according to the Shapley values for the random forest model. In total, we test 13 linguistic features; two of them (analytical thinking and achievement) are considered important for two traits simultaneously, resulting in 15 experiments in total. For further technical details about the experiment’s methodology, please see Appendix
A
.

3.4.
Examination of relationship

We performed linear regression analysis for selected pairs of traits and variables in the form of:

(1)

\text{lingustic feature}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\times\text{level}

to quantify the strength of influence of the level of specific traits on linguistic features. We applied dummy coding for the level categorical variable, treating the “low” level as a reference point and calculating
\beta_{1}
coefficient with respect to the “high” level. To aggregate the results over different models, we used the average
\langle\beta_{1}\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta^{(i)}_{1}
coefficient along with the standard error calculated as
\hat{\sigma}_{\langle\beta_{1}\rangle}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{(i)}}
, where
\hat{\sigma}_{(i)}
stands for a standard error of a single
\beta^{(i)}_{1}
coefficient and
N
is the number of models that were significant in a specific trait–linguistic feature combination. The “medium” level results are not reported as in all the examined cases. They coincided with either the “low” or the “high“ level, bringing no additional insights to the analysis.

4.
Results

4.1.
Models’ obedience

In the experiment, the LLMs were prompted 11,400 times in total. First, we aimed to assess whether the models completed the persuasion task. The primary motivation for this analysis was to exclude from the linguistic analysis cases where LLMs refused to engage in the persuasive task. After this assessment, we found that 9,171 tasks were completed. These were our analysis’s objectives. We defined task completion as active participation in the discussion by presenting arguments on the specified topic. If a model initially refused to respond but later provided arguments within the same answer, we considered the task to be completed. It is important to note that we did not evaluate the quality of the response or its compliance with the criteria outlined in the prompt.

Responses where the LLMs refused to answer were excluded from the analysis. Figure
3
presents the overall statistics across all models. The models varied in their tendency to generate persuasive content, allowing us to distinguish two groups based on their propensity to produce personalized persuasive content. The first group includes Anthropic’s model family, which often refused to engage in persuasive tasks, and Meta’s model family, which showed similar resistance. The second group comprises Mistral AI, OpenAI, and Alibaba’s model family, except for Qwen 4B, which aligned more with the first group. These model families were generally more inclined to complete the persuasive task.

5.
Discussion and Implications

5.1.
Personalized Persuasion by Linguistic Features

The threat of persuasively creating certain narratives is severe and requires understanding the mechanism behind constructing persuasive messages.
This paper demonstrated that Large Language Models tailor persuasive language based on personality information.
The primary consequence is that almost every Large Language Model can still influence human emotions and pursue to change people’s minds, despite passing different international law to counteract these practices. The AI Act, a comprehensive regulation on the use of Artificial Intelligence prepared by the European Parliament, raises significant concerns about AI systems that can not only manipulate people but also recognize human emotions and intentions, viewing them as a potential threat to human rights and freedoms. According to AI ACT, using AI systems for such purposes is banned in workplaces and educational settings, although it is permitted in medicine
(
European Commission 2018
)
. This capability is troubling as it exposes users to risks like misinformation and emotional manipulation and strengthen biases
(
Chen and Xiao 2023
)
. Failure to safeguard LLMs from misuse in personalized persuasion within education could lead to their ban in schools, potentially resulting in the underexploitation
of this promising technology in such a critical sector.

In recent years, mental health has become a priority on the global health agenda. For instance, the World Health Organization is to implement strategies for prevention in mental health
(
Organization 2013
)
, alongside the United Nations’s agenda of promoting mental health
(
United Nations 2015
)
. We should also make sure that ground-breaking technology, and we consider LLMs to be such, considering the long-term consequences they may have on their users and contribute to global efforts in mental health.
The use of negative vocabulary in communication can have a significant impact on people high on neuroticism since they tend to experience more discomfort in response to negative stimuli, which can increase their vulnerability to stress and mental health disorders
(
Watson and Clark 1984
)
.
Research shows that neurotics are more likely to experience adverse health such outcomes as cardiovascular problems, which can be strengthened by communication saturated with negative affect
(
Denollet et al. 2010
)
.
These results are particularly relevant for journalism, media, and entertainment industries, where using LLMs will become more common. Given the significant impact these industries have on public opinion and information dissemination, it is crucial that they exercise caution when integrating LLMs into their work.

The model’s ability to increase achievement-related wording in response to the conscientiousness trait supports its capacity to tailor persuasion to this specific personality trait. Using this linguistic feature to motivate individuals with high levels of this trait does not appear to be a harmful form of persuasion, provided that individuals are adequately informed about the use of this method.
The observed decrease in cognitive processing-related words for individuals high in openness to experience presents a counterintuitive outcome. This result may indicate the model’s limited understanding of the role that cognitive processing words plays in persuading individuals with this trait. Alternatively, the model may have learned to prioritize more intuitive and abstract linguistic features, which may better resonate with those who strongly need to engage with and experience reality.
Understanding this result requires further research to clarify the nature of this relationship.

5.2.
Ethics of persuasion by the LLMs

Much has already been said about the ethics of artificial intelligence.
Several suggestions were proposed to address the issue, including user guidance, accountability policies, monitoring of misuses, and correction of bias
(
Zhou et al. 2023
)
.

Different metrics show capabilities, limitations, and potential areas of improvement across different tasks and dimensions.
For instance, BLEU and ROUGE are metrics used to measure the quality of generated language
(
Papineni et al. 2002
;
Reiter and Belz 2009
)
. At the same time, perplexity is a key metric for evaluating how well a model predicts the following sequence of words.
Other vital benchmarks’ results inform us how trustworthy and responsible the model is
(
Huang et al. 2023
)
or about its factual reliability
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
.
Given that Large Language Models may continue to generate manipulative responses, we emphasize the need for the creation of new metric evaluating models for personalized linguistic persuasion, particularly for tasks that may produce text with potentially damaging consequences for humanity, such as misinformation or discrimination. We acknowledge that the adaptability of LLMs to individual users is one of their most groundbreaking features. However, we believe that technological advancements should harness the users’ individuality to help them realize their potential rather than serve the unethical objectives of third parties.

6.
Limitations and Future Work

Our research focused on the impact of a limited number of personality trait intensity levels.
In addition, we realize that a person is a combination of all the Big Five personality dimensions, which interact with each other. Further studies could consider the interactions of these traits in different configurations.

The limitations of the present study also include variations in the responses generated by LLMs. Nevertheless, we decided to show the results because the noticed tendency is stable across different models. We hope that our data will be used to start a new line of research that includes not only the interaction of different personality traits but also different parameters of the model, such as temperature, the user, or max tokens.

In this section, we describe in detail how we evaluate models’ capabilities for linguistic feature extraction. In particular, we elaborate on data preparation, model training, and extraction of the information from the SHAP values.

As an input to the model, we use variables from the prompt (listed in Table
1
). Since all values are nominal, we use a one-hot encoding. For binary variables (e.g., gender), we drop one of the categories. We did not use variables “Question”, “Pronoun”, and “Belief” as they are not relevant to this part of the study.

As a predicted variable, we used linguistic features from Figure
4
. For each of the models and each of the linguistic features, we train separate random forest models. Then, we calculate the SHAP values without interactions for each input variable. Due to one-hot encoding preprocessing, the importance is assigned to each variable’s values.

The random forest model is kept relatively small to adjust its size to the task’s difficulty (small number of variables). The number of trees is set to 100, and the max depth is set to 5. This configuration is kept across all of the experiments.

In our work, we want to verify whether the model uses the presence or absence of a specific trait to decide if using a specific linguistic feature is appropriate. In terms of classical machine learning, we want to determine if certain traits are important to the model that predicts the magnitude of specific linguistic features in LLM’s answer.
