Attentional or Non-Tactile Sensory Tricks in Idiopathic Cervical Dystonia: A Phenomenological Observation
Authors: Tara Khoeini, Gholam Ali Shahidi, Mohammad Rohani
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Sensory tricks (geste antagoniste) are well-known clinical features of focal dystonias. These are subtle tactile or proprioceptive actions that transiently relieve abnormal postures or movements [1]. These tricks, most often seen in patients with cervical dystonia, have traditionally been explained by the influence of peripheral sensory input on motor output through afferent feedback. However, growing neurophysiological and imaging evidence suggests that their effects extend far beyond simple peripheral stimulation [2,3]. This broader understanding points to the role of higher-level cognitive and attentional processes in shaping motor control in dystonia.

While classical tactile gestures are well described, sensory tricks that occur without any physical contact are seldom recognized. Earlier reports have described “imaginary” tricks, in which the mere intention or simulation of a gesture can reduce dystonic posturing [4,5]. These non-tactile or attentional sensory tricks provide unique insight into the top-down modulation of sensorimotor integration. Recognizing such subtle forms may also open possibilities for non-invasive interventions that use attention, imagery, or cognitive focus to modulate abnormal motor activity.

Here, we present three patients with idiopathic cervical dystonia who showed consistent, reproducible improvement in abnormal postures through attentional focus or imagined touch. Unlike earlier descriptions of imaginary sensory tricks, our cases highlight a sustained, clearly observable correction of dystonic posture driven solely by attentional focus or imagined proximity. This effect was consistently reproducible across multiple patients and was objectively documented on video, illustrating a distinct and underappreciated variant of the sensory trick phenomenon.

The first case involved a 55-year-old man with idiopathic right torticollis for 1 year, whose abnormal head posture was corrected by placing a ruler against the right side of his head. The same effect occurred when he mimicked the ruler’s position using his fingers in the air, several centimeters away from his face. The second case involved a 40-year-old woman with right laterocollis for 6 months, who showed marked improvement when she held her hand close to her nose without touching it. She described that imagining the same movement alleviated her symptoms. The last case was a 32-year-old male with a 3-month history of left torticollis who reported consistent relief when positioning—or even imagining—his finger near his cheek (Supplementary Video 1).

In all three cases, the improvement was immediate, reproducible, and documented on video. All patients exhibited true sensory tricks through tactile stimulation (touching the face) and were able to recognize both tactile and non-tactile tricks concurrently since the onset of dystonia. No voluntary suppression or placebo cues were observed. Although placebo effects cannot be formally excluded, the immediacy, reproducibility, and consistency across repeated examinations support physiological modulation rather than voluntary suppression alone. The patients were followed for 12–18 months, during which time no habituation or reduction in the efficacy of the sensory tricks was observed.

Classical geste antagoniste has traditionally been defined as a light touch that transiently improves dystonia [1]. Several variants of sensory tricks—including motor, forcible, and imaginary tricks—have been described, which exist on a continuum, ranging from peripherally triggered tactile gestures to internally generated or attentional maneuvers [2]. Our observations describe a distinct variation of tricks that does not require any tactile input. In these non-tactile tricks, a specific posture or mental representation of the gesture is sufficient to induce improvement.

Neurophysiological data suggest that higher-order cortical mechanisms play a crucial role in the phenomenon of sensory tricks. The execution of these maneuvers has been associated with changes in cortical activity, notably decreased synchronization in the alpha and theta rhythms in the relevant motor regions, which aligns with clinical symptom reduction [3]. These findings suggest that tricks modulate top-down attentional and motor control networks rather than merely tactile feedback pathways [1]. In fact, the benefit often began before any potential contact. This resembles the “closing-the-loop” phenomenon, where symptom relief preceded actual touch [6]. On the other hand, patients who respond to sensory tricks exhibit better visuotactile discrimination and more efficient sensory integration than non-responders. These findings may reflect an internal recalibration of aberrant sensorimotor loops through attentional redirection, potentially involving parietal–premotor circuits implicated in body schema and motor intention [7].

The recognition of non-tactile sensory tricks broadens our understanding of dystonia as a network disorder involving impaired inhibition, maladaptive plasticity, and abnormal sensorimotor integration [3,7]. The reproducible relief observed in our patients through visualization or attentional focus implies that cognitive and imagery-based interventions—such as mirror therapy, focused attention training, or mental rehearsal—might represent future non-invasive adjuncts in dystonia therapy [8]. Furthermore, awareness of this subtle form can improve clinical recognition; since no tactile contact occurs, such tricks may be overlooked during examination.

While placebo effects are not necessarily ruled out, the immediate and consistent benefit points in the direction of genuine physiological modulation. The process by which patients identified these non-tactile tricks was not systematically assessed, representing a limitation and an area for future investigation. Controlled neurophysiological recording at the time of non-tactile tricks could be used to further define their neural substrates and help characterize the cortical circuitry mediating this effect. Specifically, demonstrating task-related event-related desynchronization using electroencephalography–electromyography co-registration or functional magnetic resonance imaging-measured cortical and subcortical activity would strongly support a genuine physiological mechanism.

Recognizing non-tactile sensory tricks has clear implications for bedside examination and patient counseling. Their presence further supports the view of dystonia as a network disorder that can be modulated through cognitive and attentional mechanisms and can help guide the development of future noninvasive therapeutic approaches.
