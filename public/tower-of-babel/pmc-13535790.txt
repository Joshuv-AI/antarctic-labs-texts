Maternal Body Mass Index, Gestational Weight Gain, and Cardiovascular Disease in Offspring
Authors: Hui Wang, Jette Möller, Yajun Liang, Imre Janszky, Krisztina D. László
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionAre higher maternal body mass index in early pregnancy and gestational weight gain associated with cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk in offspring?FindingsIn this cohort study of Swedish national registry data for 2 496 335 offspring, CVD risk was higher among those exposed to maternal overweight and increased with the severity of obesity; greater maternal gestational weight gain was also associated with elevated CVD risk. Sibling analyses suggested that genetic or shared familial factors may not fully explain these associations.MeaningThe findings of this study highlight the importance of managing maternal weight during pregnancy as a potential strategy for preventing CVD in future generations.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading causes of mortality worldwide.1 With advancements in the primary prevention of CVD, the incidence of CVD has decreased over the past decades in high-income countries among middle-aged and older adults.2 In contrast, the increasing CVD rates in younger populations represent a new public health challenge.2,3 Given that the etiology of early-onset CVD may differ from that presenting in later ages,4 it is important to study potential risk factors for CVD in younger populations.5

It has been increasingly recognized that CVD may have origins in the prenatal period.6,7 According to 1 study, maternal overweight and obesity affect 44% of women of reproductive age.8 Maternal overweight and obesity in early pregnancy are associated with adverse birth outcomes9 and could result in a suboptimal intrauterine environment, which may increase the risk of cardiovascular dysfunction in offspring.10 To our knowledge, only 3 studies investigated the association between maternal overweight and obesity and the risk of offspring CVD.11,12,13 These studies have been limited by relatively small sample sizes11,13 and short follow-up periods (up to the age of 25 years).12

The prevalence of inadequate and excessive gestational weight gain (GWG) has increased among women of reproductive age.14,15 Higher maternal GWG has been associated with adverse birth outcome16 and worse cardiometabolic profiles in offspring during childhood and adolescence.17,18,19,20,21 However, the long-term association of inappropriate GWG with the offspring’s CVD risk in adulthood remains unclear.

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted a nationwide cohort study, in which we investigated the associations of maternal body mass index (BMI) and GWG, stratified by BMI, with offspring CVD risk and the potential roles of shared familial factors and adverse pregnancy and perinatal outcome.22,23

Methods

Study Population and Design

The Swedish Medical Birth Register (MBR) contains information on antenatal, obstetric, and neonatal care for virtually all births (98%-99%) in Sweden since 1973.24 It contains data on maternal weight and height since 1982, except for the years 1990 and 1991.24 This unavailability likely reflects changes in data-collection procedures or registry-coding practices in the MBR during that period.24 Thus, we restricted our study period to January 1, 1982, through December 31, 2014, with the exclusion of data from 1990 and 1991. After excluding 51 526 offspring diagnosed with congenital heart disease, offspring with available data on maternal BMI were comparable across all covariates with those excluded due to missing maternal BMI (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). To obtain further data on offspring, mothers, and/or their family members, we cross-linked the MBR with the Patient Register, the Total Population Register, the Education Register, the Multigeneration Register, and the Cause of Death Register.25,26,27,28 This cohort study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority, which waived the requirement for informed consent because the study used only pseudonymized registry-based data. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies.

Study Variables and Their Source

Maternal BMI

We retrieved data on maternal height and weight during early pregnancy from the MBR.24 Weight was measured in light clothing at the first prenatal visit, which occurred in the first 14 weeks of gestation for 90% of pregnant women in Sweden; height was self-reported.24 We calculated BMI by dividing self-reported weight in kilograms with height in squared meters and classified it according to the World Health Organization’s classification into the following categories: underweight (BMI <18.5), normal weight (18.5-24.9), overweight (25.0-29.9), obesity class 1 (30.0-34.9), obesity class 2 (35.0-39.9), and obesity class 3 (≥40.0). These categories were used to enhance clinical and epidemiologic interpretability and comparability with previous studies.12,29

Maternal GWG

Maternal GWG was calculated as the weight on admission to the maternal delivery ward minus the weight at the first prenatal visit.24 GWG was standardized into z scores using the published Swedish chart that accounted for gestational age and stratified by early-pregnancy BMI.30 The GWG z score was treated both as a continuous and a categorical variable (≤−1, >−1 to <1, and ≥1). To facilitate interpretability for the reader, we converted z scores back to the corresponding weight at 40 weeks’ gestation on the Swedish chart.

CVD

Follow-up started on the date of birth and ended on the date of the first diagnosis of CVD, the date of death, emigration from Sweden (available until December 31, 2014), or December 31, 2023, whichever came first. Our primary outcome was offspring major CVD, defined as a composite measure of the following conditions: 1) vascular disease (ischemic heart disease and cerebrovascular disease), 2) heart failure, 3) hypertensive disease, 4) cardiomyopathy, and 5) atrial fibrillation. These outcomes were identified from the Patient Register using primary and secondary diagnoses and from the Cause of Death Register. The primary outcome was further stratified into secondary outcomes based on the specific types of CVD. The validity of these outcomes was shown to be high.25,31,32,33 We used the International Classification of Diseases, Eighth Revision (ICD-8) and ICD-9 and the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision codes to identify these medical conditions (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).

Covariates

Covariates we considered as potential confounders based on a priori knowledge12,18 included maternal age, parity at the index birth, birth country, educational level and income before the index birth, smoking in early pregnancy, history of psychiatric disorders and CVD before the index birth, and offspring sex and birth year. Adverse pregnancy and perinatal outcomes included preterm birth, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, gestational diabetes, placental abruption, large or small for gestational age,34 asphyxia-related morbidity, and neonatal hypoglycemia. Detailed information of these covariates is provided in eMethods 1 in Supplement 1.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed from February 1, 2025, to June 16, 2026. We examined the associations between maternal BMI and offspring CVD using Cox proportional hazards regression models, with offspring-attained age as the underlying time scale. The corresponding hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were calculated after adjusting for the aforementioned covariates. The Cox proportional hazards regression assumption was tested with Schoenfeld residuals. To examine the associations of maternal BMI and the GWG z score with offspring CVD, we first applied restricted cubic splines with knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles to assess potential nonlinearity. Analyses of the GWG z score were further stratified by maternal BMI in early pregnancy. Subsequently, the GWG z score was categorized, with the reference group defined as those with a z score of great than −1 to less than 1. To account for multiple offspring to the same mother, we applied the robust sandwich estimator for the covariance matrix in all Cox proportional hazards regression models.

We conducted sibling analyses to evaluate whether the observed associations of maternal BMI and GWG with offspring CVD could be attributed to shared familial factors. We fitted stratified Cox proportional hazards regression models with a separate stratum for each family, such that only siblings who were discordant for both the exposure and outcome were used for the estimates.

Data on GWG were missing for 49.4% of the deliveries. The pattern of missing data on GWG appeared to be random, as supported by previous studies35,36 and by our data in eTable 3 in Supplement 1. Therefore, to minimize potential bias and to enhance statistical power, we conducted sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation by chained equations to account for the missing data for GWG and all covariates included in the main analyses.37 Fifty imputed datasets were created, and Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed separately for each dataset. The results were then combined using Rubin rules to produce the final summary estimates.37 We conducted mediation analyses using the counterfactual framework developed by VanderWeele38 to examine the mediating association with adverse pregnancy and perinatal outcomes. A detailed description of the methods is provided in eMethods 2 in Supplement 1. To address potential misclassification of offspring CVD, in further sensitivity analyses, we defined a CVD diagnosis as either at least 1 record in the inpatient register or at least 2 records in the outpatient register7 or as using the primary diagnosis recoded in the inpatient and outpatient register. To account for the potential BMI underestimation due to self-reported height, we lowered the upper bounds of the BMI category by 0.5. To assess potential confounding by maternal comorbidities, we performed additional analyses with sequential adjustment for preexisting diabetes and autoimmune disease. We also examined whether the associations varied by offspring sex and calendar period.

All statistical analyses were performed using R, version 4.4.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing). A 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Among 3 006 061 live singleton births, information on maternal BMI was available for 2 496 335 offspring (83.0%), of whom 1 212 687 (48.6%) were female and 1 283 648 (51.4%) were male. At the end of follow-up, the mean (SD) age was 23.3 (9.8) years. During a median (IQR) follow-up of 22.0 (14.7-30.4) years, 22 510 offspring (0.9%) were diagnosed with CVD; 17 605 (0.7%) died, and 55 068 (2.2%) emigrated before CVD. Maternal younger age, lower educational level, and smoking during early pregnancy were associated with higher rates of CVD. Rates of CVD in offspring were also higher in adverse pregnancies and pregnancies with perinatal outcomes (eTable 4 in Supplement 1). Maternal mean (SD) BMI in early pregnancy was 23.8 (4.1). Mothers with overweight or obesity in early pregnancy were more likely to have higher parity, be an older age, have a lower educational level, have a history of psychiatric disorders, experience cardiovascular diseases, and smoke during pregnancy (Table 1).

Similar patterns were observed in the sibling analyses, although higher BMIs were associated with attenuated risk of offspring CVD (Table 2).

In restricted cubic spline analyses, offspring CVD risk increased gradually with higher maternal BMI (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). A J-shaped association was observed between maternal GWG z score and offspring CVD in the whole population (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). Compared with offspring of mothers whose GWG z scores were in the reference group (ie, >−1 to <1), the HR for major CVD was 1.13 (95% CI, 1.08-1.19) for offspring of mothers with z scores of 1 or more. When stratified by maternal BMI, similar patterns were observed among offspring of mothers with normal BMI; greater GWG (z score ≥1, corresponding to a BMI of 19.35 at 40 gestational weeks) was associated with an increased risk of major CVD (HR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.09-1.22]). This observed pattern for major CVD remained consistent in analyses for specific types of CVD, including vascular disease, heart failure, and hypertensive disease (Figure 1) and cardiomyopathy and atrial fibrillation (Figure 2). In sibling analyses, the HRs remained elevated (Table 3).

Discussion

In this population-based cohort study involving 2 496 335 individuals, we found that the risk of clinically diagnosed CVD in offspring was higher with maternal overweight and the severity of obesity. Additionally, greater maternal GWG was associated with higher risks of CVD in offspring. Adverse pregnancy and perinatal outcomes explained only a small proportion of these associations. Sibling analyses suggested that genetic or shared familial factors may not fully account for the observed associations.

Previous evidence of the association of maternal obesity with offspring CVD is limited but generally consistent with our findings. A Scottish (N = 37 709)13 and a Finnish (N = 13 345)11 birth cohort study reported higher risks of cardiovascular events, coronary heart disease, and stroke among adult offspring of mothers with higher BMI. A previous Swedish study12 involving 2 230 115 live singleton births also found increased CVD risk among offspring of mothers with overweight or obesity, although follow-up was limited to a median of 11.9 years, with participants being no older than aged 26 years at the end of follow-up. Our study extends this previous study by providing over a decade of additional follow-up (ie, up to the age of 41 years) and by examining a broader spectrum of CVD types. Consistent with the previous study,12 we did not observe an association of maternal underweight with offspring CVD risk in our study. Further studies are warranted to investigate this association across different populations and follow-up periods.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to analyze the association between GWG and risks of clinical diagnoses of CVD in offspring, stratified by maternal BMI during early pregnancy. A Scottish cohort study involving 3781 individuals born between 1950 and 1956 and followed up from 1981 to 200139 found that a larger GWG was associated with increased risks of cerebrovascular events but not overall CVDs in offspring. However, this study did not account for maternal prepregnancy BMI; furthermore, the characteristics of participants in this historical cohort may differ from those in contemporary obstetric populations. In the present study, greater GWG was associated with a higher risk of major CVD in offspring. Compared with offspring in the reference GWG group, offspring of mothers with GWG z scores of 1 or higher had an increased risk of major CVD. Importantly, this association was also observed among offspring of mothers with a normal weight in early pregnancy. This finding suggests that excessive GWG may provide information on offspring cardiovascular risk beyond maternal adiposity at the start of pregnancy. The observed pattern was generally consistent across several specific CVD outcomes, including vascular disease, hypertensive disease, cardiomyopathy, and atrial fibrillation. In sibling analyses, the estimates remained elevated, although with wide CIs. This finding aligns with the broader literature suggesting that excessive GWG is associated with higher levels of cardiometabolic risk markers in offspring, such as BMI17,19; blood pressure18,20; and plasma levels of fasting glucose, insulin, and triglycerides.20,21 Given that GWG is routinely monitored during antenatal care and is potentially modifiable, our findings suggest that appropriate gestational weight management may be relevant to long-term cardiovascular health in the next generation. However, previous intervention studies targeting maternal lifestyle and GWG have not consistently demonstrated beneficial effects on offspring outcomes.40,41 Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence that reducing GWG during pregnancy would necessarily lower offspring CVD risk. Instead, they highlight the need for future studies with long-term follow-up to determine whether optimizing GWG during pregnancy can improve cardiovascular health in the next generation.

The underlying mechanisms of the association of maternal overweight, obesity, and excessive GWG with early-onset CVD in offspring are presumed to be similar.42 A higher BMI during early pregnancy and excessive GWG, both considered as indicators of overnutrition, may program the fetus for an increased risk of CVD.43 Fetal life is a critical period for heart development, during which the heart grows primarily through hyperplasia of cardiomyocytes, whereas after birth, heart growth is mainly due to hypertrophy.44 The fetal origins of health and disease hypotheses posit that overnutrition during pregnancy may lead to detrimental intrauterine programming, including fetal hypoxia, hyperinsulinemia, hyperglycemia, and excessive oxidative stress, which could predispose the developing fetus to cardiovascular dysfunction later in life.45,46 Exposure to an obesogenic environment has also been suggested to induce epigenetic changes that may alter the development of cardiac tissues and structures, potentially contributing to the increased risk of cardiometabolic disorders in adult offspring.47,48 However, our study did not include direct measures of maternal metabolic status, placental function, fetal cardiovascular adaptation, or molecular markers. Future studies incorporating detailed information are needed to clarify the biologic mechanism underlying these associations. The decreased risks in sibling analyses also suggest that shared genetic and environmental characteristics between mother and the offspring may, in part, account for the observed associations.42,46

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, we were unable to consider the timing of GWG because data on maternal weight were only available at the first prenatal visit and at delivery. Second, even if our study was the largest study to date, we lacked sufficient power to evaluate subgroup effects. Third, GWG values were available for only approximately half of study participants, which could have potentially led to biased effect estimates. However, estimates from multiple imputation analyses were consistent with those in the primary analyses. Fourth, although we adjusted for maternal smoking during early pregnancy, we lacked data on other maternal lifestyle factors such as diet or physical activity, leaving a possibility for residual confounding. Fifth, offspring BMI, which is associated with maternal obesity and independently associated with increased risks of CVD, was unavailable. Sixth, although the Patient Register has high validity for CVD events,25 a substantial proportion of hypertension or other milder CVDs cases may be either undiagnosed or managed in primary care settings. This may lead to an underestimation of the true associations. Seventh, although sibling comparison analyses account for unmeasured familial factors shared by siblings, they could not control for pregnancy-specific or time-varying factors that differed between siblings and were restricted to families with exposure-discordant siblings and informative outcome variation. Additionally, maternal BMI classification may also have occurred, but it was likely nondifferential with respect to offspring CVD and may have biased estimates toward the null.

Conclusions

In this Swedish cohort study of CVD risk in offspring, we found that higher maternal BMI in early pregnancy and GWG were associated with increased risks of offspring early-onset CVD. Our findings may help inform early-life intervention strategies aimed at mitigating the intergenerational transmission of CVD risk.
