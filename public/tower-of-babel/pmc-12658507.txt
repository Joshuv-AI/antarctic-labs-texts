Trends in missed paediatric preventive primary care visits during the COVID-19 pandemic using routinely collected electronic medical records in Ontario, Canada (2015–2022)
Authors: Imaan Bayoumi, Kimberley Mcfadden, Helen Valkanas, Karen Tu, Sumeet Kalia, Tao Chen, Chelsea D Christie, James Rourke, Leslie Rourke, Michelle Greiver, Denis Leduc, Patricia Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWell child visits (WCV) are fundamental to preventive primary care. We examined trends in WCV attendance during the COVID-19 pandemic and characterised variation by patient and provider characteristics.MethodsDeidentified electronic medical records from two academic practice-based research networks in Ontario were used to create age-specific cohorts of children under age six attending WCVs from 2015 to 2022. Patients’ residential postal codes were linked to neighbourhood-level measures to estimate socioeconomic status. Monthly visit rates were modelled using segmented linear regression with autoregressive residuals. Changes associated with COVID-19 were assessed using level change and trend change of monthly visit rates.FindingsFor the 53 256 included children, WCV attendance increased from 2015 to 2020 for cohorts aged 15 months and younger and was stable for 18-month, 2–3-year and 4–6-year visits. The COVID-19 pandemic was associated with decreased WCV attendance in all ages except ages 1–2 weeks, 1 month, 12 months, 15 months and 18 months, in whom attendance was unchanged. The rate of change in WCV attendance rates pre-COVID-19 compared with post-COVID-19 was unchanged, with the exception of increased rate of change for the 1–2 weeks and 2–3 years old cohorts. Lower attendance rates were observed in children residing in neighbourhoods with the highest material deprivation, rural regions and those whose family physicians were men or older than 65 years.InterpretationPrepandemic gains in WCV attendance were stable or improved after the initial reductions observed at the pandemic onset, suggesting that WCVs were prioritised by family physicians and families. Targeted strategies are needed to improve WCV attendance for vulnerable groups.Keywords: Primary Health Care, Child Health, COVID-19, Preventive Health Services

Introduction

Primary preventive care for young children is an important component of high-quality healthcare for children.1 2 Evidence-informed guidelines such as the Bright Futures guidelines from the American Academy of Pediatrics,3 the Canadian Paediatric Society and the Rourke Baby Record (www.rourkebabyrecord.ca) in Canada highlight the fundamental role of well child visits (WCV) for early detection and management of developmental delays, feeding problems and obesity, addressing food insecurity, preventing injuries and administering immunisations. Adherence to the recommended schedule of preventive primary care in young children has been associated with reduced emergency department use and reduced hospitalisations for ambulatory care sensitive conditions.4 Building on trusting relationships and frequent contact with families in the early years, primary care providers are uniquely positioned to promote health and prevent disease.

Primary care delivery was widely disrupted during the COVID-19 pandemic in many countries due to rapid changes in delivery and access, including early office closures, integration of virtual care and new infection control protocols.5,12 These changes were difficult for primary care providers and parents/caregivers to navigate because of inadequate access to personal protective equipment, patient perceptions of reduced office hours or closures and patient anxiety related to potential exposure to COVID-19.13 Early in the pandemic, reports from Canada and the USA indicated reduced provision of preventive primary care for young children, with most published literature evaluating the initial pandemic period at the height of COVID-19-related restrictions (ie, March–May 2020).5 8 10 14 Multiple studies found reduced access to WCV in spring 2020, which improved over the next year but had not yet returned to baseline by the end of 2021.7 14

Objective

We sought to examine attendance rates at WCV for children under age 6 years from March 2020 to March 2022, when many restrictions had been lifted and non-urgent health services had resumed,15 compared with prepandemic WCV attendance rates. Specific objectives of this study were to: (1) examine whether monthly and yearly attendance at WCV for children who are 6 years old or less differs between the prepandemic time period and during the COVID-19 pandemic time period; and (2) examine whether the rate of WCV differed by patient and providers’ demographic characteristics (eg, child age and sex, and provider age and sex) and patient geographical characteristics (eg, neighbourhood income, ethnic concentration, material deprivation, rurality) for each of the age-specific cohorts.

Design and setting

This retrospective cohort study used deidentified patient electronic medical record (EMR) data from family medicine practices included in two networks in Ontario, Canada: the University of Toronto Practice-Based Research Network (UTOPIAN) and the Northern Ontario School of Medicine University’s network (NORTHH). The majority of UTOPIAN practices are located in the Greater Toronto Area. Practices in the NORTHH database come from Sudbury, Thunder Bay and the surrounding area. We extracted sociodemographic characteristics (date of birth, sex and postal code), billing records and immunisation records from these databases. Each patient’s residential postal code was linked to neighbourhood-level measures to estimate socioeconomic status (SES; income, material deprivation and proportion of residents who are recent immigrants and/or from racial and ethnic minoritised groups). Neighbourhood income quintiles were measured using data from the 2016 Statistics Canada Census16 (Statistics Canada, 2017), while the material resources dimension quintiles and the recent immigrants and/or racial and ethnic minoritised populations dimension quintiles were measured using the Ontario Marginalization Index (ON Marg).17 The racialised and newcomer populations dimension of the ON Marg measures the proportion of newcomers and/or non-white, non-Indigenous populations and relates to the impacts of racialisation and xenophobia. ‘Race’ is a social construct without a biological meaning. The categorisation of people as Indigenous, black and other racial categories has been historically and is currently used to mark certain groups for exclusion, discrimination and oppression. Racialised groups include people who experience differential treatment on the basis of race, ethnicity, language, religion or culture. Disparities across this measure are the result of interpersonal and structural racism, and not the result of individual-level causal factors.17 ‘Rural postal codesOM are identifiable by the presence of a zero (0) in the second position of the FSA code. Urban postal codesOM are composed of FSAs with numerals 1–9 in the second position of the code.16 The study period included five prepandemic years (1 April 2015–31 March 2020) and 2 years of the COVID-19 pandemic (1 April 2020–31 March 2022). The beginning of the pandemic was defined based on the timing of government directives. The Ontario Ministry of Health implemented temporary new fee codes for virtual care on 14 March 2020, which was communicated to physicians on 19 March 2020. We allowed for a short adjustment period after this nearly overnight announcement and selected 1 April as a rational time to define the beginning of the pandemic period. The study end date was selected based on the date by which many restrictions had been lifted and non-urgent health services had resumed.

Outcomes

The WCV was measured using billing and immunisation records. A patient was identified as having a WCV if they had at least one of the following indicators: (1) a diagnosis code for WCV; (2) a service code for enhanced 18-month WCV or periodic health visit exam (no comparable code is available for visits at other ages, but this code was used at all ages to ensure data completeness; (3) a service code for an intermediate assessment and at least one service code for a vaccination; or (4) a service code for an intermediate assessment and at least one Anatomic Therapeutic Chemical code for a vaccination (see online supplemental file 2 for full list of codes).

Participants

We created an open cohort of children who met the following eligibility criteria: (1) were 6 years old or younger during the study period; (2) had valid sex and date of birth recorded in their EMR; (3) met minimum data quality criteria (see online supplemental file 1); and (4) were rostered to a physician or had at least two visits within the previous 3 years (to ensure they were active patients of the physician).

Age-specific cohorts

Age-specific cohorts were generated at the following milestones for the paediatric population with the same inclusion and exclusion criteria: (1) 1 and 2 weeks (combined); (2) 1 month; (3) 2 months; (4) 4 months; (5) 6 months; (6) 9 months; (7) 12 months; (8) 15 months; (9) 18 months; (10) 2 and 3 years (combined); (11) 4 and 6 years (combined). The outcome of WCV was measured within a specified age band (in days) for each age-specific cohort (see figure 1 and online supplemental file 3).

Statistical analysis

Demographic characteristics were summarised using frequencies and percentages. Monthly WCV rates were calculated from March 2015 to March 2022. Monthly visit rates were calculated by dividing the number of children who received a WCV by the number of children currently in the cohort. In a similar fashion, we calculated the fiscal year (ie, 1 April–31 March) rates of WCV from 2015 to 2022, and further stratified the results (and tested for statistical interactions) with respect to patient demographics (neighbourhood income, material deprivation, recent immigrants and/or racial and ethnic minoritised groups, and rurality) and by providers’ demographics (sex, age group). We modelled the monthly visit rate using an interrupted time series approach. We fitted segmented linear regression models with autoregressive residuals (of order two) to account for serial correlation in the percentage of WCV.18 19 We estimated the level change and trend change of monthly visit rates, comparing pre-COVID-19 to COVID-19 months, while accounting for serial dependency over time. Statistical significance was based on p values at the 0.05 level and analyses were performed using R V.4.1.1 and SAS V.9.4.

Results

Of the 625 281 patients in the UTOPIAN and NORTHH databases, 73 855 patients were aged 6 years or less at their first EMR entry; by the end of the study period, the longitudinal cohort was comprised of 53 256 children who were aged 6 years or less (figure 2). The demographic characteristics of each age cohort are summarised in table 1. Overall, the majority of patients resided in urban settings, and the cohort included approximately equal proportions of male and female children, with a higher proportion of children living in neighbourhoods with the lowest levels of material deprivation and highest levels of racial and ethnic diversity. Their primary care physicians were mostly women and approximately equally divided for all provider age groups except for the oldest group (65 years and older), which represented about 5% of the providers. Higher proportions of those not attending WCV were seen among children residing in neighbourhoods with the highest material deprivation (online supplemental file 4b), rural regions (online supplemental file 4d) and among those whose primary care physicians were women (online supplemental file 4e) or older than 65 years (online supplemental file 4f). In examining the pattern by racialised and newcomer populations, children residing in the most diverse neighbourhoods had the lowest WCV attendance rates at baseline, but their rates increased more quickly over the study period. By the end of the study period, for most cohorts, there was little variation by neighbourhood racialised and newcomer diversity (online supplemental file 4c). There was little variation observed by child sex (online supplemental file 4a).

Demographic characteristics of child age cohorts

The proportion of children attending a WCV per year increased from 2015 to 2020 for the cohorts aged 15 months and younger, while there was little change in WCV attendance at the 18-month, 2–3-year and 4–6-year visits in the pre-COVID-19 era (figure 2). The parameter estimates for the segmented linear regression modelling of per cent monthly attendance of WCV are presented in table 2. Pre-COVID-19, the slope was positive for the cohorts aged 15 months and younger, ranging from 0.06 (95% CI 0.04 to 0.08) in the 15-month-old to 0.19 (95% CI 0.15 to 0.23) in the 1-month-old cohort. The onset of the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with decreased WCV attendance in ages 2, 4, 6 and 9 months, 2–3 years and 4–6 years. There were no observed changes for children aged 1–2 weeks, 1 month, 12 months, 15 months and 18 months. The rate of change (slope change) in WCV attendance rates pre-COVID-19 compared with post-COVID-19 was not different except for the 1–2 weeks and 2–3 years old cohorts, which increased compared with prepandemic estimates.

Discussion

We found important trends in WCV attendance for young children currently affiliated to a primary care provider in Ontario, Canada during the COVID-19 pandemic. Prior to the onset of the pandemic, attendance rates were increasing for all younger age groups from ages 1 week to 15 months. The reason for this trend is unclear; however, it follows government-led education of clinicians and parents on the importance of preventive care for developmental surveillance,20 which may have helped raise awareness. The onset of the pandemic in March 2020 was associated with decreased WCV in six of the 11 age-specific cohorts. After the initial change at the onset of the pandemic, there were no significant changes in the rate of change of attendance from the prepandemic rates for any age group with the exception of 1–2 weeks and 2–3 years, which both increased from prepandemic estimates. Overall, prepandemic gains observed in attendance were stable or improved after the initial reductions observed at the onset of the pandemic.

Our finding of reduced WCV at the onset of the pandemic, followed by increased visit rates, is consistent with previous reports.714 21,23 We extend this work in a number of important ways. First, our analysis extends the study period to March 2022, whereas previous reports have examined trends up to September 2021. Our analysis accounted for pre-COVID-19 trends in annual attendance rates, which differ from most previously published reports which compared COVID-19 era counts with mean pre-COVID-19 era annual counts without accounting for pre-COVID-19 temporal trends.7 14 Salas et al used joinpoint regression to examine linear trends, with the former analysis beginning in 2018,21 and found that visits were unchanged over the study period for children less than 1 year old, while those aged 1–4 years dropped in March 2020 and rebounded to pre-COVID-19 rates by July 2020. Finally, we examined age groups consistent with recommended WCV schedules, whereas others have examined broader age groups.

It is noteworthy that at the end of the study period, estimated attendance rates for children aged 12 months and younger remain in the 40–65% range and estimated attendance rates for those over 2 years ranged from 12% to 15%. Our study did not assess immunisation rates. However, the population seeking immunisations in alternate settings (such as public health venues) is likely small, given the limited availability of these services during the pandemic and that our cohort was comprised of children with a regular family physician. Attendance is also consistently lower for children residing in lower-income neighbourhoods, or in rural settings, and for those whose primary care physicians are men or older. Importantly, changes in visit rates could be either parent or clinician driven. Our team,24 as well as others25 have studied strategies that were implemented during the pandemic to maintain or improve WCV attendance, especially for populations experiencing barriers to accessing care. WCVs were prioritised generally and for in-person visits in particular. Redesign of clinic space to enable social distancing helped reduce parental concerns about risk of exposure to COVID-19 during a visit. Clinic changes in communication strategies such as email or text message notification may also have been helpful.24 In addition, Garg et al reported that system-wide changes to streamline clinician requests for access to transportation for Medicaid patients and tip sheets for providers on accessing interpretation services were associated with improved attendance rates for all groups and narrowing of disparities based on race and ethnicity.25

Strengths of our study include access to a large database within a primary care practice-based research network. Data on visit frequency is largely complete within the dataset. Our study has some limitations. Our results may have limited generalisability. The study participants mostly resided in a large, diverse metropolitan area, and those residing in other parts of Ontario and specifically in rural settings were underrepresented. In addition, our results are limited to children with a consistent source of primary care. Families lacking attachment to a primary care provider or losing attachment during the pandemic may have had lower WCV attendance rates.

Conclusion

Prepandemic gains observed in WCV attendance were stable or improved after the initial reductions observed at the onset of the pandemic, suggesting that WCVs were prioritised by primary care clinicians and families during the COVID-19 pandemic. Additional research is needed to characterise barriers to WCV attendance for vulnerable groups and subsequently to develop targeted strategies to improve WCV attendance.
