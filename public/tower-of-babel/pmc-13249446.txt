Temperature exposure and time adolescents spend in physical activity across intensity levels
Authors: Esmée Essers, Maria Llopis-Cidad, Sarah Koch, Joren Buekers, Alícia Josa-Culleré, Sami Petricola, Ainhoa Bereziartua, Paula Carrasco, Alba Jimeno-Romero, Martine Vrijheid, Henning Tiemeier, Hanan El Marroun, Judith Garcia-Aymerich, Carmen Iñiguez, Mònica Guxens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Environmental factors such as temperature may influence how much time adolescents spend being active, but evidence remains limited. We aimed to evaluate the relationship between same-day temperature exposure and accelerometer-measured time spent in physical activity at different intensities in adolescents in two European cohorts.Methods:We used data from 1316 Dutch adolescents (mean age [standard deviation]: 12.7 [1.5] years, Generation R Study, 2015–2018) and 531 Spanish adolescents (13.6 [2.3] years, INMA Project, 2017–2022). Ambient residential-level daily temperature at 100 × 100-m resolution was estimated using the UrbClim model. Daily light, moderate, and vigorous physical activity duration was measured with wrist-worn accelerometers up to nine consecutive days. Cohort-specific distributed lag mixed-effect models assessed same-day temperature effects on physical activity, adjusting for temperatures in the two previous days.Results:Adolescents spend more time in light and moderate physical activity on warmer days. In Generation R, light activity increased by up to 10.9 (95% confidence interval = 4.7, 17.0) minutes and moderate activity by 4.8 (1.2; 8.4) minutes at the 95th versus 5th percentile (21.5 vs. 2.3ºC). In INMA, increases were 16.3 (3.8, 28.9) and 14.2 (6.1, 22.4) minutes, respectively (25.1 vs. 7.7ºC). No associations were observed for vigorous activity.Conclusion:Warmer temperatures were associated with more time spent in light and moderate, but not vigorous, physical activity in Dutch and Spanish adolescents. These findings highlight the importance of considering ambient temperature when designing activity interventions, particularly in the context of climate change.

What this study adds

This study provides novel evidence on how ambient temperature shapes physical activity behaviors in adolescents across different intensity levels and climatic contexts. Using repeated accelerometer measurements from two population-based cohorts and linking them to high-resolution temperature exposure data, we found that warmer temperatures were associated with increased light and moderate activity, but not vigorous activity. By accounting for lagged temperature and including data from both temperate (the Netherlands) and Mediterranean (Spain) climates, this work advances understanding of how climatic factors influence youth behavior, an essential consideration for assessing the health implications of climate change.

Introduction

Physical activity during youth is important for establishing lifelong movement habits and promoting cardiometabolic, psychological, and developmental health.1,2 However, over 80% of adolescents globally are insufficiently active.2 The World Health Organization recommends at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity daily for youth age 5–17 years.3 Yet, many adolescents fall short of this standard. Only 28% to 41% of Dutch adolescents (ages 12–18) met activity guidelines recommendations between 2014 and 2023, while just 37% of Spanish adolescents (ages 8–16) were sufficiently active in 2019.4,5

Global warming, driven by human activity, has raised global land surface temperatures by about 1.6 ºC in 2011–2020 compared with the 1850–1900 baseline.6 Recent years have brought record-breaking temperatures and unpredictable heat events, underscoring the need to understand their impacts, including on human health.7,8 Climate change has been linked to adverse health outcomes, including increased mortality, cardiovascular and respiratory morbidity, and poor mental and health-related lifestyle behavior such as physical health.9–13 Ambient temperature may play a role in shaping physical activity behavior.12–14 Seasonal trends consistently show increased physical activity for all ages during warmer months across global regions.13–17 A meta-analysis of six studies in youth (ages 8–17) found that warmer ambient temperatures were associated with more accelerometer-measured moderate-to-vigorous physical activity.14 However, studies also suggest heat thresholds beyond which physical activity duration declines.18–20 For example, moderate-to-vigorous physical activity duration peaked between 20–25ºC in Australian and Canadian children age 8–13 years.18,19 Similarly, a multinational study in children across 12 countries found that activity (counts per minute) increased up to 20°C, then declined.20 Heat stress, a combination of high temperature, humidity, and solar radiation that can lead to heat-related illness and physiological strain during outdoor activity, may be a contributing factor.21 In 2023, global exposure to hours of heat stress risk for light outdoor physical activity reached an all-time high, nearly 28% higher than in the 1990s.21 Exercise in hot conditions increases cardiovascular and thermoregulatory burden.22 Children and adolescents are particularly vulnerable to these effects due to their developing physiology and behavioral patterns.8,23 Specifically, they have inefficient sweating and reduced heat dissipation, placing them at elevated risk during exertion in hot environments.22

Despite the potential importance of ambient temperature for youth physical activity, few studies have assessed how it affects different intensity levels of activity (light, moderate, and vigorous) separately.14,15 In addition, most research has not adjusted for the effects of temperature exposure from previous days.14,15,24 Finally, these relationships may vary by climatic region. The aim of this study was therefore to evaluate the relationship between same-day ambient temperature exposure and time spent in accelerometer-measured light, moderate, and vigorous physical activity in adolescents from cohort studies in the Netherlands and Spain. The Netherlands has a temperate oceanic climate with mild summers, while Spain has a warmer, Mediterranean climate.25

Methods

Study design and participants

This longitudinal study used data from two ongoing European population-based birth cohort studies: The Generation R Study, based in Rotterdam, the Netherlands, and the INfancia y Medio Ambiente (INMA) Project, based in various regions in Spain.26,27 These cohorts recruited a total of 9898 (Generation R), 638 (INMA-Gipuzkoa), 777 (INMA-Sabadell), and 855 (INMA-Valencia) women during their pregnancy or shortly after giving birth. Births occurred between 2002 and 2006 for Generation R, and between 2003 and 2008 for INMA. For this study, we included adolescents who were singleton births and had at least 1 day of valid physical activity data (measured as daily physical activity in minutes) and corresponding daily mean ambient temperature data in °C (Figure S1; https://links.lww.com/EE/A431). In the Generation R Study and due to logistical reasons, physical activity data collection was done in two independent subgroups with average ages of approximately 12 (range 10.3–12.1) and 15 (13.0–15.6) years. In the INMA cohort, age at data collection varied by site. In Gipuzkoa, data were collected at around 11 years (10.3 to 11.3), in Sabadell at both 11 (9.8 to 12.1) and 15 (13.2 to 17.5) years (only the 15-year measurement was used if adolescents were included in both waves), and in Valencia at 15 (14.7–15.9) years (Figure S2; https://links.lww.com/EE/A431). Analysis samples finally consisted of a total of 1316 participants from Generation R and 531 from the three INMA regions. In both cohorts, ethical approval was granted (for Generation R by the Medical Ethical Committee of Erasmus University Medical Center Rotterdam, and for INMA by the Ethical Committees of the hospitals involved), and informed consent was obtained from adolescents and their parents.

Ambient temperature

Ambient air temperature for both cohorts was estimated with the physics-based urban climate model UrbClim, developed by VITO Belgium, within a spatial resolution of 100 × 100 m.28 VITO modeled hourly temperature data across the years of outcome assessment (2015–2018 for Generation R, 2018–2019 for INMA-Gipuzkoa, 2017 and 2020–2022 for INMA-Sabadell, and 2019–2021 for INMA-Valencia). More detailed methodology regarding UrbClim can be found in Methods S1; https://links.lww.com/EE/A431. Subsequently, hourly data were averaged to a mean 24-hour level and coupled to the participants’ geocoded addresses, accounting for any address changes, to obtain daily residential-level data. For this study, we used daily residential-level ambient temperature data on the day of the physical activity measurement as the main exposure and the temperature during the 2 days prior as adjustment variables.

Physical activity

Time spent in physical activity was assessed using the GeneActiv wrist-worn triaxial accelerometer (Activinsights Ltd., Kimbolton, United Kingdom). While accelerometers provide objective measures of movement intensity and duration, they cannot distinguish the context or type of physical activity (e.g., leisure, commuting, and organized sports) nor whether activity occurred indoors or outdoors. Participants were asked to wear the device continuously on their nondominant wrist for up to nine consecutive days, including during sleep. Participants were informed that the device was waterproof and could be worn while showering, but should be removed for prolonged water exposure (e.g., swimming or bathing), and they were explicitly instructed to keep the device on during sports except when engaging in activities that posed a safety risk to themselves or others (e.g., karate). If removal during sports was necessary, participants were asked to report daily whether this occurred. Accelerometer data were processed using the GGIR package (version 3.0.6)29 in R, where the raw triaxial acceleration data were auto-calibrated using built-in procedures, and nonwear time was detected using a validated algorithm based on sustained periods of low acceleration.29–31 A valid physical activity measurement day was defined as ≥10 hours of accelerometer wear time during waking hours.32 For each valid physical activity measurement day, physical activity metrics were derived from the Euclidean Norm Minus One in 5-second epochs. Since age-specific Euclidean Norm Minus One cut-points for physical activity intensities using the GeneActiv on the nondominant wrist are not available for the age ranges in this study, we estimated cut-points using data for children age 7–11 years and adults age 21–61 years.33,34 Cut-points were linearly interpolated between 11 and 21 years of age to derive age-specific cut-points for light, moderate, and vigorous intensity physical activity for each participant based on their age at the physical activity measurement (Table S1; https://links.lww.com/EE/A431). After data processing through GGIR, daily measurements with >21 hours of waking time (i.e., <3 hours of sleep, see our previous work for a detailed description of how waking time is estimated)35 were assumed erroneous and excluded, and those with activity durations >360 minutes were winsorized at 360 minutes to reduce the influence of outliers.36

Potential confounding variables

We created a directed acyclic graph using a priori knowledge of potential confounding variables based on previous research and data availability in Generation R and INMA (Figure S3; https://links.lww.com/EE/A431).15,24 Information on population characteristics was collected through questionnaires administered at enrollment and during follow-up assessments in childhood. We used the information closest to the outcome assessment. Covariates included parental characteristics: age at enrollment (in years), country of origin (categorized as the Netherlands (Generation R) or Spain (INMA), other European countries, and other non-European countries), education level (low, medium, or high), and employment status (paid job or unpaid job); marital status was categorized as either dual parent (married, unmarried but living together, or registered partnership) or single parent (single and not cohabiting, living alone, or widowed); and the number of children under 18 years living in the home (1, 2, or 3+). In Generation R only, monthly net household income was collected and categorized into four levels (low, low-medium, medium-high, and high). To account for changes in income distributions over time, the thresholds for these categories differed between the 12- and 15-year waves. For the 12-year wave, the categories were defined as follows: low (<€2000), low-medium (€2000–€3200), medium-high (€3200–€4400), and high (>€4400). For the 15-year wave, they were: low (<€2000), low-medium (€2000–€3600), medium-high (€3600–€6000), and high (>€6000). Additional variables were included: adolescent biological sex-assigned-at-birth (female or male), and the month of and age (in years) at outcome assessment. Finally, residential characteristics were assessed based on two indicators at the time of outcome measurement: greenness around the home and neighborhood socioeconomic status. Greenness exposure was quantified using the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index within a 300-m buffer, derived from Landsat satellite imagery. This index captures vegetation density by measuring the contrast in light reflectance, particularly the difference between red and near-infrared wavelengths, producing values from −1 to 1, where higher values indicate greater vegetation cover.37 Socioeconomic status at the neighborhood level was evaluated differently across cohorts: in Generation R, data from Statistics Netherlands were used to characterize postal code areas based on average wealth, educational attainment, and labor market participation of residents; in INMA, a deprivation index for 2011 was applied, which integrates six socioeconomic indicators: percentage of manual workers, temporary workers, unemployment, population with insufficient education, young people (age 16–29) with insufficient education, and dwellings without internet access.38,39

Statistical analysis

All analyses were done separately for Generation R and INMA. Due to significant differences between included and excluded participants (Table S2; https://links.lww.com/EE/A431), we applied inverse probability weighting to correct for potential attrition bias. Individual weights were estimated with the covariate balancing propensity score method, which calculates the inverse probability of study inclusion while optimizing covariate balance across groups, improving the robustness of causal inferences.40 Variables used to predict study inclusion and the distribution of final weights are presented in Table S3; https://links.lww.com/EE/A431 and Figure S3; https://links.lww.com/EE/A431. To address missing data of potential confounding variables, we used an expectation-maximization algorithm to generate a single imputed dataset (as the main statistical model cannot handle multiple imputed datasets) for the analysis samples of each cohort/region (Figure S1; https://links.lww.com/EE/A431).41 The proportion of missing data across potential confounding variables was below 30%. Comparisons between the original and imputed datasets revealed similar distributions, indicating that the imputation process preserved the overall data structure. Further details on the expectation-maximization imputation process are available in Table S4; https://links.lww.com/EE/A431.

To examine the association between daily ambient temperature and physical activity duration for each intensity separately (i.e., light, moderate, and vigorous activity) on the same day, we used participant-day as the unit of analysis and applied cohort-specific distributed lag mixed-effect models to consider the repeated nature of the data and to adjust the models for the temperature levels 2 days before the index day. In these models, each observation corresponds to a single participant-day, with participants contributing between one and nine valid days of measurement. Random intercepts were included for each participant (in both cohorts) and for region (in the INMA cohort) to account for within-region correlation. Residual autocorrelation over time was addressed by specifying a continuous-time exponential correlation structure for participant-level repeated measurements. To determine the appropriate model parameterization of the exposure-response and lag-response relationships, we first explored the association between same-day temperature exposure and physical activity using generalized additive models. Visual inspection of the resulting curves suggested a linear relationship, which we adopted in the final models. The reference temperature for interpreting results was set at the 5th percentile of the cohort-specific temperature distribution (i.e., 2.3ºC for Generation R and 7.7ºC for INMA). We estimated the effect of exposure to various temperature percentiles (defined as the 6th to 95th percentiles specific to each cohort, corresponding to 2.7 to 21.5ºC in Generation R and 8.1 to 25.1ºC in INMA) on same-day light, moderate, and vigorous physical activity levels. Estimates represent the model-based difference in expected minutes of physical activity at each temperature percentile (displayed as the corresponding temperature in ºC) compared with the reference temperature, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Consequently, positive estimates indicate higher and negative estimates indicate lower expected activity levels relative to the reference temperature. All models were adjusted for previously mentioned potential confounding variables. The month of physical activity measurement was included to account for seasonality.

Using participant-reported information on whether they removed the accelerometer due to sports per day (2% of physical activity days for Generation R and 15% of days for INMA), we ran a sensitivity analysis to account for potential misclassification of the outcome. Thus, we re-ran the distributed lag mixed-effect model for all physical activity intensities, adding 30 minutes onto light, 20 minutes onto moderate, and 10 minutes onto vigorous physical activity for the days the accelerometer was removed due to sports.

Participants generally had mothers with a high level of education (68% in Generation R and 38% in INMA) and a paid job (85% in both cohorts). For most participants, the mothers’ country of origin was that of the cohort (i.e., 81% the Netherlands and 94% Spain). The 1316 Generation R participants yielded 11,316 participant-days (96% of participants had at least 7 days of measurements), and the 531 INMA participants yielded 3428 participant-days (58% of participants had at least 7 days) (Table S6; https://links.lww.com/EE/A431). The mean non-wear of the accelerometers was 2.4% (median of 26, range 0.1–158 minutes) in Generation R and 2.3% (median of 26, range 3–167 minutes) in INMA. Most participants had their assessments in the spring for Generation R (35%) and autumn for INMA (32%). Generation R measurements covered the period from August 31, 2017, to July 7, 2018, while INMA covered the period from January 17, 2017, to July 19, 2022 (Figure S4; https://links.lww.com/EE/A431). During the physical activity measurement days, the mean (standard deviation and range) ambient temperature was 11.3ºC (6.1, −2.8 to 28.0ºC) in the Generation R cohort and 14.7ºC (5.2, 3.2 to 31.5ºC) in the INMA cohort (Figure 1). The median daily time spent in light, moderate, and vigorous activity in Generation R was 183 (interquartile range 158–211), 57 (43–74), and 7 (4–13) minutes, respectively, and 177 (144–210), 57 (42–76), and 5 (2–11) minutes, respectively, in INMA (Figure S5; https://links.lww.com/EE/A431).

Exposure to warmer temperatures (from the 6th to the 95th percentile) compared with the reference temperature (5th percentile) was associated with higher levels of light and moderate physical activity levels in both cohorts on the same day. For example, in Generation R, exposure to an ambient temperature of 21.5 versus 2.3ºC (percentile 95 vs. 5) was associated with an increase of 10.9 (95% CI = 4.7, 17.0) minutes in light physical activity and 4.8 (95% CI = 1.2, 8.4) minutes in moderate activity (Figure 2 and Table S7 https://links.lww.com/EE/A431). In the INMA cohort, exposure to 25.1 versus 7.7ºC (percentile 95 vs. 5) was associated with increases of 16.3 (95% CI = 3.8, 28.9) minutes in light physical activity and 14.2 (95% CI = 6.1, 22.4) minutes in moderate activity. There were no accompanying changes in vigorous physical activity in Generation R or INMA. Sensitivity analyses adjusting the time spent in light, moderate, or vigorous physical activity based on participant-recorded information stating whether the accelerometer was removed due to sports, revealed similar associations for both cohorts (Figure S6; https://links.lww.com/EE/A431).

Discussion

In this cohort study of adolescents from two European birth cohorts, exposure to higher ambient temperatures was associated with increases in time spent in light and moderate physical activity on the same day. This increase was more pronounced in the INMA cohort compared with Generation R. No associations were observed for vigorous physical activity.

Previous studies in children and adolescents from various countries, evaluating mainly moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, but in some also light physical activity, found same-day warmer temperatures or warmer seasons (spring and summer) associated with increased duration.13–15,19 Interestingly, some studies reported a non-linear relationship between temperature and moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, with declines beyond certain thresholds (e.g., 20–25ºC in Australian and Canadian children ages 8–13).19,42 In our study, we observed that time spent in activity intensities requiring less exertion (i.e., light to moderate) gradually increased with warmer temperatures (up to 22ºC in Generation R and to 25ºC in INMA), with no evidence of increases in vigorous activity. Contrary to some previous studies, we did not observe a temperature threshold after which physical activity declines. An explanation could be that our temperature range, moderate in both cohorts, did not reach levels high enough to trigger reductions in time spent in physical activity. Nonetheless, our findings suggest that while warmer temperatures may promote light and moderate movement, this does not extend to higher-intensity activity in these populations. The lack of association for vigorous physical activity could either reflect thresholds for physical activity intensity or indicate that individuals engaging in vigorous activity do so regardless of the temperature (e.g., organized sports). Both behavioral and physiological mechanisms likely underlie the observed associations. Warmer weather generally supports increased outdoor time, which encourages walking, cycling, and other light- to moderate-intensity activities. These forms of activity are accessible, comfortable, and socially reinforced during milder warmer weather.13 From a biological perspective, the thermoregulatory demands of light or moderate activity are relatively low, allowing individuals to remain active even as temperatures rise.22

Adolescents’ sensitivity to heat, particularly during physical activity, is influenced by puberty- and sex-related hormonal and metabolic changes that affect thermoregulation.43 These developmental changes can alter how adolescents perceive and respond to heat stress, making certain physical activities more challenging under high temperatures. Due to limited sample, we did not stratify our analyses by biological sex-assigned at birth. However, future research investigating sex-differences that integrate physiological changes due to puberty on how heat affects adolescent physical activity is needed. While our findings do not directly show declines in physical activity due to heat, identifying how temperature influences activity patterns is essential for anticipating potential barriers. According to the World Health Organization, children and adolescents should engage in at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity daily.3 Although combined mean daily moderate and vigorous activity levels in both cohorts met this guideline, only about half of the observed participant-days actually achieved it. This underscores the relevance of environmental conditions in supporting (or suppressing) daily activity goals. The observed increases, in Generation R up to 11 minutes more in light and 5 minutes in moderate physical activity and in INMA up to 18 and 14 minutes, represent meaningful contributions towards daily physical activity targets. Ensuring equitable access to temperature-adaptive environments and resources will be crucial for supporting active lifestyles among all adolescents. Future studies should build on this work by integrating detailed physical activity information obtained through questionnaire (e.g., type of physical activity, changes in behavior, and self-perceived strain), while also collecting accelerometer data over longer durations (i.e., across all seasons) to better capture behavioral variation throughout the year. Incorporating contextual and spatial information, such as location tracking, indoor versus outdoor setting, and whether activities are structured versus unstructured, would help disentangle behavioral and physiological mechanisms.

This study has several strengths. It draws on data from two large, population-based birth cohorts in climatically and culturally distinct European regions, enhancing exposure contrast and ecological validity. Device-measured physical activity via accelerometers minimized recall bias and enabled the capture of fine-grained daily activity, with high compliance. High-resolution temperature estimates at residential addresses improved exposure precision. The repeated-measures design allowed us to model participant-day-level associations while accounting for within-subject correlation. Finally, applying age-specific thresholds for classifying physical activity intensities ensures that activity duration levels were interpreted in an age-appropriate manner.

However, several limitations should be considered. One key limitation is the uneven seasonal distribution of physical activity measurements, with limited data collected during the hottest summer months.24 This may have reduced our ability to detect threshold effects at higher temperatures. While exposure to extreme heat may be underrepresented, children’s and adolescents’ physical activity patterns are likely more habitual during the school year and thus reflect more representative activity patterns. In contrast, summer routines may differ due to factors such as the suspension of organized sports. Although we ran a sensitivity analysis updating the different intensity durations based on participant-reported days when the watch was removed, we lacked detailed information on exact timing or reasons for removal, particularly in Generation R. This could introduce measurement error, potentially differential if device removal was associated with temperature or activity type (e.g., more swimming on hotter days). Additionally, intrinsic motivation of the individual could not be considered, some adolescents might have been motivated to always participate in physical activity, regardless of the weather. While temperature exposure was assigned based on residential address at high spatial resolution, individual-level exposure may vary during the day due to travel and microclimate differences. While emerging wearable technologies (e.g., body-worn temperature sensors or GPS) could address this in future studies, they were not available in our cohorts. We also did not include precipitation as a covariate in the main models due to the low spatial resolution (10 × 10 km grids) of available data. Finally, research has shown a well-established relationship between nutritional status and physical activity levels.44,45 We did not adjust for diet directly, as it may be influenced by temperature and thus act as a potential mediator,46,47 but our models include socioeconomic characteristics (e.g., parental education and household income) that may partially capture related variation in dietary behaviors.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that in adolescents from Dutch and Spanish cohorts, same-day warmer temperatures are associated with increased durations of light and moderate physical activity. Vigorous physical activity was not associated with ambient temperature. While weather itself is not directly modifiable, it can shape how much and at what intensity youth engage in physical activity and may influence the effectiveness of behavioral interventions. In the context of climate change, understanding the temperature conditions that support active lifestyles is increasingly important, particularly as future extremes may pose new challenges for maintaining regular activity.
