Association between carotene intake and hospital-acquired pneumonia in elderly men: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jun He, Boyang Wei, Xue-Geng Hong, Yan Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElderly inpatients are at high risk for hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP). The association between dietary carotene and HAP remains unclear.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 165 patients aged ≥ 80 years. Dietary carotene intake was assessed via 72-hour recall and food weighing, analyzed using the China Food Composition Table. Based on tertiles of dietary carotene intake, patients were stratified into low- (T1, < 713.3 µg/day; n = 55), medium- (T2, 713.4–2101.4 µg/day; n = 54), and high-intake groups (T3, ≥ 2101.6 µg/day; n = 56). Multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic splines, threshold analysis and subgroup analyses were used to evaluate the relationship between dietary carotene intake and HAP.ResultsA total of 165 hospitalized senior patients aged 80 years or older were included, with an average age of 89.7 ± 9.8 years. Of these, 30.9% (51/165) had HAP. After adjusting for confounding factors (age, BMI, co-morbidities, energy intake, etc.), the risk of pneumonia in the moderate dietary carotene intake group (T2) was 80% lower than that in the lowest dietary carotene intake group (T1) (OR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.06–0.71). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a nonlinear association between carotene intake and HAP risk (P for nonlinearity = 0.003). The inflection point analysis indicated a significant reduction in pneumonia risk with increasing carotene intake below 1273.7 µg/d. Beyond this threshold, however, the dose-response curve plateaued, suggesting no additional benefit from higher intake. Subgroup analyses (by diabetes, CKD, nutrition risk) showed consistent results.ConclusionsIncreased dietary carotenoids intake was inversely associated with the prevalence of HAP, demonstrating a dose-response relationship below a threshold of 1273.7 µg/d.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03237-8.Keywords: Carotene, Hospital-acquired pneumonia, The elderly, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Pneumonia refers to end-stage inflammation of the airways, alveoli, and lung interstitial, which can be caused by disease microorganisms, physical and chemical factors, immune damage, allergies, and medications. Bacterial pneumonia is the most frequent type of pneumonia and one of the most prevalent infectious diseases. Lower respiratory tract infections and pneumonia are the two leading causes of viral respiratory tract infections (RTI), which cause more than 4 million deaths globally each year [1]. The immune system is closely associated with inflammation and clinical outcomes, and multiple micronutrients have been shown to play important roles in immune cell proliferation and maturation, cytokine release, and immune cell activity for antioxidant host defense [2]. Nutrient deficits can decrease host immunity, increasing vulnerability to infection [3]. Therefore, dietary nutrients play an important role in maintaining the immune system and inhibiting inflammation. Carotene a phytochemical that occurs naturally in plants, including fruits and vegetables, β-carotene is the most active form of carotenoids. β-carotene have beneficial immune-modulating effects that occur within the innate immune system by priming it to face microbial challenges and by mitigating the inflammatory response [4]. A review of epidemiological, preclinical, and clinical studies demonstrated that carotene intake is related to pathophysiological conditions such as insulin resistance and inflammation. It also has beneficial effects on obesity [5]. Dietary carotene intake is closely related to bone health and the risk of osteoporotic fractures [6]. Studies also have shown antioxidant nutrients such as carotenoids, have been associated with lower rates of COPD [7]. β-carotene have beneficial immune-modulating effects that occur within the innate immune system by priming it to face microbial challenges and by mitigating the inflammatory response [4]. However, whether carotene intake levels are associated with pneumonia is controversial [8–10]. Therefore, we investigated the relationship between dietary carotene consumption and HAP in elderly men patient. This study was limited by hospital departments and only collected elderly male cases as research samples. In addition, the dose-response relationship between dietary carotene consumption and HAP was also assessed.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study included consecutive patients with chronic diseases aged ≥ 80years who were hospitalized at the 960th hospital of the Joint Logistics Support Force of the People’s Liberation Army (Shandong Province, China) between June 1, 2023 and March 31, 2024. Participants without carotene intake data in the first 72 h after admission were excluded. Admissions due to community-acquired pneumonia or other inflammatory conditions were also excluded. One hundred and sixty-five eligible participants with complete data were ultimately included in the analysis.

The study followed to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Review Board of the 960th hospital of the Joint Logistics Support Force of the People’s Liberation Army. Participants or their authorized agent provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Data collection and measurements

Chronic diseases, physical examination and laboratory results were collected from the electronic medical record system. Data on dietary intake was collected by a professional dietitian in the first 72 h after admission to the hospital, including total energy, protein, fat, carbohydrate, insoluble dietary fiber, Total vitamin A, Vitamin E, Retinol. A, Thiamine.B1, Riboflavin.B2, Niacin, VC and Purine intake. Baseline examinations included aspartate aminotransferase (AST), r-glutamyl transferase (r-GT), albumin (ALB), total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), indirect bilirubin (IBIL), uric acid (UA), potassium (K+), sodium (Na+), choride (Cl−), calcium (Ca2+), magnesium (Mg2+), glucose (Glu), creatinine (CRE) and Blood urea nitrogen (BUN), white blood cell count(WBC) AND C-reactive protein (CRP). All laboratory data were obtained from the initial examination of hospitalized patients tested within 48 h of admission. Moreover, systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure were obtained from three consecutive blood pressure measurements. Furthermore, chronic kidney disease (CKD), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), coronary heart disease and Cerebral infarction were diagnosed according to the doctor’s diagnosis in electronic medical records.

Dietary carotene intake

Dietary intake was assessed within the first 72 h of admission through one-on-one interviews conducted by trained nutritionists using a 72-hour dietary recall method. The carotene and other nutrient contents of the reported food items were determined according to the China Food Composition Table (6th Edition, Vol. 1.2) [11]. Nutrient intake for each participant was calculated using the formula: (consumed food weight / 100) × nutrient content per 100 g of food (as listed in the composition table). The data were compiled and preliminarily analyzed using Microsoft Excel and subsequently verified by two independent nutritionists. Final data aggregation and statistical analysis were conducted using a validated, in-house nutritional analysis system developed based on the China Food Composition Table, with all calculations undergoing a rigorous two-nutritionist verification protocol.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was HAP occurring more than 48 h after hospital admission.

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables are expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD) for normal distributions or median and interquartile range (IQR) for skewed distributions. We used the chi-square test, one-way ANOVA, and Kruskal-Wallis test for the comparison of categorical, normally distributed, and nonnormally distributed continuous variables, respectively.

Patients were divided into three groups based on tertiles of dietary carotene intake: a low-intake group (T1, < 713.3 µg/d, n = 55), a medium-intake group (T2, 713.4–2101.5 µg/d, n = 54), and a high-intake group (T3, ≥ 2101.6 µg/d, n = 56). To investigate the association between dietary carotene intake and HAP, we performed logistic regression analyses with carotene intake included both as a continuous variable (per-SD increase) and as a categorical variable (tertiles). In all models, the lowest tertile (T1) was used as the reference category. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Variables were selected as adjusted if the matched odds ratio changed by at least 10%. Model 1 was a model that was crude and had no covariates adjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age and BMI. Model 3 adjusted energy and vitamin A based on model 2, while Model 4 adjusted CRP, COPD based on model 3.

A restricted cubic spline model was created to establish OR curves at 3 knots to examine the potential nonlinear dose response relationship between dietary carotene intake levels and HAP. To further characterize the nonlinear association identified by the restricted cubic spline model, a two-piecewise linear regression model was applied to calculate the threshold (inflection) point where the relationship between carotene intake and pneumonia risk changed. The inflection point was determined using an iterative method that identified the point providing the best fit based on the maximum likelihood model.

A post-hoc power analysis was conducted using PASS software (version 2021, NCSS, LLC) to determine the statistical power of the study to detect the observed effect between groups T1 and T2. Given a total sample size of 165, a baseline event rate of 45.45% in group T1, and an observed odds ratio of 0.20, the analysis demonstrated a high statistical power of 0.98 at a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. Subgroup analyses were performed stratifying by diabetes status, CKD status, and nutritional risk (NRS2002 ≥ 3 vs. <3). Interaction was formally tested using the likelihood ratio test.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 165 subjects was included in this study, aged 80 years and above, with an average age of 89.7 ± 9.8 years, of whom 100% were male, and a total of 51 (30.9%) developed pneumonia. The baseline characteristics of the 165 study participants are presented in Table 1 based on their carotene intake tertiles. The level of carotene intake was lower in patients with Nutritional Risks (NRS2002 ≥ 3 points) than those without Nutritional Risks (NRS2002 < 3 points) (55 [33%] versus 110 [66.7%], respectively), and in T1 group compared with T2 and T3 groups with Nutritional Risks (34 [61.8%] versus 10 [18.5] and 11 [19.6%], respectively). Compared with T2 and T3 groups, carotene intake was also lower in T1 group patients with enteral nutrition (EN) alone and EN combined with Parenteral nutrition (PN), especially in patients on enteral tube feeding. Patients who eat orally had a higher intake of carotene. Retinol. A and purine intake levels and plasma albumin level were also lower in the T1 group compared to the T2 and T3 groups. A highly significant difference was observed in hemoglobin (P < 0.001). T1group had notably lower hemoglobin (106.8 g/L) compared to T2group (120.4 g/L) and T3group (117.5 g/L). No significant differences were observed in inflammatory markers (WBC, neutrophil and lymphocyte percentages, absolute neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, platelets, CRP) across the groups (all P > 0.05). The prevalence of pneumonia was lowest in the T2 group and highest in the T1 group, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05).

Association between carotene intake level and the prevalence of pneumonia

When carotene intake level was analyzed as a continuous variable, A high carotene intake level was not associated with a decreased prevalence of HAP (OR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.58–1.39; P = 0.631), after adjusting for age, BMI, energy, fat, protein, carbohydrates, blood glucose, diabetes, COPD, coronary heart disease and CKD. However, while the association was statistically significant when the carotene intake level was transformed into a categorical variable as tertiles. Compared with individuals in the lowest tertile (T1) of carotene intake (< 713.3 µg/d), those in the medium tertile (T2: 713.4–2101.5 µg/d) had significantly lower odds of HAP (OR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.06–0.71; P = 0.012). No significant association was observed for the highest intake group (T3: ≥2101.6 µg/d) (OR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.16–1.59; P = 0.246), as presented in Table 2.

The nonlinearity relationship between carotene intake levels and the prevalence of HAP

The nonlinear association between carotene intake and the prevalence of HAP was examined using a restricted cubic spline model, which revealed a significant nonlinear relationship (P = 0.003; Fig. 1). Based on a two-piecewise linear regression model, an inflection point was identified at 1273.7 µg/d. Below this threshold, carotene intake was inversely associated with the prevalence of HAP (OR = 0.998, 95% CI: 0.996–0.999; P = 0.012). Subgroup analyses and interaction tests

Subgroup analyses were performed to assess the consistency of the association between carotene intake and HAP risk. The inverse association was consistently observed across all subgroups—stratified by diabetes status, CKD status, and nutritional risk (NRS2002 ≥ 3 vs. <3). Interaction tests were not significant (all p-interaction > 0.05), suggesting that the protective effect of carotene is independent of these factors and that the primary finding is both stable and generalizable across these patient characteristics (Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

Our study revealed that participants in the medium intake group (713.4 ≤ T2 < 2101.5 µg/d) had a 88% lower risk of HAP compared to those in the low intake group (T1 < 713.3 µg/d) (OR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.06–0.71; P = 0.012). A non-linear association was observed between carotene intake and the risk of HAP. Inflection point analysis indicated that carotene intake below 1273.67 µg/d was associated with a decreasing risk of pneumonia. However, above this threshold, the inverse association plateaued, and higher intake was no longer associated with a further reduction in risk.

Oxidative stress plays a key role in the pathogenesis of respiratory diseases. Studies on antioxidant vitamins and respiratory outcomes are controversial. The relevance of beta-carotene, a pro-vitamin A compound and antioxidant, to respiratory infectious diseases remains unclear. A study investigating antioxidant vitamins and respiratory health analyzed nationally representative data from 34,000 U.S. participants and found that low serum vitamin A levels were associated with increased respiratory morbidity and/or mortality in adults [12]. A meta-analysis examined supplementation of nutrients, vitamins and micronutrients known to have a role in immune function, such as beta-carotene, vitamin A, folate, vitamin B12, C, D, E, zinc, iron, and long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids in healthy people, seeking evidence of their prevention of lower respiratory tract infection RTI. It was found that nutrient supplementation had no or very limited effect on preventing infection in the general population [13]. Taking high doses of certain antioxidant supplements, such as beta-carotene, vitamins A (retinol) and E, can even be potentially harmful in terms of preventing disease. Another study evaluated the effect of blood levels of eight micronutrients (copper, iron, selenium, zinc, beta-carotene, vitamin B12, vitamin C, and vitamin D) on the risk of three infections (gastrointestinal infections, pneumonia, and urinary tract infections) and found no correlation between blood levels of carotene and infection risk [14]. Another study on the effect of beta-carotene on mortality challenged previous meta-analyses of 29,133 Finnish male smokers aged 50–69 years (≥ 5 cigarettes/d) and found that beta-carotene supplementation was associated with initiation of smoking at ≥ 21 years of age. People who smoked at least 21 cigarettes per day (7% of the study population) had a four-fold increased risk of pneumonia [15]. Subsequent studies pointed out that the specific biochemical mechanism of β-carotene harm to heavy smokers is not clear, and this harm factor may be related to the change of β-carotene metabolism by smoking. Plasma beta-carotene levels are reduced in smokers [16–18], while quitting smoking results in increased β-carotene concentrations [19]. Exposure to cigarette smoke containing plasma or β-carotene solutions causes β-carotene to degrade into many cleavage products [20–22]. Bronchial epithelial cells exposed to cigarette smoke also produce several beta-carotene cleavage products [23]. Some β-carotene cleavage products may have toxic effects. They inhibit NA-1-K1 ATPase activity [24], impair mitochondrial respiration [25], and alter neutrophils’ respiratory burst [26]. The combination of β-carotene and cigarette tar in the lung microsomal membrane causes greater lipid peroxidation than either of the two substances alone [27], which may explain why beta-carotene is particularly harmful to heavy smokers. Our study population was relatively old, and the high percentage of missing data on smoking history precluded stratified analysis.

Most of the previous studies focused on the community population, while there were few clinical pneumonia studies, especially in the field of elderly people [28]. In contrast to previous studies [27], this study investigated the relationship between the risk of HAP and dietary carotene in hospitalized elderly people, and revealed a non-linear relationship between carotenoid intake level and HAP. In particular, the strength of the inverse association plateaued above a carotenoid intake of 1273.7 µg/d, with no evidence of further risk reduction beyond this threshold. whereas the risk for HAP decreased with an increasing carotene intake level in those below 1273.7 µg/d. These findings suggest that the protective association between carotene intake and HAP may be limited to a specific range of intake. However, no significant difference was found in the T3 group (intake >2101.6 µg/d). This may be related to the saturation effect of the body’s absorption and metabolism of carotenoids, leading to a decrease in bioavailability due to excessive intake. Halliwell et al. proposed that low intake of vitamin E, ascorbic acid, carotenoids, polyphenols, etc. (below recommended daily intake) is associated with an increase in oxidative damage levels, but in most human studies, high intake does not seem to further reduce oxidative damage levels [29]. This is also consistent with our research findings. Additionally, the association between carotene intake levels and HAP remained stable in sensitivity analysis.

While the mechanism of carotene reducing the incidence of pneumonia is not clear, animal studies have found that the ability of carotene to restore the antioxidant system and inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokines may show lung protection by up-regulating the antioxidant status, down-regulating the expression of MMP-7 and inflammatory cytokines, and reducing the accumulation of collagen (hydroxyproline). Farouk et al. found that natural β-carotene may exert anti-inflammatory effects on CP-ALI in mice by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway. It has a protective effect on chronic bronchitis caused by long-term smoking by reducing different pro-inflammatory factors including IL-1α, IL-6, IL-8, and Gro I [30]. Ying Yang et al. investigated the protective effect of β-carotene on pediatric pneumonia by inhibiting METTL3 expression and suppressing LPS induced inflammatory response [31]. Qiong Duan et al. based on network pharmacology and molecular docking investigated the possible action of β-carotene on the TNF, IL17, and HIF-1 signaling pathways, and its therapeutic effect on radiation pneumonitis by regulating the expression of inflammatory factors [32].Some studies have found that carotene can reduce BLM-induced lung injury by inhibiting apoptosis and inducing the expression of TGF-β1/Smad, PI3K/Akt/mTOR and NF-κB signaling pathways, and can be used as a candidate pathway for future pulmonary fibrosis treatment [33]. It also provides a good research idea for our future mechanism exploration.

The data in this study were collected from clinical retrospective studies, and accurate estimates of dietary nutrient intake are critical to the validity of the results of such studies. We used a 72-hour dietary intake survey method to provide more accurate dietary intake data compared with the recall method [34]. However, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes determination of causal relationships between carotene intake and pneumonia risk. Second, although dietary intake was assessed using a detailed 72-hour recall method—a well-established tool for nutritional assessment—the reliance on self-reported data is inherently susceptible to measurement errors, such as recall bias and misestimation. This potential for exposure misclassification was further compounded by the lack of serum β-carotene measurements, which could have provided an objective biomarker to validate the dietary intake data. Third, the exclusive inclusion of male participants (due to the hospital’s patient demographics) limits generalizability to female populations. Fourth, Although the total sample size of 165 participants may be considered moderate, a post-hoc power analysis confirmed that the study was sufficiently powered (98%) to detect the large and clinically significant effect (OR = 0.20) observed between the key comparison groups. Fifth, potential residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., physical activity levels, smoking history, and micronutrient supplement use) could influence the observed associations. Finally, the absence of inflammatory biomarker data prevents mechanistic exploration of carotene’s immunomodulatory effects. However, since carotenoids are fat-soluble, different types of obesity may also affect their bioavailability and effects. Carotenoids from supplements have greater bioavailability than those from food, and may therefore be of more research value for plasma carotenoid concentrations [35]. The metabolism and absorption of carotenoids may be influenced by other physiological or lifestyle factors, including inflammatory characteristics [36], obesity [37] and smoking [38]. Because chronic low-grade inflammation is common in the elderly. These could all be contributing factors. This study focused on the relationship between dietary carotene intake level and the risk of pneumonia. This will be the direction of our research group in the future. However, the results still support our hypothesis that carotenoid intake level is closely related to the risk of pneumonia in a certain dose range and is a protective factor, and it is suggested that reasonable increase of dietary carotenoid intake level can be an important intervention strategy for the occurrence of pneumonia in the elderly.

In summary, this cross-sectional study revealed a significant inverse association between carotene intake and the prevalence of HAP in an elderly population. Our analysis identified an L-shaped dose-response relationship, wherein a lower carotene intake was associated with a higher prevalence of pneumonia. These findings suggest that maintaining an adequate intake of fruits and vegetables rich in carotene and other antioxidants may be beneficial for respiratory health in older adults. Further longitudinal or interventional studies are warranted to confirm this association and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
