Nodular amyloid deposits with collagen fibre rupture in tenosynovial carpal tissues are associated with transthyretin cardiomyopathy: a single-centre prospective diagnostic study
Authors: Jozef Zustin, Florian Plenagl, Cem Saracel, Lisa Karczmarek, Karin Klingel, Thomas Schreck, Andreas Luchner, Marcus Spies
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSystemic amyloidosis affects various organs, including soft tissues, with cardiac involvement (ATTR-CM) associated with the poorest prognosis. Because current treatments are most effective in the early stages, early diagnosis is essential. However, little is known about the significance of distinct morphological patterns of amyloid deposits in musculoskeletal red flag lesions, including the bilateral carpal tunnel tissues. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the incidence and morphological patterns of amyloidosis in patients with bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome; the secondary aim was to screen for cardiac forms. We proposed a novel three-tiered histopathological classification of amyloid deposits within carpal tenosynovial tissues.MethodsFrom June 2021 until May 2023, we conducted a single-centre prospective diagnostic study to investigate microscopic patterns of amyloid deposits in tenosynovial tissues obtained during open median nerve release surgery in patients with bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome. Our study cohort included 83 patients (42 women, 41 men), with a mean age of 65.7 years (range, 27–89 years). All patients underwent a cardiac examination. Scintigraphy and endomyocardial biopsy were performed in highly suspicious cases.ResultsAmyloid deposits were identified in Congo red-stained specimens from 24 patients (28.9%; 16 men and 8 women) and categorised as linear (n = 12), nodular (n = 5), or nodular with collagen fibre rupture (n = 7). The semiquantitative assessed amyloid burden did not correlate with the histologic types of amyloid. Scintigraphy and endomyocardial biopsy were performed in three male patients and confirmed a wild-type form of ATTR-CM. All three patients showed type 3 nodular amyloid deposits with collagen rupture in their carpal tissues. Although the extent of amyloid deposition in tenosynovial tissues did not correlate with cardiac involvement, we observed an association between the nodular pattern with collagen fibre rupture (type 3) and the presence of wild-type ATTR-CM.ConclusionsWe defined three distinct morphological types of amyloid deposition in tenosynovial tissues. The type 3 nodular pattern with collagen fibre rupture appeared to be associated with ATTR-CM. This proposed histopathological classification might significantly enhance screening for ATTR-CM in patients with bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome. Our exploratory findings require validation in larger studies.

Background

Transthyretin-mediated amyloidosis (ATTR) is a heterogeneous, multisystem disease in which a significant proportion of patients develop a mixed phenotype polyneuropathy and cardiomyopathy [1–4]. Diagnosis can be challenging or delayed because of the heterogenous, non-specific nature of ATTR and symptom overlap with other diseases [1, 5–7]. Early suspicion and recognition of ATTR can facilitate an earlier diagnosis and treatment of the cardiac involvement. There is evidence suggesting that a delay in treatment leads to irreversible loss of quality of life and the progression of the polyneuropathic and cardiac manifestations for most patients [8, 9].

Bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome is widely recognised as a potential red flag for possible development of transthyretin cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) [10–18]. Tenosynovial biopsy obtained during the surgical release of carpal tunnel syndrome has been considered a low-risk procedure that may help in the early diagnosis of amyloidosis [12]. Most previous studies have treated carpal amyloid as a binary finding (positive/negative), with few studies examining its quantity in a more detailed manner [17, 18]. Here, we propose a reproducible morphological classification and examine its association with ATTR-CM.

Although carpal tunnel release is the most common hand and wrist surgery in the United States, with more than 400,000 procedures performed annually [19–21], the reported positive rate of amyloid deposition in tissue samples obtained during these procedures varies widely from 1.4% to 37.0% [12, 22–24].

In this single-institution clinicopathological study, we aimed to determine the prevalence, amount, and histopathological patterns of amyloid deposits in the carpal transverse ligament (CTL) and synovial tissues (ST) of patients undergoing surgical treatment for carpal tunnel syndrome. We also investigated whether simultaneous cardiac involvement could be identified. We hypothesised that recognition of different morphological patterns of amyloid deposition within the soft tissues removed during carpal tunnel release surgery might help stratify the risk of cardiac involvement.

Methods

Inclusion criteria

This was a prospective, cross-sectional, single-institution study in which consecutive patients with carpal tunnel syndrome who underwent surgical open carpal tunnel surgery by participating surgeons were screened for inclusion from June 2021 to May 2023. All patients presented with typical clinical symptoms, and the diagnosis of carpal tunnel syndrome was confirmed by both neurophysiological testing and neurosonography. The surgical procedure was performed using the Wide-Awake Local Anaesthesia No Tourniquet (WALANT) technique. All patients who consented to cardiac screening were included. Patients with known cardiac amyloidosis and renal insufficiency requiring dialysis were excluded. Both the CTL and ST removed during surgery were fixed in buffered formalin immediately after excision and sent to the histopathological laboratory.

Histopathological analysis

The formalin-fixed soft tissue specimens (few soft tissue particles with a diameter up to 6 mm) removed during surgery were fully embedded in paraffin wax. Four serial sections, 4 µm thick, were cut and stained with haematoxylin and eosin as well as Congo red. Two unstained sections were prepared for immunohistochemical examination. Amyloid deposits were identified in Congo red-stained sections viewed under cross-polarised light (Fig. 1a). Immunohistochemistry was performed in Congo red-positive cases using a commercially available monoclonal antibody directed against transthyretin (anti-transthyretin, clone: TTR/4292, dilution 1:100; BioGenex; Fig. 1b) and amyloid light chain (AL) (lambda light chain, clone: N10/2, dilution: 1:100; Diagnostic BioSystems). Immunostaining was carried out with an IntelliPath immunostainer (Zytomed Systems), using the ZytoChem Plus (HRP) Polymer Kit detection system (Zytomed Systems) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The specificity of immunostaining was confirmed using manufacturer-recommended positive controls and by omitting the primary antibodies as a negative control. Immunohistochemical analysis was repeated on deeper sections in cases with unclear or non-informative cases (Fig. 2). Additional techniques (e.g., mass spectrometry) were not available. Histopathological workup for carpal tenosynovial tissues

A semiquantitative evaluation of amyloid deposition was performed by visual estimation of the proportion (expressed as percentage) of amyloid within the soft tissues, assessed in a single high-power microscopic field at the site of maximal deposition.

The qualitative assessment was based on the morphological pattern of the amyloid deposition within the carpal soft tissue:

The deposits showed a fairly homogeneous appearance, with no other structural abnormalities detectable by light microscopy. A subset of fibroblasts within the fibrous tissue showed enlarged, rounded, hyperchromatic nuclei. Clinical work-up

Each patient was further evaluated for cardiac involvement through a comprehensive physical examination, blood analysis (including measurements of troponin T and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide), and cardiac examination (including echocardiography). We assessed left ventricular wall thickness, left ventricular mass index, diastolic function, and features that might be suggestive of cardiac amyloidosis (e.g., sparkling myocardium, right ventricular hypertrophy, pericardial effusion) according to recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography and the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging [25, 26]. Patients who showed evidence of potential cardiac involvement were subsequently investigated with bone scintigraphy, which is specific for cardiac ATTR-amyloidosis. Scintigraphy was positive in three patients. All three patients underwent myocardial biopsy, which histologically confirmed the presence of wild-type ATTR-CM.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel. Continuous variables are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR) and categorical variables as count and percentage. Patient characteristics, tissue distribution, and amyloid patterns were summarized descriptively. Cross-tabulations were used to assess associations between categorical variables, including sex and amyloid type, as well as the presence of tissue-specific amyloid. Inferential statistics were performed using R (version 4.5.1). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Amyloid deposits were identified in 24 of 83 patients (28.9%, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 19.0–40.4%). The amyloid-positive cohort comprised 16 men with a median age of 79 years (IQR: 71–81) and 8 women with a median age of 76.5 years (IQR: 72–82.5), indicating a male predominance and an older age distribution among affected patients.

Three distinct morphological patterns of amyloid deposition were observed. Type 1 linear deposits were the most prevalent, occurring in 12 patients (50.0% of amyloid-positive cases, 95% CI: 29.1–70.9%), with an equal distribution between men (n = 6) and women (n = 6). Type 3 nodular deposits with collagen fibre ruptures were identified in seven patients (29.2%, 95% CI: 12.6–51.1%), occurring more frequently in men (n = 5) than in women (n = 2). Although the amount of amyloid deposition did not correlate significantly with the morphological patterns of carpal tissue amyloidosis, we observed clear sex-related differences in the distribution of amyloid types. In men, amyloid-positive tissues were evenly distributed across the three types (nine cases each). Type 1 linear deposits occurred equally in men and women (25.0% each of amyloid-positive cases), whereas type 2 and type 3 deposits showed distinct sex patterns: type 2 deposits occurred exclusively in men, and type 3 deposits were more common in men than in women (20.8% vs. 8.3%, respectively). Notably, none of the amyloid-positive tissues from female patients displayed type 2 nodular amyloid deposition.

Immunohistochemical staining for transthyretin was positive in 36 of 40 Congo red-positive tissues at the first immunohistochemistry. In the subsequent staining, these 4 cases were negative for AL. In the repeated immunohistochemistry, 3 cases were finally positive for transthyretin. Despite deeper serial sectioning and repeated staining, the single tissue that was initially Congo red-positive but immunohistochemistry-negative for both antibodies remained non-informative, and no further amyloid was detected.

Three male patients from the cohort were suspected of having cardiac involvement during cardiac evaluation and subsequently underwent endomyocardial biopsy, which confirmed wild-type ATTR-CM. All three had type 3 amyloid depositions with collagen fibre ruptures in their carpal tissues. This specific deposition pattern was observed in carpal specimens from five men and two women older than 65 years of age. Overall, it represented 3.6% of the entire study cohort (95% CI: 0.8–10.2%) or 12.5% of amyloid-positive patients (95% CI: 2.7–32.4%).

Discussion

In this study, we propose a novel three-tiered histopathological classification of amyloid deposits within the carpal tissues. Whereas previous studies treated carpal amyloid deposition as a binary variable (absent/present), our approach offers a reproducible histopathological classification that may hold predictive value.

Previous studies have reported Congo red-stained amyloid deposits in 1.4% to 37% of soft tissue biopsies taken during carpal tunnel release surgery [12, 22–24, 27–29]. These microscopic analyses focused on identifying amyloid within the tenosynovial tissues and, in a smaller number of studies, were supplemented by immunohistochemical, mass spectrometric [12], or genetic analyses of the deposited amyloid [23]. In the present clinicopathological study, we evaluated the potential value of semiquantitative assessment and morphological pattern analysis of amyloid deposits within the tenosynovial tissues of the carpal canal for the screening of ATTR-CM. We quantified the extent of amyloid deposition and defined three distinct morphological patterns of amyloid accumulation in tenosynovial tissues removed during open carpal tunnel release surgery. Our goal was to determine whether detailed histopathological assessment might support further risk stratification in amyloid-positive patients undergoing carpal tunnel surgery and facilitate the early diagnosis of this serious cardiac condition.

Of the seven patients with histological type 3 amyloidosis of the carpal tunnel, three had histologically proven cardiac amyloidosis. The high incidence of cardiac ATTR-amyloidosis among patients with type 3 carpal tunnel amyloidosis suggests that this pattern may be an important indicator and high-risk factor for more severe cardiac involvement, and it should therefore prompt cardiac evaluation. Because the myocardial biopsies in our study were performed only when echocardiography and scintigraphy were highly suggestive, earlier stages of cardiac disease may have been missed (false negative). It remains possible that patients with lower histological stages were in earlier or subclinical phases of cardiac ATTR-amyloidosis and may develop a cardiac phenotype later on.

Although we did not observe a strong correlation between the amount of amyloid and its histopathological pattern, the morphological features of Congo red-positive amyloid deposits were associated with ATTR-CM as confirmed by endomyocardial biopsy.

This is a novel finding, as we describe three clearly recognisable morphological patterns of amyloid deposition in tenosynovial tissues, identifiable on Congo red-stained slides under polarized light. Importantly, this simple three-tiered morphological classification does not require additional specialised testing. Our results underscore the importance of thorough histopathological evaluation of musculoskeletal tissues in routine clinical practice, particularly for the early detection of ATTR-CM.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small. Nonetheless, the use of clearly defined histopathological criteria and a semiquantitative approach to evaluating amyloid deposition yielded reproducible results. Our histopathological classification of amyloid deposits within tenosynovial tissues, however, should be validated in larger cohorts. Further studies on interobserver agreement are needed to assess its reliability in routine diagnostics. Notably, our findings corroborate -and may further clarify- certain aspects of sex dimorphism observed in systemic transthyretin amyloidosis [30, 31]. It should also be mentioned that endomyocardial biopsy was performed in only three patients, in whom wild-type ATTR-CM was confirmed at the time of hand surgery, but follow-up data for the cohort are not yet available. Because endomyocardial biopsies were performed only in clinically highly suspicious cases, there remains a risk of underestimating the true prevalence of ATTR-CM. Although follow-up examinations are planned at 5 and 10 years for all patients (including those negative for amyloid as well as those positive with types 1, 2, and 3 deposits), at present we cannot predict whether some of the type 1 or 2 cases might later progress or develop ATTR-CM.

Second, the high rate of immunohistochemical positivity for transthyretin in our Congo red-positive cases raises interpretive challenges. If the staining was accurate and specific to transthyretin, it would suggest that our histopathological classification may be highly specific for this amyloid type. It is conceivable, however, that other amyloid types -such as AL- might display distinct, as-yet undescribed morphological patterns. Conversely, if our immunohistochemical staining yielded false positives for Congo red-positive amyloid of other protein types, then our classification could potentially be applicable more broadly to amyloid deposits in general. It should be noted that we are a non-academic pathology institute and use only widely available, commercially validated antibodies. The transthyretin antibody used in this study was tested on a variety of tissue types -including amyloidosis associated with plasma cell myeloma and medullary thyroid carcinoma- prior to its incorporation into routine diagnostics. We had employed this antibody in routine diagnostic practice for more than 1 year before applying it to the study cases. By contrast, immunohistochemical and histopathological analyses in academic or reference centres may not be entirely reproducible because they often utilise non-commercial antibodies for amyloid subtyping [18, 23, 32]. Mass spectrometry was not available for further confirmation of our findings. A recent report by another group also documented a similarly high proportion of transthyretin-positive amyloid by immunohistochemistry [33], suggesting that our findings may not substantially differ from those of other non-academic centres.

Third, because of the observational nature of this study, we cannot draw conclusions about the sequence or progression of amyloid deposition in tenosynovial tissues. We propose a hypothetical sequential development that starts with type 1 linear deposits (without marked structural alterations of collagen fibres), progresses to type 2 nodular deposits with substantial collagen thickening due to denser amyloid accumulation, and culminates in type 3 deposits marked by ruptured, thickened collagen fibres harbouring locally concentrated amyloid. This hypothesis, however, requires further longitudinal studies for validation.

Finally, all patients in our cohort were Caucasian. Given the well-documented variability in ATTR prevalence among different populations [34–37], the clinical relevance of our classification should be assessed in more diverse demographic groups.

The principal strength of this study lies in its integrated approach: we performed both histopathological analysis of tissues obtained during carpal tunnel release surgery and a cardiological workup in all patients. Importantly, in three cases, ATTR-CM was confirmed via endomyocardial biopsy—all of which had been classified as type 3 nodular amyloid deposition with collagen fibre rupture. Furthermore, our study identified a sex-based difference in the morphological patterns of amyloid deposition within carpal tissues. This observation may enhance our understanding of sex dimorphism in patients with ATTR-CM [38, 39], which was also reported in the THAOS registry [30, 31, 40, 41]. The significant difference in the sex distribution between type 1 and type 2 should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size. The absence of significant differences between the other types may reflect either a true lack of differences or insufficient statistical power.

Conclusions

We propose a histopathological classification of amyloid deposition in tenosynovial tissues removed during carpal tunnel release surgery that might help stratify patients at risk for ATTR-CM. Patients with type 3 nodular amyloid deposits, characterised by ruptures of collagen fibres, warrant further cardiological evaluation for ATTR-CM and might be considered for prioritisation. Our exploratory observation needs validation in larger studies.
