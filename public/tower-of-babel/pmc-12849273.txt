The impact of chronic hepatitis B and chronic hepatitis C on sexual function: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jin-Zhi Zhang, Juan Zheng, Yu-Pei Zhang, Chang-Hai Liu, Xiao-Na Song, Ya-Lin Shen, Hong Tang, Hong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSeveral studies have identified an association between chronic hepatitis B (CHB), chronic hepatitis C (CHC), and an elevated risk of sexual dysfunction (SD). However, no systematic review or meta-analysis has been conducted to investigate this relationship.ObjectiveThis study aimed to provide an overview of the prevalence of SD and its associated risk factors in patients with CHB and/or CHC.MethodsDatabases including EMBASE, MEDLINE, and the Cochrane Library, were systematically searched from their inception until June 14, 2023, to identify studies that reported SD in CHB and CHC patients. Subgroup analysis, meta-regression, and sensitivity analysis were employed to detect potential sources of bias.ResultsOut of 734 identified records, 18 studies (encompassing 2,898 patients, 77.6% of whom were men, with a mean age ranging from 36 to 59 years) were included to calculate the overall pooled prevalence of SD. The prevalence of SD was found to be 47.8% (95% CI: 33.9%-61.9%, I2 = 98.4%, p < 0.001) among patients with CHB and/or CHC. Subgroup analysis, meta-regression, and sensitivity analysis did not reveal any potential sources of bias. The prevalence of SD was 56.9% (95% CI: 40.1%-73.0%, I2 = 94.7%, p < 0.001) in women and 44.4% (95% CI: 28.1%-61.4%, I2 = 98.5%, p < 0.001) in men. Synthesis of the data showed that CHB and CHC were significantly associated with an increased risk of developing SD in both sexes (RR = 3.58, 95% CI: 2.76–4.66, I2 = 82.5%, p < 0.001). Hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection (OR: 4.30, 95% CI: 2.74–6.74, I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.397) and depression (OR: 1.51, 95% CI: 1.31–1.75, I2 = 48.3%, p < 0.001) were identified as risk factors for SD in men, and HCV infection (OR: 7.57, 95% CI: 4.16–13.77, I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.747) was also identified as a risk factor for SD in women.ConclusionThe prevalence of SD in patients with CHB and/or CHC is notably high, and the risk of SD increases in both male and female patients with CHB and CHC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-025-02254-0.

Introduction

Viral hepatitis refers to an infectious disease caused by various hepatitis viruses, including the hepatitis C virus (HCV) and the hepatitis B virus (HBV). Viral hepatitis that lasts for more than six months is classified as chronic viral hepatitis, including chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and chronic hepatitis C (CHC) [1]. CHB and CHC pose significant global health challenges. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), there are approximately 1.2 million new HBV infections each year, and an estimated 1.1 million deaths mostly from HBV-related cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [2]. Additionally, CHC infects 71 million people worldwide, and the WHO has called for the elimination of CHC as a continuous development goal by 2030 [3].

Clinicians are increasingly focusing on patients’ quality of life. Research has shown that patients with chronic liver disease experience a gradual decline in their quality of life [4]. CHB and CHC, both of which are associated with numerous extrahepatic manifestations, have a negative impact on patients’ quality of life [5–7]. In particular, infections with HCV or HBV are associated with sexual dysfunction (SD) in both men and women [8, 9].

SD is defined as disturbances in sexual desire and the psychophysiological alterations that characterize the sexual response cycle, significantly contributing to interpersonal conflict and unhappiness. Female SD is mostly characterized by issues such as dyspareunia (pain during intercourse), diminished sexual desire, arousal difficulties, and orgasmic disorders. In contrast, male SD is predominantly characterized by erectile dysfunction (ED), premature ejaculation, and reduced sexual desire [10, 11].

The International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF) and the Female Sexual Index Function (FSFI) are the most widely used assessment scales for sexual function globally. Most current studies evaluating sexual function in patients with CHB or CHC have employed the IIEF, including its simplified version, the IIEF-5, as well as the FSFI, demonstrating good sensitivity and specificity [8, 12–23].

It has been reported that SD is significantly impaired in patients with CHB and/or CHC [8, 9]. However, the prevalence of SD in these patients was found to fluctuate greatly, ranging from 8.6% and 94.5% [8, 9, 12–30]. This variability can be attributed to differences in the assessment methods for SD employed by various studies, as well as the characteristics of the patient populations. This inconsistency hampers future intervention studies aimed at accurately estimating the prevalence of SD. Furthermore, Ma et al. identified age and depression as independent factors contributing to ED in male patients with chronic viral hepatitis [8]. In a multivariate analysis, Liao et al. found that SD was independently correlated with female gender, depression, liver fibrosis, and upper respiratory tract infection/urinary system infections and/or frequent diarrhea [9]. Since the risk factors for SD in chronic viral hepatitis are diverse, it is crucial to conduct a meta-analysis to consolidate the prevalence and identify the relevant influencing factors associated with SD. By systematically reviewing these studies, we aimed to provide an accurate estimate of the prevalence and summarize the risk factors, thereby enabling clinicians to deliver appropriate care tailored to the needs of their patients.

Methods

The review was registered in advance with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42023439075) and was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 statement (PRISMA 2020) [31].

Data sources and searches

Via OVID SP, Databases including EMBASE, MEDLINE, and the Cochrane Library were searched from their inception until 14 June 2023. The search strategy is detailed in Table S1. Additionally, a manual search was conducted for the reference lists of each included article.

Diagnosis of CHC, CHB and SD

The international classification of diseases codes was followed in the definitions of CHB, CHC and SD. CHB and CHC were defined as positivity for HBsAg and HBV-DNA and anti-HCV antibodies and HCV RNA lasting more than half a year, respectively [32–34]. IIEF, FSFI, the Arizona Sexual Experiences Scale (ASEX), and other validated measures were used to diagnose SD.

Study selection

Any available studies that reported the prevalence of SD in patients with CHB and/or CHC were included. The guiding question for this meta-analysis, defined using the Patient, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study design (PICOS) framework, was: “what is the prevalence and what are the risk factors for SD in patients with CHB and CHC?”. The components of the PICOS framework included patients with CHC and/or CHB (P); individuals with a history of SD (I); comparisons with the general population (C); the diagnosis of SD (O); and acceptance of all research designs (S).

The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) duplicate research studies; (2) conference abstracts; (3) reviews, editorials, consensus papers, letters, and case reports; (4) animal experiments; and (5) studies lacking information on the prevalence and/or diagnosis of SD. In instances where multiple publications for the same study were available, only the most recent publication was included. After eliminating duplicate records, the titles and abstracts of the remaining publications were independently reviewed by J.Z.Z. and J.Z. Subsequently, the full-text articles were assessed independently based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The final selection was determined by H.L.

Outcomes

The outcomes of the meta-analysis included a summary of the prevalence of SD and the identification of associated risk factors.

Data extraction and quality assessment

The following information was extracted from the original articles: first author’s name, publication year, country, study design, sex, age, sample sizes, diagnostic methods for CHB, CHC, and SD, prevalence of SD, and the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for SD risk factors. Data extraction was performed by J.Z.Z. and verified by C.H.L. The methodological quality of the included articles was assessed independently by J.Z.Z. and J.Z. using the cross-sectional study quality methodology checklist [35] and the Newcastle–Ottawa quality assessment scale [36]. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consultation with H.L. The overall quality of the evidence was graded using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) [37].

Statistical analysis

We conducted a meta-analysis using STATA 12.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) to determine the pooled prevalence of SD and the pooled OR for risk factors associated with SD in patients with CHB and CHC. The association between CHB, CHC, and the development of SD in the included studies was assessed using relative risk (RR) and its corresponding 95% CI. Point estimates along with their respective 95% CI were reported. To investigate heterogeneity, I2 and the Cochrane Q tests were employed (I2 > 50% was thought to be consistent with substantial heterogeneity, while a P value of Q test < 0.10 was regarded statistically significant) [38]. In the absence of heterogeneity, a fixed-effects model (Mantel–Haenszel method) was applied [39]. In cases of considerable heterogeneity, a random-effects model (DerSimonian and Laird method) was employed [40]. Subgroup analyses were performed based on the assessment methods for SD, the viral hepatitis pathogens, study design, and geographical region. The chi-square test was used for assessment. Meta-regression was conducted considering mean age and year of publication. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the results. Publication bias was evaluated using the Egger bias test and the Begg-Mazumdar Kendall’s tau, publication bias was assessed [41, 42]. If any publication bias was detected, the trim-and-fill approach was applied to adjust for this bias [43].

SD prevalence

In patients with CHB and/or CHC, the overall prevalence of SD was 47.8% (95% CI: 33.9%−61.9%, I2 = 98.4%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). The prevalence of SD in men and women was 44.4% (95% CI: 28.1%−61.4%, I2 = 98.5%, p < 0.001) and 56.9% (95% CI: 40.1%−73.0%, I2 = 94.7%, p < 0.001), respectively (Figure S1). The prevalence of SD in CHC and CHB patients was 51.2% (95% CI: 35.8%−66.5%, I2 = 98.3%, p < 0.001) and 39.6% (95% CI: 15.4%−66.8%, I2 = 95.1%, p < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 3). Synthesis of the data showed that CHB and CHC was significantly associated with an increased risk of SD in both sexes (8 studies, RR = 3.58, 95% CI: 2.76–4.66, I2 = 82.5%, p < 0.001), in females (3 studies, RR = 3.54, 95% CI: 2.58–4.85, I2 = 59.4%, p = 0.085), and in males (5 studies, RR = 3.61, 95% CI: 2.40–5.43, I2 = 88.4%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4).Fig. 2Prevalence of sexual dysfunction in chronic hepatitis B and hepatitis C patients. Subgroup analysis, meta-regression, and sensitivity analysis

The prevalence of SD, as diagnosed by IIEF or IIEF-5 and/or FSFI, was found to be 53.3% (11 studies, 95% CI: 33.3%−72.9%, I2 = 98.5%, p < 0.001), which was higher than the prevalence diagnosed by alternative methods (7 studies, 39.1%, 95% CI: 27.1%−51.8%, I2 = 95.2%, p < 0.001). However, the difference between these results was not statistically significant (p = 0.243) (Figure S2). The overall prevalence of SD in cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, and case–control studies was 38.1% (10 studies, 95% CI: 26.0%−51.0%, I2 = 95.3%, p < 0.001), 57.3% (6 studies, 95% CI: 29.5%−82.8%, I2 = 99.1%, p < 0.001), and 67.2% (2 studies, 95% CI: 60.8%−73.2%), respectively (Figure S3). The cumulative prevalence of SD by region was 53.6% in Africa (7 studies, 95% CI: 25.9%−80.2%, I2 = 99.1%, p < 0.001), 32.7% (4 studies, 95% CI: 13.9%−54.7%, I2 = 93.9%, p < 0.001) in Asia, 54.7% (4 studies, 95% CI: 39.2%−69.6%, I2 = 92.0%, p < 0.001) in Europe, 34.0% (2 studies, 95% CI: 29.2%−38.9%) in North America, and 57.1% (1 studies, 95% CI: 47.1%−66.8%) in South America, respectively (Figure S4).

In the meta-regression analysis, the effect size was not found to be predicted by the average age of patients or the year of publication (Figure S5-S6). A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the impact of each study on the overall prevalence of SD. No significant changes were observed in the pooled prevalence after the exclusion of any individual study (Figure S7).

Risk factors of SD

Six studies provided data regarding the risk factors for SD. A meta-analysis was conducted, incorporating data from three studies on HCV infection, two studies on age, three studies on depression, and two studies on unemployment as risk factors for SD in men. HCV infection (OR: 4.30, 95% CI: 2.74–6.74, I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.397) and depression (OR: 1.51, 95% CI: 1.31–1.75, I2 = 48.3%, p < 0.001) were identified as risk factors for SD in men. Conversely, age (OR: 5.35, 95% CI: 0.85–33.73, I2 = 80.3%, p < 0.001) and unemployment (OR: 4.82, 95% CI: 0.74–31.32, I2 = 78.8%, p = 0.030) were not associated with SD. In women, HCV infection (OR: 7.57, 95% CI: 4.16–13.77, I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.747) was found to be a risk factor for SD (Fig. 5). Publication bias

The Funnel plot, along with the Egger and Begg tests, indicated no publication bias in the prevalence of SD (Figure S8, Egger test: p = 0.783, Begg test: p = 0.850).

Discussion

Following a systematic and comprehensive literature search, a total of 18 studies were selected for inclusion, of which 8 studies had a control group. Our study revealed a prevalence of SD among patients with CHB and CHC at 47.8%, with rates of 44.4% in men and 56.9% in women. Furthermore, the risk of SD development was significantly higher in both male and female patients compared to the control group. According to GRADE, the average incidence of SD events in male patients was 72.3%, which was significantly higher than the control rate of 19.5%. In female patients, the average prevalence of SD was 78.1%, which was also significantly higher than the control rate of 14.7%. Subgroup analysis revealed that the assessment methods for SD, the viral pathogens causing chronic viral hepatitis, study design, and geographical region were not significant sources of heterogeneity in the prevalence of SD. Among the studies meeting the inclusion criteria, the majority utilized IIEF and/or FSFI for evaluating SD. However, the choice of assessment methods had minimal impact on the prevalence of SD. Regarding the pathogen causing chronic viral hepatitis, most studies focused on the occurrence of SD during HCV infection, while there were limited studies pertaining to HBV. Meta-regression and sensitivity analysis found no significant sources of heterogeneity affecting SD prevalence.

The present study identified HCV infection as a risk factor for SD in both male and female patients. The potential mechanisms by which HCV infection may cause SD include: 1) the progression of extrahepatic disease and increased psychological discomfort [44]; 2) treatment with interferon alpha and ribavirin [29]; 3) abnormalities in one or more components of the hypothalamic-pituitary–gonadal axis [29]; 4) depression (which remains a controversial factor) [25, 30]; 5) elevated alanine aminotransferase levels and plasma HCV RNA levels [29]. It was found that SD related to HCV infection significantly improved in both sexes following the clearance of the virus through treatment with direct-acting antiviral drugs [12, 25, 45–48]. Additionally, a separate study reported that entecavir antiviral treatment exhibited significant efficacy in improving SD in patients with CHB. However, further investigation is needed to determine whether this effect is direct or occurs indirectly through improvements in liver function [49]. Thus, antiviral treatment without interferon alpha and ribavirin may improve SD in patients with CHB and CHC.

In studies involving other populations, depression has been identified as a risk factor for the development of SD [50]. Similar findings were noted in extant research pertaining to CHB and CHC patients, where the concurrent presence of depression was found to be an independent risk factor for persistent SD [19, 25, 26, 28, 30, 51, 52]. After analyzing the existing studies, we found that depression was a risk factor of SD in male patients with CHB and/or CHC. Additionally, we found that age and unemployment were not associated with the occurrence of SD in male patients, which is inconsistent with the results of some original studies [18, 26–28]. This inconsistency may be attributed to the limited number of original studies available, as only two studies investigated the relationships between age, employment status, and SD. However, a meta-analysis of the literature could not be conducted for female patients due to the limited availability of data on other potential risk factors apart from HCV infection.

Other risk factors that could not be pooled were summarized in this study. Several studies indicated that anxiety [26], hypertension [19], lower hemoglobin levels (g/dL) [26], antidepressant use [26], HCV viral load > 2 million copies/mL [29], current interferon alpha use [26], and elevated alanine aminotransferase levels [29] were associated with an increased risk of SD in male patients. Conversely, higher serum albumin levels [19], higher Short Form-36 physical component summaries score [28], higher total cholesterol levels (mg/dL) [28], and Hispanic ethnicity [29] have been identified as protective factors against SD in male patients. Among female patients, risk factors for SD include fatigue, antidepressant use, and benzodiazepine use [26].

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first comprehensive summary of studies investigating the prevalence and risk factors of SD in patients with CHB and CHC. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, this study included 18 studies characterized by heterogeneity in sample size, study design, geographical location, and diagnosis methods for SD, which led to high level of heterogeneity in this meta-analysis (I2 = 98.2%). Despite employing subgroup analysis, sensitivity analysis, and meta-regression, no specific source of heterogeneity could be identified. Theoretically, the method of assessing SD may be one of the sources of heterogeneity in its prevalence. However, due to the limited number of studies utilizing certain SD assessment methods, we did not find that the assessment method significantly influenced the prevalence of SD. It is important to note that heterogeneity is frequently observed in meta-analyses of prevalence, primarily due to the large sample sizes and precise estimates derived from individual studies, leading to statistical heterogeneity [53]. Secondly, most of the included studies did not clarify the temporal relationship between risk factors and SD, which prevented us from determining causation based on the risk factors analyzed. Thirdly, the literature included in our meta-analysis provided limited research on risk factors, such as age and unemployment, which may lead to type II errors. Additionally, due to the scarcity of available studies, it was not feasible to conduct a pooled analysis on certain risk factors, such as anxiety and antidepressant use. Moreover, the risk factors for SD in this study were analyzed based solely on the included studies, which may have overlooked other potential influences on SD, such as metabolic syndrome. Finally, the observed prevalence of SD in this special population may be partially contributed to advanced liver disease, but we did not have access to raw data for a subgroup analysis of patients with advanced liver disease. Notwithstanding these limitations, our findings offer valuable insights into the prevalence and risk factors associated with SD in patients with CHB and/or CHC. Future research should prioritize a high-quality, well-designed, and longitudinal studies with large sample sizes to comprehensively investigate the prevalence, risk factors, prognosis, and intervention strategies related to SD in patients with CHB and/or CHC, thereby involving efforts to improve their quality of life.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the meta-analysis indicated a risk of SD in both male and female patients with CHB and/or CHC. In male patients, HCV infection and depression were identified as risk factors for SD, while in female patients, HCV infection was found to be a risk factor for SD. Given the observed correlations, it is recommended that patients undergo evaluation of their sexual function and receive targeted treatments accordingly.
