QnAs with Jane E. Parker
Authors: Sandeep Ravindran
Published: 2025

Over the course of her career, Jane Parker has made major contributions to the scientific understanding of plant innate immunity. Her research has helped elucidate the mechanisms by which plants recognize pathogens and mount an immune response against them. She is known for her work on the large families of intracellular nucleotide-binding/leucine-rich repeat (NLR) immune receptors, which detect pathogen attack and form large oligomeric complexes. Known as resistomes, such complexes promote a strong immune response against pathogens. In her Inaugural Article (IA) (1), Parker describes her latest research on uncovering the role of NLR postactivation signaling for inducing resistance against disease. Based at the Max-Planck Institute for Plant Breeding Research, Parker, a professor at the University of Cologne in Germany, was elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 2023 as an international member.

How did you first become interested in studying NLRs?

I have been interested in NLR biology and how their signaling is transduced for a long time. I was actually involved in cloning some of the first NLR genes back in the 1990s. We were interested in sensor NLRs, which are specifically activated by a particular pathogen strain, and it took a long time to figure out how such sensor NLRs work. But, over the past six years, there have been a lot of extremely interesting findings. We began to learn a lot from cryoelectron microscopy about how these big multidomain sensor proteins work at a biochemical level. That then opened up all sorts of questions about how pathogen recognition causes resistance.

We also became very interested in another group of NLRs called helper’ NLRs that are involved in defense signaling. Together with colleagues, we established the mechanism by which downstream signaling components we had already identified activate the helper NLRs. But there was still a gap in our knowledge about how the helper NLR then signals and mobilizes defenses. Recent structure–function analyses have been very helpful in answering these kinds of questions, and this study emerges from that kind of information (1).

How did your IA come about?

There was structural and functional evidence that when some sensor NLRs as well as helper NLRs are activated, they function as pathogen-induced oligomeric calcium ion channels or pores. So that was one strand underlying this new study. The release of calcium into cells by these channels would turn on various calcium decoding systems for transcriptional reprogramming, but how this link operates was not understood. There were also indications that the helper NLRs can form a smaller intermediate complex together with its activating factors in the plant nucleus. This raised the nagging possibility that perhaps a different NLR (nonchannel) activity contributes to the rapid transcriptional changes we see in NLR-triggered immunity. So we wanted to know whether this smaller NLR assembly is important or not for defense.

We could capitalize on a sensor NLR oligomer (resistosome) cryo-EM structure to design and test various mutations in the helper NLR, which would pin down whether it needs to form an oligomeric ion channel to function. We were also curious whether components that are transcriptionally induced by the helper NLR help to mount the immune response. We decided to look at two glutamate receptor-like calcium ion channels (GLR2.9a and GLR2.9b), which were known to be important in innate immunity in Arabidopsis, because their expression was strongly boosted by the activated helper NLR—over and above other GLRs. We wanted to find out if these canonical ion channels contribute to the immune response triggered by NLRs.

What are the main takeaways from this study?

One takeaway was that it’s the oligomeric helper NLR ion channel that does the job in transmitting signals downstream of a pathogen-activated sensor NLR. We therefore concluded that the smaller NLR complex is probably a signaling-inactive intermediate in assembly of the active NLR ion channel, which then releases calcium into cells.

Another takeaway was that the identified GLR2.9a and GLR2.9b canonical calcium channels that are transcriptionally boosted by the helper NLR are important additional players in executing the NLR-triggered immune response. It was important to establish that these immune effects are due to the outputs promoted by NLRs.

Another take-home message from our experiments, and something we would like to resolve, is that the helper NLRs must be producing something other than GLRs for the immune response to work. So there’s an additional stimulus, maybe glutamate or another small molecule, that enables effective coordination between the helper NLR-assembled and these transcriptionally induced canonical ion channels in pathogen resistance.

Something else that was surprising and interesting is that these GLRs seem to have different subcellular preferences. One of them tends to go to or be stabilized at the nuclear envelope, whereas the other seems to be more prevalent at the plasma membrane. Maybe these different localizations are important for the decoding of calcium in different parts of the cell for defense.

What are some potential applications of this work?

A fuller understanding of the controls for sensor and helper NLR systems could help us optimize immunity outputs in the face of new pathogen strains, for example. So these findings, coupled with NLR engineering studies, have great potential for improving crop resistance to disease.

What were some of the challenges you faced in this work?

These helper NLRs, and in fact most NLRs, are not easy proteins to work with. In addition, detailed functional information on GLRs in the plant field was sparse, so we also used insights from animal work. Expressing GLR proteins, which retained function so we could track them inside cells, was quite a challenge, so we faced some hurdles there.

This project required a lot of careful analysis by the postdoctoral researcher who led this work, Junli Wang, and the first coauthor, Xinhua Sun. Our collaboration with Qiaochu Shen and Takaki Maekawa was also very helpful because they had experience in measuring intracellular calcium in leaf cells over time. Using structural chemistry on top of genetics to unpick some of these immune signaling processes was immensely interesting and significant.
