County-level social determinants of health and suicide mortality trends among formerly incarcerated persons in North Carolina, 2010–2018
Authors: Monica E. Swilley-Martinez, Brian W. Pence, Kate Vinita Fitch, Vanessa E. Miller, Matthew M. Turnure, Pasangi S. Perera, Timothy S. Carey, Shabbar I. Ranapurwala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicide mortality represents a growing public health crisis in the United States, disproportionately affecting people with recent incarceration histories. The purpose of this study was to assess how county-level Social Determinants of Health—including educational attainment, unemployment, poverty, aging, racial composition, and rurality—are associated with suicide mortality among formerly incarcerated individuals in North Carolina in order to develop geographically focused, socially responsive suicide prevention resources.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of people released from North Carolina prisons between 2010 and 2018 and compared suicide mortality with that of the North Carolina general population aged 18–69 years. Using linked administrative data, we calculated age-, sex-, and year-standardized suicide mortality rates and standardized mortality ratios (SMRs) by county-level social determinants of health, including educational attainment, unemployment, poverty, aging, racial composition, and rurality.ResultsSuicide mortality among formerly incarcerated individuals was consistently higher than in the general population, with the greatest disparities in counties that are urban (SMR: 2.04, 95% CI: 1.50, 2.58), have below average educational attainment (SMR: 2.53, 95% CI: 1.69,3.37), below average unemployment (SMR: 2.33, 95% CI: 1.66, 3.01), low poverty (SMR: 2.13, 95% CI: 1.56, 2.70), below-average racial diversity (SMR: 3.12, 95% CI: 2.20, 4.04), or an above-average proportion of older adults (SMR: 2.40, 95% CI: 1.22, 3.57).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that suicide mortality disparities between formerly incarcerated individuals and the general population are greatest in more advantaged counties, where community-level protective factors appear to benefit the general population but do not extend to those returning from prison. Addressing these disparities will require investments in place-based mental health infrastructure and reentry support tailored to local community contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40352-026-00409-0.

Background

Suicide, a substantial cause of untimely mortality, remains a public health burden in the United States (US), with suicide rates climbing steadily in recent years (Garnett MF & Curtin SC, 2023). In 2022, over 49,000 people died by suicide, surpassing previous records and reinforcing the urgency of comprehensive suicide prevention strategies (Curtin et al., 2023). The US Surgeon General’s issuance of the National Suicide Prevention Strategy in 2021 further emphasized the importance of addressing rising rates, particularly among resource-poor groups (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2021). Suicide death rates in North Carolina (NC), the ninth most populous state, mirror national patterns, with notable increases among vulnerable populations, including formerly incarcerated individuals who are often at heightened risk due to lack of access to health care, employment, housing, and social support (Binswanger, 2013; Brinkley-Rubinstein, 2013; Brinkley-Rubinstein & Cloud, 2020; Ranapurwala et al., 2018; Rosen et al., 2011; Wildeman & Wang, 2017).

Formerly incarcerated individuals have an elevated risk of suicide, particularly during the period immediately following release from incarceration, a time when social, economic, and mental health stressors intersect to increase vulnerability (Binswanger et al., 2007; Bukten & Stavseth, 2021; Morgan et al., 2022; Pratt et al., 2006; Rosen et al., 2011; Zlodre & Fazel, 2012). This period of reentry is characterized by persistent structural barriers—including legal restrictions, labor market exclusion, housing instability, and fragmented access to health care—that constrain formerly incarcerated individuals’ ability to benefit from community resources in the same way as the general population (Wildeman & Wang, 2017). As a result, county-level social and structural conditions that may be protective for the general population could confer less benefit among formerly incarcerated individuals who remain socially excluded within these environments.

In NC, the risk of suicide death among formerly incarcerated people is twice that of the general population, emphasizing the need to examine the factors contributing to this disparity (Fitch et al., 2023; Lize et al., 2015). Recent research by Fitch et al. (2023) provides a longitudinal perspective on suicide mortality among all individuals released from prison in NC from 2000 to 2020, identifying substantial variation by race, gender, and age. Specifically, formerly incarcerated individuals who identified as female, white or Hispanic/Latino, or aged 18 to 21 experienced particularly elevated suicide mortality rates relative to their counterparts in the general population. However, less is known about how broader county-level social determinants of health (SDOH) shape suicide risk within this population, or whether these determinants operate differently for formerly incarcerated individuals compared to the general population, given persistent barriers to social integration following incarceration. Understanding these differences is critical, because persistent reentry barriers may limit the protective influence of favorable county-level conditions on suicide mortality among formerly incarcerated individuals.

In this study, we examine whether suicide mortality among formerly incarcerated individuals varies across county-level social determinants of health—including educational attainment, unemployment, poverty, aging, racial composition, and rurality—and how these patterns compare with those observed in the general population in North Carolina from 2010 to 2018. This study aims to identify social and structural contexts in which formerly incarcerated individuals may be uniquely vulnerable.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study to evaluate the association between various SDOH and the suicide mortality rate among formerly incarcerated people and the general population in NC between 2010 and 2018.

Study population

The formerly incarcerated population consisted of people released from NC state prisons between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2018. The general population consisted of all NC residents. The study population was restricted to persons aged between 18 and 69 years old due to few outcomes in formerly incarcerated people age 70 and older. Formerly incarcerated individuals were followed beginning at release and until reincarceration, death, or study end on December 31, 2019. An individual did not contribute any person-time while incarcerated but may have contributed multiple stretches of person-time if reincarcerated and rereleased during the study period.

Data sources

We received data on the NC formerly incarcerated population from the NC Department of Adult Corrections (NCDAC). NCDAC data included demographic data, such as name, date of birth, self-reported sex, and self-reported race and ethnicity, admission and release dates, unique identification numbers, and county of residence. County of residence was collected upon release from prison. All other data were collected upon prison admission. Using deterministic linkage based on last and first names, date of birth, and sex, we linked the NCDAC data to death certificate records from the NC Division of Public Health (NCDPH) to identify suicide deaths among formerly incarcerated people. Formal documentation of the linkage process has been previously published (Ranapurwala et al., 2022).

NCDPH death certificate data were used to identify suicide deaths among all NC residents, including formerly incarcerated people. NCDPH data included sex, age at death, decedent date of death, and cause of death, as determined by International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. The total NC general population was constructed using 2010–2018 National Center for Health Statistics annual bridged-race population estimates by county (National Center for Health Statistics, 2021). We obtained 2013 urban-rural classifications for each county from the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA)(USDA, Economic Research Service, 2024). Though 2023 estimates are now available, we used 2013 estimates to accurately reflect the geography of rurality in NC during the study period. We determined the racial composition of each county using the Diversity Index rankings from the 2010 United States Census (US Census Bureau, n.d.). The Diversity Index indicates the probability that two randomly selected people from an area will be of different races or ethnicities, with values closer to 100% indicating more diverse racial and ethnic characteristics in the population (Meyer & McIntosh, 1992). There was a minimal increase in NC’s Diversity Index score from 2010 to 2020 (52.1% vs. 57.9%); we therefore assume the 2010 Diversity Index scores best represent the racial composition of NC counties during the study period. County-level educational attainment, poverty level, proportion of elderly people, and unemployment rates were obtained from the American Community Survey conducted by the United States Census Bureau for the years 2010–2018 (US Census Bureau, 2010). Poverty level information became available in 2012 (US Census Bureau, American Community Survey, 2012).

Outcomes

We defined death by suicide as any death with a primary cause associated with an ICD-10 code of X60–X84 (intentional self-poisoning and self-harm), Y87.0 (sequelae of intentional self-harm), or U03 (terrorism involving intentional self-harm), or codes. Suicide mortality rates were calculated per 100,000 person-years (PY).

Covariates

Age was used as a continuous variable, ranging from 18 to 69 years old. Sex was a binary variable (male, female). Calendar year was used as a continuous variable from 2010 to 2018. SDOHs examined in this study included educational attainment, poverty level, proportion of elderly people, racial composition, rurality, and unemployment rate. County-level SDOH were dichotomized using established thresholds or state-level averages to enhance interpretability. Educational attainment was defined as the percentage of county residents with a bachelor’s degree relative to the North Carolina state average for the same year. Poverty was measured as the percentage of residents living below the Federal Poverty Level (FPL), with counties classified as low poverty (< 20% below FPL) or high poverty (≥ 20%), consistent with USDA definitions (U.S. Department of Agriculture, Economic Research Service, n.d.). Counties were classified as having an above-average elderly population if the proportion of residents aged 65 years and older exceeded the state average for that year. Racial diversity was defined using the U.S. Census Bureau 2010 Diversity Index, with counties scoring above 50% classified as high diversity and all others as low diversity to indicate counties in which no single racial or ethnic group constitutes a majority. In order to contextualize our findings within the United States, we performed a sensitivity analysis using the National average diversity index score as a cut-point (54.1%) so that we could compare counties that were more homogenous than the national average with counties that were as diverse and more heterogeneous than the average. Rurality was determined using the USDA 2013 Rural–Urban Continuum Codes, with metropolitan counties classified as urban and all others as rural. Counties with unemployment rates exceeding the state average for a given year were classified as having above-average unemployment; all others were classified as below average. These measures were selected a priori to capture multiple conceptually distinct domains of social and structural conditions that have been consistently associated with suicide mortality in prior research (Cammack et al., 2024; Casant & Helbich, 2022; CDC, 2025; Kerr et al., 2017; Martínez-Alés et al., 2022; Phillips & Hempstead, 2017; Rehkopf & Buka, 2006). These measures were also chosen based on their availability and stability at the county level across the study period.

Statistical analyses

We calculated suicide mortality rates amongst formerly incarcerated people per 100,000 PY by dividing the number of suicide deaths that occurred within one year of release by the total person-time at risk accrued during the same period. We used direct standardization by age, sex, and year of release to derive standardized one-year suicide mortality rates for formerly incarcerated people. We calculated suicide rates among the NC general population by dividing the number of suicide deaths in each county by county annual population estimates, which represent the number of person-years contributed by residents of each county per year. We calculated SMRs comparing the suicide mortality rate among the two populations by using indirect standardization. To examine effect measure modification by SDOH, we calculated stratified estimates. Additionally, we stratified age- and sex-adjusted one-year suicide mortality rates and SMRs by calendar year to examine temporal trends in suicide mortality for both populations. Sensitivity analyses using alternate thresholds for educational attainment, racial composition, and unemployment rate were conducted to determine the robustness of our results. Alternate thresholds included the state average percentage of residents with a bachelor’s degree between 2010 and 2018 (13.2%), the National average diversity index score (54.1%), and the state average unemployment rate between 2010 and 2018 (10.0%), respectively.

Results

Between 2010 and 2018, 144,811 people between ages 18 and 69 were released from NC prisons. Some individuals were incarcerated and released multiple times, culminating in a total of 211,009 releases during the study period. Of these releases, 40.4% (n = 85,237) resulted in reincarceration. 3% (n = 7,222) of the releases during the period resulted in death prior to the end of the study. Of those deaths, 352 (4.9%) were caused by suicide. Over the study period, 842,915.16 post-release PY were accrued among formerly incarcerated people, with a median of three PY following each release. In the general population, a total of 53,410,236 person-years were accrued during the study period. In the formerly incarcerated population, 7,222 people died prior to the end of the study, of whom, 352 (4.9%) were caused by suicide. In the NC general population, 278,323 people between the ages 18 and 69 died during the study period, of whom 10,245 (3.7%) were caused by suicide.

Formerly incarcerated people were mostly male (86.4%) and either black (49.7%) or white (43.0%). The median age at release was 32 years (IQR: 25–42). In comparison, the mid-study NC general population in 2014 had a more even distribution of sex and had a higher median age of 42.3 years (IQR: 25). Most of the general population was white (66.0%) (Table 1).

The overall age, sex, and calendar year adjusted suicide mortality rate for formerly incarcerated people was 30.21 per 100,000 PY, for an SMR of 1.89 when compared to the general population, whose overall suicide mortality rate was 15.95 per 100,000 PY.

Educational attainment

The majority of both the NC general population in 2014 and the formerly incarcerated population lived in counties where the percentage of residents with a bachelor’s degree exceeded the state average for that year (Table 1). Among formerly incarcerated people, the age-, sex-, and year-adjusted rate of suicide in counties with above average educational attainment was 30.61 per 100,000 PY, slightly lower than the rate in counties with below average educational attainment (36.04 per 100,000 person-years). In comparison, the general population suicide mortality rate in counties with above average educational attainment was 13.96 per 100,000 PY, whereas the rate in below average counties was 23.13 per 100,000 PY.

The resulting SMR comparing formerly incarcerated population to the NC general population in counties with above average educational attainment was 2.17 (95% CI: 1.57, 2.77). Comparing the rate of formerly incarcerated people living in counties with below average educational attainment to the general population residing in counties with above average educational attainment, the SMR was 1.67 (95% CI: 1.61, 1.70). After 2012, the suicide mortality rate among formerly incarcerated people in above average educational attainment counties was greater than the general population and increased over time (Fig. 1A). Conversely, the suicide mortality rate among formerly incarcerated people was similar or lower than the NC general population in below average educational attainment counties (Fig. 1B). Using an alternative dichotomization of county educational attainment based on the state average percentage of residents with a bachelor’s degree from 2010 to 2018 yielded similar results, with no substantive differences in suicide mortality patterns (Supplementary Table 1).

Unemployment rate

Roughly two-thirds of both the NC general population in 2014 and the formerly incarcerated population lived in areas of above average unemployment. The suicide mortality rate among formerly incarcerated people residing in counties with above average unemployment (28.5 per 100,000 PY) was lower than those residing in counties with below average unemployment (31.4 per 100,000 PY). General population rates had a reverse pattern. The SMRs for formerly incarcerated individuals in counties with above and below average unemployment, relative to NC general population living in areas of below average unemployment were 2.1 (95% CI: 1.29, 2.90) and 2.3 (95% CI: 1.66, 3.01), respectively. While suicide mortality rates among formerly incarcerated people residing in counties with above average unemployment rates were generally similar to the rates among their general population counterparts, rates among formerly incarcerated people living in counties with below average unemployment rates were mostly higher in comparison to general population rates (Fig. 2). Similar patterns were observed when county unemployment rate was dichotomized using the state average from 2010 to 2018 (Supplementary Table 1).

Poverty level

Nearly one-fifth of both populations lived in counties where 20% or more of residents were below the federal poverty line (FPL) (Table 1). Formerly incarcerated people in low poverty counties had the highest suicide mortality rate (34.95 per 100,000 PY), while their general population counterparts had the lowest suicide mortality rate at 16.28 per 100,000 PY, yielding an SMR of 2.13 (95% CI: 1.56, 2.70). The SMR comparing the suicide mortality rate among formerly incarcerated people living in high poverty counties to that in the NC general population living in low poverty counties was 1.77 (95% CI: 0.77, 1.77). While in the general population, high poverty counties had higher suicide mortality rates compared to the low poverty counties; that relationship was reversed for the formerly incarcerated population. In high poverty counties, the suicide morality rate among formerly incarcerated people was variable over time, and in some years below that in the general population, including no suicide deaths among formerly incarcerated people in 2015 (Fig. 3A). In low poverty counties, suicide mortality rates among formerly incarcerated people remained higher than the NC general population from 2013 onwards and increased over time (Fig. 3B). Proportion of elderly people

While the NC general population in 2014 was evenly distributed by age group, most people (61.3%) released from an NC prison were between ages 22 and 39 (Table 1). Further, only 1.7% of formerly incarcerated people were aged 60–69, whereas 16.3% of the general population were between this age range. Suicide mortality rates among formerly incarcerated people residing in counties with an above average elderly population (31.2 per 100,000 PY) and in counties with a below average elderly population (29.5 per 100,000 PY) were similar. In the general population, however, counties with above average elderly population had a suicide mortality rate (26.4 per 100,000 PY) that was twice that in counties with below average elderly population (12.9 per 100,000 PY) (Table 2). The SMR for formerly incarcerated individuals in counties with an above and below average elderly population, relative to NC general population with below average elderly population was 2.4 (95% CI: 1.22, 3.57) and 2.3 (95% CI: 1.67, 2.85), respectively. Among those residing in above average elderly counties suicide death rates remained high among both formerly incarcerated people and the NC general population averaging between 20 and 50 suicide deaths per 100,000 PY from year to year, except in 2012 and 2015 when there were no deaths among formerly incarcerated people (Fig. 4A). In comparison, in counties with below average elderly population, formerly incarcerated individuals consistently had higher suicide mortality rates compared to the general population (Fig. 4B).

Racial composition

Over 80% of people were either black or white in both populations. However, as the NC general population in 2014 was 66.0% white and only 22.1% black, black people were highly overrepresented in the formerly incarcerated population (Table 1). Suicide mortality rates among formerly incarcerated people were higher than in the general population, regardless of county level racial composition. NC general population residents who resided in counties with highest racial diversity experienced the lowest suicide death rates (12.7 per 100,000 PY). The rate was highest among formerly incarcerated people residing in counties with low racial diversity (40.26 per 100,000 PY) for an SMR of 3.12 (95% CI: 2.20, 4.04) when compared with general population peers living in counties with high racial diversity. General population rates remained stable over time in counties with both low and high racial diversity (Fig. 5). Thus, SMR trends were largely influenced by rates among formerly incarcerated people. Suicide mortality rates among formerly incarcerated people in low racial diversity counties were variable over time, reaching the highest rate in 2018. In counties with high racial diversity, however, rates were stable over time. Results were robust to alternative dichotomization of county racial composition using the national average diversity index (54.1%), with no meaningful changes in suicide mortality patterns (Supplementary Table 1).

Rurality

The distribution of urban residence at a given release was 74.3%, which is reflective of the distribution of urban residency in the mid-study general population. Adjusted suicide mortality rates among formerly incarcerated people in urban counties and rural counties were similar at 30.1 per 100,000 PY and 29.3 per 100,000 PY. In both urban and rural counties, formerly incarcerated people died of suicide at a standardized rate roughly twice that of the NC general population (Table 2). Rates among formerly incarcerated people increased over time especially between 2015 and 2018 in both rural and urban counties and mostly remained higher than the general population (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The findings of this study underscore the heightened vulnerability of formerly incarcerated individuals to suicide, with suicide mortality rates among this population exceeding those of the NC general population across multiple county-level social determinants of health. The increased risk of suicide among individuals transitioning from incarceration aligns with previous studies that highlight the vulnerable period following reentry into society (Binswanger et al., 2007; Brinkley-Rubinstein, 2013; Fitch et al., 2023; Khatri et al., 2025). This study builds upon that foundation by clearly demonstrating that, unlike in the general population where suicide mortality is higher in counties with more adverse characteristics, the elevated suicide mortality rate among formerly incarcerated people is consistent across county contexts.

Disparities in suicide mortality between formerly incarcerated individuals and the general population were most pronounced in counties classified as urban and in counties with below-average educational attainment, below-average unemployment, low poverty rates, low racial diversity, or an above-average proportion of older adults, when compared with the general population residing in counties with more favorable levels of these social determinants of health. In these contexts, suicide mortality among formerly incarcerated persons was more than twice, and in some cases, more than three times that of the general population. This pattern arose not because suicide mortality among formerly incarcerated people was higher in these contexts, but because rates in the general population were lower, suggesting that some community-level protective factors benefit the general population but fail to extend to those returning from prison.

This pattern of disparities disproportionately impacting formerly incarcerated people in more advantaged, resource-rich counties can be explained by theories of relative disadvantage and social exclusion. These theories posit that disparities in well-being are shaped not only by absolute deprivation but by perceived or actual inequality within communities (Chen, 2015; Link & Phelan, 1995; Townsend, 1987). Formerly incarcerated people experience substantial barriers to formal employment, educational attainment, and social integration and living in advantaged communities may deepen the social and economic exclusion they experience. The consequences of this exclusion may feel more pronounced, especially for individuals who possess qualifications or aspirations that are systematically undermined by their legal record and access to support remains unequal (Ridgeway, 2014). This theory aligns with prior research demonstrating that formerly incarcerated people may experience heightened distress due to social comparison, community stigma, and labor market exclusion, with these processes being more noticeable in more advantaged contexts where dominant norms of stability, employment, and civic participation intensify the visibility of exclusion (Link & Phelan, 1995; Schnittker & John, 2007; Wakefield & Uggen, 2010; Wilkinson & Pickett, 2006).

The largest disparity in suicide mortality between formerly incarcerated individuals and the general population was observed in counties with low racial diversity, where formerly incarcerated people died by suicide at more than three times the rate of the general population. Prior literature suggests that structural racism embedded in residential segregation and community surveillance practices may exacerbate post-release disadvantage (Brayne, 2014; LaVeist et al., 2011; Massey & Denton, 1989). In more racially homogeneous, minoritized counties, the effects of over-policing, surveillance, and limited cultural competency in service delivery may further isolate formerly incarcerated individuals (M. K. Chen et al., 2022; English et al., 2014; Thomas Tobin et al., 2023; Wendel et al., 2022).

Suicide mortality rates were also higher in counties with a larger proportion of older adults. Although few formerly incarcerated people were themselves older adults, these counties may reflect structural characteristics—such as lower labor force participation, fewer community reintegration programs, and higher concentrations of fixed-income households—that create barriers to reentry and mental health care (Brenes et al., 2015; Maestas et al., 2023; Novisky et al., 2025). These environments may also be characterized by greater social isolation, a higher prevalence of chronic illness, and limited availability of age-integrated community supports, all of which can contribute to elevated suicide risk (Conwell et al., 2011).

Our analyses are limited by incomplete ascertainment of suicide deaths among formerly incarcerated individuals who migrated out of North Carolina following release, as mortality data were restricted to in-state deaths. Because suicides occurring outside North Carolina were not captured, even though these individuals contributed person-time to the analysis, our estimates may be underestimation of suicide risk among formerly incarcerated people released from incarceration in NC. However, if migration after release is associated with underlying mental health status, social integration, or economic opportunity, this could result in selection bias. Individuals who remain in North Carolina may experience greater barriers to reintegration, including persistent stigma and limited access to supportive resources, whereas those who relocate may have different risk profiles. Future research that incorporates post-release residential mobility and captures out-of-state mortality would be important for more fully characterizing these dynamics and improving the accuracy of suicide risk estimates in this population.

Unlike studies focused solely on individual-level factors, this research provides evidence that population-level conditions—such as educational opportunity, economic structure, racial demographics, and rurality—interact with incarceration history to shape vulnerability to suicide. Nevertheless, there are limitations. County-level SDOH data may mask within-county variation and fail to capture neighborhood-level structural inequities. To address disparities in suicide mortality between formerly incarcerated individuals and the general population, policy efforts should invest in place-based mental health infrastructure, especially in rural and low-diversity counties with limited behavioral health capacity. Along with expanding community reentry programs, public campaigns aimed at reducing stigma and promoting reintegration should be tailored to address the needs of communities with high reentry volume but limited acceptance of formerly incarcerated individuals. Overall, this study demonstrates that formerly incarcerated individuals in North Carolina experience disproportionately high rates of suicide mortality, with significant variation depending on the structural conditions of the communities to which they return.
