Rethinking Efficient Mixture-of-Experts for Remote Sensing Modality-Missing Classification
Qinghao Gao, Jiahui Qu, Yunsong Li, Wenqian Dong

Abstract
Multimodal classification in remote sensing often suffers from missing modalities caused by environmental interference, sensor failures, or atmospheric effects, which severely degrade classification performance. Existing two-stage adaptation methods are computationally expensive and assume complete multimodal data during training, limiting their generalization to real-world incompleteness. To overcome these issues, we propose a Missing-aware Mixture-of-Loras (MaMOL) framework that reformulates modality missing as a multi-task learning problem. MaMOL introduces a dual-routing mechanism: a task-oriented dynamic router that adaptively activates experts for different missing patterns, and a modality-specific-shared static router that maintains stable cross-modal knowledge sharing. Unlike prior methods that train separate networks for each missing configuration, MaMOL achieves parameter-efficient adaptation via lightweight expert updates and shared expert reuse. Experiments on multiple remote sensing benchmarks demonstrate superior robustness and generalization under varying missing rates, with minimal computational overhead. Moreover, transfer experiments on natural image datasets validate its scalability and cross-domain applicability, highlighting MaMOL as a general and efficient solution for incomplete multimodal learning.

Introduction

In the field of remote sensing, multimodal data such as optical imagery, SAR, LiDAR, and hyperspectral data are widely applied to tasks including land-cover classification, change detection, and obeject detection, since different modalities often provide complementary information and thus make joint modeling highly valuable
Liu et al. 2025a
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
;
Wei et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025a
. However, in real-world applications, the completeness of multimodal data is difficult to guarantee
Yang et al. 2025
;
Huang et al. 2024
. For instance, optical imagery is frequently affected by clouds and atmospheric conditions, hyperspectral data are costly to acquire with limited coverage, LiDAR data may be unavailable in mountainous or disaster-stricken regions, and sensor failures can further result in incomplete observations. Such modality incompleteness not only increases the complexity of model training but also leads to significant degradation in classification accuracy and generalization ability
Pipoli et al. 2025
;
Ramasinghe et al. 2024
.

To address modality missing, existing studies have primarily employed methods such as modality completion or learning from incomplete information, which have achieved certain success in remote sensing classification tasks
Chen et al. 2024b
;
Wei et al. 2023a
;
Wei et al. 2023b
. However, most of these methods rely on a pretrained multimodal framework followed by a second-stage adaptation: additional training or parameter updates are typically required after pretraining to mitigate the performance drop caused by missing modalities. As illustrated in Fig.
1
, this paradigm suffers from two main limitations: (1) the two-stage retraining incurs considerable computational and storage overhead, lacking an effective mechanism to reduce adaptation costs; and (2) the training process typically assumes complete modalities and only handles missing modalities at inference time, failing to model the inherent incompleteness of real-world scenarios from the source. Consequently, its robustness and practicality remain limited in real remote sensing applications.

In multimodal natural image and natural language processing tasks, fine-tuning large-scale foundation models has been shown to efficiently handle modality-missing scenarios during training
Lee et al. 2023
;
Hu et al. 2024
. These approaches often construct missing-aware prompts under different missing cases to fine-tune the large models with low-parameter adaptation. For example, when the text modality is missing, a text-missing-aware prompt can be concatenated with the input. Inspired by this, we ask:
Can multiple modality-missing scenarios be unified as a multi-task learning problem?
However, in prompt-based methods, whether static or dynamically generated, the number of prompt configurations grows exponentially with the number of modalities, while interactive designs tend to introduce additional branches and substantially increase network complexity. Compared with explicit completion or prompt learning, we therefore reconsider whether a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) mechanism can directly model modality missing at the input level, thereby achieving a more robust solution.

In this work, we propose a Missing-aware Mixture-of-Loras (MaMOL) module, which internally models dynamic modality-missing scenarios through a mixture-of-experts mechanism. Specifically, MaMOL activates task-oriented missing-aware dynamic routing and modality-specific–shared static routing for different missing cases. Dynamic routing enables the model to flexibly adapt to diverse missing patterns, while static routing ensures stable cross-modality knowledge sharing. Their synergy allows the model to adaptively handle locally missing information while maintaining global consistency. Under different input conditions, MaMOL automatically selects the most appropriate expert branches, effectively avoiding the amplification of information gaps caused by modality missing. Compared with methods that design dedicated network structures for each missing scenario, MaMOL unifies different modality-missing cases into an expert-selection problem, thereby significantly improving the efficiency and scalability of both training and inference. In this way, the modality-missing problem is reframed from “how to recover missing information” to “how to efficiently select and combine experts,” which greatly enhances task adaptability and robustness.

Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We first propose a multi-task learning perspective to address generalized modality-missing problems and introduce a novel missing-aware MoE paradigm applicable to remote sensing modality-missing classification tasks.

•

We design a Missing-aware Mixture-of-Loras (MaMOL), where task-oriented dynamic experts and modality-specific–shared static experts are activated to effectively address diverse modality-missing scenarios with only lightweight parameter updates.

•

We validate the robustness of different MoE variants on multiple public remote sensing classification datasets and further extend our approach to natural image tasks, demonstrating strong generalization capability.

Problem definition.

We investigate multimodal classification in remote sensing, where data are collected from multiple complementary sensors. In general, a remote sensing system may provide
M
heterogeneous modalities such as optical, SAR, hyperspectral, or LiDAR. For clarity of notation, we describe the formulation with two representative modalities
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
(e.g., hyperspectral and SAR), which can be easily extended to more. To preserve the unified format of multimodal inputs, we introduce learnable dummy inputs
\tilde{x}^{m_{1}}
and
\tilde{x}^{m_{2}}
to represent the missing modalities. Accordingly, the dataset can be expressed as
{\mathcal{D}}=\{\mathcal{D}^{c},{\mathcal{D}}^{m_{1}},{\mathcal{D}}^{m_{2}}\}
, where
\mathcal{D}^{c}=\{x^{m_{1}},x^{m_{2}},y\}
denotes the modality-complete samples, and
{\mathcal{D}}^{m_{1}}=\{x^{m_{1}},\tilde{x}^{m_{2}},y\},{\mathcal{D}}^{m_{2}}=\{\tilde{x}^{m_{1}},x^{m_{2}},y\}
represent the samples with one missing modality (e.g., missing SAR or missing hyperspectral observations). Here, the dummy inputs serve as placeholders to maintain the same input dimension across different missing patterns. The objective is to learn a unified model
f_{\theta}
that predicts the class label
\hat{y}=f_{\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
robustly under arbitrary missing-modality configurations without retraining or designing separate models for each case.

3.1
Overall Framework

As illustrated in Fig.
2
, the proposed framework aims to achieve unified and efficient multimodal classification under arbitrary modality-missing configurations. Given multimodal inputs
\mathbf{x}=\{x^{m_{1}},x^{m_{2}},\dots,x^{m_{M}}\}
, where some modalities may be absent, each available modality is first encoded into a modality-specific embedding space:

\mathbf{h}^{(m)}=\mathcal{E}^{(m)}(x^{(m)}),\quad m\in\{1,\dots,M\}.

(1)

For each image modality, a pretrained Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone is employed as the encoder to extract semantic embeddings. To adapt the model to modality-missing scenarios with minimal training cost, the backbone parameters are frozen during training, and only lightweight adaptation layers are introduced. On top of the frozen backbone, we propose a
Missing-aware Mixture-of-Loras (MaMOL)
architecture that enables flexible expert selection conditioned on the missing pattern. Specifically, we integrate low-rank adaptation (LoRA) experts into the feed-forward layers of several transformer blocks. Each expert learns modality-aware residuals that adjust the frozen features toward the current input configuration.

To further enhance the adaptability of the LoRA-based architecture, we decompose the expert modules into two complementary categories:
Dynamic experts
and
Static experts
. The dynamic experts are composed of a group of pattern experts, which are selectively activated according to the specific modality-missing pattern. They capture the distinct adaptation behavior of each missing configuration, allowing the model to view different modality-missing cases as related subtasks within a unified framework. In contrast, the static experts consist of two subtypes: shared experts and modality-specialized experts. The shared experts encode general cross-modal representations that remain stable across all patterns, while the modality-specialized experts provide fine-grained residuals that preserve the priors of individual modalities and stabilize feature alignment when certain modalities are absent.

3.2
Missing-aware Mixture-of-Loras

Building upon the frozen transformer backbone, we design the MaMOL module to enable efficient and adaptive feature modulation under varying modality-missing conditions. Each expert is implemented as a low-rank LoRA module inserted into the feed-forward sublayer of the backbone transformer, producing a residual adaptation conditioned on the input feature
\mathbf{z}
. We denote the aggregated LoRA residual as

\Delta\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{dyn}}+\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{stat}},

(2)

where
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{dyn}}
and
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{stat}}
denote the outputs of the dynamic and static expert branches, respectively. The aggregated LoRA residual
\Delta\mathbf{h}
is then injected into the frozen transformer layer to refine its hidden representation:

\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{layer}}=\mathrm{LayerNorm}\big(\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{frozen}}+\Delta\mathbf{h}\big),

(3)

where
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{frozen}}
represents the original output of the frozen feed-forward block. The updated representation
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{layer}}
is then propagated to subsequent layers or the classification head to produce the final prediction
\hat{y}=f_{\mathrm{cls}}(\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{layer}})
.

In addition, to investigate whether the modality-missing problem can be regarded as a form of multi-task learning, we further explore several representative MoE variants for comparison. As illustrated in Fig.
3
, architectures are considered: the Baseline Feed-Forward network without experts, the Adapt-Based MoE that adapts parameters via lightweight adaptation branches, the Task-Driven MoE that explicitly routes features based on task embeddings, and our proposed MaMOL that performs low-rank, pattern-aware adaptation. Figure
3
provides an overview of their structural differences and routing mechanisms. This comparative design allows us to rethink whether efficient MoE architectures are truly promising for remote sensing modality-missing classification tasks. By doing so, we aim to understand whether modality-missing learning can be effectively modeled as a multi-task adaptation problem.

3.3
Dynamic experts

To achieve task-aware adaptation under varying modality-missing conditions, we design a Dynamic Mixture-of-Experts mechanism that adaptively selects specialized experts based on both the observed modality configuration and the semantic content of the input. This design allows the model to
regard each missing-modality pattern as a distinct yet related subtask
within a unified learning framework.

A standard MoE layer comprises a set of
N
experts
\{f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{N}\}
and a router
R
responsible for determining which experts should be activated. In our setting, each expert corresponds to a
pattern expert
that captures the characteristic feature transformation required for a specific missing pattern. The router
R
sparsely activates only the most relevant experts to balance model expressiveness and efficiency. Formally, given an input representation
\mathbf{z}
, the output of the MoE layer is defined as:

\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{dyn}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}g_{t}^{(k)}f_{k}(\mathbf{z}),

(4)

where
g_{t}^{(k)}
denotes the gating weight of the
k
-th expert obtained from the router, and each expert function
f_{k}(\mathbf{z})
follows a low-rank adaptation form:

f_{k}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{B}_{d}^{(k)}\mathbf{A}_{d}^{(k)}\mathbf{z},

(5)

where
\mathbf{A}_{d}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
and
\mathbf{B}_{d}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}
are the trainable low-rank matrices (
r\ll d
) of the
k
-th dynamic expert.

Task-aware Routing.

Unlike conventional MoE layers that depend solely on input features, our dynamic router explicitly incorporates both the
modality-missing type
and the
visual content
for gating.
Specifically, we design the router as:

g_{t}=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(W_{t}f_{t}([\mathbf{z};\mathbf{m}_{\text{type}}])\right),

(6)

where
f_{t}(\cdot)
is a lightweight projection network,
W_{t}
is a trainable routing matrix, and
\mathbf{m}_{\text{type}}
encodes the missing-modality configuration. During training, only the top-
K
experts with the highest gating weights
g_{t}^{(k)}
are selected, ensuring sparse activation and efficient computation.

Through this task-aware gating, the dynamic experts learn to specialize in different missing patterns while maintaining smooth knowledge sharing across related tasks. This enables the model to perform flexible and robust adaptation without retraining for each missing configuration.

3.4
Static experts

To ensure stable and consistent feature alignment across diverse missing-modality configurations, we introduce Static Experts that complement the pattern-aware dynamic experts. While dynamic experts specialize in adapting to specific missing patterns, static experts preserve modality-invariant representations and provide reliable residuals that maintain knowledge continuity when certain modalities are absent.

The static expert module consists of two complementary subtypes:
Shared experts
that learn cross-modal representations common to all modality configurations, and
Modality-specific experts
that encode modality-dependent priors to stabilize adaptation under incomplete inputs. Formally, the aggregated static output can be expressed as:

\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{stat}}=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{s}}s^{(j)}E_{s}^{(j)}(\mathbf{z}),

(7)

where
E_{s}^{(j)}
denotes the
j
-th static expert, and
s^{(j)}
are fixed coefficients determined by the corresponding modality or missing type. Each static expert
E_{s}^{(j)}
is implemented as a LoRA module inserted into the feed-forward sublayer of the transformer. The shared and modality-specific experts correspond to different LoRA parameter sets
\{\mathbf{A}_{s}^{(j)},\mathbf{B}_{s}^{(j)}\}
. Their aggregated residual is injected into the frozen layer as:

\Delta\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{stat}}=\sum_{j=1}^{K_{s}}s^{(j)}\mathbf{B}_{s}^{(j)}\mathbf{A}_{s}^{(j)}\mathbf{z}.

(8)

Together with the dynamic experts, the static experts form a balanced architecture that jointly achieves task-adaptive flexibility and representation stability, providing a robust foundation for modality-missing multimodal classification.

Experiments

4.1
Experimental Setup

Datasets Description

We evaluate MaMOL on three publicly available multimodal remote sensing datasets. Houston2013
Contest 2013
provides hyperspectral (144 bands) and LiDAR data with 15 classes over the University of Houston campus. Augsburg
Baumgartner et al. 2012
includes hyperspectral (180 bands), SAR (4 polarization features), and LiDAR data from Augsburg, Germany, with 7 classes. Trento
Rasti et al. 2017
contains co-registered hyperspectral (63 bands) and LiDAR data acquired over an agricultural area near Trento, Italy, covering 6 land-cover classes.

Metrics

We evaluate our method using Overall Accuracy (OA) and kappa across all datasets. OA measures the ratio of correctly predicted samples to the total number of samples, while kappa evaluates the agreement between predicted and true labels by accounting for the possibility of random chance, thus offering a more robust measure of classification reliability.

Implementation details

We adopt CLIP
Radford et al. 2021
as our multimodal backbone, with the ViT-B/16 as the visual encoder. During training, we freeze all parameters of the encoders and only train the modality projection layers, the lightweight expert modules, and the fully connected (FC) layer for the target task. The number of dynamic experts is set to 2, with top-
k=1
expert selected, and these experts are appended to the features of 6 designated Transformer layers. We use the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of
2\times 10^{-3}
and a weight decay of
2\times 10^{-2}
. The learning rate is linearly warmed up over the first 10% of the total training steps and then linearly decayed to zero. All experiments are conducted on 2 NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs with a batch size of 128. In the case of missing modalities, we directly replace the corresponding encoder output with a zero-filled tensor.

Setating of Missing Modality

In this work, we consider realistic multimodal remote sensing scenarios, where the availability of different modalities may vary during both training and testing. To emulate such conditions, we define the
missing rate

\eta
as the ratio of modality-incomplete samples to the total number of samples, following the general formulation in MMP
Lee et al. 2023
. Depending on which modalities are absent, several missing configurations can occur, including single-modality absence (only LiDAR missing). In the case of two missing modalities, the dataset is composed of
\frac{\eta}{2}
samples missing the first modality,
\frac{\eta}{2}
samples missing the second modality, and
(1-\eta)
complete samples. For single-modality missing situations,
\eta
of the data lacks one modality, while the remaining
(1-\eta)
samples retain all modalities. This formulation can be naturally generalized to datasets with
M
modalities, where each missing configuration occupies
(\frac{\eta}{M^{2}-2})
of the data, and
(1-\eta)
remains modality-complete.

Setating of MOE-based Method

We construct several MoE-based variants upon the ViLT baseline to investigate the robustness of expert integration strategies under modality-missing conditions. Specifically, we adjust the forward network using three existing paradigms: replacement-based MoE
Rajbhandari et al. 2022
that substitutes missing modality representations with expert-generated estimations; adapter-based MoE
Maidacundo 2024

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose Missing-aware Mixture-of-Loras(MaMOL), a unified and efficient framework for multimodal classification under modality-missing conditions. MaMOL reformulates modality missing as an expert selection problem from a mixture-of-experts perspective. By combining task-oriented dynamic experts and modality-specific–shared static experts, it enables flexible adaptation to diverse missing patterns while maintaining cross-modal consistency. Extensive experiments on multiple remote sensing datasets demonstrate the superior performance and robustness of MaMOL and other MoE-based variants under various missing rates and modality combinations. MaMOL also generalizes well to more complex three-modality scenarios and even to natural image tasks, showing strong scalability and cross-domain generalization. Overall, MaMOL offers a lightweight, robust, and extensible solution for real-world multimodal incompleteness, providing new insights toward building missing-aware and generalizable multimodal learning systems.
