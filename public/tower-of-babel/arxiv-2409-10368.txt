On the tensorization of the variational distance
Aryeh Kontorovich

Abstract
If one seeks to estimate the total variation between two product measures ‖ P 1 : n ⊗ − Q 1 : n ⊗ ‖ TV \left\|P^{\otimes}_{1:n}-Q^{\otimes}_{1:n}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}} in terms of their marginal TV sequence 𝜹 = ( ‖ P 1 − Q 1 ‖ TV , ‖ P 2 − Q 2 ‖ TV , … , ‖ P n − Q n ‖ TV ) \boldsymbol{\delta}=\left(\left\|P_{1}-Q_{1}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}},\left\|P_{2}-Q_{2}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}},\ldots,\left\|P_{n}-Q_{n}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}\right) , then trivial upper and lower bounds are provided by ‖ 𝜹 ‖ ∞ ≤ ‖ P 1 : n ⊗ − Q 1 : n ⊗ ‖ TV ≤ ‖ 𝜹 ‖ 1 \left\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|P^{\otimes}_{1:n}-Q^{\otimes}_{1:n}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\right\|_{1} . We improve the lower bound to ‖ 𝜹 ‖ 2 ≲ ‖ P 1 : n ⊗ − Q 1 : n ⊗ ‖ TV \left\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\right\|_{2}\lesssim\left\|P^{\otimes}_{1:n}-Q^{\otimes}_{1:n}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}} , thereby reducing the gap between the upper and lower bounds from ∼ n \sim n to ∼ n \sim\sqrt{n} . Furthermore, we show that any estimate on ‖ P 1 : n ⊗ − Q 1 : n ⊗ ‖ TV \left\|P^{\otimes}_{1:n}-Q^{\otimes}_{1:n}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}} expressed in terms of 𝜹 \boldsymbol{\delta} must necessarily exhibit a gap of ∼ n \sim\sqrt{n} between the upper and lower bounds in the worst case, establishing a sense in which our estimate is optimal. Finally, we identify a natural class of distributions for which ‖ 𝜹 ‖ 2 \left\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\right\|_{2} approximates the TV distance up to absolute multiplicative constants.

Introduction

Combining several probability distributions into a single
product measure is one of the most basic operations in probability
theory.
Among the myriad notions of distance on distributions,
the total variation metric occupies an amply motivated central role
(
Devroye and Lugosi, 2001
;
Gibbs and Su, 2002
)
. It is therefore
of fundamental importance to understand the basic ways in which
these two objects interact: how do product measures behave
under the variational metric? Perhaps surprisingly, our
understanding of this basic problem is still somewhat rudimentary
and although considerable progress has been made, natural and
challenging
open questions remain.

We will restrict our discussion to distributions on finite sets,
since for the kind of problems we study, these already capture the general case
(
Polyanskiy and Wu, 2024
, Theorem 7.6)
.
If
P,P^{\prime}
are distributions on
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}
,
then
P\otimes P^{\prime}
is the product measure on
\Omega\times\Omega^{\prime}

given by
(P\otimes P^{\prime})(\omega,\omega)=P(\omega)P^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})
. For families of distributions
P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}
,
we
write

P^{\otimes}_{1:n}=P_{1}\otimes P_{2}\otimes\ldots\otimes P_{n}
.
Our central object of interest is
the total variation distance between two product measures:

\left\|P^{\otimes}_{1:n}-Q^{\otimes}_{1:n}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}
.
The form

\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1}

(see (
1
))
suggests
that the complexity of computing
this
distance scales linearly with the support size.
However, in the interesting case
of product measures,
the
support size
will grow exponentially with
n
,
and hence direct computation will be intractable.

Polyanskiy and Wu (2024, p. 124)

note that
“computing the total variation between two
n
-fold product distributions is typically difficult”, which motivates
various approximations by surrogate distances; more on this below.

Turning for the moment to algorithmic aspects,

Bhattacharyya et al. (2023)

showed
even for support size
2
,
the general problem of computing the variational distance
between two
n
-fold product measures exactly
is
computationally hard
in the
\#
P-complete sense.

Feng et al. (2023)
gave an efficient randomized algorithm for obtaining a
1\pm\varepsilon

multiplicative approximation with confidence
\delta
, in time
O(\frac{n^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta})
,
which was later derandomized by

Feng et al. (2024)
.
A novel connection between TV distance estimation and probabilistic inference was recently established by
Bhattacharyya et al. (2024)
.

Notation.

\Omega
will always denote a finite set.
We will lighten notation by writing
P(\omega)=P(\left\{\omega\right\})

for
distributions

P
on
\Omega

and

\omega\in\Omega
.
For two distributions
P,Q
on

\Omega
, their total variation
distance is defined by

\displaystyle\left\|P-Q\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}|P(\omega)-Q(\omega)|.

(1)

We write
[n]:=\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}

and use
standard vector norm notation

\left\|x\right\|_{r}^{r}=\sum_{i\in[n]}|x_{i}|^{r}

and
\left\|x\right\|_{\infty}=\max_{i\in[n]}|x_{i}|

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For
p\in[0,1]
,

\operatorname{Ber}(p)

denotes
the Bernoulli measure on
\left\{0,1\right\}
:

\operatorname{Ber}(p)(0)=1-p

and

\operatorname{Ber}(p)(1)=p
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}
,

\operatorname{Ber}(p)
denotes
the product of
n
Bernoulli distributions with parameters
p_{i}
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ber}(p)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ber}(p_{1})\otimes\operatorname{Ber}(p_{2})\otimes\ldots\otimes\operatorname{Ber}(p_{n}).

(2)

For two families of distributions,

P_{i\in[n]}

and

Q_{i\in[n]}
,
we define their marginal total variation
sequence
by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}(P_{i\in[n]},Q_{i\in[n]})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\left\|P_{1}-Q_{1}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}},\left\|P_{2}-Q_{2}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}},\ldots,\left\|P_{n}-Q_{n}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(3)

Tensorization
generally refers to
analyzing a complex, high-dimensional object in terms of its
simpler
low-dimensional components. In our context, it means
estimating

\left\|P^{\otimes}_{1:n}-Q^{\otimes}_{1:n}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}

in terms of the
marginal TV sequence

\boldsymbol{\delta}=\boldsymbol{\delta}(P_{i\in[n]},Q_{i\in[n]})
.
A trivial bound of this form is

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\right\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;\left\|P^{\otimes}_{1:n}-Q^{\otimes}_{1:n}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}\;\leq\;\min\left\{1,\left\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\right\|_{1}\right\},

(4)

where the lower bound follows from the
data processing inequality (Lemma
1
)
and the upper bound is an immediate consequence of
the coupling
estimate

\left\|P\otimes P^{\prime}-Q\otimes Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}\leq\left\|P-Q\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}+\left\|P^{\prime}-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}-\left\|P-Q\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}\left\|P^{\prime}-Q^{\prime}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}

(
Kontorovich, 2012
, Lemma 2.2)
.
Each of the bounds in (
4
) is tight in the sense
of being achievable, but there is a factor of
n
gap between the upper and lower.

Our main result is the following sharpening of (
4
):

Theorem 1
.

For two families of distributions,

P_{i\in[n]}

and

Q_{i\in[n]}
,
and
their marginal total variation
sequence

\boldsymbol{\delta}=\boldsymbol{\delta}(P_{i\in[n]},Q_{i\in[n]})

defined in
(
3
),
we have

\displaystyle\left\|P^{\otimes}_{1:n}-Q^{\otimes}_{1:n}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c\min\left\{1,\left\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\right\|_{2}\right\},

where
c>0.1798

is a universal constant.

Since
\left\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\right\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{n}\left\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\right\|_{2}
,
Theorem
1
improves the gap between the upper
and lower bounds in (
4
) from
n
to
\sqrt{n}
.
It turns out that this is the best one can do in terms of
the
marginal total variation sequence:

Theorem 2
.

For every
n\geq 1
, there exist

p,p^{\prime},q,q^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{n}

such that

\left\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\right\|_{2}=\left\|p-q\right\|_{2}=\left\|p^{\prime}-q^{\prime}\right\|_{2}
,
while

\displaystyle\frac{\left\|\operatorname{Ber}(p)-\operatorname{Ber}(q)\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}}{\left\|\operatorname{Ber}(p^{\prime})-\operatorname{Ber}(q^{\prime})\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c\sqrt{n},

where
c>0
is an absolute constant.

We are able to pinpoint the precise step in the proof
where
the
\sqrt{n}
gap between the upper and lower bounds
is introduced: namely, in the use of symmetrization
(Lemma
3
)
to reduce
the analysis of

\left\|\operatorname{Ber}(p)-\operatorname{Ber}(q)\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}

from the case of general
p,q\in[0,1]^{n}

to the symmetric special case where
p=1-q
.
As our next result shows, in the symmetric case,
the gap disappears:

Theorem 3
.

For
p\in[0,1]^{n}

and
q=1-p
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\operatorname{Ber}(p)-\operatorname{Ber}(q)\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|p-q\right\|_{2}.

Open problem.

In light of the hardness result of

Bhattacharyya et al. (2023)
,
we do not expect a simple, analytically tractable
formula
(or even just an efficient algorithm)
for computing

\left\|\operatorname{Ber}(p)-\operatorname{Ber}(q)\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}

exactly.
An efficient algorithm for approximating TV to arbitrary
precision was recently obtained by
Feng et al. (2024)
.
We ask whether there are simple, analytically tractable
upper and lower bounds
\operatorname{UB}(p,q),\operatorname{LB}(p,q)

whose ratio is bounded by some absolute constant independent
of
n
,
p
, and
q
.

Related work.

Gibbs and Su (2002)
discuss a number of important distance functions over probability measures
commonly used in statistics and probability
and their relation to the total variation metric.
In
information theory and
statistics,
KL-divergence and the Hellinger distance
are frequently used as surrogates for the latter.
These are defined, respectively, by

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(P||Q\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}P(\omega)\log\frac{P(\omega)}{Q(\omega)},

\displaystyle H^{2}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\left(\sqrt{P(\omega)}-\sqrt{Q(\omega)}\right)^{2},

and feature the convenient property of tensorizing,
in the sense of

D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(P^{\otimes}_{1:n}||Q^{\otimes}_{1:n}\right)

and

H^{2}(P^{\otimes}_{1:n},Q^{\otimes}_{1:n})

both being easily
expressible
in terms of
the component

D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(P_{i}||Q_{i}\right)

and

H^{2}(P_{i},Q_{i})
,
respectively. The following relations between these
distance measures and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}

are known
(
Polyanskiy and Wu, 2024
, Eq.((7.22))
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}H^{2}(P,Q)\;\leq\;\left\|P-Q\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}\;\leq\;H(P,Q)\sqrt{1-\frac{H^{2}(P,Q)}{4}},

(5)

and

(
Even-Dar et al., 2007
, Lemma 3.10)
,

(
Polyanskiy and Wu, 2024
, Theorem 7.10)
:

\displaystyle\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(P||Q\right)}{2\log P_{\min}}\;\leq\;\left\|P-Q\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}\;\leq\;\sqrt{\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(P||Q\right)}{2}},

(6)

where

P_{\min}=\min_{\omega\in\Omega}P(\omega)

and
P_{\min}<\frac{1}{2}
is assumed.
See

Sason and Verdú (2016)

for additional relationships between the various
f
-divergences,
of which

D_{\mathrm{KL}}

and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}

are special cases,
and

Cohen et al. (2023)

for bounds involving
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}
and moments of information.

Although the bounds
in (
5
) and (
6
)
are useful for various applications,
there is a clear
sense in which
these are inferior to
(
4
).
If we express generic lower and upper bounds as

\operatorname{LB}(P,Q)\leq\left\|P-Q\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}\leq\operatorname{UB}(P,Q)
,
we say that these have the bounded-ratio property if

\displaystyle\sup_{P,Q}\frac{\operatorname{UB}(P,Q)}{\operatorname{LB}(P,Q)}<\infty.

(7)

Then (
4
) satisfies this property with a ratio bound of
n
,
while neither of (
5
), (
6
)
does.

Proofs

2.1
Scheffé’s identity,
data processing, reduction to Bernoulli

We will make
repeated
use of
Scheffé’s identity

(
Tsybakov, 2009
, Lemma 2.1)
:

\displaystyle\left\|P-Q\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}\;=\;\max_{A\subset\Omega}P(A)-Q(A)\;=\;1-\left\|\min\left\{P,Q\right\}\right\|_{1},

(8)

where

\left\|\min\left\{P,Q\right\}\right\|_{1}=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\min\left\{P(\omega),Q(\omega)\right\}
.

A general form of the data processing inequality for
f
-divergences
may be found in

Polyanskiy and Wu (2024, Theorem 7.4)
. Since total variation
is a special case (Eq. (7.3) ibid.), we restrict our statement to TV.

Lemma 1

(Data processing inequality)
.

Let
T
be a Markov operator mapping distributions to distributions.
Then, for all distributions
P,Q
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|T(P)-T(Q)\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|P-Q\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}.

Lemma 2
.

If Theorem
1
holds for
the special case
where

P=\operatorname{Ber}(p)
,
Q=\operatorname{Ber}(q)

for

p,q\in[0,1]^{n}
,
then it
holds in the general case.

Proof.

Let
P_{i}
and
Q_{i}
be distributions over
\Omega_{i}
,

i\in[n]
.
For each
i\in[n]
, define

A_{i}=\left\{\omega\in\Omega_{i}:P_{i}(\omega)>Q_{i}(\omega)\right\}

and

p_{i}=P_{i}(A_{i})
,

q_{i}=Q_{i}(A_{i})
.
Clearly, there is a Markov operator
T_{i}
that maps

P_{i}
and
Q_{i}
to
\operatorname{Ber}(p_{i})
and
\operatorname{Ber}(q_{i})
, respectively
(by collapsing the states in
A_{i}
into a single state and doing the
same for the complement). Applying all of the
T_{i}
s simultaneously yields
a Markov operator
T
mapping

P^{\otimes}_{1:n}

and

Q^{\otimes}_{1:n}

to

\operatorname{Ber}(p)

and

\operatorname{Ber}(q)
,
respectively, where

p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})

and

q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})
. It follows from
Lemma
1
that

\left\|P^{\otimes}_{1:n}-Q^{\otimes}_{1:n}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}\geq\left\|\operatorname{Ber}(p)-\operatorname{Ber}(q)\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}
.
On the other hand,
by
(
8
),

\left\|P_{i}-Q_{i}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}=|p_{i}-q_{i}|
,
and so

\boldsymbol{\delta}(P^{\otimes}_{1:n},Q^{\otimes}_{1:n})=\boldsymbol{\delta}(\operatorname{Ber}(p),\operatorname{Ber}(p))=(|p_{1}-q_{1}|,\ldots,|p_{n}-q_{n}|)
,
where
\boldsymbol{\delta}

is
the
marginal total variation sequence defined
in (
3
).

∎

2.2
Symmetrization

A key step in our proof
reduces the problem of lower-bounding

\left\|\operatorname{Ber}(p)-\operatorname{Ber}(q)\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}

in terms of
\left\|p-q\right\|_{2}

for general
p,q\in[0,1]^{n}

to the

symmetric

special case
where
p_{i}=1-q_{i}
,
incurring a loss of
only a factor of
2
.

Lemma 3

(symmetrization
(
Steinke, 2024
)
)
.

For
p,q\in[0,1]^{n}
, define

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{i}=\frac{|p_{i}-q_{i}|}{1+|p_{i}+q_{i}-1|},\quad\hat{p}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\hat{\gamma}_{i}}{2},\quad\hat{q}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\hat{\gamma}_{i}}{2},\qquad i\in[n].

(9)

Then

\displaystyle\left|\hat{p}_{i}-\hat{q}_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left|p_{i}-q_{i}\right|,\qquad i\in[n];

(10)

\displaystyle\left\|\operatorname{Ber}(p)-\operatorname{Ber}(q)\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left\|\operatorname{Ber}(\hat{p})-\operatorname{Ber}(\hat{q})\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}.

(11)

Proof.

There is no loss of generality in assuming
p_{i}>q_{i}
,
since the
p_{i}=q_{i}
case is trivial, and if
p_{i}<q_{i}
,
we can apply the transformation
p_{i}\mapsto 1-p_{i}
,
q_{i}\mapsto 1-q_{i}
,
which leaves both
|p_{i}-q_{i}|

and

\left\|\operatorname{Ber}(p)-\operatorname{Ber}(q)\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}
unchanged.
We will use the notation
X\sim P

to denote a random variable
X
distributed according to the law
P
.
We proceed to construct a
randomized
function
F_{p,q}:\left\{0,1\right\}\to\left\{0,1\right\}

with the following property: for any
p,q\in[0,1]
,
if

X\sim\operatorname{Ber}(p)

then

F_{p,q}(X)\sim\operatorname{Ber}(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2})

and
if

Y\sim\operatorname{Ber}(q)

then

F_{p,q}(Y)\sim\operatorname{Ber}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2})
,
where

\gamma=\frac{|p-q|}{1+|p+q-1|}.

We define
F_{p,q}

by its stochastic matrix

\displaystyle M=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{P}(F_{p,q}(1)=1)&\mathbb{P}(F_{p,q}(1)=0)\\
\mathbb{P}(F_{p,q}(0)=1)&\mathbb{P}(F_{p,q}(0)=0)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{p-q}\frac{2-p-q}{2}&\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{p-q}\frac{2-p-q}{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{p-q}\frac{p+q}{2}&\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{p-q}\frac{p+q}{2}\end{array}\right).

To verify that
F_{p,q}
is a valid randomized map, we must check that
M
is indeed a stochastic matrix. Its rows obviously sum to
1
and its entries are non-negatives, since

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{p-q}\frac{2-p-q}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{2-p-q}{1+|1-p-q|}\leq\frac{1}{2};

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{p-q}\frac{p+q}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{p+q}{1+|p+q-1|}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Next, we verify
that for
X\sim\operatorname{Ber}(p)
,

F_{p,q}(X)\sim\operatorname{Ber}(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2})
,
which boils down to a matrix-vector multiplication:

\displaystyle\big(\mathbb{P}(F(X)=1),\mathbb{P}(F(X)=0)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\mathbb{P}(X=1),\mathbb{P}(X=0)\big)M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(p,1-p\big)M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{p+1-p}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2(p-q)}\big(p(2-p-q)-(1-p)(p+q)\big)\right.,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{p+1-p}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2(p-q)}\big(p(2-p-q)-(1-p)(p+q)\big)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2},\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\right).

A similar calculation shows that

F_{p,q}(Y)\sim\operatorname{Ber}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2})

for

Y\sim\operatorname{Ber}(q)
, as required.

We now extend

F_{p,q}

from a random map
\left\{0,1\right\}\mapsto\left\{0,1\right\}

determined by

p,q\in[0,1]

to a random map
\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}\mapsto\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}

determined by

p,q\in[0,1]^{n}

in the natural way.
Namely,
for

p,q\in[0,1]^{n}
,
if

X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\sim\operatorname{Ber}(p)

and

Y=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})\sim\operatorname{Ber}(q)
,
we define

\hat{X}=(\hat{X}_{1},\ldots,\hat{X}_{n})

by

\hat{X}_{i}=F_{p_{i},q_{i}}(X_{i})

and

\hat{Y}=(\hat{Y}_{1},\ldots,\hat{Y}_{n})

by
\hat{Y}_{i}=F_{p_{i},q_{i}}(Y_{i})
.
Since all of the coordinates are independent, we have

\hat{X}\sim\operatorname{Ber}(\hat{p})

and

\hat{Y}\sim\operatorname{Ber}(\hat{q})
,
with
\hat{p},\hat{q},

as defined in (
9
).
Then (
11
)
follows from
the
data processing inequality (Lemma
1
),
and
(
10
)
from the elementary fact that

\displaystyle\frac{|u-v|}{1+|u+v-1|}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|u-v|,\qquad u,v\in[0,1].

∎

2.3
Proof of Theorem
1

The following
numerical
relations
are elementary to verify:

\displaystyle\min\left\{u,v\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{uv}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left|\log\frac{u}{v}\right|\right),\qquad u,v>0,

(17)

\displaystyle\log\frac{1+x}{1-x}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2x,\qquad x\geq 0,

(18)

\displaystyle 1-\exp(-x)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(2-2{\mathrm{e}}^{-1/2}\right)\min\left\{x,\frac{1}{2}\right\},\qquad x\geq 0.

(19)

By Lemma
2
,
it suffices to prove the claim for

P=\operatorname{Ber}(p)
and
Q=\operatorname{Ber}(q)

with
arbitrary

p,q\in[0,1]^{n}
.
Now
Lemma
3

yields
\hat{p},\hat{q}\in[0,1]^{n}

such that

\hat{q}=1-\hat{p}
,

\left\|\hat{p}-\hat{q}\right\|_{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\|p-q\right\|_{2}
,
and

\left\|\operatorname{Ber}(p)-\operatorname{Ber}(q)\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}\geq\left\|\operatorname{Ber}(\hat{p})-\operatorname{Ber}(\hat{q})\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}
.
Writing

\operatorname{Ber}(\hat{p})=\hat{P}=\hat{P}^{\otimes}_{1:n}

and

\operatorname{Ber}(\hat{q})=\hat{Q}=\hat{Q}^{\otimes}_{1:n}

and invoking
(
8
),
we have

\displaystyle 1-\left\|\hat{P}-\hat{Q}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}}\min\left\{\hat{P}(\omega),\hat{Q}(\omega)\right\}

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{(a)}}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}}\sqrt{\hat{P}(\omega)\hat{Q}(\omega)}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left|\log\frac{\hat{P}(\omega)}{\hat{Q}(\omega)}\right|\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\hat{p}_{i}\hat{q}_{i}}\sum_{\omega\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left|\log\frac{\hat{P}(\omega)}{\hat{Q}(\omega)}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{(b)}}{=}

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}2\sqrt{\hat{p}_{i}(1-\hat{p}_{i})}\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left|\log\frac{\hat{P}(Z)}{\hat{Q}(Z)}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left|\log\frac{\hat{P}(Z)}{\hat{Q}(Z)}\right|\right),

where
(a)
follows
from (
17
)
and
(b) by multiplying each of the two factors
by

2^{n}

and
2^{-n}
, respectively.
Thus,

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{P}-\hat{Q}\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\left\|\min\left\{\hat{P},\hat{Q}\right\}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Z}\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{\hat{P}_{i}(Z_{i})}{\hat{Q}_{i}(Z_{i})}\right|\right)\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{(c)}}{\geq}

\displaystyle 0.4571\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Z}\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\log\frac{\hat{P}_{i}(Z_{i})}{\hat{Q}_{i}(Z_{i})}\right)^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{(d)}}{=}

\displaystyle 0.4571\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\log\frac{\hat{p}_{i}}{\hat{q}_{i}}\right)^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{(e)}}{\geq}

\displaystyle 0.4571\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(2(\hat{p}_{i}-\hat{q}_{i})\right)^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.4571\left[1-\exp\left(-\left\|\hat{p}-\hat{q}\right\|_{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{(f)}}{\geq}

\displaystyle 0.3597\min\left\{\left\|\hat{p}-\hat{q}\right\|_{2},\frac{1}{2}\right\},

where (c) follows by
applying
Lemma
4

to the
concave function

t\mapsto 1-\exp(-t/2)

and the
symmetric random variables

\log\frac{\hat{P}_{i}(Z_{i})}{\hat{Q}_{i}(Z_{i})}
,
(d) by noticing that

\left(\log\frac{P_{i}(Z_{i})}{Q_{i}(Z_{i})}\right)^{2}=\left(\log\frac{\hat{p}_{i}}{\hat{q}_{i}}\right)^{2}

almost surely,
(e) by applying
(
18
)
to

\log\frac{\hat{p}_{i}}{\hat{q}_{i}}=\log\frac{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\hat{\gamma}_{i}}{2}}{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\hat{\gamma}_{i}}{2}}

and (f)
via (
19
).
Since

\left\|\hat{p}-\hat{q}\right\|_{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\|p-q\right\|_{2}
, we are done. ∎

2.4
Proof of Theorem
2

For any
n\geq 1
, put
p=(1/n,1/n,\ldots,1/n)

and
q=(0,0,\ldots,0)
. Then

\left\|\operatorname{Ber}(p)-\operatorname{Ber}(q)\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}=1-(1-1/n)^{n}\geq 1-\mathrm{e}^{-1}>0.63
.
For
p^{\prime},q^{\prime}
, put
\gamma=1/n

and define

p^{\prime}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{2}

and

q^{\prime}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{2}
.
Then by
Theorem
3
,
we have

\left\|\operatorname{Ber}(p^{\prime})-\operatorname{Ber}(q^{\prime})\right\|_{\textup{\tiny{TV}}}\leq\left\|p^{\prime}-q^{\prime}\right\|_{2}=n^{-1/2}
.

\sim\sqrt{n}
gap between

2.5
Proof of Theorem
3

For
p\in[0,1]^{n}

and
q=1-p
,
put

P=\operatorname{Ber}(p)

and

Q=\operatorname{Ber}(q)
.

Kontorovich (2024, Theorem 4)

implies
that

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}}\min\left\{P(\omega),Q(\omega)\right\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{\prod_{i=1}^{n}2\sqrt{p_{i}q_{i}}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\log\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\right)^{2}}\right).

We first
reparametrize

p,q
as

p_{i}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{x_{i}}{2}

and

q_{i}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{x_{i}}{2}

for
x\in[0,1]^{n}
.
Thus, to prove the claim, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle 1-\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{1-x_{i}^{2}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\log\frac{1+x_{i}}{1-x_{i}}\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|x\right\|_{2},\qquad x\in[0,1]^{n}.

Reparametrizing by
t_{i}=\tanh^{-1}x_{i}
,
the equivalent claim is

\displaystyle 1-\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\cosh t_{i}-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tanh^{2}t_{i}},\qquad t_{i}\geq 0.

It suffices to prove the claim for
n=2
, as the general
case will follow the same argument.
To this end, we define

\displaystyle F(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{2}\tanh^{2}t_{i}}+\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{2}\log\cosh t_{i}-\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{2}t_{i}^{2}}\right)-1

and compute

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{1}}F

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{sech}(t_{1})\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{t_{1}^{2}+t_{2}^{2}}}\text{sech}(t_{2})\left(\sqrt{t_{1}^{2}+t_{2}^{2}}\tanh(t_{1})+t_{1}\right)}{\sqrt{t_{1}^{2}+t_{2}^{2}}}+\frac{\tanh(t_{1})\text{sech}(t_{1})}{\sqrt{\tanh^{2}t_{1}+\tanh^{2}t_{2}}}\right),

the latter clearly
nonnegative.
The same holds for
\frac{\partial F}{\partial t_{2}}
,
which implies that
F
is minimized
at
t_{1}=t_{2}=0
. Since

F(0,0)=0
,
the proof is complete.
∎

Appendix A
Auxiliary lemma

The following result was proved by George Lowther,
answering a question we had posed. We provide a proof sketch for completeness.

Lemma 4

(
Lowther (2024)
)
.

Let
X_{i}
,
i\in[n]
, be independent symmetric random variables
and
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
an increasing concave function with
f(0)=0
.
Define the random variables
Y=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right|
and

Z=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}f(Z)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c\mathop{\mathbb{E}}f(Y),

(20)

where
c\leq(1/\sqrt{2}-1/4)^{-1}\approx 2.187

and can conjecturally be improved to
2
.

Proof sketch.

Since the
X_{i}
are symmetric,
the expectations
in (
20
)
are unchanged if each
X_{i}
is
replaced by
X_{i}^{\prime}=\varepsilon_{i}X_{i}
,
where the

\varepsilon_{i}

are
independent
Rademacher random variables
with
\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon_{i}=\pm 1)=1/2
. Then a standard argument
lets us prove (
20
) for
X_{i}^{\prime}

conditionally
X_{i}
,
from which the full claim will follow.
Thus, there is no loss of generality in taking

\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=\pm a_{i})=1/2
for some
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Further, we may assume
\left\|a\right\|_{2}=1
, since
any normalizing factor can be absorbed into
f
.

There is no loss of generality in assuming
f
to be twice
differentiable, in which case we have the well known
integral representation

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\min\left\{x,u\right\}(-f^{\prime\prime}(u))\mathrm{d}u.

Thus, it suffices to consider
f

of the form
f(t)=\min\left\{t,u\right\}
. Since we have assumed

\left\|a\right\|_{2}=1
, the claim has been reduced to

\displaystyle c\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\min\left\{Y,u\right\}\geq f(Z)=\min\left\{Z,u\right\}.

A
straightforward
monotonicity argument shows that the case
u=1
implies all the rest.

It remains to lower-bound

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\min\left\{Y,1\right\}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}Y-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\max\left\{Y-1,0\right\}
.
Khintchine’s inequality with the optimal constant

(
Haagerup, 1981
)
yields
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}Y\geq 1/\sqrt{2}
.
Since
y-1\leq y^{2}/4
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}Y-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\max\left\{Y-1,0\right\}\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[Y^{2}/2]=1/4
.
∎
