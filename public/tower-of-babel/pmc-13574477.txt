Association of inflammatory indices with coronary artery calcium burden in fibromyalgia
Authors: Sabri Onur Çağlar, Serdar Hira, Hilal Çağlar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fibromyalgia (FM) may involve systemic abnormalities in addition to its characteristic nociplastic pain features. This study aimed to investigate the association between routinely available inflammatory indices, particularly the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and coronary artery calcium (CAC) burden in patients with FM. A total of 142 patients with FM and 64 controls were included. Computed tomography-derived CAC scores were categorized as < 100 or ≥ 100, with CAC ≥ 400 examined as an exploratory threshold. Multiple inflammatory indices were evaluated, and multivariable logistic regression models included demographic and cardiovascular risk factors, with additional adjustment for low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and statin use. NLR differed across the 3 groups after Bonferroni correction for multiple testing; pairwise analysis showed a higher NLR in patients with FM and CAC ≥ 100 than in those with CAC < 100. Among the evaluated inflammatory indices, NLR showed the strongest correlation with CAC score (r = 0.371, Bonferroni-adjusted P < .001). After additional adjustment for diabetes mellitus, hypertension, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and statin use, NLR remained associated with CAC ≥ 100 (adjusted odds ratio 3.57, 95% confidence interval 1.15–11.07, P = .028) and CAC ≥ 400 (adjusted odds ratio 3.97, 95% confidence interval 1.14–13.81, P = .030). Receiver operating characteristic analyses showed limited-to-modest discriminatory performance of NLR for CAC ≥ 100 (area under the curve 0.67) and CAC ≥ 400 (area under the curve 0.69). This study identified an independent association between NLR and CAC burden in patients with FM, despite modest between-group differences and limited-to-modest discriminatory performance. These findings are hypothesis-generating and support prospective evaluation of NLR as part of multimarker cardiovascular assessment in FM.

1. Introduction

Fibromyalgia (FM) is a chronic disorder in which widespread musculoskeletal pain commonly coexists with fatigue, disturbed sleep, and cognitive complaints.[1] Although its clinical framework has traditionally centered on altered pain processing, increasing evidence indicates that FM may also involve systemic abnormalities, including low-grade inflammatory activity, autonomic dysregulation, and endothelial dysfunction.[2] Observational evidence has linked FM with a greater burden of conventional cardiovascular risk factors, abnormalities of vascular function, and cardiovascular events compared with the general population.[3] Collectively, these observations suggest that the clinical relevance of FM may extend beyond nociplastic symptoms to include cardiovascular involvement.

Cardiovascular involvement in FM has received increasing attention independently of the disorder’s symptom burden. The mechanisms connecting FM with cardiovascular disease are not fully defined, and imaging-based evidence of subclinical coronary atherosclerosis in this population remains scarce. Much of the available literature has assessed cardiovascular risk through clinical characteristics, reported comorbidities, or surrogate markers, whereas direct quantification of coronary atherosclerosis has been used less frequently.[4,5]

Systemic inflammation plays a central role in the development and progression of atherosclerosis. A number of readily available indices based on peripheral blood cell counts have been examined as surrogate markers of cardiovascular inflammation and risk.[6] By capturing complementary features of the inflammatory response, these indices have shown associations with coronary disease severity and cardiovascular outcomes in a variety of clinical settings.[6] Among these measures, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) is one of the most extensively studied indicators of systemic inflammatory activity.[7] Elevated NLR has been associated with coronary artery disease (CAD) severity, coronary artery calcium (CAC) burden, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes in diverse populations.[6,8] Because it combines neutrophil predominance with relative lymphocyte depletion, NLR provides a simple composite representation of systemic inflammatory activity. NLR has also been studied in chronic inflammatory and autoimmune rheumatic conditions, with reported relationships to disease activity, inflammatory status, and vascular involvement.[9] Collectively, these findings provide a rationale for examining whether NLR is also related to vascular involvement in FM.

Despite increasing interest in cardiovascular involvement in FM, the association between routinely available inflammatory indices and computed tomography (CT)-assessed CAC burden has not been systematically evaluated in this population.[4] CAC scoring provides a standardized imaging-based estimate of coronary calcific burden, with increasing scores corresponding to higher cardiovascular risk.[10]

Integrating CAC measurements with simple inflammatory markers may help characterize the inflammatory component of subclinical coronary involvement in FM.[10] Although NLR and related inflammatory markers have been linked to coronary disease and calcification in other clinical populations, evidence combining a broader panel of inflammatory indices with CT-based CAC assessment in FM remains limited. To our knowledge, no prior study has specifically examined a panel of routinely available inflammatory indices in relation to CT-assessed CAC burden in FM. A previous study from our group, derived from the same institutional retrospective database and study period, evaluated metabolic indices, specifically the Triglyceride-Glucose Index and Atherogenic Index of plasma, in relation to CAC; the present analysis addresses a distinct research question focused on systemic inflammatory indices.[11]

Accordingly, the present study examined the association between routinely available inflammatory indices and CT-assessed CAC burden in patients with FM, with particular emphasis on identifying the marker showing the most consistent relationship with increasing CAC burden.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and population

We performed a retrospective cross-sectional analysis using records from Royal Hospital, Bandirma, Balikesir, Turkey, covering the period from January 2019 through October 2025. Data extraction from electronic medical records was performed in December 2025. Eligible adults with FM were identified retrospectively from the hospital records. FM diagnoses were based on the 2016 revised American College of Rheumatology criteria, which use the Widespread Pain Index and Symptom Severity Scale without requiring tender-point examination.[12] The present study represents a secondary analysis of data derived from the same institutional retrospective database and study period used in our previously published analysis of metabolic indices and CAC.[11] However, participants for the present analysis were selected independently according to the study-specific eligibility criteria and the availability of variables required for the inflammatory-index assessment. Accordingly, the analytic samples substantially overlapped but were not identical. Controls were selected from individuals undergoing coronary CT for clinical reasons who had neither FM nor a known inflammatory rheumatic disorder and had no evidence of obstructive coronary artery disease. Exclusion criteria included established coronary artery disease, prior myocardial infarction or coronary revascularization, active or chronic infection, systemic inflammatory or autoimmune rheumatic disease, active cancer, advanced renal or hepatic dysfunction, pregnancy or breastfeeding, and recent treatment with systemic corticosteroids or immunosuppressive drugs. Records were omitted when the clinical, laboratory, or imaging information required for this analysis was incomplete or when cardiac CT image quality was insufficient; missing values were not imputed. The number of excluded individuals and the reasons for exclusion are summarized in Figure 1. The final analytic sample therefore included only participants who satisfied the study criteria and had all data required for the present analysis. No a priori sample-size calculation was performed; the sample comprised all eligible participants with complete data available during the predefined study period. For the primary group comparisons, participants with FM were categorized as having CAC < 100 or CAC ≥ 100, with the control group analyzed separately. Additional exploratory analyses used a CAC threshold of ≥ 400 to evaluate associations with severe coronary calcification.

The study protocol was approved by the Faculty of Medicine, Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee of Bandirma Onyedi Eylül University (Approval No: E-67961857-050.04-2500085611) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective nature of this study, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

2.2. Clinical assessment

Demographic and cardiovascular data were obtained from the electronic hospital records, including age, sex, family history of coronary artery disease, smoking status, diabetes, hypertension, and medication history. Statin use was specifically recorded because of its potential relevance to CAC burden and lipid levels. Menopausal status was recorded and summarized among women. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). For patients with FM, symptom and disease-impact measures were obtained from assessments documented during the corresponding clinical evaluation. The Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire Revised (FIQR) was used to evaluate physical function, overall disease impact, and symptom severity; scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater impairment.[13] Pain intensity was rated on a 10-cm Visual Analog Scale (VAS), anchored at 0 for no pain and 10 for the most severe pain. Fatigue was assessed using the Visual Analog Fatigue Scale (VAFS; 0–10) and the 9-item Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS), whose items are rated from 1 to 7. The final FSS score was calculated as the mean of all items.[14]

2.3. Laboratory measurements and inflammatory indices

Laboratory measurements required for the present analysis were obtained retrospectively from the electronic records. Blood samples had been collected after a minimum 10-hour overnight fast and processed using routine automated laboratory procedures. Absolute neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, and platelet counts were recorded. Lipid parameters included total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglycerides, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C).

Composite inflammatory indices were calculated using the following formulas: the NLR was calculated as the absolute neutrophil count divided by the absolute lymphocyte count, the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio as the platelet count divided by the lymphocyte count, and the monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio as the monocyte count divided by the lymphocyte count. The Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) was calculated as platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count, the Systemic Inflammation Response Index (SIRI) as neutrophil count × monocyte count/lymphocyte count, and the aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI) as neutrophil count × monocyte count × platelet count/lymphocyte count. The neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHR) was calculated by dividing the absolute neutrophil count by HDL-C level. These indices were analyzed comparatively to determine which showed the most consistent relationship with CAC burden.

2.4. Coronary CT and calcium scoring

Coronary CT had been obtained as part of routine clinical care, primarily for assessment of atypical chest symptoms or cardiovascular risk. Coronary calcium was quantified from cardiac CT images acquired under a standardized protocol. Before imaging, participants were advised to abstain from caffeine, and beta-blockers were administered when clinically required to achieve adequate heart-rate control for image acquisition. Image acquisition was performed during a single breath hold using electrocardiographic gating.

Experienced readers, unaware of the participants’ clinical and laboratory findings, quantified coronary calcium using the Agatston scoring method.[15] CAC scores were categorized using thresholds of ≥ 100 for moderate-or-greater calcification and ≥ 400 for severe calcification.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics (version 24.0; IBM Corp.). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables are presented as median [interquartile range]. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages.

In the primary comparison across controls, FM with CAC < 100, and FM with CAC ≥ 100, continuous variables were analyzed using one-way ANOVA when normally distributed and the Kruskal–Wallis test otherwise. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. For the 6 inflammatory parameters evaluated across the 3 groups, Bonferroni correction was applied to the 6 overall comparisons. When an overall group difference warranted post hoc evaluation, Tukey’s honestly significant difference test was used following ANOVA, whereas Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni adjustment for the 3 pairwise comparisons were used for non-normally distributed variables.

Correlations between CAC score and inflammatory indices were evaluated using Spearman rank correlation coefficients in patients with FM. To account for multiple testing across 8 inflammatory parameters, Bonferroni-adjusted P values were additionally calculated. Correlations between inflammatory indices and FM symptom measures (FIQR, FSS, VAS, and VAFS) were evaluated in patients with FM only. Because age is a major determinant of CAC burden, an additional partial correlation analysis adjusted for age was performed for the association between NLR and CAC score.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were conducted within the FM group to assess discrimination of CAC ≥ 100 and CAC ≥ 400 by selected inflammatory indices. Areas under the curve (AUCs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Exploratory NLR thresholds were identified using the Youden index; these values were not treated as validated cutoffs because derivation and evaluation occurred within the same analytic sample.

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed in patients with FM with CAC ≥ 100 and CAC ≥ 400 as binary outcomes. Model 1 included age, sex, BMI, and NLR. Model 2 additionally included diabetes mellitus (DM) and hypertension. Model 3 further included LDL-C and statin use to account for additional cardiovascular confounding. Odds ratios (ORs) are reported with 95% CI. Because of the limited number of CAC ≥ 400 events, the extended models for this outcome were considered exploratory and interpreted cautiously.

As an additional sensitivity analysis, LDL-C was replaced by total cholesterol in the fully adjusted model while the remaining covariates were unchanged. This sensitivity analysis tested whether the NLR–CAC association remained stable when total cholesterol was used instead of LDL-C. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Study population

In total, 142 patients with FM and 64 controls were included. Patients with FM were stratified according to CAC scores into CAC < 100 (n = 82) and CAC ≥ 100 (n = 60) groups.

3.2. Baseline characteristics

Age varied significantly across the 3 groups (P < .001), with the FM + CAC ≥ 100 subgroup having the highest median age (Table 1). Cardiovascular risk profiles differed across the groups. DM and hypertension were more prevalent in the FM + CAC ≥ 100 group (P = .005 and P < .001, respectively), whereas BMI and smoking status did not differ significantly across groups. Statin use was also more frequent in the FM + CAC ≥ 100 group (P = .001). Total cholesterol, LDL-C, and HDL-C did not differ significantly across groups, whereas triglyceride levels showed a modest overall group difference (P = .045). Among women, postmenopausal status differed across groups (P = .003), with the highest proportion observed in the FM + CAC ≥ 100 group. Within the FM cohort, VAS and FIQR scores did not differ between CAC categories, while FSS and VAFS scores were higher among patients with CAC ≥ 100.

3.3. Inflammatory and laboratory parameters

Among the 6 inflammatory parameters evaluated across the 3 groups, NLR remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction (adjusted P = .017), whereas the overall differences in neutrophil count and NHR did not remain significant after correction (adjusted P = .072 and.207, respectively; Table 2). Median NLR was 1.97 [1.76–2.27] in the FM + CAC ≥ 100 group, 1.77 [1.56–1.99] in the FM + CAC < 100 group, and 1.84 [1.47–2.17] in controls (raw P = .003; Bonferroni-adjusted P = .017). SII, SIRI, and AISI did not differ significantly across groups after correction for multiple testing.

3.4. Post hoc analyses

The post hoc pairwise comparisons are presented in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1. After multiplicity adjustment, NLR was significantly higher in the FM + CAC ≥ 100 group than in the FM + CAC < 100 group (adjusted P = .001), whereas the difference between the FM + CAC ≥ 100 and control groups was not statistically significant (adjusted P = .078). Neutrophil count was higher in the FM + CAC ≥ 100 group than in both controls and the FM + CAC < 100 group in Tukey-adjusted comparisons.

3.5. Correlation analyses

Among the evaluated inflammatory indices, NLR showed the strongest correlation with CAC score (r = 0.371, Bonferroni-adjusted P < .001; Table 3). After Bonferroni correction, significant correlations with CAC score also remained for SIRI (r = 0.249, adjusted P = .022) and NHR (r = 0.265, adjusted P = .011), whereas the associations for SII and AISI no longer met the corrected significance threshold. Associations between inflammatory indices and FM symptom measures were generally weak; NLR showed only a small correlation with VAS (r = 0.167, nominal P = .048), while no meaningful correlations were observed with FIQR, FSS, or VAFS scores. In the age-adjusted analysis, the association between NLR and CAC score remained significant (partial r = 0.30, P < .001).

3.6. ROC analyses

ROC curve analyses showed limited-to-modest discriminatory performance of NLR for both CAC thresholds. For the identification of CAC ≥ 100, an exploratory NLR cutoff value of 1.64 yielded a sensitivity of 88% and a specificity of 39%, with an AUC of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.59–0.76; Table 4 and Fig. 2). For severe coronary calcification (CAC ≥ 400), an exploratory NLR cutoff value of 2.16 yielded 47% sensitivity and 86% specificity (AUC 0.69, 95% CI: 0.57–0.80; Table 4 and Fig. 2). Among the evaluated indices, NLR had the numerically highest AUC at both CAC thresholds, although all AUC values were below 0.70.

3.7. Multivariable and sensitivity analyses

Model 1 included age, sex, BMI, and NLR; Model 2 additionally included DM and hypertension; and Model 3 further included LDL-C and statin use. For CAC ≥ 100, NLR was independently associated with the outcome in Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, and BMI (OR 4.00, 95% CI 1.39–11.51, P = .010). The association remained significant after additional adjustment for DM and hypertension in Model 2 (OR 3.70, 95% CI 1.21–11.29, P = .022) and after further adjustment for LDL-C and statin use in Model 3 (OR 3.57, 95% CI 1.15–11.07, P = .028). In Model 3, hypertension was independently associated with CAC ≥ 100 (OR 2.97, 95% CI 1.26–6.98, P = .013), and statin use was also associated with CAC ≥ 100 (OR 2.41, 95% CI 1.06–5.48, P = .036), whereas DM and LDL-C were not statistically significant (Table 5).

4. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first analysis specifically examining a panel of routinely available inflammatory indices in relation to CT-assessed CAC burden in FM. The principal finding was a consistent relationship between NLR and increasing CAC burden in patients with FM. Although multiple inflammatory indices were examined, NLR emerged as the most consistent marker associated with both CAC ≥ 100 and CAC ≥ 400, showing the strongest correlation with CAC and retaining an independent association in multivariable models that included demographic and cardiovascular risk factors. Because age is a major determinant of coronary calcification, we specifically accounted for age in both partial correlation and multivariable analyses; the association between NLR and CAC burden remained statistically significant after adjustment. These findings support prospective investigation of NLR as 1 component of future multimarker approaches to cardiovascular assessment in FM.

Previous studies in non-FM populations have associated NLR with both angiographic CAD severity and CAC burden.[6,8,16] The present findings extend this association to FM, with NLR remaining related to CAC after adjustment for age, sex, BMI, DM, hypertension, LDL-C, and statin use.

Consistent with this evidence, CT-based evaluations have linked higher NLR values to greater CAC burden and obstructive CAD, while invasive angiographic studies have associated NLR with the severity and complexity of coronary atherosclerosis.[17,18] Together, these findings support an association between NLR and coronary atherosclerotic burden across different clinical settings.

In FM, however, studies directly relating inflammatory markers to objectively measured coronary calcification remain limited.[4] The current analysis addresses this gap by evaluating routinely available inflammatory indices against quantitatively assessed CAC, with NLR showing the most consistent association.

The relationship between inflammatory indices and coronary calcification varied across the FM subgroups. Patients with CAC < 100 showed inflammatory profiles broadly similar to those of controls, whereas the clearest NLR elevation was observed in the CAC ≥ 100 group. Although the between-group difference in median NLR was statistically significant, its absolute magnitude was small (1.77 vs 1.97), indicating that the principal finding lies in the consistency of the NLR–CAC association across correlation, multivariable, and sensitivity analyses rather than in a large separation of NLR values between CAC groups. This pattern suggests heterogeneity in the association between FM and coronary calcification and warrants further evaluation in prospective studies.

Another relevant finding was the weak relationship between inflammatory indices and FM symptom severity. Although several inflammatory indices were associated with the CAC score, their relationships with clinical measures such as FIQR, FSS, and VAFS were weak or absent, and NLR showed only a small correlation with pain intensity. This pattern suggests that the association between NLR and CAC burden is unlikely to be explained solely by greater FM symptom severity.

ROC analyses provided exploratory estimates of the discriminatory performance of NLR at different CAC thresholds. For CAC ≥ 100, the NLR threshold of 1.64 was characterized by high sensitivity but low specificity, whereas the threshold of 2.16 for CAC ≥ 400 showed higher specificity but lower sensitivity. However, the corresponding AUC values of 0.67 and 0.69 indicate only limited-to-modest discrimination. Accordingly, these cutoff values should be regarded as cohort-specific exploratory estimates rather than clinically validated thresholds. Their potential utility requires prospective validation in independent populations.[19]

Clinically, the observed NLR–CAC association suggests that routine inflammatory measures may offer complementary information when cardiovascular status is being evaluated in FM. Importantly, the modest AUC values indicate that NLR should not be viewed as a stand-alone discriminator of coronary calcification. Rather, the findings support future evaluation of NLR alongside established cardiovascular risk factors within multimarker risk-assessment strategies. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether such combined strategies can improve identification of patients with greater subclinical coronary atherosclerotic burden and whether they provide incremental value beyond conventional risk assessment. The observed association between statin use and CAC ≥ 100 should not be interpreted as evidence of a harmful statin effect, because the cross-sectional design cannot exclude confounding by indication; patients with a greater underlying cardiovascular risk burden are also more likely to receive statin therapy.

The present study has several strengths. First, coronary calcification was quantified directly using CT-based CAC scoring. Second, multiple inflammatory indices derived from routinely available laboratory parameters were evaluated simultaneously, allowing direct comparison of their associations with CAC burden. Third, multivariable analyses incorporated major cardiovascular covariates, including age, sex, BMI, DM, hypertension, LDL-C, and statin use. Fourth, the robustness of the NLR association was further examined in sensitivity analyses using total cholesterol as an alternative lipid measure. Together, these approaches allowed the NLR–CAC relationship to be examined across complementary analytical frameworks.

This study has several limitations that merit consideration. The retrospective cross-sectional design precludes the determination of temporal or causal relationships, and the findings should therefore be interpreted as associative. The present analysis was derived from the same institutional retrospective database and study period as our previous report on metabolic indices and CAC.[11] Accordingly, the analytic samples substantially overlapped but were not identical because study-specific eligibility and data-completeness requirements differed between the 2 analyses. The previous study focused on metabolic markers, whereas the current analysis specifically examined inflammatory indices; nevertheless, the shared data source should be considered when interpreting the independence of the 2 reports. Residual confounding cannot be excluded despite adjustment for several major cardiovascular risk factors. In particular, physical activity was not available in the retrospective dataset and therefore could not be incorporated into the analyses. Menopausal status was available and was described among women; however, sex-specific multivariable modeling was limited by the smaller number of women and outcome events, particularly for CAC ≥ 400. Some multivariable estimates were imprecise, as reflected by relatively wide CIs. In addition, the single-center design may have limited the generalizability of the findings. Age differed across the CAC categories, as expected given its established relationship with coronary calcification. However, the association between NLR and CAC remained significant in age-adjusted correlation analyses and in multivariable models including age. Although cardiovascular risk factors differed across the CAC groups, the number of covariates included in the regression models was restricted to clinically relevant variables to limit overfitting. This consideration was particularly important for the CAC ≥ 400 analyses, which included only 28 events and should therefore be regarded as exploratory. Control-group selection represents an additional limitation. Because controls were selected from individuals referred for clinically indicated coronary CT rather than from a community sample, they should not be considered representative of the general healthy population. A practical advantage of this approach was the use of comparable imaging and laboratory procedures across groups. Because the control group predominantly had very low or absent CAC scores, correlation and regression analyses were restricted to patients with FM to avoid potential distortion of associations due to a large proportion of zero CAC values in the controls. In addition, the ROC-derived thresholds were generated and evaluated within the same analytic sample and were not externally validated. Finally, multiple inflammatory indices were evaluated, increasing the potential for type I error; however, Bonferroni correction was applied to the principal group and correlation analyses to reduce this risk.

5. Conclusions

In this study, the NLR was independently associated with CAC burden in patients with FM and showed the most consistent relationship among the evaluated inflammatory indices. These findings support a measurable association between routinely available inflammatory markers, particularly NLR, and subclinical coronary calcification in FM and require confirmation in prospective studies. Given its routine availability and low cost, NLR may be suitable for further evaluation as 1 component of future multimarker cardiovascular assessment, but its modest discriminatory performance does not support stand-alone clinical use. Prospective multicenter studies integrating inflammatory biomarkers with imaging-based cardiovascular assessments are needed to validate these findings and determine whether NLR provides incremental value beyond established cardiovascular risk factors.
