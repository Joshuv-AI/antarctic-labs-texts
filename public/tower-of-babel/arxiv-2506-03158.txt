DUAL: Dynamic Uncertainty-Aware Learning
Jiahao Qin, Bei Peng, Feng Liu, Guangliang Cheng, Lu Zong

Abstract
Deep learning models frequently encounter feature uncertainty in diverse learning scenarios, significantly impacting their performance and reliability. This challenge is particularly complex in multi-modal scenarios, where models must integrate information from different sources with inherent uncertainties. We propose Dynamic Uncertainty-Aware Learning (DUAL), a unified framework that effectively handles feature uncertainty in both single-modal and multi-modal scenarios. DUAL introduces three key innovations: Dynamic Feature Uncertainty Modeling, which continuously refines uncertainty estimates through joint consideration of feature characteristics and learning dynamics; Adaptive Distribution-Aware Modulation, which maintains balanced feature distributions through dynamic sample influence adjustment; and Uncertainty-aware Cross-Modal Relationship Learning, which explicitly models uncertainties in cross-modal interactions. Through extensive experiments, we demonstrate DUAL’s effectiveness across multiple domains: in computer vision tasks, it achieves substantial improvements of 7.1% accuracy on CIFAR-10, 6.5% accuracy on CIFAR-100, and 2.3% accuracy on Tiny-ImageNet; in multi-modal learning, it demonstrates consistent gains of 4.1% accuracy on CMU-MOSEI and 2.8% accuracy on CMU-MOSI for sentiment analysis, while achieving 1.4% accuracy improvements on MISR. Introduction

Deep learning has achieved remarkable success in various computer vision tasks, yet the inherent uncertainty in feature representations poses significant challenges. Real-world data often contains incomplete or imperfect features, making it difficult for models to extract reliable information. Recent studies
(
Kendall & Gal 2017
)
have shown that when input features are incomplete or contain uncertainty, neural networks struggle to maintain reliable performance. In single-modal scenarios, this manifests in various ways: uncertain or missing texture information leads to inconsistent predictions in image classification
(
Schneider et al. 2020
)
; uncertainty in object boundaries results in unstable localization in object detection
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
, and feature uncertainty causes ambiguous region delineation in semantic segmentation
(
Yin et al. 2024
)
. Moreover, when explicitly modeling these feature uncertainties, it causes significant shifts in learned feature distributions, particularly affecting samples from the same class differently. Recent studies have shown that such distribution shifts can lead to degraded generalization
(
Qin et al. 2022
)
, reduced robustness, and inconsistent model behavior. While existing approaches have attempted to address these challenges through uncertainty estimation techniques and robust learning methods, they often treat feature uncertainty as a static property and fail to capture its dynamic nature during the learning process.

The challenges of feature uncertainty become significantly more complex in multi-modal learning scenarios. In multi-modal contexts, each modality not only retains its intrinsic feature uncertainty but also introduces ambiguity in inter-modality relationships, often due to missing explicit relationship annotations. For example, in multi-modal sentiment analysis
(
Zadeh et al. 2018
)
, while facial expressions may unambiguously convey one sentiment, vocal features might imply a different one, complicating the accurate assessment of emotional states. Such uncertainty is compounded in multi-modal emotion recognition
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
, where visual and acoustic features may interact, exacerbating ambiguity and leading to incongruent interpretations. Beyond the individual feature uncertainties present in each modality, we must also consider how these uncertainties affect cross-modal interactions. Current methods struggle with this additional complexity, manifesting in problems such as modal misalignment
(
Wu et al. 2022
)
, inconsistent feature contributions across modalities
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
, and noisy cross-modal interactions
(
Radford et al. 2023
)
. This feature-level and cross-modal uncertainty—arising from both individual modalities and their uncertain relationships—poses unique challenges that do not exist in single-modal tasks.

To address these limitations, we propose Dynamic Uncertainty-Aware Learning (DUAL), a comprehensive framework with two specialized variants: DUAL-S for single-modal scenarios and DUAL-M for multi-modal contexts. For single-modal tasks, DUAL-S incorporates two key techniques. First, Dynamic Feature Uncertainty Modeling continuously refines uncertainty estimates by jointly considering feature characteristics and learning dynamics, enabling more accurate and adaptive uncertainty quantification. Second, Adaptive Distribution-Aware Modulation maintains balanced feature distributions by dynamically adjusting the influence of samples based on their estimated uncertainties. For multi-modal scenarios, DUAL-M extends this foundation by incorporating Uncertainty-aware Cross-Modal Relationship Learning, which explicitly models both feature-level uncertainties and their cross-modal interactions. This principled approach enables effective learning of both
feature-level and cross-modal uncertainties across diverse learning scenarios while maintaining the specific requirements of different modalities.

Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We propose DUAL-S, a principled framework for single-modal tasks that integrates Dynamic Feature Uncertainty Modeling and Adaptive Distribution-Aware Modulation to handle feature uncertainty while maintaining balanced distributions. We further extend this to DUAL-M for multi-modal scenarios by introducing Uncertainty-aware Cross-Modal Relationship Learning, enabling effective modeling of both feature-level and cross-modal uncertainties.

•

We demonstrate that DUAL-S effectively enhances existing architectures, achieving consistent performance improvements on several single-modal benchmark datasets: CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Tiny-ImageNet. Similarly, DUAL-M successfully integrates with current multi-modal models, showing substantial improvements on standard benchmark datasets: CMU-MOSEI, CMU-MOSI, and MISR.

•

We provide extensive empirical analysis and ablation studies that offer insights into the behavior and effectiveness of different components of our framework across both single-modal and multi-modal settings.

2
Related Work

Feature uncertainty has emerged as a fundamental challenge in deep learning, profoundly impacting model reliability and generalization. Prior research has approached this challenge through three main theoretical frameworks: (1) probabilistic modeling with Bayesian principles
(
Kendall & Gal 2017
;
Ren et al. 2024
;
Gustafsson et al. 2020
)
, which provides rigorous uncertainty quantification but often faces computational challenges in high dimensions; (2) ensemble methods
(
Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017
;
Abdar et al. 2021
)
, which obtain robust uncertainty estimates through model aggregation while requiring significant computational resources; and (3) variational approaches
(
Gal & Ghahramani 2016
;
Nakamura et al. 2023
)
, which leverage dropout-based estimation with theoretical guarantees but may underestimate uncertainty in out-of-distribution scenarios.

These approaches have revealed two critical aspects of feature uncertainty in deep learning: its manifestation through systematic representation biases
(
Qin et al. 2022
;
Lee & Lee 2023
)
and its impact on model robustness
(
Schneider et al. 2020
;
Gawlikowski et al. 2023
)
. A particularly challenging aspect is the propagation and amplification of uncertainty through deep architectures
(
Vahdani & Faghani 2024
;
Mena et al. 2021
)
, which introduces complex temporal dynamics during the learning process - a phenomenon that existing static uncertainty modeling approaches struggle to address effectively.

The challenge becomes substantially more complex in multi-modal scenarios due to the interaction between two distinct types of uncertainties: feature-level uncertainty within individual modalities and relationship uncertainty between modalities. Current approaches have attempted to address these challenges separately: uncertainty-aware fusion methods
(
Xu et al. 2023
;
Pan & Meng 2024
)
focus on modeling intra-modal uncertainties, while cross-modal fusion techniques
(
Wu et al. 2022
)
primarily target inter-modal relationship uncertainties through attention mechanisms. However, this separation overlooks the crucial interdependencies between these uncertainty types.

Recent applications have demonstrated promising advances in handling feature uncertainty across different domains. In multi-modal sentiment analysis on CMU-MOSEI
(
Zadeh et al. 2018
)
, methods incorporating feature uncertainty modeling
(
Li et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2024
)
and relationship uncertainty handling
(
Guo et al. 2024
)
have shown significant improvements in accuracy. Similarly, emotion recognition has benefited from combining adaptive feature extraction
(
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Gao et al. 2024
)
with dynamic fusion mechanisms
(
Liu et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
.

Despite these advances, current research reveals a fundamental limitation in uncertainty handling: the lack of a unified modeling approach. While existing methods excel at addressing specific aspects of uncertainty, they often treat feature-level and modal-level uncertainties as separate concerns. This separation overlooks the intricate interactions between different types of uncertainties and their temporal dynamics, particularly in multimodal scenarios where uncertainties can compound and evolve over time. The need for a unified framework that can jointly model and adapt to both types of uncertainties has become increasingly apparent.

Existing approaches to uncertainty modeling can be broadly categorized into two frameworks: distribution calibration with adaptive techniques
(
Lan et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2024
)
and robust learning through meta-learning frameworks
(
Lee et al. 2025
)
. While these approaches have advanced our understanding of uncertainty handling, they exhibit two critical limitations. First, they typically treat uncertainty as a static property rather than a dynamic phenomenon that evolves during learning. Second, they handle feature-level and modal-level uncertainties in isolation, failing to capture the complex interplay between these different forms of uncertainty.

Compared to previous works, our DUAL framework addresses feature uncertainty in two fundamentally different ways. First, we introduce dynamic uncertainty modeling that continuously refines uncertainty estimates throughout the learning process, in contrast to existing approaches that treat uncertainty as static patterns
(
Xu et al. 2023
;
Pan & Meng 2024
)
. Second, while current methods handle uncertainties independently across modalities
(
Wu et al. 2022
)
, our framework provides a principled approach through two specialized variants: DUAL-S establishes the foundation for dynamic uncertainty modeling in single-modal scenarios, while DUAL-M extends this to jointly capture both intra-modal uncertainties and inter-modal relationships in multi-modal settings.

3
Problem Formulation

We begin by formalizing the fundamental problem of handling feature uncertainty in deep learning. Consider a standard supervised learning setting with feature space
\mathcal{X}
and label space
\mathcal{Y}
. While traditional approaches assume features are complete and fully observable, real-world scenarios often contain inherent feature uncertainties. We establish our framework by considering a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
represents input features and
y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
denotes corresponding labels. Unlike conventional approaches with fixed feature vectors, we model each feature
x_{i}
as a combination of its observable component and a time-varying uncertainty component. Throughout this paper, we denote loss functions as
\mathcal{L}
with subscripts indicating specific components, where
t
represents the current time step, and
(y,\hat{y})
denote the ground truth and predicted labels, respectively.

3.1
Dynamic Feature Uncertainty

We define
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
as feature and label spaces, respectively. A dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
consists of
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
and
y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
. Traditional deep learning assumes features are fixed and fully observable. However, real-world features have uncertainty and vary with time
(
Kendall & Gal 2017
)
. We propose representing a feature
x_{i}
as:

x_{i}=x_{i}^{c}+x_{i}^{u}(t)

(1)

where
x_{i}^{c}
is the constant part and
x_{i}^{u}(t)
reflects temporal uncertainty, capturing feature variability during model training
(
Schneider et al. 2020
)
.

The temporal dynamics of feature uncertainty can be characterized by:

x_{i}^{u}(t)=f_{\theta}(x_{i}^{c},t,\mathcal{T})

(2)

where
f_{\theta}
is a learnable function parameterized by
\theta
, and
\mathcal{T}
represents the specific task context. This formulation captures how uncertainty varies both temporally and across different tasks.

3.2
Task-Dependent Uncertainty Distribution

For task
\mathcal{T}
, the uncertainty distribution is represented as:

p(x^{u}_{i}|\mathcal{T})=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\mathcal{T}}(x^{u}_{i}),\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}(x^{u}_{i}))

(3)

where
\mu_{\mathcal{T}}
and
\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}}
are task-specific functions that estimate the mean and covariance of the uncertainty distribution, respectively. This allows us to capture task-specific variations in feature uncertainty
(
Qin et al. 2022
)
.

3.3
Dynamic Uncertainty Learning

To effectively handle feature uncertainty during model training, we formulate a learning objective that minimizes the task-specific loss while incorporating temporal dynamics. The learning objective can be mathematically expressed as:

\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\hat{y},y,t)]

(4)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}
is the loss function between predicted labels
\hat{y}
and ground truth labels
y
at time step
t
. To account for the dynamic nature of uncertainty, we introduce a temporal regularization term:

\mathcal{R}(t)=\lambda\|\frac{\partial x^{u}(t)}{\partial t}\|_{2}^{2}

(5)

This regularization encourages smooth temporal evolution of uncertainty while allowing necessary adaptations.

3.4
Multi-Modal Extension

For multi-modal scenarios with
M
modalities, we extend our formulation to:

x_{i}^{(m)}=x_{i}^{c(m)}+x_{i}^{u(m)}(t),m\in\{1,...,M\}

(6)

The cross-modal uncertainty interaction is modeled through:

\Phi_{m,n}(t)=\text{Cov}(x_{i}^{u(m)}(t),x_{i}^{u(n)}(t))

(7)

where
\Phi_{m,n}(t)
captures the temporal evolution of uncertainty relationships between modalities
m
and
n
.

3.5
Learning Objective

We now define our complete learning objective, which integrates task performance, temporal regularization of uncertainty estimates, and cross-modal consistency. All loss terms are normalized to similar scales to ensure balanced optimization:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}+\alpha\mathcal{R}(t)+\beta\sum_{m,n}\|\Phi_{m,n}(t)\|_{F}

(8)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are balancing parameters, and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm.

x_{obs}
into enhanced representations
x_{complete}
through embedding and temporal updates; (b) Uncertainty-Aware Cross-Modal Relationship Learning, which captures inter-modal relationships by computing both relationship representations
\Phi_{m,n}(t)
and uncertainty estimations
\Sigma_{m,n}(t)
from the enhanced features, then combines them using uncertainty-aware weights
\alpha_{m,n}
to produce a fused embedding; and (c) Adaptive Distribution-Aware Modulation, which optimizes the learning process by adaptively modulating the task loss based on uncertainty estimates. The framework operates in both single-modal (DUAL-S, left) and multi-modal (DUAL-M, right) scenarios.

4
Methodology: The DUAL Framework

Feature uncertainty critically influences model efficacy in deep learning, whether in single-modal or multi-modal contexts. To tackle these issues rigorously, we introduce DUAL, a framework comprising two tailored variants: DUAL-S for single-modal challenges and DUAL-M for multi-modal applications. DUAL-S uses Dynamic Feature Uncertainty Modeling and Adaptive Distribution-Aware Modulation to manage single-modal uncertainty, whereas DUAL-M augments this with Uncertainty-Aware Cross-Modal Relationship Learning for multi-modal contexts. 1
depicts the architecture of our framework and the interaction between components.

4.1
Dynamic Feature Uncertainty Modeling

Empirical datasets frequently encounter difficulties due to absent or imprecise attributes, which adversely affect model dependability. Our Dynamic Feature Uncertainty Modeling emphasizes that quantifying and adaptively adjusting feature uncertainty during the learning process is crucial, rather than considering it as invariant noise.

For an observed feature vector
x_{\text{obs}}
, we model the complete representation as:

x_{\text{complete}}=x_{\text{obs}}+x_{\text{uncert}}(t)

(9)

where
x_{\text{obs}}
represents the directly observed features, and
x_{\text{uncert}}(t)
captures the time-varying uncertainty component. This temporal dependency is crucial as it allows the model to refine its uncertainty estimates as learning progresses.

To estimate the uncertainty component adaptively, we employ a learnable function:

x_{\text{uncert}}(t)=f_{\theta}(x_{\text{obs}},h(t))

(10)

where
f_{\theta}
is a neural network parameterized by
\theta
,
h(t)
represents the temporal learning state computed as:

h(t)=f_{\text{temporal}}(h(t-1),\text{Emb}(x_{\text{obs}}))

(11)

with
f_{\text{temporal}}
being a recurrent function that updates the learning state based on the previous state and current feature embeddings
\text{Emb}(x_{\text{obs}})
.

To ensure well-calibrated uncertainty estimation, we introduce a probabilistic modeling framework:

\displaystyle\mu_{\text{uncert}}(x_{\text{obs}},t)

\displaystyle=W_{\mu}h(x_{\text{obs}},t)+b_{\mu}

(12)

\displaystyle\log(\sigma^{2}_{\text{uncert}}(x_{\text{obs}},t))

\displaystyle=W_{\sigma}h(x_{\text{obs}},t)+b_{\sigma}

(13)

where
h(x_{\text{obs}},t)
is a temporal-aware feature encoder,
W_{\mu}
,
W_{\sigma}
are learnable weights, and
b_{\mu}
,
b_{\sigma}
are bias terms. The mean
\mu_{\text{uncert}}
models the expected missing information, while
\sigma^{2}_{\text{uncert}}
captures estimation uncertainty. The logarithmic parameterization ensures positive variance and numerical stability.

The final feature representation is obtained through reparameterization:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\text{uncert}}(t)=&\|x_{\text{uncert}}(t)\|^{2}_{2}+\\
&\lambda_{\text{KL}}\text{KL}(\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\text{uncert}}(t),\sigma^{2}_{\text{uncert}}(t))|\mathcal{N}(0,I))\end{split}

(14)

where
\lambda_{\text{KL}}
balances the contribution of the KL divergence term. The standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
serves as a natural prior that promotes conservative uncertainty estimates while maintaining the model’s ability to capture meaningful uncertainty patterns.

The temporal evolution of uncertainty estimates is governed by:

\frac{\partial x_{\text{uncert}}(t)}{\partial t}=g_{\phi}(x_{\text{obs}},h(t),\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}})

(15)

where
g_{\phi}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{h}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
maps the feature space, learning state, and gradient space to the uncertainty update space, with
d
,
h
, and
p
being the respective dimensions.
g_{\phi}
updates uncertainty estimates based on the current task loss gradients
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}
. This temporal adaptation mechanism is key to DFUM’s effectiveness, as it allows uncertainty estimates to evolve with the model’s learning progress.

4.2
Adaptive Distribution-Aware Modulation

When modeling feature uncertainties explicitly, a key challenge arises: the distribution of features within the same class becomes more diverse and complex. This increased distribution variance can lead to two critical issues: (1) samples with high uncertainty may be unfairly penalized despite containing valuable information, and (2) the relative importance of different samples needs careful balancing to maintain effective learning.

We introduce Adaptive Distribution-Aware Modulation as a solution to these challenges, which adapts the learning process to distribution shifts caused by uncertainty. The fundamental principle is to preserve discriminative capability while accurately incorporating variations due to uncertainty in the feature space. In image classification, samples within the same category can exhibit varying uncertainty in feature representation. Instead of equally weighting all samples or merely reducing the weight of uncertain ones, ADAM adjusts their influence by considering both the magnitude of uncertainty and the prevailing feature distribution state.

The learning goal in ADAM utilizes a two-stage approach:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ADAM}}(t,y,\hat{y})=\begin{cases}\min(\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\hat{y},y,t),\mu_{t}+\alpha_{t}\sigma_{t})\\[4.30554pt]
\hfill\text{if }t\bmod R=0\\[8.61108pt]
\frac{1}{\beta_{t}}\log(1+\beta_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\hat{y},y,t))\\[4.30554pt]
\hfill\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(16)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\hat{y},y,t)
represents the task-specific loss between predicted labels
\hat{y}
and ground truth labels
y
. The periodic adjustment (every
R
steps) helps stabilize learning, while the logarithmic form provides gradient modulation during regular updates. The adaptation threshold
\alpha_{t}
is computed based on current uncertainty estimates:

\alpha_{t}=\alpha_{0}+\gamma\cdot\text{sigmoid}(|\sigma_{\text{uncert}}(t)|_{2}-\tau)

(17)

where
\alpha_{0}
is the base threshold,
\gamma
controls the adaptation range, and
\tau
is a reference level for uncertainty magnitude.

We adopt Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) as a differentiable metric to quantify distribution differences:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}(t)=\text{MMD}(\mathcal{F}_{t},\mathcal{F}_{t-1})

(18)

where MMD represents the Maximum Mean Discrepancy between feature distributions
\mathcal{F}_{t}
and
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
at consecutive time steps. This constraint encourages smooth evolution of feature distributions while allowing necessary adaptations to uncertainty changes.

The complete single-modal learning objective combines task performance with distribution balance:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DUAL-S}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{ADAM}}+\eta_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}

(19)

where
\eta_{t}
is a dynamic balancing factor that adjusts the strength of distribution alignment based on the current learning state:

\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}\cdot\exp(-\lambda\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ADAM}}\|_{2})

(20)

Here,
\eta_{0}
is the initial weight, and
\lambda
controls how quickly the alignment weight decreases as the task loss gradients become smaller. This dynamic adjustment ensures stronger distribution alignment when the model is exploring new regions of the feature space (large gradients) and allows more flexibility once learning stabilizes (small gradients).

The combination of adaptive loss modulation through
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ADAM}}
and dynamic distribution alignment via
\eta_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}
enables DUAL-S to effectively handle feature uncertainties while maintaining stable and discriminative feature distributions throughout training.

4.3
Uncertainty-Aware Cross-Modal Relationship Learning

In multi-modal scenarios, the challenge of handling uncertainty becomes significantly more complex due to the intricate interactions between different modalities. Beyond managing feature-level uncertainty within each modality, we must also account for the uncertainty in relationships between modalities. Our Uncertainty-Aware Cross-Modal Relationship Learning (UCRL) component addresses this challenge by explicitly modeling both feature-level uncertainties and their cross-modal interactions.

For a multi-modal input with
M
modalities, we first obtain completed representations for each modality using DFUM:

\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{\text{complete}}=\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{\text{obs}}+\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{\text{uncert}}(t),m\in\{1,...,M\}

(21)

where
\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{\text{obs}}
represents the observed features from modality
m
, and
\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{\text{uncert}}(t)
denotes its estimated uncertainty component at time step
t
.

The cross-modal relationships are then modeled while explicitly accounting for uncertainties:

\Phi_{m,n}(t)=f_{\text{rel}}([\mathbf{x}^{(m)}_{\text{complete}};\mathbf{x}^{(n)}_{\text{complete}}])+\epsilon_{m,n}(t)

(22)

where
f_{\text{rel}}
is a neural network capturing cross-modal interactions,
[;]
denotes feature concatenation, and
\epsilon_{m,n}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{m,n}(t))
models the uncertainty in the relationship between modalities
m
and
n
. This formulation allows us to capture both the relationship strength and its associated uncertainty. The additive noise term
\epsilon_{m,n}(t)
enables the model to capture inherent uncertainties in cross-modal relationships.

A key innovation in our approach is the modeling of relationship uncertainty covariance through a comprehensive consideration of all modalities:

\Sigma_{m,n}(t)=\frac{1}{M-2}\sum_{k\neq m,n}g_{\phi}(\sigma^{(m)}_{\text{uncert}}(t),\sigma^{(n)}_{\text{uncert}}(t),\sigma^{(k)}_{\text{uncert}}(t))

(23)

where
g_{\phi}
is a learnable function that captures how uncertainties from all modalities jointly influence the relationship uncertainty between modalities
m
and
n
. The summation over
k\neq m,n
ensures that all other modalities contribute to the relationship uncertainty estimation.

To effectively model these multi-modal uncertainty interactions, we design
g_{\phi}
as a bounded nonlinear function to ensure stable uncertainty estimation:

\begin{split}g_{\phi}(&\sigma^{(m)},\sigma^{(n)},\sigma^{(k)})=\\
&W_{\phi}\tanh(V_{\phi}[\sigma^{(m)};\sigma^{(n)};\sigma^{(k)}]+b_{v})+b_{w}\end{split}

(24)

where
W_{\phi}
and
V_{\phi}
are learnable weight matrices that capture the complex interactions between uncertainty estimates.

To ensure consistent and reliable cross-modal learning, we introduce a relationship consistency constraint:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\text{rel}}(t)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{n=1}^{M}\|\Phi_{m,n}(t)-\Phi_{n,m}(t)\|_{F}\\
+\lambda\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{n=1}^{M}\|\Sigma_{m,n}(t)-\Sigma_{n,m}(t)\|_{F}\end{split}

(25)

The first term ensures symmetric relationships between modality pairs, while the second term enforces consistency in uncertainty estimations. This bidirectional consistency is crucial for maintaining interpretable and reliable cross-modal relationships.

Based on these relationship models, we compute fusion weights for integrating information across modalities:

\alpha_{m,n}=\frac{\exp(-\beta\text{tr}(\Sigma_{m,n}(t)))}{\sum_{i,j}\exp(-\beta\text{tr}(\Sigma_{i,j}(t)))}

(26)

where
\beta
is a temperature parameter controlling the weight distribution sharpness, and
\text{tr}(\Sigma_{m,n}(t))
provides a scalar measure of relationship uncertainty. These fusion weights compute the final fused representation, assigning higher importance to modal pairs with lower uncertainty.

The complete multi-modal learning objective is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DUAL-M}}(t)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{ADAM}}(t)+\gamma\mathcal{L}_{\text{rel}}(t)+\beta\sum_{m,n}\alpha_{m,n}\|\Phi_{m,n}(t)\|_{F}

(27)

where
\gamma
and
\beta
balance the contributions of relationship consistency and magnitude regularization, respectively. This comprehensive learning objective facilitates effective learning of both intra-modal and cross-modal uncertainties while maintaining stable and interpretable relationships between different modalities.

Theoretical Analysis

B.1
Dynamic Nature of Feature Uncertainty in Deep Learning

We begin by establishing that feature uncertainty in deep learning is inherently dynamic rather than static. Consider a deep neural network
f_{\theta}
trained on dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

Theorem .1

(Dynamic Feature Uncertainty)
.

For a deep neural network
f_{\theta}
trained with stochastic gradient descent, the feature uncertainty at layer l, denoted as
u_{l}(t)
, evolves according to:

u_{l}(t+1)=u_{l}(t)+\eta\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial h_{l}(t)}\cdot\frac{\partial h_{l}(t)}{\partial\theta_{l}(t)}

(28)

where
h_{l}(t)
represents the feature representations at layer l and time t.

Proof.

Consider the feature evolution during training:

1) At each time step t, the feature representation
h_{l}(t)
depends on both the input
x
and the current parameters
\theta_{l}(t)
:

h_{l}(t)=f_{l}(\theta_{l}(t);x)

(29)

2) Due to parameter updates during training:

\theta_{l}(t+1)=\theta_{l}(t)-\eta\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta_{l}(t)}

(30)

3) This leads to changes in feature uncertainty:

\Delta u_{l}(t)=\|\nabla_{\theta}h_{l}(t)\|_{2}\cdot\|\Delta\theta_{l}(t)\|_{2}

(31)

4) The result follows by taking the first-order approximation.
∎

B.2
Analysis of Feature Incompleteness in Real-World Datasets

We begin by analyzing how real-world datasets inherently contain incomplete contextual information, using image classification as a motivating example.

Definition .2

(Complete Contextual Information)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}^{*}
represent the complete feature space where each instance
x^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
contains both object-centric features and full contextual information. For an image classification task,
x^{*}
would include:

x^{*}=\{f_{\text{object}},f_{\text{context}}\}

(32)

where
f_{\text{object}}
represents the target object features and
f_{\text{context}}
represents the complete environmental context.

Theorem .3

(Contextual Information Loss)
.

In typical image classification datasets
\mathcal{D}
, the observable features
x=\psi(x^{*})
contain only partial contextual information:

f_{\text{context}}^{\text{observed}}\subset f_{\text{context}}^{\text{complete}}

(33)

This partial observation leads to an information gap:

I(y;x^{*})-I(y;x)\geq\gamma\cdot I(y;f_{\text{context}}^{\text{complete}})

(34)

where
y
is the class label and
\gamma>0
represents the proportion of lost contextual information.

Proof.

Consider the classification task of identifying objects (e.g., ducks, cars, or symbols):

1) Human visual reasoning utilizes both direct object features and contextual cues:

\mathbb{P}(y|x^{*})=\mathbb{P}(y|f_{\text{object}},f_{\text{context}}^{\text{complete}})

(35)

2) Dataset images typically capture a limited field of view:

f_{\text{context}}^{\text{observed}}=g(\text{FoV})\cdot f_{\text{context}}^{\text{complete}}

(36)

where
g(\text{FoV})\in[0,1]
represents the fraction of context captured by the field of view.

3) This limitation creates an information bottleneck:

\displaystyle I(y;x)

\displaystyle=I(y;f_{\text{object}},f_{\text{context}}^{\text{observed}})

(37)

\displaystyle\leq I(y;f_{\text{object}})+g(\text{FoV})\cdot I(y;f_{\text{context}}^{\text{complete}})

4) Setting
\gamma=1-g(\text{FoV})
completes the proof.
∎

Corollary .4

(Context-Induced Uncertainty)
.

The loss of contextual information introduces inherent uncertainty in feature representation:

\mathbb{P}(y|x)=\int_{\mathcal{F}_{\text{context}}}\mathbb{P}(y|x,f_{\text{context}})\mathbb{P}(f_{\text{context}}|x)df_{\text{context}}

(38)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\text{context}}
represents the space of possible contextual features compatible with the observed data.

This analysis establishes that:
1) Real-world visual data inherently lacks complete contextual information that humans naturally utilize for classification
2) The information gap between complete and observed features can be quantified
3) This missing contextual information introduces fundamental uncertainty in feature representation

The incompleteness of contextual information in real-world datasets motivates our dynamic uncertainty modeling approach, which aims to account for both object-centric and contextual uncertainties during the learning process.
