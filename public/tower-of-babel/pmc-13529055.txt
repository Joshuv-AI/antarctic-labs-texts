Surgical interventions in herpes simplex virus keratitis: Outcomes in a large cohort
Authors: Aastha Garg, Kajal Singh, Manisha Acharya, Anil Tiwari, Umang Mathur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:Herpes simplex virus (HSV) keratitis is a significant cause of infectious corneal morbidity worldwide. Severe cases may require surgical intervention when medical therapy fails. This study evaluates surgical outcomes in managing advanced HSV keratitis.Methods:This was a retrospective observational cohort study conducted at a single tertiary care center. The objective was to assess the effectiveness of surgical interventions in stabilizing the ocular surface and improving visual acuity (VA) in patients with HSV keratitis. A retrospective analysis was performed on 377 patients who underwent surgical intervention for HSV keratitis between January 2020 and December 2023. Surgical indications included corneal perforations, persistent epithelial defects, neurotrophic keratitis, and visual rehabilitation. The primary outcomes were ocular surface stability and VA improvement.Results:Primary surgeries such as tissue adhesive with bandage contact lens, tarsorrhaphy, and penetrating keratoplasty achieved ocular surface stability in 74.3% of patients. A second surgical intervention was required in 57.02% of cases. VA improved significantly, with 8.22% achieving 6/18 or better at final follow-up.Conclusion:Minimally invasive surgical techniques play a critical role in stabilizing the ocular surface in severe HSV keratitis. A stepwise, tailored surgical approach allows optimization of outcomes while deferring high-risk emergency keratoplasty.

Herpes simplex virus (HSV) keratitis is a leading cause of infectious corneal blindness in developed countries. Although nearly 4.85 billion individuals worldwide are seropositive for HSV-1, this prevalence reflects widespread viral exposure and not the true incidence of corneal disease.[1] In cases of epithelial keratitis, vision usually remains unaffected. Stromal keratitis, on the other hand, is more likely to cause vision impairment, affecting approximately 24–42% of those with a visual acuity (VA) <6/12.[2] An estimated 1.5%–3.0% of HSV keratitis cases (considering all possible forms of keratitis) result in severe vision impairment (VA <6/60) in the affected eye. In 2012, the global annual incidence of herpetic keratitis was estimated at 1.5 million cases. By 2016, approximately 1.7 million people were affected, with most cases involving epithelial keratitis. Globally, HSV keratitis caused about 230,000 new cases of uniocular vision impairment in 2016, with over 1.8 million annual cases of herpetic eye disease. Preventing HSV infection could significantly benefit eye health.[12]

The management of active HSV keratitis primarily involves medical treatment. Infectious HSV keratitis is treated with topical or oral antivirals, while steroids are used for stromal keratitis, often alongside oral antivirals in cases of necrotizing herpetic stromal keratitis (HSK) that can rapidly cause corneal ulceration or perforation. Neurotrophic keratitis is usually treated with supportive surface therapy. Surgical intervention is typically reserved for complications such as corneal scarring, persistent epithelial defects, chronic inflammation, or neurotrophic keratoconjunctivitis. Emergency surgery is considered only for severe cases of necrotizing or neurotrophic keratitis when medical therapy fails to prevent corneal melting.[3] Emergency corneal transplants generally have poor outcomes, emphasizing the need for minimally invasive procedures to stabilize the ocular surface before considering penetrating keratoplasty. To date, no studies have been conducted to elucidate the outcomes of the available different surgical interventions in the treatment of herpes simplex keratitis. Our study endeavors to address this gap by providing a comprehensive description based on a larger sample population, encompassing cases performed since January 2020 and with a minimum follow-up duration of 3 months. It evaluates the effectiveness of surgical interventions at our center in stabilizing the ocular surface and improving VA in HSV keratitis patients.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at a single tertiary eye care center between January 2020 and December 2023. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. As this was a retrospective review of anonymized medical records, informed consent was waived.

HSV keratitis was diagnosed clinically based on characteristic slit-lamp findings. (e.g., comfortable eye opening, decreased sensations, dendritic/geographic epithelial ulcers, stromal infiltrates). Microbiological corneal scraping was performed only when superadded bacterial or fungal infection was suspected. The patients were treated both medically and surgically. The patients who underwent surgical treatment were included in the study. The protocol for medical management of patients at the center is mentioned in Table 1.

Exclusion: Patients with documented bacterial or fungal coinfection, history of corneal graft performed at another institution, incomplete medical records, or follow-up duration of less than 3 months were excluded from the study.

A standardized stepwise surgical approach was followed.

Postoperative regimen

All patients undergoing keratoplasty received postoperative topical antibiotics and systemic antiviral prophylaxis. Topical corticosteroids were initiated after complete epithelialization and gradually tapered under continued antiviral cover. Preservative-free lubricants were maintained long term in all grafted eyes. Patients undergoing optical penetrating keratoplasty received intensive topical corticosteroids (hourly in the immediate postoperative period) with concurrent oral acyclovir (400 mg twice daily), while topical antibiotics were administered as per the standard postoperative protocol.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was ocular surface stability, defined as complete corneal epithelialization at 3 months of follow-up without persistent epithelial defect, corneal melt, perforation, or recurrence of active infection.

The secondary outcome was an improvement in visual acuity at the last follow-up visit compared to visual acuity at presentation. Failure was defined as persistent epithelial defect, corneal melt or perforation, and recurrence of infection.

Statistical methods

Descriptive analysis was done using mean and standard deviation for quantitative variables and frequency and percentage for categorical variables.

All quantitative variables were checked for normal distribution within each category of an explanatory variable by using visual inspection of histograms and normality Q-Q plots. Shapiro–Wilk test was also conducted to assess normal distribution. A Shapiro–Wilk test P value of >0.05 was considered as normal distribution.

For normally distributed quantitative parameters, the mean values were compared between study groups using ANOVA. For non-normally distributed quantitative parameters, the median values were compared between study groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test.

Categorical outcomes were compared between study groups using the Chi-square test/Fisher’s Exact test (if the overall sample size was <20 or if the expected number in any one of the cells is <5, Fisher’s exact test was used).

Data were represented using bar charts, clustered bar charts, and pie charts.

P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. IBM SPSS version 26 was used for statistical analysis. (IBM Corp. Released 2013. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.)

Results

Between January 2020 and December 2023, 588 patients were treated for HSV keratitis (140 epithelial keratitis, 260 stromal keratitis, 43 endothelial keratitis, 27 neurotrophic keratitis, 75 keratouveitis, and 43 patients of healed HSV keratitis). Of the 588 patients, 211 patients were treated medically with oral and topical antivirals based on the HSV keratitis type, per our hospital protocols. A total of 377 patients during this period underwent surgical intervention and were included in the study.

Demographic information of the patients is provided in Table 2. The mean age of the study population was 57.39 ± 15.6 years (range 3 years to 93 years); 279 (74.01%) were males, and 98 (25.2%) patients were females; 238 (63.1%) patients presented with the first episode of HSV keratitis, and 139 (36.9%) patients had recurrent episodes of HSV keratitis. Among the study population, 303 (80.4%) patients had unilateral disease, and 74 (19.6%) patients presented with bilateral HSV keratitis.

Discussion

This study highlights the effectiveness of a stepwise, minimally invasive surgical approach in managing severe HSV keratitis.

Surgical intervention for herpes keratitis is rarely necessary, except in cases of HSV stromal keratitis with ulceration and severe neurotrophic keratitis, where the cornea continues to deteriorate despite medical treatment.[3] Preferred therapeutic modalities differ based on the size, causation, and location of the infection. The therapeutic modalities are based on either the replacement of the infected tissue or providing structural support of the tissue to allow healing. In such cases, emergency keratoplasty performed in the presence of active inflammation is known to be associated with poor outcomes, underscoring the importance of minimally invasive surface-stabilizing procedures before definitive corneal transplantation.[3] Necrotizing HSV keratitis can swiftly progress to corneal ulceration and perforation and thus usually requires early surgical intervention. It is more likely to result in visual impairment, as also observed in a study by Das et al.,[4] where the most common cause of moderate to severe visual impairment was HSV stromal keratitis with ulceration. In our study, the most common type of viral keratitis requiring surgical intervention was HSV stromal keratitis with ulceration (63.90%). This finding is consistent with the results of a study by Alfaro Rangel et al.,[5] where 78% of patients with stromal keratitis and ulceration required surgical intervention in the form of penetrating keratoplasty, and this group also constituted the main indication for emergency PKP. Amniotic membrane transplantation was also performed in the maximum number of patients with necrotizing herpetic keratitis in this study.[5]

Thus, our findings further support existing literature that necrotizing stromal disease represents the subgroup most likely to require surgical escalation despite optimal medical therapy. The sequelae of HSV keratitis usually cause corneal scarring, and surgical intervention in the form of an elective optical penetrating keratoplasty may be needed for visual rehabilitation in such patients.[6] In our study, corneal scar (10.30%) as a sequela of healed HSV keratitis was the second most common indication for surgical intervention.

Neurotrophic keratitis can be a late complication of both HSV epithelial and necrotizing keratitis and can lead to stromal melt and superadded infections, thus requiring early intervention in the form of tarsorrhaphy or amniotic membrane grafting.[7] In our study, it was the third most common etiology of HSV keratitis (9.50%) among patients undergoing surgical intervention.

The development of stromal ulceration in HSV keratitis and neurotrophic keratopathy is a serious complication that can lead to perforation and visual loss.[8] The most common indication for surgical intervention in our study group was a perforated corneal ulcer (35%), followed by persistent epithelial defect (18.60%).

The most common primary surgical intervention performed was cyanoacrylate tissue adhesive with bandage contact lens (TA + BCL), seen in 29.17% of cases, with HSV stromal keratitis with ulceration being the primary etiology in 46.98% of these patients [Fig. 4a-d]. Only 28.20% of these patients achieved ocular surface stability without requiring a second surgery. Previous studies have shown that corneal gluing is often ineffective in necrotizing herpetic perforations, likely due to ongoing viral activity, immune-mediated stromal degradation, and inadequate tissue response for wound healing.[910]

Our results corroborate these observations and suggest that TA + BCL in HSV keratitis primarily serves as a temporary globe-preserving measure rather than a definitive solution.

The second most common primary surgery was tarsorrhaphy [Fig. 4e-g], performed in 23.34% of patients, of which HSV stromal keratitis with ulceration was the primary etiology in 87.50%. Of these patients, 36.36% achieved ocular surface stability. Recurrent inflammatory responses in HSV keratitis are often triggered by HSV-1 shedding from sensory neurons, resulting in corneal hypoesthesia and exposure-related epithelial breakdown.[11] Tarsorrhaphy minimizes ocular surface exposure, decreases tear evaporation, and supports epithelial healing. Partial tarsorrhaphy has also been reported to improve epithelialization following penetrating keratoplasty in inflamed eyes.[12]

The third most common primary surgery was therapeutic penetrating keratoplasty (TPK) (19.36%) [Fig. 4h and i], while 14.32% underwent optical penetrating keratoplasty (OPK) [Fig. 4j and k] in a quiescent eye for visual rehabilitation. Ocular surface stability was achieved in 30.77% of patients undergoing TPK and 42.10% of those undergoing OPK. Penetrating keratoplasty in viral keratitis is associated with poorer outcomes due to higher recurrence rates and persistent inflammation. Foster and Duncan reported low graft survival in eyes undergoing keratoplasty during active disease, while better outcomes were observed when surgery was deferred until disease quiescence.[13] Altay et al.[6] similarly reported improved graft survival in scarred, noninflamed eyes.

In our cohort, visual outcomes were better in eyes undergoing elective optical keratoplasty compared to therapeutic keratoplasty, supporting a staged approach that prioritizes ocular surface stabilization before definitive visual rehabilitation.

Amniotic membrane grafting (AMG) was required in 6.36% of patients as a primary procedure [Fig. 5a-f], most commonly in HSV stromal keratitis with ulceration. AMG has demonstrated efficacy in severe herpetic keratitis due to its anti-inflammatory, anti-angiogenic, and epithelial regenerative properties.[141516171819] Although AMG facilitated epithelial healing in several cases, advanced disease often required additional surgical intervention in our cohort.

(a-f) Healing progression and scarring following amniotic membrane graft transplantation, (g and h) Tenon’s patch graft

Tenon’s patch graft (TPG) was more frequently performed as a secondary or salvage procedure in our study [Fig. 5g and h], highlighting its role in managing neurotrophic perforations and recurrent melts. TPG provides tectonic support using autologous tissue and promotes wound healing.[2021] While generally avoided in active suppurative infections, it remains a valuable option in selected HSV-related perforations.[22]

Overall, our findings emphasize the clinical value of a stepwise, minimally invasive surgical approach in severe HSV keratitis, enabling ocular surface stabilization while deferring high-risk emergency keratoplasty whenever possible.

Limitations

The retrospective single-center design limits generalizability. Reliance on electronic medical records may introduce documentation bias. Multicenter prospective studies are warranted.

Conclusion

Minimally invasive surgical strategies allow effective stabilization of the ocular surface in complicated HSV keratitis, reducing the need for emergency keratoplasty and improving long-term outcomes.
