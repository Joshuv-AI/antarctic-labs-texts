Abstract
. Despite their remarkable reasoning capabilities across diverse domains, large language models (LLMs) face fundamental challenges in natively functioning as g enerative r easoning r ecommendation m odels ( GRRMs ), where the intrinsic modeling gap between textual semantics and collaborative filtering signals, combined with the sparsity and stochasticity of user feedback, presents significant obstacles. This work explores how to build GRRMs by adapting pre-trained LLMs, which achieves a unified understanding-reasoning-prediction manner for recommendation tasks. Towards this, we propose GREAM , an end-to-end framework that integrates three components: (i) Collaborative-Semantic Alignment , which fuses heterogeneous textual evidence to construct semantically consistent, discrete item indices and auxiliary alignment tasks that ground linguistic representations in interaction semantics; (ii) Reasoning Curriculum Activation , which builds a synthetic dataset with explicit Chain-of-Thought supervision and a curriculum that progresses through behavioral evidence extraction, latent preference modeling, intent inference, recommendation formulation, and denoised sequence rewriting; and (iii) Sparse-Regularized Group Policy Optimization (SRPO) , which stabilizes post-training via Residual-Sensitive Verifiable Reward and Bonus-Calibrated Group Advantage Estimation , enabling end-to-end optimization under verifiable signals despite sparse successes. Distinct from prior LLM recommenders that trade off efficiency for interpretability, GREAM natively supports two complementary inference modes: Direct Sequence Recommendation for high-throughput, low-latency deployment, and Sequential Reasoning Recommendation that first emits an interpretable reasoning chain for causal transparency. Extensive experiments on three public benchmarks show consistent gains over strong generative and LLM baselines in both direct and reasoning settings. The results demonstrate that GREAM achieves a balanced trade-off among efficiency, accuracy, and end-to-end interpretability, providing a practical path for verifiable-RL-driven LLM recommenders. Introduction

In web-centric information ecosystems, users face persistent information overload and limited attention. As the backbone of content distribution, recommender systems algorithmically model user behavior to predict and deliver items of potential interest. Traditional two-tower architectures
(
Wang et al. 2021
;
Hidasi 2015
;
Li et al. 2017
;
Kang and McAuley 2018
;
Johnson et al. 2019
;
Guo et al. 2020
)
learn low-dimensional embeddings for users and items separately, retrieve candidates via ANN search, and re-rank them with a downstream model. Emerging generative recommendation
(
Rajput et al. 2024
;
Hua et al. 2023
;
Zhou et al. 2025a
;
Zhou et al. 2025b
)
reframes next-item prediction as autoregressive generation (e.g., sequences of discrete item-index tokens), enabling end-to-end learning under a unified language-modeling objective; key challenges include building semantically stable item indices, aligning collaborative semantics, and balancing calibration with efficiency.

Pushing further, LLM-based recommendation
(
Hou et al. 2024
;
Cui et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Bao et al. 2023
;
Zheng et al. 2024
;
Hong et al. 2025b
;
Geng et al. 2022
;
Hua et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Zhu et al. 2024
)
imports the understanding–reasoning-prediction capacity of large language models, either by textualizing and instructionalizing behaviors and tasks to exploit linguistic priors, or by semantic indexing that maps items to discrete tokens aligned with the LLM vocabulary—thereby injecting cross-domain knowledge, improving zero-shot generalization, and enhancing interpretability. Nonetheless, a semantic gap between natural-language and collaborative signals, semantic drift from text-only representations, and sparse, verifiable end-to-end training signals continue to limit the attainable performance of LLMs on complex sequential decision-making in recommendation.

Beyond these advances, existing RL paradigms
(
Tang et al. 2025
;
Shi et al. 2025
;
Luo et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025a
;
Gao et al. 2025
)
for LLM4Rec largely fall into two camps, each with structural drawbacks. (i) Latent-policy or pure-ID optimization: policies are optimized over non-linguistic item identifiers or latent vectors that lack discrete semantic structure. This decouples the policy from the LLM’s pretrained semantics, limits transfer of rich linguistic priors, and yields opaque credit assignment with minimal natural-language supervision. (ii) Text-only reasoning with external retrieval: models produce CoT-style explanations but delegate item selection to an external retriever or a black-box recommender. This breaks end-to-end training, introduces retrieval-induced semantic drift between textual reasoning and final items, and replaces verifiable rewards with exogenous, unverifiable scores—amplifying the unreliability of already sparse signals.

Recently, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has delivered notable gains in natural language, mathematical reasoning, and code generation, especially for LLM post-training; methodological advances from PPO
(
Schulman et al. 2017b
)
through GRPO
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
, DAPO
(
Yu et al. 2025
)
, and GSPO
(
Zheng et al. 2025
)
have further strengthened complex reasoning. However, directly transplanting RLVR to recommender systems is nontrivial:

(1)
Recommendation data are highly structured and the action space often spans millions of items; the semantics learned during LLM pretraining are difficult to align with recommendation objectives and cannot be exhaustively expressed in natural language.

(2)
RLVR hinges on per-sample verifiable rewards, whereas clicks and purchases are counterfactual outcomes conditional on exposure and are confounded by position bias, popularity (Matthew) effects, and feed ordering—thus they cannot be treated as ground-truth labels without careful correction.

(3)
Feedback is extremely sparse, rendering reward/critic learning unstable; many RLVR optimizers presume that each rollout contains both successful and failed samples, a condition rarely satisfied in practice.

(4)
RLVR typically exploits explicit multi-hop discrete reasoning paths (e.g., proofs or execution traces) to support credit assignment, yet the linguistic signals available in recommendation exhibit a substantial semantic gap from such path-structured reasoning, which helps explain the limited benefits observed so far in complex sequential recommendation.

To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose a large language model (LLM)-based generative reasoning recommendation framework that jointly integrates Collaborative-Semantic Alignment, Reasoning Curriculum Activation, and Sparse-Regularized Policy Optimization. This unified framework enables the model to bridge pre-trained linguistic semantics with recommendation-specific collaborative semantics, activate interpretable reasoning capabilities, and perform end-to-end optimization under verifiable rewards.
First, we establish a high-fidelity
collaborative-semantic alignment
mechanism between the pre-trained semantic space and the recommendation domain. By fusing heterogeneous textual sources—such as item titles, descriptions, and user reviews—we construct semantically consistent and structurally stable discrete item indices. A suite of collaborative alignment tasks is further designed to integrate temporal behavioral patterns and user preferences, ensuring that the model internalizes both linguistic and interaction-level semantics during training.
Second, to endow the model with
causal and interpretable reasoning ability
, we construct a synthetic reasoning dataset with explicit Chain-of-Thought (CoT) supervision and introduce a curriculum learning strategy that progressively enhances reasoning difficulty. This process guides the model through a structured, five-stage logical chain—behavioral evidence extraction, latent preference modeling, intent inference, recommendation formulation, and denoised sequence rewriting—thus transforming the recommendation task from pattern matching into causal reasoning.
Finally, in the reinforcement learning phase, we propose a
Sparse-Regularized Group Policy Optimization (SRPO)
algorithm that combines
Residual-Sensitive Verifiable Reward
,
Bonus-Calibrated Group Advantage Estimation
. Together, these mechanisms alleviate the instability caused by sparse and stochastic feedback, enhance gradient diversity, and ensure stable policy updates for large-scale recommendation environments.

Unlike previous LLM-based recommendation models that sacrifice interpretability for efficiency, our framework supports two complementary inference modes:
Direct Sequence Recommendation,
which directly generates the target item index for high-throughput, low-latency inference;
Sequential Reasoning Recommendation,
where the model first generates an interpretable reasoning chain before producing the final recommendation, enabling causal transparency and verifiable decision-making. This dual-mode design achieves a balanced trade-off between efficiency, accuracy, and interpretability, supporting both large-scale online deployment and research-oriented analyses that require transparent and traceable reasoning.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
LLMs as Recommenders

Recently, large language models (LLMs) have been adopted for recommendation because their language understanding, generation, and reasoning capabilities enable high-quality textual representations and the incorporation of external knowledge, helping alleviate poor generalization and sparse-interaction regimes
(
Liu et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2023
)
.
Two main lines have emerged. (i) Textual/instructional formulations: P5
(
Geng et al. 2022
)
unifies multiple recommendation subtasks in an instruction-tuned framework, using a SentencePiece tokenizer to map randomly assigned pseudo-IDs to tokens; M6
(
Cui et al. 2022
)
directly uses item text (e.g., names) as identifiers for generative recommendation. While effective for task unification and end-to-end language modeling, these approaches rely solely on text, leading to semantic drift and weak integration of collaborative signals. (ii) Semantic indexing and alignment: LC-Rec
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
learns discrete IDs via vector quantization and introduces alignment tasks to inject collaborative semantics into LLMs; EAGER-LLM
(
Hong et al. 2025b
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
proposes a decoder-only generative framework that non-intrusively integrates endogenous/exogenous behavioral and semantic information.
To further improve decision quality, recent work introduces reinforcement learning (RL). LatentR
3

(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
optimizes latent reasoning without CoT data, and Rec-R1
(
Lin et al. 2025
)
learns from feedback given by a fixed black-box recommender. Despite gains, two limitations persist: (i) implicit reasoning hampers interpretability and controllability, and (ii) reward signals are sparse/ambiguous or depend on external teachers/retrievers, misaligning the optimization objective with end-to-end generation. In contrast, we introduce an explicit reasoning chain together with a verifiable RL objective on top of LLM-based generative recommendation, enabling truly end-to-end, reasoning-style recommendation.

2.2.
Reinforcement Learning for LLMs

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become a cornerstone in advancing large language models (LLMs)
(
Schulman et al. 2017b
;
Shao et al. 2024
;
Hong et al. 2025a
;
Hu et al. 2025
;
Guo et al. 2025
)
, often yielding superior generalization compared to purely supervised approaches.The evolution from Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO)
(
Schulman et al. 2017a
)
to Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
(
Schulman et al. 2017b
)
, and later to Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
, marks a pursuit of greater stability, sample efficiency, and scalability for massive model training. Building on these, Decoupled Clip and Dynamic Sampling Policy Optimization (DAPO) introduces decoupled clipping and adaptive sampling to mitigate entropy collapse and improve training stability
(
Yu et al. 2025
)
, while Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) extends group-based methods by defining importance ratios and optimization at the sequence level, achieving enhanced efficiency and robustness in large-scale RL fine-tuning
(
Zheng et al. 2025
)
. These methods have achieved remarkable success in improving reasoning and alignment across diverse domains.
However, when applied to recommendation tasks, post-training RL methods face a significant gap between language pre-training objectives and user-behavior-driven rewards, which are inherently sparse and stochastic. Our method bridges this gap, enabling stable and effective RL-based alignment of LLMs within recommendation scenarios.

3.1.
Generative Recommendation.

3.1.1.
Problem Formulation.

Sequential recommendation is a crucial metric in LLM-based recommender systems. We transform the traditional two-tower model, which computes similarity followed by reordering, into a generative recommendation paradigm. In this framework, each item
\mathbf{x}
is represented by a set of tokens
{\bf Y}=[{\bf y}_{1},{\bf y}_{2},\cdots,{\bf y}_{k}]\in\mathcal{Y}

\mathbf{X}

\mathbf{Y}
is generated directly in an autoregressive manner. The probability can be calculated by:

(1)

p({\bf Y}|{\bf X})={\prod}_{i=1}^{k}p({\bf y}_{i}|\bf{X},{\bf y}_{1},{\bf y}_{2},\dots,{\bf y}_{i-1})

This end-to-end formulation replaces retrieval–re-ranking and directly optimizes representation, generation, and decision.

4.
Collaborative-Semantic Alignment and Reasoning Activation

Figure 2.

The successful application of LLMs to recommendation requires bridging the significant gap between their general-purpose, pre-trained semantic space and the specialized, collaborative semantics inherent in user-item interaction data. A model trained solely on textual data risks semantic drift, while one relying only on collaborative signals fails to harness the rich knowledge and reasoning capabilities of LLMs. To address this, we introduce a preparatory stage that constructs two distinct yet synergistic types of data, designed to (i) establish a high-fidelity, semantically-aligned item representation space and (ii) activate the model’s latent reasoning abilities for complex sequential decision-making.

4.1.
High-Fidelity Indexing and Collaborative Alignment Data

A core challenge in generative recommendation is creating discrete item indices that are both semantically meaningful and computationally efficient. To this end, we first enhance the quality of our item representations. Recognizing that item titles are often concise but lack detail, while user reviews provide rich, user-centric perspectives, we synthesize these sources. For each item, we aggregate its title, official description, and a curated set of high-quality user reviews. This composite text is then processed by a powerful, state-of-the-art generative model (i.e., GPT-5) instructed to rewrite a comprehensive, feature-rich description that encapsulates both objective attributes and subjective user perceptions. By encoding the concatenation of the original item title and this newly synthesized description through our LLM, we produce embeddings that are substantially more informative. Applying RQ-KMeans
(
Luo et al. 2024
)
to these enriched embeddings significantly reduces semantic ambiguity, leading to an approximate
50% reduction in index collision rates
compared to using titles alone.

With these high-fidelity indices, we then construct the alignment dataset,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{align}}
, by formulating a diverse suite of tasks designed to instill collaborative knowledge. These tasks fall into three main categories:

•

Sequential Recommendation Tasks:
These tasks frame next-item prediction as a language modeling problem (e.g., given a user’s history, predict the index of the next item). This teaches the model the temporal dynamics and sequential patterns of user behavior.

•

Semantic Reconstruction Tasks:
These tasks create a strong link between textual semantics and our discrete indices. Examples include predicting an item’s tokenized index from its textual description, and vice versa. This ensures the model’s internal representations are well-grounded in both collaborative and semantic spaces.

•

User Preference-related Tasks:
These tasks focus on modeling a user’s general, long-term interests. For instance, the model might be asked to generate a textual summary of a user’s preferences based on their interaction history.

This multiscale alignment dataset ensures that the model understands not just
what
an item is, but also
how
it fits within the broader ecosystem of user behaviors and collaborative patterns.

4.2.
Reasoning Activation via Synthetic Chain-of-Thought Data

To elevate the model from a pattern-matching predictor to a veritable reasoner, we construct a high-quality, synthetic dataset,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{reason}}
, designed to explicitly teach Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning for recommendation. A critical insight guiding our approach is that sequential recommendation is not a deterministic task with a single “correct” answer; the observed next item is merely one of many plausible outcomes. Therefore, the primary goal of our data synthesis is not to perfectly replicate historical sequences but to forge a
plausible and interpretable causal link
between a user’s interaction history and a subsequent, recommended item.

We achieve this through a reverse-reasoning process, employing a powerful LLM to generate structured reasoning paths that culminate in a logical recommendation. Our framework follows the reasoning template proposed in DeepSeek-R1, where system prompts enforce output-format constraints (e.g., <think>…</think> and <answer>…</answer>), ensuring structural consistency between reasoning logic and the generated recommendation results. Specifically, we redefine the Reason component of the sequential reasoning recommendation task as a standardized logical chain composed of five stages:

•

Extraction of Behavioral Evidence:
The model first analyzes the user’s historical interaction sequence to identify salient patterns, such as evolving interests, functional preferences (e.g., preference for a certain brand), and underlying motivations.

•

Modeling of Latent Preferences:
Based on the extracted evidence, the model infers a coherent user persona, including long-term characteristics, brand affinities, and value orientations, forming a causally interpretable profile.

•

Inference of User Intent and Goal Formulation:
The model synthesizes the user persona with recent behaviors to deduce the user’s current latent need and formulate a specific goal for the next interaction (e.g., “find a complementary product” or “upgrade an existing item”).

•

Formulation of Recommendation and Justification:
The model generates a concrete item recommendation and provides an explicit justification that connects the recommendation directly back to the inferred intent and historical evidence, ensuring transparency.

•

Denoised Sequence Rewriting:
As a final step, the model learns to autonomously refine the input history by identifying and filtering out noisy or irrelevant interactions. This enhances policy robustness by enabling the model to focus on the most signal-rich elements of a user’s sequence.

This structured CoT data effectively transforms the recommendation task from a black-box prediction into a transparent, step-by-step reasoning problem, activating the LLM’s inherent capacity for complex logical deduction. Specific case studies that show the concrete responses of
GREAM
can be found in Appendix
C
.

4.3.
Hybrid SFT with Curriculum Learning

We discovered that the alignment data (
\mathcal{D}_{\text{align}}
) and the reasoning data (
\mathcal{D}_{\text{reason}}
) are highly synergistic. The former grounds the model in the factual, collaborative structure of the item space, while the latter teaches it how to navigate this space through logical inference. Consequently, we train the model using Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on a hybrid mixture of both datasets,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}=\mathcal{D}_{\text{align}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\text{reason}}
. The training objective is to minimize the standard auto-regressive negative log-likelihood loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta)=-\sum_{(X,Y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}}\sum_{t=1}^{|Y|}\log p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},X)

where
(X,Y)
is an input-output pair from the combined dataset, and
\theta
represents the model parameters. The resulting model is versatile, capable of performing both direct, low-latency item ID generation and more deliberative, reasoning-backed recommendation where the model first produces an interpretable CoT before outputting the final item ID.

To maximize the benefits of this hybrid approach, we employ a curriculum learning strategy over three epochs. A naive uniform mixing can be suboptimal, as the model may struggle with complex reasoning without a solid semantic foundation. Our curriculum allows the model to first establish a strong grasp of collaborative semantics before progressively focusing on reasoning tasks. During the first two epochs, for each alignment batch
b_{\text{align},i}\in B_{\text{align}}
processed, we insert a reasoning batch from
B_{\text{reason}}
with a probability
p_{\text{insert}}(i)
that increases with the training step
i
:

p_{\text{insert}}(i)=\min\left(1,\gamma\cdot\frac{i}{N_{\text{align}}}\cdot\frac{N_{\text{reason}}}{N_{\text{align}}}\right),

where
N_{\text{align}}=|B_{\text{align}}|
and
N_{\text{reason}}=|B_{\text{reason}}|
are the total number of batches for each data type, and
\gamma
is a hyperparameter scaling the curriculum pace (empirically set to 1.5). Any remaining reasoning batches are appended at the end of the epoch to ensure all data is utilized. In the final epoch, we switch to a uniform sampling distribution, mixing the two data types evenly to fine-tune both capabilities concurrently.

5.
Sparse-Regularized Group Policy Optimization (SRPO)

5.1.
Residual-sensitive Verifiable Reward Shaping

In our residual discretization indexing scheme, higher-level indices encode coarser-grained and more discriminative semantics, where prefix consistency directly determines both the semantic correctness of the neighborhood and the contraction speed of the candidate space.
Traditional binary rewards (i.e., “completely correct or completely wrong”) in generative recommendation often suffer from reward sparsity and hinder accurate credit assignment.
To address this issue, we propose a
residual-sensitive verifiable reward shaping
mechanism that leverages the longest common prefix length between the generated and target indices as a computable and verifiable fine-grained signal, ensuring goal consistency while substantially alleviating reward sparsity.

Let the target item index be
y=(c_{1},\dots,c_{H})
,
and the model-generated index be
\hat{y}=(\hat{c}_{1},\dots,\hat{c}_{H})
,
where
H
denotes the number of hierarchical levels (e.g.,
H=4
).
We define the longest common prefix length as:

(13)

\ell=\max\{k\in\{0,\dots,H\}\mid\hat{c}_{i}=c_{i},\ \forall i\leq k\}.

Accordingly, the
residual-sensitive verifiable reward
is formulated as:

(14)

r^{\text{rs}}=\left(\frac{\ell}{H}\right)^{\beta},

where
\beta\in(0,1]
controls the concavity of the reward curve.
We set
\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}
by default.

This reward design provides four key advantages.
First, it introduces
densification and stability
: whenever
\ell>0
, the model receives a non-zero reward, effectively mitigating sparsity and reducing variance compared to binary all-or-nothing signals.
Second, it highlights
prefix importance
: since
\beta\in(0,1)
makes
r_{\text{rs}}
a concave function, each incremental prefix match (
\Delta\ell=1
) yields a larger marginal gain at earlier levels, aligning with the hierarchical property that upper-level indices encode coarser and more critical semantics, thereby assigning higher implicit weights to earlier indices.
Third, it facilitates
curriculum optimization
, as the training process naturally follows a coarse-to-fine trajectory—first capturing semantic categories at higher levels and progressively refining to instance-level representations.
Finally, it ensures
verifiability
: the computation of
\ell
is deterministic and unambiguous, satisfying the verifiable reward criterion of RLVR without requiring additional annotations or external retrieval modules.

5.2.
Bonus-Calibrated Group Advantage Estimation

In the early stages of generative recommendation, exactly correct generations are typically rare. While the residual-sensitive dense reward
r^{\mathrm{rs}}
supplies stable gradients, it may not sufficiently drive these rare success events. To address this, beyond GRPO’s group-relative advantage, we introduce a group success–oriented bonus term that explicitly optimizes the objective “at least one generation is exactly correct.” Inspired by
Pass@k

(
Chen et al. 2025b
)
, we measure this target via the group success probability under without-replacement sampling. However, unlike using
Pass@k
alone, we note a key limitation: when the sample size exceeds the number of incorrect generations, the success probability
\rho
saturates toward
1
and its variance collapses toward
0
, causing all advantages to approach zero and hindering further optimization. To avoid this degeneration, we add this bonus term directly to the residual dense advantage, yielding complementary signals that are dense–stable (from
A^{\mathrm{rs}}_{i}
) and rare–exact (from the bonus).

Let
G
be the rollout size, and let
v_{i}\in\{0,1\}
indicate whether the
i
-th generation is exactly correct (
v_{i}=1
) or not (
v_{i}=0
). Denote
c=\sum_{i=1}^{G}v_{i}
as the count of exactly correct generations. For without-replacement sampling of size
k
with
1\leq k\leq G
, the group success probability that at least one of the
k
samples is exactly correct is

(15)

\rho(c;G,k)\;=\;1\;-\;\frac{\binom{G-c}{k}}{\binom{G}{k}}\,.

where
\binom{n}{m}
denotes the binomial coefficient. We also define the group-level Bernoulli variance

(16)

\sigma\;=\;\sqrt{\rho\,(1-\rho)}\,,

Dense–stable advantage.
The group-normalized residual advantage is

(17)

A^{rs}_{i}\;=\;\frac{\,r^{\mathrm{rs}}_{i}\;-\;\mathrm{mean}\!\big(\{r^{\mathrm{rs}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\big)\,}{\,\mathrm{std}\!\big(\{r^{\mathrm{rs}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\big)\,}\,.

Rare–exact bonus advantage.
For
exactly correct
samples (
v_{i}=1
), we assign the positive bonus

(18)

\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{bonus}}_{\mathrm{pos}}\;=\;\frac{\,1-\rho\,}{\,\sigma\,}\,,

which grows with the
gap-to-perfection

(1-\rho)
. For
incorrect
samples (
v_{i}=0
), we consider their counterfactual marginal contribution to group success if flipped to correct. Let

(19)

\delta\;=\;\frac{\binom{G-c-1}{\,k-1\,}}{\binom{G-1}{\,k-1\,}}\,,

the probability that this sample is selected and the remaining
k-1
draws are all incorrect. We then define the negative bonus

(20)

\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{bonus}}_{\mathrm{neg}}\;=\;\frac{\,(1-\rho)-\delta\,}{\,\sigma\,}\,.

Together, (
18
)–(
20
) yield a group-level counterfactual credit assignment: positives share the group’s gap-to-perfection, while negatives receive signals proportional to their potential flip-to-success contributions.

To prevent rare–exact bonus advantage saturation (
\rho\to 1
,
\sigma\to 0
) from collapsing all advantages while preserving the dense–stable properties of
A^{rs}_{i}
, we form the final advantage by direct addition:

(21)

A_{i}^{\mathrm{final}}\;=\;A^{rs}_{i}\;+\;\mathbbm{1}[v_{i}=1]\;\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{bonus}}_{\mathrm{pos}}\;+\;\mathbbm{1}[v_{i}=0]\;\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{bonus}}_{\mathrm{neg}}\,,

where
\mathbbm{1}[\cdot]
is the indicator function. This ensures that early training benefits from the stability and prefix alignment of
A^{rs}_{i}
, while the bonus
\tilde{A}^{\mathrm{bonus}}
strongly drives exactly correct events later on; even when
k>G-c
yields
\rho\approx 1
and
\sigma\to 0
, overall learning does not collapse because
A^{rs}_{i}
remains active.

5.3.
Final Objective.

Recommendation rewards are typically sparse. While DAPO’s dynamic sampling mitigates the gradient-decreasing pathology whereby identical group rewards yield zero group-normalized advantages and thus vanishing policy updates, its criterion is tailored to 0/1 accuracies. In our setting, per-rollout rewards are continuous. We therefore adopt a variance-guided dynamic sampling strategy: reject groups with sampling variance equal to zero. Then the objective can be reformulated as:

(22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{SRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(q,a)\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot|q)}

\displaystyle\Biggl[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|o_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_{i}|}\Bigl(\min\bigl(r_{i,t}(\theta){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A^{\mathrm{final}}_{i,t}},\,

\displaystyle\mathrm{clip}\bigl(r_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A^{\mathrm{final}}_{i,t}}\bigr)

\displaystyle-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\Bigr)\Biggr]

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrm{std}\!\big(\{r^{\mathrm{rs}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\big)\neq 0}.

6.1.
Experimental Setting

6.1.1.
Dataset.

To comprehensively evaluate our approach, we conducted experiments on three real-world public datasets of Amazon product reviews
(
McAuley et al. 2015
;
He and McAuley 2016
)
, which are widely recognized benchmarks in sequence recommendation research. Specifically, we used data from three subcategories—Beauty, Sports and Outdoors, and Musical Instruments. Consistent with prior work
(
Hou et al. 2022
;
Rendle et al. 2010
;
Zhang et al. 2019
)
, we applied the 5-core filtering protocol, excluding users and items with fewer than five interactions to retain meaningful behavioral sequences. The detailed dataset statistics are provided in Table
1
. In addition, we leverage multiple open- and closed-source large language models (e.g., Qwen3, GPT-5, and Llama-3.1) to generate large-scale textual data based on these datasets for alignment purposes. The structure of the generated data can be referred to in the case study provided in the Appendix
C
.

6.1.2.
Evaluation Metrics.

We follow standard evaluation protocols
(
Kang and McAuley 2018
)
and adopt the leave-one-out strategy for assessment. For direct sequence recommendation tasks, we employ two widely recognized metrics—Recall and Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG)—and report results at cutoffs of k = 5 and k = 10. For generative recommendation methods utilizing beam search, the beam size is consistently fixed at 20. In the case of sequential recommendation tasks, we evaluate performance using Pass@k accuracy at k = 1, 5, and 10, with the generation temperature set to 0.7 and the top-p value to 0.9.

6.1.3.
Implementation Details.

All experiments are conducted on a cluster equipped with 24 NVIDIA A100 GPUs. We adopt Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 as the backbone model.
The item indexing scheme
is designed with a fixed length of 5, where the fifth position serves as a conflict-resolution slot, and each hierarchical layer is assigned a structured identifier capable of representing 256 distinct values. During
Collaborative-Semantic Alignment and
Reasoning Activation phase
, we employ multiple large language models—Qwen3-30B, GPT-5, Llama-3.1, and Gemini—to synthesize domain-diverse textual data and mitigate overfitting. In the
alignment phase
, the model is fully fine-tuned using the LLaMA-Factory framework, with a learning rate of 5.0e-5 for 3 epochs. For
reinforcement learning optimization
, we adopt the Verl
(
Sheng et al. 2024
)
framework with the following hyperparameters: learning rate = 4e-6, weight decay = 0.01, training batch size = 256, mini-batch size = 128, and KL-loss coefficient = 0.001. The rollout configuration sets temperature = 0.5, top-p = 1.0, and samples 10 responses per query. We utilize SGLang as both the RL rollout backend and the inference acceleration framework for
evaluation
.

7.
Conclusion

We presented a unified LLM-based generative reasoning recommendation framework that integrates Collaborative-Semantic Alignment, Reasoning Curriculum Activation, and Sparse-Regularized Group Policy Optimization (SRPO). By bridging the semantic gap between language modeling and collaborative interaction data, our approach enables both efficient direct recommendation and interpretable reasoning-based generation under verifiable reinforcement signals. The proposed framework not only advances verifiable-RL-driven recommendation but also provides a practical path toward transparent, causally grounded, and scalable LLM recommender systems.

Appendix A
Limitation

While our framework demonstrates consistent improvements across benchmarks, the current study is bounded by computational and resource constraints. The scaling trajectory of reasoning performance indicates that the model remains within the non-saturated regime of the scaling law, suggesting further potential with larger compute budgets. Due to these limitations, we have not yet explored training with larger backbones or extended reasoning curricula, which may yield additional gains in semantic alignment and reinforcement learning stability. Future work will investigate these directions to fully exploit the scalability and generalization potential of our approach.
