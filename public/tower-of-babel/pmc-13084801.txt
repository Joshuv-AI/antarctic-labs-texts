In vitro characterisation of fluticasone propionate and ciclesonide delivered with and without valved holding chambers using cascade impaction and an adult simulated breathing profile
Authors: Leon L Csonka, Lauri Lehtimäki, Péter Csonka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundValved holding chambers (VHCs) are not interchangeable. Because VHC design and formulation type influence aerosol behaviour, each VHC–drug combination should be evaluated to inform clinical use and device selection.AimTo compare the aerodynamic particle size distribution (APSD) of two inhaled corticosteroids delivered from pressurised metered dose inhalers with and without commonly used VHCs under realistic, adult-representative breathing.MethodsWe assessed fluticasone propionate (FP, suspension) and ciclesonide (CIC, solution) using no VHC and three VHCs (AeroChamber, EasyChamber and OptiChamber Diamond). A Next Generation Impactor was connected to an anatomically representative adult throat model and a breathing simulator generating an adult-type inhalation profile.ResultsWithout a VHC, throat deposition was high. With any VHC, throat deposition fell to only a few per cent of the label claim for both FP and CIC. The 1–5 µm respirable fraction was approximately one-third of the label claim for both drugs, irrespective of VHC use, while CIC generated a larger submicron fraction than FP. Small statistically significant differences between VHCs were observed, but absolute differences in APSD and throat deposition were modest.ConclusionsIn adult-representative conditions, VHCs substantially reduce oropharyngeal deposition but do not influence fine-particle delivery. Differences between the tested VHCs were minor and unlikely to be clinically meaningful, whereas FP and CIC showed distinct APSD profiles consistent with formulation type. These findings support performance-based, drug-specific evaluation of VHCs and underscore the value of systematic in vitro testing to guide evidence-based spacer–drug selection and to reduce throat deposition in clinical practice.Keywords: Asthma, Inhaler devices, Asthma Pharmacology

Introduction

Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) are first-line anti-inflammatory therapy in asthma, with fluticasone propionate (FP) and ciclesonide (CIC) widely used.1 FP pressurised metered dose inhalers (pMDIs) are suspensions, whereas CIC pMDIs are solutions.2 These formulations produce distinct aerosol characteristics; the reported mass median aerodynamic diameter (MMAD) is larger for FP than for CIC (about 2.6 µm vs 1.0 µm).3 Particle size influences lung deposition and clinical effect. Particles around 1–5 µm are most likely to reach the lower airways; larger particles deposit in the oropharynx or are swallowed, and particles below 1 µm are more likely to be exhaled.4 Accordingly, the aerodynamic particle size distribution (APSD) is central to efficient delivery and therapeutic response.5,7 APSD reflects formulation-dependent atomisation and subsequent droplet evaporation, influenced by propellant volatility, excipients and metering nozzle dynamics.8 9 Because these mechanisms differ between solution and suspension products, findings with one ICS should not be extrapolated to another.1 3 8 9

A pMDI may be used without a valved holding chamber (VHC), although oropharyngeal deposition remains a concern; VHCs can improve lung delivery and potentially reduce local adverse effects such as dysphonia and oral candidiasis.110,16 VHCs vary in volume, valve function, materials and electrostatic properties, and are therefore not interchangeable; drug-delivery performance differs between devices.17,23 Many published investigations have assessed ICS delivery using filter collection methods that do not characterise APSD24,28 or under constant flow conditions that do not reflect patient inhalation profiles.29,34 Few studies have combined patient-representative adult breathing profiles with a cascade impactor for FP or CIC.15 16 Given these differences, each VHC should be evaluated with each ICS to establish reliable performance. We assessed FP and CIC delivered with three common VHCs and without a VHC, deriving APSD by cascade impaction using an anatomical throat model under an adult simulated inhalation profile.

Methods

Instrumentation, sample collection and analyses

This study was performed in vitro (online supplemental figure S1) in an ambient laboratory environment: temperature 22.3 °C (95% CI 22.0 to 22.6) and relative humidity 41.8% (95% CI 40.9% to 42.7%). The VHCs were connected to an anatomical adult throat model (Adult Alberta Idealised Throat, Copley Scientific Limited, Nottingham, UK) with a silicone adapter to ensure an airtight seal. The throat model was connected to the mixing inlet, followed by the Next Generation Impactor (NGI, Copley Scientific Limited, Nottingham, UK).

The preparation of the test system included coating the cups of the NGI stages with a fixation solution (Brij35/ethanol/glycerol) to minimise particle bounce and re-entrainment. Aerosol samples were collected from the anatomical throat model and all eight stages of the NGI using a constant internal flow rate of 45 L/min within the NGI (see also online supplemental file 1). The calculated particle diameter cut-off values of various stages were: 9.4 µm, 5.2 µm, 3.3 µm, 1.9 µm, 1.1 µm, 0.7 µm, 0.4 µm and 0.1 µm (Micro-Orifice Collector, MOC).

To collect the samples, 30 mL of solvent (ethanol–water mixture 50% v/v) was added to the throat model and 15 mL to the NGI cups. The outlets of the throat model were covered, and the model was shaken. The cups were shaken with a gentle rocker (Copley Scientific Limited, Nottingham, UK) for 15 min. After adequate mixing, 1.5 mL of each sample was collected into a vial.

The NGI samples were analysed by high-performance liquid chromatography carried out by Emmace Consulting AB (Lund, Sweden) with the following setup: mobile phase; 96% ethanol/0.1% ammonium acetate 43/57 (v/v), pump flow rate; 1.5 mL/min, injection volume; 100 µL, detection wavelength; 226 nm, column; Waters XTerra RP18 3.5 µm, 50×4.6 mm. Validation showed linearity 0.03–32 µg/mL. The limit of quantitation was 0.03 µg/mL.

Inhalation simulations

For the three VHCs, inhalation simulation was started concurrently with pMDI actuation. A breathing simulator (Breathing simulator BRS 3100, Copley Scientific Limited, Nottingham, UK) was used to represent slow, adult-type single inhalation designed to mimic normal adult inhalation in a cooperative patient, in line with commonly recommended inhalation technique in this population (online supplemental figure S2)35: the flow rate reached 30 L/min within 0.5 s, followed by a steady flow maintained at 30 L/min for 3.5 s.36 The flow then ceased over the subsequent 0.5 s, resulting in a total inhalation duration of 4.5 s with a total inhalation volume of 2030 mL. With the no-VHC measurements, a constant flow of 30 L/min was used.

Valved holding chambers

Three different antistatic VHCs were studied (online supplemental table S1): AeroChamber plus Flow-Vu (AC, Monaghan Medical Corporation, Plattsburgh, USA), EasyChamber (EC, TriOn Pharma Ltd, London, UK) and Optichamber Diamond (OD, Philips Respironics, Chichester, UK). Before and in between the experiments, the components of the VHCs were washed and dried according to the manufacturers’ instructions to minimise residual electrostatic charges on the chamber surfaces.

Drugs

The pharmaceutical products studied were FP pMDI (Flixotide Evohaler 125 µg/dose, GlaxoSmithKline, Evreux, France) and CIC pMDI (Alvesco 160 µg/dose, Covis Pharma, Amsterdam, Netherlands). The FP pMDI was shaken vigorously for at least 10 s and primed five times before connecting it to the test system.

Replication

For each experimental setup, two doses of the drug were delivered sequentially using a pMDI. Each actuation was performed manually, with a 30-second interval between doses. Following each actuation, a single inhalation was simulated to draw the aerosol through the system. Samples were then collected from the anatomical throat model and the impactor stages.

Device variability was addressed by testing three units of the same VHC model, each sourced from a distinct manufacturing lot, for each condition. The entire procedure was repeated three times for each VHC, resulting in a total of nine measurements per VHC model.

Statistics

The software IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, V.27 (IBM Corp, NY, USA), was used for the data analysis. Proportions of the label claim of FP and CIC deposited in the throat model and as particles 1–5 µm and under 1 µm in diameter, achieved with no VHC and three different VHCs, were compared using the mean. CIs of 95% were established. To compare the APSD of FP and CIC using no VHC and three different VHCs, a general linear model of repeated measures was used. Since the data were not normally distributed, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to assess whether the differences in drug deposition for FP and CIC among the VHCs were statistically significant. To minimise the risk of Type I error associated with multiple comparisons, the Bonferroni correction was applied by dividing the level of significance by the number of comparisons made.

Results

Figure 1 presents the APSD of FP with no VHC and with three different VHCs. Without a VHC, throat deposition exceeded 110 µg, compared with approximately 10 µg when any of the VHCs were used. For all VHCs, deposition peaked at a particle size of 1.9 µm. Within the respirable range, particle deposition was slightly skewed toward smaller particles without a VHC compared with when a VHC was used. However, this was not observed for the smallest particles. Additionally, although some individual differences between the VHCs were evident from non-overlapping 95% CIs, these were all minimal in magnitude (figure 1).

Discussion

In this in vitro investigation, we found that when delivered without a VHC, throat deposition was almost half of the label claim for FP and over a quarter of the label claim for CIC. Using any of the VHCs, throat deposition became almost negligible for both medications. The deposition of 1–5 µm particles for both FP and CIC was approximately one-third of the label claim, regardless of whether a VHC was used. Yet, while FP had only a small proportion of particles under 1 µm, CIC exhibited a similar fraction of particles in both the under 1 µm and 1–5 µm ranges. As a result, the delivered dose beyond the throat model for CIC was higher than that for FP. There were some differences between the VHC models, as indicated by non-overlapping 95% CIs, but none of these differences was of a magnitude likely to reach clinical significance.

In accordance with our results, previous in vivo research suggests that without a VHC, CIC has significantly lower oropharyngeal deposition compared with FP.37 Additional studies confirm the inherently low throat deposition of CIC relative to other ICSs.38 39 With fewer drug particles trapped in the oropharyngeal area, it is expected that local side effects, such as oral candidiasis, dysphonia and throat irritation, would be less frequent and less severe.40 This expectation is supported by clinical studies, which have reported that CIC has a more favourable side effect profile compared with FP.41 42

We observed that the use of any of the three studied VHCs reduced throat deposition to only a few per cent of the label claim for both FP and CIC. This observation is consistent with prior findings for FP, although similar data for CIC have been scarce.21 22 Based on our results, we hypothesise that any differences in local side effects between FP and CIC are unlikely to persist when VHC is used for drug delivery. This hypothesis is supported by the principles described in the aerosol maturation phase of solution-based pMDIs. According to Zhu et al,8 once droplets exit the actuator, they undergo rapid evaporation of volatile components and possible secondary atomisation, a process that defines the final size and mass of respirable particles. The evaporation kinetics are influenced by formulation (such as ethanol content), actuator design and environmental conditions. Importantly, VHCs act as an extension of the mouthpiece that allows more time and space for aerosol maturation. This leads to lower plume velocity and more complete evaporation of the propellant and cosolvent before the aerosol reaches the oropharynx. In addition, larger, high-momentum particles are preferentially retained within the chamber through impaction on the internal walls and valve components, so that the aerosol reaching the oral cavity consists of smaller, lower-momentum particles, thereby minimising inertial impaction in the throat. This mechanism helps explain why throat deposition for both FP and CIC was markedly reduced when administered via a VHC, and why differences in deposition between the two drugs may become negligible under these conditions.

In our study, the proportion of the label claim delivered as particles within the respirable range was comparable for FP and CIC. Interestingly, the pMDI used without a VHC produced a slightly higher fraction of fine particles within the respirable range compared with when any VHC was used. This may be explained by the retention of fine particles within the VHC itself, particularly on the chamber walls, baffles or valve components. Factors such as internal surface area, electrostatic charge and spacer design can influence this deposition. In contrast, the direct actuation of the pMDI without a VHC may result in less particle agglomeration and higher initial velocity, leading to a greater proportion of smaller particles being detected in the cascade impactor.

CIC generated a substantial fraction of particles smaller than 1 µm, resulting in a higher total delivered dose compared with FP. Notably, approximately half of the CIC dose consisted of submicron particles. The behaviour and therapeutic relevance of particles with a diameter below 1 µm remain a topic of debate. While it is commonly assumed that these particles are largely exhaled due to their minimal settling velocity, their capacity to contribute meaningfully to clinical efficacy within the lungs is still uncertain.4 43 Pooled analysis of scintigraphic studies conducted on healthy volunteers and patients with asthma, using pMDIs or DPIs, demonstrated a different trend. The findings indicated that lung deposition increases as MMAD decreases, with particles <1 µm achieving lung deposition rates exceeding 50% of the delivered drug dose.44 Additionally, the mean proportion of particles exhaled was less than 5% of the emitted dose, suggesting that such extra-fine particles are well suited for inhalation therapy.39 44 Moreover, the clinical efficacy of CIC and FP in the treatment of asthma has been shown to be comparable, with neither drug demonstrating a clear advantage.45 46 Hence, while small particles may have a slightly higher potential for exhalation, this is more than balanced by their ability to distribute throughout the lungs and achieve high pulmonary deposition, including in the distal airways.43

There are relatively few in vitro studies directly comparing how different VHCs affect the APSD of FP using a cascade impactor.15 16 21 31 47 Additionally, an anatomical adult throat model was used in only three studies.15 16 22 While some studies have reported considerable variation in VHC performance, others have found no meaningful differences.2122 31,33 47 Ojanperä et al examined FP delivered using AC, EC and OD with a child throat model and both tidal breathing and a 3-second steady single inhalation. They found larger differences in the APSD of FP between different VHCs with child breathing patterns, but the 3-second inhalation measurements were consistent with our results, showing that the performance of AC, EC and OD was mostly comparable. It seems that, at least for AC, EC and OD, larger differences in VHC performance are observed in children than in adults.21

In a pharmacokinetic study, Drollmann et al reported that using a specific VHC model (AeroChamber Plus) with an Andersen impactor did not influence lung deposition and pharmacokinetics of the pharmacologically active metabolite of CIC, desisobutyryl-CIC. They reported an APSD similar to that in our study but did not measure throat deposition or compare different VHCs.48

In our study, we observed some statistically significant differences in the performance of the AC, EC and OD chambers. However, the absolute differences in APSD and throat deposition were small and thus unlikely to translate into clinically relevant effects on treatment outcomes or local side effects when delivering either FP or CIC. It is important to note that our study was not designed to prove that VHCs are or are not interchangeable, nor did we have any prior assumptions about their relative performance. The aim was to generate objective, comparative data to determine whether differences exist between these devices, and if so, how they manifest in terms of APSD characteristics and oropharyngeal deposition. The VHCs included in our study were selected based on their prior use in clinical and experimental contexts and were generally expected to perform well, which likely explains the modest differences observed.21

Our study has several strengths. We employed a highly repeatable in vitro setup using the NGI, a state-of-the-art cascade impactor system specifically designed for pharmaceutical aerosol characterisation.49 The NGI enables precise and reproducible measurements of APSD, which is critical for evaluating inhaled drug delivery performance. The use of an anatomically representative adult throat model allowed us to simulate upper airway geometry more accurately and to quantify throat deposition, a critical factor in assessing the risk of local side effects. Additionally, the incorporation of a breathing simulator closely mimicked real-life inhalation profiles, enhancing the clinical relevance of the aerosol performance data.36 By testing multiple VHCs from different manufacturing lots, we improved the robustness and generalisability of our findings. A key advantage of our controlled in vitro approach was the ability to isolate and study specific variables, such as VHC design and formulation characteristics, without the confounding effects present in in vivo studies.

It is also important to note the limitations of our study. We used an adult throat model and a steady inhalation-only breathing profile that may not reflect the smaller throat and tidal breathing patterns typical of young children. Therefore, our findings are not directly applicable to paediatric use. Additionally, our data are only valid for the VHCs and drugs studied.

The question of whether in vitro data alone are sufficient to support the use of a new VHC with a specific inhaled drug formulation has been the subject of ongoing debate. Regulatory authorities such as the European Medicines Agency and the US Food and Drug Administration acknowledge the value of well-designed in vitro studies, particularly when they are conducted under standardised conditions and include relevant performance metrics such as APSD, emitted dose and throat deposition. These studies can provide detailed mechanistic insight into drug delivery performance that would be difficult or even impractical to measure in vivo. Nonetheless, in vitro studies cannot fully replicate the complexity of human physiology, including factors such as lung anatomy, airway humidity and patient inhalation technique, which all influence clinical outcomes. Therefore, while our results strongly support the rational selection of specific VHCs for particular drug formulations, they should be interpreted as complementary to, not a replacement for, clinical data when making regulatory or therapeutic decisions.

In conclusion, while FP and CIC achieved a similar fraction of label claim deposited as particles in the 1–5 µm range, CIC had a higher delivered dose, a higher proportion of particles smaller than 1 µm and lower throat deposition compared with FP. Using a VHC did not improve drug delivery, but it reduced throat deposition to almost negligible levels for both FP and CIC. The minor differences observed in the performance of AC, EC and OD are unlikely to have clinical relevance, as the VHCs generally performed similarly. Nevertheless, our findings do not imply that all VHCs perform equally but rather underline the importance of evaluating each VHC individually and in combination with specific inhaled medications. Systematic in vitro studies are essential to build an evidence base that informs the optimal selection of VHC-drug combinations. This approach ensures that therapeutic decisions are guided by robust data, ultimately supporting effective use of inhaled medications for the benefit of the patient.
