Association between the systemic immune-inflammation index and mortality risk in myocardial infarction patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Baobin Cui, Yufei Sun, Di Cao, Jian Zhang, Xin Sui, Yilun Liu, Wei Sun, Chang Liu, Lei Zhang, Chengke Yin, Wenjia Xu, Jingshun Zhao, Runfeng Zhou, Tong Wu, Hongbo Gao, Yifan Zhao, Meijiao Yang, Fei Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) has shown potential as a prognostic biomarker in several diseases. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between SII and mortality in patients with myocardial infarction (MI).MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted from the MIMIC-Ⅳ database (n = 774) and Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital (n = 482). Patients were divided into four quartiles based on SII, and mortality at 28, 90, and 180 days was analyzed. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox regression models adjusted for confounders were used to assess the relationship between SII and mortality. Additionally, restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to explore the nonlinear relationship between SII and 28-day mortality.ResultsRCS analysis revealed a significant nonlinear J-shaped association between SII and mortality risk, with the lowest mortality risk observed at approximately 770 × 10⁹/L of SII. In quartile analysis, the highest SII (Q4) had significantly higher mortality rates at 28 days (30.93% vs. 8.29%, P < 0.001), 90 days (38.14% vs. 11.92%, P < 0.001), and 180 days (42.78% vs. 12.44%, P < 0.001), while the lowest SII group (Q1) demonstrated a modest increase compared to the second quartile (Q2). Multivariate analysis confirmed SII as an independent predictor of 28-day mortality (adjusted HR for Q4: 3.04, 95% CI 1.30–7.10, P = 0.005). Subgroup analysis showed the strongest association in patients with congestive heart failure (HR: 1.48, 95% CI 1.26–1.88, P = 0.005).ConclusionsSII exhibits a threshold effect on post-MI mortality, where values exceeding 770 × 10⁹/L independently predict adverse outcomes, suggesting its potential as a prognostic tool for early risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03399-1.

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) accounts for 18% of global cardiovascular deaths, with 30-day mortality rates exceeding 10% even in the modern reperfusion therapy era [1–4]. While current diagnostic strategies relying on Electrocardiograms (ECG) and high-sensitivity troponins (hs-Tn) have improved detection, critical gaps persist in early risk stratification: ECG demonstrates only 68% sensitivity for non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and current ESC/ACC guidelines recommend rapid 0/1-h and 0/2-h algorithms to expedite diagnosis [5–7]. Accordingly, simple markers from routine admission tests, such as complete blood counts, are attractive because they are inexpensive, universally available, and provide rapid results to support early risk stratification.

The systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII) is a novel biomarker derived from neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts, providing an integrated measure of inflammatory and thrombotic activity in acute myocardial infarction (AMI) [8–11]. Recent studies have increasingly explored its relevance in AMI. For instance, Li et al. demonstrated that SII outperformed traditional indices such as NLR and PLR in predicting in-hospital major adverse cardiovascular events among AMI patients [12]. Sun et al. reported that SII levels rose progressively with the severity of coronary artery disease, suggesting its role in reflecting coronary lesion burden [13]. In elderly AMI populations, Chen et al. showed that incorporating SII and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) into a prognostic nomogram improved the prediction of in-hospital mortality [14]. Furthermore, in STEMI cohorts, Marchi et al. found that elevated SII was associated with long-term mortality risk, although the prognostic effect diminished after adjustment for renal function and natriuretic peptides [15]. Together, these findings highlight that SII reflects systemic inflammatory processes central to AMI and holds promise as a prognostic tool.

Nevertheless, prior studies were largely limited to single-center or regional cohorts, often with relatively small sample sizes and short-term outcomes. Importantly, few investigations have systematically examined how comorbidities or clinical heterogeneity influence the prognostic value of SII in critically ill AMI patients.

To address these gaps, the present study utilizes two large-scale cohorts: a Western Intensive Care Unit (ICU) population from the MIMIC-IV (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-Ⅳ) database and an Eastern multicenter registry from China to comprehensively assess the prognostic role of SII in AMI. By integrating diverse healthcare settings and applying advanced statistical approaches, including restricted cubic splines for nonlinear modeling and propensity score matching for confounding control, our study is designed to provide a more robust and generalizable evaluation of SII as a prognostic marker.

Methods

Data source and study design

Two independent cohorts were included in this study: The primary cohort was derived from the MIMIC-Ⅳ database, a publicly available database developed by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). The MIMIC-IV database comprises anonymized data from more than 70,000 patients with MI admitted to the ICU of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) between 2008 and 2019 [16–19]. The MIMIC database includes demographics, clinical observations, laboratory test results, medication records, and patient outcomes. For permission to access the database, the author (BC) had accomplished a recognized course in the Protecting Human Research Participants (certification number: 48236799). To ensure the protection of patient information, identifiers had been replaced with random codes, thereby eliminating the need for obtaining patient consent and ethical approval. A validation cohort of 4257 MI patients from three hospitals in China: The Second Affiliated Hospital of Qiqihar Medical University (Qiqihar, Heilongjiang), The Baoquanling Hospital of Beidahuang Group (Hegang, Heilongjiang), and Shuangyashan Shuangkuang Hospital (Shuangyashan, Heilongjiang), with data collected between January 2018 and December 2023.

Study population

Both cohorts adhered to identical inclusion exclusion criteria. Patients in both cohorts have a confirmed diagnosis of MI. The primary cohort’s diagnosis was based on ICD-10 (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision) criteria, while the validation cohort’s diagnosis was confirmed through clinical evaluation, electrocardiographic findings, and elevated cardiac biomarkers (troponin I/T). Inclusion criteria required that patients were aged 18 years or older and had complete clinical datasets and documented follow-up. Exclusion criteria included patients with incomplete medical records, failure to meet the ICU admission criteria, multiple admissions to the ICU, loss to follow-up, or other comorbid conditions that could potentially confound the inflammatory biomarker interpretation. It should be noted that in the MIMIC-IV cohort, ICD-10 codes do not reliably distinguish type 1 MI, type 2 MI, or isolated myocardial injury, and STEMI/NSTEMI differentiation was not consistently feasible due to incomplete ECG and clinical data. Data collection

Data for both cohorts were collected from a range of clinical sources, including comorbidities, vital signs, laboratory parameters, and clinical scoring systems. Comorbidities include congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), severe liver disease, and chronic kidney disease, as well as the Charlson comorbidity index. Vital signs measured were temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, mean blood pressure, and percutaneous oxygen saturation (SpO2). Laboratory tests included blood cell counts, metabolic markers, and electrolyte profiles. Disease severity was assessed using the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and the Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II) for mortality risk.

The systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII) was calculated using the formula: SII = neutrophil count (× 109/L) × platelet count (× 109/L)/lymphocyte count (× 109/L) [20].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was 28-day mortality, while secondary outcomes included in-hospital mortality, ICU mortality, and 90-day and 180-day mortality. For the primary cohort, mortality outcomes were obtained from structured fields in MIMIC-IV. In-hospital mortality was defined using the hospital_expire_flag. Post-discharge mortality at 28, 90, and 180 days was determined from the date of death (dod) recorded in the patients table, which is derived from Social Security death records. Patients without a documented death date were considered alive and censored at the last available follow-up date in the database. In the validation cohort, outcomes were tracked via hospital follow-up systems until death or the last recorded visit; patients lost to follow-up were censored as alive.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages). Patients were stratified into quartiles (Q1–Q4) based on SII values. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were applied to explore potential nonlinear associations between SII and mortality. Knot locations were placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the SII distribution, following established recommendations. Internal validation of the RCS model was performed using bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations, and a calibration curve was generated to evaluate the agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities. Kaplan–Meier survival curves with log-rank tests were used to compare mortality across groups. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to evaluate the association between SII and mortality, adjusting for confounders (age, comorbidities, and laboratory parameters). To address potential collinearity, we examined variance inflation factors (VIFs) for SII. Variables with VIF > 5 were considered to indicate multicollinearity. Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the association of SII with mortality across different demographic and clinical subgroups, including patients with and without specific comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, or coronary artery disease. The validation cohort followed identical analytical methods (e.g., stratified by sex or comorbidities). Missing data (< 20% missingness) were handled via multiple imputation. Analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 3.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), GraphPad Prism software (version 5.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and IBM SPSS Statistical software (Version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 774 patients with MI met the eligibility criteria for this study, comprising 261 males (33.7%) and 513 females (66.3%). In this study, patients were divided into four quartiles (Q1: < 642 × 109/L, Q2: 642–1094 × 109/L, Q3: 1094–2059 × 109/L, Q4: > 2059 × 109/L), based on their SII. The baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Patients in the highest quartile (Q4) were older (median age: 75 vs. 70 years in Q1, P = 0.002) and had higher rates of congestive heart failure (60.82% vs. 42.49%, P < 0.001) and chronic kidney disease (43.30% vs. 29.02%, P < 0.001). Laboratory results showed higher white blood cell counts (13.34 vs. 10.03 × 10⁹/L, P < 0.001), neutrophils (13.71 vs. 6.90 × 10⁹/L, P < 0.001), and platelets (234.30 vs. 149.71 × 10⁹/L, P < 0.001) in the higher quartile, while lymphocytes were lower (0.89 vs. 2.27 × 10⁹/L, P < 0.001). Mortality rates were significantly higher in Q4, with 28-day mortality at 30.93% vs. 11.40% in Q1 (P < 0.001). These findings suggest that higher SII levels are associated with increased disease severity and mortality in MI patients. Cox regression analysis

The univariable Cox regression analysis indicated that SII, age, heart failure, hypertension, acute kidney failure, and pneumonia significantly influenced 28-day mortality (Table S1). For the multivariable Cox regression analysis, two models were constructed (Table 2). Model 1 adjusted for variables like age, mean blood pressure, congestive heart failure, and pneumonia, while Model 2 further included white blood cell count, serum glucose, potassium in blood, and other key parameters. Collinearity diagnostics showed that all variables had VIFs < 3, suggesting no significant multicollinearity between SII and its component variables (Table S2). In Model 2, SII was a significant predictor of mortality, with the third quartile (Q3) showing a hazard ratio (HR) of 2.96 (95% CI 1.29–5.85 P = 0.015) and the fourth quartile (Q4) showing a HR of 3.04 (95% CI 1.30–7.10, P = 0.005) compared to the second quartile (reference group). These results underscore the significant association between higher SII levels and increased mortality risk in MI, even after adjusting for potential confounders. Baseline characteristics and prognosis in the validation group

In the validation group, a total of 482 patients were included, divided into four quartiles (Q1–Q4) based on their SII. The number of patients in each quartile was as follows: 120 patients in Q1, 120 patients in Q2, 121 patients in Q3, and 121 patients in Q4 (Table S3). Significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed across these quartiles, particularly in age, gender, and comorbidities such as a history of hypertension, a previous stroke, COPD, congestive heart failure, and malignant cancer. For instance, patients in the Q4 group were older and had higher rates of comorbidities, with congestive heart failure occurring in 16.53% and COPD in 34.71% of Q4 patients. As SII increased, laboratory parameters such as white blood cell count, neutrophils, and platelets were significantly higher, while lymphocyte levels decreased. Mortality rates also varied across the quartiles, with 28-day mortality in Q4 being 36.4%, significantly higher than the 8.3% observed in Q2. Similar trends were seen for hospital mortality. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that patients in the higher SII quartiles (Q3 and Q4) had lower survival probabilities at 28 days and hospital days (Figure S1). The survival curves indicated that higher SII levels were closely associated with poorer survival outcomes. The univariable analysis of 28-day mortality is presented in Table S4. Cox regression analysis further confirmed that SII is a significant predictor of 28-day mortality, with patients in the higher quartiles facing a significantly higher risk of death, even after adjusting for factors such as laboratory parameters and comorbidities (Table S5).

Collectively, the results from the validation group indicate that higher SII levels are associated with worse prognosis. This highlights the potential of SII as a valuable prognostic tool in clinical settings for predicting mortality risk in MI patients.

Discussion

This study provides new insights into the prognostic role of the SII in AMI. We found a clear J-shaped association between SII and mortality, with the lowest risk occurring at approximately 770 × 109/L, suggesting that both excessive inflammation and immune suppression may be harmful. Importantly, our findings were replicated in two complementary cohorts, including a Western ICU-based population from MIMIC-IV and an Eastern multicenter registry, which supports the robustness and generalizability of SII across diverse patient populations. In addition, subgroup analyses indicated that the prognostic effect of SII was particularly pronounced in patients with comorbidities such as congestive heart failure. Finally, SII showed consistent predictive value at 28, 90, and 180 days, extending prior literature that mainly reported in-hospital or short-term outcomes.

SII emerges as a compelling prognostic tool in MI by uniquely integrating three pivotal components of the inflammatory and thrombotic cascades: neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets [21, 22]. Unlike traditional biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) or interleukin-6 (IL-6), which primarily reflect acute-phase responses with limited specificity for immune dysregulation, SII captures a broader spectrum of pathophysiological processes [23–26]. By integrating neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets into a single index, SII reflects both inflammation and thrombosis [27–29]. This triad of mechanisms positions SII as a more holistic indicator of systemic inflammation, particularly relevant in MI where both hyperinflammation and immune exhaustion coexist. For instance, elevated neutrophils and platelets accompanied by lymphopenia may create a synergistic interplay of microvascular obstruction, impaired tissue repair, and secondary infection risk, thereby directly linking the index to adverse outcomes [30, 31]. SII has already been shown to effectively predict prognosis in fields like cancer and sepsis [32, 33]. For example, in patients with lung cancer, gastrointestinal cancer, and liver cancer, those with higher SII have a higher risk of postoperative recurrence and mortality [33–35].

In this study, we explored the nonlinear relationship between SII and 28-day mortality using RCS. The results showed a clear J-shaped curve for SII and mortality, with higher mortality at lower SII levels and again at higher SII levels. This trend may be closely related to the complexity of the body’s inflammatory response. In MI patients, low SII may reflect a weaker immune response, indicating that these patients have a less robust inflammatory reaction, potentially leading to poorer immune function and higher infection risks, which in turn result in higher mortality. When SII is high, an excessive inflammatory response may lead to more severe organ damage and organ dysfunction, further increasing the risk of death. Therefore, both low and high SII values are associated with a higher risk of death, and this nonlinear relationship highlights the complex immune and inflammatory mechanisms in MI patients.

This study also performed a subgroup analysis to explore the relationship between SII and 28-day mortality in different patient groups. The results showed that SII was more strongly associated with mortality in male patients, and those with comorbidities (such as congestive heart failure, hyperlipidemia, etc.). Compared to other studies, our findings further confirm the prognostic value of SII in these specific subgroups. For instance, a study by Zheng et al. found that the relationship between SII and mortality was more pronounced in patients with hypertensive myocardial infarction [36]. This phenomenon might be due to the increased number of comorbidities in hypertensive patients, which leads to more intense inflammatory reactions, thereby making the increase in SII a better reflection of the severity of the patient’s condition. The subgroup analysis also showed that SII predicted mortality more strongly in male patients compared to female patients, which could be related to gender differences in immune responses. In addition, comorbidities such as congestive heart failure and diabetes may enhance systemic inflammation, leading to increased SII levels and further exacerbating the risk of mortality [37–40].

From a clinical perspective, SII provides an immediately available and inexpensive measure of systemic inflammation that may complement established biomarkers such as troponin and CRP, as well as validated risk scores like Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) [41]. Although it cannot replace these tools, a higher SII at admission could alert clinicians to an increased risk of adverse outcomes and support decisions for intensified monitoring or earlier consultation, particularly in ICU settings where inflammatory burden is substantial. Conversely, values near the observed nadir should not be considered definitive low-risk thresholds but should be interpreted in the broader clinical context. Thus, SII may be most useful as an adjunctive marker to refine early risk stratification in AMI patients.

This study has several limitations. First, AMI cases in the MIMIC-IV cohort were identified using ICD-9/ICD-10 discharge codes. Although these codes reflect clinicians’ documentation at discharge, they do not ensure full adherence to the Universal Definition of MI. Consequently, some misclassification between type 1 MI, type 2 MI, and myocardial injury may exist, and consistent STEMI/NSTEMI differentiation was not possible. Second, the MIMIC-IV cohort included only ICU-admitted patients, representing a critically ill subset of the AMI population; thus, our findings should be extrapolated to less severe patients with caution and require validation in broader cohorts. Third, our outcome analyses were restricted to in-hospital mortality, which may underestimate longer-term risks. In addition, as a retrospective database study, residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly due to unavailable variables such as reperfusion strategy, medication adherence, and detailed treatment protocols. Finally, residual confounding, particularly from factors like infection or sepsis, especially in the highest SII quartile, cannot be excluded. The role of infection as a mediator or time-varying covariate was not explored in this study and should be addressed in future research. Lastly, we did not compare the prognostic performance of SII with established scores such as GRACE or SAPS II; therefore, incremental prognostic value was not assessed and should be addressed in future studies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, SII’s ability to synthesize neutrophil-driven injury, lymphocyte-mediated immunity, and platelet hyperactivity into a single metric offers a pragmatic advance in MI prognostication. SII could refine early therapeutic strategies, ultimately improving outcomes in this high-stakes population. However, further research is needed to explore the role of low SII levels, especially in conjunction with dynamic changes during patient treatment, to investigate its potential in personalized treatment. Moreover, combining SII with other clinical indicators (such as SOFA scores, blood oxygen saturation, etc.) may further enhance its clinical application value.
