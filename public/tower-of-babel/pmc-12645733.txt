Collagenase-mediated extracellular matrix targeting for enhanced drug penetration and therapeutic efficacy in nanoscale delivery systems for cancer therapy
Authors: Pengyu Gong, Fei Wang, Yixin Hua, Jie Ying, Jianxiang Chen, Yiting Qiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The highly fibrotic extracellular matrix (ECM) constitutes a major barrier that restricts the penetration and delivery efficiency of anticancer drugs, particularly in fibrotic tumors such as pancreatic cancer, in which cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) secrete excess collagen and cross-linking enzymes to establish a mechanically resistant and poorly compressible tumor microenvironment (TME). This review summarized the pathological mechanisms underlying collagen enrichment in the ECM and its pivotal role in tumor drug resistance, with a systematic overview on recent advances in collagenase-based anticancer therapies, especially those incorporated nano-carrier targeted drug delivery technology. Such approaches degrade the ECM collagen barrier, reducing interstitial pressure and collagen density, thereby substantially improving drug penetration and distribution in TME. Moreover, nano-carrier-mediated collagenase delivery not only circumvents the off-target toxicity associated with systemic administration but also facilitates precise local release and microenvironmental modulation, significantly enhancing the efficacy of other therapies. Critically, this review uniquely differentiates itself from existing literature by providing a comprehensive and comparative evaluation of the burgeoning landscape of nanocarriers specifically engineered for collagenase delivery. We meticulously categorize, analyze, and tabulate diverse nanocarrier platforms (e.g., liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, micelles, inorganic nanoparticles, hydrogel​) employed in this context over recent years. Their demonstrated efficacy in overcoming the ECM barrier and enhancing subsequent therapeutic outcomes in preclinical models of fibrotic cancers. To our knowledge, this work represents the first dedicated and systematic review within the past five years that consolidates and critically contrasts this rapidly evolving array of nanotechnological approaches for collagenase delivery aimed at ECM modulation. As an emerging synergistic treatment strategy, nano-carrier targeted collagenase delivery exhibits remarkable translational potential as an adjuvant therapy in the treatment of cancer.Graphical abstract

Introduction

The extracellular matrix (ECM) is a dynamic and intricate network that constitutes a critical component of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Synthesized and secreted by cells into the extracellular space, the ECM primarily comprises carbohydrates and proteins, including collagen, elastin, proteoglycans, hyaluronic acid, laminin, and fibronectin [1]. These molecules undergo cross-linking to form a structural scaffold, which provides essential physical support and biochemical cues for cellular survival. Within TME, ECM exerts multifaceted regulatory roles in tumor progression through alterations in molecular composition, biomechanical properties, signal transduction, cell–cell interactions, and other key biological processes [2–6].

ECM plays a critical role in tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Prominent alterations in the tumor-associated ECM include compositional changes, spatial structural abnormalities, and fibroblast activation. The biomechanical properties of ECM, particularly stiffness and structural organization, significantly influence tumor progression. Tumor tissues typically exhibit elevated ECM stiffness compared to normal tissues, which promotes malignant behavior through mechanotransduction pathways such as the Hippo signaling cascade [7]. Fibrosis and ECM remodeling are major contributors to increased stiffness, primarily driven by cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) via secreting ECM components, thereby creating a tumor-permissive mechanical microenvironment. Beyond mechanical regulation, the ECM mediates intracellular and extracellular signaling via transmembrane receptors, notably integrins. Upon binding to ECM ligands such as fibronectin and laminin, integrins activate downstream pathways including FAK, Src, and PI3K/AKT to promote tumor cell survival, proliferation, and migration [8, 9]. Moreover, ECM also modulates immune cell function through physical and chemical interactions. For instance, ECM stiffness and collagen can polarize tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward the pro-tumor M2 phenotype, and dense fibrotic structures act as physical barriers which impair T cell infiltration and antitumor activity. Additionally, the ECM facilitates tumor angiogenesis via CAF-secreted growth factors that stimulate endothelial cells, which supports neovascularization and facilitates intratumoral nutrient supply [10, 11].

ECM, as the core architectural component of TME, undergoes abnormal compositional changes and biomechanical remodeling. These alterations, coupled with associated complex signaling networks, not only drive malignant behavior of tumor cells but also establish a dense physical barrier and inhibitory signaling milieu that severely compromises the functions of endothelial cells and immune cells. Within the fibrotic network of TME, collagen-the primary structural component of ECM-forms a significant physical barrier to drug penetration through abnormal deposition and cross-linking [12]. Fibrillar collagens, particularly types I and III, accumulate excessively in TME, displaying disorganized arrangement and extensive cross-linking that creates a dense reticular structure [13]. This phenomenon not only substantially increases tissue stiffness but also severely impedes the diffusion of therapeutic molecules (including chemotherapeutic agents, targeted antibodies, and nucleic acid therapeutics) and immune effector cells (such as chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) cells) [14–17]. The resulting ECM densification leads to uneven drug distribution and subtherapeutic concentrations within tumor tissue, ultimately compromising treatment efficacy [14]. Therefore, targeted degradation of these aberrant collagen deposits has emerged as a crucial strategy for overcoming the ECM barrier and improving drug delivery. Collagenase has been demonstrated to be a potent tool for remodeling ECM, reducing tissue density, and thereby effectively enhancing drug delivery efficiency, owing to its ability to specifically hydrolyze the triple-helix structure of collagen. However, the systemic delivery of free collagenase faces numerous challenges, including poor in vivo stability, short half-life, risks of non-specific tissue damage, and difficulties in achieving effective concentration accumulation at tumor sites [18]. Against this backdrop, nanomedicine offers a robust platform for the safe, efficient, and targeted delivery of collagenase, synergistically enhancing drug permeability through its unique advantages [19]. By encapsulating collagenase within meticulously engineered nanocarriers, preserved enzymatic activity, extended collagenase circulation time, as well as the rational co-delivery with other therapeutic agents can be achieved, which enables precisely targeted degradation of the collagen barrier, paving the way for efficient penetration of subsequent drug molecules and ultimately realizing synergistic therapeutic outcomes.

ECM degradation has emerged as promising adjuvant strategies for precision cancer therapy since reducing intratumoral collagen fibers may enhance drug accumulation and immunocyte infiltration in the core of tumor. This review summarizes and discusses about current therapeutic approaches employing nanocarriers to: (1) deliver collagenase specifically to TEM, and (2) target CAFs to suppress collagen deposition (Fig. 3). These strategies aim to disrupt the matrix barrier in tumors, thereby modifying both mechanical and immune microenvironments while improving drug permeability and delivery efficiency for cancer therapy.

Tumor microenvironment fibrosis and drug delivery barriers

Mechanisms of collagen deposition in the extracellular matrix

The activation of CAFs is widely recognized as a major contributor to collagen deposition and cross-linking by producing enzymes such as lysyl oxidase (LOX), thus leading to tissue stiffness in TME [20–22]. At the cellular level, CAFs originate from quiescent fibroblasts in normal tissues and undergo functional transformation during tumorigenesis and progression in response to diverse stimuli. These stimuli include, but are not limited to, cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors secreted by tumor cells [23, 24]. Upon pathway activation, CAFs exhibit altered gene expression profiles, with significant upregulation of collagen synthesis-related genes, ultimately leading to increased collagen production [26]. In addition to direct secreting excess collagen and cross-linking enzymes, CAFs indirectly facilitate ECM remodeling to promote collagen accumulation and stabilization through interactions with neighboring cells, such as tumor cells and immune cells [27]. Moreover, the activation state of CAFs is further modulated by other characters of TME. For example, hypoxia, a hallmark of solid tumors, enhances both CAF activation and collagen secretion [28, 29]. Collagen deposition reduces tumor drug delivery efficiency

ECM is a critical barrier for drug penetration in solid tumors. Traditional chemotherapy compounds exhibit impaired efficacy in cancer types characterized by ECM hardening and excessive collagen deposition [31, 32]. Specifically, dense ECM has been observed in multiple malignancies, including breast, pancreatic, colorectal, ovarian, and lung cancers [27, 33–35], among which pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) features an exceptionally rigid ECM with collagen fiber deposition reaching up to 90% [36]. However, first-line PDAC therapies, such as gemcitabine and paclitaxel, demonstrate poor antitumor efficacy, largely due to the formidable ECM barrier and excessive collagen accumulation [37–39]. Mounting evidences have demonstrated that elevated levels of collagen or hyaluronic acid in the ECM correlate with worse patient prognosis and treatment failure of conventional chemotherapy [40–44]. In the recent era of precise oncology, targeted therapies represent a significant advancement by acting on specific molecular targets of tumor cells to inhibit their malignant behaviors selectively. However, targeted drugs still exhibit suboptimal accumulation and penetration in the core of tumor in vivo, which could be attributed to two key factors: (1) the irregular and heterogeneous tumor vasculature hinders uniform drug distribution, particularly in the central tumor region [35], and (2) the dense collagen network in the ECM reduces inter-fiber porosity, impairing drug diffusion and penetration (Fig. 2) [21, 45].Fig. 2Schematic diagram of drug delivery and penetration difficulties caused by ECM collagen deposition and cross-linking. The excessive deposition of collagen within ECM hinders drug diffusion as physical barrier, preventing therapeutic molecules to navigate through tightly packed fibers to reach target cells or tissues [21, 34, 46–49]. The pore size within collagen network progressively diminishes as fiber accumulation increases. While normal tissue permits relatively unimpeded diffusion of small-molecule drugs, excessive collagen deposition restricts passage to only those with molecular weights below a few thousand Dalton [50–54]. However, most anticancer drugs, particularly large molecules like monoclonal antibodies, can exceed tens of thousands of Dalton in molecular size, which encounter difficulties in the process of traversing these narrowed pores produced by excessive collagen buildup.To address the problem of drug delivery barriers caused by excessive collagen deposition and cross-linking, researchers have developed various therapeutic strategies targeting ECM during cancer therapy. Among these, targeting CAFs has emerged as a key approach [22], given their dual role as the primary source of pathological collagen deposition and enzymes catalyzing collagen cross-linking [55]. This approach facilitates selective recognition of CAFs, enabling cytotoxic drugs to eliminate them, thereby reducing collagen deposition and suppressing tumor cell proliferation and invasion [61, 62]. Similarly, strategies targeting CAFs and FAP-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}—including antibody immunotoxins, nanoparticle delivery systems, and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) systems—have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy. Moreover, CAFs not only overproduce collagen but also sustain their activated state through autocrine and paracrine signaling, stimulating neighboring CAFs and tumor cells. This interaction establishes a self-reinforcing feedback loop that exacerbates fibrosis and tumor progression. Disrupting these key signaling pathways represents another critical strategy to attenuate the ECM barrier. Further elaboration is provided in subsequent sections of this review.

In the other hand, direct degradation of barrier-forming collagen has gained traction as another strategy. Specifically, Local administration of collagenases, such as bacterial collagenase or matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), is expected to cleave collagen fiber networks, as well as to reduce ECM density and interstitial hydraulic pressure, thereby improving drug penetration and distribution. For instance, Eikenes et al. examined the impact of collagenase on interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) and microvascular pressure (MVP) in osteosarcoma xenografts, assessing its potential to enhance tumor uptake and distribution of the monoclonal antibody TP-3 [63]. Their findings revealed that 0.1% collagenase substantially reduced IFP (by 45%) and MVP (by 60%). Notably, differing recovery kinetics caused MVP to rebound before IFP reached its minimum, thereby amplifying the transcapillary pressure gradient. Consequently, tumor uptake of the monoclonal antibody doubled, and its intratumoral distribution exhibited remarkable improvement. Additionally, collagenase-induced MVP reduction stemmed primarily from diminished vascular resistance induced by microvascular collagen degradation, while such treatment did not show significant effect on mean arterial pressure. Collectively, this study underscores that ECM degradation improves the transvascular pressure gradient for enhanced antibody penetration and recognition in tumor tissues. In a 2012 study, Kato et al. examined how collagenase-1 (also known as Matrix Metalloproteinase-1, one of mammalian endogenous collagenases) modulates intratumoral interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) and facilitates the accumulation and transfection efficiency of cationic liposome/plasmid DNA complexes (lipoplexes) within tumors [64]. Employing a Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) murine model, they observed that intravenous collagenase-1 administration substantially reduced tumor-associated type I collagen content, leading to a rapid 65% decrease in IFP within an hour post-injection. This modification resulted in a 1.5-fold increase in intratumoral lipoplex accumulation and a concomitant twofold enhancement of transgene expression. These results indicate that collagenase-1-mediated IFP reduction significantly improves systemic lipoplex delivery, suggesting a promising approach for tumor-targeted gene therapy. McKee et al. investigated the propagation efficiency of the oncolytic herpes simplex virus (HSV) vector MGH2 in human melanoma Mu89 and found that fibrillar collagen in the impeded viral diffusion [65]. Their study revealed that viral size directly influences diffusion efficiency, as nanoparticles of comparable dimensions exhibited similar distribution ranges within tumors. The limited viral penetration prevented complete tumor eradication. However, co-injecting bacterial collagenase to modify the matrix significantly improved the virus’s initial intratumoral distribution. This modification enhanced the coverage of infected tumor cells, increased viral replication efficiency, and ultimately improved therapeutic efficacy. These findings underscore fibrillar collagen as a critical barrier to viral diffusion and demonstrate that ECM modification can substantially enhance oncolytic virus therapy. Collectively, these examples have significant implications for gene delivery strategies and nanotechnology-based delivery systems. Nevertheless, systemic collagenase administration induces severe off-target toxicity, including hemorrhage in treated tumors. Although McKee et al. suggested that bleeding did not significantly affect tumor growth, this adverse effect highlights the clinical challenges of collagenase therapy and limits its translational potential. Nonspecific enzymatic degradation poses significant risks, including damage to critical collagen structures in healthy tissues (e.g., skin, blood vessels, and tendons). Such damage can compromise tissue integrity, increase bleeding susceptibility, and induce systemic inflammatory responses. To address these challenges, precise and controlled collagenase delivery strategies are essential for improving therapeutic safety and efficacy.

From the aforementioned discussion, it is evident that degrading excessively deposited collagen represents a pivotal strategy for disrupting the physical barrier imposed by ECM, thereby improving drug penetration. Two highly effective approaches include employing collagenase to degrade pre-existing collagen or suppressing CAF activity to prevent aberrant collagen deposition at its source. Collagenase and its applications in medicine

Types of collagenases and their mechanisms of action

Collagenases are a group of enzymes that specifically hydrolyzes natural collagen and water-soluble denatured collagen. Based on their origin, collagenases can be classified into three major types: plant-derived, animal-derived, and microbial-derived collagenases. For instance, collagenase has been isolated from ginger rhizomes and fig latex [66, 67]. Animal collagenases, typically sourced from vertebrates such as bovine Achilles tendons or porcine pancreas, cleave individual peptide bonds on the three \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}-chains of native collagen, hydrolyzing the triple helix structure at the X-Gly bond and producing fragments approximately three-quarters the length of the original molecule. In contrast, microbial collagenases, such as those from Clostridium and Vibrio species (MEROPS peptidase family M9) [68–70], exhibit broader substrate specificity and more extensive hydrolysis, enabling the degradation of both water-soluble and -insoluble collagen [71, 72]. Notably, Clostridium histolyticum collagenase consists of two isoforms: AUX-I (Type I) and AUX-II (Type II) [73]. Type I displays high activity toward collagen but moderate activity toward the synthetic peptide FALGPA, a substrate resistant to other proteases like trypsin and elastase. In contrast, Type II shows moderate collagenase activity but high FALGPA hydrolysis [74]. Subsequent studies by Matsushita et al. identifed that Clostridium histolyticum collagenases are encoded by colG (Type I) and colH (Type II) genes [75–77].

Collagen constitutes a superfamily of proteins comprising three homologous or non-homologous \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}-chains. These chains are characterized by repetitive “Gly-X–Y” amino acid motifs, where Gly denotes glycine, while X and Y represent variable amino acids—typically proline and hydroxyproline, respectively. The typical triple-helical structure originated from these repetitive “Gly-X–Y” motifs endows collagen with remarkable mechanical strength and stability. To date, 28 distinct collagen types have been identified, categorized into fibrillar and non-fibrillar collagens (further subdivided into fibril-associated collagens, reticular collagens, and membrane-anchored collagens) based on structural and domain composition differences [78, 79]. Fibrillar collagen maintains tissue integrity and structure by providing structural support and tensile strength, which accounts for approximately 90% of total collagen under physiological conditions [80]. Clostridium histolyticum collagenase exhibits high specificity in hydrolyzing native collagen, preferentially targeting Gly-Pro-X and Gly-X-Hyp sequences which are prevalent in canonical collagen structures. The collagenase molecule features specialized surface domains capable of recognizing specific amino acid sequences or tertiary conformations within collagen. In 2011, Eckhard et al. elucidated the chew and digest mechanism of bacterial collagen degradation through crystallographic analysis of Clostridium histolyticum collagenase G (ColG) [81]. Their study determined that collagenases from Clostridium species and other bacteria possess a molecular weight of approximately 120 kDa. The resolved structure of ColG revealed three key domains (Fig. 4 a-b): (1) an N-terminal catalytic domain harboring a zinc ion (Tyr119–Gly790), (2) a polycystic kidney disease-like (PKD-like) domain (Leu791–Asn880), and (3) one or more collagen-binding domains (CBDs: Gln881–Gly1001 and Leu1002–Lys1118). ColG adopts a saddle-shaped architecture consisting of two primary subdomains: a smaller N-terminal activation domain (Tyr119–Asp388) featuring 12 parallel \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}-helices and a larger catalytic domain (Asp398–Gly790) linked by a glycine-rich segment (Gly389–Tyr397). The catalytic domain contains a five-stranded \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}-sheet, with edge residues forming the substrate recognition motif. The interaction between the activation and catalytic domains generates a quadruple-helix bundle, forming the structural “seat” essential for collagen binding and triple-helix destabilization. During hydrolysis, distinct domains orchestrate a sequential mechanism (Fig. 4 c-h): (1) the peptidase domain binds the triple helix, facilitating collagen entry into the collagenase core while the enzyme adopts an open conformation; (2) structural closure enables interaction between the activation/catalytic domains and the triple helix, initiating hydrolysis; (3) a semi-open conformational transition permits gradual unwinding and degradation of the three chains. Post-hydrolysis, the collagenase reverts to its open state for subsequent catalytic cycles [81]. Although both ColG and ColH cleave the peptide bond between Y and Gly in the “Gly-X–Y” repeat, their substrate specificity diverges [82]. Type I collagenase prefers triple-helix collagen as its substrate, with cleavage sites at the N-terminus and C-terminus, while type II collagenase prefers substrates in descending order of affinity: peptides, denatured collagen, and triple-helix collagen, with preferred cleavage sites within the sequence. Applications of collagenase in medical practices

Collagenase, owing to its collagen-degrading properties, has extensive industrial applications in sectors such as meat processing, leather production, food manufacturing, cell culture preparation, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals. Its medical applications date back to the 1970 s when collagenase derived from Clostridium histolyticum was first employed clinically to treat third-degree burns, pressure ulcers, diabetic ulcers, and arterial ulcers [85]. Bacterial collagenase from Clostridium is particularly renowned for its efficacy in wound debridement, including the management of chronic wounds, pressure injuries, and severe burns [86]. Notably, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved Santyl as the only commercially available collagenase ointment, with Clostridium histolyticum collagenase as its active ingredient [87]. This enzymatic preparation facilitates effective eschar and burn debridement, promotes wound healing, and reduces both hospitalization duration and the need for surgical debridement procedures [88–92]. With advances in the research and enzyme extraction techniques of collagenase, its clinical application in treating collagen-related diseases has become feasible. Collagen, the primary component of the ECM, undergoes abnormal accumulation in fibrotic conditions, contributing to pathologies such as lumbar disc herniation, Peyronie’s disease (PD), and Dupuytren’s contracture. PD is characterized by fibrous plaque formation in the tunica albuginea of the penis, leading to penile curvature and deformity. Clostridium histolyticum-derived collagenase remains the only FDA-approved enzymatic therapy for PD [93, 94]. Clinical studies demonstrate that collagenase clostridium histolyticum (CCH) effectively degrades collagen deposits, alleviating penile curvature and improving symptoms. Similarly, Dupuytren’s contracture arises from excessive collagen deposition in the palmar fascia, causing progressive metacarpophalangeal joint contractures. This condition manifests as palmar fascial thickening and finger flexion deformities, significantly impairing hand function. The U.S. FDA has approved CCH as a non-surgical treatment for Dupuytren’s contracture [95]. Compared to surgical division of the palmar fascia, collagenase injection offers advantages including reduced invasiveness, lower cost, fewer side effects, and improved therapeutic outcomes [96, 97].

Collagenase also holds potential for tumor gene therapy. In conventional approaches, CAFs and excessive ECM components, such as collagen and hyaluronic acid, contribute to high interstitial pressure and impaired drug delivery [98, 99]. Yata et al. explored this by modulating the tumor ECM to improve the targeting and delivery efficiency of an adeno-associated virus/phage (AAVP) vector (Fig. 5a-e). Using both 2D monolayer cultures and 3D multicellular tumor spheroids (MCTS), they demonstrated that ECM degradation via collagenase and hyaluronidase significantly enhanced AAVP diffusion, cellular internalization, and transgene expression, ultimately improving tumor cell eradication [100]. In another report, Cemazar et al. investigated the enhancement of gene electroporation efficiency in various murine solid tumors by modulating the ECM [101]. They employed collagenase and hyaluronidase to degrade the ECM and conducted experiments in subcutaneous tumors of differing histological types, including LPB fibrosarcoma, SA-1 fibrosarcoma, EAT carcinoma, and B16 melanoma. The results indicated that in tumors with high ECM content, the combined enzymatic treatment significantly improved electroporation efficiency. Conversely, tumors with low ECM content and irregular collagen networks exhibited no significant improvement, underscoring the ECM’s barrier role in gene delivery and highlighting ECM modulation as a viable strategy to enhance gene therapy efficacy. Additionally, the researchers examined the ECM’s influence on drug delivery and therapeutic outcomes in solid tumors. Using rat mammary carcinoma primary cells, they constructed 3D MCTS to simulate the native ECM microenvironment (Fig. 5f). These spheroids displayed elevated levels of ECM-related proteins and formed an endogenous type I collagen matrix, closely resembling the ECM distribution in vivo (Fig. 5g-h). Following ECM degradation via recombinant collagenase, the permeability and cytotoxicity of the anti-cancer agent doxorubicin were markedly enhanced, confirming the ECM as a critical barrier to drug resistance (Fig. 5j-m). Strategies for targeted delivery of collagenase to TME

As previously mentioned, collagenase plays a critical role in various biological processes and therapeutic applications. However, its in vivo application is constrained by several limitations. Traditional systemic collagenase administration lacks tissue-specific targeting, which may lead to non-selective degradation of the ECM in disease-unrelated regions. This not only hinders the attainment of effective therapeutic concentrations at tumor sites but also disrupts the structural integrity of healthy tissues, potentially causing severe side effects. These include systemic fibrinolysis, which may manifest as mucosal or visceral bleeding and, in extreme cases, life-threatening intracranial hemorrhage [63, 64]. Additionally, collagenase is susceptible to rapid degradation by circulating proteases and clearance by liver and kidney, significantly reducing its half-life and bioavailability. Consequently, frequent dosing is necessary to sustain therapeutic efficacy, further increasing the risk of systemic toxicity [18]. To address these challenges, optimized strategies for targeting collagenase delivery to the tumor ECM have emerged as a key research focus, with the design and functionality of carrier systems playing a pivotal role. Nanomedicine-based targeted delivery systems offer a promising solution [103]. For instance, nanoparticle-encapsulated collagenase can passively accumulate in tumors via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect or actively target CAFs and the ECM through surface-modified ligands [104–107]. Advances in nanomedicine have led to the widespread adoption of nanoparticle drug delivery platforms for remodeling aberrant ECM. These systems utilize nanoscale materials to encapsulate, adsorb, or conjugate therapeutic agents, facilitating targeted drug delivery [108–111]. Employing nanoparticles (NPs) as carriers for collagenase demonstrates significant advantages in overcoming the dense ECM collagen barrier. First, nanocarriers can effectively encapsulate and protect collagenase, thereby significantly reducing nonspecific leakage and premature degradation in systemic circulation. This minimizes off-target damage to healthy tissues, including vascular basement membranes and skin, as well as the associated risks of systemic toxicity. Second, functionalizing nanoparticle surfaces, for instance, by conjugating ligands, antibodies, or peptides targeting specific TME markers, overexpressed receptors, or abnormal ECM components confers active targeting capabilities. Such modifications facilitate selective accumulation in tumors or fibrotic regions abundant in dense or cross-linked collagen. Even without active functionalization, nanoparticle carriers can achieve passive targeting via the EPR effect observed in solid tumors, thereby increasing the local concentration of collagenase at the lesion site. Intelligent nanocarriers can be designed to release encapsulated collagenase selectively in response to endogenous stimuli within TME, such as acidic pH, specific MMPs, or reducing conditions, as well as exogenous stimuli, including light, heat, or ultrasound. This spatiotemporal control over drug release prolongs the enzyme’s activity at the target site, increases its local effective concentration, and enhances stability during delivery, thereby preventing premature inactivation or degradation and improving bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy. Moreover, these nanoplatforms serve as multifunctional co-delivery systems capable of synergistically transporting collagenase alongside chemotherapeutic agents, immunomodulators, or contrast agents. By degrading the ECM barrier, they enhance drug penetration and promote the deep, uniform distribution of therapeutic payloads. This strategy not only softens the ECM but also integrates therapeutic and diagnostic functions, offering promising synergistic anti-tumor and anti-fibrotic effects.

Capitalizing on these advantages, various nanocarriers—such as liposomes, polymeric micelles, inorganic nanoparticles, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), nanogel hydrogels, and exosomal vesicles—have been widely explored in preclinical studies for collagenase encapsulation. Nanocarrier delivery systems

Liposomal nanocarriers are closed vesicular structures composed of phospholipid bilayers, capable of encapsulating both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs. Their aqueous core accommodates water-soluble enzymes such as collagenase, while their lipid bilayer facilitates interaction with biological membranes, promoting carrier-cell fusion and drug endocytosis [132, 133]. Zinger et al. developed a drug delivery system employing collagenase-loaded liposomes to enhance drug penetration in PDAC (Fig. 6a) [112]. The study utilized 100 nm liposomes to encapsulate collagenase, shielding it from premature inactivation and prolonging its release at the target site. These nanoparticles effectively degraded PDAC’s dense collagen matrix, substantially improving drug penetration into pancreatic tumors (Fig. 6b-f). Subsequent intravenous administration of paclitaxel micelles markedly enhanced therapeutic efficacy, reducing tumor volume by 87% compared to untreated controls (Fig. 6g-h). A recent study developed a collagenase-modified quercetin liposome system (QU-CLP) to enhance drug penetration in the ECM, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [113]. The researchers prepared two liposomal formulations: quercetin long-circulating liposomes (QU-LP) and collagenase-modified quercetin liposomes (QU-CLP). The utilization of polymer nanocarriers for collagenase loading enables targeted delivery to the tumor ECM, thereby enhancing drug penetration and therapeutic efficacy. Poly lactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA), a biodegradable copolymer, exhibits excellent drug-loading capacity and controlled-release characteristics. Through optimized preparation methods, collagenase can be efficiently encapsulated within PLGA nanoparticles, whose surfaces can be further functionalized to achieve site-specific delivery [134, 135]. In one study, researchers developed a thermosensitive hydrogel system using PLGA-PEG-PLGA triblock copolymers [127] (Fig. 6i-j). This composite hydrogel (Col/Tra/Gel) incorporated both collagenase and trastuzumab, a human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-targeting monoclonal antibody, for peritumoral administration. The Col/Tra/Gel treatment markedly reduced intratumoral collagen density. Notably, administration of just one-quarter the standard dose of Col/Tra/Gel demonstrated superior antitumor effects compared to intravenous trastuzumab formulations, while simultaneously enhancing tumor cell apoptosis and exhibiting reduced systemic toxicity relative to control groups. A glycyrrhetinic acid (GA)-conjugated prodrug micelle system (CHG), combined with collagenase I, was developed for the co-delivery of sorafenib to enhance ECM degradation and hepatic stellate cell (HSC) targeting [114]. The CHG micelles facilitate deeper drug penetration into fibrotic liver tissue by degrading the collagen-rich ECM barrier and selectively target HSCs via CD44 receptor-specific binding. This nano-delivery system reduces collagen deposition, suppresses HSC activation, and downregulates fibrosis-related factors while reversing liver fibrosis through angiogenesis inhibition, liver microenvironment modulation, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) suppression.

Additionally, collagenase-modified multi-arm polymers exhibit notable advantages in improving tumor penetration. For instance, Yu et al. synthesized collagenase-modified multi-arm polymers (e.g., DPCol and DPDCol). In the 4T1 multicellular spheroid model, DPCol demonstrated significantly deeper tumor tissue penetration, superior tumor accumulation and enrichment compared to DPD-1 and free doxorubicin, as well as an extended half-life [115].

In recent years, inorganic nanocarriers, such as gold nanoparticles, have attracted increasing attention for tumor drug delivery due to their EPR effect [136]. These nanocarriers demonstrate superior tumor accumulation compared to conventional chemotherapy drugs. Nevertheless, the dense ECM of tumors remains a major obstacle to deep drug penetration. To address this challenge, researchers have explored strategies combining collagenase with inorganic nanocarriers to remodel the ECM’s mechanical microenvironment, thereby improving nanodrug penetration depth, tumor accumulation, and therapeutic efficacy. Maksoudian et al. systematically evaluated the potential of enhancing gold nanoparticle delivery efficiency by modulating TAMs, hypoxia, and the ECM within TME [124] (Fig. 7a). Their findings revealed that while TAMs depletion improved tumor vascular maturity and nanoparticle uptake, the overall enhancement in delivery efficiency was marginal. In contrast, combining the hypoxia inhibitor acriflavine (a potent HIF-1 inhibitor) with ECM-degrading collagenase significantly improved the delivery efficiency of larger nanoparticles in tumor tissues. This study underscores the effectiveness of dynamically regulating TME’s ECM to enhance nanoparticle permeability and drug efficacy, highlighting the therapeutic potential of collagenase-loaded inorganic nanoparticles. Murty et al. investigated the efficacy of collagenase-modified nanoparticles in enhancing tumor accumulation. The study employed polyethylene glycol (PEG)-modified gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) as carriers, functionalizing their surfaces with collagenase to overcome the dense ECM barrier and high intratumoral pressure, which typically limit nanoparticle accumulation [125]. The results demonstrated that collagenase retained its enzymatic activity upon surface conjugation, and the functionalized AuNPs exhibited a 35% increase in tumor accumulation compared to unmodified AuNPs, highlighting their potential for improving tumor targeting and drug permeability. In a separate study, Yang et al. developed a collagenase-functionalized biomimetic gold nanoparticle delivery system (Fig. 7b-c). The system involved coating doxorubicin-loaded gold nanocages (AuNCs) with PDAC cell-derived membranes, followed by collagenase conjugation via lipid insertion [126]. This design integrated multiple functionalities, including ECM degradation, homologous targeting, near-infrared (NIR) light-triggered drug release, and synergistic therapy-diagnosis capabilities. Upon intravenous administration, the system efficiently accumulated at the tumor site through homologous targeting. During tissue penetration, collagenase degraded the dense PDAC-associated ECM, facilitating deep nanoparticle infiltration (Fig. 7d-h). Unlike conventional inorganic nanocarriers, MOFs have attracted considerable attention owing to their distinctive structural advantages. MOFs are formed through the coordination-driven self-assembly of metal ions or clusters and organic ligands, resulting in highly ordered porous architectures [137, 138]. Their tunable pore structures render them excellent drug carriers, capable of efficiently encapsulating therapeutic agents such as chemotherapeutic drugs and enzymes. Moreover, precise control over pore size and surface properties enables controlled drug release, enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing dosing frequency. In the application of MOFs for collagenase delivery, Liu et al. developed an MOF-based system with ultrahigh enzyme-loading capacity for photodynamic immunotherapy of deep-tissue pancreatic cancer [118]. By precisely regulating the ratio of metal ions, histidine residues in proteins, and organic ligands, the researchers achieved a collagenase encapsulation efficiency of 80.3 wt% while preserving high enzymatic activity (Fig. 8). The system demonstrated efficient collagenase release within TME, modulating collagen deposition in the tumor ECM. Compared with conventional zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) biomineralized protein carriers, this delivery strategy exhibited reduced toxicity. Additionally, the study incorporated a heat-generating agent with strong near-infrared absorption to induce immunogenic cell death, activate the host immune response, and establish systemic immune memory, effectively suppressing tumor progression and recurrence. Similarly, AbuCAFsy et al. developed a porous MIL-100(Fe) capsule as a dual-drug delivery system based on MOFs [119]. This design encapsulates the antitumor drug methotrexate (MTX) within the MOF nanoparticle pores while trapping collagenase in the mesoporous cavities between particles. This spatial separation strategy substantially increases the loading capacity of both molecules and improves their controlled-release performance, allowing the MTX@COL@MIL-100 capsules to exhibit superior release kinetics for MTX and collagenase under simulated physiological conditions. To address the challenge of low delivery efficiency of MOF particles in TME, Oh et al. introduced an innovative multifunctional protein pre-coating strategy. They functionalized the surface of nanoscale MOF-808 particles with a fusion protein complex containing glutathione S-transferase (GST)-affinity body (Afb) and collagenase. In summary, strategies for delivering collagenase, including liposomal, polymeric, inorganic nanocarriers, and metal–organic frameworks, have been applied to overcome the tumor ECM barrier. These methods actively degrade collagen, a key ECM component, thereby reducing tissue stiffness and interstitial fluid pressure (IFP). Consequently, drug penetration and concentration in tumor tissues, especially in hypoxic core regions, are significantly enhanced by nanocarrier drug delivery system, and these approaches demonstrate superior delivery performance and markedly improves therapeutic efficacy in preclinical tumor models.

The stimulus-responsive delivery system for the delivery of collagenase

Nanoparticle delivery systems serve as highly promising carriers for collagenase delivery by encapsulating collagenase or bioactive molecules within their structure or adsorbing them onto their surface, thereby enabling targeted transport and controlled release. Stimulus-responsive delivery systems further enhance nanoparticles with responsive motifs, allowing precise drug release in specific physiological or pathological microenvironments in response to stimuli such as pH, enzymes, temperature, reactive oxygen species (ROS), light, and magnetic fields. This targeted approach improves therapeutic efficacy while minimizing side effects [139–143]. pH-responsive drug release

The acidic TME (pH 6.5–6.8) results from disrupted acid–base homeostasis due to lactic acid accumulation from Warburg effect-related glycolysis, hypoperfusion, and hypoxia, which mutually interacts with ECM remodeling and synergistically promotes malignant transformation [154–158]. Specifically, excessive ECM deposition exacerbates tissue hypoxia, and hypoxia-induced acidosis would in turn enhance the activity of lysyl oxidase (LOX) family to catalyzes aberrant collagen cross-linking, as well as the release of pro-invasive factors such as TGF-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}, which establishes a self-reinforcing “fibrosis-acidification” loop [159–161]. Vice versa, ECM stiffening aggravates tumor acidosis. Excessive ECM deposition forms a dense barrier that restricts microvascular perfusion and metabolic waste diffusion (e.g., H⁺ and lactate), leading to H⁺ accumulation in the tumor core [162]. Concurrently, stiffened ECM upregulates acid-tolerant proteins (e.g., CAIX, MCT4) via integrin-FAK signaling, promoting H⁺ efflux to maintain intracellular pH and further acidifying the microenvironment [163–165]. Ultimately, acidity and ECM stiffening act synergistically: acidity enhances invasiveness via HIF-1 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}-MMP activation and ECM-degrading enzyme release [166–168], while simultaneously impairing immune function by suppressing T cell and natural killer (NK) cell activity and polarizing macrophages toward an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype [169–171]. These effects hinder immune infiltration, weaken antitumor immunity, and contribute to treatment resistance by limiting drug penetration and intracellular accumulation. This pathological feature serves as a biological foundation for designing pH-responsive nanocarriers. These carriers consist of proton-sensitive materials that remain structurally stable at physiological pH (7.4) to safeguard encapsulated drugs while undergoing protonation or deprotonation in the acidic TME, facilitating controlled drug release [172, 173]. For instance, poly-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}-amino ester (PBAE) exemplifies such a material. PBAE contains numerous amino groups that are partially protonated at physiological pH, inducing electrostatic repulsion between molecules to maintain nanoparticle stability. Upon exposure to the acidic tumor microenvironment, further protonation of the amino groups increases the nanoparticle surface’s positive charge density while reducing inter-molecular electrostatic repulsion, destabilizing the nanoparticle structure and enabling drug release [174, 175]. By modifying PBAE’s chemical structure and composition, the drug release rate can be finely tuned to specific pH conditions, allowing for precise on-demand drug delivery. Xu et al. developed novel size-variable collagenase-modified nanoparticles (CS/Col-TCPPB NPs) [116]. The TCPPB micelle is first formed by self-assembly of maleimide-terminated poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}-amino ester) (MAL-PEG-PBAE) and succinic anhydride-modified cisplatin-conjugated poly(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon$$\end{document}-caprolactone)-block-poly(ethylene oxide)-triphenylphosphonium (CDDP-PCL-PEO-TPP). Subsequently, thiolated collagenase was conjugated to the maleimide groups on the TCPPB micelles via a “click” chemical reaction, yielding collagenase-modified TCPPB (Col-TCPPB) nanoparticles. These nanoparticles were further coated with chondroitin sulfate (CS) to form CS/Col-TCPPB NPs with an average size of approximately 98 nm (Fig. 9a). In a 4T1 breast cancer mouse model, CS/Col-TCPPB NPs significantly suppressed tumor growth. This effect was attributed to their mitochondria-targeting capability via TPP modification, which enhanced nanoparticle accumulation in tumor cells. Furthermore, the acidic tumor microenvironment triggered the release of both collagenase and cisplatin, facilitating collagen degradation and improving cisplatin penetration into deep tumor regions, thereby enhancing antitumor efficacy (Fig. 9a). Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) exhibit a well-ordered pore structure, adjustable particle size, and outstanding biocompatibility, rendering them an effective pH-responsive drug delivery platform [177, 178]. Their pH-dependent behavior arises from an electrostatic gating mechanism: the isoelectric point of mesoporous silica is approximately pH 2. At higher pH values, the material acquires a negative surface charge, enabling electrostatic interactions with positively charged drugs such as doxorubicin, docetaxel, and collagenase. Consequently, these drugs are efficiently encapsulated within the MSN pores. Under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), the strong electrostatic attraction restricts premature drug release, minimizing systemic exposure. Conversely, in weakly acidic environments, the reduced electrostatic interaction enhances drug release kinetics, achieving precise pH-responsive delivery [179]. Gu et al. developed a drug delivery system utilizing PEG-and galactose-modified mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs-P/G) [179]. PEG, a hydrophilic polymer with excellent biocompatibility and targeting modifiability, is widely employed in nanoparticle surface modification to prolong systemic circulation and evade phagocytosis by mononuclear-macrophage cells. In this study, a dense PEG layer was grafted onto the MSN surface, substantially reducing human serum albumin (HSA) adsorption while facilitating pH-responsive drug release under acidic conditions. In a study investigating pH-dependent drug release behavior, researchers validated the pH-sensitive release properties of MSNs at pH 5.4, 6.8, and 7.2. MSNs loaded with collagenase demonstrated significantly higher drug release efficiency in acidic environments [121]. To address the paucity of in vivo studies, this work employed a rat in situ hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) model (Fig. 9b). The collagenase-loaded MSNs effectively degraded the tumor ECM, enhancing doxorubicin permeability and, consequently, therapeutic efficacy.

Enzyme-responsive drug release

Certain enzymes, such as MMPs and cathepsin B esterases, are highly expressed in TME and play a critical role in tumor progression, invasion, and metastasis. Exploiting the enzymatic activity unique to tumor tissues, researchers have designed enzyme-responsive nanoparticles for targeted drug delivery [180–182]. These nanoparticles are typically functionalized with enzyme-sensitive peptide segments that act as substrates for specific enzymes. Upon reaching the tumor site, the overexpressed enzymes cleave these peptide segments, disrupting the nanoparticle structure and triggering the release of encapsulated therapeutics. In a study focused on TME remodeling via CAF targeting, an amphiphilic SN38 prodrug polymer micelle (PSN38) and an esterase-responsive nanoparticle encapsulating a Triptolide (TPL) prodrug (PSN38@TPL-nsa) were developed (Fig. 9c). By integrating a prodrug strategy with an esterase-responsive release system, PSN38@TPL-nsa achieved site-specific drug delivery within tumors [176]. TPL effectively suppressed CAF activity, attenuating CAF-driven gastric cancer (GC) cell proliferation, migration, and drug resistance. Moreover, PSN38@TPL-nsa not only inhibited primary tumor growth and metastasis but also remodeled TME, significantly reducing collagen deposition and the expression of FAP-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}-SMA. Similarly, Sun et al. developed a dual-enzyme-responsive polymer-drug conjugate that facilitates mutual endocytosis between tumor cells and CAFs, thereby overcoming the dense ECM barrier in pancreatic tumors [148]. Within TME, the conjugate exploits the enzymatic activity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) and fibroblast activation protein, which cleave specific amide bonds to generate primary amine groups (Fig. 9d). This cationizes the conjugate, enhancing its permeability in pancreatic tumors and promoting sustained endocytosis between cancer cells and CAFs. Furthermore, by modulating the enzyme-responsive behavior and overall hydrophobicity of the conjugate, the authors optimized both cationization and endocytosis efficiency, establishing an active transport strategy to bypass delivery barriers in matrix-rich tumors. The incorporation of different enzyme-sensitive peptide segments enables selective drug release in response to specific enzymes, further improving the targeting precision and specificity of the nanoparticle delivery system.

Temperature-responsive drug release

Local tumor tissue temperature is often slightly elevated compared to normal tissue, particularly following hyperthermia treatments such as radiofrequency ablation or microwave therapy, which substantially increase intratumoral heat. Taking advantage of this temperature differential, thermosensitive nanoparticles can be engineered for triggered drug release [183, 184]. These nanoparticles are typically synthesized using temperature-responsive materials with a lower critical solution temperature (LCST) near physiological body temperature. Below the LCST, thermosensitive polymers remain hydrophilic, ensuring stable drug encapsulation within nanoparticles. However, when the temperature approaches or exceeds the LCST, the material’s hydrophilicity sharply declines, inducing a phase transition from hydrophilic to hydrophobic on the nanoparticle surface. This structural change causes nanoparticle compaction or aggregation, ultimately facilitating drug release. For instance, poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM), a widely used thermosensitive polymer, exhibits an LCST of ~ 32 °C [185, 186]. By copolymerizing PNIPAM with other materials or modifying its surface, the LCST can be fine-tuned to accommodate specific therapeutic needs [187]. In a prior study, researchers employed rheological analysis to assess the therapeutic efficacy of LiquoGel (LQG), a thermosensitive polymer-collagenase delivery system for uterine fibroid softening. The team developed a PNIPAM-based tetrameric thermosensitive polymer system that undergoes a sol–gel transition upon heating, enabling localized collagenase release. The combined LQG-collagenase treatment reduced fibroid stiffness by two orders of magnitude and markedly enhanced tissue viscoelasticity [128]. Similarly, Sung et al. developed a thermoresponsive gelatin-based colloidal microgel system for controlled drug delivery [188]. The researchers fabricated these thermoresponsive microgels by physically incorporating PNIPAM chains as secondary components within a genipin-crosslinked, three-dimensional gelatin network. Their results demonstrated that the temperature-dependent deswelling behavior of the microgels exhibited a positive correlation with PNIPAM concentration, consequently facilitating controlled release of bovine serum albumin (BSA). However, polymer materials based solely on PNIPAM exhibit several drawbacks, including poor biodegradability, slow degradation rates, and diminished thermal stability when temperatures exceed the LCST. Beyond the LCST, dehydration-induced shrinkage and structural collapse may occur. To mitigate these limitations, acrylic acid (AA) monomers can be copolymerized with PNIPAM to enhance hydrophilicity, while conjugation with biodegradable biomolecular polymers can facilitate degradation and improve in vivo clearance.

Magnetically responsive drug release

Magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) possess unique properties, including superparamagnetism, high specific surface area, and the ability to achieve precise localization, controlled movement, and rapid response under an external magnetic field. These attributes make them highly promising for applications in nanomedicine, particularly in targeted drug delivery systems [189, 190]. To address the barrier effect of the dense ECM on drug penetration, researchers have developed an innovative strategy: functionalizing MNPs with collagenase to combine magnetic field-driven movement with localized ECM degradation, thereby enhancing nanocarrier penetration. For instance, one representative study described the covalent conjugation of collagenase to superparamagnetic nanoparticles (SPM NPs) [145]. This process involved three key steps: (1) linking BSA to SPM NPs using 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride and N-hydroxysuccinimide; (2) introducing thiol groups into collagenase via N-succinimidyl-S-acetylthiopropionate; and (3) covalently binding the modified collagenase to maleimide groups on the SPM NPs via click chemistry. In vitro experiments demonstrated that, under magnetic guidance, these collagenase-functionalized MNPs effectively penetrated dense collagen gel matrices at a migration speed of 90 ± 28 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu m/h$$\end{document}, comparable to the migration rate of invasive cells. Moreover, immobilizing collagenase on the nanoparticle surface significantly improved its stability, with enzyme activity decreasing linearly by only 50% over 48 h—a timeframe compatible with clinical requirements.

This combined MNP-collagenase strategy ingeniously integrates the spatiotemporal precision of magnetic navigation with enzymatic ECM barrier dissolution, offering a novel approach to enhance therapeutic agent distribution depth and delivery efficiency in target tissues.

Cell membrane modification and cell delivery systems for collagenase delivery

Recent advances in targeted nanoparticle delivery have highlighted cell membrane modification as a promising strategy for encapsulating drug-loaded nanoparticles and cell delivery systems [191–194]. By leveraging specific cell membranes, such as those derived from red blood cells, immune cells, or tumor cells, researchers can exploit membrane-bound receptors, ligands, or markers to achieve precise recognition and binding, thereby directing drugs to target tissues or cells. For instance, Li et al. developed a multifunctional nanomaterial system (MP@H-MnO2-DOX-Col) based on mesoporous manganese dioxide (H-MnO2), which encapsulates doxorubicin (DOX) in its core and is surface-coated with collagenase (Fig. 10a) [122]. Notably, this system is enveloped by a fusion membrane (MP) composed of inflammation-targeting RAW264.7 macrophage membranes and pH-sensitive liposomes. The macrophage membrane enhances tumor homing and prolongs circulation time, while the pH-sensitive liposome enables controlled drug release in the acidic tumor microenvironment, ensuring precise drug delivery (Fig. 10b). This biomimetic approach not only improves the antitumor efficacy of DOX but also mitigates its cardiotoxicity through targeted collagenase and DOX co-delivery. Yang et al. developed a strategy in which PDAC tumor cell membranes were extracted and coated onto doxorubicin-loaded gold nanocages, followed by collagenase functionalization via lipid insertion [126]. This approach achieved ECM degradation, active tumor targeting, immune evasion, near-infrared light-triggered drug release, and synergistic antitumor therapy with diagnostic capabilities. In a related study, Zhou et al. designed a multifunctional nanocarrier platform to modulate the mechanical and metabolic tumor microenvironment in breast cancer while enhancing drug delivery efficiency [144]. The platform, encapsulating doxorubicin and 1-methyl tryptophan (1-MT) within 4T1 tumor cell membranes and collagenase, demonstrated improved tumor targeting, deep tissue penetration, hypoxia alleviation, immunogenic cell death induction, and enhanced therapeutic efficacy. It is crucial to note that during tumor cell membrane extraction and purification, residual intact cancer cells or nuclear material must be thoroughly removed to prevent potential tumorigenic effects. In cell-based delivery systems, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and their derived exosomes represent two pivotal therapeutic tools, harnessing their distinct biological properties for targeted drug delivery. MSCs, primarily isolated from bone marrow, adipose tissue, and umbilical cord blood, exhibit multipotent differentiation capacity and robust homing abilities. These cells respond to chemotactic signals released from pathological sites (e.g., tumors, fibrotic tissues, or neurotrauma regions), such as the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis, to enable active migration and localization. Additionally, MSCs secrete anti-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic factors, which synergize with drug payloads to amplify therapeutic outcomes, thus positioning them as versatile carriers [195–198]. Unlike MSCs, exosomes are nanoscale (30–150 nm) lipid bilayer vesicles secreted by eukaryotic cells, inherently enriched with bioactive molecules (e.g., proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids). Their structural attributes confer high biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and efficient membrane permeability, rendering them ideal natural nanocarriers [199–203]. Crucially, exosomes participate in precise regulatory processes, such as ECM remodeling, by attenuating fibrosis, promoting collagen degradation, and reducing pathological deposition. Thus, leveraging MSCs or exosomes for drug loading capitalizes on their intrinsic homing or penetration mechanisms, enabling targeted delivery and modulation of disease-specific microenvironments.

Tumor cell-derived exosomes can transport and deliver MMPs, including the transmembrane protein MT1-MMP (also known as MMP-14), which directly hydrolyze key ECM components such as collagen, thereby disrupting the dense matrix structure [204, 205]. Conversely, certain therapeutically promising exosomes, such as those from MSCs, can deliver tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), specifically TIMP-1 and TIMP-2. These inhibitors not only suppress excessive ECM degradation but may also help restore the balance between degradation and synthesis. Furthermore, exosomes can modulate fibroblast activity and inhibit collagen production. For instance, MSC-derived exosomes effectively suppress the profibrotic TGF-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}/Smad signaling pathway, significantly reducing the synthesis and deposition of type I and type III collagen. In a liver fibrosis model, such exosomes reduced collagen content by 40–60% [206–213]. This stands in stark contrast to the pathological role of exosomes secreted by hepatic stellate cells and myofibroblasts, which are enriched with pro-fibrotic factors like TGF-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} 1 and connective tissue growth factor (CTGF). These factors drive fibroblast activation into collagen-secreting myofibroblasts, exacerbating fibrosis [214–216]. As efficient nucleic acid carriers, exosomes can precisely target and regulate the expression of ECM-related genes through their cargo of specific microRNAs. For instance, miR-29a, a member of the miR-29 family, reduces collagen production, inhibits hepatocellular carcinoma cell migration, and promotes cancer cell apoptosis [217, 218], whereas miR-27b-3p suppresses collagen fiber cross-linking by targeting lysyl oxidase-like protein 2 (LOXL2), thereby decreasing ECM stiffness and mitigating fibrosis [211]. Additionally, exosomes modulate the inflammatory microenvironment to indirectly attenuate ECM deposition. Anti-inflammatory exosomes, such as those derived from MSCs, deliver cytokines like IL-10 and TSG-6 to inhibit macrophage activation and the production of pro-inflammatory mediators (e.g., TNF-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} and IL-6), ultimately diminishing fibroblast stimulation and pathological ECM collagen over-deposition [211, 219].

In summary, exosomes play a central regulatory role in collagen degradation, fibrosis suppression, and ECM homeostasis maintenance. Their mechanisms include targeted delivery of degradative enzymes and their inhibitors, suppression of fibroblast activation and collagen synthesis pathways, downregulation of collagen and crosslinking-related gene expression, and modulation of the pro-fibrotic inflammatory microenvironment. While pathologically derived exosomes may facilitate aberrant ECM remodeling by transmitting pro-fibrotic factors, harnessing and amplifying their inhibitory functions presents a highly promising therapeutic strategy for fibrosis treatment.

Local delivery for collagenase delivery

Restricted intratumoral injection is a spatially targeted delivery strategy. Intratumoral administration of hydrogels or nanoparticles enables direct anchoring of collagenase within TME. The physical barrier effect of hyaluronic acid hydrogels restricts drug diffusion, enhancing localized therapeutic efficacy [220–222]. To amplify the synergistic effects of photothermal and chemotherapy, an injectable nanocomposite hydrogel (CD@IPH) was developed (Fig. 11a) [129]. Upon intratumoral injection, the hydrogel facilitates rapid collagenase release within the first 12 h, effectively degrading the ECM to promote deep penetration and uniform distribution of doxorubicin-loaded nanoparticles. Subsequently, glutathione (GSH) and low pH conditions in TME trigger controlled doxorubicin release, further enhancing therapeutic outcomes. Interestingly, the polyaniline core of the nanoparticles exhibits excellent photoacoustic and photothermal properties, enabling real-time monitoring of drug penetration and precise guidance for optimal photothermal therapy timing. Similarly, Yang et al. recently developed a novel nanogel system (HCSC-gel) to enhance photodynamic-immunotherapy combination therapy for breast cancer [130]. This system co-encapsulates a mitochondria-targeted photosensitizer and a STING agonist while incorporating collagenase to degrade the tumor ECM, thereby improving the penetration of therapeutic agents and oxygen (Fig. 11b). In a murine breast cancer model, local injection of the hydrogel followed by laser irradiation concurrently activated photodynamic therapy and STING-mediated immune responses, which reversed the low immunogenicity of breast cancer and exhibited potent efficacy against both primary and metastatic tumors. Ultrasound serves as a non-invasive adjunctive therapeutic modality in clinical applications [223]. Sonodynamic therapy (SDT), an emerging non-invasive treatment approach, combines low-intensity focused ultrasound with sonosensitizers. The underlying mechanism involves ultrasound-triggered activation of sonosensitizers to generate reactive oxygen species and mechanical effects, which induce cellular apoptosis, vascular damage, and immune activation. This process enables effective treatment of tumors, infectious diseases, and fibrotic disorders [224–227]. Inorganic nanoparticles encapsulating collagenase show promise for ultrasound-guided SDT. Specifically, Luo et al. developed hollow titanium dioxide nanoparticles (Col-H-TiO2 NPs) loaded with collagenase (Fig. 11c). These nanoparticles enable controlled collagenase release under ultrasound irradiation, effectively degrading tumor ECM fibers and reducing interstitial fluid pressure. Consequently, this enhances nanoparticle penetration and retention within tumor tissue [123]. A recent study reported the development of a multifunctional nanoacoustic agent platform (FePO2@HC) capable of degrading the ECM and supplying oxygen, thereby addressing the dual challenges of high ECM density and tumor hypoxia [147]. The system consists of HAS nanoparticles incorporating collagenase and iron porphyrin (FePO2@HC), achieved by fusing collagenase with HSA and encapsulating oxygen in a reduced state (Fig. 11d). Upon exposure to tumor-overexpressed reduced GSH, FePO2@HC disintegrates, releasing its payload. The liberated collagenase cleaves tumor collagen fibers, disrupting the tissue structure and enhancing intratumoral FePO2 accumulation. Under ultrasound irradiation, FePO2@HC produces substantial singlet oxygen, which, synergizing with collagenase-mediated ECM degradation, significantly improves drug accumulation and augments the efficacy of SDT. Additionally, research has demonstrated that ultrasound-guided intratumoral injection of collagenase-2 enhances lipid drug accumulation and diffusion in solid tumor xenografts. Specifically, in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma models (SCC-4 cell), this approach effectively reduces IFP and sustains low IFP levels, thereby promoting the homogeneous distribution and increased accumulation of doxorubicin within tumors [228].

Safety and minimization of off-target effects

Direct systemic administration of collagenase poses substantial risks, including uncontrolled degradation of healthy connective tissues—such as vascular basement membranes—and hemorrhage due to its broad proteolytic activity. Studies demonstrate that collagenase injections below 500 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu g$$\end{document}/mouse are non-lethal in murine models. However, higher doses can induce severe adverse effects, such as peritonitis and pulmonary hemorrhage [18, 63, 64]. By encapsulating collagenase within a nanoscale delivery system, its enzymatic activity is spatially and temporally controlled, thereby preventing premature activation. This confinement substantially mitigates untimely release and nonspecific distribution in the bloodstream, reducing off-target damage to healthy tissues and systemic toxicity. To further enhance specificity, active targeting moieties are incorporated into the nanoparticle design. Surface modification with ligands—such as dipeptide linkers targeting FAP-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}, integrin-binding peptides (e.g., RGD or iRGD (Arg-Gly-Asp or internalizing Arg-Gly-Asp)), or small molecules responsive to tumor microenvironmental cues (e.g., pH or glutathione gradients)—enables preferential accumulation of collagenase-loaded nanosystems in pathological regions rich in cross-linked collagen. Such regions include connective tissue-proliferative tumors and fibrotic lesions. This targeted approach not only spares areas undergoing physiological collagen turnover in healthy tissues but also maximizes enzymatic activity within therapeutic zones where ECM remodeling confers clinical benefits. Additionally, passive targeting via the EPR effect synergizes with active strategies by leveraging the hyperpermeable vasculature characteristic of tumors and fibrotic tissues. Most nanocarriers, including liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and inorganic platforms are fabricated from biocompatible materials such as PEG-modified lipids, FDA-approved polymers (e.g., PLGA), or inorganic compounds (e.g., silica, calcium carbonate). These materials undergo surface modifications to minimize immune activation and ensure colloidal stability in vivo. For instance, PLGA nanoparticles degrade into nontoxic metabolites, whereas silica nanoparticles of specific sizes undergo hydrolysis in vivo and are subsequently excreted renally. Based on these advantages, the targeted delivery of collagenase to the ECM via nanodelivery systems offers a safe and effective strategy that minimizes off-target effects to the greatest extent possible.

Targeted inhibition of CAFs

The effective delivery of anti-cancer drugs and the remodeling of tumor matrix fibrosis represent major challenges in improving drug penetration efficacy within TME. CAFs play a pivotal role in this process. As the primary cellular component of TME matrix, CAFs are key drivers of tumor matrix fibrosis and abnormal protein deposition. They facilitate tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis by secreting pro-malignant factors and mediating multiple oncogenic processes, including ECM remodeling, suppression of anti-tumor immune responses, and induction of treatment resistance [3, 229–231]. Consequently, targeted inhibition of CAF has emerged as a critical therapeutic strategy to reduce ECM collagen deposition, suppress tumor progression, and enhance anti-tumor immunity.

FAP-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}, a membrane-bound serine protease selectively expressed by CAFs in most human epithelial tumors, represents an ideal biomarker and therapeutic target for CAF-directed therapies [232]. FAP-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} exhibits both proline peptidase and endopeptidase activities. Notably, while FAP-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} expression in normal adult tissues is transient and restricted to tissue remodeling or wound healing, it is consistently overexpressed in CAFs within breast, colorectal, skin, and pancreatic cancer [233–235]. This highly specific expression profile, coupled with its unique substrate selectivity, renders FAP-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} a promising candidate for developing selective CAF-targeted therapies. In recent years, chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy has demonstrated significant efficacy in treating hematological malignancies [245–247]. However, its application in solid tumors remains limited due to three major challenges: the lack of ideal target antigens, insufficient tumor infiltration, and an immunosuppressive TME, which collectively restrict clinical outcomes. CAR-T cells are engineered by substituting the variable region of the T cell receptor (TCR) with an antibody-derived scFv, allowing direct antigen recognition independent of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC). With technological advancements, first-generation CARs incorporated only the essential CD3 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\zeta$$\end{document} activation domain, whereas second- and third-generation CARs integrated co-stimulatory domains, such as CD28 and 4-1BB, as well as multi-signal modules. These modifications substantially improved T cell proliferation, persistence, and antitumor efficacy [248, 249].

To overcome the ECM barrier in solid tumors, CAR-T strategies targeting the CAF surface marker FAP-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} have garnered significant attention. FAP-CAR-T cells typically fuse the scFv of anti-FAP monoclonal antibodies with CD28 or 4-1BB co-stimulatory domains, forming effector cells capable of specifically recognizing and eliminating FAP-positive CAFs [250]. Preclinical studies demonstrate that FAP-CAR-T cells efficiently eliminate FAP-expressing tumor cells and CAFs, exhibiting potent anti-tumor activity in vitro and in vivo while maintaining low toxicity toward normal tissues [251–255]. Kakarla et al. developed a second-generation FAP-CAR construct, designated mhFAP-CAR, which targets both human and murine FAP. This CAR comprises the MO36 scFv, the human IgG1-CH2CH3 domain, the CD28 transmembrane domain, and the signaling domains of CD28 and CD3ζ. FAP-specific T cells generated with this construct effectively recognize and lyse FAP-expressing target cells [256]. In the established A549 lung cancer model, the adoptive transfer of FAP-CAR-T cells significantly reduced FAP-positive stromal cells and inhibited tumor growth. Moreover, the combination with CAR-T cells targeting the EphA2 antigen on the tumor cell surface further enhanced the anti-tumor efficacy and prolonged survival. The fibrotic barrier formed by CAFs and their secreted collagen impedes the infiltration of chemotherapy drugs and immune cells, resulting in low sensitivity of PDAC to chemotherapy and immunotherapy. To address this challenge, Wang et al. developed a FAP-targeted chimeric antigen receptor macrophage (FAP-CAR-M) by reprogramming M2 macrophages using mannose-modified mRNA-liposome delivery technology [257]. In vivo murine studies demonstrated that FAP-CAR-M effectively reduced FAP expression, collagen volume fraction, and type I collagen secretion. this approach enhanced the permeability of gemcitabine and immune cells, improving PDAC response to therapy and significantly prolonging survival.

Recent efforts have explored the use of dual-targeting CAR-T cell therapies to enhance tumor treatment efficacy. For instance, Ruixin Sun et al. developed FAP/CLDN18.2 dual-target CAR-T cells for pancreatic cancer therapy [258]. In the PANC02-A2 tumor model, these CAR-T cells markedly suppressed tumor growth, reduced myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) infiltration, and enhanced CD8 + T cell accumulation. Additionally, they attenuated CAF recruitment and T-cell exhaustion by downregulating TGF-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} and CXCL12 secretion, thereby remodeling TME and improving survival outcomes. Preclinical studies confirmed that dual-target CAR-T therapy exhibits superior efficacy without significant toxicity while mitigating drug resistance compared to single-target approaches, offering a promising and diversified strategy for clinical translation.

CAFs not only synthesize excessive collagen but also sustain their activated state by secreting soluble factors via autocrine and paracrine signaling [259, 260]. These factors further stimulate adjacent CAFs and tumor cells, eventually forming a self-reinforcing loop that perpetuates fibrosis and tumor progression. Therefore, targeting these factors presents a viable strategy to attenuate the ECM barrier by disrupting such positive feedback loop. For instance, inhibiting stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1/CXCL12) and its receptor CXCR4 substantially attenuates fibrosis and collagen deposition. SDF-1, predominantly secreted by CAFs, binds to CXCR4 on tumor and endothelial cells, thereby promoting tumor progression, angiogenesis, and metastasis [58, 261–263]. Regarding ECM remodeling, the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis potently activates CAFs, upregulates collagen synthesis genes (e.g., COL1A1 and COL3A1), and recruits precursor cells to differentiate into pro-fibrotic CAFs [264]. Employing CXCR4 antagonists or SDF-1-neutralizing antibodies effectively disrupts this signaling axis, suppresses CAF activation and collagen production, diminishes pro-tumor effects, and synergistically enhances drug distribution within TME. Ullah et al. developed a CXCR4-targeted liposomal co-delivery system (CTC liposome) for liver fibrosis treatment, designed to synergistically deliver pirfenidone (PFD) and the CXCR4 inhibitor AMD3100 (AMD) [265]. The researchers prepared liposomes using the thin-film hydration method and verified their targeting efficiency and endocytosis mechanisms through CXCR4 redistribution assays, confocal microscopy, and flow cytometry. In TGF-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}-activated HSC-T6 cells, CTC liposomes demonstrated substantial anti-fibrotic effects, inducing 87.3% apoptosis and significantly downregulating \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}-SMA (65%), CXCR4 (77%), TGF-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} (89%), and P-p38 (66%) expression. Similarly, Chen et al. proposed enhancing sorafenib efficacy by mitigating hypoxia-induced HCC fibrosis through inhibition of the SDF-1 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}/CXCR4 axis and Gr-1+ myeloid cell infiltration [266]. Their study was the first to demonstrate that the SDF-1/CXCR4 pathway directly promotes hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation and differentiation, while CXCR4 inhibition markedly reduces tumor fibrosis. Combining AMD3100 with sorafenib significantly lowered collagen I and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}-SMA expression levels in tumor tissues, thereby suppressing tumor fibrosis. Interleukin-6 (IL-6) is a pivotal inflammatory cytokine within TME, predominantly secreted by CAFs, tumor-associated macrophages, and malignant cells. Through activation of the downstream JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway, IL-6 not only directly enhances tumor cell proliferation, survival, and invasion, but also functions as a critical regulator of CAF activation and maintenance [267]. Elevated IL-6 levels markedly stimulate CAFs to synthesize and secrete greater quantities of collagen and other ECM components, consequently exacerbating tissue fibrosis [268, 269]. Targeting IL-6 or inhibiting the JAK/STAT3 pathway (e.g., with Ruxolitinib) directly suppresses IL-6-mediated CAF activation and collagen synthesis, reducing ECM deposition. Studies have demonstrated synergistic effects of Ruxolitinib combined with Prednisone and Nilotinib in myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) cell lines and primary cells [270]. Although Ruxolitinib is a first-line, non-palliative therapy for myelofibrosis (MF), suboptimal responses, drug resistance, and adverse effects limit its efficacy. To overcome these challenges, the authors assessed 17 Ruxolitinib-based drug combinations in MF patient-derived peripheral blood mononuclear cells and cell lines, and the results demonstrated that combining Ruxolitinib and Nilotinib produces synergistic effects in myelofibrosis (MF) cells, with Prednisone further potentiating this interaction. Mechanistic studies revealed that this triple therapy concurrently suppressed JAK/STAT (STAT5 inhibition: 69.2 ± 11.8%) and MAPK (ERK inhibition: 29.4 ± 4.5%) signaling pathways while downregulating collagen production and COL1A1 gene expression in human bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells. In a related study, Han et al. explored the therapeutic efficacy of the JAK/STAT3 inhibitor nintedanib (Ninte) in chronic pancreatitis (CP) using a rain frog toxin-induced murine model [271]. Oral Ninte administration significantly attenuated CP-associated weight loss and histopathological markers of pancreatic damage, including acinar atrophy, collagen deposition, inflammatory infiltration, and impaired cell proliferation regeneration. Furthermore, Ninte suppressed fibrotic and proinflammatory gene transcription in pancreatic tissue. In vitro analyses confirmed that Ninte inhibited pancreatic stellate cell (PSC) activation and proliferation, consequently downregulating fibrosis- and proliferation-related genes.

These inhibitory strategies target CAF markers and pro-fibrotic factors to address the root cause of fibrosis—limiting excessive collagen accumulation in the ECM. This is achieved by suppressing CAF activation, reducing collagen synthesis and secretion, and blocking key signals that sustain CAF activity and ECM deposition. Notably, combining collagenase-mediated collagen degradation (direct removal of existing barriers) or CAF inhibition/factor blockade (prevention of de novo barrier formation) with conventional therapies—including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or cell therapy—may yield synergistic effects. This dual approach not only reduces ECM density and stiffness to enhance drug penetration in a more effective manner, but also comprehensively suppresses tumor progression by remodeling TME and attenuating pro-tumor signaling. Consequently, it improves the efficacy of anti-tumor therapies, including chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy.

Synergistic combination strategy of collagen degradation and other cancer therapies

Collagenase-chemotherapy combination therapy

Collagenase-mediated ECM degradation not only disrupts physical barriers but also acts as a pivotal mechanism for enabling multimodal synergistic therapy. Through spatiotemporal control of collagenase pretreatment to remodel TME, the efficacy of both conventional chemotherapy and emerging immunotherapy can be substantially improved. Localized collagenase delivery prior to chemotherapy enhances the penetration and accumulation of chemotherapeutic agents in deep tumor tissues. For instance, studies demonstrate that pretreatment with collagenase-loaded nanoparticles increased doxorubicin liposome accumulation in tumors by 2.8-fold [117]. Similarly, in PDAC, where collagen deposition constitutes up to 90% of the tumor mass, pretreatment with liposome-encapsulated collagenase nanoparticles significantly improved the therapeutic efficacy of paclitaxel micelles, reducing tumor volume by 87% compared to untreated controls. Notably, ECM degradation did not elevate circulating tumor cells or metastatic risk [112]. A recent innovative study employed a bioengineered Escherichia coli expression system to co-express collagenase and hyaluronidase in PDAC, augmenting gemcitabine penetration and efficacy [272]. By genetically modifying E. coli BL21, the researchers created a strain capable of simultaneous secretion of both enzymes, enabling targeted ECM degradation in pancreatic cancer models. This approach’s key advantage lies in leveraging a single bacterial vehicle to disrupt collagen and hyaluronan networks synergistically, mitigating fibrosis and remodeling TME into a more drug-permissive state. Animal model validations confirmed that engineered bacterial pretreatment significantly enhanced gemcitabine efficacy, yielding reduced tumor burden and prolonged survival.

Synergistic effects with immunotherapy

Collagen degradation facilitates T cell infiltration

The synergistic effect of collagenase delivery with immunotherapy primarily arises from the degradation of collagen fiber barriers in the ECM, which enhances immune cell infiltration and improves immunotherapy efficacy. Existing studies have demonstrated that collagen not only promotes cancer cell growth and migration but also modulates the function and phenotype of tumor-infiltrating immune cells, suggesting an immunoregulatory role for tumor-associated collagen in TME [273–278]. Specifically, collagen indirectly influences T cell activity by altering macrophage polarization. In high-density collagen environments, macrophages preferentially differentiate into M2-type macrophages, which exhibit anti-inflammatory properties. These macrophages suppress T cell proliferation and activity by secreting immunosuppressive cytokines and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Furthermore, M2 macrophages inhibit T cell activation via intercellular contact by upregulating PD-L1 expression and downregulating antigen-presenting molecules, such as MHC class II. Additionally, collagen-conditioned macrophages exhibit increased chemokine secretion, which recruits more immune cells including T cells into tumor tissue, thereby shaping the overall immune response in TME. Compared to low-density matrices, high-density collagen matrices significantly reduce T cell proliferation and increase the CD4+:CD8+ T cell ratio, further indicating collagen’s role in modulating T cell activity [279–283]. These findings collectively demonstrate that collagen regulates immune cell behavior by altering their functional states. Consequently, collagen degradation may overcome ECM physical barriers and enhance immune cell infiltration. For instance, in a KPC pancreatic cancer model, combining LOX inhibition with anti-PD-1 therapy delayed tumor progression (Fig. 13a), increased intratumoral effector CD8+ T cells, reduced polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs), and elevated MHC II-expressing TAMs and granzyme B levels (Fig. 13b-c)[284]. Similarly, in a Panc02 murine pancreatic tumor model treated with collagenase-loaded MOF nanoparticles, immunohistochemistry revealed significantly increased CD8+ T cell infiltration (Fig. 13d) [118]. Collagen degradation improves the effectiveness of immune checkpoint inhibitors

Immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors, are pivotal in cancer immunotherapy. However, their efficacy in solid tumors is constrained by the immunosuppressive TME and physical barriers. Studies indicate that degrading collagen or disrupting interactions between tissue collagen receptors and their ligands can markedly enhance immune cell infiltration and function, thereby improving the therapeutic outcomes of immune checkpoint inhibitors [286–289]. Quanyin Hu’s research team at the University of Wisconsin-Madison developed an innovative nanodrug-bacteria conjugate strategy [152]. Prior research revealed that PDAC cells activate the FAK/AKT/MAPK signaling pathway via aberrant oncogenic collagen production and binding to integrin \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha 3\beta 1$$\end{document} receptors, promoting immune suppression and tumor proliferation. This process ultimately diminishes the effectiveness of immune checkpoint blockade therapy [290–292]. The proposed strategy employs ROS-responsive linkers to conjugate collagenase and anti-PD-L1 antibodies, forming a protein cage. Under elevated ROS levels, the linker degrades, releasing collagenase, which is subsequently covalently attached to the surface of Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 to form Col/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a$$\end{document} PD-L1@ECN (Fig. 13e). Leveraging the hypoxic chemotaxis and motility of ECN, the nanodrug-bacteria conjugate successfully penetrates the dense PDAC stroma. Experimental results demonstrate that this approach degrades oncogenic collagen, inhibits the integrin \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha 3\beta 1$$\end{document}-FAK signaling axis, reverses immune suppression, and potentiates anti-PD-L1 therapy. Compared to directly degrading collagen in the ECM to improve immune checkpoint therapy efficacy, alternative approaches focus on disrupting interactions between preformed collagen and its receptors (e.g., LAIR-1, DDR1, and integrins), which suppress the tumor immune microenvironment and diminish therapeutic effectiveness. For instance, LAIR-1 is frequently overexpressed in multiple cancers, and its binding to collagen ligands impairs immune function in TME, including T cell dysfunction and attenuated antigen-presenting cell responses [131, 293]. Bioinformatics analyses have confirmed LAIR-1’s widespread expression, prompting the development of LAIR-2-Fc, a recombinant protein with high sequence homology to LAIR-1, designed to competitively inhibit LAIR-1–collagen binding. This intervention enhances cytotoxic T cell infiltration and function, promoting anti-tumor immunity and synergizing with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade therapy [288]. Similarly, Peng et al. demonstrated that ECM collagen mediates CD8+ T cell exhaustion via LAIR1, contributing to PD-1/PD-L1 resistance [287]. Proteomic and RNA sequencing analyses revealed elevated collagen levels in resistant lung tumors, correlating with reduced CD8+ T cell abundance and increased exhausted T cell subsets. LOXL2 inhibition reduced collagen deposition, improved T cell infiltration, and restored PD-L1 inhibitor sensitivity, while LAIR1 blockade reversed drug resistance. Clinically, high collagen, LAIR1, and TIM-3 expression correlates with poorer immunotherapy response and survival in melanoma patients. Thus, strategies such as collagenase-mediated ECM degradation, LOX enzyme inhibition to prevent collagen cross-linking, or receptor-ligand blockade can augment immune cell infiltration and checkpoint therapy efficacy, offering promising avenues for improving patient outcomes.

Collagen degradation enhances the efficacy of CAR-T therapy in solid tumors

As previously mentioned, CAR-T therapy targeting the FAP in ECM-associated CAFs induces immune remodeling of TME, significantly suppressing tumor growth and improving therapeutic efficacy. A recent breakthrough study combined CAR-T therapy with a collagenase-loaded nanohydrogel system [285]. In this strategy, collagenase nanogels are modified with CXCR4 antagonist peptides to form “cell backpacks” that are directly conjugated to CAR-T cells. (Fig. 13f) This innovative design facilitates ECM collagen barrier degradation within TME, enhances CAR-T cell infiltration, disrupts CXCL12/CXCR4-mediated T cell retention, and improves tumor localization efficiency. This approach represents the first attempt to integrate immunotherapy with direct ECM degradation via collagenase, departing from conventional nanoparticle-based delivery of small molecules or antibody drugs to the tumor ECM. By synergizing collagenase-mediated ECM degradation with cell-level immunotherapy, this strategy establishes a novel paradigm for improving CAR-T therapy efficacy in solid tumors.

Clinical translation, challenges and controversies

A clear statement on the absence of registered clinical trials specifically for nanoparticle-delivered collagenase is revealed based on our ClinicalTrials.gov search. To provide valuable context on the clinical landscape of ECM-modulating strategies relevant to our discussion, we expanded our analysis to include approaches targeting other key ECM components. We noticed a highly pertinent and well-documented case study: a clinical trial of PEGPH20, a PEGylated recombinant human hyaluronidase (NCT02715804) [294]. A previous Phase 2 clinical study showed that the combination of PEGPH20 with nab-paclitaxel and GEM can double the progression-free survival and improve overall survival in patients with hyaluronan-high metastatic PDAC. Currently this Phase 3 large-scale study evaluated PEGPH20 in combination with nab-paclitaxel and gemcitabine (PAG treatment) versus the nab-paclitaxel and gemcitabine (AG treatment) in patients with hyaluronan (HA)-high, stage IV pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), enrolling 492 HA-high PDAC patients across 20 countries: 327 received PAG, while 165 received AG [295]. Primary endpoints included overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and severe adverse events. Analysis results demonstrated the PAG group still exhibited a higher objective response rate (ORR; 47.1% vs. 36.4%) compared to AG group, but there was no statistically significant difference in median OS (PAG: 11.2 months; AG: 11.5 months) or PFS (7.1 months for both groups). Morever, the FDA temporarily halted the trial due to PEGPH20-associated thromboembolic events, and subsequent negative outcomes regarding the lack of survival benefits led to the discontinuation of PEGPH20 development.

The sobering outcome of the PEGPH20 Phase 3 trial (NCT02715804) serves as a potent, clinically grounded illustration of the profound translational complexities inherent in therapeutic strategies aimed at enzymatic ECM modulation. While PEGPH20 targeted HA degradation in PDAC and the proposed nanodelivery systems in this review focus on localized collagenase delivery for collagen remodeling, the fundamental challenges encountered in its clinical development are highly instructive and likely paradigmatic for any enzyme-based ECM-targeting approach, including nanoparticle-delivered collagenase. The termination of PEGPH20 development, despite a strong preclinical rationale and initial promise in Phase 2, underscores critical, unresolved barriers that directly translate to the context of collagenase nanotherapy.

First, a primary challenge lies in maintaining the stability and activity of collagenase. As a protease, collagenase exhibits inherent instability in complex physiological environments and suffers from a short in vivo half-life, complicating effective delivery. Such instability arises from multiple factors, including degradation by endogenous proteases, immune-mediated clearance of exogenous (particularly bacterial-derived) enzymes, and inhibition by endogenous metalloproteinase inhibitors that competitively bind its active sites [296–300]. Furthermore, physiological variations in pH, temperature, ionic strength, and shear stress can induce conformational changes, leading to functional inactivation. Consequently, unmodified collagenase typically persists in vivo for mere minutes to hours, hindering the achievement and maintenance of therapeutic concentrations at target sites. The rapid activity decay also complicates precise control over the extent and duration of ECM degradation. Although approaches such as carrier encapsulation, chemical modification, and fusion protein design have been explored to improve stability, these strategies still face limitations, including relatively low drug-loading efficiency, intricate preparation processes, potential activity loss due to active-site masking or modification-induced structural changes, and concerns regarding carrier biocompatibility and clearance [300, 301].

Another highly debated scientific issue in this field concerns the relationship between collagen degradation and tumor metastasis risk. Traditional perspectives suggest that ECM degradation is a critical step in tumor cell invasion and metastasis. Consequently, the active application of collagenase raises concerns about inadvertently promoting tumor spread. Supporting evidence includes: MMPs facilitate tumor migration by degrading the ECM and basement membrane [302, 303]; ECM degradation can release stored growth factors (e.g., VEGF, TGF-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}, FGF) and chemokines, directly stimulating tumor proliferation, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition [304, 305]; Systemic degradation may establish a favorable microenvironment for metastatic colonization [306]. However, counterarguments highlight that the controversy hinges on the degree and specificity of degradation. Targeted delivery strategies aim to achieve localized, controlled, and moderate collagen degradation, primarily to enhance drug delivery efficiency and immune cell infiltration while overcoming the tumor’s physical immune barrier. Proponents argue that such approaches could enhance immune surveillance and inhibit metastasis. Critics contend that many pro-metastatic findings arise from non-targeted, systemic high-dose applications or model-specific limitations, which may not generalize. The “time window” hypothesis further suggests that under effective anticancer therapy, transient and localized degradation may ultimately inhibit metastasis. This issue remains unresolved and context-dependent, warranting rigorous preclinical and clinical studies to evaluate the safety of specific delivery systems. Achieving precise targeting, strict degradation control, and synergy with anticancer therapies are essential for minimizing metastasis risks.

Although targeted collagenase delivery has demonstrated clear benefits in enhancing tumor drug penetration and immune infiltration in short-term pre-clinical animal models, its long-term clinical impact remains uncertain. This uncertainty arises from several key factors: Firstly, TME exhibits high plasticity, and transient collagen degradation may activate CAFs, triggering compensatory ECM synthesis and remodeling. For example, post-degradation activation of Toll-like receptors 2 (TLR2) or Discoidin Domain Receptor 2 (DDR2) receptors may stimulate fibroblast secretion of TGF-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} and CTGF, thereby initiating a damage-repair feedback loop that promotes excessive collagen deposition [307–310]. This “fibrotic rebound” can reverse initial improvements in vascular permeability and foster treatment resistance [311, 312]. Additionally, ECM degradation products or released bioactive factors may exert prolonged influences on TME, exacerbating malignant phenotypes—such as stemness, invasiveness, and drug resistance—while recruiting immunosuppressive cells or triggering pathological angiogenesis. Together, these mechanisms establish a tumor-promoting niche that facilitates cancer survival, recurrence, and metastasis [312, 313]. For instance, collagen-derived fragments (e.g., endostatin-like peptides) may activate pro-survival PI3K/AKT pathway through integrin signaling, thereby stimulating fibroblast proliferation and activation [314, 315]. Similarly, residual cancer stem cells (CSCs) also exhibit enhanced proliferative capacity under reduced mechanical stress [316, 317]. Thirdly, improved initial permeability or immune infiltration may impose stronger selective pressure, accelerating the evolution of drug-resistant clones. Additionally, collagenase-immunotherapy interactions are complex. While it enhances effector T-cell infiltration, it may also activate MDSCs or regulatory T cells, or amplify immunosuppression via factors like TGF-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}. The long-term net immune effect requires careful evaluation. Thus, while short-term synergy is promising, matrix intervention remains a “double-edged sword.” Its influence on long-term tumor behavior, recurrence and metastasis patterns, and ultimate survival benefits will determine whether this strategy can be successfully translated into clinical practice. While ECM degradation poses theoretical risks, including recurrence, metastasis, and compensatory fibrosis, nanomedicine offers precise intervention strategies to mitigate these challenges. Through spatiotemporally controlled collagenase delivery, optimized degradation—moderate softening rather than complete disruption, and intelligent synergy with anti-tumor and anti-fibrotic agents, nanoplatforms may overcome drug delivery barriers while reducing long-term pathological effects. Future research should systematically assess these risks in advanced animal models and investigate closed-loop ECM regulation systems based on dynamic biomaterial responses, facilitating the safe clinical translation of this approach.

Future perspectives and conclusions

Perspectives

The optimization and advancement of collagenase-mediated ECM degradation strategies depend critically on the development of smart, stimuli-responsive delivery carriers and precise temporal control. Future carrier designs must surpass conventional passive or active targeting mechanisms and adopt intelligent, microenvironment-sensitive approaches. Specifically, carriers should be engineered to respond to tumor or fibrotic microenvironmental cues, enabling site-specific collagenase activation while minimizing off-target effects and collateral tissue damage. Furthermore, multifunctional carriers capable of co-delivering collagenase and therapeutic agents—or integrating targeting ligands and imaging probes—are essential to achieve theranostic applications. Successful ECM degradation and subsequent drug delivery necessitate stringent temporal coordination. An optimal strategy should facilitate on-demand degradation followed by cascade drug release. This can be accomplished by designing stimuli-responsive systems that sequentially release collagenase to remodel the ECM and subsequently deploy therapeutics at predefined time points or in response to biochemical signals. Alternatively, external stimuli (e.g., light, heat, or magnetic fields) could enable real-time, reversible regulation of enzyme activity and drug release. Such precision enhances drug penetration while mitigating inefficiencies from premature drug clearance and reduces adverse effects from prolonged exposure to enzymes or drugs. A thorough investigation of ECM heterogeneity is essential for optimizing carrier design and delivery strategies, as it encompasses compositional, density, and structural variations across different disease types and stages. For example, multi-omics analyses—including proteomics and spatial transcriptomics—of patient biopsy samples can elucidate tumor-specific ECM characteristics, such as collagen isoform ratios, LOXL-2 expression levels, and fibronectin splicing variants. Leveraging these ECM profiles enables the design of personalized nanocarriers, such as those incorporating high-affinity targeting peptides or antibodies screened from patient-derived ECM components. Additionally, integrating imaging and biopsy data facilitates the assessment of patient-specific ECM barriers, further refining personalized therapeutic strategies.

Even though preclinical studies have demonstrated considerable potential of nano-delivered collagenase in modulating ECM for cancer therapy (e.g., 4T1 breast cancer model, orthotopic hepatocellular carcinoma mode, PANC02-A2 tumor model), an obvious limitation can still be noticed in this field: the paucity of data relevant to clinical translation. As of 2025, no registered clinical trials explicitly investigating nano-delivered collagenase for ECM disruption (e.g., in solid tumors or fibrotic diseases) have been identified in U.S. Clinical Information Database. Consequently, conclusions regarding their safety, optimal delivery regimens, and long-term efficacy in humans remain speculative. Therefore, the translation of nano-delivered collagenase from pre-clinical animal models bearing tumors to clinical trials in cancer patients might be challenged in multiple aspects, such as inadequate enzyme stability in vivo, toxicity to off-target tissues, immune responses to nanocarriers and collagenase, and long-term metastasis risk following ECM degradation. By transitioning from a “one-size-fits-all” collagenase delivery approach to an adaptive platform customized according to patient- and lesion-specific characteristics, we can achieve precise matrix remodeling. Future advancements depend on overcoming these challenges via intelligent biomaterial engineering and rigorous preclinical validation. Artificial intelligence will be instrumental in optimizing the trade-offs between barrier degradation efficacy and long-term safety, thereby expediting the clinical translation of this therapeutic paradigm. We anticipate that ongoing innovations in nanomedicine will bridge this translational gap within the next decade.

Conclusion

In summary, targeted delivery of collagenase to degrade the ECM and enhance drug penetration constitutes a dynamic and clinically significant research area. Selectively degrading ECM components, particularly the collagen network, offers a promising solution to overcome the primary barrier to drug delivery in pathological conditions such as solid tumors and fibrotic diseases. Studies have demonstrated that this strategy effectively disrupts the ECM’s physical barrier and aberrant fluid dynamics, substantially improving the penetration depth and distribution uniformity of therapeutic agents, including chemotherapy drugs, antibodies, and immunomodulators. Consequently, preclinical models have shown remarkable enhancements in treatment efficacy. While challenges related to safety and precise regulation persist, emerging technologies, such as smart responsive carriers and temporally controlled delivery systems, are driving further optimization. Continued research and refinement of these advanced methodologies may unlock novel strategies to combat solid tumor resistance, ultimately benefiting a broader patient population.
