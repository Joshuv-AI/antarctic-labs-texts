Cyclic-imide derivative (5e) attenuates hyperglycemia in type 1 diabetic rats by protecting pancreatic cells and enhancing insulin secretion
Authors: Wael A Alanazi, Ali H Alenezi, Doaa M El-Nagar, Mohammed M Alanazi, Mohammed M Almutairi, Alaa A-M Abdel-Aziz, Adel S El-Azab
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes is a major cause of cardiovascular complications, accounting for nearly 70% of diabetes-related deaths. In a previous study, several novel cyclic-imide compounds were evaluated as hypoglycemic agents, and one compound, 5e, showed promising effects in controlling blood glucose in a type 1 diabetes (T1D) rat model. The purpose of this investigation was to clarify the processes by which 5e reduces hyperglycemia through tolerance tests, metabolic hormone assessment, and evaluation of anti-apoptotic effects on pancreatic tissue. Forty male Sprague–Dawley rats allocated to five categories: control, diabetic, diabetic administered glibenclamide (10 mg/kg), and diabetic treated with 5e at two doses (10 and 15 mg/kg). Over two weeks, blood glucose and tolerance tests were performed, and on day 15, blood and pancreatic tissues were collected for analysis of insulin, glucagon, lipase, and amylase, along with histological evaluation. Both 5e and glibenclamide reduced blood glucose by approximately 36%, while the higher dose of 5e lowered glycemia by 50%. All treated groups showed improved glycemic control in tolerance tests, with the 15 mg/kg 5e group demonstrating the greatest glucose clearance and insulin sensitivity. Compound 5e modulated insulin, glucagon, and lipase levels, indicating metabolic improvement, with the strongest effects at the higher dose. Histological analysis revealed enhanced islet morphology and significantly reduced pancreatic apoptosis, particularly in the higher dose 5e group. These findings suggest that 5e improves glycemic control and exerts protective effects on pancreatic structure and function in T1D rats, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent and warranting further pharmacological investigation.Keywords: Diabetes, Antidiabetic agent, Cyclic-imide derivative, Hyperglycemia, Hypoglycemic agent

Introduction

Diabetes is a leading global health challenge, claiming one life every ten seconds and causing more annual deaths than HIV/AIDS (Kaul et al. 2010). Over the past decades, its prevalence has surged worldwide, with studies projecting an increase in adult diabetes rates from 4% in 1995 to 6.4% by 2025 (Kaul et al. 2010). This rising burden is felt most acutely in low- and middle-income countries, which account for nearly 80% of all cases of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) (Deshmukh and Jain 2015).

In the United States alone, nearly 37 million Americans—equivalent to roughly 11% of the total population—are currently living with diabetes (Sachs et al. 2020). Alarmingly, the global number of adults with diabetes has already reached 415 million, with expectations of rising to roughly 642 million in 2040 if current trends continue (Unnikrishnan et al. 2016). Reflecting this widespread impact, diabetes was the seventh-highest cause of death in the United States in 2019, based on around 87 thousand death certificates listing it as the underlying cause (Vital Statistics Reports 2021).

Traditionally, β-cell dysfunction has been attributed to β-cell death; however, emerging evidence indicates that in T2D, β-cell dysfunction arises from a complex interplay between environmental factors and multiple molecular pathways regulating cellular biology (Galicia-Garcia et al. 2020). In contrast, T1D is characterized as an autoimmune disorder, wherein autoimmune-mediated cytotoxic activity of T lymphocytes against pancreatic β-cells culminates in a marked reduction of insulin secretion, thereby precipitating persistent hyperglycemia (Banday et al. 2020). Although the precise mechanisms underlying this autoimmunity remain incompletely understood, both genetic predisposition and environmental triggers have been implicated in its pathogenesis (Banday et al. 2020). Furthermore, hyperglycemia and hyperlipidemia, often associated with excessive nutritional intake as seen in obesity, promote insulin resistance and chronic low-grade inflammation (Banday et al. 2020). These metabolic derangements, combined with genetic susceptibility, expose β-cells to deleterious stressors—including inflammation, endoplasmic reticulum stress, metabolic and oxidative stress, and amyloid accumulation—which collectively threaten islet integrity and β-cell survival (Galicia-Garcia et al. 2020).

Sulfonylureas, introduced in the 1950 s, are among the earliest oral antidiabetic agents and are characterized by a phenyl-sulfonyl-urea structure that enhances glycemic control (Ithape et al. 2023). First-generation sulfonylureas, including acetohexamide, chlorpropamide, tolazamide, and tolbutamide, exhibit lower binding affinity for their molecular target—the ATP-sensitive potassium (KATP) channel—necessitating higher doses to achieve therapeutic efficacy (Feingold 2024). Subsequently, introduced in the 1980 s, second-generation sulfonylureas—including glibenclamide (Glib), glipizide, and glimepiride—have become commonly used in clinical practice (Feingold 2024). These second-generation agents are approximately 100 times more potent than their predecessors (Sola et al. 2015). Unlike meglitinides, sulfonylureas can be employed either as monotherapy or in combination with other classes of oral antidiabetic drugs, owing to their shared mechanism of lowering blood glucose levels (Feingold 2024).

Sulfonylureas are insulin secretagogues by prompting pancreatic β-cells to release insulin directly, independent of glucose levels (Ithape et al. 2023). Under physiological conditions, pancreatic β-cells sense blood glucose concentrations and modulate insulin secretion through the coordinated actions of GLUT2, glucokinase, and downstream glucose metabolism (Feingold 2024). Glucose metabolism elevates intracellular ATP levels, increasing the ATP/ADP balance, which causes KATP channels on the β-cell plasma membrane to close (Feingold 2024). Inhibition of this channel leads to membrane depolarization and consequent activation of voltage-gated calcium channels, triggering a rapid influx of calcium ions (Ithape et al. 2023). The subsequent rise in intracellular calcium promotes cytoskeletal rearrangement, facilitating the translocation of insulin-containing secretory granules to the plasma membrane and ultimately enhancing insulin exocytosis (Feingold 2024).

Despite the availability of numerous effective antidiabetic drugs, there remains a global demand for novel therapies with enhanced efficacy and reduced side effects (Kushwaha and Kaushik 2016). In this context, N-substituted phthalimides have emerged as a promising class of compounds, demonstrating noteworthy biological activities, including anti-hyperlipidemic and antidiabetic effects (Kushwaha and Kaushik 2016). Interestingly, researchers at the College of Pharmacy, King Saud University synthesized twenty-four N-substituted phthalimide derivatives, including several halogenated cyclic imides, and screened them for potential hypoglycemic and hypolipidemic activities in type 1 diabetic rat models (Abdel-Aziz et al. 2011). Among the tested compounds, one derivative designated as 5e demonstrated remarkable in vivo efficacy, reducing hyperglycemia and hyperlipidemia by more than 50%. Notably, compound 5e exhibited superior potency compared to the standard drugs glibenclamide and clofibrate (Abdel-Aziz et al. 2011).

Therefore, this study was designed to investigate the metabolic and hormonal impacts of compound 5e in a rat model of T1D, including assessment of plasma insulin, glucagon, lipase, amylase levels; to evaluate pancreatic morphology; and to examine potential anti-apoptotic effects through assessment of mRNA expression of Bax, Bcl-2, and Bcl-xL.

Materials and methods

Animal

Male Sprague–Dawley rats (260–280 g, 8–10 weeks of age) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Care Center, College of Pharmacy, King Saud University. Throughout the duration of the trial, all animals were kept in optimal research laboratory environments, fed a normal pellet diet, and allowed unrestricted access to water. Rats were housed two per cage and maintained in a 12 h dark:12 h light (12:12) cycle. Animals were handled according to the scientific research ethics protocols at King Saud University. Institutional review board (IRB) approval for the research was approved via the local institutional research ethics committee of King Saud University May 16, 2023 (approval #KSU-SE-23–33).

Diabetes induction

A fresh solution of streptozotocin (STZ) was prepared by dissolving the compound in 0.1 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.5), which was made by mixing citric acid and trisodium citrate dihydrate in distilled water and adjusting the pH using a calibrated meter. The buffer was kept at 4 °C until use. STZ was then administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) at a dose of 60 mg/kg to 24 healthy rats (Ghasemi and Jeddi 2023). To prevent hypoglycemia, the rats received a 5% sucrose solution for two days following the injection. The non-diabetic group received an i.p. injection of citrate buffer as the vehicle. On the third day, rats with blood glucose levels below 250 mg/dL were excluded from the study. During the experiment, glycemia levels, water and food consumption, and urination were measured.

Treatment

A total of 30 male Sprague–Dawley rats were distributed across five experimental groups, with six rats in each group as follows: a control group receiving vehicle (Normal), a diabetic group receiving vehicle (Diabetic), a diabetic group treated with Glib (10 mg/kg/day, orally) (Diabetic + Glib), and two diabetic groups treated orally with compound 5e at doses of 10 mg/kg/day (Diabetic + 5e10) and 15 mg/kg/day (Diabetic + 5e15), respectively (Fig. 1). All in-vivo experiments were performed 2 h after treatment. During the two-week treatment period, blood glucose levels were monitored every two days, and oral glucose tolerance (OGTT) and insulin tolerance (ITT) tests were conducted. On day 15, rats were anesthetized via a ketamine/xylazine mixture (ketamine 100 mg/kg and xylazine 10 mg/kg, i.p.) (Abdollahi et al. 2024). Blood samples were then collected for plasma separation and subsequent biochemical analyses, while pancreatic tissues were harvested for histological evaluation and real-time PCR studies (Fig. 1).

Diagram illustrating the experimental design for evaluating the regulatory effects of compound 5e in a type 1 diabetic rat model

Sample size determination was guided by previously published data on compound 5e in a T1D rat model (Abdel-Aziz et al. 2011). The reported findings showed measurable effects on blood glucose levels with notable variability; therefore, n = 6 animals per group was selected for the in vivo experiments to ensure adequate statistical power. For molecular analyses, sample sizes ranged from n = 3–6, depending on tissue availability and assay requirements.

Glucose and insulin tolerance test

OGTT and ITT were performed to evaluate glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity, respectively. The OGTT assesses the body’s ability to maintain glucose homeostasis and facilitate efficient glucose clearance through insulin secretion and peripheral glucose utilization, while the ITT indicates the responsiveness of peripheral tissues to exogenous insulin.

After 12 h of fasting, baseline blood glucose levels were measured 30 min before administering glucose. Rats received an i.p injection of glucose solution (1.5 g/kg), and blood glucose levels were subsequently recorded at 30, 60, 90, and 120 min by a glucometer, following the protocol of (Benedé-Ubieto et al. 2020). For the insulin tolerance test, animals were fasted for 6 h prior to receiving an i.p injection of insulin (0.75 U/kg). Blood glucose levels were determined before insulin administration and every 30 min for the following 2 h (Benedé-Ubieto et al. 2020).

Assessment of plasma insulin and glucagon concentrations by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Concentrations of insulin and glucagon were measured using commercial ELISA kits (ABclonal Technology, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Plasma samples underwent incubation at 37 °C for 1 h using biotin-labeled antigen. After washing, each well received HRP reagent, which was incubated for 30 min; afterward, TMB substrate was added. The reaction was stopped after 20 min with stop solution, and a microplate reader was employed to measure the optical density.

Assessment of plasma lipase and amylase by Seamaty 120VP vet chemistry analyzer

Plasma samples (100 µL) were analyzed using the VetScan VS2 biochemical analyzer with a dedicated cartridge. The analyzer automatically mixed the samples with reagents and measured lipase and amylase levels across all groups.

Pancreatic histology

Pancreatic samples from each rat group were obtained during euthanasia and promptly fixed using 4% formalin. Samples were then processed for paraffin embedding. Using a microtome, tissue slices approximately 3–4 μm thick were obtained, followed by deparaffinization and staining with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histological evaluation of pancreatic injuries was performed under a light microscope (Gawad et al. 2016).

Assessment of mRNA expression levels of Bcl-x, Bcl-2 and Bax in pancreatic tissues using RT-PCR

Pancreatic tissues were used to extract total RNA with the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) at a ratio of 1 mL per 50–100 mg of tissue. The samples were homogenized, then treated with chloroform, and finally subjected to centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min under 4 °C conditions. The aqueous layer was retrieved, precipitated using isopropanol, rinsed with 70% ethanol, then the RNA pellet was allowed to air-dry before being resuspended in RNase-free water (20–50 µL). RNA quality and concentration were determined using a NanoDrop™ 8000 spectrophotometer. Reverse transcription was performed with 10 µL of reverse transcriptase, and cDNA synthesis was carried out in a thermocycler. Quantitative real-time PCR analysis was performed on a QuantStudio 6 Flex System utilizing SYBR Green Master Mix. The expression of Bcl2, Bcl-xl, and Bax was normalized to GAPDH. All primers (Table 1) were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies.

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as mean ± SD, with statistical significance considered at P < 0.05. For one-way ANOVA, homogeneity of variances was assessed using the Brown-Forsythe test, and Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons. For repeated-measures or two-way ANOVA, sphericity was assessed to evaluate variance homogeneity; when sphericity was violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. All analyses and figure creation were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, California, USA).

Results

Compound 5e decreases hyperglycemia in T1D Rats

The impact of compound 5e on glycemic levels was evaluated by measuring blood glucose every 2 days, and the corresponding percent change was calculated. The results found that administration of 5e at 15 mg/kg/day significantly decreased fasting blood glucose (FBG) in the Diabetic + 5e15 group. The reduction was observed from day 8 and continued until the end of the study, reaching 240 mg/dL (≈50% decrease). This decline was significant compared with both baseline (508 mg/dL) and the untreated diabetic group (578 mg/dL) (Fig. 2A, B). At 10 mg/kg/day, 5e also lowered FBG, with significant reductions from day 10, reaching 328 mg/dL (36%) by the end of treatment (Fig. 2A, B). In contrast, the reference drug (Glib) showed a delayed onset, with significant FBG reductions starting only at day 12 and reaching 367 mg/dL (36% reduction) (Fig. 2A, B). Notably, FBG levels in the Diabetic + 5e15 group were markedly reduced compared to the Diabetic + Glib group on both days 12 and 14 (Fig. 2A). These results were further supported by percentage changes in glucose levels (Fig. 2B).

Compound 5e enhances food intake and body weight while reducing water consumption and urine output in T1D Rats

The effects of compound 5e on additional metabolic parameters, including water and food intake, body weight, and urine output, were monitored throughout the study to evaluate its impact on overall metabolic status. The results showed that the diabetic groups treated with Glib or 5e (10 and 15 mg/kg) exhibited improvements water and food intake, weight gain, and urination (Fig. 3). At the lower dose of 10 mg/kg, 5e demonstrated similar effects on these parameters as observed in the Diabetic + Glib group. In contrast, the 15 mg dose of 5e had a more pronounced impact on these parameters than the Diabetic + Glib group (Fig. 3). The metabolic improvements associated with 5e may be attributed to its ability to lower blood glucose levels.

Compound 5e increases the glycemia response to oral glucose and improves insulin sensitivity in T1D rats

The oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) and insulin tolerance test (ITT) were performed to evaluate the effect of compound 5e on glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity, respectively. The OGTT showed an increase in blood glucose in all diabetic groups 30 min after glucose administration (Fig. 4A). By 60 min, treatment with Glib or 5e (10 and 15 mg/kg) significantly reduced blood glucose compared with untreated diabetic controls. Notably, rats treated with 5e at 15 mg/kg showed a further significant reduction in blood glucose at 90- and 120-min post-glucose loading compared with rats treated with 5e at 10 mg/kg and those treated with Glib (Fig. 4A). The area under the curve (AUC) analysis confirmed a significant difference between the untreated diabetic group and all treated groups (Fig. 4B). Moreover, treatment with 5e at 15 mg/kg produced a greater reduction in AUC compared with the 10 mg/kg dose (Fig. 4B). These results indicate a dose-dependent improvement in glucose tolerance with 5e treatment.

In the ITT, rats treated with 5e at 15 mg/kg showed a significant reduction in blood glucose at both 90 and 120 min compared with untreated diabetic rats (Fig. 4C). In contrast, the 5e 10 mg/kg group exhibited a significant decrease only at 120 min post-insulin injection (Fig. 4C). Additionally, no notable difference was detected between the untreated diabetic group and the Diabetic + Glib group (Fig. 4C). AUC analysis confirmed a notable difference between the Diabetic + 5e15 and Diabetic groups (Fig. 4D). Notably, the higher dose of 5e (15 mg/kg) produced a significantly greater effect compared to the lower dose (10 mg/kg) (Fig. 4C, D).

Compound 5e elevates plasma insulin and reduces glucagon and lipase levels in T1D rats

Plasma insulin, glucagon, lipase, and amylase levels were measured to assess the effects of compound 5e on pancreatic hormone and enzyme regulation. The findings indicated that treatment with 5e led to a dose-dependent enhancement of insulin release. The Diabetic + 5e15 group exhibited a marked increase in plasma insulin levels compared with the untreated diabetic group (Fig. 5A). Although insulin levels were higher in both 5e-treated groups relative to the Diabetic + Glib group, these differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 5A). Treatment with Glib or 5e at doses of 10 and 15 mg/kg markedly lowered glucagon levels in diabetic rats (Fig. 5B). Notably, the 15 mg/kg dose of 5e produced a greater reduction in glucagon levels compared to the Diabetic + Glib group (Fig. 5B). The Diabetic + 5e15 group showed a marked decrease in lipase levels compared to the diabetic groups treated with the vehicle, Glib, and 5e10 (Fig. 5C). Glib treatment significantly improved amylase levels, whereas 5e treatment at both doses resulted in a modest but non-significant increase (Fig. 5D).

Compound 5e improves pancreatic morphology in T1D rats

Histological studies were conducted to assess morphological changes associated with compound 5e. Pancreatic sections from untreated control rats displayed normal islets of Langerhans with numerous endocrine cells and intact acinar architecture (Fig. 6A). In contrast, untreated diabetic pancreases exhibited marked degeneration and atrophy of the islets (Fig. 6B). Treatment of diabetic rats with Glib resulted in partial restoration of islet morphology (Fig. 6C). Notably, diabetic rats treated with 5e at 10 mg/kg (5e10) and 15 mg/kg (5e15) showed greater improvement in islet structure, with well-defined islets containing abundant endocrine cells in the Diabetic + 5e10 and Diabetic + 5e15 groups, respectively (Fig. 6D, E).

Compound 5e mitigates pancreatic apoptosis in T1D rats

Apoptotic markers, including Bcl-x, Bcl-2, and Bax, were assessed to evaluate the effect of compound 5e on pancreatic cell apoptosis (Fig. 6F–I). The mRNA expression of the anti-apoptotic markers Bcl-x and Bcl-2 was significantly upregulated following two weeks of treatment with Glib, 5e at both doses (10 and 15 mg/kg) compared to the diabetic group (Fig. 6F, G). Notably, 5e treatment at 15 mg/kg showed a marked increase in both Bcl-x and Bcl-2 expression in the Diabetic + 5e15 group, while 5e treatment at 10 mg/kg significantly elevated Bcl-2 alone in the Diabetic + 5e10 group (Fig. 6F, G). In contrast, the pro-apoptotic marker Bax was significantly downregulated in the Glib, 5e10, and 5e15 groups compared to the diabetic group (Fig. 6H). Similarly, the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio was significantly reduced in all treated groups, indicating a shift towards anti-apoptotic signaling (Fig. 6I). These findings suggest that compound 5e effectively attenuates diabetes-induced pancreatic apoptosis at both tested doses.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to explore the mechanisms through which compound 5e exerts its glucose-lowering effects. Originally developed at the College of Pharmacy, King Saud University, compound 5e had previously demonstrated significant hypoglycemic activity in a T1D rat model (Abdel-Aziz et al. 2011). In that study, it reduced blood glucose levels by more than 55%, surpassing the 53% reduction achieved by the standard antidiabetic agent glibenclamide (Abdel-Aziz et al. 2011). Building on these promising results, the present research focused on elucidating the role of compound 5e in controlling hyperglycemia through multiple pathways, including the modulation of plasma insulin and glucagon levels, enhancement of metabolic functions, and promotion of pancreatic cell regeneration via the regulation of apoptosis.

The current study demonstrates that compound 5e exhibits potent antihyperglycemic effects in a dose-dependent manner. At a dose of 10 mg/kg, 5e produced a comparable reduction in FBG levels to Glib, achieving a 36% decrease after two weeks of treatment. Increasing the dose of 5e to 15 mg/kg further enhanced this effect, resulting in a 50% reduction in FBG, indicating a dose–response relationship. Beyond its effect on FBG, compound 5e also influenced additional metabolic parameters, including body weight, food and water intake, and urine output. At 10 mg/kg, 5e produced effects similar to those of Glib, while at 15 mg/kg, it led to significantly greater improvements in these parameters. These findings imply that 5e not only reduces blood glucose but also enhances overall metabolic stability, with the higher dose demonstrating a more pronounced impact.

To further evaluate glucose homeostasis and insulin responsiveness, both OGTT and ITT were conducted. All treated groups (Glib, 5e10, and 5e15) exhibited improved glycemic control during OGTT. Notably, the 5e15 group demonstrated significantly greater glucose clearance compared to the Glib group, reinforcing the dose-dependent efficacy of the compound. Similarly, the ITT results revealed enhanced insulin sensitivity in all treatment groups, with the 5e15 group showing the most substantial improvement relative to untreated diabetic controls. These results suggest that compound 5e may improve insulin action as well as glucose metabolism.

To better understand the mechanisms underlying these improvements, key biochemical markers were assessed. All treated groups exhibited increased insulin levels, indicative of improved pancreatic β-cell function. Glucagon levels were decreased in all treated groups compared to untreated diabetic rats, with a significantly greater reduction observed in the 5e15 group. Similarly, lipase levels were lower in all treatment groups relative to the diabetic controls, and 5e15 showed a significant reduction compared to both 5e10 and Glib. The decrease in glucagon may reflect enhanced peripheral glucose uptake and utilization, while the reduction in lipase suggests improved lipid metabolism and suppressed lipolysis (Wewer Albrechtsen et al. 2023). Together, these modulations of insulin, glucagon, and lipase indicate a shift toward metabolic normalization in diabetic rats, with the most pronounced effects occurring at the higher dose of 5e. These biochemical improvements were supported by histological findings. Examination of pancreatic tissues revealed marked improvements in islet morphology in all treated groups, with the 5e15 group exhibiting the most pronounced restoration. In addition, treatment with 5e significantly reduced pancreatic apoptosis, with the strongest anti-apoptotic effect again observed at the higher dose. These structural and cellular changes further support the protective role of 5e against STZ-induced pancreatic damage.

Collectively, current findings provide an evidence that compound 5e improves glycemic status in T1D rats in a dose-dependent manner. While the 10 mg/kg dose of 5e exhibits similar effects to Glib, the 15 mg/kg dose delivers significantly greater therapeutic benefits, including improved insulin secretion, enhanced glucose and lipid regulation (as evidenced by decreased glucagon and lipase levels), and better preservation of pancreatic structure and function. These results suggest that the antidiabetic effects of 5e are mediated through a combination of metabolic regulation and cytoprotective mechanisms.

According to the structure–activity relationship, it was previously found that the compounds falling within the category of 5,6-dichloro-isoindoline-1,3-dione, including compound 5e, demonstrated the highest level of hypoglycemic activity (Abdel-Aziz et al. 2011). Increasing the distance between carbons in compound 5e improved the antidiabetic effect, making it the most active compound in this category (Abdel-Aziz et al. 2011). Furthermore, a pilot toxicological study was conducted on rats via the staircase method, followed by the determination of the lethal dose (LD50) of compound 5e using Miller and Tainter's method, which was found to be approximately 5260 ± 442 mg/kg. These findings demonstrate that 5e shows no toxic effects and is effectively tolerated by the experimental animals (Abdel-Aziz et al. 2011).

In addition to compound 5e’s ability to reduce serum cholesterol levels, its role in regulation of hypercholesterolemia within 24 N-substituted phthalimides compounds has been investigated in a rat model receiving diet-induced hypercholesterolemia (Abdel-Aziz et al. 2011). The study found that 5e reduced serum cholesterol levels by 56%, whereas the positive control drug decreased it around 50% after two weeks of treatment (Abdel-Aziz et al. 2011). Thus, the previous findings showed that compound 5e may have dual actions, hypoglycemic and anti-hyperlipidemic.

Recently, several spectroscopic techniques have been employed to investigate the interaction between compound 5e and bovine serum albumin, with the aim of assessing its binding properties for future pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies (Alanazi et al. 2019). Both spectroscopic and molecular docking analyses demonstrated that the hypoglycemic compound 5e interacts with serum albumin in a spontaneous manner, primarily through hydrogen bonding, and induces conformational changes in the protein. These results indicate that 5e forms a stable complex with albumin, which may have important implications for its pharmacokinetic development (Alanazi et al. 2019).

Conclusion and future direction

The findings show that compound 5e improves glycemia in T1D rats, with effects comparable to standard treatment and even greater therapeutic benefits. These include enhanced insulin secretion, better regulation of glucose and lipid metabolism as indicated by lower glucagon and lipase levels, and improved pancreatic structure and function. Overall, the antidiabetic action of 5e appears to involve both metabolic regulation and protective effects on pancreatic cells. However, this study did not investigate in detail the effects of 5e on pancreatic β-cells or its potential to enhance glucose uptake in muscle tissue. Further pharmacological studies in T1D models are therefore required. Moreover, given its structural similarity to Glib, 5e should also be evaluated in T2D models with hyperlipidemia to explore its possible dual role as both a hypoglycemic and anti-hyperlipidemic agent, along with comprehensive assessments of its toxicological, pharmacokinetic, and pharmacodynamic profiles.
