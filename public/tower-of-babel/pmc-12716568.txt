Assessment of palliative care knowledge among hospital physicians and nurses in conflict-affected, resource-limited settings: a multicentre cross-sectional study
Authors: Bader Nael Remawi, Rawan Abdelbaset, Dima Nimer, Leen Hussary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesMost people who require palliative care reside in low- and middle-income countries, particularly those living in areas affected by geopolitical conflict such as Palestine. Given the potential impact of conflicts on the palliative care knowledge of healthcare providers, it is crucial to assess how it is affected by the recent political instability occurring in the area. This study aimed to assess the overall and domain-specific palliative knowledge among physicians and nurses and evaluate the factors associated with palliative care knowledge.DesignCross-sectional multicentre study.SettingThe study sample was recruited from eight hospitals in two districts in the West Bank, Palestine.ParticipantsPhysicians and nurses working in the following hospital departments were eligible for inclusion in the study: internal medicine/cardiology/nephrology, surgical department, intensive care unit/emergency department and oncology department. Among 227 physicians and nurses approached, 197 participated (response rate 87%), and all of them completed the study.Outcome measuresThe palliative care knowledge test was used to assess the palliative care knowledge of study participants. Bivariate analysis and multiple binary logistic regression were used to evaluate associations with palliative care knowledge. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThere was no significant difference between physicians and nurses in overall palliative care knowledge. Study participants scored poorly in all palliative care knowledge domains. However, physicians scored significantly higher in the ‘philosophy’ domain (p 0.035), while nurses scored significantly higher in the ‘psychiatric’ domain (p 0.009). Questions with the lowest rate of right answers were related to opioids and dyspnoea. Previous training in palliative care was significantly associated with palliative care knowledge (OR 2.529, 95% CI 1.267 to 5.049, p 0.009).ConclusionsPhysicians and nurses in conflict-affected, resource-limited settings had insufficient knowledge in all palliative care domains. The lowest knowledge scores were for the ‘dyspnoea’ and ‘opioid’ questions. Participants with previous training in palliative care had significantly more palliative care knowledge. National guidelines and comprehensive palliative care training programmes should be developed to facilitate palliative care delivery.Keywords: PALLIATIVE CARE, Knowledge, Hospitals, Physicians, Nurses, Cross-Sectional Studies

Introduction

Palliative care improves the quality of life of patients with life-limiting illnesses by relieving their physical, psychological, social and spiritual suffering.1 The WHO recognises palliative care under the human right to health, where all people should have access to palliative care for the relief of suffering, regardless of income or country.1 Globally, the need for palliative care is increasing because of ageing and the increased burden of chronic diseases. Still, a small proportion of people requiring palliative care actually receive it because of several challenges at the individual, organisational and health system levels.2 A major barrier is the lack of knowledge on palliative care, including misconceptions among the public and policy makers about what palliative care can offer and the limited training on palliative care among healthcare providers.1 2

Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) experience a rapid increase in the burden of chronic diseases, which puts strain on the healthcare system and makes it difficult to address the palliative care needs of the residents.3 People living in LMICs are more vulnerable to suffering from severe illnesses because of poor access to good-quality care and lack of formal and informal support structures.4 Although the need for palliative care is greater in LMICs than in developed countries, the demand for palliative care outweighs its supply and many countries do not have palliative care services.3 Major barriers to providing palliative care in LMICs are knowledge deficits among the public and healthcare providers about palliative care, limited training workforce, poor physical infrastructure, limited access to opioids and lack of a national plan for implementing palliative care.3

When people in LMICs live under difficult humanitarian conditions, such as geopolitical conflict and economic disruption, their suffering from severe illnesses significantly increases because of the increased burden on the healthcare and social support systems.5,7 The WHO recommends integrating palliative care in humanitarian settings to alleviate suffering, preserve human dignity and address care needs.4 Still, palliative care is minimally integrated in humanitarian settings because of the limited resources, life-saving ethos, lack of policies and guidelines and insufficient training and expertise among healthcare providers.5 8 Although these obstacles are general barriers to palliative care delivery across all settings, they are exacerbated in humanitarian settings.8 In Palestine, an LMIC with active geopolitical conflict, a national strategy for palliative care does not exist.9 There are limited specialised palliative care services, poor palliative care funding, limited inclusion of palliative care in academic curricula and insufficient palliative care awareness among healthcare providers and the public.9 10 Increasing palliative care awareness in such settings is essential to relieve patient suffering and address their palliative care needs.

Delivering good-quality palliative care requires healthcare providers to have sufficient knowledge of palliative care.11 In hospitals, patients with severe illnesses often present with significant palliative care needs.12 Physicians and nurses are key members of the hospital palliative care team and have a role in managing symptoms, supporting patients and developing care plans.11 Inadequate palliative care knowledge among physicians and nurses leads to insufficient quality of provided care and subsequently unmet palliative care needs among patients. The assessment of palliative care knowledge among hospital physicians and nurses is important to enhance their palliative care competencies. This, in turn, will improve the quality of care provided and reduce patient suffering.

Globally, there is a scarcity of research on palliative care in conflict-affected, resource-limited settings.8 13 In terms of research on palliative care knowledge in LMICs, we found a few multicentre studies that assessed general palliative care knowledge among both physicians and nurses working in different hospital departments. This includes studies conducted in Rwanda, Lebanon, Syria and Nepal.14,17 In Palestine, the palliative care knowledge of healthcare providers had been assessed in several studies.1118,24 However, given the ever-changing political situation and recent escalating conflicts occurring in the country, there is a need to continually assess palliative care knowledge to determine how much it has been affected by the recent circumstances. The recent escalating conflicts and the resultant geopolitical and economic instability in the region may affect the palliative care knowledge of healthcare providers for several reasons, including disruption of education, restricting access to training and changes in resource allocation. Hence, it is crucial to assess how conflict affects knowledge. On the other side, previous studies in Palestine that evaluated palliative care knowledge focused primarily on nursing students and nurses and mostly included oncology and critical care units.1118,24 There is a need to assess palliative care knowledge of a more diverse sample and in other hospital departments. This agrees with the ethos of palliative care in that it should be provided not only to the critically ill but to all who need it.1

To address these research gaps, this study was conducted to assess the general palliative care knowledge of healthcare providers across different hospital departments in conflict-affected, resource-limited settings. This step is crucial to identify palliative care knowledge gaps and subsequently develop context-specific educational programmes to enhance the confidence of healthcare providers in providing good-quality palliative care. This, in turn, would increase patient access to palliative care and may contribute to improving their quality of life.

Methods

Study design

Observational, cross-sectional multicentre study. The study was written in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for reporting cross-sectional studies.25

Sample and settings

Study participants were recruited from two districts in the West Bank, Palestine: Ramallah and Al-Biereh district and East Jerusalem district, which together represent approximately 25% of the West Bank population.26 Five out of nine hospitals in Ramallah and Al-Biereh district and three out of six hospitals in East Jerusalem district were included in the study (a total of eight hospitals). Physicians and nurses working in the following hospital departments were approached: internal medicine/cardiology/nephrology, surgical department, intensive care unit/emergency department and oncology department. Researchers (RA, DN, LH) made multiple visits to the target hospital departments over 3 months, from January to March 2024, to recruit study participants. All physicians and nurses working at the aforementioned hospital departments, regardless of their work experience and education level, were eligible for inclusion in the study. Eligible participants were invited by researchers to participate in the study in a face-to-face contact at their workplace after explaining the aims of the study and signing consent forms.

Accurate estimates of the total number of physicians and nurses in Palestine could not be found, so a formal sample size calculation was not possible. Previous studies that assessed palliative care knowledge in Palestine mostly included fewer than 200 participants, with a range from 70 to 449 and a median of 181 participants.1118,24 Among 227 physicians and nurses approached for this study, 197 agreed to participate (response rate 87%), which is consistent with the median sample size of previous studies. Data collection

A standard data collection sheet was prepared to gather demographic and clinical data from study participants (online supplemental file 1). Data were collected about gender, age, profession, education level, previous training in palliative care, duration of work experience, hospital department and current work location. Palliative care knowledge of physicians and nurses was assessed using the palliative care knowledge test (PCKT) (online supplemental file 2).27 This tool was developed to assess general palliative care knowledge of healthcare professionals. It was chosen since it showed superior methodological quality and psychometric properties compared with other palliative care knowledge assessment tools.28 Paper copies of the tool were self-administered by researchers (RA, DN, LH) to study participants at their workplace after signing consent forms. Participants read and answered the tool questions independently. Researchers waited until participants finished answering the tool questions before collecting them back at the same encounter.

The PCKT tool has a total of 20 questions, divided into five knowledge domains (subscales): philosophy (two questions), pain (six questions), dyspnoea (four questions), psychiatric problems (four questions) and gastrointestinal problems (four questions). Each question has three possible responses: correct, incorrect and unsure. Right answers are marked 1, while false, ‘unsure’ and missing answers are marked 0. The minimum possible score is 0 (worst knowledge), and the maximum possible score is 20 (best knowledge). The level of palliative care knowledge was classified into two categories: ‘insufficient knowledge’ and ‘sufficient knowledge’, with a cut-off point of 10, which is adapted from a previous study.29 ‘Insufficient knowledge’ comprises ‘bad knowledge’ (total score 0–5 points) and ‘poor knowledge’ (total score 6–10), while ‘sufficient knowledge’ comprises ‘good knowledge’ (total score 11–15) and ‘excellent knowledge’ (total score 16–20).

Data analysis

Data were analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences V. 27. Descriptive statistics were used to analyse continuous data in terms of the mean and SD for normally distributed data and the median and IQR for non-normally distributed data, while categorical data were summarised using frequencies and percentages. Valid percentages were reported where there were missing data. Inferential statistics were used to assess the differences in palliative care knowledge scores between physicians and nurses. Differences in the palliative care knowledge mean total scores (normally distributed) were assessed using the independent samples t-test, while differences in the subscale scores (non-normally distributed) were assessed using the Mann-Whitney test. Bivariate analysis using the χ2 test was used to identify the factors associated with the level of palliative care knowledge. The factors which were significantly associated with palliative care knowledge in the bivariate analysis were assessed using a multiple binary logistic regression model, where the dependent variable was having sufficient or insufficient palliative care knowledge. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

Ethics approval was obtained for this study from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Pharmacy, Nursing and Health Professions at Birzeit University in the West Bank, Palestine (BZUPNH2313). Participation in the study was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from the study participants before taking part. Participants’ anonymity and confidentiality were preserved.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Assessment of overall general palliative care knowledge

The palliative care knowledge mean total scores for study participants are shown in figure 2. The PCKT mean total score for the whole sample was 9.49 out of 20 (SD 2.467), ranging from 3 to 18. The mean total score was 9.57 (SD 3.074) for physicians and 9.47 (SD 2.204) for nurses. This difference was not statistically significant, as revealed by a p value of 0.792 using an independent samples t-test.

The percentages of participants with sufficient and insufficient palliative care knowledge are demonstrated in figure 3. The vast majority of participants (71.6%) had ‘insufficient knowledge’ in palliative care, which means they scored 10 or less in the PCKT. This is divided into those having ‘bad knowledge’ (6.1%, scoring 0–5) and those having ‘poor knowledge’ (65.5%, scoring 6–10). On the other hand, fewer participants (28.4%) had ‘sufficient knowledge’ in palliative care, which means they scored more than 10 in the PCKT. Assessment of specific palliative care knowledge domains

The PCKT subscale scores for study participants are shown in table 2. Study participants scored poorly in all knowledge domains, with the lowest median score for ‘dyspnoea’. Physicians scored significantly higher in the ‘philosophy’ knowledge domain than nurses, as shown by a p value of 0.035 using the Mann-Whitney test. On the other hand, nurses scored significantly higher in the ‘psychiatric’ knowledge domain than physicians, as shown by a p value of 0.009. The differences in the mean rank scores for the other knowledge domains between physicians and nurses were not statistically significant.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This study aimed to assess the general knowledge of palliative care among hospital physicians and nurses in conflict-affected, resource-limited settings and to evaluate the factors associated with palliative care knowledge. About two-thirds of study participants had no prior training in palliative care, and three-quarters had insufficient overall knowledge of palliative care. The palliative care knowledge mean total score for the study sample was low (9.49 out of 20), with no statistically significant difference between physicians and nurses. Study participants scored poorly in all knowledge domains (subscale scores), with the lowest median score for ‘dyspnoea’. Physicians scored significantly higher in the ‘philosophy’ knowledge domain, while nurses scored significantly higher in the ‘psychiatric’ knowledge domain. Questions with the lowest rate of right answers were related to opioids and dyspnoea. A multiple binary logistic regression model showed that having a previous training in palliative care significantly increases the odds of having sufficient palliative care knowledge.

Interpretation

The poor knowledge of palliative care among the physicians and nurses is consistent with the findings of other studies conducted in LMICs. In Rwanda and Nepal, physicians and nurses working in hospitals and hospices perceived their palliative care knowledge as low.14 17 A study conducted in Syria, a neighbouring country to Palestine which is also under conflict, showed that only 14 out of 602 participants (including residents, nurses, medical students and nursing students) were considered knowledgeable about palliative care.16 Other studies conducted in Palestine reported similar findings, though the percentages of participants having insufficient palliative care knowledge ranged from 22% to 79%.11 18 20 22 24 The poor palliative care knowledge among physicians, nurses and medical/nursing students in different healthcare settings was also evident in India,30 Mozambique,31 Sri Lanka,32 Uganda,33 Vietnam29 34 and other LMICs.35 These findings indicate that the inadequate palliative care knowledge among healthcare providers in LMICs is a global problem that is not confined to one country. This creates an obstacle to providing palliative care to millions of people living in LMICs who urgently need this type of care, especially those living in conflict settings.3 5 8 There is an urgent need to develop educational programmes to enhance the palliative care knowledge among healthcare providers and increase patient access to palliative care.

In our study, there was no significant difference in the palliative care knowledge total score between physicians and nurses, which indicates a gap in training and poor inclusion in the academic curricula for both professions. This agrees with a recent study conducted among healthcare providers in Palestinian oncology hospitals, where profession (medical doctor vs registered nurse) was not significantly associated with palliative care knowledge.11 Similarly, in a Syrian study, palliative care knowledge was not associated with the specialty of healthcare providers.16 Opposite findings were reported in a Vietnamese study, where physicians had significantly better knowledge than nurses regarding geriatric palliative care.29 This was explained by the higher educational level of physicians, more engagement in palliative care training and more involvement in clinical patient care. Given the key role physicians and nurses play in managing patients with severe illnesses, palliative care education programmes should target both professions, taking into account the different and complementary roles they play in patient care.

Physicians and nurses had low median subscale scores in all palliative care knowledge domains, which indicates a need for comprehensive training programmes. This is similar to the findings of other studies in Palestine,20 24 Uganda33 and Vietnam.34 However, physicians in our study scored significantly higher in the ‘philosophy’ subscale, while nurses scored significantly higher in the ‘psychiatric’ subscale. This is consistent with the findings of Syrian and Rwandan studies.14 16 The ‘philosophy’ domain in the tool asks about the provision of palliative care alongside curative and anti-cancer treatments.27 The superior ‘philosophy’ knowledge among physicians may be explained by their direct and active role in treating patients with cancer and making decisions regarding the goals of therapy. On the other side, as nurses spend more time with terminally ill patients caring for them, they may know more about ‘psychiatric’ issues experienced by dying patients. A study in Uganda reported contradictory findings, where the knowledge of ‘psychosocial and spiritual care’ was higher among medical students than nursing students.33 However, the study sample was different from ours, as we did not include students. Likewise, while Vietnamese physicians had significantly higher knowledge scores than nurses in four out of the five PCKT subscale scores, including ‘philosophy’ and ‘psychiatric’ domains, the study sample included healthcare providers working at a single geriatric hospital, which limits the generalisability of the findings.29

The lowest median subscale score in our study was for ‘dyspnoea’ knowledge, and questions with the lowest rate of right answers were related to dyspnoea (Question 11) and opioids (Question 4 and Question 7). This agrees with the findings of other studies in Palestine,20 24 Syria,16 Sri Lanka,32 Uganda33 and Vietnam.29 34 This is likely due to the limited opioid availability and use in LMICs (especially in conflict settings where resources are scarce), poor education about using opioids for pain and dyspnoea and lack of national policies and guidelines for opioid use and dyspnoea management.9 National policies for opioid use and comprehensive training programmes focused on opioids and dyspnoea are urgently needed. Of note, the opioid ‘pentazocine’ has limited availability in Palestine, which may explain the high rate of false answers in Question 4. Future studies may need to adapt this item in countries where the drug is unavailable.

In this study, the only factor that was significantly associated with palliative care knowledge in the binary logistic regression was having a previous training in palliative care. The same association was reported in other studies in Palestine19 20 22 and Rwanda.14 Other factors associated with palliative care knowledge that were reported in similar studies in LMICs include educational level, professional qualification, work experience, hospital ward (critical care units, oncology and surgical), age and gender.1114 19 22 24 29 32,34 36 37 In our study, healthcare providers working in the oncology department had higher palliative care knowledge, although the association did not reach statistical significance in the regression model. This is an expected finding because palliative care is usually and mistakenly linked to cancer only,2 and hence those working in the oncology departments may know more about palliative care. This also explains the higher palliative care knowledge among those working in East Jerusalem, as one of the hospitals provides specialised cancer care. It is crucial to raise awareness that palliative care is not confined to cancer and should be delivered to patients with other severe illnesses who have comparable symptom burden, such as heart failure and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.38 Healthcare providers working in non-oncology departments should not be excluded from palliative care training programmes.

It is important to note that although participants with previous training in palliative care had significantly more palliative care knowledge than those without training (38% vs 20%), the percentage is relatively low. In other words, only about four out of ten healthcare professionals who joined palliative care training showed sufficient palliative care knowledge. Similar findings were observed in Ayed et al study in Palestine, where although 60% of participants had previous training in palliative care, only 20% reported good overall palliative care knowledge.22 This may indicate a problem in the existing palliative care training programmes in the country or inadequate training. It is crucial to assess the adequacy of training programmes in future pre-post interventional studies, where the intervention is the training programme and the outcome is the change in knowledge. Improvement in existing educational programmes or development of new programmes is urgently needed.

Strengths and limitations

This study adds to the limited literature of palliative care knowledge among physicians and nurses in conflict-affected, resource-poor settings. A strength point is the multicentre nature of the study, as it was conducted across eight hospitals in two districts in the West Bank, which represent one-fourth of its population. The study has some limitations. First, we were unable to collect data from other districts due to the risk of transportation, given the unstable political situation in the country at the time of data collection. However, some healthcare providers included in the study sample also hold part-time jobs in other districts, which enhances the sample’s representativeness and the generalisability of the findings to similar contexts. Using an online tool to reach out to other districts would have been inappropriate to assess palliative care knowledge because of the potential to use resources to help answer the tool questions, which would invalidate the study findings. Second, attitudes toward and practices of palliative care among physicians and nurses were not assessed in this study. These domains were extensively evaluated in previous studies.1118,24 We thought that asking the busy physicians and nurses to fill in several tools would overburden them and would not add much to the literature. Thirdly, the palliative care knowledge of pharmacists and allied healthcare professionals was not assessed in the study, though they are important members of the palliative care team.1 In Palestine, palliative care is mainly provided by physicians and nurses, while most pharmacists and allied healthcare professionals believe that this field is not related to their practice. Future studies should consider addressing this gap by evaluating the knowledge, attitudes and practices of such professionals.

There were a few issues about the appropriateness of the PCKT tool for the Palestinian context, despite its robust psychometric properties.28 The opioid ‘pentazocine’, which is asked about in Question 4 and Question 6, is not widely available in Palestine. Modification to these questions should be considered according to drug availability in the country. Besides, while six out of the 20 tool questions were specific to cancer, other chronic severe illnesses that require palliative care were not explicitly mentioned in the tool, which implies that the tool may be more appropriate for those working with oncology patients. Lastly, although the medical language used in Palestine is English, an Arabic-translated and validated version of the tool would have been more appropriate for a few healthcare providers whose English is weak. Translating and validating the tool in the Arabic language is recommended.

Conclusion

Both physicians and nurses in conflict-affected, resource-limited settings had insufficient general knowledge of palliative care. The lowest knowledge scores were for the ‘dyspnoea’ and ‘opioid’ questions. Physicians scored higher in the ‘philosophy’ domain, while nurses scored higher in the ‘psychiatric’ domain. Previous training in palliative care was significantly associated with palliative care knowledge. National guidelines and comprehensive palliative care training programmes should be developed for physicians and nurses working across different hospital departments in LMICs to facilitate palliative care delivery to patients with severe illnesses. Palliative care courses should be mandatory in the academic curricula for medicine and nursing. Assessment of the palliative care knowledge among other healthcare providers, including pharmacists and allied healthcare professionals, is recommended in future research.
