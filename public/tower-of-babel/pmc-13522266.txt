A novel composite score based on NLR, PLR, and PNI for constructing a nomogram to predict overall survival in patients with gastric cancer
Authors: Zhengwei Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop an NPPs composite score integrating the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and prognostic nutritional index (PNI) as a comprehensive measure of host nutritional status and immune-inflammatory response. The association between the NPPs composite score and survival outcomes in patients with gastric cancer was further evaluated using a prognostic nomogram.MethodologyA total of 206 patients who underwent radical gastrectomy for gastric cancer between May 2019 and April 2021 were retrospectively enrolled and followed after surgery. The median follow-up time was 36 months. Overall survival (OS), defined as the interval from surgery to death or last follow-up, was the primary endpoint. All candidate predictors used to construct the prognostic model were collected preoperatively. Survival probabilities were assessed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed based on survival time and survival status to identify independent prognostic factors. A nomogram for predicting OS was then developed and internally evaluated using bootstrap resampling, the concordance index (C-index), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis. Time-dependent ROC analyses were also performed to assess prognostic discrimination at predefined time points.ResultsKaplan–Meier analysis revealed that patients with a high NPPs composite score had significantly worse overall survival (log-rank P < 0.001). Multivariable Cox regression analysis identified age (hazard ratio [HR] > 1, P < 0.001), vascular invasion (HR > 1, P = 0.016), and NPPs composite score (HR > 1, P < 0.001) as independent factors associated with OS. Furthermore, the nomogram demonstrated excellent discrimination (C-index = 0.858, 95% CI 0.828–0.889) and exhibited favorable clinical utility based on decision curve analysis.ConclusionThe NPPs composite score was independently associated with OS in patients with gastric cancer after radical surgery. The nomogram based on survival-time analysis may be useful for individualized postoperative prognostic assessment; however, its clinical applicability should be interpreted cautiously until further external validation is performed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12672-026-05337-x.

Introduction

Among all malignant tumors in China, gastric cancer has the highest incidence [1, 2]. Common etiological factors include Helicobacter pylori infection, precancerous lesions, family history of gastric cancer, and irregular dietary habits [3]. Presenting symptoms may include a feeling of fullness or discomfort in the stomach, dyspepsia, epigastric pain, anemia, loss of appetite, lethargy, and/or hematemesis [4]. Surgery remains the only potentially curative treatment for gastric cancer. Patients diagnosed at an early stage generally achieve favorable outcomes with surgical intervention, whereas those with advanced disease often require a combination of surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, and/or molecularly targeted agents to prolong survival and maintain quality of life [5, 6]. Despite continuous advances in diagnostic and therapeutic techniques, the proportion of patients diagnosed with early-stage gastric cancer remains relatively low; many patients are still diagnosed at intermediate or advanced stages and continue to have a poor long-term prognosis even after radical resection [7]. Studies have demonstrated that the prognosis of gastric cancer is influenced by multiple factors, including tumor stage, histological type, tumor aggressiveness, and available treatment options [8].

In addition to traditional clinicopathological factors, two biologically based determinants have been identified as key contributors to tumor progression and patient survival: systemic inflammation and immune-nutritional status. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) reflects the balance between pro-inflammatory processes that promote tumor growth and spread and anti-cancer immunity. Elevated neutrophil levels promote tumor growth through the production of cytokines, proteases, and reactive oxygen species (ROS), whereas reduced lymphocyte counts indicate an impaired immune response against the tumor, thereby increasing the risk of disease progression. Thus, an elevated NLR signifies an inflammatory–immune imbalance that favors tumor development and is associated with poor outcomes [9, 10].

The platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) captures the relationship between thrombosis-related inflammatory effects and cancer-associated immune suppression. In addition to promoting angiogenesis, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and immune evasion, platelets protect circulating tumor cells and release angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). Lymphocytes, in contrast, contribute to tumor control by supporting host antitumor immune responses. Accordingly, an elevated PLR suggests a tumor microenvironment conducive to growth and metastasis. The prognostic nutritional index (PNI) integrates serum albumin level and lymphocyte count into a single measure of nutritional and immune status [11]. Low serum albumin levels may result from systemic inflammation, malnutrition, or impaired hepatic function, while low lymphocyte counts indicate compromised immune responsiveness. Therefore, a low PNI reflects reduced host capacity to withstand oncologic stress and is strongly correlated with poor postoperative survival.

The simultaneous integration of the NLR, PLR, and PNI represents a composite biomarker strategy based on systems-immunology principles, rather than using each index independently as three separate indices. Compared with previously reported composite indicators such as the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS), and controlling nutritional status (CONUT) score, the proposed NPPs composite score was designed to jointly reflect neutrophil-mediated inflammation, platelet-related tumor support, and nutritional–immune reserve in a simple preoperative framework. By integrating inflammatory burden, immune suppression, platelet-mediated tumor support, and nutritional-immune reserve, this composite approach offers a comprehensive and biologically meaningful representation of host–tumor interactions. This integrated framework is hypothesized to provide more accurate prognostic information than single-parameter models, which tend to be inherently unstable and biologically redundant. Consequently, inflammatory and nutritional indices such as NLR, PLR, and PNI are not merely associative factors; they mechanistically reflect the interconnected pathways that drive gastric cancer progression, facilitate immune evasion, and contribute to heterogeneous survival outcomes.

Nomograms allow clinicians to visually integrate multiple prognostic variables to estimate individualized survival probability [12]. In the present study, we developed a novel NPPs composite score based on NLR, PLR, and PNI, and incorporated it into a nomogram to predict overall survival in patients undergoing radical surgery for gastric cancer. The model was constructed using time-to-event survival data rather than a simple binary classification of survival status at the end of follow-up.

Information and methodology

Participants for the study (patient cohort)

A total of 206 patients with histologically confirmed gastric cancer who underwent radical surgery at the same tertiary referral center between May 2019 and April 2021 were included in this retrospective study. Patients were followed postoperatively until death or the last available follow-up. For descriptive purposes only, patients were additionally categorized into a death group (n = 73) and a survival group (n = 133) according to survival status at the end of follow-up; however, these groupings were not used as the primary analytical framework for prognostic model construction.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Three Gorges Hospital, Chongqing University School of Medicine (Approval No. Research No. (68) of 2023 ). All the procedures in this study were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion & exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria:

Histologically confirmed primary gastric adenocarcinoma.

Curative (R0) surgical procedure.

Complete pre-operative laboratory evidence and clinical pathology documentation.

Documented survival follow-up for a minimum of 24 months.

Exclusion Criteria:

Any acute/chronic inflammatory disorder or other currently existing acute infections/disorders.

Hematological disorder and/or Autoimmune Disorder.

Immunosuppressive drugs/systemic anti-inflammatory drugs (< 3 months before surgery).

Pre-operative chemotherapy/radiation prior to sampling blood for testing.

Incomplete clinical data/loss of follow-up documentation.

Data collection & terminology for variables

Venous blood samples were collected preoperatively from all patients within 7 days before surgery and prior to the initiation of any adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

Recorded variables for each patient included sex, age, body mass index, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, tumor location, tumor size, differentiation, vascular invasion, perineural invasion, lymph node metastasis, pathological TNM stage (pTNM), NLR, PLR, and PNI.

NLR and PLR were calculated using absolute neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts obtained from each patient. PNI was defined as serum albumin (g/L) + 5 × total lymphocyte count (×10⁹/L).

Outcomes and follow-up

Overall survival (OS) was the primary endpoint of this study and was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to death from any cause or the date of the last follow-up. Therefore, prognostic factor selection and nomogram construction were performed on the basis of survival time and censoring status.

Patient follow-up was conducted through scheduled outpatient visits and structured telephone interviews. If patients were alive at the end of follow-up, they were censored at the date of last contact. The median follow-up time was 36 months (interquartile range, 30–42 months).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviation or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as n (%). Baseline characteristics were compared between patients who were alive and those who had died at the end of follow-up only as descriptive analyses, using the independent-samples t-test or chi-squared test as appropriate.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to graphically represent OS, and differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test.

To identify factors associated with OS, univariable Cox regression analysis was first performed for all candidate variables using survival time and survival status. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariable analysis, together with variables considered clinically relevant, were then entered into the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model to identify independent prognostic factors. To explore potential multicollinearity among clinicopathological variables and inflammatory–nutritional markers, variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis was additionally performed.

Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% CIs were presented.

The prognostic value of each variable was evaluated using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Optimal cutoff values for NLR, PLR, and PNI were determined in the study cohort using the Youden index; therefore, the potential risk of overfitting should be acknowledged when interpreting these thresholds. A nomogram for predicting OS was developed using R software (version 4.0, rms package) based on the independent prognostic factors identified in the multivariable Cox model. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling to reduce optimism in model performance estimation. Model discrimination was quantified using the concordance index (C-index), and model calibration was assessed using calibration curves. Decision curve analysis was used to evaluate its potential clinical utility. In addition, time-dependent ROC curves and AUC values at predefined survival intervals (1-year and 2-year OS) were calculated to further assess the discrimination of both the NPPs composite score and the nomogram over time. Because this was a single-center retrospective study with a limited sample size, an external validation cohort was not available. Statistical significance was established as a 2-sided P-value < 0.05.

In univariable Cox regression analysis based on OS time and censoring status, age, vascular invasion, perineural invasion, lymph node metastasis, NLR, PLR, and PNI were significantly associated with overall survival (all P < 0.05). In contrast, sex, body mass index, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, tumor location, tumor size, degree of differentiation, and pTNM stage were not significantly associated with survival (P > 0.05). Given the established prognostic importance of TNM stage, this unexpected result may be related to the limited sample size, the relatively short follow-up period, and possible overlap between tumor burden and systemic inflammatory–nutritional variables included in the analysis.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that patients with high NPPs composite scores (> 1) had significantly poorer overall survival compared with those with low NPPs composite scores (≤ 1) (Figure S1, log-rank P < 0.001). Similarly, patients with vascular invasion exhibited significantly reduced overall survival compared with those without vascular invasion (Figure S2, log-rank P < 0.001).

Calibration and decision curve analysis of the nomogram for predicting overall survival in patients with gastric cancer

A nomogram-based prognostic model incorporating age, vascular invasion, and the NPPs composite score was developed to estimate individualized 1-year and 2-year overall survival probability (Fig. 3). The nomogram showed good discriminative ability, with a bootstrap-corrected C-index of 0.858 (95% CI, 0.828–0.889). Time-dependent ROC analysis further demonstrated the good predictive performance of the nomogram for 1-year and 2-year overall survival (Figure S3). The model was derived from the multivariable Cox regression results and was intended to predict time-to-event outcomes rather than simple end-of-follow-up survival status.

Discussion

Prognosis in gastric cancer is strongly correlated with disease stage at diagnosis. Stage II through IV (metastatic) gastric cancer is associated with poor prognosis despite multimodal treatment strategies, including surgery combined with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted therapies, whereas early-stage disease is associated with high survival rates following curative treatment [13, 14]. Beyond tumor stage, numerous factors influence tumor growth and behavior, including complex interactions between the biological characteristics of the cancer and its surrounding microenvironment; in many patients, tumor-associated inflammation plays a critical biological role in promoting growth, invasion, and immune evasion [15].

Peripheral blood-based markers of the inflammatory response have been increasingly investigated as cost-effective tools for assessing tumor burden, predicting survival, and evaluating therapeutic response. Neutrophils promote tumor growth by producing pro-inflammatory cytokines, facilitating angiogenesis, and inhibiting cytotoxic lymphocyte activity, whereas lymphocytes mediate cytolytic immune responses against tumors. The NLR thus captures both pro-tumor and anti-tumor inflammatory components in patients with gastric cancer [16, 17]. Platelets also serve as indicators of host immune status; through their roles in promoting angiogenesis, enabling immune evasion, and facilitating tumor cell dissemination, they reflect the extent of platelet accumulation at the tumor site and support of tumor-promoting processes.

The PNI reflects the nutritional and immunological status of patients. Numerous studies have demonstrated that PNI is associated not only with surgical outcomes but also with traditional prognostic measures, including overall survival, across several cancer types [11, 12].

In this study, we developed a combined NPPs composite score integrating the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, and prognostic nutritional index to provide a comprehensive evaluation of systemic inflammation, immune function, and nutritional reserve. Importantly, the prognostic nomogram was established on the basis of OS time and censoring status using Cox regression analysis, whereas the comparison between survival and death groups was performed only to describe baseline differences and was not used as the principal modeling strategy. By incorporating these three indices concurrently, the NPPs composite score reflects the combined biological effects of inflammatory burden, immune responsiveness, and nutritional status rather than assessing each parameter in isolation. This design differs conceptually from SII, which emphasizes neutrophils, platelets, and lymphocytes, and from mGPS or CONUT, which place greater weight on systemic inflammation or nutritional depletion. Therefore, the NPPs composite score may provide a broader view of host–tumor interaction in operable gastric cancer. Recent evidence has also highlighted the prognostic relevance of inflammation-based composite indices in upper gastrointestinal malignancies. For example, a 2025 study published in Medicina (Kaunas) evaluated the systemic immune-inflammation index in gastric and gastroesophageal junction cancers receiving perioperative FLOT therapy and similarly emphasized the importance of inflammatory biomarkers for risk stratification and the need for external validation before routine clinical application [19]. The findings of the present analysis suggest that the NPPs composite score demonstrates superior prognostic discrimination for overall survival compared with NLR, PLR, or PNI evaluated individually. In the present cohort, the nomogram showed a C-index of 0.858, and the NPPs composite score yielded time-dependent AUCs of 0.918 for 1-year OS and 0.887 for 2-year OS, indicating favorable discrimination. Although direct head-to-head comparison with previously published SII-, mGPS-, or CONUT-based models was beyond the scope of this study, our findings support the potential value of combining inflammatory and nutritional parameters within a single prognostic framework. In addition, time-dependent ROC analysis showed acceptable discriminatory ability at predefined follow-up intervals, including 1-year and 2-year OS. In addition to the prognostic value of the NPPs composite score, age and vascular invasion were identified as factors associated with poor survival; both associations are biologically plausible. Notably, TNM stage did not show a significant association with OS in the univariable analysis, which differs from its well-established prognostic role in gastric cancer. This finding may reflect the limited sample size, the relatively short follow-up duration, and partial overlap between stage-related tumor burden and systemic inflammatory–nutritional markers. In this context, inflammatory biomarkers may capture certain aspects of tumor aggressiveness and host response that are not fully represented by TNM stage alone. Age is often associated with reduced physiological reserve and a diminished ability to recover from extensive oncologic surgery [19, 20]. Vascular invasion is characteristic of biologically aggressive tumors and facilitates tumor dissemination; thus, both variables are plausible adverse prognostic factors [21–27].

The authors acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, this was a single-center retrospective analysis, which may limit generalizability and introduce selection bias. Second, although internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling, the optimal cutoff values for NLR, PLR, and PNI were derived and tested within the same cohort, which may have introduced overfitting bias. Moreover, the nomogram was not validated in an independent external cohort, and no separate training and validation cohorts were established because of the limited sample size. Therefore, the stability of the selected cutoff values and the generalizability of the model require further confirmation in larger multicenter studies with independent validation cohorts. Third, although treatment modalities were recorded, heterogeneity in adjuvant therapy regimens may have influenced survival outcomes. Finally, inflammatory markers were assessed at a single preoperative time point, and dynamic changes during treatment were not evaluated, which warrants cautious interpretation of the findings.

In summary, the results of this study indicate that the NPPs composite score may serve as a useful tool for postoperative risk stratification in patients with gastric cancer; however, multicentre prospective studies are required to validate its clinical utility. In particular, future studies should define specific risk thresholds linked to concrete clinical actions, including surveillance intensity and adjuvant treatment decision-making.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the NPPs composite score may represent an independent prognostic factor for overall survival in patients with gastric cancer undergoing radical surgery. A nomogram incorporating the NPPs composite score, age, and vascular invasion showed acceptable discriminative ability for postoperative risk stratification based on time-to-event survival analysis. However, given that this was a retrospective single-center study without external validation, these findings should be considered preliminary. Further multicenter prospective studies are necessary to confirm the reliability and clinical utility of this prognostic model.
