Residents’ perspectives on forensic issues and the impact of postgraduate forensic medicine education on perceived competence and attitudes: a multidisciplinary pre- and post-test study
Authors: Alper Özkök, Seda Saçal, Ümit Şimşek, Cem Terece, Özant Helvacı, Taner Akar, Birol Demirel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysicians frequently encounter medicolegal issues, necessitating adequate forensic knowledge to manage these situations effectively. Evaluating residents’ views on forensic issues is crucial for assessing forensic medicine education’s current status and identifying future specialists’ needs.Methods171 residents from five specialties (pediatrics, internal medicine, general surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, emergency medicine) participated in a pre- and post-survey after attending a course covering forensic reports and documentation, physicians’ legal responsibilities, and medical malpractice. The study assessed residents’ perceived competence, concerns-attitudes, and opinions on these topics and how the training influenced their approaches.ResultsBefore the course, a significant proportion of residents reported feeling incompetent, particularly in their knowledge of forensic report preparation (77.8%), malpractice (78.4%), and physicians’ legal responsibilities (88.9%). Many residents felt pressured by the risk of malpractice (66.1%) and were concerned about repercussions from legal authorities due to potential mistakes in reports (58.5%). Reflecting these apprehensions, the vast majority preferred issuing provisional forensic reports (%90.1) rather than final reports. Most residents believed they did not receive sufficient forensic medicine training during medical school and expressed a strong need for further education. Post-training assessments showed significant improvements in residents’ perceived competence (p < 0.001) and their preference for preparing final reports (p < 0.001), alongside a significant decrease in concerns about legal authorities (p = 0.032) and malpractice (p = 0.014).ConclusionsResidents’ approaches toward medicolegal issues are of great importance as they shape their future practices. Adequate education fosters a proper medicolegal perspective and self-confidence, shielding them from anxiety, defensive practices, malpractice claims, and burnout throughout their careers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08259-4.Keywords: Forensic medicine; Education, medical, graduate; Internship and residency; Mandatory reporting; Liability, legal; Malpractice

Background

Physicians, regardless of their specialty, frequently encounter situations that fall within the scope of forensic medicine [1, 2]. Therefore, possessing adequate medicolegal knowledge is essential for effectively fulfilling their professional duties throughout their careers. By internalizing forensic principles and understanding their medicolegal responsibilities, physicians can manage forensic issues more competently, ensuring compliance with legal requirements while maintaining high standards of patient care [1, 3–5]. However, given the exponentially increasing and continuously updating medical knowledge across all specialties, combined with the demanding working conditions of physicians, these critical aspects may be overlooked or deprioritized [6].

Physicians’ lack of competence in medicolegal knowledge can directly impact the health outcomes of individuals involved in criminal acts and the potential legal consequences of cases arising from such criminal acts [7, 8]. Therefore, the World Health Organization stresses the importance of adequately training healthcare professionals who conduct forensic examinations, including instruction on proper report writing [9]. Beyond patient-related consequences, medicolegal knowledge incompetency can also lead to defensive medical practices and increased malpractice risk, which carry significant implications for physicians and healthcare systems [4, 10]. Although the overall number of medical malpractice lawsuits has declined in recent decades [11], a nationwide research in the U.S. estimated that by the age of 65, approximately three-quarters of physicians in low-risk specialties and nearly all (99%) in high-risk specialties will have faced at least one malpractice claim [12].

Currently, forensic medicine is a required component of medical curricula in almost every country [4]. While the duration, timing, content, and structure of these courses vary, forensic topics are typically introduced at the undergraduate level [1, 13, 14]. On the other hand, medicolegal education at the postgraduate level is frequently either limited to in-service training sessions or is not addressed at all. Consequently, physicians’ forensic knowledge often remains inaccurate or inadequate [15].

This study focused on the self-perception of forensic competencies among residents specializing in Pediatrics, Internal Medicine (IM), General Surgery (GS), Obstetrics and Gynecology (OG), and Emergency Medicine (EM). There are several reasons for selecting residents from these particular specialties. First, these disciplines—particularly EM—frequently involve clinical forensic medicine services as a part of their routine practice [6, 14, 16]. Second, residents and junior doctors in these fields are often the first point of contact in forensic cases [6, 17]. Finally, as recent medical graduates, residents are more likely to provide objective insights into the current state of forensic medicine education compared to more senior physicians. Therefore, evaluating residents’ sense of competence, concerns, attitudes, and perspectives regarding medicolegal issues is crucial in presenting the current state of forensic medicine education and identifying necessary improvements to address future specialists’ educational needs in this domain.

Participants and design of the study

Due to the potential for encountering forensic cases, certain clinical specialties need to be competent in forensic issues. However, some departments may overlook or fail to consistently address forensic topics throughout residency. Therefore, this study involved residents from selected specialties with forensic responsibilities—Pediatrics, Internal Medicine, Obstetrics and Gynecology, General Surgery, and Emergency Medicine—at Gazi University Faculty of Medicine. The aim was to assess residents’ baseline self-perception regarding forensic issues and the impact of regular coursework. In this context, the present study analyzed the following aspects:

The courses conducted for the study were held at different times in 2024 for each specialty, according to the related department’s annual calendar. The course program was developed by forensic medicine specialists involved in this study. These instructors had previously participated in faculty development programs, including training-of-trainers courses, and were therefore well-versed in adult education principles relevant to medical education. While developing the content, the National Core Undergraduate and Postgraduate Medical Curricula for each specialty were first reviewed, and forensic topics that are relevant to each specialty and frequently encountered in clinical practice were identified. Consultations typically requested from the forensic medicine department were also taken into account. Before the training, professors and residents were asked about common medicolegal challenges, ethical dilemmas, complex situations, and previous experiences regarding forensic cases. The final content was reviewed according to up-to-date forensic medicine literature, contemporary medical ethics, and current Turkish legislation. The first three-hour session of the course was the same for all specialties and covered general and common forensic issues concerning all participants. This session was composed of general principles of forensic report preparation and documentation, the common responsibilities of physicians according to Turkish law, and the basic concepts of medical malpractice. In this session, each subject lasted approximately a single lecture duration. The subsequent parts of three-session course program was composed of different subjects according to the field of specialty. For instance, gender-based violence was emphasized in gynecology and obstetrics, while child abuse in pediatrics and surgical malpractices in general surgery were explicitly highlighted (The course content for each specialty has been provided in supplementary file 1.). In topics with clinical applications, case examples related to the topic were shared at the beginning of the lesson, and residents’ evaluations were taken. At the end of the lesson, the cases were re-evaluated together. An interactive discussion about challenging situations was also carried out. All residents who had the means to attend (except for assistants who had other duties in the hospital at that time and were on leave) were participating in the classes. Thus, residents from all years could participate in the training. The same test was applied as a pre-and post-test to these participants.

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1.9.4) to estimate the required sample size for a Wilcoxon signed-rank test (two-tailed). Based on a medium expected effect size (r = 0.3), an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power of 0.95, the analysis indicated that a minimum of N = 170 participants would be required. This estimation was made under the conservative assumption of minimum asymptotic relative efficiency (min ARE), which is appropriate when non-parametric tests are used. The responses of 171 participants in total who completed both pre-and post-tests were included in the study.

The questionnaire was comprehensively reviewed by four professors representing forensic medicine, emergency medicine, internal medicine, and obstetrics and gynecology.

All questions, except for the demographic and open-ended items, were designed using a 5-point Likert scale.

Following the review, a pilot test was conducted with 17 residents to assess the questionnaire’s validity, reliability, and readability. Participants completed pre- and post-training questionnaires assessing their self-perceived competence, concerns, attitudes, and opinions on the topics covered. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha (α) for the overall questionnaire and its four subdimensions. The overall scale showed high internal consistency (α = 0.844). Subdimensions also demonstrated generally acceptable reliability: perceived self-competence (α = 0.845), concerns and attitudes (α = 0.635), opinions on forensic medicine education (α = 0.848), and opinions on malpractice litigation (α = 0.774).

The questionnaire was created using Google Forms. Participants had the option to complete the questionnaire through a digital link sent to their email addresses or via printed forms distributed before and after the training sessions. Before beginning the questionnaire, participants were required to read and approve an information text outlining the study’s purpose and emphasizing the voluntary nature of participation. On average, completing the questionnaire took approximately 10 min; however, no time limit was imposed.

In the study, forensic report preparation is one of the topics where self-competency is questioned. The legal framework for forensic report preparation in Turkey primarily relies on the criminal procedure law. In this context, physicians are required to issue forensic reports as part of their legal and professional duties, especially upon requests by judicial authorities. The medical decisions stated in these reports can impact the penalty amount and detention decision. For instance, as a result of an intentional act, if the victim is in a life-threatening situation according to the report, the perpetrator is kept in custody during the investigation period. Namely, prosecutors take action according to these reports, and all physicians, including residents, are obliged to issue forensic reports when necessary. In practice, these reports can be prepared either provisionally or as final reports. Final reports are prepared later by forensic medicine specialists upon the prosecutor’s request if the initial report is provisional. Although residents have the authority to issue final forensic reports, they tend to prepare them provisionally, which can cause delays in the justice system. Provisional forensic reports are prepared in cases where additional examinations, observation, or expert consultations are required. However, since the initial demands of judicial authorities in most forensic cases (such as assessing the presence of life-threatening injuries or the need for medical intervention) can typically be met through basic clinical evaluation and examinations, issuing a preliminary report is generally considered an exception rather than the norm. This report is preliminary in nature, subject to revision, and should be updated later into a final forensic report. On the other hand, final forensic reports are prepared when the medical findings are sufficient to address the inquiries of judicial authorities. They serve as the primary basis for the forensic process and are valid for use in legal proceedings such as trials and investigations.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study conformed to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants gave informed consent to participate. Ethics approval was granted by the Ethics Commission of Gazi University (09.07.2024/12).

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the rstatix and effectsize packages. Descriptive statistics were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR) for ordinal variables, as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Following the demographic analysis, all pre-training responses (n = 171) were analyzed collectively to describe the baseline perspectives of the residents. Subsequently, data were stratified by specialty to explore interspecialty differences. Interspecialty comparisons were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test; where statistically significant, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni-adjusted Mann–Whitney U test. Pre- and post-training responses were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired ordinal data. Effect sizes were calculated as rank-biserial correlation coefficients (r) using the “rank_biserial()” function in R. Additionally, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the effect sizes were reported. Subgroup analyses by specialty and gender were also performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired ordinal data. Propositions deemed unlikely to be influenced by the intervention, such as opinions on malpractice lawsuits, the impact of malpractice on specialty choice, and the perceived adequacy of undergraduate forensic medicine education, were excluded prior to the training impact analysis, as they were not expected to reflect changes directly attributable to the educational intervention. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, except where Bonferroni-adjusted thresholds were applied for multiple comparisons.

Results

A total of 171 residents completed both surveys, distributed across specialties as follows: Pediatrics (n = 45), IM (n = 60), OG (n = 34), GS (n = 14), and EM (n = 18). The mean age of participants was 27.94 ± 2.87 years. The cohort comprised 62.6% females (n = 107) and 37.4% males (n = 64). The participants were in 2.57 ± 1.42 years of their residency on average.

Collective results of the residents across all five specialties

Based on the responses to the Likert scale questions, nearly half of the residents reported having limited or no knowledge regarding the purposes of forensic report preparation (48.6%) and the types of cases requiring forensic documentation (48%). Fewer than one-quarter of participants (22.2%) rated themselves as competent or quite competent in forensic report preparation. Additionally, only a small proportion of participants believed their knowledge was sufficient or highly sufficient regarding physicians’ legal responsibilities (11.1%) and medical malpractice (21.6%) (Table 1).

Differences between specialties

Significant differences were found between specialties in the propositions “I know the purposes of forensic report preparation (p = 0.004),” “I know which cases require a forensic report (p = 0.002),” and “I feel competent in forensic report preparation (p < 0.001).” Pairwise comparisons revealed that, for the first two propositions, the differences were due to higher scores given by EM residents and lower scores given by OG residents. For the third proposition, the difference stemmed from the higher scores given by EM residents and the lower scores given by both OG and GS residents. In addition, although the differences were not statistically significant, EM residents gave higher scores to the propositions related to perceived competence regarding the physician’s legal responsibilities and medical malpractice knowledge (Table 2).

In the Concerns and Attitudes section, significant differences were found between specialties in the propositions “concern about making mistakes in forensic reports (p = 0.030)” and “preference for issuing a final report instead of a provisional one (p = 0.004).” The difference in the former proposition was due to higher scores given by GS residents, while in the latter proposition, it stemmed from higher scores given by both OG and GS residents (Table 2). It was notable that GS residents, who expressed the highest concern about making mistakes in forensic reports (mean: 4.29), were also the most likely to avoid issuing final reports (mean: 1.57). Furthermore, although there was no significant difference between specialties, the highest scores for the statement “Malpractice is one of the important agenda items in my professional life” was given by OG residents (Table 2). Finally, residents from each specialty reported similar average scores, around 4 (range: 3.70 to 4.16), indicating that the concept of malpractice creates pressure in their professional practice.

There were no statistically significant differences between specialties regarding the propositions related to education and malpractice lawsuits. While residents generally gave low scores to the propositions concerning the forensic medicine education they received during medical school, they assigned higher scores to propositions emphasizing the need for education on these topics. Although the propositions ‘Lawsuits against physicians alleging medical malpractice are filed for fair reasons’ and ‘Medical malpractice lawsuits positively affect the quality of healthcare services provided by physicians’ received low scores across all specialties, it is noteworthy that the lowest scores were given by residents in OG (Table 2). The question regarding the impact of malpractice lawsuits on the quality of healthcare services received the lowest average score across the entire survey (mean: 1.56). Additionally, residents from each specialty considered the compensation requested (min: 4.18 – max: 4.57) and awarded (min: 4.13 – max: 4.36) in malpractice lawsuits to be excessive.

Impact of the training

After the training, residents’ sense of competence significantly increased (p < 0.001) across all propositions, evaluating the knowledge of forensic reports, physicians’ legal responsibilities, and malpractice. The median score for each sense of competence-related proposition showed a notable increase, 1 point on the 5-point Likert scale. In addition, effect sizes were found to be large for all items in this section. Residents’ preference for preparing forensic reports as final rather than provisional reports significantly increased following the training (p < 0.001), with a large effect size (r = 0.563). Moreover, the scores related to concerns about making mistakes in front of legal authorities and the feeling of pressure caused by the concept of malpractice significantly decreased after the training (p = 0.032 and p = 0.014, respectively); however, both changes showed small effect sizes (r < 0.3). Also, all propositions concerning the necessity of forensic medicine education yielded significantly higher scores and large effect sizes in the post-test compared to the pre-test, with most reaching p < 0.001 and r > 0.5, except for one item (p = 0.010, r = 0.318) (Table 3).

Among male residents, positive yet non-significant changes were observed in the items ‘concerns about potential mistakes in forensic reports’ and ‘pressure caused by the concept of malpractice.’ (The results regarding the impact of training by gender and specialty are presented in the Supplementary Table.)

Other opinions

36 participants responded to the open-ended question “Other opinions.” 17 of these participants emphasized the need for more education, with statements such as “forensic topics should be incorporated more into medical education,” “these subjects should be taught as a separate internship,” “forensic topics should be taught in the sixth year before graduation,” “in-service training should be provided,” and “these topics should be repeated.” Five comments highlighted a lack of forensic knowledge, while four participants noted that, in addition to this knowledge gap, malpractice litigations cause anxiety and pressure. Another four comments stated that complaints through CIMER (a nationwide system for handling citizens’ complaints) are too frequent, arbitrary, and unnecessary, increasing workload and causing a loss of motivation. These participants suggested complaints should be reviewed and some should be eliminated before requesting a defense.

Discussion

Collective evaluation of the residents across all five specialties

The overall assessment of the study indicated that residents in major clinical specialties frequently feel inadequate regarding their medicolegal knowledge. It is also revealed that the residents feel anxiety when managing forensic cases, which may explain their defensive attitudes in medicolegal documentation. Additionally, the findings suggested the insufficiency of medicolegal education at the undergraduate level and the need for targeted postgraduate or in-service training. Furthermore, residents perceive malpractice litigations as often being filed for unjustified reasons, negatively impacting healthcare services, with compensation amounts considered excessive. The following paragraphs will analyze these issues in greater depth.

Nearly half of the residents participating in the study reported feeling deficient regarding fundamental concepts of medicolegal reports, and the vast majority (over three-quarters) did not consider the forensic reports they prepared to be adequate. Supporting this perceived incompetency, a study conducted in the same city (Ankara, Turkey) found that emergency physicians’ medicolegal knowledge was deficient when assessed using forensic case scenarios [18]. The participants in our study graduated from various medical schools across Turkey before beginning their postgraduate education at the university hospital where the study was conducted. This suggests that the issue is not confined to a single institution but may be nationwide. Indeed, a recent nationwide study from Turkey revealed that even forensic medicine specialists consider the topics covered in the core education curriculum they received only partially adequate, with approximately half of them finding the number of instructors insufficient [19].

Numerous studies conducted in different countries have also highlighted deficiencies in physicians’ knowledge of medicolegal matters. A study in Wales revealed that few pediatric junior doctors were competent in basic legal principles regarding children [17]. Skiba et al. evaluated Western Australian medical practitioners’ understanding of medicolegal responsibilities related to informed consent and found it to be insufficient [20]. Franchitto and Rouge reported that the vast majority of sixth-year French medical students at a university hospital felt inadequate in examining assault victims and drafting medical certificates [5]. A study from Saudi Arabia showed that fewer than one-third of adult and pediatric emergency physicians knew how to complete the medicolegal reports [21]. Lastly, Elizabeth et al. reported that just over half of Boston’s emergency department physicians and nurses at level-one trauma centers feel confident when managing forensic patients [8].

As a potential reason for this perceived incompetence, most participants in our study believed they had not received sufficient training on these subjects during medical school and emphasized the necessity of such education. Also, numerous studies consistently report that most physicians perceive their undergraduate forensic medicine education as inadequate and express a strong desire for postgraduate training [21–24]. Even some of these studies reveal that the vast majority of physicians have never received any postgraduate or in-service training in forensic medicine [23, 24]. In conclusion, physicians currently practicing in healthcare services consider undergraduate forensic medicine education insufficient and are eager to pursue further training.

The European Council of Legal Medicine (ECLM), the official organization for legal and forensic medicine in Europe, conducted a study evaluating the current state of forensic and legal medicine education. The study indicated a strong consensus on the necessity of forensic medicine courses in undergraduate medical curricula across Europe [1]. However, in addition to undergraduate enhancements, the minimum requirements for post-graduates should also be met to close the crucial gap in their education that could impact their efficacy in this vital area [13].

It is well established that defensive medicine can harm patients through improper or unnecessary testing and contribute to the overutilization of healthcare resources [25, 26]. Furthermore, defensive practices may adversely affect judicial processes in forensic cases. Results demonstrated that most residents prefer preparing provisional forensic reports rather than final ones, which may cause delays in the country’s justice system. Consequently, the low sense of competence regarding forensic reporting may lead to heightened anxiety, which can subsequently promote a defensive attitude in the medicolegal documentation process.

Another potential factor contributing to defensive attitudes among residents is their concern about malpractice. Most participants reported that malpractice concerns exert pressure on them during professional activities and identified malpractice as a significant agenda item in their professional lives. A survey-based study on attitudes toward medical malpractice revealed that 81.7% of physicians and dentists believe malpractice concerns negatively impact the quality of healthcare services they provide. Furthermore, 77.6% of respondents in the same study expressed that malpractice fears cause them to adopt a more timid approach toward their patients [27]. Similarly, in the study by Kayipmaz et al., over 40% of ED physicians reported that legal or administrative investigations influenced their medical decisions [18]. In line with these findings, residents across all specialties in our study consistently gave the lowest average scores to the question evaluating whether malpractice lawsuits positively influence service quality, reflecting their perception that such lawsuits have no positive impact. In conclusion, while acknowledging the subjective nature of the responses in these studies—given that participants may have direct involvement in such cases—it can still be inferred that physicians predominantly believe malpractice lawsuits are often not filed for justified reasons and that the compensation requested and awarded in these cases is excessive.

Impact of the training

Although many studies highlight the necessity of forensic medicine education, few have investigated the impact of education. These few studies have generally reported positive changes with the training. For instance, Drake et al. assessed the effect of training on fundamental forensic knowledge and perceived ability in emergency nurse practitioners using pre- and post-tests, finding a significant improvement [28]. Similarly, Wasser et al. reported significantly enhancing psychiatry residents’ knowledge of forensic psychiatry principles after completing two online learning modules [29]. Among the most recent studies on the subject, Tambuzzi et al. conducted a randomized controlled trial to evaluate the impact of training on the forensic skills and procedural compliance of emergency medicine physicians when examining cases of interpersonal physical violence. The intervention group demonstrated a statistically significant increase in the mean total score after a 6-hour course (19.0) compared to their pre-course score (14.0) and the control group (14.0). However, the authors noted that while the training had a positive impact, the modest 5-point improvement—considering a maximum possible score of 41.0—highlighted that medicolegal aspects remain largely neglected [6]. Lastly, another recent study by Aulino et al. found that completing a forensic medicine degree course significantly improved Italian medical students’ understanding of malpractice and defensive medicine [30].

Our study detected significant positive changes, generally with large effect sizes in parameters following the training, including participants’ sense of competence, attitudes, and opinions about education. Although concerns about making mistakes in front of legal authorities and the feeling of pressure caused by the concept of malpractice also showed positive changes, their effect sizes were small. Furthermore, the impact of the training was generally similar across gender subgroups, which was consistent with the overall sample. Improvements in competence and attitudes are expected in a survey conducted immediately after a training session. However, we believe that these findings still underscore the critical importance of postgraduate and in-service training. The study demonstrated that even a short, structured course can improve residents’ perceived competence and reduce their concerns about medical malpractice and legal sanctions. Therefore, to provide and sustain these positive changes over time, it is essential to implement regular, comprehensive, and repetitive forensic training for all medical practitioners, including residents.

Turkey’s National Core Undergraduate Medical Curriculum includes nearly all fundamental forensic topics, along with the expected competency levels for medical graduates [31]. For instance, competencies such as examining forensic cases, preparing forensic reports, and managing forensic situations are assigned a level 3 out of 4, indicating that students should be capable of independently managing common, uncomplicated cases. However, our findings, supported by previous national studies cited earlier in this section, reflect a lack of standardization and a general sense of inadequacy among medical graduates regarding forensic medicine topics.

On the other hand, the Core Postgraduate Medical Curricula contain more significant gaps, and several clinical specialties entirely omit forensic content [32–36]. While many clinical disciplines routinely encounter medico-legal cases in practice, the fact that only the anatomy residency includes a formal forensic medicine rotation suggests that forensic training is largely overlooked by clinical specialties. Beyond curricular limitations, possible shortcomings in the quality or depth of instruction, the time elapsed since the initial training, and limited opportunities for practical application stemming from structural constraints may also contribute to the low levels of competence. This is particularly evident for procedures such as sexual assault examinations, which require hands-on experience but are challenging to gain due to their sensitive nature, the need for patient privacy, and their relative rarity in clinical settings.

To address these challenges, it is crucial that clinical residency curricula allocate adequate space for forensic topics. Moreover, implementing standardized and repetitive educational programs that incorporate practical training components would be an essential step toward enhancing the lasting impact of forensic medicine education.

Differences between specialties

According to interdepartmental results, EM residents demonstrated a higher sense of competence regarding forensic report and documentation knowledge, whereas OG and GS residents generally reported a lower sense of competence in this area. However, the validity of these findings is limited due to the small sample sizes, particularly for EM and GS. Nevertheless, the results are consistent with the fact that EM is frequently the first contact point for forensic cases [6, 18]. Frequent exposure to such cases and the associated responsibility of preparing numerous forensic reports may explain EM residents’ relatively greater proficiency. On the other hand, the lower sense of competence among OG and GS residents may also be attributed to the fact that forensic cases are usually first handled by internal medicine departments, particularly EM, at the time of hospital admission. Once the forensic reports are prepared at that stage, the cases are then referred to surgical departments for further management, which may result in fewer opportunities for surgical physicians to engage in forensic reporting.

Although no statistically significant difference was observed due to the common low scores across all departments, OG residents provided the lowest scores in response to the questions concerning the legitimacy of malpractice lawsuits and their impact on the quality of healthcare services. Conversely, they also assigned the highest scores to the statement, “Malpractice is one of the important agenda items in my professional life.” OG is often regarded as a high-risk specialty for malpractice worldwide. It is also among the specialties most frequently targeted by malpractice claims in Turkey [12]. Three studies examining the decisions of Turkey’s supreme judicial bodies on malpractice litigations—spanning the periods from 2000 to 2011, 2015 to 2021, and in 2021—consistently identified OG as the leading specialty facing such claims [37–39]. When these outcomes are considered together, it may be speculated that OG residents perceive the potential risks associated with malpractice lawsuits more acutely than residents in other departments.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, participation was voluntary, which may have resulted in selection bias by attracting residents who were already interested in or more motivated to engage with forensic medicine education. Second, the presence of instructors during the administration of the surveys may have influenced responses due to social desirability bias, potentially leading participants to provide more socially desirable responses. Third, the use of a pre- and post-test design raises the possibility of regression to the mean, particularly among participants who initially reported extreme scores (e.g., very low competence), which may have naturally regressed toward the mean after the intervention and thus inflated the observed effect of the training. Taken together, these factors may limit the generalizability of the results and should be considered when assessing the true impact of the educational intervention.

As the study was conducted at a single center, ideal participant numbers could not be achieved for two specialties (EM and GS). This limitation made it challenging to evaluate inter-specialty differences comprehensively. Conducting larger-scale multicenter studies would enable more representative sampling across specialties, leading to more valid and reliable results. Thus, the specific needs of each specialty can be identified more effectively, and targeted training programs tailored to address those needs can be developed.

Finally, it should be noted that legislation—and consequently, the practice of forensic medicine—may vary between countries. In Turkey, the current legislation and legal regulations are largely similar to those of many continental European countries. Although minor differences exist, these frameworks have been aligned to a great extent through the European Union Expert-Witness Matching Workshops [40]. Nevertheless, this situation may limit the international generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

We focused this research on residents to assess the recent status of undergraduate forensic medicine education and identify the needs of future medical specialists regarding medicolegal issues. The findings of the research led us to conclude the following points:

Establishing a standardized forensic medicine curriculum remains a challenge in many countries. The absence of structured forensic education can have significant implications for resident physicians, who are future specialists in their respective fields. Consequently, how these physicians develop their approach to medicolegal issues is critically important. With adequate training, residents can cultivate a sound medicolegal perspective and gain the self-confidence needed to mitigate anxiety, avoid unnecessary defensive medical practices, and prevent burnout over the course of their careers. In this context, we believe that our study offers valuable insights for shaping educational policies, assessing academic standards in forensic medicine, implementing necessary reforms, and promoting continuous improvement.
