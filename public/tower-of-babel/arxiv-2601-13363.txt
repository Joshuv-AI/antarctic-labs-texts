Center of distances of ultrametric spaces generated by labeled trees
Oleksiy Dovgoshey

Abstract




Abstract.

The center of distances of a metric space
(X,d)
is the set

C(X)
of all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
for which the equation
d(x,p)=t
has a solution for each
p\in X
.
We prove that the equalities

C(X)=\{0\}
or

C(X)=\{\operatorname{diam}X,0\}

hold if
(X,d)
is an ultrametric space generated by labeled trees.
The necessary and sufficient conditions under which
\operatorname{diam}X\in C(X)
are found.

Keywords:

center of distances; diametrical graph; labeled tree; ultrametric space.

MSC (2020):
54E35.

Introduction

Ultrametric spaces generated by non-negative vertex labelings on arbitrary trees were introduced in
[
19
]
and subsequently studied in
[
11
,
13
]
.
A characterization of totally bounded ultrametric spaces generated by labeled almost rays was provided in
[
18
]
. Furthermore,
[
16
]
contains a metric characterization of ultrametric spaces generated by labeled star graphs.

It was shown in
[
10
]
that the compact ultrametric spaces generated by labeled star graphs are the completions of totally bounded ultrametric space generated by decreasingly labeled rays.
The labeled trees generating separable ultrametric spaces are characterized, up to isomorphism, in
[
15
]
.

In the present paper we investigate the class
{\bf UT}
of all ultrametric spaces which are isometric to spaces generated by labeled trees. The main goal of the paper is the characterization of the center of distances
C(X)
of
{\bf UT}
-spaces
(X,d)
.

The paper is organized as follows.
The next section contains some definitions and facts from the theory of ultrametric spaces and graph theory. The main results of the paper are proved in Sections
3
–
5
.

Theorem
3
of Section
3
shows that the center of distances
C(X)
of arbitrary
{\bf UT}
-space
(X,d)
satisfies either the equality

C(X)=\{0,\operatorname{diam}X\}

(1)

or the equality

C(X)=\{0\}.

(2)

In Section
4
we introduce the concept of centered spheres and use it in Theorem
4
to describe the geometry of
{\bf UT}
-spaces
(X,d)
which satisfy (
1
). Theorem
5
of this section gives a necessary and sufficient condition under which each open ball of a given
{\bf UT}
-space is a centered sphere in this space.
Theorem
6
of Section
4
shows that an ultrametric space
(X,d)
is equidistant if and only if each centered sphere of
(X,d)
is an open ball of
(X,d)
.

Theorem
7
of Section
5
shows that equality (
1
) holds for
{\bf UT}
-space
(X,d)
if and only if the diametrical graph of
(X,d)
has a spanning star subgraph.
Corollary
4
of Theorem
8
of Section
5
shows that equality (
2
) holds for
{\bf UT}
-space
(X,d)
with
|X|\geq 2
if and only if
(X,d)
is weakly similar to an unbounded ultrametric space.

2
Preliminaries

In what follows we will use the symbol
\mathbb{R}^{+}
to denote the set
[0,+\infty)
.

Definition 1
.

Let
X
be a non-empty set. A metric on
X
is a symmetric function
d\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
satisfying the equality

d(x,x)=0
for each
x\in X
, the inequality
d(x,y)>0
for all distinct
x,y\in X
, and the triangle inequality

d(x,y)\leq d(x,z)+d(z,y)

for all
x
,
y
,
z\in X
.

A metric space
(X,d)
is called an
ultrametric space
if the
strong triangle inequality

d(x,y)\leq\max\{d(x,z),d(z,y)\}

holds for all
x
,
y
,
z\in X
. The function
d
is called
an ultrametric
on
X
.

Following
[
7
, p. 46]
we say that a metric
d:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is
equidistant
if there is a constant
k>0
such that

d(x,y)=k

whenever
x
and
y
are distinct points of
X
.

Definition 2
.

Let
(X,d)
and
(Y,\rho)
be metric spaces. A bijective mapping

\Phi\colon X\to Y

is said to be an isometry if

d(x,y)=\rho(\Phi(x),\Phi(y))

holds for all
x,y\in X
. The metric spaces are
isometric
if there is an
isometry of these spaces.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. In what follows we will denote by
D(X)
the
distance set
of the space
(X,d)
,

D(X):=\{d(x,y):x,y\in X\}.

(3)

The concept of center of distances was introduced in
[
4
]
as follows.

Definition 3
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space.
The center of distances of
(X,d)
is the set
C(X)

defined as

C(X):=\{t\in D(X):\forall p\in X\ \exists x\in X\ d(p,x)=t\}.

(4)

Remark 1
.

Some properties of the center of distances are described in
[
2
,
3
,
24
,
25
]
.

Example 1

(The
p
-adic ultrametric)
.

Let
p\geq 2
be a prime number,
\mathbb{Q}
be the set of rational numbers, and
\mathbb{Z}
be the set of integers. The
p
-adic valuation of
t\in\mathbb{Q}
is defined as

|t|_{p}:=\begin{cases}p^{-\gamma},&\text{if }t\neq 0,\\
0,&{\text{o}therwise},\end{cases}

(5)

where
\gamma=\gamma(t)
is the unique
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

t=p^{\gamma}\frac{m}{n},

and
m,

n
are coprime to
p
.
The
p
-adic valuation satisfies the inequality

|t+w|_{p}\leq\max\{|t|_{p},|w|_{p}\}

for all
t,w\in\mathbb{Q}
.
This implies that the mapping

d_{p}:\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{R}^{+},

d_{p}(t,w):=|t-w|_{p}

(6)

is an ultrametric on
\mathbb{Q}
. (See formula (1.10) in Chapter 1 of
[
1
]
.)
It directly follows from (
6
) that the mapping

{\mathbb{Q}}\ni x\mapsto x+y\in{\mathbb{Q}}

is a self-isometry of the space
({\mathbb{Q}},d_{p})
for every
y\in\mathbb{Q}
. Which gives us the equalities

D(\mathbb{Q})=C(\mathbb{Q})=\{\,p^{\gamma}:\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}\,\}\cup\{0\}

(7)

in accordance with formulas (
3
)–(
6
).

Let
A
be a non-empty subset of a metric space
(X,d)
. The quantity

\operatorname{diam}A:=\sup\{d(x,y):x,y\in A\}

(8)

is the
diameter
of
A
. If the inequality
\operatorname{diam}A<\infty

holds, then we say that
A
is a
bounded
subset of
(X,d)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. The
open ball
with a
radius

r>0
and a
center

c\in X
is the set

B_{r}(c):=\{x\in X\colon d(c,x)<r\}.

(9)

Let us denote by
{\bf B}_{X}
the set of all open balls of metric space
(X,d)
.

The next proposition is a part of Proposition 18.4 from
[
27
]
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
(X,d)
be an ultrametric space. Then, for every ball
B_{r}(c)\in{\bf B}_{X}
and every
a\in B_{r}(c)
, we have

B_{r}(c)=B_{r}(a).

Now, we recall the concepts of
compactness
and
total boundedness
.

A standard definition of compactness is usually formulated as:
a metric space
(X,d)
is compact if every open cover of
X
has a finite subcover.

Definition 4
.

A metric space
(X,d)
is totally bounded if for every

r>0
there is a finite set

\{B_{r}(x_{1}),\ldots,B_{r}(x_{n})\}\subseteq{\bf B}_{X}

such that

X\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B_{r}(x_{i}).

It follows directly from Definition
4
that every compact metric space is totally bounded.

The next proposition is a corollary of Theorem 3.6 from paper
[
17
]
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
(X,d)
be an infinite totally bounded ultrametric space and let
D(X)
be the distance set of the space
(X,d)
. Then there is a
strictly decreasing sequence

(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of points of
\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}=0

holds and the equivalence

(x\in D(X))\iff(x=0\text{ or }\exists n\in\mathbb{N}:x_{n}=x)

is valid for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

Further, let us recall some definitions and facts from graph theory.

A
graph
is a pair
(V,E)
consisting of a non-empty set
V
and a set
E
whose elements are unordered pairs
\{u,v\}
of different points
u,v\in V
. For a graph
G=(V,E)
, the sets
V=V(G)
and
E=E(G)
are called
the set of vertices
and
the set of edges
, respectively. A graph
G
is
finite
if
V(G)
is a finite set. If
\{x,y\}\in E(G)
, then the vertices
x
and
y
are
ends
of
\{x,y\}
and called
adjacent
. In what follows, we will always assume that
E(G)\cap V(G)=\emptyset
.
A graph
G
is
empty
if
E(G)=\emptyset
.

The following concept is well-known for finite graphs (see, for example,
[
8
, p. 17]
).

Definition 5
.

Let
G
be a graph and let
k\geq 2
be a cardinal number. The graph
G

is complete
k
-partite if the vertex set
V(G)
can be partitioned into
k
non-empty
disjoint parts, in such a way that no edge has both ends in the same part and
any two vertices in different parts are adjacent.

We say that
G
is a
complete multipartite graph
if
G
is complete
k
-partite for some
k
.

Our next definition is a modification of Definition 2.1 from
[
26
]
.

Definition 6
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. Denote by
G_{X}
a graph
such that
V(G_{X})=X
and, for
u,v\in V(G_{X})
,

(\{u,v\}\in E(G_{X}))\iff(d(u,v)=\operatorname{diam}X\text{ and }u\neq v).

(10)

We call
G_{X}
the diametrical graph of
(X,d)
.

Remark 2
.

It directly follows from (
10
) that
G_{X}
is empty if and only if
X
is a singleton or
\operatorname{diam}X\notin D(X)
.

The following theorem is a direct consequence of Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 from
[
9
]
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(X,d)
be an ultrametric space with
|X|\geq 2
. Then the following
statements are equivalent:

(i)

The diametrical graph
G_{X}
is non-empty.

(ii)

The diametrical graph
G_{X}
is complete multipartite.

Corollary 1
.

Let
(X,d)
be an ultrametric space with
|X|\geq 2
,
let
D(X)
be the distance set of
(X,d)
, and let
C(X)
be the center of distances of
(X,d)
.
If
\operatorname{diam}X
belongs to
D(X)
,

\operatorname{diam}X\in D(X),

(11)

then
\operatorname{diam}X
also belongs to
C(X)
,

\operatorname{diam}X\in C(X).

(12)

Proof.

If (
11
) holds, then the diametrical graph
G_{X}
is non-empty by Remark
2
.
Hence
G_{X}
is complete multipartite by Theorem
1
.
Now Definitions
3
and
5
imply (
12
).
∎

Let
G
be a graph.
A graph
G_{1}
is a
subgraph
of
G
if

V(G_{1})\subseteq V(G)\quad\text{and}\quad E(G_{1})\subseteq E(G).

In this case we will write
G_{1}\subseteq G
.

A
path
is a finite graph
P
whose vertices can be numbered without repetitions so that

V(P)=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}\quad\text{and}\quad E(P)=\{\{x_{1},x_{2}\},\ldots,\{x_{k-1},x_{k}\}\}

(13)

with
k\geqslant 2
. We will write
P=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
or
P=P_{x_{1},x_{k}}
if
P
is a path satisfying (
13
) and said that
P
is a
path joining
x_{1}
and
x_{k}
. A graph
G
is
connected
if for every two distinct vertices of
G
there is a path
P\subseteq G
joining these vertices.

A finite graph
C
is a
cycle
if there exists an enumeration of its vertices without repetitions such that
V(C)=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
and

E(C)=\{\{x_{1},x_{2}\},\ldots,\{x_{n-1},x_{n}\},\{x_{n},x_{1}\}\}\quad\text{with }n\geq 3.

Definition 7
.

A connected graph
T
without cycles is called a tree.

Let us recall the concept of star graphs.

Definition 8
.

A tree
T
is a star graph if there exists a vertex

c\in V(T)
such that

E(T)=\{\{v,c\}\colon v\in V(T)\setminus\{c\}\}.

A subtree
T
of a connected graph
G
is called a
spanning tree
of
G
if the equality
V(T)=V(G)
holds.

Let us introduce the concept of labeled trees.

Definition 9
.

A labeled tree is a pair
(T,l)
, where
T
is a tree and
l
is a mapping defined on
V(T)
.

If
(T,l)
is a labeled tree, then we write
T=T(l)
instead of
(T,l)
. Moreover, in what follows, we will consider only the non-negative real-valued labelings
l\colon V(T)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

Following
[
19
]
, for arbitrary labeled tree
T=T(l)
, we define a mapping
d_{l}\colon V(T)\times V(T)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
as

d_{l}(u,v):=\begin{cases}\max\limits_{w\in V(P_{u,v})}l(w),&\text{if}\,\,u\neq v,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(14)

where
P_{u,v}
is a path joining
u
and
v
in
T
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
T=T(l)
be a labeled tree. The mapping
d_{l}
is an ultrametric on the set
V(T)
if and only if the inequality

\max\{l(u),l(v)\}>0

(15)

holds for every
\{u,v\}\in E(T)
.

A proof of Theorem
2
can be obtained by simple modification of the proof of Proposition 3.2 in
[
19
]
.

As in
[
18
,
16
]
we shall say that a labeling
l\colon V(T)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is
non-degenerate
if inequality (
15
)
holds for every
\{u,v\}\in E(T)
.

The following lemma will be used in the next section of the paper.

Lemma 1
.

Let
T=T(l)
be a labeled tree with a non-degenerate labeling

l\colon V(T)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
and let
D(V(T))
be the distance set of the ultrametric space
(V(T),d_{l})
.
Then there exists a non-degenerate labeling

l^{*}\colon V(T)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

d_{l^{*}}=d_{l}

(16)

and the equality

D(V(T))=\{l^{*}(u)\,:\,u\in V(T)\}

(17)

holds.

Proof.

Let us define a labeling
l^{*}\colon V(T)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
as

l^{*}(u):=\begin{cases}l(u),&\text{if}\,\,l(u)\in D(V(T)),\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(18)

Equalities (
16
) and (
17
) evidently hold if
|V(T)|=1
. Suppose that
|V(T)|\geq 2
and consider an arbitrary edge
\{u^{\circ},v^{\circ}\}\in E(T)
.
Using (
14
) we obtain

0<d_{l}(u^{\circ},v^{\circ})=\max\{l(u^{\circ}),l(v^{\circ})\}

(19)

and, consequently,

\max\{l(u^{\circ}),l(v^{\circ})\}\in D(V(T)).

(20)

Membership relation (
20
) and formula (
18
) imply the equality

\max\{l(u^{\circ}),l(v^{\circ})\}=\max\{l^{*}(u^{\circ}),l^{*}(v^{\circ})\}.

(21)

The last equality and formula (
18
) show that

l^{*}\colon V(T)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a non-degenerate labeling of
V(T)
because
l\colon V(T)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is non-degenarate.
Thus
(V(T),d_{l^{*}})
is an ultrametric space by Theorem
2
.

Let us prove equality (
16
).
Let
x
and
y
be two distinct vertices of
T
and let
P_{x,y}\subseteq T

be the path joining
x
and
y
.
Then using (
14
), (
19
), and (
21
) we obtain

d_{l}(x,y)=\max_{w\in V(P_{x,y})}l(w)=\max_{\{u,v\}\in E(P_{x,y})}\{l(u),l(v)\}=\max_{\{u,v\}\in E(P_{x,y})}\{l^{*}(u),l^{*}(v)\}=

\max_{w\in V(P_{x,y})}\{l^{*}(w)\}=d_{l^{*}}(x,y).

Thus equality (
16
) holds.

To prove equality (
17
) it suffices to note that
equality (
14
) with
l=l^{*}
implies the inclusion

D(V(T))\subseteq\{l^{*}(u):u\in V(T)\}

and that the converse inclusion

D(V(T))\supseteq\{l^{*}(u):u\in V(T)\}

follows from (
18
).

The proof is completed.
∎

In what follows we will denote by
{\bf UT}
the class of all ultrametric spaces
which are isometric to the space generated by labeled trees
with non-degenerate vertex labelings.

The main goal of the present paper is to describe the structure of centers of distances

C(X)
for
(X,d)\in{\bf UT}
.

3
Center of distances of UT-spaces

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space, let
p\in X
, and
let
D_{p}(X)
be a subset of
the distance set
D(X)

defined as

D_{p}(X):=\{d(p,x):x\in X\}.

(22)

Proposition 3
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and let
C(X)
be the center of distances of
(X,d)
. Then the equality

C(X)=\bigcap_{p\in X}D_{p}(X)

(23)

holds.

Proof.

All metric spaces are non-empty by Definition
1
.
Consequently, the right side of equality (
23
) is correctly defined.
Now (
23
) follows from Definition
3
and formulas (
4
), (
22
).
∎

Proposition 4
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and let
C(X)
be the center of distances of
(X,d)
.
Then
C(X)
contains the point
0
.

Proof.

Since we have
d(p,p)=0
for every
p\in X
, equality (
22
) implies

0\in D_{p}(X)

for all
p\in X
.
Hence

0\in\bigcap_{p\in X}D_{p}(X)

holds. The last membership relation and Proposition
3
imply

0\in C(X)

as required.
∎

Remark 3
.

Propositions
3
and
4
are known, see, for example,
[
25
]
and
[
4
]
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
T=T(l)
be a labeled tree with a non-degenerate labeling

l\colon V(T)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
satisfying the equality

D(V(T))=\{l(u):u\in V(T)\}.

(24)

Then

\alpha\notin C(X)

(25)

holds whenever

0<\alpha<\operatorname{diam}V(T).

(26)

Proof.

Let us consider an arbitrary
\alpha
satisfying (
26
).
Then, using (
8
), (
24
), and (
26
), we can find a vertex
v^{*}\in V(T)
such that

\alpha<l(v^{*}).

(27)

Write

D_{v^{*}}(V(T)):=\{d_{l}(v^{*},u):u\in V(T)\}.

Equality (
14
) implies the inequality

d_{l}(v^{*},u)\geq l(v^{*})

for each
u\in V(T)\setminus\{v^{*}\}
.
Hence
\alpha
does not belong to the set
D_{v^{*}}(V(T))
by (
27
).
Consequently (
25
) holds by Proposition
3
.
∎

The next theorem completely describes the center of distances for ultrametric spaces
(X,d)\in{\bf UT}
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(X,d)\in{\bf UT}
have at least two points, let
D(X)
be the distance set of
(X,d)
, and let
C(X)
be the center of distances of
(X,d)
.
Then
we have either

C(X)=\{0\},

(28)

or

C(X)=\{0,\operatorname{diam}X\}

(29)

and, moreover, the last equality holds if and only if

\operatorname{diam}X\in D(X).

(30)

Proof.

Let us prove the equivalence (
29
)

\Leftrightarrow
(
30
).

Since
C(X)
is a subset of
D(X)
, equality (
29
) implies membership relation (
30
).

Suppose now that we have (
30
). Then Corollary
1
implies

\operatorname{diam}X\in C(X).

Proposition
4
gives us

0\in C(X).

Consequently, the inclusion

C(X)\supseteq\{0,\operatorname{diam}X\}

(31)

is valid.
Since
(X,d)
belongs to the class
{\bf UT}
, there exists a labeled tree
T=T(l)
such that
(X,d)
and
(V(T),d_{l})
are isometric. By Lemma
1
we can also suppose that

D(V(T))=\{\,l(u)\colon u\in V(T)\,\}.

(32)

Then using Lemma
2
we obtain

C(V(T))\subseteq\{0,\operatorname{diam}V(T)\}.

Since
(X,d)
and
(V(T),d_{l})
are isometric, the last inclusion gives the inclusion

C(X)\subseteq\{0,\operatorname{diam}X\}.

(33)

Equality (
29
) follows from (
31
) and (
33
). Thus the
equivalence (
29
)

\Leftrightarrow
(
30
) is valid.

Let us now assume that equality (
29
) does not hold.
Then, as was shown above, the formula

\operatorname{diam}X\notin D(V(T))

(34)

is valid. Let
T=T(l)
be a labeled tree such that
(X,d)

and
(V(T),d_{l})
are isometric and equality (
32
) holds.
Then using Proposition
4
, Lemma
2
and (
34
) we see that

C(V(T))=\{0\}.

(35)

Since
(X,d)
and
(V(T),d_{l})

are isometric, (
28
) follows from (
35
).

The proof is completed.
∎

Remark 4
.

If
(X,d)
contains exactly one point, then we have

\operatorname{diam}X=0

and, consequently, equalities (

) and (

) are equivalent.

Example 2

(The Delhommé-Laflamme-Pouzet-Sauer ultrametric)
.

Let us define a mapping

d^{+}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}

as

d^{+}(p,q):=\begin{cases}\max\{p,q\},&\text{if }p\neq q,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then
d^{+}
is an ultrametric on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
and the inequality

d^{+}(0,p)\leq d(p,q)

holds whenever
0\neq p\neq q
. The last inequality and Theorem 2.1 from
[
16
]
imply the existence of a labeled star graph
S=S(l)
such that
V(S)=\mathbb{R}^{+}
and

The next concept was introduced in

Definition 10
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space.
A set
S\subseteq X
is a centered sphere in

if there exist

c\in S
, the center of
S
, and
r\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, the radius of
S
, such that

S=\{x\in X:d(x,c)=r\}\cup\{c\}.

(36)

If
r=0
, then (
36
) gives the equality
S=\{c\}
. Hence the following proposition holds.

Proposition 5
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. Then the singleton
\{c\}
is a centered sphere in
(X,d)
for every
c\in X
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
(X,d)\in{\bf UT}
have at least two points and let
C(X)
be the center of distances of
(X,d)
.
Then the equality

C(X)=\{0,\operatorname{diam}X\}

(37)

holds if and only if
X
is a centered sphere in
(X,d)
and
\operatorname{diam}X
is the radius of this sphere.

Proof.

Let
X
be a centered sphere in
(X,d)
.
Then, by Definition
10
, there are
c\in X
and
r\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

X=\{x\in X:d(x,c)=r\}\cup\{c\}.

(38)

The last equality and the strong triangle inequality imply

d(x,y)\leq\max\{d(x,c),d(c,y)\}\leq r

for all
x,y\in X
.
Consequently we have

\operatorname{diam}X=r

and

\operatorname{diam}X\in D(X),

where
D(X)
is the distance set of
(X,d)
.
Hence (
37
) follows from Theorem
3
.

Now we must show that (
37
) implies (
38
).
Let us do it.
Since
(X,d)\in\bf UT
holds, there is a labeled tree
T=T(l)
such that
(X,d)
and
(V(T),d_{l})
are isometric.
Consequently, we have the equalities

D(X)=D(V(T)),\quad C(X)=C(V(T))

and can rewrite equality (
37
) as

C(V(T))=\{0,\operatorname{diam}V(T)\}.

(39)

By Lemma
1
we may also suppose that

D(V(T))=\{l(u)\colon u\in V(T)\}.

(40)

It follows from (
39
) that
the membership relation

\operatorname{diam}V(T)\in D(V(T))

holds, and, consequently, by (
40
) there exists
u^{*}\in V(T)
such that

\operatorname{diam}V(T)=l(u^{*})\geq l(u)

(41)

for each
u\in V(T)
.

Using formulas (
14
) and (

holds for each

The last statement implies (

and

where
\Phi(u^{*})
is the image of the point

under some isometry

\Phi:V(T)\to X

of the spaces
(V(T),d_{l})
and

.

The proof is completed.
∎
