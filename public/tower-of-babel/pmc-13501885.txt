Gas Invasion Patterns in Saturated Marine Sediments
Authors: Peng Wang, Lujun Wang, Bin Zhu, Sheng C. Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gas invasion into fluid-saturated marine sediments is relevant to various geophysical and environmental processes, such as the formation of gas hydrate reservoirs and their behavior during depressurization-based gas production. The link between pore-scale mechanisms and macroscopic invasion patterns as well as their governing parameters, especially under high stress and intense flux conditions, remains unclear. We study gas invasion into water-saturated marine sediments using a coupled framework of the discrete element method and pore network modeling. We show that two dimensionless numbers, the stress number Σ and the seepage number D, govern five representative gas invasion patterns of capillary fracturing, capillary invasion, viscous fingering, seepage fracturing, and the transitional morphologies among them. The capillary number C, the stress number Σ, and the seepage number D collectively determine the shear, tensile, or cavity dominated fracturing in marine sediments. The results provide a description of gas migration regime transitions in marine sediments, which can help establish a scientific foundation to explain the development of marine cold seeps in nature and to design gas hydrate exploitation strategies.

Introduction

Marine sediments usually contain biogenic or petrogenic gases in nature, such as methane (CH4), carbon dioxide (CO2), and hydrogen sulfide (H2S). Among these, methane is the most abundant in marine sediments and mainly exists in the pore spaces as dissolved gas, free gas, or gas hydrate. For methane trapped in the hydrate form, which is a solid compound of methane and water molecules at high pressure and low temperature conditions, the global mass of carbon in hydrates ranges from 3 to 455 Gt. − This is comparable to the total carbon content of proven reserves of petroleum and natural gas worldwide. Therefore, oceanic gas hydrate is considered a promising alternative energy resource. Gas hydrate is therefore widely regarded as a substantial, largely untapped energy resource, and understanding how methane is transported within marine sediments is essential to evaluating this resource and to designing production strategies.

A central open question in hydrate reservoir formation is how methane migrates from its source to the accumulation site. Diffusion-driven short migration, , and advection-driven fluid flow, − have been proposed as the primary methane supply pathways that control the distribution and formation rate of gas hydrates. Besides, the complex hydro-mechanical interaction during gas release from hydrate dissociation may lead to drastic changes in sediment deformation and intrinsic permeability. This thereby affects the gas production efficiency during hydrate exploitation. − Many existing studies have focused on the interpretation of in situ exploration data and laboratory tests on field core samples. Pore-scale investigations are therefore expected to provide critical support for elucidating the underlying mechanisms. In particular, understanding the methane transport in marine sediments at the pore scale is essential for understanding the evolution of submarine methane migration pathways during gas hydrate accumulation, as well as providing insight into the efficient and sustainable exploitation of oceanic gas hydrate reservoirs.

In the past two decades, numerous experimental and numerical studies − have investigated fundamental fluid displacement patterns in marine sediments, such as viscous fingering, capillary invasion, and fracture opening, which are affected by factors like pore size, effective stress, and fluid viscosity. − Numerically, the cavity expansion theory based on the Finite Element Method (FEM) , and the Boundary Element Method (BEM) , has been developed to describe the process of a single bubble growth in sediments. The combined Finite-Discrete Element Method (FDEM) , and the Discontinuous Deformation Method (DDM) , mainly focused on capturing the fracture propagation process, but neglected the gas–water interactions. The Pore Network Modeling method (PNM) is proficient at simulating the gas invasion process with fixed channels, but ineffective in characterizing the complex mechanical interactions between fluids and particles. ,

Gas migration and hydrate exploitation in oceanic sediments are large-scale processes ranging from hundreds to thousands of meters, , and inherently involve complex dynamic multiphase flow. The interwoven interactions of gravity, effective stress, pore pressure, capillarity, and gas fluxes result in various gas invasion patterns. Fracturing typically emerges in marine sediments with small particle sizes under low effective stress. ,− However, laboratory experiments have also documented fracturing events in marine sediments during gas hydrate dissociation under conditions of large pore sizes or high effective stress., − as well as in many natural processes such as the formation of deep submarine gas chimneys, − and the vent-fill deposits in volcanic systems. These phenomena occur at stresses or flow flux conditions that exceed the range accessible to current laboratory experiments and numerical simulation studies. The linkage between pore-scale forces and macroscopic gas invasion patterns in sediments under extreme stress and flux conditions that are relevant to production operations, as well as effective models to simulate the coupled processes of gas transport in deformable sediments across scales, remains poorly understood. In addition, fracturing in marine sediments during fluid invasion, under varying boundary conditions, has been attributed to three primary mechanisms: elastic deformation induced tension, , frictional sliding induced shear, or overall fluidization, , and the transition boundary among various mechanisms remains unclear.

In this work, the framework coupling the discrete element method and pore network modeling (PNM) is developed to investigate the gas invasion patterns in water-saturated marine sediments, aiming to unravel the dimensionless parameters governing different invasion patterns. The results also address the ambiguity of the pore-scale fracturing mechanism in granular media during gas invasion, thereby advancing mechanistic understanding of gas migration regimes in marine sediments, particularly during the accumulation of gas hydrate reservoirs, the exploitation of gas hydrate resources, and the evolution of oceanic cold seeps.

When running the gas invasion algorithm, the iteration and development of gas pressure or flux assume the ideal gas law, and all the pore throats located at the gas–water interface are involved in determining the gas migration patterns. If the sum of capillary force and water pressure at a pore throat is lower than the gas pressure, gas will break through the capillary blockage and fill the connected pore by displacing pore water. However, in the situation of a lower effective stress and a higher capillary pressure, the gas phase will push particles away, thereby propagating a fracture before capillary invasion. Calculation Process

Figure illustrates the sequential steps in calculating the process of gas invasion into marine sediments. After setting the physical parameters of particles, the model is consolidated at a certain effective stress to reach the initial static equilibrium. Following, the pore fluid mesh is constructed based on the contact relationships between particles, forming interconnected and overlapped hydro-mechanical coupling networks.

Calculation flowchart.

The gas flux Q, with a constant volumetric rate at standard conditions, was applied at the central position of the marine sediments model to assess the gas invasion process. At the start of each fluid time step t_flow, the gas invasion algorithms are executed and update pore pressure. At the end of gas invasion algorithms, the fluid force in each pore space is exerted on the surrounding particles. The deformation and motion of particles under pore fluid forces and effective stress are then continuously calculated in per mechanical time step t_mech (t_mech must be smaller than t_flow), until the cumulative mechanical time ∑t_mech reaches t_flow. During this period, once a microfracture forms within granular media, the fluid mesh and model parameters are updated, as well as the fracture types are also recorded after the formation of each microfracture. Once ∑t_mech aligns with t_flow, the calculation proceeds to the next fluid time step, until the calculation terminates when real time t reaches the preset maximal calculation time t_max.

Due to the relatively small scale of the model, it is assumed that the drainage process during gas invasion is instantaneous for simplification. The fluid time step t_flow was set as 1 × 10–6 s to improve the stability during the hydro-mechanical coupling process. The mechanical time step t_mech was modified automatically and capped at one-tenth of the fluid time step t_flow, based on the contact force variation and particle velocity to ensure numerical robustness.

Discussions

Effect of Stress and Capillarity

A dimensionless stress number Σ is proposed to normalize the effective stress σ′ by the capillary pressure p c 15 Σ=σ′pc=σ′d5020γ⁡cos⁡θ where γ is the gas–water interfacial tension; θ is the contact angle of the interface meniscus, which is assumed to be θ = 0° (so cos θ = 1) to simplify the analysis; and d 50/20 is assumed to represent the pore size, with d 50 the medium particle size.

Under isotropic or anisotropic stress conditions, the stress number can be defined as. 16a Σiso=σiso′pc=σiso′d5020γ⁡cos⁡θ 16b Σmin=σmin′pc=σmin′d5020γcos⁡θ 16c Σmax=σmax′pc=σmax′d5020γcos⁡θ where σ′iso, σ′min, and σ′max are the isotropic, the minimum, and the maximum effective confining stresses.

Figure shows the variation in gas invasion patterns with the stress number Σ. At Σ < 1, where capillarity exceeded the skeleton stress in the system, gas invasion could mobilize particles and result in fractures (marked in blue). Generated fractures generally propagated along the major principal stress, with localized deviation governed by localized force chains. As the stress number Σ increases, gas displacing water (marked in pink) gradually emerged, coexisting with particle displacement. When Σ > 10, water was displaced by gas without moving the particles. For the capillary dominated invasion regimes with anisotropic stress boundaries, the capillary invasion path is mainly governed by pore characteristics, not by stress obliquity.

Gas invasion patterns with the stress number Σ (Capillary fracturing, transitional regime, and capillary invasion are represented with blue, green, and pink marks).

The stress number Σ proposed here is conceptually consistent with earlier grain-scale and hydrate-morphology studies that identify the balance between effective stress and capillary pressure as the primary control on invasion versus fracturing behavior. Jain and Juanes showed, using a grain-scale DEM model of gas migration in brine-saturated sediment, that grain size is the dominant factor controlling the mode of gas transport, with coarse-grained sediments favoring capillary invasion (Σmin = 320 and Σmax = 600) and fine-grained sediments favoring fracture-dominated migration (Σmin = 0.64 and Σmax = 1.2). A closer analog is found in Dai et al., where a dimensionless parameter Ψ (which is the inverse of the Σ here, Ψ = 1/Σ) balancing effective stress against hydrate-water capillary pressure is defined to distinguish various hydrate morphologies in marine sediments. Nongrain-displacive pore-filling hydrate occurs when Ψ < 10–3, i.e., Σ > 103; hydrate forms in nodules and chunks when Ψ > 1, i.e., Σ < 1; and in between is a mixed mode. These results agree with the gas invasion patterns shown in Figure .

Capillary Fracturing Mechanism

The relative intensity between the flow viscous force and interfacial tension was characterized by the capillary number C. ,, For simplicity, all constant terms are omitted, and the gas velocity is characterized by the gas injection flux Q 17 C=μgQγd50 where μg and Q are the gas viscosity and 2D gas flux (unit: m2/s).

Figure shows the shear and tensile nature of microfractures at different capillary number C and stress number Σ conditions during gas invasion in the capillary fracturing regime. The green short bars represent shear (i.e., particle sliding or rotation) microfractures at the contacts, and the orange ones for tensile (i.e., particle separation perpendicular to the contact) microfractures. ,, The results showed that tensile fracturing dominates at high stress number Σ and low capillary number C; while shear fracturing prevails at low stress number Σ and high capillary number C. At high stress number and low capillary number conditions, i.e., slow gas injection into the marine sediment under high confining stress, tensile microfractures predominantly developed at contacts where gas–water interfaces pass by, as the contact tensile strength is lower than its shear strength. However, at high capillary number and low stress number conditions, i.e., high-flux injection into the marine sediment under low confinement, the gas pressure built up rapidly and exerted high seepage stress onto the granular skeleton. Such high skeleton stress led to the formation of the shearing zone beyond the gas–water interfaces, resulting in predominately shear microfractures.

Tensile and shear microfractures governed by the capillary number C and the stress number Σ. The blue triangles and green squares represent the gas invasion regimes of pure capillary fracturing and coexistence of fracturing and capillary invasion, respectively.

Generally, the capillary fracturing mechanism of shear and tensile dominated fracturing was separated by an approximate boundary of C/Σ = 1 in the C-Σ phase diagram. For gas invasion with high capillary number and low stress number (C/Σ > 1), the gas kinetic energy was primarily dissipated through particle rearrangement in the form of sliding and rotation, leading to a denser packing within the shear failure area. In contrast, for gas invasion exhibits low capillary number and high stress number (C/Σ < 1), the low fluid kinetic energy was efficiently released through the development of tensile microfractures and the elastic deformation of the unfractured matrix. For gas invasion at high stress number, i.e., Σ > 10, the fluid kinetic energy is expected to be released through matrix elastic deformation and gas flow.

It should be noted that while the macroscopic fracture initiation pressures predicted by the model agree well with experimental measurements (Figure b), the microscale attribution of shear- versus tensile-dominated fracturing (Figure ) is inferred directly from the classification of parallel bond failures within the DEM contact model, and has not been independently verified against direct experimental observation. Direct experimental verification of the shear-versus-tensile partitioning, possibly via acoustic emission monitoring, X-ray micro-CT imaging of post-test crack morphology, or particle image velocimetry (PIV) in transparent-soil analog experiments capable of resolving individual grain rotation versus separation, , would strengthen confidence in the mechanistic interpretation as future work.

Effect of Seepage Drag Force

At high gas injection flux, the seepage force onto particles becomes non-negligible. Seepage fracturing could manifest under high effective stress and high fluid flux, when the drag force exerted by fluid flow exceeded the effective interparticle contact force that resists particle displacement. For a sphere particle subjected to fluid flow with low Reynolds number, the drag force F d can be described by 18 Fd=3πμgvd∼μgQ where v is fluid velocity, and d is particle diameter.

The characteristic contact force F c among particles can be represented as F c ∼ σd 2. Thus, a dimensionless seepage number D is proposed here to quantify the relative magnitude of the seepage drag force over the interparticle contact force. 19 D=FdFc=μgQσiso′d502

As shown in Figure , water displacement occurred when the seepage number D < 1000. Seepage fracturing emerged when D > ∼1000, resulting in cavities around the gas injection point, regardless of the effective stress number (e.g., even at high stress number Σ = 80 condition). This also explains from a microlevel perspective that gas cavity structures can form in marine sediments of low gas-entry pressure (i.e., large pore sizes) and at high effective stress. This regime is mainly induced by localized pressurization and sediment softening, which arise from limited fluid seepage and high gas kinetic energy. These findings are consistent with the existing poro-elasto-viscoplastic-dynamic constitutive model of sediment, and concurrently bridge pore-scale phenomena to macroscopic geomechanical responses during fast gas injection. Specifically, the localized gas pressurization and particle fluidization dynamically alter the sediment stress state, leading to sudden stiffness degradation and cumulative plastic deformation of the sediments.

Gas invasion transitions from water displacement to seepage fracturing with increasing seepage number D.

Besides, cohesion can impact the occurrence of seepage fracturing as well. Specifically, a higher cohesion is expected to elevate the threshold value of the seepage number D required for the seepage fracturing regime. Nevertheless, a reliable quantification of cohesion is difficult to achieve with the current numerical results, because a constant cohesion is set for the typical fine-grained marine sediments in this study. Further work is still needed and essential to quantify this effect.

Gas Invasion Regimes in D-Σ Phase Diagram

As summarized in Figure , the patterns of gas invasion in fluid-saturated marine sediments in the D-Σ space encompass capillary fracturing, water displacement, seepage fracturing, and the transition zone among them. The water displacement contains both capillary invasion and viscous fingering, and the differences between the two have been well discussed in previous Hele-Shaw cell experiments and numerical studies. ,,,

Phase diagram of gas invading water-saturated marine sediment in the D-Σ space: regimes of capillary fracturing, seepage fracturing, water displacement, and the transitional zone among them. The seepage number D in the phase diagram has been scaled down by 3 orders of magnitude (*10–3) to have the two axes intersect at unity.

In the capillary fracturing regime (e.g., Σ < 0.1), the particle contact force fell below the capillary entry force. This allowed gas–water interfaces to displace particles and form fractures. The boundary of the capillary fracturing region was mainly determined by the minimal stress number for nonisotropic stress boundaries. Under high stress number Σ and low seepage number D, i.e., the water displacement regime, the fluids of gas and water will break through the capillary blockage of pore throat before displacing the surrounding particles, so that the particle skeleton remained stable and the pore water was continuously drained by gas through the voids. Previous work has also widely reported the transition from capillary invasion to fracturing with increasing grain size and sediment softness, ,, which is consistent with the pattern transition governed by the stress number Σ and the seepage number D.

Even at a high stress number (e.g., Σ > 10), the high seepage number D > 1000 could still induce microfractures by mobilizing particles, leading to seepage fractures. With increasing D values, the gas cavity formed around the gas injection point could expand outward. This phenomenon aligns well with laboratory results observed during hydrate dissociation in sandy sediments or high-stress conditions, − and provides a quantitative basis for evaluating gas invasion in seepage-fracturing regimes. Among the three distinguished regimes in the D-Σ phase diagram is the transitional zone, where fracturing and capillary invasion coexist, owing to the nonuniform distributions of force chains and pore capillarity. In 3D granular media with complex geophysical conditions, gas invasion beyond the 2D plane increases both the area and spatial tortuosity of the gas–water interface. This enlarged interface broadens the distributions of throat capillary pressures and interparticle contact forces at the gas–water front. Consequently, a wider transitional zone between capillary fraction and water capillary invasion is expected, and the actual deviation of this regime is associated with the difference in capillarity and stresses at different positions within the sediments.

Model Limitations

Admittedly, this study faces several limitations that can be improved in future work.

The present framework employs a 2D DEM-PNM formulation, with the 2D porosity (0.15) mapped to an equivalent 3D porosity (0.365, Table ) following established scaling relations. While this approach is computationally efficient and captures the essential coupling between particle mechanics and pore-scale fluid transport, a 2D granular assembly necessarily constrains fluid pathways to a single plane and cannot represent the full three-dimensional tortuosity of a real granular pore network. In 3D, gas invading a constricted or high-capillary-entry-pressure region can often circumvent it by migrating around the obstruction in the out-of-plane direction, whereas in 2D the same gas front has no such escape route and must either build sufficient pressure to breach the local capillary or mechanical threshold or divert entirely along the observable plane. This constraint is likely to sharpen the apparent boundaries between invasion regimes in the Σ, D, and C phase diagrams. In other words, phase transitions would be more gradational and diffuse in 3D, owing to the additional degrees of freedom for the gas front to find a locally favorable path, rather than abrupt phase boundaries in the present 2D results. The reported threshold values, e.g., Σ < 1, D > 1000, and C/Σ = 1, should therefore be interpreted as representative of the relative ordering and scaling of the governing mechanisms rather than as precise, quantitatively transferable thresholds for 3D sediments.

The model also assumes instantaneous pressure equilibration at the local scale of a newly formed fracture connection. The seepage flow between distinct pore bodies remains fully time-resolved via the Hagen–Poiseuille and compressibility relations across the simulation. This local simplification is only valid when the pore-scale hydraulic diffusion time is much shorter than the fluid time step. This assumption should not be extended uncritically to low-permeability clayey sediments at larger (field or reservoir) length scales. For permeabilities characteristic of marine clays, the equivalent diffusion time at the meter scale grows to the order of days to years, meaning transient pore-pressure dissipation and time-dependent consolidation would become rate-limiting and could not be neglected. The present framework is therefore best interpreted as applicable to the pore- to submillimeter length scales and the permeability range for silty and sandy sediments. Extending it to slower-draining, lower-permeability, or larger-scale systems would require explicitly resolving the coupled consolidation time scale.

The capillary pressure formulation is derived from two idealized throat geometries: three particles arranged in a triangle and four particles arranged in a rectangle, with the effective particle radius around each throat taken as the arithmetic mean of the two adjacent particle radii. This approach captures the leading-order dependence of capillary entry pressure on local grain size but homogenizes the pore throat size distribution relative to a real, disordered granular packing, where throat apertures and coordination numbers vary considerably even among pores of similar host-particle size. Because the transition between capillary-fingering and viscous-fingering behavior in porous media is known to be highly sensitive to the breadth and correlation structure of the underlying pore-size distribution, a narrower effective throat-size distribution in the present model may understate the roughness and branching complexity of invasion fronts observed in real sediments, and could shift or sharpen the phase boundaries reported in Figures –. In particular, the transitional zone between capillary invasion and viscous fingering may be wider in real, more heterogeneous sediments than predicted here, since a broader throat-size distribution provides more opportunities for the invading gas to locally bypass high-entry-pressure throats.

In addition, the gas invasion regimes reported in this work are evaluated under boundary conditions in which the vertical and horizontal confining stresses are held constant throughout each simulation. This treats the confining stress as a static classification parameter rather than as a state variable that itself evolves in response to the invading gas. In real reservoir geomechanics, however, localized gas pressurization during invasion or production dynamically perturbs the local effective stress field, potentially driving the sediment along a stress path that approaches or crosses its failure envelope independently of the externally imposed boundary stress, and giving rise to macroscopic phenomena such as shear dilation, compaction banding, or, at larger scale, seabed instability. The present model does not resolve this feedback between local gas pressurization and the evolving global stress state, and the reported Σ-D-C phase diagram should therefore be understood as describing invasion pattern selection under a prescribed, quasi-static stress state, rather than the fully coupled dynamic stress path that would develop during sustained gas production.

Conclusions

To summarize, this letter developed a hydro-mechanical coupled numerical framework combining the discrete element method with the pore network modeling to investigate gas invasion into water-saturated marine sediments. The results show that

Under different stress and gas injection conditions, gas invasion patterns could be categorized as capillary fracturing, capillary invasion, viscous fingering, seepage fracturing, and transitional morphologies. Two dimensionless parameters of stress number Σ and seepage number D were proposed to govern the gas invasion regimes. The stress number Σ represents the relationship between capillarity and effective stress of marine sediments. The seepage number D quantifies the relative magnitude of the seepage drag force over the interparticle contact force.

Fractures generated during gas invasion into saturated marine sediments can be shear-, tensile-, or cavity-dominated, governed by the dimensionless capillary number C, stress number Σ, and seepage number D. Shear-dominated fracturing emerged at high capillary number and low stress number (i.e., C/Σ > 1). While the tensile-dominated fracturing occurred at low capillary number and high stress number (C/Σ < 1). Under a high stress number of Σ > 10, cavity-dominated fracturing appeared with the seepage number D > 1000.

Within the D-Σ space, gas invasion into fluid-saturated marine sediments shows distinct regimes of capillary fracturing, water displacement, seepage fracturing, and the transitional regions among them. The linkage between pore-scale forces and macroscopic gas invasion patterns, as well as the effective model developed to simulate the intricate gas-fluid–solid interactions, offers insight into a variety of scientific and industrial processes pertinent to mixed fluid and gas transport in discrete marine sediments.

These findings are subject to several limitations related to dimensionality, instantaneous drainage assumption, relatively homogenized pore throat sizes, static confining stress and the effective stress path, and micromechanical validation of fracture mode, which can be a direction for future work on this topic.
