Bleomycin sclerotherapy for venous vascular malformations of the tongue
Authors: Noa Agid, Ze'ev Itsekzon, Eef J Hendriks, Karel Terbrugge, Ronit Agid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVenous vascular malformations (VVMs) are congenital vascular anomalies, often impacting quality of life due to discomfort, swelling, and functional impairments. This study evaluates the efficacy of bleomycin sclerotherapy as a treatment for symptomatic VVMs of the tongue.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective review of all patients treated with this method in our institution between 2004 and 2024. Sclerotherapy procedures were performed under general anesthesia in the neuroangiography suite with fluoroscopic guidance using a negative subtraction technique. Outcomes were assessed through subjective patient reports and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) where available.ResultsIn total, 140 percutaneous bleomycin sclerotherapy procedures were performed on 36 patients (aged 19–80) with tongue VVMs. On average, patients required 2.06±1.8 treatment sessions and 2.29±1.4 procedures per session. The average interval between treatment sessions was 28.6±19.4 months. Per procedure, the total units of bleomycin injected was 11.2±3.9 mg/mL (2–15 mg/mL). Results revealed that 93.8% of procedures yielded subjective symptom improvement. Follow-up imaging was available in 14 patients and confirmed lesion reduction in nine (64.3%). Post-procedural complications were minimal, with transient swelling as the most common.ConclusionPercutaneous bleomycin sclerotherapy presents an effective, well-tolerated treatment for tongue VVMs, offering significant symptom relief. To our knowledge, this study represents the largest retrospective series of bleomycin sclerotherapy for tongue VVMs to date. Our findings support bleomycin sclerotherapy as a valuable option for managing VVMs in this challenging anatomical site.Keywords: Vascular Malformation, Lesion, Intervention

Introduction and background

Venous vascular malformations (VVMs) are a type of congenital vascular anomaly resulting from abnormal vein development. These malformations may either be completely isolated from or have connections with normal venous structures. VVMs are characterized by irregularly formed vascular channels lined by flattened endothelial cells, and a propensity for slow venous blood flow.1 2

VVMs represent the most prevalent category of vascular anomalies and can occur throughout the body, although they are most frequently observed in the head and neck regions. Although these lesions are classified as congenital, they may become clinically apparent at any stage of life. Clinically, VVMs can present as either superficial cutaneous lesions with a bruised appearance or as palpable non-pulsatile subcutaneous masses. When situated near the skin, these malformations often exhibit a bluish coloration. Patients with VVMs may experience discomfort, swelling, localized pain, and intermittent bleeding at the site of the lesion. These malformations frequently pose cosmetic concerns.1 2Optional treatment methods for VVMs include surgical resection, laser therapy, and intralesional injection of sclerosing agents.

In this study we describe our experience with the treatment of VVMs of the tongue using percutaneous sclerotherapy with bleomycin. The aim of our study was to evaluate the subjective experiences and satisfaction of patients undergoing this treatment. Our focus on tongue VVMs is particularly significant because tongue VVMs represent a relatively rare subgroup within head and neck vascular malformations, with limited literature specifically addressing this anatomical location. In addition, prior experience with alcohol sclerotherapy for tongue lesions at our institution resulted in a high incidence of complications, including severe swelling requiring emergent surgical intervention. By focusing on bleomycin sclerotherapy, we aim to demonstrate a safer option for this challenging anatomical site.

Methods

A retrospective review was conducted of clinical charts and procedural data of all consecutive patients who underwent elective bleomycin sclerotherapy at our institution for symptomatic VVMs of the tongue between 2004 and 2024. Patients with deep posterior base-of-tongue lesions were not included in this study. Our study received approval from our institutional ethical review board.

Treatment scheduling

Each patient’s treatment course consisted of one or more treatment sessions. A treatment session comprised 1–6 sclerotherapy procedures, spaced 4–6 weeks apart. This 4–6 week interval between procedures allows the bleomycin to have its sclerosing effect on the VVM and provides the tongue tissue adequate time to recover from the injection and any associated swelling. Additional procedures within a session were added based on treatment response and patient preference. Additional treatment sessions were initiated by the patient if symptoms recurred. Typically, treatment sessions were spaced a few years apart.

Sclerotherapy technique

All procedures were performed under fluoroscopic guidance in a sterile neuroangiography suite. Before the procedure, the patient’s face was draped, but the tongue itself was not prepped with any special solutions. To prevent skin discoloration, a known complication of bleomycin treatment, no strong adhesive was used on the patient’s skin to keep any lines in place. Instead, the team used a very delicate paper-based tape or mesh to stabilize intravenous lines and the intubation tube. In addition, ECG leads were connected to the patient’s skin using gel rather than stickers.

Sclerotherapy treatments for tongue VVMs were performed under general anesthesia provided by the anesthesia team (in contrast to superficial head and neck VVMs which are treated under conscious sedation). All patients underwent orotracheal intubation for airway management. The endotracheal tube was routinely positioned at the corner of the mouth by the anesthesiologist to facilitate unobstructed access to the tongue lesion during the procedure.

To access the lesion, metal or single-use plastic sponge forceps (9.5”) were used to pull the tongue out as far as required for optimal needle access. This approach allows for good visualization and manipulation of the lesion. The lesion was directly punctured without ultrasound guidance, targeting the most symptomatic portion of the VVM as directed by the patient. This was usually the most bulky area of the lesion seen on the surface of the tongue.

Bleomycin administration was conducted via percutaneous injections utilizing one or more 22-gauge intravenous access Angiocathneedles. As a first step, correct needle placement was confirmed by observing slow venous blood return into the needle hub. This was followed by a venogram, with a small volume of contrast injection of 1 mL or less to further confirm appropriate needle tip placement within the VVM and VVM diagnosis. The resulting venogram was recorded using fluoroscopy. Bleomycin (1 mg/mL) was then injected into the same needle under negative subtraction fluoroscopy, to a maximum of 15 mg/mL per procedure. Employing the negative subtraction technique under fluoroscopy allows for the visualization of the sclerosant distribution without the need for diluting bleomycin with additional contrast agent3 (figure 1).

Notably, no patients in this cohort were treated using bleomycin mixed with albumin foam. During the study period, bleomycin sclerotherapy was the sole treatment modality utilized for the patients in our cohort. While it is possible that some of the patients may have undergone surgical intervention or other treatments in the past, none of the patients underwent additional treatment modalities such as laser therapy, alcohol sclerotherapy, sodium tetradecyl sulfate, or other medications concurrently with their bleomycin treatment at our institution in this study period.

Post-procedural protocol

Post-procedural monitoring was conducted for a total of 4 hours. Patients initially recovered in the Post Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) for 2 hours under anesthesiologist observation, as all patients were intubated for this treatment. Patients were then transferred to the Medical Day Unit (MIDU) for an additional 2 hours of recovery.

Following tongue sclerotherapy, patients were equipped with a rescue prescription of steroids (8–12 mg/d dexamethasone for 4 days), nystatin suspension (10 000 Units, three times a day (TID) for 1 week), and oral clindamycin antibiotic treatment (500 mg, four times a day (QID) for 1 week). Patients were instructed to take steroids only in case of post-procedural tongue swelling and pain, and antibiotics and nystatin only if they developed throat pain and oral thrush. Patients were instructed to contact the team if the rescue medications did not relieve post-procedural symptoms.

Post-procedural complications were identified through patient-reported concerns of symptoms that were not alleviated by the provided rescue prescriptions. These were communicated via email, phone, or during follow-up clinic visits. All patients were provided with the direct contact information of the clinical team to ensure prompt communication of any issues. If patients experienced persistent issues, they were scheduled for in-person evaluation in the clinic.

Response to treatment

Treatment response was analyzed both subjectively and objectively. The subjective response was categorized according to patients’ reported outcomes into three groups: no response, mild response (defined as less than 50% symptom reduction), and significant response (defined as more than 50% symptom reduction). Patient feedback was documented in patient files, procedure reports, and during clinical follow-ups, where patients were asked to rate improvements based on lesion size reduction (eg, feeling more room in the mouth, less frequent biting of the lesion, reduced swelling, etc.) and alleviation of associated symptoms (eg, reduced intermittent bleeding, reduced pain, less difficulty chewing, and improved speech).

Objective response to treatment was assessed by comparing pre- and post-sclerotherapy axial, coronal, and sagittal T2 fat-saturated magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans. These MRI scans were evaluated by head and neck radiologists who were not part of the interventional team or the treatment sessions. We defined an objective reduction in lesion size as explicitly stated in the radiologists’ reports. Pre-treatment MRI was performed on all patients to confirm the VVM diagnosis. However, post-treatment MRIs were not routinely conducted.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive and statistical analyses were performed on the raw data, with findings presented quantitatively to elucidate the efficacy of bleomycin sclerotherapy in this patient population. Simple descriptive statistics were employed to quantify and present the collected data. These included measures of central tendency (mean and median), measures of variability (range and standard deviation (SD)), and percentages for categorical data.

Results

A total of 36 tongue VVM patients, with a mean age of 43.8±16.5 (19–80 years) were treated by bleomycin sclerotherapy in our center between 2004 and 2024. Slightly more than half of the patients were female (54.05%); 26 (72.2%) patients in our cohort had a focal lesion, while 10 (27.8%) had a diffuse lesion involving a majority of the tongue tissue. On average, focal lesions were 3.5±0.7 mm in size. None of the patients suffered from metameric vascular syndrome.

Discussion

VVMs of the tongue are relatively rare entities, which can potentially cause significant functional disability. This article focuses on our experience using bleomycin sclerotherapy for VVMs of the tongue, its clinical effect, safety profile, and long-term durability. Our study included all patients treated with bleomycin since the beginning of its use at our institution following the first description of this particular treatment method by Muir et al.4

Various treatment options exist for VVMs of the head and neck. Surgical resection is one such method, considered when the malformation is relatively small, well-defined, and easily accessible.5 However, this approach may present challenges, including the risk of intraprocedural bleeding, scarring, and the potential for damage to adjacent tissues or nerves.6 For VVMs of the tongue, surgical resection is typically reserved for cases of extreme tongue enlargement. Surgical excision of lesions infiltrating the tongue may, however, lead to excessive tongue volume reduction and functional impairment.7

A less invasive treatment modality for VVMs is endovenous diode laser photocoagulation. This technique involves the use of diode lasers that emit light onto the lesion, producing a thermal effect. This induces the sclerosis and subsequent closure of the malformation’s vessels.8 At our institution, laser therapy is reserved for superficial skin lesions of the head and neck and for deep oropharyngeal lesions that narrow the airway and cause respiratory difficulty. Such treatment of deep oropharyngeal lesions typically requires preventative tracheostomy due to the risk of post-procedural swelling and airway compromise. In our series, only patients with lesions involving the tongue itself were included, excluding those with base-of-tongue or deep oropharyngeal lesions. Consequently, none of the patients in our cohort received laser treatment for their tongue VVMs.

Sclerotherapy is another therapeutic approach for head and neck VVMs that involves the intralesional injection of a sclerosant solution, either in liquid or foamed form.9 This induces endothelial damage and inflammation within the abnormal vessels, leading to their occlusion. This process can result in a reduction of the malformation’s size and alleviation of associated symptoms.10 For years, ethanol was the main sclerosant utilized in sclerotherapy. However, its use has been largely discontinued due to significant drawbacks such as extreme post-procedural swelling and greater patient discomfort, pain during injection requiring general anesthesia, and a higher risk of complications, including potential skin necrosis, facial nerve palsy, and more.9

Today, bleomycin, an anti-tumor agent, is frequently utilized as a sclerosant in the treatment of venous malformations of the head and neck. It is noted for its comparatively low incidence of complications.11 12 Bleomycin causes minimal pain and less swelling on injection, which allows for its use in the treatment of VVMs in deep and sensitive locations. VVMs of the tongue especially benefit from these propensities of bleomycin, as extreme swelling of the tongue has the potential to affect breathing and swallowing.

In our study, bleomycin was used with a maximum dose of 15 mg/mL per procedure. This dose was established to minimize the risk of pulmonary fibrosis and pneumonitis, the most serious potential complications associated with bleomycin use. We followed the recommendation of Muir et al,4 as well as the recommendation of our institution’s pharmacists and oncologists. Studies on germ-cell cancer treatment have shown that bleomycin doses below 270 mg are considered safe for patients.13 Given that patients often require multiple procedures and may have VVMs in different locations, this dosage limit ensures patients remain well below the cumulative dose threshold associated with increased risk, even with multiple repeated procedures.

We evaluated treatment effectiveness using subjective and objective measures. In our study, a majority of the patients (92.4%) indicated a subjective response to bleomycin sclerotherapy treatment. In patients with available post-procedural imaging, 64.3% showed a reduction in lesion size on MRI. This discrepancy between subjective and objective satisfaction rates is not surprising. Objective lesion size reduction on imaging has already been shown to not always correlate with patient-reported satisfaction following sclerotherapy treatment.10 Despite unchanged imaging results, the improvement in symptoms associated with the malformation may be attributed to sclerotherapy inducing subtle flattening of the lesion surface and reducing the frequency of intermittent bleeding or swelling episodes, thereby contributing to patient satisfaction. Accordingly, our study focuses on the subjective experiences and satisfaction of patients undergoing bleomycin sclerotherapy for symptomatic VVMs of the tongue.

The efficacy of percutaneous bleomycin sclerotherapy for tongue VVMs has been investigated in a limited number of studies. Most existing literature consists of institutional studies of head and neck VVMs that have included tongue VVMs within a broader analysis of vascular malformations of various anatomical locations. Jan et al,14 reported on bleomycin sclerotherapy treatment in 30 patients with maxillofacial VVMs, including those of the tongue, but did not provide comprehensive tongue-specific response data. Similarly, Arafat et al,15 examined 15 pediatric patients with maxillofacial VVMs, of which only two had tongue involvement. Both tongue VVM patients in this study experienced a >90% lesion reduction after treatment with bleomycin, although the sample size limits generalizability. Bhardwaj et al,16 presented a larger cohort of tongue VVM patients, with 16 out of 55 total patients having lingual lesions. They reported that 15 of these 16 patients achieved >50% reduction in lesion size post-bleomycin sclerotherapy, with one patient showing <20% improvement. However, this study, like others, did not focus exclusively on tongue VVMs or provide detailed subjective outcome measures.

A common limitation across these studies is the lack of comprehensive reporting on subjective outcomes specific to tongue VVMs. Additionally, the sample sizes for tongue lesions are generally small, ranging from 2 to 16 patients in the aforementioned studies. This paucity of data highlights the need for more focused research on bleomycin sclerotherapy for tongue VVMs, with larger sample sizes and more detailed reporting of both objective and subjective outcomes and complications specific to this location.

The largest studies on sclerotherapy treatment for tongue VVMs originate from China. However, these studies use a sclerosant called pingyangmycin (PYM), also known as bleomycin A5. While structurally similar to the bleomycin formulation (bleomycin A2 and B2 mixture) employed in our study, PYM is a distinct compound. Both agents are antineoplastic compounds with similar signaling pathways and structural similarities.17 18 Yuan & Wang19 conducted a comparative study evaluating the efficacy of electrochemical therapy (ECT) combined with PYM vs PYM alone for tongue VVMs. Their results for 30 patients who underwent PYM monotherapy demonstrated excellent therapeutic outcomes based on hemisphere (objective) measurements, with 77% of patients exhibiting a >50% lesion size reduction. While these outcomes appear superior to those observed in our study it is important to note the difference in sclerosing agents used.

The documentation of post-procedural complications varies across studies, making direct comparisons challenging. For example, Arafat et al noted postoperative swelling lasting 3–5 days in all patients but did not classify this as a complication per se. In our study, swelling was only documented as a complication if it was not controlled by the provided rescue prescription of steroids. Yuan & Wang reported that postoperative symptoms such as pain, swelling, and fever occurred “often” but resolved within 1 week, without providing specific quantification.

Despite variations in reporting methods and specific agents used, a consistent finding across previous studies and our own is the generally low rate of complications associated with bleomycin and PYM sclerotherapy treatment for tongue VVMs. This trend supports the overall safety profile of this treatment modality.

On average, patients in our cohort required approximately two sclerotherapy sessions, with an average of two procedures per session. The avarage interval between treatment sessions was nearly 30 months, which is relatively significant. This suggests that, while bleomycin sclerotherapy does not provide a definitive cure for the malformation, the durability of this treatment is commendably effective.

Our study contributes to the limited body of literature on percutaneous bleomycin sclerotherapy treatment for tongue VVMs. By providing data on both objective and subjective outcomes, we offer insight into the efficacy of this treatment modality for a relatively rare anatomical location. Our cohort of 36 patients represents a relatively large sample size considering the rarity of tongue VVMs, enhancing the reliability and generalizability of our findings.

Our results demonstrate promising outcomes for bleomycin sclerotherapy in the treatment of tongue VVMs. When objective responses were available, we usually observed a reduction in lesion size. More importantly, the subjective responses from patients were overwhelmingly positive, indicating high levels of satisfaction with the treatment outcomes.

Limitations

Although this study represents one of the largest series in the literature, our sample size of 36 patients remains relatively small. We also acknowledge that subjective outcome measures to treatment may be considered less accurate compared with objective detailed MRI measurements. However, previous studies have demonstrated that objective lesion size reduction on MRI does not always correlate with patient satisfaction and perceived clinical improvement following sclerotherapy treatment.10

Conclusion

These findings suggest that bleomycin sclerotherapy is an effective and well-tolerated treatment option for patients with tongue VVMs. While further research with larger sample sizes would be beneficial to confirm these results, our study provides a strong foundation for the continued use of bleomycin sclerotherapy in the management of tongue venous vascular malformations.
