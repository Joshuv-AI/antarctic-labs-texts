Perfect Matchings and Popularity in the Many-to-Many Setting Thanks: This result appeared in FSTTCS 2023 [ 29 ] . This version gives a simple proof that corrects a technical gap in the proof for the many-to-many setting in the conference version.
Telikepalli Kavitha, Kazuhisa Makino

Abstract
We consider a matching problem in a bipartite graph G G where every vertex has a capacity and a strict preference order on its neighbors. Furthermore, there is a cost function on the edge set. We assume G G admits a perfect matching, i.e., one that fully matches all vertices. It is only perfect matchings that are feasible for us and we are interested in those perfect matchings that are popular within the set of perfect matchings. It is known that such matchings (called popular perfect matchings) always exist and can be efficiently computed. What we seek here is not any popular perfect matching, but a min-cost one. We show a polynomial-time algorithm for finding such a matching; this is via a characterization of popular perfect matchings in G G in terms of stable matchings in a colorful auxiliary instance. This is a generalization of such a characterization that was known in the one-to-one setting.

For the problem of selecting a

A well-studied relaxation of stability that offers a meaningful and tractable solution to the problem of finding a “best perfect matching”

Popularity.

Popularity is based on voting by vertices on matchings.
In the one-to-one setting, the preferences of a vertex over its neighbors extend naturally to preferences
over matchings—so every vertex orders matchings in the order of its partners in these matchings.
Popular matchings are weak
Condorcet winners

[
13
,
32
]
in this voting instance where vertices are voters and matchings are candidates.
In other words, a popular matching
M
does not lose a head-to-head election against any matching
N

where each vertex
v
either casts a vote for the matching in
\{M,N\}
that it prefers or
v
abstains from voting if its assignment is the same in

M
and
N
.

For any vertex
v
and its neighbors

, let
\mathsf{vote}_{v}(u,u^{\prime})
be

u
versus
u^{\prime}
.
More precisely,
\mathsf{vote}_{v}(u,u^{\prime})=1
if

prefers

to

, it is

if

prefers
u^{\prime}
to

, and it is 0 otherwise (so

).
Thus in the one-to-one setting, every vertex
v
casts its vote which is
\mathsf{vote}_{v}(u,u^{\prime})\in\{0,\pm 1\}
, where
M(v)=\{u\}
and
N(v)=\{u^{\prime}\}
,
for
M
versus
N
in their head-to-head election.

Recall that we are in the many-to-many setting, i.e., vertices have capacities. So we need to specify how a vertex votes over different
subsets of its neighbors. Thus we need to compare two subsets
M(v)
and
N(v)
of
\mathsf{Nbr}(v)
for any vertex
v
, where
\mathsf{Nbr}(v)
is the set
of neighbors of

. We will follow the method from
[

]

\mathsf{cap}(v)

as follows:

•

a bijection

S^{\prime}=S\setminus T

;

2

2

If the two sets are not of equal size, then dummy vertices that are less preferred to all non-dummy vertices are added to the smaller set.

•

every neighbor

is compared with
\psi(u)\in T^{\prime}
;

•

the number of wins minus the number of losses is
v
’s vote for
S
versus
T
.

The bijection
\psi

S^{\prime}
to

that is chosen will be the one that

.
More formally, the vote of

as follows where

\mathsf{vote}_{v}(S,T)=\min_{\sigma\in\Pi[k]}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{vote}_{v}(S^{\prime}[i],T^{\prime}[\sigma(i)]).

(1)

Here

is the

S^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime}[\sigma(i)]
is the
\sigma(i)
-th ranked resident in
T^{\prime}
.
Consider the following example from
[
7
]

\mathsf{Nbr}(v)=\{u_{1},u_{2},u_{3},u_{4},u_{5},u_{6}\}
and

’s preference order is

u_{1}\succ u_{2}\succ u_{3}\succ u_{4}\succ u_{5}\succ u_{6}
.
Let

. So
S^{\prime}=S
and
T^{\prime}=T
.
The bijection
\psi:S^{\prime}\rightarrow T^{\prime}
is
\psi(u_{1})=u_{6}

\psi(u_{3})=u_{2}
, and
\psi(u_{5})=u_{4}

with

. This results in
\mathsf{vote}_{v}(S,T)=\mathsf{vote}_{v}(u_{1},u_{6})+\mathsf{vote}_{v}(u_{3},u_{2})+\mathsf{vote}_{v}(u_{5},u_{4})=1-1-1=-1
.
Similarly,
\mathsf{vote}_{v}(T,S)=\mathsf{vote}_{v}(u_{2},u_{1})+\mathsf{vote}_{v}(u_{4},u_{3})+\mathsf{vote}_{v}(u_{6},u_{5})=-3
.

For any pair of matchings
M,N
and vertex
v
, let
\mathsf{vote}_{v}(M,N)=\mathsf{vote}_{v}(S,T)
where

.
So
\mathsf{vote}_{v}(M,N)
counts the number of votes by

versus

when the two sets
M(v)\setminus N(v)
and
N(v)\setminus M(v)
are compared
in the order that is most adversarial or
negative
for

.
The two matchings
M
and

are compared using
\Delta(M,N)=\sum_{v\in A\cup B}\mathsf{vote}_{v}(M(v),N(v))
.
We will say matching
M
is more popular than matching

\Delta(M,N)>0
.

Definition 2

A matching
M
is popular if
\Delta(M,N)\geq 0
for all matchings
N
in
G
.

Thus
M
is popular if there is no matching more popular than
M
.
Recall our assumption that the set of feasible solutions is the set of
perfect matchings.
Hence the matchings of interest to us are
popular perfect matchings
, defined below.

Definition 3

A perfect matching
M
is a popular perfect matching if
\Delta(M,N)\geq 0
for all perfect matchings
N
in
G
.

So a popular perfect matching need not be popular—it may lose to a smaller size matching; nevertheless, it never loses to a
perfect matching. Since weak Condorcet winners do not always exist, it is not a priori clear if a popular perfect matching always exists.
It was shown in
[

]
that there always exists a popular perfect matching in the one-to-one setting;

3

3

It was shown in
[

]
that

moreover, such a matching can be computed in polynomial time.

The following problem in the many-to-many setting was considered in
[
33
]
: vertices have capacity lower bounds and feasible matchings are those
that satisfy these lower bounds.
It was shown that popular feasible matchings always exist and can be computed in polynomial time. Popular perfect matchings are a special case of this
problem where every vertex has to be fully matched, i.e., every vertex
v
has a lower bound of
\mathsf{cap}(v)
. Thus popular perfect matchings always exist in the
many-to-many setting and can be computed in polynomial time.

Our problem.

We assume there is a function
\mathsf{cost}:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which is part of the
input. Hence the cost of any matching
M
is
\sum_{e\in M}\mathsf{cost}(e)
.
There might be exponentially many popular perfect matchings in
G
, hence we would like to find an
min-cost
one, i.e.,
a popular perfect matching whose cost is least among all popular perfect matchings.
Solving the min-cost popular perfect matching problem efficiently implies efficient
algorithms for several desirable popular perfect matching problems such as finding one with the highest utility
when every edge has an associated utility or one with forced/forbidden edges
or an egalitarian one. In the conference management system application discussed earlier, we would like to find a popular
perfect matching that matches as many PC members as possible along top ranked edges, subject to that, as many PC members
as possible along second ranked edges, and so on
[
22
]
. Such a popular perfect matching is
a min-cost popular perfect matching for an appropriate cost function.

A polynomial-time algorithm for the min-cost popular perfect matching problem in the one-to-one setting was shown in
[
28
]
.
The conference version of our paper
[
29
]
gave a polynomial-time algorithm for this problem in the
many-to-one
setting (i.e.,
\mathsf{cap}(a)=1
for
all
a\in A
) by reducing it to the min-cost popular perfect matching
problem in the one-to-one setting and using the polynomial-time algorithm for computing a min-cost popular perfect matching in the one-to-one setting
[
28
]
.

The hospitals/residents setting.

The many-to-one setting is usually referred to as the hospitals/residents setting.
The reduction in
[
29
]
from the hospitals/residents setting to the one-to-one setting is via the
cloned
instance
G^{\prime}=(A\cup B^{\prime},E^{\prime})
corresponding to the
given hospitals/residents instance
G=(A\cup B,E)
. Every
b\in B
is replaced by
\mathsf{cap}(b)
many clones
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots
in
B^{\prime}
and the preference order of
every
b_{i}
is the same as the preference order of
b
. Furthermore, every
a\in A
that is a neighbor of
b
in
G
replaces the occurrence of

in its
preference order with
b_{1}\succ\cdots\succ b_{\mathsf{cap}(b)}
.

There is a natural map
f
from the set of popular perfect matchings in the one-to-one instance

to the set of perfect
matchings in the original instance

, where for any popular perfect matching
M^{\prime}
in

, the many-to-one matching
f(M^{\prime})
is
obtained by replacing each edge
(a,b_{i})
in

with the original edge
(a,b)
. It can be shown that

is a popular perfect matching in
G
.
So we have
f:\{
popular perfect matchings in
G^{\prime}\}\rightarrow

\{
popular perfect matchings in

.
It was shown in
[
29
]
that the mapping

is surjective.
Hence solving the min-cost popular perfect matching problem in
G^{\prime}
solves the min-cost popular perfect matching problem in

.
Though it was claimed in
[
29
]
that the same approach can be generalized to the many-to-many setting, unfortunately
it does not extend to the many-to-many setting.

The many-to-many setting.

Consider
G=(A\cup B,E)
where
A=\{a,a^{\prime}\}
and
B=\{b,b^{\prime}\}
and every vertex has capacity 2.
Vertex preferences are as follows:
a\colon b\succ b^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime}\colon b^{\prime}\succ b
along with

b\colon a\succ a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}\colon a^{\prime}\succ a
.

There is only one perfect matching
M=\{(a,b),(a,b^{\prime}),(a^{\prime},b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\}
in
G
,
hence
M
is a popular perfect matching. But
M
cannot be realized as a popular perfect matching in
the corresponding one-to-one instance
G^{\prime}
. The vertex set of
G^{\prime}=(A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime},E^{\prime})
is given by

A^{\prime}=\{a_{1},a_{2},a^{\prime}_{1},a^{\prime}_{2}\}
and
B^{\prime}=\{b_{1},b_{2},b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}\}
. Vertex preferences in
G^{\prime}
are as follows.

\displaystyle a_{1}\colon b_{1}\succ b_{2}\succ b^{\prime}_{1}\succ b^{\prime}_{2}\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle b_{1}\colon a_{1}\succ a_{2}\succ a^{\prime}_{1}\succ a^{\prime}_{2}

\displaystyle a_{2}\colon b_{1}\succ b_{2}\succ b^{\prime}_{1}\succ b^{\prime}_{2}\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle b_{2}\colon a_{1}\succ a_{2}\succ a^{\prime}_{1}\succ a^{\prime}_{2}

\displaystyle a^{\prime}_{1}\colon b^{\prime}_{1}\succ b^{\prime}_{2}\succ b_{1}\succ b_{2}\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle b^{\prime}_{1}\colon a^{\prime}_{1}\succ a^{\prime}_{2}\succ a_{1}\succ a_{2}

\displaystyle a^{\prime}_{2}\colon b^{\prime}_{1}\succ b^{\prime}_{2}\succ b_{1}\succ b_{2}\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle b^{\prime}_{2}\colon a^{\prime}_{1}\succ a^{\prime}_{2}\succ a_{1}\succ a_{2}

Observe that
S^{\prime}=\{(a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2}),(a^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{1}),(a^{\prime}_{2},b^{\prime}_{2})\}
is a stable matching in
G^{\prime}
. Hence it is popular
[
18
]
, so
S^{\prime}
is a popular perfect matching in
G^{\prime}
.
However the matching
\{(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\}
obtained by replacing edges in
G^{\prime}
with original edges is

not
perfect in
G
.
Note that we are not allowed to have
two
copies of any edge here, hence both
(a,b)
and
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
are present with multiplicity 1. Thus popular perfect matchings in
G
and
G^{\prime}
are quite different from each other.

Our approach.

We need a different approach to solve the min-cost popular perfect matching problem in the many-to-many setting. Our approach is to reduce the min-cost
popular perfect matching problem in
G=(A\cup B,E)
to the min-cost
stable matching problem in an auxiliary many-to-many instance
G^{*}=(A\cup B,E^{*})
. This instance
G^{*}
is a multigraph, i.e., parallel edges
are present in
G^{*}
.
A min-cost stable matching in a many-to-many instance can be computed in polynomial time
[
15
,
20
]
.
Furthermore, Fleiner’s algorithm
[
15
]
works even in the case when the input instance is a multigraph.
Hence a min-cost popular perfect matching in the original many-to-many instance
G
can be computed in polynomial time.
Thus we show the following result.

Theorem 1.1

Let
G=(A\cup B,E)
be a many-to-many matching instance where vertices have strict preferences (possibly incomplete) and there is
a function
\mathsf{cost}:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
If
G
admits a perfect matching then a min-cost popular perfect matching in
G
can be computed in polynomial time.

1.2
Techniques

We will use the characterization of popular perfect matchings in the one-to-one setting. It was shown in

[
27
]
that in order to find a popular maximum matching in a given one-to-one instance, it suffices to run the Gale-Shapley algorithm in an
appropriate auxiliary instance (a multigraph). It was shown in
[
28
]
that this mapping (essentially, a projection) from the set of stable matchings
in this auxiliary instance to the set of popular maximum matchings in the original instance is surjective.
Thus for every popular maximum matching in the original instance, there exists a corresponding stable matching in the auxiliary instance.
Hence in the one-to-one setting,
the min-cost stable matching algorithm in the auxiliary instance solves the min-cost popular maximum matching problem in the original instance.

Our problem is to find a min-cost popular perfect matching in a many-to-many instance
G
. We will show a many-to-many instance
G^{*}
(as before, a multigraph)
such that there is a surjective mapping from the set of stable matchings in
G^{*}
to the set of popular perfect matchings in
G
.
Let
M
be any perfect matching in
G
. We will show in Section
2
that
M
is a popular perfect matching in
G
if and only if there is a realization
M^{\prime}
of
M
such that
M^{\prime}
is
a popular perfect matching in an appropriate one-to-one instance—this instance is a subgraph of the one-to-one cloned instance
G^{\prime}
described earlier.
This subgraph depends on the matching
M^{\prime}
, so let us call it
G^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
. Thus this reduction from the many-to-many setting to the one-to-one setting
does not seem particularly helpful since it depends on the matching
M
that we seek.

We will overcome the above bottleneck in Section
3
by using the result for one-to-one instances in
[
28
]
to construct a one-to-one multigraph
G^{0}_{M}
such that
M^{\prime}
can be realized as a stable matching in
G^{0}_{M}
.
We will then use this to show a many-to-many multigraph
G^{*}
such that

M
can be realized as a stable matching
M^{*}
in the many-to-many instance
G^{*}
.
Conversely, every stable matching in the instance
G^{*}
projects to a popular perfect matching in
G
. Thus finding a min-cost stable matching in
G^{*}
(by Fleiner’s algorithm
[
15
]
) solves the min-cost popular perfect matching problem in
G
.

2
A characterization of popular perfect matchings

This section contains our reduction from the popular perfect matching problem in a many-to-many instance
G=(A\cup B,E)

to the popular perfect matching problem in a one-to-one instance—recall that this one-to-one instance will depend on the matching that we seek.

As mentioned in Section
1
, corresponding to the many-to-many instance
G
, there is a one-to-one instance

G^{\prime}=(A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime},E^{\prime})
obtained via cloning.
So
A^{\prime}=\{a_{i}:a\in A\ \text{and}\ 1\leq i\leq\mathsf{cap}(a)\}
,
B^{\prime}=\{b_{j}:b\in B\ \text{and}\ 1\leq j\leq\mathsf{cap}(b)\}
,
and
E^{\prime}=\{(a_{i},b_{j}):(a,b)\in E\ \text{and}\ 1\leq i\leq\mathsf{cap}(a),1\leq j\leq\mathsf{cap}(b)\}
.
Each vertex in
G^{\prime}
has capacity 1 and a strict preference order over its neighbors.

•

For any vertex
v
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{cap}(v)\}
: the preference order of
v_{i}
in
G^{\prime}
is the same as
v
’s preference order in
G
where every neighbor
u
of
v
gets replaced by all its clones in the order
u_{1}\succ\cdots\succ u_{\mathsf{cap}(u)}
.

Let
M
be a perfect matching in
G
and let
M^{\prime}
be any
one-to-one realization
of
M
in
G^{\prime}
. A one-to-one realization
M^{\prime}

of a many-to-many matching
M
is obtained as follows.

•

For each edge
(a,b)\in M
, choose an index
i\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{cap}(a)\}
and an index
j\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{cap}(b)\}
such that
the indices
i
and
j
have not been chosen so far and include the edge
(a_{i},b_{j})
in
M^{\prime}
.

Thus
M^{\prime}
is a perfect matching in the one-to-one instance
G^{\prime}
.
The following subgraph
G^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}=(A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime},E^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}})
of
G^{\prime}
(originally from
[
7
]
)
will be useful to us. Its edge set
E^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
is as follows.

E^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}=M^{\prime}\cup\{(a_{i},b_{j}):(a,b)\in E\setminus M\ \text{where}\ 1\leq i\leq\mathsf{cap}(a)\ \text{and}\ 1\leq j\leq\mathsf{cap}(b)\}.

For any edge
(a,b)\notin M
, all its
\mathsf{cap}(a)\cdot\mathsf{cap}(b)
many copies
(a_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(a_{\mathsf{cap}(a)},b_{\mathsf{cap}(b)})

are in
E^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
while for each edge
(a,b)\in M
, exactly
one
edge, which is the edge in
M^{\prime}
– say,
(a_{i},b_{j})
– is in
E^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
.

We will use the following edge weight function
\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}
in the one-to-one instance
G^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
.
Recall the
\mathsf{vote}_{v}
-function defined in Section
1
for a vertex
v
.
For any edge
e=(a,b)
in
G^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
, let
\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(a,b)=\mathsf{vote}_{a}(b,M^{\prime}(a))+\mathsf{vote}_{b}(a,M^{\prime}(b))
. Thus for any
e\in E^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
:

\mathrm{let}\ \mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(e)=\begin{cases}2&\text{if\ $e$\ blocks\ $M^{\prime}$;}\\
-2&\text{if\ the\ endpoints\ of\ $e$\ prefer\ their\ partners\ in\ $M^{\prime}$\ to\ each\ other;}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that the above weight function has been defined so that for any perfect matching
N^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
,
we have
\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(N^{\prime})=\Delta(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})=-\Delta(M^{\prime},N^{\prime})
.

The following theorem gives a characterization of popular perfect matchings in a many-to-many instance
G
.
Popular matchings in a many-to-many instance were characterized in
[
31
, Theorem 2.1]
. Theorem
2.1

is a simpler characterization since it deals with popular
perfect
matchings.
This was proved in the hospitals/residents setting in
[
29
]
; the proof of the “only if” part in the many-to-many setting
(given below) is more involved than the proof in the hospitals/residents setting.

Theorem 2.1

A perfect matching
M
is a popular perfect matching in a many-to-many instance
G=(A\cup B,E)

if and only if there is a realization
M^{\prime}
of
M
such that there is no alternating cycle
C
with respect to
M^{\prime}
in
the one-to-one instance
G^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}=(A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime},E^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}})

such that
\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(C)>0
.

Proof

Suppose
M^{\prime}
is a realization of
M
that satisfies the property that

\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(C)\leq 0
for all alternating cycles
C
in
G^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
.
Let
N
be any perfect matching in
G
.
Our first goal is to show a realization
N^{\prime}
of
N
in the one-to-one instance
G^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
such that
\Delta(M^{\prime},N^{\prime})=\sum_{v\in A\cup B}\mathsf{vote}_{v}(M,N)
.
Then we will have
\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(N^{\prime})=-\sum_{v\in A\cup B}\mathsf{vote}_{v}(M,N)
because
\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(N^{\prime})=\Delta(N^{\prime},M^{\prime})=-\Delta(M^{\prime},N^{\prime})
by the definition of
\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}
.
Such a realization
N^{\prime}
of
N
can be obtained in
G^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
as follows.

•

For every edge
(a,b)\in N\cap M
: the edge
(a_{i},b_{j})
is in
N^{\prime}
where
(a_{i},b_{j})\in M^{\prime}
.

•

For every
(a,b)\in N\setminus M
:

–

In the evaluation of
\mathsf{vote}_{a}(M,N)
while comparing
M(a)\setminus N(a)
with
N(a)\setminus M(a)
,
let
b^{\prime}
be the hospital that
a
compares
b
with. So the matching
M^{\prime}
contains the edge
(a_{i},b^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}})
for some

i\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{cap}(a)\}
and
j^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{cap}(b^{\prime})\}
.

–

In the evaluation of
\mathsf{vote}_{b}(M,N)
while comparing
M(b)\setminus N(b)
with
N(b)\setminus M(b)
,
let
a^{\prime}
be the agent that job
b
compares
a
with. So the matching
M^{\prime}
contains the edge

(a^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}},b_{j})
for some
i^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{cap}(a^{\prime})\}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{cap}(b)\}
.

–

The edge
(a_{i},b_{j})
will be included in
N^{\prime}
.

Thus
\sum_{v\in A\cup B}\mathsf{vote}_{v}(M,N)=-\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(N^{\prime})=-\sum_{C}\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(C)
where the summation is over all alternating cycles
C

in
M^{\prime}\oplus N^{\prime}
. Since
M^{\prime}
and
N^{\prime}
are perfect matchings in
G^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
, note that their symmetric difference
M^{\prime}\oplus N^{\prime}
contains
only alternating cycles. Since
\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(C)\leq 0
for all alternating cycles
C
in
M^{\prime}\oplus N^{\prime}
, we have

\Delta(M,N)=\sum_{v\in A\cup B}\mathsf{vote}_{v}(M,N)\geq 0
.
Thus
\Delta(M,N)\geq 0
for any perfect matching
N
, hence
M
is a popular perfect matching in
G
.

Conversely, let
M
be a popular perfect matching in
G
and let
M^{\prime}
be any realization of
M
in
G^{\prime}
.
Let
C
be an alternating cycle with respect to
M^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
.
Let us call
C

valid
if the collection of edges in
G
that correspond to edges in

C
is not a multiset. Not every alternating cycle

with respect to
M^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
is necessarily valid since

C
may consist of multiple edges
(a_{i},b_{i^{\prime}}),\ldots,(a_{j},b_{j^{\prime}})

that correspond to the same edge
(a,b)
in

. The following claim will be very useful.

Claim

If there exists an alternating cycle
C
with respect to
M^{\prime}
in

such that

then there exists a valid alternating cycle

with respect to

.

C
with respect to
M^{\prime}
in

such that
\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(C)>0
.
Then the above claim tells us that there is a valid alternating cycle

such that
\mathsf{wt}_{M^{\prime}}(C^{\prime})>0
.
Moreover,

does not become a multiset with respect to

. Observe that

.
Since

is not a multiset, by identifying all clones of the same vertex,

in

in

.
For any vertex

For each vertex

in

, there is a way of comparing elements in

with those in

such that summed over all vertices in

.

Thus adding up the votes of all vertices

So

i\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{cap}(a)\}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{cap}(b)\}
are chosen such that neither
i
nor
j
has been chosen so far
and
(a_{i},b_{j})_{c}
is included in
M^{0}
. Thus the endpoints of edges in
M^{0}
are in
A^{\prime}\cup B^{\prime}
.
Note that the same color
c
, that was used for
(a,b)
in
M^{*}
, is used for
(a_{i},b_{j})
in
M^{0}
.

Observe that
M^{0}
is a matching in
G^{0}_{M^{\prime}}
where
M^{\prime}
is the projection or colorless version of
M^{0}
.
So
M^{\prime}
is a one-to-one realization of
M

and
G^{0}_{M^{\prime}}
is the colorful one-to-one instance (from Section
3.1
)
corresponding to the graph
G^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
defined in Section
2
.
The following lemma will be useful.

Lemma 1

If
M^{*}
is a stable matching in
G^{*}
then
M^{0}
is a stable matching in
G^{0}_{M^{\prime}}

where
M^{0}
is any realization of
M^{*}
.

Proof

Suppose
M^{0}
is not stable in
G^{0}_{M^{\prime}}
. Then there is an edge
e_{c}
that blocks
M^{0}
where

e_{c}=(x_{i},y_{j})_{c}
for some
(x,y)\in E
and
c\in\{1,\ldots,n_{0}\}
.
Note that it has to be the case that
(x,y)\notin M
where
M
is the projection of
M^{*}
.
Suppose
(x,y)\in M
. Then there is exactly
one
edge corresponding to
(x,y)
in the graph
G^{\prime}_{M^{\prime}}
and it has to be
e=(x_{i},y_{j})
. So there are
n_{0}
edges
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n_{0}}
in
G^{0}_{M^{\prime}}
. None of them can block
M^{0}
because if
e_{c^{\prime}}\in M^{0}

then agent
x_{i}
prefers
e_{c^{\prime}}
to
e_{c^{\prime}+1},\ldots,e_{n_{0}}
(agents prefer lower color edges) while job
y_{j}
prefers
e_{c^{\prime}}
to
e_{1},\ldots,e_{c^{\prime}-1}
(jobs prefer higher color edges).

So there is some edge
(x,y)\notin M
such that for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{cap}(x)\},j\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{cap}(y)\}
, and color
c\in\{1,\ldots,n_{0}\}
, the edge
e_{c}
in
G^{0}_{M^{\prime}}
blocks
M^{0}
where
e=(x_{i},y_{j})
.
So either
x_{i}
is unmatched or prefers
e_{c}
to its
M^{0}
-edge and similarly, either
y_{j}
is unmatched or prefers
e_{c}
to its
M^{0}
-edge.
This means in the instance
G^{*}
, there is an edge
e^{\prime}=(x,y)\notin M
and color
c\in\{1,\ldots,n_{0}\}

such that
either
x
is not fully matched in
M^{*}
or prefers
e^{\prime}_{c}
to its worst
M^{*}
-edge and similarly,
either
y
is not fully matched in
M^{*}
or prefers
e^{\prime}_{c}
to its worst
M^{*}
-edge. Thus
e^{\prime}_{c}
blocks
M^{*}
, a contradiction to its stability in
G^{*}
. ∎

As we show next, the converse of the above lemma also holds.

Lemma 2

For any matching
M^{*}
in
G^{*}
, if there is a realization
M^{0}
of
M^{*}
such that
M^{0}
is stable in the one-to-one instance
G^{0}_{M^{\prime}}

(where
M^{\prime}
is the projection of
M^{0}
) then
M^{*}
is stable in
G^{*}
.

Proof

Suppose
M^{*}
is not stable in
G^{*}
. Then there is an edge
e\notin M

and color
c\in\{1,\ldots,n_{0}\}
such that
e_{c}
blocks
M^{*}
. Let
e=(x,y)
.
So either
x
is not fully matched in
M^{*}
or
prefers
e_{c}
to its worst
M^{*}
-edge and similarly, either
y
is not fully matched in
M^{*}
or prefers
e_{c}
to its worst
M^{*}
-edge.
If
x
(resp.,
y
) is fully matched in
M^{*}
then let
e^{\prime}_{c^{\prime}}
(resp.,
e^{\prime\prime}_{c^{\prime\prime}}
) be the worst edge in
M^{*}
that is incident to
x
(resp.,
y
);
let
e^{\prime}=(x,y^{\prime})
and
e^{\prime\prime}=(x^{\prime},y)
.

For some
i\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{cap}(x)\}
, the one-to-one matching
M^{0}
either leaves
x_{i}
unmatched or contains the edge
(x_{i},y^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}})_{c^{\prime}}

where
j^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{cap}(y^{\prime})\}
. Similarly, for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{cap}(y)\}
, the matching
M^{0}
either leaves
y_{j}
unmatched or
contains the edge
(x^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}},y_{j})_{c^{\prime\prime}}
where
i^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,\mathsf{cap}(a^{\prime})\}
.
Since
(x,y)\notin M
, all the
n_{0}
edges
(x_{i},y_{j})_{1},\ldots,(x_{i},y_{j})_{n_{0}}
are in
G^{0}_{M^{\prime}}
. Thus the edge
(x_{i},y_{j})_{c}
belongs to
E^{0}_{M^{\prime}}
and it blocks
M^{0}
,
a contradiction to its stability in
G^{0}_{M^{\prime}}
. Hence it has to be the case that
M^{*}
is stable in
G^{*}
. ∎

Thus we can conclude the following.

•

For any popular perfect matching
M
in
G
, there exists a colorful version of
M
that is stable in
G^{*}

(by Theorem
2.1
, Theorem
3.1
, and Lemma
2
).

•

Conversely, for any stable matching
M^{*}
in
G^{*}
, the matching obtained by ignoring its edge colors is a popular perfect matching in
G

(by Lemma
1
, Theorem
3.1
, and Theorem
2.1
).

Summarizing, a perfect matching in
G
is a popular perfect matching if and only if there is a
colorful
version of it that is stable in the colorful multigraph
G^{*}
. Thus solving the min-cost stable matching problem in the many-to-many instance
G^{*}
solves the min-cost popular perfect matching problem in
G
.
As mentioned earlier,
there are polynomial-time algorithms to solve the min-cost stable matching problem in a many-to-many instance
[
15
,
20
]
.

Recall that our many-to-many instance is a
multigraph
. However that does not pose any additional difficulty because Fleiner’s algorithm
[
15
]
solves the min-cost matroid kernel problem in the intersection of two strictly ordered matroids. The matroid kernel problem
generalizes the stable matching problem in a many-to-many instance where the underlying graph is a multigraph.

5

5

Many-to-many matchings in a simple bipartite graph are the intersection of two partition matroids while many-to-many matchings in a bipartite graph with parallel edges are the intersection of two laminar matroids.

Thus the min-cost stable matching problem in a many-to-many instance, where the underlying graph is a multigraph, can be solved in polynomial time by Fleiner’s algorithm
[
15
]
.
Hence we can conclude Theorem
3.2
stated in Section
1
.
We restate this result below for convenience.

Theorem
1.1

Let
G=(A\cup B,E)
be a many-to-many matching instance where vertices have strict preferences (possibly incomplete) and there is
a function
\mathsf{cost}:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
If
G
admits a perfect matching then a min-cost popular perfect matching in
G
can be computed in polynomial time.

4
Conclusions and open problems

We characterized popular perfect matchings in a many-to-many instance as projections of stable matchings in a colorful auxiliary instance. This yielded
a polynomial-time algorithm for the min-cost popular perfect matching problem in a many-to-many instance.

An interesting open problem is to show a polynomial-time algorithm for the min-cost popular
maximum
matching
problem in a hospitals/residents instance. Recall that a polynomial-time algorithm is known for this problem in the one-to-one
setting. This is by a reduction to a min-cost stable matching problem in a colorful instance
[
28
]
.
However this reduction does not extend to the hospitals/residents setting
because there may exist popular maximum matchings in a hospitals/residents instance
that do not occur as stable matchings in the corresponding colorful instance; such an example was given in
[
30
]
.
A more general open problem is to solve the min-cost popular maximum matching problem in a
many-to-many
instance.
