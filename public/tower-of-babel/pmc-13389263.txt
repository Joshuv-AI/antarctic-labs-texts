Severe Mitral Regurgitation and Constrictive Pericarditis in a Patient with Systemic Lupus Erythematosus
Authors: Austin J. Jolly, Albert A. Huang, Anaelle C. Zimmowitch, Noweeda N. Mirza, Joseph J. Maleszewski, Juan A. Crestanello, Vuyisile T. Nkomo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Cardiac involvement in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) encompasses a heterogeneous spectrum of valvular, myocardial, and pericardial disease.1 Although often clinically silent, advanced disease can result in significant hemodynamic consequences and affect morbidity and mortality, especially when multiple cardiac processes coexist.

Libman-Sacks endocarditis (LSE) is a rare cardiac manifestation of SLE characterized by sterile valvular vegetations that can induce valvular dysfunction and heart failure. Although mild or moderate pericardial involvement is common in SLE, affecting up to 20% to 30% of patients symptomatically,2 progression to constrictive pericarditis is uncommon. The simultaneous occurrence of these entities is exceedingly rare.

We report the case of a young patient with poorly controlled SLE who presented with acute decompensated heart failure due to severe mitral regurgitation (MR) from LSE and concomitant constrictive pericarditis, ultimately requiring urgent surgical intervention. Echocardiography and cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) provided essential data to identify the drivers of the patient’s pathology, underscoring the value of multimodal imaging for clinical management in patients with heterogenous cardiac lesions in the setting of SLE.

Case Presentation

A 36-year-old man with a history of SLE presented with acute progressive dyspnea and hypoxemic respiratory failure. The patient reported worsening exertional dyspnea over several days, culminating in an inability to climb stairs. During emergency medical services transport, the patient was hypoxemic to 80% and required noninvasive ventilatory support. Review of systems revealed subacute worsening of polyarticular joint pain, intermittent fevers, and night sweats but no recent cough, wheezing, or infectious exposures. Medical history was notable for SLE diagnosed 1 year before presentation with pancytopenia with an antinuclear antibody titer of 1:2,560 (homogeneous pattern), strongly positive anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies, weakly positive anticardiolipin immunoglobulin G, and a positive direct antiglobulin test result via C3. Despite treatment with hydroxychloroquine, prednisone, and later belimumab, the patient experienced recurrent flares with refractory anemia, hypocomplementemia, and persistently elevated inflammatory markers. History was notable for nephrolithiasis status post lithotripsy but no documented lupus nephritis. During hospitalization, C-reactive protein was elevated at 98.7 mg/L and erythrocyte sedimentation rate at 56 mm/h.

Semiurgent bedside transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) demonstrated findings consistent with constrictive hemodynamics and concomitant severe MR. There were marked respirophasic changes in interventricular septal motion with septal flattening and mitral annulus reversus, evidenced by a medial e′ velocity of 0.12 m/s, exceeding the lateral e′ velocity of 0.08 m/s. Left ventricular systolic function was normal, with a biplane ejection fraction of 71%, despite mild left ventricular dilation (end-diastolic volume index 99 mL/m2). The mitral valve (MV) leaflets were markedly thickened with suspected thrombotic material, and MR was severe, with an effective regurgitant orifice area of 0.96 cm2. The left atrium was enlarged, with a volume index of 51 mL/m2.

Transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) was performed to better characterize the MV lesion (Figure 2). TEE confirmed severe thickening of the anterior and posterior leaflet, with thrombus on the ventricular side measuring 10 to 18 mm in depth from the leaflet tip to the base, with lesion extension onto the adjacent ventricular septal endocardium, forming a contact lesion (Figure 2). A large, systolic regurgitant jet extended into the left atrium (Video 1), and three-dimensional rendering demonstrated incomplete coaptation of the MV leaflets during ventricular systole (Video 2). Although echocardiography demonstrated features suggestive of constrictive physiology, assessment of pericardial thickness and active inflammation was limited. CMR was therefore pursued for further tissue characterization. CMR demonstrated circumferential pericardial thickening measuring up to 4 mm with diffuse late gadolinium enhancement, consistent with active inflammatory constrictive pericarditis (Figure 3). Free-breathing cine images revealed exaggerated ventricular interdependence with respirophasic flattening of the interventricular septum. CMR confirmed severe MR with left atrial dilation (Video 3). Collectively, these findings established active pericardial inflammation superimposed on advanced valvular disease. Comprehensive infectious workup (Bartonella, Coxiella, Brucella, Tropheryma whipplei) was negative. Given that the patient was clinically improving with diuretics and a preference to avoid surgery, MV replacement and pericardiectomy were deferred, and the case was managed medically with aggressive anticoagulation to reduce thrombus burden. Therapeutic dosing of enoxaparin (1 mg/kg twice daily) and bridging to warfarin (international normalized ratio goal 2-3) were used. Colchicine and ibuprofen were used to treat constrictive pericarditis, and the patient was transitioned to daily oral furosemide. Belimumab was resumed because of ongoing hypocomplementemia.

Despite reassuring outpatient follow-up encounters, the patient re-presented 6 weeks after discharge with several days of worsening dyspnea, orthopnea, and dizziness. On presentation, the international normalized ratio was supratherapeutic at 7.9, and the patient was found to be in decompensated heart failure, with evidence of end-organ hypoperfusion, including acute kidney injury (creatinine ∼ 2 mg/dL) and elevated liver enzymes (aspartate aminotransferase 581 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 819 U/L). There was concern for low cardiac output with impending cardiogenic shock, and the patient was admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit for further evaluation and hemodynamic monitoring.

Chest radiography again demonstrated pulmonary edema. Repeat TTE demonstrated partial interval reduction in echogenic material along the ventricular aspects of the anterior and posterior MV leaflets. Despite this anatomic improvement, MR remained severe (effective regurgitant orifice area 0.89 cm2). Left atrial enlargement had progressed, with a volume index of 73 mL/m2 (Figure 4). Hemodynamic assessment with a pulmonary artery catheter demonstrated elevated right- and left-sided filling pressures and a low cardiac index (1.8-2.2 L/min/m2). The patient required aggressive medical management, including high-dose intravenous diuretics with thiazide augmentation, inotropic support with milrinone, and eventual intra-aortic balloon pump placement for persistently low cardiac output. The patient underwent MV replacement with an On-X 27/29 bileaflet mechanical prosthesis (Artivion) and complete pericardiectomy. Gross inspection of the resected native MV demonstrated fibrous deposition along the anterior leaflet. Histologic analysis revealed noncalcific fibrous thickening of the pericardium consistent with organizing pericarditis, postinflammatory fibrotic deposition of the anterior MV leaflet, and multiple reactive mediastinal lymph nodes (Figure 5). Postoperatively, the patient required transient inotropic and ventilatory support, with subsequent weaning of mechanical circulatory support and vasoactive medications. Renal and hepatic function improved with resolution of cardiogenic shock. Bivalirudin was used until therapeutic anticoagulation with warfarin was achieved, and the patient was discharged in stable condition. Discussion

This case illustrates the important role of multimodality cardiac imaging in diagnosing and managing complex autoimmune cardiac disease. In patients with SLE, valvular masses raise a broad differential diagnosis with different therapeutic implications. TTE served as an essential initial screening modality, identifying severe MV pathology, chamber remodeling, and constrictive hemodynamics, prompting further evaluation.

TEE was pivotal in defining the valvular anatomy and mechanism of MR. The identification of extensive, echodense lesions involving both MV leaflets, predominantly on the ventricular aspect, in the absence of bacteremia or systemic infection, supported a diagnosis of LSE. Compared with TTE, TEE provided superior spatial resolution, allowing accurate assessment of lesion extent, leaflet involvement, and the severity of MR.

Beyond valvular assessment, imaging played a central role in identifying concomitant pericardial disease. Although echocardiography suggested pericardial thickening and raised concern for constrictive physiology, CMR provided definitive confirmation. CMR demonstrated circumferential pericardial thickening with diffuse late gadolinium enhancement, supporting a diagnosis of inflammatory constrictive pericarditis. Importantly, CMR added tissue characterization not available by echocardiography, allowing distinction between active inflammatory disease and chronic fibrotic constriction, an assessment with direct implications for medical therapy and surgical planning.

Unlike infective endocarditis, there are no established indications for surgical intervention in LSE.3 Several reports of LSE conclude that anticoagulation plus immunosuppression including methylprednisolone, cyclophosphamide, and intravenous immunoglobulin, is sufficient to treat the disease.4, 5, 6 However, these cases did not identify concomitant constrictive pericarditis. The risk for recurrent thromboembolic events in patients with LSE has been reported to be 10% to 15%, even with therapeutic anticoagulation, suggesting that surgery should be considered if the surgical candidacy of the patient is satisfactory.4 There are reported cases of severe regurgitation refractory to medical therapy ultimately requiring valve replacement, but right heart catheterization was used for operative planning in some of these cases, without the use of noninvasive techniques such as CMR.7 Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair has been reported as an intervention to treat a patient with SLE with thickened MV leaflets, but there was no evidence of active thrombus on that patient’s valve leaflets.8 There are reports of constrictive pericarditis treated with pericardiectomy in patients with SLE, but concomitant LSE was not identified in these cases, and right heart catheterization was used to measure the intracardiac pressures instead of noninvasive CMR.9,10 Simultaneous presence of LSE and constrictive pericarditis, and the complementary role of echocardiography and CMR in guiding staged medical and surgical decision-making, is not well established.

This case drives the field forward by highlighting how coexistent advanced autoimmune valvular and pericardial disease can limit effectiveness of medical therapy alone which can help guide and set expectations of care. Although anticoagulation led to a reduction in thrombotic burden on follow-up TTE, severe MR persisted, underscoring that structural valvular damage may not be reversible despite apparent echocardiographic improvement in vegetation size. This interval study illustrates a potential limitation of medical therapy alone in advanced LSE: reduction in thrombotic valvular material may not translate into improvement in regurgitant severity or clinical symptoms. Serial imaging was therefore essential not only for monitoring lesion burden but also for recognizing failure of medical therapy and supporting timely surgical intervention.

Conclusion

LSE is an uncommon cardiac manifestation of SLE, and progression to constrictive pericarditis is rare. This case demonstrates the essential role of multimodal imaging in distinguishing autoimmune valvular disease from infective endocarditis, identifying concomitant constrictive pericarditis, and guiding therapeutic decision-making. Although medical therapy may reduce thrombotic burden, advanced valvular dysfunction may persist, necessitating early surgical consideration. Coordinated multidisciplinary care and longitudinal follow-up imaging are salient in managing complex autoimmune cardiovascular disease.
