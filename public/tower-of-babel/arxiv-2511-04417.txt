The selective advantage of neighborhood-aware mutants in Moran process
Michal Pecho, Josef Tkadlec, Martin A. Nowak

Abstract
Evolution occurs in populations of reproducing individuals. In stochastic descriptions of evolutionary dynamics, such as the Moran process, individuals are chosen randomly for birth and for death. If the same type is chosen for both steps, then the reproductive event is wasted, because the composition of the population remains unchanged. Here we introduce a new phenotype, which we call a replacer . Replacers are efficient competitors. When a replacer is chosen for reproduction, the offspring will always replace an individual of another type (if available). We determine the selective advantage of replacers in well-mixed populations and on one-dimensional lattices. We find that being a replacer substantially boosts the fixation probability of neutral and deleterious mutants. In particular, fixation probability of a single neutral replacer who invades a well-mixed population of size N N is of the order of 1 / N 1/\sqrt{N} rather than the standard 1 / N 1/N . Even more importantly, replacers are much better protected against invasions once they have reached fixation. Therefore, replacers dominate the mutation selection equilibrium even if the phenotype of being a replacer comes at a substantial cost: curiously, for large population size and small mutation rate the relative fitness of a successful replacer can be as low as 1 / e 1/e .

Here we consider the evolutionary dynamics of a phenotype which we call a
replacer
.
In the standard Moran process, the offspring of the reproducing parent might end up replacing an individual that has the same type as the parent.
Whenever this occurs, the reproductive step is effectively wasted, since the population composition has not changed (only time was gained).
We say that a phenotype is a replacer, if it avoids those situations whenever possible.
Thus, when a replacer is reproducing, the offspring replaces a random individual among those that have a different type than the parent.
It replaces the “other type” whenever such an individual is available (and within reach).

It is clear that being a replacer should be an advantageous trait.
But how much selective advantage is gained?
To answer this question, we calculate three measures of success for a mutant who is a replacer.
First, we calculate
fixation probability

Being a replacer should increase the fixation probability.
Second, we calculate the
elimination probability

N-1

Being a replacer should decreases the elimination probability.
Third, we calculate the expected frequency in a mutation-selection equilibrium

Being a replacer should increase the expected frequency.

For well-mixed populations of size

1/\sqrt{N}

This quantity is substantially larger than the

For non-neutral mutants, the difference in the fixation probability is less pronounced:
For disadvantageous mutants (

N

For advantageous mutants (
r>1
), the fixation probability tends to the same constant
1-\frac{1}{r}
as for the non-replacers, when the population size
N
becomes large.
However, we find that replacers get a major boost in terms of the elimination probability.
In particular, while disadvantageous non-replacers are eliminated with constant probability, for replacers the elimination probability is exponentially small.
Thus, once replacers become established, they are much better protected against further invasions, even when they have a fitness disadvantage.
As a consequence, we show that replacers dominate in the mutation-selection equilibrium even when they are disadvantageous.
Curiously, for large population size and small mutation rates, the relative fitness of a successful replacer can be as low as
1/e\doteq 0.37

Moreover, those effects occur (and are often even more pronounced) for small population sizes
N

In particular, on a large one-dimensional lattice the fixation probability of a neutral replacer approaches the value

Model

Here we describe in detail the Moran process that governs the evolutionary dynamics, the phenotype of replacers, and the key quantities of fixation and elimination probability that characterize the evolutionary dynamics.

Evolutionary dynamics: Moran process.

Throughout most of the paper, we consider a competition between mutants and residents in a well-mixed population of size
N
.
Residents have normalized fitness 1, while mutants have relative fitness
r
.
Mutations may be neutral (
r=1
), beneficial/advantageous (
r>1
), or deleterious/disadvantageous (
r<1
).
The evolutionary dynamics is governed by the
Moran Birth-death process
.
The basic setting is as follows:
The evolution occurs in discrete time-steps.
In each step, one individual is selected for reproduction, where the probability of each individual being selected is proportional to their fitness.
Then, another individual is chosen for death uniformly at random, and the dying individual is replaced by the copy of the reproducing individual.
When the reproducing and the dying individual are of the same type, nothing changes; otherwise the reproducing type becomes more frequent.
Such steps are repeated until all individuals in the population are of the same type (either all mutants or all residents).
This basic setting can be extended in several ways, see below.

Replacers.

In this work, we consider mutants who are replacers. When a replacer is selected for reproduction, the dying individual is not chosen randomly from among all the other individuals, but only from among all the residents. Thus, a reproducing replacer never “wastes their turn”, and will always cause the replacer frequency to increase.
For clarity, mutants who are not replacers are called
oblivious
mutants.

In each step of the Moran Birth-death process, an offspring of a reproducing mutant (yellow circle) migrates along one of the red arrows to replace the individual at its other end.

Key quantities: Fixation and elimination probability.

To characterize the long-term fate of an evolving population we study the following two classic quantities. First, the
fixation probability
, denoted
\rho_{r}(N)
, is the probability that Moran process starting with a single invading mutant with fitness
r
ends with all individuals being mutants.
Fixation probability measures the ability of mutants to establish themselves.

Second, the
elimination probability
, denoted
\psi_{r}(N)
, is the probability that Moran process starting with
N-1
mutants ends with all individuals being residents.
Elimination probability quantifies the extent to which mutant populations are protected from subsequent resident invasions.

When the mutant is a replacer, we denote the fixation probability by
\rho^{R}_{r}(N)
and the elimination probability by
\psi^{R}_{r}(N)
. When the mutant is neutral, we use a shorthand notation
\rho(N)
to mean
\rho_{r=1}(N)
.

Extension: Mutation-selection process and lattice spatial structure.

We consider two extensions of the standard Moran process.

First, we consider the mutation-selection process.
In the mutation-selection process with mutation rate
u\in(0,1)
, during each reproductive event there is a probability
u
that mutation occurs and the offspring of the reproducing individual becomes the other type than the parent.
Therefore, no type ever gets established forever, and instead of studying fixation and elimination probabilities, we measure the average number (frequency) of mutants in the long term.

Second, Moran process can be adapted to accommodate spatial structures.
Here we consider a special case of a one-dimensional lattice with periodic boundary condition. That is, there are
N
sites arranged along a cycle
C_{N}
, and any time an individual reproduces, the offspring migrates to one of the two neighboring sites.
If the reproducing individual is
not
a replacer, the offspring migrates to a neighbor selected uniformly at random.
If the reproducing individual is a replacer, the offspring migrates to a random resident neighbor (or to a random neighbor, if no resident neighbor exists). The corresponding fixation and elimination probabilities are denoted
\rho_{r}(C_{N})
,
\psi_{r}(C_{N})
for oblivious mutants, and
\rho^{R}_{r}(C_{N})
,
\psi^{R}_{r}(C_{N})
for replacers.

Asymptotic notation.

To concisely express results about the fixation probability
\rho(N)
and the elimination probability
\psi(N)
for large population sizes
N
, we employ the following standard notation
[
42
]
.
We write
A(N)\approx B(N)
to denote that
A(N)
and
B(N)
are asymptotically equal, that is,
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{A(N)}{B(N)}=1
.
We write
A(N)\sim B(N)
to denote that
A(N)
is proportional to
B(N)
, that is, there exist positive constants
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
such that
c_{1}\leq\frac{A(N)}{B(N)}\leq c_{2}
.
We write
A(N)\lesssim B(N)
to denote that
A(N)
is less than or proportional to
B(N)
, that is, there exists a positive constant
c_{2}
such that
\frac{A(N)}{B(N)}\leq c_{2}
.
So for example if
\rho(N)=\frac{2}{N-1}
then we would write
\rho(N)\approx\frac{2}{N}
and
\rho(N)\sim\frac{1}{N}
.

Results

Neutral evolution

In a well-mixed population of size
N
, the situation at any given time point of the evolutionary dynamics is completely described by just the number
i
of invading mutants.
To track how the number of mutants changes in time, for each
1\leq i\leq N-1
we denote by
p_{i}
(resp.
q_{i}
) the probability that the number of mutants increases (resp. decreases) in a single step of the Moran process.
Many aspects of the evolutionary process depend only on the ratios
\gamma_{i}=p_{i}/q_{i}
of those two probabilities. We call this ratio
\gamma_{i}
the
forward bias
.

The forward bias
\gamma_{i}
in a well-mixed population can be expressed explicitly, for any population size
N
and any number
i
of mutants. When the mutants are neutral and both types of individuals are oblivious, standard calculation yields

p_{i}=\frac{i}{N}\cdot\frac{N-i}{N}\quad\text{and}\quad q_{i}=\frac{N-i}{N}\cdot\frac{i}{N},

thus
\gamma_{i}=\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}=1
, regardless of
N
and
i
.
In other words, when both types are oblivious, the size of the mutant subpopulation is always as likely to increase as it is to decrease.

In contrast, when mutants are replacers, an analogous calculation yields

p^{R}_{i}=\frac{i}{N}\cdot 1\quad\text{and}\quad q^{R}_{i}=q_{i}=\frac{N-i}{N}\cdot\frac{i}{N},

thus
\gamma^{R}_{i}=\frac{p^{R}_{i}}{q^{R}_{i}}=\frac{N}{N-i}
.
Therefore, in this setting the forward bias of replacers increases with
i
.
In particular, for
i=1
we have
\gamma^{R}_{1}=1=\gamma_{1}
, meaning that being a replacer does not bring any advantage if there is only a single mutant.
At the other extreme, for
i=N-1
we have
\gamma^{R}_{N-1}=N-1
, so being a replacer helps immensely.

Fig.

2
for a direct comparison.

The above differences have a profound effect on the fixation probability of a single mutant who attempts to invade a population of size
N
.
When the mutant is oblivious, its fixation probability is known to satisfy
\rho(N)=1/N
.
When the mutant is a replacer, we show that the fixation probability
\rho^{R}(N)
is instead proportional to
1/\sqrt{N}
.

Theorem 1

(Neutral fixation probability)
.

In a well-mixed population of size
N
, the fixation probability
\rho^{R}
of a single replacer satisfies
\rho^{R}(N)\approx\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\cdot N}}
.

Here the symbol
\approx
means that for large
N
, the ratio of the two quantities tends to 1.
In particular, in the limit of large population size
N\to\infty
, the fixation probability
\rho^{R}(N)
of a neutral replacer still decreases to 0, but it does so substantially more slowly than the fixation probability
\rho(N)
of an oblivious mutant.

Another important quantity that depends only on the forward biases is the elimination probability of the mutants, once they become established. This measures the stability of the mutant population with respect to invasions of a single resident.

In the setting where both mutants and residents are oblivious, it is known that the elimination probability
\psi(N)

of
N-1
mutants, when facing a single resident, is again equal to
\psi=1/N
.
When the mutants are replacers, we show that the elimination probability
\psi^{R}(N)
is exponentially small.

Theorem 2

(Neutral elimination probability)
.

In a well-mixed population of size
N
, the elimination probability
\psi^{R}(N)
of
N-1
replacers satisfies
\psi^{R}(N)\approx 2/e^{N-1}
.

Thus, once established, the replacer subpopulation is extremely unlikely to be wiped out by the oblivious residents.

Non-neutral evolution

Not all mutations are neutral – some increase the individual’s reproductive rate while others decrease it.
Moran process accommodates for such effects by assigning a relative fitness
r\neq 1
to each mutant, as compared to a baseline fitness
1
assigned to each resident.
The case
r>1
corresponds to advantageous (beneficial) mutations, the case
r<1
corresponds to disadvantageous (deleterious) mutations.

When both the mutants and the residents are oblivious, standard calculation yields that the forward bias satisfies
\gamma_{i}=\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}=r
, regardless of the population size
N
and the number
i
of mutants.
As a consequence, the fixation probability of a single mutant with relative fitness
r
is
\rho_{r}(N)=\frac{1-\frac{1}{r}}{1-\frac{1}{r^{N}}}
.
In particular, when
r>1
and the population size
N
is large, the fixation probability tends to a positive constant
1-1/r
.
In contrast, when
r<1
, the fixation probability is roughly
r^{N}
, that is, it tends to 0 exponentially quickly.
Thus, in large populations, beneficial mutations have a constant chance of fixing, whereas deleterious mutations are exponentially unlikely to fix.

Somewhat surprisingly, both those claims remain true also when the invading mutant with fitness
r
is not oblivious, but rather a replacer. We can prove the following result.

Theorem 3

(Fixation probability for large
N
)
.

In a large well-mixed population of size
N
, the fixation probability
\rho^{R}(N)
of a single replacer with relative fitness
r
satisfies

\displaystyle\rho^{R}_{r}(N)

\displaystyle\sim 1-\frac{1}{r}\text{\ \ if $r>1$ and }

\displaystyle\rho^{R}_{r}(N)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{(c_{r})^{N}}{\sqrt{N}}\text{\ \ if $r<1$},

where
c_{r}=r\cdot e^{1-r}>r
is a constant that depends on
r
.

Note that when
r<1
, both
\rho_{r}(N)
and
\rho^{R}_{r}(N)
are exponentially small in
N
.
However, the ratio
\rho^{R}_{r}(N)/\rho_{r}(N)
grows unbounded since
c_{r}/r>1
.
Thus, the fixation probability
\rho_{r}(N)
of a deleterious oblivious mutant decays towards 0 exponentially faster than the fixation probability
\rho^{R}_{r}(N)
of an equally deleterious replacer.

As in the case of neutral mutations, we also consider the elimination probability of
N-1
mutants with fitness
r\neq 1
.
For oblivious mutants, the elimination probability can be computed in the same fashion as the fixation probability.
In particular, when
r>1
and the population size
N
is large, the elimination probability of
N-1
mutants with fitness
r
is roughly
1/r^{n}
, that is, it tends to 0 exponentially quickly.
In contrast, when
r<1
, the elimination probability of
N-1
mutants with fitness
r
tends to a positive constant
1-r
.
Thus, in large populations, established beneficial mutations are strongly protected, whereas established deleterious mutations are highly susceptible to resident invasions.

In contrast, we show that mutants who are replacers, are strongly protected regardless of whether they are beneficial or deleterious.

Theorem 4

(Elimination probability for large
N
)
.

In a large well-mixed population of size
N\geq 3
, the elimination probability
\rho^{R}
of a
N-1
replacers with relative fitness
r
satisfies

\displaystyle\psi^{R}_{r}(N)

\displaystyle\lesssim 1/r^{N}\text{\ \ if $r>1$ and }

\displaystyle\psi^{R}_{r}(N)

\displaystyle\sim(c^{\prime}_{r})^{N}\text{\ \ if $r<1$},

where
c^{\prime}_{r}=1/e^{r}<1
is a constant that depends on
r
.

r

The replacer cost

It is conceivable that in practice the ability to express the replacer phenotype comes with an associated cost that can be represented as a reduction in the mutant fitness.
Here we study how much fitness is a mutant willing to sacrifice, in order to become a replacer.
More formally, what is the cost
s
such that a replacer with relative fitness
r=1-s
performs equally well as an oblivious mutant with fitness
r=1
?
In reality, this cost
s
depends on the population size
N
, so we denote it by
s_{N}
.
And it also depends on whether we measure the mutant performance in terms of the fixation probability, or the elimination probability, or the frequency in the mutation-selection process.

In

For the fixation probability, we show that as the population size
N
grows large, the cost
s_{N}
tends to zero.
However, we show that for a broad range of population sizes
N
this cost is quite substantial.
In particular, the replacer cost peaks for
N=8
, where it satisfies
s_{8}>0.27
. For
N=100
it is still substantial, satisfying
s_{100}>0.16
, see

Cycle spatial structure

All our results so far have been for a well-mixed population.
In this section, we consider a spatially structured population where the
N
sites are arranged along a cycle
C_{N}
, and the offspring always migrates to one of the two adjacent sites. Thus, the spatial structure is a one-dimensional lattice with a periodic boundary condition. We denote the corresponding fixation and elimination probabilities by
\rho_{r}(C_{N})
,
\rho^{R}_{r}(C_{N})
,
\psi_{r}(C_{N})
, and
\psi^{R}_{r}(C_{N})
.

For oblivious mutants, living on a cycle
C_{N}
or in a well-mixed population does not make a difference when it comes to fixation and elimination probabilities.
That is, the top rows of
Figs.

3
and
4
apply to the lattice
C_{N}
as well as to the well-mixed population
[
11
]
.
In particular, the fixation and elimination probability are equal to
1/N
when
r=1
, otherwise they either tend to a constant, or they are exponentially small as the population size
N
becomes large.

If the mutant is a replacer, the spatial structure does make a difference.
We are able to compute the fixation and elimination probabilities exactly, see Appendix for details.
It turns out that the threshold fitness value shifts to
r^{\star}=1/2
.
When
r^{\star}=1/2
, both the fixation probability
\rho^{R}_{r=1/2}(C_{N})
and the elimination probability
\psi^{R}_{r=1/2}(C_{N})
are of the order of
1/N
.
The following result lists the approximate probabilities also in the cases
r<1/2
and

Discussion

In summary, we have studied stochastic evolutionary dynamics of replacer phenotypes
in well-mixed populations, which can be represented by complete graphs, and on cycles.
The offspring of a standard, oblivious individual replaces a random neighbor in the population.
If the replaced individual is of the same type, then the reproductive event was wasted in so far as it did not change the composition of the population.
In contrast, the offspring of a replacer phenotype replaces a random neighbor of the other type (if available).
Therefore, replacers are more efficient.
They only waste reproductive events if the other type is not available in the neighborhood of the reproducing individual.
On a complete graph this never happens unless the population is homogeneous.

We find that replacers are efficient competitors.
The fixation probability of a single neutral replacer in a population of size
N
scales as roughly
1/\sqrt{N}
.
In comparison, the fixation probability of a standard, oblivious neutral mutant is
1/N
.
The probability that the replacers get eliminated once they become established in the population is exponentially small.
Therefore, replaces enjoy strong evolutionary stability.
Because of this stability, replacers tend to dominate oblivious types in the mutation-selection equilibrium, even if their relative reproductive rate is much lower.
When the evolutionary dynamics takes place on a cyclic structure, then replacers remain prevalent in the mutation=selection equilibrium for relative reproductive rate
r
as low as
r\approx 1/2
.

In this paper, we have concentrated on two biologically relevant population structures: the well-mixed population and the one dimensional lattice (the cycle).
Studying replacers on other spatial structures is an interesting direction of future research.
We note that in a well-mixed population and on a cycle it suffices to track the number of mutants.
On general spatial structures it is crucial to track the exact configuration of the population (the coloring of the graph).
Therefore, it becomes a formidable problem.

Curiously, it turns out that for both the well-mixed population and for the cycle, the evolutionary dynamics between neutral replacers and oblivious individuals closely resembles the dynamics of an evolutionary game with a payoff matrix.
For the well-mixed population, consider the game with matrix
\bordermatrix{&R&O\cr R&1&1\cr O&0&1}.

That is, replacer receives payoff 1 from replacer and from oblivious individuals, while the oblivious individual receives payoff 1 only from the oblivious individuals.
The payoff of each individual determines its reproductive rate or fitness. Individuals are selected for reproduction with a probability proportional to their fitness.
It can be shown that the fixation probabilities of the respective types in this evolutionary game are equal to the fixation probabilities of replacers and oblivious individuals as described above (see Appendix for details).
Similarly, the dynamics of replacers and oblivious individuals on a cycle can be interpreted as a game with matrix

\bordermatrix{&R&O\cr R&3&1\cr O&1&1}.

But for general graphs, the evolutionary dynamics of replacers and oblivious types cannot be described by a game with a simple
2\times 2
payoff matrix.

Finally, we mention one more direction for future work.
So far we have assumed that the offspring of a replacer migrates to a random site among the neighbors that are occupied by the other type.
But with even higher levels of sensing, the offspring could migrate to specific sites, perhaps the ones that are “most beneficial” for maximizing either the fixation probability or evolutionary stability of these “smart replacers”.

1
Preliminaries

We start with a few definitions.

We consider a population spread over
N
sites, where each site is occupied by a single individual.

A spatial structure of the population can be captured by a graph (network)
G
that prescribes which pairs of sites neighbor each other.
We consider two possible spatial structures.
A well-mixed population of size
N
is represented by a complete graph
K_{N}
, where every two sites neighbor each other.
The cycle spatial structure is represented by a cycle graph
C_{N}
, where each site has two neighbors (one clockwise, one counter-clockwise).

Initially, some individuals are mutants with relative fitness
r>0
, while the other individuals are residents with fitness 1.

Moran process changes the initial composition of the population as follows.
In each discrete time-step, one individual from the population is selected as a parent (with probability proportional to its fitness), the parent produces an offspring, and the offspring migrates to one of the neighboring sites.

Definition 1

(Oblivious mutants and replacers)
.

We call an individual
oblivious
if its offspring migrates to a neighboring site selected uniformly at random from among all the neighboring sites.
We call an individual a
replacer
if the offspring migrates to a random neighboring site from among those sites that are currently occupied by the other type than the reproducing parent.

In this paper, we consider mutants who are either oblivious or replacers. The residents are always oblivious.

In the absence of mutation, Moran process terminates with the mutants either fixating (that is, spreading over all the sites of the graph
G
), or being wiped out.

Definition 2

(Fixation and elimination probability)
.

We define the (mutant)
fixation probability
as the probability that starting with a single mutant and
N-1
residents, the mutants fixate.
We define the (mutant)
elimination probability
as the probability that starting with
N-1
mutants and a single resident, the mutants get wiped out.

To concisely talk about the fixation and elimination probability of oblivious mutants or replacers, in either the well-mixed population
K_{N}
or the cycle spatial structure
C_{N}
, we employ the following notation.
Note that we consider only two types of graphs
G_{N}
, namely the complete graphs
K_{N}
and the cycle graphs
C_{N}
.

Definition 3
.

Let
G_{N}
be a graph on
N
vertices and let
r>0
be the mutant fitness. Then:

•

\rho_{r}(G_{N})
denotes the fixation probability of a single
oblivious
mutant in graph
G_{N}
against
N-1

oblivious
residents.

•

\rho^{R}_{r}(G_{N})
denotes the fixation probability of a single
replacer
mutant in graph
G_{N}
against
N-1

oblivious
residents.

•

\psi_{r}(G_{N})
denotes the elimination probability of mutants when a single
oblivious
resident starts in graph
G_{N}
against
N-1

oblivious
mutants.

•

\psi^{R}_{r}(G_{N})
denotes the elimination probability of mutants when a single
oblivious
resident starts in graph
G_{N}
against
N-1

replacer
mutants.

In both spatial structures
K_{N}
and
C_{N}
, the current composition of the population can be fully described by the number
i
of mutants.
Thus, we can represent the Moran process as a Markov chain on a set of states labeled from 0 up to
N
, where in each step the number
i
of mutants may change to
i-1
or
i
or
i+1
.
The main text uses a notion of a forward bias. Here we define its reciprocal, the backward bias.

Definition 4

(Backward bias)
.

Let

be the probability of moving from state

to state
i+1
and

p^{-}_{i}
the probability of moving from state
i
to state
i-1

\gamma_{i}=\frac{p^{-}_{i}}{p^{+}_{i}}.

Definition 5
.

Let

be the total fitness of states with
k

.

We will also make use of the following well known result.

Lemma 1

(One-dimensional random walk)
.

Consider a one-dimensional random walk with states

and absorbing boundaries at

and

.
Given
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{N-1}
as in Definition

\rho=\frac{1+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\prod_{k=1}^{j}\gamma_{k}}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\prod_{k=1}^{j}\gamma_{k}}.

The following notation is helpful when stating results for large population sizes

.

Definition 6
.

For functions

of
N
we write

f(N)\approx g(N)\quad\text{if}\quad\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{f(N)}{g(N)}=1,

and

f(N)\sim g(N)

such that

c_{1}\leq\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{f(N)}{g(N)}\leq c_{2}.

It is known that the fixation probability of a single oblivious mutant with fitness

Lemma 2

Let

, then we have

\rho_{r}(C_{N})=\rho_{r}(K_{n})\approx r^{N-1}(1-r)\sim r^{N}.

Proof

It is known that

In this section we compute the exact formulas for fixation probability and elimination probability on complete graphs in the neutral case (

), and derive their asymptotic behaviors.

We first compute the general formula for fixation probability of a single replacer on a complete graph
K_{N}
. We will use this lemma in the proof of

1
and later in

in the proof of

.
The formula involves a term
\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Pois}(r(N-1))\leq N-1)
which is the probability that a random variable distributed according to a Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda=r(N-1)
attains a value which is at most

.

Lemma 3
.

Let
r>0
. For a complete graph
K_{N}
we have

\rho^{R}_{r}(K_{N})=\frac{r^{N-1}(N-1)^{N-1}}{(N-1)!\cdot e^{r(N-1)}\cdot\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Pois}(r(N-1))\leq N-1)}.

Proof
.

The probability of gaining a mutant is the probability of choosing one of the
k
mutants for reproduction and as mutants are smart, they will choose a resident for sure. Therefore
p^{+}_{k}=\frac{k}{T_{k}}\cdot r
.

The probability of losing a mutant is the probability of choosing one of the
N-k
residents for reproduction and then choosing one of the
k
mutants. The probability of choosing a resident for reproduction is
\frac{N-k}{T_{k}}
and as residents are normal, they will choose a mutants with probability
\frac{k}{N-1}
. Hence,
p^{-}_{k}=\frac{N-k}{T_{k}}\cdot\frac{k}{N-1}
.

Then
\gamma_{k}=\frac{p^{-}_{k}}{p^{+}_{k}}=\frac{N-k}{(N-1)\cdot r}
and using
Lemma

1
we have

\rho^{R}_{r}(K_{N})=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\prod_{k=1}^{j}\frac{N-k}{r(N-1)}}=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{(N-1)!}{(N-1-j)!(N-1)^{j}r^{j}}}=\frac{1}{\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{(N-1)!}{(N-1-j)!(N-1)^{j}r^{j}}}=

=\frac{1}{\frac{(N-1)!}{(r(N-1))^{N-1}}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{(r(N-1))^{N-j-1}}{(N-1-j)!}},

and by re-indexing
l=N-1-j
we get that the expression above is equal to

\rho^{R}_{r}(K_{N})=\frac{1}{\frac{(N-1)!}{(r(N-1))^{N-1}}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\frac{(r(N-1))^{l}}{l!}}=\frac{1}{\frac{(N-1)!}{(r(N-1))^{N-1}}e^{r(N-1)}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\frac{(r(N-1))^{l}}{l!}e^{-r(N-1)}}=

=\frac{r^{N-1}(N-1)^{N-1}}{(N-1)!\cdot e^{r(N-1)}\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Pois}(r(N-1))\leq N-1)}.

∎

To understand the asymptotics of the exact formula in

Lemma 4
.

For

\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda)\leq\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1/2}).

Proof
.

From

\mathbb{P}(A_{\lambda}\leq s)=\Phi(\beta)-\frac{\phi(\beta)(\beta^{2}-1)}{6\sqrt{\lambda}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right),

where

is the sum of
\lambda

, number
\beta
is any constant, and
s=\lambda+\beta\sqrt{\lambda}
. Let
\beta=0
. Then
s=\lambda
and
\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda)
can be written as a sum of
\lambda

\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Pois}(\lambda)\leq\lambda)=\Phi(0)-\frac{\phi(0)(-1)}{6\sqrt{\lambda}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{6\sqrt{2\pi\lambda}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)=\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1/2}).

∎

Lemma 5

\psi^{R}_{r}(K_{N})=\frac{1}{e^{r(N-1)}\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Pois}(r(N-1))\leq N-1)}.

Note that the extinction probability of

Advantageuos replacers

We first derive the asymptotic behavior of thefixation probability.

Theorem 8
.

Let
r>1
. For a complete graph
K_{N}
we have

\rho^{R}_{r}(K_{N})\approx 1-\frac{1}{r}.

Proof
.

The probability of gaining a mutant is the probability of choosing one of the
k
mutants for reproduction and as mutants are smart, they will choose a resident for sure. Therefore
p^{+}_{k}=\frac{k}{T_{k}}\cdot r
.

The probability of losing a mutant is the probability of choosing one of the
N-k
residents for reproduction and then choosing one of the
k
mutants. The probability of choosing a resident for reproduction is
\frac{N-k}{T_{k}}
and as residents are normal, they will choose a mutants with probability
\frac{k}{N-1}
. Hence,
p^{-}_{k}=\frac{N-k}{T_{k}}\cdot\frac{k}{N-1}
.

Then
\gamma_{k}=\frac{p^{-}_{k}}{p^{+}_{k}}=\frac{(N-k)\cdot k}{(N-1)\cdot k\cdot r}=(1-\frac{k-1}{N-1})\cdot\frac{1}{r}
. We can use
Lemma

1
to compute the fixation probability.

\rho^{R}_{r}(K_{N})=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\prod_{k=1}^{j}\frac{1}{r}\left(1-\frac{k-1}{N-1}\right)}.

We will prove that the fixation probability converges to
1-\frac{1}{r}
as
N\to\infty
. We will start by showing that the sum converges. To that end, we compare the finite and the infinite sum as follows:

\left|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{j}}\prod_{k=1}^{j}\left(1-\frac{k-1}{N-1}\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{r^{j}}\prod_{k=1}^{j}\left(1-\frac{k-1}{N-1}\right)\right|=

\left|\sum_{j=N}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{j}}\prod_{k=1}^{j}\left(1-\frac{k-1}{N-1}\right)\right|\leq\sum_{j=N}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{j}}=\frac{1}{r^{N}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{r}}.

Note that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{r^{N}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{r}}=0,
therefore the sum converges and

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{r^{j}}\prod_{k=1}^{j}\left(1-\frac{k}{N}\right)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{j}}\prod_{k=1}^{j}\left(1-\frac{k}{N}\right),

as the difference of infinite sum and finite sum converges to
0
.

We use the dominated convergence theorem. Let
f_{N}(j)=\frac{1}{r^{j}}\prod_{k=1}^{j}\left(1-\frac{k}{N}\right)
and
f(j)=\frac{1}{r^{j}}
. We have that
f_{N}(j)\leq f(j)
and
\lim_{N\to\infty}f_{N}(j)=f(j)
for all
j
. By the dominated convergence theorem we have that

1+\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{j}}\prod_{k=1}^{j}\left(1-\frac{k}{N}\right)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{r^{j}}\prod_{k=1}^{j}\left(1-\frac{k}{N}\right)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{j}}=\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{r}}.

Hence
\lim_{N\to\infty}\rho^{R}_{r}(K_{N})=1-\frac{1}{r}
.

∎

from the main text.

5
Matrix Games

Here we describe the connection between the evolutionary dynamics of replacers and the evolutionary games with frequency-dependent selection.

An evolutionary game between two types of individuals
A
and
B
on a spatial structure
G_{N}
is given by a
2\times 2
payoff matrix

M=\bordermatrix{&A&B\cr A&p_{A,A}&p_{A,B}\cr B&p_{B,A}&p_{B,B}}

of real numbers called
payoffs
.
Initially, each site of
G_{N}
is occupied by an individual of type
A
or
B
.
In each step, each individual plays a game with each of its neighbors in
G_{N}
and receives a payoff from each such interaction as prescribed by the matrix. (For instance, a type-
A
individual receives payoff
p_{A,B}
from interaction with a type-
B
individual.)
The fitness of each individual is then defined to be the sum of the payoffs obtained from all its interactions.
Then, one step of the standard Moran process is performed.
This might change the composition of the population. The fitness of each individual is then recomputed using the same payoff matrix, and the steps are repeated until one type takes over the whole structure.

Let
p(M,G_{N})
be the probability that a single individual of type
A
reaches fixation in the evolutionary game with matrix
M
, when played on spatial structure
G_{N}
.

The following theorem formalizes a connection between Moran process with replacers and an evolutionary game with a specific matrix.
Apart from the two spatial structures
K_{N}
and
C_{N}
, it also involves a spatial structure
K^{\prime}_{N}
, where each individual is considered a neighbor all other individuals and also to itself.

Lemma 7
.

Fix a population size
N
and replacer fitness
r>0
. Let
M_{1}=\bordermatrix{&R&O\cr R&r&r\cr O&0&1}

and
M_{2}=\bordermatrix{&R&O\cr R&3r&r\cr O&1&1}.

Then

\rho^{R}_{r}(K^{\prime}_{N})=p(M_{1},K^{\prime}_{N})\quad\text{and}\quad\rho^{R}_{r}(C_{N})=q(M_{2},C_{N}).

Proof.

In each of the two cases, we show that the backward biases in Moran process with replacers are equal to the backward biases in the evolutionary game with the given matrix.

We start with
K_{N}^{\prime}
. In Moran process with replacers we have
p^{+}_{k}=r\cdot\frac{k}{T_{k}}
and
p^{-}_{k}=\frac{N-k}{T_{k}}\cdot\frac{k}{N}.
So the backward bias satisfies

\gamma_{k}=\frac{p_{k}^{-}}{p_{k}^{+}}=\frac{N-k}{T_{k}}\cdot\frac{k}{N}\cdot\frac{1}{r}\cdot\frac{T_{k}}{k}\cdot=\frac{1}{r}\cdot\frac{N-k}{N}.

In the evolutionary game, if there are
k
type
R
individuals, then each type
R
individual receive a total payoff
k\cdot r
from all the type
R
individual, and total payoff
(N-k)\cdot r
from all the type
O
individuals, so the fitness of a single type
R
individual is
k\cdot r+(N-k)\cdot r=N\cdot r
.
Each type
O
individual receives no payoff from all the type
R
individuals and receives total payoff
N-k
from all the type
O
individuals, so the fitness of a single type
O
individual is
N-k
. Using this we can compute the transition probabilities
q_{k}^{+}=\frac{N\cdot r\cdot k}{T_{k}^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{N-k}{N}
and
q_{k}^{-}=\frac{(N-k)\cdot(N-k)}{T_{k}^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{k}{N},
where
T_{k}^{\prime}
is the total fitness in a state with
k
type
R
individuals. Therefore the backward bias in this case is equal to

\gamma_{k}^{\prime}=\frac{q_{k}^{-}}{q_{k}^{+}}=\frac{(N-k)\cdot(N-k)}{T_{k}^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{k}{N}\cdot\frac{T_{k}^{\prime}}{N\cdot r\cdot k}\cdot\frac{N}{N-k}=\frac{1}{r}\cdot\frac{N-k}{N}=\gamma_{k}.

We now show it for
C_{N}
. In Moran process with replacers we have
\gamma_{1}=\frac{1}{r}
and for
k\geq 2
we have
\gamma_{k}=\frac{1}{2r}
.
In the evolutionary game, both type
R
individuals and type
O
individuals form a contiguous block. To find a backward bias, it is enough to focus on individuals at the boundaries of the blocks. If there is a single type
R
individual, he receives total payoff
2\cdot r
from the neighboring type
O
individuals. If there are at least two type
R
individuals in the block, then each of the type
R
individual at the boundary receives payoff
3r+r=4r
from its neighbors. Therefore
q_{1}^{+}=\frac{2r}{T_{1}}
and for
k\geq 2
we have
q_{k}^{+}=\frac{2\cdot 4r}{T_{k}^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=\frac{4r}{T_{k}^{\prime}}
.

Now we focus on the type
O
individuals at boundaries. As all the type
O
individuals get the same payoff from type
R
and type
O
, all the type
O
individuals have fitness
2\cdot 1
. Thus for
k\leq N-2
we have
q_{k}^{-}=\frac{2\cdot 2}{T_{k}^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=\frac{2}{T_{k}}=q_{N-1}^{-}
. So we have, that the backward bias when there is a single type
R
individual is equal to

\gamma_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{q_{1}^{-}}{q_{1}^{+}}=\frac{2}{T_{1}^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{T_{k}^{\prime}}{2r}=\frac{1}{r}=\gamma_{1},

and for
k\geq 2
we have

\gamma_{k}^{\prime}=\frac{q_{k}^{-}}{q_{k}^{+}}=\frac{2}{T^{\prime}_{k}}\cdot\frac{T_{k}^{\prime}}{4r}=\frac{1}{2r}=\gamma_{k},

as we wanted to show.

∎
