Short-term exposure to ambient air pollution and emergency ambulance dispatches for precipitous labor: a time-series study in Guangzhou, China
Authors: Huiyan Xie, Shujun Fan, Tianji Lin, Fangjun Chen, Li Bu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe association between maternal exposure to air pollutants and precipitous labor is still unclear.Emergency ambulance dispatch (EAD) data provide a near real-time, population-wide indicator of acute health exacerbations, enabling the detection of acute population health responses to air quality fluctuations.We examine the relationship between EADs for precipitous labor and short-term exposure to six criteria ambient pollutants using a time-series study. Distributed lag nonlinear models (DLNM) were adopted to explore the impact of air pollutants on precipitous labor emergency ambulance dispatches (PL-EADs) from 2015 to 2024 in Guangzhou, China. In order to clarify the influence of age, season and NDVI greenness on the association, subgroup analysis was carried out. Our findings revealed significant positive associations between short-term exposure to PM2.5, PM10, CO, NO2 and O3 with PL-EADs. NO2 exhibited a statistically significant cumulative lag effect on PL-EADs over the 10-day study period. In the cumulative lag mode, a 10-µg/m3 increase in NO2 was associated with higher risk of precipitous labor with a relative risk of 1.162 (95%CI:1.032,1.309) at lag010.The risk effect of O3 on PL-EADs manifested immediately, followed by a rapid decline. Subgroup analyses revealed that age≥35years, during cold season, and higher NDVI greenness were more susceptible to the adverse impacts of air pollutants. This study provides robust evidence of a significant association between short-term exposure to PM2.5, PM10, CO, NO2 and O3 and PL-EADs.These findings contribute valuable insights into the detrimental effects of air pollution on PL-EADs, which can inform rational allocation and planning of ambulance services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43343-9.Keywords: Air pollution, Emergency ambulance dispatches, Precipitous labor, Health effect, Distributional lag nonlinear modelSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Risk factors

Introduction

Precipitous labor is characterized by the delivery of the fetus within less than three hours from the onset of regular contractions. This condition, recognized as a common obstetric emergency, has been documented to occur with an incidence rate of 3% to 6.8% globally1,2. In China, studies indicate that the incidence rate of precipitous labor ranges from 5.48% to 6.78%3.Several reports have identified adverse effects of precipitous labor on both the mother and the neonate. Maternal complications reported include extensive lacerations of the birth canal, uterine rupture, and postpartum hemorrhage3,4. Perinatal outcomes associated with precipitous labor include low Apgar scores and, in severe cases, neonatal mortality5,6.

Precipitous labor arises from complex, multifaceted pathophysiological factors, including reduced birth canal resistance, excessively strong uterine contractions, and some obstetric complications, such as hypertension2 and placental abruption7.In addition to these established clinical risk factors, emerging evidence suggests that external environmental stressors, particularly ambient air pollution, may act as modifiable triggers or exacerbating factors, potentially influencing the regulation of labor onset and progression8.Evidence from epidemiological studies highlights an association between air pollution and adverse birth outcomes, mainly focusing on preterm birth, stillbirth, and low birthweight9.Association between short-term exposure to nitrogen dioxide (NO2), nitrogen oxide (NO)10, aerodynamic diameter of particulate matter less than or equal to 2.5 μm (PM2.5), aerodynamic diameter of particulate matter less than or equal to 10 μm (PM10), Carbon monoxide (CO) and sulfur dioxide (SO2)11 and the risk of prelabor rupture of membranes was found in Spain and China12,13.Some studies found that ozone (O3) exposure significantly increased the risk of preeclampsia14.There is a lack of studies investigating the impact of ambient pollutants on precipitous labor. Existing studies on pollution and birth outcomes have primarily employed cohort or case-control designs to assess chronic or trimester-specific exposures, often with limited sample sizes or focusing on a narrow set of pollutants15,16. There is a critical lack of investigation into the acute, short-term effects of air pollution—captured by same-day or lagged exposures of several days—which are more relevant to triggering an immediate event like precipitous labor.

Numerous studies have been conducted to examine the relationship between emergency ambulance dispatches (EADs) and exposure to air pollution, categorizing these as acute health events17.The majority of existing research has focused on the correlation between air pollutants and EADs associated with all-cause, cardiovascular, and respiratory diseases17,18.However, there is a paucity of studies investigating the link between precipitous labor emergency ambulance dispatches (PL-EADs) and air pollutant exposure. Precipitous labor represents a significant aspect of obstetric ambulance dispatch services. Our data indicate that precipitous labor cases made up 71.3% (35352/49582) of obstetric emergency dispatches in Guangzhou from 2015 to 2024.This condition not only presents substantial health risks to both mothers and newborns but also challenges the emergency response capabilities of medical teams.

This study analyzed the data of EADs in Guangzhou, China, from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2024. We employed distributional lag nonlinear model (DLNM) to examine the relationship between EADs due to precipitous labor and short-term exposure to six criteria ambient pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, O3, and CO).To examine risk stratification across subgroups, we further assessed effect modifications by age, season and greenness.

Methods

Research area

Guangzhou, a coastal megacity in South China, serves as the capital of Guangdong Province. The region has a humid subtropical climate, featuring hot, humid summers with significant rainfall due to the East Asian monsoon, and mild, relatively dry winters. The annual average temperature is 22 °C. As a pivotal economic hub, Guangzhou boasts a massive population, with its metropolitan area being home to over 15 million residents according to recent census data. Historically, its rapid industrialization and urban expansion have led to significant environmental challenges. The city has faced issues related to air pollution due to emissions from manufacturing, vehicular exhaust, and energy production.

Research population

The data of EADs from January 1, 2015 to December 31, 2024 were obtained from Guangzhou emergency center.Each emergency ambulance was staffed with at least one professional, and records contained essential details including the date of attendance, patient gender and age, chief complaint with preliminary diagnosis, and the emergency center’s name. The preliminary diagnosis was determined following the collection of medical history, physical examination, and further assessments by the doctor.The EADs data included details such as the ambulance attendance date, address, gender, age, chief complaint, diagnosis, and emergency center name.The study included 601,654 EADs. Considering data completeness, emergency medical data from Guangzhou’s six central districts—Tianhe, Yuexiu, Baiyun, Liwan, Huangpu, and Haizhu—were selected. These districts exhibit significant socio-economic diversity and encompass the central urban area of the city. Together, they have a combined permanent resident population of approximately 11.5 million, which constitutes about 61.2% of Guangzhou’s total permanent population. Precipitous labor was classified using the 10th Revision of International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), coding as O62.3. The collected precipitous labor EADs (PL-EADs) data were cleaned and put into daily-scale time series data. Samples excluded cases with unclear diagnosis, missing key information (date/age/address) and dispatch address was not within six central districts and the study ultimately included 35,352 cases of PL-EADs. The flowchart of data collection and case inclusion is presented in Figure S1.

Pollutants and meteorological data

Data on daily ambient pollution levels were obtained from 11 national-level air quality monitoring stations in Guangzhou. For this study, we extracted data from 8 stations located within the six central study districts, ensuring spatial coverage across all research areas (Fig. 1). The concentrations of particulate matter with aerodynamic diameters ≤ 2.5 μm (PM2.5) and ≤ 10 μm (PM10), sulfur dioxide (SO2), carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and ozone (O3) were obtained. The 24-hour average concentrations for each pollutant from 2015 to 2024 were calculated and consolidated into a 10-year daily time-series data. Previous studies have emphasized the need to control for weather conditions as confounding factors19,20. Therefore, atmospheric data from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2024, were obtained from the Guangzhou Meteorological station, and the daily mean temperature, relative humidity, surface pressure, total precipitation, sunshine duration and wind speed were derived by averaging the monitoring points in the data.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed on the data, encompassing the mean daily PL-EADs cases, the overall population count, the average daily concentration of each pollutant and meteorological factor. A possible correlation was revealed between daily ambient pollution, atmospheric factors and daily precipitous labor EADs through time series graphs and scatter plots. The relationship between the factors was analyzed using the Spearman test.

A distributed lag nonlinear model (DLNM) using a generalized additive model (GAM) with Poisson distribution (to account for potential overdispersion in daily PL-EADs) was employed to analyze the relationship between air pollution exposure and PL-EADs. Utilizing bidimensional cross-basis functions, a DLNM can concurrently assess nonlinear exposure–response relationships and delayed effects21. We performed different maximum lag days (from 7 to 14), and the maximum lag of 10 days was chosen based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC)22. Single-lag models (lag 0 to 10) and moving average lag models (lag 01 to 010) were employed to investigate lag effect characteristics. Pollutant concentrations at a relative risk (RR) of 1 served as the reference point for estimating other RRs. Ambient pollution exposures were quantified as increments of 10 µg/m3 for PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and O3, 1 mg/m3 for CO, and 5 µg/m3 for SO2.

The results were adjusted for time trends, meteorological conditions, day of the week, and the impact of public holidays.A natural cubic spline with 7 degrees of freedom per year was applied to control for long-term and seasonal trends, determined by the minimum AIC assessment. To control for potential confounding by meteorological conditions, the main models included daily mean temperature and relative humidity. Other collected meteorological variables (surface pressure, total precipitation, sunshine duration, and wind speed) were initially considered but were not included in the main model specification to avoid overfitting and collinearity among some of these factors. The potential influence of these additional variables was rigorously assessed in subsequent sensitivity analyses (see the Sensitivity Analyses section). Additionally, a binary variable was included to control for holiday effects, distinguishing between holidays (1) such as New Year’s Day, the Spring Festival, the Qing Ming festival, Labor Day, the Dragon-Boat Festival, the Mid-Autumn Festival, and National Day, and non-holidays (0).

We modeled the concentration-response function using a natural cubic spline to account for potential nonlinear relationships and a natural cubic spline for the lag-response dimension, with knots placed at equally spaced intervals on the log scale of lags. The optimized parameters for models of different groups and model diagnostics are summarized in Table 1. The autocorrelation coefficients at all lags in the ACF plots fall within the confidence bounds which is corroborated by the results of Breusch-Godfrey test (Figure S2). These results jointly indicate the absence of significant residual autocorrelation, supporting the adequacy of the model specification. This study follows these specific modeling steps:

Subgroup analyses

According to previous studies, we conducted subgroup analyses after identifying the lag days with the greatest effect stratified by age (< 35y,≥35y)23, season (cold season: from November to April, warm season: from May to October)12 and NDVI greenness24.Greenness was measured as the 16-d mean normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) from the 250-m resolution monthly Terra Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) data set (https://modis.gsfc.nasa.gov/data/dataprod/mod13.php).NDVI were dichotomized based on their median values. Z-tests were conducted on these stratified groups to assess the statistical significance of the observed differences.

Sensitivity analyses

A two-pollutant model test was implemented to evaluate the robustness of our findings by incorporating cross-basis functions of additional pollutants, extending beyond the single-pollutant model. Z-tests were employed to examine the differences between the nested single-pollutant and dual-pollutant models. Additionally, we sequentially incorporated additional meteorological variables—namely, surface pressure, total precipitation, sunshine duration, and wind speed—into the model, employing the same natural cubic spline approach used for mean temperature and relative humidity. Subsequently, we assessed their impact on the primary effect estimates. We also extended the lag period of pollutant exposures to 14 days to observe the lag–response structures.

Our study dataset spanned the period from 1 January 2015 to 31 December 2024, a timeframe that fully encompasses the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic in China in early 2020. To rigorously assess whether the pandemic exerted a measurable impact on PL-EADs, we employed interrupted time series (ITS) analysis—a quasi-experimental design well-suited for evaluating population-level health interventions.ITS is a widely recognized method in public health research, with established applications in evaluating the effects of policy changes and public health measures25,26.

In the present study, we conceptualized the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic as a natural “intervention” event. Using ITS analysis, we sought to identify whether the pandemic was associated with significant changes in PL-EADs, as specified in the following model:

Yt = β 0 + β 1 time + β 2 covidt + β 3(time-T0) covidt + ε t​.

Where β1 is the change in PL-EADs associated with each unit increase in time (representing the underlying pre-covid trend), β2 is the level change following the covid, and β3 is the slope change post-covid (with T0 as the time of covid-19 outbreak).Newey‑West standard errors with a lag of 7 days were used to account for autocorrelation in the daily time series.

Data analyses were conducted using R statistical software (v4.5.1; R Development Core Team).Reported RRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) with two-sided tests at α = 0.05.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically assessed the impact of six common air pollutants—PM10, PM2.5, CO, NO2, SO2, and O3—on PL-EADs. We also conducted a detailed analysis of how age, season and greenness influence these associations. Our findings indicated significant positive associations between short-term exposure to PM2.5, PM10, CO, NO2, and O3 and PL-EADs. These associations generally remained significant after additional adjustments for copollutants in two-pollutant models. The relationships between air pollutants and PL-EADs varied according to age, season, and NDVI greenness. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study examining the relationship between air pollutants and precipitous labor, thereby significantly contributing to the understanding of how environmental factors may influence labor outcomes.This study fills a critical gap in the literature by providing evidence that air pollution could potentially accelerate the onset of labor, leading to precipitous labor.

Numerous studies have identified positive links between air pollutant exposure and adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as preterm birth, stillbirth, prelabor rupture of membranes, and low birthweight11,12,23,27,28.No study has specifically examined the associations between air pollution and precipitous labor.In recent years, EADs have emerged as a promising indicator in the field of environmental health research, attracting significant attention from numerous relevant studies29.In a study on the relationship between air pollutants and cause-specific emergency ambulance dispatches in Shanghai30, researchers found a certain degree of correlation between emergency ambulance dispatches related to gynecology diseases and PM2.5, CO, NO2, and O3 throughout the 3-day study period.We obtained similar results using models with a lag of up to 10 days; however, we have also detected a significant risk of PM10 to PL-EADs.According to recent studies, exposure to PM10 is associated with a higher risk of adverse birth outcomes.A study conducted in Paris found a strong link between PM10 exposure and both preterm birth rates and infant mortality31.

Among all six air pollutants, NO2 sustained a statistically significant cumulative lag effect on PL-EADs throughout the 10-days study period. This suggests that NO2 exhibit long-term impacts on PL-EADs. Research indicates a strong correlation between NO2 exposure and adverse pregnancy outcomes, particularly when high concentrations are encountered during gestation. A meta-analysis of cohort studies indicates that in Asian and European countries, each 1 µg/m3 increase in NO2 is associated with a higher risk of preterm birth, as estimated by OR and HR models16. However, there have been no reports on the long-lag effects of NO2 on adverse pregnancy outcomes, which requires further research.For ozone (O3), the risk effect on PL-EADs was significant on the day of exposure (lag0), followed by a rapid decline. The impact of O3 exposure on adverse birth outcomes remains inconsistent across studies. A case-control cross-sectional study conducted in California identified a significant association between short-term O3 exposure and an increased risk of stillbirth, particularly when exposure occurred four days post-delivery32. Conversely, other studies have found no significant correlation between O3 exposure and adverse birth outcomes11. It has been proposed that O3 exposure during early pregnancy may impair maternal uterine artery and cardiac function, which is associated with heightened risks of gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, and perinatal cardiomyopathy.

A suggested biological mechanism through which air pollution may lead to adverse pregnancy outcomes is the reduction in transplacental oxygen and nutrient transport33. Air pollution exposure might related to perturbed DNA transcription, which could impaired placental function34. Exposure to ambient pollutants is recognized for causing systemic inflammation and oxidative stress35,36. Inflammatory factors (such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β) are known to be powerful signals that can stimulate prostaglandin synthesis37,38. Prostaglandins are critical substances responsible for initiating and maintaining uterine contractions39. Therefore, systemic and placental inflammation may disrupt the normal rhythm of uterine contractions, potentially increasing the risk of precipitous labor.

Subgroup analyses indicated that the detrimental effects of NO2 exposure on PL-EADs were more pronounced in cases of advanced maternal age (≥ 35 years). This age group is associated with an increased risk of adverse delivery outcomes, a relationship that has been substantiated by previous research40–42. However, it is important to acknowledge that the heightened risk of precipitous labor in older pregnant women may be partially attributed to the fact that a significant proportion of women of advanced maternal age are multiparous—for instance, reaching 62.3% in Beijing43, which is a known risk factor for precipitous labor2 and could potentially modify susceptibility to air pollution. We found that the associations of CO with PL-EADs were stronger in the cold season. This finding is consistent with a previous study on the relationship between air pollution and preterm birth, which was also conducted in Guangdong Province44. Numerous studies have identified a correlation between extreme low temperatures and adverse birth outcomes45. Low temperatures may induce oxidative stress, affecting placental growth and physiology by reducing blood flow and activating neuroendocrine and inflammatory systems to release various factors46, potentially leading to precipitous labor. Unexpectedly, we observed a stronger positive correlation between CO and PL-EADs in mothers residing in areas with higher NDVI greenness. While urban green spaces are generally associated with health benefits, evidence regarding their impact on pregnancy outcomes remains inconsistent47,48. As this is the first study examining air pollution’s effect on precipitous labor, no prior studies were available for comparison. Further research is needed to understand how residential greenness influences the impact of air pollutants on precipitous labor.

This study possesses three unique strengths that enhance the importance of the reported observations. We initially examined EADs as endpoint events, addressing a gap in precipitous labor research. Analyzing EADs allows for the strategic adjustment of ambulance numbers and distribution based on varying demand across times and locations, ensuring effective coverage and response capability. Additionally, these findings can guide resource allocation and service optimization based on local conditions by identifying populations most impacted by air pollution and environmental health issues. The extended 10-year study period enhanced our capacity to analyze the periodicity of sudden labor events and various ambient pollutants, supported by a substantial sample size of 35,352 EAD cases.

This study acknowledges several limitations. Firstly, as a population-based time-series analysis, we utilized spatially averaged daily pollutant concentrations from eight monitoring stations to represent the exposure for all precipitous labor cases within the study area. This ecological exposure assessment is a standard approach for studying acute effects but inherently introduces exposure misclassification. It is well-established that such non-differential exposure misclassification typically biases effect estimates toward the null. Therefore, our reported associations between short-term air pollution exposure and PL-EADs are likely conservative, and the true effects might be stronger than observed. Future studies with access to detailed residential coordinates or personal monitoring data would be valuable to refine exposure estimates and confirm our findings. Secondly, our attempt to explore socioeconomic differences using district-level per capital disposable income (PCDI) was limited by its ecological nature and showed no significant effect modification; thus, more refined individual socioeconomic indicators are warranted in future research.Thirdly, due to the nature of the emergency ambulance dispatch records, we lacked individual-level clinical data on gestational age and parity. These unmeasured factors could be potential confounders or effect modifiers. This limitation may impact the interpretation of the relationship between air pollutants and precipitous labor in this study. Finally, pregnant women in late pregnancy spend more time indoors, which may lead to a discrepancy between individual actual exposure levels and outdoor monitoring concentrations, resulting in non-differential exposure misclassification. The true association between personal exposure to these pollutants and the risk of precipitous labor might be stronger than what we reported. Despite this limitation, outdoor monitoring concentrations remain a valid population-level exposure proxy in environmental epidemiology, and our population-level time-series analysis can still reveal the macro association between air pollution and PL-EADs. Future research will combine individual exposure monitoring data (e.g., portable air quality monitors) to improve the accuracy of exposure assessment.

Conclusion

This study presents compelling evidence of a significant association between short-term exposure to PM2.5, PM10, CO, NO2, and O3 and PL-EADs. Notably, NO2 exhibited a statistically significant cumulative lag effect on PL-EADs over the 10-day study period. The risk effect of O3 on PL-EADs manifested immediately, followed by a rapid decline. Subgroup analyses indicated that individuals aged 35 years and older, those exposed during the cold season, and those in areas with higher NDVI greenness were more susceptible to the adverse impacts of air pollutants. The results can inform evidence-based strategies for rational allocation and pre-planning of obstetric ambulance services, particularly in regions with high air pollution levels.
