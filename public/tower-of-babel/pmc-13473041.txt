Isolated brainstem death: case presentation demonstrating preserved supratentorial perfusion on 99mTc-bicisate brain scintigraphy
Authors: Ghazal Shadmani, Amin Haghighat Jahromi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe diagnosis of brain death (BD) is primarily clinical and usually straightforward when both supratentorial and infratentorial structures are irreversibly injured. In contrast, isolated catastrophic posterior fossa lesions—particularly those involving the brainstem—are uncommon and may demonstrate preserved supratentorial perfusion or cortical electrical activity on ancillary testing, even in the absence of brainstem function. This apparent dissociation is typically transient and invariably progresses to global cerebral circulatory arrest and death. Few reports have documented the natural evolution of this phenomenon, highlighting the rarity of such cases and their uniformly poor outcome.Case presentationA 47-year-old woman with no known history of chronic seizure disorder presented in an obtunded state. On arrival, she experienced continuous seizures and the clinical status rapidly deteriorated. Initial head CT scan without contrast revealed basilar artery thrombosis. She received intravenous tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and levetiracetam. She underwent mechanical thrombectomy with partial recanalization (TICI 2b). Despite intervention, neurologic examination remained poor with absent spontaneous respiration and brain stem reflexes. Subsequent non contrast head CT scan demonstrated worsening brain stem edema and obstructive hydrocephalus. Due to poor clinical status, a 99mTc-bicisate brain perfusion scan was performed which demonstrated absent posterior fossa perfusion with preserved supratentorial tracer uptake. Due to this preserved supratentorial perfusion, life support was continued. Follow-up head CT scan without contrast revealed progression to diffuse cerebral edema, and massive trans tentorial and tonsillar herniation. The patient was later clinically declared brain dead. Organ donation followed in accordance with her previously expressed wishes.ConclusionsThis is a rare case of isolated posterior fossa injury with preserved supratentorial perfusion compatible with “brainstem death”. The case highlights the importance of ancillary testing in uncommon supra/infra tentorial dissociation to differentiate brain death from brainstem death.

Background

The fundamental concept of BD involves a comatose state, loss of all cranial nerve function, and the irreversible loss of spontaneous respiration. Before making the diagnosis, it is crucial to identify the underlying disorder responsible for brain injury and to rule out confounders and reversible causes of coma and apnea, such as drug intoxication, hypothermia, acute metabolic disturbances, and neuromuscular disorders (Varelas and Lewis 2016; Rizvi et al. 2018). BD is primarily a clinical diagnosis, and when all the criteria are met, the patient is considered legally deceased (Wijdicks 2010). Brain perfusion scintigraphy is an established adjunctive test for the determination of BD, particularly in patients in whom clinical examination or EEG is unreliable or cannot be performed. Using either lipophilic tracers (Tc-99 m bicisate), which assess both cerebral and posterior fossa perfusion, or non-lipophilic agents (Tc-99 m DTPA) for radionuclide angiography, the study evaluates cerebral blood flow at the bedside. Demonstration of absent intracranial perfusion is highly specific and associated with a grave prognosis, providing a practical alternative to invasive cerebral angiography. Two anatomical approaches to determine death by neurologic criteria are recognized globally: whole-brain death and brainstem death (Shemie et al. 2023; Greer et al. 2023). Whole-brain death is typically applied in scenarios such as (Varelas and Lewis 2016) large supratentorial lesions (e.g., ischemia, trauma, or hemorrhage) causing progressive transtentorial herniation with eventual brainstem dysfunction, and (Rizvi et al. 2018) diffuse brain injury, such as post-cardiac arrest hypoxia, affecting both supratentorial and infratentorial structures. In these situations, and in the absence of confounders, brain death can generally be determined based on a thorough neurologic examination confirming deep coma, absent cranial nerve reflexes, and apnea (Walter et al. 2018). A distinct clinical situation arises in patients with isolated fata infratentorial lesions, particularly affecting the brainstem. In these cases, patients may lose all brainstem reflexes while retaining functional supratentorial structures. Ancillary s—including electroencephalography (EEG), visual evoked potentials (VEP), or brain perfusion scintigraphy—may demonstrate preserved cortical activity or supratentorial perfusion, even when clinical criteria for BD are fulfilled. Isolated fatal brainstem lesions resulting in a brain-death-like state are rare. In a series of 161 BD cases, Varela et al. reported only three with isolated posterior fossa injury, accounting for a 1.9% prevalence (Varelas et al. 2017). Such lesions can result from basilar artery thrombosis, subarachnoid hemorrhage, massive trauma, or complications of posterior fossa surgery (Zwarts and Kornips 2001; Ogata and Imakita 1988). Although the concept of BD is relatively consistent internationally, the criteria and use of ancillary ing vary across countries. For example, in the UK and some U.S. states, a combination of coma, confirmed apnea, and absent brainstem reflexes is sufficient to declare BD regardless of lesion location. However, reports of patients with isolated infratentorial injury who retain supratentorial function have prompted consideration of guideline-based ancillary ing to confirm the diagnosis (Ferbert et al. 1986; Darby et al. 1987). The debate continues as to whether the definition of BD should require death of the entire brain or only the brainstem (Walter et al. 2018; Machado 2022). The 2023 international consensus guidelines emphasize careful evaluation in primary infratentorial injury and recommend ancillary testing when portions of the clinical examination cannot fully confirm brain death (Shemie et al. 2023; Greer et al. 2023). Here, we present the clinical course, imaging, and outcome of a patient with basilar artery thrombosis, absent brainstem reflexes, and preserved supratentorial perfusion on brain scintigraphy, highlighting a rare presentation in line with contemporary guideline recommendations.

Case presentation

A 47-year-old woman was transported to the emergency department in an obtunded state following the sudden onset of right facial droop and aphasia. Her history was notable for one week of migraines and recent heavy pipe tobacco use ‘all day.’ She had no prior history of chronic seizures.

Upon arrival, the patient experienced continuous seizure activity and her clinical status rapidly decompensated, requiring emergent intubation. Her National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score was estimated at 31. A non-contrast head CT revealed a dense basilar artery, suggestive of thrombosis (Fig. 1A). Her family confirmed that the last time she was seen well was approximately three hours prior, she received intravenous tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and levetiracetam.

CT angiography (CTA) demonstrated distal basilar artery occlusion extending into the right P1 segment (Fig. 1B). She underwent mechanical thrombectomy, achieving Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (TICI) 2b reperfusion after two passes.

The patient was admitted to the neuro-intensive care unit (ICU) following the procedure. Due to poor awakening from anesthesia, a follow-up head CT without contrast (Fig. 1C) was obtained, which revealed increased brainstem edema and obstructive hydrocephalus, with decreased size of the fourth ventricle and enlargement of the third and lateral ventricles. Upon neurosurgical evaluation, she was deemed not a candidate for further intervention due to a poor neurological exam and recent administration of tPA.

Subsequent head CT without contrast demonstrated evolving edema and infarction involving the bilateral cerebellar hemispheres, brainstem, and right occipital lobe. Additionally, there was widespread cerebral sulci effacement with preserved gray-white matter differentiation of the frontal, parietal, and anterior temporal lobes. Her clinical condition continued to deteriorate. Neurologic examination revealed a comatose patient who was unresponsive with eyes closed and no spontaneous opening. Pupils were bilaterally dilated at 6 mm and nonreactive. Brainstem reflexes, including corneal responses, horizontal oculocephalic movements, cough, and gag reflexes, were absent. There were no spontaneous movements of the extremities, nor responses to noxious stimulation. The seriousness of the patient’s condition and poor prognosis were discussed with her family members, and the possibility of organ donation was discussed with the family, who confirmed this aligned with the patient’s previously expressed wishes. A brain perfusion study using 99mTc-bicisate was obtained (Fig. 2). Anterior cerebral radionuclide angiogram and 20 min delayed static images demonstrated complete absence of perfusion to the posterior fossa while cortical perfusion in both cerebral hemispheres was preserved. From a medicolegal standpoint, the preserved supratentorial perfusion precluded declaration of brain death at that time, and life-sustaining treatment was continued. A follow-up head CT without contrast the next day (Fig. 3) demonstrated extensive cytotoxic edema involving the brainstem, bilateral cerebellar hemispheres, and occipital lobes, along with signs of upper transtentorial and descending tonsillar herniation and progressive obstructive hydrocephalus. Later that day, the patient was clinically declared brain dead. No additional imaging was performed. Her liver and kidneys were successfully recovered for transplantation.

Preserved supratentorial perfusion in the absence of infratentorial perfusion has been previously described in patients with isolated posterior fossa lesions. A similar pattern has also been reported in a case of widespread subarachnoid hemorrhage (Schmidt and Schraml 2017). However, the clinical significance of supratentorial perfusion in the setting of severe brainstem damage remains unclear. In previously reported cases, patients were ultimately declared brain dead within a few days, with or without repeated imaging.

The proposed mechanism for the inevitable supratentorial tissue injury includes aqueductal obstruction due to progressive brainstem edema, leading to hydrocephalus and gradual loss of supratentorial perfusion. In addition, collapse of the vein of Galen due to sustained intracranial pressure may further impair venous outflow, eventually overwhelming the brain’s ability to autoregulate.

This case illustrates the distinct clinical scenario of isolated catastrophic posterior fossa injury with preserved supratentorial perfusion on brain scintigraphy. While we did not obtain serial perfusion studies to document the temporal course in this patient, prior reports suggest that such preservation is often transient and uniformly associated with poor outcomes (Varelas et al. 2017). The 2023 international consensus guideline on brain death/death by neurologic criteria (BD/DNC) highlights the need for caution in cases of primary infratentorial injury and supports the use of ancillary testing when diagnostic uncertainty arises. Our case contributes to the limited literature by emphasizing the importance of recognizing this pattern, applying guideline-based ancillary evaluation, and ensuring accurate and timely brain death determination with implications for ethical decision-making and organ donation.

Conclusion

This case highlights isolated brainstem injury with initially preserved supratentorial perfusion. Despite this finding, the patient progressed to clinical brain death. The 2023 international consensus guideline on brain death/death by neurologic criteria emphasizes the need for caution in cases of primary infratentorial injury and supports the use of ancillary testing when diagnostic uncertainty exists.
