Persistent functionalized triarylmethyl radicals: when spin meets chirality
Authors: Imma Ratera, Jaume Veciana, Paula Mayorga-Burrezo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For decades, persistent trityl radical derivatives have been the object of intense research in a wide variety of disciplines, ranging from fundamental to applied science, thanks to the versatility of their properties (magnetic, optical, redox, etc.) and their potential as multifunctional open-shell building blocks. Nonetheless, far from becoming less appealing after such extensive study, these propeller-shaped radicals are nowadays gaining momentum thanks to their intrinsic chirality, which arises from the clockwise or counterclockwise torsion of their aryl rings. Indeed, increasing attention is being devoted to the implications of combining this feature with other properties, such as magnetism, enabling applications such as EPR probes, spin-dependent electronic transport through the CISS effect, and quantum technologies. From an optical perspective, their performance as circularly polarized light emitters has emerged as one of the most promising research directions. In this review, we present the advances made in understanding and exploiting the handedness of trityl-based radicals, from tetrathiatriarylmethyl radicals to halogen-shielded derivatives. We will cover the evolution from intrinsic propeller chirality to more complex diastereomeric systems resulting from their functionalization with additional chiral centers, axes, and planes, as well as the incorporation of additional propeller units into multipropeller architectures or their involvement in chiral supramolecular assemblies. Finally, we will discuss how these advances are shaping emerging applications.

Introduction

The discovery of persistent organic radicals has expanded the field of molecular materials by demonstrating that open-shell molecules can combine chemical stability with electronic, magnetic and optical functionalities that are not possible with conventional closed-shell compounds. Unlike transient radical species, persistent radicals retain an unpaired electron under ambient conditions owing to a careful balance between kinetic protection and electronic delocalization, enabling their integration in functional molecular materials. During the last two decades, this class of compounds have emerged as a versatile platform for applications ranging from molecular magnetism and organic electronics to spintronics, quantum technologies, bioimaging and sensing. Their singly occupied molecular orbital (SOMO), together with their unique excited-state electronic structure, provides opportunities for charge transport, spin manipulation and doublet emission that have no direct counterpart in traditional π-conjugated materials.1–6

Among persistent organic radicals, triarylmethyl (trityl) radicals occupy a privileged position due to their exceptional chemical versatility and highly tunable physicochemical properties using synthetic tools. Since Gomberg's pioneering report of the triphenylmethyl radical in 1900,7 molecular design strategies have progressively transformed these initially reactive species into robust open-shell platforms by introducing sterically protecting substituents around the radical center. This strategy has given rise to different families of persistent triarylmethyl radicals, including tetrathiatriarylmethyl (TAM) radicals and halogenated ones, like perchlorotriphenylmethyl (PTM), tris(2,4,6-trichlorophenyl)methyl (TTM), and tris(2,4,6-tribromophenyl)methyl (TTBrM) derivatives. These radicals have become reference systems in electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) and widely used as efficient polarizing agents for dynamic nuclear polarization (DNP), molecular electronics, organic light-emitting materials and spin-based technologies. These applications illustrate the extraordinary adaptability of the trityl scaffold to the structural and electronical characteristics required across different scientific disciplines.8–13

In parallel, with the rapid development of radical-based functional materials, chirality has become one of the central design principles in chemistry and materials science. Beyond the classical importance of stereochemistry in biological recognition, molecular chirality has recently acquired renewed relevance due to its intimate relationship with spin-dependent phenomena. Indeed, the discovery of the chirality-induced spin selectivity (CISS) effect demonstrated that chiral molecules can preferentially transport electrons of one spin orientation, establishing a direct connection between molecular handedness and electron spin polarization. This breakthrough has stimulated intense research efforts toward the development of chiral molecular systems capable of combining optical activity, spin filtering, magnetic response and circularly polarized luminescence (CPL). Consequently, the convergence of chirality and open-shell electronic structures has emerged as a very attractive research direction for designing multifunctional materials at the interface between photonics, spintronics and quantum information technology.14–17

Triarylmethyl radicals are especially well suited to exploit this interplay because chirality is already intrinsically embedded within their molecular architecture. The propeller-like arrangement due to hindered rotation of the three aryl rings around the central sp2 carbon generates two enantiomeric conformations – atropisomers – commonly denoted as Plus (P) and Minus (M). Although these conformers rapidly interconvert in simple trityl radicals through low-energy ring flipping processes, appropriate steric engineering can substantially increase the racemization barrier and even afford conformationally persistent enantiomers, which can be separated and, in a few cases, even isolated showing long lifetimes of years. More importantly, chemical functionalization may extend the stereochemical complexity beyond intrinsic propeller chirality. The incorporation of stereogenic centers, chiral axes, planar chiral motifs or supramolecular organization introduces additional levels of structural asymmetry, leading to a plethora of stable diastereomeric radicals and hierarchical chiral architectures.18–21 These advances have considerably enriched the stereochemical landscape of persistent triarylmethyl radicals while simultaneously opening new opportunities to control their electronic structure, magnetic interactions and excited-state properties.

The significance of chirality in trityl radicals extends well beyond structural considerations. In recent years, a growing number of studies have demonstrated that stereochemistry can directly influence the physical properties of these radicals. In biological applications, chirality has been exploited to modulate exchange interactions in biradicals designed for dynamic nuclear polarization, while maintaining the excellent EPR characteristics of TAM radicals.22 In molecular electronics, enantiopure PTM derivatives have been investigated as potential spin-selective selective molecular junctions inspired by the CISS effect.23 More recently, the incorporation of chiral structural elements into trityl radicals has enabled the development of intrinsically chiral doublet emitters displaying circularly polarized luminescence, a rapidly expanding field with promising technological implications for advanced displays, optical communication, sensing and quantum photonics.24,25 Furthermore, chiral diradicals have recently attracted attention as potential molecular platforms for quantum technologies, where stereochemistry can influence spin coupling and zero-field splitting, two key parameters governing quantum coherence and information processing.10,26–29 Recent comprehensive reviews have highlighted the remarkable progress achieved in persistent organic π-radicals, their stabilization strategies and multifunctional properties, including luminescent radical emitters and doublet-state photophysics. However, these contributions have primarily focused on radical chemistry, photophysics or optoelectronic applications rather than on the emergence of chirality in triarylmethyl radicals.6,13,17

Despite the remarkable advances achieved in this field, studies on the chirality of triarylmethyl radicals remain highly fragmented, having largely evolved independently across synthetic organic chemistry, stereochemistry, molecular magnetism, spintronics, supramolecular chemistry, photophysics and optoelectronics. However, none has specifically focused on chiral triarylmethyl radicals by integrating molecular design, stereochemistry, spin-related phenomena, chiroptical properties, and multifunctional applications into a unified framework. Such a comprehensive perspective is particularly timely in view of the rapid emergence of configurationally persistent radical emitters, chiral spin systems, supramolecular radical assemblies, and the growing interest in their potential for sensing, spintronics, bioimaging, and quantum technologies.

In this Review, we present an overview of chirality in triarylmethyl radical derivatives from a materials chemistry perspective. We first discuss the molecular design strategies that govern the conformational persistence of trityl radicals and the influence of chemical functionalization on their stereochemical behavior and how these issues impact on their physicochemical properties. Then, we propose a classification of chiral trityl radicals according to the nature of the chiral element involved, encompassing from the intrinsic propeller chirality, the diastereomeric systems incorporating stereogenic centers, chiral axes or planar chirality, and supramolecular chiral assemblies. We then examine how chirality influences spin-related properties, including magnetic exchange interactions, spin transport, EPR performance and emerging quantum applications. Finally, we discuss the rapidly growing field of intrinsically chiral radical emitters, highlighting the molecular design principles that govern circular dichroism and circularly polarized luminescence in open-shell systems, as well as current challenges and future opportunities. By bringing together these different research directions, this Review aims to establish a unique picture of chirality in triarylmethyl radicals and to provide molecular design guidelines for the next generation of multifunctional chiral open-shell materials (Scheme 1).

Molecular design of persistent trityl radicals: the impact of the chemical functionalization on chirality

The triarylmethyl (trityl) radical presents a C3 symmetry arising from the disposition of the aromatic rings, that are twisted out of the reference plane defined by the three (ipso) carbon atoms bonded to the central sp2 hybridized carbon. This spatial configuration gives rise to two possible enantiomeric forms, Plus (P) or Minus (M), depending on the right- or left-handed propeller-like torsion, respectively (Scheme 1B). Both enantiomers readily interconvert through ring-flip mechanisms, in which a low-energy barrier is overcome for the rotation of the aryl groups, resulting in a racemic mixture at room temperature. Steric factors, such as bulky substituents on the aromatic rings, are needed to hinder such rotations, obtaining conformationally persistent molecular propellers, preventing the reversibility of the helicity as well as the typical dimerization of trityl radicals that kills their open-shell character.7 Partially driven by the vast potential of the trityl radical modification to generate conformational persistent and versatile building blocks for multifunctional materials with magnetic, optical, and redox properties, organic chemists in the last century rapidly developed efficient strategies to obtain sterically hindered persistent open-shell structures through side-ring substitution. As a result, numerous trityl radical derivatives bearing functional groups of varying size and steric demand at the terminal positions of the aromatic rings have been reported. Importantly, the substitution pattern plays a decisive role in determining both the racemization barriers and the flip mechanisms governing the interconversion between enantiomeric pairs.30 Mislow et al.31 carried out a detailed topological analysis of isomerization in propeller-like structures with restricted rotation of aryl rings, elucidating the different matrices associated with the number of rings involved in each rotational (flip) mechanism. This analysis, based on the possible configurations of the systems, is broadly applicable to a wide variety of molecular structures. In this sense, it is easy to explain why the functionalization of the ortho-position of a single aromatic unit with a bulky group, even one as large as CH2SMe,32 is not enough to significantly affect the energy barrier (5.5 kcal mol−1). This is because any steric hindrance during rotation can be alleviated by the ring adopting an orientation perpendicular to the reference plane defined by the three bonds to the central carbon.33 However, a different scenario arises when all the ortho-positions of the aromatic rings are occupied, for instance with halogenated atoms of increasing size, leading to higher interconversion barriers and ultimately effectively locking the molecular configuration into stables enantiomers at room temperature.21 On the other hand, an asymmetric arrangement of the functional groups may induce the conversion of the enantiomeric forms (non-superimposable mirror images) into diastereomers (non-mirror image stereoisomers), as in the case of a trityl radical with three different aryl rings, where at least one of them has an ortho- or meta-position occupied.33 Moreover, the functionalization can also introduce new dimensions of chirality into the radical. In scenarios where the propeller-like structure coexists with additional chiral centers, axes and/or planes, the Principle of Chirality Hierarchy, valid for three-blade propeller systems,34 will dictate the overall chirality. In addition, when incorporated into a supramolecular assembly, the chiral configuration of the propellers can dictate the outcome of self-assembly, leading to well-defined chiral architectures.35,36

Chirality in trityl radicals: a classification

Propeller chirality

This category is the natural host for propeller-like trityl-based structures, as long as their substitution pattern does not include any other alternative element of dissymmetry that perturbs their intrinsic chiral framework. It is the case, for instance, of tetrathiatriarylmethyl-type (TAM) radicals,37,38 which are sterically substituted trityl radicals with two 5-membered rings fused to the aryl rings, incorporating four sulfur atoms into each aryl ring through thiophene-like linkages. Within this family, Halpern et al.,39 determined the C3 symmetry in methanol precursors and demonstrated a two-ring flip interconversion mechanism. However, Marchand-Brynaert et al.40,41 were the first to separately isolate the P- and M-enantiomeric forms of a representative TAM radical (Chart 1A), such as Finland trityl radical CT-03, analyzing the effect of different para-substitutions. They confirmed that the conformational persistence in all cases allowed safe storage over several months, although slow racemization processes in solution at room temperature were observed.

Propeller chirality. Chemical structures of: (A) TAM radicals.40,41 and (B) halogenated trityl derivatives: PTM,25,43 TTM,25 TTBrM,21 nCzTTBrM series,44 and TTBrM-(Me)Cz series.45

Propeller chirality is also the main category for the trityl radical derivatives functionalized with bulky halogenated atoms, widely exploited as components for materials like multifunctional switches, (spin) electronics, as well as light emitters or for biological applications, among others.1,42 Irurre et al.43 pioneered the successful isolation of the P- and M-atropisomers of the perchlorotriphenylmethyl (PTM) radical (Chart 1B.1) by chiral stationary phase (CSP)-HPLC but, remarkably, no progress was made on chiral halogenated trityls until Veciana et al.,25 reopened the debate about chirality on this family, highlighting the advantages of its combination with alternative properties decades later. In addition, they revised the absolute configuration assignment of the PTM radical. Their analysis, based on a comparison of the experimental and theoretical chiroptical properties, established that the absolute configuration should be assigned based on the sign of the lowest-energy Cotton effect, rather than that of the main ECD electronic transition, which has the opposite sign, as previously reported by Irurre et al.43 They also provided further insight into the impact of the bulky chlorinated functionalization on the racemization barrier from the comparison with the tris(2,4,6-trichlorophenyl)methyl TTM radical (Chart 1B.2). Veciana et al.21 then focused their attention on overcoming the racemization issues detected for the chlorinated derivatives at room temperature and presented the brominated alternative to TTM radical. They demonstrated that the so-called TTBrM radical (Chart 1B.3), with heavier halides, showed no hints of racemization for at least two hours at 60 °C. Since then, the robustness of TTBrM has caught the attention of alternative authors in the field, such as Albrecht et al.44 (Chart 1B.4) or Kuehne et al.,45 (Chart 1B.5) currently working on TTBrM variants bearing carbazole units to improve the optical properties.

A different strategy to increase the steric bulkiness of trityl radicals has recently been explored by Gong et al.,46 who replaced the three phenyl blades with hexa-peri-hexabenzocoronene (HBC) nanographene units. The resulting “supertrityl” radical preserved the characteristic propeller-shaped geometry while introducing a large π-surface that promotes intermolecular pancake bonding. Unfortunately, the radical derivative with such π-extended substituents was found to be unstable under O2. Moreover, even though the authors were able to report the propeller-like structure of the methanol precursor, its low racemization barrier prevented the chiral resolution of the enantiomers too. On the other hand, Martin et al.47 have recently used the hydrogenated closed-shell version of this C3-symmetric molecular nanographene to gain further insight into the generation of permanently porous supramolecular frameworks through the self-assembly of an all-carbon nanographene. These recent works highlight the relevance that this synthetic strategy is gaining nowadays and, consequently, one may expect that it will not be long before open-shell-derived structures are reported.

Diastereoisomerism: trityl radicals with additional elements of chirality

When one or more chiral elements are used for the functionalization of the aryl rings in trityl radicals, different diastereosisomers – non-mirror image stereoisomers – are formed, arising from the combination of the P/M propeller chirality with the intrinsic configuration of the additional stereogenic elements such as chiral centers, axes,planes or multipropellers.

Chiral centers

In the simplest case, the conjugation of trityl radicals with a stereogenic center (like a substituent with an assymmetric C* atom with R or S configuration) adds a central (0D) chirality and gives rise to four different diastereoisomers: (M,S), (M,R), (P,S), and (P,R). In the case of the trityl-nitroxide biradicals based on TAM radicals, reported by Liu et al.,22 not one but two chiral centers were introduced (Chart 2A). The authors reported the coupling of a N-Boc-l-proline chiral linker with a racemic mixture of the nitroxide APO affording two nitroxide diastereosisomers, (S) and (R), followed by their conjugation with the (M) and (P) propellers of the CT-03 radical. Thus, with the chiral configuration of l-proline fixed, four different diastereoisomers were obtained: (M,S,R), (M,S,S), (P,S,R), and (P,S,S)-configurations. This synthetic approach not only incorporated stereogenic centers but also an additional radical moiety. However, in an effort to generate additional chiral centers in TAM radicals, the authors were not limited to evaluating the para-position of the aryl rings. On the contrary, Liu et al. also investigated, in a subsequent study,20 the substitution on the terminal methylene bridges, which had remained almost unexplored due to the associated synthetic difficulty. In this second case, they selected cis-hydroxylmethyl groups for the functionalization of the given position in one of the aryl rings (Chart 2B). Interestingly, the authors did not observe any interconversion over 5 hours at 100 °C in toluene. They therefore concluded that the stability of the four resulting diasteroisomers was likely associated with the possible hydrogen-bonding interactions between the peripheral hydroxyl groups with hydrogen or sulfur atoms on alternative aryl rings during the two-ring flip process.

Chiral axes

Axial or 1D chirality can be introduced into trityl radical-based structures with the help of stereogenic axis. For instance, Kawai et al.48 used an axially chiral biphenyl bridge derived from octahydrobinaphthyl to link two pyridyl-substituted triarylmethyl (PyBTM) radical units (Chart 3A). The resulting compounds were described as the (R/S) isomers based on the axial chirality of the spacer, while the authors did not provide an explicit M/P assignment for the PyBTM propeller units. Trityl derivatives functionalized with helicene-based moieties can also be included in this category, since the helical chirality is generally regarded as a special form of axial chirality arising from the presence of a helical axis. As an example, Kuehne et al.24 functionalized the para-position of one of the aryl rings in the propeller structure of TTM radical with heteroatom-doped aza[7]-helicenes of two different configurations (dinaphthocarbazole (Chart 3B.1) and diphenanthro-carbazole (Chart 3B.2)). Being the racemization barriers of aza[7]helicene units higher than the one for TTM radical, which racemizes at room temperature (i.e., >126 kJ mol−1vs. 88.4 kJ mol−1, respectively) the authors only considered the chirality of the aza[7]-helicenes groups. However, theoretical calculations helped them to determine the absolute configuration of the isolated enantiomers, concluding that the M,P (and therefore also P,M) enantiomers were more likely to occur at room temperature than the homochiral alternatives. Consequently, the authors were able to verify the influence of the aza[7]helicene's helicity on the propeller rotation. Remarkably, same approach was also followed and simultaneously reported by Chen et al. (Chart 3C).49

Chiral planes

Trityl radicals showing planar or 2D chirality can be achieved through the functionalization with chiral groups that induce a high steric congestion, restricting the free rotation of the aryl rings and, consequently, the interconversion of the propellers. Kuehne et al.50 opted for a bis-ethynylene-PCP bridge, derived from the [2.2]paracyclophane (PCP) unit, as a planar backbone with the ortho-positions connecting two TTM radicals (Chart 4A). The racemic mixture of the so-called pseudo-o-PCP-TTM2 was resolved into its respective R and S isomers, corresponding to the planar chirality of the PCP scaffold. Each isomer adopted the energetically stable same-handed conformation of the two TTM propellers, both adopting the P conformation with a clockwise rotational sense, as indicated by theoretical calculations. Alternatively, Chen et al.51 functionalized planar chiral pillar[5]arenes with TTM and triarylamine or triarylborane units (P5N-TTM and P5B-TTM, respectively, in Chart 4B). The authors demonstrated that highly stable isomers (pS/pR) could be successfully isolated, showing no hints of racemization for several days at room temperature, thanks to the concerted effect generated between the planar-chiral pillar[5]arenes framework and the propeller-like TTM units. No specific P/M assignment was provided for the TTM propellers but, compared to their complete racemization in 1 h at room temperature, the persistent CD response after storage for 5 days at room temperature observed for pS-P5N-TTM and pS-P5B-TTM further supports their enhanced configurational stability.

Multipropellers

The presence of two, three or even more propellers in a single molecule gives rise to a plethora of stereoisomers with distinct symmetries and physicochemical properties. This is exemplified with the PTM-based triradical reported by Veciana et al.,52 a quartet molecule composed of three propeller-like subunits arranged symmetrically around one common, central aromatic ring. Accordingly, for its stereochemical analysis, the molecule can be considered as an assembly of three Ar2ZAr′ subunits. Since all the six outer aryl groups of this molecule possess local C2 axes that are coincident with their bonds to the three Z atoms, and such atoms have a sp2 hybridization, the molecule has only three stereogenic elements. Therefore, the maximum number of stereomeric forms expected are 23 = 8 (four d/l pairs) stereoisomers. However, since the six outer Ar groups and the three central carbon atoms are identical, there must be an isomeric degeneracy. Thus, the three d/l pairs with one of the three propeller-like subunits differing in their helicity with respect to the two others become equivalent. Consequently, only the two d/l pairs with D3 and C2 symmetries exist (Chart 5). These two diastereomeric forms show a remarkably persistency and lifetimes of years, being resolved and isolated by HPLC or by crystallization, and yielding stable solids that exhibit quartet ground states and distinct zero-field splitting in accordance with their symmetries.

Supramolecular chirality

Beyond the molecular level, trityl radical derivatives can also be incorporated into supramolecular assemblies such as open-framework structures formed from coordination bonds, where alternating neighboring (P) and (M) configurations serve as polytopic ligands.53 Thus, 2D stacked layers of supramolecular coordinated radicals are formed, exhibiting global bulk magnetic ordering and a reversible, highly selective solvent-induced ‘shrinking–breathing’ process involving large volume changes (25–35%) that strongly influence the magnetic properties. This strategy has also yielded racemic, purely organic nanoporous magnets.54,55 Alternatively, chiral supramolecular assemblies can also be prepared, such as the self-assembled homochiral tetrahedral radical cage, reported by Li et al.35 These authors pioneered the use of dynamic covalent chemistry for the generation of a tetrahedral structure consisting of four triangular faces derived from a trisaldehyde precursor bearing a PTM radical moiety and four tris(2-aminoethyl)amine (TREN) vertices (Fig. 1A). Two enantiomeric tetrahedral cages were successfully isolated and exhibited relatively stable chirality in solution, probably due to the steric hindrance within the cage, which prevents the flipping of the phenyl units. Interestingly, a homochiral helical conformation was deduced for the four PTM subunits in a single cage, as well for the TREN vertexes. However, opposite chirality was always observed between PTM and TREN. Super-structures were also formed from the packing of each cage with its enantiomer (Fig. 1B), thanks to the [CH⋯π] and [CH⋯Cl] interactions between cages. In an alternative approach for 3D chirality in trityl radicals, Wang et al.36 reported an original metallo-supramolecular radical cage combining Zn2+ with a tritopic terpyridinyl ligand consisting of TTM radical and carbazole (Fig. 1C), where the propeller-shaped conformation of the radical ligand contributed to the chiral conformation of the resulting cage. The authors further confirmed the formation of a twisted trigonal prism (Fig. 1D), which arises from the assembly of two homochiral layers and is stabilized by CH–π interactions and π–π interactions between them.

Chirality and spin in trityl radicals: from bio-to electronic, and quantum applications

The lone electron at the central carbon in the trityl radical and its functionalized derivatives confers, among other properties, an appealing magnetic nature to be exploited in many fields, like electronics or biomedicine. Among trityl-based radicals, tetrathiatriarylmethyl-type (TAM) radicals, such as the so-called Finland trityl (also known as CT-03) and its hydrophilic counterpart, OX063, are representative examples of the biomedical potential of trityl derivatives. Their bulky aryl-ring substituents enhance the electron delocalization and increase the stability of the unpaired electron. Their good water solubility and sharp singlet EPR signal have triggered their use as spin labels56,57 DNP,58–60 EPR imaging (EPRI)61 or as oximetric probes,62 thanks to their high O2 sensitivity, among others. It is also important to highlight that the integration of multiple radical units into a single molecule gives rise to novel properties or functions that are not available in systems containing only an individual radical. In this context, Y. Liu et al.22 prepared TAM-nitroxide biradicals (Chart 2A) and focused their attention on the role of chirality over spin–spin exchange (J) interactions. They concluded that the chiral configuration of the nitroxide moieties – highly dependent on temperature and solvents effects due to the flexibility of the linkers – had a huge impact on the modulation of the exchange interactions, key to design improved biradicals with enhanced DNP properties (Fig. 2). On the other hand, the same group proved that an additional chiral center on the methylene bridges in TAM radicals had little influence on the EPR properties.20 Moreover, they also developed an EPR-based technique for the simultaneous detection and quantitation of multiple biothiols in a single sample without the need for chromatographic separation.63

In terms of electronic transport properties, it is known that the single-unoccupied molecular orbital (SUMO) in chlorinated trityl radicals, such as PTM derivatives, which is associated with their unpaired electron, is closely aligned with the Fermi level of the gold electrodes typically used in electronic devices. Thus, a significative enhancement of the conductivity through molecular junctions and self-assembled monolayers involving PTM radicals has been proved.64–66 Alternatively, the CISS effect, discovered by Naaman et al.,67–70 demonstrated that chiral materials can act as spin filters modulating the charge transport of spin-polarized electrons through self-assembled structures. In this context, Crivillers et al.23 explored the performance of trityl radicals as spin filters aiming at a more efficient electronic transport (Fig. 3). They prepared enantioenriched self-assembled monolayers from a PTM radical derivate functionalized with anchoring groups and studied the charge transport under an external magnetic field. Unfortunately, no CISS effect was observed due to the dominant contribution of the spin radical and its thermal fluctuations at room temperature, as highlighted by quantum transport calculations. Highly robust surface-confined switch based on an electroactive, persistent organic PTM radical immobilized on indium tin oxide substrates can be electrochemically and reversibly converted to the anion form. This molecular bistable system behaves as an extremely robust redox switch in which an electrical input is transduced into optical as well as magnetic outputs under ambient conditions. The fact that this molecular surface switch, operating at very low voltages, can be patterned and addressed locally, and also has exceptionally high long-term stability and excellent reversibility and reproducibility, makes it a very promising platform for non-volatile memory devices.71 Conformational distortions in chiral trityl derivatives strongly influence their magnetic ground states too. For example, the PTM-based triradical (Chart 5) exhibits a distortion-induced reversal of the magnetic exchange coupling, leading to a switch from a high-spin quartet to a low-spin ground state.52 Its structural flexibility also enables the electrical tuning of the exchange coupling, highlighting its potential for controllable magnetic switching in molecular nanodevices.72

Finally, the open-shell nature in trityl radicals can also be advantageous within the emergent field of quantum technology as demonstrated by the extraordinary long ensemble coherence times up to 148 µs exhibited by PTM radical, a mono-functionalized PTM, and a biradical PTM dimer below 100 K.73 Accordingly, trityl-based diradicals, with two interacting unpaired electrons, are appealing candidates for quantum sensing of magnetic fields for medical imaging or quantum communication. In this context, Kuehne et al.50 evaluated the impact of chirality on the spin properties of paracyclophane-bridged TTM diradical and found that, unlike its achiral counterpart, the chiral diradical did exhibited a zero-field splitting (ZFS) in the EPR spectrum, thanks to the dipolar coupling between spins. It is important to highlight that the ZFS – defined as a quantum mechanical phenomenon that leads to the splitting of the energy levels of a molecule with multiple unpaired electrons split even in the absence of an external magnetic field – is a key feature in quantum computing and spintronics, as it provides the intrinsic, non-zero energy gaps required to govern quantum states.

Spin, chirality and light: chiroptical properties of trityl-based radical emitters

Nowadays luminescence properties of halogenated trityl derivatives constitute one of the most active research areas in the field of organic radicals, owing to the advantages conferred by their open-shell electronic configuration, including non-quenched and red-shifted emission from the doublet excited state, as well as long emission lifetimes.3–6,17

Irurre et al.,43 were the first to successfully isolate the P- and M-atropisomers of the polychlorinated PTM radical (Chart 1B.1) in 1995. However, the chiroptical properties of these radicals remained unexplored until more than two decades later when, in 2019, Duan et al.74 co-assembled achiral halogenated PTM derivatives with chiral gelators and prepared free-standing luminescent films by dispersing the resulting co-gels in PMMA (Fig. 5). This work was motivated by the growing interest in circularly polarized luminescence (CPL), driven by its broad range of applications in optical sensing,75,76 bioimaging,77 biomedical diagnosis,78–81 or quantum communication.82 Concurrently, Veciana et al.25 published a pioneering work that renewed interest in their chirality and its interplay with the optical activity, reporting the first performance of organic free radicals as intrinsic chiral light emitters. CD and CPL spectra of PTM and TTM (Chart 1B.1 and 2) were recorded in viscous CCl4 at −20 °C to minimize the racemization issues without observing any particular effect of the halogenated shield, in line with the similar values estimated for the luminescence dissymmetry factor (glum) in each case: |glum| (×10−3) = 0.8 (λem = 605 nm) and 0.5 (λem = 569 nm) for PTM and TTM, respectively. Comparable results were reported by the research group in a subsequent work in 2020,21 when a |glum| (×10−3) = 0.7 (λem = 593 nm) was attributed to TTBrM radical (Chart 1B.3), analogue to TTM with bulky Br atoms instead. Despite exhibiting only moderate glum values, these seminal works opened new avenues for the development of more efficient chiral organic radical emitters (Fig. 4). Since then, numerous studies have been reported focusing on enhancing the performance of these organic radicals and their glum values. For instance, Chen et al.51 decided, in 2022, to face the racemization issues combining the TTM radical with planar macrocyclic pillararene and additional π-conjugated luminophores, like Ar3N or Ar3B (Chart 4B), expecting to achieve sterically stable diasteroisomers with appealing near-infrared (NIR) emission for (bio)imaging application. Compared to TTM, red-shifted emissions were successfully achieved (λem P5N- TTM = 653 nm (Φ = 7.6%) and λem P5B-TTM = 651 nm (Φ = 4.9%)). Unfortunately, no CPL emission was observed in solution, whereas measurable CPL signals were recorded in PMMA-doped films: |glum| (×10−3) = 0.8 and 1.5 for P5N-TTM and P5B-TTM, respectively, claiming an effect of the solvent media (Fig. 5A). With the aim of improving thephotostability and luminescence quantum yield, two research groups independently and simultaneously reported, in 2023, the chiral emission of TTM units conjugated to electron-donating aza[7]helicene substituents. On one hand, a Φ = 50% (λem = 709 nm) was quantified by Chen et al.49 for N7-TTM (Chart 3C), being higher than the values reported for the individual open π-extended aza[7]helicene (Φ = 28%) and TTM. Moreover, a higher |glum (≈709 nm)| (×10−3) = 3.57 was achieved for this red-shifted emission. Interestingly, the authors claimed that the dominant helical chirality, linked to the aza[7]helicene, was unaltered after storage for 1 week under ambient conditions, as well as the emission spectrum upon exposure to 365 nm UV over 10 h, minimizing the racemization and photostability issues (Fig. 5B). On the other hand, Kuehne et al.24 prepared two TTM derivatives with different configurations of the aza[7]helicene units: TTM-DNC (DNC: dinaphthocarbazole, Chart 3B.1) and TTM-DPC (DPC: diphenanthro-carbazole, Chart 3B.2) for the extended version, comparable to the reported by Chen et al.49 The authors obtained a Φ = 34% for TTM-DNC (λem = 696 nm) and 43% for the TTM-DPC (λem = 712 nm) while, in terms of dissymmetry factor, only data for the TTM-DNC were shown |glum| (×10−3) = 0.5. Nonetheless, in terms of the efficiency, as evaluated by the CPL brightness (BCPL = ε × Φ × |glum|/2),83 the functionalized radical exhibited superior performance (BCPL = 0.25 M−1 cm−1) than the TTM itself, owing to its higher values of extinction coefficients (ε) and Φ (Fig. 5C). Surprisingly, this value is nearly two orders of magnitude lower than that reported by Chen et al. for N7-TTM (BCPL = 23 M−1 cm−1).49 Dissimilar values were also reported in the case of the dissymmetry factors, being |glum| (×10−3) = 0.5 and 3.57 for TTM-DNC24 and N7-TTM,49 respectively, despite being almost structurally identical analogues. In 2025, Kawai et al.48 went a step beyond when they decided to study the chiroptical properties of trityl-based diradicals and used a biphenyl bridge as donor spacer to connect two pyridyl-substituted triarylmethyl (PyBTM) radicals (Chart 3A), due to their higher stability when compared to TTM unit (50 times longer in CHCl3). A red-shifted emission (λem = 663 nm) compared to that of the PyBTM alone (λem = 585 nm) was reported, with a maximum Φ of 10% in CHCl3 and a |glum| (×10−3) = 1.2. However, the authors noted that this value may have been overestimated due to significant noise in the spectrum. The luminescent diradical approach was also recently followed by Kuehne et al.,50 who chose a paracyclophane bridge between two TTM units and analyzed the chiral ortho-configuration (pseudo-o-PCP-TTM2, Chart 4A). A red-shifted emission (λem = 688 nm) compared to individual components was observed. Nonetheless, the Φ < 1% due to the symmetry-breaking charge separation in the excited state, preventing a reliable quantification of the CPL signal and resulting in an estimated |glum| (×10−3) < 0.1.

Notably, three independent studies published in 2026 by different research groups have reported the chiroptical properties of TTBrM-based molecules, highlighting the enhanced configurational stability against racemization imparted by the bulky Br atoms. Surprisingly, two of them revolved around the functionalization of the radical with a carbazole donor, a strategy that had already proven successful in racemic chlorinated derivatives, by affording longer-wavelength emissions, higher photoluminescence quantum yields, and improved photostability compared to the non-functionalized radical.74,84–90 On one hand, Albrecht et al.44 evaluated the impact of the number of aryl ring conjugated to carbazole units, varying from one to three (Chart 1B.4). Compared with TTBrM, red-shifted emissions ranging from 689 to 701 nm were observed with the decreasing number of carbazole units. By contrast, the photoluminescence quantum yield increased with the number of carbazole units, reaching values of 70–76%. Although the |glum| values of the carbazole-functionalized radicals remained within the same order of magnitude as that of TTBrM, they were consistently lower, ranging from approximately 0.15–0.19 × 10−3, compared with 0.7 × 10−3 for TTBrM. Nevertheless, their markedly higher absorptivity and photoluminescence quantum yields resulted in BCPL values of 0.76–1.1, nearly an order of magnitude higher than that of TTBrM (BCPL = 0.16), as expected from the BCPL relationship. On the other hand, Kuehne et al.45 opted for functionalizing only one aryl ring of TTBrM but with three different carbazole derivatives to adjust the donor strength (Chart 1B.5). The authors observed a positive effect of carbazole methylation, with the emission maximum red-shifting from 646 to 688 nm as the number of methyl groups increased. More recently, the scope of TTBrM-based chiral materials has expanded beyond molecular engineering to the nanoscale. Rather than modifying the radical itself, Mayorga-Burrezo et al.91 reported the first water-compatible enantiomerically enriched organic nanoparticles (ONPs) displaying CPL. By varying the concentration of chiral trityl radicals dispersed in an optically inactive matrix of the corresponding hydrogenated analogue, they modulated the balance between monomeric and excimeric emission and selectively enhanced the corresponding CPL signals. Increasing the radical loading to 20% promoted the formation of intrinsically chiral excimers, giving rise to a red-shifted CPL band centered at 720 nm, whereas ONPs prepared with a lower radical content (5%) displayed only monomeric chiral emission centered at 595 nm (Fig. 6). Beyond these findings, this work establishes a route to water-compatible chiral radical emitters.

Finally, it is worth mentioning that in all the cases mentioned above, enantiopure or enantioenriched samples were used and, therefore, costly and time-consuming enantioenrichment steps involving chiral stationary phase (CSP) HPLC were required. Importantly, it has been demonstrated that chiroptical activity can be induced under a longitudinal external magnetic field. Particularly, the magnetic circular dichroism (MCD) and circularly polarized luminescence (MCPL) spectra from racemic mixtures of TTM derivatives in PMMA films74 and solution92 have been reported. This approach not only eliminates the need for laborious enantioenrichment steps but also avoid racemization issues.

Summary and future perspectives

The rapid emergence of chiral triarylmethyl radicals has transformed chirality from a purely structural feature into a powerful molecular design principle for persistent open-shell materials. As highlighted throughout this Review, different approaches at the molecular and supramolecular levels provide versatile strategies to tune spin, magnetic and chiroptical properties within a single molecular platform. These advances have considerably broadened the scope of trityl radicals, extending their relevance from molecular magnetism and EPR spectroscopy to spintronics, circularly polarized luminescence and quantum technologies.

From a molecular design perspective, increasing the steric hindrance around the trityl core, either through bulky substituents, as in the case of TAM radicals, or by occupying the ortho-positions with sterically demanding atoms such as bromine, can preserve the intrinsic propeller chirality and suppress the configurational interconversion. Moreover, bromination provides a useful handle for subsequent synthetic modification, enabling further derivatization and the enhancement of the chiroptical response, as demonstrated for brominated trityl derivatives bearing carbazole units. Alternatively, the complementary conjugation of trityl radicals with chiral scaffolds introduces additional, configurationally stable elements of chirality and open new venues for their applicability through the modulation of the resulting chiral-based properties. In this context, helicenes, paracyclophanes, and related rigid frameworks have been shown to strongly influence the overall stereochemical behaviour while imposing a defined chiral configuration on the trityl radical, as supported by theoretical calculations. Nonetheless, one of the major challenges will be the transition from discrete molecular systems to functional materials and devices. The integration of chiral radicals into thin films, self-assembled architectures, organic nanoparticles and hybrid materials based on polymers or metal–organic frameworks offers exciting opportunities to preserve and amplify chiral information while enabling scalable processing. Such hierarchical organization will be essential for translating the unique molecular properties of chiral radicals into practical technologies.

The combination of chirality with open-shell electronic structures also opens new opportunities across multiple disciplines. In spintronics, stable enantiopure radicals represent promising candidates for spin-selective transport and CISS-based devices. In molecular magnetism and quantum technologies, chiral radical architectures can provide new approaches to control spin coupling, magnetic relaxation and zero-field splitting. Also, the recent development of intrinsically chiral radical emitters paves the way toward circularly polarized light-emitting devices, advanced optical sensors, bioimaging and quantum photonic platforms. In parallel, the inherently chiral nature of biological systems suggests exciting possibilities for enantioselective molecular recognition, where persistent radicals could combine selective interactions with EPR, optical or redox readouts for imaging and sensing applications.

Despite these remarkable advances, important challenges remain. The limited availability of conformationally persistent chiral radicals, the generally modest chiroptical responses, and the scarcity of device-level demonstrations restrict more technological implementation. Addressing these limitations will require improved synthetic methodologies, deeper understanding of the relationship between molecular chirality and spin-dependent phenomena, and interdisciplinary efforts bridging chemistry, materials science, physics and engineering.

Overall, chiral triarylmethyl radicals are evolving into a unique class of multifunctional molecule-based materials in which chirality and open-shell electronic structures act synergistically to generate properties inaccessible to conventional closed-shell systems. We anticipate that continued progress in molecular design, supramolecular organization and materials integration will establish these radicals as key building blocks for next-generation spintronic, photonic, quantum and biomedical technologies.
