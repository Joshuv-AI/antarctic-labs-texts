A comprehensive review on the role of zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs) in facilitating catalytic transformations
Authors: Kevin George, Abin Philip, Gautham Krishna K.
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Catalysis plays an important role in modern chemistry by enabling efficient and sustainable chemical processes. Zeolitic Imidazolate Frameworks (ZIFs), a type of metal–organic framework, have gained attention because of their good stability, adjustable pore structure, and well-defined metal sites. This review focuses on ZIFs and their derived materials in three main areas: heterogeneous catalysis, electrocatalysis, and photocatalysis. It highlights how simple design strategies, such as metal doping and conversion into porous carbon materials, can improve catalytic activity, selectivity, and durability. In many cases, ZIF-derived catalysts show better stability and comparable or improved performance compared to traditional catalysts, especially in reactions like oxygen reduction and carbon dioxide conversion. Overall, this review summarizes the key structure–performance relationships of ZIF-based catalysts and points out their potential as efficient and cost-effective materials for sustainable chemical and energy applications, while also noting challenges related to long-term stability and large-scale production.

Introduction

Catalysis is a cornerstone of chemistry, playing a pivotal role across the physical, chemical, and biological sciences. The term “catalysis” was first introduced by Berzelius in 1836 to describe the acceleration of chemical reactions by substances that themselves remain unchanged.1 Catalysts operate by reducing the activation energy of a reaction, thereby increasing its rate while maintaining the fundamental thermodynamic properties. Moreover, catalysts enhance reaction selectivity, ensuring that reactions yielding multiple products favour the desired compound.2,3 This selectivity is crucial for minimizing byproducts, improving atom economy, and reducing environmental pollution. As a result, catalysts are indispensable in the development of sustainable technologies. Today, more than 90% of chemical processes depend on catalysis for the production of fine and commodity chemicals.4

The development of renewable energy technologies is a promising approach to addressing global environmental concerns and the energy crisis. Since the pioneering work of Fujishima and Honda on titanium dioxide (TiO2) photoelectrodes in 1972,5 catalysis has gained considerable attention in environmental remediation and sustainable energy applications. In this context, Metal–Organic Frameworks (MOFs) have emerged as a highly versatile class of porous crystalline materials with significant catalytic potential. Over the past three decades, MOFs and their derivatives have attracted widespread interest due to their structural tunability, high surface area, and well-defined porosity. These properties make them highly suitable for applications such as gas storage and separation, sensors, photocatalysis, electrochemical processes, and biomedical applications.6–13

MOFs generally consist of metal nodes coordinated with organic linkers, forming extended networks characterized by high porosity and large surface areas.14 These structural features make MOFs highly valuable as catalytic materials by providing accessible active sites and facilitating the mass transport of reactants.15 The catalytic activity of MOFs primarily arises from their uncoordinated metal centers, functionalized organic linkers, or embedded catalytic species such as metal nanoparticles, metal oxides, or biomolecules.16,17 Through in situ or post-synthetic functionalization, MOFs' catalytic efficiency can be further enhanced, enabling the design of highly active and selective catalysts for specific applications. Moreover, the ability to tailor MOF structures through careful selection of metal nodes and linkers offers a broad range of catalytic functionalities, making them highly adaptable to diverse chemical transformations.17–19

A specific category of MOFs, Zeolitic Imidazolate Frameworks (ZIFs), has garnered particular interest due to their unique structural features. ZIFs consist of transition-metal cations (M) and imidazole-based ligands (Im), where the metal components are bridged by imidazolate linkers at an M–Im–M angle of ∼145°, mirroring the Si–O–Si bond angle in conventional zeolites. This structural resemblance grants ZIFs a zeolite-type topology while integrating the advantages of MOFs.20–22 Among ZIFs, ZIF-67 (Co(mIm)2, mIm = 2-methylimidazole) is a well-characterized example, composed of cobalt ions (Co2+) and 2-methylimidazolate anions, exhibiting cubic crystal symmetry with unit cell parameters of a = b = c = 16.9589 Å.22–24 These properties contribute to the robust stability of ZIFs, making them highly attractive for various applications, particularly in catalysis. ZIFs exhibit zeolite-like topologies with tetrahedrally coordinated metal ions linked by imidazolate moieties, possessing exceptional structural stability, chemical resistance, and tunability, which make them ideal precursors for MOF-derived catalysts. Their robustness allows them to be utilized in catalytic processes under extreme conditions, such as high temperatures, acidic or basic environments, and exposure to organic solvents.25–29 Controlled pyrolysis of ZIFs can yield carbon-based materials doped with heteroatoms, metals, or metal oxides, significantly enhancing their catalytic performance. This enhancement results from improved electron transfer, increased active site availability, and enhanced overall stability during catalytic reactions. The derivatization of ZIFs enables the development of hybrid catalysts with tolerance to extreme reaction conditions and prolonged catalytic cycles.30,31

Over 150 different ZIF structures have been synthesized,32 many of which exhibit microporosity, large surface areas, and adjustable pore apertures. These characteristics make ZIFs highly promising for applications beyond catalysis, including adsorption, electrode materials, drug delivery, sensors, bioimaging, and supercapacitors. The inherent stability and tunability of ZIFs provide new scientific and technological opportunities, integrating the benefits of zeolites and MOFs, such as high surface area, permanent porosity, and abundant active sites.33–35

Reticular chemistry has transformed the design of MOF structures by employing a rational approach to constructing extended crystalline frameworks. The fundamental principle of reticular design involves linking organic and inorganic building units through strong directional chemical bonds, yielding stable structures with precise control over pore size. This methodology allows for modifications in pore size by adjusting the length of organic linkers without altering the framework's topology. Key characteristics of reticular materials include exceptional porosity, tunable pore size, post-synthetic modification (PSM) for optimizing guest interactions, and facile characterization via X-ray diffraction due to their periodic arrangement. These properties have driven extensive research into MOFs, facilitating their widespread application in catalysis and beyond.36–38

The modification of ZIF structures through guest species incorporation, metal impregnation, or linker functionalization provides additional flexibility in catalyst design. Various strategies, including direct pyrolysis, solution impregnation, ion exchange, and mixed-linker approaches, have been employed to tailor ZIF-derived materials for enhanced catalytic efficiency. Incorporating transition metals such as Cu, Co, Fe, and Ni into ZIF frameworks has been extensively explored for redox catalysis, while heteroatom doping (e.g., N, P, S) improves the electronic properties of ZIF-derived carbon materials, making them highly effective in electrocatalysis and heterogeneous catalysis.39–41 Additionally, hybrid ZIF-derived catalysts that integrate metal nanoparticles or metal oxides with carbon supports have demonstrated exceptional catalytic activity and durability in oxidation–reduction reactions, acid–base catalysis, and C–C coupling reactions. These catalytic processes are critical in the synthesis of fine chemicals, pharmaceuticals, and specialty chemicals.42–44

A major advantage of ZIF-derived catalysts is their ability to maintain structural integrity while offering a high density of active sites, often highly dispersed at the atomic or nanoscale level. This unique feature enhances selectivity and efficiency in catalytic reactions.45,46 Additionally, the inherent porosity of ZIFs facilitates the diffusion of reactants and intermediates, improving mass transport and reaction kinetics. The high metal-loading capability of ZIF-derived materials ensures prolonged catalytic performance and recyclability, making them highly attractive candidates for sustainable chemical manufacturing.47–49

While several recent reviews have summarized the synthesis, properties, and catalytic applications of ZIF-based materials, most reports primarily focus on individual catalytic domains or provide descriptive overviews of specific reactions without establishing clear correlations between structure, properties, and catalytic performance.17,33,34 In addition, limited attention has been given to systematically comparing the performance of ZIF-based catalysts across photocatalytic, electrocatalytic, and heterogeneous catalytic processes, particularly in terms of stability, durability, and practical applicability under realistic conditions.

In this context, the present review aims to bridge these gaps by offering (i) a systematic structure–property–performance correlation of ZIF and ZIF-derived catalysts, (ii) a comparative evaluation across three major catalytic domains—heterogeneous catalysis, electrocatalysis, and photocatalysis—and (iii) a critical discussion on stability challenges, including framework degradation and metal leaching under different reaction environments. By integrating these perspectives, this review provides a more unified and application-oriented understanding of ZIF-based catalysis, highlighting pathways for the rational design of next-generation catalytic materials.

Literature survey methodology

The literature underlying this review was compiled through systematic searches of Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, covering publications from 2015 to 2026, using combinations of the search terms “zeolitic imidazolate framework,” “ZIF-derived,” “catalysis,” “photocatalysis,” “electrocatalysis,” and related reaction-specific terms (e.g., “oxygen reduction,” “CO2 reduction,” “Knoevenagel”). Inclusion criteria comprised peer-reviewed primary research articles reporting ZIF or ZIF-derived materials with catalytic performance data supported by structural characterization; conference abstracts, patents, and studies lacking catalytic performance data were excluded. Those primary studies met these criteria and form the basis of the tables and discussion presented. Electrocatalytic applications adjacent to redox catalysis, including dopamine sensing and supercapacitor electrodes, were included where the underlying charge-transfer mechanism overlaps with the core reactions discussed (ORR, OER, HER, CO2RR); ZIF membrane-based reactive separation and tandem/cascade catalytic systems fell outside the scope of this survey and are noted as directions for future review in the Conclusion.

ZIF synthesis, structural chemistry, and conversion to ZIF-derived catalysts

ZIFs are crystalline coordination networks formed by tetrahedrally coordinated metal nodes linked by imidazolate-type ligands (see Table 1). Their zeolite-like topologies provide molecular-scale cages and apertures, but the catalytic consequences depend on both topology and the chemical identity of the node/linker. Dimensions and surface areas are representative literature ranges and are not universal constants because synthesis, activation, defects, and particle size alter measured porosity. Pyrolysis and post-treatment must be treated as a separate materials-conversion step. Temperature, heating rate, dwell time, atmosphere, precursor composition, and post-pyrolysis acid treatment can determine Zn volatilization, carbon ordering, pore development, metal aggregation, and the abundance of M–Nxversus metallic/oxide phases. In Zn-containing precursors, substantial Zn loss can occur at high temperature, while acid leaching may remove exposed nanoparticles but can also alter porosity and active-site distributions. Consequently, “ZIF-derived” does not imply preservation of the parent ZIF crystal structure, and post-treatment characterization is essential. It is important to distinguish pristine crystalline ZIFs from their pyrolysis-derived counterparts. Pristine ZIFs retain the ordered metal–organic coordination framework, and their properties are primarily governed by the coordination environment of the metal centers, linker chemistry, framework topology, and microporous molecular-sieving characteristics. In contrast, high-temperature pyrolysis substantially decomposes the parent ZIF framework, resulting in N-doped/graphitized carbon matrices containing M–N–C sites and/or dispersed metal or metal-oxide nanoparticles. Consequently, the electrocatalytic behavior of pyrolyzed ZIF derivatives is predominantly associated with electrical conductivity, degree of graphitization, defect chemistry, heteroatom coordination, and the nature and dispersion of the resulting inorganic species, rather than the intact ZIF cage structure.

The narrow window apertures of several ZIFs discussed in this review—notably ZIF-8 and ZIF-67, both 3.4 Å—are smaller than the kinetic diameter of many substrates examined in the liquid-phase organic transformations discussed below, including substituted benzaldehydes, microalgal triglycerides, and long-chain esters. Consequently, for these substrate classes, catalysis should be understood as occurring predominantly at the external crystal surface and at defect or grain-boundary sites, rather than within the internal micropore volume implied by the high BET surface areas reported. This distinction is made explicit for ZIF-8-catalyzed Knoevenagel condensation later in this review and applies more broadly wherever bulky substrates are discussed; high BET surface area should not be read as a proxy for accessible active-site density in these cases.

Photocatalytic application

Photocatalysis, a light-driven catalytic process, has gained significant attention for its efficiency in facilitating redox reactions, particularly in environmental and energy-related applications.50,51 It operates by harnessing photon energy (hν ≥ Eg, where Eg is the semiconductor bandgap energy) to excite electrons from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB) of a semiconductor photocatalyst, generating electron–hole (e–h) pairs that drive oxidation and reduction reactions. However, rapid e–h recombination remains a major challenge, necessitating strategies such as nanostructuring, doping, and heterojunction formation to enhance charge separation and catalytic activity52–57 (Fig. 1(a)). Among various approaches, the incorporation of porous hybrid materials, such as zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs), has emerged as an effective method for improving photocatalytic performance. As a subclass of metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), ZIFs exhibit high surface area, tunable porosity, and strong chemical stability, making them highly suitable for photocatalysis.58–64 Their ability to form heterojunctions with semiconductor photocatalysts like TiO2, g-C3N4, and ZnO significantly enhances charge separation and suppresses e–h recombination, thereby improving catalytic efficiency. Additionally, the incorporation of transition metals (Co, Ni, Fe, Cu) within ZIF structures facilitates electron transfer and active site formation, further optimizing reaction kinetics. The structural versatility of ZIFs allows for tunable bandgaps, enhancing light absorption across a broader spectrum, particularly in the visible region. Their role extends to various photocatalytic applications, including pollutant degradation, hydrogen evolution, and CO2 reduction, where they promote efficient charge transfer and reaction selectivity. Furthermore, integrating ZIFs with nanostructured photocatalysts, such as nanorods, nanospheres, and nanoflowers, further modulates active site accessibility and enhances light-harvesting efficiency. Strategies like self-doping and co-doping fine-tune the electronic structure of these hybrid materials, improving redox potential and catalytic stability (Fig. 1(b)). Additionally, π–π interactions in conjugated photocatalysts facilitate charge delocalization, promoting efficient interfacial charge transfer. Thus, the precise control of composition, morphology, and electronic structure in ZIF-based photocatalytic systems remains fundamental to advancing catalytic efficiency and broadening their applicability in sustainable chemical processes, including renewable energy generation and wastewater treatment.32,65–67

Recent work has centered on ZIF-8 as a sacrificial template for photoactive metal oxides. Thermally converting ZIF-8 into a porous ZnO-based photocatalyst gave a reduced band gap, better charge separation and good reusability, and it degraded ciprofloxacin and atrazine under natural sunlight.68 Doping extends this further. In ZIF-8-derived Co–ZnO, the band gap fell from 2.50 to 1.60 eV as Co content increased, and the 10 at% sample fully degraded crystal violet within 90 min under visible light, with hydroxyl radicals as the dominant species.69 Retaining the framework is also viable: Co@ZIF-8 monoliths made by a rapid sol–gel route showed Co-induced mid-gap states at 1.9 eV and removed 86.0 ± 1.6% of tartrazine under visible light.70

Composites push activity higher. A ZnO/ZIF-8/carbon quantum dot composite reached complete methylene blue degradation in 60 min under visible light, which the authors attribute to the porous ZIF-8 acting together with the surface modification.71 A recent mechanistic overview adds that ZIF-8 alone acts mainly as an adsorbent while the metal oxide supplies the photoactive sites, so the hybrids work through adsorption-assisted photocatalysis.72

For solar fuels, a ZnO–Co3O4/C S-scheme photocatalyst was obtained by pyrolysing a Zn/Co bimetallic ZIF at 600 °C under N2.73 It delivered 2340 µmol g−1 h−1 of H2 (apparent quantum yield 8.2%, Xe lamp, >420 nm) and converted CO2 to CO at 58.7 ± 2.1 µmol g−1 h−1, keeping 95.5% of its activity over five cycles. For ZIF-67 systems, a 2026 review assesses ZIF-67 synthesis and composites for combined energy conversion and pollutant degradation, and concludes that more thorough research is needed to realize their potential (Table 2).74

Studies on ZIF-based photocatalysts

M. E. Potter and co-workers studied two cobalt-based ZIFs. ZIF-9 (benzimidazole linker, 2.4 Å pores) and ZIF-67 (2-methylimidazole linker, 3.4 Å pores), for photocatalytic C–H activation in the aza-Henry reaction. Where both materials retained structural integrity but ZIF-9 formed larger crystals. Although ZIF-67 showed stronger ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT), ZIF-9 delivered activity (84 mol% vs. 55 mol% yield of 1-(nitromethyl)-N-phenyl-1,2,3,4-tetrahydroisoquinoline in 6 h), attributed to its favorable cobalt coordination and broader visible-light absorption rather than particle size or Co content, with both systems requiring light and oxygen (inactive in dark/inert conditions). Showing that electron-donating substituents enhance reactivity via a radical cation pathway involving photoexcited Co(ii), electron transfer, and regeneration by oxygen or nitromethane; importantly, ZIF-9 was stable and reusable with consistent yields, confirming its better photocatalytic efficiency despite weaker LMCT (Scheme 1).75 Similarly, Tao Jiang and co-workers improved photocatalytic hydrogen evolution by modifying ZIF-L with 1-vinylimidazole via a water-assisted method, where characterization (XRD, FTIR, XPS, SEM, TEM, BET, UV-vis DRS, PL, EIS) confirmed structural and optical tuning, including a phase transition from ZIF-L to ZIF-8 starting at 25% and completing at 50% ligand incorporation; BET showed maximum surface area for VZ-12 (238.6 m2 g−1), UV-vis indicated band gap reduction (5.00 eV to 4.47 eV), and PL/EIS confirmed improved charge separation with lowest resistance for VZ-12, leading to optimal H2 evolution (162.70 µmol g−1 h−1) compared to poor performance of VZ-0 (56.33 µmol g−1 h−1) due to recombination, while excess ligand (e.g., VZ-50) reduced activity likely from structural changes limiting active sites, and VZ-12 showed good stability, identifying controlled ligand incorporation as key for enhanced photocatalysis.76

Heterojunction construction is a powerful strategy to enhance photocatalytic efficiency by improving charge separation and suppressing recombination, as demonstrated in several ZIF-based systems. For instance, Zongping Shao and co-workers developed a ZnO@ZIF-8 nanocomposite via a simple wet-chemical method, where ZnO nanoparticles acted as templates for ZIF-8 growth, resulting in increased surface area (256 m2 g−1vs. 31 m2 g−1 for ZnO), enhanced light absorption, and reduced charge recombination due to the electron-trapping ability of ZIF-8; this led to 98% methylene blue degradation under visible light within 45 minutes compared to 57% for ZnO, with hydroxyl radicals identified as the dominant reactive oxygen species (confirmed by EPR and quenching studies), and a mechanism involving photoinduced electron–hole generation on ZnO and ZIF-8 surfaces driving ROS formation and pollutant degradation.77 Similarly, J. Ran et al. fabricated a ZIF-8/TiO2 composite on polydopamine-coated cotton fabric, where dopamine polymerization formed a pDA layer enabling TiO2 growth via Ti4+ chelation and subsequent ZIF-8 deposition through Zn2+ interaction; characterization (FTIR, XPS, XRD, SEM) confirmed successful integration, while reduced fluorescence indicated efficient charge separation, leading to nearly complete methylene blue degradation in 130 minutes with pseudo-first-order kinetics (k = 0.01813 min−1) and excellent reusability (>91%), attributed to synergistic effects, high adsorption capacity of ZIF-8, and pDA heat absorption, with a mechanism involving electron transfer from TiO2 to ZIF-8 generating O2˙− and hole transfer producing ˙OH radicals.78 Moving to more advanced systems, Nayab Arif et al. designed a ternary g-C3N4–MoS2–ZnNi-ZIF photocatalyst (ZnM-ZIF, M = Co, Ni), achieving a high hydrogen evolution rate of 77.8 mmol g−1 h−1 under UV-vis light—far exceeding individual components—due to improved band alignment, efficient charge transfer from g-C3N4 and ZnNi-ZIF to MoS2, enhanced visible-light absorption, increased surface area, and excellent stability over 30 hours, along with simultaneous H2O2 production.79 In another study, Radhapada Manna et al. developed an S-scheme ZMO/Z67 heterostructure for CO2 reduction, where ZMO nanospheres on Z67 improved light absorption, surface area, and charge separation, leading to higher methanol (6.08 µmol g−1 h−1) and ethanol (3.79 µmol g−1 h−1) yields; isotope labeling confirmed CO2 as the carbon source, while EPR, EIS, and photocurrent studies showed improved charge dynamics, and DFT supported COOH* intermediate formation, with an S-scheme mechanism preserving strong redox ability and good stability after multiple cycles.80 For photocatalytic CO2 reduction, product quantification is vulnerable to adventitious carbon contamination from residual organics on the catalyst surface or in the reactor. 13CO2 isotope-labelling control experiments, which unambiguously confirm that the detected carbon products originate from the supplied CO2 rather than surface contaminants, were performed in only one of the studies compiled here. We note this as the minimum standard of proof for photocatalytic CO2 reduction claims and flag that the remaining compiled studies do not meet it. Additionally, K. C. Devarayapalli et al. prepared a g-C3N4/ZIF-67 composite via microwave irradiation, which showed enhanced photocatalytic dye degradation (95% CV removal vs. 50 mol% ZIF-67 and 84 mol% g-C3N4) and significantly improved hydrogen production (up to 3329.33 µmol g−1, 10.6% quantum efficiency), due to efficient heterojunction charge transfer, reduced recombination (confirmed by PL), and active ROS (˙OH and O2−) formation supported by photocurrent and EIS studies.81 Core–shell architectures further improved performance, as shown by Xintao Rong et al., who synthesized ZIF67@ZIF8-x composites followed by carbonization at 800 °C, forming a CoO core and porous carbon shell that enhanced charge transfer, stability, and active site exposure, leading to photocatalytic oxygen evolution under visible light; characterization (SEM, TEM, XRD, XPS, BET) confirmed uniform structure, hierarchical porosity, and optimal composition, with durability and maximum quantum yield at 485 nm.82 In contrast, Iqra Rabani et al. developed a reverse core–shell ZIF-8@ZIF-67 catalyst via seed growth, achieving very high surface area (2500 m2 g−1) and excellent degradation of pharmaceutical pollutants (98.4% CAP and 97.4% 5-FU), with high rate constants, dominant O2˙− involvement, low charge transfer resistance (5.2 Ω), and strong stability (89.2% after five cycles), though performance depended on catalyst dosage and neutral pH conditions.83 Amar Nath Samanta et al. designed a Zn2GeO4/ZIF-67 heterostructure, where Zn2GeO4 nanorods on ZIF-67 enhanced CO2 reduction, giving higher methanol (5.145 mmol g−1 h−1) and ethanol production through strong interfacial interaction, reduced bandgap (2.005 eV), improved charge separation (confirmed by PL, EIS, photocurrent), and a direct Z-scheme mechanism supported by DFT involving COO*, COOH*, and CHO* intermediates, along with good selectivity (31.17% methanol) and stability over cycles, highlighting its potential for solar fuel generation.84

Metal doping and plasmonic modification are effective approaches to improve photocatalytic activity by enhancing light absorption and promoting charge separation, as shown in several ZIF-based systems. For example, Sang-Wha Lee and co-workers studied ZIF-8-derived nanostructures for methyl orange (MO) degradation, where pristine ZIF-8 showed strong adsorption under dark, visible, and UV conditions, with UV light enhancing interaction between excited MO and active sites, and direct excitation of ZIF-8 generating hydroxyl radicals confirmed by photoluminescence; further improvement was achieved by preparing Ag-loaded ZnO (Ag@ZnO) through calcination of Ag-impregnated ZIF-8, which led to nearly complete MO degradation within 120 minutes under simulated solar light due to plasmonic Ag reducing bandgap and enhancing charge separation, where electrons formed superoxide radicals and holes generated hydroxyl radicals, while characterization (XRD, FTIR) confirmed ZIF-8 to ZnO transformation, increased pore size, reduced surface area, and uniform Ag distribution, and adsorption studies showed that ions like Ca2+ and SO42− hindered MO uptake due to competitive interactions.85 Similarly, Mohammad Mehdi Sadughi et al. developed a magnetic γ-Fe2O3@SiO2@ZIF-8@Ag composite for norfloxacin degradation, synthesized by coating γ-Fe2O3 with SiO2 followed by ZIF-8 growth and Ag doping, where characterization (XRD, FTIR, SEM, EDX, VSM, UV-vis) confirmed structure and a reduced bandgap (1.5 eV), enabling 100% degradation under UV and 96.2% under visible light within 45 minutes, outperforming individual components, with a mechanism involving adsorption (enhanced by SiO2) and photocatalysis (via ZIF-8 and Ag), where ˙OH and holes were dominant species, optimal performance occurred at pH 5 and catalyst doses ≤0.4 g L−1, and first-order kinetics (k = 0.082 min−1 UV, 0.056 min−1 visible) showed UV was 1.46 times more effective; the catalyst was stable (95.5% after five cycles), effective in real wastewater, non-toxic, and energy efficient (7.8–21.4 kWh m−3).86 In transition metal doping, D. Xu et al. synthesized Co-doped ZIF-8 (CoxZn1−x-ZIF), where Co2+ replaced Zn2+ without changing the SOD structure (confirmed by XRD), and SEM-EDS/BET showed uniform distribution and increased surface area (1662 m2 g−1), while optical studies revealed bandgap reduction from 5.15 eV to ∼2.0 eV with extended visible absorption (450–700 nm), leading to enhanced photocatalytic MB degradation, with Co0.5Zn0.5-ZIF showing optimal performance (81%, k = 0.0228 min−1), though Co-rich samples suffered poor stability due to weak coordination; XPS, EPR, and Mott–Schottky analyses confirmed improved charge transfer, oxygen vacancies, and ROS generation (˙O2−, ˙OH), with a mechanism involving radical-driven degradation and good stability (83.7% after 12 h).87 Likewise, Y. Han et al. developed Ni-doped ZIF/CN composites, where Ni substitution in ZIF-67 integrated with g-C3N4 improved morphology and electronic properties, confirmed by XRD, FTIR, SEM, and XPS, while optical studies showed reduced bandgap (2.68 → 1.87 eV), enhanced visible absorption (500–700 nm), lower recombination (PL), and improved charge transport (EIS, photocurrent), resulting in nearly complete MO degradation (99.96%) with higher rate constants, making Ni-ZIF/CN the most efficient catalyst.88 Furthermore, in ternary systems, F. D. Ali et al. designed an AgCl/ZIF-8/C–TiO2 heterojunction, where structural studies (XRD, SEM, TEM) confirmed well-integrated phases and UV-vis showed enhanced absorption (up to 670 nm) with reduced bandgap (2.76 eV), while PL, EIS, and photocurrent indicated efficient charge separation, leading to 98% levofloxacin degradation in 60 minutes with a high rate constant (0.0433 min−1), excellent stability (96% after five cycles), and superoxide radicals as the dominant species; the mechanism involved electron transfer from AgCl to C–TiO2 and hole migration via ZIF-8 to generate ˙OH, resulting in effective pollutant mineralization (73.8% TOC removal).89

Defect engineering is an important strategy to improve photocatalytic performance by enhancing light absorption and charge transport, as shown in ZIF-derived systems. For example, Zhou et al. used ZIF-8 as both a zinc source and structural template to synthesize ZnS nanosheets with controlled sulfur and zinc vacancies for photocatalytic hydrogen production; by varying thiourea concentration, they tuned defect density, with ZnS-15 showing the highest vacancy concentration and best performance, while structural studies (XRD, Raman) confirmed a cubic sphalerite phase with lattice distortion due to defects, and XPS verified the presence of sulfur and zinc vacancies with altered electron density; TEM and SEM revealed transformation from bulk crystals to ultrathin nanosheets as defect density increased, improving charge transport and light absorption, leading to a high hydrogen evolution rate (3709.23 µmol g−1 in 5 h), supported by UV-vis and PL showing broadened absorption and reduced recombination, although excessive defects in ZnS-20 caused recombination losses; DFT calculations further showed that sulfur vacancies created impurity levels enabling visible-light activity, and the formation mechanism involved ZIF-8 dodecahedrons collapsing during hydrothermal treatment followed by sulfur diffusion and nanosheet growth via Ostwald ripening, with stability tests confirming that ZnS-15 retained activity over multiple cycles, highlighting the importance of optimized defect concentration.90 Similarly, L. Zhang and co-workers developed a defective ZIF-67 (ZIF-67 V) using VAN as a regulator to introduce linker vacancies and increase Co2+ active sites, where VAN competes with 2-methylimidazole for cobalt coordination and its aldehyde group reduces Co3+ to Co2+, resulting in more active sites and mesopore formation for improved diffusion; characterization (SEM, XRD, BET) showed retained crystallinity with smaller, rounded particles and mesoporosity, while HRTEM and SAED indicated increased lattice spacing, FTIR confirmed VAN incorporation, and XPS/EPR revealed higher Co2+ content and defect density; UV-vis and HPLC confirmed altered coordination environment without structural collapse, and photocatalytic water-splitting showed enhanced activity with AQE of 12.2% and TOF of 0.079 s−1 per Co, outperforming conventional ZIF-67 due to improved charge transfer and active site availability, with good stability over cycles, although excess VAN destabilized the structure and an optimal 2-MI : VAN ratio of 4 : 1 was required, demonstrating how controlled defect engineering improves photocatalytic oxygen evolution efficiency.91

Li et al. synthesized Co@NC/g-C3N4 composites by one-step thermal decomposition of ZIF-67, where carbonization produced nitrogen-doped carbon (NC) with highly dispersed Co atoms that enhanced electron capture from g-C3N4 and facilitated rapid charge transfer, resulting in a sixfold increase in hydrogen evolution compared to pure g-C3N4; structural studies (XRD, FTIR, SEM, TEM) confirmed mesopore formation and increased surface area, while XPS identified Co2+ species and UV-vis DRS showed a blue shift with bandgap increase (2.65 to 2.90 eV) due to N-doping and quantum confinement; the optimal Co@NC/g-C3N4-2 sample showed the highest H2 evolution rate (161 µmol g−1 h−1), proving that interfacial interaction, not physical mixing, drives activity, and photocurrent studies confirmed improved charge transport; stability tests showed consistent performance over three cycles, and removal of Co reduced activity, confirming its key role, with a mechanism involving light absorption by g-C3N4, electron transfer to Co@NC for H2 generation, and hole scavenging by TEOA, demonstrating strong synergy between Co@NC and g-C3N4 for efficient photocatalytic water splitting.92

Another key advantage of ZIF-based materials is their high adsorption capacity, which enhances photocatalysis by increasing contact between pollutants and active sites. For example, Vy Anh Tran et al. studied ZIF-8 nanoparticles for rhodamine B removal, where the material showed a very high surface area (1802 m2 g−1) and mesoporosity, enabling rapid adsorption equilibrium within 10 minutes, and improved performance under light with adsorption capacities of 9.05 mg g−1 (dark), 9.69 mg g−1 (visible), and 9.88 mg g−1 (UV); kinetic studies indicated pseudo-second-order behavior (chemisorption), while UV-vis, zeta potential, fluorescence, and DFT confirmed π–π interactions and electrostatic attraction with Zn centers, along with hydroxyl radical involvement in degradation, with a bandgap of ∼4.9 eV (close to DFT value), good recyclability (only 7.7% loss after four cycles), minimal effect from anions but reduced adsorption in the presence of Ca2+, and high structural and thermal stability up to 345 °C.93 Similarly, Padikkamannil Abishad et al. synthesized ZIF-8(Fe) nanoparticles using water, showing improved visible-light photocatalytic degradation of methylene blue and rhodamine B, where UV-vis indicated a 5.30 eV bandgap, PXRD confirmed crystallinity (36.10 nm size), SEM showed uniform spherical morphology, and FTIR verified bonding, with kinetic studies showing MB degraded twice as fast as RhB due to hydroxyl radical generation from excited electrons.94 In biocomposite systems, Niyaz Mohammad Mahmoodi et al. developed a chitosan/ZIF-8/TiO2 (CZT) bead catalyst via hydrothermal synthesis, where ZIF-8 improved adsorption of malachite green, TiO2 reduced recombination, and chitosan enabled bead formation (∼1 mm) for easy recovery; characterization (XRD, FESEM, FTIR, Raman, EDS, DRS, TGA) confirmed structure, bandgap tuning, and thermal stability, while photocatalytic efficiency depended on pH, catalyst dose, dye concentration, H2O2, and aeration, with good recyclability and better dispersion via in situ synthesis, making it a sustainable system.95 In membrane systems, YunZe Long et al. developed PTFE@ZIF-8 nanofiber membranes via electrospinning and in situ growth, where characterization confirmed uniform ZIF-8 distribution and strong mechanical/thermal stability, achieving high degradation efficiencies (99.98% MB, 99.99% RhB, 79% TC in 180 min) under visible light, with optimal performance at pH 7 and good recyclability over five cycles, driven by ROS (O2−, H2O2) formation.96 Finally, Swain et al. developed BaTiO3@ZIF-8 composites with different loadings, where Z/BTO-25 showed optimal performance due to better charge separation, surface area, and light absorption, confirmed by XRD, FTIR, Raman, XPS, PL, BET, SEM/TEM, and EDX, achieving 89% MB and 94% Congo red degradation under sunlight, with performance influenced by catalyst dose, pH, and dye concentration, and good stability over four cycles, following mainly pseudo-second-order kinetics, highlighting its efficiency for sustainable dye removal.97

ZIF-based systems also enable size-selective photocatalysis due to their well-defined microporous structure, as demonstrated by Hyun Sung Kim and co-workers, who encapsulated CdS quantum dots (QDs) into ZIF-8 using 2-mercaptoimidazole as a capping agent. Which is structurally similar to ZIF-8's imidazolate linker and thus allowed simultaneous QD stabilization and incorporation during framework growth. Unlike conventional agents such as PVP or MPA; the CdS QDs (3–5 nm), initially stabilized with oleic acid to prevent aggregation, retained their uniform size after ligand exchange as confirmed by TEM and FTIR. While XRD and nitrogen adsorption studies showed that ZIF-8 maintained its microporous structure with only slight porosity reduction due to QD loading. UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectra indicated that the optical properties of CdS QDs were preserved, with absorption increasing as QD content increased. Photocatalytic studies under blue LED light (415 nm) using methyl viologen (MV2+) and diquat (DQ2+) as redox mediators clearly demonstrated a molecular sieving effect, where smaller MV2+ molecules could diffuse through ZIF-8 pores and undergo photoreduction to MV+ (observed at 397 and 605 nm), while larger DQ2+ molecules were excluded, confirming size-selective reactivity. Importantly, 2-mercaptoimidazole provided better steric control and selectivity than other capping agents, enabling efficient charge transfer and selective reduction, thereby highlighting that CdS@ZIF-8 composites are highly effective for controlled, size-selective photocatalytic redox processes.98

Hybrid and composite ZIF-based systems significantly enhance photocatalytic performance by improving light absorption, charge separation, and surface reactivity. For instance, S. H. Ammar et al. developed a novel SPPy/ZIF-8 heterojunction, where XRD confirmed retention of ZIF-8 crystallinity and UV-vis DRS showed enhanced visible-light absorption with reduced bandgap, while PL indicated suppressed charge recombination; FESEM and TEM revealed partial coverage of ZIF-8 by SPPy and N2 adsorption showed mesoporosity with slight pore blockage, and photocatalytic tests identified SPPy (2 wt%)/ZIF-8 as optimal, achieving 98.5% Congo red degradation in 90 min and 92.7% amoxicillin degradation in 150 min with pseudo-first-order kinetics (k = 0.0443 and 0.0172 min−1), where electrons generated superoxide radicals and holes drove oxidation, confirmed by scavenger studies, with EIS showing low resistance, TOC confirming high mineralization (89% CR, 78% AMX), and stability tests proving better durability with minimal Zn2+ leaching.99 Similarly, B. Rabeie and N. M. Mahmoodi synthesized a ternary TiO2/GQD/ZIF-8 (T/G/Z) composite, where FTIR, Raman, and XRD confirmed integration and SEM showed uniform morphology, with mesoporosity aiding catalysis; optimal GQD loading (0.02 g) gave maximum doxycycline degradation, while excess caused aggregation, and photocatalysis followed first-order kinetics with superoxide radicals as the dominant species, supported by a mechanism involving TiO2 excitation enhanced by GQD sensitization and ZIF-8 electron transfer, reducing recombination, with good stability (only 2% loss after three cycles) and additional dye removal ability.100 In another system, Seyed Mohammad Samianifard et al. developed starch/ZIF-67/graphene oxide (SZ67GO) biocomposites, where FTIR, SEM, and XRD confirmed successful incorporation without structural loss, and SZ67GO-1 showed the best activity (86.4% acid blue 92 degradation in 135 min) under visible light due to synergistic effects, while adsorption in dark was low (20–24%), and performance improved with catalyst dosage but decreased with higher dye concentration, following zero-order kinetics, with ROS (˙OH, O2˙−) confirmed by trapping experiments and good recyclability over three cycles.101 Additionally, Mahboobeh Shahsavar et al. studied ZIF-Cu(mIm)2 as a new photocatalyst, where UV-vis showed a bandgap of 2.76 eV enabling visible-light activity, and degradation of malachite green proceeded via hydroxyl radicals, strongly influenced by pH (optimal at 10), dye concentration, catalyst dosage (optimal 0.01 g), and temperature (maximum 99.9% removal at 60 °C), although reusability declined from 99% to 74% after three cycles due to catalyst degradation, indicating durability limitations despite high efficiency.102

ZIF-based systems are highly effective for pharmaceutical pollutant degradation due to their tunable porosity, strong adsorption, and improved charge separation, as demonstrated by Iqra Rabani and co-workers, who developed ZIF-8@ZIF-67 and further optimized ZIF-8 structures with controllable pore sizes (9.1–6.5 Å) for the degradation of anticancer drugs under visible light; five variants (IPA, MEK, ACE, MeOH, and DI water) were synthesized, among which ZIF-8 (MeOH) showed the highest crystallinity, smallest pore size (6.5 Å), and largest surface area (2100 m2 g−1), as confirmed by XRD, XPS, FESEM, PALS, and BET analysis, leading to superior photocatalytic activity with 99.7% degradation of 5-fluorouracil in 80 minutes (k = 0.064 min−1) and 97.9% degradation of capecitabine in 70 minutes (k = 0.044 min−1), following pseudo-first-order kinetics; the catalyst also exhibited good stability with 88% efficiency retained after five cycles, while pH studies showed optimal performance at neutral pH (7) and reduced efficiency under acidic or basic conditions; mechanistic studies using scavengers such as K2Cr2O7, IPA, and TEOS confirmed that hydroxyl (˙OH) and superoxide (O2˙−) radicals are the main active species responsible for degradation, highlighting that optimized pore size, high surface area, and efficient charge separation make ZIF-8 (MeOH) a highly efficient and reusable photocatalyst for cytotoxic drug removal under visible light.103

In terms of stability, most ZIF-based photocatalysts show good recyclability and structural stability. ZIF-9 and ZIF-8 maintain performance over multiple cycles,75,93 while heterostructures and composites also show high stability due to strong interactions between components.83,96 However, excessive metal loading or defect concentration may reduce stability over time.87 Overall, heterojunction-based systems provide the best balance between activity and stability, followed by metal-doped and defect-engineered systems, while pure ZIFs generally require modification to achieve high photocatalytic performance.

ZIF-based photocatalysts have demonstrated significant potential in environmental remediation, hydrogen evolution, and CO2 reduction due to their light absorption, enhanced charge separation, and heterojunction formation. Various modifications, including metal doping (Co, Ni, Fe, Cu), heterostructure formation (ZIF-67/g-C3N4, ZIF-8/TiO2, ZnO/TiO2 NBT), and composite integration (AgCl/ZIF-8/C–TiO2, BaTiO3@ZIF-8), have improved photocatalytic efficiency by lowering bandgaps, increasing surface area, and promoting reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. In dye degradation applications, ZIF-based catalysts have achieved near-complete removal of pollutants like methylene blue, rhodamine B, and acid blue, often within 60–240 minutes under visible light. For antibiotic degradation, materials such as AgCl/ZIF-8/C–TiO2 and SPPy/ZIF-8 effectively removed levofloxacin and amoxicillin with high efficiency and recyclability. In CO2 reduction, heterostructures like ZMO/Z67 and Zn2GeO4/ZIF-67 enhanced methanol and ethanol production, demonstrating superior selectivity and conversion rates compared to pure MOFs or oxides. For hydrogen evolution, composites like Co@NC/g-C3N4 and g-C3N4/ZIF-67 significantly improved H2 production rates by efficiently transferring photogenerated electrons. The incorporation of MOFs into photocatalytic frameworks has also enabled long-term stability and multi-cycle reusability, ensuring practical application viability. These findings confirm that engineered ZIF-based photocatalysts are highly effective for sustainable environmental and energy-related applications.

The wide optical band gaps reported for several materials discussed above, 5.00 eV, 5.15 eV, 5.30 eV, and 4.9 eV for ZIF-8 and ZIF-L, are inconsistent with their subsequent description as visible-light-active photocatalysts, since band gaps of this magnitude fall well outside the visible range. This apparent contradiction is not fully resolved by the data presented. A substantial fraction of the reported dye-removal performance may instead be attributable to dark adsorption, a well-documented strength of ZIF-8's high microporosity, or to dye-sensitized pathways in which the adsorbed dye chromophore, rather than the framework itself, absorbs the visible photon and injects charge into the catalyst. Confirming genuine visible-light photocatalytic activity requires dark-adsorption equilibration prior to irradiation, a photolysis (no-catalyst) control, and mineralization metrics such as TOC or COD reduction rather than decolorization alone, since decolorization can occur via adsorption or dye degradation without ring-opening or full mineralization. In the specific case of Co doping reportedly reducing the apparent band gap from 5.15 eV to 2.0 eV, we note that Tauc-plot analysis in this energy window is more consistent with Co2+ d–d and ligand-to-metal charge-transfer absorption features than with a genuine band-to-band electronic transition, and this result should be interpreted accordingly rather than as true band-gap narrowing.

Electrocatalytic application

Electrocatalysis, a crucial field in catalytic science, employs an electric field to modulate Gibbs free energy, optimizing catalyst–reactant interactions for enhanced efficiency. Occurring at the interface of electrocatalyst, reactant, and electrolyte, these reactions involve adsorption, conversion, desorption, mass diffusion, and electron transfer, making them vital for applications such as fuel cells, water splitting, CO2 reduction, and selective hydrogenation104–108 Understanding reaction mechanisms through thermodynamics, kinetics, electric double-layer effects, and mass transport is essential for electrocatalyst development, where noble metals like Pt, Au, Ru, and Ir have been widely used, though their cost and availability drive the search for non-precious alternatives.109–116 Structural transformations during electrocatalysis, influenced by electrolyte composition, applied potential, and thermodynamic stability, range from surface atomic rearrangements to bulk phase transitions, with pH-dependent redox reactions often dictating morphological evolution.117–119 Stability remains a challenge due to harsh electrochemical environments that induce degradation via dissolution, phase segregation, and surface reconstruction. Strategies to mitigate these effects include electronic modulation—such as oxidation-state control and lattice oxygen mechanisms—and topographical engineering, which tailors surface dynamics to optimize reaction pathways while preventing overoxidation and structural collapse.120–123 Additional stabilization approaches involve strong catalyst–support interactions, self-healing mechanisms, optimized reaction cycles, and novel material engineering techniques like selective doping and nanoparticle shape control.124–126 Future advancements in electrocatalysis will depend on integrating these strategies to enhance activity, selectivity, and longevity, paving the way for more efficient, scalable industrial applications.127–129

The oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) remains the main testing ground. Pyrolysis is not always required: ball-milled, non-pyrolysed ZIF-67 showed strong alkaline ORR activity, which spectroscopic and theoretical analyses trace to coordinatively unsaturated Co–N4−x sites that convert to Co–N4−x(OH) under reaction conditions.130 For pyrolysed catalysts, mass transport is a recurring limit. Pre-oxidation introduces oxygen species and lattice strain that etch the framework during pyrolysis, turning micropores into mesopores. The resulting catalyst had a mass activity of 205.98 A g−1, against 56.24 A g−1 for commercial Pt/C, and kept nearly 100% activity for 48 h.131

Ligand and precursor control matter as well. A ZIF-L-derived Fe–N–C catalyst prepared with a phenanthroline ligand gave a half-wave potential of 0.883 V in 0.1 M KOH, with only a 16 mV negative shift after 2500 cycles.132 Controlled pyrolysis of Fe-doped ZIF-8 has likewise been used to tune dodecahedral Fe–N–C ORR catalysts.133 Bifunctional and multi-reaction systems are also advancing. In a ZIF-derived catalyst with FeCo nanoparticles on FeN4 sites, DFT indicates the synergy lowers the Fe d-band center and weakens *OH binding, which improves intermediate adsorption, and the catalyst was tested in zinc–air batteries.134 Beyond oxygen electrocatalysis, a RuNi-ZIF-8 precursor calcined at 900 °C gave a Ni single-atom catalyst on a carbon–nitrogen framework for nitrate-to-ammonia conversion and the oxygen evolution reaction.135 Two reviews summarise the field: one covers ZIF-8 as a precursor for ORR and methanol oxidation catalysts and as a fuel-cell membrane component,136 and another covers imidazole-based MOFs for photo- and electrocatalytic CO2 conversion, including in situ characterization and machine learning (Fig. 2).137

Schematic overview of challenges in electrocatalysis and the ZIF-derived solution: pyrolytic transformation of zeolitic imidazolate frameworks into metal–nitrogen-doped porous carbon with high-density M–Nx active sites and hierarchical porosity, enabling enhanced activity and stability for ORR/OER, HER, CO2RR, and MOR.

Zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs) have emerged as promising precursors for advanced electrocatalysts due to their unique structural and chemical properties. These metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) consist of transition metal ions (such as Co, Ni, Fe, Mn, and Cu) coordinated with nitrogen-rich organic linkers (e.g., imidazolate), forming highly porous, thermally stable, and tunable architectures.159–162 Upon pyrolysis, ZIFs transform into metal-doped carbon-based materials with uniformly dispersed active sites, high surface areas, and conductive networks—key features that enhance their electrocatalytic performance.163–169

One of the most critical factors contributing to ZIF-derived catalysts' efficiency is the formation of M–Nx (metal–nitrogen) active sites, which closely resemble the active centers of enzymes and noble metal catalysts. These sites facilitate electron transfer, optimize adsorption energy for reactants, and improve catalytic selectivity.170–173 Additionally, the hierarchical porosity of ZIF-derived materials enhances mass transport and electrolyte accessibility, reducing diffusion limitations in reactions such as the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), oxygen evolution reaction (OER), hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR), and methanol oxidation reaction (MOR).167,174,175

ZIFs also allow for precise elemental doping (e.g., N, S, F, P), further modifying their electronic structure to optimize catalytic activity. The synergy between metal oxides (e.g., Co3O4, Mn3O4) and carbonaceous matrices enhances conductivity, corrosion resistance, and catalytic durability.163,176 These properties make ZIF-derived electrocatalysts superior to conventional materials, offering cost-effective, high-performance alternatives to noble metals like Pt, Ir, and Ru177,178 and the above discussion is summarised in Table 3.

Studies on ZIF-based electrocatalysts

A key strategy in ZIF-derived systems is structural engineering using hollow and hierarchically porous architectures, which improves mass transport and exposes more active sites for catalysis. For instance, YaYan et al. prepared a hollow nanocarbon polyhedron with embedded ultrafine Co nanoparticles (NC@Co/NC) by pyrolyzing ZIF-8@ZIF-67 core–shell structures, where the ZIF-8 core formed a hollow carbon framework and the ZIF-67 shell converted into a Co/NC layer, increasing active sites and enhancing diffusion. Structural studies showed uniform polyhedral morphology with ∼8 nm Co nanoparticles encapsulated in carbon, high Co–N content (40.8%), and improved conductivity. Electrochemical results demonstrated activity in hydrazine oxidation, oxygen reduction, and hydrogen peroxide reduction, with low charge-transfer resistance, high turnover frequency (0.0746 s−1), and strong stability (95% retention after 500 cycles). Active M–Nx sites in pyrolyzed ZIF-derived electrocatalysts are frequently assigned on the basis of XPS N 1s peak deconvolution alone. This method cannot reliably distinguish pyridinic/pyrrolic M–Nx coordination from co-existing metallic or carbide nanoparticles embedded in the same carbon matrix, nor can it quantify the catalytic contribution of each phase. Rigorous site assignment requires complementary techniques: X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XANES/EXAFS) to resolve oxidation state and first-shell coordination number, Mössbauer spectroscopy for Fe-containing systems to differentiate Fe–Nx from Fe-carbide or Fe-oxide phases, and selective site-poisoning experiments (e.g., SCN− or CO titration) to isolate the fractional contribution of M–Nx sites to the overall measured activity. Where the studies discussed here relied on XPS deconvolution alone, the corresponding M–Nx site assignments should be regarded as tentative.

Zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIF)-derived electrocatalysts have demonstrated outstanding performance across various electrochemical reactions, including the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), oxygen evolution reaction (OER), hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR), and methanol oxidation reaction (MOR). Structural modifications such as metal doping (Co, Ni, Cu, Fe, Mn, Mo), heteroatom incorporation (N, S, F), and nanostructuring (core–shell, hollow, or 2D nanosheets) have significantly enhanced catalytic activity, stability, and selectivity.

For ORR, catalysts like CNTs@Co–N-PC, S-CoNPC, and Co@CNT-NC exhibited half-wave potentials (0.813–0.87 V vs. RHE) approaching, and in select cases modestly exceeding, typical commercial Pt/C benchmarks (0.85–0.90 V vs. RHE), attributed to well-dispersed Co–N active sites, hierarchical porosity, and enhanced electron transfer. OER performance was improved in materials such as CoMo-30, Co3O4/Mn3O4/CNx@CNFs, and CoNiM fluorides, achieving low overpotentials (η < 300 mV at 10 mA cm−2) due to synergistic metal-oxide interactions and surface reconstruction. HER activity was optimized in Ni-ZIF/NC (η 163 mV in alkaline) through Ni–Nx site stabilization and porous carbon networks. CO2RR catalysts like Fe-SA/ZIF and Cu-ZIF-8 selectively converted CO2 to CO with faradaic efficiencies above 95%, benefiting from atomically dispersed metal sites.

Across all applications, these comparisons to Pt/C are sensitive to catalyst loading, electrolyte, and rotation rate, which are not uniformly reported across the compiled studies, and do not support a categorical claim of superiority over Pt/C. Durability in the compiled studies is also reported using incompatible metrics, chronoamperometric retention (seconds to hours), potential-cycling counts, and total operating hours, which cannot be directly compared on a common basis and are presented here as reported, not as equivalent measures of stability. Acidic and alkaline ORR durability are not distinguished in the summary above, despite the mechanism of M–N–C degradation differing substantially between the two media (demetallation and carbon corrosion dominate in acid; Fenton-type Nx-site oxidation and carbon oxidation dominate in alkaline media); this distinction should be considered when comparing durability figures across entries.

Heterogeneous catalysis

Catalytic processes are fundamental to chemical manufacturing, accounting for approximately 25% of industrial energy consumption, with the most energy-intensive reactions relying on heterogeneous catalysis. Enhancing catalytic efficiency is a key strategy for reducing energy usage and environmental impact. Heterogeneous catalysts, which exist in a phase distinct from reactants or products, were traditionally developed through empirical screening, as demonstrated by the ammonia synthesis process in the early 1900s. These catalysts possess diverse active sites, including solid acid sites that facilitate proton donation and electron withdrawal, as well as redox sites where valence state fluctuations drive catalytic activity, often involving ionic or covalent bond formation. The distinction between redox and metal sites depends on atomic dispersion versus bulk metal properties.157,158 Several factors influence catalytic activity, such as particle size, exposed facets, under-coordinated sites, and pore structure, while electronic interactions optimize adsorbate-substrate binding. Confinement effects, particularly in zeolites, enhance catalytic efficiency due to localized pressure effects.180–182 Among heterogeneous catalysts, bimetallic nanoparticles and metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) have gained attention for applications in electrochemical energy storage, gas separation, sensing, and catalysis. Bimetallic MOFs exhibit catalytic performance over monometallic catalysts due to synergistic metal interactions and facile synthesis, making them promising for energy conversion and storage.183–185

Within the broader category of MOFs, zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs) have emerged as particularly versatile heterogeneous catalysts, offering tunable porosity, high surface area, and well-defined metal–organic structures.17,186,187 Their unique properties enable them to catalyze a diverse range of organic transformations and environmental processes with high efficiency.163 Various ZIF-based catalysts, including Ni-ZIF-L, ZIF-78, ZIF-9, ZIF-12, and ZIF-8, have demonstrated remarkable catalytic activity. For instance, Ni-ZIF-L efficiently catalyzes ethylene dimerization, leveraging its high external surface area for enhanced Ni dispersion and reactivity. ZIF-78 facilitates CO2 cycloaddition with epoxides, utilizing dual Lewis acidic (Zn) and basic (–NH) sites for efficient carbon fixation without co-catalysts or solvents. Cobalt-based ZIF-9 and ZIF-12 activate peroxymonosulfate (PMS) for rhodamine B degradation, with differences in morphology and porosity influencing activation kinetics and degradation efficiency. Meanwhile, ZIF-8 catalyzes the dehydrogenation of alcohols to carboxylates via Zn–N coordination sites, providing a sustainable and reusable alternative to noble metal catalysts. These studies emphasize the importance of ZIF structural properties—such as pore size, topology, and functionalization—in optimizing catalytic performance across various reactions. Their stability, recyclability, and ability to function under mild conditions position ZIFs as promising candidates for green and cost-effective catalytic applications in organic synthesis and environmental remediation.188–192

CO2 conversion and cycloaddition reactions

The catalytic conversion of CO2 into value-added chemicals has become a key focus in sustainable chemistry, with zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs), particularly ZIF-67, showing outstanding efficiency in CO2 cycloaddition with epoxides. Bibimaryam Mousavi et al. synthesized cobalt-based ZIF-67 via a simple room-temperature method and demonstrated its catalytic performance under mild, solvent-free, and co-catalyst-free conditions (Scheme 2, ref. 193), achieving >99% conversion at optimized conditions (100 mg catalyst, 18 mmol substrate, 100 °C, 8 bar CO2, 8 h). Comprehensive characterization (XRD, TGA, BET, FT-IR, SEM, ICP-AES) confirmed its crystalline microporous structure, high surface area, thermal stability up to 400 °C, and negligible cobalt leaching. The catalyst was essential for the reaction, as no product formed in its absence, and it retained activity over at least four cycles without structural degradation, outperforming MOF-5 and ZIF-8 in terms of stability and efficiency. Increased catalyst loading and CO2 pressure enhanced conversion, whereas excessive temperature reduced recyclability due to framework degradation. Mechanistically, ZIF-67 operates via a Lewis acid–base pathway where Co(ii)/Co(iii) sites activate the epoxide and nitrogen atoms activate CO2, with polar pore walls facilitating interaction, leading to cyclic carbonate formation through ring opening and cyclization. Further advancements include the hollow bimetallic H-ZnCo-ZIF developed by Bo Tang et al. through tannic acid etching, which preserved the rhombic dodecahedral framework while creating a hollow structure that significantly improved mass transfer and catalytic efficiency (Scheme 3, ref. 194); although its BET surface area (920 m2 g−1) was lower than that of solid ZnCo-ZIF (1529 m2 g−1), mesoporosity and enhanced diffusion led to more than double the turnover number (TON). Structural and compositional analyses (SEM, TEM, EDS, XRD, FT-IR) confirmed integrity, while TGA showed stability up to ∼550 °C; CO2 adsorption behavior was modified, and the etching process was self-limiting. Catalytically, H-ZnCo-ZIF outperformed solid ZnCo-ZIF and monometallic ZIFs, showed no Zn/Co leaching, and maintained activity over six cycles, unlike solid ZnCo-ZIF, which showed ∼13% deactivation. Terminal epoxides exhibited higher reactivity than internal ones due to steric effects, and optimal conditions were 7 bar CO2 and 100–140 °C, while the addition of tetraalkylammonium halides such as nBu4NBr enabled high yields even at room temperature. The mechanism involves bifunctional activation where Zn/Co sites activate the epoxide, nitrogen sites activate CO2, and nucleophiles open the epoxide ring to form intermediates that cyclize into cyclic carbonates, with the hollow structure enhancing accessibility and diffusion. Similarly, D. Ma et al. developed Fe@ZIF-67 using a solvent-free solid-state thermal (SST) method for direct iron incorporation into the framework (ref. 195), offering a scalable and environmentally friendly alternative to solvothermal synthesis; XRD showed preserved crystallinity at low Fe content but reduced order at higher loading, BET analysis revealed decreased surface area but increased pore width, ICP confirmed up to 50% Fe incorporation, and TGA indicated slightly reduced thermal stability due to defects. FT-IR and XPS revealed hydroxyl groups and altered electronic environments that enhanced catalytic activity, and the catalyst showed performance and recyclability in CO2 cycloaddition. The mechanism involves cooperative activation by Fe2+/Fe3+ and Co2+ Lewis acid sites and nitrogen-based basic sites, leading to epoxide activation, CO2 polarization, nucleophilic ring opening, and intramolecular cyclization to form cyclic carbonates. Additionally, Y.-F. Lin et al. synthesized ZIF-78 via a hydrothermal method and demonstrated its catalytic efficiency for CO2 cycloaddition with propylene oxide without co-catalysts or solvents (Scheme 4,189); ZIF-78 consists of Zn nodes and nitro-functionalized imidazolate linkers that enhance CO2 adsorption. PXRD, FESEM, FTIR, and TGA confirmed crystallinity, morphology, functional groups, and stability up to 350 °C, and catalytic studies showed that the catalyst was essential, achieving 61% selectivity and 54% yield at 150 °C and 0.98 MPa CO2, with lower temperatures and higher pressures favoring product formation. Compared to Al–Mg–O and MOF-5, ZIF-78 offers a more sustainable and simpler system with easy recovery due to its heterogeneous nature. Mechanistically, it follows a Lewis acid–base dual activation pathway where Zn centers activate the epoxide and nitrogen sites activate CO2, promoting ring opening, intermediate formation, and cyclization into propylene carbonate efficiently.

Transesterification and esterification reactions

Cheng Gao and co-workers developed an environmentally friendly heterogeneous catalyst, ZIF-67, for the synthesis of ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) via transesterification of dimethyl carbonate (DMC) and diethyl carbonate (DEC) (Scheme 5), where ZIF-67 showed catalytic performance compared to ZIF-8, achieving an EMC yield of 83.39% due to its higher surface area (912 m2 g−1) and the presence of balanced Lewis acid and base sites confirmed by NH3-TPD and CO2-TPD analyses. Structural characterization using XRD, nitrogen adsorption–desorption, SEM, and TGA revealed that ZIF-67 possesses a crystalline microporous framework with particle sizes of 300–500 nm and thermal stability up to 400 °C. Reaction optimization demonstrated that no EMC formation occurred without the catalyst, confirming its essential role, and the optimal conditions were a 1 : 1 molar ratio of DMC to DEC, 100 °C, and 2% catalyst loading. Temperature had a strong influence, with EMC yield increasing from 2.35% at 70 °C to 83.39% at 100 °C, after which no significant improvement was observed, while reaction time studies showed gradual yield increase up to 24 hours before equilibrium was reached. A leaching test confirmed true heterogeneous catalysis, as removal of ZIF-67 after 18 hours did not affect the final yield, and recyclability studies showed that the catalyst retained activity over four cycles with no structural changes as confirmed by XRD. Unlike ZIF-8, which has stronger acid–base sites but lower catalytic efficiency, ZIF-67 benefits from moderate acid–base strength that enhances adsorption and selectivity. Mechanistically, the reaction proceeds via Lewis acid–base activation, where preheating ZIF-67 at 150 °C removes coordinated methanol and water to expose more active cobalt sites; DMC and DEC adsorb onto these sites, followed by carbonyl activation, formation of methoxide and ethoxide intermediates, nucleophilic attack on carbonate groups, and formation of EMC, which then desorbs to regenerate the active sites, with balanced acid–base sites ensuring efficient activation and selectivity.196 Further enhancement was achieved by J. Cheng et al. through the development of a bifunctional catalyst by incorporating phosphotungstic acid (HPW) into ZIF-67 for biodiesel production from microalgal lipids (Scheme 6), where the HPW/ZIF-67 catalyst showed an excellent conversion efficiency of 98.5% due to the synergistic combination of strong Brønsted acid sites from HPW and Lewis acid–base sites from ZIF-67. Characterization using FT-IR, XPS, XRD, pyridine FT-IR, and CO2-TPD confirmed successful HPW incorporation, which modified the electronic environment and increased active sites; XPS showed disruption of Co–N bonds, formation of N–O bonds, and an increase in the Co3+/Co2+ ratio, enhancing acidity and catalytic performance. Pyridine FT-IR and CO2-TPD confirmed that increasing HPW content increased both Brønsted and Lewis acidity, though excessive HPW caused surface accumulation, blocking pores and reducing efficiency. Structural studies showed that HPW incorporation transformed ZIF-67 from microporous to mesoporous, improving mass transfer initially but causing pore blockage at higher loadings. Optimal reaction conditions were an oil-to-methanol molar ratio of 1 : 20, 3 wt% catalyst loading, and 200 °C for 2 hours, with maximum conversion at optimal HPW loading, while excess HPW reduced lipid diffusion and catalytic activity. Product analysis showed formation of fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs), with higher HPW favoring polyunsaturated FAMEs, though excessive loading led to oxidative degradation and reduced yield. The catalyst exhibited good recyclability and resistance to free fatty acids, confirming its stability and efficiency. Mechanistically, the process involves two reactions: esterification and transesterification, where Brønsted acid sites protonate free fatty acids to increase electrophilicity and facilitate reaction with methanol to form FAMEs and water, while Lewis acid sites from Co2+ activate triglyceride carbonyl groups for nucleophilic attack by methanol, leading to ester bond cleavage, glycerol formation, and additional FAMEs; the synergistic effect of Brønsted and Lewis acid sites, along with the mesoporous structure, enhances mass transfer and catalytic efficiency while maintaining stability over multiple cycles.197

Knoevenagel condensation reactions

ZIF-based catalysts such as ZIF-8, ZIF-9, and ZIF-67 have shown excellent performance in Knoevenagel condensation reactions due to their tunable porosity and acid–base properties. Lien T. L. Nguyen et al. synthesized ZIF-9 using cobalt nitrate hexahydrate and benzimidazole in DMF via a solvothermal method and characterized it using SEM, TEM, XRD, FT-IR, thermal analysis, and BET measurements, where elemental analysis showed a cobalt content of 4.17 mmol g−1 and an average particle size of 215 µm. SEM and XRD confirmed a well-defined crystalline structure with high crystallinity, while TEM revealed a porous framework containing both micropores and mesopores, and FT-IR confirmed complete deprotonation of benzimidazole. Thermal studies showed negligible weight loss between 200–500 °C with decomposition beyond 500 °C and 53% weight retention up to 900 °C, while a high BET surface area of 1144 m2 g−1 indicated strong catalytic potential. ZIF-9 was applied as a heterogeneous catalyst in the Knoevenagel condensation of benzaldehyde with malononitrile (Scheme 7), achieving 99% conversion at room temperature in toluene using 5 mol% catalyst and a 1 : 3 reactant ratio within 4 hours. Lower ratios (1 : 2 and 1 : 1) slowed the reaction, reducing conversion, while increasing catalyst loading to 8 mol% did not improve performance, and decreasing it to 3 mol% and 1 mol% reduced conversion to 81% and 51%, respectively. Control experiments confirmed true heterogeneous catalysis, as no reaction proceeded after catalyst removal. Solvent effects showed tetrahydrofuran as the fastest (2 h), followed by toluene (4 h), ethyl acetate (88% in 6 h), while dichloromethane was ineffective; the higher rate in tetrahydrofuran was attributed to partial fragmentation of ZIF-9, exposing more active sites. Comparison with ZIF-8 and ZIF-10 showed catalytic activity in the order ZIF-10 > ZIF-8 > ZIF-9, linked to differences in hydrophobicity. Recyclability studies showed >99% conversion for three cycles, decreasing slightly to 97% and 93% in the fourth and fifth cycles, with FT-IR and XRD confirming retained crystallinity despite minor structural changes. Substrate scope studies revealed that both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing substituted benzaldehydes gave high conversions, with electron-withdrawing groups reacting faster (complete in 1 h) and methyl-substituted derivatives achieving full conversion within 2 h. Mechanistically, ZIF-9 acts as a base catalyst where benzimidazole sites deprotonate malononitrile to form an enolate, which attacks the benzaldehyde carbonyl to form an intermediate that undergoes dehydration to give the α,β-unsaturated product, with the porous structure stabilizing intermediates and enhancing reaction rate.198 Similarly, Uyen P. N. Tran et al. synthesized ZIF-8 from zinc nitrate hexahydrate and 2-methylimidazole in DMF followed by solvent exchange with dichloromethane, resulting in a material with 4.2 mmol g−1 Zn loading, cubic morphology (100–200 µm, avg. 143 µm), high crystallinity (XRD peak at 2θ = 7.2°), high surface area (1705 m2 g−1), and pore size of 10.1 Å with narrow pore windows (3.4 Å), restricting bulky molecules; thermal stability was maintained up to 400 °C and FT-IR confirmed complete ligand deprotonation. ZIF-8 efficiently catalyzed the Knoevenagel condensation of benzaldehyde with malononitrile at room temperature in toluene (Scheme 8), achieving full conversion in 2 h at a 4 : 1 reactant ratio and 5 mol% catalyst loading, while lower catalyst loading (1 mol%) reduced conversion to 85% in 6 h. The catalyst outperformed many Lewis acids but was slightly less active than amine-functionalized materials, and control experiments confirmed heterogeneous catalysis with negligible Zn leaching; Zn(NO3)2 showed poor activity and free ligand was not reusable. Recyclability studies showed >88% conversion after five cycles with no structural degradation, and smaller particles showed higher activity due to increased surface area. Substrate scope indicated that electron-withdrawing substituents enhanced reactivity, while polar solvents such as methanol inhibited the reaction due to the hydrophobic nature of ZIF-8. Mechanistically, ZIF-8 acts via Lewis basic nitrogen sites that deprotonate malononitrile to form a carbanion, which attacks benzaldehyde to form an intermediate that dehydrates to the final product, with catalysis occurring mainly on the external surface due to restricted pore size.192 Furthermore, Nandigama Satish Kumar et al. developed a highly efficient Knoevenagel condensation method using ZIF-8 and ZIF-67 for rapid reactions of isatins and indeno[1,2-b]quinoxalin-11-ones with malononitrile, achieving completion within 10 minutes (Scheme 9); characterization confirmed retention of sodalite structures (PXRD), metal–ligand coordination (FT-IR), rhombic dodecahedral morphology (FE-SEM), particle sizes of 100 nm to 1 µm, and thermal stability up to 400 °C. Optimization showed DMF as the best solvent, though ethanol also gave good results as a greener alternative, and ZIF-67 showed better performance than ZIF-8 under optimized conditions (10 mg catalyst, 70 °C, DMF). The method was applicable to various substrates with excellent yields, and gram-scale synthesis confirmed scalability with minimal catalyst (50 mg). Recyclability studies showed stable performance over four cycles with negligible metal leaching (ICP), and structural integrity was confirmed by PXRD and IR, while control experiments with individual components (Zn2+, Co2+, H-Meim) gave lower yields, highlighting the importance of the intact framework. Mechanistically, the reaction involves synergistic activation where Zn2+/Co2+ centers activate the carbonyl group, while H-Meim deprotonates malononitrile to form a nucleophile, which attacks the activated carbonyl, followed by dehydration to give the final product, and the product desorbs, regenerating the catalyst, with this dual activation enhancing reaction rate and efficiency under mild and green conditions.199

Oxidation and redox transformations

ZIF-based catalysts have shown significant potential in oxidation and redox reactions due to their tunable structure and redox-active metal centers. V. D. Nguyen et al. synthesized ZIF-67 by reacting 2-methylimidazole with cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (73% yield) and characterized it using XRD, SEM, TEM, nitrogen adsorption–desorption, TGA, and FT-IR (Scheme 10), where XRD confirmed a highly crystalline structure with a peak at 2θ = 7.6°, SEM showed cubic crystals (<500 µm), and nitrogen adsorption revealed micropores (<20 Å) with high Langmuir (1809 m2 g−1) and BET (1309 m2 g−1) surface areas, despite TEM not clearly showing porosity; TGA confirmed excellent stability between 200–400 °C, and structural analysis indicated a 3D porous framework with 12 Å cages. This catalyst was applied in the cascade redox condensation of 2-nitroaniline with benzylamine to form 2-phenylbenzimidazole without external oxidants or reductants, achieving 97% yield under optimal conditions (140 °C, 5 mol% catalyst, 3 : 1 benzylamine ratio, DMSO, 2 M concentration), and the catalyst was essential as no reaction occurred without it. ZIF-67 outperformed several MOFs (Co-MOF-74, Fe-MOFs, Zn-MOF-74, Co-ZIF-4, Co-ZIF-9) and showed activity comparable to homogeneous Co salts but with easier recovery and minimal contamination; heterogeneous nature was confirmed by hot filtration and negligible Co leaching (0.1 mol%). A Co(ii)/Co(iii) redox cycle enabled simultaneous benzylamine oxidation and nitro reduction, and the reaction worked under air, O2, and Ar. The catalyst retained 89% yield after seven cycles with minimal structural changes (XRD, FT-IR, TEM) and slight BET decrease (1309 → 1119 m2 g−1), and ICP showed small Co loss. Substrate scope included substituted benzylamines and nitroanilines with moderate to high yields (56–87%), influenced by electron-donating and withdrawing groups. Mechanistically, benzylamine is oxidized to benzylimine, nitroaniline reduced to phenylenediamine, followed by condensation and aromatization to benzimidazole via a four-step cascade at Co redox sites.200 Similarly, Fereshteh Hosseini Eshbala et al. developed ZIF-9@Zeolite as a green heterogeneous catalyst for selective oxidation of benzylic alcohols to aldehydes (Scheme 11), synthesized under ambient conditions and characterized by FTIR, SEM, TEM, EDX, XRD, and BET, where FTIR confirmed integration of ZIF-9 with zeolite, SEM/TEM showed ∼20 nm particles, XRD confirmed retained crystallinity, and BET showed increased surface area (14.5 → 225 m2 g−1). The oxidation of benzyl alcohol using H2O2 was optimized in water at 80 °C with a 2 : 1 oxidant ratio, giving 98% yield with high selectivity, while excess oxidant caused overoxidation. The catalyst worked well for substituted substrates and was reusable for four cycles, though gradual deactivation occurred due to coke-like deposits observed in FTIR. Mechanistically, cobalt sites activate H2O2 to generate reactive oxygen species, which oxidize benzyl alcohol via radical or alkoxide intermediates to benzaldehyde, while the zeolite support enhances dispersion and selectivity.201 In another approach, F. Li et al. developed metal–imidazole–salen complexes encapsulated in Y zeolite. Although this system employs a conventional zeolite rather than a ZIF, it is included here as a structural and mechanistic benchmark: the cage-confinement strategy it demonstrates—preventing metal leaching and maintaining monomeric active-site dispersion—parallels the encapsulation approach used in ZIF-9@Zeolite (Scheme 11) and informs the design rationale for future ZIF-based single-site oxidation catalysts. DFT showed Cu(ii)- and Zn(ii)–salen complexes undergo electron transfer and adopt square planar geometry for accessibility; FT-IR, UV-vis, and XRD confirmed successful encapsulation without damaging the framework, while nitrogen adsorption showed reduced surface area due to pore occupation, and TGA confirmed improved stability. Among catalysts, Cu-salenIIY showed the best performance for benzyl alcohol oxidation, achieving maximum conversion in 4 h at 60 °C and maintaining activity over six cycles with only 10.9% loss, outperforming Zn analogues and free complexes, which suffered from aggregation and poor recyclability. The zeolite cage prevented leaching and maintained monomeric dispersion. Mechanistically, benzyl alcohol coordinates to Cu(ii), undergoes oxidation to benzaldehyde via electron transfer, and Cu(i) is reoxidized to Cu(ii), completing the catalytic cycle (Scheme 12).202 Additionally, J.-F. Gu, C. Chen, and Z.-H. Zheng studied ZIF-8 for the heterogeneous dehydrogenation of alcohols to carboxylates (Scheme 13), where ZIF-8 synthesized at room temperature showed high porosity (1300–1900 m2 g−1), crystalline structure (XRD), Zn–N coordination (FT-IR), rhombic dodecahedral morphology (∼171.5 nm, SEM/TEM), and thermal stability up to 250 °C. Catalytically, ZIF-8 converted benzyl alcohol to benzoic acid in mesitylene with KOH under reflux, achieving 86% yield, with KOH being the most effective base; without catalyst, only 4% conversion occurred. The catalyst showed broad substrate scope, where electron-donating groups enhanced reactivity, electron-withdrawing groups reduced yields, steric hindrance decreased activity, and aliphatic alcohols required longer times, while amino alcohols were selectively converted to amino acids. ZIF-8 remained stable over five cycles without significant loss. Mechanistically, KOH forms benzyl alkoxide, Zn sites assist hydride abstraction to form intermediates (radical/aldehyde), which undergo further oxidation to benzoic acid, with the robust ZIF-8 framework ensuring stability and recyclability.203

Environmental catalysis and advanced oxidation processes

ZIF-based catalysts have been widely explored for environmental remediation, especially in advanced oxidation processes due to their high surface area, tunable porosity, and redox-active metal centers. Kun-Yi Andrew Lin and Hsuan-Ang Chang synthesized ZIF-67 by reacting cobalt ions with 2-methylimidazole and characterized it using SEM, TEM, XRD, FT-IR, TGA, and zeta potential analysis, confirming a sodalite (SOD) structure where the cobalt-to-ligand ratio influenced morphology, with a 1 : 4 ratio producing well-defined crystals, and cobalt nitrate yielding smaller particles; TGA showed stability up to 800 °C with ligand decomposition between 250–500 °C, and BET analysis revealed a high surface area of 1717 m2 g−1. This material was applied for Rhodamine B (RB) degradation using peroxymonosulfate (PMS) (Scheme 14), where ZIF-67 alone showed poor adsorption and PMS alone was ineffective, but their combination significantly enhanced degradation due to PMS activation into sulfate radicals; increasing catalyst loading from 10 to 50 mg L−1 accelerated degradation, achieving nearly complete removal within 60 minutes. Minimal cobalt leaching (0.8 mg L−1) confirmed predominantly heterogeneous catalysis, and the reaction followed pseudo-first-order kinetics with rate constants increasing from 0.015 min−1 (PMS alone) to 0.793 min−1 (with 50 mg L−1 ZIF-67). The optimal RB/PMS/ZIF-67 ratio was 1/3/1, and temperature strongly influenced the rate, with faster kinetics at higher temperatures (up to 2.02 min−1 at 60 °C). pH affected performance, where acidic conditions reduced adsorption due to positive surface charge but still allowed degradation due to PMS stability, while alkaline conditions improved adsorption but reduced efficiency due to PMS self-decomposition. UV irradiation and ultrasonication enhanced performance, with ultrasonication significantly boosting the rate (10.25 min−1). The mechanism involves Co2+/Co3+ redox cycling to activate PMS and generate sulfate radicals, and the catalyst retained >90% activity after three cycles with no structural changes, outperforming Co3O4 due to its higher surface area and porosity.204 Similarly, T.-B. Nguyen et al. developed nitrogen-doped ZIF-67 (N@ZIF-67) for PMS activation to degrade ciprofloxacin (CIP), where characterization (XRD, FTIR, XPS) confirmed nitrogen incorporation causing defects and lattice changes, BET showed reduced surface area due to pore blockage, and microscopy revealed smaller truncated cubic morphology. N@ZIF-67 achieved 99% CIP removal within 30 minutes, outperforming ZIF-67 and PMS alone, following pseudo-first-order kinetics, and electrochemical studies showed improved electron transfer due to nitrogen doping. Adsorption followed Langmuir and Freundlich models with a capacity of 588 mg g−1, and optimal performance occurred at pH 5–9, while extreme pH reduced efficiency. Excess catalyst caused aggregation and high PMS caused radical scavenging, while water matrix components like Ca2+, Mg2+, SO42−, and HCO3− inhibited activity, and NOM showed mixed effects. The catalyst also removed other pollutants (>99%) and showed excellent recyclability over seven cycles with minimal Co leaching (<30 µg L−1). XPS confirmed the Co2+/Co3+ cycle, while EPR and scavenger studies identified superoxide radicals (O2˙−) and singlet oxygen (1O2) as dominant ROS, with minor roles for SO4˙− and ˙OH. Mechanistically, PMS is activated at Co sites generating ROS, CIP is oxidatively degraded into intermediates and eventually mineralized, and nitrogen doping enhances electron transfer and catalyst stability (Scheme 15, ref. 205). Furthermore, Thanh Q. C. Nguyen et al. developed bimetallic CuZn-ZIFs via a heat-assisted ultrasonic method for dibenzofuran (DBF) removal, where XRD confirmed ZIF-8-like structure with Cu incorporation, FT-IR showed Zn–N and Cu–N bonds, SEM/TEM revealed ∼200 nm rhombic dodecahedral particles, BET showed a surface area of 1004.41 m2 g−1, EDX confirmed composition, and TGA showed stability up to 500–700 °C. The system removed DBF via both adsorption and catalytic degradation using H2O2 (Fenton-like process), with optimization of catalyst loading, temperature, and DBF concentration showing high efficiency and good recyclability. Mechanistically, Cu2+/Cu+ redox cycling activates H2O2 to generate ˙OH and radicals that degrade DBF, while Zn2+ stabilizes the framework and improves electron transfer, and the porous structure enhances adsorption, enabling efficient degradation with good stability.206 Additionally, J. Cong et al. synthesized ZIF-9 and ZIF-12 using benzimidazole ligands, confirming SOD and RHO topologies via SEM and XRD, where ZIF-9 had smaller particles (200–400 nm) and lower micropore volume (0.207 cm3 g−1), while ZIF-12 had larger particles (2–4 µm) and higher micropore volume (0.582 cm3 g−1). In PMS-activated rhodamine B degradation, PMS alone showed low activity (17.7% in 80 min), while ZIF-9 initially showed faster degradation (54.8% in 5 min) due to smaller size, but ZIF-12 achieved higher overall removal (92.7% in 35 min vs. 90.7%) due to better PMS diffusion from larger pore volume. The mechanism involves Co2+/Co3+-mediated PMS activation to generate sulfate radicals that degrade RhB, demonstrating that morphology, pore volume, and topology significantly influence catalytic efficiency and kinetics in ZIF-based advanced oxidation processes.207

Polymerization reactions

ZIF-based catalysts have also shown strong potential in polymer synthesis, particularly in the ring-opening polymerization (ROP) of l-lactide, where Somboon Chaemchuen and co-workers systematically studied the effect of different metal centers (Zn, Co, and ZnCo bimetallic) using ZIF-8, ZnCo-ZIF, and ZIF-67 synthesized at room temperature and characterized by XRD, SEM, BET, TGA, and ICP. Although all three catalysts shared the same sodalite topology, ZIF-67 showed the highest catalytic activity, achieving 90% monomer conversion within 4 hours, significantly outperforming ZnCo-ZIF (9 hours) and ZIF-8 (>14 hours), which was attributed to the stronger Lewis acidity of Co2+ sites as confirmed by NH3-TPD analysis. The polymerization followed first-order kinetics with respect to l-lactide, and increasing catalyst loading enhanced the reaction rate. Comparison with CoO and Co(NO3)2 showed that while Co2+ species are active, they are far less efficient than when incorporated into the ZIF framework, and although 2-methylimidazole (2-MIM) as a homogeneous catalyst showed high conversion, it lacked recyclability, highlighting the advantage of ZIF-67 as a reusable heterogeneous system. The produced polylactide (PLA) showed an isotactic microstructure with high enantiomeric purity using ZIF catalysts, whereas 2-MIM led to atactic PLA. Thermal analysis showed that PLA obtained from ZIF-67 had the highest degradation temperature (Td = 326 °C), followed by ZnCo-ZIF (303 °C) and ZIF-8 (291 °C), indicating a direct relationship between catalyst efficiency and polymer stability. Defect engineering also played a role, as spray-dried ZIF-8 with higher defect density showed improved catalytic activity, and stability studies confirmed that ZIF-67 retained full activity over four cycles with no structural changes (XRD, BET). CO2-TPD analysis further indicated higher basicity in ZIF-67 compared to ZIF-8, contributing to enhanced performance, while ICP analysis of ZnCo-ZIF showed slightly higher Co content, suggesting preferential Co incorporation. Mechanistically, ROP using ZIF-67 proceeds via a Lewis acid–base pathway, where Co2+ sites activate the lactide carbonyl group, increasing electrophilicity, while imidazole linkers provide basic sites that stabilize the growing alkoxide species; nucleophilic attack by an alcohol initiator or propagating chain opens the lactide ring to form a metal-alkoxide intermediate, followed by successive monomer insertions leading to polymer growth, with the framework maintaining stability throughout the process (Scheme 16).208 In a related study, Francis Verpoort and co-workers explored ZIF-8 as a heterogeneous catalyst for ROP of l-lactide to produce cyclic polylactide (cPLA), comparing solvothermal and spray-dried ZIF-8, where the spray-dried material showed superior catalytic performance due to higher porosity (>1600 m2 g−1) and a greater number of acid–base sites as confirmed by NH3- and CO2-TPD; characterization using XRD, SEM, FT-IR, TGA, and nitrogen physisorption confirmed structural integrity. The spray-dried ZIF-8 achieved nearly complete conversion of 100 equivalents of l-LA at 160 °C in 3 hours, whereas solvothermal ZIF-8 achieved only 44% conversion under similar conditions, and extended reaction times allowed polymerization of up to 1000 equivalents, yielding high molecular weight PLA (Mn = 11.1 kg mol−1, PDI = 1.94), as confirmed by MALDI-TOF. The reaction followed first-order kinetics, and higher catalyst loading increased conversion and molecular weight, although some nonlinearity was observed due to macrocyclic polymer formation. NMR confirmed high stereoregularity without racemization, indicating that Zn2+ centers are the main active sites rather than the imidazolate linkers. The catalyst was reusable with minimal Zn leaching, and XRD/SEM confirmed structural stability after reuse. Mechanistically, ZIF-8 catalyzes ROP via cooperative Lewis acid–base activation, where Zn2+ coordinates with the lactide carbonyl, enhancing electrophilicity, while basic sites assist in forming a zinc-alkoxide intermediate that initiates polymerization; intramolecular transesterification during propagation leads to cyclic PLA formation rather than linear chains, and the intact ZIF-8 framework enables efficient catalyst recovery and reuse (Scheme 17, ref. 209).

Other catalytic transformations

ZIF-based catalysts have also been successfully applied in a variety of additional organic transformations due to their tunable acidity and structural versatility. M. Ammar et al. incorporated phosphotungstic acid (PTA) into ZIF-67 through a simple one-step method to form PTA@ZIF-67(ec), which was used as a solid acid catalyst for Friedel–Crafts acylation of anisole with benzoyl chloride (Scheme 18). Structural characterization using XRD showed that PTA loading in the range of 14.6–31.7 wt% preserved the ZIF-67 framework, while higher loading (46.4 wt%) caused framework degradation due to excessive acidity disrupting metal–ligand bonds. BET analysis indicated a decrease in surface area and pore volume after PTA incorporation, though the material remained microporous, allowing substrate access to active sites. SEM confirmed that moderate PTA loading retained the hexagonal morphology, while higher loading led to partial structural collapse. FT-IR verified the coexistence of PTA and ZIF-67, and TGA demonstrated high thermal stability. Catalytically, PTA@ZIF-67(ec) outperformed pure ZIF-67 due to additional Brønsted acid sites, with optimal activity observed at 26.5 wt% PTA, achieving complete conversion under optimized conditions, whereas higher PTA loading did not improve performance due to excessive site blocking. The catalyst showed reusability over multiple cycles, and increasing catalyst loading resulted in 100% conversion. It also exhibited superior efficiency compared to other solid acid catalysts, requiring less catalyst and shorter reaction times, with high selectivity toward p-benzoylanisole (94%), and optimal performance at 120 °C, indicating the importance of temperature in accelerating the reaction. Mechanistically, the reaction proceeds via a heterogeneous electrophilic aromatic substitution pathway, where PTA provides strong Brønsted acidity to protonate benzoyl chloride, generating an acylium ion (PhCO+), which attacks anisole preferentially at the para position due to the electron-donating methoxy group, followed by deprotonation to restore aromaticity and form the product, while the ZIF-67 framework stabilizes active sites and prevents leaching.210 In another study, Xinglong Dong et al. (2022) developed nickel-loaded ZIF-L (Ni-ZIF-L) catalysts that retained the 2D leaf-like morphology and crystallinity of ZIF-L while achieving high Ni loading (0.49 wt%) due to the large external surface area; characterization using XRD, TEM, and EDS mapping confirmed uniform Ni dispersion without disrupting the framework, and nitrogen adsorption studies showed retention of high surface area for efficient catalysis. UV-vis and EPR analyses indicated that Ni exists in a square-planar coordination environment bonded to imidazole ligands and nitrate ions. These catalysts showed excellent activity in ethylene dimerization, achieving a very high turnover frequency (330 320 h−1 (TOF per Ni site)) at 40 °C and 30 bar, comparable to leading catalysts, with the optimized 45Ni-ZIF-L showing 96.9% butene selectivity, including 91.4% 1-butene, outperforming Ni-ZIF-8. Pure ZIF-L showed no activity, confirming the essential role of Ni sites, and methylaluminoxane (MAO) was required for catalyst activation, with optimal concentration improving both activity and selectivity. Reaction conditions strongly influenced performance, where higher temperature increased activity but reduced selectivity due to isomerization and trimerization, higher ethylene pressure improved both activity and selectivity, and longer reaction times reduced selectivity due to product buildup. Isotope labeling confirmed that the reaction follows the Cossee–Arlman mechanism (Scheme 19), where ethylene coordinates to a Ni–H species, inserts into the metal–hydride bond to form a butylnickel intermediate, and undergoes chain transfer to release 1-butene as the main product, while β-hydride elimination leads to 2-butene isomers and further insertion leads to higher oligomers. The heterogeneous nature of Ni-ZIF-L allows easy recovery and good stability, making it a promising catalyst for industrial ethylene oligomerization processes.188

Zeolitic Imidazolate Frameworks (ZIFs) have gained prominence as highly efficient heterogeneous catalysts due to their high surface area, tunable porosity, thermal stability, and well-defined metal coordination sites. Various ZIF-based materials have demonstrated exceptional catalytic activity in organic transformations, CO2 fixation, olefin oligomerization, and pollutant degradation. For organic reactions, ZIF-8 efficiently catalyzes the dehydrogenation of benzyl alcohols to carboxylates with an 86% yield, utilizing Zn–N coordination sites, while ZIF-78, functionalized with nitro (–NO2) groups, serves as a dual-site catalyst for the cycloaddition of CO2 with propylene oxide (PO), eliminating the need for co-catalysts or solvents. In olefin oligomerization, Ni-ZIF-L exhibits catalytic performance in ethylene dimerization, achieving 96.9% selectivity for butenes and a high turnover frequency (TOF) of 330 320 h−1 due to its high external surface area and efficient Ni dispersion. In environmental applications, cobalt-based ZIF-9 and ZIF-12 effectively activate peroxymonosulfate (PMS) for rhodamine B degradation, leveraging a redox cycle between Co2+ and Co3+ to generate sulfate radicals (SO4˙−), with ZIF-9 providing rapid initial degradation and ZIF-12 maintaining sustained activity due to its larger microporous volume. The key advantages of ZIF-based catalysts include high stability and reusability, dual-functional catalysis through Lewis acidic metal centers and Lewis basic linkers, tunable porosity enhancing substrate accessibility, and their sustainability as cost-effective alternatives to noble-metal catalysts. These properties make ZIFs versatile catalysts with broad industrial applications, offering a greener, more sustainable approach to organic synthesis, CO2 utilization, olefin oligomerization, and environmental remediation, positioning them as valuable candidates for future advancements in heterogeneous catalysis.

Structure–property–performance relationships and critical design considerations

The catalytic performance of ZIF-based materials is governed by the interplay between active-site chemistry, pore accessibility, electronic structure, and mass transport. Structural modifications such as defect generation, metal/heteroatom incorporation, heterogeneous junction formation, and changes in pore architecture can modify these factors and thereby enhance catalytic activity. However, these modifications do not always produce positive effects. Excessive defects or dopant concentrations may compromise structural stability, block pores, or introduce undesirable recombination centres, while metal aggregation can reduce the availability of catalytically active sites.

In ZIF-derived materials, the final catalytic properties are also strongly dependent on the conversion conditions. Pyrolysis temperature and atmosphere influence pore development, carbon ordering, metal dispersion, and the formation of M–Nx, metallic, or oxide species. Therefore, the catalytic behaviour should be correlated with the structure and composition of the resulting material rather than solely with the parent ZIF framework. Pore architecture is particularly important for heterogeneous catalysis because high surface area does not necessarily indicate high accessible active-site density. The relatively narrow apertures of ZIF-8 and ZIF-67 can restrict the diffusion of bulky substrates, making external surfaces, defects, and grain boundaries important catalytic sites. Consequently, an appropriate balance between microporosity, hierarchical pore development, active-site accessibility, and structural stability is essential. For photocatalytic systems, heterojunctions and defect/doping engineering can promote charge separation and interfacial charge transfer, whereas excessive defects or dopants may act as recombination centres. Similarly, in electrocatalysis, M–Nx coordination environments, heteroatom incorporation, electrical conductivity, and hierarchical porosity collectively determine active-site utilization and reaction kinetics. These observations demonstrate that structural modification should be optimized rather than maximized.

Overall, the catalytic performance of ZIF-based materials arises from a coupled structure–property–performance relationship rather than from any single structural parameter. A critical assessment of active-site accessibility, electronic structure, pore architecture, mass transport, and stability is therefore necessary when comparing different ZIF-based catalysts and designing improved materials for future catalytic applications.

Stability, framework degradation, and metal leaching

Although recyclability is frequently reported as evidence of heterogeneity, several observations compiled in this review point to genuine chemical vulnerability that warrants closer scrutiny. ZIF-8 and ZIF-67 are known to be susceptible to protonation-driven linker displacement under acidic and fully aqueous conditions, and linker exchange or hydrolysis can occur in phosphate-containing media, both of which compromise long-term structural integrity beyond what short recyclability tests (typically ≤5 cycles) can reveal. In advanced oxidation applications, in particular PMS/PDS activation, trace dissolution of the framework's transition-metal centers (Co2+, Fe2+) can itself catalyze radical generation, meaning apparent “heterogeneous” activity may be partly homogeneous in origin unless explicitly excluded. Several entries compiled in this review already report measurable leaching, Mo leaching from hollow CoMo-30 nanocages, Zn2+ leaching from the SPPy/ZIF-8 heterojunction, and declining performance in ZIF-Cu(mIm)2 after three reaction cycles, but these observations have not previously been considered together. We emphasize that establishing genuine heterogeneity requires, at minimum, a hot filtration (leaching) test, ICP-OES/MS quantification of dissolved metal in the post-reaction supernatant, and where relevant, a control experiment using the homogeneous metal salt at the leached concentration to rule out a homogeneous contribution to the observed activity.

Defect engineering, single atoms, tandem catalysis, CO2 conversion and computation-guided design

The first generation of ZIF-derived catalysts followed a simple recipe: pick a metal, pyrolyse, and test whatever comes out. Work from the last few years is more deliberate. Defects are introduced or controlled on purpose, the metal is pushed to single atoms, active sites are paired so that two reactions can run in sequence, and calculation is used to decide what to make next. Defect engineering of the framework and of the derived carbon

Defects in ZIFs are not a side issue. Möslein et al. combined near-field infrared nanospectroscopy with DFT to look at individual ZIF-8 nano- and microcrystals.211 Zinc-rich regions faded as the crystals ripened, whereas missing-linker defects stayed. The open metal sites left behind lower the Young's modulus, and the authors point out that the same sites could be used to tune adsorption and catalysis. Defects also decide how long a ZIF lasts under electrochemical bias. Huang et al. compared conventional single-ligand ZIFs, which convert fully to CoOOH during stepwise oxidation, with ligand-engineered ZIFs that keep their basic framework by forming a protective cobalt (oxy)hydroxide layer at the surface.212

After pyrolysis the question moves to the carbon. Shen et al. built Fe–N4 sites on ZIF-8 particles by pyrolytic etching at 900 °C under ammonia and linked part of the activity to intrinsic carbon defects, which they describe as a 585 ring structure. The catalyst gave a CO faradaic efficiency of 96.9% at −0.6 V vs. RHE in an H-cell, with a CO partial current density of 9.088 mA cm−2. In a flow cell the maximum CO partial current density reached 361.6 mA cm−2.213 That gap of more than an order of magnitude between cell types is a useful reminder that numbers from different set-ups cannot be ranked against each other directly.

Single-atom catalysts from ZIF precursors

The ZIF route to single atoms depends on using zinc as a spacer. Yin et al. pyrolysed bimetallic Zn/Co frameworks; the carbonised linker reduced the cobalt, and zinc evaporated above 800 °C.214 The resulting Co–Nx sites, at more than 4 wt% loading, gave a half-wave potential of 0.881 V, against 0.811 V for commercial Pt/C, and resisted sintering at 900 °C. Shen et al. took a different entry point for CO2 reduction. They exchanged Zn nodes in ZIF-8 for Ni ions adsorbed in the cavities and obtained a catalyst with a CO faradaic efficiency above 71.9% and a current density of 10.48 mA cm−2 at an overpotential of 0.89 V (turnover frequency 5273 h−1).215

It would be wrong to assume that a single-atom site stays a single atom while it works. Karapinar et al. prepared a Cu–N–C material from a Zn-based ZIF in which copper is present only as CuN4 sites. The material gave ethanol with a faradaic efficiency of 55% at −1.2 V vs. RHE in 0.1 M CsHCO3, using gas-phase recycling, and converted CO to C2 products with 80% efficiency. Operando X-ray absorption showed that the isolated sites turn transiently into metallic copper nanoparticles during electrolysis, probably the true active species, and then recover.216 For a review this matters. Reporting the as-prepared coordination is not enough; the state of the site under bias should be stated or measured.

Tandem catalysis

Reducing CO2 beyond two electrons needs a supply of CO and a surface that can couple it. Tandem designs split these jobs. Möller et al. describe three scales for the split: two neighbouring sites linked by CO spillover, separate regions inside one electrode, or two electrolysers in series. They point out that the first two are sensitive to how the catalysts are distributed and that this is hard to hold over long runs, because copper moves under reaction conditions. Their own system used the third option. A Ni–N–C gas diffusion cathode, made by carbonising a Ni-imidazolate at 800 °C, ran at near-100% CO efficiency in the first cell, and the CO2/CO exit stream fed a Cu cathode in the second. Compared with a single CO2 electrolyser, ethylene rates rose by up to 50% and alcohol rates by up to 100%, at current densities up to 700 mA cm−2, and C2+ energy efficiency increased by up to 100%. Part of the gain was traced to cathodically generated hydroxide scrubbing CO2 out of the mixed feed, which raises the effective CO concentration at the copper.217 A noble-metal-free, imidazolate-derived Ni–N–C is a natural CO source for this kind of cascade.

CO2 conversion by photocatalysis

The same frameworks work without a potentiostat. Wang et al. showed that a cobalt imidazolate framework, paired with a ruthenium-based photosensitiser, reduced CO2 to CO with a turnover number of about 450 within 2.5 h under mild conditions.218 In a noble-metal-free version, g-C3N4 served as the light absorber and Co-ZIF-9 as a cocatalyst that captures CO2 and promotes charge separation; the apparent quantum yield was 0.9%, and 13C labelling confirmed CO2 as the carbon source.219 This is the heterojunction picture of Fig. 1 applied to CO2.

DFT- and machine-learning-guided design

Computation now picks candidates as well as explaining them. Zhong et al. cycled between DFT, machine-learning regression and prioritisation, and identified Cu–Al electrocatalysts that gave the highest ethylene faradaic efficiency reported at the time.220 Mok et al. merged a trained model with a CO2 reduction selectivity map to screen 465 metallic catalysts for four products, found Cu–Ga and Cu–Pd, and confirmed them experimentally.221 Both studies deal with metals, not with M–N–C materials. For ZIF-derived catalysts, DFT has mostly been used to rationalise measured structures, as with the defect models in ref. 211. In the literature we surveyed we found few reports in which a surrogate model chooses the defect type, metal and coordination of a ZIF-derived catalyst and the result is then made and tested. The synthesis variables that fix the final site, such as pyrolysis temperature, time and atmosphere, are seldom reported in a form a model can use. Closing that gap is, in our view, the most useful next step for the field.

ZIF-derived single-atom catalysts

Work in 2026 has shifted from simply making atomically dispersed sites to making them accessible and well-defined. The review by Zesheng Li and co-workers covers how ZIFs form hollow structures and how these are converted into hollow carbon single-atom catalysts for the ORR.222 On utilization, a Nature Communications study on near-100% site utilization uses surfactant-assisted freeze-casting of Fe/ZIF-8, where dense particle packing and a rigid interfacial layer cause anisotropic shrinkage on pyrolysis, producing porous, concave FeNC structures.223 A dual-molten-salt (NaCl/KCl) strategy applied to ZIF-8-derived Fe–N–C raised accessible Fe–Nx site density to 4.0 × 1019 sites per g and utilization to 27%, with better intrinsic ORR activity.224

Substitution chemistry offers another route. Atomic substitution between a RuNi alloy and ZIF-8, followed by 900 °C calcination, gave a Ni single-atom catalyst, and EXAFS indicated coordinatively unsaturated Ni single atoms with minor clusters acting synergistically.135 The coexistence of single atoms and nanoparticles can also be deliberate: FeCo nanoparticles anchored on FeN4 single-atom substrates modulated the electronic structure and boosted ORR/OER activity.134 These results show that site density, accessibility and the single-atom/cluster balance now matter as much as the metal–nitrogen coordination itself.

Operando characterization of M–N–C systems

Ex situ images of ZIF-derived M–N–C catalysts show the as-prepared state, not the working one, and 2026 studies by Jiahong Jiang et al. make that gap concrete. Operando HERFD-XAS on bimetallic FeCu–N–C during CO2 reduction tracked oxidation states and coordination in real time. It showed that isolated Fe and Cu single atoms reconstruct into catalytically active metallic clusters, alongside a 2-fold gain in CO faradaic efficiency.225 A broader 2026 review by Dinesh Bhalothia and co-workers notes that in situ and operando XAS reveal active-site evolution, transient intermediates and surface restructuring across ORR, water-splitting and CO2 conversion, bridging the gap between structure and performance.226 Dynamic site changes are also reported for dual-atom systems, for example in an Fe–Co dual-atom electrocatalyst whose active site evolution under working conditions.227

Cell design is a practical constraint. Peidong Yang and coworkers adapted an XAS cell to resemble the H-cell used for activity testing, with simultaneous CO2-derived product detection during operando XAS.228 For photocatalysis, a new operando cell for time-resolved XAS/GC analysis of gas-phase CO2 photoreduction linked a shift in the Mo absorption edge to photocatalyst deactivation.229 For ZIF-67 itself, spectroscopic and theoretical analysis showed that unsaturated Co–N4−x sites become Co–N4−x(OH) under reaction conditions.130 Taken together, these studies argue for pairing operando probes with ZIF-derived catalysts, since the true active motif may differ from the pyrolysed precursor.

Machine-learning-assisted catalyst design

Machine learning (ML) is moving from screening to mechanism. Tian, Y.; et al. in 2026 studied combined DFT with ML to deconvolute ORR activity across possible coordination configurations of M–N–C single-atom catalysts, and found an interpretable descriptor that captures both coordination number and metal–support interaction.230 A 2026 review by Jian-Feng Li and co-workers frames the broader picture: it covers ML-guided SAC design, including structure–performance relationships, high-throughput screening and stability prediction, as well as ML analysis of characterization data such as electron microscopy images and X-ray absorption spectra.231 A companion review by Shuangyin Wang and co-workers classifies descriptors as geometric, electronic, energetic or integrated, and names database quality, model transferability and lack of standardization and reproducibility as the main open challenges.232

ZIF-specific uses are emerging. An ML study of ALD ZnO-to-ZIF-8 conversion, trained on 68 experimental conditions, identified the primary solvent as the most influential predictor, followed by temperature and reaction time.233 For structure, ML classifiers trained on classical and MACE machine-learning-potential trajectories were used to distinguish ZIF polymorphs and resolve subtle crystalline transformations.234 ZIF-8 polymer nanocomposites, including catalysis uses, have also been surveyed from an ML-guided design perspective.235 For CO2 conversion, imidazole-based MOF work now covers high-throughput catalyst screening and reverse structural design with ML.7

Conclusion

The novelty of this review lies in establishing a unified structure–property–performance framework that connects fundamental ZIF architecture with catalytic function across heterogeneous catalysis, electrocatalysis, and photocatalysis. Unlike prior studies focused on isolated aspects, this work integrates comparative insights while critically addressing operational stability, including framework degradation and metal leaching—key challenges often overlooked in performance-driven reports. By combining experimental observations with theoretical and data-driven approaches, a rational pathway for the design of reliable next-generation ZIF-based catalysts is proposed.195–198

Advanced structural engineering—such as core–shell architectures, hollow structures, and M–Nx active sites—plays a decisive role in governing catalytic kinetics and charge transfer. Core–shell systems enable spatial separation of function and improved durability, while hollow morphologies enhance mass transport by shortening diffusion pathways. M–Nx sites further optimize electronic structure, facilitating charge transfer, stabilizing intermediates, and lowering activation barriers in key transformations. These features collectively enable simultaneous optimization of intrinsic activity and transport properties.

Quantitative descriptors such as turnover frequency (TOF) and apparent quantum yield (AQE) are reported in a subset of the compiled studies and, where available, provide useful insight into material design and catalytic performance. TOF reflects intrinsic activity through active site accessibility and electronic tuning, whereas AQE captures charge separation and utilization efficiency in photocatalytic systems. Broader and more systematic reporting of these metrics across future studies would enable more rigorous, comparable evaluation beyond conventional conversion-based assessments. Although noble-metal catalysts (e.g., Pt/C, IrO2, RuO2) often exhibit higher intrinsic activity, ZIF-derived systems offer competitive performance through structural tunability, use of earth-abundant metals (Co, Zn, Fe), and in many cases favorable stability and recyclability, although several compiled studies in this review report measurable metal leaching or cycle-to-cycle activity decline that should be considered in practical deployment. Their advantages in cost, scalability, and durability position them as viable alternatives for practical applications.

The practical applicability of ZIF-based catalysts should be evaluated using quantitative parameters rather than catalytic conversion alone. The studies compiled in this review report catalyst loadings, recyclability, durability, productivity, energy requirements, and metal content with substantial variation between systems. For example, ZIF-67 has been reported at a catalyst loading of 2% with activity retained over four cycles, while a Co@NC-Co1Zn3 system was reported with a production cost below 0.01 $ per m2 and high durability. However, such metrics are not consistently reported across the literature, and differences in reaction conditions, reactor configuration, catalyst loading, and performance definitions prevent a rigorous universal cost comparison. Future studies should therefore report standardized metrics including catalyst productivity, space–time yield, catalyst loading, energy consumption, metal content, synthesis yield and complexity, recyclability, and post-use structural integrity to enable meaningful scale-up and techno-economic assessment.

Looking forward, key challenges include achieving precise control over active site distribution and structural integrity during synthesis and transformation. Emerging techniques such as atomic layer deposition, vapor-assisted conversion, and in situ self-assembly hold promise for atomic-level precision. Integration of computational tools, particularly DFT and machine learning, will further accelerate rational catalyst design.

Future efforts should also focus on hybrid and multifunctional ZIF-based systems for tandem and cascade catalysis, as well as stimuli-responsive materials capable of dynamic catalytic regulation. In energy-related applications, advances in heterojunction engineering, defect modulation, and conductivity enhancement are expected to further improve performance in water splitting, CO2 reduction, and nitrogen fixation.

Addressing scalability through green synthesis routes, renewable precursors, and energy-efficient processes remains essential for industrial translation. Additionally, the development of biomimetic ZIF systems and strategies to enhance long-term stability—such as defect healing and structural reinforcement—will be critical.

Overall, the continued advancement of ZIF-based catalysis will depend on the synergistic integration of materials design, mechanistic understanding, and process engineering, enabling the development of efficient, robust, and sustainable catalytic technologies.
