Closing the Sim2Real Performance Gap in RL
Akhil S Anand, Shambhuraj Sawant, Jasper Hoffmann, Dirk Reinhardt, Sebastien Gros

Abstract
Sim2Real aims at training policies in high-fidelity simulation environments and effectively transferring them to the real world. Despite the developments of accurate simulators and Sim2Real RL approaches, the policies trained purely in simulation often suffer significant performance drops when deployed in real environments. This drop is referred to as the Sim2Real performance gap. Current Sim2Real RL methods optimize the simulator accuracy and variability as proxies for real-world performance. However, these metrics do not necessarily correlate with the real-world performance of the policy as established theoretically and empirically in the literature. We propose a novel framework to address this issue by directly adapting the simulator parameters based on real-world performance. We frame this problem as a bi-level RL framework: the inner-level RL trains a policy purely in simulation, and the outer-level RL adapts the simulation model and in-sim reward parameters to maximize real-world performance of the in-sim policy. We derive and validate in simple examples the mathematical tools needed to develop bi-level RL algorithms that close the Sim2Real performance gap.

Introduction

Sim2Real
rl
(
rl
) addresses the challenge of obtaining real-world data by training
rl
agents in simulation and then transferring them to real-world environments
(
Talvitie 2018
)
. The overarching objective of Sim2Real
rl
is to train agents capable of achieving optimal real-world performance, which we refer to as
Sim2Real optimality
. Sim2real
rl
has been supported by significant advances in capabilities and fidelity of simulators
(
Makoviychuk et al. 2021
;
Todorov et al. 2012
;
Collins et al. 2021
)
. Despite these advances, the inevitable discrepancies between simulated and real-world conditions lead to a significant performance gap upon policy transfer, often referred to as the
Sim2Real gap

(
Da et al. 2025
)
.
To mitigate this gap, methods such as domain randomization
(
Tobin et al. 2017
;
Zhao et al. 2020
)
or domain adaptation
(
Höfer et al. 2021
)
are used to ensure that the agent is
robust
to distribution shifts that occur while transferring to the real world. However, robustness does not yield Sim2Real optimality
(
Zhou et al. 1996
)
.

Sim2Real
rl
methods typically do not directly optimize policy performance for the real-world conditions; instead, they rely on proxies such as simulator accuracy, parameter variability, or prediction fidelity
(
Da et al. 2025
)
. However, the conditions required for Sim2Real optimality are distinct from conditions on simulator accuracy or robustness
(
Skelton 1989
;
Anand et al. 2024
)
. Consequently, these methods cannot inherently guarantee optimal real-world performance. The Sim2Real performance gap in
rl
is closely tied to the
objective mismatch
in
mbrl
(
mbrl
)
(
Wei et al. 2024
;
Eysenbach et al. 2022
;
Janner et al. 2019
)
, emphasizing that standard metrics for prediction accuracy in simulation models often fail to correlate with the real-world performance of the policy developed in simulation. In theory, for any stochastic real-world system, it is possible to adjust the simulation environment such that the optimal policy in simulation is also optimal in the real world
(
Anand et al. 2025
)
.

However, there is still no general consensus on the correct objective for training the simulation model, beyond the intuitive goal that both the learned model and policy should ultimately optimize the expected return in the real-world environment
(
Wei et al. 2024
)
.
Following previous works
(
Anand et al. 2025
;
Nikishin et al. 2022
;
Amos and Yarats 2020
)
, we frame the problem of finding or adapting the simulation environment parameters that will guarantee Sim2Real optimality, and cast it as an
rl
problem on the real environment data. This forms a bi-level
rl
framework, where the inner-level consists of a standard in-simulation
rl
agent, and the outer-level comprises a real-world
rl
agent that updates the simulator parameters by evaluating the real-world performance of the in-sim agent.

In this paper, we consider the general case where the
rl
agent in simulation is obtained through
spg
(
spg
) methods, considering it is generalizable to both discrete and continuous action spaces. To that extent, we derive the mathematical tools that serve as the foundation for bi-level policy gradient
rl
algorithms that can achieve Sim2Real optimality both in Sim2Real
rl
and dyna-style
mbrl
frameworks. The central contribution is the estimation of sensitivities of the in-sim
rl
process using
spg
.

Related Work

Sim2Real RL Approaches:

The problem of the Sim2Real gap has been traditionally addressed by either enriching the simulator fidelity or exposing agents to diverse simulated scenarios.
Domain randomization
is a common approach that perturbs the simulator parameters during training to improve robustness, such that the resulting in-sim policy generalizes better to the real-world
(
Tobin et al. 2017
;
Duan et al. 2024
)
. Such approaches often demand extensive domain knowledge and manual feature engineering. Few works have explored automated curriculum-based randomization to reduce manual tuning
(
Akkaya et al. 2019
;
Yuan et al. 2024
)
. In another approach,
domain adaptation
rather than randomizing aligns feature or parameter distributions between the simulator and real environment, commonly via adversarial training on observations
(
Chen et al. 2020
;
Long et al. 2018
;
Pei et al. 2018
;
Höfer et al. 2021
)
. In
meta
rl
, the agent is trained on a distribution of environments with the goal that the agent can then quickly adapt to the real environment
Finn et al. 2017
;
Brown et al. 2020
. Here, the environment distribution during training needs to be carefully designed such that it covers the real-world environment during testing. Other approaches include grounded action methods to adjust simulator dynamics to match observed real trajectories
(
Hanna and Stone 2017
;
Desai et al. 2020
)
and distributionally robust
rl
formulates the problem as maximizing worst-case returns under bounded transition shifts
(
Liu and Xu 2024
)
. Recently,
llm
have been used to improve simulator predictions in a zero-shot fashion
(
Da et al. 2024
)
.

Most Sim2Real
rl
approaches are robustness-focused rather than performance-oriented; they seek to reduce the Sim2Real performance gap by optimizing for proxy objectives (e.g., simulator accuracy, variability) rather than the real-world performance directly
(
Da et al. 2025
)
. As a result, they can improve stability under distribution shift but do not offer a guarantee on optimal performance. Few works have explored embedding task performance in domain adaptation. For example, RL-CycleGAN fine-tunes scene parameters by minimizing an
rl
-consistency loss on a learned Q-function
(
Rao et al. 2020
)
, yet this is an indirect surrogate for real-world performance. In contrast, our approach focuses purely on optimality and couples the domain adaptation directly with the real-world performance of the in-sim policy. We achieve this by casting domain adaptation as a bi-level
rl
problem, where the outer-level
rl
directly optimizes simulator parameters to maximize real-world performance.

Objective Mismatch in
mbrl
:

Objective mismatch
is a well-recognized problem in
mbrl

(
Lambert et al. 2020
;
Eysenbach et al. 2022
;
Janner et al. 2019
)
. An overview of a broad class of decision-aware
mbrl
approaches that aim to solve this problem is provided in
(
Wei et al. 2024
)
.
Value-based
learning is a prominent decision-aware
mbrl
approach which seeks models that reproduce equivalent value functions instead of state transitions of the environment, following the
(Proper) Value Equivalence Principle

(
Grimm et al. 2020
;
Grimm et al. 2021
)
. In line with this,
value-aware
modeling methods
(
Farahmand et al. 2017
)
use the Bellman error in the model updates. Empirical evidence further underscores the issue, demonstrating a substantial Sim2Real performance gap due to objective mismatch across various frameworks
(
Hansen et al. 2022
;
Farahmand et al. 2017
;
Schrittwieser et al. 2020
;
Wan et al. 2024
;
Voelcker et al. 2023
)
. In contrast to value-based methods, the category of
policy-based
methods considers the real-world policy gradient as the most important feedback signal to adapt the simulation environment. An approach in this direction is using a modified reward signal that guides the parameter updates of the simulation environment in a direction such that the policy gradient in the simulator reproduces the real-world policy gradient
(
Jia et al. 2024
)
. Similar other approaches update the model such that the resulting distance between the respective policy gradients is minimized in the
\ell_{2}
or
\ell_{q}
norm
(
D’Oro et al. 2020
;
Abachi et al. 2021
)
.

Differentiable Planning
is another class of policy-based methods where the model-based policy-optimization process is embedded in a differentiable program to jointly optimize policy and model towards the same objective
Wei et al. 2024
. The approach we take in this paper can be categorized as policy-based differentiable planning. Works in this direction include the
Differentiable Cross Entropy Method

(
Amos and Yarats 2020
)
or the
Optimal Model Design

(
Nikishin et al. 2022
)
, which proposes implicit differentiation of the Bellman operator to identify performance-oriented models through gradient-based optimization.
The work presented here can be seen as an extension of
(
Nikishin et al. 2022
)
, where the in-sim policy is assumed to be a softmax of a learned Q-function allowing to tune the policy by implicit differentiation of the Bellman equation of the Q-function. Instead, in this work we highlight the possibility of the implicit differentiation of the
spg
(
spg
), without assuming a specific structure of the policy.
In general,
model-based implicit functions has been proposed in the machine learning literature
Amos et al. 2018
, which is theoretically and practically justified by the
mpc
(
mpc
) community
(
Gros and Zanon 2019
;
Reiter et al. 2025
)
.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Reinforcement Learning and Optimal Decision-Making

rl
is a framework for sequential decision-making where an agent interacts with an environment to maximize cumulative rewards
(
Sutton and Barto 2018
)
. Formally, an
rl
problem is defined as a
mdp
(
mdp
),
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},r,\gamma)
,
where
\mathcal{S}
is the state space,
\mathcal{A}
is the action space,
\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)
is the real-world transition model defining the probability of transitioning to state
s^{\prime}
given
s,a
.
r:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is the scalar reward function provided by the environment,
\gamma\in[0,1]
is the discount factor. A policy
\pi(a|s)
defines the agent’s behavior, mapping states to a distribution over actions. The objective of
rl
is to find the optimal policy
\pi^{\star}
that maximizes the expected return:
{J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(s_{t},a_{t})\right]}
. The state-value function and action-value function are defined as:
{V^{\pi}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(s_{t},a_{t})\mid s_{0}=s\right],}\,{Q^{\pi}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(s_{t},a_{t})\mid s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a\right]}
. The optimal value function
V^{\star}(s)
and optimal Q-function
Q^{\star}(s,a)
satisfy the Bellman optimality conditions:
V^{\star}(s)=\max_{a}Q^{\star}(s,a),\,Q^{\star}(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[V^{\star}(s^{\prime})\right]
. The optimal policy satisfies:
\pi^{\star}(s)=\arg\max_{a}Q^{\star}(s,a)
.

3.2
In-sim Reinforcement Learning

We consider a simulation environment as a abstract function
f_{\theta}
, approximating the real-world distribution
\mathcal{P}
, used to generate the next state given a current state-action pair

s^{\prime}\sim f_{\theta}(.\mid s,a).

(1)

and used to generate simulation-based trajectories mimicking the real system. The
\theta
may include the parameters of the mathematical function representing the simulator dynamics, such as
ode
,
dnn
, etc. We consider a reward function
R_{\theta}(s,a)
in the simulation as a model of the real-world reward
r
. The parameters of
R_{\theta}
and
f_{\theta}
together constitute what we refer to as simulator parameters. The in-sim policy
\pi_{\phi}
is an implicit function of simulator parameters
\theta
through
f_{\theta}
and
R_{\theta}
, derived by solving the in-sim
rl
problem:

\pi_{\phi^{\star}}=\arg\max_{\phi}\mathbb{E}_{s_{t},a_{t}\sim\pi_{\phi},f_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})\Bigm|s_{0}=s\right].

(2)

The optimality of the
\pi_{\phi^{\star}}
can be established through the Bellman equations:
V^{\star}_{\theta}(s)=\max_{a}Q^{\star}_{\theta}(s,a),\,Q^{\star}_{\theta}(s,a)=R_{\theta}(s,a)+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim f_{\theta}}[V^{\star}_{\theta}(s^{\prime})],\,\text{and}\,\pi_{\phi^{\star}}(s)=\arg\max_{a}Q^{\star}_{\theta}(s,a)
. Note that the objects
V_{\theta},Q_{\theta}
and
\pi_{\phi}
are implicitly defined by
f_{\theta}
and
R_{\theta}
.

4
Optimal Sim2Real RL

In this section, we establish the notion of
Sim2Real Optimality
and describe how it can be realized. We consider a real-world
mdp
as defined in Section
3.1
, and its approximation as an in-sim
mdp
described in Section
3.2
. Assuming the real-world and in-sim
mdp
share the same state-space
\mathcal{S}
and have a unique optimal policy, the in-sim
mdp
achieves Sim2Real optimality if the optimal policy obtained in simulation is also optimal for the real-world environment, i.e.,
\pi_{\phi^{\star}}=\pi^{\star}
. This can be expressed as the necessary and sufficient optimality condition:

\mathrm{arg}\max_{{a}}\,Q_{\theta}^{\star}({s},{a})=\mathrm{arg}\max_{{a}}\,Q^{\star}({s},{a}),\quad\forall\,{s}\,.

(3)

Sim2Real optimality is critical in applications where real-world performance is the priority. Conventionally, the performance objective is pursued indirectly by constructing highly accurate models of real-world dynamics within the simulation environments, for example, through supervised learning. However, for stochastic systems, the prediction accuracy of the simulation model does not necessarily correlate with the real-world performance of the resulting in-sim policy
(
Anand et al. 2025
)
. This stems from the near-impossibility of perfectly replicating the complex conditional distributions that govern real-world dynamics in simulation
(
Ljung 1999
)
. However, it has been shown that even an in-sim policy under an imperfect simulation model
f_{\theta}
can achieve Sim2Real optimality under specific conditions on the model or on the in-sim reward function
(
Anand et al. 2024
;
Gros and Zanon 2019
)
.

To close the
Sim2Real performance gap
, we propose framing the identification of the optimal simulator parameters
\theta^{\star}
satisfying the necessary condition for Sim2Real optimality in (
3
) as a policy gradient
rl
problem:

\theta^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\theta^{\star}}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi^{\star}_{\phi},\mathcal{P}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(s_{t},a_{t})\right]\,\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\eqref{eq:pi_phi}\,.

(4)

The
rl
formulations in (
2
) and (
4
) together constitute a bi-level
rl
. The inner level constitutes the in-sim
rl
problem to derive
\pi_{\phi^{\star}}
which is implicitly defined by
f_{\theta}
and
R_{\theta}
(
2
). Upon deployment in the real-world environment, policy
\pi_{\phi}
generates real transition data
(s,a,s_{+},r)
. The outer-level
rl
then adapts the simulator dynamics
f_{\theta}
and reward function
R_{\theta}
to optimize real-world policy performance under the real-world transition data. Note that the outer-level
rl
does not fit the simulator parameters to mimic real-world dynamics, but instead optimizes them to maximize the performance of the policy implicitly defined by these parameters. Additionally, the solution
\theta^{\star}
to (
4
) is not unique, allowing the selection of simulator parameters aligned with the real-world dynamics according to a user-specified loss function
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
as follows:

{{\theta}^{\star}}=\argmin_{\theta}\,\,\mathcal{L}\left(f_{\theta},\rho;\,R_{\theta},r\right),\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\eqref{eq:bi_level_rl}\quad\forall\,s,a\,.

(5)

mle
(
mle
), Bayesian estimation, or expected-value losses are common choices of
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
.

4.1
Stochastic Policy Gradient for Bi-level RL

In this section, we will derive the mathematical tools to solve problem (
4
). For generality across continuous and discrete state-action domains and stochastic simulation models, we consider the case that both the in-sim and real-world
rl
agents are trained using
spg
. Consider the
spg
of the in-sim
rl
:

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}(\phi,\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\hat{\rho}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}}[\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}(a|s)\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s,a)]\,,

(6)

where
\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}
is the critic of policy
\pi_{\phi}

as seen
by the simulations, and
\hat{\rho}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}
is the simulated discounted Markov chain density associated to policy
\pi_{\phi}
under the model
f_{\theta}
. Under local convergence, i.e.,
{\hat{\varphi}(\phi,\theta)=0}
,
spg
provides a locally optimal policy
\pi_{\phi}
. We consider this locally converged
\phi
in the rest of our analysis, thereby formulating the optimal bi-level
rl
problem (
4
) as:

\theta^{\star}=\arg\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\phi},\mathcal{P}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(s_{t},a_{t})\right]\,\quad\text{s.t.}\quad{\hat{\varphi}(\phi,\theta)=0}\,.

(7)

Given
{\hat{\varphi}(\phi,\theta)=0}
, it implicitly defines
\phi
as a function of
\theta
. Therefore, the sensitivity of the in-sim policy parameters
\phi
with respect to the simulator parameters
\theta
can be given by the
ift
(
ift
)
(
Krantz and Parks 2002
)
,

\nabla_{\theta}\phi\;=\;-\,\bigl(\nabla_{\phi}\hat{\varphi}\bigl(\phi,\theta\bigr)\bigr)^{-1}\,\nabla_{\theta}\hat{\varphi}\bigl(\phi,\theta\bigr)\,.

(8)

Note that there exist iterative methods and their approximation to invert the Jacobian term
\nabla_{\phi}\hat{\varphi}\bigl(\phi,\theta\bigr)

(
Liesen and Strakos 2013
;
Yang et al. 2024
)
. Using
\nabla_{\theta}\phi
, we can then formulate solution to (
7
) as the outer-level
spg
using the the real-world transition data:

\displaystyle\varphi\bigl(\phi,\theta\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho^{\pi_{\phi}}}\Bigl[\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\phi}(a\mid s)\,Q^{\pi_{\phi}}(s,a)\Bigr],

(9a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\phi}(a\mid s)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\phi\;\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}(a\mid s)\,,

(9b)

where
Q^{\pi_{\phi}}
is the critic of in-sim policy
\pi_{\phi}

as seen
by the real world,
\rho^{\pi_{\phi}}
is the discounted Markov chain density resulting from using in-sim policy
\pi_{\phi}
in the real world, and (
9b
) is result of chain-rule with
ift
condition. To update the simulation parameters via (
9a
), we must compute the sensitivity of the in-sim
rl
solution with respect to the simulation parameters, represented by the gradient
\nabla_{\theta}\phi
. Computing
\nabla_{\theta}\phi
is the only additional component of (
9a
) compared to the classic
spg
. We now introduce two lemmas that will help us compute
\nabla_{\theta}\phi
and derive the full expression for the real-world
spg
in (
9a
). Note that the
\partial
in Lemma
1
and Lemma
2
denotes either the partial derivative with respect to
\phi
or
\theta
.

Lemma 1

(Sensitivity of a Markov Chain)
.

Sensitivity of the expected value operation of a function
\eta(s_{k},a_{k})
under a Markov chain induced by policy
\pi_{\phi}
under the transition model
f_{\theta}
is given by:

\displaystyle\partial\,\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\hat{\rho}_{\theta,k}^{\pi_{\phi}}}\bigl[\eta(s_{k},a_{k})\bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{0}\sim\rho^{0},\,a_{i}\sim\pi_{\phi},\\
s_{i+1}\sim f^{\pi_{\phi}}_{\theta}\end{subarray}}\Bigl[\,\eta(s_{k},a_{k})\,\Bigl(\partial\log\pi_{\phi}(a_{k}\!\mid\!s_{k})+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\partial\log f^{\pi_{\phi}}_{\theta}(s_{i+1}\!\mid\!s_{i},a_{i})\Bigr)\Bigr]\,.

(10)

Lemma 2

(Sensitivities of
Q^{\pi}
and
V^{\pi}
)
.

Given the Bellman conditions,

\displaystyle Q^{\pi}(s,a)

\displaystyle=R(s,a)+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s_{+}\sim f}[V^{\pi}(s_{+})],\quad\text{and}\quad V^{\pi}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[Q^{\pi}(s,a)],

the sensitivity of
Q^{\pi}
and
V^{\pi}
are given by:

\displaystyle\partial Q^{\pi}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\partial R(s,a)+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s_{+}\sim f}\left[\partial V^{\pi}(s_{+})+V^{\pi}(s_{+})\partial\log f(s_{+}|s,a)\right],

(11a)

\displaystyle\partial V^{\pi}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}\left[\partial Q^{\pi}(s,a)+Q^{\pi}(s,a)\partial\log\pi(a|s)\right].

(11b)

With the help of these two Lemmas, we now present the
spg
theorem for the bi-level
rl
problem.

Theorem 1

(Sensitivity of in-sim
spg
)
.

Given a simulation model
f_{\theta}
, reward function
R_{\theta}
, and a resulting in-sim policy
\pi_{\phi}
optimal in its parameter (i.e.,
{\hat{\varphi}(\phi,\theta)=0}
), the sensitivity of the in-sim policy parameters
\phi
w.r.t. to the simulation model and reward parameters
\theta
is given by:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\phi

\displaystyle=-\Bigl(\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\hat{\rho}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}}\bigl[\nabla_{\phi\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}\,\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}+\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}\,\nabla_{\phi}\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\bigr]}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathrm{Gradient\,of\,}\hat{\varphi}\mathrm{\,w.r.t.\,}\phi\end{subarray}}\;+\;\underbrace{\nabla_{\phi}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\hat{\rho}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}}\bigl[\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}\,\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\bigr]}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathrm{Gradient\,of\,dist.\,}\hat{\rho}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\mathrm{\,w.r.t.\,}\phi\end{subarray}}\Bigr)^{-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\Bigl(\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\hat{\rho}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}}\bigl[\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}\,\nabla_{\theta}\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\bigr]}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathrm{Gradient\,of\,}\hat{\varphi}\mathrm{\,w.r.t.\,}\theta\end{subarray}}\;+\;\underbrace{\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\hat{\rho}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}}\bigl[\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}\,\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\bigr]}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathrm{Gradient\,of\,dist.\,}\hat{\rho}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\mathrm{\,w.r.t.\,}\theta\end{subarray}}\Bigr).

(12)

In (
1
), the terms
\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}
,
\nabla_{\phi\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}
are the gradient and hessian of the in-sim policy w.r.t to its parameters. The critic sensitivity term,
\nabla_{\theta}\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}
is the influence of changing the reward and model parameters
\theta
on the simulation-based critic
under a fixed policy
\pi_{\phi}
, given by the recursions:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}R_{\theta}(s,a)+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s_{+}\sim f_{\theta}}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s_{+})+\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s_{+})\nabla_{\theta}\log f_{\theta}(s_{+}|s,a)\right],

(13a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\phi}}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s,a)\right]\,.

(13b)

The second critic sensitivity term,
\nabla_{\phi}\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}
is the influence of changing the policy parameters
\phi
on the simulation-based critic
under a fixed reward
R_{\theta}
and model
f_{\theta}
, given by:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\phi}\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s_{+}\sim f_{\theta}}\left[\nabla_{\phi}\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s_{+})\right],

6
Discussions

This paper presents a general mathematical framework for bi-level
rl
using an implicit differentiation of in-sim
spg
, see Theorem
1
, for Sim2Real approaches. Using such sensitivities can achieve local optimality of Sim2Real
rl
and dyna-style
mbrl
settings. By estimating the optimal simulator parameters with outer-level
rl
, we can exploit a full suite of
rl
techniques in simulation, including domain randomization, to develop a policy that is optimal for the real world. In contrast, simply fine-tuning a policy that was learned in simulation on real-world data does not afford this flexibility. Moreover, learning an optimal simulation model provides valuable insights into which scenarios drive decision-making in practice, thereby enhancing explainability. Indeed, industrial practitioners often strictly prefer in-sim optimization over fine-tuning the policy separately for the reasons outlined above. Moreover, fine-tuning the in-sim reward parameters provides extra flexibility in closing the Sim2Real performance gap.

Since the simulation model parameters typically obtained via classical model-fitting approaches
(
Ljung 1995
)
approximate the real world dynamics with reasonable accuracy, the optimal simulator parameters likely lie within a neighbourhood of those estimates. Consequently, in practice, the outer-level
rl
can be implemented as a local search on the simulator parameter space
(
Grudic and Ungar 2000
)
similar to
(
Jia et al. 2024
)
. Additionally, the parameter search space in Sim2Real
rl
is often not large, since practitioners would prefer to modify only a small set of simulator parameters. Indeed, Sim2Real optimality can be achieved by adapting only the reward parameters while keeping the simulation model parameters fixed
(
Gros and Zanon 2019
)
. Moreover, initializing the outer-loop critic with the in-sim critic could accelerate convergence of the outer-loop
rl

(
Yin et al. 2025
)
.

Although Theorem
1
assumes the learning of the in-sim policy has fully converged to local optimality, in practice, a not-fully-converged in-sim policy only perturbs the already noisy real-world gradient of the outer-level
rl
. Therefore, inner-level
rl
not converging is a concern only when the outer-level solution is close to optimal and requires accurate gradients. However, in that situation the simulator-parameter updates are naturally small, making it easier for the in-sim policy to converge.

The bi-level
rl
framework is applicable to any in-sim policy optimization scheme, provided one can estimate the sensitivity of the in-sim policy with respect to the simulator parameters. Notable examples include bi-level
rl
schemes for
mpc

(
Reiter et al. 2025
)
, and
cem
(
cem
)
(
Amos and Yarats 2020
;
Wan et al. 2024
)
. Consequently, the key contribution of the framework presented in this paper is the sensitivity analysis of the
spg
-based
rl
used to train the in-sim policy. This extends the previous work of
Nikishin et al. 2022
that assumed that the policy is derived from a Q-function via a softmax allowing to derive a model gradient by implicit differentiation of the Bellman equations. The bi-level
rl
framework is directly applicable to dyna-style
mbrl
settings, where dynamics model and reward functions typically learned for prediction accuracy can be adapted by an outer-level
rl
loop to improve the real-world performance (
4
).

The sensitivity analysis in Theorem
1
could be incorporated into standard
rl
algorithms such as
ppo
(
ppo
)
(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
and
sac
(
sac
)
(
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
with the addition of differentiating extra terms in their policy gradient equations without additional complexity. Given the challenges inherent in real-world online learning, offline
rl
is a suitable and promising framework for the outer-level
rl
algorithms. Such algorithmic development is a natural and promising avenue for future work.

6.1
Limitations

While this paper presents a mathematical framework and proof of concept for the bi-level
rl
, additional algorithmic development is needed to develop a scalable bi-level
rl
algorithm that can be used by Sim2Real
rl
practitioners. The practical deployment will require the development of specific bi-level
rl
algorithms based on the results of Section
4
. Moreover, the proposed bi-level
rl
framework is computationally expensive, due to the estimation of the critic sensitivities(
13
) and of the optimal in-sim policy (
14
) with respect to the simulation parameters. Since, in practice, these sensitivities need to be approximated using
dnn
, the computational burden increases significantly with the size of in-sim policy parameters. Therefore, efficient approximations of the critic sensitivities are required to make the framework efficiently scalable to larger problems.

While bi-level
rl
accounts for the aleatoric uncertainties in the real-world environment, it does not account for the epistemic uncertainties. However, in real-world decision-making problems, epistemic uncertainty can significantly impact the value function estimates, leading to issues such as overestimation. To address this, epistemic uncertainties must be managed using probabilistic models of dynamics, or by penalizing the in-sim selection of out-of-distribution state-action pairs
(
Kidambi et al. 2020
)
.

One key assumption in our framework is that the simulator and the real system share the same state representation. While this assumption simplifies the analysis, real-world environments often provide only partial or noisy observations (e.g., images or sensor readings). Therefore, extending the bi-level
rl
to work directly with the observation space is an important direction for future work.

The bi-level
rl
framework requires the dynamics model and reward functions in the simulation to be differentiable and sufficiently smooth for accurate sensitivity estimates. Although this may seem limiting, differentiable simulators are rapidly becoming standard, including domains with discrete state or action spaces and applications such as contact robotics, where dynamics are inherently discontinuous
(
Newbury et al. 2024
)
. In the case of
mbrl
, when both dynamics and reward functions are parameterized by neural networks, they are inherently differentiable.

7
Conclusion

The bi-level
rl
framework provides a principled method for closing the Sim2Real performance gap, which is essential for deploying
rl
in real-world environments. In contrast to existing Sim2Real
rl
approaches that optimize the simulator fidelity or inject variability as indirect proxies for real-world performance, the bi-level
rl
framework performs objective-oriented adaptation of simulator parameters driven directly by real-world policy performance. It aligns simulator adaptation with the true performance objective and, in theory, achieves Sim2Real optimality. Similarly, the bi-level
rl
framework overcomes the objective-mismatch issue in
mbrl
by directly coupling model learning to real-world performance, rather than relying on indirect criteria. While this paper establishes the theoretical foundations and mathematical tools for bi-level
rl
, algorithmic developments are necessary to implement this approach in large-scale
rl
problems. Advancements in simulation environments, such as differentiable simulators and
gpu
(
gpu
) acceleration, will be critical to scaling the bi-level
rl
framework for practical deployment.

\displaystyle\otimes\left(W_{\theta,k}^{j}+\partial\log\pi_{\phi}\left(\left.a_{k}^{j}\right|s_{k}^{j}\right)\right)

(37)

where
W^{j}_{\theta,0}=0
and

\displaystyle W_{\theta,k}^{j}

\displaystyle=W_{\theta,k-1}^{j}+\nabla_{\theta}\log f^{\pi}_{\theta,\phi}\left(s^{j}_{k},s^{j}_{k-1},a^{j}_{k-1}\right)

(38)

\displaystyle=W_{\theta,k-1}^{j}+\nabla_{\theta}\log f_{\theta}\left(\left.s^{j}_{k}\right|s^{j}_{k-1},a^{j}_{k-1}\right)

These terms ought to be added to (
22b
), (
22a
) respectively. In implementations, these terms would simply weigh the contributions in forming the policy gradient sensitivities.

A.2
Proof of Lemma 2: Sensitivities of the policy evaluation

The simulation-based critic ideally satisfies the following equations:

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s,a\right)

\displaystyle=R_{\theta}\left(s,a\right)+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s_{+}\sim f_{\theta}}\left[\left.\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s_{+}\right)\,\right|\,s,a\right]

(39a)

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\phi}}\left[\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s,a\right)\right]

(39b)

To compute the sensitivities “safely", we observe that (
39
) in full reads as:

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s,a\right)

\displaystyle=R_{\theta}\left(s,a\right)+\gamma\int\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s_{+}\right)f_{\theta}\left(s_{+}|s,a\right)\mathrm{d}s_{+}

(40a)

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s\right)

\displaystyle=\int\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s,a\right)\pi_{\phi}\left(a|s\right)\mathrm{d}a

(40b)

Their sensitivities are then given by:

\displaystyle\partial\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s,a\right)=

\displaystyle\,\partial R_{\theta}\left(s,a\right)+\gamma\int\partial\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s_{+}\right)f_{\theta}\left(s_{+}|s,a\right)\mathrm{d}s_{+}

\displaystyle\,+\gamma\int\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s_{+}\right)f_{\theta}\left(s_{+}|s,a\right)\partial\log f_{\theta}\left(s_{+}|s,a\right)\mathrm{d}s_{+}

(41a)

\displaystyle\partial\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s\right)=

\displaystyle\,\int\partial\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s,a\right)\pi_{\phi}\left(a|s\right)\mathrm{d}a+\int\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s,a\right)\pi_{\phi}\left(a|s\right)\partial\log\pi_{\phi}\left(a|s\right)\mathrm{d}a

(41b)

In the compact “expected value" format, they then read as:

\displaystyle\partial\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s,a\right)=

\displaystyle\;\partial R_{\theta}\left(s,a\right)+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s_{+}\sim f_{\theta}\left(s,a\right)}\left[\partial\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s_{+}\right)+\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s_{+}\right)\partial\log f_{\theta}\left(s_{+}|s,a\right)\right]

\displaystyle\partial\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s\right)=

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\phi}\left(a|s\right)}\left[\partial\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s,a\right)+\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\left(s,a\right)\partial\log\pi_{\phi}\left(a|s\right)\right]

Note that this “format" of sensitivity (
Q
and
V
split) is possibly best for a critic that has function approximators dedicated to
V
and
Q
separately (or rather
V
and
A
).

Sensitivity of the Policy Evaluation w.r.t.
\theta
and
\phi

We want to derive separately the two sensitivities

\nabla_{\theta}\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{\phi}\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}},

starting from the general (“compact”) policy-evaluation equations (
42
):
Here,
each
of the two sensitivity questions below assumes that the
other
parameter is held fixed.

1. Sensitivity w.r.t.
\theta
, keeping
\phi
fixed

We want

\nabla_{\theta}\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s,a)\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{\theta}\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s).

Starting from (
42
):

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s,a)\;=\;\nabla_{\theta}R_{\theta}(s,a)+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{s_{+}\sim f_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s,a)}\Bigl[\nabla_{\theta}\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s_{+})+\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s_{+})\,\nabla_{\theta}\log f_{\theta}(s_{+}\mid s,a)\Bigr].

(43)

Since,
\pi_{\phi}
is fixed w.r.t.
\theta
, hence:
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\phi}(a\mid s)\;=\;0.
We get

\nabla_{\theta}\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid s)}\bigl[\nabla_{\theta}\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s,a)\bigr].

(44)

These two coupled equations show how to backpropagate the effect of changing
\theta
(the model or cost parameters)
under a fixed policy

\pi_{\phi}
.

2. Sensitivity w.r.t.
\phi
, keeping
\theta
fixed

Now we want

\nabla_{\phi}\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s,a)\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{\phi}\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s),

with
\theta
fixed. That means

\nabla_{\phi}\log f_{\theta}(s_{+}\mid s,a)\;=\;0\,,

(45)

and the reward sensitivity is

\nabla_{\phi}R_{\theta}(s,a)\;=\;0\,.

(46)

From (
42
) again:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\phi}\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s,a)\;=

\displaystyle\;\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s_{+}\sim f_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s,a)}\Bigl[\nabla_{\phi}\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s_{+})\;\Bigr]\,,

(47)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\phi}\hat{V}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s)\;=

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid s)}\Bigl[\nabla_{\phi}\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s,a)\;+\;\hat{Q}_{\theta}^{\pi_{\phi}}(s,a)\,\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}(a\mid s)\Bigr].

(48)

Here, we iteratively compute the required gradients,
\nabla_{\theta}\hat{Q}^{\pi_{\phi}}_{\theta},\nabla_{\theta}\hat{V}^{\pi_{\phi}}_{\theta},\nabla_{\phi}\hat{Q}^{\pi_{\phi}}_{\theta},\nabla_{\phi}\hat{Q}^{\pi_{\phi}}_{\theta}
, similar to temporal difference learning in value-based methods.
For the illustrative examples, we calculate these values till convergence, but they can be approximated using a generic function approximator for bigger problems.

Appendix B
Details on Illustrative Examples

B.1
Example 1: Discrete MDP

We considered a finite MDP with state space
\mathcal{S}=\{0,1,2\}
and action space
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
. The real and model MDP parameters have the following form:

Transition logits

\theta_{\text{trans}}[s,a]\;=\;\bigl[\theta_{\text{trans}}[s,a,0],\,\theta_{\text{trans}}[s,a,1],\,\theta_{\text{trans}}[s,a,2]\bigr]

so that

P(s^{\prime}\!\mid s,a)=\mathrm{softmax}\bigl(\theta_{\text{trans}}[s,a]\bigr)_{s^{\prime}}.

The real-MDP transition logits are

\theta_{\text{real}}^{f}=\bigl\{\,\underbrace{[\,0.5,2.0,0.5],\,[\,1.0,1.5,0.5]}_{s=0},\;\underbrace{[\,1.0,1.0,1.0],\,[\,1.5,1.0,0.5]}_{s=1},\;\underbrace{[\,0.5,1.0,0.1],\,[\,1.0,0.5,1.0]}_{s=2}\,\bigr\}.

R(s,a)=\theta^{R}[s,a],

with

\theta_{\text{real}}^{R}=\begin{bmatrix}1.0&0.5\\
0.0&3.0\\
0.01&2.0\end{bmatrix}.

A similar structure holds for the simulation MDP, where the simulation model and reward parameters for the trials shown in Figure
3
were initialized at random in
[0,5]
.

The in-sim policy is solved using
dp
with a convergence threshold of
1\times 10^{-2}
, which provides a deterministic policy, which is then converted into a stochastic policy. After solving the discrete MDP by soft value iteration, we obtain a state–action value table

Q^{\star}(s,a)\;=\;R(s,a)\;+\;\gamma\,\sum_{s^{\prime}}P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)\,V^{\star}(s^{\prime})\,,

where
P(s^{\prime}\!\mid s,a)
and
R(s,a)
come from the MDP parameters. A greedy policy would be

\pi_{\mathrm{det}}(s)=\arg\max_{a}Q^{\star}(s,a)\,,

but this is non-differentiable. Instead, we define a
soft
(stochastic) policy

\pi(a\mid s)\;=\;\frac{\exp\bigl(Q^{\star}(s,a)/\tau\bigr)}{\sum_{b}\exp\bigl(Q^{\star}(s,b)/\tau\bigr)}\,,

where
\tau>0
is a temperature parameter controlling exploration, which we set as 2.0.
Because every entry of
\pi(\cdot\mid s)
is a smooth function of the underlying
Q^{\star}
, and
Q^{\star}
itself depends differentiably on the simulator parameters
\theta
, this construction makes the whole inner loop fully differentiable.

Once
Q^{\star}
is computed, we set the policy logits as:

\text{logits}(s,a)\;=\;\log\bigl(\pi(a\mid s)\bigr)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\pi(a\mid s)=\mathrm{softmax}\bigl(\text{logits}(s,\cdot)\bigr)\,.

Using an on-policy, in-simulation trajectory of length 1000 drawn from the
dp
solution, we compute the Markov-chain sensitivities (Eq.
4.1
and
16
) and the critic sensitivities (Eq.
13
and
14
). Each sensitivity table is represented in tabular form and updated iteratively until convergence with a threshold of
1\times 10^{-2}
.
For each outer-level iteration, we use a trajectory of length 1000 generated by applying the in-sim policy to the real
mdp
to update the model and reward parameters using
spg
steps according to Eq. (
9a
) with a learning rate of 0.1.

B.2
Example 2: Continuous MDP

We use a one-dimensional linear system with additive Gaussian noise and an exponential reward:

\displaystyle s_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\theta_{s}\,s_{t}\;+\;\theta_{a}\,a_{t}\;+\;\epsilon_{t},\quad\epsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),

(49)

\displaystyle R_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})

\displaystyle=\exp\!\bigl(-\lambda\,[\,\theta_{q}\,s_{t}^{2}+\theta_{r}\,a_{t}^{2}\,]\bigr).

(50)

The true system uses

\theta_{s}=\theta_{a}=\theta_{q}=\theta_{r}=1
,
while all five simulation parameters

\{\theta_{s},\theta_{a},\theta_{q},\theta_{r}\}

are initialized uniformly at random in
[0,1]
.

By taking logarithms of the stage reward, we obtain an equivalent quadratic stage cost

\,c(s,a)=\lambda\,(q\,s^{2}+r\,a^{2})
,
with
q=\theta_{q}
,
r=\theta_{r}
. In the infinite-horizon,
\gamma
-discounted setting, the optimal control law is linear,

a_{t}^{\star}=-K\,s_{t},

where the gain
K
and value-function curvature
P
solve the discrete Algebraic Riccati Equation (DARE)

P=\lambda q+\gamma\,(\theta_{s}-\theta_{a}\,K)^{2}\,P,\qquad K=\frac{\theta_{a}\,P\,\theta_{s}}{r+\theta_{a}^{2}\,P}.

Once
P
is found, the optimal value function is
V^{\star}(s)=P\,s^{2}
and the greedy policy is
a=-K\,s
.

To test the robustness of our sensitivity estimates, we fit two small feed-forward
nn
of one hidden layer of size 6 for the policy and 64 for the value function:

\hat{V}_{\psi}(s)\approx V^{\star}(s),\qquad\hat{\pi}_{\phi}(a\mid s)\approx\delta(a+K\,s),

where
\hat{\pi}_{\phi}
is trained to match the exact linear feedback as a stochastic Gaussian policy. The learned
\hat{V}
and
\hat{\pi}
are then used in place of the true LQR quantities in the sensitivity computations inner level.

Using an on-policy, in-simulation trajectory of length 1000 drawn from the approximated
nn
policy, we compute the Markov-chain sensitivities (Eq.
4.1
and
16
) and the critic sensitivities (Eq.
13
and
14
). Sensitivities are computed for each sample until convergence of
1\times 10^{-2}
.
For each outer-level iteration, we use 20 trajectories of length 200 generated by applying the in-sim
nn
approximated policy to the real
mdp
to update the model and reward parameters using
spg
steps according to Eq. (
9a
) with a learning rate of 0.1.
