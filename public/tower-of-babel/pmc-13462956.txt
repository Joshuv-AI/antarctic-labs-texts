Unsupervised multivariate analysis of multiple analysis data fusion combining hydrogen distribution and crystallographic orientation image data of vanadium alloy sample
Authors: Maho Hayase, Satoka Aoyagi, Tomoyasu Fujimaru, Yoshihisa Matsumoto, Tomoko Kusawake, Akiko N. Itakura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Evaluating the relationship between crystal structures of V and hydrogen diffusion to extract key features is important for understanding the relationship between hydrogen diffusion behavior and crystal structure in metals. We applied an image fusion method to surface image datasets of the vanadium alloy (a single-phase bcc V–10 mol% Fe alloy) sample, obtained through different measurement methods, such as optical microscopy, scanning electron microscopy/energy-dispersive X-rays, and electron backscatter diffraction, to construct a multimodal dataset comprising hydrogen distribution and crystallographic orientation image data. The analysis of fused multimodal data by two unsupervised learning methods, such as principal component analysis and multivariate curve resolution, revealed that the hydrogen diffusion behavior differed depending on the crystallographic orientation. For example, grains oriented along the [111] and [101] directions exhibit greater hydrogen diffusion behavior than those oriented along the [001] direction. This trend was consistently observed across various analysis methods, but not when analyzed individually. In addition, it was also suggested that the shift from a pure orientation or the presence of other orientations changes the hydrogen diffusion behavior. Through multimodal data analysis of vanadium alloys, key characteristics of crystal orientation that affect hydrogen diffusion rate were extracted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55758-5.

Introduction

Evaluating the relationship between metal crystal structures and hydrogen diffusion is important for understanding the process of hydrogen embrittlement in metals. The importance of hydrogen embrittlement analysis has increased owing to the growing utilization of hydrogen energy1–5. Hydrogen diffusion in body centered cubic (bcc) vanadium is strongly affected by site occupancy and crystallographic orientation6–9. Previous first-principles studies reported that hydrogen preferentially occupies tetrahedral-site (T site) and diffuses mainly through T site networks with relatively low migration barriers6,7, whereas octahedral-site (O site) occupation induces larger lattice distortion and slower diffusion8,9. It is crucial to clarify the relationship between the crystal structure of metals and the hydrogen diffusion process to better comprehend the mechanism of hydrogen embrittlement10–13. In order to identify crystal factors associated with hydrogen distribution or diffusion, crystal structure maps and hydrogen distribution images of the same sample must be analyzed together, despite these images being obtained using different analysis methods. Therefore, we have developed an image fusion method that extracts the same area from image datasets obtained using different surface analysis methods, creating a multimodal dataset10,14. Previous studies have analyzed multimodal data consisting of hydrogen distribution images on the surface of steel samples, obtained using operando hydrogen microscopy, and crystal structure distribution images, obtained using electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) to extract the relationship between hydrogen diffusion and crystal structures10,11,14.

In this study, the multimodal data analysis method was applied to vanadium alloy samples to understand the relationship between hydrogen distribution and crystal structures. Vanadium has been identified as a promising material for hydrogen separation15–20, and thus, elucidating the relationship between hydrogen diffusion and crystal structure is important for developing high-performance vanadium membranes21. The surface distribution of hydrogen was observed using an optical microscope and scanning electron microscope (SEM) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDS) after the silver (Ag) decoration reaction between hydrogen and Ag ion22. The crystal information such as orientation was observed using electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD). EBSD provides crystal structure mappings, such as the inverse pole figures (IPF) of [001], [101], and [111] directions, Euler angle maps, kernel average misorientation (KAM). In addition to these images, the classified information from the EBSD raw data composed of the Kikuchi patterns at each measurement point23,24 were also included in the multimodal data. The Kikuchi bands in each Kikuchi pattern at each measurement point (pixel) were treated as variables and one of the non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) methods — multivariate curve resolution (MCR) — was then applied to extract Kikuchi patterns representing pure crystal structures or groups of similar crystal structures23. As the IPF maps, showing the distributions of main crystal orientations [001], [101] and [111], are part of the crystal information of the sample, other crystal structures should also be included in the multimodal dataset to extract unknown but important factors in terms of hydrogen diffusion. Furthermore, as the angle range set to display an orientation is generally wider than that required for just one orientation, each IPF map may include slightly different orientations. Areas with the same Kikuchi pattern, meaning the same orientation, are classified by analyzing the EBSD raw data, which is composed of Kikuchi bands in each Kikuchi pattern at each pixel. The details of this EBSD raw data analysis are described in our previous study23.

The same area on the vanadium alloy sample was extracted from all images obtained using different surface analysis methods to construct a multimodal image fusion dataset. The multimodal data containing both hydrogen distribution and crystal structures was then analyzed using unsupervised learning methods such as principal component analysis (PCA) and MCR to reveal key features indicating hydrogen diffusion mechanisms in specific vanadium crystal orientations. Unsupervised learning methods are useful for extracting important features, including unknown factors, from the dataset. PCA and MCR are both multivariate analysis techniques that have been applied to a variety of surface analysis datasets14,23,25–30. Although PCA reduces data dimensionality by extracting orthogonal components that capture the maximum variance, these components do not necessarily have physical meaning. In contrast, MCR decomposes data into pure components based on a bilinear mixing model with constraints such as non-negativity. Therefore, while PCA is useful for understanding the outline of the data, MCR is more directly useful for separating physically meaningful information.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

The sample used in this study was a 0.5 mm thick rolled plate of vanadium alloy containing 10% iron, which was observed using scanning electron microscopy energy-dispersive X-rays spectrometry (SEM EDS; Oxford Instruments INCA X-Act, 51-ADD0069)31. The material used in this study was a single-phase body-centered cubic (bcc) vanadium alloy, specifically a V–10 mol% Fe (approximately V–11 mass% Fe) alloy17. The alloy was prepared from high-purity vanadium (99.9 mass%) and iron (99.99 mass%) by electromagnetic levitation melting, followed by repeated cold rolling and intermediate vacuum heat treatment (1200 °C, 10⁻⁴ Pa or lower) to produce a rolled plate. The final sample contained V (89.0 at%) and Fe (10.9 at%) uniformly distributed, with trace amounts of Au and Si (< 0.1 at%). The alloy was designed for hydrogen separation membrane applications, where Fe addition improves workability and suppresses hydrogen embrittlement while maintaining sufficient hydrogen diffusion behavior. The crystal structure of vanadium was body-centered cubic (bcc)16. The plate was cut into a disk with a diameter of 16 mm using wire-cut electrical discharge machining, and then subjected to electrolytic polishing. Four indentations (Vickers indentations) were made on the front surface, within 2 mm from the center of the sample, using a micro-Vickers hardness tester, which served as an indicator of image fusion. The sample was then heat-treated at 1173 K for 2 h.

Hydrogen diffusion observation using the silver(Ag) decoration method

A 0.1 M sodium hydroxide aqueous solution was used as the hydrogen diffusion source on the back of the sample. A 4.3 mM K[Ag(CN)2] solution was used to coat the surface of the vanadium film. A constant current hydrogen charge was performed at a current density of − 5.3 A/m2, and Ag decoration was initiated at room temperature (~ 25 ℃). The starting point of hydrogen charge was set as zero, and the hydrogen distribution on the surface was observed every 30 min up to 90 min using an optical microscope. Subsequently, Ag EDS images and SEM images were obtained. After observing Ag decoration, the sample was ultrasonically cleaned in acetone and ethanol for 3 min to remove surface Ag. Without polishing, EBSD images were then obtained in the same state.

Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD)

The vanadium samples were analyzed using a SEM (JSM-7100 F, JEOL) and EBSD (Symmetry, Oxford Instruments). The measurement area was 290 × 216 μm² before hydrogen diffusion and 456 × 342 μm² after diffusion. A Kikuchi pattern image was obtained every 1 μm across the EBSD measurement area. The Kikuchi pattern image size was 336 × 256 pixels. In addition to the Kikuchi band patterns at each measurement point, i.e., the raw EBSD data, we obtained representative EBSD results, including IPF maps of the [001], [101], and [111] directions, Euler angle maps, and KAM maps. The EBSD data acquired after hydrogen diffusion were used for data analysis. IPF maps are visualizations in which the direction of the specific sample is color-coded according to its corresponding crystallographic orientation. Red, green and blue represent orientations [001], [101] and [111], respectively. Euler angle maps show the crystal angle at each measurement point. The spatial orientation of the crystal lattice at each measurement point is expressed using three rotation angles (φ1, Φ, φ2). KAM map represents the local strain distribution, which is estimated from the average misorientation between each measurement point and its neighboring points.

Analysis of EBSD Kikuchi band patterns

The detailed procedures of the EBSD raw data, Kikuchi band patterns, have been described in the previous study18. The size of the Kikuchi patterns at each measurement point was initially 336 × 256 pixels and then was reduced to 168 × 128 pixels to reduce computational effort. The image size of the EBSD measurement area (Fig. 1c, d, g, h and k, and 1l) was 399 × 300 pixels, while the image size of the target area (Fig. 1b, e, f, i, j and m, and 1n) around the bottom-right indentation (highlighted in the red square of Fig. 1a and b) was 100 × 75 pixels. The Kikuchi patterns were used to extract variables describing the physical conditions at each measurement point. The EBSD raw data (i.e., Kikuchi band patterns) consist of 100 × 75 = 7,500 measurement points in the target area, each represented by a Kikuchi pattern image with 168 × 128 = 21,504 pixels. This matrix was analyzed using MCR implemented in the PLS Toolbox (Eigenvector Research Inc.). The Kikuchi patterns and distribution images for each MCR-extracted component were reconstructed. These distribution images were subsequently used in the image fusion process described in the next section.

Image fusion and multivariate analysis

In this study, the area around the bottom-right indentation (highlighted in the red square of Fig. 1a and b) was selected for detailed analysis. The surrounding region was cropped from the optical microscope images (original area: 328 × 246 µm2) obtained at 30, 60, and 90 min post Ag decoration. The following images were used for image fusion: the EDS maps (401 × 301 µm2) showing Ag distribution (reflecting hydrogen distribution), SEM images, and EBSD images (368 × 277 µm2)—including three Euler angle images, IPF (vertical component along Z-direction) images of [001], [101], and [111], KAM images—and 20 component images, which were extracted from the EBSD raw data (the Kikuchi patterns) using MCR. All images from the same area were aligned and fused using MATLAB image registration based on Affine transformation4,19,20. The final size of the image fusion data was 118 × 91 × 33. This multimodal dataset was transformed into a matrix size [118 × 91] × 33 matrix and normalized via variance scaling. PCA and MCR were then performed on the data. The details of the multimodal dataset containing 33 images are described in the supplementary material (Figure S0 and Table S0).

Results and discussion

Investigation of the relationship between a crystal orientation and hydrogen diffusion rate

The multimodal data, consisting of optical microscope images taken at 30, 60, and 90 min after Ag decoration, the EDS image of Ag (hydrogen) distribution, the secondary electron image, and EBSD images, including three Euler angle images, IPF images of [001], [101], and [111], KAM images, 20 components images extracted from the EBSD raw data (Kikuchi patterns) using MCR (C01 – C20: shown in Figure S1 and Table S1 in the supplementary material), and its residual (C res in Figure S1 and Table S1) were analyzed by multivariate analysis, such as PCA and MCR. Although the PCA results of the multimodal data (Figure S2 and Table S2) showed the hydrogen distribution and crystal structures separately, they did not indicate their relationship. On the other hand, the MCR results showed several components that are highly related to both hydrogen images and crystal information images (Figure S3 and Table S3). Although PCA is an effective method for understanding overall trends in the data because it extracts principal components that maximize variance, it does not always directly extract latent correlations between individual variables, such as the relationship between specific properties and structures. MCR can decompose mixed signals into pure components and can extract factors directly related to hydrogen distribution and crystal structures.

The components contributed by similar variables are grouped based on the MCR spectrum matrix, which indicates the contribution of each variable to each component. Figure 2 shows the grouping of components in the multimodal dataset based on MCR variables with high contribution, and compares grouped components exhibiting similar behavior alongside IPF maps representing the [001], [101] and [111] orientations. Figure 2(a) shows that five component images were grouped according to the 12 variables with the highest contribution, which are dominated by those related to the [111] orientation (Table 1). Additionally, hydrogen diffusion at all measured time points (30, 60 and 90 min) is ranked among the top contributors. This indicates a relationship between the [111] orientation and hydrogen diffusion. Similarly, Fig. 2(b) shows components associated with the [001] and [111] orientations (Table 2), while Fig. 2(c) shows that variables related to the [101] orientation also rank among the highest contributors (Table 3). Figure 2(d) shows that the [001] orientation is one of the dominant high-contribution variables (Table 4).

For example, the components 3, 6, 9, 13, and 19 (Fig. 2(a)) included optical microscopy images at all time points and represented the hydrogen distribution as shown in Table 1. They also included the region along the [111] direction (the third IPF map: IPF3) and EBSD components related to three directions such as C01, 03, 4, and 05 (Table S1). In addition, components 11 and 15 (Fig. 2(b) and Table 2) are mainly associated with the [111] direction and hydrogen distribution. Component 11 is mainly related to hydrogen distribution at earlier times (30 and 60 min) and also includes the [101] and [001] orientations, whereas component 15 is mainly related to hydrogen distribution at a later time (90 min) and includes the [001] direction. These components suggest that the regions along the [111] direction is suitable for hydrogen diffusion. In addition, the brightest crystal grains of components 3, 6 and 11 are located within the [111] IPF map. While Components 3 and 6 relate to similar hydrogen diffusion conditions, Component 11 exhibits different characteristics. This level of detail can be obtained by incorporating the Kikuchi pattern analysis results.

Components 4, 10, 12, and 14 (Fig. 2(c) and Table 3) are mainly associated with hydrogen distribution at earlier times (30 and 60 min) and the [001] and [101] directions. In contrast, EBSD components 5 and 20 (Fig. 2(d) and Table 4) are mainly related to hydrogen distribution at a later time point (90 min) and the [001] direction.

To understand the general trends in hydrogen diffusion rates as a function of metal crystal structure, the pixel intensities corresponding to the distributions along the [001], [101], or [111] directions in the optical microscopy images obtained after Ag decoration were analyzed. The three squares labeled [001], [101], and [111] in Fig. 3 represent the regions used to evaluate the hydrogen diffusion rate. Pure red, green, and blue regions were selected to represent the planes oriented along the [001], [101], and [111] directions, respectively. The pixel intensities of each original image were normalized using min-max scaling. The maximum intensity was observed in the Vickers indentation area at the center of every image. Table 5 summarizes the average pixel intensities of the regions, oriented along the [001], [101], and [111] directions, used to evaluate the hydrogen diffusion rate for each crystal orientation. In addition to the averaged values, the time courses of hydrogen diffusion behavior for individual pixels in the [001], [101], and [111] regions are provided in the Supplementary Material (Fig.S4). Since the optical microscope images at 0 min were homogeneous, except for the indentation region, the pixel intensity values for all three orientations were nearly identical at that time. In contrast, the pixel intensities for the regions oriented along the [101] and [111] directions are higher than those oriented along the [001] direction. This indicates a higher hydrogen diffusion rate in the regions oriented along the [101] and [111] directions than in those oriented along the [001] direction. In addition, the EBSD results represent only the surface crystal structures. The hydrogen diffusion can be influenced by the crystal structures of the inside of the metal sample. Some of the regions on the sample surface that have the same crystal structures, as shown in Fig. 2, showed different hydrogen distribution tendency may have different inside crystal structures.

As shown in Table 5, the [001] direction corresponds to slower hydrogen diffusion than those along other directions. The MCR results align with the hydrogen diffusion rates observed from Ag decoration (Table 5). The [101] and [111] orientations may result in higher hydrogen diffusion rates; however, these rates tend to decrease as the orientation shifts toward the [001] direction according to the IPF color map. In addition, regions with mixed directions, such as [101] and [001], may enhance the hydrogen diffusion rate. Although the [001] direction is less favorable for hydrogen diffusion, its rate can increase with orientation shifts towards [101] or [111] directions. If a target area shows pure red in the IPF color map, then the area contains only [001] orientation. On the other hand, if the color of the target area looks like a mixture of red and green or blue, the interpretation of such an area can be complicated because the measurement step size of this study was 1 μm, which was sufficiently large to include grains with different orientations. In addition, the tolerance angle used in EBSD analysis was also broader than that for an exact crystallographic orientation. Therefore, the orientation of the mixed color area may lie between [001] and [101] or [111], or the area may contain mixed orientations, such as mixed grain boundaries. These results are consistent with the hydrogen diffusion behavior depending on orientation. As shown in Table 5, the hydrogen diffusion rate is higher at the [101] and [111] orientations than at the [001] orientation. In addition, the KAM map (Fig. 1j) shows that the misorientation has spread, particularly in the [111] grains, while much less misorientation was observed in the [001] grains. KAM is an indicator of dislocation density and local strain, and the orientation-dependent increase in KAM observed in this study may reflect hydrogen-enhanced dislocation mobility and localized plastic deformation32.

Typically, the hydrogen diffusion behavior and crystallographic data of metals are obtained and analyzed separately, which makes it difficult to understand the direct correlations between the two. However, this study shows that image data fusion and multimodal data analysis can effectively identify correlations across different measurement methods and extract critical information that cannot be obtained through isolated analyses of hydrogen diffusion behavior and crystal orientation.
