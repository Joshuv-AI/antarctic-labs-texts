Associated Radiofrequency Ablation and Portal Vein Ligation for Staged Hepatectomy Compared to Portal Vein Ligation Induces Prolonged and Enhanced Liver Regenerative Response
Authors: Andrea Lund, Kasper Jarlhelt Andersen, Anders Riegels Knudsen, Michelle Meier, Aage Kristian Olsen Alstrup, Kim Sivesgaard, Mona Kjærbøl Kristiansen, Michael Sørensen, Marie Øbo Larsen, Jens Randel Nyengaard, Frank Viborg Mortensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSeveral techniques have been designed to increase the size of the future liver remnant (FLR) prior to liver resection. This experimental study in pigs builds on our previous rat study, in which we demonstrated that portal vein ligation (PVL) combined with radiofrequency ablation – termed associated radiofrequency ablation and portal vein ligation for staged hepatectomy (ARAPS) – induces accelerated FLR growth comparable to the ALPPS procedure.MethodsEighteen female pigs were randomly assigned to PVL, ARAPS, or controls. On postoperative day (POD) 0, blood samples were collected, and [18F] fluoro-2-deoxy-galactose (18F-FDGal) positron emission tomography (PET) and contrast-enhanced computer-assisted tomography (ceCT) scans were conducted. Intervention pigs underwent either PVL or ARAPS. On POD 7, intervention pigs were re-evaluated with blood samples and 18F-FDGal PET/ceCT scans. Liver biopsies were obtained from the devascularized liver on POD 0 and POD 7, and from the FLR on POD 7 for histological analysis and growth rate kinetics. Control pigs were assessed only on POD 0.ResultsThe median FLR volume increase was comparable between the two groups at POD 7 (p = 0.52). Further, a shift in the distribution of liver function between the FLR and the deportalized liver was observed at POD 7 without significant difference in 18F-FDGal accumulation (percentage of injected dose in the liver) between groups (p = 0.15). The biochemical liver response did not differ between groups at any time. Additionally, no differences in hepatocyte morphology were found at POD 0. At POD 7, increased hepatocyte hypertrophy (p < 0.004) and proliferation (p < 0.001) were found in the ARAPS group compared to PVL. The regenerative capacity was most prominent in the FLR compared to the deportalized liver.ConclusionARAPS may enhance the proliferative response of the FLR compared to PVL alone, while still maintaining liver function comparable to PVL.

Introduction

Curative intended liver resection is the gold standard for treatment of both primary and metastatic malignancies of the liver [1, 2]. Because of the liver’s unique ability to regenerate, up to 70% of the liver volume can be resected in patients with no underlying liver disease such as cirrhosis [3, 4]. Exceeding this threshold increases the risk of post-hepatectomy liver failure, a feared complication associated with a high mortality [5]. When planning major liver resections in cases where the future liver remnant (FLR) is below 30%, strategies to increase FLR volume are essential to reduce the risk of postoperative liver failure. In many patients, preoperative portal vein embolization (PVE) or perioperative portal vein ligation (PVL) are sufficient to stimulate adequate hypertrophy of the remnant liver [6–12]. Alternatively, more advanced surgical strategies may be required, such as conventional two-stage hepatectomy or, more frequently, associating liver partition and PVL for staged hepatectomy (ALPPS). So far, the ALPPS procedure seems superior to PVL alone and PVE in terms of size increase and accelerated growth of the FLR [13]. Especially the kinetic growth rate (volume increase in standardized future liver remnant per week) appears to be superior [12, 13]. The major drawback of ALPPS is that the patient is subjected to two open and technically challenging procedures associated with a high morbidity and mortality. Moreover, not all patients can successfully undergo the full procedure due to potential complications or a decline in their condition following the initial procedure, rendering them unfit for the second stage. Contemporary ALPPS is increasingly performed using hybrid or minimally invasive approaches, often combining laparoscopic techniques with PVE, highlighting the clinical relevance of this method as a minimally invasive alternative.

In our previous rat study, the ARAPS (associated radiofrequency ablation and portal vein ligation for staged hepatectomy) method successfully induced accelerated growth of the FLR as potent as the ALPPS procedure [14]. In brief, the ARAPS technique involves ligation of the portal vein, after which an ablation necrosis is induced in the deportalized part of the liver using a standard RFA single needle [14–17]. The ARAPS technique allows the initial step of the procedure to be performed percutaneously, in contrast to the traditional two-stage ALPPS approach. While PVE and RFA are typically feasible percutaneously, PVL is not. Due to technical limitations at our institution, PVL was utilized as an alternative to PVE in this study, and similarly, both techniques redirect portal blood flow to stimulate liver regeneration.

In the present study, we compared ARAPS and PVL in healthy pigs. The aim of the present study was to examine, in a porcine model, whether the addition of RFA to the deportalized lobe, combined with PVL (the ARAPS technique), augments the morphological and functional dynamics of the future liver remnant compared with PVL alone. Liver volumes were determined using contrast-enhanced computer-assisted tomography (ceCT), and metabolic liver function was evaluated using [18F] fluoro-2-deoxy-galactose (18F-FDGal) positron emission tomography (PET). In addition, blood tests and histological examination were performed. The overall hypothesis was that ARAPS would induce a stronger growth response than PVL alone without compromising metabolic liver function.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

Eighteen female pigs were preoperatively randomized to one of the following groups:(1)Controls (n = 6): no surgery(2)PVL (n = 6): midline incision and PVL(3)ARAPS (n = 6): midline incision, PVL and RFA of the deportalized liver

All pigs were anesthetized and had blood tests as well as a combined 18F-FDGal PET and ceCT scan performed on postoperative day (POD) 0. Immediately after this, pigs in groups 2 and 3 underwent either PVL or ARAPS via an abdominal midline incision. After this procedure, the pig was woken up and observed closely and re-examined with blood tests and 18F-FDGal PET/ceCT on POD 7. Liver biopsies were obtained from the devascularized liver (left liver section) on POD 0 and POD 7, as well as from the FLR (right liver section) on POD 7 for histological examination and growth rate kinetics. Control pigs were evaluated only on POD 0.

Animals and Ethics

The study was reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines [18]. The experiments involved the use of commercial female Danish Landrace-Yorkshire crossbred pigs from a local breeder, approximately 100 days old, with a weight range of 40–47 kg. These pigs were determined to be in good health by a trained veterinarian.

The pigs were raised in a specific pathogen-free environment and received a restricted pellet diet (Svin Foder VAK, Danish Agro, Denmark) twice daily. They had continuous access to tap water. Group housing was provided in boxes with solid flooring, and the pigs were bedded with a combination of straw and sawdust. A minimum acclimatization period of 2 weeks was observed prior to the start of the experiment. The lighting conditions followed a 12:12-h light-dark cycle, while the room temperature was maintained at 20°C. The humidity level was set at 51%, and the air in the facility was exchanged eight times per hour.

Anesthetics and Analgesia

The pigs fasted overnight. On the morning of the experiments, they were transported to the surgical facilities while being lightly sedated with ½ mg/kg midazolam administered intramuscularly (IM).

During both the 18F-FDGal PET/ceCT scan and surgery, the pigs were anesthetized. They received a premedication consisting of 6.3 mg/kg ketamine and 1.3 mg/kg midazolam IM. Following a rest period of 5 to 10 min, an ear vein catheter (21 G Venflon) was inserted for anesthesia induction (1–2 mg/kg propofol). The pigs were intubated (size 7-0), mechanically ventilated, and anesthesia maintained through an IV infusion of approximately 10 mg/kg/h propofol. Pulse, oxygen saturation, and body temperature were continuously monitored as described by Alstrup and Winterdahl [19]. Anesthesia was adjusted if there were sudden changes in pulse rate, oxygen saturation dropped below 92%, or if the body temperature fell outside the reference range of 38.7–40.0°C.

Prior to the surgery, the pigs were administered 0.015–0.030 mg/kg buprenorphine IM for analgesia. Additionally, local anesthesia in the form of 50 mg bupivacaine in a total volume of 10 mL was administered as abdominal infiltration analgesia. As a prophylactic measure against postoperative infections, 25 mg/kg ampicillin was administered IV.

After the surgery, the pigs were awakened and extubated. They received once-daily treatment for at least 3 days with 0.4 mg/kg meloxicam IM for post-surgery analgesia. Additionally, for infection prophylaxis, they received once-daily treatment for 5 days with 10,000 IU/kg procaine benzylpenicillin IM. Clinical observations were conducted twice daily, and the pigs’ weight was recorded every other day. At the end of the study, all pigs were euthanized with an overdose of phenobarbital (100 mg/kg, IV).

Surgical Procedure

The abdomen was accessed by performing a laparotomy through a midline incision, and the liver was mobilized (control procedure). PVL was performed by dissection and ligation of the main left stem of the portal vein. A 3-0-monofilament ligature (Biosyn, Covidien, Mansfield, MA, USA) was used for the ligation, following the technique illustrated in Figure 1. Throughout the procedure, the arteries and bile ducts were carefully preserved. Cyanosis of the left liver section indicated that the ligation of the left stem of the portal vein was successful.

The anatomy of the pig liver including the portal vein system. For the portal vein ligation procedure, the portal branches supplying the left sector of the liver (left lateral lobe [LLL], left medial lobe [LML], and right medial lobe [RML]) were ligated (deportalized sector). The right sector of the liver (right lateral lobe [RLL] and caudate lobe [CL]) was used as the future liver remnant. In the ARAPS group, a peripheral radiofrequency ablation-necrosis was generated in the deportalized liver (grey circle).

For the ARAPS group, an RFA-necrosis of 3 cm in diameter was created using a single electrode connected to the Covidien Cool-tip RF Ablation System E Series (Covidien, Mansfield, MA, USA). A power of 100–120 W was applied for 10 min, following standard settings to ensure consistent and appropriate treatment. The necrosis was strategically placed in the periphery of the devascularized hemi-liver to avoid interference with the blood flow. Subsequently, the abdominal cavity was closed using PDS 2-0 sutures for the fascia and surgical staples for the skin.

ceCT and 18F-FDGal PET Scans

The pigs were positioned in supine position and scanned using a 64-slice Siemens Biograph True Point PET/CT nuclear medicine scanner (Siemens AG, Erlangen, Germany). A ceCT scan was performed in the portovenous phase after an IV bolus of 65–82 mL (weight-adjusted dose) of Iomeron 350. A dose of 100 MBq 18F-FDGal (96–113 MBq) was administered IV as a bolus at the dynamic 20-min PET scan.

Biochemical Analyses

Blood was processed and stored at −80°C until analysis. Prothrombin-proconvertin ratio (pp ratio), alanine aminotransferase, bilirubin, haptoglobin, and alkaline phosphatase levels were measured using the Modular p system (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany).

Tissue Preparation and Stereological Quantifications

Liver tissue underwent the same preparation process and stereological quantification as outlined comprehensively in our prior publication [20]. Counting rules are illustrated and explained in Figure 2. In the ARAPS group, approximately 25 positively stained hepatocyte nuclei were detected in the FLR per animal. The intra-class correlation coefficient, as a measure of intra-observer agreement in hepatocyte nuclei counts, was 0.94 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.91–1.00).

Stereological evaluations of hepatocyte morphology of the FLR in an ARAPS pig. a The counting frames used for analysis are illustrated with green lines representing inclusion lines and red lines representing exclusion lines. According to the conventional counting rule, a hepatocyte profile is considered countable if its nucleus exhibits significant staining and meets certain criteria: It must either be fully contained within the counting frame or touch an inclusion line without touching an exclusion line (green arrows) [21]. Nuclei that touch an exclusion line (red arrow), however, are not included in the count. b The volume of hepatocytes is determined by employing the 3D isotropic nucleator method on three thin sections measuring 3 µm each. The nucleator is applied at the nucleus’s center, generating two test lines that traverse the nucleus in a random and systematic manner. The points where these lines intersect the cell membrane are identified as the intercept lengths. By measuring the distances along these two isotropic lines radiating from the nucleus to the cell membrane boundary, the software calculates an estimate of the hepatocyte volume.

Liver Volume and Metabolic Function

The ceCT scans were analyzed using an open-source DICOM viewer (Horos, https://horosproject.org). The hepatic vein was best found to differentiate the FLR, and the deportalized liver lobe was identified on the POD 0 and POD 7 scans to ensure identical anatomic landmarks. A line was drawn through this hepatic vein and propagated in the cranial and caudal direction. Thereafter, free-hand regions of interest were drawn to segment FLR, the devascularized liver lobe, and, if present, the RFA necrosis and the respective volumes were calculated. The corresponding regions of interest on the PET images were drawn on a merged image of the PET images and the CT images, see Figure 3. The drawing on the CT images served as a template.

PET images showing tissue radioactivity in the FLR to be enhanced on POD7 compared to POD0. a ARAPS pig preoperatively at POD 0. b The same ARAPS pig postoperatively at POD 7. A, anterior; p, posterior; R, right; L, left.

The standardized uptake value of 18F-FDGal was determined by calculating the average tissue radioactivity concentration, as recorded by the PET camera, between 10 and 20 min following the injection of 18F-FDGal. This value was then divided by the injected dose per gram of body weight (kBq/g), while considering an average tissue density of 1 g/mL. The image analyses were performed using PMOD software version 3.605 (PMOD Technologies Ltd., Switzerland). Liver FDGal metabolism was determined by calculating the percentage of injected dose (%ID) accumulated in the whole liver and sectors of the liver (FLR, deportalized liver).

Statistical Analysis

Data were tested by an analysis of variance (ANOVA) and presented as mean values with a corresponding 95% CI. CT volumetric and FDGal PET data were tested using the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test and presented as median values with a corresponding range. p values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were carried out using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, US).

To assess the intra-observer variance, the number of Ki-67-positive hepatocytes was initially counted for each pig. Subsequently, the investigator randomly selected eight sections from various intervention pigs and performed a second count of the positive hepatocytes. The agreement between the first and second counts was evaluated using intra-class correlation coefficients (two-way mixed model).

Results

In total, 18 pigs were included in the study: 6 in the ARAPS group, 6 in the PVL group, and 6 in the control group. One animal from the PVL group died postoperatively on POD 3 without any identifiable cause at autopsy. None of the surviving pigs showed any clinical signs of pronounced discomfort or liver failure. No significant variations in body weights among any of the groups were found during the experiment (p > 0.05).

Biochemistry

We found a difference in Haptoglobin levels between the PVL and ARAPS group on day 7 (p = 0.0007). The levels in both groups are within the normal range of haptoglobin levels in pigs. No other significant differences in biochemical measurements were found between the intervention groups, their baseline measurements, and the control groups. This is illustrated in Figure 4.

Data included 20 CT scans obtained from 10 pigs, with eight scans related to the PVL group and 12 to the ARAPS group. Additionally, 18 PET/CT scans were obtained from 9 pigs: 6 from the PVL group and 12 from the ARAPS group.

Liver Function

At POD 7, total liver function was unchanged as compared to POD 0 in both intervention groups as assessed by FDGal percentage of injected dose (%ID), being approximately 30% (POD 0: 29.5 [24.6–33.7], POD 7: 32.5 [28.0–39.8], p = 0.14). While maintaining overall stability in total liver function, there was a shift in the distribution of liver function between the FLR and the deportalized liver with an increased %ID FDGal in the FLR (%ID FDGal; POD 0: 10.5 [9.5–14.0], POD 7: 17.7 [13.7–26.4], p = 0.008) and a simultaneous decrease in the deportalized liver (%ID FDGal; POD 0: 18.3 [14.2–20.5], POD 7: 14.4 [8.7–17.0], p = 0.008). No significant changes in Liver Function assessed by FDGal were observed between the PVL and ARAPS group (Table 2).

Hepatocyte Volume and Proliferation

Figure 5a shows the time course of the proliferation ratio, defined by the number of Ki-67-positive cells. The greatest proliferation response was observed for the FLR in the ARAPS group on POD 7. This response was significantly higher compared to both baseline and to the FLR in the PVL group (p < 0.004). Proliferation did not increase significantly in the FLR in the PVL group at POD 7 compared to baseline (p > 0.05). No differences in proliferation ratio were found in the deportalized liver after PVL and ARAPS, respectively, compared to baseline (p = 0.7; p = 0.09).

Discussion

The present study in healthy pigs confirmed our working hypothesis, namely that ARAPS induced an accelerated regeneration response, while still maintaining liver function, compared to PVL. Liver regeneration is a complex process primarily driven by hypertrophy and proliferation of existing hepatocytes [22]. In a previous study in rats, we found ARAPS to induce accelerated liver growth of the FLR comparable to the ALLPS procedure [14]. The results from the present porcine study align with those findings, indicating that the ARAPS-induced FLR hypertrophy could be a universal feature in mammals with clinical potential.

Our findings demonstrate significantly increased hepatocyte proliferation at POD7 following ARAPS compared to the PVL group. This is consistent with our previous rat study, in which ARAPS – but not PVL – induced increased hepatocyte proliferation at POD4 [14]. Although proliferation at other time points cannot be excluded given the design of the present study, the overall pattern aligns with our earlier observations [14]. Whether this enhanced proliferative response translates into improved regenerative capacity following subsequent liver resection remains to be determined. The mechanism underlying this enhanced regenerative response remains uncertain. One possible explanation is that RFA-induced coagulative necrosis provides an additional regenerative stimulus through local tissue injury and the release of regenerative mediators. However, this mechanism was not investigated in the present study.

The difference in hepatocyte size between ARAPS and PVL likely reflects distinct regenerative dynamics that are dependent on the regenerative stimulus. Accordingly, we previously showed that hepatocyte hypertrophy is part of the early regenerative response that precedes proliferation and that hypertrophy increases with increasing regenerative stimulus, depending on the size of hepatectomy: 30%; <70%; <90% [4]. Accordingly, the finding that ARAPS at POD7 induces hepatocyte hypertrophy compared to PVL may support a stronger regenerative stimulus, also reflected by the higher Ki-67 proliferation index. Hypertrophy and proliferation are dynamic processes that may vary with the intensity of the regenerative stimulus and may occur at different time points. The finding suggests that ARAPS promotes a more robust or accelerated transition from hypertrophy to proliferation, supporting a mechanistic distinction from ALPPS and other models of portal deprivation.

Termination of porcine hepatocyte proliferation following PVL at POD 7 has been reported by Schadde et al., who also noted that PVE/HVE conforms to the patterns of regenerative cessation preceding POD 7 [23]. Despite ongoing proliferation, both the total liver volume and the volume of the FLR following ARAPS were comparable to that of PVL. Importantly, the liver function assessed by %ID 18F-FDGal was also similar between the groups at POD 7. ARAPS enhanced the proliferative response of the FLR compared to PVL alone, while still maintaining liver function comparable to PVL.

PVL and the ALPPS technique are suggested as an alternative to PVE and PVE/HVE for enhancing the FLR in an attempt to treat extended liver malignancy. The ALPPS procedure seems to be superior to the other techniques in achieving increased size and accelerated growth of the FLR. However, it has also been implied that rapid hypertrophy by ALPPS leads to immature hepatocytes and a higher risk of post-hepatectomy liver failure [24].

Our results show no sign of volume or functional incongruence following ARAPS. Further, concerning the rapidity of regeneration, most patients in clinical practice do not need extensive FLR growth within a few days. PVE remains the standard of care in many patients with a relatively small FLR growth requirement. More recently, combined PVE/HVE (liver venous deprivation) has emerged as an alternative strategy to accelerate FLR hypertrophy and should be recognized among current regenerative approaches. PVE/HVE is used by several high-volume centers to induce faster and more pronounced FLR growth when needed due to a lower reported risk of morbidity and mortality. If PVL is replaced by PVE, the perspective of the ARAPS technique is that the first step, in contrast to the two-stage ALPPS procedure, is possible to perform percutaneously in an attempt to reduce the ALPPS-associated high morbidity and mortality. We believe that ARAPS could be an even more safe, simple, and equivalent FLR growth technique compared to other interventional alternatives and to ALPPS.

The total liver volume was preserved following both PVL and ARAPS, despite dynamic changes within the liver. While the FLR increased in volume, the deportalized liver underwent atrophy, reflecting the expected redistribution of liver mass following portal vein occlusion. This finding is consistent with compensatory regeneration of the FLR accompanying atrophy of the deportalized liver.

We used stereological methods to assess alterations in hepatocyte morphology. Stereological methods offer a distinct advantage over qualitative or semi-quantitative histological approaches as they enable the acquisition of accurate quantitative information on three-dimensional structures from two-dimensional samples in a randomized and unbiased manner [25]. In addition to profound histological and morphological analyses, volumetric and functional assessments of the liver were accomplished by functional PET scans. Only a few other studies on porcine liver regeneration have used CT scans for assessing liver volume [26].

One limitation of the current study is the lack of histological observations between POD 0 and POD 7 and thereafter. The constrained availability of resources alongside ethical considerations pertaining to animal welfare, specifically focused on the principles of reduction and refinement, precluded the feasibility of executing a more expansive and comprehensive study design encompassing these specific timeframes. The determination of the evaluation period was predicated upon the expectation that the regenerative capacity in pigs would exhibit a pace comparatively slower than that observed in rats [27], albeit quicker than that in humans. Also, POD 7 has been the most used evaluation period in former studies [26]. Furthermore, cytokine measurements (e.g., IL-6 and HGF) were not included, limiting mechanistic interpretation of the regenerative response. Finally, the relatively small sample size of this study, especially within the PVL group, where only 3 pigs were scanned. Consequently, statistical differences between groups might have been missed. Nevertheless, scan results were consistently comparable between groups.

The control group consisted of pigs undergoing PVL. Although a comparison between ARAPS and PVE would have addressed a clinically more relevant question, PVE in rats was not technically available at our institution at the time the study was conducted. Consequently, PVL was selected as the comparator. We acknowledge that this choice may limit the direct clinical applicability of the model, particularly with regard to evaluating whether interventional radiology approaches could replace surgical strategies in this setting.

In conclusion, the ARAPS procedure seems to serve as a surplus stimulant for promoting the growth of the FLR not only in rats but also in pigs. ARAPS represents a promising approach to hepatic regeneration with the potential to reduce morbidity associated with traditional surgical techniques. For future applications, performing the initial step using a combination of PVE and RFA would allow for a fully percutaneous and minimally invasive procedure, thereby potentially enhancing patient outcomes and broadening the clinical applicability of this technique.
