Substance Use Emergency Department Visits Among Young People: Exploring Individual and Contextual Risk Factors
Authors: Sophia C Ryan, Jennifer D Runkle, Martie P Thompson, David Karp, Margaret M Sugg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEvidence demonstrates that individual and contextual factors are critical predictors of poor health outcomes at key life stages, including youth and young adulthood. Little is still known how the unprecedented COVID‐19 pandemic affected health outcomes like substance use and how this varied across key demographics and community characteristics. This analysis aims to examine how individual and community contexts interact to predict the risk of substance use outcomes among young people (ages 24 and younger) with additional consideration of the COVID‐19 pandemic. We employ generalized logistic mixed models to examine the main and interactive effects of individual and community context in predicting substance use‐related emergency department (ED) visits. We assess effect modification of this association by key individual variables (age, race, sex, income—using insurance as proxy), community variables (community economic deprivation—derived from principal component analysis), and the COVID‐19 pandemic. Results show low‐income youth are associated with higher risks of substance use compared to their higher income peers (ORUninsured:1.82, CI:1.81–1.83). Community context moderated risk, particularly for children in low‐income communities (ORAlcohol:3.39, CI:1.94–5.93). Findings further demonstrate a higher risk of substance use ED visits during the pandemic among low‐income youth (ORPublicInsurance:1.09, CI:1.07–1.10) and youth in low‐income communities (OR:1.03, CI:1.01–1.05), those 18 and younger (ORAdolescents:1.25, CI:1.23–1.27), and Black (OR:1.32, CI:1.30–1.33), Indigenous (OR:1.17, CI:1.12–1.22), and Hispanic youth (OR:1.13, CI:1.11–1.15). Results emphasize the ongoing need for substance use resources targeted to young people in low‐income and median‐income communities, particularly younger youth and youth of color, who are associated with marked increases in substance use in recent years.Keywords: adolescents, alcohol, children, COVID‐19, neighborhood, opioids, youth

Introduction

Risk‐taking behaviors, including substance use, often begin in adolescence, sometimes as a counterproductive way of managing poor mental health (WHO 2024). At a critical life stage, youth and young adults can be particularly vulnerable to addictive substances (Rahmandar et al. 2021). Alcohol is the most used substance by youth and young people, despite a legal drinking age of 21, and is a substantial contributor to death among young people (e.g., car accidents, suicides, homicides) (Ryan et al. 2019). For example, youth are more likely to engage in excessive alcohol use (e.g., binge drinking) compared to adults, which increases the risk of alcohol poisoning and overdose (Ryan et al. 2019). Alcohol use risks among youth are further compounded by a higher likelihood to engage in risky behaviors, notably driving while impaired, with the 2016–17 National Youth Risk Behavior survey finding 16.5% of youth reported driving while impaired or being a passenger where the driver was impaired (Ryan et al. 2019; Kann et al. 2018).

The prevalence of underage drinking has been declining since the late 1990s, yet the implications and lasting repercussions of consuming alcohol in childhood and adolescence are substantial, including adverse effects to brain development and significantly higher odds of developing alcohol and drug dependence (Ryan et al. 2019). More than half of individuals with substance use disorders, including opioid use disorder and alcohol abuse, report initiating substance use before age 18, and over 80% report first use before age 24 (Compton et al. 2007; Wilson et al. 2021). Beyond alcohol, opioid use has emerged as a particularly timely concern. Recent work by Wilson et al. (2021) stresses the increasing usage and addictive risk of opioids among young people, with almost 15% of young people who visit the emergency department receiving an opioid prescription, which is a primary pathway through which youth develop opioid dependence (Wilson et al. 2021). The prevalence of opioid use (Chang et al. 2018) and deaths have skyrocketed in the United States, increasing by over 300% between 2001 and 2016, accounting for over 12% of deaths among young people (Gomes et al. 2018) and in 2019–20, young people (ages 15–34) accounted for roughly 25% of benzodiazepine overdoses (Liu et al. 2021). This evidence points to the need to extend understanding of how individual and community contexts interact to shape substance use among youth and young people (Visser et al. 2021), particularly in the context of compounding stressors like the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Patterns of substance use and abuse have been linked to individual (Goodman and Huang 2002; Wallace and Muroff 2002) and community context, such as economic deprivation (Cambron et al. 2020) and rurality (Sugg et al. 2023a; Gunlicks‐Stoessel et al. 2025; Morales et al. 2020). Young people may be sensitive to their neighborhood environments, with high risk behaviors, including drug and alcohol use, more common in neighborhoods characterized as economically disadvantaged (Visser et al. 2021). The mechanisms through which neighborhood disadvantage could increase the risk of substance use include distress related to a lack of employment opportunities and career prospects at a formative time, which can depreciate self‐esteem (Curtis et al. 2013). In rural communities, youth face stigmatization, limited psychological health services, and social isolation, compounding mental health and substance use concerns (Gunlicks‐Stoessel et al. 2025; Morales et al. 2020).

At an individual level, youth from low‐income backgrounds who engage with substances at a young age (i.e., around age 13) are associated with a higher likelihood of poor social (e.g., poor relationships) and economic (e.g., low earning potential) outcomes later in life (e.g., adulthood), compared to their peers who waited to engage with substances until later adolescence (i.e., around age 17) (Vergunst et al. 2022). Differences have also been noted by race, with Black youth far less likely to engage with substances compared to white youth (Chen et al. 2009), yet Black youth are associated with shocking increases in overdose mortality in recent years (Liu et al. 2021; Friedman et al. 2022) emphasizing the immediate need for more research at the intersection of youth substance use and individual and community context.

This study focuses on North Carolina, where youth are associated with clustered mental health and substance use outcomes (Sugg et al. 2023a; Blackburn et al. 2026), predicted by neighborhood disadvantage and rurality (Sugg et al. 2023a, 2024). Overall, higher‐income communities and white adolescents are associated with higher risks of emergency department utilization for mental and behavioral health concerns; yet these associations are moderated by community context and systemic inequities (Sugg et al. 2024; Molina et al. 2012). This evidence emphasizes the importance of investigating how individual and community context shape adolescent substance use.

In addition to individual and community context, compounding stressors like the COVID‐19 pandemic, may moderate substance use among young people. Notably, mental health outcomes increased globally during the COVID‐19 pandemic (Panchal et al. 2023; Ravens‐Sieberer et al. 2022), and evidence suggests prolonged increases in poor mental health outcomes among young people (Panchal et al. 2023; Ravens‐Sieberer et al. 2022), with isolation due to social distancing measures, remote learning, and drastic changes in social norms and routines identified as potential mechanisms explaining this marked increase (Ravens‐Sieberer et al. 2022). Potentially linked to the mental health crisis, some evidence also suggests substance use increased during the pandemic among young people (Sugg et al. 2023b; Temple et al. 2022). Yet, other work demonstrates a more nuanced association, with overall declines in substance use among youth (Hoots et al. 2023), but increases in deadly overdoses (Friedman and Hansen 2022).

Emerging evidence suggests changing substance use during the pandemic was moderated by individual context, including gender (Hoots et al. 2023; Carreira Caro et al. 2024), race (Hoots et al. 2023), and social context (Temple et al. 2022; Carreira Caro et al. 2024). Minority youth, notably Indigenous, Black, and Hispanic youth, experienced the greatest increases in opioid overdose rates during the initial months of the pandemic (Friedman and Hansen 2022; Wilson and Bagley 2022). Young people may be particularly vulnerable to compounding social stressors, like the COVID‐19 pandemic and opioid epidemic (Wilson and Bagley 2022; Jayasinha et al. 2020). However, to date, research has been limited to early pandemic periods (e.g., (Friedman and Hansen 2022; Alinsky et al. 2022), mortality measures (e.g., (Friedman et al. 2022)), and select geographic regions (e.g., (Carreira Caro et al. 2024)) and little is known how the COVID‐19 pandemic influenced substance use across diverse populations (e.g., children, adolescents, young adults) and community contexts (e.g., low‐income), supporting the need for more research to understand these concerning trends (Jayasinha et al. 2020).

Current Analysis

While prior work has illustrated potential links between neighborhood context and substance use, and individual context and substance use, few studies have examined the implications of substance use risk across different life stages (childhood, adolescence, and young adulthood), demographic subgroups (e.g., race, gender, income) and community types (e.g., low‐income vs. high‐income). This analysis aims to identify how individual and community contexts and the COVID‐19 pandemic moderate substance use emergency department (ED) visit risk (i.e. substance use, opioids, alcohol) among youth in North Carolina, USA. We hypothesize higher substance use burdens, exacerbated by the COVID‐19 pandemic, in underserved communities and among low‐income youth; we further hypothesize age, and race will moderate the associations of community context in predicting substance use‐related ED visits. This study leverages a large ED dataset to investigate variable place‐based risk in substance use outcomes among young people (children, adolescents, and young adults), considering community context (e.g., income, education, race, employment) and individual demographics (age, race, sex, socioeconomic status). Results offer population and community‐specific insights that can be leveraged to help mitigate and reduce substance use at a key life stage.

Methods

Framework

The rationale for our analysis is guided by a past framework from Curtis et al. (Curtis et al. 2013), which investigated neighborhood risk factors for common mental disorders (CMDs) among youth ages 10–24, hypothesizing that community and immediate social context moderate the risk of CMDs (Curtis et al. 2013). Young people in economically disadvantaged communities may experience social isolation and perceptions of social exclusion (Curtis et al. 2013), which are known risk factors of risk‐taking behaviors. We expand on this framework to investigate the variable risk of ED visitation for substance‐use‐related outcomes across three stages of youth: childhood (12 and under), adolescence (Cambron et al. 2020; Sugg et al. 2023a; Gunlicks‐Stoessel et al. 2025; Morales et al. 2020; Curtis et al. 2013) and young adulthood (Vergunst et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2009; Friedman et al. 2022; Blackburn et al. 2026; Sugg et al. 2024; Molina et al. 2012; Panchal et al. 2023), with consideration of variable risk among demographic and economic subgroups (Figure 1).

Framework building on Curtis et al. (2013) to explore multi‐level intersecting risk factors for youth substance use.

We hypothesize differential risk at the community level across individual contexts, further drawing from Intersectionality Theory, which acknowledges that structural and systemic inequities can amplify health disparities and place disproportionate health burdens on systematically marginalized communities (Collins 1998; Glass et al. 2017). In the context of this analysis, we hypothesize the risk of substance‐use‐related ED visits will be more prevalent in economically disadvantaged communities, with the COVID‐19 pandemic associated with compound and emergent risks, notably among youth of color, and that this association is moderated by individual context (i.e., race, sex, age, socio‐economic status), providing a further pandemic context.

Health Data

De‐identified emergency department (ED) visit data was obtained from the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill SHEPs Center for the years 2011–2022 (UNC Sheps Center 2022). SHEP's ED data contains complete spatio‐temporal reporting of de‐identified ED visit information (age, race, sex, insurance, billing ZIP code) for all ED visits across the state. ED data was coded using ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 CMS codes to isolate a suite of substance use health outcomes: (WHO 2024) substance use (aggregate outcome — any substance‐related ED visit) (F10–F19), (Rahmandar et al. 2021) alcohol use disorder (F10–F10.99), and (Ryan et al. 2019) opioid use disorder (F11–F11.99). Health outcomes were included as a binary (0 = no, 1 = yes). ED visits were restricted to those aged 24 and younger to produce a cohort of young people.

Community Economic Context

To characterize community context, we classified ZIP code tabulation areas (ZCTAs) into distinct groups based on high similarity among a set of neighborhood characteristics. We employed Principal Component Analysis (PCA), a common data reduction technique used to generate a limited set of components that explain the variability in a large database (Greenacre et al. 2022). PCA uses probabilities to identify factors (components) within the database by assigning each variable the probability of being assigned to one of the identified factors. The final factors capture the maximum amount of variability in the dataset with the fewest factors possible (Greenacre et al. 2022). PCA is often used as it addresses multicollinearity in the data, while capturing important variability, with the production of a subset of distinct factors.

PCA was conducted on a set of 59 variables representing community race, employment, median income, and education (Supplemental Table S1); data were obtained from the American Community Survey (ACS) 5‐year estimates (2015–19) (Wilson and Bagley 2022; US Census 2020). Principal components (PCs) were selected if the eigenvalue is greater than 1 (n = 15, accounts for 63.1% of variation). These PCs were input to a cluster analysis, using a k‐medoids algorithm, where data are partitioned into unique groups‐‐this analysis uses the k‐medoids variant to take advantage of its improved performance and sensitivity to outliers (Arora et al. 2016; Ding and He 2004). The number of clusters, K, was chosen using a Gap statistic, which evaluates the marginal benefit of each additional segmentation of the data. An optimal grouping solution is one where intra‐group similarity and inter‐group dissimilarity are maximized. The Gap statistic is assessed for its inflection point, where values converge. The optimal solution was selected for k = 8 socio‐economic clusters. ZCTAs were uniquely assigned to one of eight distinct clusters and then grouped based on low, middle, and high median household income. ED records were linked to these clusters based on residential ZCTA. Clusters were produced using data for the entire United States. ZCTAs in North Carolina were then sub‐selected for this study. These steps follow the PCA/clustering workflow described in Bagchi‐Sen et al. (2025).

Generalized Logistic Mixed Models

Our analysis employed Generalized Logistic Mixed Models (GLMMs) with a binomial distribution and ZCTA random effect to explore the association between community economic context and the risk of substance use ED visits. The inclusion of a random‐effect term for ZCTA reduces correlation within the data, allowing for modeling at the individual and aggregate (i.e., ZCTA) scales (Rabe‐Hesketh and Skrondal 2010). Substance use outcomes were included as a binary (0/1, yes/no), comparing the risk of admission for our outcomes of interest to all ED visits among young people.

We ran three models: (1) The base model included all individual variables: age (under 13, 13 to 17, and 18–24), race and ethnicity (American Indian, Asian, Non‐Hispanic Black, Hispanic, Other, Non‐Hispanic White), gender (male, female), insurance (Public [Disability, Medicaid, Medicare, VA], uninsured/underinsured [Workers Comp, Self‐Pay] and Private), as well as community economic clusters (low‐income, middle‐income, high‐income), and COVID‐19 (ED visits after March 1, 2020 were coded as COVID‐19). (2) Model 2 included the same variables as the base model but also added interaction terms to see how individual factors (insurance, sex, age, race) and community economic cluster moderate the risk of these health outcomes. (3) Model 3 included the same variables as the base model and added an interaction term for COVID‐19 onset (ED visits after March 1, 2020, were coded as COVID‐19).

As a sub‐analysis, we also compared the risk of substance use ED visitation as the primary concern (coded as the main complaint), compared to substance use ED visits as a secondary concern (coded as an additional outcome assessed/discovered during ED visit). Models were set up the same as above and run for all substance use visits coded as a binary (1=primary concern, 0=secondary concern).

All analyses were conducted in R (RStudio Team 2022) using the ‘tableone’ (Yoshida 2020) and ‘glmmTMB’ (Brooks et al. 2024) packages.

Results

Summary of Socio‐Economic Clusters

Three community socioeconomic groups were identified using PCA and k‐medoids clustering (Figure 2). The first group, high‐income, was characterized as affluent, well‐educated, with a high prevalence of homeowners and high property values. The second, middle‐income, had a somewhat lower median income level compared to the first group and was characterized as middle‐class, majority white, predominantly rural, with high unemployment and high prevalence of agricultural workers. The third group, low‐income, was characterized as lower median income compared to the other groups, majority Black, Hispanic, Spanish‐speaking, and less educated. The low‐income cluster ZCTAs had a median income value of $42,500, middle‐income cluster ZCTAs had a median income value of $59,375, and high‐income cluster ZCTAs had a median income value of $96,659.

Panel Map of (a) community economic clusters identified in this analysis, derived from principal component analysis (data from American Community Survey, 2019), and (b) spatial distribution of substance‐use‐related emergency department visit rate (data from SHEPs center, 2011–2022).

High‐income communities clustered in metropolitan areas, with rural communities more represented by the middle‐income and low‐income communities (Figure 2). The western Appalachian Mountains and eastern coastal plain, both rural regions, were overwhelmingly characterized as middle and low‐income.

Substance use outcomes were most prevalent in urban areas, though pockets of high prevalence were noted in the western mountains and eastern coastal plain (Figure 2).

Community X Demographic Moderation Results

Substance use risk was significantly higher among children (ages 12 and younger) in middle‐income communities (OR:1.15, CI: 1.04–1.27) (Figure 3), compared to young adults, despite children having a very low substance use risk overall (Table 2). Black youth (ORLowIncome:1.53, CI:1.43–1.63) and Hispanic youth (ORLowIncome:1.48, CI:1.36–1.62) in low‐ and middle‐income communities were associated with higher alcohol‐related risks (Supplemental Table S2; Figure 3).

COVID‐19 Onset X Demographic Moderation Results

Risk of substance‐use (OR:0.96, CI:0.96–0.97), alcohol (OR:0.95, CI:0.94–0.97), and opioid‐related ED visits (OR:0.92, CI:0.90–0.95) were lower during the pandemic (see Table 2) with significant effect modification noted by community type, with higher risks identified during the pandemic in low‐income communities [(ORSubstanceUse:1.03, CI:1.01–1.05), (ORAlcohol:1.18, CI:1.12–1.26), (OROpioids:1.12, CI:1.01–1.24)] (Figure 4; Supplemental Table S3).

Individual context, notably insurance, race, and age also significantly modified the association. Individuals with public insurance [(ORSubstance Use:1.09, CI:1.07–1.10), (ORAlcohol:1.32, CI:1.26–1.39), (OROpioids:1.64, CI:1.53–1.77)], and those who are uninsured and underinsured [(ORSubstance Use:1.06, CI:1.04–1.07), (ORAlcohol:1.33, CI:1.27–1.39), (OROpioids:1.64, CI:1.52–1.78)] had significantly higher odds of substance use‐related ED visits during the pandemic (Figure 4). Black [(ORSubstance Use:1.32, CI:1.30–1.33), (ORAlcohol:1.08, CI:1.03–1.13), (OROpioids:2.27, CI:2.10–2.46)], Hispanic [(ORSubstance Use:1.13, CI:1.11–1.15), (OROpioids:1.64, CI:1.44–1.87)], and Indigenous [(ORSubstance Use:1.17, CI:1.12–1.22), (OROpioids:1.20, CI:1.00–1.43)] youth were associated with markedly higher odds of substance‐use related ED visits during the pandemic, compared to before, despite minority youth associated with significantly lower risks of substance use, compared to white youth, overall (Table 2). Younger youth were associated with higher risks of substance use [(ORAdolescents:1.25, CI:1.23–1.27), (ORChildren:1.22, CI:1.14–1.30)], alcohol [(ORAdolescents:1.11, CI:1.04–1.17), (ORChildren:1.61, CI:1.15–2.26)] and opioids [(ORAdolescents:1.28, CI:1.12–1.45)] during the pandemic, compared to young adults (ages 18–24).

Substance Use as Primary ED Concern

As a sub‐analysis, we ran a set of models investigating risk of substance use as the primary concern (i.e., main complaint) compared to secondary concern (Figure 5; Supplemental Table S4). Results indicate Asian youth (OR:1.76, CI:1.61–1.93), Hispanic youth (OR:1.24, CI:1.20–1.28), adolescents (OR:2.34, CI:2.29–2.39), and children (OR:1.94, CI:1.77–2.13) were more likely to have substance use labeled as their primary concern, and the odds of substance use as a primary concern increased during the pandemic (OR:1.23, CI: 1.21–1.25).

Having public insurance (OR:1.46, CI: 1.37–1.56) or being uninsured/underinsured (OR:1.21, CI: 1.15–1.28) and living in a low‐income community was associated with higher odds of visiting the ED for substance use as a primary concern. Indigenous youth (OR:1.44, CI:1.04–1.99), Hispanic (OR:1.23, CI: 1.11–1.36), and Black youth (OR:1.20, CI:1.13–1.29) in low‐income communities were also associated with higher odds, along with adolescents (OR: 1.18, CI: 1.11–1.26) and children (OR:2.12, CI: 1.51–2.96).

The pandemic was associated with significantly increased odds of substance use as a primary concern in low‐income (OR:1.34, CI:1.26–1.42) and middle‐income communities (OR:1.17, CI:1.11–1.24), and among those with public insurance (OR: 1.50, CI:1.43–1.57), uninsured/underinsured youth (OR: 1.45, CI: 1.38–1.52), Black youth (OR:1.12, CI: 1.07–1.17), and children (OR:1.41, CI: 1.14–1.75) (Figure 5; Supplemental Table S4).

Discussion

This analysis identified significant differences in substance use risk among young people in North Carolina, moderated by individual and community context and the COVID‐19 pandemic. We hypothesized substance use risk was exacerbated by the COVID‐19 pandemic, particularly in underserved communities, with age and race moderating risk. Our results emphasize higher risk of substance‐use‐related outcomes among lower‐income young people (publicly insured, uninsured, underinsured), Black and Hispanic youth, and children (ages 12 and under) living in low‐income communities. Substance use risk was also moderated by the pandemic, amplifying substance‐use‐related risks among low‐income youth (i.e., youth with private insurance, uninsured and underinsured) and contributing to emerging substance‐use‐related risks particularly in low‐income communities, among younger youth (i.e., adolescents and children), and Indigenous, Black, Hispanic, and Other Race and Multiracial youth (Sugg et al. 2024).

Counter to our hypothesis, substance use risk was lower in low‐income communities, except for alcohol‐related ED visits, which were associated with significantly higher risk. However, this association was modified by individual context, with lower income youth (public insurance, underinsured), Indigenous youth, Black youth, Hispanic youth, and younger youth (children, adolescents), associated with higher odds of substance use in low‐ and middle‐income communities. Low‐income youth and youth in low and middle‐income communities may experience greater stress related to social norms (e.g., employment) (Curtis et al. 2013), which could increase risk of engaging in risky behaviors (Chang et al. 2018; Jayasinha et al. 2020), including alcohol use. Our analysis found a higher risk among younger youth (i.e., 12 and under, 13–17) in low‐income communities. Younger adolescents (e.g., under 17) may be more vulnerable to some of the risk factors associated with community economic deprivation, such as few employment opportunities (Curtis et al. 2013). Our results add further knowledge that this risk factor may be increasingly prevalent among the youngest youth (ages 12 and under) in low‐income communities.

Black and Hispanic youth in low‐income communities were also at significantly higher odds of alcohol‐related ED visits, but lower odds of substance‐use and opioid‐related ED visits. While Black youth are less likely to drink alcohol, compared to white and Hispanic youth (Chen et al. 2009), the risk factors of living in a low‐income area may be more pronounced among Black youth, compared to youth of other racial groups (Glass et al. 2017). Intersectionality theory (Collins 1998; Glass et al. 2017) posits that multiple institutional structures can amplify risks among systematically marginalized populations (Glass et al. 2017). Centuries of institutionalized systemic racism in the healthcare sector contributes to current health disparities (Feagin and Bennefield 2014). The intersecting structural risk factors of living in low‐income communities (e.g., higher likelihood of risk‐taking behaviors), in addition to being a racial minority (Feagin and Bennefield 2014), and a young person (Compton et al. 2007; Wilson et al. 2021) could be one explanation for why Black and Hispanic youth in low‐income communities are associated with up to 50% higher odds of ED admission for alcohol use, compared to white youth in low‐income communities.

In North Carolina, the low‐income principal component analysis clusters are characterized as low‐income, less educated, with a high prevalence of Black and Hispanic individuals. These communities largely cluster in eastern coastal plain and western Carolina mountains. These socio‐physiographic regions in North Carolina are associated with significantly higher occurrences of adolescent mental health and substance use emergency department utilization (Sugg et al. 2023a; Blackburn et al. 2026). Our results add to this evidence, suggesting that intersecting risks associated with individual‐level and community‐level characteristics may help explain the geographic disparities in young people's mental health (Sugg et al. 2024).

Our results are largely in line with existing literature investigating youth mental health and substance use in North Carolina highlighting that neighborhood disadvantage predicts emergency department utilization among youth and young people, with significant modification across individual characteristics (Sugg et al. 2024). Notably, our results contribute to recent evidence which found that racial and ethnic minority youth, while less likely to present to the emergency department with psychological health concerns, were more likely to utilize the ED for mental health concerns in racially segregated neighborhoods (Sugg et al. 2024). Our results suggest similar trends with substance use, such that Black youth living in lower income majority Black and Hispanic communities may be at higher risk of engaging with substance use in early life. These results point to the need for investment in proactive behavioral health interventions for youth of color in lowincome, majority Black and Hispanic communities.

The COVID‐19 pandemic was associated with an overall decline in substance use prevalence, in line with other recent work (Friedman et al. 2022), which may be partially attributable to young peoples' changing social contexts during the pandemic. However, our results also stress that the COVID‐19 pandemic amplified existing substance‐use‐related risks among low‐income youth (i.e., youth with private insurance, uninsured and underinsured) and increased substance‐use‐related risks, particularly in low‐income communities, among younger youth (i.e., adolescents and children) and among Indigenous, Black, Hispanic, and Other Race and Multiracial youth (Sugg et al. 2024).

Youth in low‐ and median‐income communities were at substantially higher risk of ED visitation for any substance use and notably for opioid and alcohol‐related ED visits during the pandemic. Our results also found substantially higher substance‐use risks among children and adolescents during the pandemic, compared to young adults. Youth substance use is strongly linked with social context (Henneberger et al. 2021; Pearson et al. 2006) and in general, youth are far more likely to engage in risk‐filled coping strategies, including increased substance use, due to life stage (Chang et al. 2018). Urgent warnings related to youth substance use risk were issued early in the pandemic, as youth were particularly vulnerable to the stressors of the COVID‐19 pandemic (Wilson and Bagley 2022; Jayasinha et al. 2020). Our results contribute to growing knowledge, indicating effect modification with regards to youth substance use risk during the pandemic, with significantly higher substance use risk identified during the pandemic among youth in low‐ and median‐income communities and younger youth. These concerning results are in line with emerging work highlighting that younger youth (ages 12–17) experienced some of the greatest increases in opioid usage during the pandemic (Alinsky et al. 2022).

Black, Hispanic, and Indigenous youth were associated with higher risks of opioid‐related ED visits and Black and Hispanic youth were far more likely to visit the ED with opioids as their primary concern during the pandemic. Our results are in line with recent work which found higher opioid‐related mortality among Indigenous and Hispanic youth (Friedman et al. 2022), and increasing prevalence of opioid use among Black youth (Baiden et al. 2023), stressing the opioid epidemic as a racial justice issue in the United States (Friedman et al. 2022). In this analysis we found Black youth were associated with higher odds of visiting the ED with opioids as their primary concern during the pandemic. Prior to the pandemic, concerning increases were noted in opioid use among Black youth (Baiden et al. 2023), with some evidence finding Black youth are more likely to develop opioid dependence than their white peers, even though Black youth are much less likely to be prescribed opioids compared to white youth (Appiah et al. 2023; Lagisetty et al. 2019).

The ED is a primary pathway through which youth encounter opioids and develop opioid dependence (Wilson et al. 2021). The prevalence of opioid prescriptions is increasing in the United States, amplifying young people's risk of encountering and potentially misusing opioids (Lagisetty et al. 2019), which is one of the main mechanisms experts hypothesize an increase in risk among Black youth in particular. Intersectionality theory could be applied here as well, as the COVID‐19 pandemic enhanced existing health disparities, with Black communities associated with substantially higher COVID‐19 case and mortality rates (Millett et al. 2020). The compounding stressors of the COVID‐19 pandemic may have been more pronounced for Black youth, with substantial increases in primary opioid ED visits also noted among Asian, Hispanic, and Other Race and Multiracial youth. Ford & Rigg (Ford and Rigg 2015) noted the need for more culturally tailored opioid intervention opportunities in response to growing prevalence of prescription opioid misuse among Black youth in the early 2010s, yet between 2019 and 2020 the opioid death rate more than doubled among Black youth (Friedman et al. 2022; Wilson and Bagley 2022). Our results add to this growing body of knowledge highlighting marked increased risk of opioid use among Black youth and youth of color, stressing the sustained need for targeted substance use interventions.

Implications

Young people in low‐income and median income communities overall showed an increase in risk‐taking behaviors during the pandemic, including heightened risks of substance use, alcohol use disorder, and opioid use disorder ED visits, while young people in high‐income communities overall were associated with a decreased prevalence of substance use ED visits during the pandemic. Given ongoing psychosocial development, youth are uniquely vulnerable to the negative impacts of the COVID‐19 pandemic, including abrupt changes in social norms, school closures, and social isolation (Lucero et al. 2024; Samji et al. 2022). Exposure to extreme social and community stress at a young age can impact and predict poor mental health outcomes decades later in life (Pearce et al. 2018), with children particularly vulnerable to community economic deprivation as a risk factor (Curtis et al. 2013). These results stress the importance of continued efforts aimed at expanding youth mental health and substance use care access, with digital platforms proving effective for marginalized youth (Gould et al. 2022).

Strengths & Limitations

This analysis has numerous strengths. First, we employ a large emergency department dataset with daily reporting across the entire state. The ED does not capture all substance use occurrences in a community and therefore results may not be generalizable to the general population. However, ED data captures a vulnerable demographic who may not have access to other healthcare resources, with one study indicating that about 33% of youth with substance or mental health‐related ED visits have no record of alternative care (Kim et al. 2023). Second, this analysis considered how individual context, community context, and the COVID‐19 pandemic impact youth substance use risk, building from and expanding on an existing youth mental health risk framework from Curtis et al (Curtis et al. 2013). to investigate community and individual risk factors in the context of the COVID‐19 pandemic. Our results contribute to growing knowledge of how the pandemic exacerbated and introduced risk factors, particularly among youth, a vulnerable demographic in the context of COVID‐19 and substance use (Jayasinha et al. 2020).

This analysis has several limitations. First, we define neighborhoods at the ZCTA‐scale, which may not accurately capture an individual's movement and exposures (Kwan 2012), though ZCTAs are an established proxy for neighborhoods (Duncan and Kawachi 2018). Second, past evidence suggests family, school, and peer context are important predictors of mental health (Curtis et al. 2013), which were not considered in this analysis. Third, we consider the pandemic broadly; vaccination rollout, changing distance learning policies and pandemic guidelines could have impacted substance use behaviors. Fourth, results were derived from a single population health surveillance data source and emergency department visit data does not capture all substance use among young people.

Conclusions

This analysis investigated how individual and community contexts moderate substance use ED visit risk (i.e. any substance use, opioids, alcohol) among youth in North Carolina, USA, with additional consideration of how the COVID‐19 pandemic was associated with changing risk across community and individual contexts. Results stress disparate substance use risks, particularly among children, low‐income youth, and youth of color, with the pandemic associated with amplification and emergence of substance use risk. The COVID‐19 pandemic was associated with compounding risk among low‐income youth and emergent risk of substance use ED visits for those under age 18, and Black, Indigenous, and Hispanic youth. These findings are critical for targeting youth in ED settings associated with disproportionately higher substance‐related burdens and build further knowledge of how youth substance use risk changed during the pandemic.
