Optical signatures of Euler superconductors
Chun Wang Chau, Wojciech J. Jankowski, Robert-Jan Slager

Abstract
We study optical manifestations of multigap band topology in multiband superconductors with a nontrivial topological Euler class. We introduce a set of lattice models for non-Abelian superconductors with the Euler invariant signified by a nontrivial quantum geometry. We then demonstrate that the topological Bogoliubov excitations realized in these models provide for a characteristic first-order optical response distinct from those of the other known topological superconductors. We find that the spectral distribution of the optical conductivity universally admits a topological jump originating from the Euler class in the presence of d d -wave superconducting pairings, and naturally differs from the features induced by the quantum geometry in the noninteracting bands without pairing terms. Further to uncovering observable signatures in first-order optical conductivities, we showcase that the higher-order optical responses of the non-Abelian Euler superconductor can result in enhanced nonlinear currents that fingerprint the exotic topological invariant. Finally, by employing a diagrammatic approach, we generalize our findings beyond the specific models of Euler superconductors.

In this work, we investigate the optical response in superconductors realizing a nontrivial Euler class invariant using concrete lattice models and a model-independent diagrammatic approach. We obtain signatures in the linear optical conductivity and higher-order photoconductivities unique to superconducting systems, which we further elucidate within the framework of quantum geometry. The unique features of the Euler superconductors retrieved in this work involve a signature linear optical conductivity jump at the frequency corresponding to the quasiparticle photoexcitation across the superconducting gap to the Euler node carrying the integer patch Euler class between the dispersive and flat bands. Moreover, the quantum metric bounds due to the Euler curvature present in the Euler superconductors provide for an enhancement of nonlinear optical responses captured by the third-order photoconductivity tensors. The uniqueness of these features arises, respectively, from the singular and topologically lower-bounded quantum geometries induced by the Euler invariant, which we detail in Appendix
A
. To expose these optical features, we more specifically start from a tight-binding model that hosts Euler bands on a Lieb lattice, then introduce intraband interactions, i.e., pairing terms, and construct a Bogoliubov-de-Gennes (BdG) Hamiltonian to describe a topological Euler superconductor at the mean-field level.
We find that in the superconducting state, tuning the phase difference between order parameters of the Euler bands can enhance the optical transition between Euler nodes of different sectors, i.e., particle/hole (
\pm
) sectors, in the BdG Hamiltonian spectrum. Furthermore, we retrieve a signature jump originating from the
Euler class in the real part of the linear optical conductivity in the presence of a
d
-wave superconducting pairing, which is proportional to the Euler invariant and is induced by the singular quantum geometry realized in the Euler superconductor.
We furthermore investigate noncentrosymmetric Euler superconductors, where both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{T}
symmetry are broken, which allows for nonvanishing responses at second order in optical fields. We then observe that the response is dominated by the injection currents, which are governed by the multiband quantum metric.
However, we note that the intersector quantum metric, i.e., quantum metric combining states of both particle and hole sectors, exactly at the Euler node, is vanishing. Thus, no direct topological, e.g., quantized, signature of the Euler class can be observed in the second-order response, unlike in the first-order response. We further find that beyond the first-order optical response, there are topologically enhanced diagrammatic contributions at third order in optical electric fields.
In that context, we construct additional selection rules that arise from the difference in the coupling of electrons and holes with electromagnetic fields. We show that these could forbid certain third-order responses manifested in the normal state but otherwise introduce signatures that are unique to the superconducting system.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.

IV
and
V
, we correspondingly employ linear and nonlinear response theories to numerically benchmark the optical responses at linear and nonlinear orders. Sec.

then provides the diagrammatic analysis of the linear and nonlinear responses of non-Abelian Euler superconductors beyond the introduced specific models. In this context, we retrieve Euler class-induced topological contributions within one-loop diagrams for first- and third-order responses. We further discuss these findings in Sec.
VII
, before concluding and providing an outlook on the experimental feasibility of measuring the Euler superconductor invariant using optical probes, as applied to physical materials, in Sec.
VIII
.

\displaystyle\alpha_{a,\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon_{a}\pm\sqrt{|\Delta_{a}|^{2}+\epsilon_{a}^{2}}}{\Delta_{a}^{*}}\;,

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}_{a,\pm}

III
Pairing symmetry in the Euler superconductor

Further to introducing the Lieb lattice Euler superconductor model, we elaborate on the relevant pairing symmetries consistent with the considered lattice Hamiltonian. In particular, we discuss the relationship between the order parameter phase difference
\phi
in the band basis and the nontrivial pairing in the orbital basis.
We begin with the trivial coupling case of
\phi=0
, which can be connected to the case
\Delta({\bf k})=\Delta_{0}\mathbbm{1}_{3}
, assuming that
\Delta_{+}=\Delta_{-}=\Delta_{f}\equiv\Delta_{0}
corresponds to a uniform
s
-wave pairing in momentum space. On the contrary, without changing the band structure, i.e., keeping
|\Delta_{a}|=\Delta_{0}
for all bands, by setting the phase difference to
\phi=\pi
between
\Delta_{-}
and
\Delta_{f}
(
\Delta_{-}=e^{\text{i}\phi}\Delta_{f}=-\Delta_{f}
), with
\Delta_{+}=\Delta_{-}=-\Delta_{0}
, we have a multiband order
parameter
:

\displaystyle\Delta(\phi=\pi)

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{0}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\cos 2\theta&\sin 2\theta\\
0&\sin 2\theta&-\cos 2\theta\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{0}\oplus\frac{1}{r^{2}}(-\Delta_{x^{2}-y^{2}}\tau_{z}-2\Delta_{xy}\tau_{x})\;,

(10)

where we have defined

-wave order parameters
\Delta_{x^{2}-y^{2}}=\Delta_{0}r^{2}\cos 2\theta
and
\Delta_{xy}=\Delta_{0}r^{2}\sin\theta\cos{\th}
.
In the orbital basis, the order parameter can be written as a tensor sum of the
s
-wave in the
A

d
-wave in
B
and
C

\phi
within the order parameter, we are effectively considering nontrivial pairing in the orbital basis, although there is no change in the quasiparticle band dispersion spectrum. The

3

and
\phi=\pi

-wave superconductivity observed, for example, in Lieb lattice cuprates

[

35

39

IV
Linear optical conductivity

We now address the linear optical conductivity in the introduced Euler superconductor.
The linear optical conductivity captures ac current densities
j^{\mu}(\omega)
in response to the optical electric field
\mathbf{E}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{E}_{0}e^{i\omega t}+\mathbf{E}^{*}_{0}e^{-i\omega t})
, with a frequency
\omega
and field strength
|\mathbf{E}_{0}|
, as

j^{\mu}(\omega)=\sum_{\nu=x,y}\sigma^{\mu\nu}(\omega)E_{\nu}(\omega)\;.

(11)

The optical conductivity derived within the linear response theory applied to the superconducting state
[
63
]
reads

\sigma^{\mu\nu}(\omega)=\frac{i}{2(\omega+i\eta)}\sum_{a,b}\left(\frac{J^{\mu}_{ab}J^{\nu}_{ba}f_{ab}}{\omega+i\eta-E_{ba}}+J^{\mu\nu}_{ab}f_{a}\delta_{ab}\right)\;,

(12)

where
f_{ab}=f_{a}-f_{b}
are the differences in the filling factors,
E_{ba}=\tilde{\epsilon}_{b,+}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{a,-}
, and we have set
e=\hbar=1
. Here we also set
\eta\rightarrow 0^{+}
, and defined the generalized velocity operator for a superconductor (see Appendix
B
) with a vector potential denoted as
{\bf A}
:

J^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}({\bf k})=\left.(-1)^{n}\frac{\partial H({\bf k},{\bf A})}{\partial A_{\mu_{1}}\ldots\partial A_{\mu_{n}}}\right|_{{\bf A}\rightarrow 0}.

(13)

We note that the generalized velocity operators can be naturally computed as numerical derivatives. As such, one can easily determine the linear optical conductivity using Eq. (
12
) for a specific model; see Appendix
D
. In our case, using the above Lieb lattice model, we can investigate possible optical signatures that encapsulate the Euler class. We, however, comment on a minor but important complication when connecting the generalized velocity operator in quasiparticle band basis back to the velocity operator in the normal state. Namely, because of the difference in coupling of the electron and hole sectors with the vector potential, the optical response associated with the topology of the Euler class originally present in the normal state
[
28
]
is not directly inherited in the superconducting state.

\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{P}

t_{2}\sin{k_x}
, to all diagonal terms of the Hamiltonian while keeping the
\mathcal{PT}

\mathcal{P}
symmetry along the

, evolves into a divergence, as the parity-odd velocity operator is now nonvanishing,
as illustrated in Fig.

. In the next section, we focus on the numerical results concerning higher-order optical responses.

V
Higher-order responses

To address the higher-order responses of an Euler superconductor, we first study the second-order photoconductivities. The second-order photoconductivity
\sigma^{\mu\nu\rho}(\omega;\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
obtains current densities,

j^{\mu}(\omega)=\sum_{\nu,\rho=x,y}\sigma^{\mu\nu\rho}(\omega;\omega_{1},\omega_{2})E_{\nu}(\omega_{1})E_{\rho}(\omega_{2})\;,

(14)

as a function of the combined frequencies,
\omega=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}
, of a pair of optical fields
E_{\nu}(\omega_{1}),E_{\rho}(\omega_{2})
. In the presence of
\mathcal{P}
symmetry, even-order optical responses generically vanish. Thus, in a centrosymmetric system, the third-order response would be the next nonvanishing higher-order response. To obtain a nonvanishing second-order response, we can break
\mathcal{P}
symmetry by adding parity-odd diagonal terms, which results in a modified Hamiltonian:

h_{\mathrm{PB}}({\bf k})=h_{0}({\bf k})+t_{2}\sin{k_x}\mathbbm{1}_{3}\;.

(15)

We note that the eigenstates of the modified and original Hamiltonians are identical, thus the Euler node topology and the phase diagram remain unaltered.
The dispersion is globally shifted by
t_{2}\sin{k_x}
, since
\mathbbm{1}_{3}=\sum_{a}P_{a}({\bf k})
, with
P_{a}({\bf k})
representing a projector onto band
a
. We can then promote the parity-breaking perturbation to the BdG Hamiltonian with a modified form:

H_{\mathrm{PB}}({\bf k})=H({\bf k})+t_{2}\sin{k_x}\mathbbm{1}_{6}\;.

(16)

As such, the eigenstates of the BdG Hamiltonian under parity breaking term similarly remain unaltered, while the band energy dispersions are globally shifted by
t_{2}\sin{k_x}
, since
\mathbbm{1}_{6}=\sum_{a,s}P_{a,s}({\bf k})
, with
P_{a,s}
a projector onto a BdG band. We delegate a further symmetry analysis of the parity effects central to the context of the second-order responses to Appendix
E
.

VI
Diagrammatic analysis

Previously, we used a response theory approach to calculate the optical conductivity for our specific model. However, such an approach proves difficult to provide clear general insights, linking the Euler class to the response observed numerically.
In the following, we therefore analyze general diagrammatic features within linear and nonlinear response theories
[
69
]
, which pinpoint the nontriviality of the Euler invariant in the superconducting state. We stress that these field-theoretic features are beyond the specific model realizations of the Euler superconductor, see Appendixes
C
-
J
for full technical details. Given the numerically retrieved anomalous jump originating from the
Euler class in optical conductivity in Sec.
IV
, we begin by analyzing the associated diagrammatics of the first-order optical conductivity.

The total first-order optical conductivity
\sigma^{\mu\nu}(\omega)
of a superconductor can be decomposed in terms of two diagrammatic contributions; see Fig.
7
(a)
[
66
]
. Accordingly, expressed in terms of Green’s functions
G(k_{0},{\bf k})
corresponding to the propagators of quasiparticles with four-momenta
k^{\mu}=(k_{0},{\bf k})
, the conductivity
\sigma^{\mu\nu}(\omega)
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu\nu}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\omega}\int[d{\bf k}]\int dk_{0}\;\mathrm{Tr}[J^{\mu\nu}({\bf k})G(k_{0},{\bf k})

\displaystyle+J^{\mu}({\bf k})G(k_{0}+\omega,{\bf k})J^{\nu}({\bf k})G(k_{0},{\bf k})],

(19)

where
J^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}({\bf k})
is the
n
th order generalized velocity operator in the superconducting state
[
63
]
. The generalized velocity operator of the superconducting state can be related to that of the normal state
j^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}({\bf k})
, as long as the Hamiltonian is centrosymmetric, i.e., invariant under
\mathcal{P}
symmetry,

\displaystyle j^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial h({\bf k})}{\partial k_{\mu_{1}}\ldots\partial k_{\mu_{n}}}\;,

(20)

\displaystyle J^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=\left.(-1)^{n}\frac{\partial H({\bf k},{\bf A})}{\partial A_{\mu_{1}}\ldots\partial A_{\mu_{n}}}\right|_{{\bf A}\rightarrow 0}

\displaystyle=\tau_{z}^{n+1}\otimes j^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}({\bf k})\;,

(21)

where
\tau_{z}=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)
is the Pauli matrix in Nambu basis. We note that
G^{-1}(k_{0},{\bf k})=k_{0}-H({\bf k})
, which also defines the Green’s function of the BdG Hamiltonian, can be decomposed as a tensor product:

\displaystyle G(k_{0},{\bf k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}G_{a}(k_{0},{\bf k})\otimes P_{a}({\bf k})\;,

(22)

\displaystyle G_{a}(k_{0},{\bf k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega^{2}_{0}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{a}^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}k_{0}+\epsilon_{a}&\Delta_{a}\\
\Delta_{a}^{*}&k_{0}-\epsilon_{a}\\
\end{pmatrix}\;.

(23)

We can now compute the conductivity using Eq. (
19
), in terms of the velocity operator of the normal state

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu\nu}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\omega}\sum_{a,b}\int[d{\bf k}]\;\big[j_{aa}^{\mu\nu}\delta_{ab}I_{1}+j^{\mu}_{ab}j^{\nu}_{ba}I_{2}\big]\;,

(24)

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int dk_{0}\Tr[\tau_{z}G_{a}(k_{0})]\;,

(25)

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int dk_{0}\Tr[G_{b}(k_{0}+\omega)G_{a}(k_{0})]\;,

(26)

where
j_{aa}^{\mu\nu}=\partial^{\mu}\partial^{\nu}\epsilon_{a}
and
j^{\mu}_{ab}=\epsilon_{ab}\xi^{\mu}_{ab}
, with
\epsilon_{ab}=\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{b}
and
{\xi}^{\mu}_{ab}=i\langle a|\partial^{\mu}b\rangle
. The calculation of the integrals
I_{1},\;I_{2}
is included within Appendix
H
. We can identify the first diagram as the Drude contribution, which contains no information about the geometry. For the rest of the section, we will calculate only the second diagram, which encodes the information about the quantum geometry of our system. In particular, we are interested in the intersector response, since the intrasector response cannot be efficiently distinguished from the Drude contribution numerically.

Before proceeding, we should recall some features of Euler nodes; see also Appendix
A
. First, the multiband connection is purely imaginary due to the reality of the Hamiltonian, resulting in a vanishing Berry curvature
\Omega^{\mu\nu}_{ab}=-2\mathrm{Im}[\xi^{\mu}_{ab}\xi^{\nu}_{ba}]
. Furthermore, the connection between the pair of Euler bands is inversely proportional to
r
around the Euler node, where
r
is the momentum-space distance from the node. These features have a direct physical consequence, particularly with regard to the real part of the conductivity, as we will discuss later. To proceed with an analytical calculation, we further make the following assumptions: (i)
|\Delta_{a}|=\Delta_{0}
for all bands, (ii) we work in zero temperature limit, (iii)
h({\bf k})
is real and invariant under the action of
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{T}
, and
\mathcal{PT}
symmetries, and (iv) one of the bands is flat (i.e.,
\epsilon_{a}=0
) and touches a dispersive band, as known to occur commonly in Lieb and kagome lattices in the material context. At zero temperature, one retrieves only an intersector response, while due to
\mathcal{P}
symmetry,
\sigma^{xy}
vanishes; see Appendix
C
.

The total response between band indices
a,\;b
, namely,
a,+\leftrightarrow b,-
and
a,-\leftrightarrow b,+
, is

\sigma^{\mu\mu}(\omega)=\frac{i}{\omega}\int[d{\bf k}]\;\epsilon_{j}^{2}|\xi_{ab}^{\mu}|^{2}\frac{(\Delta_{0}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{b})(\Delta_{0}\cos\phi-\tilde{\epsilon}_{b})}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{b}[(\Delta_{0}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{b})^{2}-\omega^{2}]}\;,

(27)

where
\phi
is the phase difference between
\Delta_{a}
and
\Delta_{b}
. Despite the fact that the phase difference
\phi
has no effect on the dispersion spectrum of the BdG Hamiltonian, we note that
\phi
has a geometrical consequence, manifested in the multiband connection, by introducing a phase difference between Bloch band vectors. In particular, when
\omega=2\Delta_{0}
, which corresponds to the transition between the nodes of electron and hole sectors,
\sigma^{\mu\mu}\propto 1-\cos\phi
,
namely, the response is suppressed when
\phi=0
, and maximized when
\phi=\pi
. The analytical finding quantitatively agrees with the numerical result in Fig.
4
.

Due to the aforementioned
\xi^{\mu}_{ab}\propto r^{-1}
dependency around the Euler node, for each Euler node of integer Euler class
\chi
, constituted by a flat band and another band with dispersion
\epsilon_{j}\propto r^{2|\chi|}
, such scalings introduce a jump in
\mathrm{Re}[\sigma^{\mu\mu}]
, on crossing the gap energy
\omega=2\Delta_{0}
:

\mathrm{Re}[\sigma^{\mu\mu}(\omega\rightarrow 2\Delta_{0}^{+})]=\frac{e^{2}}{32\hbar}|\chi|(1-\cos\phi)\;,

(28)

where we have used
\omega\rightarrow 2\Delta_{0}^{+}
to indicate
\omega
approaching the gap energy
2\Delta_{0}
from above. This precisely corresponds to the optical conductivity jump of
e^{2}/(16\hbar)
in the Lieb lattice (see Fig.
4
), as the frequency matches a singular node with Euler class
\chi=1
, which resides between a flat and a quadratic band when
m=0
.
In particular, when the Euler node is split for
m\neq 0
, each resulting node can be viewed as carrying a patch Euler class of
\chi=1/2
, and is approximately linear in dispersion. This splitting results in a discrete jump in optical conductivity that is no longer quantized (see Appendix
K
), but is geometrically bounded by the total patch Euler class of
\chi=1
at
e^{2}/(8\hbar)
, as discussed further in Appendix
D
. We note that the discrete jump vanishes when the system becomes fully gapped for
m>m_{c}
, where the total patch Euler class is zero, thus trivializing the geometric bound. To demonstrate that these features are universal, i.e., not specific to particular lattice model realizations, we also retrieve an analogous discrete conductivity jump due to an Euler node in a kagome lattice Hamiltonian, as detailed and illustrated in Appendix
L
.

On the contrary, for second-order conductivity, the results can be well understood within the nonlinear response theory, where the injection current is the major contribution, and no topological terms are acquired. Thus, we leave the discussion of the diagrammatics for the second-order response out of this work.

At third order in optical fields, we find that the dominant contribution arises from a loop diagram introducing four quasiparticle propagators that target the pairs of Euler bands twice; see Fig.
7
(b). This observation has been verified numerically in Figs.
6
(a) and (c). We note that the analytical calculation is mostly analogous to the linear case and is therefore detailed in Appendix
G

{\bf k}

6
(a) is continuous, and
\sigma^{xxxx}\propto\sqrt{\omega-2\Delta_{0}}
; see also Appendix
G

[
28
]
, we note that all diagrams corresponding to the jerk conductivities
[
70
]
contributed by the transitions only between Euler bands in the particle and hole sectors are suppressed due to a set of third-order selection rules. The third-order selection rules are derived and further detailed in Appendix

VII
Discussion

We further comment on the nature and applicability of our findings concerning the optical responses of Euler superconductors. First, it should be noted that anomalous optical conductivity was previously reported in multiorbital superconductors in Ref.
[
62
]
. Here, we show that a certain class of multiorbital, i.e., multiband, superconductors with nontrivial Euler class moreover inherits an anomalous quantum-geometric first-order photoconductivity response bounded by the total Euler class. We unravelled the topological and geometric character of this anomaly with the diagrammatic perspective in the previous section. In that regard, we also note that within the applied assumptions of intraband interactions, i.e., pairings, the vertex correction remains purely of intraband character. Therefore, the intersector contributions to the linear response are not modified when we introduce vertex corrections, as we also demonstrate in Appendix
J
. The vertex corrections contributed by the anomalous propagators admitting hybridized interband pairing channels provide for an interesting future direction to investigate.

We now discuss the physical picture underlying the anomalous optical conductivity features in the studied Euler superconductors. The jump in the real part and the peaked imaginary part of the first-order conductivity can be linked to the vertex coupling term in diagrammatics shown in Fig.
7
. The vertex coupling is proportional to the non-Abelian connection
\xi^{\mu}_{ab}
. The key feature of the Euler node is that
\xi^{\mu}_{ab}
is purely imaginary, and from the mathematical point of band geometry, it has a
1/r
divergence. The divergence leads to a diverging imaginary part of conductivity, which reduces to a finite peak with broadening from
\eta
. For the real part, which vanishes in the absence of divergence, this can be regarded as an anomalous response, as captured by the paramagnetic current bubble term represented by the second diagram in Fig.
7
(a), which diverges for the quasiparticles at the nodal singularity. Intuitively, the physical meaning of this result is the following. The divergent imaginary part reflects the singular absorption of light by the quasiparticles in the superconductor, which arises because the coupling present in the vertices of the diagrams, and which physically represents interband dipole currents
J^{\mu}_{ab}
, is enhanced due to topological degeneracy associated with the Euler node. The Euler topology is realized in the orbital basis, as a winding in the non-Abelian Berry connection, and this winding translates into the mixing of orbitals in the eigenstates, which yields enhanced interband dipole moments and associated interband dipole currents
\xi^{\mu}_{ab}
on introducing photoexcitations
[
28
]
. In particular, these interband dipole currents are precisely the vertices of the optical conductivity diagrams capturing photoexcitations in Fig.
7
. As follows from the Kramers-Kronig causal relations, the absorptive response must be accompanied by a reactive response to light, which gives the jump in the conductivity, when the light couples quasiparticle states with singular interband electric dipole moment induced by the Euler degeneracies. We stress that given the selection rules for optical transitions, our nonvanishing quantization findings hold only specifically for the
d
-wave pairing symmetry. We note that from the physical point of view, the on-site pairing with
\phi=0
introduces no interband dipole moment and no interband electric dipole currents, consistent with the vanishing of the vertex coupling. On the contrary, the
d
-wave pairing symmetry of
\phi=\pi
introduces interband dipole moments in the superconducting state, consistent with the presence of the Euler class, which originally introduced the interband dipole moments in the normal state.

In contrast to the normal Euler phases, such as Euler semimetals, we find distinct effects at the third order in electric fields, namely, the jerk photoconductivity ratios no longer take values inherited from the Euler class
[
28
]
as soon as the pairing terms are introduced. Instead, we identified a one-loop four-vertex third-order response diagram that provides a topological enhancement rather than a quantization, which can be further traced to the quantum geometry associated with the
Euler

invariant

[
71
,
27
]
.

While both the first- and third-order optical features are generic to the Euler invariant, given the diagrammatic and geometric nature of these anomalies, the second-order responses remain model-dependent.
We showed that the strength of an inversion symmetry-breaking perturbation can significantly alter the response of an Euler superconductor and the injection currents cannot be diagrammatically associated with the nontrivial Euler class. We further note that if the quantizing symmetry, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}_{2}\mathcal{T}
or
\mathcal{PT}
, is broken, the Euler class in Eq. (
4
) is no longer well-defined, and hence the related quantization of the jump in the first-order optical conductivity is generically no longer preserved. Similarly, one could expect the breakdown of the quantization in the presence of impurity effects as, e.g., modeled with random potential disorder. While we expect that preserving
\mathcal{C}_{2}\mathcal{T}
or
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry on average could preserve the Euler invariant in the renormalized Hamiltonian in principle
[
38
]
, and the anomalous optical signatures could still be retrieved in some form in the presence of disorder, we leave a detailed study of the effects of nonmagnetic and magnetic impurities on Euler superconductors for future work.

Our findings shed light on the experimentally measurable optical responses in Lieb or kagome superconductors, which correspond to the lattices that naturally realize the Euler class invariant. Importantly, a class of cuprate superconductors, in particular, realizing
d
-wave superconducting pairing
[
68
]
, can be modeled with Lieb lattice Hamiltonians
[
72
]
, which offers for further interesting connections of our optical feature analysis to the exotic correlated states experimentally realizable in these systems. The phase-sensitive character of the optical responses further allows us to optically distinguish Euler superconductors from the other known topological superconductors.
Finally
, the interplay of the superconducting Euler invariant, quantum geometry, and different possible multiband pairings in the presence of additional bands or disorder, remains an attractive direction for future studies.

VIII
Conclusion and Outlook

We studied anomalous optical signatures of superconductors with a nontrivial multiband Euler class invariant.
We introduced a tunable Lieb lattice model for an Euler superconductor. We found that the superconducting Euler invariant introduces anomalous discrete first-order and enhanced third-order optical responses. We further recast these signatures in terms of diagrammatics at the one-loop order that are applicable to general topological superconductors.
We expect our findings to be relevant to the superconducting states realized on Lieb and kagome lattices, where the nontrivial Euler class can be naturally induced. Our results show that the presence of an exotic multigap topological invariant can be unravelled with anomalous optical responses in multiband superconductors.

where
\hat{P}=\sum_{\{c\}}\ket{c}\bra{c}
is a projector onto a set of bands. In the case of Hamiltonians with the nontrivial Euler invariant
\chi_{ab}
, we choose to project onto the Euler bands:

\hat{P}_{\chi_{ab}}=\ket{a}\bra{a}+\ket{b}\bra{b}\;.

(33)

Due to the reality condition on the Hamiltonian
H({\bf k})=H^{*}({\bf k})
, the eigenvectors can be chosen real
\ket{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with
N
given by the number of the orbitals. Hence, the QGT is manifestly real and reduces purely to its real part, the quantum metric
(g_{ab})_{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{Re}~(Q_{ab})_{\mu\nu}
:

(g_{ab})_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\bra{\partial^\mu a}1-\hat{P}\ket{\partial^{\nu} b}+\bra{\partial^\nu b}1-\hat{P}\ket{\partial^{\mu} a}\Big)\;.

(34)

Due to positive-semidefiniteness of QGT
[
71
,
27
,
28
]
, one arrives at a bound between the quantum metric and the Euler curvature,

(g_{aa})_{xx}+(g_{bb})_{xx}+(g_{aa})_{yy}+(g_{bb})_{yy}\geq 2|\text{Eu}_{ab}|\;,

(35)

where we use the projector
\hat{P}=\hat{P}_{\chi_{ab}}
. In the context of this work, the quantum metric decomposes in terms of the interband matrix elements as

(g_{aa})_{\mu\nu}=\sum_{c\neq b}\xi^{\mu}_{ac}\xi^{\nu}_{ca}\;,

(36)

with the intraband matrix elements
\xi^{\mu}_{aa}=0
vanishing under the reality condition. This allows us to conclude with a geometric bound on the sum of the matrix elements entering the optical conductivities and higher-order responses studied diagrammatically in the following sections:

\sum_{c\neq b}\xi^{x}_{ac}\xi^{x}_{ca}+\sum_{c\neq a}\xi^{x}_{bc}\xi^{x}_{cb}+\sum_{c\neq b}\xi^{y}_{ac}\xi^{y}_{ca}+\sum_{c\neq a}\xi^{y}_{bc}\xi^{y}_{cb}\geq|\text{Eu}_{ab}|\;.

(37)

In the above, we showed how the Euler curvature provides a lower bound on the matrix elements corresponding to the transitions
from
and
to
Euler bands. Furthermore, we can consider the quantum geometry induced by the quadratic band touching hosting patch Euler class
\chi=1
, and the resultant geometric conditions on the transition
between
the Euler bands. Within an effective
{\bf k}\cdot{\bf p}
model for such node, it was shown in Ref.
[
28
]
that the multiband quantum metric for a rotationally symmetric Euler nodes with patch invariant
\chi
obtains,

\xi^{x}_{ab}\xi^{x}_{ba}+\xi^{y}_{ab}\xi^{y}_{ba}=\frac{\chi^{2}}{q^{4}}q^{\mu}q^{\nu}(2\delta_{\mu\nu}-1)\;,

(38)

where
q
is the momentum-space displacement from the position of the Euler node. We note that exactly at the node
q=0
, the quantum metric formally diverges. Furthermore, we show that the presence of such momentum space singularity induces a jump in the first-order optical conductivity (see Appendix
D
), as also discussed in the main text.

Appendix B
Generalized velocity operators

Below, for completeness, we show the computation of generalized velocity operator matrix elements in terms of the band dispersion and geometrical quantities
[
63
,
66
]
. We begin with the non-Abelian multiband Berry connection

\displaystyle\xi^{\mu}_{ab}

\displaystyle=i\langle a|\partial^{\mu}b\rangle\;,

(39)

which determines the matrix elements for the generalized operator (
\mathcal{O}
) derivatives:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{\mu}_{ab}

\displaystyle=\langle a|\partial^{\mu}\mathcal{O}|b\rangle

\displaystyle=\partial^{\mu}\langle a|\mathcal{O}|b\rangle-\langle\partial^{\mu}a|\mathcal{O}|b\rangle-\langle a|\mathcal{O}|\partial^{\mu}b\rangle

\displaystyle=\partial^{\mu}\mathcal{O}_{ab}-\sum_{c}(\langle\partial^{\mu}a|c\rangle\langle c|\mathcal{O}|b\rangle+\langle a|\mathcal{O}|c\rangle\langle c|\partial^{\mu}b\rangle)

\displaystyle=\partial^{\mu}\mathcal{O}_{ab}-\sum_{c}(i\xi^{\mu}_{ac}\mathcal{O}_{cb}-i\mathcal{O}_{ac}\xi^{\mu}_{cb})

\displaystyle=\partial^{\mu}\mathcal{O}_{ab}-i[\xi^{\mu},\mathcal{O}]_{ab}\;.

(40)

We first consider the case of a normal state with vanishing pairing terms, as introduced on the Lieb lattice in the main text. For the bulk Hamiltonian, the derivative of which defines the velocity operator, we therefore have

\displaystyle h^{\mu}_{ab}

\displaystyle=\partial^{\mu}h_{ab}-i[\xi^{\mu},h]_{ab}

\displaystyle=\partial^{\mu}\epsilon_{a}\delta_{ab}-i\xi^{\mu}_{ab}\epsilon_{b}+i\epsilon_{a}\xi^{\mu}_{ab}

\displaystyle=\partial^{\mu}\epsilon_{a}\delta_{ab}+i\epsilon_{ab}\xi^{\mu}_{ab}\;.

(41)

Furthermore, in the superconducting state, the velocity operator can be defined in terms of the velocity operator of the normal state:

\displaystyle J^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=\left.(-1)^{n}\frac{\partial H({\bf k},{\bf A})}{\partial A_{\mu_{1}}\ldots\partial A_{\mu_{n}}}\right|_{{\bf A}\rightarrow 0}

\displaystyle=\tau_{z}^{n+1}\otimes h^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}({\bf k})\;.

(42)

Correspondingly, in the band basis of the superconductor model introduced in the main text, we have

\displaystyle J^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}_{a,s_{1};b,s_{2}}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=\langle a,s_{1}|J^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}|b,s_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}+\alpha^{*}_{a,s_{1}}\alpha_{b,s_{2}}}{\sqrt{(1+|\alpha_{a,s_{1}}|^{2})(1+|\alpha_{b,s_{2}}|^{2})}}h_{ab}^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}({\bf k})\;,

(43)

which provides a convenient representation of the current operators for the diagrammatic calculations performed in the next appendixes.

Appendix C
Diagrammatic approach to the superconducting state

We hereby provide a calculation of the optical responses of Euler superconductors within a diagrammatic approach
[
66
]
.

The BdG Hamiltonian with
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry, and specifically, with intraband pairing interactions, can be decomposed as

H({\bf k})=\sum_{a}\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{a}({\bf k})&\Delta_{a}({\bf k})\\
\Delta^{*}_{a}({\bf k})&-\epsilon_{a}({\bf k})\\
\end{pmatrix}\otimes P_{a}({\bf k})\;,

(44)

where
P_{a}
is the projector matrix to band
a
in normal state. Diagrammatically, as shown in Fig.
7
, the total first-order photoconductivity is given by
[
69
,
66

\sigma^{\mu\nu}(\omega)=\frac{i}{\omega}\int[d{\bf k}]\int dk_{0}\Tr[J^{\mu\nu}G(k_{0})+J^{\mu}G(k_{0}+\omega)J^{\nu}G(k_{0})]\;.

(45)

The Green’s function
G^{-1}(k_{0},{\bf k})=k_{0}-H({\bf k})
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle G(k_{0},{\bf k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\begin{pmatrix}k_{0}-\epsilon_{a}({\bf k})&-\Delta_{a}({\bf k})\\
-\Delta^{*}_{a}({\bf k})&k_{0}+\epsilon_{a}({\bf k})\\
\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\otimes P_{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}G_{a}(k_{0},{\bf k})\otimes P_{a}\;,

(46)

\displaystyle G_{a}(k_{0},{\bf k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}_{0}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{a}^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}k_{0}+\epsilon_{a}&\Delta_{a}\\
\Delta_{a}^{*}&k_{0}-\epsilon_{a}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(47)

Furthermore, as the velocity operators can be decomposed as
J^{\mu\nu}=\tau_{z}\otimes h^{\mu\nu}
and
J^{\mu}=I_{2}\otimes h^{\mu}
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu\nu}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\omega}\int[d{\bf k}]\int dk_{0}\Tr[J^{\mu\nu}G(k_{0})+J^{\mu}G(k_{0}+\omega)J^{\nu}G(k_{0})]

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\omega}\sum_{a,b}\int[d{\bf k}]\int dk_{0}\;\{h^{\mu\nu}_{aa}\Tr[\tau_{z}G_{a}(k_{0})]+h^{\mu}_{ab}h_{ba}^{\nu}\Tr[G_{b}(k_{0}+\omega)G_{a}(k_{0})]\}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2\omega}\sum_{a,b}\int[d{\bf k}]\;\big[h^{\mu\nu}_{aa}\tilde{I}_{1}+h^{\mu}_{ab}h_{ba}^{\nu}\tilde{I}_{2}(\omega)\big]\;,

(48)

where these integrals are calculated in Appendix
H
. We note that the form is identical to the normal state, with the velocity operator inherited from the normal state, up to the correction that is included within the integral. Moreover, this can be computed explicitly at zero temperature:

\displaystyle\frac{i}{\omega}\int[d{\bf k}]\;h^{\mu\nu}_{aa}\tilde{I}_{1}

\displaystyle\sim-\frac{i}{\omega}\int[d{\bf k}]\;\partial^{\mu}\partial^{\nu}\epsilon_{a}\frac{\epsilon_{a}}{\sqrt{\Delta^{2}_{a}+\epsilon_{a}^{2}}}\;,

(49)

\displaystyle\frac{i}{\omega}\int[d{\bf k}]\;h^{\mu}_{ab}h_{ba}^{\nu}\tilde{I}_{2}(\omega)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{i}{\omega}\int[d\mathbf{k}]\;h^{\mu}_{ab}h_{ba}^{\nu}\frac{(\tilde{\epsilon}_{a}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{b})(\epsilon_{a}\epsilon_{b}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{a}\tilde{\epsilon}_{b}+\Delta_{a}\Delta_{b}\cos\phi)}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{a}\tilde{\epsilon}_{b}[(\tilde{\epsilon}_{a}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{b})^{2}-\omega^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\omega}\int[d{\bf k}]\;\epsilon_{ab}^{2}\xi^{\mu}_{ab}\xi^{\nu}_{ba}\frac{(\tilde{\epsilon}_{a}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{b})(\epsilon_{a}\epsilon_{b}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{a}\tilde{\epsilon}_{b}+\Delta_{a}\Delta_{b}\cos\phi)}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{a}\tilde{\epsilon}_{b}[(\tilde{\epsilon}_{a}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{b})^{2}-\omega^{2}]}\;.

(50)

Appendix D
Linear response of Euler superconductors

Below we detail the calculation for linear response of an Euler superconductor, where we directly apply Eq. (
50
). We begin by noting that due to the
C_{4}
symmetry, and given the reality condition of the considered Hamiltonians,
\sigma^{\mu\nu}
vanishes identically for
\mu\neq\nu
in the considered models by symmetry in
g^{xy}_{ab}
(see Appendix
A
), which amounts to zero when integrated over the BZ. As such the non-Drude part of the response, at zero temperature, is given by

\sigma_{\text{Euler}}\equiv\frac{\sigma^{\mu\mu}_{ab}+\sigma^{\mu\mu}_{ba}}{2}=\frac{i}{\omega}\int[d{\bf k}]\;\epsilon_{ab}^{2}|\xi_{ab}^{\mu}|^{2}\frac{(\tilde{\epsilon}_{a}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{b})(\epsilon_{a}\epsilon_{b}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{a}\tilde{\epsilon}_{b}+\Delta_{a}\Delta_{b}\cos\phi)}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{a}\tilde{\epsilon}_{b}[(\tilde{\epsilon}_{a}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{b})^{2}-\omega^{2}]}\;.

(51)

We first consider a simplified case where only two bands are optically probed and relevant. We also assume
{\epsilon_{a}=0},{\Delta_{a}=\Delta_{b}=\Delta_{0}}
. Having further assumed no angular dependence for band dispersion, we have the following:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{Euler}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{i}{\omega A}\int^{r_{0}}_{0}\int^{2\pi}_{0}rdrd\theta\;\frac{(\Delta_{0}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{b})(-\Delta_{0}\tilde{\epsilon}_{b}+\Delta_{0}^{2}\cos\phi)}{\Delta_{0}\tilde{\epsilon}_{b}[(\Delta_{0}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{b})^{2}-\omega^{2}]}\epsilon_{b}^{2}|\xi_{ab}^{\mu}|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\omega A}\int^{E_{0}}_{2\Delta_{0}}\int^{2\pi}_{0}rdEd\theta\;\left(\frac{d\tilde{\epsilon}_{b}}{dr}\right)^{-1}\frac{E[E-\Delta_{0}(1+\cos\phi)]}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{b}(E^{2}-\omega^{2})}\epsilon_{b}^{2}|\xi_{ab}^{\mu}|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\omega A}\int^{E_{0}}_{2\Delta_{0}}\int^{2\pi}_{0}rdEd\theta\;\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{b}}\frac{d\epsilon_{b}}{dr}\right)^{-1}\frac{E[E-\Delta_{0}(1+\cos\phi)]}{E^{2}-\omega^{2}}|\xi_{ab}^{\mu}|^{2}

\displaystyle\sim-\frac{i}{\omega A}\int^{2\pi}_{0}d\theta\;f^{2}(\theta)\int^{E_{0}}_{2\Delta_{0}}dE\;\frac{r^{2(1+n)}}{m}\frac{E[E-\Delta_{0}(1+\cos\phi)]}{E^{2}-\omega^{2}}\;,

(52)

where
E=\Delta_{0}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{b}
,
A
is the area of the BZ, and in the third line, we make an ansatz
|\xi^{\mu}_{ab}|^{2}\sim r^{2n}f^{2}(\theta)
and
\epsilon_{b}\sim\alpha r^{m}
, locally around the node. The ansatz follows from considering a minimal effective
{\bf k}\cdot{\bf p}
model for an Euler superconductor,

H^{\chi}_{\text{SC}}({\bf k})=\begin{pmatrix}H^{\chi}({\bf k})&\Delta_{0}\mathbbm{1}_{2}\\
\Delta_{0}\mathbbm{1}_{2}&-H^{\chi}({\bf k})\\
\end{pmatrix}=\alpha(k^{2}_{x}+k^{2}_{y})[\mathbbm{1}_{2}\otimes\tau_{z}]+\begin{pmatrix}2\alpha k_{x}k_{y}&\alpha(k^{2}_{x}-k^{2}_{y})&\Delta_{0}&0\\
\alpha(k^{2}_{x}-k^{2}_{y})&-2\alpha k_{x}k_{y}&0&\Delta_{0}\\
\Delta_{0}&0&-2\alpha k_{x}k_{y}&\alpha(k^{2}_{y}-k^{2}_{x})\\
0&\Delta_{0}&\alpha(k^{2}_{y}-k^{2}_{x})&2\alpha k_{x}k_{y}\end{pmatrix},

(53)

where
\otimes
denotes a Kronecker product, and the first term only contributes to the appropriate quadratic or flat band dispersions. The Hamiltonian above corresponds to the case in which the phase difference is
\phi=0
. The nonzero phase difference can be introduced with nontrivial pairing, as detailed in Sec.
III
. In the above, the nodal Hamiltonian
H^{\chi}({\bf k})
in the individual particle and hole sectors more compactly reads
[
26
,
55
]
:

H^{\chi}({\bf k})=\alpha[(k^{2}_{x}+k^{2}_{y})\mathbbm{1}_{2}+(k^{2}_{x}-k^{2}_{y})\tau_{x}+2k_{x}k_{y}\tau_{z}]\;.

(54)

We note that the form of dispersion controlled by
\alpha
only contributes to the prefactor of the integral and to the eigenstate normalization factors, which cancel, as
n=-1
for the quadratic node with Euler class
\chi=1
that is centered around
r=0

[
28
]
. Specifically in the case of a single Euler node, we retrieve
f(\theta)=|\chi|\sin\theta
from the eigenstates
[
28
]
. Having related the dispersion to nodal Euler class as
m=2|\chi|

[
55
]
, after setting
e=\hbar=1
, we have:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{Euler}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\pi|\chi|}{2\omega A}\int^{E_{0}}_{2\Delta_{0}}dE~\frac{E[E-\Delta_{0}(1+\cos\phi)]}{E^{2}-\omega^{2}},

(55)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}[\sigma_{\mathrm{Euler}}(2\Delta_{0}<\omega<E_{0})]

\displaystyle\sim-\frac{i\pi|\chi|}{8\pi^{2}\omega}(\pi i)\frac{\omega[\omega-\Delta_{0}(1+\cos\phi)]}{2\omega}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\chi|}{16}\left(1-\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\omega}(1+\cos\phi)\right),

(56)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}[\sigma_{\mathrm{Euler}}(\omega)]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi|\chi|}{2\omega A}\left.\mathrm{Re}\left(E-\frac{\omega}{2}\ln\frac{\omega+E}{\omega-E}-\Delta_{0}\frac{1+\cos\phi}{2}\ln(E^2-\om^2)\right)\right|_{E=2\Delta_{0}}^{E_{0}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}[\sigma_{\mathrm{Euler}}(\omega\sim 2\Delta_{0})]

\displaystyle\sim\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\pi|\chi|}{8A}(1-\cos\phi)\ln|2\Delta_{0}-\omega|&\mathrm{for}\quad\phi\neq 0\\
&\frac{\pi|\chi|}{2A}\left(2+2\ln\frac{2\Delta_{0}+E_{0}}{4\Delta_{0}}-\frac{E_{0}}{\Delta_{0}}\right)&\mathrm{for}\quad\phi=0\;.\end{aligned}\right.

(57)

In the above, we set
A=(2\pi)^{2}
when calculating the real part. The real part vanishes outside of

\omega\sim 2\Delta_{0}
we have a simplified form
\mathrm{Re}[\sigma_{\mathrm{Euler}}]=|\chi|{(1-\cos\phi)}/32
in the units of

\omega\sim 2\Delta_{0}
, which corresponds to the transition between Euler nodes in different sectors, there is a divergent peak, except when
\phi=0

Below we further check with the exact form of an eigensystem of our concrete model (see Sec.
II
). When the third band is isolated, only the transition between the flat band to the neighboring dispersive band could give rise to a response. We thus have
\epsilon_{a}=0,\;\epsilon_{b}=\epsilon_{-},\;\tilde{\epsilon}_{a}=\Delta_{0},\;\tilde{\epsilon}_{b}=\sqrt{\Delta_{0}^{2}+\epsilon_{-}^{2}},\;|\xi_{ab}|^{2}=(\sin^{2}\theta,\cos^{2}\theta)/(r^{2}+\epsilon_{-}^{2})

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{Euler}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\pi}{\omega A}\int^{r_{0}}_{0}rdr~\frac{\epsilon_{-}^{2}}{r^{2}+\epsilon_{-}^{2}}\frac{\left(\Delta_{0}+\sqrt{\Delta_{0}^{2}+\epsilon_{-}^{2}}\right)\left(-\Delta_{0}\sqrt{\Delta_{0}^{2}+\epsilon_{-}^{2}}+\Delta_{0}^{2}\cos\phi\right)}{\Delta_{0}\sqrt{\Delta_{0}^{2}+\epsilon_{-}^{2}}\left[\left(\Delta_{0}+\sqrt{\Delta_{0}^{2}+\epsilon_{-}^{2}}\right)^{2}-\omega^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\pi}{2\omega A}\int^{E_{0}}_{2\Delta_{0}}dE~\frac{E[E-\Delta_{0}(1+\cos\phi)]}{E^{2}-\omega^{2}},

(58)

where
E_{0}=\Delta_{0}+\sqrt{\Delta_{0}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}_{-}(r_{0})}
. Note the function form is identical to that of Euler node with
\chi=1

\chi=1

56

\phi=\pi

We now also briefly discuss the case when

\sigma_{\mathrm{Euler}}=-\frac{i\pi|\chi|}{\omega A}\int^{E_{0}}_{\Delta_{a}+\Delta_{b}}dE~\frac{E[E-\Delta_{b}(1+\cos\phi)]}{E^{2}-\omega^{2}}\;,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}[\sigma_{\mathrm{Euler}}(\Delta_{a}+\Delta_{b}<\omega<E_{0})]

D.1
Beyond flat band limit

We now discuss the case where both Euler bands are dispersive, respectively, with dispersion
\epsilon_{\pm}=\alpha_{\pm}r^{2|\chi|}
, to illustrate that the discrete jump in linear conductivity is a general feature for the Euler node, and is anomalous in nature due to divergence in the geometry which is a consequence of the topology.
Applying Eq. (
51

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{Euler}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{i}{\omega}\int^{r_{0}}_{0}dr~\frac{(\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-})^{2}|\chi|^{2}\Delta_{0}(\cos\phi-1)}{4\Delta_{0}^{2}+2(\alpha_{+}^{2}+\alpha_{-}^{2})r^{2|\chi|}-\omega^{2}}r^{2|\chi|-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{i|\chi|\Delta(1-\cos\phi)}{16\pi\omega}\frac{(\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-})^{2}}{\alpha_{+}^{2}+\alpha_{-}^{2}}\int^{\gamma_{0}}_{4\Delta_{0}^{2}}d\gamma~\frac{1}{\gamma-\omega^{2}}\;,

(61)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}[\sigma_{\mathrm{Euler}}(\omega\rightarrow 2\Delta_{0}^{+})]

\displaystyle\sim\frac{i|\chi|\Delta_{0}(1-\cos\phi)}{16\pi\omega}\frac{(\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-})^{2}}{\alpha_{+}^{2}+\alpha_{-}^{2}}(i\pi)

\displaystyle=\frac{|\chi|}{32}\frac{(\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-})^{2}}{\alpha_{+}^{2}+\alpha_{-}^{2}}(1-\cos\phi)\;.

(62)

In the limit where one of the bands is flat, namely,
|\alpha_{+}|
or
|\alpha_{-}|\rightarrow 0
, we again have a jump of

for
\phi=\pi
and
\chi=1
, agreeing with the previous calculation.
Another interesting case appears when

, resulting in a system similar to a graphene multilayer

For this case, the jump in the real part of the linear conductivity is maximized and given by
|\chi|e^{2}/(8\hbar)
for
\phi=\pi

Given there are
N
Euler nodes each with nodal Euler class
\chi_{i}
for

at chemical potential
\mu

\mathrm{Re}[\sigma_{\mathrm{Euler}}(\omega\rightarrow 2\Delta_{0}^{+})]\leq\frac{e^{2}}{8\hbar}\sum^{N}_{i=1}|\chi_{i}|\;,

(63)

where we have showcased that the discrete jump is bounded by the total nodal Euler class
\chi_{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\chi_{i}|
.

Appendix E
Decomposition and analysis of parity effects on the second-order optical responses

We briefly comment on the effects of parity breaking, in particular focusing on the second-order optical responses. We begin by recognizing that, given a Hamiltonian
h({\bf k})
, we can decompose it in terms of its even [
e({\bf k})
] and odd [
o({\bf k})
] parts:

\displaystyle h({\bf k})

\displaystyle=o({\bf k})+e({\bf k})\;,

(68)

\displaystyle o(-{\bf k})

\displaystyle=-o({\bf k})\;,

(69)

\displaystyle e(-{\bf k})

\displaystyle=e({\bf k})\;.

(70)

We now employ a parity-based decomposition to the generalized velocity operators (see also Appendix
B
), which obtains

\displaystyle J^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}({\bf k})

\displaystyle=\left.(-1)^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial A^{\mu_{i}}}H({\bf k},\bm{A})\right|_{\bm{A}\rightarrow 0}

\displaystyle=\tau_{3}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}j({\bf k})&0\\
0&-j(-{\bf k})\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\tau_{3}^{n}\left[\begin{pmatrix}j_{e}^{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}({\bf k})&0\\

\displaystyle=\frac{(\alpha_{-,-}\alpha_{-,+}-1)j_{o}^{\mu}+(\alpha_{-,-}\alpha_{-,+}+1)j_{e}^{\mu}}{\sqrt{(1+|\alpha_{-,-}|^{2})(1+|\alpha_{-,+}|^{2})}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\sqrt{\Delta_{0}^{2}+\epsilon_{e}^{2}}}\partial^{\mu}\epsilon_{o}\;,

(73)

\displaystyle J_{-,s;-,s}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{(|\alpha_{-,s}|^{2}-1)j_{o}^{\mu}+(|\alpha_{-,s}|^{2}+1)j_{e}^{\mu}}{1+|\alpha_{-,s}|^{2}},

\displaystyle=j_{e}^{\mu}+\frac{s\epsilon_{e}}{\sqrt{\Delta_{0}^{2}+\epsilon_{e}^{2}}}j_{o}^{\mu}\;,

\displaystyle=\frac{2\epsilon_{e}}{\sqrt{\Delta_{0}^{2}+\epsilon_{e}^{2}}}j_{o}^{\mu}\;,

(75)

where

and

are the even and odd parts of the dispersion respectively, and

Hence, unlike the first-order response, the second-order response cannot be directly used to target and infer the topological Euler invariant.

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{l^{\prime}}\int[d{\bf p}]\;\{I_{1}(l,l^{\prime})(\Delta_{x,l}+\Delta_{x,l^{\prime}})+I_{0}(l,l^{\prime})[\omega\Delta_{x,l^{\prime}}+i(\epsilon_{l}\Delta_{y,l^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{l^{\prime}}\Delta_{y,l})]\}h^{\mu}_{l,l^{\prime}}({\bf p})\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-2\int[d{\bf p}]\;[2\Delta_{x,l}I_{1}(l,l)+\omega\Delta_{x,l}I_{0}(l,l)]h^{\mu}_{l,l}({\bf p})\;,

(98)

\displaystyle Q^{\mu}_{y,l}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{l^{\prime}}\int[dp]\;\frac{\Delta_{l^{\prime}}(p_{0}+\omega+\epsilon_{l})-\Delta_{l}^{*}(p_{0}+\epsilon_{l^{\prime}})+\Delta_{l}(p_{0}-\epsilon_{l^{\prime}})-\Delta_{l^{\prime}}^{*}(p_{0}+\omega-\epsilon_{l})}{(|\Delta_{l^{\prime}}|^{2}+\epsilon^{2}_{l^{\prime}}-p^{2}_{0})[|\Delta_{l}|^{2}+\epsilon^{2}_{l}-(p_{0}+\omega)^{2}]}\Tr[P_{l^{\prime}}({\bf p})P_{l}({\bf p})h^{\mu}_{l,l^{\prime}}({\bf p})]

\displaystyle=2\sum_{l^{\prime}}\int[d{\bf p}]\;\{I_{1}(l,l^{\prime})(\Delta_{y,l}+\Delta_{y,l^{\prime}})+I_{0}(l,l^{\prime})[\omega\Delta_{y,l^{\prime}}-i(\epsilon_{l}\Delta_{x,l^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{l^{\prime}}\Delta_{x,l})]\}h^{\mu}_{l,l^{\prime}}({\bf p})\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=2\int[d{\bf p}]\;[2\Delta_{y,l}I_{1}(l,l)+\omega\Delta_{y,l}I_{0}(l,l)]h^{\mu}_{l,l}({\bf p})\;,

(99)

\displaystyle Q_{i,l;j,l^{\prime}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-\int[dp]\;\Tr\left[\tau_{i}E_{l}G(p+\omega)\tau_{j}E_{l^{\prime}}G(p)\right]

\displaystyle=-\int[dp]\;\Tr[\tau_{i}G_{l}(p+\omega)\tau_{j}G_{l^{\prime}}(p)]\Tr[P_{l}P_{l^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle=-\int[dp]\;\Tr[\tau_{i}G_{l}(p+\omega)\tau_{j}G_{l}(p)]\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}\;,

(100)

\displaystyle Q_{x,l;x,l}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-2\int[d{\bf p}]\;(\Delta_{x,l}^{2}-\Delta_{y,l}^{2}-\epsilon^{2}_{l})I_{0}(l,l)+\omega I_{1}(l,l)+I_{2}(l,l)\;,

(101)

\displaystyle Q_{y,l;y,l}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-2\int[d{\bf p}]\;(\Delta_{y,l}^{2}-\Delta_{x,l}^{2}-\epsilon^{2}_{l})I_{0}(l,l)+\omega I_{1}(l,l)+I_{2}(l,l)\;,

(102)

\displaystyle Q_{x,l;y,l}(\omega)

\displaystyle=4\int[d{\bf p}]\;(2\Delta_{x,l}\Delta_{y,l}+i\epsilon_{l}\omega)I_{0}(l,l)\;,

(103)

\displaystyle Q_{y,l;x,l}(\omega)

\displaystyle=4\int[d{\bf p}]\;(2\Delta_{x,l}\Delta_{y,l}-i\epsilon_{l}\omega)I_{0}(l,l)\;,

(104)

and where we implicitly used that
\gamma^{\mu}_{l,l^{\prime}}=I_{2}\otimes h^{\mu}_{l,l^{\prime}}
.

Interestingly, in the band basis, all interband terms vanish when we only have an intraband interaction, and all terms contain no information of the quantum geometry of the band. Above, with
\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}>0
for clarity, we have defined
I_{i}
as

\displaystyle I_{0}(l,l^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int dp_{0}~\frac{1}{(\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}}^{2}-p_{0}^{2})[\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-(p_{0}+\omega)^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{z}\frac{1}{(\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}}^{2}-z^{2})[\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-(z+i\omega)^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{z=\pm\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}},\pm\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}-i\omega}f(z)R_{0}(z)

\displaystyle\rightarrow R_{0}(-\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}})+R_{0}(-\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}-\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}}}{2\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}}[(\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{l})^{2}-\omega^{2}]}\;,

(105)

\displaystyle I_{1}(l,l^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int dp_{0}~\frac{p_{0}}{(\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}}^{2}-p_{0}^{2})[\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-(p_{0}+\omega)^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{z}\frac{iz}{(\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}}^{2}+z^{2})[\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-(z+i\omega)^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{z=\pm\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}},\pm\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}-\omega}f(z)R_{1}(z)

\displaystyle\rightarrow R_{1}(-\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}})+R_{1}(-\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}-\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega}{2\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}[(\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{l})^{2}-\omega^{2}]}\;,

(106)

\displaystyle I_{2}(l,l^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int dp_{0}~\frac{p_{0}^{2}}{(\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}}^{2}-p_{0}^{2})[\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-(p_{0}+\omega)^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{z}\frac{z^{2}}{(\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}}^{2}-z^{2})[\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-(z+i\omega)^{2}]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{z=\pm\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}},\pm\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}-\omega}f(z)R_{2}(z)

\displaystyle\rightarrow R_{2}(-\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}})+R_{2}(-\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}-\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega^{2}-\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}(\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}})}{2\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}[(\tilde{\epsilon}_{l^{\prime}}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{l})^{2}-\omega^{2}]}\;,

(107)

where
R_{i}
denotes the residue of the integrand of
I_{i}
, and
\omega_{n}
are the odd Matsubara frequencies. We also take the zero temperature limit to obtain a simple analytic form. Since
\omega
is due to the incoming photon, it has to be an element of the even Matsubara frequencies, thus the corresponding poles in the imaginary axis remain unshifted. With only the
l=l^{\prime}
integral being relevant, we list zero temperature results below:

\displaystyle I_{0}(l,l)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}(4\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-\omega^{2})}\;,

(108)

\displaystyle I_{1}(l,l)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega}{2\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}(4\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-\omega^{2})}\;,

(109)

\displaystyle I_{2}(l,l)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega^{2}-2\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}}{2\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}(4\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-\omega^{2})}\;.

(110)

As such, the correlation function can be simplified as

\displaystyle Q^{\mu}_{x,l}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-4\int[d{\bf p}]\;\frac{\omega\Delta_{x,l}}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}(4\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-\omega^{2})}\partial^{\mu}\epsilon_{l}\;,

(111)

\displaystyle Q^{\mu}_{y,l}(\omega)

\displaystyle=4\int[d{\bf p}]\;\frac{\omega\Delta_{y,l}}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}(4\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-\omega^{2})}\partial^{\mu}\epsilon_{l}\;,

(112)

\displaystyle Q_{x,l;x,l}

\displaystyle=-2\int[d{\bf p}]\;\frac{2(\Delta_{x,l}^{2}-\Delta_{y,l}^{2}-\epsilon_{l}^{2})+\omega^{2}+\omega^{2}-2\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}}{2\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}(4\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-\omega^{2})}

\displaystyle=-2\int[d{\bf p}]\frac{2\Delta_{l}^{2}\cos^{2}\phi-2\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}+\omega^{2}}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}(4\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-\omega^{2})}\;,

(113)

\displaystyle Q_{y,l;y,l}

\displaystyle=-2\int[d{\bf p}]\;\frac{2(\Delta_{y,l}^{2}-\Delta_{x,l}^{2}-\epsilon_{l}^{2})+\omega^{2}+\omega^{2}-2\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}}{2\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}(4\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-\omega^{2})}

\displaystyle=-2\int[d{\bf p}]\frac{2\Delta_{l}^{2}\sin^{2}\phi-2\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}+\omega^{2}}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}(4\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-\omega^{2})}\;,

(114)

\displaystyle Q_{x,l;y,l}(\omega)

\displaystyle=4\int[d{\bf p}]\;\frac{2\Delta_{x,l}\Delta_{y,l}+i\epsilon_{l}\omega}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}(4\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-\omega^{2})}\;,

(115)

\displaystyle Q_{y,l;x,l}(\omega)

\displaystyle=4\int[d{\bf p}]\;\frac{2\Delta_{x,l}\Delta_{y,l}-i\epsilon_{l}\omega}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}(4\tilde{\epsilon}_{l}^{2}-\omega^{2})}\;.

(116)

We note that with only the intraband interaction, no information of the quantum geometry is included within the vertex correction. Finally, we note that the vertex correction can be obtained by solving the set of linear equations that read

\displaystyle Q^{\mu}_{x,l}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{g_{l}}-Q_{x,l;x,l}(\omega)\right)\Lambda^{\mu}_{x,l}(\omega)-Q_{x,l;y,l}(\omega)\Lambda^{\mu}_{y,l}(\omega)\;,

(118)

\displaystyle Q^{\mu}_{y,l}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{g_{l}}-Q_{y,l;y,l}(\omega)\right)\Lambda^{\mu}_{y,l}(\omega)-Q_{y,l;x,l}(\omega)\Lambda^{\mu}_{x,l}(\omega)\;.

(119)

Vertex correction only affects the transitions of form
(l,\pm)\rightarrow(l,\pm)
and
(l,\pm)\rightarrow(l,\mp)
. The former correspond to the intraband response, while the latter intersector transitions are forbidden, given that
H^{\mu}_{l,\pm;l,\mp}=0
.

We conclude by stressing that there is a fundamental difference between working in the band basis and in the orbital basis. In the orbital basis, even limited to the scope of an on-site interaction only, as soon as we convert to the band basis, a general interaction would be both interband and intraband. This dissimilarity could explain why in Ref.
[
66
]
a significant difference due to the band mixing can arise, whereas in our case there is no effect beyond the intraband pairing in the linear optical response of the superconducting state. However, this subtle distinction does have an interesting consequence in the third-order response; namely, another type of transition is now possible, which is because we can have a multiple vertex correction.
