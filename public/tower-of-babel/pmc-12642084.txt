Identification of active anti-aging ingredients from asparagus cochinchinensis based on the spectrum-effect relationship
Authors: Runlan Wan, Zhiyan Cai, Zhen Liu, Jiarong You, Liuyan Wang, Xuebo Tang, Yongjun Chen, Qin Wang, Lilan Ou, Chun Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aging is an irreversible degenerative process marked by declining physiological function. Developing therapeutics to delay aging and prolong healthy life represents a significant research focus. The dried roots of Asparagus cochinchinensis (Lour.) Merr. (Asparagi Radix, AR), a traditional Chinese medicine, possesses various pharmacological properties, including antioxidative, antitumor, and anti-inflammatory effects, and is commonly used in anti-aging formulas. Although steroidal saponins are its major active components, the specific compounds responsible for its anti-aging effects remain unidentified, and their mechanistic basis is unclear. This study evaluated the anti-aging effects of AR-derived total saponin extracts (ATSE) in Caenorhabditis elegans and elucidated underlying mechanisms. We prepared multiple ATSE batches from distinct geographical sources and assessed their bioactivity through lifespan and healthspan analyses (brood size, pharyngeal pumping), stress resistance, assays (oxidative, thermal), senescence biomarkers (lipofuscin accumulation, SOD activity, ROS levels). Mechanistic insights were obtained using transgenic strains and qRT-PCR, while ELSD-HPLC fingerprinting and spectrum-effect correlation identified active compounds. Key findings demonstrated that ATSE from Sichuan (T1) most effectively prolonged C. elegans lifespan (p < 0.01), enhanced stress resistance, increased SOD activity, and decreased ROS levels. The lifespan extension was primarily mediated through the FOXO/DAF-16 signaling pathway, while spectrum-effect analysis further identified (25S)-officinalisnin-II as the most active compound, extending C. elegans longevity under oxidative stress by 13.91%. Our research revealed AR’s anti-aging mechanism and identified a key active compound, laying the foundation for quality-controlled AR therapeutics and providing new insights for the development of anti-aging drugs from traditional Chinese medicine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05166-5.

Introduction

The aging process involves irreversible degenerative alterations in the human in which the body’s ability to renew and repair itself weakens. Aging is strongly related to several degenerative and dangerous illnesses, which include diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative disorders, and cancer [1, 2]. The world is now witnessing an accelerated aging process, which will bring enormous challenges to society. With global aging accelerating, strategies to prevent or reverse functional decline are urgently needed.

Traditional Chinese medicines (TCMs), including Panax ginseng [3], Astragalus membranaceus [4], and Asparagi Radix(AR, Tiandong) [5], exhibit significant potential in promoting healthy aging. AR, derived from the dried root tubers of Asparagus cochinchinensis (Lour.) Merr., is an established TCM with a 2000-year clinical history in China. Officially listed in the Pharmacopoeia of the People’s Republic of China (2025 edition), it is traditionally prescribed for asthma, cough, constipation, thrombosis [6], and is widely incorporated into anti-aging prescriptions [7].

Modern pharmacological research confirm AR’s multi-target bioactivities, such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-tumor, neuroprotective, intestinal protective, and anti-aging properties, primarily attributed to steroidal saponins, polysaccharides, and amino acids [8–13].Among these, steroidal saponins constitute the most abundant fraction [14, 15]. Dioscin, as an important steroidal saponin, demonstrating notable anti-aging efficy [16]. A classical prescription containing AR, known as Qisheng Pill, contains 114 identified compounds, including diosgenin, methyl protodioscin, ferulic acid, and total saponins, which have been shown to inhibit inflammation, regulate intestinal dysbiosis, and ameliorate symptoms of Alzheimer’s disease [17]. The alcoholic extract of AR increased SOD activity, decreased the malonaldehyde concentration in the liver and brain, and slowed the aging process in D-galactose-aged mice [9, 14]. Consequently, we hypothesize that the total saponin fraction of A. cochinchinensis (ATSE) represents the primary anti-aging fraction.

However, despite its extensive classical use, modern pharmacological research on AR’s anti-aging mechanisms remains limited. Existing studies focus almost exclusively on aqueous or alcoholic extracts [9, 14, 18], failing to identify the specific bioactive fraction(s) responsible for efficacy. Critically, no studies have investigated AR’s anti-aging effects using C. elegans—a premier model for aging research due to its short lifespan, genetic tractability, and high human gene homology [19–22]. Furthermore, AR’s compositional variability across regions [23], complicates quality control, highlighting the need to pinpoint its active anti-aging constituents.

To address these gaps, we employed a spectrum-effect relationship approach—a method linking chemical fingerprints to bioactivity to identify bioactive TCM components in AR [24, 25]. Specifically, we first prepared ATSE of AR from different major production areas in China, and evaluated their effects in C. elegans through multi-phenotypic assays (lifespan, stress resistance, motility) while validating key anti-aging mechanisms. Crucially, we demonstrated ATSE modulates the insulin/IGF-1 signalling (IIS) pathway via DAF-16 nuclear translocation and upregulation of downstream targets (sod-3, gst-4). We then correlated ATSE’s chemical profiles with observed bioactivity to pinpoint key anti-aging constituents. This integrated strategy not only elucidates the mechanistic basis of AR’s efficacy but also establishes a foundation for developing standardized, component-targeted anti-aging applications.

Results

C. elegans’ lifespan was prolonged by ATSE

To explore ATSE’s anti-aging effect, we evaluated longevity—a key aging biomarker—in wild-type C. elegans C. elegans (N2) treated with ATSE from T1 origin at geometrically increasing concentrations (100, 200, 400, and 800 µg/mL), along with a supplemental high dose (1000 µg/mL) to assess toxicity. While 100 and 200 µg/mL ATSE induced non-significant lifespan-extending trends, doses ≥ 400 µg/mL (400, 800, and 1000 µg/mL) significantly extended both mean and maximum lifespan versus control (p < 0.05; Fig. 1A, Table S1). Specifically, mean lifespan increased by 20.55% (p < 0.01) at 400 µg/mL and 13.49% (p < 0.05) at 800 µg/mL. To further determine the anti-aging effect of ATSE from different sources, we treated C. elegans with 400 µg/mL ATSE from different origins (from the T1, T2, T3, T4, and T5 groups). Compared to the control group, ATSE from T1 and T2 origin extended mean lifespan by 15.47% and 11.26%, respectively(p < 0.05) (Fig. 1B), in worms reared at 20 °C. Induced sterility has been extensively studied for its ability to prolong the longevity of C. elegans [26].

As shown in Fig. 1C, there was not a noticeable impact on C. elegans fecundity from the different ATSE origins employed in the present study. Systemic muscle cells progressively lose vitality with increasing age, leading to a variety of phenotypes, including impaired movement and pharyngeal pumping [27].

Then, we examined the swallowing frequency of N2 C. elegans on day 7 to determine whether ATSE-induced pharyngeal pumping increases food intake (Fig. 1D). The results revealed that ATSE did not influence the swallowing frequency of C. elegans. These findings suggested that its capacity to lengthen longevity may not be dependent on the dietary restriction route.

Moreover, we measured the body bending rate of the ATSE-treated C. elegans. When comparing the ATSE-treated and control groups of C. elegans, there was no remarkable difference in the frequency of body movements (Fig. 1E).

In summary, it appears from the results presented that ATSE increases the longevity of C. elegans and maintains intact motility and fecundity.

ATSE reduces Lipofuscin accumulation

Lipofuscin, an autofluorescent aging pigment accumulating in the intestinal tract of C. elegans, serves as a biomarker of senescence [28]. Fluorescence intensity measurements revealed significant reductions in intestinal lipofuscin accumulation in all ATSE-treated groups (T1-T5) compared to the untreated control on day 10 (p < 0.05; Fig. 2A, B). Notably, the T1 and T2 groups exhibited the most pronounced reduction (p < 0.001 vs. control). These data demonstrate that ATSE significantly reduces age-associated lipofuscin deposition in C. elegans.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are the main source of oxidative damage, where reduced levels indicate heightened antioxidant capacity. Superoxide dismutase (SOD), the key antioxidant enzyme, functions by scavenging excess superoxide radicals [30]. The FOXO transcription factor homologue DAF-16 is required for ATSE’s impact on C. elegans lifespan extension

In C. elegans, metabolism, growth, longevity, and behavior are all impacted by the insulin/IGF-1 signalling (IIS) pathway [31]. The insulin/IGF-1 transmembrane receptor DAF-2 is a component of the C. elegans IIS pathway, which is regulated by insulin-like peptide ligands. DAF-2 governs a conserved PI3K/Akt kinase cascade, ultimately regulating the FOXO transcription factor (TF) DAF-16, which oversees key functions of the pathway [32].

We measured the expression levels of various IIS pathway-associated genes in C. elegans to ascertain if ATSE increases longevity via the IIS pathway. The daf-16, gst-4, sod-3, ctl-1, skn-1, hsf-1, and hsp-60 expression levels were significantly increased after treatment with ATSE (Fig. 4A). Notably, the DAF-16/FOXO TF integrates various signalling pathways, activating stress-related gene expression to regulate lifespan and fitness [33]. We investigated if DAF-16 was necessary for the ATSE-induced anti-aging benefit by estimating the CF1038 strain’s (daf-16(mu86)) lifespan after ATSE treatment. Interestingly, the mutation of daf-16 completely abolished the lifespan extension effect of ATSE. (Fig. 4B), indicating that daf-16 is crucial for the anti-aging action mediated by ATSE. We investigated the daf-16 expression of the TJ356 strain (daf-16p::GFP) to determine if ATSE improves the nuclear translocation of DAF-16. Validation of the method of fingerprint analysis

The relative peak area (RPA) and relative retention time (RRT) of the sixteen peaks exhibited relative standard deviations (RSDs) below 0.98% and 0.65%, respectively, indicating that the precision of the instrument was good. In terms of repeatability, the RSD values of RPA and RRT were lower than 3.21% and 4.32%, respectively, indicating that the method had good reproducibility. The stability test showed that the rsd of RPA and RRT were less than 2.65% and 1.32%, respectively, indicating that the sample had good stability.

The screening of the paraquat oxidative stress model can preliminarily determine whether the drug has a role in prolonging life [36]. The antioxidant effects of different ATSEs were determined by assessing paraquat-induced chronic oxidative stress. The variations in the impacts of distinct ATSEs on the average lifespan of C. elegans in the chronic oxidative stress model were illustrated in Fig. 6 and Table S3. The life extension effect of G6 is the greatest, the life extension is extended by 29.06%, and the life extension of G1 is not obvious, at only 5.46%. The relationship between the mean lifespan and the area of sixteen common peaks in terms of spectrum efficacy was investigated via the GRA (Table S4, Supporting Information). We performed Pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 7, Table S4, Supporting Information), which revealed that peaks 5, 9, 7, 8, 4, 1, 2, 11, 16, and 13 from the ATSE had significant positive correlations with the mean longevity of the C. elegans. PLSR

After standardizing the retention time and peak area for every compound, PLSR was employed to examine the correlation between the standardized peak area and the data on drug effects. An increased contribution is shown by a higher predicted variable (VIPpred) significance for the projection.

In most cases, a VIPpred value higher than 1 indicates its significance in the model [40]. The results showed that the common peaks with VIPpred values greater than 1 were X5, X9, X7, X8, and X12. Moreover, a larger coefficient indicates a more pronounced impact. The variable’s connection with efficacy is positive when the coefficient is positive and negative otherwise. The results showed that peaks 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 11, 13, and 16 had positive normalization coefficients and contributed to the anti-aging role of ATSE. Combining the results of the VIP values and coefficients, the common peaks X5, X7, X8, and X9 were positively correlated with the mean lifespan of the C. elegans. In general, these results corroborated the outcomes of the gray correlation and Pearson correlation analyses that were previously delineated, further proving that peaks 5, 7, 8, and 9 in ATSE were the major bioactive components of ATSE capable of extending nematode longevity.

Discussion

A. cochinchinensis, as a medicinal and edible homologous herb, has a wide distribution and boasts abundant germplasm resources in China. Currently, it is mainly produced in Sichuan, Guangxi, Yunnan and other regions, and has become one of the characteristic industries for local rural revitalization in China [42]. According to data from global patent databases, a large number of new food types such as anti-aging and anti-oxidant health products, dietary supplements, and functional cosmetics have been developed using A. cochinchinensis as raw material, which are favored by most developed and developing countries [42]. Steroidal saponins constitute a major class of chemical constituents found in A. cochinchinensis, as well as in plants from families such as Liliaceae and Dioscoreaceae. These compounds are widely reported to possess significant biological activities, including anti-aging, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-tumor properties [43–45]. There are research reports that A. cochinchinensis may mitigate the in vitro and in vivo kidney damage caused by elevated levels of blood glucose by acting as an antioxidant and anti-inflammatory [46]. Here, we conducted anti-aging experiments to investigate the health benefits of ATSE. We proved that ATSE increases C. elegans longevity and healthspan.

ATSE was shown to be an excellent food for increasing C. elegans longevity. We intriguingly found that different sources of ATSE improved nematode lifespan to different extents, and this difference was also present in other phenotypic experiments. As mentioned before, to live longer, certain organisms may give up some bodily functions, reduce their caloric intake, or even their fertility [47]. However, in our study, ATSE extended the nematode lifespan without impairing C. elegans progeny production, feeding ability, or motility. These results suggest that ATSE can improve fitness and increase lifespan without any apparent drawbacks, implying that it has good biosafety for future use.

Aging in C. elegans is triggered by external stimuli like ambient temperature or oxidative damage caused by paraquats [48]. Our research indicates that ATSE may increase lifespan of C. elegans by enhancing its resistance to heat and oxidative stress. This protective effect counters age-dependent declines in stress resilience, which are characterized by accumulated endogenous ROS and diminished antioxidant activity (e.g., reduced SOD activity) that disrupt redox homeostasis and promote aging. Mechanistically, ATSE significantly reduced intracellular ROS levels, enhanced SOD enzymatic activity, and attenuated lipofuscin deposition—a biomarker of oxidative macromolecular damage [49, 50].Collectively, these results indicate that ATSE’s anti-aging effects are mediated through antioxidant pathways, ultimately mitigating oxidative damage and extending longevity. This aligns with reports that Asparagus-derived steroidal saponins reduce ROS and upregulate SOD and other stress-response genes in C. elegans [51].

Resistance to heat and oxidative stress is observed in C. elegans with diminished activity of the IIS pathway; this suggests that an enhanced capacity to avoid or remediate oxidative damage could lengthen lifespan via a DAF-16/FOXO transcription factor-dependent mechanism [52, 53]. Extensive research has been devoted to the essential antioxidant-related genes controlled by the IIS pathway, which includes hsp-16.2, sod-3, ctl-2, daf-2, and daf-16, since these genes perform important functions in oxidative stress regulation and lifespan [21]. DAF-16, the C. elegans orthologue of mammalian FOXO, is the central transcription factor that mediates stress resistance and longevity [33]. It directly regulates downstream targets such as ctl-2 (catalase), sod-3 (superoxide dismutase), and hsp-16.2 (heat-shock protein) [54, 55].

In our experiments, ATSE lowered daf-2 mRNA levels and simultaneously elevated the transcripts of skn-1, daf-16, and hsf-1, as well as the downstream genes hsp-16.2, ctl-1, gst-4 and sod-3. Lifespan assays showed that the daf-16(mu86) null mutation completely abolished ATSE-mediated longevity, and ATSE further enhanced DAF-16 nuclear accumulation and the expression of its canonical targets gst-4 and sod-3. Collectively, these data establish DAF-16 as a necessary mediator of ATSE’s pro-longevity effect. Critically, DAF-16 is the C. elegans orthologue of mammalian FOXO transcription factors (FOXO1/3/4/6)—evolutionarily conserved regulators of stress resistance and longevity [56]. This homology underscores the potential translational relevance of our findings to human aging pathways, while simultaneously highlighting inherent limitations: although core signaling logic is preserved, interspecies differences in tissue complexity, metabolic regulation, and immune function necessitate caution in direct extrapolation.

Nevertheless, lifespan in C. elegans is controlled by a network of transcription factors and signalling modules. Apart from DAF-16, additional factors play an integral part i. SKN-1, for example, functions downstream of both IIS and the p38/PMK-1 MAPK pathway to promote stress resistance and longevity [57]. Consistent with this, we observed that ATSE increases skn-1 mRNA and up-regulates its target gst-4, implying that SKN-1 may also contribute to ATSE’s anti-aging action. While our genetic epistasis experiment with daf-16 identifies the IIS–FOXO axis as one required route, it does not exclude involvement of additional pathways. A current limitation is the absence of systematic testing in mutants of the p38/PMK-1, SKN-1/Nrf, and AMPK/AAK-2 pathways. Experiments comparing the lifespan effects of ATSE in skn-1(zu67), pmk-1(km25), and aak-2(ok524) mutants will be reported independently to clarify the full spectrum of ATSE’s molecular targets.

In experiments with C. elegans, we found that there were differences in the extent of life of ATSEs of different origins. Moreover, the ATSEs of Sichuan and Guangxi origin (T1, T2, and T3 groups) were more effective at extending the C. elegans’ lifespan than were the ATSEs of Yunnan origin (T4 and T5 groups). Therefore, 16 batches of samples were selected from ATSEs (T1, T2, and T3 groups) from Sichuan and Guangxi for spectral effect analysis. The fingerprints of 16 batches of ATSEs were established by HPLC-ELSD, and 16 common peaks were found in ATSEs. We measured the average C. elegans’ lifespan cultured with 16 batches of ATSEs after 10 mM paraquat treatment. We found that the compound content varies considerably from one production area to another, and differences in the active ingredients will result in different extents of life for C. elegans. Spectrum-effect relationship analyses of diverse batches of Dandelion reveal that extracts from different geographical origins exhibit variations in biological activity [58]. This observation underscores the significant impact of the source habitat on the chemical composition and associated biological activities of these medicinal materials.

The spectrum-effect relationship seeks to link the chemical composition of a TCM fingerprint with its pharmacodynamic efficacy using statistical methods, offering novel insights into the chemical basis of TCM [59]. However, current approaches, such as liquid chromatography‒mass spectrometry (LC‒MS), face challenges due to the lack of reference standards for many TCM extracts and high analysis costs, limiting their widespread application [60, 61]. Using spectrum-effect analysis, we identified X5, X7, X8, and X9 as bioactive components involved in ATSE’s anti-aging activity. We successfully isolated and identified X5 as (25S)-officinalisnin-II, whose pharmacological activity was verified by its ability to prolong C. elegans’ lifespan. This finding not only validates our spectrum-effect analysis but also highlights (25 S)-officinalisnin-II as a key active compound in ATSE’s anti-aging effects [62]. Though (25 S)-officinalisnin-II has been identified in A. cochinchinensis and other Asparagus species [63, 64], and Wang et al. have determined its content and proposed it as a quality control index for A. cochinchinensis [65], this proposal is scientifically unfounded owing to a lack of prior studies on its biological activity. Our study provides the first evidence of its potential anti-aging activity, laying the foundation for quality control and evaluation of ARs and offering new insights for the development of anti-aging drugs from TCM.

While the correlation-based spectrum-effect approach successfully identified candidate bioactive peaks (X5, X7, X8, X9) and isolated (25 S)-officinalisnin-II as a key anti-aging compound (Fig. 8), this methodology carries inherent constraints: It establishes associations but cannot confirm causality between individual constituents and observed effects, fails to resolve potential synergistic interactions among ATSE components, and relies exclusively on paraquat-induced oxidative stress as a single endpoint—which may not fully represent holistic anti-aging mechanisms. Systematic validation through compound fractionation and combinatorial bioassays remains essential to establish causal relationships and constituent interplay.

Our findings are derived exclusively from C. elegans studies. Although this model offers significant advantages for aging research (short lifespan, genetic tractability, conserved pathways), fundamental differences in metabolism, immune function, and tissue complexity between nematodes and humans limit direct extrapolation. Future validation in mammalian systems is essential to assess translational potential.

The materials used in the study

Methanol and ethanol were purchased from Jin Shan Chemical Test Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). Fisher Scientific (Pittsburgh, PA, USA) supplied Methanol and acetonitrile (HPLC grade). Millipore water purification apparatus (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA) was utilised to prepare ultrapure distilled water.

Chromatographic fingerprinting and quantitative analysis were conducted using a Waters Acquity UPLC system (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) fitted with a single quadrupole mass spectrometer, binary solvent manager, column manager, and sample manager. BioTek (Winooski, VT, USA) provided the enzyme standard instrument.

C. elegans strains and maintenance

Luo Huairong (College of Pharmacy, Southwest Medical University, Luzhou, China) supplied all the strains. The following strains were utilised in this investigation: N2 C. elegans wild type (WT), CL2166 dvIs19 [(pAF15) gst-4p::GFP::NLS] III, CF1038 [daf-16(mu86)I.], CF1553 muIs84 [(pAD76) sod-3p::GFP + rol-6(su1006)], and TJ356 zIs356 [daf-16p::daf-16a/b::GFP + rol-6(su1006)]. NGM plates that were inoculated with Escherichia coli OP50 were utilised to culture and maintain each strain [66].

Preparation of the ATSE sample

Dried powder (10 g) of A. cochinchinensis was dissolved in 200 mL of 60% methanol/water, heated, and extracted continuously at reflux twice, and the crude extract of total A. cochinchinensis saponin was obtained by collecting the filtrate and concentrating it under decreased pressure. The crude extract of A. cochinchinensis was enriched and purified using HPD-300 macroporous resin, and the purified sample of A. cochinchinensis was obtained as an ATSE.

Pharmacodynamic study on the senescence retardation of C. elegans caused by ATSE in different producing areas

C. elegans maintenance and culture

Bacterial strain and culture [67]: The uracil-auxotrophic Escherichia coli OP50 strain was used as a food source. Single colonies were isolated from solid bacterial culture media and inoculated into sterile LB liquid medium. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking (160 rpm) for 16 h. Bacterial suspensions exhibiting homogeneous turbidity with OD₆₀₀ ≥ 0.5 (measured by microplate reader) were used for subsequent experiments.

NGM preparation [67]: Nematode Growth Medium (NGM) was prepared by dissolving NaCl (1.2 g), agar (7 g), and peptone (1 g) in 400 mL distilled water. The solution was autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min. After cooling to approximately 55 °C, the following sterile solutions were added with thorough mixing: 400 µL 1 M CaCl₂, 400 µL 1 M MgSO₄,10 mL 1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 6.0), 400 µL 5 mg/mL cholesterol solution in 100% ethanol.

Nematode synchronization and routine culture [67]: Gravid adult nematodes were transferred to a centrifuge tube and treated with 2 mL alkaline hypochlorite lysis solution (1:1 mixture of 5 M NaOH and ≥ 7.5 M NaClO) to release eggs. Eggs were pelleted by centrifugation, washed with M9 buffer, and incubated in M9 buffer at 20 °C for 20 h to obtain synchronized L1 larvae. L1 larvae were transferred to NGM plates seeded with 50 µL OP50 bacterial suspension and maintained at 20 °C for subsequent culturing.

Screening of the optimal concentration for nematode experiments

Synchronized L4 stage C. elegans were introduced to NGM plates containing ATSE from T1 origin A. cochinchinensis samples (incorporated into the agar at geometrically increasing concentrations of 100, 200, 400, 800, plus 1000 µg/mL specifically for toxicity screening) and 50 µM 5-fluoro-2′-deoxyuridine (FUdR). E. coli OP50 was seeded onto these plates to form the bacterial lawn. Any plate with observed eggs was replaced on day 0. Observations were made every 24 h till the death of every C. elegans. C. elegans were deemed to be deceased in the absence of any remarkable motion upon gentle contact, and the analysis precluded specimens that perished prematurely due to internal hatching or vulval rupture.

Lifespan assay

The C. elegans culture procedure was the same as that used in the ordinary lifespan assay[A7]. C. elegans synchronized to the L4 stage were selected and provided sufficient E. coli OP50 as food on plain NGM plates supplemented with 400 µg/mL ATSE from different sources (the T1, T2, T3, T4, and T5 groups). The experimental methods used for C. elegans and the criteria for determining whether the C. elegans were dead were the same as those described in section “4.4.2. “.

Brood size assay and pharyngeal pumping assay

Egg count assays were also conducted on adult C. elegans treated with 400 µg/mL ATSE from various sources beginning at the L1 stage [68]. These C. elegans strains, in triplicate, were inoculated on NGM plates supplemented with E. coli OP50, and allowed to produce eggs for seven days at 20 °C, with transfers occurring every other day. After hatching, the eggs were counted. The test was repeated in three separate experiments.

Pharyngeal pumping experiments [68] involved observing C. elegans treated with or without ATSE for 3 days (n >20) on food-deprived NGM plates. Observations were performed using a Motic SMZ-161 stereo microscope at 40x magnification. The pharyngeal twitch rate, defined as a single contraction of the posterior bulb/grinder observed for 20 s, was calculated. A minimum of 30 C. elegans were included in each of the three independent repetitions of the experiment.

Oxidative stress resistance assay

An approach that has been reported previously was used, with some adjustments, to investigate the capacity of C. elegans to resist oxidative stress [34]. The H2O2-induced acute oxidative stress assay was conducted at 20 °C, and hydrogen peroxide (10 mM) was added to the NGM [29], similar to the lifespan assay. Synchronized L4 larvae were incubated at 20 °C under 400 µg/mL ATSE and control conditions for 72 h. After transferring the C. elegans to NGM plates supplemented with 10 mM H2O2, nematode survival was recorded hourly until all the C. elegans perished.

Paraquat-induced chronic oxidative stress assays were conducted at 20 °C by adding 10 mM paraquat to NGM [26]. Synchronized L4 larvae were incubated at 20 °C under 400 µg/mL ATSE and control conditions for 72 h. Subsequently, the C. elegans were introduced into NGM plates which contained 10 mM paraquat at 20 °C, and survival was recorded every 2 days until all the C. elegans perished. Dead C. elegans were counted using the same method used for the lifespan assay, and nematode mortality was determined similarly.

Heat compression experiments

For the heat pressure test, L4-stage C. elegans were introduced onto NGM plates with or without 400 µg/mL ATSE and subsequently replenished with fresh NGM media every 2 days. After 72 h, the C. elegans were subjected to a continuous incubation at 37 °C for the duration of the survival assay, until all nematodes were dead (approximately 11 h). Throughout this incubation period, the nematode death count was documented every hour [38]. Any C. elegans that was unresponsive to a light touch from the platinum cone was considered to be dead and documented as such.

Lipofuscin accumulation assay

For the intracellular lipofuscin accumulation experiment, age-synchronized L4 C. elegans were cultured with 5-fluorouracil (5 mg/mL) for 10 days, with or without 400 µg/mL ATSE. Ten-day-old wild-type C. elegans were observed for lipofuscin autofluorescence on microscope slides with 2% agar pads following levamisole anaesthesia (5 mM, 3 min) using a Nikon TS2R-LS fluorescence microscope [29].

Estimation of SOD activity and ROS levels

SOD activity was evaluated by incubating approximately 1,000 wild-type C. elegans on NGM plates containing 50 µM FUdR and either containing or not containing 400 µg/mL of ATSE for six days [38]. Thereafter, the C. elegans were collected on the sixth day, washed, and homogenized in M9 buffer on ice. A SOD assay kit (WST-1 method) was utilised to estimate the SOD activity as directed by the manufacturer with an enzyme marker (Multiskan GO 1510; Thermo Fisher Science, MA, USA).

The 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (H2DCF-DA) was employed to measure endogenous ROS levels [69]. C. elegans were collected on day 7 after the same lifespan assay, treated with 400 µg/mL ATSE (NGM plates also contain 50 µM FUdR, and subjected to incubation for half an hour with 10 mM H2DCF-DA at 37 °C. Following incubation, the C. elegans were placed on 2% agar slides, and the intensity of fluorescence was quantified at excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 and 535 nm, respectively.

Relative expression of mRNAs

Gene expression analysis by quantitative PCR followed the SOD activity assay protocol. Approximately 1000 synchronized C. elegans inoculated at 20 °C on NGM in the presence or absence of 400 µg/mL ATSE were selected. After 5 days, TRIzol A+ (Tiangen, China) was utilised to extract total RNA followed by reverse-transcription into cDNA. Gene quantification was performed in triplicate using an ABI 7500 DNA analyser with cdc-42 serving as the internal reference gene. Supplementary Table S6 presents a list of the primer sequences that were utilised.

CF1038 strain lifespan assay

CF1038 C. elegans were subjected to various ATSE groups for 48 h on NGM plates. To distinguish between adults and progeny during the reproductive phase, C. elegans was daily transferred to new plates and examined using the same method as for the lifespan assay.

sod-3 and gst-4 expression

ATSE (400 µg/mL) was used to treat age-synchronized CF1553 C. elegans expressing the sod-3::GFP fusion protein and CL2166 C. elegans expressing the gst-4::GFP fusion protein under normal conditions at 20 °C. The treatment spanned 6 days, during which C. elegans were placed on NGM containing 50 µM FUdR and either with or without 400 µg/mL ATSE [A20]. The C. elegans were anaesthetised with levamisole (5 mM, 3 min), the anaesthetised C. elegans were picked onto microscope slides coated with 2% agar, and the fluorescence intensity was measured with a Nikon fluorescence microscope (Nikon TS2R-LS, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). There were three repetitions of the experiment.

DAF-16 nuclear localization assays

For six days, TJ356 C. elegans that were synchronized with age were subjected to 400 µg/mL ATSE at 20 °C. Then, levamisole (5 mM for 3 min) was used to anaesthetise the C. elegans on agar-coated glass slides. DAF-16 localization was determined using fluorescence microscopy (Nikon TS2R-LS, Tokyo, Japan) at 100x magnification [29]. Based on the location of daf-16P::GFP (cell membrane, intermediate layer, nucleus), each C. elegans was classified. ImageJ software was used for intracellular daf-16P::GFP analysis.

Establishment of the HPLC-ELSD fingerprint

Preparation of ATSE samples

Sixteen batches of ATSE were prepared following the procedure outlined in section “4.3. “, 1 mg/ml methanol was used, and a 0.22 μm filter membrane was used to filter the ATSE to obtain the sample to be tested.

Establishment of the HPLC-ELSD fingerprint and similarity assessment

The analysis utilised an Agilent 1260 Series System with an Ecosil Eco-C18 (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) column and an evaporative light scattering detector at 30 °C. The following gradient elution program with acetonitrile (A) and water (B) was used: 0–55 min (A: 15%–38%), 55–56 min (A: 38%–15%), and 56–60 min (A: 15%). At 0.8 ml/min flow rate, 20 µL of the sample was injected automatically. A flow rate of 1 ml/min of nitrogen was used, with the evaporation temperature set as 95 °C.

ATSE HPLC maps from each origin were generated with the Fingerprint Similarity Evaluation System for TCM (version 2012 A) to construct fingerprint profiles. Sixteen GH sample batches were subjected to similarity evaluation based on TCM chromatographic fingerprints (2012 edition), followed by a similarity test.

Method validation for fingerprint analysis

Under optimized HPLC conditions, six consecutive injections of the identical sample solution were utilised for the precision analysis. Six sample solutions were consistently prepared to evaluate the repeatability. Stability was determined by analysing the identical sample solution at various times of the day (0, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h).

Measurement of the ability of ATSE to retard nematode senescence

The oxidative stress assays induced by paraquat involved incubating C. elegans with 400 µg/mL ATSE in 16 batches for 48 h. After incubation, the C. elegans were loaded onto fresh NGM plates supplemented with paraquat (10 mM) and 16 batches of ATSE. Survival was assessed every 2 days based on touch-provoked movement, considering nonresponsive C. elegans as deceased.

Spectrum–Effect relationship analysis

Grey relational analysis

The similarity of geometric curves was evaluated in grey relational analyses to ascertain the link that exists between the samples and the objects that are being tested. This method is optimal for analysing TCM fingerprint data. Using the mean lifespan as the parent sequence Yk, we averaged the mean lifespan of 16 batches of ATSE and C. elegans in this experiment. The areas corresponding to the common peaks were subsequently ascribed to Xi, after which the grey correlation coefficients between the parent sequence and the subsequent sequence were calculated [Ai(k), Ai(k)=(Δikmin + ρ × Δikmax)/(Δikmax + ρ × Δ ikmax)], where Δik=|Yk-Xi|, the resolution factor ρ is 0.5, and Ai(k) is averaged as the correlation degree [35].

Pearson correlation analysis

The linearity between variables was evaluated by computing Pearson correlation coefficient (r) values using SPSS. The correlation coefficient was determined by comparing the shared peak area (X) and the mean lifespan of the C. elegans (Y). The Pearson correlation coefficients for each group were also plotted in a heatmap (https://www.chiplot.online//).

PLSR analysis

PLSR was used to analyse the link between the shared peaks in ATSE and nematode lifespan. The independent variables were the peak areas on the chromatogram (X), while the dependent variable was the decrease in the lifespan of nematodes (Y). The PLSR model was developed utilizing SIMCA 14.1 software, wherein regression coefficients were employed to represent relative impacts. There was a significant correlation between the VIP values and the activities of the ATSE extract on the chromatographic peaks [70].

Extraction and isolation of the ATSE monomer and experimental validation

Separation of ATSE extraction and isolation

AR (20 kg) was extracted twice with 80% ethanol (80 L each time). After recovering the ethanol, the extract was dispersed in water and placed on an HPD-100 resin column (200 mm*1000 mm). First, 100 L of purified water was used to wash the column and subsequently eluted with 100 L of 30% ethanol, 100 L of 70% ethanol, and 90% ethanol. The solvent was recovered to obtain the corresponding eluate. A total of 480 g of the 70% ethanol-eluted fraction was placed on a silica gel column with gradient elution of a dichloromethane/methanol solvent mixture to give 7 fractions. Fraction 4 (30 g) was purified by preparative liquid chromatography (C18 column, 100 mm×250 mm) to give compound (25S)-officinalisnin-II (2.1 g).

Experimental validation of monomeric components in chronic oxidative stress model C. elegans

Oxidative stress assays involved exposing C. elegans to different monomeric components for 48 h, followed by transfer to paraquat-containing plates. C. elegans survival was monitored every 2 days based on the touch-induced movement response. Log-rank tests were used for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

A minimum of three repetitions of each test were conducted, and the results included the mean lifespan with standard deviation (SD) determined through the log-rank test for the mean lifespan and corresponding P values. The maximum lifespan represents the average of the 10% longest lifespans with SD. The number of eggs is the average with SD. Other data are presented as the means with SDs from multiple experiments. Statistical analysis and plotting were carried out using Origin8, SPSS 22.0, and GraphPad Prism 8.1.2 software. P < 0.05 and P < 0.01 were considered to show statistical significance based on the hypothesis test.

Conclusion

In the canonical aging model C. elegans, total saponin extract of ATSE markedly extends lifespan while concurrently enhancing resistance to thermal and oxidative stress; these effects are fully dependent on the DAF-16/FOXO signaling axis. By integrating GRAY–PCA–PLSR multidimensional spectrum–effect analyses of sixteen regional batches, we identified peaks X5, X7, X8, and X9 as the principal anti-aging constituents and unambiguously defined X5 as (25 S)-officinalisnin-II. This single steroidal saponin alone prolongs survival by ~ 14% under chronic oxidative stress, mirroring the full extract. Collectively, our findings illuminate the molecular underpinnings underlying the traditional claim of “prolonging life” for AR and establish a component-targeted framework for quality standardization and translational development of this botanical as a prospective anti-aging therapeutic.

Follow-up studies will employ skn-1, pmk-1, and aak-2 mutants to dissect the broader regulatory network engaged by ATSE, and will advance to mammalian models to assess the metabolic stability, dose–response profile, and safety of (25 S)-officinalisnin-II, thereby paving the way for early-phase clinical interventions aimed at healthy aging in humans.
