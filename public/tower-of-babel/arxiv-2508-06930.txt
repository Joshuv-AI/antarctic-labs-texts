Efficient GW calculations for metals from an accurate ab initio polarizability
Giacomo Sesti, Alberto Guandalini, Andrea Ferretti, Pino D’Amico, Claudia Cardoso, Massimo Rontani, Daniele Varsano

Abstract
Despite its success in the study of spectroscopic properties, the G ​ W GW method presents specific methodological challenges when applied to systems with metallic screening. Here, we present an efficient and fully ab-initio implementation for the calculation of the screened potential, specifically designed for 3D and 2D metals. It combines a Monte Carlo integration with an appropriate interpolation of the screened potential between the calculated grid points (W-av), complemented with an extrapolation to the long-wavelength limit, able to seamlessly account for the so-called intraband term. This method greatly accelerates the convergence of G ​ W GW calculations for metals while improving their accuracy, due to the correct description of the intraband transitions in the long wavelength limit, as shown here for 3D metals and doped monolayers, such as MoS 2 and graphene. The use of W-av results in an excellent agreement with ARPES measurements for monolayer doped MoS 2 . Furthermore, for graphene we show that more robust results are found with the use of higher-order Lorentzians in the description of the self-energy, together with the solution of the QP equation beyond the linearized approximation.



I
Introduction

Many-body perturbation theory provides accurate, first principles methods for the study of the spectroscopic properties of condensed matter systems
Onida et al. 2002
;
Martin et al. 2016
;
Marzari et al. 2021
.
For instance, the imaginary part of the one-particle Green’s function
G
is closely related to the spectral function obtained from photoemission experiments.
Calculations often adopt the so-called
GW
approximation
Hedin 1965
;
Strinati et al. 1982
;
Aryasetiawan and Gunnarsson 1998
;
Martin et al. 2016
;
Reining 2018
;
Golze et al. 2019
, in which quasi-particle energies are determined through an exchange-correlation self-energy
\Sigma
, given by the convolution of
G
and the screened Coulomb potential
W
.
One the one hand, mean field methods, such as Kohn-Sham (KS) density functional theory (DFT), have shown to be able to qualitative describe the band structures of bulk metals. However, the excited-state spectra measured by angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) experiments, in particular the occupied bandwidths, are often substantially narrower than those predicted by KS-DFT
Lyo and Plummer 1988
. In fact, the GW approximation has been applied to a large variety of metallic systems, ranging from bulk transition metals
Aryasetiawan 1992
;
Marini et al. 2001a
;
Nohara et al. 2009
;
Friedrich et al. 2010
;
Młyńczak et al. 2016
;
Okumura et al. 2019
;
Leon et al. 2023
;
Kandemir et al. 2024
, rare earths
Sakuma et al. 2012
;
Ahmed et al. 2014
,
over halfmetals
Kotani 2009
;
Meinert et al. 2012
, to topological semimetals
Aguilera et al. 2015
. The comparison of several electronic-structure methods in the description of simple
sp
metals, among which the one-shot
GW
approximation
Mandal et al. 2022
, shows that
G_{0}W_{0}
partially improves the calculated bandwidths with respect to experiment values, but a consistent agreement with photoemission data requires self-consistent GW calculations
Friedrich et al. 2022a
.

Moreover, one of the most successful outcomes of the
GW
approximation is the improvement of the band gap of semiconductors with respect to, for example, the results of Kohn-Sham density functional theory
Kohn and Sham 1965
;
Marzari et al. 2021
;
Reining 2018
;
Golze et al. 2019
;
Louie et al. 2021
;
van Schilfgaarde et al. 2006
;
Blase et al. 2011
. In bulk semiconductors, this band gap is hardly affected by extrinsic factors. However, at lower dimensionalities, the less effective electronic screening dramatically enhances many-electron interactions, and extrinsic factors such as doping can induce a surprisingly large band gap renormalization, as seen for one-dimensional (1D) and two-dimensional (2D) systems
Spataru et al. 2004
;
Yang et al. 2007
;
Spataru and Léonard 2010
;
Spataru and Léonard 2013
;
D’Amico et al. 2020
.
While the band gap renormalization is in principle well reproduced by the
GW
approximation, the strong coupling between the doping-induced carrier plasmons and the quasiparticle excitations requires special care in the calculation of the many-electron screening that determines the dielectric function
Liang and Yang 2015
;
Gao and Yang 2017
;
Champagne et al. 2023
.
2D doped semiconductors may fall into a broader category because of the methodological challenges associated with metallic screening in today’s
GW
implementations
Wooten 1972
;
Marini et al. 2001b
;
Cazzaniga 2012
;
Leon et al. 2023
.
For partially filled bands, there is a non-vanishing probability that an electron is excited within the same band. This leads to intraband poles in the irreducible polarizability, whose energy vanishes for
\mathbf{q}\rightarrow 0
, in bulk metals, while the Coulomb potential diverges on the same scale. A direct evaluation of their product, corresponding to the screening, results in an integral on the Fermi surface that involves all metallic bands
Marini et al. 2001a
;
Kolwas and Derkachova 2020
.
The calculation is challenging since the discretization of the Brillouin zone (BZ) can lead to a ill-defined Fermi surface, due to the limited number of grid points available in its vicinity.
Typically, the contributions from the Fermi surface are neglected, and their impact in the screening is minimized by increasing the
\mathbf{k}
-point sampling of the BZ. However, this approach leads to extremely slow convergence
Methfessel and Paxton 1989
, with, for example, spurious band gaps that vanish very slowly with increasing number of
\mathbf{k}
-points
Cazzaniga et al. 2008
.
Indeed, approaches based on explicit Fermi-surface integration
Maksimov et al. 1988
;
Lee and Chang 1994
;
Marini et al. 2001b
are computationally expensive.

Analytical models using a Taylor expansion of the dielectric function for small-
\mathbf{q}
, require fewer
\mathbf{k}
-points
Cazzaniga et al. 2008
;
Cazzaniga et al. 2010
;
Orhan and O’Regan 2019
, but often depend on external parameters.
This is the case for Drude based approaches
Lee and Chang 1994
, where the missing intraband contribution is accounted for by adding a phenomenological term to the head of the irreducible dielectric matrix in the
\mathbf{q}\to 0
limit. The Drude term depends on a plasma frequency
\omega_{D}
and a damping factor
\gamma
, parameters that can be determined either experimentally or by expensive
ab-initio
methods
Lee and Chang 1994
;
Marini et al. 2001a
;
Kohn 1974
;
Sporkmann and Bross 1994
;
Prandini et al. 2019a
;
Prandini et al. 2019b
;
Methfessel and Paxton 1989
;
Blöchl et al. 1994
;
Lee and Chang 1994
;
Friedrich et al. 2022b
.
Recently, our group tackled the treatment of intraband transitions in
GW
calculations for bulk metals, with the so-called constant approximation (CA)
Leon et al. 2023
. This method approximates the dielectric function at
\mathbf{q}=0
by its value determined at a small but finite
\mathbf{q}
, relying on its slow quadratic dispersion in bulk metals.

In contrast, for 2D systems, the dielectric function in the long-wavelength limit exhibits large variations with
\mathbf{q}
. For this reason, an accurate integration over the BZ requires very dense
\mathbf{k}
-grids
Qiu et al. 2016
;
Hüser et al. 2013
.
Several schemes have been proposed to mitigate this problem
Rasmussen et al. 2016
;
da Jornada et al. 2017
;
Xia et al. 2020
.
In a previous article
Guandalini et al. 2023a
, our group showed that, for semiconductors, the W-av method drastically accelerates convergence with respect to the BZ sampling. This scheme combines a Monte Carlo integration with an appropriate interpolation of the screened potential between the calculated grid points. In this work, we use a similar strategy to address the inclusion of intraband contributions in the response function, thereby enabling accurate
GW
calculations for 3D and 2D metals.

Another challenge when studying metals at the
GW
level is to describe the frequency dependence of the screened potential
W
.
This is often modeled using the plasmon pole approximation (PPA)
Hybertsen and Louie 1986
;
Zhang et al. 1989
;
Godby and Needs 1989
;
von der Linden and Horsch 1988
;
Engel and Farid 1993
;
Larson et al. 2013
, which is based on the homogeneous electron gas, and for which it is exact in the long-wavelength limit
Fetter and Walecka 1971
;
Hedin 1965
;
Giuliani and Vignale 2005
. The PPA has been successfully applied to the calculation of quasi-particle energies in semiconductors
Hybertsen and Louie 1986
, the homogeneous electron gas
Hedin et al. 1967

and simple metals such as Al and Na
Northrup et al. 1987
;
Surh et al. 1988
;
Northrup et al. 1989
;
Cazzaniga 2012
.
However, its applicability to transition and noble metals has been questioned
Aryasetiawan and Gunnarsson 1998
;
Marini et al. 2001a
, mainly due to the presence of strongly localized
d
-bands. In fact, these metals exhibit complex screening effects due to collective excitations
Nilsson and Larsson 1983
;
Palik 1985
, resulting in highly structured energy-loss spectra that cannot be described with a single plasmon peak
Palik 1985
. The PPA has also proved insufficient to describe the strong coupling between doping-induced carrier plasmons and quasiparticle excitations in 2D doped semiconductors
Liang and Yang 2015
;
Gao and Yang 2017
;
Champagne et al. 2023
.
In the present work, we use the multipole approach (MPA), effectively reducing computational complexity while maintaining the precision of full-frequency methods, as demonstrated in recent works
Leon et al. 2021
;
Leon et al. 2023
.
The aforementioned W-av scheme can be seamlessly combined with MPA
Guandalini et al. 2024
allowing one to compute QP band structures with coarse frequency and BZ samplings. Both methods have been implemented in the
yambo

code
Marini et al. 2009
;
Sangalli et al. 2019
.

In this work, we address the problems described above in the calculation of
GW
quasiparticle (QP) corrections for metals. We present a unified implementation, based on an extension of the W-av method, which accurately includes the intraband contributions to the response function, for both 2D and 3D metals. Here we demonstrate W-av for
G_{0}W_{0}
calculations but the method could be extended straightforwardly to, for example, self-consistent
GW
calculations. This approach eliminates the need for dense
\mathbf{k}
-grids, and remains fully compatible with the use of MPA for the frequency description of the screened potential. We show that our method accurately captures the doping-dependent band gap renormalization in monolayer MoS
2
and shows excellent agreement with ARPES data.

The paper is organized as follows. in the following subsection of the Introduction we summarize the main steps of the
GW
approximation and describe the W-av approach, as previously developed for semiconductors. Next, in
Section

II.1
, we first present the newly extended W-av method, now compliant with the description of metallic screening. We then validate the methodology on selected
GW
calculations: first we discuss two bulk metals, Na and Mg, and then two doped monolayers, MoS
2
and graphene, including results concerning band gap renormalization in 2D (see Sec.
II.5
).
Finally, we draw the main conclusions in Sec.
III
and illustrate the methodology used in the work, summarizing the computational parameters of the calculations Sec.
IV
.

I.1
The
GW
approximation and the W-av framework

Using Green’s function methods from many-body perturbation theory, the quasi-particle energies
E_{n\mathbf{k}}

can be calculated perturbatively from the single-particle energies
\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}
via the addition of the diagonal elements of the self-energy
\Sigma
evaluated on the Bloch states
|n\mathbf{k}\rangle
:

E_{n\mathbf{k}}=\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}+\langle n\mathbf{k}|\Sigma(E_{n\mathbf{k}})-\Sigma(\mu)|n\mathbf{k}\rangle,

(1)

where
\Sigma(\mu)
is the value of the self-energy at the chemical potential
\mu
, and it is useful for treating metals
Hedin 1999
. In fact,
\Sigma(\mu)
serves to align
\mu
at quasi-particle level with that of the single-particle level and it becomes trivially zero if calculated using a particle-number conserving theory. Notably,
Eq.

1
is a non-linear equation with respect to
E_{n\mathbf{k}}
, and is often approximated by its linear expansion:

E_{n\mathbf{k}}=\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}+Z_{n\mathbf{k}}\langle n\mathbf{k}|\Sigma(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}})-\Sigma(\mu)|n\mathbf{k}\rangle,

(2)

where
Z_{n\mathbf{k}}
is the renormalization factor:

Z_{n\mathbf{k}}=\left(1-\left.\frac{d\Sigma(\omega)}{d\omega}\right|_{\omega=\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}}\right)^{-1}.

(3)

The linear expansion is usually considered valid if
\Sigma
can be approximated with a linear behaviour in the surrounding of
E_{nk}
, with values of
Z_{n\mathbf{k}}
typically larger than, e.g., 0.5-0.6.
Alternatively, QP energies can be identified from the position of the peaks in the spectral function
A(\omega)
, defined as the imaginary part of
G
(expressed here neglecting the off-diagonal matrix elements of the self-energy):

A_{n\mathbf{k}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\pi}\left|\text{Im}\frac{1}{\omega-\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}-\Sigma_{n\mathbf{k}}(\omega)+\Sigma(\mu)}\right|

(4)

The most common method to calculate the self-energy is the non-iterative G
0
W
0
approximation. Under this approximation, the diagonal matrix elements of
\Sigma
can be written taking advantage of the Lehmann representation for the non-interacting Green function
G_{0}
in the Bloch plane-wave basis set, through a double integral in frequency
\omega^{\prime}
and momentum
\mathbf{q}
:

\Sigma_{n\mathbf{k}}(\omega)=-\sum\limits_{m}\sum\limits_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\int\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi i}e^{i\omega^{\prime}\eta}\int\frac{d\mathbf{q}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\\
\quad\frac{\rho_{nm}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{G})W_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega^{\prime})\rho^{*}_{nm}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{G}^{\prime})}{\omega+\omega^{\prime}-\varepsilon_{m\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}+i\eta\ \textrm{sgn}(\varepsilon_{m\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}-\mu)}

(5)

where
\rho_{nm}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{G})=\langle{n\mathbf{k}}|e^{i(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})\cdot\mathbf{r}}|m\mathbf{k-q}\rangle
are the generalized oscillator strengths, and
W_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
is the screened Coulomb potential. The index
m
runs over all bands, while
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{G^{\prime}}
are plane-waves used to represent the spatial degrees of freedom, and time order is ensured by the implicit limit
\eta\to 0^{+}
. Here, unless explicitly indicated, we use
\eta=0.1\penalty\ eV
.

The self-energy in
Eq.

5
is commonly written as the sum of a static and a dynamic part,
\Sigma_{n\mathbf{k}}(\omega)\equiv\Sigma^{x}_{n\mathbf{k}}+\Sigma^{c}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\omega)
corresponding to the exchange and correlation terms of
W=V+W^{c}
, respectively. The first term,
\Sigma^{x}_{n\mathbf{k}}
, depends only on the bare Coulomb potential
V
and it is static.
All the frequency dependence is therefore included in the correlation term,
\Sigma^{c}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\omega)
.

\Sigma^{c}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\omega)=-\sum\limits_{m}\sum\limits_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\int\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi i}e^{i\omega^{\prime}\eta}\int\frac{d\mathbf{q}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\\
\quad g_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}^{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q},\omega,\omega^{\prime})\,W^{c}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega^{\prime}).

(6)

Notably, in
Eq.

6
, all the dependencies on the plane waves and frequency originating from
G_{0}
are gathered in the factors
g^{nk}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega,\omega^{\prime})
.
The frequency dependence of
W^{c}
is determined by the density-density response function
\chi
, since:

\displaystyle W^{c}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=V_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q})\,\chi_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\,V_{\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q}).

(7)

The density-density response is computed from the irreducible response function
\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
in the random phase approximation (RPA) as:

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\left[\mathbb{1}-\chi^{0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)V(\mathbf{q})\right]_{\mathbf{G^{\prime\prime}}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}^{-1},

(8)

where we implicitly take the matrix product on
\mathbf{G^{\prime\prime}}
. In its turn, the irreducible polarizability is calculated according to Adler and Wiser
Adler 1962
;
Wiser 1963
:

\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=2\sum_{n,m}\int\frac{d\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\left[f(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}})-f(\varepsilon_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}})\right]\times\quad\\
\frac{\rho_{nm}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{G})\,\rho^{*}_{nm}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{G^{\prime}})}{\omega+\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}+i\eta\ \textrm{sign}(f(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}})-f(\varepsilon_{m\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}))},

(9)

where
f(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}})
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution, the indices
n
and
m
run on all valence and conduction bands.

Various methods exist for describing the frequency dependence of the response function
\chi
, each offering a different balance between accuracy and computational cost. In this work, we adopt the multipole approximation (MPA)
Leon et al. 2021
;
Leon et al. 2023
;
Guandalini et al. 2024
, which has proven adequate for metals, yielding results comparable to full-frequency (FF) approaches while being significantly more computationally efficient.

I.2
The W-av method

The W-av method
Guandalini et al. 2023a
is designed to improve the convergence of the correlation part of the self-energy within G
0
W
0
with respect to the
\mathbf{k}
-point sampling. It takes advantage of the fact that momentum
\mathbf{q}
and frequency
\omega
integrals of
\Sigma^{c}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\omega)
in
Eq.

6
can be solved separately. The integral in momentum is performed under the Monkhorst-Pack scheme, i.e. evaluating a finite sum after the discretization of the momentum transfer
\mathbf{q}
on a uniform grid in the BZ.

\int\frac{d\mathbf{q}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,g_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}^{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q},\omega,\omega^{\prime})\,W^{c}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega^{\prime})=\\
\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}g_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}^{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{q},\omega,\omega^{\prime})\,\overline{W}^{c}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega^{\prime})\ ,

(10)

where
\Omega
is the lattice volume. In the sum, we define

\overline{W}^{c}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\frac{1}{D_{\mathbf{q}}}\int\limits_{D_{\mathbf{q}}}\!\!\!d\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\ W^{c}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\omega),\\
\quad=\frac{1}{D_{\mathbf{q}}}\int\limits_{D_{\mathbf{q}}}\!\!\!d\mathbf{q}^{\prime}V_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q^{\prime}})\chi_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\omega)V_{\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q^{\prime}}),

(11)

as the average of

W^{c}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}
within a
D_{\mathbf{q}}
cell in the mini-BZ centered around each
\mathbf{q}
points of the Monkhorst-Pack grid. This approach effectively accounts for
W^{c}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}
, whose variations with respect to

\mathbf{q}
can be significant, particularly for the
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{G}^{\prime}=0
component. The factors
g^{nk}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega,\omega^{\prime})
, instead, are well-defined and smooth with respect to
\mathbf{q}
.

The integrals in
Eq.

11
can be conveniently performed stochastically via a Monte Carlo technique. However,
W^{c}
is known only in the grid points and its analytic form in
\mathbf{q}
-space is unknown.
In bulk semiconductors, for which the bare Coulomb potential varies far more rapidly than the dielectric matrix, it is possible to separate
W^{c}

in these two components. The bare Coulomb potential, whose analytical form is known, can be efficiently averaged using stochastic methods, while the dielectric matrix, computed on the Monkhorst-Pack grid, is kept piecewise constant. In cases such as 2D semiconductors, the dielectric matrix varies on a scale comparable to that of the Coulomb potential, and thus cannot be approximated as piecewise constant.

The W-av method proposed in Ref.
Guandalini et al. 2023a
addresses precisely the case of 2D semiconductors.
Within W-av, the analytic form of
W^{c}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}
is extended beyond the grid points, to the whole BZ, by interpolating an auxiliary function,
f_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
, which has a smoother variation with
\mathbf{q}
compared to
W^{c}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}
:

\displaystyle\!\!W^{c}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q})f_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)V_{\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})}{1-\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q})}f_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})}},

(12)

\displaystyle\!\!f_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\chi_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)}{1+\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q})}\chi_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})}}.

(13)

In each cell
D_{\mathbf{q}}
,
f_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
is interpolated through a quadratic polynomial in
\mathbf{q^{\prime}}
, whose coefficients are determined based on the numerical value of
f_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
in the center of
D_{\mathbf{q}}
and in the neighboring cells. Specifically, one has

f_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q^{\prime}})\equiv f_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})+\bar{f}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})\cdot\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\\
+\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\cdot\bar{\bar{f}}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})\cdot\mathbf{q}^{\prime},

(14)

where

\bar{f}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})=\begin{bmatrix}f^{1}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})&f^{2}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})\end{bmatrix},

(15)

and

\bar{\bar{f}}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})=\begin{bmatrix}f^{11}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})&f^{12}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})\\
f^{21}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})&f^{22}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})\end{bmatrix}.

(16)

In these expressions, both
\mathbf{q}
and
\mathbf{q^{\prime}}
are written in reciprocal lattice coordinates. For simplicity, the interpolation is performed using a bilinear form, i.e.,

f^{12}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})=f^{21}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})=0
, an approximation that has been shown to be accurate for 2D semiconductors.

For each cell, the interpolation results in a different analytic expression for
W^{c}
, then used to calculate, first the averaged potential in
Eq.

11
, and finally the correlation part of the self-energy in
Eq.

6
. In 2D semiconductors, the W-av method has shown to provide a remarkable convergence acceleration with respect to the
\mathbf{k}
-grid size
Guandalini et al. 2023a
;
Guandalini et al. 2024
.

II
Results and discussions

II.1
Extension of the W-av method for metals

The W-av method developed for semiconductors is not directly transferable to metallic systems due to the contribution of intraband transitions to the polarizability through
Eq.

9
.
The main difficulty lies in the limit
|\mathbf{q}|\rightarrow 0
, corresponding to the electronic transitions of vanishing energy. Due to the
\mathbf{q}
discretization, the intraband transitions must be treated either as a transition between the same state, with the same
\mathbf{k}
and energy, or between different states, immediately below and above the Fermi surface, resulting in a spurious energy gap. It is standard practice to simply neglect these long-wavelength intraband contributions, which, however, slows down considerably the convergence with the number of
\mathbf{k}
points, since only for very dense
\mathbf{k}
-grids their weight becomes negligible.

To extend the W-av method to metals, we introduce an extrapolation of the long-range behavior of
f
in Eq. (
13
), based on the
\mathbf{q}\rightarrow 0
element evaluated using first- and second-order neighbors along the reciprocal-cell vectors. This extrapolation is similar to
Eq.

14
, but the extrapolating polynomial is adapted to the dimensionality of the system (either 2D or 3D) and to the behavior of
f_{\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q}\rightarrow 0,\omega)
for different
\mathbf{G}
,
\mathbf{G^{\prime}}
components.
For the body elements (
\mathbf{G}\neq 0
,
\mathbf{G^{\prime}}\neq 0
), we use the standard quadratic polynomial, reflecting the regular behavior of the bare Coulomb potential.
For the head (
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{G^{\prime}}=0
) and wings (
\mathbf{G}=0
,
\mathbf{G^{\prime}}\neq 0
) terms, the functional form of the extrapolation is given by the relations between
f_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
and
\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
, whose small-
\mathbf{q}
dependence is known. We refer to
Appendix

A
for the derivation of these relations, which result in the following expressions:

f_{00}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\chi^{0}_{00}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\\
-\sum_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\chi^{0}_{0\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q})}B^{-1}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})}\,\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{G^{\prime}}0}(\mathbf{q},\omega).

(17)

f_{\mathbf{G}0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=-B^{-1}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}\,\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{G^{\prime}}0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)/\Big[1-V_{0}(\mathbf{q})f_{00}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\\
+\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q})}\,B^{-1}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{G^{\prime}}0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\sqrt{V_{0}(\mathbf{q})}\Big],

(18)

where
B^{-1}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}
is the body of the symmetrized dielectric matrix, thus defined only for
\mathbf{G}\neq 0
and
\mathbf{G^{\prime}}\neq 0
, given by:

B_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}=\delta_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}-\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q})}\,\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})}.

(19)

As in the case of
\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
, the long-wavelength limit of
f_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
differs in the static (
\omega=0
) and dynamic (
\omega\neq 0
) cases. Differences also arise with the dimensionality since for 2D systems, a truncated form of the 3D
V_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q})
is used.
At lowest order, the static term of the head is constant and isotropic, related to the Thomas-Fermi wave vector of the material. Its
\mathbf{q}\rightarrow 0
extrapolated value is here determined from the average over the different computed directions. In contrast, the finite frequency terms are not isotropic.
The lowest order terms of
f_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q}\rightarrow 0,\omega)
are, in some cases, quadratic (see
Tables

1
and
2
), and therefore third order terms must also be included in
Eq.

14
.

In the 3D case at finite frequency, we also relax the bilinear approximation of
f_{00}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
, both for the
\mathbf{q}\rightarrow 0
extrapolation and for the neighboring cells. In this case, we include mixed terms in the parameterization
f_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q^{\prime}},\omega)
, and strictly enforce a
q^{2}
long-wavelength behavior, matching the Coulomb potential
\sim q^{-2}
dispersion. In this manner, we avoid first-order terms that result in numerical noise. The coefficients of the mixed terms are determined by including in the parametrization neighboring
\mathbf{q}
points reached through combinations of non-collinear reciprocal lattice vectors.
The corresponding functional forms of
f_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

for the different components for the 3D and 2D cases are summarized in
Tables

1
and
2
, respectively.
A detailed derivation and discussion can be found in the Supplementary Information.

In doped graphene, even if the Fermi level does not coincide with the Dirac point, similar

-grids are still necessary since the Dirac cones have a steep dispersion and the Fermi surfaces have a limited
\mathbf{k}
-space extension. This increases the minimal
\mathbf{k}
-grid required to successfully apply the W-av method.
Furthermore, in a 2D metal the position in frequency of the intraband peak (
\omega_{p}
) depends on the momentum and approaches zero following
\omega_{p}\sim\sqrt{q}

Hwang and Das Sarma 2007

For instance, at small
\mathbf{q}

\mathbf{k}

In the calculation of the self-energy, according to
Eq.

5

\mathbf{q}
is weighed by the one particle Green-s function

The Lorentzian long tails, with a slow 1/

\mathbf{q}

-order generalized Lorentzian poles
Chiarotti et al. 2022

II.5
Band gap renormalization in doped 2D semiconductors

As anticipated, the reduced screening of 2D materials makes their energy levels sensible to external factors, governing their dielectric screening
Ugeda et al. 2014
;
Mak et al. 2012
;
Ross et al. 2013
;
Hüser et al. 2013
;
Louie et al. 2021
. Among other, this is the case of doping, which can affect the quasi-particle gaps of 2D systems.
However, as discussed in the previous Sections, the first principles description of these screening effects may be challenging, particularly at the low doping density that is most relevant to realistic experiments and devices.
In this Section we present the band gap renormalization of monolayer MoS
2
and graphene as a function of the doping, computed using the methodology developed and presented in this work, namely the W-av method generalized for 2D systems for the description of the
\mathbf{k}
discretization, combined with the MPA method for full-frequency GW.

Focusing on monolayer MoS
2
, in
Fig.

9
we plot the
GW
correction to the gap at
K
obtained using the newly developed W-av method, for different electronic doping.
The DFT calculations show a band gap that does not change with doping. As already established in the literature
Faridi et al. 2021
, for very small dopings, the
GW
QP correction to the band gap exhibits a sharp decrease compared to the charge neutrality point. However, for increasing doping levels, the QP corrections flatten out and further doping changes very little the band gap.
The present results accurately reproduce the expected behavior, and the MPA + W-av treatment of both the dynamical response and the long wavelength limit outperforms previously reported
GW
results
Liang and Yang 2015
;
Gao and Yang 2017
, achieving quantitative agreement with TR-ARPES measurements
Liu et al. 2019
.
The improvement in accuracy can be ascribed on the one side to the accurate response function, now including all intraband contributions, but also to the accurate description of the frequency dependence stemming from MPA. In fact, a simple plasmon pole model is not capable of describing the branch of low-energy carrier acoustic plasmon emerging with doping. In Refs.
Liang and Yang 2015
;
Gao and Yang 2017
this new branch is modeled by taking into account the variation of the response head matrix element caused by doping with the addition of a further intraband pole. Here, the use of W-av combined with MPA provides a more general full-frequency description of the response, improving the accuracy of the resulting QP energies.

Finally, we evaluate the quasiparticle corrections of graphene at the
M
point for different doping levels, as shown in
Fig.

Attaccalite et al. 2010
, indicating a sharp decrease in QPs correction upon charge injection into the system. Nevertheless, the magnitude of the quasiparticle correction computed in the present work differs significantly from Ref.
Attaccalite et al. 2010
due to the improved description we have adopted in
\mathbf{k}
and frequency space. Two lines are displayed in the figure: the linearized solution, which was calculated using
G_{0}^{(1)}
and a damping parameter of 0.1 eV (orange dashed line), and the spectral solution (i.e., via the peaks of the spectral function), which was computed using
G_{0}^{(2)}
and a damping parameter of 0.2 eV (red line). The two methods provide distinct QP corrections at large doping charge, while the difference reduces near the charge neutrality point. The solutions from the spectral function have proved numerically more robust, showing that, besides the significant improvements provided by the combination of W-av, now including intraband contributions, and MPA, an extra care must be taken in the functional description of
W
and
G

This further confirms that the combination of MPA with W-av, now including intraband
contributions, is capable to assess doped 2D systems, addressing the fine details observed in ARPES experiments.

III
Conclusions

In this work we focus on
GW
calculations for metallic systems and present a fully ab-initio approach, W-av, that includes the intraband contributions in the evaluation of the response function, for both 3D and 2D metals, without the requirement of any external parameters.
The W-av method combines an interpolation of the screened potential calculated in the discretised
\mathbf{q}
-space
Guandalini et al. 2023a

with an extrapolation to the
\mathbf{q}\rightarrow 0
limit able to account for the intraband contribution to the irreducible polarizability.
The analytic expression of the extrapolation depends on the dimensionality and is different for the static and dynamic cases, all of which considered here. It is also compatible with the use of the full frequency description of the screened potential done with MPA
Leon et al. 2021
;
Leon et al. 2023
;
Guandalini et al. 2024
, and easily extended to self consistent
GW
methods.

For bulk metals such as Na and Mg, W-av reproduces very accurately the experimental Drude peak and significantly accelerates the convergence of
GW
calculations with respect to the adopted
\mathbf{k}
-grid. The acceleration is even more remarkable for 2D systems. We consider here both doped MoS
2
and doped graphene. Importantly, for MoS
2
the accurate inclusion of intraband transitions in the long wavelength limit with W-av results in an excellent agreement with TR-ARPES measurements of the energy gap at the
K
point
Liu et al. 2019
.
Graphene proved to be a more complicated system, with the QP corrections showing a marked dependence on the damping parameter used in the self-energy calculation. Notably, the use of higher order Lorentzians
Chiarotti et al. 2022
in the description of the self-energy and of a full-frequency solution of the QP equation beyond linearization, provides numerically robust results.

IV
Methods

Computational details

In the present work we investigate both 3D and 2D metallic systems: bulk Na and bulk Mg, 2D MoS
2
and graphene with various levels of doping. The crystal structures of all systems were relaxed at the DFT level, minimizing the total energy with respect to ionic coordinates and lattice parameters. The DFT calculations were performed using the PBE
Perdew et al. 1996
exchange and correlation functional, except for graphene, for which we used LDA
Perdew and Zunger 1981
, using norm-conserving pseudo-potential, as implemented in the
Quantum ESPRESSO
suite
et al. 2009
;
et al. 2017
. Many body perturbation theory calculations such as
GW
are instead performed using the
Yambo
code
Sangalli et al. 2019
.

According to our simulations,
Bulk Na crystallizes in a BCC lattice with a computed lattice constant of
a=4.23
Å. The DFT calculations were performed using a Fermi-Dirac smearing of 0.005 Ry and a wavefunction (WF) cut-off of 100 Ry.
GW
calculations were performed using the recently developed MPA method for the matrix
\chi
, with 8 frequency poles chosen with a linear sampling between 0 and 4 Ha, 100 bands, and a cutoff of 20 Ry. The self-energy,
\Sigma
, was calculated using 100 bands.
For the HCP structure of Mg, the computed lattice constants are
a=3.193
Å and
c=5.188
Å. Calculations adopted a WF cut-off of 120 Ry and a Methfessel-Paxton (MP) smearing of 0.01 Ry. In
yambo
, the occupations are described with a Fermi-Dirac smearing of 0.038 eV that allows one to match the DFT Fermi energy.
The
GW
simulations are carried out using the MPA approach with 8 frequency poles chosen with a linear sampling between 0 and 4 Ha and 240 bands in the polarizability, together with a

\chi
matrix cutoff of 20 Ry; the
GW

\Sigma
was calculated including 500 bands.

Concerning 2D systems, we computed the relaxed lattice structure of MoS
2
and graphene in the undoped case, and found values of the lattice constants 3.184 Å and 2.46 Å, respectively. The insertion of free charges upon doping effectively changes the 2D lattice, as discussed in detail in the literature, particularly in the case of graphene
Lazzeri and Mauri 2006
.
However, in this work we neglect this effect since it goes beyond the scope of the present study. For the same reason, we do not consider the spin-orbit interaction, which would be needed for an accurate characterization of TMDs such as MoS
2
.
Since all calculations are performed using periodic boundary conditions, in order to avoid interaction between replicas, the distance between layers was set to 9.84 Å for graphene and to 10 Å for MoS
2
. The undoped calculation for MoS
2
was done with a vacuum of 20 Å. Such spacing also prevents the formation of bound vacuum states, a well-known issue in DFT calculations in the presence of explicit carrier doping.

The DFT calculation for MoS
2
was done with a Fermi-Dirac smearing of 0.005 Ry, and a cutoff of 60 Ry. The MBPT calculations at the
GW
level were performed using the MPA method for the
\chi
matrix, with 8 frequency poles chosen with a quadratic sampling between 0 and 4 Ha, 400 bands, and a WF cutoff of 10 Ry, whereas
\Sigma
was calculated using 600 bands.
In the case of Graphene, the DFT computation was performed with a MP smearing of 0.01 Ry, and a WF cutoff of 90 Ry. In this case, the Fermi level determined by a MP smearing of 0.01 Ry is matched by a Fermi-Dirac smearing of 0.0325 eV. The MBPT calculations at
GW
level were performed using the MPA method for the
\chi
matrix, with 10 frequency poles chosen with a quadratic sampling between 0 and 4 Ha, 100 bands, and a cutoff of 5 Ry, whereas
\Sigma
was calculated using 100 bands. Such a choice of the
GW
parameters is sufficiently converged to accurately characterize the electronic band structure in the region of the graphene M points, that is studied in detail.

In this Appendix, we illustrate the relations between the auxiliary functions
f_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
with the irreducible polarizability
\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{G^{\prime}}=0

\mathbf{G}=0
,
\mathbf{G^{\prime}}\neq 0

\tilde{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
as the starting point, which is here written in the symmetrized version:

\tilde{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\mathbb{1}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}-\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q})}\,\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\,\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})}.

(21)

The
\tilde{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}H_{00}&w_{0,\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}^{T}\\
w_{\mathbf{G}0}&B_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle H_{00}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-V_{0}(\mathbf{q})\chi^{0}_{00}(\mathbf{q},\omega),

\displaystyle w_{\mathbf{G}0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q})}\,\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{G}0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\,\sqrt{V_{0}(\mathbf{q})},

\displaystyle B_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}-\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q})}\,\chi^{0}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})}.

Using the same four matrix blocks, the inverse dielectric function has a compact representation of the form:

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}^{-1}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\bar{H}_{00}&\bar{w}_{0,\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}^{T}\\
\bar{w}_{\mathbf{G}0}&\bar{B}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\bar{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(H-w^{T}B^{-1}w)^{-1},

\displaystyle\bar{w}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-B^{-1}w(H-w^{T}B^{-1}w)^{-1},

\displaystyle\bar{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B^{-1}+B^{-1}w(H-w^{T}B^{-1}w)^{-1}w^{T}B^{-1}.

\displaystyle\tilde{\epsilon}^{-1}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\mathbb{1}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}+\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q})}\chi_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\sqrt{V_{\mathbf{G^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{q})},

(28)

13

2

As discussed in the main text, in a 2D metal the position in frequency of the intraband peak (

\omega_{p}\sim\sqrt{q}

shows the self-energy calculated for the metallic and the first conduction band at the

5
