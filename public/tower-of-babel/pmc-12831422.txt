Modulation of human neutrophil functions by hydroethanolic cladode extract of Opuntia ficus-indica: an in vitro experimental study
Authors: Wafa Ferjani, Pham My-Chan Dang, Hamadi Fetoui, Samia Bedouhene, Ahmed Kouki, Mossadok Ben-Attia, Jamel El-Benna, Abdelaziz Souli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOpuntia ficus-indica is recognised for its anti-inflammatory effects and potential health benefits. Its different parts are rich in bioactive compounds and are consumed in various traditional diets. However, its cellular and molecular targets are not well known. In this study, we evaluated the effects of hydroethanolic cladode extract (HECE) on human neutrophil functions.MethodsHigh-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was employed to characterise and identify the bioactive compounds of the hydroethanolic extract of Opuntia ficus-indica cladode, neutrophil chemotaxis was assessed via agarose assay, and neutrophil degranulation was assessed by the release of myeloperoxidase (MPO) and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) via western blotting. Neutrophil ROS production was evaluated by chemiluminescence, and superoxide anion generation was determined via a cytochrome C reduction assay.ResultsHPLC analysis revealed that the extract contains flavonoids and polyphenols involved in oxidative stress inhibition. HECEs significantly inhibited neutrophil chemotaxis, reduced MPO degranulation, and strongly reduced ROS production in phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)- or N-formyl-methionyl-leucyl-phenylalanine (FMLP)-stimulated neutrophils. The extract reacted with superoxide anions and inhibited hydrogen peroxide production.ConclusionsOpuntia ficus-indica hydroethanolic cladode extract modulates major neutrophil functions and has antioxidant effects. These results could explain the anti-inflammatory properties of this plant and may lead to future therapeutic applications in inflammatory diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05222-0.Keywords: Polymorphonuclear leukocytes, Oxidative stress, Polyphenols, Immune response, Cactus extract, Reactive oxygen species

Introduction

Opuntia ficus-indica, also referred to as the nopal cactus or prickly pear, is a cactus species belonging to the Cactaceae family. It is a medicinal plant native to Mexico that is widely distributed worldwide and renowned for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [1]. Different parts of the plant, such as its seeds, flowers and fruits, have been recognized for their anticancer effects through their cytotoxic effects on malignant cells [2], antihypercholesterolemic effects, and antidiabetic effects due to their ability to lower cholesterol and blood glucose levels [3, 4]. These benefits are attributed to its secondary metabolites, particularly polyphenols, which confer beneficial effects, especially in models of oxidative stress and inflammation [5]. The cladodes of Opuntia ficus-indica are rich in a variety of polyphenolic compounds, primarily flavonoids such as quercetin, kaempferol and isorhamnetin derivatives, as well as phenolic acids, including gallic, caffeic and ferulic acids, and tannins. These compounds are well documented for their potent antioxidant capacity and ability to modulate the inflammatory pathway [6–8]. During inflammatory responses, immune cells play crucial roles, particularly neutrophils, representing the majority of leukocytes in circulation and are considered the primary defenders of the innate immune system [9]. Neutrophils act as crucial modulators of both immune and inflammatory processes by migrating to the inflammatory site and destroying invading microorganisms via phagocytosis, the release of granule contents and the production of ROS [10]. The release of ROS is a key neutrophil function. Indeed, activated neutrophils produce ROS to initiate the defence process. Superoxide anion (O2•-), the first ROS generated mainly by the enzyme NADPH oxidase (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate), serves as a precursor for other reactive species, including hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), hydroxyl radical (OH.), and hypochlorous acid (HOCl) [11]. This process is thought to be initiated by the activation of the NADPH complex, which involves the assembly of cytosolic subunits (p40phox, p47phox, p67phox and Rac1/2) and membrane components (gp91phox and p22phox) [12]. An excess production of ROS beyond the defensive capacity of cells and tissues to detoxify them leads to oxidative stress, which contributes to the development of diverse diseases through radical-mediated destruction and oxidative damage [13]. Oxidative stress complements other neutrophil defense mechanisms, including proteases and antibacterial peptides [14]. Neutrophils contain granules with distinct roles in the immune response. Primary granules, or azurophilic granules, are crucial in inflammatory processes and contain MPO, an enzyme that is predominantly localized in these granules. This enzyme serves as an important biomarker, making it a valuable diagnostic tool [9, 15]. The specific or secondary granules are the main storage products of lactoferrin and lipocalin 2, also known as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL). These molecules play a key role in microbial defense and modulate the inflammatory process [16]. However, neutrophil hyperactivation can lead to the release of proteases and ROS, which participate in tissue injury and inflammatory reactions. The inhibition of neutrophil functions during inflammation by plant extracts can be a natural anti-inflammatory approach.

Our recent study [17] demonstrated that aqueous extracts of Opuntia ficus-indica cladodes possess significant anti-inflammatory and antiulcerative properties. However, extensive literature highlights the preference for binary solvents, such as water and organic solvents, in the extraction of major bioactive compounds, particularly polyphenols, to ensure the maximum biological activity of the plant material [18]. In this study, we aimed to specifically evaluate the effects of the hydroethanolic cladode extract (HECE) of Opuntia ficus-indica on human neutrophil functions and its capacity to reduce inflammation and oxidative stress. This work contributes to understanding the therapeutic potential of natural products in managing inflammatory and oxidative conditions.

Methods

Plant material and extract preparation

Fresh Opuntia ficus-indica cladodes were collected from the Menzel Bourguiba region (37° 9’ 21” North, 9°47’ 37” East) Bizerte, Tunisia. As this species is not listed as endangered or protected under Tunisian law, no specific collection permit was needed.

The cladodes were initially rinsed with distilled water, cut into small pieces, dried in a ventilated oven at 40 °C until complete dehydration, and then powdered via an electric mixer (Topmatic model no: SME-600.2G). The plant material (Opuntia ficus-indica) used was identified by Pr.Neziha Ghanem-Boughanmi at the Faculty of Sciences in Bizerte (University of Carthage).

A voucher specimen (reference number: Ofi-C/LBE 22 − 001) has been archived in the Environmental Biomonitoring Laboratory herbarium (LBE, Bizerte, Tunisia) and is publicly accessible upon request.

Extraction was carried out by dissolving 1 g of cladode powder in 10 mL of 70% ethanol and macerated for 24 h at ambient temperature. Rotary evaporation and lyophilization were used as post-extraction drying methods. The powders obtained were stored at 4 °C until use. For the preparation of HECE, the lyophilized powder was dissolved in sterile distilled water for in vitro assays.

Phytochemical profiling and assessment of the antioxidant activity of HECE

The total phenolic content (TPC) was measured using the Folin‒Ciocalteu assay, while the total flavonoid content (TFC) was assessed via the aluminum-chloride method as described previously by Zhishen et al. [19]. The antioxidant activity of HECE was determined via the ABTS●+ assay according to Re et al. [20], and the Fe2+ chelating activity was performed as described by Chew et al. [21]. All analyses were carried out in triplicate. HPLC was used for identification and quantification of the phenolic compounds present in the HECE extract. Analysis was conducted via an Agilent 1260 Infinity Quaternary LC (Germany) equipped with a C18 column (4.6 mm×250 mm) and a Shimadzu SPD6AUV UV‒vis detector maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of two eluents: solution A (70% acetonitrile in ultrapure water) and solution B (0.1% phosphoric acid in ultrapure water). Elution was carried out via a linear gradient of solution A as follows: from 10% to 25% over the first 25 min, from 25% to 80% for up to 35 min, and from 80% to 100% for up to 50 min, followed by re-equilibration to initial conditions. The flow rate was adjusted to 0.6 mL/min, and the injection volume was 20 µL. UV detection was carried out at 280 nm. A semi-quantitative analysis was performed on the basis of the peak areas (%) obtained from the HPLC chromatograms.

Human neutrophil isolation

Neutrophils were obtained from the venous blood of healthy volunteers at Bichat Hospital, Paris, as previously described [22, 23]. Erythrocytes were first removed by dextran (T500), followed by Ficoll-Paque separation to eliminate monocytes/lymphocytes, and hypnotic lysis to remove any remaining erythrocytes. Neutrophils were then washed with PBS and counted for further analysis. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board and Ethics committee of the INSERM (agreement no. 2018010827). Data analysis was performed anonymously.

Assessment of HECE effects on neutrophils viability and ROS production

Cell viability was determined via a Trypan blue exclusion assay [25]. Neutrophils in Hanks’balanced salt solution (HBSS) were incubated with increasing concentrations of HECE (2.5, 5, 10 and 20 µg/mL) for 10 min. After incubation, trypan blue solution (Sigma‒Aldrich/St. Louis, MI, USA) was added to the cells, and the number of viable cells was counted.

For ROS evaluation, isolated neutrophils (5 × 105) were resuspended in HBSS containing luminol (5 µM) and different concentrations of HECE (2.5, 5, 10, and 20 µg/mL) at 37 °C in a thermostatted chamber for 15 min. Neutrophils were subsequently stimulated with PMA (100 ng/mL) or FMLP (10− 6 M), and chemiluminescence was measured over a 15 to 30-minute period using Biolumat LB937 chemiluminometer (Berthold SAS, Thoiry, France). The experimental design included untreated neutrophils as a negative control, stimulated neutrophils without extract treatment as a positive control, and stimulated neutrophils treated with various concentrations of HECE.

Assessment of neutrophil chemotaxis

The in vitro spontaneous migration of human neutrophils in agarose was determined via the method of Nelson et al. [24]. Briefly, 0.7% agarose was dissolved in HBSS by heating in a microwave until complete dissolution. After cooling, 10% decomplemented SVF was added to the mixture. A culture plate (5 cm diameter) was prepared by adding a thin layer of the agarose mixture. After solidification, four rows of three aligned wells were made on each plate, and the agarose cores were removed via an aspiration system (Integra VACUSAFE). In the chemotaxis assay, neutrophils were resuspended at a concentration of 100 × 106 cells/mL and treated with increasing concentrations of HECE (2.5, 5, 10, or 20 µg/mL). PBS was added to each well to measure basal migration, while FMLP (10–7 M) was used as a chemoattractant. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 to 2 h and observed under an inverted microscope (ZEISS). Migration was initially assessed at 20x magnification to distinguish the starting point and the migration front, followed by 40x magnification for detailed visualization. Each experiment included untreated neutrophils to assess basal migration, the chemoattractant FMLP as a positive control, and neutrophils treated with different concentrations of HECE.

Effect of HECE on superoxide anion production via the acellular system of xanthine-xanthine oxidase and neutrophils

Effect of HECE on superoxide anion production via the acellular system of xanthine-xanthine oxidase and neutrophils

Superoxide anion production was performed via the xanthine‒xanthine oxidase method as described previously by Bedouhene et al. [25]. Increased concentrations of HECE (2.5, 5, 10 and 20 µg/mL) were incubated in the presence xanthine oxidase (10 U) and cytochrome C (1 mg/mL), followed by the addition of xanthine to initiate the reaction. Superoxide anion production was detected by measuring the reduction of cytochrome C over 15 min using a spectrophotometer (Agilent Cary 3500) at 550 nm.

To measure the effect of HECE on neutrophil superoxide anion production, different concentrations of HECE (2.5, 5, 10 and 20 µg/mL) were incubated in the presence of neutrophils (1 × 106 cells/mL HBSS) and cytochrome C (1 mg/mL) for 15 min at 37 °C, and the generation of O2•− was triggered by the addition of PMA (100 ng/mL) or FMLP (10–6 M). The effect was observed for 15 min via Agilent carry 3500 spectrophotometers at 550 nm. The experimental design included unstimulated neutrophils (negative control), stimulated neutrophils without extract (positive control), and stimulated neutrophils treated with different concentrations of HECE.

Effect of HECE on H2O2

To evaluate the effect of HECE on H2O2, HECE at concentrations of 2.5, 5, 10 and 20 µg/mL was incubated with H2O2 (0.003%) and luminol (5 µM) for 15 min in a thermostatic chamber at 37 °C as described by Chniguir et al. [26]. HRPO (5 U) was then added to initiate the reaction. The changes observed were measured for 15 min using a Biolumat LB937 chemiluminometer (Berthold SAS, Thoiry, France). The experimental design included a negative control (reaction without H2O2), a positive control (reaction mixture containing H2O2 and HRPO without HECE), and HECE-treated groups at different concentrations (2.5, 5, 10, and 20 µg/mL).

Effects of HECE on degranulation in human neutrophils

Neutrophil degranulation was evaluated following the method described by Bedouhene et al. [25], with minor modifications. Neutrophils (5 × 106 cells/mL in HBSS) were preincubated at 37 °C for 15 min in the presence or absence of increasing concentrations of HECE (2.5, 5, 10–20 µg/mL). Cytochalasin B (5 µg/mL) was then added, and the mixture was incubated for 5 min. The cells were then stimulated with PMA (100 ng/mL) or FMLP (10–6 M) for 10 min. The reaction was stopped by centrifugation at 10,000xg for 30 s, and the resulting supernatant was subjected to a second centrifugation at 12, 000xg for 10 min at 4 °C. The obtained samples were then denatured by boiling in water at 100 °C for 3 min and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

Western blotting assay

Western blotting was performed to evaluate the effects of HECE on the expression levels of MPO and NGAL during the degranulation process of neutrophils. The preserved samples were thawed, sonicated, and analysed by SDS‒PAGE following the protocol described by Belambri et al. [27]. The separated proteins were then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Blocking was performed for one hour using 5% BSA diluted in Tris-buffered saline containing Tween 20 (TBS-T). The membranes were then incubated with specific primary antibodies (anti-MPO (1:10000) and anti-NGAL (1:5000)) provided by Abcam (Cambridge, UK) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the appropriate secondary antibody, an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit antibody (1:10000) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Heidelberg, Germany). The protein bands were detected via enhanced chemiluminescence and visualized via an Amersham Imager. Analysis of the bands was performed via Image J software.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean values ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were conducted using Graph Pad Prism 8 software (San Diego, CA). Each assay was conducted in at least three independent replicates to ensure reproducibility and statistical validity, and differences between groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparison post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), and p < 0.001 (***).

Results

Phytochemical profiling and antioxidant evaluation of HECE

Preliminary analysis of HECE revealed high contents of total phenolic compounds and flavonoids, with 608.84 ± 14.94 mg of GAE (gallic acid equivalent) per gram of dry extract and 227.34 ± 0.03 mg of QE (quercetin equivalent) per gram of dry extract. The antioxidant activity of HECE was further confirmed by the ABTS●+ radical scavenging assay and Fe2+ chelating activity. The extract exhibited a notable ABTS●+ radical scavenging effect, with an IC50 of 42.14 ± 0.87 µg/mL, whereas Trolox, which is used as a standard antioxidant, had an IC50 of 25.26 ± 0.23 µg/mL. Furthermore, HECE exhibited Fe2+ chelating activity with an IC50 of 52.81 ± 0.32 µg/mL, which is comparable to that of the positive control EDTA, which is known for its strong chelating capacity (36.51 ± 0.42 µg/mL). This highlights the significant potential of HECE as a natural antioxidant and metal chelator. Polyphenolic compounds identified by HPLC were numbered and classified according to their retention time (Table 1). Several absorbance peaks were detected (Fig. 1), corresponding to gallic acid (1), catechin, hydrated catechin (2, 3), caffeic acid (4), p-coumaric acid (5), rutin (6), quercetin (7) and kaempferol (8). The relative content of each identified compound was expressed as a percentage of the total peak area, with a combined percentage of 41.08% of catechins found to be the most abundant compounds in the extract.

Effect of HECE on neutrophil viability in vitro

Before investigating the effect of HECE on neutrophil functions, we wanted to determine whether it affects neutrophil viability. The microscopic assessment of neutrophil viability (Fig. 2) revealed that HECE was not toxic to neutrophils, even at the highest concentrations tested. These results allow us to further test the effects of HECE on neutrophil functions without any toxic effects at the concentrations tested in this study.

HECE inhibits neutrophil migration toward FMLP

Neutrophils are dormant cells in the blood of healthy individuals; however, they are the first cells to migrate from the circulation to the inflammatory site. Migration, or chemotaxis, is one of the first and key neutrophil functions before its activation [25]. We thus tested the effect of HECE on neutrophil chemotaxis toward a gradient of FMLP. The results revealed that preincubation of neutrophils with HECE reduced their ability to migrate in response to FMLP (Fig. 3A), and this reduction was highly significant (p < 0.001), as indicated by the chemotaxis index (Fig. 3B) and migration levels (Fig. 3C), with reductions of 25% at 2.5 µg/mL, 57.4% at 5 µg/mL, 59.1% at 10 µg/mL and 80.2% at 20 µg/mL compared with the FMLP group. These results show that HECE inhibits FMLP-induced neutrophil chemotaxis in vitro, which suggests that it could alter neutrophil recruitment to the inflammatory site.

HECE strongly inhibits ROS production by FMLP- and PMA-stimulated neutrophils

We next investigated the effect of HECE on neutrophil activation by measuring ROS production via a luminol-amplified chemiluminescence assay. As shown in Fig. 4, compared with resting conditions, stimulation with FMLP significantly increased ROS production (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4A and B). Compared with those in the FMLP group, neutrophils pretreated with HECE (2.5, 5, 10, and 20 µg/mL) presented a dose-dependent reduction in ROS levels in response to FMLP, with decreases of 57% (2.5 µg/mL), 76% (5 µg/mL), 80.33% (10 µg/mL), and 92.33% (20 µg/mL) compared with those in the FMLP group. To further confirm the inhibitory effect of HECE on ROS generation by neutrophils, we used PMA, a direct activator of PKC. HECEs inhibited PMA-induced ROS production (Fig. 4C), and the inhibitory effect was highly significant (p < 0.001) at all concentrations used (Fig. 4D), with decreases of 78.33% (2.5 µg/mL), 86% (5 µg/mL), 89.33% (10 µg/mL), and 95.33% (20 µg/mL) compared with PMA alone. To further understand the antioxidant effect, we evaluated the ability of HECE to reduce the formation of O2•−, the first ROS molecule produced by neutrophils. Interestingly, neutrophils preincubated with HECE and stimulated with PMA or FMLP presented a marked decrease in O2•−levels, as evidenced by a reduction in cytochrome C (Fig. 5A and B). In fact, at 2.5 µg/mL, superoxide anion production decreased by 10.21% compared with that in the PMA-stimulated group. The inhibition reached 27.72% at 5 µg/mL, 43.03% at 10 µg/mL, and 37.33% at 20 µg/mL. In the case of FMLP stimulation, the extract reduced O2•−production by 27.41% at 2.5 µg/mL, 40.44% at 5 µg/mL, 42.98% at 10 µg/mL, and 62.19% at 20 µg/mL.

HECE have an antioxidant effect by scavenging superoxide anions and hydrogen peroxide

To verify whether HECE has an antioxidant effect, we tested its effect on O2•−production via the X/XO enzyme system and on the hydrogen peroxide H2O2. The results showed that HECE at all the concentrations tested markedly reduced the O2•− levels generated by the xanthine‒xanthine oxidase system (Fig. 6A). Compared with xanthine group (100% activity), the extract clearly inhibited O2•− production in a dose-dependent manner. At 2.5 µg/mL, the production was reduced by 39.2%, increasing to 50.8% at 5 µg/mL, 79.9% at 10 µg/mL, and reaching 90% inhibition at 20 µg/mL. These results suggest that HECE effectively reacts with O2•−, thereby reducing their levels in a concentration–dependent manner. The direct effect of HECE on H2O2 was evaluated via H2O2/HRPO and luminol-enhanced chemiluminescence in a cell-free system. The results demonstrated that HECE significantly inhibited the reaction (Fig. 6B). This effect was highly significant (p < 0.001) (Fig. 6C). Specifically, compared with the H2O2 control, HECE treatment caused dose-dependent inhibition of 37.3%, 55.7%, 79%, and 83.3% at the same respective concentrations.

Effect of HECE on neutrophil degranulation induced by azurophilic and specific granules

After chemotaxis, neutrophils are activated at the site of inflammation by releasing their granule contents in a process called degranulation. The effect of HECE on FMLP- and PMA-induced neutrophil degranulation of azurophilic and specific granules was assessed via the use of MPO and NGAL/lipocalin2 as markers. The results showed that HECE inhibited MPO release from PMA- and FMLP-stimulated neutrophils. Compared with PMA, HECE treatment resulted in 39.8% inhibition at both 2.5 µg/mL and 5 µg/mL, 75.1% inhibition at 10 µg/mL (p < 0.001), and 81.7% inhibition at 20 µg/mL (p < 0.001) (Fig. 7A and B). For FMLP stimulation, HECE treatment caused dose-dependent inhibition, with an approximately 30% reduction at 2.5 µg/mL and 5 µg/mL, a 64% reduction at 10 µg/mL (p < 0.01), and a 70% reduction at 20 µg/mL (p < 0.01) (Fig. 7C and D).

However, the results showed that HECE had no effect on NGAL release (Fig. 7E, F, G and H). These results show that HECE inhibited neutrophil degranulation of azurophilic granules without affecting degranulation of specific granules.

Discussion

Several natural products have demonstrated their potential as therapeutic approaches against various diseases because of their richness in diverse bioactive compounds that can inhibit several proinflammatory signalling pathways, including NF-κB [28] and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) [29], and activate antioxidant defence mechanisms, such as Nrf2 activation [30], which are responsible for their beneficial biological effects. Among them, Opuntia ficus-indica endowed with anti-inflammatory properties and several health benefits [31]. In this study, we specifically targeted the bioactive compounds present in the binary solvent (70/30 ethanol/water) of cladode extract from Opuntia ficus-indica plants via human-stimulated neutrophil cells to elucidate the mechanism by which the extract modulates neutrophil activity, contributing to the attenuation of the inflammatory response and supporting its therapeutic potential. Phytochemical analysis revealed that HECE is rich in bioactive compounds, especially total phenolics (608.84 ± 14.94 mg GAE/g) and flavonoids (227.34 ± 0.03 mg QE/g). Our results are higher than those reported by Ressaissi et al. [32] and Lee et al. [33] (180.3 mg/g). This difference can be explained by several factors: plant variety or species (different cultivars can vary significantly in secondary metabolite production), environmental and seasonal conditions affecting phytochemical biosynthesis, and differences in extraction procedures such as solvent composition, extraction temperature, and duration. On the basis of phytochemical characterization, identifying the metabolic profile of a natural extract is crucial for understanding its antioxidant and biological effects. HPLC analysis identified several phenolic compounds known for their beneficial health properties. In particular, HECE is rich in bioactive compounds with widely reported anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and nutritional properties [34]. Our HPLC results are in line with recent studies [35, 36], which identified polyphenolic compounds such as gallic acid, catechin, p-coumaric acid, and quercetin in cactus cladode extracts, although the relative proportions varied depending on the solvent system used. However, the contents of rutin and kaempferol found in our extract were not reported in those studies, suggesting that the hydroethanolic extraction employed in our work may increase the recovery of these flavonoids. Data from the literature have revealed the beneficial effects of gallic acid, a compound known for its strong antioxidant properties and ability to modulate several signalling pathways and inflammatory cytokines, thereby contributing to its anti-inflammatory effects [37, 38]. Catechin also enhances the cellular response, as highlighted by Bernatoniene and Kopustinskiene [39], and has cytoprotective effects [40]. Moreover, p-coumaric acid and rutin exhibit antioxidant and antimicrobial proprieties [41, 42]. Quercetin and kaempferol have been shown to have potent therapeutic effects, including cardioprotective and antihypertensive activities [43, 44]. Interestingly, these compounds differ from those reported in the study by Ressaissi et al. [3] on aqueous, highlighting the impact of the extraction method. Such variations may result from differences in plant growth conditions, solvents, and extraction processes, which is consistent with literature data highlighting the prevalence of binary solvents for enhancing the biological activities of plant extracts [45, 46].

The richness of phenolic compounds, which are known to neutralize free radicals, allows us to assess their effects on excessive ROS production, which is implicated in numerous pathophysiological processes [47, 48]. First, we tested the cellular toxicity of different concentrations of HECE via the trypan blue exclusion test to ensure that the effects observed were due to the biological properties of the extract and not to cytotoxicity. The results show that the extract is not toxic to neutrophils, as indicated by its intact membrane integrity. These findings are in line with our previous study conducted on aqueous cladode extracts of the same species from the plant Opuntia ficus-indica [17], as well as other studies that highlighted the safety profile of the natural extract in biological systems [49, 50]. Thus, we were interested in studying a fundamental proinflammatory mechanism in the immune response, neutrophil chemotaxis, which is a valuable method for assessing the anti-inflammatory potential of our extract. We used FMLP, which acts as a potential chemotactic agent that attracts neutrophils to assess their ability to respond to an inflammatory signal. Our results indicate that HECE effectively modulates neutrophil migration toward the chemotactic signal (FMLP). This reduction in migration occurred in a dose-dependent manner, demonstrating that our extract has anti-inflammatory effects by limiting excessive or uncontrolled neutrophils, a key process in both acute and chronic inflammation [25]. Our extract has significant therapeutic potential, which is in agreement with the results of Bedouhene et al. [25], who revealed the importance of phenolic compounds in attenuating neutrophil chemotaxis, thereby reducing inflammation associated with excessive neutrophil recruitment and infiltration. Next, we sought to evaluate the efficacy of the bioactive compounds in HECE in mitigating oxidative stress in PMA- and FMLP-stimulated neutrophils. Naturally, generated neutrophil-derived ROS are crucial for eliminating pathogens; however, excessive ROS production can disrupt homeostasis, leading to tissue damage and various health issues [9, 51]. Our results demonstrate that PMA, a potent activator of PKC, and FMLP, a chemotactic peptide, stimulate neutrophils to produce a significant amount of ROS. This overproduction reflects an oxidative imbalance and disturbance in cellular homeostasis. Interestingly, the polyphenolic compounds present in HECE strongly inhibit excessive ROS production, thereby restoring normal physiological conditions. Thus, Hussain al. [52] confirmed that polyphenolic compounds can suppress ROS overproduction by reducing the catalytic activity of enzymes involved in ROS generation or by directly neutralizing free radicals.

To highlight the effect of HECE on the production of O2•−, a highly reactive and harmful species to human health [53], we evaluated the acellular effect of HECE on O2•− generation via the X/XO system. Indeed, xanthine oxidase catalyzes the oxidation of xanthine to uric acid, generating O2•− as a byproduct. If not properly regulated, excess O2•− can lead to oxidative cell damage [10]. Therefore, HECE has strong potential to reduce superoxide anion levels, suggesting that it has an inhibitory effect on xanthine oxidase enzyme activity. This effect may be largely attributed to the presence of caffeic acid, a well-documented bioactive compound known for its potent antioxidant properties. In fact, caffeic acid has been shown to effectively scavenge superoxide, thereby preventing oxidative damage and restoring the cellular redox balance [54]. The significant presence of caffeic acid in HECE further reinforces the extract’s antioxidant efficacy, making it a promising candidate for combating oxidative stress-induced damage. To validate these observations, we performed similar tests in neutrophils, the primary source of O2•− via the NADPH oxidase system [55]. As expected, O2•− levels were significantly reduced in neutrophils pretreated with HECE, as evidenced by the reduction in cytochrome C levels. These results are consistent with our previous findings and highlight the potential of HECE to mitigate oxidative stress, reinforcing its relevance in the prevention of oxidative stress-related diseases such as cardiovascular [56], inflammatory [57], and digestive disorders [48]. In addition to targeting O2•−, we studied H2O2, another important ROS molecule commonly produced in cells, notably by NADPH oxidase activity, which plays a key role in cell signalling pathways [58]. However, at elevated concentrations, H2O2 can cause significant cellular damage by oxidizing lipids, DNA, and proteins. Moreover, it can participate in Fenton or Haber-Weiss reactions [59], leading to the generation of hydroxyl radicals, which are highly damaging to cells [60]. To evaluate the effect of HECE on H2O2, we measured its H2O2 scavenging activity via a cell-free H2O2/HRPO system. Our results revealed that HECE strongly inhibits H2O2 generation. This result suggests that the bioactive compounds in HECE are capable of either directly neutralizing H2O2 by acting as scavengers of oxidative species or modulating the activity of the NADPH oxidase system. This finding is in line with numerous studies in the literature that highlight the role of compounds such as quercetin and kaempferol in mitigating oxidative stress and protecting cells from ROS-induced damage by reducing H2O2 levels [61, 62]. Our western blot analysis results revealed that HECE significantly inhibited MPO but not NGAL expression, suggesting that HECE acts selectively on specific inflammatory mediators. The inhibition of MPO suggests that HECE has anti-inflammatory properties, specifically by targeting the oxidative pathways of neutrophils and hence MPO, which is a key enzyme in ROS production and in the amplification of the inflammatory response. This observed inhibitory effect may be attributed to the presence of bioactive flavonoids in the extract, such as quercetin and kaempferol, which have been widely reported in the literature to attenuate oxidative stress and downregulate MPO expression. These compounds are also known to influence the activity of transcription factors such as NF-κB, which are central to the regulation of proinflammatory genes [63–65]. The inhibition of this enzyme reduces ROS production, which is in line with our recent findings on aqueous extracts, revealing the high potential of Opuntia ficus-indica [17]. Compared with other plant extracts, the hydroethanolic cladode extract of Opuntia ficus-indica shows comparable efficacy in modulating the proinflammatory and pro-oxidant activities of neutrophils. Similar effects have been reported with extracts from Punica granatum [66], Syzygium aromaticum L. [67], and Ceratonia siliqua L. [68], which are known for their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Notably, regarding superoxide anion production, the effect of our extract was particularly strong compared with those of these other plant extracts. These findings further support the therapeutic potential of Opuntia ficus-indica as a natural agent that targets oxidative stress and inflammation.

Conclusions

The current study provided a phytochemical characterization of the hydroethanolic cladode extract of Opuntia ficus-indica and demonstrated its significant biological activities. The extract effectively modulates neutrophil functions and restores ROS homeostasis through the regulation of key signalling pathways involved in oxidative stress and inflammation. These findings highlight the potential of HECE as a promising natural agent in immunopharmacology and oxidative stress management. Future research should focus on in vivo validation of these effects and detailed investigations of the intracellular signalling pathways involved to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the bioactivity of HECE.
