Blow-up of the one-dimensional wave equation with quadratic spatial derivative nonlinearity
Tej-eddine Ghoul, Jie Liu, Nader Masmoudi

Abstract
We investigate the blow-up dynamics of smooth solutions to the one-dimensional wave equation with a quadratic spatial derivative nonlinearity, motivated by its applications in Effective Field Theory (EFT) in cosmology. Despite its relevance, explicit blow-up solutions for this equation have not been documented in the literature. In this work, we establish the non-existence of smooth, exact self-similar blow-up solutions and construct a five-parameter family of generalized self-similar solutions exhibiting logarithmic growth. Moreover, we prove the asymptotic stability of these blow-up solutions. Our proof tackles several significant challenges, including the non-self-adjoint nature of the linearized operator, the presence of unstable eigenvalues, and, most notably, the treatment of non-compact perturbations. By substantially advancing Donninger’s spectral-theoretic framework, we develop a robust methodology that effectively handles non-compact perturbations. Key innovations include the incorporation of the Lorentz transformation in self-similar variables, an adaptation of the functional framework introduced in [ 35 ] , and a novel resolvent estimate. This approach is general and robust, allowing for straightforward extensions to higher dimensions and applications to a wide range of nonlinear equations.



1
Introduction

We consider the following one-dimensional wave equation with quadratic spatial derivative nonlinearity

\displaystyle u_{tt}-u_{xx}=(u_{x})^{2},

(1.1)

where
u:\mathbb{R}^{1+1}\to\mathbb{R}
is the unknown function. This equation arises from Effective Field Theory (EFT) applications in cosmology, particularly in studying the Universe’s late-time accelerated expansion and dark energy behavior
[
22
]
. In this context, nonlinear terms like
(u_{x})^{2}
are often used to capture nonlinear evolution in cosmic structures
[
27
]
. Numerical simulations reveal that such nonlinearities can lead to finite-time blow-up, where the solution diverges in finite time. Without a Laplacian term, this blow-up typically takes the form of a “V-type” singularity, characterized by rapid local curvature increase
[
22
,
27
]
. However, when a Laplacian term is added, the divergence shifts to an “M-type” blow-up, where steepened edges form caustic-like structures as they propagate outward
[
22
]
. This study of singularity types in different conditions provides essential insights into the limitations of EFT models under extreme cosmic conditions and supports theoretical exploration of high-energy scale evolution in cosmic structures.

Previous studies of Eq. (
1.1
) have primarily focused on the lifespan of local solutions and the conditions under which blow-up solutions exist. For investigations in higher-dimensional settings, one may refer to
[
46
,
47
,
49
]
, while
[
22
,
48
]
addresses the one-dimensional case relevant to our study. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no explicit blow-up solutions or comprehensive qualitative results on the blow-up dynamics of Eq. (
1.1
) available in the existing literature. Our aim of this paper is to fill this gap.

1.1
Main results

Unlike the wave equation with power nonlinearities
[
16
,
38
]
, Eq. (
1.1
) does not admit ODE blow-up solutions. By scaling, the self-similar ansatz for the nonlinear wave equation (
1.1
) takes the form

\displaystyle u(t,x)=U\left(y\right),\quad\ y=\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t},\quad T>0,\ x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.

Under this ansatz, exact self-similar solutions satisfy the equation

\displaystyle 2y\partial_{y}U+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}U=(\partial_{y}U)^{2}.

In the following, we establish that there are no smooth, exact self-similar blow-up solutions to (
1.1
). Furthermore, no smooth solitary waves exist for (
1.1
). Specifically, all stationary solutions to (
1.1
) are of the form

u(x)=-\log(c_{1}+x)+c_{2},\quad c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R},

which becomes singular for
x\leq-c_{1}
. This suggests that type-II blow-up solutions may also not exist.

Nonetheless, we construct a new five-parameter family of smooth “generalized” self-similar blow-up solutions to (
1.1
). These solutions exhibit logarithmic growth and are expressed as

\displaystyle u(t,x)=U\left(s,y\right)=\alpha s+\tilde{U}(y),\quad s=-\log(T-t)+\log(T),\ y=\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t},\ \alpha\in\mathbb{R}.

The first main result of this paper is as follows.

Theorem 1.1

(Existence of smooth generalized self-similar blow-up solutions)
.

1)

For any
T>0
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, there are no smooth exact self-similar blow-up solutions to (
1.1
) in the backward lightcone

\Gamma(T,x_{0}):=\left\{(t,x)\in\left[0,T\right)\times\mathbb{R}:\left|x-x_{0}\right|\leq T-t\right\}.

2)

There exists a five-parameter family of smooth generalized self-similar blow-up solutions to (
1.1
) with logarithmic growth

\displaystyle u_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}=-\alpha\log\left(1-\frac{t}{T}\right)+\tilde{u}_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}(t,x)

well defined in the backward lightcone
\Gamma(T,x_{0})
for all
T>0,\kappa,x_{0}\in\mathbb{R},
and either
0<\beta<\infty,\sqrt{1+\alpha}\in\mathbb{N},\alpha\neq 0
or
\beta=0,\infty,\alpha>0.

Here,
\tilde{u}_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa-\alpha\log\sqrt{1+\alpha}+\int_{0}^{\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t}}\frac{\alpha(1+z)^{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}-2\alpha^{2}\sqrt{1+\alpha}\beta(1-z)^{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}}{(1+z)^{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}(\sqrt{1+\alpha}-z)+2\alpha\sqrt{1+\alpha}\beta(1-z)^{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+z)}dz.

In particular, we have

\displaystyle u_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa,T,x_{0}}=-\alpha\log\left(1-\frac{t}{T}\right)-\alpha\log\left(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t}\right)+\kappa,

\displaystyle u_{\alpha,0,\kappa,T,x_{0}}=-\alpha\log\left(1-\frac{t}{T}\right)-\alpha\log\left(\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t}\right)+\kappa.

(Other choices of parameters)

For

also solves Eq. (
1.1

By the finite propagation speed of wave equations and a cut-off of

Remark 1.4

(Free parameters and symmetries)

Parameters
\kappa,T,x_{0}
corresponds to different symmetries of the equation (
1.1

, time

, and space

.

Remark 1.5

(Interpretation as exact self-similar blow-up solutions)

The generalized self-similar blow-up solutions above can be regarded as

self-similar blow-up solutions to a transformed version of (

Remark 1.6

(Comparison with numerical simulations)
.

The logarithmic growth exhibited by the profile
u_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}
has not been observed in prior numerical simulations
[

]
. While numerical methods are invaluable tools, they may sometimes overlook subtle aspects of blow-up solutions. In particular, numerical observations of M-type blow-up typically capture the blow-up behavior at the first blow-up time

40

41

for a similar phenomenon observed in one-dimensional semilinear nonlinear wave equations with power nonlinearities.

Let
k_{\alpha}=\lceil\sqrt{1+\alpha}\rceil
denote the smallest integer that is greater than or equal to
\sqrt{1+\alpha}
. The second main result of this paper establishes the asymptotic stability of the generalized self-similar blow-up solutions

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\alpha_{0},T_{0}>0,\kappa_{0},x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
k\geq k_{\alpha_{0}}+3
. For all
0<\delta<1
, there exists a constant
\epsilon>0
such that for any real-valued functions

such that the bounds

\displaystyle(T^{*}-t)^{-\frac{1}{2}+s}\|u(t,\cdot)-u_{\alpha^{*},\infty,\kappa^{*},T^{*},x_{0}}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}(B_{T^{*}-t}(x_{0}))}\lesssim(T^{*}-t)^{1-\delta},

for

\displaystyle(T^{*}-t)^{-\frac{1}{2}+s}\|\partial_{t}u(t,\cdot)-\partial_{t}u_{\alpha^{*},\infty,\kappa^{*},T^{*},x_{0}}\|_{\dot{H}^{s-1}(B_{T^{*}-t}(x_{0}))}\lesssim(T^{*}-t)^{1-\delta},

\displaystyle|\alpha^{*}-\alpha_{0}|+|\kappa^{*}-\kappa_{0}|+\left|\frac{T^{*}}{T_{0}}-1\right|\lesssim\epsilon.

Remark 1.8
.

The same result also holds for
u_{\alpha,0,\kappa,T,x_{0}}
. Indeed, we have

u_{\alpha,0,\kappa,T,x_{0}}(t,x)=u_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa,T,-x_{0}}(t,-x),

and any solution

to Eq. (
1.1
) implies that
u(t,-x)
also solves Eq. (
1.1
).

Remark 1.9
.

For
0<\beta<\infty
and
\sqrt{1+\alpha}\in\mathbb{N},\alpha>0
, we conjecture that
u_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}
are only co-dimensionally stable. For instance, when
\alpha=3,\beta=\frac{1}{12}
, the following explicit solution

u_{3,\frac{1}{12},\kappa,T,x_{0}}=-3\log\left(1-\frac{t}{T}\right)+\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t}\right)^{2}+\kappa

solves Eq. (

) around

reveals the presence of two additional unstable eigenvalues, i.e. eigenvalues with positive real part, which not associated with any symmetries of the equation. Consequently, we expect that, for

u_{\alpha,0,\kappa,T,x_{0}}
or
u_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa,T,x_{0}}

u_{\alpha,0,\kappa,T,x_{0}}

1.3
Outline of the proof

We outline the key difficulties and new ideas involved in the proof of Theorem
1.7
. The stability analysis of the generalized self-similar blow-up solutions presents three primary challenges that significantly complicate the argument.

1.

Non-existence of dissipative energy functionals

\Longrightarrow

direct energy methods fail
. In their seminal works
[
38
,
42
,
43
]
on the stability of self-similar solutions to sub-conformal nonlinear wave equations, Merle and Zaag rely heavily on the existence of a Lyapunov energy functional that satisfies the following dissipative property:

\displaystyle\frac{dE[\eta]}{ds}=-\frac{4}{p-1}\int_{-1}^{1}(\partial_{s}\eta)^{2}\frac{\rho}{1-y^{2}}dy\leq 0,

where
E[\eta]
is an appropriately chosen Lyapunov functional,
p>1
, and
\rho\geq 0
is a weight function. However, in our setting, such a Lyapunov structure does not exist due to the non-Hamiltonian nature of the nonlinearity. Specifically, the natural energy estimate for the linearized equation (
3.1
) around
U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}
yields

\displaystyle\frac{dE[\eta]}{ds}=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{2\alpha y}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}(\partial_{s}\eta)^{2}\frac{\rho}{1-y^{2}}dy,

where the term
2\alpha y
has indefinite signs on the interval
[-1,1]
, thus preventing the energy from dissipating. This fundamental difference renders direct energy methods ineffective in our analysis.

2.

Non-self-adjoint structure of the linearized operator with unstable eigenvalues
0
and
1

\Longrightarrow

traditional spectral methods fail
. The stability analysis of self-similar blow-up solutions often involves non-self-adjoint operators with unstable eigenvalues, which arise naturally from the symmetries of the equation. This poses significant challenges, as discussed in
[
38
,
10
,
12
]
. In our case, the linearized operator has unstable eigenvalues 0 and 1, making traditional spectral methods ineffective. This limitation arises because the spectral decomposition for non-self-adjoint operators is inherently more complex and lacks the orthogonality properties of self-adjoint operators, preventing the clear separation of stable and unstable modes needed for decay estimates. Furthermore, this difficulty explains the lack of dissipative energy functionals, as noted earlier. The unstable components of the energy cannot be effectively isolated, which prevents energy functionals from exhibiting dissipative properties.

3.

Non-compact perturbations

\Longrightarrow

Losing information about the essential spectrum
. Compared to nonlinear wave equations with power nonlinearities, an additional difficulty in our setting arises from the non-compact perturbations introduced by the nonlinear term. Specifically, the linearized operator can be expressed as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1},\quad\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\
q_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
2\partial_{y}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}\partial_{y}q_{1}\end{pmatrix},

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}\mathbf{q}:=\begin{pmatrix}-y\partial_{y}q_{1}+q_{2}\\
\partial_{yy}q_{1}-q_{2}-y\partial_{y}q_{2}\end{pmatrix}

is the free wave operator and
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
is the linearized term arising from the nonlinearity, see (
3
). Notably, in our setting,
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
constitutes only a bounded perturbation in
H^{k+1}\times H^{k}
, yet it lacks compactness relative to
\mathbf{L}
due to the presence of the derivative term and the degeneracy of
\mathbf{L}
on the light cone
y=\pm 1
. Specifically, if
\mathbf{q}
and
\mathbf{L}\mathbf{q}
are bounded in
H^{k+1}\times H^{k}
, the definition only allows one to deduce
(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}q_{1}\in H^{k}
, rather than
\partial_{yy}q_{1}\in H^{k}
. This implies that no additional regularity for
\partial_{y}q_{1}
can be gained at
y=\pm 1
. Consequently,
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
is not relatively compact with respect to
\mathbf{L}
. This degeneracy is also evident from the scalar form of the linearized equation (
3.1
), which highlights the singular behavior of the free wave operator at
y=\pm 1
. Additionally, unlike the Skyrme model studied in
[
2
]
, our problem lacks structural properties that could eliminate the derivative term.

This lack of compactness results in a complete loss of information about the essential spectrum, as the essential spectrum of the linearized operator is no longer preserved under non-compact perturbations
[
29
]
, which in turn leads to the breakdown of Donninger’s standard spectral-theoretic framework. Even if spectral information were accessible, a significant challenge would remain in translating it into growth bounds for the corresponding semigroup. As highlighted in
[
2
]
, none of the soft arguments commonly used in previous studies can be applied to the non-compact case.

To address these challenges, we advance Donninger’s spectral-theoretic framework for non-self-adjoint operators, originally developed by Donninger for wave maps
[
10
,
18
]
. For an overview of this framework, we refer to the introductory note
[
12
]
. To illustrate, we briefly outline the main steps of Donninger’s approach below. Consider an abstract Cauchy problem written in the operator form

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}),\quad\mathbf{q}(0,\cdot)=\mathbf{q}_{0},

where
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
is the linearized operator and
\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q})
represents the nonlinear term.

Step 1

Establish the mode stability of the linearized operator by excluding all unstable eigenvalues except
\{0,1\}
. This reduces to solving the eigenvalue problem
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}=\lambda\mathbf{q}
,
\operatorname{Re}\lambda\geq 0
;

Step 2

Prove the generation of a semigroup
\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)
associated with the linearized operator
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
and characterize its essential spectrum. The key idea is to decompose the linearized operator into a combination of a maximally dissipative operator (i.e., the free wave operator) and a compact perturbation, given by
{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
;

Step 3

Establish linear stability within the stable subspace. This is achieved by isolating the unstable modes using the Riesz projection
\mathbf{P}_{\lambda,\alpha}:=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma_{\lambda}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}}(z)dz
where
\gamma_{\lambda}
is a suitable contour for
\lambda=0,1
.
The growth bound of the semigroup is then derived using uniform resolvent estimates;

Step 4

Prove nonlinear stability by applying modulation techniques or the Lyapunov-Perron method, based on the following semigroup formulation

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}(s,\cdot)=\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{q}(0,\cdot)+\int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s-\tau)N(\mathbf{q}(\tau))d\tau.

This spectral-theoretic framework is highly effective for analyzing non-self-adjoint operators with unstable eigenvalues and has been successfully applied to prove the stability of ODE blow-up solutions for nonlinear wave equations with power nonlinearities
[
15
,
16
,
51
,
45
]
. However, several new challenges arise in Steps 1–3 within our setting. Specifically,

•

In Step 1, the eigen-equation for the eigenvalue
\lambda
is given by

\displaystyle(\lambda^{2}+\lambda)\varphi+\left((2\lambda+2)y+\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\right)\partial_{y}\varphi+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}\varphi=0,

(1.2)

which is a Heun type ODE with four regular singular points at
\pm 1,-\sqrt{1+\alpha},\infty
. The existence of smooth solutions to such ODEs remains widely open, commonly referred to as the “connection problem”
[
12
]
. In contrast, in the context of ODE blow-up stability
[
15
,
16
,
51
,
45
]
, the corresponding ODE has only three regular singular points, making it a hypergeometric ODE. The connection problem for hypergeometric ODEs has already been solved in the literature; see, for example,
[
44
]
. To solve (
1.2
), one possible approach is to employ the ODE techniques developed by Cosin, Glogic, and Donninger
[
5
,
4
,
25
,
26
]
. However, such ODE analysis are highly problem-specific and technically delicate. To circumvent these challenges, we introduce a transformation inspired by the Lorentz transformation in self-similar variables:

\displaystyle\psi(y^{\prime})=\left(\frac{1-\gamma y^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}\right)^{-\lambda}\varphi\left(\frac{y^{\prime}-\gamma}{1-\gamma y^{\prime}}\right),\quad\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}.

This transformation reduces (
1.2
) to a hypergeometric ODE:

\displaystyle(\lambda^{2}-\lambda)\psi+\left(2\lambda y^{\prime}+2\sqrt{1+\alpha}\right)\partial_{y^{\prime}}\psi+(y^{\prime 2}-1)\partial_{y^{\prime}y^{\prime}}\psi=0.

For further details, see Section
3.2
.

•

In Step 2, as mentioned above, the non-compactness of the perturbation
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
leads to a loss of information about the essential spectrum, which in turn causes the breakdown of Donninger’s spectral-theoretic framework. Since
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
is merely a bounded operator, it becomes necessary to incorporate
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
into the free wave operator
\mathbf{L}
and identify the intrinsic dissipative structure of the full linearized operator
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
. This is achieved by establishing the following stronger dissipation estimate for higher derivatives:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\langle\langle\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle\rangle_{k}\leq-\left(k-\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\left\|\mathbf{q}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}+c_{\epsilon,k,\alpha}\left\|\mathbf{q}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k-1}},

(see Proposition
4.8
). Additionally, inspired by the abstract functional setting in
[
35
,
30
]
, we control the lower-order derivatives through a subcoercivity estimate (see Lemma
4.7
). By combining these two properties, we derive a new decomposition to recover compactness:

\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}=\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}+\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha},

where

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle\cdot,{\Pi}_{\alpha,i}\right\rangle_{k}{\Pi}_{\alpha,i},

is a finite rank projection operator and
\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
is maximally dissipative operator (see Proposition
4.8
). This new strategy for the spectral analysis of non-self-adjoint operators is robust and well-suited to handling non-compact perturbations of the free wave operator. Moreover, it provides a versatile framework that can potentially be applied to the study of self-similar solutions in other equations.

•

In Step 3, uniform resolvent estimates are crucial for translating spectral information into growth bounds for semigroups. Previous studies on uniform resolvent estimates, as noted in
[
2
]
, are limited to compact perturbations of the free wave operator. Instead of relying on soft arguments, our approach directly estimates the resolvent by establishing a lower bound for the inner product
\langle(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{k}
. The key innovation lies in decomposing the inner product into its real and imaginary parts. The real part allows us to control the highest-order norm using the aforementioned stronger dissipation of higher derivatives. The
L^{2}
norm is then controlled by exploiting the structure of the imaginary part (see Proposition
4.11
). By combining this direct resolvent estimate method with the improved spectral analysis strategy outlined above, we successfully address the limitations of Donninger’s spectral-theoretic framework for non-compact perturbations.

•

Furthermore, there are additional technical challenges that require careful consideration. For instance, due to the translation invariance of the profile
u
(i.e., if
u
is a solution to (
1.1
), then
u+\kappa
is also a solution), even the free wave operator in self-similar variables has an unstable eigenvalue
0
. To address this issue, we introduce a modified free wave operator, which is constructed by adding a compact perturbation to the original operator. This modification effectively removes the eigenvalue
0
and ensures the generation of the semigroup for the linearized operator
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
.

Another key aspect of our analysis is the existence of a generalized eigenfunction corresponding to the eigenvalue
0
(see Proposition
4.9
). This new unstable mode arises from the free parameter
\alpha
and reflects the non-self-adjoint nature of the linearized operator
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
. Consequently, the algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalue
0
exceeds its geometric multiplicity, adding complexity to the linear analysis (see Proposition
5.1
).

Finally, we emphasize that the approach developed in this paper is both general and robust. Its flexibility allows for straightforward extensions to higher-dimensional settings, providing a natural framework for analyzing similar nonlinear wave equations in diverse contexts, as long as the self-similar solution is explicit. Moreover, the methodology is not limited to the specific equation studied here; it is adaptable to a broad class of nonlinear models with analogous structures. These features make it a powerful tool for investigating complex blow-up dynamics and stability properties in various applications. While this paper focuses on the one-dimensional case, we plan to explore specific higher-dimensional examples and other nonlinear models in future work, further demonstrating the adaptability of our approach.

is devoted to establishing the mode stability of the generalized self-similar solutions. In Section
4
, we perform a detailed spectral analysis of the linearized operator, which constitutes a central part of our proof. To address the non-compact perturbation arising from the derivative nonlinear term, we develop a new decomposition of the linearized operator to restore compactness (see Section
4.3
). We then derive the uniform resolvent estimate in Section
4.5
. In Section
5
, we establish the linearized stability along the stable subspace. Finally, in Section
6
, we prove the nonlinear stability of the solutions using the Lyapunov-Perron method.

1.5
Notation

In this paper, we adopt the following notations. The open interval
(x_{0}-r,x_{0}+r)
centered at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is denoted by
B_{r}(x_{0})
, with
B_{r}=B_{r}(0),B=B_{1}
. The closure of
B_{r}(x_{0})
is denoted by
\overline{B_{r}(x_{0})}=[x_{0}-r,x_{0}+r]
. For
T>0,x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, the backward light cone is defined as

\Gamma(T,x_{0}):=\left\{(t,x)\in\left[0,T\right)\times\mathbb{R}:\left|x-x_{0}\right|\leq T-t\right\}.

For
a,b\in\mathbb{R},
we use the notation
a\lesssim b
to mean that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
a\leq Cb
, and
a\lesssim_{\alpha}b
indicates that the constant
C
depends on
\alpha
.

For functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},y\mapsto f(y)
, we denote the first derivative by
f^{\prime}=\partial_{y}f=\partial f
and the
i
-th order derivative by
\partial_{y}^{i}f=\partial^{i}f
. For a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
, the set
C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
consists of all smooth functions on
\Omega
whose derivatives are continuous up to the boundary of
\Omega
. If
\Omega
is bounded, we define the inhomogeneous Sobolev norm and homogeneous Sobolev seminorm for
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
as

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{k}(\Omega)}=\left(\sum_{0\leq i\leq k}\left\|\partial^{i}f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k}(\Omega)}=\left\|\partial^{k}f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

for
f\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
, respectively. The Sobolev space
H^{k}(\Omega)
is then defined as the completion of
C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega)}
with respect to the inhomogeneous Sobolev norm. For
k\geq 0
, we define
\mathcal{H}^{k}=H^{k+1}(-1,1)\times H^{k}(-1,1)
.

We use boldface notation for tuples of functions, for example:

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}\equiv\left(f_{1},f_{2}\right)\equiv\left[\begin{array}[]{l}f_{1}\\
f_{2}\end{array}\right]\quad\text{ or }\quad\mathbf{q}(t,.)\equiv\left(q_{1}(t,.),q_{2}(t,.)\right)\equiv\left[\begin{array}[]{l}q_{1}(t,.)\\
q_{2}(t,.)\end{array}\right].

Linear operators that act on tuples of functions are also displayed in boldface notation. For a closed linear operator
\mathbf{L}
on a Banach space, we denote its domain by
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L})
, its spectrum by
\sigma(\mathbf{L})
, and its point spectrum by
\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{L})
. The resolvent operator is denoted by
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}}(z):=(z-\mathbf{L})^{-1}
for
z\in\rho(\mathbf{L})=\mathbb{C}\backslash\sigma(\mathbf{L})
. The space of bounded operators on a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})
.

For details on the spectral theory of linear operators, we refer to
[
29
]
. The theory of strongly continuous operator semigroups is covered in the textbook
[
23
]
.

2
Construction of smooth blow-up solutions

In this section, we prove that all exact self-similar blow-up solutions to Eq. (
1.1
) are singular inside the backward lightcone. Then, we derive our construction of smooth generalized blow-up solutions.

2.1
Non-existence of smooth self-similar solutions

Eq. (
1.1
) is invariant under the scaling transformation

\displaystyle u\mapsto u^{\lambda}\quad\text{with~}\quad u^{\lambda}(t,x)=u\left(\frac{t}{\lambda},\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)

for any
\lambda>0
. For any
T>0,x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, the self-similar ansatz of the nonlinear wave equation (
1.1
) is

\displaystyle u(t,x)=U\left(s,y\right),

where

s=-\log(T-t)+\log(T)=-\log\left(1-\frac{t}{T}\right),\quad\text{and~}y=\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t}.

In terms of the self-similar variables
s
and
y
, Eq. (
1.1
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\partial_{ss}U+\partial_{s}U+2y\partial_{y}\partial_{s}U+2y\partial_{y}U+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}U=(\partial_{y}U)^{2}.

(2.1)

We first consider the exact self-similar solutions, i.e. stationary solutions
U=U(y)
to Eq. (
2.1
), satisfying

\displaystyle 2y\partial_{y}U+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}U=(\partial_{y}U)^{2},

(2.2)

which is equivalent to

\partial_{y}((y^{2}-1)\partial_{y}U)=(\partial_{y}U)^{2}.

Regarding the above equation as a first-order ODE, we can solve (
2.2
) explicitly as

\displaystyle\partial_{y}U=\left(c(y^{2}-1)+\frac{1}{4}\left(2y+(y^{2}-1)\log\left|\frac{y-1}{y+1}\right|\right)\right)^{-1}\text{~or~}0,

where
c\in\mathbb{R}
is an arbitrary constant. We find that all non-trivial solutions have a singular point inside the light cone
|y|<1
. Indeed, denote the denominator

p_{c}(y):=c(y^{2}-1)+\frac{1}{4}\left(2y+(y^{2}-1)\log\left|\frac{y-1}{y+1}\right|\right),

then we have

p_{c}(-1)=-\frac{1}{2},\quad p_{c}(1)=\frac{1}{2}.

By the Intermediate Value Theorem, there exists a point
y_{0}\in(-1,1)
such that
p_{c}(y_{0})=0
which is a singular point of
\partial_{y}U
.

2.2
Smooth generalized self-similar blow-up solutions with a log growth

Instead of taking exact self-similar ansatz
U=U(y)
, we now consider the following generalized self-similar profiles

\displaystyle u(t,x)=U(s,y)=\alpha s+\tilde{U}(y).

(2.3)

for any non-zero constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, which allows for a linear growth in
s
. Note that this introduces a logarithmic growth in
t
since
s=-\log\left(1-\frac{t}{T}\right)
. Then,
\tilde{U}
satisfies

\displaystyle 2y\partial_{y}\tilde{U}+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}\tilde{U}+\alpha=(\partial_{y}\tilde{U})^{2}.

(2.4)

Fortunately, we find that for any
\alpha>0
, there exist regular solutions to (
2.4
) inside the light cone. Actually, let
\tilde{U}=a\log(b\pm y)
, we can solve (
2.4
) for

a=-\alpha,\quad b=\sqrt{1+\alpha},

i.e.

\tilde{U}=-\alpha\log(\sqrt{1+\alpha}\pm y).

Since (
2.4
) is a Riccati type equation, we can further obtain all solutions to (
2.4
) as

\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}=-\alpha\log(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y)+W_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa},\quad y\in[-1,1],

where

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{y}W_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}&=\left(\frac{1+y}{1-y}\right)^{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}\frac{1}{(y+\sqrt{1+\alpha})^{2}}\left(\beta+\int_{-1}^{y}\frac{(1+z)^{\sqrt{1+\alpha}-1}}{(1-z)^{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+1}}\frac{1}{(z+\sqrt{1+\alpha})^{2}}dz\right)^{-1}\\
&=\frac{2\alpha\sqrt{1+\alpha}}{(\sqrt{1+\alpha}-y)(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y)}\left(\beta\left(\frac{1-y}{1+y}\right)^{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}\frac{2\alpha\sqrt{1+\alpha}(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y)}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}-y}+1\right)^{-1}\end{aligned}

(2.5)

and

\displaystyle W_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}(0)=\kappa.

The parameter
\beta
is given by

\beta=\frac{1}{(1+\alpha)\partial_{y}W_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}(0)}-\frac{1}{2\alpha\sqrt{1+\alpha}},

i.e.

\partial_{y}W_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}(0)=\frac{2\alpha}{2\alpha(1+\alpha)\beta+\sqrt{1+\alpha}}.

Moreover, we have

•

When
\beta=0,

\tilde{U}_{\alpha,0,\kappa}=-\alpha\log(\sqrt{1+\alpha}-y)+\kappa;

•

When
\beta=+\infty,

\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}=-\alpha\log(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y)+\kappa;

Thus, for
\alpha>0
, both
\tilde{U}_{\alpha,0,\kappa}
and
\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}
are smooth inside the light cone. However, when
0<\beta<\infty,\alpha>0
,
\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}
is smooth only if
\sqrt{1+\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}
. This gives countable families of smooth blow-up profiles
\tilde{U}_{n^{2}-1,\beta,\kappa},0<\beta<\infty
for
n\geq 2,n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Thus, we obtain the following smooth generalized self-similar profiles

U_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}(s,y)=\alpha s+\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}.

Going back to the original variables
t,x
, we have

\displaystyle u_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}(t,x)=-\alpha\log\left(1-\frac{t}{T}\right)-\alpha\log\left(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t}\right)+w_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}(t,x),

(2.6)

where

\displaystyle w_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}(t,x)=W_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}(s,y)

\displaystyle=\kappa+\int_{0}^{\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t}}\frac{2\alpha\sqrt{1+\alpha}}{(\sqrt{1+\alpha}-z)(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+z)}\left(\beta\left(\frac{1-z}{1+z}\right)^{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}\frac{2\alpha\sqrt{1+\alpha}(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+z)}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}-z}+1\right)^{-1}dz.

Denote
\tilde{u}_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}(t,x):=-\alpha\log\left(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t}\right)+w_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}(t,x),
we have

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa-\alpha\log\sqrt{1+\alpha}+\int_{0}^{\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t}}\frac{\alpha(1+z)^{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}-2\alpha^{2}\sqrt{1+\alpha}\beta(1-z)^{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}}{(1+z)^{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}(\sqrt{1+\alpha}-z)+2\alpha\sqrt{1+\alpha}\beta(1-z)^{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+z)}dz.

2.3
Interpretation of generalized self-similar blow-up solutions

In this subsection, we demonstrate that the generalized self-similar blow-up solutions (
2.6
) can be regarded as
exact
self-similar blow-up solutions to a transformed version of (
1.1
) obtained by taking the spatial derivative. More precisely, let
h=u_{x}
, then

\displaystyle h_{tt}-h_{xx}=2hh_{x}.

(2.7)

Consider the corresponding self-similar ansatz

\displaystyle h(t,x)=\frac{1}{T-t}H\left(s,y\right),\quad s=-\log(T-t)+\log(T),\ y=\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t}.

Then,
H
satisfies

\partial_{ss}H+3\partial_{s}H+2y\partial_{y}\partial_{s}H+4y\partial_{y}H+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}H+2H=2H\partial_{y}H.

(2.8)

The stationary solutions to (
2.8
) satisfy

\displaystyle 4y\partial_{y}H+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}H+2H=2H\partial_{y}H.

(2.9)

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\partial_{y}((y^{2}-1)\partial_{y}H+2yH)=\partial_{y}H^{2}.

Thus,

\displaystyle(y^{2}-1)\partial_{y}H+2yH+\alpha=H^{2}.

This goes back to the equation (
2.4
). Using the same method, we can solve (
2.9
) as

\displaystyle H_{\alpha,\beta}(y)=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\frac{1-2\alpha\sqrt{1+\alpha}\beta\left(\frac{1-y}{1+y}\right)^{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}}{\frac{\sqrt{1+\alpha}-y}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}+2\alpha\sqrt{1+\alpha}\beta\left(\frac{1-y}{1+y}\right)^{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}},\quad y\in[-1,1].

(2.10)

Here
H_{\alpha,\beta}(y)
is always continuous inside the light cone for all
\alpha>0,\beta\geq 0
, but
H_{\alpha,\beta}(y)
does not belong to
C^{k}[-1,1]
for
k>\sqrt{1+\alpha}
, when
\sqrt{1+\alpha}\notin\mathbb{N}
and
0<\beta<\infty
.
And for each fixed
y\in[-1,1]
,
H_{\alpha,\beta}(y)
is monotone decreasing with respect to
\beta
, i.e.

H_{\alpha,\infty}(y)\leq H_{\alpha,\beta}(y)\leq H_{\alpha,0}(y).

In particular, when
\beta=0
,

H_{\alpha,0}(y)=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}-y};

when
\beta=\infty,

H_{\alpha,\infty}(y)=\frac{-\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}.

Note that for
0<\beta<\infty
,

\displaystyle\lim_{y\to-1}H_{\alpha,\beta}(y)=-1-\sqrt{1+\alpha}=H_{\alpha,\infty}(-1),

\displaystyle\lim_{y\to 1}H_{\alpha,\beta}(y)=1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}=H_{\alpha,0}(1).

Thus,
H_{\alpha,\beta}
connects to
H_{\alpha,0}
and
H_{\alpha,\infty}
at
-1
and
1
respectively.
Moreover, we have the following symmetry

\displaystyle H_{\alpha,\beta}(-y)=-H_{\alpha,\beta^{\prime}}(y),\quad\beta^{\prime}=\frac{1}{4\alpha^{2}(1+\alpha)\beta}.

In particular, when
\beta_{*}=\frac{1}{2\alpha\sqrt{1+\alpha}}
,
H_{\alpha,\beta_{*}}
is odd,
H_{\alpha,0}(-y)=-H_{\alpha,\infty}(y)
.

3
Mode stability

In this section, we take the first step in studying the linear stability of the smooth generalized self-similar blow-up solutions
U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}
. Specifically, we aim to establish the mode stability of the linearized equation at
U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}
, demonstrating that the linearized operator has no unstable eigenvalues other than
0
and
1
, which arise from the symmetries of (
1.1
).

Let
U=U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}+\eta
, then by (
2.1
) the corresponding linearized equation of
\eta
reads

\displaystyle\partial_{ss}\eta+\partial_{s}\eta+2y\partial_{y}\partial_{s}\eta+\left(2y-2\partial_{y}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}\right)\partial_{y}\eta+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}\eta=0.

(3.1)

Denote
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},q_{2})^{T}=(\eta,\partial_{s}\eta+y\partial_{y}\eta)^{T}
, then we can rewrite (
3.1
) as a first-order PDE system

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}q_{1}\\
q_{2}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-y\partial_{y}q_{1}+q_{2}\\
-q_{2}-y\partial_{y}q_{2}+\partial_{yy}q_{1}+2\partial_{y}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}\partial_{y}q_{1}\end{array}\right)=:\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}q_{1}\\
q_{2}\end{array}\right),

where the potential

\partial_{y}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}(s,y)=-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}

is independent of time
s
. Note that the linearized operator
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
above does not depend on the parameters
\kappa
,
T
, and
x_{0}
, and it is non-self-adjoint, which makes the stability analysis of (
3
) difficult.

3.1
Unstable eigenvalues induced by symmetries

We postpone the rigorous functional setup of the linearized operator
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
to the next section, and regard
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
as a formal differential operator. We first study the possible unstable eigenvalues of
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
as a step towards proving its mode stability.

Definition 3.1
.

\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is called an eigenvalue of
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
if there exists a nontrivial smooth function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}[-1,1]
such that

\displaystyle(\lambda^{2}+\lambda)\varphi+\left((2\lambda+2)y+\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\right)\partial_{y}\varphi+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}\varphi=0.

(3.8)

We call an eigenvalue
\lambda
is unstable if its real part is non-negative, i.e.
\operatorname{Re}\lambda\geq 0
. Otherwise, we call
\lambda
a stable eigenvalue.

Actually, if
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}=\lambda\mathbf{q}
for some
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},q_{2})^{T}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
, then
q_{1}
satisfies (
3.8
) and
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
. Conversely, if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
with a smooth eigenfunction
\varphi(y)
, then
\mathbf{q}:=(\varphi,\lambda\varphi+y\partial_{y}\varphi)^{T}
satisfies
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}=\lambda\mathbf{q}
.

Remark 3.2
.

It is equivalent to define eigenvalues of
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
such that the corresponding eigenfunctions are only in the Sobolev space
H^{k+1}(-1,1)
for
k\geq k_{\alpha}+2
. This is due to the fact that the eigen-equation (
3.8
) is a second-order elliptic equation whose solutions are always
C^{\infty}
smooth in
(-1,1)
. Moreover, we shall prove that any
H^{k+1}(-1,1)
solution to (
3.8
) is also smooth (and even analytical) at the end points
\pm 1
, see Proposition
A.1
in the Appendix.

First, the symmetries of Eq. (
2.1
) give us some explicit unstable eigenvalues. More precisely, if
U(s,y)
is a solution, then the following transformations of
U(s,y)
are also solutions to (
2.1
):

•

(Space translation in
x
) for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
, the function

\displaystyle U_{1}(s,y)=U\left(s,y+ae^{s}\right);

•

(Time translation in
t
) for any
b\in\mathbb{R}
, the function

\displaystyle U_{2}(s,y)=U\left(s-\log\left(1+be^{s}\right),\frac{y}{1+be^{s}}\right);

•

(Time translation in
s
) for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
, the function

\displaystyle U_{3}(s,y)=U(s+c,y);

•

(Translation in
u
) for any
d\in\mathbb{R}
, the function

\displaystyle U_{4}(s,y)=U(s,y)+d;

•

(Scaling) for any
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, the function

\displaystyle U_{5}(s,y)=U\left(\frac{y}{1+(\mu-1)Te^{s}},s-\log\left(1+(\mu-1)Te^{s}\right)+\log(\mu)\right).

This invariance can be viewed as a composition of the time translation in
t
and
s
, with
b=(\mu-1)T
and
c=\log(\mu)
.

For generalized self-similar blow-up solutions

U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}(s,y)=\alpha s-\alpha\log(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y)+\kappa,

the parameter
\alpha
also gives another family of solutions to Eq. (
2.1
). Moreover, we observe that

•

\partial_{a}U_{1}|_{a=0}
and
\partial_{b}U_{2}|_{b=0}
generate the same 1d eigenspace of eigenvalue 1;

•

\partial_{c}U_{3}|_{c=0}
and
\partial_{d}U_{4}|_{d=0}
generate the same 1d eigenspace of eigenvalue 0;

•

\partial_{\alpha}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}
is a generalized eigenfunction of eigenvalue 0, i.e.
for
\mathbf{q}=(\partial_{\alpha}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa},y\partial_{y}\partial_{\alpha}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa})^{T}
,
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}=(1,0)^{T}\neq 0
and
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}^{2}\mathbf{q}=0
.

Remark 3.3
.

Note that when
\beta=\infty
,
\partial_{\beta}U_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}\equiv 0
; when
\beta=0
,
\partial_{\beta}U_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}
is singular at
y=-1
. Therefore, the parameter
\beta
does not generate an unstable eigenvalue of
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
.

In summary, the linearized operator
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
has at least two unstable eigenvalues,
0
and
1
, each with an explicit eigenfunction. Nevertheless, we introduce the following notion of mode stability, see
[
10
,
12
]
.

Definition 3.4

(Mode stability)
.

We say that the blow-up solution
U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}
is mode stable if the existence of a nontrivial smooth function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}[-1,1]
that satisfies Eq. (
3.8
) necessarily implies that
\lambda\in\{0,1\}
or
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0
.

Remark 3.5
.

In fact, we shall prove a stronger result than the mode stability above, replacing
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0
by
\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leq-1
. This could help us derive a uniform spectral gap, see Proposition
4.9
.

To study the mode stability of
U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}
, we need to solve Eq. (
3.8
), which can be rewritten as a Heun type ODE:

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime\prime}+\left[\frac{\lambda+\sqrt{1+\alpha}}{y-1}+\frac{\lambda-\sqrt{1+\alpha}}{y+1}+\frac{2}{y+\sqrt{1+\alpha}}\right]\varphi^{\prime}+\frac{\lambda^{2}+\lambda}{y^{2}-1}\varphi=0.

(3.9)

Note that Eq. (
3.9
) has four regular singular points
\pm 1,-\sqrt{1+\alpha}
and
\infty
. The standard form of a Heun type equation reads

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}(z)+\left[\frac{\gamma}{z}+\frac{\delta}{z-1}+\frac{\epsilon}{z-d}\right]g^{\prime}(z)+\frac{abz-c}{z(z-1)(z-d)}g(z)=0,

where
a,b,c,d,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon\in\mathbb{C}
and
\epsilon=a+b+1-\gamma-\delta.

Let
y=2z-1
, we can rewrite (
3.9
) as the standard form

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime\prime}+\left[\frac{\lambda-\sqrt{1+\alpha}}{z}+\frac{\lambda+\sqrt{1+\alpha}}{z-1}+\frac{2}{z+\frac{\sqrt{1+\alpha}-1}{2}}\right]\varphi^{\prime}+\frac{\lambda^{2}+\lambda}{z(z-1)}\varphi=0,

(3.10)

where we take
\gamma=\lambda-\sqrt{1+\alpha},\delta=\lambda+\sqrt{1+\alpha},d=-\frac{\sqrt{1+\alpha}-1}{2},a=\lambda,b=\lambda+1,c=-\frac{1}{2}(\lambda^{2}+\lambda)(\sqrt{1+\alpha}-1),\epsilon=2=\alpha+\beta+1-\gamma-\delta.

However, the existence of smooth solutions to such type of ODEs remains largely open, which is also known as “connection problem”
[
12
]
. We bypass this difficulty using the Lorentz transformation in self-similar variables to convert the Heun type ODE (
3.10
) to a new hypergeometric ODE, see (
3.23
).

3.2
Proof of the mode stability

The idea is motivated by our new observation that the blow-up solution
U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}
can be transformed to the ODE blow-up solution of a new derivative NLW equation using the Lorentz transformation. More precisely, we recall the Lorentz transformation and its inverse

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} t^{\prime}=\frac{t-\gamma x}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}},\\
x^{\prime}=\frac{x-\gamma t}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}},\end{aligned}\right.\quad\text{~and~}\left\{\begin{aligned} t=\frac{t^{\prime}+\gamma x^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}},\\
x=\frac{x^{\prime}+\gamma t^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}},\end{aligned}\right.

(3.11)

where
\gamma\in(-1,1)
. Let
v(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})=u(t,x)=u(t(t^{\prime},x^{\prime}),x(t^{\prime},x^{\prime}))
, then if
u
solves (
1.1
),
v(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})
satisfies

\displaystyle v_{t^{\prime}t^{\prime}}-v_{x^{\prime}x^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{1-\gamma^{2}}\left(v_{x^{\prime}}^{2}-2\gamma v_{x^{\prime}}v_{t^{\prime}}+\gamma^{2}v_{t^{\prime}}^{2}\right).

(3.12)

And under this transformation, the generalized self-similar blow-up solutions

\displaystyle u_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa,T,x_{0}}(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\alpha\log\left(1-\frac{t}{T}\right)-\alpha\log\left(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t}\right)+\kappa

\displaystyle=-\alpha\log\left(\sqrt{1+\alpha}(T-t)+x-x_{0}\right)+\kappa+\log(T)

correspond to

\displaystyle v_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa,T,x_{0}}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=-\alpha\log\left(\sqrt{1+\alpha}(T-t)+x-x_{0}\right)+\kappa+\log(T)

\displaystyle=-\alpha\log\left(\sqrt{1+\alpha}T-\frac{\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\gamma}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}t^{\prime}+\frac{1-\sqrt{1+\alpha}\gamma}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}x^{\prime}-x_{0}\right)+\kappa+\log(T).

Remark 3.10
.

According to the proof of Corollary
3.9
, the following transformation (induced by the Lorentz transformation
\mathcal{T}_{\gamma}
)

\displaystyle\psi(y^{\prime})=\left(\frac{1-\gamma y^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}\right)^{-\lambda}\varphi\left(\frac{y^{\prime}-\gamma}{1-\gamma y^{\prime}}\right),\quad\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}}

(3.22)

actually gives the transformation from the eigen-equation (

) to (

).

Thus, the mode stability of
U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}
is equivalent to the mode stability of
V_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}
. Instead of the Heun type ODE (
3.8
), the new eigen-equation (
3.21
) has only three singular points
\pm 1,\infty
, which is known as the
hypergeometric differential equation
, see
[
44
]
. Fortunately, the connection problem of the hypergeometric ODE can be solved which leads to a rigorous proof of the mode stability of

.

More precisely, recall that the standard form of the hypergeometric differential equation is given by

z^{\prime}(1-z^{\prime})\partial_{z^{\prime}z^{\prime}}\psi+\left(c-(a+b+1)z^{\prime}\right)\partial_{z^{\prime}}\psi-ab\psi=0,

where
a,b,c
are constants. Let
y^{\prime}=2z^{\prime}-1
, Eq. (
3.21
) can be rewritten as the following standard hypergeometric ODE

\displaystyle z^{\prime}(1-z^{\prime})\partial_{z^{\prime}z^{\prime}}\psi+\left((\lambda-\sqrt{1+\alpha})-2\lambda z^{\prime}\right)\partial_{z^{\prime}}\psi-\lambda(\lambda-1)\psi=0,

(3.23)

where we take
a=\lambda,b=\lambda-1,c=\lambda-\sqrt{1+\alpha}.
We shall prove that

Proposition 3.11
.

For all
\alpha>0
, there are no non-trivial smooth solutions
\psi\in C^{\infty}[-1,1]
to Eq. (
3.23
) for
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>-1
, expect
0
and
1
. As a corollary,
U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}
is mode stable.

To prove Proposition
3.11
, we need the following standard Frobenius analysis.

Lemma 3.12

(Frobenius theory
[
12
,
50
]
)
.

Consider

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(z)+p(z)f^{\prime}(z)+q(z)f(z)=0

(3.24)

where
p
and
q
are given functions and
f
is the unknown. Denote
\mathbb{D}_{R}:=

\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<R\}
. Let
R>0
and let
p,q:\mathbb{D}_{R}\backslash\{0\}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be holomorphic. Suppose that the limits

\displaystyle p_{0}:=\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}[zp(z)],\quad q_{0}:=\lim_{z\rightarrow 0}\left[z^{2}q(z)\right]

exist and let
s_{\pm}\in\mathbb{C}
satisfy
P\left(s_{\pm}\right)=0
, where

\displaystyle P(s):=s(s-1)+p_{0}s+q_{0}

is the indicial polynomial. Let
\operatorname{Re}s_{+}\geq\operatorname{Re}s_{-}
. Then there exists a holomorphic function
h_{+}:\mathbb{D}_{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
with
h_{+}(0)=1
and such that
f:\mathbb{D}_{R}\backslash(-\infty,0]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, given by
f(z)=z^{s_{+}}h_{+}(z)
, satisfies Eq. (
3.24
). Furthermore,

•

if
s_{+}-s_{-}\notin\mathbb{N}_{0}
, there exists a holomorphic function
h_{-}
:
\mathbb{D}_{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
with
h_{-}(0)=1
and such that
f(z)=z^{s_{-}}h_{-}(z)
is another solution of Eq. (
3.24
) on
\mathbb{D}_{R}\backslash(-\infty,0]
;

•

if
s_{+}-s_{-}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, there exist a constant
c\in\mathbb{C}
and a holomorphic function
h_{-}:\mathbb{D}_{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
with
h_{-}(0)=1
such that

\displaystyle f(z)=z^{s_{-}}h_{-}(z)+cz^{s_{+}}h_{+}(z)\log z

is another solution of Eq. (
3.24
) on
\mathbb{D}_{R}\backslash(-\infty,0]
. The constant

might be zero unless
s_{+}=s_{-}
.

By Frobenius theory, we can always find an infinite series
local
solution of the form

\displaystyle\psi(z^{\prime})=(z^{\prime}-z^{\prime}_{0})^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(z^{\prime}-z^{\prime}_{0})^{k}

(3.25)

near each singular point
z^{\prime}_{0}\in\{0,1\}
. More precisely, by substituting (
3.25
) into (
3.23
) and matching the lowest order term, we have the following indicial polynomials

\displaystyle P^{\prime}_{0}(s)=s(s-1+c)=0\text{~for~}z^{\prime}_{0}=0,

and

\displaystyle P^{\prime}_{1}(s)=s(s+a+b-c)=0\text{~for~}z^{\prime}_{0}=1.

Frobenius analysis at
z^{\prime}_{0}=0

For
z^{\prime}_{0}=0
,

s_{+}=1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\lambda,\quad s_{-}=0,\quad\textit{~if~}-1<\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leq 1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}

and

s_{+}=0,\quad s_{-}=1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\lambda,\quad\textit{~if~}\operatorname{Re}\lambda>1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}.

Therefore, we have

Case 1

if
-1<\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leq 1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}
and
s_{+}-s_{-}\notin\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the two independent local solutions are given by

\displaystyle\psi_{01}={z^{\prime}}^{s_{+}}h_{+}(z^{\prime})\quad\text{~and~}\quad\psi_{02}=h_{-}(z^{\prime}),

with

h_{+}(z^{\prime})={}_{2}F_{1}(a+1-c,b+1-c;2-c;z^{\prime}),\quad h_{-}(z^{\prime})={}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z^{\prime}),

where

{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z^{\prime})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{n}(b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}}\frac{z^{\prime n}}{n!}=1+\frac{ab}{c}\frac{z^{\prime}}{1!}+\frac{a(a+1)b(b+1)}{c(c+1)}\frac{z^{\prime 2}}{2!}+\cdots

is the hypergeometric function defined for all
|z^{\prime}|<1
, see
[
44
]
.
Note that
\psi_{01}
is not smooth at
0
since

. Thus, any local smooth solution around

must be a multiple of
\psi_{02}
, thus is also analytic at
0
.

Case 2

if
-1<\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leq 1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}
and
s_{+}-s_{-}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the two independent local solutions are given by

\displaystyle\psi_{01}=z^{\prime s_{+}}h_{+}(z^{\prime})\quad\text{~and~}\quad\psi_{02}=h_{-}(z^{\prime})+c^{\prime}z^{s_{+}}h_{+}(z^{\prime})\log(z^{\prime}),

where

h_{+}(z^{\prime})={}_{2}F_{1}(a+1-c,b+1-c;2-c;z^{\prime}),

and

h_{-}
is an analytic function around
0
and
c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
is a constant. Note that

is not a smooth function, thus a local smooth solution around
z_{0}^{\prime}=0
exists only if it is a multiple of
\psi_{01}
or

. In either case, the local smooth solution must also be analytic at 0.

Case 3

if
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}
,
the two independent local solutions are given by

\displaystyle\psi_{01}=h_{+}(z^{\prime})\quad\text{~and~}\quad\psi_{02}=z^{\prime s_{-}}h_{-}(z^{\prime})+c^{\prime}h_{+}(z^{\prime})\log(z^{\prime}),

where

h_{+}(z^{\prime})={}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z^{\prime})

and

h_{-}
is an analytic function around
0
and
c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
is a constant. Similarly, any local smooth solution around

must be also analytic at
0
.

Frobenius analysis at
z^{\prime}_{0}=1

For
z^{\prime}_{0}=1
,

s_{+}=1-\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\lambda,\quad s_{-}=0,\quad\text{if~}-1<\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leq 1-\sqrt{1+\alpha}

and

s_{+}=0,\quad s_{-}=1-\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\lambda,\quad\text{if~}\operatorname{Re}\lambda>1-\sqrt{1+\alpha}.

Therefore, we have

Case 1’

if
-1<\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leq 1-\sqrt{1+\alpha}
and
s_{+}-s_{-}\notin\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the two independent local solutions are given by

\displaystyle\psi_{11}=(z^{\prime}-1)^{s_{+}}h_{+}(z^{\prime}-1)\quad\text{~and~}\quad\psi_{12}=h_{-}(z^{\prime}-1),

where

h_{-}(z^{\prime}-1)={}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;1+a+b-c;1-z^{\prime})

and

h_{+}(z^{\prime}-1)
is an analytic function around

.
Note that
\psi_{11}
is not smooth at
1
since
s_{+}\notin\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Thus, any local smooth solution must be a multiple of
\psi_{12}
, thus is also analytic at 1.

Case 2’

if
-1<\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leq 1-\sqrt{1+\alpha}
and
s_{+}-s_{-}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then
s_{+}=s_{-}=0
, since
s_{+}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\operatorname{Re}s_{+}<1
for all

. Thus, the two independent local solutions are given by

\displaystyle\psi_{11}=h_{+}(z^{\prime}-1)\quad\text{~and~}\quad\psi_{12}=h_{-}(z^{\prime}-1)+c^{\prime}h_{+}(z^{\prime}-1)\log(z^{\prime}-1).

where

h_{+}(z^{\prime}-1)={}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;1+a+b-c;1-z^{\prime}),

and

h_{-}(z^{\prime}-1)
is an analytic function around

and the constant

c^{\prime}\neq 0
since

. Note that
h_{+}(z^{\prime}-1)\log(z^{\prime}-1)
is not smooth at
1
. Thus, any local smooth solution must be a multiple of
\psi_{11}
, and is also analytic at 1.

Case 3’

if
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>1-\sqrt{1+\alpha}
,
the two independent local solutions are given by

\displaystyle\psi_{11}=h_{+}(z-1)\quad\text{~and~}\quad\psi_{12}=(z-1)^{s_{-}}h_{-}(z-1)+c^{\prime}h_{+}(z-1)\log(z-1),

where

h_{+}(z^{\prime}-1)={}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;1+a+b-c;1-z^{\prime}),

and

h_{-}
is an analytic function around

and

is a (possibly vanishing) constant. Since

,
\psi_{12}
is not smooth at

Now we are ready to prove the mode stability of

.

Proof of Proposition
3.11
.

By the Frobenius analysis above, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>-1
and any nontrivial smooth solution
\psi(z^{\prime})\in C^{\infty}[0,1]
to (
3.23
),
\psi(z^{\prime})
must be also analytic on
[0,1]
. We aim to show that
\psi(z^{\prime})
must fail to be analytic at
z^{\prime}=0
unless
\lambda\in\{0,1\}
.

The Frobenius analysis at
z_{0}^{\prime}=1
above shows that in all three cases, any local smooth solution around
z_{0}^{\prime}=1
must be given by a multiple of

{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;1+a+b-c;1-z^{\prime}).

Therefore, without the loss of generality, we assume

\psi(z^{\prime})={}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;1+a+b-c;1-z^{\prime}).

Let
\tilde{z}=1-z^{\prime}
, then
\psi(z^{\prime})=\psi(1-\tilde{z})\equiv\tilde{\psi}(\tilde{z})
is an analytic function around
\tilde{z}=0
. Now the finite regular singular points of the hypergeometric ODE (
3.23
) are
\{0,1\}
. Thus,
\psi(z^{\prime})=\tilde{\psi}(\tilde{z})
fails to be analytic at
z^{\prime}=0
(i.e.
\tilde{z}=1
) precisely when the radius of convergence of the series

\tilde{\psi}(\tilde{z})={}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;1+a+b-c;\tilde{z})

is equal to one.

By the definition of the hypergeometric function, the coefficients of the series expansion of
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;1+a+b-c;\tilde{z})
are

a_{n}(\lambda)=\frac{(a)_{n}(b)_{n}}{(1+a+b-c)_{n}n!}=\frac{(\lambda)_{n}(\lambda-1)_{n}}{(\lambda+\sqrt{1+\alpha})_{n}n!},

where
(a)_{n}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1).
Therefore, if
\lambda\notin\{0,1\}
and
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>-1
, we have
a_{n}(\lambda)\neq 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and

\displaystyle r_{n}(\lambda):=\frac{a_{n+1}(\lambda)}{a_{n}(\lambda)}=\frac{(\lambda+n)(\lambda+n-1)}{(\lambda+\sqrt{1+\alpha}+n)(n+1)}\to 1,\quad\text{as~}n\to\infty.

This implies that the radius of convergence of
\tilde{\psi}(\tilde{z})
must be 1.

∎

4
Spectral analysis of
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}

In this section, we study the essential spectrum of
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
. The key idea is to decompose
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
as a maximally dissipative operator plus a compact perturbation. To begin with, we present the functional setup.

4.1
Functional setup

By (
3
), the linearized equation can be written as

\partial_{s}\mathbf{q}=\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\quad\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}=:\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\
q_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-y\partial_{y}q_{1}+q_{2}\\
\partial_{yy}q_{1}-\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\partial_{y}q_{1}-q_{2}-y\partial_{y}q_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

We can further decompose the operator

\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}=\tilde{\mathbf{L}}+\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1},

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{q}:=\begin{pmatrix}-y\partial_{y}q_{1}+q_{2}-q_{1}(-1)\\
\partial_{yy}q_{1}-q_{2}-y\partial_{y}q_{2}\end{pmatrix}

is a modified
free wave operator
, and

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}\mathbf{q}=\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}(-1)\\
-\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\partial_{y}q_{1}\end{pmatrix}

is called
potential
. We introduce the following sesquilinear form

\langle\mathbf{q},\tilde{\mathbf{q}}\rangle_{0}:=\int_{-1}^{1}\partial_{y}q_{1}\overline{\partial_{y}\tilde{q}_{1}}dy+\int_{-1}^{1}q_{2}\overline{\tilde{q}_{2}}dy+q_{1}(-1)\overline{\tilde{q}_{1}(-1)}

on the space
C^{1}[-1,1]\times C^{0}[-1,1]
, and for
k\geq 1

\langle\mathbf{q},\tilde{\mathbf{q}}\rangle_{k}:=\int_{-1}^{1}\partial^{k+1}_{y}q_{1}\overline{\partial^{k+1}_{y}\tilde{q}_{1}}dy+\int_{-1}^{1}\partial^{k}_{y}q_{2}\overline{\partial^{k}_{y}\tilde{q}_{2}}dy+\langle\mathbf{q},\tilde{\mathbf{q}}\rangle_{0}

on the space
C^{k+1}[-1,1]\times C^{k}[-1,1]
. Define the corresponding norms

\|\mathbf{q}\|_{k}^{2}:=\langle\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{k}.

We claim that the norm
\|\cdot\|_{k}
is equivalent to the Sobolev norm
H^{k+1}\times H^{k}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
k\geq 0
and all
\mathbf{q}\in C^{k+1}[-1,1]\times C^{k}[-1,1]
, it holds

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{q}\|_{k}\simeq\|q_{1}\|_{H^{k+1}}+\|q_{2}\|_{H^{k}}.

And the completion of
C^{k+1}[-1,1]\times C^{k}[-1,1]
with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{k}
is the Hilbert space
H^{k+1}(-1,1)\times H^{k}(-1,1)
.

Proof.

By the definition of
\|\cdot\|_{k}
, it suffices to show that

\|\mathbf{q}\|_{0}\gtrsim\|q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}

for all
\mathbf{q}\in C^{1}[-1,1]\times C^{0}[-1,1]
.
This follows from

\displaystyle|q_{1}(y)|^{2}=\left|q_{1}(-1)+\int_{-1}^{y}\partial_{z}q_{1}dz\right|^{2}\lesssim|q_{1}(-1)|^{2}+\left|\int_{-1}^{y}\partial_{z}q_{1}dz\right|^{2}\lesssim|q_{1}(-1)|^{2}+\int_{-1}^{1}\left|\partial_{y}q_{1}\right|^{2}dy

and

\displaystyle\|q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{-1}^{1}|q_{1}|^{2}dy\lesssim|q_{1}(-1)|^{2}+\int_{-1}^{1}\left|\partial_{y}q_{1}\right|^{2}dy\lesssim\|\mathbf{q}\|_{0}^{2}.

∎

4.2
Generation of the semigroup

For
\alpha>0
, let
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha})=\mathcal{D}(\tilde{\mathbf{L}})=C^{\infty}[-1,1]\times C^{\infty}[-1,1]
. Define
\mathcal{H}^{k}=H^{k+1}(-1,1)\times H^{k}(-1,1)
, then the free wave operator
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}:\mathcal{D}(\tilde{\mathbf{L}})\subset\mathcal{H}^{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{k}
is a well-defined operator on
\mathcal{H}^{k}
for all
k\geq 0
.

Lemma 4.2

(Dissipativity of the free operator)
.

For
k\geq 0
and
\mathbf{q}\in C^{k+1}[-1,1]\times C^{k}[-1,1]
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\langle\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{k}\leq-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{q}\|_{k}^{2}.

Proof.

By definition, for
k=0
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\right\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\int_{-1}^{1}\partial_{y}\left(-y\partial_{y}q_{1}+q_{2}\right)\overline{\partial_{y}q_{1}}dy+\operatorname{Re}\left(\left(-y\partial_{y}q_{1}+q_{2}-q_{1}\right)\overline{q_{1}}|_{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\operatorname{Re}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(-q_{2}-y\partial_{y}q_{2}+\partial_{yy}q_{1}\right)\overline{q_{2}}dy

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\partial_{y}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}|y\partial q_{1}-q_{2}|^{2}(\pm 1)-|q_{1}(-1)|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\operatorname{Re}\left(\left(y\partial_{y}q_{1}-q_{2}\right)(-1)\overline{q_{1}}(-1)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\partial_{y}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}|y\partial q_{1}-q_{2}|^{2}(1)-\frac{1}{2}|q_{1}(-1)|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}\left|y\partial_{y}q_{1}-q_{2}+q_{1}\right|^{2}(-1)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{q}\|_{0}^{2}.

For
k\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\right\rangle_{k}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\int_{-1}^{1}\partial_{y}^{k+1}\left(-y\partial_{y}q_{1}+q_{2}\right)\overline{\partial_{y}^{k+1}q_{1}}dy

\displaystyle\quad+\operatorname{Re}\int_{-1}^{1}\partial^{k}\left(-q_{2}-y\partial_{y}q_{2}+\partial_{yy}q_{1}\right)\overline{\partial^{k}q_{2}}dy+\operatorname{Re}\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\right\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=-\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\|\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}|y\partial^{k+1}q_{1}-\partial^{k}q_{2}|^{2}(\pm 1)

\displaystyle\quad+\operatorname{Re}\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\right\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\mathbf{q}\|_{0}^{2}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{q}\|_{k}^{2}.

∎

Next, we show that the range of
\lambda-\tilde{\mathbf{L}}
is dense in
\mathcal{H}^{k}
for some
\lambda>-1
(we simply take
\lambda=0
for convenience) and all
k\geq 0
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
k\geq 0
and
\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
. For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a function
\mathbf{q}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
such that

\|-\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{f}\|_{k}\leq\epsilon.

Proof.

Since
(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
is dense in
\mathcal{H}^{k}
, it suffices to prove that for all
\mathbf{f}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
, there exists a unique smooth function
\mathbf{q}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
such that

\displaystyle-\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{f},

(4.1)

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} y\partial_{y}q_{1}-q_{2}+q_{1}(-1)=f_{1}\\
q_{2}+y\partial_{y}q_{2}-\partial_{yy}q_{1}=f_{2}.\end{aligned}\right.

Using the first equation to solve for
q_{2}
, we see that solving this system of ODEs reduces to

\displaystyle 2y\partial_{y}q_{1}+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}q_{1}=f_{1}+y\partial_{y}f_{1}+f_{2}-q_{1}(-1).

(4.2)

Denote
F=f_{1}+y\partial_{y}f_{1}+f_{2}\in C^{\infty}[-1,1],
then we can rewrite (
4.2
) as

\displaystyle\partial_{y}((y^{2}-1)\partial_{y}q_{1})=F(y)-q_{1}(-1).

Thus, we have

\displaystyle q_{1}(y)=q_{1}(-1)+\int_{-1}^{y}\frac{\int_{-1}^{w}\left(F(z)-q_{1}(-1)\right)dz}{w^{2}-1}dw.

(4.3)

To get rid of the potential singularity at
y=1
, we have to choose

\displaystyle q_{1}(-1)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}F(z)dz.

(4.4)

Now we show that the function
q_{1}(y)
given by (
4.3
)-(
4.4
) belongs to
C^{\infty}[-1,1]
. First, since
F\in C^{\infty}[-1,1]
and
\frac{1}{w^{2}-1}\in C^{\infty}(-1,1)
, we have
q_{1}(y)\in C^{\infty}(-1,1)
. So it remains to prove
q_{1}
is also smooth at endpoints
\pm 1
.

Near
y=-1
, we have

\displaystyle q_{1}(y)=q_{1}(-1)+\int_{-1}^{y}\frac{1}{w-1}\frac{\int_{-1}^{w}\left(F(z)-q_{1}(-1)\right)dz}{w+1}dw.

Note that
\frac{1}{w-1}
and
\frac{\int_{-1}^{w}\left(F(z)-q_{1}(-1)\right)dz}{w+1}
are smooth at
-1
, thus
q_{1}(y)
is also smooth at
-1
.

Near
y=1
, by (
4.4
), we can rewrite (
4.3
) as

\displaystyle q_{1}(y)

\displaystyle=q_{1}(-1)+\int_{-1}^{y}\frac{\int_{w}^{1}\left(F(z)-q_{1}(-1)\right)dz}{w^{2}-1}dw

\displaystyle=q_{1}(-1)+\int_{-1}^{y}\frac{1}{w+1}\frac{\int_{w}^{1}\left(F(z)-q_{1}(-1)\right)dz}{w-1}dw.

Note that
\frac{1}{w+1}
and
\frac{\int_{w}^{1}\left(F(z)-q_{1}(-1)\right)dz}{w-1}
are smooth at
1
,
q_{1}(y)
is also smooth at
1
, i.e.
q_{1}\in C^{\infty}[-1,1]
. Since
q_{2}=y\partial_{y}q_{1}+q_{1}(-1)-f_{1}
,
q_{2}\in C^{\infty}[-1,1]
. So, we have
\mathbf{q}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
.

∎

Proposition 4.4
.

For
k\geq 0
, the operator
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}:\mathcal{D}(\tilde{\mathbf{L}})\subset\mathcal{H}^{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{k}
is densely defined, closable, and its closure
\mathbf{L}
generates a strongly continuous one-parameter semigroup
\mathbf{S}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{k})
which satisfies

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{S}(s)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{k})}\leq Ce^{-\frac{1}{2}s}

for all
s\geq 0
, where
C>0
is a constant.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{\mathbf{L}})=C^{\infty}[-1,1]\times C^{\infty}[-1,1]
is dense in
\mathcal{H}^{k}
,
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}
is densely defined. By Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
4.3
, we obtain that the operator
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}
is a dissipative operator and
\operatorname{rg}(-\tilde{\mathbf{L}})
is also dense in
\mathcal{H}^{k}
for all
k\geq 0
. Therefore, the claim follows from the Lumer-Phillips Theorem
[
23
, p.83, Theorem 3.15]
and Lemma
4.1
.

∎

Next, we deal with the potential operator
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
. Compare to the power nonlinearities
[
38
,
16
]
, the potential
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
is no longer a compact operator. Even worse,
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
is not a relatively compact operator with respect to
\mathbf{L}
, either. Nevertheless, we can prove that

Proposition 4.5
.

For all
\alpha>0
and
k\geq 0
, the operator
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
is bounded on
\mathcal{H}^{k}
. As a corollary,
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}:=\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
generates a strongly continuous one-parameter semigroup
\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{k})
which satisfies

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{k})}\leq Ce^{\left(-\frac{1}{2}+\|\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}\|\right)s}

for all
s\geq 0
, where
C>0
is a constant independent of
\alpha
.

Proof.

By the definition of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
, for any
\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}=\|q_{1}(-1)\|_{H^{k+1}}+\left\|-\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\partial_{y}q_{1}\right\|_{H^{k}}\lesssim_{\alpha}\|q_{1}\|_{H^{k+1}}\lesssim_{\alpha}\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}},

which implies that
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
is bounded on
\mathcal{H}^{k}
. Thus, by the bounded perturbation theorem
[
23
, p. 158]

\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
also generates a strongly continuous one-parameter semigroup with the above growth bound.
∎

4.3
A new decomposition with a compact perturbation

Since
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
is merely a bounded perturbation without any compactness compared to the free wave operator
\mathbf{L}
, we can not get any information of the essential spectrum of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
, thus no uniform growth bound of the semigroup
\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}
is available either. To overcome this difficulty, we shall introduce a new decomposition of the linearized operator
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
as a new dissipative operator plus a compact perturbation. More precisely, recall that

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}:=\begin{pmatrix}-y\partial_{y}&1\\
\partial_{yy}+2\partial_{y}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}\partial_{y}&-1-y\partial_{y}\end{pmatrix},

where the potential

\partial_{y}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}(s,y)=-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}.

Note that the dissipativity of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
itself is not guaranteed. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\left\langle\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\right\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}\times L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\partial_{y}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)+\operatorname{Re}\int 2\partial_{y}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}\partial_{y}q_{1}\overline{q_{2}}dy

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\partial_{y}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)+(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+1)\left(\|\partial_{y}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+\frac{1}{2})\left(\|\partial_{y}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right),

where we use

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{y}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(-1,1)}\leq\left\|-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(-1,1)}=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}-1}=\sqrt{1+\alpha}+1.

To gain more dissipation, we need to consider
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
in Sobolev spaces with higher regularity, i.e
\mathcal{H}^{k}=H^{k+1}(-1,1)\times H^{k}(-1,1),k\geq k_{\alpha}+2
.

Lemma 4.6

(Commuting with derivatives)
.

For
\alpha>0
and
k\geq 0
, there holds

\displaystyle\partial^{k}\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k}\partial^{k}+\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k}^{\prime}

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k}=\begin{pmatrix}-k-y\partial_{y}&1\\
\partial_{yy}+2\partial_{y}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}\partial_{y}&-1-k-y\partial_{y}\end{pmatrix},

and
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k}^{\prime}
satisfies the pointwise bound

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k}^{\prime}\mathbf{q}\right|\lesssim\binom{0}{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|\partial^{k-j+1}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}|\left|\partial^{j+1}q_{1}\right|}\lesssim_{\alpha}\binom{0}{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left|\partial^{j+1}q_{1}\right|}

Proof.

Direct computation yields the following formulae

\displaystyle\left[\partial^{k},\partial_{y}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}\partial_{y}\right]=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}C_{k}^{j}\partial^{k-j+1}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}\partial^{j+1},\quad\left[\partial^{k},y\partial_{y}\right]=k\partial^{k}.

The pointwise bound follows from

\displaystyle\|\partial^{k-j+1}U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}\|_{L^{\infty}(-1,1)}

\displaystyle=\left\|(k-j)!\frac{\alpha}{(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y)^{k-j+1}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(-1,1)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\alpha}{(\sqrt{1+\alpha}-1)^{k-j+1}}.

∎

For
k\geq 0
, we define a new inner product

\displaystyle\langle\langle\Psi,\tilde{\Psi}\rangle\rangle_{k}=(\partial^{k}\Psi,\partial^{k}\tilde{\Psi})_{L^{2}}+(\Psi,\tilde{\Psi})_{L^{2}}=\int_{-1}^{1}\partial^{k}\Psi\overline{\partial^{k}\tilde{\Psi}}dy+\int_{-1}^{1}\Psi\overline{\tilde{\Psi}}dy.

By definition, the norm induced by the inner produce
\langle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\rangle_{k}
is also equivalent to the standard Sobolev norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{k}(-1,1)}
. For tuples of function
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},q_{2})
, we introduce the corresponding inner product

\displaystyle\langle\langle\mathbf{q},\tilde{\mathbf{q}}\rangle\rangle_{k}=\langle\langle q_{1},\tilde{q}_{1}\rangle\rangle_{k+1}+\langle\langle q_{2},\tilde{q}_{2}\rangle\rangle_{k},

and its associate norm is equivalent to
\|\cdot\|_{H^{k+1}\times H^{k}}
. To control
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k}^{\prime}
, we prove

Lemma 4.7

(Subcoercivity estimate)
.

For
k\geq 0
, there exist
\epsilon_{n}>0
with
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\epsilon_{n}=0
and
\{\Pi_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}\subset H^{k+1}(-1,1),c_{n}>0
such that for all
n\geq 0,\Psi\in H^{k+1}(-1,1)
,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}\langle\langle\Psi,\Psi\rangle\rangle_{k}\geq\left\|\Psi\right\|^{2}_{H^{k}}-c_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\Psi,\Pi_{i}\right)_{L^{2}}^{2}

(4.5)

Proof.

Given
T\in L^{2}(-1,1)
, the antilinear map
h\mapsto(T,h)
is continuous on
H^{k}(-1,1)
since

\displaystyle(\Psi,\Psi)_{L^{2}}\leq\langle\langle\Psi,\Psi\rangle\rangle_{k}.

By Riesz representation theorem, there exists a unique
l(T)\in H^{k}(-1,1)
such that

\displaystyle\forall h\in H^{k}(-1,1),\quad\langle\langle l(T),h\rangle\rangle_{k}=(T,h)_{L^{2}}

(4.6)

and
l:L^{2}(-1,1)\rightarrow H^{k}(-1,1)
is a bounded linear map. By Sobolev embedding theorem, the operator
\iota:H^{k}(-1,1)\to L^{2}(-1,1),\iota(\Psi)=\Psi
is compact, thus the map
\iota\circ l:L^{2}(-1,1)\rightarrow L^{2}(-1,1)
is also compact. Moreover, if
\Psi_{i}=l(T_{i}),i=1,2
, then

\displaystyle(l(T_{1}),T_{2})_{L^{2}}=(\Psi_{1},T_{2})_{L^{2}}=\langle\langle\Psi_{1},l(T_{2})\rangle\rangle_{k}=\langle\langle\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\rangle\rangle_{k}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle(T_{1},l(T_{2}))_{L^{2}}=\langle\langle\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\rangle\rangle_{k}=\left(l\left(T_{1}\right),T_{2}\right)_{L^{2}}

i.e.
\iota\circ l
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}(-1,1)
. By (
4.6
),

(T,l(T))_{L^{2}}=\langle\langle l(T),l(T)\rangle\rangle_{k}\geq 0,

\iota\circ l
is also positive definite. Thus, by spectral decomposition of positive definite compact operators, there exists an
L^{2}
-orthonormal eigenbasis
\left(\Pi_{n,i}\right)_{1\leq i\leq I(n)}
of
\iota\circ l
with positive eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}\rightarrow 0
. The eigenvalue equation implies
\Pi_{n,i}\in H^{k}(-1,1)
. Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{n}=\left\{\Psi\in H^{k}(-1,1)\mid(\Psi,\Psi)_{L^{2}}=1,\left(\Psi,\Pi_{j,i}\right)_{L^{2}}=0,1\leq i\leq I(j),1\leq j\leq n\right\}

and consider the minimization problem

\displaystyle I_{n}=\inf_{\Psi\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\langle\langle\Psi,\Psi\rangle\rangle_{k},

whose infimum is attained at
\Psi\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
since
\iota\circ l
is compact. Also, by a standard Lagrange multiplier argument,

\displaystyle\forall h\in H^{k}(-1,1),\quad\langle\langle\Psi,h\rangle\rangle_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{I(j)}\beta_{i,j}\left(\Pi_{j,i},h\right)_{L^{2}}+\beta(\Psi,h)_{L^{2}}.

Set
h=\Pi_{j,i}
and since
\Pi_{j,i}
is an eigenvector of
\iota\circ l
, we infer
\beta_{i,j}=0
and in view of (
4.6
),
l(\Psi)=\beta^{-1}\Psi
. Together with the orthogonality conditions,
\beta^{-1}\leq\lambda_{n+1}
. Hence

\displaystyle I_{n}=\langle\langle\Psi,\Psi\rangle\rangle_{k}=\beta(\Psi,\Psi)_{L^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{\lambda_{n+1}}

(4.7)

For all
\epsilon>0,k\geq 0
, from Gagliardo-Nirenberg interpolation inequality together with Young’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\Psi\right\|_{H^{k}}^{2}\leq\epsilon\left\|\Psi\right\|^{2}_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}+c_{\epsilon,k}\|\Psi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Together with (
4.7
), we have that for all
\Psi
satisfying orthogonality condition of
\mathcal{A}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\Psi\right\|_{H^{k}}^{2}\leq(\epsilon+c_{\epsilon,k}\lambda_{n+1})\langle\langle\Psi,\Psi\rangle\rangle_{k}.

Choosing
\epsilon_{n}\rightarrow 0
such that
c_{\epsilon_{n},k}\lambda_{n+1}\leq\epsilon_{n}
yields (
4.5
).
∎

Based on this, we are able to show dissipativity of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
upon a compact perturbation.

Proposition 4.8

(Maximal Dissipativity)
.

For
\alpha>0
,
k\geq k_{\alpha}+2
and
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, there exists
\left(\mathbf{\Pi}_{\alpha,i}\right)_{1\leq i\leq N}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
such that for the finite rank projection operator

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\langle\langle\cdot,\mathbf{\Pi}_{\alpha,i}\right\rangle\rangle_{k}\mathbf{\Pi}_{\alpha,i},

(4.8)

the modified operator

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}-\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}

is dissipative:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \forall\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}),\quad\operatorname{Re}\langle\langle-\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle\rangle_{k}&\geq\left(k-\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)\langle\langle\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle\rangle_{k}\\
&\geq\left(\frac{3}{2}-\epsilon\right)\langle\langle\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle\rangle_{k},\end{aligned}

(4.9)

and is maximal:

\displaystyle\lambda-\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}\text{~is surjective~}\text{ for some }\lambda>0.

Proof.

We first prove (
4.9
) for all
\mathbf{q}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
. For
\mathbf{q}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
, by Lemma
4.6
we have

\displaystyle\langle\langle-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle\rangle_{k}

\displaystyle=(-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q})_{\dot{H}^{k+1}\times\dot{H}^{k}}+(-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q})_{L^{2}\times L^{2}}

\displaystyle=(-(\partial^{k+1}\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q})_{1},\partial^{k+1}q_{1})_{L^{2}}+(-(\partial^{k}\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q})_{2},\partial^{k}q_{2})_{L^{2}}+(-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q})_{L^{2}\times L^{2}}

\displaystyle=(-(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k+1}\partial^{k+1}\mathbf{q})_{1},\partial^{k+1}q_{1})_{L^{2}}+(-(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k}\partial^{k}\mathbf{q})_{2},\partial^{k}q_{2})_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+(-(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k+1}^{\prime}\mathbf{q})_{1},\partial^{k+1}q_{1})_{L^{2}}+(-(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k}^{\prime}\mathbf{q})_{2},\partial^{k}q_{2})_{L^{2}}+(-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q})_{L^{2}\times L^{2}}

For the first two terms, by integration by parts we derive

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}(-(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k+1}\partial^{k+1}\mathbf{q})_{1},\partial^{k+1}q_{1})_{L^{2}}+\operatorname{Re}(-(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k}\partial^{k}\mathbf{q})_{2},\partial^{k}q_{2})_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(k-\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\|\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

For the last three terms, by Young’s inequality we have

\displaystyle\left|(-(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k+1}^{\prime}\mathbf{q})_{1},\partial^{k+1}q_{1})_{L^{2}}\right|+\left|(-(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k}^{\prime}\mathbf{q})_{2},\partial^{k}q_{2})_{L^{2}}\right|+\left|(-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q})_{L^{2}\times L^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}+c_{\epsilon,k,\alpha}\left(\|q_{1}\|_{H^{k}}+\|q_{2}\|_{H^{k-1}}\right).

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\langle\langle-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle\rangle_{k}\geq\left(k-\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)\left(\|\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)-c_{\epsilon,k,\alpha}\left(\|q_{1}\|_{H^{k}}+\|q_{2}\|_{H^{k-1}}\right).

Using Lemma
4.7
for
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
respectively, we derive

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\langle\langle-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle\rangle_{k}\geq

\displaystyle\left(k-\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)\left(\|\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle-c_{\epsilon,k,\alpha}^{\prime}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}(q_{1},\Pi^{(1)}_{i})_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{2}}(q_{2},\Pi^{(2)}_{i})_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

for some
(\Pi^{(1)}_{i},\Pi^{(2)}_{i})\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
. Note that linear functionals

\mathbf{q}\mapsto\sqrt{c_{\epsilon,k,\alpha}^{\prime}}(q_{1},\Pi^{(1)}_{i})_{L^{2}}\text{~and~}\mathbf{q}\mapsto\sqrt{c_{\epsilon,k,\alpha}^{\prime}}(q_{2},\Pi^{(2)}_{i})_{L^{2}}

are continuous on
\mathcal{H}^{k}
, thus by Riesz theorem, there exists
\mathbf{\Pi}_{\alpha,i}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
such that

\langle\langle\mathbf{q},\mathbf{\Pi}_{\alpha,i}\rangle\rangle_{k}=\sqrt{c_{\epsilon,k,\alpha}^{\prime}}(q_{1},\Pi^{(1)}_{i})_{L^{2}},\quad 1\leq i\leq N_{1},

\langle\langle\mathbf{q},\mathbf{\Pi}_{\alpha,i}\rangle\rangle_{k}=\sqrt{c_{\epsilon,k,\alpha}^{\prime}}(q_{2},\Pi^{(2)}_{i})_{L^{2}},\quad N_{1}+1\leq i\leq N_{1}+N_{2}:=N.

Define the finite rank projection
\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}
as (
4.8
), we obtain the dissipativity (
4.9
) for all
\mathbf{q}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
.

Now we claim that (
4.9
) also holds for all

\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
. Indeed, if
\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
, we have
\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
and
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
,
since by Proposition
4.5

\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
is the generator of the semigroup
\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}
on
\mathcal{H}^{k}
. Note that

\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
and
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}
is a bounded operator in
\mathcal{H}^{k}
, we obtain
\mathbf{L}\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
as well. Since
(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
is dense in
\mathcal{H}^{k}
, there exists a sequence
\{\mathbf{f}_{n}\}\subset(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
such that

\mathbf{f}_{n}\to-\mathbf{L}\mathbf{q}.

By the proof of Lemma
4.3
, there exists a unique
\mathbf{q}_{n}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
such that

-\mathbf{L}\mathbf{q}_{n}=\mathbf{f}_{n}.

We show that
\mathbf{q}_{n}\to\mathbf{q}
. To see that, by
Lemma
4.2
and
\mathbf{q}_{n}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{q}_{n}-\mathbf{q}_{m}\|_{k}\lesssim\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{q}_{n}-\mathbf{L}\mathbf{q}_{m}\|_{k}=\|\mathbf{f}_{n}-\mathbf{f}_{m}\|_{k}\to 0,

i.e.
\{\mathbf{q}_{n}\}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathcal{H}^{k}
, thus there exists a
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
such that
\mathbf{q}_{n}\to\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
. By the closedness of
\mathbf{L}
we have
-\mathbf{L}\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=-\mathbf{L}\mathbf{q}
. By Proposition
4.4
,
0\in\rho(\mathbf{L})
, thus
-\mathbf{L}
is invertible. This implies that
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=\mathbf{q}
, so
\mathbf{q}_{n}\to\mathbf{q}
. Note that
\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}-\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}-\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}
and
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1},\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}
are bounded, we have
\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}_{n}\to\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}
. Since
\mathbf{q}_{n}
satisfies (
4.9
), let
n\to\infty
, (
4.9
) also holds for
\mathbf{q}
.

It remains to prove that
\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
is maximal. Note that by Proposition
4.5
,
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
is the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup with a finite growth bound
w:=-\frac{1}{2}+\|\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,1}\|
, thus for
\lambda>0
sufficiently large,
\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
is invertible and

\|(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{k})}\lesssim\frac{1}{\lambda-w}\lesssim\frac{2}{\lambda}.

Then, since
\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}
is bounded,

\lambda-\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}=\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}+\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}=(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})(\mathbf{I}+(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha})

is also invertible, thus
\lambda-\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
is surjective.

∎

4.4
Spectral analysis of the generator

Now we can give a sufficiently detailed description of the spectrum of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
k\geq k_{\alpha}+2
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma\left(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\right)\subset\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}z\leq-1\right\}\cup\{0,1\}

(4.10)

and
\{0,1\}\subset\sigma_{p}\left(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\right)
. Furthermore, the geometric eigenspaces of eigenvalues 1 and 0 are spanned by the functions
\mathbf{f}_{1,\alpha}
and
\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha}
, respectively, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{1,\alpha}(y)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\alpha\sqrt{1+\alpha}}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\\
\frac{\alpha(1+\alpha)}{(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y)^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\text{~and~}\quad\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha}(y)=\binom{1}{0}

Moreover, there is a generalized eigenfunction
\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}
of the eigenvalue 0

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}-\log(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y)-\frac{\alpha}{2\sqrt{1+\alpha}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\\
1-\frac{y}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}+\frac{\alpha}{2\sqrt{1+\alpha}}\frac{y}{(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y)^{2}}\end{pmatrix},

satisfying

\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}=\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha},\quad\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}^{2}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}=0.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.8
,
\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
is maximally dissipative, thus
\sigma(\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha})\subset\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}z\leq-1\}
for
k\geq k_{\alpha}+2
. Since
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}=\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}+\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}
and
\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}
is compact, we have

\sigma_{ess}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})=\sigma_{ess}(\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha})\subset\sigma(\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}).
To prove (
4.10
), it remains to show that all eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
in
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}z>-1\}
must belong to
\{0,1\}
, which has already been verified by the mode stability of
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\alpha}
, see Proposition
3.11
and Proposition
A.1
in the Appendix.

Moreover, a direct computation shows that
\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha},\mathbf{f}_{1,\alpha}
are eigenfunctions and
\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}
is a generalized eigenfunction of 0. By the Frobenius analysis in Proposition
A.1
, the local smooth solutions around
1
is always one-dimensional, thus the geometric eigenspaces of eigenvalues 0 and 1 are spanned by
\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha},\mathbf{f}_{1,\alpha}
respectively.
∎

Now we define the usual Riesz projections associated to the eigenvalues 0 and 1 of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
. We set

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}&:=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma_{0}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}}(z)dz\\
\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}&:=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\gamma_{1}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}}(z)dz\end{aligned}

with the curves
\gamma_{j}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{C},j\in\{0,1\}
, given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}(s)=\frac{1}{4}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}s},\quad\gamma_{1}(s)=1+\frac{1}{2}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}s}

The following important result shows that the algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalues 1 equals its geometric multiplicity, while the algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalues 0 is strictly larger than the geometric multiplicity. This is different from the power nonlinear case
[
38
,
45
]
, and the generalized 0-mode would induce a polynomial growth in time.

Lemma 4.10
.

The projections
\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}
and
\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
have rank 1 and 2, respectively.

Proof.

First of all we note that
\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}
and
\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
have finite rank. This is because the eigenvalues 0 and 1 are generated by a compact perturbation and an eigenvalue of infinite algebraic multiplicity is invariant under such a perturbation (see
[
29
, p. 239, Theorem 5.28 and p. 244, Theorem 5.35]
). By definition,
\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}
and
\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
depends continuously on the parameter
\alpha
. By
[
29
, p. 34, Lemma 4.10]
, the ranks of
\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}
and
\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
are constants for all
\alpha>0
. Thus, it suffices to prove the conclusion for some
\alpha>0
. For convenience, we take
\alpha=3
.

Next, observe that
\operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
. To see this, let
\mathbf{v}\in\operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
. By the density of
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
in
\mathcal{H}^{k}
we find
\left(\mathbf{u}_{n}\right)\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
with
\mathbf{u}_{n}\to\mathbf{v}
. Since
\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
(
[
29
, p. 178, Theorem 6.17]
) we see that
\left(\mathbf{v}_{n}\right):=\left(\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\mathbf{u}_{n}\right)\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\cap\operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
and
\mathbf{v}_{n}=\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\mathbf{u}_{n}\to\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}
by the boundedness of
\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
. The operator
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mid_{\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\cap\mathrm{rg}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}}
is bounded and this implies
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{v}_{n}\to\mathbf{f}
for some
\mathbf{f}\in\operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
. Thus, the closedness of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
implies
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
and we obtain
\operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
as claimed. Let

\mathbf{A}:=\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}}
, then
\mathbf{A}
is a bounded operator on the finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
with spectrum equal to
\{0\}
(
[
29
, p. 178, Theorem 6.17]
). This implies that
\mathbf{A}
is nilpotent, i.e., there exists a (minimal)
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbf{A}^{n}=\mathbf{0}
. We claim that
n=2
for
\alpha=3
.

If
n=1
we have
\operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{0,3}=\operatorname{ker}\mathbf{A}
and any element of
\operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{0,3}
is an eigenvector of
\mathbf{A}
(hence
\mathbf{L}_{3}
) with eigenvalue 0. From Proposition
4.9
we infer
\operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{0,3}=\left\langle\mathbf{f}_{0,3}\right\rangle
. Since
\mathbf{P}_{0,3}\mathbf{L}_{3}\subset\mathbf{L}_{3}\mathbf{P}_{0,3}
(
[
29
, p. 178, Theorem 6.17]
), we have
\mathbf{L}_{3}\mathbf{P}_{0,3}\mathbf{g}_{0,3}=\mathbf{P}_{0,3}\mathbf{L}_{3}\mathbf{g}_{0,3}=\mathbf{P}_{0,3}\mathbf{f}_{0,3}=\mathbf{f}_{0,3}\neq 0
, this contradicts
\operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{0,3}=\left\langle\mathbf{f}_{0,3}\right\rangle
, thus
n\geq 2
. Now suppose
n>2
, then there exists a
\mathbf{u}\in\operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{0,3}\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{3})
such that
\mathbf{A}^{2}\mathbf{u}
is a nontrivial element of
\operatorname{ker}\mathbf{A}\subset\operatorname{ker}\mathbf{L}_{3}=\langle\mathbf{f}_{0,3}\rangle
, i.e.
\mathbf{L}_{3}^{2}\mathbf{u}=c_{1}\mathbf{f}_{0,3}
for some constant
c_{1}\neq 0
. Without loss of generality, we take
c_{1}=1
for simplicity. Since
\mathbf{L}_{3}\mathbf{g}_{0,3}=\mathbf{f}_{0,3}
, we have
\mathbf{L}_{3}(\mathbf{L}_{3}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{g}_{0,3})=0
, i.e
\mathbf{L}_{3}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{g}_{0,3}=c_{2}\mathbf{f}_{0,3}
for some constant
c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, thus

\mathbf{L}_{3}(\mathbf{u}-c_{2}\mathbf{g}_{0,3})=\mathbf{g}_{0,3}
. However, there are no solutions
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{3})
to the equation
\mathbf{L}_{3}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{g}_{0,3},

see Lemma
B.1
in the Appendix. A similar argument shows that
\mathbf{P}_{1,3}
has rank 1.
∎

4.5
The resolvent estimate

For
0<w_{0}<1
, we define

\displaystyle\Omega_{m,n}:=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}z\in\left[-w_{0},m\right],\operatorname{Im}z\in\left[-n,n\right]\right\}

and

\displaystyle\Omega_{m,n}^{\prime}:=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}z\geq-w_{0}\right\}\backslash\Omega_{m,n}.

The next estimate confines possible unstable eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
to a compact domain
\Omega_{m,n}
.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
\alpha_{0}>0
,
k\geq k_{\alpha_{0}}+3
,
0<w_{0}<1
, and
0<\epsilon<\min\{1,\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}\}
,
there exist
m,n>0
such that
\Omega_{m,n}^{\prime}\subset\rho\left(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\right)
and

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\in\Omega_{m,n}^{\prime}}\left\|\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}}(\lambda)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{k})}\leq C

(4.11)

for all
\alpha\in\overline{B_{\epsilon}}(\alpha_{0})
, where
C>0
is a constant independent of
\alpha
.

Proof.

For
0<\epsilon<\min\{1,\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}\}
and
\alpha\in\overline{B_{\epsilon}}(\alpha_{0})
, we have
\alpha\geq\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}>0
and
\alpha<\alpha_{0}+1
, which implies that
k_{\alpha}\leq k_{\alpha_{0}}+1
. Thus,
k\geq k_{\alpha_{0}}+3\geq k_{\alpha}+2
holds for all
\alpha\in\overline{B_{\epsilon}}(\alpha_{0})
. For any
m,n>0
, by Proposition
4.9
the condition
\lambda\in\Omega_{m,n}^{\prime}
implies
\lambda\in\rho(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
. To prove the resolvent estimate (
4.11
), it suffices to show

\displaystyle\left|\left((\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\right)_{\dot{H}^{k+1}\times\dot{H}^{k}}\right|+|\langle(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{0}|\gtrsim\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2}

(4.12)

for all
\lambda\in\Omega_{m,n}^{\prime}
,
\alpha\in\overline{B_{\epsilon}}(\alpha_{0})
and
\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
. Indeed, if (
4.12
) holds, then by Cauchy’s inequality we have

\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}\lesssim\|(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}},

which implies the resolvent estimate. Now we prove (
4.12
). As in the proof of Proposition
4.8
, it suffices to prove (
4.12
) for all
\mathbf{q}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
. First, we divide the domain
\Omega_{m,n}^{\prime}=S_{1}\cup S_{2}
, where

S_{1}=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}z\geq-w_{0},|\operatorname{Im}z|\geq n\right\}

and

S_{2}=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}z\geq m,\operatorname{Im}z\in[-n,n]\right\}.

Note that

\displaystyle\left((\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\right)_{\dot{H}^{k+1}\times\dot{H}^{k}}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\partial^{k+1}\left(\lambda q_{1}+y\partial_{y}q_{1}-q_{2}\right)\overline{\partial^{k+1}q_{1}}dy

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{-1}^{1}\partial^{k}\left((\lambda+1)q_{2}+y\partial_{y}q_{2}+\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\partial_{y}q_{1}-\partial_{yy}q_{1}\right)\overline{\partial^{k}q_{2}}dy

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}+k+\operatorname{Re}\lambda\right)\left(\|\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}|y\partial^{k+1}q_{1}-\partial^{k}q_{2}|^{2}(\pm 1)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\overline{\partial^{k}q_{2}}dy+\sum_{j=1}^{k}C_{k}^{j}\partial^{j}\left(\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\right)\partial^{k+1-j}q_{1}\overline{\partial^{k}q_{2}}dy

\displaystyle\quad+i\operatorname{Im}\lambda\left(\|\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(y\partial^{2+k}q_{1}\overline{\partial^{k}q_{1}}-y\overline{\partial^{2+k}q_{1}}\partial^{k}q_{1}\right)dy+\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(y\partial^{k+1}q_{2}\overline{\partial^{k}q_{2}}-y\overline{\partial^{k+1}q_{2}}\partial^{k}q_{2}\right)dy

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\overline{\partial^{k+1}q_{2}}-\overline{\partial^{k+1}q_{1}}\partial^{k+1}q_{2}\right)dy+\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\overline{\partial^{k}q_{2}}-\overline{\partial^{k+1}q_{1}}\partial^{k}q_{2}\right)\left|{}_{-1}^{1}\right..

In the above equation, the first two terms on the right hand side are real, while the terms in the last three lines are all pure imaginary. For the two terms in the second line, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\overline{\partial^{k}q_{2}}dy\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}-1}\left(\|\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+1\right)\left(\|\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\right),

and

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{k}C_{k}^{j}\partial^{j}\left(\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\right)\partial^{k+1-j}q_{1}\overline{\partial^{k}q_{2}}dy\right|\leq M\|\partial q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}+\epsilon^{\prime}\left(\|\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\right)

for all
\alpha\in\overline{B_{\epsilon}}(\alpha_{0})
,
where the constant
M=M(\alpha_{0},\epsilon^{\prime})>0
and
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
is sufficiently small. The constant
M
is independent of
\alpha
follows from the following estimates

\left|\partial^{j}\left(\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\right)\right|\lesssim\frac{2\alpha}{(\sqrt{1+\alpha}-1)^{j+1}}=\frac{2(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+1)^{j+1}}{\alpha^{j}}\lesssim\frac{2^{j+1}(\sqrt{1+\alpha_{0}}+2)^{j+1}}{\alpha_{0}^{j}}.

Since
k\geq k_{\alpha}+2
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left((\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\right)_{\dot{H}^{k+1}\times\dot{H}^{k}}\right|+M\|\partial q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\geq\left(\frac{3}{2}+\operatorname{Re}\lambda-\epsilon^{\prime}\right)\left(\|\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\right).

Similarly, for the second term in (
4.12
), we have

\displaystyle\left\langle(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\right\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}\partial\left(\lambda q_{1}+y\partial_{y}q_{1}-q_{2}\right)\overline{\partial q_{1}}dy+\left(\lambda q_{1}+y\partial_{y}q_{1}-q_{2}\right)\overline{q_{1}}|_{-1}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{-1}^{1}\left((\lambda+1)q_{2}+y\partial_{y}q_{2}+\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\partial_{y}q_{1}-\partial_{yy}q_{1}\right)\overline{q_{2}}dy

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\operatorname{Re}\lambda\right)\left(\|\partial q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}|y\partial q_{1}-q_{2}|^{2}(\pm 1)+\operatorname{Re}\lambda|q_{1}(-1)|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\partial q_{1}\overline{q_{2}}dy+\left(y\partial_{y}q_{1}-q_{2}\right)\overline{q_{1}}|_{-1}

\displaystyle\quad+i\operatorname{Im}\lambda\left(\|\partial q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+|q_{1}(-1)|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(y\partial^{2}q_{1}\overline{q_{1}}-y\overline{\partial^{2}q_{1}}q_{1}\right)dy+\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(y\partial q_{2}\overline{q_{2}}-y\overline{\partial q_{2}}q_{2}\right)dy

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\partial q_{1}\overline{\partial q_{2}}-\overline{\partial q_{1}}\partial q_{2}\right)dy+\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial q_{1}\overline{q_{2}}-\overline{\partial q_{1}}q_{2}\right)\left|{}_{-1}^{1}\right..

In the above equation, the terms in the first line are all real, and the terms in the last three lines are all pure imaginary. For the terms in the second line, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{2\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha}+y}\partial q_{1}\overline{q_{2}}dy\right|\leq\left(\sqrt{1+\alpha}+1\right)\left(\|\partial q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

and

\displaystyle\left|\left(y\partial_{y}q_{1}-q_{2}\right)\overline{q_{1}}|_{-1}\right|

\displaystyle\leq(\|\partial q_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|q_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}})\|q_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim(\|\partial q_{1}\|_{H^{1}}+\|q_{2}\|_{H^{1}})\|q_{1}\|_{H^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{\prime}\left(\|\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\right)+M_{0}\left(\|\partial q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right).

For the terms in the last two lines, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(y\partial^{2}q_{1}\overline{q_{1}}-y\overline{\partial^{2}q_{1}}q_{1}\right)dy\right|\leq\|\partial^{2}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\|q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon^{\prime}\|\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+M_{1}\left(\|\partial q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+|q_{1}(-1)|^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(y\partial q_{2}\overline{q_{2}}-y\overline{\partial q_{2}}q_{2}\right)dy\right|\leq\|\partial q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\|q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon^{\prime}\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+M_{2}\|q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle\left|\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\partial q_{1}\overline{\partial q_{2}}-\overline{\partial q_{1}}\partial q_{2}\right)dy\right|\leq 2\|\partial q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\epsilon^{\prime}\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+M_{3}\|\partial q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left|\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial q_{1}\overline{q_{2}}-\overline{\partial q_{1}}q_{2}\right)\left|{}_{-1}^{1}\right.\right|&\leq 2|\partial q_{1}|_{L^{\infty}}|q_{2}|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim|\partial q_{1}|_{H^{1}}|q_{2}|_{H^{1}}\\
&\leq\epsilon^{\prime}\left(\|\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\right)+M_{4}\left(\|\partial q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right),\end{aligned}

where constants
M_{i}>0,i=0,\dots,4
are independent of
\alpha
. Thus, let
M^{\prime}=\sum_{i=0}^{4}M_{i}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\right\rangle_{0}\right|+4\epsilon^{\prime}\left(\|\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial^{k}q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\right)\geq\left(\operatorname{Im}\lambda-\sqrt{1+\alpha}-1-M^{\prime}\right)\left\langle\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\right\rangle_{0}

Combine the two estimates, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left((\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\right)_{\dot{H}^{k+1}\times\dot{H}^{k}}\right|+|\langle(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{0}|

when
\epsilon^{\prime}
is sufficiently small,
\operatorname{Im}\lambda
is sufficiently large and
\operatorname{Re}\lambda\geq-w_{0}
, i.e (
4.12
) holds for
\lambda\in S_{1}
if we take

large enough.

It remains to prove (
4.12
) for

. In this case, by Proposition

and Hille-Yosida theorem for the strongly continuous semigroup

and its generator

where

5
Growth bound of the linearized flow

The above spectral analysis leads to a sufficiently complete description of the linearized evolution.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\alpha_{0}>0
,
k\geq k_{\alpha_{0}}+3
,
0<w_{0}<1
,
0<\epsilon<\min\{1,\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}\}
, and
\alpha\in\overline{B_{\epsilon}}(\alpha_{0})

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s),\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}\right]=\left[\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s),\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\right]=\mathbf{0}

for all
s\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}&=e^{s}\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha},\\
\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}&=\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}+s\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha},\\
\left\|\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}(B)}&\leq Me^{-w_{0}s}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}(B)}\end{aligned}

for all
s\geq 0,\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
, and
\alpha\in\overline{B_{\epsilon}}(\alpha_{0})
, where
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}:=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}-\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
,
M=M(\alpha_{0},w_{0})
is a constant depending only on
\alpha_{0},w_{0}
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}&=\left\langle\mathbf{f}_{1,\alpha}\right\rangle\\
\operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}&=\left\langle\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha},\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}\right\rangle,\end{aligned}

where
\mathbf{f}_{1,\alpha}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
is an eigenfunction of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
with eigenvalue 1,
\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
is an eigenfunction of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
with eigenvalues 0, and
\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
is a generalized eigenfunction of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
with eigenvalues 0 satisfying
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}=\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha}
. Finally,
\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}=

\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}=\mathbf{0}
.

Proof.

Any strongly continuous semigroup commutes with its generator and hence also with the resolvent of its generator. This implies that
\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}
commutes with the spectral projections
\mathbf{P}_{\lambda,\alpha},\lambda\in\{0,1\}
. By Proposition
4.9
and Lemma
4.10
, we have

\operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}=\langle\mathbf{f}_{1,\alpha}\rangle,\quad\operatorname{rg}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}=\left\langle\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha},\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}\right\rangle.

Thus, we derive

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}\mathbf{q},

and

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\mathbf{q},

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{L}^{2}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\mathbf{q}=0,

which yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}\mathbf{q}=e^{s}\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}\mathbf{q},

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\mathbf{q}=s\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\mathbf{q}.

It remains to prove the uniform growth bound of
\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)|_{\operatorname{rg}(\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha})}
. By
[
29
, p. 178, Theorem 6.17]
,
\sigma(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\operatorname{rg}(\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha})})\subset\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}z\leq-1\right\}
and the resolvent
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\operatorname{rg}(\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha})}}(\lambda)
is analytic in
\Omega_{m,n}
for all
m,n>0
. By Proposition
4.11
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\operatorname{rg}(\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha})}}(\lambda)\right\|\leq C

for all
\lambda\in\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}z\geq-w_{0}\right\}
and
\alpha\in\overline{B_{\epsilon}}(\alpha_{0})
, where the constant
C>0
is independent of
\alpha
. Then, the uniform growth bound follows from an adaption of the Gearhart-Prüss-Greiner Theorem (
[
23
, p. 302, Theorem 1.11]
), see also
[
45
, Appendix A]
.

∎

6
Nonlinear stability

Now we derive the nonlinear stability. The full nonlinear equation can be written as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{s}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}),\\
&\mathbf{q}(0,\cdot)=\mathbf{q}_{0}\end{aligned}\right.

(6.1)

where

\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}):=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
(\partial_{y}q_{1})^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

By Duhamel’s principle, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}(s,\cdot)=\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{q}(0,\cdot)+\int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s-\tau)N(\mathbf{q}(\tau))d\tau.

Appendix A
Frobenius analysis

Recall that the eigen-equation (
3.8
) can be rewritten as the standard form

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime\prime}+\left[\frac{\gamma}{z}+\frac{\delta}{z-1}+\frac{\epsilon}{z-d}\right]\varphi^{\prime}+\frac{abz-c}{z(z-1)}\varphi=0,

(A.1)

where we take
\gamma=\lambda-\sqrt{1+\alpha},\delta=\lambda+\sqrt{1+\alpha},d=-\frac{\sqrt{1+\alpha}-1}{2},a=\lambda,b=\lambda+1,c=-\frac{1}{2}(\lambda^{2}+\lambda)(\sqrt{1+\alpha}-1),\epsilon=2=\alpha+\beta+1-\gamma-\delta.

By Frobenius theory, we can always find an infinite series
local
solution of the form

\displaystyle\varphi(z)=(z-z_{0})^{s}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(z-z_{0})^{k}

(A.2)

near each singular point
z_{0}\in\{0,1\}
. More precisely, by substituting (
A.2
) into (
A.1
) and matching the lowest order term, we have the following indicial polynomials

\displaystyle P_{0}(s)=s(s-1+\gamma)=0\text{~for~}z_{0}=0,

and

\displaystyle P_{1}(s)=s(s-1+\delta)=0\text{~for~}z_{0}=1.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>-1,
and
k\geq k_{\alpha}+2
, then any
H^{k+1}(0,1)
solution to (
A.1
) must belong to
C^{\infty}[0,1]
. Moreover, the set of local smooth solutions to (
A.1
) around
1
is one-dimensional.

Proof.

We prove by performing Frobenius analysis at
z_{0}=0
and
z_{0}=1
respectively. For
z_{0}=0
, the two roots of the corresponding indicial polynomial
P_{0}(s)
are given by

s_{+}=1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\lambda,\quad s_{-}=0,\quad\textit{~if~}-1<\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leq 1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}

and

s_{+}=0,\quad s_{-}=1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\lambda,\quad\textit{~if~}\operatorname{Re}\lambda>1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}.

Therefore, we have

Case 1

if
-1<\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leq 1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}
and
s_{+}-s_{-}\notin\mathbb{N}_{0}
, by Lemma
3.12
the two independent local solutions are given by

\displaystyle\varphi_{01}=z^{s_{+}}h_{+}(z)\quad\text{~and~}\quad\varphi_{02}=h_{-}(z),

where
h_{\pm}
are analytic functions around
0
. Note that
\varphi_{01}
does not belong to
H^{k_{\alpha}+3}(0,1)
since
s_{+}\notin\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\operatorname{Re}s_{+}-k_{\alpha}-3<-1
. Thus, any
H^{k+1}
local solutions must be a multiple of
\varphi_{02}
, thus is smooth and analytic at
0
.

Case 2

if
-1<\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leq 1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}
and
s_{+}-s_{-}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the two independent local solutions are given by

\displaystyle\varphi_{01}=z^{s_{+}}h_{+}(z)\quad\text{~and~}\quad\varphi_{02}=h_{-}(z)+c^{\prime}z^{s_{+}}h_{+}(z)\log(z).

where
h_{\pm}
are analytic functions around
0
and
c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
is a (possibly vanishing) constant. Note that when
c^{\prime}\neq 0
,
c^{\prime}z^{s_{+}}h_{+}(z)\log(z)
does not belong to
H^{k_{\alpha}+3}(0,1)
, so
\varphi_{02}\notin H^{k+1}(0,1)
. Thus, any
H^{k+1}
local solution is a multiple of
\varphi_{01}
unless
c^{\prime}=0
. In either case, the
H^{k+1}
local solution must be smooth and analytic at
0
.

Case 3

if
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}
,
the two independent local solutions are given by

\displaystyle\varphi_{01}=h_{+}(z)\quad\text{~and~}\quad\varphi_{02}=z^{s_{-}}h_{-}(z)+c^{\prime}h_{+}(z)\log(z),

where
h_{\pm}
are analytic functions around
0
and
c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
is a (possibly vanishing) constant. Since
\operatorname{Re}s_{-}=1+\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0
,
\varphi_{02}
does not belong to
H^{k+1}(0,1)
regardless the choice of
c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
. Thus, any
H^{k+1}(0,1)
local smooth solution around
z_{0}=0
is a multiple of
\varphi_{01}
, thus is smooth and analytic at
0
.

Now, we consider
z_{0}=1
, then the two roots of the corresponding indicial polynomial
P_{1}(s)
are given by

s_{+}=1-\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\lambda,\quad s_{-}=0,\quad\text{if~}-1<\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leq 1-\sqrt{1+\alpha}

and

s_{+}=0,\quad s_{-}=1-\sqrt{1+\alpha}-\lambda,\quad\text{if~}\operatorname{Re}\lambda>1-\sqrt{1+\alpha}.

Therefore, we have

Case 1’

if
-1<\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leq 1-\sqrt{1+\alpha}
and
s_{+}-s_{-}\notin\mathbb{N}_{0}
, by Lemma
3.12
the two independent local solutions are given by

\displaystyle\varphi_{01}=(z-1)^{s_{+}}h_{+}(z-1)\quad\text{~and~}\quad\varphi_{02}=h_{-}(z-1),

where
h_{\pm}
are analytic functions around
0
. Note that
\varphi_{01}
does not belong to

and

\displaystyle\varphi_{01}=h_{+}(z-1)\quad\text{~and~}\quad\varphi_{02}=h_{-}(z-1)+c^{\prime}h_{+}(z-1)\log(z-1).

\varphi_{02}\notin H^{k+1}(0,1)
. Thus, any
H^{k+1}
local solution must be a multiple of
\varphi_{01}
, thus is smooth and analytic at
1
.

Case 3’

if
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>1-\sqrt{1+\alpha}
,
the two independent local solutions are given by

\displaystyle\varphi_{01}=h_{+}(z-1)\quad\text{~and~}\quad\varphi_{02}=(z-1)^{s_{-}}h_{-}(z-1)+c^{\prime}h_{+}(z-1)\log(z-1),

where

. Thus, any

In summary, any

Appendix B
Non-existence of other generalized eigenfunctions

Lemma B.1
.

There does not exist functions
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{H}^{4}
such that
\mathbf{L}_{3}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{g}_{0,3}.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathcal{H}^{4}
satisfies
\mathbf{L}_{3}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{g}_{0,3},
then we have

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &v_{2}-y\partial_{y}v_{1}=g_{0,3,1}\\
&\partial_{yy}v_{1}-\frac{6}{2+y}\partial_{y}v_{1}-v_{2}-y\partial_{y}v_{2}=g_{0,3,2}.\end{aligned}\right.

Using the first equation to solve for
v_{2}
, we see that solving this system of ODEs reduces to

\displaystyle\left(2y+\frac{6}{2+y}\right)\partial_{y}v_{1}+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}v_{1}=g,

where

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=-g_{0,3,1}-y\partial_{y}g_{0,3,1}-g_{0,3,2}

\displaystyle=\log(2+y)+\frac{1}{(2+y)^{2}}\left(y^{2}-\frac{3}{4}y-\frac{5}{2}\right).

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{y}v_{1}=cp(y)^{-1}+p(y)^{-1}\int_{y}^{1}\frac{p(z)g(z)}{1-z^{2}}dz,\quad p(y)=\left(\frac{1-y}{1+y}\right)^{2}(2+y)^{2},

where
c\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant. Since
v_{1}\in L^{2}(\frac{1}{2},1)
, we have
c=0
.
It suffices to show that
\partial_{y}v_{1}\notin H^{3}(-1,-\frac{1}{2})
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle(1-y)^{2}(2+y)^{2}\partial_{y}v_{1}=(1+y)^{2}\int_{y}^{1}\frac{G(z)}{(1+z)^{3}}dz,

where
G(z)=(1-z)\left((2+z)^{2}\log(2+z)+z^{2}-\frac{3}{4}z-\frac{5}{2}\right)
. By Taylor expansion, we can write

G(z)=-\frac{3}{2}-\frac{11}{4}(1+z)+\frac{27}{4}(1+z)^{2}+R,

with
R=O((1+z)^{3})
for
z\to-1
. Then,

\displaystyle(1+y)^{2}\int_{y}^{1}\frac{G(z)}{(1+z)^{3}}dz

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{25}{16}+\frac{27}{4}\log 2\right)(1+y)^{2}-\frac{11}{4}(1+y)-\frac{3}{4}

\displaystyle-\frac{27}{4}(1+y)^{2}\log(1+y)+(1+y)^{2}\int_{y}^{1}\frac{R(z)}{(1+z)^{3}}dz.

Note that
(1+y)^{2}\int_{y}^{1}\frac{R(z)}{(1+z)^{3}}dz
and
\frac{1}{(1-y)^{2}(2+y)^{2}}
are smooth near
-1
, thus
\partial_{y}v_{1}\notin H^{3}(-1,-\frac{1}{2})
due to the term
-\frac{27}{4}(1+y)^{2}\log(1+y)
above.

∎
