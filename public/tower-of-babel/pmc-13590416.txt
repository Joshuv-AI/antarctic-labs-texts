Disparity in the distribution of suitable habitat as a metric for capturing responses to contrasting land‐use types
Authors: Joscha Beninde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal species observations from community science platforms offer an unprecedented opportunity to analyze large‐scale patterns of biodiversity. This holds great promise for developing data‐driven metrics to measure species' responses to landscape‐level environmental changes, essential for ameliorating the ongoing biodiversity crisis. A primary cause of declines in local populations of native species is the expansion and intensification of anthropogenic land uses, foremost urbanization. While species' responses to urbanization can remain consistent across spatial and temporal scales, city‐specific factors, such as land‐use dynamics or local biotic interactions, can profoundly influence populations and shift responses. This underscores the importance of measuring responses to urbanized landscapes at the local population level and the need for a quantitative method to track changing responses as they occur. For this purpose, I propose applying the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and its associated area under the curve (AUC), a well‐established continuous summary metric. Specifically, the novel habitat disparity (HD) metric quantifies variation in the spatial distribution of suitable habitat between two contrasting land‐use types. Applying it here to a highly urbanized landscape, HDURBAN quantifies the urban affinity of 1023 species using geographic predictions of their environmental niches. Additionally, modeling virtual species for each urban response type introduced in Blair's seminal 1996 study provides place‐based, expected HDURBAN values for urban avoiders, urban utilizers, and urban dwellers. Validation efforts demonstrate accurate predictions of expected urban responses of real species in three scenarios. First, following a route‐of‐introduction hypothesis, non‐native species exhibit significantly higher HDURBAN than native species. Second, assessing the consistency of urban responses in space and time, HDURBAN is significantly correlated with the urban responses of the same 38 bird species from Blair's study, conducted 450 km apart and 25 years earlier. Third, testing for sensitivity to temporal shifts within species, HDURBAN decreases significantly in a species displaced from urban areas due to negative interactions with a recently introduced, congeneric species. In summary, HD is a versatile metric that provides a quantitative way to compare local population‐level responses, supporting future research into the patterns and processes underlying spatial and temporal variation in response to contrasting land‐use types.Keywords: ecological niche modeling, global environmental change, habitat suitability, landscape change, range shifts, species distribution modeling, species distribution patterns, Urban biodiversity, Urbanness, Urban tolerance

INTRODUCTION

Openly accessible community science platforms, such as iNaturalist (Di Cecco et al., 2021) and eBird (Sullivan et al., 2009), are gaining popularity among researchers and casual users alike, leading to rapidly growing datasets of species occurrences worldwide. This provides an unprecedented opportunity to examine large‐scale patterns of biodiversity and holds great promise for developing data‐driven metrics quantifying species' responses to landscape and environmental changes. These metrics can be utilized to understand and mitigate the ongoing biodiversity crisis (Bayraktarov et al., 2019; Devictor & Bensaude‐Vincent, 2016; Moll et al., 2019). One of the primary causes of local species declines is the expansion and intensification of urbanized and agricultural landscapes (Aronson et al., 2014; Cooper et al., 2021; Grimm et al., 2008; Newbold et al., 2015, 2020). In urbanized landscapes, patterns in species distribution, abundance, or density are traditionally loosely categorized as a function of urban land‐use tolerance or association (Blair, 1996; Fischer et al., 2015; McDonnell & Hahs, 2008, 2015; McDonnell & Pickett, 1990; McKinney, 2002). In a seminal study published in this journal, Blair (1996) classified bird species responses using a three‐tiered approach. He defined urban avoiders as sensitive to urbanization, with peak densities in wildland areas; suburban adaptable species had the highest densities at intermediate levels of urbanization; and urban exploiters had density peaks in urban areas. Considerable further scrutiny (Fischer et al., 2015; McDonnell & Hahs, 2015; McKinney, 2002) has slightly altered Blair's typology, and species are now more commonly referred to as urban avoiders, urban utilizers, and urban dwellers (Callaghan, Major, et al., 2020; Fischer et al., 2015). Due to their ease of comprehension and intuitive nature, these response types have largely replaced terms typically used to describe species distribution patterns in other landscapes, such as resident, short‐ and long‐distance migrant, nomad, or invader (McDonnell & Hahs, 2015). Whether this shift in terminology is warranted or not, the three‐tiered typology is widely applied in research and conservation, often without quantitative justification, thereby precluding efforts to develop continuous and comparable metrics of urban responses for broader use.

Data‐driven efforts that quantify response magnitudes often focus on only a few species, a single taxonomic group, or a single urban landscape (Callaghan et al., 2019; Fidino et al., 2021; Kuussaari et al., 2021; Moll et al., 2020). More comprehensive efforts are emerging (Callaghan, Major, et al., 2020; Callaghan, Ozeroff, et al., 2020; Curti et al., 2023), and quantify responses by summarizing continuous measures of urbanization at species presence locations, for example, using night‐time lights (Callaghan, Major, et al., 2020; Callaghan, Ozeroff, et al., 2020) or PCA‐based summaries of urban intensity (Curti et al., 2023). These approaches provide continuous measures of urban responses and allow relative comparisons of species responses within the study. At the same time, variation in the underlying measurement scales makes direct quantitative comparisons with other species or landscapes challenging or impossible. Moreover, inferences about landscape‐level impacts may be confounded when analyses focus solely on the environmental conditions at locations where species occurrence records are available. This can occur when sampling effort is unevenly distributed across a species' environmental niche or when the availability of environmental conditions throughout a landscape is not considered. Species distribution modeling (SDM) can directly help address these issues and limitations by accounting for spatial observation bias and projecting suitable habitat geographically across the entire study area as a function of environmental conditions (Grether et al., 2024; Merow et al., 2013; Valavi et al., 2022). Building on these outputs, an appropriate metric could contextualize measurements of urban responses on a standardized, readily interpretable scale, enabling comparisons across taxonomic, temporal, and spatial contexts.

Metrics of responses to urban areas ideally capture their magnitude accurately while also being sensitive to detect shifts in responses. Predominantly, urban response types are conceptualized at the species level—as a species' inherent property. Therefore, regardless of environmental conditions or time periods, a species' urban response type has traditionally been considered static. For example, in avian species, similar responses of species have been observed across continental and local scales (Callaghan, Major, et al., 2020). Examples of shifts in species' urban responses are less common and are often documented only in some populations or within specific urban landscapes; for example, species' distributions may shift more towards urban areas as urban resource use increases (Cooper et al., 2021; Rutz, 2008). While this may be an exception to the species‐level consistency of urban responses, it emphasizes the importance of measuring urban responses locally and the need for a quantitative method to track changes as they occur. To address this need, I propose here the use of a well‐established continuous statistical evaluation criterion: the receiver operating characteristic (ROC; Pollack & Decker, 1958), and its associated area under the curve (AUC; Araújo et al., 2019; Hanley & McNeil, 1982). The AUC is a continuous summary metric ranging from 0 to 1 (Hanley & McNeil, 1982; Merow et al., 2013). In the SDM literature, AUC is particularly well known as a measure of a model's discriminative ability, calculated by comparing SDM predictions from presence and absence (or background) locations (Araújo et al., 2019; Phillips and Dudík 2008; Valavi et al., 2021). By comparing SDM predictions across contrasting land‐use types, this approach effectively converts AUC into a metric that summarizes disparity in the spatial distribution of suitable habitat, or habitat disparity (HD) for short.

I introduce and validate HD here as a metric to quantify local population ecological responses in urbanized landscapes and refer to it as HDURBAN in this context. Owing to the convenient statistical properties of AUC, this approach can be readily extended with a virtual species modeling framework (Hirzel et al., 2001; Meynard et al., 2019) to generate expected HDURBAN values for the three urban response types (urban avoider, urban utilizer, and urban dweller). This approach offers the unique opportunity to directly compare real species with the expected ranges of values for these urban response types in a given focal landscape and enables straightforward standardization across landscapes. Here, I calculate HDURBAN for a published SDM dataset of 1023 species from the Greater Los Angeles metropolitan area, California, USA (Beninde et al., 2023). Due to the steep environmental gradient from urban to wildland habitats in this area (Figure 1a), this dataset is particularly well suited for evaluating populations' responses to contrasting land‐use types. MATERIALS AND METHODS

Overview

To measure population‐level responses to locally contrasting land uses, this study applies the ROC (Pollack & Decker, 1958) and its associated AUC (Araújo et al., 2019; Hanley & McNeil, 1982). This approach is applied to a habitat suitability dataset available for the urbanized landscape of Greater Los Angeles (Beninde et al., 2023). Generated using a species distribution modeling (SDM) framework (also commonly referred to as environmental niche or habitat suitability modeling), the resulting habitat suitability values can be interpreted as a geographic prediction of species' realized niche space (Merow et al., 2013; Valavi et al., 2022). SDM approaches are informative and widely used, for example, in biodiversity assessments (Riva et al., 2023) or analyses of niche differentiation (Grether et al., 2024), capturing ecologically meaningful results when best practice modeling standards are met (Araújo et al., 2019). The modeling framework employed by Beninde et al. (2023) meets these standards (see the Section 2.2 below). Importantly, the analyses presented here are based on the geographic projection of species' niches, making them suitable for inferring landscape‐level impacts on species' distributions (see Grether et al., 2024 for discussion). By comparing habitat suitability values drawn separately from the focal and the non‐focal land‐use type, the resulting AUC values are effectively converted into a metric that summarizes the disparity of suitable habitat distribution, or HD for short (see Section 2.3 below).

The Greater Los Angeles dataset

This provides an overview of the taxonomic breadth, geographical setting, and modeling framework employed to train the habitat suitability models of the published dataset of 1023 species in Greater Los Angeles (Beninde et al., 2023), which underlies the analyses presented here. All species have at least one fully terrestrial life stage and represent all higher taxonomic groups: plants (45.5%), arthropods (27.45%), vertebrates (22.2%), fungi (3.2%), mollusks (1.3%), and other taxonomic groups (<0.3%). The study extent covers 7797 km2 in Greater Los Angeles, fully encompassing the City of Los Angeles, large parts of the Los Angeles‐Long Beach‐Anaheim, CA Metropolitan Statistical Area, plus parts of adjacent Ventura County in the west, small parts of Riverside and San Bernardino Counties to the east, and into Orange County in the south (Figure 1b). Developed land‐use types predominate (60.3%), followed by mostly vegetated areas (37.2%), while highly managed, working landscapes, such as agricultural areas, are uncommon (0.7%; based on the National Land Cover Dataset; U.S. Geological Survey, 2014). I used the binary U.S. Census Bureau land delineation (U.S. Census Bureau, Population Division, 2018; Figure 1c), to classify the landscape into urban and nonurban areas. This assigns an urban classification to densely developed residential and commercial areas, using an iterative approach based on minimum population sizes (50,000) or housing‐unit density (2000) and the proximity to urban areas. The study area is 65.1% urban and home to a dense human population of 13.4 million (2640 people/km2, calculated from Rose et al., 2017). The remaining 34.9% of the area is nonurban and collectively referred to as wildlands. They entail vast expanses of native vegetation, with little development, including parts or all of the Santa Monica Mountains, Simi Hills, Santa Susana Mountains, Verdugo Hills, Griffith Park, San Gabriel Mountains, Chino Hills, Santa Ana Mountains, and the San Joaquin Hills (Figure 1c). They are sparsely populated, with a modest combined population of 86,700 humans and a density almost two orders of magnitude lower (32 people/km2; calculated from data in Rose et al., 2017), which frames the extensive urban areas within the study extent. This landscape is uniquely suited to study the response of species to a sharp urban‐wildland interface separating an urban megacity from the immediately adjacent wildlands (Figure 1a).

The modeling framework used observations of species available from the community science platform iNaturalist. Such datasets are commonly referred to as unstructured datasets, which emphasizes their associated sampling biases due to the absence of an underlying (scientifically informed) sampling strategy (Fourcade et al., 2014; Kramer‐Schadt et al., 2013). The modeling framework applied by Beninde et al. (2023) employed various approaches to address these causes of potential biases, for example, by thinning occurrence records, scaling background point selection to sampling effort, modeling separately in different extents reflecting land‐use types (urban or wildland), null‐modeling validation, and using different modeling algorithms to train models. For the analyses presented here, I utilize the set of geographic habitat suitability predictions stemming from the most robust modeling approach. This approach (1) uses down‐sampled random forest modeling following Valavi et al. (2021) on (2) occurrence records thinned to one record per raster cell, (3) a random background point selection, (4) is performed within the entire study extent (as opposed to separately in urban and wildland habitats), (5) for species with a minimum of 25 occurrence records (after the spatial thinning of 2), and (6) includes only those models that exceed null model expectations. A total of 1023 species fulfilled these requirements.

Using the ROC curve and its AUC as a metric of HD

The AUC is a statistical method used to evaluate the performance of a continuous index as a binary classifier, originating in signal detection theory (Peterson et al., 1954). It has since been adopted in various fields for evaluating diagnostic tests and predictive models, including medical sciences, psychology, environmental sciences, and machine learning (Hanley & McNeil, 1982; Mason & Graham, 2002; Pollack & Decker, 1958). It provides a visual and quantitative measure of a model's ability to discriminate between two classes, with the AUC representing the model's discriminatory performance. The primary advantages of AUC are that it is scale‐free (ranging from 0 to 1) and threshold‐independent, providing an easily interpretable and readily comparable summary metric of performance across different thresholds of the index (Araújo et al., 2019; Merow et al., 2013; Valavi et al., 2022).

In species distribution modeling, the AUC is widely used to evaluate a model's ability to discriminate between presence and absence (or background) locations of species, with the predicted habitat suitability values serving as the index (Araújo et al., 2019; Pearce & Ferrier, 2000; Valavi et al., 2022). Performance is then evaluated by plotting the sensitivity (true positive rate) and 1‐specificity (false positive rate) for correctly classifying presences and absences at each threshold (e.g., from 2 × 2 confusion matrices), which allows calculating the AUC. In presence‐only modeling (the approach underlying the models used here), absences are unknown (or uncertain) and are replaced with background locations (Merow et al., 2013; Valavi et al., 2022). These can be sampled randomly within the modeling extent or scaled to the distribution of sampling effort in the landscape (Fourcade et al., 2014).

In its conventional application to evaluate model performance, an AUC score of 1 indicates a theoretically perfect model, where higher habitat suitability values are observed at all presence locations (the targeted outcome class) with no overlap with lower habitat suitability values at all absence locations (Araújo et al., 2019, Valavi et al., 2022). An intermediate AUC score of 0.5 suggests that the model performs no better than random predictions of presences and absences (note, however, that AUC values of random or null models can vary around 0.5, mainly as a function of the sample size used to train models; Beninde et al., 2023; Raes & Steege, 2007). Consequently, AUC scores reported in species distribution modeling are typically well above 0.5, indicating that models predict presences considerably better than random. Conversely, a theoretical AUC score of 0 indicates that the model performs in the opposite direction of its intended purpose, predicting lower habitat suitability values at all presence locations, with no overlap with higher habitat suitability values at absence locations. Therefore, models with AUC values approaching 0 are generally disregarded in species distribution modeling because they lack utility for predicting species presence, despite their exceptional discriminatory ability. The discriminatory potential of such models has been explored previously, showing improvements in model fit by inverting predictions from models with AUC <0.5 (Fawcett, 2004; Flach & Wu, 2005). However, this property of AUC is rarely reported or utilized. This highlights an unexplored opportunity to leverage AUC in a context where its full informational range is both meaningful and desirable. Species responses to contrasting land‐use types represent such a case, as positive, negative, and neutral responses can all be expected, rendering the entire AUC range ecologically interpretable.

Implementing the AUC in this manner requires a binary land classification of the study extent into focal and non‐focal locations (Figure 1c). Here, I classify the study extent into urban and nonurban wildland locations based on U.S. Census data (U.S. Census Bureau, Population Division, 2018; see Section 2.2 above). By drawing habitat suitability values from urban and wildland locations and defining urban locations as the targeted outcome class (rather than species‐presence locations), HDURBAN quantifies the degree to which suitable habitat is disproportionately associated with urban rather than wildland locations. Accordingly, HDURBAN increases with increasing urban affinity. Specifically, values closer to the maximum of 1 indicate high HD and strong urban affinity, characterized by habitat suitability values that are consistently higher at urban locations and exhibit little or no overlap with lower values at wildland locations. Values near 0.5 indicate no HD, meaning that habitat suitability is indistinguishable between urban and wildland locations. Conversely, values closer to the minimum of 0 also indicate high HD but low urban affinity, characterized by habitat suitability values that are consistently higher at wildland locations and exhibit little or no overlap with lower values at urban locations (Figure 2).

Columns illustrate the corresponding predicted habitat suitability, the distributions of habitat suitability values in urban and wildland areas, and the resulting HDURBAN values.

Importantly, HDURBAN measures the degree of disparity in habitat suitability values between urban and nonurban locations, rather than directly quantifying habitat preference for a specific intensity of these land‐use types. While this may seem trivial and conceptually difficult to distinguish, it is a significant difference in measurement. Using the setting of this study as an example, a species with HDURBAN approaching 0 has much higher habitat suitability in all wildland locations and much lower suitability in all urban locations; however, this does not necessitate that this species also has the highest suitability in the “wildest” locations. And vice versa, a species with HDURBAN approaching 1 may not necessarily have the highest suitability in the most heavily urbanized locations.

Modeling virtual species for urban response types

To facilitate quantitative comparisons between HDURBAN of real species and those expected for the three traditionally used urban ecological response types, I employed a virtual species modeling approach (Hirzel et al., 2001; Meynard et al., 2019). The virtual species were created reflecting the definitions by Fischer et al. (2015). Fischer defines urban avoiders to “rarely occur in developed areas” and distinguishes urban utilizers and dwellers by varying “relative importance of natural and developed areas to population dynamics”. Further, the persistence of urban dwellers is independent of natural areas, while urban utilizers still require natural areas for maintaining population dynamics, implying ecological‐trap dynamics imposed on these species by urban areas (“breeders […] are present only because of dispersal from adjacent natural areas”; Fischer et al., 2015). Here, I largely adopt Fischer's definitions, with urban avoiders and urban dwellers characterized by opposite responses to urbanization; the former dependent on resources in more natural, wildland areas, and the latter dependent on resources from urban areas. For urban utilizers, however, I relax the ecological trap dynamic denoted by Fischer and define them as showing no discernible difference (disparity) in suitable habitat between urban and nonurban habitats. This resembles Blair's initial definition and implies similar long‐term breeding success in both habitats (while acknowledging that ecological traps may govern the dynamics of some species, at least temporarily). Practically, three different virtual species were created, one characteristic for each of the three response types: (1) virtual urban avoiders were trained using presence locations exclusively located in wildland areas; (2) virtual urban utilizers using presence locations distributed across the entire modeling extent, encompassing both urban and wildland areas; and (3) virtual urban dweller using presence locations exclusively from urban areas. For each virtual species type, 100 down‐sampled random forest models were trained using the same modeling framework as for the real species, and the same environmental predictor data (see above for details). The number of presence locations used for training virtual species models was randomly drawn from the number of occurrence records used for training the 1023 real species (mean N = 146 ± 6.38 SE), and presences were distributed randomly in the landscape given the spatial constraints of each of the response types, that is, (1)–(3).

Calculating HDURBAN

HDURBAN is calculated here using habitat suitability values of 100 randomly drawn raster cells from both urban and wildland locations. The AUC is then calculated from 2 × 2 confusion matrices generated at different habitat suitability thresholds using the evalmod() function in the “precrec” R‐package (v0.14.4; Saito & Rehmsmeier, 2017), with urban values as the target outcome class. To generate representative AUC estimates, this procedure was repeated 100 times for each species, both real and virtual, and the mean was taken as the HDURBAN value for that species. This approach yielded stable AUC estimates in this study area, whereas larger or more environmentally heterogeneous landscapes may require a larger sample. Additionally, we report the SD values of AUC estimates in the supplemental data file (Beninde, 2026).

Validation

Comparing presumed modes of population establishment, I tested whether non‐native species have higher HDURBAN than native species. Given that urban areas are a frequent pathway for the introduction and subsequent establishment of non‐native species (Cadotte et al., 2017; Gaertner et al., 2017; McLean et al., 2017), a higher urban association can be expected for non‐native than for native species. This was tested by categorizing all real species with data available (N = 996) from Beninde et al. (2023) into non‐native (N = 705) and native bins (N = 291) and testing for differences in HDURBAN using Mood's Median Test in the “coin” R‐package (v1.4–3; Hothorn et al., 2008).

To test for spatiotemporal consistency in urban responses across species, I compared HDURBAN with the urban association ranks reported by Blair (1996) for a set of bird species shared by both datasets. Blair conducted fieldwork in 1992 and 1993, more than 25 years before the majority of the species observations synthesized by Beninde et al. (2023) were collected (median date of species observations: November 2019). Blair's field observations were conducted near Palo Alto, California, USA, which is more than 450 km from all locations in this study (Figure 1). This temporal and spatial difference between the two datasets enables assessment of the spatiotemporal consistency of species' urban responses. Data from Blair's study were taken from Figure 3 of his publication (1996), which ranks 40 species based on “greatest daily density in business district and greatest daily density in biological preserve.” The business district represents the most urban study site, and the biological preserve represents the most natural. This ordering was compared with the HDURBAN values generated here using a Spearman rank correlation for the 38 bird species common to both datasets, and for visualization only, we fitted a LOESS smoothing curve with 95% confidence intervals.

Urban affinity of (a) all 1023 species, (b) native species only, and (c) non‐native species only. Background shading indicates the expected HDURBAN ranges for the three urban response types, based on a virtual species modeling approach.

To assess whether HDURBAN can detect temporal shifts in urban responses, I tested for a decline in HDURBAN calculated across successive time intervals in a species known to have declined in urban areas, but not in adjacent wildland areas, within the study area. As a case study, I utilize data from the native Western Black Widow, Latrodectus hesperus, and compare it to that of the congeneric, non‐native, and recently introduced Brown Widow, Latrodectus geometrics. The Western Black Widow has historically been widespread in Southern California, including in urban areas, and it was the only species of Latrodectus spider known from Greater Los Angeles until the early 2000s (Vincent et al., 2008). In 2003, the Brown Widow was first reported from within the study extent (Torrance, Los Angeles County; Figure 1c) and subsequently spread rapidly (Vincent et al., 2008). By 2008, it had been recorded in as many as 31 cities across Los Angeles and Orange Counties, but it remains restricted in distribution to urban areas (Aragon‐Traverso, 2021; Vetter et al., 2012; Vincent et al., 2008). This rapid spread coincided with a local decline of Western Black Widows in urban areas, a pattern not observed in adjacent wildland areas. Here, Western Black Widows persist while Brown Widows are mostly absent (Aragon‐Traverso, 2021, Vetter et al., 2012). Depicting these dynamics, HDURBAN for the Western Black Widow is expected to decrease over time within the study area. In contrast, those for the Brown Widow are expected to be stable (and high) since its introduction in 2003. To test this, occurrence records of both species were chronologically ordered, grouped into temporal bins of 25 occurrence records each, and used to train habitat suitability models. This threshold was chosen as a trade‐off between the number and quality of models that could be generated from the available observations. Twenty‐five occurrence records are a suitable and conservative threshold for the number of occurrences required for accurate model training (Proosdij et al., 2016), and this threshold was also applied to the original dataset used here (Beninde et al., 2023). HDURBAN was then calculated following the same methodology as described above. As observations of both species in the iNaturalist dataset were patchy until ~2015, creating datasets of appropriate temporal resolution required including observations from GBIF (2023, 2024), collected by the Spider Survey of the Los Angeles County Natural History Museum, which began in 2002 (Kempf et al., 2021). Together with the more recent occurrences downloaded directly from the iNaturalist website, this enabled the creation of 25 temporal bins from 625 Western Black Widow observations spanning 2002–2025, and 71 temporal bins from 1775 Brown Widow observations spanning 2003–2025. Before creating bins, all observations were filtered for spatial accuracy and thinned to one per raster cell and year, following the modeling framework described above.

HDURBAN from each temporal bin served as the response variable in beta regression models with time of observation as the primary predictor variable using the “betareg” R‐package (v3.1‐4; Zeileis et al., 2021). Time of observations was calculated as the month and year of the median observation per bin (06/2002–04/2025 for Western Black Widow and 11/2009–06/2025 for Brown Widow). As alternative hypotheses, I tested the effects of seasonality, time span, and geographic area covered by bins. Using the month of the median observation per bin, I assigned one of four seasons: summer (June–August), autumn (September–November), winter (December–February), spring (March–May). Furthermore, as species observations accumulated at different rates over time, temporal bins varied considerably in the number of days between the first and last occurrence records (ranging from 10 to 3905 days; median = 53 days). This is particularly well illustrated by the first temporal bin for both species: The first observation of the Western Black Widow occurred in April 2002, and all 25 observations had accumulated by July 2002. In contrast, observations of the Brown Widow started in August 2003 and took until April 2013 to reach the first 25 observations. Therefore, the time span covered by bins was included as a predictor in the models. Finally, to address the potential effects of varying geographic coverage per bin, I calculated the area of the minimum convex polygon surrounding all observations per bin using the “terra” R‐package (Hijmans, 2021) and included it as a predictor variable in the models. Separate models were fitted for each species, with HDURBAN as the response variable and fixed effects including time of observation, seasonality, time span, and area. Parameter estimates were assessed for significance to determine their importance, and pseudo‐R‐square values were used to evaluate model fit.

RESULTS

HDURBAN of virtual species

The distribution of HDURBAN for virtual urban avoiders, urban utilizers, and urban dwellers was nonoverlapping (Figure 3). HDURBAN of urban avoiders ranged from 0.055 to 0.101 (median = 0.074), urban utilizers from 0.247 to 0.716 (median = 0.498), and urban dwellers from 0.815 to 0.926 (median = 0.9).

HDURBAN of real species

HDURBAN values of all 1023 real species ranged from 0.056 to 0.971 and, overall, leaned more towards higher urban association (median = 0.691; Figure 3a). Mean and standard deviation HDURBAN values for all species are available from Beninde (2026).

Validation

Comparing presumed modes of population establishment, HDURBAN of both native and non‐native species was significantly different from normal expectations (Shapiro–Wilk test, p < 0.001), and native species had significantly lower HDURBAN than non‐native species (Mood's Median Test, Z = −11.3, p < 0.001). Native species exhibited a uniform, slightly bimodal distribution of HDURBAN values (Figure 3b), whereas non‐native species values overlapped more frequently with virtual urban dwellers and not at all with virtual urban avoiders (Figure 3c). DISCUSSION

HD provides an easily interpretable and readily comparable metric of local population responses to contrasting land‐use types. Based on a novel application of the widely used ROC and its associated AUC to geographic predictions of species' realized niches, HDURBAN proved versatile across applications and sensitive to landscape‐level temporal changes in populations' urban affinity.

Validation of HDURBAN

As expected, results corroborated significantly lower urban affinity in native than in non‐native species, with lower HDURBAN in native species (Figure 3). Similar patterns have been observed in the Boston metropolitan area, where night‐at‐light‐based urban associations were quantified for 1004 species across plant and animal taxa (Callaghan, Ozeroff, et al., 2020). Given the well‐documented and strong association between urbanized landscapes and the introduction pathways of non‐native species (Cadotte et al., 2017; Gaertner et al., 2017; McLean et al., 2017), this finding is unsurprising but served as a first test of HDURBAN.

Significant consistency of urban responses across species in space and time was demonstrated by comparing HDURBAN values from Greater Los Angeles with the urban association ranks of the same 38 bird species observed by Blair (1996) in the Bay Area, assessed 25 years earlier and over 450 km from this study. These findings highlight that responses can be consistent across species, urbanized landscapes, and temporal scales. For spatial scales, this finding aligns with observations of birds across the Eastern United States, where continental‐level artificial light at night (ALAN)‐derived urban associations varied significantly with regional urban associations for the same species (Callaghan, Major, et al., 2020). For temporal scales, however, empirical evidence remains more limited. Notably, population shifts have been documented in two hawk species of the genus Accipiter, both of which have shown increasing urban associations over time in some European cities (Rutz, 2008) and in Southern California, USA (Cooper et al., 2021).

Testing the sensitivity to detect temporal changes in urban association within species revealed a significant decrease in HDURBAN over time for the Western Black Widow but not for the Brown Widow (Figure 4b). This decline of Black Widows is likely attributed to negative interactions with the congeneric Brown Widow, first detected within the study extent in 2003 and subsequently spreading across urban Greater Los Angeles (Figure 1). Experimental trials have demonstrated preferential predation of Brown Widows on the Southern Black Widow, Latrodectus mactans (Coticchio et al., 2023). Assuming similar predation preferences exist for the closely related Western Black Widow studied here, this offers a possible explanation for the observed decline of Western Black Widows in urban areas of Greater Los Angeles and stable population numbers in adjacent wildland areas where Brown Widows are absent (Aragon‐Traverso, 2021; Vetter et al., 2012; Vincent et al., 2008). The ability of HDURBAN to detect these temporal shifts demonstrates its potential to monitor changes in species' urban affinity more generally. For example, leveraging the increasing availability of species observation data (Di Cecco et al., 2021; Sullivan et al., 2009), HDURBAN could be deployed to track changes in urban affinity and monitor species targeted by conservation efforts, or to track the succession dynamics and spill‐over effects of non‐native species (Shaffer, 2018; Spear et al., 2018).

Comparing species to urban population ecological response types using HDURBAN

Expanding on these findings, the virtual species modeling approach employed here enables a direct comparison of HDURBAN for real species with that expected for the three urban population ecological response types within the same study extent (Blair, 1996; Fischer et al., 2015). For Greater Los Angeles, this revealed that all real species overlapping with the HDURBAN of virtual urban avoiders were native. At the same time, many native species also have high HDURBAN values more similar to urban dweller responses (Table 1). Amid these are, among birds, the Black Phoebe (Sayornis nigricans), Brewer's blackbird (Euphagus cyanocephalus), and Northern mockingbird (Mimus polyglottos), and, among insect species, the tobacco hawk moth (Manduca sexta), fiery skipper (Hylephila phyleus), and figeater beetle (Cotinis mutabilis). Native plant species overlapping with virtual urban dwellers were less common and included species frequently cultivated as ornamentals, such as the showy island snapdragon (Gambelia speciosa) and apricot mallow (Sphaeralcea ambigua). When examining the overall HDURBAN distribution among native species, one might expect a strongly right‐skewed pattern, with most species aligning with virtual urban avoiders. Instead, responses are rather uniformly distributed and slightly bimodal. Several factors may contribute to this pattern. Part of the explanation likely lies in the underlying data and methodological choices. For example, species with higher urban affinity are more likely to meet the 25‐occurrence threshold applied in the modeling framework, which was implemented to ensure robust, high‐quality models (Beninde et al., 2023). Consequently, species that strongly avoid urban areas were less likely to be included in the dataset, although the magnitude of this effect remains unknown. Additionally, HDURBAN ranges differed markedly among population ecological response types of virtual species, and the implications of scaling them equally warrant further investigation. Alternatively, this observed distribution may reflect biological reality, and more native species than expected may be able to utilize urban resources efficiently, for example, through behavioral plasticity (Gaynor et al., 2018) or evolutionary adaptation to urban areas (Johnson & Munshi‐South, 2017).

Most real species with high HDURBAN values, overlapping with virtual urban dwellers, are non‐native (Figure 3b,c). However, a long tail of low HDURBAN values for non‐native species suggests that some may pose a considerable risk of ecological spill‐over effects in urban‐adjacent wildland areas (Spear et al., 2018). For example, the Spanish broom, Spartium junceum, and the yellow star‐thistle, Centaurea solstitialis, received among the lowest HDURBAN values of any non‐native species (0.19 and 0.23, respectively). They are both flowering plants native to the Mediterranean region in southern Europe but are known for their invasive populations in similar climatic conditions worldwide, including California (Bossard et al., 2000).

Future application and refinement of HDURBAN

The data presented here demonstrate the need for a continuous metric to measure populations' urban responses locally. HDURBAN values for over 25% of the species did not overlap the expected values of any of the virtual species, instead falling between virtual utilizers and avoiders (N = 146) and virtual utilizers and dwellers (N = 122; Table 1). This highlights the continuous nature of responses to urbanized landscapes, which extend beyond the dominant three‐tiered typology, and the added value of using a meaningful metric that captures these ecological responses.

HDURBAN facilitates direct comparisons across species and urban landscapes, allowing for the quantification of city‐specific effects on urban affinity, such as the scale of cities (Blumstein et al., 2023; Uchida et al., 2021), or environmental differences between urban and urban‐adjacent areas (MacGregor‐Fors et al., 2021). Comparisons across landscapes require training virtual species separately for each landscape to ensure standardization. This, however, opens vast opportunities to quantify intraspecific variation of urban affinity and its drivers more broadly (Fidino et al., 2021; Haight et al., 2023).

Several aspects of HDURBAN calculation deserve further attention and potential refinement. For example, while effective at detecting significant temporal decreases in the Western Black Widow (Figure 4b), the results also revealed high variability across temporal bins. Future studies could explore the role of temporal bin composition by employing alternative methods to create bins and testing different statistical approaches to detect time lags. Moreover, it will be important to see whether the relatively few urban avoider species observed in this study, compared to urban utilizer or dweller species, can be corroborated across cities, or whether this reflects the relative proportions of urban and nonurban areas (here, 65.1% and 34.9%, respectively). Additionally, HDURBAN was computed at a specific spatial resolution (~1 km2; Beninde et al., 2023), and optimal resolution may vary depending on the availability and accuracy of the species observations. Furthermore, this study trained three virtual species, one for each of the three urban response types, which ultimately rest on a binary classification of the landscape into urban and nonurban habitats. Such a binary delineation of the landscape may be seen as a limitation of this approach. However, it requires applying objective criteria that define urban locations, which is frequently lacking in urban ecological studies (MacGregor‐Fors, 2011), ultimately leading to better comparability and more generalizable conclusions. In this study, I used the urban polygons delineated by the U.S. Census Bureau for this purpose, which are available nationwide. Beyond the United States, clustering of typical urban layers, such as impervious surface cover or human population density, could be used to create binary classifications of urban and nonurban areas.

Beyond the prominent urban land‐use gradient, gradient analyses are central in ecological research (Beninde et al., 2015; McDonnell & Hahs, 2008; McDonnell & Pickett, 1990; Niemelä, 2011), encompassing various land‐use types, configurations, processes, or impacts (Curtis & Vincent, 2005; Debinski et al., 2013; Grundel & Pavlovic, 2007; Hylkema et al., 2015; Lawrence et al., 2018; Tylianakis et al., 2006; Wilson & Keddy, 1986). Future investigations could apply HD in other settings, for example, to assess the impacts of agricultural land use on species and populations.

Practical considerations for HD

When utilizing and interpreting HD, two primary aspects require consideration: its variable specificity in capturing responses attributable to the focal land‐use type and the computational resources required to calculate it.

Firstly, the specificity of HDURBAN in measuring urban impacts can vary with landscape characteristics and the definition of “urban”. This is well exemplified by the varied topography of Greater Los Angeles, where urban areas are situated at considerably lower elevations than the predominantly hilly wildland areas. Consequently, HDURBAN as calculated here reflects both urban impacts and altitudinal differences. Similarly, the displacement of urban Western Black Widows is likely driven by negative interactions with the non‐native Brown Widow rather than by changes in environmental associations. Furthermore, it is conceivable that urban and nonurban populations have diverged into distinct populations. For example, landscape genetic studies revealed that urban red foxes in Berlin, Germany, are genetically distinct from foxes in adjacent rural areas (Kimmig et al., 2020). Yet, for most species, knowledge of genetic or morphological divergence at small spatial scales is unavailable, which could lead to inaccurate conclusions about their urban responses as measured by HDURBAN when distinct populations are modeled jointly. These examples illustrate that, while HDURBAN summarizes a response specific to the urbanized landscape under scrutiny, it cannot distinguish it from other local abiotic and biotic factors that covary with it. This must be considered and critically evaluated before interpreting and comparing results across species and landscapes.

Secondly, calculating HD demands substantial observational data and computational resources. A conservative minimum of 25 observations per species was used as the modeling threshold. While this allowed for the inclusion of 1023 species in Greater Los Angeles, observations of species in cities worldwide are less comprehensive and likely sparser in most other land‐use types. Importantly, detecting temporal trends requires substantially more occurrence records. Computationally, significant advances have reduced the runtime of high‐performance species distribution modeling approaches such as Maxent and random forest to minutes (Valavi et al., 2022). Yet, assembling spatial predictor datasets from various sources and basic coding proficiency are necessary to apply HD to new landscapes and to standardize it using virtual species for comparability.

CONCLUSION

Quantifying the ecological impacts of urbanized landscapes on local populations remains challenging. The metric and methodology presented here provide a path towards data‐driven and comparable assessments of these impacts, including the detection of temporal changes in urban responses. In doing so, HD has the potential to leverage the growing volume of community science observations to facilitate comparisons of populations across space and time. Finally, by linking virtual species modeling to empirical species responses, HD enables quantitative comparisons between real species and established response types, providing a common framework for studying and communicating ecological responses across contrasting land‐use types.
