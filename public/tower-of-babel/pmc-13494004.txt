The influence of gravity levels on the self-transport of bubbles on the superaerophilic wedge surface
Authors: Weixin Sun, Chaolang Chen, Wei Xiong, Jiajun Qi, Ruisong Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEfficiently separating and collecting the bubbles (oxygen and hydrogen) produced by water electrolysis is crucial for space exploration. The superaerophilic wedge surface (SWS) holds promise for handling gas in microgravity, as it enables spontaneous and directional bubble transport driven by Laplace pressure gradients. However, existing studies on SWS-based bubble transport have so far been conducted mainly under Earth’s gravity, and its actual performance in a microgravity environment remains unexplored. In this work, we developed a numerical model to study the effect of gravity level on the self-transport of bubbles on the SWS and verified the reliability of the simulation results using samples with similar properties. Systematic researches find that reduced gravity enhances bubble transport, eliminates volume constraints, and improves capture efficiency. In microgravity, the SWS can capture and transport bubbles exceeding 1000 μL, surpassing conventional critical volume limits. Moreover, lower temperatures, larger wedge angles, and wider narrow-end widths all contribute to faster bubble transport by increasing the surface energy release rate. These results provide valuable insights for advancing bubble management in space-based electrolysis systems.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Physics

Introduction

With the progress of technology and humanity’s continued expansion into space, the need to produce oxygen beyond Earth is both inevitable and critical for survival1,2. A straightforward method for oxygen production is water electrolysis3–5. However, in space, the absence of gravity causes bubbles generated by water electrolysis to readily adhere to the electrode surface, which can have serious implications for the electrolytic system6,7. Moreover, these bubbles form in water, rendering them hard to collect. While centrifugation offers one gas-liquid separation method, it is hindered by system complexity and high energy consumption8,9, thus limiting the feasibility of water electrolysis—and especially gas collection—in space10,11.

Inspired by nature, researchers have designed surfaces incorporating geometric or chemical gradients12,13 to allow for spontaneous bubble transport. Examples include copper cones inspired by cactus spines14, single wedge channels15, and tandem wedge channels16. These gradient surfaces establish a Laplace pressure gradient across adhered bubbles, which serves as the primary driver for bubble self-transportation17–21. Based on this mechanism, the superaerophilic wedge surface (SWS) shows great promise for capturing and autonomously transporting bubbles in microgravity6. Nonetheless, most studies utilizing gradient surfaces to capture and transport bubbles have focused on terrestrial environments22–29, with systematic investigations under microgravity or weightlessness remaining scarce.

In this work, a numerical model was built to investigate the self-transport behavior of bubbles on a superaerophilic wedge surface. The validity of the simulation result was verified by preparing the sample for experimentation. Using the numerical model, carried out a systematic experiment. Furthermore, the critical volume of bubbles that can be captured by SWS was investigated. By integrating the established surface-energy-based model, the ability of SWS to capture and transport bubbles was explored across conditions ranging from terrestrial to microgravity, along with the effects of SWS size and ambient temperature on the bubble transport process.

To describe bubble behavior on a wedge surface, a numerical model based on the fluid phase field method was employed (as shown in Fig. 1). The model comprises elements containing bubbles, water and the entire rectangular plane is divided into the wedge region and the non-wedge region (Fig. 1a). The dimensions of the wedge region are: initial width w0, wedge angle α, and length 30 mm. The entire model is placed in water, with the water boundary represented by lines. The spherical bubble is positioned at the narrow end of the wedge region, depicted in a brighter color (Fig. 1b). In this paper, computational fluid dynamics is employed to simulate bubble behavior on the SWS.

Results and Discussion

Model reliability

A numerical model is shown in Fig. 1. The wedge region has an opening angle α of 4° and a narrow-end width w0 of 0.6 mm, with a length of 30 mm. The gas contact angle on the wedge region was set to 0°. In contrast, the contact angle on the non-wedge region was set to 150°. A bubble with a volume (V) of 35 μL and the substrate (simulated the SWS) are fully immersed in water with a rectangular prism with a size of 34 × 7 × 7 mm3. The gravitational acceleration g was set to 1 g (9.81 m/s²), acting vertically downward.

Under normal gravity conditions, the SWS could capture the bubble against buoyancy and drive it to move rapidly toward the wide end within the wedge region (Fig. 2a). Due to the superaerophilicity of the region, the captured bubble spread to form a bulge and a gas film. At approximately 200 ms, the bubble reached the end of the SWS. This process within the wedge region is consistent with previously reported studies15,30.

To further verify the reliability of the numerical model, an SWS with similar interfacial properties under terrestrial gravity was fabricated on an aluminum sheet and used to observe the transport of bubbles. As shown in Fig. 2b, the bubble was trapped by the SWS sample, after which the bulge and the gas film formed, similar to the numerical model. Due to the difficulty in statistically analyzing the precursor film in the experiment, owing to its minimal thickness, the displacement of the leading edge of the bubble bulge was measured instead. Compared with the simulation results, the bubble moved somewhat more slowly at the beginning of the experiment (Fig. 2c). This delay is likely due to resistance from the fixed experimental apparatus hindering the bubble’s motion to the right. Moreover, the SWS in the experiment possesses a depth of 23 mm. This amplifies the curvature disparity between the leading and trailing interfaces, driving the bubble more rapidly toward the wide end during the later stage of transport (Eq. S1). Ultimately, the bubble arrived at the end of the SWS at 216 ms. The results indicate that the simulation and experimental results exhibit reasonable consistency.

We then conducted an in-depth analysis of the numerical model data. The displacement (x, z) of the bubble’s center of mass was statistically calculated, as shown in Fig. 2d. The x-displacement value represents the displacement of the bubble’s center moving from the narrow end to the wide end. After the bubble’s center reaches the wide end, it undergoes an offset towards the origin. This is in line with the observational results from both simulation and experiment (Fig. S1). The z-displacement of the bubble’s center of mass continuously decreased as the bubble spread over the substrate, until it stabilized around 150 ms. Notably, the bubble ascended by Δz = 0.006 mm due to buoyancy before 50 ms. Buoyancy (FB) hindered the movement of the bubble as the ascent pulls the bubble upward, impeding its complete spreading along the substrate surface (inset of Fig. 2d)31. This phenomenon is more pronounced in Fig. S2.

Critical volume of bubbles for capturing

As indicated by Eq. 2, bubble volume enhances the buoyancy effect. Consequently, for a given wedge geometry, bubbles exceeding a critical volume will detach and float away instead of spreading and moving along the surface. To identify this critical size, we investigated the self-transportation of bubbles with volumes ranging from 20 to 40 μL, in 5 μL increments. As shown in Fig. 3a, increasing the bubble volume reduces its velocity, with the maximum velocity decreasing from 613 mm/s (20 μL) to 466 mm/s (35 μL).

Critical volume of bubbles for capturing.

a The x-displacement and velocity of the center of mass during the motion of bubbles with different volumes. b The corresponding z-displacement and work done by buoyancy. Representative side-view snapshots of the bubble transport process under 1 g (c) and 0 g (d) levels.

We calculated the escape energy (W), work done by the FB, Fcp, and Fsl on the bubble. Figure 3b shows that a larger bubble volume leads to a more pronounced initial ascent and more W. For a 35 μL bubble, it ascended by 0.30 mm at 30 ms, with the corresponding W reaching a maximum of 1.17 μJ. When the bubble volume is 40 μL, at 30 ms, the W increases to 3.30 μJ. This imparts sufficient initial vertical velocity to the bubble, causing it to detach from the surface, as illustrated in the typical sequence in Fig. 3c. Thus, the critical bubble volume lies between 35 and 40 μL. Finally, the critical volume Vc was determined to be 37 μL (Fig. S6a).

To derive a more general criterion from the results obtained for a specific geometry, we performed a dimensionless analysis based on the Bond number (Bo)36 and introduced a critical Bond number B tailored to the wedge geometry. This dimensionless parameter quantifies the competition between the buoyancy force promoting bubble detachment and the adhesion force that pins the bubble to the wedge surface at the critical detachment state. Where w0 is the width of the narrow wedge tip, and α is the wedge angle. The denominator represents the characteristic scale of the maximum adhesion force that resists detachment, governed by the wedge geometry. Substituting data obtained under 1 g conditions yields a critical Bond number of B ≈ 187 for the present structured wetting surface (SWS). Physically, B captures a balance between geometric and physical parameters. Thus, we obtain the critical Bond number criterion (B-criterion): When B exceeds this critical threshold, buoyancy overcomes the retention force and triggers bubble detachment; otherwise, bubbles remain captured.

Under microgravity conditions, the SWS can break through the limitations of bubble volume due to the elimination of buoyancy. At 0 g, B equals 0 and a 40 μL bubble does not detach (Fig. 3d). Even larger bubbles (45, 100, and 1000 μL) can be captured and spontaneously transported (Fig. S6b-S6d). This also agrees with the B-criterion.

Research under different gravity conditions

To deeply investigate the effect of gravity levels on the bubble transport, simulations were conducted across a range of gravity levels: 0.00 g, 0.16 g (lunar gravity), 0.38 g (Martian gravity), 1.00 g (Earth gravity), 0.60 g, and 0.80 g. As shown in Fig. 4a(i), the average velocity of the transportation of the bubble increases from 113 mm/s to 152 mm/s as the gravitational acceleration decreases.

The reduction in the gravity level leads to an accelerated rate of surface energy release (ΔG, Fig. 4a(ii)), as it reduces the buoyancy force pulling on the bubble. At 100 ms, released surface energy values increase progressively from 1.38 μJ (1 g) to 2.03 μJ (0 g), respectively. This would explain the phenomenon of increasing average velocity of the bubble as the gravity level decreases (Fig. 4a(i)).

Figure 4b(i) shows that reduced gravitational acceleration decreases the z-displacement of the bubble’s center of mass throughout the entire process. Lower gravitational acceleration results in a reduced magnitude of bubble ascent during the initial 50 ms. The maximum W in this stage decreases directly from 1.17 μJ under 1.00 g to 0 J under 0.80 g (Fig. 4b(ii)). This means that the bubble falling below 0.80 g no longer generates velocity in the positive z-direction. As a result, bubbles remain stable and transport along the SWS.

Statistical analysis of bubble transport across varying volumes and gravity levels (Fig. 4c and Fig. S7) reveals that reduced gravitational acceleration consistently decreases the W while increasing average velocity, which is a trend observed across all bubble volumes (20 to 35 μL). This demonstrates that the influence trend induced by gravity variation remains consistent and is not interfered with by the bubble volume.

Figure 4d demonstrates that critical bubble volume increases monotonically with decreasing gravitational acceleration, scaling from 37 μL (1.00 g) to 260 μL (0.16 g). The data curve exhibits characteristics of an approximate inverse proportional function. According to the B‑criterion, when gravity decreases from 1 g to 0.8 g, 0.6 g, 0.38 g, and 0.16 g, the corresponding critical bubble volumes should scale as 1.25-, 1.67-, 2.62-, and 6.25-fold relative to the volume under terrestrial gravity, i.e., 46 µL, 62 µL, 98 µL, and 231 µL, respectively. These values are in good agreement, both in magnitude and trend, with our simulation results of 46 µL, 65 µL, 110 µL, and 260 µL. Theoretically, the critical volume approaches infinity as B = 0, indicating no upper volume limit for bubble transport in 0 g. As shown in Fig. S6d, the SWS successfully transports bubbles up to 1000 μL.

The geometry sizes of the wedge region

The geometry of the wedge region influences bubble transport dynamics, where the wedge angle (α) and the narrow-end width (w0) are the key parameters. Figure 5a demonstrates that the average velocity increases from 70 to 178 mm/s and the z-displacement decreases even more rapidly with increasing α (V = 30 μL and w0 = 0.60 mm). This conclusion is consistent with the existing literature40. Studies under 0 g level demonstrate that a larger wedge angle still increases the average velocity from 112 to 217 mm/s. This enhancement originates from steeper surface energy gradients at larger α (Fig. 5b), which drive more rapid energy conversion into kinetic energy. Notably, this gradient-driven mechanism remains gravity independent. This relationship is also evident from Eq. 8.

Figure 5c demonstrates that decreasing narrow-end width (w0) amplifies bubble ascent tendencies (V = 30 μL and α = 4°). At w0 = 0.45 mm, the bubble detaches from SWS (Fig. 5c(ii)) due to insufficient adhesive force during the initial 50 ms. Statistical analysis of the lTCL confirms this adhesive force deficit (Fig. S8). Under the 0 g level, reduced w0 still decreases transport velocity, declining from 167 mm/s (0.60 mm) to 147 mm/s (0.50 mm) (Fig. 5c(i)). Figure 5d indicates that variations in the narrow-end width primarily cause a phase shift in the surface energy versus time curve, with little increase in the energy release gradient, which also accounts for the relatively small variation in bubble transport velocity observed across different narrow-end widths.

Notably, variations in either the wedge angle or the narrow-end width of the wedge region have minimal impact on the total amount of surface energy ultimately released by the bubble (Fig. 5b, d). This is also consistent with the predictions of the geometric model. Conversely, the dimensions of the wedge surface have little effect on the final release value of surface energy.

In summary, this work establishes a numerical model for the transport process of bubbles in SWS and demonstrates efficient bubble transport on superaerophilic wedge structures (SWS) in microgravity. First, by comparing the bubble transport process under the 1 g gravity condition, the reliability of the bubble numerical model was validated. Specifically, under weightless conditions, the SWS overcomes conventional volume limitations and enables the successful capture of the 1000 μL bubble or larger. We have established the surface energy model for the self-transportation of bubbles on SWS, which accurately explains simulation results for multiple parameters. Energy calculations and analysis indicate that reduced gravitational acceleration enhances the rate of surface energy release while mitigating buoyancy interference, thereby improving bubble transport efficiency. Furthermore, compared to terrestrial conditions, lower temperatures, larger wedge angles, and a wider narrow-end width are shown to further enhance bubble transport velocity in microgravity. These findings are expected to advance the understanding of directional bubble transport in microgravity and demonstrate efficient bubble transport on the superaerophilic wedge surface in microgravity, indicating that utilizing such wedge structures for collecting oxygen in space holds potential application value.

Methods

Experiment methods

The detailed process and parameters for preparing the SWS on the aluminum sheet were as follows (Fig. 7). The SWS samples were prepared via laser process and chemical modification. Laser 1, Laser 2, and Laser 3 represent different laser parameters as shown in Table 1. First, one side of the aluminum sheet was scanned with Laser 1 to create a grid pattern, roughening the surface. Then the optical mask was applied to the surface. Second, Laser 2 and Laser 3 were used to scan and obtain the wedge region. Then, the sample was sequentially cleaned with anhydrous ethanol, 15% phosphoric acid, and deionized water for 2, 2, and 1 min, respectively. Third, the sample was immersed in a hydrophobic SiO2 ethanol solution for 10 min and dried at 120 °C. Finally, the SWS was obtained (Fig. S10). The dimensions of the prepared SWS correspond to the model dimensions used in the simulation. Due to the laser processing, the SWS possessed a certain depth: 23 ± 2 μm. Its surface micro-morphology and roughness were shown in Fig. S10b. The contact angles of bubbles of the wedge region (0°) and non-wedge region (151.2°) were shown in Fig. S10c.

Before the experiment, we secured the superhydrophobic copper mesh onto the syringe needle, mounted the syringe onto the contact angle measuring instrument, and fixed the SWS into the deionized water tank. Additionally, a 960-frame camera was positioned beside the water bath to capture experimental footage. During the experiment, the contact angle measuring instrument was controlled to eject a 35 μL bubble from the syringe. The ejected bubble was reliably captured by the copper mesh. The bubble was then brought into contact with the narrow end of the SWS, where it was immediately captured and transported by the SWS. Notably, dust particles in water or uneven ion concentrations may induce the Marangoni effect. Therefore, we ensured dust-free operation and used deionized water throughout the experimental process43,44.

Each bubble experiment was repeated at least 5 times. Notably, all experiments were conducted in terrestrial environments. Experiments in microgravity environments have not yet been performed.

The preparation method for the hydrophobic SiO2 ethanol solution: Mix 4 g of hydrophobic SiO2 with 96 g of anhydrous ethanol in a beaker. The mixture is stirred continuously using an electromagnetic stirrer for a minimum of 1 h, followed by 30 min of sonication. This two-step process ensures the uniform dispersion of SiO2 particles and effectively prevents their agglomeration.

The aluminum sheet used for sample preparation is purchased from Smao, Shenzhen, with the grade 1060; hydrophobic SiO2 is purchased from Degussa; optical masks are purchased from Taobao. Phosphoric acid for acid etching is purchased from McLean Company; deionized water and anhydrous ethanol are purchased from JD.com.

The Young’s contact angle (θ) for bubble-substrate interactions is defined as follows: the θ value for the wedge region is 0°, with the θ value for the non-wedge region is 150°. It indicated that the wedge region exhibits 0° contact angle with the bubble, while the non-wedge region exhibits 150° contact angle with the bubble (Fig. S11). All other interfaces unrelated to directional transport are assigned a neutral wettability (θ = 90°) to isolate wedge-driven capillary effects.

To balance computational efficiency and simulation accuracy, spherical bubbles are meshed with free tetrahedral elements, while the substrate surface is discretized with grid points. Based on empirical guidelines, the maximum mesh size (hmax) for the droplet domain was set to R/548,49, where R denotes the droplet radius. The inner wall is configured with a free triangular grid, and the interface thickness control parameter ɛ was defined as hmax/2. The ∂f/∂ϕ was set to 0 to prevent external energy input into the system. The mobility tuning parameter χ was defined as 0.67umax×hmax/ (σ × ε), where umax denotes the maximum bubble velocity during transport. σ varies with temperature.

Grid configurations were tested: hmax = R/2, R/5, R/8 yielding mesh counts of 819378, 1,597,734, and 2176184, respectively. The displacement values of finer mesh points exhibit negligible deviation at different times (Fig. S12). So, the R/5 configuration was selected as the optimal trade-off between accuracy and computational cost.

The iterative solver employed for both phase initialization and transient studies utilize the Generalized Minimum Residual Method (GMRES).
