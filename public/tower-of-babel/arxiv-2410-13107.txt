Wright–Fisher kernels: from linear to non-linear dynamics, ergodicity and McKean–Vlasov scaling limits
F. Cordero 1 and C. Jorquera 2 and H. Olivero 3 and L. Videla 4

Abstract
. We study the evolution of a pathogen with two allelic types infecting a population of hosts, where within-host type frequencies evolve in discrete time. Our framework is built on a two-parameter family of transition kernels on [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] , which describe one-step updates of type frequencies. In the absence of host interaction, the single-host type-frequency process admits, for a broad class of parameters, a moment dual with a branching–coalescing structure reminiscent of the Ancestral Selection Graph . Under suitable parameter and time scalings, it converges to a Wright–Fisher diffusion with drift. To incorporate interactions among hosts, we introduce a mean-field mechanism whereby within-host dynamics depend on the empirical type distribution across the population. We prove uniform-in-time propagation of chaos, comparing the dynamics in a typical host with a corresponding non-linear Markov chain. Under appropriate scaling, this non-linear chain converges to a McKean–Vlasov Wright–Fisher diffusion. As an illustration, we analyse a model where mutation rates depend on the current type distribution across hosts and establish uniform-in-time propagation of chaos together with ergodicity of the limiting McKean–Vlasov equation.

1.
Introduction

Consider a large population of host individuals infected by a contagious pathogen.
The pathogen reproduces much faster than its host; thus, over the evolutionary
timescale of the pathogen, the host population size may be regarded as constant.
We focus on a diallelic locus in the pathogen genome (with alleles
0
and
1
)
that determines two distinct phenotypic strains. Within each host, the allele
frequency evolves under mutation and selection. In addition, we assume that the pathogen dynamics inside each host are influenced by the global state of the population. More precisely, mutation and/or selection
rates may depend not only on the local allele frequency within a host but also on
the empirical distribution of allele frequencies across all hosts. This type of
weak, population-level coupling is captured mathematically through a mean-field
interaction and gives rise to a non-linear evolution at the host level.

This dynamic can be illustrated through the following experimental scenario. Consider many identical bacterial cultures (hosts), each infected
by bacteriophages (the pathogen) of two monitored strains. Strain
0
is highly
virulent but environmentally sensitive, whereas Strain
1
is less virulent but
more robust. Within each culture, phage replication is subject to mutation between
strains and to local selection. At regular times, the experimenter estimates the
overall frequency of Strain
1
across all cultures. This empirical average then
determines an environmental adjustment (e.g. a
pH
shift) that subsequently
affects selection locally in every culture. For instance, if resistance becomes
widespread, the induced environmental change may intensify selection against
Strain
0
globally. Thus, the selective pressure experienced by pathogen populations
within individual cultures depends, in part, on the collective state of the system.
This feedback produces a mean-field regime and leads to non-linear evolutionary
dynamics.

The setting described above is characteristic of current research at the interface of ecology and evolution (see, e.g.,
[
46
]
and, in a closely related context,
[
44
]
). Multi-level eco-evolutionary models, here involving a host level and a community level, have been considered previously. For instance,
[
33
,
34
]
study an urn–ball scheme in which a trait evolves locally according to a Moran model, being advantageous at the individual level but disadvantageous at the group level.
Here, we focus on large communities of hosts whose size remains approximately constant over relevant time scales. Our goal is to quantify how interactions between individuals, the local community, and possibly more distant communities influence pathogen evolution, with particular emphasis on the long-term dynamics.
We introduce a simple modeling framework addressing the scenario above at two levels of complexity:

•

Single-host level (one-level dynamics):
evolution of the frequency of allele
0
in the pathogen population within a single host, ignoring inter-host interactions.

•

Deme level (two-level dynamics):
evolution under non-negligible interactions among hosts within a deme.

The framework naturally extends to a third
meta-community
level in which hosts from different demes interact, allowing one to explore how abiotic or biotic heterogeneity affects pathogen evolution and coinfection dynamics. To keep the presentation focused, we restrict attention here to the first two levels; the meta-community extension will be pursued in future work.

In our model the fundamental entities are the hosts (as in mathematical epidemiology) rather than the pathogen particles (as in classical population genetics). This choice is particularly appropriate when empirical data are collected at the host level. The pathogen’s genetic evolution is represented in a mesoscopic, mass-action fashion, abstracting from the behavior of individual alleles. Nevertheless, we show that this formulation is consistent with familiar allele-level models such as the Wright–Fisher, Moran, and Cannings models. Moreover, it provides a tractable means of incorporating host-level dynamics into intra-deme interactions.

Our construction begins with a two-parameter family
(P_{\delta,p})_{\delta,p\in[0,1]}
of kernels on
[0,1]
, which govern one-step transitions in both one- and two-level dynamics. We call these
Wright–Fisher kernels
. They exhibit strong ergodicity and monotonicity properties that carry over to the models built from them.

Building on these kernels, the paper develops two modeling layers. At the
single-host level, we introduce a discrete-time Wright–Fisher–type
model in which within-host evolutionary forces are encoded by a function
{\bf p}
.
For suitable choices of
{\bf p}
, the resulting dynamics admit a moment dual. In the
neutral case
(i.e. when
{\bf p}
is the identity), the dual corresponds to a discrete-time analogue of the

\Lambda
-coalescents from population genetics (see
[
5
]
for a review). More generally, when
{\bf p}
additionally incoporates mutation and a specific form of frequency-dependent selection, the dual process takes the form of a branching–coalescing system with killing. Finally, under diffusive rescaling, the model converges to a family of
Wright–Fisher diffusions with drift.

We then consider a nonlinear extension in which the local evolutionary parameters depend not only on the within-host composition but also on the ambient composition of the host population (a mean-field effect). Under mild regularity, this nonlinear Markov chain admits a unique stationary distribution and converges to it.

The second layer consists of
M
interacting hosts coupled through their empirical distribution. Conditional on this empirical measure, pathogen populations evolve independently within hosts. We prove
uniform-in-time propagation of chaos
: when suitably initialized, the law of a typical host remains uniformly close to that of the nonlinear single-host model. In a diffusive scaling regime, the nonlinear model converges to a McKean–Vlasov–type Wright–Fisher diffusion, yielding a continuous-time description of the two-level dynamics.

The paper is organised as follows. Section
2
outlines the main results. Section
3
develops the Wright–Fisher kernels and their core properties. Section
4
presents the single-host model, its duality, and diffusion limits. Section
5
introduces the nonlinear mean-field model and establishes convergence towards a McKean–Vlasov limit. Section
6
introduces the interacting particle system modeling the two-layers dynamics and establishes a uniform propagation of chaos result. Section
7
provides a case study, and Section
8
discusses open directions. Technical material and deferred proofs are collected in the Appendices
A
and
B
.

Ergodic properties of the Wright–Fisher kernels

The single host level

2.2.3.
Non-linear chains and McKean–Vlasov limits

In Section
5
, we set up the framework needed to later analyze the second-level dynamics. As discussed in the introduction, the feedback mechanisms act at the level of the entire community and then influence within-host selection uniformly across hosts. When focusing on a single host, this results in a reproduction bias that depends on its own state together with the distribution of states across all hosts. As an intermediate step toward the full multi-host model, we therefore introduce a class of non-linear Markov chains whose transitions depend not only on the current state but also on the current law of the process itself.

The parameters of the non-linear dynamics are a constant
\delta\in(0,1)

{\overline{{\bf p}}}:[0,1]\times\mathcal{P}\to[0,1]

(

2.2.4.
The deme level

2.2.5.

Specifically, we study the McKean–Vlasov model associated with the drift

In this section, we establish ergodic properties of the kernels

, which addresses the case

. There it was shown that for

, if
\beta_{p}
denotes the law of a

We next lay the groundwork for proving Proposition

. The results presented in the next subsection will be useful all along the paper.

3.1.
Updating functions

As seen in Section
2.2
, the transitions of the processes we will consider in this article are based on the Wright–Fisher kernels
(P_{\delta,p})_{\delta,p\in[0,1]}
.
At various stages, we will compare their transitions for different initial values and/or parameters. The common element in all instances will be a coupling argument based on the
updating functions

\mathbf{u}^{\inf}_{p,q,\delta}:[0,1]^{4}\to[0,1]
and
\mathbf{u}^{\sup}_{q,\delta}:[0,1]^{3}\to[0,1]
,
0\leq p\leq q\leq 1
, defined via

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\inf}_{p,q,\delta}(x,u,v,w)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}x(1-\delta u),&\text{if $v\leq 1-q$ or $v>1-q$ and $w>p/q$,}\\
x+\delta u(1-x),&\text{if $v>1-q$ and $w\leq p/q$,}\end{cases}

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\sup}_{q,\delta}(y,u,v)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}y(1-\delta u),&\text{if $v\leq 1-q$,}\\
y+\delta u(1-y),&\text{if $v>1-q$}.\end{cases}

(3.2)

The following result summarizes some basic properties of these updating functions.

Lemma 3.1
.

The families of updating functions
\{\mathbf{u}^{\inf}_{p,q,\delta}:0\leq p\leq q\leq 1,0<\delta<1\}
and
\{\mathbf{u}^{\sup}_{q,\delta}:0\leq q\leq 1,0<\delta<1\}
satisfy the following properties:

(1)

For any
0\leq p\leq q\leq 1
,
0<\delta<1
,
u,v,w\in[0,1]
, and
0\leq x\leq y\leq 1
, we have

\mathbf{u}^{\inf}_{p,q,\delta}(x,u,v,w)\leq\mathbf{u}^{\sup}_{q,\delta}(y,u,v).

(3.3)

(2)

For any
0\leq p\leq q\leq 1
,
0<\delta<1
,
u,v,w\in[0,1]
, and
0\leq x,y\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathbf{u}^{\inf}_{p,q,\delta}(x,u,v,w)-\mathbf{u}^{\sup}_{q,\delta}(y,u,v)|

\displaystyle\leq|x-y|(1-\delta u)+\mathbf{1}_{\{v>1-q\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{w>p/q\}}\delta u.

(3.4)

(3)

Let
U,U_{1},U_{2}\sim{\rm Unif}([0,1])
be independent. Then, for any
0\leq p\leq q\leq 1
,
0<\delta<1
,
u,v,w\in[0,1]
, and
0\leq x,y\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathbf{u}^{\inf}_{p,q,\delta}(x,U,U_{1},U_{2})-\mathbf{u}^{\sup}_{q,\delta}(y,U,U_{1})|\right]

\displaystyle\leq|x-y|\bigg(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\bigg)+(q-p)\frac{\delta}{2}.

(3.5)

Moreover, if
X\sim\mu\in\mathcal{P}
and
Y\sim\nu\in\mathcal{P}
, and
U,U_{1},U_{2}
are independent uniform random variables, also independent from
X
and
Y
, we have:

\displaystyle X^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{\inf}_{p,q,\delta}(X,U,U_{1},U_{2})\sim\mu P_{\delta,p},\qquad Y^{\prime}=\mathbf{u}^{\sup}_{q,\delta}(Y,U,U_{1})\sim\nu P_{\delta,q}

(3.6)

We will use Lemma
3.1
at various stages of the paper to couple processes constructed on the basis of the Wright–Fisher kernels (see Remarks
4.4
,
5.2
and
6.1
) .

Proof.

Note that the update functions can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\inf}_{p,q,\delta}(x,u,v,w)

\displaystyle=x(1-\delta u)+\mathbf{1}_{\{v>1-q\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{w\leq p/q\}}\delta u

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\sup}_{q,\delta}(y,u,v)

\displaystyle=y(1-\delta u)+\mathbf{1}_{\{v>1-q\}}\delta u,

and hence,

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\inf}_{p,q,\delta}(x,u,v,w)-\mathbf{u}^{\sup}_{q,\delta}(y,u,v)

\displaystyle=(x-y)(1-\delta u)-\mathbf{1}_{\{v>1-q\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{w>p/q\}}\delta u.

Inequalities (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) follow directly. Taking expectations in (
3.4
) yields (
3.5
). Finally, for any measurable and bounded function
h:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(X^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbb{E}\Big[h\big((\mathbf{1}_{\{U_{1}\leq 1-q\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\{U_{1}>1-q,\,U_{2}>p/q\}})X(1-\delta U)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\mathbf{1}_{\{U_{1}>1-q,\,U_{2}\leq p/q\}}(X+\delta U(1-X))\big)\mid X\Big]\bigg]

\displaystyle=(1-q+q-p)\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\big[h(X(1-\delta U)\big)\mid X\big]+q\frac{p}{q}\mathbb{E}\big[h\big(X+\delta U(1-X)\big)\mid X\big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[P_{\delta,p}h(X)\right]=\mu P_{\delta,p}h.

An analogous computation for

shows
\mathbb{E}[h(Y^{\prime})]=P_{\delta,q}h
, achieving the proof.
∎

To simplify the notation we will omit the subscript
\delta
from the update functions

and

if the dependency is clear from the context.

3.2.
Proof of Theorem

and properties of the invariant measures

The proof of Theorem
2.1
, which establishes existence, uniqueness, and convergence to the stationary distribution

of the kernel

Proposition 3.2
.

The following properties hold.

(1)

For every

in

,

\displaystyle\Was(\mu P_{\delta,{p}},\nu P_{\delta,q})\leq\left(1-\dfrac{\delta}{2}\right)\Was(\mu,\nu)+\dfrac{\delta}{2}|{p}-q|.

(2)

For every

Proof.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}
and assume without loss of generality that
0\leq{p}\leq q
. Let
(X_{0},Y_{0})
be an arbitrary coupling of
\mu
and
\nu
defined on a probability space
(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathbb{P}_{0})

U,U_{1},U_{2}\sim{\rm Unif}([0,1])

(\Omega_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{1})
. Consider the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})\coloneqq(\Omega_{0}\times\Omega_{1},\mathcal{F}_{0}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{1})

\mathbb{E}_{0},\mathbb{E}_{1}

and

From (

),

and

. Hence applying the bound (

) we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X_{1}-Y_{1}|]

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\bigg)\mathbb{E}_{0}[|X_{0}-Y_{0}|]+\left(q-p\right)\frac{\delta}{2}.

The first part follows by choosing a coupling

Notice that if

We now use the previous result to prove Theorem

Proof of Theorem

.

According to the first point of Proposition

4.1.
Some aspects of the dynamics

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
and a measurable function
{\bf p}:[0,1]\mapsto[0,1]
. Consider a two-types (
0
and
1
) infinite population evolving in discrete generations. At each generation a fraction
\delta U
, with
U
being uniformly distributed in
[0,1]
, of the population dies and is replaced by the offspring of an individual. The latter is chosen uniformly at random from the type-
0
(resp. type-
1
) sub-population with probability
{\bf p}(x)
(resp.
1-{\bf p}(x)
), where
x
denotes the current fraction of type
0
individuals. Let
Z^{(\delta,{\bf p})}_{n}
denote the fraction of type-
0
individuals in the population at generation
n
. The process
Z^{(\delta,{\bf p})}\coloneqq(Z^{(\delta,{\bf p})}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a Markov chain with transition kernel
(x,A)\mapsto P_{\delta,{\bf p}(x)}(x,A)
. Since
\delta
remains fix until Section
4.5
, we will write until then
Z^{({\bf p})}
instead of
Z^{(\delta,{\bf p})}
. In contrast, in Section
4.5
it will be important to make the dependency in
\delta
explicit.

An important example arises when
{\bf p}
is the identity function,
which we refer to as the
neutral case
. In contrast, a general function
{\bf p}
introduces, at each generation,
a bias toward one of the two types, depending on the current type composition.
The next proposition characterizes the neutral case together with two extreme scenarios
in which one of the types is always favored. In the latter situation, we refer to the advantaged type as the
fit
type.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume that
{\bf p}(x)\leq x
(resp.
{\bf p}(x)\geq x
) for all
x\in[0,1]
. Then the Markov chain
Z^{({\bf p})}
driven by
P_{{\bf p}}
is a bounded supermartingale (resp. a bounded submartingale). In particular, if
{\bf p}
is the identity function,
Z^{({\bf p})}
is a martingale.

Proof.

By construction
Z_{n}^{({\bf p})}\in[0,1]
for all
n\geq 0
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[Z_{n+1}^{({\bf p})}\mid Z_{n}^{({\bf p})}\right]

\displaystyle={\bf p}\!\left(Z_{n}^{({\bf p})}\right)\Bigg(Z_{n}^{({\bf p})}+\big(1-Z_{n}^{({\bf p})}\big)\frac{\delta}{2}\Bigg)+\big(1-{\bf p}(Z_{n}^{({\bf p})})\big)\Bigg(Z_{n}^{({\bf p})}\Big(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\Big)\Bigg)

\displaystyle={\bf p}\!\left(Z_{n}^{({\bf p})}\right)\Bigg(Z_{n}^{({\bf p})}+(1-Z_{n}^{({\bf p})})\frac{\delta}{2}-Z_{n}^{({\bf p})}+Z_{n}^{({\bf p})}\frac{\delta}{2}\Bigg)+Z_{n}^{({\bf p})}\Big(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\big({\bf p}(Z_{n}^{({\bf p})})-Z_{n}^{({\bf p})}\big)\,\frac{\delta}{2}+Z_{n}^{({\bf p})}.

and the result follows.
∎

In the setting of Proposition
4.1
, we conclude that
Z_{n}^{({\bf p})}
converges almost surely, as
n\to\infty
, to a random variable
Z_{\infty}
. The next result provides a necessary condition for this limit to take values only in
\{0,1\}
, i.e. for type
0
to eventually fixate or become extinct.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
{\bf p}
be a continuous function such that
{\bf p}(x)=0\Leftrightarrow x=0
and
{\bf p}(x)=1\Leftrightarrow x=1
. Assume also that for some initial condition
x\in[0,1]
, the Markov chain
Z^{({\bf p})}
driven by
P_{{\bf p}}
has an a.s. limit
Z_{\infty}
. Then
Z_{\infty}\in\{0,1\}
a.s.

Proof.

For every
n\geq 2
, let
I_{n}\coloneqq[1/n,1-1/n]
. Clearly,

\displaystyle\{Z_{\infty}\in(0,1)\}=\bigcup_{n\geq 2}\{Z_{\infty}\in I_{n}\}\subseteq\bigcup_{n\geq 2}\bigcup_{m\geq 1}\bigcap_{k\geq m}\{Z_{k}^{({\bf p})}\in I_{n}\}.

Define
A^{(n)}_{k}\coloneqq\{Z_{k}^{({\bf p})}\in I_{n}\}
for
n\geq 2
.
It suffices to show that for each
n\geq 2
, we have
\mathbb{P}[\liminf_{k}A^{(n)}_{k}]=0
,
to achieve the proof.

We claim that, whenever
Z^{({\bf p})}_{0}\in I_{n}
, the chain
Z^{({\bf p})}
has probability at least
r_{n}
, for some
r_{n}>0
, of exiting
I_{n}
through its left endpoint within a fix number of steps (possibly depending on
n
). To see this, let
L^{(n)}
be the minimal integer
L
such that

\Big(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\Big)^{L}\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)<\frac{1}{n}.

Further, define
q_{n}\coloneqq 1-\sup\{{\bf p}(x):x\in I_{n}\}
. Note that
q_{n}>0
by the continuity of
{\bf p}
.

Note that, if
(U_{i})_{i=1}^{L^{(n)}}
and
(V_{i})_{i=1}^{L^{(n)}}
are independent families of independent uniform random variables on
[0,1]
, we can construct
Z^{({\bf p})}
iteratively by setting

\displaystyle Z^{({\bf p})}_{i+1}=\mathbf{u}^{\sup}_{{\bf p}(Z_{i})}(Z^{({\bf p})}_{i},U_{i+1},V_{i+1}),\qquad i<L^{(n)}.

Conditional on
Z^{({\bf p})}_{0}\in I_{n}
, on the event
E_{n}\coloneqq\{\,U_{i}\geq 1/2,\;V_{i}>q_{n},\;i=1,\ldots,L^{(n)}\,\}
, the chain exits the interval
I_{n}
via its left endpoint at or before time
L^{(n)}
. Moreover,
\mathbb{P}(E_{n})=\big(2^{-1}(1-q_{n})\big)^{L^{(n)}}\eqqcolon r_{n}>0.

Hence, by the Markov property, for any
m\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z^{({\bf p})}_{m+i}\notin I_{n}\text{ for some }i=1,2,\ldots,L^{(n)}|Z^{({\bf p})}_{m}\in I_{n})\geq r_{n}.

(4.1)

Now, for every
m\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\bigcap_{k\geq m}\{Z_{k}^{({\bf p})}\in I_{n}\}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{k\geq 0}\bigcap_{i=0}^{L^{(n)}-1}\{Z_{m+kL^{(n)}+i}^{({\bf p})}\in I_{n}\}.

Set

\displaystyle B^{(n)}_{k}\coloneqq\bigcap_{i=0}^{L^{(n)}-1}\{Z_{m+kL^{(n)}+i}^{({\bf p})}\in I_{n}\}.

Using the Markov property, we obtain, for every
K\geq 1
, that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg[\bigcap_{k=0}^{K}B^{(n)}_{k}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(B^{(n)}_{0})\prod_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(B^{(n)}_{k}|B^{(n)}_{k-1})\leq(1-r_{n})^{K},

where in the last line we used (
4.1
). Letting
K\to\infty
, we conclude that
\mathbb{P}[\liminf_{k}A^{(n)}_{k}]=0
for every
n
, ending the proof.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Note that if
{\bf p}(x)\leq x
for all
x\in[0,1]
or
{\bf p}(x)\geq x
for all
x\in[0,1]
, Proposition
4.1
implies that

is either a bounded supermartingale or a bounded submartingale. In both cases, we may conclude that

converges almost surely and in

to a random variable

. If, in addition,

We conclude this section with a result that allows us to compare Wright–Fisher chains associated with different functions
{\bf p}
. Before stating it precisely, we present in the following remark a suitable coupling for this setting.

Remark 4.4

(The standard coupling)
.

Let
{\bf p},{\bf q}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
be measurable functions. Lemma
3.1
provides a natural way to couple the chains
Z^{({\bf p})}
and
Z^{({\bf q})}
, provided that their initial values
Z^{({\bf p})}_{0}
and
Z^{({\bf q})}_{0}
are already coupled. To see this, assume that we have already coupled the chains up to generation
n
and let
U,U_{1},U_{2}\sim{\rm Unif}([0,1])
be independent between them, and independent from the two processes up to generation
n
. Set
p\coloneqq{\bf p}(Z^{({\bf p})}_{n})
and
q\coloneqq{\bf p}(Z^{({\bf q})}_{n})
and we define

Z^{({\bf p})}_{n+1}=\mathbf{u}^{\inf}_{p,q}(Z^{({\bf p})}_{n},U,U_{1},U_{2})\quad\textrm{and}\quad Z^{({\bf q})}_{n+1}=\mathbf{u}^{\sup}_{q}(Z^{({\bf q})}_{n},U,U_{1}),

if
p\leq q
, or

Z^{({\bf q})}_{n+1}=\mathbf{u}^{\inf}_{q,p}(Z^{({\bf q})}_{n},U,U_{1},U_{2})\quad\textrm{and}\quad Z^{({\bf p})}_{n+1}=\mathbf{u}^{\sup}_{p}(Z^{({\bf p})}_{n},U,U_{1}),

otherwise. Thanks to part 3 of Lemma
3.1
, the chains
Z^{({\bf p})}
and
Z^{({\bf q})}
constructed by this procedure have the desired distributions and satisfy, for all
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|Z^{({\bf p})}_{n+1}-Z^{({\bf q})}_{n+1}]

\displaystyle\leq\Bigg(1-\dfrac{\delta}{2}\Bigg)\mathbb{E}[|Z^{({\bf p})}_{n}-Z^{({\bf q})}_{n}|]+\dfrac{\delta}{2}\mathbb{E}[|{\bf p}(Z^{({\bf p})}_{n})-{\bf q}(Z^{({\bf q})}_{n})|].

(4.2)

We can now state the announced comparison result, which will prove useful in Section
4.4
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
{\bf p},{\bf q}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
be two measurable functions. Assume that
{\bf q}\geq{\bf p}
(pointwise) and that either
{\bf p}
or
{\bf q}
is monotone increasing. Assume also that
Z^{({\bf p})}_{0}
is stochastically dominated by
Z^{({\bf q})}_{0}
. Then
Z^{({\bf p})}
is stochastically dominated (at every generation) by
Z^{({\bf q})}
.

Proof.

To prove the result it suffices to construct a coupling of the processes
Z^{({\bf p})}
and
Z^{({\bf q})}
such that
Z^{({\bf p})}_{n}\leq Z^{({\bf q})}_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
. By assumption, we know we can couple the initial values so that
Z^{({\bf p})}_{0}\leq Z^{({\bf q})}_{0}
.
Moreover, note that if
{\bf p}
is increasing and
x\leq y
, then
{\bf p}(x)\leq{\bf p}(y)\leq{\bf q}(y)
. Similarly, if
{\bf q}
is increasing and
x\leq y
, then
{\bf p}(x)\leq{\bf q}(x)\leq{\bf q}(y)
. In both cases, if
x\leq y
, then
{\bf p}(x)\leq{\bf q}(y)
.
Using this and the first part of Lemma
3.1
, we see that the standard coupling
starting from
Z^{({\bf p})}_{0}\leq Z^{({\bf q})}_{0}
satisfy the desired property.
∎

4.2.
Moment duality in fittest-type wins kernels with mutation

In this section we focus on a particular case of the models introduced in the previous section, namely when
{\bf p}
is of the form

{\bf p}^{\downarrow}_{\delta}(x)=x+\frac{2\delta}{3}\left(-x(1-x)\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sigma_{i}x^{i}+\theta_{0}(1-x)-\theta_{1}x\right),

with
\delta>0
,
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\geq 0
and
(\sigma_{k})_{k\geq 0}
satisfying the following assumption.

Assumption 4.6
.

The coefficients
(\sigma_{k})_{k\geq 0}
are non-negative, non-increasing and their sum is finite. Moreover,
2\delta(\sigma_{0}+\theta_{0}+\theta_{1})/3<1
.

From now onwards, we write
{\bf p}^{\downarrow}
instead of
{\bf p}_{\delta}^{\downarrow}
; making the dependency on
\delta
explicit will be only relevant in Remark
4.14
. Note that
{\bf p}^{\downarrow}
is the identity when
\sigma_{0}=0=\theta_{0}=\theta_{1}
. Under Assumption
4.6
, the function
{\bf p}^{\downarrow}
satisfies
{\bf p}^{\downarrow}(x)\in[0,1]
for all
x\in[0,1]
. The first term inside the brackets can be interpreted as frequency dependent selection and favors always type-
1
individuals. It coincides with the drift term in the
fittest-type wins
Wright–Fisher processes introduced in
[
21
]
and further studied in
[
2
,
14
]
. The second term inside the brackets can be interpreted as mutation;
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
can be seen as the relative strength of mutations to type
0
and to type
1
, respectively. For this reason, we refer to the kernel
P_{{\bf p}^{\downarrow}}
as a
fittest type wins kernel with mutation
. In Section
4.5
we will see a more precise connection to the fittest-type wins Wright–Fisher processes studied in
[
21
]
, but let us already mention that those models are tailored to admit a moment dual.

Note also that under Assumption
4.6
, if
\sigma_{0}>0
, the vector
(\rho_{i})_{i\geq 1}
defined via

\rho_{i}\coloneqq\frac{\sigma_{i-1}-\sigma_{i}}{\sigma_{0}},\qquad i\in\mathbb{N},

is a probability measure on
\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, letting
\varphi:[0,1]\to[0,1]
be the probability generating function of
(\rho_{i})_{i\geq 1}
, i.e.

\varphi(x)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\rho_{i}x^{i}
,
x\in[0,1],

we can express the selective term in
{\bf p}^{\downarrow}
as

-x(1-x)\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sigma_{i}x^{i}=\sigma_{0}x(\varphi(x)-1).

To avoid case distinctions, we set
\rho_{i}\coloneqq 0
for all
i\geq 1
when
\sigma_{0}=0
.

In population genetics, individual-based models typically allow for a graphical representation as an interacting particle system that couples both forward and backward evolution. From such a graphical representation, one can read duality relations characterizing functionals of the forward evolution, as for example type frequencies, in terms of appropriate functionals of the backward evolution, as the number of ancestors of a given sample (see
[
27
]
for a nice survey on the various notions of duality associated with Markov processes). One of the simplest and most useful examples is moment duality, which unfortunately only holds under restrictive conditions, such as neutrality. In our case, we don’t have a particle picture at hand for our process
Z^{\downarrow}
, but nevertheless, as stated in Theorem
2.2
, we have a moment dual. We would like to point out that this is rather an exception in comparison with other classical discrete-time models (e.g. Wright–Fisher or Cannings models), where the more involved notion of
factorial or sampling duality
arises in a natural way (see
[
12
,
19
,
20
,
36
,
2
]
).

We define

\displaystyle\lambda_{m,k}\coloneqq\dfrac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}x^{k}(1-x)^{m-k}{\rm d}x,\quad m\in\mathbb{N},\,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,0\leq k\leq m.

(4.3)

Notice that
\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\lambda_{m,k}=1
, hence
\{\binom{m}{k}\lambda_{m,k}\}
defines a probability over
\{0,\ldots,m\}
. Furthermore, set
\delta_{0}\coloneqq\frac{2\delta}{3}
and
\theta\coloneqq\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}
and define the Markov chain
{M}^{\downarrow}\coloneqq M^{({\bf p}^{\downarrow})}\coloneqq({M}^{\downarrow}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
on
\overline{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\coloneqq\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\partial\}
, where
\partial
denotes a cemetery state, and transitions

\begin{split}m&\to m\qquad\qquad\text{with prob.}\quad\lambda_{m,0}+(1-\delta_{0}(\theta+\sigma_{0}))m\lambda_{m,1}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\delta_{0}\sigma_{0}\sum_{k=2}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\lambda_{m,k}\rho_{k-1},\\
m&\to m-j\qquad\text{with prob.}\quad\delta_{0}\theta_{0}\binom{m}{j}\lambda_{m,j}+(1-\delta_{0}(\theta+\sigma_{0}))\binom{m}{j+1}\lambda_{m,j+1},\\
&\phantom{\quad\text{with prob.}}\qquad\qquad\qquad+\delta_{0}\sigma_{0}\sum_{k=j+2}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\lambda_{m,k}\rho_{k-j-1},\,\quad 1\leq j\leq m,\\
m&\to m+j\qquad\text{with prob.}\quad\delta_{0}\sigma_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\lambda_{m,k}\rho_{j+k-1},\,\qquad\quad j\geq 1,\\
m&\to\partial\qquad\qquad\text{with prob.}\quad\delta_{0}\theta_{1}(1-\lambda_{m,0}),\end{split}

(4.4)

with the usual assumptions that an empty sum equals
0
and that
\binom{i}{j}=0
if
j>i
. Note that when
\theta_{0}=\theta_{1}=0
, the state space can be reduced to
\mathbb{N}
.

Now we have all the ingredients to prove Theorem
2.2
.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

The result is clearly true for
n=0
. In the discrete time setting, proving the inductive step, is equivalent to proving the result for
n=1
. By construction, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z}[({Z}^{\downarrow}_{1})^{m}]

\displaystyle=(1-{\bf p}^{\downarrow}(z))\mathbb{E}[z^{m}(1-\delta U)^{m}]+{\bf p}^{\downarrow}(z)\mathbb{E}\bigg[(z(1-\delta U)+\delta U)^{m}\bigg]

\displaystyle=(1-{\bf p}^{\downarrow}(z))z^{m}\lambda_{m,0}+{\bf p}^{\downarrow}(z)\sum_{j=0}^{m}z^{m-j}\binom{m}{j}\lambda_{m,j}.

(4.5)

Using the definition of
{\bf p}^{\downarrow}
yields

\displaystyle 1-{\bf p}^{\downarrow}(z)

\displaystyle=1-\delta_{0}\theta_{0}+\Big(-1+\delta_{0}(\sigma_{0}+\theta)\Big)z-\delta_{0}\sigma_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\rho_{i}z^{i+1},

\displaystyle{\bf p}^{\downarrow}(z)\sum_{j=0}^{m}z^{m-j}\binom{m}{j}\lambda_{m,j}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}^{-}z^{-j}+\alpha_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{j}^{+}z^{j}\right)z^{m},

with

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}^{-}

\displaystyle=\delta_{0}\theta_{0}\binom{m}{j}\lambda_{m,j}+(1-\delta_{0}(\theta+\sigma_{0}))\binom{m}{j+1}\lambda_{m,j+1}+\delta_{0}\sigma_{0}\sum_{k=j+2}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\lambda_{m,k}\rho_{k-j-1},

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=\delta_{0}\theta_{0}\lambda_{m,0}+(1-\delta_{0}(\theta+\sigma_{0}))m\lambda_{m,1}+\delta_{0}\sigma_{0}\sum_{k=2}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\lambda_{m,k}\rho_{k-1},

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}^{+}

\displaystyle=1_{\{j=1\}}(1-\delta_{0}(\theta+\sigma_{0}))\lambda_{m,0}+\delta_{0}\sigma_{0}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\lambda_{m,k}\rho_{j+k-1}+1_{\{j\geq 2\}}\lambda_{m,0}\rho_{j-1}\right).

Hence,

\mathbb{E}_{z}[({Z}^{\downarrow}_{1})^{m}]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}^{-}z^{m-j}+\widehat{\alpha}_{0}z^{m}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\widehat{\alpha}_{j}^{+}z^{m+j},

with

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}_{0}

\displaystyle=(1-\delta_{0}\theta_{0})\lambda_{m,0}+\alpha_{0}=\lambda_{m,0}+(1-\delta_{0}(\theta+\sigma_{0}))m\lambda_{m,1}+\delta_{0}\sigma_{0}\sum_{k=2}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\lambda_{m,k}\rho_{k-1},

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}_{j}^{+}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{j}^{-}-\lambda_{m,0}(1_{\{j=1\}}(1-\delta_{0}(\theta+\sigma_{0}))+1_{\{j\geq 2\}}\delta_{0}\sigma_{0}\rho_{j-1})=\delta_{0}\sigma_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\lambda_{m,k}\rho_{j+k-1}.

Since

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}^{-}+\widehat{\alpha}_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\widehat{\alpha}_{j}^{+}=1-(1-\lambda_{m,0})\delta_{0}\theta_{1},

the result follows.
∎

4.3.
A particle picture interpretation of the dual process

Although we do not have a particle-system representation for the forward dynamics at hand, the process
{M}^{\downarrow}

admits a particle interpretation, in close analogy with classical models in population genetics. This ancestral picture is not derived from the forward dynamics; it merely serves as a heuristic device to visualize and organize the transition rates. This viewpoint will nonetheless prove valuable when establishing comparison results; see Lemma
4.7
and Remark
4.8
below.

Neutral case (
\sigma_{0}=\theta_{0}=\theta_{1}=0
).

As in the interpretation of the Wright–Fisher kernels in
Section
2.2
, in the neutral case one may picture each
generation as replacing a fraction
\delta U
of the population, where

U\sim{\rm Unif}([0,1])
, by the offspring of a uniformly chosen individual.
More precisely, this mechanism is the discrete-time analog of the

\Lambda
–Wright–Fisher process described at the beginning of Section
2.2
with

\Lambda({\rm d}u)=\delta^{-1}\,\mathbf{1}_{[0,\delta]}(u)\,u^{2}\,{\rm d}u,

whose reproduction events occur in continuous time at Poisson points with
intensity
{\rm d}t\,{\rm d}v\,\Lambda({\rm d}u)\,u^{-2}
(see
[
6
,
8
]
).

Further, in this neutral setting, the transitions of the dual
{M}^{\downarrow}
simplify to

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\to m

\displaystyle\text{with probability }\ \lambda_{m,0}+m\,\lambda_{m,1},

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\to m-k+1

\displaystyle\text{with probability }\ \binom{m}{k}\,\lambda_{m,k},\qquad 1<k\leq m.

In analogy with the forward picture, it is convenient to think of
{M}^{\downarrow}_{n}
as
the number of distinct ancestors,
n
generations in the past, of a present-day
sample of size
{M}^{\downarrow}_{0}=m
. At each backward step, every ancestor is independently marked with probability
u\delta
, where
u
is a realization of an independent uniform random variable on
[0,1]
. All marked ancestors are then merged, meaning they share a common parent in the previous generation; this parent is chosen uniformly at random from the full pool of individuals present at that time.
For readers familiar with exchangeable coalescents, this leads to a discrete-time
analogue of a
\Lambda
–coalescent with the above measure
\Lambda
.

\Lambda
–coalescents were introduced independently in

[
15
,
38
,
43
]
(see
[
5
]
for a survey) and have since
been extensively studied and used as prototypical models for genealogies in neutral
populations. Theorem
2.2
can thus be interpreted as a discrete-time analogue
of the classical moment duality between
\Lambda
–coalescents and
\Lambda
–Wright–Fisher processes.

Fittest type wins with mutation.

Equipped with the intuition from the neutral case, we now turn to the process
{M}^{\downarrow}
and the interpretation of the duality relation in the general setting.
Suppose that at time
n
we sample
m
individuals and wish to compute the
probability that all of them are of type
0
. Conditioned on
Z_{n}^{\downarrow}
,
this probability is simply
(Z_{n}^{\downarrow})^{m}
, and taking expectations
yields the right-hand side of the duality relation (
2.4
). Ideally,
we would like to recover the same quantity through an ancestral process whose
line–counting process is
{M}^{\downarrow}
. The idea is to trace backward in time the
individuals,
potential ancestors
, whose types must be specified
in order to determine whether the sampled individuals are all of type
0
.
We would like the ancestral system to start with the
m
sampled lines, and
for the sample to be entirely of type
0
precisely when, at every backward
time
k
, all
{M}^{\downarrow}_{k}
ancestral lines present at that time are of type
0
.
Consequently, if we sample at forward time
n
and follow the genealogy back

n
generations, then, conditional on
{M}^{\downarrow}_{n}
, the probability that the sample
contains only type
0
individuals is
z^{{M}^{\downarrow}_{n}}
. Taking expectations then yields the right-hand side of (
2.4
), and hence the duality relation.

Since we do not have a particle picture for the forward dynamics, we instead
construct a branching–coalescing system that matches the rates of the dual
process, we describe how types propagate along its lines, and we provide a
genealogical interpretation of its various transitions. It is important to
stress, however, that this construction is obtained from the duality relation
rather than from a forward particle model that is naturally compatible with
the backward process. In particular, it does not give a new proof of the
duality; it only offers a way to visualize the backward process.

As usual in genealogical constructions, we work in an untyped manner: we trace
ancestral lineages without assigning them types, except when a mutation occurs,
at which point the type of the affected lineage is revealed. As in the neutral case, at each generation we independently mark each potential ancestor with probability
\delta u
, where
u
is the realization of an independent
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
random variable. All marked individuals share
the same parent in the previous generation, and we assume they inherit that
parent’s type; hence, their lineages can be merged. This coalescence event may be
immediately followed (in the backward direction) by one of the
following events.

\bullet

Neutral events.

With probability
1-\delta_{0}(\theta+\sigma_{0})
, the event is neutral and is
not coupled to any further update.

\bullet

Deleterious mutation.

With probability
\delta_{0}\theta_{0}
, the merger event is followed by a mutation
to type
0
(although seen backward in time, its type effect is forward in time).
This mutation may occur in any of the lineages present after the coalescence event. The affected lineage is removed from further consideration, since all of its descendants will necessarily be of
type
0
, and its lineage no longer needs to be traced into the past.

\bullet

Beneficial mutation.

With probability
\delta_{0}\theta_{1}
, the merger of the marked individuals is
followed by a mutation to type
1
. This mutation may occur in any of the lineages present after the coalescence event. Thus, at least one potential ancestor of type
1
appears, which will
propagate forward in time and produce at least one sampled individual of
type
1
. Thus, the event “all sampled individuals are of type
0
” becomes impossible,
and we terminate the genealogical exploration by sending the process to the
cemetery state
\partial
.

\bullet

Selective events.

With probability
\delta_{0}\sigma_{0}\rho_{j}
, the merger is followed by an event in which one of the lineages present after the coalescence event becomes the child
of a group of
j+1

potential parents
from the previous generation.
The true parent is selected uniformly among the potential parents of type
1
,
if any are present, and uniformly among all
j+1
potential parents otherwise.
Since type information (other than mutation) is not yet known, we must trace
back the lineages of all
j+1
potential parents.

Neutral

Summarizing, the transitions of this ancestral system, and hence of
{M}^{\downarrow}
, consist of
(i) pure mergers (neutral),
(ii) mergers with deletion (neutral + mutation to type
0
),
(iii) branching–merger events (neutral + selection),
(iv) killing events (neutral + mutation to type
1
); see Fig.

{M}^{\downarrow}
may be viewed as a generalization of the
killed Ancestral Selection Graph
(see
[

).

The particle construction of the chain
{M}^{\downarrow}
described above is a key ingredient in the proof of the following comparison principle. This principle will subsequently be used to establish the asymptotic behavior of the dual chains in Proposition
4.10
of the next section.

Lemma 4.7

(The effect of mutations)
.

There is a coupling between the chain
{M}^{\downarrow}
and the chain
M^{\downarrow,0}
without mutations such that
{M}^{\downarrow}_{n}\leq M^{\downarrow,0}_{n}
until the first time
{M}^{\downarrow}
absorbs at
\partial
.

Proof.

Set
{M}^{\downarrow}_{0}=m_{0}=M_{0}^{\downarrow,0}
and assume that at generation
n
, we have
M^{\downarrow,0}_{n}\geq{M}^{\downarrow}_{n}\neq\partial
. In that case, we make both processes transition as follows. Let
S_{0}
and
S
denote systems of particles with
M^{\downarrow,0}_{n}
and
{M}^{\downarrow}_{n}
particles, respectively. Let us identify each particle in
S
to a unique particle in
S_{0}
. Now we mark independently each particle in
S_{0}
with probability
u
, where
u
is a realization of a uniform random variable on
[0,\delta]
; the particles in
S
copy the marks of their partners in
S_{0}
. With probability
1-\delta_{0}(\theta+\sigma_{0})
we only merge the marked particles in both systems. With probability
\delta_{0}\sigma_{0}\rho_{j}
the merger event is coupled to a selective event of range
j
in both systems. With probability
\delta_{0}\theta_{0}
the merger event is coupled to a mutation event to type
0
in
S
leading to the additional loss of one particle; nothing happens in
S_{0}
. With probability
\delta_{0}\theta_{1}
the merger event is coupled to a mutation event to type
1
in
S
sending the system to
\partial
; nothing happens in
S_{0}
. Hence, either
{M}^{\downarrow}_{n+1}=\partial
, or, in all the other cases, the order is preserved, i.e.

M^{\downarrow,0}_{n+1}\geq{M}^{\downarrow}_{n+1}\neq\partial
. The results follows via a simple iteration of the argument.
∎

Remark 4.8

(Comparison results)
.

The coupling technique used in the proof of Lemma
4.7
can be adapted to compare instances of the dual process
M^{\downarrow}
with different branching rates. For example, consider
M^{\downarrow,1}
and
M^{\downarrow,2}
as instances of the dual process
M^{\downarrow}
with the same initial value and mutation parameters
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}
, but branching rates
(\sigma_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
and
({\sigma}_{j}^{*})_{j=0}^{\infty}
, respectively. If
\sigma_{j}\leq\sigma_{j}^{*}
for all
j\geq 0
, then one can couple
M^{\downarrow,1}
and
M^{\downarrow,2}
, so that
M^{\downarrow,1}_{n}\leq M^{\downarrow,2}_{n}
for all
n
.

4.4.
Application: long-term behavior via moment duality

In this section we investigate the long–term behavior of the process

Z^{\downarrow}
introduced in Section
4.2
.
Our approach is as follows. We first analyze the asymptotic behavior of the process
{M}^{\downarrow}
, and then,
by means of Theorem
2.2
, we deduce the corresponding
long–term behavior of
Z^{\downarrow}
in two specific situations:
(i) the case without mutation, and
(ii) the case with mutation but without selection.
The results presented in this section require the following additional
assumption on the parameters
(\sigma_{k})_{k\geq 0}
.

Assumption 4.9
.

The coefficients
(\rho_{k})_{k\geq 1}
satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}k\rho_{k}\eqqcolon\mathfrak{b}<\infty.

Note that, by the definition of the coefficients
(\rho_{k})_{k\geq 1}
,
under Assumption
4.6
,
Assumption
4.9
is equivalent to the existence of the limit

\lim_{k\to\infty}k\sigma_{k}
.

Proposition 4.10
.

Under Assumptions
4.6
and
4.9
the following assertions hold.

(1)

If
\theta_{0}=\theta_{1}=0
, then
{M}^{\downarrow}
is positive recurrent and admits a unique invariant distribution with support equal to
\mathbb{N}
.

(2)

If
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}>0
, then
{M}^{\downarrow}
is almost surely absorbed in
\{0,\partial\}
in finite time.

Proof.

Assume first that
\theta_{0}=\theta_{1}=0
. In this case,
{\bf p}^{\downarrow}
is differentiable and

\lim_{z\to 1}({\bf p}^{\downarrow})^{\prime}(z)=1+\dfrac{2\delta}{3}\sigma_{0}\mathfrak{b}<\infty.

Using this and Eq. (
4.5
), we deduce that

z\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{z}[({Z}^{\downarrow}_{1})^{m}]
is also differentiable. Moreover, differentiating this function first using (
4.5
) and then using
the moment duality (
2.4
) with
n=1
, and letting
z\to 1
in both expressions, we infer that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{m}[{M}^{\downarrow}_{1}]-m=-\dfrac{\delta}{2}m+\left(1+\dfrac{2\delta}{3}\sigma_{0}\mathfrak{b}\right)(1-\lambda_{m,0}),

which can be made
<-1
uniformly on
m\geq m_{0}
for some
m_{0}\geq 1
(which may depend on
\delta
). Hence, we deduce from
[
4
, Thm. 2.19]
that any finite subset of
\mathbb{N}
is positive recurrent for the chain
{M}^{\downarrow}
. Since, in addition,
{M}^{\downarrow}
is irreducible, the result follows.

Assume now that
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}>0
. As seen in Lemma
4.7
one can couple the chain
{M}^{\downarrow}
to a copy
M^{\downarrow,0}
of the chain without mutations in a way that
{M}^{\downarrow}\leq M^{\downarrow,0}
until the first time
{M}^{\downarrow}
absorbs at
\partial
. By construction, every time
M^{\downarrow,0}
is at
1
, either
{M}^{\downarrow}
is already absorbed at
0
or at
\partial
, or it will absorb at
0
with a constant positive probability in the next generation. So
{M}^{\downarrow}
will absorb at
0
or
\partial
at the latest after a geometric number of excursions of
M^{\downarrow,0}
(away from
1
). The result follows from the positive recurrence of
M^{\downarrow,0}
.
∎

4.4.1.
The case without mutations

In this section, we briefly discuss the case where
\theta_{0}=\theta_{1}=0
, i.e. when there are no mutations.
In this setting, under Assumption
4.6
, we have
{\bf p}^{\downarrow}(x)\leq x
. Thus, Proposition
4.1
implies that
{Z}^{\downarrow}
is a bounded supermartingale. Therefore,
{Z}^{\downarrow}_{n}
converges a.s. and in
L^{p}
as

to a limit
{Z}^{\downarrow}_{\infty}
. Note also that
{\bf p}^{\downarrow}
satisfies the hypotheses of Proposition
4.2
, and thus
{Z}^{\downarrow}_{\infty}\in\{0,1\}
almost surely; alternatively, under Assumption
4.9
, this also follows from the duality relation (Thm.
2.2
) and the positive recurrence of
{M}^{\downarrow}
(Prop.
4.10
). From the biological point of view, this means that there will be fixation of one of the two types almost surely. Moreover, using again that
{\bf p}^{\downarrow}(x)\leq x
, we deduce from Lemma
4.5
that we can couple the chain
{Z}^{\downarrow}
and the neutral chain
Z
starting from
{Z}^{\downarrow}_{0}=Z_{0}=z\in[0,1]
so that
{Z}^{\downarrow}_{n}\leq Z_{n}
for every
n\geq 0
. As a consequence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({Z}^{\downarrow}_{\infty}=0)\geq\mathbb{P}(Z_{\infty}=0)=1-z.

This already implies that if the population starts with an arbitrary, but positive fraction of type
1
individuals, then the probability that type
1
fixates (and hence, type
0
gets extinct) in the population is strictly positive. The next result states that under Assumption
4.9
this also holds for type
0
.

Proposition 4.11
.

Assume that
4.6
and
4.9
hold.
Then, we have for all
z\in(0,1)

\mathbb{P}_{z}({Z}^{\downarrow}_{\infty}=1)=1-\mathbb{P}_{z}({Z}^{\downarrow}_{\infty}=0)=\mathbb{E}[z^{{M}^{\downarrow}_{\infty}}]\in(0,1),

where
{M}^{\downarrow}_{\infty}
is a random variable distributed as the stationary distribution of
{M}^{\downarrow}
.

Proof.

Recall from Proposition
4.10
that under Assumptions
4.6
and
4.9

{M}^{\downarrow}
is positive recurrent and has a unique stationary distribution
\hat{\pi}
. Since, in addition,
{M}^{\downarrow}
is irreducible and aperiodic,
{M}^{\downarrow}_{n}
converges in distribution, independently of the initial condition, to a random variable
{M}^{\downarrow}_{\infty}\sim\hat{\pi}
. Thus, letting
n\to\infty
in the duality relation in Theorem
2.2
, we deduce that

\mathbb{P}_{z}[{Z}^{\downarrow}_{\infty}=1]=\mathbb{E}[z^{{M}^{\downarrow}_{\infty}}],\quad z\in[0,1].

The result follows noting that for
z\in(0,1)
:

\displaystyle 0<\mathbb{P}[{M}^{\downarrow}_{\infty}=1]z\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{P}[{M}^{\downarrow}_{\infty}=k]z^{k}=\mathbb{E}[z^{{M}^{\downarrow}_{\infty}}]<1.

The first inequality is due to the first point at Proposition
4.10
, the second inequality is obvious, the equality is just a definition, and the last inequality is again a consequence of Proposition
4.10
.
∎

4.4.2.
The case with mutations and no selection

Let us now consider the case with mutations to type
0
and
1
, i.e. when
\theta_{0},\theta_{1}>0
. The aim of this section is to describe the long-term behavior of the process
{Z}^{\downarrow}
. As in the case without mutations, the moment duality in Theorem
2.2
will play an important role.

Recall that, thanks to Proposition
4.10
,
{M}^{\downarrow}
almost surely gets absorbed at
\{0,\partial\}
in finite time. Let us then define for
r\in\overline{\mathbb{N}}_{0}

\displaystyle\tau_{r}\coloneqq\inf\{k\geq 0:{M}^{\downarrow}_{k}=r\}.

The next result shows that in the presence of bidirectional mutations the process
{Z}^{\downarrow}
is ergodic, and characterizes the moments of its stationary distribution.

Proposition 4.12
.

Assume that
4.6
and
4.9
hold.
Then, the
{Z}^{\downarrow}_{n}
converges in distribution as
n\to\infty
, independently of the initial condition, to a random variable
{Z}^{\downarrow}_{\infty}
whose moments are given by

\mathbb{E}[({Z}^{\downarrow}_{\infty})^{m}]=\mathbb{P}_{m}(\tau_{0}<\tau_{\partial}),\quad m\in\overline{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

Proof.

Recall that under the assumptions of the proposition, the process
{M}^{\downarrow}
almost surely gets absorbed at
\{0,\partial\}
in finite time. Hence, letting
n\to\infty
in the duality relation in Theorem
2.2
we deduce that, for any
z\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{z}[({Z}^{\downarrow}_{n})^{m}]=\mathbb{P}_{m}(\tau_{0}<\tau_{\partial}).

Note that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
z\in[0,1]
, the sequence

(\mathbb{E}_{z}[({Z}^{\downarrow}_{n})^{m}])_{m\geq 0}
consists of the moments of a
[0,1]
-valued
random variable, and is therefore completely monotone. Since

(\mathbb{P}_{m}(\tau_{0}<\tau_{\partial}))_{m\geq 0}
arises as the pointwise limit of
this sequence as
n\to\infty
, it is also completely monotone. Moreover,
because
\mathbb{P}_{0}(\tau_{0}<\tau_{\partial})=1
, the Hausdorff moment problem (see
[
23
]
) implies
that
(\mathbb{P}_{m}(\tau_{0}<\tau_{\partial}))_{m\geq 0}
is the moment sequence of a

[0,1]
-valued random variable. Thus, the result follows recalling that probability measures on
[0,1]
are completely determined by their positive integer moments and that convergence of positive integer moments implies convergence in distribution.
∎

Remark 4.13

(The case without selection and small
\delta
)
.

Assume now that
\sigma_{i}=0
for
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Observe that by Proposition
4.12
,
{Z}^{\downarrow}
admits a unique invariant distribution. Denote by

\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1};\delta}
this invariant law; this time we make explicit the dependency on
\delta
, as we are interested in this law for small values of
\delta
. Denote the
m
-th moment of
\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1};\delta}
by
\langle\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1};\delta}\rangle_{m}
. According to Proposition
4.12

\displaystyle\langle\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1};\delta}\rangle_{m}=\mathbb{P}_{m}(\tau_{0}<\tau_{\partial}).

Clearly,
\langle\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1};\delta}\rangle_{0}=1
. A short calculation shows as well that
\langle\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1};\delta}\rangle_{1}=\theta_{0}/\theta
, where
\theta=\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}
. More generally, a first-step decomposition yields for
n\geq 2
:

\displaystyle\langle\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1};\delta}\rangle_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\mathbb{P}_{m}(\tau_{0}<\tau_{\partial}|{M}^{\downarrow}_{1}=j)\mathbb{P}_{m}({M}^{\downarrow}_{1}=j).

Now, recall the definition of the
\lambda_{m,k}
in (
4.3
). A straightforward computation yields:

\displaystyle\lambda_{m,0}

\displaystyle=1-\dfrac{m}{2}\delta+\dfrac{m(m-1)}{6}\delta^{2}+o(\delta^{2}),

\displaystyle m\lambda_{m,1}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{m}{2}\delta-\dfrac{m(m-1)}{3}\delta^{2}+o(\delta^{2}),

\displaystyle\frac{m(m-1)}{2}\lambda_{m,2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{m(m-1)}{6}\delta^{2}+o(\delta^{2}),

\displaystyle\lambda_{m,k}

\displaystyle=o(\delta^{2}),\quad k>2.

Thus, after inspection of the transition rates of
{M}^{\downarrow}
in (
4.4
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{m}({M}^{\downarrow}_{1}=m)

\displaystyle=1-\left(\frac{m(m-1)}{6}+\frac{2\theta m}{6}\right)\delta^{2}+o(\delta^{2})

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{m}({M}^{\downarrow}_{1}=m-1)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{m(m-1)}{6}+\frac{2\theta_{0}m}{6}\right)\delta^{2}+o(\delta^{2})

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{m}({M}^{\downarrow}_{1}=m-k)

\displaystyle=o(\delta^{2}).

Hence,

\displaystyle\langle\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1};\delta}\rangle_{m}

\displaystyle=\langle\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1};\delta}\rangle_{m-1}\mathbb{P}_{m}({M}^{\downarrow}_{1}=m-1)+\langle\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1};\delta}\rangle_{m}\mathbb{P}_{m}({M}^{\downarrow}_{1}=m)+o(\delta^{2}).

and so:

\displaystyle\langle\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1};\delta}\rangle_{m}=\left(\dfrac{m-1+2\theta_{0}}{m-1+2\theta}\right)\langle\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1};\delta}\rangle_{m-1}+o(1).

Let
\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}}
be the
{\rm Beta}(2\theta_{0},2\theta_{1})
distribution. It is well known that the moments of
\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}}
satisfy the recurrence relation

\displaystyle\langle\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}}\rangle_{m}=\left(\dfrac{m-1+2\theta_{0}}{m-1+2\theta}\right)\langle\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}}\rangle_{m-1}.

Since this recurrence relation determines the moments and

\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}}
is supported on
[0,1]
, then
\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}}
is the unique
distribution satisfying the relation (see, e.g.
[
17
]
,
Theorem 3.4.5). By these recurrences, the moments of

\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1};\delta}
converge, as
\delta\to 0
, to those of

\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}}
. Because the convergence of moments for

[0,1]
-valued random variables implies convergence in distribution, we
conclude that
\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1};\delta}
converges
to
\beta_{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}}
as
\delta\to 0
.

4.5.
Scaling limit

In this section, we provide the proof of Theorem
2.4
, which establishes the convergence of the Markov chain
Z^{(\delta_{N},{\bf p}_{N})}
, with
\delta_{N}
and
{\bf p}_{N}
satisfying (
2.5
), to a Wright–Fisher diffusion with drift as
N\to\infty
under the appropriate time scaling
t\mapsto\lfloor Nt\rfloor
.

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Pathwise uniqueness for SDE (
WF
) follows from
[
17
, Chap. 5, Thm. 3.8]
(see also
[
41
, Chap IX, Thm. 3.5]
).
Existence of weak solutions for any initial condition follows from
[
17
, Chap. 5, Thm. 3.10]
. Existence of strong solutions follows then from the theorem of Yamada-Watanabe. Furthermore, a standard application of Itô’s formula, shows that
X
solves the martingale problem associated to the operator
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{C}^{2}([0,1])\mapsto\mathcal{C}([0,1])
acting on
h\in\mathcal{C}^{2}([0,1])
via

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}h(x)=b(x)\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}h(x)+\dfrac{1}{2}x(1-x)\dfrac{{{\rm d}}^{2}}{{\rm d}x^{2}}h(x).

We deduce from
[
17
, Chap. 8, Thm 2.1]
that
X
induces a Feller semigroup with infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,1])
as a core.
Therefore, according to
[
29
, Thm. 17.28]
it suffices to show that for every
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,1])

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\lvert N\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(Z_{1}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf p}_{N})})-f(x)]-\mathcal{L}(x)\rvert=0.

Setting for
k\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle a^{(k)}_{N}(x)\coloneqq N\mathbb{E}^{(N)}_{x}[(Z_{1}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf p}_{N})}-x)^{k}],

and using a Taylor expansion of
f
, we see that it suffices to show that

a_{N}^{(1)}(x)\to b(x),\quad a_{N}^{(2)}(x)\to x(1-x),\quad a_{N}^{(3)}(x)\to 0

as
N\to\infty
uniformly in
x\in[0,1]
.
A straightforward calculation shows that

\displaystyle a^{(k)}_{N}(x)=\dfrac{3^{k/2}N^{1-k/2}}{(k+1)}\left((-1)^{k}(1-{\bf p}_{N}(x))x^{k}+{\bf p}_{N}(x)(1-x)^{k}\right).

Using this for
k=1
,
k=2
and
k=3
we see that

\displaystyle a^{(1)}_{N}(x)

\displaystyle=b(x),\quad a^{(2)}_{N}(x)=x(1-x)+O(N^{-1/2}),\quad a^{(3)}(x)=O(N^{-1/2}),

where the error terms in the second and third identities are uniform in
x
. The result follows.
∎

Remark 4.14

(Fittest-type-wins revisited)
.

Note that choosing
b:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle b(x)\coloneqq-\theta_{1}x+\theta_{0}(1-x)-x(1-x)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sigma_{j}x^{j},\quad x\in[0,1],

and setting
\delta_{N}\coloneqq\sqrt{3/N}
, yields
{\bf p}_{N}={\bf p}_{\delta_{N}}^{\downarrow}
(see Section
4.2
to recall the definition of
{\bf p}_{\delta}^{\downarrow}
). Assume that Assumptions
4.6
and
4.9
are satisfied.
Then, the process
Z^{(\delta_{N},{\bf p}_{N})}
admits a moment dual
M^{(\delta_{N},{\bf p}_{N})}
(see Section
4.2
for the transitions).
According to Theorem
2.4
the process
(Z^{(N,{\bf p}_{N})}_{\lfloor Nt\rfloor})_{t\geq 0}
converges as
N\to\infty
to the solution of the SDE

{\rm d}X_{t}=\bigg(-\theta_{1}X_{t}+\theta_{0}(1-X_{t})-X_{t}(1-X_{t})\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sigma_{j}X_{t}^{j}\bigg)\,{\rm d}t+\sqrt{X_{t}(1-X_{t})}{\rm d}W_{t},

which is referred in the literature as Wright–Fisher diffusion with fittest-type-wins selection and bi-directional mutation.
An asymptotic analysis of the transition probabilities of the dual process

(similar to the one performed in the proof of Theorem

) allows one to show that

\displaystyle\mathcal{\hat{L}}h(n)=\bigg(\binom{n}{2}+n\theta_{0}\bigg)(h(n-1)-h(n))+n\sigma_{0}\sum_{j\geq 1}\rho_{j}(h(n+j)-h(n))-n\theta_{1}h(n).

This limiting branching-coalescing process can be stochastically dominated by a branching process transitioning from

at rate

,

, which is non-explosive under Assumption
4.9
. Hence, our limiting process is also non-explosive (see
[
22
, Chap. V, Thm. 9.1]
). In particular, the moment duality stated in Theorem
2.4
is preserved in the limit, and we recover the moment duality stated in
[
21
]
(see also
[
2
]
).
∎

5.
Non-linear chains

In this section we introduce the notion of
non-linear Markov chain
(see
[
31
]
for a detailed exposition) and consider a particular class constructed on the basis of Wright–Fisher kernels; we will refer to them as
non-linear Wright–Fisher chains
. We show that these chains admit a unique invariant distribution, which arises as their limit when time tends to infinity (see Section
5.2
). Section
5.3
is devoted to the study of its scaling limits. More precisely, we construct, via an appropriate scaling of time and of parameters, a sequence of non-linear Wright–Fisher chains converging, as the scaling parameter tends to infinity, to a McKean–Vlasov diffusion.

The results presented in this section generalize some of the results obtained in Section
4.5
to the non-linear setting. Moreover, as we will see in Section
6
, the non-linear Wright–Fisher chains introduced in this section approximate the evolution of the type composition of pathogens within a single host, which interacts in a mean-field fashion with a myriad of (identically distributed) hosts. Therefore, we can see this section as a step towards the modeling of a two-level population (i.e. of the population at the deme level).

5.1.
Non-linear Markov chains and their invariant distributions

Roughly speaking, a non-linear Markov chain is a sequence of random variables where the transition to the next state does not only depend on the current state, but also on its distribution. This concept bears resemblance to the notion of
non-linear Markov process
, a term initially introduced by McKean in the context of non-linear parabolic partial differential equations. For a detailed exposition on this topic, we refer to the seminal article by McKean
[
35
]
.

Definition 5.1

(Non-linear Markov chain)
.

Let
(E,\mathcal{E})
be a measurable space,
\mathbb{Q}\coloneqq(Q_{\nu})_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(E)}
be a family of Markov transition operators on
(E,\mathcal{E})
and
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(E)
. We say that a sequence of random variables
({\overline{Y}}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
on
(E,\mathcal{P}(E))
is a
non-linear Markov chain driven by
(\mu_{0},\mathbb{Q})
if
{\overline{Y}_{0}}\sim\mu_{0}
and, for all
n\geq 1
,

{\overline{Y}}_{n}\sim Q_{\mu_{n-1}}({\overline{Y}}_{n-1},\cdot),\quad\textrm{where}\quad\mu_{n-1}\coloneqq\Law({\overline{Y}}_{n-1}).

(5.1)

If the initial distribution is not specified we say that the non-linear Markov chain is driven by
\mathbb{Q}
.

Note that the one-dimensional distributions
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of the non-linear chain
({\overline{Y}}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
can be computed via the recursion

\displaystyle\quad\mu_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mu_{n}Q_{\mu_{n}},\quad n\geq 0.

More generally, the finite dimensional distributions can be computed in a recursive way as follows. Let
\vec{\mu}_{n}
denote the law of
({\overline{Y}}_{0},\ldots,{\overline{Y}}_{n})
,
n\geq 0
. Then, for any
n\geq 0
and any bounded measurable function
f:E^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\vec{\mu}_{n+1}f=\int_{E^{n}}\vec{\mu}_{n}({\rm d}x_{0},\ldots,{\rm d}x_{n})\int_{E}Q_{\mu_{n}}(x_{n},{\rm d}x_{n+1})f(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n},x_{n+1}).

In particular, if there is a transition kernel
Q
on
(E,\mathcal{E})
such that
Q_{\nu}=Q
for all
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(E)
, then
({\overline{Y}}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a Markov chain in the traditional sense. However, this is not true in general. Indeed, if
\mathcal{B}_{b}
is the set of real-valued, bounded Borel measurable functions, the operators
T_{n}:\mathcal{B}_{b}\mapsto\mathcal{B}_{b}
,
n\geq 0
, defined through
T_{n}f(x)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{x}[f({\overline{Y}}_{n})]
, do not satisfy the semigroup property, i.e.
T_{n+m}
is not necessarily equal to
T_{n}\circ T_{m}
. Nevertheless, if for
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(E)
we denote
\mu\mathbb{Q}^{(n)}
as the law of
{\overline{Y}}_{n}
when
{\overline{Y}}_{0}
has law
\mu
(with the conventions
\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{Q}^{(1)}
and
\mathbb{Q}^{(0)}
is the identity on
\mathcal{P}(E)
), then the semigroup property holds in the sense that
(\mu\mathbb{Q}^{(n)})\mathbb{Q}^{(m)}=\mu\mathbb{Q}^{(n+m)}
. This motivates the following notion.

Definition 5.2

(Invariant distributions)
.

An invariant distribution for
\mathbb{Q}\coloneqq(Q_{\nu})_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(E)}
, or equivalently for the non-linear Markov chain driven by
\mathbb{Q}
, is a probability distribution
\eta\in\mathcal{P}(E)
satisfying
\eta\mathbb{Q}=\eta
, where
\eta\mathbb{Q}:=\eta Q_{\eta}
.

Remark 5.3

(Non-linear chains are linear under invariant distributions)
.

Note that if
\eta
is an invariant distribution for
\mathbb{Q}\coloneqq(Q_{\nu})_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(E)}
, then
\eta
is an invariant distribution for the Markov chain driven by the kernel
Q_{\eta}
. Moreover, the non-linear Markov chain driven by
(\eta,\mathbb{Q})
and the Markov chain driven by the the kernel
Q_{\eta}
and initial distribution
\eta
are equally distributed.

The following result about existence and uniqueness of invariant distributions for non-linear Markov chains will be useful in the forthcoming sections.

Proposition 5.4
.

Assume that
(E,d)
is a compact metric space. Let
\mathbb{Q}\coloneqq(Q_{\nu})_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(E)}
be a family of Markov transition operators on
(E,\mathcal{B}(E,d))
. Assume that for every
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(E)
there exists a unique stationary distribution
T\nu\in\mathcal{P}(E)
for the kernel
Q_{\nu}
. Assume further that the map
\nu\mapsto T\nu
is a
\Was
-strict contraction (i.e. it is a
\Was
-Lipschitz map with Lipschitz constant smaller than
1
). Then,
\mathbb{Q}
admits a unique invariant distribution.

Proof.

Since
(\mathcal{P}(E),\Was)
is complete and
T
is a contraction, by Banach’s fixed point theorem there exists a unique element
\eta\in\mathcal{P}(E)
such that
T\eta=\eta
. Note that by definition of
\eta\mathbb{Q}
we have

\displaystyle\eta\mathbb{Q}

\displaystyle=\eta Q_{\eta}=(T\eta)Q_{\eta}=T\eta=\eta.

Hence,
\eta
is invariant for the non-linear chain driven by
\mathbb{Q}
. Moreover, if
\eta_{1}
is another invariant distribution for the non-linear chain, then
\eta_{1}=\eta_{1}\mathbb{Q}=\eta_{1}Q_{\eta_{1}}
. Hence,
\eta_{1}
is a fixed point of
T
. From the uniqueness of fixed points for
T
we conclude that
\eta_{1}=\eta
.
∎

5.2.
Ergodic properties of the Non-linear Wright–Fisher chains

In this section we introduce the non-linear Wright–Fisher chains and prove their ergodicity (Theorem
2.7
). Before proceeding to the proof of this result, we say some words regarding its motivation and relevance. First, we remark the fact that, in general, it is far from trivial to guarantee that a given McKean-Vlasov equation admits unique invariant distribution, even in very simple cases (see e.g.
[
24
]
). Then, even if one is able to guarantee the existence and uniqueness of an invariant distribution, the standard machinery of the ergodic theory of Markov process is not immediately available to prove the attraction of any initial distribution to the invariant law, let alone to estimate the rates of this convergence. Now, as we will see in section
5.3
below, for a class of McKean-Vlasov equations inspired by population genetic models, the non-linear chains we are dealing with in this section (after some scaling procedure) are good proxies of the diffusion models, and in fact, as we will see in Section
7
, the convergence rates of these non-linear chains (given by Theorem
2.7
) can be lifted up to the limiting model.

The non-linear chains we are going to deal with are constructed on the basis of the two-parameters family of kernels
(P_{\delta,p})_{\delta,p\in[0,1]}
on
[0.1]
introduced above.

Definition 5.5

(Non-linear Wright–Fisher chains)
.

Let
{\bf{\overline{p}}}:[0,1]\times\mathcal{P}\mapsto[0,1]
be a measurable function and
\delta>0
. For
x\in[0,1]
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}
, set

5.3.
Scaling limits: the McKean–Vlasov Wright–Fisher process

In Section
4.5
, we proved that, as
\delta\to 0
, Markov chains based on Wright–Fisher kernels of the form
P_{\delta,{\bf p}_{\delta}}
converge to a broad class of Wright–Fisher diffusions. The structure of these processes allowed us to apply well-known results on the weak convergence of Markov chains to diffusion processes. Our goal here is to prove Theorem
2.9
, which establishes the convergence of the non-linear chains introduced in the previous section to the McKean–Vlasov Wright–Fisher diffusion. In this setting, the techniques used for classical Markov processes are no longer directly applicable. Instead, our approach relies on convergence results for semimartingales together with uniqueness for an associated non-linear martingale problem.

The remainder of the section is organized as follows. We begin by proving Theorem
2.9
based on three auxiliary propositions, each addressing a key component of the argument: tightness, characterization of accumulation points, and the connection between uniqueness of solutions to the McKean–Vlasov diffusion and an associated martingale problem. We then establish these auxiliary propositions in separate subsections.

5.3.1.
The skeleton of the proof

In what follows we will consider a function
{\bf{\overline{b}}}:[0,1]\times\mathcal{P}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
satisfying Assumption
2.8
.
As announced in display (
2.8
) in Section
2.2
, we will consider the following scaling of parameters:

\displaystyle\delta_{N}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{N}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}(x,\mu)=x+\frac{2}{\sqrt{3N}}{\bf{\overline{b}}}(x,\mu).

Under Assumption
2.8
, the function
{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}(x,\mu)
is
(1+2L_{1}/\sqrt{3N},2L_{2}/\sqrt{3N})
-Lipschitz, and there exists
N_{1}>0
, such that, for any
N\geq N_{1}
and any
(x,\mu)\in[0,1]\times\mathcal{P}([0,1])
,
{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}(x,\mu)\in[0,1]
.

We now consider the non-linear chain
{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}
with
N\geq N_{1}
. Our goal is to
prove Theorem
2.9
; that is, we show that for any

T\geq 0
the sequence of semimartingales
({\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{[0,T]}:N\geq N_{1})
,
defined in (
2.9
) by

{\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{t}={\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{\lfloor Nt\rfloor},\,t\geq 0,

converges to the unique solution of a McKean–Vlasov equation. More precisely,
we prove that the laws of these rescaled processes, viewed as probability
measures on the Skorokhod space equipped with the
J_{1}
topology, converge weakly
to the unique solution of a non-linear martingale problem, which coincides with
the unique solution of the McKean–Vlasov diffusion (
MVWF
). By a

solution
of this equation we mean the following.

Definition 5.8
.

We say that the MV equation (
MVWF
) has a weak solution on
[0,T]
(resp.
[0,\infty)
) if there exists a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t}),\mathbb{P})
, an
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-standard brownian motion and a continuous
[0,1]
-valued
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted process such that
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., (
MVWF
) holds on
t\in[0,T]
(resp.
t\geq 0
). We say that weak uniqueness hold on
[0,T]
(resp.
[0,\infty)
) if any two weak solutions induce the same law on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}([0,T];[0,1]))
(resp.
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}([0,\infty);[0,1]))
).

The proof of Theorem
2.9
is presented in three steps, each of them encapsulated in a Proposition. First we state the tightness of the sequence of laws of
({\overline{X}}^{(N)})_{N\geq 1}
(Proposition
5.9
), then we identify the limits points of this sequence as solutions of a
nonlinear martingale problem
, which we introduce below (Proposition
5.10
). Finally, we prove the uniqueness of the this limit (Proposition
5.11
). The proofs of these propositions are deferred to the following subsections.

Proposition 5.9

(Tightness)
.

Under Assumption
2.8
, the sequence of laws
(P^{(N)})_{N}
of the semimartingales
({\overline{X}}^{(N)})_{N}
, as probability measures on the Skorokhod space
\mathbb{D}([0,+\infty);[0,1])
, is a tight sequence.

To identify the limits of the sequence
(P^{(N)})_{N}
, we introduce
a
nonlinear martingale problem.

Consider the space
\Omega=(\mathbb{D}([0,+\infty);[0,1]),\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{D}([0,+\infty);[0,1])))
, where the underlying topology is the
J_{1}
-Skorokhod topology, and endow it with the canonical filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. For
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
and
\varphi
regular enough we put:

\displaystyle L_{\mu}\phi(x):={\bf{\overline{b}}}(x,\mu)g^{\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{2}x(1-x)g^{\prime\prime}(x).

The
non-linear
martingale problem reads as follows:

Find
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{D}([0,+\infty);[0,1]))
endowed with its Borel
\sigma
-field and the canonical filtration, such that, with
X_{t}(\omega)=\omega_{t}
the canonical process, for every
g\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,1])
, the process:

\displaystyle M^{(g)}_{t}:=

\displaystyle g(X_{t})-g(X_{0})-\int_{0}^{t}L_{\mu_{s}}g(X_{s}){\rm d}s,\quad(t\geq 0)

(NLMP)

is a
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-martingale under
\mu
and, moreover,
\mu_{0}=\overline{\mu}_{0}
.

An application of Itô’s formula shows that
{\overline{\mu}}
, the law of
{\overline{X}}
solution of (
MVWF
), solves this nonlinear martingale problem.

Proposition 5.10

(Characterization)
.

Under Assumption
2.8
, any weak limit point
P
of
(P^{(N)})_{N\geq 1}
solves the non-linear martingale problem (
NLMP
).

Proposition 5.11

(Uniqueness)
.

Under Assumption
2.8
, if the McKean–Vlasov stochastic differential equation (
MVWF
) admits a unique weak solution
{\overline{X}}
, the (
NLMP
) has a unique solution in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}([0,+\infty);[0,1]))
(which is, of course, the law of the process
{\overline{X}}
).

Now we are in position to prove the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem
2.9
.

As announced in the beginning of this section, by Proposition
5.9
, there exist limit points for the sequence
(\Law({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}))_{N\geq 1}
. Moreover, any of these limit points solves the non-linear martingale problem thanks to Proposition
5.10
. Nevertheless, according to Proposition
5.11
, the non-linear martingale problem has a unique solution; which is in fact, the unique solution of the McKean–Vlasov equation. Summarizing, the the sequence
(\Law({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}))_{N\geq 1}
converges to the law of the solution of (
MVWF
).
∎

5.3.2.
Tightness

To prove the result, we rely on the well-known Rebolledo-Aldous criterion, which we recall next. We say that a family of processes
(V^{(N)}_{t}:t\geq 0)_{N\geq 1}
satisfy the tightness condition
\bf[T_{1}]
if for every
t\geq 0
the family
(\Law(V^{(N)}_{t}))_{N\geq 1}
is tight. We say that a family of processes
(V^{(N)}_{t}:t\geq 0)_{N\geq 1}
(
V^{(N)}
being a
\mathcal{F}^{(N)}
-adapted process), satisfy the Aldous’ condition
\bf{[A]}
if for each
C>0,\varepsilon>0,\eta>0
there exists
\delta>0,N^{\prime}>0
such that for any sequence of stopping times bounded by
C
,
(\tau^{(N)})_{N\geq 1}
(
\tau^{(N)}
being a
\mathcal{F}^{(N)}
-stopping time) we have:

\displaystyle\sup_{N\geq N^{\prime}}\sup_{\theta<\delta}\mathbb{P}[|V^{(N)}_{\tau^{(N)}}-V^{(N)}_{\tau^{(N)}+\theta}|>\eta]<\varepsilon.

The Rebolledo-Aldous criterion states that if for a sequence of semimartingales in
\mathbb{D}([0,\infty);[0,1])
, both, the sequence of predictable processes and the sequence of quadratic predictable processes associated to the martingale parts, satisfy the conditions
\bf[T_{1}]
and
\bf[A]
, then the sequence of semimartingales is a tight sequence on
\mathbb{D}
(see e.g. Corollary 2.3.3 at
[
28
]
).

Proof of Proposition
5.9
.

Let us start by computing the characteristics of
{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}
. Denote
{\overline{\mu}}^{(N)}_{n}=\Law({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{n})
, and:

\displaystyle A^{(N)}_{n}=\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2N^{1/2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}({\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{k},{\overline{\mu}}^{(N)}_{k})-{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{k}),

\displaystyle M^{(N)}_{n}={\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{n}-{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{0}-A_{n}^{(N)}.

It is straightforward to show that
M^{(N)}
is a square-integrable martingale, with quadratic predictable process:

\displaystyle\langle M^{(N)}\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{3}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Bigg(\dfrac{1}{3}({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{k})^{2}(1-{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{k},{\overline{\mu}}^{(N)}_{k}))

\displaystyle\qquad+\dfrac{1}{3}(1-{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{k})^{2}{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{k},{\overline{\mu}}^{(N)}_{k})-\dfrac{1}{4}({\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{k},{\overline{\mu}}^{(N)}_{k})-{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{k})^{2}\Bigg).

Write
\tilde{A}_{t}^{(N)}=A^{(N)}_{\lfloor Nt\rfloor}
and
\tilde{K}^{(N)}_{t}=\langle M^{(N)}\rangle_{\lfloor Nt\rfloor}
. We need to prove that the sequences of processes
(\tilde{A}^{(N)})_{N\geq 1}
and
(\tilde{K}^{(N)})_{N\geq 1}
satisfy the conditions
\bf[T_{1}]
and
\bf[A]
above.

Let’s start with the tightness condition. For fixed
t\geq 0
, we have, for every
N\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{A}^{(N)}_{t}|]\leq\dfrac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor Nt\rfloor-1}\bigg|{\bf{\overline{b}}}({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{k},\mu^{(N)}_{k})\bigg|\bigg]\leq Ct,

for some constant
C^{\prime}
that depends on the bounds of
{\bf{\overline{b}}}
. Analogously, the terms inside the sum at the definition of
\langle M^{(N)}\rangle
are bounded, and again, for some constant
C^{\prime\prime}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{K}^{(N)}_{t}|]\leq C^{\prime\prime}t
for every
N\geq 1
. We deduce the tightness of the laws of the sequences
(\tilde{A}^{(N)}_{t})_{N\geq 1}
and
(\tilde{K}^{(N)}_{t})_{N\geq 1}
.

As for the the Aldous’ condition, fix
C>0,\varepsilon>0,\eta>0
. For any sequence of stopping times
\tau^{(N)}
and any
\theta>0
:

\displaystyle\bigg|\tilde{A}^{(N)}_{\tau^{(N)}+\theta}-A^{(N)}_{\tau^{(N)}+\theta}\bigg|=\dfrac{1}{N}\Bigg|\sum_{k=\lfloor N\tau^{(N)}\rfloor}^{\lfloor N\tau^{(N)}+N\theta\rfloor-1}{\bf{\overline{b}}}({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{k},\mu^{(N)}_{k})\Bigg|,

The sum contains at most
N\theta+1
terms, uniformly bounded by some constant depending on
{\bf{\overline{b}}}
, say
C^{\prime}
. Thus:

\displaystyle\bigg|\tilde{A}^{(N)}_{\tau^{(N)}+\theta}-\tilde{A}^{(N)}_{\tau^{(N)}+\theta}\bigg|\ >\eta\Rightarrow C^{\prime}(\theta+1/N)>\eta,

and the right hand side fails to hold as soon as
\theta
is small enough and
N
is large enough. So, the sequence
(\tilde{A}^{(N)})_{N\geq 1}
satisfies the condition
\bf[A]
. A completely analogous reasoning leads to the verification that this condition also holds for
(\tilde{K}^{(N)})_{N\geq 1}
.
∎

5.3.3.
Identification of the limit

We have prove that the sequence of semimartingales
({\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{[0,T]}:N\geq 1)
is tight. Now we proceed to show that any of its limit points solves the non-linear martingale problem.

Proof of Proposition
5.10
.

Let
P
be any limit point of the sequence
(P^{(N)})
; without loss of generality, we can assume that the whole sequence converges weakly to
P
. As above,
X_{t}(\omega)=\omega_{t}
stands for the canonical process on the Skorokhod space. The functional
\bm{\Psi}:\mathbb{D}([0,+\infty);[0,1])\mapsto\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
given by
\omega\mapsto\sup_{t\geq 0}|\omega_{t}-\omega_{t-}|
is continuous for the Skorokhod topology, and under
P^{(N)}
,
\bm{\Psi}(X)\leq N^{-1/2}
. Thus, by definition of weak convergence:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{D}}\bm{\Psi}(\omega)P({\rm d}\omega)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\bm{\Psi}(\omega)P^{(N)}({\rm d}\omega)=0,

and consequently,
P(\mathcal{C}([0,+\infty);[0,1]))=1
. Write
P_{s}\coloneqq\Law_{P}(X_{s})=P\circ X_{s}^{-1}\in\mathcal{P}([0,1])
,
s\geq 0
. For a fixed test function
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,1])
, define the functional
\mathcal{M}_{t}:\mathbb{D}([0,+\infty);[0,1])\mapsto\mathbb{R}
by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{t}(\omega)\coloneqq\varphi(\omega_{t})-\varphi(\omega_{0})-\int_{0}^{t}L_{P_{s}}\varphi(\omega_{s}){\rm d}s.

Observe that, although
\omega\mapsto\omega_{s}
is not continuous for the Skorokhod topology, it is indeed continuous when restricted to the space of continuous paths. Thus, for each
t\in[0,1]
,
\mathcal{M}_{t}
is
P
-a.s. continuous. Let
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\ldots\varphi_{k}
be a family of continuous functions on
[0,1]
,
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{k}<s<t
. Consider the functional
\Xi:\mathbb{D}([0,+\infty);[0,1])\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
:

\displaystyle\Xi(\omega)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\varphi_{k}(\omega_{t_{k}})(\mathcal{M}_{t}(\omega)-\mathcal{M}_{s}(\omega)).

Once again we note that the set of discontinuities of
\Xi
lies outside the support of
P
.

Write
\mathbb{E}
and
\mathbb{E}^{(N)}
for expectation with respect to
P
and
P^{(N)}
, respectively. By weak convergence and the continuous mapping theorem:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Xi(X)]=\lim_{N}\mathbb{E}^{(N)}[\Xi(X)].

(5.4)

Hence, we just need to prove that the sequence
(\mathbb{E}^{(N)}(\Xi(X)))_{N}
converges to
0
.

Let
\delta=(3/N)^{1/2}
, and put:

\displaystyle L^{(N)}_{\mu}\varphi(x)=(1-{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}(x,\mu))\mathbb{E}[\varphi(x-x\delta U)-\varphi(x)]+{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}(x,\mu)\mathbb{E}[\varphi(x+(1-x)\delta U)-\varphi(x)].

Write:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(N)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\varphi(\omega_{t})-\varphi(\omega_{0})-\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor Nt\rfloor-1}L^{(N)}_{P^{(N)}_{k/N}}\varphi(\omega_{k/N});

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{(N)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\varphi(\omega_{t})-\varphi(\omega_{0})-\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor Nt\rfloor-1}L^{(N)}_{P_{k/N}}\varphi(\omega_{k/N});

and define
\Xi^{(N)}
and
\tilde{\Xi}^{(N)}
as
\Xi
but with
\mathcal{M}
replaced by
\mathcal{M}^{(N)}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{(N)}
, respectively. Clearly:

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}^{(N)}[\Xi(X)]|

\displaystyle\leq|\mathbb{E}^{(N)}[\Xi^{(N)}(X)]+\mathbb{E}^{(N)}[|\Xi^{(N)}(X)-\tilde{\Xi}^{(N)}(X)|]

(5.5)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}^{(N)}[|\tilde{\Xi}^{(N)}(X)-\Xi(X)|],

and observe that the first term vanishes since
\mathcal{M}^{(N)}
is a martingale under
P^{(N)}
. So, we have to show that the remaining terms vanish in the limit. Now, as
N
goes to infinity, through a Taylor expansion up to the second order it is easily checked that:

\displaystyle L^{(N)}_{\mu}\varphi(x)=\dfrac{1}{N}({\bf{\overline{b}}}(x,\mu)\varphi^{\prime}(x)+\dfrac{1}{2N}x(1-x)\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x))+o(1/N),

where
o(1/N)
is uniform on
x\in[0,1]
. Hence:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{M}_{t}-\mathcal{Q}^{(N)}_{t}|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{t}L_{P_{s}}\varphi(\omega_{s}){\rm d}s-\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor Nt\rfloor-1}L^{(N)}_{P_{k/N}}\varphi(\omega_{k/N})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{0}^{t}L_{P_{s}}\varphi(\omega_{s}){\rm d}s-\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor Nt\rfloor-1}L_{P_{k/N}}\varphi(\omega_{k/N})\right|+o(1).

Under
P^{(N)}
, we a.s. have
\omega_{s}=\omega_{k/N}
for
s\in[k/N,(k+1)/N)
. Consequently, under
P^{(N)}
,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{M}_{t}-\mathcal{Q}^{(N)}_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{0}^{t}L_{P_{s}}\varphi(\omega_{s}){\rm d}s-\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor Nt\rfloor-1}\int_{k/N}^{(k+1)/N}L_{P_{k/N}}\varphi(\omega_{s}){\rm d}s\right|+o(1)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor Nt\rfloor-1}\int_{k/N}^{(k+1)/N}|L_{P_{k/N}}\varphi(\omega_{s})-L_{P_{s}}\varphi(\omega_{s})|{\rm d}s+o(1)

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor Nt\rfloor-1}\int_{k/N}^{(k+1)/N}\Was(P_{k/n},P_{s}){\rm d}s+o(1),

where the constant
C
involves the Lipschitz constants of
{\bf{\overline{b}}}
and bounds on
\varphi^{\prime}
. Since
s\mapsto P_{s}
is continuous, in particular it is uniformly continuous on
[0,t]
. Hence, given
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
N_{0}>0
such that for every
N>N_{0}
,
0\leq k<\lfloor Nt\rfloor
and

\Was(P_{k/N},P_{s})<\varepsilon
. Then, again under

for
N>N_{0}

\displaystyle|\mathcal{M}_{t}-\mathcal{Q}^{(N)}_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon t+o(1).

By bounded convergence, we deduce that the third term in (

) converges to

as

. Similarly, we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{M}^{(N)}_{t}-\mathcal{Q}^{(N)}_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor Nt\rfloor-1}\left|L_{P_{k/N}}\varphi(\omega_{k/N})-L_{P^{(N)}_{k/N}}\varphi(\omega_{k/N})\right|+o(1)

\displaystyle\leq C\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor Nt\rfloor-1}\Was(P_{k/N},P^{(N)}_{k/N})+o(1),

and, thus, under
P^{(N)}
,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{M}^{(N)}_{t}-\mathcal{Q}^{(N)}_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor Nt\rfloor-1}\int_{k/N}^{(k+1)/N}\Was(P_{k/N},P_{s}){\rm d}s

\displaystyle\quad+C\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor Nt\rfloor-1}\int_{k/N}^{(k+1)/N}\Was(P_{s},P^{(N)}_{s}){\rm d}s+o(1)

\displaystyle=C\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor Nt\rfloor-1}\int_{k/N}^{(k+1)/N}\Was(P_{k/N},P_{s}){\rm d}s

\displaystyle\quad+C\int_{0}^{t}\Was(P_{s},P^{(N)}_{s}){\rm d}s+o(1).

The same argument used previously shows that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
N_{0}
large enough such that for
N\geq N_{0}
, the first sum in the last line above can be made smaller that
C\varepsilon t
. As for the integral term, since

weakly and the map

is

weakly; since

are elements of

converge to

0
. This (and a straightforward application of the bounded convergence theorem) shows that the second term in (

) converge to

∎

Uniqueness of the limit

Finally, we show here that set of limits points of
(P^{(N)}:N\geq 1)

Proof of Proposition

.

Observe that any solution to the non-linear martingale problem solves the non-linear Fokker-Planck equation in the weak form:

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left\langle\mu_{t},\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle\mu_{t},L_{\mu_{t}}\varphi\right\rangle;\quad\mu_{0}=\overline{\mu}_{0},\quad t\geq 0,

[
10
]
, and what follows can be regarded as a streamlined argument.

Observe that, under our assumptions:

(1)

For a given continuous flow of probability measures, there exists a unique strong solution for the “linearized” SDE associated to our problem. More precisely, for any measure
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}([0,+\infty];[0,1]))

\displaystyle{\rm d}X^{(\nu)}_{t}={\bf{\overline{b}}}(X_{t}^{(\nu)},\nu_{t}){\rm d}t+\sqrt{X_{t}^{(\nu)}(1-X_{t}^{(\nu)})}{\rm d}W_{t};\quad X^{(\mu)}_{0}\sim\nu_{0},\quad t\geq 0,

(5.7)

where
\nu_{t}
stands for the image measure of

under the coordinate map
(x_{s})_{s\geq 0}\mapsto x_{t}

[

]
). Moreover pathwise uniqueness follows from Yamada-Watanabe Theorem (Proposition 5.3.20 and Corollary 5.3.23 in
[
30
]
), hence there exists a unique strong solution for equation (
5.7
). Furthermore, under assumption
2.8
, it is easy to see that such solution remains in
[0,1]
.

(2)

For any fixed continuous flow of probability measures, the linearized martingale problem has a unique solution. By the linearized martingale problem we mean the following. For
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}([0,+\infty);[0,1]))
, and
x\in[0,1]

\displaystyle b^{(\nu)}(x,t)\coloneqq{\bf{\overline{b}}}(x,\nu_{t}),

and for
t\geq 0

L^{(\nu)}_{t}

\mathcal{C}^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle L^{(\nu)}_{t}g(x)\coloneqq b^{(\nu)}(x,t)g^{\prime}(x)+\dfrac{1}{2}x(1-x)g^{\prime\prime}(x).

find

\mu_{0}=\nu_{0}

\displaystyle M^{(\nu;g)}_{t}:=g(X_{t})-g(X_{0})-\int_{0}^{t}L^{(\nu)}_{s}g(X_{s}){\rm d}s,\quad(t\geq 0)

(FMP)

These conditions tell us that for a fixed flow
(\nu_{t})

\mu^{\nu}
for:

\displaystyle\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left\langle\mu_{t},\varphi\right\rangle=\left\langle\mu_{t},L_{\nu_{t}}\varphi\right\rangle;\quad\mu_{0}=\overline{\mu}_{0};

(5.8)

and that there exists a unique strong solution

for the SDE (

6.
The deme level: propagation of chaos

In this section, we set up the two-level model that describes the evolution of the fractions of allele-
0

2.2
, the main results in this context are Theorems
2.10
and
2.11
. These theorems are propagation-of-chaos results that compare the pathogen dynamics in the full two-level model with the evolution of pathogens within a single individual governed by a non-linear dynamical system.

6.1.
The two-level model and a coupling for the non-linear dynamics

We consider a population consisting of
M\in\mathbb{N}
hosts, each one being infected by a pathogen. As in the one-level dynamics, the pathogen population consists of allele-
0
and allele-
1
individuals. In addition to
M
, the other parameters of the model are
\delta\in[0,1]
and a measurable function
{\bf{\overline{p}}}:[0,1]\times\mathcal{P}\to[0,1]
. To describe the dynamics of the population it is convenient to define
\hat{\mu}_{M}(\bf z)
, for
{\bf z}\coloneqq(z_{1},\ldots,z_{M})\in[0,1]^{M}
, as the empirical measure of the points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{M}
, i.e.

\hat{\mu}_{M}({\bf z})\coloneqq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\delta_{z_{j}}.

We denote by
{Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,i}
the fraction of allele-
0
pathogens in the
i
-th host,
i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
, at generation
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and we set
\mathbf{Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n}\coloneqq({Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,1},\ldots,{Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,M})
. The evolution of the system is then encoded by the
[0,1]^{M}
-valued Markov chain
\mathbf{Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}\coloneqq(\mathbf{Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
evolving as follows. If
\mathbf{Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n}={\bf z}
, then
\mathbf{Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n+1}
is distributed according to

\displaystyle P_{\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}}(z_{1},\hat{\mu}_{M}({\bf z}))}(z_{1},\cdot)\otimes P_{\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}}(z_{2},\hat{\mu}_{M}({\bf z}))}(z_{2},\cdot)\otimes\ldots\otimes P_{\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}}(z_{M},\hat{\mu}_{M}({\bf z}))}(z_{M},\cdot)

(6.1)

Note that the pathogen dynamics is correlated across hosts only through their empirical measure.

Remark 6.1

(A two-level coupling)
.

Let us now couple the pathogen-host system
\mathbf{Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}
described above to a system of
M

\mathbb{Q}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}
, which we denote by
{\overline{\mathbf{Z}}}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},M)}\coloneqq({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,1},\ldots,{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,M})_{n\geq 0}
.

To do this, assume that we have constructed both systems up to generation
n
and consider a collection
\{U^{(j)},U_{1}^{(j)},U_{2}^{(j)},\,j\geq 1\}
of
{\rm Unif}([0,1])
random variables independent between them and independent from the evolution of both systems up to generation
n
. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
set

p_{i}\coloneqq{\bf{\overline{p}}}({Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,i},\hat{\mu}_{M}(\mathbf{Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n}))\quad\textrm{and}\quad q_{i}\coloneqq{\bf{\overline{p}}}({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,i},\mu_{n,i}),

where
\mu_{n,i}
is the the law of
{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,i}
. Further, if
p_{i}\leq q_{i}
, we set

\displaystyle{Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n+1,i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{\inf}_{p_{i},q_{i},\delta}({Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,i},U^{(i)},U^{(i)}_{1},U^{(i)}_{2}),

\displaystyle{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n+1,i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{\sup}_{q_{i},\delta}({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,i},U^{(i)},U^{(i)}_{1}),

otherwise, we set

\displaystyle{Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n+1,i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{\sup}_{p_{i},\delta}({Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,i},U^{(i)},U^{(i)}_{1}),

\displaystyle{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n+1,i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{\inf}_{q_{i},p_{i},\delta}({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,i},U^{(i)},U^{(i)}_{1},U^{(i)}_{2}).

According to Lemma
3.1
,
({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,i})_{n\geq 0}
is, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
, a non-linear chain driven by
\mathbb{Q}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}
. Moreover,
({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,1})_{n\geq 0},\ldots,({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,M})_{n\geq 0}
are independent. It also follows from Lemma
3.1
that the system
\mathbf{Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}
has the desired distribution and that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|{Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n+1,1}-{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n+1,1}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(1-\dfrac{\delta}{2}\bigg)\mathbb{E}\left[|{Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,1}-{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,1}|\right]

\displaystyle+\dfrac{\delta}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[|{\bf{\overline{p}}}({Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,1},\hat{\mu}_{M}(\mathbf{Z}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n}))-{\bf{\overline{p}}}({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta,{\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,1},\mu_{n,1})|\right].

(6.2)

6.2.
Propagation of chaos I

The aim of this section is to provide a bound for the distance between the law, at generation
n
, of the state of a given particle in the pathogen-host system and the law of an appropriate non-linear chain. Since in this section the parameter
\delta
is fixed, we will drop it from the notation.

A important ingredient in the proof is the following result, which is a direct consequence of
[
18
, Thm.1, p.709]
. It provides non-asymptotic estimates for the convergence in Wasserstein distance of the empirical laws (and thus, can be regarded as a quantitative version of Glivenko-Cantelli’s theorem ). Similar results for the Wasserstein-2 distance in different dimensions can be found in
[
13
]
.

Proposition 6.2
.

There exist a constant
C_{0}>0
such that, for any sequence of i.i.d. random variables
(\xi_{i})_{i\geq 1}
on
[0,1]
with common law
\mu\in\mathcal{P}([0,1])
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Was(\hat{\mu}_{M}(\mathbf{\xi}),\mu)]\leq\frac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{M}},

where
\hat{\mu}_{M}(\mathbf{\xi})\coloneqq M^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\delta_{\xi_{i}}
is the empirical law induced by a sample of size
M
of the sequence
(\xi_{i})_{i\geq 1}
.

Now we proof Theorem
2.10
, which state the chaos propagation property for the discrete time chains, meaning the convergence of the truly Markovian particle system to the non linear Markov chain.

Proof of Theorem
2.10
.

We use the coupling described in Remark
6.1
with the same initial value for both systems; the distribution of the initial value is
\mu_{0}^{\otimes M}
. Let
\mu_{n}
be the distribution of
{\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,1}
.
By definition of the
\Was
-distance, we have

\Was\Big(\Law({Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,1}),\Law({\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n})\Big)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,1}-{\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,1}|\right].

Furthermore, since
{\bf{\overline{p}}}
is
(L_{1},L_{2})
-Lipschitz, Eq. (
6.2
) delivers

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,1}-{\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,1}|\right]\leq

\displaystyle\bigg(1-\dfrac{\delta(1-L_{1})}{2}\bigg)\mathbb{E}\left[|{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n-1,1}-{\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n-1,1}|\right]

\displaystyle+\dfrac{\delta L_{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\Was(\hat{\mu}_{M}(\mathbf{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n-1}),\mu_{n-1})\right].

(6.3)

Note now that

\displaystyle\Was(\hat{\mu}_{M}(\mathbf{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n-1}),\mu_{n-1})\leq\Was(\hat{\mu}_{M}(\mathbf{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n-1}),\hat{\mu}_{M}({\overline{\mathbf{Z}}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},M)}_{n-1})+\Was(\hat{\mu}_{M}({\overline{\mathbf{Z}}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},M)}_{n-1}),\mu_{n-1}).

For the first term, we have

\displaystyle\Was(\hat{\mu}_{M}(\mathbf{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n-1}),\hat{\mu}_{M}({\overline{\mathbf{Z}}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},M)}_{n-1}))

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}|{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n-1,j}-{\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n-1,j}|.

Moreover, the distribution of
|{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n-1,j}-{\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n-1,j}|
is the same for all
j
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Was(\hat{\mu}_{M}(\mathbf{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n-1}),\hat{\mu}_{M}({\overline{\mathbf{Z}}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},M)}_{n-1}))\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n-1,1}-{\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n-1,1}|\right].

Moreover, using the independence of the non-linear chains and Proposition
6.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Was(\hat{\mu}_{M}({\overline{\mathbf{Z}}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},M)}_{n-1}),\mu_{n-1})\right]\leq\dfrac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{M}}.

Plugging the obtained bounds in (
6.3
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,1}-{\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,1}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(1-\dfrac{\delta(1-L_{1}-L_{2})}{2}\bigg)\mathbb{E}\left[|{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n-1,1}-{\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n-1,1}|\right]+\dfrac{\delta L_{2}}{2}\dfrac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{M}}.

Iterating this inequality and using that both systems start with the same initial value we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,1}-{\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,1}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{L_{2}}{1-L_{1}-L_{2}}\dfrac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{M}}\left(1-\Bigg(1-\dfrac{\delta(1-L_{1}-L_{2})}{2}\Bigg)^{n}\right).

(6.4)

The result follows since
L_{1}+L_{2}<1
.
∎

Remark 6.3

(On the
(L_{1},L_{2})
-Lipschitz condition)
.

The condition
L_{1}+L_{2}<1
is only used in the proof of Theorem
2.10
in the iteration step leading to Eq. (
6.4
). If
L_{1}+L_{2}=1
, the iteration steps yields

\mathbb{E}\left[|{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,1}-{\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,1}|\right]\leq\frac{\delta L_{2}}{2}\frac{C_{0}\,n}{\sqrt{M}}.

Similarly, if
L_{1}+L_{2}>1
, the iteration step yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,1}-{\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n,1}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{L_{2}}{L_{1}+L_{2}-1}\dfrac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{M}}\left(\Bigg(1+\dfrac{\delta(L_{1}+L_{2}-1)}{2}\Bigg)^{n}-1\right).

Hence, we obtain linear exponential bounds in
n
for the corresponding
\Was
-distance. This contrasts with the uniform bound obtained in Theorem
2.10
under the assumption
L_{1}+L_{2}<1
.

6.3.
Propagation of chaos II: scaled processes

In this section we prove Theorem
2.11
which is in spirit analogous to Theorem
2.10
, but for duly scaled processes in continuous time. Let us recall that, we associate to each
N\in\mathbb{N}
a measurable function
{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}:[0,1]\times\mathcal{P}\to[0,1]
and we consider a strictly increasing function
M:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
. For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the host-pathogen system with
M(N)
hosts described by the Markov chain
\mathbf{Z}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N},{M(N)})}
with
\delta_{N}\coloneqq\sqrt{3/N}
. Similarly, for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the non-linear chain
{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}
driven by
\mathbb{Q}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}
. The scaled processes
\mathbb{X}^{(N)}
and
{\overline{X}}^{(N)}
were defined (
2.9

\mathbb{X}^{(N)}_{t}=\mathbf{Z}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N},{M(N)})}_{\lfloor Nt\rfloor}

\quad{\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{t}={\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{\lfloor Nt\rfloor},\,t\geq 0

5.3

Proof of Theorem
2.11
.

Thanks to the assumption on
{\bf{\overline{b}}}
,
{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}
is
(1+2L_{1}/\sqrt{3N},2L_{2}/\sqrt{3N})

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|{Z}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}},{M})}_{n,1}-{\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{n}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{C_{0}L_{2}}{\sqrt{M(N)}}\dfrac{\Big(1+\frac{L_{1}+L_{2}}{N}\Big)^{n}-1}{L_{1}+L_{2}},

and the result follows choosing

.
∎

In the next uniform-in-time propagation of chaos result, we remove the condition imposed in Theorem
2.11
about the particular form of
{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}
. Instead, we impose a stronger Lipschitz condition on
{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}
and an additional condition on the function
M
.

Proposition 6.4
.

Assume that for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
the function
{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}
is
(L_{1}(N),L_{2}(N))
-Lipschitz for some
L_{1}(N),L_{2}(N)>0
with
L_{1}(N)+L_{2}(N)<1
. Let
\mu_{0}^{(N)}
be the law of
{\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{0}
and assume that
\mathbb{X}^{(N)}_{0}\sim(\mu_{0}^{(N)})^{\otimes M(N)}
. Then For
N
sufficiently large, we have

\displaystyle\Was(\Law(\mathbb{X}^{(N)}_{t,1}),\Law({\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{t}))\leq C_{0}\dfrac{L_{2}(N)}{1-L_{1}(N)-L_{2}(N)}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{M(N)}}.

In particular, under the additional assumption that

\displaystyle M(N)\gg\left(\dfrac{L_{2}(N)}{1-L_{1}(N)-L_{2}(N)}\right)^{2},

we have

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sup_{t\geq 0}\Was(\Law(\mathbb{X}^{(N)}_{t,1}),\Law({\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{t}))=0.

Proof of Proposition
6.4
.

The second claim follows directly from the first one. The first one follows using (
6.4
) with
L_{1}=L_{1}(N)
,
L_{2}=L_{2}(N)
,
M=M(N)
and

.
∎

7.
Self-stabilizing Wright–Fisher populations with mutation: a case study.

Let us recall the McKean–Vlasov SDE from Section
2.2

\displaystyle{\rm d}\overline{X}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(-\theta_{1}\overline{X}_{t}+\theta_{0}(1-\overline{X}_{t})-\gamma(\overline{X}_{t}-\mathbb{E}[\overline{X}_{t}])\right){\rm d}t+\sqrt{\overline{X}_{t}(1-\overline{X}_{t})}{\rm d}W_{t},

(
2.10
)

\displaystyle{\overline{X}}_{0}

\displaystyle\sim\overline{\mu}_{0}.

Here
\overline{X}_{t}
can be interpreted as the fraction of the population of type
0
at time
t
. The population is subject to mutations at rates
\theta_{0}>0,\theta_{1}>0
, but also to a
pushing towards the mean
effect modulated by
\gamma\in(0,1)
. The existence and uniqueness of solutions for (
2.10
) is not covered by the results in Section
B
, but follow from
[
25
, Theorem 3.1]
.

We will now prove Theorem
2.12
about the ergodic properties of the process
\overline{X}
using the tools developed in the previous sections.

Proof of Theorem
2.12
.

The proof consists of four main steps.

Step one - Ergodicity of the approximating non-linear chains:

Let us consider

\delta_{N}\coloneqq\sqrt{3/N}
and

\displaystyle{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}(x,\mu)

\displaystyle\coloneqq x+\frac{2}{\sqrt{3N}}\left(-\theta_{1}x+\theta_{0}(1-x)-\gamma(x-\langle\mu\rangle_{1})\right),\quad x\in[0,1].

(7.1)

On can show that
{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}(x)\in[0,1]
for all
(x,\mu)
as long as
N\geq 4(\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}+\gamma)^{2}/3
. Hence the non-linear Markov chain
{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}
is well defined.
Moreover, a straightforward computation shows that

\displaystyle|{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}(x,\mu)-{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}(y,\nu)|\leq\left(1-\frac{2(\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}+\gamma)}{\sqrt{3N}}\right)|x-y|+\frac{2\gamma}{\sqrt{3N}}\Was(\mu,\nu).

(7.2)

In other words,
{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}
is
(L_{1}(N),L_{2}(N))
-Lipschitz with
L_{1}(N)=1-2((\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}+\gamma)/\sqrt{3N}
and
L_{2}(N)=2\gamma/\sqrt{3N}
. In particular, we have

L_{1}(N)+L_{2}(N)=1-\frac{2(\theta_{1}+\theta_{0})}{\sqrt{3N}}<1\;\quad\text{for all }N\geq 4(\theta_{0}+\theta_{1})^{2}/3.

Thus, thanks to Theorem
2.7
, the non-linear Markov chain
{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}
admits a unique invariant distribution
\eta^{(N)}
and

\displaystyle\Was(\Law({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{n}),\eta^{(N)})\leq\Bigg(1-\dfrac{\theta_{1}+\theta_{0}}{N}\Bigg)^{n}\Was(\Law({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{0}),\eta^{(N)}).

(7.3)

Step two - Convergence of the sequence of invariant distributions:

A general observation about non-linear Wright–Fisher chains is needed. If
\eta
is the stationary distribution of
{\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}
, then the first moment of
\eta
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\langle\eta\rangle_{1}=\mathbb{E}_{\eta}[{\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{1}]=\mathbb{E}_{\eta}[{\bf{\overline{p}}}({\overline{Z}}^{({\bf{\overline{p}}})}_{0},\eta)].

(7.4)

In our case, this yields

\displaystyle\langle\eta^{(N)}\rangle_{1}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\theta_{0}}{\theta_{1}+\theta_{0}}\eqqcolon m.

Thus, defining

{\bf p}_{\delta_{N}}(x)\coloneqq x+\frac{2\delta_{N}}{3}\left(\bar{\theta}_{0}(1-x)+\bar{\theta_{1}}x\right),

where
\bar{\theta}_{0}=\theta_{0}(1+\gamma/(\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}))
and
\bar{\theta}_{1}=\theta_{1}(1+\gamma/(\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}))
, a straightforward computation shows that
{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N}(x,\eta^{(N)})={\bf p}_{\delta_{N}}(x)
. Consequently, the invariant measure of the non-linear chain
{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}
coincides with the invariant measure of the Markov chain
Z^{(\delta_{n},{\bf p}_{\delta_{N}})}
. Therefore, according to Remark
4.13
, the sequence
(\eta^{(N)})_{N\geq 1}
converges weakly to the
{\rm Beta}(2\bar{\theta}_{0},2\bar{\theta}_{1})
distribution.

Step three - Existence and uniqueness of an invariant distribution for
\overline{X}
:

It is well-known that the Wright–Fisher diffusion with mutation

\displaystyle{\rm d}X_{t}=\left(-\theta_{1}X_{t}+\theta_{0}(1-X_{t})\right){\rm d}t+\sqrt{X_{t}(1-X_{t})}{\rm d}W_{t},

has a unique invariant distribution
{\rm Beta}(2\theta_{0},2\theta_{1})
(see e.g.
[
16
]
). Now, fix a
\Was
-Lipschitz function
h:\mathcal{P}([0,1])\mapsto[0,1]
and consider the family of SDE parameterized by
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(0,1)
:

\displaystyle{\rm d}X^{(\mu)}_{t}=

\displaystyle\left(-(\theta_{1}+\gamma(1-h(\mu)))X^{(\mu)}_{t}+(\theta_{0}+\gamma h(\mu))(1-X^{(\mu)}_{t})\right){\rm d}t

(7.5)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{X^{(\mu)}_{t}(1-X^{(\mu)}_{t})}{\rm d}W_{t}.

Thus, according to the previous discussion,
X^{(\mu)}
has a unique invariant distribution
T\mu
, which is given by the
{\rm Beta}(2(\theta_{0}+\gamma h(\mu)),2(\theta_{1}+\gamma(1-h(\mu))))
distribution. Using Proposition
A.2
, it can be shown that

\displaystyle\textbf{Lip}(T)\leq\dfrac{\gamma\textbf{Lip}(h)}{\theta_{1}+\theta_{0}+\gamma}.

As a consequence of this result (and a fixed point argument that mimics the proof of theorem
5.4
), for every
\gamma\geq 0
, the McKean–Vlasov Wright–Fisher SDE (
2.10
) admits a unique invariant distribution which is the
{\rm Beta}\left(2\theta_{0}(1+\gamma/(\theta_{1}+\theta_{0})),2\theta_{1}(1+\gamma/(\theta_{1}+\theta_{0}))\right)
distribution.

Step four - Interpolation:

For
N\geq N_{0}\coloneqq 4(\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}+\gamma)^{2}/3
, we set
{\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{t}\coloneqq{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{\lfloor Nt\rfloor}
, for
t\geq 0
. Assume that
{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{0}=\overline{X}_{0}
. Set
\eta^{(N)}_{t}=\Law({\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{t})
and
\eta_{t}=\Law(\overline{X}_{t})
. Fix
t\geq 0
and
\varepsilon>0
. We have

\displaystyle\Was(\eta_{t},\eta)\leq\Was(\eta_{t},\eta^{(N)}_{t})+\Was(\eta^{(N)}_{t},\eta^{(N)})+\Was(\eta^{(N)},\eta).

We claim now that, by Theorem
2.9
, there exists
N_{1}\geq N_{0}
such that for every
N\geq N_{1}
the first term is smaller than
\varepsilon/4
(the
N_{1}
may depend on
t
). Indeed, for any
T\geq t
, we have proved above, that in the Skorokhod space the sequence of laws
P^{(N)}
of the processes
({\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{[0,T]})
converges weakly to the law
P
of the process
\overline{X}_{[0,T]}
. Since the projection map
\pi_{t}:\mathbb{D}([0,T];[0,1])\mapsto[0,1]
is
P

P^{(N)}\circ\pi_{t}^{-1}\to P\circ\pi_{t}^{-1}
weakly. Since
\Was
metrizes the weak convergence on
[0,1]
, the claim follows.

Now, by Step two, there exists
N_{2}\geq N_{1}
such that for every
N\geq N_{2}
the third term above is smaller than
\varepsilon/4
. As for the second term, in virtue of the estimate (

), for some
N_{3}\geq N_{2}
and for every

:

\displaystyle\Was(\eta^{(N)}_{t},\eta^{(N)})

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-(\theta_1+\theta_0)t)\Was(\eta^{(N)}_{0},\eta^{(N)})+\varepsilon/4

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-(\theta_1+\theta_0)t)\Was(\eta_{0},\eta)+2\varepsilon/4.

The result follows since

∎

Remark 7.1
.

Observe that the mean of the invariant measure is
m\coloneqq\theta_{0}/(\theta_{1}+\theta_{0})
, and thus it is independent of
\gamma
. Moreover, since the second moment of the invariant distribution equals

m\dfrac{2(\theta_{0}+\gamma m)+1}{2(\theta_{1}+\theta_{0}+\gamma)+1},

we conclude that the variance of the invariant distribution of the McKean–Vlasov Wright–Fisher SDE is decreasing in
\gamma\geq 0
. In particular, the non-linearity has, as the title of this section suggests, a stabilizing effect . See Fig.
2
for an illustration of the density of the invariant measure for different values of
\gamma
.

We close this section with the proof of Theorem

, which states that any individual particle of the Markovian system
\mathbb{X}^{(N)}
approximates the dynamics of the McKean–Vlasov Wright–Fisher diffusion (

).

Proof of Theorem
2.13
.

Note first that

\displaystyle\Was(\Law((\mathbb{X}^{(N)}_{t,1})),\Law(\overline{X}_{t}))\leq

\displaystyle\Was(\Law((\mathbb{X}^{(N)}_{t,1})),\Law({\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{t}))

\displaystyle+\Was(\Law({\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{t}),\Law(\overline{X}_{t})).

Hence, Proposition
6.4
delivers

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{t\geq 0}\Was(\Law((\mathbb{X}^{(N)}_{t,1})),\Law(\overline{X}_{t}))\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{t\geq 0}\Was(\Law({\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{t}),\Law(\overline{X}_{t})).

We claim now that for every
T>0
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Was(\Law({\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{t}),\Law(\overline{X}_{t}))=0.

Assume the claim is true. Clearly, for every
T\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq T}\Was(\Law({\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{t}),\Law(\overline{X}_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq\Was(\eta^{(N)},\eta)+\sup_{t\geq T}\Was(\Law({\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{t}),\eta^{(N)})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sup_{t\geq T}\Was(\eta,\overline{X}_{t}),

According to Remark
4.13
the first term in the right-hand side converges to
0
as
N\to\infty
. Moreover, thanks to (
7.3
) the second term can be made arbitrarily small by taking
T
sufficiently large. Similarly, by Proposition
2.12
we can make the last term arbitrarily small for
T
sufficiently large. Combining this with the claim yields the result. It remains to prove the claim.

Let
V^{(N)}_{t}
be the piecewise-linear, continuous process given by:

\displaystyle V^{(N)}_{t}={\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{\lfloor Nt\rfloor}+(Nt-\lfloor Nt\rfloor)({\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{\lfloor Nt\rfloor+1}-{\overline{Z}}^{(\delta_{N},{\bf{\overline{p}}}_{N})}_{\lfloor Nt\rfloor});\quad(t\geq 0).

Note that

for every

. In particular,

\displaystyle\Was(\Law(V^{(N)}_{t}),\Law({\overline{X}}^{(N)}_{t}))\leq N^{-1/2}.

(7.6)

Now, since the laws of the sequence
({\overline{X}}^{(N)})_{N\geq 1}
converges weakly to the law of
\overline{X}
, the same is true for the law of the processes
(V^{(N)})_{N\geq 1}
(see for example
[
17
]
, Proposition 10.4, page 149), and moreover the convergence holds as probability measures on
\mathcal{C}([0,T];[0,1])
(see
[
17
]
, Problem 25 at page 153). This space is Polish under the uniform norm, and of bounded diameter; so the Wasserstein distance
\Was(T;\cdot,\cdot)
at display (
B.2
) metrizes the weak convergence on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}([0,T];[0,1]))
(see
[

]
, theorem 6.9, page 108). Since:

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Was(\Law(V^{(N)}_{t}),\Law(\overline{X}_{t}))\leq\Was(T;\Law(V^{(N)}_{[0,T]}),\Law(\overline{X}_{[0,T]})),

the left-hand goes to

as

. This, together with (

), shows the claim.
∎

8.
Closing remarks and future research

In this article we introduced a unified framework that recovers several
well-known models from population genetics and, in some cases,
reveals new moment dualities. We further extended our analysis to the
non-classical setting of non-linear Markov chains and showed that,
under suitable scaling, their dynamics converge to McKean–Vlasov
diffusions.

To illustrate the utility of our approach, we studied in detail
non-linear Markov chains associated with Wright–Fisher models whose
mutation rates depend on the empirical measure of the population, as
well as the limiting McKean–Vlasov diffusion.

The present framework raises several directions for further research.
A natural extension is to incorporate interactions at the
meta-community scale, thereby yielding a three-level model (see the
discussion and motivation in the Introduction). While the modeling
itself is straightforward within our setup, a full mathematical
analysis comparable to that carried out at the host and deme levels
presents new challenges; this is the topic of ongoing work.

The duality results in Section
4

(Theorem
2.2
) were established in a purely
algebraic manner. In many classical models of population genetics,
moment duality reflects a coupling between forward and backward
dynamics via time reversal, often supported by a particle
representation of the forward process. In our situation, although a
particle picture exists for the backward process, its relation to the
forward dynamics remains unclear. Constructing such a coupling is part of our current research efforts.

Finally, a more conceptual challenge is to understand how the notion of
duality employed here may extend to non-linear Markov processes.
Although we are unable to provide concrete solutions at this stage, we
believe this to be a promising direction for future investigation.

\frac{{\rm d}\wedge}{{\rm d}z}(z)=\left(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\right)1_{[0,1-\delta)}(z)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{{\rm d}\vee}{{\rm d}z}(z)=\left(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\right)1_{(\delta,1]}(z),

and also:

1-\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{1}{\delta}\frac{z}{\wedge}=\frac{1}{\delta}(x-(1-\delta))1_{[1-\delta,1]}(z)\quad\text{and}\quad 1-\frac{1}{\delta}-\frac{1}{\delta}\frac{z-1}{1-\vee}=-\frac{1}{\delta}(x-\delta)1_{[0,\delta]}(z).

Consequently, the terms vanish at line (
A.5
). With this, we have verified the case
\delta\in(0,1)
. The case
\delta=1
is analogous.
∎

A.3.
Wasserstein distance between Beta distributions

The following result is used once in Section
7
. It should be standard, but we were unable to find a proof in the literature.

Proposition A.2
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2}
,
b_{1},b_{2}
be positive numbers such that
a_{1}+a_{2}=b_{1}+b_{2}=M
for some positive constant
M
. Then:

\displaystyle\Was({\rm Beta}(a_{1},a_{2}),{\rm Beta}(b_{1},b_{2}))=\dfrac{1}{M}|a_{1}-b_{1}|.

Proof.

Since the family of
{\rm Beta}
distributions is weakly continuous with respect to the parameters, it suffices to show the result for rational numbers
a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}
. So, let
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a_{i}=r_{i}/N,b_{i}=s_{i}/N
. We have
M=(r_{1}+r_{2})/N=(s_{1}+s_{2})/N
. Let
L\coloneqq r_{1}+r_{2}
, and consider an i.i.d. family
(Z_{i})_{i=1}^{L}
of random variables with common law
{\rm Gamma}(1/N,1)
. Then,
Z\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{L}Z_{i}\sim{\rm Gamma}(M,1)
and consequently
X\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{r_{1}}Z_{j}/Z\sim{\rm Beta}(a_{1},a_{2})
,
Y\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{s_{1}}Z_{j}/Z\sim{\rm Beta}(b_{1},b_{2})
. Assuming
s_{1}\geq r_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X-Y|]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\dfrac{1}{Z}\sum_{j=r_{1}+1}^{s_{1}}Z_{i}\right]=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[{\rm Beta}\left(\dfrac{s_{1}-r_{1}}{N},\dfrac{L-(s_{1}-r_{1})}{N}\right)\Bigg]=\dfrac{s_{1}-r_{1}}{NM}=\dfrac{b_{1}-a_{1}}{M}.

Since
|\mathbb{E}[X]-\mathbb{E}[Y]|=(a_{1}-b_{1})/M
, the claim follows.
∎

Appendix B
Existence and uniqueness of solutions for the McKean–Vlasov Wright–Fisher (MVWF) equation

In this Appendix we focus on simple conditions that guarantee that the MVWF equation:

\displaystyle{\rm d}{{\overline{X}}}_{t}={\bf{\overline{b}}}({{\overline{X}}}_{t},{\overline{\mu}}_{t}){\rm d}t+\sqrt{{{\overline{X}}}_{t}(1-{{\overline{X}}}_{t})}\,{\rm d}W_{t},\quad{\mu}_{t}=\Law({\overline{X}}_{t}),\quad{{\overline{X}}}_{0}\sim\overline{\mu}_{0},

(B.1)

admits a unique weak solution on
[0,+\infty)
(see definition
5.8
). The Assumption
B.1
below will be enough for MVWF models with selection; under this assumption, the existence/uniqueness problem for the McKean–Vlasov equation will be addressed via an absolutely-continuous change of measure. The situation for the non-linear Wright–Fisher diffusion with pure mutation (which is necessary in order to work out the case study at Section
7
) is somewhat different, and we will say some words about this case when necessary.

Fix
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
. For
t\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{P}_{t}\coloneqq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}([0,t];[0,1]))
be the space of probability measures on the space
\mathcal{C}([0,t];[0,1])
endowed with the Borel
\sigma
-field associated to the topology of the supremum norm such that the image measure under the coordinate map
(x_{s})_{s\in[0,t]}\mapsto x_{0}
equals
\mu_{0}
. We turn
\mathcal{P}_{t}
into a metric space through the associated Wasserstein distance:

\displaystyle\Was(t;\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi}\Bigg(\int_{\mathcal{C}([0,t];[0,1])^{2}}\bigg(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|x_{s}-y_{s}|\bigg)\pi({\rm d}x{\rm d}y)\Bigg),

(B.2)

where the infimum is taken over all the couplings
\pi
of
(\mu,\nu)
. Observe that whenever
T\geq t
, the space
\mathcal{P}_{t}
can be embedded into
\mathcal{P}_{T}
and furthermore:

\displaystyle\Was(t;\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi}\Bigg(\int_{\mathcal{C}([0,T];[0,1])^{2}}\bigg(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|x_{s}-y_{s}|\bigg)\pi({\rm d}x{\rm d}y)\Bigg),

where now the infimum is taken over all the measures on
\mathcal{C}([0,T];[0,1])^{2}
such that the restriction to
\mathcal{C}([0,t];[0,1])^{2}
has marginals
\mu
and
\nu
.

Recall that we are working under the assumption
2.8
. Let
a(x)=\sqrt{x(1-x)}
. Consider the following condition.

Assumption B.1
.

For every
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}([0,+\infty);[0,1]))
, and any
x\in[0,1]
:

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{b^{(\mu)}(x,t)-b^{(\nu)}(x,t)}{a(x)}\right|\leq C\Was(\mu_{t},\nu_{t}),

where we used the notation
b^{(\mu)}(x,t)={\bf{\overline{b}}}(x,\mu_{t})
.

Theorem B.2
.

For every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{t}
define
\Phi_{t}(\mu)\coloneqq\Law_{\mathbb{P}}(X^{(\mu)}_{[0,t]})\in\mathcal{P}_{t}
. Under Assumption
B.1
, the map
\mu\mapsto\Phi_{t}(\mu)
admits a unique fixed point. In particular, Eq. (
MVWF
) admits a unique weak solution on
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

On a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{s}),\mathbb{P})
, let
X^{(\mu)}
and
X^{(\nu)}
be strong solutions to equation (
5.7
) on
[0,t]
for
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{t}
, respectively, driven by the same Brownian motion
W
and with identical initial distribution
\mu_{0}
. Consider the adapted process
(U_{s}:s\in[0,t])
given by:

\displaystyle U_{s}=\exp\{-\int_{0}^{s}\dfrac{b^{\nu}(X^{(\nu)}_{r},r)-b^{\mu}(X^{(\nu)}_{r},r)}{a(X^{(\nu)}_{r})}{\rm d}W_{r}-\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{s}\left(\dfrac{b^{\nu}(X^{(\nu)}_{r},r)-b^{\mu}(X^{(\nu)}_{r},r)}{a(X^{(\nu)}_{r})}\right)^{2}{\rm d}r\Bigg\}.

Assumption
B.1
ensures that
U
is a
\mathcal{F}_{s}
-martingale . Let
\mathbb{Q}
be the probability measure defined on
\mathcal{F}_{t}
through
{\rm d}\mathbb{Q}/{\rm d}\mathbb{P}=U_{t}
, and for
s\leq t
write
\mathbb{Q}_{s}
for its restriction to
\mathcal{F}_{s}
. Note that applying Girsanov’s theorem (see
[
26
, Chap 4.]
,
[
39
, Sec. 6.3]
and
[
40
, Sec. 17.2,17.3]
), it follows that

\Law_{\mathbb{Q}}(X^{(\nu)}_{[0,t]})=\Law_{\mathbb{P}}(X^{(\mu)}_{[0,t]}).

So, applying (
2.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Was(t;\Phi_{t}(\mu),\Phi_{t}(\nu))

\displaystyle\leq d_{TV}(\Phi_{t}(\mu),\,\Phi_{t}(\nu))=d_{TV}(\Law_{\mathbb{P}}(X^{(\mu)}_{[0,t]}),\,\Law_{\mathbb{P}}(X^{(\nu)}_{[0,t]}))

\displaystyle=d_{TV}(\Law_{\mathbb{Q}}(X^{(\nu)}_{[0,t]}),\,\Law_{\mathbb{P}}(X^{(\nu)}_{[0,t]}))\leq d_{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{t},\mathbb{Q}_{t}),

where in the last inequality we use that the total variation distance between the pushforward measures is dominated by the total variation distance of the original measures. Then by Pinsker’s inequality (see e.g.
[
45
]
, Lemma 2.5, at page 88), the last term in the right-hand side of the last inequality is smaller than the relative entropy between
\mathbb{P}_{t}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
. Hence, for
t\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\Was(t;\Phi_{t}(\mu),\Phi_{t}(\nu))

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\log\dfrac{d\mathbb{Q}_{t}}{d\mathbb{P}_{t}}\right]}=\sqrt{2\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\log(U_t)\right]}.

Applying Itô’s lemma, Assumption
B.1
yields

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\log(U_t)\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\Was^{2}(\mu_{s},\nu_{s}){\rm d}s,

from where it follows

\displaystyle\Was^{2}(t;\Phi_{t}(\mu),\Phi_{t}(\nu))

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\Was^{2}(s;\mu,\nu){\rm d}s.

(B.3)

Fix
\mu^{(0;t)}\in\mathcal{P}_{t}
, and for
n\geq 0
define
\mu^{(n+1;t)}\coloneqq\Phi_{t}(\mu^{(n;t)})
. Then, iterating the above relation we obtain:

\displaystyle\Was^{2}(t;\mu^{(n+1;t)},\mu^{(n;t)})\leq\dfrac{C^{n}t^{n}}{n!}\Was^{2}(t;\mu^{(1;t)},\mu^{(0;t)}).

Consequently, for every
t\geq 0
, the sequence
(\mu^{(n;t)})_{n\geq 1}
is a Cauchy sequence on
\mathcal{P}_{t}
, and hence convergent to a limit
\mu^{(\infty;t)}
, say. Notice that from (
B.3
), we have

\displaystyle\Was^{2}(t;\Phi_{t}(\mu^{(\infty;t)}),\mu^{(n+1;t)})

\displaystyle\leq Ct\Was^{2}(t;\mu^{(\infty;t)},\mu^{(n;t)}),

taking limit
n\to\infty
, we obtain that
\mu^{(\infty;t)}
is a fixed point for
\Phi_{t}
. Finally, if
\mu_{1}
,
\mu_{2}
are fixed points for
\Phi_{t}
, (
B.3
) yields

\Was^{2}(t;\mu_{1},\mu_{2}))\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\Was^{2}(s;\mu_{1},\mu_{2}){\rm d}s,

and from Gronwall’s lemma we conclude
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
.

To prove the second claim, a general remark is in order. Assume that we have a sequence
(\nu_{N})_{N\geq 1}
of probability measures on the metric space
\mathcal{C}([0,t];[0,1])
converging weakly to a probability measure
\nu^{(t)}
. If
(\mathcal{G}_{s})_{s\in[0,t]}
is the canonical filtration, then the sequence of restrictions
(\nu_{N}|_{\mathcal{G}_{s}})_{N\geq 1}
converge weakly to the measure
\nu^{(t)}|_{\mathcal{G}_{s}}
for every
s\leq t
. This remark applies to the above sequences
(\mu^{(n;t)})_{n\geq 1}
, and moreover, the uniqueness just proved yields

\displaystyle\mu^{(\infty;t)}|_{\mathcal{G}_{s}}=\mu^{(\infty;s)}.

Thus, we can unambiguously define a probability measure on
\mathcal{C}([0,+\infty);[0,1])
via:

\displaystyle\mu^{(\infty)}:=\mu^{(\infty;M)}\text{ on }\mathcal{G}_{M},M\geq 1.

Define, finally, on a certain probability space
(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime},(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{t}),\mathbb{P}^{\prime})
carrying a standard Brownian motion
W
the process
{\overline{X}}
as the unique solution of the SDE:

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\overline{X}}_{t}=b^{(\mu^{(\infty)})}({\overline{X}}_{t},t){\rm d}t+\sqrt{{\overline{X}}_{t}(1-{\overline{X}}_{t})}{\rm d}W_{t},\quad{\overline{X}}_{0}\sim\mu_{0}.

By construction, for every
t\geq 0
we have
\Law({\overline{X}}_{t})=\mu^{(\infty)}_{t}
. This proves our claim.
∎
