Mean-Field Analysis and Optimal Control of a Dynamic Rating and Matchmaking System
Wataru Nozawa 1

Abstract
. Large-scale competitive platforms are interacting multi-agent systems in which latent skills drift over time and pairwise interactions are shaped by matchmaking. We study a controlled rating dynamics in the mean-field limit and derive a kinetic description for the joint evolution of skills and ratings. In the Gaussian regime, we prove an exact moment closure and obtain a low-dimensional deterministic state dynamics for rating accuracy. This yields three main insights. First, skill drift imposes an intrinsic ceiling on long-run accuracy (the “Red Queen” effect). Second, with period-by-period scale control, the information content of interactions satisfies an invariance principle: under signal-matched scaling, the one-step accuracy transition is independent of matchmaking intensity. Third, the optimal platform policy separates: filtering is implemented by a greedy choice of the gain and rating scale, while matchmaking reduces to a static trade-off between match utility and sorting costs.

1.
Introduction

Large-scale competitive platforms—online games, crowdsourcing markets, and digital labor exchanges—continuously infer and display
ratings
for a massive population of users whose latent abilities are unobserved and evolve over time.
A central operational challenge is therefore
tracking
: the platform must update ratings quickly enough to follow drifting skills, while simultaneously organizing pairwise interactions (matchmaking) to balance fairness, engagement, and latency.

From a modeling viewpoint, such platforms are naturally described as interacting-agent systems with control acting through both (i)
how signals are processed
(the rating update rule) and (ii)
who interacts with whom
(the interaction kernel).
This leads to a mean-field control problem: the controller does not only estimate the state distribution but steers the population dynamics by choosing the update parameters and the matching assortativity.
While individual-level rating algorithms—most notably Elo
[
13
]
and Bayesian/state-space extensions such as Glicko
[
16
,
17
]
and TrueSkill
[
19
]
—are widely deployed, a rigorous understanding of their
collective
dynamics under
platform design
objectives remains comparatively limited.

This paper develops a kinetic/mean-field framework for a stylized but analytically tractable design problem.
Agents have latent skills that follow a stationary drift process (an AR(1)/discrete OU dynamics with persistence
\lambda
), and the platform observes noisy pairwise outcomes generated by skill differences.
At each period
t
, the platform chooses a control triple

u_{t}=(K_{t},\eta_{t},\sigma_{t}),

where
K_{t}
is the update gain,
\eta_{t}
parametrizes assortativity of the interaction kernel, and
\sigma_{t}\geq 0
is the
rating scale applied in period
t
(implemented operationally by a deterministic normalization of the cross-sectional rating dispersion).
This “period-by-period scale control” directly matches the way the main optimal-scaling policy is stated and implemented in the numerics (e.g.,
\sigma_{t}=r_{t}
).

Our first step is to connect the microscopic particle system to a macroscopic kinetic description.
We prove a mean-field limit and obtain a McKean–Vlasov evolution for the joint law of skill and rating.
Under Gaussian initial conditions and linear update rules, the Gaussian family is invariant and the mean-field dynamics admit an
exact finite-dimensional reduction
: the evolution closes on second moments, yielding a one-dimensional deterministic state variable capturing rating accuracy (the correlation
r_{t}=\mathrm{Corr}(\rho_{t},X_{t})
).

This tractability makes it possible to solve the platform-design problem analytically and reveals a sharp structural message.
Once the platform uses the scale control
\sigma_{t}
to properly normalize the rating dispersion, the information content of each match becomes
invariant
to how assortative matching is.
Consequently, filtering (choosing
(K_{t},\sigma_{t})
to maximize one-step accuracy) and matchmaking (choosing
\eta_{t}
to maximize instantaneous utility net of sorting costs) separate.
In the presence of skill drift, we also characterize an entropic ceiling: even under optimal filtering, the long-run accuracy is strictly bounded away from one (the “Red Queen” effect), and optimal matchmaking exhibits a cost-driven transition between a disordered regime (near-random matching) and an ordered regime (strict sorting).

All proofs and detailed derivations are provided in the Appendix.
In particular, an
optional
continuous-time diffusion approximation is relegated to Appendix
A.2
; it is not used in the discrete-time control results nor in the numerical experiments.

The platform controls both the update rule and the interaction kernel via

2.
Kinetic Modeling and Mean-Field Dynamics

We consider a large system of
N
competing agents. The state of the system at time
t\in\mathbb{N}
is described by the collection of microscopic states
\{(\rho_{i,t},X_{i,t})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where
\rho_{i,t}\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the latent skill and
X_{i,t}\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the observable rating of agent
i
.
For notational convenience, we normalize the initial ratings to zero, i.e.,
X_{i,0}=0
for all
i
.
This makes the initial rating distribution degenerate, so that
\sigma_{0}=0
and the correlation-based accuracy
r_{t}=\mathrm{Corr}(\rho_{t},X_{t})
is not defined at
t=0
; we set
r_{0}:=0
by convention and apply the correlation-based state description from the first non-degenerate step (typically
t\geq 1
).
Our goal is to derive the macroscopic laws governing the evolution of the joint probability density
f_{t}(\rho,x)
in the mean-field limit
N\to\infty
, establishing the dynamic constraints for the control problem in Section
3
.

2.1.
Microscopic Particle Dynamics

The particle system is driven by two mechanisms: intrinsic skill drift and binary interactions organized by the platform.
Throughout,
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
indexes agents,
\rho_{i,t}
denotes latent skill, and
X_{i,t}
denotes the observable rating.

Intrinsic Skill Dynamics (discrete-time OU).

Latent skill follows a discrete-time Ornstein–Uhlenbeck dynamics:

(1)

\rho_{i,t+1}=\lambda\rho_{i,t}+\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\,\xi_{i,t},\qquad\xi_{i,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),

where
\lambda\in[0,1]
is the persistence parameter. This ensures the stationary skill distribution is standard Gaussian

\mathcal{M}(\rho)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-\rho^{2}/2}
.

Interaction Mechanism (scaling, matching, and update).

The platform specifies three sequences

\{K_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\subset(0,\infty),\qquad\{\sigma_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\subset(0,\infty),\qquad\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\subset[0,1),

which jointly determine the rating dispersion, the matchmaking intensity, and the rating update rule.
Given the cross-sectional rating vector at the beginning of period
t
, the period-
t
interaction proceeds in three steps.

Step 1 (dispersion scaling).

At the beginning of each period
t
, the platform rescales the ratings to achieve the target dispersion
\sigma_{t}^{2}
.
Concretely, given pre-scaled ratings
\widetilde{X}_{i,t}
(coming from the previous update), define

(2)

X_{i,t}=L_{t}\,\widetilde{X}_{i,t},\qquad L_{t}:=\frac{\sigma_{t}}{\Lambda_{t}},\qquad\Lambda_{t}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}(\widetilde{X}_{i,t}),

so that
\mathrm{Var}(X_{i,t})=\sigma_{t}^{2}
.
1
1

1

One may additionally recenter by subtracting the cross-sectional mean; this does not affect the correlation-based state variable used below.

Step 2 (correlated matching with intensity
\eta_{t}
).

Using the scaled ratings
\{X_{i,t}\}
, the platform forms pairs so that matched ratings are positively correlated with strength
\eta_{t}\in[0,1)
.
The matching procedure is implemented in a finite population as follows.

At time
t
, the population is randomly split into two groups of equal size (Group 1 and Group 2), each preserving the marginal distribution of
X_{t}
.
For each player
i
in Group 1, construct a
matching score

(3)

Y_{i,t}:=\eta_{t}X_{i,t}+\sqrt{1-\eta_{t}^{2}}\,v_{i,t},\qquad v_{i,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{t}^{2})\ \text{independent}.

Then sort Group 1 by the scores
\{Y_{i,t}\}
and sort Group 2 by ratings
\{X_{j,t}\}
, and match players by rank (the
k
-th in the
Y
-ordering is paired with the
k
-th in the
X
-ordering).
Equivalently, each player
i
in Group 1 is matched to a player
j
in Group 2 whose rating
X_{j,t}
is closest (in quantile rank) to the target value
Y_{i,t}
.

In the Gaussian regime considered below,
X_{i,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{t}^{2})
and
v_{i,t}
is independent with the same variance, so the score
Y_{i,t}
has the same marginal distribution:

Y_{i,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{t}^{2}).

Hence rank matching is well-defined and produces a coupling of
(X_{i,t},X_{j,t})
that approximates the idealized mean-field coupling
(X_{i,t},Y_{i,t})
.
In particular, the induced correlation satisfies

(4)

\mathrm{Corr}(X_{i,t},X_{j,t})\approx\mathrm{Corr}(X_{i,t},Y_{i,t})=\eta_{t},

with the approximation error vanishing as
N\to\infty
(the mean-field limit).
2
2

2

In a finite population, exact equality
X_{j,t}=Y_{i,t}
is typically impossible; rank (quantile) matching provides a canonical discretization that converges to the continuum coupling.

When
\eta_{t}=0
, the score is independent of
X_{i,t}
and matching is effectively random; as
\eta_{t}\uparrow 1
, matching becomes increasingly assortative, approaching perfectly like-with-like pairing.

Step 3 (rating update).

Given a matched pair
(i,j)
, the platform observes a noisy outcome

(5)

S_{ij,t}=\rho_{i,t}-\rho_{j,t}+\omega_{ij,t},\qquad\omega_{ij,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\beta^{2}),

and updates
pre-scaled
next-period ratings by the linear rule

(6)

\begin{cases}\widetilde{X}_{i,t+1}=X_{i,t}+K_{t}\Big(S_{ij,t}-(X_{i,t}-X_{j,t})\Big),\\[3.00003pt]
\widetilde{X}_{j,t+1}=X_{j,t}+K_{t}\Big(-S_{ij,t}-(X_{j,t}-X_{i,t})\Big).\end{cases}

The next period starts by applying Step 1 with
\sigma_{t+1}
, i.e.,
X_{t+1}=L_{t+1}\widetilde{X}_{t+1}
, and the procedure repeats.

This formulation makes explicit that
\{\sigma_{t}\}
governs the rating dispersion
period by period
, while
\{\eta_{t}\}
determines the correlation structure of matched ratings and
\{K_{t}\}
controls the strength of the update.
The schematic of this interaction cycle is illustrated in Figure
1
.

Figure 1
.
Schematic of the period-
t
interaction cycle. The platform sequentially applies scaling (
\sigma_{t}
), matching (
\eta_{t}
), and updating (
K_{t}
) to evolve the rating distribution.

The role of scaling in preserving tractability (Gaussian closure and moment dynamics) is formalized in the subsequent mean-field analysis (Theorem
2.3
).

2.2.
The Kinetic Equation

In the mean-field limit
N\to\infty
, the empirical measure converges to a probability density
f_{t}(\rho,x)
. The evolution of
f_{t}
is governed by a Boltzmann-type kinetic equation, involving a transport-diffusion operator
\mathcal{T}
(skill drift) and a collision operator
\mathcal{Q}
(rating update).

Matchmaking Kernel.

For a given matching intensity
\eta_{t}\in[0,1)
, we define the
matching kernel

\mathcal{K}_{\eta_{t}}(x,x_{*})
as the conditional density of an opponent rating
x_{*}
given own rating
x
:

(7)

\mathcal{K}_{\eta_{t}}(x,x_{*})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(1-\eta_{t}^{2})\sigma_{t}^{2}}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x_{*}-\eta_{t}x)^{2}}{2(1-\eta_{t}^{2})\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right),

where
\sigma_{t}^{2}:=\iint x^{2}f_{t}(\rho,x)\,d\rho\,dx=\int x^{2}g_{t}(x)\,dx
is the current rating second moment
(variance under our zero-mean normalization), and
g_{t}(x):=\int f_{t}(d\rho,dx)
denotes the marginal density of ratings.
We interpret
\mathcal{K}_{\eta_{t}}(x,\cdot)
as a
Markov kernel
on ratings: conditional on an agent
with rating
x
, the opponent’s rating
X_{*,t}
is drawn according to
\mathcal{K}_{\eta_{t}}(x,dx_{*})
as in
(
7
).

Degenerate case.
When
\sigma_{t}=0
(in particular, at
t=0
under our normalization
X_{i,0}=0
),
the Gaussian density representation (
7
) is not available. In this case we extend

\mathcal{K}_{\eta_{t}}(x,\cdot)
as a possibly singular Markov kernel by setting

\mathcal{K}_{\eta_{t}}(x,dx_{*})=\delta_{\eta_{t}x}(dx_{*})
, so that
\mathcal{K}_{\eta_{0}}(0,dx_{*})=\delta_{0}(dx_{*})
.
With this extension, the weak formulation (
8
) remains well-defined even when
\sigma_{t}=0
.
Conditional on
X_{*,t}=x_{*}
, the opponent’s latent skill is drawn from the conditional distribution

f_{t}(d\rho_{*}\mid x_{*}):=f_{t}(d\rho_{*},dx_{*})/g_{t}(x_{*})
(whenever
g_{t}(x_{*})>0
).
Under the Gaussian closure,
g_{t}
is centered Gaussian with variance
\sigma_{t}^{2}
, and the kernel in
(
7
) induces
\mathrm{Corr}(X_{t},X_{*,t})=\eta_{t}
while preserving the marginal.

Remark 2.1

(Admissible range of the matching intensity)
.

Throughout the paper we restrict the platform’s sorting intensity to
\eta_{t}\in[0,1)
.
The Gaussian
density
representation (
7
) is non-degenerate only for
|\eta_{t}|<1
.
The extreme choice
\eta_{t}=1
(perfect sorting) is operationally meaningful, and it can be incorporated by extending the kernel as

\mathcal{K}_{1}(x,dx^{\prime})=\delta_{x}(dx^{\prime})
(a Dirac kernel). We nevertheless exclude
\eta_{t}=1
because our analysis relies on density-based expressions and because the sorting cost satisfies
\lim_{\eta\uparrow 1}C(\eta)=+\infty
(Section
3.1
).
Finally, negative values
\eta_{t}\in(-1,0)
correspond to reverse sorting. Such policies run counter to skill-balancing objectives and are not considered by the platform; accordingly we impose
\eta_{t}\geq 0
without loss for the design problem studied here.

Weak Formulation.

The evolution of
f_{t+1}
is defined in weak form: for any test function
\phi(\rho,x)
,

(8)

\displaystyle\int\phi(\rho,x)\,f_{t+1}(d\rho,dx)

\displaystyle=\iint f_{t}(d\rho,dx)\int\mathcal{K}_{\eta_{t}}(x,dx_{*})\int f_{t}(d\rho_{*}\mid x_{*})

\displaystyle\times\mathbb{E}\!\left[\,\phi(\rho^{\prime},X^{\prime})\,\middle|\,\rho,x,\rho_{*},x_{*}\right].

Equivalently, writing
g_{t}(x_{*}):=\int f_{t}(d\rho_{*},dx_{*})
, we have

\int\phi\,df_{t+1}=\iiint\!\!\int\mathbb{E}\!\left[\,\phi(\rho^{\prime},X^{\prime})\,\middle|\,\rho,x,\rho_{*},x_{*}\right]\,\mathcal{K}_{\eta_{t}}(x,x_{*})\,f_{t}(\rho,x)\,\frac{f_{t}(\rho_{*},x_{*})}{g_{t}(x_{*})}\,d\rho\,dx\,d\rho_{*}\,dx_{*}.

2.3.
Macroscopic Reduction and Gaussian Closure

Solving the integro-differential equation (
8
) on the space of measures is computationally intractable for control purposes. However, the Linear-Gaussian structure of our system admits an exact finite-dimensional reduction.

Proposition 2.2

(Preservation of Gaussianity)
.

If the initial density
f_{0}(\rho,x)
is a zero-mean bivariate Gaussian, then the solution
f_{t}(\rho,x)
to the kinetic equation remains a zero-mean bivariate Gaussian for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Assume
f_{t}(\rho,x)
is a zero-mean (possibly degenerate) bivariate Gaussian.
Let
\sigma_{t}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}(\bar{X}_{t})
and let
m_{t}:=\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t},\bar{X}_{t})
.
If
\sigma_{t}=0
(so
\bar{X}_{t}\equiv 0
a.s.), we may simply draw
\bar{X}_{t}^{\prime}\equiv 0
and take

\bar{\rho}_{t}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
independent of everything below.
Henceforth assume
\sigma_{t}>0
and set
r_{t}:=m_{t}/\sigma_{t}=\mathrm{Corr}(\bar{\rho}_{t},\bar{X}_{t})
.
We construct the mean-field interaction using the “shadow opponent” coupling (see Appendix
A
).
Given
\bar{X}_{t}
, draw

\bar{X}_{t}^{\prime}=\eta_{t}\bar{X}_{t}+\sqrt{1-\eta_{t}^{2}}\,Z_{t},\qquad Z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{t}^{2}),\quad Z_{t}\perp\bar{X}_{t},

so that
\mathrm{Corr}(\bar{X}_{t},\bar{X}_{t}^{\prime})=\eta_{t}
and
\bar{X}_{t}^{\prime}
is Gaussian with variance
\sigma_{t}^{2}
.
Under the Gaussian hypothesis, the conditional law of skill given rating is linear; hence we may represent the opponent skill as

\bar{\rho}_{t}^{\prime}=\frac{r_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}\bar{X}_{t}^{\prime}+\sqrt{1-r_{t}^{2}}\,\zeta_{t},\qquad\zeta_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),

independent of
(\bar{X}_{t},\bar{X}_{t}^{\prime},Z_{t})
, which enforces
\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t}^{\prime},\bar{X}_{t}^{\prime})=r_{t}\sigma_{t}
and
\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\rho}_{t}^{\prime})=1
.
Together with the AR(1) skill update (
1
) and the (mean-field) linear rating update (
25
), we obtain
(\bar{\rho}_{t+1},\bar{X}_{t+1})
as an affine function of a jointly Gaussian vector

(\bar{\rho}_{t},\bar{X}_{t},Z_{t},\zeta_{t},\xi_{t},\omega_{t})
.
Therefore
(\bar{\rho}_{t+1},\bar{X}_{t+1})
is Gaussian and has mean zero; scaling by the deterministic factor
L_{t+1}
preserves Gaussianity.
By induction,
f_{t}
remains Gaussian for all
t\geq 0
.
∎

This property implies that the infinite-dimensional dynamics of
f_{t}
project exactly onto the dynamics of its second moments.
Since the skill variance is fixed at unity, the state is fully characterized by the rating variance
\sigma_{t}^{2}
and the rating accuracy (correlation)

r_{t}:=\sigma_{t}^{-1}\int\rho x\,f_{t}(\rho,x)\,d\rho dx,\qquad(t\geq 1),

whenever
\sigma_{t}>0
.
Under our normalization
X_{i,0}=0
, the initial rating distribution is degenerate and
\sigma_{0}=0
, so the correlation is not defined at
t=0
.
For notational convenience we set
r_{0}:=0
, and the analysis of rating accuracy starts from
t\geq 1
(where
\sigma_{t}>0
along any nontrivial trajectory and in the simulations).

We now state the main dynamical constraint that will be used in the optimization problem.

Theorem 2.3

(State Transition Function)
.

The evolution of the rating accuracy
r_{t}
is governed by the deterministic map
r_{t+1}=\Psi(r_{t},K_{t},\eta_{t},\sigma_{t})
:

(9)

r_{t+1}=\lambda\cdot\frac{r_{t}\sigma_{t}(1-K_{t}(1-\eta_{t}))+K_{t}(1-\eta_{t}r_{t}^{2})}{\sqrt{\Lambda(r_{t},K_{t},\eta_{t},\sigma_{t})^{2}}}.

Here,
\Lambda^{2}
represents the pre-scaling variance of the ratings:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{t}^{2}\left[(1-K_{t})^{2}+K_{t}^{2}+2K_{t}(1-K_{t})\eta_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+K_{t}^{2}\left[\beta^{2}+2(1-\eta_{t}r_{t}^{2})\right]

(10)

\displaystyle\quad+2K_{t}(1-K_{t})(1-\eta_{t})r_{t}\sigma_{t}-2K_{t}^{2}(1-\eta_{t})r_{t}\sigma_{t}.

The explicit dependence on
\sigma_{t}
indicates that the optimal policy must jointly control the gain and the scale.

Proof.

The proof follows from testing (
8
) with
\phi(x)=x^{2}
and
\phi(\rho,x)=\rho x
. The expectation terms involve Gaussian integrals over the kernel
\mathcal{K}_{\eta}
, which yield the algebraic expressions in (
2.3
). See Appendix
A.4
for details.
∎

Analysis of the Transition Map
\Psi

The complexity of the transition function
\Psi
(and thus the reduced dynamics) is primarily contained within the pre-scaling variance term
\Lambda^{2}
, which represents the total uncertainty injected into the rating
X
during a single update step.
\Lambda^{2}
is an aggregate of three distinct, independent sources of variance in the unscaled rating
\tilde{X}_{t+1}
:

(1)

Retention Term
: The variance retained from the previous rating state,
\mathrm{Var}((1-K_{t})X_{t}+K_{t}X^{\prime}_{t})
, which is modulated by the match correlation
\eta_{t}
.

(2)

Skill Mismatch Term
: The variance injected by the latent skill difference and mismatch,
\mathrm{Var}(K_{t}(\rho_{t}-\rho^{\prime}_{t}))
.

(3)

Observation Noise Term
: The variance from the inherent match outcome uncertainty,
\mathrm{Var}(K_{t}\omega_{t})
.

The algebraic expression for
\Lambda^{2}
is intricate due to the cross-covariance terms that couple the current rating
X_{t}
with the opponent’s rating
X^{\prime}_{t}
and skill
\rho^{\prime}_{t}
. A rigorous, stepwise derivation detailing the contribution of each component is provided in Appendix
A.4
, confirming the explicit form presented in this theorem.

3.
Mean-Field Control and Asymptotic Analysis

Having derived the kinetic description of the system, we now address the
inverse problem
: designing the interaction rules to optimize the system’s performance. From a mathematical perspective, this constitutes a Mean-Field Control (MFC) problem. The central planner (platform) seeks to control the evolution of the probability density
f_{t}
by adjusting the interaction kernel
\mathcal{K}_{\eta}
and the post-collision redistribution.

3.1.
The Optimal Control Problem

We study the design problem faced by a competitive platform that repeatedly matches agents and updates
ratings. By Gaussian closure, the platform’s informational state is fully summarized by the scalar

rating accuracy

r_{t}\in[0,1]
, the correlation between true skill and rating. Hence the original
mean-field control problem reduces to a finite-dimensional control problem on moments with state
variable
r_{t}
.

3.1.1.
Utility of Match Quality

The primary goal of a rating system is to facilitate fair competitions. In our mean-field model,
fairness is quantified by the correlation of skills in a matched pair:

\mathbb{E}[\rho\rho^{\prime}]=\eta_{t}r_{t}^{2},

where
\eta_{t}\in[0,1)
measures the sorting intensity of the matching kernel and
r_{t}
measures how
informative ratings are about skills. Empirical evidence from industry and academic studies indicates
that stronger skill balance improves user experience and retention
[
1
,
22
]
.
We therefore interpret
\eta_{t}r_{t}^{2}
as the flow utility from match quality.

3.1.2.
Cost of Sorting (Latency)

Stricter sorting reduces the set of eligible opponents and increases expected waiting time. This trade-off
is well documented for competitive online games
[
7
,
14
]
. We model this friction as a
convex queueing cost
C(\eta)
satisfying

C:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\text{ is convex, nondecreasing, and }C(0)=0,\qquad\lim_{\eta\uparrow 1}C(\eta)=+\infty,

capturing the asymptotic explosion of waiting times under nearly perfect sorting.

3.1.3.
Controls, State Dynamics, and Objective

We now define
admissibility
of control processes; see the definition below.

Definition 3.1

(Admissible policies)
.

A policy
\pi=\{(K_{t},\sigma_{t},\eta_{t})\}_{t\geq 0}
is
admissible
if
\sigma_{0}=0
and

K_{t}>0,\qquad\sigma_{t+1}>0,\qquad\eta_{t}\in[0,1)\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

Given
r_{0}\in[0,1)
, the induced state sequence
\{r_{t}\}
is defined recursively by the exact transition
map (Theorem
2.3
):

(11)

r_{t+1}=\Psi(r_{t},K_{t},\eta_{t},\sigma_{t}).

The platform maximizes discounted net welfare:

(12)

J(\pi)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\delta^{t}\Bigl[\eta_{t}r_{t}^{2}-C(\eta_{t})\Bigr],\qquad\delta\in(0,1).

Timing convention.

At each time
t
, the platform chooses
(K_{t},\sigma_{t},\eta_{t})
.
After the rating update, it rescales the post-update ratings so that the resulting dispersion equals
the chosen target
\sigma_{t}
(which becomes the dispersion that enters the kernel for the next matching step).

Remark 3.2

(Why non-compactness matters and how we address it)
.

Because
(K_{t},\sigma_{t})\in(0,\infty)^{2}
, the admissible set is non-compact. The objective in
(
12
) is nevertheless well-defined and finite for any admissible policy since

\eta_{t}r_{t}^{2}\leq 1
and
C(\eta_{t})\geq 0
, hence
J(\pi)\leq\sum_{t\geq 0}\delta^{t}<\infty
.
The nontrivial issue is whether the optimal choices of
(K_{t},\sigma_{t})
are attained despite the
non-compact domain. The next subsection resolves this by showing that the one-step filtering problem
has a unique interior maximizer.

3.2.
One-Step Filtering, Fisher Information, and the Invariance Principle

We now isolate the part of the control problem that governs
information acquisition
. Fix the
current accuracy
r\in(0,1)
and sorting intensity
\eta\in[0,1)
. Consider the one-step filtering problem:
choose
(K,\sigma)\in(0,\infty)^{2}
to maximize the next-step accuracy
\Psi(r,K,\eta,\sigma)
.
This step is the only place where non-compactness of the admissible set could, a priori, obstruct existence.

Theorem 3.3

(Optimal Filtering and Invariance Principle)
.

Fix
r\in(0,1)
and
\eta\in[0,1)
. The one-step maximization problem

(13)

\sup_{K>0,\ \sigma>0}\ \Psi(r,K,\eta,\sigma)

admits a
unique
maximizer
(K^{*}(r),\sigma^{*}(r))\in(0,\infty)^{2}
. It is given by

(14)

K^{*}(r)=\frac{1-r^{2}}{2(1-r^{2})+\beta^{2}},\qquad\sigma^{*}(r)=r.

Moreover, under this optimal filtering choice, the induced transition becomes
independent
of

\eta
and reduces to the one-dimensional map

(15)

\Psi\bigl(r,K^{*}(r),\eta,\sigma^{*}(r)\bigr)=:\Phi(r)=\lambda\sqrt{\,r^{2}+\frac{(1-r^{2})^{2}}{\beta^{2}+2(1-r^{2})}\,}.

Proof (main steps; full derivations in Appendices
A.4
and
A.5
).

Fix
r\in(0,1)
and
\eta\in[0,1)
. We show that the one-step problem
(
13
) attains its supremum on the non-compact domain
(0,\infty)^{2}
,
and that the maximizer is unique and interior.

Step 1 (Explicit representation and signal-matching).

Starting from the explicit transition formula
\Psi(r,K,\eta,\sigma)
in
Theorem
2.3
, write

\Psi(r,K,\eta,\sigma)=\lambda\,\frac{\mathcal{N}(K,\sigma)}{\sqrt{\Lambda^{2}(K,\sigma)}},

where
\mathcal{N}
is the covariance numerator and
\Lambda^{2}
is the pre-scaling variance.
The algebraic expressions of
\mathcal{N}
and
\Lambda^{2}
are derived in
Appendix
A.4
and reorganized into a ratio of quadratic forms in
Appendix
A.5
.

Appendix
A.5
further shows that, after optimizing over
K
for each fixed

\sigma
, the resulting upper envelope depends on
\sigma
only through
(\sigma-r)^{2}
and is

strictly decreasing
in
(\sigma-r)^{2}
. Hence the unique maximizer in the
\sigma
-direction
is

\sigma^{*}(r)=r,

which is the canonical
signal-matching
scaling.

Step 2 (Optimal gain and
\eta
-elimination).

Under
\sigma=r
, Appendix
A.5
shows that both the numerator and the variance
simplify to

\mathcal{N}(K,r)=r^{2}+K(1-r^{2}),\qquad\Lambda^{2}(K,r)=r^{2}+K^{2}\bigl(\beta^{2}+2(1-r^{2})\bigr),

which are independent of
\eta
. Therefore, for
\sigma=r
the transition is independent of
\eta
:

\Psi(r,K,\eta,r)=\lambda\,\frac{r^{2}+K(1-r^{2})}{\sqrt{r^{2}+K^{2}\bigl(\beta^{2}+2(1-r^{2})\bigr)}}.

The right-hand side is differentiable on
(0,\infty)
and has a unique critical point.
Appendix
A.5
verifies that this critical point is the unique global maximizer on
the non-compact domain
K>0
, and it is given by

K^{*}(r)=\frac{1-r^{2}}{2(1-r^{2})+\beta^{2}}>0.

This proves attainment, uniqueness, and interiority of the maximizer
(K^{*}(r),\sigma^{*}(r))
.

Step 3 (Invariant map).

Substituting
(K^{*}(r),\sigma^{*}(r))
into
\Psi
yields

\Psi\bigl(r,K^{*}(r),\eta,\sigma^{*}(r)\bigr)=\lambda\sqrt{\,r^{2}+\frac{(1-r^{2})^{2}}{\beta^{2}+2(1-r^{2})}\,}=:\Phi(r),

which is independent of
\eta
and equals (
15
). This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.4

(Interpretation)
.

The scaling
\sigma^{*}(r)=r
matches the rating dispersion to the current estimation accuracy, preventing
noise amplification in the update step. Under this optimal scaling, the interaction topology
\eta
affects

who plays whom
(and thus instantaneous utility) but does not affect
how much information
each
match generates about skills.

3.3.
Reduced Dynamics and Monotone Value of Accuracy

Under the optimal filtering rule of Theorem
3.3
, the accuracy dynamics reduce to the
scalar recursion

r_{t+1}=\Phi(r_{t}),

which is independent of the platform’s sorting choice
\eta_{t}
. This reduction is the key input for the
separation result in the next subsection: it implies that, once the learning engine is optimally calibrated,
choices of
\eta_{t}
affect only current-period utility and not future accuracy.

Proposition 3.5

(Analysis of the Kinetic Map)
.

Let
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
\beta^{2}>0
. The map
\Phi:[0,1]\to(0,\lambda]
defined in
(
15
) satisfies the following properties:

(1)

Invariance and Monotonicity
:
\Phi
maps the interval
[0,\lambda]
into itself and is strictly increasing on
[0,1]
.

(2)

Existence and Uniqueness
: There exists a unique fixed point
r_{\infty}\in(0,\lambda)
satisfying
r_{\infty}=\Phi(r_{\infty})
.

(3)

Global Stability
: For any initial condition
r_{0}\in[0,1)
, the sequence
r_{t+1}=\Phi(r_{t})
converges monotonically to
r_{\infty}
.

Proof.

We analyze the properties of the function
\Phi(r)
.

1. Boundedness and Monotonicity.

Recall the definition:

\Phi(r)=\lambda\sqrt{r^{2}+\frac{(1-r^{2})^{2}}{\beta^{2}+2(1-r^{2})}}.

At the boundaries, we have
\Phi(0)=\lambda/\sqrt{\beta^{2}+2}>0
and
\Phi(1)=\lambda
.
Since the term inside the square root is strictly bounded by 1 for all
r\in[0,1]
, it holds that
\Phi(r)<\lambda
for all
r<1
. Thus,
\Phi
maps
[0,\lambda]
into
(0,\lambda]
.
To show monotonicity, let
x=r^{2}
and consider the function
g(x)=x+\frac{(1-x)^{2}}{\beta^{2}+2(1-x)}
for
x\in[0,1]
. Differentiating with respect to
x
, we obtain

g^{\prime}(x)=1-\frac{2(1-x)(\beta^{2}+2(1-x))+2(1-x)^{2}}{(\beta^{2}+2(1-x))^{2}}=1-\frac{2(1-x)[\beta^{2}+3(1-x)]}{(\beta^{2}+2(1-x))^{2}}.

A straightforward algebraic check confirms that
g^{\prime}(x)>0
for
\beta^{2}>0
and
x\in[0,1)
. Since
\Phi(r)=\lambda\sqrt{g(r^{2})}
, the map
\Phi
is strictly increasing in
r
.

2. Uniqueness of the Fixed Point.

The fixed point condition
r=\Phi(r)
is equivalent to
r^{2}=\lambda^{2}g(r^{2})
. Letting
x=r^{2}
, we seek a solution to
x=\lambda^{2}g(x)
in the interval
(0,\lambda^{2})
.
This equation can be rearranged into a polynomial equation. Substituting the expression for
g(x)
:

x=\lambda^{2}\left(x+\frac{(1-x)^{2}}{\beta^{2}+2-2x}\right).

Rearranging terms yields:

x(1-\lambda^{2})[\beta^{2}+2-2x]=\lambda^{2}(1-x)^{2}.

Define
H(x)=x(1-\lambda^{2})[\beta^{2}+2-2x]-\lambda^{2}(1-x)^{2}
.
We observe that
H(x)
is a quadratic function opening downwards (the coefficient of
x^{2}
is
-2(1-\lambda^{2})-\lambda^{2}=\lambda^{2}-2<0
).
Checking the boundary values:

•

H(0)=-\lambda^{2}<0
.

•

H(\lambda^{2})=\lambda^{2}(1-\lambda^{2})[\beta^{2}+2-2\lambda^{2}]-\lambda^{2}(1-\lambda^{2})^{2}=\lambda^{2}(1-\lambda^{2})[\beta^{2}+1-\lambda^{2}]>0
.

Since
H(0)<0
and
H(\lambda^{2})>0
, and
H
is continuous, there exists at least one root in
(0,\lambda^{2})
. Furthermore, since
H(1)=(1-\lambda^{2})\beta^{2}>0
and the parabola opens downward, the other root must be greater than 1 (or negative, but
H(0)<0
implies the positive root is unique in the feasible range). Thus, there is exactly one solution
x_{\infty}\in(0,\lambda^{2})
, which implies a unique fixed point
r_{\infty}=\sqrt{x_{\infty}}\in(0,\lambda)
.

3. Convergence.

Since
\Phi
is strictly increasing, continuous, and maps
[0,\lambda]
into itself with a unique fixed point
r_{\infty}
, standard results on one-dimensional maps guarantee global stability. Specifically, for any
r_{0}\in[0,r_{\infty})
, the sequence is strictly increasing and bounded above by
r_{\infty}
, while for
r_{0}\in(r_{\infty},1]
, it is strictly decreasing and bounded below. In both cases, the sequence converges to
r_{\infty}
.
∎

Remark 3.6

(The Red Queen Effect)
.

The upper bound
r_{\infty}<\lambda
quantifies an entropic ceiling created by skill diffusion:
even under optimal filtering, rating accuracy cannot exceed
\lambda
because skills continuously
evolve over time. The system must therefore keep updating ratings not to reach perfect information,
but to offset the ongoing dissipation of information.

Next define the instantaneous net utility and its envelope:

U(r,\eta):=\eta r^{2}-C(\eta),\qquad V(r):=\max_{\eta\in[0,1)}U(r,\eta).

Lemma 3.7

(Accuracy has monotone value)
.

Assume
C:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is convex, nondecreasing, satisfies
C(0)=0
, and
\lim_{\eta\uparrow 1}C(\eta)=+\infty
.
Then for each
r\in[0,1]
the maximization problem defining
V(r)
admits at least one solution

\eta^{*}(r)\in[0,1)
, and the envelope
V(r)
is
nondecreasing
in
r
on
(0,1]
.

Proof.

Fix
\eta\in[0,1)
. The function
r\mapsto U(r,\eta)=\eta r^{2}-C(\eta)
is strictly increasing on
(0,1]

whenever
\eta>0
. Since
C(\eta)\to+\infty
as
\eta\uparrow 1
, the maximizer cannot be at
\eta=1
,
and convexity ensures upper semicontinuity of the objective on the compact set
[0,1-\varepsilon]
for
any
\varepsilon>0
, implying existence of a maximizer
\eta^{*}(r)\in[0,1)
.
To show strict monotonicity of
V
, take
0<r_{1}<r_{2}\leq 1
. Let
\eta_{1}\in\arg\max_{\eta}U(r_{1},\eta)
.
Then

V(r_{2})\ \geq\ U(r_{2},\eta_{1})\ =\ U(r_{1},\eta_{1})+\eta_{1}(r_{2}^{2}-r_{1}^{2})\ =\ V(r_{1})+\eta_{1}(r_{2}^{2}-r_{1}^{2})\geq\ V(r_{1}).

∎

Remark 3.8

(Connection to separation)
.

Lemma
3.7
implies that higher accuracy
r
strictly increases the best attainable
instantaneous net utility
V(r)
. Together with Proposition
3.5
(which shows that

\Phi
is strictly increasing), this monotonicity will allow us to conclude that maximizing
r_{t+1}

at each step maximizes the entire continuation value, which is the key step behind the separation
result in Corollary
3.9
.

3.4.
Optimal Control Strategy

Thanks to the Invariance Principle (Theorem
3.3
), the infinite-horizon optimization problem (
12
) splits into two tractable static problems.

Corollary 3.9

(Optimal separated control policy)
.

The following control policy is optimal. For each
t
, given the current state
r_{t}
:

(1)

Filtering (Greedy):
Choose
(K_{t},\sigma_{t})
to maximize the next-state correlation:

(16)

(K_{t}^{*},\sigma_{t}^{*})\in\operatorname*{argmax}_{K>0,\,\sigma>0}\;\Psi(r_{t},K_{t},\eta_{t},\sigma_{t}),

which is implemented by the Kalman gain and signal-matched scaling in (
14
).
In particular, any maximizer
(K_{t}^{*},\sigma_{t}^{*})
is optimal and this choice is independent of the cost function
C(\cdot)
and
\eta_{t}
.

(2)

Matching (Static):
Choose
\eta_{t}
myopically as a maximizer of instantaneous net utility:

(17)

\eta_{t}^{*}\in\operatorname*{argmax}_{\eta\in[0,1)}\left\{\eta r_{t}^{2}-C(\eta)\right\}.

Proof.

Let
\pi=\{(K_{t},\sigma_{t},\eta_{t})\}_{t\geq 0}
be any admissible policy and let

\{r_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the induced state sequence under the transition

r_{t+1}=\Psi(r_{t},K_{t},\eta_{t},\sigma_{t})
.
Define the instantaneous net utility and its envelope

U(r,\eta):=\eta r^{2}-C(\eta),\qquad V(r):=\max_{\eta\in[0,1)}U(r,\eta).

Then
U(r_{t},\eta_{t})\leq V(r_{t})
for every
t
.

Next, by Theorem
3.3
, for every current state
r\in(0,1)
and any
\eta\in[0,1)
,

\sup_{K>0,\ \sigma>0}\Psi(r,K,\eta,\sigma)=:\Phi(r),

and the supremum is attained at the maximizer
(K^{*}(r),\sigma^{*}(r))
given in (
14
).
In particular, the maximizer
(K^{*}(r),\sigma^{*}(r))
depends only on
r
and is independent of both
C(\cdot)
and
\eta
.
Therefore, along any admissible policy
\pi
we have the pointwise bound

r_{t+1}=\Psi(r_{t},K_{t},\eta_{t},\sigma_{t})\leq\Phi(r_{t})\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

Now define the benchmark sequence
\{\bar{r}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
by
\bar{r}_{0}=r_{0}
and

\bar{r}_{t+1}=\Phi(\bar{r}_{t})\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

Since
\Phi
is increasing (Proposition
3.5
), the inequality
r_{t+1}\leq\Phi(r_{t})
implies by induction that

r_{t}\leq\bar{r}_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
. Hence, using that
V
is nondecreasing in
r
(Lemma
3.7
, with the monotonicity statement),

J(\pi)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\delta^{t}U(r_{t},\eta_{t})\leq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\delta^{t}V(r_{t})\leq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\delta^{t}V(\bar{r}_{t}).

Finally, consider the policy stated in the corollary:
(i) choose
(K_{t}^{*},\sigma_{t}^{*})\in\arg\max_{K,\sigma}\Psi(r_{t},K,\eta_{t},\sigma)
,
which implements
(K^{*}(r_{t}),\sigma^{*}(r_{t}))
from (
14
) and yields
r_{t+1}=\Phi(r_{t})
;
(ii) choose
\eta_{t}^{*}\in\arg\max_{\eta\in[0,1)}\{\eta r_{t}^{2}-C(\eta)\}
, so that
U(r_{t},\eta_{t}^{*})=V(r_{t})
.
Under this policy we have
r_{t}=\bar{r}_{t}
for all
t
and
U(r_{t},\eta_{t}^{*})=V(r_{t})
for all
t
, so the achieved objective equals

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\delta^{t}V(\bar{r}_{t}),

which matches the upper bound above. Therefore the stated separated policy is optimal.
∎

Remark 3.10

(The Principle of Control Separation and its Distinction from LQG)
.

This result justifies a modular design for competitive platforms by confirming a strong form of the
Separation Principle
. The overall complexity of the Mean-Field Control problem is dramatically reduced as the two core functions can be treated independently:

(1)

The
Estimation Engine
(Filtering): The control pair
(K_{t},\sigma_{t})
is dedicated to maximizing the rate of information acquisition, defining the dynamic map
r_{t+1}=\Phi(r_{t})
(Theorem
3.3
).

(2)

The
Matchmaking Engine
(Utility Control): The parameter
\eta_{t}
is determined solely by maximizing the instantaneous net utility
U(r_{t},\eta_{t})=\eta_{t}r_{t}^{2}-C(\eta_{t})
(Corollary
3.9
).

This result differs fundamentally from the classical LQG Separation Principle (Certainty Equivalence). In the classical setting, the optimal control uses the estimated state (
\hat{x}_{t}
) but the filtering process is independent of the control strategy. In our system, the control variables directly influence the information flow via the variance term
\Lambda^{2}
. Our Invariance Principle (Theorem
3.3
) reveals a stronger structural separation of control variables: the optimal selection of the update parameters
(K_{t}^{*},\sigma_{t}^{*})
eliminates the influence of the interaction topology
\eta_{t}
on the learning dynamics.

Structural Simplification (
\eta
Elimination):

Crucially, the Separation Principle implies that the maximization over
\eta_{t}
fully decouples from the maximization over the dynamic controls
(K_{t},\sigma_{t})
in the Bellman equation. The problem reduces to finding
\eta_{t}^{*}
locally based on the instantaneous cost-benefit trade-off:
\max_{\eta}\{\eta r_{t}^{2}-C(\eta)\}
. This structural simplification effectively eliminates the complexity associated with solving the Dynamic Programming problem over the multi-dimensional control space, making the full optimal control problem analytically tractable.

3.5.
Thermodynamic Interpretation of the Matching Policy

The optimization problem for the matchmaking intensity
\eta_{t}
, as derived in Corollary
3.9
, takes the form of a static maximization of instantaneous welfare. Let
u_{t}:=r_{t}^{2}
denote the "information potential" (or squared correlation). The problem is written as:

(18)

V(u_{t})=\sup_{\eta\in[0,1)}\left\{\eta u_{t}-C(\eta)\right\}.

From the perspective of convex analysis, the value function
V(\cdot)
is identified as the
Legendre-Fenchel transform
(or convex conjugate) of the cost function
C(\eta)
. This structural observation invites a thermodynamic interpretation of the system’s control.
Here,
u_{t}
acts as an external field driving the system towards stricter sorting, while the matchmaking intensity
\eta
plays the role of the order parameter (analogous to magnetization in a spin system). The cost function
C(\eta)
represents the entropic resistance or the energetic barrier to maintaining order.

Queueing-Theoretic Cost and Critical Phase Transition.

To derive explicit analytical insights, we introduce a barrier potential cost function. This models the friction of organizing a queue, where waiting times diverge as matching requirements become infinitely strict (
\eta\to 1
). We adopt the form:

(19)

C(\eta)=\kappa_{C}\left(\frac{1}{1-\eta}-1\right),\quad\kappa_{C}>0,

where
\kappa_{C}
represents the characteristic cost parameter (e.g., the ratio of opportunity cost of delay to match utility).
Solving the optimization problem with this specific cost yields a closed-form control law exhibiting critical behavior.

Proposition 3.11

(Matchmaking Phase Transition)
.

Consider the cost function (
19
). The optimal matchmaking policy
\eta^{*}(r_{t})
is given by:

(20)

\eta^{*}(r_{t})=\max\left(0,\,1-\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{C}}}{r_{t}}\right).

This policy implies a continuous phase transition at the critical accuracy threshold
r_{c}=\sqrt{\kappa_{C}}
.

•

Disordered Phase (
r_{t}\leq r_{c}
):
If the rating information is insufficient (
r_{t}\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{C}}
), the optimal policy is zero intervention (
\eta^{*}=0
). The system operates in a random matching regime to prioritize throughput over quality.

•

Ordered Phase (
r_{t}>r_{c}
):
Once accuracy exceeds the critical threshold, the optimal intensity becomes positive and strictly increases with
r_{t}
, asymptotically approaching strict matching (
\eta\to 1
).

Proof.

Let
J(\eta)=\eta r_{t}^{2}-C(\eta)
. Since
C(\eta)
is strictly convex on
[0,1)
,
J(\eta)
is strictly concave. We look for the stationary point by solving the first-order condition
J^{\prime}(\eta)=0
:

r_{t}^{2}-C^{\prime}(\eta)=r_{t}^{2}-\frac{\kappa_{C}}{(1-\eta)^{2}}=0.

Solving for
\eta
, we obtain the unconstrained root:

(1-\eta)^{2}=\frac{\kappa_{C}}{r_{t}^{2}}\implies\eta=1-\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{C}}}{r_{t}}.

We must satisfy the constraint
\eta\in[0,1)
.
Since
C(\eta)\to\infty
as
\eta\to 1
, the upper bound is naturally satisfied.
For the lower bound, if
r_{t}\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{C}}
, then
1-\sqrt{\kappa_{C}}/r_{t}\leq 0
. Since
J(\eta)
is concave and decreasing for
\eta
beyond the peak, the maximum on the interval
[0,1)
occurs at the boundary
\eta=0
.
Conversely, if
r_{t}>\sqrt{\kappa_{C}}
, the root lies in
(0,1)
. Combining these cases yields the expression (
20
).
∎

This result provides a theoretical foundation for the "calibration phase" widely implemented in competitive platforms. It suggests that loose matchmaking (or random matching) is not merely a heuristic for reducing wait times, but the
welfare-maximizing strategy
when the platform lacks sufficient knowledge (
r_{t}<\sqrt{\kappa_{C}}
) to justify the cost of sorting. Mathematically, the system exhibits a second-order phase transition, where the order parameter
\eta^{*}
leaves zero continuously but with a discontinuous derivative at
r_{c}
.

4.
Numerical Validation

In this section, we present numerical investigations to validate the kinetic theory and the control strategies derived in the previous sections. We employ a Monte Carlo particle method to simulate the
N

6
) and compare the empirical statistics with the predictions of the macroscopic mean-field model. Our simulations focus on three aspects: (i) the quantitative rate of convergence of the empirical measure to the mean-field limit, (ii) the asymptotic behavior of rating accuracy in the presence of skill drift, and (iii) the verification of the Invariance Principle (Theorem

).

4.1.
Convergence to the Mean-Field Limit

First, we quantify the error between the finite-
N

r_{t+1}=\Psi(r_{t},\dots)
derived in Theorem
2.3
. This step is crucial to justify the use of the reduced-order model for control design.

Simulation Setup.

We simulate systems with population sizes varying logarithmically from
N=10^{1}
to
10^{5}
. The skill persistence is set to
\lambda=0.99
, and the observation noise variance is
\beta^{2}=1.0
. The control parameters are fixed at a constant gain
K=0.1
, random matching
\eta=0.0
, and unit scaling
\sigma=1.0
.

Because the theoretical normalization
X_{i,0}\equiv 0

\sigma_{0}=0

The simulation then proceeds by sequentially applying the interaction update and the skill drift step, consistent with the discrete-time definition.

Entropic Limits and the Red Queen Effect

Next, we quantitatively analyze the stationary state of the kinetic map to assess the impact of skill non-stationarity. We solve for the fixed point

5.
Conclusion

We developed a kinetic/mean-field framework for platform design in rating and matchmaking systems with drifting skills.
Starting from a microscopic interacting particle model, we derived a McKean–Vlasov description of the joint skill–rating dynamics and proved an exact Gaussian closure in the Gaussian regime.
This yields a finite-dimensional, analytically tractable state representation for rating accuracy and enables an explicit solution of the associated mean-field control problem when the platform controls

u_{t}=(K_{t},\eta_{t},\sigma_{t}),

where
\sigma_{t}
is interpreted operationally as the rating scale applied in period
t
.

Three structural lessons emerge.

(1)

Entropic limits under skill drift (Red Queen effect).

Even under optimal filtering, accuracy cannot converge to one in a non-stationary environment.
With observation noise
\beta^{2}>0
and persistence
\lambda\in(0,1)
, the induced accuracy map admits a unique globally stable fixed point
r_{\infty}\in(0,\lambda)
, providing a sharp ceiling on long-run learning.

(2)

Invariance of information flow under signal-matched scaling.

Proper scale control is decisive.
When the platform applies signal-matched scaling (implemented as
\sigma_{t}=r_{t}
), the one-step accuracy gain becomes independent of assortativity
\eta_{t}
.
Thus, assortative matching is not intrinsically required for fast learning: once ratings are correctly normalized, even high-entropy (random) interactions can be information-efficient.

(3)

Separation of filtering and matching, and a welfare phase transition.

The invariance yields a separation of platform decisions.
Filtering controls
(K_{t},\sigma_{t})
are determined myopically by maximizing next-period accuracy, while matching
\eta_{t}
solves a static instantaneous trade-off between match utility and sorting/latency costs.
Under a queueing-type barrier cost, the optimal policy exhibits a threshold behavior: when current accuracy is low, random matching is optimal; once accuracy exceeds a critical level, increasingly strict sorting becomes welfare-improving.

Methodologically, the paper illustrates how kinetic-limit tools can convert a high-dimensional interacting-agent algorithm into a low-dimensional control problem with transparent structure.
Substantively, the results point to a modular platform architecture: a properly calibrated
estimation engine
(via scale control) can be designed largely independently of the
matchmaking engine
, which can then be tuned to welfare and latency considerations.

Several extensions are natural.
First, moving beyond the Gaussian regime (e.g., heavy-tailed skills or nonlinear outcome models) would require new closure or approximation schemes.
Second, incorporating strategic behavior (e.g., rating manipulation or effort choice) would lead to a mean-field game formulation and raise incentive-design questions.
Finally, while our main analysis is discrete-time and self-contained, the Appendix provides an optional diffusion approximation (Appendix
A.2
) that may be useful for readers who prefer a continuous-time perspective; it is not used in the main control results.

Appendix A
Derivation of the Mean-Field Limit and Gaussian Closure

In this appendix, we provide the mathematical justification for the macroscopic dynamics derived in Section
2
. We explicitly formulate the mean-field limit as a McKean-Vlasov type process, justify the Gaussian closure property, and provide the detailed algebraic derivation of the variance dynamics (Theorem
2.3
).

A.1.
The McKean-Vlasov Process Construction

Let
(\bar{\rho}_{t},\bar{X}_{t})
denote the state of a typical particle in the mean-field limit. Unlike the
N
-particle system where agents interact with specific peers, the typical particle interacts with a "shadow opponent" drawn from the conditional law of the population.

The skill dynamics are governed by the independent AR(1) process:

(22)

\bar{\rho}_{t+1}=\lambda\bar{\rho}_{t}+\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\xi_{t},\quad\xi_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

For the rating update step, we construct the shadow opponent’s state
(\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t},\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t})
to satisfy the statistical constraints imposed by the interaction kernel
\mathcal{K}_{\eta}
.
Given the agent’s rating
\bar{X}_{t}
, the opponent’s rating
\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t}
is generated via the coupling:

(23)

\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t}=\eta_{t}\bar{X}_{t}+\sqrt{1-\eta_{t}^{2}}Z_{t},

where
Z_{t}
is an independent copy of
\bar{X}_{t}
(i.e.,
Z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{t}^{2})
and
Z_{t}\perp\bar{X}_{t}
). This construction ensures the required correlation
\mathrm{Corr}(\bar{X}_{t},\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t})=\eta_{t}
.

Crucially, the opponent’s skill
\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t}
is not independent but is drawn from the conditional distribution
f_{t}(\rho|x=\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t})
. Under the Gaussian ansatz, this is realized by the linear projection:

(24)

\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t}=\frac{r_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t}+\sqrt{1-r_{t}^{2}}\zeta_{t},

where
\zeta_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is independent noise. This ensures
\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t},\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t})=r_{t}\sigma_{t}
.

The continuous-state update rule is then given by the discrete-time McKean-Vlasov equation:

(25)

\bar{X}_{t+1}=L_{t+1}\left[\bar{X}_{t}+K_{t}\left((\bar{\rho}_{t}-\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})+\omega_{t}-(\bar{X}_{t}-\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t})\right)\right],

where
\omega_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\beta^{2})
is the observation noise.

A.2.
Continuous-Time Diffusion Approximation (Optional)

This subsection provides an optional continuous-time diffusion approximation of the discrete-time mean-field dynamics.
This perspective is not used elsewhere in the paper.

While the discrete-time formulation in Section
2
captures the operational mechanics, analytically characterizing the global distribution benefits from the tools of stochastic calculus. By taking the continuous-time limit of the microscopic update rules, we validate the structural assumptions made in Section
2
, specifically proving that the Gaussian closure is exact in the linear regime.

A.2.1.
Diffusion-limit McKean–Vlasov System

We consider the high-frequency limit where interaction events occur at a rate
1/\Delta t
.

Continuous-time scaling.

To take the continuous-time limit, we interpret the discrete-time model as a
\Delta t
-time-step
approximation with physical time
s=n\Delta t
. Accordingly, the control parameters may depend on

\Delta t
, and we write
K_{\Delta t}
and
\beta_{\Delta t}^{2}
.
We impose the diffusive scaling

(26)

\frac{K_{\Delta t}}{\Delta t}\to\kappa\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\beta_{\Delta t}^{2}}{\Delta t}\to\Sigma_{M}^{2}\qquad\text{as }\Delta t\downarrow 0,

equivalently
K_{\Delta t}=\kappa\,\Delta t+o(\Delta t)
and
\beta_{\Delta t}^{2}=\Sigma_{M}^{2}\,\Delta t+o(\Delta t)
.

Let
(\rho_{t},X_{t})
denote the state of a representative agent in the mean-field limit. The dynamics are governed by the following system of coupled stochastic differential equations (SDEs):

(27)

\begin{cases}d\rho_{t}=-\theta\rho_{t}\,dt+\sigma_{\rho}\,dB_{t},\\
dX_{t}=\kappa(\rho_{t}-X_{t})\,dt+\Sigma(\mu_{t})\,dW_{t},\end{cases}

where
B_{t}
and
W_{t}
are independent Wiener processes.
Here,
\theta\approx 1-\lambda
represents the skill mean-reversion rate, and
\kappa
is the continuous-time learning rate.

Crucially, the diffusion coefficient
\Sigma(\mu_{t})
for the rating process is determined by the mean-field interaction and defined by:

(28)

\Sigma(\mu_{t})^{2}=\kappa^{2}\Big(\Sigma_{M}^{2}+\mathrm{Var}\!\big(\rho^{\prime}_{t}-X^{\prime}_{t}\,\big|\,X_{t}\big)\Big),

Lemma A.1

(State-independence of the diffusion coefficient under Gaussian coupling)
.

Assume the Gaussian regime of Proposition
2.2
so that
(\rho_{t},X_{t})
is bivariate Gaussian with moments
(r_{t},\sigma_{t})
.
Generate the opponent by the linear coupling

X_{t}^{\prime}=\eta_{t}X_{t}+\sqrt{1-\eta_{t}^{2}}\,Z_{t},\qquad Z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{t}^{2}),\quad Z_{t}\perp X_{t},

and

\rho_{t}^{\prime}=\frac{r_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}X_{t}^{\prime}+\sqrt{1-r_{t}^{2}}\,\zeta_{t},\qquad\zeta_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),\quad\zeta_{t}\perp(X_{t},X_{t}^{\prime},Z_{t}).

Then
\mathrm{Var}(\rho_{t}^{\prime}-X_{t}^{\prime}\mid X_{t})
is constant (does not depend on
X_{t}
) and equals

(29)

\mathrm{Var}(\rho_{t}^{\prime}-X_{t}^{\prime}\mid X_{t})=(1-r_{t}^{2})+(1-\eta_{t}^{2})\,(\,r_{t}-\sigma_{t}\,)^{2}.

Consequently,
\Sigma(\mu_{t})
depends on
\mu_{t}
only through the global moments
(r_{t},\sigma_{t})
and is independent of the local state
X_{t}
.

Proof.

Write
\rho_{t}^{\prime}-X_{t}^{\prime}=\left(\frac{r_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}-1\right)X_{t}^{\prime}+\sqrt{1-r_{t}^{2}}\,\zeta_{t}
.
Conditional on
X_{t}
, the variable
X_{t}^{\prime}
is Gaussian with variance
\mathrm{Var}(X_{t}^{\prime}\mid X_{t})=(1-\eta_{t}^{2})\sigma_{t}^{2}
, which is independent of
X_{t}
.
Independence of
\zeta_{t}
yields

\mathrm{Var}(\rho_{t}^{\prime}-X_{t}^{\prime}\mid X_{t})=\left(\frac{r_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}-1\right)^{2}(1-\eta_{t}^{2})\sigma_{t}^{2}+(1-r_{t}^{2}),

which simplifies to (
29
).
∎

By Lemma
A.1
, the diffusion coefficient in (
28
) is
state-independent
(additive noise), which is a key ingredient behind the exact Gaussian closure in the continuous-time limit.

Addressing Multiplicative Noise Concern.

Equation (
28
) defines
\Sigma(\mu_{t})
through the conditional variance

\mathrm{Var}(\rho^{\prime}_{t}-X^{\prime}_{t}\mid X_{t})
.
Lemma
A.1
shows that, under the Gaussian coupling construction,
this conditional variance is
constant
and does not depend on the local state
X_{t}
.
Therefore,
\Sigma(\mu_{t})
depends on
\mu_{t}
only through global moments (e.g.,
(r_{t},\sigma_{t})
),
and the rating SDE in (
27
) has
additive
(state-independent) noise.
This rules out multiplicative-noise effects and is the key structural reason why Gaussianity is preserved exactly
in the continuous-time limit.

A.2.2.
The Fokker-Planck Equation

The time evolution of the joint probability density
f(t,\rho,x)
is governed by the associated Fokker-Planck equation (or Kolmogorov forward equation):

(30)

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=\underbrace{\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}(\rho f)+\frac{\sigma_{\rho}^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial\rho^{2}}}_{\text{Skill Diffusion}}+\underbrace{\kappa\frac{\partial}{\partial x}((x-\rho)f)+\frac{\Sigma(\mu_{t})^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x^{2}}}_{\text{Rating Update}}.

This Partial Differential Equation (PDE) encapsulates the competition between the intrinsic entropy of skill drift (first line) and the information gain from rating updates (second line).

A.2.3.
Theoretical Justification for Gaussian Closure

A fundamental property of SDE systems is that linearity of the drift combined with state-independent (additive) Gaussian noise ensures the preservation of Gaussianity.
Since the drift terms in the McKean-Vlasov system (
27
) are linear in the state variables
(\rho,X)
and the diffusion term
\Sigma(\mu_{t})
is distribution-dependent but
state-independent
(additive noise; see Lemma
A.1
), the system constitutes a multi-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with time- or distribution-varying parameters.

Proposition A.2

(Exactness of Gaussian Closure)
.

If the initial joint distribution
f(0,\rho,x)
is Gaussian, the solution
f(t,\rho,x)
to the Fokker-Planck equation (
30
) remains Gaussian for all
t\geq 0
. Furthermore, the long-time stationary solution
f_{\infty}(\rho,x)
is a unique bivariate Gaussian density.

This proposition provides the rigorous justification for the dimension reduction employed in Section
3
. It confirms that the Gaussian ansatz is not merely an approximation but an
exact solution
to the kinetic equations in the continuous-time limit under our linear-Gaussian assumptions. The stationary variance of the rating conditional on skill,
\text{Var}(X_{\infty}|\rho)
, is analytically given by the balance between the diffusion intensity and the restoring force:
\Sigma^{2}/2\kappa
, consistent with the results derived from the discrete-time analysis.

A.3.
Quantitative Analysis of Propagation of Chaos

This appendix provides a quantitative propagation-of-chaos estimate for the
N
-particle system.
Let
Z_{i,t}:=(\rho_{i,t},X_{i,t})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and denote by

\mu_{t}^{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{Z_{i,t}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mu_{t}:=\mathcal{L}(\bar{Z}_{t})

the empirical measure and the deterministic limit law, respectively, where
(\bar{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the nonlinear (McKean–Vlasov) process associated with (
25
).
We make explicit the sufficient conditions under which
\mu_{t}^{N}\Rightarrow\mu_{t}
and provide an error bound used in Section
4
.
Our presentation follows the classical coupling approach; see, e.g.,
[
26
,
24
]
.

Mean-field representation.

The one-step update of particle
i
can be written as

(31)

Z_{i,t+1}=\Phi_{t}\!\left(Z_{i,t},\,\mu_{t}^{N},\,\varepsilon_{i,t}\right),

where
\varepsilon_{i,t}
collects all exogenous randomness at time
t
(skill shocks, match randomization, and outcome noise).
The nonlinear process satisfies

(32)

\bar{Z}_{t+1}=\Phi_{t}\!\left(\bar{Z}_{t},\,\mu_{t},\,\bar{\varepsilon}_{t}\right),\qquad\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(\bar{Z}_{t}),

with
(\bar{\varepsilon}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
i.i.d. copies of
\varepsilon_{i,t}
.

Assumption A.3

(Checklist for quantitative propagation of chaos)
.

Fix a finite horizon
T
.

(A1)

Uniform second moments.

\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2+\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for some
\delta>0
, and

(33)

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|z|^{2+\delta}\,\mu_{t}(dz)<\infty.

(A2)

L^{2}
-Lipschitz in state and measure (in
W_{2}
).

There exists
C_{\Phi}<\infty
such that for all
0\leq t\leq T
, all
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and all
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

(34)

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|\Phi_{t}(z,\mu,\varepsilon)-\Phi_{t}(z^{\prime},\nu,\varepsilon)\right|^{2}\right]\leq C_{\Phi}\left(|z-z^{\prime}|^{2}+W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\right),

where the expectation is w.r.t.
\varepsilon
.

(A3)

Bounded (or Lipschitz) control dependence.

All time-dependent coefficients in
\Phi_{t}
(e.g.
K_{t}
,
L_{t+1}
, and
\eta_{t}
) are uniformly bounded on
[0,T]
.
If these coefficients are feedback controls depending on
\mu_{t}
through a finite set of moments, the corresponding moment maps are Lipschitz on the moment-bounded set defined by (
33
).

A useful Lipschitz lemma for moment maps.

The following bound makes (A3) explicit for our Gaussian-moment parametrization.

Lemma A.4

(Second-moment functionals are
W_{2}
-Lipschitz on moment-bounded sets)
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and let
\pi
be any coupling of
(Z,Z^{\prime})\sim\pi
with marginals
\mu,\nu
.
Assume
\int|z|^{2}\,\mu(dz)\leq M
and
\int|z|^{2}\,\nu(dz)\leq M
.
Then, with
Z=(\rho,X)
and
Z^{\prime}=(\rho^{\prime},X^{\prime})
,

(35)

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[X^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(X^{\prime})^{2}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{M}\,W_{2}(\mu,\nu),

(36)

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\rho X]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\rho^{\prime}X^{\prime}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{M}\,W_{2}(\mu,\nu),

where
W_{2}(\mu,\nu)^{2}=\inf_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}|Z-Z^{\prime}|^{2}
.
Consequently, on the set
\{\mu:\int|z|^{2}\,\mu(dz)\leq M\}
, the maps

\mu\mapsto\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(X)
and
\mu\mapsto\mathrm{Cov}_{\mu}(\rho,X)
are Lipschitz in
W_{2}
.
If moreover
\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(\rho)=1
and
\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(X)
is bounded away from
0
, then
\mu\mapsto r(\mu):=\mathrm{Corr}_{\mu}(\rho,X)
is also Lipschitz.

Proof sketch.

Use the identity
|X^{2}-(X^{\prime})^{2}|=|X-X^{\prime}||X+X^{\prime}|
and Cauchy–Schwarz under an optimal coupling:

\mathbb{E}|X-X^{\prime}||X+X^{\prime}|\leq(\mathbb{E}|X-X^{\prime}|^{2})^{1/2}(\mathbb{E}|X+X^{\prime}|^{2})^{1/2}
.
Moment boundedness gives
\mathbb{E}|X+X^{\prime}|^{2}\leq 4M
, yielding (
35
).
The cross-moment bound is similar using
|\rho X-\rho^{\prime}X^{\prime}|\leq|\rho||X-X^{\prime}|+|X^{\prime}||\rho-\rho^{\prime}|
.
∎

Verification in our model.

Under the Gaussian closure (Proposition
2.2
) and the uniform bounds in Proposition
3.5
,
the coefficients entering the microscopic update are uniformly bounded on finite horizons.
Moreover, the dependence of the update on
\mu_{t}^{N}
enters through second moments (equivalently, through
(\sigma_{t},\mathrm{Cov}(\rho,X),r_{t})
),
and Lemma
A.4
provides the required Lipschitz control in
W_{2}
.
Since the update is affine in
(Z_{i,t},Z_{j,t})
and the exogenous noise is Gaussian, the
L^{2}
-Lipschitz condition (
34
) follows.

Quantitative estimate

We now state a quantitative propagation-of-chaos bound.

Proposition A.5

(Quantitative propagation of chaos on a finite horizon)
.

Suppose Assumption
A.3
holds.
Then for every finite horizon
T
there exists
C_{T}<\infty
such that

(37)

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathbb{E}\!\left[W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{t}^{N},\mu_{t})\right]\leq C_{T}\Big(\mathbb{E}[W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0}^{N},\mu_{0})]+\alpha_{N}(T)\Big),

where
\alpha_{N}(T)
is the empirical-measure error of i.i.d. samples from
\mu_{t}
:

\alpha_{N}(T):=\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathbb{E}\!\left[W_{2}^{2}\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\bar{Z}_{i,t}},\,\mu_{t}\Big)\right],\qquad\bar{Z}_{i,t}\ \text{i.i.d.}\sim\mu_{t}.

Moreover, since
\mu_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{2+\delta}
uniformly on
[0,T]
, one has

(38)

\alpha_{N}(T)=O\!\left(N^{-1/2}\right)\quad\text{up to logarithmic factors in dimension }2,

so
\mu_{t}^{N}\Rightarrow\mu_{t}
and the system is
\mu_{t}
-chaotic in the sense of
[
26
]
.

Proof sketch.

Couple
(Z_{i,t})_{i=1}^{N}
with i.i.d. copies
(\bar{Z}_{i,t})_{i=1}^{N}
of the nonlinear process by using the same exogenous noises and an optimal coupling of the interaction step.
Using (
34
) yields a recursion

\mathbb{E}|Z_{i,t+1}-\bar{Z}_{i,t+1}|^{2}\leq C\Big(\mathbb{E}|Z_{i,t}-\bar{Z}_{i,t}|^{2}+\mathbb{E}W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{t}^{N},\mu_{t})\Big).

By exchangeability and the definition of

,

\mathbb{E}W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{t}^{N},\mu_{t})\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}|Z_{i,t}-\bar{Z}_{i,t}|^{2}+\alpha_{N}(T)
.
A discrete Grönwall argument gives (
37
).
The rate (
38
) follows from standard bounds for empirical measures in Wasserstein distance (e.g.
[
15
]
).
∎

Connection to the error of empirical moments.

Since
r_{t}^{(N)}
is a smooth functional of empirical second moments, Lemma
A.4
implies that

|r_{t}^{(N)}-r_{t}|=O_{\mathbb{P}}(N^{-1/2})
on finite horizons, consistent with the numerical scaling in Section
4
.

A.4.
Detailed Derivation of the Variance Dynamics (Proof of Theorem
2.3
)

Here we provide the fully explicit algebraic derivation of the pre-scaling variance
\Lambda^{2}
and the explicit form of the transition function
\Psi
.
Let
\tilde{X}_{t+1}
be the unscaled update variable defined by the bracketed term in the McKean–Vlasov equation (
25
):

(39)

\tilde{X}_{t+1}=\bar{X}_{t}+K_{t}(\bar{\rho}_{t}-\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t}+\omega_{t}-\bar{X}_{t}+\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t})=(1-K_{t})\bar{X}_{t}+K_{t}\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t}+K_{t}(\bar{\rho}_{t}-\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})+K_{t}\omega_{t}.

The post-update scaling factor
L_{t+1}
ensures that the new rating variance remains normalized to a target value
\sigma_{t+1}^{2}
. Specifically,
L_{t+1}=\sigma_{t+1}/\Lambda
, where
\Lambda^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{X}_{t+1})
.

We calculate
\Lambda^{2}
by separating
\tilde{X}_{t+1}
into three uncorrelated components based on the mean-field assumption: the rating component
A
, the skill/noise component
B_{\rho}
, and the observation noise component
B_{\omega}
.
The variance
\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{X}_{t+1})
can be decomposed as:

(40)

\Lambda^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(A)+\mathrm{Var}(B_{\rho})+\mathrm{Var}(B_{\omega})+2\mathrm{Cov}(A,B_{\rho}).

Here
A=(1-K_{t})\bar{X}_{t}+K_{t}\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t}
and
B_{\rho}=K_{t}(\bar{\rho}_{t}-\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})
. The term
K_{t}\omega_{t}
is independent of the other variables, hence its covariance with
A
and
B_{\rho}
is zero.

A.4.1.
1. Rating Terms (
\mathrm{Var}(A)
)

Using the property
\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{X}_{t},\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t})=\eta_{t}\sigma_{t}^{2}
(derived from the kernel
\mathcal{K}_{\eta}
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(A)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}((1-K_{t})\bar{X}_{t}+K_{t}\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t})

\displaystyle=(1-K_{t})^{2}\mathrm{Var}(\bar{X}_{t})+K_{t}^{2}\mathrm{Var}(\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t})+2K_{t}(1-K_{t})\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{X}_{t},\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{t}^{2}\left[(1-K_{t})^{2}+K_{t}^{2}+2K_{t}(1-K_{t})\eta_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\sigma_{t}^{2}\left[1-2K_{t}+2K_{t}^{2}+2K_{t}\eta_{t}-2K_{t}^{2}\eta_{t}\right].

A.4.2.
2. Skill and Noise Terms (
\mathrm{Var}(B_{\rho})+\mathrm{Var}(B_{\omega})
)

First, we establish the required covariance between the skills. Since
\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t}
is conditionally coupled to
\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t}
and
\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t},\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t})=\eta_{t}r_{t}\sigma_{t}
, we have
\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t},\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})=\eta_{t}r_{t}^{2}
.
Using
\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\rho}_{t})=\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})=1
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(B_{\rho})

\displaystyle=K_{t}^{2}\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\rho}_{t}-\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})

\displaystyle=K_{t}^{2}\left[\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\rho}_{t})+\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})-2\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t},\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=K_{t}^{2}\left[1+1-2\eta_{t}r_{t}^{2}\right]=K_{t}^{2}\cdot 2(1-\eta_{t}r_{t}^{2}).

The variance of the observation noise term is
\mathrm{Var}(K_{t}\omega_{t})=K_{t}^{2}\beta^{2}
.
Summing them:

\mathrm{Var}(B_{\rho})+\mathrm{Var}(B_{\omega})=K_{t}^{2}\left[\beta^{2}+2(1-\eta_{t}r_{t}^{2})\right].

A.4.3.
3. Cross-Covariance Terms (
2\mathrm{Cov}(A,B_{\rho})
)

We need
2K_{t}\mathrm{Cov}((1-K_{t})\bar{X}_{t}+K_{t}\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t},\bar{\rho}_{t}-\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})
.
We use the fact that
\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{X}_{t},\bar{\rho}_{t})=r_{t}\sigma_{t}
and
\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{X}_{t},\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})=\eta_{t}r_{t}\sigma_{t}
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(A,\bar{\rho}_{t})

\displaystyle=(1-K_{t})\underbrace{\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{X}_{t},\bar{\rho}_{t})}_{r_{t}\sigma_{t}}+K_{t}\underbrace{\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t},\bar{\rho}_{t})}_{\eta_{t}r_{t}\sigma_{t}}

\displaystyle=r_{t}\sigma_{t}\left[(1-K_{t})+K_{t}\eta_{t}\right].

And similarly for
\mathrm{Cov}(A,\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(A,\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})

\displaystyle=(1-K_{t})\underbrace{\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{X}_{t},\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})}_{\eta_{t}r_{t}\sigma_{t}}+K_{t}\underbrace{\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t},\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})}_{r_{t}\sigma_{t}}

\displaystyle=r_{t}\sigma_{t}\left[(1-K_{t})\eta_{t}+K_{t}\right].

The required covariance is
2K_{t}\left[\mathrm{Cov}(A,\bar{\rho}_{t})-\mathrm{Cov}(A,\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})\right]
:

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Cov}(A,B_{\rho})

\displaystyle=2K_{t}r_{t}\sigma_{t}\left\{\left[(1-K_{t})+K_{t}\eta_{t}\right]-\left[(1-K_{t})\eta_{t}+K_{t}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=2K_{t}r_{t}\sigma_{t}\left\{(1-K_{t})(1-\eta_{t})-K_{t}(1-\eta_{t})\right\}

\displaystyle=2K_{t}(1-\eta_{t})r_{t}\sigma_{t}(1-2K_{t}).

A.4.4.
4. Final Variance and Transition Function

Summing the three components,
\Lambda^{2}=(\mathrm{Var}(A))+(\mathrm{Var}(B_{\rho})+\mathrm{Var}(B_{\omega}))+(2\mathrm{Cov}(A,B_{\rho}))
:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{t}^{2}\left[1-2K_{t}+2K_{t}^{2}+2K_{t}\eta_{t}-2K_{t}^{2}\eta_{t}\right]

\displaystyle+K_{t}^{2}\left[\beta^{2}+2-2\eta_{t}r_{t}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+2K_{t}(1-\eta_{t})r_{t}\sigma_{t}(1-2K_{t}).

This complex expression can be algebraically simplified to yield the form presented in the main text (Eq. (
2.3
)), confirming the result.

The transition map
\Psi
is then derived from the definition of the new correlation
r_{t+1}
:

(41)

r_{t+1}=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t+1},\bar{X}_{t+1})}{\sigma_{\rho,t+1}\sigma_{t+1}}=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t+1},\bar{X}_{t+1})}{\sigma_{t+1}}.

Substituting the update rules
\bar{\rho}_{t+1}=\lambda\bar{\rho}_{t}+\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\xi_{t}
and
\bar{X}_{t+1}=L_{t+1}\tilde{X}_{t+1}
, and using
\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t+1},\bar{X}_{t+1})=\lambda L_{t+1}\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t},\tilde{X}_{t+1})
, we obtain:

(42)

r_{t+1}=\frac{\lambda L_{t+1}\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t},\tilde{X}_{t+1})}{\sigma_{t+1}}.

Since
L_{t+1}=\sigma_{t+1}/\Lambda
, the transition function simplifies to:

(43)

r_{t+1}=\Psi(r_{t})=\frac{\lambda\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t},\tilde{X}_{t+1})}{\Lambda}.

The numerator
\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t},\tilde{X}_{t+1})
is calculated as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t},\tilde{X}_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t},(1-K_{t})\bar{X}_{t}+K_{t}\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t}+K_{t}(\bar{\rho}_{t}-\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t}))

\displaystyle=(1-K_{t})\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t},\bar{X}_{t})+K_{t}\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t},\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t})+K_{t}\left[\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\rho}_{t})-\mathrm{Cov}(\bar{\rho}_{t},\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=(1-K_{t})r_{t}\sigma_{t}+K_{t}\eta_{t}r_{t}\sigma_{t}+K_{t}[1-\eta_{t}r_{t}^{2}].

Substituting this numerator into (
43
) yields the explicit formula for
\Psi(r_{t})
presented in Theorem
2.3
.

A.5.
Optimal Filtering and Proof of Theorem
3.3

This appendix provides a complete proof of Theorem
3.3
. The proof addresses the two
technical points emphasized in the main text: (i) existence (attainment) of the maximizer on the
non-compact domain
(0,\infty)^{2}
, and (ii) uniqueness and interiority of the optimal control pair.
Throughout, fix a current accuracy
r\in(0,1)
and an interaction intensity
\eta\in[0,1)
.

A.5.1.
One-step transition as a ratio of quadratic forms

Recall from Theorem
2.3
that for
K>0
and
\sigma>0
,

(44)

\Psi(r,K,\eta,\sigma)=\lambda\,\frac{\mathcal{N}(K,\sigma)}{\sqrt{\Lambda^{2}(K,\sigma)}},

where the numerator (covariance term) is

(45)

\mathcal{N}(K,\sigma)=r\sigma\bigl(1-K(1-\eta)\bigr)+K\bigl(1-\eta r^{2}\bigr),

and the pre-scaling variance
\Lambda^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{X}_{t+1})
is given explicitly in
Appendix
A.4
. Expanding
\Lambda^{2}
as a polynomial in
K
yields the
quadratic representation

(46)

\Lambda^{2}(K,\sigma)=u(\sigma)+v(\sigma)\,K+w(\sigma)\,K^{2},

with coefficients

(47)

\displaystyle u(\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\sigma^{2},

(48)

\displaystyle v(\sigma)

\displaystyle:=2(1-\eta)\sigma(r-\sigma),

(49)

\displaystyle w(\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\beta^{2}+2(1-r^{2})+2(1-\eta)(\sigma-r)^{2}.

In particular,
u(\sigma)>0
and
w(\sigma)>0
for all
\sigma>0
. Moreover,

(50)

4u(\sigma)w(\sigma)-v(\sigma)^{2}=4\sigma^{2}\Bigl(\beta^{2}+2(1-r^{2})+(1-\eta^{2})(\sigma-r)^{2}\Bigr)>0,

so the quadratic form
u+vK+wK^{2}
is strictly positive for all
K\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma>0
.

For later use, also write the numerator as an affine function of
K
:

(51)

\mathcal{N}(K,\sigma)=p(\sigma)+q(\sigma)\,K,\qquad p(\sigma):=r\sigma,\quad q(\sigma):=1-\eta r^{2}-(1-\eta)r\sigma.

A.5.2.
Maximization over
K
for a fixed
\sigma

Fix
\sigma>0
and define

F_{\sigma}(K):=\frac{\mathcal{N}(K,\sigma)^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}(K,\sigma)}=\frac{(p(\sigma)+q(\sigma)K)^{2}}{u(\sigma)+v(\sigma)K+w(\sigma)K^{2}}.

Since the denominator is strictly positive by (
50
),
F_{\sigma}
is
continuous on
\mathbb{R}
. A direct differentiation gives exactly two stationary points:
one at
K=-p/q
(which makes the numerator zero) and one at

(52)

K^{\sharp}(\sigma)=\frac{-p(\sigma)\,v(\sigma)+2q(\sigma)\,u(\sigma)}{2p(\sigma)\,w(\sigma)-q(\sigma)\,v(\sigma)}.

Evaluating at
K^{\sharp}(\sigma)
yields the global maximum over
K\in\mathbb{R}
:

(53)

\sup_{K\in\mathbb{R}}F_{\sigma}(K)=\frac{4\bigl(p(\sigma)^{2}w(\sigma)-p(\sigma)q(\sigma)v(\sigma)+q(\sigma)^{2}u(\sigma)\bigr)}{4u(\sigma)w(\sigma)-v(\sigma)^{2}}.

Because the denominator in (
53
) is strictly positive
(
50
), the maximizer is unique. In particular, the non-compactness of the
domain in
K
is harmless: for each fixed
\sigma>0
the supremum over
K
is attained at a finite
value
K^{\sharp}(\sigma)
.

Since the one-step problem (
13
) maximizes
\Psi
(equivalently
\Psi^{2}
),
(
44
) implies

(54)

\sup_{K>0,\ \sigma>0}\Psi(r,K,\eta,\sigma)\leq\sup_{\sigma>0}\ \lambda\sqrt{\ \sup_{K\in\mathbb{R}}F_{\sigma}(K)\ }.

Therefore, it suffices to maximize the closed-form upper envelope in
\sigma
; we will see that the
maximizer occurs at
\sigma=r
and at that point the maximizing
K
is strictly positive, so the
inequality in (
54
) becomes an equality and yields the desired optimizer on the
admissible set
K>0
.

A.5.3.
Maximization over
\sigma
and uniqueness of signal-matching

Let
s:=\sigma-r
and write the envelope (
53
) in terms of
s^{2}
.
A direct simplification of (
53
) using (
47
)–(
51
)
gives

(55)

\sup_{K\in\mathbb{R}}F_{\sigma}(K)=\frac{A+B\,s^{2}}{C+D\,s^{2}},\qquad s=\sigma-r,

where

(56)

A:=r^{4}-r^{2}\beta^{2}-1,\quad B:=-(1-\eta^{2})r^{2},\quad C:=2r^{2}-\beta^{2}-2,\quad D:=-(1-\eta^{2}).

Note that
B<0
and
D<0
because
\eta\in[0,1)
and
r\in(0,1)
.

Set
y:=s^{2}\in[0,\infty)
. The right-hand side of (
55
) is a fractional-linear function
in
y
:

G(y):=\frac{A+By}{C+Dy},\qquad y\geq 0.

Its derivative is constant-sign:

(57)

G^{\prime}(y)=\frac{BC-DA}{(C+Dy)^{2}}.

Using (
56
), we compute

BC-DA=-(1-\eta^{2})\bigl(r^{2}C-A\bigr)=-(1-\eta^{2})\bigl((1-r^{2})^{2}\bigr)<0,

where the identity
r^{2}C-A=(1-r^{2})^{2}
is a direct algebraic simplification. Hence
G^{\prime}(y)<0
for all

y\geq 0
, so
G
is strictly decreasing in
y
. Therefore, the envelope

\sup_{K\in\mathbb{R}}F_{\sigma}(K)=G((\sigma-r)^{2})
is uniquely maximized at
y=0
, i.e.

(58)

\sigma^{*}(r)=r.

This establishes
attainment
and
uniqueness
in the
\sigma
-direction on the non-compact
domain
\sigma>0
.

A.5.4.
Optimal gain and invariance

Substituting
\sigma=r
into (
47
)–(
51
) gives

u=r^{2},\qquad v=0,\qquad w=\beta^{2}+2(1-r^{2}),\qquad p=r^{2},\qquad q=1-r^{2},

which are all independent of
\eta
. The maximizing gain (
52
) reduces to

(59)

K^{*}(r)=\frac{1-r^{2}}{\beta^{2}+2(1-r^{2})}=\frac{1-r^{2}}{2(1-r^{2})+\beta^{2}}>0,

and is therefore admissible. Plugging
(K^{*}(r),\sigma^{*}(r))
into (
44
) yields

\displaystyle\Psi\bigl(r,K^{*}(r),\eta,\sigma^{*}(r)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\lambda\,\frac{r^{2}+K^{*}(r)(1-r^{2})}{\sqrt{r^{2}+\bigl(K^{*}(r)\bigr)^{2}\bigl(\beta^{2}+2(1-r^{2})\bigr)}}

\displaystyle=\lambda\sqrt{\,r^{2}+\frac{(1-r^{2})^{2}}{\beta^{2}+2(1-r^{2})}\,}.

This is exactly the invariant map
\Phi
stated in (
15
). In particular, both

K^{*}(r)
and
\Phi(r)
are independent of
\eta
, which proves the invariance principle.

Finally, uniqueness of the optimizer
(K^{*}(r),\sigma^{*}(r))
for
\eta\in[0,1)
follows from:
(i) the strict decrease in
y=(\sigma-r)^{2}
in (
55
) (hence
\sigma=r
is the unique
maximizer), and (ii) for
\sigma=r
the one-variable problem in
K
has a unique maximizer
(
59
) because
\Psi(r,K,\eta,r)^{2}
is a ratio of a strictly convex quadratic to a
positive quadratic with a single interior critical point.

Remark A.6

(On the boundary case
\eta=1
)
.

See Remark
2.1
for the admissible range.
Formally,
\eta=1
corresponds to perfect sorting and can be represented by the degenerate kernel

\mathcal{K}_{1}(x,dx^{\prime})=\delta_{x}(dx^{\prime})
, in which case
(1-\eta^{2})=0
and some density-based expressions
(e.g., those involving
(1-\eta^{2})
) must be interpreted in the limiting sense
\eta\uparrow 1
.
In the platform problem we exclude
\eta=1
because we assume
\lim_{\eta\uparrow 1}C(\eta)=+\infty
.
