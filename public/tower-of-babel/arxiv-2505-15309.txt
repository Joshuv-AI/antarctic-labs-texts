ϕ → 𝟑 ​ 𝝅 \phi\to 3\pi and ϕ ​ 𝝅 𝟎 \phi\pi^{0} transition form factor from Khuri-Treiman equations
A.P. Szczepaniak

Abstract
This work studies the ϕ → 3 ​ π \phi\to 3\pi decay and the ϕ → π 0 ​ γ ∗ \phi\to\pi^{0}\gamma^{\ast} transition form factor, utilizing the Khuri-Treiman formalism to account for analyticity, crossing, and unitarity. Using once-subtracted dispersion relations, we perform a simultaneous fit to the ϕ → 3 ​ π \phi\to 3\pi Dalitz plot distribution and the ϕ → π 0 ​ γ ∗ \phi\to\pi^{0}\gamma^{\ast} measurements from the KLOE collaboration, finding good agreement with these experimental data. These results reaffirm the applicability of the Khuri-Treiman approach in the analysis of three-body decays. An interesting result is that the subtraction constant appearing in the equations is similar to a sum rule expectation, in contrast to analogous studies of ω → 3 ​ π \omega\to 3\pi decays and ω → π 0 ​ γ ∗ \omega\to\pi^{0}\gamma^{\ast} , which shows significant deviations. Our results also provide a reasonable description of the trend of the transition form factor data from BaBar in the e + ​ e − → ϕ ​ π 0 e^{+}e^{-}\to\phi\pi^{0} scattering region. These intriguing theoretical differences between the decays of ϕ \phi and ω \omega could encourage further experimental measurements to assess the discrepancies and refine the theoretical predictions.

Formalism

2.1
Kinematics

We begin by defining the terms and conventions that will be used throughout this text, as well as examining the kinematic implications of the decay process:

\phi(k_{1})\to\pi^{+}(k_{2})\pi^{0}(k_{3})\pi^{-}(k_{4})\,.

(2.1)

where
k_{i}^{\mu}=\left(E_{i},\vec{k}_{i}\right)
represents the four-momentum of the particles involved.

While our main focus centers on the physical decay region, it is simpler to express amplitudes within the framework of scattering, transforming one of the outgoing pions with
k_{i}^{\mu}
into its ingoing antiparticle by changing
k^{\mu}_{i}\to-k^{\mu}_{i}
. In this manner, we now have three scattering processes:

\displaystyle\text{$s$-channel:}

\displaystyle\phi(k_{1})\pi^{0}(-k_{3})\to\pi^{+}(k_{2})\pi^{-}(k_{4})\,,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\text{$t$-channel:}

\displaystyle\phi(k_{1})\pi^{-}(-k_{2})\to\pi^{0}(k_{3})\pi^{-}(k_{4})\,,

\displaystyle\text{$u$-channel:}

\displaystyle\phi(k_{1})\pi^{+}(-k_{4})\to\pi^{+}(k_{2})\pi^{0}(k_{3})\,.

The three processes are identical under the limit of isospin symmetry. Therefore, we will now focus on the
s
-channel by adopting the center of mass reference frame of the two charged pions
\pi^{+}\pi^{-}
, aligning the
z
-axis with the direction of momentum of the incoming particles, which takes the form:

k_{1}=\left(E_{\phi}(s),+p(s)\;\hat{z}\right)\,,\;\;\;-k_{3}=\left(E_{\pi^{0}}(s),-p(s)\;\hat{z}\right)\,,

(2.3)

with
p(s)
the center-of-mass (CoM) momentum and
\hat{z}=\left(0,0,1\right)
. Similarly, the outgoing charged pions have:

k_{2}=\left(E_{\pi^{\pm}}(s),+q(s)\;\hat{q}_{s}\right)\,,\;\;\;k_{4}=\left(E_{\pi^{\pm}}(s),-q(s)\;\hat{q}_{s}\right)\,,

(2.4)

where
q(s)=|\mathbb{q}|
is the modulus of the outgoing momentum and the unit vector
\hat{q}_{s}
is defined as:

\hat{q}_{s}=\left(\sin\theta_{s},0,\cos\theta_{s}\right)\,.

(2.5)

The Mandelstam variables are customarily defined as:

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=(k_{1}+k_{3})^{2}=(k_{2}+k_{4})^{2}\,,

(2.6a)

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=(k_{1}-k_{2})^{2}=(k_{4}-k_{3})^{2}\,,

(2.6b)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=(k_{1}-k_{4})^{2}=(k_{3}-k_{2})^{2}\,,

(2.6c)

\displaystyle s+t+u

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}k_{i}^{\mu}k_{{i\mu}}=M^{2}_{\phi}+m^{2}_{\pi_{0}}+2m^{2}_{\pi^{\pm}}\equiv\Sigma\,.

(2.6d)

The three-momenta
p(s)
and
q(s)
are then given by:

\displaystyle p(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}(s,M^{2}_{\phi},m^{2}_{\pi^{0}})}{2\sqrt{s}}\,,

(2.7a)

\displaystyle q(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}}(s,m^{2}_{\pi^{\pm}},m^{2}_{\pi^{\pm}})}{2\sqrt{s}}\,,

(2.7b)

where we have introduced the Källén (or triangle) function defined by
[
41
]
:

\lambda(x,y,z)=x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-2(xy+xz+yx)\,.

(2.8)

For completeness and later reference, we show here the meson energies in the
\phi\pi^{0}
CoM system, which are:

\displaystyle E_{\phi}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{s+M^{2}_{\phi}-m_{\pi^{0}}^{2}}{2\sqrt{s}}\,,

(2.9a)

\displaystyle E_{\pi^{0}}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{s+m^{2}_{\pi^{0}}-M_{\phi}^{2}}{2\sqrt{s}}\,,

(2.9b)

\displaystyle E_{\pi^{\pm}}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{s}}{2}\,.

(2.9c)

The cosine of the scattering angle
\theta_{s}
is obtained by means of the dot product between Eq. (
2.3
) and Eq.  (
2.4
), giving:

\cos\theta_{s}(s,t)=\frac{2t+s-\Sigma}{4p(s)q(s)}\,.

(2.10)

To obtain
\sin\theta_{s}
, let us consider the axial four-vector known as Kibble vector
[
42
]
:

K_{\mu}=\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}k_{2}^{\nu}k_{3}^{\rho}k_{4}^{\sigma}\,,

(2.11)

where
\epsilon
is the Levi-Civita symbol which is totally antisymmetric in all indices. Noting that one can take
K_{\mu}=\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}(k_{2}^{\nu}+k_{4}^{\nu})k_{3}^{\rho}k_{4}^{\sigma}
, it follows that in the
s
-channel CoM frame:
1
1

1

Note that there is freedom in choosing the direction of the
y
-axis, which is chosen such that the momentum of the
\pi^{+}(k_{2})
has a positive
y
component.

K_{0}=0\,,\qquad\vec{K}=\sqrt{s}p(s)q(s)\sin\theta_{s}\,\hat{y}\,.

(2.12)

Therefore, we have:

\phi(s,t)\equiv-4K_{\mu}K^{\mu}=4sp^{2}(s)q^{2}(s)\sin^{2}\theta_{s}\,,

(2.13)

where we have introduced the invariant function
\phi(s,t)
known as Kibble function. Its value can be computed through the Gram determinant:

\phi(s,t)=-4K_{\mu}K^{\mu}=4\begin{vmatrix}k_{3\mu}^{2}&k_{3\mu}k_{2}^{\mu}&k_{3\mu}k_{4}^{\mu}\\
k_{2\mu}k_{3}^{\mu}&k_{2\mu}^{2}&k_{2\mu}k_{4}^{\mu}\\
k_{4\mu}k_{3}^{\mu}&k_{4\mu}k_{2}^{\mu}&k_{4\mu}^{2}\\
\end{vmatrix}\,,

which gives:

\phi(s,t)=stu-s\left(M^{2}_{\phi}-m^{2}_{\pi^{\pm}}\right)\left(m^{2}_{\pi^{0}}-m^{2}_{\pi^{\pm}}\right)-m^{2}_{\pi^{\pm}}\left(M^{2}_{\phi}-m^{2}_{\pi^{0}}\right)^{2}\,.

(2.14)

Note that the boundaries of the physical regions of the process are defined through
\phi(s,t)=0
.
Finally, using Eq. (
2.13
) we obtain
\sin\theta_{s}
as:

\sin\theta_{s}=\frac{\sqrt{\phi(s,t)}}{2\sqrt{s}p(s)q(s)}\,.

(2.15)

There is a small symmetry breaking between the
s
- and
t,u
- channels due to the mass difference
\Delta m=m_{\pi^{\pm}}-m_{\pi^{0}}\approx(139.57-134.98)\,\text{MeV}=4.59\,\text{MeV}
, which is practically irrelevant for the development of amplitudes using Khuri-Treiman equations.
2
2

2

While isospin breaking due to pion masses is not relevant, we will take into account in an effective manner the isospin breaking due to the appearance of
\rho^{0}
-
\omega
mixing in the
\pi^{+}\pi^{-}
channel, which is essential to properly describe the
\phi\to\pi^{+}\pi^{-}\pi^{0}
DP.
Hence, for the rest of the manuscript, we will work in the isospin limit
m_{\pi^{0}}=m_{\pi^{\pm}}
, where we take as reference the mass of the charged pion
m\equiv m_{\pi^{\pm}}
, which allows us to take the results of the
s
-channel as a representative of the three channels.

2.2
\phi\to 3\pi
amplitude from Khuri–Treiman equations

The amplitude of our process Eq. (
2.1
) can be given in terms of helicity amplitudes
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}(s,t,u)
, which, in turn, depend on a Lorentz-invariant amplitude
F(s,t,u)
:

\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}(s,t,u)=i\epsilon_{\lambda}^{\mu}(k_{1})K_{\mu}F(s,t,u)\,,

(2.16)

where
\epsilon_{\lambda}^{\mu}
is the polarization tensor of the
\phi
meson with helicity
\lambda
and
K_{\mu}
is the Kibble vector in Eq. (
2.11
).
The
\phi
meson has three possible helicity states
\lambda=-1,0,+1
. Thus, the differential decay width
\frac{d^{2}\Gamma}{dsdt}(s,t)
is calculated by averaging over the three helicity states:

\frac{d^{2}\Gamma}{dsdt}(s,t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{32M^{3}_{\phi}}|M(s,t)|^{2}\,,

(2.17)

where:

|M(s,t)|^{2}=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{\lambda}|\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}|^{2}=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{\lambda}\epsilon^{\mu}_{\lambda}(k_{1})K_{\mu}\ \left[K_{\nu}\epsilon^{\nu}_{\lambda}(k_{1})\right]^{*}|F(s,t,u)|^{2}\,.

(2.18)

The right-hand side of Eq. (
2.18
) can be computed using:

\sum_{\lambda}\epsilon^{\mu}_{\lambda}(k_{1})\left(\epsilon^{\nu}_{\lambda}(k_{1})\right)^{*}=-g^{\mu\nu}+\frac{k_{1}^{\mu}k_{1}^{\nu}}{M^{2}_{\phi}}\,,

(2.19)

so that Eq. (
2.18
) becomes:

\left\lvert M(s,t)\right\rvert^{2}=\frac{1}{3}(-K_{\mu}K^{\mu})|F(s,t,u)|^{2}=\frac{1}{3}\frac{\phi(s,t)}{4}|F(s,t,u)|^{2}\,,

(2.20)

where we have used the fact that
k_{1}^{\mu}K_{\mu}=0
.
Now, the Kibble function Eq. (
2.14
) must be computed in the isospin limit:

\phi(s,t)=stu-m^{2}(M_{\phi}^{2}-m^{2})^{2}\,,

(2.21)

which gives us the limits for
s
in the physical decay region by solving
\phi(s,t,u)=0
:

s_{\text{min}}=4m^{2}\,,\qquad s_{\text{max}}=(M_{\phi}-m)^{2}\,,

(2.22)

while the limits of
t(s)
can be obtained from Eq. (
2.10
) taking
\cos\theta_{s}=\pm 1
:

t_{\pm}(s)=\frac{1}{2}\left[M^{2}_{\phi}+3m^{2}-s\right]\pm 2p(s)q(s)\,.

(2.23)

Note that all the dynamics of the decay problem are encoded in the function
F(s,t,u)
. Its form can be deduced considering that the helicity amplitude Eq. (
2.16
) can be decomposed into helicity partial waves amplitudes as:

\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}(s,t,u)=\sum_{J\;\text{odd}}^{\infty}(2J+1)d^{J}_{\lambda 0}(\theta_{s})h^{J}_{\lambda}(s)\,,

(2.24)

where
d^{J}_{\lambda 0}
are the Wigner
d
-functions and
\theta_{s}
is the angle given by Eqs. (
2.10
) and (
2.15
). For our decay
\phi\to 3\pi
,
\mathcal{H}_{0}=0
and
\mathcal{H}_{+}=\mathcal{H}_{-}
, due to parity. This formula allows to write the partial wave expansion for the invariant amplitude
F(s,t,u)
as:

F(s,t,u)=\sum_{J\;\text{odd}}^{\infty}\left(p(s)q(s)\right)^{J-1}P^{\prime}_{J}(\cos\theta_{s})f_{J}(s)\,,

(2.25)

where
P^{\prime}_{J}(z)
is the derivative of the Legendre polynomial, and with
f_{J}(s)
the kinematic-singularity-free partial wave amplitude for total angular momentum
J
of the two-pion system, which has the form:

f_{J}(s)\equiv\sqrt{\frac{2}{s}}\frac{2J+1}{\sqrt{J(J+1)}}\frac{h_{+}^{J}(s)}{\left(p(s)q(s)\right)^{J}}\,.

(2.26)

In general, Eq. (
2.25
) is an infinite sum of partial waves with the expansion performed in the
s
-channel, and with
f_{J}(s)
carrying both left- and right-hand cuts (LHC and RHC, respectively). In practice, however, a truncation to a finite number of partial waves is necessary when modeling processes at low energies. Such truncation introduces a violation of the analytical properties of the crossed
t
and
u
channels. The KT formalism partially recovers these properties by replacing the infinite sum of partial waves in the
s
-channel with three truncated sums of single-Mandelstam-variable functions, the so-called isobar amplitudes, one for each
s,t
and
u
channels. The KT representation of the amplitude then reads:

\displaystyle F(s,t,u)=

\displaystyle\sum_{J\;\text{odd}}^{J_{\text{max}}}(2J+1)P^{\prime}_{J}(\cos\theta_{s})F_{J}(s)+

(2.27)

\displaystyle\sum_{J\;\text{odd}}^{J_{\text{max}}}(2J+1)P^{\prime}_{J}(\cos\theta_{t})F_{J}(t)+\sum_{J\;\text{odd}}^{J_{\text{max}}}(2J+1)P^{\prime}_{J}(\cos\theta_{u})F_{J}(u)\,.

Note that because the amplitude
F(s,t,u)
must be symmetric in
s
,
t
, and
u
, the truncation is performed at the same
J_{\text{max}}
for each isobar expansion. Truncating each sum at
J_{\text{max}}=1
(and omitting the subindex
J
in
F_{J}(s)
), we obtain the following isobar decomposition
[
19
,
20
,
24
,
32
]
:

F(s,t,u)=F(s)+F(t)+F(u)\,,

(2.28)

where each isobar amplitude,
F(x)
, has only a RHC in its corresponding Mandelstam variable. In the previous section, we demonstrated that the three channels are equivalent in the isospin limit, so we only need to calculate
F(s)
. Projecting Eq. (
2.28
) into the
s
-channel partial wave, we obtain the relation between
f_{1}(s)
and
F(s)
:

f_{1}(s)=F(s)+\widehat{F}(s)\,,

(2.29)

where:

\widehat{F}(s)\equiv\frac{3}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-z^{2}_{s})F(t(s,z_{s}))\;dz_{s}\,,

(2.30)

is known as the inhomogeneity and contains the
s
-channel projection of the contributions due to the
t
- and
u
-channels.

The contribution to the generalized unitarity relation for
F(s)
from
\pi\pi
intermediate states reads
[
17
,
43
]
:

\Delta F(s)\equiv F(s+i\epsilon)-F(s-i\epsilon)=2i(F(s)+\widehat{F}(s))\sin\delta(s)e^{-i\delta(s)}\theta(s-4m^{2})\,,

(2.31)

where
\delta(s)
is the
P
-wave
\pi\pi
phase shift. Note that we have assumed that there are only two-pion intermediate states for the
\phi\to 3\pi
decay. An unsubtracted dispersion relation (UDR) for
F(s)
can be written from Eq. (
2.31
) as:

F(s)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{4m^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{\Delta F(s^{\prime})}{s^{\prime}-s}\,ds^{\prime}\,.

(2.32)

Its solution can be written as:

F(s)=\Omega(s)\left(a+\frac{s}{\pi}\int_{4m^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{ds^{\prime}}{s^{\prime}}\frac{\sin\delta(s^{\prime})\widehat{F}(s^{\prime})}{\left\lvert\Omega(s^{\prime})\right\rvert(s^{\prime}-s)}\right)\,,

(2.33)

where
\Omega(s)
is the well-known Omnès function
[
44
]
:

\Omega(s)=\exp\left[\frac{s}{\pi}\int_{4m^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{\delta(s^{\prime})}{s^{\prime}(s^{\prime}-s)}ds^{\prime}\right]\,,

(2.34)

which is also the solution of Eq. (
2.31
) taking
\widehat{F}(s)=0
, hence the name inhomogeneity for
\widehat{F}(s)
. The subtraction constant
a
is in general complex,
i.e

a=|a|e^{i\phi_{a}}
. While
|a|
can be fixed from the measured
\phi\to 3\pi
decay width, no observable is sensitive to
\phi_{a}
. Therefore, we can simply set
\phi_{a}=0
and take
a
to be a real overall normalization of the amplitude.

One can also write a once-subtracted dispersion relation (ODR) for
F(s)
from Eq. (
2.31
) as:

F(s)=F(0)+\frac{s}{2\pi i}\int_{4m^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{\Delta F(s^{\prime})}{s^{\prime}(s^{\prime}-s)}ds^{\prime}\,,

(2.35)

with solution:

F(s)=\Omega(s)\left(a+b^{\prime}s+\frac{s^{2}}{\pi}\int_{4m^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{ds^{\prime}}{s^{\prime 2}}\frac{\sin\delta(s^{\prime})\widehat{F}(s^{\prime})}{\left\lvert\Omega(s^{\prime})\right\rvert(s^{\prime}-s)}\right)\,,

(2.36)

The subtraction constants
a
and
b^{\prime}
enter linearly into Eq. (
2.36
), and hence
F(s)
can be decomposed into a linear combination of two functions
F_{a}(s)
and
F_{b}(s)
:

F(s)=a\,F_{a}(s)+b^{\prime}\,F_{b}(s)=a\left(F_{a}(s)+b\,F_{b}(s)\right)\,,

(2.37)

where
b=b^{\prime}/a
. The
F_{a}
and
F_{b}
functions are:

\displaystyle F_{a}(s)=\Omega(s)\left(1+\frac{s^{2}}{\pi}\int_{4m^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(s^{\prime})^{2}}\frac{\sin\delta(s^{\prime})\hat{F_{a}}(s^{\prime})}{|\Omega(s^{\prime})|(s^{\prime}-s)}ds^{\prime}\right)\,,

(2.38a)

\displaystyle F_{b}(s)=\Omega(s)\left(s+\frac{s^{2}}{\pi}\int_{4m^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(s^{\prime})^{2}}\frac{\sin\delta(s^{\prime})\hat{F_{b}}(s^{\prime})}{|\Omega(s^{\prime})|(s^{\prime}-s)}ds^{\prime}\right)\,,

(2.38b)

which are independent solutions of Eq. (
2.35
). In Eqs. (
2.38
), the functions
\widehat{F}_{a,b}(s)
are obtained as in Eq. (
2.30
) with the change
F(s)\to F_{a,b}(s)
. The advantage of using these functions is that they only need to be calculated once since they do not depend on

and

. They can be solved iteratively, with rapid convergence, as shown in Fig.

Additionally, due to the high mass of the
\phi
meson and to the isospin breaking effects, in the
s

\rho^{0}
and the
\omega
mesons. Therefore, we have to introduce an additional term in the

2.28

\omega

[
2

F(s)\;\;\to\;\;F(s)+A\frac{M^{2}_{\omega}}{M^{2}_{\omega}-s-i\sqrt{s}\Gamma_{\omega}}\,,

(2.41)

where
A
is a complex coupling constant. This term is added after the iterative solution is obtained.

2.3
\phi\to\pi^{0}\gamma^{*}
transition form factor

We introduce here the
\phi\pi^{0}
TFF
f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s)
, which controls the
\phi\to\pi^{0}\gamma^{*}
amplitude. The dispersive representations of
f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s)
are determined by the following unitarity relation:

\Delta f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s)\equiv f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s+i\epsilon)-f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s-i\epsilon)=i\frac{p^{3}(s)}{6\pi\sqrt{s}}{F^{V}_{\pi}(s)}^{\ast}f_{1}(s)\theta(s-4m^{2})\,,

(2.42)

where one can see that the discontinuity only affects the RHC. Only the two charged pions annihilation has been taken into account in Eq. (
2.42
), where
f_{1}(s)
can be obtained from KT equations as in Eqs. (
2.29
) and (
2.30
). Here, in addition, one can approximate the pion vector form factor
F^{V}_{\pi}(s)
by the Omnès function
\Omega(s)
because of the relatively low invariant mass of the
\phi\pi^{0}
system explored in this work. In analogy to and for the sake of comparison with Ref.
[
35
]
, we write down a once-subtracted dispersion relation for
f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s)
as:

f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s)=\left\lvert f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(0)\right\rvert e^{i\theta_{\phi\pi^{0}}(0)}+\frac{s}{12\pi^{2}}\int_{4m^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(s^{\prime})^{3}}}\frac{p^{3}(s^{\prime}){\Omega(s^{\prime})}^{\ast}f_{1}(s^{\prime})}{(s^{\prime}-s)}ds^{\prime}\,,

(2.43)

where the complex phase
\theta_{\phi\pi^{0}}(0)
generally cannot vanish because we have imposed that the overall normalization constant
a
in the amplitude in Eq. (
2.33
) is real. Finally, we note that the modulus of the TFF at zero
|f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(0)|
is directly related to the experimental decay width for
\phi\to\pi^{0}\gamma
:

\Gamma_{\phi\to\pi^{0}\gamma}=\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi}\frac{\left(M^{2}_{\phi}-m^{2}_{\pi^{0}}\right)^{3}}{24M^{3}_{\phi}}\left\lvert f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(0)\right\rvert^{2}\,.

(2.44)

Results

3.1
Experimental information

In our approach we have a total of eight real parameters to fit. From the
\phi\to 3\pi
amplitude obtained from KT equations [
cf.
Eq. (
2.37
)] we have a real constant
a
and a complex value
b=b_{r}+ib_{i}
. The additional
\omega
-
\rho^{0}
mixing introduces another complex constant
A
[
cf.
Eq. (
2.41
)]. We require another real parameter
N
, which serves as an adjustable parameter for the total number of events in the
\phi\to 3\pi
DP distribution (
cf.
Eq. (
3.4
) below). Finally, from the TFF [
cf.
Eq. (
2.43
)], we obtain two additional parameters:
|f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(0)|
and
\theta_{\phi\pi^{0}}(0)
. To determine these unknown constants, we will use the following experimental information:

a)

Measurement of
\phi\to 3\pi
decay DP
[
2
]
and transition form factor
|f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s)/f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(0)|^{2}
by the KLOE collaboration
[
3
,
5
]
.

b)

The decay widths
\phi\to 3\pi
and
\phi\to\pi^{0}\gamma^{\ast}
, for which we take the PDG values
[
45
]
:
\Gamma_{\phi}=4.249\pm 0.013\,
, the branching ratios
\mathcal{B}(\phi\to 3\pi)=15.4\pm 0.4\ \%
and
\mathcal{B}(\phi\to\pi^{0}\gamma)=0.132\pm 0.05\ \%
.

c)

The rest mass
M_{\omega}=782.66\,
and the decay width
\Gamma_{\omega}=8.68\,
of the PDG.

To fit our parameters, we define a global
\chi^{2}
function, which takes the form:

\chi^{2}\equiv\chi^{2}_{\text{DP}}+\chi^{2}_{\Gamma_{3\pi}}+\chi^{2}_{\text{TFF}}+\chi^{2}_{\Gamma_{\pi^{0}\gamma^{\ast}}}\,.

(3.1)

The function
\chi^{2}_{\text{DP}}
refers to the fit of the DP distribution mentioned above, its expression being given by:

\chi^{2}_{\text{DP}}=\sum_{i}\left(\frac{N^{\text{exp}}_{\text{ev},i}-N^{\text{th}}_{\text{ev},i}}{\sigma_{N^{\text{exp}}_{\text{ev},i}}}\right)^{2}\,.

(3.2)

The DP is divided into bins, each one having the experimental number of events
N^{\text{exp}}_{\text{ev},i}
(and its associated error
\sigma_{N^{\text{exp}}_{\text{ev},i}}
), measured in a square region of the phase space with semi-width
\Delta X
=
\Delta Y=8.75\,\text{MeV}
and whose center is given by the parameters
X
and
Y
defined in terms of
s
and
t
by:

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\frac{2t+s-\Sigma}{2M_{\phi}}\,,

(3.3a)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=-\frac{s-(M_{\phi}-m_{\pi^{0}})^{2}}{2M_{\phi}}\,.

(3.3b)

In order to compute the number of events in each bin from the theoretical differential decay width Eq. (
2.17
), we write:

N^{\text{th}}_{\text{ev},i}=N\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\text{th}}_{3\pi,i}}{\Gamma^{\text{th}}_{3\pi}}\right)=\frac{N}{(2\pi)^{3}384M^{3}_{\phi}\Gamma^{\text{th}}_{3\pi}}\int_{s_{i}-\Delta s}^{s_{i}+\Delta s}\int_{t_{i}-\frac{(s-s_{i})-\Delta t}{2}}^{t_{i}+\frac{(s-s_{i})+\Delta t}{2}}\phi(s,t)\left|F(s,t)\right|^{2}\,dt\,ds\,,

(3.4)

where we have defined
\Delta s=\Delta t=8.75\,M_{\phi}=\Delta X\,M_{\phi}
. Note that each square bin in the
(X,Y)
-plane transforms into a parallelogram in the
(s,t)
-plane, as reflected in the
s
and
t
integration limits in Eq. (
3.4
). Also note that, as stated above, we have introduced
N
, which corresponds to the total number of theoretical events, as a fitting parameter, not as an input.

We also fit the
\phi\to 3\pi
total decay width, which corresponds to the following integral over the whole phase space:

\Gamma^{\text{th}}_{3\pi}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}384M^{3}_{\phi}}\int_{4m^{2}}^{(M_{\phi}-m)^{2}}\int_{t_{-}(s)}^{t_{+}(s)}\phi(s,t)\left|F(s,t)\right|^{2}\,dt\,ds\,,

(3.5)

for which fit we define the following
\chi^{2}_{\Gamma_{3\pi}}
function:

\chi^{2}_{\Gamma_{3\pi}}=\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\text{exp}}_{3\pi}-\Gamma^{\text{th}}_{3\pi}}{\sigma_{\Gamma^{\text{exp}}_{3\pi}}}\right)^{2}\,,

(3.6)

with
\Gamma^{\text{exp}}_{3\pi}=\Gamma_{\phi}\,\mathcal{B}(\phi\to 3\pi)
and
\sigma_{\Gamma^{\text{exp}}_{3\pi}}=\Gamma^{\text{exp}}_{3\pi}\,\sqrt{\left(\frac{\Delta\mathcal{B}(\phi\to 3\pi)}{\mathcal{B}(\phi\to 3\pi)}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\Delta\Gamma_{\phi}}{\Gamma_{\phi}}\right)^{2}}
.

To fit the TFF [
cf.
Eq. (
2.43
)], we define the
\chi^{2}
-square function
\chi^{2}_{\text{TFF}}
as:

\chi^{2}_{\text{TFF}}=\sum_{i}\left(\frac{\left|F_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s_{i})\right|^{2}_{\text{exp}}-\left|F_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s_{i})\right|^{2}_{\text{th}}}{\sigma^{\text{TFF}}_{i}}\right)^{2}\,,

(3.7)

where:

F_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s)=\frac{f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s)}{f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(0)}

(3.8)

is the normalized TFF.
\left|F_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s)\right|_{\text{exp}}
and
\sigma^{\text{TFF}}_{i}
are the experimental normalized TFF and its error, respectively, provided by the data in Ref.
[
3
]
.
\left|F_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s)\right|^{2}_{\text{th}}
is the average in each range
\left[\sqrt{s}-\Delta\sqrt{s},\sqrt{s}+\Delta\sqrt{s}\right]
of our normalized transition form factor:

\left|F_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s_{i})\right|^{2}_{\text{th}}=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{s_{i}}\Delta\sqrt{s_{i}}}\int_{(\sqrt{s_{i}}-\Delta\sqrt{s_{i}})^{2}}^{(\sqrt{s_{i}}+\Delta\sqrt{s_{i}})^{2}}\left|F_{\phi\pi^{0}}(s)\right|^{2}ds\,,

(3.9)

Finally, we also fit the
\phi\to\pi^{0}\gamma^{\ast}
decay width, involving the quantity
f_{\phi\pi^{0}}(0)
, for which we define
\chi^{2}_{\Gamma_{\phi\pi^{0}}}
as:

\chi^{2}_{\Gamma_{\phi\pi^{0}}}=\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\text{exp}}_{\phi\pi^{0}}-\Gamma^{\text{th}}_{\phi\pi^{0}}}{\sigma_{\Gamma^{\text{exp}}_{\phi\pi^{0}}}}\right)^{2}\,,

(3.10)

with
\Gamma^{\text{exp}}_{\phi\pi^{0}}=\Gamma_{\phi}\mathcal{B}(\phi\to\pi^{0}\gamma^{\ast})
,
\sigma_{\Gamma^{\text{exp}}_{\phi\pi^{0}}}=\Gamma^{\text{exp}}_{\phi\pi^{0}}\sqrt{\left(\frac{\Delta\mathcal{B}(\phi\to\pi^{0}\gamma^{\ast})}{\mathcal{B}(\phi\to\pi^{0}\gamma^{\ast})}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\Delta\Gamma_{\phi}}{\Gamma_{\phi}}\right)^{2}}
and
\Gamma^{\text{th}}_{\phi\pi^{0}}
given by Eq. (
2.44
).

4
Summary and Outlook

In this work, we analyze the
\phi\to 3\pi
decay and the
\phi\to\pi^{0}\gamma^{\ast}
transition form factor using the Khuri-Treiman formalism. This consistent framework incorporates crossing and maintains analyticity and unitarity. The model successfully reproduces the KLOE data for the Dalitz plot and the transition form factor, utilizing once-subtracted dispersion relations instead of unsubtracted ones. Notably, while our analysis of the transition form factor focuses on the decay region, the model also captures the trend in the scattering regime (
e^{+}e^{-}\to\phi\pi^{0}
), suggesting its potential for broader applications. These results reinforce the applicability of the framework to three-body decays. A comparison with the analogous
\omega\to 3\pi
case, previously analyzed with the same framework, reveals a striking difference, particularly in the value of the subtraction constant introduced in the Khuri-Treiman equations. In the
\phi
case, the fitted value is close to the one determined by the sum rule [
cf.
Eq. (
2.39
)], which would reduce the once-subtracted dispersion relation to an unsubtracted form. This sharply contrasts with the
\omega
case, where the fitted value significantly deviates from the sum rule value. As discussed in Ref.
[
35
]
, this deviation is primarily driven by the transition form factor high-energy data points from the NA60 collaboration. Additional high-precision data, especially for the
\omega
decay, will be crucial for refining the theoretical framework and enhancing our understanding of these processes. These intriguing theoretical differences should encourage and stimulate new experimental analyses.
