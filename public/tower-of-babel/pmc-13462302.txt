Effectiveness of Virtual Exercise Intervention in Adults With Major Depressive Disorder: A Systematic Review
Authors: Radietya Alvarabie, Elissa Burjawi, Junhua Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundVirtual reality‐based exercise is an emerging tool in mental health care; however, its application and effectiveness in treating Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) remain unclear. This systematic review evaluated the effectiveness of virtual exercise interventions for adults with MDD, focusing on depressive symptoms, functional outcomes, and the certainty of the available evidence.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 guidelines, four databases (PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, AMED) were systematically searched for studies evaluating virtual exercise interventions in adults with MDD. Two reviewers independently performed study selection, data extraction, methodological quality assessment using Cochrane's Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) and Risk of Bias in Non‐randomized Studies (ROBINS‐I) tools. Certainty of evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework.ResultsOf the 603 articles, four studies met the inclusion criteria, evaluating the VRelax, Pacifica, and CORE interventions. Pacifica and CORE demonstrated moderate improvements in depressive symptoms compared with control groups, while VRelax primarily improved stress and negative affect. The interventions were generally well accepted, with high participant engagement and adherence. Overall certainty of evidence ranged from moderate to low‐moderate and was limited by relatively small sample sizes, methodological limitations, and short follow‐up periods.ConclusionsVirtual exercise intervention may represent a promising adjunct to conventional therapy for MDD. Although current evidence supports improvements in depressive symptoms and emotional well‐being, further randomized controlled trials with larger samples, longer follow‐up, and objective neurobiological outcome measures are needed to confirm long‐term efficacy and clinical applicability.Keywords: depression, digital exercise, digital health, major depressive disorder, virtual exercise, virtual reality

Introduction

Major Depressive Disorder and Treatment Challenges

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a leading global cause of disability, affecting over 280 million people worldwide [1]. In Australia, epidemiological data suggest that approximately one in seven individuals will experience a depressive episode at some point during their lifetime [2]. MDD is marked by persistent low mood, anhedonia, cognitive impairment, and functional disruption. Despite various treatment options, managing MDD remains challenging. Current first‐line therapies predominantly include pharmacological interventions (e.g., selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors [SSRIs], serotonin‐norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors [SNRIs]) and psychological interventions (e.g., cognitive behavioral therapy [CBT], interpersonal therapy). Although these treatments are effective for many, their overall success is modest when considering the heterogeneous nature of MDD. Approximately 30%–50% of patients do not achieve full remission with standard treatments [3]. Treatment‐resistant depression is common, and pharmacological side effects and barriers to psychological therapy (e.g., stigma, cost, access) often hinder adherence and outcomes. High dropout rates, partial responses, and residual symptoms are common issues in clinical practice [4]. Given these limitations, there is growing interest in non‐pharmacological adjuncts that enhance quality of life, functional recovery, and self‐efficacy. Interventions promoting physical, cognitive, and emotional resilience may better support long‐term recovery, especially in chronic or treatment‐resistant cases.

Exercise Therapy for MDD

Among non‐pharmacological interventions, exercise is widely used as a promising and evidence‐supported strategy for MDD management. A substantial body of research, including systematic reviews and meta‐analyses, confirms that structured aerobic, resistance, and mind‐body exercise programs lead to significant reductions in depressive symptoms, with effect sizes comparable to those observed with antidepressant medication and psychotherapies [5, 6]. Exercise exerts therapeutic effects through multiple physiological and neurobiological mechanisms, including upregulation of brain‐derived neurotrophic factors, enhanced neuroplasticity, regulation of hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal axis activity, reduced inflammation, and improvement in metabolic function [7]. In addition to ameliorating mood symptoms, exercise interventions contribute to improvements in cognitive performance, stress resilience, sleep quality, and social connectedness, making them ideal candidates for holistic MDD management. However, despite its proven efficacy, engagement with exercise remains a major challenge for individuals with MDD. Common barriers include low motivation, fatigue, anhedonia, self‐perceived incompetence, and limited access to structured exercise environments [8]. Moreover, patients with more severe symptomatology or comorbid physical illnesses may find conventional exercise programs intimidating or inaccessible. To address these challenges, technology‐assisted and virtual exercise formats, such as virtual reality (VR)‐based workouts, exergaming, and mobile apps, could offer engaging, adaptable, and accessible alternatives. These immersive platforms can enhance adherence, stimulate neurocognitive engagement, and serve as effective adjuncts to conventional treatment, supporting both symptom relief and functional recovery.

Virtual Exercise as an Emerging Alternative

Virtual or digital exercise is an emerging tool in mental health care and management. Virtual exercise is a type of physical activity that is guided, enhanced, or simulated through digital or virtual environments or platforms [9]. It usually involves the use of computer‐generated exercise programs/settings that are delivered via interactive virtual platforms to facilitate, motivate, or replicate physical movement and training. These exercises can be performed individually or in groups, often incorporating real‐time feedback and gamification elements [10]. VR provides immersive, interactive environments that can enhance emotional engagement, increase treatment adherence, and simulate real‐world experiences in a safe and controlled setting [11]. Thus, when integrated with physical activity, virtual exercise may offer the added advantage of incorporating movement and embodied cognition—both of which have been shown to produce neurobiological and psychological benefits relevant to depression recovery [6].

Virtual exercise, broadly defined, incorporates digital platforms and immersive technologies to simulate or replicate physical exercise in controlled and often home‐based environments [9]. Though research is limited, examples include mobile health apps with guided workouts or mood tracking (e.g., Pacifica, CORE), gamified exergames (e.g., Nintendo Wii Fit, VR boxing), and VR fitness or relaxation environments (e.g., VRelax) [12]. These tools have shown benefits for mood, stress, anxiety, and motivation in individuals with mental health conditions [11, 13]. Although these interventions differ in their degree of physical engagement, cognitive involvement, and immersive experience, they share the common goal of improving mental health through digitally supported therapeutic activity.

Rationale for Virtual Exercise as a Therapy for Depression

MDD affects mood, cognition, motivation, and physical functioning, requiring treatments that address broader aspects of well‐being. Physical exercise is one such intervention, with well‐established antidepressant effects and recognized benefits for quality of life, cognitive function, and neuroplasticity [5, 6]. However, individuals with MDD often face barriers such as fatigue, low motivation, and limited access to safe environments [8]. To address this gap, virtual exercise, delivered through technologies like VR, mobile apps, and exergaming, offers flexible, engaging alternatives tailored to individual needs. These platforms simulate real‐world activities, provide structured guidance, and deliver real‐time feedback, enhancing motivation and accessibility [11].

Beyond convenience, virtual exercise could also activate embodied cognition by integrating motor, emotional, and cognitive processes. This can stimulate reward systems and promote neurobiological changes relevant to depression recovery [14]. Features such as real‐time feedback, environmental stimulation, and goal‐oriented tasks may stimulate reward systems, modulate emotional processing networks, and promote functional reorganization in brain regions implicated in MDD [11]. The combination of cognitive engagement, physical activation, and digital accessibility may make virtual exercise uniquely suited to address both the physiological and psychological dimensions of depression, suggesting that virtual exercise could serve as an effective and scalable therapeutic adjunct in the treatment of MDD. Despite these potential advantages of virtual exercise interventions, there remain comparatively fewer papers than in conventional modalities exploring its effectiveness on the depressive symptoms of adults who are diagnosed with MDD. Although numerous systematic reviews have established the benefits of conventional exercise for MDD, there have been no recent reviews that have specifically investigated the effectiveness, user engagement, and certainty of evidence of virtual exercise interventions in adults diagnosed with MDD. While two recently published reviews have examined the effects of virtual exercise on depressive symptoms, they have included healthy participants or individuals living with conditions other than MDD; hence, limiting the applicability of their findings to this clinical population.

Study Aim and Hypothesis

This systematic review aimed to synthesize the existing evidence on the effectiveness of virtual exercise interventions as a new strategy to reduce depressive symptoms in adults with MDD. It specifically aimed to address the aforementioned literature gap by investigating the following research question: among adults with MDD (population), can virtual exercise interventions delivered through digital or immersive technologies (intervention), compared with usual care, non‐virtual interventions, or no intervention (comparator), improve depressive symptoms and other clinically relevant outcomes (outcomes)? Based on the current understanding regarding the therapeutic effects of exercise and virtual modalities, it is hypothesized that virtual exercise interventions may be associated with a reduction in depressive symptoms and an enhancement in functional outcomes compared with control conditions or baseline assessments. Findings from this review may inform future research and contribute to the development of personalized, evidence‐based, and technology‐driven therapeutic interventions for MDD.

Methods

Study Design

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [15] and was pre‐registered with the Open Science Framework before study initiation (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/398JM). As this review used published data, no research ethics approval or informed consent was required for this study.

Search Strategy and Databases

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across four electronic databases, including Medline/PubMed, Embase, CINAHL (via EBSCO), and AMED (via Ovid) from database inception to identify all studies that have been published up to the research date (31 December 2025). The key search terms included “major depressive disorder,” “virtual,” and “exercise,” which were combined using relevant Boolean operators (OR, AND) and adapted for each database (Table S1). Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and truncation of the terms were considered wherever possible to capture search‐term variations. There were no filters or restrictions applied; however, only articles published in English were accepted. The complete search strategy for each database is available in the Supplementary Information file. Moreover, the reference lists of all relevant articles were manually reviewed to identify additional eligible studies.

Eligibility Criteria

Articles were included if they: (1) recruited participants aged ≥ 18 years who were diagnosed with MDD or other depressive disorders using validated diagnostic criteria, such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders‐5 or International Classification of Diseases‐10; (2) evaluated digital or virtual interventions delivered via mobile applications, web‐based platforms, VR, immersive environments, or other digital technologies; (3) aimed to improve depressive symptoms or related psychological outcomes; (4) reported on at least one quantitative measure related to depressive symptoms, anxiety, stress, quality of life (QoL), functional outcomes, or neurobiological outcomes; and (5) applied a randomized controlled trial (RCT) or quasi‐experimental study design with or without a comparator, including no intervention, usual care, waitlist, or non‐virtual intervention.

Articles were excluded if they did not meet any of the inclusion criteria or if they were: (1) reviews, meta‐analyses, reports, conference abstracts, editorials, dissertations, posters, or study protocols; (2) not written in English; (3) not published in a peer‐reviewed scientific journal; or (4) not available via institutional subscription.

Study Selection

All search results were imported into EndNote (Clarivate) for duplicate removal and subsequently exported to Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation) for screening. Two reviewers independently performed the study selection process using a double‐blinded approach based on the predefined eligibility criteria. During preliminary screening phase, titles and abstracts were assessed to identify eligible studies. Articles that met the inclusion criteria underwent full‐text assessment, and only the studies that fulfilled all eligibility requirements were included in the final review. Any disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer.

Data Extraction

Data from the included studies were extracted by two reviewers independently using a standardized data extraction form. The extracted data included information such as study details (author, year, and country), participant characteristics (cohort size, age, gender, diagnostic criteria, and comorbidities), intervention characteristics (type, frequency, and duration), comparators, outcomes, measurements, and potential follow‐ups. Any disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer.

Assessment of Quality and Risk of Bias

Two reviewers independently evaluated the methodological quality of included studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool for RCTs and the Risk of Bias in Non‐randomized Studies (ROBINS‐I) for non‐randomized retrospective studies. Any disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer. The RoB 2 tool assessed five domains, including bias due to the randomization process, deviations from the intended intervention, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of reported results. Each domain was rated as having “low,” “some concerns,” or “high” risk of bias, and an overall risk of bias classification was assigned according to the highest rating of any domain. The ROBINS‐I tool assessed seven domains, including bias due to confounding factors, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurements of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Each domain was rated as having “low,” “moderate,” “serious,” or “critical” risk of bias, and an overall risk of bias classification was assigned according to the highest rating of any domain.

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework was used to assess the certainty of the evidence for the included studies. This was assessed across five domains, including risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias. Evidence of each outcome was rated as “very low,” “low,” “moderate,” or “high” certainty based on the overall assessment of the aforementioned domains [16]. Formal assessments of publication bias were not performed as this review included fewer than 10 studies.

Data Synthesis and Analysis

A narrative synthesis was conducted following guidelines from the Cochrane Handbook framework for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and reported in accordance with the Synthesis Without Meta‐analysis guidelines [17]. Initially, key characteristics related to the studies, including design, participant characteristics, intervention details, and outcomes, were tabulated to provide an overview on the current methodologies applied in this field. Differences in engagement, adherence, and delivery platforms were also explored for their practical implications. Key findings from each study were then summarized and discussed. Where permitted, findings were summarized and interpreted using reported effect estimates (e.g., mean differences, regression coefficients, standardized effect sizes, or odds ratios), corresponding confidence intervals, p‐values, and their clinical relevance. Statistical significance was interpreted according to the criteria reported in the original studies, which used two‐sided tests with a significance level of p < 0.05. Statistical reporting throughout this review adhered to the Statistical Analyses and Methods in the Published Literature (SAMPL) guidelines wherever applicable. However, a formal statistical meta‐analysis was not feasible due to insufficient data and notable heterogeneity in the outcome measures across the studies. As such, no subgroup analyses or post hoc exploratory analyses were performed. The consistency and direction of the effects were systematically evaluated across the included studies, with a particular focus on depressive symptoms and emotional regulation. The methodological quality, risk of bias, and certainty of evidence of included studies were tabulated and considered within the synthesis.

Results

Study Selection

A total of 603 studies were identified across four databases: PubMed (n = 526), Embase (n = 37), CINAHL (via EBSCO, n = 33), and AMED (via OVID, n = 7) (Figure 1). After removing 9 duplicates, 594 records were screened by title and abstract, with 561 excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Thirty‐three full‐text articles were reviewed, and twenty‐nine were excluded due to having the wrong population (n = 5), outcomes (n = 4), intervention (n = 14), or study design (n = 6). Four studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final review (Figure 1).

The four included studies were published between 2019 and 2022, with one conducted in the Netherlands [19], one in Finland [21], and two in the United States of America [18, 20] (Table 1). Collectively, the studies comprised a total of 1118 participants, with a breakdown of 720 females, 278 males, 9 gender non‐conforming, and 8 transgender participants. Individual sample sizes ranged from 50 to 500, with balanced gender representation and mean participant ages between 30 and 51 years. All studies involved adults who were diagnosed with either MDD or persistent depression using standardized diagnostic criteria. Depression severity at baseline ranged from mild to severe and was assessed using validated scales such as the Beck Depression Inventory‐II (BDI‐II) [18], Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM‐D) [21], Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology‐Self Report (IDS‐SR) [19], as well as the Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale‐21 item (DASS‐21) [20] (Table 1).

All studies reported that participants had comorbid psychiatric or physical conditions, such as anxiety [19], bipolar disorder [19], schizophrenia [18], or chronic pain [21], reflecting the complexity of real‐world clinical populations. The inclusion of such populations underscores the potential utility of VR‐based interventions in complex clinical presentations. This diversity enhances the relevance and generalizability of VR‐based exercise interventions for individuals with MDD.

Risk of Bias and Quality Assessment

Based on the Rob 2 tool, only one [18] of the three RCTs received an overall rating of “low” risk of bias, with the remaining two studies judged as having “some concerns” [19] (Figure 2A). The study by Ben‐Zeev et al. [18] was rated as having “low” risk of bias across all domains except domain 3 due to incomplete outcome data. Similarly, the study by Veling et al. [19] was rated as having “low” risk of bias across all domains except for domain 1, which was rated as having “some concerns” due to uncertainties regarding randomization procedures. The retrospective study by Moshe et al. [21], assessed using the ROBINS‐I tool, was judged as having an overall “moderate” risk of bias (Figure 2B). This was largely attributed to the “moderate” risk of bias ratings given to domains 1 and 2, which covered confounding and selection of reported results, respectively. All remaining domains were rated as having low risk of bias for this study.

Intervention Characteristics and Outcomes

The included studies used various VR‐based exercise interventions, such as gamified environments [19] and exergaming platforms [18, 20] designed to simulate recreational physical activity. Interventions lasted from 6 [20] to 8 weeks [18], with 2 [20] to 3 [19] sessions per week, each around 20 min [21], typically preceded by an induction phase and followed by structured activity. The VR interventions were delivered using commercial headsets or simulators, incorporating features such as dynamic visual feedback [19] to enhance engagement and realism. All studies reported significant reductions in depressive symptoms with additional improvements in anxiety [18, 20] and stress [19, 20]. The Pacifica randomized controlled trial demonstrated greater improvement in depressive symptoms than the wait‐list control, with a standardized treatment effect of β (regression coefficient) = −0.59 (95% CI −0.86 to −0.33; Cohen's standardized effect size (d) = 0.54; p < 0.001) [20]. Furthermore, 41.8% of participants achieved clinically significant improvement on the Patient Health Questionnaire‐8 (PHQ‐8) compared with 16.8% in the control group (p < 0.001) [20]. The CORE trial reported a reduction in BDI‐II scores from 33.46 ± 13.75 at baseline to 26.33 ± 10.43 after 30 days, compared with a smaller reduction in the wait‐list group (34.57 ± 13.34 to 32.52 ± 11.12), with a significant treatment‐by‐time interaction (F 1,313 = 13.38, p < 0.001; Cohen's d = 0.58) [18]. The VRelax intervention primarily reduced stress and negative affect compared with standard relaxation (95% CI −18.70 to −0.28; p = 0.04) [19]. Participants across digital interventions also reported high satisfaction and engagement, with adherence generally exceeding 80% [18, 19, 20]. Additionally, participants reported high levels of satisfaction and engagement [20], with lower dropout rates than traditional exercise trials [21].

Characteristics of Virtual Exercise Intervention Components

The included studies used diverse digital platforms to deliver exercise‐ or therapy‐related interventions for individuals with MDD, reflecting the evolving landscape of digital mental health care. These interventions varied in type, features, and delivery modes (Table 2). One study used VRelax, a VR‐based relaxation tool featuring 360° nature scenes and guided meditation, offering sensory immersion and attentional engagement without physical exertion [19]. Another study implemented a web‐based CBT program for individuals with chronic pain, involving therapist‐guided weekly modules but no physical activity [21]. Pacifica, a mobile app, provided CBT‐based exercises, mindfulness tools, and mood tracking to support emotional regulation [20], while CORE focused on cognitive training through daily gamified thought‐restructuring tasks [18].

Although not all included interventions involved structured physical exercise, each incorporated digital therapeutic activities intended to improve depressive symptoms through cognitive, behavioral, immersive, or relaxation‐based mechanisms.

The reviewed interventions shared key features likely contributing to their therapeutic effects in individuals with MDD. Many platforms offered self‐guided interaction with adaptive feedback and optional notifications, supporting engagement in users with motivational challenges [18, 20]. Second, cognitive and emotional activation was facilitated through mood tracking, guided thought exercises, and psychoeducation modules [18, 21]. Third, VR‐based tools like VRelax used immersive sensory environments to promote calm and mindfulness [19]. Finally, gamified elements in apps like CORE enhanced usability and engagement, especially among younger or tech‐savvy users [18]. Together, these features integrate behavioral, cognitive, and experiential strategies that may improve adherence and therapeutic outcomes in digital interventions for depression.

Outcome Measures Used in Virtual Exercise Interventions

The included studies used a diverse range of standardized self‐report measures tailored to each intervention's scope. Depression severity was assessed using validated tools such as the BDI‐II [18], the PHQ‐8 [20], and IDS‐SR [19]. For evaluating anxiety and perceived stress, researchers commonly used the Generalized Anxiety Disorder‐7 (GAD‐7) scale [18, 20] and the Perceived Stress Scale [19, 20]. Functional recovery was tracked using tools such as the Recovery Assessment Scale, Disability Scale, and Self‐Efficacy measures, which provided insights into the participant's ability to cope and function in daily life [18, 20], while affective states were captured via visual analog scales (VAS) in the VRelax study [19]. This range of outcome tools allowed for a multidimensional assessment of intervention efficacy, covering symptomatology, emotional regulation, stress, and functional adaptation.

Among the included studies, delivery platforms varied across studies, reflecting the flexibility of digital therapy. Mobile apps (CORE, Pacifica) enhanced accessibility and real‐time use [18, 20]. VR headsets (Samsung Gear VR) supported immersive relaxation in VRelax [19]. and web‐based platforms delivered structured CBT modules in Moshe et al. [21]. These variations highlight the potential for personalized interventions tailored to user preferences, capabilities, and access.

GRADE Evaluation and Therapeutic Outcomes

This review assessed four digital and virtual exercise interventions for MDD, focusing on clinical effectiveness and evidence certainty using GRADE (Figure 3). VRelax showed reductions in stress and negative affect, with low to moderate certainty due to a small sample, single‐center design, and reliance on self‐report [19]. Pacifica demonstrated moderate improvements in depressive symptoms (β = −0.59, 95% CI −0.86 to −0.33; Cohen's d = 0.54; p < 0.001), anxiety, and stress compared with the wait‐list control [20]. CORE similarly demonstrated clinically meaningful reductions in depressive symptoms (BDI‐II: 33.46 ± 13.75 to 26.33 ± 10.43; Cohen's d = 0.58; p < 0.001) [18]. Moshe et al. [21] evaluated a digital CBT platform for chronic pain and depression but focused on dropout prediction rather than clinical outcomes, and was not rated using GRADE [21]. Although depressive symptoms were measured using the PHQ‐8, the study did not directly assess intervention effectiveness for reducing depression. These findings suggest that Pacifica and CORE apps offer moderately certain evidence of therapeutic benefit, while immersive VRelax shows promise with lower certainty (Figure 3). Future trials with larger sample sizes, longer follow‐up, and objective outcome measures are needed to strengthen the evidence base for virtual interventions in MDD.

Evidence for depressive symptom reduction was rated as moderate. Although three of the four included studies were RCTs and consistently demonstrated improvements in depressive symptoms, the certainty was downgraded for risk of bias and imprecision. Risk of bias arose primarily from methodological limitations, including incomplete outcome data, lack of participant blinding inherent to digital interventions, and reliance on self‐reported outcome measures. Imprecision reflected the relatively small sample sizes, limited number of included studies, and short follow‐up periods. No downgrade was applied for inconsistency because the direction of treatment effects was consistent across studies despite differences in intervention modalities. Indirectness was generally not considered serious for clinical outcomes, as the study populations, interventions, and outcome measures directly addressed the review question, although evidence regarding neurobiological outcomes remained limited. Publication bias could not be formally assessed because only four studies met the inclusion criteria; therefore, although no clear evidence of publication bias was identified, its presence cannot be excluded.

Discussion

This systematic review evaluated virtual exercise interventions for treating MDD, analyzing four trials, including three RCTs using the Pacifica [20], CORE app [18], and VRelax [19]. Across the included RCTs, virtual exercise interventions demonstrated moderate treatment effects for depressive symptoms. For example, Pacifica produced a standardized treatment effect of Cohen's d = 0.54, whereas CORE achieved Cohen's d = 0.58 with clinically meaningful reductions in BDI‐II scores. VRelax primarily improved stress and negative affect rather than depressive symptom severity. These findings suggest that virtual interventions may provide modest‐to‐moderate clinical benefits when used as adjunctive therapies for MDD.

Effectiveness of Digital Exercise Interventions for MDD

Among the studies evaluating clinical effectiveness, Pacifica, CORE, and VRelax reported improvements in depressive symptoms, stress, or emotional well‐being [18, 19, 20]. In contrast, Moshe et al. [21] investigated predictors of treatment dropout and did not evaluate intervention effectiveness. The Pacifica app, integrating CBT, mindfulness, and behavioral tracking, improved depression and emotion regulation [20]. Similarly, the CORE digital intervention demonstrated clinically meaningful reductions in depressive symptoms and anxiety, and improvements in quality of life among adults with depressive symptoms included in this review. The intervention combined cognitive behavioral therapy principles with daily gamified cognitive exercises designed to improve emotional regulation and behavioral activation [18]. The VRelax's immersive VR sessions significantly lowered stress and anxiety, highlighting VR's calming effects [19]. Hypothetically, the therapeutic benefits observed may be attributed to a combination of mechanisms through mechanosensory inputs to the nervous system. Cognitive restructuring and mindfulness are core features of the Pacifica and CORE apps [20]. VRelax enables sensory immersion and environmental simulation [19]. Self‐monitoring and behavioral activation have established efficacy in CBT for depression [4]. Despite a limited evidence base, findings of the included studies support that virtual exercise interventions exert potential benefits to reduce depressive symptoms and improve emotional regulation and treatment engagement [5, 6]. Importantly, high engagement and adherence were consistently reported across Pacifica, CORE, and VRelax, further suggesting digital‐based exercise interventions may improve treatment acceptability among adults with depressive disorders [18, 19, 20].

Methodological Quality and Certainty of Evidence

Overall, the quality of evidence from the included studies could be considered moderate rather than high. According to Cochrane RoB 2, two studies were rated as low risk of bias, while one (CORE App) had some concerns due to missing data. Although all three RCTs were generally well conducted, only one achieved a low‐risk rating across all RoB 2 domains [18]. The presence of concerns about missing outcome data and uncertainties in the randomization process in two of the trials introduces the possibility that the estimated intervention effects may be somewhat biased [19]. According to Cochrane RoB 2 guidance [17], studies rated as having “some concerns” do not necessarily have substantial bias, but confidence in their findings is reduced compared with studies at low risk of bias. The non‐randomized retrospective study by Moshe et al. [21], with a moderate risk of bias, particularly due to confounding, further limits the certainty of the overall evidence base. Therefore, while the findings are generally supported by studies with predominantly low‐risk ratings, the methodological limitations identified suggest that conclusions should be interpreted with moderate confidence, and additional high‐quality, low‐risk RCTs would strengthen the evidence. Also, GRADE assessments rated evidence certainty as moderate for depression and low for anxiety, stress, and quality of life, mainly due to small samples, short durations, and reliance on self‐reports. Despite limitations, consistent positive effects across studies support the reliability of findings.

Clinical and Research Implications

This review highlights the potential of virtual exercise interventions as accessible adjunctive treatments for adults with MDD, particularly for individuals who experience barriers to conventional face‐to‐face therapy [18, 20]. Mobile applications and VR platforms may improve treatment accessibility, user engagement, and adherence while supporting symptom self‐management [13, 22]. The current evidence is derived from a small number of heterogeneous studies, and further well‐designed randomized controlled trials are required before these interventions can be routinely recommended in clinical practice. Future studies should evaluate long‐term effectiveness, cost‐effectiveness, implementation in routine care, and objective outcome measures where appropriate. Emerging technologies, such as virtual sailing, may combine sensorimotor stimulation with cognitive therapy, potentially enhancing brain plasticity and mood regulation [23]. Neurobiological effects, like increased connectivity in mood‐related networks and shifts in neuro‐metabolites (e.g., GABA, glutamate) that can be detected via MRS [24], suggest promising avenues for future research in VR‐based physical therapies. Although neurobiological mechanisms have been proposed for digital and exercise‐based interventions, none of the included studies evaluated biomarkers. Future studies incorporating objective neurobiological outcomes may help clarify potential mechanisms underlying treatment effects.

Complexities and Gaps in Virtual Exercise Intervention for MDD

While virtual exercise shows promise for MDD treatment, several gaps and complexities remain. One significant complexity is the wide variability in how “virtual exercise” is operationalised. The reviewed interventions ranged from VRelax [19] to digital CBT [21] and gamified cognitive retraining apps (Pacifica, CORE) [18, 20], many lacking physical exertion or structured motor tasks. This variability complicates classification within exercise‐based frameworks and raises questions about whether simulated or sensory‐motor engagement yields comparable neurobiological effects, given exercise is traditionally associated with neuroplastic and metabolic changes [6]. Moreover, studies employed diverse and non‐standardized outcome measures, including PHQ‐8, BDI‐II, VAS scales, and recovery indices, which hinder cross‐study comparisons and meta‐analysis. No studies incorporated biomarkers, despite evidence that exercise and VR can modulate emotion‐regulation circuits [24]. Hence, Future research should include neuroimaging (e.g., fMRI, MRS, EEG) to explore mechanisms underlying therapeutic effects.

The extent of participant adherence and long‐term engagement with the virtual exercise also remains unclear. Most interventions were short‐term (6‐8 weeks) with immediate follow‐up, leaving long‐term efficacy and user retention unclear, especially for individuals with MDD who struggle with motivation [8]. In addition, current studies investigating virtual exercise for MDD focused on the adult population, representing a gap in study samples. Participants were predominantly young, tech‐literate individuals with mild to moderate MDD [13]. The generalizability to older adults, individuals with severe depression, or those with cognitive or physical impairments is unclear. This is particularly relevant when considering applications, which may require adaptation for older or mobility‐limited populations but hold potential for increasing emotional resilience through engaging, multisensory experiences.

Finally, the unique contribution of the exercise or immersive component to the therapeutic effect on depression has not yet been investigated. In multicomponent digital tools, such as Pacifica or CORE [18, 20], the specific impact of virtual or physical engagement versus cognitive therapy remains ambiguous. This limits mechanistic understanding and complicates the design of optimized, streamlined interventions, which warrants future investigation.

Future Research Opportunities in Virtual Exercise Intervention

As digital therapeutics evolve, virtual exercise shows growing promise for treating MDD, though current research remains in early stages. Future studies should address methodological gaps, explore immersive formats, and broaden intervention design. While emerging studies suggest that virtual exercise interventions may influence neurobiological pathways associated with depression, evidence remains limited. Consequently, the present review focuses primarily on clinical outcomes while also summarizing any reported neurobiological findings. Future research should prioritize larger multicentre randomized controlled trials with longer follow‐up periods and standardized outcome measures to improve comparability across studies. Further work is also needed to determine which intervention components (e.g., immersive VR, gamification, cognitive behavioral therapy, behavioral activation, or physical activity) contribute most to clinical benefit. Studies should additionally evaluate user adherence, implementation in routine clinical practice, and cost‐effectiveness. Where feasible, objective physiological or neuroimaging outcomes may help clarify potential mechanisms of action, although current evidence remains limited.

A key direction is developing multisensory virtual environments that simulate real‐world physical activities like sailing, rowing, or cycling [25]. These interventions may combine psychological benefits of movement with sensorimotor stimulation and environmental interaction;factors known to enhance mood regulation and executive function [23]. For example, virtual sailing could engage motor learning and neuroplasticity‐related brain networks, offering a novel route to recovery [23]. Given MDD's impact on brain structure and function, particularly in the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala [24], future research should incorporate objective biomarkers (e.g., fMRI, MRS, EEG) to investigate how virtual exercise modulates neural activity in mood‐related regions. Such tools can provide insight into how virtual exercise modulates activity in key brain regions associated with mood, motivation, and stress regulation [24].

To advance the field of virtual exercise interventions for MDD, long‐term efficacy and inclusivity are critical. Most studies focus on short‐term outcomes and tech‐savvy users, limiting generalizability. Research should adopt longitudinal designs with follow‐up strategies and expand to older adults, individuals with physical limitations, and underserved populations. Tailored adaptations could improve access and equity in digital mental health care.

Strength of the Review

This review has several strengths. First, it was prospectively registered with the Open Science Framework and conducted according to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, enhancing methodological transparency and reproducibility. Second, study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment were independently performed by two reviewers using validated appraisal tools (RoB 2, ROBINS‐I, and GRADE). Third, the review provides a comprehensive synthesis of emerging digital and virtual interventions for MDD, integrating evidence on intervention characteristics, user engagement, clinical outcomes, and certainty of evidence. Finally, reporting of effect estimates, confidence intervals, and methodological quality facilitates interpretation of both the statistical and clinical relevance of the findings.

Limitations of the Review

This review has several limitations. First, only four eligible studies were identified, limiting the strength and generalizability of the evidence. Second, substantial heterogeneity in intervention characteristics, outcome measures, comparator groups, and follow‐up durations precluded quantitative meta‐analysis. Third, most studies had relatively small sample sizes and short follow‐up periods, reducing certainty regarding long‐term effectiveness. Fourth, most outcomes relied on self‐reported measures, which may introduce measurement bias. Finally, restricting the review to English‐language peer‐reviewed publications may have introduced language and publication bias. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

This review reports the potential of adopting virtual exercise intervention tools to reduce depressive symptoms and support self‐management in adults with depression disorders. Despite the promising findings, the current evidence is low‐to‐moderate certainty due to only four heterogeneous studies. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and not considered definitive evidence of effectiveness. In the future, larger cohorts of RCTs with standardized interventions, longer follow‐up periods, and consistent outcome measures are required to establish the long‐term clinical effectiveness and applicability of virtual exercise interventions for adults with MDD and other depressive disorders.
