Clinical course, risk factors, and therapeutic response in multiple myeloma with central nervous system involvement
Authors: Alexander Xiao, Saurabh Zanwar, Yi Lin, Prashant Kapoor, Nadine Abdallah, Francis K Buadi, Moritz Binder, Angela Dispenzieri, David Dingli, Morie A Gertz, Wilson Gonsalves, Suzanne R Hayman, Taxiarchis Kourelis, Nelson Leung, Eli Muchtar, Mustaqeem Siddiqui, Melinda Tan, Rahma Warsame, S Vincent Rajkumar, Shaji Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Cellular and bispecific therapies show promising activity for CNS myeloma, whereas IT therapy appears to have limited efficacy.•CNS myeloma is rare, aggressive, and strongly associated with high-risk cytogenetics and multifocal EMD.

Introduction

Extramedullary disease (EMD) in multiple myeloma (MM) is an increasingly common occurrence in patients with relapsed/refractory MM, with recent clinical trial data enrolling heavily pretreated patients and real-world studies demonstrating rates of EMD approaching 20% to 30%.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Within EMD, central nervous system involvement in MM (CNS-MM) continues to be a rare and highly aggressive manifestation of MM.6, 7, 8 CNS-MM most often occurs in the setting of relapsed or refractory disease, although rare cases of presentation at initial diagnosis exist.9, 10, 11 Diagnosis is typically made with neuroimaging and/or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis. Although the prevalence of CNS-MM had previously been estimated to be ∼1%, this may be increasing because of a combination of improved overall survival (OS) from novel therapies and a growing population of patients with treatment refractory disease.8,10

EMD tumors exhibit marked genomic complexity, characterized by a high prevalence of oncogenic MAPK pathway alterations; frequent disruption of key tumor suppressor genes, such as TP53, MAX, and CDKN2C; and an abundance of highly proliferative tumor cells driven by MYC-associated transcriptional programs.12, 13, 14 The exact mechanism by which CNS-MM develops within EMD remains to be understood, but several proposed mechanisms exist, including aggressive subclone selection, decreased expression of adhesion molecules, blood-brain barrier disruption, and upregulation of epithelial-mesenchymal transition pathways.10 CNS-MM typically presents in the fifth to the sixth decades of life, with predictors of development including high-risk cytogenetics, elevated lactate dehydrogenase, and plasmablastic morphology.10 Therapeutic response remains poor despite significant advancements in MM therapy, likely because of poor penetration of the blood-brain barrier. Complicating treatment selection is the exclusion of CNS-MM from many clinical trials. Typical median OS is estimated from 2 to 6 months from time of CNS-MM diagnosis.15 Additional interventions, such as intrathecal (IT) chemotherapy, continue to remain a component of multimodal therapy despite controversial efficacy, and the efficacy of new therapies, including chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) and bispecific agents, has not been evaluated in large cohorts.10,11,15

Given the rarity of CNS-MM, there remains a need to contribute to the body of evidence characterizing its incidence, biology, treatment patterns, and outcomes. Thus, we sought to examine our institution’s experience with CNS-MM over a 23-year period to better define its clinical course, predictors of development, and therapeutic response patterns, including with novel cellular and bispecific therapies.

Patients and methods

We conducted a retrospective study of patients with CNS-MM at our institution between January 2000 and December 2023. The study was approved by the institutional review board. Through a review of the electronic medical record, we identified 304 patients with MM with pathology-proven EMD. CNS involvement was defined as the presence of parenchymal (brain or spine) or leptomeningeal involvement by MM. Clinical information, including demographics and treatment regimens, as well as laboratory test results and survival outcomes were extracted from the medical record. Univariate analysis was performed to identify predictors of CNS-MM in patients with EMD using variables including visceral organ involvement, cytogenetic risk, specific cytogenetic abnormalities, Revised International Staging System score, diagnosis of EMD, monoclonal protein spike, immunoglobulin and free light chain values, and ratio at diagnosis. Multivariate analysis was then performed on variables found significant on univariate analysis. Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank tests were performed to compare survival outcomes. All statistical analyses were performed using BlueSky Statistics software (BlueSky Statistics LLC). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

From among the 304 patients with pathology-proven EMD, we identified 20 (6.5%) with CNS-MM (Table 1). Common symptoms prompting evaluation of CNS-MM were headache in 6 patients (30%), numbness or tingling in 6 (30%), weakness in 6 (30%), visual disturbances in 5 (25%), confusion in 4 (20%), and falls in 4 (20%). Among these patients, 17 (85%) had detectable disease in CSF, 12 (60%) within the brain parenchyma (defined as cranial nerve, intracranial involvement, dura), 2 (10%) within the spinal cord parenchyma, and 6 (30%) with leptomeningeal involvement (Table 2). Among patients with CNS-MM, the median age at MM diagnosis was 58.3 years, and the median time to CNS-MM involvement from MM diagnosis was 19.2 months. Three (15%) patients had CNS involvement at MM diagnosis, and 8 (40%) patients had CNS disease at the time of first EMD identification. By the time of CNS-MM diagnosis, 6 (30%) patients had penta-refractory, and 6 (30%) had triple-class refractory disease. Among patients with EMD, the predictors of CNS-MM development were high-risk cytogenetics (odds ratio, 3.7; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.28-13.2; P = .01) and the presence of additional visceral organ involvement outside of the CNS (odds ratio, 4.6; 95% CI, 1.46-20.01; P < .01). EMD was multifocal in 18 patients with CNS-MM (90%), and additional visceral organ involvement (most commonly lymphadenopathy [30%], solid organ [15%], and pleural fluid [15%]) was present in 17 (85%) of these patients compared to 54% in patients with EMD without CNS-MM (P < .01). High-risk cytogenetics including del(17p), 1q gain/amplification, t(4;14), t(14;16), or t(14;20) were seen in 16 (80%) patients, most commonly 1q gain or amplification (35%), t(4;14) (20%), and del(17p) (25%). Four (20%) patients exhibited a double-hit profile. In comparison, 114 (40%) patients with EMD without CNS-MM had high-risk cytogenetics (P = .02).

The median OS of patients with CNS-MM from diagnosis of MM was 31.2 months (95% CI, 22.8-57.6), compared to 56.4 months (95% CI, 45.6-68.4) in patients with EMD without CNS-MM (P = .03; Figure 1). Overall response rate to first systemic therapy instituted for CNS-MM was 25%, compared to 57% in patients with EMD without CNS-MM (P = .01). In patients with CNS-MM, median progression-free survival (PFS) from CNS-MM diagnosis was 3.5 months (95% CI, 1-12) vs 3.9 months (95% CI, 3.1-5.2) in patients with EMD without CNS involvement (P = .24).

Median OS from diagnosis of MM. When measured from diagnosis of MM, patients with CNS-MM had significantly lower median OS (31.2 months; 95% CI, 22.8-57.6) compared to patients with EMD but without CNS-MM (56.4 months; 95% CI, 45.6-68.4) (P = .03).

Median OS measured from time of CNS-MM diagnosis was 4.2 months (95% CI, 0-21.6) in the CNS-MM cohort, compared to 15.6 months (95% CI, 12-19.2) from time of non-CNS EMD diagnosis (P = .001; Figure 2). Mortality in the CNS-MM cohort was driven by CNS or systemic MM progression in 13 (65%) patients, followed by infection in 4 patients (20%). In addition, 1 patient passed because of diffuse alveolar hemorrhage, and another because of complications from graft-versus-host disease. One patient remains alive.

Median OS from diagnosis of CNS-MM or EMD. When measured from diagnosis of CNS-MM or EMD, patients with CNS-MM had significantly lower median OS (4.2 months; 95% CI, 0-21.6) compared to patients with EMD but without CNS-MM (15.6 months; 95% CI, 12-19.2) (P = .001).

Five patients with CNS-MM (25%) received CAR-T or bispecific antibodies during their disease course, including before diagnosis of CNS-MM; 1 patient received idecabtagene vicleucel (ide-cel), 2 received ide-cel and teclistamab, 1 received zevorcabtagene autoleucel, and 1 received teclistamab and talquetamab. The median OS from diagnosis of CNS-MM for these patients was 19.2 months (95% CI, 8.4-not reached), compared to 1.2 months (95% CI, 0-14.4) for those who never received these therapies (P = .02; Figure 3). Of these 5 patients, 3 (15%) received CAR-T or bispecific antibodies after diagnosis of CNS-MM. The first patient had CSF and leptomeningeal disease involvement without other sites of visceral disease. They received ide-cel as well as IT cytarabine, with a PFS and OS of 1.6 months from the start of ide-cel. The second patient had CNS and intra-abdominal plasmacytomas before initiation of teclistamab and achieved a PFS of 10.7 months, followed by talquetamab with a PFS of 0.7 months. The OS from teclistamab initiation was 19.3 months. This patient did not undergo dedicated imaging to monitor CNS response to therapy. The third patient had leptomeningeal disease and plasma cell leukemia. They received ide-cel before CNS-MM diagnosis, followed by teclistamab with CNS stereotactic radiotherapy to their occipital lesion at time of CNS-MM diagnosis, then additional craniospinal radiation at progression of disease. Repeat brain magnetic resonance imaging 2 and 6 months into teclistamab therapy showed improvement in sites of intracranial disease. Ultimately, PFS on teclistamab was 9.2 months, with an OS of 13.5 months from teclistamab initiation. One of these 3 patients, the third, developed grade 1 cytokine release syndrome (CRS), whereas the second patient developed mild skin and oral mucosal toxicities. There were no cases of immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) or immune effector treatment–related mortality among these patients. Ultimately, the first and second patients passed from progression of their CNS-MM, seen on CNS imaging, with worsening neurological function and seizures. The third patient passed from systemic progression of disease, resulting in pneumonia and hypoxemic respiratory failure.

Median OS from diagnosis of CNS-MM for patients who received CAR-T or bispecific antibodies at any time point during the CNS-MM clinical course. When measured from diagnosis of CNS-MM, patients with CNS-MM who received CAR-T or bispecific antibodies at any time during their disease course had a significantly increased median OS (19.2 months; 95% CI, 8.4 to not reached) compared to patients with CNS-MM who did not receive those therapies (1.2 months; 95% CI, 0-14.4) (P = .02).

Six (30%) patients with CNS-MM, all of whom had CSF involvement by MM, received IT therapy; 4 received methotrexate with cytarabine, 1 each received methotrexate or cytarabine alone. Except for 1 patient who received a single dose of methotrexate, the remaining 5 patients received weekly IT therapy. Four of these patients experienced disease progression within 2 weeks to 2 months of IT therapy initiation, whereas the remaining 2 achieved PFS of 4 and 5 months. CSF assessment was repeated after IT for 3 patients, and only 1 patient demonstrated a decrease in nucleated cells from 179.4/μL to 2.2/μL, although this was not correlated with improvement in symptoms and unfortunately increased back to 36.7/μL within a month, and the patient ultimately passed within 3 months. Sixteen (80%) patients received radiation to the CNS through their disease course; 6 (30%) received craniospinal irradiation, 6 (30%) received focal radiation, 2 (10%) received whole brain radiation with lumbar spine irradiation, and 2 (10%) received whole brain radiation alone.

Discussion

In this retrospective, single-institution study, we characterized our experience with CNS-MM. Among 304 patients with pathology-confirmed EMD, we identified 20 with CNS-MM. Our patients had a median time to CNS-MM diagnosis from original MM diagnosis of 19.2 months. In comparison, Yamashita et al observed time between MM and CNS-MM diagnosis of 27.2 months, and Jurczyszyn et al found that their cohort developed CNS-MM roughly 24 months after MM diagnosis.6,11

In our cohort, the most common presenting symptoms were headaches, numbness or tingling, and weakness (each 30%), visual disturbances (25%), and confusion and falls (each 20%). These proportions mirror those reported in prior studies. In a multicenter study of 172 patients with CNS-MM by Jurczyszyn et al, they found that the most common symptoms were visual changes (36%), radiculopathy (27%), headache (25%), and confusion (21%).6 Chen et al found common symptoms including headache (32%), seizures/altered sensorium (30%), cranial nerve palsies (62%), and sensory changes or motor loss (46%).16 Consistently across these studies and our study, headaches, visual changes, confusion, and radiculopathy appear to be common symptoms of CNS-MM that warrant further workup, yet their similarity to common neurological conditions likely delays CNS-MM diagnosis. Within our cohort, 85% of patients had detectable disease in their CSF, emphasizing the importance of CSF evaluation in diagnostic workup.

Consistent with the existing literature, in our cohort, we found that high-risk cytogenetics and visceral EMD predicted development of CNS-MM. Eighty percent of patients with CNS-MM harbored high-risk cytogenetics including del(17p), 1q gain/amplification, t(4;14), t(14;16), and t(14;20), and 90% of the patients with CNS-MM presented with multifocal EMD.9,10 Although prior reports had found other predictors for CNS-MM including elevated lactate dehydrogenase, we did not find this to be the case in the present cohort.9 Plasmablastic morphology and elevated circulating plasma cells were also previously associated with the development of CNS-MM but were not examined by our study.10

As with prior studies, median OS for patients with CNS-MM is shorter than for those who do not have CNS-MM.9 Survival outcomes were poor for our cohort of patients with CNS-MM, with a median OS from diagnosis of CNS-MM of only 4.2 months, in accordance with prior studies reporting a median OS of 2 to 6 months.10,11 Interestingly, Katodritou et al found that patients diagnosed after 2016 had significantly improved median OS at 12 vs 2 months, which is likely a reflection of the introduction of novel myeloma therapies.9 In our cohort, 3 patients received CAR-T or bispecific antibody therapy in the presence of active CNS-MM, of whom 1 developed grade 1 CRS. No patients developed ICANS or immune effector therapy–related mortality. Compared to prior studies, Noveihed et al performed a retrospective study of 9 patients who received bispecific antibody therapy for CNS-MM and found that all 6 patients who were able to undergo response evaluation achieved a very good partial response or better.17 Bispecific antibodies were generally well tolerated, with CRS reported in 44.4% of patients with no grade ≥3 CRS or any immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome. In regard to CAR-T therapy, Gaballa et al investigated 10 patients with CNS-MM who received CAR-T therapy.18 They found an overall response rate of 80%, with a median OS of 13.5 months. CRS was common in 80% of patients, but none had grade 3 CRS. ICANS developed in 30% of patients, but none with grade 4. Only 1 patient in their cohort received a bispecific antibody in addition to CAR-T.18 Taken together, these results contribute to the small but growing body of evidence suggesting meaningful CNS activity of cellular and bispecific therapies. Additional research is also warranted into combinatorial strategies and sequential therapeutic strategies for CNS-MM. Emerging data support the use of combinatorial approaches, such as teclistamab with talquetamab, or talquetamab with daratumumab, demonstrating promising response rates in patients with heavily pretreated myeloma.19,20 Novel therapies including cereblon E3 ligase modulatory drugs also represent promising areas of research. Furthermore, refinement of treatment sequencing, such as cytoreductive strategies before use of novel therapies, may also help improve outcomes in CNS-MM.8,18, 19, 20

In our cohort, 6 patients received IT therapy, primarily methotrexate and cytarabine. Prior data regarding the efficacy of IT therapy have been conflicting.6,9 In Katodritou et al’s study of 31 patients, no benefit was found, which is in contrast to Yamashita et al’s cohort of 77 patients, in which they found the benefit.9,11,16 Chen et al found that only receiving >3 doses of IT chemotherapy was associated with survival improvement.16 Similarly, Claudel et al conducted a retrospective review of their institution’s experience with serial administration of IT thiotepa, demonstrating that this strategy could achieve CSF clearance, but with variable durability of response.21 Further evaluation into the efficacy of IT chemotherapy, especially in the setting of modern systemic therapies with CNS activity, is needed, although its utility seems to be limited.

We acknowledge several limitations to our study. As a single-institution study, our specific patient demographics and treatment patterns may limit generalizability. The small numbers involved in the analyses resulted in wide CIs and could benefit from additional data for generalizability. This is especially important for those patients with CNS-MM who received CAR-T or bispecific therapies because our sample size was limited to only 3 patients. There is a distinct possibility of selection bias for this group with fitter patients with preserved performance status being selected for immune effector therapy. Moreover, these 3 patients all received ide-cel, perhaps because of regulatory approval timing or concern for delayed neurotoxicity in regard to ciltacabtagene autoleucel, limiting broader generalizability.

Conclusion

In conclusion, central nervous system involvement in MM is a relatively uncommon but devastating manifestation of MM. We found it to be strongly associated with high-risk cytogenetics and involvement of other visceral sites. Although outcomes remain poor, our findings in a small subset of patients who received cellular or bispecific therapies suggest promising survival benefit with these therapies. In contrast, IT therapy appears to offer limited value. Further prospective studies would be valuable in validating these results to help determine optimal treatment strategies for CNS-MM.
