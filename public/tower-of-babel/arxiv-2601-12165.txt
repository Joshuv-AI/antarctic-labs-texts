Fractional Quantum Hall States: Infinite Matrix Product Representation and its Implications
Severin Schraven 1, Simone Warzel 1,2,3 1 Department of Mathematics, TU Munich, Germany 2 Munich Center for Quantum Science and Technology, Munich, Germany 3 Department of Physics, TU Munich, Germany

Abstract
We present a novel matrix product representation of the Laughlin and related fractional quantum Hall wavefunctions based on a rigorous version of the correlators of a chiral quantum field theory. This representation enables the quantitative control of the coefficients of the Laughlin wavefunction times an arbitrary monomial symmetric polynomial when expanded in a Slater determinant or permanent basis. It renders the properties, such as factorization and the renewal structure, inherent in such fractional quantum Hall wavefunctions transparent. We prove bounds on the correlators of the chiral quantum field theory and utilize this representation to demonstrate the exponential decay of connected correlations and a gap in the entanglement spectrum on a thin cylinder.



1
Introduction and main results

Models of the fractional quantum Hall effect
[
25
,
48
]
start from Laughlin’s proposal
[
30
]
of a highly correlated, quantum wavefunction for
N
indistinguishable spinless particles.
In the planar geometry and up to a normalization, this function takes the Jastrow form

\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{q}\ \times\ \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|z_{j}|^{2}\right),

(1.1)

i.e., an integer power
q\in\mathbb{N}
of the Vandermonde determinant times a Gaussian weight stemming from the fact that the single-particle Hilbert space is the lowest Landau level. The latter is the Bargmann space of entire functions that are square integrable with respect to a Gaussian weight.
Here and in the following, we use physical units in which the magnetic length is set to
1/\sqrt{2}
. The parity of
q
determines whether the wavefunction (
1.1
) describes bosons
(even) or fermions (odd).

Revealing the intricate structure behind this class of wavefunctions has continued to fascinate many communities. The Coulomb gas perspective regards the wavefunction as the Gibbs-Boltzmann distribution of an interacting gas of charged particles with logarithmic interactions in a uniform background known as Jellium
[
15
,
1
,
27
,
44
,
33
,
45
]
. This point of view reveals that the number of particles per area (measured in terms of the square of the magnetic length) in the above wavefunction is
1/q
. Important for us will be the connection of
Laughlin’s function and generalizations to the correlation functions of primary
fields in a conformal field theory (CFT), which goes back to
[
37
,
42
]
.
The topic, which we will pursue here, is to investigate the expansion of such wavefunctions with respect to a canonical orthonormal basis, i.e., the Slater determinants or permanents (depending on whether
q
is odd or even) of canonical one-particle orbitals. Such expansions have been studied early on
[
17
,
15
]
. It has been pointed out that the expansion coefficients take the form of an ’infinite matrix product’ (iMPS) of operators in a CFT
[
59
,
10
,
14
,
51
,
5
,
19
,
46
]
. Our goal is to establish these results on a sound mathematical foundation and present the first analytical results within this framework.
Products of the Vandermonde determinant and symmetric polynomials define the Jack polynomials, for which expansions of this form are also investigated in a purely algebraic context
[
13
]
.

Our results have been inspired by previous works on the subject and in particular
[
26
,
19
,
40
]
. The iMPS representation extends beyond that of
[
19
]
or
[
13
]
, as it enables analytical control of the expansion coefficients and facilitates easy proofs of structural properties, such as factorization and the associated renewal structure, which, as far as quantitative analytical bounds are concerned, go far beyond previous results in
[
26
,
27
]
.

1.1
Expansions of powers of the Vandermonde times a symmetric polynomial

To set the stage, we consider the backbone of the expansion, namely, that of the
q
th power of the Vandermonde determinant in terms of the standard monomial basis. We will prove that

\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{q}=(-1)^{qN(N-1)/2}\!\!\sum_{\lambda\preceq\lambda^{(q)}_{N}}\frac{\langle 0|\mathbb{W}(\lambda)|0\rangle}{M(\lambda)!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)^{q}\prod_{j=1}^{N}z_{\sigma(j)}^{\lambda_{j}}

(1.2)

where the first summation on the right side extends over all integer partitions
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N}
, i.e.
\lambda_{1}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{N}
,
which are dominated in the natural order on partitions by the so-called root partition

\lambda^{(q)}_{N}\coloneqq(0,q,2q,\dots,q(N-1))
, which, in particular, entails that

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}=\frac{q}{2}N(N-1),

cf. Definition
3.1
for these notions. The second summation in (
1.2
) is over all permutations of
N
elements, and
\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)
stands for the signature of the permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
. The prefactor

M(\lambda)!\coloneqq\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}m(\lambda,k)!

involves the occupation numbers
m(\lambda,k)
of the
k
th monomial, cf. Definition
3.1
.
The central player in (
1.2
) is the product

\mathbb{W}(\lambda)=W_{\lambda_{N}-q(N-1)}W_{\lambda_{N-1}-q(N-2)}\dots W_{\lambda_{2}-q}W_{\lambda_{1}}

of operators on the ’virtual’ Hilbert space of a chiral CFT. In bra-ket notation, which we use for the virtual Hilbert space, the CFT’s vacuum is denoted by
|0\rangle
. This ’virtual’ Hilbert space is introduced in Section
2
and the definition of the unbounded operators
W_{\lambda_{j}}
, including a domain, can be found in Definition
2.1
.

The Laughlin wavefunction accommodates the most particles per area. Less compressed fractional quantum Hall states with lower filling fraction are obtained by multiplying (
1.2
) with an arbitrary symmetric polynomial
[
48
]
.
An (algebraic) basis of the ring
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]^{S_{N}}
of symmetric polynomials with complex coefficients is given by products of the power-sum symmetric polynomials

p_{n}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{j}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
More convenient for us will be the monomial symmetric polynomial,

m_{b}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\coloneqq\frac{1}{M(b)!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}z_{\sigma(j)}^{b_{j}},

(1.3)

which are again enumerated by integer partitions
b=(b_{1},\dots,b_{N})
.
The monomial symmetric polynomials also form a basis of the set of symmetric polynomials. Consequently, for a given partition
b
of the integer
|b|\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}b_{j}
, there are transition polynomials, which allow to express the monomial polynomials in terms of a real polynomial
Pol_{b}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{|b|}]
of the power symmetric polynomials
[
50
, Prop. 7.7.1]

m_{b}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})=Pol_{b}(p_{1}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N}),\dots,p_{|b|}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})).

(1.4)

The generalization of (
1.2
) to fractional quantum Hall states, which are less compressed than the Laughlin state and uniquely addressed by
(N,b)
, involves the following notions.

Definition 1.1
.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
and a partition
b\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N}
, which encodes a monomial symmetric polynomial, we call the pair
(N,b)
the
root
, and

\displaystyle\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)\coloneqq(b_{1},q+b_{2},\dots,q(N-1)+b_{N}),

the
root partition
.

If
b=0
, then
\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(0)=\lambda^{(q)}_{N}
.
Given a root
(N,b)
and another partition
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N}
, we define the operator products

\mathbb{W}(\lambda,b)\coloneqq W_{\lambda_{N}-q(N-1)-b_{N}}W_{\lambda_{N-1}-q(N-2)-b_{N-1}}\dots W_{\lambda_{2}-q-b_{2}}W_{\lambda_{1}-b_{1}},

(1.5)

for which
\mathbb{W}(\lambda,0)\equiv\mathbb{W}(\lambda)
, cf. Definition
2.1
. Our main theorem generalizing (
1.2
)
then reads as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Given a root
(N,b)
, then

\displaystyle m_{b}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\times\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{q}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{qN(N-1)/2}\sum_{\lambda\preceq\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)}\frac{w_{b}(\lambda)}{M(\lambda)!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)^{q}\prod_{j=1}^{N}z_{\sigma(j)}^{\lambda_{j}},

(1.6)

where the first summation on the right side extends over all integer partitions
\lambda
dominated by the root partition
\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)
(cf. Definition
3.1
). The expansion coefficient is given by

\displaystyle w_{b}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M(b)!}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\langle 0|\mathbb{W}(\lambda,b_{\tau})|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle 0|Pol_{b}\left(\frac{a_{1}}{\sqrt{q}},\dots,\frac{a_{|b|}}{\sqrt{q}}\right)\mathbb{W}(\lambda)|0\rangle.

(1.7)

Here
b_{\tau}\coloneqq\big(b_{\tau(1)},\dots,b_{\tau(N)}\big)
, and
Pol_{b}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{|b|}]
is the unique polynomial with real coefficients such that (
1.4
).
For the root partition, we have
w_{b}(\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b))=1
.

The proof of this theorem is found in Subsection
3.2
.

Remark 1.2
.

In the physics literature, the additional annihilation operators described by
Pol_{b}
in (
1.1
) are understood as boundary charges in the CFT. In this language, the second line is a CFT-correlator with out-state
Pol_{b}(a_{1}^{*},\dots,a_{|b|}^{*})|0\rangle
(see
[
19
,
46
]
and references therein). In this context, it might be useful to note that the coefficients of the transition polynomial
Pol_{b}
depend on the partition
b
, but not explicitly on
N
, i.e. if
\tilde{b}=(0,\dots,0,b_{1},\dots,b_{N})
then
Pol_{b}=Pol_{\tilde{b}}
.

In case of fermions (
q
odd), one may restrict the summation in (
1.1
) to partitions without double occupancy, i.e.
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\dots<\lambda_{N}
, for which
M(\lambda)!=1
.

1.2
Quantum Hall geometries, root partitions and iMPS representation

It is well known that the core of the structure of fractional quantum Hall wavefunctions is geometry-independent
[
19
,
22
]
.
However, to connect Theorem
1.1
to the wavefunctions used in specific models of the fractional quantum Hall effect, we need to fix a geometry and with it a Hilbert space of one-particle states.
We describe this in the planar case, and, in more detail, the case of a cylinder geometry, since much of the analysis, which is presented later, pertains to the latter case.

1.2.1
Planar geometry

In the planar geometry, in which the particles roam the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
, the one-particle Hilbert space is the Bargmann space

\mathcal{P}\coloneqq\left\{f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}\ :\ \mbox{$f$ is entire and}\;\int|f(z)|^{2}e^{-|z|^{2}}dz<\infty\right\}.

An orthonormal basis is provided by the functions

\varphi_{k}(z)\coloneqq\frac{z^{k}}{\sqrt{\pi k!}},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(1.8)

Physically speaking, this is the eigenbasis of the angular momentum operator, and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
labels the angular-momentum orbitals on the plane.
The Hilbert space for
N
fermions or bosons is the
N
-fold antisymmetric (
q
odd) or symmetric (
q
even) tensor-product Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}_{N}\coloneqq\bigotimes_{q}^{N}\mathcal{P}
. An orthonormal basis in the latter is labeled by partitions
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N}
:

\Phi_{\lambda}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{M(\lambda)!\ N!}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\left(\textrm{sgn}\ \sigma\right)^{q}\ \prod_{j=1}^{N}\varphi_{\lambda_{j}}(z_{\sigma(j)}).

(1.9)

In other words, the
\Phi_{\lambda}
’s are the normalized Slater determinants or permanents of the orthonormal single-particle basis (
1.8
).
Varying
N
, the
\Phi_{\lambda}
’s make up the occupation number basis corresponding to the angular momentum orbitals of the fermionic or bosonic Fock space
\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{N=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{N}
.

In the planar setting, the fractional quantum Hall model wavefunctions, which include the Laughlin state (
1.1
), are uniquely labeled by roots
(N,b)
and given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{b,N}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\coloneqq\kappa_{b,N}\ m_{b}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\times\mkern-20.0mu\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{q}

(1.10)

where we choose the normalization

\frac{1}{\kappa_{b,N}}\coloneqq(-1)^{qN(N-1)/2}\sqrt{N!}\ \prod_{j=1}^{N}\sqrt{(q(j-1)+b_{j})!\ \pi}.

(1.11)

We note that
\Psi_{b,N}
is not normalized in
\mathcal{F}_{N}
. The normalizing factor is chosen in a manner that makes the underlying structure more transparent.

An immediate corollary of Theorem
1.1
is the following result on the expansion coefficients of these wavefunctions in terms of the occupation basis.

Corollary 1.3
.

Given a root
(N,b)
, the wavefunction (
1.10
) has the representation

\Psi_{b,N}=\sum_{\lambda\preceq\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)}h_{b}(\lambda)\ \Phi_{\lambda}

with expansion coefficients of the form

h_{b}(\lambda)=\frac{g_{b}(\lambda)\ w_{b}(\lambda)}{\sqrt{M(\lambda)!}},\quad\mbox{with}\quad g_{b}(\lambda)\coloneqq\prod_{j=1}^{N}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{j}!}{(q(j-1)+b_{j})!}}.

(1.12)

The normalization of
\Psi_{b,N}
is such that the coefficient of the root partition
\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)
is unity,
h_{b}\left(\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)\right)=1
.

The proof can be found in Subsection
3.2
.
The basic structure of the expansion coefficient (
1.12
) is similar in other geometries:
g_{b}(\lambda)
is geometry-dependent, and the rest remains invariant.

1.2.2
Cylinder geometry

In this work, we primarily focus on the cylinder geometry, which is derived from the planar geometry through the conformal transformation
z\mapsto e^{\gamma z}
with parameter
\gamma>0
. The single-particle space is then the Bargmann space

\left\{f:\mathbb{R}+i\big[0,\tfrac{2\pi}{\gamma}\big)\to\mathbb{C}\ :\ \mbox{$f$ is entire and}\;\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\big[0,\tfrac{2\pi}{\gamma}\big)}|f(x+iy)|^{2}e^{-x^{2}}dxdy<\infty\right\}.

An orthonormal basis of this Hilbert space is given by

\varphi_{k}(z)\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2\pi^{3/2}}}e^{-\gamma^{2}k^{2}/2}\exp\left(\gamma zk\right),\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

(1.13)

These functions represent orbitals lined up on a cylinder of radius
\gamma^{-1}
roughly at
\gamma k
as their
x
-coordinate.

In the following, we will restrict attention to the closed subspace

\mathcal{B}\coloneqq\overline{\spa\left\{\varphi_{k}\ |\ k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\}}

spanned by orbitals with
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
An orthonormal basis
\Phi_{\lambda}
for the Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}_{N}\coloneqq\bigotimes_{q}^{N}\mathcal{B}
of
N
fermions or bosons is again labeled by partitions
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N}
and given by
the Slater determinants or permanents (
1.9
) in which one substitutes the function (
1.13
).
By varying
N
, this basis is naturally identified with the occupation number basis of the associated Fock space
\mathcal{F}=\bigoplus_{N=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{N}
.

Fractional quantum Hall model wavefunctions, which live on the non-negative
x
-axis, take the form of a symmetric polynomial of
e^{\gamma z}
times the corresponding Laughlin function,

\Psi_{b,N}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})=\kappa_{b,N}\ m_{b}\left(e^{\gamma z_{1}},\dots,e^{\gamma z_{N}}\right)\times\mkern-10.0mu\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\left(e^{\gamma z_{j}}-e^{\gamma z_{k}}\right)^{q}.

(1.14)

These functions are indexed by partitions
b\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and
we choose the normalization

\frac{1}{\kappa_{b,N}}\coloneqq(-1)^{qN(N-1)/2}\sqrt{N!}\ \sqrt{\frac{2\pi^{3/2}}{\gamma}}^{N}\exp\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[q(j-1)+b_{j}\right]^{2}\right).

(1.15)

Again, we do not choose
\kappa_{b,N}
such that
\Psi_{b,N}
is normalized in
\mathcal{F}_{N}
, but such that the structure is more transparent. An immediate consequence of Theorem
1.1
is the following representation of these states in terms of the occupation basis.

Corollary 1.4
.

Given a root
(N,b)
, the wavefunction (
1.14
) has the representation

\Psi_{b,N}=\sum_{\lambda\preceq\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)}h_{b}(\lambda)\ \Phi_{\lambda},\qquad h_{b}(\lambda)\coloneqq\frac{g_{b}(\lambda)\ w_{b}(\lambda)}{\sqrt{M(\lambda)!}}

in terms of the orthonormal Slater determinants or permanents
\Phi_{\lambda}
given by (
1.9
) with
\varphi
substituted by (
1.13
) and

g_{b}(\lambda)\coloneqq\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{b}(\lambda)\right),\quad\mbox{where $\displaystyle\Delta_{b}(\lambda)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\left(q(j-1)+b_{j}\right)^{2}-\lambda_{j}^{2}\right]$.}

(1.16)

The normalization of
\Psi_{b,N}
is such that the coefficient of the root partition
\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)
is unity,
h_{b}\left(\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)\right)=1
.

The proof can be found in Subsection
3.2
.

1.3
Tilings, squeezings and factorization over irreducible segments

As pointed out in
[
26
]
for the case
b=0
in the cylinder geometry, the expansion coefficients
h_{b}(\lambda)
exhibit a renewal structure, i.e. they factorize over irreducible segments of the partition
\lambda\preceq\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)
. This is most easily explained by associating the root
(N,b)
and its partition
\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)
with an occupation configuration
\mathbf{m}\big(\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)\big)
and segmenting this configuration into tiles and elementary blocks. To do so, we note that for any partition
\lambda
, there is a unique occupation configuration
\mathbf{m}(\lambda)=(m(\lambda,0),m(\lambda,1),\dots)
of the non-negative orbitals. In this way, the root partition
\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)
corresponds to the configuration which has exactly one particle in each of the orbitals
\big(\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)\big)_{j}
with
j=1,\dots,N
. This occupation pattern uniquely corresponds to a root tiling of the orbital set
\{0,1,2,\dots,qN+b_{N}-1\}
with

•

’monomers’ of length
q
with particle configuration
10^{q-1}
, and

•

’voids’ of length
1
with particle configuration
0
, which are placed in between the monomers.

The
j
th monomer starts at
\big(\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)\big)_{j}
, and the total number of voids ahead of the
j
th monomer equals
b_{j}
with
j=1,\dots,N
, see Figure
1
.

An example of the occupation configuration
\mathbf{m}
of a root partition
\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)
with
q=3
and
N=6
and
b=(1,1,2,3,3,5)
. The bottom line illustrates the orbitals and their occupation with red balls symbolizing particles. The top line is the equivalent tiling picture with monomer and void tiles on which the occupations are imprinted.
The dashed lines mark separations of elementary blocks, which carry exactly one monomer and potentially frontal voids.

An example of twice two elementary squeezing moves on a root with
q=2
in
a.)
the occupation picture with red balls symbolizing particles and
b.)
in the tiling picture with occupation numbers printed on the tiles. The orange vertical lines mark segmentations, which are consistent with the squeezings. In each picture, the top line represents the root, and the bottom line represents the result after the two squeezing moves.

As will be explained in detail in Section
3
, any partition dominated by a root partition,
\lambda\preceq\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)
, can be obtained from that root by a sequence of elementary squeezing operations. In the occupation picture, an elementary squeezing constitutes of two particles moving towards each other, thereby respecting their ’center of mass’, see Figure
2
. The fact that the expansions (
1.1
) and those in Corollaries
1.3
and
1.4
, are supported on partitions dominated by a root partition is hence in accordance with what is known in the physics literature
[
9
,
10
,
51
]
for fractional quantum Hall wavefunctions.

Through the monomer-void tiling picture, any root partition
\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)
can be decomposed into
N
elementary blocks, each of which contains exactly one monomer and potentially frontal voids, see Figure
1
. In terms of the composition operation on partitions, this amounts to writing the root partition as

\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)=\lambda_{1}^{(q)}\big(b_{1}\big)\cup\lambda_{1}^{(q)}\big(b_{2}-b_{1}\big)\cup\dots\cup\lambda_{1}^{(q)}\big(b_{N}-b_{N-1}\big),

(1.17)

see Definition
3.4
.

To harvest the factorization structure inherent in the expansion coefficients, we will consider arbitrary segmentations of these elementary blocks.

Definition 1.2
.

Given a root
(N,b)
,
we call
(N_{1},N_{2},\dots,N_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
with
\sum_{j=1}^{r}N_{j}=N
and

r\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, a
segmentation of the root
and
r
the segmentation’s length. For any segmentation of length
r
, we denote by
b^{(j)}=\big(b^{(j)}_{1},\dots,b^{(j)}_{N_{1}}\big)
,
j\in\{1,\dots,r\}
, the unique partitions such that

\displaystyle b=\big(b^{(1)},\dots,b^{(r)}\big),

where we define iteratively for two partitions
b^{(1)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N_{1}}
,
b^{(2)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N_{2}}
, their composition

\big(b^{(1)},b^{(2)}\big)\coloneqq\big(b^{(1)}_{1},\dots,b^{(1)}_{N_{1}},b^{(1)}_{N_{1}}+b^{(2)}_{1},\dots,b^{(1)}_{N_{1}}+b^{(2)}_{N_{2}}\big).

(1.18)

Finally, the segments corresponding to
r=N
and
N_{1}=\dots=N_{N}=1
are referred to as
elementary blocks
.

In the language of tilings, a segmentation of the root
(N,b)
of lengths
r
corresponds to marking
r
blocks on the tiling. Each segment consists of elementary blocks, which in turn are made up from the void tiles preceding a monomer (in case there are voids) and a monomer tile, cf. Figures
1
and
3
. In the language of partitions, a segmentation of length
r
corresponds to a (de)composition

\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)=\lambda_{N_{1}}^{(q)}\big(b^{(1)}\big)\cup\lambda_{N_{2}}^{(q)}\big(b^{(2)}\big)\cup\dots\cup\lambda_{N_{r}}^{(q)}\big(b^{(r)}\big)

(1.19)

cf. Definition
3.4
.
As is specified in Definition
3.5
, any partition which is dominated by a given root partition,
\lambda\preceq\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)
, can be uniquely decomposed into irreducible segments

\lambda=\lambda^{(1)}\cup\dots\cup\lambda^{(r)},

(1.20)

which are each dominated by the root partition corresponding to the respective segment,
\lambda^{(j)}\preceq\lambda_{N_{j}}^{(q)}\big(b^{(j)}\big)
,
j\in\{1,\dots,r\}
. The segmentation instances
(N_{1},\dots,N_{r})
are then referred to as the renewal points of
\lambda
, cf. Definition
3.5
.
For any such partition, the occupation states are products by construction,

\displaystyle\Phi^{(c)}_{\lambda^{(1)}\cup\dots\cup\lambda^{(r)}}=\Phi^{(c)}_{\lambda^{(1)}}\odot\Phi^{(c)}_{\lambda^{(2)}}\odot\cdots\odot\Phi^{(c)}_{\lambda^{(r)}}.

(1.21)

In terms of the occupation numbers
\mathbf{m}\left(\lambda^{(1)}\cup\dots\cup\lambda^{(r)}\right)
the operation
\odot
corresponds to a simple concatenation of the individual occupations
\mathbf{m}\left(\lambda^{(j)}\right)
of the segments, cf. Figure
5
.
The (non-commutative) product corresponding to
\lambda=\lambda^{(1)}\cup\lambda^{(2)}
with
\lambda^{(j)}\preceq\lambda_{N_{j}}^{(q)}\big(b^{(j)}\big)
is defined by shifting the second segment to the end of the first,

T_{b^{(1)},N_{1}}\lambda^{(2)}\coloneqq\big(0,\dots,0,q(N_{1}-1)+|b^{(1)}|+\lambda_{1}^{(2)},\dots,q(N_{1}-1)+|b^{(1)}|+\lambda_{N_{2}}^{(2)}\big),

and taking the
q
-symmetrized product,

\Phi^{(c)}_{\lambda^{(1)}}\odot\Phi^{(c)}_{\lambda^{(2)}}\coloneqq\Phi^{(c)}_{\lambda^{(1)}}\otimes_{q}\Phi^{(c)}_{T_{b^{(1)},N_{1}}\lambda^{(2)}}.

(1.22)

The definition of
\odot
on this basis extends by linearity. In this notation, our main factorization result for wavefunctions in the cylinder geometry reads as follows.

Theorem 1.5
.

Given a root
(N,b)
, the functions (
1.14
) have the representation

\Psi_{b,N}=\sum_{r=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N_{1},\dots,N_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
\sum_{j=1}^{r}N_{j}=N\end{subarray}}\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(1)},N_{1}}\odot\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}\odot\cdots\odot\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(r)},N_{r}},

(1.23)

Here
\big(b^{(1)},\dots,b^{(r)}\big)
is the decomposition of
b
corresponding to the segmentation
(N_{1},\dots,N_{r})
, and the term for fixed
N_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
and
b^{(j)}
is

\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(j)},N_{j}}\coloneqq\mkern-10.0mu\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\preceq\lambda^{(q)}_{N_{j}}(b^{(j)})\\
\lambda\;\textrm{irreducible}\end{subarray}}h_{b^{(j)}}(\lambda)\ \Phi^{(c)}_{\lambda},

(1.24)

where the sum is over the irreducible partitions dominated by
\lambda^{(q)}_{N_{j}}(b^{(j)})
only (cf. Definition
3.5
for the notion of irreducibility). Moreover, the vectors in the right side of (
1.23
) are orthogonal, and the norm-square of an irreducible contribution is estimated by

\big\|\widehat{\Psi}_{b,N}\big\|^{2}\leq\exp\left(-\left(q(N-1)+b_{N}-b_{1}\right)C_{q}(\gamma)\right)\leq\exp\left(-q(N-1)C_{q}(\gamma)\right),

(1.25)

where

C_{q}(\gamma)\coloneqq(\gamma^{2}-2(4q+1))-2q^{-1}\ln(1-e^{-(4q+1)})-\pi\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}

(1.26)

is strictly positive for sufficiently large
\gamma
.

The proof is found in Subsection
4.1
.

Remark 1.6
.

The fractional quantum Hall wavefunctions also exhibit a renewal structure in other 2D geometries, since the geometry-independent contribution
w_{b}
to the coefficients
h_{b}=g_{b}w_{b}
determines the irreducible segments in the factorization (
1.23
). However, the geometry-dependent contribution
g_{b}
is more complicated under compositions. E.g. in the plane,
g_{b}(\lambda^{(1)}\cup\lambda^{(2)})
for
\lambda^{(j)}\preceq\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b^{(j)})
,
j=1,2
, would factor into orbital-dependent terms. From an analytical point of view, most important is that, e.g., in the planar situation, we do not have a small parameter
\gamma^{-1}
, which enables the estimate (
1.25
)

Repeatedly, we will take advantage of the fact that the wavefunctions
\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(j)},N_{j}}
on each segment
j
have a non-fluctuating particle number
N_{j}
and orbital momentum associated with
\lambda^{(q)}_{N_{j}}(b^{(j)})
.

The decomposition (
1.20
) of a partition
\lambda
into segments dominated by root segments,
\lambda^{(j)}\preceq\lambda^{(q)}_{N_{j}}(b^{(j)})
, ensures that the squeezing operations, by which one obtains
\lambda
from the root partition
\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)
, are restricted to the individual segments. In other words, no squeezing occurred in between different segments. The representation (
1.23
) can hence be interpreted as an expansion along the partial order of squeezing operators restricted to all possible segmentations, cf. Figure
2
and
3
. It is non-trivial to realize that (
1.23
) is an orthogonal expansion.

Due to orthogonality, the contribution of the irreducible segments to the total norm-square is

\left\|\Psi_{b,N}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{r=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N_{1},\dots,N_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
\sum_{j=1}^{r}N_{j}=N\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\left\|\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(j)},N_{j}}\right\|^{2}.

(1.27)

For sufficiently large
\gamma
, i.e. on thin cylinders, the norm bound (
1.25
) is exponential in the length of the root partition
\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)
as defined by the number of orbitals its tiles cover.
This should be compared with the fact that irreducible segments with only one occupancy carry unit weight,
\|\widehat{\Psi}_{b,1}\|^{2}=|h_{b}(\lambda_{1}^{(q)}(b))|^{2}=1
irrespective of the number
b\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
of voids by Corollary
1.4
.
The renewal process governing the expansion (
1.23
) hence exponentially favors short segments. This observation is the key behind our results on the entanglement gap and exponential clustering on thin cylinders.

An immediate consequence of (
1.27
) is the super-multiplicativity of the square of the norm, i.e. for any
N_{1},N_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
and
b^{(1)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N_{1}}
,
b^{(2)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N_{2}}
with
N=N_{1}+N_{2}
and
b=\big(b^{(1)},b^{(2)}\big)
, we have:

\left\|\Psi_{b,N}\right\|^{2}\geq\left\|\Psi_{b^{(1)},N_{1}}\right\|^{2}\left\|\Psi_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}\right\|^{2}\geq\left\|\Psi_{b^{(1)},N_{1}}\right\|^{2}.

(1.28)

Here the second equality follows from the fact that
\left\|\Psi_{b,1}\right\|^{2}=1
and hence
\left\|\Psi_{b,N}\right\|^{2}\geq 1
.

More can be said for the norm-squared (
1.27
) of the Laughlin wavefunction
b=0
. As is well known
[
15
,
26
]
, the quantity
C_{N}\coloneqq\left\|\Psi_{0,N}\right\|^{2}
can be interpreted as the partition function of a Coulomb gas, and

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}\ln C_{N}
is the corresponding pressure, which exists thanks to super-multiplicativity and Fekete’s lemma. The finite-
N
corrections to this infinite-volume pressure are expected to be suppressed. Since the cylinder is essentially a one-dimensional system, this goes along with the breaking of the translational symmetry, first established in
[
26
]
using renewal theory, and later in
[
1
]
with the help of the Aizenman-Martin argument. In Appendix
A
, we show how our estimate (
1.25
) can be used to establish quantitative versions of finite-
N
corrections to the pressure.

1.4
Entanglement gap and exponential clustering on thin cylinder

In the cylinder geometry, it is natural to investigate the entanglement properties of the wavefunctions
\Psi_{b,N}
concerning a left-right bipartition of the orbitals. In view of Theorem
1.5
,
it is not surprising that these wavefunctions approximately factorize with respect to bipartitions of their root partitions. This is expressed in the following

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
N_{1},N_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
and
b^{(1)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N_{1}}
,
b^{(2)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N_{2}}
be partitions. If
N=N_{1}+N_{2}
and
b=\big(b^{(1)},b^{(2)}\big)
, then in case
\gamma
is large enough such that the constant from (
1.26
) is strictly positive,
C_{q}(\gamma)>0
, the functions (
1.14
) approximately factorize:

\left\|\frac{\Psi_{b,N}}{\left\|\Psi_{b,N}\right\|}-\frac{\Psi_{b^{(1)},N_{1}}}{\left\|\Psi_{b^{(1)},N_{1}}\right\|}\odot\frac{\Psi_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}}{\left\|\Psi_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}\right\|}\right\|\leq\frac{2e^{-C_{q}(\gamma)q/2}}{1-e^{-C_{q}(\gamma)q}}.

The proof is found in Subsection
4.2
.

Theorem
1.7
implies that the largest eigenvalue of the left and right reduced state of the (normalized) state associated with
\Psi_{b,N}
is larger or equal to
1-4\sqrt{2}/\sqrt{e^{C_{q}(\gamma)\ q}-1}
. This follows from standard estimates, e.g. using Uhlmann’s bound
[
53
]
, see e.g.
[
2
, Eq. (3.5)]
. In the thin cylinder limit, the largest eigenvalue is hence close to its maximal value of one. Since all eigenvalues sum to one, the other eigenvalues must be small. This explains the common notion of the ’entanglement gap’ in the so-called entanglement spectrum, i.e. the negative logarithm of the eigenvalues of the reduced state of such a bipartition.

In the physics literature, the entanglement spectrum of the Laughlin state is well studied numerically
[
32
,
52
,
29
]
. Li and Haldane have proposed
[
32
]
that the part of the entanglement spectrum, which consists of the small eigenvalues, exhibits universal features predicted by the CFT. Theorem
1.7
does not address this conjecture.

Our last result concerns the exponential decay of correlations of local observables in each of the states
\Psi_{b,N}
. Any local observable is composed of the sums of products of
the canonical annihilation and creation operators,
c_{k}
,
c_{k}^{*}
, with
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, associated with the one-particle orbitals (
1.13
) on the non-negative half-cylinder. They obey the canonical anticommutation or commutation rules,

[c_{k},c^{*}_{l}]_{q}\coloneqq c_{k}c_{l}^{*}-(-1)^{q}c^{*}_{l}c_{k}=\delta_{k,l},\quad[c_{k},c_{l}]_{q}=[c_{k}^{*},c_{l}^{*}]_{q}=0,

where
[\cdot,\cdot]_{q}
stands for the anti-commutator or commutator, depending on whether we deal with fermions (
q
odd) or bosons (
q
even). Subsequently, we denote for a partition
L=(L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{r})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}
,

c_{L}\coloneqq c_{L_{1}}c_{L_{2}}\dots c_{L_{r}}

In this notation, the algebra of local observables is generated by such products and their adjoints, and we abbreviate their building blocks as

\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{loc}}\coloneqq\left\{c^{*}_{L}c_{L^{\prime}}\ :\ L\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r},\,L^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\;\mbox{for some $r,s\in\mathbb{N}$}\right\}.

We will refer to the orbitals in the partitions
L,L^{\prime}
as the support,
\supp O
, of the observable
O=c^{*}_{L}c_{L^{\prime}}
.
In the case of fermions (
q
odd), it is straightforward that the expectation values

\langle O\rangle_{N,b}\coloneqq\frac{\langle\Psi_{b,N},O\ \Psi_{b,N}\rangle}{\left\|\Psi_{b,N}\right\|^{2}}

are well defined for any
O\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{loc}}
and any root
(N,b)
. Since the occupation number of bosons is a priori unbounded on any orbital, already the finiteness of these expectation values, which we will establish in Lemma
4.2
, is non-trivial for bosons (
q
even).
Our next theorem shows that, on sufficiently thin cylinders, any such expectation value exhibits exponential decay of the correlations of local observables in the distance,
\dist(\supp A,\supp B)\coloneqq\min\{|x-y|\ |\ x\in\supp A,\;y\in\supp B\}
, of their supports.

Theorem 1.8
.

For all
\gamma>0
large enough such that the constant from (
1.26
) is strictly positive,
C_{q}(\gamma)>0
, there is some
d_{q}(\gamma)>0
such that for all local observables
A,B\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{loc}}
with
\max\supp A<\min\supp B
and
\dist(\supp A,\supp B)\geq d_{q}(\gamma)
, and all roots
(N,b)
:

|\langle AB\rangle_{N,b}-\langle A\rangle_{N,b}\langle B\rangle_{N,b}|\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{C_{q}(\gamma)}{6}\ \dist(\supp A,\supp B)\right).

(1.29)

with a constant
C<\infty
, which only depends on the cardinalities of the support of
A
and
B
.

The proof and explicit values for the constants
c_{q}(\gamma),C
are found in Subsection
4.3
.

Exponential clustering for the Laughlin state
b=0
has been shown in
[
26
]
. However, their proof method, which is based on the implicit function theorem and renewal theory, yields no explicit threshold for the inverse radius
\gamma
, at which exponential clustering applies. The follow-up
[
27
]
to
[
26
]
uses Coulomb-gas methods for an attempt to improve estimates, however, without reaching a proof of quantitative clustering.

1.5
Outlook

The set of fractional quantum Hall wavefunctions
\Psi_{b,N}
with arbitrary root
(N,b)
is known to span a dense subspace of the kernel of the Haldane pseudopotential corresponding to filling fraction
1/q

[
43
,
35
]
. Haldane pseudopotentials are many-body operators modelling quantum Hall physics in rather general 2D geometries
[
21
,
25
,
41
,
6
,
46
]
. They emerge from a generic interacting Landau Hamiltonian in the scaling limit of short-range interactions
[
47
]
.
For filling fraction
1/q
, the Haldane pair potentials are frustration-free and non-negative, and hence ’parent Hamiltonians’ for
\Psi_{b,N}
.

Pseudo-potentials are conjectured to have a uniform spectral gap above their ground-state – a feature, which is responsible for the incompressibility of the quantum fluid
[
45
,
40
]
as well as the quantization of the Hall conductance
[
24
,
3
,
4
]
. Proving this so-called Haldane-gap conjecture has remained elusive. Rigorous results
so far address only truncated versions of pseudopotentials
[
40
,
55
,
56
,
57
]
.
Notably, the identification of the root
(N,b)
with a void-monomer tiling (cf. Figure
1
) proves that the set of fractional quantum Hall wavefunctions
\Psi_{b,N}

is in one-to-one correspondence with the set of VMD states introduced in
[
40
,
39
]
. The latter are an orthonormal basis of the kernel of the truncated Haldane Hamiltonian
[
8
,
40
]
, and the analogue of Theorem
1.8
for the VMD states has also been established in
[
40
]
. In contrast to VMD states, however, the wavefunctions
\Psi_{b,N}
corresponding to the same particle number
N
and total momentum
|\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)|=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)\big)_{j}
, but yet different
b
, are not necessarily orthogonal.
The correspondence raises the question and hope of an adiabatic connection of truncated and untruncated pseudopotentials. If this could be achieved adiabatically without closing the gap via a stability analysis
[
12
,
38
]
, Haldane’s gap-conjecture would be proven. Other known methods for many-body spectral gaps
[
58
]
, such as the martingale method, Knabe’s method, or the induction method
[
31
]
, also rely on a good understanding of the structure of the ground state. With this work and the techniques developed in its body, we hope to provide a first non-trivial step – at least in the cylinder geometry.

2
Infinite MPS representation

A virtual Hilbert space and a set of operators characterize MPS representations. This section sets the stage by defining these fundamental quantities and by exploring their basic properties.
Our iMPS representation adopts the framework of the free chiral conformal field theory (CFT) for quantum Hall states, as described in the physics literature. It is inspired, yet slightly different from predecessors
[
19
]
. The relation is explained in Appendix
B
.

2.1
Virtual Hilbert space and momentum eigenspaces in a chiral CFT

For the virtual Hilbert space, we take the Fock space
\mathcal{H}
generated from the vacuum
|0\rangle
and a set of the bosonic annihilation and creation operators
(a_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}},(a_{j}^{*})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, which satisfy the commutation relations

[a_{n},a_{m}]=0=[a_{n}^{*},a_{m}^{*}],\qquad[a_{n},a_{m}^{*}]=n\delta_{n,m},\qquad\text{for all }n,m\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.1)

We will denote by
\mathcal{H}_{0}
the subspace

\mathcal{H}_{0}=\text{span}\left\{\left(\prod_{j=1}^{M}(a_{j}^{*})^{n_{j}}\right)|0\rangle\ :\ M\in\mathbb{N}_{0},n_{1},\dots,n_{M}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\},

(2.2)

which is dense in
\mathcal{H}
by construction.
The scalar product on
\mathcal{H}
is chosen in such a way that
a_{j}^{*}
is the adjoint of
a_{j}
and such that
|0\rangle
is normalized. In accordance with the interpretation of conformal field theory, we will subsequently refer to
a_{j}^{*}
as the creation of moment
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and to the operator

L_{0}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}^{*}a_{n},

(2.3)

which is non-negative and symmetric on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
,
as the operator of total momentum. By Friedrich’s extension theorem, it hence uniquely extends to a self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}
.
A straightforward computation using (
2.1
) shows that

L_{0}a_{j}=a_{j}(L_{0}-j),\qquad a_{j}^{*}L_{0}=(L_{0}-j)a_{j}^{*}

(2.4)

for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and hence

L_{0}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{K}(a_{j}^{*})^{n_{j}}\right)|0\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{K}jn_{j}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{K}(a_{j}^{*})^{n_{j}}\right)|0\rangle.

(2.5)

The finite-dimensional subspaces

\mathcal{H}(M)\coloneqq\mathrm{span}\left\{\left(\prod_{j=1}^{K}(a_{j}^{*})^{n_{j}}\right)|0\rangle\ :\ K\in\mathbb{N},n_{1},\dots,n_{K}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\text{ such that }\sum_{j=1}^{K}jn_{j}=M\right\}

(2.6)

are the eigenspaces of
L_{0}
corresponding to
a fixed total momentum
M\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. They are hence mutually orthogonal, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}(M)\perp\mathcal{H}(\tilde{M})
for
M\neq\tilde{M}
, and span the full space:

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{M\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathcal{H}(M).

(2.7)

2.2
Motivation and definitions of operators

To introduce the set of operators used in the iMPS representation on the virtual Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, we first recall the construction used in
[
19
]
, which
considers formal power series with coefficients in
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{H}_{0})
, i.e., the coefficients are linear operators from
\mathcal{H}_{0}
into itself – most importantly

S_{+}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}z^{-n},\qquad S_{-}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{-1}{n}a_{n}^{*}z^{n}.

(2.8)

Informally, one would like to define

W(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}W_{n}z^{n}=\exp\left(-\sqrt{q}S_{-}(z)\right)\exp\left(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z)\right).

(2.9)

For this, one would consider Laurent series with coefficients in
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{H}_{0})
. Unfortunately, these do not form a ring, as multiplication is, in general, not defined. Indeed, formally, the coefficients of a product of two Laurent series will be a series of terms in
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{H}_{0})
, which has no meaning in an algebraic setting. These objects are also problematic from an analytic perspective, since it is unclear in what sense the series of unbounded operators should converge. This is a standard problem in algebraic quantum field theory, and a solution involves taking the formal product and realizing that the infinite series becomes a finite sum when tested against an element in a suitable domain. In our case,
\mathcal{H}_{0}
will do the trick.

Before we spell out a rigorous version, let us briefly recall the relevance of the above operators in relation to the Laughlin wavefunction if
N=2
.
Informally, we compute the quantity

\langle 0|W(z_{1})W(z_{2})|0\rangle=\langle 0|\exp\left(-\sqrt{q}S_{-}(z_{1})\right)\exp\left(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z_{1})\right)\exp\left(-\sqrt{q}S_{-}(z_{2})\right)\exp\left(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z_{2})\right)|0\rangle.

As
a_{j}|0\rangle=0
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
, one concludes

\displaystyle\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z))|0\rangle=|0\rangle,\qquad\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z))^{*}|0\rangle=|0\rangle.

Thus, it remains to evaluate

\displaystyle\langle 0|\exp\left(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z_{1})\right)\exp\left(-\sqrt{q}S_{-}(z_{2})\right)|0\rangle.

To do so, we will commute
\exp\left(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z_{1})\right)
to the right by computing the commutator of
\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z_{1}))
and
\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{-}(z_{2}))
. This is a setting where we can use the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff theorem (by embedding the problem into
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{H}_{0})[[z_{1}^{-1},z_{2}]]
, which is an associative
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra, see
[
11
, Corollary 4.5]
). Since all higher commutators vanish as is seen from

\displaystyle[S_{+}(z_{1}),S_{-}(z_{2})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{-1}{nm}[a_{n},a_{m}^{*}]z_{1}^{-n}z_{2}^{m}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{-1}{n}z_{1}^{-n}z_{2}^{n},

the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula takes the form

\displaystyle\exp

\displaystyle(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z_{1}))\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{-}(z_{2}))

\displaystyle=\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{-}(z_{2}))\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z_{1}))\exp(q[S_{+}(z_{1}),S_{-}(z_{2})]).

The last factor can be simplified by using the Taylor series

\log(1-z)=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n}
, which yields

\displaystyle\exp\left(-q\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n}\right)=\exp(q\log(1-z))=(1-z)^{q}.

Comparing coefficients (respectively, formally applying this with
z=z_{2}/z_{1}
), we obtain

\displaystyle\exp(q[S_{+}(z_{1}),S_{-}(z_{2})])=(1-z_{1}^{-1}z_{2})^{q}=z_{1}^{-q}(z_{1}-z_{2})^{q},

and therefore

\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z_{1}))\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{-}(z_{2}))=\left(1-\frac{z_{2}}{z_{1}}\right)^{q}\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{-}(z_{2}))\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z_{1})).

(2.10)

Formally, one arrives at

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}\langle 0|W_{k_{1}}W_{k_{2}}|0\rangle\ z_{1}^{k_{1}+q}z_{2}^{k_{2}}=(z_{1}-z_{2})^{q},

which is the
q
th power of the Vandermonde for
N=2
.

The only non-rigorous step in the above computation was the separation of
\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{-}(z))
and
\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z))
to simplify. We cannot do this, as they do not belong to any well-behaved ring where both exponentials are individually defined. The way to avoid this issue is to study in more detail how the creation operators of
\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{-}(z))
get coupled to the annihilation operators in
\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z))
when the two power series get multiplied. To do so, we expand both as power series,

\displaystyle\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{-}(z))=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\sqrt{q})^{k}}{k!}\left(-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}^{*}}{n}z^{n}\right)^{k}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}z^{\ell}D_{\ell}^{-},

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mbox{and similarly}\quad\exp(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z))=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}z^{-\ell}D_{\ell}^{+}

with coefficients in
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{H}_{0})
, which are defined the following.

Definition 2.1
.

For
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define the operators
D_{\ell}^{\pm}:\mathcal{H}_{0}\to\mathcal{H}_{0}
by

\displaystyle\begin{split}D_{\ell}^{+}&\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathbbm{1},&\ell=0,\\
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{\sqrt{q}^{k}}{k!}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\dots,j_{k}>0:\\
j_{1}+\dots+j_{k}=\ell\end{subarray}}(-1)^{k}\left(\prod_{s=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j_{s}}\right)\prod_{t=1}^{k}a_{j_{t}},&\ell>0,\\
0,&\ell<0\end{cases}\\
D_{\ell}^{-}&\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathbbm{1},&\ell=0,\\
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\frac{\sqrt{q}^{k}}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\dots,j_{k}>0:\\
j_{1}+\dots+j_{k}=\ell\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{s=1}^{k}\frac{1}{j_{s}}\right)\ \prod_{t=1}^{k}a_{j_{t}}^{*},\qquad&\ell>0,\\
0,&\ell<0.\end{cases}\end{split}

(2.12)

Moreover, for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
we set
W_{m}:\mathcal{H}_{0}\to\mathcal{H}_{0}
as

W_{m}\coloneqq\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}D_{m+\ell}^{-}D_{\ell}^{+}.

(2.13)

The operator
D_{\ell}^{+}
annihilates a total momentum
\ell
and
D_{\ell}^{-}
creates it as long as
\ell\geq 0
. They hence act as ladders among the family of subspaces (
2.6
). In particular, the above operators are indeed well defined on the dense subspace
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, which they leave invariant. As
D_{\ell}^{+}
vanishes on
\mathcal{H}(k)
for
k<\ell
, we conclude that

\displaystyle W_{m}|_{\mathcal{H}(k)}:\mathcal{H}(k)\rightarrow\mathcal{H}(k+m),v\mapsto\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}D_{m+\ell}^{-}D_{\ell}^{+}v.

Thus, when tested against an element in
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, each operator
W_{m}
reduces to a finite sum, which also makes sense in an algebraic setting. As we are effectively dealing with finite sums, we subsequently never have to worry about convergence issues when taking commutators.

Lemma 2.1
.

With the convention
\mathcal{H}(M)=\{0\}
in case
M<0
:

1.

D_{\ell}^{\pm}\mathcal{H}(M)\subseteq\mathcal{H}(M\mp\ell)
for any
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
,
M\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Moreover:

(a)

for all
m,k\in\mathbb{Z}
:
\displaystyle D_{m}^{+}D_{k}^{-}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{q}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{q}{\ell}D_{k-\ell}^{-}D_{m-\ell}^{+}
,

(b)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
:
\displaystyle a_{n}D_{\ell}^{-}=D_{\ell}^{-}a_{n}+\sqrt{q}\ D_{\ell-n}^{-}
.

2.

2.3
Polynomial expansion of powers of the Vandermonde

The following is the core for the MPS representation of the coefficients of any integer power of the Vandermonde determinant as a complex multinomial.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
q,N\in\mathbb{N}
one has

\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}(z_{i}-z_{j})^{q}=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}}\langle 0|W_{k_{1}}\dots W_{k_{N}}|0\rangle\ z_{1}^{k_{1}+q(N-1)}\cdot\dots z_{N-1}^{k_{N-1}+q}z_{N}^{k_{N}}.

(2.14)

Proof.

We will derive a formula for the coefficients of the multinomial and check that
\langle 0|W_{k_{1}}\dots W_{k_{N}}|0\rangle
satisfy those too. The left side of (
2.14
) is expanded as:

\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}(z_{i}-z_{j})^{q}=z_{1}^{q(N-1)}\cdot\dots z_{N-1}^{q}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\left(1-\frac{z_{j}}{z_{i}}\right)^{q}

\displaystyle=z_{1}^{q(N-1)}\cdot\dots z_{N-1}^{q}\ \widetilde{\sum_{\ell_{i,j}}}(-1)^{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\ell_{i,j}}\left(\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\binom{q}{\ell_{i,j}}\right)\prod_{s=1}^{N}z_{s}^{\sum_{t=1}^{s-1}\ell_{t,s}-\sum_{u=s+1}^{N}\ell_{s,u}},

where
\widetilde{\sum}_{\ell_{i,j}}
denotes the sum over a collection of integers
0\leq\ell_{i,j}\leq q
with
1\leq i<j\leq N
.
To compare the coefficients with the right side of (
2.14
),
we use the definition (
2.13
) together with Lemma
2.1
(
1a
) to bring all the
D^{-}
to the left:

\displaystyle W_{m_{1}}\dots W_{m_{N}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}}D_{m_{1}+k_{1}}^{-}D_{k_{1}}^{+}\dots D_{m_{N}+k_{N}}^{-}D_{k_{N}}^{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}}\ \widetilde{\sum_{\ell_{i,j}}}\ (-1)^{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\ell_{i,j}}\left(\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\binom{q}{\ell_{i,j}}\right)

\displaystyle\mkern 50.0mu\times\left(\prod_{s=1}^{N}D^{-}_{m_{s}+k_{s}-\sum_{t=1}^{s-1}\ell_{t,s}}\right)\left(\prod_{s=1}^{N}D^{+}_{k_{s}-\sum_{t=s+1}^{N}\ell_{s,t}}\right).

Since
D_{\ell}^{+}|0\rangle=\delta_{\ell,0}|0\rangle=(D_{\ell}^{-})^{*}|0\rangle
,
we hence obtain

\displaystyle\langle 0|W_{m_{1}}\dots W_{m_{N}}|0\rangle=

\displaystyle\ \widetilde{\sum_{\ell_{i,j}}}\ (-1)^{\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\ell_{i,j}}\left(\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\binom{q}{\ell_{i,j}}\right)

\displaystyle\mkern 10.0mu\times\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\prod_{s=1}^{N}\delta_{k_{s}+m_{s},\sum_{t=1}^{s-1}\ell_{t,s}}\right)\left(\prod_{s=1}^{N}\delta_{k_{s},\sum_{t=s+1}^{N}\ell_{s,t}}\right),

which implies the result.
∎

As is also well known
[
19
, Section 2.2]
, the above may be generalized to include factors of power sum symmetric polynomials

\displaystyle p_{n}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{j}^{n}.

In the iMPS, they are accommodated in terms of boundary charges.

Corollary 2.3
.

For any
n_{1},\dots,n_{K}\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{j=1}^{K}\sqrt{q}\ p_{n_{j}}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})\right)\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}(z_{j}-z_{k})^{q}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}}\langle 0|\left(\prod_{j=1}^{K}a_{n_{j}}\right)W_{k_{1}-q(N-1)}\dots W_{k_{N-1}-q}W_{k_{N}}|0\rangle\ z_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots z_{N}^{k_{N}}.\qquad

(2.15)

Proof.

We shift the momenta,
k_{j}\to k_{j}+q(N-j)
in the right side and pull through all annihilation operators one by one to the right employing the commutation rule in the second item of Proposition (
2.1
). In case
K=1
we hence get using
a_{n}|0\rangle=0
and (
2.14
):

\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{N}\in\mathbb{Z}}\!\!\langle 0|a_{n}W_{k_{1}}\dots W_{k_{N}}|0\rangle\ z_{1}^{k_{1}+q(N-1)}\cdot\dots z_{N-1}^{k_{N-1}+q}z_{N}^{k_{N}}=\sqrt{q}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{j}^{n}\right)\!\!\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}(z_{i}-z_{j})^{q}

The proof for general

is exactly the same.
∎

2.4
Fundamental properties of operator products

A repeated application of the commutation rule from Lemma
2.1
in the coefficients on the right side of (
2.3

W_{k_{1}-q(N-1)}\dots W_{k_{N-1}-q}W_{k_{N}}=(-1)^{qN(N-1)/2}\ W_{k_{N}-q(N-1)}W_{k_{N-1}-q(N-2)}\dots W_{k_{1}}

\mathcal{H}_{0}
for any collection of momenta
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{N})\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}

The following theorem captures all the fundamental properties of these operator products.

Theorem 2.4
.

For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
the operator product

\mathbb{W}(\mathbf{k})\coloneqq W_{k_{N}-q(N-1)}W_{k_{N-1}-q(N-2)}\dots W_{k_{2}-q}W_{k_{1}}

(2.17)

with
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{N})\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
defined on
\mathcal{H}_{0}
has the following properties:

1.

permutation symmetry, i.e., for any permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}

\mathbb{W}(\mathbf{k})=\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)^{q}\ \mathbb{W}(k_{\sigma(1)},k_{\sigma(2)},\dots,k_{\sigma(N)}).

(2.18)

2.

factorization property,
\displaystyle\mathbb{W}(\mathbf{k})|0\rangle\in\mathcal{H}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}(k_{j}-q(j-1))\big)
.
For any
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N}
with
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}k_{j}\leq q\frac{N(N-1)}{2}
:

\mathbb{W}(\mathbf{k})|0\rangle=\langle 0|\mathbb{W}(\mathbf{k})|0\rangle\ |0\rangle.

(2.19)

3.

normalization,
\langle 0|\mathbb{W}\left(\mathbf{k}^{(q)}_{N}\right)|0\rangle=1
, for

\mathbf{k}^{(q)}_{N}\coloneqq\left(0,q,\dots,q(N-2),q(N-1)\right).

(2.20)

4.

\displaystyle\langle 0|\mathbb{W}(\mathbf{k})|0\rangle=0
unless
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N}
, and for any
s\in\{1,\dots,N\}

\sum_{j=1}^{s}k_{j}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{s}q(j-1),\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sum_{j=s}^{N}k_{j}\leq\sum_{j=s}^{N}q(j-1),

(2.21)

and, consequently,
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}k_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}q(j-1)=q\frac{N(N-1)}{2}

Proof.

1. The permutation symmetry (
2.18

2. The inclusion

\mathbb{W}(\mathbf{k})|0\rangle\in\mathcal{H}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}(k_{j}-q(N-j))\right)

3. The claimed normalization in case

\langle 0|W_{0}|0\rangle=1
and the previous item.

4. The fact that the momenta

2.5
Key estimate

While the operators
W_{m}
entering the product (

L_{0}
defined in (

). This idea is inspired by findings in

Theorem 2.5
.

For every
m\in\mathbb{Z}
we have the operator-norm bound

\|e^{-(4q+1)L_{0}/2}W_{m}e^{-(4q+1)L_{0}/2}\|\leq 1

(2.22)

Proof.

Let
P_{M}
with
M\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
stand for the orthogonal projection onto the subspace
\mathcal{H}(M)
of total momentum
M
, again with the convention that
P_{M}=0
in case
M<0
.
Since
W_{m}\mathcal{H}(M)\subseteq\mathcal{H}(M+m)
and
L_{0}
is diagonal with respect to the orthogonal decomposition (
2.7
), we have

\|e^{-(4q+1)L_{0}/2}W_{m}e^{-(4q+1)L_{0}/2}\|\leq\sup_{M\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\left\|P_{M+m}e^{-(2q+1)L_{0}/2}W_{m}e^{-(4q+1)L_{0}/2}P_{M}\right\|.

(2.23)

To estimate the norm, we may assume without loss of generality that
m\geq-M
, since otherwise the norm is zero.
We now distinguish two cases. In case
M\in\mathbb{N}
, we estimate for
x\in\mathcal{H}(M+m)
and
y\in\mathcal{H}(M)
:

\displaystyle|\langle x|W_{m}y\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=\min\{0,-m\}}^{\infty}|\langle\left(D_{\ell+m}^{-}\right)^{*}x|D_{\ell}^{+}y\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{\sqrt{q}^{k}}{k!}\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{j_{1}\dots j_{k}}\|a_{j_{1}}\dots a_{j_{k}}x\|\right)

\displaystyle\mkern 30.0mu\times\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{\sqrt{q}^{n}}{n!}\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{j_{1}\dots j_{n}}\|a_{j_{1}}\dots a_{j_{n}}y\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{\sqrt{2q}^{k}}{k!}\|L_{0}^{k/2}x\|\right)\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{\sqrt{2q}^{n}}{n!}\|L_{0}^{n/2}x\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{2q}\ \|e^{L_{0}/2}x\|\ \|e^{L_{0}/2}y\|=e^{(4q+2M+m)/2}\ \|x\|\|y\|

The second inequality used the triangle and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. The third inequality follows from a Cauchy-Schwarz estimate applied to the
j
-sums together with the crude bound
\sum_{n=1}n^{-2}=\pi^{2}/6<2
and Lemma
2.6
below. The fourth inequality is by Cauchy-Schwarz again and the self-adjointness of
L_{0}
.
Since
2M+m\geq M\geq 1
by assumption, the exponential on the right side is bounded by
\exp((4q+1)(2M+m)/2)
. Passing this exponential to the left side, and using (
2.5
) yields

|\langle x|e^{-(4q+1)L_{0}/2}W_{m}e^{-(4q+1)L_{0}/2}y\rangle|\leq\|x\|\|y\|,

and hence the claim in this case.

In case
M=0
, the norm on the right side of (

) is zero if
m<0
and one if

. We then estimate for

and

similarly as above:

\displaystyle|\langle x|W_{m}|0\rangle|\leq\|(D_{m}^{-})^{*}x\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{\sqrt{q}^{k}}{k!}\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{j_{1}\dots j_{k}}\|a_{j_{1}}\dots a_{j_{k}}x\|

\displaystyle\leq e^{q}\ \|e^{L_{0}/2}x\|=e^{(2q+m)/2}\ \|x\|\leq e^{(4q+1)m/2}\ \|x\|,

which again yields the desired bound.
∎

The proof was based on the following

Lemma 2.6
.

For all
m\in\mathbb{N}
and

:

\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{m}\in\mathbb{N}}\|a_{j_{1}}\dots a_{j_{m}}x\|^{2}\leq\langle x|L_{0}^{m}|x\rangle=\|L_{0}^{m/2}x\|^{2}.

(2.24)

We prove (

) by induction. The case

) yield

, such that
for any

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j,k\in\mathbb{N}}\langle x|a_{j}^{*}a_{k}^{*}a_{k}a_{j}|x\rangle=\sum_{j,k\in\mathbb{N}}\langle x|a_{j}^{*}a_{j}a_{k}^{*}a_{k}|x\rangle-\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}j\langle x|a_{j}^{*}a_{j}|x\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j,k\in\mathbb{N}}\langle x|a_{j}^{*}a_{j}a_{k}^{*}a_{k}|x\rangle=\langle x|L_{0}^{2}|x\rangle.

For

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{m}\in\mathbb{N}}\|a_{j_{1}}\dots a_{j_{m}}x\|^{2}\leq\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{m}\in\mathbb{N}}\langle x|a_{j_{1}}^{*}a_{j_{2}}^{*}\dots,a_{j_{m-2}}^{*}L_{0}^{2}a_{j_{m-2}}\dots a_{j_{1}}|x\rangle

which, by iteration, implies the claim.

Corollary 2.7
.

For all
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N}

\|\mathbb{W}(\mathbf{k})|0\rangle\|\leq\exp\left((4q+1)\sum_{j=1}^{N}(N+\frac{1}{2}-j)(k_{j}-q(j-1))\right).

(2.25)

Proof.

Using the commutation relations (

This is used to move an exponential of the momentum operators through:

\displaystyle\quad=W_{k_{j}-q(j-1)}e^{-(4q+1)(N+1/2-j)L_{0}}e^{-(4q+1)L_{0}/2}

\displaystyle\quad=e^{-(4q+1)(N+1/2-j)(L_{0}-(k_{j}-q(j-1)))}W_{k_{j}-q(j-1)}e^{-(4q+1)L_{0}/2}

3.1
Partitions, their addition and dominance order

Our definition of partition is slightly different from the one typically used in the mathematics literature (e.g. the standard definition of a partition considers positive integers and monotone decreasing families).

Definition 3.1
.

1. A
partition

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}
of an integer
|\lambda|\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is a monotone increasing family of nonnegative integers summing to
|\lambda|=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{k}
. The integer
k
is called the
length of the partition

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k})
, and we write
\ell(\lambda):=k
.

We call

m(\lambda,j)\coloneqq|\{k\in\{1,\dots,N\}\ :\ \lambda_{k}=j\}|

(3.1)

Remark 3.4

1. One should distinguish dominance order from the lexicographic order. Two partitions

This is established by the following short argument. Using

The reverse implication does not hold in general. For example,

2. In the definition of dominance order we could have replaced (

as we require

3.3
Squeezing and behavior of the cylindrical weight function

The squeezing operation is a method for sorting through the tree of partitions in dominance order.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\lambda
be a partition,
0\leq i<j\leq\ell(\lambda)
and
s\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
s\leq(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i})/2
,
\lambda_{i}<\lambda_{i+1},\lambda_{j}>\lambda_{j-1}
. Then a squeezing operator
R_{ij}^{s}
is called
\lambda
-admissible
, and it acts in the following way

\displaystyle R_{ij}^{s}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)})=S(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{i-1},\lambda_{i}+s,\lambda_{i+1},\dots,\lambda_{j-1},\lambda_{j}-s,\lambda_{j+1},\dots,\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}),

where
S
reorders the sequence into increasing order.

Squeezing operators applied to a partition yield a partition of the same integer and with the same length. As is well known
[
34
, Ch. 1]
, squeezings induce the dominance order. We summarize several facts from
[
34
, Ch. 1]
adapted to our setting in the following proposition. It is mainly included to be able to connect with the physicist’s notion of squeezing.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\lambda,\mu
be partitions of the same integer and the same length.

1.

For all
\lambda
-admissible squeezing operators
R_{i,j}^{s}
, we have

R_{i,j}^{s}\lambda\preceq\lambda
.

2.

The following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\lambda
dominates
\mu
.

(b)

There exists a finite sequence of
\lambda
-admissible squeezing operators

R_{i_{1},j_{1}}^{s_{1}},\dots,R_{i_{m},j_{m}}^{s_{m}}
such that

\mu=R_{i_{m},j_{m}}^{s_{m}}\dots R_{i_{1},j_{1}}^{s_{1}}\lambda
.

(c)

There exists a finite sequence of
\lambda
-admissible squeezing operators

R_{i_{1},j_{1}}^{1},\dots,R_{i_{m},j_{m}}^{1}
such that

\mu=R_{i_{m},j_{m}}^{1}\dots R_{i_{1},j_{1}}^{1}\lambda
.

Proof.

1. This is a straightforward computation using the definition of the dominance order.

2. Clearly,
c)
implies
b)
Using
1.
item
b)
implies
a)
Finally, we show that
a)
implies
c)
by induction over the length
\ell=\ell(\lambda)
. In case
\ell=1
there is nothing to show and the statement is trivially true. We provide an algorithm for the induction step. If
\lambda_{\ell}=\mu_{\ell}
, then we are back in the case
\ell-1
. Thus, we can assume that
\lambda_{\ell}>\mu_{\ell}
. We then define

s\coloneqq\max\{k\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}\ :\ \mu_{k}>\lambda_{k}\}
and
\tilde{\ell}\coloneqq\min\{k\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}\ :\ \lambda_{k}=\lambda_{\ell}\}
.
As
\lambda
and
\mu
partition the same integer, such an
s
must exist. The sqeezing algorithm sets
\tilde{\lambda}\coloneqq R_{s,\ell}^{1}\lambda
. We need to show that we still have
\mu\preceq\tilde{\lambda}
. Indeed, for
1\leq t\leq s
we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{t}+\dots+\tilde{\lambda}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{t}+\dots+\lambda_{s-1}+(\lambda_{s}+1)+\lambda_{s+1}+\dots+\lambda_{\ell-1}+(\lambda_{\ell}-1)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{t}+\dots+\lambda_{\ell}\geq\mu_{t}+\dots+\mu_{\ell}.

On the other hand, for
j\in\{s+1,\dots,\ell\}
we have, by definition of
s
,
\lambda_{j}\geq\mu_{j}
and
\lambda_{\ell}\geq\mu_{\ell}+1
. Thus, if
s+1\leq t\leq\tilde{\ell}
, this implies

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{t}+\dots+\tilde{\lambda}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{t}+\dots+\lambda_{\tilde{\ell}-1}+(\lambda_{\tilde{\ell}}-1)+\lambda_{\tilde{\ell}+1}+\dots+\lambda_{\ell}

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{t}+\dots+\mu_{\ell}.

The case
t>\tilde{\ell}
is trivial.
We can now repeat this algorithm until the leading coefficient is equal to
\mu_{\ell}
. Then we are back in the case
\ell-1
.
∎

A one-step squeezing operation may be thought of as a descent from a root partition on the directed graph of the partial order on partitions dominated by that root.
The quantity

\Delta_{b}(\mu)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)_{j}^{2}-\mu_{j}^{2}\right]

(3.12)

which, in the cylinder geometry, will help us control the geometric part of the fractional quantum Hall wavefunctions characterized by a root
(N,b)
, is easily seen to be strictly monotone under squeezing,

\Delta_{b}(R_{i,j}^{s}\mu)=\Delta_{b}(\mu)+2s(\mu_{j}-\mu_{i}-s)>\Delta_{b}(\mu)
for any
\mu
-admissible squeezing
R_{i,j}^{s}
. Moreover,
\Delta_{b}(\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b))=0
.
Hence, it is a measure of the distance to the root
\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)
in the graph of the partial order, i.e., the Hasse diagram.

The function

can be used to estimate the right side of the key estimate in Corollary

For any root

\Delta_{b}(\mu)-\Gamma_{b}(\mu)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\lambda_{j}^{2}-\mu_{j}^{2}-(N+\frac{1}{2}-j)(\mu_{j}-\lambda_{j})\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\lambda_{j}-\mu_{j})(N+\frac{1}{2}-1+\lambda_{j}+\mu_{j}).

\displaystyle d_{k}-d_{k-1}=\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{k-1}+\mu_{k}-\mu_{k-1}-1\geq q-1\geq 0.

Proposition 3.8

Let

be families of real numbers, then we have

The proof follows from a straightforward computation by exchanging two summations.

\sum_{j=1}^{s}\mu_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{s}(\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b))_{j}
.

Proof.

If
s
is a renewal point, then we can write
\mu=\mu^{(1)}\cup\mu^{(2)}
with
\mu^{(1)}\preceq\lambda^{(q)}_{s}\big(b^{(1)}\big)
,
\mu^{(2)}\preceq\lambda^{(q)}_{N-s}\big(b^{(2)}\big)
and
b=(b^{(1)},b^{(2)})
. Thus,
\sum_{j=1}^{s}\mu_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\mu^{(1)}_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{s}(\lambda_{s}^{(q)}(b^{(1)}))_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{s}(\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b))_{j}
.
To establish the reverse implication, we define
\mu^{(1)}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{s})
and
\mu^{(2)}=(\mu_{s+1}-qs-b_{s},\mu_{s+2}-qs-b_{s},\dots,\mu_{N}-qs-b_{s})
. We then have for any
k\in\{1,\dots,N-s\}
thanks to the domination order
\mu\preceq\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k}^{N-s}\mu^{(2)}_{j}=\sum_{j=k}^{N-s}(\mu_{s+j}-qs-b_{s})=\sum_{j=s+k}^{N}(\mu_{j}-qs-b_{s})\leq\sum_{j=s+k}^{N}((\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b))_{j}-qs-b_{s})=\sum_{j=k}^{N-s}(\lambda_{N-s}^{(q)}(b^{(2)}))_{j}.

If
\sum_{j=1}^{s}\mu_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)_{j}
, then
\mu\preceq\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)
implies that
\sum_{j=s+1}^{N}\mu_{j}=\sum_{j=s+1}^{N}\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)_{j}
. Hence, for any
k\in\{1,\dots,s\}
we arrive at:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k}^{s}\mu^{(1)}_{j}=\sum_{j=k}^{N}\mu_{j}-\sum_{j=s+1}^{N}\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)_{j}\leq\sum_{j=k}^{s}\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)_{j}=\sum_{j=k}^{s}\lambda_{s}^{(q)}(b^{(1)})_{j}.

It is hence straightforward to check that
\mu^{(1)}\preceq\lambda_{s}^{(q)}(b^{(1)})
.
∎

The concatenation of partitions is tailored such that the operator product

involving a partition

of the coefficient in the expansion of fractional quantum Hall wavefunctions, cf. (

.

Let

2.

\displaystyle w_{b}(\mu)=w_{b^{(2)}}\big(\mu^{(2)}\big)w_{b^{(1)}}\big(\mu^{(1)}\big)

For the decomposition of the fractional quantum Hall wavefunctions into sums of products of irreducible parts, in addition to the above factorization result of the geometry-independent part

of the expansion coefficients, we also need to ensure that their geometric part factorizes.
In the cylinder geometry, this is equivalent to the additivity of the quantity

Lemma 3.11
.

1.

If

and
\mu^{(2)}\preceq\lambda^{(q)}_{N_{2}}(b^{(2)})
with partitions

with

from (

), and we have

(3.20)

2.

Let

be irreducible, then

\Delta_{b}(\mu)\geq q(N-1)+b_{N}-b_{1}=:D_{b,N}.

(3.21)

Proof.

1. The first claim follows from a straightforward computation using the definition of dominance order. For (

) we compute

\displaystyle\Delta_{b}(\mu^{(1)}\cup\mu^{(2)})=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}+N_{2}}\left((q(j-1)+b_{j})^{2}-(\mu^{(1)}\cup\mu^{(2)})_{j}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}\left((q(j-1)+b_{j})^{2}-(\mu^{(1)}_{j})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=N_{1}+1}^{N_{2}}\left(q(j-1)+b_{N_{1}}^{(1)}+b_{j-N_{1}}^{(2)})^{2}-(qN_{1}+b_{N_{1}}^{(1)}+\mu^{(2)}_{j-N_{1}})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\Delta_{b^{(1)}}(\mu^{(1)})+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}\left((q(N_{1}+j-1)+b_{N_{1}}^{(1)}+b_{j}^{(2)})^{2}-(qN_{1}+b_{N_{1}}^{(1)}+\mu^{(2)}_{j})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\Delta_{b^{(1)}}(\mu^{(1)})+\Delta_{b^{(2)}}(\mu^{(2)})+2(qN_{1}+b_{N_{1}}^{(1)})\sum_{j=1}^{N_{2}}\left(q(j-1)+b_{j}^{(2)}-\mu_{j}^{(2)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta_{b^{(1)}}(\mu^{(1)})+\Delta_{b^{(2)}}(\mu^{(2)}),

which completes the proof of the first item.

2. In case
b=0
, this has already been established within the proof of
[
26
, Lemma 5]
(based on an argument from
[
15
]
), which we repeat here for the general case.
If
\mu\preceq\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)

\delta_{s}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left(\mu_{j}-q(j-1)-b_{j}\right)\geq 0
for all

. Moreover, since
\mu
is irreducible, only
\delta_{N}=0
and
\delta_{s}\geq 1
for all

. Setting

\displaystyle\Delta_{b}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(q(j-1)+b_{j}+\mu_{j}\right)\left(\delta_{j-1}-\delta_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\left(q+b_{j+1}-b_{j}+\mu_{j+1}-\mu_{j}\right)\delta_{j}+\left(q(N-1)+b_{N}+\mu_{N}\right)\delta_{N}

\displaystyle\geq q(N-1)+b_{N}-b_{1},

where the last step used

∎

3.5
Estimating irreducible contributions

The following is the key estimate of the geometry-independent coefficient of fractional quantum Hall wavefunctions.

Lemma 3.12
.

For any root
(N,b)
and all partitions
\lambda\preceq\lambda^{(q)}_{N}(b)
:

\left|w_{b}(\lambda)\right|\leq\frac{\exp\left((4q+1)\Gamma_{b}(\lambda)\right)}{\left(1-\exp\left[-(4q+1)\right]\right)^{N-1}}.

(3.22)

Proof.

We combine the key estimate from Corollary
2.7
with the representation (
3.19
) to obtain

\left|w_{b}(\lambda)\right|\leq\frac{1}{M(b)!}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{S}_{N}}\exp\left((4q+1)\Gamma_{b_{\tau}}(\lambda)\right),

(3.23)

where
b_{\tau}=(b_{\tau(1)},\cdots,b_{\tau(N)})
. To estimate the sum, we isolate the contribution involving the permutation
\tau
:

\Gamma_{b_{\tau}}(\lambda)=\Gamma_{b}(\lambda)+\sum_{j=1}^{N}j\left(b_{\tau(j)}-b_{j}\right).

In the following, we abbreviate

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{M}(\tau)\coloneqq(4q+1)\sum_{j=1}^{M}j(b_{\tau(j)}-b_{j})

and define
M_{j}=M(b_{1},\dots,b_{j})
. Our goal is to prove for
N\in\mathbb{N}
the recursive bound

\frac{1}{M_{N+1}!}\sum_{\tau\in S_{N+1}}\exp\left(\mathcal{S}_{N+1}(\tau)\right)\leq\frac{1}{1-e^{-(4q+1)}}\frac{1}{M_{N}!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\exp\left(\mathcal{S}_{N}(\sigma)\right).

(3.24)

For a proof, we first note that we can rewrite the left side:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M_{N+1}!}\sum_{\tau\in S_{N+1}}\exp\left(\mathcal{S}_{N+1}(\tau)\right)=\frac{1}{M_{N+1}!}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\in S_{N+1}\\
\tau(k)=N+1\end{subarray}}\exp\left(\mathcal{S}_{N+1}(\tau)\right).

In order to reduce to
S_{N}
we want to use the change of variables
Q_{k}:S_{N+1}\rightarrow S_{N+1},\tau\mapsto\tau\circ(N+1,k)
, where
(N+1,k)
denotes the transposition which swaps
N+1
and
k
and fixes everything else. If
\tau\in S_{N+1}
has the property
\tau(k)=N+1
, then
Q_{k}(\tau)(N+1)=N+1
, which is effectively a permutation in
S_{N}
. To make use of the change of variable
Q_{k}
, we need to express everything in terms of
Q_{k}(\tau)
.

We write

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}j(b_{\tau(j)}-b_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}j(b_{Q_{k}(\tau)(j)}-b_{j})-(N+1-k)(b_{N+1}-b_{Q_{k}(\tau)(k)})

and change variables in

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M_{N+1}!}\sum_{\tau\in S_{N+1}}\exp\left(\mathcal{S}_{N+1}(\tau)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M_{N+1}!}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau\in S_{N+1}\\
\tau(N+1)=N+1\end{subarray}}\exp\left(\mathcal{S}_{N+1}(\tau)\right)\exp\left(-(4q+1)(N+1-k)(b_{N+1}-b_{\tau(k)})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M_{N+1}!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\exp\left(\mathcal{S}_{N}(\sigma)\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\exp\left(-(4q+1)(N+1-k)(b_{N+1}-b_{k})\right)\right).

Abbreviating the set
[N+1]\coloneqq\{k\in\{1,\dots,N+1\}\ :\ b_{k}=b_{N+1}\}
,
there are exactly
|[N+1]|
elements
k\in\{1,\dots,N+1\}
for which
b_{k}=b_{N+1}
and for the remaining
k
we have
b_{N+1}-b_{k}\geq 1
. Thus, we may bound

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\exp\left(-(4q+1)(N+1-k)(b_{N+1}-b_{k})\right)\leq|[N+1]|+\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}e^{-(4q+1)k}\leq\frac{|[N+1]|}{1-e^{-(4q+1)}}.

Using
|[N+1]|/(M_{N+1}!)=1/(M_{N}!)
, we arrive at (
3.24
), which finishes the proof.
∎

In the cylinder geometry, the above estimate allows us to show that the contribution of the irreducible partitions to the norm of the fractional quantum Hall wavefunctions decays exponentially for sufficiently small cylinders. The decay rate

C_{q}(\gamma)=(\gamma^{2}-2(4q+1))-2q^{-1}\ln(1-e^{-(4q+1)})-\pi\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}
from (
1.26
)
is strictly positive for sufficiently large

.

Corollary 3.13
.

For any root
(N,b)
:

\big\|\widehat{\Psi}_{b,N}\big\|^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\preceq\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)\\
\lambda\;\small\textrm{irreducible}\end{subarray}}\frac{\big|g_{b}^{(c)}(\lambda)\ w_{b}(\lambda)\big|^{2}}{M(\lambda)!}\leq\exp\left(-C_{q}(\gamma)D_{b,N}\right).

Moreover,
\alpha_{1}(b)=1
for any

.

Proof.

We use (
3.22
) with the abbreviation

together with (
3.14
) and the trival bound

to estimate

\displaystyle\big\|\widehat{\Psi}_{b,N}\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\preceq\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)\\
\lambda\;\small\textrm{irreducible}\end{subarray}}\exp\left(-\left(\gamma^{2}-2(4q+1)\right)\Delta_{b}(\lambda)+(N-1)c_{q}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\lambda\preceq\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(b)}\exp\left(-\left(\gamma^{2}-2(4q+1)\right)\left(q(N-1)+b_{N}-b_{1}\right))+(N-1)c_{q}\right).

The last line is by (
3.21
).
To estimate the number of partitions
\lambda\preceq\lambda_{N}^{(q)}(N)
, we first note that the number
b_{1}
can be ignored. This is most easily see by noting that
\tilde{\lambda}=\lambda-(b_{1},\dots,b_{1})

Using the bound (

) on the number of partitions

and

4.3
) as

\displaystyle\sum_{M_{2}=2}^{N}\mkern-5.0mu\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M_{1},M_{3}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\\
M_{1}+M_{2}+M_{3}=N\end{subarray}}\mkern-15.0mu1\big[N_{1}\in[M_{1}+1,M_{1}+M_{2}-1]\big]\;\|\Psi_{b^{(1)},M_{1}}\odot\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},M_{2}}\odot\Psi_{b^{(3)},M_{3}}\|^{2},

with the convention that
\Psi_{b^{(1)},0}\odot\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},M_{2}}\odot\Psi_{b^{(3)},M_{3}}=\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},M_{2}}\odot\Psi_{b^{(3)},M_{3}}
and similarly, when
M_{3}=0
. Using the estimate (
3.25
) on the norm of the irreducible parts and the fact (
1.28
) that the norms of the wavefunctions are supermultiplicative, we arrive at

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{b^{(1)},M_{1}}\odot\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},M_{2}}\odot\Psi_{b^{(3)},M_{3}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\Psi_{b^{(1)},M_{1}}\|^{2}\ \|\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},M_{2}}\|^{2}\ \|\Psi_{b^{(3)},M_{3}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Psi_{b,N}\|^{2}\ e^{-C_{q}(\gamma)D_{b^{(2)},M_{2}}}.

For fixed
M_{2}
there are at most
M_{2}-1
choice for
(M_{1},M_{2},M_{3})
such that
N_{1}\in[M_{1}+1,M_{1}+M_{2}-1]
. Furthermore,
D_{b^{(2)},M_{2}}\geq q(M_{2}-1)
. Thus, we arrive at

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{b,N}-\Psi_{b^{(1)},N_{1}}\odot\Psi_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}\|^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{M_{2}=1}^{\infty}M_{2}e^{-C_{q}(\gamma)qM_{2}}\right)\|\Psi_{b,N}\|^{2}=\frac{e^{-C_{q}(\gamma)q}}{\left(1-e^{-C_{q}(\gamma)q}\right)^{2}}\|\Psi_{b,N}\|^{2},

which concludes the proof of (
4.2
).
∎

4.3
Proof of the exponential clustering

The proof of exponential clustering, Theorem
1.8
, proceeds in three steps. We first approximate the given wavefunction
\Psi_{b,N}
by a truncation, whose factor representation of Theorem (
1.5
) contains only irreducible segments intersecting the interval covering the support of the two observables. We then show exponential clustering for the truncated expectation value. The proof is concluded by a series of auxiliary results, in particular, the bounds on the expectation values in the bosonic case.

4.3.1
Truncation

We introduce the truncated versions of the fractional quantum Hall wavefunctions. The main idea is to allow only short segments in the representation (
1.23
). More precisely,
for a given interval
I=\{a,\dots,b\}=[a,b]\cap\{1,\dots,N\}
on the line of
N
monomers, we discard from (
1.23
) all segments, which extend over more than exactly one monomer and which contain particles indexed by an element in
I
, in case they have length as measured by (
3.21
) greater or equal to
l
.

Definition 4.1
.

For a root
(N,b)
and
l\in\mathbb{N}
and an interval
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}
, we abbreviate by
\mathcal{M}_{b,N}(I,l)
the set of segmentations,
(N_{1},\dots,N_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
,
\sum_{j=1}^{r}N_{j}=N
, which have the property that for all
j\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}N_{k},\sum_{k=1}^{j}N_{k}\right]\cap I\neq\emptyset\quad\Rightarrow\quad D_{b^{(j)},N_{j}}=q(N_{j}-1)+b^{(j)}_{\ell(b^{(j)})}-b^{(j)}_{1}<l.

(Recall that
\ell(b^{(j)})
is the length of the partition
b^{(j)}
which is the
j
th segment of
b
associated to the segmentation
(N_{1},\dots,N_{r})
.)
The corresponding truncated wavefunction is

\Psi_{b,N}^{(I,l)}\coloneqq\sum_{r=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N_{1},\dots,N_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
\sum_{j=1}^{r}N_{j}=N\end{subarray}}\mkern-10.0mu1[(N_{1},\dots,N_{r})\in\mathcal{M}_{b,N}(I,l)]\;\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(1)},N_{1}}\odot\cdots\odot\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(r)},N_{r}}.

(4.4)

Any fractional quantum Hall wavefunction is close to its truncated counterpart.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that
\gamma
is large enough such that
C_{q}(\gamma)>0
.
There exist a constant
C>0
, which depends only on
\gamma
and
q
, such that for any root
(N,b)
and interval
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}
and all
l>0

we have

\|\Psi_{b,N}-\Psi_{b,N}^{(I,l)}\|\leq C\sqrt{|I|}\ \sqrt{l^{2}+1}e^{-C_{q}(\gamma)l/2}\|\Psi_{b,N}\|.

(4.5)

Proof.

We start with the case, where
I=\{x\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}
, i.e., in the tiling decomposition of the root
(N,b)
, there is a single block, which contains
x
.
We proceed as in the proof of Theorem
1.7
. Comparing terms in (
1.23
) and (
4.4
) and using the orthogonality of the expansion (
1.23
), we conclude

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{b,N}-\Psi_{b,N}^{(\{x\},l)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{N_{2}=1}^{N}\mkern-5.0mu\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N_{1},N_{3}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\\
N_{1}+N_{2}+N_{3}=N\end{subarray}}\mkern-15.0mu1\big[x\in[N_{1}+1,N_{1}+N_{2}],D_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}\geq l\big]\;\|\Psi_{b^{(1)},N_{1}}\odot\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}\odot\Psi_{b^{(3)},N_{3}}\|^{2}

were he have again adopted the convention that
\Psi_{b^{(1)},0}\odot\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}\odot\Psi_{b^{(3)},N_{3}}=\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}\odot\Psi_{b^{(3)},N_{3}}
and similarly, when
N_{3}=0
.
Using the estimate (
3.25
) on the norm of the irreducible parts and the supermultiplicativity of the norms (
1.28
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{b^{(1)},N_{1}}\odot\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}\odot\Psi_{b^{(3)},N_{3}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\Psi_{b^{(1)},N_{1}}\|^{2}\ \|\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}\|^{2}\ \|\Psi_{b^{(3)},N_{3}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Psi_{b,N}\|^{2}\ e^{-C_{q}(\gamma)D_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}}.

For fixed
N_{2}
there are at most
N_{2}
choice for
(N_{1},N_{2},N_{3})
where
x
is contained in the second block. Thus, we arrive at

\displaystyle\|\Psi_{b,N}-\Psi_{b,N}^{(\{x\},l)}\|^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{N_{2}=1}^{l}N_{2}e^{-C_{q}(\gamma)\,l}+\sum_{N_{2}=l+1}^{\infty}N_{2}e^{-C_{q}(\gamma)q(N_{2}-1)}\right)\|\Psi_{b,N}\|^{2},

which yields the claim in the case
|I|=1
. The case of a general interval
I
, follows from the case
I=\{x\}
by a union-type bound.
∎

4.3.2
Bounds on the bosonic expectation values

In the fermionic case, local observables
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{loc}}
are bounded in norm. In the bosonic case, this ceases to be the case. The following lemma ensures that expectation values of observables in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{loc}}
on (truncated) fractional quantum Hall states are bounded. For the Laughlin state (
b=0
), sharper estimates have been obtained via Coulomb gas methods in
[
27
, Prop. 5.5]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For a root
(N,b)
and an interval
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}
and
l>0
,
and all finite multisets
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}
:

\begin{split}\|c_{X}^{*}c_{Y}\Psi_{b,N}\|&\leq C\|\Psi_{b,N}\|\\
\|c_{X}^{*}c_{Y}\Psi_{b,N}^{(I,l)}\|&\leq C\|\Psi_{b,N}\|,\end{split}

(4.6)

with
C
depending only on
C_{q}(\gamma),|X|
and
|Y|
.

Proof.

Since
\|c_{X}^{*}c_{Y}\Psi_{b,N}\|^{2}=\langle\Psi_{b,N},c_{Y}^{*}c_{X}c_{X}^{*}c_{Y}\Psi_{b,N}\rangle
, the canonical commutation rules allow to deduce the claim from a bound on terms of the form
\langle\Psi_{b,N},n_{X^{\prime}}n_{Y^{\prime}}\Psi_{b,N}\rangle
with suitable subsets
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
and
Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y
and
n_{X}=\prod_{x\in X}n_{x}
,
n_{x}=c_{x}^{*}c_{x}
.
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, this case is in turn traced back to the case
X=\{x\}=Y
. Indeed, using Cauchy-Schwarz repeatedly, we have

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{b,N},n_{X}n_{Y}\Psi_{b,N}\rangle\leq\|n_{X}\Psi_{b,N}\|\|n_{Y}\Psi_{b,N}\|\leq\left(\prod_{x\in X}\|n_{x}^{|X|}\Psi_{b,N}\|\right)^{\frac{1}{|X|}}\left(\prod_{y\in Y}\|n_{y}^{|Y|}\Psi_{b,N}\|\right)^{\frac{1}{|Y|}}.

It remains to consider a single factor
n_{x}^{s}
with
1\leq s\leq\max\{|X|,|Y|\}
arbitrary. We pick
L\in\{1,\dots,N\}
such that
x\in[b_{L-1}+q(L-1),b_{L}+qL-1]
(with the convention
b_{0}=0
). The orbital
x
is then contained in the
L
th elementary segment of the monomer segmentation (
1.17
) of the orbitals. Using the decomposition (
1.23
), we sort the irreducible parts according to the unique segment on which the orbital
x
lies and the rest. Resumming the rest (which is potentially to the right and left of that unique segment), we obtain

\|n_{x}^{s}\Psi_{b,N}\|^{2}=\mkern-5.0mu\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N_{1},N_{2},N_{3}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\\
N_{1}+N_{2}+N_{3}=N,\\
N_{2}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\mkern-5.0mu1[N_{1}<L,N_{1}+N_{2}\geq L]\|n_{x}^{s}(\Psi_{b^{(1)},N_{1}}\odot\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}\odot\Psi_{b^{(3)},N_{3}})\|^{2},

(4.7)

with again the convention that
\Psi_{b^{(1)},0}\odot\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}\odot\Psi_{b^{(3)},N_{3}}=\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}\odot\Psi_{b^{(3)},N_{3}}
and similarly, when
N_{3}=0
.
Using the fact that the second segment has
N_{2}
particles, the norm estimate (
3.25
) and the supermultiplicativity of the norms (
1.28
), we estimate

\displaystyle\|n_{x}^{s}(\Psi_{b^{(1)},N_{1}}\odot\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}\odot\Psi_{b^{(3)},N_{3}})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq N_{2}^{2s}\|\Psi_{b^{(1)},N_{1}}\|^{2}\|\widehat{\Psi}_{b^{(2)},N_{2}}\|^{2}\|\Psi_{b^{(3)},N_{3}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq N_{2}^{2s}e^{-C_{q}(\gamma)q(N_{2}-1)}\|\Psi_{b,N}\|^{2}.

For fixed
N_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
there are at most
N_{2}
choices of
(N_{1},N_{2},N_{3})
satisfying the constraints in (
4.7
). Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\|n_{x}^{s}\Psi_{b,N}\|^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{2s+1}e^{-C_{q}(\gamma)q(k-1)}\right)\|\Psi_{b,N}\|^{2}.

The second inequality in (
4.6
) follows from the previous argument combined with the estimate

\displaystyle\|n_{x}^{s}\Psi_{b,N}^{(I,l)}\|\leq\|n_{x}^{s}\Psi_{b,N}\|,

which follows from the orthogonality of the expansion (
1.23
).
∎

4.3.3
Preparations and auxiliary results

As already done in Lemma
4.2
, it will become convenient to identify partitions with multisets on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
via its occupation numbers.
Multisets are given by functions
A:\mathbb{N}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We define the union of two multisets
A,B
as
A\cup B:\mathbb{N}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}_{0},(A\cup B)(n)=A(n)+B(n)
, the intersection as
A\cap B:\mathbb{N}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}_{0},(A\cap B)(n)=\min\{A(n),B(n)\}
, and the difference as
(A\setminus B):\mathbb{N}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}_{0},(A\setminus B)(n)=\min\{0,A(n)-B(n)\}
. Finally, the cardinality of a multiset
A
is
|A|=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}A(n)
. Furthermore, we define
\max(A)=\max_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}A(n),\min(A)=\min_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}A(n)
and
\dist(A,B)=\dist(A^{-1}(\mathbb{N}),B^{-1}(\mathbb{N}))
.

From now on, we fix the observables

A=c_{K^{\prime}}^{*}c_{K}\quad\mbox{and}\quad B=c_{L^{\prime}}^{*}c_{L}

corresponding to finite multisets
K,K^{\prime},L,L^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We define
\mathrm{supp}(A)=K^{\prime}\cup K
and
\mathrm{supp}(B)=L^{\prime}\cup L
. Our standing assumption will be
\max\mathrm{supp}(A)<\min\mathrm{supp}(B)
. To prove exponential clustering, we will define a truncated Laughlin wavefunction tailored to
A,B
. Through

\begin{split}\textstyle\min_{q}(A)&=\min\{n\in\{1,\dots,N\}\ :\ [b_{n-1}+q(n-1),b_{n}+qn)\cap\mathrm{supp}(A)\neq\emptyset\},\\
\textstyle\max_{q}(A)&=\max\{n\in\{1,\dots,N\}\ :\ [b_{n-1}+q(n-1),b_{n}+qn)\cap\mathrm{supp}(A)\neq\emptyset\},\end{split}

(4.8)

with the convention
b_{0}\coloneqq 0
, we identify the first and last elementary block (’monomer plus potentially preceding voids’) in the root tiling, which has a non-empty intersection with
A
. Similarly,
\min_{q}(B)
and
\max_{q}(B)
identify the first and last elementary segment that intersects the support of
B
. We further abbreviate

\begin{split}d_{AB}&=\mathrm{dist}(\mathrm{supp}(A),\mathrm{supp}(B)),\quad I_{AB}=[\textstyle\max_{q}(A)-d_{AB},\textstyle\min_{q}(B)+d_{AB}]\cap\{1,\dots,N\},\\
l_{AB}&=d_{AB}/3,\hskip 85.35826pt\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}=\Psi_{b,N}^{(I_{AB},l_{AB})}.\end{split}

(4.9)

This truncation yields a good approximation for our purpose.

Corollary 4.3
.

Suppose that
\gamma
is large enough such that
C_{q}(\gamma)>0
. There exist a constant
C>0
, which depends only on
\gamma
and
q
, such that for any root
(N,b)
, we have

\|\Psi_{b,N}-\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}\|\leq Cd_{AB}^{3/2}\,e^{-C_{q}(\gamma)d_{AB}/6}\|\Psi_{b,N}\|.

(4.10)

Proof.

The claim follows from Lemma
4.1
and the crude bound
\min_{q}(B)-\max_{q}(A)\leq d_{AB}
.
∎

Our next aim is to show that
\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}
exhibits exponential clustering. To do so, we need some auxiliary combinatorial lemmata. We abbreviate by
\mathcal{M}_{b,N}^{(A,B)}
the set of partitions which have a nonzero expansion coefficient in
\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}
, such that

\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{M}_{b,N}^{(A,B)}}\mkern-10.0muh_{b}(\lambda)\ \Phi_{\lambda},

(4.11)

and identify through

\begin{split}L_{1}&\coloneqq\max\{n\in\{1,\dots,N\}\ :\ b_{n-1}+q(n-1)\leq\max\mathrm{supp}(A)+d_{AB}/3\},\\
L_{2}&\coloneqq\ \min\{n\in\{1,\dots,N\}\ :\ b_{n}+qn\geq\min\mathrm{supp}(B)-d_{AB}/3\},\end{split}

(4.12)

the last elementary block to the right of the support of
A
at a distance at most
d_{AB}/3
from it, respectively the first elementary block which starts at a distance at most
d_{AB}/3
to the left of the support of
B
.

Because of the truncation, the elements in
\mathcal{M}_{b,N}^{(A,B)}
have a special renewal structure. Either the elementary blocks corresponding to
\max_{q}(A)
and
\min_{q}(B)
are shorter than
d_{AB}/3
, or there is a long elementary block, which is separated by renewal points.

Let

and before

L_{2}
and

.

In the first case of Lemma

Let

For an estimate of each of the sums in case

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{r}\|\widehat{\Psi}_{b(n+\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}N_{k},n-1+\sum_{k=1}^{j}N_{k})}^{(AB)}\|^{2}\leq\exp\left(-C_{q}(\gamma)(d(n,m)-qr-R(\mathbf{N}))\right)

4.3.4
Proof of Theorem
1.8

We first prove the exponential clustering for the truncated wavefunction
\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

In the situation of Theorem
1.8
, there exists a constant
C>0
depending only on the cardinality of
\supp(A)
and
\supp(B)
such that

\begin{split}&\big|\langle\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)},AB\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}\rangle\ \|\Psi_{b,N}\|^{2}-\langle\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)},A\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}\rangle\langle\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)},B\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}\rangle\big|\\
&\qquad\leq Cd_{AB}^{4}e^{-C_{q}(\gamma)d_{AB}/3+2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2d_{AB}}{3}}}\|\Psi_{b,N}\|^{4}.\end{split}

(4.29)

Proof.

According to Lemma
4.4
, there are two cases.

If there is an elementary block of length greater or equal to
d_{A_{B}}/3
with non-empty intersection with the orbitals in
[\max\supp A+d_{AB}/3,\min\supp B-d_{AB}/3]
, the wavefunction factorizes over the long elementary block (
4.20
) and hence the left side in (
4.29
) is zero.

In the other case,
\max_{q}(A)<L_{1}<L_{2}<\min_{q}(B)
, we expand and use the conditioning on the first and last renewal point in (
4.23
). Since every
\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{b,N}^{(A,B)}
with
\langle\Phi_{\lambda},AB\Phi_{\mu}\rangle\neq 0
must share all renewal points in
(\max_{q}(A),\min_{q}(B))
, we write

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)},AB\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}\rangle\|\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}\|^{2}-\langle\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)},A\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}\rangle\langle\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)},B\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in(\max_{q}(A),L_{1}]\\
m\in[L_{2},\min_{q}(B))\end{subarray}}f_{A}(n)f_{B}(m)\left(\|\Psi_{b(n+1,m)}^{(AB)}\|^{2}\|\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}\|^{2}-\|\Psi_{b(m+1,N)}^{(AB)}\|^{2}\|\Psi_{b(1,n)}^{(AB)}\|^{2}\right).

Here, we abbreviated for
n\in(\max_{q}(A),\min_{q}(B))
:

\displaystyle f_{A}(n)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mkern-30.0mu\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{b(1,n)}(A,B)\\
\text{no renewal point in }(\max_{q}(A),n)\end{subarray}}\mkern-30.0muh_{b(1,n)}(\lambda)\ h_{b(1,n)}(\mu)\ \langle\Phi_{\mu},A\Phi_{\lambda}\rangle,

\displaystyle f_{B}(m)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mkern-30.0mu\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda,\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{b(m+1,N)}(A,B)\\
\text{no renewal point in }[1,\min_{q}(B)-m)\end{subarray}}\mkern-30.0muh_{b(m+1,N)}(\lambda)\ h_{b(m+1,N)}(\mu)\ \langle\Phi_{\lambda^{(q)}_{b(1,m)}\cup\mu},B\Phi_{\lambda_{b(1,m)}^{(q)}\cup\lambda}\rangle.

A Cauchy-Schwarz estimate and Lemma
4.2
implies that there are
C_{A},C_{B}\in(0,\infty)
, which only depend on the cardinality of
\supp(A)
and
\supp(B)
, such that

\displaystyle|f_{A}(n)|\leq C_{A}\|\Psi_{b(1,n),n}\|^{2},\qquad|f_{B}(m)|\leq C_{B}\|\Psi_{b(m+1,N),N-m}\|^{2}.

Combining this with (
4.26
) and the crude estimates,
L_{1}-\max_{q}(A)-1\leq d_{AB},\min_{q}(B)-1-L_{2}\leq d_{AB}
, as well as the supermultiplicativity of the norm (
1.28
), we arrive at the claim.
∎

We finally have all the ingredients to prove exponential clustering of the untruncated wavefunction.

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

The triangle inequality combined with a Cauchy-Schwarz estimate yields

\displaystyle|\langle\Psi_{b,N},O\Psi_{b,N}\rangle-\langle\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)},O\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}\rangle|\leq\left(\|O\Psi_{b,N}\|+\|O\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}\|\right)\|\Psi_{b,N}-\Psi_{b,N}^{(AB)}\|

for any

∎

Appendix A
Renewal analysis and the norm of the Laughlin wavefunction

In this appendix, we gather known results on the analysis of discrete renewal equations and prove some variations of such results. We refer to
[
36
]
and references therein for the general background on renewal theory. We then show how to combine these results with Theorem
1.5
for a quantitative estimate of the norm of the Laughlin wavefunction. This improves results in
[
26
]
.

A.1
Basics

In the following we consider solutions
(C_{n})
of the discrete renewal equations

C_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{j}C_{n-j}+\beta_{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

(A.1)

defined in terms of two non-negative sequences
(\alpha_{n}),(\beta_{n})\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
.
Associated to these sequences are the power series

\alpha(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}z^{n},\qquad\beta(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\beta_{n}z^{n},

(A.2)

whose radii of convergence we denote by
R_{\alpha}
and
R_{\beta}
, respectively. By resumming terms, it is easy to see that the power series

C(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}C_{n}z^{n}

associated to any solution of (
A.1
) satisfies:

C(z)\left(1-\alpha(z)\right)=\beta(z).

(A.3)

Lemma A.1
.

If
R_{\alpha}>0
and
\alpha_{1}>0
, then
the radius of convergence
r
of the power series
C(z)
satisfies:

r=\min\left\{R_{\beta},\min\{t\in[0,R_{\alpha}]\ :\ \alpha(t)=1\}\right\}\leq\min\left\{R_{\beta},\alpha_{1}^{-1}\right\}.

Proof.

Since
(C_{n})\in[0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
, the radius of convergence of
C(z)
equals

r=\sup\left\{t\in[0,\infty)\ :\ C(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}C_{n}t^{n}<\infty\right\}.

By (
A.3
), this radius is restricted by: 1. the radius of convergence of
\beta(z)
, and 2. the first solution on
[0,\infty)
of the equation
\alpha(t)=1
. Since

\alpha(0)=0
and, for any
0\leq t<R_{\alpha}
also
\alpha^{\prime}(t)\geq\alpha^{\prime}(0)=\alpha_{1}>0
, the first solution on
[0,\infty)
of
\alpha(t)=1
is in the interval
(0,\alpha_{1}^{-1}]
. This finishes the proof.
∎

A.2
Criteria for exponential convergence

By the above lemma, in case
0<r<\min\{R_{\alpha},R_{\beta}\}
, then
r=\min\{t\in[0,R_{\alpha}]\ \big|\ \alpha(t)=1\}
, which means that
p_{n}\coloneqq\alpha_{n}r^{n}
is a probability distribution on
\mathbb{N}
whose mean

\mu\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\alpha_{n}r^{n}

(A.4)

is finite. In this situation, it is known
[
20
, XIII, Theorem 1]
that

\lim_{n\to\infty}C_{n}r^{n}=\frac{\beta(r)}{\mu}.

(A.5)

The following criterion ensures that the rate of this convergence is exponential with an explicit rate. The idea of the proof is very similar to the one in
[
7
]
.

Theorem A.2
.

Assume that
0<r<1\leq R_{\beta}
and that there exist constants
c,C>0
such that

\alpha_{n}\leq Ce^{-c(n-1)}\qquad\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

(A.6)

Then, we have

|C_{n}r^{n}-\beta(r)/\mu|=\mathcal{O}(R^{-n}),\qquad\text{ for }n\rightarrow\infty.

(A.7)

for any

0<R<\min\left\{\frac{R_{\beta}}{r},\frac{e^{c}}{r}\left(\frac{1}{1+\frac{C}{1-e^{-c}}}\right)\right\}.

Proof.

Note that
R_{\alpha}\geq e^{c}>1
by the exponential bound (
A.6
), and hence
\mu<\infty
.
We want to estimate

\displaystyle x_{n}=C_{n}r^{n}-\frac{\beta(r)}{\mu}.

For
|z|<1
all the power series below converge due to our assumptions and we have, using (
A.3
),

X(z):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{n}z^{n}=C(rz)-\frac{\beta(r)}{\mu}\frac{z}{1-z}=\frac{\beta(rz)}{1-\alpha(rz)}-\frac{\beta(r)}{\mu}\frac{z}{1-z}.

(A.8)

As
\alpha(r)=1
, we get
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{n}=1
and therefore,

\displaystyle\alpha(rz)-1

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{n}z^{n}-1=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{n}(z^{n}-1)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{n}(z-1)\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}z^{j}

\displaystyle=(z-1)\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}p_{n}\right)z^{k}\right)

where the last step is by interchanging the sums. Inserting this into (
A.8
) yields

\displaystyle X(z)=\frac{1}{1-z}\left(\frac{\beta(rz)}{1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}p_{n}\right)z^{k}}-\frac{\beta(r)}{\mu}z\right).

The second factor vanishes for
z=1
, since
\mu=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}p_{n}\right)
. Moreover, it has radius of convergence
R_{X}
equal to the minimum of
R_{\beta}/r>R_{\beta}\geq 1
and

\displaystyle R_{0}\coloneqq\min\left\{|z|\ :\ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}p_{n}\right)z^{k}=-1\right\}.

Thus,
X(z)
also has radius of convergence
R_{X}=\min\{R_{\beta}/r,R_{0}\}
.

We now need to establish a lower bound for
R_{0}
. For this we define

\displaystyle G(z)\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}p_{n}\right)z^{k}.

As
p_{n}\geq 0
, we get

|G(z)|\leq G(|z|)
, and hence

R_{0}\geq\min\left\{t\in[0,\infty)\ :\ G(t)=1\right\}
.
Using
r\leq 1
and (
A.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle G(|z|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}r^{n}\right)|z|^{k}\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}e^{-c(n-1)}\right)|rz|^{k}

\displaystyle=C\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-ck}}{1-e^{-c}}|rz|^{k}=\frac{C}{1-e^{-c}}\frac{|rz|e^{-c}}{1-|rz|e^{-c}}.

The solution of

\frac{C}{1-e^{-c}}\frac{|rz|e^{-c}}{1-|rz|e^{-c}}=1

is equal to

|z|=\frac{e^{c}}{r}\left(\frac{1}{1+\frac{C}{1-e^{-c}}}\right)
.
Thus, the radius of convergence of
X(z)
is bounded from below by

\displaystyle R_{X}\geq\min\left\{\frac{R_{\beta}}{r},\frac{e^{c}}{r}\left(\frac{1}{1+\frac{C}{1-e^{-c}}}\right)\right\}.

∎

A.3
Implication for the norm of the Laughlin wavefunction on cylinder

In case of the Laughlin wavefunction,
b=0
, on the cylinder, the norm-squared
\|\Psi_{0,N}\|^{2}
satisfies the discrete renewal equation (
A.1
) with
\alpha_{n}=\beta_{n}

given by the irreducible contribution (
3.25
) to the norm (with
b=0
).
More generally, if
M\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
b^{(2)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{M}
is a partition, then
b=\big(0,\dots,0,b^{(2)}\big)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N}
with
N>M
models a fractional quantum Hall state
which is a Laughlin state in the bulk, but a thinned-out version at the right boundary. Renewal theory then implies

Corollary A.3
.

Assume that
C_{q}(\gamma)>0
and let
M\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be fixed and
b^{(2)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{M}
be a partition. Then the norm-squared

C_{N}\coloneqq\big\|\Psi_{b,N}\big\|^{2}

of the fractional quantum Hall wavefunction associated with
b=\big(0,\dots,0,b^{(2)}\big)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{N}
and
N>M
grows exponentially with a rate

r^{-1}\coloneqq\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\ln C_{N}}{N}=\sup_{N\geq M}\frac{\ln C_{N}}{N}>1,

(A.9)

which does not depend on
b^{(2)}
.
The power series (
A.2
) with
\alpha_{n}=\|\widehat{\Psi}_{0,n}\|^{2}
and
\beta_{n}=\|\widehat{\Psi}_{b,n}\|^{2}

converge for
|z|<e^{C_{q}(\gamma)q}\leq R_{\beta}
and we have
r>0
.
Moreover, the finite-
N
estimate (
A.7
) holds with

R<\frac{e^{C_{q}(\gamma)q}}{r}\left(\frac{1}{1+\frac{e^{-b^{(2)}_{N}}}{1-e^{-C_{q}(\gamma)q}}}\right).

(A.10)

Proof.

Keeping
M
fixed, the norm-squared
C_{n}\coloneqq\|\Psi_{b,n}\|^{2}
satisfies (
A.1
)
with
\alpha_{n}=\|\widehat{\Psi}_{0,n}\|^{2}
and
\beta_{n}=\|\widehat{\Psi}_{b,n}\|^{2}
. This is the content of (
4.1
).

The existence of the limit and its representation as a supremum in (
A.9
) follows from Fekete’s lemma by the supermultiplicativity (
1.28
).
Together with (
A.1
), this implies
C_{N+2}\geq(1+\alpha_{2})C_{N}
, and therefore
r^{-1}\geq 1+\alpha_{2}>1.
The rate does not depend on
b^{(2)}
, as we get from Theorem
1.7
that there exists a constant
K>0
such that for all
N>M
we have
K^{-1}C_{M}\|\Psi_{N}\|^{2}\leq C_{N}\leq KC_{M}\|\Psi_{N}\|^{2}
. The claim about the radii of convergence follows from the exponential bound (
3.25
).
By general renewal theory
[
20
, XIII, Theorem 1]
, one then has
r>0
and (
A.5
) holds. The proof is finished by an application of Theorem
A.2
.
∎

Appendix B
Comparison with previous constructions

In this appendix, we review the construction of the iMPS representation of
[
19
]
and explain how to derive it from our representation rigorously. In particular, we rigorously link the mode operators of
[
19
]
with the operators defined and studied in Section
2
.

B.1
Formal construction of chiral CFT fields

The work
[
19
]
formally starts with a (pre-)Hilbert space generated by operators
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\mathrm{exp}(\pm i\varphi_{0})
acting on some abstract vacuum vector
|0\rangle
, which satisfy the following commutation relations

[a_{n},a_{m}]=n\delta_{n+m,0},\qquad[\varphi_{0},a_{n}]=i\delta_{n,0},\qquad\text{for all }n,m\in\mathbb{Z},

(B.1)

and which annihilate the vacuum for positive integers,

a_{n}|0\rangle=0,\qquad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

(B.2)

One needs to be cautious with the interpretation of these requirements.
As is well known
[
54
]
, all irreducible representations of
[\varphi_{0},a_{0}]=i
, when realized as hermitian operators as is assumed here, are unitarily equivalent to the position and momentum operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which do not admit a vacuum as in (
B.2
).

This is bypassed by not working with irreducible representations and
\varphi_{0}
directly. We rather give meaning to a suitable version of
\exp(\pm i\varphi_{0})
only. To do so,
one takes copies of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
defined in Section
2
, and sets

\mathcal{G}=\bigoplus_{N\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{H}.

(B.3)

If one labels the vacuum in the
N
th copy of
\mathcal{H}
by
|N\rangle
with
N\in\mathbb{Z}
, the copies are linked by the action of a unitary group,

|N\rangle=\exp\left(i\frac{N}{\sqrt{q}}\varphi_{0}\right)|0\rangle,

and the fact that these vacua are the highest weight states with respect to the operators
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
:

a_{0}|N\rangle=\frac{N}{\sqrt{q}}|N\rangle,\qquad a_{n}|N\rangle=0\qquad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Moreover, the scalar product is chosen in such a way that

\displaystyle a_{n}^{*}=a_{-n},\qquad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Central operators in the construction of
[
19
]
are the vertex operators
V(z)
labeled by
z\in\mathbb{C}
and acting in
\mathcal{G}
. They are formal Laurent series with coefficients in the space of linear operators
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{G})
. This means
V(z)\in\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{G})[[z,z^{-1}]]
can be represented formally as

\displaystyle V(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}b_{n}z^{n},\qquad b_{n}\in\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{G}).

Such operators are called a field if for each
v\in\mathcal{G}
there exists
K_{v}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that we have
b_{n}v=0
for all
n<K_{v}
. For a product
a_{n_{1}}\dots a_{n_{m}}
, its normal order to be given by

:a_{n_{1}}\dots a_{n_{m}}:=a_{\tau(n_{1})}\dots a_{\tau(n_{m})}

where
\tau
is a permutation such that
\tau(n_{j})\geq\tau(n_{k})
for all
1\leq j\leq k\leq m
.
In
[
19
]
it is proposed to use the normal-ordered exponential
:\exp(i\sqrt{q}\varphi(z)):
as the vertex operator, where
\varphi
is the free field

\displaystyle\varphi(z)=\varphi_{0}-ia_{0}\log(z)+i\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{n}a_{n}z^{-n}.

Despite its name, the free field is not a field in the above sense, and the use of
\log(z)
does not make sense: in order to expand the logarithm into a Laurent series centred around zero it would need to be analytic in an annulus around the origin, which is not the case. One should think of
:\exp(\sqrt{q}a_{0}\log(z)):
as some formal object which one needs to attach meaning to a posteriori.

B.2
Definition of vertex and mode operators and iMPS representation

By the definition of the normal ordering, the vertex operators in the above construction are equal to

\displaystyle V(z)=\exp\left(-\sqrt{q}S_{-}(z)\right)\exp\left(-\sqrt{q}S_{+}(z)\right)\exp(i\sqrt{q}\varphi_{0})\exp(\sqrt{q}a_{0}\log(z)),

(B.4)

with
S_{-}(z),S_{+}(z)
as in (
2.8
). To give an analytical meaning to these operators, one proceeds as in Section
2
and defines the subspaces

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{0,N}\coloneqq\mathrm{span}\left\{\left(\prod_{k=1}^{L}(a_{k}^{*})^{n_{k}}\right)|N\rangle\ :\ L\in\mathbb{N}_{0},n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\}

whose closures
\mathcal{G}_{N}\coloneqq\overline{\mathcal{G}_{0,N}}
then yield the full Hilbert space
\mathcal{G}=\bigoplus_{N\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{G}_{N}
.
We denote by
P_{N}
the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{G}_{N}
, and set
T
on the dense domain
\mathcal{G}_{0}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{N\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{G}_{0,N}

\displaystyle T:\mathcal{G}_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{G}_{0}\quad T|N\rangle\coloneqq|N+q\rangle,\;\mbox{and extended through the requirement}\quad[T,a_{n}^{*}]=0\;\mbox{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$.}

The operator
T
then extends to a unitary operator on
\mathcal{G}
.
Also extending the operators
W_{m}
defined in (
2.13
) to the direct sum
\mathcal{G}_{0}
, the mode operator for
m\in\mathbb{Z}
is then defined on the dense domain
\mathcal{G}_{0}
by

V_{-m-h}\coloneqq\sum_{N\in\mathbb{Z}}W_{m-N}TP_{N}.

(B.5)

The integer offset
h
is referred to as the conformal dimension of the vertex operator

V(z)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}V_{-m-h}z^{m}.

(B.6)

when interpreted as a formal Laurent series. Note that
the operators
T
and
P_{N}
are bounded and both commute with all
W_{m}
’s. Since
a_{0}v_{N}=(N/\sqrt{q})v_{N}
for all
v_{N}\in\mathcal{G}_{N}
, the action of the last factor in (
B.4
) is hence

\displaystyle\exp(\sqrt{q}a_{0}\log(z))v_{N}=\exp(\sqrt{q}(m/\sqrt{q})\log(z))v_{N}=z^{N}v_{N}=z^{N}P_{N}v_{N}

for all
z\neq 0
.
The second factor in (
B.4
) equals
T
. The first factor agrees with
W(z)
in (
2.9
). Consequently, the definition (
B.5
) indeed gives an analytical meaning to
the normal ordered vertex operator (
B.4
).

The iMPS representation of the fractional quantum Hall wavefunctions derived in Section
2
may be reformulated in terms of the mode operators. To do so, we note that for every
w_{N}\in\mathcal{G}_{N}
, we have

V_{-m-h}w_{N}=W_{m-N}Tw_{N}
, and hence for any
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
:

\displaystyle\langle qN|V_{-k_{1}-h}\dots V_{-k_{N}-h}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle qN|W_{k_{1}-q(N-1)}W_{k_{2}-q(N-2)}\dots W_{k_{N-1}-q}W_{k_{N}}|qN\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle 0|W_{k_{1}-q(N-1)}W_{m_{2}-q(N-2)}\dots W_{k_{N-1}-q}W_{k_{N}}|0\rangle,

(B.7)

where we have used that the
W_{m}
’s commute with
T
and that
T
is unitary.
Using the permutation property (
2.18
) of the
W_{m}
’s, we also conclude
that for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
:

\langle qN|V_{-k_{\sigma(1)}-h}\dots V_{-k_{\sigma(N)}-h}|0\rangle=\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)^{q}\ \langle qN|V_{k_{1}-h}\dots V_{k_{N}-h}|0\rangle.

(B.8)

Remark B.1
.

Despite being repeated throughout the literature, the mode operators
V_{-m-h}
do not (anti)commute with each other in general. Indeed, one easily computes

V_{-m-h}V_{-k-h}|\ell\rangle=W_{m-q-\ell}W_{k}|2q+\ell\rangle

and hence for
m=0,k=-q
and
\ell=-2q
, we arrive at

\displaystyle V_{0-h}V_{-(-q)-h}|0\rangle=W_{q}W_{-q}|0\rangle=0\neq|0\rangle=W_{0}W_{0}|0\rangle=V_{-(-q)-h}V_{0-h}|0\rangle.
