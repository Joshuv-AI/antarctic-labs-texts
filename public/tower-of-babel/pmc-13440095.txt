Lumbar–ventricular cerebrospinal fluid pressure discrepancy in leptomeningeal metastasis associated intracranial hypertension
Authors: Kyung Won Chang, Wu Jin Jeon, Won Jae Lee, Kyuha Chong, Seung Hoon Lee, Jung-Won Choi, Doo-sik Kong, Ho Jun Seol, Do-Hyun Nam, Jung-Il Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeptomeningeal metastasis (LM) frequently causes intracranial hypertension (IH) due to disruption of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation. However, the relationship between lumbar and ventricular CSF pressures in LM has not been systematically evaluated. We investigated the frequency, magnitude, and determinants of lumbar–ventricular CSF pressure discrepancy in LM patients undergoing ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS) placement.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 34 patients with LM who underwent VPS between 2015 and 2023. Lumbar opening pressure was measured preoperatively by lumbar puncture, and ventricular pressure was measured intraoperatively before CSF diversion. A clinically significant pressure discrepancy (SPD) was defined as a lumbar–ventricular pressure difference (ΔP) ≥ 5 cmH₂O, and a marked pressure discrepancy (MPD) as ΔP ≥ 10 cmH₂O. Clinical, radiologic, and cytologic variables were compared between groups. Univariate logistic regression was performed to identify predictors of SPD and MPD.ResultsMean lumbar and ventricular CSF pressures were 20.9 ± 8.9 cmH₂O and 31.1 ± 11.3 cmH₂O, respectively (mean ΔP = 10.2 cmH₂O; p < 0.001). SPD was present in 25 patients (73.5%), and MPD in 18 patients (52.9%). Ventricular CSF pressure was significantly higher in patients with SPD and MPD, whereas lumbar pressure did not differ between groups. More than half of the cohort (52.9%) exhibited IH without ventriculomegaly. In logistic regression analyses, ventricular pressure was the only variable significantly associated with both SPD (OR 1.216, p = 0.012) and MPD (OR 1.137, p = 0.009).ConclusionsLumbar–ventricular CSF pressure discrepancies are common in LM and are primarily driven by elevated ventricular pressure, reflecting impaired craniospinal pressure transmission. Lumbar pressure may substantially underestimate true intracranial pressure, particularly in the absence of ventriculomegaly. Recognition of this dissociation is critical for accurate assessment and management of LM-associated intracranial hypertension.Keywords: Leptomeningeal metastasis, Intracranial hypertension, Cerebrospinal fluid dynamics, Pressure discrepancy, Ventriculoperitoneal shunt, Lumbar puncture

Background

Leptomeningeal metastasis (LM), also known as meningeal carcinomatosis, represents the diffuse or multifocal infiltration of malignant cells into the leptomeninges, with or without parenchymal involvement [1, 2]. It develops in approximately 5% of patients with solid tumors [3, 4]; however, autopsy studies have revealed a prevalence exceeding the incidence rate known, suggesting that LM is frequently underdiagnosed [5–7].

Malignant cells can access the subarachnoid space through hematogenous dissemination, direct parenchymal invasion, or perineural and perivascular extension [7]. Once infiltrated within the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways, they spread throughout the neuroaxis, coating the meninges, cranial nerves, and spinal cord surfaces [7, 8]. This diffuse infiltration disrupts CSF circulation and absorption, leading to pleomorphic neurological symptoms and complications such as hydrocephalus and intracranial hypertension (IH) [8].

The diagnosis of LM relies on positive CSF cytology and/or characteristic contrast-enhanced MRI findings [6]. Although cytological confirmation remains the diagnostic gold standard, MRI has become increasingly indispensable for demonstrating leptomeningeal enhancement and assessing CSF flow obstruction [6, 9].

Ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS) is commonly performed as a palliative intervention for LM patients presenting with IH or hydrocephalus [1, 3]. Despite limited high-level evidence, previous studies have reported symptomatic relief in 70–80% of cases, and some suggest that VPS can facilitate continuation of systemic anticancer therapy [10–12]. Nevertheless, criteria for VPS candidacy remain ambiguous, particularly in patients with borderline or low lumbar opening pressure and without overt hydrocephalus on imaging [13].

Clinically, we have repeatedly observed that during VPS procedures for LM-associated IH, intraventricular CSF pressure measured intraoperatively often exceeds the lumbar opening pressure recorded preoperatively. These recurrent discrepancies raise questions regarding altered CSF pressure transmission between the cranial and spinal compartments in LM.

The present study therefore aims to quantitatively compare lumbar and ventricular CSF pressures in LM patients undergoing VPS, and to characterize the frequency, magnitude, and potential clinical relevance of pressure discrepancies, which may reflect impaired CSF hydrodynamics or barrier dysfunction within the subarachnoid pathways.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective study included patients diagnosed with leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) who underwent ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS) placement at Samsung Medical Center between January 2015 and December 2023. All patients presented with symptoms suggestive of intracranial hypertension (IH), in the absence of radiographic evidence of overt cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathway obstruction.

The diagnosis of LM was established either by positive CSF cytology or by characteristic gadolinium-enhanced MRI findings consistent with leptomeningeal enhancement.

VPS candidacy was determined through a multidisciplinary consensus among neurosurgeons, neuro-oncologists, and neuroradiologists. The surgical indication was persistent IH-related symptoms refractory to medical management. Patients with clearly obstructive hydrocephalus due to mass effect or anatomical CSF blockage were excluded.

This study was approved by the institutional review board of Samsung Medical Center (IRB No. 2025-10-063).

CSF pressure measurement

Lumbar CSF pressure was obtained during diagnostic lumbar puncture performed in the lateral decubitus position, ensuring alignment of the head and lumbar spine to minimize hydrostatic influence. The opening pressure (cmH₂O) was measured using a standard manometer under spontaneous respiration, with no sedation or general anesthesia.

Intraventricular pressure was measured intraoperatively during VPS catheter insertion under general anesthesia. Patients were positioned supine without head elevation, with mild head rotation to facilitate catheter tunneling. The ventricular puncture was made at Kocher’s or Frazier’s point, and pressure was recorded immediately after successful ventricular cannulation and before CSF diversion.

General anesthesia was typically induced with propofol and maintained predominantly with sevoflurane (30 of 34 patients), with a small number of cases using desflurane or total intravenous anesthesia. Pressure measurements were obtained only after stabilization of anesthetic conditions.

Lumbar and ventricular CSF pressures were measured at different time points but under standardized conditions, reflecting routine clinical practice in patients undergoing VPS for suspected intracranial hypertension.

The manometer was zeroed at the level of the tragus, corresponding to the foramen of Monro, to standardize measurements across patients.

The significant pressure discrepancy (SPD, ΔP) was defined as the difference between intraventricular and lumbar pressures:

Based on physiological CSF pressure wave amplitudes (< 5 cmH₂O), a discrepancy of ≥ 5 cmH₂O was considered clinically meaningful [14–16].

To further characterize discrepancies of greater magnitude, a marked pressure discrepancy (MPD) was additionally defined as a ΔP of ≥ 10 cmH₂O, which exceeded the approximate average pressure discrepancy observed in our cohort and indicating a more pronounced craniospinal pressure gradient.

Data collection

Clinical and demographic data were retrieved from electronic medical records, including age, sex, primary cancer type, spinal LM involvement, and presenting symptoms (e.g., headache, nausea/vomiting, cranial nerve palsy, gait disturbance). Both lumbar and ventricular pressures were documented, and pressure discrepancy (ΔP) was calculated for each patient.

Imaging and CSF cytology, fluid analysis

Preoperative brain MRI with gadolinium contrast was reviewed for LM characteristics, and categorized according to the EANO-ESMO clinical guideline: (Type A: Linear meningeal enhancement, Type B: Nodular parenchymal enhancement, Type C: Mixed linear and nodular, Type D: No visible enhancement)

Ventricular dilatation was evaluated using Evans index, with values ≥ 0.3 indicating ventriculomegaly. CSF cytology was performed using a liquid-based cytological method in all patients.

Cerebrospinal fluid biochemical parameters, including protein and glucose levels, were obtained from samples collected during diagnostic lumbar puncture or VPS placement. CSF protein concentration (mg/dL) and glucose levels (mg/dL) were measured using standard laboratory methods at our institution. These parameters were analyzed in relation to pressure discrepancy status.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (v25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Python 3.10. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using paired t-tests (lumbar vs. ventricular pressure) or independent t-tests (group comparisons). Categorical variables were analyzed using the Fisher’s exact test.

To identify independent predictors of clinically significant SPD (≥ 5 cmH₂O) and MPD (≥ 10 cmH₂O), Univariate logistic regression was performed including age, sex, MRI enhancement pattern, lumbar and ventricular CSF pressures, Evans index, CSF cytology, and CSF biochemical parameters (protein and glucose). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 34 patients with leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) were included (20 females [58.8%], 14 males [41.2%]; mean age 59.2 years, range 39–79). The most frequent primary tumor was non-small cell lung cancer (n = 23, 67.6%), followed by breast cancer (n = 4, 11.8%), small-cell lung cancer (n = 2, 5.9%), and other malignancies (n = 5, 14.7%) including melanoma, pancreatic, parotid gland, and gastric cancers, as well as metastasis of unknown origin (Table 1). CSF cytology was positive in 28 patients (82.4%). On MRI, leptomeningeal enhancement was categorized as type A (linear) in 11 (32.3%), type B (nodular) in 5 (14.7%), and type C (mixed) in 18 (52.9%) cases; none were type D (non-visible). Ventriculomegaly (Evans index ≥ 0.3) was observed in 15 patients (44.1%).

Preoperative symptoms

All patients exhibited symptoms consistent with intracranial hypertension (IH). The most common were headache (24 patients, 70.6%), followed by nausea/vomiting (15, 44.1%), neurocognitive dysfunction (14, 41.2%), gait disturbance (11, 32.4%), and cranial nerve palsy (3, 8.8%).

Lumbar and ventricular CSF pressure discrepancy

The mean lumbar opening pressure was 20.9 cmH₂O (range 4–40), whereas the mean ventricular pressure, measured intraoperatively before shunt placement, was 31.1 cmH₂O (range 15–50). The average lumbar–ventricular pressure difference (ΔP) was 10.2 cmH₂O (range − 3 to 31), and the paired t-test confirmed a statistically significant discrepancy (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). These results demonstrate that lumbar CSF pressure tends to underestimate actual intracranial pressure in patients with LM.

Discrepancy between lumbar and ventricular cerebrospinal fluid pressures. Box plots illustrate that ventricular CSF pressure (mean 31.1 cmH₂O; range 15–50; SD ± 11.3) was markedly higher than lumbar CSF pressure (mean 20.9 cmH₂O; range 4–40; SD ± 8.9)

Intracranial hypertension without ventriculomegaly

The Evans index was below 0.3 in 18 of 34 patients (52.9%), indicating that more than half of the LM cohort exhibited intracranial hypertension without ventriculomegaly. Among patients without ventriculomegaly, mean lumbar and ventricular CSF pressures were 22.8 cmH₂O and 32.2 cmH₂O, respectively, compared with 18.9 cmH₂O and 29.8 cmH₂O in those with ventriculomegaly. Although pressures tended to be higher in the non-ventriculomegaly group, independent t-tests showed no statistically significant differences (lumbar p = 0.202; ventricular p = 0.533).

Clinically significant pressure discrepancy (SPD)

A clinically significant pressure discrepancy (SPD) was defined as a lumbar–ventricular pressure difference of ≥ 5 cmH₂O, a threshold exceeding the normal physiologic fluctuation range. Using this criterion, 25 patients (73.5%) demonstrated SPD, while 9 (26.5%) did not.

No significant demographic or tumor-related differences were observed between SPD-positive and SPD-negative groups (Table 2). Mean age (57.6 ± 10.9 vs. 63.8 ± 6.7 years, p = 0.124), sex distribution (female 66.7% vs. 56.0%, p = 0.591), and primary cancer type were similar across groups (p = 0.106). Likewise, CSF cytology positivity (77.8% vs. 84.0%, p = 0.686) and Evans index (0.30 ± 0.03 vs. 0.29 ± 0.04, p = 0.741) did not differ significantly, indicating that SPD was not simply explained by overt ventricular enlargement or tumor burden.

The pattern of leptomeningeal enhancement on MRI (EANO–ESMO type A/B/C) and spinal involvement were also not associated with SPD status (p = 0.951), suggesting that the pressure gradient was independent of the radiologic distribution of LM.

Comparative pressure analysis revealed a clear physiologic asymmetry between groups. Lumbar opening pressure did not differ significantly (20.8 ± 9.9 vs. 21.5 ± 5.7 cmH₂O, p = 0.789), but ventricular pressure was substantially higher in the SPD-positive group (34.3 ± 11.4 cmH₂O) than in the SPD-negative group (22.1 ± 4.2 cmH₂O, p = 0.001).

This finding suggests that the discrepancy primarily arises from elevated intracranial pressure at the ventricular level rather than underestimation by lumbar measurement. The data support a model in which LM-related CSF flow impedance or compartmentalization—likely at the basal cisterns or arachnoid villi—hinders craniospinal pressure equilibration.

Such localized resistance could result in higher supratentorial pressure despite near-normal lumbar readings, highlighting that lumbar opening pressure alone may not accurately represent true intracranial pressure in LM patients.

Together, these results indicate that significant lumbar–ventricular pressure discrepancies are common and physiologically meaningful in LM-associated intracranial hypertension, reflecting altered CSF dynamics rather than mere measurement variation.

Marked pressure discrepancy (MPD)

To further assess the magnitude of craniospinal pressure dissociation, a marked pressure discrepancy (MPD), defined as a lumbar–ventricular pressure difference of ≥ 10 cmH₂O, was evaluated. MPD was observed in 18 of 34 patients (52.9%), indicating that a substantial subset of patients exhibited a pronounced pressure gradient beyond the threshold for clinical significance.

Patients with MPD demonstrated significantly higher ventricular CSF pressures compared with those without MPD, whereas lumbar CSF pressures showed no significant difference between groups. These findings suggest that MPD represents a more severe form of pressure dissociation driven predominantly by elevated ventricular pressure rather than by changes in lumbar pressure. Importantly, this pattern was consistent with analyses using the 5 cmH₂O threshold (SPD), suggesting that ventricular pressure is the principal determinant of lumbar–ventricular pressure discrepancy regardless of the cutoff applied.

CSF biochemical profiles and pressure discrepancy

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biochemical parameters were compared according to pressure discrepancy status. CSF protein levels tended to be higher in patients with pressure discrepancy, although the differences did not reach statistical significance. In patients with clinically significant pressure discrepancy (SPD ≥ 5 cmH₂O), mean CSF protein levels were 109.1 ± 19.9 mg/dL compared with 66.4 ± 24.6 mg/dL in those without SPD (p = 0.252). Similarly, patients with marked pressure discrepancy (MPD ≥ 10 cmH₂O) showed higher CSF protein levels than those without MPD (123.0 ± 26.7 vs. 69.4 ± 14.7 mg/dL, p = 0.100).

CSF glucose levels did not differ significantly between groups. Mean CSF glucose was 44.7 ± 4.1 mg/dL in the SPD-positive group and 51.2 ± 7.1 mg/dL in the SPD-negative group (p = 0.431), and 42.8 ± 4.2 mg/dL versus 50.5 ± 5.8 mg/dL in the MPD-positive and MPD-negative groups, respectively (p = 0.292).

Logistic regression analysis

Univariate logistic regression was performed to identify potential predictors of clinically significant pressure discrepancy (SPD ≥ 5 cmH₂O) (Table 3). The variables analyzed included age, sex, MRI enhancement pattern, lumbar and ventricular CSF pressures, Evans index, and CSF cytology. Ventricular CSF pressure was the only variable significantly associated with SPD (odds ratio = 1.216, 95% CI 1.044–1.415, p = 0.012), whereas age, sex, lumbar CSF pressure, MRI enhancement type, Evans index, and cytology status showed no significant associations (p > 0.05 for all).

To determine whether this relationship persisted with larger pressure differences, a separate logistic regression analysis was conducted using marked pressure discrepancy (MPD ≥ 10 cmH₂O) as the outcome variable. Ventricular CSF pressure again emerged as the sole significant predictor of MPD (odds ratio = 1.137, 95% CI 1.033–1.253, p = 0.009), while all other clinical and radiologic variables remained non-significant.

CSF biochemical parameters, including protein and glucose levels, were also not significantly associated with either SPD or MPD in univariate logistic regression analyses.

Collectively, these results indicate that elevated ventricular CSF pressure is the primary determinant of lumbar–ventricular pressure dissociation, independent of demographic factors, imaging findings, or CSF biochemical characteristics.

Discussion

This study investigated the discrepancy between lumbar and intraventricular cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pressures in patients with leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) who presented with clinical features of intracranial hypertension (IH) and underwent ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS) surgery. We found that a substantial proportion of patients demonstrated a clinically significant pressure discrepancy (SPD ≥ 5 cmH₂O) between lumbar and ventricular measurements. The mean difference of 10.2 cmH₂O, with SPD present in 73.5% of cases, supports our clinical impression that lumbar puncture alone may underestimate true intracranial pressure in LM.

Patients with SPD exhibited markedly higher intraventricular pressures, whereas lumbar pressures were comparable between groups. This suggests that the gradient arises primarily from elevated supratentorial pressure rather than low lumbar pressures. Such findings underscore a key diagnostic limitation: lumbar opening pressure alone may not accurately reflect the intracranial pressure burden in LM, where CSF flow is frequently heterogeneous and partially compartmentalized.

In logistic regression analysis, ventricular CSF pressure was the only variable significantly associated with SPD (p = 0.012), whereas age, sex, lumbar pressure, MRI enhancement pattern, Evans index, and CSF cytology were not significant. This indicates that the degree of cranial pressure elevation itself is the dominant determinant of lumbar–ventricular pressure dissociation, independent of demographic or radiologic factors. The absence of correlation with ventriculomegaly or enhancement type reinforces that IH in LM can occur without radiographic hydrocephalus or clear anatomic obstruction.

More than half of our patients exhibited markedly elevated CSF pressure despite having no ventriculomegaly, emphasizing that radiologic markers such as the Evans index may fail to detect clinically significant intracranial hypertension in LM. This finding is consistent with previous reports showing a similar proportion (54.2%) of LM patients with IH but normal ventricular size [17]. Clinically, this indicates that ventriculomegaly or markedly elevated lumbar pressure should not be prerequisites for considering VPS; surgical decisions should rely primarily on symptoms and overall clinical judgment. The presence of elevated ICP without ventricular enlargement parallels mechanisms described in idiopathic intracranial hypertension, where impaired CSF absorption and altered craniospinal compliance occur without radiographic dilation. A similar pattern has also been reported in vestibular schwannoma with or without radiosurgery, where intracranial hypertension may coexist with normal ventricular size and elevated CSF protein [18, 19].

CSF biochemical alterations have been proposed as one potential contributor to impaired craniospinal pressure transmission by increasing microscopic resistance to CSF flow. In our cohort, mean CSF protein levels showed a trend toward higher values in patients with greater lumbar–ventricular pressure discrepancy, particularly in those with MPD; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance, and neither CSF protein nor glucose levels were independently associated with SPD or MPD in logistic regression analyses. These findings indicate that, despite observable differences in absolute values, CSF biochemical parameters alone are insufficient to account for lumbar–ventricular pressure dissociation in LM. Nevertheless, the observed elevation of CSF protein may still reflect a potential contribution to microscopic resistance within the subarachnoid CSF pathways, which could further impair craniospinal pressure transmission in advanced leptomeningeal disease.

The observed pressure discrepancy is therefore more likely attributable to multifactorial disturbances in CSF dynamics. Tumor cell infiltration along the leptomeninges and arachnoid villi, together with inflammatory fibrosis and meningeal thickening, may impair pressure transmission between the cranial and spinal compartments. Focal resistance at the basal cisterns or convexity subarachnoid space can isolate the ventricular system, producing a localized cranial pressure compartment despite patent CSF flow pathways on imaging. This mechanism is consistent with earlier radionuclide cisternography studies describing craniospinal flow block and CSF compartmentalization in LM [20]. As a result, ventricular pressure measured intraoperatively may more accurately reflect the true intracranial pressure burden than preoperative lumbar readings.

Clinically, these findings emphasize the limitations of relying solely on lumbar puncture to assess IH in LM. Direct ventricular pressure measurement remains the most accurate but is invasive and limited to surgical settings. Therefore, VPS candidacy should not be dismissed solely because of a “normal” lumbar opening pressure when clinical features and imaging suggest raised intracranial pressure. Recognizing the possibility of lumbar–ventricular dissociation may help prevent underdiagnosis and allow earlier CSF diversion in symptomatic patients.

This study has several limitations. It was a single-center retrospective analysis with a modest sample size, and all included patients underwent VPS, introducing selection bias toward those with significant IH. Intraventricular pressure measurements were obtained intraoperatively under anesthesia, which may affect absolute pressure values despite standardized zeroing at the tragus. However, anesthetic conditions were relatively uniform in our cohort, with most procedures performed under propofol induction followed by sevoflurane maintenance, and ventricular pressure was recorded only after stabilization of anesthetic conditions. Nevertheless, the consistent finding of higher ventricular than lumbar pressures across most patients strongly suggests a physiologic phenomenon rather than measurement artifact. Because cytological analysis was primarily performed on lumbar CSF samples, ventricular CSF cytology was not routinely assessed during VPS placement, limiting direct comparison of tumor cell distribution between cranial and spinal CSF compartments.

In summary, significant lumbar–ventricular CSF pressure discrepancies are common in LM and reflect altered CSF hydrodynamics rather than measurement error. Our results suggest that lumbar pressure may substantially underestimate intracranial pressure, especially when CSF flow is compartmentalized by leptomeningeal tumor infiltration. These findings underscore the need for physiologic assessment of CSF dynamics in LM and support a more nuanced, multidisciplinary approach to managing IH in this challenging condition.

Conclusion

In summary, significant lumbar–ventricular CSF pressure discrepancies are common in LM and are primarily driven by elevated ventricular pressure. These results indicate that the lumbar pressure frequently underestimates intracranial pressure due to impaired craniospinal pressure transmission. Recognition of this pressure dissociation is clinically important, as incorporating physiologic assessment of CSF dynamics into diagnostic and surgical decision-making may improve the management of LM-associated intracranial hypertension.
