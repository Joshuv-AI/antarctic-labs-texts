Adaptive immunity of type VI CRISPR-Cas systems associated with reverse transcriptase–Cas1 fusion proteins
Authors: María Dolores Molina-Sánchez, Francisco Martínez-Abarca, Vicenta Millán, Mario Rodríguez Mestre, Pavlo Stehantsev, Artem Stetsenko, Albert Guskov, Nicolás Toro
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCas13-containing type VI CRISPR-Cas systems specifically target RNA; however, the mechanism of spacer acquisition remains unclear. We have previously reported the association of reverse transcriptase–Cas1 (RT–Cas1) fusion proteins with certain types of VI-A systems. Here, we show that RT–Cas1 fusion proteins are also recruited by type VI-B systems in bacteria from gut microbiomes, constituting a VI-B1 variant system that includes a CorA-encoding locus in addition to the CRISPR array and the RT–Cas1/Cas2 adaptation module. We found that type VI RT-CRISPR systems were functional for spacer acquisition, CRISPR array processing and interference activity, demonstrating that adaptive immunity mediated by these systems can function independently of other in trans systems. We provide evidence that the RT associated with these systems enables spacer acquisition from RNA molecules. We also found that CorA encoded by type VI-B1 RT-associated systems can transport divalent metal ions and downregulate Cas13b-mediated RNA interference. These findings highlight the importance of RTs in RNA-targeting CRISPR-Cas systems, potentially enabling the integration of RNA-derived spacers into CRISPR arrays as a mechanism against RNA-based invaders in specific environments.

Introduction

The reverse transcriptases (RTs) of prokaryotes are highly diverse, and recent research has revealed an important role for these enzymes in defense mechanisms against phages and other mobile genetic elements. The RTs involved in defense are classified into various lineages, including retrons, Abi-like RTs, RTs of the so-called ‘unknown group’ and those associated with CRISPR-Cas systems (1–4).

CRISPR-Cas (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats and CRISPR-associated proteins) systems are adaptive immune systems found in archaea and bacteria that can integrate fragments of invasive nucleic acids, known as spacers, into CRISPR arrays through a process requiring the Cas1/Cas2 proteins known as adaptation. The CRISPR array is transcribed and processed into short CRISPR RNAs (crRNAs) that serve as guides for CRISPR-associated (Cas) proteins, enabling them to recognize and eliminate invasive DNA through complementarity. CRISPR-Cas systems can be classified into two classes, six types and 33 subtypes (5). Type III CRISPR-Cas systems are the only ones able to recognize and cleave both DNA and RNA targets in a transcription-coupled reaction (6,7), whereas type VI systems are unique in their specific targeting of RNA molecules (8–10). The RT loci associated with CRISPR-Cas systems are either adjacent to or fused to the cas1 gene (RT–Cas1), and most are linked to a subset of type III CRISPR-Cas systems (1,11,12).

Phylogenetic analyses have suggested that most RTs associated with CRISPR-Cas adaptation modules probably evolved from the RTs encoded by group II introns (1,11,13) after retrotransposition and subsequent domestication of the encoded RTs. Independent studies have shown that type III CRISPR-associated RT systems can acquire new spacers directly from RNA in vivo in an RT-dependent manner (14–16). Interestingly, we have also reported the recruitment of RT–Cas1 fusion proteins in certain types of VI-A CRISPR-Cas systems (17).

Type VI CRISPR-Cas systems often lack Cas1/Cas2, which has led to suggestions that they may acquire spacers in trans (8,18–20). In addition, a type VI-B system has been shown to acquire spacers using the Cas1/Cas2 machinery from a type II-C system (21). However, the process of adaptation has yet to be elucidated for type VI CRISPR systems.

In this study, we investigated the adaptive immunity of type-VI CRISPR-RT-associated systems. We identified type VI-B systems in bacteria with RT–Cas1 fusions from an analysis of gut microbiomes. In addition to the CRISPR array and adaptation module, these systems harbor a linked CorA-encoding locus. We found that these RT-CRISPR systems were fully functional for spacer acquisition and CRISPR interference. The associated CorA functions as a membrane divalent metal ion transporter and downregulates the interference capacity of the Cas13b effector protein. We provide evidence here that the RT associated with type VI-B systems is involved in spacer acquisition from RNA molecules. These findings highlight the crucial importance of integrating RTs into CRISPR-Cas systems, particularly those targeting RNA, to provide strong defense against RNA-based threats in particular environments.

Materials and methods

Strains and culture conditions

The Escherichia coli strains used in this study were DH5α (Bethesda Research Laboratories) for cloning, and Rosetta 2 and BL-21 (DE3) (Novagen) for protein production. For spacer acquisition assays, several different E. coli strains were used, as appropriate for the experiment: HFR3000 F+ or HMS174 (DE3) for pUC-based experiments and BW25113 for pBAD-based experiments to ensure effective arabinose induction. The bacteria were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium (10 g/l tryptone, 5 g/l yeast extract and 5 g/l NaCl) at 37°C with orbital shaking. Antibiotics were added as required (ampicillin, 200 μg/ml; chloramphenicol, 50 μg/ml; and kanamycin, 50 μg/ml).

Plasmid construction for spacer acquisition assays and protein production

The plasmids used for spacer acquisition experiments were derived from the pBAD or pUC backbone, depending on whether the construct needed to include the coding sequence for the effector protein Cas13. Rk plasmids were obtained by cloning polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products obtained with the indicated primers (Supplementary Table S1) and genomic DNA supplied by the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH, DSMZ (DSM#19783) as a template. The remaining systems were generated by and obtained from GenScript Biotech Corporation (EU Headquarters). Unlike the Rk system, the entire RT–Cas1_Cas2 adaptation module [wild-type (WT) and mutant] was codon-optimized for expression in E. coli under the control of a constitutive promoter in the pUC backbone. Each individual gene coding sequence was cloned as an SphI/SalI block on a scaffold digested with SphI/XhoI. In each case, the CRISPR array consisted of the leader region, the first direct repeat (DR) and the first spacer (Supplementary Figure S1A). The Cas13-encoding gene was also ordered to GenScript Biotech Corporation (EU Headquarters) in an inducible pBAD backbone for the induction of complete CRISPR-Cas operon expression.

The plasmids used for the production and purification of RT containing proteins were based on the pMal-Flag-3C backbone (16). RT–Cas1 was amplified from the corresponding pUC vector (or genomic DNA from Rk) and inserted into pMAL-3C-Flag as a BamHI/BglII fragment. The Rhodovulum kholense RT-deficient mutant was obtained by two rounds of PCR with oligonucleotide primers containing the desired nucleotide substitution. pET28a (+)-TEV derived plasmids were used for the production and purification of the CorA protein according to a published protocol (22). The plasmids and oligonucleotides used in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables S1–S3, and the sequences of the different constructs used are also included in Supplementary File S1. The td intron construct was obtained by inserting the 393 bp intron sequence and its native exons into pUC_Pm immediately upstream from the RT–Cas1 coding sequence. The receptor plasmid was digested with XhoI and dephosphorylated. The sequence containing tdI was inserted as a SalI/XhoI fragment from pG-tdI (Δ1–3) s (23).

Protein purification

pMal-Flag derivatives carrying RT–Cas1-coding genes

Single colonies of freshly transformed E. coli strain Rosetta2 (DE3) cells were cultured overnight at 37°C in LB medium supplemented with ampicillin, chloramphenicol and 0.2% glucose, with shaking. A 1/100 dilution of the resulting culture was used to inoculate a flask containing 50 ml LB supplemented as indicated, which was then incubated at 37°C, with shaking, until the culture reached the exponential growth phase. The cultures were then induced by adding IPTG to a final concentration of 1 mM and incubating overnight at 20°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, the pellet was washed with PBS buffer (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na₂HPO₄, 1.8 mM KH2PO4), and quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Cells were lysed in column buffer [CB: 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1 mM 1,4-Dithiothreitol (DTT) and 1× EDTA-free protease inhibitor (Roche)] using a French press. The lysate was cleared by centrifugation (16 000 × g for 15 min at 4°C). Proteins were purified by affinity liquid chromatography. Amylose resin was placed in a 10 ml Econo-Pac disposable column (Bio-Rad) and washed several times with CB. Crude protein extract was incubated with the column for 2 h at 4°C with horizontal shaking. The bound proteins were eluted by incubation in CB supplemented with 10 mM maltose for 1 h at 4°C. The proteins were concentrated with an Amicon ultracentrifugation filter (YM-30) and dialyzed against storage buffer [SB: 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 1 mM DTT, 50% glycerol]. Protein extracts were stored at −80°C until required. Protein concentration was determined by the Bradford method (Bio-Rad), according to the kit manufacturer’s protocol.

pET28a (+)-TEV -derivative plasmid containing the corA gene

CorA protein was produced in 2 l LB medium (10 g/l Bacto tryptone, 5 g/l Bacto yeast extract, 10 g/l NaCl), with E. coli BL-21(DE3) cells grown at 37°C, induced with 0.1 mM IPTG, and collected after 3 h after induction. A cell pellet was obtained by centrifugation and the cells were washed in buffer A (50 mM Tris/HCl, pH 8.0) and resuspended in buffer B (50 mM Tris/HCl, pH 8.0, 250 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol). Membrane vesicles were prepared according to a published protocol (22), collected by ultracentrifugation and resuspended in buffer C (50 mM Tris/HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 15% glycerol). They were then solubilized in buffer D [50 mM Tris/HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 10% glycerol, 1% (w/v) n-dodecyl-β-D-maltopyranoside (Anatrace)] and the extracted proteins were subjected to a two-step purification process based on immobilized metal ion affinity chromatography and size-exclusion chromatography.

Exogenous RT assay

RT reactions were performed as previously described (24). Briefly, 500 ng of protein extract was incubated with poly(rA)/oligo(dT)18 or poly(rA) substrates in the presence of RT buffer [10 mM KCl, 25 mM MgCl2, 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 5 mM DTT] and 2.5 μCi [α-32P] dTTP (800 Ci/mmol; Perkin Elmer) for 10 min at 37°C. The reaction was stopped by spotting 8 μl of the reaction mixture onto Whatman DE81 paper. The paper was dried, washed with 250 ml 2× Saline-sodium citrate buffer (SSC) and radioactivity was quantified with a scintillation counter (Liquid Scintillation Analyzer Tri-Carb 1500, Packard).

Preparation of large unilamellar vesicles/proteoliposomes and fluorescent transport assays

For the preparation of large unilamellar vesicles (LUVs), we dissolved E. coli polar lipid extract (Avanti, USA) and egg L-α-phosphatidylcholine (Avanti, USA) in a chloroform in a 3:1 (w/w) ratio. The solution was dried in a rotary evaporator and the residue was then resuspended in SB E (50 mM KPi, pH 7.5) at a concentration of 20 mg/ml. LUVs were obtained by three freeze–thaw cycles and frozen in liquid nitrogen for long-term storage. For proteoliposome preparation, LUVs were extruded through a polycarbonate filter with 400 nm pores (Avestin, 11 passages), diluted to a concentration of 4 mg/ml in buffer F (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.0) and further destabilized by treatment with Triton X-100 as described (25). The purified protein of interest was added to the liposomes in a 1:250 (w/w, protein/lipid) ratio. The detergent was removed with Bio-beads (50 mg/ml, four times, after incubations for 0.5 h, 1 h, 2 h and overnight). Following the overnight incubation, proteoliposomes were collected by centrifugation (20 min, 285 775 x g, 4°C), resuspended in buffer F to a lipid concentration of 20 mg/ml (w/w) and frozen stored in liquid nitrogen for subsequent experiments.

Fluorescent transport assays

A zinc-sensitive fluorophore, FluoZin-1 (Thermo Fisher, USA), was used to measure zinc, nickel and cadmium transport. FluoZin-1 was encapsulated into LUVs at a final concentration of 5 μM via three freeze–thaw cycles and subsequent extrusion through polycarbonate filters with 400 nm pores (11 passages). The extravesicular dye was then removed from the LUVs by size exclusion chromatography on a 2 ml Sephadex G-75 column equilibrated with buffer F. The proteoliposomes were collected by ultracentrifugation (25 min, 285 775 x g, 4°C), the supernatant was removed and proteoliposomes were resuspended in fresh buffer F (20 μl per 4 mg proteoliposomes).

The time course of fluorescence was followed in a 1 ml cuvette with a stirrer speed of 350 rpm, with an excitation wavelength of 490 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm. The cuvette contained 0.3 mg proteoliposome diluted in 1 ml buffer F for the first 55 s. For each measurement, the transport assay was initiated at the 55th s by adding a divalent cation stock solution [Zn(CH₃CO₂)₂, NiSO4 or CdCl2] to the desired final concentration for the experiment. Parallel experiments were performed with empty liposomes as a control. All measurements were obtained at least in triplicate.

Spacer acquisition assay

The appropriate E. coli strain was transformed by electroporation with a plasmid expressing the desired adaptive operon: the RT–Cas1–Cas2-CRISPR array and its derivatives (Supplementary Table S2). At least nine individual colonies were cultured overnight at 37°C in liquid LB medium supplemented with ampicillin. The culture was diluted 1:500 in LB medium, with the addition of 0.005% arabinose if required for induction, and incubated for 14–18 h at 37°C. The bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation, and the plasmids were isolated by standard procedures. Leader proximal spacers were amplified by PCR with 2 ng plasmid DNA as the template, a forward primer binding to the leader sequence, and a reverse primer binding to the first native spacer (Supplementary Table S3) under the following cycling conditions: 94°C for 2 min; 30 cycles of 94°C for 20 s and 58–62°C for 20 s. The dominant PCR product was an unexpanded array. We therefore performed a blind purification of the expanded CRISPR array consistent with a band approximately 70 nt larger, with the mi-Gel Extraction Kit (Metabion International AG, Germany). The purified samples were then used for a second round of PCR with divergent primers binding to the DR sequence (DR-PCR, 26; Supplementary Figure S1B). An amplicon band was detected only if an acquisition event occurred. Quantification was performed with a Qubit fluorometer (Life Technologies), with analysis on a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). Libraries were sequenced with Illumina technology at the Genome Sequencing Unit of IPBLN-CSIC (Granada, Spain). The detection of spacers matching the exon junction after td intron splicing was maximized by performing experiments with 30 individual colonies as the starting material for the leader-spacer PCR. The samples were then processed according to the standard procedure, as described above.

Data-processing pipeline

FASTQ files were refined with a custom script and SEED 2.1.1 software: size selection, trimming of adaptor sequences, grouping into unique sequences, and clustering of the unique sequences at a threshold of 90% identity to reduce the experimental background due to PCR bias (Supplementary Figure S1C). Spacers were identified with a custom-written Ruby script determining the sequence between the 3′- and 5′- ends of the DR sequence. Mapping onto the plasmid and genome was performed with Bowtie2.0, with two mismatches allowed. For protospacer sequence analyses, we used a new custom-written Ruby script reporting the 15 nt upstream from the first position matching the spacer sequence and the next 30 nt within the spacer. We also obtained the last 30 positions of the spacer and 15 nt at the 3′ end of the protospacer. Logos were generated with both WebLogo 3.0 [https://weblogo.threeplusone.com/ (27)] and kpLogo [http://kplogo.wi.mit.edu/ (28)], using default parameters.

Northern blot hybridization

RNA was extracted from cultures of E. coli BW25113 containing pBAD plasmids with or without sequences encoding the WT Cas13b or the corresponding mutants, as previously described (29). Bacterial cultures in the exponential growth phase (6 ml, 0.2 OD600 units) were left uninduced or were induced by incubation with 1% arabinose for 4 h at 37°C. We subjected 30 μg of DNAse I-digested total RNA samples to electrophoresis in 6% denaturing polyacrylamide gels (8 M urea), and then blotted the bands obtained onto positively charged nylon membranes (Roche, Germany). The RNA bands were fixed to the filter by ultraviolet-crosslinking for 2 min. Hybridization probes were radioactively labeled as follows: 100 pmol oligonucleotide was incubated with 10 μCi [γ-32P]-ATP in the presence of 10 U T4 polynucleotide kinase (NEB) at 37°C for 2 h. Non-incorporated radiolabeled nucleotides were removed with Sephadex G25 columns (Cytiva, UK). Two probes were used: the complementary DR Probe (DRAS: 5′-GTTGTAGAAGCCTACATTTGAGTAG-3′) and the 5S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) Probe (5′-TACTCTCGCATGGGGAGACCCCAC-3′). The membranes were incubated for 1 h in phosphate buffer supplemented with 7% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and hybridization was then performed overnight at 42°C with 5 × 106 Ci probe. Successively more stringent washing steps [2× SSC/0.1% SDS; 1× SSC/0.1% SDS; 0.1× SSC/0.1% SDS] were performed, with each step lasting 15 min at room temperature. Hybridization signals were obtained by digital imaging of the exposure plate (Fuji), with visualization on a Personal Molecular Imager FX scanner and analysis with Quantity One software (Bio-Rad).

MS2 phage drop plaque assay

The Pm CRISPR array with two spacers targeting the phage MS2 genome was ordered from the gene synthesis company GenScript Biotech Corporation (EU Headquarters). This cassette was cloned in pBAD vectors containing PmCas13b and derivatives. Following transformation of the E. coli host strain (HFR3000 F+), individual clones were picked and grown overnight at 37°C in LB containing the appropriate antibiotics. Cultures were then diluted 1:100, grown to an OD600 of 0.9 and mixed with 8 ml antibiotic-containing top agar (10 g/l tryptone, 5 g/l yeast extract, 10 g/l NaCl, 5 g/l agar) and poured onto LB base plates supplemented with antibiotic. We spotted 10-fold serial dilutions of the MS2 phage (ATCC 15597-B1) in LB onto the hardened top agar. Plaque formation was assessed after overnight incubation of these plates at 37°C.

MS2 phage infection dynamics in liquid medium

Overnight cultures of E. coli HFR3000 F+ host strain bacteria with the corresponding plasmids or a negative control (empty vector) were diluted 1:100 in LB medium supplemented with ampicillin and incubated at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm until early exponential growth phase (OD600 = 0.1) was reached. We then transferred 180 μl of the diluted culture into the wells in a 96-well plate, each containing 20 μl phage lysate, to achieve a final mutiplicity of infection (MOI) of 2 or 0.02, or we added 20 μl phage buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 100 mM MgCl2, 10 mM NaCl) as an uninfected control. Infections were performed in triplicate from overnight cultures prepared from separate colonies. Plates were incubated at 37°C with shaking in a Thermo Scientific Varioskan LUX plate reader with OD600 measurements at 10 min intervals. For the plotting of OD600 values, we subtracted 0.09 from all y-axis values to remove the optical density due to the growth medium.

Results

Spacer acquisition by CRISPR type VI-A systems with adaptation modules including RT–Cas1 fusion proteins

We previously reported two different CRISPR type VI-A systems featuring adaptation modules containing RT–Cas1 fusion proteins (17). We investigated the spacer acquisition process within these VI-A systems, by focusing specifically on CRISPR-Cas loci representative of the two subtypes (Supplementary Table S4). For the type VI-A/RT1 system, we studied that of R. kholense (designated Rk), whereas, for the type VI-A/RT2 system, we studied two examples: Eubacterium rectale AF25-15 (Er) and Eubacteriaceae bacterium CHKCI004 (CH), as shown in Figure 1A.

Based on the hypothesis that the type VI-A system-associated RT–Cas1 fusion protein might play a role in RNA spacer acquisition, we began by evaluating the ability of these RT–Cas1 fusion proteins to synthesize DNA from an RNA template. In our in vitro experiments, all maltose-binding protein–RT–Cas1 fusion proteins tested displayed exogenous RT activity with poly (A)/oligo (dT) substrates that was reduced to background levels in the RT-deficient mutants (Figure 1B). Furthermore, we found that mutations of critical residues of the Cas1 domain did not significantly disrupt DNA polymerization (Supplementary Figure S2).

We then investigated whether either of the RT–Cas1-associated type VI-A systems studied could acquire new spacers from E. coli, used as a heterologous host. Escherichia coli HMS174 (DE3) was transformed with a single plasmid expressing the adaptation module and the corresponding CRISPR array comprising the leader region, first DR and first natural spacer from a constitutive (pUC derivative) or inducible (pBAD backbone) promoter. In the case of the pBAD construct, the strain BW25113 was used instead of HMS174 (DE3). Using the CAPTURE protocol with divergent primers (26) (Supplementary Figure S1), we subjected the PCR products to NGS. For the Rk system, we detected no acquisition events for either the Rk_A1 or the Rk_A2 arrays. By contrast, in the Er system, new spacers were incorporated into the Er_A1 array (Figure 1C). Spacer acquisition efficiency was greater for the CH system than for the Er adaptation module but remained considerably lower than that of the V. vulnificus III-D/RT–Cas1 system (Figure 1C) described in a previous study (16). Importantly, we found that spacer acquisition efficiency was reduced five-fold in a mutant with alterations to the DR sequence, specifically with +1A and +2C substitutions at the first and second residues of the DR and six-fold in an RT-deficient mutant. Additionally, spacer acquisition was almost undetectable in a mutant lacking Cas2 protein (Figure 1D). These findings strongly suggest that new spacers are not acquired in trans through the acquisition machinery of other host CRISPR adaptation modules.

In the CH type VI-A system, the most common spacer length was 34 bp for the chromosome (35 bp for the plasmid) (Figure 2A). An analysis of the GC content of the acquired spacers revealed a close match to that of both the plasmid and the bacterial genome (Figure 2B). Moreover, examination of the protospacer regions flanking the aligned spacers revealed no sequence preference of the acquisition machinery (Figure 2C). However, there was a tendency for the sequences at both ends of the spacers to be more AT-rich.

The activity of the conserved Cas1–Cas2 integrase complex is sufficient for type I CRISPR acquisition, but for type II-C CRISPR systems, the acquisition of new spacers also requires cas9 and csn2 (30–32). For the CH-type VI-A/RT–Cas1 system, the addition of an effector module (Cas13a) did not affect the acquisition efficiency or the genomic regions used as the source of the acquired spacers, suggesting that the Cas13a effector is unlikely to play a key role in the acquisition process (Figure 2D).

Association of RT–Cas1 fusion proteins with CRISPR-Cas type VI-B systems

Given the relatively low efficiency observed in spacer acquisition by the type VI-A systems tested, we decided not to continue analyzing spacer acquisition from RNA sources. Instead, we conducted a comprehensive database analysis, with a published computational pipeline (1) to identify additional type VI systems associated with RTs. Remarkably, we identified eight Cas13b effector proteins linked to adaptation modules featuring RT–Cas1 fusion proteins and Cas2 proteins in the non-redundant (nr) protein sequence database of the NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and the MGnify (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/metagenomics) database. These proteins were identified exclusively in analyses of human, murine and cockroach gut microbiomes (Supplementary Table S4), suggesting a particular ecological niche for these defense systems. The Cas13b proteins within these systems had a pairwise sequence identity of only 34.6%. However, they appeared to cluster together in a monophyletic group (Figure 3A). This group includes certain Cas13b effectors not associated with RTs, such as those found in Prevotella sp. MA2016 and previously described as a variant of the subtype VI-B1 system (18). The Cas13b effector proteins within this branch are found predominantly in bacteria from phylum Bacteroidetes. Furthermore, the linked RTs are phylogenetically more closely related to those associated with the adaptive machinery of type III systems in RT clade 2 (1). These systems are commonly found in genus Bacteroides, suggesting a potential source for their recruitment.

The two known variants of subtype VI-B systems, VI-B1 and VI-B2, can be differentiated on the basis of the presence of additional small accessory proteins containing predicted transmembrane helices: Csx27 (VI-B1) and Csx28 (VI-B2). These accessory proteins regulate Cas13b-mediated RNA interference (18,33). The variants are believed to have diverged during the course of evolution, leading to different architectural features associated with these unique accessory transmembrane proteins (34). Interestingly, the type VI-B systems within the branch, including those associated with RTs, are characterized by an absence of Csx27 and Csx28 accessory proteins. Instead, the Cas13b effector protein appears to be linked to upstream WYL domain-containing proteins or, in the case of Prevotella sp. MA2016 and Alitispes sp. isolate RGIG9019, a corA-like gene downstream from the encoded Cas13b effector gene that serves as a distinctive feature (Supplementary Figure S3A). Not only do the type VI-B systems associated with RTs include the CRISPR array and the adaptation module, but they also appear to be linked to a gene encoding the divalent cation channel protein CorA, with diverse genomic architectures. These CorA proteins have two transmembrane helices at their C-terminal ends and an origin in common with the CorA proteins encoded by genes at various subtype III-B loci (CorA-1), as previously reported (35–37) (Figure 3B and Supplementary Figure S3B). There may therefore be a functional association between CorA and these type VI-B/RT systems, linking their defense mechanisms to membrane transport processes.

Spacer acquisition in CRISPR type VI-B systems with adaptation modules including RT–Cas1 fusion proteins

Six of the eight VI-B/RT–Cas1 systems identified here comprise unique CRISPR arrays, each containing two to 13 spacers of 31–37 nt in length (Supplementary Table S4). Three of the eight type VI-B CRISPR-associated RT systems identified here appeared to be complete (Figure 3C), while the other may be incomplete due to gaps in genome sequences and/or reduced size of contigs (see Supplementary Table S4). The DRs in these CRISPR arrays present characteristic features unique to Cas13b-associated CRISPR arrays (Figure 3D and E). They are similar in size, sequence and structure, with a length of 35 nt, complementary sequences (GUUG and CAAC) at the ends of the repeat, and a U-rich stretch in the open-loop region, resulting in a predicted secondary structure mediated by intramolecular base-pairing and resembling that of the Bz- and PbuCas13b-associated CRISPR arrays (18,38). These data suggest that VI-B/RT–Cas1 CRISPR arrays are probably associated with and processed by the corresponding Cas13b effector proteins (see below).

We assessed the ability of these systems to acquire new spacers, by inserting the genes encoding their RT–Cas1 and Cas2 proteins into a constitutive expression plasmid (pUC) along with a stretch of at least 250 nt of the corresponding predicted CRISPR array containing only one repeat and the next spacer. Using the spacer acquisition assay previously described for type VI-A systems, we showed that the system corresponding to P. muris (Pm) was the only one of the three orthologous complete systems that actively acquired new spacers, with an efficiency greater than that of the CH-type VI-A/RT–Cas1-associated system. The other two types of VI-B/RT–Cas1 systems had much lower levels of activity (two orders of magnitude lower for the Dysgonomonas sp. construct) or no activity at all (for the human gut metagenome assembly-derived construct) (Figure 4A). For the Pm system, constructs lacking the orthologous cas2 gene or with a YAAA mutation affecting the catalytic active site of the RT (RT−) or an E655A mutation affecting the Cas1 active site had >90% lower rates of spacer acquisition (Figure 4B). In vitro RT assays revealed that the RT− mutant had only background levels of RT activity, whereas the RT activity of the Cas1 mutant was only slightly lower than that of the WT protein (Figure 4C). Taken together, these findings suggest that the acquisition of new spacers by the Pm type VI-B system with an associated RT is independent of other CRISPR adaptation units present in the E. coli genome.

Features of spacer acquisition by type VI-B/RT CRISPR-Cas systems

We further investigated the characteristics of spacer acquisition by the Pm-type VI-B/RT–Cas1 system, by analyzing the pool of newly acquired spacers. Over 95% of the spacers were between 32 and 35 bp long, consistent with the length distribution of the natural spacers present in these arrays (Figure 5A and Supplementary Table S4). In addition, their median GC content was consistent with that of the two templates used: the E. coli genome and the plasmid (Figure 5B). Acquisition was not biased towards rRNA genes and was not correlated with the level of gene expression (Supplementary Figure S4). Moreover, there was no bias towards a specific antisense orientation of coding sequences, indicating an absence of complementarity between the newly acquired spacers and the predicted messenger RNA (Figure 5C). A significant deviation of GC content from expected values was observed at various positions within the spacer unit (Figure 5D). A symmetric bias therefore emerged, with a stretch of 2–3 nt rich in AT residues observed at either end of the spacers. An AT bias was also observed at position +17 of the spacer. Interestingly, there was a preference for the first nucleotide flanking the acquired spacers in the protospacer, with mostly G nucleotides at the 5′ end and C nucleotides at the 3′ end.

PmCas13b displays RNA interference downregulated by CorA activity

The Cas13b protein from P. muris consists of 1265 amino acids and contains two predicted Higher Eukaryotes and Prokaryotes Nucleotide-binding (HEPN) sites, identified as ‘RNXXXH’ motifs at positions 148–153 and 1214–1219. Furthermore, the alignment of its sequence with that of the PbuCas13b protein revealed the presence of a potential Lid region, a domain that caps the 3′ end of the crRNA with two β-hairpins. Within this region, the PmCas13b residues K444 and K445 were predicted to correspond to K393 in PbuCas13b, a critical residue in the Lid domain responsible for crRNA processing (38, Supplementary Figure S5).

For the confirmation of pre-crRNA processing, we designed constructs containing an array with two spacers targeting the MS2 genome (Figure 6A) placed immediately downstream from the WT or a variant Cas13b sequence under the control of an arabinose-inducible promoter (the pBAD plasmid series) (Figure 6B). Northern blot analysis of total RNA from a plasmid containing the cas13b gene yielded a band corresponding to the two mature forms of crRNA (64 nt), demonstrating processing. This processing was abolished in the K444A-K445A mutant, but maintained in the D1-D2 HEPN site double mutant, as expected (Figure 6C). The CorA-encoding gene did not affect the processing of pre-crRNA (compare lanes 2 and 3 in Figure 6C).

CorA proteins play a major role in Mg2+ transport in prokaryotes and in eukaryotic mitochondria (39). In vitro, the RNA cleavage activity of Cas13b is abolished by EDTA, suggesting a divalent metal ion-dependent mechanism (18). We therefore investigated the possible role of the associated CorA in Cas13b RNase activity. In vitro fluorescence-based transport assays on purified recombinant CorA protein reconstituted in proteoliposomes (see the ‘Materials and methods’ section) demonstrated that they readily transported Zn2+, Ni2+ and Cd2+ (Supplementary Figure S6). These ions, alongside Mg2+, are the commonly transported substrates within CorA family of proteins (22,39,40); hence our results strongly suggest that this CorA is also capable to transport Mg2+.

We then investigated in vivo interference with the lytic, single-stranded RNA bacteriophage MS2, as previously described (18). We used the same constructs as for the crRNA processing northern blot, with a CRISPR array, including two spacers targeting the lys-rep interface of the MS2 genome. We performed an MS2 drop plaque assay with serial dilutions of phage and bacteria (Figure 6D) containing the various constructs depicted in Figure 6B. In contrast to the empty vector, the Cas13b-only construct (Figure 6D), or constructs containing only the antiMS2 array or an empty array (Supplementary Figure S7), no plaque formation was observed for constructs containing both the cas13b gene and array with targeting spacers. This confirms the sequence-specific targeting of RNA by the PmCas13b system. The presence of the corA gene did not affect the interference measured in the phage drop plaque assay with serial dilutions (Figure 6D) or the viability of E. coli cells infected by MS2 (Supplementary Figure S8). Interestingly, we observed a significant negative effect on interference, as measured by increased phage production compared to E. coli host containing only Cas13b and antiMS2 array (Figure 6E). These results suggest that the CorA protein associated with VI-B/RT–Cas1 systems downregulates Cas13b activity, in a manner similar to that by which Csx27 downregulates the interference capacity of Cas13b in the type VI-B1 variant.

The effector module does not alter the features of spacer acquisition by type VI-B/RT CRISPR-Cas systems

We then used constructs containing the Cas13b gene and derived mutants (Figure 7A) to analyze the pattern of spacer acquisition by the RT–Cas1 and Cas2 adaptation machinery. The presence of the Cas13b effector gene did not alter the spacer acquisition profile and we observed no bias towards targeting of the complementary strand relative to gene-coding sequences or IGRs (Figure 7B). Moreover, the nucleotide position data were similar to those obtained in the absence of the effector locus (Figure 7C). These findings suggest that, unlike its counterpart in the type II-C system, the type VI-B effector protein is not involved in the adaptation process.

The P. muris type VI-B/RT–Cas1 CRISPR adaptation module acquires spacers directly from RNA

Only three type III CRISPR-associated RT systems have been shown to acquire spacers directly from RNA in vivo: a type III-A system from Marinomonas mediterranea (Mm), and two type III-D systems, one from Fusicanibacter saccharivorans (Fs) and the other from V. vulnificus (Vv) (14–16). We investigated whether the CRISPR adaptation module of a novel RT–Cas1 system, the Pm type VI-B/RT–Cas1 system, could acquire spacers from RNA molecules. We used a previously described acquisition assay, in which we searched for spacers harboring the ligated exon junction of the self-splicing td group I intron, a ribozyme that catalyzes its own excision from the original transcript and is not present in DNA (14,16). We designed a construct containing the td intron immediately upstream from the adaptation module operon, expressed under the control of a constitutive promoter (Figure 8). As in previous designs, the detection of the exon junction as a DNA spacer was considered to provide evidence that the system was indeed able to acquire spacers directly from RNA. We then searched for newly integrated spacers and identified >50 000 new spacers mapping onto plasmids or the E. coli genome. We discovered two unique spacers spanning the splice junction (Figure 8), confirming, as observed with type III CRISPR-associated RT systems, that type VI-B/RT–Cas1 CRISPR-Cas adaptation modules can indeed acquire spacers from RNA molecules in E. coli.

Discussion

Spacer acquisition has been studied in several CRISPR-Cas systems (41,42). However, the adaptation process of type VI CRISPR systems remains to be deciphered and, to date, only a trans-adaptation model has been proposed (21). We report here the presence of a subtype VI-B1 variant of the CRISPR-Cas system associated with RT–Cas1 fusion proteins in gut microbiomes. In addition to the Cas13b effector protein, this CRISPR-Cas variant carries a characteristic type VI CRISPR array, RT–Cas1/Cas2 integrase complex and linked CorA-encoding locus. We investigated the acquisition of spacers in vivo by the RT–Cas1/Cas2 integrase complex machinery associated with these type VI-B and type VI-A systems associated with RT–Cas1 fusion proteins (17). Our findings indicate that these variant-type VI-A and VI-B systems are fully functional in terms of the acquisition of new spacers from DNA. We also showed that the Pm-type VI-B system associated with an RT–Cas1 fusion protein could acquire RNA-derived spacers in an RT-dependent manner.

The phylogenetic relationships between the VI-B1-associated RTs and the CorA-encoded loci and their taxonomic distribution predominantly in Bacteroidetes suggest that the adaptation module (RT–Cas1, Cas2) and CorA of type VI-B systems are probably derived from those found in type III systems. This evolutionary transition may have occurred in response to environmental pressures within specific habitats, such as the intestinal tracts of animals. A plausible scenario involves a single evolutionary event, with gains and losses. The acquisition of CorA proteins, which are characterized by two transmembrane helices, probably rendered the Csx27 loci typical of type VI-B1 systems redundant, eventually leading to their loss.

It has been suggested that the CorA effector associated with type III-B systems can bind to the SAM-AMP generated, leading to cell dormancy or death (37). Conversely, it has been suggested that the type VI-B1-associated ancillary protein, Csx27, which downregulates the activity of Cas13b, may form membrane channels for ssDNA uptake, and that its nascent transcripts are degraded by the interference machinery of the type VI-B1 system (43). In addition, the Csx28 protein associated with the VI-B2 system forms pores, and its antiviral activity requires the sequence-specific cleavage of viral messenger RNAs (mRNAs) by Cas13b, leading to membrane depolarization (33). The precise role of CorA in interference in type VI-B/RT-associated systems remains unclear; however, the results presented here indicate a downregulation of the interference activity of Cas13b, resembling the action of Csx27. This downregulation may involve the control of effector RNase activity via intracellular magnesium homeostasis.

It remains challenging to understand spacer acquisition in type-VI systems, particularly as many of these systems lack an adaptation module. Crosstalk between CRISPR-Cas systems and the trans-adaptation model might shed light on the mechanisms operating in some type VI systems lacking adaptation genes. For instance, studies on Flavobacterium columnare have shown that despite the presence of an acquisition-deficient subtype VI-B system, this microorganism can nevertheless acquire spacers in trans, possibly through interactions with other CRISPR-Cas systems (21). The identification of type VI-A- and type VI-B-associated RT–Cas1/Cas2 systems enabled us to explore the acquisition of spacers by type VI systems in vivo, including their ability to acquire spacers from RNA sources. Our findings suggest that these variants of the type VI system are fully functional for the acquisition of new spacers from DNA. Interestingly, this acquisition did not appear to involve the adaptation modules of any other CRISPR-Cas system. Spacer acquisition was mediated by the associated RT–Cas1/Cas2 integrase complex. Furthermore, our results indicate that the acquisition process of these systems, unlike that of type II-C systems (44), does not require associated effector proteins (Cas13a or Cas13b). Spacer acquisition was relatively inefficient in type VI-A/RT systems, but acquisition efficiency was higher in the Pm-type VI-B/RT system, making it possible to characterize the spacer acquisition process in vivo with greater confidence. This particular system was also fully functional for the interference step, as demonstrated by the ability of its Cas13b effector to process the associated pre-crRNA and achieve RNA virus interference. Interestingly, acquisition by the Pm system is not biased towards highly transcribed regions or specific genomic locations, indicating an absence of preference for spacers potentially targeting mRNA. This observation may reflect a preference for DNA over RNA substrates in this type of VI-B/RT-associated system, as shown for the Thiomicrospira type III Cas6–RT–Cas1–Cas2 complex in vitro (45). The Pm system was able to acquire spacers from RNA molecules as expected for CRISPR-associated RT systems. However, assessing the efficiency of this reaction in vivo is challenging as it may vary depending on the host and the availability of spacer sources. Therefore, it is not currently possible to draw definitive conclusions about the primary source of spacers in these systems.

In both VI-A and VI-B RT-associated systems, spacers end tend to be enriched in AT nucleotides. Additionally, in the Pm-type VI-B/RT system, target sequences showed a preference for G and C nucleotides at positions −1 and +1 of the protospacer, respectively, along with a bias toward AT nucleotides at position +17 of the spacer. This suggests that the integrase complex may recognize protospacers with these specific sequence features. Our study primarily focused on the spacer acquisition step, and since we did not investigate protospacer flanking sequence (PFS) preferences during RNA targeting and cleavage, we cannot draw conclusions regarding PFS in this context. However, further research is needed to explore the presence and variability of PFS constraints during the interference phase of RT-associated type VI systems and their relationship to the sequence preferences of the acquisition machinery.

CRISPR-Cas systems are involved in microbial defense mechanisms. Type VI effector proteins cleave RNA targets only (8,10,18,34). Many of these systems have no adaptation module, but the presence of such a module in other systems suggests the possibility of acquiring spacers directly from RNA phages. The discovery of novel type VI-B systems associated with RT–Cas1 fusion proteins made it possible to decipher the details of spacer acquisition in type VI systems and to show that these systems can indeed acquire spacers from RNA sources. All the type VI-B1/RT systems reported here were derived from gut metagenomes. This finding raises questions about the significance of type VI-B1/RT systems in this particular habitat and whether their presence is related to specific threats to human health, environmental factors, diet or lifestyle. The presence of type VI-B/RT systems in the gut microbiome may indicate interactions with RNA viruses or other novel RNA genetic entities (46). By studying the association between CRISPR-Cas systems and specific health outcomes or disease states, we may be able to identify novel biomarkers or indicators of gut health and disease susceptibility.
