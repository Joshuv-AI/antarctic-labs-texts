Navigating Complexity in Endodontic Therapy in Children: Case Series on Radix Entomolaris
Authors: Mridula Goswami, Rimshheanam Rimshheanam, R Mona Pattu, Akansha Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims and backgroundThis case series aims to highlight the identification and management of radix entomolaris (RE), an anatomical variation of the mandibular molar, emphasizing its clinical implications on the development of dentition, occlusion, and endodontic treatment. This anatomical variant can lead to diagnostic challenges, inadequate cleaning, persistent infection, or potential treatment failure if not recognized.Case descriptionThis case series presents three cases of permanent mandibular first molar that had periapical radiolucency and the presence of an additional distal root lingually, which was identified by the same lingual, opposite buccal (SLOB) rule. During endodontic therapy, the additional canal within the RE was successfully located and treated, leading to the resolution of symptoms.ConclusionThe presence of a RE necessitates thorough preoperative assessment and careful management during endodontic procedures. Awareness of this anatomical variation is critical for successful outcomes of endodontic therapy.Clinical significanceThese cases underscore the importance of understanding variations in dental anatomy for effective diagnosis and treatment planning in pediatric endodontics. Enhanced knowledge of such anatomical features can improve clinical outcomes and reduce the likelihood of complications in similar cases.Keywords: Anatomical variations, Pediatric dentistry, Pediatric endodontics, Root canal therapy

Introduction

Mandibular first molar typically has two roots (mesial and distal) with three or four canals.1 The mesial root often has two canals (mesiobuccal and mesiolingual), while the distal root usually has one canal.2 Shahi et al. reported that 98.56 and 1.44% of mandibular first molars are two- and three-rooted, respectively, with 65.56% having three canals and 31.57% having four canals.3 Anatomical variations are a recognized feature of mandibular molars, including radix paramolaris (RP) and radix entomolaris (RE). RP is an uncommon macrostructure, occurring less frequently than RE. RE is the occurrence of a third root located lingually to the distal root.4 RE demonstrates an association with specific ethnic groups. Its prevalence is observed at a maximum frequency of 3% among African populations,5 while it occurs at rates below 5% in Eurasian and Indian populations. In Caucasian populations, the prevalence is comparatively lower, ranging from 3.4 to 4.2%, and is considered dysmorphic root morphology.6 It is common in Mongoloid groups (5–30%), considered eumorphic root morphology.3,7 Among first, second, and third molars, the second mandibular molar has the lowest incidence of RE, and the bilateral incidence of RE ranges from 50 to 67%.8 Potential causes for RE include environmental variables, developmental disruptions during odontogenesis, penetration of an atavistic gene, or polygenetic systems, although the exact cause of the formation of RE still remains unidentified.3 Types of RE according to the location of the cervical part were described by Carlsen and Alexandersen (Table 1).9

Radix entomolaris in pediatric patients presents unique challenges, necessitating early diagnosis and treatment planning for successful prognosis and treatment outcomes. During the preoperative radiographic assessment, there is a possibility of superimposition occurring when the buccolingual planes of the RE and the distal root, or RP and the mesial root, align. These variations can be detected on radiographs from different angulations [same lingual, opposite buccal (SLOB) rule]. Advanced diagnostic techniques like cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) and three-dimensional (3D) imaging help pediatric dentists anticipate complications, ensuring comprehensive treatment plans and improved patient care.

Case Description

This case series includes three cases of pediatric patients who reported to the outpatient department (OPD) of the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry. All three cases reported with pain in their lower left back tooth region. Detailed case histories and preoperative radiographs were taken, and the cases were managed according to the treatment needs of the patients after taking informed consent.

Case Report 1

A 10-year-old female reports a sharp, localized pain in tooth 36 that started gradually 2 months ago after eating, with no radiation, associated with sensitivity to hot, worsened by eating, and rated seven out of 10 in severity score. Clinical examination revealed a carious lesion on the left mandibular first molar, corresponding to a score of six (ICDAS II), indicating extensive cavitation with visible dentin. Radiographic investigation demonstrated radiolucency extending through the enamel, dentin, and pulp, along with periapical radiolucency affecting all three roots. Additionally, the presence of a supplementary root was observed (Fig. 1A). A second radiograph was taken following the SLOB rule, confirming the presence of an additional canal on the lingual aspect. Based on the clinical and radiographic findings, symptomatic irreversible pulpitis was diagnosed, and root canal treatment was planned.

Case Report 2

The 13-year-old female presented with severe, throbbing, intermittent pain in tooth 36 that started suddenly 1 week ago, occurring nocturnally, with no radiation, worsened by chewing, and rated eight out of 10 in severity. Clinical examination revealed proximal caries in the left mandibular first molar, with a score of six (ICDAS II). Radiographic assessment showed periapical radiolucency, along with the presence of a radix. The location of the radix was confirmed using the SLOB rule (Fig. 2A). Based on the clinical and radiographic findings, symptomatic irreversible pulpitis was diagnosed, and root canal treatment was planned.

Case Report 3

The 10-year-old female reports spontaneous pain in tooth 36 that has lasted for the past 3 months, relieved by medication, and is intermittent in nature, rated six out of 10 in severity. Clinical examination revealed extensive caries [score of five (ICDAS II)] in the left mandibular first molar. Radiograph demonstrated radiolucency involving the pulp, with associated periodontal ligament widening, as well as the presence of an additional root. Another radiograph was obtained using the SLOB rule to confirm the location of the radix (Fig. 3A). Based on the clinical and radiographic findings, symptomatic irreversible pulpitis was diagnosed, and root canal treatment was planned.

Root Canal Treatment Procedure

A trapezoidal-shaped access cavity was prepared under local anesthesia, lignocaine 2% (Indoco), with an Endo access bur and Endo-Z bur (SuperEndo, India). A DG-16 endodontic explorer was used to locate all the root canal orifices, and the patency of the canals was established using a #10 K-file, and a #6 K-file was used in the RE (Dentsply, Switzerland). Negotiation of the canal of the RE was challenging due to its complex anatomy, narrow orifice, and tortuous canal, especially in pediatric patients with smaller mouths and less cooperative behavior, limited access, and visualization. Working length was determined radiographically, and cleaning and shaping of the root canals (MB, ML, and distal) were done with a hand ProTaper file system (Dentsply, Switzerland) (Figs 1B, 2B, and 3B). Due to the curved and narrow morphology of the radix, biomechanical preparation was initiated with a #6 K-file and continued with NiTiFlex (Dentsply, Switzerland) files up to size 25. The files were precurved to adapt the canal anatomy and progressively enlarged up to the F1 hand ProTaper file (Dentsply, Switzerland) as the master file. Precautions were taken to minimize the risk of procedural errors, such as ledge formation or canal transportation, given the complex and tortuous anatomy of the canal. A combination of normal saline followed by 1% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) irrigants was used with volumes of 5–10 mL per canal after every instrumentation. Final irrigation with saline was performed to flush out debris. Following preparation, calcium hydroxide was used as an intracanal medicament, Cavit™ G (3M, India) temporary restoration material was placed, and the patients were recalled after 2 weeks.

At the next appointment, the patients were asymptomatic. Master cone radiograph was taken, canals were dried with absorbent paper points (Meta Biomed, Korea), and obturation was done with gutta-percha cones (Meta Biomed, Korea) using zinc oxide eugenol sealer (Dentsply De Trey, Germany) (Figs 1C, 2C, and 3C). The access cavity was restored with type II glass ionomer cement (GIC) (Shofu Glass ionomer RX Ease Set GIC, Japan). Postobturation radiographs were taken in all the cases.

For all the cases, follow-up was planned after 2 weeks, 1 month, 3, and 6 months for clinical and radiographic evaluation. Additionally, a stainless steel crown was used as a definitive restoration in cases one and three, following the completion of endodontic therapy to ensure long-term success.

Discussion

Radix entomolaris can be present in both primary and permanent mandibular molars, occurring with a frequency of <1% in the deciduous dentition and more common in permanent dentition, with a frequency of 4.5%. According to Nagaven and Umashankara, bilateral occurrence of RE in primary dentition was 2.6% and 50 to 67% in permanent dentition.10 In the preoperative radiographic assessment, the buccolingual alignment of the RE with the distal root may lead to superimposition, potentially resulting in missing canals or root. To overcome this limitation, it is recommended to obtain two radiographs at different horizontal angulations using the SLOB rule. Missed canals are one of the primary causes of failure of root canal treatment.11

The management of RE posed several challenges, primarily due to the complex and tortuous anatomy. De Moor et al. examined the morphology of RE and found that these canals were typically curved.12 Therefore, it is imperative to prioritize establishing a straight-line access and gliding path preparation. Sequential filing and recapitulation between file sizes were performed to enhance canal cleaning, maintain patency, and ensure thorough canal debridement. These procedures are challenging in pediatric patients, emphasizing the critical need for effective behavior management strategies. Hence, positive peer influence was used to reduce anxiety and encourage the patient's cooperation during the procedure.

Also, mandibular first permanent molars are essential for maintaining balanced occlusion and proper dental arch development. Therefore, it is crucial to preserve these teeth to prevent adverse effects such as tooth tilting, supraeruption, and the onset of malocclusion.13 In all cases, follow-up appointments after the completion of root canal treatment of RE showed satisfactory outcomes, with the absence of clinical symptoms and radiographic evidence of resolution of the periapical lesion at a follow-up period of 1 month.

Conclusion

Recognition of anatomical variations such as RE is essential in pediatric dentistry to ensure accurate diagnosis and comprehensive treatment. Awareness and understanding of such anomalies help prevent procedural errors and improve long-term treatment success.

Clinical Significance

Early identification and management of RE can prevent complications affecting occlusion, growth, and overall oral development in children. This contributes to improved patient comfort and promotes healthier dentofacial outcomes during the developmental years.
