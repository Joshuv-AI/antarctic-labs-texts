External and internal load profiles by age and sex in youth basketball players
Authors: Anna Gál-Pottyondy, Kata Tóth, Áron Mészáros, Gábor Farkasinszky, Lukasz Trzaskoma, Rita M Kiss
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding youth basketball demands is key for data-driven training and injury prevention. An accurate picture of the physical loads experienced by basketball players during matches can be obtained with Local Positioning System.MethodsA total of 224 basketball players (U16, U18, U20; both genders) participated in the study. The inclusion criterion was membership in a Hungarian national youth team. Fifty-one international pre-matches were monitored using WIMU PROTM Local Positioning System (Almería, Spain) during preparatory training camps. Background

The ever-increasing performance demands of modern sports are closely tied to the growing application of performance analysis techniques and the expertise of professionals in this field. Performance analysis enables objective measurement of player stress, including both external and internal load, which are collectively described by the term game-related performance variables (GRP). External load refers to the measurable aspects of physical activity, such as the volume, intensity, and duration of movements (such as covered distance, maximal accelerations, number of high intensity decelerations etc.), while internal load denotes physiological responses (such as maximal heart rate, time of different training zone etc.) [1] A basketball game represents a high external load, as a professional player performs around a thousand movement changes during a match, meaning that speed, direction, and type of movement shift almost every second [2]. Moreover, the importance of agility and multidirectional movement is highlighted by the fact that lateral movements constitute around 20% of the total distance covered [3]. Furthermore, research consistently shows that enhanced agility has a positive effect on performance across all age groups and genders [4–8] In addition to the high external load, the internal load recorded during basketball games is also substantial, as actions are performed at high intensity, with players spending approximately 15% of game time in heart rate zones exceeding 90% of their maximum heart rate) [2] and operate above 85% of HRmax for nearly 75% of the match duration [9].

Considering the high match load and the tight competition schedule the primary objective of training design is to prepare athletes for the physical demands of competition, which requires that certain portions of training sessions reach, and at times exceed, match intensity [10]. In this context, understanding the load characteristics of official matches is essential, as they represent the reference point for peak physical demands. According to Svilar et al.l [11], different types of games can be categorized based on their physical load, with pre-matches reaching approximately 95% of intensity of official games – making them, the closest match – like stimulus available outside of official competition. However, consistently high training loads and insufficient recovery are associated with an increased risk of injury, highlighting the need for carefully balanced workload management [12] and the precise knowledge of match load by age group and gender.

Although basketball game performance analysis dates back to 1931 [13], recent technological advances—such as Global Positioning System (GPS), Local Positioning System (LPS), and optical tracking—have significantly improved the accuracy of athlete monitoring. This has led to increase in related research and scientific publications [14]. This technological advancement has enabled a more detailed and objective evaluation of athletic performance, opening new possibilities for practical application. Nevertheless, there is still a lack of studies that provide practical guidance for coaches and sport science professionals through the precise presentation of percentile-based reference values. As Vázquez-Guerrero et al. [15] have highlighted, knowing only the average GRP values is insufficient; understanding the minimum and maximum values is also essential. Unlike mean-based statistics, percentiles provide clearer differentiation between athletes by identifying both high-and low-end performers. They also allow for more precise monitoring of an athlete’s development through changes in relative standing overtime.

Although the tools are suitable for objectively measuring player performance, inconsistencies exist in definitions, threshold values and measurement methods of GRP indicators. For example, discrepancies exist in the definition of playing time—some studies use total-time, while others focus on live-time—leading to significant differences in results [9]. Specifically, total playing time includes all moments when the game clock is stopped (such as time-outs, free throws, or when the ball is out of bounds), whereas live time only considers periods when the game clock is actually running. Notably, a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05) in average heart rate (HRAVG) has been observed between these measurement protocols: HRAVG during total playing time ranges from 132 to 165 bpm (66.7%–89.1% of HRmax), whereas during live-time analysis, it rises to 160–186 bpm (81.8%–94.6% of HRmax) since, in first case, a greater proportion of inactive or stoppage periods are included, during which heart rate tends to decrease due to reduced physical demand [16]. Another methodological gap in the literature concerns the definition of high-intensity accelerations (HIAcc) and decelerations (HIDec). Few studies address this issue, and those that do use varying criteria—some define HIAcc and HIDec as movements exceeding ± 3 m/s², while others apply separate thresholds of >3 m/s² for acceleration and <–3 m/s² for deceleration [4]. These varying threshold values are highlighted by García et al. [17] whose recommendation settings for the threshold values are ± 3 m/s².

To the best of our knowledge, no study has provided percentile-based reference GRP values by age and gender for national-level youth basketball players, despite the fact that such values could offer coaches practical benchmarks for individualized training load management and performance evaluation. What is more there is also lack of research in this area conducted on Hungarian basketball players. Based on these points, the well-defined GRP reference values not only position Hungarian basketball within the global context but also could offer an evidence-based benchmark that helps coaches and practitioners calibrate training intensity and volume more precisely to reflect actual match demand. This supports targeted development and reduce the risk of overtraining and injury. A clear understanding of sport-specific physical and physiological requirements also strengthens talent identification, ensuring testing protocols reflect real match demands and improving the validity of player evaluation [18, 19]. Furthermore, continuous monitoring of specific indicators can help detect early signs of athlete fatigue. For example, decreasing in HIAcc and HIDec during match-to-match analysis may indicate the player’s fatigue and lower number of decelerations and less distance covered were significantly associated with injury during professional basketball games [20]. Finally integrating GRP reference values into standardized testing protocols, allows practitioners to set objective threshold for competition readiness [21], making return-to-play decisions more evidence-base and lowering the risk of re-injury.

In the present study, we aimed to provide reference GRP values for U16, U18, and U20 male and female basketball players of the Hungarian national teams. The data were collected using the WIMU Pro system (Almería, Spain), and the GRP values were analyzed using a percentile-based method for each subgroup. Our second aim was to examine differences in GRP values with respect to age and gender, specifically within the population of national-level youth basketball players. Firstly, we hypothesized that a reference table for GRP values of the Hungarian youth national basketball teams could be established based on data collected from official matches. Secondly, based on differences in physical and motor coordination abilities across age groups and between sexes [22], we hypothesized that significant differences exist in GRP indicators across age categories and between genders. The reference values derived from the Hungarian national teams, along with the identified intergroup differences, may serve as valuable benchmarks for coaches and performance experts in interpreting and applying their own performance data.

Methods

Participant

224 adolescent basketball players (31 U16 male, 28 U16 female, 30 U18 male, 51 U18 female, 28 U20 male 56 U20 female) of Hungarian national teams participated in the present study. Table 1 presents the average anthropometric data of the players. The inclusion criteria required participants to be officially selected members of the national team, chosen by youth basketball coaches and professional staff based on their overall club performance throughout the entire season and their suitability for integration within the team. As such, the sample represents the top-level players of their respective age and gender categories at the national level. We monitored fifty-one international pre-matches (twelve U16 male, nine U16 female, six U18 male, seven U18 female, seven U20 male, and ten U20 female) during their preparatory training camps between 2021 and 2024. The exclusion criteria were (a) players who played less than 10 min of total game time, and (b) players who sustained injuries during the matches, resulting in a final dataset of 542 individual records (129 U16 male, 94 U16 female, 63 U18 male, 76 U18 female, 79 U20 male, and 101 U20 female). Each match observation was treated as an independent sample, regardless of whether multiple observations originated from the same player across different games. This decision was based on basketball is an extrinsic team sport, in which individual performance metrics are substantially influenced by the opposing team, thus justifying the treatment of each match as contextually independent. Gameplay was conducted according to International Basketball Federation (FIBA) rules. All players, coaches, and parents were informed both orally and in writing about the research protocol, requisites, benefits, and risks and agreed to participate by providing their written consent prior to the start of the experiment, which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The present study was carried out in accordance with the recommendations of The Ethics Committee of the Hungarian University of Sport Science (TE-KEB/No6/2019), which also approved the study’s institutional approval.

Method

The GRP of the basketball players were tracked using the WIMU PRO™ RealTrack Local Positioning Systems (LPS) (Almería, Spain), which incorporated Garmin heart rate monitoring bands during international pre-matches (Fig. 1). The RealTrack System was used to segment the game into quarters, with data collected only while players were actively competing on the court, including timeouts and free throws. Although the WIMU PRO system is considered valid for heart rate monitoring [23], to ensure the exclusion of erroneous data, heart rate values were continuously monitored live during the matches. If measured values did not correspond to the observed movement intensity, the case was immediately recorded. When players were out of the court the placement of the heart rate band was checked and adjusted if necessary. If erroneous was detected heart rate data of the player for those given games were deleted. Each player wore the same microtechnology device [24], positioned in the center of their upper back, within an adjustable top, which is part of the WIMU PRO system and specifically designed to ensure proper placement of the device (Fig. 1). Previous studies have utilized WIMU PRO™ to measure and monitor basketball players during matches [25, 26], demonstrating good accuracy (for the x-position: 5.2 ± 3.1 cm and for the y-position: 5.8 ± 2.3 cm) and inter- and intra-unit reliability for ultra-wideband positioning (for the x-coordinate ICC = 0.65 and for the y-coordinate ICC = 0.85) [27–30].

Data recording and processing procedure

The ultra-wideband system consisted of six antennas, which were calibrated beforehand and communicated wirelessly via UWB. The localization of players was determined through triangulation between the antennas and the tracking units at a frequency of 18 Hz. The internal measurement units featured four 3D accelerometers, a 3D magnetometer, three 3D gyroscopes and a barometer, all with a sampling frequency of 100 Hz. Data on external and internal demands were downloaded and analyzed using SPRO™ software (version 977, RealTrack Systems) (Figure 1).

Following methodologies established in prior research [8, 25, 26, 31], which suggest that the quality of basketball matches is primarily determined by the amount of agility and high-intensity movements, variables representing these factors (as listed in Table 2) were analyzed. For all variables, we applied the threshold values recommended and used by the WIMU PRO system. The distance covered during the matches was evaluated to accurately represent proportional distributions. Heart rate data were recorded using a Garmin sensor. In defining the heat rate zones, we followed the recommendations of the WIMU PRO system and based on the review of Berkelmans et al. [32], the lowest range we analyze was the 60–70% heart rate zone. Detailed average GRP values for each player are available from the authors upon request.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was carried out using JASP [33] (JASP Team, 2024). Distributional characteristics of the data were explored with visual methods (histogram, Q-Q plot).

Reference values were determined with percentile method: reference interval fell within 5-95th percentiles, below/above reference within 1-5th and 95-99th percentiles respectively, while extreme values were represented by the lowest and highest percentiles.

Effects of gender (female, male), age group (U16, U18, U20) as well as their interaction (U16 female, U16 male, U18 female, U18 male, U20 female, U20 male) were analyzed using two-way interaction MANOVA. MANOVA was chosen based on the fact that it reduces Type I Error Inflation compared with ANOVA [34]. We also conducted an effect size analysis using partial eta squared (η²ₚ). The interpretation of partial eta squared values was as follows: values below 0.01 were considered trivial, values from 0.01 indicated a small effect, values from 0.06 a medium effect, and values above 0.14 a large effect. The MANOVA detected significant statistical differences in any of the mentioned aspects, we proceeded with the two-way interaction ANOVA for each dependent variable, followed by the Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test [34]. Level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, while results with p ≤ 0.001 were interpreted as strongly significant.

Results

The average percentile GRP values for males is presented in Tables 3 and 4 while those for females is presented in Tables 5 and 6. The complete GRP reference tables are provided as supplementary files (Addition Tables 6 and 7). The recording of HR values during basketball games is a complex process, as incorrect placement of the sensors, mislabeling, possible detachment, or data transmission failure can lead to inaccurate data [3, 35]. For these reasons, many HR data had to be excluded from the analysis in the present study (25 invalid cases for U16 girls, 35 for U18 girls, 46 for U20 girls, 43 for U16 boys, 11 for U18 boys, and 12 for U20 boys). As a result, the internal load values obtained for these reduced sample cannot be considered reference values and in the case of detecting potential differences, the smaller sample size also results in reduced statistical validity. When interpreting differences in internal load, it is important to note that the analyses were conducted on smaller sample sizes; however, these sample sizes met the necessary requirements for statistical analysis.

Discussion

In the present study, the GRP indicators of Hungarian youth basketball national teams were examined during their matches. The goals of the present study were (1) to establish reference table for GRP variables for U16, U18 and U20 male and female basketball players of the Hungarian national teams and (2) to examine differences by age group and gender. We firstly hypothesized that a reference table for GRP values of the Hungarian youth national basketball teams could be established based on data collected from official matches. Our second hypothesis was that there are significant differences in GRP between different age and gender groups. We accepted our first hypothesis regarding external load indicators, as we were able to establish reference values for the examined variables, whereas the hypothesis was rejected in the case of internal load indicators since the sample size was insufficient to consider the results as reliable reference values. We accept our second hypothesis, as the groups showed differences in terms of age, gender, and in the interaction between the two.

The reference GRP values established by gender and age group provide a scientifically grounded framework to support coaches and performance staff in defining objective targets across multiple domains, including training periodization [10, 12], talent identification [18], and the formulation of return-to-play criteria following injury [21].

Although internal load indicators cannot be considered reference values due to the limited sample size, they are still discussed below to provide a more comprehensive and accurate understanding of match load and to place the GRP reference values in a broader context. Before discussing our results in detail, it must be noted that repeated observations from the same players were treated as independent in the statistical analysis, which could introduce potential bias. Supplementary Tables 1–5, provided in the supplementary file, summarize the measurement data available in the literature for variables.

Cross-reference with literature

The distance values we measured among Hungarian basketball players are like those recorded in the NBA [36] and in case of U18 male Spanish First League [37], but they differ significantly from the values reported by Petway et al. [4] for youth athletes (see in additional Table 1). Among female athletes we recorded higher Dis value than those reported in female Spanish First League [37].

The SpeedMAX values of Hungarian national-level players most closely resemble those of sub-elite Spanish players (see in additional Table 2) and similarly, sub-elite Spanish (22.5 ± 2.7 km/h) [38] and youth players [39] (guards: 19.9 ± 1.2 km/h; forwards: 19.6 ± 1.0 km/h; centers: 19.4 ± 1.0 km/h) we measured significantly lower maximum speeds compared to NBA players [4] (29.1 km/h) (see in additional Table 2). In the case of SpeedAVG, our results are consistent with those found in the literature, which suggests that SpeedAVG, measured in youth sports (5.11–5.46 km/h) [40] and lower-level leagues (3,9 − 4,5 km/h) [20] is lower than in the much higher-level adult NBA [41] (5.87–7.05 km/h).

However, AccMAX and DecMAX values indicate the explosiveness of athletes, and DecMAX is significantly (p ≤ 0.05) and moderately (r = 0.423) correlated with the 1-minute normalized Efficiency index [42], most of the literature does not examine maximum acceleration and deceleration. We found only one study that examined AccMAX, which reported significantly (p ≤ 0.05) different values compared to those of Hungarian national-level youth players (Pino-Ortega et al. [43] guards: 3.4 ± 0.5 m/s2, forwards: 3.3 ± 0.5 m/s2, centers: 2.9 ± 0.7 m/s2 vs. our study female 5.4–6.83, male 5.5–7.46 m/s2) (see in additional Table 6).

According to Petway et al. [4] systematic review an average of 81 accelerations (Acc), 7.3 HIAccN, 73.1 decelerations (Dec) and 16.4 HIDecN were measured during one basketball matches. For HIDecN and HIAccN, these values differ from our measured averages (Tables 4 and 5), which may be due to the different considerations of HIAcc and HIDec. Because of this inconsistency in the literature, we do not present specific values for these variables, but instead, we present our findings based on trends and observations when discussing sex- and age-related differences.

Although the limited sample size and data quality issues warrant caution in interpreting internal load indicators—and prevent their use as normative benchmarks—we still consider the discussion of our results to be meaningful. Petway et al. [4] found that adult elite basketball players have lower HRMAX (ranged from 187 to 198 beats/min (bpm), an average 190 bpm) than sub-elite players (HRMAX ranged from 192 to 195 bpm, an average of 194 bpm). According to our research the HRMAX of Hungarian youth national basketball teams is ranged from 192 bpm to 205 bpm which effectively reflects the higher HRMAX of younger individuals.

Berkelmans et al.’s [35] comprehensive review study indicates that the HRAVG of female basketball players ranges from 86.6 ± 2% to 93.6 ± 4% of HRMAX, while for males, it varies from 90 ± 5% to 94 ± 2%. In the present study we measured lower values; HRAVG of Hungarian youth female players ranged from 76.6% to 79.2%, while for male player it ranged from 70.6 to 74.48%. his difference may be attributed to the fact that we recorded the data during time-outs and free throws, periods during which players’ heart rates significantly decrease.

To gain a more accurate understanding of internal load, it is essential to consider the percentage distribution of time spent in different heart rate zones. Tunisian male U19 age grouped basketball players spent 7.4 ± 6.1% of total time in HRzones65-74%, 17.3 ± 5.5% in HRzones75-84%, 56.0 ± 6.3% in HRzones85-95%, and 19.3 ± 3.5% in HRzones95 <% [3]. Similar values have also been measured in adult Australian, male basketball players, since they found that they spent 65% of total time in HRzones85<% [44]. Compared to the values found in the literature [3, 44, 45], Hungarian basketball players spent less time in high-intensity zones (see in additional Table 7). This difference may also be due to the previously mentioned varying measurement methodologies.

Trends in GRP variables across age and gender groups

However, to effectively define training goals, plan long-term athletic development, and gain deeper insights into biological and sport-specific developmental trends, it is essential to identify age- and sex-related differences as well. It is important to emphasize that these trends were specifically observed in Hungarian national-level basketball players.

Although most studies examine the distance covered during matches, the literature consistently states that the magnitude of distance covered is not decisive for match outcomes [4, 6, 46]. This is in line with our results since we did not find any significant (p ≤ 0,05) differences in terms of age groups, genders, or the interaction between the two groups for Dis (Table 7). Despite this Petway et al. [4] found that players competing at higher level covered the least Dis compared to those at lower levels and youth players. This difference was not attributed to age or playing level variations, but rather to the team’s tactical preparedness. A similar conclusion was reached by Kalén et al. [36], whose study on NBA players found that those aged 19–25 run the most and at the fastest pace during games, while peak movement efficiency is observed in later years, as the distance covered during matches decreases.

The ability to perform repeated explosive movements throughout a game may be associated with playing at a higher competitive level [38]. In the case of ExplDis there were differences according to age groups, gender, and their interaction as well with small effect sizes (Table 7). The males’ ExplDis value was higher than that of the females (Table 8) and this trend is particularly evident in the U16 age group (Fig. 2). Although these observations were associated with small effect size, they can be considered meaningful in practical terms, as our findings are corroborated by previous studies in the literature such as Portes et al. [37] who found that male players have higher Hight Intensity Distance (HIDis) than female players. This observation is also supported by motion analysis studies, which reveal that in terms of overall movement, higher-level male players exhibit a significantly (p ≥ 0.05) greater ratio of sprints and fast run measured during matches compared to players at lower levels and female basketball player [47, 48]. Although the comparison across age groups shows that older players achieve higher values [9], we found that the U18 group achieved a lower ExplDis value than both the U16 and U20 groups. Despite the low effect size, we regard this finding as practically meaningful, since, consistent with the literature, an increase was observed from the U16 to the U20 age groups, whereas a decrease was evident at the U18 level in both female and male players. We believed this decrease at U18 age group can be explained by two main factors. First, Evolution of game techniques and tactics, from systems based on a large number of simple running tasks to systems requiring more advanced tactics based on lower volume but higher quality (intensity). Second, Anthropometric changes – in 16-year-old players, a relatively lower body mass allows for the performance of simple basketball tasks with a higher volume. In 20-year-old players, a higher level of strength and power allows for more complex tactical and technical tasks with greater intensity and volume. The transition between these two systems occurs specifically in 18-year-old players. Based on these a U16 or U18 player exceeding the 95th percentile in ExplDis may be suited for high-intensity transition roles or targeted for advanced conditioning programmes.

The SpeedMAX reached during basketball games is a positive indicator of a player’s basketball-specific performance, as our previous research found the SpeedMAX is significantly (p ≤ 0.05) correlated with players’ match performance [42] and higher playing level [4]. In the present study, we observed a difference between gender- with strong effect size - and between age groups- with small effect size (Table 7). Males and U20 groups achieving higher values than females and U16 age groups (Table 8). Although repeated sprint tests show a speed difference between the U16 and U18 age groups [49], our results indicate that this increase in speed is effectively integrated into match play only by players in the U20 age group.

Although SpeedAVG is significantly correlated with match statistics [42], higher SpeedAVG may indicate lower tactical preparedness [41]. Consistent with the literature [41], we found that as age increases, the SpeedAVG during the match decreases, although with small effect size. Specifically, the U16 group achieved a higher value than the U18 or U20 groups (Table 8). Kallén et al. [36] explained this by stating that the higher level of technical and tactical knowledge, which allows older players to move more energy-efficiently (development of more optimal step length and frequency, more precise coordination of muscle activation patterns, improved movement economy, shorter route to reach the optimal position etc.), leads to less skilled younger players not only covering the most Dis but also achieving the highest SpeedAVG. In the comparison between genders, we found that the SpeedAVG of female players is higher than that of male players’, with small effect size (Table 8). Given that our findings are in agreement with the existing literature, they may serve as practically relevant guidance, even in light of the low effect size. We recommend that when evaluating SpeedAVG, SpeedMAX, and agility indicators should also be assessed simultaneously. If a player exceeding the high percentile in SpeedAVG, but SpeedMAX, and agility values are in average or lower percentile ranges, this likely indicates tactical unpreparedness rather than a higher match intensity.

Agility is one of the most important abilities in basketball, and it can be effectively characterized by the magnitude and frequency of accelerations and decelerations. Acceleration and deceleration capacity – that is, explosiveness – is closely related to maximal strength of lower limbs [50]. As net leg strength generally increases with advancing age, it can be assumed that performance indicators associated with accelerations and decelerations also show an improving trend over time. Moreover, we found differences in agility indicators for gender, age, and their interaction, but all these differences had small effect sizes. Therefore, for those results that could not be corroborated by the existing literature, further research is required before drawing practical conclusions due to the small effect size. Regarding AccMAX, there was a significant (p ≤ 0.05) difference between age groups, as well as between the U16 and U20 males (Fig. 2). The U20 age group exhibited the highest acceleration, which aligns with the fact that maximal strength development typically begins at this age. In terms of DecMAX, U20 males also stood out, as there was a significant (p < 0.05) difference between U20 males and females (Fig. 2), as well as between the U16 and U20 males (Fig. 2). Higher level [48], and more successful players [43] exhibit a higher number of HIAcc and HIDec. Consistent with the literature [9, 48], we found significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences only between age groups for HIAccN and HIDecN., with the highest values in the U20 age group (Table 8). While most studies [9, 47, 48] focus on these counts, our research shows that gender differences among Hungarian national-level basketball players are reflected in this distance (HIAccD and HIDecD), with males covering greater distances (Table 7). Both HIAccD and HIDecD also increase significantly (p ≤ 0.05) in the U20 age group (Table 8).

Accurate and valid measurement of internal load indicators presents a significant challenge. Moreover, internal load values are influenced by numerous factors such as hydration, sleep duration, humidity, and temperature. Additionally, the measurement methodology used during basketball games (live time vs. actual playing time) can cause discrepancies [35]. Despite these challenges, analysing internal load is essential for understanding and assessing the load experienced by the player. To better understand trends across sexes and age groups, we also analysed differences in internal load, as our sample size (boys: U16–112, U18–50, U20–41; girls: U16–59, U18–60, U20–66) is sufficiently large for such comparisons.

However, HRMAX is strongly influenced by the player’s age and fitness level [51], we did not observe statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences between genders, age groups and their interactions (Tables 5 and 6) in our sample of Hungarian national-level youth basketball players.

The HRAVG, did not differ between age groups but showed significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences between genders, with small effect size (Table 7). In our dataset, the HRAVG of female players tended to be higher than that of males (Table 8). Our findings align with Berkelmans et al. [35] who observed that gender differences should be considered when analyzing HR data. However, our findings do not align with Stojnovic et al. [9] and Narazaki et al. [52] who found that the HRAVG does not differ by gender but may vary by positions. The literature is not consistent in HRAVG of players at different levels. There are also studies that found higher values in elite players [3] while others did not [53]. Due to the inconsistencies in the literature and the small effect size, further research is required to more accurately examine gender- and age-related differences in HR AVG measured during basketball games. To gain a more accurate understanding of the cardiovascular stress experienced by players, it is more useful to examine HR zones rather than average and maximum HR [35]. Based on our sample, no significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences were observed in the lower zones (HRzones60-70% and HRzones70-80%), between age groups, genders, or their interactions (Table 7) among Hungarian national-level youth basketball players. In line with the literature, we noted differences in the time spent in high-intensity zones based on gender and age. Female players in our sample appeared to spend more time in the HRzone 80–90% (small effect size), 90–95% (medium effect size) and 95–200% (small effect size) compared to their male counterparts (Table 8). A similar conclusion was drawn from the literature, as it was found that women spent approximately 76% [45] of the total time in HR zones above 85%, while for men, this value was around 65% [44] (see in additional Tables 5 and 6). The age grouped-related differences we identified were associated with small effect sizes and should be interpreted with caution given the reduced sample sizes. Specifically, U20 players spent significantly (p ≤ 0.05) more time in HRzone 80–90% compared to U18 players, while U16 players spent more time in HRzones 90–95% and 95–200% than the U18 group (Tables 3 and 4). These differences may stem from age group characteristics rather than tactical and technical knowledge, as international-level players, i.e., those with higher qualifications, spend more time above the HRzones95% compared to lower-level, nationally qualified players [16]. Overall, when interpreting internal load metrics in our study, it is important to account for potential limitations due to data quality issues and reduced sample sizes. Therefore, when planning post-match recovery strategies, the observed trends in internal load differences between genders and across age groups might provide useful insights.

Summarizing the trends by sex and age group among Hungarian national-level youth basketball players, we observed the following: Although differences in mean values (5th, 95th percentiles, and median) were not statistically significant, males showed a slight age-related improvement. For three indicators (AccMAX, DecMAX, and HIDecD), this improvement was significant between U16 and U20. In females, the U18 group often had lower average values than U16 in several variables, but these differences were not significant. Most female performance indicators stagnated across age groups, except for HIDecD, which improved significantly from U18 to U20. We suggest three reasons for this stagnation in females: First, differences between chronological and biological age can be large, so group averages may appear stable despite individual progress. Second, hormonal changes cause rapid body mass increases, which can limit gains in relative strength. Third, many girls already compete with senior teams, resulting in training that may not match their age group and slow individual development.

When analysing the combined data across sexes, a general improvement can be observed from U16 to U20 in nearly all variables, except for ExplDis, where the U18 group performs worse than U16—a result that has been addressed in the discussion.

Based on the statistical analysis combining age groups, it can be stated that Hungarian national-level youth males performed movements at higher speeds and greater intensity, whereas females exhibited higher SpeedAVG and HRAVG during matches.

Practical advice

LPS monitoring during basketball matches provides an objective means of assessing players’ actual performance. For the sake of comparability and validity of results, it is essential to clearly define the measurement methodology, specifying the parts of the match and the threshold values for each variable. In addition, to more effectively capture internal load, during live-time monitoring, it is important to pay close attention to unrealistic heart rate data and to correct the position of heart rate belt as quickly as possible in order to avoid invalid heart rate values. According to present study, we concluded that measuring SprintDis in youth basketball players is not relevant and during match analyzing the AccMAX, DecMAX, ExplDis, and HIDec variables, players should be grouped according to both age group and gender. For Dec, no grouping is necessary, while for the other variables, grouping can be done based on either age or gender, as indicated in Table 7. Finally, the percentile-based reference GRP tables (additional Tables 6 and 7) developed from several years of research on top youth basketball players of Hungary, offer a robust benchmark for evaluating measures values, defining objective return-to play criteria, and optimizing training loads. Interpreting percentile ranges can support targeted training decisions. Athletes in lower percentiles may benefit from focused development, those around the average range from performance stabilization, while those in higher percentiles may require strategies to maintain and refine elite-level abilities.

Limitation

Despite the valuable insights provided, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged in greater detail. Due to numerous errors and missing data in the HR measurements, the internal load values reported here cannot be considered as normative reference values. Additionally the post-match analysis did not allow for the publication of values on a per-minute basis, which means that quantitative values are strongly influenced by individual playing time duration what is more the data collection period (2021–2024) coincided with external factors – such as pandemic-related disruptions and scheduling changes – that may have influenced training loads and performance metrics. In addition, although positional differences in certain GRP variables have been documented in the literature, we refrained from further categorizing players by position in order to avoid excessive fragmentation of the sample and sufficient statistical power. As the sample is limited to Hungarian national youth teams, the findings may not be directly generalizable to other countries or competitive levels. Finally, one methodological limitation of this study is that the statistical models assume independence of observations, although repeated measurements were conducted for some participants. This may have introduced intra-individual dependencies that violate the assumption of independence underlying standard ANOVA and MANOVA procedures. Such violations can increase the risk of Type I errors and distort effect size estimates (e.g., due to underestimated standard errors). For future studies, the application of mixed-effects models may help address the issue of repeated measurements, and particular attention should be paid to improving the reliability of heart rate recordings.

Conclusion

Novelty of the present study lies in the joint examination of changes in external and internal load indicators during international pre-matches of national-level basketball players, as well as in providing a reference table for external load variables. Furthermore, it offers age and gender specific values that reflect the physical performance characteristics of youth basketball players, thereby supporting coaches in optimizing training programs and guiding player development. The percentile-based reference GRP table (additional Tables 6 and 7) help identify players’ strengths and areas for improvement and assist in planning an optimal return-to-play process after injury. Furthermore, these data enable coaches to better align training intensity and content with actual match demands and manage weekly training loads more effectively. In summary, we recommend that practitioners incorporate these percentile-based GRP reference values into daily training planning and load management, taking into account players’ age, gender, and the real physical demands observed in competitive games. Future research should aim to expand the percentile-based reference tables by including time-normalized values for quantitative variables and increasing the sample size for internal load indicators, to ensure these variables are reliably represented in the reference data. Moreover, increasing the sample size would allow for the development of position-specific percentile reference tables as well.

All players, coaches, and parents were informed both orally and in writing about the research protocol, requisites, benefits, risks and inclusion criteria and agreed to participate by providing their written consent prior to the start of the experiment, which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The present study was carried out in accordance with the recommendations of The Ethics Committee of the Hungarian University of Sport Science (TE-KEB/No6/2019), which also approved the study’s institutional approval.

Consent for publication

All players, coaches, and parents were informed both orally and in writing about the research protocol, requisites, benefits, and risks, and agreed to participate by providing their written consent prior to the start of the experiment. The study participants and their legal representatives also consented to the anonymous use of the measurement results for research purposes and to the publication of our findings.
