Integrative genetic analysis identifies shared regulation of DNA methylation and gene expression in migraine risk
Authors: Ammarah Ghaffar, Dale R Nyholt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigraine affects approximately 14% of adults, but the molecular mechanisms underlying susceptibility remain incompletely understood. Although genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified many migraine risk loci, translating these associations into biological insight remains challenging. Environmental and physiological migraine triggers, including hormones, diet and stress, may act partly through epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation. Because many migraine risk variants are non-coding and may influence disease through regulatory effects, integrating genetic association data with DNA methylation and gene expression may help refine migraine-associated loci into candidate molecular mechanisms.MethodsWe performed a novel methylome-wide association study (meWAS) of migraine, imputing genetically regulated DNA methylation at 86,518 cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) sites using GWAS summary statistics derived from 102,084 migraine cases and 771,257 controls of European ancestry. We then linked DNA methylation signals to imputed gene expression using transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) evidence across 49 Genotype-Tissue Expression tissues. Bayesian colocalisation analyses were used to prioritise CpGs and genes supported by shared causal variants with migraine risk, and to map shared regulatory signals between methylation and expression quantitative trait loci.ResultsWe identified 258 migraine-associated CpG sites after Bonferroni correction (P < 5.78 × 10–7), of which 177 colocalised with migraine GWAS variants (PP4 > 0.5) and mapped to 69 independent genomic loci, including 58 established and 11 putative novel loci. Integration with TWAS evidence prioritised 199 genes (candidate-set Bonferroni-corrected TWAS P < 7.84 × 10–5) near these CpG sites, including 91 genes with evidence of colocalisation with migraine GWAS variants. Further colocalisation between methylation and expression quantitative trait signals mapped shared regulatory signals to 120 CpGs and 78 genes across 40 independent genomic loci, comprising 32 established and eight putative novel migraine risk loci. Protein–protein interaction analysis showed modest but significant network enrichment, with local modules implicating signalling organisation, mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, and related regulatory processes.ConclusionsThese findings show that migraine susceptibility is partly shaped by shared genetic regulation of DNA methylation and gene expression. By refining GWAS loci into candidate genes and molecular signals, this integrative multi-omic framework highlights distributed molecular pathways relevant to neuronal and vascular responsiveness and identifies candidates for future functional characterisation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02469-x.Keywords: Migraine genetics, DNA methylation, Epigenetic mechanisms, GWAS, Multi-omic analysis

Background

Migraine is a complex and debilitating neurological disorder with strong familial aggregation [1, 2] and heritability estimates ranging from 35% to 60% [3]. It is characterised by recurrent episodes of severe pulsating headache, often accompanied by photophobia, phonophobia, nausea, vomiting, and visual disturbances, including, in some cases, sensory or speech disturbances may also occur [4]. These symptoms contribute substantially to disability and reduced quality of life worldwide. Genetic studies indicate that migraine is influenced by numerous genetic variants of small effect, as well as environmental factors [5]. Although many risk loci have been identified across the genome, translating these associations into biological insight remains a major challenge due to the disorder’s polygenic architecture and complex gene-environment interactions.

The advent of high-throughput DNA sequencing technologies and cost-effective genotyping platforms has enabled large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS), which are now central to investigating the genetic basis of complex traits. GWAS systematically test for associations between common genetic variants—typically those with a minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.01—and phenotypic traits across the entire genome [6]. In migraine, these studies have been particularly successful in identifying reproducible genetic risk loci.

The largest migraine GWAS to date, conducted by Hautakangas et al. in 2022 [7], included 102,084 migraine cases and 771,257 controls. This study reported 123 distinct genomic regions associated with migraine (P < 5 × 10− 8). Downstream computational analyses focused on enrichment of expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) across human tissues, revealing significant enrichment in cardiovascular tissues and cell types. Despite these advances, pinpointing the causal genes and regulatory mechanisms underlying most GWAS loci remains unresolved.

Genetic variants located within regulatory regions can influence disease risk by modulating gene expression at the mRNA level, as captured by eQTLs. However, most disease-associated variants identified by GWAS are located outside of coding regions and may not directly affect gene expression through known mechanisms. Furthermore, eQTLs appear to explain only a small fraction of GWAS signals [8]. For instance, the GTEx Consortium found that only 43% of GWAS hits (median 21% of hits per trait) were colocalised with eQTLs [9], and on average, only 11% of heritability was mediated by gene expression in GTEx tissues [10].

Several explanations have been proposed for the limited overlap between GWAS and eQTLs. Trait-relevant eQTLs may be context specific—active only during certain developmental stages [11, 12], in specific cell types [13] or under immune stimulation [14, 15]—and these conditions are often difficult to capture in traditional expression studies, which typically use postmortem tissue samples. Additionally, many eQTLs may remain undiscovered due to limited statistical power and sample sizes [16]. Collectively, these findings motivate the integration of complementary molecular layers beyond gene expression.

Genetic effects on gene expression can be mediated by epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation [17]. DNA methylation involves the addition of a methyl group (CH3) at the C-5 position of cytosine residues, typically within CpG dinucleotides. Although methylation patterns can change dynamically, they are often stably maintained or re-established during cell division, resulting in persistent regulatory changes that influence gene expression and disease susceptibility [18]. Importantly, DNA methylation represents a plausible molecular interface between genetic variation and environmental exposures [19]. While promoter CpG island hypermethylation has classically been associated with transcriptional repression, emerging evidence indicates a more complex relationship [20], including instances where hypermethylation is associated with increased gene expression [21, 22].

Multiple environmental factors—including hormones, diet, and mental stress—have been reported as triggers of migraine, and these factors can influence DNA methylation [23]. Several lines of evidence support a role for DNA methylation in mediating long-term cellular responses to genetic and environmental influences that modify migraine risk. For example, Gerring et al. performed an epigenome-wide association study in 67 migraine cases and controls, identifying 62 independent differentially methylated regions associated with migraine at FDR P < 0.05 [24]. A longitudinal study further demonstrated reduced DNA methylation at an intronic CpG site within the HDAC4 gene was observed in chronic migraine patients who responded to medication withdrawal, and lower DNA methylation at MARK3 was also associated with symptom reduction [25]. Another study examined DNA methylation patterns in 15 monozygotic twin pairs and found that 99 genes (132 CpGs) associated with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) also overlapped with genes associated with migraine [26]. Aberrant methylation of key migraine-related genes—including CALCA, RAMP1, MTHFR, ESR1, and NOS1—has been linked to altered gene expression, potentially contributing to migraine pathophysiology through dysregulated protein production [27]. Several studies have highlighted a potential role for DNA methylation in modulating headache- and migraine-related pathways [28]. Additionally, a smaller number of studies support a role for DNA methylation in migraine chronification, suggesting that these stable but reversible epigenetic modifications may influence the transition from episodic to chronic migraine [29].

Like gene expression, DNA methylation at specific loci can be influenced by genetic variation, referred to as methylation quantitative trait loci (meQTLs) [30]. However, the relationship between genetic variants identified through GWAS, DNA methylation (meQTLs), and gene expression (eQTLs) at specific loci remains poorly characterised and has not been studied in the context of migraine [31]. This represents a critical gap in our understanding of how genetic risk is translated into molecular dysfunction in migraine. The in silico methylome-wide association study (meWAS) approach, developed by Freytag et al. [32], integrates GWAS data with meQTL data to impute genetically regulated DNA methylation differences between GWAS cases and controls. In this approach, the joint additive effects of cis-meQTLs at a CpG site are used to derive DNA methylation prediction models, which are then tested for association with a trait in an independent GWAS dataset. Similarly, in silico transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) use joint additive effects of cis-eQTLs near a gene’s transcription start site to predict genetically-regulated gene expression, which is then tested for trait association. Both methods aim to identify horizontal pleiotropy, where an intermediate molecular trait (e.g., CpG methylation or gene expression) and a disease phenotype (e.g., migraine) are influenced by the same genetic variant. These imputation-based approaches offer the advantage of predicting genetically driven molecular phenotypes, thereby minimising confounding from environmental factors such as diet, lifestyle, and medication, which can affect traditional case-control studies of directly measured DNA methylation and gene expression [33].

In the present study, we applied a multi‑omic framework that integrates migraine GWAS summary statistics with meWAS, TWAS, and colocalisation analyses to identify genetically driven DNA methylation changes associated with migraine risk and to identify shared genetic regulation of DNA methylation and gene expression. Using the largest available migraine GWAS dataset [7], we aimed to (i) identify CpG sites with robust evidence of association with migraine, (ii) prioritise genes influenced by these methylation signals, and (iii) characterise both established and putative novel migraine risk loci by investigating the joint contribution of DNA methylation and gene expression mechanisms.

Methods

GWAS studies

The migraine GWAS summary statistics by Hautakangas et al. [7] were used in this study. It consisted of 102,084 migraine cases and 771,257 controls with European ancestry [7]. This GWAS dataset contained the Gormley et al. (2016) migraine GWAS [34] and additional GWAS data from 23andMe, Inc. (22,644 cases and 87,729 controls), UK Biobank (10,881 cases and 330,170 controls), GeneRISK (1,084 cases and 4,857 controls), and HUNT (7,801 cases and 32,423 controls). A total of 8,117 genome-wide significant (P < 5 × 10–8) SNPs were identified, of which 170 were LD-independent (r2 < 0.1) index SNPs. The 170 index SNPs mapped to 123 independent genomic risk loci of which 86 were novel. The independent genomic risk loci were defined by grouping all SNPs in high LD (r2 > 0.6) with the index SNPs and merging loci that were closer than 250 kb. Further details, including quality control and imputation, are provided in the original publication [7].

Differential methylation using meWAS

Differentially methylated CpGs were identified using the meWAS approach implemented in the MetaMeth R package [32]. MetaMeth imputes genetically regulated DNA methylation at 86,518 CpGs using meQTL-based prediction models derived from the Basel Imaging meQTL dataset, which comprises whole-blood methylation and genotype data from 533 healthy young individuals of European ancestry aged 18–37 years [35]. DNA methylation was profiled using the Illumina HumanMethylation450 array, with full details of sample collection and processing reported previously. For each CpG, prediction models were constructed using elastic net-regularised linear regression of methylation levels on common cis-acting SNPs (minor allele frequency > 0.05) within ± 1 Mb from the CpG, restricted to variants present in the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 European reference panel (n = 503). These models served as the reference panel for methylation imputation. Whole-blood meQTLs were used as a reference given their availability, large sample size, and demonstrated utility for capturing genetically regulated DNA methylation effects relevant to complex traits.

Methylation values were then imputed into an independent dataset containing GWAS summary statistics using the reference prediction models. Differential methylation Z-scores were calculated as a linear combination of SNP weights derived from the meQTL prediction models, the SNP covariance structure (LD matrix), and GWAS effect sizes. LD was estimated using the European subset of the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 reference panel. Resulting Z-scores were converted to P-values assuming a standard normal distribution and adjusted for multiple testing using a Bonferroni correction based on the total number of CpGs analysed.

Differential expression using TWAS

Differentially expressed genes associated with migraine were identified using the summary‑based transcriptome‑wide association study (TWAS) approach implemented in SMultiXcan. As with meWAS, TWAS tests for association between disease risk and genetically predicted molecular traits. Gene expression prediction models were derived from cis‑eQTLs obtained from the Genotype‑Tissue Expression (GTEx) project (v8), comprising genotype and expression data across 49 tissues (n ≥ 70 per tissue) from 838 donors, totalling 15,201 samples [9]. A multi-tissue framework was used to maximise power to detect genetically regulated gene expression effects, while accommodating shared regulatory architecture across tissues.

SMultiXcan was used to impute genetically regulated gene expression associated with migraine risk using cross‑tissue models. Gene expression was first imputed separately within each tissue using elastic net-based prediction models. A correlation matrix of predicted expression across tissues was then constructed, and principal components were used as explanatory variables, with components of minimal variance discarded to mitigate collinearity (λmax / λi < 30, where λi is an eigenvalue of the correlation matrix). Joint effects for each gene were subsequently estimated by integrating single‑tissue results with the reduced correlation matrix. SMultiXcan outputs a combined association P‑value per gene, along with the most and least informative tissues and summary statistics of tissue‑specific Z‑scores [36]. Analyses were performed across all 49 GTEx tissues.

Colocalisation analysis

Colocalisation analyses were performed using COLOC [37] to assess whether the same causal variant underlies the association between a pair of traits. COLOC applies a Bayesian framework to evaluate five competing hypotheses: no association with either trait (H0), association with trait 1 only (H1), association with trait 2 only (H2), association with both traits driven by distinct variants (H3), or association with both traits driven by a shared causal variant (H4). Prior probabilities were set to P1 = 1 × 10⁻⁴ and P2 = 1 × 10⁻⁴ for association with each trait individually, and P12 = 1 × 10⁻⁵ for a shared association, consistent with previous studies [38].

Colocalisation was conducted in three analytical settings: (i) between migraine GWAS signals and CpG meQTLs (migraine–CpG‑meQTL), (ii) between migraine GWAS signals and gene eQTLs identified by TWAS (migraine–gene‑eQTL), and (iii) between migraine‑associated CpG meQTLs and gene eQTLs (CpG‑meQTL–gene‑eQTL). For each analysis, summary statistics were extracted for cis‑acting variants within ± 1 Mb of the CpG site or gene transcription start site. Posterior probabilities were estimated for each hypothesis, and evidence for colocalisation was defined as a posterior probability for H4 (PP4) greater than 0.5, indicating support for a shared causal variant influencing both traits, consistent with previous GWAS–QTL colocalisation studies [39, 40].

Mapping CpGs to Genes

CpG sites were mapped to genes using the Illumina HumanMethylation450 array manifest file (GPL13534; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GPL13534), consistent with the methylomic profiling of the BASEL1 dataset used to generate the MetaMeth methylation prediction models. CpGs were annotated according to their genomic position relative to UCSC-defined CpG islands. CpGs located within 0–2 kb of a CpG island were classified as shores, while those located 2–4 kb from an island were classified as shelves. North (N) and south (S) designations indicate upstream and downstream locations relative to CpG islands, respectively. CpGs located outside CpG islands, shores, and shelves were classified as open sea regions [41].

Protein–protein interaction analysis

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis was performed using STRING v12.0 (https://string-db.org/) to assess enrichment and network connectivity among the prioritised genes. The analysis was conducted in Homo sapiens using default interaction sources and a minimum required interaction score of 0.4 (medium confidence). PPI enrichment statistics and functional enrichment were obtained from STRING.

Loci characterisation

MeWAS-significant CpGs that colocalised with migraine GWAS signals were assigned to migraine risk loci based on genomic proximity. CpGs were classified as mapping to an established migraine risk locus if they were located within ± 500 kb of a genome‑wide significant index SNP reported by Hautakangas et al. [7]. CpGs located more than 500 kb away from any previously reported migraine GWAS index SNPs on chromosomes 1–22 were classified as putative novel migraine risk loci according to our distance-based definition.

Results

To investigate the joint contribution of genetically regulated DNA methylation and gene expression to migraine risk, we applied an integrative multi‑omic framework that combined migraine genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics with methylome‑wide association study (meWAS), transcriptome‑wide association study (TWAS), and colocalisation analyses (Fig. 1).

Genetically regulated and differentially methylated CpGs in migraine

To identify genetically regulated CpG methylation associated with migraine risk, we conducted a methylome‑wide association study (meWAS) using MetaMeth [32]. This approach imputes DNA methylation levels under genetic regulation and tests their association with migraine using GWAS summary statistics. For each CpG, MetaMeth generates a Z-score representing the standardised association between genetically predicted methylation and migraine risk, with positive and negative values indicating increased and decreased methylation, respectively. Genetically regulated methylation was imputed for 86,518 CpGs. The resulting test statistics showed a clear deviation from the null distribution (Supplementary Fig. 1), consistent with the presence of true association signals.

A total of 258 CpGs reached genome-wide significance after Bonferroni correction (P < 5.78 × 10–7; 0.05/86,518; Supplementary Data 1). Because LD between migraine index SNPs and CpGs (meQTLs) used for methylation imputation may generate spurious associations, these CpGs were further evaluated using COLOC colocalisation analysis [37]. Of the 258 significant CpGs, 177 showed evidence of colocalisation with migraine GWAS signals (PP4 > 0.5), indicating shared causal variants (Supplementary Data 2). A Manhattan plot summarising the meWAS results is shown in Fig. 2.

Each point represents a CpG site. The red dashed line indicates the Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold (P = 5.78 × 10–7). Coloured points indicate CpGs showing evidence of colocalisation with migraine GWAS signals (n = 177 CpGs). Red points indicate colocalised CpGs located within ± 500 kb of 58 established genome‑wide significant migraine GWAS loci (n = 147 CpGs). Green points indicate colocalised CpGs located outside established migraine GWAS loci, mapping to 11 putative novel migraine risk loci (n = 30 CpGs)

Several studies have shown that including genome-wide significant SNPs in prediction models improves imputation accuracy [42]. Consistent with this, many migraine-associated CpGs identified in the meWAS were predicted using MetaMeth models that included at least one genome-wide significant migraine GWAS SNP. Indeed, of the 177 migraine-associated CpGs, 125 were predicted using models that contained ≥ 1 genome-wide significant migraine GWAS SNP (P < 5 × 10–8; Supplementary Data 3).

Among these 177 CpGs, 88 showed positive meWAS Z-scores, indicating that increased methylation (hypermethylation) was associated with increased migraine risk, while 89 showed negative Z-scores, indicating that decreased methylation (hypomethylation) was associated with increased risk. Of the 177 CpGs showing evidence of colocalisation, 147 mapped within ± 500 kb of 58 established migraine GWAS risk loci (Supplementary Data 4). For each locus, the most significant CpG (lowest meWAS P-value) is reported in Table 1. Independent GWAS index SNPs within ± 500 kb were merged and treated as a single locus, with the most significant index SNP shown.

Integration of methylation imputation with migraine GWAS data therefore enabled the identification of CpGs in genomic regions that did not reach genome-wide significance in SNP-based analyses, likely reflecting reduced multiple-testing burden and increased power arising from the aggregation of multiple variants in methylation prediction models.

Annotating CpGs to genes using Illumina annotation

Of the 177 CpGs, 59 (33.3%) were located in open sea regions, 8 (4.5%) in N shelves, 12 (6.8%) in S shelves, 27 (15.3%) in S shores, 27 (15.3%) in N shores, and 44 (24.9%) within CpG islands. Supplementary Fig. 2 shows the distribution of CpGs across these genomic contexts. Many epigenome-wide association signals for complex traits are often enriched in open sea regions [43], which tend to contain regulatory elements and tissue-specific methylation. The distribution of 177 CpGs is also consistent with the genomic distribution of probes on common methylation arrays and with previous epigenome-wide studies of complex traits, where trait-associated cis-CpGs are frequently enriched in regulatory regions outside canonical CpG islands [44].

We mapped CpGs to genes using the “UCSC_RefGene_Name” column in the Illumina manifest file, which refers to the gene name in the UCSC database. CpG-gene annotation follows a many-to-many relationship, such that a single CpG may be associated with multiple genes and a single gene may have multiple CpGs. CpGs were annotated to genes based on proximity, defined as locations within 1,500 bp upstream of a transcription start site through to the poly-A signal in the 3’ untranslated region (UTR). Of 177 CpGs that were differentially methylated and colocalised with migraine, 140 CpGs were annotated to 87 genes using this approach, while 37 CpGs did not map to any gene in the Illumina manifest file.

Genetically regulated and differentially expressed genes in migraine

We also performed a transcriptome‑wide association study (TWAS) using the SMultiXcan framework across 49 GTEx tissues. SMultiXcan estimates gene-level association P-values by accounting for the correlation of gene-eQTL effects across tissues. Migraine-associated differential gene expression was imputed in 21,646 genes. For each gene, SMultiXcan also identifies the tissue showing the strongest evidence of differential expression. The most significant gene-tissue eQTL was subsequently used for colocalisation analyses between gene-eQTLs and the migraine GWAS variants. Of the 87 genes annotated to 140 CpGs in Sect. 3.2 (using Illumina Manifest file), 82 genes showed evidence of differential expression predicted by SMultiXcan (Supplementary Data 6).

Annotating CpGs to genes using a ± 250 kb window

We next annotated CpGs to genes using a ± 250 kb window around the gene transcription start site. CpGs located within this window were assigned to the corresponding gene. This served three purposes. First, because Illumina manifest CpG-gene annotations are based solely on genomic proximity, extending the annotation window allowed incorporation of broader biological evidence of association. Second, it enabled annotation of 37 previously unassigned CpGs to genes. Third, it facilitated identification of additional genes linked to CpGs, supporting prioritisation of genes influenced by both DNA methylation and gene expression, as genomic distance alone is not always a reliable predictor of regulatory influence.

We mapped the 37 previously unassigned CpGs to 253 TWAS genes. In total, 177 CpGs were annotated to 638 genes using a ± 250 kb window (Supplementary Data 7). For the downstream analysis, we selected TWAS genes that exceeded the candidate-set Bonferroni-corrected P < 7.84 × 10–5 (0.05/638). A total of 199 genes within ± 250 kb of the migraine-associated CpGs passed this multiple-testing threshold. These 199 genes were subjected to colocalisation analysis using eQTLs from the most informative TWAS tissue and migraine GWAS summary statistics. Of these 199 genes, 91 genes, mapping to 133 CpGs, colocalised with migraine GWAS signals (Supplementary Data 8).

Colocalisation of meQTLs and eQTLs

To characterise the relationship between migraine-associated CpGs and genes, we next performed colocalisation analyses between CpG meQTLs and gene eQTLs associated with migraine. The 91 genes identified in Sect. 3.4 were annotated to 133 associated CpGs. CpG meQTLs and gene eQTLs within ± 1 Mb of CpGs and genes were extracted from BASEL data and GTEx data, respectively. We then assessed whether migraine-associated CpG-meQTLs and gene-eQTLs colocalised, providing insight into shared regulatory mechanisms. Of the 133 migraine‑associated CpGs, 120 CpGs showed evidence of colocalisation (PP4 > 0.5) with 78 differentially expressed genes (Supplementary Data 9). These CpGs and genes were not only individually associated with migraine but also showed strong evidence for a shared causal variant, consistent with coordinated genetic regulation of DNA methylation and gene expression.

To further assess functional relationships among the prioritised genes, we performed protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis using STRING. This analysis demonstrated significant network enrichment (PPI enrichment P = 6.0 × 10–5), with 27 observed interactions compared to 11 expected by chance. The resulting network was sparse (average node degree = 0.72) and comprised predominantly small connected components (Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Data 10). Analysis of interactions with at least medium confidence (combined score ≥ 0.4) identified one multi-node module centred on BCAR1, involving PARD6A, CFDP1 and TMEM170A, supported by multiple edges including a high-confidence interaction between CFDP1 and TMEM170A (combined score = 0.86). This module links proteins involved in cell polarity and signalling, suggesting local organisation of these pathways. In addition, a loosely connected component was observed among mitochondrial and redox-related proteins, centred on NDUFS3 and linking DLST, FXN, HMOX2 and NMRAL1, with the strongest interaction in this group between NDUFS3 and NMRAL1 (combined score = 0.874). These genes are involved in mitochondrial function and oxidative stress, indicating convergence on bioenergetic pathways. Several high-confidence pairwise interactions were also observed, including LATS1–YAP1 (combined score = 0.999), ATOX1–CCS (0.973), and FXN–HMOX2 (0.924), but these did not form larger clusters. These interactions represent well-characterised regulatory relationships, including signalling pathway regulation (LATS1–YAP1) and processes related to metal ion homeostasis (ATOX1–CCS) and oxidative stress (FXN–HMOX2). Other interactions were of moderate confidence and did not form additional connected components. Consistent with this, functional enrichment analysis of the STRING network highlighted over-representation of genes involved in “negative regulation of signalling” and “negative regulation of cell communication” (FDR = 0.0392).

Collectively, these findings indicate modest functional connectivity within the prioritised gene set, characterised by a small number of local modules and several strong pairwise interactions rather than a single highly interconnected network. The observed modules implicate biological processes spanning signalling organisation and mitochondrial function, supporting a model in which migraine risk is influenced by distributed regulatory effects across multiple cellular processes rather than a single dominant pathway. The full set of STRING protein–protein interaction edges is provided in Supplementary Data 10.

Migraine-associated risk loci

To determine whether these CpG–gene associations correspond to known migraine risk regions, we assessed how many of the 120 migraine-associated CpGs mapped to established versus putative novel migraine risk loci. Hautakangas et al. identified 170 independent index SNPs associated with migraine. A CpG-gene pair located within ± 500 kb of an index SNP was classified as an established locus. A CpG-gene pair located > 500 kb from a migraine index SNP was classified as a putative novel locus, as these regions did not overlap established (genome-wide significant) GWAS risk loci.

Of the 120 migraine-associated CpGs, 97 CpGs (annotated to 66 differentially expressed genes using a ± 250 kb window) mapped to 32 established migraine GWAS risk loci (Table 3, Supplementary Data 11). These 32 loci represent the subset of genome‑wide significant migraine risk loci supported by convergent evidence of both differential methylation and differential gene expression. Table 3 summarises, for each locus, the most significant CpG together with the corresponding gene(s) and direction of effects (indicated by the Z-score) from the meWAS and TWAS analyses. For example, increased methylation of cg04219099 is associated with increased expression of the MTF1 gene at the rs28739509 locus, whereas decreased methylation of cg08943375 is associated with increased expression of MEF2D at the rs2274319 locus.

Discussion

Using an integrative multi-omic framework, we identify genetically regulated DNA methylation and gene expression associated with migraine risk, implicating 78 genes across 40 independent genomic loci (32 established and 8 putative novel). Complementary PPI analysis further supports functional coherence within the prioritised gene set, demonstrating greater connectivity than expected by chance but a largely sparse network structure. Rather than forming a single highly interconnected network, the STRING analysis revealed a small number of local modules and several strong pairwise interactions. In particular, a multi-node module centred on BCAR1 links proteins involved in cell polarity and signalling organisation, while a loosely connected component centred on NDUFS3 connects genes involved in mitochondrial function and oxidative stress. Several high-confidence pairwise interactions, including LATS1–YAP1, ATOX1–CCS, and FXN–HMOX2, further highlight well-characterised regulatory relationships. Collectively, these findings indicate that the prioritised genes converge on multiple biological processes—most notably signalling organisation and cellular bioenergetics—while remaining distributed across distinct pathways, consistent with a modular and distributed regulatory architecture underlying migraine susceptibility, with implications for neuronal excitability and vascular responsiveness.

CpG- and gene-based imputation approaches provide a complementary strategy to single-variant GWAS by aggregating cis-acting effects into interpretable molecular traits. By modelling genetically regulated DNA methylation and gene expression, meWAS and TWAS increase power to detect disease associated regulatory signals while focusing specifically on heritable components of molecular variation [45]. When combined with colocalisation analyses, these approaches enable prioritisation of loci where genetic risk for migraine is consistently reflected across multiple molecular layers, strengthening confidence that observed associations reflect shared regulatory architecture rather than coincidental overlap.

In this study, we leveraged the largest available migraine GWAS summary statistics from Hautakangas et al. [7], which identified 170 independent migraine risk loci. While Hautakangas et al. also performed TWAS analyses, to our knowledge this is the first study to integrate imputation-based differential DNA methylation with gene expression to identify CpGs and refine GWAS signals to a set of genes supported by colocalised DNA methylation and gene expression associations. These genes represent loci where genetically predicted DNA methylation and gene expression signals are consistent with a shared causal variant influencing migraine risk.

A key contribution of this work is the biologically informed prioritisation of genes linked to migraine-associated CpG sites. Rather than relying solely on array-based proximity (manifest) annotations [46], we integrated TWAS and colocalisation evidence to identify genes whose genetically predicted expression converges with migraine‑associated methylation signals.

Genes were assigned to significant CpGs if located within a ± 250 kb window. We used this window because strong LD is not expected to persist over greater distances for typical cis meQTL–eQTL relationships, consistent with previous studies examining relationships between DNA methylation and gene expression [47, 48]. While this relatively broad window increased sensitivity and the number of candidate CpG–gene pairs considered, these assignments were further evaluated using colocalisation to prioritise pairs supported by shared genetic signals. This approach enabled prioritisation of genes supported by convergent evidence across multiple molecular layers, refining CpG–gene relationships beyond genomic distance alone and yielding a coherent set of candidate genes and molecular signals implicated in migraine risk.

Applying a candidate-set Bonferroni-corrected threshold, we identified 199 genes significantly associated with migraine, of which 91 showed robust evidence of association and colocalisation with CpG methylation signals. This integrative two‑step procedure enabled the identification of a biologically coherent set of CpGs and genes linked to migraine risk. Notably, some tissues that are not obviously migraine‑relevant (e.g. breast mammary tissue) emerged as the most significant tissue for certain genes. This likely reflects differences in GTEx sample sizes, shared genetic regulatory architecture across tissues, and context‑specific gene regulation, rather than direct tissue‑specific relevance to migraine.

Colocalisation analyses further clarified the relationships between migraine‑associated CpGs and genes by identifying loci where the same genetic variants jointly influence DNA methylation, gene expression, and migraine risk. At these loci, convergence of meQTL and eQTL signals supports coordinated regulatory effects observed across multiple molecular layers. Mapping these CpG–gene pairs to established and putative novel migraine risk regions highlights a subset of loci where multi‑omic evidence consistently implicates specific regulatory mechanisms, providing a refined interpretation of migraine GWAS signals at the locus level.

Of the eight putative novel risk loci, two loci (containing SNPs rs1268083 and rs1109151) overlap loci previously identified in a sex‑stratified GWAS [49]. At both loci, the top predictor SNPs for CpG methylation were in LD with the reported GWAS signals, supporting alignment between our methylation-based signals and the prior sex-stratified GWAS findings. Comparison with Bjornsdottir et al. [50], who applied variant‑impact‑weighted significance thresholds [51] and focused on rare variant identification, showed limited overlap, consistent with differences in analytical approach and locus definition. Together, these comparisons highlight how differences in variant class, analytical framework, and locus definition shape gene discovery across migraine studies.

The eight putative novel risk loci identified in this study contained 23 CpGs annotating to 12 differentially expressed genes (AFAP1, ATOX1, LINC01933, HEY2, TMEM41B, ONECUT1, RP11-209K10.2, KCTD19, LRRC36, PARD6A, SYMPK, and ST13). Collectively, these genes are associated with regulatory and metabolic processes, suggesting cellular mechanisms that may modulate neuronal and vascular responsiveness in migraine.

Actin Filament-Associated Protein 1 (AFAP1) is a protein that organises a network linking other proteins, influencing the structure and function of actin filaments. This protein plays a role in various cellular processes, including cell movement, invasion and metastasis, and is implicated in several cancers [52] and cardiovascular disorders [53]. Given established genetic links between migraine and cardiovascular disease, altered cytoskeletal and vascular regulatory processes may represent a shared biological substrate [54, 55].

ATOX1 is a copper chaperone protein that plays a crucial role in copper homeostasis and cellular function and is implicated in various neurodegenerative disorders [56]. Copper‑dependent oxidative stress and mitochondrial function have been proposed as contributors to altered neuronal excitability, providing a plausible link between ATOX1‑mediated regulation and migraine susceptibility.

LINC01933 is a long non-coding RNA that does not encode a protein and plays a role in regulating gene expression [57]. One study exploring the link between insomnia and migraine identified 30 susceptible loci associated with both traits [58]. Altered expression of LINC01933 in the brain was found to be associated with insomnia [59], suggesting that regulatory non‑coding RNAs may contribute to shared genetic architecture across sleep and migraine‑related traits, although a direct role in migraine remains to be established.

TMEM41B is a transmembrane protein associated with mitochondrial-associated endoplasmic reticulum dysfunction, a cellular interface increasingly implicated in metabolic stress responses and neurodegeneration [60]. ONECUT1 regulates multiple cellular processes, including cell cycle regulation and glucose metabolism [61]. SYMPK is involved in polyadenylation and regulating gene expression [62], while ST13 functions as a proliferation regulator and is a candidate tumor suppressor gene [63]. PARD6A (a member of the PAR6 family) is involved in asymmetrical cell division and cell polarisation processes [64]. Together, these genes point to genetically mediated variation in cellular stress handling, transcriptional regulation, and metabolic homeostasis, processes that are increasingly recognised as relevant to migraine pathophysiology.

Overall, the convergence of these putative novel loci on regulatory, metabolic, and structural cellular pathways is consistent with emerging models of migraine as a disorder of altered neuronal and vascular responsiveness shaped by cumulative regulatory variation, rather than disruption of a single dominant molecular pathway [54, 55, 65].

A limitation of this study is that the DNA methylation prediction models were derived from whole-blood meQTL data from the Basel dataset, as this was the available resource with the individual-level genotype and DNA methylation data required to construct the prediction models. Although DNA methylation patterns typically vary substantially between different tissues, germline DNA sequence variants influencing methylation are often consistent across tissues [66]. Because our analyses focus on genetically regulated methylation effects mediated by cis-meQTLs, many of the identified genetically regulated methylation effects are likely to be shared across tissues [66]. Moreover, many cis-meQTL effects are detectable across tissues, meaning that accessible peripheral tissues, such as blood leukocytes or buccal swabs, can provide useful proxies for some genetically regulated methylation effects, although they cannot capture all tissue- or cell-type-specific regulatory relationships [67]. Furthermore, colocalisation between migraine GWAS signals, methylation signals and gene expression signals provides independent support that many associations reflect shared genetic regulation, rather than arising solely from tissue-specific artefacts. Nevertheless, some methylation effects may be restricted to specific cell types, such as neuronal, glial or vascular cells, and would therefore not be detected using blood-based prediction models. Future studies leveraging larger brain- and cell-type-specific meQTL resources will help refine the tissue-specific regulatory architecture underlying migraine susceptibility. A related interpretive issue applies to the TWAS component of our analysis, which used GTEx eQTL prediction models across 49 tissues. In some cases, the strongest eQTL association was observed in a tissue without an obvious direct relevance to migraine biology (Supplementary Table 6). Because our objective was to identify shared genetic regulation of DNA methylation, gene expression and migraine susceptibility, the detected associations should not necessarily be interpreted as evidence that the corresponding reference tissue is the biological site through which risk is mediated. Rather, it should be interpreted as the dataset in which the shared genetic regulatory effect was most readily detectable.

A recent study by Liu et al. [31] integrated migraine GWAS with methylation and expression QTLs and used two‑sample Mendelian randomization (MR) to nominate CpG sites and genes with purported causal effects on migraine, followed by colocalisation analyses between the regulatory QTL signals and migraine GWAS loci. However, valid causal interpretation of MR requires that the genetic instruments influence migraine risk only through the exposure (here, methylation or expression)—that is, the exclusion‑restriction/no‑horizontal‑pleiotropy assumption. In the highly polygenic setting of migraine, where regulatory variants commonly exert pleiotropic effects across multiple molecular traits, the observation that many MR‑nominated meQTL/eQTL instruments also colocalise with migraine GWAS signals is most parsimoniously interpreted as shared genetic architecture rather than a directional methylation→migraine pathway. Importantly, under such pleiotropy, MR estimates cannot be interpreted as causal and do not, on their own, establish an independent methylation/expression–migraine association beyond the fact that the same variant (or variants in LD) is associated with both methylation/expression and migraine. By contrast, our imputation‑based meWAS and TWAS analyses directly test association between genetically predicted methylation or gene expression and migraine risk and then apply colocalisation as a robustness filter to prioritise signals supported by a shared causal variant, without invoking causal directionality that cannot be reliably justified in the presence of pleiotropy. Consistent with both analyses being anchored to migraine GWAS architecture, there is overlap at several established loci, including signals near LRP1, MEF2D, HEY2, and CFDP1. However, using our imputation‑based meWAS/TWAS framework, we identify a conservative and interpretable set of migraine‑associated regulatory signals, including loci jointly supported across DNA methylation and gene expression and additional putative novel loci prioritised through GWAS–meQTL–eQTL triangulation.

Throughout this study, associations are interpreted as evidence of shared genetic regulation rather than causal or directional relationships between DNA methylation, gene expression, and migraine risk.

Conclusions

In summary, our integrative multi‑omic analysis identified genetically driven DNA methylation and gene expression changes associated with migraine risk, implicating 40 independent genomic loci, including eight putative novel loci. By prioritising loci supported by convergent meWAS, TWAS, and colocalisation evidence, our framework emphasises robustness of association over causal directionality, yielding a conservative and interpretable set of regulatory signals within a highly polygenic and pleiotropic genetic architecture. These results highlight regulatory and metabolic processes as associated with migraine susceptibility, consistent with emerging models of migraine as a disorder shaped by cumulative regulatory variation affecting neuronal and vascular responsiveness, rather than disruption of a single dominant molecular pathway. More broadly, this work demonstrates how multi‑omic integration can translate GWAS signals into biological insight and identify putative novel candidate genes for future functional characterisation and therapeutic investigation.
