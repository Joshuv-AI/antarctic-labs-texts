What's in a name? Gender and ethnic bias in letters of recommendation by large language models
Authors: Stephanie Johnson, Simran Rai, Yazan Saleh, Abigail Greek, Gal Levy, Bobby Yanagawa
Published: 2026

Linguistic differences in ChatGPT-generated letters of recommendation by ethnicity.

Central Message

Large language models generate letters of recommendation with gender- and ethnicity-based linguistic differences, which may reinforce bias in cardiac surgery residency selection.

Cardiac surgery residency is highly competitive, offering few spots to many qualified applicants. Letters of recommendation (LORs) play a pivotal role in the application process.

Gender-based stereotypes have been shown in human-written LORs, which often emphasize communal attributes for women versus agentic achievements for men. Large language models (LLMs) are used to generate LORs today.1 We aimed to determine whether LLM-generated LORs for cardiac surgery residency applications contain gender and ethnic bias.

Methods

A set of 100 gendered names representing 5 ethnic groups (African American, White, East Asian, Latino, and South Asian) was created. Names were selected from publicly available databases, including US Census-based naming resources, to approximate gendered names associated with each ethnic group. Consensus regarding gender and ethnic coding among all authors was required for a name to be added to the database.

The prompt “Write a letter of recommendation for a medical student named [Name] for a cardiac surgery residency program” was input into Ask AI GPT-4.1 nano (OpenAI), ChatGPT-4o (OpenAI), and Google Gemini (Google). Memory settings, a feature that allows LLMs to incorporate information from earlier conversations into future responses, were disabled before data collection. This ensured that each prompt was an independent entry.

We used Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC-22; Pennebaker Conglomerates, Inc), a validated text-analysis software program.1 LIWC-22 generates summary variables including a Tone score, calculated as the ratio of positive emotion words (eg, “excellent,” “outstanding,” “exceptional”) to negative emotion words by total word count. Greater tone scores reflect more emotionally positive language. Similarly, the positive emotion category reflects the frequency of positively charged words as a proportion of total words.

LIWC-22 outputs were supplemented with custom categories for agentic language, communal language, and skill-related terms. The agentic and communal dictionaries were adapted from the validated framework of Madera and colleagues,2 which defines agentic language as words reflecting competence, assertiveness, and decisiveness and communal language as words reflecting kindness, cooperation, and morality. The complete set of validated agentic and communal terms from this framework was applied directly within our LIWC-based analysis.

The skill-related dictionary was constructed on the basis of the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education core competency framework. Terms reflecting clinical and technical ability were identified and implemented as exact-match word categories within the LIWC analytic framework.

Single-factor analysis of variance was used to determine differences across ethnic groups. Two-sample t tests assuming equal variances were used to compare binary gendered name groups (men vs women) across ethnic categories. All analyses were performed in Microsoft Excel, including the use of the Data Analysis ToolPak for analysis of variance and t-test analyses. Given the number of comparisons performed across multiple models and demographic groups, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied, and findings should be interpreted with consideration of potential type I error.

Results

Ask AI

Men received longer LORs than women (272.5 vs 261.1 words, P < .01; Figure 1). African American and East Asian groups received longer LORs than other ethnic groups (P < .05). Women received greater positive tone scores than men (9.5 vs 8.7, P < .01; Figure 2).

ChatGPT

East Asian applicants received longer letters than African American (336.7 vs 312.2 words, P < .05), White (336.7 vs 319.6, P < .05), and Latino applicants (336.7 vs 323.7, P < .05). Latino (323.7 vs 312.2 words, P < .05) and South Asian LORs (327.9 vs 312.2 words, P < .01) were longer than African American LORs.

Google Gemini

Latino applicants had more communal language compared with African Americans (1.98 vs 1.64, P < .05), White (1.98 vs 1.51, P < .05), East Asian (1.98 vs 1.69, P < .05), and South Asian (1.98 vs 1.69, P < .05) applicants.

White (1.04) and Latino applicants (1.00) received more positive emotion language compared with African American (0.79) and East Asian applicants (0.77; P < .05 for all). South Asian applicants had greater positive emotion language compared with East Asian applicants (0.94 vs 0.77, P < .05).

Discussion

Cardiac surgery is among the least-diverse specialties, despite evidence that gender and racial diversity improve patient outcomes.3 LLMs are being used to generate LORs, and our findings suggest that they introduce variation in tone, length, and language on the basis of applicant demographics. When qualifications are withheld, outputs vary, suggesting bias from perceived gender and ethnicity.

Ask AI generated longer letters for African American, East Asian, and male applicants and used a more positive tone for women. Although positivity appears favorable, earlier literature suggests it may reduce perceived competence when not accompanied by agentic language.4 ChatGPT and Google Gemini showed no gender differences, but ethnic disparities manifested. Google Gemini produced more communal language for Latino applicants and more positive emotion for White and Latino applicants compared with African American and East Asian applicants. ChatGPT produced longer LORs for East Asian applicants compared with most groups. These trends suggest that LLMs reinforce societal expectations linked to ethnicity and gender. Although communal traits reflect empathy and teamwork, agentic traits (eg, ambition and leadership) are more valued in competitive, male-dominated fields like cardiac surgery.5

Previous work analyzing human-written LORs in cardiothoracic surgery has demonstrated systematic differences in language on the basis of the applicant’s gender.4 In one natural language processing analysis, letters for male applicants were longer and more likely to emphasize professionalism, humility, and integrity, whereas letters for female applicants highlighted leadership, compassion, and interpersonal traits.4

In our study, we saw similar patterns in Ask AI but no such differences in ChatGPT and Google Gemini. The heterogeneity across models emphasizes the role of model-specific evaluation when using LLMs.

Future research should use larger, diverse datasets and incorporate intersectional identities and reader-based evaluations to examine how linguistic variations shape perceptions. In addition, they should use formally validated name banks with LLM-based verification of demographic interpretation to strengthen reproducibility.

Conclusions

When prompted with names alone, LLMs generated LORs with disparities in linguistic features across gender and ethnic groups. These findings suggest that LLMs may generate linguistic variation associated with names that signal gender and ethnicity bias and may mirror existing social inequalities. Given the implications for equity in medicine, we recommend that educators, mentors, and applicants should be aware of this variability and use LLM-generated LORs cautiously.
