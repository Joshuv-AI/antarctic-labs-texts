Background

Chemical restraint during law enforcement encounters has become an increasingly visible site of preventable morbidity and mortality in the United States. High-profile deaths, including those of Elijah McClain and others since, have drawn public attention to the clinical risks inherent in administering sedatives in uncontrolled, custodial environments. While chemical restraint is sometimes framed as a necessary intervention for severe agitation, mounting evidence demonstrates that its use in the field, particularly under police custody, differs fundamentally from sedation in hospital settings (Armour, 2020; Linder et al., 2019; Roberts et al., 2018).

In 2022, the American Medical Association, American Psychiatric Association, and American College of Emergency Physicians formally rejected “excited delirium” as a valid medical diagnosis. Despite this, the term continues to appear in emergency medical services (EMS) protocols, police training materials, and medico-legal narratives. Its persistence obscures the true mechanisms of death, often restraint-related asphyxia, medication-induced respiratory depression, or untreated medical emergencies, and disproportionately implicates Black individuals.

This manuscript synthesizes recent evidence (2022–2025) to clarify the risks of chemical restraint in custody, explain why “excited delirium” is a problematic and dangerous construct, and propose concrete protocol reforms to interrupt unsafe practices.

Why “excited delirium” is problematic

The term “excited delirium” lacks a consistent definition, diagnostic criteria, or evidence-based treatment pathway. Historically, it has been used to describe individuals, often Black men, exhibiting agitation during police encounters. Contemporary analyses demonstrate that behaviors labeled as “excited delirium” frequently reflect pain, fear, intoxication, hypoxia, or neurologic emergencies rather than a discrete psychiatric condition.

Walsh et al. (2023) and others have shown that the construct functions less as a diagnosis and more as a post hoc justification for aggressive restraint and sedation. Its use shifts attention away from iatrogenic contributors to death, including prone positioning, multiple officers applying force, and sedative overdosing. The rejection of the term by medical authorities underscores that continued reliance on “excited delirium” is not merely outdated but actively harmful.

Law enforcement influence and protocol interruptions

In hospital environments, chemical restraint is governed by layered safeguards: physician oversight, continuous monitoring, airway equipment, and rapid escalation pathways. None of these guardrails reliably exist in prehospital custodial settings. Earlier reviews of prehospital chemical sedation have long recognized that the out-of-hospital environment presents unique challenges related to patient assessment, medication dosing, airway management, and monitoring (Armour, 2020). Board-certified emergency physicians are not present on scene, and paramedics operate under standing orders designed for rare, tightly defined circumstances.

Law enforcement presence can disrupt these safeguards by exerting pressure to sedate individuals for behavioral control rather than medical necessity. When paramedics administer chemical restraint in excess of protocol or medical control recommendations, this reflects a breakdown in clinical autonomy. Officers may request or implicitly coerce sedation to facilitate restraint, transport, or custody transfer, prioritizing scene control over patient safety. These operational pressures have become increasingly reflected in national evaluations of EMS agitation protocols, which demonstrate substantial variability in indications, medication selection, dosing limits, and monitoring requirements (Mearkle et al., 2025).

Importantly, EMS protocols often lack clear interruption mechanisms when dosing thresholds are exceeded or when restraint conditions themselves create hypoxic risk. This absence contributes directly to adverse outcomes.

Clinical evidence on adverse outcomes

Multiple peer-reviewed studies demonstrate the elevated risks associated with prehospital ketamine use. Earlier work identified increased airway complications and the need for advanced airway interventions following field administration, particularly among patients with stimulant co-ingestion (Linder et al., 2019; Roberts et al., 2018). More recent investigations by Muldowney et al. (2025) and Kwong et al. (2025) report intubation rates approaching 30–40% following field administration, compared with under 5% in emergency department settings.

Comparative studies show that ketamine produces higher rates of hypersalivation, laryngospasm, and oxygen desaturation than benzodiazepines when used prehospital. Meta-analytic evidence similarly supports ketamine's rapid sedative efficacy while emphasizing its greater incidence of respiratory and airway adverse events relative to alternative agents, particularly when used outside controlled hospital settings (Roberts et al., 2018). These effects are particularly dangerous when combined with physical restraint and prone positioning, both common in police encounters.

National investigative reporting corroborates these findings. The AP/FRONTLINE Lethal Restraint project documented at least 94 sedative-associated custody deaths between 2012 and 2021, with continued cases emerging through 2025. Autopsy findings frequently cite airway compromise or sudden cardiopulmonary collapse following sedation.

Racial inequities

Racial disparities are a defining feature of chemical restraint practices. Brunson et al. (2023) demonstrated that Black patients are significantly more likely to receive ketamine for agitation and less likely to receive analgesia for pain. Studies examining the application of “excited delirium” reveal disproportionate labeling of Black men, reinforcing longstanding stereotypes of superhuman strength and dangerousness.

These patterns persist even after controlling for presenting symptoms, suggesting that implicit bias and structural racism shape clinical decision-making. Chemical restraint thus functions not only as a medical intervention but as a mechanism through which racialized social control is enacted.

Policy gaps and fragmentation

Despite growing evidence of harm, EMS protocols remain highly inconsistent across states and municipalities. National analyses demonstrate considerable variation in indications for chemical restraint, approved medications, dosing strategies, and monitoring requirements, with many protocols continuing to reference "excited delirium" despite its rejection by major medical organizations (Mearkle et al., 2025). Some jurisdictions have restricted ketamine use or removed "excited delirium" language, while others retain permissive policies with minimal oversight. Professional society statements have not been uniformly translated into operational standards.

This fragmentation allows unsafe practices to persist and complicates accountability when deaths occur.

Recommendations for updated protocols

These recommendations are consistent with recent evidence supporting standardized dosing limits, mandatory physiologic monitoring, and greater medical oversight of prehospital sedation practices (O'Brien et al., 2024; Mearkle et al., 2025).

To interrupt error and improve patient safety, updated protocols should include:

Methods: inclusion and exclusion criteria

This review included peer-reviewed articles published between 2020 and 2025 addressing prehospital sedation, ketamine use, custodial deaths, and racial disparities. Government reports, court documents, and national investigative journalism were included to contextualize clinical findings. Excluded were case reports lacking custody context and studies conducted exclusively in controlled hospital environments. This approach enhances transparency and replicability.

Discussion

The convergence of clinical risk, protocol weakness, and racial inequity makes chemical restraint in custody uniquely dangerous. Earlier evidence documenting respiratory complications associated with prehospital ketamine (Linder et al., 2019; Roberts et al., 2018) together with more recent protocol analyses and fixed-dose investigations (Mearkle et al., 2025; O'Brien et al., 2024) indicates that improving patient outcomes requires both evidence-based medication practices and stronger operational safeguards. The persistence of these patterns despite clear warnings reflects systemic failure rather than isolated error. The American Academy of Emergency Medicine (2024) Clinical Practice Statement does not formally reject the use of ketamine in the prehospital (field) setting, nor does it warn against its use in the sense of recommending that it be avoided. Instead, it concludes that ketamine is an appropriate option when used under proper clinical circumstances without careful considerations of all of the evidence to the contrary exposed in this commentary.

Conclusions

Chemical restraint in police-associated prehospital settings continues to impose unacceptable clinical and ethical risks. The persistence of racial disparities and preventable deaths underscores the urgency of reform. Eliminating “excited delirium,” strengthening protocol safeguards, and centering patient safety over custodial convenience are essential to aligning practice with evidence and equity. Without coordinated national standards, these harms will persist.
