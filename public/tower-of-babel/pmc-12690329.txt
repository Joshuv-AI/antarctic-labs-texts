Insights in efficacy of simple oral nutritional supplements after gastrectomy for gastric cancer
Authors: Guanmo Liu, Jie Li, Chenggang Zhang, Pan Li, Yihua Wang, Shihan He, Weiming Kang, Xin Ye
Published: 2025

Persistent postoperative body weight loss (BWL) remains a major barrier to functional recovery, tolerance of adjuvant therapy, and long-term outcomes after gastrectomy for gastric cancer (GC). Contemporary perioperative pathways within enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) promote the re-establishment of oral feeding as early as possible after surgery, and the updated 2025 European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) guideline on clinical nutrition in surgery recommends initiating nutrition therapy early once nutritional risk is identified (1). Within this framework, oral nutritional supplements (ONS) are attractive because they can be added to regular diets without tubes and extra staffing that often limit enteral or parenteral routes (2). However, a systematic review focused on gastrointestinal cancer found postoperative ONS compliance ranged from 26.2% to 71.1% and predominant reasons for non-compliance were symptoms of gastrointestinal discomfort and palatability issues (3). Against this background, Ueno et al. conducted a multicenter, randomized controlled trial to test a deliberately simple, highly palatable ONS strategy aimed at mitigating early postoperative BWL (4).

In previous studies, evidence on ONS after gastrectomy has been inconsistent. Miyazaki et al. indicated that in a phase-3 multicenter randomized controlled trial the administration of ONS for 12 weeks after gastrectomy did not improve BWL at 1 year (5). Early randomized trials using elemental diets or standard ONS showed short-term reductions in BWL after total gastrectomy but inconsistent effects after distal gastrectomy, with compliance varying across studies (6,7). The postoperative nutritional strategy was not solely a question of calories but of feasibility and acceptability of standard ONS, where taste, texture, viscosity, and patient education significantly shaped adherence and, by extension, clinical benefits, which might be one of the dilemmas currently faced in routine nutrition care (8-10). Furthermore, effects of ONS on handgrip strength, skeletal muscle mass, and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) are inconsistent in present studies (7,11).

The study by Ueno et al. intentionally simplified the ONS formula made as a ready-to-drink product with low viscosity and recloseable packaging, which contained 40 g of carbohydrates, 10 g of whey protein and 166 g of water, without fat in per 200-mL pack for 8 weeks after gastrectomy, alongside routine diet (4). The strategy to remove fat and limit ingredients likely reduced aftertaste and mouth-feel issues (12,13). Also in the study by Ueno et al., adherence was further promoted through bedside education, a short instructional video and brochure, in-hospital supervision until discharge, and systematic review of patient ONS diaries at follow-up. Reported intake exceeded 75% on average, and the simple ONS group experienced less BWL at 8 weeks than the control group (4).

The choice to prioritize simplicity, palatability, and routine monitoring aligned tightly with guidance in ESPEN’s 2025 update (1). The guideline recommended tailoring the texture and volume of the oral diet to the surgical procedure and the patient’s tolerance. It highlighted taste, appetite, bloating, dumping, and diarrhea as common barriers to ONS use after gastrectomy. The guideline also noted that compliance with ONS was frequently limited by sensory and gastrointestinal factors after gastrectomy rather than prescription alone, resulting to documented low intake volumes in weeks 2–4 postoperatively. In this context, an approach was operationalized in this study through a palatable, drinkable and low-burden simple ONS that could be sipped in small fractions when early satiety and reduced gastric reservoir were expected after gastrectomy, which represented the guideline-concordant strategies. Furthermore, in this study, diary-based tracking and planned outpatient review at 1 month postoperatively where adherence is most likely to slip aligned with guideline-endorsed, structured and patient-centered monitoring to further strengthen compliance and treat intolerance as early as possible.

Miyazaki et al. also demonstrated that the improvement in BWL remained until 1 year after gastrectomy in patients who took more than 200 kcal/day of ONS. Patients who achieved adequate intake tended to derive more durable benefits (5). When adherence is suboptimal and intake intolerance is present, ONS is not a panacea, which underscores the importance of palatability and structured follow-up rather than pursuing to escalate formula complexity (14). Notably, the simple ONS formula in the study by Ueno et al. was fat-free, which might limit generalisability to those who required fat supplementation or higher energy density (15). Furthermore, carbohydrate-dominant simple ONS are more likely to precipitate early or late dumping. Conversely, a modest amount of fat might slow gastric emptying and help mitigate dumping symptoms after gastrectomy.

In the study by Ueno et al., no significant between-group differences in handgrip strength were observed at 8 weeks (4). Restoration of strength typically lags behind energy intake and is modulated by activity levels and systemic inflammation. Handgrip strength indexes muscle mass and quality and serves as a prognostic biomarker in gastrointestinal oncology, but it typically changes slowly. The recent study also suggested that the trajectory of muscle loss rather than a single static measure was prognostic for outcomes, underscoring the value of tracking body-composition change over time (16). Without concomitant activity or mobilization, short-term nutrition alone seldom yields detectable functional gains within two months (17). The guidance in ESPEN’s 2025 update emphasized early mobilization to stimulate protein synthesis and muscle function (1), suggesting that during the first two postoperative months or with appropriately extended follow-up, bundled nutrition-plus-activity interventions were more likely to produce measurable functional benefits.

In addition, during the intervention period, the simple ONS group in this study reported higher symptom scores for insomnia, appetite loss, and diarrhea (4). However, events were generally mild and did not result in frequent use of hypnotics or antidiarrheals. The ESPEN guideline advised to individualize both the volume and pacing of oral intake according to tolerance (2). Clinicians may further encourage patients to sip the supplements in small aliquots, serve it at a slightly lower temperature, and avoid doses immediately before bedtime to reduce reflux and fullness. As gastric accommodation improves, the total daily volume can be distributed across the day. The guideline called for the development of new patient-centered, clinically relevant outcome parameters to evaluate the success of nutritional therapy and to capture experiential aspects of care, which aligned with the signals of insomnia and diarrhea observed in this study. Future research and quality-improvement initiatives should consider these points (18).

The magnitude and timing of the between-group difference at 8 weeks postoperatively in BWL reported by Ueno et al. warrant careful interpretation (4). Although the difference reached statistical significance, the absolute effect size was relatively modest, and the authors also prudently indicated that, within such a short observation window, its clinical relevance remained uncertain and needed further investigation. From a longer-term perspective, observational data manifest that postoperative weight trajectories prognosticate oncologic outcomes, with greater weight loss associated with higher risks of recurrence and poorer survival. Accordingly, even small early differences may be meaningful if they translate into longer-term weight maintenance. Although this study did not extend to long-term endpoints, the biological direction of effect was plausible and generated hypotheses for maintenance-phase studies.

In sum, Ueno et al.’s study advanced the nutrition field not by introducing a novel formula, but by showing that a simple and palatable ONS, backed by planned follow-up, could feasibly attenuate early BWL after gastrectomy and was in keeping with the guidance in ESPEN’s 2025 update regarding tolerance-based diet adaptation, early initiation, and structured follow-up. Directing clinical resources toward acceptability and monitoring is more likely to yield tangible nutritional benefits than pursuing increasingly complex formulations.
