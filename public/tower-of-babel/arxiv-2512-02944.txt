The Convex Matching Distance in Multiparameter Persistence
Patrizio Frosini, Ulderico Fugacci, Eloy Mosig García, Nicola Quercioli, Sara Scaramuccia, Francesca Tombari

Abstract
We introduce the convex matching distance, a novel metric for comparing functions with values in the real plane. This metric measures the maximal bottleneck distance between the persistence diagrams associated with the convex combinations of the two function components. Similarly to the traditional matching distance, the convex matching distance aggregates the information provided by two real-valued components. However, whereas the matching distance depends on two parameters, the convex matching distance depends on only one, offering improved computational efficiency. We further show that the convex matching distance can be more discriminative than the traditional matching distance in certain cases, although the two metrics are generally not comparable. Moreover, we prove that the convex matching distance is stable and characterize the coefficients of the convex combination at which it is attained. Finally, we demonstrate that this new aggregation framework benefits from the computational advantages provided by the Pareto grid, a collection of curves in the plane whose points lie in the image of the Pareto critical set associated with functions assuming values on the real plane.



Keywords

Biparameter persistent homology, Matching distance, Pareto grid.

This pseudo-metric is introduced by observing that, given a continuous function

This theorem allows one to recover, from the Pareto grid of

The proposed strategy can be reinterpreted within the framework of

Our new pseudo-metric is indeed associated with the GENEO

GENEOs have been employed in TDA to tune the invariance properties of persistence diagrams to the requirements of specific applications

Interestingly, infinitely many other examples of parametric families of GENEOs can be defined. For instance:

The approach described in this paper is related to, but distinct from, the
persistent homology transform
(PHT)
[
33
]
.
The PHT is a topological transform that takes as input a subset of a Euclidean space and associates to every unit vector the collection of persistence diagrams of the height functions on that subset in the direction given by the vector.
A distance between two subsets is then defined by integrating over the sphere the distances between the respective persistence diagrams.
The convex matching distance, instead, is obtained as a maximum over the unit vectors of non-negative slope, thus, over a smaller set.
Considering only vectors of positive slope is a crucial aspect of our techniques because, together with some regularity assumptions, it enables the use of a convenient differential geometry tool, called the
Pareto grid
.
Another key difference between the two approaches lies in their scope.
While the PHT is designed to recover the geometry of the object
X
, our method, based on convex combinations of a vector-valued function, focuses on making inferences about the function whose domain is
X
.

3
Convex matching distance

Let us consider a continuous function
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}=({\varphi}_{1},{\varphi}_{2})\colon X\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
For every
t\in[0,1]
, we set
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}:=(1-t){\varphi}_{1}+t{\varphi}_{2}
.

Definition 1
.

Considering
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and an integer
k
, we define the
convex matching distance

\cmd_{k}
between
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}
and
\boldsymbol{{\psi}}
as

\cmd_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}}):=\max_{t\in[0,1]}d_{B,k}\left(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t},\boldsymbol{{\psi}}^{t}\right)=\max_{t\in[0,1]}d_{B}\left(\dgm_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}),\dgm_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\psi}}^{t})\right).

(2)

Remark 2
.

We now show that (
2
) is well defined and that the maximum actually exists.
If the function
\boldsymbol{\varphi}=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})
is fixed, then for any
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,1]
we have

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t_{1}}-\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t_{2}}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\big\lVert(1-t_{1})\varphi_{1}+t_{1}\varphi_{2}-\big((1-t_{2})\varphi_{1}+t_{2}\varphi_{2}\big)\big\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\big\lVert\varphi_{1}-t_{1}\varphi_{1}+t_{1}\varphi_{2}-\varphi_{1}+t_{2}\varphi_{1}-t_{2}\varphi_{2}\big\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\lVert(t_{1}-t_{2})(\varphi_{2}-\varphi_{1})\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=|t_{1}-t_{2}|\,\lVert\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\rVert_{\infty}.

It follows that the map
t\mapsto\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t}
is
\lVert\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\rVert_{\infty}
-Lipschitz.
From the stability of the matching distance
[
16
]
—which implies

d_{B,k}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t_{1}},\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t_{2}})\leq\lVert\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t_{1}}-\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t_{2}}\rVert_{\infty}
and

d_{B,k}(\boldsymbol{\psi}^{t_{1}},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{t_{2}})\leq\lVert\boldsymbol{\psi}^{t_{1}}-\boldsymbol{\psi}^{t_{2}}\rVert_{\infty}
—and
by applying the
triangle inequality, we get

\displaystyle\Bigl|d_{B,k}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t_{1}},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{t_{1}}\bigr)-d_{B,k}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t_{2}},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{t_{2}}\bigr)\Bigr|\;

\displaystyle\leq d_{B,k}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t_{1}},\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t_{2}}\bigr)+d_{B,k}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\psi}^{t_{1}},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{t_{2}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t_{1}}-\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t_{2}}\rVert_{\infty}+\lVert\boldsymbol{\psi}^{t_{1}}-\boldsymbol{\psi}^{t_{2}}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq|t_{1}-t_{2}|\,\lVert\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\rVert_{\infty}+|t_{1}-t_{2}|\,\lVert\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\rVert_{\infty}.

It follows that the map

t\mapsto d_{B,k}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{t})
is continuous on
[0,1]
, as claimed.

We also observe that the map

\boldsymbol{\varphi}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t}
is
1
-Lipschitz for every
t\in[0,1]
, since

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t}-\boldsymbol{\psi}^{t}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\big\lVert(1-t)\varphi_{1}+t\varphi_{2}-\big((1-t)\psi_{1}+t\psi_{2}\big)\big\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\big\lVert(1-t)(\varphi_{1}-\psi_{1})+t(\varphi_{2}-\psi_{2})\big\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(1-t)\|\varphi_{1}-\psi_{1}\|_{\infty}+t\|\varphi_{2}-\psi_{2}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(1-t)\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{\infty}+t\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{\infty}.

Remark 3
.

If
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}
is
k
-times differentiable, with
k
possibly infinite, then so it is the function
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}
for each
t\in[0,1]
, due to being defined as the composition of
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}=({\varphi}_{1},{\varphi}_{2})
with the smooth function
(s_{1},s_{2})\mapsto(1-t)s_{1}+ts_{2}
.
This is a key difference between
\cmd
and the classical matching distance, for the operator sending
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}
to
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}_{(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})}
preserves continuity only.

Moreover, the function
t\mapsto\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}
defined on the interval
[0,1]
is smooth (i.e., infinitely differentiable) on
[0,1]
(where for the endpoints we mean that the function has continuous left-sided or right-sided derivatives of all required orders). The claim has been already proven for the order 0 in Remark
2

For the other orders, it is enough to notice that, independently from the chosen
t

-{\varphi}_{1}+{\varphi}_{2}
while, for higher orders, the derivative is
the null function.

Remark 4
.

We call
\cmd_{k}
the
convex matching distance
because it is based on the convex combination of the components of
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}
and
\boldsymbol{{\psi}}
.
This idea generalises to the case of functions with values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
: given the function
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}=({\varphi}_{1},\ldots,{\varphi}_{n})
, we consider the map assigning to each vector
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
t_{i}\geq 0

\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}=1
the function
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}{\varphi}_{i}
.

Proposition 1
.

\cmd_{k}
is a pseudo-metric.

Proof.

It can be easily seen that
\cmd_{k}
is a non-negative function, for which the symmetry property and the fact that, for any
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}
,
\cmd_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})=0
are directly inherited from the bottleneck distance. Thus, to complete the proof, it remains to show that the triangle inequality holds.
Since
d_{B,k}
is a pseudo-metric, for any
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
we have that

\displaystyle\cmd_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}})

\displaystyle=\max_{t\in[0,1]}d_{B,k}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t},\boldsymbol{{\psi}}^{t})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{t\in[0,1]}(d_{B,k}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{t})+d_{B,k}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{t},\boldsymbol{{\psi}}^{t}))

\displaystyle\leq\max_{t\in[0,1]}d_{B,k}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{t})+\max_{t\in[0,1]}d_{B,k}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{t},\boldsymbol{{\psi}}^{t})

\displaystyle=\cmd_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{\xi})+\cmd_{k}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{{\psi}}).

∎

Remark 5
.

We claim that in general
\cmd_{k}
is not a metric. Since there exist two real-valued functions

such that
f\neq g
and
d_{B}(\dgm_{k}(f),\dgm_{k}(g))=0
, we can consider
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}=(f,f)
and
\boldsymbol{{\psi}}=(g,g)
. Thus,
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}=f
and
\boldsymbol{{\psi}}^{t}=g
for any

. Then we obtain that
\cmd_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}})=d_{B}(\dgm_{k}(f),\dgm_{k}(g))=0
, but

.

Proposition 2

(Stability of
\cmd_{k}
)
.

For any

,
\cmd_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}})\leq\|\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}-\boldsymbol{{\psi}}\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

For any

, by the stability of the bottleneck distance

d_{B}\left(\dgm_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}),\dgm_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\psi}}^{t})\right)\leq\|\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}-\boldsymbol{{\psi}}^{t}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}-\boldsymbol{{\psi}}\|_{\infty}.

∎

Comparison with the matching distance

In this section, we compare the newly introduced convex matching distance with the classical matching distance
[

]
.

In Example

Vice versa, Example

provides functions for which the convex matching distance is strictly smaller than the classical matching distance.

Example 1
.

Let us consider the circumference

of

. Then, let us consider the circle

(see Figure

The sets

cited in the Examples
1
,

.

We set
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}(x,y,z):=(x,z)
for

\boldsymbol{\psi}(x,y,z):=(x,z)

Given that

Let us now compute the following persistence diagrams in degree

However, the convex matching distance is not always greater than the classical matching distance, as shown by the following example.

Example 2
.

If we consider degree
0

In fact, it is easy to verify that

Remark 6

The above examples can be suitably modified to satisfy more restrictive conditions on the smoothness of the involved functions or on their domains, taken to be manifolds.
This shows that their existence is not due to a lack of regularity properties but rather follows from the very definition of the two distances.
By identifying the boundaries of two disjoint copies of

thus obtaining two smooth functions

\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\colon\mathbb{S}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

;

•

\cmd_{0}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}})=0<\frac{1}{2}\leq d_{\mathrm{match},0}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}})
.

5
Pareto grid and Position Theorem

Let
M
be a closed (i.e., compact and without boundary) smooth Riemannian manifold of dimension
r\geq 2
and
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}=({\varphi}_{1},{\varphi}_{2})\colon M\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
a smooth function.

Definition 2
.

1.

The
Jacobi set
,
\mathbb{J}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
, is the set of all points
x\in M
at which the gradients of
{\varphi}_{1}
and
{\varphi}_{2}
are linearly dependent, namely

x\in\mathbb{J}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
if and only if there exists
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\neq(0,0)
such that
\lambda_{1}\nabla{\varphi}_{1}(x)+\lambda_{2}\nabla{\varphi}_{2}(x)=0
.

2.

The
Pareto critical set
of
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}
,
\mathbb{J}_{P}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
, is the set of all points
x\in\mathbb{J}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
such that there exists

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\neq(0,0)
with
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\geq 0
such that
\lambda_{1}\nabla{\varphi}_{1}(x)+\lambda_{2}\nabla{\varphi}_{2}(x)=0
.
The elements of
\mathbb{J}_{P}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
are called
Pareto critical points
.
Note that
\mathbb{J}_{P}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
contains both the critical points of
{\varphi}_{1}
and those of
{\varphi}_{2}
.

Unless otherwise specified, throughout the rest of the article the smooth functions considered will satisfy the following conditions.

Assumption 1
.

1.

No point
x
exists in
M
at which both
\nabla{\varphi}_{1}(x)
and
\nabla{\varphi}_{2}(x)
vanish.

2.

\mathbb{J}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
is a 1-dimensional manifold smoothly embedded in
M
, consisting of finitely many components, each diffeomorphic to a circle.

3.

\mathbb{J}_{P}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
is a 1-dimensional closed submanifold of
\mathbb{J}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
with boundary.

4.

The connected components of
\mathbb{J}_{P}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})\setminus\mathbb{J}_{C}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
are finite in number, each being diffeomorphic to an interval,
where

\mathbb{J}_{C}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
is
the set of cusp points of
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}
, i.e.,
the set of points of
\mathbb{J}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
at which the restriction of
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}
to
\mathbb{J}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
fails to be an immersion. In other words,
\mathbb{J}_{C}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
is the subset of
\mathbb{J}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
where both
\nabla{\varphi}_{1}
and
\nabla{\varphi}_{2}
are orthogonal to
\mathbb{J}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
.
With respect to any parameterisation of each component, one of
{\varphi}_{1}
and
{\varphi}_{2}
is strictly increasing and the other is strictly decreasing. Each component may meet critical points of
{\varphi}_{1}
or
{\varphi}_{2}
only at its endpoints.

We call the closures of the images of the connected components of
\mathbb{J}_{P}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})\setminus\mathbb{J}_{C}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
(proper)
contours

[
17
]
, and denote by
\mathrm{Ctr}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
the set of contours of
{\varphi}
.
Given two smooth functions
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}}\colon M\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we set

\mathrm{Ctr}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}}):=\mathrm{Ctr}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})\cup\mathrm{Ctr}(\boldsymbol{{\psi}})
.
It is important to observe that Assumption
1
is generic within the class of smooth functions from
M
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

[
34
]
.

In our setting, we make one further technical assumption.

Assumption 2
.

1.

The set
\mathbb{J}_{C}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
of cusp points is empty.

2.

Each contour
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathrm{Ctr}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
is a smooth regular curve in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,1];{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
.

Definition 3
.

The
Pareto grid
of
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}
is the subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
defined as the image
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}(\mathbb{J}_{P}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}))
.

Figure
2
shows the Pareto grid of the projection of a 2-sphere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

Figure 2
:
The Pareto grid of the function
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
defined by setting
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}(x,y,z):=(x,z)
.

Before presenting the main result of this section, we recall the following well-established theorem, which provides a useful relation between the persistence diagram of a smooth function and its critical values.
This statement is a technical but standard fact in the literature, and we include it here without proof
(cf., e.g., Theorem 3.1 in
[
32
]
, and
[
27
]
).

Theorem 1
.

Let
M
be a closed smooth manifold and
{\varphi}\colon M\to{\mathbb{R}}
a smooth function.
If
w
is a finite coordinate of a point
{p}\in\dgm_{k}({\varphi})\setminus\{\Delta\}
, then
w
is a critical value for
{\varphi}
.

We are now ready to state the main result of this section.
Relying on

Theorem

1
,
it provides a geometric characterisation of the points in the persistence diagram of
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}
in terms of the
contours associated with
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}
.
Next theorem is analogous to
[
17
, Theorem 2]
.
In what follows, the symbol
\cdot
denotes the dot product.

Theorem 2

(Position Theorem for
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}
)
.

Let
t\in[0,1]
and let

w
be a finite coordinate of a point in
\dgm_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t})\setminus\{\Delta\}
.
Then there exist a
contour
denoted as
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a
\bar{\tau}\in[0,1]
such that:

1.

\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\bar{\tau})\cdot(1-t,t)=w
;

2.

\dfrac{d\boldsymbol{\alpha}}{d\tau}(\bar{\tau})\cdot(1-t,t)=0.

Proof.

By Theorem
1
we know that
w
must be a critical value for
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}
.
Therefore, there exists a point
\bar{x}\in M
such that
w=\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}(\bar{x})
and
\nabla\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}(\bar{x})=\boldsymbol{0}
, where
\boldsymbol{0}
denotes the null vector.
Hence,
(1-t)\nabla{\varphi}_{1}(\bar{x})+t\nabla{\varphi}_{2}(\bar{x})=\boldsymbol{0}
.
This means that
\bar{x}
is a Pareto critical point of
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}
.
Therefore, there exist a
contour

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}):[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a
\bar{\tau}\in[0,1]
such that
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}(\bar{x})=\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\bar{\tau})
, and

w=\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}(\bar{x})=(1-t){\varphi}_{1}(\bar{x})+t{\varphi}_{2}(\bar{x})=\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}(\bar{x})\cdot(1-t,t)=\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\bar{\tau})\cdot(1-t,t).

Consider a smooth regular curve
\boldsymbol{\gamma}:[0,1]\to M
such that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\tau)=\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}\circ\boldsymbol{\gamma}(\tau)
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(\bar{\tau})=\bar{x}
,
which implies that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\bar{\tau})=(\alpha_{1}(\bar{\tau}),\alpha_{2}(\bar{\tau}))=({\varphi}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(\bar{\tau})),{\varphi}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(\bar{\tau}))=({\varphi}_{1}(\bar{x}),{\varphi}_{2}(\bar{x}))
.
By Assumption
2
,
\mathbb{J}_{C}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}})
is empty, hence
\frac{d}{d\tau}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}\circ\boldsymbol{\gamma}(\tau))
does not vanish for any
\tau\in[0,1]
.
Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\begin{split}0&=\boldsymbol{0}\cdot\frac{d\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{d\tau}(\bar{\tau})\\
&=\nabla\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}(\bar{p})\cdot\frac{d\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{d\tau}(\bar{\tau})\\
&=\left((1-t)\nabla{\varphi}_{1}(\bar{x})+t\nabla{\varphi}_{2}(\bar{x}))\right)\cdot\frac{d\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{d\tau}(\bar{\tau})\\
&=(1-t)\nabla{\varphi}_{1}(\bar{x})\cdot\frac{d\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{d\tau}(\bar{\tau})+t\nabla{\varphi}_{2}(\bar{x})\cdot\frac{d\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{d\tau}(\bar{\tau})\\
&=(1-t)\frac{d{\varphi}_{1}\circ\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{d\tau}(\bar{\tau})+t\frac{d{\varphi}_{2}\circ\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{d\tau}(\bar{\tau})\\
&=(1-t)\frac{d\alpha_{1}}{d\tau}(\bar{\tau})+t\frac{d\alpha_{2}}{d\tau}(\bar{\tau})\\
&=\frac{d\boldsymbol{\alpha}}{d\tau}(\bar{\tau})\cdot(1-t,t).\end{split}

(3)

∎

It may be useful here to observe how the Position Theorem
2
can be used to find the finite coordinates of the points in

\dgm_{k}({\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}}^{t})\setminus\{\Delta\}
.
The idea is to consider the family of parallel lines having the direction of the vector
(1-t,t)
and to look for the points where these lines intersect the contours associated with the function

\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}\colon M\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
orthogonally.
For each such point
P
, one computes the scalar product
P\cdot(1-t,t)
.
As
P
varies, one thus obtains all the finite coordinates of the points in

\dgm_{k}({\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}}^{t})\setminus\{\Delta\}
.

We observe that when the convex matching distance is zero, every

Theorem 3
.

If
\cmd_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}})>0
and it is realised at
\bar{t}

\cmd_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}})=d_{B,k}\left(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{\bar{t}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}}^{\bar{t}}\right),

\bar{t}\in\mathrm{Sp}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}})

Proof.

By contradiction, suppose
\bar{t}\not\in\Sp(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}})

\cmd_{k}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}})=d_{B,k}\left(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{\bar{t}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}}^{\bar{t}}\right)

By Remark
1

Moreover, by Theorem

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\tau_{\alpha})\cdot(1-\bar{t},\bar{t})=w_{0},

where with a slight abuse of notation we denote with

some smooth parametrisations of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
such that

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
at

By Definition

Moreover, note that

7
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced the convex matching distance, a new pseudo-metric
to compare functions with values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
If
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}
and
\boldsymbol{{\psi}}
are two such functions, the convex matching distance is
defined by maximising the bottleneck distance between the persistence diagrams of the convex combinations
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}
and
\boldsymbol{{\psi}}^{t}
of the coordinates of
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}
and
\boldsymbol{{\psi}}
.
Whereas the classical matching distance requires the computation of a two-parameter family of persistence diagrams (see
[
16
,
2
,
25
]
), our metric depends only on the one-parameter family indexed by
t\in[0,1]
.
This reduction in dimensionality, combined with the additional regularity of the operator sending
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}
to
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}^{t}
, leads to both conceptual simplifications and computational advantages improving the effective computability of the metric.
Moreover, the smoothness preservation property of the proposed operator
allows for its incorporation into learning-based frameworks, ensuring stable and well-behaved gradients and enabling the use of standard optimization and training procedures.

Under generic regularity assumptions on the input functions,
we show in Theorem
3
that the convex matching distance is realised at some parameter in a distinguished subset of
[0,1]
, that we called special set.
We conjecture that this subset is finite under generic conditions on the input pair
(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{{\psi}})
.
It is not hard to find examples of pairs of functions having an infinite special set.
For example, this happens when
\mathrm{Ctr}(\boldsymbol{{\varphi}},\boldsymbol{\psi})
contains two contours such that one is the translated of the other, or when a contour has zero curvature.
However, in our opinion, these phenomena occur when the pair of functions is not generic, as the functions can be infinitesimally modified locally, so not to have this properties any more.

Future work will focus on the effective computation of the parameters belonging to this special set as well as on the identification of the proper genericity assumptions under which such a set is finite.
Moreover, we aim to weaken the assumptions under which we proved Theorem
3
and to prove their genericity.

Finally, as mentioned in the introduction the operator that given a parameter
t
maps each function
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
to the function
\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t}
is just one of the infinitely many possibilities for assigning a parametric family of functions to
\boldsymbol{{\varphi}}
. In the near future, we intend to investigate alternative operators and evaluate the advantages and drawbacks associated with their adoption.
