Selective radial thickness growth of compositionally graded shells on colloidal quantum rods for more efficient light-emitting diodes
Authors: Yicheng Zeng, Xiaonan Liu, Duanyang Liu, Yuan Liu, Qingya Wang, Weiwei Chen, Jing Wei, Jian Xu, Fangze Liu, Hongbo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColloidal quantum nanorods (NRs) exhibit linearly polarized emission and fast radiative recombination owing to their two-dimensional confinement, enabling high-performance light-emitting diodes (LEDs). However, due to facet-dependent growth kinetics, anisotropic shell growth often leads to preferential overgrowth along the long axis and insufficient radial (short axis) coverage. This structural imbalance weakens exciton confinement within the core and compromises the suppression of Förster resonance energy transfer. Here, we report a dual-ligand (organic phosphorus/carboxylic acids) slow-injection strategy to synthesize wurtzite CdSe/CdZnSe/ZnSeS core/shell NRs featuring compositionally graded thick shell ( ≈ 4.5 nm) with ZnSe as the dominant component. The organic phosphorus ligands create an environment of high monomer concentration (cadmium monomer concentration from 1.4% to 2% of cadmium by mass) to drive anisotropic growth, while the carboxylic acids promote isotropic growth due to their nearly equal binding energy across different crystal facets. Simultaneously epitaxial growth of compositionally graded alloy shells alleviates lattice mismatch at core/shell interfaces while reducing defect density and boosting carrier recombination efficiency. The resulting NR-LEDs achieve an external quantum efficiency of 32% for anisotropic nanocrystals. This work establishes graded thick-shell NRs as a generalizable platform for efficient, stable and polarized optoelectronics.Subject terms: Quantum dots, Inorganic LEDs

Introduction

Colloidal quantum dots (QDs) are promising emitters for light-emitting diodes (LEDs) owning to their spectrally narrow emission, broad color tunability across the visible spectrum, and excellent solution processability, which offers significant advantages for large-area film fabrication1,2. Compared with isotropic QDs, one-dimensional nanorods (NRs) exhibit additional advantages, including linearly polarized emission along the long axis and faster radiative recombination rates owing to their two-dimensional quantum confined structure3–5, which can substantially enhance both the external quantum efficiency (EQE) and luminance of nanocrystal-based LEDs6. However, the enlarged surface area of NRs increases the probability of surface trapping and potentially faster Auger recombination, resulting in a lower photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) of NRs compared to isotropic QDs7,8. Additionally, aggregation and energy transfer losses of the emitting cores during the solution-to-film transformation further degrade device performance.

To translate solution-processable NRs into a functional solid-state film, the growth of a protective shell is typically required. This core/shell structure effectively passivates surface defects on the luminescent core, suppresses aggregation and Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) between cores, and enables efficient charge confinement. The epitaxial shell growth via seeded-growth approach has been widely studied. However, achieving isotropic shell growth around NRs remains a major synthetic challenge due to the pronounced facet-dependent growth kinetics7,9. Conventional core/shell NRs synthesized using rapid-injection methods with organic phosphorus ligands typically exhibit highly anisotropic shell growth due to the high binding affinity to crystal facets perpendicular to the long axis10,11. This preferential binding creates excessive elongation along the long axis and insufficient short axis coverage11,12. The resulting high-aspect-ratio, thin shell ( ≈ 0.9 nm) NRs suffer from disordered stacking and aggregation during spin-coating, reducing photon out-coupling efficiency while increasing leakage currents and FRET (Fig. 1a)13,14.

Previous studies have found that growing thick shell on QDs can significantly enhance the performance of QD-LEDs15–17. In parallel, recent reports have shown that for NR systems, increasing the radial shell thickness can reduce their aspect ratio, further improve film uniformity, and effectively suppress leakage current14. Therefore, NRs featuring thick shell and low aspect ratio are beneficial for improving film uniformity and light out-coupling efficiency, as well as suppressing leakage current and FRET (Fig. 1a). Controlled shell growth via slow-injection method has been demonstrated to effectively increase radial shell thickness and reduce aspect ratio18. Furthermore, precursor reactivity modulation has been shown to regulate the aspect ratio, with higher-reactivity precursors favoring the formation of lower-aspect-ratio NRs19. Despite these advances, achieving uniform thick-shell growth while maintaining a low aspect ratio remains unresolved in the field of NRs.

Besides controlling the uniform thick-shell growth, regulating the composition and structure of the shell is equally critical for reducing interfacial lattice mismatch and enhancing carrier confinement, thereby improving the performance of NR-based optoelectronic devices. In colloidal QDs, gradient alloy shells have been demonstrated to effectively alleviate core/shell interface strain, suppress Auger recombination, and optimize carrier injection balance20–22. However, extending such gradient structures to NRs is more difficult. Compared to isotropic QDs, anisotropic NRs exhibit more pronounced interfacial strain during shell growth due to the strong facet-dependent reactivity, severely limiting the choice of shell materials and complicating control over composition and structure23,24. Consequently, CdSe/CdS core/shell NRs have long dominated NR material systems owing to their facile synthesis and high stability12,25,26. However, its small conduction band offset (0.2 eV) promotes electron delocalization27, reduces radiative recombination efficiency and prolongs radiative lifetime, while the deep valence band of CdS creates barriers to hole injection, resulting in unbalanced carrier injection that has persistently limited the efficiency and brightness of NR-LEDs28.

To overcome these limitations, continuously graded heterostructures incorporating alloyed interlayers and a wide-bandgap barrier shell have been proposed. For example, CdZnSe interlayers combined with ZnSeS outer shells have been applied in CdSe QDs to smooth the confinement potential and reduce interfacial strain (Fig. 1b)21,29. In addition, ZnSe provides a shallower valence band to reduce hole-injection barriers, while ZnS suppresses excessive electron injection, thereby improving the carrier injection balance (Fig. 1b)28,30,31. However, realizing such complex gradient shells in anisotropic NRs remains highly challenging due to facet-dependent reaction kinetics and strain accumulation during growth. In addition, the wurtzite phase of ZnSe is metastable at room temperature, and ZnSe nanocrystals typically adopt the thermodynamically stable zinc-blend structure32,33. When ZnSe is grown on CdSe NR seeds, this phase mismatch often disrupts the original rod morphology. Although several methods have been reported for the synthesis of ZnSe NRs34,35, they are not suitable for growing anisotropic wurtzite ZnSe shells on wurtzite CdSe NR seeds. Therefore, ZnSe shells reported to date predominantly grow isotropically31,36,37.

Here, we report a dual-ligand (organic phosphorus/carboxylic acids) slow-injection strategy to achieve compositionally graded, thick-shell growth with wurtzite ZnSe as the dominant component. The organic phosphorus ligands provide an environment of high monomer concentration (as monitored by the Cd monomer concentration ranging from 1.4% to 2% of cadmium by mass) that promotes anisotropic shell growth, whereas the carboxylic acid ligands exhibit equivalent binding energies across different facets, facilitating isotropic growth. This increases the radial thickness of the NRs and alleviates excessive growth of the long axis and insufficient coverage of the short axis caused by the facet-dependent kinetic disparities. The resulting core/shell NRs exhibit 98% PLQY and a linear polarization ratio of 0.67. They self-assemble into a dense, near-monolayer film with parallel substrate alignment, yielding an in-plane transition dipole moment orientation distribution of 85%, minimizing current leakage and FRET. When integrated into NR-LEDs, the devices exhibit an EQE of 32.0% and a peak luminance of 1.1 × 106 cd m-2, as well as an outstanding T95 operation lifetime (time for the luminance to decrease to 95% of its initial value) exceeding 6.8 × 104 h at a luminance of 1000 cd m-2.

Results

Synthesis of CdSe/CdZnSe/ZnSeS core/shell NRs

Based on a fast-injection protocol38, we synthesized monodisperse wurtzite CdSe NRs with uniform size (length = 10.9 ± 0.1 nm; diameter = 3.2 ± 0.1 nm) (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). The accelerated nucleation and growth kinetics were achieved by utilizing a highly reactive Se precursor coordinated with a secondary phosphine (diphenylphosphine), which exhibits higher reactivity compared to the conventional tertiary phosphine (trioctylphosphine)38,39, thereby promoting faster nucleation and growth rates of the CdSe NRs (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). These NRs serve as seeds for subsequent shell growth. To extend the radial dimensions, we employed a high-temperature, slow-injection reaction process, which allowed precise deposition of multiple epitaxial shells (Fig. 1c). A critical innovation in our approach is the use of a dual-ligand system comprising organic phosphorus and carboxylic acids. The former can provide an environment of high monomer concentration to drive anisotropic growth40,41, while the latter can promote isotropic growth due to their similar binding energies on different crystal facets42. To validate this mechanism, we used first-principles density functional theory (DFT) simulations and found that oleic acid exhibits slightly different binding energy to the (001) and (100) facets of CdSe NRs compared to the hexylphosphonic acid (see calculation in Supplementary Note 1), corresponding to the long axis and short axis, respectively (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Tables 1–4). We further experimentally verified that, when only cadmium oleate precursors are used without organic phosphorus-cadmium precursors, shell growth becomes completely isotropic, resulting in core/shell NRs with morphology close to that of spherical QDs (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Direct epitaxial growth of ZnSe shells on wurtzite CdSe NRs is inherently difficult due to the metastable nature of wurtzite ZnSe at room temperature32,33. Attempts at direct deposition often result in lattice mismatch, strain accumulation, and the formation of stacking faults. To circumvent this challenge, we induced a CdZnSe transition alloy shell. The rationale rests on the reactivity differences of Cd and Zn. Specifically, Cd precursors possess higher activity than Zn, allowing initial shell growth to remain Cd-rich and conformal to the CdSe seed surface. As the reaction progressed, Cd was gradually consumed, and Zn became incorporated, guiding the formation of ZnSe epitaxial shell while maintaining wurtzite phase (Fig. 1c). The gradual transition of chemical composition also effectively reduced lattice mismatch20,29.

On the wurtzite CdZnSe alloy shell, subsequent ZnSeS shell growth preferentially adopts the wurtzite structure under high-temperature and slow-injection conditions, ultimately yielding CdSe/CdZnSe/ZnSeS core/shell NRs with a thick and compositionally gradient shell. These NRs reached a length of ≈ 26.2 nm and a diameter of ≈ 12.1 nm, representing a substantial increase in the radial thickness (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 4). We used first-principles-based DFT simulations to calculate the (100) and (001) interfacial energies of CdSe/ZnSe core/shell (see calculation in Supplementary Note 2). Theoretical calculations confirmed that wurtzite ZnSe epitaxial shell preferentially grows along the short-axis (100) facet of wurtzite CdSe NRs, consistent with the increased radial thickness (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 5). Structural analysis further approved these findings. As the gradient shell grows, X-ray diffraction peaks gradually shifted toward wurtzite ZnSe signatures, with the final core/shell NRs maintaining the wurtzite structure (Fig. 1f).

Systematic statistical results of high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) from 50 randomly selected CdSe/CdZnSe/ZnSeS core/shell NRs shows that about 96% of the core/shell NRs exhibit continuous and intact lattice fringes without observable stacking faults (Supplementary Figs. 6–8). This indicates that our optimized synthesis method enables high-quality epitaxial growth of gradient shells on the majority of NRs, effectively suppressing the formation of stacking faults. Elemental mapping and line-scan profiles confirmed the compositional gradient, showing a Cd/Se-rich core transitioning smoothly into CdZnSe/ZnSeS alloy and ultimately Zn/S-rich outer shell (Fig. 1g, Supplementary Figs. 9, 10a). The results showed a significant contrast with the EDS line scan results of the non-gradient CdSe/CdS/ZnS core/shell NRs (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Indeed, atomically resolved aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images provided atomically resolved evidence of the gradual compositional transition (Supplementary Fig. 11). We further calculated the lattice mismatch values of CdSe/ZnSe, CdSe/ZnS, and ZnSe/ZnS core/shell interfaces in the wurtzite phase (Supplementary Tables 6, 7). The results indicate that the transition alloy shell, mainly composed of wurtzite ZnSe, is beneficial for reducing the lattice mismatch between the CdSe core and the ZnS shell, thereby reducing the defect formation.

Optical performance characterization of core/shell NRs

Absorption and PL spectra highlight the impact of the gradient alloy shells (Fig. 2a). Compared with bare CdSe NRs (PLQY = 6%), core/shell NRs displayed significantly enhanced absorption below 500 nm and a PLQY 98%, demonstrating effective passivation of surface defects and strong confinement of charge carriers in the core29,31 (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Figs. 12, 13). The gradient alloy shells modified the band-edge excitonic states, relieved strain-induced defects31,43, and narrowed the PL full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) from ≈ 34 nm for the cores to ≈ 25 nm for the core/shell NRs (Supplementary Fig. 13). Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements revealed that bare CdSe cores display triexponential decay (τav = 11.8 ns), where the presence of defects accelerates nonradiative recombination pathways (Fig. 2b). In contrast, TRPL of core/shell NRs approached a single exponential decay (τ = 14.9 ns), indicating that the multiple shells suppress nonradiative recombination.

To elucidate how the gradient alloy shells enhance the optical properties of the CdSe core, we systematically examined exciton dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 14). First, the biexciton lifetime was extracted from exciton bleach dynamics under varying pump intensities (1.81-905 μJ cm-2, Fig. 2c inset) using transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy. The fitted biexciton lifetime of the core/shell NRs reached 1246 ps (Fig. 2c), significantly exceeding previously reported values of other NRs (142-714 ps)31,38, demonstrating that the gradient alloy shells strongly suppress Auger recombination44,45. Second, temperature-dependent PL characterization yielded an exciton binding energy of 125.8 meV, higher than previously reported core/shell NRs (Supplementary Table 8). This indicates that the type-I band alignment of the continuously graded shells (CdZnSe/ZnSeS) incorporating alloyed interlayers and a wide-bandgap outer barrier provides stronger exciton confinement than shells dominated by CdS (Supplementary Fig. 15). Finally, the PL blinking of the single-NR was significantly suppressed (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Figs. 16, 17). We attribute this primarily to the CdZnSe/ZnSeS high-quality gradient alloy shell formed through a slow, controlled growth, which effectively passivates interfacial and surface defects, suppressing carrier trapping by defect states. This is consistent with the widely accepted mechanisms for blinking suppression in state-of-the-art core/shell QDs by constructing uniform and highly crystalline shells43,46. Together, these results emphasize the crucial role of graded alloy shells in suppressing nonradiative processes and stabilizing excitonic emission.

The growth of the thick shell increases the distance between the emitting cores16,25, while the type-I band alignment of the core/shell provides strong exciton confinement17,31. These two effects together suppress FRET in the NR films. PL spectra revealed a 6.1 nm red-shift for core NRs in film relative to solution, whereas core/shell NRs exhibit merely a 0.7 nm red-shift (Supplementary Fig. 18a). Correspondingly, the energy transfer efficiency drops from 54.2% (core NRs) to 12.1% (core/shell NRs) (Supplementary Fig. 18b, Supplementary Tables 9–11). PLQY measurements showed a 67% reduction in core NR film (2%) compared to solution (6%), whereas core/shell NRs only decreased by 3.1% (from 98% in solution to 95% in film) (Supplementary Fig. 18c).

The anisotropic optical properties were characterized by single-NR PL spectroscopy. Polarization-dependent PL intensity fits well to a cos(2θ) function (Fig. 2e), representing linearly polarized transition-dipole moment. Across 50 single core/shell NRs, the average linear polarization ratio (p) is 0.67 ± 0.02 (Supplementary Fig. 19). In colloidal dispersions, the anisotropy parameter r = 0.24 (Supplementary Fig. 20). Increasing the radial shell thickness of CdSe NRs enhances exciton recombination efficiency and suppresses energy transfer, but reduces aspect ratio and consequently decreases polarization anisotropy, in agreement with ref. 7. Nevertheless, compared with polarized QDs with thick shells, the core/shell NRs retain significantly higher polarization47, underscoring their potential for polarized electroluminescent devices.

Device performance characterizations

To directly compare the effects of thick-shell versus thin-shell structures on the emissive layer of NR-LEDs, CdSe/CdS core/shell NRs were synthesized with PL characteristics (PL peak position = 622 nm and PL FWHM = 26 nm) matching those of thick-shell NRs (Supplementary Fig. 21). These CdSe/CdS NRs exhibited uniform size distribution (length ≈ 41.8 nm and diameter ≈ 5.1 nm) with an aspect ratio of ≈ 8 (Supplementary Figs. 22, 23). Using identical spin-coating conditions, thick-shell NRs achieved uniform, compact films with parallel alignment to the substrate without random aggregation (Fig. 3a–c). In contrast, thin-shell NRs tended to form disordered films with reduced film density and substantial voids.

NR-LEDs based on high PLQY core/shell NRs with graded-composition thick shells were fabricated with a structure of indium tin oxide (ITO)/poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)-poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS)/poly(9,9-dioctylfluorene-alt-N-(4-sec-butylphenyl)-diphenylamine) (TFB)/NRs/ZnMgO/Al (Fig. 3d). Cross-sectional TEM image of the device reveals clear interfaces and precise thickness control across all functional layers (Supplementary Fig. 24). EDS mapping confirms quasi-monolayer NR assembly with in-plane alignment (Fig. 3e), consistent with the SEM images (Fig. 3c). HR-TEM and selected-area electron diffraction patterns further reveals the high crystallinity of core/shell NRs (Fig. 3e). The high-quality core/shell NR films exhibit exceptional uniformity in PL intensity mapping (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 25), which are superior to NR films reported in refs. 25,37. Notably, the NR film exhibits an average in-plane transition dipole moment orientation ratio of ≈ 85% (Fig. 3g, Supplementary Fig. 26), surpassing QDs with a gradient alloy shell (79%)47. These results indicate that our strategy of intentionally reducing the aspect ratio of NRs has improved film uniformity and packing order, while still preserving the intrinsic anisotropic emission of NRs. This directly contributes to the improved device efficiency in display and lighting applications6,48.

To further compare the impact of thick-shell versus thin-shell structures, CdSe/CdS NR-LEDs were fabricated under identical conditions. Thin-shell NRs produced rough films with significant leakage current, whereas thick-shell NRs formed densely packed, uniform films that effectively suppressed leakage current (Fig. 3h). Meanwhile, the gradient alloy thick-shell structure enabled balanced charge injection and enhanced carrier recombination, resulting in substantially higher efficiency and luminance compared to thin CdS-shell NRs (Supplementary Fig. 27). Additionally, device heating was greatly reduced. While the CdSe/CdS-based devices exhibit a temperature rise of > 7 °C as the voltage increases from 2 to 7 V, CdSe/CdZnSe/ZnSeS-based devices show only a ≈ 2 °C rise (Fig. 3i, Supplementary Fig. 28).

The NR-LEDs with multi-layer gradient alloy shell exhibit spectrally pure red emission with a dominant electroluminescence (EL) peak at 625 nm, narrow FWHM of 25 nm, and International Commission on Illumination (CIE) chromaticity coordinates of (0.663, 0.327) (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 29). Notably, the emission wavelength shows exceptional stability under operating voltages ranging from 3-10 V, with negligible color drift (Δx = 0.012, Δy = 0.007) (Fig. 4a), confirming excellent color stability attributed to the superior charge confinement provided by the gradient alloy shells.

The core/shell NR-LEDs have a low turn-on voltage of ≈ 1.6 V (Fig. 4b), matching the performance of state-of-the-art red Cd-based QD-LEDs47,49. The valence-band maximum and conduction-band minimum values of core/shell NRs measured by ultraviolet photon spectroscopy were ≈ 6.19 eV and ≈ 4.25 eV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 30). The ZnSe-rich intermediate shell, with a higher valence-band maximum ( ≈ 0.2 eV above CdS), reduces the hole-injection barrier, while the ZnS outer shell elevates the electron injection barrier, promoting balanced charge injection30,50. Benefiting from balanced carrier injection, enhanced recombination efficiency, and uniform dense films, the core/shell NR-LEDs achieved a maximum EQE of ≈ 32.0% and a maximum current efficiency of ≈ 50.0 cd A-1 under a lower bias voltage of ≈ 2.5 V, as well as a maximum brightness of ≈ 1,102,742 cd m-2 (Fig. 4b, c, Supplementary Fig. 31). Device reproducibility was demonstrated by fabricating 10 NR-LEDs, and all devices have a peak EQE higher than 29.5%, with an average EQE as high as 31.3% (Supplementary Fig. 32). Notably, the EQE remains above 25.0% across a wide current density range (1–1700 mA cm-2) and brightness range (300–560,000 cd m-2), confirming that radiative recombination dominates under carrier injection (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 33). These results surpass all previously reported NR-LEDs and isotropic QD-LEDs (Fig. 4d, e, Supplementary Table 12), establishing a solid foundation for high-performance NR-LEDs.

The operational stability was characterized by a T₉₅ lifetime of 377 h under continuous operation at 18,548 cd m-2 (Fig. 4f). The lifetime data at varying luminance were fitted with the empirical equation L0n×T95=constant (where L0 is the initial luminance)51, yielding an acceleration factor n ≈ 1.78 (Supplementary Fig. 34). Model extrapolation predicts T₉₅ lifetimes of ≈ 68,219 h at 1000 cd m-2, which is about 49 times higher than the state-of-the-art NR-LEDs benchmark reported (T₉₅ ≈ 1,400 h at 1000 cd m-2)13, and also higher than QD-LEDs (Fig. 4g). Beyond static operational lifetime, dynamic cycling stability under varying driving voltage or current is a critical performance metric for NR-LEDs, particularly for applications requiring frequent on-off switching such as commercial displays52. The core/shell NR-LEDs were systematically monitored over 2000 voltage scans (0-4 V bias range). After cycling, the devices showed only 11.5% luminance loss and 3.8% EQE reduction (Fig. 5a, b), demonstrating superior dynamic stability compared to reported ZnCdSe/ZnSe QD-LEDs, ZnCdSe/ZnS QD-LEDs, and CdSe/CdZnS/ZnS NR-LEDs13,28.

The CdSe/CdZnSe/ZnSeS NR-LEDs demonstrate exceptional operational lifetime and dynamic cycling stability through their graded thick shell. As illustrated in Fig. 5c, the gradient alloy shell minimizes lattice mismatch and defect formation while reducing carrier trapping. The CdZnSe/ZnSeS type-I potential barrier effectively confines excitons, balances carrier injection, and suppresses leakage, while increased radial shell thickness improves film uniformity and reduces current leakage. Voltage-capacitance measurements reveal reduced capacitance and increased breakdown voltage, confirming mitigated interface aging from charge accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 35). In the designed core/shell system, the gradient ZnSe-to-ZnS alloy shells provides a smooth energy level transition, while the interface barrier between the electron transport layer and the NRs remains relatively small, enabling efficient electron injection. Furthermore, the quasi-monolayer NR structure shortens the carrier transport path and reduces trap-assisted accumulation53. EL data further demonstrates suppressed electron leakage into HTL (Fig. 5d, e), consistent with the reduced leakage current in J-V characteristics (Fig. 3h). Thermal imaging at a constant current density of 50 mA cm-2 demonstrate the superior stability of thick-shell devices, exhibiting only 0.7 °C temperature rise after 100 h compared to the 7.8 °C increase in thin-shell counterparts within 10 h (Fig. 5f). Compared with a CdS shell, the CdZnSe/ZnSeS shell with ZnSe as the main component reduced the hole-injection barrier by ≈ 0.41 eV25, thereby improving the balance of carrier injection. Fitting the J-V characteristics below the turn-on voltage using a single exponential function reveals a significantly stronger suppression of leakage current in thick-shell NR-LEDs compared to thin-shell NR-LEDs (Supplementary Fig. 36a). Concurrently, the thick-shell devices show much lower heating power54 at the same luminance level than the thin-shell devices (Supplementary Fig. 36b), which mainly originates from the higher radiative recombination current in the thick-shell devices. In summary, the lower leakage current and higher radiative recombination efficiency in thick-shell NR-LEDs directly lead to reduced heating power, which explains their ability to achieve both high brightness and high efficiency simultaneously.

To further explore the generality of anisotropic growth control in various nanocrystal systems using the organic phosphorus/carboxylic acids dual-ligand approach, we synthesized pure wurtzite ZnSe NRs without any templates (Supplementary Fig. 37a, b). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 37c, the resulting NRs exhibit anisotropic morphology with wurtzite crystal structure, demonstrating that this dual-ligand strategy provides an effective route for achieving uniform anisotropic growth of ZnSe. Importantly, we also achieved epitaxial growth of wurtzite ZnSe shells on CdSe NRs in the complete absence of Cd precursors (Supplementary Fig. 38), confirming that Cd precursors are not essential for wurtzite ZnSe shell formation. Furthermore, employing blue-emitting CdSeS cores, we performed shell growth (CdZnS) under the same dual-ligand conditions. The obtained core/shell NRs display excellent dispersion and uniform size distribution (Supplementary Fig. 39), along with bright blue emission while maintaining the wurtzite crystal structure (Supplementary Fig. 40). These collective results substantiate the general applicability of our dual-ligand strategy across different material systems, and we are currently extending this approach to additional material platforms for further validation.

Discussion

We developed a robust synthetic strategy for CdSe/CdZnSe/ZnSeS core/shell NRs featuring thick compositionally gradient shells with well-controlled dimensions (length: 26.2 ± 3.8 nm, diameter: 12.1 ± 2.1 nm, aspect ratio ≈ 2.2). By combining the dual-ligand strategy with a slow-injection approach, we achieved precise epitaxial growth of wurtzite ZnSe-dominant shells while overcoming facet-dependent growth-rate limitation, thus preserving the rod-like morphology. The graded thick shell not only alleviates lattice strain at core/shell interfaces but also enhances excitonic radiative recombination. The NRs exhibit 98% PLQY and a linear polarization ratio of 0.67. Owing to their optimized aspect ratio and thick shells, these core/shell NRs self-assemble into dense, uniform quasi-monolayer films with 85% in-plane transition dipole moment orientation, effectively suppressing leakage current and FRET. The resulting NR-LEDs exhibit state-of-the-art performance with a 32.0% EQE, a peak luminance of 1,102,742 cd m-2, and outstanding stability, retaining 95% initial brightness over 7.8 years at a brightness of 1000 cd m-2. Beyond the specific CdSe/CdZnSe/ZnSeS system demonstrated here, the dual-ligand slow-injection strategy provides a generally applicable framework for tuning nanocrystal dimensions, aspect ratios and graded shell architectures. We anticipate this strategy can be readily extended to blue- and green-emitting NRs as well as nanoplatelets, providing a route to address the challenges of efficiency, brightness, and operation lifetime across the visible spectrum.

Methods

Materials

Cadmium oxide (CdO, 99.99%), trioctylphosphine oxide (TOPO, 99%), trioctylphosphine (TOP, 97%), selenium (Se, 99,99%), sulfur (S, 99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Oleic acid (OA, 90%), oleylamine (OAM, 70%) and 1-octadecene (ODE, 90%) were purchased from Alfa Aesar. Hexylphosphonic acid (HPA, 98%), octadecylphosphonic acid (ODPA, 98%), diphenylphosphine (DPP, 95%), and zinc acetate (Zn(ac)2, 99.99%) were purchased from Aladdin. All reagents were used without further purification.

Preparation of precursors

Zn(OA)2 stock solution (0.5 M). Zn(ac)2 (3.6696 g), ODE (20 mL), and OA (20 mL) were loaded into a 100 mL flask and dried for 1 h under vacuum at 120 °C. Then, the temperature was increased to 160 °C under N2 atmosphere until the Zn(ac)2 was completely dissolved.

Cd(OA)2 stock solution (0.5 M). CdO (1.2842 g), ODE (10 mL), and OA (10 mL) were loaded into a 50 mL flask and dried for 1 h under vacuum at 120 °C. Then, the temperature was increased to 240 °C under N2 atmosphere until the CdO was completely dissolved.

Cd(ODPA/HPA) stock solution. CdO (0.18 g), TOPO (6.00 g), HPA (0.332 g), and ODPA (0.666 g) were loaded into a 50 mL flask and dried for 2 h under vacuum at 150 °C. Then, the temperature was increased to 320 °C under N2 atmosphere until the CdO was completely dissolved.

Se-TOP stock solution (2 M). Se powder (1.5792 g) was dissolved in 10 mL of TOP.

S-TOP stock solution (2 M). S powder (0.6413 g) was dissolved in 10 mL of TOP.

Synthesis of CdSe NRs

The CdSe NRs were synthesized following a modified version of a protocol reported in ref. 38. Briefly, TOPO (3.00 g), HPA (0.166 g), ODPA (0.333 g), and CdO (0.09 g) were placed in a three-necked round-bottom flask and degassed at 150 °C for 2 h. The mixture’s temperature was 280 °C under N2 atmosphere, after which a Se precursor solution (0.04 g of Se powder dissolved in 0.5 mL of TOP and 0.1 mL of DPP) was injected quickly, and the solution was stirred for 4 min at 280 °C. After the synthesis, the NRs are precipitated with methanol, washed twice with hexane, reprecipitated with methanol, and finally dissolved in TOP.

Synthesis of CdSe/CdZnSe/ZnSeS NRs

To grow CdZnSe shells around the cores, the purified CdSe NRs in TOP, Cd(ODPA/HPA) precursor solution and 5 mL of ODE were mixed in a 100 mL round-bottom flask. After quickly increasing the temperature to 300 °C, 2 mL Zn(OA)2 was added to the reaction mixture and a precursor solution consisting of 2.5 mL Cd(OA)2, 2.5 mL Se-TOP, and 5 mL ODE was continuously fed into the reaction at 300 °C at a rate of 6 mL h-1. To prevent solidification of the precursors, 2 mL Zn(OA)2 was separately added into the reactor every 20 min. To coat these particles with ZnSeS shells, 2 mL Zn(OA)2 was added to the reaction mixture, and a precursor solution consisting of 1mL S-TOP, 1mL Se-TOP, and 2 mL ODE was continuously fed into the reaction at 310 °C at a rate of 4 mL h-1. To prevent solidification of the precursors, 3 mL Zn(OA)2 was separately added into the reactor every 30 min. The reaction was terminated by rapid cooling to room temperature. The fabricated core/shell NRs were purified three times by a precipitation/redispersion procedure (methanol/hexane) and finally dispersed in hexane.

Synthesis of CdSe/CdZnSe/ZnSeS NRs (without Cd(ODPA/HPA))

To grow CdZnSe shells, the purified CdSe NRs in TOP and 5 mL of ODE were mixed in a 100 mL round-bottom flask. After quickly increasing the temperature to 300 °C, 2 mL Zn(OA)2 was added to the reaction mixture and a precursor solution consisting of 2.5 mL Cd(OA)2, 2.5 mL Se-TOP and 5 mL ODE was continuously fed into the reaction at 300 °C at a rate of 6 mL h-1. To prevent solidification of the precursors, 2 mL Zn(OA)2 was separately added into the reactor every 20 min. To grow ZnSeS shells, 2 mL Zn(OA)2 was added to the reaction mixture and a precursor solution consisting of 1 mL S-TOP, 1 mL Se-TOP and 2 mL ODE was continuously fed into the reaction at 310 °C at a rate of 4 mL h-1. To prevent solidification of the precursors, 3 mL Zn(OA)2 was separately added into the reactor every 30 min. The reaction was terminated by rapid cooling to room temperature. The fabricated core/shell NRs were purified three times by a precipitation/redispersion procedure (methanol/hexane) and finally dispersed in hexane.

Synthesis of ZnSe NRs

TOPO (3.00 g), HPA (0.166 g), ODPA (0.333 g), OA (1 mL), and Zn(ac)2 (0.092 g) were placed in a three-necked round-bottom flask and degassed at 150 °C for 2 h. The mixture’s temperature was heated to 320 °C under N2 atmosphere, waited for the solution to clarify, and then lowered the temperature to 260 °C. Then quickly inject Se precursor solution (0.04 g of Se powder dissolved in 0.5 mL of TOP and 0.1 mL of DPP). After the synthesis, the NRs are precipitated with methanol, washed twice with hexane, and again precipitated with methanol, and finally dissolved in hexane.

Synthesis of CdSe/ZnSe core/shell NRs

To grow ZnSe shells around the cores, the purified CdSe NRs in TOP, Zn(ac)2, ODPA, HPA, and 5mL of ODE were mixed in a 100 mL round-bottom flask and degassed at 100 °C for 30 min. After quickly increasing the temperature to 300 °C, 2 mL Zn(OA)2 was added to the reaction mixture, and a precursor solution consisting of 2.5 mL Se-TOP and 5 mL ODE was continuously fed into the reaction at 300 °C at a rate of 6 mL h-1. To prevent solidification of the precursors, 2 mL Zn(OA)2 was separately added into the reactor every 20 min. The fabricated core/shell NRs were purified three times by a precipitation/redispersion procedure (methanol/hexane) and finally dispersed in hexane.

Synthesis of CdSeS/CdZnS core/shell NRs

CdO (0.0152 g), ODPA (0.082 g), TOPO (3.00 g), HPA (0.022 g) and OA (1 mL) degassed at 150 °C for for 2 h with vigorous stirring. Then, the suspension was heated to 370 °C under N2 atmosphere, after which it became a transparent colorless solution. At this point, 1.8 mL of TOP is injected in the flask and the temperature is allowed to recover to 370 °C for the injection of the solution of S precursor and CdSeS seeds. Such a solution was prepared by dissolving S powder (0.023 g) in TOP (1.6 mL) with vigorous stirring, followed by the addition of 0.4 mL of CdSeS seed solution. The resulting solution is quickly injected in the flask. Subsequently, Zn-OA (3 mL) was injected at 9 mL h-1 right after the addition of the S solution. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 25 min, and then cooled down to room temperature. After the synthesis, the NRs are precipitated with methanol, washed by repeated re-dissolution in hexane and precipitation with the addition of methanol, and finally dissolved in hexane.

Synthesis of ZnMgO nanoparticles

ZnMgO nanoparticles were synthesized according to ref. 55. An ethanol solution (10 mL) of tetramethylammonium hydroxide (5 mmol) was added to a dimethyl sulfoxide solution of zinc acetate hydrate (2.55 mmol) and magnesium acetate hydrate (0.45 mmol) with an injection rate of 40 mL h-1 at 50 °C. The solution was stirred for 1 h at 50 °C. The ZnMgO nanoparticles were precipitated from solution by adding ethyl acetate, then dispersed in ethanol for device fabrication.

Fabrication of core/shell NR-LEDs devices

The ITO-coated glass substrates were thoroughly cleaned sequentially with deionized water, acetone and isopropanol for 30 min each, and were then treated with O3-plasma for 8 min. Subsequently, a PEDOT:PSS layer was spin-coated onto ITO substrates at 4000 rpm for 40 s, followed by annealing at 150 °C for 15 min. Then, the coated substrates were transferred to a N2-filled glove box. The TFB (8 mg mL-1 in chlorobenzene) layer was spin-coated on top of PEDOT:PSS at 3000 rpm and then baked at 150 °C for 30 min. The core/shell NRs (in octane, 25 mg mL-1) and ZnMgO nanoparticles (in ethanol, 30 mg mL-1) were prepared by spin-coating at 3000 and 2000 rpm for 30 s, respectively, and baked at 60 °C for 30 min. Next, the aluminum anode was deposited via thermal evaporation at a rate of ≈ 0.1 nm s-1 under a vacuum of 4 × 10−6 Torr. Finally, the devices were encapsulated by bonding the glass cover sheet to the devices using a UV-curable resin.

Characterization

UV-vis absorption spectra were measured on a UV 2310-Ⅱ spectrophotometer. The PL spectra and PLQY were collected on a spectrofluorometer (Edinburgh FS5) with an integrating sphere. The fluorescence lifetimes were recorded on a TPL-200 spectrometer. The temperature-dependent PL spectra were measured on an Edinburgh Instruments FLS 1000. TEM and HR-TEM images were acquired on a JEM-2100 electron microscope with an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. HAADF-STEM images and elemental maps were taken on a FEI Titan3 G2 60-300 TEM. X-ray diffraction (XRD) characterizations were obtained by a D8 Focus X-ray diffractometer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted on an ULVAC-PHI QUANTERA-Ⅱ SXM spectrometer. The SEM images were recorded on a Nano430, FEI microscope at a voltage of 10 kV and a test current of 10 µA. Tapping-mode AFM measurement was carried out on an AFM (Bruker Dimension Icon). Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) spectra were collected on Kratos Axis Supra, with a He (I) ultraviolet radiation source (21.22 eV). The 2D PL mapping was acquired by a home-built laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with a 405-laser diode and a galvo scanner. The back focal plane (BFP) was measured by an oil immersion objective (Nikon Plan Apo 100×/NA1.3) using a 405 nm laser diode. The BFP of the objective was directly imaged using a CCD camera (QHY5III174) and two conjugate lenses. The cross-sectional TEM image of the NR-LEDs was characterized by a dual-beam focused ion beam, which was performed with a scanning electron microscope and Omniprobe AutoProbe 200.2 robot hand. TA measurements were performed using a regenerative amplified Ti:sapphire laser system (Coherent; 800 nm, 70 fs, 6 mJ per pulse, and 1 kHz repetition rate) as the laser source and a Femto-100 spectrometer (Time-Tech LLC) as the spectrometer. Single-NR PL intensity traces were measured using a home-built confocal fluorescence microscope system. NR samples were diluted to have a low concentration such that the NR density was below 0.01 μm-2 when the sample was drop-cast onto a glass cover slip. A pulsed laser (405 nm wavelength, 30 mW laser power) was used for optical excitation, which was focused onto the sample using a 50 × objective lens (NA 0.95). The emitted PL signal was collected using an HSPC-L high-speed single photon counter for 10 min. The J-V characteristics of the NR-LEDs were analyzed using an Agilent 4155 C semiconductor parameter analyzer with a calibrated Newport silicon diode under ambient conditions. The luminance was calibrated using a Photo Research spectroradiometer (PR735). EL spectra were obtained with an Ocean Optics spectrometer (USB2000, relative irradiance mode) and a Keithley 2400 source meter. The half-lifetime of NR-LEDs was measured using ZJZCL-1 aging system.

Calculation of binding energy

First-principles calculations based on DFT were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)56. The exchange-correlation interactions were described using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional57. A plane-wave basis set with a kinetic energy cutoff of 400 eV was employed. Structural relaxations were performed until the total energy and forces converged to within 1 × 10-5 eV and 1 × 10-2 eV Å-1, respectively.

Calculation of surface energy

First-principles calculations based on DFT were performed using the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method with the PBE exchange-correlation functional, as implemented in the VASP56–58. A plane-wave kinetic energy cutoff of 500 eV was employed. The electronic energy and atomic forces were converged to less than 1 × 10-6 eV and 1 × 10-2 eV Å-1, respectively.

Temperature-dependent PL analysis

The temperature dependent PL spectra were measured on an Edinburgh Instruments FLS 1000. The temperature dependence of energy gap can be fitted by an empirical Varshni relation.

Polarized PL of core/shell NRs

Polarized PL was measured using the same system as the 2D PL mapping. The p was quantified using p = (Imax − Imin)/(Imax + Imin), where Imax and Imin represent the PL intensities measured parallel and perpendicular to the NR’s longitudinal axis, respectively. The r was determined using r = (I∥ – I⊥) / (I∥ + 2I⊥), where I∥ and I⊥ are the PL intensity with linear polarization direction parallel and perpendicular to that of the excitation light.
