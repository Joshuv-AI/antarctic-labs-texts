Associations of inflammation-related hematological profile with the early-stages of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome and the mediating role of body composition: evidence from the China National Health Survey
Authors: Lunhui Huang, Binbin Lin, Yueyi Mu, Yansong Ren, Qiang Li, Yong Li, Yueshen Ma, Yulong Fan, Guoqing Zhu, Zhen Song, Yonghui Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic low-grade inflammation is increasingly recognized as a pivotal driver in the development of early-stage cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome. Nonetheless, the complex interplay among inflammation-associated hematological indices, body composition, and CKM risk remains inadequately understood.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, data from 5,692 participants of the China National Health Survey (CNHS) were analyzed. We employed advanced machine learning techniques—including Random Forest, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator​(LASSO) regression, and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)—in conjunction with traditional epidemiological methods to assess the predictive value of an inflammation-related hematological profile for early-stage CKM. Restricted cubic spline regression was used to explore nonlinear dose–response relationships, and generalized structural equation modeling investigated the mediating role of body composition in the inflammation–CKM pathway.ResultsSix biomarkers—Neutrophil-to-HDL ratio (NHR), Monocyte-to-HDL ratio (MHR), High-sensitivity C-reactive protein/albumin ratio (CAR), High-fluorescence reticulocyte fraction (HFR), Reticulocyte production index (RPI), and Reticulocyte count (RET#)—were consistently prioritized across models. Participants in the highest quartiles of NHR, MHR, and RET# exhibited markedly elevated odds of early-stages of CKM (OR = 10.4, 7.75, and 6.99, respectively; all P for trend < 0.0001). Nonlinear analyses revealed critical thresholds—specifically, NHR > 7.05 and MHR > 0.66—beyond which early-stages of CKM risk escalated steeply. Mediation analyses indicated that imbalances in body composition, particularly increased adiposity and reduced muscle mass, accounted for 20–57% of the association between systemic inflammation and early-stage of CKM syndrome. Subgroup analyses further underscored that the predictive impact of reticulocyte parameters was amplified in smokers and individuals aged < 60 years.ConclusionThis study validates NHR and MHR as robust, clinically actionable biomarkers for early CKM screening. The delineated nonlinear thresholds and the mediating effects of body composition provide a strategic framework for targeted interventions—prioritizing anti-inflammatory treatments in high-risk groups (e.g., smokers with RET# >143.03 × 10³/µL) and muscle-preserving therapies to mitigate sarcopenic adiposity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-02062-3.Keywords: Inflammation-related hematological profile, Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, Machine learning, Body composition mediation, Threshold effects

Background

The global prevalence of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome has been rising rapidly, [1, 2] particularly in its early-stages, which represent a continuum from a healthy state to obesity and the subsequent development of metabolic risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, metabolic syndrome (MetS), or moderate-to-high-risk chronic kidney disease (CKD) [3–5]. This trend is largely driven by sedentary behavior, unhealthy diet, and obesity—alongside rising health awareness and routine monitoring, highlighting the need for simple inflammation-based biomarkers to capture early metabolic and vascular disturbances [6, 7] The pathophysiological process of CKM syndrome is driven by a complex interplay of interrelated mechanisms, including excessive adipose tissue accumulation, insulin resistance, renal dysfunction, dyslipidemia, atherosclerosis, and chronic low-grade inflammation, which collectively contribute to a heterogeneous and multifaceted clinical phenotype [3, 8–10]. Emerging evidence has indicated that survivors of COVID-19 may experience prolonged metabolic and inflammatory disturbances, which could predispose them to cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) abnormalities even after recovery [11]. As a result, early diagnosis and precise risk stratification remain major challenges, directly influencing the timeliness and effectiveness of clinical interventions.

Chronic low-grade inflammation, a persistent state of immune activation, has emerged as a central mechanism underlying MetS, type 2 diabetes, CKD, and cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [12–14]. Established inflammatory biomarkers include high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6) and novel hematological indices such as the neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein ratio (NHR) and monocyte-to-high-density lipoprotein ratio (MHR) [15–17]. Notably, a comprehensive panel of prognostic parameters which integrates peripheral blood cell derivatives (including platelets, lymphocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, and red blood cells) with metabolic components such as albumin and High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol​(HDL-C), provides a more holistic reflection of the host’s immune-inflammatory status [18–23]. These markers are widely utilized by researchers worldwide, both as standalone indicators and as components of broader diagnostic panels [24–29]. A deeper understanding of their clinical utility is critical for refining early CKM syndrome risk assessment strategies.

An increasing body of evidence has consistently linked inflammation to body composition, although the direction of these associations remains unclear. In two large cohort studies, taller individuals and those with lower waist-to-hip ratios were negatively associated with C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, suggesting that greater height may attenuate the pro-inflammatory effects of increased waist circumference (WC) or hip circumference in women [30]. Additionally, a Copenhagen study on sarcopenia found that elevated levels of hsCRP were weakly associated with reduced muscle mass but positively correlated with visceral adiposity and diminished physical function [31]. Conversely, research by Saima et al. demonstrated inconsistent relationships between baseline inflammatory biomarkers and changes in body composition, underscoring the potential importance of body composition markers as predictors of inflammation risk [32].

In recent years, rapid advancements in machine learning (ML) have provided powerful tools for deciphering complex, high-dimensional interactions among inflammation, metabolism, and CVD, particularly when leveraging routine blood and biochemical test data [33–35]. Methods such as random forest(RF), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator​(LASSO), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) have been widely applied in biomarker selection and risk stratification, effectively capturing nonlinear patterns, optimizing feature selection, and enhancing the reliability of individualized risk assessment [36–38]. Furthermore, the integration of SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) has enhanced the interpretability of ML-based predictive models, [39, 40] facilitating more precise risk prediction for early-stage CKM syndrome. In this primarily exploratory study, we adopt an integrative approach that combines advanced ML techniques with traditional epidemiological methods to refine early risk assessment for CKM syndrome.

Methods

Study design and population

The current study was based on the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CNHS), which is an ongoing nationally representative investigation. Detailed information regarding the CNHS design and sampling methodology was previously reported by He et al. [41]. In brief, a multistage stratified cluster sampling method was employed to recruit residents aged ≥ 20 years from urban and rural areas. Standardized protocols were used for data collection, encompassing demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, personal medical history, physical examinations, and laboratory assessments. Participants with serious mental or physical conditions, women who were pregnant or breastfeeding, and individuals on active military duty were excluded. Our study utilized data collected from Tianjin, one of the CHNS survey sites, during the period from June 24 to August 1, 2024. The inclusion criteria for this analysis were as follows: (1) participants aged between 20 and 80 years and (2) of Han ethnicity. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a diagnosed of CVD (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke) or an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73 m² calculated by Chinese-based Eq. [42]; and (2) missing values for key variables, including the inflammation-related hematological profile and CKM-related diagnostic variables.

Variable definitions and measurements

The inflammation-related hematological profile

In this study, the term inflammation-related hematological profile refers to a set of composite indices derived from routine hematology and biochemical measurements that reflect chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, immune-cell activity, and erythropoietic stress. Initially, a total of 77 candidate indices were considered, with their definitions and calculation formulas provided in Table S1. Following variable selection using machine learning models, six indices were retained for further analysis: NHR, MHR, hsCRP-albumin ratio (CAR), reticulocyte count (RET#), red cell distribution width-platelet ratio (RPI), and high fluorescence reticulocytes percentage (HFR). Blood samples were collected in the morning after a minimum 8-hour fast. High-sensitivity CRP levels were measured using an immunoturbidimetric assay on a Roche Cobas c702 analyzer, while albumin concentrations were determined via a colorimetric assay. The remaining indices were obtained from the Sysmex XN-B3 hematology analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Japan) as part of the complete blood count (CBC).

Body composition

Trained personnel collected the anthropometric and body composition data using standardized protocols and calibrated instruments. Height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a SECA stadiometer (SECA, Germany) with participants standing barefoot. Body weight and composition were then assessed using a Tanita MC780MA analyzer (Tanita Inc., Japan) via bioelectrical impedance analysis, with the participants wearing light clothing and no shoes. Weight was recorded to the nearest 0.1 kg. Muscle-fat ratio (MFR) was calculated by dividing the total fat mass by the estimated muscle mass in corresponding body regions, reflecting the ratio of fat to muscle. Muscle mass index (MMI) was derived from total muscle mass adjusted for height or body surface area, reflecting overall muscle distribution and quantity. Fat mass index (FMI) reflects the distribution and density of body fat and is calculated as the total fat mass divided by height squared. Visceral fat index (VFI) represents visceral fat content, which is important for evaluating cardiometabolic risk related to central adiposity.

Definition of CKM syndrome across stage 0–2

Blood pressure (BP) was measured three times using a clinically validated automated electronic sphygmomanometer (OMRON, HEM-907). Systolic and diastolic BP were recorded at one-minute intervals, and the mean of the three readings was used for analysis. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Overweight/obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 23 kg/m², based on the Asian-specific cutoff. Hypertension was defined as a mean systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg, a mean diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg, a self-reported physician diagnosis of hypertension, or current use of antihypertensive medication. Diabetes was defined as a fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, a self-reported physician diagnosis, or current use of antidiabetic medication. Prediabetes was defined as an FPG of 6.1–7.0.1.0 mmol/L in participants without a prior diagnosis of diabetes and not taking antidiabetic medication. Hypertriglyceridaemia was define as a serum triglyceride level ≥ 2.3 mmol/L. Metabolic syndrome was defined according to the ATPIII guidelines as meeting at least two of the following criteria [43]: (1) serum triglyceride level ≥ 1.7 mmol/L or current use of lipid-lowering medication; (2) HDL-C level < 1.04 mmol/L in men and < 1.29 mmol/L in women; (3) DBP ≥ 85 mmHg or SBP ≥ 130 mmHg or current use of antihypertensive medications; and (4) FPG ≥ 5.6 mmol/L or current use of antihyperglycemic medications.

Following previous studies, [3] CKM syndrome was classified into three stages (0–2) in our study, as detailed in Table S2. Stages 0 and 1 were combined and defined as the pre-early CKM stage, whereas stage 2 was defined as the early CKM stage. Specifically: Early CKM stage (stage 2): presence of any of the following: diabetes, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, metabolic syndrome, or an eGFR of 30–60 mL/min/1.73 m². Pre-early CKM stage (stages 0–1): includes individuals with overweight/obesity or prediabetes (stage 1) or those without CKM risk factors (stage 0), but who do not meet criteria for stage 2.

Covariate variables

Sociodemographic variables included age (continuous), sex (male/female), residence (urban/rural), education (≤ middle school or ≥ high school), and annual income (< 30,000 or ≥ 30,000 RMB). Lifestyle factors covered smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity. Participants were classified as smokers or nonsmokers and drinkers or nondrinkers. Physical inactivity was defined as exercising less than 1–2 times per week in the past year.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median (IQR), while categorical data were summarized as counts (percentages). Group differences were analyzed using Student’s t-tests, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, or χ² tests, depending on the data distribution. To assess associations, multivariable logistic regression models were applied to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Given the strong correlations among the 77 inflammatory parameters (Figure S1), we applied three independent machine learning approaches—LASSO regression, RF, and XGBoost—for feature selection. These methods are robust to multicollinearity and can effectively identify the most informative predictors [44]. Specifically, RF excels at capturing non-linear relationships and interactions, LASSO performs sparse linear feature selection while controlling for multicollinearity, and XGBoost implements high-performance gradient boosting with regularization. To mitigate overfitting, training used repeated 10-fold cross-validation (5 repeats) with grid search for hyperparameter tuning, early-stopping for XGBoost, λ selection by cv.glmnet for LASSO, and a 70%/30% train/test split (seed = 123). Final model performance was assessed on the hold-out test set and optimism-corrected AUCs were estimated using 2000 bootstrap iterations. Detailed hyperparameter grids and final parameter values are listed in Table S3. Early-stage CKM syndrome was treated as a categorical outcome.

Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic curves and the area under the curve (AUC). Feature importance in the RF was examined using the random forest::importance function, which provided a ranked list of the top 10 variables and the relationship between the number of trees and out-of-bag (OOB) error. LASSO regression was conducted using the glmnet package with 10-fold cross-validation (cv.glmnet) to optimize the penalty parameter (λ) and select features with nonzero coefficients. XGBoost uses the DMatrix format, and the variable importance is extracted using the xgb importance. SHAP analysis, performed using shapviz and iml, quantified the contribution of each feature; a venn diagram identified core predictive variables across the three models.

To examine potential nonlinear associations between the inflammatory indices and CKM risk, RCS regression was employed to generate dose-response curves. The number of knots (ranging from three to five) was selected based on the minimum Akaike information criterion. The inflammatory indices were categorized into sex-specific quartiles. Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between inflammatory indices and the risk of early CKM stage, with the lowest quartile serving as the reference. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex, whereas Model 2 was further adjusted for residence area, education level, marital status, annual income, smoking history, alcohol consumption, and exercise frequency. Receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted and the AUC was calculated. Differences between AUCs of different models were assessed using DeLong’s Z-tests, and P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method to control for type I error. In the subgroup analyses, multiplicative interactions were assessed by including cross-product terms in the multivariable logistic regression models. To test the robustness of the results, we performed sensitivity analyses by excluding participants who used antihypertensive, antidiabetic, or lipid-lowering medications and those with cancer, mental disorders, liver diseases, respiratory diseases, or autoimmune diseases. The mediation effects of body composition were evaluated using the processR package in R, with 10,000 bootstrap iterations to estimate confidence intervals.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3 and SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA.). Two-sided tests were used throughout, with a P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Result

Basic characteristics of participants by CKM stage

A total of 5,692 participants were included in the analysis (mean age 52.3 ± 13.5 years; 37.6% male) (Table 1). Participants in the early CKM stage were more likely to be older, male, living in rural areas, have lower annual income, lower educational attainment, and a history of smoking and alcohol consumption. The levels of FMI, VFI, RET#, HFR%, RPI, MHR, NHR, and CAR were significantly higher in the early CKM stage group than in the pre-early CKM stage group, whereas MFR and MMI were significantly lower.

Key variables of inflammation-related hematological profile in early-stage CKM risk assessment

Figures 1A1-A3 shows the results of variable selection by the RF model. The model achieved an AUC of 0.746 on the test set, indicating a moderate to high predictive efficacy. Among the predictors, MHR, NHR, RPI and RET# were the most significant. The stabilization of the OOB error after a sufficient number of trees further confirmed the reliability of the model.

Three machine learning methods—Random Forest (RF), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)—were employed to identify the most relevant variables. Overall, the ML models, including RF, LASSO regression, and XGBoost, consistently identified NHR, MHR, HFR, RPI, RET#, and CAR as key biomarkers for predicting early-stage CKM risk (Fig. 1D). These models demonstrated robust predictive performance, confirming the clinical utility of systemic inflammatory parameters for risk stratification.

RCS analysis

RCS analysis identified significant nonlinear associations between inflammation-related hematological profiles and early-stage CKM risk (P for nonlinearity < 0.001, except for RPI). Both NHR and MHR exhibited steep risk escalations beyond the critical thresholds of 7.05 and 0.66, respectively, indicating strong inflection points for heightened CKM susceptibility. An inverse U-shaped relationship was observed, with risk increasing sharply at lower CAR levels and reaching a maximum at approximately 0.11. Beyond this threshold, the estimated risk decreased.

RPI, HFR%, and RET# displayed positive associations with early-stage CKM risk, with risk increasing progressively as values exceeded 1.81, 6.83%, and 143.03 × 10³/µL, respectively. The association with HFR followed an inverted U-shaped pattern, with the odds ratio peaking at approximately 6.83% before gradually declining at higher values. These results underscore the prognostic significance of specific inflammation-related hematological profiles as robust predictors of early-stage CKM (Fig. 1).

Associations between inflammation-related hematological profile and risk of early-stage CKM

As outlined in Table 2, significant positive associations were observed between the inflammation-related hematological profiles (NHR, MHR, HFR, RPI, RET#, and CAR) and the risk of early-stage CKM after adjusting for potential confounders. A robust dose-response relationship was evident for all parameters. Specifically, the odds ratios (ORs) for the highest versus lowest quartile were as follows: NHR in the highest quartile (Q4) exhibited the most pronounced association, with an OR of 10.4 (95% CI: 8.43, 12.7) (P for trend < 0.0001). The next most significant parameter was MHR, with an OR of 7.75 (95% CI: 6.35, 9.45) (P for trend < 0.0001). Notably, RET# showed a similarly strong association, with an OR of 6.99 (95% CI: 5.76–8.48) in Q4. The associations for HFR, RPI, and CAR were also substantial, with ORs for the highest quartile consistently ranging between 4 and 5 compared with the reference group. These findings reinforce the strong link between inflammation-related hematological profiles and early-stage CKM risk.

Subgroup analysis of inflammation-related hematological profile and their association with early-stage CKM risk

Subgroup differences (Table 2) examined the impact of sex, age, smoking history, drinking history, and physical inactivity on the associations between inflammation-related hematological profile and early-stage CKM syndrome risk. In males, HFR and RPI exhibited strong positive associations across quartiles, with the highest quartile showing an OR of 6.57 (95% CI: 4.65–9.30; P for interaction = 0.0001) for HFR and 6.72 (95% CI: 4.77–9.47; P for interaction = 0.0003) for RPI. Although similar trends were observed in females, the interactions for other indices were not statistically significant, suggesting comparable predictive power between the sexes.

Participants aged < 60 years showed a higher risk of early-stage CKM compared to those aged ≥ 60 years, with HFR demonstrating a significant interaction (P for interaction = 0.0355). Among smokers, the associations between the HFR, RPI, RET #, and early-stage CKM risk were markedly stronger. For example, the highest quartile of HFR among smokers yielded an OR of 7.87 (95% CI: 5.08–12.2; P for interaction = 0.0005), RPI reached an OR of 7.09 (95% CI: 4.61–10.9; P for interaction = 0.0145), and RET# exhibited an OR of 9.02 (95% CI: 5.82–14.0; P for interaction = 0.0380). In contrast, these associations were attenuated among nonsmokers, indicating that smoking significantly amplified the impact of inflammation-related hematological profiles on early CKM risk (Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

In sensitivity analysis, similar findings were found when excluding participants who used antihypertensive, antidiabetic, or lipid-lowering medications and those with major comorbidities (cancer, mental, liver, respiratory, or autoimmune diseases) (Table S4-S5), indicating the robustness of the associations between inflammatory parameters and early-stages of CKM.

Mediation effects on body composition

Results from mediation analyses demonstrated that all four body composition indicators with potential mediation in the relationship between inflammation-related hematological profiles and early-stage CKM syndrome. Notably, for CAR, the indirect effect via FMI yielded an OR of 1.76 (95% CI: 1.64–1.90), accounting for 57.63% of the total effect, while the mediation via VFI presented an OR of 1.67 (95% CI: 1.57–1.79) with a mediation proportion of 52.97%. Furthermore, MMI exhibited a robust mediating role with an indirect effect OR of 1.69 (95% CI: 1.58–1.80) and a mediation proportion of 54.53%, whereas MFR showed more moderate mediation effects, typically explaining between 20% and 37% of the association. These results underscore the prognostic significance of specific inflammation-related hematological profiles as robust predictors of early-stage CKM (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This large-scale, representative, cross-sectional study provides novel insights into the interplay between inflammation-related hematological profiles, body composition, and early-stage CKM syndrome. By integrating advanced ML algorithms with traditional epidemiological analyses, we identified six key inflammatory biomarkers (NHR, MHR, CAR, RET#, RPI, and HFR) as robust predictors of early-stage CKM. Notably, body composition indices showed potential mediation associations accounting for 20–57% of these associations, highlighting the complex interplay between systemic inflammation and fat-muscle imbalance.

The consistent identification of NHR and MHR as the top predictors across all ML models (AUC range: 0.746–0.788) underscores their clinical relevance. Participants in the highest quartile of NHR had an OR of 10.4 (95% CI: 8.43–12.7), while those with elevated MHR exhibited OR up to 7.75, indicating a steep, dose-dependent escalation in CKM risk. Notably, RCS analyses further revealed nonlinear dose-response relationships, with critical thresholds (NHR > 7.05 and MHR > 0.66) marking inflection points where the risk of early-stage CKM escalates exponentially (P for nonlinearity < 0.001). These thresholds are broadly consistent with recent large-scale studies. For example, Shu et al. reported nonlinear dose–response relationships of MHR = 0.42 and NHR = 5.43 with the severity of new-onset coronary artery disease, indicating inflection points for atherosclerotic burden and Gensini score progression [45]. Similarly, Song et al. found that elevated NHR and MHR were independently associated with advanced stages of CKM syndrome in NHANES 1999–2020 data, highlighting their predictive potential in systemic cardiometabolic dysfunction [46]. Taken together, these findings reinforce the biological plausibility of NHR and MHR as integrated markers linking inflammation, lipid metabolism, and cardiometabolic disorders. From a clinical perspective, both indices are inexpensive and readily obtainable from routine complete blood counts and HDL-C measurements, which supports their potential utility in large-scale cardiometabolic screening programs, particularly in resource-limited settings.

These patterns support emerging pathophysiological mechanisms such as neutrophil-mediated endothelial dysfunction via reactive oxygen species production and cytokine release [47, 48] Additionally, monocyte-macrophage-driven inflammatory responses may contribute to metabolic dysregulation and hepatic lipid accumulation [49, 50] HFR and CAR exhibit a clear threshold effect, indicating the presence of a “risk inflection point.” This suggests that the progressive activation of inflammation can lead to a significant increase in risk, highlighting the importance of early monitoring. RET#, RPI, and HFR, which are markers of erythropoietic activity, further highlight the interplay between hematopoiesis, iron metabolism, and metabolic dysfunction, which are increasingly implicated in cardiorenal pathology. The reproducibility of these findings across algorithms mitigates the concerns of model-specific overfitting and strengthens their candidacy for clinical translation.

In our study, mediation analysis revealed that changes in body composition, particularly the coexistence of adiposity accumulation and sarcopenia, mediated 20–57% of the relationship between systemic inflammation and early-stage CKM manifestations. Notably, this finding aligns with and complements the findings of previous studies on the interplay between body composition and inflammation. Bibi et al. reported that classical anthropometric markers, including total body fat, subcutaneous fat, visceral fat, and hepatic fat, were positively associated with all inflammatory markers [51]. Additionally, a 7-year follow-up of the SHIP-TREND cohort highlighted the significance of body composition and fat distribution markers as risk factors for inflammation progression [33]. Our findings further support the potential of targeted interventions aimed at improving body composition and mitigating inflammation, such as resistance training combined with anti-inflammatory dietary modifications.

Moreover, the subgroup analysis revealed that elevated RET# levels were associated with a significantly increased CKM risk, particularly among males, individuals under 60 years of age, and those with a history of smoking. These findings underscore the importance of developing tailored preventive strategies for high-risk populations. Integrating reticulocyte parameters with smoking status and sex in clinical risk assessments could further refine early detection protocols.

Consistent with findings from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) cohort [52], systemic inflammation—as reflected by elevated IL-6 and hsCRP—was shown to modify the cardiovascular risk across CKM stages. Our study extends this evidence by identifying reticulocyte-based hematological indicators that mirror low-grade inflammatory activation and hematopoietic stress in early-stage CKM. This integration of systemic and cellular inflammatory markers provides a more nuanced understanding of CKM progression and could refine early risk stratification frameworks.

In addition, recent evidence suggests that glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), such as tirzepatide, semaglutide, and CagriSema, may offer integrated benefits in CKM management by improving glycemic control, lipid metabolism, and systemic inflammation [53, 54] These agents also show cardioprotective effects and may represent a promising adjunctive therapy, particularly when combined with resistance exercise to prevent muscle loss during weight reduction.

Despite the use of machine learning models with rigorous cross-validation to enhance internal validity.This study has several limitations. First, cross-sectional design restrict causal inference. Consequently, the observed mediation effects should be interpreted as statistical associations compatible with mediation rather than evidence of causal pathways. Longitudinal cohorts or Mendelian randomization studies are needed to clarify causal relationships in the further. Second, as the study population was drawn from Tianjin, China, caution is warranted when generalizing to other regions or ethnic groups, as differences level of inflammation, body composition, diet habit, and genetic background; external validation is required before clinical application elsewhere. Third, we did not collect information on dietary energy intake, which is a significant factor in metabolic health. This omission may introduce potential confounding bias in overestimation or underestimation of inflammatory parameters on early-stages of CKM. Finally, accumulating cohort studies suggest that the post-COVID-19 state is characterized by persistent low-grade inflammation, endothelial injury, and metabolic dysregulation, potentially accelerating CKM development. Future studies should consider stratifying participants by prior COVID-19 exposure when validating CKM risk model.

Conclusion

In summary, our study highlights the pivotal role of chronic low-grade inflammation in the onset of early-stage CKM, with NHR, MHR, HFR, RPI, RET#, and CAR emerging as key biomarkers for risk prediction. The significant mediating effect of body composition, particularly the imbalance between adiposity and muscle mass, provides a rationale for developing precise prevention strategies. By combining ML insights with robust epidemiological methods, our findings pave the way for threshold-based monitoring and targeted lifestyle interventions aimed at mitigating the global burden of early-stage CKM syndrome (Fig. 3).
