Association of door-to-reperfusion time with in-hospital mortality in shockable-rhythm out-of-hospital cardiac arrest undergoing primary percutaneous coronary intervention

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction is a common and potentially reversible cause of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. Early landmark studies and subsequent registry analyses demonstrated that a substantial proportion of resuscitated out-of-hospital cardiac arrest patients harbor acute culprit coronary lesions and that emergent coronary angiography with Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (PCI), when indicated, is associated with better in-hospital outcomes.1, 2, 3

Despite advances in post-arrest care, prognosis after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest remains strongly determined by prehospital injury, particularly cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) duration and the presence of bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation.4, 5, 6

When acute myocardial infarction is the precipitating cause of arrest, however, in-hospital care continues to represent a critical and potentially modifiable phase. Within contemporary acute myocardial infarction systems of care, reducing treatment delay—traditionally operationalized as door-to-balloon time—has long served as a pragmatic and measurable quality target.5, 6, 7, 8 National initiatives have successfully shortened these in-hospital process times, although population-level mortality reductions have been inconsistent, highlighting the influence of case mix and competing risks.5, 6

In the highest-risk acute myocardial infarction subsets, including ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) complicated by cardiogenic shock, recent data continue to indicate that faster reperfusion is associated with better survival.9, 10 Shockable-rhythm out-of-hospital cardiac arrest frequently reflects severe ongoing ischemia, and earlier reperfusion may stabilize arrhythmogenic substrate, limit infarct size, and support hemodynamics during the post-resuscitation phase. Nonetheless, this population also presents unique practical challenges that can delay intervention, such as refractory ventricular arrhythmias, ongoing resuscitation, and the need to secure return of spontaneous circulation before or during transfer to the catheterization laboratory.10, 11 A key methodological limitation of conventional “door-to-balloon time” is that culprit-vessel reperfusion can occur before balloon inflation in contemporary practice (e.g., guidewire passage restoring flow, thrombus aspiration, or excimer laser coronary atherectomy). Accordingly, we focused on door-to-reperfusion time, defined as the time from arrival to the earliest successful reperfusion maneuver in the culprit vessel. We examined the association between door-to-reperfusion time and in-hospital mortality in shockable-rhythm out-of-hospital cardiac arrest undergoing primary PCI for acute myocardial infarction, while adjusting for major prehospital determinants and physiological severity. We also evaluated clinical predictors of delayed reperfusion (door-to-reperfusion time >90 min), a commonly used in-hospital delay threshold.12

In addition, because hypoxic brain injury is a major competing determinant of outcome after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, we evaluated poor neurological status at discharge (CPC 3–5) as a secondary endpoint in a supplementary analysis.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

We performed a retrospective, single-center cohort study at the National Hospital Organization Disaster Medical Center (Tokyo, Japan), a regional cardiac arrest referral center with 24/7 catheterization capability and an annual out-of-hospital cardiac arrest volume of approximately 300–400 cases. The institutional review board approved the protocol and waived individual informed consent owing to the retrospective design.

Study population

We screened consecutive out-of-hospital cardiac arrest patients who (1) had an initial shockable rhythm (pulseless ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation), (2) were transported to our emergency department, (3) underwent emergent coronary angiography, (4) were diagnosed with acute myocardial infarction as the etiology of arrest, and (5) received primary PCI during the index presentation.

Acute myocardial infarction as the presumed etiology of arrest was defined by integrated clinical judgment consistent with the Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction, based on angiographic identification of a presumed culprit lesion, serial biomarker changes, ischemic ECG findings when assessable, supportive imaging findings when available, and exclusion of a more likely competing non-coronary diagnosis.13

Exclusions were traumatic or non-cardiac causes of arrest, absence of an acute culprit coronary lesion on angiography, no PCI performed, or missing timestamps for emergency department arrival or the first reperfusion maneuver.

Patients were included only when emergent coronary angiography identified a presumed acute culprit lesion considered responsible for the presentation and primary PCI was performed to that lesion during the index presentation. Cases in which the initially targeted lesion was subsequently judged not to represent an acute culprit lesion (for example, chronic total occlusion rather than acute occlusion) were excluded.

Because this was a retrospective study, emergent coronary angiography was not mandated by a study-specific protocol. Rather, the decision was made by the treating physicians based on the institutional emergency care pathway and the overall clinical assessment at presentation. Our hospital is a tertiary emergency care center, and a substantial proportion of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest patients have clearly non-cardiac causes, such as trauma, suicide attempt, or other non-coronary etiologies. In addition, patients with prolonged arrest duration, absence of bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation, or other features suggesting severe hypoxic brain injury were often not considered suitable candidates for invasive coronary evaluation. When a cardiac cause was suspected, emergent coronary angiography was generally considered in accordance with contemporary guideline-based practice, including patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and selected patients without ST-segment elevation who had hemodynamic instability, electrical instability, suspected ongoing myocardial ischemia, preceding chest pain before collapse, or no clear non-cardiac cause of arrest. In patients without clear ST-segment elevation, CT was often performed before angiography to assess alternative causes of arrest and possible hypoxic brain injury; emergent coronary angiography was generally pursued when CT did not identify a clear non-coronary cause and an acute coronary etiology remained clinically suspected.

Among patients otherwise eligible for inclusion, those with missing key variables were excluded before final cohort construction. The final analytical cohort consisted of 141 patients with complete data, and all multivariable analyses were performed using this same complete-case cohort. Data sources and variables

Data were abstracted from standardized emergency medical services (EMS) records, the electronic medical record, and the catheterization laboratory reporting system using a prespecified codebook. Prehospital variables included bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and time intervals.

CPR time was defined as the interval from the first documented initiation of cardiopulmonary resuscitation (i.e., the first documented start of chest compressions) to sustained return of spontaneous circulation or initiation of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, regardless of whether compressions occurred prehospital or in-hospital. Arrival variables included arterial pH and estimated glomerular filtration rate. Procedural variables included culprit vessel, mechanical circulatory support, key time intervals, and final angiographic results (including Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction flow). Patients with missing core time-stamp variables were excluded.

Definition of door-to-reperfusion time

Door-to-reperfusion time was defined as the interval from emergency department arrival to the earliest time at which culprit-vessel flow reached Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) ≥2. In patients with initial TIMI 0–1 flow, this corresponded to the earliest successful reperfusion maneuver (e.g., guidewire passage, thrombus aspiration, excimer laser coronary atherectomy activation, or balloon inflation). In patients with initial TIMI 2–3 flow at the first diagnostic angiogram, door-to-reperfusion time was defined as the time of the first angiographic confirmation of TIMI 2–3 flow.

In some patients, final TIMI flow deteriorated despite initial reperfusion, reflecting distal embolization, secondary no-reflow, re-occlusion, and/or severe irreversible myocardial injury. Door-to-reperfusion time was treated as a process-of-care metric capturing in-hospital time-to-reperfusion in contemporary PCI workflows.

Outcome

The primary endpoint was all-cause in-hospital death.

As a secondary outcome, poor neurological status at discharge was defined as a Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) score of 3–5.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians (IQR) and categorical variables as counts (percentages). Group comparisons used Wilcoxon rank-sum and χ2/Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate.

The primary multivariable logistic regression model was constructed using a limited number of prespecified clinically relevant covariates commonly associated with outcome after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest: age (per year), bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (yes/no), CPR time (per minute), arterial pH, and door-to-reperfusion time (per minute). Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance rather than automated variable selection, with attention to the limited number of outcome events. For interpretability, arterial pH was scaled and reported per 0.1-unit increase.

Although several angiographic and procedural variables (including multivessel disease and final TIMI flow grade) differed between survivors and non-survivors, these variables were not included in the primary multivariable model because of the limited number of outcome events and their potential role as intermediate markers of disease severity and/or treatment effect.

To assess nonlinearity, door-to-reperfusion time was additionally modeled using natural cubic splines (degrees of freedom = 3) and compared against the linear term using a likelihood-ratio test.

Linearity was formally assessed for door-to-reperfusion time using a natural cubic spline model because it was the primary exposure of interest. Other continuous covariates in the primary model, including age, arterial pH, and CPR time, were retained as linear terms a priori for clinical interpretability and model parsimony given the limited number of outcome events.

In a prespecified secondary analysis, a logistic model identified predictors of delayed reperfusion defined as door-to-reperfusion time >90 min.

Because predictors of delayed door-to-reperfusion time should be known before reperfusion, the revised model included only pre-reperfusion variables available at or before the time of coronary intervention.

Sensitivity analysis (addressing potential overlap)

Because total CPR time includes in-hospital CPR and could partially overlap with door-to-reperfusion time in patients arriving without sustained ROSC, we performed a sensitivity analysis replacing total CPR time with prehospital CPR time (collapse-to-arrival CPR duration), which does not overlap with door-to-reperfusion time.

For the secondary neurological endpoint (CPC 3–5), we performed a supplementary multivariable logistic regression using the same prespecified covariates to evaluate whether door-to-reperfusion time retained an independent association with neurological outcome.

As an additional supplementary sensitivity analysis, we performed univariable logistic regression after excluding patients treated with extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation.

We also repeated the primary multivariable model with additional adjustment for calendar year as a supportive sensitivity analysis.

All analyses were performed using R (version ≥4.2).

Results

Baseline characteristics

We analyzed 141 patients; 44 in-hospital deaths occurred (31.2%). Median (IQR) CPR time was 20 (7–44) min, arterial pH was 7.21 (6.98–7.35), and door-to-reperfusion time was 91 (72–119) min. Compared with survivors, non-survivors had longer CPR time and longer door-to-reperfusion time (both p < 0.001), lower pH (p < 0.001), lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (p < 0.001), lower total protein (p < 0.001), and lower platelets (p < 0.001). Bystander CPR was less frequent among non-survivors (50% vs 76%, p = 0.003). Non-survivors more often underwent intubation (98% vs 58%, p < 0.001) and ECMO (71% vs 19%, p < 0.001). Culprit-vessel distribution differed between groups (p = 0.013), and achieving final TIMI 3 flow was less common among non-survivors (p < 0.001). Initial and final TIMI flow grades are reported in Table 1 to describe procedural perfusion status. Some patients had poor final TIMI flow despite initial reperfusion, consistent with distal embolization, secondary no-reflow, re-occlusion, and/or severe irreversible myocardial injury. Multivariable prediction of in-hospital death

In the prespecified multivariable model, longer door-to-reperfusion time remained independently associated with higher mortality (OR 1.01 per minute, 95% CI 1.00–1.03; p = 0.023). For clinical interpretability, this corresponds approximately to an odds ratio of 1.10 per 10-min increase in door-to-reperfusion time. Older age (OR 1.08 per year, 95% CI 1.03–1.15; p = 0.004), longer CPR time (OR 1.04, 95% CI 1.01–1.08; p = 0.011), and lower arterial pH (per 0.1-unit increase: OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.45–0.88; p = 0.011) were also independently associated with mortality, whereas bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation was not statistically significant after adjustment (OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.22–1.54; p = 0.276). Model coefficients and confidence intervals are listed in Table 2. The correlation between door-to-reperfusion time and CPR time was moderate (Spearman’s ρ = 0.413, p < 0.001), and variance inflation factors in the final multivariable model were low (door-to-reperfusion time 1.13; CPR time 2.61), suggesting that problematic multicollinearity was not present.

A natural cubic spline for door-to-reperfusion time (degrees of freedom = 3) did not show evidence of nonlinearity versus a linear term (likelihood-ratio test p = 0.871). The adjusted odds of death increased approximately monotonically across the observed door-to-reperfusion time range (Fig. 2).

Predictors of delayed reperfusion (door-to-reperfusion time >90 min)

In the exploratory model for delayed door-to-reperfusion time (>90 min), pre-PCI CT remained independently associated with delay (OR 3.73, 95% CI 1.29–10.8; p = 0.015), whereas age, bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation, CPR time, arterial pH (per 0.1-unit increase), and cardiac arrest on arrival were not independently associated (Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis using prehospital CPR time

To address potential overlap between total CPR time and door-to-reperfusion time in patients arriving without sustained ROSC, we performed a sensitivity analysis in which total CPR time was replaced with prehospital CPR time, defined as the duration of cardiopulmonary resuscitation before hospital arrival. In this model, longer door-to-reperfusion time remained independently associated with in-hospital mortality (OR 1.02 per minute; 95% CI 1.00–1.03; p = 0.012). Prehospital CPR time showed a borderline association after adjustment (OR 1.04 per minute; 95% CI 0.99–1.09; p = 0.077). Older age remained associated with mortality (OR 1.08 per year; 95% CI 1.03–1.14; p = 0.002), and arterial pH was strongly associated with mortality in the expected direction (Table S1).

Sensitivity analysis excluding patients treated with extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation

We performed a supplementary sensitivity analysis excluding patients treated with extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation. In the remaining 92 patients, 13 in-hospital deaths occurred. In this subgroup, longer CPR time and lower arterial pH remained significantly associated with in-hospital mortality in univariable analysis, whereas door-to-reperfusion time was not significantly associated (Table S2).

Sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with initial TIMI 0–1 flow

In supportive sensitivity analyses restricted to patients with initial TIMI 0–1 flow, longer door-to-reperfusion time remained associated with in-hospital mortality in parsimonious exploratory models despite the smaller number of events in this subgroup (Table S3).

Sensitivity analysis additionally adjusting for calendar year

In a supplementary sensitivity analysis additionally adjusting for calendar year, calendar year was not independently associated with in-hospital mortality (OR 0.91, 95% CI 0.80–1.04; p = 0.153), whereas door-to-reperfusion time remained associated (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.03; p = 0.024) (Table S4).

Neurological outcome (secondary analysis)

Poor neurological outcome (CPC 3–5) occurred in 57 patients. In the supplementary multivariable model, older age (OR 1.07 per year, 95% CI 1.02–1.13; p = 0.004), longer CPR time (OR 1.04 per minute, 95% CI 1.00–1.07; p = 0.032), and lower arterial pH (per 0.1-unit increase: OR 0.58, 95% CI 0.41–0.83; p = 0.002) were independently associated with poor neurological outcome. Bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation showed a borderline association after adjustment (OR 0.42, 95% CI 0.16–1.09; p = 0.075), whereas door-to-reperfusion time was not independently associated (OR 1.01 per minute, 95% CI 0.99–1.02; p = 0.080) (Table S5).

A retrospective chart review of the 44 in-hospital deaths suggested that the presumed primary mode of death was cardiovascular/circulatory in 20 patients, neurological in 10, other non-cardiac complications in 5, and mixed or indeterminate in 9; explicit withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy for poor neurological prognosis was documented in 3 patients.

Discussion

In this cohort of patients with shockable-rhythm out-of-hospital cardiac arrest undergoing primary percutaneous coronary intervention, longer door-to-reperfusion time was associated with higher in-hospital mortality after adjustment for major prehospital determinants and physiological severity. These findings suggest that in-hospital time-to-reperfusion may serve as an informative process-of-care marker in this high-risk population, although the observational design precludes causal inference. For clinical interpretability, the primary multivariable estimate corresponds approximately to an odds ratio of 1.10 per 10-min increase in door-to-reperfusion time.5, 6, 9

Shockable rhythms commonly reflect ongoing coronary ischemia. Earlier reperfusion may stabilize the arrhythmogenic substrate, limit infarct size, and support hemodynamics and end-organ perfusion during the post-resuscitation phase.14, 15, 16

However, shockable-rhythm out-of-hospital cardiac arrest presents substantial practical challenges that can delay intervention (ongoing resuscitation, hemodynamic instability, diagnostic work-up, and stabilization), making it important to understand whether in-hospital delay retains prognostic relevance after accounting for prehospital factors.

Prior observational studies in arrest populations and high-risk acute coronary syndromes suggest benefits of shortening time to revascularization,5, 6, 10, 17 and system-level initiatives have shown that compressing processes to reperfusion can improve outcomes in ischemic disease.7, 9 The present study extends these concepts specifically to shockable-rhythm out-of-hospital cardiac arrest undergoing primary PCI, suggesting that door-to-reperfusion time retains prognostic information even when strong prehospital predictors are included in the model.10, 11, 18

Randomized trials in resuscitated out-of-hospital cardiac arrest without ST-segment elevation have generally not shown a survival benefit of immediate, unselected coronary angiography versus a delayed/selective strategy.19, 20 Our cohort, however, differs in two key respects: (1) all patients had an initial shockable rhythm and underwent primary PCI for angiographically confirmed acute myocardial infarction, and (2) the presenting ECG categories were ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) 67.4%, non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) 16.3%, and ST-segment assessment not feasible 16.3% (e.g., ongoing ventricular fibrillation or other impediments to reliable ST evaluation). Accordingly, our findings speak to the in-hospital process interval once an invasive strategy with primary PCI has been selected, rather than to the upstream question of immediate angiography in non-ST-elevation out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. Within this defined treatment pathway, door-to-reperfusion time appears to carry independent prognostic information.

Because total CPR time could partially overlap with door-to-reperfusion time in patients arriving without sustained return of spontaneous circulation, we performed a sensitivity analysis using prehospital CPR time instead. The association between longer door-to-reperfusion time and mortality remained, supporting the robustness of the main finding.

The present cohort included patients treated with extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation, so we considered the term “CPR time” more appropriate than “low-flow time” for the measured interval. In addition, no-flow time could not be determined reliably in this retrospective cohort. These features may limit direct comparability with studies using formal Utstein time definitions.

Preprocedural computed tomography was associated with delayed reperfusion and remained the only independent pre-reperfusion factor in the exploratory model, suggesting that additional diagnostic workflow may have contributed more strongly to in-hospital delay than baseline physiological severity after adjustment.

These findings suggest that streamlined triage and parallelized workflows may help reduce avoidable in-hospital delay while preserving safety.

By contrast, poor neurological outcome appeared to be driven mainly by prehospital and physiological factors rather than in-hospital reperfusion timing. This difference suggests that door-to-reperfusion time may be more relevant to survival than to neurological recovery, which is more strongly influenced by hypoxic–ischemic brain injury.

Patients treated with extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation represented a clinically distinct and more severely ill subgroup. In a supplementary sensitivity analysis excluding these patients, door-to-reperfusion time was not significantly associated with mortality; however, this subgroup included only 92 patients and 13 in-hospital deaths, and the absence of a significant association may have partly reflected limited statistical power. Because the present study specifically focused on shockable-rhythm out-of-hospital cardiac arrest undergoing primary percutaneous coronary intervention, inclusion of some extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation-treated patients also reflects the real-world spectrum of this population. These findings indicate that in-hospital mortality after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest was not explained solely by hypoxic–ischemic neurological injury, and that door-to-reperfusion time should therefore be interpreted as an in-hospital process-of-care marker within a complex post-arrest pathway rather than as a simple direct causal determinant of mortality independent of neurological injury.

Patients treated more rapidly may also have been neurologically less injured and more clinically stable at presentation, and residual confounding by illness severity therefore cannot be excluded.

Taken together, our findings suggest that door-to-reperfusion time is an important process indicator, while its optimization remains context dependent. In particular, some patients will reasonably require CT before catheterization, and patients with prolonged CPR time may be more prone to in-hospital delays due to necessary stabilization. In clinical practice, it may therefore be useful to anticipate potential delay in such contexts and to minimize avoidable waiting where feasible, while maintaining diagnostic appropriateness and safety in individual cases.8, 21

Study limitations

This retrospective, single-center study is subject to inherent limitations. Although we adjusted for key confounders, residual and unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded. Because the number of in-hospital deaths was limited, the multivariable model was intentionally restricted to a small number of clinically central covariates. Nevertheless, the possibility of residual overfitting cannot be completely excluded. Because the study period spanned approximately 14 years, secular changes in PCI workflow, CT triage, extracorporeal support use, and post-arrest care may also have influenced treatment timing and outcomes. Although additional adjustment for calendar year did not materially alter the main findings, this supportive sensitivity analysis should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of events, and residual calendar-time confounding cannot be excluded. Because emergent coronary angiography was performed according to treating-physician judgment within the institutional emergency care pathway rather than a predefined study protocol, referral and selection bias cannot be excluded, and generalizability may be limited. All multivariable analyses used complete-case data, which may have reduced precision and introduced additional selection bias. In addition, the cohort was restricted to patients with angiographically confirmed acute myocardial infarction who underwent primary percutaneous coronary intervention, limiting applicability to patients with non-occlusive etiologies, alternative causes of arrest, or non-shockable rhythms. Patients treated with extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation also represented a clinically distinct and more severely ill subgroup, which contributed to cohort heterogeneity.

As a retrospective study, procedural time stamps may have been imprecise, including the exact timing of first effective reperfusion, particularly in cases where flow improvement occurred before balloon inflation. Some patients also exhibited poor final TIMI flow despite initial reperfusion; these cases likely reflect distal embolization, secondary no-reflow, re-occlusion, and/or severe irreversible myocardial injury. Such patients were retained because the present study focused on door-to-reperfusion time as an in-hospital process-of-care metric rather than final angiographic success. Cause-of-death classification and treatment-limitation decisions were assessed retrospectively without formal adjudication, and withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy may have been incompletely documented. In addition, long-term neurological outcomes and post-discharge mortality were not assessed.

Conclusions

Among patients with shockable-rhythm out-of-hospital cardiac arrest undergoing primary percutaneous coronary intervention, longer door-to-reperfusion time was associated with higher in-hospital mortality. Preprocedural computed tomography was associated with delayed door-to-reperfusion time in the exploratory delay model. These findings suggest that door-to-reperfusion time may be an informative process-of-care marker in this high-risk population, although residual confounding cannot be excluded.
