The cost of resetting discrete-time random walks
John C. Sunil, Richard A. Blythe, Martin R. Evans, Satya N. Majumdar

Abstract
We consider a discrete-time continuous-space random walk, with a symmetric jump distribution, under stochastic resetting. Our aim is to understand how costs, associated with the random walk jumps and the resetting, contribute to the total cost of the motion. We calculate the distribution of the total cost up to first passage of the random walker to a target. By using the backward master equation approach we demonstrate that the distribution of the total cost can be reduced to a Wiener-Hopf integral equation. The resulting integral equation can be exactly solved (in Laplace space) for arbitrary cost functions for the jump and selected functions for the reset cost. We show that the large cost behaviour is dominated by resetting or the jump distribution according to the choice of the jump distribution. In the important case of a Laplace jump distribution, which corresponds to run-and-tumble particle dynamics, and linear costs for jumps and resetting, the Wiener-Hopf equation simplifies to a differential equation which can easily be solved.

2
Set-up of the Problem

We consider a discrete time resetting process
[
7
,
5
]
where the particle starts at
x_{0}
and resets to
x_{r}
with probability
r
. If the particle does not reset in a given time step, it jumps a distance drawn from the symmetric, continuous and normalized jump distribution
f(\eta)
. Hence, the evolution of the position of the particle,
x_{k}
, at time step
k
is given by

\displaystyle x_{k}=\begin{cases}x_{k-1}+\eta_{k}&\text{with probability $1-r$}\;,\\
x_{r}&\text{with probability $r$}\;,\end{cases}

(1)

with initial position
x_{0}
.
We focus on the first passage properties of this system. The process is terminated when the random walker crosses the origin at time step
n_{f}
. A schematic of a sample trajectory is given in Fig.
1
.

Figure 1
:
Schematic of discrete time resetting jump process that is terminated upon first passage to the origin. The red dashed lines represent the resets that occur for the given trajectory.

To each jump we associate a cost
h(\eta)
and with each reset we associate a cost
g(\eta)
where
\eta
is the distance traversed in the jump or reset. For the moment, we only impose that the cost function is positive (
h(\eta)\geq 0,g(\eta)\geq 0
) and symmetric (
h(\eta)=h(-\eta),g(\eta)=g(-\eta)
). Thus the total cost for the process evolves as,

\displaystyle C_{k}=\begin{cases}C_{k-1}+h(\eta_{k})&\text{with probability $1-r$}\;,\\
C_{k-1}+g(x_{r}-x_{k-1})&\text{with probability $r$}\;.\end{cases}

(2)

We are interested in the cost that the process incurs up to the first passage to the origin, which occurs when
x_{k}<0
for the first time. The cost of similar discrete-time processes without resetting has been studied in
[
33
,
34
,
32
]
. The costs,
g(\eta)
and
h(\eta)
could be interpreted as the time required to implement the respective motions, the monetary costs associated or the energy consumed in undergoing the motions of jumps and resets. Depending on the physical context
g(\eta)
and
h(\eta)
will have various particular forms.
To begin with, we keep
g(\eta)
and
h(\eta)
to be general symmetric functions and specialise to the cases of constant or linear
g(\eta)
in Sections
5
and
6
.

First passage problems for discrete-time random walkers can be extended to asymmetric jump distributions, which imply a drift or bias in the jump motion. The first passage properties of such asymmetric jump distributions have been studied in
[
41
]
. Furthermore, dropping the requirement that
g(\eta)
and
h(\eta)
are even leads us to anisotropic costs where jumping or resetting in one particular direction has a different cost. In what follows, we restrict the problem to symmetric jumps and costs for simplicity.

2.1
Backward Master Equation

The quantity of interest is
Q_{r}(x_{0},C)
, which is the distribution of the total cost for a random walker up to the first passage to the origin, given that it starts at
x_{0}
and resets to
x_{r}
with probability
r
. Note that
Q_{r}(x_{0},C)
implicitly depends on the resetting position
x_{r}
, but we do not display the dependence here for brevity of notation. In order to calculate such distributions, it is natural to use a backward master equation approach. Using the Markov property for the process, we can write down the backward master equation as an integral equation

\displaystyle Q_{r}(x_{0},C)=

\displaystyle(1-r)\Bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}dC^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx_{1}Q_{r}(x_{1},C^{\prime})f(x_{1}-x_{0})\delta(C^{\prime}+h(x_{1}-x_{0})-C)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{-\infty}^{0^{-}}dx_{1}\delta(C-h(x_{1}-x_{0}))f(x_{1}-x_{0})\Bigg]

\displaystyle+r\Bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}dC^{\prime}Q_{r}(x_{r},C^{\prime})\delta(C^{\prime}+g(x_{r}-x_{0})-C)\Bigg]\;.

(3)

The first term in (
3
) corresponds to the event when there is no resetting in the first time step, which occurs with probability
(1-r)
, while the second term corresponds to the case when the particle undergoes a reset at the very first step, which happens with probability
r
. The first part of the first term in (
3
) corresponds to an initial jump where the particle ends up at a position
x_{1}>0
, after which the process repeats again, starting from
x_{1}
. The second part accounts for an initial jump which takes the particle to a position
x_{1}<0
. There is a cost associated with this jump, but the process is then terminated. The upper limit of this integral is
0^{-}
as the process does not terminate if the random walker is exactly at
0
. However, this does not affect the calculations as we later assume
f(x_{1}-x_{0})
to be a continuous function. Note that the process is not immediately terminated on the first passage to the origin, but the random walker is allowed to finish the complete step and the cost is accounted for the complete step, as shown in Fig.
1
. This choice of including the cost associated with the leapover length is for analytical simplicity. A detailed analysis of the distribution of the leapover length for various kinds of Lévy flights is presented in
[
42
]
. The final possibility at the initial step is that the particle resets to the resetting position,
x_{r}
, and this is accounted for by the last term in (
3
).

We define the Laplace transform of (
3
) with respect to the total cost as

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{r}(x_{0},p)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dC\;e^{-pC}Q_{r}(x_{0},C)\;.

(4)

Taking the Laplace transform of (
3
) we obtain the following integral equation,

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{r}(x_{0},p)=

\displaystyle(1-r)\Bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}dx_{1}\widetilde{Q}_{r}(x_{1},p)e^{-ph(x_{1}-x_{0})}f(x_{1}-x_{0})+

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0^{-}}dx_{1}e^{-ph(x_{1}-x_{0})}f(x_{1}-x_{0})\Bigg]+re^{-pg(x_{r}-x_{0})}\widetilde{Q}_{r}(x_{r},p)\;.

(5)

The problem now is to solve equation (
5
) to obtain the Laplace transform of the total cost distribution up to first passage to the origin.

3
Exactly Solvable Case: Laplace Jump Distribution with Linear Costs

The integral equation (
5
) is quite difficult to solve in general, but for a particular choice of jump length distribution and cost functions, it can be reduced to a differential equation, which can then be easily solved.
In this way the Laplace jump distribution with linear costs provides a case which may be solved exactly in a self-contained way.

We consider a Laplace distribution for the jump lengths
f(\eta)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-\left|\eta\right|}
, and linear costs for jumps and resets,
h(\eta)=c_{j}\left|\eta\right|
and
g(\eta)=c_{r}\left|\eta\right|
. As explained in the introduction, the Laplace jump distribution
furnishes a description of a ‘run and tumble particle’ which is a persistent random walker that continues moving in the same direction for exponentially distributed time intervals before ‘tumbling’ i.e. switching to another direction
[
43
,
44
,
35
]
.
The linear cost models the consumption of some resource in proportion to the distance travelled between tumbles.

With these choices, we obtain from (
5
)

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{r}(x_{0},p)=

\displaystyle(1-r)\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}dx_{1}\widetilde{Q}_{r}(x_{1},p)\frac{1}{2}e^{-(pc_{j}+1)\left|x_{1}-x_{0}\right|}+\int_{-\infty}^{0^{-}}dx_{1}\frac{1}{2}e^{-(pc_{j}+1)\left|x_{1}-x_{0}\right|}\right]

\displaystyle+re^{-pc_{r}\left|x_{r}-x_{0}\right|}\widetilde{Q}_{r}(x_{r},p)\;.

(6)

The integral equation in (
6
) can be converted to a differential equation by making use of the following identity,

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}e^{-a\left|x-b\right|}=-2a\delta(x-b)+a^{2}e^{-a\left|x-b\right|}\;.

(7)

Then, taking the second derivative with respect to
x_{0}
and integrating over the delta functions, we obtain from (
6
) the following differential equation,

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dx_{0}^{2}}\widetilde{Q}_{r}(x_{0},p)=

\displaystyle(pc_{j}+1)(pc_{j}+r)\widetilde{Q}_{r}(x_{0},p)+r\Big[(pc_{r})^{2}e^{-pc_{r}\left|x_{r}-x_{0}\right|}

\displaystyle-(pc_{j}+1)^{2}e^{-pc_{r}\left|x_{r}-x_{0}\right|}-2pc_{r}\delta(x_{r}-x_{0})\Big]\widetilde{Q}_{r}(x_{r},p)\;.

(8)

In (
8
), we have an inhomogeneous term,
\widetilde{Q}_{r}(x_{r},p)
, that itself depends on the function to be solved, so the equation has to be solved self-consistently.
This method of taking the second derivative to convert an integral equation to a differential equation has been used previously in
[
45
,
46
]
.

The general solution to (
8
) for all
x_{0}
and
x_{r}
is presented in
A
. As the general solution is cumbersome, here we look at the special case of
x_{0}=x_{r}=0
, which is the case where the particle both starts and resets to the origin. We then obtain the final result,

\widetilde{Q}_{r}(0,p)=\\
\frac{p\left(pc_{j}^{2}+rc_{j}+c_{j}-pc_{r}^{2}\right)+r}{p\left(p^{2}c_{j}^{3}+pc_{j}^{2}(\mu+r+2)+c_{j}\left(pc_{r}\left(r-pc_{r}\right)+\mu+2r+1\right)+c_{r}\left(r-(\mu+1)pc_{r}\right)\right)+r}\;,

(9)

where
\mu=\sqrt{(1+pc_{j})(r+pc_{j})}
.
Expression (
9
) is the main result of this section.
It simplifies considerably for certain choices of
c_{j}
,
c_{r}
, which we now discuss.

3.1
Reset cost only

If only resets have cost,
c_{j}=0,c_{r}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{r}(0,p)=\frac{p+\sqrt{r}}{p\sqrt{r}+p+\sqrt{r}}\;,

(10)

This expression for the Laplace transform may be inverted exactly to obtain

Q_{r}(0,C)=\frac{1}{1+r^{1/2}}\delta(C)+\frac{r}{(1+r^{1/2})^{2}}e^{-Cr^{1/2}/(1+r^{1/2})}\;.

(11)

We see there is a probability
1/(1+r^{1/2})
of zero cost which corresponds to first passage to the origin without any resets. The other piece of the distribution is an exponential distribution with
characteristic cost
1+r^{-1/2}
, which diverges as
r\to 0
.

3.2
Jump cost only

If only jumps have cost,
c_{j}=1,c_{r}=0
we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{r}(0,p)=\frac{(p+1)(p+r)-p\sqrt{(p+1)(p+r)}}{(p+1)(p+r+pr)}\;.

(12)

This Laplace transform is difficult to invert exactly, but we can obtain the asymptotic large
C
behaviour from the pole at
p=-r/(1+r)
:

Q_{r}(0,C)\sim\frac{2r^{2}}{(1+r)^{2}}e^{-Cr/(1+r)}\;.

(13)

Thus the large cost distribution is exponential with
characteristic cost
1+r^{-1}
, which has a stronger divergence as
r\to 0
than the reset-only distribution.

Finally we note that plugging in
r=0
in (
12
) recovers the result for the jump cost distribution without resetting as obtained in
[
35
]

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{0}(0,p)=1-\sqrt{\frac{p}{1+p}}\;.

(14)

An exact inversion of this Laplace transform was found in
[
35
]
, and the asymptotic behaviour determined to be a power law

Q_{0}(0,C)\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\frac{1}{C^{3/2}}\;.

(15)

3.3
Jump and reset cost

If the jump and reset costs are weighted equally,
c_{j}=c_{r}=1
, we obtain from (
9
),

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{r}(0,p)=\frac{\sqrt{1+p}(r+(2r+1)p)-p\sqrt{p+r}}{\sqrt{1+p}(4rp^{2}+(4r+1)p+r)}\;.

(16)

Inverting (
16
) numerically
[
47
]
, we obtain the distribution of the total cost at first passage to the origin with
x_{0}=x_{r}=0
, plotted in Fig.
2
. The asymptotic behaviour for large costs can again be obtained by finding the largest singularity of (
16
). There are four singularities: two branch points at
p=-1,-r
and two simple poles at
p_{\pm}
given by

\displaystyle p_{\pm}=\frac{-1-4r\pm\sqrt{1+8r}}{8r}\;.

(17)

The largest singularity of (
16
) is then found to be the pole at
p_{+}
and from this pole we obtain the leading large
C
behaviour as an exponential decay

\displaystyle Q_{r}(0,C)\sim\exp\left[-\left(\frac{{(4r+1)-\sqrt{1+8r}}}{8r}\right)C\right]\;.

(18)

For
r=0.5
, we obtain from this expression (including the prefactor)
Q_{r}(0,C)\sim 0.1056e^{-0.191C}
, which is plotted in Fig.
2
. We observe again that
the large cost behaviour is an exponential decay.

Thus for a Laplace jump distribution with linear costs, in all three cases (reset cost, jump cost, jump and reset cost) we have seen that
the presence of resetting changes the large tail behaviour
of the cost distribution from a power-law decay (
15
) in the absence of resetting, to an exponential decay.

:
Distribution of cost obtained for Laplace jump distribution with linear jump and resetting costs for
r=0.5
. Note the logarithmic y-axis, indicating an exponential asymptotic behaviour.

4
General Jump Distribution

In the case of general jump distribution
f(\eta)
we must
return to the backward equation (
5
).
To convert (
5
) to a known integral equation called the Wiener-Hopf integral equation
[
48
,
41
,
35
]
, we normalise the kernel of the integrals,
e^{-p(\eta)}f(\eta)
, by defining,

\displaystyle\chi_{p}(\eta)=\frac{e^{-ph(\eta)}f(\eta)}{2A(p)}

(19)

where

\displaystyle\quad A(p)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\eta\;e^{-ph(\eta)}f(\eta)\;,

(20)

using which we can re-write (
5
) as

\widetilde{Q}_{r}(x_{0},p)-2(1-r)A(p)\int_{0}^{\infty}dx_{1}\;\widetilde{Q}_{r}(x_{1},p)\chi_{p}(x_{1}-x_{0})\\
=2(1-r)A(p)\int_{-\infty}^{0^{-}}dx_{1}\;\chi_{p}(x_{1}-x_{0})+re^{-pg(x_{r}-x_{0})}\widetilde{Q}_{r}(x_{r},p)\;.

(21)

Note that the limits of the integral term on the l.h.s of (
21
) are from
0
to
\infty
, which is different from the limits for a convolution which would be from
0
to
z
. This prevents us from directly using the convolution theorem for Laplace transforms for solving this integral equation. Equation (
21
), is now of the form of a Wiener-Hopf integral equation that, in principle, can be solved for
\widetilde{Q}_{r}(x_{0},p)
using the recipe provided in
B
. However, the general solution of (
21
), for general resetting cost
g(x_{r}-x_{0})
, is not in a tractable closed form. Nevertheless, we obtain
tractable forms for our chosen resetting costs: a constant cost or linear cost per reset back to the origin. We can still let the jump distribution
f(\eta)
and jump cost
h(\eta)
be arbitrary.

4.1
Simple solution cases of inhomogeneous Wiener-Hopf equation

Let us summarise here some simple cases of the inhomogeneous Wiener-Hopf equation.
We consider the Wiener-Hopf equation in the form

\displaystyle\psi(z,p)-B(p)\int_{0}^{\infty}dz^{\prime}\psi(z^{\prime},p)\chi(z-z^{\prime})=J(z,p)\;,

(22)

where
\psi(z,p)
is the function we seek a solution for,
\chi(\eta)
is symmetric and normalised, and
J(z,p)
is the inhomogeneous term.

The solution of (
22
), detailed in
B
, is given in terms of the Laplace transform

\widetilde{\psi}(\lambda,p)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,e^{-\lambda z}\psi(z,p)\;.

(23)

In principle, the Laplace transform (
23
) can be inverted to obtain
\psi(z,p)
however it is simplest
to obtain an expression for
\psi(0,p)
by taking
\lambda\to\infty

and using Watson’s lemma

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\lambda\widetilde{\psi}(\lambda,p)=\psi(0,p)\;.

(24)

We also define the function

\displaystyle\phi(\lambda,p)=\exp\left[-\frac{\lambda}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\;\frac{\ln(1-B(p)\widehat{\chi}(k))}{\lambda^{2}+k^{2}}\right]\;.

(25)

where
B(p)
is the function appearing in the l.h.s of (
22
) and

\displaystyle\widehat{\chi}(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\eta\;e^{ik\eta}\chi(\eta)\;.

(26)

We now enumerate the relevant known solution cases that we will require.

1.

In this case the inhomogeneous term is constant with respect to
z
:

J(z,p)=j(p)\;.

(27)

The Laplace transform (
23
) is given by

\widetilde{\psi}(\lambda,p)=\frac{j(p)}{\lambda}\frac{\phi(\lambda,p)}{\sqrt{1-B(p)}}

(28)

and we obtain, using (
24
) and
\phi(\infty,p)=1
,

\psi(0,p)=\frac{j(p)}{\sqrt{1-B(p)}}\;.

(29)

2.

In this case the inhomogeneous term decays exponentially with
z

J(z,p)=j(p)e^{-pz}\;.

(30)

The Laplace transform (
23
) is given by

\widetilde{\psi}(\lambda,p)=\frac{j(p)}{\lambda+p}\phi(\lambda,p)\phi(p,p)

(31)

and we obtain

\psi(0,p)=j(p)\phi(p,p)\;.

(32)

3.

In this case the inhomogeneous term is of the following integral form:

J(z,p)=b(p)\int_{-\infty}^{-z}d\eta\,f(\eta)\;.

(33)

The Laplace transform (
23
) is given by

\widetilde{\psi}(\lambda,p)=\frac{b(p)}{B(p)}\frac{1}{\lambda}\left[1-\sqrt{1-B(p)}\phi(\lambda,p)\right]

(34)

and we obtain

\psi(0,p)=\frac{b(p)}{B(p)}\left[1-\sqrt{1-B(p)}\right]\;.

(35)

In order to use the above results to construct the solution to (
21
), we first note that in (
21
) the first inhomogeneous term on the right hand side may be written as

2(1-r)A(p)\int_{-\infty}^{0^{-}}dx_{1}\;\chi_{p}(x_{1}-x_{0})=2(1-r)A(p)\int_{-\infty}^{-x_{0}^{-}}d\eta\;\chi_{p}(\eta)\;,

(36)

which is a term of the form (iii).
The second inhomogeneous term in (
21
) depends on the choice of

g(x_{r}-x_{0})
, the cost of a reset: for
g(x_{r}-x_{0})
a constant, it is of the form (i) and for
g(x_{r}-x_{0})
linear
it is of the form (ii).
In the following two sections
5
and
6
we use the above results to construct the solution to (
21
) for these choices of the resetting cost.

5
Constant Cost per Reset

If we assume a constant cost per reset of the form

\displaystyle g(x_{r}-x_{0})=c_{r}\;,

(37)

making use of (
36
), we obtain from (

7
Conclusions and Outlook

In this work we have extended the general framework to calculate the total cost distribution for discrete-time random walks up to first passage to the target presented in
[
35
]
to the case of random walks with resetting. We have obtained a general expression for the total cost distribution for constant resetting cost (
40
) and linear resetting cost (
74
), which is valid for arbitrary jump distributions and jump cost functions.

Furthermore, we observed that the dominant large cost behaviour is determined by the jump distribution. We saw that for the case of constant resetting cost, the presence of a power law tail for the jump distribution results in the large cost behaviour obtaining a power-law decay, which is similar to that of the case without resetting. In the absence of such a power-law tail, resetting dominates and the total cost function obtains an exponential decay at large values of total cost.

It would be of significance to explore whether other reset cost functions, beyond constant and linear, could yield an analytically tractable solution from the Wiener-Hopf integral equation.
Also for systems where there is a trade-off between jump and reset costs, a function which encapsulates the competition could be constructed and optimised in a similar fashion to
[
13
]
.
Other interesting directions to pursue would be to calculate the penalty associated with overshooting the target barrier and compare the effect of resetting on it. Computing the results for the system in higher dimensions and multiple time step dependent cost functions would also be interesting.

Appendix B
Wiener-Hopf Integral Equation

An equation of the form

\displaystyle\psi(z,p)-B(p)\int_{0}^{\infty}dz^{\prime}\psi(z^{\prime},p)\chi(z-z^{\prime})=J(z,p)\;,

(110)

is called a Wiener-Hopf integral equation. This appendix gives a recipe to solve the Wiener-Hopf problem. For more details we refer the reader to
[
50
,
41
,
48
]
.

If
\chi(\eta)
is continuous, symmetric and normalized to unity, (
110
) has a solution given in terms of the Green’s function,

\displaystyle\psi(z,p)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dz_{1}\;G(z,z_{1},p)J(z_{1},p)\;,

(111)

where there exists an expression for
G(z,z_{1},p)
in terms of the double Laplace transform,

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(\lambda,\lambda_{1},p)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\;e^{-\lambda z}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz_{1}\;e^{-\lambda_{1}z_{1}}G(z,z_{1},p)\;,

(112)

which is given by,

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(\lambda,\lambda_{1},p)=\frac{\phi(\lambda,p)\phi(\lambda_{1},p)}{\lambda+\lambda_{1}}\;.

(113)

Finally, the expression for
\phi(\lambda,p)
is given by,

\displaystyle\phi(\lambda,p)=\exp\left[-\frac{\lambda}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\;\frac{\ln(1-B(p)\widehat{\chi}(k))}{\lambda^{2}+k^{2}}\right]\;,

(114)

where
\widehat{\chi}(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dz\;e^{ikz}\chi(z)
is the Fourier transform of
\chi(z)
.

The above set of steps are quite difficult to perform in general and extract useful information out of the solution, but the solution can be obtained in a relatively tractable form for a few special cases of
J(z,p)
.

B.1
Constant
J(z,p)
:
J(z,p)=j(p)

If the inhomogenous term in (
110
) is a constant, then the Laplace transform of the solution,
\widetilde{\psi}(\lambda,s)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}(\lambda,p)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\;e^{-\lambda z}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz_{1}\;j(p)G(z,z_{1},p)=j(p)\widetilde{G}(\lambda,0,p)\;.

(115)

From (
113
), we obtain,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}(\lambda,p)=\frac{j(p)}{\lambda}\phi(\lambda,p)\phi(0,p)\;,

(116)

and
\phi(0,p)
can be expressed as
[
50
]

\displaystyle\phi(0,p)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-B(p)}}\;,

(117)

which leads to the final solution

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}(\lambda,p)=\frac{j(p)}{\lambda\sqrt{1-B(p)}}\phi(\lambda,p)\;.

(118)

B.2
Exponential
J(z,p):J(z,p)=e^{-pz}

If the inhomogeneous term in (
110
) has an exponential form, then we can identify the integral over the inhomogeneous term as a Laplace transform and write,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}(\lambda,p)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\;e^{-\lambda z}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz_{1}\;e^{-pz_{1}}G(z,z_{1},p)=\widetilde{G}(\lambda,p,p)\;.

(119)

From (
113
), we get

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(\lambda,p,p)=\frac{\phi(\lambda,p)\phi(p,p)}{\lambda+p}\;,

(120)

with
\phi(\lambda,p)
given by (
114
).

So we obtain the final solution as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}(\lambda,s)=\frac{\phi(\lambda,p)\phi(p,p)}{\lambda+p}\;.

(121)

B.3
Integral of form
J(z,p)=b(p)\int_{-\infty}^{-z}d\eta\,\chi(\eta)

Consider a linear transformation of the form

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(z,p)=a(p)\psi_{1}(z,p)-b(p)\;,

(122)

where
\psi_{0}(z,p)
satisfies,

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(z,p)-B(p)\int_{0}^{\infty}dz^{\prime}\psi_{0}(z^{\prime},p)\chi(z-z^{\prime})=j(p)\;.

(123)

Using (
122
), we get

\displaystyle a(p)\psi_{1}(z,p)-b(p)-B(p)\int_{0}^{\infty}dz^{\prime}\left[a(p)\psi_{1}(z,p)-b(p)\right]\chi(z-z^{\prime})=j(p)\;,

(124)

which can be rearranged to obtain

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(z,p)-B(p)\int_{0}^{\infty}dz^{\prime}\psi_{1}(z^{\prime},p)

\displaystyle\chi(z-z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{b(p)}{a(p)}+\frac{j(p)}{a(p)}-\frac{B(p)b(p)}{a(p)}+\frac{B(p)b(p)}{a(p)}\int_{-\infty}^{-z}d\eta\,\chi(\eta)\;,

(125)

where to obtain (
125
), we have made use of the symmetry and normalization of
\chi(\eta)
, which gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dz^{\prime}\chi(z-z^{\prime})=1-\int_{-\infty}^{-z}d\eta\,\chi(\eta)\;.

(126)

Now choose
a(p)=B(p)
and
j(p)=-b(p)+B(p)b(p)
, which leads us to

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(z,p)-B(p)\int_{0}^{\infty}dz^{\prime}\psi_{1}(z^{\prime},p)\chi(z-z^{\prime})=b(p)\int_{-\infty}^{-z}d\eta\,\chi(\eta)\;.

(127)

Now taking the Laplace transform of (
122
) and rearranging, we obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}_{1}(\lambda,p)=\frac{1}{B(p)}\left(\widetilde{\psi}_{0}(\lambda,p)+\frac{b(p)}{\lambda}\right).

(128)

Finally making use of the solution to the Wiener-Hopf equation with the a constant cost (
118
) with
j(p)=-b(p)+B(p)b(p)
, we obtain the solution to the integral form of inhomogeneous term as,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}_{1}(\lambda,p)=\frac{b(p)}{B(p)}\frac{1}{\lambda}\left[1-\phi(\lambda,p)\sqrt{1-B(p)}\right]\;.

(129)

Appendix C
Simulation Details

To perform the simulation we first need to generate random variables according to the given jump length distribution,
f(x)
. This is obtained using an inverse transform sampling algorithm. We generate a uniform random number,
u\in[0,1]
and obtain the jump length
x
with the required distribution
f(x)
by solving,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{x}d\eta\;f(\eta)=u\;.

(130)

We then sum up the costs up to the first passage time using the given jump cost function,
h(x)
and reset cost function
g(x)
and bin the results obtained appropriately. We sample over
10^{8}
samples for each of the plots obtained in the paper. The program used for generating the total cost distribution is provided in
[
49
]
.
