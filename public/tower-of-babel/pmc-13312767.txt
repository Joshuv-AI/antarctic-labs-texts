Predicting MRI-derived total brain volume from DXA-derived head composition in middle-aged and older adults: WASEDA’S Health Study
Authors: Toshiharu Tsutsui, Suguru Torii, Kumpei Tanisawa, Toru Takahashi, Kaori Usui, Nobuhiro Nakamura, Taishi Midorikawa, Kento Nakagawa, Reiji Ohkuma, Hiroaki Kumano, Kaori Ishii, Katsuhiko Suzuki, Shizuo Sakamoto, Mitsuru Higuchi, Koichiro Oka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTotal brain volume (TBV) derived from brain MRI is an important marker of brain structural health in middle-aged and older adults, but MRI is resource-intensive and not always feasible in largescale or repeated assessments. We examined whether dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA)-derived head composition measures can estimate MRI-derived TBV in middle-aged and older adults.MethodsThis study included 314 participants (≥ 40 years) who underwent whole-body DXA (head ROI manually defined using a sub-region tool) and 3T brain MRI within 1 year. MRI-derived TBV was defined as the sum of gray and white matter volumes. We developed multivariable linear regression models using either DXA-derived head lean-and-fat mass or head fat mass as the primary predictor. Nested models were fitted: Model 1 (predictor only), Model 2 (+ age and sex), and Model 3 (+ BMI). Apparent model performance was summarized using R² and RMSE, and internal validation was performed using 1,000 bootstrap resamples to obtain optimism-corrected performance estimates. Calibration was evaluated using calibration-in-the-large (CITL) and calibration slope. Agreement between observed and predicted TBV was assessed using Bland–Altman analysis. Sensitivity analyses additionally adjusted for the MRI–DXA measurement interval and evaluated sex-stratified performance.ResultsModel 3 was treated as the prespecified primary model because it was the fully adjusted model including clinically relevant covariates. In Model 3, both head lean-and-fat mass and head fat mass were positively associated with TBV, whereas age was negatively associated and male sex was associated with larger TBV. Across the nested models, optimism-corrected bootstrap validation showed broadly similar performance, with numerically slightly higher R² values and lower RMSE values for Model 3. Calibration was favorable in both predictor-based primary models (CITL approximately 0; calibration slope approximately 1.00). Bland–Altman analyses showed small mean bias with evidence of proportional bias across the TBV range. Bootstrap validation indicated stable performance. Sensitivity analyses yielded similar results after accounting for measurement interval and across sex strata.ConclusionsDXA-derived head composition measures can provide a practical approximation of MRI-derived TBV in middle-aged and older adults, with good calibration and stable internal validation performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02341-z.Keywords: Brain volume, Magnetic resonance imaging, Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, Body composition, Prediction model, Calibration, Bland–Altman, WASEDA’S Health Study

Introduction

Brain volume derived from structural MRI is widely used as an index of brain structural integrity across the adult lifespan and has been linked to clinically meaningful outcomes. Large MRI studies have consistently shown that total and regional brain volumes decline with aging, with heterogeneity across brain regions and individuals [1, 2]. Moreover, MRI-derived brain morphometry has been used to characterize normative age- and sex-related differences in population cohorts, supporting its value in epidemiologic research [3]. In older adults, brain volumes of the medial temporal lobe particularly have also been associated with memory performance, underscoring the relevance of volumetric measures to functional brain health [4]. In addition, lower total brain volume (TBV) have been associated with poorer congnitive performance and aging [5]. However, brain MRI remains resource-intensive requiring substantial scanner time and post-processing and is not always feasible in large-scale or repeated assessments, motivating the development of simpler surrogate approaches for estimating brain size-related measures in broader settings.

Several attempts have been made to approximate brain volume using non-MRI measurements. Anthropometric indicators such as head circumference have been proposed as a pragmatic proxy for intracranial volume and brain reserve in epidemiologic studies, showing strong correlations with MRI-derived intracranial volume in older adults (e.g., r = 0.69–0.73) [6, 7]. In addition, regression equations relating cranial capacity to brain volume have been reported, suggesting that simple head-size measures may partially capture variance in brain volume [8]. Nevertheless, these approaches have inherent limitations: they primarily reflect skull size and cannot directly quantify tissue composition, and they may be insensitive to age-related changes in brain tissue that occur within a relatively fixed intracranial cavity. Consequently, there remains interest in alternative, scalable measurements that are easy to obtain and potentially more informative than external head dimensions alone.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is a widely available tool for body composition assessment and offers practical advantages, including high precision, low X-ray exposure, and short scanning time [9]. In DXA-based body composition analysis, lean soft tissue generally refers to soft tissue excluding bone and fat, whereas fat mass reflects adipose tissue. Importantly, DXA has increasingly been evaluated as a lower-cost and more accessible alternative to MRI for quantifying adipose tissue compartments, and multiple validation studies have compared DXA-derived estimates of abdominal adipose tissue, particularly visceral adipose tissue against MRI-based references [9–11]. These lines of work collectively support the broader concept that DXA-derived measures can serve as practical surrogates for imaging-derived outcomes when direct imaging is impractical—an idea that may be particularly relevant for large cohort studies, repeated measurements, and potential applications in populations where MRI access is limited.

Despite this, the relationship between DXA-derived head composition and MRI-derived total brain volume (TBV) has not been directly examined as a prediction problem. Prior studies linking body composition or obesity-related indicators to brain structure have typically treated adiposity as an exposure associated with MRI-derived brain structural outcomes including total and regional brain volumes, rather than developing and validating models to predict brain volume from DXA measurements [12, 13]. Because whole-body DXA is often collected as part of health examinations and cohort studies, including large epidemiologic cohorts [14, 15], a DXA-based estimation of TBV could enable large-scale epidemiologic analyses when brain MRI is not available. Therefore, we aimed to develop and internally validate multivariable models to predict MRI-derived TBV using DXA-derived head composition measures. Specifically, we evaluated two DXA predictors including head lean-and-fat mass (reflecting lean soft tissue and fat mass within the head region) and head fat mass (reflecting fat mass within the head region), and quantified prediction accuracy and agreement between predicted and observed TBV to assess whether DXA-based head measurements can provide a practical surrogate for MRI-derived brain volume. Middle-aged and older adults represent a primary target population for epidemiologic studies of brain aging, in which TBV is frequently used as a structural marker related to cognitive health. Accordingly, we focused on this age group to develop a practical proof-of-concept prediction model using routinely collected DXA data for settings where MRI is unavailable [16].

Methods

Participants

This study was conducted as part of the Waseda Alumni’s Sports, Exercise, Daily Activity, Sedentariness and Health Study (WASEDA’S Health Study), an ongoing prospective cohort study of Waseda University alumni and their spouses aged ≥ 40 years. WHS comprises four cohorts (Courses A–D) that differ in measurement components. Course A includes a web-based health survey. Course B includes Course A plus an accelerometer-based assessment. Course C includes Courses A and B plus a standardized health examination conducted at multiple sites nationwide. Course D includes Courses A and B plus comprehensive health and physical fitness assessments. In the present study, we used dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) data obtained from participants who attended the Course D examination, which has been conducted since March 2015. We also used brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data collected in an ancillary brain MRI project that has been offered to Course D participants since 2023. For the present analysis, brain MRI data acquired through July 2025 were eligible for inclusion. The Course D examination and the brain MRI assessment were conducted on separate days. Accordingly, the final analytic sample for the present study was determined based on the availability of matched Course D DXA data and brain MRI data.

Among the 355 individuals who participated in the brain MRI project, we excluded 29 participants whose Course D examination and brain MRI assessment were more than 1 year apart, 7 participants with missing DXA data (MRI only), 1 participant with marked head rotation on DXA, and 4 participants with insufficient image quality (image blurring/haze, n = 1; excessive head tilt, n = 1; abnormally increased signal intensity, n = 1; regional signal loss, n = 1) on MRI. Consequently, 314 participants were included in the final analyses (Table 1). No participants in the final analytic sample had a current or prior history of central nervous system demyelinating disease (e.g., multiple sclerosis) or arachnoidal cyst.

The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee of Waseda University and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Anthropometric measurements

Participants attended the D-course visit between 08:30 and 10:30 h after an overnight fast of at least 12 h, during which anthropometric measurements and DXA assessments were conducted by a team of trained investigators. For the brain MRI visit, participants were asked to avoid caffeine and nicotine within 2 h before the visit and to refrain from alcohol consumption from waking on the day of the scan until completion of the measurement. Height was measured using the same stadiometer (YHS-200D, YAGAMI Inc.). Body weight was measured using the same electronic scale (MC-980 A, Tanita Corp.), with the participants wearing light clothing and no shoes. BMI was calculated as body weight (kg) divided by the square of the body height (m).

MRI acquisition and analysis

MRI data were acquired on a 3 T GE Healthcare system (SIGNA Premier) at Waseda University using a 48-channel head coil for signal reception. A high-resolution T1-weighted structural image was acquired using a three-dimensional magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence on the same scanner and head coil. Images were obtained in the sagittal plane. The imaging parameters were as follows: repetition time = 2486 ms, echo time = 3.47 ms, inversion time = 1000 ms, flip angle = 8°, with an isotropic voxel size of 0.8 mm. The field of view was 256 × 256 mm² with a phase field of view of 1.00. The acquisition matrix size was 320 in the phase-encoding direction and was reconstructed to 512. Parallel imaging was applied with an in-plane acceleration factor of 2 and an out-of-plane acceleration factor of 2.

DICOM files were converted to NIfTI format and organized in the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) using dcm2bids, which calls dcm2niix for DICOM-to-NIfTI conversion. T1-weighted images were segmented in SPM12 using the unified segmentation approach [17], which estimates posterior tissue probability maps for six tissue classes (gray matter [GM], white matter [WM], cerebrospinal fluid [CSF], skull, non-brain soft tissue, and other/air). Tissue volumes were computed from the native-space tissue probability maps by summing voxel-wise tissue probabilities and multiplying by voxel volume. This approach is widely used in structural MRI studies, and scan–rescan studies have shown excellent reliability of global tissue volumes derived from T1-weighted imaging [18]. The primary outcome was total brain volume (TBV) defined as GM and WM, and expressed in liters (L).

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry acquisition and analysis

Whole body dual X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scans were performed at Waseda University using a Hologic QDR densitometer and analyzed with version 12.4.3 software (Hologic, Bedford, MA, USA) after calibration by qualified personnel (S.T. and T.M.).

The sub-region analysis mode of DXA was used for data analysis. The sub-region analysis mode of the Hologic DXA software was used to manually define a head ROI within the whole-body DXA image. For the present study, a head region of interest (ROI) was manually defined using the sub-region tool. To quantify head composition, a head region of interest (ROI) was manually defined on the whole-body DXA image using the sub-region tool. The head ROI was defined using an inter-zygomatic line, drawn between the medial borders of the left and right zygomatic bones on the frontal scan image (Fig. 1). From this head ROI, head lean-and-fat mass as well as head fat mass were extracted as predictors. Examiners were blinded to MRI-derived outcomes and to each other’s results. Inter-rater reliability of the extracted head measures were evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with 95% confidence intervals. Inter-rater reliability of the DXA-derived head ROI measures was evaluated using DXA images randomly selected from 10 participants, with two independent examiners placing the head ROI according to the predefined protocol while blinded to each other’s results and MRI-derived outcomes. Inter-rater reliability was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient based on a two-way mixed-effects model for single measurements [19]. The inter-rater ICC(3,1) was 0.885 (95% CI 0.707–0.970) for head lean-and-fat mass and 0.855 (95% CI 0.720–0.962) for head fat mass (n = 10 participants; 20 measurements).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was conducted using R 4.5.1. Participant characteristics are presented as mean ± SD for continuous variables. Distributional assumptions for continuous variables were assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Characteristics included age, height, weight, BMI, and DXA-derived whole body fat mass and lean mass, summarized for the overall participants and stratified by sex.

We developed multivariable linear regression models to predict MRI-derived TBV from DXA-derived head composition measures. Separate model sets were fitted using either (i) DXA-derived head lean-and-fat mass or (ii) DXA-derived head fat mass as the primary predictor. For each predictor, we specified three nested models: Model 1 included the DXA-derived predictor only; Model 2 additionally adjusted for age and sex (individual variables); and Model 3 further adjusted for BMI (anthropometric variable). Regression coefficients are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We reported model development and performance in accordance with the TRIPOD statement [20, 21]. Apparent model performance in the development dataset was summarized using the coefficient of determination (R²) and the root mean square error (RMSE).

To assess internal validity and estimate optimism, we performed bootstrap internal validation with 1,000 resamples. In each resample, participants were sampled with replacement, the model was refitted in the bootstrap sample, and performance metrics (R², RMSE, calibration-in-the-large [CITL], and calibration slope) were evaluated both in the bootstrap sample and in the original dataset. Optimism for each metric was defined as the difference between performance in the bootstrap sample and performance in the original dataset. The mean optimism across bootstrap samples was then subtracted from the apparent performance in the original dataset to obtain optimism-corrected performance estimates. These optimism-corrected estimates were used to compare the prespecified nested models. Model 3 was treated as the primary model for calibration and Bland–Altman evaluations because it was the fully adjusted model including age, sex, and BMI, which were considered clinically relevant covariates for adjustment. Thus, the choice of Model 3 as the primary model was based primarily on its clinical relevance and more comprehensive adjustment, whereas the optimism-corrected bootstrap results were used as supportive information on internal performance across the nested models.

For the primary model, calibration was evaluated in the original dataset using a calibration plot of observed versus predicted TBV and quantified using CITL and the calibration slope, obtained from a linear model regressing observed TBV on predicted TBV (ideal CITL = 0; ideal slope = 1) [22]. CITL assesses whether predictions are systematically shifted upward or downward on average, whereas the calibration slope evaluates whether predictions are too extreme or too modest across the range of values.

Agreement between predicted and observed TBV was assessed using Bland–Altman analysis [23]. Differences were defined as observed minus predicted TBV and plotted against the mean of observed and predicted TBV. Bias was estimated as the mean difference, and 95% limits of agreement were calculated as bias ± 1.96 × SD of the differences. Proportional bias was evaluated by regressing the differences on the means.

As supplementary analyses, we conducted the following evaluations: (1) we refitted the primary linear regression model with additional adjustment for the MRI–DXA measurement interval days, to examine whether timing differences were associated with systematic changes in prediction performance; and (2) we confirmed the calibration and Bland–Altman evaluations separately in men and women for both DXA-derived predictors (head lean-and-fat mass and head fat mass).

Results

Results of the multivariable linear regression models predicting MRI-derived TBV from DXA-derived head composition measures are shown in Table 2 (head lean-and-fat mass) and Table 3 (head fat mass). In Model 1, both DXA-derived head lean-and-fat mass and DXA-derived head fat mass (each scaled per 100 g) were positively associated with TBV (all p < 0.001). These associations remained robust after adjustment for age and sex (Model 2) and further adjustment for BMI (Model 3). In the primary Model 3, head lean-and-fat mass (per 100 g) was associated with a larger TBV (estimate = 0.023; 95% CI 0.019 to 0.026; p < 0.001), and head fat mass (per 100 g) was also associated with a larger TBV (estimate = 0.098; 95% CI 0.083 to 0.112; p < 0.001). Across both predictor sets, age was negatively associated with TBV (Model 3: estimate = − 0.004 per year for head lean-and-fat; estimate = − 0.004 per year for head fat; both p < 0.001), and male sex was associated with a larger TBV (Model 3: head lean-and-fat, p < 0.001; head fat, p < 0.001). BMI was not significantly associated with TBV in Model 3 for either predictor set (head lean-and-fat, p = 0.066; head fat, p = 0.099). First, refitting the primary linear regression model with additional adjustment for the MRI–DXA measurement interval (days) yielded similar performance for both predictor sets (Supplementary Tab S1–2), indicating that timing differences did not materially affect prediction accuracy. Discussion

In this cohort of middle-aged and older adults, DXA-derived head composition measures enabled moderate to good prediction of MRI-derived TBV. Both head lean-and-fat mass and head fat mass showed robust positive associations with TBV after adjustment for age, sex, and BMI, and the prediction equations could be provided to support reproducibility. Using the prespecified primary models, calibration plots and calibration metrics suggested good overall calibration, and bootstrap internal validation indicated stable performance with limited optimism. Agreement analyses further suggested small mean bias, although proportional bias was observed, implying that prediction errors varied across the TBV range.

Anthropometric and imaging literature supports the notion that head related measures can serve as surrogates of intracranial size and brain reserve. Previous studies reported that head circumference is strongly correlated with MRI-derived intracranial volume in older adults, and other head size proxies also explain substantial variance in intracranial volume [6, 24]. These studies linking cranial capacity and brain volume further suggest a predictable relationship between cranial size and brain volume across adulthood. Since the DXA head ROI reflects projected composition of a craniofacial region, it may incorporate multiple contributors, including cranial size and extra-cranial tissues. Collectively, these observations suggest that, once a whole-body DXA scan is available, a head ROI derived from the same image could offer a scalable approach to approximate brain size-related metrics more directly than external tape measures alone.

The similar performance of head lean-and-fat mass and head fat mass suggests that the predictive signal is not confined to a single compartment. The lean-and-fat measure may function as a proxy for overall head soft tissue mass (and indirectly head size), whereas fat may be more sensitive to adipose containing components within the defined region. Importantly, the brain is a lipid-rich organ, and myelin—abundant in white matter—is particularly enriched in lipids, with lipid accounting for the majority of myelin dry mass [25, 26]. This biological context provides a plausible rationale for why composition-related metrics could correlate with MRI-derived volumetric measures at the population level, even though DXA cannot isolate intracranial tissues and the fat compartment on DXA does not directly quantify myelin. The negative age coefficient in the prediction equations is consistent with extensive MRI literature showing that total brain volume declines with healthy aging [2]. Likewise, male sex was associated with larger TBV in our models, aligning with evidence that males, on average, have larger global brain volumes and intracranial volumes than females [27, 28]. Importantly, head intracranial volume is often considered a marker of maximally attained brain size and is relatively stable in later life even as brain tissue volume decreases [29], supporting the conceptual separation between stable cranial size and age-related brain tissue loss.

From a benchmarking perspective, our explained variance (R² = 0.62–0.63) was somewhat higher than what has been reported for simple head-size proxies when used to predict intracranial size (e.g., head circumference and intracranial volume correlations corresponding to approximately R² = 0.5 in older adults) [6, 24]. Although these benchmarks are not directly comparable because they target intracranial size rather than brain tissue volume, they provide context that our DXA-based models achieve potentially better predictive performance in a pragmatic setting. Notably, beyond discrimination-like summaries such as R² and RMSE, we observed favorable calibration (CITL near 0 and slope near 1), which is particularly important for prediction models intended for quantitative estimation. Together with stable bootstrap internal validation results, these findings suggest that DXA-based head measures can provide a practically useful approximation of TBV in similar populations, while motivating external validation across devices and settings.

This study has several practical strengths. DXA is widely available, involves low radiation exposure, and can be completed rapidly, making it attractive for scalable assessment with minimal participant burden. In addition, we provided transparent prediction equations and reported model performance with internal validation in accordance with recommended prediction-model reporting practices. This study has several limitations. First, proportional bias in Bland–Altman analysis implies that agreement depends on the TBV magnitude. Bland–Altman analyses indicated small mean bias, with only limited evidence of proportional bias across the TBV range. From a practical perspective, these findings support interpreting estimated TBV primarily within the central range of the population distribution, while exercising caution at the extremes. Bland–Altman methodology emphasizes that, even when mean bias is small, limits of agreement and any proportional bias should guide interpretation, particularly when applying estimates to individuals or to values at the extremes of the distribution [2, 30]. Second, the DXA head ROI is not anatomically specific to intracranial structures and inevitably includes non-brain tissues; thus, the model should be interpreted as a practical surrogate rather than a direct brain measurement. Third, because participants were volunteers from an alumni-based cohort, generalizability to the broader population may be limited and selection bias is possible. In addition, because the present study did not include participants with major neurological or other disease-specific conditions, the applicability of these models to clinical populations remains uncertain. Lastly, although we restricted the MRI–DXA interval to within one year and observed similar performance after additional adjustment for the interval, residual temporal mismatch may still contribute to prediction error because TBV changes over time.

Conclusion

In this cohort, DXA-derived head composition measures provided a practically useful approximation of MRI-derived TBV, with moderate-to-good predictive performance (R² = 0.62–0.63; RMSE = 0.059–0.060) and favorable calibration (CITL = 0; calibration slope = 1) in internal validation. Agreement analyses indicated small mean bias, although proportional bias suggests caution when interpreting individual-level estimates at the lower and higher ends of the TBV distribution.
