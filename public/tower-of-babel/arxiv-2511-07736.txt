Improved Bounds for Context-Dependent Evolutionary Models Using Sequential Monte Carlo
Joseph Mathews, Scott C. Schmidler

Abstract
Statistical inference in evolutionary models with site-dependence is a long-standing challenge in phylogenetics and computational biology. We consider the problem of approximating marginal sequence likelihoods under dependent-site models of biological sequence evolution. We prove a polynomial mixing time bound for a Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithm that samples the conditional distribution over latent sample paths, when the chain is initialized with a warm start. We then introduce a sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) algorithm for approximating the marginal likelihood, and show that our mixing time bound can be combined with recent importance sampling and finite-sample SMC results to obtain bounds on the finite sample approximation error of the resulting estimator. Our results show that the proposed SMC algorithm yields an efficient randomized approximation scheme for many practical problems of interest, and offers a significant improvement over a recently developed importance sampler for this problem. Our approach combines recent innovations in obtaining bounds for MCMC and SMC samplers, and may prove applicable to other problems of approximating marginal likelihoods and Bayes factors.

Let

\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n})

10

[

,

]

denote the state of the sequence at time

. Standard evolutionary models assume each site

evolves according to a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) with rate matrix

[
10
]
. Typically, the processes

\displaystyle\Pr(\mathbf{x}_{T}=\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{x})=\prod^{n}_{i=1}p_{(T,\mathbf{Q})}(y_{i}\mid x_{i})=\prod^{n}_{i=1}(e^{T\mathbf{Q}})_{x_{i},y_{i}}

(1)

Some well-known choices for

[

]

[

While independent site models are appealing in their simplicity and computational convenience, they fail to capture known important features of biological evolution that create dependence among sites; examples include CpG di-nucleotide mutability
[

A variety of

However, computing marginal sequence likelihoods exactly under these models of site dependence is difficult or intractable since the corresponding likelihood no longer factors.

As a result, Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms have been proposed which address statistical inference in these problems by sampling unobserved sequence evolution paths from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
, with the desired transition probability obtained by marginalization over all such paths

35
,
17
,

However, MCMC has important disadvantages for use in evaluating likelihoods within iterative sampling (Bayesian) or optimization (MLE) algorithms, due to its inherently serial nature

]
, the need to assess convergence empirically

,
6
]

and the difficulty of doing so in high-dimensions

]

and the rarity of available quantitative mixing time and approximation error bounds

,
19

. Recently

proposed an alternative approach based on importance sampling, using an ISM as an instrumental distribution. This approach is attractive in its ability to leverage the substantial body of existing phylogenetics software, which often provides the ability to sample evolutionary paths under the site independence assumption. However, while the the sample complexity of this importance sampler grows much slower than the problem dimension (sequence length
n
), the complexity nonetheless grows exponentially in the number
r
of observed mutations, rendering the importance sampling complexity prohibitively large for many applied problems of interest.

Our results are two-fold. First, we establish a
upper bound on the mixing time for a component-wise Metropolis algorithm for this problem
[
35
,
23
]
under a warm start. Although this algorithm has been used in applications,
[
35
,
23
]
, to our knowledge this bound constitutes the first rigorous convergence rate analysis. The main technical difficulty addressed in doing so is the failure of the density ratio between the ISM and DSM models
\mu
and

to be uniformly bounded. This is because there is no limit on the number of possible unobserved jumps (mutations followed by subsequent reversion mutations) along any endpoint-conditioned path from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
. Thus to establish our result, we bound the

Our second contribution is a sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) algorithm for approximating marginal sequence likelihoods under DSMs, along with corresponding finite sample error bounds on the resulting estimator, establishing a randomized approximation scheme for this problem with a sample complexity that significantly improves on previous results
[

with the context-dependence ‘tempered’, such that the initial distribution

This result combines our newly-obtained mixing time bound for the Metropolis algorithm with recent results of

\pi_{v-1}

denotes total variation distance.

2.3
An SMC Algorithm for DSMs

Here, we introduce an alternative to the importance sampling scheme of
Mathews and Schmidler 2025a
, based on sequential Monte Carlo
[
7
,
4
]
. This SMC scheme replaces the single-stage importance sampling of
Mathews and Schmidler 2025a
with
a multi-stage procedure which more finely controls the variance. Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) introduces a set of intermediate “bridging" distributions, along with resampling, to form a telescoping product estimator for the marginal likelihood
p_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}})}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
. This reduces the
L^{2}
distance required by any individual IS estimation step, where

\displaystyle L^{2}(\pi,\mu):=\int_{\scrP}\left(\frac{\pi(\Pa\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\mu(\Pa\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}\right)^{2}\mu(d\Pa\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

is the squared
L^{2}(\mu)
norm of
\pi/\mu
.
The SMC algorithm introduced here sequentially samples from a sequence of distributions
\mu=\pi_{0},\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\ldots,\pi_{V}=\pi
all defined on
\scrP
. Let
q_{v}
denote the corresponding unnormalized densities for
v\in\{0,\ldots,V\}
, so

\displaystyle\pi_{v}(\Pa)=q_{v}(\Pa)/z_{v},

(15)

with
z_{v}=\int_{\scrP}q_{v}(\Pa)d\nu(\Pa)
the normalizing constant of
\pi_{v}
. The algorithm proceeds as follows:

Algorithm 1

Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) Sampler for DSMs

1:

Initialization:
Sample
\hat{\Pa}_{0}^{(1)},\ldots,\hat{\Pa}_{0}^{(N)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{iid}}}}{{\sim}}\pi_{0}
.

2:

for

v=1,\ldots,V

do

3:

Resampling:
For
i=1,\ldots,N
, sample
\tilde{\Pa}_{v}^{(i)}=\hat{\Pa}_{v-1}^{(i)}
with probability

4:

\displaystyle\frac{w_{v}(\hat{\Pa}_{v-1}^{(i)})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{v}(\hat{\Pa}_{v-1}^{(j)})}
, where
w_{v}(\Pa)=q_{v}(\Pa)/q_{v-1}(\Pa)
.

5:

Mutation:
For
i=1,\ldots,N
, sample

\hat{\Pa}^{(i)}_{v}\mid\tilde{\Pa}^{(i)}_{v}\sim\tK^{s}_{v}(\tilde{\Pa}^{(i)}_{v},\cdot)
,
where
\tK_{v}
is an ergodic
\pi_{v}
-invariant Markov kernel.

6:

end

for

We define the run time of the SMC sampler as
NVs
, which is the total number of Markov transition steps required in a single run of the algorithm. Here, we choose
\pi_{0},\ldots,\pi_{V}
to be a sequence of ‘tempered’ DSMs with decreasing interaction strength as follows. Let
0=\beta_{0}<\beta_{1}<\ldots<\beta_{V}=1
denote a set of inverse temperatures and define

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{i,v}(b;\tilde{x}_{i})=\gamma_{i}(b;x_{i})\phi^{\beta_{v}}(b;\tilde{x}_{i})\quad\text{ for }\;b\in\scrA\setminus x_{i}.

(16)

The (unnormalized) conditional joint density (
6
) of a path
\Pa
from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
under the tempered model becomes

\displaystyle\tP_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{v})}(\mathbf{y},\Pa\mid\mathbf{x}):=\left[\prod^{m(\Pa)}_{j=1}\tilde{\gamma}_{s^{j},v}(b^{j};\tilde{x}^{j-1}_{s^{j}})\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta^{t}(j-1)\tilde{\gamma}_{v}(\cdot;\mathbf{x}^{j-1})}\right]\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta^{t}(m)\tilde{\gamma}_{v}(\cdot;\mathbf{y})}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbf{x}(m(\Pa))=\mathbf{y}}(\Pa),

(17)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{v}
is the
a^{n}\times a^{n}
rate matrix obtained from (
16
). This defines a sequence of endpoint-conditioned path distributions
\mu=\pi_{0},\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{V}=\pi
with

\displaystyle\pi_{v}(\Pa):=\pi_{v}(\Pa\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{\tP_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{v})}(\mathbf{y},\Pa\mid\mathbf{x})}{\int_{\scrP}\tP_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{v})}(\mathbf{y},\Pa\mid\mathbf{x})\nu(d\Pa)}=\frac{\tP_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{v})}(\mathbf{y},\Pa\mid\mathbf{x})}{z_{v}}.

(18)

Here
z_{v}=p_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{v})}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
denotes the transition probability under the DSM with rates (
16
)
. Define the product estimator of
z_{v}
by the recursion

\displaystyle\hat{z}_{v}(\hat{\Pa}^{(1:N)}_{1:v})=\hat{z}_{v}:=z_{0}\prod^{v}_{v^{\prime}=1}\hat{z}_{v^{\prime}}

and so an estimate of
p_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}})}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
is obtained by

\displaystyle\hat{z}_{V}(\hat{\Pa}^{(1:N)}_{1:V})=\hat{z}_{V}:=z_{0}\prod^{V}_{v=1}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}w_{v}(\hat{\Pa}^{(i)}_{v-1})\right).

(19)

We let
\tK_{v}
denote the
\pi_{v}
-invariant blocked component-wise Metropolis chain (
12
) defined in Section
2.2
that randomly selects a block of mutated sites and proposes a joint update to the paths at all sites in the block. Similarly, let
\tau_{v}(\epsilon,\omega)
be the
\omega
-warm mixing time for the kernel
\tK_{v}
. Denote by
\tK_{1},\ldots,\tK_{V}
the mutation kernels targeting
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{V}
, respectively.

3
Main Results

We now state the main results of the paper, which concern the convergence rate of the MCMC algorithm defined in Section
2.2
and the sample-size requirements for approximating the marginal likelihood
p_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}})}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
using the SMC algorithm introduced in Section
2.3
(Algorithm
1
) using the MCMC algorithm as a mutation kernel. Supporting results are established in the following sections. Of primary interest is the scaling of these quantities with the size of the input problem; here measured by the length
n
of the input sequences.
As we will see, the number of observed mutations
r=\text{d}_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
and the time interval
T
also play important roles; thus we must consider the relative growth of
r(n)
and
T(n)
as
n
increases. Luckily, there is a natural interval of interest for
T
determined by
n
and
r
, centered at
r/n

[
30
]
. Hence we will adopt the following assumption, the justification for which is discussed immediately after:

Assumption 1
.

The time interval
T(n)=\mathcal{O}(\frac{r(n)}{n})
and the observed mutation count
r(n)=\mathcal{O}(n^{\frac{1}{2}})
.

In what follows, we often write
r
and
T
instead of
r(n)
and
T(n)
for brevity, except where we wish to emphasize the dependence explicitly.

The efficiency of the MCMC and SMC algorithms depend critically on the assumption that
T=\mathcal{O}(r/n)
, i.e. that
T
not be too far from
r/n
.
Because
T
and mutation rates are not simultaneously identifiable, rate matrices are commonly scaled to one expected substitution per site per unit time, making
r/n
– a well known measure of genetic distance often called the
p-distance
– a natural estimate of
T
. However under DSMs, estimators of
T
such as the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) or posterior mean are not available in closed form and require iterative optimization or MCMC sampling, with the marginal likelihood evaluated at each iteration.
Mathews and Schmidler 2025b
show that the likelihood decays exponentially for values of
T
far from
r/n
, and therefore the posterior distribution of
T
concentrates close to
r/n
, under any
reasonable prior distribution, so that larger values of
T
can be safely omitted from consideration in such algorithms without compromising their accuracy.

Our first main result is a bound on the
\omega
-warm mixing time of the blocked component-wise Metropolis chain introduced in Section
2.2
. This bound depends on the size of the largest subset of observed mutations having overlapping contexts. Formally, let

\displaystyle r_{\star}\coloneqq\max\{m:\exists i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{m}\in\mathcal{S}\text{ with }\mathcal{C}_{i_{j}}\cap\mathcal{C}_{i_{j^{\prime}}}\neq\emptyset\text{ for some $j^{\prime}<j$}\},

so
r_{\star}
is the largest component (connected subgraph) in the connectivity graph of
\mathcal{S}
.
We have the following result for
k/2
-nearest-neighbor models such as the CpG model (
3
) and the S5F model of somatic hypermutation
[
50
]
:

Theorem 1
.

Suppose the rate function
\gamma_{i}
at each site
i
depends only on its immediate
k/2
neighbors to the left and right, and Assumption
1
holds. Then the blocked component-wise Metropolis-Hastings chain with blocks given by the connected components of
\mathcal{S}
has
\omega
-warm mixing time upper bounded by

\displaystyle\tau\left(\epsilon,\omega\right)=\mathcal{O}\big(\exp(c\cdot r_{\star})\big),

for error tolerance
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and model-dependent constant
c\in(0,\infty)
.

Theorem
1
shows that the computational complexity of approximately sampling from
\pi(\Pa\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
under the block chain, when initialized according to a warm start, grows
exponentially only in the size of the largest
contiguous block
of observed mutation contexts, denoted
r_{\star}
. In practice
r_{\star}
is often significantly smaller than the total number of mutations
r
on which the importance sampler of
Mathews and Schmidler 2025a
depends exponentially. As we will see, this mixing time bound enables a similar complexity bound for the SMC algorithm (Algorithm
1
), leading to a significant reduction in computational complexity for approximating
p_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}})}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
using SMC compared to the importance sampling approach of
Mathews and Schmidler 2025a
:

Theorem 2
.

Suppose each site
i
depends on its immediate
k/2
neighbors to the left and right, and Assumption
1
holds.
Then Algorithm
1

approximates the marginal sequence likelihood
p_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}})}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
with
\epsilon
-relative error in time

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\big(\exp(c\cdot r_{\star})\big),

(20)

where
c\in(0,\infty)
is a model-dependent constant.

Theorem
2
says that the SMC algorithm (Algorithm
1
) provides a significant improvement in computational complexity compared to the importance sampler studied in
[
29
]
. Indeed, we will see that SMC provides a fully polynomial randomized approximation scheme (FPRAS) for the worst-case CpG model problem used to establish the exponential
lower
bound for the importance sampler in Theorem 2 of
[
29
]
, thus providing an
exponential
speed-up in runtime.

Theorems
1
and
2
apply to DSMs where the context
\mathcal{C}_{i}
of each site
i
is restricted to the
k
contiguous nearest neighbors (Assumption
2
in section
4.1.1
below). DSMs with long-range interactions (e.g. Example 3 in section
2.1
) can violate this condition. The following result generalizes Theorems
1
and
2
to such “non-neighboring" contexts:

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathscr{I}
be any partition of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
and let

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{j,\text{e}}\coloneqq\{i\in\mathcal{I}_{j}:\mathcal{C}_{i}\cap\mathcal{I}_{j}^{c}\neq\emptyset\}

denote the set of
edge sites
in
\mathcal{I}_{j}
. If
\mathscr{I}
satisfies

\max_{j}|\mathcal{I}_{j,e}|=\mathcal{O}(r)

and
x_{i}=y_{i}
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}_{j,e}
(no observed mutations at edge sites),
then under Assumption
1

1.

the
\omega
-warm mixing time of the component-wise Metropolis-Hastings chain is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\tau\left(\epsilon,\omega\right)=\mathcal{O}\big(\exp(c\cdot\min_{\mathscr{I}}r_{\star}(\mathscr{I}))\big),

for error tolerance
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and model-dependent constant
c\in(0,\infty)
, and

2.

Algorithm
1
approximates the marginal sequence likelihood
p_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}})}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
with
\epsilon
-relative error in time

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\exp(c^{\prime}\cdot\min_{\mathscr{I}}r_{\star}(\mathscr{I}))),

(21)

with
c^{\prime}\in(0,\infty)
a model-dependent constant.

Theorem
3
thus provides conditions under which the marginal likelihood
p_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}})}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
(
5
) can be efficiently approximated even in DSMs exhibiting long-range dependencies.

In this section we provide the proof of Theorem

4.1
Background and Notation

Define the inner product
\langle f,g\rangle_{\pi}:=\int f(x)g(x)\pi(dx)
. Our approach to bounding
\tau(\epsilon,\omega)
(defined in (
14
)) will frequently involve the
spectral gap
of a Markov kernel
\tK
:

\displaystyle\Gap(\tK):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in L^{2}(\pi)\\
\V_{\pi}[f]\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\tK}(f,f)}{\V_{\pi}[f]},

(22)

where
\mathcal{E}_{\tK}(f,f)=\langle f,(I-\tK)f\rangle_{\pi}
is the Dirichlet form and
\V_{\pi}[f]=\langle f,f\rangle_{\pi}-\langle f,1\rangle_{\pi}
. The spectral gap characterizes the rate at which
\tK
converges to
\pi
. For example, a standard argument (see e.g.
[
28
]
) gives

\displaystyle\tau(\epsilon,\omega)\leq\frac{\log(2\epsilon^{-1})+\log(\omega-1)}{\Gap(\tK)}.

(23)

Moreover, motivated by the notion of the

-conductance
[
25
]
,
Atchadé 2021

showed that under a warm start it suffices to bound
\Gap(\tK_{\mid\scrP_{0}})
for any
\scrP_{0}\subset\scrP
satisfying
\pi(\scrP_{0})\geq 9/10
, where
\tK_{\mid\scrP_{0}}
denotes the
restriction of

\tK
to the subset
\scrP_{0}
:

\displaystyle\tK_{\mid\scrP_{0}}(\Pa,A):=\tK(\Pa,A)+\delta_{A}(\Pa)\tK(\Pa,\mathscr{P}^{c}_{0})\quad\text{ for }A\subset\scrP_{0}

(24)

(Note that
\tK_{\mid\scrP_{0}}
is reversible with respect to
\pi_{\mid\scrP_{0}}=\pi\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\scrP_{0}}/\pi(\scrP_{0})
when
\tK
is reversible with respect to
\pi
.) For technical reasons, we will assume that
\tK
is
lazy
and remains in its current state with probability
1/2
so that
\tK(\Pa,\Pa)\geq 1/2
; if necessary this can be ensured by taking
\tK^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}(I+\tK)
. A bound on
\Gap(\tK^{\prime})
implies a bound on

as the two quantities differ only by a factor of

.

Theorem 4
.

(
Atchadé 2021
)
Let

be fixed. Assume
\tK
is reversible and lazy. If

, then

\displaystyle\tau(\epsilon,\omega)\leq\frac{\log(2\epsilon^{-2})+\log(\omega^{2})}{\Gap(\tK_{\mid\scrP_{0}})}.

We will refer to the block-update chain
\tK
(
12
) defined in
Section
2.3
as a
product chain
in the special case that

is a product distribution, with
\pi_{j}
a distribution defined on
\scrP_{\mathcal{I}_{j}}
. Later we will use the following result regarding product chains:

Theorem 5
.

(
Diaconis and
Saloff-Coste 1996
)

Let
\tK
be a product chain. Then

\displaystyle\Gap(\tK)=\frac{1}{B}\min_{j}\Gap(\tK_{(j)}).

Proof Overview

.

Let
\scrP_{0}=\cap^{B}_{j=1}\scrP_{0,j}

\displaystyle\tK_{\mid\scrP_{0}}(\Pa,d\Pa^{\prime})=\frac{1}{B}\sum^{B}_{i=1}\tK_{(j)\mid\scrP_{0,j}}(\Pa_{\mathcal{I}_{j}},d\Pa_{\mathcal{I}_{j}}^{\prime})\delta_{\Pa_{\mathcal{I}_{[-j]}}}(d\Pa^{\prime}_{\mathcal{I}_{[-j]}}).

\hat{\gamma}_{i}(b;\tilde{x}_{i})=\begin{cases}\gamma_{i}(b;x_{i})&\text{ for }i\in\scrD\\

(26)

\hat{\mathbf{Q}}
be the

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}(\Pa):=\hat{\pi}(\Pa\mid\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\propto\tP_{(T,\hat{\mathbf{Q}})}(\mathbf{y},\Pa\mid\mathbf{x}).

\mathcal{I}_{j}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{j}

4.2
Main Results for Mixing Times

The bounds below are stated in terms of the following quantities. For a subset of sites
\mathcal{A}\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
we let
\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{A}}
denote the corresponding subsequences and define

\displaystyle n_{\mathcal{A}}:=\left|\mathcal{A}\right|\quad\quad r_{\mathcal{A}}:=\text{d}_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{A}},\mathbf{y}_{\mathcal{A}})\quad\quad\zeta_{\mathcal{A}}:=r_{\mathcal{A}}+r_{\mathcal{A}}T+(n_{\mathcal{A}}-r_{\mathcal{A}})T^{2}.

(30)

In addition, let
\zeta\coloneqq\zeta_{\{1,\ldots,n\}}
denote the special case that
\A=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Key to our analysis is the following bound on the MGF of
m(\Pa_{\A})
, the number of jumps in sequence path
\Pa
which occur at sites in the subset
\A

\pi

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
be a set of contiguous site indices and
\theta\in(0,\infty)
. If
\pi
is a
k
-neighborhood
DSM (Assumption
2
), then there exists a model-dependent constant
\lambda(\theta)
such that

\E_{\pi}[\theta^{m(\Pa_{\mathcal{A}})}]\leq e^{\lambda(\theta)\zeta_{\mathcal{A}}}.

(31)

Lemma
2
provides a bound on the MGF
M(t)
of
m(\Pa)
(taking
\theta=e
). Applying the Chernoff bound
\Pr[m(\Pa_{\mathcal{A}})>M]\leq\E[e^{m(\Pa_{\mathcal{A}})}]e^{-M}
gives that the number
m(\Pa_{\mathcal{A}})
of jumps in
\mathcal{A}
is bounded above by
\lambda(e)\zeta_{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{O}(\zeta_{\mathcal{A}})

with high probability, decaying as
e^{-(M-\lambda(e)\zeta_{\mathcal{A}})}
, while applying Jensen’s inequality
\E_{\pi}[e^{m(\Pa_{\mathcal{A}})}]\geq e^{\E_{\pi}[m(\Pa_{\mathcal{A}})]}
gives a bound on the expected number of jumps in
\mathcal{A}
:

\displaystyle\E_{\pi}[m(\Pa_{\mathcal{A}})]\leq\mathcal{O}(\zeta_{\mathcal{A}}).

(32)

Therefore, the expected number of jumps in
\mathcal{A}
is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(r_{\mathcal{A}}+r_{\mathcal{A}}T+(n_{\mathcal{A}}-r_{\mathcal{A}})T^{2})
, which under Assumption
1
is
\mathcal{O}(1)
when
r_{\mathcal{A}}=0
.
This fact will play an important role in bounding the number of extra jumps among the edge sites
\scrD
of a partition defined in Section
5
, recalling that
r_{\scrD}=0
for
island partitions
(Assumption
3
).

We will use Lemma
2
to find a high probability subset of paths
\scrP_{0}\subset\scrP
on which
m(\Pa)
is uniformly bounded. The set
\scrP_{0}
will play an important role in bounding the warm mixing time of
\tK
to establish Theorem

.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and for
\mathcal{A}\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
define the event

\displaystyle\scrP_{0}(\epsilon,\mathcal{A})=\{\Pa:m(\Pa_{\mathcal{A}})\leq M_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{A})\},

where

(section

Lemma 5
.

Recall that the spectral gap of a
\pi
-invariant Markov kernel
\tK
is defined by

\displaystyle\Gap(\tK):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in L^{2}(\pi)\\
\V_{\pi}(f)\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\tK}(f,f)}{\V_{\pi}(f)}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in L^{2}(\pi)\\
\V_{\pi}[f]\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\int\int\pi(dx)\tK(x,dy)(f(x)-f(y))^{2}}{\int\int\pi(dx)\pi(dy)(f(x)-f(y))^{2}}.

(40)

Let
\hat{K}
be a
\hat{\pi}
-invariant Markov kernel, with
\hat{\pi}(x)
and
\pi(x)
densities defined on a common state space
\mathcal{X}
and with respect to a common dominating measure
\rho
, i.e.,
\hat{\pi}(A)=\int_{A}\hat{\pi}(x)\rho(dx)
and
\pi(A)=\int_{A}\pi(x)\rho(dx)
for

. Suppose further that

1.

The ratio of each density with respect to
\rho
is bounded:
a_{0}\leq\pi(x)/\hat{\pi}(x)\leq a_{1}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
for some
a_{0},a_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
with
0<a_{0}\leq a_{1}
.

2.

There exists
a_{2}>0
such that for all

\displaystyle\int_{A}\tK(x,dy)(f(x)-f(y))^{2}\;\geq\;a_{2}\int_{A}\hat{\tK}(x,dy)(f(x)-f(y))^{2}\quad\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{X}\text{ and }A\subset\mathcal{X}.

Then
\Gap(\tK)\geq\frac{a_{0}a_{2}}{a_{1}}\Gap(\hat{\tK})
.

Note that
\V_{\pi}[f]\neq 0\iff\V_{\hat{\pi}}[f]\neq 0
and
f\in L^{2}(\pi)\iff f\in L^{2}(\hat{\pi})
since
a_{0}<\pi(x)/\hat{\pi}(x)<a_{1}
uniformly for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. By assumption
\V_{\pi}[f]\leq a_{1}\V_{\hat{\pi}}[f]
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\tK}(f,f)\geq a_{0}a_{2}\mathcal{E}_{\hat{\tK}}(f,f).

Taking the infimum over all non-constant
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
yields the stated bound.

6
Conclusion

Calculation of marginal likelihoods under context-dependent evolutionary models is an important problem in phylogenetics and molecular evolution. Given the extensive machinery available for independent site models, the idea of performing inference under DSMs by importance sampling from ISMs is an attractive one. However, as shown by
Mathews and Schmidler 2025a
the sample complexity of importance sampling on this problem grows exponentially in the number of observed mutations, which can be prohibitively expensive in some real-world applications. Here, we
introduce an SMC algorithm for this problem, and show that this algorithm provides a significant improvement in the sample complexity required to accurately approximate the marginal likelihood. Section
5
shows that when sites in
\mathcal{S}
can be grouped into islands consisting of neighboring mutated sites, the complexity of the SMC algorithm is at most exponential in the size of the largest island, yielding an exponential improvement over the importance sampler. Along the way, we have also obtained a mixing time bound on the component-wise Metropolis algorithm used previously in applications, the first such result for an MCMC algorithm for this problem. It remains an open question whether this mixing time bound can be improved, and whether matching
lower
bounds for this algorithm can be obtained, as well as to explore the effects of alternative MCMC algorithms for the mutation kernel of the SMC algorithm.
It is also worth noting again that our results hold under the assumption that
T=\mathcal{O}(r/n)
and
r^{2}\leq n

(Assumption
1
); this scaling assumption on
T
is justified by the probability concentration tail bound on
T
established in
Mathews and Schmidler 2025b
, but that result differs from Assumption
1
by containing an additional factor of
\log(n)
.

Finally, the proof strategy used here combines several recent results from the literature on Monte Carlo theory. In particular, we (1) established concentration of the target distribution on a restricted set, (2) used that restriction to simplify the obtaining of spectral warm-start mixing bounds on the Markov kernel
[
2
]
, and (3) applied recent results establishing warm-start conditions for SMC
[
28
,
27
]
to obtain finite sample error bounds for approximating the marginal sequence likelihood. This strategy may be of broader interest for analyzing other problems of marginal likelihood and Bayes factor approximation, where posterior concentration bounds may be available, or in some cases already exist, to satisfy the first step.
