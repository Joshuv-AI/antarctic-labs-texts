Joint analysis of atherogenic index of plasma clusters and a body shape index trajectories in incident stroke risk
Authors: Shuang Li, Hongxuan Fan, Tianjiao Li, Shanyi Zhou, Zhuolin Huang, Lei Liu, Yafen Yang, Jiahui Li, Zhaoyu Ren, Yanyan Lu, Weihao Meng, Boda Zhou, Hongqiang Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAs emerging biomarkers for stroke risk, the clinical value of the atherogenic index of plasma and a body shape index has gained increasing attention. However, current research on their combined use for stroke risk stratification remains limited. This study aims to analyze the combined effects of Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP) and A Body Shape Index (ABSI) trajectories to explore their potential contribution to improving stroke risk prediction accuracy.MethodsThe study data were derived from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study conducted between 2011 and 2018, ultimately including 4,942 participants with two AIP measurements and three ABSI measurements collected for each participant. AIP was classified using K-means clustering analysis, and cumulative AIP values were calculated. The latent class trajectory model was employed to identify characteristic ABSI trajectory patterns over time. Cox proportional hazards models were used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs).ResultsThe median follow-up duration in China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) was 3.0 years, during which 395 of 4,942 participants (7.99%) developed stroke. Adjusted multivariable Cox regression models demonstrated that both the high AIP clustering combined with high ABSI trajectory model (HR = 2.256, 95% CI: 1.346–3.781, P = 0.002) and the high cumulative AIP with high ABSI trajectory model (HR = 2.455, 95% CI: 1.514–3.983, P < 0.001) showed significant associations with stroke in their respective groups, with both associations remaining robust in sensitivity analyses. The AIP clustering combined with ABSI trajectory model exhibited the highest diagnostic performance for stroke (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC]: 0.612).ConclusionThe combined prediction of AIP and ABSI enables earlier identification of stroke risk in the general population, demonstrating significant clinical value for stroke prevention and treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02771-8.Keywords: Atherogenic index of plasma, A body shape index, Stroke, Cluster analysis, Trajectory analysis, AIP cluster, ABSI trajectory, Cumulative AIP, CHARLS

Introduction

Stroke is a complex and devastating cerebrovascular disease characterized by the sudden disruption of blood flow to the brain, leading to ischemic or hemorrhagic damage. This pathological process can result in severe neurological deficits, long-term disability, and even death, imposing a significant burden on healthcare systems and societies worldwide [1]. As one of the leading causes of mortality and morbidity globally, stroke affects millions of people each year, with its prevalence expected to rise due to aging populations and increasing risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and obesity [2, 3]. The sequential time series analysis reveals a significant and substantial decline in stroke mortality rates in the USA from 1975 to 2019, with a more pronounced decrease observed for ischemic strokes compared to hemorrhagic strokes [4]. However, recent data indicate that stroke incidence in China remains a significant public health concern. According to the latest statistics, China experiences over 2.5 million new stroke cases annually, with ischemic stroke accounting for approximately 70% of these cases. Moreover, the age-standardized incidence rate of stroke in China has reached over 336 per 100,000, the highest globally [5]. This high incidence, coupled with the significant disability and mortality rates associated with stroke, underscores the urgent need for enhanced prevention and treatment strategies [6]. The development of stroke is influenced by a multifactorial interplay of genetic, metabolic, and lifestyle factors, with key risk factors including hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking, obesity, physical inactivity, and a history of cardiovascular disease. Effective prevention and management strategies are crucial for reducing the incidence and impact of stroke, particularly in high-risk populations [7, 8]. These strategies often include lifestyle modifications (such as a healthy diet, regular exercise, and smoking cessation) and pharmacological interventions (such as antihypertensive and anticoagulant therapies) to mitigate risk factors and prevent future events.

AIP has emerged as a novel and integrative biomarker that combines key aspects of lipid metabolism and atherogenic risk. It is derived by calculating the logarithm of the triglyceride (TG) to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) ratio, providing a more comprehensive assessment of an individual’s lipid profile and cardiovascular disease risk [9]. From a biological mechanism perspective, the AIP reflects lipid-/atherosclerosis-related pathological processes, including elevated levels of small, dense low-density lipoprotein (sd-LDL), dysfunctional HDL, as well as inflammatory responses and endothelial injury [10, 11]. These processes constitute the critical pathological basis for stroke occurrence. Consequently, the AIP serves as a significant predictive tool for identifying individuals at high risk for stroke and other vascular diseases. By integrating measures of triglycerides and HDL-C, AIP offers a more holistic view of an individual’s lipid status compared to traditional biomarkers such as low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) alone [12]. The ABSI is an innovative anthropometric measure that provides a more nuanced evaluation of body fat distribution, with a particular emphasis on abdominal adiposity [13]. Unlike traditional measures such as body mass index (BMI), which do not account for the distribution of body fat, ABSI offers a more accurate representation of an individual’s body shape and associated cardiovascular risk. Pathophysiologically, ABSI primarily reflects visceral fat accumulation, which is a known source of chronic low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, and impaired vascular reactivity [14]. These pathways are complementary to and may interact with the pathological processes represented by AIP. Furthermore,

ABSI trajectories over time provide a dynamic and informative indicator of health risks related to body shape and fat distribution [15]. These trajectories can reveal patterns of fat accumulation or loss, offering a clearer picture of an individual’s metabolic health and potential risk for obesity-related diseases such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and stroke.

Given that AIP and ABSI represent distinct yet complementary biological pathways—namely, atherogenesis reflected by dyslipidemia and metabolic dysfunction reflected by visceral adiposity—we hypothesized that their combination might exert synergistic effects on stroke risk. This a priori hypothesis regarding their potential interaction provided the rationale for jointly analyzing these two biomarkers. Based on this premise, our study is the first to investigate the combined effect of cumulative AIP exposure and dynamic ABSI changes on stroke risk within a large prospective cohort, and to evaluate their association with long-term cerebrovascular outcomes. Furthermore, this study reveals the synergistic interplay between lipid metabolism dysfunction and body composition changes in the pathogenesis of stroke, which may refine risk stratification strategies and enable more precise risk identification, particularly among populations traditionally considered at low risk. Ultimately, our findings highlight the clinical significance and application potential of integrating metabolic and anthropometric indicators for long-term stroke risk assessment, providing a theoretical foundation for precise prediction and individualized intervention.

Methods

Study design and participants

Figure 1 shows the specific process of this study. The data for this study were derived from a national large-scale longitudinal survey - the CHARLS. The first national baseline survey (Wave 1) of CHARLS was conducted in 2011, covering 17,708 middle-aged and elderly individuals from 28 provinces, 150 counties/districts, and 450 villages/communities across China [16]. The study used standardized questionnaires to collect core information from all participants, with follow-ups conducted every 2–3 years to track health status. Data from four survey waves conducted between 2011 and 2018 were analyzed. The CHARLS methodology and data collection procedures have been thoroughly documented in the literature. The project was approved by the Peking University Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Flowchart of participant screening

The inclusion criteria for this study were: (1) individuals with complete height, weight, and waist circumference data in Waves 1, 2, and 3; (2) individuals with recorded HDL-C and TG levels in Waves 1 and 3. Exclusion criteria comprised participants diagnosed with stroke before Wave 3 or those with missing relevant data. The CHARLS database initially included 17,708 baseline participants in Wave 1. Individuals with missing data on height, weight, waist circumference, HDL-C, or triglycerides, as well as those lacking baseline characteristics in Wave 1, were excluded. Participants with a prior diagnosis of stroke, missing stroke outcome records, or loss to follow-up were also excluded. After these exclusions, a total of 12,766 individuals were removed, resulting in a final analytical cohort of 4,942 participants.

Definitions of outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was incident stroke reported in CHARLS between 2015 and 2018. Stroke occurrence was determined based on self-reported physician diagnosis (“Were you told by your doctor that you had been diagnosed with a stroke?”), consistent with previous studies using CHARLS data [16]. For participants who did not experience stroke during any follow-up wave, the event time was calculated as the last survey date minus the baseline survey date.

AIP cluster analysis and cumulative analysis

This study employed the “cluster” software package to perform K-means clustering analysis on AIP measures from two cohorts, aiming to establish grouping criteria for study subjects. Based on the elbow method, three cluster centers were identified (Fig. S1). Ultimately, three AIP-based clusters were established and participants were categorized accordingly: “Class 1 (low-level group, CHARLS cohort: −0.311, −0.216)”, “Class 2 (moderate-level group, CHARLS cohort: 0.073, 0.041)”, and “Class 3 (high-level group, CHARLS cohort: 0.489, 0.394)” (Fig. 2A-D). The effects of long-term AIP exposure were assessed by employing longitudinal biomarker analysis, whereby time-integrated AIP metrics were calculated to quantify cumulative exposure. Cumulative AIP was calculated as the product of mean baseline and follow-up AIP values and intervening years, capturing both exposure intensity (mean levels) and temporal dimensions, serving as a reliable indicator for assessing chronic disease dose-response relationships.

ABSI trajectory model construction

ABSI was calculated using waist circumference, height, and weight data from Waves 1, 2, and 3 of the CHARLS database. Latent class trajectory modeling (LCTM) was applied to analyze the trajectories of ABSI over time. Following Nagin’s recommendations, a two-stage model selection process was implemented: first determining the optimal number of groups (2–6 classes), then adjusting the polynomial order (linear, quadratic, or cubic) to define trajectory shapes. Time was calculated with 2006 as the reference year, and developmental trajectories were estimated using three-wave ABSI levels and lifestyle scores. Through iterative comparisons of models with varying group numbers and trajectory shapes, the optimal dual-trajectory model was selected based on: minimal Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), minimal Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and maximal entropy. Model reliability was ensured by setting an average posterior probability threshold of ≥ 0.7 for each class and requiring a predicted subgroup membership probability greater than 5% for all identified subgroups. The final model identified three ABSI trajectories: Trajectory 1 (high-decreasing group: initially high ABSI levels with gradual decline), Trajectory 2 (moderate-increasing group: intermediate baseline ABSI with significant upward trend), and Trajectory 3 (stable-low group: consistently low ABSI throughout observation). Model evaluation metrics for the three BRI trajectories are shown in Table S1 Fig. 2E, with latent class density plots providing additional classification evidence (Fig. 2E-H).

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), with between-group comparisons performed using independent samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR), with between-group differences assessed by Kruskal-Wallis H tests. Categorical variables were described using frequencies (n) or proportions (%), with between-group comparisons conducted via chi-square tests. Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to analyze associations between AIP clusters, cumulative AIP, ABSI trajectories, and stroke risk. Nonlinear relationships between cumulative AIP exposure and outcomes were examined using restricted cubic splines (RCS). Model predictive performance was evaluated by time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve area under the curve with 95% confidence intervals for discrimination, while continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) indices were used to verify reclassification performance. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted to further assess model robustness. All statistical analyses were performed using R software, with two-sided P-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographics and baseline characteristics

A total of 4,942 participants from the CHARLS database were included in this study based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 1). Among the included participants, 2,205 (44.6%) were men, and the mean age at baseline was 58.40 ± 8.95 years. The average BIM of the participants was 23.555 ± 3.674. According to the AIP, participants were divided into three groups. Age, sex, alcohol use, smoking status, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, heart problems, and serum biochemical parameters all showed statistically significant variations among the groups (P < 0.001). The baseline characteristics of the 4,942 included participants and the 12,766 excluded individuals are compared in Table S2. Although some continuous variables showed nominal statistical significance (P < 0.05) due to the large sample size, all standardized mean differences (SMDs) were below the threshold of 0.2. This indicates that the differences between the two groups were minimal and not clinically meaningful, supporting the robustness of our final cohort against significant selection bias.

Association between AIP, ABSI and incidence stroke

From 2015 to 2018 a total of 395 new cases of stroke were documented among 4,942 participants. Multivariable Cox regression analysis revealed significant associations between AIP clusters and stroke risk. In the unadjusted model (Model 1), both medium AIP clusters (Class 2: HR = 1.485, 95% CI 1.171–1.882, P = 0.001) and high AIP clusters (Class 3: HR = 1.924, 95% CI 1.461–2.534, P < 0.001) demonstrated significantly elevated stroke risks compared to the low AIP cluster (Class 1) (Table S3). These associations remained statistically significant in the fully adjusted model (Model 3) after accounting for potential confounders, with HRs of 1.362 (95% CI 1.071–1.733, P = 0.012) for Class 2 and 1.623 (95% CI 1.218–2.162, P = 0.001) for Class 3, indicating a dose-response relationship between increasing AIP levels and stroke risk (Table S4). Further analyses evaluated the impact of cumulative AIP exposure on stroke risk. Cumulative AIP exposure analysis showed that each 1-SD increment in AIP was associated with a 28.8% increased stroke risk (HR = 1.288, 95% CI 1.175–1.411, P < 0.001). The hightest cluster (Q3) maintained particularly strong associations in Model 3 (HR = 1.225, 95% CI 1.110–1.351, P < 0.001).

In addition, trajectory analysis of ABSI identified three distinct trajectory classes as the optimal classification, demonstrating significant prognostic value for stroke risk. Multivariable-adjusted Cox models revealed differential stroke risks across ABSI trajectory groups. Participants following the high-risk trajectory (traj3) exhibited a 10.38% event rate (113/1089), representing a 37% increased hazard compared to the reference group (HR = 1.37, 95% CI 1.03–1.82, P = 0.031) after making variable adjustments (Table S5). These findings suggest that ABSI trajectory analysis provides clinically meaningful risk stratification beyond single-timepoint measurements, with the high-risk trajectory particularly identifying individuals who may benefit from targeted monitoring and intervention.

Joint association of AIP and ABSI with stroke

In the joint analyses, combinations of high AIP clusters (class3) and high trajectory cluster (traj3) were associated with the stroke. Specifically, after fully adjusting for confounding factors, compared with the reference group (AIP cluster 1 + ABSI trajectory 1), individuals in the high AIP clusters and the high trajectory cluster had the highest risk (HR 2.256, 95% CI: 1.346–3.781, P = 0.002) (Table 2). In ABSI trajectory 3, compared with the reference group, the stroke risk increased by 83.7% in the medium AIP cluster (HR = 1.837, 95% CI: 1.158–2.912; P = 0.01). For ABSI trajectory 2, the stroke risk increased progressively with higher AIP levels: the medium AIP cluster (HR = 1.618, 95% CI: 1.066–2.455; P = 0.024) and the high AIP cluster (HR = 1.707, 95% CI: 1.065–2.737; P = 0.026). The risk difference (ARD) analysis indicated that, using the low-risk group (AIP Cluster 1 + ABSI Trajectory 1) as reference, the combination of AIP Cluster 3 and ABSI Trajectory 3 (predicted stroke rate 9.07%) demonstrated a significantly higher predicted 3-year stroke risk compared to the seven other combinations (Fig. S2). Further sensitivity analysis was conducted by adjusting for LDL and UA based on model 3(Table S6). The results showed that individuals in the high AIP clusters and the high trajectory cluster had the highest risk of stroke (HR = 2.201, 95% CI: 1.310–3.697, P = 0.003).Moreover, the combined analysis of ABSI and cumulative AIP indicated that participants in the AIP clusters (Q3) and the high-risk trajectory cluster (traj3) had the highest risk of stroke (HR 2.455, 95% CI: 1.514–3.983, P < 0.001) (Table 3) (Fig. 3). In the sensitivity analysis, the associations remained robust in both models (Table S7).Among the combinations of AIP clusters and ABSI trajectories, the highest 3-year stroke risk was observed in ABSI trajectory 3 + AIP cluster 3 (13.32%), representing a threefold increase compared to the lowest risk group (ABSI trajectory 1 + AIP cluster 1, 4.25%) (Fig. S3). Interaction analysis between AIP clusters and ABSI trajectories indicated no significant modifying effect of ABSI trajectories on AIP-associated stroke risk (P = 0.888). Stratified analysis by AIP revealed that in the high AIP group (Class 3, n = 892), both ABSI trajectory 2 (HR 0.72, 95% CI: 0.46–1.12) and ABSI trajectory 1 (HR 0.78, 95% CI: 0.44–1.37) were associated with lower stroke risk compared to ABSI trajectory 3, with the same trend observed in Class 1 and Class 2 (Table S8). Although lower ABSI trajectories consistently demonstrated a protective trend across all AIP strata, the statistical evidence was limited as some confidence intervals included 1.0, potentially due to the small number of events in each stratum (e.g., only 32 events in traj 3), warranting further validation in larger samples.

Subgroup analysis of the association between AIP, ABSI and incidence stroke and its subtype risk

To further investigate the associations between AIP, ABSI, and stroke incidence, subgroup analyses were performed (Fig. 4). Stratified analyses by potential risk factors revealed that the combination of high AIP clusters (class3) and high-risk trajectory cluster (traj3) was associated with a higher incidence of stroke. Specifically, in the female subgroup, the combination of high AIP clusters (class3) and high-risk trajectory cluster (traj3) was associated with an increased risk of stroke (HR = 2.42, 95% CI: 1.02–4.91). Although the male subgroup did not show a statistically significant association, the HR remained above 1, indicating a potential trend. In the age subgroups, individuals aged less than 60 years showed a significant association (HR = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.07–5.41), whereas those aged 60 years or older did not. In the smoking status subgroups, participants without a history of smoking showed a significant association (HR = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.30–4.48). In the alcohol consumption subgroups, participants without a history of alcohol consumption also showed a significant association (HR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.11–3.83). These findings suggest that sex, age, smoking status, and alcohol consumption may play important modulatory roles in the occurrence and development of stroke.

Subgroup analysis of cumulative AIP and ABS

In subgroup interaction analyses, a significant synergistic effect was observed in the additive model (RERI = 0.0988, 95% CI: 0.0830–0.2660). Approximately 4.86% of the disease risk was attributable to this interaction (AP = 0.0486, 95% CI: 0.0082–0.0946). The synergy index (S = 1.1556, 95% CI: 1.0939–1.2776) further supported a positive additive interaction, while no multiplicative interaction was detected (Table S9).

Nonlinear associations of AIP with incidence stroke

The RCS analysis was performed to evaluate potential nonlinear associations of AIP and ABSI with stroke risk (Fig. 5.A). The results indicated that in the overall population, when the cumulative AIP was less than 0.503, lower values of cumulative AIP were associated with a reduced risk of stroke (P = 0.040). However, within the specific subgroups of cumulative AIP, namely AIPQ1, AIPQ2, and AIPQ3, a linear dose-response relationship was observed between cumulative AIP and stroke risk (Fig. 5.B-D).

Predictive ability of AIP and ABSI index for stroke

The ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of different models incorporating AIP and ABSI for stroke risk (Fig. S4). The results showed that the AUC for AIP class alone was 0.568. For ABSI trajectory alone, the AUC was 0.550. When cumulative AIP (categorical) was considered alone, the AUC was 0.574. The combined model of AIP class and ABSI trajectory yielded an AUC of 0.612, indicating improved predictive performance compared to either factor alone. Furthermore, the model combining cumulative AIP (categorical) and ABSI trajectory resulted in an AUC of 0.592, also demonstrating enhanced predictive capacity. These findings suggest that integrating AIP and ABSI trajectory information can enhance the prediction of stroke risk, with the combination of AIP class and ABSI trajectory providing the highest predictive accuracy among the models examined. The calibration curve for the 3-year stroke risk of the combined AIP class and ABSI trajectory model demonstrated a Brier score of 0.072, indicating good model calibration (Fig. S5). Further decision curve analysis demonstrated that the intervention strategy based on combined AIP class and ABSI trajectory prediction model yielded higher net benefits than both the “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies, indicating its potential to provide additional value for clinical decision-making in stroke prevention (Fig. S6).

Sensitivity analysis

The results of the sensitivity analysis showed that the three models based on different combinations of ABSI trajectories and AIP clusters had significant differences in predicting the risk of stroke (Table S10).In Model 1, traj1-Class3, traj2-Class2, traj2-Class3, traj3-Class2, and traj3-Class3 all showed significantly higher stroke risks compared to Class1, with HRs of 2.37 (95% CI: 1.34–4.20, P = 0.003), 1.89 (95% CI: 1.25–2.85, P = 0.002), 2.19 (95% CI: 1.38–3.48, P = 0.001), 2.38 (95% CI: 1.52–3.73, P < 0.001) and 3.04 (95% CI: 1.85–4.98, P < 0.001) respectively. Similar trends were observed in Model 2 and Model 3, where all trajectory-cluster combinations exhibited significantly higher HRs than Class1 (all P < 0.05). These findings indicate that the integration of ABSI trajectories with AIP clustering can effectively stratify stroke risk, providing valuable references for early stroke warning and intervention strategies.

Model comparison analyses

The incremental predictive value of combining ABSI trajectories with AIP clusters was evaluated using the IDI and NRI metrics. The results show that when AIP clustering is combined with ABSI trajectories, the predictive ability of the model is significantly improved (Table S11). Specifically, the model combining AIP clustering and ABSI trajectories had an IDI value of 0.006 (95% CI: 0.002–0.012, P < 0.001) compared with the ABSI trajectory alone. The NRI value was 0.124 (95% CI: 0.03, 0.193, P < 0.001). Furthermore, the model combining cumulative AIP clustering and ABSI trajectories also demonstrated good predictive efficacy, with an IDI value of 0.005 (95% CI: 0.002, 0.01, P = 0.02). The NRI value was 0.121 (95% CI: 0.035, 0.204, P = 0.02). These results suggest that combining AIP clustering or cumulative AIP clustering with ABSI trajectories can effectively improve the prediction accuracy of stroke risk and provide a new perspective and method for clinical risk assessment.

Discussion

This study examined the association between AIP changes, ABSI trajectories, and stroke risk using the large prospective CHARLS cohort. Results demonstrated that individuals with high AIP levels showed significantly increased stroke risk in both cluster and cumulative AIP models. ABSI trajectory analysis revealed three distinct long-term body shape evolution patterns, providing a dynamic perspective that cannot be captured by single time-point measurements. Participants in the “high-declining” ABSI trajectory, despite starting with higher levels, showed a gradual decline over time, potentially reflecting successful lifestyle interventions or metabolic adaptations, with corresponding attenuation of risk profiles. Conversely, individuals in the “moderate-rising” trajectory exhibited continuously increasing ABSI, indicating progressive visceral fat accumulation and deteriorating metabolic health, ultimately elevating stroke risk. Trajectory model analysis demonstrated that the dynamic evolution of ABSI over time carries significant prognostic value. This temporal dimension allows for the identification of individuals with worsening metabolic health even before they are flagged as high-risk by cross-sectional measurements. Multivariable joint models revealed a significant synergistic effect between high AIP clusters and high ABSI trajectories, indicating that the combination of dyslipidemia (reflected by elevated AIP) and adverse body composition changes (reflected by rising ABSI) was associated with the highest stroke risk, with predictive value significantly superior to either indicator alone. This synergistic effect suggests that these factors may exacerbate stroke risk through potentially complementary pathological pathways.

AIP serves as a crucial indicator of atherosclerotic risk and shows an inverse correlation with lipoprotein particle size. Elevated AIP reflects increased concentrations of small, dense LDL (sdLDL) particles [18] and reduced HDL-C particle quantity and functionality. Compared to larger LDL particles, sdLDL particles are smaller, denser, and more susceptible to atherogenic modifications (e.g., oxidation and glycation) that enhance their ability to penetrate vascular endothelium, promote lipid accumulation, and trigger inflammatory responses [19]. Increased AIP not only indicates decreased HDL quantity but also reflects impaired quality and functionality in reverse cholesterol transport [20], antioxidant activity, and anti-inflammatory properties [9]. These factors collectively accelerate atherosclerotic plaque formation. Elevated ABSI suggests visceral fat accumulation, where highly active adipocytes secrete excessive free fatty acids and inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 [21]. Consequently, individuals with high ABSI often exhibit chronic low-grade inflammation and insulin resistance, leading to glucose and lipid metabolism disorders [22] and vascular endothelial dysfunction. Prolonged exposure to inflammatory mediators activates endothelial cells, increasing expression of adhesion molecules like VCAM-1 and promoting leukocyte adhesion and transendothelial migration [23]. Concurrently, endothelial nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability decreases - NO being a key mediator of vasodilation [24]. Although existing literature has not reported a direct correlation between AIP and ABSI, the joint association observed in our study suggests a potential mechanistic interaction between these pathways: visceral adipose tissue-driven inflammation may exacerbate the atherogenicity of dyslipidemia by promoting sdLDL oxidation and impairing HDL function. Conversely, pro-inflammatory lipid profiles may further aggravate adipokine-induced endothelial dysfunction, creating a vicious cycle that synergistically accelerates cerebrovascular injury. This explains why the risk when both factors coexist exceeds simple additive effects.

Previous studies have confirmed that both TG/HDL-C ratio and BMI serve as biomarkers of lipid metabolism disorders and core indicators of insulin resistance [25, 26]. Insulin resistance can lead to various conditions including hyperglycemia, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, all of which are closely associated with arteriosclerosis, inflammatory responses, endothelial dysfunction, and thrombosis, thereby accelerating cerebral atherosclerosis progression [27]. Therefore, as a cost-effective and practical approach, the TG/HDL-C ratio is regarded as the gold standard for assessing insulin sensitivity [25]. The AIP used in this study is derived from the logarithmic transformation of the triglyceride to HDL-C ratio (TG/HDL-C). This transformation mitigates the influence of extreme values and approximates a normal distribution, thereby enhancing the robustness and interpretability of lipid profile analyses [28]. BMI fails to differentiate between adipose tissue and lean mass composition, nor does it reflect body fat distribution patterns when assessing weight status. This limitation leads to the “obesity paradox” phenomenon, wherein some individuals classified as obese by BMI criteria demonstrate better clinical outcomes [29]. In contrast, ABSI more effectively captures visceral fat-associated risks by evaluating waist circumference relative to BMI [30], thereby improving stroke prediction. The findings of this study indicate positive linear associations between AIP levels, ABSI trajectories, and stroke risk, demonstrating that both AIP and ABSI serve as reliable predictors for stroke. Furthermore, by integrating dynamic AIP clustering with ABSI trajectory analysis, our study revealed a synergistic effect between metabolic and body composition interactions on stroke risk. This spatiotemporal dynamic analytical approach not only captures risk progression patterns more effectively than traditional cross-sectional studies but also provides a more robust and clinically relevant risk stratification framework. It enables clinicians to identify at-risk populations based on single-timepoint data while simultaneously tracking how temporal changes in these parameters influence future risk, thereby highlighting the importance of longitudinal monitoring in primary stroke prevention.

In exploratory subgroup analyses, we observed that the combination of high AIP clustering (class 3) and high ABSI trajectory (traj 3) was associated with higher stroke incidence among women, individuals aged < 60 years, non-smokers, and non-drinkers. These findings reflect potentially distinct biological pathways across different populations. The elevated stroke risk observed in women may be partially attributed to the loss of cardioprotective estrogen during the menopausal transition, which exacerbates visceral fat distribution and lipid metabolic disorders, promoting a shift toward central obesity and a more atherogenic lipid profile [12, 31]. In younger individuals (< 60 years), long-term exposure to the ‘dual burden of metabolism and obesity’ may accelerate the progression of subclinical atherosclerosis [32]. The mechanisms underlying the observed associations in non-smokers and non-drinkers may be more directly related to inherent metabolic disorders (e.g., insulin resistance) and visceral adipose tissue-driven inflammation, which are not mitigated by the absence of traditional risk factors [31, 33]. This finding is noteworthy as it identifies high-risk individuals potentially overlooked by traditional risk screening approaches that primarily focus on smoking and alcohol consumption.

Study strengths and limitations

This study leveraged data from CHARLS cohort to comprehensively investigate the combined effects of dynamic AIP changes and ABSI trajectories on stroke risk, addressing the regression dilution bias associated with single-point AIP measurements. By integrating cluster analysis and trajectory modeling, the study captured temporal patterns and synergistic interactions between lipid metabolism and body composition, providing a more precise characterization of the “metabolic-obesity dual burden” in stroke risk prediction. Notably, Both AIP and ABSI can be readily obtained from routine laboratory tests and physical measurements. Their integration forms a simple yet effective tool for stroke risk stratification, demonstrating high feasibility in primary care settings. This approach enables earlier identification of high-risk populations without requiring additional costly or invasive examinations, thereby facilitating the implementation of targeted preventive interventions. Moreover, this work offers novel insights into the pathophysiological interplay underlying stroke development, supporting the rationale for integrated metabolic and body composition-targeted intervention strategies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged: First, as CHARLS participants were exclusively Chinese, generalizability to other populations may be limited. Second, stroke diagnosis in CHARLS relied solely on self-report without neuroimaging confirmation, although self-reported medical history has demonstrated reasonable reliability [34], this approach may still affect result accuracy. Additionally, the study did not compare the predictive performance of ABSI with other adiposity indicators like BMI, waist circumference (WC), and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR). Finally, exclusion of individuals with abnormal or missing exposure data may have introduced selection bias.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides robust evidence that dynamic changes in the AIP and trajectories of ABSI are significantly associated with stroke risk. By leveraging longitudinal data from a large cohort and integrating cluster analysis with trajectory modeling, we highlight the synergistic interplay between lipid metabolism and body composition-capturing the evolving nature of the “metabolic-obesity dual burden.” Clinically, these results support the utility of incorporating routinely available indicators like TG, HDL-C, and waist circumference into stroke risk stratification, facilitating earlier identification and targeted prevention. Future research should validate these findings in diverse populations, explore mechanistic pathways underlying these associations, and assess whether interventions targeting both metabolic and body composition abnormalities can reduce long-term stroke risk.
