Gravitational decoherence and recoherence of a composite particle: the interplay between gravitons and a classical Newtonian potential
Thiago H. Moreira, Lucas C. Céleri

Abstract
The fact that gravitational environments cannot be shielded (since gravity is universal) makes them of great theoretical interest to decoherence mechanisms and to the quantum-to-classical transition. While past results seemed to indicate that graviton-induced decoherence of spatial superpositions happens only for macroscopic systems, recently it was shown that this mechanism can be enhanced through the system’s own dynamical internal structure. In this work, we extend this analysis by including the interaction with a classical Newtonian potential. We show that, although the graviton bath alone dominates the mechanism for short times compared to a timescale established by the size of the quantum spatial superposition, the interplay between the gravitons and the internal degrees of freedom of the system renders decoherence inevitable in the long-time limit, even for microscopic masses. We also show that this mechanism is slightly slowed down by the interplay with the classical Newtonian potential, which, for systems without dynamical internal degrees of freedom, can even lead to recoherence, at least in principle. Keywords: gravitons, decoherence, composite, Newtonian, recoherence

Since the gravitational force is much weaker than the other fundamental interactions, the experimental realisation of such gravitational effects faces the problem of controlling competing sources of decoherence. Despite considerable theoretical efforts

Regardless of which theory of quantum gravity (if any) ever proves to be the most adequate, we can expect the usual quantum field theoretical treatment to be suitable in the limit of weak gravitational fields. In this limit, the spacetime metric is described by an expansion of the gravitational propagating DoFs around some known classical solution to Einstein’s equation. This treatment describes the so-called classical gravitational waves, which were detected by LIGO in 2015

In particular, the decoherence of spatial superpositions of a quantum point particle induced by the interaction with a graviton bath was analysed in

Kanno et al. 2021

In Ref.
Moreira and Céleri 2023
, this decoherence mechanism was analysed in the context of a quantum composite particle, namely a system described by both external and internal DoFs. Due to the universal aspect of gravity, the gravitons couple with all dynamical DoFs of the system. For quantum superpositions of the external variables, the internal DoFs enter as a second environment, which interacts with the gravitational one. The interplay between the system internal structure and the gravitons (which does not amount to a simple sum of their individual effects due to their mutual interaction) was found to significantly enhance decoherence, allowing for the possibility of graviton-induced spatial localisation of quantum superpositions involving microscopic masses. An interesting application of the results for graviton-induced decoherence was analysed in
Moreira and Céleri 2024
, where the entropy production due to the interaction with the graviton bath was quantified by the establishment of a fluctuation theorem (see
Basso et al. 2023
;
Basso et al. 2025
;
Costa et al. 2026

In this work, we extend the analysis of Ref.
Moreira and Céleri 2023
to include the interaction of the composite system with a classical Newtonian gravitational potential, in addition to the graviton bath. While both gravitational interactions induce a coupling between the external and internal DoFs of the system, the graviton bath also acts as a quantum environment, and we shall see how the classical gravitational potential affects the graviton-induced decoherence mechanism.

Just as in Ref.
Moreira and Céleri 2023
, the analysis is performed by considering four different initial states of the graviton bath. The most natural choice is the vacuum state, but we also consider an initial thermal state, for example. Throughout this work, we loosely refer to a parameter
T_{\rm g}
as the graviton temperature, which characterises the thermal initial state. However, one should keep in mind that this quantity may best be thought of as a phenomenological parameter that characterises the power spectral density of gravitational noise, which plays the same role as the temperature of the usual thermal baths, but without necessarily being associated with any precise thermodynamic definition, since graviton thermalization cannot generally be assumed
Anastopoulos and Hu 2013
. Additionally, we explore the analogy with quantum optics and consider gravitons in initial coherent and squeezed states. Although a coherent graviton state is expected to be the quantum-mechanical state whose properties most closely resemble those of a classical gravitational wave, it has been argued that gravitons created from quantum fluctuations in the course of cosmological evolution should now be in strongly squeezed states
Grishchuk and Sidorov 1990
.

We also address the possibility of gravitational recoherence, which is made possible by the interplay between the graviton bath and the classical Newtonian potential. Since the explicit expressions for the decoherence functions (for the different initial states) are quite cumbersome, we list them in the Appendix

II.2
The graviton action

We show in Appendix
A
that the interaction between gravitons and the Newtonian potential is described by the action

S_{\rm grav}=\frac{1}{64\pi}\int\differential^{4}x\,\quantity(h_{ij}\Box h^{ij}+2\phi\,h_{ij}\delta_{\mu\nu}\partial^\mu\partial^\nu h^{ij}).

(15)

Note that the interaction between the metric perturbation field and the Newtonian potential is of order
O(\phi h^{2})
, while in Eq. (
II.1
) they simultaneously couple with the system through an interaction of order
O(\phi h)
, which is therefore the dominant term. Furthermore, we remark that the interaction in Eq. (
15
), upon quantisation of the radiation degrees of freedom, is physically associated with graviton scattering, for which the differential cross section is shown in Appendix
A
. This cross section is found to be strongly dominated by regions in which
\theta\ll 1
, scaling as
\derivative*{\sigma}{\Omega}\sim(G^{2}M_{N}^{2}/c^{4})\theta^{-4}
, with
\theta
being the angle between the directions of propagation of incident and scattered gravitons. This leads us to conclude that such an interaction is less dominant than the one that is coupled with the system. In fact, this interaction term is neglected in Ref.
Chawla and Parikh 2023
, where the authors compute quantum gravity corrections to the fall of test masses, for instance.

From now on, let us drop this interaction term and write the graviton action in Fourier space. We introduce the expansion in Fourier modes

h_{ij}(t,\mathbf{x})=\int\differential^{3}k\,\tilde{h}_{ij}(t,\mathbf{k})e^{i\mathbf{k}\dotproduct\mathbf{x}}.

(16)

The reality of
h_{ij}(t,\mathbf{x})
implies that
\tilde{h}^{*}(t,\mathbf{k})=\tilde{h}(t,-\mathbf{k})
. We can take the Fourier transform of the metric perturbation field to be of the form

\tilde{h}_{ij}(t,\mathbf{k})=\sum_{s=+,\crossproduct}\epsilon_{ij}^{s}(\mathbf{k})q_{s}(t,\mathbf{k}),

(17)

where
q_{s}(t,\mathbf{k})
is the wave amplitude in Fourier space with polarisation

, and
\epsilon_{ij}^{s}(\mathbf{k})
are the polarisation tensors satisfying the normalisation conditions
\epsilon_{ij}^{s}(\mathbf{k})\epsilon^{ij}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})=2\delta^{s}_{s^{\prime}}

k^{i}\epsilon_{ij}^{s}(\mathbf{k})=0
and the traceless conditions
\delta^{ij}\epsilon_{ij}^{s}(\mathbf{k})=0

Using the expansion (
16

S_{\rm grav}=\int\differential t\int\differential^{3}k\,\sum_{s}\frac{m_{\rm g}}{2}\quantity[\abs{\dot{q}_s(t,\vb{k})}^2-\vb{k}^2\abs{q_s(t,\vb{k})}^2],

(18)

where we defined
m_{\rm g}=\pi^{2}/2

(19)

with

III
Time evolution of the reduced density matrix

Let us now quantize the systems described in the last section. We will be interested in analysing quantum superposition states of the external DoFs of the composite particle, so both the internal DoFs and the gravitons will be treated as environments. Since they interact with each other, one cannot simply evaluate their separate contributions and then add everything together, as this would not take their coupling into account. We address this problem by using the Feynman-Vernon approach to open quantum systems
Feynman and Vernon 1963
;
Feynman and Hibbs 2010
;
Calzetta and Hu 2008
. First, we integrate the gravitational and internal DoFs variables. The entire procedure was described in
Moreira and Céleri 2023
, so here we shall only outline the main steps and the modifications that arise by the introduction of the classical Newtonian potential.

Within the Feynman-Vernon approach to open quantum systems, the time evolution of the system reduced density matrix
\rho_{\rm sys}
, which is obtained from the total density matrix by taking a partial trace with respect to the environment variables, is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm sys}(X,X^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=\int\differential X_{0}\differential X_{0}^{\prime}\,\mathcal{J}_{\rm sys}(X,X^{\prime},t|X_{0},X_{0}^{\prime},0)

\displaystyle\times\rho_{\rm sys}(X_{0},X_{0}^{\prime},0),

(23)

where
\rho_{\rm sys}(X,X^{\prime},t)=\matrixelement{X}{\rho_{\rm sys}(t)}{X'}
, with
\ket{X}
denoting a position eigenstate in the reduced Hilbert space of the system, and the evolution operator for the reduced density matrix reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\rm sys}(X,X^{\prime},t|X_{0},X_{0}^{\prime},0)

\displaystyle=\int\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}X(0)\,=\,X_{0}\\
X^{\prime}(0)\,=\,X_{0}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}^{\begin{subarray}{c}X(t)\,=\,X\\
X^{\prime}(t)\,=\,X^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{D}X\mathcal{D}X^{\prime}\,e^{i\quantity(S_{\rm sys}[X]-S_{\rm sys}[X'])}e^{iS_{\rm IF}[X,X^{\prime},t]},

(24)

with
S_{\rm sys}
denoting the part of the action that describes the system alone and
\mathcal{D}X
denoting the integral measure of the path. Also, we assume that, at
t=0
, the system and the graviton bath were not correlated
\rho(0)=\rho_{\rm sys}(0)\otimes\rho_{\rm grav}(0)
. The functional
e^{iS_{\rm IF}[X,X^{\prime},t]}
is the influence functional, while
S_{\rm IF}
represents the influence action.

In the case of Eq. (
20
), the system is coupled with the infinite set of graviton modes, which are all independent of each other, as well as the two polarizations. They can thus be treated separately in such a way that the total influence action is the sum of the action corresponding to each mode and polarisation. Furthermore, we note that the environment action (the graviton action) is quadratic in the field amplitudes, and the coupling with the system variable
X^{s}(t,\mathbf{k})
, Eq. (
II.3
), is linear. This is then a special case of the linear coupling model

;

;
Hsiang et al. 2024
;
Moreira and Céleri 2023
, for which the influence action is found to be

\begin{split}S_{\rm IF}[x,x^{\prime}]=\int\differential t\differential t^{\prime}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\quantity[x_{ij}(t)-x'_{ij}(t)]D^{ijkl}_{\rm g}(t,t^{\prime})\quantity[x_{kl}(t')+x'_{kl}(t')]\right.\\
\left.+\frac{i}{2}\quantity[x_{ij}(t)-x'_{ij}(t)]N^{ijkl}_{\rm g}(t,t^{\prime})\quantity[x_{kl}(t')-x'_{kl}(t')]\right\},\end{split}

(25)

where we have defined the gravitational dissipation

\begin{split}D_{\rm g}^{ijkl}(t,t^{\prime})&=\frac{1}{16}\sum_{s}\int\differential^{3}k\,\left[\epsilon^{ij}(\mathbf{k})\epsilon^{kl}(\mathbf{k})\derivative[2]{t}\derivative[2]{{t'}}d_{s}(t,t^{\prime},\mathbf{k})\right.\\
&\hskip 8.5359pt\left.+\epsilon^{ij}(\mathbf{k})\epsilon^{nl}(\mathbf{k}){T_{n}}^{k}\derivative[2]{t}d_{s}(t,t^{\prime},\mathbf{k})+\epsilon^{nj}(\mathbf{k})\epsilon^{kl}(\mathbf{k}){T_{n}}^{i}\derivative[2]{{t'}}d_{s}(t,t^{\prime},\mathbf{k})\right],\end{split}

(26a)

and noise kernels

\begin{split}N_{\rm g}^{ijkl}(t,t^{\prime})&=\frac{1}{16}\sum_{s}\int\differential^{3}k\,\left[\epsilon^{ij}(\mathbf{k})\epsilon^{kl}(\mathbf{k})\derivative[2]{t}\derivative[2]{{t'}}n_{s}(t,t^{\prime},\mathbf{k})\right.\\
&\hskip 8.5359pt\left.+\epsilon^{ij}(\mathbf{k})\epsilon^{nl}(\mathbf{k}){T_{n}}^{k}\derivative[2]{t}n_{s}(t,t^{\prime},\mathbf{k})+\epsilon^{nj}(\mathbf{k})\epsilon^{kl}(\mathbf{k}){T_{n}}^{i}\derivative[2]{{t'}}n_{s}(t,t^{\prime},\mathbf{k})\right],\end{split}

(26b)

with
d_{s}(t,t^{\prime},\mathbf{k})
and
n_{s}(t,t^{\prime},\mathbf{k})
defined by

d_{s}(t,t^{\prime},\mathbf{k})=i\expectationvalue{\comm{q_s(t,\vb{k})}{q_s(t',\vb{k})}}_{\rm g}\theta(t-t^{\prime}),

(27a)

and

n_{s}(t,t^{\prime},\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{2}\expectationvalue{\acomm{q_s(t,\vb{k})}{q_s(t',\vb{k})}}_{\rm g}.

(27b)

Also, we define

x_{ij}(t)=L_{\rm rest}(\varrho,\dot{\varrho}\bar{t})\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t).

(28)

In Eqs. (
27
) the
q
’s stand for position operators in the Heisenberg picture,
\commutator{\cdot}{\cdot}
and
\anticommutator{\cdot}{\cdot}
denote the commutator and anti-commutator of operators, respectively, while
\theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function. The expectation values with the subscript ’g’ are computed with respect to the initial state of the gravitons. Note that the influence action (
25
) has the same form as the one obtained in
Moreira and Céleri 2023
. The difference now is that both the dissipation and noise kernels contain new terms that arise due to the presence of a Newtonian potential.

Just as is usually done, we can rewrite the term that contains the noise kernel by using a Gaussian stochastic variable
\mathcal{N}

Cho and Hu 2022
;
Moreira and Céleri 2023
. This term will then be of order
O(\xi^{2})
while the one with the dissipation kernel will be of order
O(\xi^{4})
, allowing us to approximate the influence functional by

e^{iS_{\rm IF}[x,x^{\prime}]}\simeq\int\mathcal{D}\mathcal{N}\,\mathscr{P}[\mathcal{N}]\,e^{i\int\differential t\,\mathcal{N}^{ij}(t)\quantity[x_{ij}(t)-x'_{ij}(t)]},

(29)

in a perturbative approach. Here,
\mathscr{P}[\mathcal{N}]
stands for a Gaussian probability density.

The influence functional (
29
) describes the influence of the graviton bath on the time evolution of the system, which is no longer unitary. Next, we can proceed to give the same treatment to the system internal DoFs. Following Ref.
Moreira and Céleri 2023
, we assume that the external and internal DoFs are initially uncorrelated, and we take an internal Lagrangian of the form

\mathscr{L}(\varrho,\dot{\varrho}\,\bar{t})\simeq\sum_{\alpha}\quantity(\frac{1}{2}\mu_\alpha\dot{\varrho}_\alpha^2-\vartheta_\alpha\varrho_\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\mu_\alpha\varpi_\alpha^2\varrho_\alpha^2),

(30)

where
\mu_{\alpha}
,
\vartheta_{\alpha}
and
\varpi_{\alpha}
are constants. This leads, once again, to the linear coupling model, and one obtains another influence action in terms of (internal DoFs) dissipation and noise kernels. Here also, one can argue that the dominant contribution comes from noise, and the external DoFs density matrix is found to be given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm ext}(\xi,\xi^{\prime},t)=\int\differential\xi(0)\differential\xi^{\prime}(0)\,\rho_{\rm ext}(\xi(0),\xi^{\prime}(0),0)

\displaystyle\times\int\mathcal{D}\xi\mathcal{D}\xi^{\prime}\,e^{\frac{i}{2}m\delta^{ij}\int\differential t\,\quantity(\dot{\xi}_i\dot{\xi}_j-\dot{\xi}_i'\dot{\xi}_j')}

\displaystyle\times\int\mathcal{D}\mathcal{N}\,\mathscr{P}[\mathcal{N}]\,e^{-im\int\differential t\,\mathcal{N}^{ij}\quantity(\xi_i\xi_j-\xi_i'\xi_j')}

\displaystyle\times e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int\differential t\differential t^{\prime}\,\quantity[Y(t)-Y'(t)]N_{\rm int}(t,t^{\prime})\quantity[Y(t')-Y'(t')]},

(31)

where

Y(t)=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{ij}\dot{\xi}^{i}\dot{\xi}^{j}-\mathcal{N}_{ij}\xi^{i}\xi^{j}-1

(32)

and

N_{\rm int}(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha}\vartheta_{\alpha}^{2}\expectationvalue{\acomm{\varrho_\alpha(t)}{\varrho_\alpha(t')}}_{\rm int}

(33)

is the internal DoFs noise kernels. Now, the
\varrho
’s are operators in the Heisenberg picture, and expectation values with the subscript ’int’ are computed with respect to the initial state of the internal DoFs. Also, in arriving at Eq. (
III
) we made a change of variables such that the stochastic variable
\mathcal{N}_{ij}
satisfies

\expectationvalue{\mathcal{N}^{ij}(t)}_{\rm sto}=-\frac{1}{2}T_{ij}

(34a)

and

\expectationvalue{\mathcal{N}^{ij}(t)\mathcal{N}^{kl}(t')}_{\rm sto}=N_{\rm g}^{ijkl}(t,t^{\prime})+O(T_{ij}^{2}),

(34b)

where the averages are computed with the Gaussian probability density. These stochastic averages can be employed, provided that we work on a perturbative regime, in order to obtain the external degrees of freedom density matrix as

\begin{split}&\rho_{\rm ext}(\xi,\xi^{\prime},t)=\int\differential\xi(0)\differential\xi^{\prime}(0)\,\rho_{\rm ext}(\xi(0),\xi^{\prime}(0),0)\int\mathcal{D}\xi\mathcal{D}\xi^{\prime}\,e^{i\quantity(S_{\rm ext}[\xi]-S_{\rm ext}[\xi'])}\\
&\hskip 8.5359pt\times\exp\quantity{-\frac{1}{4}\int\dd t\dd t'\,\qty[\frac{1}{2}\delta^{ij}\delta^{kl}y_{ij}(t)N_{\rm int}(t,t')y_{kl}(t')+\delta^{ij}T^{kl}y_{ij}(t)N_{\rm int}(t,t')w_{kl}(t')]}\\
&\hskip 8.5359pt\times\exp\quantity{-\int\dd t\dd t'\,w_{ij}(t)\qty[\frac{m^2}{2}+N_{\rm int}(t,t')]N_{\rm g}^{ijkl}(t,t')w_{kl}(t')},\end{split}

(35)

where

S_{\rm ext}[\xi]=\frac{m}{2}\int\differential t\,\quantity(\delta^{ij}\dot{\xi}_i\dot{\xi}_j+T^{ij}\xi_i\xi_j),

(36)

and we have defined

w_{ij}(t)=\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t)-\xi_{i}^{\prime}(t)\xi_{j}^{\prime}(t)

(37a)

and

y_{ij}(t)=\dot{\xi}_{i}(t)\dot{\xi}_{j}(t)-\dot{\xi}_{i}^{\prime}(t)\dot{\xi}_{j}^{\prime}(t).

(37b)

Equation (
35
) is the main result of this section. It encapsulates how the graviton bath and the system own internal structure affect the time evolution of external centre-of-mass variables in an effective non-unitary manner. Note that these contributions affect only non-diagonal elements in the position representation of the density matrix, and we will see how they lead to decoherence of quantum superpositions of the external DoFs.

IV
The decoherence function

From now on, let us consider a special case in which the system can move only along two classically distinguishable paths
\xi^{(1)}(t)
and
\xi^{(2)}(t)
. Then, Eq. (
35
) can be written as

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm ext}(\xi,\xi^{\prime},t)=\int\differential\xi_{0}\differential\xi_{0}^{\prime}\rho_{\rm ext}(\xi_{0},\xi_{0}^{\prime},0)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{m,n=1}^{2}e^{i\quantity{S_{\rm ext}\qty[\xi^{(m)}]-S_{\rm ext}\qty[\xi^{(n)}]}}

\displaystyle\hskip 8.5359pt\times\evaluated{e^{-\Gamma\qty[\xi^{(m)},\xi^{(n)},t]}}_{\xi^{(m)}(0)=\xi_0,\,\,\xi^{(n)}(0)=\xi_0'}^{\xi^{(m)}(t)=\xi,\,\,\xi^{(n)}(t)=\xi'},

(38)

where we introduced the functional
\Gamma\quantity[\xi^{(m)},\xi^{(n)},t]
whose explicit form can be read from Eq. (
35
) setting
\xi\to\xi^{(m)}
and
\xi^{\prime}\to\xi^{(n)}
. This is a functional of the paths
\xi^{(1)}(t)
and
\xi^{(2)}(t)
that satisfies

\Gamma\quantity[\xi^{(m)},\xi^{(n)},t]=\Gamma\quantity[\xi^{(n)},\xi^{(m)},t],

(39a)

and

\Gamma\quantity[\xi^{(m)},\xi^{(m)},t]=0.

(39b)

Due to these properties, we only need to consider
\Gamma\quantity[\xi^{(1)},\xi^{(2)},t]\equiv\Gamma(t)
. When
\Gamma(t)\geq 0
, we call it the
decoherence function
, since in that case it leads to an exponential decay of the off-diagonal elements of the density matrix (the coherences).

For simplicity, let us take
\xi_{i}^{(m)}(t)=\xi^{(m)}(t)\delta_{i3}
, that is, unidimensional paths in the
z-
direction. Now, consider a scheme in which both paths start at the same point
\xi_{0}=\xi_{0}^{\prime}
and end at another point
\xi=\xi^{\prime}
. This is the typical scenario in which an initially localised quantum system undergoes a superposition of paths and is then recombined after time
t_{f}
in order for its interference patterns to be analysed.

First, let us define the variables

\Xi(t)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\quantity[\xi^{(1)}(t)+\xi^{(2)}(t)],\hskip 8.5359pt\Delta\xi(t)\equiv\xi^{(1)}(t)-\xi^{(2)}(t),

(40a)

and

V(t)\equiv\derivative{t}\Xi(t),\hskip 14.22636pt\Delta v(t)\equiv\derivative{t}\Delta\xi(t).

(40b)

The noise kernel
N_{\rm int}(t,t^{\prime})
was computed in
Moreira and Céleri 2023
considering the internal DoFs to represent an Ohmic bath described by the coupling constant
\eta
in thermal equilibrium at high temperature
T_{\rm int}\gg\absolutevalue{t-t'}^{-1}
, resulting in

N_{\rm int}(t,t^{\prime})=\eta\pi T_{\rm int}\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(41)

The decoherence function can then be written as

\displaystyle\Gamma(t_{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\eta\pi T_{\rm int}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\differential t\,\quantity[V(t)\Delta v(t)]^{2}

\displaystyle+\eta\pi T_{\rm int}T_{zz}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\differential t\,V(t)\Delta v(t)\Xi(t)\Delta\xi(t)

\displaystyle+2m^{2}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\differential t\differential t^{\prime}\,\Xi(t)\Delta\xi(t)N_{\rm g}(t,t^{\prime})\Xi(t^{\prime})\Delta\xi(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle+4\eta\pi T_{\rm int}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\differential t\,\quantity[\Xi(t)\Delta\xi(t)]^{2}N_{\rm g}(t),

(42)

where we denote
N_{\rm g}(t,t^{\prime})\equiv N_{\rm g}^{3333}(t,t^{\prime})
and
N_{\rm g}(t)\equiv\lim_{t^{\prime}\to t}N_{\rm g}(t,t^{\prime})
.

Now we can consider specific configurations of the superposition state. Our main choice, which we shall refer to as
Configuration 1
, is described by
Kanno et al. 2021

\Xi(t)=\Xi=\textrm{constant in time}

(43a)

and

\Delta\xi(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2vt&\textrm{for}\hskip 5.69046pt0<t\leq t_{f}/2\\
2v(t_{f}-t)&\textrm{for}\hskip 5.69046ptt_{f}/2<t<t_{f}\end{array}\right.,

(43b)

for some constant velocity
v
. Note that this implies
V(t)=\derivative*{\Xi}{t}=0
.

For Configuration 1, the decoherence rate becomes

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}(t_{f})

\displaystyle=8m^{2}\Xi^{2}v^{2}\left[\int_{0}^{t_{f}/2}\differential t\differential t^{\prime}\,tt^{\prime}N_{\rm g}(t,t^{\prime})\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\int_{t_{f}/2}^{t_{f}}\differential t\differential t^{\prime}\,(t_{f}-t)(t_{f}-t^{\prime})N_{\rm g}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\left.+2\int_{0}^{t_{f}/2}\differential t\int_{t_{f}/2}^{t_{f}}\differential t^{\prime}\,t(t_{f}-t^{\prime})N_{\rm g}(t,t^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle+16\eta\pi T_{\rm int}\Xi^{2}v^{2}\left[\int_{0}^{t_{f}/2}\differential t\,t^{2}N_{\rm g}(t)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\left.+\int_{t_{f}/2}^{t_{f}}\differential t\,(t_{f}-t)^{2}N_{\rm g}(t)\right].

(44)

This is the same expression we obtained in
Moreira and Céleri 2023
, except that now there are corrections to the gravitational noise kernel coming from the Newtonian potential. Note from Eq. (
IV
) that within this configuration, all decoherence comes either from the gravitons alone or their interplay with the internal DoFs. If there were no graviton bath, we would have
\Gamma_{1}(t)=0
. This is not generally true, as we can see by considering a different configuration for the superposition state.

For illustrative purposes, let us take a second configuration (
Configuration 2
), which is described by linear paths with different constant speeds,
\xi^{(m)}=v_{m}t
. In that case, we have

\Xi(t)=Vt,\hskip 14.22636pt\Delta\xi(t)=\Delta vt,

(45a)

and

V=\frac{v_{1}+v_{2}}{2},\hskip 14.22636pt\Delta v=v_{1}-v_{2}.

(45b)

This could be relevant for systems in a superposition state such that its components travel at different speeds, like mass superpositions whose wave packets propagate with different group velocities.

For Configuration 2, the decoherence rate becomes

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}(t_{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\eta\pi T_{\rm int}\quantity(v_1^2-v_2^2)^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\times\quantity[\frac{t_f}{2}+\frac{T_{zz}t_f^3}{3}+4\int_0^{t_f}\dd t\,t^4N_{\rm g}(t)]

\displaystyle+\frac{m^{2}}{2}\quantity(v_1^2-v_2^2)^{2}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\differential t\differential t^{\prime}\,(tt^{\prime})^{2}N_{\rm g}(t,t^{\prime}).

(46)

Note that if we were to turn off the graviton bath, one would still have decoherence due to the coupling between internal and external DoFs mediated by special relativistic effects and the Newtonian potential (this is the kind of gravitational decoherence explored in
Pikovski et al. 2015
, for instance). Additionally, one can also see that while
\Gamma_{1}(t)
scales as
v^{2}
,
\Gamma_{2}(t)
depends on
v^{4}
, making the former more relevant to nonrelativistic systems such as the ones we consider here.

It is now only a matter of a long but straightforward calculus to work out the various explicit expressions for the decoherence function considering both configurations and gravitons to be in the four distinct initial states we considered in
Moreira and Céleri 2023

IV.3
Gravitational recoherence

Finally, let us turn our attention to the possibility of gravitational recoherence. Recall that, when we consider only the pure graviton contribution to the decoherence function, it saturates at a value given by Eq. (
57
) (for the vacuum state). This is no longer the case when we include the Newtonian potential, since the contribution coming from the interplay between the gravitons and the classical potential does not saturate to a fixed value, but keeps increasing with time. For simplicity, let us now consider a particle with no dynamical internal DoFs, such that the decoherence function for the vacuum state becomes

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}^{(\textrm{v})}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{16\Xi^{2}v^{2}m^{2}}{5\pi E_{\rm P}^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\left\{\quantity(\frac{\Lambda}{\hbar})^{2}f_{\rm v}^{(I)}\quantity(\frac{\Lambda t}{\hbar})-\frac{GM_{N}}{R_{N}^{3}}f_{\rm v}^{(III)}\quantity(\frac{\Lambda t}{\hbar})\right\}.

(67)

In the short-time limit, this expression reduces to Eq. (
54
), but now there is no internal DoFs contribution to dominate in the long-time regime. From Table
1
, we see that
f_{\rm v}^{(I)}(x)\sim 1
and
f_{\rm v}^{(III)}(x)\sim 8\ln x
for
x\gg 1
, and the decoherence function for
t\gg\hbar/\Lambda
becomes

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}^{(\textrm{v})}(t)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{16}{5\pi}\quantity(\frac{v}{c})^{2}\quantity(\frac{m}{M_{\rm P}})^{2}

\displaystyle\times\quantity[1-8\qty(\frac{\hbar}{\Lambda})^2\frac{GM_N}{R_N^3}\ln\qty(\frac{\Lambda t}{\hbar})].

(68)

This means that, when there are no internal DoFs to couple with the gravitons and guarantee gravitational decoherence, not only the decoherence function stops increasing with time but also starts to decrease, with the possibility of eventually becoming negative (and thus no longer being called a
decoherence
function). This can happen, at least in principle, and a quick analysis shows that
\Gamma_{1}^{(\textrm{v})}(t)<0
for

t\gtrsim\frac{\hbar}{\Lambda}\exp\quantity[\frac{1}{8}\qty(\frac{\Lambda}{\hbar})^2\frac{R_N^3}{GM_N}].

(69)

A quick numerical estimate shows that this is typically an infinitely long time. For example, even for a superposition size on the order of kilometres,
L_{0}\sim 10^{3}
m, we find
\quantity(\frac{\Lambda}{\hbar})^{2}\frac{R_{N}^{3}}{GM_{N}}\sim 10^{16}
near Earth.

For the coherent and squeezed states, the situation is similar, since their G+N contributions also increase as
\ln x
for
x\gg 1
. However, for the thermal state, we see from Table
3
that
f_{\rm t}^{(III)}(x)\sim 4x
. Therefore, for a particle with no dynamical internal DoFs, the thermal decoherence function in the long-time limit reads

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}^{(\textrm{t})}(t)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{16}{5\pi}\quantity(\frac{v}{c})^{2}\quantity(\frac{m}{M_{\rm P}})^{2}

\displaystyle\times\quantity[1+\qty(\frac{\pi k_B T_{\rm g}}{\Lambda})^2\qty(1-\frac{4\hbar}{\pi k_B T_{\rm g}}\frac{GM_N}{R_N^3}t)],

(70)

and condition
\Gamma_{1}^{(\textrm{t})}(t)<0
is achieved by

t\gtrsim\frac{\pi k_{B}T_{\rm g}}{4\hbar}\frac{R_{E}^{3}}{GM_{E}}\quantity[1+\qty(\frac{\Lambda}{\pi k_B T_{\rm g}})^2].

(71)

Even though the behaviour is slightly different for the thermal initial state, this result also translates to an infinitely long time. For
T_{\rm g}\sim 1
K, we have
\frac{\pi k_{B}T_{\rm g}}{4\hbar}\frac{R_{N}^{3}}{GM_{N}}\sim 10^{17}
s (near Earth), which is about the age of the universe.

Our results indicate that the presence of the Newtonian potential contributes to slightly slowing down the decoherence mechanism, which eventually can lead to recoherence on the system. Although this is possible in principle, it is not expected to happen for typical parameters in a reasonable amount of time, and even if that was the case, decoherence will inevitably prevail when we include the interaction with the system dynamical internal degrees of freedom. In general, recoherence mechanisms can appear in non-Markovian models for open quantum systems, such as when one describes quantum fields in cosmological backgrounds.
Colas et al. 2023
. The connection between memory (non-Markovian) effects and recoherence is explored in
Kranas et al. 2025
.

V
Conclusions

The unavoidability of the quantum spacetime fluctuations, here understood as a graviton bath, was explored in the context of decoherence of a composite particle. We showed that while the interaction with the gravitons alone dominates for short times, the spacetime-induced coupling with the internal degrees of freedom of the system inevitably leads to gravitational decoherence for long times. This effect was also found to be enhanced by different initial graviton states, with the decoherence time even exhibiting an exponential decay for an initial squeezed state.

Although previous results in the literature indicated that graviton-induced decoherence occurs only for a macroscopic system
Kanno et al. 2021
, we showed that the situation can change when we consider composite systems. Since gravity also couples with the dynamical internal degrees of freedom, which themselves are to be treated as another environment for the centre-of-mass variable, their interaction amounts to an amplification of such a decoherence mechanism. We then raise the possibility of considering more general scenarios in which a system simultaneously interacts with both a gravitational and a non-gravitational environment, like an electromagnetic bath, for instance. Since environments will inevitably interact with each other, one may wonder what the effects of such an interplay on the system are. We leave the question of non-gravitational environments being used to mediate and amplify gravitational decoherence, just as the internal structure of the system, for future investigation.

In this work, we also considered the effects of a classical Newtonian potential, which not only contributes to slightly slowdown decoherence but also opens up the possibility of a recoherence mechanism if the internal dynamics is turned off. Such an effect usually appears in non-Markovian models for open quantum systems, and, although gravitational recoherence is shown to be possible in principle, it seems to happen for timescales compared to the age of the universe. Nevertheless, decoherence will prevail for general composite systems. It is a matter for future investigation to determine if other classical backgrounds beyond the Newtonian approximation, such as the Schwarzschild solution or the Robertson-Walker expanding universe, will have a significant impact for the time evolution of composite open quantum systems. The dynamical nature of the metric may provide conditions that protect the system from decoherence
Soares-Pinto et al. 2011
;
Céleri et al. 2008

Finally, we remark that we considered a nonrelativistic particle in this work, and also in Ref.
Moreira and Céleri 2023
, but it would be interesting to see how these extend to relativistic particles and quantum fields. For example, one could study how (amplified) graviton-induced decoherence affects neutrino oscillations through its effects on the propagation of neutrino wave packets
D’Esposito and Gubitosi 2024
;
Domi et al. 2024
.

Appendix A
Gravitational waves interacting with a Newtonian potential

In this appendix, we compute the action describing the interaction between gravitational waves (which lead to gravitons upon quantisation) and a classical Newtonian potential.

A.1
Linearized Einstein equation

Consider a given background metric
\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}
that is an exact known solution to Einstein’s equation. In situations where there is a small deviation from such exact solution, one can look for an approximate solution by writing

g_{\mu\nu}=\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu},\hskip 14.22636pt\absolutevalue{h_{\mu\nu}}\ll\absolutevalue{\Tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}},

(72)

linearising Einstein’s equation in
h_{\mu\nu}
. The metric perturbation
h_{\mu\nu}
satisfies the linear equation
Wald 1984

\tilde{\nabla}_{(\nu}\tilde{\nabla}^{\mu}\bar{h}_{\sigma)\mu}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\nabla}^{\mu}\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}h_{\sigma\nu}-\tilde{R}_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}h^{\rho\mu}=0,

(73)

where

\bar{h}_{\mu\nu}\equiv h_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}h,

(74)

with
h={h^{\mu}}_{\mu}=\tilde{g}^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}
. Here,
\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}
and
\tilde{R}_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}
denote the covariant derivative and the Riemann curvature tensor associated with the background metric
\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}
, respectively.

Now, the gauge invariance of linearised theory states that
h_{\mu\nu}
and
h_{\mu\nu}^{\prime}
represent the same physical perturbation, where
Wald 1984

h_{\mu\nu}^{\prime}=h_{\mu\nu}+\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}+\tilde{\nabla}_{\nu}\xi_{\mu},

(75)

for some arbitrary vector
\xi_{\mu}
. This gauge freedom allows us to simplify the equations of motion for
h_{\mu\nu}
. For example, starting with any given
h_{\mu\nu}
, such that
\tilde{\nabla}^{\nu}\bar{h}_{\mu\nu}\neq 0
in general, one can perform a gauge transformation according to Eq. (
75
). For the new perturbation, we will have
\tilde{\nabla}^{\nu}\bar{h}^{\prime}_{\mu\nu}=\tilde{\nabla}^{\nu}\bar{h}_{\mu\nu}+\tilde{\nabla}^{\nu}\tilde{\nabla}_{\nu}\xi_{\mu}
. We may then choose
\xi^{\mu}
to satisfy
\tilde{\nabla}^{\nu}\tilde{\nabla}_{\nu}\xi_{\mu}=-\tilde{\nabla}^{\nu}\bar{h}_{\mu\nu}
, such that, for the perturbation in the new gauge, we now have
\tilde{\nabla}^{\nu}\bar{h}_{\mu\nu}=0
, where we changed
h^{\prime}\to h
for notational convenience. In this gauge, the linearised Einstein equation (
73
) reads

\tilde{\nabla}^{\mu}\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}h_{\sigma\nu}+2\tilde{R}_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}h^{\rho\mu}=0,

(76)

with the metric perturbation satisfying the gauge condition,

\tilde{\nabla}^{\nu}h_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}h=0.

(77)

In the limit of flat spacetime, this equation reduces to
\partial^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}h_{\sigma\nu}=0
.
In that limit
, one can then perform a further gauge transformation parametrised by a vector
\varepsilon^{\nu}
as long as
\partial^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\varepsilon_{\nu}=0
, so that both the gauge condition and the equation of motion remain satisfied. Then, one uses this residual freedom to choose
h=0
and
h_{0i}=0
, which, together with the equations of motion, implies
h_{00}=0
for suitable boundary conditions. This is the usual
transverse-traceless
(TT) gauge choice.

However, in the case of curved background, this is generally not possible. We can try to perform such an additional gauge transformation parametrized by a vector
\varepsilon^{\nu}
satisfying
\tilde{\nabla}^{\mu}\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}\varepsilon_{\nu}=0
(which is necessary to keep Eq. (
77
) satisfied), but now the left-hand side of the equation of motion (
76
) transforms to

\tilde{\nabla}^{\mu}\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}h^{\prime}_{\sigma\nu}+2\tilde{R}_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}h^{\prime\rho\mu}=\tilde{\nabla}^{\mu}\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}h_{\sigma\nu}+2\tilde{R}_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}h^{\rho\mu}-\quantity[\Tilde{\nabla}^\mu\qty(\Tilde{R}_{\mu\sigma\rho\nu}+\Tilde{R}_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu})]\varepsilon^{\rho}.

(78)

Clearly, the last term on the right-hand side prevents us from making such gauge transformation.

Let us then see what happens with this term when the background itself can be seen as a small perturbation of Minkowski spacetime,

\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+h^{(B)}_{\mu\nu}.

(79)

A direct calculation yields

\begin{split}\tilde{\nabla}^{\mu}\quantity(\Tilde{R}_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}+\Tilde{R}_{\mu\sigma\rho\nu})&=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\sigma}\Box h^{(B)}_{\rho\nu}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\rho}\partial^{\mu}h^{(B)}_{\mu\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\rho}\partial_{\sigma}\partial^{\mu}h^{(B)}_{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\nu}\Box h^{(B)}_{\rho\sigma}\\
&-\partial_{\rho}\Box h^{(B)}_{\nu\sigma}-\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\sigma}\partial^{\mu}h^{(B)}_{\rho\mu}.\end{split}

(80)

Since the background metric is assumed to satisfy the vacuum equation,
h^{(B)}_{\mu\nu}
must satisfy the linearized equation
Carroll 2019

\Box h^{(B)}_{\mu\nu}=\partial^{\sigma}\partial_{\nu}h^{(B)}_{\sigma\mu}+\partial^{\sigma}\partial_{\mu}h^{(B)}_{\sigma\nu}-\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}h^{(B)}.

(81)

By plugging Eq. (
81
) into Eq. (
80
), one immediately finds

\tilde{\nabla}^{\mu}\quantity(\Tilde{R}_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}+\Tilde{R}_{\mu\sigma\rho\nu})=0.

(82)

This means that,
when the background metric can be treated as a small perturbation of Minkowski spacetime
, the term keeping us from performing the residual gauge transformation on
h_{\mu\nu}
vanishes up to the first order in
h^{(B)}_{\mu\nu}
. Then, one is allowed to choose
\varepsilon^{\mu}
satisfying
\tilde{\nabla}^{\mu}\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}\varepsilon_{\nu}=0
, such that
h=h_{0i}=0
. The gauge condition then becomes

\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}h^{\mu\nu}=0.

(83)

In particular, the
\nu=0
component of this equation reads

\partial_{0}h^{00}+\quantity(\Tilde{\Gamma}_{\mu 0}^\mu+\Tilde{\Gamma}_{00}^0)h^{00}+\tilde{\Gamma}_{ij}^{0}h^{ij}=0.

(84)

If the metric perturbation is static
, we get
\tilde{\Gamma}_{\mu 0}^{\mu}=\tilde{\Gamma}_{00}^{0}=0
. Additionally,
if
h^{(B)}_{0i}=0
we also have
\tilde{\Gamma}_{ij}^{0}=0
, and thus

\partial_{0}h^{00}=0.

(85)

Then, since
h_{00}
is just a constant, one can choose suitable boundary conditions such that
h_{00}=0
.

In summary,
for a background metric that is a small static perturbation of flat spacetime with
h^{(B)}_{0i}=0
, the Einstein equation for
h_{\mu\nu}
reads as follows

\tilde{\nabla}^{\mu}\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}h_{ij}+2\tilde{R}_{ikjl}h^{kl}=0,

(86)

with the TT gauge conditions

\begin{split}\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}h^{\mu\nu}=0,\\
\tilde{g}^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}=0,\\
h_{0\mu}=0.\end{split}

(87)

The linearized vacuum equation in the TT gage can be obtained from the extremization of the action
Chawla and Parikh 2023

S_{\rm grav}=\frac{1}{32\pi}\int\differential^{4}x\,\quantity(\frac{1}{2}h_{ij}\Tilde{\nabla}^\mu\Tilde{\nabla}_\mu h^{ij}+h_{ij}\Tilde{R}^{ikjl}h_{kl}).

(88)

We can check that setting
\delta S=0
leads to Eq. (
86
).

A.2
Newtonian limit

Let us see how the linearized Einstein equation works when the background is described in the Newtonian limit. In that case, we consider the expansion (
79
) with
h^{(B)}_{\mu\nu}=-2\phi\delta_{\mu\nu}
, with
\phi
being the time-independent Newtonian potential. The gravitational action is then reduced to

\displaystyle S_{\rm grav}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{64\pi}\int\differential^{4}x\,\tilde{g}^{\mu\nu}h_{ij}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}h^{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{64\pi}\int\differential^{4}x\,\quantity(h_{ij}\Box h^{ij}+2\phi\,h_{ij}\delta_{\mu\nu}\partial^\mu\partial^\nu h^{ij}).

(89)

The equation of motion for the metric perturbation,
\delta S_{\rm grav}=0
, reads

\Box h_{ij}+2\phi\delta_{\mu\nu}\partial^{\mu}\partial^{\nu}h_{ij}=0.

(90)

For a potential of the form
\phi(r)=-GM/r
, and upon quantization of the degrees of freedom of gravitational radiation, one finds that this coupling leads the mass
M
to scatter an incident graviton by an angle
\theta

Appendix B
Gravitational noise kernel

In this appendix, we compute the noise kernel encoding the influence of the quantized gravitational field, defined by Eq. (
26b
), for different initial states. In what follows, we shall consider the position operators to be independent of the polarizations and of the direction of
\mathbf{k}
, that is,
q_{s}(t,\mathbf{k})=q_{\omega}(t)
,
\omega=\absolutevalue{\vb{k}}
. Within this assumption, we may write the gravitational noise kernel as

N_{\rm g}^{ijkl}(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{1}{32}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential\omega\,\omega^{2}\quantity[A_{\Omega}^{ijkl}\dv[2]{t}\dv[2]{{t'}}G_\omega(t,t')+A_{\Omega}^{ijnl}{T_n}^k\dv[2]{t}G_\omega(t,t')+A_{\Omega}^{njkl}{T_n}^i\dv[2]{{t'}}G_\omega(t,t')],

(92)

where we introduced the Hadamard function

G_{\omega}(t,t^{\prime})=\expectationvalue{\acomm{q_\omega(t)}{q_\omega(t')}}_{\rm g}.

(93)

The angular integrals are given by
Cho and Hu 2022
;
Moreira and Céleri 2023

A_{\Omega}^{ijkl}\equiv\int\differential\Omega\sum_{s}\epsilon_{s}^{ij}(\mathbf{k})\epsilon_{s}^{kl}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{8\pi}{15}\quantity[3\qty(\delta^{ik}\delta^{jl}+\delta^{il}\delta^{jk})-2\delta^{ij}\delta^{kl}].

(94)

As we discussed in Sec.
IV
, we shall be interested only in the components
z
,
N_{\rm g}(t,t^{\prime})\equiv N_{\rm g}^{3333}(t,t^{\prime})
, which are then given by

N_{\rm g}(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{\pi}{15}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential\omega\,\omega^{2}\quantity{\dv[2]{t}\dv[2]{{t'}}G_\omega(t,t')+T_{zz}\qty[\dv[2]{t}G_\omega(t,t')+\dv[2]{{t'}}G_\omega(t,t')]}.

(95)

Next, let us explicitly evaluate the Green’s function (
93
) for different initial states. Since the free Hamiltonian for each mode of the gravitational field is the one of a harmonic oscillator, Eq. (
19
), we may write the position operators in the Heisenberg picture as

q_{\omega}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2m_{\rm g}\omega}}\quantity(a_\omega e^{-i\omega t}+a_\omega^\dagger e^{i\omega t}),

(96)

where the
a
’s (
a^{\dagger}
’s) are annihilation (creation) operators satisfying the usual commutation relations,

\begin{split}\commutator{a_\omega}{a_{\omega'}}&=\commutator{a_\omega^\dagger}{a_{\omega'}^\dagger}=0,\\
\commutator{a_\omega}{a_{\omega'}^\dagger}&=\delta_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}.\end{split}

(97)

Then a direct calculation yields

G_{\omega}(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{2}{m_{\rm g}\omega^{2}}\expectationvalue{H_\omega}_{\rm g}\cos\omega(t-t^{\prime})+\frac{1}{m_{\rm g}\omega}\quantity[\expval{a_\omega^2}_{\rm g}e^{-i\omega(t+t')}+\expval{\qty(a^\dagger_\omega)^2}_{\rm g}e^{i\omega(t+t')}],

(98)

where

H_{\omega}=\omega\quantity(a_\omega^\dagger a_\omega+\frac{1}{2})=\frac{\omega}{2}\anticommutator{a_\omega}{a_\omega^\dagger}

(99)

is the free Hamiltonian operator of the harmonic oscillator with frequency
\omega
. We may now compute the noise kernel for different initial states of the gravitational field. These were explicitly computed in
Moreira and Céleri 2023
, so here we shall only list the results.

If the initial state is the vacuum state, one finds

G_{\omega}^{\rm(vac)}(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{1}{m_{\rm g}\omega}\cos{\omega(t-t')}.

(100a)

Alternatively, if we take the gravitons to be initially in a thermal state with temperature
T_{\textrm{g}}
, we have

G_{\omega}^{\rm(th)}(t,t^{\prime})=G_{\omega}^{\rm(vac)}(t,t^{\prime})+\frac{2}{m_{\rm g}\omega}\frac{1}{e^{\omega/T_{\rm g}}-1}\cos{\omega(t-t')}.

(100b)

For gravitons initially in a coherent state with displacement parameter
\alpha
, the Green’s function reads

G_{\omega}^{\rm(coh)}(t,t^{\prime})=G_{\omega}^{\rm(vac)}(t,t^{\prime})+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{m_{\rm g}\omega}\cos(\omega t)\cos(\omega t').

(100c)

Finally, for gravitons initially in a squeezed state with squeeze parameter
\zeta=re^{i\varphi}
, we obtain

G_{\omega}^{\rm(sq)}(t,t^{\prime})=(\cosh{2r})G_{\omega}^{\rm(vac)}(t,t^{\prime})-\frac{\sinh{2r}}{m_{\rm g}\omega}\cos[\omega(t+t^{\prime})-\varphi].

(100d)

Our next task is then to simply plug in the results of Eqs. (
100
) into Eq. (
95
) for the gravitational noise kernel. For instance, we have, for the initial vacuum state,

\displaystyle N_{\rm g}^{\rm(vac)}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{15\pi}\quantity[\int_0^\Lambda\dd\omega\,\omega^5\cos{\omega(t-t')}-2T_{zz}\int_0^\Lambda\dd\omega\,\omega^3\cos{\omega(t-t')}]
