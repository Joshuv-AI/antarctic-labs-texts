Cutting-edge developments in plastic biodegradation and upcycling via engineering approaches
Authors: Zeinab Rezaei, Amir Soleimani Dinani, Hamid Moghimi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The increasing use of plastics has resulted in the production of high quantities of plastic waste that pose a serious risk to the environment. The upcycling of plastics into value-added products offers a potential solution for resolving the plastics environmental crisis. Recently, various microorganisms and their enzymes have been identified for their ability to degrade plastics effectively. Furthermore, many investigations have revealed the application of plastic monomers as carbon sources for bio-upcycling to generate valuable materials such as biosurfactants, bioplastics, and biochemicals. With the advancement in the fields of synthetic biology and metabolic engineering, the construction of high-performance microbes and enzymes for plastic removal and bio-upcycling can be achieved. Plastic valorization can be optimized by improving uptake and conversion efficiency, engineering transporters and enzymes, metabolic pathway reconstruction, and also using a chemo-biological hybrid approach. This review focuses on engineering approaches for enhancing plastic removal and the methods of depolymerization and upcycling processes of various microplastics. Additionally, the major challenges and future perspectives for facilitating the development of a sustainable circular plastic economy are highlighted.

Introduction

Plastic, a product of the 20th-century petrochemical revolution, has revolutionized industries worldwide, offering unparalleled versatility and durability (Filho et al., 2021). Synthetic plastics include polypropylene (PP), polyethylene (PE), polystyrene (PS), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), and polyvinylchloride (PVC), which are derived from fossil hydrocarbons (Zhao et al., 2022b). Owing to the many applications of plastic-based products, remarkable amounts of plastics have been released, resulting in severe environmental challenges (Lee et al., 2023). To overcome these challenges and manage plastic pollution, recycling, and upcycling approaches can be used.

Upcycling is an emerging concept that aims at converting plastic monomers into valuable materials, aiming to prevent waste and use fewer raw materials (Lee et al., 2023). Among various recycling and upcycling technologies, microbial or enzymatic decomposition offers a potential solution, with microorganisms capable of metabolizing plastics under mild conditions without hazardous chemicals, enabling waste breakdown without prior sorting or cleaning (Kumar et al., 2023; Maleki Rad et al., 2022). Integrating plastic waste degradation with bio-upcycling in a single bioprocess presents a novel approach to managing plastic waste, offering prospects for a more environmentally friendly and sustainable plastic industry (Lee et al., 2023).

Moreover, plastic valorization can be optimized by various metabolic engineering and synthetic biological techniques such as improving uptake and conversion efficiency, engineering transporters and enzymes, and metabolic pathway reconstruction. However, few investigations have focused on plastic bio-upcycling approaches and innovative biotechnological processes for efficiently valorizing plastic waste. Therefore, this review offers detailed insights into enzyme and metabolic engineering approaches to improve plastic biodegradation. Upcycling technology and future perspectives for facilitating the development of a sustainable circular plastic economy are also considered.

Enhancing plastics biodegradation by engineering approaches and synthetic biology

There has been a notable increase in academic and industry efforts to develop polymer recycling methods over the past few years (Gilani et al., 2023). The rate of plastic removal depends on the chemical structure of the plastic and the presence of suitable microbes. Until now, various studies have examined the structure of microplastics and the ability of microorganisms and their enzymes to degrade them (Giyahchi and Moghimi, 2023; Ahmed et al., 2022; Cai et al., 2023; Miri et al., 2022; Othman et al., 2021; Tareen et al., 2022; Vaksmaa et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2023). Moreover, ISO and ASTM standards provide various detailed guidelines for analyzing biodegradation in distinct environments (refer to ASTM D6400 & D6868, EN13432, ISO 17088, and ISO, 18606 for composting environment, ISO 17556 and ASTM D 5988 for soil, and ASTM D6691, D7991for marine environments) (Funabashi et al., 2009; Sawada, 1998; Zumstein et al., 2019). The most essential aspect of these standards is to show the plastic utilized by microorganisms through respirometric measurements (Zumstein et al., 2019). Microbial cells can be genetically engineered to degrade plastic contaminants in the environment more effectively. Genetic engineering involves genetic modification, genetic manipulation, gene cloning, and recombinant DNA technology (Jaiswal et al., 2020). The application of this approach for plastic degradation is still in the early steps. The advancements in genetic engineering tools including Zinc finger proteins, TALENs, and CRISPR/Cas9, have made it easier for researchers to conduct genetic studies. The incorporation of genes encoding enzymes for plastic remediation in nondegradable organisms can be considered using these strategies (Jaiswal et al., 2020; Priya et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022).

On the other hand, the field of systems biology provides insights into the expression of DNA, RNA, and metabolites, as well as their interactions during remediation through various omics methods like genomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics, and proteomics (Priya et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022). The systems biology method uncovers the essential microbial processes for optimizing, predicting, and assessing cellular function, gene products, and survival strategies in stressful environments. The use of systems biology tools also provides valuable information about the design, and optimization of synthetic consortia (Jaiswal et al., 2020).

Despite achieving success with the genetic manipulation of microorganisms in laboratory conditions, these organisms have exhibited unsatisfactory outcomes in the field. The biodegradability process can be greatly enhanced by the full knowledge of the chemical structure of the plastic and the metabolic system of the microorganism. Thus, bioinformatics has become a useful tool for the prediction and assessment of bioremediation. Some well-known databases, such as UM-BBD/PPS (The University of Minnesota Biocatalysis/Biodegradation Database and Pathway Prediction System), enviPath tool (The Environmental Contaminant Biotransformation Pathway Resource), MetaCyc and BioCyc, provide useable data about metabolic routes, genes, and different enzymatic reactions that aid biodegradation (Priya et al., 2022). However, the unavailability, inaccessibility, and validation of bioinformatics data are the major drawbacks that require consideration in future research (Zhou et al., 2022). To resolve these challenges, computational tools are utilized alongside experimental protocols, which are usually reinforced with synthetic biology standards and fine-tuned by adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE). The Design-Build-Test-Analyze (DBTA) cycle continues until optimal performance and cost-effective processes can be established for the engineered cell factory (Dvo et al., 2017).

Metabolic engineering

The objectives of systems biology align perfectly with the goals of metabolic engineering. These fields are complementary and have formed the field of systems metabolic engineering. Metabolic engineering is the process of optimizing genetic processes to achieve several goals. These goals include improving the yield of native products, introducing the production of new products in the host cell, and enhancing the ability of the cell to degrade substrates or improve substrate uptake. Additionally, metabolic engineering can increase cell tolerance towards toxic compounds (Dvo et al., 2017; Nielsen et al., 2014; Tran et al., 2021).

There are three main steps for engineering bioremediation routes using systemic biology methods. The first step is to identify the pollutant and find an appropriate catabolic pathway. Computational tools provide information on the physicochemical characteristics of target compounds, enzymes, and metabolites in catabolic pathways. To date, several useful databases and prediction systems (UM-BBD/PPS, enviPath tool, MetaCyc, and BioCyc) have been developed (Bhatt et al., 2020; Dvo et al., 2017).

Recent advances in metagenomic analysis and engineering of uncultivated microorganisms can aid in developing bioremediation processes for different pollutants. Microbial DNA from environmental samples is extracted and inserted into an expression vector. This is done to create metagenomic libraries in laboratory bacteria like E. coli. The sub-clones are then screened for identifying the functional enzymes using a specific medium that contains the target pollutant. Finally, DNA sequencing and genome assembly are used to discover new enzymes and biodegradation pathways from the bacterial communities (Tran et al., 2021). The essential genetic sequences for the desired routes are synthesized and incorporated into plasmids. The kinetic characteristics of the realized routes can be investigated by high-throughput methods (Tran et al., 2021).

In the second step, the genetic material is transferred to other microbes (hosts) manually using plasmids as carriers, and the effectiveness of biodegradation is then evaluated. The selection of an appropriate microbial host is important in any engineering study for the improvement of a whole-cell degrader (Tran et al., 2021). This host should pose high tolerance and metabolic capacity. No single naturally occurring bacterium has all the necessary characteristics to achieve optimum degradation. The rational selection of appropriate hosts has been significantly developed by systems biology, especially omic techniques. The advancements in first- or next-generation sequencing approaches alongside advanced algorithms and computational tools have led to significant progress in our comprehension of the interactions that occur both within cells and between cells and their surrounding environments (Dvo et al., 2017).

The final step is to construct and optimize the pathway, which is the most challenging aspect of the engineering process. This is particularly the case for de novo synthetic routes composed of enzymes from various sources, which are transferred to a new host bacterium. Furthermore, enzymes may not be balanced, interfering with the native metabolic network. Accumulation of intermediates can also hinder cell growth, and there may be a shortage of cofactors. The range of tools available to address these problems is constantly expanding (Dvo et al., 2017; Tran et al., 2021). For more details see Dvo et al. (2017).

Additionally, cells are damaged and eventually die as a result of exposure of host cells to contaminants. Some methods were applied to enhance cellular tolerance like changes in membrane lipids, phenotypic screening using ALE, alternation in gene expression, genome shuffling, and directed evolution. Among these methods, ALE is widely utilized to increase microbial tolerance in harsh circumstances (Jaiswal et al., 2020; Tran et al., 2021).

Enzyme engineering

One effective method to increase the efficiency of biodegradation is the engineering of enzymes involved in the plastic degradation pathways. Protein engineering primarily employs two approaches: guided evolution and rational design. Despite recent advancements, the absence of effective high-throughput screening methods remains a major challenge for the guided evolution of plastic-degrading enzymes. Recent studies focusing on designing plastic-degrading enzymes predominantly utilize rational design owing to the wealth of mechanistic and structural data available for these enzymes (Zhu et al., 2022). There are several ways to optimize enzyme performance via a rational protein engineering strategy, such as improving enzyme thermostability, increasing the attachment of the substrate to the enzyme's active site, and developing contact between the enzyme surface and substrate (Fig. 2). The elevated thermostability is particularly crucial for breaking down industrially significant plastics like PET. The flexibility of the amorphous regions of the polymers increases as the reaction temperature nears or surpasses the glass transition temperature of plastics, facilitating substrate-enzyme surface interaction and access to the active site, thereby increasing enzymes' activity (Wei and Zimmermann, 2017). Strategies like introducing disulfide links or salt bridge, engineering hydrogen bond formation, and Incorporating glycosylation into enzymes have a determining role in increasing the thermostability of enzymes by ensuring proper protein folding and enhancing heat resistance (Then et al., 2016; Shirke et al., 2018).

Another approach to engineering enzymes involves expanding the active site aperture to improve access to polymeric substrates (Araújo et al., 2007). The efficacy of various enzymes (e.g., PETase, Pseudomonas aestusnigri hydrolase, Cut190, and MHETase) has been successfully improved through this strategy. It should be noted that while enlarging the substrate binding area may not always lead to improved catalytic efficiency, excessively broad active sites may reduce substrate affinity owing to decreased binding capacity (Kawabata et al., 2017).

There is another option that involves changing the surface characteristics of enzymes and improving enzyme-substrate contact. This interaction depends on hydrophobic and electrostatic bonds between amino acids on the enzyme surface and substrate molecules. Neutralization of the enzyme surface can reduce the electrostatic repulsion with the plastic substrate and increase the binding efficiency (Herrero Acero et al., 2013). Increasing the hydrophobicity of the enzyme can also enhance the contact surface between the enzyme and the substrate (Hiraishi et al., 2010a, Hiraishi et al., 2010b). However, excessive hydrophobic residues can impair catalytic performance by inducing enzyme aggregation or disrupting protein structure (Polyester et al., 2016). Furthermore, certain enzymes can be combined with foreign binding modules, such as carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs), fungal hydrophobins, and bioactive polypeptides to enhance their connection with plastic surfaces (Biundo et al., 2019; Islam et al., 2019).

Other efforts to optimize enzyme activity include addressing product inhibition by combining effective enzymes in pathways, enhancing catalytic promiscuity, and developing multifunctional biocatalysts (Groβ et al., 2017). It should be considered that the promoter, the expression elements, and the signal peptide should be optimized to maximize the expression of enzymes (Thakur et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020). Silent mutations and amino acid substitutions in signal peptides can also led to improved secretion of the enzymes. However, it is currently impossible to predict the impacts of specific mutations on a particular signal peptide. The only engineered signal peptide example involved mutating pelB, resulting in a 1.7-fold increase in PETase secretion (Shi et al., 2021).

Plastic valorization into value-added materials

Developing alternatives to microplastic pollutants can address the increasing concern about rising plastic pollution (Behera and Das, 2023). Within the process of microplastic degradation, if plastic wastes or intermediates can be utilized to generate valuable products, which is commonly called upcycling or valorization, we can further achieve the circular economic feasibility of plastic waste (Mudondo et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2021). The upcycling process starts with the degradation of plastics (Sohn et al., 2020). Ultimately, the waste materials are valorized into products of greater value and quality (which may or may not have chemical similarities with plastics) (Fig. 3) (Connor et al., 2023; Sohn et al., 2020).Fig. 3Schematic diagram of chemical upcycling, biological upcycling, and chemo-biological hybrid upcycling processes.Fig. 3

The common plastic manufacturing process consumes a large share of the total fossil fuel, resulting in significant greenhouse gas emissions (Singh et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024). Therefore, the linear plastic economy, “take-make-waste”, should be substituted by a circular model, where used plastic is recycled back into the manufacturing process to create useful materials (Connor et al., 2023). Enhancing value chain efficiency, recycling, and producing renewable plastics provides win-win opportunities to close the carbon loop and reduce greenhouse gas emissions (Kajaste and Oinas, 2021).

Nowadays, the upcycling of plastics into chemical feedstocks has been successfully commercialized by Dow Chemical Company and Fuenix Eco Group, proving to be a sustainable and profitable business when applied correctly (Zhao et al., 2022). Therefore, there is a growing interest in valorization technologies, and novel upcycling processes are now extensively investigated. Mechanical recycling requires a sorting process and the number of times plastics can be recycled is limited as the recycled plastic deteriorates during plastic pelletizing or grinding (Lomwongsopon and Varrone, 2022). Chemical and thermal processes are usually applied for the depolymerization of general industrial plastics. Chemical processes include alcoholysis, hydrolysis, glycolysis, hydrogenolysis, aminolysis, and ammonolysis, while thermal processes include pyrolysis, hydrothermal liquefaction, and microwave irradiation (Lomwongsopon and Varrone, 2022).

On the other hand, bio-upcycling, using microbial enzymes and/or whole-cell biocatalysts, can play a crucial role in plastics valorization (Ellis et al., 2021). Compared to chemical upcycling, the bio-upcycling process does not require harsh operational circumstances such as high temperatures, thus exhibiting a significant reduction in energy consumption (Lee et al., 2023). In addition, biological conversion has the advantage of being able to be used for polluted plastic waste which does not need prior separation of various fractions. The high selectivity of enzymes could facilitate the gradual elimination of individual components in plastic mixtures, making the downstream process easier (Ellis et al., 2021; Valavanidis, 2023). Based on the products, it can also decrease greenhouse gas emissions by 36–70 % (Lee et al., 2023).

However, remarkable drawbacks are observed in bio-upcycling such as high operating costs (especially, for enzyme production), time-consuming, and low substrate loading (Mudondo et al., 2023). Therefore, chemo-biological upcycling has been considered a practical and effective approach to plastic waste conversion (Mudondo et al., 2023). In this hybrid biochemical method, plastics can degraded through chemical or thermal pretreatment and then, microorganisms can use these depolymerized materials as substrates for the production of high-value products (Kim et al., 2019). Polyethylene terephthalate (PET) upcycling

Although PET has a higher rate of recycling than other microplastics, 69% of the recycled PET was utilized for lower-grade applications like fiber, film, or strapping. Thus, the current PET management system seems to lack a high level of circularity, indicating a requirement for novel upcycling methods to transform PET into value-added materials (Lomwongsopon and Varrone, 2022). Generally, PET can be metabolized into its monomers (Terephthalic acid (TPA) and ethylene glycol (EG)) via chemical or enzymatic hydrolysis, and the resulting waste stream can be used as feedstock for microbial production of chemicals such as hydroxyalkanoyloxy alkanoates (HAAs), vanillic acid, gallic acid, muconic acid, and catechol (Diao et al., 2023; Jung et al., 2023; H. T. Kim et al., 2021).

TPA is widely recognized as a favorable material for the generation of diverse beneficial substrates. Microorganisms transport TPA through specific TPA transporters and convert it to protocatechuate (PCA) via 1,6-dihydroxycyclohexa-2,4-diene-dicarboxylate (DCD). TphA12A3 is a dioxygenase that converts TPA to DCD, while TphB is a dehydrogenase that converts DCD to PCA. PCA is broken down into different metabolites like pyruvate, acetyl-COA, oxaloacetate, and succinyl-COA. The metabolism of TPA has been identified in diverse microbes, like Comamonas, Pseudomonas, Rhodococcus, Ramlibacter, and Idonella (Dissanayake and Jayakody, 2021; S. Lee et al., 2023; Mudondo et al., 2023). Besides TPA, two different ways of metabolizing EG have been identified in microorganisms. Under aerobic conditions, EG is oxidized to glyoxylate by using O2 and nicotinamide. On the other hand, under anaerobic conditions, EG is converted into acetaldehyde, which then forms acetyl-CoA, and is metabolized in the TCA cycle (Lee et al., 2023). Several investigations focus on PET bio-upcycling using TPA and/or EG as substrate (Table 3). For example, research conducted by Sadler and Wallace (2021) exhibited the bioconversion of TPA into vanillin by using an engineered E.coli. Two plasmids encoding different necessary enzymes for desired metabolic pathways were transferred to this strain of E.coli and a production yield of 79% was achieved (Sadler and Wallace, 2021). Energy and time-consuming processes like depolymerization of PET, valorization of its monomers, purification of products, and evaporation of water can be connected with the high production cost of upcycled materials from PET. The economic viability of upcycling is highly associated with the effectiveness of depolymerization (Kim et al., 2019). Enzymatic hydrolysis of PET leads to low substrate loading and slow removal of PET. In order to increase the effectiveness of the bioconversion of plastic monomers, it has been attempted to combine biological methods with chemical hydrolysis (Lee et al., 2023). In this hybrid biochemical method, PET is degraded through chemical pretreatment like glycolysis into hydrolysates with low molecular weight (BHET, MHET, and PET oligomers). Enzymatic production of TPA from PET hydrolysates is more efficient due to improved enzyme accessibility compared to direct production from PET polymers (Kim et al., 2019). For instance, Kang et al. (2020) applied a chemo-biological process to upcycle PET waste to 2-pyrone-4, 6-dicarboxylic acid (PDC), a useful material for bio-degradable plastic (production yield: 96.08%) (Kang et al., 2020).

Advanced synthetic microbiology tools have been used to develop several models and non-model microorganisms for the valorization of PET in recent years (Dissanayake and Jayakody, 2021). TPA and EG can be transformed into target materials through metabolic engineering, but disadvantages include the slow rate of growth and limited engineering approaches for the direct use of microbial chassis for generating valuable chemicals (Mudondo et al., 2023). Future research can concentrate on improving chassis engineering to enhance productivity, eliminate unnecessary metabolic routes, and strengthen substrate utilization ability for the development of industrial strains.

Polyurethane (PU) and polystyrene (PS) upcycling

The recycling of PU polymers is challenging due to their complex composition, consisting of different monomers, like 1,4-butanediol (1,4-BDO), adipic acid (AA), 2,4-toluene diamine (2,4-TDA), and EG. Additionally, 2,4-TDA is a carcinogenic chemical that poses a significant environmental and human health risk (Lee et al., 2023). The enzymes' low accessibility has also prevented efficient biodegradation of PU (Lee et al., 2023; Orlando et al., 2023).

Pseudomonas species can be engineered to use 1,4-BDO, 2,4-TDA, and EG as carbon sources (Espinosa et al., 2020; Schwanemann et al., 2020). In one study, three strains of P. putida were evolved to use PU waste as a carbon source, resulting in the production of 0.10 g/L of rhamnolipids. Due to the inhibition of the growth of the consortium by 2,4 TDA, reactive extraction was performed before upcycling (Utomo et al., 2020). In another research, P. putida KT2440 was evolved to utilize AA and convert it to polyhydroxyalkanoate (PHA) using metabolic engineering and ALE (Ackermann et al., 2021). Additionally, the new strain of P.capeferrum TDA 1 was identified to produce PHA from 2,4-TPA (Utomo et al., 2021).

Although the research on the valorization of plastic monomers is in the early steps, the bioconversion of styrene has also been studied. Styrene is a monomer of polystyrene and it can be converted into mandelic acid. Mandelic acid is a beneficial product with diverse applications, but its chemical synthesis often requires the use of toxic cyanide. Thus, biosynthesis is a great alternative (Lee et al., 2023). The yield of up to 328 mM of mandelic acid was achieved via 4-step enzyme cascades in E. coli (Lukito et al., 2021). Moreover, PHA can be produced from styrene by microbial conversion. For example, Pseudomonas putida CA-3 was engineered to grow on styrene and transform it to MCL-PHAs (0.28 g/g styrene) (Goff et al., 2007). In another study, Cupriavidus necator H16 was reported to transform post-consumer PS to PHA using thermal oxidation followed by bioconversion (Johnston et al., 2018).

Other plastic monomers' upcycling

It has been shown that a combination of thermochemistry and biological conversion is commonly used to upcycle PEs (Lomwongsopon and Varrone, 2022). Pyrolysis materials derived from PE can be converted into fatty acids, PHA, biosurfactants, and succinic acid by a variety of metabolic routes (Qin et al., 2021). Guzik et al. (2014), for instance, utilized the hydrocarbon products from PE thermal treatment as the substrate for bioconversion. They showed that Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO-1 can upcycle this substrate into PHA in the presence of rhamnolipids (20% of CDW) (Guzik et al., 2014). In another study, Ralstonia eutropha H16 has been reported to generate PHAs from oxidized PE wax (33.8% CDW). Researchers also indicated that the production rate of PHAs was lower (32%) when non-oxidized PE wax was used as a substrate (Radecka et al., 2016). It could be due to the presence of less recalcitrant carbon sources in oxidized PE wax which bacteria are able to gain access to. However, some studies have been carried out to directly use untreated PE for the production of PHA by various strains like Acinetobacter pittii IRN19, Pseudomonas putidella LS46, and Cuprividus necator H16; but their yield is very low (Lomwongsopon and Varrone, 2022).

The developed upcycling strategies for polypropylene rely on high-temperature depolymerization processes (Lomwongsopon and Varrone, 2022). Johnston et al. (2019) reported the bioconversion of thermal-oxidized PP into PAH through the engineering of Cupriavidus neactor H16 and 1.36 g/L of PAH was produced after 2 days (Johnston et al., 2019). Another study showed a hybrid process using pyrolysis and biological valorization (by Yarrowia lipolytica), to transform PP into fatty acids (Mihreteab et al., 2021). Unlike PE and PP, there are no reports of valorization of PVC in valuable products.

A key technology for dealing with real post-consumer plastics would be the ability to upcycle mixed plastic waste. Only one study has suggested the most promising technologies in this field and applied chemical oxidation and biological upcycling for the valorization of mixed plastic waste (including PET, HDPE, and polystyrene). The plastics were broken down into a mixture of oxygenated materials by applying metal-promoted autoxidation. These materials were utilized as substrates for biological conversion to β-ketoadipate or polyhydroxyalkanoates by an engineered P.putida (Sullivan et al., 2022). In addition, utilizing mixed consortia may be a possible approach for the recycling and upcycling of mixed plastic wastes.

Challenges and future perspectives

Biological upcycling offers a promising approach to addressing plastic waste pollution. However, some challenges should be considered. One of these challenges could be the excessive content of additives and plasticizers. These additives cannot be removed from plastics because of the low rate of molecular diffusion and they inhibit microbial upcycling (Jung et al., 2023). Some fungi and bacteria have been identified with the ability to utilize plasticizers and convert them to nutrients (Lee et al., 2023). Further research into developing microbes that can use both plastic backbones and plasticizers should be carried out by metabolic engineering and synthetic biology to extend the applicability of bio-upcycle technology. The other problem is that the enzyme and the microbe must be compatible in terms of pH, temperature, or media when the removal and valorization are performed in a single-pot process. For plastics that should be degraded at high temperatures, the application of thermophiles can be a potential approach for enabling one-pot processing (Ellis et al., 2021).

In order to increase the efficiency of degradation of plastics and biomass recycling, as well as expand the range of target chemicals for that purpose, it is possible to reconstruct a metabolic pathway in host strains (Lee et al., 2023). The metabolic pathway reconstruction has been significantly facilitated by recent developments in the availability of genome, metabolism, or enzyme databases as well as pathways prediction tools. Moreover, developing novel enzymes by engineering or de novo enzyme creation is another strategy for increasing the recycling and upcycling of plastics. However, the major challenge for designing biosensing and bioremediation pathways and considering the potential uses of genetically modified microorganisms is the complexity of interactions between cells and species, as well as the not fully understood relationship between the abiotic and biotic elements that control the breakdown of pollutants in contaminated environments (Dvo et al., 2017). Furthermore, improving microbial adhesion ability and engineering of transporters makes it possible to enhance the efficiency of bio-upcycling (Ellis et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2023).

Nowadays, artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms can change our approach to plastic degradation by providing innovative ways to identify, separate, and predict the degradation rate of various types of plastic waste. The recycling process of plastic materials can be enhanced with these algorithms through the efficient sorting and processing of recyclable plastics, resulting in a more sustainable recycling system (Gopalan et al., 2024; Maraveas et al., 2023). Additionally, there has been a growing use of AI in directed evolution, reducing the amount of experimental cycles required to obtain specific enzymes. This is accomplished by using AI to analyze the characteristics of previously identified variants during screening, making it possible to choose sequences more likely to show the desired functions (Lee et al., 2023). By applying AI, researchers are able to detect microplastics in the environment through machine learning methods, develop biodegradable plastic materials with improved degradation properties, model the decomposition of plastic waste in landfills, and evaluate the environmental effects of plastic degradation processes (Gopalan et al., 2024; Maraveas et al., 2023).

Despite remarkable efforts to identify microbes involved in the removal of plastic waste and upcycling, the industrialization of these processes has not reached the expected level of effectiveness (Lee et al., 2023). The main challenges to large-scale plastic waste conversion are high costs, time demands, the need for presorting, and significant variability in plastic composition, particularly with mixtures. To speed up the commercialization of plastics upcycling, techno-economic evaluations, and specific bioprocessing designs should be carried out on a larger scale using various analytical studies.

Conclusion

In this review, the current status of plastic valorization into valuable products was described. To date, several investigations have demonstrated that different enzymes and microbes can be involved in both depolymerizing and converting plastics into useable materials. However, engineering the microbial strains and their enzymes can increase the efficiency of the decomposition process. Furthermore, by applying synthetic biology and engineering tools, plastic recycling and upcycling can be optimized and accelerated. Although challenges in the area of industrial development continue to exist, it is expected that they will be resolved in the future and allow for the establishment of a sustainable circular plastic economy.
