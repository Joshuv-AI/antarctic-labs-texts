Quantitative Measures for Passive Sonar Texture Analysis


Abstract
Passive sonar signals contain complex characteristics often arising from environmental noise, vessel machinery, and propagation effects. While convolutional neural networks (CNNs) perform well on passive sonar classification tasks, they can struggle with statistical variations that occur in the data. To investigate this limitation, synthetic underwater acoustic datasets are generated that centered on amplitude and period variations. Two metrics are proposed to quantify and validate these characteristics in the context of statistical and structural texture for passive sonar. These measures are applied to real-world passive sonar datasets to assess texture information in the signals and correlate the performances of the models. Results show that CNNs underperform on statistically textured signals, but incorporating explicit statistical texture modeling yields consistent improvements. These findings highlight the importance of quantifying texture information for passive sonar classification. Code is publicly available: https://github.com/Advanced-Vision-and-Learning-Lab/Passive_Sonar_Texture_Analysis .

Introduction

Texture is a fundamental property in both image and audio domains, capturing the underlying structural and statistical patterns in signals
[
1
]
. In images, texture refers to spatial elements such as edges, repetitive structures, and intensity variations. In the audio domain, structural texture corresponds to periodic features like harmonics and modulations
[
2
]
, while statistical texture reflects stochastic variations in amplitude and frequency
[
3
]
. These texture characteristics influence how models interpret signals, especially for complex tasks like passive sonar classification. In underwater sensing, variations in passive sonar may arise due to environmental noise, turbulence, and reflections from dynamic sources
[
4
]
. Conventional features such as Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs), Gammatone filters, and wavelet transforms are widely used. However, they often fail to capture how statistical randomness interacts with structured periodicity
[
5
,
6
]
.

To represent texture more effectively, prior work has explored statistical descriptors including mean, variance, skewness, entropy, and other higher-order distributional measures
[
7
,
8
]
. These help distinguish between noise-like patterns and harmonically rich signals
[
9
]
. Structural texture has been examined using methods such as autocorrelation and harmonic envelope modeling, which capture temporal repetition and spectral regularity
[
10
]
. Despite the importance of both texture types, most deep learning models, especially convolutional neural networks, are optimized for capturing local structural features
[
8
]
. As a result, they may struggle with capturing statistical texture features
[
11
,
12
]
.

The Histogram Layer Time-Delay Neural Network (HLTDNN)
[
13
]
, a particular type of CNN demonstrated that incorporating statistical representations improves passive sonar classification. Building on this work, this manuscript explores whether explicitly quantifying audio texture can provide more guidance on model selection for passive sonar classification. Synthetic datasets are developed to investigate new metrics. Specifically, texture characteristics are validated using two quantification metrics: one entropy-based for statistical texture and another autocorrelation-based for structural texture. These metrics are subsequently applied to real-world datasets to examine how texture composition relates to model performance.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

•

Synthetic underwater acoustic dataset with controlled statistical and structural textures variations.

•

Formal definitions to quantify passive sonar texture.

•

Empirical analysis demonstrating the correlation between texture characteristics of the data and model performance.

Methodology

Three synthetic datasets are designed to simulate sonar-like recordings with controlled texture characteristics that focus on variations of (1) amplitude (statistical texture), (2) period (structural texture), and (3) both amplitude and period (mixed statistical and structural textures). Each dataset includes four vessel classes with 10,000 samples per class, resulting in 40,000 samples per dataset. The signal design is guided by observations from the DeepShip dataset
[
14
]
, where cargo and tanker vessels tend to exhibit lower-frequency signatures, while passengership and tug vessels show greater activity in higher-frequency bands. Reflecting this, two vessel classes in each subset are assigned lower-frequency bands and the other two higher-frequency bands.

2.1
Statistical Texture Dataset

This dataset emphasizes stochastic variation in amplitude and background noise while introducing partial spectral overlap to simulate subtle structural cues. Each ship class is assigned three frequency components, with one shared across adjacent classes to mimic real-world spectral overlap. For instance, Vessel 1 spans 3000–5500 Hz while Vessel 2 spans 4000–5500 Hz; similar overlap continues across all four classes. Each signal is segmented into four equal-duration regions. Within each segment, the amplitude envelope
A^{f}(t)
, defined for each frequency component
f
over time
t\in[0,T]
, is randomly sampled from either a Rayleigh or K-distribution
[
3
]
as shown in
Eq.

1
:

A^{f}(t)\sim\begin{cases}\text{Rayleigh}(\sigma),&\text{with probability }p,\\
\text{K}(k,\theta),&\text{with probability }1-p,\end{cases}

(1)

where
\sigma=0.5
controls the spread of the Rayleigh distribution, while
k=14
and
\theta=1.0
define the shape and scale of the K-distribution. These segment-wise transitions are smoothed using sinusoidal amplitude modulation to avoid abrupt energy shifts. Each class uses a unique combination of modulation depth and rate to vary temporal dynamics.

In
Eq.

2
, background noise
\eta(t)
is added to each signal that consists of class-specific colored noise
\nu(t)
and time impulses:

\eta(t)=\nu(t)+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}}a_{k}\cdot\delta(t-t_{k}),

(2)

where
\nu(t)
represents white or pink noise depending on the class,
\mathcal{I}
is a random set of impulse time indices, and
a_{k}\sim\mathcal{U}[-1,1]
determines the impulse amplitude. For example, Vessel 1 combines white and pink noise, Vessel 2 uses pink noise only, Vessel 3 adds short impulses over white noise, and Vessel 4 incorporates impulsive pink noise. This configuration enriches temporal and spectral variability while preserving distinguishable class characteristics.

2.2
Structural Texture Dataset

This dataset emphasizes periodic and deterministic temporal structure with minimal randomness. Each signal consists of three harmonics derived from a class-specific base frequency
f_{c}
, with slight jitter applied to introduce spectral variation as defined in
Eq.

3
:

f_{k}=k\cdot f_{c}+\delta_{k},\quad\delta_{k}\sim\mathcal{U}[-50,50],

(3)

where
f_{k}
is the frequency of the
k
-th harmonic, and
\delta_{k}
introduces small deviations (sampled from a uniform distribution) to mimic natural variability. Each vessel class is assigned a unique base frequency and amplitude envelope shape to produce distinct structural patterns. Vessels 1 through 4 use base frequencies of 2800 Hz, 3600 Hz, 5400 Hz, and 6200 Hz, respectively, paired with triangular, exponential decay, ramp-up, and ramp-down envelopes. These deterministic envelopes, defined over normalized time
t\in[0,1]
, promote strong periodicity and reduce intra-class randomness. Low-amplitude zero-mean Gaussian noise is added to each signal to not mask the harmonic structure. The standard deviation for the noise was randomly sampled from the range
[0.006,0.01]
. The overall design ensures that each class exhibits strong structural regularity while preserving slight temporal and spectral variability, enabling the evaluation of model sensitivity to well-defined periodic features.

2.3
Mixed Statistical and Structural Textures Dataset

This dataset is designed to simulate acoustic signals that simultaneously exhibit variations in amplitude and period. This combination reflects more realistic underwater sounds where both deterministic patterns and random fluctuations coexist. Each amplitude envelope
\hat{A}^{f}(t)
is generated by modulating a probabilistic envelope with a sinusoidal component as shown in
Eq.

4
:

\hat{A}^{f}(t)=\alpha(t)\cdot\left(1+d\cdot\sin(2\pi rt)\right),

(4)

where
\alpha(t)\sim\text{Rayleigh}(\sigma)
or
\text{K}(k,\theta)
represents the statistical envelope shape, and the sinusoidal term introduces periodic structure. The modulation depth
d
and rate
r
are sampled from class-specific uniform ranges to emulate realistic fluctuations and vessel-dependent vibration patterns.

To ensure smooth temporal evolution between statistical textures, a Gaussian-based blending function is applied to transition between Rayleigh and K-distributed envelopes over the full signal duration. Specifically, for each time point
t
, a weight function
w(t)
based on a normalized Gaussian kernel is used to compute a blended amplitude envelope using
Eq.

5
:

\alpha(t)=w(t)\cdot\alpha_{\text{Rayleigh}}(t)+\left(1-w(t)\right)\cdot\alpha_{\text{K}}(t),

(5)

where
w(t)=\exp\left(-\frac{(t-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)
controls the influence of each distribution at time
t
, and
\mu,\sigma
are class-specific parameters determining the center and width of the texture transition.

Each class uses a unique combination of frequency bands, amplitude distribution sequences, and modulation properties. Vessel classes with lower-frequency profiles (Vessel 1 and Vessel 2) are assigned slower amplitude modulations, reflecting the slower mechanical oscillations of large ships such as cargo or tankers. In contrast, higher-frequency classes (Vessel 3 and Vessel 4) simulate smaller, faster vessels that typically exhibit quicker modulation patterns due to faster propeller activity and particular structural dynamics
[
15
]
.

2.4
Statistical Texture Quantification via Bidirectional Temporal Entropy

To quantify statistical texture in passive sonar signals, the bidirectional entropy-based method inspired by the temporal texturedness indicator proposed in
[
16
]
is adopted for this work. This approach evaluates how Shannon entropy accumulates over time in both forward and reverse directions of a signal. The underlying premise is that statistically textured signals exhibit consistent randomness over time, leading to minimal asymmetry between the two entropy trajectories. Let
x(t)
be the input signal defined over time
t\in[0,T_{\text{obs}}]
, where
T_{\text{obs}}
is the total observation duration. The signal is sampled at a frequency
f_{s}
and divided into overlapping frames of length
L
with hop size
H
. For each frame index
i
, the signal’s amplitude values are binned into a histogram with
M
total bins. The resulting histogram is then normalized to obtain the probability distribution
\{p_{i,k}\}
, where
p_{i,k}
denotes the probability corresponding to the
k
-th bin. The entropy at each step is computed using
6
:

H_{i}=-\sum_{k=1}^{M}p_{i,k}\log_{2}p_{i,k}.

(6)

Entropy is accumulated in two directions: forward using the original signal
x(t)
, and reverse using the time-inverted signal
x_{r}(t)=x(T_{\text{obs}}-t)
. This results in two cumulative entropy curves for the forward and reverse directions, denoted
H_{d}(i)
and
H_{r}(i)
, respectively.

The surface area
S
between these curves captures directional entropy asymmetry and is normalized by the theoretical maximum
S_{\text{max}}=T_{\text{obs}}\cdot\log_{2}M
, which corresponds to the entropy of a white noise signal. The relative entropy is defined as
P_{\text{rel}}=\frac{H_{\text{final}}}{\log_{2}M}
, where
H_{\text{final}}
is the final cumulative entropy in the forward direction. The variable
P_{T}
represents the proportion of the signal duration required for the forward and reverse entropy curves to converge. Using these components, the final Statistical Texture Score (StaTS) is defined in
Eq.

7
:

\text{StaTS}_{\text{stat}}=5\cdot P_{\text{rel}}\cdot\left(1-P_{T}\cdot\frac{S}{S_{\text{max}}}\right),

(7)

Higher scores reflect signals with strong statistical texture, characterized by high entropy, rapid forward–reverse convergence, and minimal directional asymmetry.

2.5
Structural Texture Quantification via Autocorrelation

The Structural Texture Score (StrTS) is introduced to quantify periodicity and structural regularity in passive sonar signals using the normalized autocorrelation function. Autocorrelation measures the similarity between a signal and its time-shifted versions, making it effective for identifying repeating harmonic or modulated patterns
[
17
]
. The signal is first transformed to be zero-mean before computing the autocorrelation. To ensure robustness across signals of different durations, the maximum lag is set adaptively as 25% of the total signal duration, with safeguards to prevent it from being too short or excessively long. This approach ensures that the method captures relevant structural patterns without being affected by noise or irrelevant long-term dependencies. Only positive lags beyond zero are considered, and the first significant positive peak is selected as the dominant periodic component. Negative peaks, indicating anti-phase alignment, are excluded.

Let
R(\tau)
denote the normalized autocorrelation value at lag
\tau
, where
\tau\in[1,\tau_{\text{max}}]
, and
\tau_{\text{max}}
is the maximum lag considered. The lag corresponding to the first significant positive peak is identified as the dominant periodic component as shown in
Eq.

8
:

\tau^{*}=\arg\max_{\tau\in[1,\tau_{\text{max}}]}R(\tau),

(8)

The corresponding autocorrelation value at this lag,
R(\tau^{*})
, reflects the strength of the periodic structure and is scaled to produce the StrT in
Eq.

9
:

\text{StrTS}=5\cdot R(\tau^{*}),

(9)

The score lies within the range
[0,5]
, where higher values indicate stronger and more regular periodicity. For consistency, both StrTS and StaTS share this 0–5 scale, where lower scores indicate weak periodicity or less texture and higher scores reflect stronger structural or statistical patterns.

In addition to the scalar score, self-similarity matrices offer a complementary visual representation of structural repetition. These matrices capture pairwise similarity between overlapping frames of the signal, highlighting the repeated temporal pattern. Strong diagonal structures in the matrix correspond to high self-similarity, while noisy or unstructured matrices indicate a weak or lack of repetition.

As shown in
Fig.

, these visualizations qualitatively reflect periodic structure and align well with the StrTS scores. Higher StrTS scores correspond to clearer, more structured diagonal patterns, reinforcing the metric’s interpretability and relevance as a measure of structural texture.

All audio signals are resampled to 32 kHz for consistency. Each resampled signal is segmented into five-second intervals, except for the VTUAD dataset, which provides pre-segmented one-second clips with predefined dataset partitions. Segments are converted into logarithmic mel-frequency spectrograms using a Hanning window (window size 1024, hop 320) with 1024 mel-frequency bins. No data augmentation is applied to preserve the native texture characteristics. For all datasets (except VTUAD), samples are split into 70% training, 10% validation, and 20% test sets. Classification experiments use two models: TDNN as the baseline, and HLTDNN, which incorporates a histogram-based feature layer to enhance statisical texture representation. Both models are trained for 100 epochs using the Adagrad optimizer (learning rate
1\times 10^{-3}
, batch size 128). Test set accuracy is used for model evaluation. In addition to model performance, each dataset is analyzed using the two texture metrics (StrTS and StaTS) to provide insight into the texture properties of the signals.

Results and Discussion

4.1
Synthetic and Real-World Datasets Evaluation

In the mixed statistical and structural textures dataset, where both texture types coexist, HLTDNN achieves near-perfect accuracy (
99.08\pm 0.88
) and shows a +26.90% gain over TDNN. This highlights HLTDNN’s adaptability in handling hybrid texture profiles. On the DeepShip dataset, which exhibits moderate statistical and lower structural texture complexity, HLTDNN outperforms TDNN by +8.06%. Despite being a real-world dataset with inherent variability, the gain shows HLTDNN’s effectiveness in generalizing to practical scenarios. VTUAD, the largest dataset, contains high values in both StrTS and StatS, and HLTDNN again shows a strong improvement of +10.53%. This result confirms the model’s scalability and ability to learn from complex, large-scale real-world data.

5
Conclusion

This work investigated the role of structural and statistical texture in CNN-based passive sonar classification. Statistically rich signals frequently occur in passive sonar environments due to stochastic propagation, scattering, and background interference — phenomena that standard CNNs may fail to capture effectively. By constructing synthetic underwater datasets with controlled texture attributes, it was possible to isolate and analyze model behavior across varying degrees of signal complexity. Experimental findings show that traditional CNN architectures such as TDNN are more sensitive to structural regularities, whereas the texture-aware HLTDNN demonstrates improved performance on signals characterized by strong statistical texture.

These results support the importance of explicitly modeling statistical texture in passive acoustic target classification. The proposed statistical and structural texture quantification metrics further contribute to model interpretability by linking signal properties to performance characteristics. Future directions include generating synthetic data samples to adapt audio foundation models such as AudioPaLM
[
20
]
and AudioGPT
[
21
]
for passive sonar, analyzing texture sensitivity in transformer-based architectures
such as the Audio Spectrogram Transformer (AST)
[
22
]
, embedding texture-aware regularization into training
objectives, leveraging more advanced approaches for data generation such as diffusion models
[
23
]
and applying the texture metrics to other audio domains.
