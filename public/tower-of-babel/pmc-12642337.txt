Yield and fruit quality responses of ‘Kabkab’ date palm (Phoenix dactylifera L.) to potassium and iron fertilization under calcareous soil conditions
Authors: Abdolali Hesami, Razieh Bavi, Ali Hashemi, Morteza Pouzesh Shirazi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis two-year field study (2021–2022) evaluated the effects of soil-applied potassium sulfate (K₂SO₄; 45% K₂O) and trunk-injected ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO₄·7 H₂O) on yield, fruit quality, and leaf mineral composition of ‘Kabkab’ date palm under calcareous, semi-arid conditions. Forty-eight 20-year-old palms were arranged in a factorial randomized complete block design with K₂O at 0, 400, 800, and 1200 g palm⁻¹ and FeSO₄·7 H₂O at 0, 11, and 23 g L⁻¹ (1.0 L per palm per application). Injections were performed once per season (two injections in total over the study). Potassium and iron each affected fruit set, total yield, bunch traits, fruit weight, and sugar indices, and several traits exhibited K × Fe interactions. A representative treatment (400 g K₂O combined with 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O) achieved up to 64.4 kg palm⁻¹ in total yield and a mean fruit weight of 17.1 g. Higher K inputs tended to increase total soluble solids and sugars, whereas responses of some physical attributes plateaued at the upper rates. Leaf K, Fe, Zn, and Cu generally increased with fertilisation, while Mn decreased at higher rates. Within the limits of this study, moderate nutrient rates can support productivity and selected quality attributes of ‘Kabkab’ date palm on calcareous soils. Outcomes are site- and cultivar-specific and should be verified across additional locations, seasons, and Fe formulations before broad recommendation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07682-5.Keywords: Date palm, Phoenix dactylifera, Potassium, Iron, Calcareous soil, Yield, Fruit quality

Introduction

Date palm (Phoenix dactylifera L.) is among the oldest cultivated fruit trees, domesticated more than 5,000 years ago in Mesopotamia, and it remains vital to food security and rural livelihoods, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions where few fruit crops can thrive [1]. Global production exceeds 9 million tons annually [2], and demand continues to rise owing to the fruit’s high nutritional value, including sugars, dietary fiber, potassium, and antioxidant compounds [3]. Iran is a major producer; within Iran, Bushehr Province—especially the Dashtestan region—is recognised for high-quality ‘Kabkab’ dates. Despite more than six million female palms, yields often fall below potential, largely because of suboptimal fertilisation and cultural practices [4–6].

The notion that date palms require minimal fertilisation has been challenged by evidence that both macro- and micronutrients are critical for yield and fruit quality [7, 8]. Among macronutrients, potassium (K) is essential for water regulation, carbohydrate translocation, photosynthesis, and enzyme activation, thereby enhancing fruit size, sweetness, and yield [8–10]. In date palm, K fertilisation improves sugar accumulation and dry matter content, with positive responses reported across cultivars and soil conditions [5, 11–14].

Micronutrient deficiencies—particularly iron (Fe)—are widespread in the calcareous soils common in southern Iran. Iron is indispensable for chlorophyll biosynthesis and many enzymatic functions; deficiency leads to chlorosis, reduced photosynthesis, and yield loss [15, 16]. Because Fe is largely phloem-immobile, external supplementation is often required [17, 18]. Trunk injection of FeSO₄ effectively alleviates Fe deficiency in date palms [19, 20], and similar improvements have been reported in olive, pear, and citrus [21–23].

Previous research indicates that concurrent management of macro- and micronutrients can yield complementary benefits in perennial fruit trees by enhancing photosynthetic capacity, carbohydrate transport, and nutrient acquisition [8, 24, 25]. In date palm, optimising K supply consistently improves sugar accumulation, dry matter, fruit size, and yield across cultivars and environments [5, 7, 11–14, 26, 27]. Iron deficiency is frequent in calcareous soils and depresses chlorophyll formation and productivity [15, 16, 28]; targeted Fe supplementation—including trunk injection of ferrous salts—has alleviated chlorosis and improved performance in date palm and other fruit trees [19, 21, 23, 29]. Nevertheless, the interactive effects of K and Fe in date palm remain insufficiently characterised under the calcareous, semi-arid conditions typical of southern Iran; most prior studies have evaluated these nutrients separately or at limited dose ranges [5, 11–14, 19, 29].

Given the importance of K and Fe in palm nutrition and the prevalence of calcareous soils, further work is warranted to clarify their combined effects. Accordingly, this study investigated the interactive effects of soil-applied potassium sulfate and trunk-injected ferrous sulfate on yield, fruit quality, and leaf mineral composition of ‘Kabkab’ date palm under arid conditions.

Materials and methods

Site and soil

The field experiment was conducted during 2021–2022 at the research orchard of the College of Agriculture and Natural Resources, Persian Gulf University (Dashtestan, Bushehr Province, Iran), on calcareous soils under semi-arid conditions. The region experiences hot summers and mild winters, with mean annual precipitation of approximately 250 mm and a mean air temperature of 27 °C. The surface soil (0–30 cm) is classified as sandy loam with electrical conductivity (EC) of 4.56 dS m⁻¹ and pH 7.8; the subsoil (30–60 cm) has a similar texture, lower organic carbon, and a slightly lower pH. Experimental design and treatments

A randomised complete block design (RCBD) with a 4 (K₂O) × 3 (Fe) factorial arrangement and four blocks was used. Forty-eight single-tree experimental units were assigned at random (12 treatments × 4 blocks = 48 palms). Potassium was supplied as potassium sulfate (K₂SO₄; 45% K₂O) at 0, 400, 800, and 1200 g K₂O palm⁻¹. Iron was supplied as ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO₄·7 H₂O) at 0, 11, or 23 g L⁻¹ palm⁻¹. Injections were performed once per season (two injections in total over the two-year study).

Fertiliser treatments and application protocol

Potassium (K₂O) was applied as potassium sulfate (K₂SO₄; 45% K₂O) at 0, 400, 800, or 1200 g palm⁻¹ on 15 March each season as a single basal dose. Fertiliser was placed in three holes per palm, each approximately 30 cm deep and ≈ 1.0 m from the trunk, then backfilled and irrigated the same day.

Iron (Fe) was supplied by trunk injection of FeSO₄·7 H₂O at 0, 11, or 23 g L⁻¹ palm⁻¹. Each palm received 1.0 L once per season on 15 March in 2021 and 2022 (two injections in total). Each annual dose was delivered via a single injection hole at ≈ 1.5 m height, drilled at a 45° downward angle (≈ 12 mm diameter; ≈30 cm depth). The solution was administered using a gravity-fed IV set (sterile bag and tubing), with the roller clamp adjusted to a dripwise flow to achieve complete uptake over ≈ 8 h. After delivery, the hole was sealed with a wooden dowel and grafting wax to prevent leakage.

All palms were managed under drip irrigation (two lateral lines per row; emitters 4 L h⁻¹ at 1 m spacing), uniform hand pollination using a single male source, routine pruning, and pest control according to local recommendations.

Assessment of yield and yield components

Harvests were conducted in both seasons during the second half of August at the commercial Rutab stage. All marketable bunches on each palm were harvested on the same day to minimise stage-related bias and to ensure comparable maturity. For each palm, the number of bunches was defined as the total count of marketable bunches present at Rutab on the harvest day. Immediately after cutting, each bunch was weighed at the field station using a calibrated digital scale (accuracy ± 0.01 kg); the average bunch fresh weight per palm was the arithmetic mean of all bunch weights recorded for that palm. To characterise bunch structure, three representative bunches per palm (basal, median, and apical crown positions where possible) were selected from the harvested set and the total number of strands on each was counted; the per-palm strand number was the mean across these bunches. On the same representative bunches, fruits were counted on ten systematically selected strands per bunch, sampled from base to tip; the number of fruits per bunch was then estimated as the product of the mean number of fruits per strand and the total number of strands on that bunch, and the per-palm value was obtained by averaging across the representative bunches. Fruit set was calculated on the counted strands as the percentage of nodes bearing retained fruits, i.e. (retained fruits per strand ÷ total nodes per strand) × 100, and expressed for each palm as the mean of strand-level values. From the same representative bunches at the Rutab stage, a subsample of fifty fruits per palm was collected to determine average single-fruit fresh weight by dividing the subsample’s total fresh weight by the number of fruits. Finally, fruit yield per palm (kg palm⁻¹) was calculated as:

(mean number of fruits per bunch) × (mean single-fruit fresh weight, converted to kilograms) × (number of marketable bunches per palm at the Rutab stag).

Fruit physicochemical analysis

At the Rutab stage, 50 fruits per palm were randomly sampled to assess physical traits.

Fruit length and diameter (mm) were measured with a micrometer caliper, and the length-to-diameter ratio (L/D) was calculated accordingly.

Seed length and diameter (mm) were recorded with the same instrument.

At the Rutab stage, 20 fruits per palm were randomly sampled to assess chemical traits.

Total soluble solids (TSS) were measured with a handheld refractometer (temperature-compensated) and expressed as °Brix.

Total sugars and reducing sugars were determined according to AOAC standard methods [30].

Leaf nutrient analysis

Leaf sampling was performed in August of both years. Five fully expanded, mature leaves were randomly selected from each palm, and five leaflets were collected from the middle portion of each leaf (south-facing canopy where possible). Samples were washed with tap water followed by distilled water, air-dried, and then oven-dried at 70 °C for 72 h. Dried material was ground in an electric mill and stored in paper bags at room temperature until analysis. For each palm, ground leaflets were composited to obtain a single analytical sample.

Wet digestion was carried out using hydrogen peroxide and sulfuric acid following the method of Wolf [31]. Potassium concentration was determined by flame photometry [32], and Fe, Mn, Zn, and Cu concentrations were measured by atomic absorption spectrophotometry [33].

Statistical analysis

All measurements were analysed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) according to a factorial randomised complete block design (RCBD) with potassium (K; four levels) and iron (Fe; three levels) specified as fixed factors and block specified as a random factor. Residual normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Percentage data (fruit set) were arcsine–square-root transformed when required to stabilise variances; untransformed means are reported in the tables for clarity.

Mean separation was performed using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT). For traits in which the K × Fe interaction was significant, pairwise comparisons were carried out among the K × Fe treatment combinations. For traits in which only main effects were significant, means were compared within the relevant main factor. For fruit set, the main effect of potassium (K) was evaluated at P ≤ 0.05, whereas the main effect of iron (Fe) and the K × Fe interaction were evaluated at P ≤ 0.01; these levels were used in assigning DMRT groupings.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.1 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and effects were considered statistically significant at P ≤ 0.05 or P ≤ 0.01, as indicated.

Results

The combined analysis of variance (ANOVA) showed that soil-applied potassium (K₂O) had significant effects on multiple horticultural and physiological traits. Potassium treatments affected total yield, average bunch weight, number of bunches per palm, number of strands per bunch, number of fruits per bunch, fruit weight, fruit length-to-diameter (L/D) ratio, and leaf K, Fe, Zn, and Cu concentrations (P ≤ 0.01). Fruit set was also affected by potassium (P ≤ 0.05). Potassium further had significant effects on fruit quality traits including total soluble solids (TSS), reducing sugars, and total sugars (P ≤ 0.01). Trunk injection of ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO₄·7 H₂O) likewise showed significant main effects (P ≤ 0.01) on fruit set, total yield, average bunch weight, number of bunches per palm, number of strands per bunch, number of fruits per bunch, fruit weight, L/D ratio, and leaf K, Fe, Zn, and Cu concentrations. Fe effects on TSS, reducing sugars, and total sugars were also significant (ANOVA: Tables 2, 3 and 4; means/DMRT: Tables 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10).

Significant K × Fe interactions (P ≤ 0.01) were detected for fruit set, total yield, average bunch weight, number of bunches per palm, number of strands per bunch, number of fruits per bunch, fruit weight, L/D ratio, and for TSS, reducing sugars, and total sugars. No significant K × Fe interaction was detected for leaf K, Fe, Zn, or Cu (ANOVA: Tables 2, 3 and 4; means/DMRT: Tables 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10).

Year was included in the statistical model, and variance homogeneity between seasons was verified prior to pooling. Although absolute values differed slightly between 2021 and 2022, no significant year × K, year × Fe, or year × K × Fe interactions were detected for the reported traits at the selected significance thresholds. Therefore, treatment effects are presented as means pooled across both seasons (2021–2022) in Tables 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10.

Fruit set and yield

Application of potassium at 400 g K₂O palm⁻¹ was associated with a higher fruit set compared with the unfertilised control across both seasons (65.05% vs. 60.61%; Table 5). Trunk injection of ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO₄·7 H₂O) likewise increased fruit set, with the 23 g L⁻¹ level (1.0 L per palm per application; i.e., 23 g per palm per injection) reaching 67.23% (Table 5). The combined application of K and Fe produced the highest fruit set values; for example, 400 g K₂O with either 11–23 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O, and 800 g K₂O with 23 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O, yielded 72.45%,68.72%, and 72.98%, respectively (Table 5).

All K and Fe treatments increased fruit yield per palm relative to the control (Table 5). Among K rates, 400 g K₂O palm⁻¹ increased mean yield from the control level to 50.80 kg palm⁻¹, and Fe increased yield from the control level to 54.09 kg palm⁻¹ at 11 g L⁻¹ (1.0 L per palm). The greatest yields were obtained under combined K × Fe treatments; in particular, 400 g K₂O with 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O achieved 64.41 kg palm⁻¹ (Table 5).

Bunch number and bunch weight

ANOVA indicated significant main effects of potassium and iron on bunch number and bunch weight (P ≤ 0.01; Table 6). Under the K main effect, the control averaged 5.13 bunches palm⁻¹, while 400 g K₂O palm⁻¹ produced the highest mean of 6.00 bunches palm⁻¹. Under the Fe main effect, the 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O level (1.0 L per palm per application) averaged 6.16 bunches palm⁻¹ compared with 5.59 bunches palm⁻¹ at 0 g L⁻¹.

For bunch weight, the K control averaged 6.90 kg, and 400 g K₂O palm⁻¹ increased the mean to 8.44 kg. Under Fe, the 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O level increased bunch weight to 8.72 kg relative to its corresponding control. Detailed effect sizes and post-hoc groupings are provided in Table 6.

A significant K × Fe interaction was detected for both traits (P ≤ 0.05; Table 6). The highest means were obtained under combined applications, notably 400 g K₂O with 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O, yielding 6.50 bunches palm⁻¹ and 10.04 kg per bunch. Differences among the top-ranked combinations were not significant (Table 6).

Number of fruits and strands per bunch

According to treatment means with DMRT (Table 7), both potassium (K₂O) and iron (FeSO₄·7 H₂O) influenced the number of fruits per bunch, and combined applications ranked among the highest groups. Under K, application of 400 g K₂O palm⁻¹ increased the mean fruit count to ≈ 617 compared with ≈ 532 in the control. Under Fe, 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O (trunk injection) was associated with ≈ 694 fruits per bunch. The highest means were observed under combined applications—most notably 400 g K₂O + 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O and 800 g K₂O + 23 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O—with ≈ 784 and ≈ 788 fruits per bunch, respectively; differences among the top-ranked combinations were not significant according to DMRT (Table 7).

For the number of strands per bunch, the means table (DMRT; Table 7) likewise shows higher values under K and Fe, with combined applications ranking among the leading groups. Potassium increased strand number across rates (≈ 52.5 at 400 g K₂O palm⁻¹ vs. ≈ 44.5 in the control), and 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O was associated with ≈ 52.5 strands per bunch vs. ≈ 44.6 in untreated palms. The highest means occurred under combined application—particularly 400 g K₂O + 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O—with ≈ 54 strands per bunch; differences among the leading combinations were not significant by DMRT (Table 7).

Fruit Physical Characteristics

Several fruit traits—such as diameter, fruit-to-seed weight ratio, and seed length-to-diameter ratio—were not significantly affected by potassium or iron and are therefore not discussed in detail.

The fruit length-to-diameter (L/D) ratio was affected by potassium, iron, and their interaction (P ≤ 0.01; Table 8). Among K rates, 400, 800, and 1200 g K₂O palm⁻¹ were associated with higher L/D ratios (1.71, 1.70, and 1.72, respectively) compared with the control (1.67). Under Fe, trunk injection of FeSO₄·7 H₂O at 11 g L⁻¹ (1.0 L per palm per application) averaged 1.76 versus 1.68 at 0 g L⁻¹. Combined applications yielded the highest values; for example, 400 g K₂O with 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O reached an L/D ratio of 1.88 (Table 8).

Average fruit weight responded to both K and Fe main effects (P ≤ 0.01; Table 8). At 400 g K₂O palm⁻¹, mean fruit weight was 15.67 g compared with 13.73 g in the K control; 400 and 800 g K₂O did not differ, and both exceeded the control and the 1200 g rate. Under Fe, mean fruit weight at 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O averaged 15.19 g compared with 14.24 g in the 0 g L⁻¹ control. Across combinations, 400 g K₂O with 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O produced a mean fruit weight of 17.08 g (Table 8).

Fruit chemical properties

According to Table 9, potassium fertilisation significantly affected total soluble solids (TSS) across both seasons (P ≤ 0.01). Under the K main effect, 800 g K₂O palm⁻¹ was associated with a TSS mean of 60.45%, while the K control averaged 58.63%. Iron also showed a significant main effect on TSS (P ≤ 0.01); trunk injection of FeSO₄·7 H₂O at 11 g L⁻¹ (1.0 L per palm per application) averaged 59.96% compared with the 0 g L⁻¹ control. Combined K × Fe applications yielded the highest TSS values; for example, 800 g K₂O with 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O reached 63.34% (Table 9).

Reducing sugars responded to both potassium and iron (P ≤ 0.01; Table 9). The K control averaged 47.81%, whereas higher means were observed at elevated K rates (e.g., 55.34% at 1200 g K₂O palm⁻¹). Under Fe, 23 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O increased reducing sugars relative to the 0 g L⁻¹ control. Significant K × Fe interactions were detected; combinations such as 800 g K₂O + 23 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O and 1200 g K₂O with 0, 11, or 23 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O showed higher means than their respective controls (Table 9).

Total sugars were significantly influenced by K and Fe main effects and their interaction (P ≤ 0.01; Table 9). Under K, total sugars increased across the tested rates, with higher means at 800 and 1200 g K₂O palm⁻¹ compared with the K control. Under Fe, 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O averaged 55.29% compared with 51.38% at 0 g L⁻¹. Among combinations, 1200 g K₂O with 23 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O produced one of the highest means (57.30%), and 800 g K₂O with 23 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O as well as 400 g K₂O with 23 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O also ranked among the higher groups (Table 9).

Leaf elemental content

According to Table 10, potassium and iron each had significant main effects on foliar mineral concentrations (P ≤ 0.01). Increasing soil-applied K₂O raised leaf K, Fe, Zn, and Cu, with the highest means observed at 1200 g K₂O palm⁻¹ (leaf K: 1.81% vs. 1.10% in the K control; leaf Fe: 151.40 mg kg⁻¹ vs. 140.09 mg kg⁻¹; leaf Zn: 37.10 mg kg⁻¹ vs. 28.00 mg kg⁻¹; leaf Cu: 3.70 mg kg⁻¹ vs. 2.80 mg kg⁻¹).

Under the Fe main effect, trunk injection of ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO₄·7 H₂O) increased foliar K, Fe, Zn, and Cu relative to the 0 g L⁻¹ level, with the largest means at 23 g L⁻¹ (1.0 L per palm per application): leaf K 1.65% vs. 1.1%, leaf Fe 164.20 mg kg⁻¹ vs. 130.00 mg kg⁻¹, leaf Zn 42.10 mg kg⁻¹ vs. 22.00 mg kg⁻¹, and leaf Cu 3.58 mg kg⁻¹ vs. 3.13 mg kg⁻¹.

No significant K × Fe interaction was detected for leaf K, Fe, Zn, or Cu (Table 10), indicating that potassium and iron acted independently in determining foliar composition.

Discussion

Fruit set and yield

The higher fruit set and yield under individual and combined applications of potassium (K) and iron (Fe) are consistent with the physiological roles of these nutrients in reproductive development of date palm. Fruit set is a nutrient-sensitive process governed by resource availability, pollen viability, hormonal signaling, and sink strength during flowering and early fruit growth [8, 34]. Improved performance with K is plausibly related to its functions in carbohydrate synthesis and translocation during energy-demanding stages such as floral initiation and fertilisation, together with its roles in osmotic regulation, stomatal control, and enzyme activation [24, 25]. Fe supports chlorophyll biosynthesis and photosynthetic capacity and thereby can benefit pollen development and ovule fertilization [28, 35].

Combined K and Fe applications were associated with higher fruit set than single-nutrient treatments, suggesting complementary effects: K facilitates assimilate transport to reproductive tissues, whereas Fe sustains photosynthetic energy supply. This interpretation is consistent with reports in other perennial fruit crops in which integrated macro- and micronutrient supply improves reproductive outcomes [29, 36]. Nutrient status can also influence hormonal signalling (e.g., cytokinins, gibberellins), which may contribute to fruit set and retention.

Gains in fruit set generally translate to yield increases when source–sink balance is maintained. In calcareous, high-pH soils, Fe availability to roots is limited; trunk injection can circumvent this constraint by delivering Fe to the canopy, supporting chlorophyll formation and light harvesting [4, 16, 20]. Within this context, the present results indicate that combining soil K fertilisation with trunk-injected Fe is a practical approach to mitigate nutritional limitations and improve productivity under semi-arid conditions. Increases in bunch weight and fruit number observed here reinforce the value of balanced nutrient management where micronutrient availability is restricted. Nevertheless, effects are likely cultivar- and site-specific and should be verified across locations and seasons.

Bunch number, bunch weight, and number of strands per bunch

Applications of potassium (K) and iron (Fe)—especially in combination—were associated with higher bunch number, bunch weight, strand count, and fruit number in ‘Kabkab’. These quantitative responses are consistent with the physiological roles of K and Fe in supporting reproductive development. Potassium contributes to carbohydrate metabolism, osmotic regulation, protein synthesis, and phloem loading, processes that facilitate fruit retention and expansion; efficient translocation of assimilates from source leaves to sink organs is strongly influenced by K status [24–27].

Previous work in date palm and other fruit crops has reported similar improvements in bunch- and fruit-related traits with K fertilisation [11–14, 26, 27] and with Fe supply in systems where calcareous soils limit Fe availability [19–21, 23]. In such soils, trunk injection or other targeted Fe delivery can circumvent low soil Fe availability and support chlorophyll formation and metabolic activity relevant to reproductive performance [19, 21, 23].

When K and Fe were applied together, responses tended to exceed those under single-nutrient treatments, which is compatible with complementary functions: K facilitates assimilate loading and transport, whereas Fe supports photosynthetic and respiratory energy supply. This combination can strengthen source–sink dynamics and thereby contribute to larger bunch size and greater fruit number [8, 24, 28, 29, 36]. From an applied perspective, a dual-pathway strategy—supplying macronutrients via soil and Fe via trunk injection under alkaline conditions—appears agronomically effective [15, 16, 19, 20, 37]; however, economic feasibility and site- and cultivar-specific responses should be evaluated in multi-site trials.

Fruit physical characteristics

Increases in fruit weight and L/D ratio with potassium (K) and iron (Fe) applications are consistent with their physiological roles in fruit growth. Potassium can enhance fruit fresh weight by facilitating phloem loading and sugar translocation, promoting dry‑matter accumulation in developing fruits; it also contributes to osmotic adjustment, cell expansion, and enzyme regulation under high sink demand [8, 24–27]. Fe supports chlorophyll biosynthesis and photosynthetic energy supply, which can increase assimilate availability to fruits [28, 35]. When supplied together, K and Fe appeared to confer complementary effects—K supporting assimilate transport and osmotic balance, and Fe sustaining photosynthetic and respiratory metabolism—consistent with improved physical attributes observed here.

These interpretations align with previous reports in date palm and other perennial fruit crops, where K fertilisation has improved fruit size and sugar accumulation [11–14, 26, 27], and targeted Fe supply has supported canopy function and fruit development under calcareous conditions [19, 21, 23, 28]. Mechanistically, K‑mediated turgor maintenance and Fe‑dependent enzyme activities may contribute to both cell division and expansion, with downstream effects on fruit uniformity and marketable quality [8, 24, 35].

Conversely, Fe deficiency impairs chlorophyll formation and central metabolism, often reducing fruit size and quality in high‑pH soils; trunk injection of FeSO₄·7 H₂O can mitigate these constraints by improving canopy Fe status [19, 21, 23, 28]. Overall, the present findings support balanced K and Fe nutrition as a practical approach to enhance fruit physical attributes under calcareous, semi‑arid conditions, while recognising that responses may vary with site and cultivar.

Fruit chemical properties

Consistent with Table 9, reducing sugars, total sugars, and total soluble solids (TSS) were influenced by potassium (K) and iron (Fe) supply. Reducing sugars increased with higher K rates, and combinations with trunk-injected FeSO₄·7 H₂O produced the highest means (e.g., 1200 g K₂O with 23 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O). These responses are compatible with K-mediated activation of carbohydrate metabolic pathways (including sucrose–phosphate synthase activity) and with its roles in osmotic regulation and phloem loading that facilitate assimilate movement to the fruit [8, 24, 25]. Fe can support these processes indirectly via chlorophyll biosynthesis and photosynthetic energy supply during ripening [28, 35]. Reports from date palm and other fruit crops similarly show that balanced macronutrient–micronutrient nutrition is associated with higher reducing sugar concentrations [11–14].

Total sugars were also higher under K and Fe treatments, with the largest means observed when elevated K was combined with Fe injection (e.g., 1200 g K₂O with 23 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O). Mechanistically, increased total sugars are consistent with improved phloem loading and assimilate partitioning promoted by K, while Fe supports electron transport and central metabolic functions that sustain carbohydrate conversion during maturation [8, 24, 28, 35]. Where K × Fe interactions were significant, the combined supply appears to have provided complementary benefits to both source activity (photosynthesis) and sink strength (fruit growth).

For TSS, moderate K in combination with Fe injection (e.g., 800 g K₂O with 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O) yielded among the highest values in this study. K likely contributed through increased carbohydrate accumulation and dry-matter content, while Fe may have supported canopy photosynthetic capacity and sucrose formation that underpin TSS [24, 28]. Collectively, these findings support balanced K and Fe management as a practical approach to enhance fruit sweetness-related indices under calcareous, semi-arid conditions, noting that responses can vary with site and cultivar.

Leaf elemental content

Consistent with the Results, potassium (K) and iron (Fe) each had significant main effects on foliar K, Fe, Zn, and Cu concentrations, whereas no K × Fe interaction was detected for these nutrients. Increases under K supply are compatible with K-mediated improvements in enzyme activation, osmotic regulation, and phloem transport that can support root function and whole-plant nutrient distribution [8, 24]. Fe applied by trunk injection can bypass limited Fe availability in calcareous, high-pH soils and raise canopy Fe status, with concurrent increases in other micronutrients observed at the leaf level [15, 16, 19, 21, 23].

The decline in foliar Mn at higher fertiliser rates is consistent with cation antagonism and competitive uptake processes reported in perennial fruit crops [8, 38]. This finding highlights the need to balance macronutrient and micronutrient supply and to monitor tissue composition to avoid inducing secondary deficiencies.

Localised solution chemistry associated with FeSO₄·7 H₂O injection may also influence nutrient mobility within vascular tissues—e.g., by improving Fe availability under alkaline conditions—although such effects were not quantified here [15, 16]. Overall, integrating soil K fertilisation with trunk-injected Fe appears to be a practical strategy to enhance nutrient status under calcareous conditions, while recognising that responses are site- and cultivar-specific and warrant confirmation across locations and seasons.

Conclusions

Across two seasons at a calcareous, semi-arid site, potassium (K) and iron (Fe) fertilisation were associated with improvements in yield- and quality-related traits of Phoenix dactylifera L. cv. ‘Kabkab’. Soil-applied potassium sulfate and trunk-injected ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO₄·7 H₂O) at moderate rates were linked to higher fruit set, total yield, bunch weight, fruit weight, and numbers of bunches and fruits per palm.

Quality indices, including total soluble solids (TSS), reducing sugars, total sugars, and fruit length-to-diameter (L/D) ratio, also increased under K and Fe supply. Where K × Fe interactions were detected, combined applications tended to rank among the higher groups; in particular, the combination of 400 g K₂O per palm with 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O (1.0 L per palm per application) produced consistent responses in this experiment. These patterns are most parsimoniously explained by complementary functions of K and Fe rather than by strong physiological synergy, and their magnitude may vary by site and season.

Mechanistically, K can support osmotic regulation, carbohydrate translocation, and cell expansion, while Fe can enhance chlorophyll formation, photosynthetic capacity, and enzyme activity. Increases in foliar K, Fe, Zn, and Cu under fertilised treatments are consistent with improved nutrient status, whereas the decline in Mn indicates potential cation antagonism and the need for balanced nutrition.

For agro-ecological zones similar to the present study, an integrated approach—annual soil application of ~ 400 g K₂O per palm combined with trunk injection of 11 g L⁻¹ FeSO₄·7 H₂O prior to flowering—appears practical for improving yield performance, fruit quality, and foliar nutrient status. This rate combination should be regarded as a context-specific guideline rather than a universal prescription and merits validation, including agronomic and economic assessments, across additional cultivars, sites, and seasons.

Limitation

This study was conducted at a single experimental site on calcareous soil using one date palm cultivar (‘Kabkab’) over two consecutive seasons (2021–2022). Consequently, the findings may not be directly generalisable to other cultivars, soil types, irrigation systems, or climatic conditions. The potassium and iron rates were evaluated at discrete levels, and response curves or economic optima were not established. Similarly, the effects of split versus single annual applications and their interactions with irrigation frequency were not assessed.

The experimental duration (two years) was adequate for short-term responses but did not capture longer-term soil–plant feedbacks. Possible cumulative effects on soil nutrient dynamics, salinity, organic matter, and microbial activity were not monitored. Foliar analysis was limited to K, Fe, Zn, Cu, and Mn, and other nutrients (e.g. N, P, Ca, Mg, B) were not measured; therefore, broader nutrient interactions remain uncertain. Pest and disease responses to the treatments were also not evaluated.

Economic aspects—including fertiliser cost, labour requirements for trunk injection, and marginal yield returns—were not quantified and should be addressed before on-farm recommendation. Finally, this study used FeSO₄·7 H₂O as the iron source; results may differ with chelated or nano-formulations that have different solubility or uptake characteristics.

Future research should include multi-site, multi-year trials encompassing additional cultivars, continuous rate–response designs, and alternative Fe formulations. Integration of physiological, economic, and environmental analyses would provide a more comprehensive basis for nutrient management recommendations under arid and semi-arid conditions.
