Stabilizing PDE–ML Coupled Systems
Saad Qadeer, Panos Stinis, Hui Wan

Abstract
A long-standing obstacle in the use of machine-learnt surrogates with larger PDE systems is the onset of instabilities when solved numerically. Efforts towards ameliorating these have mostly concentrated on improving the accuracy of the surrogates or imbuing them with additional structure, and have garnered limited success. In this article, we study a prototype problem and draw insights that can help with more complex systems. In particular, we focus on a viscous Burgers’-ML system and, after identifying the cause of the instabilities, prescribe strategies to stabilize the coupled system. To improve the accuracy of the stabilized system, we next explore methods based on the Mori–Zwanzig formalism.

Introduction

Partial differential equations (PDEs) are an essential modeling tool in engineering and physical sciences. The numerical methods used for solving the more descriptive and sophisticated of these models comprise many computationally expensive modules. Machine learning (ML) provides a way of replacing some of these modules by surrogates that are much more efficient at the time of inference. The resulting PDE–ML coupled systems, however, can be highly susceptible to instabilities

2
Stabilizing the coupled system

We study the viscous Burgers’ equation with periodic boundary conditions

\displaystyle u_{t}=\partial_{z}\left(-\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\right)+\nu u_{zz},\quad u(0,z)=u_{0}(z),\quad z\in[0,2\pi),

(1)

and consider the effect of replacing the diffusion term by a machine-learnt surrogate. We conduct online training by using the exact solutions for initial conditions of the form

\displaystyle u_{0}(z)=\sum_{l=0}^{L}a_{l}\cos(lz)+\sum_{l=1}^{L}b_{j}\sin(lz),\qquad a_{l},b_{l}\sim\frac{\text{unif}(-1,1)}{1+l^{2}},

(2)

to train the surrogate term. We first find that successful training necessarily requires the diffusion term to be written in divergence form, i.e.,

\displaystyle w_{t}=\partial_{z}\left(-\frac{1}{2}w^{2}+\nu\mathcal{N}[w]\right),

(3)

where
\mathcal{N}
is a trainable architecture. Even then, the inability of the trained network to learn the high frequencies (the
spectral bias
phenomenon) means that naively solving the the resulting system (
3
) leads to instabilities for some initial conditions, or vastly incorrect solutions (relative errors of
O(1)
) for the rest (even within the temporal training interval).

These observations can be explained by noting that the spectral bias limits the ability of (
3
) to correctly treat the high frequencies, in effect leading to a lopsided under-resolved model, since the convective term keeps transferring energy to the high frequency modes. To ameliorate this, we include a low-pass filter in this term, to wit,

\displaystyle\phi_{t}=\partial_{z}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{P}_{M}\left[\phi^{2}\right]+\nu\mathcal{P}_{M}\left[\mathcal{N}[\phi]\right]\right),

(4)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{M}\left[g\right](z)=\sum_{|j|\leq M}\widehat{g}_{j}e^{ijz}\text{ for }g(z)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{g}_{j}e^{ijz}.

(5)

(b)

3
Introducing memory terms

Given a system of ODEs

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}_{k}(t)=R_{k}({\bm{\phi}}),\quad\phi_{k}(0)=x_{k},\qquad k=1,\ldots,N,

(6)

we wish to construct a reduced-order model (ROM) for the “resolved” variables
\widehat{\bm{\phi}}=\left(\phi_{l}\right)_{1\leq l\leq N}
for some
N_{\text{rom}}<N
that reproduces the dynamics of the full system. Let
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}R_{j}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}
be the Liouville operator corresponding to (
6
). Define the projection operator
P
by
[Pg](\widehat{x})=g(\widehat{x},0)
for any function
g(\widehat{x},\widetilde{x})
of both
\widehat{x}
, the resolved, and
\widetilde{x}
, the unresolved initial conditions. Further set
Q=I-P
and

\displaystyle\widecheck{g}(\widehat{x})=[Pg](\widehat{x}),\qquad\overline{g}(x)=[Qg](x)=g(x)-[Pg](\widehat{x}).

The Mori–Zwanzig (MZ) formalism then allows us to write, for
1\leq k\leq N_{\text{rom}}
,
[
8
]

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}_{k}(t;\widehat{x},0)=\widecheck{R}_{k}\left(\widehat{\phi}(t;\widehat{x},0)\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\left[\bm{R}\cdot\nabla\overline{R}_{k}\right]\left(\bm{\eta}\left(s;\widehat{\phi}\left(t-s;\widehat{x},0\right),0\right)\right)\ ds,

(7)

where
\bm{\eta}=\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{1}&\ldots&\eta_{N}\end{pmatrix}
is the solution to the orthogonal dynamics equations
\eta_{l}^{\prime}(t)=\overline{R}_{l}(\bm{\eta})
(the terms after the semicolons indicate the initial conditions). The integral in (
7
) is termed the memory; because of its reliance on the solution to the orthogonal dynamics, however, the system is not closed. Following
[
9
]
, we develop an approach that allows us to rewrite (
7
) in terms of memory kernels by relying on an ensemble of solutions generated according to a well-defined rule.

Define the weight function

\displaystyle w(x)=\Pi_{i=1}^{N_{\text{rom}}}\frac{e^{-\frac{(x_{i}-\mu_{i})^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}}}{\sigma_{i}\sqrt{2\pi}}\Pi_{j=N_{\text{rom}}+1}^{N}\delta_{0}(x_{i}),

(8)

and denote

\displaystyle\left\langle g_{1},g_{2}\right\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}g_{1}(x)g_{2}(x)w(x)\ dx.

(9)

The projection
P
can be characterized as the conditional expectation

\displaystyle\left[Pg\right](\widehat{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{M-N}}g(\widehat{x},\widetilde{x})w(x)\ d\widetilde{x}=g(\widehat{x},0),

as it simply sets all the unresolved variables to zero. It can also be viewed as an orthogonal projection with respect to
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
on the subspace of all functions of
\widehat{x}
since, for any function
h
in this subspace,

\displaystyle\left\langle g-Pg,h\right\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left[g(x)-g(\widehat{x},0)\right]h(\widehat{x})w(x)\ dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{rom}}}}\left[g(\widehat{x},0)-g(\widehat{x},0)\right]h(\widehat{x})w(\widehat{x},0)\ d\widehat{x}=0.

Let
\{h_{j}(\widehat{x})\}_{j\geq 1}
be an orthonormal basis for the range of
P
. We can then write

\displaystyle[Pg](\widehat{x})=\sum_{j\geq 1}\left\langle h_{j},g\right\rangle h_{j}(\widehat{x}).

(10)

Using
R_{k}=\mathcal{L}x_{k}
yields
\overline{R}_{k}=QR_{k}=Q\mathcal{L}x_{k}
. Applying Dyson’s identity to
Q\mathcal{L}x_{k}
leads to

\displaystyle e^{tQ\mathcal{L}}Q\mathcal{L}x_{k}=e^{t\mathcal{L}}Q\mathcal{L}x_{k}-\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}}P\mathcal{L}e^{sQ\mathcal{L}}Q\mathcal{L}x_{k}\ ds.

(11)

Noting that
\eta_{k}(t)=e^{tQ\mathcal{L}}Q\mathcal{L}x_{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\eta_{k}(t;x)=e^{t\mathcal{L}}\eta_{k}(0;x)-\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}}P\left(\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}(s;x)\right)\ ds.

Applying
P\mathcal{L}
throughout gives

\displaystyle P\left(\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}(t;x)\right)=P\left(\mathcal{L}e^{t\mathcal{L}}\eta_{k}(0;x)\right)-P\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}}P\left(\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}(s;x)\right)\ ds.

(12)

Expand

\displaystyle\left[P\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}\right](t;\widehat{x})=\sum_{j\geq 1}\left\langle h_{j},\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}(t)\right\rangle h_{j}(\widehat{x})=\sum_{j\geq 1}K_{jk}(t)h_{j}(\widehat{x})

(13)

where
K_{jk}(t):=\left\langle h_{j},\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}(t)\right\rangle
are termed memory kernels. Plugging this expansion in (
12
) and taking inner products with
h_{l}
throughout then yields

\displaystyle\left\langle h_{l},\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}(t)\right\rangle=\left\langle h_{l},\mathcal{L}e^{t\mathcal{L}}\eta_{k}(0)\right\rangle-\sum_{j\geq 1}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle h_{l},\mathcal{L}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}}h_{j}\right\rangle K_{jk}(s)\ ds,

that is,

\displaystyle K_{lk}(t)=f_{lk}(t)-\sum_{j\geq 1}\int_{0}^{t}g_{lj}(t-s)K_{jk}(s)\ ds,

(14)

where

\displaystyle f_{lk}(t):=\left\langle h_{l},e^{t\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}(0)\right\rangle,\quad g_{lj}(t):=\left\langle h_{l},e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{L}h_{j}\right\rangle.

(15)

Observe that we have used the commutativity of
e^{t\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{L}
in (
15
) to simplify the resulting calculations; we also note that in both expressions, the operator
\mathcal{L}
acts on functions of
x
(and not just
\widehat{x}
), i.e., we have
\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}(0;x)
and
\mathcal{L}h_{j}(x)
. The corresponding evolution operators then act on all the variables so we need to track the trajectories of all the variables in the full-order system; the implicit projection
P
then has the effect that we only need to consider initial conditions with the unresolved variables set to zero. Discretizing the convolution integral in (
14
) by, e.g., the trapezoidal rule then yields a linear system that we can solve at each timestep to compute
K_{lk}(t_{n})
(observe that
\left(g_{lj}(t)\right)
is a square matrix for each
t
).

The evaluation of the inner products can be carried out either by Monte–Carlo integration, i.e., by sampling many points according to the weight function
w(x)
and averaging, or by employing an accurate quadrature rule. The particular form of
w(x)
makes the latter approach more appealing. Specifically, we note that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)\frac{e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\ dx=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g\left(\sigma z+\mu\right)e^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2}}\ dz\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N_{q}}g(\sigma z_{i}+\mu)\omega_{i},

(16)

where
\{(z_{i},\omega_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq N_{q}}
is the Gauss–Hermite quadrature rule, exact up to polynomials of degree
(2N_{q}-1)
. Taking tensor products of this one-dimensional rule for all the resolved variables yields the high-dimensional analog. If the same sized grid is used for each resolved variable, we end up requiring
(N_{q})^{N_{\text{rom}}}
full-order solutions. With that said, the solution trajectories can be calculated in parallel and used to compute the
\left(f_{lk}(t)\right)
and
\left(g_{lj}(t)\right)
.

In a similar spirit, the orthonormal basis
\{h_{j}(\widehat{x})\}_{j\geq 1}
can be taken to be tensor products of one-dimensional Hermite polynomials (corresponding to weight function
e^{-z^{2}/2}
). In practice, we can only employ a finite basis; if we allow polynomials up to highest degree
d
, we need a total of
J={N_{\text{rom}}+d\choose d}
polynomials. The resulting reduced-order model is

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}_{k}(t;\widehat{x},0)=\widecheck{R}_{k}\left(\widehat{\phi}(t;\widehat{x},0)\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\int_{0}^{t}h_{j}\left(\widehat{\phi}(s;\widehat{x},0)\right)K_{jk}(t-s)\ ds.

(17)

For an illustration of this approach, consider the two-dimensional nonlinear system

\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\phi_{1}^{2}+8\phi_{1}\phi_{2}

\displaystyle\phi_{2}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}),

(18)

with initial conditions
\left(\phi_{1}(0),\phi_{2}(0)\right)=(x_{1},x_{2})
, and
\phi_{1}
as the only resolved variable. Since

\displaystyle\eta_{1}(0;x_{1},x_{2})=8x_{1}x_{2}

(19)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\eta_{1}(0;x_{1},x_{2})=8x_{1}\left[-x_{1}x_{2}+8x_{2}^{2}+\cos(x_{1}+x_{2})\right]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{L}\eta(0;x_{1},x_{2})=8\phi_{1}\left[-\phi_{1}\phi_{2}+8\phi_{2}^{2}+\cos(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2})\right]

and

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L}h_{j}](x_{1},x_{2})=h_{j}^{\prime}(x_{1})(-x_{1}^{2}+8x_{1}x_{2})\Rightarrow e^{t\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{L}h_{j}(x_{1},x_{2})=h_{j}^{\prime}(\phi_{1})(-\phi_{1}^{2}+8\phi_{1}\phi_{2}),

(20)

we can calculate
f_{l1}
and
g_{lj}
for
0\leq l,j\leq d
by solving the full system numerically for initial conditions of the form
\phi_{1}(0)=\mu+\sigma z_{i}
and
\phi_{2}(0)=0
. Figures
2
,
3
and
4
show the results for the initial conditions considered in the previous section and for various values of
d
. In each case, we choose
\mu
and
\sigma
so the quadrature points are located in
[1,4]
, and employ a sufficiently high-order grid to ensure (empirical) convergence; we end up using
N_{q}=20
, 100, and 200 for
d=1
, 2, and 3 respectively. In Figure (
5
), we also show the memory kernels
K_{l1}(t)
computed for the various values of
d
. We note that these decay exponentially in time before plateauing off.

We find that the memory kernels outperform the rudimentary Markovian system and that the performance improves when we retain more terms in the memory expansion. In particular, note the ability of the larger systems to correctly reproduce the limiting values as well as the dynamics before the steady state is achieved. This suggests that the inclusion of memory terms can help achieve significant gains in accuracy.

4
Returning to viscous Burgers’

The viscous Burgers’ equation (
1
) can be solved numerically by expanding the solution in terms of Fourier modes

\displaystyle u(t,z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\theta_{k}(t)e^{ikz},\quad u(0,z)=u_{0}(z),

and solving the resulting system of ODEs

\displaystyle\theta_{k}^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{ik}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p,q\in\mathbb{Z}\\
p+q=k\end{subarray}}\theta_{p}(t)\theta_{q}(t)-\nu k^{2}\theta_{k}(t)=:R_{k}(\vec{\theta}),

(21)

with
\theta_{k}(0)=\widehat{\left(u_{0}\right)}_{k}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-ikz}u_{0}(z)\ dz
, and where
\theta_{-k}(t)=\overline{\theta_{k}}(t)
to ensure reality of
u
. In order to apply our machinery to this, we rewrite this system of ODEs in terms of the real and imaginary parts of
\theta_{k}(t)
, to wit,
\theta_{k}(t)=\phi_{k}(t)+i\psi_{k}(t)
, to obtain

\displaystyle\phi_{k}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p,q\in\mathbb{Z}\\
p+q=k\end{subarray}}\phi_{p}(t)\psi_{q}(t)-\nu k^{2}\phi_{k}(t)=:T_{k}(\vec{\phi},\vec{\psi})

\displaystyle\psi_{k}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{k}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p,q\in\mathbb{Z}\\
p+q=k\end{subarray}}\left(\phi_{p}(t)\phi_{q}(t)-\psi_{p}(t)\psi_{q}(t)\right)-\nu k^{2}\psi_{k}(t)=:S_{k}(\vec{\phi},\vec{\psi}),

(22)

with
\phi_{-k}(t)=\phi_{k}(t)
and
\psi_{-k}(t)=-\psi_{k}(t)
for all
k
. This allows us to only focus only on the non-negatively indexed modes, and also yields
\psi_{0}(t)\equiv 0
.

For a given
M\geq 0
, we take the resolved variables to be
\{\phi_{k}\}_{0\leq k\leq M}
and
\{\psi_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq M}
, yielding a total of
N_{\text{rom}}=2M+1
resolved modes. Keeping this in mind the fact that linear projections in Figure
2
yield fairly good results, for now we restrict ourselves to only using up to linear polynomials in the memory expansion (so
J=2(M+1)
). Denoting the initial values of
\widehat{\phi}
and
\widehat{\psi}
by
\widehat{x}
and
\widehat{y}
respectively, we define

\displaystyle h_{j}\left(\widehat{x},\widehat{y}\right)=\left\{\begin{matrix}1&j=1\\
\frac{x_{j-2}}{\sigma_{j-2}}&2\leq j\leq M+2\\
\frac{y_{j-M-2}}{\sigma_{j-M-2}}&M+3\leq j\leq 2M+2\\
\end{matrix}\right.

(23)

The resulting reduced-order models are

\displaystyle\phi_{k}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widecheck{T}_{k}(\widehat{\phi}(t),\widehat{\psi}(t))+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\int_{0}^{t}K^{(1)}_{kj}(t-s)h_{j}\left(\widehat{\phi}(s),\widehat{\psi}(s)\right)\ ds,\quad 0\leq k\leq M

\displaystyle\psi_{k}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widecheck{S}_{k}(\widehat{\phi}(t),\widehat{\psi}(t))+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\int_{0}^{t}K^{(2)}_{kj}(t-s)h_{j}\left(\widehat{\phi}(s),\widehat{\psi}(s)\right)\ ds,\quad 1\leq k\leq M,

(24)

where

\displaystyle\widecheck{T}_{k}(\widehat{x},\widehat{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|p|,|q|\leq M\\
p+q=k\end{subarray}}x_{p}y_{q}-\nu k^{2}x_{k}

\displaystyle\widecheck{S}_{k}(\widehat{x},\widehat{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{k}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|p|,|q|\leq M\\
p+q=k\end{subarray}}\left(x_{p}x_{q}-y_{p}y_{q}\right)-\nu k^{2}y_{k},

(25)

are the Markovian terms. To keep the expressions in (
25
) compact, we set
x_{-p}=x_{p}
and
y_{-p}=-y_{p}
.

Denoting the one-dimensional Hermite quadrature rule by
\{(r_{i},\omega_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq N_{q}}
, we solve the full system (
22
) for initial conditions chosen over all combinations of
\phi_{k}(0)/\sigma_{k},\psi_{l}(0)/\sigma_{l}\in\{r_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq N_{q}}
for
0\leq k\leq M
,
1\leq l\leq M
, and zero otherwise. The
g_{lj}(t)
terms in (
15
) can then be calculated by using

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}h_{j}\left(\widehat{x},\widehat{y}\right)=\left\{\begin{matrix}0&j=1\\
\frac{1}{\sigma_{j-2}}T_{j-2}\left(\widehat{x},\widehat{y}\right)&2\leq j\leq M+2\\
\frac{1}{\sigma_{j-M-2}}S_{j-M-2}\left(\widehat{x},\widehat{y}\right)&M+3\leq j\leq 2M+2\\
\end{matrix}\right.

(26)

For the
f_{lk}(t)
terms, we note that the initial values of the orthogonal dynamics corresponding to the resolved “
\phi_{k}
” are

\displaystyle\eta_{k}^{(1)}(0;\vec{x},\vec{y})=T_{k}(\vec{x},\vec{y})-\widecheck{T}_{k}(\widehat{x},\widehat{y})=k\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|p|\text{ or }|q|>M\\
p+q=k\end{subarray}}x_{p}y_{q}

so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}^{(1)}(0;\vec{x},\vec{y})=k\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|p|\text{ or }|q|>M\\
p+q=k\end{subarray}}y_{q}T_{p}(\vec{x},\vec{y})+x_{q}S_{p}(\vec{x},\vec{y}).

Setting
\vec{v}=\vec{x}+i\vec{y}
and noting that
\overrightarrow{R}(\vec{v})=\overrightarrow{T}(\vec{x},\vec{y})+i\overrightarrow{S}(\vec{x},\vec{y})
, we can write

\displaystyle y_{q}T_{p}(\vec{x},\vec{y})+x_{q}S_{p}(\vec{x},\vec{y})=\text{Im}\left(v_{q}R_{p}(\vec{v})\right)

with the result that

\displaystyle k\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|p|\text{ or }|q|>M\\
p+q=k\end{subarray}}y_{q}T_{p}(\vec{x},\vec{y})+x_{q}S_{p}(\vec{x},\vec{y})=-\text{Re}\left\{ik\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|p|\text{ or }|q|>M\\
p+q=k\end{subarray}}v_{q}R_{p}(\vec{v})\right\},

or alternatively

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}^{(1)}(0;\vec{x},\vec{y})=-\text{Re}\left\{ik\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p,q\in\mathbb{Z}\\
p+q=k\end{subarray}}v_{q}R_{p}(\vec{v})-ik\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|p|,|q|\leq M\\
p+q=k\end{subarray}}v_{q}R_{p}(\vec{v})\right\}.

The convolutional sums in this expression are more easily computed in the physical space; upon applying the evolution operator
e^{t\mathcal{L}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}^{(1)}(0;\vec{x},\vec{y})=-\text{Re}\left\{\partial_{z}\left[u(-uu_{z}-\nu u_{zz})\right]-\partial_{z}\left[\left(\mathcal{P}_{M}u\right)\mathcal{P}_{M}(-uu_{z}-\nu u_{zz})\right]\right\}^{\widehat{}}_{k}.

(27)

Similarly, for the orthogonal dynamics corresponding to the resolved “
\psi_{k}
”, we have
\eta^{(2)}_{k}(0;\vec{x},\vec{y})=S_{k}(\vec{x},\vec{y})-\widecheck{S}_{k}(\widehat{x},\widehat{y})
and

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{L}\eta_{k}^{(2)}(0;\vec{x},\vec{y})=-\text{Im}\left\{\partial_{z}\left[u(-uu_{z}-\nu u_{zz})\right]-\partial_{z}\left[\left(\mathcal{P}_{M}u\right)\mathcal{P}_{M}(-uu_{z}-\nu u_{zz})\right]\right\}^{\widehat{}}_{k}.

(28)

5
Discussion

Our preliminary results indicate that the instabilities in PDE–ML coupled systems can be avoided by coarse-graining the “PDE modules”, i.e., parts of the system that are computed using conventional methods. This ensures that the inability of the ML surrogates to accurately mimic the high frequency components does not lead to a build-up of energy that cannot be suitably balanced or drained away. The resulting systems are then stable but possess only limited accuracy. We show that the use of memory-based correction terms stemming from the MZ formalism can aid in achieving better accuracy. Our future work aims to improve the efficiency of this approach, thereby enabling the use of higher degree polynomial expansions and hence yielding more accurate results.
