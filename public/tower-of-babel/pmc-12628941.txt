Sex differences in joint pain, limb function and quality of life among patients with Kashin-Beck disease in Northwest China
Authors: Dimiao Wang, Mohammad Imran Younus, Hua Fang, Hansi Mu, Chao Xu, Xiong Guo, Yin-Ping Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKashin–Beck disease (KBD) remains a significant public health challenge in northwestern China, where chronic joint pain and functional limitations severely compromise quality of life (QoL). Despite growing recognition of sex-related disparities in musculoskeletal conditions, sex-specific differences in pain, limb function, and QoL among KBD patients remain underexplored. This study aimed to investigate these disparities to enhance understanding and guide tailored management.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 329 patients with KBD (160 males and 169 females) in endemic areas of Shaanxi Province, Northwest China. Validated assessment tools were employed, including the Graded Chronic Pain Scale (GCPS), Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand Questionnaire (QuickDASH), Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC), and three-level EuroQol five-dimensional questionnaire (EQ-5D-3 L), to evaluate pain, limb function, and QoL. Sex differences were analyzed using Chi-square tests, independent-sample t-tests, and Mann-Whitney U tests.ResultsFemale patients exhibited significantly poorer upper-limb function than males (QuickDASH: 55.4 ± 23.7 vs. 39.1 ± 24.1; p < 0.001), surpassing the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) threshold of 14.0. Pain distribution varied by sex: females reported more hand/foot pain, whereas males more commonly experienced knee/ankle pain (all p < 0.05). Although differences in pain intensity (6.9 ± 1.6 vs. 6.5 ± 1.6; p = 0.016) and lower-limb function (WOMAC: 50.6 ± 20.6 vs. 45.2 ± 18.1; p = 0.012) were statistically significant, they did not exceed the corresponding MCID thresholds (1.0 and 16.1, respectively). Moreover, females had significantly poorer QoL scores (EQ-5D-3 L: 0.589 ± 0.252 vs. 0.684 ± 0.233, p < 0.001; EQ-VAS: 45.7 ± 21.7 vs. 52.7 ± 19.1, p = 0.002).ConclusionsThis study revealed clinically significant sex differences in upper-limb function among KBD patients in Northwest China, with females experiencing greater impairment. While other observed sex differences in pain and lower-limb function were statistically significant, they lacked clinical meaningfulness. The distinct patterns of pain distribution and the significantly poorer QoL observed in females warrant clinical attention. These findings highlight the importance of prioritizing upper limb rehabilitation in treatment strategies, particularly for female patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09304-5.Keywords: Kashin-Beck disease, Sex difference, Joint pain, Limb function, Quality of life

Background

Kashin–Beck disease (KBD) is a chronic endemic osteoarthropathy with childhood onset, characterized by progressive cartilage degeneration and chondrocyte necrosis or apoptosis [1]. The endemic area spans 378 counties across 13 provinces in China, stretching from Northeast to the Southwest [2]. Clinically, KBD manifests as chronic joint pain, skeletal deformities, restricted mobility, and, in severe cases, growth retardation [3]. Its etiology is multifactorial, involving micronutrient deficiencies, mycotoxins, and water contaminants [4]. Following national prevention programs, the incidence of new pediatric cases has markedly declined, yet approximately 177,000 individuals remain affected, and nearly 20 million people continue to reside in endemic areas as of 2023 [2]. The disease burden is particularly severe in Northwest China, primarily involving Shaanxi Province, Qinghai Province, and Gansu Province [5].

Joint pain and deformities in KBD are associated with impaired limb function and reduced quality of life (QoL) [6–8]. QoL—broadly defined as an individual’s perception of well-being and functioning across life domains, is strongly influenced by the severity and duration of chronic pain as well as psychosocial factors at the individual level [9, 10]. Consequently, pain alleviation and functional improvement remain central goals in KBD management [11]. Given that KBD shares similar symptoms with osteoarthritis, its treatments often resemble those of osteoarthritis, including analgesics, intra-articular hyaluronic acid injections, physical therapy, and surgical interventions [12–17]. However, these approaches have limitations: analgesics and physical therapy provide only short-term relief [14, 15, 18]; the efficacy of hyaluronic acid declines with disease progression [16, 18]; and surgical procedures, although beneficial in advanced stages, are complicated by the polyarticular involvement characteristic of KBD [16–18]. Therefore, effective management remains challenging [11], highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of disease manifestations, including sex-specific differences.

Despite its clinical importance, sex-specific differences in joint pain, limb function, and QoL among KBD patients in endemic Northwest China remain poorly understood. Existing research typically reports clinical features or QoL without sex-disaggregated analysis or fails to retain sex as a covariate in multivariable models [6, 8, 19–21]—for example, studies from Aba Tibetan area (n = 989), Sichuan (n = 75), and Tibet Autonomous Region (n = 136). Clarifying these differences is important not only for KBD but also for the broader field of musculoskeletal disorders, where sex-related disparities in pain, disability, and patient-reported outcomes are well documented. Prior studies consistently show that women report higher pain severity, greater functional limitations, and poorer QoL, with psychosocial factors such as depression, anxiety, and catastrophizing exerting stronger influences in women than in men [22–26], Although national-level burden metrics—Years Lived with Disability (YLD) show no clear sex differences [5], such aggregate estimates may mask patient-level disparities in symptom severity and functional impairment. Accordingly, sex-stratified analyses using individual-level data are warranted.

To address this gap, we conducted a cross-sectional study in endemic communities of Northwest China to examine sex differences in joint pain, limb function, and patient-reported QoL. We hypothesized that female patients would report greater pain severity and report poorer function and QoL than male patients.

Methods

Definition of sex and gender

In this study, “sex” was defined as biological characteristics (chromosomal, hormonal, and anatomical), whereas “gender” was defined as socially constructed roles and identities [27]. The analysis focused exclusively on sex differences between male and female patients.

Study design and setting

This was a cross-sectional study conducted from October 20 to October 30, 2019, in KBD endemic areas of Shaanxi Province, Northwest China, which accounts for more than one-third of all KBD cases in China [28].

Participants and sampling

A multistage stratified sampling method was employed, consisting of three stages. First, two counties with KBD high prevalence (Linyou and Yongshou) were selected from among 62 counties in Shaanxi Province. Sample size estimation indicated that 168 participants were required from each county. Second, villages or communities within these counties were stratified into three categories based on prevalence rates: low (< 10%), moderate (10–20%), and high (≥ 20%). Two villages or communities were randomly selected from each category, yielding six villages per county. From each selected village, approximately 26–30 participants were recruited. Finally, in the third stage, patients were randomly chosen from the local Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) KBD database using a random number generator. Selected patients were contacted by telephone to explain the survey and the investigators provided necessary instructions, such as avoiding pain medication on the day of the assessment.

During the screening process, participants underwent a medical history evaluation, physical examination, and hand radiography. Two senior orthopaedic surgeons from a local hospital conducted a clinical diagnosis of KBD according to the official diagnostic criteria (WS/T 207–2010) issued by the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China (http://www.nhc.gov.cn/wjw/s9500/201006/47920.shtml).

The diagnostic criteria for KBD include radiological changes in the distal end of the bones of the middle and proximal phalanges of the index and ring fingers; focal or irregular premature closure of the epiphysis; include limited motion and enlargement of the peripheral joints, deformities and dwarfism; and non-inflammatory lesions in multiple joints. KBD severity was classified into three stages according to these criteria, with higher stages reflecting greater severity.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for KBD; (2) residing in the sampled area for at least six months; and (3) being able to read or understand Chinese. Exclusion criteria were: (1) inability to understand the questionnaires; (2) presence of other musculoskeletal or growth-related disorders, including rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, fibromyalgia, hypertrophic osteoarthropathy, familial short stature, primary dwarfism, epiphyseal dysplasia, chondrodysplasia, pseudoachondroplasia, or other short stature diseases; and (3) hearing impairment.

Sample size calculation

The three-level EuroQol five-dimensional questionnaire (EQ-5D-3 L) was selected as the primary outcome tool due to its high sensitivity and reliability in detecting clinically meaningful changes [29], as well as its alignment with the study objectives. The required sample size for a two-tailed independent samples t-test was estimated using pilot data [30, 31]. Group means and standard deviations obtained from the pilot study were used to calculate the effect size according to the formula: Cohen’s d = (M1 - M2)/SDpooled, where M1 and M2 represent the means of the two groups, and SDpooled is the pooled standard deviation. The estimated effect size was 0.336, corresponding to a small to medium effect [31]. With a significance level of α = 0.05 (two-tailed) and 80% power, the required sample size was 280 participants (141 per group). To account for a potential dropout rate of 10%–20%, the final sample size was adjusted to 155–169 participants per group. All calculations were conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7, University of Düsseldorf).

Measurements

Graded Chronic Pain Scale(GCPS) for pain intensity

The GCPS is widely applied to measure both pain intensity and associated disability in chronic musculoskeletal conditions [32, 33]. It consists of seven items: three items assess pain intensity (current, worst, and average pain in the past month) on 0 to 10 Numerical Rating Scales (NRS) (0 = “no pain at all”; 10 = “pain as bad as could be”), three items capture functional interference (with daily activities, social activities, and working ability) using the same 0–10 NRS (0 = “no interference/change”; 10 = “unable to carry on activities/extreme change”), In this study, only the three pain intensity items (i.e., current pain, worst pain, and average pain in the past month) were administered. Scores of the three pain intensity items were averaged and multiplied by ten to yield a 0–100 scale (named Characteristic Pain Intensity Score), where higher scores reflect more severe pain [34]. The Chinese version of the GCPS questionnaire has good psychometric properties: the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the Characteristic Pain Intensity Scale (the scale measuring pain intensity) is 0.68, and the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the GCPS Disability Scale (the scale measuring functional interference) is 0.87 [35]

Pain frequency

Pain frequency, defined as the regularity with which an individual experiences pain over a specific timeframe, is crucial in the assessment of chronic pain [36]. Participants were asked about their pain frequency in the past month, with four response options available: never, some days, most days, or every day [37]. Persistent pain was defined as frequent (most days) or constant (every day) pain, as opposed to intermittent pain [36].

Pain medication history

Accurate assessment is crucial for effective clinical management of pain. Participants were asked if they had used any pain medication in the past month. If they had, additional questions were posed regarding the types of pain medication used, such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), or other anti-inflammatory medications. Participants also reported the frequency of medication use over the past month (never, rarely, some days, often, or every day), as well as their satisfaction with the medication effects on a five-point Likert scale (ranging from very dissatisfied to very satisfied) [38].

A body pain drawing for pain location

A body pain diagram is the most commonly employed method for identifying pain areas [39]. Participants shaded regions where they had experienced pain in the previous month, including the knee, ankle, hip, foot, shoulder, elbow, wrist, and hand. The number of painful joints was summed per subject, ranging from 0 to 16.

The shortened version of the disabilities of the Arm, shoulder and hand questionnaire (QuickDASH)

The QuickDASH is a shortened version of the original 30-item DASH, developed to reduce respondent burden while preserving psychometric robustness [40]. It includes 11 items and assesses upper-limb function related to daily activities, work, pain, and sensations such as tingling or numbness. Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 0 (no disability) to 100 (severe disability). The QuickDASH correlates strongly with the original DASH [41], and the simplified Chinese version has shown high reliability and validity [42].

The Western Ontario and McMaster universities osteoarthritis index (WOMAC)

The WOMAC is widely used as a patient-reported outcome measure for individuals with osteoarthritis of the lower extremities [43]. It includes 24 items distributed across three dimensions: pain (5 items), stiffness (2 items), and physical function (17 items). Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = none, 4 = extreme), yielding a total score from 0 to 96. Higher scores indicate worse health status. The Chinese version of WOMAC has been validated for use in KBD patients, showing good psychometric properties [44].

The three-level EuroQol five-dimensional questionnaire (EQ-5D-3 L)

QoL was assessed using the three-level EuroQol five-dimensional questionnaire (EQ-5D-3 L) [45, 46]. The EQ-5D-3 L assesses five dimensions of health: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, each rated on a three-level scale (no problems, some problems, extreme problems). It also includes a visual analogue scale (VAS) ranging from 0 (worst imaginable health) to 100 (best imaginable health) [47]. Utility values were calculated using the Chinese EQ-5D-3 L value set developed by Zhuo et al. (2018), based on a nationally representative sample, with index values ranging from 0.170 to 1.000, where higher values indicate better health status and 1.000 denotes full health [48]. The simplified Chinese version of the EQ-5D-3 L used in this study was adapted from our research group [29], and in agreement with the EuroQol Research Foundation, it was used as the ‘suboptimal version’, which was not supplied by the Foundation.

The minimal clinically important difference (MCID)

The MCID is defined as the smallest change in score that patients perceive as beneficial, warranting a change in management in the absence of significant side effects or excessive costs [49]. MCID thresholds are critical for calculating statistical power and interpreting clinical outcomes [50]. A between-group mean difference that meets or exceeds the MCID threshold indicates both statistical and clinical significance, whereas a smaller difference—although statistically significant—may lack clinical relevance [49].

MCID values are instrument- and context-specific. For the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), a 1-point or approximately 15% reduction is considered clinically meaningful improvement in pain based on anchor-based approaches [51]. For the WOMAC, MCID thresholds have been established at 4.2 for pain, 1.9 for stiffness, 10.1 for function, and 16.1 for the total score [52]. The recommended anchor-based MCID for QuickDASH is 14.0 [53].

The EQ-5D-3 L utility index poses unique challenges, as its MCID depends on country-specific value sets, reflecting local health preferences. For example, an MCID of 0.22 has been proposed for the UK value set and 0.14 for the US set [54]. No consensus exists regarding standardized MCID thresholds for EQ-5D-3 L [55], confirming that uniform application of MCIDs across instruments or populations is inappropriate [56, 57].

In this study, MCID thresholds for NRS, WOMAC, and QuickDASH were prespecified based on previous studies in orthopedic and musculoskeletal populations, as KBD-specific MCIDs have not yet been determined. Due to the absence of an agreed-upon MCID for EQ-5D-3 L in KBD, no prespecified threshold was applied for this measure.

Data collection

Data were collected from October 20 to October 30, 2019. Trained investigators assisted participants with reading or writing difficulties by reading items aloud and recording responses, without influencing answers. To ensure independence, neither the investigators nor family members were allowed to influence participants’ choices.

Investigators included three graduate nursing students from Xi’an Jiaotong University and one staff member from the local CDC. All investigators underwent six hours of standardized training, which covered questionnaire use, communication in the local dialect, item explanation, medical history taking, and physical examination.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), depending on the data distribution. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and confirmed by visual inspection of Q–Q plots and histograms. For normally distributed data, between-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test, whereas non-normally distributed data were analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and compared using the Chi-squared test; Fisher’s exact test was applied when the expected frequency in any cell was less than 5. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS software (version 22.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sex differences in socio-demographic characteristics of KBD patients

A 2019 survey of 332 Han Chinese patients with KBD showed that only three participants reported no joint pain in the past month, resulting in a final sample size of 329. Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics stratified by sex. The sex distribution was relatively balanced, with 160 males (48.6%) and 169 females (51.4%). No significant sex differences were observed in age, marital status, occupation, annual household income, or KBD stage (all p > 0.05). However, females had a significantly lower education level, with 66.3% reporting illiteracy, compared with 23.8% of males (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Sex differences in the joint pain characteristics of KBD patients

Among the 329 KBD patients, the mean number of reported pain sites was 7.5 ± 3.7. Females reported significantly more pain sites in the upper limbs than males (4.0 ± 2.4 vs. 3.3 ± 2.6, p = 0.011) (Table 2). Females also reported higher average pain scores (6.9 ± 1.6 vs. 6.5 ± 1.6, p = 0.016) and worst pain scores (8.4 ± 1.7 vs. 8.0 ± 1.8, p = 0.022). However, these sex differences in pain intensity, despite being statistically significant, were not considered clinically meaningful as they did not reach the MCID threshold for the NRS (1.0). For pain frequency within the past month, daily pain was reported by 65.6% of males and 65.7% of females, with no significant sex difference (p = 0.759). Persistent pain (defined as pain present on most days or every day) was also comparable between males (91.3%) and females (95.9%), χ² = 3.517, df = 1, p = 0.061).

Sex differences in analgesic usage and medication satisfaction

Analgesic usage was prevalent, with 88.7% of males and 86.4% of females reporting analgesic use, and there were no significant sex differences in usage frequency (p = 0.394). Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) were the most commonly used analgesics, with 66.9% of males and 57.5% of females reporting their use (Table 2). Overall satisfaction with analgesic effectiveness was low, with only 4.2% of males and 11.6% of females reporting satisfaction (p = 0.143). Although a higher proportion of females reported satisfaction, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Table 2).

Sex differences in pain distribution

Figure 1 shows sex differences in pain distribution among KBD patients. Females reported higher prevalence of hand and foot pain, whereas males reported higher prevalence of knee and ankle pain. The knees and ankles were the most commonly affected joints. Among males, the prevalence of left and right knee pain (93.8% each) was significantly higher than in females (80.5% and 82.3%, respectively; p < 0.001 and p = 0.001). Similarly, ankle pain was more prevalent in males (85.6% for left ankle and 87.5% for right ankle) than in females (left ankle: 75.2%, right ankle: 75.2%; p = 0.017 and p = 0.004).

The distribution of joint pain sites of KBD patients by sex

Hip and foot pain were less common. No significant sex differences were observed in hip pain (males: 18.1% for left, 18.1% for right; females: 17.8% for the left and 15.9% for the right). In contrast, foot pain was more frequent in females than males (left foot: 15.9% vs. 6.3% in males, p = 0.005; right foot: 14.2% vs. 6.3% in males, p = 0.018).

In the upper limbs, approximately half of the patients reported pain in the wrists, shoulders, or elbows, with no significant sex differences (all p > 0.05). However, hand pain showed significant sex disparities, with females reporting a higher prevalence (left hand: 47.9% vs. 32.5% in males, p = 0.004; right hand: 46.2% vs. 35.0%, p = 0.040)

Sex differences in QuickDASH scores and WOMAC scores among KBD patients

Table 3 presents the comparison of QuickDASH and WOMAC scores between males and females with KBD. Females had significantly higher QuickDASH scores than males (55.4 ± 23.7 vs. 39.1 ± 24.1, p < 0.001), surpassing the MCID threshold (14.0) [58], indicating clinically worse upper-limb function in female KBD patients. Additionally, females reported significantly higher WOMAC pain (12.9 ± 4.4 vs. 11.6 ± 3.8, p = 0.029), function (33.2 ± 15.2 vs. 28.9 ± 13.8, p = 0.007), and total scores (50.6 ± 20.6 vs. 45.2 ± 18.1, p = 0.012). Although these WOMAC differences were statistically significant, they did not reach clinical significance, as they were below the respective MCID thresholds (4.2 for pain, 10.1 for function, and 16.1 for total scores) [52].

Sex differences in EQ-5D-3 L among KBD patients

Table 4 summarizes sex differences across the five dimensions of the EQ-5D-3 L in KBD patients. “Extreme problems” were most frequently reported in the pain/discomfort dimension (49.2%), followed by mobility (38.6%) and usual activities (23.3%), with self-care showing the lowest proportion (18.2%). Females reported a significantly higher prevalence of anxiety/depression than males (85.5% vs. 67.0%, p < 0.001). Overall, females reported higher prevalence of “extreme problems” in all EQ-5D-3 L dimensions, with statistically significant sex differences in all five domains (all p < 0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study identified statistically significant sex differences in pain intensity, joint function, and QoL among KBD patients in Northwest China, with females exhibiting poorer outcomes (p < 0.05). Notably, only the sex difference in QuickDASH score, reflecting upper-limb function, exceeded its MCID threshold (14.0), indicating a clear clinical significance. In contrast, while statistically significant, differences in pain intensity and lower-limb function (WOMAC) did not reach their respective MCID thresholds, suggesting limited clinical value. This finding emphasizes the crucial importance of distinguishing between statistical significance and clinical relevance [59]—a key consideration often overlooked when interpreting research results.

Sex differences in the upper-limb function

The greater impairment of upper-limb function in females may be explained by several factors. First, women in rural Northwest China frequently perform repetitive, intensive upper-limb tasks, including handwashing, laundry, and agricultural work (e.g., sowing, weeding, and harvesting). These activities likely impose excessive mechanical stress on upper limb joints, exacerbating existing functional impairments and creating a “double burden” effect [60]. Beyond occupational roles, broader mechanisms are pertinent. Women generally report greater pain sensitivity and intensity across various conditions [22], which may influence KBD-related NRS scores, even if the observed difference did not reach MCID. Additionally, disparities in health-seeking behavior and access to social support can affect functional recovery and QoL [61, 62]. Additionally, the significantly lower educational attainment among females in this study (66.3% illiterate vs. 23.8% of males) may further restrict their access to rehabilitation knowledge or timely medical intervention [63]. Biological differences, including variations in skeletal structure, muscle strength, and hormonal regulation (e.g., estrogen’s protective effect on cartilage), may also play a role [22, 27, 64]. Furthermore, psychosocial factors such as depression, anxiety, and catastrophizing are more prevalent or exert stronger effects in women [23, 26], potentially amplifying the negative impact of KBD on daily life.

Sex differences in pain intensity and lower-limb function

Although statistically significant sex differences were observed in pain intensity (NRS) and lower-limb function (WOMAC) among female patients, consistent with prior reports in arthritis patients [27, 65, 66], their clinical significance remained limited as they did not meet MCID thresholds. Such “statistically significant but clinically meaningless” differences are common in large-sample studies [59, 67, 68] and warrant cautious interpretation of their clinical impact. For instance, the 0.4-point higher NRS score in females was below the MCID threshold of 1.0 point, suggesting no immediate need to adjust existing analgesic regimens based solely on this difference. In addition, no significant sex difference was found in pain frequency—persistent pain (most days or every day) was reported by 91.25% of males and 95.86% of females (p = 0.061), with only a slight increasing trend observed in females. This suggests that the challenge of pain management in KBD patients is more likely derived from differences in pain intensity and location rather than pain persistence.

Sex differences in the pain distribution

An important finding was the sex-specific distribution of pain: females were more likely to report hand/foot pain, whereas males predominantly experienced knee/ankle pain. This pattern diverges from osteoarthritis, where females often report more severe pain across multiple joints [69], suggesting a unique interplay of disease-specific pathological mechanisms and sex-related lifestyles in KBD. The higher incidence of hand pain in females aligns with their upper-limb dysfunction, which may be closely related to their repeated engagement in manual labor [60]. Conversely, the higher prevalence of knee/ankle pain in males may correlate with physically demanding, lower limb weight-bearing labor [70]. These distinct patterns suggest that clinical analgesic strategies should be more targeted, for example, prioritizing local analgesia for female hand pain and emphasizing joint protection for male knee/ankle pain.

Sex differences in the QoL

This study revealed that female KBD patients had significantly lower EQ-5D-3 L utility scores compared to males (0.589 vs. 0.684, p < 0.001), with both sexes significantly below the general Chinese population norms. This aligns with the QoL characteristics of patients with osteoarthritis [71], among whom chronic joint damage similarly impairs overall well-being.

To identify the specific drivers of this sex difference in QoL, we further analyzed outcomes across the five EQ-5D-3 L dimensions: Females also reported a significantly higher proportion of “extreme problems” across all five EQ-5D-3 L dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression) compared to males. The most pronounced difference was in the “anxiety/depression” dimension (25.0% in females vs. 12.5% in males), directly supporting the notion that psychological distress (depression, anxiety, catastrophizing) is more prevalent or impactful in women with chronic conditions [23, 26]. These psychological factors may amplify pain perception and reduce tolerance to functional impairments, potentially contributing to a vicious cycle of psychological distress, reduced activity, and further functional degradation, which corroborates the observed worse upper-limb function and lower QoL in females [62, 72].

Currently, a KBD-specific MCID threshold for EQ-5D-3 L is unavailable, and MCID definitions vary significantly by disease and context [50]. Therefore, direct clinical interpretation of this sex difference is challenging. However, the combined evidence of lower utility values and higher rates of “extreme problems” in females may imply clinical importance, necessitating focused attention in clinical management. This includes integrating psychological interventions (e.g., anxiety counseling) alongside joint function rehabilitation for female patients.

Educational attainment is a well-recognized factor that affects pain and quality of life [73]. Previous research suggests that lower educational attainment is often associated with more severe pain and poorer health outcomes, while higher education generally correlates with better socioeconomic resources and improved QoL [63, 73–75]. Our finding that females had significantly lower educational levels than males is noteworthy. This educational disparity among female KBD patients may be associated with their observed worse QoL and potentially influence their pain perception, for instance, by restricting access to health information, understanding of KBD management, and engagement in health-seeking behaviors [76]. Notably, a previous study by Wang et al. specifically investigated the association between educational attainment and QoL in KBD patients, reporting that while bivariate analysis might show an association, this link could lose statistical significance in multivariate analysis when other factors are controlled [77]. This highlights the complex interplay of various factors influencing QoL in KBD, and suggests that educational disparity alone may not fully explain the observed sex differences. However, due to the cross-sectional design and the scope of our current analysis, we could not establish a direct causal pathway or quantify the mediating/moderating role of educational attainment in the relationship between sex, pain, and quality of life. This remains an important area for future investigation.

Sex differences in analgesic usage and medication satisfaction

Regarding analgesic usage, both sexes showed high prevalence (88.7% males, 86.4% females), with no significant sex differences in usage frequency or preference for NSAIDs. However, overall satisfaction with analgesic effectiveness was notably low (4.2% males, 11.6% females reporting satisfaction), with no significant sex difference. These findings suggest that conventional pharmacological treatments alone are insufficient to adequately manage chronic musculoskeletal pain in KBD [78]. The low satisfaction levels underscore the necessity for multimodal pain management strategies, which integrate non-pharmacologic and psychosocial interventions, particularly for female patients who experience greater functional impairment and poorer QoL.

Study limitations and future research directions

This study has several limitations. First, as a cross-sectional study, causal relationships cannot be inferred between sex and clinical outcomes. Second, the sample, primarily consisting of moderate-to-severe KBD cases, may not represent early disease stages. Third, reliance on self-reported pain and analgesic use introduces potential recall bias. Fourth, while the MCID thresholds referenced in this study are generally accepted for chronic musculoskeletal diseases [79, 80], their direct applicability to the KBD population has not been validated. This limitation highlights the need to establish disease-specific thresholds for more accurate clinical interpretation in future research.

Future research should focus on three aspects: First, conduct multi-center longitudinal cohort studies across various KBD-endemic provinces to track the long-term evolution of sex differences and disease progression. Second, employ mediation analysis to elucidate the roles of educational attainment, psychological factors (e.g., anxiety), and labor load in mediating the relationship between sex, QoL, and function, thereby clarifying the “driving paths of sex differences.” Third, establishing KBD-specific MCID thresholds for EQ-5D-3 L and other relevant instruments to provide more precise benchmarks for clinical significance.

Conclusions

Among KBD patients in Northwest China, only the sex difference in upper-limb function (QuickDASH) reached clinical significance, with females reporting more severe impairment. Differences in pain intensity and lower-limb function, although statistically significant, likely lacked clinical meaningfulness. Female patients also exhibited statistically lower EQ-5D-3 L utility scores, a higher prevalence of extreme problems—particularly in anxiety/depression—and a distinct pain distribution mainly in the hands and feet. These findings underscore the importance of differentiating statistical significance from clinical relevance, and suggest that clinical attention should focus on enhancing upper-limb functional recovery in female patients as part of future KBD treatment strategies; however, prospective studies are needed to validate these approaches.
