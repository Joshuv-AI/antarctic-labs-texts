Effects of myopia on health-related quality of life in adolescents: a population-based cross-sectional causal empirical survey
Authors: Liyong Lu, Yu Zheng, Yanjiao Xu, Junchao Feng, Shunping Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMyopia is a prevalent disease affecting adolescent vision and harms various health outcomes. However, the evidence about the effects of myopia on adolescents’ overall health status is limited. Using health-related quality of life (HRQoL), a comprehensive health measurement indicator, this study aims to evaluate the effects of myopia on adolescents’ HRQoL.Methods and analysisAdolescents’ HRQoL was measured by the Child Health Utility 9D instrument. Data were collected from a population-based cross-sectional survey conducted in Shandong province of China. To mitigate the potential endogeneity bias, parents’ myopia degree was used as the instrumental variable for adolescents’ myopia status. The causal model was built to explore the effects and potential mediators of myopia on the HRQoL of adolescents. Heterogeneity analysis was used to identify susceptible groups.Results108 657 adolescents with an average age of 12 were included in the analysis. Compared with adolescents without myopia, the health utility value of individuals with low, moderate and high decreases by 0.5%, 1.1% and 2.2%, respectively. The reduction of outdoor activity time and increase in electronic device usage time play the mediator role. Myopia leads to a greater decrease in the health utility value of adolescents in higher grades, boarding schools and low-income households compared with their counterparts.ConclusionMyopia impairs adolescents’ HRQoL and damages the overall health level of adolescents. The frequency of outdoor activity and electronic device usage could be the potential mediators. The susceptibility of adolescents in higher grades, boarding schools and low-income households would be exacerbated.

Introduction

Myopia is a prevalent eye condition of adolescents worldwide with prevalence ranging from 80% to 90% among adolescents in some parts of East and Southeast Asia.1 In 2022, the overall myopia rate among children and adolescents in China was 51.9%, with specific rates of 36.7% among primary school students (typically aged 7–13 years), 71.4% among junior high school students (typically aged 13– 16 years) and 81.2% among high school students (typically aged 16–19 years).2 Myopia is a type of refractive error wherein parallel light entering the eye focuses before reaching the retina when the eye is in a relaxed state, leading to a blurred image formation on the retina.1 3 Hereditary, environmental, and lifestyle factors play crucial roles in myopia.38

Myopia affects physical and psychological health by jeopardising the adolescents’ vision. Many psychological and physical illnesses suffered from adolescence can persist into adulthood and even the whole life, leading to enduring health and economic burdens.9 Therefore, the evidence about the effects of myopia on the overall health of adolescents is crucial. However, the related assessment or research is scarce and limited.

One of the main challenges in researching on the effect of myopia on the overall health of adolescents is the measurement of the outcome. The health-related quality of life (HRQoL) would be a potential indicator. HRQoL, as defined by the US Food and Drug Administration, is a multifaceted concept encompassing an individual’s overall perception of their physical, psychological and social well-being,10 and it also is a comprehensive measurement of an individual’s health.11 Extensive research has demonstrated its correlation with the burden of preventable diseases, injuries and disabilities.12 Furthermore, several studies consistently indicate a significant association between HRQoL and mortality.13

The evidence about the effects of myopia on adolescents’ HRQoL is not only scarce but also constrained by some limitations, especially for the endogeneity bias. The challenge of endogeneity, stemming from the bidirectional relationship between myopia and health conditions, complicates the establishment of causal inferences, particularly in cross-sectional observational studies, making the results biased.

To bridge the literature gap, this study aims to explore the effects of myopia on the HRQoL of adolescents, assessed through the Child Health Utility 9D (CHU9D) instrument. This population-based cross-sectional study was based on participants from a survey of the students in Shandong province of China, with 108 657 observations ultimately analysed. The parents’ myopia degree was used as the instrumental variable for adolescents’ myopia status, and the causal model as well as the causal mediation model were built to explore the effects and potential mediators of myopia on the HRQoL of adolescents. Additionally, heterogeneity analysis was used to identify susceptible groups.

Materials

Participants and data collection

The data for this study were obtained from a population-based cross-sectional survey conducted in primary, middle and high schools across Shandong province from July to September 2021. The details of this survey are shown in online supplemental file text S1. Shandong province, located in the eastern coastal areas of China between 34.23°N and 38.24°N latitude, and 114.48°E and 122.42°E longitude, comprises 16 prefectures and 136 counties, covering an expansive 157 100 square kilometres. In 2021, Shandong, the second-largest population province in China, has approximately 102 million residents,14 with about 7.6 million primary students, 3.9 million middle students and 1.8 million high students.15 Online supplemental figure S1 illustrates the location of Shandong province in China. Further details on why Shandong province was chosen as the study area are discussed in online supplemental file text S2.

In addition to the above data set, the county-level socioeconomic and health resources information was collected from the 2022 Shandong Health Statistical Yearbook, 2022 Shandong Statistical Yearbook and the official website of the Shandong Provincial Bureau of Statistics, including the gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, urbanisation rate, hospital number, registered nurse per 1000, beds per 1000 and health institutions number.

Refraction and HRQoL assessment

The questionnaires, including those for students and parents, were administered using an online survey tool called Sojump. The student questionnaire, completed by the students themselves, collected data on basic information, myopia status and HRQoL, measured using scales such as the CHU9D. The CHU9D scale is designed based on interview data with children, ensuring its feasibility and relevance to their experiences.16 The scale has been validated through multiple studies, demonstrating its applicability across different cultural and linguistic contexts. These include assessments of its effectiveness in Australian adolescents,17 evaluations of its feasibility, reliability and effectiveness among Swedish school-aged children and adolescents,10 and a review of the Chinese version in Chinese schools.18 The parent questionnaire was filled out by either parent (father or mother) of the students and used to collect the basic information about parents and their children, and subjective psychological well-being measurements of parent.

Myopia status for students and their parents was self-reported and categorised into five groups: no myopia (>−3.00 diopters), less myopia (LM; ≥−3.00 diopters to <−6.00 diopters), middle myopia (MM; −3.00 to −6.00 diopters) and high myopia (HM; ≤−6.00 diopters).19 Participants were instructed to report the degree of myopia in their eye with the greatest severity.

Adolescents’ HRQoL in this study was assessed using the CHU9D instrument, which was completed by the students themselves. The CHU9D evaluates nine dimensions of adolescents’ health, encompassing physical, emotional and social aspects. These dimensions include worry, sadness or unhappiness, pain, tiredness, annoyance, learning or schoolwork, sleep quality, daily routines (eg, eating, bathing, dressing, getting ready and mobility) and the ability to participate in activities (eg, playing outside with friends, engaging in sports). The impact of each dimension on adolescents’ health has been detailed in previous studies,20 with further details provided in online supplemental file text S3. Each dimension is rated on a 5-point scale: ‘Not at all’, ‘A little’, ‘A bit’, ‘Quite a bit’ and ‘Very much’. Respondents select the option that most accurately reflects their experience for each dimension. In this study, the health state utility value of the CHU9D was calculated using the Chinese tariff, with a minimum value of 0.0563 and a maximum value of 1, where a higher utility score indicates better health status.21

In addition to using the CHU9D to measure adolescents’ health utility in the student questionnaire, we also employed the Assessment of Quality of Life-7D (AQoL-7D). This study used the vision-related quality of life (VisQoL) dimension of AQoL-7D to assess the subjective impact of myopia. The VisQoL component includes six self-reported items: risk of injury, coping, friendship, organising assistance, fulfilling roles and everyday activities. An introduction to AQoL-7D is provided in online supplemental file text S4. Employing multiple measurement instruments allows for a more comprehensive validation of the results and enhances the robustness of our findings.

Data cleaning and data merging

The data exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) observations with logical errors, such as primary school students selecting the option of middle school or high school when responding to the question ‘When did you find yourself suffering from myopia’; (2) observations filling out the questionnaire in a short time (<3 min); (3) observations with abnormal age or body mass index (BMI). A total of 108 657 observations were ultimately used in the analysis. The specifics of the data cleaning process were displayed in online supplemental file text S5.

Covariates

A series of factors associated with both myopia status and adolescents’ health conditions were controlled in the regression model to control the confounding effects. Referring to relevant literature,7 22 characteristics of adolescents, parents and school, as well as living conditions were included as covariates in this study. Specifically, adolescents’ characteristics include gender, age, BMI and whether boarding students at the time of the interview; parents’ characteristics include whether the mother answers the parents’ questionnaire, the educational background of parents who answered the questionnaire and the well-being of parents; school characteristics include the ownership of the school and the grade level; living conditions include whether living in the city, monthly household income level and the socioeconomic status of the residence measured by GDP per capita, urbanisation rate, hospital number, registered nurse per 1000, beds per 1000 and health institutions number. The specific definition and level of each covariate are detailed in online supplemental file text S6.

Analysis methods

To comprehensively understand the effects of myopia on adolescent HRQoL, three analytical steps were undertaken: (1) Causal analysis aimed at exploring the causal effects of myopia on adolescent HRQoL; (2) mediation analysis aimed at elucidating the mechanism through which myopia affects adolescent HRQoL; (3) subgroup analysis aimed at identifying susceptible populations. Instrumental variables and two least square methods (IV-2SLS)

Step 1: We adopt instrumental variables and two least square methods as the main regression to infer the causal effects of myopia on HRQoL of adolescents.

Adolescents with lower health conditions may be more likely to increase electronic screen device use and reduce the frequency of outdoor activity (OA), thereby exacerbating the progression of myopia.2328 The endogeneity could lead to biased estimations in traditional regression models, such as ordinary least squares (OLS).29 30 Instrumental variables combined with two least square methods were used in our main analysis, as this approach is robust to endogeneity bias caused by reverse causality, omitted variables and measurement bias.31 32 The main principle of this method is to find an instrumental variable for adolescents’ myopia degree that would only be related to HRQoL by affecting adolescents’ myopia.33 The specific process in this study is as follows: The first stage is an ordered probit regression model of endogenous variables (Myopia) explained by the instrumental variable (Z), and a set of covariates (X).34 The model of the first stage is shown in equation (1). The second stage is the OLS regression of HRQoL explained by the fitted values of Myopia from the first stage (Myopiai^), and a vector of covariates (X). The model of the second stage is displayed in equation (2).

Among them, NM denotes non-myopia, and using as the reference group; LM less myopia (>−3.00 D), MM middle myopia (−3.00 to −6.00 D), HM high myopia (<−6.00 D); X is the vector of covariates; Zi represents the instrumental variables. The ideal exogenous instruments (Z) must meet two prerequisites.29 33 First, they should adequately explain the change in myopia degree (known as the relevant prerequisite); second, they should not independently explain adolescents’ HRQoL (referred to as the valid prerequisite). In essence, the ideal instruments should only impact adolescents’ HRQoL by affecting their myopia. The Cragg-Donald Wald F statistic was employed to assess the relevant prerequisites. A Cragg-Donald Wald F statistic greater than the Stock-Yogo weak ID test critical values of 10% indicates the validity of the relevant prerequisite.35 36 The second prerequisite cannot be directly verified by statistical methods and is typically supported by relevant evidence from previous literature.

Based on the prerequisites for ideal instrument variables, we selected the self-reported myopia degree of adolescents’ parents as the instruments for adolescents’ myopia. There are two main reasons for this choice. First, heredity is a conclusive trait of myopia,36 and the myopia status of parents can influence that of their children. Particularly for parents with high myopia, the likelihood of their children developing myopia significantly increases. Therefore, the relevant prerequisite would be theoretically satisfied. Second, parents’ myopia degree cannot directly or independently explain adolescents’ HRQoL, especially when controlling for a vector of obvious confounding factors, including family income level, parents’ psychological well-being and living conditions. In other words, the degree of parents’ myopia is at least conditionally exogenous. After controlling for a series of obvious covariates, we can theoretically infer that parents’ myopia degree can only impact adolescents’ HRQoL by influencing their myopia status. The valid prerequisite for ideal instruments would be theoretically satisfied. Further details on why we employed the causal model are discussed in online supplemental file text S7.

Mediation analysis and susceptible subpopulation identifying

Step 2: To explore the potential mediators through which myopia affects adolescents’ HRQoL, we conducted the mediation analysis based on the causal model. Existing literature has established associations between myopia, participation in outdoor activities and the use of electronic devices (ED).728 3739 These factors are also linked to the adolescent health.2325 Our hypothesis posits that the inconvenience caused by myopia might lead adolescents to reduce outdoor activities and spend more time on ED for relaxation, thereby impairing their HRQoL. Details of the regression model and mediation analysis are provided in online supplemental file text S8. The distinction between causation and mediation was discussed in online supplemental file text S9.

Step 3: To identify the susceptible subpopulation, subgroup analysis was conducted. Various factors, including gender, living conditions, economic level, etc, are associated with myopia and adolescents’ HRQoL,83941 modifying the effects of myopia on adolescents’ HRQoL. To identify the susceptible populations, this study focused on the heterogeneous effects of six factors: adolescent’s gender, the ownership of the adolescent’s school, adolescent’s class, whether living in an urban area, whether boarding in the school and household income level. Details of identifying susceptible subpopulations are described in online supplemental file text S10.

All data management tasks were carried out using Excel (version Office 2019); the map was generated using ArcMap V.10.6; the figures were created using the R V.4.0.2 statistical environment; and statistical analyses were conducted using Stata (V.15.0 SE, StataCorp, Chicago, Illinois, USA). P value<0.05 was used to determine statistical significance, and robust SE was employed to mitigate potential issues of heteroskedasticity.

Discussion

This study explored the causal effects of myopia on adolescents’ HRQoL, providing implications for the effects of myopia on the adolescents’ overall health status. This study found that myopia significantly impairs adolescents’ HRQoL, and the decrease in outdoor activities and the increase in the use of ED are potential mediators. Moreover, this study also found that the susceptibility of adolescents studying in higher grades, boarding in schools and coming from low-income families would be greater than their counterparts.

The HRQoL was used in this study to measure the overall health status to explore the health hazard of myopia. This study chose the CHU9D scale to measure adolescents’ HRQoL. While HRQoL is commonly assessed using scales such as 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36), EuroQol-5 dimension (EQ-5D) and CHU9D, these tools differ in their target populations.42 43 SF-36 and EQ-5D are well-established for adults but may lack sensitivity for younger populations’ specific health conditions and preferences.44 In contrast, the CHU9D is specifically designed for children and adolescents.45 Given that our study focuses on adolescents with an average age of 12 years, we selected the CHU9D for its appropriateness in capturing their HRQoL.

Some clinical studies examined the impairment of vision caused by myopia from pathological and physiological perspectives, while observational studies based on real-world data explored its influence on specific mental health aspects, like depression and anxiety.22 46 These available studies are not able to fully reveal the effect of myopia on the overall health of adolescents, but also fail to address endogeneity issues due to limited data, hindering the inference of causal effects. The analysis methods in this study, namely IV-2SLS, provide implications for future studies to explore the causal effects of myopia on health-related factors based on cross-section data.

The inconvenience caused by myopia, especially the need to wear glasses,47 might influence adolescents’ willingness to participate in outdoor activities and lead adolescents more likely to use ED, such as smartphones to relax. The reduction in OA and the increase in ED use can both negatively affect adolescents’ physical, emotional and social well-being.48 49

The adverse impacts of myopia vary across different demographic groups. Students studying in high grades face more pressure, especially in countries like China where the national college entrance examination determines their future.50 Myopia can introduce difficulties in their studies, such as unclear vision of the blackboard, which increases anxiety about learning and performance. The detrimental effects of myopia on health conditions are greater for boarding students compared with day students. Adolescence is a critical period of psychological maturity and growth,51 52 and day students receive more parental attention, which would help to alleviate the damaging effects of myopia.53 54 These findings highlight the need for teachers to pay special attention to boarding students. Higher household income can mitigate the impact of myopia on adolescents’ HRQoL. Greater household income is often associated with improved nutritional status and higher parental educational attainment, factors that may offer enhanced protection against the adverse health effects of myopia in adolescents.

This study has several limitations. First, being a cross-sectional survey, it does not allow for the exploration of temporal trends. Nevertheless, the IV-2SLS causal model used in this study remains robust regardless of the data type. Second, relying on self-reported data would cause bias, especially regarding myopia degree, OA time and ED usage. To mitigate this bias, the survey was conducted with the support of the Ministry of Education in China, ensuring adequate supervision and explanation by teachers and minimising bias. Furthermore, unknown or unclear options were provided for important questions, allowing students who were unsure of how to answer to choose accordingly. Robust tests were also conducted to ensure the reliability of the results. However, while these measures reduce the influence of recall bias, they cannot eliminate it. Additionally, in situations where recall bias is present, adolescents with genuine myopia may erroneously categorise themselves as non-myopic, while those with severe myopia may perceive their condition as mild. Consequently, if myopia adversely influences adolescent HRQoL, the HRQoL assessment within the reference group (non-myopic) would be underestimated, thereby leading to a diminished estimation of myopia’s impact on HRQoL. The outcomes of our study, which emphasise the detrimental effects of myopia on adolescent HRQoL, suggest that the true health ramifications of myopia are greater than those elucidated in this study, rather than lesser. Third, this study was conducted in Shandong province, and caution should be exercised when generalising the findings. Although the burden of myopia in Shandong province is significant and representative, additional research is necessary for broader applicability. Fourth, due to data limitations, this study focused on two potential mechanisms through which myopia affects adolescents’ HRQoL. Future research should explore additional mechanisms by collecting more comprehensive data. Lastly, we have not found clinical evidence regarding differences in HRQoL between refractive groups among Chinese adolescents, which limits the ability to infer the clinical significance of this study’s findings.

Conclusion

Myopia would damage the adolescents’ HRQoL, and the reduction in OA and the increase in ED usage would be the potential mediators. The adolescents studying in higher grades, boarding in schools and coming from low-income families would face more health hazards of myopia compared with their counterparts.
