Phaeochromocytoma mimicking acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction
Authors: Theresa Ackfeld, Hadrien Beuret, Francesca Mosar, Stephane Cook
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryPhaeochromocytomas are rare catecholamine-secreting tumours, usually benign, originating from chromaffin cells of the adrenal glands. Their typical presentation includes the triad of headaches, sweating and tachycardia due to excessive catecholamine release. However, many patients do not exhibit all three symptoms, making diagnosis challenging. A significant proportion of cases (around 40%) are linked to genetic disorders. Cardiac complications, including life-threatening events, are common.This report details the case of a 43-year-old woman presenting with chest pain, headaches and diaphoresis. Initial ECG showed an anterolateral ST-elevation myocardial infarction pattern, but coronary angiography revealed no arterial disease. The left ventricular angiogram indicated apical ballooning. Elevated blood pressure prompted suspicion of phaeochromocytoma, which was confirmed through positive metanephrine tests.The tumour, located in the right adrenal gland, was successfully removed laparoscopically, and pathological examination confirmed the diagnosis.Phaeochromocytoma should be suspected in patients with unexplained myocardial infarction, labile hypertension and normal coronary arteries, as beta-blockers can be harmful if used in untreated cases.

Background

Phaeochromocytomas are mostly non-metastatic catecholamine-secreting tumours that originate from chromaffin cells in the adrenal medulla. The term phaeochromocytoma is reserved exclusively for tumours originating from the adrenal medulla, whereas paraganglioma refers to a tumour of the paraganglia, often originating from the carotid body or other parasympathetic sites. These tumours are usually asymptomatic and rarely metastatic.1 2 Phaeochromocytomas are rare tumours, with an annual incidence of 2–9.1 per million. However, these numbers are most likely underestimated as autopsy studies revealed up to 50% of the diagnoses to be postmortem.3 4

The prognosis is generally good with a 10% malignancy rate and surgical survival rates of 98–100%, but the disease may be fatal if undiagnosed.57 Catecholamine-secreting tumours can occur at any age, but the average age of diagnosis is in the fourth to fifth decade.8

Phaeochromocytomas typically present with symptoms due to catecholamine excess. While the classic clinical triad of symptoms (Menard triad) includes headache, sweating and tachycardia, a wide range of symptoms can occur. These include numerous cardiac manifestations such as hypertensive crisis, shock, acute coronary syndrome, acute heart failure, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, lethal arrhythmia, cerebrovascular events and aortic dissection.9 10 It is estimated that 1–3% present with arterial hypotension or syncope.

Although most phaeochromocytomas are sporadic, approximately 40% of cases are associated with a genetic disorder.2 There are currently ten well-described genetic syndromes associated with phaeochromocytomas, the most common ones being von Hippel–Lindau disease, multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 and neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1).

Case presentation

A woman in her 40s presented to the emergency department with sudden onset of chest pain at rest, accompanied by headaches and diaphoresis. Her cardiac risk factors included active smoking and hypertension. Initial blood pressure was significantly elevated at 235/147 mm Hg, and she had sinus tachycardia at 107 beats per minute. The ECG revealed ST-elevations in anterolateral leads (V2 to V6), indicating an acute ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) (figure 1).

Initial electrocardiography revealing sinus tachycardia and ST-segment elevation in anterolateral leads (V2 to V6).

Investigations

The patient underwent urgent coronary angiography. At the time of arterial puncture, the patient was diaphoretic and sweating, with a weakly palpable pulse and sinus tachycardia measured at 184 beats per minute. The initial invasive blood pressure reading was 263/148 mm Hg.

Coronary angiography confirmed early coronary sclerosis without significant epicardial lesions. (figure 2) The left ventricular angiogram demonstrated apical ballooning with acute mid-ventricular dysfunction (LVEDF 25–30%), suggestive of Takotsubo syndrome (figure 2, online supplemental video).

Coronary angiogram (Panel A: left coronary artery; Panel B: right coronary artery) demonstrating discrete sclerosis. The left ventricular angiogram (Panel C: diastole; Panel D: systole) demonstrated apical ballooning with mid-ventricular a-/dyskinesis.

Differential diagnosis

The cardiologist suspected either cocaine intoxication or phaeochromocytoma.

The patient denied any drug use. This shifted the diagnosis towards an adrenergic crisis related to phaeochromocytoma.

Treatment

Treatment with 5 mg of intravenous urapidil was initiated immediately, and then 10 mg of intravenous labetalol was given.

Outcome and follow-up

Blood was drawn directly from the arterial line for serum catecholamine measurement and toxicology screening. The toxicology screen was negative, ruling out cocaine use. Plasma metanephrine levels were increased.

A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) 24 hours later showed persistent left ventricular dysfunction with an estimated ejection fraction of 25–30%, without complications (video). Oral doxazosin, a selective alpha-1 adrenergic blocker, was introduced.

Urinary metanephrines (4658 nmol/24 hours, normal < nmol/24 hours) and normetanephrines (8533 nmol/24 hours, normal < 3800 nmol/24 hours) were elevated, supporting the diagnosis of phaeochromocytoma. Elevated urinary cortisol (166.3 nmol/24 hours, normal 9.5–111 nmol/24 hours) suggested acute stress.

Thoraco-abdominal CT (figure 3) identified a 52 × 45 × 52 mm mixed-density mass in the right adrenal gland, along with a 19 × 15 × 50 mm lesion in the right paratracheal mediastinum, suspicious for metastasis.

(A) Thoraco-abdominal CT identified a 52 × 45 × 52 mm mixed-density mass in the right adrenal gland. (B, C and D) The 18 FDOPA PET scan confirming intense uptake in the right adrenal mass, indicating phaeochromocytoma.

An I-meta-iodobenzylguanidine scan showed increased uptake in the right adrenal mass, consistent with phaeochromocytoma, without evidence of metastasis. The paratracheal lesion showed no significant uptake.

A follow-up TTE 5 days later revealed recovery of cardiac function, with an LVEF of 55%.

An 18 FDOPA PET scan (figure 3) confirmed intense uptake in the right adrenal mass, indicating phaeochromocytoma, and no other hypermetabolic lesions were detected. The paratracheal lesion disappeared, suggesting it was non-neoplastic.

At discharge, arterial hypertension was well-controlled with doxazosin (4 mg two times per day) and labetalol (100 mg two times per day, with the potential to increase to 200 mg two times per day). At the 1 month follow-up, the patient showed improvement in blood pressure control and left ventricular function. However, she experienced dizziness and palpitations, with a blood pressure reading of 186/99 mm Hg.

Eight weeks after starting alpha-adrenergic and beta-adrenergic blockade therapy, the adrenal tumour was removed laparoscopically, and histopathology confirmed the diagnosis of phaeochromocytoma. During surgery, the patient experienced labile haemodynamics, necessitating high doses of adrenergic blockers and aminergic support postoperatively. No further perioperative complications were noted.

Discussion

Phaeochromocytomas are characterised by the excessive secretion of catecholamines, leading to the stimulation of both alpha and beta-adrenoceptors. This can result in severe cardiovascular manifestations, such as hypertension, myocardial infarction and cardiomyopathy. Preoperative management is crucial, with alpha-blockers administered for at least 7–14 days to control blood pressure and increase the blood volume.6 11 Beta-blockers are only added after effective alpha-blockade to avoid unopposed vasoconstriction and hypertensive crisis.1113

Our patient’s presentation, with signs of myocardial infarction and labile hypertension, was suggestive of phaeochromocytoma, which was confirmed through biochemical testing. The case highlights the importance of suspecting phaeochromocytoma in patients with unexplained acute coronary syndromes, particularly in the absence of significant coronary artery disease.

In phaeochromocytoma patients, the increase of catecholamines can trigger a Takotsubo-like syndrome. The excessive catecholamines exert toxic effects on the heart, causing the myocardium to become ‘stunned’ due to overstimulation of beta-adrenergic receptors. This leads to calcium overload in the heart cells, impairing their ability to contract. The apex of the heart, which is more affected in Takotsubo syndrome, has a higher density of beta-adrenergic receptors compared with the base, making it more vulnerable to the effects of catecholamine level surges. This explains the characteristic wall motion abnormalities seen in the Takotsubo-like syndrome of phaeochromocytoma.1416

The development of a prolonged QT interval is typical during a phaeochromocytoma crisis and significantly increases the risk of sudden cardiac death. This occurs due to excessive catecholamine release, which disrupts normal cardiac electrical activity, leading to delayed repolarisation. A prolonged QT interval increases the likelihood of life-threatening arrhythmias, such as torsades de pointes, further complicating the clinical course and highlighting the critical need for timely diagnosis and management.

Finally, this case illustrates the complex interplay between acute coronary syndromes, hypertensive crises and Takotsubo syndrome. While according to the current definition of myocardial infarction the initial diagnosis is a type 2 anterolateral STEMI, it occurs in the specific context of an adrenergic crisis related to a phaeochromocytoma.17 In the case at hand, both entities—functional dysfunction due to saturation of beta-adrenergic receptors (Takotsubo) and imbalance between oxygen supply and demand (elevated heart rate and blood pressure, type 2 myocardial infarction)—are responsible for the elevation of troponin and cardiac enzyme levels. This demonstrates how acute coronary syndromes form a broad spectrum of cardiovascular conditions and how toxic phaeochromocytomas can be for the heart.

Surgical resection of the tumour remains the definitive treatment, with careful perioperative management to avoid haemodynamic instability. Genetic conditions, such as NF1, should also be considered, given the association with catecholamine-secreting tumours.
