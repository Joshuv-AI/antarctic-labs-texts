Heat and cold stresses exert disparate effects on expression of proenkephalin, and concentrations of Met-enkephalin in the hypothalamus, pituitary and adrenal glands, plasma concentrations Met-enkephalin, Leu-enkephalin, β-endorphin and corticosterone and opioid receptor binding in chickens
Authors: Colin G Scanes, Krystyna Pierzchała-Koziec
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffects of heat and cold stress on opioid peptides and their receptors together with plasma concentrations of corticosterone was examined in 12-week-old female chickens. There were increased plasma concentrations of Met-enkephalin, Leu-enkephalin β-endorphin and corticosterone in pullets exposed to elevated environment temperature (30°C) for 12 hours. In contrast, cold stress (0°C) for 12 hours was accompanied by decreased plasma concentrations of Met-enkephalin, no effect on plasma concentrations of Leu-enkephalin but increased plasma concentrations of β-endorphin and corticosterone. Heat and, to a less extent, cold stressed pullets had decreased concentrations of Met-enkephalin in hypothalamic, anterior pituitary and adrenal tissues but increased concentrations of total Met-enkephalin (enzymically released) and proenkephalin (PENK) expression. There were also effects of heat- or cold- stress on delta (Δ), kappa (K) and mu (M) opioid receptor binding in the hypothalamus, anterior pituitary and adrenal glands.Keywords: Heat stress, Cold stress, Met-enkephalin

Introduction

Physiological responses to heat stress in poultry have received considerable interest because poultry can be exposed to episodes of high temperatures (reviewed: Oluwagbenga and Fraley, 2023). Heat stress decreases growth of chickens and influences expression of inflammatory cytokines (Baxter et al., 2020). There are also marked endocrine shifts with heat stress. Plasma concentrations of corticosterone were elevated in the majority of studies of the effects of heat stress on chickens, (Table 1). To the best of our knowledge, there have not been reports on the effects of heat or cold stress on plasma corticosterone in pullets (Table 1). One goal of the present study is examined these. The overall thrust of the present research program is to determine whether opioid peptides together with corticosterone are stress related hormones and, consequently, potential biomarkers for stress in poultry.

Heat stress affects other components of the hypothalamo-pituitary adrenocortical (HPA) axis, with increased expression of corticotropin releasing hormone (CRH) receptor (CRHR) 2, decreased expression of CRHR1 but had no effect on proopiomelanocortin (POMC) expression in the anterior pituitary gland (Beckford et al., 2020). Heat stress was followed by increased serum concentrations of ACTH, and CRH in broiler chickens (Li et al., 2022, 2023). Moreover, heat stress increased pituitary expression of prolactin and POMC genes (Pritchett et al., 2023). Heat stressed chickens also exhibit increased hypothalamic expression of AVPR1a and natriuretic peptide A (Aloui et al., 2024). Heat stress was also associated with increased serum concentrations of epinephrine in broiler chickens (Li et al., 2022). What is also not known is whether heat or cold stress influences opioid peptides, namely Met-enkephalin, Leu-enkephalin and β-endorphin in chickens. This is examined in the present study.

The amino-acid sequences of the opioid peptides were the following using single-letter codes: Met-enkephalin - YGGFM, Leu-enkephalin - YGGFL, and β-endorphin YGGFMSLEHSQTPLMTLFKNAIVKSAYKKGQ (deduced from Genbank Reference Sequence: NM_001398117.1). The structures of Met-enkephalin and Leu-enkephalin are identical across the vertebrates. The source of β-endorphin is proteolytic cleavage of proopiomelanocortin (POMC), the translation product of the POMC gene. Based on the number of Met-enkephalin motifs (YGGFM), the source is most likely to be derived by proteolytic cleavage of proenkephalin (PENK), the translation product of the PENK gene. The possible sources of Leu-enkephalin include proenkephalin and prodynorphin.

Opioid peptides are presumed to ameliorate the effects of pain, exerting an analgesia when animals are subjected to stressors (Inturrisi et al., 1980). Hence, knowledge of opioid peptides has the potential of improving the well-being of poultry and other livestock. In addition, at least one opioid peptide, Met-enkephalin, acts on cell proliferation and has been consequently referred to as opioid growth factor (e.g. Isayama et al., 1991; Zagon and McLaughlin, 1991; reviewed: reviewed for tumor cells: Zagon et al., 2009; Qu et al., 2019; Budka et al., 2021).

There is limited information on effects of stress on circulating concentrations of opioid peptides in any species. Heat stress influences plasma concentrations of β-endorphin at least in humans. There were increases in plasma concentrations of β-endorphin and ACTH but no change in plasma concentrations of Met-enkephalin in young human volunteers during a sauna with elevated temperatures (Vescovi et al., 1990; Vescovi and Coiro, 1993; Podstawski et al., 2021).

There are also effects of cold stress on the HPA axis. Hypothalamic expression of CRH were decreased in chickens acutely subjected to cold stress (Xu and Wang, 2008). Plasma concentrations of corticosterone were markedly elevated by immersion of chickens in cold water (Nir et al., 1975). In contrast, cold stressed chickens (10 °C) have depressed plasma concentrations of corticosterone (Hangalapura et al., 2003, 2004). There are also effects of cold stress on the thyroid axis with plasma concentrations of (T3) were increased (or tended to be increased) in cold stressed chickens (Collin et al., 2003; Hangalapura et al., 2004).

The present study examines the effects of heat or cold stress on plasma concentrations of Met-enkephalin, Leu-enkephalin β-endorphin and corticosterone together with both Met-enkephalin concentrations and PENK expression in the hypothalamus, anterior pituitary and adrenal glands of pullets subjected to heat or cold stress.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal procedures were conducted with prior institutional ethical approval in accordance with the Local Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). The animal study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and the First Local Ethical Committee on Animal Testing in Krakow, Poland (protocol 120/2013). The experiment was conducted in accordance with the principles and specific guidelines presented in Guide for the Care and Use of Agricultural Animals in Research and Teaching, 4th edition, 2020 except where they would compromise the experimental design

Animals

The studies were performed on 12-week-old female chickens (line - ISA Brown), weighing 1.10 ± 0.10 kg. During the acclimatization period (7 days), the birds were kept in individual cages (60 × 60 × 40 cm) such they they could see and hear conspecifics. Pullets received commercial feed and water ad libitum. The chickens were maintained in a controlled environment with a temperature of 20°C and a photoperiod of 12L:12D (lights on from 7.00 a.m. to 7.00 p.m.). After acclimatization, the chickens were divided into three groups: 1. thermoneutral controls (TN, n=5), kept in the same conditions as during acclimatization; 2. Heat stress (n=5) kept in the room with a temperature 30°C for 12 hours with water and food ad libitum; 3. Cold stress (n=5) kept in the room with a temperature 0°C for 12 hours with water and food ad libitum. Both blood and tissue samples were taken between 9.a.m. to 10 a.m. during the photophase.

Blood sampling

Blood samples (2 ml) were taken after heat or cold treatment stress by venipuncture from the right branchial vein at the same time of day. Blood samples were deposited in polypropylene tubes containing either heparin (1000 IU) for T3 and T4 assay or EDTA 2.7 nmole mL−1 together with citric acid (17.7 nmole mL−1) and aprotinin (Trasylol, 200 KIU mL−1) for assay of Met-enkephalin or Leu-enkephalin or β-endorphin. After centrifugation (30 min at 4°C and 4000 × g), plasma was stored at −80°C until further processing.

Tissue collection

Immediately after the blood samples were collected, the chickens were euthanized by i.v. injection of 70 mg kg−1 of pentobarbital (Exagon, Richter Pharma AG) according to the NIH (2020) guidelines. Tissues (hypothalamus, anterior pituitary glands, and adrenal glands) were dissected, placed on dry ice, and, following freezing to 80°C, used for gene expression analysis, tissue concentration of Met-enkephalin and receptor binding (following homogenization).

Hormone assays

Concentrations of Met-enkephalin, Leu-enkephalin and β-endorphin were determined after extraction of the peptide from the plasma on Porapak Q columns.

Plasma and tissue of concentrations of Met-enkephalin

Concentrations of Met-enkephalin (native and total) in the plasma (100 µL) or tissues were determined by radioimmunoassay (Pierzchała-Koziec et al., 2019). In brief, enkephalin-containing peptides (total enkephalin) were hydrolyzed with trypsin and carboxypeptidase B. Native and total enkephalins were purified on Porapak Q (Waters, 100,120 mesh) in 2 mL of absolute ethanol, then lyophilized, and subjected to radioimmunoassay. Met-enkephalin immunoreactivity was quantified using a commercial rabbit antiserum (Antibody 18R 2 from INCSTAR Co. Minnesota USA) with the following cross-reactivities: Met-Enkephalin - 100%, Leu-Enkephalin - 65%, Met-Enkephalin-Arg-Phe - 70%, Met-Enkephalin Arg-Gly-Leu - 45%, Peptide E and Peptide F < 10% and both Tyr-Gly-Gly-Phe and Gly-Gly-Phe-Met < 5%. Together with 125I-Met-enkephalin, and a Met-enkephalin standard. Lyophilized samples were reconstituted with 100 µL of 0.06 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.5; 0.2% bovine serum albumin and 0.002% sodium azide). Then, 50 µL of antiserum (1:10,000) and 50 µL of 125I-Met-enkephalin (∼1,500 cpm) were added, and the samples were incubated (4°C). After 24 h, 50 µL of rabbit γ-globulin (1%) was added, and incubation was maintained for 30 min. Bound and free complexes were separated by adding 250 µL of 25% polyethylene glycol (PEG 8000). After 30 min of incubation, samples were centrifuged (2000 × g, 4°C, 20 min); the supernatants were discarded, and the pellets were counted in a γ counter (Wizard). The inter-assay and intra-assay coefficients of variance were, respectively, 7% and 11%

Plasma of concentrations of β-endorphin

Plasma of concentrations of β-endorphin were determined after purification by the radioimmunoenzyme method according to the instructions for the kit (Cat. No. RK-022-14, Endorphin RIA Kit, Phoenix Pharmaceuticals, Inc., CA, USA). Intra-assay variation in our laboratory for this kit was 7%; inter-assay variation was 6 %, and assay sensitivity was 15 pg mL−1.

Plasma of concentrations of Leu-enkephalin

Plasma of concentrations of Leu-enkephalin was determined by radioimmunoassay using kit RK-024-21 from Phoenix Pharmaceuticals INC. Intra-assay variation in our laboratory for this kit was 6.7%, interassay variation was 6.2%, assay sensitivity was 94.6 pg mL−1.

Plasma of concentrations of T3

Plasma concentrations of T3 was determined by radioimmunoassay using kit (KIP 1631, DIAsource ImmunoAssays, Louvain-la-Neuve, Belgium). Intra-assay variation in our laboratory for this kit was 5.1%, interassay variation was 3.5%, and assay sensitivity was 0.15 nmole L−1.

Plasma of concentrations of T4

Plasma concentrations of T4 was determined by the radioimmunoassay method using kit (KIP 1641, DIAsource ImmunoAssays, Louvain-la-Neuve, Belgium). Intra-assay variation in our laboratory for this kit was 4.1%, interassay variation was 6.6%, and assay sensitivity was 0.15 nmole L−1.

Plasma of concentrations of corticosterone

The circulating levels of corticosterone were measured in duplicate using a radioimmunoassay (RIA) with a Corticosterone Double Antibody RIA Kit (07–120102, MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA, United States) using 10 µL of plasma. The intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variance were 9.1% and 15.5%, respectively.

Pro-enkephalin mRNA gene expression

Pro-enkephalin gene expression was estimated by a modification of the in situ hybridization method proposed by Lightman and Young (1987). In brief, the frozen adrenals were sectioned (14 µm sections) using a Leica cryostat microtome (−22°C). From the left adrenal gland, 15 slices were collected: from the anterior (5), middle (5), and posterior (5) parts. The sections/slices were thaw mounted on gelatin-covered microscopic slides and stored for three days at −20°C before the assay. Then, tissue sections were thawed and fixed in 4% formaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4) for 10 min. Then, sections were acylated for 10 min in triethanolamine/acetic anhydride (0.25%). Sections were dehydrated by immersion through graded ethanol (70%, 80%, 95%, and 100%) and air-dried. After pre-hybridization, a synthetic deoxyoligonucleotide, complementary to the fragment of hens PENK (5’AGC TCT ACG AGG CGC CCG GCA GCG TCG CTG 3’, was labeled using 35S-dATP (1,200 Ci. nmol-1) to obtain a specific activity of approximately 4 × 106 cpm/μL. The probes were diluted in a hybridization buffer [formamide, dextran sulfate, saline–sodium citrate (SSC) buffer, Denhardt’s solution, yeast tRNA, and herring sperm DNA]. Hybridization was conducted for 20 h in a humidified chamber at 37°C. The sections were then washed once in SSC for 10 min, followed by four washes of 15 min each in SSC/50% formamide at 40°C, rinsed in SSC and distilled water at room temperature, and air-dried. The sections were exposed to Kodak film for 4 weeks (−80°C). The photo stimulated luminescence (PSL) density of the irradiated plates was measured using a BAS-1000 readout system. Optical density was measured at 20 locations in each slice, resulting in 300 readings per adrenal. These readings were averaged. A computer image analysis system was used to determine PSL/mm2 in film images.

Receptor opioid receptor binding

Delta, mu and kappa opioid receptor binding was determined using the method of Belcheva et al. (1994) and Hytrek et al. (1996), with some modification (Pierzchała-Koziec et al., 2017). In brief, the dissected tissues (hypothalamus, anterior pituitary gland, and adrenal gland) were homogenized in ice-cold buffer 50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.4, and the homogenate was centrifuged at 20,000 x g for 15 min. Cell membrane preparations (1 mL, 1 mg of protein) were incubated at 30°C for 30 min with the 6.80 nM titrated δ-opioid receptor agonist D-Ala2-N-Me-Phe4-Gly-ol; 26.00 nM of 3H-DAGO (D-Ala2, Me Phe4, Gly(ol)5 enkephalin for mu receptor) and 59.18 nM of 3H-EKC (ethylketocyclazocine for kappa receptor). Radio- ligands were purchased from Amersham International (3H-DAGO, 3H-DPDPE) and from New England Nuclear (3H-EKC). Nonspecific binding was estimated with 10 μM of unlabeled Met-enkephalin for delta, morphine for mu and Leu-enkephalin-Arg for kappa receptors (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, United States). Free ligand was separated from the membrane-bound radioligand through filtration under reduced pressure using GF/B Whatman glass filters.

Protein concentrations in tissues were determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method (Olson and Markwell, 2007).

Statistics

One way ANOVAs were conducted using Excel with, when significant (P < 0.05) means were separated manually employing Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference test.

Relationships between data for different parameters were analyzed by regression analysis.

Results

Effect of heat and cold stress on plasma concentrations of opioid peptides

Plasma concentrations of Met-enkephalin were depressed (P < 0.001) by 30.0% in cold stressed pullets (Table 2 and Fig. 1). In contrast, there was no effect of cold stress of plasma concentrations of Leu-enkephalin and an 78.1 % increase (P < 0.001) in plasma concentrations of β-endorphin (Table 2 and Fig. 1). Heat stress was followed by increases (P < 0.001) in the plasma concentrations of each of the opioid peptides: Met-enkephalin by 121 %, Leu-enkephalin by 79 % and β-endorphin by 77 % (Table 2 and Fig. 1).

Effect of heat and cold stress on hypothalamic, pituitary and adrenal tissues concentrations of Met-enkephalin

There were decreases in the concentrations of Met-enkephalin in the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary gland of pullets subjected to either cold or heat stress (Table 2); the decreases being 12.7 % (hypothalamus), 9.1 % (anterior pituitary gland) and 30.7 % (adrenal gland) in cold stressed pullets and 50.4 % (hypothalamus), 45.7 % (anterior pituitary gland) and 59.2 % (adrenal gland) in cold stressed pullets.

Tissue concentrations of Met-enkephalin in thermoneutral conditions were highest in the anterior pituitary gland, lower in the hypothalamus and lowest in the adrenal glands (Table 2). The ratio of concentrations in thermoneutral conditions were hypothalamus: anterior pituitary gland: adrenal glands was 2.91: 7.41: 1.00.

Effect of heat and cold stress on PENK gene expression in hypothalamic, pituitary and adrenal tissues

Expression of the PENK gene was increased (P < 0.001) by heat stress and to a less extent by cold stress in pullets in the hypothalamus, anterior pituitary gland and adrenal glands (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Effects of heat or cold stress on opioid receptor binding in hypothalamic, anterior pituitary and adrenal membranes

There were consistent decreases (P < 0.001) of delta (Δ) opioid receptor binding to membranes irrespective of whether from hypothalamic, anterior pituitary and adrenal glands of both heat and cold stressed pullets (Table 3). In contrast, there were multiple and different effects on kappa (K) and mu (M) opioid receptor binding to membranes from the three tissues (Table 3). There were increases (P < 0.001) in K opioid receptor binding to hypothalamic and adrenal membranes of heat stressed pullets but decreases (P < 0.01) in K opioid receptor binding to anterior pituitary membranes (Table 3). Cold stress was accompanied by decreases (P < 0.001) in K opioid receptor binding to hypothalamic and anterior pituitary membranes but increases (P < 0.001) in K opioid receptor binding to adrenal membranes (Table 3). There were increases (P < 0.001) in M opioid receptor binding to hypothalamic and anterior pituitary membranes but decreases to adrenal membranes from heat stressed pullets (Table 3). In contrast, cold stress was followed by decreases (P < 0.001) in M opioid receptor binding to membranes from the hypothalamus, anterior pituitary gland and adrenal glands (Table 3).

Discussion

In chickens, plasma concentrations of Met-enkephalin are increased in response to a series of stresses including physical restraint, fasting, withholding water, crowding, withholding feed or light (Scanes et al., 2024; Scanes and Pierzchała-Koziec, 2024). In the present study, plasma concentrations of Met-enkephalin were elevated in heat stressed young female chickens (Fig. 1; Table 2). However, plasma concentrations of Met-enkephalin were decreased in cold stressed pullets (Fig. 1, Table 2). It is suggested that the physiological response of pullets to a hot environment is a much more severe stress than to cold stress. This may reflected the history of chicken being domesticated in tropical or sub-tropical environments (Eda, 2021). Another possibility that the Met-enkephalin is involved in temperature control decreasing metabolism in conditions of hot stress but not cold stress. In contrast to the effects on plasma concentrations, heat stress and cold stress were accompanied by increases in PENK expression in the hypothalamus, anterior pituitary and adrenal glands of pullets (Table 2). Similarly, expression of the PENK gene in anterior pituitary is increased in response to physical restraint, with-holding water, withholding light and crowding (Scanes et al., 2024; Scanes and Pierzchała-Koziec, 2024). However, expression of the PENK gene in adrenal glands is decreased in response to crowding and with-holding light but increased with water deprivation or physical restraint (Scanes et al., 2024; Scanes and Pierzchała-Koziec, 2024).

The available evidence, albeit scant, is that stress increases circulating concentrations of another opioid peptide, Leu-enkephalin. For instance, plasma concentrations of Leu-enkephalin are increased in dogs in response to challenge with endotoxin and glucose (Merrill and Anderson, 1987), hemorrhage (Brückner et al., 1984) and norepinephrine administration (Johnson and Laasberg, 1987). Similarly, in the present study, plasma concentrations of Leu-enkephalin were increased in pullets subjected to either cold or heat stress (Table 2, Fig. 1).

There is limited information on effects of stress on circulating concentrations of β-endorphin in birds or even mammals. For instance, in mammals, hemorrhage is accompanied by increases in circulating concentrations of both β-endorphin and ACTH in conscious pigs (O’Benar et al., 1987). Plasma concentrations of β-endorphin are elevated prior to surgery in children (Constantopoulos et al., 1995). Plasma concentrations of β-endorphin are increased in dogs following an endotoxin challenge (Merrill and Anderson, 1987). Moreover, release of β-endorphin from porcine pituitary cells is stimulated by oxytocin and vasoactive intestinal peptide (Bogacka et al., 2002). There are no reports of effects of stress on circulating concentrations of β-endorphin in chickens. In the present study, plasma concentrations of β-endorphin were increased in pullets subjected to either cold or heat stress (Table 2, Fig. 1).

There is evidence that β-endorphin influences the HPA axis. Indeed, Pilozzi and colleagues (2020) concluded that β-endorphin are “primarily involved in attenuating acute stress responses”. There is evidence that β-endorphin influences the stress response. Administration of β-endorphin peripherally attenuates the rise in circulating concentrations of cortisol induced by ACTH in humans (Beyer et al., 1986). Similarly, peripherally administered β-endorphin depressed circulating concentrations of both ACTH and cortisol (Taylor et al., 1983).

In the present study, plasma concentrations of T3 were decreased in heat stressed pullets (Fig. 4). Similarly, plasma concentrations of T3 were reported to be decreased in heat stressed broiler chickens (Beckford et al., 2020). In the present study, plasma concentrations of T4 were also decreased in pullets undergoing heat stress. This is similar to the effects of heat stress on plasma concentrations of T4 in broiler chickens in one study (Li et al., 2021). In contrast, Beckford and colleagues (2020) reported increases in plasma concentrations of T4 in heat stressed broiler chickens.

As in the majority of studies of the effects of heat stress of chickens (Table 1), plasma concentrations of corticosterone were elevated in pullets subjected to heat stress (Table 2, Fig. 4). There were also increases in plasma concentrations of corticosterone in cold stressed pullets (Table 2, Fig. 4). This is consistent with plasma concentrations of corticosterone being a released during stresses and leaves open the possibility that plasma concentrations of corticosterone are biomarkers for stress. There were relationships (P < 0.001) between plasma concentrations of each opioid peptide examined. Similarly, in humans that were heat stressed and subjected to exercise, circulating concentrations of cortisol correlated with those of β-endorphin (Kraemer et al., 1987).

Were there to be independent control of Met-enkephalin release in the hypothalamus, anterior pituitary gland and adrenal glands, relationships (adjusted R2) between tissue concentrations of Met-enkephalin would not be expected. This was not the case. Instead, there were strong positive relationships (adjusted R2 >0.80). This is at least suggestive of a common control mechanism(s) for Met-enkephalin release. Tissue concentrations of Met-enkephalin reflect the disparity between rate release and rate of synthesis (Figs. 3, 4). Thus, tissue concentrations of Met-enkephalin would be expected to be inversely related to release. Moreover, if the same stimuli influence release and synthesis of Met-enkephalin then inverse relationships between tissue concentrations of Met-enkephalin and expression of PENK would be found. This was indeed found with very strong negative relationships (adjusted R2 > 0.92) between Met-enkephalin concentrations and PENK expression in both pituitary and adrenal tissue. This supports there being a common mechanism for the control of Met-enkephalin release and synthesis.

In conclusion, in sexually immature female chickens subjected to either heat or cold, there were increases in plasma concentrations of Met-enkephalin, β-endorphin and corticosterone together with PENK expression and concentrations of Met-enkephalin in the hypothalamus, anterior pituitary and adrenal glands. In contrast, plasma concentrations of Leu-enkephalin were increased in pullets subjected to elevated temperatures but were unaffected by cold stress. Moreover, it is proposed that opioid peptides, opioid receptors and plasma concentrations of corticosterone play a role in the physiological responses to both heat and cold stress. This study is a component of a research program is to determine whether opioid peptides together with corticosterone are stress related hormones and, consequently, potential biomarkers for stress (the converse of welfare). When activated by stressors, the HPA axis adversely affect growth and reproduction of poultry by interacting with the gonads, liver, muscles and appetite controls. It is proposed that Met-enkephalin is released by severe stresses and this attenuates the adrenal response. In addition, it is presumed that opioid peptides are acting to reduce the perception of pain and, consequently, foster comfort and well-being.
