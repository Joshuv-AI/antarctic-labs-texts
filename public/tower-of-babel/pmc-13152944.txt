Preoperative CT-based quantitative assessment of hernial SAC predicts early recurrence following primary hiatal hernia repair
Authors: Takahiro Ochiai, Masanobu Nakajima, Masatoshi Nakagawa, Junki Fujita, Shuhei Takise, Yu Ueta, Masaki Yoshimatsu, Noboru Inoue, Hiroto Muroi, Shinji Morita, Kazuyuki Kojima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecurrence of hiatal hernia after primary repair is a major concern, particularly early recurrence related to technical factors. Reliable preoperative predictors are essential for optimal surgical strategies. Because the hernial sac area can also affect operative complexity, this study aimed to evaluate early recurrence-associated factors and their relationship with operative time using quantitative computed tomography (CT).MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 48 patients who underwent laparoscopic hiatal hernia repair between July 2012 and July 2025. Preoperative CT was used to measure the maximum diameter and area of the hernial sac in the axial and coronal planes. We examined the association between these parameters and surgical outcomes including recurrence.ResultsEarly recurrence was observed in four patients (8.3%), all of whom required reoperation within 3 days. Patients with early recurrence had longer operative times and larger axial hernial sac areas (p < 0.05). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that the axial maximum diameter provided the highest predictive performance for early recurrence (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.89), with an optimal cutoff value of 117.6 mm. Other parameters, including axial area, coronal area, coronal diameter, and hiatal width, showed moderate predictive ability but none exceeded axial diameter accuracy.ConclusionAxial hernial sac measurements are associated with early recurrence. Preoperative CT-based quantitative assessments may provide valuable information for predicting surgical complexity and recurrence, thereby supporting preoperative risk stratification and surgical planning. Therefore, prospective validation using a larger cohort is warranted.Keywords: Hiatal hernia, Hiatal hernia repair, Hernial sac area, Recurrence, Gastropexy

Introduction

Hiatal hernia is a condition in which the stomach prolapses into the mediastinum through the esophageal hiatus, and patients may present with symptoms such as chest discomfort, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), and dysphagia [1]. Patients with giant hiatal hernias, classified as type III or IV, are prone to severe symptoms due to esophageal axis deviation and gastric volvulus. Cases with obstructive symptoms have risks of complications such as strangulated gastric necrosis or perforation increases, and surgical intervention may become necessary [2, 3].

Postoperative recurrence remains a significant concern, and the presence or absence of adjunctive procedures such as gastropexy or mesh augmentation around the hiatus may influence recurrence rates. Although mesh augmentation has been reported to reduce recurrence, it has also been associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications, such as dysphagia and stenosis, requiring careful selection of surgical procedures [4]. On the other hand, the addition of gastropexy has attracted attention as a relatively safe and effective method for preventing recurrence, with several studies supporting its utility [5, 6]. In particular, early recurrence is often considered to reflect technical failure rather than the long-term durability of the repair. Predicting recurrence risk preoperatively and selecting the most appropriate surgical strategy are important to prevent recurrence. Quantitative assessment of the hernial sac area using preoperative computed tomography (CT) can reflect the anatomical characteristics and may serve as a useful parameter for predicting early recurrence and operative complexity [7]. Morphological evaluation that considers not only the maximal diameter but also the area may contribute to optimizing surgical procedure selection and improving postoperative outcomes.

In this study, we retrospectively investigated the relationship between preoperative CT–based morphological parameters of the hernial sac (maximal diameter and area) and postoperative outcomes, with a particular focus on early recurrence in patients undergoing hiatal hernia repair at our institution. We aimed to identify the risk factors for early recurrence, thereby contributing to the optimization of surgical procedure selection and personalized treatment strategies.

Patients and methods

Patients

A total of 56 patients who underwent surgical repair of hiatal hernia at the Dokkyo Medical University Hospital between July 2012 and July 2025 were included. One case that was converted to hand-assisted laparoscopic surgery (HALS) and seven cases in which additional gastropexy was performed were excluded in order to maintain procedural uniformity, leaving 48 eligible patients. Preoperative CT and upper gastrointestinal endoscopy were routinely performed in all patients with hiatal hernia at our institution. The majority of patients were referred from other institutions, and acute presentations were relatively uncommon. The CT images were evaluated by multiple gastrointestinal surgeons and radiologists. Postoperative follow-up evaluation included CT and upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. Postoperative recurrence was defined as reherniation of the stomach more than 2 cm above the diaphragm detected on these examinations. Recurrence within 30 days after surgery was classified as early recurrence.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Dokkyo Medical University (approval number: R-21–6 J).

Surgical procedure

All procedures were performed laparoscopically by seven gastrointestinal surgeons with surgical experience ranging from 6 to 22 years at the time of surgery. The hernial sac was circumferentially dissected from the mediastinum and resected after reducing the stomach and other herniated organs into the abdominal cavity. The esophagus was carefully dissected and straightened while preserving both the anterior and posterior vagal trunks, and at an intra-abdominal esophageal length of at least 2 cm was obtained. Short gastric vessels were divided as required to achieve adequate mobilization of the gastric fornix. Hiatal closure was performed using multiple interrupted nonabsorbable sutures. Mesh augmentation was selectively used when the diaphragmatic crura were considered fragile after dissection. After confirming adequate mobility of the gastric fornix to allow proper wrapping around the dorsal side of the esophagus, Toupet fundoplication was performed to reduce the risk of postoperative dysphagia, particularly in elderly patients. Intraoperative endoscopy was performed to confirm adequate passage through the cardia and appropriate formation of the His angle. In patients with early recurrence, surgery was repeated with reduction of the herniated stomach, re-establishment of at least 2 cm of intra-abdominal esophageal length, and re-closure of the hiatus. Mesh reinforcement was added when the crural tissue was considered fragile. Estimated blood loss was calculated by subtracting the irrigation fluid from the suction canister volume. Minimal blood loss was recorded as 1 mL according to institutional recording practices.

Morphological assessment of the hernial sac by CT

Preoperative chest and abdominal CT (slice thickness, 5 mm) were used to evaluate the maximal diameter and area of the hernial sac in the axial and coronal orientations.

Measurement of maximal diameter: The largest cross-section of the herniated contents through the hiatus was selected, and the longest distance across the hernial sac was measured (Fig. 1a, b).

Quantitative assessment of hernial sac morphology on preoperative CT: measurement of maximal diameter (a, b) and cross-sectional area (c, d)

Measurement of area: The contour of the hernial sac was manually traced on the same slice used to determine the maximal diameter, and the area (mm²) was calculated using the polygonal region of interest (ROI) tool in the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS; SYNAPSE, Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) (Fig. 1c, d). CT measurements were independently performed by two gastrointestinal surgeons, and interobserver agreement was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way random-effects model for absolute agreement (ICC (2, 1)).

Data collection

Patient demographics, surgical outcomes, and endoscopic findings were collected from medical records.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were analyzed using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the data distribution. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed for each preoperative CT parameter to evaluate their ability to predict recurrence. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated and the optimal cutoff values were determined using the Youden index with the corresponding reported sensitivity and specificity. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using EZR (version 1.68; Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics and surgical outcomes

The clinical characteristics, surgical outcomes, and preoperative CT measurements of the study population are summarized as follows. The median patient age was 76 years, and the cohort included 10 men and 38 women. The median preoperative body mass index (BMI) was 23.2 kg/m². Preoperative GERD (Los Angeles classification grade A or higher) was present in 28 patients (58.3%), and type IV hernias were observed in 13 patients (27.1%). Regarding preoperative CT measurements, the median hiatal width was 41.3 mm, axial sac diameter 100.9 mm, coronal sac diameter 71.9 mm, axial sac area 4,817.5 mm², and coronal sac area 5,392.0 mm². The median operative time and blood loss volume were 220 min and 1 mL, respectively. Postoperative complications of Clavien-Dindo grade II or higher occurred in 13 patients (27.1%), with a median hospital stay of 8 days and no surgery-related mortality. Grade II complications included delayed gastric emptying (n = 3) and cardiac tamponade (n = 1), whereas grade III complications included early recurrence requiring reoperation (n = 4), stenosis requiring endoscopic balloon dilation (n = 4), and pneumothorax requiring chest tube placement (n = 1). The median follow-up period was 8.6 months. A total of 21 patients (43.8%) underwent follow-up beyond 12 months and were available for evaluation 1 year after surgery.

Early recurrence occurred in four patients (8.3%), all of whom developed recurrence within 3 days postoperatively and required reoperation.

Association of CT indicators with operative time

Operative time was dichotomized based on the median (220 min) into prolonged (n = 24) and non-prolonged (n = 24) groups. Prolonged cases had significantly larger hiatal width (48.9 mm vs. 37.8 mm, p = 0.033), sac maximal diameter (axial: 117.1 mm vs. 84.1 mm, p = 0.033; coronal: 95.4 mm vs. 65.7 mm, p = 0.040), and sac area (axial: 5,740.1 mm² vs. 3,350.8 mm², p = 0.041; coronal: 7,813.2 mm² vs. 4,511.2 mm², p = 0.039) than the non-prolonged group. The frequency of type IV hernia tended to be higher in the prolonged group (37.5% vs. 16.7%); however, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.108) (Table 2). ROC analysis demonstrated that all CT measurements showed modest discriminative ability for prolonged operative time (AUC range: 0.68–0.71); however, no parameter showed clearly superior performance (Table 3).

Association of CT indicators with early recurrence

A comparison between the early recurrence group (n = 4) and the non-recurrence group (n = 44) revealed that the operative time was significantly longer in the early recurrence group (274 min vs. 218 min, p = 0.029). The hiatal width was greater in the early recurrence group (53.3 mm vs. 40.9 mm), but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.117).

Regarding sac size, the maximal diameter in the axial orientation was significantly larger in the early recurrence group (157.3 mm vs. 98.1 mm, p = 0.010), whereas in the coronal orientation it tended to be longer (112.8 mm vs. 69.5 mm, p = 0.051) without reaching statistical significance. The sac area was significantly larger in the early recurrence group for both axial (11,007.4 mm² vs. 4,438.3 mm², p = 0.017) and coronal (13,053.2 mm² vs. 4,527.2 mm², p = 0.033) orientations.

Although the frequency of type IV hernia was higher in the early recurrence group (50.0% vs. 25.0%), the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.294). Additionally, surgeon experience was not significantly associated with early recurrence (p = 0.234). Likewise, mesh augmentation was not significantly associated with early recurrence (p = 0.798). (Table 4). Discussion

Postoperative recurrence rates after hiatal hernia repair vary widely from 5% to 59%, depending on the surgical approach and evaluation methods, and remain high [2, 8–10]. The etiology of hiatal hernia recurrence after repair is thought to involve repetitive movement of the diaphragm and esophagus as well as tension at the crural closure site caused by primary repair [11]. However, early recurrence represents a clinical entity distinct from late anatomical recurrence and has been suggested to be associated with technical factors, including inadequate mediastinal mobilization, insufficient crural closure, and early wrap migration [12], as reported in studies of recurrence overall. Although advanced age, female predominance, and obesity are commonly observed in patients with hiatal hernia [2, 3], Japanese cohorts often include a relatively high proportion of elderly patients and have been reported to have a higher proportion of female patients and a relatively lower body mass index [13, 14]. In addition, a higher prevalence of spinal deformities such as kyphosis, which may influence disease presentation and the risk of recurrence, has been reported in this population [14]. These characteristics were also observed in our cohort and may affect the generalizability of our findings.

In terms of morphology, factors such as large hernia size and increased hiatal surface area (HSA) have also been reported to be associated with recurrence [15, 16]. Armijo et al. reported that large hernias (> 5.0 cm) were observed in 82.9% of patients in the recurrence group compared with 54.2% in the non-recurrence group [15]. Grubnik et al. demonstrated that, using the intraoperative HSA measurement method described by Granderath et al. [17], an HSA ≥ 10 cm² was associated with a higher recurrence rate (11.9% vs. 3.5%) [18]. However, no other studies have confirmed hernia size as a significant predictor of recurrence [9]. Overall, these findings are primarily based on long-term recurrence, and the relevance of hernia size and HSA to early recurrence remains unclear. Therefore, more objective and reproducible preoperative parameters are needed.

Recently, quantitative preoperative CT evaluation has gained attention, particularly parameters such as hernial sac volume (HSV) and hiatal defect area (HDA), which may influence surgical strategy and outcomes. Kao et al. performed CT volumetric analysis and proposed a morphological classification of hiatal hernia, showing its potential utility in predicting recurrence and postoperative complications [19]. Their study also emphasized HSV and HDA as reproducible and non-invasive preoperative markers. Similarly, Elhage et al. reported that increases in HSV and HDA were significantly correlated with the need for emergent surgery, with a 17% higher risk per 100 cm³ HSV increase (OR 1.17, p = 0.022) [7]. Collectively, these findings support the use of CT-based morphological evaluation as a valuable tool for preoperative risk stratification and surgical planning. In the present study, we quantitatively analyzed the maximal diameter and area of the hernial sac using CT data obtained from the PACS. Measurements were performed on standardized slices without specialized software, making this method practical and potentially reproducible across institutions. The ICC for CT measurements was high, supporting its reliability.

Our CT-based analysis demonstrated that axial measurements provided better predictive performance than coronal measurements.

The axial plane reflects the horizontal protrusion of the hernial sac relative to the hiatus. Larger axial dimensions increase the tension at crural closure, restrict the operative field, and complicate laparoscopic maneuverability, leading to a more complex dissection, prolonged operative time, and a higher risk of recurrence. In contrast, the coronal plane mainly represents vertical extension, which less often compromises exposure or increases crural tension and thus has a more limited impact on operative complexity. This difference may explain why coronal enlargement, although moderately associated in the ROC analysis, was not as strongly predictive of recurrence as axial enlargement in our cohort. Surgeon experience was not significantly associated with early recurrence, and such events were not clustered in the initial phase of the learning curve. Less experienced surgeons were supported by more experienced assistants, and this approach, which compensated for differences in surgeon experience, may have contributed to the lack of association between surgeon experience and early recurrence. These findings indicate that CT-based quantitative parameters can function not only as predictors of early recurrence but also as objective markers of operative complexity, thus helping anticipate intraoperative challenges during sac reduction, visualization, and hiatal closure.

Previous studies have suggested that hernia type influences recurrence risk, with type III generally showing lower rates than type IV (13.3% vs. 37.5%), although recurrence has also been reported in type III hernias [20]. In our cohort, one patient with type III hernia experienced recurrence despite a hiatal width of only 35 mm (below the reported 50 mm threshold [16]). However, this patient had marked sac enlargement (axial diameter 164 mm, axial area 11,178 mm², and coronal area 13,426 mm²), with parameters exceeding the cutoff values. These findings suggest that relying solely on hiatal width or type classification is insufficient and that incorporating quantitative CT evaluation may enable more accurate risk stratification and treatment planning.

Technical strategies to reduce postoperative recurrence include mesh augmentation and anterior gastropexy. Mesh augmentation has been reported to lower short-term recurrence compared to suture repair (2.6% vs. 9.4%) [21]; however, but several long-term studies found no significant difference [9, 22, 23]. Moreover, the mesh carries potential late complications such as dysphagia, erosion, and fibrosis, occasionally requiring major reoperations, including esophagectomy [4]. Barazanchi et al. further noted that prior mesh placement increases the difficulty and risk of reoperative surgery [24], underscoring the need for careful selection in primary repairs. In our cohort, mesh augmentation was not significantly associated with early recurrence; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of recurrence events. Anterior gastropexy has recently attracted attention as a simple and safe adjunctive procedure. In a randomized trial, Petro et al. showed that gastropexy reduced the 1-year recurrence rate to 15% compared to 36% in cases without gastropexy [5]. Fukushima reported that gastropexy significantly lowered recurrence and reflux symptoms in mesh-reinforced repairs [6]. Yokouchi et al. demonstrated its efficacy and safety in older patients aged ≥ 75 years [13]. At our institution, anterior gastropexy has been performed in seven type IV cases since 2024, with no recurrence to date. Despite the short follow-up period, this approach appears promising, especially in patients with high-risk features, such as an enlarged sac area or hiatal width, as identified in this study. However, routine application should be performed cautiously, as gastropexy may prolong operative time and cause fixation-related discomfort or pain, underscoring the importance of careful patient selection [5].

Based on these findings, our results may inform future surgical strategy. Rather than relying solely on subjective intraoperative assessments such as tissue fragility or crural tension after dissection, selective mesh augmentation may be considered based on objective anatomical factors, particularly in cases with hiatal defects exceeding approximately 5 cm or an enlarged hernial sac area. Anterior gastropexy may continue to be used because of its technical simplicity and favorable balance between recurrence prevention and safety, particularly in patients with high-risk anatomical features such as type IV hernia or enlarged hernial sac area. Meticulous surgical technique aimed at reducing axial tension—such as adequate mediastinal mobilization and minimization crural tension—may be important for minimizing early recurrence.

This study had some limitations. First, this was a retrospective, single-center analysis with a relatively small sample size, and only four early recurrence events were observed, which may limit the strength of the conclusions. Second, the follow-up duration was short and most recurrences were detected very early, suggesting that technical factors, rather than long-term durability, may have influenced the outcomes. Moreover, given the aging population in Japan, a relatively high proportion of patients were elderly, which may have affected follow-up duration, as some patients may have experienced difficulty with long-term postoperative visits. Third, recurrence assessment may have been influenced by observer-dependent interpretation, and further studies with consistent radiological and clinical criteria are required. Fourth, multivariate analysis was not performed because the small number of recurrence events would have resulted in unreliable and unstable estimates; therefore, the independent contribution of the axial area relative to other risk factors remains uncertain. Fifth, patients who underwent gastropexy were excluded to maintain uniformity, which may have introduced selection bias, as these cases may represent higher-risk anatomical features. However, given the small number of such cases, the effect of gastropexy could not be adequately assessed. Finally, the cutoff value proposed for the axial area was derived and evaluated within the same dataset without internal validation, which may introduce optimism bias. In addition, it may be influenced by institutional CT protocols; therefore, it requires external validation in prospective multicenter studies using standardized imaging protocols is required to confirm the generalizability of these findings.

In conclusion, preoperative quantitative assessment of the hernial sac, particularly the axial parameters, demonstrated a strong univariate association with early recurrence and warrants further evaluation. Incorporating CT-based measurements into surgical planning may improve risk stratification beyond hiatal width and type classification and help guide the use of adjunctive procedures such as gastropexy, thus supporting more tailored surgical decision-making.
