Creatine monohydrate supplementation for recovery from muscle disuse: Timing matters
Authors: Jeffrey R Stout, Patrick S Pabian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCreatine monohydrate supplementation is best known as an ergogenic aid. However, its potential role in recovery from muscle disuse atrophy has attracted increasing scrutiny in healthy and clinical populations. This narrative mini-review examines human studies across immobilization, reloading, orthopedic and medical rehabilitation, and exercise-induced muscle damage, with close attention to supplementation timing relative to the recovery phase. During brief periods of immobilization, creatine can increase intramuscular creatine availability, but controlled lower-limb studies show that it does not consistently preserve muscle cross-sectional area or strength. Once progressive loading resumes, findings are more favorable. Creatine has accelerated recovery of quadriceps cross-sectional area and knee extensor power during resistance-based rehabilitation after immobilization, and individual trials report added benefit when paired with strengthening in knee osteoarthritis, Parkinson's disease, and inflammatory myopathies. In contrast, evidence is null or mixed for total knee arthroplasty, early inpatient stroke care, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and peripheral arterial disease (PAD). Studies of exercise-induced muscle damage provide a parallel recovery model, but trial-level findings and meta-analyses remain inconsistent. Current evidence does not support creatine as a stand-alone strategy during strict unloading. Its more plausible role is as a low-risk adjunct during reloading, when progressive resistance exercise and adequate nutrition restore an anabolic environment. Future studies should separate immobilization from active rehabilitation, quantify rehabilitation dose, verify creatine uptake when possible, and prioritize clinically meaningful functional outcomes.Keywords: Phosphocreatine, Unloading, Rehabilitative exercise, Rehabilitation, Resistance training, Responder status

Introduction

Disuse atrophy is a recurring phenotype across orthopedics, neurology, and internal medicine. Musculoskeletal immobilization, post-injury or post-operative activity restriction, bed rest, and inflammatory or neurologic illness can each produce rapid losses in muscle size, force, and power, followed by slower and sometimes incomplete recovery. Low-cost nutritional adjuncts are attractive because they are scalable and can be layered onto existing rehabilitation programs.1,2 (see Table 1, Table 2)

Creatine monohydrate is among the most studied dietary supplements. It increases intramuscular creatine and phosphocreatine availability and can augment high-intensity training adaptations in healthy populations.3,4,5 Its rehabilitation value, however, appears to be phase dependent. In a 7-day full-leg immobilization model, creatine loading increased muscle creatine content but did not preserve quadriceps cross-sectional area or knee-extension strength.6 In a 14-day unilateral casting model followed by 10 weeks of progressive knee-extensor resistance training, creatine did not attenuate atrophy during immobilization but did accelerate recovery of quadriceps cross-sectional area and maximal knee-extension power during the rehabilitation period.7 Conversely, an upper-limb casting study reported better maintenance of lean tissue mass, strength, and endurance during a 1-week immobilization period, but the very small sample size and fixed-order crossover design limit the certainty of its findings.8

This mini-review advances a specific interpretation of the human literature on creatine use in rehabilitation populations. These populations span individuals with musculoskeletal injuries, chronic conditions, or critical illness that may lead to disuse, deconditioning, and negative functional outcomes without appropriately timed skilled care. Current evidence does not support creatine as a dependable stand-alone countermeasure during strict unloading or deconditioning. The more plausible role is as a rehabilitative exercise adjunct once progressive loading, strengthening, and adequate nutrition return. This review therefore evaluates creatine by recovery phase and rehabilitation context, with emphasis on timing, training dose, and outcome selection.

Scope and approach

This narrative mini-review is focused on the use of creatine monohydrate in skeletal muscle disuse and rehabilitation. Priority was given to randomized or placebo-controlled human studies in immobilization, injury rehabilitation, and clinical rehabilitation or exercise retraining. Narrative reviews and meta-analyses were used only to contextualize the trial literature and to avoid overinterpreting isolated positive findings. This article is not a systematic review.

The emphasis throughout is on human rehabilitation relevance rather than mechanistic novelty alone. Accordingly, randomized trials and controlled clinical studies are weighted more heavily than uncontrolled observations, case reports, editorials, or laboratory studies. Where evidence is limited to lower-level designs, that limitation is made explicit.

Evidence across recovery phases

Creatine as a recovery-biology adjunct

The creatine kinase system buffers adenosine triphosphate (ATP) during rapid shifts in energy demand. In rehabilitation, the relevant question is not whether creatine helps athletes maximize performance, but whether it helps patients execute rehabilitation or exercise protocols with greater efficacy within time-constrained therapy sessions. Original human loading studies show that oral creatine can increase skeletal-muscle creatine availability and improve repeated maximal contraction performance, supporting the plausibility of greater phosphocreatine (PCr) availability for repeated high-effort contractions, shorter recovery between sets, and sustained force output during functional tasks.4,5

Mechanistically, creatine may matter in recovery biology through at least three routes. First, it may increase the effective exercise stimulus by allowing more work at a given effort.5 Second, human post-immobilization data suggest that creatine can influence glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) content and glycogen-related responses during reloading periods.9 GLUT4 is the major insulin-responsive glucose transporter in skeletal muscle, and greater GLUT4 availability during reloading may support glucose uptake and glycogen restoration for repeated rehabilitative contractions. Third, post-immobilization training studies report creatine-associated differences in myogenic-factor protein expression, including myogenic regulatory factor 4 (MRF4), consistent with an interaction between creatine and muscle remodeling when a hypertrophic stimulus is present.7 MRF4 is a myogenic regulatory factor involved in muscle differentiation and remodeling; therefore, its association with muscle-fiber size provides a plausible mechanistic bridge between creatine, myonuclear regulation, and hypertrophic adaptation during progressive loading.

These effects should not be overstated. In the absence of exercise, creatine does not appear to increase muscle protein synthesis or suppress muscle protein breakdown at rest.10 This helps explain why saturation of muscle creatine stores during immobilization does not reliably translate into preservation of muscle size or strength in controlled lower-limb disuse models.6,7 If unloading suppresses mechanical signaling and limits net protein accretion, creatine may have little opportunity to influence clinically meaningful outcomes until reloading begins.

This distinction is clinically important. Nutritional strategies that work by amplifying a training signal should not be expected to behave like pharmacologic anti-catabolic agents. Framing creatine as a training amplifier rather than a universal anti-atrophy therapy better matches the available physiology and the human trial data.

Evidence during immobilization and disuse

The strongest human evidence during the disuse phase comes from lower-limb immobilization studies in healthy young adults. Although these models were conducted in healthy populations, they are relevant to common musculoskeletal care pathways involving temporary unloading, such as fractures, ligamentous injuries, or post-surgical restrictions. Backx et al. used creatine supplementation at 20 g/day for 5 days before a 7-day full-leg cast and confirmed that muscle creatine content increased, yet quadriceps cross-sectional area and knee-extension strength declined similarly in the creatine and placebo groups. Excluding biologic nonresponders did not alter that conclusion, and there was no between-group difference during the short recovery period that followed.6

Hespel et al. reported a similar pattern in a 14-day unilateral casting model. Creatine did not attenuate the decline in quadriceps cross-sectional area or maximal knee-extensor power during immobilization, despite later benefits during rehabilitation.7 The small upper-limb casting study by Johnston et al. remains the main counterexample, with better preservation of lean tissue mass, strength, and endurance in elbow flexion and extension during 1 week of immobilization.8

Viewed together, the disuse literature is mixed but weighted toward no clinically important anti-atrophy effect in controlled lower-limb models.6, 7, 8 This broader interpretation is reinforced by a 2023 systematic review of dietary supplement interventions during muscle disuse. Across 20 randomized trials involving 339 participants, supplements did not preserve muscle strength, cross-sectional area, muscle volume, or aerobic capacity, although some analyses suggested small benefits for lean leg mass.2 Creatine should therefore not be presented as a dependable stand-alone strategy for preventing immobilization-induced muscle loss when mechanical loading is absent.

The negative lower-limb studies are also the studies that most closely resemble common clinical immobilization scenarios in which muscle loss is rapid and loading is absent. That makes them especially influential for interpretation. The Johnston study is still relevant, but its upper-limb model, very small sample, and crossover design make it a weaker basis for broad clinical claims than the larger body of lower-limb evidence.

Evidence during rehabilitation and rehabilitative exercise

The literature becomes more clinically useful once progressive loading begins. At that point, the more important question is whether creatine enhances the response to rehabilitative exercise rather than simply counteracting the effects of inactivity.

Immobilization followed by structured resistance rehabilitation

The clearest human model remains the 14-day cast study followed by 10 weeks of progressive knee-extensor resistance training. In that study, creatine was ineffective during the disuse phase but accelerated recovery of quadriceps cross-sectional area and maximal knee-extension power during rehabilitation.7 The same study reported creatine-associated differences in myogenic-factor protein expression, including a positive relationship between changes in MRF4 expression and changes in muscle-fiber size. Because MRF4 participates in myogenic regulation and muscle remodeling, this observation is clinically relevant as a mechanistic signal that creatine may amplify hypertrophic adaptation when progressive loading is present, rather than merely increasing muscle water or body mass.7

A closely related immobilization model reported altered GLUT4 and glycogen-related responses across disuse and subsequent training, reinforcing that creatine's biologic signal may emerge during reloading rather than during unloading itself.9

Orthopedic and musculoskeletal rehabilitation

Orthopedic evidence is mixed and is currently stronger for nonoperative musculoskeletal rehabilitation than for early postsurgical recovery. After anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction, creatine did not improve strength recovery, power, or single-leg hop performance at 6 or 12 weeks postoperatively, or at 6 months, when added to standard rehabilitation.11 The authors noted that the early postoperative protocol may not have permitted sufficient overload and that a later start, such as 3 months after surgery, could have produced different results. Similarly, creatine did not improve body composition, strength, or functional recovery after total knee arthroplasty (TKA), although outcomes were assessed only 30 days after surgery.12 These null findings should remain central to interpretation, but they also highlight a timing problem: early pain, swelling, restricted weight-bearing, and tissue-healing constraints may limit the high-effort loading through which creatine is most likely to add value.

Knee osteoarthritis may offer a more favorable model, provided that creatine is used in conjunction with strengthening. The addition of creatine to a strength-training program was associated with increased physical function, lower-limb lean mass, and quality of life in postmenopausal women with knee osteoarthritis compared with placebo plus strength training.13 In contrast, creatine without a strengthening program did not improve inflammatory biomarkers, cartilage degradation, Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score subscales, or muscle strength in a 12-week randomized trial of individuals with mild to moderate knee osteoarthritis.14 More recently, Osama et al. reported greater short-term improvements in pain, muscle strength, and physical function when creatine was combined with physical therapy and resistance exercise over 4 weeks.15 Thus, creatine is not established as a conventional adjunct after major orthopedic surgery, but it may have more potential in rehabilitation for musculoskeletal pain conditions when progressive resistance exercise is a central component of care.

The major orthopedic takeaway is that creatine may be largely ineffective during the early acute stages after major injury or surgery, when pain, swelling, limited weight-bearing, and low training intensity restrict the anabolic stimulus. It is more plausible later in recovery, when effortful progressive strengthening is introduced and can be dosed safely.

Chronic disease and neurorehabilitation

Clinical population data are heterogeneous and again reinforce the importance of training context. In rheumatoid arthritis, Wilkinson et al. found increases in appendicular lean mass and intracellular water without corresponding gains in strength or objective physical function.16 This finding is included to distinguish body-composition signals from functional recovery: creatine-related intracellular water can influence fluid-sensitive lean-mass measures, so such outcomes should be interpreted alongside strength and performance rather than treated as direct evidence of meaningful rehabilitation benefit.

There are, however, positive examples when creatine is paired with exercise. In clinically stable patients with polymyositis or dermatomyositis, creatine plus home exercise improved aggregate functional performance and increased PCr-to-β-nucleoside triphosphate ratios on phosphorus-31 magnetic resonance spectroscopy (31P-MRS).17 In Parkinson's disease (PD), creatine combined with resistance training improved upper-body strength and chair-rise performance relative to training plus placebo.18

Lower-level evidence points in the same direction. In a case report involving a man with myasthenia gravis, 15 weeks of resistance exercise combined with 5 g/day creatine was associated with increased training volume, fat-free mass, and knee extensor and flexor strength, without an adverse blood-chemistry signal.19 Because this was an uncontrolled single-case report, the independent contribution of creatine cannot be separated from training and recovery, so the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

More acute and medically complex settings require additional caution. Intensive care unit (ICU)-acquired sarcopenia and myosteatosis provide an important example of clinically meaningful muscle loss after critical illness, where systemic inflammation, immobilization, catabolic stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and immune dysregulation converge to impair recovery.20 However, in hospitalized older adults during the acute phase after stroke, high-dose creatine (10 g twice daily; 20 g/day) given with high protein intake and daily mobility training did not improve functional capacity, strength, or muscle mass during hospitalization or at 90-day follow-up.21 These null findings may reflect the short intervention window, the limited dose of true resistance loading, or competing neurologic and inflammatory constraints.

Many chronic conditions share the downstream problem of sarcopenia, whether associated with aging, frailty, chronic disease, or critical illness. Contemporary reviews conclude that creatine, primarily when combined with resistance training, has favorable effects on muscle accretion, strength, and function in older adults.22 Earlier Society for Sarcopenia, Cachexia, and Wasting Disease recommendations also identified creatine as a nutritional adjunct that may enhance the effects of exercise in sarcopenic patients.23

Cardiopulmonary and vascular rehabilitation

The chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) literature should be interpreted as mixed at the trial level but null at the pooled level. Fuld et al. reported increases in fat-free mass, peripheral muscle strength and endurance, and health status during pulmonary rehabilitation with creatine supplementation, although exercise capacity did not improve.24 Faager et al. did not observe a significant additive effect of creatine on physical performance during training in COPD.25 A later adequately powered randomized trial by Deacon et al. also found no augmentation of pulmonary rehabilitation outcomes despite evidence of muscle creatine uptake.26

The pooled evidence remains cautious. A systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that creatine does not meaningfully improve exercise capacity, muscle strength, or health-related quality of life in patients with COPD receiving pulmonary rehabilitation.27 In symptomatic peripheral arterial disease (PAD), creatine likewise did not improve 6-min walk distance or calf-muscle tissue oxygen saturation.28 These findings suggest that when the primary limitation is cardiopulmonary or vascular, or when rehabilitation is dominated by aerobic rather than high-intensity strength work, creatine's added value may be limited.

This contrast also highlights an important boundary condition. Creatine is most mechanistically plausible when local phosphagen availability is likely to constrain repeated high-force work. It is less likely to overcome limitations driven primarily by ventilatory capacity, gas exchange, peripheral perfusion, or the clinical realities of short inpatient stays.

Exercise-induced muscle damage as a recovery model

Exercise-induced muscle damage is not a disuse model, but it offers a useful recovery analog because it tests whether creatine improves restoration of force and function after a catabolic insult once contractions resume. Yue and Rahimi reported lower creatine kinase as a marker of muscle damage overall and at selected follow-up points, but not a uniformly positive signal across all biomarkers.29 Northeast and Clifford did not quantify acceleration as a single time-to-recovery metric; they evaluated recovery using pooled indirect markers and functional symptoms, including creatine kinase, muscle strength, soreness, and range of motion, after human muscle-damaging exercise protocols such as eccentric/resistance exercise, plyometrics, and running.30 They concluded that creatine supplementation does not clearly accelerate recovery after exercise-induced muscle damage and did not recommend it as a practical recovery aid based on the available evidence.30 A later meta-analysis by Doma et al. separated acute and chronic training responses and found lower indirect muscle-damage markers at 48-90 h after acute damaging exercise, but a reversed pattern after several weeks of training, reinforcing that creatine's recovery effects may be context dependent.31

Trial-level findings were also mixed. Cooke et al. reported enhanced force recovery and lower creatine kinase after eccentric exercise in healthy individuals,32 while McKinnon et al. found no benefit for force loss, soreness, or rate of recovery after elbow-flexor damage with creatine supplementation.33 This literature does not establish efficacy in clinical rehabilitation; at most, it suggests that any benefit may be context-dependent and more likely during repeated contractions and ongoing reloading than during complete unloading.

The exercise-damage literature remains useful conceptually because it tests whether creatine can improve recovery once contraction has resumed after a catabolic insult. That circumstance is closer to the reloading phase of rehabilitation than to complete immobilization and may help explain why positive signals, when they appear, tend to cluster around force recovery rather than true prevention of atrophy.

Discussion

Why timing seems to matter

The mixed literature becomes more coherent when creatine is treated as a conditional adjunct whose effects depend on recovery phase and the quality of the anabolic environment. Rehabilitation dose, timing relative to reloading, and individual responsiveness are especially useful moderators for synthesis.

Across models, creatine's most consistent signal appears when progressive resistance exercise is a central part of care and the training stimulus is strong enough to challenge the phosphagen system.7,13,15,17,18 The cast-and-rehabilitation study that demonstrated accelerated recovery used a heavy knee-extensor program three times per week for 10 weeks.7 Positive osteoarthritis and inflammatory-myopathy studies likewise paired creatine with structured strengthening or home exercise.13,15,17 By contrast, the COPD trials and early stroke study offered less opportunity for creatine to influence repeated high-force work.21,24, 25, 26, 27

Timing also appears central. The lower-limb disuse trials suggest that saturating muscle creatine before and during immobilization does not reliably prevent atrophy,6,7 although the small upper-limb study by Johnston et al. reported preservation of lean mass and performance during short-term casting.8 Orthopedic rehabilitation studies also need designs that align supplementation and outcome assessment with phases in which tissue healing permits patients to impose a sufficient resistance-exercise stimulus. Taken together, the weight of evidence favors a phase-dependent interpretation: creatine is more plausible as a reloading adjunct than as a stand-alone anti-atrophy strategy during strict unloading.

Variation in baseline muscle creatine and in the magnitude of increase after supplementation is a well-described feature of creatine biology.3 In the Backx trial, excluding biologic nonresponders did not change the null result during unloading.6 Even so, future trials should verify exposure where feasible, because a negative study without confirmation of uptake is harder to interpret than a negative study in which muscle creatine clearly rose.

This point matters because a null clinical trial can fail for more than one reason. The supplement may be biologically ineffective, the dose may be inadequate, uptake may be poor, the rehabilitation stimulus may be too weak, or the outcomes may be insensitive to the mechanism being targeted. Future work should be designed to distinguish among these possibilities rather than collapsing them into a single negative result.

Clinical implications, dosing, and safety

Current evidence supports a practical shift in messaging. Creatine should not be marketed to clinicians or patients as a dependable stand-alone strategy for preventing disuse atrophy during strict unloading. Its more plausible role is as an adjunct during reloading, when progressive strengthening and adequate nutrition are re-established.6, 7, 8 This is the context in which the human signal is most favorable.

In outpatient rehabilitation, a pragmatic regimen is either a brief loading phase of about 20 g/day in divided doses for 5 to 7 days, followed by 3 to 5 g/day, or 3 to 5 g/day from the outset when rapid saturation is not essential.3,34 Adequate protein intake and consistent participation in progressive strengthening likely matter more than precise within-day timing.

Current safety data remain reassuring. Reviews focused on the safety and efficacy of creatine in exercise and medicine, on older adults and clinical populations, on common misconceptions, and on common safety concerns do not support a consistent signal of serious harm when creatine monohydrate is used appropriately.3,34, 35, 36 The main clinical caveat is interpretation of kidney function, because creatine intake can raise serum creatinine without necessarily indicating kidney injury.35,36 Caution remains appropriate in people with pre-existing kidney disease or when formal renal monitoring is required.

From a rehabilitation perspective, the supplement's practical advantages are straightforward: it is relatively inexpensive, widely available, easy to dose, and familiar to many patients and clinicians. Those advantages do not establish efficacy, but they lower the implementation barrier in settings where a modest adjunctive effect could still be worthwhile if paired with high-quality exercise therapy.

Research priorities

Several design features would improve the interpretability of the next generation of trials. First, investigators should quantify rehabilitation dose with objective metrics such as sets, repetitions, load, velocity, effort, and attendance. Second, they should confirm biologic exposure through direct measures of muscle creatine or validated proxies when feasible. Third, imaging-based muscle outcomes should be paired with patient-relevant functional endpoints appropriate for the population, such as chair rise, gait speed, return-to-sport criteria, and validated patient-reported outcomes. Fourth, trials should be designed around the transition from immobilization through progressive strengthening phases of rehabilitation rather than the immobilization window alone.

An additional priority is better participant characterization. Trials should report habitual dietary creatine intake where possible, clarify whether participants are older adults, younger healthy volunteers, or clinical patients, and consider stratifying analyses by factors that may influence responsiveness, such as baseline muscle creatine availability, sex, and the amount of supervised strengthening actually completed.

Major orthopedic surgery remains a plausible translational target, but that proposition is still hypothesis-generating rather than established. Recent commentary has argued that creatine could fit within both nutritional prehabilitation and post-operative rehabilitation for at-risk orthopedic patients.37 At present, however, the direct trial evidence in major surgery is sparse and mixed, and the null total knee arthroplasty trial should remain central to interpretation, albeit with its own timing limitations.12

Finally, the field would benefit from rehabilitation-specific designs rather than generic supplementation studies appended to loosely described therapy programs. Creatine is unlikely to rescue an underdosed or poorly specified rehabilitation protocol. It is more realistic to test whether it adds value to a well-defined, progressive program in which adherence and exercise quality are already high.

Conclusions

Current evidence does not support creatine monohydrate supplementation as a dependable stand-alone strategy to prevent skeletal muscle loss during strict unloading. The more plausible clinical role is during reloading, when progressive resistance exercise and adequate nutrition re-establish an anabolic environment. In that context, creatine may increase the effective exercise stimulus and accelerate recovery of muscle size, power, and function in selected populations. Future reviews and trials should keep immobilization and active rehabilitation analytically separate, because the weight of the evidence differs across those phases.
