Cross-Partisan Interactions on Twitter
Yusuf Mücahit Çetinkaya*, Vahid Ghafouri*, Guillermo Suarez-Tangil, Jose Such, Tuğrulcan Elmas

Abstract
Many social media studies argue that social media creates echo chambers where some users only interact with peers of the same political orientation. However, recent studies suggest that a substantial amount of Cross-Partisan Interactions (CPIs) do exist — even within echo chambers, but they may be toxic. There is no consensus about how such interactions occur and when they lead to healthy or toxic dialogue. In this paper, we study a comprehensive Twitter dataset that consists of 3 million tweets from 2020 related to the U.S. context to understand the dynamics behind CPIs. We investigate factors that are more associated with such interactions, including how users engage in CPIs, which topics are more contentious, and what are the stances associated with healthy interactions. We find that CPIs are significantly influenced by the nature of the topics being discussed, with politically charged events acting as strong catalysts. The political discourse and pre-established political views sway how users participate in CPIs, but the direction in which users go is nuanced. While Democrats engage in cross-partisan interactions slightly more frequently, these interactions often involve more negative and nonconstructive stances compared to their intra-party interactions. In contrast, Republicans tend to maintain a more consistent tone across interactions. Although users are more likely to engage in CPIs with popular accounts in general, this is less common among Republicans who often engage in CPIs with accounts with a low number of followers for personal matters. Our study has implications beyond Twitter as identifying topics with low toxicity and high CPI can help highlight potential opportunities for reducing polarization while topics with high toxicity and low CPI may action targeted interventions when moderating harm.

Introduction

Social media is often criticized for creating
echo chambers
, where individuals are primarily exposed to and interact with content that aligns with their beliefs
(
Barberá 2015
)
.
Such spaces reinforce existing views, limit exposure to diverse perspectives, and contribute to polarization.
However, recent studies argue that there is a significant amount of
Cross-Partisan Interactions
, CPIs, engagements among users with different political views, challenging the notion that social media only deepens divides
(
Rajadesingan et al. 2021
)
.

CPIs hold significance on social media as they serve as potential bridges between divided ideological groups, facilitating exposure to diverse perspectives and fostering dialogue among disparate communities.
Meanwhile, there is a debate on whether such interactions lead to healthy dialogue and mitigate polarization, or are toxic and exacerbate it.
Such contrasting opinions suggest that the social media dynamics that affect the presence and quality of CPIs are complex.
Understanding these dynamics is crucial to understanding CPIs and subsequently, how information and differing viewpoints propagate through networks, potentially influencing societal polarization and discourse quality.
This broadened exposure can lead to a more informed populace and potentially temper the polarization that is rampant in online spaces.
Furthermore, understanding CPIs is crucial for platform designers and policymakers who aim to create healthier online environments.

Our main contribution is investigating the dynamics of CPIs by identifying the interplay between actors, topics, and stances that are more associated with CPIs. To do so, we conduct the first large-scale topic-independent quantitative analysis of Cross-Partisan Interactions to the best of our knowledge.
We also study in which cases the CPIs are
healthy
, i.e., non-toxic.
For this, we formulate the following Research Questions (RQs):

RQ1:
What are the characteristics of
users
who typically engage with (un)healthy cross-partisan interactions?

RQ2:
Which
topics
are more prevalent in healthy or unhealthy cross-partisan interactions?

RQ3:
What type of
content
, in terms of sentiment and stance, appears in healthy cross-partisan interactions?

We collect a comprehensive reply dataset and devise a methodology to determine the political alignment of users, and analyze the stance of toxicity of the content (§
3
) to answer these questions (§
4
-
6
). While doing so, we find that popular users are more likely to foster CPIs as they attract replies from users with opposite views. In contrast, the repliers in CPIs tend to have lower number of followers, followings and tweet counts. Certain topics such as politics, economics, and golf has higher rates of CPI in contrast to public health, religion, and daily life. CPIs are correlated with heightened toxicity, which suggests that while these interactions can bridge ideological gaps, they also risk escalating tensions. Furthermore, the study confirms that more polarized users tend to use offensive language. We discuss the findings in (§
7
).

Related Work

Cross-partisan interactions (CPIs) can potentially create bridges in polarized political environments and disrupt echo chambers. In juxtaposition, they may also exacerbate existing divides and increase toxicity online. We now provide a brief survey on these research themes.

Echo Chambers:
Echo chambers are environments where people are exposed only to information and opinions that reinforce their existing beliefs, leading to a lack of exposure to diverse perspectives and the potential for increased polarization. Past studies argue for the existence of echo chambers online by employing user data
(
Garimella et al. 2018
;
Elmas et al. 2021
;
Torres-Lugo, Yang, and Menczer 2022
)
. However, some studies suggest that their prevalence and impact may be overstated
(
Dubois and Blank 2018
;
Guess, Nyhan, and Reifler 2018
)
, A substantial portion of the audience accesses news from outlets with varying ideological perspectives from both online and offline platforms
(
Gentzkow and Shapiro 2011
)
.
Such cross-partisan media consumption indicates that individuals are not wholly isolated within echo chambers but rather encounter diverse viewpoints.

Cross-Partisan Interactions:
The impact of cross-partisan interactions on polarization and dialogue health is a subject of ongoing debate. Past studies have not reached a consensus on whether such interactions bridge divides or exacerbate tensions, e.g.:
Bail et al. 2018
(
Bail et al. 2018
) and
Rajadesingan et al. 2021
(
Rajadesingan et al. 2021
) suggest cross-partisan interaction increases polarization, while works like
Rajadesingan et al. 2021
(
Rajadesingan et al. 2021
) and
Çetinkaya et al. 2024
(
Çetinkaya et al. 2024
) highlight the potential for CPIs to foster understanding and reduce polarization.

Others show that the impact of cross-partisan interactions on polarization is more complex.
Balietti et al. 2021
(
Balietti et al. 2021
) find that exposure to cross-partisan content can decrease polarization if the exposed feel close to the author.
Marchal 2022
(
Marchal 2022
) report that negative cross-partisan interactions increase political polarization, but positive ones mitigate it on Reddit.
Zade et al. 2024
(
Zade et al. 2024
) reveal that the reply feature is often used to re-frame and shift the attention to different dimensions of the debate that were not salient in the original tweet, which may or may not exacerbate tensions.

These studies suggest that the dynamics of cross-partisan interactions are complex, and their impact varies on a case-by-case basis. Our study adopts a comprehensive approach to understanding which actors and topics are more associated with cross-partisan interactions and on which occasions such cross-partisan interactions are more toxic. To the best of our knowledge, such a case-by-case understanding of the likeliness and impact of CPIs is not clearly established.

Perhaps the most similar work to ours, by
An et al. 2024
(
An et al. 2024
), that uses COVID-related Twitter data suggests that Republicans were more likely to initiate cross-party interactions, but such interactions were driven by a small set of users. We extend this work by employing a general dataset that stems from the 1% sample of Twitter instead of limiting it to Covid so that we can analyze which actors and topics are more likely to create cross-partisan interactions extensively. We also propose an unbounded analysis using LLMs to understand the prevalent stances in the data.

Shugars and Beauchamp 2019
(
Shugars and Beauchamp 2019
) employs a dataset of tweets related to Trump and proposes a model that predicts user participation in prolonged conversations. Our problem differs from theirs as they predict if a given user will reply to a conversation thread while we try to predict the factors that are more associated with cross-partisan interactions.

Finally,
Santoro and Broockman 2022
(
Santoro and Broockman 2022
) find that the effectiveness of cross-partisan conversations depends on the topic through a randomized controlled trial. They employed two topics of discussion: a non-political topic discussing the perfect day, and the other discussing their favorite political party. The former mitigates the polarization, while the other has no effect. We complement this work by testing their hypothesis in practice and employing a unique, comprehensive social media analysis that is not limited to only two topics.

Text-mining on Online Interactions:
To analyze user interactions at scale, social media studies employ text-mining methods such as detecting the sentiment, stance, toxicity, or discourse act of the content, employing classification using a predefined set of classes, or assigning a score.

For instance, to analyze the online debate on the Italian 2016 referendum
Lai et al. 2019
(
Lai et al. 2019
) use a semi-supervised approach by manually annotating a corpus of Italian tweet-replies as in “
FAVOR
”, “
AGAINST
”, or neutral (“
NONE
”) to the root tweet, and training a SVM classifier on the labels using
bag-of-words
,
bag-of-hashtags
, and
bag-of-mentions
as features. The labels they annotate by such prediction indicate that users use replies more frequently to express divergent opinions than other communication types.
Matalon et al. 2021
(
Matalon et al. 2021
) employ VADER
(
Hutto and Gilbert 2014
)
to evaluate the sentiment of tweets related to Israel to obtain a sentiment score in the range: [-1, +1]. The results indicate that a root tweet’s sentiment influences the likelihood of receiving a quote that inverts its opinion.

While past studies mainly employ lexicon based
(
Hutto and Gilbert 2014
)
or transformer-based
(
Karande et al. 2021
)
approaches, recent studies suggest that LLMs are capable of text mining tasks such as
stance detection

(
Lan et al. 2024
)
,
sentiment analysis

(
Lee et al. 2024
)
,
hate speech

(
Kaya, Ozcelik, and Toraman 2024
)
, and sociopolitical affiliation of texts
(
Ghafouri et al. 2023
)
.

In our work, we employ two text-mining methods: unbounded content annotation and toxicity detection. We employ the former to analyze the stance and sentiment of the interactions qualitatively. We opt for this method to go beyond predefined sentiment and stance classes and understand nuances in online interactions, which is novel. For toxicity detection, we use the transformers-based Perspective API
(
Lees et al. 2022
)
which provides a toxicity score for a given text between 0 and 1. Prior research identifies a threshold to classify if a given social media post is toxic or not. For instance,
Kumar et al. 2021
(
Kumar et al. 2021
)
survey 17,280 participants to rate toxic content. They then identify a personalized threshold for each participant. They find that the median optimal threshold across participants 0.61.
Similarly,
Aleksandric et al. 2024
(
Aleksandric et al. 2024
)
reports that Perspective API achieves the highest accuracy of 0.95 at a threshold of 0.6 for the “Toxicity” attribute on a Twitter dataset they annotated.

3
Data & Methodology

We define a
cross-partisan interaction
, CPI for short, as a direct interaction between two users of different political orientations. We use Twitter (X) as the platform to study. Twitter features four types of interactions between users: retweets, likes, quotes, and replies. We limited our analysis only to replies, as only replies provide evidence that people intend to engage in a direct reciprocal interaction that can lead to a dialogue
(
An et al. 2024
;
Zade et al. 2024
)
.

To study CPIs, we first collect a dataset of interactions in the form of replies, replied tweets, and root tweets. We then employ political orientation detection to identify partisanship and discover cross-partisan interactions.

We limit our focus to the U.S. context and define partisanship as left-aligned (leaning towards liberals or Democrats) or right-aligned (leaning towards conservatives or Republicans). We limit the data period to 2020 as it captures the general discussions, such as the pandemic (often non-political) and the political discussions related to the 2020 U.S. presidential election. The dataset and related labels used in the analysis are shared on GitHub.
*
*

*

https://github.com/tweetpie/cross-partisan-interactions
.

Replies & Roots:
To have an unbiased sample of replies, we employed the 1% random sample of Twitter provided by the Internet Archive
(
Archive 2024
)
. We recollected the replies and the tweets they replied to in March 2023 using the Twitter API. The dataset comprises 3,029,231 reply tweets in English, responding to 2,299,444 unique tweets. However, on Twitter, tweets can be part of a reply chain. To simplify the analysis, we discard the nested replies and limit the analysis where the replied tweet is not a reply of the original tweet (namely,
root
). This brings the dataset to 1,870,794 direct replies (61.8% of all replies), replying to 1,175,151 root tweets. There are 683,450 unique repliers and 211,584 root authors.
The dataset is further filtered by only keeping the users with non-neutral political orientation, using the methodology that we will describe now.

Political Orientation:
We employ the methodology of
Barberá 2015
(
Barberá 2015
) to measure users’ political orientation. The method uses Bayesian inference on users following data to assign a political orientation score to them, which ranges between -5 and +5. Negative values signify leaning toward Democrats and positive values mean leaning toward Republicans. Our dataset with only direct replies contains 821,791 users. Among these, 57,037 users (7.0%) are not assigned a score due to the absence of the following data and are excluded from the analysis. We see 493,954
(60.1%)
users classified as left-aligned and 232,186
(28.3%)
classified as right-aligned due to having an absolute score above 0.1. There are 38,614 users with a score between -0.1 and 0.1, that are considered neutral and discarded from the analysis.

The direct replies and root tweets from users with unassigned political orientations and from neutral users are discarded from the dataset and are not used in the rest of the analysis. The final dataset comprises 2,561,846 tweets from 726,140 users, including 1,594,271 replies from 596,749 users and 967,575 root tweets from 142,219 users.

CPI Data:
We classify an interaction as a CPI if the replier and the root author are assigned a different political orientation. There are 661,661 replies classified as CPI (34%). Of these, 196,642 are from Republicans replying to Democrats, making up 33.2% of all Republican tweets, and 432,004 are from Democrats replying to Republicans, accounting for 34.3% of all Democrat tweets. While cross-partisan interactions originating from Democrats constitute 65% of all CPIs, this is due to the dataset containing a larger number of users labeled as Democrats.

Toxic Language Classification:
To determine whether the interactions are healthy or toxic, we employ Google Jigsaw’s Perspective API. It is a public tool based on a transformer model that assigns a score which indicates the toxicity of the language within the text
(
Lees et al. 2022
)
. The API outputs scores ranging from 0 to 1, indicating the presence of various forms of hate speech, including “
Toxicity
,” “
Severe Toxicity
,” “
Insult
,” “
Threat
,” “
Profanity
,” and “
Identity
”. We streamline the process for our analysis by assigning each tweet the maximum score across these categories. We define a threshold over the score that determines whether a tweet contains offensive language. We set the threshold at 0.61 which is identified by
Kumar et al. 2021
(
Kumar et al. 2021
) by employing a survey on 17,280 participants and
Aleksandric et al. 2024
(
Aleksandric et al. 2024
) who found that Perspective API gets the highest accuracy on this threshold on their independent test set.

Sentiment & Stance Annotation:
To better understand user interactions, we characterize tweets with annotations describing their sentiment and stance. We use these annotations to perform a comparative content analysis. Due to the large size of our dataset, we resort to automated characterization mechanisms. In particular, we leverage state-of-the-art LLMs. We prompt the root tweets and replies to an LLM and task the model to describe them using three adjectives.
For the replies, the task is to describe the
stance
against the root tweet with three adjectives while we offer both the reply and the root tweet to the LLM. Since root tweets are not usually directed to another tweet, we ask the LLM also to annotate its
sentiment
. This method is an alternative to constraining LLMs by predefined classes and helps us qualitatively analyze the sentiment and stances of the tweets.

Table
2
(in Appendix
A
) provides sample prompts passed to LLM and the completion provided by LLM. Later, in Section
7.2
we discuss the reason and limitations of our choice of prompt engineering and possible future configurations.

The interactions we characterize in our dataset are of four categories: Democrats replying to Democrats (D→D), Republicans replying to Republicans (R→R), Republicans replying to Democrats (R→D), and Democrats replying to Republicans (D→R).
As, for a fair comparison, we intend to have a balanced amount of annotations from each interaction type, we randomly sample 100,000 (the approximate size of the smallest category of interaction) tweet-reply pairs resulting in a total of 400,000 instances.

We employ “
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2
’’
*
*

*

https://huggingface.co/mistralai/Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2
.
This open-source model allows for efficient processing of the large dataset while maintaining adequate language-understanding capabilities. We downloaded the model locally from the Hugging-Face. Each query completion took 5̃ seconds on an NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU. Due to the light size of the model, we parallelized the process into 10 folds and obtain the answers in 1̃0 days.

We validate the annotations by manually inspecting a random sample of 100 AI-annotated root tweet & reply pairs and labeling them as correct or incorrect.
Two annotators independently performed this task
The LLM (
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2
) achieves an accuracy of 97% and 88% (as evaluated by annotator 1 and annotator 2, respectively) for determining the sentiments of root tweets, and 88% and 85% for identifying the stances of replies toward root tweets. The calculated Kappa inter-annotator agreement scores are 0.26 for the sentiments of root tweets, and 0.44 for the stances of replies toward root tweets, which correspond to fair agreement and moderate agreement respectively
(
Landis and Koch 1977
)
. The former score is lower due to high prevalence of data points annotated as true.
Although not classified as incorrect, we observe that LLMs sometimes annotate the sentiment of the reply instead of its stance, e.g., “
happy
” instead of “
happy for them
” or “
supportive
.” We do not correct these annotations and leave them as a limitation.

4
RQ1: User Analysis

This section investigates the characteristics of users who engage in CPIs, addressing RQ1. We first focus on profile attributes to identify the individuals most commonly associated with CPIs. We then adopt a user-specific approach, analyzing each user. We use regression to understand the correlation across features.

4.1
Profile & Tweet Attributes vs. CPI Rates

Certain profile and tweet attributes may be more likely to foster CPIs. Thus, we investigate the prevalence of certain user-based attributes in the promotion of CPIs. In particular, we look at the following profile and tweet attributes:
(1) number of followers, (2) followings, (2) total tweet count, and (3) tweets’ replies, (4) retweets, (5) quote, and (6) like count. We also incorporate the same attributes (1-3) to understand if they have a larger influence than the root user.

Fundamentals of the Analysis:
We use Logistic Regression to compute the coefficients for these attributes to identify their effect, using 20% of the dataset as the test set to measure the models’ performances.
We train three models: one using the entire dataset, one focusing solely on replies from Democrats, and another focusing on replies from Republicans.

The dependent variable
Y
is whether a tweet is part of a cross-partisan conversation (
Y=1
) or not (
Y=0
). The logistic regression model can be expressed as follows:

\text{logit}(P(Y=1))=\log\left(\frac{P(Y=1)}{1-P(Y=1)}\right)=\beta_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}X_{i},

(1)

where:

•

\beta_{0}
is the intercept of the model.

•

\beta_{i}
are the coefficients associated with the
n
predictor variables
X_{i}
.

•

X_{i}
represent the various author-related metrics included in the model.

The logistic regression model
for the entire dataset
is estimated using Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) on a dataset of 1,902,704 observations. Figure
1
displays the distribution of attributes used in the model. The model converges after 6 iterations with a Pseudo R-squared of 0.05413. This indicates that the model explains about 5.4% of the variance with the given predictors in the outcome variable, which is typical for models in complex social or behavioral datasets where many factors are at play. The model’s ROC AUC score is 0.656, which suggests a moderate discriminative ability.
The model that uses Democrats subset achieves a significantly higher Pseudo R-squared value of 0.1598, indicating that the predictors explain about 15.98% of the variance, compared to only 3.48% in the Republican model. Similarly, the ROC AUC score for Democrats is 0.6357, reflecting moderate discriminative ability, while the Republican model’s ROC AUC score is only 0.538.

Key Predictors:
We observe that accounts fostering CPIs by replying have fewer count of followers, following, and tweets as these have negative coefficients. This means that new or small accounts are more likely to engage in CPIs, possibly to attract attention and grow their accounts. Alternatively, people may be using their fake accounts to engage in CPIs. The differences among the two type of repliers are rather small as in Figure
1
. On the other hand, root tweets from users with high follower count are more associated with CPIs from Democrats but less with CPIs from Republicans. Root users’ tweet counts have negative association while their following counts have contrasting but negligible associations with CPIs. We observe that the coefficients for the root’s attributes are higher than the repliers’ attributes. This suggests that the CPIs depend mostly on root users.

Interestingly, the attributes of root tweets have contrasting effects. The reply count of the root tweet has the biggest coefficient by far for the entire dataset. This suggests a rich-get-richer effect, i.e., popular tweets with many replies also attract many CPIs. This findings is even more pronounced for Democrats, but does not generalize to Republicans, which has a negative coefficient for the same attribute. The tweet’s retweet count has a similar association and generalizes to Republicans although the coefficient is low. Quote count is less associated with CPIs for both camps, possibly because users tend to quote tweets without replying to them, thereby reducing CPIs in the form of replies.

Surprisingly, like count has a very high negative coefficient for Democrats and a very high positive coefficient for Republicans. This indicates that Republicans are more inclined to engage in CPIs with highly liked tweets from the opposing camp, whereas Democrats may tend to create discussions on tweets they (and the general public) dislike. Alternatively, the likes may amplify the content in the users’ echo chamber, while retweets may go beyond and create cross-partisan interactions. Note that since the data is from 2020, the disparity between these attributes may have changed due to changes in Twitter’s algorithms.

The differences between the camps underline how cross-partisan engagement is influenced not only by root tweet attributes but also by the alignment of the repliers, reflecting distinct behavioral tendencies and engagement strategies.

We would like to clarify that the attributes of root tweets with negative coefficients (e.g., quote and like count for the entire dataset) fostering CPIs are still higher as seen in Figure
1
, since they are correlated with other attributes, e.g., a tweet with many likes and quotes also receive many quotes and replies. However, these attributes contribute negatively to the model, i.e., likes and quotes may be lower for the tweets with comparable retweet and reply counts in CPIs.

4.2
User-Specific CPI Rates

While user profile attributes partly explain the type of users that are more associated with CPIs, it does not provide the whole picture. For instance, popular users may differ in their likelihood of initiating CPIs. For a more in-depth analysis, we focus on identifying the key predictors among users.

Fundamentals of the Analysis:
We employ Lasso regression to model these users. This method is particularly suited to our dataset, which is characterized by high dimensionality and sparsity, i.e., there are many users but a limited number of interactions among them, which is typical of social media
(
Tibshirani 1996
;
Emmert-Streib and Dehmer 2019
)
.

We prepare a sparse matrix where rows represent interactions, columns represent users, and each entry is a binary indicator denoting whether the interaction is associated with a specific user. The output variable is binary, with a value of 1 indicating the conversation is CPI and -1 indicating non-CPI. We apply a threshold to eliminate noise from users with only a few interactions, considering only those users who acted as a replier or a root more than 10 times. The filtered dataset has 1,702,019 interactions created by 72,923 users. 24,729 of these users were exclusively repliers, 16,407 users were exclusively roots, and 31,787 users interacted both ways.

The Lasso regression model is defined as follows:

\hat{\beta}=\underset{\beta}{\text{argmin}}\left\{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\beta)^{2}+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\beta_{j}|\right\},

(2)

where
y_{i}
is the response variable,
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is the vector of predictors for the
i
-th observation,
\beta
is the vector of coefficients, and
\lambda
is the regularization parameter that controls the degree of shrinkage applied to the coefficients
(
Friedman, Hastie, and Tibshirani 2010
)
. Users with non-zero coefficients are deemed significant contributors to the CPIs. This feature selection process is intrinsic to the Lasso technique and aids in simplifying the model by removing irrelevant predictors.

Key Predictors:

shows the political orientation scores of the authors with their coefficient signatures. There are 26,581 users with negative, 26,649 users with positive, and 19,693 users with zero coefficients.

The coefficients of the users show a Pearson correlation of 0.78 with their CPI versus non-CPI ratios, indicating a strong positive relationship. This suggests that as the coefficient values increase, the likelihood of a user contributing to CPIs also rises proportionally. Our analysis reveals that Democrats tend not to be involved in CPIs, as their bins have more negative coefficient portions compared to Republicans, who are more likely to engage in CPIs. Moreover, the political orientation scores show an inverse relationship with the probability of creating CPIs; users close to the political center have a higher percentage of CPI involvement, which can be interpreted as being in more moderate positions. In contrast, those with more extreme scores on the political spectrum are less likely to be involved in CPIs. We further document this in Figure

CPI Toxicity:
We categorize the replies into bins based on the political orientation of the replier.
For each bin, we calculate the average toxicity score of the replies. Additionally, we determine the percentage of toxic tweets in each bin for interpretability, using a toxicity threshold of 0.61. We removed the bins where the replier has a political orientation score greater than 3.5 as there were only 205 replies in total. We repeat this analysis by averaging the reply toxicity per user, so that each data point in a bin represents a user rather than a tweet. Since the results do not differ significantly, we visualize only the first experiment. Furthermore, we tested the sensitivity of our findings by varying the toxicity threshold from 0.4 to 0.9. This adjustment yielded no substantial change in the behavior of the toxicity distribution.

illustrates the mean reply toxicity scores (bars) and percentage of toxic tweets (bar labels) across different political orientation score bins. The overall trend shows that toxicity levels for CPI replies (red bars) are consistently higher than for non-CPI replies (blue bars) as the political orientation score moves away from the center. As moving toward the center, toxicity scores gradually decrease for both CPI and non-CPI interactions, reaching their lowest around the center, where CPI toxicity drops to 3.1% and non-CPI toxicity to 2.7%. Interestingly, toxicity decreases at the edges of the spectrum. Overall, the toxicity scores have a symmetric behavior with respect to the point zero for both parties.

RQ2: Topic Analysis

To answer RQ2, we investigate the characteristics of the topics that are likely to foster CPIs.
We employ BERTopic
(
Grootendorst 2022
)
pipeline for topic modeling to identify the topics. We first generate dense vector representations of the texts using BERT, a pre-trained transformer model. We then reduce the dimensionality of these embeddings using UMAP to make the clustering more efficient. Finally, we apply HDBSCAN, a clustering algorithm, to group similar embeddings, forming distinct topics. We process “root tweets” to generate embeddings for further analysis.

The topic modeling identifies 177 distinct topics within the dataset. We further categorize these topics to ease comprehension and visualization. We utilize the top 10 keywords provided by
BERTopic
, along with the 10 closest tweets to the centroid of each topic, to create headline summaries and human-readable titles for each topic using ChatGPT-4o. We manually group the topics into 11 overarching
categories
using these summaries to simplify the analysis.
Political topics comprise approximately 35% of these 177 topics, encompassing discussions on governance, civil rights, election campaigns, and policy debates. A notable subset of these, around 10-15%, specifically pertains to election-related discourse, reflecting the temporal and sociopolitical context of the data. 7
Discussion

We discuss the takeaways of our analysis and its limitations.

7.1
Takeaways

Our work provides valuable new insights into online CPIs from a multi-fold perspective, i.e.: user, topic, and content.
In particular, we find common characteristics of users engaging in CPIs, analyzing the discourse in terms of topics, and content; where the content breaks down to the analysis of
sentiments
of the roots,
stances
of the replies, and
toxicity
.
When we put these three dimensions in context, we see how social media exchanges influence societal polarization and discourse quality from a complementary perspective when compared to prior work. In particular, we derive the following novel insights and discuss the implications they have.

The Who:

Those who participate in CPI show
distinct patterns based on their political orientation
.
Users with extreme political views, especially on the far-right, tend to use more toxic language.
Interestingly, the center-right replies with less
toxicity
than the center-left, yet the
extreme toxicity
category is still higher in Republicans.
Although Democrats are proven to be more likely to engage in CPI than Republicans
(
Barberá 2015
)
, their participation often includes more negative and non-constructive stances, such as being “
critical
”, “
accusatory
”, or “
hostile
”.
In contrast, Republicans show less differentiation in stance between interactions within their own group (PI) and cross-partisan replies (CPI),
with instances of even positive CPI stances (“
loving
” and “
congratulatory
”)
, suggesting a more consistent approach to discourse regardless of political affiliation.

Moreover, we detect an association between the CPI rate of a user and their popularity:

Popular users receive more attention, leading to higher CPIs, while smaller accounts mainly engage with the opposite group.
One potential explanation for this could be that since the less followed accounts are viewed less, they may feel more secure to engage in a controversial debate with the opposite party. Alternatively, they may be fake or new & small accounts craving for increasing their impressions and followers.

The What:

CPIs are significantly influenced by the nature of the topics being discussed, with politically charged events acting as strong catalysts.

However, not all topics foster constructive dialogue.
Topics such as gender, race, and discrimination tend to foster less CPI but higher toxicity, indicating more adversarial and polarized exchanges.
Conversely, discussions around financial aid and social welfare demonstrate a healthier balance, with more CPI and lower toxicity, suggesting these areas offer opportunities for more constructive and cooperative cross-party engagement.
Identifying topics with low toxicity and high CPI can help highlight potential opportunities for reducing polarization, while high-toxicity, low-CPI topics represent areas of concern.

Our results show the distribution of CPI and toxicity across different discussion themes, highlighting potential opportunities for reducing polarization and identifying areas of concern.

The How:

To foster a healthy online dialogue, how you talk matters.
Users who talk in a friendly way, receive less toxic or critical replies. For instance, in Figure
9
, “
eager
” and “
positive
” sentiments in the root tweets associate with “
motivating
” and “
eager
” stances in the replies and correlate negatively with “
dismissive
” stance. On the other hand, negative sentiments in root tweets are more likely to provoke toxic (see Figure
10
) or negative stance in the replies (e.g. “
dismissive
” or “
critical
”). Interestingly, this phenomenon is independent of whether the interaction is a CPI or a PI as we observed a 95% correlation for the values in Figure
9
.

Understanding this dynamic is paramount to reducing the widespread polarization in online spaces and disrupting the idea that toxicity is inherent to certain online communities.

7.2
Limitations

Continuous CPIs
: The CPIs between users close to the political center, and the CPIs between extremes may be inherently different. We initially experimented with a continuous CPI value by multiplying the political orientation score of the poster and the replier. However, we did not notice a drastic change in the results of our initial experiments. We plan to address a deeper analysis as part of our future work.

Reply chain and media
: To simplify the analysis, we limit it to root tweets and their direct replies, and discard the replies of replies.
Our content analysis is limited to the textual content present in the tweets. The analysis of the links, images, and videos in the tweets falls beyond our scope.

LLM’s annotation
: We used a heuristic approach with Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 for stance/sentiment annotation. While effective overall, some replies were misclassified due to overlap between stance and sentiment.
Without setting a limit for the number of adjectives, the LLM generated sentences rather than words as in Table
2
. Moreover, in some cases, the LLM did not differentiate the concept of
stance
and
sentiment
when asked to annotate the
stances
of the replies (e.g. the word “loving” in Figure
8
is more of a
sentiment
than a
stance
).
However, we find such confusions to be rare, and we observe that they do not hinder the objective of our analysis.
This is because understanding any salient semantic quality in the content of replies is insightful for us; whether it is
stance
or
sentiment
.
Experiments with other LLMs and configurations are left to the future work.

Platform
:
Our study focuses on X (formerly Twitter), which has its own unique dynamics. These dynamics may influence the interaction patterns and content characteristics observed in our analysis. Expanding this research to other social media platforms, such as Reddit, YouTube, Instagram, and others, would provide a broader understanding of cross-platform user behavior and interactions. Such platforms offer different interaction paradigms, media-sharing mechanisms, and community structures that could yield additional insights and validate the generalizability of our findings.

Context
:
The data collection period was chosen to be 2020 as this year has seen massive debates both on political (riots, elections) and non-political debates (the pandemic). However, the presence of a Republican government in the U.S. may have influenced the results, which could differ under a Democrat government. Additionally, the results may have differed during a period with less politically charged events, such as in 2021. Extending the study over a longer period could help capture evolving patterns. We acknowledge these limitations and leave extending the study to a longer period for future work due to current restrictions with collecting Twitter data. Finally, as the dataset was limited to the U.S. context, the findings may not be a global context.

8
Ethical Impact

In this work, we collect and analyze the posts and replies among users and their profile attributes. We collect this data using Twitter’s Academic API before May 2023. We mitigate the potential ethical risks of analyzing user data by limiting our focus to public accounts and analyzing aggregate data. That is, we do not examine and report individual profiles. To further protect user privacy, we anonymize the user data before sharing our dataset. Our regression models do not use any personally identifiable information. Our study aims to bridge divides among the public by understanding the dynamics of non-toxic cross-partisan interactions. It should not be misused to create or amplify content that would provoke toxic content within echo chambers or manipulate the public discourse in any other way.
