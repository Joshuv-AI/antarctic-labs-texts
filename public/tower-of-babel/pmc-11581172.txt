Exploring the potential of Fargesin from Chrysanthemum indicum for chronic migraine: in-silico and pharmacokinetic study
Authors: Rapuru Rushendran, Vellapandian Chitra
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic migraine is recognized by the WHO as one of the most debilitating chronic conditions. It is primarily caused by central sensitization of the trigeminal nucleus caudalis. Key biomarkers associated with migraine include NFkB, IL-1β, CGRP, and iNOS. While CGRP antagonists have proven effective in treating migraines, Chrysanthemum indicum L., a traditional herbal remedy, has not been established as a treatment.MethodsTo address this, we investigated whether Fargesin, a lignan found in CI, could potentially reduce migraines by targeting these biomarkers. We conducted pharmacokinetic, toxicological, and in-silico studies. Molecular docking studies revealed a strong interaction between Fargesin and CGRP/iNOS.ResultsThe pharmacokinetic analysis indicated robust intestinal absorption and effective penetration of the blood-brain barrier. In-silico toxicity assessments showed favorable results. Promising interactions with drug targets were observed in bioactivity tests.ConclusionFargesin from Chrysanthemum indicum may hold potential as a therapeutic agent for migraines.

Introduction

Chronic migraine (CM) is a severe and common form of headache that is diagnosed when the patient experiences a headache with pulsating pain on at least 15 days per month on one or both sides of the head [1]. The World Health Organization has recognized it as one of the four most deadly chronic dysfunctional diseases. Central sensitization, characterized by a sustained elevate in the excitability of neurons in the trigeminal nucleus caudalis in response to pain stimuli, is now largely regarded as one of the primary pathogenic mechanisms of CM. The biomarkers Nuclear factor kappa beta-NFkB, Calcitonin gene related peptide-CGRP, Interleukin IL-1β, and Nitric oxide synthase-iNOS have all been linked to migraine attacks. Migraine pain is caused by the transmission of nociceptive impulses from the peripheral nervous system to the central nervous system, which is facilitated by circulating substances in the blood due to a compromised blood-brain barrier [2,3]. The primary symptoms of migraine can be alleviated, and the sickness can be managed, but there is no cure. CGRP antagonists like Erenumab, Eptinezumab, Galcanezumab, Fremanezumab, Atogepant, and Zavegepant are typically prescribed first to lessen the severity of pain and other symptoms; Non steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and other pain relievers are available to prevent migraine attacks, but having severe cardiac adverse effects mean that few people take them [4–6]. Chrysanthemum indicum L. (CI), commonly referred to as Queen of Eastern Ghats, one of numerous herbal plants that have not yet been clinically proven for migraines but are being used as an alternative remedy and the herbal plants have high antioxidant activity [7–9] which gives alternative therapy to reduce the free radicals formed during diseased condition. Tanacetum parthenium L. is an herb that has been traditionally used to treat headaches, including migraines. It is available in supplement form, and some studies suggest that it may help reduce the frequency and severity of migraines in some people [10]. Butterbur is another herb that has been studied for its potential benefits in migraine treatment. It may help reduce the frequency of migraines when taken regularly, although it’s essential to choose a product that is labeled PA-free, as certain compounds in butterbur can be harmful [11]. Ginger has anti-inflammatory properties and may help relieve migraine symptoms. It can be consumed fresh, as a tea, or in supplement form [12]. Magnesium is a mineral found in many plant foods, such as nuts, seeds, and leafy green vegetables. Some research suggests that magnesium supplements may help prevent migraines, particularly in people with low magnesium levels [13]. Riboflavin, or vitamin B2, is found in foods like almonds, spinach, and mushrooms. Some studies have shown that riboflavin supplements may reduce the frequency and severity of migraines in some individuals [14]. While caffeine is not a plant itself, it is found in various plant-based products like coffee, tea, and dark chocolate. In some cases, consuming caffeine in moderate amounts may help relieve migraine symptoms, although excessive caffeine intake can also trigger migraines in some people [15]. Certain essential oils, such as peppermint oil and lavender oil, may provide relief from migraine symptoms when applied topically or inhaled. However, it’s essential to dilute essential oils properly and use them cautiously, as they can be potent and may cause skin irritation in some individuals [16,17]. It’s important to note that while these plant-based treatments may offer relief for some individuals, they may not work for everyone, and it’s essential to consult with a healthcare professional before starting any new treatment regimen, especially if you have underlying health conditions or are taking medication. Additionally, incorporating lifestyle changes such as managing stress, getting regular exercise, and maintaining a healthy diet can also help reduce the frequency and severity of migraines. Traditional Chinese medicine has relied on CI, a perennial plant belongs to Asteraceae family, for over two thousand years to treat conditions like pemphigus, swelling, pain [18], and scrofula. Flavonoids, lignans, terpenoids, and phenylpropanoids are just a few of the more than 190 chemical components of this plant that have been extracted and characterized [19]. Specifically, a decoction of CI leaves is believed to alleviate headaches in tribal communities [20], However, it has not yet been proven scientifically. Various literature found that one of the lignan compounds “Fargesin” is a good antihypertensive [21] and anti-inflammatory [22,23]. Based on this, Fargesin has been studied as a potential biomarker for the treatment of antimigraine symptoms by targeting CGRP [24], iNOS [25], NFkB [26], IL-1β [27], TNFα [28], NOD-like receptor-3-NLRP3 [27,29,30], and Matrix metalloproteinase MMP-9 [31].

The objective of this research is that we screened and anticipated potential target molecules with their signal pathways for Chrysanthemum indicum active component Fargesin in migraine treatment through In-silico, pharmacokinetic, and toxicity evaluation. To treat migraine sufferers safely and effectively, we’re hoping that a natural remedy will prove effective.

Materials and methods

Molecular docking analysis

Protein Data Bank was mined for the crystal structure, such as 7KNT, 1M8D, 2Z65, 1NFI, 1S9K, 4YR8, and 5R8E (http://www.rcsb.org/). The PDB structure was refined by eliminating the inhibitor ligand and all other chains. The proteins used in this study were designed with the help of MMV and AutoDock. A second improvement round was performed on the modified protein by stabilizing the ionized and tautomeric states of the amino acid residues, which required the removal of water molecules and the addition of hydrogen atoms. A PDB file was created to keep the updated protein structure accessible for docking research. The proteins in this study were optimized with the use of the Molegro molecular viewer. The bond order was modified when water and covalently attached ligands were removed. After charge and protonation states were assigned, the energy was decreased using the molecular mechanics force field. The AutoDock programme automatically calculates Gasteiger charges and evaluates the ligand’s rotatable bonds to create several conformers for docking. The receptor grids were constructed from the points of each designated receptor, and the grid boxes were formed with the axes of the receptor grids as the domain. The macromolecule was centrally located within the checkerboard. The many sorts of maps were made using Autogrid 4. All molecular docking simulations were run using the Lamarckian genetic method. The docking process used the following settings: 50 iterations, 150 subjects, 2.5 million assessments, and 27,000 generations. Biovia Discovery Studio 2021 was used to bring in the docking snap photos, and the resulting docked structures were exported to pdbqt. Biovia Discovery Studio 2021 was used to visualize the docking results, which showed hydrogen and hydrophobic contacts at the inhibitor sites of docked targets [3,32–34].

Pharmacokinetic assessment

Using information from the PubChem server, the standard grins of Fargesin are revealed. Pharmacokinetic studies were finished utilizing pkCSM and Swiss ADME. The ADMET profile is retrieved from the host computer using canonical smiles. Both the pkCSM and the Swiss ADME provide information on the drug’s pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD), and toxicology (Toxicity). pkCSM, a web-based app, helps investigate drugs’ pharmacokinetic properties. Fargesin was analyzed for its physicochemical properties using the pkCSM software [35,36]. Based on their binding affinities and pharmacokinetic features, the best-docked compounds were identified, indicating their potential as therapeutic options. However, while employing the right strategies in drug design, development, and discovery endeavors, it is critical to incorporate a number of important pharmacokinetic aspects or ADMET qualities (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity). In order to learn about the physicochemical properties and ADMET elements of the substance under evaluation, it was necessary to do comprehensive online web-based tool study. The Fargesin was analyzed using Lipinski’s rule of five, which identifies five expected physicochemical features that have a major impact on a molecule’s efficacy, safety, or metabolism. LogP violations were found across all three Consensus LogPo/w implementations tested (WLOGP, XLOGP3, and LOGP). Using the atomistic and topological implementations of Moriguchi’s topological approach, XLOGP3, WLOGP, and MLOGP, the logarithm of the partition coefficient between n-octanol and water was determined for lipophilicity values between -0.7 and +5.0.

Toxicological modeling and simulation

To prevent complications during drug withdrawal, such as organ system failure or damage, toxicological testing is essential. The OSIRIS property explorer software was consulted with PubChem structures to evaluate the compounds’ toxicity levels. Substances are ranked on a color scale according to their potential to cause cancer, mutate, irritate, impact reproduction, and act as a potential drug. An overall drug score, drug-likeness score, and TPSA were calculated based on the following toxicity criteria: high risk represented in red, medium risk represented in yellow, and low risk represented in green [37].

Analysis of Fargesin’s biological activity

Pass (Prediction of action Spectra for Substances) predicted the medicines’ biological action. The drug’s biological activity spectrum is expected based on its structural formula. The correlation between structure and behavior is the sole basis for the analysis. The ability of a chemical compound to have pharmacological or biological effects is essential since it opens up potential uses for the compound in medicine. The possible adverse effects of the drugs were also anticipated with the help of the PASS ONLINE tool. Biologically active chemicals are analyzed using the web-based PASS program. The software can forecast over 300 different pharmacological activities and biological pathways. The structural formula of the natural material forms the basis for the prediction. New pharmaceutical targets could be found with the help of this information [38,39].

Molecular property analysis

In-silico testing of the selected drugs was performed using MOLINSPIRATION software to evaluate drug similarity and predict bioactivity. As the score increases, so does the possibility that the molecule in dispute will be active. A chemical with a bioactivity score higher than 0.00 is considered to have significant biological activities. Scoring between -0.50 and 0.00 on the bioactivity scale is highly active, while values below -0.50 are inactive [40].

Discussion

Studies on the chemical Fargesin, which shows potential as a treatment for migraines, are particularly instructive. Fargesin was subjected to molecular docking simulations with several target genes associated with migraine, including CGRP [5,41], iNOS [25], TLR4 [42], NFKBIA [26], c-Jun/Fos [43], MAPK8 [44,45], and IL-1β [46,47]. The binding energies obtained from these simulations revealed that Fargesin exhibited the highest binding energy with CGRP (-7.35 kcal/mol) and iNOS (-9.3 kcal/mol), suggesting strong interactions with these key migraine-related proteins was illustrated in Figure 2. Moreover Fargesin shown good binding affinity compare to the standard Zavegepant CGRP antagonist, this revealed that later Fargesin will play a key role in migraine therapy. Notably, Fargesin formed hydrogen bonds with specific amino acid residues in these targets, which further supports the accuracy of the docking predictions. The docking results indicate that Fargesin has the potential to directly interact with CGRP and iNOS, which are crucial players in migraine pathophysiology. However, it’s important to note that molecular docking is a computational approach, and further empirical experiments are needed to validate these predictions. Assessing the pharmacokinetic properties of Fargesin is essential for understanding its potential as a drug candidate. The results indicate that Fargesin has favorable pharmacokinetic properties; Fargesin is moderately absorbed and distributed throughout the body. Its high human intestinal absorption rate (96.662%) is noteworthy, suggesting efficient absorption in the digestive system. Furthermore, Fargesin exhibits enhanced CNS penetration potential, crucial for migraine treatment. Fargesin’s distribution characteristics indicate its potential to reach target tissues effectively. Fargesin is both a substrate and an inhibitor of various cytochrome P450 enzymes, including CYP3A4. This information is valuable for understanding its metabolic profile. The calculated total clearance of Fargesin is low, suggesting a longer half-life in the body. In-silico toxicity predictions indicate that Fargesin has a clean bill of health, with no mutagenic, carcinogenic, irritant, or reproductive effects. This favorable toxicity profile enhances its safety as a potential therapeutic agent. The bioactivity score assessment using Molinspiration indicates that Fargesin exhibits a good bioactive score for a range of important drug targets, suggesting its potential efficacy in targeting these receptors and pathways associated with migraine. The accuracy of extrapolating computational findings to in vivo scenarios depends on the fidelity of the model to biological reality and the validity of the underlying assumptions. Limitations

In-silico simulations may not fully capture the complexities of real biological systems. Experimental validation is needed to confirm the predicted binding affinities and elucidate the actual mechanism of action. The pharmacokinetic assessment relies on computational tools like pkCSM and Swiss ADME, which provide estimations based on structural and physicochemical properties. However, these predictions might not always correlate perfectly with actual pharmacokinetic behavior in humans due to factors such as metabolism, protein binding, and tissue distribution, which can vary widely among individuals. In-silico toxicological predictions offer valuable insights into potential adverse effects, they are based on computational algorithms and may not capture all possible toxicological outcomes accurately. Experimental studies, including in vitro and in vivo assessments, are necessary to validate the safety profile of Fargesin comprehensively. Perhaps the most significant limitation is the absence of clinical validation. While the study lays a solid foundation for further investigation, including preclinical studies, clinical trials are ultimately needed to assess the safety and efficacy of Fargesin in human migraine sufferers.

Conclusion

Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) can directly measure the binding affinity and kinetics of Fargesin with migraine-related proteins in real-time. By immobilizing one of the interacting partners on a sensor chip and flowing the other partner over it, you can observe binding events. Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC) measures the heat released or absorbed during a biomolecular interaction, providing information on binding affinity, stoichiometry, and thermodynamics of the interaction between Fargesin and migraine-related proteins. Fluorescence Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET) can be used to study the proximity and conformational changes between Fargesin and migraine-related proteins. By labeling the proteins with suitable fluorophores, you can monitor changes in fluorescence intensity upon binding. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) can confirm the physical interaction between Fargesin and migraine-related proteins in cells or tissues. By immunoprecipitating one protein and detecting the presence of the other in the immunoprecipitate, you can validate their interaction. X-ray Crystallography and NMR Spectroscopy: These techniques can provide atomic-level structural information about the complex formed between Fargesin and migraine-related proteins, confirming the binding mode and identifying key interacting residues. Cellular Assays: Functional assays such as cell-based reporter assays or assays measuring downstream signaling pathways affected by Fargesin binding to migraine-related proteins can confirm the biological relevance of the observed interactions. The actual dosage, formulation, and side effects of Fargesin would need to be determined through rigorous scientific investigation in accordance with regulatory guidelines. The nanoformulation can target the specific pain receptor that might be assist migraineurs for the fast relief from the migraine attack. Further preclinical studies are needed to better understand the mechanism of action of Fargesin in alleviating migraine symptoms. By conducting the comprehensive toxicological studies to evaluate the safety profile of Fargesin and ensure its suitability for further development as a migraine treatment. This could involve investigating its interactions with specific receptors or signaling pathways implicated in migraine pathophysiology. Studies to elucidate the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of Fargesin, including its ADME would provide valuable insights into its bioavailability and potential dosing regimens. Additionally, exploring the effects of Fargesin in various animal models of migraine, including models of different migraine subtypes or comorbidities, could help assess its efficacy across a range of conditions. Optimization of the formulation of Fargesin is essential to enhance its stability, solubility, and bioavailability, thereby improving its therapeutic efficacy. This could involve formulation strategies such as nanoparticle encapsulation, prodrug design, or novel delivery systems to enhance its targeted delivery to the central nervous system. Formulation studies should also consider factors such as route of administration, dosing frequency, and potential drug interactions to maximize patient compliance and minimize adverse effects. Conducting well-designed clinical trials is crucial to establish the efficacy and safety of Fargesin as a migraine treatment in human subjects. Phase I trials would focus on evaluating its safety profile, pharmacokinetics, and optimal dosing regimen in healthy volunteers. Phase II trials would assess the efficacy of Fargesin in reducing migraine frequency, severity, and duration in patients with migraine. These trials may include randomized, placebo-controlled studies with appropriate endpoints, such as reduction in migraine days or improvement in quality of life. Phase III trials would aim to confirm the efficacy of Fargesin in larger patient populations and compare its effectiveness to existing migraine treatments. These trials would provide valuable data on its long-term safety and tolerability. Given the multifactorial nature of migraine, exploring combination therapies involving Fargesin and other migraine medications or therapies could offer synergistic benefits in improving treatment outcomes. This could include combination with existing acute or preventive migraine medications, non-pharmacological interventions such as behavioral therapies or neuromodulation techniques, or lifestyle modifications. Long-term safety and efficacy monitoring of Fargesin in real-world clinical settings are essential to assess its durability of effect, potential for tolerance or dependence, and occurrence of rare or long-term adverse effects. Post-marketing surveillance studies would complement pre-approval clinical trials in providing comprehensive data on its safety profile. Our findings were added to the growing bodies of evidence that the molecular docking analysis and pharmacokinetic profiling of Fargesin provide preliminary solid evidence of its potential as a therapeutic agent for migraine and further investigation in drug development. Its BBB penetrance and P-glycoprotein II inhibition are advantageous pharmacological features that may contribute to its therapeutic success. Its potential as a medication candidate is enhanced because it has no PAINS warnings. Further experimental studies and clinical trials are necessary to confirm its therapeutic potential and safety in the context of migraine treatment.
