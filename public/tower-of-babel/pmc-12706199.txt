Background

Palliative care aims to improve the quality of life of patients and their families facing life-threatening illness. This includes not only the relief of pain and other distressing symptoms, but also support with psychosocial and spiritual problems.1 Occupational therapy, a profession that focuses on promoting health and well-being through activity, plays a crucial role in the holistic care of these patients.2

Occupational therapists help individuals to maintain as much independence as possible in everyday life despite illness, injury or disability. In palliative care, occupational therapists can provide specific interventions to help patients maintain their independence for as long as possible. These interventions promote participation in social and physical activities, enhancing quality of life. This can be done by adapting the environment, advising on the provision of assistive devices or developing personalised strategies for pain management and activity adaptation.3

Although the aims of occupational therapy and the needs of palliative care appear to overlap, practice shows that occupational therapy services are often underused in palliative settings.4,13 This may be partly due to the fact that occupational therapists’ training does not always include specific modules or courses on palliative care, which can lead to uncertainty among professionals about how best to apply their skills in this area.14

In some countries, the need for greater integration of palliative care into occupational therapy education has been recognised. In Australia, for example, palliative care is regarded as an essential component of occupational therapy training, and specific modules are offered to address the needs of patients in palliative settings.15 Similarly, studies in Canada have indicated that occupational therapy students often feel unprepared for the challenges of palliative care. This can result in a lack of confidence and practical competence.12 Research by Meredith in Australia and New Zealand showed that many programmes included only 2–10 lessons on this topic, leaving new graduates feeling overwhelmed.16 Although these studies are more than a decade old, more recent analyses confirm that limited curricular progress has been made and that the integration of palliative care in occupational therapy education remains insufficient.17

The motivation for this study was to gain a deeper understanding of whether and how occupational therapy trainees and students are prepared to work in palliative care settings. Specifically, the study aimed to explore the perceived importance of palliative care among learners in Germany and to assess the extent to which palliative care is represented in occupational therapy curricula, including levels of knowledge, practical experience and attitudes. By identifying gaps in education and highlighting areas for improvement, this study seeks to contribute to the development of more comprehensive curricular integration. Strengthening the role of palliative care in occupational therapy education is essential to ensure that future practitioners are not only motivated but also adequately equipped to make a meaningful contribution to palliative care practice, thereby enhancing the quality of life for individuals with life-limiting conditions.12

Objective

This study aims to gain a comprehensive understanding of how occupational therapy trainees and students can be prepared for their future roles in palliative care. Several specific aims have been identified:

1. Assessment of the level of training: To identify the integration of palliative care into occupational therapy educational programmes.

2. Assessment of knowledge and skills: To analyse trainees’ and students’ knowledge and skills in palliative care.

3. Analysis of practical experiences: Examination of trainees’ and students’ experiences with palliative care clients.

3. Identification of training needs: To identify needs and desires for palliative care educational content.

4. Promotion of curriculum development: To develop recommendations for improved curriculum integration of palliative care into occupational therapy training to ensure that students are optimally prepared for their future tasks.

Methods

Study design

A quantitative cross-sectional survey design was employed to examine the integration of palliative care content among occupational therapy trainees and students in Germany. A quantitative approach was chosen to enable a systematic analysis of a large group of participants and to generate insights that can be generalised to current educational practices.

Recruitment

Participants were recruited from occupational therapy training centres and universities across Germany. The objective of this study was to create a broad database based on the voluntary participation of the respondents. Eligible participants were individuals who were currently enrolled in an occupational therapy education programme and had provided informed consent. No further exclusion criteria were applied.

Data were collected using a structured online questionnaire developed by the study team in collaboration with occupational therapists and palliative care specialists following intensive literature research on the topic. Data collection

Data was collected with an online survey via the survey platform surveymonkey.com among occupational therapy trainees and students in Germany. A nationwide invitation was distributed by email to vocational schools (n=180) and universities (n=25) offering occupational therapy education programmes, asking them to forward the survey link to their trainees and students. Participation was further encouraged through repeated reminders (26 February 2024 and 24 April 2024) and announcements on the websites of the German Federal Association of Occupational Therapists (27 March 2024) and the German Occupational Therapy Association (25 April 2024). To prevent multiple participation in the web-based survey, the SurveyMonkey setting ‘Multiple Responses’ was disabled, ensuring that each participant could complete the survey only once.

The survey was open for 14 weeks, closing on 1 June 2024. The sample size was not formally calculated because this study was exploratory, aiming to provide an initial overview of trainees’ and students’ perceptions of palliative care education. The number of participants was determined pragmatically based on their availability and willingness to participate during the data collection period. Quantitative responses were analysed descriptively, and free-text comments were categorised thematically prior to analysis. Data analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel V.16.100.1.

Ethics and data protection

The study was registered in the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS00033464). Participation in the survey was voluntary, and participants were informed about the study’s purpose and data handling procedures on the first page of the online questionnaire. Completion of the survey was considered as implied consent. Data were collected anonymously, and no personal identifiers (eg, IP addresses) were stored, ensuring that participants could not be traced. Data protection regulations were followed in accordance with German law and the European Union General Data Protection Regulation. A separate data protection concept was developed and reviewed by the university’s data protection officer. All data were handled confidentially and stored securely by the research team.

Patient involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting of this study.

Expected benefits and insights from the study

The objective of the survey is to gain insight into the importance of palliative care in the training of occupational therapists and to gather information about the extent to which palliative care is integrated into training. The knowledge gained can contribute to the further development of training and ensure that future occupational therapists are not only willing but also able to make a significant contribution to palliative care, thereby improving the quality of life of those affected.

Results

Recording the level of education

A total of 451 participants completed the survey, corresponding to a response rate of 3.76% of the estimated 12 000 occupational therapy trainees and students in Germany at the time.19 20 Most participants (89.4%) identified themselves as female, 9.3% as male and 1.3% as non-binary. The majority of participants (91.3%) were in vocational training at the time of the survey, while 8.7% were students. The distribution of years in training showed that 36.9% of respondents were in their first year of training, 32.6% in their second year and 27.9% in their third year (see table 1).

Assessment of knowledge and skills

The survey results showed that 69.2% of participants stated that they knew what palliative medicine or palliative care is, while 24.4% were unsure. The majority (92.6%) were familiar with the concept of a hospice. However, only 31.9% of respondents had received training in palliative care as part of their education or studies. On average, only eight lessons of 45 min each were taught on palliative care topics, indicating a potential gap in the curricular integration of this subject area.

Analysis of practical experience

Practical experience with palliative care clients is limited. Only 23.9% of respondents had worked with this patient group, with most experience gained in nursing homes (52.5%). These results suggest that there is still a lack of opportunities to gain practical experience in palliative care in many training institutions.

Identification of training needs

However, among the 31.9% of respondents who had received palliative care training during their education, satisfaction with the teaching content was overwhelmingly positive: 84.2% stated that they found the instruction helpful for their professional development. Furthermore, a resounding 95.1% of all participants expressed a desire to learn more about the role of occupational therapy in palliative care. This strong level of interest underscores the importance of integrating palliative care as a core component of occupational therapy education.

Personal attitudes and preparation

The results of the survey showed that 22.3% of respondents were very interested in palliative care and 50.5% were somewhat interested. In addition, most respondents (79.9%) agreed to the statement that palliative care should be established as an integral part of training.

Regarding the emotional stress of working with seriously ill or dying people, 17.7% of respondents reported no stress, 45.5% reported stress and almost as many (36.8%) were neutral.

A total of 243 participants stated that they felt ‘not at all prepared’ (23.9%) and ‘not prepared’ (38.6%) to accompany dying people. Only 39 trainees (10.0%) said that they felt ‘well prepared’ and only 1 person (0.3%) stated that they felt ‘very well prepared’. There were similar results in relation to giving bad news, with 250 participants stating that they felt ‘not at all prepared’ (25.4%) and ‘not prepared’ (38.8%).

Implementing pain management at the end of life was perceived as a challenge by many respondents, with 127 participants (32.6%) stating that they felt ‘not prepared at all’. For the management of dyspnoea at the end of life, 150 participants (38.6%) reported feeling ‘not prepared at all’. When it came to dealing with the emotional needs of patients, 147 respondents (37.8%) felt neutral, while 114 participants (29.3%) said that they were ‘well prepared’. Regarding decision-making, particularly with regard to stopping treatment, 80 participants (20.6%) stated that they felt ‘not at all prepared’, while 114 (29.3%) were neutral. Supporting relatives during the grieving phase was also perceived as a challenge, with 82 participants (21.1%) stating that they felt ‘not prepared at all’. When dealing with religious issues related to dying, 85 participants (21.9%) reported feeling ‘not prepared at all’. The psychological needs of patients were rated as neutral by 138 participants (35.5%), while 93 (23.9%) reported feeling ‘well prepared’. Existence of and willingness to draw up an own advance directive

When asked if they had an advance directive, only 62 participants (16.2%) responded in the affirmative. The majority of this group had drawn up their advance directive more than 6 months ago (84.1%). The remaining respondents (n=322) stated that they had not yet considered drawing up an advance directive (56.8%). 37 respondents stated that they were not ready to draw up an advance directive at all, while 102 were considering drawing up an advance directive in the next 30 days (9.3%) or 6 months (22.4%).

Discussion

The present findings provide valuable insights into the status and prospects of integrating palliative care content into occupational therapy education. They highlight that despite basic knowledge and high levels of interest among students and trainees, there are still significant gaps in curriculum implementation. In the following, the key findings are placed in the context of the current literature with the intention of deriving recommendations for a future educational design.

Demographic findings and their relevance for training

The demographics of the study participants show that the target group for occupational therapy education is predominantly female, and that most study participants were under the age of 25. These findings are consistent with the study by Reeves et al, which indicates that young women in healthcare professions are particularly receptive to psychosocial topics such as palliative care.21 This suggests that training programmes need to be designed to address both the professional and personal development needs of this target group.

Discrepancy between knowledge and practice: a known challenge

With 69.2% of respondents stating that they were aware of palliative care, the topic already has some presence in occupational therapy education. This is an encouraging sign that the importance of palliative care is being recognised in occupational therapy practice. However, the discrepancy with the only 31.9% of respondents who reported receiving specific teaching content on this topic suggests a gap in curriculum implementation.12 Although trainees and students demonstrate basic knowledge of the subject, the results of this study indicate that they perceive the absence of structured modules and practical learning opportunities as a considerable gap in their education. This finding is consistent with the scoping review by Talbot-Coulombe and Guay, which highlights the importance of distinguishing between palliative care and end-of-life care within professional training. While palliative care focuses on enhancing quality of life and supporting participation throughout the trajectory of a life-limiting illness, end-of-life care addresses the unique needs and challenges associated with the final stage of life and dying. Therefore, comprehensive and advanced education is required to equip occupational therapists with the conceptual understanding, emotional competence and practical skills necessary for meaningful engagement in both domains.12

Similar results are found in previous studies, conducted by our research team, which show that greater integration of palliative care into occupational therapy education not only increases trainees’ specialist knowledge, but also their ability to develop practical solutions to the complex needs of palliative care patients.10 13

In contrast, knowledge of hospice is remarkably high at 92.6%, suggesting that although basic knowledge of palliative care concepts is widespread, targeted integration into occupational therapy education is still insufficient. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that palliative care content is under-represented in many healthcare professions.4,13

Practical experience: a central building block for skills development

Only 23.9% of participants reported having practical experience with palliative care clients. This lack of practical experience is concerning as practical experience plays a central role in developing skills and building confidence in occupational therapy.12 22 Interestingly, most of this experience was gained in retirement or nursing homes (52.5%)—settings not typically recognised within a palliative context. This aligns with research showing that many healthcare professionals are often unaware of the extent to which they already provide general palliative care in their daily work and therefore identify little with the concept. Palliative care is still frequently associated primarily with cancer and end-of-life situations, while its relevance to non-malignant chronic diseases, multimorbidity and high symptom burden remains underappreciated.23 24 This limited understanding underscores the importance of broadening educational approaches in occupational therapy to include the wider scope of palliative care beyond specialised oncology settings. Training institutions should therefore strengthen curricula by combining theoretical instruction with practical learning opportunities—such as placements in specialised facilities or classroom simulations. Recent evidence by Farah and El Hachem25 confirms that simulation-based learning, particularly in emotionally demanding contexts such as palliative care, can significantly enhance students’ competence and self-efficacy.25

Advance directives and need for education

Only 16.2% of respondents reported having an advance directive, which is consistent with Wurm et al, who found that only a small proportion of younger adults have such documents in place.26 Most of these advance directives had been completed more than 6 months ago, while over half of the remaining participants had not yet considered drawing one up. Notably, 22.4% intended to complete an advance directive within the next 6 months, highlighting an openness to early engagement with advance care planning.26

Similar patterns have been observed in other healthcare professions: training experience is strongly associated with higher readiness, perceived relevance and confidence in discussing advance care planning.27 Structured education in advance directives and communication skills has been shown to improve awareness and preparedness among medical and nursing students,28 29 while targeted teaching also enhances attitudes and practical competence in ethically sensitive decision-making.30

For occupational therapy trainees and students, early engagement with advance directives is particularly important, as palliative care often involves supporting not only patients but also their relatives in end-of-life decision-making.27,30 Integrating advance directives into training may therefore strengthen professional competence, ethical reflection and confidence in client-centred communication, preparing students to respond effectively to complex palliative care situations.30

Satisfaction with previous course content and the desire for in-depth study

The high level of satisfaction with palliative care educational content to date (84.2%) is a positive finding. This shows that the limited content available is perceived as relevant and helpful. At the same time, there is a strong desire for more in-depth learning content, as 95.1% of respondents would like to learn more about occupational therapy in palliative care. This finding highlights the urgent need for greater integration of palliative care into curricula. This is consistent with several studies.4 5 7 11 12 31 Greater integration could be achieved through the introduction of dedicated modules, interdisciplinary teaching approaches and the use of guest lectures by palliative care experts. International examples, like the guidelines of the World Federation of Occupational Therapists, show that such approaches are successful in combining theory and practice.32 In Germany, the basic curriculum ‘Palliative Care for Therapeutic Professionals’ of the German Society for Palliative Medicine (DGP) offers further training tailored to occupational therapists.33 Programmes in Australia and the USA rely on practical formats such as simulations and case studies to strengthen professional knowledge and skills for palliative care.6 7 These approaches could also help to standardise training formats in other countries through international networking and adaptation to local contexts.

Studies from different countries show that such interventions significantly improve the palliative care skills of occupational therapy students.34,40 In Canada, for example, a curriculum has been developed that includes modules on palliative care that specifically prepare students for the role of occupational therapy in pain control, activity adaptation and quality of life promotion. These modules include both theoretical knowledge and practice-based approaches such as case studies and simulations.12 In Australia, occupational therapy students have worked in interdisciplinary teams to develop their skills in working with other health professions in palliative care, which has strengthened their interprofessional practice. For example, the German textbook by Berge et al (2018) emphasises that the introduction of interdisciplinary teaching formats into occupational therapy education is essential for a more comprehensive preparation for palliative care.14 This would not only allow trainees and students to develop their skills in adapting activities and aids, but also to learn how they can contribute to improving the quality of life of palliative care patients within interdisciplinary teams.14 Another example from the UK shows that by integrating modules on ethical decision-making and dealing with death, the emotional resilience of trainee occupational therapists is strengthened and that they are better prepared for the psychological stresses of palliative care.41

Assessments on working with palliative clients

The survey found that 44.6% of respondents found working with palliative care clients enriching, while 43.5% found it stressful. These ambivalent feelings reflect the emotional challenge of working in palliative care. Despite this, 65.9% of respondents said they would like to work with palliative patients. This shows a high level of commitment and interest, but also highlights the need for better preparation for the specific demands on the field, particularly in areas such as pain management and managing emotional needs.12 The work of Ferrell et al highlights that comprehensive training programmes can increase not only competence but also the emotional resilience of participants.42

Implications for palliative care

The enhancement of occupational therapy training in palliative care has been demonstrated to exert a significant impact on both the quality of training and the quality of life experienced by patients receiving palliative care. Research has demonstrated that the provision of holistic care by an interdisciplinary team, comprising occupational therapists, can result in a substantial enhancement in patients’ quality of life.43 The integration of palliative care into educational curricula has the potential to enhance the quality of care for terminally ill people.

Limitations

There are several limitations to consider when interpreting the results of this study. The sample of 451 participants, recruited through occupational therapy training institutions and universities in Germany, could lead to bias and may not be representative of all occupational therapy trainees and students. Data collection via a structured online questionnaire is based on self-reporting, which carries the risk of socially desirable responses.

Furthermore, qualitative insights into the experiences and needs of the trainees are lacking, as the study is limited to quantitative analysis and does not fully consider influencing factors such as teaching methods or teacher qualifications. In addition, the temporal nature of the data collection may have influenced participants’ perceptions, as these may change over the course of the training.

The gender distribution of the sample (89.4% female) indicates a possible bias that may limit the generalisability of the results. Finally, the focus of the study is on knowledge and experience of palliative care, without gaining deeper insights into the attitudes or emotions of the participants, which would have been helpful for a more comprehensive understanding.

Conclusion

The results of this study show that there is a high level of interest and acceptance for the integration of palliative care into occupational therapy education. While some of the participants already had some basic knowledge of palliative care, there was a clear desire for more in-depth teaching and practical experience. The findings therefore highlight the need to include palliative care as an integral part of the curriculum, with the intention of strengthening the skills of future occupational therapists and improving palliative care in the long term. These findings provide a solid foundation for the development of specific curricular recommendations for action in this key subject area.
