Recursive perturbation approach to time-convolutionless master equations: Explicit construction of generalized Lindblad generators for arbitrary open systems
Alessandra Colla, Heinz-Peter Breuer, Giulio Gasbarri

Abstract
We develop a recursive perturbative expansion for the time-convolutionless (TCL) generator of an open quantum system in a generalized Lindblad form. This formulation provides a systematic approach to derive the generator at arbitrary order while preserving a Lindblad-like structure, without imposing assumptions on the system or environment beyond an initially uncorrelated state. The generator is written, at all orders, in a canonical form, which also corresponds to the minimal dissipation condition, which uniquely specifies the decomposition of the generator into Hamiltonian and dissipative contributions. To validate the method and show its effectiveness in addressing non-Markovian dynamics and strong-coupling effects, we compute the generator explicitly up to fourth order.



I
Introduction

The theory of open quantum systems provides a foundational framework for describing quantum dynamics in realistic environments, where system-environment interactions lead to energy and information exchange, often resulting in decoherence and irreversible behavior
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
;
Vacchini 2024
.

A widely used framework for describing such dynamics is the Lindblad master equation, which can be derived either phenomenologically or from a microscopic system-environment model. This formalism assumes Markovian dynamics, which holds under weak coupling and a clear separation of timescales between the system and the environment. However, in the presence of strong coupling or structured spectral densities, memory effects become significant, and the Lindblad equation no longer provides an accurate description.

Time-convolutionless (TCL) master equations provide a robust framework for modeling non-Markovian dynamics in open quantum systems by utilizing time-local generators that account for environmental back-action. However, their analytical derivation is generally limited to a few solvable models, typically involving Gaussian environments or linear system-bath interactions. Notable examples include the Jaynes-Cummings model
Smirne and Vacchini 2010
, pure dephasing models
Łuczka 1990
;
Doll et al. 2008
, and Gaussian reservoirs
Diósi and Ferialdi 2014
;
Ferialdi 2016
, as well as paradigmatic models such as quantum Brownian motion
Hu et al. 1992
and the Fano-Anderson model
Huang and Zhang 2022
;
Picatoste et al. 2024
;
Colla and Breuer 2024
.

In complex scenarios such as systems interacting with non-Gaussian reservoirs or governed by nonlinear couplings, the time-convolutionless (TCL) generator is typically derived through perturbative expansions. Traditional methods – including projection operator techniques, stochastic methods
Shibata et al. 1977
;
Chaturvedi and Shibata 1979
;
Strunz and Yu 2004
, and other analytical/numerical
de Vega and Alonso 2017
strategies – often grow intractable at higher orders due to the rapidly increasing complexity of nested time integrals and environment correlation functions.

To address this issue, a recursive perturbative expansion was developed in
Gasbarri and Ferialdi 2018
, systematically constructing the TCL generator using commutators and anticommutators of system operators and bath correlation functions. This method recursively yields higher-order terms from lower-order contributions, avoiding redundant calculations and enabling a diagrammatic representation of perturbative terms. By explicitly incorporating multi-time correlation functions, the approach handles non-Gaussian effects and nonlinear couplings, significantly improving the tractability of non-Markovian dynamics in open quantum systems. However, convergence of the perturbative series depends on system parameters, and singularities in the exact TCL generator can arise in specific regimes
Vacchini and Breuer 2010
.

In this work, we extend this framework by developing a recursive perturbative expansion of the TCL generator expressed in terms of left- and right-acting system operators. This representation clarifies the structural decomposition of the generator, enabling the identification of its canonical form, which consists of a coherent (Hamiltonian) component and a dissipative component that has the structure of a Lindblad dissipator but with time-dependent and potentially negative rates
Gorini et al. 1976
;
Breuer 2012
;
Hall et al. 2014
. The emergence of negative rates is a signature of non-CP-divisibility, indicating that the evolution cannot be represented as a sequence of completely positive propagators, a signature of potential non-Markovian dynamics
Rivas et al. 2014
;
Breuer et al. 2016
.

While the decomposition into coherent and dissipative components is generally not unique
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
, uniqueness can be enforced by requiring the Lindblad operators to be traceless – a minimal dissipation condition that ensures the dissipator is minimal (as a superoperator) with respect to a Hilbert-Schmidt-averaged norm
Sorce and Hayden 2022
. Although this criterion is not strictly necessary, it yields a well-defined canonical decomposition, resolving ambiguities inherent to non-Markovian regimes.

The coherent part of the TCL master equation in this canonical form – and, in particular, the effective system Hamiltonian generating it – has received significant attention, as it describes the renormalization of the system’s energy levels due to interaction with the environment.
While in the Markovian case this contribution corresponds to the well-known Lamb shift
Bethe 1947
;
Lamb and Retherford 1947
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
, in the non-Markovian and strong-coupling regime the renormalization is generally time-dependent and can be much more pronounced. Indeed, recent experimental results have confirmed that a two-level system interacting with a single mode can exhibit energy splitting renormalization of up to 15%, with a time-dependent profile consistent with the predictions of the effective Hamiltonian obtained from the coherent part of the system’s TCL master equation
Colla et al. 2025
. Furthermore, the effective Hamiltonian has been proposed as a key operator in the generalization of the laws of quantum thermodynamics to the strong-coupling regime
Colla and Breuer 2022
;
Picatoste et al. 2024
;
Colla and Breuer 2024
.

In summary, we present a recursive perturbative expansion for the TCL generator,
formulated in terms of left- and right-acting operators, and use this approach to obtain a systematic
expression for the generator in a generalized Lindblad form.
Our formulation does not impose constraints on the system-bath structure beyond an initially uncorrelated state, making it broadly applicable to a wide range of non-Markovian quantum systems.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: Sec.
II
details the recursive perturbative expansion of the TCL master equation in terms of left-right operators. Sec.
III
introduces the canonical decomposition of the generator into its coherent and dissipative components, with a focus on traceless Lindblad operators and the role of minimal dissipation. Finally, we apply this method in Sec.
IV
to present an explicit expansion up to fourth order.

II
Recursive perturbation expansion of the generator

II.1
Assumptions

We consider a general bipartite quantum system, where one part of the bipartition corresponds to the system of interest
S
and the other to its environment
E
. To describe the effective dynamics of
S
, we trace out the degrees of freedom of
E
, obtaining the reduced evolution of the system.

We assume that the total Hamiltonian governing the evolution of the combined system is given by

\displaystyle H_{t}=H_{S,t}+H_{E,t}+\lambda A^{\mathcal{S}}_{t}\otimes B^{\mathcal{S}}_{t}\;,

(1)

where
H_{S,t}
and
H_{E,t}
describe the intrinsic dynamics of the system and the environment, respectively. The interaction between them is characterized by the coupling parameter
\lambda
, with
A^{S}_{t}
and
B^{S}_{t}
being time-dependent operators acting on the Hilbert spaces of the system and the environment, respectively.

We assume the interaction term to be in a tensor product form and that
A^{\dagger}=A
,
B^{\dagger}=B
for simplicity and clarity.
However, the calculations can be easily extended to a more general interaction of the form
\lambda\sum_{i}A_{i,t}\otimes B_{i,t}
. The subscript
t
indicates the explicit time dependence of all terms in
H_{t}
, which allows us to describe bipartite systems that are not isolated but still evolve unitarily. The superscript
\mathcal{S}
indicates that the operator is written in the Schrödinger picture.

The total initial state is assumed to be uncorrelated, i.e.
{\rho}_{SE}(0)=\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}
.
If initial correlations are present, one may define the correlation matrix

\chi:={\rho}_{SE}(0)-\mathrm{Tr}_{S}\{{\rho}_{SE}(0)\}\otimes\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\{{\rho}_{SE}(0)\}
and treat
\chi
as a known contribution to the initial conditions for the irrelevant degrees of freedom.
This approach yields an affine, yet linearizable, dynamical map for the reduced system
Stelmachovic and Buzek 2001
;
Colla et al. 2022
.

We move into the interaction picture by evolving the bipartite system under the free local evolution given by
H_{S}
and
H_{E}
. We denote the interaction picture Hamiltonian of system and environment by
\tilde{H}_{t}=\lambda A_{t}\otimes B_{t}
and the interaction picture density matrix by
\tilde{\rho}_{SE}(t)
, so that the total evolution is given by
\dot{\tilde{\rho}}_{SE}(t)=-i[\tilde{H}_{t},\tilde{\rho}_{SE}(t)]
. Under these assumptions, the total unitary evolution of the bipartite system can be described by the following expression

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{t}[{\rho}_{SE}(0)]=\mathcal{T}\left(e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}d\tau(\tilde{H}_{\tau}^{L}-\tilde{H}_{\tau}^{R})}\right)\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}\;,

(2)

where
\mathcal{T}
represents time-ordering and the superscripts
L
and
R
denote left-acting and right-acting (super)operators respectively, namely:

\displaystyle X^{L}[\rho]

\displaystyle=X\rho,\;\hskip 28.45274ptX^{R}[\rho]=\rho X\;,

(3)

where both
X
and
\rho
are operators.

II.2
Expansion of the reduced dynamical map

The dynamical map describing the evolution of the reduced system is obtained by tracing out the environmental degrees of freedom:

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}[\rho_{S}]=\mathrm{Tr}_{E}{\mathcal{U}_{t}[\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}]}.

(4)

To expand this map in powers of the coupling strength
\lambda
, we express the unitary evolution operator as a Taylor series, yielding

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-i\lambda)^{n}\mu_{n},

(5)

where each term
\mu_{n}
is time-dependent (we suppress the explicit subscript
t
for clarity) and given by

\displaystyle\mu_{n}[\rho_{S}]=\frac{1}{n!}\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\left\{\mathcal{T}\left(\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\left(\tilde{H}_{\tau}^{L}-\tilde{H}_{\tau}^{R}\right)\right)^{n}\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}\right\}\;.

(6)

We can now make the dependency on the system and bath operators
A
and
B
explicit. We make use of the binomial theorem, and recognize that left- and right- acting operators can be separately time ordered, as they commute at all times, to obtain

\displaystyle\mu_{n}[\rho_{S}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{n-k}}{(n-k)!k!}\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\left\{\mathcal{T}\left(\int_{0}^{t}d\tau A_{\tau}^{L}B_{\tau}^{L}\right)^{k}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\left.\mathcal{T}\left(\int_{0}^{t}d\tau A_{\tau}^{R}B_{\tau}^{R}\right)^{(n-k)}\hskip-14.22636pt\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}\right\}\;.

(7)

The idea is now to separate the action of
A
and
B
on
\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{E}
in order to easily perform the partial trace with respect to the environment. We however have to keep track of the ordered time-dependencies. We thus define for left- and right-acting superoperators the notation

\displaystyle{A^{L/R}}(\bm{t}_{1}^{k})[\,\cdot\,]:=

\displaystyle A^{L/R}_{t_{1}}\circ{A^{L/R}}_{t_{2}}\circ...\circ{A^{L/R}}_{t_{k}}[\,\cdot\,]

(8)

\displaystyle{A^{L/R}}^{\dagger}(\bm{t}_{1}^{k})[\,\cdot\,]:=

\displaystyle A^{L/R}_{t_{k}}\circ{A^{L/R}}_{t_{k-1}}\circ...\circ{A^{L/R}}_{t_{1}}[\,\cdot\,]

(9)

and analogously for products of operators

\displaystyle{A}(\bm{t}_{1}^{k})

\displaystyle:=A_{t_{1}}\cdot{A}_{t_{2}}\cdot...\cdot{A}_{t_{k}}

(10)

\displaystyle{A}^{\dagger}(\bm{t}_{1}^{k})

\displaystyle:=A_{t_{k}}\cdot{A}_{t_{k-1}}\cdot...\cdot{A}_{t_{1}}\;.

(11)

With this notation we can rewrite each contribution
\mu_{n}
, eq. (
II.2
), as a sum of terms

\displaystyle\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mu_{n}^{k}

(12)

where each contribution is given by

\displaystyle\mu_{n}^{k}=(-1)^{n-k}\int_{0}^{t}d\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}d\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}

\displaystyle A^{L}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k})A^{R}(\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})\times

\displaystyle\times D(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})\;,

(13)

which contains a product of
k
left-acting operator times
n-k
right-acting operators.
For the sake of readability, we have introduced a few more notation conventions. The first is the integral notation, which is made to mean the following:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}d\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}d\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\;(...)\;=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}d\tau_{1}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau_{2}\;...\int_{0}^{t}d\tau_{k}\times

(14)

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{t}ds_{1}\int_{0}^{t}ds_{2}\;...\int_{0}^{t}ds_{n-k}\;(...)\;.

Then, we have defined the coefficients
D
, which include the environmental correlation functions (at various orders) as well as the time-ordering. Explicitly, they read

\displaystyle D(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{E}\left\{B^{\mathrm{R}}(\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})\circ B^{\mathrm{L}}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k})[\rho_{E}]\right\}\theta_{\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}}\theta_{\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}}\;,

(15)

where the
\theta_{\bm{\tau}_{1}^{n}}
-functions will time-order the set
\{t_{j}\}_{j}
so that
t_{1}>t_{2}>...>t_{n}
. Notice that the function
D
satisfies

\displaystyle D^{*}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})=D(\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k},\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k})\;.

(16)

II.3
Expansion of the time-local generator

The exact time-local generator for the reduced system is given by composing the derivative of the dynamical map with its inverse
Hall et al. 2014
;
Breuer 2012
, i.e.
\mathcal{L}_{t}=\dot{\Phi}_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}^{-1}
.
If
\lVert\Phi_{t}-\text{id}\rVert<1

1
1

1

here
\lVert\cdot\rVert
must be intended as the operator norm
, then
\mathcal{L}_{t}
can be then written as a combination of the terms
\mu_{n}^{k}
and their time derivatives. Those can in turn be rearranged, in order to collect different terms according to the exponent of the coupling coefficient
\lambda
. This procedure was already performed in Ref.
Gasbarri and Ferialdi 2018
for an analogous expansion. Following the steps within, one obtains the expansion of the generator in terms of
\lambda
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}\mathcal{L}_{n}\;,

(17)

where again we have dropped the subscript
t
from the
n
-th order term of the generator, which is however still time-dependent and is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}

\displaystyle=(-i)^{n}\sum_{q=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{q}\hskip-20.00003pt\sum_{(m_{0}+...+m_{q}=n)}\sum_{k_{0}=0}^{m_{0}}\sum_{k_{q}=0}^{m_{q}}\dot{\mu}_{m_{0}}^{k_{0}}\mu_{m_{1}}^{k_{1}}...\mu_{m_{q}}^{k_{q}}

\displaystyle=(-i)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{q=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{q}\hskip-20.00003pt\sum_{(m_{0}+...+m_{q}=n)}\sum_{(k_{0}+\dots+k_{q}=k)}\hskip-15.00002pt\dot{\mu}_{m_{0}}^{k_{0}}\mu_{m_{1}}^{k_{1}}...\mu_{m_{q}}^{k_{q}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{k}\;.

(18)

In the above,
\dot{\mu}_{m}^{k}
represents the first time derivative of
{\mu}_{m}^{k}
, which can be expressed as

\displaystyle\dot{\mu}_{n}^{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}d\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}d\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}A^{L}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k})A^{R}(\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}){\dot{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})}\;,

(19)

with the time derivative of the bath correlation functions given by

\displaystyle\dot{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}):=D(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})\big(\delta_{\tau_{1},t}+\delta_{s_{1},t}\big)\;,

(20)

since
\tau_{1}
and
s_{1}
are the only largest times in each term due to time-ordering. Notice that in the above we have assumed the following convention for each
\delta
-function:
\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\delta_{\tau,t}f(\tau)=f(t)
for any function
f
.

In the last line of eq. (
II.3
), each term
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{k}
describes the
n
-th element of the series that contains a string of
k

\mu_{m_{i}}^{k_{i}}
with their explicit formula and relabelling in increasing order the time integrals. Collecting all coefficients together, one then obtains the expression for each term
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{k}
in terms of left and right-acting operators, namely

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}^{k}=i^{n}(-)^{k}\int_{0}^{t}d\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}d\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\mathcal{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})A^{L}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k})A^{R}(\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})\;.

(21)

In the above, we have defined the environmental generalized cumulants, which read:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})

\displaystyle\hskip 2.84544pt=\sum_{q=1}^{n}(-)^{q+1}\hskip-25.6073pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq k_{1}\leq...\leq k_{q}=k\\
0\leq m_{1}\leq...\leq m_{q}=n-k\end{subarray}}\hskip-28.45274pt\dot{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k_{1}},\bm{s}_{1}^{m_{1}})\prod_{j=1}^{q-1}D(\bm{\tau}_{1+k_{j}}^{k_{j+1}},\bm{s}_{1+m_{j}}^{m_{j+1}})\;.

(22)

Equation (
II.3
) is general for all terms and quite cumbersome. However, several terms
D
appearing in it will often not be present in practice, as we have assumed the following rule of notation:

D(\tau_{a_{1}}^{b_{1}},s_{a_{2}}^{b_{2}})=\begin{cases}D(\tau_{a_{1}}^{b_{1}},s_{a_{2}}^{b_{2}})\quad&\text{for }b_{1}\geq a_{1}\land b_{2}\geq a_{2}\\
D(\tau_{a_{1}}^{b_{1}})&\text{for }b_{1}\geq a_{1}\land b_{2}<a_{2}\\
D(s_{a_{2}}^{b_{2}})&\text{for }b_{1}<a_{1}\land b_{2}\geq a_{2}\\
0&\text{for }b_{1}<a_{1}\land b_{2}<a_{2}\end{cases}\;

(23)

which can also be summarized as the following:

\displaystyle D(\tau_{a_{1}}^{b_{1}},s_{a_{2}}^{b_{2}})=

\displaystyle D(\tau_{a_{1}}^{b_{1}},s_{a_{2}}^{b_{2}})\theta_{a_{1},b_{1}}\theta_{a_{2},b_{2}}

\displaystyle+D(\tau_{a_{1}}^{b_{1}})\theta_{a_{1},b_{1}}(1-\theta_{a_{2},b_{2}})

\displaystyle+D(s_{a_{2}}^{b_{2}})(1-\theta_{a_{1},b_{1}})\theta_{a_{2},b_{2}}\;,

(24)

where
\theta_{a,b}
is a discrete theta function (with convention
\theta_{a,b}=1
for
a=b
).

Keep in mind that the times in the vectors
\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}
and
\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}
appearing in eq. (
II.3
) are not actually ordered in a particular direction, as the

are responsible for the time ordering of all operators and functions in the integrals. We have named them generalized cumulants as they share formal similarities to the ordered cumulants of van Kampen
Van Kampen 1974a
;
Van Kampen 1974b
.

Each term in equation (
II.3
) can be obtained recursively if all the terms of lower
n
are known. We can see this by rearranging eq. (
II.3
) in order to highlight lower order contributions (see Appendix
V
). This gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})=

\displaystyle\dot{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})

\displaystyle-\sum_{l=0}^{k}\sum_{r=0}^{n-k}\mathcal{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{l},\bm{s}_{1}^{r}){D}(\bm{\tau}_{l+1}^{k},\bm{s}_{r+1}^{n-k})\;,

(25)

where, analogously as before, some terms may drop out due to the rule of notation (
II.3
).

As we will see later in Sec.

, the recursive expression for the cumulants is particularly helpful to compute contributions at higher orders, especially whenever the system is such that some lower order cumulants simplify or vanish entirely.
Combining eqs.(
II.3
), (

) and (

), we find that

-th order contribution to the generator of the dynamics acts as the following on any operator:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}[X]=(i)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-)^{k}\int_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle d\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}d\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\mathcal{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})\times

\displaystyle\times A(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k})XA^{\dagger}(\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})\;.

(26)

The term described by this expression is analogous to eq. (16) in
Gasbarri and Ferialdi 2018
, but here expressed in terms of left- and right-acting operators.
This representation, unlike the expression found in
Gasbarri and Ferialdi 2018
, allows the series to be rewritten in a canonical form, which enables the distinction between the coherent and incoherent contributions to the dynamics, as we will discuss in the next section.

III
Canonical representation of the Time-Local Generator

The time-local generator governing the reduced dynamics of an open quantum system can always be expressed in the canonical form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[X]=

\displaystyle-i[K,X]

\displaystyle+\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}\left(L_{i}XL_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{i},X\right\}\right)\;.

(27)

Here, all contributions are explicitly time-dependent, and the coefficient matrix
\gamma_{ij}
is Hermitian. The structure of this generator ensures that the dynamics preserve Hermiticity and trace but do not necessarily guarantee complete positivity
Hall et al. 2014
;
Breuer 2012
. If
\gamma_{ij}
remains positive semi-definite at all times, then the evolution is CP-divisible, corresponding to a Markovian process. However, when some eigenvalues of
\gamma_{ij}
become negative, the dynamics exhibit non-CP-divisibility, a possible signature of non-Markovianity
Rivas et al. 2014
;
Breuer et al. 2016
.

The coherent part of the generator, determined by the effective Hamiltonian
K
, governs unitary-like evolution. Importantly,
K
differs from the bare system Hamiltonian
H_{S}
due to renormalization effects induced by system-environment interactions. The dissipative part describes irreversible evolution of the system state and is characterized by the Lindblad operators
L_{i}
and the time-dependent rates
\gamma_{ij}
.

The possibility to recast the perturbation series described by (
26
) into the general form of eq. (
27
) facilitates a physical interpretation of its individual components, enabling a systematic distinction between coherent and dissipative contributions. However, this decomposition is inherently non-unique, introducing an additional layer of complexity.

III.1
Ambiguity of the effective Hamiltonian and the principle of minimal dissipation

It is well known that in a standard Lindblad master equations, the Hamiltonian term can include not only the Lamb shift but also additional contributions arising from invariance transformations
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
.

For a generalized time-local generator in eq. (
27
), the following transformations preserve the overall structure but modify the explicit representation of the coherent and dissipative dynamics:

\displaystyle L_{i}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow L_{i}-\alpha_{i}\mathbb{1}\;,

(28)

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle\longrightarrow K+\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}\left(\alpha_{i}L_{j}^{\dagger}-\alpha_{j}^{*}L_{i}\right)\;.

(29)

These transformations show that the effective Hamiltonian
K
can acquire additional terms that depend explicitly on the Lindblad operators
L_{i}
and dissipation rates
\gamma_{ij}(t)
.

Moreover, any purely Hamiltonian contribution to
K
(involving only system degrees of freedom) can always be rewritten in a dissipator-like form
Chruściński 2022
;
Sorce and Hayden 2022
, further emphasizing the inherent ambiguity in defining
K
.
While the generator
\mathcal{L}
uniquely determines the system’s dynamics, the specific division between coherent and dissipative contributions remains representation-dependent.

Given this ambiguity, an additional constraint is needed to define a physically meaningful decomposition of the generator in terms a coherent and dissipative component. One approach is the principle of minimal dissipation, which minimizes the dissipative contribution of
\mathcal{L}
using a norm that averages over random input and output states
Sorce and Hayden 2022
;
Colla and Breuer 2022
.
It has been shown that this criterion is equivalent to writing the dissipator using traceless Lindblad operators, thereby recovering the standard Lamb-shifted Hamiltonian in the CP semigroup case. By enforcing this condition, one obtains a well-defined representation of the generator.

In the next section, we reformulate the microscopic generator derived in Sec.
II
into a generalized Lindblad form satisfying the minimal dissipation principle.

III.2
Perturbative structure of the generator

The generator defined via the perturbative expansion in eq. (
17
), eq.(
26
) and eq. (
II.3
) exhibits the following general structure

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[X]=\sum_{ij}\omega_{ij}V_{i}^{L}(V_{j}^{\dagger})^{R}[X]=\sum_{ij}\omega_{ij}V_{i}XV_{j}^{\dagger}\;,

(30)

where
\omega_{ij}^{*}=\omega_{ji}
(see eq. (
16
)) and where the set of operators
\{V_{i}\}
may be overcomplete or linearly dependent.
Without loss of generality, we label
V_{0}=\mathbb{1}
.

We recall that the TCL generator satisfies the conditions of Hermiticity preservation and trace annihilation, i.e.

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\omega^{*}_{ij}(V_{j})^{L}(V_{i}^{{\dagger}})^{R}=\sum_{ij}\omega_{ij}V_{i}^{L}(V_{j}^{{\dagger}})^{R},

(31)

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\omega_{ij}V_{j}^{{\dagger}}V_{i}=0\;.

(32)

From trace annihilation, it is not difficult to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\neq 0}\omega_{ij}V_{j}^{{\dagger}}V_{i}=-\sum_{i\neq 0}(\omega_{i0}V_{i}+\omega_{0i}V_{i}^{{\dagger}})\;.

(33)

One may notice the resemblance of this equation to the unitarity condition commonly used in scattering theory to derive the optical theorem
Reed and Simon 1979
;
Taylor 2012
. Similarly, in collisional dynamics, such conditions are employed to derive Lindblad-like master equations when interactions are described via a scattering operator
Diosi 1995
;
Hornberger and Sipe 2003
;
Hornberger and Vacchini 2008
.
Moreover, using the identity

\displaystyle\omega_{i0}V_{i}X+\omega_{0i}XV^{\dagger}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\{\omega_{i0}V_{i}+\omega_{0i}V^{\dagger}_{i},X\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}[\omega_{i0}V_{i}-\omega_{0i}V^{\dagger}_{i},X]\;,

(34)

along with eq. (
33
), one can rewrite the generator in a generalized Lindblad form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[X]=

\displaystyle-i\sum_{i}\left[\frac{1}{2i}(\omega_{0i}V^{\dagger}_{i}-\omega_{i0}V_{i}),X\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{ij\neq 0}\omega_{ij}\left(V_{i}XV_{j}^{{\dagger}}-\frac{1}{2}\{V_{j}^{\dagger}V_{i},X\}\right).

(35)

This result generalizes the work of Gorini et al.
Gorini et al. 1976
by extending it to non-Markovian dynamics and allowing for arbitrary sets of operators
V_{i}
.
However, in the general case, the Hamiltonian-like contribution is guaranteed to be Hermitian only if the condition
\omega^{*}_{0i}=\omega_{i0}
is satisfied.
This condition ensures that the effective Hamiltonian remains a valid observable and prevents the introduction of spurious non-Hermitian terms. Notably, this symmetry property holds naturally within the perturbative expansion considered in this work (see eq. (
16
)).
However, it is clear that the choice of the operator set influences the form of the Hamiltonian contribution.

Inspired by scattering theory, where the transition operator is taken to be traceless, or in a different manner by minimal dissipation principle (see discussion above), we impose the further constraint of traceless Lindblad operators.
This is achieved by applying the transformation

\displaystyle V_{i}\to\overline{V}_{i}\equiv V_{i}-\braket{V_{i}}_{1/d}

(36)

where
\braket{V_{i}}_{1/d}
referes to the average over the maximally mixed state
\mathbb{1}/d
. This transformation, together with the invariance transformations in eqs. (
28
,
29
), leads to the following structure for the generator:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[X]=

\displaystyle-i[K_{S},X]

\displaystyle+\sum_{ij}\omega_{ij}\left(\overline{V}_{i}X\overline{V}_{j}^{{\dagger}}-\frac{1}{2}\{\overline{V}_{j}^{\dagger}\overline{V}_{i},X\}\right).

(37)

where the effective Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle K_{S}=\frac{1}{2i}\sum_{ij}\omega_{ij}\left(\braket{V_{i}}_{1/d}V^{\dagger}_{j}-\braket{V^{\dagger}_{j}}_{1/d}V_{i}\right)\;.

(38)

Due to eq. (
31
), the effective Hamiltonian is always Hermitian, and can be equivalently rewritten as

\displaystyle K_{S}=\sum_{ij}\text{Im}\left\{\omega_{ij}\braket{V_{i}}_{1/d}V^{\dagger}_{j}\right\}\;.

(39)

This formulation leads to a well-defined expression for the effective Hamiltonian, even if
\omega_{0i}^{*}\neq\omega_{i0}
, providing a systematic and straightforward method for deriving a Lindblad-like generator that preserves both Hermiticity and trace annihilation.

Using this strategy, we can express each order
(\mathcal{L}_{n})
of the pertubative series for the TCL generator
\mathcal{L}_{t}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}\mathcal{L}_{n}
in a generalized Lindblad form with traceless Lindblad operators, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}[X]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-)^{k}\int_{0}^{t}d\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}d\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\Bigg\{-i\left[\text{Im}\left\{(i)^{n}\mathcal{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})\langle A(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k})\rangle_{1/d}{A}(\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})^{\dagger}\right\},X\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt+i^{n}\mathcal{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})\left(\overline{A}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k})X\overline{A}(\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\overline{A}(\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})^{\dagger}\overline{A}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}),X\right\}\right)\Bigg\},

(40)

where
\bar{A}
is the null trace operator defined as
\bar{A}=A-\braket{A}_{1/d}\mathbb{1}
.
With this formulation in place, we now turn our attention to the Hamiltonian part of the generator, examining its structure and perturbative expansion.

III.3
Perturbative expansion of the effective Hamiltonian

The structure of the effective system Hamiltonian naturally emerges from eq. (
40
) as a perturbative series in powers of the coupling strength
\lambda
, i.e.

\displaystyle K(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}K_{n}\;

(41)

with

\displaystyle K_{n}=\frac{-(+i)^{n}}{2i}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-)^{k}\int_{0}^{t}d\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}d\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\times

(42)

\displaystyle\times\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}){\mathbb{A}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)-(-)^{n}\text{h.c.}\right]\;,

where, for later convenience, we have defined the operator
{\mathbb{A}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right):=\langle A(\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k})^{\dagger}\rangle_{1/d}A(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k})
.

This equation provides a systematic method for computing each perturbative term of the emergent Hamiltonian directly from the underlying system-environment microscopic model. The recursive nature of eq. (
II.3
), as previously mentioned, often simplifies calculations, particularly when the environmental generalized cumulants exhibit special properties, such as vanishing contributions.

On the other hand, the explicit structure of eq. (
42
) highlights the operatorial structure of
K
, revealing its dependence on the system-side interaction operator. This structure simplifies significantly when the dynamics lead to pure dephasing (namely when
[H_{S},A]=0
), as
A(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k})=A^{k}
. Moreover, for finite-dimensional systems (particularly at lower dimensions) one can exploit algebraic properties of the operators to infer possible features of each contribution (see, for example, the discussion on spin systems in
2
2

2

See companion paper
Colla et al.
).

To gain further insight into the structure of the effective Hamiltonian, we define the following operator:

\displaystyle K_{n}^{k}=(-)^{k}\int_{0}^{t}d\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k}d\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}){\mathbb{A}}\!\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)\;,

(43)

This expression represents the partial contribution to
K
at order
n
, specifically arising from the product of
k
interaction-picture operators
A
. The full
n
-th order contribution to
K
can then be written as

\displaystyle K_{n}=\frac{-(+i)^{n}}{2i}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left[K_{n}^{k}-(-)^{n}\text{h.c.}\right]\;,

(44)

This expression reveals that the contributions to
K
take different forms depending on whether
n
is even or odd, specifically:

\displaystyle K_{2m}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m+1}\sum_{k=0}^{2m}\text{Im}\left(K_{2m}^{k}\right)\;,

(45)

\displaystyle K_{2m+1}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m+1}\sum_{k=0}^{2m+1}\text{Re}\left(K_{2m+1}^{k}\right)\;.

(46)

Thus, for even-order terms only the imaginary part of
K_{n}^{k}
contributes to the Hamiltonian, while for odd-order terms only the real part does. This distinction plays an important role in understanding how different orders of the perturbative expansion affect the system’s effective dynamics
3
3

3

for further details on the topic and a discussion on the role of fluctuations and dissipation on the Hamiltonian term see
Colla et al.
.

In the next section, we provide explicit calculations of each term up to third order, as well as the fourth-order contribution in cases where the lower-order terms vanish.

IV
Explicit expansion up to fourth order

In this section, we exploit our method – namely, eqs. (
II.3
) or (
II.3
) (recommended) for the coefficients – to calculate the explicit form of the generalized cumulants. We then apply these results to eq. (
42
) to obtain an explicit expression for the effective Hamiltonian up to low orders of perturbation. The dissipative part of the dynamics is trivially obtained once the form of the cumulants is given (see eq. (
40
)).
We perform the procedure for the most general case until third order, while we exploit in particular eq. (
II.3
) to calculate the fourth order contribution under the assumption of vanishing first order.

IV.1
First order

At first order there are only two possible coefficients which are simply

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}\right)\;,

(47)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}\right)\;.

(48)

Inserted into (
42
), they give the following contribution to the effective Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle K_{1}=\braket{B_{t}}\left(A_{t}-\braket{A_{t}}_{1/d}\right)\;.

(49)

Most often, the interaction operator
A
is taken to be traceless – if not, the contribution responsible for it can be absorbed into the environment’s bare Hamiltonian. In this case, the Schrödinger picture contribution to
K
is easily found to be
K_{1}^{\mathcal{S}}(t)=\braket{B_{t}}A
.
It represents driving on the system degrees of freedom due to a non-vanishing
\braket{B_{t}}
. Furthermore, a commutator with this Hamiltonian is the only first-order contribution in the expansion of the generator, while there are no dissipative contributions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}=-i[K_{1},\cdot]\;.

(50)

This can be interpreted as the emergence of effective driving on the system, which is often due to coherences in the initial state of the environment. The same expression for this contribution can be obtained using known techniques in quantum optics, for example
Cohen-Tannoudji et al. 1998
. Moreover, in certain models this contribution is the only emergent driving term, with negligible dissipation, that survives under a suitable semiclassical limit (weak coupling
\lambda
and large
\braket{B_{t}}
)
Colla and Breuer 2024
.

IV.2
Second order

At second order, the coefficients
\mathcal{D}
are given by two-point correlation functions of the bath. From the different time-dependencies, there are three different coefficients which read

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}^{2}\right)=\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}^{2}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{2}\right)\;,

(51)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1},\bm{s}_{1}\right)=\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1},\bm{s}_{1}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}\right)\;,

(52)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{2}\right)=\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{2}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{2}\right)\;.

(53)

Inserted into the expression for
K
, they give the following second order contribution to the Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle K_{2}=\frac{1}{2i}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}

\displaystyle\left(\braket{B_{t}B_{t_{1}}}-\braket{B_{t}}\braket{B_{t_{1}}}\right)\times

\displaystyle\times\big[A_{t}A_{t_{1}}-\braket{A_{t}A_{t_{1}}}_{1/d}

(54)

\displaystyle\quad+A_{t}\braket{A_{t_{1}}}_{1/d}-A_{t_{1}}\braket{A_{t}}_{1/d}\big]-\text{h.c.}\;.

(55)

In the common case where the system operator
A
in the interaction Hamiltonian is traceless, and the bath operator
B
is also such that
\braket{B_{t}}=0
, the above reduces to

\displaystyle K_{2}=\frac{1}{2i}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\braket{B_{t}B_{t_{1}}}\left[A_{t}A_{t_{1}}-\braket{A_{t}A_{t_{1}}}_{1/d}\right]-\text{h.c.}\;.

(56)

It corresponds to the Hamiltonian contribution that one would naturally obtain from a TCL expansion at second order
Colla 2024
.

IV.3
Third order

The expansion at third order, in absence of any further assumption, becomes already more involved. There are four coefficients, which are the following:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}^{3}\right)=\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}^{3}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{2}^{3}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}^{2}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{3}\right)+\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{2}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{3}\right)\;,

(57)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1},\bm{s}_{1}^{2}\right)=\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1},\bm{s}_{1}^{2}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}^{2}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1},\bm{s}_{2}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1},\bm{s}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{2}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}^{2}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{2}\right)+2\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{2}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}\right)\;,

(58)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{2},\bm{s}_{1}\right)=\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{2},\bm{s}_{1}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{2}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{2},\bm{s}_{1}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1},\bm{s}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{2}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{2}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{2}\right)+2\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{2}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}\right)\;,

(59)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{3}\right)=\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{3}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{2}^{3}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{2}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{3}\right)+\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{2}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{3}\right)\;.

(60)

Inserted into (
42
) give the following third order contribution:

\displaystyle K_{3}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}

\displaystyle\Big[f(t,t_{1},t_{2})X(t,t_{1},t_{2})

(61)

\displaystyle\;-g(t,t_{1},t_{2})Y(t,t_{1},t_{2})+\text{h.c.}\Big]\;,

where we have defined the coefficients

\displaystyle f(t,t_{1},t_{2})=

\displaystyle\braket{B_{t}B_{t_{1}}B_{t_{2}}}\theta_{t_{1}^{2}}-\braket{B_{t}}\braket{B_{t_{1}}B_{t_{2}}}\theta_{t_{1}^{2}}

\displaystyle-\braket{B_{t}B_{t_{1}}}\braket{B_{t_{2}}}+\braket{B_{t}}\braket{B_{t_{1}}}\braket{B_{t_{2}}}\;,

(62)

\displaystyle g(t,t_{1},t_{2})=

\displaystyle\braket{B_{t_{1}}B_{t}B_{t_{2}}}-\braket{B_{t}}\braket{B_{t_{1}}B_{t_{2}}}

\displaystyle-\braket{B_{t_{1}}B_{t}}\braket{B_{t_{2}}}-\braket{B_{t}B_{t_{2}}}\braket{B_{t_{1}}}

\displaystyle+2\braket{B_{t}}\braket{B_{t_{1}}}\braket{B_{t_{2}}}\;,

(63)

and the operators

\displaystyle X(t,t_{1},t_{2})=

\displaystyle A_{t}A_{t_{1}}A_{t_{2}}-\braket{A_{t}A_{t_{1}}A_{t_{2}}}_{1/d}

\displaystyle-A_{t_{1}}A_{t_{2}}\braket{A_{t}}_{1/d}+A_{t}\braket{A_{t_{1}}A_{t_{2}}}_{1/d}\;,

(64)

\displaystyle Y(t,t_{1},t_{2})=

\displaystyle A_{t}A_{t_{2}}\braket{A_{t_{1}}}_{1/d}-A_{t_{1}}\braket{A_{t}A_{t_{2}}}_{1/d}\;.

(65)

Whenever
\mathrm{Tr}{A}=0
and
\braket{B_{t}}=0
, the expression simplifies but does not grant further insights. Nonetheless, it is also frequent the case where the mechanism for which
\braket{B_{t}}=0
implies that also all odd-ordered cumulants vanish (such as for linearly coupled thermal bosonic environments); in that case,
K_{3}
is identically zero – as is the contribution to the whole generator
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
.

IV.4
Fourth order

At fourth order the full expansion becomes quite cumbersome. For this reason, we assume from the start that
\braket{B_{t}}=0
, which helps reduce the number of terms as we have seen already at lower orders. In particular, first order disappears and all coefficients for
n=2,3
are simply given by
\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)=\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{k},\bm{s}_{1}^{n-k}\right)
. Using the recursive formula (
II.3
) we can then easily compute the fourth order coefficients, which read

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}^{4}\right)=\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}^{4}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}^{2}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{3}^{4}\right)\;,

(66)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1},\bm{s}_{1}^{3}\right)=\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1},\bm{s}_{1}^{3}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1},\bm{s}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{2}^{3}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}^{2}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1},\bm{s}_{3}\right)\;,

(67)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{2},\bm{s}_{1}^{2}\right)=\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{2},\bm{s}_{1}^{2}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1},\bm{s}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{2},\bm{s}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\dot{D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}^{2}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{2}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{2}\right){D}\left(\bm{s}_{1}^{2}\right)\;,

(68)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{3},\bm{s}_{1}\right)=\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{3},\bm{s}_{1}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1},\bm{s}_{1}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{2}^{3}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{2}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{3},\bm{s}_{1}\right)\;

(69)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{4}\right)=\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{4}\right)-\dot{D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{1}^{2}\right){D}\left(\bm{\tau}_{3}^{4}\right)\,

(70)

and give the following contribution to the Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle K_{4}=-\frac{1}{2i}\int_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle dt_{1}dt_{2}dt_{3}\Big[\bar{f}(t,t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})\bar{X}(t,t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})

(71)

\displaystyle\;-\bar{g}(t,t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})\bar{Y}(t,t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})-\text{h.c.}\Big]\;.

Similarly to what we did for third order, we defined the coefficients

\displaystyle\bar{f}(t,t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})=

\displaystyle\braket{B_{t}B_{t_{1}}B_{t_{2}}B_{t_{3}}}\theta_{t_{1}^{3}}

\displaystyle-\braket{B_{t}B_{t_{1}}}\braket{B_{t_{2}}B_{t_{3}}}\theta_{t_{2}^{3}}\;,

(72)

\displaystyle\bar{g}(t,t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})=

\displaystyle\braket{B_{t_{1}}B_{t}B_{t_{2}}B_{t_{3}}}\theta_{t_{2}^{3}}

\displaystyle-\braket{B_{t_{1}}B_{t}}\braket{B_{t_{2}}B_{t_{3}}}\theta_{t_{2}^{3}}

\displaystyle-\braket{B_{t}B_{t_{2}}}\braket{B_{t_{1}}B_{t_{3}}}\;,

(73)

and the operators

\displaystyle\bar{X}(t,t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})=

\displaystyle A_{t}A_{t_{1}}A_{t_{2}}A_{t_{3}}-\braket{A_{t}A_{t_{1}}A_{t_{2}}A_{t_{3}}}_{1/d}

\displaystyle-A_{t_{1}}A_{t_{2}}A_{t_{3}}\braket{A_{t}}_{1/d}

\displaystyle+A_{t}\braket{A_{t_{1}}A_{t_{2}}A_{t_{3}}}_{1/d}\;,

(74)

\displaystyle\bar{Y}(t,t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})=

\displaystyle A_{t}A_{t_{2}}A_{t_{3}}\braket{A_{t_{1}}}_{1/d}

\displaystyle-A_{t_{1}}\braket{A_{t}A_{t_{2}}A_{t_{3}}}_{1/d}\;.

(75)

The latter will slightly simplify under the assumption that
\mathrm{Tr}{A}=0
.

The calculation of the coefficients (
66
)-(
70
) was made significantly easier through the use of the recursive formula, in particular because the simplification of lower-order coefficients can be quickly carried through to higher orders.

V
Conclusions

In this work, we have developed a recursive perturbative expansion for the time-convolutionless (TCL) generator of an open quantum system, explicitly formulated in a generalized Lindblad structure using left- and right-acting operators. This approach provides a systematic framework for deriving the generator at arbitrary orders without imposing constraints on the system or environment beyond the assumption of an initially uncorrelated state.

A key advantage of this formulation is its ability to transparently separate the coherent and dissipative contributions of the TCL generator. This decomposition naturally leads to a recursive expression for the effective system Hamiltonian, capturing environment-induced energy renormalization beyond the weak-coupling and Markovian limits. By maintaining a Lindblad-like structure, our method offers insights into non-Markovian and strong-coupling effects, as well as their thermodynamic implications.

To illustrate the effectiveness of this approach, we computed explicit expressions up to fourth order, illustrating how the recursive structure simplifies the calculations by propagating lower-order simplifications to higher orders. This framework paves the way for systematically studying open quantum systems with complex environments while preserving a physically interpretable form of the TCL generator.
