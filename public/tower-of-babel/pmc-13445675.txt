When phones ring in silence: intolerance of uncertainty amplifies the association between being phubbed and online vigilance in face-to-face interaction
Authors: Zhaoyan Meng, Mengyu Qu, Shisan Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmartphones are increasingly embedded in face-to-face interaction, making being phubbed a common negative relational experience. Although prior research has documented its interpersonal and emotional costs, less is known about whether such offline relational disruption is followed by heightened attentional orientation toward digital cues. Online vigilance may be especially likely to emerge when the relational meaning of phubbing remains uncertain. Drawing on social monitoring system theory, the present research examined whether being phubbed is associated with, and experimentally increases, online vigilance, and whether intolerance of uncertainty (IU) moderates this association.MethodsThe research consisted of two studies. Study 1 used a cross-sectional survey design (N = 845). Study 2 used a between-subjects experimental design (N = 220), manipulated phubbing during face-to-face interaction, and recorded phone-checking behavior during a waiting period.ResultsIn Study 1, being phubbed was associated with online vigilance, and IU significantly strengthened this association. In Study 2, phubbing significantly increased state online vigilance, but this effect emerged primarily among individuals with moderate to high levels of IU. Analyses using online-monitoring frequency as the primary confirmatory behavioral outcome likewise showed a significant interaction, indicating that high-IU individuals were more likely to engage in more frequent digital information monitoring after being phubbed. Broader behavioral indicators, including first-check latency, phone-checking frequency, and total phone-use duration, provided directionally consistent supplementary support, whereas the evidence from dichotomous behavioral indicators was weaker.ConclusionsBeing phubbed may not only undermine the immediate interaction experience but also be followed by increased online vigilance and heightened attention to digital cues. IU may strengthen the link between relationally ambiguous cues and online vigilance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04904-9.Keywords: Being phubbed, Online vigilance, Intolerance of uncertainty, Face-to-face interaction

Introduction

Mobile internet has not only increased the efficiency of information transmission but also changed how individuals maintain connectedness, allocate attention, and organize everyday communication. As smartphones make online access continuously available, online availability has gradually become a default condition of daily life. Vorderer et al. (2016) [1] described this communication environment as permanently online, permanently connected (POPC). In this context, Reinecke et al. (2018) [2] defined online vigilance as a psychological state in which individuals maintain a sustained cognitive orientation toward online content and digital communication, heightened readiness to respond, and active monitoring tendencies. Unlike mere device-use frequency, online vigilance is a nonpathological orientation toward connectivity that includes both stable individual differences and situational fluctuations, and is associated with offline attention allocation, activity interruption, and mental fatigue [1–5].

Existing research on online vigilance has primarily focused on its measurement, structural validity, and associations with outcomes such as well-being, fatigue, and stress. Discussions of its antecedents have tended to emphasize the long-term shaping effects of the POPC environment, media affordances, and habits of connectivity [2, 4]. This leaves an important question insufficiently examined: Can online vigilance also be activated in the moment by specific relational events occurring offline during face-to-face interaction? If so, online vigilance should not be regarded only as a background orientation gradually internalized within a permanently connected environment; it should also be understood as a digitally oriented attentional state that may be triggered by social contexts.

Among offline interpersonal events, phubbing provides a suitable entry point for addressing this question. Phubbing refers to the act of turning one’s attention to a phone during a co-present interaction and thereby snubbing the current interaction partner; being phubbed refers to the target’s experience of being ignored and marginalized [6]. Prior research shows that phubbing impairs impression formation and interaction evaluations [7], reduces affiliation and conversational quality in face-to-face conversation [8], and undermines relationship satisfaction in broader social or romantic relationships [9, 10]. At the psychological level, phubbing threatens basic psychological needs such as belonging and induces negative mood [11, 12], and it is also associated with reduced trust and dehumanization [11, 13]. David and Roberts (2017) [14] further found that individuals who were phubbed experienced greater social exclusion and stronger attachment to social media. Thus, the consequences of being phubbed may extend beyond relational evaluations and emotional experience to later attention allocation and orientation toward digital cues.

The present study uses social monitoring system theory as its main theoretical reference. This theory proposes that when belonging needs are threatened, individuals increase their sensitivity to socially diagnostic cues in order to reassess their social standing and identify opportunities for restoring connection [15]. Empirical evidence shows that a stronger need to belong is associated with more accurate decoding of vocal tone and facial emotion [15, 16]; social exclusion improves the detection of real and fake smiles, which are socially diagnostic cues [17]; and individuals show rapid and sensitive detection of minimal ostracism cues [18]. These findings support the possibility that when phubbing is experienced as a relational threat, individuals may become more attentive to digital cues that provide information about responsiveness, availability, and social feedback. It should be emphasized, however, that the present research did not directly measure belonging threat, relational uncertainty, or cue sensitivity. Thus, social monitoring system theory is used here as an interpretive framework rather than as a directly tested mediating mechanism.

In the POPC environment, digital cues such as notifications, online status indicators, likes, comments, and social updates do not merely signal what is happening online; they also provide relational information about responsiveness, availability, and social feedback [19, 20]. Experience-sampling research further indicates that notifications and others’ phone use in co-present settings increase the likelihood of phone checking in social situations, suggesting that digital connection cues themselves have become situational triggers of media behavior [21]. Online vigilance may therefore be understood as a digitally oriented attentional state that may be related to social monitoring processes.

It is also important to clarify that online vigilance, as discussed in the present study, is not equivalent to general phone use, problematic smartphone use, fear of missing out (FoMO), habitual checking, compensatory digital coping, or reassurance seeking. Online vigilance emphasizes a sustained cognitive orientation toward online content and digital communication, readiness to respond, and active monitoring tendencies [2]. It does not presuppose excessive or uncontrolled use [22, 23], a specific anxiety about missing out [24], a concrete behavioral script [25], or the use of media to alleviate negative affect, obtain validation, or seek emotional reassurance [26, 27]. Accordingly, the present study more cautiously treats online vigilance as a state that may be triggered by relational threat and expressed as heightened attention to subsequent digital cues.

Being phubbed may be an important antecedent of online vigilance not because a phone appears in the interaction per se, but because it communicates a relationally meaningful signal of neglect [28]. Vanden Abeele (2020) [29] further argued that the relational consequences of phubbing are not driven by a single mechanism, but may involve concurrent processes such as expectancy violation, social exclusion, and attentional conflict. In this sense, phubbing is best understood as a complex interactional cue that has relational diagnostic value but whose meaning is not fully transparent.

Experimental research has also shown that phone involvement during interaction impairs impression formation and interaction quality, and that different forms of gaze diversion and phubbing weaken affiliation and belonging during interaction [7, 8]. Cell phone-induced ostracism threatens basic needs for belonging, self-esteem, control, and meaningful existence [30]. Being phubbed is therefore not a neutral distraction; it is a relationally diagnostic cue that may simultaneously involve expectancy violation, visible neglect, attentional conflict, and ambiguity about relational meaning. The present research focuses primarily on the ambiguity of relational meaning because this pathway is most closely aligned with intolerance of uncertainty (IU).

Broader social-psychological and digital-media research also suggests that negative and unresolved social experiences may alter subsequent information processing and behavioral readiness through affective and uncertainty-related processes [31, 32]. In digital contexts, phubbing threatens basic needs and is accompanied by emotional reactions and reduced trust; online exclusion evokes immediate affective changes and is related to regulation processes; and negative digital experiences, negative emotions, and affective styles are closely interrelated [11, 33, 34]. Thus, phubbing may first elicit relational threat and immediate regulatory demands, which in turn may orient individuals toward digital cues that can provide responsiveness, availability, and social feedback.

The present study further introduces IU as a boundary condition. IU originally refers to individuals’ unacceptability of uncertain events and their potential negative consequences [35]. Later research has linked IU to anxiety, worry, threat-interpretation bias, and information seeking and checking behavior under uncertainty [36–38]. In digital-media contexts, IU is also related to problematic smartphone use, problematic social media use, and reassurance-related motives underlying FoMO [39, 40]. The present study focuses on IU not because it has been shown to be more central than other vulnerabilities, but because a key psychological feature of being phubbed is the opacity of its relational meaning: individuals know that they are being ignored, but they do not know what this neglect means [13, 29].

Thus, for individuals high in IU, being phubbed may be more likely to be processed as an unresolved relational event that requires further confirmation, thereby increasing attention to online prompts, feedback, and availability cues. Nevertheless, the present study does not claim that phubbing operates through a single mechanism, nor does it assume that IU is the only boundary condition explaining reactions to phubbing. Fear of negative evaluation associated with social anxiety, attachment anxiety, rejection sensitivity, neuroticism, and baseline smartphone dependence or problematic smartphone use may also shape how individuals interpret ambiguous social threats or independently influence subsequent phone-checking behavior [22, 23, 41–44]. Because these alternative vulnerability factors were not measured simultaneously, the present research cannot claim that IU has unique specificity; instead, IU is treated as a theoretically relevant boundary condition that received empirical support in the present studies.

Based on this reasoning, we proposed H1: Being phubbed will positively predict online vigilance. H2: IU will positively moderate the relationship between being phubbed and online vigilance, such that the positive association will become stronger as IU increases.

To test these hypotheses, we conducted two complementary studies. Study 1 tested the ecological association among being phubbed, online vigilance, and IU and its boundary-condition pattern in a naturalistic sample. Study 2 experimentally manipulated phubbing in face-to-face interaction to further test its immediate effect on state online vigilance and used phone-checking behavior during a waiting period as supplementary behavioral indicators.

Study 1

Participants and procedure

Study 1 used a cross-sectional survey design. A total of 1,000 questionnaires were distributed. Inclusion criteria were being at least 18 years old, using a smartphone in daily life, and having stable face-to-face social interaction experiences during the past month. During data cleaning, responses were excluded if they showed extremely short completion time, failed attention checks, obvious straight-lining, or logical inconsistencies. The final valid sample included 845 participants, yielding a valid response rate of 84.50%. The mean age was 24.44 years (SD = 4.72); 470 participants were women, and 453 were in a romantic relationship.

All participants volunteered to take part in the study and provided informed consent before participation. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Inner Mongolia Normal University. Participants first reported their ID number, age, sex, relationship status, and general phone-use tendency. Age was treated as a continuous variable; sex was coded as 0 = female and 1 = male; relationship status was coded as 0 = in a romantic relationship and 1 = not in a romantic relationship. Participants then completed measures of being phubbed, online vigilance, IU, and the marker variable.

Measures

General phone-use tendency

General phone-use tendency was measured with four items rated on a 7-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). The items assessed daily phone-checking frequency, habitual checking when no notification was present, checking frequency for instant messaging or social media apps, and discomfort after not checking the phone for a period of time. The four items were averaged, with higher scores indicating a stronger general tendency to check the phone in daily life. In Study 1, Cronbach’s alpha for this scale was 0.847.

Being phubbed

Being phubbed was measured with the Generic Scale of Being Phubbed (GSBP). The scale includes 22 items rated on a 7-point scale (1 = never, 7 = always) and contains three dimensions: perceived norms, feelings of being ignored, and interpersonal conflict. Items were averaged, with higher scores indicating more frequent experiences of being phubbed during face-to-face interaction [45]. In Study 1, Cronbach’s alpha for the total GSBP was 0.940, and the alphas for the three subscales were 0.890, 0.894, and 0.836, respectively.

Online vigilance

Online vigilance was measured with the Online Vigilance Scale (OVS). The scale includes 12 items rated on a 5-point scale (1 = does not apply at all, 5 = fully applies) and consists of three dimensions: salience, reactibility, and monitoring. Items were averaged, with higher scores indicating a stronger tendency to maintain a cognitively persistent online state [2]. In Study 1, Cronbach’s alpha for the total OVS was 0.902, and the alphas for the three subscales were 0.832, 0.793, and 0.809, respectively.

IU

IU was measured with the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (IUS-12). The scale includes 12 items rated on a 5-point scale (1 = does not apply at all, 5 = fully applies) and comprises two dimensions: prospective anxiety and inhibitory anxiety. Items were averaged, with higher scores indicating lower tolerance of uncertainty [37]. In Study 1, Cronbach’s alpha for the total IUS-12 was 0.905, and the alphas for the two subscales were 0.899 and 0.783, respectively.

Marker variable

Attitude Toward the Color Blue (ATCB) was used as a marker variable. It includes seven items rated on a 7-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). Proposed by Miller et al. (2024), ATCB can be used as a theoretically unrelated marker variable in the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) marker technique for assessing common method bias [46]. In Study 1, Cronbach’s alpha for ATCB was 0.921.

Data analysis

Study 1 first scored each scale and examined reliability. Reliability was calculated for the three GSBP dimensions and the total scale, for the three OVS dimensions (salience, reactibility, and monitoring) and the total scale, for the two IUS-12 dimensions (prospective anxiety and inhibitory anxiety) and the total scale, and for the four-item general phone-use tendency measure.

Second, common method variance (CMV) was examined using a CFA marker-variable approach. We first tested whether the marker variable showed low correlations with being phubbed, online vigilance, and IU. We then estimated a multidimensional baseline CFA model and compared it with a marker-CMV model that included a marker method factor. In the multidimensional baseline CFA model, being phubbed was specified as three first-order factors, online vigilance as three first-order factors, IU as two first-order factors, and the marker variable as an independent marker factor. In the marker-CMV model, the theoretically unrelated marker variable was used to identify a common method factor, which loaded equally on all substantive indicators. If adding the marker variable did not substantially improve model fit and the common method loading was small and nonsignificant, common method bias would be unlikely to seriously influence the conclusions.

Next, Pearson correlations were used to examine zero-order associations among being phubbed, online vigilance, and IU. Finally, moderation regression analyses were conducted to test whether IU moderated the relationship between being phubbed and online vigilance. Both models without covariates and models with covariates were reported. All continuous variables were standardized before being entered into the regression models, and the interaction term was computed by multiplying standardized being phubbed by standardized IU. Covariates included age, sex, relationship status, and general phone-use tendency. Simple slopes were examined at low and high levels of IU, defined as one standard deviation below and above the mean. Johnson–Neyman analysis was further used to identify the range of IU values for which the effect of being phubbed on online vigilance was significant. Model assumptions were checked using VIF, residual plots, heteroscedasticity tests, and Cook’s distance. To assess robustness, we reported both models without covariates and models including covariates.

These results indicate that ATCB was suitable as a theoretically unrelated marker variable.

In other words, across most of the IU range, being phubbed was significantly associated with higher online vigilance, and the association increased as IU increased.

Summary

Study 1 provided initial support for the proposed moderation model based on cross-sectional data from a naturalistic sample. Being phubbed was positively associated with online vigilance, and this association strengthened as IU increased. However, Study 1 mainly provides ecological association evidence and is insufficient for strict causal inference. Individuals’ own online vigilance, general phone-use tendency, or interpersonal sensitivity may also influence their perception of being phubbed. Therefore, Study 2 further used an experimental manipulation to test the immediate effect of phubbing on state online vigilance and subsequent phone behavior.

Study 2

Participants and design

Study 2 used an experimental design to examine whether phubbing during face-to-face interaction would increase state online vigilance and whether IU would moderate this effect. Participants were randomly assigned to a non-phubbing condition or a phubbing condition. Experimental condition was coded as 0 = non-phubbing and 1 = phubbing.

An a priori power analysis was conducted in G*Power 3.1 with alpha = 0.05, power = 0.90, and an interaction effect of f² = 0.05. The analysis indicated that at least 213 valid participants were required to test the moderation effect [47]. A total of 240 participants were recruited. Inclusion criteria were being at least 18 years old, using a smartphone in daily life, and having stable face-to-face social interaction experience during the past month. All participants volunteered and provided informed consent before the experiment. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Inner Mongolia Normal University.

After excluding participants with incomplete experimental procedures, failed attention checks, failure to follow experimental instructions, suspicion of the study purpose, or invalid behavioral records, the final sample included 220 valid participants. The valid response rate was 91.67%. The mean age was 22.62 years (SD = 2.29); 126 participants were women, and 119 were in a romantic relationship.

Procedure

Upon arriving at the laboratory, participants were told that they would complete an introductory face-to-face conversation task with another participant. In fact, the interaction partner was a trained confederate, and participants had no prior personal relationship with the confederate. Before the interaction, participants completed demographic information, the IUS-12, and the baseline phone-use tendency measure.

Participants then engaged in a face-to-face interaction with the confederate. In the phubbing condition, the confederate checked and briefly used a smartphone at predetermined time points during the interaction, and the phone content was not integrated into the conversation. In the non-phubbing condition, the confederate maintained normal interaction and did not engage in phubbing behavior.

After the interaction, participants completed the manipulation check and measures of state online vigilance, negative affect, boredom, arousal, and exclusion. They then entered a 2-minute waiting period. During this period, phone-use behaviors were recorded, including whether participants checked their phones, latency to first phone check, number of phone checks, phone-use duration, and whether they opened a social or instant messaging app.

To avoid overinterpreting general phone checking as online vigilance, we further coded the content of phone use during the waiting period. Phone behaviors were classified into two categories. Online-monitoring behaviors included checking messages, checking notifications, and using social or instant messaging apps. Non-monitoring phone behaviors included checking the time, using the camera, opening settings, and random scrolling.

Measures

Baseline phone-use tendency

Baseline phone-use tendency was measured before the experimental manipulation using the same four items as in Study 1, rated on a 7-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). The four items were averaged, with higher scores indicating a stronger habitual phone-checking tendency before the manipulation. In Study 2, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.780.

IU

IU was measured in the same way as in Study 1. In Study 2, Cronbach’s alpha for the total IUS-12 was 0.884, and the alphas for the two subscales were 0.886 and 0.757, respectively.

Manipulation check

The manipulation check consisted of three context-specific items assessing the partner’s phone-use frequency, whether participants felt ignored, and whether the interaction was interrupted by the partner’s phone use. Items were rated on a 7-point scale (1 = not at all, 7 = extremely frequently), with higher scores indicating stronger perceived phone use or phubbing during the interaction. In Study 2, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.811.

State online vigilance

State online vigilance was measured using an adapted version of the OVS. The original three-dimensional structure of the OVS—salience, reactibility, and monitoring—was retained, but the temporal frame of the items was adapted to the immediate post-interaction state. In Study 2, Cronbach’s alpha for the total state OVS was 0.802, and the alphas for the three dimensions were 0.715, 0.678, and 0.710, respectively.

Negative affect

Negative affect was measured with the Negative Affect subscale of the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) [48]. The scale contains 10 items rated on a 5-point scale (1 = very slightly, 5 = extremely). Participants reported their current negative affect after the interaction. The 10 items were averaged, with higher scores indicating stronger negative affect. In Study 2, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.883.

Boredom

Boredom was measured using the 8-item version of the Multidimensional State Boredom Scale [49]. Participants rated their immediate post-interaction feelings on a 7-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). The items were averaged, with higher scores indicating stronger state boredom. In Study 2, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.875.

Arousal

Arousal was measured using six adjective items from the energetic arousal dimension of the Activation-Deactivation Adjective Check List [50]. Participants rated their immediate post-interaction state on a 4-point scale (1 = definitely do not feel, 4 = definitely feel). The six items were averaged, with higher scores indicating stronger energetic arousal. In Study 2, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.701.

Exclusion

Perceived exclusion was measured using the Need-Threat Scale [51]. The scale contains 20 items rated on a 7-point scale (1 = not at all, 7 = very much so). It includes four dimensions: belonging threat, self-esteem threat, control threat, and meaningful-existence threat, with five items per dimension. All items were coded such that higher scores indicated stronger perceived exclusion or need threat. In Study 2, Cronbach’s alpha for the total Need-Threat Scale was 0.887, and the alphas for the four dimensions were 0.740, 0.733, 0.780, and 0.753, respectively.

Phone-use behaviors

Participants’ phone-use behaviors were recorded during the waiting period. Broad phone-use indicators included whether participants checked their phones, latency to first phone check, number of phone checks, total phone-use duration, and whether they opened a social or instant messaging app.

In addition, phone behaviors were coded as online-monitoring behaviors or non-monitoring phone behaviors. Online-monitoring behaviors included checking messages, checking notifications, and using social or instant messaging apps; non-monitoring phone behaviors included checking the time, using the camera, opening settings, and random scrolling. We calculated the occurrence, frequency, and total duration of online-monitoring behaviors and non-monitoring behaviors separately. Duration variables were log1p-transformed in regression analyses to reduce skewness. To facilitate interpretation of duration effects, model-implied estimates for key duration indicators were back-transformed to the original time scale in seconds in the Supplementary Materials; conditional means, differences, and percentage changes are reported in Supplementary Table S3.

Data analysis

Study 2 first examined whether the manipulation was successful by comparing manipulation-check scores between the phubbing and non-phubbing conditions. Scale scores and reliabilities were then calculated. Baseline phone-use tendency was formed by averaging the four items measured before the experimental manipulation. IUS-12, state online vigilance, negative affect, boredom, arousal, and exclusion were scored as dimension means and/or total means as appropriate.

To validate state online vigilance, we first used CFA to compare a one-factor model and a three-factor model to determine whether the adapted measure retained the salience, reactibility, and monitoring structure. We then examined criterion-related associations with actual phone-use behaviors. To avoid overinterpreting general phone checking as online vigilance, phone behaviors were coded as online-monitoring behaviors and non-monitoring phone behaviors, and the correlations between state online vigilance and these two types of behavior were compared.

In addition, discriminant-validity CFA was used to test whether state online vigilance could be distinguished from negative affect, arousal, and exclusion. Specifically, we compared a four-factor model with models in which state online vigilance was merged separately with negative affect, arousal, or exclusion. Discriminant validity was further assessed using the Fornell–Larcker criterion.

In moderation analyses, all continuous predictors and covariates were standardized before model estimation. Experimental condition was coded as 0 = non-phubbing and 1 = phubbing. The primary model controlled for age, sex, relationship status, baseline phone-use tendency, negative affect, and boredom. Arousal and exclusion were mainly used in discriminant-validity analyses and were included as additional covariates in robustness models.

The primary confirmatory psychological outcome was state online vigilance, and the primary confirmatory behavioral outcome was online-monitoring frequency. Other behavioral indicators were treated as exploratory or robustness outcomes, and exploratory behavioral outcomes were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure. Simple slopes were examined at low and high levels of IU, defined as one standard deviation below and above the mean. Johnson–Neyman analysis was conducted for the main continuous outcome. Linear regression was used for continuous outcomes. For count outcomes, Poisson overdispersion was first examined; if the overdispersion ratio exceeded 1.50, negative binomial regression was used. Logistic regression was used for binary outcomes.

Results

Manipulation check

The manipulation check was analyzed using Welch’s t-test. Participants in the phubbing condition perceived significantly stronger phubbing than those in the non-phubbing condition, n = 111, M = 5.05, SD = 1.16 versus n = 109, M = 3.34, SD = 1.36, t(212) = 10.016, p < 0.001. Thus, the manipulation was successful.

Factor structure of the state online vigilance

To examine whether state online vigilance retained the original three-dimensional structure, we used CFA to compare a one-factor model and a three-factor model. The one-factor model fit the data poorly, χ²(54) = 181.714, CFI = 0.761, TLI = 0.708, RMSEA = 0.104, SRMR = 0.076. By contrast, the three-factor model fit well, χ²(51) = 77.302, CFI = 0.951, TLI = 0.936, RMSEA = 0.048, SRMR = 0.047. The robust chi-square difference test indicated that the three-factor model fit significantly better than the one-factor model, Δχ²(3) = 129.752, p < 0.001. All standardized factor loadings in the three-factor model were significant, ranging from 0.501 to 0.707, ps < 0.001. Thus, state online vigilance retained the salience, reactibility, and monitoring structure.

Discriminant validity

To examine whether state online vigilance could be distinguished from related state responses, we conducted discriminant-validity CFA. In the four-factor model, state online vigilance, negative affect, arousal, and perceived exclusion were modeled as separate latent variables. As shown in Table 3, the four-factor model fit the data well. In contrast, when state online vigilance was merged with negative affect, arousal, or exclusion, model fit was worse, with decreases in CFI of 0.052, 0.125, and 0.055, respectively.

Criterion-related validity: phone-use behaviors

As shown in Table 4, state online vigilance was significantly associated with broad phone-checking behaviors, |r| = 0.241–0.462, ps < 0.001. Content-based coding further showed stronger and more consistent correlations between state online vigilance and online-monitoring behaviors, r = 0.275–0.443, ps < 0.001. Overall, these results indicate that state online vigilance is more strongly related to phone behaviors that better reflect digital information monitoring. However, these behaviors should still be interpreted as behavioral correlates consistent with state online vigilance rather than as direct behavioral measurements of online vigilance.

Exploratory behavioral analyses and multiple-testing correction

Beyond the primary confirmatory behavioral outcome, we conducted exploratory analyses of other behavioral indicators and corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure. Complete results are reported in Supplementary Table S2.

Specifically, the Condition × IU interaction significantly predicted latency to first phone check, B = − 11.363, pBH = 0.004; phone-checking frequency, IRR = 2.082, pBH < 0.001, 95% CI for IRR [1.549, 2.799]; log1p-transformed phone-use duration, B = 0.765, pBH < 0.001; and whether participants checked their phones, OR = 2.775, pBH = 0.008, 95% CI for OR [1.380, 5.580]. However, the Condition × IU interaction did not significantly predict whether participants opened a social app, OR = 1.361, pBH = 0.394, 95% CI for OR [0.670, 2.764].

For online-monitoring behaviors, the Condition × IU interaction significantly predicted log1p-transformed online-monitoring duration, B = 0.695, pBH < 0.001. However, the effect on whether any online-monitoring behavior occurred did not reach significance after FDR correction, OR = 1.884, pBH = 0.060, 95% CI for OR [0.989, 3.589].

At the same time, the Condition × IU interaction also reached FDR-corrected significance for some non-monitoring indicators, including non-monitoring frequency, IRR = 1.717, pBH = 0.011, 95% CI for IRR [1.152, 2.559]; log1p-transformed non-monitoring duration, B = 0.307, pBH = 0.021; and whether any non-monitoring behavior occurred, OR = 2.343, pBH = 0.011, 95% CI for OR [1.258, 4.363]. Therefore, the exploratory results should not be interpreted as showing that the interaction affected only online-monitoring behavior. A more cautious interpretation is that the phubbing × IU interaction was supported for the theoretically specified online-monitoring frequency outcome, while also possibly reflecting broader increases in phone engagement among high-IU individuals after phubbing.

Incremental validity of state online vigilance

To further examine the incremental validity of state online vigilance, we used state online vigilance as a predictor of online-monitoring behaviors after controlling for negative affect, arousal, exclusion, baseline phone-use tendency, experimental condition, and IU. Outcomes included log1p-transformed online-monitoring duration, whether any online-monitoring behavior occurred, and online-monitoring frequency.

Results showed that state online vigilance still significantly predicted log1p-transformed online-monitoring duration, B = 0.308, SE = 0.123, t = 2.496, p = 0.013, 95% CI [0.066, 0.550]; significantly predicted whether any online-monitoring behavior occurred, B = 0.371, SE = 0.181, z = 2.055, p = 0.040, OR = 1.449, 95% CI for OR [1.017, 2.065]; and significantly predicted online-monitoring frequency, B = 0.425, SE = 0.107, z = 3.965, p < 0.001, IRR = 1.530, 95% CI for IRR [1.240, 1.887]. These results indicate that state online vigilance explained unique variance in online-monitoring behavior beyond related alternative state responses, baseline phone-use tendency, experimental condition, and IU.

Summary

Study 2 provided further support for the core hypotheses under experimental conditions. Phubbing significantly increased state online vigilance, and IU showed a stable positive moderation effect: phubbing significantly increased state online vigilance only at moderate and high levels of IU. When online-monitoring frequency was used as the primary confirmatory behavioral outcome, the Condition × IU interaction was also significant, indicating that high-IU individuals were more likely to show more frequent digital information monitoring after phubbing. Other broad phone-use indicators provided directionally consistent supplementary support, but the results were not fully consistent. Thus, behavioral responses were more stably reflected in digital monitoring frequency and some intensity-based indicators rather than in all forms of phone behavior equally.

General discussion

The present research examined whether being phubbed during face-to-face interaction increases individuals’ sustained vigilance toward the online world. Across two studies, the evidence was relatively consistent. Study 1 showed that being phubbed was positively associated with online vigilance and that this association strengthened as IU increased. Study 2 further showed that a single experimentally manipulated phubbing episode increased state online vigilance, although this immediate effect emerged primarily among individuals with moderate to high levels of IU. Behavioral results showed a differentiated pattern: the primary confirmatory behavioral outcome, online-monitoring frequency, supported the Condition × IU interaction; intensity-based indicators such as first-check latency, phone-checking frequency, and total phone-use duration provided directionally consistent supplementary support; and evidence from dichotomous behavioral indicators was weaker. Thus, a more cautious conclusion is that, among high-IU individuals, being phubbed is more consistently followed by heightened state online vigilance, more frequent digital information monitoring, and stronger subsequent phone engagement.

First, the present study advances understanding of the antecedents of online vigilance. Prior research has mainly situated online vigilance within the POPC environment and emphasized its close relationship with mobile media availability, repeated exposure to online communication opportunities, and the resulting learning of attentional and response patterns [1–3]. The present findings further indicate that online vigilance not only contains a relatively stable component shaped over time by a continuously connected environment, but may also be activated immediately by specific relational events. Study 2 is especially informative in showing that phubbing during face-to-face interaction can increase state online vigilance over a short period.

Second, the findings are broadly consistent with the interpretive framework of social monitoring system theory. This theory proposes that when belonging needs are threatened, individuals become more sensitive to socially diagnostic cues in order to reassess their social position and seek opportunities for restoring connection [15]. In the POPC environment, notifications, social updates, online status indicators, and quantifiable feedback constitute a continuously available system of digital social cues [19, 20]. Prior research also indicates that phubbing is often accompanied by basic need threat, negative mood, and reduced trust [10–13]. The present research further suggests that being phubbed is not only related to impaired interaction but may also be associated with subsequent increased attention to online cues, digital communication opportunities, and social feedback information. However, this pattern only indicates that the results are consistent with social monitoring system theory. It does not demonstrate that belonging threat, perceived exclusion, relational uncertainty, or digital cue sensitivity operated as mediating processes.

Another contribution concerns the role of IU. IU is closely related to anxiety, worry, threat-interpretation bias, and information seeking and checking under uncertainty [37, 38], and in digital-media contexts it is also related to problematic smartphone use, problematic social media use, and FoMO [36, 39, 40]. The present results suggest that IU may influence whether relational ambiguity is processed as an uncertain event that requires additional cues for confirmation. In Study 2, phubbing did not significantly increase state online vigilance at low IU, but the effect became significant and stronger at moderate and high IU. Nevertheless, because the present research did not simultaneously measure social anxiety, attachment anxiety, rejection sensitivity, fear of negative evaluation, neuroticism, or baseline smartphone dependence, the current findings cannot demonstrate the specificity of IU, nor can they rule out the possibility that IU partially proxies broader anxiety-related or interpersonal sensitivity processes.

In addition, the weaker response among low-IU individuals may also be related to how they understand norms of co-present phone use. Co-present phone use is not necessarily experienced as negative. It is more likely to be regarded as phubbing or relational interference only when it violates expected attention, responsiveness, or joint participation [52–54]. Low-IU individuals may therefore be more likely to interpret phone involvement during interaction as a situationally understandable and normatively tolerable co-present disturbance rather than as an unresolved relational threat requiring further confirmation.

Importantly, different types of behavioral indicators in Study 2 were not fully consistent. The strongest support was for the conclusion that after being phubbed, especially among high-IU individuals, participants were more likely to show heightened state online vigilance, more frequent digital information monitoring, and stronger subsequent phone engagement. Dichotomous behavioral indicators and the specific opening of social apps did not provide equally stable support. Thus, the post-phubbing response is better understood as a relatively general digitally oriented state rather than as directly demonstrated social information seeking or narrowly defined social repair behavior. This interpretation is also consistent with research showing that broader online coping behaviors may follow social threat [34, 55]. However, the present research did not directly measure monitoring motives, digital cue sensitivity, social information seeking, or coping motives; therefore, the social-monitoring explanation remains based primarily on theoretical inference and prior literature.

The theoretical contributions of the present research are threefold. First, it extends the antecedents of online vigilance from technological environments and media experience to relational events occurring in face-to-face interaction, and it refines online vigilance as a psychological response that contains both background and state-like components. Second, it shows that social monitoring system theory can provide an interpretive framework for understanding the meaning of digital social cues after disrupted face-to-face interaction, while acknowledging that monitoring motives, digital cue sensitivity, and social information seeking were not directly tested. Third, it reveals that IU may influence when relationally ambiguous cues are translated into stronger attention to digital cues, rather than merely increasing the risk of general digital use.

The practical implications of the present findings should be understood cautiously. The current results only indicate that, in young adult samples and laboratory interaction conditions, being phubbed may be accompanied by subsequent higher online vigilance and phone engagement. Seemingly minor phubbing behavior may have interactional costs, but these findings should not be directly generalized to specific contexts such as classrooms, organizations, counseling settings, or intimate relationships. High-IU individuals may show stronger digital orientation after being phubbed, but this finding is better viewed as a clue for future research and potential intervention development rather than as an application recommendation that has already been directly supported.

The present research cannot reduce the aftereffects of being phubbed to a single process. The experimental manipulation simultaneously included visible neglect, attentional withdrawal, reduced responsiveness, and the presence of phone use in a co-present setting. It may therefore have activated a set of intertwined interpersonal processes rather than one purified mechanism. The present study emphasizes ambiguity of relational meaning mainly because the moderation pattern involving IU in Study 2 fits the logic of uncertainty-related meaning processing, not because other mechanisms have been empirically excluded. In other words, the current evidence supports the conclusion that after being phubbed, especially among high-IU individuals, people are more likely to show sustained attention to digital cues and stronger subsequent phone engagement. However, the present research cannot determine whether this effect is primarily driven by ambiguity of relational meaning, social exclusion, expectancy violation, attentional conflict, or behavioral modeling.

Several limitations should be noted. First, Study 1 used a cross-sectional self-report design. It can establish ecological associations, but it cannot rule out perceptual sensitivity bias or reverse explanations. Second, although Study 2 provides stronger evidence for the short-term effect of phubbing on state online vigilance through experimental manipulation and provides preliminary structural, discriminant, and incremental validity evidence for the state-adapted OVS, these validation results are still limited to the same experimental sample. Moreover, the study did not directly measure relational uncertainty, monitoring motives, digital cue sensitivity, desire to disengage, or specific coping motives, so the mechanistic interpretation remains inferential. Third, Study 2 was not preregistered; laboratory demand characteristics may have been present; and confederate behavior, although trained, may still have involved some standardization variability. Fourth, the waiting period was short, so dichotomous indicators may have been influenced by opportunity thresholds and situational contingency and may have had limited statistical power because they contained less information. Fifth, the sample consisted mainly of young adults in a Chinese cultural context. Although Study 2 controlled for baseline phone-use tendency, it did not simultaneously measure trait smartphone dependence, social anxiety, or trait phubbing. Thus, the external validity of the findings and the exclusion of alternative vulnerability factors require further testing in future research.

Conclusion

The present research shows that after being phubbed in face-to-face interaction, individuals are more likely to show sustained attention to online content and digital communication, and this pattern is especially pronounced among individuals high in IU. Specifically, this change is more stably reflected in heightened state online vigilance, increased digital information-monitoring frequency, and some intensity-based phone-engagement indicators, rather than in all phone behaviors equally. The influence of being phubbed may therefore extend beyond the impairment of the immediate interaction and may relate to subsequent attention shifts toward online cues. This study expands the outcome domain of phubbing research and provides new empirical support for understanding the connection between offline relational threat and sustained online vigilance in the digital age.
