Tx and Rx IQ Imbalance Compensation for JCAS in 5G NR
Andreas Meingassner, Oliver Lang, Moritz Tockner, Bernhard Plaimer, Matthias Wagner, Günther Lindorfer, Michael Hofstadler, Mario Huemer

Abstract
Beside traditional communications, JCAS ( JCAS ) is gaining increasing relevance as a key enabler for next-generation wireless systems. The ability to accurately transmit and receive data is the basis for high-speed communications and precise sensing, where a fundamental requirement is an accurate I ( I ) and Q ( Q ) modulation. For sensing, imperfections in IQ modulation lead to two critical issues in the RDM ( RDM ) in form of an increased noise floor and the presence of ghost objects, degrading the accuracy and reliability of the information in the RDM . This paper presents a low-complex estimation and compensation method to mitigate the IQ imbalance effects. This is achieved by utilizing, amongst others, the leakage signal, which is the direct signal from the transmitter to the receiver path, and is typically the strongest signal component in the RDM . The parameters of the IQ imbalance suppression structure are estimated based on a mixed complex-/real-valued bilinear filter approach, that considers IQ imbalance in the transmitter and the receiver of the JCAS -capable UE ( UE ). The UE uses a 5G NR ( NR )-compliant OFDM ( OFDM ) waveform with the system configuration assumed to be predefined from the communication side. To assess the effectiveness of the proposed approach, simulations are conducted, illustrating the performance in the suppression of IQ imbalance introduced distortions in the RDM .

4.3
Ambiguity of the Estimation Process

Using the bilinear model of (
14
), it can be seen that for a given
\mathbf{X}_{i}
, the estimates
\hat{\mathbf{f}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{g}}
are not unique. This can be illustrated by extending the equation as

\displaystyle h_{\text{IQ},i}

\displaystyle=\lambda\hat{\mathbf{f}}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{X}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{g}}\frac{1}{\lambda^{*}},

(20)

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is an arbitrary complex factor fulfilling
\lambda\neq 0
. Hence, the same output of the bilinear model can be described by a scaled
\hat{\mathbf{f}}
and inversely scaled
\hat{\mathbf{g}}
. Interestingly, for the derived bilinear model in (
15
) it turns out that not one but two closely related scaling factors can be identified, which is shown in the following. For this derivation
(
15
) is given with general matrix and vector elements
x_{i},f_{i}
and
g_{i}
to limit the length of the equations

\displaystyle h_{\text{IQ},i}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}^{*}&f_{2}^{*}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&jx_{1}&x_{2}&jx_{2}\\
x_{3}&-jx_{3}&x_{4}&-jx_{4}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re(g_{1})\\
\Im(g_{1})\\
\Re(g_{2})\\
\Im(g_{2})\end{bmatrix}.

(21)

By scaling
f_{1}^{*}
with
\lambda_{1}
and
f_{2}^{*}
with
\lambda_{2}
, (
21
) can be written as

\displaystyle h_{\text{IQ},i}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}f_{1}^{*}&\lambda_{2}f_{2}^{*}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&jx_{1}&x_{2}&jx_{2}\\
x_{3}&-jx_{3}&x_{4}&-jx_{4}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\tilde{g}_{1})\\
\Im(\tilde{g}_{1})\\
\Re(\tilde{g}_{2})\\
\Im(\tilde{g}_{2})\end{bmatrix},

(22)

with unknown parameters
\tilde{g}_{1}
and
\tilde{g}_{2}
. It will be shown that the scaling of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
can be compensated by choosing
\tilde{g}_{1}
and
\tilde{g}_{2}
as scaled versions of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
with appropriately chosen scaling factors. Rewriting (
21
) and (
22
) and setting them equal produces

\displaystyle h_{\text{IQ},i}

\displaystyle=f_{1}^{*}x_{1}g_{1}+f_{1}^{*}x_{2}g_{2}+f_{2}^{*}x_{3}g_{1}^{*}+f_{2}^{*}x_{4}g_{2}^{*}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}f_{1}^{*}x_{1}\tilde{g}_{1}+\lambda_{1}f_{1}^{*}x_{2}\tilde{g}_{2}+\lambda_{2}f_{2}^{*}x_{3}\tilde{g}_{1}^{*}+\lambda_{2}f_{2}^{*}x_{4}\tilde{g}_{2}^{*}

(23)

yielding

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{1}

\displaystyle=g_{1}\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}},~\tilde{g}_{2}=g_{2}\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}},~\tilde{g}_{1}^{*}=g_{1}^{*}\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}},~\tilde{g}_{2}^{*}=g_{2}^{*}\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}}.

(24)

This shows that equality holds if
\lambda_{1}
equals
\lambda_{2}^{*}
, and proofs that the IQ imbalance parameters are ambiguous after the bilinear estimation process, however, the IQ imbalance afflicted channel
\mathbf{h}_{\text{IQ}}
is not affected by this ambiguity since these scaling factors cancel. The scaled parameters in
\mathbf{f}
and
\mathbf{g}
are passed to the subsequently discussed compensation approach to mitigate the IQ imbalance effects in the
RDM
. It will be shown, that the increased noise floor can be reduced drastically.

6.3
MSE Evaluation

The complex amplitudes of the objects in the
RDM
is of importance, e.g., for digital beam forming in a
MIMO
(
MIMO
) setting. Especially for, e.g.,
DOA
estimation, the reconstruction performance of the complex amplitudes is crucial. The true complex amplitudes are stored in a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{obj}}\times N_{\text{sim}}}
, where each row corresponds to an object, and each column corresponds to a simulation. The number of surrounding objects and the number of simulations is given with
N_{\text{obj}}
and
N_{\text{iter}}
, respectively. With the estimated complex amplitudes stored in the matrix
\hat{\mathbf{A}}
, the
MSE
(
MSE
) is evaluated by

\displaystyle\text{MSE}=\frac{1}{N_{\text{obj}}N_{\text{sim}}}\sum_{o=1}^{N_{\text{obj}}}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{\text{sim}}}|[\mathbf{A}]_{o,s}-[\hat{\mathbf{A}}]_{o,s}|^{2}.

(30)

Additionally, the averaged amplitude error
\epsilon
and phase error
\Delta\phi
is investigated via

\displaystyle\epsilon=\frac{1}{N_{\text{obj}}N_{\text{sim}}}\sum_{o=1}^{N_{\text{obj}}}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{\text{sim}}}\frac{|[\mathbf{A}]_{o,s}|-|[\hat{\mathbf{A}}]_{o,s}|}{|[\mathbf{A}]_{o,s}|},

(31)

and

\displaystyle\Delta\phi=\frac{1}{N_{\text{obj}}N_{\text{sim}}}\sum_{o=1}^{N_{\text{obj}}}\sum_{s=1}^{N_{\text{sim}}}\left|\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\Im([\hat{\mathbf{A}}]_{o,s}/[\mathbf{A}]_{o,s})}{\Re([\hat{\mathbf{A}}]_{o,s}/[\mathbf{A}]_{o,s})}\right)\right|.

(32)

This is done for each discussed method, and the
MSE
values corresponding to the 3GPP-based, and the literature-based IQ imbalance scenario are given in Table
5
and Table
6
, respectively. For that, 600 simulations and three different objects were evaluated (excluding the leakage). For each simulation run, the
SNR
was randomly chosen from a uniform distribution between
40\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}
and
50\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}
. It can be observed, that the Wiener approaches clearly outperform the other implemented algorithms. With that, it can be summarized that the corresponding complex amplitude of an object in the surrounding can be accurately reconstructed after IQ imbalance compensation. Simulations have shown that this is the case for each single simulated object as well, however, the averaged values are given in this paper. This is explicitly the case for objects with a relative velocity of
0\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{s}
, where the ghost object directly interferes with the true reflection. This might, e.g., provide the basis for
DOA
implementations.

7
Conclusion

This paper introduced a low-complexity method to compensate IQ imbalance effects for
JCAS
systems. Unlike conventional approaches, which often struggle with leakage, our method exploits this effect as a source of information. The proposed approach is built on a mixed complex-/real-valued bilinear model, for which several applicable algorithms were investigated. The simulation results are based on the 5G
NR
standard ensuring practical applicability in real-world applications. With these simulations, we demonstrated the performance in IQ imbalance compensation. Notably, the
MSE
evaluation highlights a precise spectral peak reconstruction, while the
SFDR
evaluation illustrates the noise floor reduction. Especially the Wiener approaches outperform competitive methods and provide efficient and robust solutions for IQ imbalance compensation.
