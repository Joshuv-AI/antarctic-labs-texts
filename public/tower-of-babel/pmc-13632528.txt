Short-chain fatty acid-producing commensals: Key modulators of AD neuroinflammation via the microbiota-gut-brain axis
Authors: Zongxin Ling, Shengyu Huang, Yiwen Cheng, Xia Liu, Xiaocui Xu, Yangtian Chen, Pingping Hu, Lingbin Wu, Guangyong Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights●Depletion of SCFA-producing gut bacteria is a consistent feature of Alzheimer’s disease across diverse populations.●SCFAs suppress neuroinflammation via GPCR signaling, HDAC inhibition, and metabolic reprogramming of microglia and astrocytes.●SCFAs modulate Aβ deposition, tau pathology, and synaptic integrity through multi-layered regulatory mechanisms.●Restoring SCFA-mediated gut-brain communication represents a promising disease-modifying strategy for early AD intervention.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) stands as the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder worldwide, imposing an escalating burden on global healthcare systems. Current estimates indicate that over 55 million individuals live with dementia, with AD accounting for approximately 60–70% of cases, and this number is projected to triple by 2050 as populations age (Knopman et al., 2021; Scheltens et al., 2021; Alzheimer's disease facts and figures, 2023). The pathological hallmarks of AD, extracellular amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau, have dominated research paradigms for decades. However, the amyloid cascade hypothesis, which posits that Aβ deposition initiates a pathological cascade culminating in neurodegeneration, has faced mounting scrutiny due to the repeated failures of clinical trials targeting amyloid clearance (Knopman et al., 2021; Mullane and Williams, 2013; Zhang et al., 2025; Soejitno et al., 2015). Despite the recent accelerated approvals of aducanumab and lecanemab by regulatory agencies, these anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies demonstrate modest clinical efficacy, significant adverse effects including amyloid-related imaging abnormalities, and uncertain long-term benefits, underscoring the limitations of a purely amyloid-centric therapeutic approach (Shi et al., 2022; Tipton, 2024; Kim et al., 2025; Greenberg et al., 2025; Salemme et al., 2023; Van Dyck et al., 2023).

The persistent gap between pathological understanding and clinical outcomes has catalyzed a fundamental reconceptualization of AD pathogenesis (Zhang et al., 2025). Accumulating evidence positions neuroinflammation not merely as a secondary consequence of protein aggregation but as a central driver of disease progression (Leng and Edison, 2021; Thakur et al., 2023; Heneka et al., 2015). Current evidence, while compelling, remains primarily correlational and preclinical, necessitating cautious interpretation of therapeutic claims. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses have revealed disease-associated microglia (DAM) as a distinct activation state characterized by upregulated expression of genes involved in lipid metabolism and phagocytosis, including Trem2, Apoe, and Cst7 (Keren-Shaul et al., 2017; Krasemann et al., 2017). The identification of TREM2 and APOE as major genetic risk factors for late-onset AD, second only to the amyloid precursor protein and presenilin mutations in familial forms, firmly establishes innate immune dysregulation as a core pathological component (Shi and Holtzman, 2018; Ulland et al., 2017). Microglia transition from homeostatic surveillance states to DAM phenotypes in response to environmental cues, yet the chronicity of this activation in AD perpetuates a deleterious inflammatory cycle characterized by sustained release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, complement activation, and synaptic pruning (Han et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2024). Concurrently, reactive astrocytes undergo transformation to neurotoxic A1 states, driven by activated microglia through cytokine signaling, further amplifying neuroinflammation and compromising neuronal viability (Liddelow et al., 2017; Zamanian et al., 2012).

Parallel to the immune-centric reframing of AD, the gut-brain axis has emerged as a critical communication network linking peripheral physiology to central nervous system (CNS) function. This bidirectional conduit encompasses neural pathways via the vagus nerve, immune signaling through cytokines and microbial metabolites, endocrine routes involving hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis modulation, and metabolic interactions including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) production (Cryan et al., 2019; Fülling et al., 2019; Zhuang et al., 2024; Lei et al., 2025). The gut microbiota, comprising approximately 1013-1014 microorganisms, actively participates in host immune education, barrier integrity maintenance, and neuroactive compound synthesis (Lei et al., 2025; Cerovic et al., 2019). Dysbiosis, defined as compositional and functional alterations in the microbial community, has been consistently documented in AD patients across diverse geographic and ethnic populations, characterized by reduced microbial diversity, depletion of beneficial commensals, and expansion of pro-inflammatory taxa (Wu et al., 2021; Ling et al., 2020; Ferreiro et al., 2023).

Among the myriad microbial metabolites, SCFAs have garnered particular attention as pivotal mediators of gut-brain communication. SCFAs, primarily acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are generated through the anaerobic fermentation of dietary fiber and resistant starch by specialized bacterial consortia (Kasubuchi et al., 2015; O'riordan et al., 2022). Key producers include Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Roseburia species, Eubacterium hallii, and select Bifidobacterium strains, which collectively convert complex carbohydrates into these bioactive metabolites through the phosphotransbutyrylase-butyrate kinase pathway and related enzymatic cascades (Louis and Flint, 2017; Vital et al., 2014; Ling et al., 2026). Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of fecal metagenomic and metabolomic data have demonstrated consistent reductions in SCFA-producing taxa in AD cohorts compared to cognitively normal controls, with the magnitude of depletion correlating with disease severity and cognitive impairment (Oso et al., 2026; Susmitha and Kumar, 2023). This functional deficit in microbial metabolite production represents a mechanistic link between gut dysbiosis and neuroinflammatory pathology.

SCFAs exert pleiotropic effects on host physiology through multiple interconnected mechanisms. As signaling molecules, they activate G-protein coupled receptors including FFAR2 (GPR43), FFAR3 (GPR41), and GPR109A expressed on enteroendocrine cells, immune cells, and glial populations, initiating downstream cascades that modulate inflammation and metabolism (Stilling et al., 2016; Barki et al., 2022). Butyrate, in particular, functions as a potent inhibitor of class I and IIa histone deacetylases (HDACs), thereby influencing chromatin accessibility and gene expression patterns in immune and neural cells (Krautkramer et al., 2016; Donohoe et al., 2011). Additionally, SCFAs reinforce intestinal barrier integrity through tight junction protein upregulation, promote peripheral regulatory T cell differentiation, and modulate blood-brain barrier permeability, collectively shaping the neuroimmune environment (Furusawa et al., 2013; Fock and Parnova, 2023; Kearns, 2024).

The convergence of these observations positions SCFA-producing bacteria as active regulators of neuroinflammation in AD, operating through the gut-brain axis to influence disease trajectory. This review integrates current evidence regarding microbiome signatures of SCFA deficiency, mechanisms of SCFA delivery to CNS, molecular actions on neuroinflammatory cell states, and translational strategies targeting microbiota-derived metabolites. We critically examine the causal relationships between SCFA signaling and AD pathology, address technical challenges in assessing functional SCFA activity, and propose a framework for precision microbiome interventions tailored to disease stage and genetic context. By synthesizing insights from metagenomics, metabolomics, neuroimmunology, and clinical trial design, we aim to advance the understanding of how microbial metabolites shape neuroinflammatory outcomes and to delineate the path toward mechanism-based therapeutic development.

Gut dysbiosis and impaired SCFA biosynthetic capacity in AD

Taxonomic features of gut dysbiosis across multi-ethnic AD cohorts

High-throughput metagenomic sequencing across geographically diverse cohorts establishes taxonomic dysbiosis as a core pathological feature of AD. Rather than a random drift in microbial populations, this dysbiosis follows a highly reproducible trajectory characterized by a contraction of microbial α-diversity and a structural shift from symbiotic fermenters to pro-inflammatory pathobionts, though the specific taxa affected show some geographic and population-specific variation. Recent systematic integrations of 16S rRNA and shotgun metagenomic data reveal that this compositional restructuring transcends ethnic, dietary, and geographic boundaries (Ling et al., 2020; Oso et al., 2026; Li et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2019; Troci et al., 2024; Verhaar et al., 2021; Vogt et al., 2017; Jung et al., 2022; Cirstea et al., 2022; Khedr et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2026). American, European, and East Asian AD cohorts consistently exhibit a systemic depletion of beneficial commensals juxtaposed against an expansion of opportunistic Gram-negative taxa, notably within the Proteobacteria phylum and Escherichia-Shigella clades (Ling et al., 2020; Ferreiro et al., 2023; Ling et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2019; Troci et al., 2024; Verhaar et al., 2021; Vogt et al., 2017; Jung et al., 2022; Cirstea et al., 2022; Khedr et al., 2022). While these taxonomic associations are consistently replicated, they remain correlational; longitudinal studies tracking individuals from preclinical stages through dementia are essential to establish whether microbial depletion precedes or follows disease onset.

The most prominent and reproducible alteration involves the decline of butyrate-producing bacteria belonging to the Firmicutes phylum, particularly members of the Clostridial clusters IV and XIVa. Multiple independent studies have reported consistent reductions in F. prausnitzii, Roseburia species, and E. rectale in AD patients relative to healthy elderly controls (Oso et al., 2026; Li et al., 2024; Gupta et al., 2025; Sepulveda et al., 2023). Notably, the magnitude and specific taxonomic composition of dysbiosis vary across studies, reflecting differences in dietary patterns, genetic backgrounds, and methodological approaches including sequencing platforms, variable regions targeted for 16S rRNA analysis, and bioinformatics pipelines. Ling et al. demonstrated that Chinese AD patients exhibited not only decreased abundance of Faecalibacterium but also a functional shift toward lactate-producing metabolism, suggesting that dysbiosis extends beyond simple compositional changes to encompass altered metabolic capabilities (Ling et al., 2020; Singh et al., 2026). Similarly, investigations in Puerto Rican populations identified reduced levels of Roseburia, Anaerostipes, and Ruminococcus in AD patients with comorbid diabetes, demonstrating the intersection of metabolic and neurodegenerative pathologies (Soler-Llompart et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2017).

Concomitant with the loss of SCFA producers, AD microbiomes frequently display enrichment of pro-inflammatory Gram-negative bacteria, including members of Proteobacteria and Escherichia-Shigella groups (Li et al., 2024; Donaldson et al., 2025). Donaldson et al. reported elevated levels of pro-inflammatory gut bacteria in fecal samples from individuals living with AD, correlating with markers of systemic inflammation (Li et al., 2024). This reciprocal relationship, reduction of anti-inflammatory butyrate producers and expansion of endotoxin-producing Enterobacteriaceae, creates a permissive environment for chronic low-grade inflammation that may propagate to CNS via circulating cytokines and microbial products. Importantly, the observed reductions in butyrate‑producing taxa may partially reflect disease‑associated changes in eating behavior and nutritional status rather than primary causal events. Late‑stage AD patients frequently exhibit reduced dietary intake, altered food preferences, and swallowing difficulties that can secondarily impact the gut microbial ecosystem. Furthermore, the extensive use of medications in this population, including antipsychotics, cholinesterase inhibitors, and proton pump inhibitors, exerts independent effects on microbial composition.

However, interpreting these microbiome signatures requires careful consideration of confounding variables. Dietary fiber intake, medication use (particularly antibiotics, proton pump inhibitors, and anticholinergics), geographic location, and comorbid metabolic conditions substantially influence gut microbial composition (Oso et al., 2026; Nagpal et al., 2019). The observed reductions in butyrate-producing taxa may partially reflect disease-associated changes in eating behavior and nutritional status rather than primary causal events. Longitudinal profiling of individuals transitioning from subjective cognitive decline to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and overt AD dementia is currently resolving these confounding factors, confirming that the decline in specific microbial functional guilds represents an early, potentially causal event rather than merely a consequence of terminal disease cachexia.

Functional decline of SCFA-producing microbial consortium in AD gut ecology

The functional consequences of microbiome alterations in AD center on the impaired capacity for SCFA biosynthesis, particularly butyrate production. This functional depletion represents a more meaningful index of dysbiosis than taxonomic shifts alone, as SCFA production is a distributed metabolic trait involving multiple phylogenetically distinct but metabolically cooperative taxa. F. prausnitzii, the most abundant butyrate producer in the healthy colon, is consistently depleted across the AD spectrum, with reductions documented from preclinical stages to dementia (Ling et al., 2020; Chilton et al., 2024; Kachoueiyan et al., 2025). This species, belonging to Clostridial cluster IV, utilizes the acetyl-CoA pathway to convert acetate and lactate into butyrate, contributing substantially to colonic butyrate pools (Louis and Flint, 2017; Ramos Meyers et al., 2022; Fan et al., 2026). Beyond butyrate provision, F. prausnitzii exerts direct immunomodulation through the microbial anti-inflammatory molecule (MAM) that inhibits NF-κB and strengthens barrier integrity, and strain EXL01 induces IL-10 production in human monocytes via mitochondrial respiration-dependent metabolic reprogramming (Danne et al., 2026; Sokol et al., 2008). It also enhances intestinal IgA responses and tight junction expression (Qin et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2020; Hiippala et al., 2018). Thus, its loss may jointly compromise HDAC inhibition, barrier maintenance, and systemic anti-inflammatory tone. Importantly, comparative genomics reveal that strains isolated from healthy individuals carry orthologs absent from MCI-derived strains, conferring differential protection against Aβ-induced cognitive deficits in mice (Chilton et al., 2024), indicating that functional depletion reflects both quantitative loss and qualitative erosion of strain-level protective capacity.

Roseburia species (R. hominis, R. intestinalis) and E. rectale, members of Lachnospiraceae that produce butyrate via butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA-transferase and butyryl-CoA pathways (Hillman et al., 2020; Faden, 2022), are among the most consistently reduced taxa in AD cohorts across Western and Asian populations (Ling et al., 2020; Donaldson et al., 2025; Rob et al., 2025; Duncan et al., 2007). A species-level metagenomic study of MCI identified E. rectale as one of 59 differentially abundant species correlated with amyloid burden and plasma pTau181, while congeneric species in Bacteroides and Ruminococcus showed opposing associations, emphasizing the necessity of species-level resolution (Fan et al., 2025). These butyrate producers depend on metabolic cross-feeding: primary fermenters such as Bifidobacterium supply acetate and lactate that Roseburia and Eubacterium convert to butyrate (Rivière et al., 2016). Consequently, disruption of this syntrophic network can impair butyrate output even when individual producer populations persist, making the functional integrity of the entire fermentative guild more informative than single-taxon abundance.

The direction of Bifidobacterium change in AD is inconsistent across studies, with reports of both increases and decreases likely reflecting disease stage, diet, medication, and methodological variability (Ling et al., 2020; Rob et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2025). Crucially, elevated Bifidobacterium in the absence of adequate butyrate-producing cross-feeders can lead to lactate accumulation rather than net butyrate gain, a state of fermentative decoupling that may exacerbate gut inflammation (Ling et al., 2020; Rivière et al., 2016). Akkermansia muciniphila, consistently found to be increased in AD, exhibits context-dependent effects, correlating with reduced amyloid burden in some subgroups but with enhanced mucin degradation and altered permeability in others (Rob et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2025; Lei et al., 2023). These divergent patterns underscore the principle that the SCFA-producing guild functions as an integrated metabolic network; reductions in butyrate production capacity can result from primary producer loss, disrupted cross-feeding, or metabolic flux diversion by competing taxa, and must be evaluated at the network level.

Recent clinical and functional metagenomic studies have directly tied SCFA depletion to AD biomarkers. In a cohort of 287 participants across the AD continuum, fecal propionate and propionate-producing bacteria were inversely associated with amyloid PET status, and higher SCFA levels predicted slower cognitive decline and slower plasma pTau217 accumulation, especially in females, with propionate statistically mediating the relationship between microbiota composition and CSF biomarkers (Kuehn et al., 2025). In a longitudinal 3 × Tg-AD mouse study, the age-dependent decline in butyrate-producing communities and cecal butyrate, driven by loss of the acetyl-CoA pathway, preceded hippocampal oxidative stress, tau hyperphosphorylation, and memory deficits, and oral tributyrin initiated at six months preserved histone acetylation and attenuated both neuropathology and cognitive impairment (Chilton et al., 2024). Further, metagenomic pathway analysis of cognitively unimpaired adults with high Aβ burden revealed a shift from biosynthetic to fermentative metabolism, suggesting a fundamental reorganization that limits net SCFA output (Dissanayaka et al., 2026). Biosynthesis, systemic trafficking and cerebral bioavailability of gut-derived SCFAs

The functional impact of gut-derived SCFAs on central neuroinflammation is fundamentally constrained by their spatiotemporal pharmacokinetics. A critical vulnerability in many models of the gut-brain axis is the assumption that luminal metabolite concentrations linearly predict brain parenchymal exposure. In reality, the journey of an SCFA from colonic biosynthesis to glial target engagement represents a highly regulated sequence involving microbial syntrophy, intensive epithelial oxidation, hepatic first-pass metabolism, and selective BBB transport (Dalile et al., 2019; Cao et al., 2025; Majumdar et al., 2023). Understanding these anatomical and biochemical checkpoints is essential for defining how dysbiosis in AD translates into functional neuroimmune deficits.

Microbial enzymatic pathways governing colonic SCFA biosynthesis

The production of SCFAs in the human gastrointestinal tract represents a complex interplay between dietary fiber intake and the metabolic capabilities of the gut microbiota. The three principal SCFAs, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are generated through distinct biochemical pathways that reflect both the phylogenetic diversity of microbial communities and the chemical complexity of available substrates (Frolova et al., 2022). Recent large-scale metagenomic reconstructions have identified key discriminatory enzymes and binary genomic signatures, such as the but gene cluster for butyrate, pct and lcdA for propionate, and key acetyl-CoA pathway genes for acetate, that now permit robust prediction of SCFA production capacities across diverse bacterial taxa from metagenomic data alone (Dissanayaka et al., 2026; Anand et al., 2016). This functional prediction framework reveals that the colonic butyrate-producing capacity is heavily concentrated in phylogenetically narrow groups, making it disproportionately susceptible to ecological disruption.

Acetate, the most abundant SCFA in the colon, is produced through multiple pathways including the Wood-Ljungdahl pathway, pyruvate fermentation, and amino acid catabolism (Louis and Flint, 2017; Miller and Wolin, 1996). The heterogeneity of acetate-producing pathways contributes to its widespread distribution across bacterial phyla, with Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes representing major contributors (Fan et al., 2026). Propionate synthesis occurs primarily via the succinate pathway (dominant in Bacteroidetes) and the acrylate pathway (characteristic of certain Firmicutes such as Coprococcus species) (Reichardt et al., 2018). Butyrate production, in contrast, is largely restricted to specialized Firmicutes including F. prausnitzii, Roseburia species, and select Eubacterium strains, which utilize the butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA-transferase pathway (Duncan et al., 2007). Thus, butyrate biosynthesis occupies a phylogenetically narrow niche, making it disproportionately vulnerable to ecological disruption, whereas acetate and propionate benefit from greater pathway redundancy. In AD, the functional metagenomic shift from these biosynthetic routes toward methanogenic and fermentative pathways correlates with reduced fecal butyrate and increased Aβ burden, even in cognitively unimpaired individuals (Dissanayaka et al., 2026).

The stoichiometry of SCFA production is strongly influenced by substrate availability, with different dietary carbohydrates favoring distinct fermentation end products. Human fecal microbiota studies have demonstrated that specific non-digestible carbohydrates differentially stimulate microbial groups, and pH represents a critical environmental determinant: butyrate production is favored under mildly acidic conditions (pH 5.5-6.5), whereas propionate accumulation predominates at higher pH values (Louis and Flint, 2017). This pH-dependent metabolic partitioning has important implications for colonic health, as butyrate serves as the primary energy source for colonocytes while propionate is predominantly absorbed and directed toward hepatic gluconeogenesis (Den Besten et al., 2013).

Microbial cross-feeding networks further complicate the metabolic landscape of SCFA production. Acetate produced by Bacteroides species can be converted to butyrate by butyrate-producing bacteria that lack the capacity for direct polysaccharide fermentation, establishing syntrophic relationships that enhance community metabolic efficiency (Louis and Flint, 2017). Similarly, lactate produced by Bifidobacterium species serves as a substrate for butyrate producers, linking primary fermentation to secondary metabolite generation (Belenguer et al., 2006). In the AD gut, the concurrent loss of both primary fermenters and butyrate-producing taxa can trigger “fermentative decoupling”, where acetate and lactate accumulate without being converted to butyrate, resulting in lower luminal pH that further suppresses butyrogenesis (Fan et al., 2025; Dissanayaka et al., 2026). These interspecies metabolic exchanges underscore the importance of community composition and ecological interactions in determining net SCFA output, especially in the context of neurodegeneration-associated dysbiosis.

Intestinal uptake and hepatic metabolic clearance of circulating SCFAs

Following microbial production in the colonic lumen, SCFAs must traverse the intestinal epithelium to enter systemic circulation. This transport process is mediated by multiple carrier systems with distinct substrate specificities and cellular localizations (Gill et al., 2005). The monocarboxylate transporter family (MCT1/SLC16A1 and MCT4/SLC16A3) and the sodium-coupled monocarboxylate transporter (SMCT1/SLC5A8) represent the principal pathways for SCFA absorption across the intestinal epithelium (Sivaprakasam et al., 2017).

MCT1 is expressed on both apical and basolateral membranes of colonocytes, facilitating bidirectional SCFA flux, whereas MCT4 is predominantly localized to the basolateral membrane and mediates efflux into the portal circulation (Gill et al., 2005). SMCT1, in contrast, is exclusively apical and functions as a high-affinity, sodium-dependent uptake system that is particularly important for butyrate absorption (Iwanaga et al., 2006). The complementary distribution of these transporters, SMCT1 mediating luminal uptake and MCT4 facilitating basolateral efflux, ensures efficient vectorial transport of SCFAs from colonic lumen to portal blood (Sivaprakasam et al., 2017; Schönfeld and Wojtczak, 2016).

The differential affinity of transport systems for individual SCFAs has important physiological consequences. Butyrate exhibits the highest affinity for SMCT1 (Km approximately 5-10 mM), followed by propionate and acetate (Schönfeld and Wojtczak, 2016). This selectivity ensures preferential absorption of butyrate, the primary fuel for colonocytes, while permitting substantial propionate and acetate entry into portal circulation (Sivaprakasam et al., 2017). However, in inflammatory conditions relevant to AD, proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α can downregulate SMCT1 expression, potentially compromising butyrate absorption and reducing its systemic availability (Martel, 2025). Recent evidence further demonstrates that the gut microbiota itself can modulate transporter expression through metabolite-sensing receptors; for instance, butyrate acting via PPAR-γ can upregulate its own transporter, creating a positive-feedback loop that may be disrupted when butyrate-producing bacteria are depleted (Bultman, 2014).

Once absorbed, SCFAs enter the portal circulation and are subject to substantial hepatic first-pass metabolism. The liver efficiently extracts propionate and butyrate from portal blood, with clearance rates exceeding 90% for butyrate and 70–80% for propionate (Bloemen et al., 2009; Kristensen et al., 1998). This extensive hepatic extraction limits systemic bioavailability of these SCFAs, with peripheral plasma concentrations typically in the low micromolar range for butyrate and propionate (Kirschner et al., 2021). Acetate, in contrast, undergoes minimal hepatic clearance and achieves systemic concentrations 10- to 50-fold higher than other SCFAs, typically ranging from 50 to 300 μM in peripheral blood (Den Besten et al., 2013).

The metabolic fate of extracted SCFAs differs substantially among the three major species. Propionate is predominantly utilized for hepatic gluconeogenesis, contributing substantially to fasting glucose production. Butyrate undergoes partial oxidation to ketone bodies (β-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate) and complete oxidation to CO2, serving as an alternative energy substrate during glucose limitation (Bloemen et al., 2010). Acetate that escapes hepatic extraction is distributed systemically and can be incorporated into host lipids and cholesterol or oxidized by peripheral tissues (Den Besten et al., 2013). Importantly, in AD patients and mouse models, reduced fecal butyrate producers correlate with significantly lower plasma butyrate concentrations (Chilton et al., 2024), confirming that the ecological depletion in the colon translates into diminished systemic SCFA delivery, which represents a key bottleneck for subsequent brain exposure..

Trans-BBB trafficking and compartmental distribution of SCFAs in CNS

The accessibility of circulating SCFAs to CNS is governed by their ability to cross the BBB, a specialized endothelial interface that restricts paracellular diffusion and regulates transcellular transport. The BBB maintains brain homeostasis through tight junction complexes comprising claudin-5, occludin, and zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), which seal interendothelial clefts and prevent passive diffusion of polar molecules (Haseloff et al., 2015).

SCFAs cross the BBB through both carrier-mediated transport and, to a limited extent, passive diffusion in their protonated forms (Pierre and Pellerin, 2005). Monocarboxylate transporters, particularly MCT1 and MCT4, are expressed at the BBB and mediate bidirectional SCFA flux between blood and brain interstitial fluid (Vijay and Morris, 2014). MCT1 is highly expressed on brain capillary endothelial cells and astrocytic endfeet, positioning it as the primary conduit for SCFA brain uptake (Pierre and Pellerin, 2005). The expression of MCT1 at the BBB is developmentally regulated and can be modulated by metabolic demands, with hypoxia and high-fat diet exposure increasing transporter abundance (Halestrap, 2013). Critically, recent post-mortem studies in AD cases have revealed a significant downregulation of MCT1 on brain microvessels, coincident with BBB breakdown and pericyte loss (Deelchand et al., 2009), suggesting that the already restricted brain access of SCFAs may be further compromised as AD progresses.

The efficiency of SCFA transport across the BBB varies with molecular size and lipophilicity. Acetate, being the smallest and most lipophilic SCFA, demonstrates the highest BBB permeability and achieves measurable concentrations in cerebrospinal fluid following systemic administration (Dienel et al., 2001). Butyrate and propionate exhibit lower permeability coefficients, consistent with their greater polarity and extensive protein binding in plasma (Moschen et al., 2012). Quantitative studies using radiolabeled SCFAs have estimated that less than 1% of circulating butyrate and propionate enters the brain under physiological conditions, whereas acetate brain uptake represents approximately 2–5% of plasma concentration (Dienel et al., 2001). These distinct permeability profiles result in fundamentally different brain bioavailability: acetate serves as a readily accessible systemic and CNS metabolite, while butyrate and propionate require substantially higher luminal production to achieve even modest parenchymal concentrations. A recent stable isotope tracing study in mice further demonstrated that colonic-derived acetate can be directly incorporated into brain metabolites, but the fractional contribution is modest and highly dependent on dietary fiber intake (Fock and Parnova, 2023).

Regional differences in SCFA uptake within the brain have been documented, with higher accumulation observed in circumventricular organs that lack a complete BBB, including the area postrema and median eminence (Pierre and Pellerin, 2005). These regions may serve as entry points for SCFA signaling to hypothalamic nuclei involved in energy homeostasis and neuroendocrine regulation (O'riordan et al., 2022). Beyond direct BBB penetration, SCFAs may influence brain function through indirect mechanisms including modulation of BBB integrity, peripheral immune signaling, and vagal afferent activation (O'riordan et al., 2022). This indirect route is particularly relevant in AD, where systemic inflammation is elevated and circulating cytokines can independently activate brain endothelial cells and perivascular macrophages.

Cellular metabolic turnover of SCFAs within brain parenchyma

Direct measurement of SCFA concentrations in brain tissue remains challenging due to rapid metabolism and technical limitations in sample preparation. Available evidence suggests that brain parenchymal SCFA concentrations are substantially lower than plasma levels, with acetate representing the predominant species at estimated concentrations of 10-50 μM, while butyrate and propionate are present at nanomolar to low micromolar concentrations (Silva et al., 2020). In AD mouse models, brain butyrate levels are further reduced compared to wild-type controls, mirroring the gut dysbiotic pattern (Chilton et al., 2024).

Within the brain, astrocytes appear to be the primary cellular site of SCFA metabolism. They possess the full enzymatic machinery for β-oxidation and have been shown to readily oxidize butyrate to CO₂, with some evidence indicating that they may preferentially oxidize butyrate over acetate when both are available (Ameen et al., 2025). This metabolic preference could be significant, as butyrate has been shown to enhance astrocytic mitochondrial respiration and promote a neuroprotective phenotype (Schönfeld and Wojtczak, 2016). The capacity of astrocytes to oxidize butyrate suggests that even the low nanomolar concentrations achievable in brain tissue may exert meaningful bioenergetic and epigenetic effects on these glial cells.

Neurons, in contrast, exhibit limited capacity for direct SCFA oxidation under physiological conditions, though they express MCTs and can utilize lactate and ketone bodies derived from SCFA metabolism (Pierre and Pellerin, 2005). The neuron-specific expression of SMCT1 (SLC5A8) suggests a potential role for sodium-coupled monocarboxylate uptake in neuronal energy metabolism, particularly during fasting or ketogenic conditions when alternative fuel sources become critical (Martin et al., 2006). However, the predominant impact of SCFAs on neurons is likely indirect, mediated through astrocyte-derived metabolites or regulation of the neuroinflammatory milieu.

The metabolic fate of SCFAs that enter the brain includes complete oxidation to CO2, incorporation into amino acids and lipids, and utilization for acetyl-CoA-dependent processes including histone acetylation (Stilling et al., 2016). Butyrate, in particular, serves as a substrate for histone acetyltransferases, linking its availability to epigenetic regulation of gene expression in neural cells (Chilton et al., 2024; Astbury and Corfe, 2012). This dual function of butyrate as both an energy source and an epigenetic modifier distinguishes it from other SCFAs and underlies its unique neurobiological effects. The convergence of reduced gut production, limited intestinal and hepatic delivery, restricted BBB transport, and rapid astrocytic metabolism creates a “butyrate bottleneck” in AD that collectively diminishes the brain’s capacity to maintain neuroimmune homeostasis. SCFA-dependent homeostatic maintenance of BBB integrity

Emerging evidence indicates that SCFAs, particularly butyrate and propionate, play critical roles in maintaining BBB structural and functional integrity (Fock and Parnova, 2023). The BBB is vulnerable to disruption in neuroinflammatory conditions, with tight junction protein downregulation and increased paracellular permeability contributing to neurovascular dysfunction in AD and other neurological disorders (Berndt et al., 2019).

Butyrate has been shown to upregulate expression of tight junction proteins including claudin-5, occludin, and ZO-1 in brain endothelial cells through multiple mechanisms (Knox et al., 2022). HDAC inhibition represents a primary pathway, with butyrate-mediated hyperacetylation of histone H3 and H4 at promoter regions of tight junction genes enhancing their transcription (Fock and Parnova, 2023). Additionally, butyrate activates the transcription factor Nrf2, which induces antioxidant response element-driven expression of cytoprotective genes including those encoding tight junction components (Fock and Parnova, 2023). In a seminal study, germ-free mice colonized with butyrate-producing bacterial consortia exhibited restored BBB integrity, whereas colonization with a consortium genetically deficient in butyrate production failed to do so, establishing causal links between microbial butyrate and barrier maintenance (Leitão-Gonçalves et al., 2017).

Propionate similarly protects BBB integrity, though its mechanisms appear to involve G-protein-coupled receptor signaling. Activation of FFAR2 on brain endothelial cells by propionate triggers downstream signaling cascades that stabilize the actin cytoskeleton and reinforce tight junction complexes. Recent studies using antibiotic-induced gut dysbiosis models have demonstrated that propionate supplementation restores BBB tight junction protein expression and prevents barrier disruption, with these effects dependent on FFAR2 expression (Chenghan et al., 2025). A multi-omics analysis further revealed that propionate and butyrate synergistically regulate endothelial gene expression programs, with propionate primarily engaging GPCR-mediated pathways and butyrate dominating epigenetic remodeling (Guo et al., 2025).

The clinical relevance of SCFA-mediated BBB protection in AD is supported by observations of reduced tight junction protein expression and increased BBB permeability in AD patients and animal models (Berndt et al., 2019). Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from healthy donors to APP/PS1 mice restores BBB integrity and reduces cerebral amyloid angiopathy, effects that correlate with increased circulating SCFA levels (Doifode et al., 2021). In the 3 × Tg AD mouse model, oral tributyrin preserved hippocampal histone acetylation and attenuated BBB leakage, directly implicating butyrate-mediated HDAC inhibition in barrier protection during AD progression (Chilton et al., 2024). SCFA-driven maintenance of homeostatic microglial phenotype

Microglia are the principal innate immune cells of the brain parenchyma, functioning both as the first line of defense and as a chronic source of neurotoxic mediators in AD. Their phenotypic spectrum ranges from a homeostatic surveillance state to multiple activated states collectively termed DAM (Keren-Shaul et al., 2017; Deczkowska et al., 2018). SCFAs have emerged as critical environmental determinants of microglial positioning along this continuum.

Transition dynamics between homeostatic and DAM microglial states

Homeostatic microglia are characterized by high surface expression of P2RY12, CX3CR1, and TMEM119, and they continuously monitor the parenchyma while providing trophic support to synapses (Keren-Shaul et al., 2017; Butovsky et al., 2014). During AD progression, a transcriptional program orchestrated in part by TREM2 drives a transition to a DAM state, marked by concomitant downregulation of homeostatic genes and upregulation of phagocytic and lipid-metabolism genes, including APOE, ITGAX (CD11c), and CST7 (Keren-Shaul et al., 2017; Deczkowska et al., 2018). Single-cell transcriptomic atlases of the AD brain have resolved multiple DAM substates, indicating that DAM activation is not a binary switch but a graded, context-dependent process (Mathys et al., 2019).

The functional consequences of DAM polarization are highly contextual: at early disease stages or in the immediate vicinity of amyloid plaques, DAM can promote plaque compaction and restrict Aβ-induced neurotoxicity; however, when chronically engaged, these cells lose their neuroprotective capacities and contribute to synaptic pruning, complement-driven inflammation, and tau propagation (Shi and Holtzman, 2018; Huang et al., 2021). Zhou et al. demonstrated in the 5 × FAD mouse model that dietary fiber and SCFA receptors FFAR2, FFAR3, and GPR109A are required for maintaining cognitive function and neurogenesis, and that acetate- or butyrate-enriched diets delay cognitive decline, whereas receptor depletion exacerbates pathology (Zhou et al., 2023). These findings position SCFAs as critical modulators of the DAM state, capable of restraining excessive activation while preserving protective properties. In in vitro systems, Gold et al. further showed that propionate decreases microglial activation and reprograms the phagocytic machinery, leading to more efficient clearance of aggregated Aβ and attenuated pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion (Gold et al., 2024).

SCFA insufficiency-induced microglial priming in aging milieu

Aging is the strongest risk factor for sporadic AD, and a key feature of the aging brain is a progressive lowering of the microglial activation threshold—a phenomenon termed priming (Franceschi and Campisi, 2014; Perry and Holmes, 2014). Primed microglia respond to otherwise subthreshold stimuli with exaggerated and protracted production of IL-1β, TNF-α, and oxidative radicals, thereby establishing a chronic, low-grade neuroinflammatory state that accelerates neurodegeneration (Perry and Holmes, 2014). The age-associated decline in butyrate-producing gut bacteria is now recognized as a permissive factor for this priming, as SCFAs normally elevate the microglial activation set point (Caetano-Silva et al., 2023; Mishra et al., 2024).

Caetano-Silva et al. provided definitive evidence that dietary fiber and SCFAs inhibit inflammatory microglial responses both in vivo and in vitro, and that these effects are mediated substantially through epigenetic mechanisms rather than exclusively through GPCR-dependent signaling (Caetano-Silva et al., 2023). This finding recalibrates the prevailing model by emphasizing that SCFAs must attain intracellular access, where they function as HDAC inhibitors and acetyl-CoA donors, to fully reprogram microglial transcription. Mishra et al. independently demonstrated that deficiency of the SCFA receptor FFAR2, caused by reduced SCFA production in aging, drives cognitive decline, gut permeability, and neuroinflammation; conversely, pharmacological activation of FFAR2 with the natural agonist fenchol mitigated AD pathology in a receptor-dependent manner (Mishra et al., 2024; Razazan et al., 2021). Together, these studies establish that age‑dependent SCFA depletion and consequent FFAR2 hyposignaling lower the microglial activation threshold, rendering the brain permissive to inflammatory cascades.

Epigenetic reprogramming mediated by HDAC inhibitory activity

Butyrate’s activity as a class I/IIa HDAC inhibitor represents one of the most mechanistically detailed routes through which SCFAs remodel microglial phenotype (Fischer et al., 2007). Direct HDAC inhibition, as corroborated by both in vitro enzymatic assays and chromatin immunoprecipitation, is mechanistically orthogonal to the ensuing transcriptional and phenotypic shifts. These latter changes, though commonly invoked as surrogate indicators of HDAC activity, do not constitute evidence of direct enzymatic blockade. By chelating the zinc ion in the catalytic pocket of HDACs, butyrate increases the acetylation of histones H3K9, H3K14, and H4K16 at the promoters of anti-inflammatory genes while repressing pro-inflammatory loci. Patnala et al. demonstrated in a cerebral ischemia model that butyrate-induced histone hyperacetylation in microglia shifts their transcriptional program from a pro-inflammatory (TNF-α⁺, NOS2⁺) to a neuroprotective (IL-10⁺) phenotype (Patnala et al., 2017). In the AD context, similar HDAC inhibition is thought to reduce the expression of microglial IL1B, TNF, and NLRP3, while enhancing expression of phagocytic receptors such as TREM2 and complement regulators (Lei et al., 2025; Patnala et al., 2017).

HDAC inhibition by butyrate also has profound effects on neuronal support through BDNF. Calfa et al. established that HDAC activity is required for BDNF-induced increases in quantal neurotransmitter release and dendritic spine density in CA1 pyramidal neurons (Calfa et al., 2012), suggesting that disinhibition of this pathway by butyrate could enhance synaptic resilience. Bagheri et al. further demonstrated that multiple hydroxamic acid-based HDAC inhibitors promote BDNF expression and neurite outgrowth in human neural progenitor-derived neurons (Bagheri et al., 2019). A breakthrough study by Thomas et al. revealed an additional epigenetic dimension: butyrate and propionate activate the p300 acetyltransferase through auto-acylation, meaning that SCFAs promote histone acetylation not only by blocking deacetylases but also by directly fueling the acetylation machinery (Thomas and Denu, 2021). This dual mechanism including HDAC inhibition plus acetyltransferase activation explains how low nanomolar concentrations of butyrate can produce sustained and coordinated chromatin remodeling in microglia.

TREM2-APOE-metabolic axis restoration by SCFAs

Microglial lipid metabolism is a central determinant of their functional state, and the TREM2-APOE axis is the master coordinator of this program (Keren-Shaul et al., 2017; Ulland and Colonna, 2018). TREM2 senses phospholipid and lipoprotein ligands, and its downstream signaling promotes the phagocytic and lipid-catabolic phenotype required for myelin and amyloid clearance (Ulland and Colonna, 2018; Jaitin et al., 2019). Nugent et al. showed that TREM2 deficiency leads to pathogenic accumulation of cholesteryl esters in microglia and consequent failure of myelin phagocytosis (Nugent et al., 2020). Claes et al. demonstrated that plaque-associated human microglia accumulate lipid droplets in a TREM2-dependent manner and that the AD-risk variant R47H reduces this lipid burden but also impairs phagocytosis, revealing a delicate balance between lipid storage and clearance (Claes et al., 2021).

The interplay between SCFA signaling and TREM2-APOE function is bidirectional and clinically significant. First, butyrate-mediated HDAC inhibition directly upregulates TREM2 transcription in microglia, enhancing their capacity for lipid uptake and plaque compaction (Patnala et al., 2017). This epigenetic effect may be particularly relevant for TREM2 variant carriers, whose reduced receptor expression could be partially rescued by SCFA-mediated transcriptional enhancement. Second, butyrate-driven GPR109A activation improves mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation via PPAR-γ co-activation, providing the ATP necessary for sustained phagocytosis and preventing the lipotoxic stress that triggers NLRP3 activation (Schönfeld and Wojtczak, 2016; Wei et al., 2023). This metabolic support is especially critical for APOE ε4 carriers, who exhibit impaired microglial lipid metabolism and reduced oxidative phosphorylation capacity. Third, acetate and butyrate serve as substrates that can be converted to acetyl-CoA and incorporated into lipid synthesis pathways needed for membrane turnover during phagocytosis (Den Besten et al., 2013).

The APOE ε4 allele, the strongest genetic risk factor for sporadic AD, exerts profound effects on gut microbial SCFA biosynthesis capacity. Tran et al. demonstrated that APOE genotype influences gut microbiome structure and function in both humans and mice, with APOE ε4 carriers exhibiting reduced abundance of butyrate-producing taxa and diminished fecal SCFA concentrations (Tran et al., 2019). This genotype-dependent microbial shift suggests a self-reinforcing pathogenic loop: APOE ε4 impairs SCFA production, which in turn compromises the TREM2-APOE lipid clearance pathway, accelerating amyloid deposition and neuroinflammation. Conversely, APOE ε2, the protective allele, is associated with enriched SCFA-producing communities and higher circulating butyrate levels, potentially contributing to its neuroprotective effects via enhanced anti-inflammatory signaling and BBB integrity.

Critical unanswered questions remain regarding the cell-type specificity of SCFA-TREM2-APOE interactions. While microglia are the primary CNS cells expressing TREM2, APOE is produced by both microglia and astrocytes, with astrocytic APOE playing a dominant role in lipid trafficking. Whether SCFAs modulate astrocytic APOE expression independently of microglial signaling remains unknown. Additionally, the functional consequences of SCFA supplementation in TREM2 R47H or APOE ε4 humanized mouse models have yet to be systematically evaluated. Such studies are essential to determine whether SCFA-based interventions can overcome the functional deficits imposed by these genetic risk variants, or whether genotype-specific dosing and combination strategies will be required.

Mitochondrial support is especially critical: aged and chronically activated microglia exhibit mitochondrial dysfunction with defective oxidative phosphorylation; butyrate, acting as an efficient oxidative substrate, restores the mitochondrial membrane potential and reduces electron leak, thereby limiting the generation of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species that would otherwise drive inflammasome assembly (Schönfeld and Wojtczak, 2016; Cavaliere et al., 2022). This metabolic-epigenetic-lipid axis positions SCFAs as holistic modulators of the energetic and functional state of DAM, preserving their protective competence while constraining their neurotoxic potential.

Repression of neurotoxic A1-type reactive astrocytic transformation

Astrocytes are the most abundant glial cell type in the CNS and perform diverse functions, including neurotransmitter recycling, ion homeostasis, metabolic support, and BBB maintenance (Liddelow and Barres, 2017). In AD, astrocytes undergo reactive transformation, with the A1 neurotoxic phenotype receiving particular attention as a driver of neuroinflammation and synaptic dysfunction (Liddelow et al., 2017; Zamanian et al., 2012).

Molecular triggers underlying pathological astrogliosis

Reactive astrocytes, like microglia, do not adopt a single “activated” state but rather transition through a spectrum of transcriptomic states depending on the nature and chronicity of the insult (Zamanian et al., 2012; Lawrence et al., 2023). At one end of this spectrum are complement-associated astrocytes, often termed neurotoxic or A1-like, that upregulate complement component 3 (C3), serpins, and other damage-associated factors, while downregulating neurotrophic support genes (Liddelow et al., 2017; Zamanian et al., 2012). Lawrence et al. systematically reviewed neuropathology-associated reactive astrocytes and highlighted their central role in neurovascular unit disruption during aging and neurodegeneration (Lawrence et al., 2023). Pekna et al. demonstrated that the complement peptide C3a exerts starkly context-dependent effects on astrocytes: in naïve astrocytes C3a upregulates Tnf and Il1b, whereas in ischemic astrocytes it downregulates Gfap, C3, and Nes (Pekna et al., 2023). This bidirectional response pattern underscores the difficulty of classifying a given astrocyte state as purely beneficial or harmful and mandates that SCFA effects be interpreted within specific disease contexts.

Interferon-responsive astrocytes constitute another subgroup, induced by microglial type I interferon secretion, which can both restrict pathogen spread and, when chronically stimulated, contribute to synaptic dysfunction (Rothhammer et al., 2018). The balance between these states is influenced by the local cytokine milieu, which in turn is heavily shaped by microglial activation and systemic inflammation. By attenuating microglial inflammatory output, SCFAs are positioned to indirectly modulate the astrocyte state spectrum (Zhou et al., 2023; Erny et al., 2021).

Inhibition of NF-κB/complement cascades coupled with Nrf2 activation

SCFAs regulate astrocyte reactivity through both direct intracellular signaling and indirect microglia-dependent routes. The NF-κB pathway, a principal driver of astrocyte inflammatory gene expression, is suppressed by butyrate-mediated HDAC inhibition at the RELA and downstream gene promoters, as well as by FFAR2-mediated inhibition of IκB kinase (Hoyles et al., 2018; Braniste et al., 2014). In parallel, SCFAs activate the Nrf2-ARE antioxidant axis, upregulating astrocytic expression of heme oxygenase-1, glutathione synthesis enzymes, and tight junction proteins, thereby fortifying astrocyte resilience to oxidative stress (Johnson and Johnson, 2015; Kim et al., 2020).

Crucially, the induction of neurotoxic A1 astrocytes is dependent on factors secreted by activated microglia, specifically IL-1α, TNF-α, and C1q (Liddelow et al., 2017). By silencing the microglial release of these factors, SCFAs indirectly prevent the polarization of astrocytes toward the neurotoxic phenotype (Zhou et al., 2023; Caetano-Silva et al., 2023). Lin et al. provided direct in vivo evidence that SCFAs suppress astrocyte activation and preserve neurovascular function in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis by amplifying the tryptophan-aryl hydrocarbon receptor-aquaporin 4 (Trp-AhR-AQP4) signaling axis, resulting in improved glymphatic clearance and reduced AQP4 polarity loss (Lin et al., 2024). This study defines a novel receptor-ligand pathway through which bacterial metabolites modulate astrocytic water transport and waste removal, with direct implications for Aβ clearance in AD.

Preservation of polarized astrocytic AQP4 and neurovascular integrity

Astrocytic endfeet form the glia limitans perivascularis and, together with brain endothelial cells and pericytes, constitute the neurovascular unit (Abbott et al., 2006). The expression of AQP4 in endfoot membranes is essential for the convective flow of interstitial fluid that drives glymphatic clearance of Aβ and tau oligomers (Iliff et al., 2012; Nedergaard and Goldman, 2020). In AD, reactive astrocytes lose their polarized expression of AQP4, resulting in impaired interstitial solute drainage and accelerated parenchymal amyloid accumulation (Nedergaard and Goldman, 2020).

SCFAs support astrocytic endfoot function through several pathways. Butyrate epigenetically upregulates AQP4 expression in cultured astrocytes and promotes its polarization toward the perivascular membrane (Lin et al., 2024). Propionate signals through endothelial FFAR2 to increase tight junction protein expression, which indirectly stabilizes the astrocytic endfoot-endothelial interface (Hoyles et al., 2018; Lin et al., 2024). Moreover, by reducing the systemic inflammatory load and preventing IL-1β-mediated disruption of the BBB, SCFAs mitigate the endotheliopathy that triggers astrocyte endfoot retraction (Erickson and Banks, 2019). The gut–brain–glymphatic axis has thus emerged as a mechanistic framework in which microbial SCFA production sustains a clearance-competent state of the neurovascular unit, and its failure in AD contributes to the progressive deposition of proteopathic aggregates (Lin et al., 2024; Nedergaard and Goldman, 2020).

Peripheral immune tolerance induction orchestrated by SCFA-GPCR axes

The recognition that SCFAs function as endogenous ligands for G protein-coupled receptors transformed our understanding of microbiota–host communication (Koh et al., 2016; Mishra et al., 2020). FFAR2 (GPR43), FFAR3 (GPR41), and GPR109A (HCAR2) are now appreciated as cardinal sensors that translate microbial metabolic activity into host immune and metabolic adjustments, and their expression on CNS-resident and CNS-infiltrating cells positions them as central mediators of SCFA neuroimmune effects (Zhou et al., 2023; Mishra et al., 2024; Erny et al., 2021). Receptor-specific evidence enables a robust distinction among these signaling pathways. FFAR2 is preferentially activated by acetate and propionate, a finding substantiated by ligand-binding assays and cryo-EM structural analysis. FFAR3 responds selectively to butyrate and propionate, as established through pharmacological intervention and knockout models. GPR109A, in contrast, is activated by butyrate and mediates Gαᵢ-dependent anti-inflammatory responses.

FFAR2/FFAR3-dependent intracellular signaling cascades

FFAR2 is activated most potently by acetate and propionate, whereas FFAR3 preferentially responds to butyrate and propionate (Mishra et al., 2020; Brown et al., 2003). Both receptors couple predominantly to Gαᵢ/o proteins, inhibiting adenylyl cyclase and reducing intracellular cAMP; FFAR2 also couples to Gαq/11 in certain cell types to mobilize intracellular calcium (Brown et al., 2003). Mishra et al. provided a comprehensive pharmacophysiological review of FFAR2/3, detailing their roles in neurological signaling, immune function, and metabolism (Mishra et al., 2020). Senarath et al. specifically reviewed the evidence that SCFAs modulate microglial and astrocytic activation states in AD via FFAR2, FFAR3, and GPR109A, at concentrations that correlate with their brain bioavailability (Senarath et al., 2024).

The immunological consequences of FFAR2 signaling are context-dependent. In microglia, FFAR2 activation reduces NF-κB activity and suppresses Tnf and Il1b transcription (Mishra et al., 2024; Hoyles et al., 2018); in peripheral neutrophils and macrophages, FFAR2 mediates both pro- and anti-inflammatory effects depending on the simultaneous availability of other danger signals (Brown et al., 2003). The recently solved cryo-electron microscopy structures of FFAR2 in complex with acetate, propionate, and synthetic agonists reveal a shallow orthosteric binding pocket that can accommodate multiple SCFA chain lengths, providing a structural basis for the development of biased agonists that selectively activate anti-inflammatory pathways (Ke et al., 2024; Kugawa et al., 2025).

GPR109A-governed anti-inflammatory macrophage polarization

GPR109A, also known as hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 2 (HCAR2), is activated by butyrate and niacin (Schönfeld and Wojtczak, 2016; Offermanns, 2014). This receptor couples to Gi proteins and mediates anti-inflammatory effects through multiple mechanisms. Wei et al. demonstrated that sodium butyrate alleviates chronic alcoholic neuroinflammation by regulating microglial polarization through the GPR109A/PPAR-γ/NF-κB signaling pathway. This study established a clear mechanistic link between GPR109A activation and suppression of pro-inflammatory microglial phenotypes.

GPR109A signaling has been implicated in macrophage and microglial polarization toward anti-inflammatory M2-like states (Schönfeld and Wojtczak, 2016; Offermanns, 2014). Activation of this receptor promotes IL-10 production while suppressing TNF-α and IL-6 secretion. The involvement of PPAR-γ as a downstream mediator suggests that GPR109A effects extend beyond acute signaling to include transcriptional reprogramming of immune cells (Offermanns, 2014; Ahmed et al., 2009). Kaisar et al. showed that butyrate conditions human monocyte-derived dendritic cells to produce IL-10 and to drive the differentiation of IL-10-producing Tr1 cells; this tolerogenic reprogramming requires both HDAC inhibition and GPR109A signaling, indicating convergent epigenetic and receptor-mediated mechanisms (Kaisar et al., 2017). In the brain, GPR109A activation dampens NLRP3 inflammasome priming and reduces caspase-1-mediated IL-1β processing, directly linking butyrate sensing to the inflammasome axis (Schönfeld and Wojtczak, 2016; Den Besten et al., 2013).

Structural basis and biased agonism of SCFA receptors

The structural resolution of SCFA receptors has opened the door to rational biased agonism—the design of ligands that stabilize specific receptor conformations to uncouple beneficial anti-inflammatory signaling from potentially undesirable effects on metabolism or tumor surveillance (Ke et al., 2024; Kugawa et al., 2025). For example, a FFAR2-biased agonist that preferentially activates Gαᵢ over β-arrestin pathways could suppress microglial NF-κB without inducing receptor desensitization or neutrophil chemotaxis (Mishra et al., 2024; Kugawa et al., 2025). The therapeutic potential of such molecules for AD has been highlighted in recent preclinical studies demonstrating that FFAR2 agonism improves Aβ clearance and cognitive function without the gastrointestinal side effects associated with non-selective SCFA supplementation (Mishra et al., 2024; Senarath et al., 2024). Multilayered suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome cascade

The NLRP3 inflammasome is a cytosolic multiprotein complex that senses diverse danger signals, including Aβ aggregates, lysosomal damage, and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, and catalyzes the autocatalytic cleavage of caspase-1, which in turn proteolytically activates the potent pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 (Xu et al., 2025; Heneka et al., 2018). In AD, NLRP3 is chronically activated in microglia, where it drives a feed-forward loop of inflammation, tau hyperphosphorylation, and synaptic loss (Xu et al., 2025; Ising et al., 2019). SCFAs suppress NLRP3 activity through multiple converging mechanisms.

Canonical NLRP3 activation linked to AD-associated neuroinflammation

The NLRP3 inflammasome plays a pivotal role in driving neuroinflammation and contributes to the pathogenesis of various CNS diseases (Xu et al., 2025). In AD, Aβ and other disease-associated stimuli activate the NLRP3 inflammasome in microglia, leading to caspase-1 activation, gasdermin D cleavage, and the release of mature IL-1β and IL-18 (Xu et al., 2025; Heneka et al., 2018). These cytokines perpetuate inflammatory cycles, impair synaptic function, and promote tau hyperphosphorylation (Ising et al., 2019). Gustin et al. established that the NLRP3 inflammasome is expressed and functional in mouse brain microglia but not in astrocytes, identifying microglia as the primary brain-resident immune cells capable of forming functional inflammasomes and producing inflammasome-linked pro-inflammatory cytokines in response to classical activators (Gustin et al., 2015). This cell-type specificity has important implications for therapeutic targeting, as interventions aimed at NLRP3 suppression will primarily affect microglial rather than astrocyte function.

Transcriptional and metabolic blockade of inflammasome assembly

Butyrate suppresses the NLRP3 inflammasome at both transcriptional and post-translational levels. At the transcriptional level, butyrate-mediated HDAC inhibition reduces NF-κB-dependent transcription of NLRP3 and IL1B, thereby limiting the availability of both the sensor protein and its pro-cytokine substrate (Wang et al., 2024). At the post-translational level, butyrate improves mitochondrial respiration and reduces the production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species and oxidized mitochondrial DNA, two of the most potent endogenous triggers of NLRP3 assembly (Schönfeld and Wojtczak, 2016; Wang et al., 2022). This improvement in mitochondrial fitness is mediated in part through GPR109A signaling, which activates PPAR-γ and shifts microglial metabolism toward oxidative phosphorylation while dampening glycolytic flux (Schönfeld and Wojtczak, 2016; Wei et al., 2023). In parallel, butyrate-driven FFAR2 signaling elevates intracellular cAMP and promotes CREB-dependent transcription of IL-10, which further inhibits NLRP3 at the post-translational level (Mishra et al., 2024; Erny et al., 2021).

In addition to these direct cellular actions, butyrate suppresses NLRP3 activation indirectly by reinforcing the intestinal epithelial barrier. By upregulating tight junction proteins and reducing gut permeability, butyrate limits the translocation of bacterial lipopolysaccharide into the systemic circulation, thereby reducing the tonic stimulation of TLR4 on circulating monocytes and perivascular macrophages (Lv et al., 2024; Chakraborty et al., 2024). This reduction in systemic endotoxin exposure lowers the NLRP3 priming signal throughout the body, creating a peripheral anti-inflammatory buffer that complements the direct glial effects of butyrate within the CNS.

It is critical to note that the relationship between SCFAs and NLRP3 is not uniformly suppressive. Wang et al. demonstrated that under conditions of concurrent TLR stimulation, butyrate and propionate paradoxically activate the NLRP3 inflammasome in human macrophages through an epigenetic mechanism (Wang et al., 2024). Specifically, these SCFAs inhibit HDACs 1-3 and 10, producing an uneven distribution of histone hyperacetylation that silences the CFLAR (cFLIP) and IL10 loci, thereby relieving two key brakes on NLRP3 activation. The resulting inflammasome assembly proceeds via caspase-8, is independent of potassium efflux, and leads to IL-1β release without pyroptotic cell death (Wang et al., 2024). This finding reveals that SCFAs act as context-dependent “viability-associated pathogen-associated molecular patterns” that can either restrain or promote inflammasome activity depending on the inflammatory milieu. For AD, this implies that SCFA-based interventions may be most safely and effectively deployed at early disease stages, before the establishment of the florid, TLR-driven neuroinflammation that characterizes advanced pathology.

Synergistic integration of GPCR, epigenetic and cytokine signaling

The suppression of NLRP3 by SCFAs is reinforced by concurrent inhibition of other inflammatory signaling modules. Butyrate-mediated activation of GPR109A attenuates TLR4-induced NF-κB activity, directly reducing the priming signal required for NLRP3 and IL1B transcription (Wei et al., 2023). Acetate and propionate engagement of FFAR2 stimulates CREB phosphorylation and enhances the production of IL-10, which interferes with NLRP3 assembly at the post-translational level (Mishra et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2014). In parallel, butyrate-driven HDAC inhibition silences pro-inflammatory gene loci while promoting histone acetylation at the promoters of IL10 and other anti-inflammatory mediators, creating a chromatin landscape that opposes inflammasome-driven transcription (Caetano-Silva et al., 2023; Patnala et al., 2017). The simultaneous operation of GPCR-mediated signaling, epigenetic remodeling, and metabolic reprogramming enables SCFAs to block NLRP3 at the transcriptional, assembly, and amplification stages. This unique multi-node targeting explains why dietary or probiotic interventions that restore physiological SCFA levels often produce broader and more sustained anti-inflammatory outcomes than pharmacological blockade of any single pathway (Chilton et al., 2024; Caetano-Silva et al., 2023), and suggests that the loss of SCFA-producing bacteria in AD removes a coordinated anti-inflammatory rheostat, leaving the NLRP3 inflammasome and its associated pathways tonically disinhibited.

Establishment of systemic anti-inflammatory immune buffering network

SCFA effects on neuroinflammation are not limited to resident CNS cells. A substantial component of SCFA-mediated neuroprotection is exerted through the education of peripheral immune populations that communicate with the brain via soluble mediators, meningeal lymphatics, and the choroid plexus (Baruch and Schwartz, 2013; Kipnis, 2016). This peripheral immune education establishes a tolerogenic systemic milieu that raises the threshold for neuroinflammation.

Epigenetic facilitation of Foxp3+ Treg differentiation

The gut is the body’s largest immunological organ, and the gut-associated lymphoid tissue is the principal site where naïve CD4⁺ T cells differentiate into regulatory T cells (Tregs) in response to microbial signals (Furusawa et al., 2013; Arpaia et al., 2013). Furusawa et al. demonstrated that commensal microbe-derived butyrate induces the differentiation of colonic regulatory T cells, establishing a mechanistic link between gut microbiota composition and peripheral immune tolerance (Furusawa et al., 2013). This study revealed that butyrate enhances histone acetylation at the Foxp3 promoter and conserved non-coding sequences, thereby stabilizing Foxp3 expression and promoting Treg lineage commitment.

Arpaia et al. provided complementary evidence that metabolites produced by commensal bacteria promote peripheral regulatory T-cell generation (Arpaia et al., 2013). Their study demonstrated that SCFAs facilitate extrathymic differentiation of Foxp3+ Treg cells via histone acetylation, highlighting the role of microbial metabolites in immune regulation and inflammation control (Furusawa et al., 2013; Arpaia et al., 2013). The effects of butyrate on Treg function are mediated at least partially through HDAC inhibition, though additional mechanisms including GPR109A signaling and metabolic support may contribute (Kaisar et al., 2017; Smith et al., 2013).

The relevance of Treg induction for AD pathogenesis has been increasingly recognized. Tregs can migrate to the CNS under inflammatory conditions and suppress microglial activation through cell-contact dependent mechanisms and cytokine production, particularly IL-10 and TGF-β (Kaisar et al., 2017; Kipnis, 2016). Additionally, systemic Treg expansion may reduce peripheral inflammatory tone, thereby decreasing the production of inflammatory mediators that can access the CNS and activate microglia (Kipnis, 2016; Dansokho et al., 2016).

Induction of tolerogenic dendritic cell phenotype

Dendritic cells serve as the primary antigen-presenting cells that orchestrate adaptive immune responses. SCFAs modulate dendritic cell function, promoting tolerogenic phenotypes that favor Treg induction over effector T cell activation (Kaisar et al., 2017; Singh et al., 2014). Kaisar et al. demonstrated that butyrate effectively induces tolerogenic phenotypes in human dendritic cells, suppressing inflammatory responses and promoting regulatory polarization of naive CD4+ T cells into IL-10-producing Tr1 cells. This process relies on retinaldehyde dehydrogenase 1 activity within dendritic cells, mediated through histone deacetylase inhibition and GPR109A signaling (Kaisar et al., 2017). The tolerogenic effects of SCFA-conditioned dendritic cells have implications for CNS autoimmunity and neuroinflammation. By promoting regulatory rather than inflammatory T cell responses, SCFAs may attenuate T cell-mediated damage in neuroinflammatory conditions (Kipnis, 2016; Dansokho et al., 2016). Additionally, tolerogenic dendritic cells may produce anti-inflammatory cytokines and metabolites that directly suppress microglial activation.

Restriction of pathological monocyte CNS infiltration

SCFAs restrain monocyte trafficking into the CNS by simultaneously acting on endothelial cells and monocytes. On the endothelium, butyrate and propionate suppress the expression of adhesion molecules such as VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 through FFAR2-mediated inhibition of NF-κB and HDAC-dependent chromatin remodeling, thereby reducing monocyte capture and arrest (Hoyles et al., 2018). In parallel, SCFAs lower the expression of the chemokine receptor CCR2 on circulating classical monocytes and dampen their secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, rendering them less responsive to the chemokine gradients emanating from the inflamed brain (Mishra et al., 2020; Senarath et al., 2024). This dual-pronged mechanism limits the initial margination and subsequent diapedesis of monocytes across the BBB.

Crucially, SCFAs also lower the systemic inflammatory tone that primes monocyte activation in the first place. By upregulating tight junction proteins and fortifying the intestinal epithelial barrier, SCFAs reduce the translocation of bacterial LPS into the portal and systemic circulations, consequently decreasing the tonic stimulation of TLR4 on circulating monocytes and tissue macrophages (Chilton et al., 2024). In AD models, restoration of butyrate-producing bacteria or direct butyrate supplementation lowers plasma endotoxin levels and key monocyte-derived cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α), linking improved gut barrier integrity to a less activated peripheral monocyte pool (Kuehn et al., 2025). Through these complementary actions, direct inhibition of monocyte-endothelial interactions and indirect suppression of systemic inflammation, SCFAs help maintain a peripheral immune environment that is permissive to CNS homeostasis rather than neuroinflammatory amplification.

Construction of peripheral anti-inflammatory homeostasis

The peripheral immune effects of SCFAs integrate with their direct CNS actions to create a comprehensive anti-inflammatory program. Treg expansion, tolerogenic dendritic cell programming, gut barrier reinforcement, and systemic inflammation suppression collectively reduce the inflammatory burden on the CNS. This integrated model suggests that SCFA interventions may be particularly effective when initiated early in disease progression, before the establishment of self-perpetuating neuroinflammatory cycles.

The concept of the gut-brain immune axis provides a framework for understanding these complex interactions. SCFAs serve as signaling molecules that communicate gut microbial status to the immune system and CNS, coordinating adaptive responses that maintain homeostasis. Disruption of this communication pathway through dysbiosis and reduced SCFA production may contribute to the chronic neuroinflammation observed in AD (Table 2).

Regulatory effects of SCFAs on core AD pathological features

The pathological cascade of AD centers on two hallmark lesions: extracellular Aβ plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein. Although these proteinaceous aggregates have long been considered the primary drivers of neurodegeneration, accumulating evidence positions neuroinflammation as an integral component that accelerates both Aβ deposition and tau propagation (Heneka et al., 2015; Kinney et al., 2018). SCFAs modulate these core pathologies through multiple convergent mechanisms, influencing protein aggregation, clearance pathways, and the synaptic and neurovascular dysfunction that underlies cognitive decline.

Modulation of Aβ deposition and microglial phagocytic function

The relationship between SCFA signaling and Aβ pathology presents a complex picture that demands careful interpretation. Microglia serve as the primary cellular mediators of Aβ clearance, and their phagocytic efficiency is intimately linked to metabolic state and inflammatory tone. Recent investigations have revealed that individual SCFAs exert distinct effects on microglial responses to Aβ aggregates. Propionate, for instance, demonstrates a nuanced biphasic action: while it attenuates inflammatory activation and reduces nitric oxide production in microglia exposed to fibrillar Aβ, this anti-inflammatory benefit appears to come at the cost of impaired phagocytic capacity (Gold et al., 2024). Critically, a causal link between SCFAs and Aβ pathology in humans has not been established; preclinical findings are conflicting, with some studies showing reduced plaque burden and others reporting exacerbated deposition (Gold et al., 2024; Colombo et al., 2021). These findings suggest that SCFA-mediated suppression of microglial activation may inadvertently compromise plaque clearance mechanisms, highlighting the delicate balance between inflammation control and phagocytic function (Gold et al., 2024).

This observation gains additional complexity from earlier work demonstrating that microbiota-derived SCFAs can promote Aβ plaque deposition under specific experimental conditions. Colombo et al. reported that SCFA supplementation in germ-free APP transgenic mice increased plaque burden, an effect associated with altered microglial morphology and reduced phagocytic receptor expression (Colombo et al., 2021). The apparent contradiction between these findings and the generally neuroprotective profile of SCFAs underscores the context-dependent nature of metabolite effects, which vary with disease stage, SCFA concentration, and the specific microbial community producing these metabolites.

Nevertheless, substantial evidence supports beneficial effects of SCFAs on Aβ clearance under physiological or moderately pathological conditions. Butyrate and propionate enhance microglial phagocytosis through HDAC inhibition-dependent upregulation of scavenger receptors and lysosomal machinery (Patnala et al., 2017; Ho et al., 2018). The TREM2-APOE signaling axis, critical for microglial transition to DAM states that facilitate plaque compaction and clearance, is positively modulated by SCFA treatment. Liu et al. demonstrated that acetate administration upregulates GPR41 expression and suppresses ERK/JNK/NF-κB signaling, resulting in improved cognitive function and reduced microglial activation in APP/PS1 mice (Liu et al., 2020). These receptor-mediated effects complement the epigenetic actions of butyrate in promoting microglial homeostasis and phagocytic competence.

The spatial organization of plaques within the brain parenchyma also responds to SCFA availability. Microglial clustering around amyloid deposits, a process essential for plaque compaction and isolation, is enhanced by SCFA supplementation in association with metabolic reprogramming toward oxidative phosphorylation (Wenzel et al., 2020; Baik et al., 2019). This metabolic shift supports the sustained energetic demands of phagocytosis and migration, processes that are compromised in glycolytic, pro-inflammatory microglial states (Pan et al., 2022). Furthermore, SCFA-induced upregulation of ApoE expression in microglia facilitates lipid trafficking and cholesterol efflux from plaque-associated cells, promoting the resolution of foam cell phenotypes that characterize chronic amyloidosis (Claes et al., 2021; Colombo et al., 2021).

Despite the predominantly protective role of SCFAs, conflicting evidence warrants consideration. Colombo et al. reported that SCFA supplementation in germ-free APP transgenic mice unexpectedly increased Aβ plaque deposition, associated with altered microglial morphology and reduced phagocytic receptor expression (Colombo et al., 2021). Similarly, Gold et al. showed that propionate attenuated microglial inflammatory activation but concurrently impaired phagocytic capacity against fibrillar Aβ (Gold et al., 2024). At the inflammasome level, Wang et al. demonstrated that butyrate and propionate could paradoxically activate NLRP3 in human macrophages under concurrent TLR stimulation, via epigenetic silencing of CFLAR and IL10 (Wang et al., 2024). These divergent outcomes can be reconciled by recognizing that SCFA effects are concentration‑dependent, stage‑dependent (beneficial in early disease, potentially detrimental in advanced neuroinflammation), and context‑dependent (influenced by concurrent microbial and inflammatory signals). Thus, rather than uniform benefit, SCFA-based interventions require careful dose optimization, stage-specific timing, and assessment of the broader inflammatory milieu.

Attenuation of pathological tau hyperphosphorylation and propagation

While Aβ pathology initiates early in the AD cascade, tau pathology correlates more closely with cognitive decline and neurodegeneration (Long and Holtzman, 2019; Nelson et al., 2012). The propagation of pathological tau species follows prion-like mechanisms involving templated misfolding and intercellular transfer, processes that are accelerated by neuroinflammatory signaling. SCFAs modulate tau pathology through both direct effects on neuronal kinase signaling and indirect actions via suppression of inflammatory mediators that drive tau hyperphosphorylation (Ising et al., 2019).

The kinases responsible for pathological tau phosphorylation, including glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β) and cyclin-dependent kinase 5 (CDK5), are activated by pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress (Llorens-Martín et al., 2014). By suppressing NF-κB signaling and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, SCFAs reduce the inflammatory milieu that promotes kinase activation and tau hyperphosphorylation (Heneka et al., 2013). Butyrate specifically inhibits GSK-3β activity through HDAC inhibition-mediated upregulation of protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A), the primary tau phosphatase, thereby restoring the balance between phosphorylation and dephosphorylation (Wei et al., 2023).

Beyond direct kinase modulation, SCFAs influence tau propagation by regulating the expression and activity of extracellular matrix components and extracellular vesicle release. Microglial-derived exosomes can transmit pathological tau seeds between neurons, a process enhanced by inflammatory activation (Asai et al., 2015). SCFA-mediated suppression of microglial exosome secretion and modification of exosome cargo composition may limit the intercellular spread of pathological tau species (Ruan et al., 2021; Dejanovic et al., 2018). Additionally, the restoration of neurovascular function by SCFAs enhances glymphatic clearance of extracellular tau aggregates, reducing the pool of seeds available for templated misfolding (Iliff et al., 2012; Nedergaard and Goldman, 2020).

The interaction between Aβ and tau pathologies is also modulated by SCFA signaling. Aβ-induced neuroinflammation drives tau phosphorylation and aggregation, creating a feed-forward loop that accelerates neurodegeneration (Busche and Hyman, 2020). By interrupting this inflammatory amplification, SCFAs decouple the synergistic interaction between these pathologies, potentially accounting for the dissociation between plaque burden and cognitive symptoms observed in some intervention studies (Zhou et al., 2023; Pascoal et al., 2021). The temporal dynamics of SCFA intervention appear critical: treatment during preclinical or early symptomatic stages, when tau pathology is regionally restricted, shows greater efficacy than intervention in advanced disease with widespread tau deposition (Mishra et al., 2024).

Protection against AD-associated synaptic deterioration

Synaptic loss represents the strongest pathological correlate of cognitive impairment in AD, preceding neuronal death and often occurring in the absence of overt plaque or tangle pathology in early stages. SCFAs preserve synaptic integrity through multiple mechanisms involving neurotrophic factor expression, regulation of complement-mediated synaptic pruning, and maintenance of neurovascular coupling (Sokolova et al., 2021).

BDNF, essential for synaptic plasticity, learning, and memory, is downregulated in AD and correlates with disease severity (Gao et al., 2022; Numakawa and Odaka, 2021). Butyrate robustly upregulates BDNF expression through HDAC inhibition at the Bdnf promoter, enhancing histone acetylation and transcriptional activation (Calfa et al., 2012; Bagheri et al., 2019). This epigenetic mechanism operates in both neurons and glial cells, with astrocytic BDNF contributing to synaptic maintenance and microglial BDNF supporting phagocytic function (Ferrini and De Koninck, 2013). Xue et al. highlighted the connection between SCFA-producing bacteria, BDNF signaling, and lifestyle factors in AD pathophysiology, suggesting that dietary interventions enhancing SCFA availability may promote cognitive resilience through BDNF-mediated mechanisms (Xue et al., 2022).

The TrkB receptor, through which BDNF exerts its neurotrophic effects, is also modulated by SCFA signaling. Chronic butyrate treatment maintains TrkB expression in the hippocampus and cortex, preventing the receptor downregulation that occurs in response to chronic stress and inflammation (Song, 2024). Preservation of BDNF/TrkB signaling supports long-term potentiation, the cellular correlate of learning and memory, and maintains dendritic spine density in vulnerable circuits (Lu et al., 2014). These effects complement the anti-apoptotic actions of BDNF in promoting neuronal survival under conditions of proteotoxic and metabolic stress (Bathina and Das, 2015).

Complement-mediated synaptic pruning, while essential for normal brain development and plasticity, becomes pathologically exaggerated in AD (Hong et al., 2016; Stevens et al., 2007). Microglial recognition and elimination of complement-tagged synapses, driven by C1q and C3 deposition, contributes to the circuit dysfunction underlying cognitive symptoms (Gomez-Arboledas et al., 2021; Kovács et al., 2020). SCFAs modulate this process through multiple intersecting pathways. By suppressing microglial activation and promoting homeostatic phenotypes, butyrate reduces the phagocytic capacity of microglia for synaptic elements (Luchena et al., 2018). Concurrently, HDAC inhibition downregulates C1q expression in astrocytes and microglia, limiting the complement tagging that initiates synapse elimination (Wu et al., 2020; Györffy et al., 2018). Wen et al. reviewed the mechanisms of complement-mediated synapse loss in AD, emphasizing the therapeutic potential of modulating this pathway to preserve cognitive function (Wen et al., 2024).

The neurovascular unit, comprising endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, and neurons, coordinates the delivery of oxygen and nutrients to active neural circuits (Iadecola, 2017). Neurovascular coupling, the local increase in blood flow in response to neuronal activity, is impaired in AD and contributes to hypometabolism and cognitive dysfunction (Kisler et al., 2017; Sweeney et al., 2018). SCFAs preserve neurovascular function through maintenance of BBB integrity, promotion of endothelial nitric oxide synthesis, and regulation of astrocytic calcium signaling (Hoyles et al., 2018; Braniste et al., 2014). The upregulation of tight junction proteins and aquaporin-4 by butyrate ensures efficient water and metabolite transport, supporting the clearance of neurotoxic proteins and the delivery of glucose to energy-demanding synapses (Lin et al., 2024; Nedergaard and Goldman, 2020).

Astrocytic end-feet coverage of cerebral vessels, essential for neurovascular coupling, is maintained by SCFA signaling through GPR41 and GPR43 activation. This structural preservation ensures that neuronal activity is appropriately matched by hemodynamic responses, preventing the functional hypoxia that accelerates synaptic failure (Attwell et al., 2010). Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory actions of SCFAs on pericytes prevent the contractile responses that restrict capillary flow, maintaining microcirculatory perfusion in vulnerable brain regions (Winkler et al., 2015).

The integration of these mechanisms, enhanced BDNF signaling, suppression of pathological synaptic pruning, and preservation of neurovascular coupling, creates a supportive environment for synaptic maintenance even in the presence of Aβ and tau pathology (Villeda et al., 2011). This synaptic resilience may account for the dissociation between pathological burden and cognitive symptoms observed in some individuals with AD pathology, a phenomenon that has been termed “cognitive reserve” or “resilience” (Arenaza-Urquijo and Vemuri, 2018). SCFA-producing bacteria and their metabolites emerge as modifiable factors that can enhance this resilience, offering a therapeutic window for intervention before irreversible synaptic loss occurs (Chilton et al., 2024; Koh et al., 2016).

Translational interventions targeting SCFA homeostasis

The convergence of mechanistic insights into SCFA-mediated neuroprotection and the persistent clinical need for disease-modifying AD therapies has catalyzed substantial translational efforts. These strategies span dietary modifications, microbial ecosystem restoration, direct metabolite supplementation, and precision biomarker-guided trial designs. Each approach presents distinct advantages and limitations that must be carefully weighed in the context of disease stage, host genetics, and microbiome composition.

Precision dietary manipulation for elevating endogenous SCFA production

Dietary patterns rich in fermentable fibers represent the most accessible and cost-effective means of modulating SCFA production. The Mediterranean and MIND diets, characterized by high consumption of vegetables, legumes, whole grains, and polyphenol-rich foods, have demonstrated consistent associations with reduced AD risk and slower cognitive decline (Ticinesi et al., 2022; Scarmeas et al., 2018). Recent mechanistic studies reveal that these benefits extend beyond cardiovascular protection to encompass direct microbiome modulation. In a 5 × FAD mouse model, dietary fiber intervention enriched SCFA-producing taxa and enhanced cognition through FFAR2-dependent signaling pathways, with effects abolished by antibiotic treatment, confirming microbiota dependency (Zhou et al., 2023). A modified Mediterranean-ketogenic diet administered to individuals with mild cognitive impairment induced significant shifts in gut microbiota composition and increased fecal SCFA levels, correlating with improved AD biomarker profiles (Nagpal et al., 2019).

Soluble fiber supplementation, particularly inulin and resistant starch, has emerged as a targeted approach. Böswald et al. demonstrated that inulin supplementation in APP/PS1 mice increased cecal and plasma acetate concentrations, reduced Aβ plaque burden, and altered brain proteome signatures indicative of reduced neurodegeneration (Böswald et al., 2025). Notably, the ratio of butyrate to propionate appears critical; Cuervo-Zanatta et al. showed that soluble fiber preferentially promotes butyrate over propionate production, resulting in reduced astrocyte activation and improved cognitive performance (Cuervo-Zanatta et al., 2023). These findings suggest that fiber type and dose optimization should account for the specific SCFA stoichiometry required for neuroprotection rather than simply maximizing total SCFA output (Louis and Flint, 2017).

However, dietary interventions face substantial heterogeneity in individual responses due to baseline microbiome composition, host genetics, and medication use (Johnson et al., 2019). Ticinesi et al. emphasize that while polyphenol- and fiber-rich diets show promise, clinical trials must address long-term adherence, dosing refinement, and individual microbiome variability to optimize precision nutrition strategies (Ticinesi et al., 2022). The development of enterotype-based dietary recommendations represents a logical next step, wherein baseline microbiome profiling informs personalized fiber supplementation protocols (Arumugam et al., 2011).

Gut microbiota modulators

Gut microbiota modulators cover a wide range of bioactive interventions, including prebiotics, probiotics, synbiotics, and next-generation functional commensal strains, which collectively remodel microbial community structure, metabolic profile, and host immune homeostasis. Prebiotic regimens based on galactooligosaccharides (GOS), fructooligosaccharides (FOS), and resistant starch effectively elevate fecal SCFA levels and regulate immune phenotypes in ageing populations (Gibson et al., 2017). Synbiotic formulations, combining selective probiotic strains with matched prebiotic substrates, exploit microbial cross-feeding interactions to improve intestinal colonization durability and metabolic functionality (Rastall and Gibson, 2015). Specifically, Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus can indirectly promote butyrate synthesis via acetate cross-feeding to canonical butyrate producers such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Roseburia species (Meiners et al., 2025).

Conventional single-strain probiotics have shown inconsistent efficacy in AD models, largely attributed to strain-dependent functional variability and insufficient engraftment in the distal colon, a primary niche for SCFA biosynthesis (Suez et al., 2019). Accordingly, research focus has shifted to next-generation probiotics (NGPs), consisting of functionally validated anaerobic commensals with intrinsic SCFA-producing capability (O'toole et al., 2017). NGPs represented by F. prausnitzii and A. muciniphila exert superior benefits over traditional probiotics by potentiating SCFA production and modulating neuroinflammatory pathways (Jadhav et al., 2026), whereas their clinical translation is hindered by strict anaerobe oxygen sensitivity, large-scale manufacturing constraints, and regulatory systems originally established for conventional probiotic products (Martín and Langella, 2019).

Beyond individual NGP strains, rationally defined microbial consortia and synthetic biology-enabled engineered live biotherapeutics further allow precise chassis customization for optimized SCFA biosynthesis and targeted intestinal delivery (Mimee et al., 2015). Advances in chassis screening, colonization optimization, genetic circuit design, and biocontainment strategies have substantially improved the clinical feasibility of engineered probiotic platforms (Tan et al., 2020). In particular, Rahmanian et al. constructed cortisol-sensitive operons to dynamically modulate SCFA synthesis in F. prausnitzii in response to host stress signals, providing a physiologically responsive strategy for enhancing microbial adaptive resilience (Rahmanian et al., 2024). Such context-regulated engineered consortia can dynamically adjust metabolic output according to host physiological status; nonetheless, major challenges persist, including reliable biological containment, long-term functional stability, and standardized regulatory supervision for in situ intervention (Sola-Oladokun et al., 2017). Further optimization of strain robustness and genetic stability is still required to ensure sustained metabolic performance prior to formal clinical implementation (Pedrolli et al., 2019).

Postbiotic and direct SCFA supplementation strategies

Direct SCFA supplementation bypasses interindividual microbiome heterogeneity and enables controlled, precise metabolite dosing for neuroprotective intervention. Sodium butyrate represents a well-characterized candidate postbiotic with robust therapeutic efficacy in preclinical AD models. Fernando et al. validated that early-stage sodium butyrate intervention effectively reduces cerebral Aβ deposition and rescues cognitive memory deficits in 5 × FAD mice (Fernando et al., 2020). Mechanistically, sodium butyrate alleviates cognitive impairment by driving astrocyte differentiation toward a neuroprotective phenotype and restoring astroglial mitochondrial homeostasis (Wang et al., 2022), while also enhancing synaptic plasticity via suppressing neuroinflammation, lowering pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and restraining excessive microglial activation (Jiang et al., 2021).

Nevertheless, oral sodium butyrate is constrained by inherent pharmacokinetic drawbacks, including rapid proximal intestinal absorption, prominent hepatic first-pass metabolism, and poor brain bioavailability, driving the innovation of advanced delivery formulations (Stilling et al., 2016; Bourassa et al., 2016). As a promising butyrate prodrug, tributyrin exhibits improved pharmacokinetic profiles and tangible anti-AD effects. Oral tributyrin supplementation mitigates AD-related neuropathology and preserves cognitive and behavioral function in 3 × Tg-AD mice, accompanied by suppressed neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and tau hyperphosphorylation (Chilton et al., 2023). Moreover, tributyrin counteracts the depletion of intestinal butyrate-producing taxa and subsequent cognitive dysfunction linked to AD progression (Rao et al., 2023). Given that age-related attrition of butyrate-producing bacteria critically fuels neuropathological initiation and progression, tributyrin supplementation serves as an effective strategy to reverse such microbial and functional deficits (Chilton et al., 2024).

Novel delivery platforms, such as mesoporous silica nanoparticles and lipid-based encapsulation, are being developed to optimize SCFA stability, systemic bioavailability, and site-specific release in the distal gut or peripheral circulation (Kachoueiyan et al., 2025; Han et al., 2021). Innovative administration routes further expand translational potential; for instance, mesoporous silica nanoparticle-encapsulated Bifidobacterium delivered intranasally alleviates cerebral Aβ burden and ameliorates olfactory impairment in APP/PS1 mice (Liu et al., 2022). Pharmacokinetic profiling further reveals that sodium butyrate and lysine butyrate achieve faster systemic exposure relative to tributyrin, providing pivotal evidence for rational design and regimen optimization of future SCFA-based therapeutic strategies.

Therapeutic potential of FMT

FMT constitutes the most holistic intervention strategy for gut microbiome reconstruction, enabling the transfer of intact microbial ecosystems from healthy donors to diseased recipients to reset host microbial and physiological homeostasis (Kelly et al., 2015). Accumulating preclinical evidence has validated the robust neuroprotective capacity of FMT in AD experimental models. Sun et al. demonstrated that FMT derived from wild-type donors markedly ameliorates AD-related neuropathology in APP/PS1 mice, accompanied by diminished cerebral amyloid deposition and rescued cognitive performance (Sun et al., 2019). Consistently, Elangovan et al. confirmed that FMT intervention reduces brain amyloid burden and restores cognitive deficits in AD murine models, with therapeutic outcomes strongly dictated by donor microbial profiles (Elangovan et al., 2022). Independent work by Holsinger and Elangovan further verified the neuroprotective efficacy of FMT in 5 × FAD mice, establishing fundamental proof-of-concept for microbiome transplantation-based AD intervention (Holsinger and Elangovan, 2020).

Growing research has further refined optimal donor screening criteria and standardized therapeutic dosing regimens for FMT application. Zhou et al. revealed the bidirectional regulatory role of gut microbiota in AD progression: FMT from healthy donors alleviates AD pathology, whereas transplantation from colitis-associated dysbiotic donors exacerbates disease phenotypes, underscoring the indispensable role of rigorous donor qualification screening (Zhou et al., 2025). In terms of administration strategy, Jiang et al. observed that alternate-day FMT efficiently attenuates Aβ pathology and reverses gut microbial dysbiosis, while weekly intervention fails to yield measurable benefits, highlighting the necessity of personalized dosage and frequency optimization (Jiang et al., 2025). Notably, Chevalier et al. reported that FMT from human donors with low AD risk improves short-term recognition memory in 3 × Tg-AD mice, albeit with regionally elevated neuroinflammatory responses, reinforcing the requirement for rigorous efficacy evaluation and long-term safety surveillance (Chevalier et al., 2025).

Despite promising preclinical outcomes, the clinical translation of FMT for AD remains confronted with considerable challenges, including the lack of unified donor selection guidelines, potential risks of pathogen transmission and metabolic adverse events, and ambiguous regulatory positioning between biological products and conventional pharmaceuticals (Mullish et al., 2018). As a safer and more reproducible alternative, rationally defined synthetic gut microbial consortia are emerging as a viable substitute for conventional FMT, offering standardized composition, controllable functionality, and improved safety profiles for future therapeutic implementation (Li et al., 2023).

Completed multi-strain probiotic trials show modest cognitive benefits in MCI and mild AD, accompanied by increased fecal SCFAs and reduced systemic inflammatory markers. The postbiotic tributyrin is currently being evaluated in a controlled trial for early AD. FMT has entered early-phase exploration, though long-term safety, donor selection, and dosing remain challenging. Biomarker-guided trial design via pharmacomicrobiomics

Clinical trial design for microbiome-directed AD interventions necessitates the incorporation of multi-omic biomarker panels to enable precise participant stratification, longitudinal target engagement monitoring, and objective therapeutic efficacy evaluation (Hampel et al., 2018). Circulating plasma and CSF biomarkers, including neurofilament light chain (NfL), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and soluble TREM2 (sTREM2), provide complementary readouts reflecting neuronal degeneration, astrocytic reactivity, and microglial activation, pathological processes tightly regulated by gut microbial SCFA signaling cascades (Forloni, 2020; Zetterberg and Bendlin, 2021). Thijssen et al. further validated that combinatorial profiling of plasma biomarkers coupled with APOE genetic stratification substantially improves diagnostic resolution and enables refined clinical trial cohort allocation and protocol optimization (Thijssen et al., 2020).

Among these molecular signatures, GFAP has emerged as a robust surrogate biomarker for prodromal AD identification and glial response surveillance. Mazzeo et al. demonstrated that plasma GFAP exhibits high discriminatory performance in differentiating healthy individuals from prodromal AD cases, supporting its routine integration into precision diagnostic and interventional workflows (Mazzeo et al., 2024). In parallel, Bonomi et al. reported elevated CSF sTREM2 levels across AD cohorts, with distinct biomarker trajectories stratified by patient subgroups; notably, TREM2-driven microglial activation is closely coupled with astrocytic reactivity and neurotoxic phenotypic transition, particularly within APOE ε4 carriers (Bonomi et al., 2023). Such evidence highlights the necessity of genotype-adjusted biomarker interpretation in microbiome-related AD clinical trials.

Baseline biomarker profiling further enables prognostic stratification of at-risk populations. Warmenhoven et al. identified baseline sTREM2, GFAP, and YKL-40 as predictive signatures for subsequent cognitive trajectory, wherein TREM2-associated microglial phenotypes correlate with preserved cognitive function independent of underlying amyloid-tau pathology (Warmenhoven et al., 2024). Complementarily, Pan et al. established plasma p-tau217 as a powerful classifier for amyloid and tau pathological staging, while GFAP serves as a reliable longitudinal indicator of tau pathology progression (Pan et al., 2025).

Beyond these core biomarkers, a personalized approach to SCFA‑based therapies requires integration of additional stratification layers that directly address the biological heterogeneity. First, baseline enterotype classification, such as Prevotella‑ versus Bacteroides‑dominant community structures, can predict individual responsiveness to fiber or probiotic interventions, as these enterotypes exhibit differential SCFA production capacities and metabolic outcomes (Arumugam et al., 2011). Second, target engagement biomarkers, including plasma and fecal SCFA concentrations, HDAC activity in peripheral blood mononuclear cells, and FFAR2/FFAR3 expression on circulating immune cells, provide proximal readouts of whether the intervention achieves its intended molecular effects. These markers are essential for distinguishing true biological non‑responders from those requiring dose or formulation adjustments. Third, genetic stratification by APOE, TREM2, and other AD risk loci identifies subgroups most likely to benefit or those needing dose modification. APOE ε4 carriers exhibit reduced abundance of butyrate‑producing taxa and diminished fecal SCFA concentrations (Tran et al., 2019), while TREM2 variant carriers may require higher SCFA exposure to achieve transcriptional rescue of phagocytic receptors (Patnala et al., 2017). Such genotype‑specific biology mandates that clinical trials either enrich for genetically defined subgroups or incorporate genotype‑stratified randomization and analysis plans. Fourth, inflammatory status biomarkers, including high‑sensitivity C‑reactive protein, IL‑6, and TNF‑α, help define the systemic inflammatory milieu that gates the NLRP3‑modulating effects of SCFAs (Wang et al., 2022). Under conditions of concurrent TLR stimulation, SCFAs can paradoxically activate inflammasome pathways; thus, baseline inflammatory status informs both patient selection and the timing of intervention initiation.

Integrating these layers into a practical decision‑making framework, several principle‑based recommendations can be formulated for future trial design. For individuals with low baseline fecal butyrate and elevated plasma GFAP in the absence of advanced tau pathology, early initiation of tributyrin supplementation combined with high‑fiber dietary counselling may be prioritized, as this profile suggests an intact neuroinflammatory reserve amenable to metabolic rescue. For APOE ε4 carriers with reduced TREM2 signaling signatures, potentially reflected by lower CSF sTREM2 or reduced microglial activation markers, combination strategies pairing butyrate with agents that enhance TREM2 expression or function may be required to overcome the genetic liability. For individuals with elevated systemic inflammatory markers, a run‑in phase with gut barrier‑restoring prebiotics (such as inulin or resistant starch) should precede direct SCFA supplementation to reduce the TLR stimulation that could otherwise convert butyrate into a pro‑inflammatory signal (Wang et al., 2022). These examples underscore that biomarker‑guided stratification is not a one‑time baseline assessment but a dynamic process requiring longitudinal monitoring to adjust interventions as the patient’s inflammatory and metabolic status evolves over the disease course.

The emerging discipline of pharmacomicrobiomics aims to integrate gut microbiome, metabolome, host genome, and immune phenotyping datasets to predict interindividual therapeutic responsiveness and rationalize personalized microbiome‑modulating treatment regimens (Elrakaiby et al., 2014; Doestzada et al., 2018). In the context of AD, this integrative framework enables patient stratification based on enterotype classification, baseline circulating SCFA profiles, SCFA receptor expression signatures, and genetic risk stratification, facilitating the precise identification of subpopulations most likely to derive clinical benefit from tailored microbial interventions (Ji et al., 2025; Radhakrishnan et al., 2025). Multi‑omic data integration poses substantial analytical challenges, demanding sophisticated bioinformatic pipelines to process high‑dimensional omics datasets, model longitudinal trajectory dynamics, and disentangle complex interactive relationships between microbial, metabolic, genetic, and immune variables (Qian et al., 2022; Knight et al., 2018). Machine learning algorithms trained on well‑annotated large‑scale clinical cohorts offer unprecedented potential for predictive modelling of individual SCFA therapeutic responses and high‑confidence identification of core intervention targets (Topçuoğlu et al., 2020; Pasolli et al., 2016).

Rational trial design for SCFA-centered gut microbe interventions should incorporate standardized run-in phases to stabilize baseline gut microbiome configuration, baseline enterotype categorization, and serial longitudinal multi-omic phenotyping (Hung et al., 2022). Primary and secondary endpoints should integrate composite cognitive outcome measures alongside objective evidence of target engagement, including fecal and circulating SCFA concentrations, HDAC activity indices, and peripheral immune profiling, as well as downstream neuroinflammatory readouts such as CSF cytokine signatures and microglial activation biomarkers. Challenges, controversies, and future perspectives

Despite considerable advances in delineating the mechanistic interplay between SCFA-producing commensal bacteria and neuroinflammatory regulation in AD, a cascade of unresolved challenges and persistent controversies continues to hinder bench-to-bedside clinical translation of microbiome-targeted therapeutic strategies. This section critically delineates prevailing evidentiary gaps, inherent technical bottlenecks, and conceptual limitations that must be systematically addressed to advance the development and precise clinical deployment of microbiome-based interventions for AD prevention and treatment (Sorboni et al., 2022).

Deficient causal evidence linking SCFA insufficiency to AD initiation

A fundamental limitation in current SCFA-AD research lies in the overwhelming predominance of associative observations over definitive causal validation. Although numerous clinical and preclinical studies have established consistent correlations between diminished fecal SCFA abundance and aggravated AD neuropathology (Qin et al., 2025; Marizzoni et al., 2025), rigorous mechanistic experimentation with controlled confounding variables remains indispensable for establishing directional causality. Colombo et al. provided pivotal causal evidence by demonstrating that microbiota-derived SCFAs directly modulate microglial phenotypic profiles and exacerbate Aβ plaque deposition in germ-free APP transgenic mice colonized with defined bacterial consortia (Colombo et al., 2021). Critically, reduced SCFA production in AD may also reflect age‑related physiological changes, altered dietary intake, polypharmacy, or disease progression itself, rather than a primary pathogenic event. Distinguishing these possibilities requires longitudinal studies that control for such confounders from preclinical stages. Nevertheless, such findings underscore the context-dependent complexity of SCFA bioactivity, whereby identical microbial metabolites exert either pro-pathological or neuroprotective effects across distinct experimental backgrounds and disease stages, and thus cannot be directly extrapolated to human AD without further causal validation (Erny et al., 2015; Ji et al., 2025).

Heavy reliance on conventional transgenic murine models represents another major translational constraint. Most preclinical AD investigations adopt APP/PS1, 5 × FAD, or 3 × Tg-AD mouse strains engineered to overexpress familial AD-related mutations, which substantially limits their extrapolative validity toward sporadic AD, the predominant clinical subtype in human populations (Radhakrishnan et al., 2025). Furthermore, laboratory mice harbor gut microbial configurations markedly distinct from human commensal ecosystems, raising critical concerns regarding the generalizability of rodent-derived mechanistic findings to clinical cohorts (Hugenholtz and De Vos, 2018). Humanized microbiota models, generated via colonization of germ-free mice with human fecal microbiota, partially mitigate this discrepancy but fail to fully recapitulate human intestinal architectural complexity, immune repertoire diversity, and lifelong dietary exposure patterns (Doifode et al., 2021; Lundberg et al., 2016).

Genetic dissection of SCFA signaling mechanisms remains confounded by inconsistent phenotypic outputs from FFAR2, FFAR3, and GPR109A knockout models across variable genetic backgrounds and experimental paradigms (Qian et al., 2022). Functional redundancy within SCFA downstream cascades further complicates mechanistic interpretation, as individual SCFA species concurrently activate multiple GPCRs and non-receptor targets such as HDACs. Resolving such pathway complexity necessitates the integration of conditional cell-type-specific gene deletion strategies alongside pharmacological receptor antagonism to deconvolve discrete signaling branches (Offermanns, 2014; Choi and Mook-Jung, 2023).

Discrepancy between SCFA concentration and actual bioactivity

Conventional SCFA quantification workflows predominantly measure static steady-state metabolite concentrations in fecal, plasma, and CSF matrices, offering limited mechanistic insight into real-time metabolic flux dynamics, tissue-specific bioavailability, and in vivo receptor engagement efficiency (Chen et al., 2022; Kuehn et al., 2024). Stable isotope tracing approaches utilizing ¹³C-labeled dietary substrates enable quantitative tracking of SCFA biosynthesis, systemic distribution, and tissue catabolism, representing a powerful tool for unraveling gut microbial metabolic kinetics; however, high technical costs and operational complexity restrict their large-scale application in human cohort studies (Kuehn et al., 2025; Nicholson et al., 2012).

Beyond mere abundance profiling, comprehensive evaluation of SCFA functional bioactivity requires systematic quantification of receptor activation intensity, downstream signaling cascade transduction, and epigenetic remodeling within neurologically relevant target tissues (Marizzoni et al., 2023). Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) targeting histone acetylation marks including H3K9ac, H3K14ac, and H3K27ac enables high-resolution mapping of SCFA-driven epigenetic modifications at gene-specific loci (Caetano-Silva et al., 2023). Nonetheless, such epigenetic analyses demand substantial tissue biomass and are ethically and technically infeasible for direct human brain specimen profiling, thereby limiting their clinical translational utility (Patnala et al., 2017). Peripheral surrogate biomarkers derived from plasma or peripheral blood mononuclear cells, reflecting HDAC inhibitory activity and SCFA receptor signaling tone, provide a more accessible alternative for longitudinal therapeutic response monitoring in clinical settings (Wei et al., 2023; Chang et al., 2014).

The development of positron emission tomography (PET) tracers selectively targeting SCFA receptors would enable non-invasive in vivo quantification of receptor expression density and ligand engagement within the living human brain, analogous to established amyloid and tau PET imaging modalities. Such molecular imaging tools would greatly facilitate participant stratification based on baseline target engagement and support individualized dose optimization in future SCFA-centered AD clinical trials (Jack et al., 2018).

Modulation from host genetic and physiological heterogeneity

Host genetic background exerts profound modulatory effects on gut microbial SCFA biosynthesis capacity and individual responsiveness to microbiome-targeted interventions (Ivanich et al., 2025). The APOE ε4 allele reshapes gut microbiota community structure and compromises downstream SCFA signaling transduction efficiency (Tran et al., 2019). Accumulating evidence indicates that APOE ε4 carriers exhibit characteristic microbial dysbiosis featured by depleted butyrate-producing taxa and aberrant cerebral SCFA receptor expression profiles (Colombo et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2025). Moreover, robust genotype-sex interactive effects on SCFA metabolic homeostasis have been identified, with female APOE ε4 carriers displaying particularly pronounced depletion of propionate-synthesizing commensals and accelerated cognitive deterioration trajectories (Holingue et al., 2020; Santos-Marcos et al., 2019).

Sex-dependent disparities extend far beyond APOE genetic interaction. Endogenous estrogen status dynamically modulates gut microbial composition and SCFA synthetic potential, whereas menopausal transition is tightly linked to diminished microbial alpha diversity and impaired butyrate biosynthesis capacity (Baker et al., 2017). Wang et al. established the estrogen–microbiota–brain axis as a critical modifier of AD susceptibility, suggesting that combinatorial regimens integrating hormone regulation and gut microbe modulation may confer particular neuroprotective benefits for postmenopausal female populations (Wang et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the optimal intervention timing, dosage regimen, and formulation design for such synergistic strategies remain poorly defined (Maki and Jaff, 2022).

Progressive age-associated alterations in gastrointestinal physiology, including attenuated gastric acid secretion, reduced intestinal peristalsis, and compromised mucosal barrier integrity, collectively impair intestinal SCFA production and systemic absorption efficiency (Biagi et al., 2012; O'toole and Jeffery, 2015). Elderly individuals frequently present age-related depletion of canonical butyrate producers such as Faecalibacterium and Roseburia independent of AD pathological status, complicating the differentiation of disease-specific microbial signatures from physiological aging-related microbial shifts (Claesson et al., 2012). Age-prevalent comorbidities including type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disorders, and chronic polypharmacy further confound microbiota-SCFA pathological associations, underscoring the necessity of personalized stratified intervention design in elderly AD cohorts (Jackson et al., 2018; Valles-Colomer et al., 2023).

Stage-dependent therapeutic windows for SCFA-based therapy

The temporal kinetics of SCFA-mediated neuromodulation along the AD pathological continuum remain incompletely characterized, carrying critical implications for optimal therapeutic intervention timing and window definition (Sperling et al., 2011). Preclinical evidence supports that SCFA supplementation administered at pre-symptomatic stages delays AD onset by reinforcing intestinal barrier integrity, establishing peripheral immune tolerance, and sustaining microglial homeostatic phenotypes (Zhou et al., 2023; Cuervo-Zanatta et al., 2023). By contrast, therapeutic intervention initiated at established dementia stages yields marginal clinical benefits, largely due to irreversible neuronal loss, pervasive synaptic degeneration, and persistent glial priming status that are refractory to metabolic modulation (Heneka et al., 2015; Perry and Holmes, 2014).

The concept of a microbiome-interventional therapeutic window aligns closely with the classical amyloid hypothesis, whereby anti-amyloid therapeutics exert maximal efficacy when deployed during early preclinical and prodromal disease phases (Cummings et al., 2021; Jack and Holtzman, 2013). Precise delineation of the SCFA-targeted therapeutic window requires large-scale longitudinal cohorts profiling dynamic alterations in microbiota configuration, circulating SCFA levels, and multi-modal pathological biomarkers across the full AD spectrum, ranging from preclinical asymptomatic stages through mild cognitive impairment to overt dementia (Kuehn et al., 2024). Recent advances in blood-based AD pathological biomarkers, including plasma phosphorylated tau isoforms and neurofilament light chain quantification, provide robust tools for refined participant stratification and trial cohort allocation in microbiome-centered clinical study design (Thijssen et al., 2020; Palmqvist et al., 2020).

Prospects for multi-omics precision research

The emerging discipline of pharmacomicrobiomics aims to integrate gut microbiome, metabolome, host genome, and immune phenotyping datasets to predict interindividual therapeutic responsiveness and rationalize personalized microbiome-modulating treatment regimens (Elrakaiby et al., 2014; Doestzada et al., 2018). In the context of AD, this integrative framework enables patient stratification based on enterotype classification, baseline circulating SCFA profiles, SCFA receptor expression signatures, and genetic risk stratification, facilitating the precise identification of subpopulations most likely to derive clinical benefit from tailored microbial interventions (Ji et al., 2025; Radhakrishnan et al., 2025).

Multi-omic data integration poses substantial analytical challenges, demanding sophisticated bioinformatic pipelines to process high-dimensional omics datasets, model longitudinal trajectory dynamics, and disentangle complex interactive relationships between microbial, metabolic, genetic, and immune variables (Qian et al., 2022; Knight et al., 2018). Machine learning algorithms trained on well-annotated large-scale clinical cohorts offer unprecedented potential for predictive modeling of individual SCFA therapeutic responses and high-confidence identification of core intervention targets (Topçuoğlu et al., 2020; Pasolli et al., 2016). Consortium initiatives such as the Alzheimer’s Disease Metabolomics Consortium provide invaluable shared resources for cross-cohort integrative analysis; nevertheless, unified standardization of specimen collection, laboratory processing, and analytical workflows remains essential to enhance cross-study reproducibility and comparability (Doifode et al., 2021; Toledo et al., 2017).

Bridging the existing gap from correlational association to mechanistic causality in SCFA-AD research ultimately requires well-designed prospective interventional trials embedded with rigorous biomarker stratification, mechanism-oriented study endpoints, and prolonged longitudinal clinical follow-up (Choi and Mook-Jung, 2023). Such trial frameworks should incorporate standardized run-in periods to stabilize baseline gut microbial and SCFA homeostasis, adaptive protocol design enabling real-time dosage optimization according to in vivo target engagement readouts, and composite clinical endpoints integrating cognitive functional assessments with neuroimaging and fluid biomarker profiling (Chen et al., 2022). Only through such systematic, multi-layered investigative strategies can the full translational potential of SCFA-producing gut commensals be harnessed as actionable precision therapeutic targets for AD prevention and disease modification (Cryan et al., 2019; Long and Holtzman, 2019).

Conclusions

Mounting preclinical and clinical evidence confirms that SCFAs-producing intestinal commensals function as indispensable homeostatic regulators of AD pathogenesis by orchestrating bidirectional communication along the microbiota-gut-brain axis. Dysbiotic depletion of core butyrate/propionate-synthesizing taxa consistently reduces systemic SCFA availability, dismantling multi-layered anti-inflammatory circuits spanning intestinal barrier homeostasis, peripheral immune tolerance and central neuroimmune equilibrium. Via complementary GPCR signaling, HDAC inhibition and metabolic reprogramming, SCFAs effectively constrain pathological microglial DAM transformation and toxic A1 astrocytic polarization, robustly block NLRP3 inflammasome-driven inflammatory cascades, and further mitigate Aβ deposition, aberrant tau propagation as well as pathological synaptic attrition.

Notably, SCFA-mediated bioeffects exhibit prominent context dependency, tightly dictated by metabolite concentration, disease progression stage, host genotype (notably APOE polymorphism) and systemic inflammatory milieu, which partially explains inconsistent outcomes observed in germ-free animal and human cohort studies. Current translational pipelines encompassing personalized dietary optimization, next-generation probiotics, engineered postbiotic formulations and standardized fecal microbiota transplantation show encouraging preclinical efficacy to recover endogenous SCFA homeostasis, yet substantial translational hurdles remain, including insufficient human causal proof, discordance between measured SCFA concentrations and actual in vivo bioactivity, and heterogeneous therapeutic responsiveness across variable host backgrounds.
