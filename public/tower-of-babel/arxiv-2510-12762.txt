The Motivic Picard–Lefschetz Formula Preliminary version Statements whose proofs will be expanded are marked with asterisks.
Ran Azouri, Emil Jacobsen

Abstract
We prove a motivic enhancement of the classical Picard–Lefschetz formula. Our proof is completely motivic, and yields a description of the motivic nearby cycles at a quasi-homogeneous singularity, as well as its monodromy, in terms of an embedding of projective hypersurfaces.

Introduction

Our main theorem is a motivic enhancement of the Picard–Lefschetz formula.
That is,
in the presence of an isolated singularity
in the special fibre,
we compute the nearby cycles motive
and the associated monodromy.
The nearby cycles functor
is an important tool
in complex geometry,

\ell
-adic theory,
and motives alike.
It plays a key role in the theory of perverse sheaves,
Hodge modules,
singularities,
etc.
The
\ell
-adic Picard–Lefschetz formula
is used in Deligne’s proof of the Weil conjectures.

In order to introduce the setting,
fix a base field
k
,
write
\mathbb{A}^{1}:=\mathbb{A}^{1}_{k}
,
and
let

f\colon X\to\mathbb{A}^{1}

be a family of schemes.
Denote the fibre over
0\in\mathbb{A}^{1}
by
X_{0}
.
Ayoub constructs
[
Ayo07a
,
Ayo14
]

the
(unipotent) motivic nearby cycles complex

\Psi_{f}\bm{1}\in\DA(X_{0})
,
in the infinity category of étale motives
with rational coefficients
over
X_{0}
.

under the Betti and

Ayoub also constructs [ibid.] a monodromy operator

The Picard–Lefschetz problem concerns the following situation:
assume that the generic fibre of
f\colon X\to\mathbb{A}^{1}
is smooth,
and that there is a single, isolated singularity
o

in the special fibre

.
The problem of computing

\Psi_{f}\bm{1}
and its monodromy
then reduces to
computing the
variation
, which describes the monodromy at the point.

\mathrm{var}\colon o^{*}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}\to o^{!}\Psi_{f}(-1).

In the context of singular and
\ell
-adic cohomology,
the classical Picard–Lefschetz formula provides a full answer
when

is assumed to be a quadratic homogeneous singularity.
The
\ell
-adic formula is due to Deligne
[
SGA7
, Exp. XV]
.
Illusie
[
Ill02
]
gives a purely algebraic proof,
without relying on transcendental methods.
Our proof is motivic,
and does not use any realization functors.
We also work with a general, (quasi-)homogeneous singularity.

Proof strategy.

Our proof of the theorem goes through a series of reductions.
For the first step, we show how to
replace
f
by a semistable family

g\colon Y\to\mathbb{A}^{1}

with two branches,
similar to the approach of Illusie,
obtaining the map
g
from
f
by
base-change, blowup, and normalization.
We then have to keep track of how
nearby cycles motive
and the monodromy
changes as we replace
f
in this way.
The outcome is
a computation of the motives

o^{*}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}

and
o^{!}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}

as in the statement of Thm.
1.1
.
We also compute the variation,

\mathrm{var}
,
but only up to
an
a priori
unknown rational number
\lambda
.
This is the content of
§
3
.

In order to determine
\lambda

we first replace the general semistable family
g

by the one-dimensional family

g\colon\Spec k[t,x,y]/(xy-t)\to\mathbb{A}^{1},

(Prop.
3.11
).
We then reduce computing the variation of this family
to computing the monodromy of the Kummer motive

\mathcal{K}\in\DA(\mathbb{G}_{m})
,
as it turns out that
\Psi_{g}\bm{1}

is closely related to
\Psi_{\mathrm{id}}\mathcal{K}
.
This is the content of §
4
.

In the last step, we compute this monodromy, i.e.,

N\colon\Psi_{\mathrm{id}}\mathcal{K}\to\Psi_{\mathrm{id}}\mathcal{K}(-1).

By opening up Ayoub’s construction
of the motivic monodromy operator,
we reduce this to a concrete question about the Kummer and logarithm motives.
We then ascertain the value of
\lambda
to be
-1
,
finishing the proof of
Thm.
1.1
.
This is the content of §

Historical context.

The formula originates in complex geometry,
in the work of Picard
[
PS97
]

for a holomorphic function on a surface,
and Lefschetz
[
Lef24
]

for higher dimensional manifolds.
In
[
SGA7
, éxposé XV]
,
Deligne proves an algebro-geometric formula
in the setting of étale cohomology.
This result plays a crucial role in
his proof of the Weil conjectures.
While Deligne’s proof uses transcendental methods,
a purely algebraic proof for the formula
is given by Illusie
[
Ill02
]
.

Remark 1.4
.

It has been brought to our attention that the problem
of motivically enhancing the Picard–Lefschetz formula
was considered independently by Roland Casalis,
a former graduate student of Frédéric Déglise,
with no results published.

2.4
The Kummer motive

Key to Ayoub’s definition of the motivic monodromy operator,
and to the proof of our main theorem,
is the Kummer motive.
Before we recall its definition,
we remind the reader about the standard computation
of the motive of
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}
.

Remark 2.13

(The motive of
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}
.)
.

Consider the usual, complementary embeddings

j\colon\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{A}^{1}
,
and
i=0\colon\mathrm{pt}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{A}^{1}
.
Gluing along these (
[
Ayo07
, Lemme 1.4.6]
)
gives a fibre sequence

i_{!}i^{!}\bm{1}\to\bm{1}\to j_{*}j^{*}\bm{1}.

As
i
is proper,
i_{!}=i_{*}
,
and by purity (
[
Ayo07
, § 1.6.3]
),

i^{!}\bm{1}=\bm{1}(-1)[-2]
.
Our sequence becomes

i_{*}\bm{1}(-1)[-2]\to\bm{1}\to j_{*}\bm{1}.

Upon shifting the sequence,
applying
p_{*}
,
and using
h(\mathbb{A}^{1})=h(\mathrm{pt})=\bm{1}
,
we get a fibre sequence

\bm{1}\to h(\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}})\to\bm{1}(-1)[-1].

The canonical point

1\colon\mathrm{pt}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}

splits the leftmost map,
yielding

h(\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}})=\bm{1}\oplus\bm{1}(-1)[-1].

With this,
we are ready to define to recall
Ayoub’s definition of the Kummer motive.
We use the notation

\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}\xrightarrow{\Delta}\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}\times\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{pr}_{1}}\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}},

for the diagonal and the projection onto the first factor, respectively.
We also need the horizontal section

\mathrm{id}\times 1\colon\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}\to\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}\times\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}
.
As in Rmk.
2.13
,
the point
1\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}
induces
an isomorphism

\mathrm{pr}_{1,*}\mathrm{pr}_{1}^{*}\bm{1}\simeq\bm{1}\oplus\bm{1}(-1)[-1].

In particular there is a map

\bm{1}(-1)[-1]\to\mathrm{pr}_{1,*}\mathrm{pr}_{1}^{*}\bm{1}
,
which we use in the following definition.

Definition 2.14

(
[
Ayo07a
, Def. 3.6.22, Lemme 3.6.28]
)
.

1.

The composition

\bm{1}(-1)[-1]\to\mathrm{pr}_{1,*}\mathrm{pr}_{1}^{*}\bm{1}\to\mathrm{pr}_{1,*}\Delta_{*}\Delta^{*}\mathrm{pr}_{1}^{*}\bm{1}\simeq\bm{1},

is the
Kummer map
.
We denote it by

.

2.

The
Kummer motive
, denoted

,
is the cofibre of the Kummer map

\bm{1}(-1)[-1]\xrightarrow{e_{\mathrm{K}}}\bm{1}\xrightarrow{c_{\mathrm{K}}}\mathcal{K}.

2.6
Monodromy and variation

Consider a quasi-projective family
f\colon X\to\mathbb{A}^{1}

and recall Notation
2.1
.
Let

\chi_{f}:=i^{*}j_{*}

denote the canonical specialization functor
(
[
Ayo07a
, Ex. 3.1.4]
).
By
[
Ayo14
, Thm. 11.16]
,
there is a canonical cofibre sequence

\chi_{f}\to\Psi_{f}\xrightarrow{N}\Psi_{f}(-1)

and the map
N\colon\Psi_{f}\to\Psi_{f}(-1)

is called the
monodromy operator
.

There is no monodromy on
\Psi_{f}\bm{1}
when
f
is smooth:
in this case,

\Psi_{f}\bm{1}\simeq\bm{1}

(by Prop.
2.17

and Prop.
2.18
),
and there is no non-zero map
\bm{1}\to\bm{1}(-1)

(Prop.
2.12
).
This leads to the monodromy operator being
supported on the singular locus of
f
.
More precisely,
let

i\colon Z\hookrightarrow X_{\sigma}

be a closed subscheme containing the singular locus of
X_{\sigma}
,
and denote its open complement by
j\colon U\hookrightarrow X_{\sigma}
.
By gluing
[
Ayo07
, Lemme 1.4.6]
,
and the vanishing of
j^{*}N
, we get

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces j_{!}j^{*}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Psi_{f}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{*}i^{*}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces j_{!}j^{*}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}(-1)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Psi_{f}(-1)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{*}i^{*}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}(-1).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces N}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{*}i^{*}N}

Thus,
the monodromy operator factors through the unit map

\Psi_{f}\bm{1}\to i_{*}i^{*}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}
.
Using the dual localization sequence,
we get in the same way that
it factors through the natural map

i_{*}i^{!}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}(-1)\to\Psi_{f}\bm{1}(-1)
.
In summary:

Proposition 2.21
.

There is a unique map

\mathrm{var}\colon i^{*}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}\to i^{!}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}(-1)
,
which we call the
variation map
(over
Z
),
that factors the monodromy operator

as

\Psi_{f}\bm{1}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{un}}i_{*}i^{*}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}\xrightarrow{i_{*}\mathrm{var}}i_{*}i^{!}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}(-1)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{coun}}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}(-1).

∎

There are times when we need to know
a bit more about the construction of the monodromy operator.

Construction 2.22

(
[
Ayo14
, § 11]
)

3
First reduction:
to a semistable family

In this section we describe how to reduce
computing the monodromy from
the case of a homogeneous or quasi-homogeneous singularity to
that of a special semistable family.

3.1
The variation map and semistable reduction

Here we wish to express
\mathrm{var}_{f}
in terms of
\mathrm{var}_{g}
for a semistable reduction construction. In order to do that we have to keep track of how base change, proper morphisms, and smooth morphisms affect the variation map. First we inspect how base change affects the unipotent nearby cycles and the monodromy.

Notation 6.1

Proposition 6.2

.

Let

be a smooth quadric of dimension n. Then

\bigoplus_{i=0}^{n}\bm{1}\{-i\}\oplus\bm{1}\{-n/2\}&\text{n even}\end{cases}.

These computations have been done by Rost in
[

]
for Chow motives. Since effective Chow motives embed fully faithfully in Voevodsky motives (
[

, Prop. 20.1]
), this is also true in
\DA(\text{--})
.
∎

Definition 6.3
.

We say that

is a smooth affine quadric of dimension

if it is isomorphic to

where

is a smooth projective quadric of dimension
n
,

is a projective hyperplane, and

is smooth. Note that

is then a smooth projective quadric of dimension

, embedded in

.

Proposition 6.4

(*)
.

be a smooth affine quadric of dimension

\bm{1}(-\lfloor n/2\rfloor)[-n]\xrightarrow{m_{2}}o^{!}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}\to\bm{1}(-n)[-2n].

First, changing coordinates and completing to a square, we may assume that

is given by a quadratic form

, and

is given by

We now recall Rost’s proof for the motive of a quadric, which goes by induction as follows (

): first we can change coordinates if necessary to write

. Then consider

is isomorphic to an affine space and therefore its motive is trivial.
Note that

has the point

, which allows proceeding by induction. The same can be done with

, and we get that

is described by the following matrix
(see

):

\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{id}&0&0\\

0&s\{-1\}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Here

is the inclusion
C_{0}\setminus p\hookrightarrow Q_{0}\setminus p
, and

is the fundamental class.
Since
i^{\prime}
is equivalent to an immersion of quadrics, induced by quadrics of dimensions lower by
2
, we may proceed by induction and get an explicit description for

.

If

is odd,

is given by

\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{id}&0&0\\
0&0&1\\

n=2m
is even, then

i^{*}\colon h(Q)=\bigoplus_{i=0,i\neq m}^{n}\bm{1}\{-i\}\oplus\bm{1}\{-m\}^{\oplus 2}\to h(C)=\bigoplus_{i=0,i\neq m}^{n-1}\bm{1}\{-i\}\oplus\bm{1}\{-m\},

\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{id}&0&0&0\\

Theorem 6.5

(The quadratic Picard–Lefschetz formula, *)
.

Assume
k
is algebraically closed of characteristic different from
2
.
Let
X
be regular and let

f\colon X\to{\mathbb{A}}^{1}_{k}

be a flat, quasi-projective morphism of relative dimension
n
,
smooth except for
an isolated quadratic singularity
o\in X_{\sigma}
. If
n
is even, then the map
\mathrm{var}
is the zero map.
If
n=2m+1
is odd,

-\mathrm{var}
factors as

o^{*}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}\xrightarrow{m_{1}}\bm{1}(-m-1)[-n]\xrightarrow{m_{2}(-1)}o^{!}\Psi_{f}\bm{1}(-1),

with notation as in Prop.
6.4
.

Proof.

When
n
is even, the result follows immediately from Prop.
6.4
, applying Prop.
2.12
.

For the odd case consider the following diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bm{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bm{1}(-n)[-2n]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h(A)}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h_{\mathrm{c}}(A)(-1)}

.
