VoQA: Visual-only Question Answering
Luyang Jiang, Jianing An, Jie Luo, Wenjun Wu, Lei Huang

Abstract
We propose Visual-only Question Answering (VoQA) , a novel multimodal task in which questions are visually embedded within images, without any accompanying textual input. This requires models to locate, recognize, and reason over visually embedded textual questions, posing challenges for existing large vision-language models (LVLMs), which show notable performance drops even with carefully designed prompts. To bridge this gap, we introduce Guided Response Triggering Supervised Fine-tuning (GRT-SFT) , a structured fine-tuning strategy that guides the model to perform step-by-step reasoning purely based on visual input, significantly improving model performance. Our work enhances models’ capacity for human-like visual understanding in complex multimodal scenarios, where information, including language, is perceived visually. 1 1 1 The code and data are available at: https://github.com/LuyangJ/VoQA

Introduction

Visual Question Answering (VQA) is a multimodal task that requires a model to answer a question based on the content of a given image. Since the release of the VQA v1/v2 datasets
[
4
,
9
]
, the field has rapidly evolved, spawning a wide array of benchmark datasets targeting diverse skills and domains. For instance, GQA
[
13
]
focuses on compositional reasoning, OK-VQA
[
23
]
requires external knowledge, TextVQA
[
27
]
introduces OCR-based visual language understanding, while ScienceQA
[
22
]
emphasizes scientific reasoning and multimodal explanation.

Fueled by the advancement of Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs)
[
3
,
1
,
16
,
18
,
5
]
, recent models have achieved remarkable performance on these VQA tasks. A key assumption in most benchmarks is that the question is given explicitly in natural language, but this is often overlooked. This assumption greatly simplifies the real-world setting, where textual queries may appear embedded within the visual environment, on signboards, diagrams, screens, or scanned documents, without being separately parsed and provided to the model.

In contrast, humans process such information through vision alone. When navigating the physical world, we perceive language visually (e.g., reading a street sign or a poster), without a distinct language channel apart from vision. Similarly, a future AI agent interacting with a computer through physical control, such as using a camera to "watch" the screen, must interpret all on-screen text purely through visual perception, without access to underlying text data. This raises a fundamental question:
Can an AI system understand and reason over visually presented texts, and answer questions accurately, using only visual input, without access to explicit textual prompts?

To bridge this gap, we introduce
Visual-only Question Answering (VoQA)
, a novel task that simulates a more realistic interaction scenario for intelligent agents by modeling the way humans understand textual information solely through visual perception. In VoQA, the input consists solely of a single composite image that visually embeds a textual question alongside contextual visual content. The model must perceive, understand, and reason over both the visual scene and the embedded question, all through a unified visual input stream. A conceptual comparison between traditional VQA and VoQA is illustrated in Figure
1
.

To support this task, we construct a large-scale dataset,
VoQA Dataset
, and a comprehensive evaluation benchmark,
VoQA Benchmark
. The training set of VoQA Dataset is derived by transforming instruction tuning data from LLaVA
[
18
]
into rendered visual-only inputs through text-image rendering and composition techniques. For evaluation, VoQA Benchmark is built by converting existing VQA datasets into the visual-only format, using the same rendering pipeline. It includes tasks adapted from VQAv2, GQA, POPE
[
17
]
, TextVQA, and ScienceQA, resulting in over
3.35M
training samples and
134k
evaluation samples. We evaluate open-source LVLMs on this benchmark and observe a significant performance drop compared to traditional VQA setting, revealing the limitations of current models in visual-language reasoning under visual-only inputs.

Following prior works
[
18
,
34
,
8
]
, we first apply standard supervised fine-tuning on composite images and the corresponding responses of the embedded question, expecting the model to learn to directly generate correct responses. However, this naive SFT yields significantly lower accuracy on VoQA compared to traditional VQA benchmarks, demonstrating its inadequacy for tasks with visually embedded questions. Failure cases reveal that models often resort to generic image captions or merely repeat the embedded question, indicating they cannot reliably parse the visual prompt or perform the necessary reasoning to produce the correct answer.

To address this challenge, we propose
Guided Response Triggering Supervised Fine-tuning (GRT-SFT)
, a novel supervised fine-tuning strategy tailored for VoQA.
GRT-SFT inserts a dedicated trigger token (e.g.,
ASSISTANT
) and applies loss over the entire
question + trigger token + answer
sequence. This explicit structure guides the model first to locate and interpret the embedded question and then reason to generate responses. Empirically, GRT-SFT brings noticeable gains on most VoQA benchmark tasks.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

•

We propose
VoQA
, a new task for vision-only multimodal reasoning, which removes the dependency on separate natural language input.

•

We build the
VoQA Dataset
and
VoQA Benchmark
, a large-scale dataset and evaluation suite for the VoQA task.

•

We introduce
GRT-SFT
, a structured fine-tuning method that significantly boosts model performance on the VoQA task.

Related Works

2.1
Visual Question Answering

Visual Question Answering (VQA) began with static-image datasets emphasizing object recognition and basic reasoning, such as VQAv1
[
4
]
and its balanced successor VQAv2
[
9
]
. Region-level grounding and diverse “W” questions appeared in Visual7W
[
36
]

[
14
]
. GQA scaled up to 22 M scene-graph-annotated questions
[
13
]
, and VizWiz-VQA introduced real-world robustness with user-generated, low-quality images
[
10
]
. Task-bias analyses have been conducted in TDIUC
[
15
]
and external-knowledge challenges have been explored in OK-VQA
[
23
]
. TextVQA
[
27
]
and OCR-VQA
[
25
]
pushed models to read and reason over scene text, while ChartQA probed data-driven logical reasoning on charts
[
24
]
. More recently, large-scale, multilingual, and multi-scenario benchmarks have broadened coverage: MMBench-EN/CN evaluates bilingual generalization across 20 ability dimensions
[
19
]
, and MEGA-Bench scales to over 500 real-world multimodal tasks with fine-grained metrics
[
6
]
. Finally, multilingual text-centric QA in video and multi-image settings is benchmarked by MTVQA
[
28
]
.

Although these methods vary and explore different aspects of multimodal capabilities, they all use a textual description of the question as a separate input to the model. In contrast, our VoQA task embeds the question directly as rendered text within the image, requiring unifying perception, text recognition, and reasoning without any explicit textual prompt.

Large Vision-Language Models

Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) have emerged as a powerful paradigm for integrating visual and textual modalities. Early breakthroughs, such as CLIP

With the advancement of Large Language Models(LLMs)

with LLMs. This architecture enables a wide range of capabilities, including image captioning
[
11

, visual question answering(VQA)
[

,

,
25

. Representative works such as BLIP-2
[

[

bridge vision and language via carefully designed pretraining and alignment strategies. Among them, LLaVA
[
18
]
stands out by performing instruction tuning with multi-turn vision-language dialogue data, allowing the model to generate context-aware and coherent responses. Recently, the field has seen a surge in high-performance, open-source LVLMs, including Qwen2.5-VL
[
5

, Deepseek-VL2
[

Despite these advances, existing LVLMs predominantly rely on explicit natural language prompts as guidance for perception and reasoning. Their cross-modal understanding heavily depends on language priors provided in the input, raising the question of whether these models can still function effectively in the absence of explicit textual instructions. This motivates us to investigate the fundamental visual understanding capabilities of LVLMs when instructions are embedded within the visual input.

VoQA Dataset and Benchmark

3.1
Dataset Construction

Figure 2
:
Examples of concatenation and watermark rendering. (a) One example of bottom concatenation without resizing, and two examples with resizing (left and right). (b) Two examples of watermark rendering with different text colors.

In this part, we introduce the construction of the VoQA Dataset and VoQA Benchmark, including the text-image rendering methods used to generate visual-only inputs and the data sources used for training and evaluation.

Text-Image Rendering Methods.

Given an image
\mathbf{X}
and a textual question or instruction
\mathbf{Y}_{q}
, to generate the composite image
\mathbf{X}_{c}
in the VoQA task (see Figure
2
), we first convert the textual question
\mathbf{Y}_{q}
into RGB-format images
\mathbf{X}_{q}
of size
224\times 224
using the
DejaVuSans-Bold
font. These rendered images are integrated with the original visual images via two methods:

(1) Concatenation.
We concatenate

\mathbf{X}_{q}
and
\mathbf{X}
either by resizing or padding to align shapes, and explore four placement positions (top, bottom, left, right). This method maintains the independence of textual and visual information in the layout.

(2) Watermark Rendering.
To enable more integrated visual-textual understanding, we render
\mathbf{X}_{q}
as a watermark within the image
\mathbf{X}
. Inspired by the sliding window approach, we select the embedding region based on multiple criteria (variance, contrast, and gradient) to ensure minimal occlusion and maximal readability. Final watermark colors are selected based on WCAG contrast guidelines. Technical implementation details, including candidate region scoring, contrast-aware color selection, are provided in Appendix
A.1
.

Training Dataset.

We construct the VoQA training dataset by repurposing the vision-language instruction tuning data released by LLaVA
[
18
]
. Each training sample consists of a composite image, which is generated from an original image and its corresponding textual question using the methods described above, along with an associated textual answer. The final training set comprises over
3.35 million
samples.

Evaluation Benchmark.

To evaluate model performance on the VoQA task, we build the VoQA Benchmark by transforming five widely-used VQA datasets, VQAv2
[
9
]
, GQA
[
13
]
, POPE
[
17
]
, TextVQA
[
27
]
, and ScienceQA-IMG
[
22
]
(denoted as SQA), into the visual-only format. Each image-question pair is converted into a single composite image via text-image rendering. To ensure visual clarity, we filter out overly long questions in SQA to avoid unreadable text regions. The benchmark covers a broad range of question types and reasoning skills, and consists of over
134k
evaluation samples. For all subsequent experiments, we adopt the watermark rendering strategy to generate the composite images used for both training and evaluation.

4
Fine-tuning on the VoQA Dataset for Enhancing Model Performances

Figure 4
:
Comparison of three supervised fine-tuning strategies: (a) Baseline-SFT directly supervises on the answer tokens only; (b) QA-SFT includes both the question and answer in the label sequence; (c) GRT-SFT (Ours) inserts a trigger token between the quesiton and answer, and applies supervision over the the entire sequence:
question + trigger token + answer

Given the poor zero-shot performance of existing LVLMs on the VoQA task, fine-tuning emerges as a necessary step to improve model capability under this setting. In the vision-language community, Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) has become a standard approach for aligning pre-trained LVLMs with downstream tasks. Methods such as InstructBLIP
[
8
]
, LLaVA
[
18
]
, and MiniGPT-4
[
35
]
construct instruction-response pairs to fine-tune models on multi-turn or diverse task formats, enabling them to better follow user instructions and generalize to new tasks.

Baseline-SFT.

Following these prior works, we first adopt a naive SFT strategy tailored to the VoQA task, where composite images are used as inputs and direct answers are targets. This supervised fine-tuning approach aims to directly guide the model to produce an answer given an image containing an embedded question. This naive approach indeed improves the model’s ability to produce concise answers, avoiding generic captions or repetitions of the embedded text. However, our experiments reveal that it still falls short: models often generate hallucinated responses that are semantically irrelevant to the actual question, and overall performance remains lower than on traditional VQA benchmarks. We hypothesize that this limited performance stems from the model’s inability to fully comprehend the embedded question. Without explicitly attending to the question, the model tends to produce uncontrolled or contextually misaligned responses. See Section
4.2.1
and Appendix
A.3
for detailed analyses and examples.

QA-SFT.

To address this issue, we reconsider how humans perform visual-only question answering: typically, one first identifies the question within the image, and then performs reasoning based on the visual content. Motivated by this intuition, we explore
QA-SFT
, a fine-tuning strategy that explicitly supervises both the question and answer tokens. This encourages the model to focus on the visually embedded question before generating a response. However, we observe that while the model becomes better at interpreting the embedded question, it often fails to distinguish clearly between the question and the answer during generation. In some cases, it repeats the question, omits the answer, or generates responses that blend both. We attribute this to the lack of structural guidance in the decoding process. This motivates us to introduce a more controlled fine-tuning strategy, one that explicitly separates recognition from generation through a guided signal, which we detail next. We provide further analysis in Section
4.2.1
, with representative failure cases discussed in Appendix
A.3
.

4.1
Guided Response Triggering Supervised Fine-tuning (GRT-SFT)

To overcome the limitations of QA-SFT, we propose
Guided Response Triggering Supervised Fine-tuning (GRT-SFT)
, a structured fine-tuning strategy specifically designed to the VoQA setting. GRT-SFT builds upon QA-SFT by introducing a special
trigger token T
(e.g.,
ASSISTANT
) inserted between the embedded question and its answer during training. This token serves as an explicit signal to guide the model from understanding the question to generating the answer.

By supervising the full sequence,
question + trigger token + answer
, GRT-SFT enforces a structured reasoning process that mirrors human behavior: first locating and interpreting the question, then producing a focused and coherent response. This structured supervision significantly improves answer relevance and reduces response omissions or hallucinations, which are common failure modes in previous strategies. A visual comparison of the supervision strategies used in Baseline-SFT, QA-SFT, and GRT-SFT is illustrated in Figure
4
, highlighting the evolution from flat answer supervision to structured reasoning guidance.

Formally, given the input composite image

\mathbf{X}_{c}
, the textual question about the image
\mathbf{Y}_{q}
, the corresponding assistant’s response
\mathbf{Y}_{a}
, and the trigger token
\mathbf{T}
(
ASSISTANT
), the text sequence is structured as:

\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbf{Y}_{q},\mathbf{T},\mathbf{Y}_{a}).

We optimize the model to autoregressively generate the sequence
\mathbf{Y}
conditioned on
\mathbf{X}_{c}
by maximizing the log-likelihood:

\max_{\theta,\psi,\phi}\log p(\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{X}_{c})=\max_{\theta,\psi,\phi}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\log F_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},P_{\psi}\circ V_{\phi}(\mathbf{X}_{c})),

(1)

where
F_{\theta}
is the language model,

V_{\phi}
is the visual encoder,
P_{\psi}
is the modality connector, and
\mathbf{Y}=\{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{N}\}
denotes the tokenized output sequence.

Compared to conventional multimodal instruction tuning approaches such as LLaVA
[
18
]
, which leverage manually designed templates and system prompts to inject task-specific priors, GRT-SFT offers a fundamentally different supervision paradigm. In LLaVA-style tuning, the instruction is provided as explicit textual input, serving as a strong prior that the model can directly attend to during training. In contrast, GRT-SFT does not feed the instruction text as a separate input. Instead, it trains the model to infer and structure its reasoning path entirely from the visual input: first predicting the embedded question, then generating the trigger token, and finally producing the answer. By introducing a structured prior into the response process, the model is better guided to follow the task-specific reasoning path required by VoQA, resulting in stronger semantic alignment.

4.2
Experiments for Empirical Study

We conduct experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed GRT-SFT strategy in comparison with two alternative fine-tuning strategies: Baseline-SFT and QA-SFT. To ensure a comprehensive assessment, we select three representative models for fine-tuning under each strategy: (1) TinyLLaVA-Qwen2-0.5B-SigLIP (denoted as TinyLLaVA-0.5B), a small model pretrained following the framework of Zhou et al.
[
34
]
; (2) Qwen2-VL-2B
[
30
]
, a mid-sized model that has not undergone visual instruction tuning; and (3) LLaVA-v1.5-7B
[
18
]
, a larger model that has been instruction-tuned on traditional VQA tasks.

Experimental Setup.

For fair comparisons, each model is fine-tuned with identical hyperparameters across the three strategies. During the fine-tuning stage, we fine-tune the connector and language model of TinyLLaVA-0.5B for one epoch, using the Adamw
[
20
]
optimizer, a cosine scheduler
[
21
]
with a warmup ratio of 0.03, a learning rate of 2e-5, and a batch size of 128. For Qwen2-VL-2B and LLaVA-v1.5-7B, we initialize both models from the publicly available checkpoints and train them for one epoch with LoRA
[
12
]
of rank 8, using the Adamw optimizer, a cosine scheduler with a warmup ratio of 0.1, a learning rate of 1e-5 for Qwen2-VL-2B, a learning rate of 5e-6 for LLaVA-v1.5-7B and a batch size of 64. All experiments are conducted on 8 x NVIDIA A100 GPUs with 40GB of memory each.

Conclusion

In this work, we propose
VoQA
, a novel multimodal task for visual-only question answering, where textual questions are embedded within images. Our results reveal that existing LVLMs, despite excelling in traditional VQA, struggle in this setting. To address this gap, we introduce the
VoQA Benchmark
and
GRT-SFT
, a structured fine-tuning strategy that boosts model performance on VoQA and lays a foundation for future research in this direction.

Limitation and Future Work.

Due to limited computational resources, we are unable to verify the effectiveness of GRT-SFT on larger-scale models. However, we expect that with stronger vision-language backbones, GRT-SFT would achieve even better performance on the VoQA benchmark, given its model-agnostic and scalable design.

Future work includes exploring richer visual text styles (e.g., varied fonts, orientations, or layouts), extending VoQA to multilingual or more realistic image domains, and applying it to embodied agents requiring visual instruction following. Moreover, leveraging reinforcement learning to optimize structural supervision may further enhance the generalization of GRT-SFT across diverse tasks and domains.

Details of Data Rendering Methods

Concatenation

Following CLIPPO
[
29
]
, we first try to concatenate the image
\mathbf{X}
with the rendered text image
\mathbf{X}_{q}
.

Considering the difference of side lengths between
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{X}_{q}
, we use two concatenation methods with different effects, and also take into account the four relative positions of the textual question on the top, bottom, left and right of the composite image.

For the concatenation method with resizing, we fix the larger size in
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{X}_{q}
, and enlarge the smaller size to the same side length. For the concatenation method without resize, we align the center of the two images and fill in the blank space with the padding of the white background.

Watermark Rendering

We first identify the most suitable region for rendering the watermark to minimize the impact of poor text readability. The watermark’s side length is set to 25% of the short side of the image
\mathbf{X}
. To balance efficiency and quality, candidate regions are generated using a stride of 1/4 the image side length. Each region is scored based on gradient, variance, and contrast (weighted 40%, 40%, and 20%, respectively), reflecting smoothness and color contrast to ensure readability.

Next, we determine the watermark color in HSV space based on the average hue, saturation, and value of the selected region. The hue is set to the complementary hue (offset by 180° on the color wheel). If the saturation exceeds 200, the watermark saturation is set to 0.8 times that of the region; otherwise, it is set to the maximum value of 255. The value (brightness) is inverted: if the region’s value exceeds 127, the watermark value is set to 0; otherwise, 255.

Finally, we render
\mathbf{X}_{q}
onto
\mathbf{X}
. Following the WCAG standard
2
2

2

https://www.w3.org/Translations/WCAG21-zh/
, if the contrast ratio between the watermark and background exceeds 4.5, we retain the computed color; otherwise, we choose either black or white, selecting the one with greater contrast.

A.2.2
Response Filtering Method

To fairly evaluate model performance on the VoQA Benchmark, we apply standardized response filtering, since models may produce answers in varying formats, while the benchmark datasets require concise responses, typically a single letter, word, or phrase.

For zero-shot models, we adopt multiple parsing strategies to extract the actual answer from the response. These include formats such as “The answer is <Answer>” or “Answer: <Answer>”. Dataset-specific rules are also considered. For instance, POPE only evaluates whether the response contains “no” or “not”, so when the exact answer location is uncertain, we retain the full sentence to preserve contextual clues.

For fine-tuned models, filtering strategies vary depending on the fine-tuning approach. For all models fine-tuned using Baseline-SFT, QA-SFT, and GRT-SFT, we extract the segment following the last occurrence of a trigger token as the answer. Specifically for QA-SFT, where responses often follow a “Question + Answer” format, we use the last sentence as the final predicted answer.

q
is the ground-truth question and
\hat{q}
is the question predicted by the model. Here,
EditDistance
refers to the minimum number of character-level operations (insertions, deletions, substitutions) needed to transform one string into another. A higher QRA value indicates better question recognition, which correlates with reasoning success.

We investigate the impact of inserting the
ASSISTANT
token during inference by comparing model performance with and without it (Table
7
). The three SFT variants, Baseline-SFT, QA-SFT, and GRT-SFT, differ fundamentally in their training objectives and decoding strategies, which explains the varying influence of the
ASSISTANT
token.

For
Baseline-SFT
, the model is directly trained to generate answers without intermediate steps. Thus, appending the
ASSISTANT
token during inference does not significantly affect performance, as the model’s decoding process remains largely unchanged.

In contrast,
QA-SFT
and
GRT-SFT
are trained with a structured approach where the model first generates the visual question understanding step before producing the answer. Introducing the
ASSISTANT
token at inference essentially signals the model to skip this critical question comprehension phase and directly produce an answer. Consequently, this leads to a notable drop in accuracy across all sub-benchmarks, as the model misses important reasoning steps required to correctly interpret the visually embedded question.

This effect is especially pronounced in
GRT-SFT
, where the model is explicitly trained to use the
ASSISTANT
token as a deliberate transition point between question comprehension and answer generation. Interrupting this learned sequence at inference degrades performance significantly.

In summary, the
ASSISTANT
token serves as a response-triggering cue during inference.
