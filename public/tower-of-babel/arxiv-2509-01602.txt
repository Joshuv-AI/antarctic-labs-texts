Joint equidistribution of newforms
Asbjørn Christian Nordentoft 1, Radu Toma 2

Abstract
. Let Y 1 Y_{1} be a compact arithmetic hyperbolic surface associated to a maximal quaternion order, let Y q Y_{q} be a cover associated to an Eichler suborder of prime level q q , and let ι q \iota_{q} be embedding of Y q Y_{q} as the Hecke correspondence into Y 1 × Y 1 Y_{1}\times Y_{1} . Let μ 1 \mu_{1} and μ q \mu_{q} be the invariant probability measures on Y 1 Y_{1} and Y q Y_{q} , respectively. If F F is a newform on Y q Y_{q} , we conjecture that the pushforward measure ( ι q ) ∗ ​ ( | F | 2 ​ μ q ) (\iota_{q})_{\ast}(\lvert{F}\rvert^{2}\mu_{q}) converges weakly to the uniform measure μ 1 × μ 1 \mu_{1}\times\mu_{1} , as q q tends to infinity. We prove this conjecture with an effective rate of equidistribution, assuming GRH.



1.
Introduction

Understanding the distribution of mass of Laplace eigenfunctions on hyperbolic manifolds is a profound subject with roots in quantum chaos (see
[
Ber02
]
).
It has proven particularly fruitful to consider arithmetic manifolds and employ number theory to address this problem (see
[
Sar95
]
).
This article proposes a novel and deeper study of the interplay between quantum chaos and number theory by investigating an equidistribution problem that combines both quantum chaos and the equidistribution of Hecke points.

Let
B
be an indefinite quaternion algebra over
\mathbb{Q}
(in this introduction, the reader may simply consider the split case
B=M_{2}
).
Choose a maximal order and, for each integer
q\geq 1
coprime to the discriminant of
B
, take an Eichler order of level
q
and define its associated hyperbolic surface
Y_{q}=\Gamma_{q}\backslash\mathbb{H}
(in the split case, these are the modular curves
Y_{0}(q)
of level
q
).
We endow
Y_{1}
and
Y_{q}
with invariant probability measures
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{q}
, respectively.

There are two natural covering maps
\alpha_{q},\beta_{q}:Y_{q}\rightarrow Y_{1}
, given by
z\mapsto z
and
z\mapsto qz
in the split case.
Together, they define the
Hecke correspondence

\iota_{q}:Y_{q}\rightarrow Y_{1}\times Y_{1},

by setting
\iota_{q}=(\alpha_{q},\beta_{q})
.

If
F
is a cuspidal Hecke–Maaß newform on
Y_{q}
,
L^{2}
-normalized with respect to
\mu_{q}
, then
\mu_{F}:=\lvert{F}\rvert^{2}\mu_{q}
defines a new probability measure on
Y_{q}
.
It was proposed by Kowalski–Michel–VanderKam
[
KMV02
]
that the push-forward measures
(\alpha_{q})_{\ast}\mu_{F}
on
Y_{1}
should converge to the uniform measure
\mu_{1}
as
q
goes to infinity.
This is an analogue of the original quantum unique ergodicity conjecture of Rudnick and Sarnak
[
RS94
]
, which states that
\lvert{\psi}\rvert^{2}\mu_{1}
converges to
\mu_{1}
as the eigenvalue of a Maaß cuspform
\psi
on
Y_{1}
tends to infinity.
Note that assuming
F
is a newform is important, although not quite necessary.
1
1

1

We recall that, at least for
q
prime, newforms are defined as those automorphic forms orthogonal to old forms of the shape
\alpha_{q}^{\ast}(\psi)
and
\beta_{q}^{\ast}(\psi)
for
\psi
defined on
Y_{1}
.
Clearly, requiring that
F
be a newform in the conjecture of
[
KMV02
]
is wise, since the measures attached to old forms
\alpha_{q}^{\ast}(\psi)
for some cuspform
\psi
on
Y_{1}
are always equal to
\mu_{\psi}
, which is independent of
q
.
However, the newform assumption is not quite necessary, and it is an amusing observation that the measures attached to old forms
\beta_{q}^{\ast}(\psi)

do
converge to the uniform measure.
Indeed, this reduces to the equidistribution of Hecke points, as can be seen in the definition of the Hecke operator in (
2.1
) below.

We propose the following stronger conjecture, which combines the two maps
\alpha_{q}
and
\beta_{q}
and takes the equidistribution of the Hecke correspondence into account.

Conjecture A
.

Let
(F_{q})
be a sequence of Hecke–Maaß newforms, where
F_{q}
is defined on
Y_{q}
.
Then, as
q
goes to infinity, the measures
(\iota_{q})_{\ast}\mu_{F_{q}}
converge to the uniform measure
\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{1}
on
Y_{1}\times Y_{1}
.

A similar conjecture can be made for holomorphic modular forms.
Notice that, since
\alpha_{q}
is equal to the composition of
\iota_{q}
and the projection to the first factor, this conjecture implies the one in
[
KMV02
]
.

In
[
Nel11
]
, Nelson proves the Kowalski–Michel–VanderKam conjecture for holomorphic modular forms, generalizing the work of Holowinsky–Soundararajan
[
HS10
]
and Watson
[
Wat02
]
.
Nelson’s work also shows that the case of Maaß forms follows from conjectured subconvexity results for certain
L
-functions, or the Generalized Lindelöf Hypothesis (GLH), more generally.

The main result of the present paper is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
B
a division algebra, along a sequence of prime levels
q
, Conjecture
A
holds under the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH) with an effective rate
(\log q)^{-1/4+\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
.

1.1.
Comparison with the mixing conjecture

Conjecture
A
and Theorem
1.1
share many features with the Mixing Conjecture of Michel–Venkatesh
[
MV06
]
and the currently available partial results towards it.
To state it, let
D<0
be a fundamental discriminant and let
\operatorname{Cl}(D)
be the class group of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
.
Take a class
\sigma\in\operatorname{Cl}(D)
and denote by
q
be the minimal norm of an integral ideal in
\sigma
.
Now consider a sequence of such data
(D_{i},\sigma_{i},q_{i})
and let
\mathcal{H}_{D_{i}}
be the set of Heegner points in the modular curve
Y_{0}(1)
attached to
\operatorname{Cl}(D_{i})
.
Michel and Venkatesh conjecture that, as
q_{i}
tends to infinity (and
D_{i}\to\infty
implicitly, as discussed below), the sets

\{(z,\sigma_{i}z)\mid z\in\mathcal{H}_{D_{i}}\}\subset Y_{0}(1)\times Y_{0}(1)

equidistribute with respect to the uniform product measure
\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{1}
.
Note that, when projecting to one of the factors, equidistribution was already shown by Duke in
[
Duk88
]
using subconvexity of
L
-functions (in the guise of bounds for Fourier coefficients of half-integral weight forms).

We recall Minkowski’s theorem (see
[
MV06
, Sec. 6.5]
), which implies that
q=O(\sqrt{\lvert{D}\rvert})
.
In particular, if
q\to\infty
, then
D\to\infty
, the latter being a condition that is commonly found in the statement of the Mixing Conjecture.
We also observe that the set
\{(z,\sigma_{i}z)\mid z\in\mathcal{H}_{D_{i}}\}
lies in the Hecke correspondence
\iota_{q}(Y_{0}(q))
, as explained in Section 2.2 of
[
BBK22
]
.

Thus, the Mixing Conjecture is about point masses inside a Hecke correspondence and their equidistribution inside the product space, given that the projections are already known to equidistribute.
This is similar to Conjecture
A
, where we replace the points by the mass of an automorphic form.
However, we note that the required subconvexity to prove the equidistribution in the projections, i.e. the conjecture of Kowalski–Michel–VanderKam, is not yet known in full generality.

To deepen the analogy, we suggest comparing the quantity
D/q^{2}
to the Laplace eigenvalue
\lambda_{F}
.
Minkowski’s theorem shows that
D/q^{2}\gg 1
and the uniform spectral gap or Selberg’s conjecture imply that
\lambda_{F}\gg 1
.
Moreover, if
D/q^{2}\gg q^{\eta}
and
\lambda_{F}\gg q^{\eta}
for some
\eta>0
, we expect both conjectures to be easier.
This is the idea of equidistribution
in stages
explored by Ellenberg–Michel–Venkatesh
[
EMV13
]
for the Mixing Conjecture (see
[
BBK22
, Sec. 2.5]
for a nice discussion).
Namely, if measures on subsets
Y_{i}
of a space
X
converge fast enough to the uniform measure on
Y_{i}
and, in turn, the uniform measures on
Y_{i}
converge fast enough to the uniform measure on
X
, then one may piece these facts together and obtain full equidistribution.

In both settings, the Hecke correspondences equidistribute fast by the spectral gap of Hecke operators.
In the Mixing Conjecture, we recall that
\mathcal{H}_{D}
contains approximately
\sqrt{D}
points and the
q
-th Hecke correspondence has volume approximately
q
.
We thus need
\sqrt{D}
much larger than
q
to have good equidistribution in the correspondence.
Similarly, for a version of quantum unique ergodicity to manifest itself on the correspondence, we would need a large eigenvalue
\lambda_{F}
.
In Section
4.2
, we show that GLH is enough to prove our conjecture under the assumption
\lambda_{F}\gg q^{\eta}
.
The difficult range
\lambda_{F}\asymp 1
then requires the stronger input of GRH.

In terms of results towards the two conjectures, the present paper plays a similar role as the work of Blomer–Brumley
[
BB24
]
and Blomer–Brumley–Khayutin
[
BBK22
]
, where the Mixing Conjecture and related results are proved under GRH.
We point out that, using spectral methods, Conjecture
A
involves
L
-functions of higher degree, namely

L(1/2,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F\times\varphi)L(1/2,\varphi),

where
F
and
\varphi
are newforms, whilst Blomer–Brumley–Khayutin deal with

L(1/2,\varphi\times\chi_{D})L(1/2,\varphi),

where
\chi_{D}
is a quadratic character
2
2

2

We note that, for the difficult range of parameters
q\asymp D^{1/2}
, both
[
BB24
]
and
[
BBK22
]
consider a spectral expansion, not over the Hecke correspondence, but over the torus of discriminant
-D
. This results in the relevant family being given by twists by class group characters
\chi
, of the shape
L(1/2,f_{1}\otimes\theta_{\chi})L(1/2,f_{2}\otimes\theta_{\chi})
, where
f_{1},f_{2}
are Hecke–Maaß forms on
Y_{1}
and
\theta_{\chi}
denotes the theta series associated to
\chi
.
.

1.2.
Proof sketch

Our approach is spectral in nature, relying on the Watson–Ichino formula, Proposition
3.6
below, to relate the problem to
L
-functions.
We recall that, in the context of Theorem
1.1
, the spaces
Y_{1}
and
Y_{q}
are compact.
Thus, to show equidistribution by Weyl’s criterion, Lemma
4.1

f_{1},f_{2}
Hecke–Maaß forms on
Y_{1}

\langle|F|^{2},f_{1}\cdot(l_{q}f_{2})\rangle_{q}\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{as }q\rightarrow\infty,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{q}
is the inner product on
Y_{q}
normalized so that
\langle 1,1\rangle_{q}=1
and
(l_{q}f_{2})(z):=(\beta_{q})_{\ast}f_{2}(z)
(for the split algebra, this is
z\mapsto f_{2}(qz)
).
If either one of
f_{1},f_{2}
is constant, then we are reduced to the setting of the Kowalski–Michel–VanderKam conjecture and we have by the Watson–Ichino formula that

|\langle|F|^{2},f_{1}f_{2})\rangle_{q}|^{2}\approx q^{-1}(|t_{F}|+1)^{-1/2}L(1/2,F\otimes F\otimes f_{i})

which is
o(1)
given any subconvexity bound in
q
and convexity exponent in
t_{F}
.
This is the content of Proposition
4.2
.

If both
f_{1},f_{2}
are non-constant, we get by spectrally expanding over a Hecke eigenbasis
\mathcal{B}_{0}(Y_{q})\cup\{1\}
of

\langle|F|^{2},f_{1}\cdot(l_{q}f_{2})\rangle_{q}=\langle 1,f_{1}\cdot(l_{q}f_{2})\rangle_{q}+\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{0}(Y_{q})}\langle|F|^{2},\varphi\rangle_{q}\langle\varphi,f_{1}\cdot(l_{q}f_{2})\rangle_{q}.

(1.1)

\approx\delta_{f_{1}=f_{2}}\tilde{\lambda}_{f_{2}}(q)+q^{-1}(t_{F}+1)^{-1/4}\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{0}(Y_{q}),t_{\varphi}\leq 100}\pm\mathcal{L}(\varphi)^{1/2},

where

We use the crucial fact that, on

Remark 1.2
.

Note that, due to the fact that
Y_{q}
is compact, there is no continuous spectrum in spectral expansion. We are not currently able to resolve the fractional moment problem when
f_{1},f_{2}
are Eisenstein series.
This is analogous to the situation in
[
BB24
]
.
We can, however, bound the

Y_{0}(q)

1.2.1.
New aspects of the proof

The blueprint for bounding fractional moments under GRH was laid out in
[
LR20
]
.
In
loc. cit.
the authors introduced a certain splitting into different ranges, according to the size of
\log\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
, and then applied different high moments estimates in each of the cases to bound the fractional moment.
Applying this blueprint implies that, if one assumes GRH
and
GRC, then one can recover the bound one obtains from the heuristics in Section
5
(up to a factor
(\log q)^{\varepsilon}
).
Here, we note that executing the heuristics involves analyzing the holomorphicity of the
L
-functions of various functorial lifts (e.g.
L(s,\operatorname{sym}^{4}F)
).

We now list some of the new features appearing in our treatment, comparing to
[
LR20
]
and
[
BB24
]
.
In particular, a lot of work goes into controlling various factors which are known to be small under both GRH and GRC.

•

First of all, we introduce a slight simplification of the splitting of ranges: by
first
doing the change of variable by the mean
\mu_{q}
and
then
ruling out very large values, we avoid using any second moment estimates as in
[
LR20
, Lemma 5.6]
or
[
BB24
, Sec. 8]
. See Table
1
for details.

•

The
L
-functions that show up are of degree up to
12
and this pushes our knowledge of functoriality to the limit.
In particular, the automorphy of
\operatorname{sym}^{4}
due to Kim
[
Kim03
]
and the fact that the bound towards Ramanujan due to Kim–Sarnak
[
Kim03
, Appendix 2]
satisfies
\frac{7}{64}<1/8
are crucial for our arguments.
These facts are gathered in Section
3.1.1
and appear in many places throughout the argument.

•

In the case
f_{1}=f_{2}
, the two
L
-functions we consider are actually
not
independent on the
\log\log q
-scale, as opposed to the ones appearing in
[
BB24
]
and
[
LR20
]
.
However, the dependence is fortunately compensated for by the mean of
\log L(1/2,f_{1}\otimes f_{2}\otimes\varphi)
being a factor of
2
more negative when
f_{1}=f_{2}
.

•

The method of Soundararajan yields an upper bound for
\log\mathcal{L}(\varphi)
corresponding to taking the logarithm of a short truncation (
p<x
for some
x\geq 2
) of the relevant Euler product, but with weights of the slightly awkward shape
p^{-1/\log x}\frac{\log x/p}{\log x}
.
Removing these weights without GRC requires some additional work.
For comparison, in
[
BB24
]
, a certain positivity argument is used that is not available in our case.
In fact, in the case of
L(1/2,F\otimes F\otimes\varphi)
, the corresponding term is
negative
.
To circumvent this, we do a change of variables by
(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{q}
instead of the mean
\mu_{q}
itself and use the negativity to our advantage. To make this work we are required to truncate at
x
of larger size compared to
[
BB24
, Cor. 7]
, see eq. (
8.16
) below.
Ultimately, since the exponents in Lemmas
8.6
and
8.7
are allowed to depend on
\varepsilon
, this is admissible. We refer to Remark
6.10
for more details on this technical point.

Remark 1.3
.

The condition that
q
is prime can be slightly weakened (e.g. a product of a bounded number of primes of roughly the same size).
However, in the case where
q
is, say, the product of the first
n
primes, some new input is needed to deal with the old spectrum.

2.
The Hecke correspondence

2.1.
Quaternion algebras and arithmetic hyperbolic surfaces

Let
B
be an indefinite quaternion algebra over
\mathbb{Q}
with reduced discriminant
\operatorname{disc}(B)=D_{B}
and let
\mathcal{O}\subset B
be a maximal order.
For a prime
q
such that
(q,\operatorname{disc}(B))=1
, let
a_{q}\in\mathcal{O}
satisfy
\operatorname{nr}(a_{q})=q
(for existence see Eichler’s theorem on norms
[
Voi21
, Cor. 28.6.1]
and the fact that all maximal orders are conjugate
[
Voi21
, Cor. 28.5.6]
), and define

\mathcal{O}_{0}(q)=a_{q}^{-1}\mathcal{O}a_{q}\cap\mathcal{O}.

Thus,
\mathcal{O}_{0}(q)
is an Eichler order (see
[
Voi21
, Sec. 23.4]
).

Locally, at a prime
p\neq q
, the order
\mathcal{O}_{0}(q)_{p}
is maximal, equal to
\mathcal{O}_{p}
.
At the prime
q
, the order
\mathcal{O}
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{M}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{q})
and, under this isomorphism,
\mathcal{O}_{0}(q)_{q}
is conjugate to the order

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}_{q}&\mathbb{Z}_{q}\\
q\mathbb{Z}_{q}&\mathbb{Z}_{q}\end{pmatrix}.

Fix an embedding of
B
into
\operatorname{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Let
\Gamma_{q}:=\mathcal{O}_{0}(q)^{1}
be the group of norm 1 units of
\mathcal{O}_{0}(q)
as a discrete subgroup of
G=\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and define
Y_{q}=\Gamma_{q}\backslash\mathbb{H}
, where
\mathbb{H}\cong\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\operatorname{SO}(2)
is the hyperbolic plane.
Analogously, define
\Gamma_{1}:=\mathcal{O}^{1}
and
Y_{1}=\Gamma_{1}\backslash\mathbb{H}
using the maximal order.

For example, if
B=M_{2}(\mathbb{Q})
,
\mathcal{O}=M_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, and
a_{q}=\operatorname{diag}(q,1)
, then
\Gamma_{q}
is the standard Hecke congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{0}(q)
.
Otherwise, if
B
is a division algebra, then
Y_{1}
and
Y_{q}
are compact hyperbolic surfaces (see
[
Voi21
, Sec. 38.4]
).

Equip
Y_{q}
with the
G
-invariant probability measure
\mu_{q}
and let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{q}
be the corresponding inner product on
L^{2}(Y_{q})
and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
the corresponding norm.
6
6

6

We remove the subscript
q
from norms to avoid confusion with
L_{q}
-norms. It is clear from context. See Remark
2.2
for additional comments.

We also consider
Y_{1}\times Y_{1}
as a space with a
G\times G
-action and equip it with the invariant probability measure
\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{1}
.
These invariant measures with full support are also often called uniform measures.

2.2.
Hecke correspondences and operators

Let

\iota_{q}:Y_{q}\to Y_{1}\times Y_{1}

be the map that sends a matrix
g\in G
to the pair
(g,a_{q}g)
.
It is well-defined, since
a_{q}\Gamma_{q}a_{q}^{-1}\subset\Gamma_{1}
, and injective, since
\Gamma_{q}=a_{q}^{-1}\Gamma_{1}a_{q}\cap\Gamma_{1}
.

The image of
\iota_{q}
is called the
q
-th
Hecke correspondence
.
This name is also used to describe the system of maps

\alpha_{q},\beta_{q}:Y_{q}\to Y_{1},\quad\alpha_{q}(g)=g,\quad\beta_{q}(g)=a_{q}\cdot g.

They are, respectively, the composition of
\iota_{q}
with the projections
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
onto the first and second factor of
Y_{1}\times Y_{1}
.
When pulling back functions through
\alpha_{q}
and
\beta_{q}
, we often simplify notation by writing
\alpha_{q}^{\ast}f(z)=f(z)
and
\beta_{q}^{\ast}f(z)=f(a_{q}z)=l_{q}f(z)
, by viewing functions on
Y_{1}
as periodic functions on
\mathbb{H}
.

By pulling back functions through
\beta_{q}
and pushing forward through
\alpha_{q}
, we obtain the Hecke operator
T_{q}
, that is,

(2.1)

\sum_{w\in\alpha_{q}^{-1}(z)}f(\beta_{q}(w))=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}\backslash\Gamma_{1}a_{q}\Gamma_{1}}f(\gamma z)

for functions

and we write

.
Finally, define

acts on full level cuspidal eigenfunctions with eigenvalues bounded by

Note also that the eigenvalues of

It is useful to state the previous observation in the following form, which follows immediately after unpacking normalizations.

Lemma 2.1

If

and

are functions on

Remark 2.2
.

To avoid any confusions regarding normalizations, we collect here a few observations.
Let

and assume that
\langle f,f\rangle_{1}=1
.
Then we have
\langle\alpha_{q}^{\ast}f,\alpha_{q}^{\ast}f\rangle_{q}=1
and we often write

Therefore, there is no confusion in simply writing

Involutions

We also note the existence of the involution
W
on

given by switching components, i.e.
W(g,h)=(h,g)
.
Observe that, on the Hecke correspondence

.
In the case of

where

For general quaternion algebras, we may follow

and argue locally to essentially reduce to the operator above.

We recall that there is a prime ideal

and

To relate

and

Fix an isomorphism

Since

a_{q}=\xi_{1}\begin{pmatrix}q&\\

and

\xi_{2}\mathcal{O}_{0}(q)_{q}\xi_{2}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}_{q}&\mathbb{Z}_{q}\\

The prime ideal

\xi_{2}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}q\mathbb{Z}_{q}&\mathbb{Z}_{q}\\
q\mathbb{Z}_{q}&q\mathbb{Z}_{q}\end{pmatrix}\xi_{2}=\xi_{2}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}&1\\
q&\end{pmatrix}\mathcal{O}_{0}(q)_{q}\xi_{2}=\xi_{2}^{-1}\mathcal{O}_{0}(q)_{q}\begin{pmatrix}&1\\

Thus, the localization of

at

for some

It is now easy to check, by direct computation using the local descriptions above, that

and

This directly implies the identity

\iota_{q}\circ W_{q}=W\circ\iota_{q}.

Remark 2.3
.

The Fricke involution

acts on a newform

by multiplication by

The main goal of this section is to recall definitions of

3.1.
L
-functions

We say that a meromorphic function
L(s,\pi)
with at most poles at
s=0,1
is an
L
-function of degree

if we have the following data:

(1)

There is a Dirichlet series with an Euler product of degree
d\geq 1

(3.1)

L(s,\pi)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\lambda_{\pi}(n)}{n^{s}}=\prod_{p}(1-\alpha_{\pi}(p,1)p^{-s})^{-1}\cdots(1-\alpha_{\pi}(p,d)p^{-s})^{-1},

converging absolutely for
\operatorname{Re}s>1
. We refer to
\alpha_{\pi}(p,i)
as the
Satake parameters at
p
.

(2)

A gamma factor

L_{\infty}(s,\pi)=\pi^{-ds/2}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\Gamma\left(\frac{s+s_{\pi}(j)}{2}\right).

We refer to
s_{\pi}(j)
as the
Satake parameters at
\infty
.

(3)

An integer
q(\pi)\geq 1
, called the conductor of
L(s)
such that
\alpha_{\pi}(p,i)\neq 0
for
p\nmid q(\pi)
and
1\leq i\leq d
, and a functional equation

(3.2)

\Lambda(s,\pi):=q(\pi)^{s}L_{\infty}(s,\pi)L(s,\pi)=\epsilon(\pi)\Lambda(1-s,\overline{\pi}),

where
\epsilon(\pi)
is of absolute value 1 and referred to as the
root number
and
\overline{\pi}
is the
dual of
\pi
satisfying
\lambda_{\overline{\pi}}(n)=\overline{\lambda_{\pi}(n)}
.

We define the
analytic conductor of
\pi
as

(3.3)

\mathbf{c}(\pi):=q(\pi)\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1+|s_{\pi}(j)|)

We say that
L(s,\pi)
belongs the the
extended Selberg class
if it satisfies the following additional properties

(4)

There exists a positive
\delta<1/2
such that
\lvert{\alpha_{j}(p)}\rvert\leq p^{\delta}
for all primes
p
and
\lvert{\operatorname{Re}s_{\pi}(j)}\rvert\leq\delta
.

It is believed that any
L
-function as defined above satisfies the
Generalized Riemann Hypothesis

[
IK04
, Section 5.7]
:

Conjecture B

(GRH)
.

Let
L(s,\pi)
be an
L
-function. Then all zeroes in the critical strip

lies on the critical line
\operatorname{Re}s=1/2
.

This is known to imply the

.

Conjecture C

(GLH)
.

Let
L(s,\pi)
be an
L
-function. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
, it holds that
L(1/2,\pi)\ll_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{c}(\pi)^{\varepsilon}
where the implied constant is allowed to depend on the degree
d
.

Lemma 3.1

Let
\pi

Proof.

From

3.1.2.
Hecke relations

Let
\lambda(p^{k})
be the eigenvalue of an automorphic form on
Y_{q}
with respect to the unramified Hecke operators
T_{p^{k}}
.
It is well-known (see
[
Iwa02
, (8.39)]
) that these satisfy the relations

(3.10)

\lambda(p^{n})\lambda(p^{m})=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(m,n)}\lambda(p^{m+n-2j}).

For instance, we often use that
\lambda(p)^{2}=\lambda(p^{2})+1
or that

(3.11)

\lambda(p)^{3}=\lambda(p^{3})+2\lambda(p).

Applying the formula iteratively, for every
d\geq 1
, we may find coefficients
\alpha^{(d)}_{j,n}\in\mathbb{Z}
that satisfy

(3.12)

\lambda(p^{d})^{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{dn}\alpha^{(d)}_{j,n}\lambda(p^{j}).

Recall also that the Hecke eigenvalue is given in terms of the two Satake parameters
\alpha(p,1),\alpha(p,2)
through

\lambda(p)=\alpha(p,1)+\alpha(p,2).

Thus, the
L
-factor at
p
is

(3.13)

(1-\alpha(p,1)p^{-s})^{-1}\cdot(1-\alpha(p,2)p^{-s})^{-1}=(1-\lambda(p)p^{-s}+p^{-2s})^{-1},

since
\alpha(p,1)\alpha(p,2)=1
.

Another standard use of the Hecke relations that can be found in our work is to compute power sums like
\alpha(p,1)^{k}+\alpha(p,2)^{k}
.
For this, we may use Newton’s identities and the facts above to express the latter as a polynomial in
\lambda(p)
.
If necessary, this polynomial can be expressed as a linear combination of
(\lambda(p^{j}))_{j}
by using (
3.10
) iteratively to compute each power of
\lambda(p)
.
For instance,

\alpha(p,1)^{2}+\alpha(p,2)^{2}=\lambda(p)^{2}-2=\lambda(p^{2})-1.

To study the combinatorics of the Hecke relations further, it is useful to consider the following generating functions.
The standard Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind are defined by

(1-2tX+X^{2})^{-1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}U_{n}(t)X^{n}.

It is well-know and easy to see that
U_{0}(t)=1
and
U_{1}(t)=2t
.
Due to the relations (
3.10
) and (
3.13
), we may substitute
t\mapsto\lambda(p)/2
and this gives
\lambda(p^{n})=U_{n}(\lambda(p)/2)
.
The
U_{n}
thus also satisfy the analogue of (
3.12
), meaning that

U_{d}(t)^{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{dn}\alpha^{(d)}_{j,n}U_{j}(t).

Lemma 3.4
.

For the coefficients given in (
3.12
), it holds that

(1)

\alpha^{(d)}_{j,n}\geq 0
,

(2)

\sum_{j=0}^{dn}\alpha^{(d)}_{j,n}\leq(d+1)^{n-1}
,

(3)

\alpha^{(d)}_{0,1}=0
,

(4)

\alpha^{(1)}_{0,n}=\delta_{2|n}\frac{n!}{(\frac{n}{2})!(\frac{n}{2}+1)!}
.

Proof.

The Hecke relations (
3.10
) imply that

\alpha^{(d)}_{j,n+1}=\sum_{i=\max(0,\lceil\frac{d-j}{2}\rceil)}^{d}\alpha^{(d)}_{j-d+2i,n}.

In particular, we conclude that
\alpha^{(d)}_{j,n}\geq 0
and

\sum_{j=0}^{d(n+1)}\alpha^{(d)}_{j,n+1}\leq(d+1)\sum_{j=0}^{dn}\alpha^{(d)}_{j,n}\leq\ldots\leq(d+1)^{n}\alpha^{(d)}_{d,1}=(d+1)^{n},

yielding the second property.

The third property is obvious.
The last one can be deduced, for example, using the orthogonality of Chebyshev polynomials.
More precisely, recall that
(U_{n}(t))_{n}
is an orthonormal system in
L^{2}([-1,1])
with respect to the measure given by
\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,dx
.
Therefore,

\alpha_{0,n}^{(1)}=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}U_{1}(x)^{n}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,dx.

Recalling that
U_{1}(x)=2x
and substituting
x\mapsto\sin(x)
, we obtain

\alpha_{0,n}^{(1)}=2^{n}\cdot\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\sin(x)^{n}\cos(x)^{2}\,dx.

Since
\sin(x)
is odd, the integral vanishes if
2\nmid n
.
Next, we recall the well-known fact (the following is one of Wallis’ integrals) that

\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\sin(x)^{2k}\,dx=2\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{2k}}\cdot\frac{(2k)!}{(k!)^{2}}.

Writing
\cos(x)^{2}=1-\sin(x)^{2}
and applying these formulae, we obtain that

\alpha_{0,2k}^{(1)}=2\left(\frac{(2k)!}{(k!)^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{(2k+2)!}{(k+1)!^{2}}\right).

The claim follows by a simple calculation.
∎

We record here a consequence of the Hecke relations that plays an important technical role in this paper.

Lemma 3.5
.

For
\pi,\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}

Note that

Local representations and triple product

Remark 3.7
.

It is clear that
\int_{Y_{q}}\phi_{1}\phi_{2}\bar{\phi}_{3}
vanishes if the product of the Fricke eigenvalues of

,
\phi_{2}
,

given by

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}
be non-constant Hecke–Maaß forms on
Y_{1}
or
Y_{q}
, as in any of the cases of Proposition
3.6
, with parameters
s_{j}=\sigma_{j}+it_{j}
, where we may assume that
t_{j},\sigma_{j}\geq 0
, and signs
\delta_{j}\in\{0,1\}
.
Assume that
\sum_{j}\delta_{j}
is even.
Then it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{L_{\infty}(1/2,\phi_{1}\times\phi_{2}\times\phi_{3})}{\prod_{i}L_{\infty}(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}\phi_{i})}

\displaystyle\ll\exp\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\sum_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\in\{\pm 1\}}|t_{1}+\epsilon_{1}t_{2}+\epsilon_{2}t_{3}|-2\sum_{i}t_{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\in\{\pm 1\}}(|t_{1}+\epsilon_{1}t_{2}+\epsilon_{2}t_{3}|+1)^{-1/2}.

Proof.

Recall, by the classification of admissible principal series, that
s_{j}\in i\mathbb{R}\cup[-7/64,7/64]
.
By duality, we may also swap
s_{j}
for
-s_{j}
and we can thus assume that
\sigma_{j},t_{j}\geq 0
.

By observing that
\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(s)\asymp\Gamma(s/2)
for
\operatorname{Re}(s)\ll 1
, we recall from Section
3.1
(see also Remark
3.7
) that

L_{\infty}(s,\phi_{1}\times\phi_{2}\times\phi_{3})\asymp\prod_{\{\pm 1\}^{3}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s+\sum_{j}\pm s_{j}}{2}\right),

and

L_{\infty}(s,\operatorname{sym}^{2}\phi)\asymp\Gamma\left(\frac{s+2s_{\phi}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{s-2s_{\phi}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{s}{2}\right).

In the case that all representations are tempered, i.e.
\sigma_{i}=0
for all
i
, the result follows directly from Stirling’s approximation
[
Iwa02
, eq. (B.7)]
, namely

\Gamma(\sigma+it)\asymp t^{\sigma-1/2}e^{-\pi t/2}

for large

, which holds uniformly in vertical strips of bounded width.
When
t
is small, we replace
t^{\sigma-1/2}
with
(1+t)^{\sigma-1/2}
and take care that
\sigma
is not too close to a pole of the Gamma function.
If, for instance,
\phi_{1}
is tempered, but
\phi_{2}
or
\phi_{3}
is non-tempered, then we may still use Stirling’s formula where adequate, and, otherwise, the bounds towards Selberg’s conjecture ensure that we stay away from any poles of the Gamma function.
In the triple product, the exponents in the factors

sum up and, since the signs alternate,
\sigma_{2}
or
\sigma_{3}
do not contribute.
If all
\phi_{i}
are non-tempered, the bounds towards Selberg imply a uniform bound since
1/2-\sum\sigma_{i}>0.17
.

We now restrict to

Let

The goal is to prove effective equidistribution on

of the push-forward measure

Our approach is spectral and will rely on the following version of Weyl’s criterion. Let

be a fixed orthonormal basis of Hecke–Maaß forms for

Lemma 4.1

(Weyl’s criterion)
.

Let
\nu_{n}
be a sequence of probability measures on
Y_{1}\times Y_{1}
. Let
E:\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a function such that
E(n)\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Assume that there exists a constant
A>0
such that for
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(Y_{1})
which are not both constant,

\nu_{n}(f_{1}\otimes f_{2})\ll(t_{f_{1}}+1)^{A}(t_{f_{2}}+1)^{A}E(n).

Then
\nu_{n}
equidistributes on
Y_{1}\times Y_{1}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
with effective rate
E(n)
.
Explicitly, for any smooth function
\psi:Y_{1}\times Y_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
it holds that

\nu_{n}(\psi)=(\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{1})(\psi)+O(\mathcal{S}_{A}(\psi)E(n)),

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, where
\mathcal{S}_{A}
denotes an appropriate Sobolev norm depending on
A
.

Proof.

The proof is quite standard and we will simply sketch the arguments.
We spectrally expand

\displaystyle\nu_{n}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(Y_{1})}\langle\psi,f_{1}\otimes f_{2}\rangle_{\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{1}}\nu_{n}(f_{1}\otimes f_{2})

\displaystyle=(\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{1})(\psi)+O\left(E(n)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(Y_{1}),\\
(f_{1},f_{2})\neq(1,1)\end{subarray}}\langle\psi,f_{1}\otimes f_{2}\rangle_{\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{1}}(t_{f_{1}}+1)^{A}(t_{f_{2}}+1)^{A}\right),

using that
\mu_{n}
are probability measures to pick out the contribution from
f_{1}=f_{2}=1
.
Now, by self-adjointness of the Laplacian
\Delta
, we have for any
B>1
that

\langle\psi,f_{1}\otimes f_{2}\rangle_{\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{1}}=\lambda_{f_{1}}^{-B}\lambda_{f_{2}}^{-B}\langle(\Delta\otimes\Delta)^{B}\psi,f_{1}\otimes f_{2}\rangle_{\mu_{1}\otimes\mu_{1}}.

Clearly
|\!|(\Delta\otimes\Delta)^{B}\psi|\!|_{\infty}\leq\mathcal{S}_{B}(\psi)
where
\mathcal{S}_{B}
denotes the
L^{\infty}
-Sobolev norm depending on the
2B
first derivatives.
Picking
B
sufficiently large and using the Weyl law, we conclude.
Note that we may approximate
\lambda_{f_{1}}
by
1+t_{f_{1}}^{2}\asymp(1+t_{f_{1}})^{2}
since we have uniform bounds for non-tempered spectral parameters and a uniform spectral gap (
3.6
), for example.
∎

In the setting of the above lemma, we refer to
\nu_{n}(f_{1}\otimes f_{2})
as the
Weyl sums
of the equidistribution problem. In our setting we will apply the Weyl criterion with error-term
(\log q)^{-1/4+\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon
.

Note first of all that, if exactly one of
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(Y_{1})
is constant, then we can easily bound the Weyl sums under the assumption of GLH.
By Remark
2.3
regarding the Fricke involution, we may switch

and

and thus assume that
f_{2}
is the constant function.
We are now in the case of the Kowalski–Michel–VanderKam conjecture.

Proposition 4.2
.

Under GLH, for any
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\langle|F|^{2},f_{1}\rangle_{q}\ll_{\varepsilon,f_{1}}q^{-1/2+\varepsilon},

where the implied constant depends polynomially on the eigenvalue of

This follows directly from the Watson-Ichino formula, Proposition

, and a theorem of Hoffstein–Lockhart
[

that asserts

and

For the archimedean factors, we note here that

\sum_{\{\pm\}^{2}}\lvert{t_{F}\pm t_{F}\pm t_{f_{1}}}\rvert-4t_{F}-2t_{f_{1}}=2\max(t_{\psi}-2t_{F},0)\geq 0,

and we observe that

.
To add
l_{q}\psi
, we simply recall Lemma
2.1
which computes that

\langle\psi,l_{q}\psi\rangle_{q}=\tilde{\lambda}_{\psi}(q).

We finish by applying the same result to compute that

\displaystyle\left\lVert l_{q}\psi-\tilde{\lambda}_{\psi}(q)\cdot\psi\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert l_{q}\psi\right\rVert-\tilde{\lambda}_{\psi}(q)^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle l_{q}(\psi\bar{\psi}),1\rangle_{q}-\tilde{\lambda}_{\psi}(q)^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi\bar{\psi},\tilde{T}_{q}1\rangle_{q}-\tilde{\lambda}_{\psi}(q)^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\tilde{\lambda}_{\psi}(q)^{2},

using that the Hecke operator is self-adjoint and that it acts on constant functions with eigenvalue
1
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Under GLH, the old spectrum is negligible.
More precisely,

\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{old}}(Y_{q})}\langle f_{1}\cdot(l_{q}f_{2}),\varphi\rangle_{q}\cdot\langle\varphi,\lvert{F}\rvert^{2}\rangle_{q}\ll_{f_{i}}q^{\theta+\varepsilon}

where
\theta=\frac{7}{64}-\frac{1}{2}
is the current best bound towards Ramanujan.
Here the implied constant depends polynomially on the analytic conductor of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
.

Proof.

Consider the contribution of the basis elements
\psi_{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{old}}(Y_{q})
in (
4.1
) for
\psi\in\mathcal{B}(Y_{1})
.
By Lemma
2.1
, we have

\langle f_{1}\cdot l_{q}f_{2},\psi\rangle_{q}=\langle l_{q}f_{2},\bar{f}_{1}\psi\rangle_{1}=\tilde{\lambda}_{f_{2}}(q)\langle f_{1}f_{2},\psi\rangle_{1}

and therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{B}(Y_{1})}\langle f_{1}\cdot(l_{q}f_{2}),\psi\rangle_{q}\cdot\langle\psi,\lvert{F}\rvert^{2}\rangle_{q}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\lambda}_{f_{2}}(q)\langle\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{B}(Y_{1})}\langle f_{1}f_{2},\psi\rangle_{1}\psi,\lvert{F}\rvert^{2}\rangle_{q}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\lambda}_{f_{2}}(q)\langle f_{1}f_{2},\lvert{F}\rvert^{2}\rangle_{q}\ll_{f_{1},f_{2}}q^{\theta},

where the implied constant depends polynomially on the eigenvalue of
f_{i}
using any sup-norm bound, e.g.
[
IS95
]
.
We used here the assumption that
f_{2}
is not the constant function (see Proposition
4.2
) and also Remark
2.2
to pass between
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{q}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{1}
for old forms.

Next, consider the contribution of the elements
\psi_{q}
, where we may assume that
\psi
is not the constant function.
Recall that

\psi_{q}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}_{\psi}(q)^{2}}}(l_{q}\psi-\tilde{\lambda}_{\psi}(q)\cdot\psi),

and that
\tilde{\lambda}_{\psi}(q)\ll q^{\theta}
with
\theta<0
.
The latter also implies the bound
(1-\tilde{\lambda}_{\psi}(q)^{2})^{-1/2}\ll 1
.
For the second term, a treatment as above would be desirable, except the spectral expansion is now twisted by the normalization factor.
We therefore apply the Watson-Ichino formula.

Consider the first term in the definition of
\psi_{q}
, that is
(1-\tilde{\lambda}_{\psi}(q)^{2})^{-1/2}\cdot l_{q}\psi
.
Observe that, by self-adjointness of the Hecke operator,

\langle f_{1},l_{q}(\bar{f_{2}}\psi))\rangle=\langle\tilde{T}_{q}f_{1},\bar{f}_{2}\psi\rangle=\tilde{\lambda}_{f_{1}}(q)\cdot\langle f_{1}f_{2},\psi\rangle.

Additionally, by changing variables using the Fricke involution in the integral defining the inner product, we obtain

\langle(l_{q}\psi),\lvert{F}\rvert^{2}\rangle_{q}=\langle\psi,\lvert{F}\rvert^{2}\rangle_{q},

since
l_{q}W_{q}(\psi)=\psi
, as observed in Section
2.3
, and
F
is an eigenfunction of the involution
W_{q}
.

By the Watson-Ichino formula, Proposition
3.6
, we deduce that

\langle f_{1}\cdot(l_{q}f_{2}),\psi_{q}\rangle\ll q^{\theta}\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda(1/2,f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\psi)}{\prod_{i}\Lambda(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}f_{i})\Lambda(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}\psi)}}

and

\langle\psi_{q},\lvert{F}\rvert^{2}\rangle\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda(1/2,\psi\times F\times F)}{\Lambda(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}\psi)\Lambda(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)^{2}}}.

Here, we simply bounded
(1-\tilde{\lambda}_{\psi}(q)^{2})^{-1/2}\ll 1
and
\tilde{\lambda}_{\psi}(q),\tilde{\lambda}_{f_{1}}(q)\ll q^{\theta}
, using the assumption that
f_{1}
is non-constant.
Looking towards applying Lemma
3.8
, we compute that

(4.2)

\sum_{\{\pm\}^{2}}|t_{\psi}+\pm t_{f_{1}}+\pm t_{f_{2}}|-2(t_{\psi}+t_{f_{1}}+t_{f_{2}})\geq 2\max(t_{\psi}-t_{f_{1}}-t_{f_{2}},0).

and

(4.3)

2t_{\psi}+|2t_{F}+t_{\psi}|+|2t_{F}-t_{\psi}|-2t_{\psi}-4t_{F}=2\max(t_{\psi}-2t_{F},0)\geq 0.

Using also a theorem of Hoffstein–Lockhart, as in the proof of Proposition
4.2
, we have that

\langle f_{1}\cdot(l_{q}f_{2}),\psi_{q}\rangle\langle\psi_{q},\lvert{F}\rvert^{2}\rangle\\
\ll q^{\theta-1/2+\varepsilon}e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}\max(t_{\psi}-t_{f_{1}}-t_{f_{2}},0)}\sqrt{L(1/2,f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\psi)L(1/2,\psi\times F\times F)}

The Generalized Lindelöf Hypothesis implies now that

\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{B}(Y_{1})}\langle f_{1}\cdot(l_{q}f_{2}),\psi_{q}\rangle_{q}\cdot\langle\psi_{q},\lvert{F}\rvert^{2}\rangle_{q}\ll q^{\theta-1/2+\varepsilon}\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{B}(Y_{1})}e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}\max(t_{\psi}-t_{f_{1}}-t_{f_{2}},0)}.

The contribution in the latter sum from
t_{\psi}<t_{f_{1}}+t_{f_{2}}
is bounded by

by the Weyl law and the tail

is uniformly bounded in terms of
\operatorname{vol}(Y_{1})\asymp\operatorname{disc}(B)
by the Weyl law and a dyadic decomposition.
∎

4.2.
Equidistribution in stages

We consider now the case where
t_{F}
goes to infinity fast enough in terms of
q
.
The theory of quantum chaos suggests that the mass of
F
should equidistribute along
Y_{q}
.
Together with the equidistribution of the Hecke correspondence, we expect that proving our conjecture would require weaker hypotheses in this regime.
Indeed, we describe in this section how the Generalized Lindelöf Hypothesis is enough.

Corollary 4.5
.

For
L^{2}
-normalized Hecke–Maaß cuspidal newforms

on
Y_{q}
and
f_{1},f_{2}
Hecke–Maaß cuspforms on

, it holds that

\displaystyle\langle f_{1}\cdot(l_{q}f_{2}),\varphi\rangle_{q}\cdot\langle\varphi,\lvert{F}\rvert^{2}\rangle_{q}

\displaystyle\ll_{f_{1},f_{2}}e^{-\pi/2\max(t_{\varphi}-t_{f_{1}}-t_{f_{2}},0)}(\lvert{2t_{F}+t_{\varphi}}\rvert\cdot\lvert{2t_{F}-t_{\varphi}}\rvert+1)^{-1/4}

\displaystyle\cdot\frac{\sqrt{L(1/2,F\times F\times\varphi)L(1/2,f_{1}\times f_{2}\times\varphi)}}{qL(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}\varphi)L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)},

with polynomial dependence on
t_{f_{1}},t_{f_{2}}
.

Proof.

This follows from the Watson–Ichino formula in Proposition
3.6
together with Lemma
3.8
and the elementary inequalities (
4.2
) and (
4.3
).∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Under GLH, it holds that

\displaystyle\langle|F|^{2},f_{1}(q.f_{2})\rangle_{q}\ll_{\varepsilon,f_{i}}(q^{\theta+\varepsilon}+q^{\varepsilon}(t_{F}+1)^{-1/2+\varepsilon}),

with polynomial dependence
t_{f_{1}}
and
t_{f_{2}}
(with exponents allowed to depend on
\varepsilon
) and on

.

In particular, for any

A
holds for

and

.

Proof.

We start with the trivial estimate

(4.4)

\langle f_{1}\cdot(l_{q}f_{2}),\lvert{F}\rvert^{2}\rangle_{q}\ll(t_{f_{1}}+1)^{100}(t_{f_{2}}+1)^{100}\left\lVert F\right\rVert^{2},

using sup-norm bounds for
f_{1},f_{2}

[
IS95
]
.
Since
F
is
L^{2}
-normalized and the implied constant is allowed to depend polynomially on
t_{f_{1}},t_{f_{2}}
, we may assume that
t_{f_{1}}+t_{f_{2}}<q^{\varepsilon}
.

We apply the spectral expansion (
4.1
) where, by Lemma

, we may restrict our attention to the newforms

.
Under GLH, Corollary

gives the bound

\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{new}}(Y_{q})}\langle f_{1}\cdot(l_{q}f_{2}),\varphi\rangle_{q}\cdot\langle\varphi,\lvert{F}\rvert^{2}\rangle_{q}\\

∎

.

4.3.
Reduction to a fractional moment

.

, to translate to a fractional moment problem, which we phrase in terms of

More precisely, let

and

(4.5)

\max(t-r,0)>\frac{t}{r+3}-2,\quad e^{-|t|}\leq(\cosh t)^{-1},\quad t,r>0,

5.
A random model for fractional moments

In this section we explain a heuristic for calculating fractional moments of
L
-functions and how to apply it in the setting of Theorem
4.9
. See also
[
BB24
, Sec. 5]
and
[
LR20
, Sec. 5.1]
.

For any reasonable family
\mathcal{F}
of
L
-functions one should expect the analogue of Selberg’s central limit theorem to hold.
This says that the numbers
\{\log|L(1/2,\pi)|:\pi\in\mathcal{F}(X)\}
are asymptotically normally distributed with mean and variance of size
\asymp\log\log|\mathcal{F}(X)|
, where
\mathcal{F}(X)=\{\pi\in\mathcal{F}:\mathbf{c}(\pi)\leq X\}
.
We will now present a random model that explains this and that allows one to calculate the mean and variance.
We will apply this in the case of the family

\mathcal{F}(q)=\{\pi_{0}\otimes\varphi:\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{new}}(q),t_{\varphi}\leq 100\},\quad\pi_{0}=F\otimes F\boxplus f_{1}\otimes f_{2},

where
F
has level
q
and spectral parameter
t_{F}\leq q^{100}
.
This means that

L(1/2,\pi_{0}\otimes\varphi)=L(1/2,F\otimes F\otimes\varphi)L(1/2,f_{1}\otimes f_{2}\otimes\varphi)
.

The starting point for the random model is the assumption that we can approximate the central value by a short Dirichlet polynomial.
That is, after Taylor expanding the logarithm, we have

\displaystyle\log L(1/2,f_{1}\otimes f_{2}\otimes f_{3})=

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\lambda_{f_{1}}(p)\lambda_{f_{2}}(p)\lambda_{f_{3}}(p)}{p^{1/2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p^{2}\leq x}\frac{(\lambda_{f_{1}}(p^{2})-1)(\lambda_{f_{2}}(p^{2})-1)(\lambda_{f_{3}}(p^{2})-1)}{p}+(\text{bounded}),

where
x=Q^{\varepsilon}
is a small power of the analytic conductor
Q
of
f_{1}\otimes f_{2}\otimes f_{3}
. Here we are using the Ramanujan conjecture to bound the contribution from higher powers of
p
.
Assume for the moment that
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
are pairwise distinct, with trivial central characters and square-free levels.
We then get by expanding the product that

\displaystyle\sum_{p^{2}\leq x}\frac{(\lambda_{f_{1}}(p^{2})-1)(\lambda_{f_{2}}(p^{2})-1)(\lambda_{f_{3}}(p^{2})-1)}{p}-

\displaystyle=-\sum_{p^{2}\leq x}\frac{1}{p}+\sum_{\pi\in\{\operatorname{sym}^{2}f_{1},\ldots,\operatorname{sym}^{2}f_{1}\otimes\operatorname{sym}^{2}f_{2}\otimes\operatorname{sym}^{2}f_{3}\}}\pm\sum_{p^{2}\leq x}\frac{\lambda_{\pi}(p)}{p}.

The terms in the last sum are all heuristically equal to the logarithm of a holomorphic
L
-function at
s=1
up to a bounded error.
This is true under GRH by Corollary
6.6
, using here the assumptions on
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
, as well as appropriate instances of functoriality (see Section
3.1
).
By Mertens’ second theorem we arrive at

\displaystyle\log L(1/2,f_{1}\otimes f_{2}\otimes f_{3})=-\frac{1}{2}\log\log Q+\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\lambda_{f_{1}}(p)\lambda_{f_{2}}(p)\lambda_{f_{3}}(p)}{p^{1/2}}

\displaystyle+\log\left\{\text{holomorphic $L$-functions at $s=1$}\right\}+(\text{bounded}).

Assuming GRH and the Ramanujan conjecture, we know that the contribution from the
L
-functions at
s=1
is of size
\log\log\log Q
and is thus negligible.
When
f_{1}=f_{2}
a similar calculation yields the same expression but with
-\log\log Q
in place of
-\frac{1}{2}\log\log Q
. This way we arrive at the approximation

(5.1)

\log L(1/2,\pi_{0}\otimes\varphi)\approx\mu_{q}+\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\left(\lambda_{F}(p)^{2}+\lambda_{f_{1}}(p)\lambda_{f_{2}}(p)\right)\lambda_{\varphi}(p)}{p^{1/2}},\quad x=q^{\varepsilon}

where
\mu_{q}=-2\log\log q
or
\mu_{q}=-\frac{3}{2}\log\log q
, according to whether
f_{1}=f_{2}
or not.

Now, for each prime
p\leq x
, we can think of

\varphi\mapsto\frac{\lambda_{\varphi}(p)}{p^{1/2}},

as corresponding to independent random variables
X_{p}
with mean zero and variance
p^{-1}
, since
|\lambda_{\varphi}(p)|^{2}
is
1
on average by an application of the Kuznetsov trace formula.
In this case, we would expect by the central limit theorem that, for a sequence
b(p)
of real numbers,
\sum_{p\leq x}b(p)X_{p}
should be well approximated by a normal distribution with mean zero and variance
\sum_{p\leq x}b(p)^{2}p^{-1}
.
Applying this to (
5.1
) and using the Hecke relations as in Lemma
3.5
, we arrive at the following value for the variance:

(5.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{(\lambda_{F}(p)^{2}+\lambda_{f_{1}}(p)\lambda_{f_{2}}(p))^{2}}{p}

\displaystyle\approx\begin{cases}3\log\log q,&f_{1}\neq f_{2}\\
6\log\log q,&f_{1}=f_{2}.\end{cases}

\displaystyle+\log\left\{\text{holomorphic $L$-functions at $s=1$}\right\}.

Combining all of the above we conclude that the logarithms of the family of
L
-functions appearing in our fractional moment is well-modeled by a Gaußian with mean
-\frac{3}{2}\log\log q
, respectively
-2\log\log q
, and variance
3\log\log q
, respectively
6\log\log q
, according to whether
f_{1}=f_{2}
or not.

Finally, let
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
be a Gaußian random variable. Then a quick calculation shows that for any
t>0
,

(5.3)

\mathbb{E}(e^{tX})=e^{t\mu+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\sigma^{2}}.

Applying this to our Gaußian model above we would thus expect that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|F(q)|}\sum_{\varphi}\sqrt{L(1/2,F\otimes F\otimes\varphi)L(1/2,f_{1}\otimes f_{2}\otimes\varphi)}\approx\begin{cases}(\log q)^{-3/8},&f_{1}\neq f_{2}\\
(\log q)^{-1/4},&f_{1}=f_{2}.\end{cases}

We will see that, under GRH, we can recover the upper bound up to a factor
(\log q)^{\varepsilon}
.

Write

We recall that the theory of Jacquet-Langlands gives a one-to-one correspondence between Hecke–Maaß forms on

6.1.
The Bruggeman–Kuznetsov formula

Let

be a test function that can be extended to the strip
|\operatorname{Im}(t)|<1/2+\delta
for some
\delta>0

(1)

h
is even and holomorphic,

(2)

h(t)\ll(1+|t|)^{-2-\delta}
for
|\operatorname{Im}(t)|<1/2+\delta
.

In particular, this is satisfied by
t\mapsto\cosh(\pi t/r)^{-1}
for
r\geq 2
. For a positive integer
N
, let
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{new}}(Y_{0}(N))
be an orthonormal basis of newforms of level
N
.

Theorem 6.1

(
[
Iwa02
, Theorem 9.3]
)
.

Let
N>0
be a square-free integer.
Then, for
h
as above and
m,n>0
coprime to
N
, it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{LM=N}\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{new}}(Y_{0}(M))}\frac{h(is_{\varphi})}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}\varphi)}\mathcal{W}_{\varphi}(L)\lambda_{\varphi}(m)\lambda_{\varphi}(n)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{h(t)}{|\zeta(1+2it)|^{2}}\mathcal{W}_{t}(N)\lambda_{t}(m)\lambda_{t}(n)dt

\displaystyle=N\delta_{m=n}h^{0}+\sum_{c\geq 1}\frac{S(m,n;Nc)}{c}h^{+}\left(\frac{4\pi\sqrt{mn}}{Nc}\right),

where
\mathcal{W}_{\varphi},\mathcal{W}_{t}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
are completely multiplicative positive functions, uniquely determined by

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\varphi}(p):=\frac{p}{2}\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{\varphi}(p)^{2}}{p(1-(\lambda_{\varphi}(p^{2})-1)p^{-1}+p^{-2})}\right),

(6.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{t}(p):=\frac{p}{2}\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{t}(p)^{2}}{p(1-p^{-1+2it})(1-p^{-1-2it})}\right),

and

\displaystyle h^{0}:=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(t)\,t\tanh\pi t\,dt,

\displaystyle h^{+}(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(t)\frac{2it\,J_{2it}(x)}{\cosh\pi t}\,dt

Proof.

This follows from the explicit formulas in
[
HK20
, Lemma A.9]
, which simplifies significantly since
(mn,N)=1
, and that by (
3.18
) the local symmetric square
L
-function at unramified finite places is given by

\displaystyle L_{p}(s,\operatorname{sym}^{2}\varphi)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{\varphi,p}^{2}p^{-s})(1-\alpha_{\varphi,p}^{-2}p^{-s})(1-p^{-s})

\displaystyle=(1-(\lambda_{\varphi}(p^{2})-1)p^{-s}+p^{-2s})(1-p^{-s}),

where
\alpha_{\varphi,p}^{\pm 1}
denotes the Satake parameters of
\varphi
at
p
. The fact that
\mathcal{W}_{\varphi}(p)
is non-negative follows from the trivial bound
|\lambda_{\varphi}(p^{2})|\leq p
. A similar argument applies to the continuous spectrum.
∎

We will now calculate the transforms for the relevant test functions.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
r\geq 1
and
h(t)=(\cosh(\pi t/r))^{-1}
. Then it holds that

h^{0}\ll r^{2}.

Proof.

By the trivial inequalities
|\tanh t|\leq 1
and
(\cosh t)^{-1}\leq 2e^{-|t|}
,

\displaystyle h^{0}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(t)\,t\tanh\pi t\,dt\ll\int_{\mathbb{R}}|t|e^{-\pi|t|/r}dt\ll r^{2},

as wanted.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
r\geq 3
and
h(t)=(\cosh(\pi t/r))^{-1}
. Then it holds that

h^{+}(x)=\frac{x}{\cos\frac{\pi}{2r}}+O(r^{1/2}x^{2}),\quad 0<x<2.

Proof.

By the Taylor expansion of the
J
-Bessel function
[

, with

in the above sum (using here that

\Omega(n)=k\end{subarray}}\frac{(2k)!b(n^{2})}{\nu(n^{2})n}c_{1,n^{2}},

where we used that

Note that for each prime power it holds that

Thus, the diagonal contribution is bounded by

which is admissible by Lemma

\ll_{f_{1},f_{2}}q^{-1/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|n\Rightarrow p<x,p\nmid Dq\\
\Omega(n)=2k\end{subarray}}\frac{(2k)!\cdot\lvert{b(n)}\rvert}{\nu(n)\sqrt{n}}\sum_{m|n}c_{m,n}\sqrt{m},

where the implied constant depends polynomially on

. Since

, we get by (

. Using that

\ll q^{-1/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|n\Rightarrow p<x,p\nmid Dq\\
\Omega(n)=2k\end{subarray}}\frac{(2k)!\cdot\lvert{b(n)}\rvert 2^{2k}}{\nu(n)}\ll q^{-1/2}\left(2\sum_{p<x,p\nmid Dq}|b(p)|\right)^{2k}.

∎

Given the heuristic in Section

For technical reasons we will also need a slight variant of the above. In this case we will settle for suboptimal bounds, which will however suffice for our purposes.

Proposition 7.2

(High moment estimate, variant)
.

Let

and let

\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{new}}(Y_{0}(Dq))}\frac{h(is_{\varphi})}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}\varphi)}\left(\sum_{p<x,p\nmid Dq}\frac{b(p)\lambda_{\varphi}(p^{d})}{p^{1/2}}\right)^{2k}

\displaystyle\ll_{f_{1},f_{2}}q(d+1)^{2k}\sum_{2\ell_{1}+3\ell_{2}=2k}\frac{(2k)!}{\ell_{1}!\ell_{2}!}\left(\sum_{p<x,p\nmid Dq}\frac{b(p)^{2}}{p}\right)^{\ell_{1}}\left(\sum_{p<x,p\nmid Dq}\frac{|b(p)|^{3}}{p^{3/2}}\right)^{\ell_{2}}

(7.3)

\displaystyle+q^{-1/2}\left((d+1)\sum_{p<x,p\nmid Dq}|b(p)|\right)^{2k},

with polynomial dependence on

.

Proof.

As in the proof of Proposition

, we expand the

Now we apply the Kuznetsov formula in Corollary

By the bound

Lemma 8.2

8.1.2.
Applying high moment estimates, heuristically

We will now define the key quantities needed for the proof of the estimate (

For

, we apply (
8.12
).
Notice that
\mathbb{P}(X_{\mathrm{error}}>V_{\mathrm{error}})=0
.

On the right-hand side of the inequality (
8.12
), we may extend the measure to include also the average over
t_{\varphi}\geq q^{1/100}
by positivity.
Thus, for
1\leq n\leq 5
, we put

\mathcal{I}_{n/2}(V):=\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{new}}(Y_{0}(qD))\colon\mathcal{S}_{n/2}(\varphi)>V}\frac{h(is_{\varphi})}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}\varphi)}.

We include the range
t_{\varphi}\geq q^{1/100}
also on the left-hand side simply by adding
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{tail}}
to the inequality.
We finally obtain the bound

(8.21)

\mathcal{I}(V+(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{q})\leq\mathcal{I}_{1/2}((1-C\varepsilon)V)+\sum_{n=2}^{5}\mathcal{I}_{n/2}(\varepsilon V)+\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{tail}}.

We will now apply Propositions
7.1
and
7.2
to bound
\mathcal{I}_{1/2}(V)
and
\mathcal{I}_{n/2}(V)
with
n\geq 2
, respectively.
The former,
\mathcal{I}_{1/2}(V)
requires more subtle arguing.
For
V\asymp\operatorname{var}_{q}
,
k
as in (
8.9
) and
x
as in (
8.16
) we see that the inequality
x^{k}<qD/2\pi
is violated. To avoid this we put

(8.22)

z=x^{1/\Delta^{2}}=\exp\left(\frac{9A\log q}{\varepsilon\Delta^{2}V}\right),

with
\Delta=\log\log\log q
as above, and write

\mathcal{S}_{1/2}(\varphi)=\sum_{p<z,p\nmid D}+\sum_{z\leq p<x,p\nmid D}=:\mathcal{S}_{1/2}(\varphi;z)+\mathcal{S}_{1/2}(\varphi;z,x),

and put

\mathcal{I}_{1/2}(V;z):=\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{new}}(Y_{0}(qD))\colon\mathcal{S}_{1/2}(\varphi;z)>V}\frac{h(is_{\varphi})}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}\varphi)},

and similarly for
\mathcal{I}_{1/2}(V;z,x)
. We note that

(8.23)

\mathcal{I}_{1/2}((1-C\varepsilon)V)\leq\mathcal{I}_{1/2}((1-(C+1)\varepsilon)V;z)+\mathcal{I}_{1/2}(\varepsilon V;z,x),

and we will now apply Proposition
7.1
to each of the two terms separately.

8.3.
Applying high moment estimates, rigorously

In this section we will apply Propositions
7.1
and
7.2
to each choice of a pair consisting of a range for
V
and one of the terms in Proposition
6.9
, as in explained in Table
1
in the previous section.

8.3.1.
Contribution from
p^{-1/2}

Firstly, we bound the main contribution which will come from
p<z
, i.e. the first term in the right-hand side of (
8.23
).
In doing so, we split into two ranges according to whether
V
is in the bulk of the integral (
8.10
) or not.
Consider first the contribution from the bulk.

Lemma 8.5

(Bulk range,
p<z
)
.

Let
\Delta^{-1}\operatorname{var}_{q}<V\leq\Delta\operatorname{var}_{q}
. Then, for
q
sufficiently large, it holds that

\mathcal{I}_{1/2}((1-(C+1)\varepsilon)V;z)\ll q\exp\left(-(1-(2C+3)\varepsilon)V^{2}/2\operatorname{var}_{q}\right).

Proof.

Note that, by an inequality analogous to (
8.7
), we have

\mathcal{I}_{1/2}((1-(C+1)\varepsilon)V;z)\\
\leq((1-(C+1)\varepsilon)V)^{-2k}\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{new}}(Y_{0}(Dq))}\frac{h(is_{\varphi})}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}\varphi)}\left(\mathcal{S}_{1/2}(\varphi;z)\right)^{2k}.

We now apply Proposition
7.1
with
x=z
as in equation (
8.22
) and a
k
such that
z^{k}<qD/2\pi
, which we determine below.
This yields an upper bound of

(8.24)

q\frac{(2k)!}{k!}\left(\frac{1}{2(1-(C+1)\varepsilon)^{2}V^{2}}\sum_{p<z,p\nmid D}\frac{b(p)^{2}}{p}\right)^{k}+q^{-1/2}\left(2\sum_{p<z,p\nmid D}|b(p)|\right)^{2k},

where

(8.25)

b(p)=p^{-1/\log x}|\lambda_{f_{1}}(p)\lambda_{f_{2}}(p)+\lambda_{F}(p)^{2}|\frac{\log(x/p)}{\log x}\leq|\lambda_{f_{1}}(p)\lambda_{f_{2}}(p)+\lambda_{F}(p)^{2}|.

We simplified the second term using that
(1-(C+1)\varepsilon)V\gg 1
.

By expanding the square and applying the Hecke relations as in Lemma
3.5
, we see that

\displaystyle b(p)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{f_{1}}(p)^{2}\lambda_{f_{2}}(p)^{2}+\lambda_{F}(p)^{4}+2\lambda_{f_{1}}(p)\lambda_{f_{2}}(p)\lambda_{F}(p)^{2}

\displaystyle=3+\lambda_{\operatorname{sym}^{2}f_{1}\otimes\operatorname{sym}^{2}f_{2}}(p)+\lambda_{\operatorname{sym}^{2}f_{1}}(p)+\lambda_{\operatorname{sym}^{2}f_{2}}(p)+2\lambda_{f_{1}\otimes f_{2}}(p)

(8.26)

\displaystyle+2\lambda_{\operatorname{sym}^{2}F\otimes f_{1}\otimes f_{2}}(p)+\lambda_{\operatorname{sym}^{4}F}(p)+3\lambda_{\operatorname{sym}^{2}F}(p),

for
f_{1}\neq f_{2}
and similarly for
f_{1}=f_{2}
.
In both cases we conclude that

\sum_{p<z,p\nmid D}\frac{b(p)^{2}}{p}\leq\operatorname{var}_{q}+O(1)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\operatorname{var}_{q},

for
q
sufficiently large, by extending the range to
p<q
using positivity and applying Lemma
6.5
.
To bound the second term in (
8.24
), we use that

|b(p)|\ll p^{7/32},

by the Kim–Sarnak bound (
3.5
) and so

\left(2\sum_{p<z,p\nmid D}|b(p)|\right)^{2k}\leq\left(2z^{1+7/32}\right)^{2k}\leq z^{O(k)}.

Using the inequality (
8.8
) we arrive at the upper bound

(8.27)

\mathcal{I}_{1/2}((1-(C+1)\varepsilon)V;z)\ll q\left(\frac{2k(1+\varepsilon)\operatorname{var}_{q}}{e(1-(C+1)\varepsilon)^{2}V^{2}}\right)^{k}+q^{-1/2}z^{O(k)}.

We pick

k=\left\lfloor\frac{(1-(C+1)\varepsilon)^{2}}{1+\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{V^{2}}{2\operatorname{var}_{q}}\right\rfloor=(1+o(1))\frac{(1-(C+1)\varepsilon)^{2}}{1+\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{V^{2}}{2\operatorname{var}_{q}},

which is permissible since

(8.28)

\displaystyle k\cdot\log z\leq\frac{V^{2}}{2\varepsilon\operatorname{var}_{q}}\frac{\log q}{\Delta^{2}V}\leq\log q\frac{\Delta\operatorname{var}_{q}}{2\varepsilon\Delta^{2}\operatorname{var}_{q}}=o(\log q).

Note that the second term in (
8.27
) is bounded by

\exp(O(k\log z))=q^{o(1)}
.
This yields the result since

\frac{(1-(C+1)\varepsilon)^{2}}{1+\varepsilon}>1-(2C+3)\varepsilon.

∎

Lemma 8.6

(Bulk range,
z\leq p<x
)
.

Let
\Delta^{-1}\operatorname{var}_{q}<V<\Delta\operatorname{var}_{q}
. Then, for sufficiently large
q
, there exists a constant
c(\varepsilon)>0
such that

\mathcal{I}_{1/2}(\varepsilon V;z,x)\ll q\exp(-c(\varepsilon)\Delta V).

Proof.

Applying Proposition
7.1
, we arrive at the upper bound

\displaystyle\ll q\frac{(2k)!}{k!}\left(\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}V^{2}}\sum_{z\leq p<x,p\nmid D}\frac{b(p)^{2}}{p}\right)^{k}+q^{-1/2}\left(2\sum_{z\leq p<x,p\nmid D}|b(p)|\right)^{2k},

with
b(p)
as in (
8.25
).
Now, for
\pi\in\{\operatorname{sym}^{2}F,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F\otimes f_{1}\otimes f_{2},\ldots,\}
, one of the forms appearing in (
8.26
), we have
|\lambda_{\pi}(p)|\ll p^{7/16}
by the Kim–Sarnak bound (
3.5
). Note also that, by Lemma
8.1
, in the range
\Delta^{-1}\operatorname{var}_{q}<V<\Delta\operatorname{var}_{q}
it holds for sufficiently large
q
that

x\geq z\geq\exp\left(O_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\log q}{\Delta^{3}(\log q)^{15/16}}\right)\right)\geq(\log q)^{100}.

This means that Lemma
6.5
applies and yields

\displaystyle\sum_{z\leq p<x,p\nmid D}\frac{\lambda_{\pi}(p)}{p}=\sum_{p<x,p\nmid D}\frac{\lambda_{\pi}(p)}{p}-\sum_{p<z,p\nmid D}\frac{\lambda_{\pi}(p)}{p}=O(1).

Thus, we conclude that

\sum_{z\leq p<x,p\nmid D}\frac{b(p)^{2}}{p}\ll\log\log x-\log\log z=2\log\Delta.

Applying the bound (
8.8
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1/2}(\varepsilon V;z,x)\ll q\left(\frac{c^{\prime}(\varepsilon)k\log\Delta}{V^{2}}\right)^{k}+q^{-1/2}\left(c^{\prime}x^{23/16}\right)^{2k},

for some constants
c^{\prime}(\varepsilon),c^{\prime}>0
.
Now, we put
k=\lfloor\varepsilon V/900A\rfloor
, which is admissible since

(8.29)

\displaystyle k\cdot\log x\leq\frac{\varepsilon V}{100}\frac{\log q}{\varepsilon V}\leq\frac{\log q}{100}.

The same bound shows that

\displaystyle\left(c^{\prime}x^{23/16}\right)^{2k}\ll q^{23/800+o(1)},

so we arrive at the bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1/2}(\varepsilon V;z,x)\ll q\exp\left(-c(\varepsilon)V\log\frac{V}{\log\Delta}\right)+1.

Since
V/\log\Delta\geq(\log\log q)^{1+o(1)}
, we obtain the result (recalling that
\Delta=\log\log\log q
).
∎

For
V
very large, we have the following crude estimate for the contribution from
\sigma=1/2
.

Lemma 8.7

(Large range,
\sigma=1/2
)
.

Let
\Delta\operatorname{var}_{q}<V<A\log q/\log\log q
. Then there exists a constant
c(\varepsilon)>0
such that

\mathcal{I}_{1/2}((1-(C+1)\varepsilon)V)\ll q\exp\left(-c(\varepsilon)V\log\Delta\right).

Proof.

We may follow the first steps of the proof of Lemma
8.6
and arrive at the analogue of (
8.27
), namely

\mathcal{I}_{1/2}((1-(C+1)\varepsilon)V)\ll q\left(\frac{2k(1+\varepsilon)\operatorname{var}_{q}}{e(1-(C+1)\varepsilon)^{2}V^{2}}\right)^{k}+q^{-1/2}x^{O(k)}.

We pick
k=\lfloor\varepsilon V/900A\rfloor
, which is admissible since

(8.30)

\displaystyle k\cdot\log x\leq\frac{\varepsilon V}{100}\frac{\log q}{\varepsilon V}\leq\frac{\log q}{100}.

For some
c^{\prime}(\varepsilon)>0
, we obtain the bound

\ll q\left(c^{\prime}(\varepsilon)\frac{\operatorname{var}_{q}}{V}\right)^{\varepsilon V},

which implies the result since
\operatorname{var}_{q}/V\leq\Delta^{-1}
.
∎

8.3.2.
Contribution from
p^{-n/2}
with
n\geq 2

Next, we bound the contribution with
n\in\{2,\ldots,5\}
, which can be seen to be negligible even using crude estimates.

Lemma 8.8

(Higher order terms)
.

Let
\Delta^{-1}\operatorname{var}_{q}<V<A\log q/\log\log q
. Then, for
q
sufficiently large, there exists a constant
c(\varepsilon)>0
such that for
2\leq n\leq 5
:

\mathcal{I}_{n/2}(\varepsilon V)\ll q\exp\left(-c(\varepsilon)V\log V\right).

Proof.

We recall for the convenience of the reader that

\mathcal{S}_{n/2}(\pi_{1}\otimes\pi_{2}\otimes\pi_{3};x)\\
:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{p^{n}\leq x,p\nmid D}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{2}(\sum_{m=0}^{n}c(m,n)\lambda_{\pi_{i}}(p^{m}))(\sum_{m=1}^{n}c(m,n)\lambda_{\pi_{3}}(p^{m}))}{p^{n/2+n/\log x}}\frac{\log(x/p^{n})}{\log x},

for certain constants
c(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We pull out the sum
\sum_{m=1}^{n}c(m,n)\lambda_{\varphi}(p^{m})
in
\mathcal{S}_{n/2}(\varphi)
and thus organize the latter according to the factors
\lambda_{\varphi}(p^{m})
for
1\leq m\leq n
, writing

\mathcal{S}_{n/2}(\varphi)=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\mathcal{S}_{n/2,m}(\varphi).

By the principle of subadditivity in (
8.12
), we can bound
\mathcal{I}_{n/2}(\varepsilon V)
by the sum of

\mathcal{I}_{n/2,m}(\varepsilon V/n):=\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{new}}(Y_{0}(qD))\colon\mathcal{S}_{n/2,m}(\varphi)>\varepsilon V/n}\frac{h(is_{\varphi})}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}\varphi)}.

We follow the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
8.6
, using now Proposition
7.2
.
We conclude that
\mathcal{I}_{n/2}(\varepsilon V)
is bounded by

q\frac{6^{2k}}{(\varepsilon V/n)^{2k}}\sum_{2\ell_{1}+3\ell_{2}=2k}\frac{(2k)!}{\ell_{1}!\ell_{2}!}\left(\sum_{p<\sqrt{x},p\nmid D}\frac{b(p)^{2}}{p^{n}}\right)^{\ell_{1}}\left(\sum_{p<\sqrt{x},p\nmid D}\frac{|b(p)|^{3}}{p^{3n/2}}\right)^{\ell_{2}}\\
+q^{-1/2}\frac{6^{2k}}{(\varepsilon V/n)^{2k}}\left(\sum_{p<\sqrt{x},p\nmid D}\frac{|b(p)|}{p^{n/2}}\right)^{2k},

where

\displaystyle|b(p)|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n}c(m,n)\lambda_{f_{1}}(p^{n})\right)\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n}c(m,n)\lambda_{f_{2}}(p^{n})\right)+\left(\sum_{m=0}^{n}c(m,n)\lambda_{F}(p^{n})\right)^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\ll p^{7n/32}.

where we used the Kim–Sarnak bound (
3.5
).
We see that the series over
p
are convergent and we can find
c>0
such that

\sum_{p<\sqrt{x},p\nmid D}\frac{b(p)^{2}}{p^{n}}<c,\quad\sum_{p<\sqrt{x},p\nmid D}\frac{|b(p)|^{3}}{p^{3n/2}}<c.

Inserting this, for
q
sufficiently large, we arrive at the upper bound

q\sum_{2\ell_{1}+3\ell_{2}=2k}\frac{(2k)!}{\ell_{1}!\ell_{2}!}\left(\frac{30}{\varepsilon V}\right)^{2k}c^{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}}+q^{-1/2}(36x)^{k},

for some constant
c>1
. Using that
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\leq k
and the crude estimate

\sum_{2\ell_{1}+3\ell_{2}=2k}\frac{1}{\ell_{1}!\ell_{2}!}\ll k\cdot\frac{1}{k!},

we arrive at

\displaystyle qk\left(\frac{c^{\prime}k}{\varepsilon^{2}V^{2}}\right)^{k}+q^{-1/2}(36x)^{k},

for some constant
c^{\prime}>0
.
We now pick
k=\lfloor\varepsilon V/900A\rfloor
, which is admissible since
k\log x\leq\frac{\varepsilon V}{100}\frac{\log q}{\varepsilon V}=\frac{\log q}{100}
.
In particular,
(cx)^{k}\ll q^{1/100+o(1)}
.
The bound now follows for
q
large enough by observing that
V
goes to infinity when
q\to\infty
in our range.
∎

8.4.
Putting everything together

By inserting the bounds from Lemmas
8.4
,
8.5
,
8.6
and
8.8
, which cover the entire range of
V
by the inequalities (
8.14
), (
8.21
) and (
8.23
) , we have for any
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small and
q
sufficiently large in terms of
\varepsilon
that

\displaystyle\langle|F|^{2},f_{1}\cdot(l_{q}f_{2})\rangle_{q}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{1}{qL(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)}\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{new}}(Y_{0}(qD))}\frac{h(is_{\varphi})}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}\varphi)}\mathcal{L}(\varphi)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{e^{(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{q}/2}}{qL(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)}\Biggr(\int_{\Delta^{-1}\operatorname{var}_{q}}^{A\frac{\log q}{\log\log q}}e^{V/2}\Biggr(\mathcal{I}_{1/2}((1-O(\varepsilon))V;z)+\mathcal{I}_{1/2}(\varepsilon V;z,x)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=2}^{5}\mathcal{I}_{n/2}(\varepsilon V)+\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{tail}}\Biggr)dV+O(1)\Biggr)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{e^{(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{q}/2}}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)}\Biggr(\int_{\Delta^{-1}\operatorname{var}_{q}}^{\Delta\operatorname{var}_{q}}e^{V/2-(1-O(\varepsilon))V^{2}/2\operatorname{var}_{q}}dV+\int_{\Delta^{-1}\operatorname{var}_{q}}^{\Delta\operatorname{var}_{q}}e^{V/2-c(\varepsilon)V\Delta}dV

\displaystyle+\int_{\Delta\operatorname{var}_{q}}^{A\frac{\log q}{\log\log q}}e^{V/2-c(\varepsilon)V\log V}dV+\int_{\Delta^{-1}\operatorname{var}_{q}}^{A\frac{\log q}{\log\log q}}e^{V/2-c(\varepsilon)V\log\Delta}dV+q^{-100}\Biggr).

We see that the last three integrals can be bounded by
O_{\varepsilon}(1)
(since the exponent is negative for
q
sufficiently large) and so the total contribution from these terms is
O(e^{(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{q}/2}/L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F))
.
To estimate the remaining integral we extend the range of integration and apply the formula (
8.11
) to arrive at the bound

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}_{q}}e^{(1-\varepsilon)\mu_{q}/2+(1-O(\varepsilon))^{-1}\operatorname{var}_{q}/8}}{L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)}.

Since
L(1,\operatorname{sym}^{2}F)\gg(\log\log q)^{-O(1)}
from Corollary
6.7
we conclude the bound (
8.5
) and consequently Theorem
4.9
follows.
