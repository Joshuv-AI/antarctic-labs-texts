Reshaping immunotherapy sequencing strategy: equivalent survival with induction plus consolidation vs. consolidation-only strategy in unresectable stage III NSCLC
Authors: Wenlu Chen, Anqi Zhao, Yu Liang, Xin Cao, Baixue Wu, Yuying Zhou, Wenbo Zhang, Meng Zhao, Qing Hou, Ningning Yao, Feng Li, Jianchun Duan, Shuangping Zhang, Nan Bi, Jianzhong Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the efficacy and safety of a sequential combination of chemoradiotherapy (CRT) with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) administered as induction plus consolidation versus consolidation-only in patients with unresectable stage III non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled 265 patients treated at two centers between March 2019 and August 2023. Patients were categorized into a consolidation-only group (n = 153) and an induction plus consolidation group (n = 112). Propensity score matching (PSM, 1:1 ratio) was used to balance baseline characteristics. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis and log-rank tests. Adverse events (AEs) were evaluated according to CTCAE v5.0.ResultsBefore PSM, no significant survival differences were observed between groups (2-y PFS: 52.6% vs. 63.5%, p = 0.085; 2-y OS: 91.7% vs. 81.2%, p = 0.822). After PSM, outcomes remained comparable (2-y PFS: 56.8% vs. 62.2%, p = 0.179; 2-y OS: 94.9% vs. 87.9%, p = 0.514). Before PSM, the most common AE was pneumonitis (60.7% vs. 55.1%, p = 0.442). The induction plus consolidation group showed a lower incidence of anemia (p = 0.004) but a higher incidence of ALT/AST elevation (14.3% vs. 5.1%, p = 0.022). The incidence of all other AEs was comparable (p > 0.05). After PSM, only anemia was significantly higher in the consolidation-only group (52.8% vs. 27.8%, p = 0.004).ConclusionIn treatment-tolerant patients, induction plus consolidation therapy demonstrated comparable efficacy and safety to consolidation-only therapy in unresectable stage III NSCLC.Keywords: Unresectable stage III non-small cell lung cancer, chemoradiotherapy, immunotherapy, efficacy, safety

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 1.8 million deaths annually.1 Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) constitutes 80%–85% of all cases, with roughly 30% diagnosed at stage III.2 For unresectable stage III NSCLC, definitive chemoradiotherapy (CRT) yields limited outcomes, with reported 5-y progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) rates of only 18.3% and 32.1%, respectively.3

The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has transformed the therapeutic landscape for stage III NSCLC. The landmark PACIFIC trial4 established durvalumab consolidation post-cCRT as a standard of care, demonstrating a significantly improvement in progression-free survival compared with placebo. Subsequent studies, including GEMSTONE-301,5 further validated the survival benefits of consolidation ICIs, solidifying this sequential approach as the current standard.6

Nevertheless, real-world implementation faces significant challenges. A substantial proportion of patients fail to initiate or complete consolidation ICIs due to early disease progression, treatment-related toxicities, or clinical deterioration, all associated with poorer survival.7,8 This efficacy-effectiveness gap underscores the need for optimized strategies.

Recent investigations have explored introducing ICIs during the induction phase before CRT. Preclinical and clinical evidence9-11 suggests that induction chemoimmunotherapy may reduce radiotherapy target volumes by debulking tumors, potentially minimizing radiation toxicity, improving treatment adherence, and enhancing survival. The rationale centers on early immune activation, suppression of micrometastases, and prevention of distant spread. However, not all studies confirm these benefits. One trial10 reported no OS improvement despite the theoretical advantages of early ICI administration. Thus, a systematic evaluation of the efficacy and safety of induction chemoimmunotherapy followed by CRT and consolidation ICIs is imperative.

Accordingly, this multicenter retrospective study aims to compare the efficacy and safety of consolidation ICIs alone versus induction chemoimmunotherapy followed by consolidation ICIs in unresectable stage III NSCLC, and to determine the optimal sequencing strategy of ICIs relative to CRT, thereby providing evidence to guide clinical decision-making and improve patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study population

A total of 265 patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC who received CRT combined with ICIs in sequential regimens, either induction chemoimmunotherapy followed by consolidation ICIs or consolidation ICIs alone, were retrospectively enrolled from two centers (Shanxi Provincial Cancer Hospital and Cancer Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences) between March 2019 and August 2023. The inclusion criteria were: (1) pathologically or cytologically confirmed NSCLC; (2) Unresectable stage III disease confirmed by imaging per the WHO classification12 and IASLC 8th edition TNM staging13; (3) Completion of both CRT and ICI therapy; (4) Absence of targetable driver gene mutations confirmed by genetic testing; and (5) participants must have received at least one cycle of immunotherapy, either before radiotherapy (in combination with chemotherapy) or after radiotherapy. Exclusion criteria were: (1) treatment regimens other than the two predefined strategies; (2) incomplete definitive radiotherapy; (3) missing baseline or follow-up data; (4) follow-up duration < 6 months; and (5) receiving more than five cycles of induction therapy. This study was approved by the institutional medical ethics committee (No. KY2025006). Informed consent was waived due to its retrospective design.

Treatment strategy

In the consolidation-only group, patients underwent CRT followed by consolidation therapy with ICIs. In the induction plus consolidation group, patients received one to four cycles of induction chemoimmunotherapy, followed by CRT and subsequent ICI consolidation (Figure 1).

Treatment paradigms of two immunotherapy-based regimens in stage III unresectable NSCLC. Chemo: chemotherapy; CRT: chemoradiotherapy; ICIs: immune checkpoint inhibitors. Created in BioRender. Cao J. (2026) https://BioRender.com/arszhk3.

Completion of ICI therapy for study inclusion was defined by the treatment strategy. In the consolidation-only group, patients were required to receive at least two cycles of ICI after CRT. In the induction plus consolidation group, patients were required to receive at least one cycle of induction ICI combined with chemotherapy prior to CRT, followed by at least two cycles of consolidation ICI after CRT. These criteria ensured adequate exposure to the intended treatment sequence. The median number of ICI cycles was 3 (range, 1–4) during induction and 6 (range, 2–44) during consolidation. Patients receiving more than five cycles of induction therapy were excluded, as prolonged induction may delay definitive CRT without additional benefit.14-16 The PD-1 inhibitors used included pembrolizumab, camrelizumab, sintilimab, tislelizumab, nivolumab, and penpulimab. PD-L1 inhibitors included durvalumab, atezolizumab, and sugemalimab. Chemotherapy regimens consisted of carboplatin or cisplatin combined with paclitaxel for squamous cell carcinoma, and carboplatin or cisplatin combined with pemetrexed for adenocarcinoma.

Radiotherapy was delivered using either intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) or volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT). Completion of CRT was defined as delivery of the full prescribed radiotherapy dose (60–66 Gy, most commonly 60 Gy in 30 fractions of 2 Gy each) together with at least two cycles of concurrent or sequential platinum-based doublet chemotherapy. All radiotherapy plans were designed using the Philips Pinnacle planning system based on four-dimensional computed tomography (4D-CT) imaging. Treatment plans were designed to ensure that at least 95% of the planning target volume (PTV) received 100% of the prescribed dose. The gross tumor volume of the primary lesion (GTVp) was delineated on simulation CT images. The nodal gross tumor volume (GTVn) included lymph nodes with a short-axis diameter > 1 cm or those showing elevated fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake on positron emission tomography (PET). The clinical target volume (CTV) was defined by expanding the combined GTVp and GTVn by 0.5 cm to account for potential microscopic disease. The PTV was generated by adding an additional margin of at least 0.5 cm to the CTV.

Data collection and follow-up

Baseline demographics and treatment-related data were extracted from electronic medical records, including age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, smoking history, histological subtype, TNM stage, PD-L1 expression, radiotherapy parameters, number of ICI cycles, and chemotherapy cycles. Routine follow-up assessments included imaging studies, tumor markers, and documentation of any adverse events. Toxicities were graded using v5.0 of the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE). Imaging evaluations were performed every 12 weeks for up to two years or until tumor recurrence or progression. The final follow-up date was May 22, 2025. Tumor response was evaluated according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1, using computed tomography (CT) or positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET/CT) to assess complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.3). Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to disease progression or death from any cause, and overall survival (OS) as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause or the date of last follow-up. The primary endpoint of this study was PFS, while OS was designated as the secondary endpoint. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. To minimize confounding, 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper width of 0.06, as 0.1 may not achieve optimal balance, whereas 0.05 may result in substantial sample loss. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with SMD ≤ 0.2 indicating acceptable balance. Matching was performed on key baseline characteristics, including age, sex, ECOG performance status, smoking history, TNM stage, WHO histology, PD-L1 expression, bulky tumor status, and CRT modality. Survival outcomes between groups were compared using Kaplan–Meier analysis and the log-rank test. In exploratory analyses, to align more closely with the PACIFIC trial design, OS1 and PFS1 were also calculated starting from 6 weeks after the completion of CRT. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

This study included 265 consecutive eligible patients. Of these, 112 patients (42.3%) received induction plus consolidation therapy, while 153 patients (57.7%) received consolidation-only therapy (Figure 2). Patients aged ≥ 60 y comprised 64.9% (172/265) of the cohort. The majority were male (240/265, 90.6%), and 80.0% (212/265) had a history of smoking. Histologically, squamous cell carcinoma predominated, observed in 180 patients (67.9%), while 85 patients (32.1%) had non-squamous histology. The TNM stage distribution was: stage IIIA in 88 patients (33.2%), stage IIIB in 122 (46.0%), and stage IIIC in 55 (20.8%). A total of 104 patients (39.3%) presented with a bulky tumor, defined as a primary tumor diameter ≥ 5 cm or metastatic lymph nodes with a short axis ≥ 2 cm. Before PSM, significant imbalances (SMD > 0.2) were observed between the two groups in ECOG performance status (SMD = 0.381), smoking history (SMD = 0.466), PD-L1 expression (SMD = 0.576), and bulky tumor status (SMD = 0.230). After 1:1 PSM, all baseline covariates achieved adequate balance, with all SMD values ≤ 0.2. Detailed characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Survival outcomes

As of the last follow-up on May 22, 2025, the median follow-up for the entire cohort was 17.7 months. In the overall cohort, the median PFS was 39.4 months, the corresponding 1-y, 2-y, and 3-y PFS rates were 82.2%, 60.5%, and 51.1% (Figure 3A). The median OS had not been reached, the 1-y, 2-y, and 3-y overall survival (OS) rates were 98.2%, 83.6%, and 74.6%, respectively (Figure 3B). Before PSM, the median PFS was 25.9 months in the induction plus consolidation group and 41.8 months in the consolidation-only group. The 2-y PFS rates were 52.6% vs. 63.5% (HR = 1.49, 95% CI: 0.95–2.34, p = 0.085; Figure 3C). The median OS was not reached in either group, and the 2-y OS rates were 91.7% for induction plus consolidation vs. 81.2% for consolidation-only (HR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.36–2.25, p = 0.822; Figure 3D). After PSM, the median PFS was 26.2 months in the induction plus consolidation group and not reached in the consolidation-only group, with 2-y PFS rates of 56.8% vs. 62.2% (HR = 1.53, 95% CI: 0.82–2.84, p = 0.179; Figure 3E). The median OS remained unreached in both groups, the 2-y OS rates were 94.9% vs. 87.9% (HR = 1.54, 95% CI: 0.42–5.60, p = 0.514; Figure 3F). Before PSM, subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, ECOG, smoking, TNM stage, WHO histology, PD-L1, bulky tumor, and CRT showed no significant differences in PFS (HR range: 1.01–3.69) or OS (HR range: 0–Inf) between the two treatment groups (p > 0.05) (Supplementary Figure 1A and B). After PSM, the results remained consistent, with no statistically significant differences in PFS (HR range: 0.65–Inf) or OS (HR range: 0–Inf) between the groups (p > 0.05)(Supplementary Figure 1C and D).

Best tumor response

After excluding patients with missing or unevaluable response data, a total of 220 patients were included in the efficacy analysis. In the consolidation-only group (n = 137), complete response (CR) was observed in 6 patients (4.4%), partial response (PR) in 81 patients (59.1%), stable disease (SD) in 46 patients (33.6%), and progressive disease (PD) in 4 patients (2.9%). The objective response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR) were 63.5% (87/137) and 97.1% (133/137), respectively (Figure 4A). In the induction plus consolidation group (n = 83), 3 patients (3.6%) achieved CR, 47 (56.6%) had PR, 27 (32.5%) had SD, and 6 (7.2%) had PD. The ORR and DCR were 60.2% (50/83) and 92.8% (77/83), respectively (Figure 4B).

Safety

Patients from two centers were excluded due to incomplete data, resulting in 250 evaluable patients (112 in the induction plus consolidation group and 138 in the consolidation-only group). Safety was assessed with CTCAE v5.0. Before PSM, the overall incidence of adverse events (AEs) was similar between the two groups, with comparable tolerability observed (Table 2). Comparing the induction plus consolidation group to the consolidation-only group, the overall incidence of pneumonitis was 60.7% vs. 55.1% (p = 0.442), and the incidence of grade ≥ 3 pneumonitis was 6.2% vs. 2.9% (p = 0.228), respectively. A statistically significant difference was observed in the overall incidence of anemia between the two groups (28.6% vs. 47.1%, p = 0.004); however, no significant difference was noted for grade ≥ 3 anemia (0% vs. 2.2%, p = 0.255). Radiation- or chemotherapy-induced elevations in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were more frequent in the induction plus consolidation group (14.3% vs. 5.1%, p = 0.022), though the percentages of grade ≥ 3 events were not significantly different (2.7% vs. 0.7%, p = 0.328). No significant differences were found between the groups in either the overall or grade ≥ 3 incidence of other AEs, including leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, radiation esophagitis, and enteritis (p > 0.05). Notably, after PSM, a statistically significant difference was observed in the overall incidence of anemia. The all-grade incidence of anemia was significantly higher in the consolidation-only group than in the induction plus consolidation group (52.8% vs. 27.8%, p = 0.004). Importantly, all anemia adverse events were low grade (Grades 1 and 2), and no significant difference in the incidence of grade ≥ 3 anemia was observed between the two treatment cohorts (5.6% vs. 0%, p = 0.120) (Table 3).

Exploratory analysis (PACIFIC model)

To enhance comparability with the PACIFIC trial, patients who experienced disease progression within six weeks after completion of CRT were excluded, and survival metrics were recalculated. OS1 and PFS1 were redefined with a new starting point at six weeks post-CRT. Before PSM, the median PFS1 was 21.0 months in the induction plus consolidation group and 39.6 months in the consolidation-only group, while the median OS1 was 31.8 months and not reached, respectively. A significantly lower 2-y PFS1 rate was observed in the induction plus consolidation group compared with the consolidation-only group (31.6% vs. 64.6%; HR = 1.85, p = 0.011), whereas no significant difference was observed in the 2-y OS1 rates (75.6% vs. 82.8%; HR = 1.09, p = 0.870; Figure 5A and B). After PSM, the median PFS1 was 21.0 months in the induction plus consolidation group and 33.2 months in the consolidation-only group, while the median OS1 was 31.8 months and not reached, respectively. The results showed no significant differences in 2-y PFS1 (38.8% vs. 59.2%, HR = 1.62, p = 0.130) or 2-y OS1 (78.6% vs. 82.5%, HR = 1.38, p = 0.610; Figure 5C and D).

Discussion

This multicenter retrospective study directly compared the efficacy and safety of induction chemoimmunotherapy followed by consolidation ICIs versus consolidation ICIs alone in patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC. Our key findings indicate that, after PSM, there were no statistically significant differences in OS or PFS between the two treatment groups. ORR and DCR were also comparable. Furthermore, the AE profiles were similar, suggesting comparable efficacy and tolerability for both strategies in this real-world cohort.

The management of unresectable stage III NSCLC continues to pose challenges.17 The PACIFIC regimen established consolidation ICIs following CRT as the standard of care,4 and subsequent studies such as GEMSTONE-301,5 PACIFIC-6,18 and PACIFIC-R19 have further validated this approach. In recent years, several studies have explored shifting ICIs to the induction phase prior to CRT, aiming to activate the tumor immune microenvironment earlier and enhance synergy with CRT. Preliminary clinical trials and preclinical data support the feasibility and safety of this strategy.11,20,21 Our results are consistent with prior research. Yang et al.10 reported no significant differences in OS (HR = 0.81, p = 0.650) or PFS (HR = 1.07, p = 0.801) between the two modalities. Similarly, Guan et al. found comparable 2-y OS (78.0% vs. 79.5%, p = 0.495) and PFS (52.2% vs. 53.1%, p = 0.966),16 closely mirroring our findings (OS: 91.7% vs. 81.2%, HR = 0.90, p = 0.822; PFS: 52.6% vs. 63.5%, HR = 1.49, p = 0.085). However, the findings of the present study appear to contrast with those reported by Li et al.22 After careful comparison of the study cohorts, we propose that the following key differences may largely explain the divergent results. First, differences in the overall duration and intensity of immunotherapy exposure represent a major confounding factor. In the present study, the consolidation-only group received a median of 6 cycles of ICIs. Similarly, Guan et al.16 reported a median of 10 cycles and observed no significant survival differences between the two strategies. In contrast, in the study by Li et al.22, the consolidation-only group was defined as receipt of at least one cycle of ICIs after radiotherapy. This crucial difference suggests that a substantial proportion of patients in consolidation-only group may have received minimal consolidation therapy, which could attenuate its efficacy and consequently exaggerate the relative benefit of the induction plus consolidation approach. Second, the baseline efficacy of the control group differed substantially. The 2-y PFS rate in our consolidation-only group (63.5%) and in other real-world studies10,16,23,24 was markedly higher than the 2-y PFS rate of 17.0% reported for the consolidation group in Li et al. This suggests that the patient population or disease characteristics in Li et al. may have been associated with a poorer prognosis, which could also influence the observed relative treatment effect. In the multicenter, single-arm, phase II APOLLO trial,25 the reported 2-y OS and PFS rates were 60.5% and 50.0%, respectively. The more favorable outcomes observed in the present study likely reflect inclusion of only patients who completed planned treatment, representing a more treatment-tolerant subgroup, which may overestimate survival benefit relative to an unselected population.

Our exploratory analysis using the PACIFIC trial framework revealed a significantly lower 2-y PFS1 in the induction‑consolidation group before PSM (31.6% vs. 64.6%, p = 0.011). After PSM, this difference was no longer statistically significant (p = 0.130), but the group still showed an unfavorable trend (HR = 1.62). The PFS curves began to diverge only after about 21 months. This suggests that the difference lies more in the durability of treatment response than in the initial efficacy of consolidation immunotherapy. We hypothesize that this long‑term pattern may result from several interacting mechanisms. These include potential immune modulation or exhaustion induced by intensive chemoimmunotherapy, a prolonged pre‑radiotherapy interval allowing micrometastases to progress, and the effects of extended systemic treatment. These findings refine the clinical profile of the induction plus consolidation strategy. It remains valuable for patients needing rapid cytoreduction. However, for those wanting sustained disease control, careful counseling and enhanced long‑term follow‑up are warranted. Further studies with longer follow-up and biomarker analyses are needed to clarify the biology and identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from this sequential approach.

Although the PACIFIC regimen remains the standard of care, real-world data show that many patients receiving CRT are ineligible for durvalumab consolidation.7,8 Therefore, initiating ICIs during the induction phase, in an induction plus consolidation immunotherapy strategy, has been proposed as an alternative. It is critical to emphasize that the present study, by requiring completion of all therapy, only included patients who were both eligible for and tolerant of consolidation immunotherapy. Therefore, our findings do not directly inform the management of the substantial real-world population that fails to initiate or complete consolidation due to progression, toxicity, or deterioration. Theoretically, induction plus consolidation approach may expand the population benefiting from combined therapy by providing early tumor control, potentially enhancing therapeutic synergy26-28; however, this hypothesis remains to be validated in prospective studies that include unselected patient populations, including those who might progress or become intolerant during induction. Early clinical evidence supports this rationale. For example, induction chemoimmunotherapy significantly prolonged median progression-free survival (mPFS) compared with chemotherapy alone.26,27 In the phase II APOLLO trial,25 the 24-month PFS and OS were 50.0% and 60.5%, respectively, for the induction plus consolidation approach. For comparison, the same outcomes for the PACIFIC regimen were 45.0% and 66.3%, respectively.24 These data suggest that an induction plus consolidation immunotherapy strategy may achieve survival benefits broadly comparable to those with PACIFIC.

Regarding safety, pneumonitis was the most common AE, with no significant difference in incidence between groups (p > 0.05). Overall treatment tolerance was favorable: the induction plus consolidation group had a 60.7% overall pneumonitis rate (≥grade 3: 6.2%), aligning with Guan et al. (54.8% overall; 8.1% ≥ grade 3) and KEYNOTE-799 (≥grade 3: 8.0%). However, the induction plus consolidation group had a higher incidence of ALT/AST elevation (14.3% vs. 5.1%, p = 0.022). The chemotherapy agents used during the induction phase (platinum, taxanes, or pemetrexed) may have cumulative hepatotoxicity, although most events were grade 1–2 and resolved with hepatoprotective therapy. After PSM, the consolidation-only group demonstrated a higher incidence of anemia; however, no other significant differences in adverse event profiles were observed between the two strategies. This difference may be attributable to the combined effects of chemoradiotherapy-induced bone marrow suppression and subsequent immune-mediated inflammatory responses. Overall, this strategy's safety profile was manageable.

This study has several limitations inherent to its design: first, its retrospective nature, limited number of centers, sample size, potential selection bias, and heterogeneity in ICIs used constrain the findings. Prospective validation in larger populations is needed. Second, by excluding surgical candidates, the results may not fully generalize to all stage III patients, and comparisons with other treatment modalities require further exploration. Third, the median follow-up time was relatively short (17.7 months), and OS data are immature; longer follow-up may yield additional insights. Fourth, squamous cell carcinoma predominated (67.9%), limiting the generalizability of the findings to adenocarcinoma patients. Fifth, the lack of detailed radiotherapy dosimetric data (e.g., mean lung dose, V5, and V20) may confound the assessment of pulmonary toxicity and clinical outcomes. Finally, the inclusion criteria required completion of both planned CRT and ICIs therapy, which may have selected for patients with better treatment tolerance and a lower risk of early progression. Consequently, the observed efficacy might overestimate that achievable in a broader real-world population where treatment discontinuation due to progression or toxicity is more common.

Conclusion

For patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC who could tolerate the full treatment course, a sequential strategy of induction chemoimmunotherapy followed by consolidation immunotherapy yielded survival outcomes comparable to those achieved with consolidation immunotherapy alone. This approach demonstrates clinical feasibility and a generally acceptable safety profile. However, the higher incidence of transaminase elevation observed in the induction plus consolidation group highlights the importance of vigilant monitoring of liver function during treatment.
