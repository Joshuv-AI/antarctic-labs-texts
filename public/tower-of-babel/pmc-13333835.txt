Multimodal computational discovery of MvfR inhibitors targeting quorum sensing in multi-drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa
Authors: Tope Abraham Ibisanmi, Xiaotao Jiang, Rasel Ahmed Khan, Tsz Tin Yu, Mark Willcox, Naresh Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a major global health concern due to its multidrug resistance (MDR), necessitating the urgent development of novel therapeutic strategies. Understanding the molecular basis of resistance in clinical isolates is critical for designing next-generation antimicrobials. This study analysed recent clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa obtained from the NCBI for their resistance gene and virulence factor profiles. Among the virulence-associated targets, MvfR, a key transcriptional regulator of quorum sensing and biofilm formation, was prioritized based on its functional relevance. AI modelling of MvfR identified from the genome analysis was performed, followed by molecular docking against library of compounds, phylogenetic comparisons to compare with previously identified homologs, ADMET-profiling, 500 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, binding free energy, and Density Functional Theory (DFT). Genes critical for antimicrobial resistance, drug targeting, and virulence factors were identified across multiple databases. The antimicrobial resistance genes and receptors revealed key resistance mechanisms, including antibiotic-inactivating enzymes, efflux pumps, quorum sensing, and alterations in cell wall charge or permeability. Notably, (S)-1-(2-(difluoromethyl)-1 H-benzo[d]imidazol-5-yl)-3-(2-hydroxy-2-(pyridin-4-yl)ethyl)urea exhibited the highest docking score against MvfR. DFT and MD simulations over 500 ns demonstrated stability of the top ligands, supported by favourable molecular stability parameters such as RMSD, SASA, RMSF, and Rg plots. Furthermore, the top-ranking ligands satisfied Lipinski’s rule of five, suggesting favourable drug-like properties. This study provides an integrated computational characterization of MvfR in recent P. aeruginosa isolates and identifies genetic variations that may influence disease manifestation. It further demonstrates an integrative computational strategy to accelerate discovery of promising antimicrobial agents against multidrug-resistant bacteria.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50613-z.

Introduction

The ad hoc Intergovernmental Advisory Committee on AMR of the United Nations projects that by 2050 drug-resistant diseases will kill up to 10 million people a year and put 24 million people in catastrophic poverty by 20301 This situation is exemplified in emerging nations, where the usage of antibiotics has almost doubled. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is listed by WHO as a critical pathogen. It is an opportunistic pathogen that has a high rate of antimicrobial resistance. Worryingly, it is estimated that annual deaths due to antibiotic-resistant P. aeruginosa could reach 300,000 cases2,3, with a mortality rates ranging between 18 and 61% among patients with bacteremia and 27–48% among critically ill patients with hospital acquired pneumonia4. It is the most common cause of nosocomial infections and causes approximately 10% of all hospital-acquired infections, particularly in immunocompromised and in cystic fibrosis patients5,6.

P. aeruginosa causes infections across various parts of the body, including the eyes, lungs, urinary tract, and bloodstream, that are often severe and life-threatening7. These infections are particularly challenging to treat due to the organism’s inherent resistance to multiple antimicrobial agents and its ability to rapidly develop antibiotic resistance during treatment8–10. Moreover, it can produce biofilms on medical devices, such as catheters and ventilators11 that protects cells from antibiotics by allowing the bacteria to develop tolerant/persister cells4.

P. aeruginosa adaptability is often mediated by its quorum sensing (QS) system, which regulates a multitude of virulence factors. Among the key players in this regulatory network is MvfR (multiple virulence factor regulator, also known as PqsR)12 MvfR is one of the three interconnected P. aeruginosa QS regulators that govern many virulence functions in P. aeruginosa13(Fig. 1). MvfR can directly control the other QS regulators, LasR and RhlR14. In addition, MvfR controls the synthesis of distinct low-molecular-weight compounds via the transcriptional regulation of the pqs operon15. A previous study has shown that the loss of MvfR leads to a reduction in virulence factor production. This makes MvfR a highly desirable target for drug discovery16.

Current drug discovery efforts are hindered by challenges in identifying compounds that effectively target bacterial receptors, the rapid evolution of resistance mechanisms, and structural constraints associated with known chemical scaffolds17,18. Although the availability of genomic and structural datasets has expanded substantially, their full potential remains underutilized due to difficulties in integrating and systematically analysing large scale biological data. This limitation is particularly evident in studies investigating quorum sensing inhibition in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, where many approaches rely on previously characterised reference strains and structural models deposited in public databases. Such strategies often overlook the genetic variability present in contemporary clinical isolates, which may influence receptor architecture, ligand binding behaviour, and ultimately therapeutic effectiveness. In addition, many computational screening efforts employ a relatively narrow methodological framework, frequently focusing primarily on molecular docking without incorporating deeper physicochemical evaluation or dynamic analyses. Consequently, leveraging advanced computational tools, comprehensive database screening, and structure based drug design, alongside the exploration of natural and synthetic small molecules, offers a more robust strategy for identifying and optimising compounds with enhanced antimicrobial activity.

This study performed genomic analysis using the bacterial genome datasets to profile resistance factors and identify targets for new drugs. Leveraging computational tools, and the integration of genomic insights to identify genetic variation in new isolates, this approach offers a pathway to uncover and optimize compounds with enhanced antimicrobial activity, paving the way for therapeutic interventions and addressing the limitation in the field. While there are 3D structures available in databases, this study also aims to compare those identified from recent bacterial genomes with those in online databases, as genetic variation can influence disease expression in microbes. The study focused on identifying compounds that might bind to MvfR due to its central role in virulence of P. aeruginosa.

Results

Genome annotation and general features

Multiple strains were initially used as input, resulting in a consensus assembly consisting of 22 contigs with a total genome length of 6,129,342 bp, an average GC content of 66.72%, an N50 length of 1,909,286 bp, and an L50 count of 2. No plasmids or chromosomes were detected in the assembly. The annotation identified 5,731 coding sequences (CDS), 57 tRNA genes, and 12 rRNA genes, with a genome identifier of 287.40222. Of the CDS, 971 were hypothetical proteins, and 4,760 were functionally assigned. The genomic features include 5,731 protein-coding sequences (CDS), 57 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, and 12 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes (Table 1). A circular graphical display (Fig. 2) illustrates the genome annotations, including outer to inner rings, the contigs, CDS on the forward strand, CDS on the reverse strand, RNA genes, CDS with homology to known antimicrobial resistance genes, CDS with homology to known virulence factors, GC content and GC skew, the colors of the CDS on the forward and reverse strand indicate the subsystem that these genes belong (Fig. 3).

Specialty genes and antimicrobial resistance determinants

Annotation highlighted the presence of genes involved in virulence, antimicrobial resistance, efflux transport, and stress responses (Fig. 7; Table 2). Notably, genes encoding antibiotic inactivation enzymes were prominent, including members of the APH(3’) family, CatB, OXA-50, and PDC β-lactamases. A substantial number of resistance determinants were linked to mutations or modifications in intrinsic antibiotic targets such as Alr, Ddl, dxr, EF-G, EF-Tu, folA, Dfr, folP, gyrA, gyrB, inhA, fabI, iso-tRNA, kasA, MurA, rho, rpoB, rpoC, S10p, and S12p, which may contribute to reduced antibiotic binding efficacy. Additionally, the genome harbored several efflux systems, particularly EmrAB-OMF, EmrAB-TolC, MacA, MacB, and Mex family members (including MexAB-OprM, MexEF-OprN, MexHI-OpmD), these are capable of expelling diverse antibiotics, thereby conferring multidrug resistance (Fig. 4). Genes such as fabV and HtdX, known for target replacement mechanisms, were also identified, potentially supporting functional bypass of susceptible targets. Additional resistance mechanisms included gene absence (gidB), this gene has been shown to contribute to aminoglycoside resistance, and cell wall charge modifications mediated by GdpD and PgsA, affecting antimicrobial peptide binding. Moreover, reduced permeability, conferred by Occ/Opr family genes (OccD2/OpdC, OccK1/OpdK, etc.) can further restrict antibiotic entry into the bacterial cell. Regulatory elements such as OxyR were also identified, modulating the expression of resistance genes and contributing to adaptive resistance strategies.

Ligand library acquisition

Following the database query, extensive compound libraries were successfully compiled for each group during the ligand acquisition phase. Specifically, the libraries consisted of 1,026 compounds in group A, 1,852 compounds in group B, 712 compounds in group C, and 3,609 compounds in group D. Importantly, the inclusion of the original template compounds within these large datasets served as a critical validation step for the pharmacophore models employed in this study. Building upon this foundation, molecular docking analyses were subsequently performed on the selected compounds to further evaluate their potential interactions and binding affinities with the target sites.

Docking interaction

Interaction analysis revealed that the ligands form stable complexes through hydrogen bonding, electrostatic contacts, hydrophobic interactions, and aromatic stacking. The ligand formed hydrogen bonds with Ala190, Asn191, Glu151, and Tyr192, along with a pi-cation interaction with LYS266. Hydrophobic contacts involved Phe217, Val218, and Tyr165. Negatively charged residues including Asp150, Glu151, and Glu152 were involved in electrostatic interactions, and polar residues such as Thr265 and Asn191 contributed additional contacts, a halogen bond involving a fluorine atom on the ligand and indications of hydration sites were also noted. In the B1 complex, hydrogen bonds were observed with Glu151, Glu152, Lys266, Arg193, and Asp150. Pi-pi stacking interactions occurred with Tyr192, and some of the hydrophobic contacts noticed included Ile149, Val218, and Phe217. For the C1 ligand, hydrogen bonds were observed with Glu151, Tyr192, Asn191, Tyr268, and Ala190, and pi-pi stacking with Tyr192 was present. Hydrophobic interactions were observed with Ala187, Ile186, and Leu257. Halogen bonding and metal coordination interactions were also identified. Furthermore, in the D1 complex, hydrogen bonding involved Tyr192, Lys266, and Glu151, salt bridge were also observed with Asp264, also, hydrophobic contacts were observed with Ile149, Ile263, Ala190, Tyr268and Phe217 (Fig. 9).

Figure 6 shows the interaction frequency between ligand structural motifs and binding site residues in top ranked compounds. The interaction profile of the identified motif families showed that hydrogen-bonding was the dominant binding feature across the top-ranked compounds, with the amide contributing the highest number of H-bond interactions (88), followed by urea (49), imidazole (48), phenyl (45), benzimidazole (39), aminoglycoside (37), pyridine (30), and indole (28). Among these, amide-containing motifs also displayed notable electrostatic interactions (20), pi-stacking interactions (9), and pi-cation contacts (6), indicating that this motif provided a highly versatile interaction platform within the receptor binding site. Urea, imidazole, benzimidazole, and pyridine motifs likewise combined strong hydrogen-bonding capacity with secondary electrostatic and aromatic interactions, suggesting that these heterocyclic and linker-containing motifs made substantial contributions to ligand stabilization.

The amino acid distribution further showed that GLU151, GLU152, TYR192, and LYS266 were the most frequently engaged residues across multiple motif classes, indicating that these residues constituted the principal interaction hotspots within the binding pocket. For example, amide-mediated H-bonds were especially concentrated around GLU151, TYR192, GLU152, and LYS266, while urea and imidazole motifs also showed recurrent contacts with the same residues. Aromatic interaction patterns were additionally evident for phenyl, indole, benzimidazole, and imidazole motifs, which formed pi-stacking or pi-cation contacts mainly with TYR192, TYR268, PHE217, and LYS266. Aminoglycoside motifs were distinguished by a high number of hydrogen-bond (37) and electrostatic (17) interactions, reflecting their highly polar nature and strong capacity for residue engagement. Less frequent interaction profiles were observed for purine, benzene, pyrimidine, benzothiadiazole, cyclohexanol, hydrazide, and hydrazine motifs, each contributing fewer total contacts but still participating in specific hydrogen-bond, pi-cation, or electrostatic interactions.

Drug likeness

The drug-likeness predictions for the top compounds from Groups A, B, C, and D are summarized based on their solubility, absorption, bioavailability, and related properties. For Group A, most compounds exhibit high gastrointestinal (GI) absorption and zero violations of Lipinski’s rule of five (Ivanović et al., 2020), with consistent bioavailability scores (0.55). Solubility is generally favorable, but compounds A4 and A7 showed reduced solubility in certain categories. None of the compounds permeate the blood-brain barrier (BBB), and P-glycoprotein (Pgp) substrate activity was frequently observed (Table 4). Around 90% comply with Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, and PAINS rules, reflecting optimal molecular weight, polarity, and structural features. Solubility is high, with 80% predicted as soluble, and all compounds show oral bioavailability above 0.55, with 90% having high gastrointestinal absorption. Lower blood-brain barrier permeability (30%) and P-gp non-substrate status (40%) may limit CNS exposure (Fig. 7).

Discussion

The escalating threat posed by multi-drug-resistant (MDR) pathogens, particularly Pseudomonas aeruginosa, has become one of the most critical challenges in modern antimicrobial therapy, infections caused by this versatile and highly resilient pathogen are often associated with significant morbidity and mortality, and the rise in antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has further compounded treatment difficulties. The dynamics of antimicrobial resistance in microbes are not static19, and the continuous exposure of bacteria such as P. aeruginosa to selective pressures, such as widespread antibiotic use, can lead to the emergence of new resistance mechanisms. Investigating the genomic evolution of clinical isolates could offers valuable insight into emerging resistance mechanisms and the shifting landscape of virulence20. Among the mechanisms contributing to the pathogenicity and drug resistance of P. aeruginosa, quorum sensing (QS) plays a pivotal role in regulating virulence factor production, biofilm formation, and antibiotic resistance, targeting MvfR is an attractive strategy for disrupting QS and potentially mitigating the virulence and resistance of P. aeruginosa.

The results obtained from the analysis showcase diverse antimicrobial resistance (AMR) determinants and virulence-associated genes, notably, the genome harbored multiple genes encoding antibiotic-inactivating enzymes, including those from the APH(3’)-II/APH(3’)-XV, CatB, OXA-50, and PDC families, known to mediate resistance through enzymatic degradation or modification of antibiotic molecules21. These findings are consistent with previous reports of OXA-50 in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, where the enzyme was implicated in carbapenem resistance22, as well as with studies documenting the diversity and prevalence of PDC β-lactamases in P. aeruginosa23.

This enzymatic resistance mechanism allows the organism to neutralize commonly used antibiotics, enhancing its survival in clinical environments subjected to antimicrobial pressure24. Moreover, genetic determinants associated with antibiotic target modification were detected, such genes were Alr, Ddl, dxr, EF-G, EF-Tu, folA, Dfr, folP, gyrA, gyrB, inhA, fabI, iso-tRNA, kasA, MurA, rho, rpoB, rpoC, S10p, and S12p. These genetic elements alter antibiotic targets, reducing drug binding efficiency and conferring resistance25,26, In parallel, genes like FabG, fabV, and HtdX suggest alternative metabolic bypass pathways (target replacement), further compounding the organism’s resistance profile.

Efflux-mediated resistance was also prominent, with the identification of multiple efflux pumps, including EmrAB-OMF, MacAB, and members of the Mex family (MexAB-OprM, MexEF-OprN, MexHI-OpmD). This system has also been detected in a previous study as a key factor in resistance development of Pseudomonas aeruginosa27. These multidrug transporters are known for their ability to expel a wide range of antibiotics, reducing intracellular concentrations below therapeutic thresholds15, among these, the Mex systems are particularly notorious for their broad substrate specificity and clinical relevance in resistance to fluoroquinolones, β-lactams, and aminoglycosides28.

In addition to these primary resistance mechanisms, mutations or deletions in regulatory and structural genes that further enhance resistance were observed from the study, for example, the absence of gidB, a gene involved in methylation of 16 S rRNA, has been implicated in increased resistance to aminoglycosides29,. Likewise, alterations in genes modulating cell envelope permeability (Occ/Opr family proteins) and cell surface charge (GdpD, PgsA) reduce drug uptake or binding, creating a formidable barrier against antimicrobial peptides30. Further, OxyR observed is a link between oxidative stress responses and the modulation of AMR determinants, further emphasizing the genome’s complexity. The multifaceted resistance profile of genomes analyzed highlights the importance of genetic determinants that bolster its survival in hostile environments, the presence of multiple AMR genes contributes to the increase in resistance in recent years, the organism’s potential to withstand various antimicrobial interventions, posing challenges for clinical management. Furthermore, to achieve a more comprehensive understanding, future studies should incorporate pan-genome analyses and stratified assemblies across representative isolates over the use of consensus assembly. This would enable robust characterization of accessory genome diversity and its functional implications in resistance evolution and clinical outcomes.

Among the virulence-associated targets and genes identified, MvfR, a key transcriptional regulator of quorum sensing and biofilm formation, was prioritized based on its functional relevance31. AI modelling of MvfR identified from the genome analysis was performed using AlphaFold2, followed by molecular docking, phylogenetic comparisons, ADMET-profiling, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. According to results obtained from docking against datasets from database query, Compounds in Group A exhibited consistently strong interactions with MvfR, as reflected in their docking scores and binding free energies. Notably, A1 (ZINC93351388) recorded the most favorable docking score indicating a robust and stable ligand–receptor interaction. The consistent binding affinities across this group suggest a coherent pharmacophoric pattern potentially relevant to MvfR inhibition. Group B compounds followed closely in performance, with B1 (ZINC64683340) and B2 (ZINC64683508) showing docking scores comparable to Group A’s top performers. Their predicted binding strengths highlight chemical scaffolds that warrant further in vitro validation, especially given their close clustering of scores within the high-affinity range. Group C presented a narrower docking score distribution with generally weaker predicted affinities. C10 (ZINC77270903) emerged as the most promising within this group, suggesting that despite the overall trend, individual structural variations may enhance binding. However, the relatively shallow docking profile of this group limits its prioritization. In Group D, only D1 (ZINC15685828) approached the binding performance of compounds from Groups A and B, the remaining compounds exhibited moderate to weak predicted affinities. This variation implies lower binding efficiency within the chemical space sampled for this group.

The interaction analysis among the top performing compounds from each group revealed that the ligand forms stable complexes through hydrogen bonding, electrostatic contacts, hydrophobic interactions, and aromatic stacking. A1 formed hydrogen bonds with Ala190, Asn191, Glu151, and Tyr192, along with a pi-cation interaction with LYS266. Hydrophobic contacts involved Phe217, Val218, and Tyr165. Negatively charged residues including Asp150, Glu151, and Glu152 were involved in electrostatic interactions, and polar residues such as Thr265 and Asn191 contributed additional contacts, a halogen bond involving a fluorine atom on the ligand and indications of hydration sites were also noted. Electrostatic interactions, including hydrogen bonds and salt bridges, are crucial for protein structure, folding, and ligand binding32. A halogen bond involving fluorine on the ligand adds further stability, and hydration sites indicate potential solvent interactions. These interactions collectively ensure a well-defined and strong binding of the ligand within the protein’s active site33. In the B1 complex, hydrogen bonds were observed with Glu151, Glu152, Lys266, Arg193, and Asp150. Pi-pi stacking interactions occurred with Tyr192, and some of the hydrophobic contacts noticed included Ile149, Val218, and Phe217. Previous study has shown that these hydrophobic interactions contribute to the overall binding energy, indicating their general importance in stabilizing ligand-receptor complexes34. For the C1 ligand, hydrogen bonds were observed with Glu151, Tyr192, Asn191, Tyr268, and Ala190, and pi-pi stacking with Tyr192 was present. Hydrophobic interactions were observed with Ala187, Ile186, and Leu257. Halogen bonding and metal coordination interactions were also identified. The π–π stacking interactions observed are also known to contribute to the rigidity and specificity of the complex, aromatic stacking provides a stable, non-covalent interaction that further stabilizes the ligand binding in a precise orientation35. Furthermore, in the D1 complex, hydrogen bonding involved Tyr192, Lys266, and Glu151, salt bridge were also observed with Asp264, also, hydrophobic contacts were observed with Ile149, Ile263, Ala190, Tyr268and Phe217, salt bridges are particularly crucial in maintaining the structural integrity of the ligand-receptor complex under physiological conditions36.

The scaffold-level analysis of the top-ranked ligands shows that amide was the most frequently represented motif, followed by imidazole, phenyl, urea, benzimidazole, and pyridine, indicating that amide-bearing or its linker and heterocyclic frameworks dominate the preferred chemical space of the binding pocket. Nevertheless, frequency did not translate directly into superior docking performance. Although amide occurred most often, its mean docking score was less favorable than those of purine, imidazole, urea, and benzimidazole, whereas the single cyclohexanol-containing ligand produced the most favorable score overall. Some of these motifs identified in this study have previously been reported as inhibitors of the receptor. For example, the leading MvfR inhibitor M64 is a benzamide–benzimidazole derivative reported37. Similarly, another study demonstrated that imidazole-based compounds can also inhibit this receptor38. This result suggests that commonly occurring scaffolds may provide a broadly compatible structural basis for binding, but optimal docking depends on the specific way in which each motif is incorporated into the overall ligand architecture.

The interaction analysis provides a mechanistic explanation for the stronger-performing scaffold classes. Across the top compounds, hydrogen bonding was the dominant interaction mode, with amide, urea, imidazole, benzimidazole, and pyridine showing repeated engagement with the principal hotspot residues GLU151, GLU152, TYR192, and LYS266. Motifs with better average docking performance were not only capable of extensive hydrogen-bonding but also benefited from complementary electrostatic and aromatic contacts. In particular, imidazole-, benzimidazole-, phenyl-, and indole-containing ligands formed pi-stacking or pi-cation interactions with TYR192, TYR268, PHE217, and LYS266, thereby enhancing binding stabilization within the pocket. Further, some of this amino acid residues identified in this study have also been reported in previous investigations of MvfR. For example, interaction with Glu151 was reported in a study investigating curcumin analogs as potential wound-healing agents targeting MvfR39. Similarly, interactions involving Lys266 have been documented in molecular modeling studies of micro- and nanocurcumin with in vitro and in vivo antibacterial validation40.

Based on the data, the ADME analysis shows that the molecules from Groups A and B have better pharmacokinetic features overall. They exhibit high gastrointestinal absorption, almost the same bioavailability scores (≈ 0.55), and no violations of the main drug-likeness rules, which reflect good oral suitability and give a rationale for their high docking scores. Also, their relatively good solubility and proper polarity may help them to make a stable fit in the binding pocket through a mix of hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. On the other hand, the molecules from Group C have a less favorable pharmacokinetic profile. Although they do not have major structural problems, these compounds have low gastrointestinal absorption, many drug-likeness rule violations, and a lower predicted bioavailability, all of which might decrease their usefulness in practice and point towards a need for structure modification. The compounds from Group D show a mix of features, with some of the substances having a decent GI absorption and bioavailability, while the infringement of the rules and uneven solubility point to less consistent drug-like characteristics. Altogether, the best results of Groups A and B seem to be related to properly balanced physicochemical properties such as appropriate molecular size, polarity, and capability of interactions that could support effective docking and possible biological activity.

In the context of rational drug design, it is essential to not only evaluate the binding affinity of potential ligands but also to consider the evolutionary aspects of the target proteins, This comparison helps in understanding whether the protein’s structural integrity and biological activity have been conserved or if evolutionary changes have led to significant modifications in binding site, and also to evaluate the consistency of docking results across different protein variants, which is critical for the design of broadly effective therapeutics. The observed phylogenetic variations, although minor, may have functional implications in species-specific virulence and regulatory pathways. The moderate bootstrap confidence with 6Q7V suggests that the modelled MvfR maintains a structurally conserved core despite evolutionary divergence.

The molecular dynamics simulation results offer detailed insight into the conformational stability and structural dynamics of the ligand–MvfR complexes41. All RMSD values remain within an acceptable range throughout the simulation, and also in harmony with previous study by with observed average fluctuation for M64(antagonist) to be 2.2 ± 0.2 5, Among the four complexes, B1–MvfR complex displayed early stabilization followed by a notable deviation and recovery, indicating an initial conformational adjustment before achieving equilibrium, this pattern, coupled with its relatively low average RMSD values in the early phase, suggests strong early-stage binding, albeit with subsequent transient flexibility. Moreso, the A1–MvfR complex maintained a steady and gradual RMSD increase, which is indicative of a progressive stabilization process and consistent structural integrity throughout the simulation.

The C1– and D1–MvfR complexes exhibited moderate to higher RMSD fluctuations, including prominent peaks during the mid-to-late simulation stage, these observations imply a degree of conformational flexibility or local rearrangement within the binding site, potentially reflecting weaker or transient interactions between ligand and receptor compared to B1 and A1. Notably, the D1–MvfR complex, despite its broader RMSD range, ultimately achieved stable convergence, suggesting that the complex was able to adapt structurally to accommodate the ligand within the simulation timeframe.

When considered alongside molecular docking scores, binding energy estimations, and ADMET property predictions, these findings support the potential of these ligands as promising candidates for targeting the MvfR.

Despite the comprehensive computational framework employed in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. Some of which are expanding the genomic dataset to include a wider range of isolates obtained from diverse geographical regions and clinical sources would provide a more comprehensive perspective on the evolutionary processes shaping antimicrobial resistance and virulence in Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Such broader sampling would enable more robust characterization of strain level variation and improve understanding of how resistance determinants and virulence associated genes evolve across different epidemiological contexts. In addition, future studies should strengthen the molecular simulation framework by incorporating ligand focused analytical metrics and experimental studies.

Materials and methods

Resistance gene identification

WGS data (FASTQ) of clinical Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates was retrieved from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) at www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov maintained by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). Using NCBI API access, the genomes were retrieved from clinical samples, and then filtered and clipped45. Quality control was performed to exclude adaptor sequences and low-quality reads in order to increase the precision and dependability of subsequent studies.

The assembled contigs were used as input for ResFinder 4.6 46. To analyze chromosomal point mutations, the threshold for percentage identity (%ID) was set at 90%, ensuring that only mutations with high sequence similarity to the reference genome were considered. A minimum alignment length of 60% was applied to enhance the accuracy and relevance of mutation detection. Unknown mutations were included in the analysis to explore potentially novel genetic variations. To maintain focus on functionally significant changes, premature stop codons and frameshift indels were excluded, as they may introduce noise in downstream interpretation. For the identification of acquired antimicrobial resistance genes, a similar threshold of 90% identification was established, complemented by a 60% minimum length criterion, to reliably identify resistance-conferring genetic elements. Additionally, species selection was set to “Other,” enabling the analysis to accommodate non-standard or less-studied organisms. The input data type was specified to match the format of the genomic dataset, ensuring seamless integration into the analytical pipeline. This approach provides a robust framework for uncovering meaningful genetic variations and resistance markers, supporting in-depth research and clinical applications.

Genome analysis

The genomes were assembled using Unicycler. The assembled genomes were annotated using the RAST tool kit (RASTtk) using PATRIC pipeline47,48. Functional categorization of genes into biological processes or structural complexes was conducted. Key subsystems included metabolism, protein processing, stress response, energy production, membrane transport, DNA processing, cellular processes, RNA processing, and cell envelope formation. Homology searches identified genes related to transporters, virulence factors, drug targets, and antimicrobial resistance49. These were mapped to the databases CARD, NDARO, DrugBank, TTD, TCDB, and VFDB50,51. Resistance-related genes were detected using PATRIC’s curated k-mer-based method. Circular genome plots and subsystem distribution Figures were generated to visually represent the annotations and subsystem categorizations. PATRIC also provides reference and representative genomes, and includes them in the phylogenetic analysis that is part of the Comprehensive Genome Analysis report. The closest reference and representative genomes were identified by Mash/MinHash52.

Proteins were mapped to Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers, Gene Ontology (GO) terms, and KEGG pathways53. Mechanisms identified included antibiotic inactivation enzymes, efflux pumps, permeability modifications, and resistance gene regulators. Predicted antimicrobial resistance phenotypes were determined using AdaBoost machine learning classifiers54. Protein sequences from closest reference and representative genomes were aligned with MUSCLE55. The joint set of amino acid and nucleotide alignments was concatenated into a data matrix, and RaxML was used to analyze this matrix, with fast bootstrapping used to generate the support values in the tree19.

Ligand acquisition using structure-based pharmacophore

Using the structure-based pharmacophore strategy specific for a ligand and a receptor, fluoroquinolones (group A), cephalosporins (group B), aminoglycosides (group C), and levofloxacin (group D) were screened against Modelled MvfR. Ten compounds from each group were downloaded from PubChem, a generalized pharmacophore for each group (Table S1) was generated from PharmaGist (https://bioinfo3d.cs.tau.ac.il/PharmaGist/php.php), with focus more on the pharmacophores rather than functional groups. Four pharmacophores were randomly selected based on their ranking for each group56. Afterwards, the generated pharmacophore was filtered and used as a query search on the ZINCPharmer database (http://zincpharmer.csb.pitt.edu)57. A total of 1,026 fluoroquinolones (group A), 1,852 cephalosporins (group B), 712 aminoglycosides (group C), and 3,609 levofloxacin (group D) were obtained from the search, and the presence of the compounds used for the pharmacophore building served as a validation step for the analysis. Query data can be located in the associated github repository.

Protein structure preparation

The amino acid sequence of MvfR translated from the WGSs was used for protein structure prediction using the AlphaFold2 58, and uploaded in FASTA format. Multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) were generated automatically by the notebook using built-in tools, including JackHMMER, to query sequence databases. No homologous sequences or structural templates were identified in the Protein Data Bank (PDB)59. AlphaFold utilized the MSA data and its deep learning-based approach to predict the three-dimensional structure de novo. Five models were generated, each scored and ranked based on their predicted TM-scores and Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) confidence scores. The top-ranked model, representing the most reliable prediction, was selected for further analysis. The predicted structure was visualized and analyzed using PyMOL and UCSF Chimera60,61. Structural features, including secondary structure elements, domains, and potential functional sites, were annotated using bioinformatics tools. The reliability of the model was assessed by examining pLDDT scores and functional annotation.

Molecular docking and visualization

Protein preparation

The 3D model of the predicted MvfR structure was prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard in Maestro 14.1 62. Protein was prepared by adding the hydrogen atoms, optimizing hydrogen bonds, removing atomic clashes, adding formal charges to the hetero groups and then optimizing at neutral pH63. Finally, the structure was minimized using optimized potential for liquid simulations (OPLS-2005) force field40.

Binding pocket prediction

The DeepSite algorithm has been used for the prediction and identification of the various binding sites of the protein while the Computed Atlas of Surface Topography of proteins (CASTp)64 had been utilized to depict further information on the binding pocket’s structure (Fig. 11), such as its volume and surface area, after which the results were compared to sitemap analysis of Schrodinger.

Ligand and library preparation

Ligands were obtained in the structural data file (SDF) format from the database. The molecules were subjected to ligand preparation using Ligand Prep module of Maestro 14.1 of Schrodinger suite (Schrodinger)62. The ligand preparation process of molecules involves preserving the definite chiralities, to generate minimum five low-energy stereoisomers per ligand, using default conditions at pH 7.0 ± 2.0. The resulting ligands are subjected to high throughput virtual screening using the GLIDE (Grid based Ligand and Docking with Energetics) module of Schrodinger suite65.

Protein, ligand and grid preparation

The protein structure was prepared using the Maestro suite (Schrödinger)62, the structure preprocessing involved filling missing side chains using default templates in Maestro, optimizing hydrogen bond assignments to address overlapping hydrogens, and capping termini where required, after which Restraint-based energy minimization was performed to optimize the protein structure, while crystallographic waters were optionally removed to enhance ligand docking. Metal-binding states were generated using the ionizer module to include relevant coordination geometries, Additionally, stereoisomers, tautomers, and protonation states were generated, with all possible combinations sampled up to 32 per ligand, the prepared protein structure was validated and saved in Maestro format for subsequent receptor grid generation66. Receptor grid generation was performed using the Glide module in Maestro14.1, the binding site was defined by specifying key residues from the PDB structure, Van der Waals radii adjustments were applied to enhance receptor flexibility, Constraints for metal coordination geometries and excluded volumes were incorporated into the receptor grid to reflect realistic binding interaction66. Ligands were prepared using the LigPrep module, structures were optimized to generate stereoisomers, tautomers, and desalted forms, followed by conformational sampling was performed to account for flexibility, and outputs were saved in both Maestro and SDF formats for subsequent docking54,56.

Molecular Docking

The docking process was conducted using a three-tiered approach in Glide68, High-Throughput Virtual Screening (HTVS) was used as the initial step to rapidly filter ligands and eliminate low-scoring conformers. Standard Precision (SP) docking was then applied to refine the binding poses of filtered ligands69,70 Finally, Extra Precision (XP) docking provided rigorous docking and ranking of the highest-scoring ligands to identify optimal binding conformations. Sampling options included flexible ligand docking, nitrogen inversion sampling, and ring conformation sampling, with macrocyclic ligands analysed using Prime. Non-planar amides were penalized to improve conformational accuracy, Docked ligands were evaluated based on the Glide (G) Score, which considers binding energy, intramolecular hydrogen bonding, excluded volume penalties, and amide planarity59,61.

Docking interaction

Protein–ligand interaction results were obtained from the top-ranked docking poses for each compound across Groups A–D (total of 40 complexes). For each docked complex, the protein–ligand PDB structure was visualized to extract protein and ligand atom coordinates. Each contact was classified by residue identity, ligand atom or functional group, and interaction type (hydrogen bond, electrostatic, pi-cation, pi-stacking, hydrophobic, halogen bond, or related types). Each record included the protein residue, position, interaction type, exact ligand atom or group, ligand structural part, and a concise interaction description. Additional structural moieties visible in the 2D interaction diagrams but not forming direct bonds were recorded separately. The resulting interaction datasets were organised by group (groupA, groupB, groupC, groupD). Residue-contact events were tabulated by interaction class to identify dominant amino-acid hotspots and scaffold-dependent binding behaviour across the evaluated compounds. The data were deposited to the associated github page.

ADMET analysis

To evaluate the possible inhibitors’ drug-like qualities, ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) analysis will be performed19. ADMET analysis will be performed using software like SwissADME and ADMETlab71,72. For each variable, the compliance percentage among the top 10 compounds in Groups A–D was calculated as the proportion of compounds meeting the specified criterion within that group and expressed as a percentage.

Structural motifs analysis

Structural motif analysis of the top-ranked ligands was performed on docking and interaction datasets for Groups A–D with Python 3.13.2. Scaffold/motifs families were assigned through structural annotations and motif-pattern matching, with compounds containing multiple motifs contributing to multiple motif categories. Ligands in each group were ranked by docking score, and the top five compounds were defined as the high-performing subset. Motif occurrence was summarized as frequency within this subset, total frequency across the dataset, and proportion relative to the 40 compounds evaluated across Groups A–D. Docking performance for each motif class was described using the mean docking score and the best observed docking score. Interaction profiling aggregated residue contacts by interaction type, including hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions, π-cation interactions, π-stacking interactions, hydrophobic contacts, and halogen bonds. Residue frequencies were then summarized for each motif family to identify dominant amino-acid hotspots associated with motif-dependent binding.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the protein-ligand complexes were performed using GROMACS 2021.1-MODIFIED73 on the GPU-enabled UNSW Katana platform. The system was prepared using CHARMM-GUI74,75, and input files were exported in GROMACS format. Protein coordinates were initialized from the `.gro` files, and interactions were defined through topology files generated with the CHARMM36m force field for the protein and CGenFF-derived parameters for the ligands. The complexes were solvated in a rectangular TIP3P water box with a 10 Å buffer around the protein, and periodic boundary conditions were applied using a cubic unit cell. The systems were neutralized and ionized with K⁺ and Cl⁻ to a concentration of 0.15 M KCl using the Monte Carlo ion placement option in CHARMM-GUI. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm for 5000 steps with positional restraints applied on protein heavy atoms (force constants of 400 kJ·mol⁻¹·nm⁻² for backbone and 40 kJ·mol⁻¹·nm⁻² for side chains). Van der Waals and short-range Coulomb interactions were treated with a 1.2 nm cutoff and force-switching for van der Waals terms, while long-range electrostatics were handled using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method. Constraints were applied to hydrogen bonds using the LINCS algorithm. An NVT equilibration was carried out for 5 ns using a 1 fs timestep at 303.15 K, employing a velocity-rescale thermostat with separate temperature coupling groups for solute and solvent and maintaining the same positional restraints on protein heavy atoms. Production MD simulations were then performed in the NPT ensemble at 303.15 K and 1 bar using a 2 fs timestep for a total simulation time of 500 ns, with a velocity-rescale thermostat and a C-rescale barostat with isotropic pressure coupling. Production MD simulations were run in the NPT ensemble for 500 ns using a 2 fs timestep. To analyze the computed trajectories, we used the Mdanalysis package to calculate essential parameters such as the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), residue area (RESArea) and root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the protein-ligand complexes76.

Density functional theory analysis

The top compounds under study were subjected to quantum chemical calculations using density functional theory (DFT) to evaluate their electronic structure and reactivity properties. The procedure was carried out using ORCA 5.0 software77, following standard protocols for geometry optimization and frequency analysis to ensure true minima on the potential energy surface. Geometry optimizations were performed using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) generalized gradient approximation (GGA)78 functional without symmetry constraints. All energy values in Hartree units were converted to electronvolts using the standard conversion factor 1 Hartree = 27.2114 eV.

Conclusion

Making helpful predictions utilizing various tools that could be obtained through molecular modelling is one of the primary goals of computational medicinal chemistry and a way to make positive contribution to the amidst the alarming rise in AMR in our society today. This fact is crucial when putting strategies into practice for drug development projects or protocols because of the various tasks and challenges involved in finding the right hit compound. Advances in computational power combined with imaging methods, artificial intelligence strategies, and protein fold prediction tools, suggest a golden age for the application of the computational approaches to drug discovery, contributing to the development of targeted and selective new therapeutics while also lowering the expenses of testing.

We investigated the variation between MvfR identified and its corresponding homologs for MvfR in publish database. The results show a slight variation in the bootstrap confidence of the modelled MvfR from genomics analysis and those identified in the past, this slight variation may influence on disease expression of the newly isolated species. The present study screened a large dataset of compounds using multimodal computational approach to identify a promising candidate. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to identify 1-[2-(difluoromethyl)-1 H-benzimidazol-5-yl]-3-[(2 S)-2-hydroxy-2-(4-pyridyl)ethyl]urea (ZINC93351388 or A1) emerged as a potential inhibitor of MvfR.

Molecular dynamics simulations further revealed the dynamic behavior of four MvfR–ligand complexes. While all complexes achieve equilibrium, B1-MvfR and A1-MvfR exhibit the most consistent stability, whereas C1-MvfR and D1-MvfR undergo transient fluctuations before stabilizing. The RoG and RMSF analyses further highlight structural compactness and flexibility. These findings provide valuable insights into the dynamic behaviour of MvfR complexes, which may guide further structural and functional studies. Furthermore, the quantum chemical calculations reinforce the results from molecular docking, MD simulations, and DFT by confirming the favorable stability and reactivity balance of A1 in particular. Hence, this study provides a foundation for future research in drug discovery, particularly in the field of antimicrobial and antibiotic resistance, which remains a major global healthcare concern. It offers an accelerated approach to identifying effective candidates against key components of multidrug-resistant bacteria.
