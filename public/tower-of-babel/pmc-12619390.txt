Polystyrene and low-density polyethylene degradation by Bacillus cereus L1 isolated from plastic contaminated wastewater
Authors: Syeda Saira Iqbal, Yasir Rehman, Muhammad Afzaal, Sikandar Ali, Sami A Al-Hussain, Rab Nawaz, Iqra Nasim, Ali Irfan, Magdi E A Zaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe environmental disruption caused by enduring pollution has been aggravated by the excessive use of low-density polyethylene (LDPE) and polystyrene (PS) plastics due to their non-biodegradable composition. Microbial bioremediation could be an environmentally friendly approach to degrade these pollutants. In this research, plastic-degrading bacteria were isolated from plastic-polluted wastewater collected from a wastewater drain.MethodologyFor this study, enrichment culture techniques were employed to isolate plastic-degrading bacteria from wastewater contaminated with plastic. Characterization was performed on the purified isolate with the highest efficiency. The bacteria utilizing PS and LDPE as the sole carbon sources were purified and characterized (FTIR, SEM) based on morphology, antibiotic resistance profile, and phylogenetic analysis through 16 S rRNA gene sequencing.ResultsThe bacteria showing the most significant PS and LDPE degradation were identified as B. cereus L1. The strain also exhibited biofilm-forming ability, confirmed by a qualitative crystal violet ring test, a pivotal factor in plastic breakdown. B. cereus L1 reduced the weight of LDPE beads from 1.08 g to 0.87 g and from 1.15 g to 0.92 g in four weeks. Fourier Transform Infrared Radiation (FTIR) spectroscopy revealed changes in the structural configuration of PS and LDPE after incubation with B. cereus L1, as distinct peaks at 2196 cm⁻¹ for alkynes, nitriles, and 1715 cm⁻¹ for carbonyl groups were observed, indicating the degradation of the polymers.ConclusionThis work highlights the potential of B. cereus L1 in bioremediation strategies for managing plastic waste, offering a sustainable and eco-friendly alternative for combating plastic pollution in both terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems.Keywords: Polystyrene, Low-Density polyethylene, Bacillus, Bioremediation, Plastic degradation

Background

The world’s population has grown exponentially, resulting in increased industrial activity and the production of substantial amounts of plastic waste. Plastic is a durable, man-made material that persists in the environment and poses ecological risks [1]. The lack of waste management options and inadequate waste legislation have hindered the implementation of a sustainable solid waste management system, particularly for plastic waste [2]. Durability and reduced cost make petroleum products preferable to glass and paper [3]. Plastic has increased 24 times from 1964 to 2020 [4]. In two decades, this will quadruple if it continues. Incineration of 250 MT of plastic garbage annually releases 400 metric tons (MT) of CO2, which increases global warming [3]. Plastic fragmentation and leaching may create a future peak release of harmful compounds, including nano plastics and chemical additives [5]. Nanoplastics can impair endocrine function and quantity, affect metabolic and reproductive health, and bind to biological targets. There is growing evidence that these polymers can infiltrate cells [6]. Plastic waste pollution is one of the most harmful threats to human health and the environment, and it must be prioritized, especially in developing countries. Pakistan’s air and water pollution are mainly caused by plastic pollution, and urbanization, industrialization, and population growth will exacerbate this issue [7]. The fifth-largest employment sector in Pakistan, the plastic industry, is growing at a rate of 7–9% per year [7]. Due to their non-biodegradability, plastic bags in landfills clog sewage systems and increase operational costs [8]. The most widely used polymers such as polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polyethylene (PE), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polypropylene PP, polyurethane (PUR), and polystyrene (PS) generate plastic trash [9]. The decomposition of plastics in the natural environment can take anywhere from 20 to 100 years, or even 500 years. Polystyrene (Styrofoam) and plastic bags may disintegrate over 1000 years, potentially causing soil infertility [10]. Many land and aquatic animal species may readily absorb this, causing respiratory and digestive system issues, as well as harmful bioaccumulation along the food chain. A large amount of plastic waste is being ingested by aquatic species, particularly fish, which ultimately leads to the bioaccumulation of plastic. It is estimated that by 2050, 99% of seabirds may ingest plastic, a concerning situation [10]. The rate at which plastic breaks down depend on various factors, including crystallinity, molecular weight, functional groups, additives, and plasticizers. Several physical and chemical processes have made plastic recycling and reuse conceivable, but biodegradation seems to be the most promising and ecologically friendly solution [11]. Plastic waste is compacted by squeezing, crushing, and grinding. Other physical methods like UV and photo-oxidation make plastic flimsier yet produce harmful byproducts [12, 13].

Temperature (which may exceed 45 °C, depending on the plastic), pH (which can range from 3 to 8, depending on the plastic), and UV/infrared light (which can induce surface fractures and regulate fragmentation) all impact the physicochemical breakdown of plastics. Chemical breakdown involves the dissociation of molecular connections, although there are restrictions. However, it will be costly due to the use of expensive chemicals. Second, degrading chemical disposal would be the biggest challenge [14]. Plastic polymers degrade into monomers and produce CO2, CH4, and H2O. Biodegradation primarily involves microorganisms that break down plastics through their enzymatic activities. This mechanism is essential for plastic biodegradation [15].

The illustration depicts the microbial colonization, enzymatic cleavage, and partial degradation of PS and LDPE. Although this study focused on intermediate stages, the ultimate pathways of mineralization are to the gases CO₂, CH₄, and H₂O.

To date, biological plastic-degrading technologies have proven effective and eco-friendly. These microbial enzymes have also been cloned and sequenced. Esterases, lipases, and cutinases can hydrolyze polyethylene adipate (PEA) and polycaprolactone (PCL) [16]. The molecular weight of plastic decreases when microbial colonies develop on it. Mineralization, assimilation, biodeterioration, and fragmentation are microbe-mediated biochemical degradation strategies [14]. However, these are time-consuming processes, and only a few microorganisms are known to break down such polymers [17].

Methods

Aim and design

This investigation sought to isolate native bacteria from wastewater that degrade plastics and characterize their abilities against Polystyrene (PS) and Low-Density Polyethylene (LDPE) plastics. The experimental design encompassed enrichment culture methods, morphological and biochemical analysis, biofilm production, degradation analysis via spectroscopy and microscopy, and molecular identification of key variants. There are limited studies documenting biofilm-producing plastic degrading Bacillus species from Pakistan, thus making this isolate novel. The rationale is supported by recent reports documenting microbial and enzymatic degradation [11, 18].

Polystyrene powder (PS) and low-density polyethylene (LDPE) granules

Polystyrene (PS) ((C8H8) n) 1 kg and Low-density polyethylene (LDPE) (C2H4) 1 kg granules were obtained from Muntajat Qatar Petrochemical Company (QAPCO). Both LDPE and PS granules were irradiated and treated for one hour with UV irradiation (0.00 M/s UV 63 M) in a biological safety cabinet to enhance susceptibility to microbial degradation.

Sampling

For screening the plastic-utilizing bacteria, random wastewater samples were collected from a drain located in Johar Town, Lahore (31.452060 N, 74.286830 E) (Fig. 1-A), where plastic trash has been dumped for many years (Fig. 1-B). The samples were taken in a sterile container and transferred immediately to the laboratory for further processing. The temperature and pH of the samples were recorded on-site.

A Site map of the sampling area, B plastic dumped, wastewater sampling site drain

Isolation of PS and LDPE degrading bacteria

The isolation of plastic-degrading microorganisms was carried out using the enrichment culture technique. Minimal Salt Media (MSM) was chosen to proceed with the experiment because it provides essential salts while forcing the bacteria to use PS/LDPE as the sole carbon source [19]. 60 mL of the wastewater sample was inoculated in 200 mL MSM containing 1 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 0.2 g/L MgSO4, 0.1 g/L NaCl, 0.02 g/L CaCl2, 0.01 g/L FeSO4, 0.0005 g/L NaMoO4, 0.0005 g/L MnSO4, 1.6 g/L K2HPO4 and 0.2 g/L KH2PO4 and 1 mL tween 80 (pH 6.8) [20]. The media was supplemented with either PS or LDPE as the carbon source (1.0 g/L). Both PS and LDPE were pre-treated with UV radiation (0.00 M/s UV 63 M) after washing with 95% ethanol, then dissolved in toluene, and filter-sterilized using a 0.22 μm injection disc filter. Plastics were dried overnight after dissolution to ensure complete evaporation of toluene before bacterial exposure. The inoculated media was incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 120 RPM for two weeks. After the incubation, samples were drawn from the enrichment culture and serially diluted. 50 µL of 10−2 and 10−3 dilutions were plated on MSM agar media containing PS and LDPE as the carbon source, and incubated at 37 °C for 3 days.

Characterization of the isolated bacteria Bacillus cereus

The bacterial isolates were analyzed through Gram staining. The bacteria were also analyzed for their ability to utilize various sugars, including sodium citrate, sodium acetate, sodium lactate, and glucose. Antibiotic sensitivity profiling of the bacteria was also carried out to characterize the isolate’s resistance profile and to ensure biosafety considerations before potential environmental application. The bacteria were inoculated on Muller-Hinton agar (MHA) using sterile cotton swabs, and antibiotic discs, including Erythromycin (15 µg/mL), Neomycin (30 µg/mL), Rifampicin (30 µg/mL), Tetracycline (30 µg/mL), and Vancomycin (30 µg/mL), were dispensed on the inoculated agar surface. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h. The plates were observed for the presence of clear zones around the antibiotic discs [21] following the incubation. Zones were interpreted according to CLSI guidelines, where ≤ 10 mm was resistant and ≥ 15 mm susceptible [22].

Determination of optimum pH and temperature

The extent of bacterial growth was determined at different pH. Flasks of nutrient broth were prepared and maintained at pH 3, 5, 7, and 9. The flasks were inoculated with the bacteria and incubated at 37 °C, 120 RPM for 24 h. Optical density (OD600) of bacterial growth was measured through a spectrophotometer (STA-4300) after incubation.

Similarly, the extent of bacterial growth was also analyzed at different temperatures. Bacteria were inoculated in the nutrient broth media, and the flasks were incubated at 25 °C, 30 °C, and 37 °C for 24 h at 120 RPM shaking (FS-70B). Bacterial growth was measured at OD600 using a spectrophotometer (STA-4300).

Carbon utilization test

The ability of the bacteria to utilize different carbon sources was also analyzed. MSM agar media was prepared, supplemented with sodium citrate, sodium acetate, sodium lactate, or glucose (10 mM each). The plates were inoculated with the bacterial isolates and incubated for 24–48 h at 37 °C [23].

Determination of the biofilm-forming ability of the bacterial isolates

To check the biofilm-forming ability of the bacteria, a qualitative ring test was performed via crystal violet staining [23]. Bacteria were inoculated in sterile 10 mL LB media in test tubes. These tubes were incubated under static conditions at 37 °C for one week. Biofilm formation was evaluated on the 2nd, 4th, and 6th day of incubation. The liquid cultures were decanted, and the test tubes were gently washed with deionized water. The test tubes were air-dried at room temperature and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. The tubes were rewashed with de-ionized water and air-dried. The appearance of a purple ring confirmed the formation of a biofilm.

Screening the most effective PS and LDPE degrading bacterial isolate

The bacterial isolates were cultured in MSM media containing PS or LDPE as the sole carbon source. The flasks were inoculated with the bacterial isolates and incubated at 37 °C, 120 RPM for four weeks. After the incubation, the samples were taken from the flasks and analyzed.

In the case of LDPE beads, the weight of the beads was also measured before and after the incubation, and the difference was recorded. For the accurate measurement of the dry weight of residual LDPE, the LDPE beads were removed from the samples and dried overnight at room temperature before weighing. The percentage of weight loss was determined using the following formula [24].

The degradation of PS and LDPE by the bacteria was also evaluated using FTIR spectroscopy (Shimadzu IR Prestige-21, 400–4000 nm, interval 1.93, datapoints 1739, dimensions 2).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The LDPE beads were removed from the culture medium after four weeks of incubation to observe the bacterial colonization by SEM [25]. The sample consisted of treated and untreated samples with a bacterial strain and an untreated control. Beads were washed with 70% ethanol and then dried for 24 h. Structural, morphological, and surface changes displayed by the polyethylene beads were visualized using Scanning Electron Microscopy (JEOL-JSM6480LV) at 2700 magnification (2a, R1) and 1300 magnification (2b, R2), voltage (10|), spot size [9] and width was 14, as shown in Fig. 6

Genomic DNA isolation

The bacteria were cultured in LB broth for 18 h at 37 °C, 120 RPM shaking. After the incubation, the cells were harvested at 5000 RPM in a centrifuge for 10 min. Genomic DNA was isolated using the GeneJET Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Thermo Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA yield was confirmed through agarose gel electrophoresis. DNA samples were stored at −20 °C for further analysis.

Polymerase chain reaction of 16 S rRNA gene and phylogenetic analysis

16 S rRNA gene of the bacteria was amplified through polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the primers 27 F (5-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3) and 1522R (5-AAGGAGGTGTGCCARCCGCA-3) [26]. The PCR reaction consisted of 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 45 s, annealing at 50 °C for 60 s, and extension at 72 °C for 90 s using an Advanced Primus 96 thermocycler. PCR results were confirmed through agarose gel electrophoresis using a 1-kb ladder. PCR products were sequenced from Macrogen (Seoul, South Korea) using Sanger Sequencing technology. Two sequencing reactions were performed, using forward and reverse primers, to obtain the full length of the gene. The sequences thus obtained were checked for quality and cleaned using Finch TV software. The sequences were classified through the NCBI BLAST tool (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). Nearest homologues were downloaded, and a Neighbor-Joining phylogenetic tree was constructed in MEGA 7.0 [27] using 500 Bootstrap values as a measure of confidence [28].

Results

Isolation and characterization of PS and LDPE degrading bacteria

The temperature and pH of the sampling site were 38 °C and 6.0, respectively. Seven morphologically distinct colonies were selected after plating serial dilutions from the enrichment cultures on MS agar media containing PS and LDPE as carbon sources. The bacteria were purified through quadrant streaking. The Gram staining results showed that all the isolated bacteria were Gram-positive rods and cocci (Fig. 2).

Gram staining of selected isolate L1showing gram-positive rods and cocci

Phylogenetic analysis and identification of the bacteria

Isolate L1 showed the best results (as detailed in the following sections) and was therefore selected for further analysis. 16 S rRNA sequence of the bacterial isolate L1 showed 99.92% similarity with both Bacillus tropicus and Bacillus cereus. However, in the neighbor joining phylogenetic tree of isolate L1 with the top homologues from the BLAST results, the bacteria grouped closely with B. cereus. Therefore, the identity of the bacterial isolate L1 was confirmed as B. cereus (Fig. 3).

The qualitative assay for biofilm formation, the ring test, demonstrated that B. cereus L1 was capable of forming biofilms. After staining with 0.1% crystal violet and incubating, two distinct purple rings appeared on the test tube walls (Fig. 4).

Polyethylene degradation by B. cereus L1

a. Scanning electron microscopy analysis

The scanning electron micrographs showed clear visual evidence of plastic degradation by the bacteria. Bacterial colonization was observed on LDPE beads. The bacterial-treated LDPE bead surfaces had cracks and pits, which were absent in controls. The LDPE beads’ previously smooth and unbroken surface had entirely degraded, indicating considerable microbial activity and polymer degradation (Fig. 5). The presence of these structural changes substantially supports the bacterial strains’ ability to degrade LDPE, suggesting their potential use in bioremediation efforts to reduce plastic pollution.

c. Fourier transform infrared radiation (FTIR) analysis

FTIR analysis was performed to determine the degradation of PS and LDPE by the bacterial isolates. The obtained FTIR spectrum for treated PS reveals distinct peaks at 894.97, 1051.205, and 2196 cm⁻¹ which do not appear in the control sample. These peaks are associated with the C–H bonds of aromatic rings, C–O stretching or ring deformation, and C ≡ N or C ≡ C groups which could mean frameless Nitriles or Alkynes. This supports that strain L1 indeed caused some modification and degradation of PS (Fig. 7).

FTIR analysis of PS degradation by B. cereus L1

Equally, the FTIR analysis showed marked structural alterations in LDPE as a result of the treatment done with the L1 strain, indicative of biodegradation. Both the treated and control samples showed the characteristic peaks of LDPE, however, a new distinct peak appeared around 1715 cm⁻¹ in the treated sample which corresponds to the carbonyl C = O bond. This peak was not present in the control sample suggesting that L1 caused oxidation of the polymer chain, resulting in the cleavage to form either aldehydes, ketones, or even carboxylic acids (Fig. 8). All these observations confirm prior works which state that microbial degradation of polystyrene yields corresponding new peaks of oxidized and nitrogenous functional groups. This supports the claim that B. cereus L1 has the ability to degrade plastics (Table. 6).

Discussion

The significant increase in plastic manufacturing worldwide has resulted in a large portion being disposed of in landfills, burned, or directly released into the environment, with serious repercussions. The aim of this research was to isolate and characterize plastic-degrading bacteria from indigenous sites and to determine their ability to degrade PS and LDPE. Such bacteria could be augmented at plastic disposal sites for bioremediation purposes. In this study, PS and LDPE were pre-treated with UV to make them susceptible to microbial degradation. Many other researchers have also reported similar approaches like [30] for UV-pretreated LDPE and [31] for Bacillus spp. degrading polupropylene.

Research literature emphasizes the significance of MSM medium for culturing plastic-degrading bacteria [32, 33]. Similarly [34], used MSM broth containing plastic powder for culturing bacteria derived from landfill soil, which caused a significant decrease in the mass of low-density polyethylene (LDPE). Moreover, Mahdiyah and Mukti [33] also reported the use of MSM media for isolating plastic-degrading bacteria with the ability to degrade plastic by up to 17%. Zurier and Goddard [35] utilized MSM media in Winogradsky columns to investigate native bacteria capable of effectively degrading synthetic materials, such as plastics.

B. cereus L1 was able to grow at a variety of temperatures and pH levels. This demonstrates the bacteria’s ability to grow under various environmental conditions. The bacteria also utilized different sugars to increase, indicating their ability to survive using various carbon sources. These attributes enable the strains to adapt ecologically and survive in different habitats. This can also be helpful for their practical application in bioremediation.

The qualitative ring tests in our study showed bacterial biofilm development, which can enhance the plastic degradation by the bacteria. This can improve the degradation of polystyrene (PS) and low-density polyethylene (LDPE) in aquatic and terrestrial environments. Many researchers have focused on biofilm development as one of the processes in the biological transformation of microbial plastics. Zurier and Goddard [35] have pointed out that biofilm development is one of the commonly used methods for breaking down plastics, thus highlighting the significance of biofilms. Moreover, a study by Tu, Chen [36] on plastic degradation in marine environments reported an acceleration in plastic degradation through biofilm-mediated processes. Several studies [37, 38] provide more information about the complex relationship between plastic breakdown and bacterial layers. In one report, Syranidou, Karkanorachaki [37] created a bacterial layer on natural, worn-down polyethylene (PE) and then applied additional PE to it. The results showed that the bacterial layer had a significant effect on the appearance and features of PE. In the same way, Lobelle and Cunliffe [38] examined the initial growth of bacterial layers on PE over three weeks and observed the layers increasing in size.

B. cereus L1 was identified as the best bacterium to degrade PS and LDPE in this study. Singh, Gilcrease [31] demonstrated that Bacillus sp. can degrade various types of plastics, including polypropylene (PP). Tareen, Saeed [39] found that plastic beads break down more quickly because bacteria can adhere to them.

In FTIR analysis, evident signs of changes in the PS and LDPE chemical structures were observed after incubation with B. cereus L1. Research indicates that microbes PS’s cleavage processes changes FTIR spectra in the range of 2100–2300 cm⁻¹, attributable to the appearance of new absorption bands either alkyne or nitrile groups resulting from oxidative depolymerization [40, 41]. The emergence of new functional groups, especially carbonyls, indicates significant polymer degradation. For example, Restrepo-Flórez, Bassi and Thompson [42] reported the presence of carbonyl groups in LDPE at 1700–1750 cm⁻¹ after undergoing microbial degradation. In parallel, Bertolacci, Goldoni [43] reported the emergence of peaks near 1715 cm⁻¹ in LDPE due to oxidative cleavage. Earlier work by Albertsson [44] demonstrated that carbonyl (C = O) groups, often seen as characteristic of polyethylene degradation, are formed due to UV or biological oxidation, and Shah, Hasan [45] confirmed that biodegraded PE has distinct FTIR peaks near 1715 cm⁻¹ due to carbonyls formed by C–C bond scission.

This can also relate to the weight loss of LDPE beads. The observed weight loss (~ 20%) is comparable to Bacillus isolates reported by Munir, Sipayung [34] and higher than rates reported by Mahdiyah and Mukti [33]. This indicates that B. cereus L1 possibly possesses enzymes capable of degrading and metabolizing PS and LDPE, though specific enzymes were not identified in this study. This also suggests that this strain could be utilized in environmental cleanup campaigns or processes involving the decomposition of plastics. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) confirmed this by showing holes, cracks, and pits on the LDPE bead surfaces, proving the bacteria can break down plastic. Further research is needed to understand better their biodegradation mechanisms and the roles of their enzymes. Nevertheless, this study is limited by the absence of enzyme identification, a relatively short incubation period with no mineralization, and potential abiotic factors such as UV pretreatment contributing to weight loss.

Conclusion

This study isolated and characterized B. cereus L1 from wastewater, showing its ability to degrade PS and LDPE and form biofilms. Such indigenous strains may help mitigate plastic pollution in Pakistan’s aquatic systems. Based on these results and its ability to make biofilms, this bacterium can serve as a potential candidate for the bioremediation of plastic-polluted sites. Future work should identify degradative enzymes and assess performance under field conditions.
