Abstract
We present a simple linear-time algorithm that finds a spanning tree T T of a given 2 2 -edge-connected graph G G such that each vertex v v of T T has degree at most ⌈ deg G ⁡ ( v ) 2 ⌉ + 1 \lceil\frac{\deg_{G}(v)}{2}\rceil+1 .

Problem statement and our result

In 2015, Hasanvand
[
4
]
proved that every
k
-edge-connected graph
G=(V_{G},E_{G})
,
k\geq 1
, has a spanning tree
T
such that for each vertex
v\in V_{T}
, we have

\deg_{T}(v)\leq\left\lceil\frac{\deg_{G}(v)}{k}\right\rceil+1,

and this bound is tight. The particular case for
k=2
had been already solved much earlier, independently, by several authors, see for example
[
1

5
,
6
]
, leading to polynomial-time algorithms for determining a relevant spanning tree, in particular, to the one of (inexplicit)
\mathcal{O}(nm)
-time complexity
[

]
or the one of (explicit)
\mathcal{O}(m^{2})
-time complexity
[
5
]
, where
n=|V_{G}|
and
m=|E_{G}|
.

To the best of our knowledge, the question of the existence of a linear-time algorithm for the
k=2
problem in
2
-edge-connected graphs remains open, and herein, we answer this question affirmatively, that is, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

There exists a
\mathcal{O}(m)
-time algorithm that for any
2
-edge-connected graph
G=(V_{G},E_{G})
finds its spanning tree
T
such that for each vertex
v\in V_{G}
, it holds

\deg_{T}(v)\leq\left\lceil\frac{\deg_{G}(v)}{2}\right\rceil+1.

We emphasize that
Czumaj and Strothmann
[
3
]
proposed an
\mathcal{O}(m+n^{3/2})
-time algorithm for that
problem,
n=|V_{G}|
, but in the class of
2
-connected graphs (a subclass of
2
-edge-connected graphs); its randomized version achieves running time of
\mathcal{O}(m+n\log^{4}n)
, with high probability. Therefore, our algorithm significantly improves their result as well. We note in passing, that for an arbitrary integer
k\geq 3
, Czumaj and Strothmann
[
3
]
also gave an
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\cdot k\cdot\alpha(n,n)\cdot\log n)

Background.

Conceptually, we follow the idea of
[

D

2

Preliminaries

Let
G=(V_{G},E_{G})
be a connected graph. Let
N_{G}(v)
denote the set of neighbors of a vertex
v
in
G
, and let
E_{G}(v)
be the set of edges incident to
v
in
G
.
We say that a list
\mathcal{L}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{l})
is an
edge DFS
if the following conditions hold.

(i)

a_{j}=(u_{j},v_{j})
, where
e_{j}=\{u_{j},v_{j}\}\in E_{G}
for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
.

(ii)

E_{G}=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{l}\}
.

(iii)

Define for any
v\in V_{G}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
,
X_{j}(v)=E_{G}(v)\setminus\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{j}\}
. (Informally, the
X_{j}(v)
is the set of edges incident to
v
and not traversed by the prefix of
\mathcal{L}
of length
j
).
Let
j\in\{1,\ldots,l-1\}
.

•

If
X(v_{j})\neq\emptyset
, then
e_{j+1}\in X(v_{j})
, where
u_{j+1}=v_{j}
.

•

If
X(v_{j})=\emptyset
, then there exists
1\leq j^{\prime}<j
for which
X(v_{j^{\prime}})\neq\emptyset
.
For the maximum such
j^{\prime}
it holds
e_{j+1}\in X(v_{j^{\prime}})
, where
u_{j+1}=v_{j^{\prime}}
.

Intuitively, Condition
(i)
restricts the list
\mathcal{L}
to the edges of
G
, while Condition
(ii)
says that it ‘covers’ all edges of
G
.
Condition
(iii)
makes
\mathcal{L}
a DFS-like traversal.
In particular, if
\mathcal{L}
arrives at
v_{j}
that has some incident untraversed edge(s), then one of those edges serves as
a_{j+1}
.
Otherwise,
\mathcal{L}
backtracks to the last vertex
v_{j^{\prime}}
that has untraversed edges and traverses one of them.
Condition
(ii)
adds here that
X_{l}(v)=\emptyset
for each
v\in V_{G}
.

We point out that we encode the traversals as a sequence of directed pairs since this notation will be more natural in our proofs.
In particular, we heavily use that each
a_{i}
provides not only the next traversed edge but also encodes the direction of the traversal.
In what follows, to shorten the notation, we will write
e(a_{i})
to refer to
e_{i}
in
(i)
.

The complexity and correctness

Let
\mathcal{L}=((u_{1},v_{1}),\ldots,(u_{l},v_{l}))
be an edge DFS.
We say that
\mathcal{L}

crosses

v_{i}
when
v_{i}=u_{i+1}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,l-1\}
, otherwise
\mathcal{L}

backtracks to

u_{i+1}
, or simply
backtracks
when the vertex is not important.
Whenever
\mathcal{L}
backtracks to
u_{i+1}
,
v_{i}
is called
final in

\mathcal{L}
.
The vertex
u_{1}
is called the
starting vertex
.

For the purpose of analysis, we denote by
T_{j}
’s all intermediate trees obtained during the execution of the algorithm.
Namely,
T_{0}
is the initial tree from line
1
, and if
T_{j}
is the tree
T

at the beginning of an iteration of the
while
loop in Algorithm
1
, then
T_{j+1}
is the tree
T
at the end of this iteration.

Note that we suggested in the above definition that
T_{j}
’s are trees.
This follows from the fact that whenever the algorithm adds an edge
\{u,v\}
to
T
in line
1
, it checks that
u
does not belong to
T
yet, and
v
is in
T
.
Hence, each
T_{j}
is connected and has no cycles, which immediately results in the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

For each
j\geq 0
,
T_{j}
is a tree.
∎

Having proved that
T
‘grows correctly’, it remains to argue that it eventually becomes a spanning tree, i.e., the last tree
T_{k}
in the sequence satisfies
V_{T_{k}}=V_{G}
.
We say that
(X,Y)
is a
partition
of
G
if
X\cup Y=V_{G}
and
X\cap Y=\emptyset
.
We have the following straightforward property.

Lemma 3
.

If
(X,Y)
is a partition of a
2
-edge-connected
G
, then there exist at least two different edges, each having one endpoint in
X
and one in
Y
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

The last tree obtained during the execution of Algorithm
1
is a spanning tree of
G
.

Proof.

Let
n=|V_{G}|
.
We prove by induction on
j\in\{0,\ldots,n-2\}
that there exists an item
(u,v)
in
\mathcal{L}
such that
u\notin V_{T_{j}}
and
v\in V_{T_{j}}
, and hence
T_{j+1}
is computed by the algorithm, provided that the algorithm obtained
T_{j}
.
The base case of
j=0
and the inductive step for
j>0
are identical, hence pick any
j\in\{0,\ldots,n-2\}
.
Consider the partition
(X,Y)=(V_{T_{j}},V_{G}\setminus V_{T_{j}})
of
G
.

Take
(u_{i},v_{i})\in\mathcal{L}
such that
u_{i}\in X
and
v_{i}\in Y
.
If no such item in
\mathcal{L}
exists, then each edge
\{x,y\}
with
x\in X
and
y\in Y
provides an item
(y,x)\in\mathcal{L}
, and then Algorithm
1
can extend
T_{j}
to obtain
T_{j+1}
using this edge.
So suppose that the item
(u_{i},v_{i})
exists in
\mathcal{L}
and let
i
be the minimum such index.
Let
\widetilde{Y}\subseteq Y
be such that
v_{i}\in\widetilde{Y}
and
\widetilde{Y}
is the maximum subset that induces a connected component in
G
.
By Lemma
3
applied for partition
(V_{G}\setminus\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{Y})
, there exists an edge
\{x,y\}
different than
\{u_{i},v_{i}\}
such that
x\in V_{G}\setminus\widetilde{Y}
and
y\in\widetilde{Y}
.
By the maximality of
\widetilde{Y}
, we must have
x\in X
.

Find the minimum
i^{\prime}>i
such that the edge
e=\{u_{i^{\prime}},v_{i^{\prime}}\}
gives the item
(u_{i^{\prime}},v_{i^{\prime}})
in
\mathcal{L}
, such that one endpoint of
e
is in
X
and the other – in
\widetilde{Y}
.
Due to the existence of
\{x,y\}
such an
i^{\prime}
exists, although it may be the case that
e\neq\{x,y\}
if many edges connecting
X
and
\widetilde{Y}
exist.
If
u_{i^{\prime}}\in Y
and
v_{i^{\prime}}\in X
, then Algorithm
1
can add
e
to
T_{j}
in line
1
, which proves that
T_{j+1}
is obtained and completes the proof.
However, we note that we have not proved that
e
is the edge added to
T_{j}
to obtain
T_{j+1}
but we only have that
e
is a suitable candidate for this extension.

The case when
u_{i^{\prime}}\in X
and
v_{i^{\prime}}\in\widetilde{Y}
remains to be discussed.
We prove by contradiction that this is not possible.
Consider the part of
\mathcal{L}
that follows the item
(u_{i},v_{i})
.
A simple iterative argument gives that, for this part, it holds that if the last vertex visited is in
\widetilde{Y}
, which initially holds because we start with
v_{i}\in\widetilde{Y}
, then we have three possibilities.
Either an item
(a,b)
follows in
\mathcal{L}
, where
a\in\widetilde{Y}
and
b\in X
.
Or, an item
(a,b)
follows in
\mathcal{L}
, where
\{a,b\}\subseteq\widetilde{Y}
and
\mathcal{L}
crosses
a
.
Or,
\mathcal{L}
backtracks to a vertex
a
.

In the first case, where informally speaking
\mathcal{L}
‘crosses’ from
\widetilde{Y}
back to
X
, we have
\{a,b\}=\{u_{i^{\prime}},v_{i^{\prime}}\}
by the minimality of
i^{\prime}
.
But this means that
a=u_{i^{\prime}}
and
b=v_{i^{\prime}}
, thus contradicting
u_{i^{\prime}}\in X
and
v_{i^{\prime}}\in\widetilde{Y}
.
In this case, the induction takes us to the finish of the proof of the lemma.
For the second case,
b\in\widetilde{Y}
, which takes us to the next iteration of our argument.
In the last case
\mathcal{L}
backtracks.
Note that the existence of the edge
\{x,y\}
implies that
\widetilde{Y}
has at least one vertex that has an incident edge

not traversed by
\mathcal{L}
till this point.
Thus, by the definition of the edge DFS, i.e., Condition
(iii)
with constraints for backtracking,
\mathcal{L}
backtracks to a vertex that belongs to
\widetilde{Y}
, which also takes us to the next step of the iterative reasoning.
Thus, this induction eventually finishes with a contradiction, as required.
∎

Lemma 5
.

Given any
2
-edge-connected graph
G=(V_{G},E_{G})
as an input, the complexity of Algorithm
1
is
\mathcal{O}(|E_{G}|)
.

Proof.

The complexity of constructing an edge DFS
\mathcal{L}
is
\mathcal{O}(|E_{G}|)
.
To claim the linear running time it is enough to argue that the execution of line
1
can be done in constant time using a data structure whose total processing time is
\mathcal{O}(|E_{G}|)
.
This data structure is a queue
\mathcal{Q}
that is used in the following way.
It is initialized in line
1
where the tree
T
is set to contain a single vertex
x
.
The
\mathcal{Q}
is populated with all pairs from
\mathcal{L}
having
x
as one of the endpoints.
Then,
\mathcal{Q}
is used during the execution of line
1
as follows.
First, repeat the following: remove the first element
(u,v)
from
\mathcal{Q}
and if
(u,v)
satisfies the condition in line
1
, then the desired pair is found.
Otherwise, dequeue
(u,v)
and repeat with the next item in
\mathcal{Q}
.
Finally,
\mathcal{Q}
is updated during the execution of line
1
by enqueueing all pairs from
\mathcal{L}
having
v
as one of the endpoints; denote all such pairs by
Z(v)
.

Note that enqueueing to
\mathcal{Q}
all items with one endpoint equal to a vertex
x
for each vertex
x\in V_{G}
occurs exactly once for each
x
.
Thus exactly
2|E_{G}|
items are added to
\mathcal{Q}
in total.
Moreover, the operation of enqueueing them to
\mathcal{Q}
can be done in time
\mathcal{O}(|E_{G}(x)|)
using pre-processing: once
\mathcal{L}
is computed, iterate over the elements of
\mathcal{L}
and for each item add it to
Z(v)
if the item has
v
as an endpoint.
∎

5
Vertex degrees in
T

For a vertex
u
and an edge DFS
\mathcal{L}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{l})
, denote

E^{+}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)=\left\{e(a_{i})\colon a_{i}=(v_{i},u)\right\},

E^{-}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)=\left\{e(a_{i})\colon a_{i}=(u,v_{i+1})\right\}.

Each edge in
E^{+}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)
is called
incoming
and each edge in
E^{-}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)
is
outgoing
.

Lemma 6
.

For each
\mathcal{L}
and each vertex
u
that is visited by
\mathcal{L}
,
|E^{+}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)|\leq|E^{-}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)|+1
if
\deg_{G}(u)
is odd, and
|E^{+}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)|\leq|E^{-}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)|
if
\deg_{G}(u)
is even.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{L}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{l})
and first assume that
u
is not the starting vertex.
Whenever
\mathcal{L}
has an item
a_{i}=(u_{i},v_{i})
such that not all edges incident to
v_{i}
have been traversed yet, i.e.,
E_{G}(v_{i})\setminus\{e(a_{1}),\ldots,e(a_{i})\}\neq\emptyset
, then
a_{i+1}=(v_{i},v_{i+1})
due to Condition
(iii)
in the definition of the edge DFS (
\mathcal{L}
crosses
v_{i}
).
Hence,
e(a_{i+1})\in E^{-}_{\mathcal{L}}(v_{i})
.
This means that the above element in
E^{+}_{\mathcal{L}}(v_{i})
is uniquely paired with the corresponding element in
E^{-}_{\mathcal{L}}(v_{i})
.
The other case is when
E_{G}(v_{i})\setminus\{e(a_{1}),\ldots,e(a_{i})\}=\emptyset
.
Then,
\mathcal{L}
backtracks to
u_{i+1}
according to Condition
(iii)
.
This, however, may happen only once for each vertex
v_{i}
.

Hence, if the degree of a vertex
u
is even, then either each element in
E^{+}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)
is uniquely paired with an element in
E^{-}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)
, which completes the proof,
or one element in
E^{+}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)
is not paired, which occurs when
u
is final in
\mathcal{L}
.
Then, it follows from the parity of
\deg_{G}(u)
that there is at least one edge in
E^{-}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)
without a pair in
E^{+}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)
, which also implies the required bound.

The reasoning when
\deg_{G}(u)
is odd is analogous, except for the argument that when
u
is final, it may be the case that each element in
E^{+}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)
is in fact paired with an element in
E^{-}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)
, which gives the worst case of
|E^{+}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)|=|E^{-}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)|+1
.

The remaining cases are when
u
is the starting vertex for
\mathcal{L}
or when it is final in
\mathcal{L}
.
We skip those as being analogous.
∎

Now, for a vertex
u\in V_{T}
, let us analyze its degree
\deg_{T}(u)
in the final tree
T=(V_{T},E_{T})
returned by Algorithm
1
.
First, we argue that only one outgoing edge, among those in
E_{G}(u)
, can belong to
E_{T}
.
Indeed, for an outgoing edge
\{u,v\}\in E^{-}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)
to be added to
T
it must hold
(u,v)\in\mathcal{L}
(see line
1
in Algorithm
1
).
However, this may occur only once because, after the first such event,
u\in V_{T}
, and subsequent outgoing edges do not result in adding another edge to
T
due to the condition
u\notin V_{T}
in line
1
.
Having in mind the worst case when all incoming edges incident to
u
are added to
T
, we obtain:

\deg_{T}(u)\leq|E^{+}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)|+1.

(1)

If the degree of
u
is even, then by Lemma
6
,

\deg_{T}(u)\leq\frac{1}{2}(|E^{+}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)|+|E^{-}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)|)+1=\deg_{G}(u)/2+1.

If the degree of
u
is odd, then again by (
1
) and Lemma
6
,

\deg_{T}(u)\leq\frac{|E^{+}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)|}{2}+\frac{|E^{-}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)|+1}{2}+1.

Since for an odd
\deg_{G}(u)
we have

\frac{|E^{+}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)|+|E^{-}_{\mathcal{L}}(u)|+1}{2}=\frac{\deg_{G}(u)+1}{2}=\left\lceil\deg_{G}(u)/2\right\rceil,

we have proved Theorem
1
.
