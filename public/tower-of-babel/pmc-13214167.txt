Beyond the placebo effect: a systematic review and a network meta-analysis of treatment strategies for vulvodynia
Authors: Arsenio Spinillo, Mattia Dominoni, Lara Tiranini, Chiara Cassani, Rossella Elena Nappi, Barbara Gardella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo comprehensively evaluate the comparative and absolute effectiveness of available treatments for vulvodynia on pain and sexual function outcomes through network meta-analysis (NMA).MethodsSystematic searches extracted data from randomized controlled trials (blinded or unblinded) evaluating pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments in women with vulvodynia, from inception through September 2025. Of 40 studies assessed for eligibility, 34 studies involving 1,893 participants were included in the final analysis. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2 tool.Confidence in network meta-analysis results was evaluated using the Confidence in Network Meta-analysis (CINeMA) framework.ResultsPain and sexual function outcomes were rescaled to percentage of maximum possible score (POMP, 0–100 scale) to enable pooling across heterogeneous measurement instruments. Both relative treatment effects (comparing interventions to placebo/no treatment) and absolute effects (pre-post changes) were evaluated using Bayesian NMA. Ten treatment categories were analysed: acupuncture, botulinum toxin, topical anaesthetics, topical non-anaesthetics, multimodal/physical therapy, psychological approaches, surgery, tissue-based therapies (photobiomodulation, low-level laser, vaginal transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, shockwave therapy), placebo, and no treatment. For pain immediately post-therapy (33 studies, 1,873 women), tissue-based therapies demonstrated superior efficacy compared to placebo in both NMA (POMP difference: -16.1, 95% CI: -24.1, -7.9, mixed evidence, moderate confidence), and pairwise meta-analyses (POMP difference: -20.2, 95% CI: -28.6, -11.7, I2 = 77%) and ranking highest in surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) analysis (85.2%). Pharmacological interventions showed no superiority over placebo. Compared to no treatment, multimodal-physical therapy (POMP: -25.3, 95% CI: -40.8, -9.7, mixed evidence, low confidence), acupuncture (POMP: -25.6, 95% CI: -43.4, -8.1, mixed evidence, moderate confidence), and psychological approaches (POMP -27.4, 95% CI: -44.6, -10.3, indirect evidence, low confidence) all demonstrated moderate-to-high pain reduction. At 3–6 months follow-up (16 studies, 1,106 women), tissue-based treatments remained the only therapy significantly superior to placebo (POMP: -24.5, 95% CI: -42.2, -6.7, mixed evidence, moderate confidence; SUCRA = 77.9%). In absolute effects analysis, placebo produced significant pain reduction (Absolute effect: -13.7, 95% CI: -18.3, -9.3 immediately; -11.7, 95% CI: -17.7, -6.0 at 3–6 months), with confidence intervals overlapping all treatments except tissue-based therapies (Absolute effect: -28, 95% CI: -33.4, -21.7 immediately; -31.6, 95% CI: -43.9, -19.1 at follow-up). Placebo had no effect on sexual function improvement (14 studies, 942 women), (Absolute effect: 2.5, 95% CI: -8., 12.9). At 3–6 months of follow-up (8 studies, 429 women), psychological-based treatments, when added to multimodal treatment (POMP = 9.0, 95% CI = 1.8, 16.7, I2 = 41.6%), improved modestly sexual functioning.ConclusionsIn NMA, tissue-based therapies were the most effective treatment for vulvodynia-associated pain, achieving reductions that clearly exceeded placebo effects with moderate evidence quality. Commonly prescribed pharmacological interventions lack robust evidence of superiority over placebo. Multimodal approaches combined with psychological interventions show promise, particularly for sexual function recovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04469-0.

Introduction

Vulvodynia is a chronic idiopathic pain syndrome characterized by vulvar discomfort, such as burning, stinging, or irritation, often exacerbated by sexual intercourse, tampon insertion, or prolonged sitting [1, 2]. This condition profoundly impairs quality of life, particularly affecting physical functioning, emotional well-being, and sexual relationships, with many women reporting dyspareunia and reduced sexual satisfaction [1, 2]. Epidemiological studies indicate that vulvodynia affects between 8 and 16% of women at some point in their lifetime [1, 2]. Despite its considerable prevalence, fewer than half of affected women seek treatment, and remarkably, only 1.4% receive a correct diagnosis [1]. This diagnostic delay, coupled with the condition's multifactorial aetiology and heterogeneous symptomatic presentation, has contributed to persistent challenges in establishing evidence-based therapeutic guidelines. Therapeutic approaches for vulvodynia are multifaceted, targeting its presumed neuropathic, musculoskeletal, and psychosocial components. Pharmacological options include topical agents like lidocaine, oestrogen creams, or compounded neuromodulators (e.g., gabapentin or amitriptyline) for localized relief, and systemic medications such as tricyclic antidepressants (e.g., desipramine), anticonvulsants (e.g., pregabalin), or serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors to address central sensitization [3, 4]. Non-pharmacological interventions encompass pelvic floor physical therapy, biofeedback, cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT), and emerging modalities like photobiomodulation or internet-based programs combining education and mindfulness [3, 4]. A critical limitation of the existing evidence base is that the majority of randomized controlled trials in vulvodynia have enrolled relatively small numbers of participants, substantially limiting statistical power to detect clinically meaningful treatment effects [4, 5]. The evaluation of treatment efficacy in vulvodynia is additionally complicated by substantial placebo effects documented across multiple randomized controlled trials [4, 5]. Systematic reviews indicate that placebo responses can exceed 40–50% in pain reduction, with no pharmacological intervention consistently outperforming placebo across studies [5]. Although pairwise meta-analyses have attempted to synthesize evidence on vulvodynia treatments, such as those showing small effects for nonpharmacological therapies, standard meta-analytic investigations are constrained by reliance on direct comparisons, limiting integration of the diverse interventions and often precluded by heterogeneity [5, 6]. Network meta-analysis (NMA) addresses many of these limitations by allowing indirect comparisons between treatments that have not been directly compared in head-to-head trials, incorporating more comprehensive data, and providing a broader perspective on the therapeutic options [7]. Furthermore, network meta-analysis offers methodological advantages including increased statistical power, the ability to rank treatments, and more precise effect estimates [8]. Critically, for conditions like vulvodynia where placebo effects are substantial, Bayesian network meta-analysis enables the estimation of both relative treatment effects (comparing active interventions against each other or placebo) and absolute effects (quantifying the magnitude of change from baseline for each intervention). These absolute effect estimates are essential for clinical decision-making, as they allow clinicians and patients to understand not only which treatment is superior in comparative terms, but also the expected magnitude of benefit that can be anticipated from each therapeutic option including placebo [9]. The present network meta-analysis was conducted to comprehensively evaluate the comparative and absolute effectiveness of available treatments for vulvodynia on pain and sexual function outcomes.

Materials and methods

The protocol for this network meta-analysis (NMA) was registered in PROSPERO (n. CRD420251140836). We included both blinded and unblinded randomized studies that comparatively assessed the impact of pharmacological and nonpharmacological treatments on pain intensity and sexual functioning in women with vulvodynia. Following the PICO framework (Population, intervention, comparator, outcome), the population consisted of women diagnosed with vulvodynia (primary or secondary, provoked, spontaneous, mixed, or undefined) according to standard methods [10]. Specifically, the diagnosis included women with vulvar pain of unknown aetiology lasting more than three months. No age restriction was applied. Studies including women of any age (reproductive, perimenopausal, or postmenopausal) were eligible provided they met the vulvodynia diagnostic criteria. Interventions comprised the therapeutic procedures evaluated in each study; comparators (controls) included placebo, sham treatment, no treatment, or other procedures; and outcomes included measurements of pain and sexual functioning before and after treatment. Given the wide variety of treatments used to manage vulvodynia symptoms, we initially categorized treatments and comparators as follows: 1. Acupuncture. 2. Botulinum toxin injections 3. Topical anaesthetic treatment (lidocaine). 4. Topical non-anaesthetic treatment (oestrogen, nifedipine, steroids, diazepam, emollient creams). 5. Multimodal/physical therapy (a comprehensive approach including physical therapy, pelvic floor muscle exercises, massage and cognitive behavioural therapy involving active behavioural desensitization) 6. Psychological approaches (psychotherapy, mindfulness, and cognitive therapies focused solely on acceptance) that were typically used as adjunctive treatments in women already receiving topical or multimodal treatments. 7. Surgical treatment. 8. Tissue-based therapies (vaginal transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, photobiomodulation, shockwave therapy, low-level laser therapy). 9. Systemic treatment (gabapentin, amytriptyline, desipramine, others) 10. Placebo (true pharmacological placebo or sham treatment for nonpharmacological approaches). 11. No treatment (waitlist or no additional treatment provided). Pharmacological treatments were analysed separately or as a whole category. Treatment categories were created based on several considerations. First, individual agents within categories (e.g., different topical non-anaesthetics or systemic medications) were each evaluated in only 1–3 studies with small sample sizes, making separate analysis statistically underpowered and resulting in very wide, imprecise confidence intervals. Second, while we acknowledge that treatments within categories have different specific mechanisms of action (e.g., topical oestrogen addresses hormonal factors while topical diazepam targets muscle hypertonicity), they share a common route of administration and clinical positioning as local therapies, reflecting pragmatic treatment choices in clinical practice. Third, creating overly granular treatment nodes would have resulted in a sparse, disconnected network that would prevent indirect comparisons and defeat the purpose of network meta-analysis. Fourth, this approach follows established precedent in network meta-analyses of complex interventions where treatments are grouped into clinically meaningful categories to balance statistical feasibility with interpretability. Finally, we conducted sensitivity analyses examining pharmacological treatments both individually and combined to assess whether grouping influenced conclusions about their efficacy. Finally, we adhered to the standard NMA principle for "Add-on" designs: when a background therapy was present in both the intervention and control arms, the node assignment was determined by the distinct intervention being tested [7–9]. We assumed that the relative effect of the distinct intervention (e.g., Diazepam) compared to its control was consistent, regardless of the background therapy (e.g., Physical Therapy).

Search methods and eligibility criteria

The search strategy was published in PROSPERO (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251140836) and reported in Suppl. 1. In brief, the main search terms included "vulvodynia," "vestibulodynia," "vulvar pain," or "vulvar vestibulitis" combined with "randomized." Databases searched were Embase, Embase via Ovid, MEDLINE, PubMed, Science Citation Index, Scopus, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. Pain and sexual function were measured before treatment initiation, immediately post-treatment and 3–6 months thereafter.

Study selection

Three reviewers (MD, BG, LT) independently screened abstracts of selected studies and extracted data from full-text articles. The extracted data were tabulated and compared for consistency. Only treatments evaluated in at least two studies were included in the analysis. The study selection process is reported in a PRISMA diagram (Fig. 1). Of the 40 studies assessed for eligibility [11–50], three studies [46, 47, 50] were excluded due to unavailable data, one study was a randomized investigation of two different doses of the same treatment (no comparator) [49], and two studies involved unique interventions that could not form viable treatment nodes: vestibulectomy [45] (the only eligible surgical study, precluding a surgical node with the required minimum of two studies, and transcranial direct current stimulation [48] leaving 34 studies for analysis (Table 1). Three included studies used a crossover design [12, 19, 21]. To avoid unit-of-analysis errors and eliminate potential carry-over effects, we extracted only data from the first period of these trials, before participants crossed over to the alternate treatment. This approach, recommended by the Cochrane Handbook (Sect. 23.2.4), effectively treats crossover trials as parallel-group trials and avoids bias from period effects or insufficient washout [51]. Consequently, we applied the standard Cochrane RoB 2 tool rather than the crossover-specific tool, as we analysed only the pre-crossover phase. One included study [17] compared two different psychological interventions (mindfulness-based cognitive therapy vs. cognitive behavioural therapy) without a placebo or no-treatment control arm. We included this study because these represent genuinely distinct therapeutic modalities (cognitive vs psychological) with different theoretical frameworks, techniques, and mechanisms of action, allowing network meta-analysis to indirectly estimate their comparative effectiveness through connections to other studies. Assessment of risk of bias

The risk of bias (RoB) in individual studies was assessed using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) by three reviewers (MD, BG, LT) [51], with oversight by a senior author (AS). The graphical and analytical classification of RoB for each study is reported in Supplementary Material 1.

Synthesis of results

Pain intensity in the included studies was measured using various methods and at different sites or situations (e.g., Q-tip test, tampon test, pain during intercourse). The Visual numeric scale was used in 4 studies [14, 15, 17, 28], the McGill Pain Questionnaire in 2 studies [19, 35], and other methods (Likert, Friedrich, etc.) in the remaining 6 studies [12, 22, 26, 27, 34, 40]. When multiple pain evaluation sites were reported, we prioritized measures in the following order: cotton swab testing on the vulvar vestibule, pressure threshold testing, and reported pain during intercourse. Sexual functioning was measured using the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) in 13 of the 14 studies reporting this outcome. To evaluate treatment effects on pain intensity and sexual function, we rescaled all scores to a 0–100 scale and expressed severity of pain as a percentage of the maximum possible score (POMP) [52, 53]. This unitless and intuitive measure avoids standardized mean differences, which are less intuitive and more statistically problematic for evaluating relative and absolute treatment effects on psychometric measures [53, 54]. Meta-analyzed data included the absolute difference in pre-post POMP scores (POMP differences) and their corresponding standard deviations, computed according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, accounting for the correlation between pre- and post-treatment scores [55]. In the main analysis, standard deviations of POMP differences were calculated assuming a high correlation coefficient between pre- and post-treatment measurements (r = 0.8) [53, 55]. For the evaluation of relative treatment effects, a sensitivity analysis was performed using POMP differences and standard deviations calculated assuming moderate (r = 0.5) and low (r = 0.3) correlations between pre-post measurements. Differences in pre-post POMP scores between treatments were used to compute relative effects [53]. We applied the IMMPACT recommendations [9] to POMP scores to facilitate a clinical, as well as a statistical, interpretation of our findings. According to these recommendations, a ~ 30% reduction from baseline is considered a moderately important change, and a 10% reduction is considered a minimally important change. For the overall median baseline POMP of 67 in this study (IQR = 15.4), these correspond to absolute reductions of approximately 20 and 7 POMP points, respectively. Both thresholds are used throughout the results to contextualise effect sizes. Reducing pain after treatments was computed as increasingly negative POMP scores whereas improvement of sexual function as increasingly positive scores. Main analysis was performed using a standard random-effects Bayesian network meta-analysis and was conducted using GEMTC (General Mixed Treatment Comparisons) and BUGS (Bayesian Inference Using Gibbs Sampling) routines in R (version 4.5.2 The R Foundation for Statistical Computing 2025) to evaluate the relative effects of treatments compared to placebo and no treatment [56, 57]. Network plots were generated using ggplot2 package in R. GEMTC and BUGS routines were also used for pairwise comparisons, nodesplit analysis, ranking analysis, and meta-regression to evaluate the role of potential moderators of effects. We assessed potential small-study effects by including study sample size as a covariate in a Bayesian random-effects network meta-regression model, implemented in GEMTC [57, 58]. Ranking analysis was performed by evaluating the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA). To evaluate model inconsistency and heterogeneity, we compared the deviation of the inconsistency model with that of the NMA model [56, 57]. Differences in the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC) between models with and without covariates in meta-regression analysis were used to assess the influence of moderators on relative effects, with DIC differences > 3 considered significant [58]. Previous vulvodynia trials [6] suggest that placebo has a significant effect on pain, and absolute pre-post changes in pain scores are critical for evaluating treatment efficacy clinically [9]. Therefore, we also evaluated the absolute effects of treatments, including placebo, as differences in pre-post POMP scores using a Bayesian arm-based model with the PCNETMETA routine in R [59]. Confidence in the NMA results was assessed using the CINeMA web-based program [60], which simultaneously and semi-automatically evaluates six domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. Based on the results for these domains in each analysis, confidence was graded from high to very low [60]. CINeMA assessments were performed for all NMA relative-effect comparisons involving pain-reduction (immediately post-therapy and 3–6 months follow-up) and for sexual function outcomes where NMA was feasible. For the 3–6 month sexual function outcome, where the network was disconnected, CINeMA assessment was not applicable.

Results

Study selection and network characteristics

The final analysis included 34 studies and 1893 subjects with vulvodynia (Table 1). Twenty-one studies included only women with provoked vulvodynia [11, 12, 14, 15, 18–20, 22–24, 28, 31, 34, 35, 37, 38, 41–44] while the remaining 13 included women with mixed or undefined forms [13, 16, 17, 21, 25–27, 29, 30, 32, 33, 36, 39, 40]. The overall median age of enrolled women was 28 years (SD = 6.2). Studies were conducted in the USA (10 studies) [12, 18, 19, 22, 24, 27, 35, 40, 42, 43] Canada (8 studies) [11, 13–15, 17, 23, 32, 36], Europe (12 studies) [20, 21, 28–31, 34, 37–39, 41, 44] Israel (3 studies) [16, 25, 33], and Brazil (one study) [26].

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

Risk of bias was assessed across 34 RCTs using the RoB 2 tool (Suppl 1). Overall, four studies (12%) were rated at low risk [11, 21, 24, 25], 26 (76%) at some concerns [12–16, 18–20, 22, 23, 27, 29, 31–44], and four (12%) at high risk [17, 26, 28, 30]. The most consistently problematic domain was selection of the reported result (D5), which raised concerns in 27 studies, primarily owing to absent pre-registration. The randomisation process (D1) was rated at high risk in three studies [17, 30, 36] and at some concerns in four [22, 23, 31, 42]. Missing outcome data (D3) and outcome measurement (D4) were rated at low risk in all or nearly all studies. Deviations from intended interventions (D2) raised some concerns in 11 studies, largely reflecting open-label designs inherent to behavioural and physical interventions.

Certainty of NMA estimates was evaluated using CINeMA (Suppl. 2–4. Reporting bias was at low risk across all comparisons. For pain immediately post-therapy, the placebo vs tissue-based comparison (5 direct studies: [11, 25, 31, 33, 37]) achieved moderate certainty, downgraded for heterogeneity; local non-anaesthetics vs tissue-based ( [38]) and systemic vs tissue-based ( [39]) were both rated low, downgraded for within-study bias and imprecision or heterogeneity. Among comparisons vs no treatment, acupuncture ( [43]) was rated moderate (heterogeneity), multimodal/physical therapy ( [30, 34]) was rated low (within-study bias, heterogeneity), and no treatment vs systemic therapies (indirect network evidence through [19, 26]) was rated very low, additionally penalised for imprecision and incoherence. For pain at 3–6 months, placebo vs tissue-based (4 direct studies: [11, 25, 31, 37]) was moderate (heterogeneity), while all remaining comparisons, botulinum, local anaesthetics, local non-anaesthetics, multimodal/physical, and psychological vs tissue-based, were low based on indirect evidence, downgraded for indirectness and imprecision. For sexual functioning post-therapy, placebo vs tissue-based (3 direct studies: [11, 25, 37]) and all indirect comparisons were rated low, due to within-study bias, heterogeneity, and imprecision. No comparison reached high certainty across any outcome.

Pain immediately post-therapy

A total of 1,873 women reported pain severity before and immediately after therapy in 33 studies across 10 treatment types (Fig. 2A). The overall median POMP score before starting therapy was 67 (IQR = 15.4) suggesting that initial pain was moderate. For a population with this severity of initial pain, a 30% reduction (moderate effect) corresponds to an absolute reduction of approximately 20 POMP points. A 10% reduction from baseline, often considered a minimally important change, corresponds to an absolute reduction of approximately 7 POMP points in this analysis. This translation from relative percent reduction to absolute pre-post percent reduction of pain allows for a direct clinical interpretation of our effect estimates. Discussion

In this network meta-analysis evaluating the comparative and absolute effectiveness of various treatments for vulvodynia, tissue-based therapies (photobiomodulation, shockwave therapy, TENS, low-level laser therapy) emerged as the most effective intervention for reducing pain intensity both immediately post-therapy and at 3–6 months follow-up when compared to placebo, with moderate confidence in the evidence. These interventions also ranked highest in probabilistic analysis and, critically, exhibited absolute effect sizes with confidence intervals that did not overlap with placebo effects, indicating a magnitude of benefit clearly exceeding non-specific treatment factors. These therapies were grouped as a single category because they share a common mechanism of delivering non-invasive physical energy, such as light, electrical stimulation, or acoustic waves, to modulate local vulvar tissue, enabling a unified analysis of their collective efficacy despite differences in specific modalities [3, 5, 10]. Results should be interpreted at the treatment-category level and, given the grouping of heterogeneous interventions within network nodes, should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. The grouping strategy, while methodologically necessary to maintain a connected network and achieve sufficient statistical power, limits inference about any single agent within a category and represents a central methodological limitation of this study. In particular, the heterogeneity of agents within the tissue-based category,spanning distinct physical mechanisms such as photobiomodulation, acoustic shockwave, low-level laser, and electrical stimulation,means that the observed category-level effect estimate cannot be extrapolated to any individual modality without further dedicated evidence.

Individual modalities within the tissue-based category are each supported by only 1–3 studies; category-level effect estimates should therefore not be interpreted as equivalently strong evidence for each specific intervention. The mechanisms underlying tissue-based therapies likely involve neuromodulation, improved tissue perfusion, reduced inflammation, and enhanced cellular repair through stimulation of mitochondrial ATP production and release of endogenous analgesics [10, 11, 31, 37]. Contrary to widespread clinical use [61], pharmacological interventions, whether topical anaesthetics, topical non-anaesthetic treatments, or systemic neuromodulators, failed to demonstrate statistically significant superiority over placebo in pain reduction. This finding persisted across both individual drug categories and when pharmacological treatments were analysed collectively. Other non-pharmacological approaches, such as acupuncture demonstrated moderate pain reduction vs. no treatment supported by moderate-certainty evidence. In contrast, multimodal and psychological approaches showed similar effect sizes but with low-certainty evidence, indicating that these results should be interpreted more cautiously. Regarding sexual function outcomes, tissue-based treatments showed borderline significant improvement immediately post-treatment, while psychological interventions added to multimodal treatment demonstrated benefit at medium-term follow-up. A striking finding of this NMA was the substantial placebo response observed across studies. Placebo alone was associated with a mean absolute pain reduction of 13.7% (95% CI: −18.3, −9.3) immediately post-therapy and 11.7% (95% CI: −17.8, −6.0) at 3–6 months but without significant effect on sexual functioning. This robust placebo effect highlights the role of psychological factors and patient expectations in pain management, suggesting that future interventions should incorporate strategies to harness these non-specific effects. Additionally, the modest improvements in sexual function underscore the need for integrated approaches that address not only pain but also relational and emotional dimensions, potentially through combined therapies. From a practical standpoint, the accessibility and cost-effectiveness of tissue-based therapies warrant further investigation, as they may vary by healthcare setting and could influence adoption in clinical practice. Patient preferences, including tolerance for invasive procedures versus non-invasive options, should also guide treatment selection to optimize adherence and outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is its methodological comprehensiveness, integrating both relative and absolute treatment effects through a Bayesian NMA. This approach allowed simultaneous comparison across diverse treatment classes, increasing statistical power, facilitating treatment ranking, and addressing limitations inherent in traditional pairwise meta-analyses that are often constrained by heterogeneity and sparse direct evidence [7, 8]. The use of percentage of maximum scores, rather than standardized mean differences, permitted pooling of heterogeneous scales while preserving clinical interpretability [52–54]. Furthermore, by modelling absolute pre–post changes, we quantified the therapeutic magnitude of both active treatments and placebo, enabling an assessment of efficacy beyond relative comparisons. The use of sensitivity analyses to evaluate the role of pre-post measurements, the use of meta-regression for potential moderators of effects, and the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis framework further strengthened the validity of the results. Although sample size was not a significant predictor in the meta-regression, small studies may have limited power to detect subtle effects and increased the imprecision of the estimates, as indicated by the CINeMA assessments. Heterogeneity in outcome measures (e.g., varying pain assessment methods like VAS or NRS) and vulvodynia subtypes (provoked versus mixed) could have introduced bias, although meta-regression analyses suggested these factors did not significantly moderate effects. The magnitude of placebo effects varied across studies, complicating the interpretation of relative treatment effects. Factors such as patient expectations, the therapeutic context, and the nature of the placebo intervention (e.g., sham procedures vs. oral placebos) may have contributed to this variability. In the 3–6 month follow-up analysis, a single no-treatment arm was merged with the placebo node for network connectivity. Since no-treatment and placebo differ in the level of therapeutic contact and patient expectation, this merging may have modestly inflated or deflated the placebo effect estimate at follow-up. This represents a minor analytical limitation of the follow-up model. Finally, the majority of studies were conducted in high-income countries, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to low- and middle-income settings where access to specialized treatments (e.g., tissue-based therapies) may be restricted. Despite this, most included trials received a rating of 'some concerns' or 'high risk' under RoB 2, primarily due to the inherent difficulty of blinding participants to non-pharmacological interventions, limiting certainty of effect estimates. Furthermore, the included studies were predominantly conducted in premenopausal women (overall median age 28 years, SD = 6.2); findings may not generalise to postmenopausal women, in whom hormonal contributions to vulvodynia may differ substantially. A further limitation concerns the core assumptions of network meta-analysis. Transitivity, the requirement that effect modifiers be similarly distributed across trials,may not be fully satisfied given differences in vulvodynia subtype, patient age, and the nature of comparator arms across included studies. Similarly, grouping agents with distinct mechanisms of action within the same node (e.g., topical oestrogen and topical diazepam) may affect the interpretability of indirect comparisons. Comparisons relying exclusively on indirect evidence should therefore be interpreted with particular caution. Importantly, however, the global tests for inconsistency were non-significant across all outcomes, and sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of the main findings, providing some reassurance regarding the internal validity of the network.

Comparison with existing literature

Although, to our knowledge, this is the first network meta-analysis evaluating treatments for vulvodynia, in the past years several standard meta-analyses both on pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatment of vulvodynia have been published [3–6, 63–66]. Starzec-Proserpio et al. [5], using standardized mean differences as outcome measures, conducted a meta-analysis on non-pharmacological therapies for chronic pelvic pain (including vulvodynia subsets) and reported small to moderate effects for physical therapy and psychological interventions on pain. Our findings corroborate the benefit of multimodal approaches compared to no treatment, though we found less consistent evidence of superiority over placebo when examining vulvodynia-specific populations. Differences in the population studied (chronic pelvic pain vs. exclusive vulvodynia) and methods (traditional meta-analysis vs. NMA, relative effects vs. relative + absolute effects) used could explain the discrepancies. According to Bohm-Starke et al. [4], who conducted a systematic review limited to provoked vulvodynia, our study acknowledges the scarcity of high-quality trials and the heterogeneity of interventions. They concluded that multimodal physiotherapy showed the most promise, though their synthesis remained qualitative due to inconsistent outcome measures. The placebo effect found in this NMA is consistent with previous reports in vulvodynia and other chronic pain conditions, where placebo responses can exceed 40–50% [6]. The inclusion of absolute effect estimates in this NMA is particularly noteworthy, as it allows clinicians and patients to contextualize the magnitude of benefit that can be expected from each treatment, including placebo. Our analysis matches the result of a previous classical meta-analysis showing that placebo-associated pain reductions are clinically meaningful and that overlap with most active pharmacological treatments [6]. This NMA and previous data strengthen confidence in the conclusion that commonly prescribed pharmacological interventions for vulvodynia lack robust evidence of superiority over placebo [6, 62, 65]. Physiotherapy and rehabilitative techniques have been evaluated in two additional meta-analyses using both a qualitative and a classic quantitative approach showing a beneficial effect on pain reduction although, small sample sizes and heterogeneity reduced the certainty of the results [63–66]. Finally, a recent meta-analysis on psychotherapy [64] found significant improvements in vulvar pain, sexual function, and psychological adjustment (e.g., pain acceptance), with low certainty, aligning with our low-confidence benefits for psychological interventions over no treatment; however, limited data (only two studies) emphasize the need for rigorous trials.

Clinical implications

Given their comparatively superior efficacy in reducing pain at the category level, tissue-based therapies may deserve greater attention in the clinical evaluation of treatment options for vulvodynia-associated pain. However, this recommendation must be framed within the limitations of the available evidence: findings reflect category-level estimates derived from grouping modalities with distinct mechanisms (photobiomodulation, low-level laser, TENS, shockwave therapy), each individually supported by only 1–3 small trials. Based on moderate-certainty evidence for the category as a whole compared to placebo, these approaches warrant consideration for patients with prominent neuropathic pain characteristics, [11, 25, 31, 33, 37] but definitive prioritisation of any individual modality,or over other established interventions,requires confirmation from larger, well-designed, head-to-head trials. The exploratory nature of these findings should be communicated explicitly to patients when discussing treatment options. Commonly prescribed pharmacological interventions, including topical anaesthetics and systemic neuromodulators, lack robust evidence of superiority over placebo in reducing pain, challenging current practice patterns and highlighting the need for re-evaluation of first-line therapeutic approaches. Improvements in sexual function in this NMA were generally modest across treatments studied. Tissue-based therapies showed borderline significance in improving sexual function compared to placebo, while psychological interventions combined with multimodal therapy demonstrated more sustained benefits at 3–6 months. These findings suggest that while pain reduction is a critical first step, addressing the psychological and relational aspects of vulvodynia is essential for restoring sexual health [15, 35]. In particular, multimodal approaches combining physical therapy with psychological interventions show promise for medium-term sexual function improvement, supporting the conceptualization of vulvodynia as a biopsychosocial condition requiring comprehensive rather than single-modality treatment [5]. While multimodal and psychological approaches did not consistently demonstrate superiority over placebo for pain outcomes, their effects on sexual function and their benefit compared to no treatment support their inclusion in comprehensive care models. Finally, the significant placebo effect on pain outcome underscores the importance of therapeutic relationship, patient expectation management, and psychological factors in treatment outcomes.

Conclusions

This network meta-analysis provides the most comprehensive comparative effectiveness evaluation of vulvodynia treatments to date, using Bayesian dual-analysis to disentangle relative and absolute treatment effects in the context of substantial placebo responses. At the category level, tissue-based therapies demonstrated the most consistent pain reduction, with effects clearly exceeding placebo under moderate-certainty evidence and confirmed across both pairwise and network analyses and at multiple timepoints. While the grouping of heterogeneous interventions within nodes renders these findings exploratory with respect to individual modalities, the convergence of evidence across multiple analytical approaches supports their overall direction. Pharmacological interventions lacked evidence of superiority over placebo. Multimodal approaches combined with psychological interventions show promise for sexual function recovery, though on the basis of low-certainty evidence. Future research should prioritise well-powered trials of individual modalities, direct head-to-head comparisons, and longer follow-up in diverse populations.
