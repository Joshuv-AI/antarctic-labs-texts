A longitudinal analysis of Clostridioides difficile virulence profiles across three geographic regions in Israel (2020–2022)
Authors: Orna Schwartz, Avi Peretz, Maya Azrad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is one of the leading infectious diarrhea worldwide. C. difficile pathogenicity is mediated by various virulence factors, including toxins and biofilm formation. This study included 313 C. difficile isolates that were previously collected from four medical centers. We aimed to investigate the prevalence, distribution, and associations of virulence factors in C. difficile strains from both healthcare-associated (HA)- and community-acquired (CA)-CDI patients across Israel from 2020 to 2022.MethodsMulti-locus sequence typing (MLST) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) were performed for strain classification and virulence gene detection. Toxin and biofilm production were also characterized.ResultsSequence type (ST) 42 (12.5%) and ST2 (11.5%) were the most prevalent strains. Toxin production patterns were significantly associated with setting of infection acquisition (p < 0.0001). Isolates producing both toxin A and B were 2.5-fold more common among HA-CDI strains, while toxin-A-producers were more frequent in CA-CDI. Biofilm was produced by 96.9% of isolates, with strong biofilm-producing strains significantly more prevalent in hospitals (p < 0.001). Furthermore, isolates producing both toxins A and B were predominantly moderate or strong biofilm producers (p < 0.0001). agrD presence was significantly associated with ST, toxin production patterns and biofilm-production capacity (p < 0.0001 for ST and biofilm, p = 0.0016 for toxins).ConclusionsThis study revealed a high diversity of C. difficile strains in Israel and demonstrated that virulence factor patterns differ significantly across STs and the acquisition setting. The differences between HA-CDI and CA-CDI isolates in some virulence factors underscores the importance of continuous epidemiological surveillance to guide tailored treatment and prevention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available athttps://doi.org/10.1186/s12941-026-00868-1.Keywords: C. difficile, Antibiotic susceptibility, Strains, WGS, Virulence genes

Background

Clostridioides difficile (C. difficile) is a leading cause of infectious diarrhea worldwide hospital-associated (HA) diarrhoea [1]. Traditionally CDI was considered a nosocomial disease [2, 3] (associated with hospitalization, antibiotic exposure, and advanced age [2, 4]. However, CDI epidemiology has evolved significantly in recent decades, with a clear rise in community-acquired cases (CA-CDI) [2, 5]. According to a recent study, 25% of CDI cases are community-acquired [5]. In another study which conducted a survey among 13 Asia-Pacific countries, CA-CDI comprised 16.5% of all CDI cases [6]. In a study from Lebanon, 30% of CDI patients had no risk factors of HA-CDI, such as recent hospitalization and antibiotic exposure [7].

Patients with CA-CDI tend to be younger and are more frequently female compared to those with HA-CDI [2, 4]. While antibiotic exposure remains a primary risk factor for both, a considerable portion of CA-CDI patients has not received antibiotics during the 2–6 months preceding diagnosis time [5].

Additionally, CA-CDI patients are less likely to have severe comorbidities or to use gastric acid suppressants like proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) compared to hospitalized counterparts [2, 4].

This epidemiological shift may be associated with previously underappreciated transmission reservoirs, including asymptomatic carriers, environmental sources, animal reservoirs, and community antimicrobial exposure [3]. In parallel, the global emergence and dissemination of genetically diverse C. difficile strains, including hypervirulent lineages characterized by enhanced toxin production and additional virulence determinants, have significantly influenced disease incidence, severity, and outbreak dynamics [2, 5]. Understanding the distribution and evolution of virulence factors in both healthcare-associated and community-associated strains is therefore essential for elucidating CDI epidemiology and for improving surveillance and infection-control strategies.

The main virulence factors of C. difficile are toxin A (TcdA) and toxin B (TcdB), whose activities induce cell damage and trigger the host immune response, eventually resulting in fluid secretion, leading to watery diarrhoea [2, 8]. Binary toxin, a third toxin, is expressed by some epidemic strains, such as ribotype 027 and 078 and enhances CDI severity [8]. Additional virulence factors are spore formation, presence of surface layer (S-layer) proteins (SLPs), flagella and heat shock proteins, biofilm production and quorum sensing. C. difficile spores survive for long periods, pass easily from person to person and resist a variety of disinfectants [9]. Expression of the sporulation regulator spo0A in C. difficile was associated with high spore production and biofilm formation, which contribute to bacterial persistence and recurrences [10, 11].

Delation of Cwp66 gene, which encodes one of the most abundant SLPs, resulted in decreased adhesion and motility [12]. Cwp84, another common SLP, is involved in adhesions’ cleavage and degradation of collagen, fibronectin and fibronectin, contributing to C. difficile dissemination in the host [13, 14]. Some C. difficile strains feature a flagellum, which is comprised of two main proteins, Flic and FliD [15]. Post-translational modification of the flagellant gene prevented biofilm formation and C. difficile colonization [10, 15, 16]. The heat shock GroEL proteins were also associated with attachment to host cells; antibodies to these proteins inhibited C. difficile adhesion to host cells in vitro [17].

Biofilm, an organized bacterial community shielded by a self-generated matrix. Bacteria inside these structures are safeguarded from external stresses, such as the host immune response and antibiotics. Biofilm contributes to sustained and repeated episodes of infections [18], partly due to the accumulation of spores and toxins within the biofilm [11].

Quorum sensing (QS) molecules, known as autoinducers, are generated by bacteria and regulate virulence factors’ expression (biofilm production, motility, etc.), with relation to cell density; surrounding bacteria detect these molecules when their density is high [19]. The accessory gene regulator (agr) system encode four proteins (AgrB, AgrD, AgrC, and AgrA) [19].

C. difficile strains differ in their repertoire of virulence factors, which may explain the various presentations of CDI, ranging from mild to severe cases. Nevertheless, strain identification is not routinely performed, as most methods are laborious and expensive. The current study investigated the distribution of C. difficile’s virulence factors collected over 3 years (2020–2022) from CA- and HA-CDI patients in several medical centers located across different geographic areas in Israel. The work sought to identify trends in strain characteristics across study years and geographic regions. Finally, it investigated possible associations between different virulence factors. This information is valuable for understanding the evolving epidemiology and may affect prevention, diagnosis and treatment guidelines.

Methods

Study isolates

C. difficile isolates were collected in previous studies [20, 21] from specimens obtained from patients admitted to four medical centres in Israel, within the years 2020–2022. These studies were approved by institutional review boards (approval numbers POR-0085-15, WOMC-0115-20, SOR-0307-20). All participants provided signed informed consent. CDI cases were divided into community-acquired CDI (CA-CDI) and hospital-acquired CDI (HA-CDI; according to the Israeli Ministry of Health, CDI that developed within 48 h of admission, was defined as CA-CDI, while CDI that developed > 48 h after admission was defined as HA-CDI.

Sequence type determination

Strains were typed by multi-locus sequence typing (MLST), as previously described [21]. Briefly, For MLST determination, the PubMLST C. difficile curated database MLST was used. This approach compares the sequences of 7 housekeeping genes against a global database of C. difficile strains, ensuring accurate sequence type (ST) determination regardless of the initial alignment reference. Isolates were categorized into major ST groups, each of which included at least six isolates, and an additional group called “others”, with less than 6 isolates per ST [20, 21].

Detection of virulence-associated genes

WGS was performed as previously described [21], with several key steps:

DNA extraction: Total genomic DNA was extracted from bacterial isolates using the MagCore® Genomic DNA Bacterial Kit (ATRIDAB.V, Amersfoort, Netherlands), with the MagCore® automated extraction instrument (RBCBioscience, New Taipei, Taiwan), per manufacturer’s instructions.

Library preparation and sequencing: Following DNA clean-up, WGS libraries were prepared using the Illumina DNA Prep kit (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Samples were tagmented and subjected to adapter ligation using IDT for Illumina Nextera UD Indexes Set A, B, C, D. Subsequent enrichment and clean-up steps were performed according to manufacturer’s instructions. Pooled libraries were quantified using a Qubit 4.0 fluorimeter (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Fragment sizes were determined using the TapeStation 4150 and the DNA HS D1000 kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The content of pooled libraries was normalized to 4 nM. Dual-indexed paired-end sequencing with a 149-bp read length was performed using the NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina, Inc.).

WGS data analysis and gene detection: FastQ files were analyzed (alignment and quality assurance) using Partek® Flow® software.

The presence of the toxin loci and the following virulence -associated genes was determined: Cwp2, cwp66, secA, slpA, groEL, secA, fbpA, tcdR, sp0A, codY, ccpA, rstA, sigH, sigD, agrD, luxS, pilA, fliC, fliD, sinR, by comparing the average coverage of genes against both C. difficile strain 630 and strain 196.

The BAM files of samples that initially failed analysis (generated in Partek Flow) were further analyzed using the Genius program for visual inspection of the depth and quality of missing genes.

Detection of toxin protein

The CerTest Clostridium difficile GDH + Toxin A + B combo card test kit (Certest Biotec, S.L, Spain) was used for detection of toxin A and B in stool samples, as previously described [21].

Biofilm detection assay

Biofilm production was evaluated as previously described [21]. C. difficile strains were divided into four categories: non-, weak, moderate and strong producers.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as count and percentages; continuous variables are presented as mean and standard variation.

Differences in distribution of categorical parameters were analysed using the Chi-squared or Fisher exact test for independent samples. All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance set as p value < 0.05. Data were analysed using Graphpad® version 9.5.1 (733).

Results

Of 313 C. difficile isolates, 187 were recovered from patients with HA-CDI and 126 were from patients with CA-CDI. Sixty isolates were recovered in the year 2020, while161 and 86 isolates were recovered in 2021 and 2022, respectively. One-hundred and twenty-two isolates were recovered from patients from north Israel, 104 were recovered in central Israel and 87 were from south Israel (Table S1).

As previously reported, the most prevalent strains were ST42 (12.5%, 39/313) and ST2 (11.5%, 36/313) (Table S2) [22]. ST distribution significantly differed across study years (p = 0.018) (Fig. 1). For example, ST3 and ST37 were identified in 2021 and 4 new STs emerged in 2022 (ST7, ST21, ST43, ST103). While no significant differences were seen in ST distribution across geographic regions (p = 0.127) (Figure S1), some variations were noted. For example, ST54 was not identified in south Israel and ST37 was not identified in central Israel. ST104 was less common in north Israel as compared to Israel’s center and south, while the distribution of ST11, ST2 and ST42 was more uniform across the different regions.

Virulence factors profiles and their distribution per years and geographic areas

Forty-three (13.7%) isolates produced toxin A, 95 (30.3) produced toxin B and 175 (56%) produced both toxins (Table S3). Twenty-three (7.3%) isolates expressed the binary toxin. Most (96.9%) isolates produced biofilm.

Differences in toxin production patterns were found across bacterial STs (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2). For example, while some strains produced either toxin A, or toxin B, or both toxins, other strains did not produce toxin A alone (ST37, ST34, ST3).

Toxin production patterns were similar across study years (p = 0.48), and across geographic regions (p = 0.43), Figure S2). Biofilm-production capacity was associated with ST (p < 0.0001, Fig. 4) and acquisition type (p < 0.001, Fig. 5) but showed no distinct patterns by study year or geographic region. For example, most of ST42 (56.4%), ST104 (56.4%), and ST2 (72.2%), had moderate or strong biofilm-production capacity,, while all ST37, and most (12/13, 92.3%) ST3 isolates were weak biofilm producers. Additionally, strong biofilm-producing strains were more common in hospitals compared to community (p < 0.001, Fig. 5). Biofilm-production capacity and toxin-production patterns were also associated (p < 0.0001, Fig. 6); almost 50% of the isolates producing both toxins A and B were moderate or strong biofilm producers, while most toxin A producers (67.4%) and toxin B producers (76.4%) were weak biofilm producers.

Presence of virulence genes

A search for genes related to flagella, biofilm production and sporulation was conducted in 241 isolates. agrD, detected in 21.2% of the isolates, was the only gene whose presence was found to be associated with ST (p < 0.0001, Figure S3). While all ST11 isolates carried the agrD gene, most ST2 (96.8%) and all ST42 isolates did not. No association was found between agrD presence and acquisition type (p = 0.74). In contrast, its presence correlated with toxin production patterns (p = 0.0016, Figure S4); most isolates that were positive for agrD were either toxin B (51%) or toxin A + B (46%) producers. The presence of agrD was also associated with biofilm-production capacity (p < 0.0001, Fig. 7); most (65.3%) agrD-negative isolates were weak biofilm producers.

Discussion

Adjustment of both effective prevention strategies and treatment protocols is crucial for CDI management. To this end, periodic and detailed evaluation of CDI epidemiology is required.

Distribution of ST across study years and geographic regions

The observed differences in ST distribution over the study years aligned with the literature, which has shown changes in CDI epidemiology in the last decade. For example, in the mid-2000s, the epidemic strain Nap1/ST01 was prevalent in most parts of the world, while its incidence declined during 2007–2011, accompanied by emergence of new strains such as ribotype 078 (RT078) [23]. Changes in strain distribution may result from the high genetic evolution of C. difficile and its development of antibiotic resistance.

Data regarding the dominant ST strains in Israel are limited. Outbreaks of ST01/Nap1 were reported in 2015 [24], while ST04 and ST37 were the most frequent strains in a study conducted in 2017 [25]. Thus, distribution of C. difficile strains has been changing over the years and new strains have emerged.

Although the geographic distribution of specific STs varied, the differences were not statistically significant. In contrast, an inter-regional variation was reported in China, where ST2 and ST81 were common in north China, whereas ST54, ST3 and ST37 were more prevalent in the south [26]. To the best of our knowledge, no other studies characterized strain distribution within one country. Further studies should be performed to understand whether there are differences in ST distribution in different areas of one country. This information is valuable for prevention control and treatment strategies as specific strains are more violent than others.

Toxin production

The majority of strains in the current study produced both toxin A and B (55.7%), while 32.9% produced toxin B and 11.4% produced toxin A only. A similar distribution was observed in the 2017 study conducted by our group, although it only included samples from north Israel [25]. In contrast, a study conducted in 2020 in Camerron found a higher proportion of toxin B producers (55%) and lower percentage of toxin A + B producers (7.31%) [27]. Binary toxin was identified in 7.3% of study isolates. Studies in other countries found considerably smaller or higher percentage of isolates expressing this toxin [28–30]. Comparison of toxin patterns between different studies is complicated, as most studies do not assess the toxin proteins’ production, but, rather, test for gene presence. Moreover, Zhang et al. found a great variation in toxin gene expression across geographic regions [28], which suggest that toxin protein production also varies across countries. Furthermore, as shown here, some toxin expression patterns were associated with specific STs/Ribotypes (RTs), which can explain the correlation between toxins and strain distribution across geographic regions.

To best of our knowledge, no previous studies have tested the link between toxins patterns and ST. Yet, two studies suggested an association between RT and toxin gene patterns (toxinotype) [31, 32]. For example, all RT017 isolates had a toxinotype of A−B+ [32]. Further confirmation of the association between toxin patterns and ST will require analysis of a larger database.

Regarding infection acquisition type, more toxin AB producers were detected among isolates from hospitals as compared to isolates from community. While no previous study compared toxin production patterns between CA- and HA-CDI isolates, and virulence is not solely attributed by toxin production, the present finding may explain the higher prevalence of severe cases and higher mortality rate among HA-patients compared to CA-patients, as it is possible that strains that produce both toxins are more virulent, as compared to strains that produce one toxin type.

Biofilm production

Most of the isolates were weak biofilm producers. This findings is in sharp contrast to those of a previous study conducted in north Israel, which found that most isolates (from both HA- and CA- CDI) were either moderate or strong biofilm producers [33]. A study conducted in Mexico reported that most (87.25%) of the isolates were strong biofilm producers. However, most of their isolates were RT027, and may have all originated from the same strain [34]. The current study included isolates from a three-year period and from several locations in Israel, which may explain these variations.

Biofilm-production capacity was associated with ST, with the two most-common strains, ST42 and ST2, demonstrating higher capacity compared to other strains. This result strengthens previous reports according to which biofilm formation is strain-specific [10, 23, 35, 36]. C. difficile strain RT027 had the highest biofilm biomass among 20 isolates belonging to four RTs [36]. The authors hypothesized that the higher production of biofilms by RT027 strains may underlie their hyper-virulence.

The present work also noted that the same STs collected in different medical settings showed different biofilm-formation capacities. For example, most ST42 and ST11 isolates from HA-CDI patients were strong producers, while 92.3% of ST42 and 100% of ST11 CA-CDI isolates were weak biofilm producers. This raises a question whether ST characteristics can be influenced by origin/environment. In addition, it may explain the increased virulence of HA isolates, as manifested by higher biofilm-production capacity, which enable protection against antibiotics and contributes to infection persistence due to spore accumulation inside the biofilm [11].

It is important to note that most STs, including strains that belong to the same phylogenetic clade, are genetically divergent, due to frequent horizontal gene transfer and homologous recombination [37, 38]. If such an association between specific STs, infection origin and virulence factors truly exists, ST determination at CDI diagnosis may aid in identification of hypervirulent isolates and rapid adjustment of specific treatment.

Overall, strong-producer strains were more common in hospitals compared to the community. This correlates with the assumption that nosocomial strains may be more violent, and may elicit more severe CDI as compared to CA-CDI. The higher prevalence of strong-producer strains in hospitals calls for improved infection prevention strategies, with specific intervention against biofilms.

As biofilm was associated with CDI recurrence, it will be interesting to investigate whether HA-patients carrying stronger biofilm-producing strains experienced recurrent disease more frequently than CA-patients. Thus far, reports concerning recurrence rates of CDI in HA- vs. CA-patients are contradicting [39, 40].

Isolates producing two toxins were mostly moderate or strong biofilm producers. In contrast, isolates producing one toxin (A or B) were mostly weak biofilm producers. Biofilm and toxin production share common regulators, such as Spo0A, LexA and c-di-GMP [41]. However, most studies showed that mutations in these regulators have an opposite impact on biofilm and toxin productions [38, 42, 43]. Additionally, some studies determined toxins levels without separating toxin A levels from toxin B levels. Therefore, this point should be further studied in order to determine whether each toxin has its own regulators, which may also regulate biofilm formation. If toxin-specific regulators are identified, it may mean that a strain that produces both toxins is more virulent than a strain producing one toxin, not just because of the action of the toxins, but also because the strain produces a stronger biofilm.

Virulence-associated genes

agrD was the only virulence gene associated with ST. A broader investigation of the QS system of different STs will be needed to confirm these results. agrD is part of the QS system in C. difficile. In S. aureus, this system constitutes agrA, which encodes a response transcriptional regulator, agrB, which encodes a protease, agrC, which encodes a sensor histidine kinase and agrD, which encodes the signaling peptide. AgrD processing by AgrB into a thiolactone-containing autoinducing peptide (AIP) occurs during bacterial growth. AIP accumulation outside the bacterial cell induces a cascade that eventually results in altered gene expression [44]. Three forms of the Agr system (Agr 1–3) were demonstrated in C. difficile strains, each with a different organization of the agr genes; Agr1 was found in all strains, while Agr2 and Agr3 were found in RT027 and RT078, respectively. Agr1 includes AgrB1 and AgrD1 genes [44].

Presence of agrD correlated with toxin production patterns, with most agrD-positive isolates being either toxin B (51%) producers or Toxins A and B (46%) producers. Yet, one report demonstrated that deletion of agrD1 resulted in a 3-fold increase in tcdA expression levels, with no effect on tcdB expression levels [44]. Future studies will be needed to further characterize the link between agrD on C. difficile toxin production.

The presence of agrD was also associated with biofilm production, with most of the agrD-negative isolates being weak biofilm producers. This finding correlates with a former study in Listeria monocytogenes, which showed that deletion of agrD resulted in reduced biofilm formation [45]. However, as agrD was only present in 21.2% of the isolates in the current study, further studies should be performed to understand agrD involvement, whether exist, in biofilm production.

One limitation of the study is the univariate data analysis. Future study should take into consideration possible confounders.

Conclusions

C. difficile strains in this study exhibited different toxin profiles and biofilm-production capabilities, which may be clinically meaningful. Overall, HA-CDI isolates tended to display higher expression of some virulence factors as compared to CA-CDI and may require different management. Differences between strains can explain why some patients develop mild disease while others develop severe disease, and why some patients suffer recurrences. Further profiling of strains may facilitate therapeutic decisions and development of infection prevention strategies.

The presented results highlight the need for continuous monitoring of C. difficile strain distribution and characteristics. This can be fulfilled by the foundation of a national reference center for C. difficile. Such a center should incorporate data regarding strains from all over Israel, which will be valuable in shaping of both treatment protocols and preventive plans.
