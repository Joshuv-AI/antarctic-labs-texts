Development of quantitative PET/MR imaging for measurements of hepatic portal vein input function: a phantom study
Authors: Zacharias Chalampalakis, Markus Ortner, Masar Almuttairi, Martin Bauer, Ernesto Gomez Tamm, Albrecht Ingo Schmidt, Barbara Katharina Geist, Marcus Hacker, Oliver Langer, Roberta Frass-Kriegl, Ivo Rausch
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate pharmacokinetic modelling in PET necessitates measurements of an input function, which ideally is acquired non-invasively from image data. For hepatic pharmacokinetic modelling two input functions need to be considered, to account for the blood supply from the hepatic artery and portal vein. Image-derived measurements at the portal vein are challenging due to its small size and image artifacts caused by respiratory motion. In this work we seek to demonstrate, using phantom experiments, how a dedicated PET/MR protocol can tackle these challenges and potentially provide input function measurements of the portal vein in a clinical setup.MethodsA custom 3D printed PET/MR phantom was constructed to mimic the liver and portal vein. PET/MR acquisitions were made with emulated respiratory motion. The PET/MR imaging protocol consisted of high-resolution anatomical MR imaging of the portal vein, followed by a PET acquisition in parallel to a dedicated motion-tracking MR sequence. Motion tracking and deformation information were extracted from PET data and subsequently used in PET reconstruction to produce dynamic series of motion-free PET images. Anatomical MR images were used post PET reconstruction for partial volume correction of the input function measurements.ResultsReconstruction of dynamic PET data with motion-compensation provided nearly motion-free series of PET frame data, suitable for image derived input function measurements of the portal vein. After partial volume correction, the individual input function measurements were within a 16.1% error range from the true activity in the portal vein compartment at the time of PET acquisition.ConclusionThe proposed protocol demonstrates clinically feasible PET/MR imaging of the liver for pharmacokinetic studies with accurate quantification of the portal vein input function, including correction for respiratory motion and partial volume effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40658-024-00694-4.Keywords: PET/MR, Portal vein, Input function, Motion correction, Liver phantom

Background

Positron emission tomography (PET) is a well-established non-invasive imaging modality used to visualize and quantify functional and metabolic processes in vivo. It is widely used in routine cancer diagnosis, staging and follow-up. Likewise, it is also a valuable tool in experimental medicine to study physiological or pathophysiological processes, and in drug development to answer important questions related to the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of drugs [1]. Dynamic PET imaging is important for studying the organ and tissue kinetics of radiotracers after being introduced to the body. Mathematical models can be applied to the organ and tissue kinetics of radiotracers to obtain parameters that quantitatively describe the underlying biological process. These models commonly require information of the amount of available tracer over time, which serves as the input function to the model [2].

Non-invasive measurements of the input function (IF) from PET image data are preferred over invasive arterial blood sampling for reasons of safety and ease of implementation in research and clinical studies. Measurements of image-derived input functions (IDIF) are made from dynamic PET series to extract a time-activity curve (TAC) from blood pools that are available within the PET field of view (FOV). Unfortunately, IDIF measurements are susceptible to partial volume effects due to the limited resolution of PET imaging. Thus, large blood pools are preferred for IDIF measurements, in which a volume of interest (VOI) can be carefully positioned to avoid spill-out and spill-in effects, such as the heart cavities or parts of the aorta. When this is not possible, due to the small size of the available vessels, partial volume correction (PVC) must be applied to the IDIF measurements [3].

Among the use cases of PET, the liver is an organ of specific interest. The liver is a major clearance organ for drugs [4] and PET with radiolabeled drugs has been used to assess their hepatic disposition and biliary excretion [5–7]. Dynamic PET of the liver can not only be used for drug disposition studies, but it has also been shown to be potentially useful in the e.g. assessment of hepatocellular carcinoma [8] or the evaluation of other liver diseases [9]. In contrast to most major organs, the liver is supplied with blood from two vessels, the hepatic artery (HA) and the portal vein (PV), which are assumed to account for approximately 25% and 75% of the total blood supply respectively [10, 11]. An IDIF that is representative of the HA can be easily derived from PET image data of the abdominal aortic region. But for the PV, which accounts for most of the liver blood supply, an IDIF cannot be derived from any other major vessel. Direct IDIF measurements of the PV are highly susceptible to partial volume effects due to its small size (of the order of 10 mm in diameter) and further compromised by respiratory motion during PET acquisition [12].

Animal studies have been conducted in the past to study the IF characteristics of the PV, and to model its behavior in relation to the arterial IF. The derived models account for delay and dispersion, as a result of tracer passage through the splanchnic area before reaching the PV [13]. Although attempts have been made to create population-based PV-IF models, population values need to be tracer-specific to account for tracer-dependent dispersion characteristics [14]. Optimization-derived PV-IF methods, where the IF is estimated at the same time as the kinetic model, have been proposed and demonstrate variation of the PV-IF across small samples of patients [15]. Although this optimization based methodology is appealing and demonstrates the need for individual case-by-case estimation of the PV-IF, the optimization based methods can be sensitive to noise in the TAC data and require careful evaluation in their behavior for each use-case with different kinetic models [9]. As such, a measured PV-IF is preferable, but since the PV is not accessible to sampling in humans, measurements necessitate the use of an IDIF.

Fully integrated PET/ Magnetic Resonance (MR) Imaging devices have the means to tackle the challenges in dynamic liver PET. The high-resolution anatomical details gained from MRI can be used to identify the PV and correct for PET partial volume effects, enabling the extraction of an IDIF. Furthermore, fast MRI sequences can be acquired simultaneous to dynamic PET, providing motion information that can be used in the PET reconstruction process for motion compensation [16].

This work aims at demonstrating a clinically feasible protocol for dynamic liver imaging using an integrated PET/MR system. Specifically, the aim was to make use of MR-based motion information to produce motion-free dynamic PET imaging data of the liver without compromising on acquisition count statistics. Furthermore, we aimed to use high-resolution anatomical MR images of the PV to account for partial volume effects in PET measurements and estimated an accurate PV-IF. To validate the proposed methodology, we developed a custom liver phantom designed to emulate realistic acquisition conditions, while ensuring precise knowledge of the activity levels within the liver and portal vein (PV).

Materials and methods

Liver Phantom design

A 3D printed fillable liver phantom was used in this study to emulate IDIF measurements of a PV and compare with the ground truth. A contrast enhanced Computed Tomography (CT) image was used as the basis for the design of the liver 3D model. To fit the model within the printer limits, the overall dimensions of the liver were scaled down to approximately half of its original dimensions in each direction. By contrast, the portal vein was modelled at original dimensions to maintain the same PET/MR acquisition characteristics in the experiments. The inferior vena cava was also included in the model, as it touches with the liver. Wall size thickness of the PV and vena cava were kept below 1.5 mm to avoid influence of the non-visible plastic material on PET images.

The final model, shown in Fig. 1, consists of 3 fillable compartments which are sealed with luer locks. The total volume of the 3D printed liver is 465 cm3. The included portal vein compartment measures between 15 mm and 10 mm in diameter, as it continuously changes in size while entering the liver. The portal vein compartment measures 75 mm across its maximum length. These values are representative of the expected portal vein dimensions [12]. The scaled vena cava measures approximately 15 mm in diameter across its modeled length.

Illustration and dimensions of the designed 3D liver model with Inferior-Posterior (A), Anterior-Posterior (C) view and an axial cut-view through the portal vein insert of the model (B)

The model was printed in a 3D printer (Form 3B+, Formlabs, USA) using the clear resin V5 material (Formlabs, USA) and attached to the torso-section of a custom-built MR phantom that was used to simulate respiratory motion, as described in the phantom experiments section below.

PET-MR acquisition protocol

The suggested PET/MR acquisition protocol in this study is summarized in Fig. 2. This was designed based on the available capabilites of the mMR PET/MR scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). In this protocol, before tracer administration, high-resolution anatomical MR images are acquired using a breath hold protocol to avoid motion artifacts. An accelerated gradient echo sequence (True Fast Imaging with Steady-State Free Precession, TRUFI-3D) is used with isotropic 1.2 mm voxel resolution (repetition time (TR): 478.91 ms, echo time (TE): 1.92 ms, flip angle (FA): 29 °, FOV: 265 × 273 mm), in which blood vessels have high contrast and can be easily segmented.

Timeline diagram of a PET-MR acquisition protocol using the proposed methodology for image derived portal vein input function estimation of the initial 4 min post PET tracer injection. Following the completion of the BodyCOMPASS™ sequence, PET acquisition and other MR sequences can proceed for the duration needed in the protocol

Following this short acquisition, an MR attenuation correction (MRAC) DIXON sequence (TR: 3.96 ms, TE: 1.23 ms, FA: 9 °, FOV: 312 × 500 mm) is acquired synchronously with the start of PET acquisition for producing the necessary attenuation maps. Following these two acquisitions, the BodyCOMPASS™ (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) motion tracking sequence is performed with free breathing for approximately 4 min (TR: 2.97 ms, TE: 1.32 ms, FA: 10 °, FOV: 400 × 400 mm). This is a stack-of-stars MRI sequence that radially samples the k-space while also recording a respiratory trace from the center of k-space (referred to as self-gating signal) [17]. The signal is recorded at a frequency of approximately 7 Hz and saved in the BodyCOMPASS™ acquisition raw data. The acquired MR data are automatically split and reconstructed in 5 respiratory gates by the mMR system, which are then used to estimate deformations between gates for use with PET reconstruction and obtain motion-free PET images. In this work we did not reconstruct the PET data using the PET/MR system, as the PET motion correction implementation of the BodyCOMPASS™ is limited to static PET reconstructions only. Instead, we made use of the recorded respiratory trace for offline PET reconstruction with motion compensation.

In a hypothetical clinical protocol using the proposed methodology, PET tracer administration would be set to start with the BodyCOMPASS™ sequence in order to have motion tracking data available from the beginning of tracer arrival to the liver (by its blood supplying vessels).

After the completion of the BodyCOMPASS™ sequence, PET acquisition and other MR sequences can proceed for the duration needed in the study. For the phantom experiments in this work, no further PET or MR acquisitions were made after the run of the BodyCOMPASS™ sequence.

PET reconstruction

The PET data were extracted and reconstructed off-line using the CASToR open-source reconstruction platform [18]. During reconstruction, for each update using the data of each gate a forward and backward transformation was used to result in reconstructed images at the reference position. This strategy ensures the use of all data statistics within the reconstruction loop for each frame of the dynamic series and produces motion-compensated PET images. The respiratory trace extracted from the MR BodyCOMPASS™ sequence was used to split the data into gates of different respiratory phases, as described further in the Motion estimation section.

The PET data were reconstructed into a dynamic series of 12 × 20 s frames using the OSEM algorithm (2it21sub). The framing duration was chosen on the rationale that after input function dispersion, 20 s frames would be adequate for capturing the input function shape characteristics (see supplementary material).

All available corrections were applied during reconstruction, namely normalization, attenuation, scatter and random corrections. Due to the image transformation accounted within the reconstruction, corrections that were initially calculated for the reference position can be used during these gated reconstructions. The attenuation maps for each acquisition were generated using a previous CT scan of the phantom, after manual registration to anatomical MR images of each experiment. The use of the CT image was necessary as the phantom contains several plastic components which do not produce MR signal and therefore are not visible in the generated MRAC. Normalization, scatter and random corrections were extracted from Siemens off-line reconstruction tools and used in CASToR off-line reconstructions. The applied corrections are matching the corrections used in standard clinical PET/MR practice. No time-of-flight was used in the reconstruction as the mMR system does not offer time-of-flight information. For comparison with standard PET reconstructions without motion compensation, reconstructions of a single gate at end-expiration were performed for all experiments and included in the analysis.

Motion estimation

The BodyCOMPASS™ sequence provides MR images for each gate position, which can be used for subsequent registration of gated images or within static PET reconstruction as described above. In this work, the MR visible components of the phantom did not produce a strong MR signal, such as the signal expected from a human torso with tissue surrounding the liver. As a result, the MR images for each gate were deteriorated by strong streak artifacts. Therefore, in our experiments, we made use of the MR respiratory trace to reconstruct static PET images for each gate, as seen for in a single acquisition example in the middle row of Fig. 6. Motion between gates was estimated using a well validated tool for registration using PET data [19], and since in our experiments motion was limited to linear translations in the cranio-caudal direction, only rigid registration parameters were estimated. In particular, the gate that represented end-expiration was used as the target, to which all other gates were registered to.

Gated PET and MR images corresponding to the five distinct gates of the respiratory cycle in experiment 5s35mm. The PET images (top row) were used for the image registration between the gate positions, while MR images (bottom row) were not used for image registration of this particular phantom due to the presence of strong streak artifacts

For comparison with the ground truth phantom displacement, the location data from the piezoelectric controller were averaged over individual gate positions and reported for comparison. PET count-rate data (prompts) were also extracted from the PET list-mode data to evaluate if they could be a suitable alternative to the MR trace for retrospective data driven gating using PET data alone.

Phantom experiments

The 3D printed phantom was fitted within the abdomen section of a custom-built dynamic torso phantom, as seen in Fig. 3, which has been developed for MRI motion simulations [20, 21]. The dynamic torso phantom is designed to simulate 3 different motion types (i.e., breathing motion of the chest wall, the heart and the abdominal organs, as well as heartbeat motion) using non-magnetic linear piezoelectric motors (Xeryon, Belgium) and a full-plastic pneumatic system. In our experiments we made use of a single piezoelectric motor to emulate respiratory motion of the liver along the cranio-caudal direction.

The experimental setup of the 3D printed liver phantom, positioned within the abdomen section of a phantom designed to simulate respiratory motion in the cranio-caudal direction. The motion is driven by the displayed piezoelectric motors

Measurements with the proposed PET/MR protocol were made initially without motion and then repeated for 3 different motion patterns. The motion patterns differed in respiratory cycle period and the range of motion. Namely the experiments 5s20mm, 5s35mm and 7s20mm were conducted with 5 s, 5 s and 7 s respiratory cycle period and 20 mm, 35 mm and 20 mm range of motion respectively. All measurements were made on an mMR PET/MR scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). The motion patterns were limited to linear shifts between selected maximum-expiration and -inspiration points, with varying respiration periods. Real time feedback from the piezoelectric controller enabled precise recording of the displacement over time with 0.1 s sampling rate.

The liver compartment was filled with an [18F]FDG activity concentration of 4.1 kBq/cm3, the portal vein with 78.8 kBq/cm3 and the vena cava with 17.6 kBq/cm3. These values were approximately selected based on a range of expected values from arterial IF sampling and liver uptake in FDG studies. The activity used to fill the various compartments of the phantom was prepared in separate stock solutions. The activity used in the preparation of each solution was measured using a dose calibrator, which was cross-calibrated with the PET/MR system. All activity values in this work are reported with decay correction to the start of the first experiment.

Discussion

This study aims to demonstrate that the use of dedicated PET/MR acquisition protocols enables accurate measurement of the PV-IF from PET image data. We successfully and reproducibly tackled challenges related to the small size of the portal vein and motion artifacts caused by respiration. To develop and demonstrate the use of such a protocol, we conducted phantom experiments using a custom-made liver phantom with 3 different fillable compartments. As the PV in humans is inaccessible for direct measurements without surgical intervention, this phantom work was designed to aid in the development and evaluation of the dedicated PET/MR protocol under realistic conditions. Motion during measurements was induced by incorporating the liver phantom in a torso MR phantom that emulates respiratory motion using a piezoelectric motor. The phantom measurements served as the ground truth for the evaluation of the IDIF quantification. Following PET motion compensation and PVC of PV-IDIF measurements, the accuracy of the measurements over all experiments was found to be within a 16.1% error range for individual measurements and 5.1% error in average for measurements of each experiment.

Integrated PET/MR systems combine two very well-established modalities used in patient care, allowing for synchronous acquisition of high quality functional and anatomical information. Clinical applications span from single organ studies such as in neuroimaging [22] to whole-body examinations, with a major focus on oncology studies [23]. Clinical research applications of PET/MR have more recently expanded to multi-parametric imaging by combining multiple novel MR sequences and dynamic PET [24]. One of the weaknesses of PET imaging is the presence of artifacts caused by motion, mainly respiratory motion which can cause blurring of images of abdominal organs and structures. Integrated PET/MR, owing to its synchronous acquisition capability, offers unique solutions for motion tracking and motion estimation without the need for external devices or additional scanning time [16].

In our experiments motion was accurately tracked with the dedicated BodyCOMPASS™ sequence of the mMR scanner, which does not require the use of external devices and can be easily incorporated in a dynamic PET/MR protocol [17]. In addition to motion tracking, the use of high-resolution MR imaging can permit the delineation of the portal vein, due to the high-contrast PV images from the TRUFI-3D sequence. In this work the delineation of the PV was made manually, but automated techniques could have easily been employed. For clinical applications of the proposed protocol, thresholding-based segmentation or artificial intelligence (AI) solutions for MR image segmentation [25] can be applied to aid automation and improve segmentation reproducibility.

Our study has limitations which need to be considered before generalizing our findings to clinical PET/MR protocols. Our PV-IDIF measurements were accurate within a range of approximately 16.1%, but our measurements exhibited higher noise when compared to the those made on the phantom without induced motion. The maximum increase seen is approximately 150% as measured by the changes in standard deviation. This noise increase in turn is caused by the noise increase seen in the motion compensated PET images, compared to the motion free acquisition images. This effect can also be seen in the liver background VOI measurements presented in the supplementary material.

Although the reconstruction method used to produce motion compensated PET images uses all the acquired data with approximately the same acquisition statistics as in the case of the phantom experiment without motion, the result images exhibit more noise. This could be attributed to the addition of repeated backward and forward image transformations within each loop of the iterative reconstruction, which could result in small interpolation errors being amplified with each iteration resulting in an increase of image noise. A simulation study would be required to look deeper into how the repeated transformations affect the final image. This increase in noise of PV-IDIF measurements has the potential to affect the results of kinetic modelling, to a degree that would depend on the model. Nevertheless, the coefficient of variation is kept well below 10%, which is less than half in comparison to measurements taken from PET images of end-expiration gate data alone (comparison with Supplementary Table 2).

Regarding our experimental setup, our phantom is realistic in the anatomical structure of the PV, but the induced motion was limited to simple translations along the longitudinal axis. Although this is the predominant direction of motion, in a real clinical case the motion of the liver is expected to be elastic within the breathing cycle and the cycle pattern to be varying with time. Nevertheless, the registration method used in this study between PET gates can be extended to account for elastic motion registration, as validated before [19], which then can be included in motion compensated PET reconstruction. Furthermore, MR gated images can also be produced and used for the registration process, as intended for the Biocompass sequence [17]. In this phantom work we did not make use of the gated MR images due to corruption by streak artifacts (Fig. 6), which did not permit the use of standard registration techniques. The streak artifacts can be attributed to the very small MR signal produced by the fillable volume of the phantom in combination with the sharp signal drop at the phantom edges as it is surrounded by air. Although these artifacts are not expected to be as pronounced in human studies, techniques to suppress them can be employed and allow for MR registration [26]. Varying breathing patterns can also be accounted for by the MR respiratory tracking. These types of breathing patterns were not tested in this work as the piezoelectric internal position tracking operates only for fixed triangular waveform type of motion patterns.

In addition to the use of the PET instead of the MR for motion tracking, this phantom work necessitated the use of an CT based attenuation map due to the amount of plastic materials in the FOV. In clinical use of the protocol, this would not be necessary and the MRAC can be applied similarly to most standard clinical protocols in PET/MR.

The proposed PET/MR protocol, as outlined in Fig. 2, can be easily incorporated in a routine clinical dynamic protocol. Assuming that the desired bed position is centered over the liver, the MR sequences necessary for the PV delineation and motion tracking require only 4 min of additional MR time. No additional hardware or external devices are required. The PET framing used in this work can be adjusted to the needs of the clinical protocol and kinetic modeling, without requiring any changes in the presented methodology. This can include shorter frames and frames of varying duration over time.

The duration of the motion tracking sequence is adjustable but limited by the specific absorption rate (SAR), so as to not prohibit the run of other desired MR sequences in the protocol. The run of the sequence for approximately 4 min is a good compromise between capturing information during the initial phase of the PV input function and the SAR exposure. Following those initial few minutes, for many tracers (including FDG) the PV input function can be considered approximately equal to the arterial IF and as such easily estimated from other measurements of large artery vessels in the FOV [10]. Nevertheless, the duration of the sequence can be adjusted based on the needs of each tracer and protocol, at the cost of higher SAR exposure which can restrict the inclusion of additional MR sequences in the protocol. Following the completion of the proposed PET/MR protocol, the scan can continue with more PET acquisition time and additional MR sequences. If motion tracking is required for the remaining clinical protocol, a breathing belt or PET signal fluctuations can be used to monitor the breathing cycle. This information can be applied similarly to our proposed protocol, utilizing the pre-estimated motion vector fields, for PET and MR reconstructions.

Although commercial PET/MR systems have been available for more than a decade, their integration in clinical routine has been slow. This can be in part attributed to limited availability of such systems, to contraindications that do not allow the use of MR with all patients and to quantification inaccuracies due to discrepancies caused by vendor’s attenuation correction methods [27]. For such reasons it would also be of great interest to translate the findings of this work to PET/CT systems, which do not pose these limitations but on the other hand cannot provide the unique MR information on which the proposed protocol is based upon. Moreover, with the advancement of total body PET/CT systems there is increasing interest in performing kinetic modelling over the whole-body, for which the PV input function needs to be considered for accurate modelling of the liver.

For motion tracking, we briefly explored the use of the PET count rate over time as a substitute for the MR tracking sequence (Fig. 4). Although the count rate curve exhibits more noise compared to the MR tracking curve, it can provide similar information regarding the phase of the respiratory motion and triggers for gating. The comparison would need to be conducted with real clinical studies to verify that these phantom findings hold equally true with patients. In addition, more sophisticated methods can be used for extraction of PET data-driven gating signals [28], many of which are these days also available on commercial PET/CT systems.

The second challenge in the protocol’s translation to PET/CT would be the anatomical delineation of the PV on CT images. Standard quality CT images, acquired in regular PET/CT protocols, provide poor contrast of the PV. Although attempts have been made for PV segmentations from CT data [29, 30], the low image contrast of the PV makes these segmentations unreliable. The quality of the segmentation is not only important for completely capturing the activity within the PV, but also for accurate PVC of the measurements. A contrast-enhanced high-resolution CT could provide good contrast for delineation but is not desirable due to the added radiation dose given to the patient by the additional CT scan. AI tools, as for example for the creation of synthetic contrast enhanced CT from non-contrast CT images [31], could be specifically trained with focus on the liver area for PV contrast enhancement from non-contrast CT images. Alternatively, AI segmentation tools could specifically be trained for accurate delineation of the PV from unenhanced CT images, if a suitable training dataset is available.

Conclusion

We have demonstrated the use of a dedicated protocol on an integrated PET/MR system for accurate measurements of the PVIF from PET image data, by successfully accounting for motion and for PVC on the PET data. The proposed protocol does not make use of any additional hardware and can be easily included in existing PET/MR protocols.
