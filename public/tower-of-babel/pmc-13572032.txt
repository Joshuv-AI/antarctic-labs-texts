Optimizing Endoscopic Assessment of Postoperative Crohn's Disease: A Scoping Review
Authors: Sarianne G Bosch, Michiel T J Bak, Sun‐Ho Lee, Oddeke Van Ruler, Léon Maggiori, Johan Burisch, Jakob B Seidelin, Byong Duk Ye, Sang Hyoung Park, Mark S Silverberg, Matthieu Allez, Annemarie C DeVries, Nassim Hammoudi, the IMPACT consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPostoperative endoscopic recurrence after ileocecal resection in Crohn's disease (CD) occurs frequently and typically precedes clinical and surgical relapse. Endoscopic scoring systems are central to postoperative management, yet their optimal use remains debated. The aim was to provide a summary and critical appraisal of established and novel endoscopic scoring systems used for recurrence in adult CD.MethodsA scoping review was conducted based on a systematic search in PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library databases. Following qualitative synthesis, narrative reviews and studies involving pediatric or animal populations were excluded. Remaining evidence was integrated and critically appraised. The interpretation of findings was further refined through expert discussion within the IMPACT consortium.ResultsThe Rutgeerts score (RS) remains the most widely used index in clinical studies, but shows important limitations, including moderate interobserver reproducibility, limited differentiation between anastomotic and ileal lesions, and reduced applicability to modern anastomotic configurations. Modified and updated RS variants, as well as newer indices, emphasize lesion location and postoperative anatomy, and may improve prognostic stratification in selected cohorts. The choice of postoperative endoscopic index and the threshold used to define recurrence has major clinical implications. Heterogeneity in anatomic lesions considered, cutoffs for adapting treatments, and reproducibility limit the universal adoption of any single index.ConclusionEndoscopic indices remain essential for classification and standardization for postoperative disease recurrence in CD but should be interpreted together with their current level of validation. These findings highlight the need for further validation, harmonization of definitions, and refinement of postoperative endoscopic evaluation.

Background

Despite the growing arsenal of immunosuppressive therapies, ileocecal resection (ICR) remains a common intervention for patients with Crohn's disease (CD) [1]. Endoscopic postoperative recurrence (ePOR) within the first year after surgery occurs frequently in approximately 50% of patients [2, 3]. ePOR is known to precede both clinical and surgical recurrence and its prevention is important to avoid poor long‐term outcomes [4, 5, 6].

To prevent ePOR, accurate identification of patients at high risk is of clinical importance. The ECCO‐IBD guidelines identify smoking at time of surgery, penetrating disease behavior, prior surgical resection, perianal disease, granulomas in the resection material, myenteric plexitis, and absence of postoperative prophylactic treatment as established clinical risk factors for postoperative recurrence (POR) [7]. In contrast, the American guidelines state active smoking at time of surgery, ≥ 2 prior surgeries for penetrating disease with or without perianal disease, or younger age < 30 years as clinical risk factors [8]. Evidence supporting risk factors remains inconsistent across studies, reflecting substantial heterogeneity in the literature [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Notably, the recent study from REMIND group identified prior exposure to biologic therapy as a risk factor for POR and a recent systematic review that included studies from 2000 onward, only found consistent evidence for active smoking at time of surgery and previous bowel resection [14, 15].

Especially since risk stratification is difficult, early reassessment of postoperative strategy is mandatory. Postoperative ileocolonoscopy within 6–12 months after surgery is considered the gold standard for detection of recurrence and guidance for therapeutic intensification [7, 8, 16]. A reliable and reproducible endoscopic scoring system is particularly important, as postoperative treatment strategies are directly influenced by endoscopic findings. Currently, the Rutgeerts score (RS) and its modified versions remain the most widely used indices for assessing ePOR in clinical practice and clinical trials, even though their prognostic value was never validated. Several modifications and alternative scoring systems have been recently developed in response to postoperative lesion heterogeneity, intra‐observer variability, and changes in surgical techniques and anastomotic configurations [2, 17, 18, 19].

In response to these clinical challenges and the uncertainties in the developing field of postoperative endoscopic scoring, this review aims to provide a summary and critical appraisal of established and novel endoscopic scoring systems used for assessing ePOR in CD and gives an expert‐based opinion on the optimal approach to postoperative endoscopic evaluation.

Methods

This review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) guidelines [20]. A review of the literature was performed in December 2025. PubMed, EMBASE, the Cochrane Library, and congress abstracts were searched for articles published in English. The detailed search strategy is provided in Appendix 1 and the PRISMA‐ScR checklist is provided in Appendix 2.

The search resulted in 1046 studies eligible for screening. Studies that evaluated an endoscopic scoring system for POR in adult CD were included in a qualitative synthesis by two authors (NH and SB), with particular focus on studies describing the interobserver variability and predictive value for long‐term postoperative outcomes. Studies conducted in pediatric populations or animal models were excluded, as well as review articles. No restrictions were applied regarding publication date or study design. In addition, to minimize the risk of selection bias arising from the omission of relevant studies, articles not identified through the search were included following manual review by both authors. Findings were summarized narratively, highlighting areas of consistency and gaps in the existing literature, thereby providing an integrated overview of the current state of knowledge. Experts from the newly formed International Multidisciplinary Postoperative CD Consortium (IMPACT—bringing together international experts in postoperative CD) critically reviewed this draft to obtain this final version.

Endoscopic Assessment of POR Is the Gold Standard

The assessment of recurrence solely based on clinical symptoms is unreliable, as many studies consistently demonstrated a discordance between clinical and endoscopic recurrence rates [4, 5, 21]. Regueiro et al. reported for instance poor agreement between the Crohn's Disease Activity Index (CDAI) and concurrent endoscopic recurrence, defined as RS > i2 [21].

For this reason, an objective evaluation is important in the postoperative setting. The current gold standard for assessing POR is ileocolonoscopy between 6 and 12 months after surgery [7, 8]. This recommended timing was adopted after the POCER trial demonstrated that early treatment optimization based on a standard 6‐month ileocolonoscopy significantly improved long‐term recurrence outcomes, a finding that was later confirmed [5, 16, 22, 23]. However, the definition of long‐term recurrence remains inconsistent across studies, warranting clearer and standardized outcome definitions [24]. In this context, the present scoping review focuses specifically on the assessment and interpretation of early ePOR.

The First Endoscopic Scores Developed: CDEIS And SES CD

The Crohn's Disease Endoscopic Index of Severity (CDEIS) represents one of the earliest attempts to quantify endoscopic disease activity in CD. By evaluating deep and superficial ulcerations, the extent of surface involvement, and the presence of stenosis across five predefined bowel segments, the CDEIS provides a sensitive assessment of mucosal inflammation (Table 1) [25]. Developed and published in the late 1980s, the score demonstrated a strong correlation with the endoscopist's global assessment of lesion severity (r = 0.83) and was subsequently prospectively validated in a series of 113 colonoscopies (r = 0.81), establishing its validity. Owing to these methodological strengths, the CDEIS became the reference endoscopic scoring system for luminal CD in clinical trials and has long served as a benchmark in research settings [26]. However, no study specifically evaluating the prognosis of CDEIS in the postoperative setting has been published.

Later, the Simple Endoscopic Score—Crohn's Disease (SES‐CD) was developed to provide a simplified but precise measure of endoscopic CD disease activity by quantifying four key lesion characteristics: ulcer size, ulcerated surface, affected surface, and narrowing (Table 1). These features are scored from 0 to 3 across five predefined bowel segments. In luminal CD, the SES‐CD demonstrated good responsiveness to disease activity changes [27]. Owing to its relative simplicity, reproducibility, and ability to reflect global disease burden, it has become one of the most used endoscopic indices in clinical trials and routine practice [26, 28].

Additionally, in 2021, a monocentric work including 207 patients operated for CD (75% with ileal lesions) showed that a SES‐CD > 2 at the first postoperative ileocolonoscopy was significantly associated with subsequent clinical recurrence (p < 0.001). Interestingly, optimal prognostic thresholds differed according to disease distribution: a SES‐CD of 1 was sufficient to predict clinical recurrence in patients with isolated small bowel disease, whereas a higher cutoff of 5 was required in colon‐dominant disease [29]. More recently, a modified SES‐CD score was developed: increased weighting was assigned based on baseline ulcer size, extent of ulceration, presence of non‐passable strictures, and disease located within certain segments of the bowel such as the ileum, as these were demonstrated to have the strongest association with 1‐year endoscopic remission [30]. By emphasizing ileal disease, this approach may be conceptually more adapted with postoperative CD. In a post hoc analysis of the PREVENT trial, Narula et al. assessed the prognostic value of the modified SES‐CD at week 76 for very long‐term clinical recurrence [31]. Both the original and modified SES‐CD performed comparably, with similar area under curve (AUC) to the RS (cutoff: i2) in predicting subsequent clinical recurrence, using a threshold of ≥ 26 for the modified SES‐CD. Additionally, combining components of the SES‐CD and the CDEIS, the novel continuous Endoscopic ulcer Activity Score for Evaluating Crohn's Disease (EASE‐CD) score adds evaluation of ulcer size, depth, and extent. However, it does not assess strictures, and its prognostic value requires further validation [32].

A key advantage of the CDEIS, SES‐CD, and its modified version lie in their ability to capture colonic recurrence, in contrast to ileum‐focused postoperative scores. However, when applied to postoperative CD, several features limit CDEIS and SES‐CD applicability. First, their complexity and the time required for their calculation limit their feasibility. More importantly, the framework of those scores assumes native bowel anatomy and fails to account for postoperative‐specific features, particularly the presence of an anastomosis.

The Rutgeerts Score

The Original Rutgeerts Score

The most recognized endoscopic scoring system dedicated to assessment of POR is the RS [4]. It was designed to grade lesions at the neo‐terminal ileum and ileocecal anastomosis stratifying their severity into five categories based on number, size and distribution of ulcerations, and the extent of mucosal inflammation between ulcerations (Table 1).

The RS has been shown to correlate with postoperative outcomes. A significant relationship between lower (i0–i1) and higher (i2–i4) scores in predicting surgical and clinical recurrence was observed in the landmark study and confirmed later on [31, 33, 34]. Consequently, it rapidly became the gold standard for grading the severity of ePOR and is widely used in routine clinical practice, even though it was never prospectively validated [35].

However, several other limitations have emerged. The original RS was developed for end‐to‐end anastomoses, whereas side‐to‐side anastomoses are now commonly performed and other types emerged in CD surgery (Figure 1). As a result, it may underestimate disease activity in regions not present in end‐to‐end configurations [10, 19, 36]. Furthermore, the score does not distinguish between ileal and anastomotic lesions, as an i2 score may reflect either neo‐terminal ileal or anastomotic lesions. Isolated anastomotic lesions are frequently observed and their pathophysiology remains incompletely understood. As they are more common in patients with CD as compared to those who underwent right hemicolectomy for colon cancer and have been associated with an increased long‐term risk of POR, their development may appear to be driven by CD rather than ischemia [37, 38, 39, 40]. Moreover, a prospective study underlined the concept of anastomotic lesions as a distinct postoperative entity, as 40% of patients with such lesions experienced endoscopic progression during follow‐up [37]. However, local ischemia‐related factors may partly contribute to lesion development, a hypothesis supported by their occurrence in patients without CD and observed differences in patterns of anastomotic ulcerations between everted and inverted stapled anastomoses [18, 40]. Indeed, a recent study demonstrated that inverted stapling, in which the bowel edges are folded into the lumen, was associated with more ulcerations at the anastomotic stapled line at the postoperative endoscopy, possibly due to secondary wound healing [18].

Modified Rutgeerts Score (mRS)

The lack of distinction between anastomotic and ileal lesions, led to the development of the modified RS (mRS). This score differentiates i2 lesions of the original RS into i2a for lesions limited to the anastomosis, and i2b for > 5 ulcerations surrounded by normal mucosa in the neo‐terminal ileum [41, 42].

Some studies reported no significant differences between the i2a and i2b lesions regarding clinical or surgical POR [43, 44]. Conversely, multiple retrospective and prospective studies highlighted the prognostic importance of distinguishing anastomotic from ileal lesions, demonstrating worse long‐term outcomes in patients with ileal involvement at postoperative endoscopy [6, 45, 46, 47]. Notably, some analyses reported that i2a lesions did not differ significantly from i0–i1 disease, whereas i2b lesions were consistently associated with a higher risk of severe endoscopic postoperative recurrence and re‐resection [6, 46]. Interestingly, in some studies, i1 lesions were even associated with a higher risk of clinical recurrence than i2a, depending on whether inflammation was confined to the anastomosis or involved the neo‐terminal ileum [45]. These observations have raised the questions about whether a ≥ i2b threshold on the mRS is appropriate to define POR and guide treatment adaptation [48].

The Updated Rutgeerts Score

A limitation of both the original and modified RS is that their development is based on end‐to‐end anastomoses. To address recurrence at anatomical sites created by other anastomotic configurations, Rivière et al. proposed an updated RS [19]. It incorporates assessment of six separate anatomical areas created by side‐to‐side or side‐to‐end anastomosis, as seen in Figure 2. These include the ileal body, the segment between the anastomosis and the ileal inlet, and the ileal inlet of the neo‐terminal ileum, defined as the region near its entry into the colon in side‐to‐end and side‐to‐side anastomoses. The evaluation of these regions is important, as these might be overlooked with the RS or mRS [19, 48].

Dedicated Postoperative Endoscopic Scores: REMIND And POCER Index

The REMIND Score

The REMIND score was developed within a French multicenter prospective cohort and separates assessment into two distinct components, namely REMIND‐A and REMIND‐I. REMIND‐A focuses exclusively on anastomotic lesions, evaluating their extent, depth, circumferential involvement, and the presence of associated stenosis. REMIND‐I, in contrast, assesses lesions in the neo‐terminal ileum using a grading system derived from the RS (Table 1) [45].

Data from the original REMIND cohort suggested that ileal involvement is the primary driver of long‐term clinical recurrence. Clinical recurrence‐free survival was significantly shorter in patients with ileal lesions detected at early postoperative endoscopy, regardless of lesion severity (I0 vs. I1: p = 0.0008; I1 vs. I2–4: p = 0.43). Moreover, patients with isolated ileal lesions (A0I1–4) experienced worse long‐term clinical outcomes than those with isolated anastomotic lesions (A1–3I0: p = 0.009). In contrast, isolated anastomotic lesions were linked to anastomotic stenotic complications. A recent Asian cohort further supported the prognostic relevance of mild ileal lesions using the REMIND score [50].

From a clinical perspective, the REMIND score represents an important conceptual advance by distinguishing lesions that likely have different pathophysiological and prognostic meanings. Its role in routine clinical practice remains, however, to be fully defined.

The POCER Index

A post hoc analysis of the POCER‐trial evaluated the association of the subsequent lesions with endoscopic recurrence: neo‐terminal ileum lesions, anastomotic ulcer depth, number of ulcers, ulcer size, circumferential extent of ulceration, and the presence of stenosis. Among these variables, the combination of ulcer depth and circumferential extent strictly at the anastomosis was independently associated with endoscopic recurrence at 18 months (odds ratio [OR], 1.6; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.03–2.50; p = 0.035) [51]. Interestingly, ileal lesions alone were not independently associated with recurrence.

The main strength of the POCER index lies in its simplicity, facilitating its use in clinical trials and routine practice. However, although it incorporates lesion location, its strong focus on anastomotic findings may not adequately distinguish inflammatory recurrence from postoperative changes related to surgical healing or mechanical stress. Furthermore, the prognostic performance of the POCER index outside the original trial setting has been only modestly explored. Only in a recent Italian multicenter study, a POCER index < 1 showed a specificity of 100% for predicting the absence of clinical recurrence at the 6‐month endoscopic evaluation [52]. Interestingly, the combination of the index with the RS was shown to outperform each of them with a good compromise between sensitivity and specificity.

Why Reproducibility Remains a Critical Limitation of Postoperative Endoscopic Indices

Depending on the choice of postoperative endoscopic index and the selected threshold, reported recurrence rates may vary substantially. This directly affects clinical trial design, sample size estimation, and the perceived efficacy of preventive therapies. More broadly, the lack of standardized definitions and assessment frameworks complicates the interpretation and implementation of guideline recommendations and contributes to heterogeneous real‐world practices. Consistently, current guidelines do not specifically address postoperative CD recurrence and instead recommend applying endpoints defined for luminal disease [24, 53].

Standardization depends on reproducibility, relying not only on score simplicity, but also on the clarity of assessed lesions. For global scores, while the CDEIS demonstrates good interobserver agreement in luminal CD, its performance appears weaker when applied to postoperative recurrence. Conversely, several studies have reported acceptable intra‐ and interobserver agreement for the SES‐CD in the postoperative setting [26, 54].

A study from the French GETAID group involving 13 expert endoscopists demonstrated only moderate interobserver agreement for the RS, particularly when differentiating <i2 from ≥i2 lesions. Importantly, this variability was associated with potentially inappropriate therapeutic decisions in more than 10% of cases [55]. These findings were later confirmed in a study that further showed that endoscopist experience only modestly improved reproducibility [56].

More recently, a large multicenter study by Bak and colleagues evaluated the interobserver agreement of the mRS and newer indices using postoperative endoscopy videos assessed by multiple inflammatory bowel disease specialists. Overall, interobserver agreement was substantial for the mRS (weighted κ ≈0.67) and for the updated Rutgeerts score (weighted κ ≈0.69) [49]. When using the REMIND score, agreement was substantial for lesions located in the neo‐terminal ileum (weighted κ ≈0.73) but only moderate for anastomotic lesions (weighted κ ≈0.46). In analyses derived from the PREVENT trial, the most reproducible scores were SES‐CD, CDEIS, REMIND for ileal lesions, and the mRS overall [54]. Interestingly, estimates of index reliability and responsiveness were consistently lower when assessed at the anastomosis or distal colonic segment compared with the neo‐terminal ileum. These findings may provide a key explanation for the limited reproducibility of the original RS, as it integrates anatomical regions that differ markedly in both interpretability and reliability.

Score misinterpretations highlight the need for structured educational programs and standardized training tools to improve score interpretation and the reliability of postoperative scoring [57]. Such programs showed to reduce interobserver variability and the CONCORDIA trial (NCT06961942) is awaited to confirm these findings [58].

Beyond Scores: Anastomotic Complexity and the Limits of Scores in Postoperative CD

The proliferation of postoperative endoscopic indices underscores a central unresolved question in clinical practice: Is there a preferred scoring mechanism to guide postoperative management?

Beyond training and interobserver variability, the intrinsic complexity of postoperative anatomy and scoring systems deserves critical evaluation [19]. How can lesion location be reliably standardized within a scoring system when the underlying anatomical definitions themselves remain uncertain? Precisely distinguishing the anastomosis from the neo‐terminal ileum can be challenging, even for experienced endoscopists.

Several endoscopic lesions that are clearly visualized during colonoscopy are not captured by existing scores, despite their potential clinical relevance. For instance, lesions located in the ileal blind loop are often ignored by current scoring systems. A recent study demonstrated that inflammation at this anatomical area, that was described in only one third of postoperative endoscopies, was associated with a significantly increased risk of both clinical and surgical recurrence. However, the study did not adjust for the mRS as a confounder in the analysis, and the significance of inflammation at this site remains uncertain [59]. In the same idea, both clinical and pathophysiological data suggest that anastomotic and peri‐anastomotic lesions may have a different prognostic significance compared with ileal lesions [47]. In this context, additional layers of assessment, such as conventional histopathology or endomicroscopic evaluation, may be particularly informative in patients with absent or minimal endoscopic lesions and should be explored in the future [60, 61, 62, 63].

An important limitation lies in the uncertain relationship between endoscopic scoring categories and long‐term clinical outcomes. Existing thresholds, such as ulcer number and size in the RS, remain partly empirical and insufficiently validated. This issue is further compounded by the heterogeneity in the definition of endoscopic recurrence and the lack of standardization of clinically meaningful long‐term endpoints [53]. This highlights the need for future outcome‐driven refinement of postoperative scoring systems.

Endoscopic scores remain essential for classification and standardization in postoperative CD. However, recently proposed scoring systems still require external validation before routine implementation, and further indices may emerge. Based on this scoping review, the IMPACT consortium suggests that scores should be complemented by a systematic, detailed description of elementary lesions in endoscopy reports. Precise documentation of lesion size, location, morphology, depth, and extent, particularly distinguishing anastomotic from neo‐terminal ileal lesions, may provide a more comprehensive and durable framework for assessment. This approach would also allow retrospective calculation of different scores over time.

Future Directions in Postoperative CD Monitoring: Toward Standardization and Multimodal Assessment

Several unmet needs persist in the field of postoperative endoscopic assessment in CD. A major priority is the standardization of endoscopic documentation. Harmonized photo and video acquisition protocols, coupled with centralized reading in clinical trials, would improve reproducibility and comparability across studies. In clinical routine, for challenging cases, video recording could be considered as standard practice. In this context, artificial intelligence emerges as a critical integrative tool to improve endoscopic evaluation [64]. Advanced endoscopic technologies may allow earlier and more precise detection of lesions [65]. Automated lesion detection and grading could enhance both interobserver agreement and longitudinal disease monitoring. The unique anatomical complexity of the anastomotic site requires, however, advanced algorithms capable of detecting lesions in the context of a modified postoperative anatomy.

Beyond ileocolonoscopy, complementary modalities are increasingly evaluated [7, 8]. Capsule endoscopy, allowing detection of small bowel lesions beyond the ileal side of the anastomosis, could be of great interest. Indeed, small bowel lesions have earlier been associated with subsequent clinical or surgical recurrence and could be of interest in the context of assessment of endoscopic recurrence [66]. Cross‐sectional imaging, magnetic resonance enterography, and particularly intestinal ultrasound (IUS) offer noninvasive approaches to postoperative monitoring [67, 68]. Multiple meta‐analyses demonstrated acceptable accuracy and high sensitivity of these techniques [67, 69, 70]. Further refinement and standardization of ultrasonographic definitions of postoperative recurrence could ultimately support therapeutic decision‐making without systematic endoscopic confirmation, and the INSIGHT trial (NCT05713409) is anticipated to provide further evidence on this topic [71].

Discussion

This scoping review provides an overview of currently available endoscopic scoring systems for postoperative CD. The conclusions reflect the perspective of the IMPACT consortium and should be interpreted as an expert‐informed synthesis rather than formal recommendations.

By design, this scoping review does not include formal risk of bias assessment or evidence grading. The heterogeneity of study designs, definitions, particularly of recurrence, and endpoints limits direct comparisons and may introduce bias. In addition, the manual inclusion of selected studies may have introduced selection bias, which should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings.

Our conclusions do not aim to challenge existing guidelines, but rather to highlight persistent uncertainties. While current indices are essential for standardization, several, particularly more recent ones, remain incompletely validated, and further refinement or development of new scores indices may emerge. Importantly, descriptive evaluation and scoring systems should be viewed as complementary rather than opposing approaches. Scores enable comparability, whereas detailed lesion description may better capture postoperative anatomical and disease complexity and allow flexibility as the field evolves.

Finally, limitations in prognostic performance likely reflect not only the constraints of scoring systems but also the underlying biological complexity of CD, supporting the need for integrated, multimodal assessment strategies.

Conclusion

Endoscopic assessment after ICR remains the cornerstone of postoperative CD management. While RS is widely used, limitations, particularly in terms of reproducibility and interpretation of anastomotic lesions, are increasingly evident. Dedicated postoperative scores represent a conceptual advance by separating anastomotic from ileal disease and reflecting postoperative anatomy. Key gaps persist, including the lack of standardized definitions of recurrence, limited external validation of recent scores, and suboptimal reproducibility across anatomical regions. In this context, the available evidence is insufficient to recommend one scoring system over another. Until further evidence becomes available, scoring systems should be supplemented with a comprehensive and structured description of lesion characteristics. Future progress will rely on outcome‐driven validation of scoring systems, harmonization of definitions and reporting, and integration of multimodal approaches.
