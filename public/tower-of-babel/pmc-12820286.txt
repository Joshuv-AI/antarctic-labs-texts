Performance analysis and dead layer profiling of a carbon-fiber encapsulated p-type HPGe detector
Authors: Soni Devi, Katyayni Tiwari, Sanjeet S Kaintura, Pushpendra P Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate geometry-dependent efficiency calibration of High-Purity Germanium (HPGe) detectors is critical for the applications in low radiation level -spectrometry, rare decay studies, and assessments of environmental radioactivity. At IIT Ropar, a low-background facility, ILMI (IIT Ropar Low-background Measurement Infrastructure) is being developed to feature an array of coaxial p-type HPGe detectors with 40% relative efficiency. These detectors can operate in coincidence and are shielded with radiopure lead to minimize environmental background interference. This work presents a comprehensive spectrometric characterization and dead layer assessment of an HPGe detector, GEM40P4-83-RB/ORTEC, which is housed in a radiopure carbon fiber. The detailed Monte Carlo simulation in GEANT4 was used to model the detector response across a wide energy range (80.99-2614.50 keV). The methodology was validated by comparing the simulated results with experimental data using various gamma sources, including Cd, Cs, and Co, over a range of source-to-detector distances. Excellent agreement has been observed between simulated and experimental efficiencies, with a deviation of 2.5% for close distances (less than 5 cm) and 3.8% for farther distances (5 cm or greater). These minor discrepancies may be attributed to excluding the electronic acquisition chain effects in the simulation or photon scattering from the environment in actual experiments. The validated simulation model demonstrates high reliability and predictive accuracy, establishing a robust methodology for determining efficiency calibration in complex detector geometries.Keywords: Gamma spectrometry, HPGe detector, GEANT4, Dead layer, Efficiency calibration, Coincidence measurementSubject terms: Engineering, Physics

Introduction

The coaxial High-Purity Germanium (HPGe) detectors are widely recognized for their excellent energy resolution and large active volume, which makes them highly suitable for precise measurements in -spectroscopy. In a coaxial configuration, the electrical contact for signal acquisition is located at the center of the Germanium crystal, allowing the axial length of the detector to be significantly extended without compromising charge collection efficiency1. This configuration provides a substantial effective volume, enabling high detection efficiency across a broad energy range, especially for applications such as environmental radiation monitoring2–7 and low-background -ray spectroscopy8. Additionally, low-background HPGe detectors are valuable tools for investigating processes with extremely low probability, including rare decays9–13 with long half-lives - often exceeding - years10,14, radiopurity assessments for rare decay experiments15, and identifying trace levels of fission and activation products in samples. An array of HPGe detectors is generally employed to enhance sensitivity and increase the likelihood of detecting rare events. Such a configuration provides larger solid angle coverage and improved detection capabilities, significantly enhancing signal-to-noise ratios.

With this motivation, a low-background measurement infrastructure, ILMI (IIT Ropar Low-background Measurement Infrastructure), is being developed at the Indian Institute of Technology Ropar, integrating HPGe detectors for high-sensitivity radiopurity assessments and radiation background characterization. The demonstrator project ILMI-0 has been successfully optimized, of which the details are presented elsewhere16. The subsequent phase will establish ILMI-1 by incorporating three identical HPGe detectors arranged symmetrically to achieve greater detection sensitivity. Prior to deployment, these detectors require a comprehensive performance analysis for accurate determination of the Full Energy Peak Efficiency (FEPE)17–19 and characterization. It can be determined experimentally and complemented through Monte Carlo (MC) simulations. Experimental methods are often costly and time-consuming, whereas MC simulations provide a versatile and increasingly adopted approach for modeling HPGe detectors and -ray spectrometry20. In the modeling of HPGe detectors, accurate representation of the physical (dead layer, window material, crystal purity, and doping, etc.) and geometrical parameters (crystal radius, length, depth, and gap between crystal and endcap, etc.) is critical21–23. It has been noted that the discrepancies between simulated and experimental FEPE responses often arise from differences in dead layer thickness24–26 and geometrical specifications of the detector. Generally, a significant source of the discrepancy is an undefined and evolving dead layer due to temperature fluctuations and operational conditions, which reduces the active volume and efficiency of the detector17,27,28. Traditional non-invasive imaging techniques, such as X-ray tomography or gamma scanning, often lack the spatial resolution and sensitivity necessary to characterize small variations in the dead layer29. To overcome these limitations and for continuous monitoring of change in the dead layer, MC simulation has become the standard tool to model radiation interactions with the detector material, allowing for a more precise estimation of the dead layer and performance characteristics of the detector30. The volumetric effect, resulting from the geometric parameters of the detector29, is predominantly observed at higher energies. In contrast, the shielding effect, primarily attributed to the dead layer of the detector, becomes more significant at lower energies17. In this regime, photoelectric absorption is the dominant interaction mechanism. As a result, the low-energy photons are attenuated within the dead layer before reaching the active volume of the detector, reducing their contribution to energy deposition and detection efficiency. In p-type HPGe detectors, the outer n+ contact is formed by lithium diffusion into the germanium crystal31, resulting in an inactive layer of 0.7 mm on the front and lateral surfaces of the crystal32. This outer dead layer, formed by lithium diffusion during the fabrication process33,34, does not collect charges and absorbs low-energy -rays, significantly affecting the detection efficiency, especially in the 30–400 keV range. Thus, the dead layer thickness becomes a crucial parameter in -spectroscopy30,35,36. However, the inner p+ contact, formed by boron implantation, produces a much thinner inactive layer, typically of the order of 0.3 m32. To validate the accuracy of the model, it is advantageous to apply the procedure to a newly manufactured detector, for which the dead-layer thickness is already reported in the literature32. Therefore, in this study, a newly manufactured detector has been examined to estimate and independently verify the dead-layer thickness after fabrication. The crystal radius (R) and gap (g) between the crystal and carbon fiber window have been optimized using MC simulations20 so that the relative deviation between simulated and experimental measurements can be minimized, particularly for the higher energy range, i.e., 400 keV. The top dead layer () of the detector has been estimated using the relative deviation method and FEPE to further minimize the deviation, particularly at the lower energy range, i.e., 400 keV. Multiple simulations have been conducted by systematically varying the thickness of the dead layer across various configurations until the resulting efficiencies match the experimentally observed values. The estimated parameters have been validated by comparing the simulated efficiencies with the experimental ones for multiple sources.

Section "Results and discussion" discusses the performance parameters and their analysis, experimental results, comparison with simulated data, and verification of the simulated results for the estimated parameters. The summary and conclusions are presented in Section "Summary and conclusions".

Experimental setup and simulation methodology

Experimental setup

This study uses a p-type coaxial HPGe detector (GEM40P4-83-RB/ORTEC) with the physical specifications detailed in Fig. 1. A schematic diagram of signal processing electronics for the HPGe detector used in this work is illustrated in Fig. 2. The detector was coupled with a classical 5L dewar and operated at 1.8 kV using an ORTEC 660 dual 5 kV supply. The preamplifier was coupled to an ORTEC 672 high-performance spectroscopic amplifier and ASPEC 927 MCA (equipped with a built-in 16k ADC and memory), all housed in a 4001 A NIM bin. The -spectra were recorded and analyzed online using MAESTRO-32 software, compatible with Windows. The automatic Pole-Zero adjustment feature in the model 672 significantly streamlines the amplifier tuning process, allowing for precise compensation of the preamplifier pulse decay time. The standard point sources, e.g., Cd, Cs, Co, Ba, and Eu, have been used in this characterization. The -ray energies considered in this study are 88.00 keV for Cd, 80.99 keV, 276.39 keV, 302.85 keV, 356.01 keV, and 383.84 keV for Ba, 121.78 keV, 244 keV, 344.34 keV, 778.98 keV, 964.06 keV, 1112.20 keV and 1408.01 keV for Eu, 661.66 keV for Cs, 1173.23 keV and 1332.50 keV for Co37. Measurements for each point source were conducted to maintain statistical uncertainty of the full-energy peak area below 1%, ensuring reliable counting statistics. All data acquisitions were performed at a controlled room temperature of C to minimize environmental fluctuations and associated systematic uncertainties. In this experiment, both distance and radial scans of the detector were performed. The sources were systematically positioned along the axial direction, with a range extending from 5 to 30 cm at intervals of 5 cm for distance scans. However, the radial scan was carried out at 8 mm from the face of the detector across its radius, covering a total span of ±70 mm in steps of 5 mm. The offline data analysis was performed using an open source software, LAMPS, developed at the Bhabha Atomic Research Center, Mumbai, India, to support -spectroscopy experiments38.

GEANT4 simulation

The Monte Carlo simulations were performed using GEANT4 (version v-11.1.1) in conjunction with CLHEP libraries (version 2.4.6.4) to investigate the impact of dead layer thickness on FEPE1,18. GEANT420 is a comprehensive MC simulation toolkit developed at CERN, which is widely used for modeling particle interactions with matter, particularly in high-energy physics, nuclear physics, and radiation detection applications. It enables precise modeling of key physical processes such as photon interactions, multiple scattering, charge collection, and energy deposition within the detector material. This methodology is especially beneficial in low-background experiments designed for rare-decay searches, where meticulous detector modeling is crucial for achieving high detection sensitivity39,40.

In the simulation, the crystal geometry was constructed according to the specifications outlined in the detector’s datasheet, incorporating every detail such as the germanium crystal, dead layers, inner hole, and the carbon fiber encapsulation, as detailed in Fig. 1. The 3D representation of the simulated coaxial HPGe detector crystal is presented in Fig. 3. The radioactive sources were modeled same as the standard size provided by the manufacturer, reflecting the absolute strength of the -sources. The lithium diffusion dead layer was modeled as a fully absorptive region. Custom classes were implemented to define geometry and physical processes, including DetectorConstruction.cc, EventActionManager.cc, PhysicsList.cc, and PrimaryGenerator.cc. To extract relevant information from the simulation, additional user action classes, such as RunAction.cc, EventAction.cc, SteppingAction.cc, and SteppingVerbose.cc, along with their corresponding header files, were utilized. The simulation was executed through a shell script. A custom PhysicsList was developed to account for all particle matter interactions, incorporating Decay Physics, Radioactive Decay Physics, and Electromagnetic Physics. Essential -ray interaction processes, such as G4ComptonScattering, G4GammaConversion, and G4PhotoElectricEffect, were explicitly included in the code. The primary particles were defined from input files and generated using the G4ParticleGenerator class. The cutoff energy of photons was set at 1 keV using the electromagnetic processes in PhysicsList. For comparison with the experimental data, a spectrum range of 0–2700 keV was considered, and the bin size in the simulation was set to 0.16 keV to fit the spectrum into 16k channels. Various standard radioactive sources covering the full energy range have been used to model the gamma interactions in the detector material. events were generated uniformly from the radioactive source, which resulted in a statistical uncertainty of < 1%41. The simulation results have been evaluated using the ROOT (version v6.28.04) data analysis framework42.

Results and discussion

Detector response parameters: shaping time, gain, peak shape, and resolution

Shaping time () in a spectroscopic amplifier is a crucial parameter that affects the resolution of the signal and noise characteristics. Generally, the electronic noise is classified into (i) Parallel, (ii) Series, and (iii) Flicker noise. The electronic noise dependencies on shaping time relate as, parallel noise , series noise , and the flicker noise is independent of . For HPGe detectors, selecting the correct shaping time is essential to balance out better energy resolution with efficient signal processing. In a given spectroscopic amplifier, there will be an optimal value of that minimizes the overall electronic noise and provides the signal with good energy resolution. Comprehensive measurements were conducted by systematically varying the fine and coarse gain of the spectroscopic amplifier in relation to different shaping time constants. Figure 4 shows the energy spectra corresponding to a 1332.50 keV -line obtained from a Co source, highlighting the dependence of FWHM on shaping time. As can be seen from this figure, the broadest peak corresponds to the shortest shaping time (i.e., 0.5 s) with an FWHM of 9.54 keV, while the sharpest peak with FWHM of 1.67 keV is observed at a shaping time of 6 s. It is evident from Fig. 4 that a longer enhances the energy resolution of the detector. For better insights into this aspect, the variation in signal-to-noise (P/C) ratio has been plotted as a function of in Fig. 5. As can be seen from this figure, the highest value of P/C ratio has been observed for = 6 , and almost saturates for higher values of shaping time. However, the longer value is undesirable at high counting rates due to the pulse pile-up effect and processing delays. The shorter can maintain high throughput, but at the expense of some energy resolution. As per data presented in Figs. 4 and 5, an optimal is set to 6 to achieve a practical balance between energy resolution and count rate capabilities.

Initially, the simulation model was optimized by adjusting the detector geometrical parameters (mainly crystal radius(R) and gap(g))44. As a result of this optimization, the deviation at higher energies reduced significantly, dropping from 12% to 4%, as shown in Table 2. However, the discrepancies persisted at lower energies (i.e., 400 keV), predominantly due to uncertainties in dead layer thickness. Accurately quantifying the dead layer thickness of the detector is essential to resolve low-energy discrepancies and enhance the overall accuracy of the model39. In this work, the optimization focused on the top dead layer, as the manufacturer provided the side dead layer, and the inner dead layer has a negligible effect on low-energy efficiencies. The top dead layer thickness systematically varied from 0.35 mm to 1.1 mm in steps of 0.05 mm. For each selected thickness, the Monte Carlo simulation was performed using point sources at source–detector distances of 10, 15, 20, and 25 cm, covering a range of -ray energies. To ensure accurate measurements using multi- sources, close distances, shorter than 10 cm, were excluded due to significant summing effects. Similarly, the 30 cm distance was omitted to minimize the impact of scattering effects, where -rays deviate in energy and trajectory after interacting with surrounding materials. These exclusions improve the reliability of efficiency measurements and comparisons. Two approaches were employed to estimate the dead layer thickness of the detector.

In the first approach, the relative deviation () between experimental and simulated efficiencies corresponding to 80.99 keV and 121.78 keV -lines obtained from Ba and Eu source, respectively, was analysed and plotted as a function of dead layer thickness by considering all source-to-detector distances (n=10, 15, 20, and 25 cm), as shown in Fig. 11 (a-b). Initially, an increase in leads to a decrease in , indicating enhanced alignment between simulation and experiment in the assessment of detector efficiency. However, beyond a specific optimal thickness, the value of begins to increase, suggesting that an excessive dead layer attenuates -ray photons before reaching the active volume of the detector, thereby diminishing their contribution to detection efficiency. Consequently, an optimal thickness was established, effectively balancing energy absorption while minimizing losses to ensure optimal detector performance. This optimal thickness corresponds to the minimum relative deviation between simulated and experimental efficiency measurements. As shown in Fig. 11, this value was found to be 0.70 mm and 0.75 mm for 80.99 keV and 121.78 keV (represented by the dotted line), respectively. The average of these minima, 0.72 mm was considered the optimized top dead layer thickness, as listed in Table 4.

Validation of estimated parameters

The Full Energy Peak Efficiency (FEPE) is a critical parameter for validating the GEANT4 simulation model. In view of this, experimentally measured and simulated FEPE have been compared, as shown in Table 5, corresponding to lower and higher energies for radial scan spanning over −35 mm to +35 mm, covering the full diameter of the crystal. The slight relative deviation showed a good agreement between the simulation and the experimental results across the entire range. The experimental and simulated efficiencies as a function of -energies are shown in Fig. 14, measured at 25 cm for low energies using nominal and different optimized parameters. The inset of Fig. 14 shows the comparison over the whole energy range. The results indicate that the simulation has accurately reproduced the experimentally obtained efficiency curve after using a dead layer thickness of the detector, , demonstrating the effectiveness of the model implemented in this work. In conclusion, the comparison robustly supports the reliability of the optimized parameters in replicating the performance of the detector.

Summary and conclusions

In summary, the spectroscopic characterization and performance analysis of a carbon fiber-encapsulated p-type coaxial HPGe detector have been successfully carried out with a particular focus on determining top dead layer thickness. Various parameters, such as peak shape, gain, shaping time, and resolution, have been assessed for the performance evaluation. The GEANT4 simulation was employed to iteratively adjust the geometrical and physical parameters. After optimizing parameters R and g, the experimental and simulated results are found to be in good agreement at higher energies but diverge at lower energies. These discrepancies were resolved through precise determination of the top dead layer, ultimately estimated to be 0.72 and 0.71 mm by comparing experimental data with Monte Carlo simulations using two different approaches. Given that the detector was newly procured, significant dead layer growth beyond what occurred during the crystal growth process was not anticipated32. The consistent values of obtained from the GEANT4 simulation support the accuracy of the model implemented in this work and provide a foundation for future studies, including the optimization of other detectors to be installed for setting up ILMI at IIT Ropar. Additionally, this study emphasized the reliability of using multi- sources as an effective method for estimating the dead layer of the detector. The approach was further validated by comparing simulated and experimental efficiencies for various point sources, including Cd, Cs, and Co, at different distances. The results demonstrated that the measured efficiencies agree reasonably well with the GEANT4 simulations, within a relative deviation of 2.5% for close distances and 3.8% for farther distances, over an energy range of 80.99 to 2614.50 keV. The results confirm the accuracy and robustness of the GEANT4 model in reproducing the detector response, highlighting its utility in performance evaluation when manufacturer specifications are insufficient. Overall, this study lays a foundational framework for future high-precision low background investigations, thereby facilitating more accurate and reliable experimental outcomes.
