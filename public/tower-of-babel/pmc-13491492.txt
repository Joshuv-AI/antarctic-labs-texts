A Full-Process Experimental Evaluation of Water-Blocking Mitigation in Tight Sandstones by Supercritical CO2 Shut-In Soaking Coupled with N2 Displacement
Authors: Shuyang Wang, Yongbo Zhang, Xuanlong Shan, Ying Bian, Yufeng Shen, Nan Jiang, Le Kong, Wentong He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water blocking severely restricts gas mobility in tight sandstone reservoirs because of capillary-dominated retention of aqueous phases within micro–nanopore systems. This study establishes an integrated experimental framework to quantify the full-process evolution of water blockingfrom water invasion and entrapment to gas displacement and supercritical CO2 (SC–CO2) treatment. Vacuum-assisted water saturation, pressure-controlled N2 displacement, and SC–CO2 shut-in soaking experiments were conducted on tight sandstone cores, and multiscale characterization methods, including nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), microcomputed tomography (micro-CT), powder X-ray diffraction (XRD), and thin-section casting, were used. The results show that water invasion follows a three-stage temporal pattern, with rapid initial imbibition causing most of the water-blocking damage. Conventional N2 displacement effectively removes mobile water from pore domains with NMR-equivalent radii larger than approximately 300 nm, but it fails to mobilize capillary-trapped water in smaller pore domains, yielding displacement efficiencies less than 40%. SC–CO2 shut-in soaking significantly alters the pore network through mineral dissolution, possible wettability-related changes, and enlargement of micro–nanothroat structures, leading to enhanced pore connectivity and reduced capillary resistance. Following SC–CO2 pretreatment, the N2 displacement efficiency increases to 50–70% and the effective porosity shows a 10–20% improvement. The combined SC–CO2–N2 approach has a strong potential for mitigating water blocking in ultratight formations and may provide an experimental basis for future field-scale optimization of gas recovery and CO2-assisted reservoir treatment.

Introduction

With unconventional natural gas becoming a major target for exploration and development, tight sandstone reservoirs have emerged as key replacement domains for continental gas resources and have received sustained global attention. − Controlled by depositional environments, diagenetic evolution, and structural activities, these reservoirs typically exhibit low porosity, low permeability, and strong heterogeneity. Their pore systems consist of multiple storage unitsincluding micro/nanoscale pores, intergranular pores, dissolution pores, and fractures. − The pore-throat sizes are restricted, and the connectivity is weak. Such complex pore-throat architectures not only limit gas migration within the matrix but also increase reservoir sensitivity to external fluids, posing major challenges for effective stimulation and industrial-scale development.

Because tight sandstones lack natural deliverability, hydraulic fracturing has become the primary engineering method for enhancing the flow capacity. However, large volumes of water-based fracturing fluids tend to remain trapped in micro/nanoscale pore-throat systems because of strong capillary forces and low formation pressure, making flowback insufficient and leading to persistent water blocking. − Compared with conventional reservoirs, tight sandstones contain higher proportions of bound water, and fluid exchange between fractures and the matrix is more restricted, making water blocking even more difficult to remove. , Water blocking suppresses the development of gas-flow pathways, reduces flowback efficiency, and may impair fracture-network conductivity, resulting in an early production decline and stimulation failure. , Therefore, identifying water-blocking characteristics, quantifying their evolution, and evaluating effective mitigation strategies are essential scientific and engineering issues for the efficient development of tight sandstone reservoirs. ,

Multiple approaches have been proposed to mitigate water blocking, including nitrogen (N2) displacement, surfactant-assisted wettability modification, CO2 purging or displacement, and optimization of fracturing-fluid systems. These methods are often combined with core displacement, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), CT/microcomputed tomography (micro-CT), and blue-epoxy-impregnated casting thin-section analyses to assess changes in reservoir properties. − Although these studies have improved near-wellbore flow conditions to some extent, most reported methods focus on a single stage or a single dominant mechanismfor example, evaluating only the gas-displacement performance during flowback or examining only localized CO2-induced dissolution. Such limitations hinder the development of a systematic understanding of water-blocking formation, displacement evolution, and mitigation performance as a full process. The dominance of micro/nanoscale pore-throat structures, strong capillary forces, and low-pressure systems in tight sandstones makes conventional water-based or gas-based displacement techniques insufficient for removing residual trapped water. In addition, existing CO2-related studies remain largely phenomenological and lack quantitative coupling between microstructural evolution and macroscopic flow capacity. , A unified experimental framework capable of characterizing water-blocking behavior, quantifying pore-throat structural changes, and evaluating mitigation effectiveness is still lacking.

Given its low interfacial tension, high diffusivity, and ability to induce weakly acidic fluid–rock reactions, supercritical CO2 (SC–CO2) has attracted attention as a potential agent for mitigating water blocking in tight sandstones. However, systematic experimental evidence regarding SC–CO2 displacement dynamics, its ability to modify micro/nanoscale pore-throat structures, and its relationship with enhanced reservoir flow capacity remains limited. To address this gap, in this study, a comprehensive experimental evaluation system encompassing the full process of water-blocking formation, N2-displacement response, and SC–CO2 shut-in soaking was established. By integrating core displacement experiments with multiscale characterization techniques, this framework enables the quantitative assessment of reservoir–property evolution, pore-throat structural changes, and fluid distribution characteristics and develops a coupling relationship between SC–CO2 treatment and improved flow capacity. The results show that SC–CO2 decreases interfacial tension to enhance fluid mobility, accelerates water-phase migration because of its high diffusivity, and enlarges effective pore-throat sizes through weak dissolution. These combined effects clarify the key mechanisms by which SC–CO2 mitigates water blocking in tight sandstones. These findings provide scientific guidance and experimental support for optimizing water-blocking mitigation strategies and improving the development efficiency of tight sandstone reservoirs.

Materials and Methods

Core Samples and Preparation

Tight sandstone core samples were collected from wells drilled in the Shahezi Formation on the northern slope of the Lishu Fault Depression in the Songliao Basin. The sandstones are mainly medium- to coarse-grained and are associated with underwater distributary-channel microfacies within a fan-delta system. The water saturation pressure was set to 30 MPa to approximate the in situ stress condition of the target formation. After coring, all the samples were immediately sealed and transported to the laboratory. Standard cylindrical samples (Φ25 mm × 50 mm) were prepared using a coring machine to ensure sample integrity and consistency for subsequent experiments. All cylindrical samples used in the comparative tests were taken from the same core plug to maintain internal consistency across the experimental procedures.

Experimental Setup and Characterization Techniques

Blue-Epoxy-Impregnated Casting Thin Sections

Blue-epoxy-impregnated casting thin sections were used to examine pore types, porosity, and pore structure characteristics. Dyed epoxy resin was injected into the rock pores under vacuum conditions, which were subsequently cured and cut into thin sections approximately 0.03 mm thick. Pore morphology and connectivity were analyzed using a polarizing microscope (Leica D05GS005), − and image data were processed using a color image analysis system (CIAS-SU-ZL 3.0) for classification of reservoir storage spaces. Alizarin Red S staining was applied to identify carbonate cements, which appeared as orange–red precipitates when they were complexed with calcium-bearing minerals.

Casting thin sections were prepared for both the untreated and SC–CO2-treated samples. Comparisons of the thin sections allowed an evaluation of SC–CO2-induced modifications to pore-throat structures and carbonate dissolution features.

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR)

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements were performed using a MesoMR12–060H–I low-field NMR imaging and analysis system at the Oil Shale Laboratory of Jilin University, following the general procedure of SY/T 6490–2023. The instrument operates at a magnetic field strength of 0.3 T, with a radio frequency pulse range of 1–42 MHz, and is suitable for characterizing water-bearing pore systems in cylindrical tight sandstone cores.

NMR can be used to determine the distribution of the transverse relaxation time (T 2) of hydrogen nuclei in pore fluids. For a fully water-saturated porous medium, the conversion from T 2 relaxation time to an equivalent pore radius is based on the classical surface-relaxation model 1T2=1T2B+ρ2SV+D(γGTE)212 where T 2 B is the bulk relaxation time of the saturating fluid, ρ2 is the transverse surface relaxivity, S/V is the pore surface-area-to-volume ratio, and the last term represents diffusion relaxation in an internal magnetic field gradient. Under the low-field NMR conditions used in this study, deionized water was used as the saturating fluid, and a short echo spacing was applied in the CPMG sequence; therefore, the bulk relaxation and diffusion terms are generally much smaller than the surface-relaxation term. The equation can thus be approximated as follows 1T2≈ρ2SV

For pores with similar geometric characteristics, S/V can be expressed as an equivalent pore radius, and the NMR-equivalent pore radius can be written as follows rNMR=Fsρ2T2=CT2 where F s is a pore shape factor and where C = F sρ2 is the empirical T 2-to-radius conversion coefficient. In the NMR software processing, an empirical conversion coefficient of C = 0.010 μm·ms–1 was applied to the Shahezi tight sandstone samples. Because this coefficient was not independently calibrated by mercury intrusion capillary pressure (MICP) data for the present samples, all radius values converted from T 2 spectra are reported as NMR-equivalent pore radii rather than absolute geometric pore-throat radii. All compared samples were collected from the same core interval and have similar lithology and mineral composition; therefore, applying the same conversion coefficient allows internally consistent comparisons of T 2 spectral components, NMR-equivalent pore size distributions, movable-water proportions, and displacement efficiencies among different treatment stages.

Notably, MICP can provide independent pore-throat entry radius information and is useful for calibrating the surface relaxivity of NMR. However, MICP is destructive and cannot be performed on the same core samples after the continuous water saturation, SC–CO2 soaking, and N2 displacement sequences. In addition, MICP mainly reflects mercury-accessible pore-throat entry radii, whereas NMR reflects the relaxation behavior of hydrogen-bearing fluids within water-filled pore bodies and connected pore spaces. Therefore, the two methods provide complementary but not identical pore size information. In this study, the NMR-derived radius distribution is used primarily to evaluate relative changes in the water distribution, movable-water proportion, and pore system response before and after N2 displacement and SC–CO2 treatment.

Micro-CT Imaging

X-ray microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) imaging was conducted using a Carl Zeiss Xradia 520 Versa system to nondestructively obtain three-dimensional pore structure information from cylindrical sandstone samples (Φ2.5 cm × 2.5 cm). A total of 1400 projections were acquired over 360° at 90 kV and 10 W. The reconstructed images were processed using XRM Reconstructor software with a filtered back-projection algorithm, and Avizo 9 was used for image enhancement, noise reduction, segmentation, and quantitative pore structure analysis. − Notably, although the voxel size of the reconstructed images was approximately 70 nm, the effective spatial resolution of micro-CT after tomographic reconstruction, filtering, and segmentation was lower than the nominal voxel size. Therefore, the micro-CT analysis in this study was used mainly to characterize CT-resolvable pore domains, larger connected pore spaces, and microfractures rather than to directly visualize the complete submicron- to nanoscale pore system inferred from the NMR T 2 spectra.

Supercritical CO2 Experimental Setup

The supercritical CO2 (SC–CO2) shut-in soaking experiments were carried out using a sealed pressurization system consisting of a CO2 cylinder, booster pump, high-pressure valves, gas flow meter, high-temperature/high-pressure reactor, constant-temperature water bath, and control console (Figure ). All the components were connected with corrosion-resistant perfluoroether tubing and leak-checked before the operation. Cylindrical sandstone samples were placed inside the reactor and sealed. The system pressure was increased to above 10 MPa using a CO2 cylinder and booster pump, and the inlet-gas conditions were adjusted via a high-pressure valve and flow meter. Real-time monitoring ensured that CO2 remained in the supercritical state. The reactor was immersed in a 40 °C constant-temperature water bath filled with an ethylene-glycol solution to maintain stable temperature conditions. The pressure sensors continuously recorded the inlet and outlet pressures, and all the data were automatically logged through the control console.

Powder X-ray Diffraction (XRD)

The mineral composition was analyzed using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD). XRD patterns were collected under Cu Kα 1.5406 radiation with a 45 kV, 40 mA generator at a rotational speed of 120 rpm and a time step of 1 s. Additionally, clay particle size classification was performed using Millipore technology, while clay analysis was conducted via XRD.

Experimental Procedures

To comprehensively characterize water-blocking formation, gas-displacement response, and the modification effect of supercritical CO2 on pore-throat structures in tight sandstones, a full-process experimental workflow was established in this study. This integrated system covers three sequential components: water-phase invasion, gas-phase displacement, and SC–CO2 shut-in soaking.

First, a standardized pressurized water saturation experiment (Section ) was conducted to simulate the formation of water blockage under insufficient flowback during hydraulic fracturing. This experiment investigated the temporal evolution of water-phase invasion and the corresponding pore occupancy characteristics under different saturation durations.

Second, conventional nitrogen (N2) displacement experiments (Section ) were performed to examine the migration and release behavior of the water phase under varying displacement pressure differentials. These tests enabled a quantitative assessment of the effectiveness and limitations of traditional gas displacement in mitigating the water blocking.

Finally, SC–CO2 shut-in soaking followed by N2 displacement (Section ) was used to investigate how CO2 soaking alters pore-throat structures, alters fluid distribution states, and enhances subsequent displacement efficiency. The three experiments are methodologically connected and mutually comparable, providing a complete and coherent experimental basis for the subsequent analysis of the results and mechanistic interpretation.

Experimental Simulation of Water-Blocking Formation in Tight Sandstones

Before the experiments were performed, the core samples were dried at 100 °C for at least 90 h to remove pore water and surface-adsorbed water. The samples were then placed in a sealed pressure vessel and evacuated to approximately −0.1 MPa for 3 h using a vacuum saturation system (Figure ). This step ensured a nearly air-free pore system and created a sufficient pressure gradient for subsequent water injection.

Workflow and vacuum saturation setup for the simulation of water-blocking formation in tight sandstones.

After vacuum stabilization, deionized water was injected into the vessel using a high-pressure pump, and the saturation pressure was rapidly increased to 30 MPa to simulate the in situ overburden and fracturing-fluid pressures. Eight water saturation durations were established (1, 3, 5, 10, 30 min, 2, 5, and 10 h) under constant pressure. At each target duration, the pressure was released, the sample was retrieved, and T 2 spectra were measured using a low-field NMR instrument. The evolution of T 2 peak positions, peak areas, and the proportion of short- versus long-T 2 components was used to characterize the progressive invasion of water into micro/nanoscale pore-throat systems.

By comparison of porosity and T 2-spectral changes at different saturation durations, the water invasion rate, dominant pore throat size, and saturation-equilibrium time (i.e., the stabilized water-blocking state) were determined. This experiment provides a standardized water-blocked baseline for subsequent N2 displacement and SC–CO2 soaking tests.

Experimental Evaluation of Water-Blocking Mitigation via Conventional Nitrogen (N2) Displacement

To evaluate the effectiveness of conventional gas displacement in mitigating water blocking, the core samples first underwent standardized pretreatment. The samples were dried at 100 °C for at least 90 h, evacuated for 3 h, and subsequently saturated at 30 MPa for 24 h to ensure complete occupation of micro/nanoscale pores by the aqueous phase. After saturation, the baseline porosity and T 2 spectra were obtained by NMR.

The saturated cores were then mounted in the core-displacement–online-NMR system (Figure ). Under stable confining pressure, N2 displacement was conducted by applying pressure differentials of 1, 3, 4, 6, and 8 MPa. Each displacement stage lasted 2 h. Real-time T 2 spectra were acquired during displacement to monitor water migration, release, and retention. Variations in porosity recovery, T 2 peak attenuation, and residual water fractions under different pressure gradients were used to quantify the removal of water from pore-throat systems.

Workflow and apparatus configuration for evaluating water-blocking mitigation via nitrogen (N2) displacement.

By comparison of the recovery efficiency and spectral evolution across pressures, the overall mitigation capacity of conventional N2 displacement was evaluated, and the optimal displacement pressure range for effective water-blocking removal was determined.

SC–CO2 Shut-In Soaking Followed by Nitrogen (N2) Displacement for Water-Blocking Mitigation

A combined SC–CO2 shut-in soaking and N2 displacement experiment was conducted to investigate the modification of pore-throat structures and the improvement of water-blocking removal. Two sets of experiments were carried out: a long-duration SC–CO2 soaking test (Experiment A) and a soaking-duration comparison test (Experiment B).

Long-Duration SC–CO2 Shut-In Soaking

Two core samples from the same depth and lithology (samples a and b) underwent identical pretreatment: drying at 100 °C, evacuation for 3 h, and saturation at 30 MPa for 24 h. Sample b was then placed in a high-temperature/high-pressure reactor and exposed to SC–CO2 at 40 °C and 7.9 MPa for 15 days, enabling interactions between SC–CO2, pore water, and carbonate-rich cement. Sample a served as the untreated control.

After soaking, both samples were subjected to N2 displacement at 8 MPa for 3 h in the core-displacement–online-NMR system. Comparisons of porosity, T 2-spectral evolution, and water release across different pore-throat scales were used to evaluate SC–CO2–induced structural modification and the resulting increase in water-blocking removal.

Effect of SC–CO2 Soaking Duration

Three additional cores were pretreated identically and soaked in SC–CO2 for 1, 3, and 5 days (40 °C, 7.9 MPa). Subsequent N2 displacement (8 MPa, 3 h) and NMR analyses were conducted under identical conditions. Changes in porosity, T 2 spectral redistribution, and residual water fractions were used to evaluate the short-term time-dependent response of pore structure reorganization and water-blocking mitigation within the 1–5-day soaking range (Figure ).

Results

Water-Lock Formation and Evolution under Different Water Saturation Times

Systematic variations in water saturation time were applied to the tight sandstone cores, and NMR tests were conducted to obtain the corresponding T 2-relaxation-time distributions. All samples exhibit a stable bimodal T 2 pattern (Figure ), indicating two dominant NMR-equivalent pore domains: a long-T 2 component corresponding to relatively larger and better-connected pore space and a short-T 2 component associated with smaller or more strongly surface-relaxation-controlled pore space. With increasing saturation time, the amplitudes of both T 2 peaks increase, reflecting the progressive invasion of water into the pore system and the corresponding increase in the overall water content. The NMR-derived water saturation values reveal that the initial dry cores contain far less water than they do after saturation, and the water saturation continues to increase with increasing saturation time. This difference represents the volume of immobile water that enters the pore system under an external pressure and remains trapped.

T 2 spectra of tight sandstone samples under different durations of water saturation.

The evolution of water saturation with time reveals clear stage-dependent saturation behavior (Figure ). During the first 1–5 min, the water saturation increases rapidly, indicating a rapid saturation stage. Between approximately 5 min and 5 h, saturation continues to increase but at a much slower rate, corresponding to a gradual invasion stage. After approximately 5 h, saturation approaches a stable level and only slightly changes, indicating that the pore system has reached a near-equilibrium state under the applied pressure.

Water saturation curves illustrating the staged imbibition behavior of the tight sandstone cores.

Overall, the pressure saturation process in tight sandstones strongly depends on time: rapid early-stage changes, moderate mid-stage increases, and late-stage stabilization. These stage characteristics provide a basis for understanding water invasion behavior and provide an essential reference for interpreting water blocking development and evaluating the performance of subsequent mitigation measures.

Effectiveness of Nitrogen Displacement at Different Pressures in Mitigating Water Blocking

In this experiment, conventional nitrogen (N2) displacement was combined with online nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements to analyze the evolution of the water distribution in the pore system of tight sandstone at different displacement pressures (Figure ). Before displacement, the core samples exhibited a typical bimodal T 2 spectrum, corresponding to two dominant pore domains: a long-T 2 component associated with relatively larger and better-connected pores and a short-T 2 component associated with smaller or less-mobile pore domains. As the displacement pressure increased from 1 to 3 MPa, the long T 2 component associated with large pores decreased markedly, indicating that the movable water in larger pore spaces was preferentially displaced. In contrast, the short-T 2 component showed only minor changes, suggesting that bound water in small pores remained largely immobile at low pressures.

T 2 relaxation spectra of tight sandstone samples under different N2 displacement pressures.

When the displacement pressure increased to 4–5 MPa, the amount of movable water in large and some intermediate NMR-equivalent pore domains further decreased, reflected by an overall decrease in the T 2 amplitude. However, once the pressure exceeded 6–8 MPa, additional reductions in the T 2 amplitude became limited, implying that most of the movable water had already been displaced and that only less-mobile water remained, mainly in short-T 2 or micro/nanoscale pore domains. Across all the samples, a consistent trend was observed: the displacement efficiency was strongly controlled by the long-T 2 movable-water component, whereas the bound water in the micro/nanoscale pores remained largely unaffected throughout the increase in pressure.

The evolution of porosity during N2 displacement (Figure ) further supports these observations. The total porosity decreased progressively with an increase in pressure, reflecting the volume of water removed from the pore system. The results indicate that the movable-water porosity ranged from 1.2 to 3.6%, whereas the irreducible water porosity was between 1.2 and 6.7% (Table ). As the pressure approached approximately 5 MPa, the rate of porosity reduction decreased significantly, which is consistent with the stabilization of the T 2 spectra and indicates that most of the movable water was displaced within this pressure window.

Overall, conventional N2 displacement effectively mobilizes water in long-T 2 pore domains but has a limited influence on bound water retained in short-T 2 or micro/nanoscale pore domains. The results demonstrate that a displacement pressure of approximately 4–5 MPa is optimal for mobilizing movable water in tight sandstone, whereas further pressure increases yield only marginal improvement. Therefore, enhancing water-blocking mitigation in tight reservoirs requires fluids with stronger penetration and displacement capabilitiessuch as supercritical CO2capable of interacting with the nanopore network where conventional N2 displacement is ineffective.

Combined Effect of SC–CO2 Soaking and Nitrogen Displacement on Pore Structure Modification

This experiment evaluates how supercritical CO2 (SC–CO2) shut-in soaking enhances the ability of subsequent nitrogen (N2) displacement to remove water blocking in tight sandstone reservoirs. Following the experimental workflow (Section ) and apparatus configuration (Figure ), two groups of samples were prepared: (1) a control group subjected directly to conventional N2 displacement after water saturation and (2) a treatment group exposed to SC–CO2 shut-in soaking for 15 days prior to N2 displacement. The T 2 relaxation distributions (Figure ) demonstrate that SC–CO2 pretreatment markedly modified the pore system. Compared with the water-saturated baseline, conventional N2 displacement primarily reduced the long-T 2 signals associated with relatively larger and more mobile pore domains, whereas the short-T 2 components showed limited changes. To further evaluate the pore structure changes induced by SC–CO2 at the CT-resolvable scale, selected samples were analyzed using three-dimensional micro-CT reconstruction (Figure ). The quantitative voxel-based pore-network parameters (Table ) consistently improved the resolvable pore network after SC–CO2 shut-in soaking: the effective pore volume increased by +11.8 to +38.5%, the average pore-throat radius increased by 14–40%, the average coordination number increased by 50–200%, and the pore connectivity index improved by 15–44%. These results indicate that SC–CO2 treatment enhanced the connectivity and geometric complexity of larger pore domains and microfracture-related pathways, which can be resolved by micro-CT. However, these micro-CT results should not be interpreted as direct visualization of the complete submicrometer- to nanoscale pore system reflected by the NMR T 2 spectra. Instead, they provide complementary three-dimensional morphological evidence for the improvement of larger connected pore spaces, while the changes in micro/nanoscale water-bearing pores are supported mainly by the NMR results.

Casting thin-section observations provide additional mineralogical evidence (Figure ). SC–CO2 treatment was associated with several characteristic microstructural features: 1–10 μm microfractures linked adjacent pores, which are interpreted as a dissolution-related microcrack–connectivity pattern (Figure a); selective edge dissolution of calcite cement (Figure b); grain-boundary alteration zones resulting from partial dissolution (Figure c); removal of fine-grained infill, creating connected micropore clusters (Figure d); widening of intergranular contacts by dissolution (Figure e); and intense dissolution in calcite-rich regions, producing a continuous pore-cavity network (Figure f). No significant secondary precipitation was observed in the cast slab. These petrographic features support the interpretation that SC–CO2 enhances pore-throat conductivity through both chemical (carbonate dissolution) and mechanical (microcrack generation) pathways.

Following supercritical CO2 treatment, XRD quantitative analysis of carbonate-rich sandstone (Table ) revealed significant changes in mineral composition: the content of primary carbonate minerals such as calcite decreased markedly from 28.5 to 12.2%, while the total amounts of plagioclase and potassium feldspar decreased from 27.8 to 17%. In contrast, the relative content of inert quartz increased slightly from 35 to 43% because of the dissolution of other minerals. Concurrently, kaolinite formed through feldspar dissolution increased from 4 to 12%, illite rose from 4.5 to 8.2%, and new diffraction peaks of secondary minerals such as dolomite (7.6%) emerged. These mineralogical changes indicate that SC–CO2–water–rock interactions exert a dual effect on reservoir quality. On one hand, dissolution of carbonate and feldspar minerals contributes to pore-throat enlargement, enhanced pore connectivity, and improved water-blocking mitigation. On the other hand, the increase in secondary clay minerals such as kaolinite and illite may promote fine migration, local pore-throat plugging, and permeability impairment, potentially offsetting part of the beneficial effects. Therefore, although the overall experimental results demonstrate a net improvement in pore structure and displacement efficiency, the balance between pore enhancement and clay-related formation damage should be carefully evaluated in future studies through direct permeability measurements and in situ monitoring of fines migration.

A timed-soaking comparison experiment (Experiment B) further examines the short-term influence of the duration of SC–CO2 exposure on pore structure evolution and water-blocking mitigation within the 1–5-day soaking range. The results (Table ) indicate that porosity enhancement generally increases with soaking time within the tested range of 1–5 days, with the greatest improvement observed at 5 days. Compared with 1-day and 3-day soaking, 5-day soaking resulted in greater enhancement, but the present time series experiment did not include intermediate or longer durations beyond 5 days; therefore, the optimal soaking duration and the approach to dissolution–precipitation equilibrium cannot be conclusively determined from these data.

Overall, compared with conventional methods alone, SC–CO2 shut-in soaking significantly strengthens the pore-system response of tight sandstones and enables subsequent N2 displacement to mobilize far more water. Integrated analysis of NMR, micro-CT, and thin-section data revealed that SC–CO2 pretreatment improved not only the pore volume but also the connectivity and architecture of pore-throat networks. These findings provide a mechanistic foundation for enhanced displacement efficiency and offer reliable experimental support for the development of optimized water-blocking mitigation strategies in tight sandstone reservoirs.

Discussion

Microscale Mechanisms of Water Blocking in Tight Sandstones and the Limitations of Conventional Nitrogen (N2) Displacement

Tight sandstones are highly susceptible to the water-blocking effect because of their low porosity, low permeability, and the presence of a dense network of micro/nanoscale pore-throat structures. During drilling, completion, and hydraulic-fracture operations, invading fluids readily enter these fine pore systems and become trapped. The NMR results obtained in this study provide a high-resolution characterization of this invasion process. Under capillary-dominated conditions, external fluids quickly enter micro/nanoscale pore-throat networks, causing a rapid increase in water saturation. Once water enters these confined pores, the extremely high capillary forces and strong trapping effects make natural flowback ineffective, ultimately leading to severe water blocking. This dynamic process is consistent with observations from fractal structure analyses, which indicate that reservoirs with higher structural complexity (higher fractal dimensions) exhibit stronger capillary retention and more significant water blocking. Previous numerical simulation studies suggest that water blocking can reduce gas-well productivity by 30–70%. NMR measurements further indicate that high interfacial tension, pore-throat size distribution, and a limited pressure gradient during production are the primary factors controlling the severity of water blocking. These factors restrict the migration of retained water from microscale pores into larger pore spaces, severely hindering gas flow and reducing the effective permeability of the reservoir.

Conventional N2 displacement is widely used to mitigate water blocking. High-pressure nitrogen can partially overcome capillary forces through a combined “unplugging, aiding-flowback, and displacement” effect, thereby removing part of the movable water. Our displacement experiments confirm that when the displacement pressure difference increases to 3–5 MPa, N2 effectively removes movable water from long-T 2 pore domains, particularly from pores with NMR-equivalent radii larger than approximately 300 nm and, to some extent, from those with NMR-equivalent radii of 50–300 nm. This improves local flow pathways and partially restores flow capacity.

However, the experiments also reveal the significant limitations of conventional N2 displacement in ultratight formations (permeability <0.1 mD). Water retained in micro/nanoscale pores forms liquid-film barriers with capillary pressures reaching 5–20 MPa, far exceeding the pressure difference achievable in typical N2 displacement operations. As a result, conventional N2 displacement cannot effectively mobilize bound water in pore domains with NMR-equivalent radii smaller than approximately 50 nm under the present experimental conditions, and the overall displacement efficiency in this study remains below 40% (Figures and ). Moreover, strong heterogeneity and complex NMR-equivalent pore size distributions further reduce the sweep efficiency. In summary, the effectiveness of conventional N2 displacement is fundamentally limited by the micro/nanoscale pore system that controls water blocking in tight sandstones. It cannot break the dominant capillary constraints or improve pore connectivity at the relevant scales. Therefore, a more powerful mitigation strategycapable of reducing capillary retention, restructuring pore-throat geometries, and enhancing the mobility of confined fluidsis needed. This establishes the technical basis for introducing supercritical CO2 (SC–CO2) shut-in soaking, which is discussed in the following section.

Another limitation of the present displacement experiments is that spatially resolved water saturation profiles along the core axis were not obtained. In water-wet tight sandstones, capillary end effects may cause local water accumulation near the outlet boundary during gas drainage, thereby influencing the measured residual water saturation and the displacement efficiency. Therefore, the retained water after N2 displacement should not be attributed solely to capillary trapping in micro/nanoscale pore domains. A contribution from capillary end effects cannot be fully excluded in the absence of Sw­(x) profiles. Future work should combine spatially resolved NMR imaging, segmented core saturation measurements, longer core displacement experiments, or in situ CT scanning to quantify the magnitude and length scale of capillary end effects.

Synergistic Enhancement Mechanisms of SC–CO2-Induced Pore Structure Modification and Subsequent N2 Displacement

SC–CO2-induced pore structure modification and the subsequent increase in N2 displacement are interpreted to improve pore-throat structures and possibly modify water–solid interactions at the micro/nanoscale, thereby reducing the pressure limitations of conventional N2 displacement in tight sandstones. Once CO2 exceeds its critical point (31.1 °C, 7.38 MPa), it exhibits extremely low interfacial tension and high diffusivity, enabling preferential penetration into the 10–50 nm pore-throat systems that are inaccessible to conventional fluids. −

During the SC–CO2 shut-in soaking stage, multiple physicochemical processes occur simultaneously within the core samples. First, dissolved SC–CO2 forms weakly acidic fluids that selectively dissolve carbonate cements such as calcite and dolomite, as well as clay-bearing cemented zones, thereby enlarging the effective radii of fine pore throats and improving pore connectivity. At the CT-resolvable scale, micro-CT 3D reconstructions clearly reveal an increase in the number of larger connected pore domains and in the number of microfracture-related pathways after SC–CO2 treatment (Figure ). The reconstructed models indicate increases in the effective pore volume, average pore-throat coordination number, and overall connectivity within the resolvable pore network. These observations suggest that CO2–mineral reactions can enlarge pore-throat pathways and improve local connectivity. However, because the effective spatial resolution of micro-CT is limited by tomographic reconstruction, filtering, and segmentation, the micro-CT images cannot directly resolve the complete submicron- to nanoscale pore system indicated by the NMR T 2 spectra. Therefore, the micro-CT results are used here as complementary morphological evidence, whereas the evolution of micro/nanoscale water-bearing pores is interpreted mainly from the NMR data. Second, SC–CO2 may reduce the adhesion of water films on pore walls by modifying fluid–solid interactions and extracting organic components, although this process was not directly verified by contact angle measurements in this study. This may weaken the affinity of immobile water, lower the apparent capillary resistance, and improve water mobility in confined pores. ,, This behavior is also confirmed by thin-section observations (Figure ), where the extent of blue-epoxy impregnation clearly increases after treatment. Carbonate cements such as calcite and dolomite show clear dissolution features along pore walls and grain-contact areas, forming small secondary dissolution pores and locally connected pore clusters. These characteristics suggest that SC–CO2-driven dissolution and fluid–rock interactions may weaken water-film retention on mineral surfaces and create more favorable pore-scale conditions for gas displacement. In addition, under high pressure, interfacial stress variations among SC–CO2, water, and minerals may induce microfractures in strongly cemented zones. These fractures can merge with dissolution-enlarged throats to form more continuous flow pathways. , This combined “dissolution–microfracture–connectivity” effect is particularly evident in SC–CO2-treated casting thin sections (Figure a), where microfractures 1–10 μm in width penetrate adjacent pore clusters and produce local “bridging structures,” significantly enhancing fluid-flow connectivity. This synergistic enlargement of pore throats and development of microfractures constitute one of the key physical mechanisms through which SC–CO2 improves flow capacity. Notably, the inferred bridging effect and gas–liquid interaction mechanisms are based mainly on static observations from micro-CT reconstruction, casting thin sections, and NMR T 2 responses and mineralogical changes before and after SC–CO2 treatment. These observations provide indirect but complementary evidence for pore-throat modification, microfracture-related connectivity enhancement, and improved water mobility. However, they do not directly capture the time-resolved evolution of pore-scale fluid flow, mineral dissolution, secondary precipitation, or gas–liquid redistribution during SC–CO2 soaking and subsequent N2 displacement. Therefore, the proposed bridging mechanism should be regarded as an interpretative framework supported by multiscale static evidence rather than as a directly observed dynamic process. Future work should incorporate high-pressure microfluidic visualization, in situ NMR/CT imaging, and dynamic permeability measurements to directly observe SC–CO2–water–rock interactions and gas–water displacement behavior under reservoir-relevant conditions. −

Collectively, these physical and chemical processes define the core enhancement mechanism of SC–CO2: it preferentially unblocks and enlarges nanoscale pore throats that control water blocking, weakens water-film confinement through mixing reactions, and improves overall pore-throat connectivity. As a result, the flow resistance during subsequent N2 displacement is significantly reduced. Multiscale characterization provides complementary evidence for these interpretations: the NMR results indicate reduced bound-water fractions and enhanced water mobility in micro/nanoscale pore domains, and the micro-CT reconstructions reveal marked increases in the effective pore volume and pore-throat connectivity within the CT-resolvable pore network (Figure ); moreover, thin-section analyses provide petrographic evidence of carbonate dissolution and microfracture development (Figure ), and the results of the XRD analysis provide quantitative characterization of the mineral changes, collectively confirming the synergistic enhancement between SC–CO2 and N2 in tight pore systems. Notably, the pore size information derived from NMR depends on the selected surface relaxivity. Because surface relaxivity is influenced by mineral composition, pore-wall roughness, and fluid–rock interactions, the absolute pore-radius values converted from T 2 spectra may contain uncertainty, especially after SC–CO2 treatment, which may alter carbonate cements and clay minerals. Therefore, the NMR-derived pore-size distribution in this study is interpreted as an NMR-equivalent pore-size distribution rather than an absolute geometric pore-throat-size distribution. Nevertheless, the interpretation of pore structure improvement does not rely solely on NMR-derived pore size changes. The combined evidence from micro-CT reconstruction, casting thin sections, and XRD mineralogical variations supports the occurrence of pore-throat enlargement, carbonate dissolution, and improved pore connectivity after SC–CO2 treatment. The experimental results indicate that SC–CO2 treatment increases the effective porosity, enhances the mobilization of bound water, and increases the N2 displacement efficiency from less than 40% under conventional conditions to 50–70%. These findings demonstrate that SC–CO2 has both chemical modification and physical unblocking effects, optimizing pore-throat structures and enabling more efficient gas-flow pathways. Consequently, SC–CO2 achieves multiscale enhancement from microscale pore modification to macroscale flow improvement. This mechanism aligns with previous findings that “SC–CO2 preferentially improves nanoscale pore-throat structures, whereas gas displacement mobilizes remaining water in medium and large pores.” , These results further validate the strong potential of SC–CO2 shut-in soaking coupled with the N2 displacement process for mitigating water blocking in micro/nanoscale pore networks of tight sandstones.

Engineering Implications and Technical Prospects

On the basis of the microscale mechanisms of reservoir modification induced by SC–CO2 and the enhanced N2 displacement performance revealed in this study, the proposed SC–CO2 presoaking strategy clearly demonstrates its engineering value for tight sandstone reservoirs. SC–CO2 shut-in soaking may improve micro- to nanoscale water-bearing pore domains, increase effective flow pathways, and reduce water retention on pore walls under the present experimental conditions. These effects significantly reduce water trapping and expand the displacement range of the subsequent gas injection. The experimental results show that SC–CO2 treatment increases the porosity, enhances the pore connectivity, and increases the release of movable water. Consequently, N2 displacement is associated with a higher water-blocking mitigation efficiency, indicating that this workflow can systematically restore the near-wellbore flow capacity and achieve noticeable productivity enhancement.

Possible wettability-related changes induced by SC–CO2 may reduce apparent capillary resistance and accelerate water flowback, but direct contact angle measurements are still needed to verify this mechanism. Considering the strong heterogeneity of tight reservoirs and the varying responses of different pore-throat types, field applications of this workflow require targeted optimization on the basis of the mineral composition and permeability. For example, in intervals dominated by carbonate cementation, extending the shut-in duration may be necessary to ensure sufficient reaction, whereas in zones with high clay content, combining SC–CO2 treatment with clay-stabilizing additives may help prevent permeability loss caused by mineral swelling. ,

In addition, the proposed workflow offers both productivity enhancement and potential environmental benefits. , CO2 sequestration during the shut-in stage enables partial long-term storage of CO2 in the near-wellbore region, contributing positively to carbon reduction efforts. To promote large-scale application, future research should focus on (i) the phase behavior of SC–CO2–N2 coinjection systems; (ii) the quantitative relationships between the SC–CO2 soaking time and mineral dissolution kinetics; (iii) flow responses under multiple cycles of shut-in and displacement; and (iv) the long-term chemomechanical stability of treated reservoirs. Integrating multiphysics numerical simulations with field-scale pilot tests could lead to the development of optimized SC–CO2 shut-in presoaking (SIP) and N2 displacement design guidelines applicable to various types of tight sandstone reservoirs and support their engineering implementation.

Conclusion

On the basis of systematic experimental investigations and mechanistic analysis, this study reveals the fundamental mechanism and control processes of water blocking in tight sandstone reservoirs and evaluates the effectiveness of the combined integrated SC–CO2 shut-in soaking and N2 displacement processes.

The results demonstrate that water blocking in tight sandstones is governed primarily by capillary-dominated invasion and entrapment of aqueous phases within micro/nanoscale pore domains, particularly in the 10–300 nm NMR-equivalent radius range identified from the T 2 spectral interpretation. The process follows a distinct three-stage temporal evolution, with the most severe damage occurring during the initial rapid invasion stage (1–5 min). High capillary pressures (5–20 MPa) within confined pore systems are considered a major cause of persistent liquid retention and a consequent reduction in gas permeability, although local capillary end effects may also contribute to residual water accumulation under core-scale drainage conditions.

The combined SC–CO2 shut-in soaking and N2 displacement process significantly enhances the subsequent N2 displacement efficiency through multiscale modification of the pore network. SC–CO2, characterized by its high diffusivity, low interfacial tension, and chemical reactivity, may facilitate mineral dissolution, organic extraction, and possibly wettability-related changes. These synergistic effects expand effective flow pathways, reduce capillary resistance, and improve phase mobility, thereby increasing the water displacement efficiency from less than 40% (conventional N2 displacement) to 50–70%.

This integrated process has substantial potential for field application, particularly in heterogeneous tight formations. On the basis of the results of the present core-scale experiments, a shut-in duration of several days and a N2 displacement pressure of approximately 4–5 MPa are favorable for improving water-blocking mitigation efficiency. However, the corresponding field-scale productivity response still requires verification through reservoir simulations and pilot tests. Overall, this work bridges microscale pore structure modification with field-scale production enhancement, providing a technically robust and environmentally sustainable strategy for mitigating formation damage in tight gas reservoirs.
