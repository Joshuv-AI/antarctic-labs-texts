Estimation of Toeplitz Covariance Matrices using Overparameterized Gradient Descent
Daniel Busbib and Ami Wiesel

Abstract
We consider covariance estimation under Toeplitz structure. Numerous sophisticated optimization methods have been developed to maximize the Gaussian log-likelihood under Toeplitz constraints. In contrast, recent advances in deep learning demonstrate the surprising power of simple gradient descent (GD) applied to overparameterized models. Motivated by this trend, we revisit Toeplitz covariance estimation through the lens of overparameterized GD. We model the P × P P\times P covariance as a sum of K K complex sinusoids with learnable parameters and optimize them via GD. We show that when K = P K=P , GD may converge to suboptimal solutions. However, mild overparameterization ( K = 2 ​ P K=2P or 4 ​ P 4P ) consistently enables global convergence from random initializations. We further propose an accelerated GD variant with separate learning rates for amplitudes and frequencies. When frequencies are fixed and only amplitudes are optimized, we prove that the optimization landscape is asymptotically benign and any stationary point recovers the true covariance. Finally, numerical experiments demonstrate that overparameterized GD can match or exceed the accuracy of state-of-the-art methods in challenging settings, while remaining simple and scalable.

•

Gradient-based covariance estimation.

We propose a simple gradient descent framework to estimate Toeplitz covariance matrices via an overparameterized Carathéodory decomposition with variable amplitudes and frequencies.
This formulation ensures positive-definiteness automatically, connects classical signal processing with modern optimization, and scales efficiently to large systems.

•

Overparameterization helps.
We validate the proposed method across multiple scenarios including structured covariances, autoregressive models, and random settings. The results clearly show that the exactly-parameterized GD may fail but overparameterized GD matches or outperforms state-of-the-art methods like ATOM.

•

Separate learning rates accelerate convergence.

We derive the full Hessian and show that the loss function has much higher curvature with respect to the frequencies than to the amplitudes. Based on this analysis, we derive a GD variant that uses smaller step sizes for the frequencies and significantly accelerates the convergence in practice.

•

Benign landscape for amplitude optimization.
The Toeplitz covariance estimation is non-convex and may scare researchers

In this setting, we prove the landscape is well behaved: any stationary point of the negative log likelihood recovers the true covariance in the population setting, and remains close to it under small sample covariance perturbations.

II
Problem Formulation

We consider the problem of estimating an unknown Toeplitz covariance matrix given its independent realizations. We focus on the simplest case of zero mean stationary and Gaussian signals. The data
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{P}
is multivariate normal with

\displaystyle E[\bm{x}]=\bm{0}

\displaystyle E[\bm{x}\bm{x}^{\mathrm{H}}]=\bm{C}\in{\mathcal{T}}.

(2)

where
{\mathcal{T}}
is the set of

P\times P
positive definite Toeplitz covariances:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=\{\bm{C}\succ 0:\;\bm{C}=\bm{C}^{\mathrm{H}},\;\bm{C}_{ij}=\mathrm{function}(i-j)\}

(3)

Other than the structure in (
3
), we do not assume any prior on the deterministic unknown covariances.

In order to estimate
\bm{C}
we have access to
M
independent and identically distributed realizations of
\bm{x}
denoted by
\bm{x_{m}}
for
m=1,\cdots,M
. We assume the data is multivariate normal and use the sample covariance
\bm{S}
which is a sufficient statistic:

\bm{S}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\bm{x}_{m}\bm{x}_{m}^{\mathrm{H}}.

(4)

The goal is therefore to estimate an unknown
\bm{C}\in{\mathcal{T}}
given
\bm{S}\succeq 0
.

III
Maximum Likelihood via Gradient Descent

In this section, we propose a GD approach for computing the maximum likelihood estimator of a positive definite Toeplitz covariance matrix given its Gaussian realizations. For this purpose, we exploit a parametrization that ensures positivity while bypassing the constraints and derive the associated gradients in closed form. The negative log likelihood is non-convex and GD may converge to a spurious local minima. Motivated by the recent successes of over-parameterization in deep learning, we too address this challenge via an over-parameterized decomposition.

The starting point of our algorithm is the parameterization of the unknown Toeplitz covariance matrix using the Carathéodory decomposition:

\hat{\bm{C}}(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{\omega}})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}s(\hat{a}_{k})\bm{v}(\hat{\omega}_{k})\bm{v}(\hat{\omega}_{k})^{\mathrm{H}}+\varepsilon\bm{I}_{P},

(5)

where
\hat{\bm{\omega}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
are the frequency parameters and
\hat{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
are the amplitude parameters.
The vectors
\bm{v}(\cdot)
are complex sinusoids defined as

\bm{v}(\hat{\omega}_{k})=\begin{bmatrix}1&e^{j\hat{\omega}_{k}}&\cdots&e^{j\hat{\omega}_{k}(P-1)}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

(6)

and
s(\cdot)
is the softplus operator (or any other operator that ensures positivity). The term
\varepsilon\bm{I}_{P}
with
\varepsilon>0
is introduced to ensure that the covariance matrix is always positive definite. It can also be interpreted as a noise component but we keep it fixed and model both the signals and the noise via the complex sinusoids. Altogether, the decomposition in (
5
) ensures that
\hat{\bm{C}}(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{\omega}})
can model arbitrary positive definite Toeplitz matrices, yet it is always strictly positive definite and stable.
The number of components
K
controls the model complexity:

•

K<P
: low-rank plus small noise approximations.

•

K=P
: arbitrary positive definite Toeplitz matrices.

•

K\geq P
: overparameterized models.

Our goal is to compute the maximum likelihood estimate

\min_{\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{\omega}}}\;\mathcal{L}(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{\omega}})

(7)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the negative log-likelihood (NLL) up to an additive constant given the Gaussian data:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{\omega}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\bm{x}_{m}^{\mathrm{H}}\hat{\bm{C}}(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{\omega}})^{-1}\bm{x}_{m}+\log|\hat{\bm{C}}(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{\omega}})|

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{S}\,\hat{\bm{C}}(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{\omega}})^{-1})+\log|\hat{\bm{C}}(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{\omega}})|

(8)

We propose to solve (
7
) using a standard GD approach as detailed in Algorithm 1. The algorithm is completely straightforward, simple and scalable. It uses the most natural initialization and then iteratively updates the amplitudes and frequencies based on their gradients.

The computational complexity of the proposed GD algorithm can be understood by separating the cost per iteration from the total number of iterations required for convergence. At each iteration, the heaviest is the inversion of the Toeplitz covariance matrix
\hat{\bm{C}}(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{\omega}})\in\mathbb{C}^{P\times P}
. A naive inversion scales as
\mathcal{O}(P^{3})
, which quickly becomes expensive for large
P
. Fortunately, Toeplitz structure can be exploited to accelerate this step: classical algorithms such as Levinson-Durbin or Schur recursions reduce the complexity to
\mathcal{O}(P^{2})
, while FFT-based methods can achieve nearly
\mathcal{O}(P\log P)
. Other operations, including gradient evaluations, matrix-vector multiplications, and line-search updates, are comparatively cheap and do not affect the overall scaling.

The main factor in the computational complexity is the number of GD iterations. The convergence rate of GD depends on many factors including initializations, step size choice and the smoothness of the objective. In practice, in the case of Toeplitz estimation the rate is quite slow. In the next section, we propose a simple trick to accelerate it and a numerical analysis of the complexity is provided in the experimental section below.

IV
Separate Learning Rates

In this section, we propose a simple modification to Algorithm 1 that significantly improves its convergence rate. Extensive experiments with the algorithm suggest that its main inefficiency stems from the different sensitivities of the amplitude and frequency parameters in the loss landscape. It is well known that better performance can be obtained using distinct step sizes for different parameter blocks of variables
[
33
,
34
]
.
Specifically, in our setting, the amplitudes can be updated more aggressively without compromising stability, whereas the frequencies require more cautious steps due to the higher nonlinearity in their gradient. To address this, we propose to assign separate learning rates to the amplitudes and the frequencies. The complete procedure is summarized in Algorithm 2.
This minor modification results in a significant practical speedup. Empirical experiments show that convergence is accelerated by a factor of
3
to
5
compared to the baseline Algorithm 1 with a single learning rate.

Algorithm 2 uses an Armijo backtracking line search with two separate learning rates. At each iteration, the step sizes are initialized at
\eta^{(a)}_{t}=\eta^{(a)}_{0}
and
\eta^{(\omega)}_{t}=\eta^{(\omega)}_{0}

\beta\in(0,1)
until the following condition is satisfied:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\hat{\bm{a}}_{t}-\eta^{(a)}_{t}\nabla_{\hat{\bm{a}}}\mathcal{L}(\hat{\bm{a}}_{t},\hat{\bm{\omega}}_{t}),\,\hat{\bm{\omega}}_{t}-\eta^{(\omega)}_{t}\nabla_{\hat{\bm{\omega}}}\mathcal{L}(\hat{\bm{a}}_{t},\hat{\bm{\omega}}_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}(\hat{\bm{a}}_{t},\hat{\bm{\omega}}_{t})

\displaystyle\quad-\alpha\left(\eta^{(a)}_{t}\|\nabla_{\hat{\bm{a}}}\mathcal{L}(\hat{\bm{a}}_{t},\hat{\bm{\omega}}_{t})\|^{2}+\eta^{(\omega)}_{t}\|\nabla_{\hat{\bm{\omega}}}\mathcal{L}(\hat{\bm{a}}_{t},\hat{\bm{\omega}}_{t})\|^{2}\right)

where
\alpha\in(0,0.5)
is a fixed constant.

To motivate and justify the use of separate learning rates, in the rest of this section, we analyze the local smoothness of the loss function with respect to amplitude and frequency blocks. The convergence rate of gradient descent depends on how smooth the loss landscape is in different directions, which is captured by the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}\!\mathcal{L}(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{\omega}})
:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\!\mathcal{L}(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{\omega}})=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{H}_{aa}&\bm{H}_{a\omega}\\[2.84526pt]
\bm{H}_{\omega a}&\bm{H}_{\omega\omega}\end{bmatrix},

(16)

When some parameters are much less smooth than others (have larger curvature), using a single learning rate performs poorly. The optimal step size for each parameter block is inversely proportional to its local Lipschitz constant, defined as the spectral norm of the corresponding Hessian block. Using separate rates can be interpreted as block-diagonal preconditioning or metric gradient descent.

For the Toeplitz covariance problem, the block diagonal Hessians are defined through the following key derivatives. Recall that
s(\hat{a}_{k})=\log(1+e^{\hat{a}_{k}})
is the softplus activation that converts unbounded parameters
\hat{a}_{k}
into positive gains. We have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{C}}}{\partial\hat{a}_{j}}

\displaystyle=s^{\prime}(\hat{a}_{j})\bm{v}_{j}\bm{v}_{j}^{\mathrm{H}},

(17)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{C}}}{\partial\hat{\omega}_{j}}

\displaystyle=s(\hat{a}_{j})\,j\big(\bm{D}\bm{v}_{j}\bm{v}_{j}^{\mathrm{H}}-\bm{v}_{j}(\bm{D}\bm{v}_{j})^{\mathrm{H}}\big),

(18)

and the derivative of
\bm{E}=\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}-\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\bm{S}\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}
with respect to either parameter
\hat{\theta}_{j}\in\{\hat{a}_{j},\hat{\omega}_{j}\}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{E}}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{j}}

\displaystyle=-\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{C}}}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{j}}\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}+\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{C}}}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{j}}\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\bm{S}\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\quad+\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\bm{S}\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{C}}}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{j}}\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}.

(19)

The Hessian blocks are then given by:

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{aa}[i,j]

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij}s^{\prime\prime}(\hat{a}_{i})\,\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathrm{H}}\bm{E}\bm{v}_{i}+s^{\prime}(\hat{a}_{i})\,\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathrm{H}}\frac{\partial\bm{E}}{\partial\hat{a}_{j}}\bm{v}_{i},

(20)

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{\omega\omega}[i,j]

\displaystyle=2s(\hat{a}_{i})\,\mathrm{Im}\left\{\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathrm{H}}\bm{D}\frac{\partial\bm{E}}{\partial\hat{\omega}_{j}}\bm{v}_{i}\right\}(1-\delta_{ij})

\displaystyle\quad+2s(\hat{a}_{i})\delta_{ij}\,\mathrm{Im}\Big\{(j\bm{D}\bm{v}_{i})^{\mathrm{H}}\bm{D}\bm{E}\bm{v}_{i}

\displaystyle\qquad+\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathrm{H}}\bm{D}\frac{\partial\bm{E}}{\partial\hat{\omega}_{j}}\bm{v}_{i}+\bm{v}_{i}^{\mathrm{H}}\bm{D}\bm{E}(j\bm{D}\bm{v}_{i})\Big\}.

(21)

Computing the spectral norms of these Hessian blocks exactly is difficult. Instead, but we now provide simple approximations that capture the dominant scaling behavior. The local Lipschitz constants (inverse smoothness) at any point
(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{\omega}})
can be approximated as:

\displaystyle L_{a}(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{\omega}})

\displaystyle=\|\bm{H}_{aa}\|_{2}\approx P\,\|\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\|_{2}

(22)

\displaystyle L_{\omega}(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{\omega}})

\displaystyle=\|\bm{H}_{\omega\omega}\|_{2}\approx P^{1.5}\ \|\bm{s}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\|_{2}^{3/2}

(23)

where
\|\bm{s}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{k}s(\hat{a}_{k})^{2}}
.
These are not global constants but rather local quantities that depend on the current parameter estimates. Larger Lipschitz constants mean less smooth landscapes requiring smaller step sizes. The approximations ignore subdominant cross-terms and assume the parameters are not near pathological configurations.

These approximations are numerically illustrated in Figure
1
where we compare the estimates with the exact values over 1000 trials across different problem configurations with varying values of
P
,
K
and uniformly random variables.

The trends are obvious:
L_{\omega}
is up to four order of magnitude larger than
L_{a}
. It is much more sensitive to
P
and to
\left\|\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\right\|_{2}
.

L_{\omega}
also increases when the amplitudes
\|s\|
are large.
Altogether, these observations suggest two different smoothness regimes and motivate two separate step sizes. In fact, these also hint that future work may consider stronger second order optimization methods that exploit the closed form Hessian matrices.

V
Analysis with Known Frequencies

The main idea of this paper is that Toeplitz covariance estimation can be approached through direct optimization of an overparameterized Carathéodory decomposition.
Since the optimization problem is non-convex, understanding its landscape is crucial.
A full analysis with respect to both the amplitudes
(\bm{a})
and the frequencies
(\bm{\omega})
is beyond the scope of this work.
Here, we build intuition by considering the simpler case in which the frequencies are fixed and we only optimize the amplitudes:

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{a}}\;=\;\arg\min_{\bm{a}}\mathrm{NLL}(\bm{a}),

(24)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{NLL}(\widehat{\bm{a}})\;=\;\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\bm{S}\,\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}\big)+\log\!\det\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}}).

(25)

Our first result shows that the landscape of
\mathrm{NLL}(\bm{a})
is benign in the
population setting
, where the sample covariance is exact and equal to the true covariance
\bm{S}=\bm{C}
.
In this case, any stationary point of the non-convex NLL objective corresponds to a global minimum that recovers the true covariance.
In the overparameterized case, the amplitudes are not uniquely identifiable, but all global minima yield the same true covariance.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that the complex sinusoids
\{\bm{v}(\omega_{k})\}
span
\mathbb{R}^{P}
, and
\bm{S}=\bm{C}
is the true positive definite covariance matrix.
If
\widehat{\bm{a}}
is a stationary point of
\mathrm{NLL}(\bm{a})
with
\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})\succ 0
, then
\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})=\bm{C}
.

This result may appear trivial since we input the true covariance and recover it back. But it establishes that the non-convex optimization is globally well-behaved: any initialization of the decomposition converges to an optimal solution.

This result also extends to the more realistic asymptotic setting where the sample covariance
\bm{S}
is close to, but not equal to, the true covariance. In this case, we assume the estimated covariance to be well conditioned, which is easily achieved in practice by adding regularization
\varepsilon\bm{I}
as in our case or by bounding the amplitudes.
We show that when
\|\bm{\Delta}\|_{2}
is small, any stationary point of NLL yields a covariance estimate that is close to the true covariance.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that the complex sinusoids
\{\bm{v}(\omega_{k})\}
span
\mathbb{R}^{P}
, and the sample covariance is a perturbation of the true positive definite and Toeplitz covariance:
\bm{S}=\bm{C}+\bm{\Delta}
where
\|\bm{\Delta}\|_{F}\leq\varepsilon
.
If
\widehat{\bm{a}}
is a stationary point of
\mathrm{NLL}(\bm{a})
and the estimated covariance satisfies
\mu\bm{I}\preceq\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})\preceq\lambda\bm{I}
, then:

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})-\bm{C}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}

(26)

The theorem shows that NLL optimization is stable with respect to perturbations in the sample covariance. Asymptotically, when
\bm{\Delta}
is small, the landscape of NLL is benign and any stationary point is near the global minima.

For completeness, we note that the seminal SPICE estimator
[
25
]
solves a similar problem. It also fixes the frequencies and only optimizes the amplitudes. However, to ensure global optimality, SPICE relies on a convex approximation to the NLL:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{SPICE}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})\;=\;\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\bm{S}\,\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}\big)+\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})\,\bm{S}^{-1}\big),

(27)

Asymptotically, SPICE and NLL coincide and SPICE is preferable due its convexity. Traditionally, the recommended approach in non-convex optimization was to approximate the objective and minimize it exactly. Recently, there is a growing tendency to directly minimize the non-convex objective. Theorem
2
shows that, in the case of asymptotic Toeplitz covariance estimation, this approach is theoretically justified: the landscape is benign and there is no need for convexification.

VII
Discussion and Future Work

This paper shows that simple overparameterized gradient descent works surprisingly well for Toeplitz covariance estimation. The key is to also optimize the frequencies and using more parameters than strictly necessary.

Our theoretical analysis proves that when frequencies are fixed, the optimization landscape is well behaved. While we cannot yet prove this for joint amplitude and frequency optimization, our experiments suggest that it works well in practice.

Another contribution of our work is that the amplitudes and frequencies in the Toeplitz decomposition have very different curvatures. Using separate learning rates for each of them significantly accelerated convergence. This suggests that second-order methods could further improve performance in future work.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
1

Due to the theory of maximum likelihood estimation, if
\bm{S}=\bm{C}
then

\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}}_{\mathrm{NLL}})=\bm{S}=\bm{C}

is clearly a global minimum.
The main part of the proof is that all other stationary points must lead to the same covariance
(but not necessarily the same
\widehat{\bm{a}}
values).

Let
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{P\times K}
be the matrix of steering vectors with full row rank

\mathrm{rank}(\bm{V})=P
, where the columns are
\bm{v}_{k}=\bm{v}(\omega_{k})
.
Recall that
\bm{C}
denotes the true covariance matrix in the population limit (
M\!\to\!\infty
).
For
\widehat{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
such that
\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})\succ 0
, define

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})=\bm{V}\,\mathrm{diag}(\widehat{\bm{a}})\,\bm{V}^{H},\qquad\bm{C}=\bm{V}\,\mathrm{diag}(\bm{a})\,\bm{V}^{H}

(29)

and

\mathrm{NLL}(\widehat{\bm{a}})=\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\bm{C}\,\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}\big)+\log\det\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}}).

Then every stationary point
\widehat{\bm{a}}
satisfies

\nabla_{\widehat{\bm{a}}}\mathrm{NLL}(\widehat{\bm{a}})=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})=\bm{C}.

Since
\bm{V}
has full row rank and
\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})\succ 0
by assumption, the matrix is invertible and the NLL is well-defined.

Define

\displaystyle\bm{M}(\widehat{\bm{a}})

\displaystyle:=\frac{\partial\mathrm{NLL}}{\partial\widehat{\bm{C}}}=\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}-\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}\bm{C}\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}.

(30)

By the chain rule and

\tfrac{\partial\widehat{\bm{C}}}{\partial\widehat{a}_{k}}=\bm{v}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}^{H}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathrm{NLL}}{\partial\widehat{a}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\big\langle\bm{M}(\widehat{\bm{a}}),\,\bm{v}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}^{H}\big\rangle_{F}

(31)

\displaystyle=\bm{v}_{k}^{H}\bm{M}(\widehat{\bm{a}})\bm{v}_{k},\qquad k=1,\dots,K,

(32)

where
\langle\bm{X},\bm{Y}\rangle_{F}=\operatorname{tr}(\bm{X}^{H}\bm{Y})

is the Frobenius inner product.
Thus, at a stationary point
\widehat{\bm{a}}
,

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{k}^{H}\bm{M}(\widehat{\bm{a}})\bm{v}_{k}

\displaystyle=0,\qquad\forall k.

(33)

Let

\mathcal{S}:=\mathrm{span}\{\bm{v}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}^{H}:\,k=1,\dots,K\}
.
Since
\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}}),\bm{C}\in\mathcal{S}
, their difference satisfies

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}

\displaystyle:=\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})-\bm{C}\;\in\;\mathcal{S}.

(34)

Consider the weighted bilinear form on Hermitian matrices

\displaystyle\langle\bm{X},\bm{Y}\rangle_{\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}\bm{X}\,\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}\bm{Y}\big)

(35)

\displaystyle=\big\langle\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}\bm{X}\,\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1},\,\bm{Y}\big\rangle_{F}.

(36)

This defines an inner product on
\mathcal{S}
: for any
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}
,

\displaystyle\langle\bm{X},\bm{X}\rangle_{\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})}

\displaystyle=\big\|\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1/2}\bm{X}\,\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1/2}\big\|_{F}^{2}\;\geq\;0,

(37)

with equality iff
\bm{X}=0
.

Now observe

\displaystyle\bm{M}(\widehat{\bm{a}})

\displaystyle=\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}-\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}\bm{C}\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}

(38)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}\big(\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})-\bm{C}\big)\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}

(39)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}\bm{\Gamma}\,\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1}.

(40)

For each
k
,

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{k}^{H}\bm{M}(\widehat{\bm{a}})\bm{v}_{k}

\displaystyle=\big\langle\bm{M}(\widehat{\bm{a}}),\,\bm{v}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}^{H}\big\rangle_{F}

(41)

\displaystyle=\big\langle\bm{\Gamma},\,\bm{v}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}^{H}\big\rangle_{\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})}.

(42)

and the stationary conditions (
33
) can be written as

\displaystyle\big\langle\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Y}\big\rangle_{\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall\quad\bm{Y}\in\{\bm{v}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}^{H}\}_{k=1}^{K},

(43)

By linearity we get

\displaystyle\big\langle\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Y}\big\rangle_{\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})}

\displaystyle=0,\qquad\forall\quad\bm{Y}\in\mathcal{S}.

(44)

Since
\bm{\Gamma}\in\mathcal{S}
, we can take
\bm{Y}=\bm{\Gamma}
and conclude

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{\Gamma},\bm{\Gamma}\rangle_{\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})}

(45)

\displaystyle=\big\|\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1/2}\bm{\Gamma}\,\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})^{-1/2}\big\|_{F}^{2}.

(46)

Thus
\bm{\Gamma}=0
, i.e.

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{C}}(\widehat{\bm{a}})

\displaystyle=\bm{C}.

(47)

∎

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
2

This proof follows the same ideas as before but allows small deviations from zero. At the stationary point:

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{k}^{H}\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\Big[\hat{\bm{C}}-\bm{S}\Big]\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\bm{v}_{k}=0,\quad\forall k.

(48)

Since
\hat{\bm{C}},\bm{C}\in\mathcal{S}
(both are feasible Toeplitz covariances), define:

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}:=\hat{\bm{C}}-\bm{C}\in\mathcal{S}.

(49)

Define the weighted inner product:

\displaystyle\langle\bm{X},\bm{Y}\rangle_{\hat{\bm{C}}}:=\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\bm{X}\,\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\bm{Y}\big).

(50)

Substituting
\bm{S}=\bm{C}+\bm{\Delta}
into (
48
):

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{k}^{H}\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\Big[\hat{\bm{C}}-\bm{C}-\bm{\Delta}\Big]\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\bm{v}_{k}

\displaystyle=0

(51)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{k}^{H}\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\Big[\bm{\Gamma}-\bm{\Delta}\Big]\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1}\bm{v}_{k}

\displaystyle=0.

(52)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\Gamma},\,\bm{v}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}^{H}\rangle_{\hat{\bm{C}}}=\langle\bm{\Delta},\,\bm{v}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}^{H}\rangle_{\hat{\bm{C}}},\quad\forall k.

(53)

Thus, we get to a similar condition as in (
44
) where the right hand side is a small error instead of zero:

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\Gamma},\bm{Y}\rangle_{\hat{\bm{C}}}=\langle\bm{\Delta},\bm{Y}\rangle_{\hat{\bm{C}}},\quad\forall\bm{Y}\in\mathcal{S}.

(54)

Since
\bm{\Gamma}\in\mathcal{S}
, take
\bm{Y}=\bm{\Gamma}
in (
54
):

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\Gamma},\bm{\Gamma}\rangle_{\hat{\bm{C}}}=\langle\bm{\Delta},\bm{\Gamma}\rangle_{\hat{\bm{C}}}.

(55)

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle|\langle\bm{\Delta},\bm{\Gamma}\rangle_{\hat{\bm{C}}}|

\displaystyle\leq\langle\bm{\Delta},\bm{\Delta}\rangle_{\hat{\bm{C}}}^{1/2}\cdot\langle\bm{\Gamma},\bm{\Gamma}\rangle_{\hat{\bm{C}}}^{1/2}.

(56)

Therefore:

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\Gamma},\bm{\Gamma}\rangle_{\hat{\bm{C}}}

\displaystyle\leq\langle\bm{\Delta},\bm{\Delta}\rangle_{\hat{\bm{C}}}^{1/2}\cdot\langle\bm{\Gamma},\bm{\Gamma}\rangle_{\hat{\bm{C}}}^{1/2},

(57)

which gives:

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\Gamma},\bm{\Gamma}\rangle_{\hat{\bm{C}}}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\langle\bm{\Delta},\bm{\Delta}\rangle_{\hat{\bm{C}}}^{1/2}.

(58)

Thus:

\displaystyle\big\|\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\bm{\Gamma}\,\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\big\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\bm{\Delta}\,\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\big\|_{F}.

(59)

Using submultiplicativity of norms and
\|\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\|_{2}=\lambda_{\max}(\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1})^{1/2}\leq\mu^{-1/2}
:

\displaystyle\big\|\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\bm{\Delta}\,\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\big\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\|\bm{\Delta}\|_{F}

(60)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mu}\cdot\|\bm{\Delta}\|_{F}.

(61)

Since
\|\bm{\Delta}\|_{F}\leq\varepsilon
:

\displaystyle\big\|\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\bm{\Delta}\,\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\big\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mu}\cdot\varepsilon.

(62)

Thus, from (
59
):

\displaystyle\big\|\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\bm{\Gamma}\,\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\big\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\mu}\cdot\varepsilon.

(63)

Using
\|\hat{\bm{C}}^{1/2}\|_{2}=\lambda_{\max}(\hat{\bm{C}})^{1/2}\leq\lambda^{1/2}
:

\displaystyle\|\bm{\Gamma}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{\bm{C}}^{1/2}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\big\|\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\bm{\Gamma}\,\hat{\bm{C}}^{-1/2}\big\|_{F}

(64)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\cdot\frac{1}{\mu}\cdot\varepsilon.

(65)

Thus:

\displaystyle\|\hat{\bm{C}}-\bm{C}\|_{F}\leq\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\cdot\varepsilon.

(66)

Squaring both sides:

\displaystyle\|\hat{\bm{C}}-\bm{C}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}.

(67)

∎
