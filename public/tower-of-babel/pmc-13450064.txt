Exploratory study of self-reported disability, accommodations, and mistreatment among residents at an academic medical center
Authors: Sandra Carpenter, Henrike Schmalfuss, Nensi Ruzgar, Carrie Tibbles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited research has examined self-reported disability and disability-related experiences among residents. Background

Residents with disabilities represent a significant population of physician learners with distinct needs related to accommodations and support systems throughout residency training [1–6]. Approximately 2.6–3.1% of practicing physicians in the United States report having a disability, though this is likely an underestimate [7, 8]. National surveys estimate the prevalence of disability among trainees to be between 5 and 12% with variations by specialty; however, these surveys often examine residents at only one training level or from a single specialty [9–17]. In a 2023 survey of interns (first-year residents) across 86 surgical and non-surgical residency programs at 64 US institutions, 11.8% of respondents self-reported disability [9].

Previous studies demonstrate that many trainees with disabilities do not request accommodations because of concerns regarding stigma or bias, as well as unclear institutional processes for requesting accommodations [9, 13, 18, 19]. Compared with residents without disabilities, residents with disabilities who have unmet access needs exhibit increased depressive symptoms and self-reported medical errors [13]. Physicians with disabilities may also experience disability-related mistreatment in the workplace which can lead to burnout and attrition [17, 20, 21].

Although disability policies exist at many GME institutions, review of these policies suggest that many lack key elements identified as best practices by the Association of American Medical Colleges [22]. These gaps may go unrecognized and adversely impact resident wellbeing and education, underscoring the need to better understand resident experiences and improve institutional support for residents with disabilities. This single-site study sought to explore disability experiences among residents at a single academic medical center to inform strategies for improving support systems for trainees with disabilities in accordance with best practices.

Methods

The Beth Israel Deaconess Institutional Review Board approved this study (Protocol number: 2024P000376; 7/1/2024). Between October 2024 and February 2025, a web-based survey was sent to all resident physicians in 15 core residency programs employed at an academic medical center, excluding non-ACGME accredited programs (podiatry) and fellows. Residents received periodic reminders via email to complete the survey [Supplemental Appendix: Survey Instrument]. The recruitment emails were required to state that the study purpose was to assess the experiences of residents with disabilities. In order to mitigate selection bias, the recruitment emails stated that all residents were eligible to complete the survey, but that some questions may apply only to certain residents. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. Respondents who answered “yes” to the disability status question were presented with follow-up questions on disability type, accommodations, and mistreatment. Demographic and disability-related questions were adapted from the prior Intern Health study, a longitudinal cohort study that assessed the factors underlying depression as well as disability status among U.S. interns [9, 23]. The mistreatment questions were adapted from those used by the American Association of Medical Colleges year 2 and graduation questionnaire [24]. The survey questions aimed to assess self-reported disability and type of disability, accommodations, mistreatment, and support systems in the residency program. In addition to the survey, semi-structured interviews with residents with disabilities were also conducted as an exploratory qualitative component of the study; however, because the number of interviews was insufficient to achieve thematic saturation and to protect resident confidentiality, interview data were not included in the present analysis.

Results

102 individual surveys were entered and reviewed for completeness (18.3% of the target population). Of those, 91 respondents completed > 50% of demographic questions, including the disability status question, and were included for analysis, representing an overall 16.3% response rate (91/558). Survey respondents represented all 15 core residency programs across both surgical and non-surgical specialties. Approximately half of respondents were female (48/90; 53%), and nearly all were under the age of 40 years (72/74; 97%). Residents from all clinical training years responded. About one-quarter of respondents (25/91; 27.5%) were from surgical specialties [Table 1: Respondent Demographics, Self-Reported Disability, and Accommodations]. 12 respondents (12/91; 13.2%) self-reported having a disability; of those, four (4/12; 33.3%) reported having more than one disability. Respondents with disabilities represented all clinical years, and five (5/12; 41.6%) were from surgical specialties. The most frequent disability type was neurodevelopmental/learning disability followed by chronic health conditions.

Among residents with disabilities, four (4/12; 33.3%) answered “yes” to having accommodations provided by the residency program, while the remaining eight responded “no” (5/12; 41.7%) or “somewhat” (3/12; 25%). The most commonly cited reasons for non-accommodation were perceived lack of need and an unclear institutional process for requesting accommodations, however residents also indicated fear of stigma or bias as a reason why they did not request accommodations.

On the survey, five residents with disabilities (5/12; 41.7%) indicated neutrality regarding their satisfaction with the accommodations process, while four (4/12; 33.3%) were very satisfied or satisfied and three (3/12; 25%) were very dissatisfied. Whereas 36% (28/78) of respondents without disabilities indicated that they were aware of disability-specific resources, only one (1/12, 8%) of the respondents with disabilities was aware of resources. Therefore, the majority of total respondents, including all but one of the respondents with disabilities, indicated that they were not aware of any disability-specific resources provided by the residency program.

With respect to mistreatment, 75% (58/89) of respondents without disabilities indicated awareness of policies regarding resident mistreatment, however only about half of respondents with disabilities were aware of such policies (6/11; 54.5%) [Table 2: Disability-Related Mistreatment, Disclosure, and Support]. Four respondents with disabilities (4/12; 33%) reported experiencing disability-related mistreatment, most frequently denial of opportunities for training or rewards and offensive remarks/names. Half of residents who experienced disability-related mistreatment reported it to the appropriate channels and all felt neutral or dissatisfied with the outcome of reporting. The most commonly cited reason for not reporting incidents of disability-related mistreatment was fear of reprisal.

Disability-related mistreatment, disclosure, and support

The survey respondents with disabilities disclosed their disability status during residency to co-residents (9/11; 81.2%) and residency program leadership (6/11; 54.5%). Approximately one-third of respondents with disabilities (4/11; 36.4%) agreed that their residency supervisors were understanding and respectful of the disability, while the majority (8/11; 72.7%) indicated that their co-residents were understanding and respectful of the disability.

Discussion

This exploratory study examines self-reported disability and disability-related experiences across residents from 15 core specialties and all training levels at academic medical center. In this sample of residents from a single institution, 13% of residents self-reported disability which is consistent with prior literature from national surveys documenting a rate of 7.5–11.9% [9, 13–16, 18]. Aligned with other studies, the most common disability types were neurodevelopmental/learning and chronic health disabilities [9]. The findings highlight that a substantial portion of residents with disabilities at our institution do not have formal disability accommodations which has been shown in other larger, national studies as well [9, 13, 18]. While the reasons are multifactorial, survey responses indicated that residents do not perceive they need accommodations or are uncertain how to request them. However, other barriers may be at play, as some residents also cited fear of reprisal as a reason they did not have accommodations. In a prior qualitative interview study with internal medicine program directors, GME leaders acknowledged significant barriers to disability disclosure and accommodations for residents, like stigma and discrimination, inadequate disability representation, and poor programmatic marketing of accommodations [25]. One of the issues identified in the survey was a lack of clear institutional processes for requesting formal protected accommodations, an issue that has been echoed in prior studies and likely exists at many GME institutions. This may lead residents to use ad hoc, informal accommodations which may be easier to arrange with their residency program, but are not necessarily protected. It is critical for institutions to provide clear, accessible, and proactive information regarding formal accommodation requests, including during GME orientation, through online resources, and at regular intervals throughout training. Importantly, in the prior study of program directors, the directors emphasized the need for “information sharing” across programs to learn from others’ accommodations experiences and enhance disability representation [25]. At our institution, the accommodations process is typically initiated at the program-level, and this may be the case at other institutions as well. Therefore, we recognize a need to streamline the accommodations process; safeguard resident confidentiality while eliminating conflicts of interest within GME; and increase resident awareness of institution-wide GME disability policy in accordance with best practices [4, 26].

One-third of residents with disabilities experienced disability-related mistreatment in this study. While disability-related mistreatment has been studied among practicing physicians and medical students, this is one of the few studies in the literature to our knowledge that explicitly assesses disability-related mistreatment among residents [13, 17, 20, 21, 27–29]. A survey study of 1800 medical students with self-disclosed disability on the Association of American Medical Colleges graduation questionnaire reported that 12.4% experienced disability-based discrimination during medical school, most commonly from clinical faculty and residents themselves [17]. It is possible, then, that residents with disabilities experience mistreatment from those same sources, potentially their supervisors and peers. While the survey questions in this study did not ask specifically who perpetrated the mistreatment, the findings at our institution indicate a need to better educate residents and faculty to recognize disability-related mistreatment, which can manifest differently from other forms of mistreatment, and to raise awareness of the confidential channels for reporting it. It is especially critical that residents are able to report disability-related mistreatment to an individual that is external to their program and does not have any supervisory role in GME as confidentiality concerns and conflicts of interest may lead to underreporting [4, 26].

Currently, disability-specific resources at our institution are publicized through GME-wide emails and integrated into other wellness and inclusion initiatives, but the survey raised some concerns about resident awareness of these resources. Notably, the survey revealed that a greater proportion of respondents without disabilities indicated they were aware of disability-specific resources and mistreatment policies compared to residents with disabilities, potentially reflecting meaningful differences in access to information and program culture for residents with disabilities. The reasons for these discrepancies remain unclear and may indicate a lack of truly accessible or disability-tailored resources, limited awareness of existing supports, or reluctance among residents with disabilities to engage with them. Further research is needed at our institution to clarify these possibilities. Importantly, gaps in perception may work against collective advocacy for change if an institution perceives themselves as more inclusive and accessible than they actually are. Although many trainees with disabilities at our institution reported that their program leadership and co-residents were accepting of disability on the survey, there is still a need for dedicated peer support networks to share information among residents and cultivate a more inclusive institutional culture. These findings underscore the need for institutions to periodically evaluate whether existing resources are truly accessible, well publicized, and tailored to the needs of residents with disabilities.

This study has several limitations. Nevertheless, by sharing our institutional findings, we hope to encourage other programs to evaluate the experiences of trainees with disabilities and critically assess whether existing support systems are effective and aligned with best practices. These results reflect the resident cohort at a single academic medical center and may not be generalizable to other programs or hospitals. The response rate was low (16%), and the small sample size limited the ability to perform adequately powered statistical comparisons; therefore, the findings are presented descriptively. Additionally, because the recruitment materials necessarily identified the study as focusing on disability, selection bias may have impacted participation. Furthermore, involvement of the director of GME as one of the authors may have influenced study participation, however their involvement was fundamental to the conception, development, and implementation of the study and continued work on related quality improvement initiatives at the institution. To mitigate possible conflict of interest, the study was conducted by trainees, the survey was anonymous, and the GME director did not have access to or a role in data acquisition or analysis. In addition, given the private and potentially stigmatizing nature of the questions, all survey questions were voluntary, and review of responses for completion identified 11 surveys that lacked necessary demographic information, thereby excluding them from analysis. In addition, some survey results with fewer than 10 respondents were combined or not reported to safeguard respondent confidentiality. Questions related to disability, accommodations, and mistreatment may have been interpreted differently by different trainees. The assessment of disability, accommodations, and mistreatment were based on resident self-report, and it is not possible to confirm the validity of the responses. Further studies are needed to explore the barriers and facilitators residents with disabilities face throughout their training. Quality improvement initiatives are planned at our institution to evaluate the impact of changes to the disability accommodations request and mistreatment reporting processes.

Conclusions

This exploratory study found that 13% of residents at a single academic medical center self-reported having a disability. More than half of respondents with disabilities lacked formal accommodations, and one-third experienced disability-related mistreatment. These findings underscore the need for GME stakeholders to streamline disability accommodation processes, ensure confidential reporting systems, and actively foster supportive training environments for residents with disabilities in accordance with disability policy best practices.
