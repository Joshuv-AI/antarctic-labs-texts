Dietary metal chelator, phytochelatin 2, increases selenium and alters metal homeostasis and associated lipid metabolism in the liver
Authors: Zachery R Jarrell, Ho Young Lee, Choon-Myung Lee, Michael L Orr, Dean P Jones, Young-Mi Go
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiological systems have evolved highly regulated systems to ensure homeostatic levels of trace minerals, such as selenium (Se), which are important to metabolic function and signaling. Much of the understanding of these systems is limited to endogenous proteins and small molecules used for trafficking of minerals. Phytochelatins, a class of plant-derived metal chelating peptides with the general structure, (γ-Glu-Cys)n-Gly, are ubiquitous in the diet and were recently found associated with Se and other metals in human urine. These findings suggest that diet-derived phytochelatins could influence metal homeostasis alongside known endogenous metal-binding compounds. In the present study, we investigated the impact of long-term, oral phytochelatin supplementation on metal homeostasis in a murine model. Phytochelatin supplementation increased Se, zinc and cobalt in the liver and increased urinary Se. Integrative analysis of liver metal profiles with untargeted, high-resolution liver metabolomics revealed dynamic metallome interaction with lipid and carbohydrate metabolism. These results highlight an active role of dietary phytochelatins in modulating mammalian metal homeostasis and associated metabolism. Such dietary components could play a pivotal role in regulating trace metal homeostasis and metal-driven pathophysiology.Keywords: Metabolomics; Metallomics; Nutrition; PyC, Trace metals

Introduction

Trace mineral nutrients, such as the metalloid, selenium (Se), along with several trace metals, are vital to life. They are integral to numerous aspects of physiological function, including metabolism and redox homeostasis [1–4]. These minerals contribute catalytic activity to enzymes and contribute to protein structure [1–4]. Some also engage in important signaling cascades as free metal ions [2]. Se, specifically, is an important contributor of electrophilic activity to many enzymes important to maintaining redox systems [1]. These trace minerals require fine regulation of their abundance in biological compartments, with the liver, small intestines and kidneys having important roles in homeodynamic regulation [1,2]. Too little of a given mineral results in loss of metabolic function and disruption of signaling cascades, while even minor overabundance can result in metal toxicity [1–3,5]. As a result, biological systems have evolved intricate controls for the transport and distribution of nutrient minerals [3,4]. Much of the understanding of these systems is limited to endogenous proteins, such as ceruloplasmin and the cysteine-rich metallothionein, with some attention given also to endogenous small molecule carriers such as glutathione (GSH) [1,6–8]. Additional metal chelating compounds, however, are abundant in the diet.

Phytochelatins (PyCs) are a major group of metal chelating compounds present in plant-based components of the diet [9–11]. PyCs are short peptides produced enzymatically from GSH (γ-Glu-Cys-Gly) in plants with increased production in response to metal exposures [12]. Importantly, however, PyC production tends to occur in excess relative to metal abundance [12,13], so that consumption of PyC in plant-based diets could modulate metal homeostasis in animals. Phytochelatin synthase polymerizes γ-Glu-Cys subunits from GSH to produce PyCs with the general structure, (γ-Glu-Cys)n-Gly [10,12]. This results in low-molecular weight peptides with multiple vicinal thiol, carboxyl and amino groups available for interaction with metals [10,12,14,15]. PyCs are capable of binding numerous metal species to some degree, but they exhibit the strongest interactions with metals which behave as soft acids, with the highest demonstrated affinity for cadmium [12,15,16].

Humans ingest PyCs daily via food consumption. Although estimates are limited, available data indicate that humans consume 0.4 to 9 mg/day of the most abundant PyC, PyC2-Gly [(γ-Glu-Cys)2-Gly] [11]. In cell culture models, human intestinal epithelia absorb short PyCs, both metal-bound and unbound [17]. Recently, we discovered PyCs in urine from healthy humans and found several associations with urinary metals, including the mineral nutrients Se and magnesium (Mg) [18]. We also found that PyC supplementation in female mice leads to increased Se in the kidney, suggesting that dietary PyCs could modify bioavailability of dietary Se [16,18].

The current study was designed to complement our prior studies in female mice with studies in male mice to determine the impact of PyC supplementation on Se content in major organ systems, examine whether PyC supplementation also impacted selected transition metal contents, and determine whether metabolic changes occurred in association with PyC-dependent changes in Se. Results show that PyC supplementation increases uptake of Se from the diet and results in increased hepatic Se and increased urinary excretion. An integrative analysis of trace metals with high-resolution metabolomics further shows that dietary PyC supplementation impacts several other mineral nutrients, including zinc (Zn) and cobalt (Co), and associated lipid and carbohydrate metabolism.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Unless otherwise stated, materials were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Synthetic PyC2-Gly was purchased from CPC Scientific (Rocklin, CA). All metal standards were purchased from either RICCA Chemical Company (Arlington, TX) or Inorganic Ventures (Christiansburg, VA). A mixture of ten stable isotopic chemicals used as internal standards for LC-MS analyses included [trimethyl-13C3] caffeine, [15N,13C5]-L-methionine, [15N]-L-tyrosine, [13C5]-L-glutamic acid, [13C6]-D-glucose, [3,3–13C2]-cystine, [13C18]-linoleic acid, [3′,4′,5′—13C3]-nicotine, [13C6]-L-histidine, and [2,3,4–13C3]-cortisol, all purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories (Tewksbury, MA).

Animal experiments

Experimental protocols for the animal study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Emory University (Protocol #201700691), and experiments aligned with relevant guidelines and regulations. Because our previous findings were in female mice, male mice were investigated in this experiment. Eight-week-old, male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). Mice were housed in a clean facility and given standard mouse food (Laboratory Rodent Diet 5001; LabDiet, St. Louis, MO) and sterile-filtered water ad libitum. Mice were randomized to a control group (Ctrl) or a group receiving 18 μM PyC2-Gly in drinking water (PyC). This level of PyC2-Gly mimics gut lumen concentrations of a person with high intake of plant foods [11]. Treatments (n = 10 per group) lasted for 10 weeks. Drinking water containing PyC2-Gly was replaced weekly. PyC2-Gly was > 95 % in its dithiol form when added to drinking water. By the end of one week, PyC2-Gly was found to be mostly in the intramolecular disulfide form when analyzed by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) using the methods described below. No degradation of the peptide beyond this reversible oxidation was observed. During the study, mice appeared healthy with no apparent adverse effects from the supplemental PyC. Mice were group housed so possible effects on food or water intake were not measured. On the eighth and tenth week of treatment, urine and feces samples were collected from individual mice. After termination of treatments, mice were euthanized using CO2 asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation, and liver, kidney, brain, spleen, lung and testis samples were collected for downstream analysis.

Metal quantification by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS)

Mouse urine, feces, liver, kidney, brain, spleen, testis and lung samples were analyzed by ICP-MS, as described previously [19] with modifications. Briefly, 200 μL urine (minimum of 100 μL for some samples), 60 mg feces and tissue samples were processed by wet acid digestion using concentrated Optima-grade nitric acid and hydrogen peroxide. Scandium and indium were added to samples as internal standards prior to digestion. Samples were randomized within sample type and digested in a MARS 6 microwave digestion system (CEM, Matthews, NC). After digestion and cooling to room temperature, samples were diluted to 10 mL with 2 % nitric acid. Samples were analyzed in triplicate in a random sequence using a Thermo Scientific iCAP Q ICP-MS (Thermo Fisher, Bremen, Germany). Analysis occurred in collision cell mode using kinetic energy discrimination, with helium as the collision gas. All samples were analyzed for Se content. Digested liver samples were later analyzed for Zn, copper (Cu), iron (Fe), Co and Mg content. Linear standard curves were established for each metal: Se – 0.25 to 64 ppb, Zn – 1 to 256 ppb, Cu – 0.25 to 64 ppb, Fe – 4 to 1024 ppb, Co – 0.0156 to 64 ppb, and Mg – 4 to 2048 ppb. All digestions included an analytical batch blank and NIST SRM 2668 levels 1 and 2 which were used for quality control. To compensate for natural variability in excretory samples, post-analysis measures of week 8 and week 10 urine and feces Se were mean summarized within individual.

Determination of relative PyC2-Gly abundance in mouse livers by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

Tissue samples were prepared as described previously for tissue samples [20] with the following modifications. Approximately 30 mg of liver tissues (n = 5) were prepared by carboxymethylation and dansyl derivatization as described. Prepared samples were analyzed using a Waters e2695 Separations Module HPLC with a Waters 2475 fluorescence detector (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA). Separation was achieved on an aminopropyl column (5 μm; 4.6 mm x 25 cm; Supelcosil LC–NH2). Initial solvent conditions were 75 % Solvent A (80 % methanol) and 25 % Solvent B (64 % methanol, 4 M sodium acetate buffer, pH 4.6). A linear gradient ramped from 25 % to 80 % B from 10 min to 52 min. From 50 to 52 min, B ramped linearly back down to 25 % and the column was equilibrated for 8 min. The column temperature was maintained at 30 ◦C, and flow was maintained at 1 mL/min. Detection was obtained with excitation at 335 nm and emission at 518 nm. γ-Glutamyl-glutamate internal standard eluted at 9.2 min. PyC2-Gly in the dithiol form eluted at 38.6 min, and this was confirmed by derivatized chemical standard analyzed both alone and when added to sample matrix. Relative liver dithiol PyC2-Gly abundance was calculated by normalization of the dithiol PyC2-Gly peak intensity to internal standard peak intensity, followed by normalization to tissue mass. Measures are reported relative to the control group average.

Untargeted high-resolution metabolomics (HRM)

Liver tissues were prepared for untargeted HRM by LC-MS as described previously [19,21] with some modifications. Briefly, 20–30 mg liver samples were homogenized by pestle in 2:1 acetonitrile:water containing isotope-labeled internal standards. Fifteen μL mixture per mg tissue was used. Homogenates were kept on ice for 30 min and then centrifuged for 10 min at 21,000 × g and 4 ◦C. Acetonitrile extracts were collected from the supernatant and analyzed on a Q Exactive HF Orbi-trap system (Thermo Fisher). Samples were analyzed in a randomized sequence, with NIST SRM 1950 analyzed at the beginning and end of the sample set, and an additional pooled reference standard (Qstd4) was analyzed at the beginning, middle, and end of the sample set. Qstd4 was pooled from two separate lots of human plasma from Equitech-Bio, Inc (Kerrville, TX). Samples were analyzed using hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography using a Waters XBridge BEH Amide XP HILIC (50 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 µm particle size) column. Electrospray ionization was performed in positive mode. Data collection was continuous throughout chromatography, and mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) were measured in the range of 85–1275 at 120,000 resolution, as previously described [21]. Samples were analyzed in triplicate. Mass spectral data were converted to .mzXML using ProteoWizard v3 [22], and data were extracted using apLCMS v6.6.9 [23] and xMSanalyzer v 2.0.6.1 [24], resulting in metabolic features, defined by a paired m/z and retention time with corresponding signal intensities for each sample. Feature filtering retained features with a median CV of 75 % or less and a minimum mean within-replicate Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.7. Triplicate analyses were median summarized prior to statistical analysis.

HRM data processing and analysis

Metabolomics data were pre-filtered to remove features with > 50 % missing values globally or > 80 % missing values within groups. Data were quantile normalized and log2 transformed prior to analysis, and missing values were imputed using the half of the minimum value detected for a given feature. Liver metabolomes were compared for group-wise differences using limma v3.42.2, and hierarchical clustering of selected features was investigated with differences evaluated at p < 0.05. No features were selected when a false discovery rate < 0.2 was applied. Pathway enrichment analysis, using mummichog v1 [25], was applied to study metabolic changes on the pathway level. This approach protects against type 2 statistical error by including all features at p < 0.05 while protecting against type 1 statistical error by aggregating probabilities from the metabolite level of organization to the pathway level. Only pathway results for which at least 4 selected features could be matched to pathway metabolites by mummichog were considered.

Metabolite annotation and identification

Detected metabolic features were annotated using xMSannotator v1.3.2 [26] with reference to the Human Metabolome Database (http://www.hmdb.ca/). Annotations generated by xMSannotator with a confidence Level of 3 or 2 can be considered Level 4 identifications on the Schymanski scale [27]. Meanwhile, metabolites found to coelute with previously validated metabolites in reference standards are assigned a Level 1 identification [21,27].

Integrative network analysis of metals by metabolome

Integrative network analysis of the liver metabolome and liver metals of interest was performed using xMWAS v1 [28]. Within-group networks were constructed using partial least squares in canonical mode. The correlation threshold was set to |r| ≥ 0.64, the critical r value for an α = 0.05 and n = 10. Community detections in resulting networks were performed by xMWAS, and eigenvector centralities of nodes were calculated using weighted edges, with |r| used for edge weight.

Statistics

All analyses were performed in either R v3.6.3 or R v4.3.1. P-values <0.05 were considered significant. For between-group metal comparisons, Student’s T test was used where appropriate. Comparison of liver PyC2-Gly abundance required use of Welch’s t-test. Comparisons of liver Mg and Co required use of the Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test. For all reported data, n = 10 for both treatment groups unless otherwise noted. In all bar charts, mean ± SEM is provided, and individual data points are displayed.

Results

PyC supplementation modified Se excretion and hepatic Se content

Following the prior observed relationship between urinary PyC and urinary Se, we examined the effects of PyC supplementation on Se excretion. Given that PyC2-Gly is the simplest and among the most naturally abundant glycine-terminating PyCs, PyC2-Gly was used at 18 μM, a level chosen to reflect the upper extreme of expected daily human intake. Without a change in dietary content of food, ten weeks of supplementation with PyC2-Gly resulted in a 25 % increase in urinary Se (p = 0.0214; Fig 1A, top). Likewise, fecal Se trended toward decreasing with PyC supplementation (p = 0.0619; Fig 1A, bottom), suggesting the increase in urinary Se was due to either an increase in Se absorption from the diet or a decrease in biliary excretion. These effects of PyC supplementation on Se excretion also suggested that PyC supplementation may have wider effects on Se content of major organs, so we quantified Se in the liver (Fig 1B), kidney (Supplemental Fig 1A), brain (Supplemental Fig 1B), spleen (Supplemental Fig 1C), lung (Supplemental Fig 1D), and testis (Supplemental Fig 1E). All organs had no changes in their Se content, except for the liver (Fig 1B). The livers of PyC-supplemented mice had an 11 % increase in Se content (p = 0.0312).

Bars represent mean ± SEM. * denotes p < 0.05. n = 10, individual measures supplied as black dots.

PyC supplementation increased PyC2-Gly abundance in the liver

Because the impact of PyC supplementation was primarily in the liver, relative abundances of PyC2-Gly in liver tissues were measured. The livers (n = 5) from the PyC supplemented group were found to have dithiol PyC2-Gly abundance at 5.3-fold that of controls (p = 0.0312; Supplemental Fig 2).

PyC supplementation impacted key metabolic pathways of the liver

Given that the systemic effects of PyC supplementation on Se abundance were limited to the liver, we further examined the liver metabolome using untargeted HRM. As shown by two-way hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA), the liver metabolome exhibited clear separation between the two treatment groups (Fig 2A). A total of 399 metabolic features were found significantly different between the control and PyC-supplemented groups (Fig 2A). Clustering of these samples and metabolic features resulted in complete segregation of the two experimental groups along the sample axis, though not completely at the first branch point in the hierarchy. Features clustered into three major groups, noted on Fig 2A. Clusters 1 and 3 both contained a mixture of features annotated as free fatty acids and complex sugars and fats. Cluster 1 contained 183 features with higher abundance in PyC-supplemented livers, while cluster 3 contained 144 features with lower abundance in PyC-supplemented livers. Cluster 2 contained 72 additional features with lower abundance in PyC-supplemented livers, but it was enriched with features annotated as bile acids. Most of the unannotated features found in cluster 2 co-eluted with the bile acid-annotated features and had masses and adduct distributions which could be expected of bile acid metabolites, which may not have been in the database referenced. Further details on all selected features and their clustering are provided in Supplemental Table 1. Among these metabolites were two selenometabolites, selenosugar 3 (m/z 334.9434, 60 s, M + 2K-H, Schymanski Level 4; p = 0.0309; Fig 2B) and selenomethionine (m/z 261.0113, 59 s, M+ACN+Na, Schymanski Level 1; p = 0.0446; Fig 2C). Both selenometabolites were significantly increased in the liver with PyC supplementation.

To identify the metabolic pathways associated with these 399 differentially abundant features in the liver, we performed pathway enrichment analysis using mummichog v1. The key liver pathways altered by PyC treatment includes, porphyrin metabolism (p = 0.0007), methionine and cysteine metabolism (p = 0.0017), fatty acid metabolism (p = 0.0069), glycolysis (p = 0.0110), bile acid biosynthesis (p = 0.0201), glycerophospholipid metabolism (p = 0.0255), the pentose phosphate pathway (p = 0.0264), leukotriene metabolism (p = 0.0391), and galactose metabolism (p = 0.0492; Fig 2D). Almost all enriched pathways contained a mixture of increases and decreases in differentially abundant features with PyC supplementation, relative to controls. The one exception was pentose phosphate pathway metabolites, for which all differentially abundant features were increased with PyC supplementation. Features used as evidence for enrichment of these pathways are noted in Supplemental Table 1. The two most significant pathways, porphyrin metabolism and methionine and cysteine metabolism, were of particular interest. Both are closely related to metal homeostasis, the first through metal containing metabolites such as heme and cobalamin and the second through numerous interactions of metals with thiol-containing molecules. A major metabolite contributing to the porphyrin metabolism result was urobilinogen (m/z 591.3152, 28 s, M + H, Schymanski Level 4), a bilirubin breakdown product. It was decreased with PyC supplementation (p = 0.0206; Supplemental Table 1). Meanwhile, in the methionine and cysteine pathway, S-adenosylmethionine (m/z 399.1443, 134 s, M + H, Schymanski Level 1) was a prominent, affected metabolite. It was also decreased with PyC supplementation (p = 0.0059; Supplemental Table 1).

PyC supplementation altered metabolic centrality of major mineral nutrients in the liver

Given that porphyrin metabolism and cysteine and methionine metabolism were the leading pathways affected by PyC supplementation, we examined changes in hepatic metal levels linked to these pathways. These included Fe, Co, and Mg for porphyrin-binding metals and Zn and Cu for metals that interact with thiols along with Se. Fe and Mg levels were unaffected, but Zn (p = 0.0358; Fig 3A) and Co (p = 0.0211; Fig 3B) were both increased in the liver with PyC supplementation. Cu showed an increasing trend as well (p = 0.0767; Fig 3C). Given that PyC supplementation broadly affected liver metal homeostasis, reflected in both bulk metal abundance and metabolic alterations, we subsequently assessed the association of these metals with liver metabolism using network analysis (Fig 3D). A comparison of liver metal-metabolome networks generated from the control and PyC-supplemented groups showed a large change in centrality of these six metals. Centrality was measured using eigenvector centrality, which reflects the level of influence of a node in a network. The measure is a composite of both the number of connections a node possesses and the level of influence of those connected nodes. Fe was the most influential metal in the control network, with the other five metals all having much lower eigenvector centralities. This pattern shifted in the PyC-supplemented group as Fe became the metal with the lowest centrality. Se, Zn, Cu, and Mg all saw sharp increases in their centrality with PyC supplementation, and Se became the most influential metal in the network (Fig 3D). The total number of metabolites correlated with Fe in the PyC-supplemented network, i.e., the degree of Fe, was less than one-third-that in the control network. Meanwhile, the degree of each metal other than Fe increased, with the degree of Se increasing almost four-fold. Additionally, where Fe had a large community of metabolic features in the control network, it collapsed into a much smaller community with Co with PyC supplementation. Conversely, Se, which shared a small community with Cu in the control group, gained a larger, more central community under PyC supplementation. As can be seen in Fig 3E, much of the growth and shrinking of communities is due to nodes appearing in or disappearing from the network altogether when comparing the PyC-supplemented network to the control network. Still, when accounting for these changes in communities, Fe passes many more of its community members to other metal communities than it receives from other metals in the transition from the control network to the PyC supplemented network (Fig 3E). Metabolic features included in the network were also more connected under PyC supplementation. The PyC-supplemented network contained roughly 13 % more metabolic features (3194) than the control network (2826 metabolic features). Metabolic features in the control network had an average degree of 1.25, while those in the PyC-supplemented network had an average degree of 1.37, which demonstrates an increase in connectedness of the network. Full details regarding metabolic feature membership in communities in both networks are provided in Supplemental Table 2.

Discussion

In this study, we found that supplementation with the GSH-derived metal chelator, PyC2-Gly, led to changes best characterized as an increase in Se absorption and associated with changes in hepatic metal ion homeostasis and energy metabolism. The impacts on organ Se homeostasis were prominent in the liver, where hepatic Se and dithiol PyC2-Gly were increased by PyC supplementation. PyC2-Gly supplementation resulted in shifts in major liver-related pathways, including bile acid biosynthesis and fatty acid and sugar metabolism. The most pronounced effects, however, were on porphyrin metabolism and methionine and cysteine pathways, both of which are strongly linked to the homeostasis of several trace metals [29–32]. Using an integrative analysis of the liver metabolome and relevant trace metals, we found that PyC2-Gly supplementation had much broader effects on the trace metal-metabolome network. Taken together, these findings indicate that dietary metal chelators exert both broad effects on mineral nutrient availability and more subtle influences on the interactions between individual trace metals.

Dietary PyC2-Gly supplementation exhibited clear effects on Se up-take and excretion. Increased uptake of Se from the diet was expected. When combined in solution, PyC2-Gly reduces selenite SeO3−2 to form a selenotrisulfide bridge, which previously showed over 10-fold higher rates of absorption than selenite alone in renal epithelia [16,18]. The increase in urinary Se aligns with the previous observation that urinary Se correlates with urinary PyCs in humans [18]. The trend towards decreased fecal Se by PyC2-Gly must be due to either increased Se uptake from the gut or a decrease in biliary Se excretion. Because urine is the dominant route of Se excretion [1,33], the decrease in fecal Se is likely due to increased absorption of Se from the diet. However, a decrease in biliary excretion remains a possible contributor, given the changes in bile metabolism observed.

Increased dietary uptake of Se led to increased hepatic Se. The liver is the central organ for Se regulation and excretion of selenoproteins to the rest of the body, making it one of the more likely organs in which to observe a change [34,35]. We did not observe an effect on renal Se, contrary to previous findings in female mice [18]. This may be attributed to the shorter period of PyC2-Gly supplementation or to previously reported sex differences in Se excretion [36]. All other investigated organs were unaffected, likely due to the well-documented regulation of whole-body Se delivery, which begins in the liver [1,35]. The increase in hepatic Se resulted in some metabolic alterations. Both selenosugar 3 and selenomethionine were increased in the liver. Selenosugars are an excretory form of Se produced mainly in the liver [1,37], and the increase in selenosugar 3 aligns with the increased urinary excretion observed. Selenomethionine can only come from the diet or microbiome, but it is used as a source of Se for selenocysteine production [1]. It is expected that the increase in selenomethionine is due to effects of PyC in the intestines rather than a change in selenomethionine metabolism. There remains, however, the possibility that increased seleno-methionine results from an indirect effect of PyCs on the microbiome, which will require further investigation to discern.

In addition to increases in selenometabolites, we observed alterations in hepatic lipid metabolism and decreases in bile acid synthesis consistent with previous studies involving supplementation of Se in mice [38]. These effects were previously explained by upstream changes in gene expression, but the ultimate driving mechanism was not found. These metabolic and gene expression changes could be the result of Se-driven shifts in metal homeostasis. The top areas of metabolism impacted by PyC2-Gly supplementation, however, were porphyrin metabolism and methionine and cysteine metabolism. Both pathways are closely linked with metal and redox systems. Porphyrin metabolism, when considering also plant-derived porphyrins, includes close relationships with Fe, Co, and Mg [29,30]. Methionine and cysteine metabolism is the center of low-molecular weight thiol metabolism, connecting the pathway to much of endogenous antioxidant metabolism and thiol-interacting mineral nutrients such as Se, Zn, and Cu [31,32]. This led to an investigation of PyC2-Gly supplementation’s effects on these additional metals in the liver. In addition to Se, increases were observed in Zn and Co, with a similar trend for Cu. Beyond these bulk increases in metal abundance, a shift in metal-metabolome interactions was also evident. The metal-metabolome network became more inter-connected with PyC2-Gly supplementation. Metabolic crosstalk between metal abundances is expected for trace metals, as they often use over-lapping transport mechanisms and can co-occur in the same enzymes [39,40]. The increase in connectedness of the network with PyC2-Gly treatment suggests that PyC2-Gly presence promoted additional metal interactions to occur. Moreover, the overall shift in metal centralities shows that PyC2-Gly treatment reduced the influence of Fe on the liver’s metabolism, while metabolic abundances showed increased interactions with Se, Zn and Cu. This shift towards Se and Zn centrality could be explained by relatively greater metabolic influence from excess Se and Zn; however, a shift away from Fe centrality could in part be due to increased Co with PyC supplementation. Inorganic Co abundance influences both erythropoiesis and heme degradation, and increased hepatic Co may have disrupted Fe-centric metabolic networks through these mechanisms [41,42]. It is unclear how PyC supplementation caused this increase in hepatic Co, as direct PyC–Co interactions are expected to have low stability relative to other metals [43]. The mechanism of Co enrichment and potential impact on Fe-related and oxygen-dependent metabolism will require additional investigation. Nonetheless, these results demonstrate that the effects of dietary PyC on metal homeostasis go beyond changes in metal abundances. An extensive network of metabolic interactions shifts with the trace metals in response to PyC supplementation, a change which could not be captured by bulk metal analyses alone.

PyC supplementation resulted in an increase in dithiol PyC2-Gly in livers. This marks the first report of PyCs in animal tissues, despite prior evidence demonstrating their uptake by intestinal epithelia [17] and presence of intramolecular disulfide PyC2-Gly and metabolites in human urine [18]. Though oxidation of PyC2-Gly readily occurred in treated water, the major oxidation product was the reducible intramolecular disulfide form. Given prior demonstrations of reduction of glutathione disulfide (GSSG) in the gut [44], it is possible that PyC2-Gly was reduced to the dithiol form by abundant reductants and endogenous enzymes, such as glutathione reductase. Further work will be needed to determine the exact mechanism for reduction of PyCs in the gut. Here, relative abundances of liver dithiol PyC2-Gly were determined using available and sensitive methods for quantification of GSH and GSSG in animal tissues. Quantitative methods for determination of PyCs, PyC metabolites, and PyC-metal complexes in animal tissues are needed for further study of PyC activity in animal systems. Dietary PyCs are likely present in animal tissues and fluids in a variety of combinations of precursor lengths, C-terminal amino acid variants, degrees of proteolysis, redox states, and PyC-metal complex species. Robust methodology that captures this variety of species will be needed to make substantial progress towards defining the physiological significance of dietary PyC bioactivity.

These results, taken together, demonstrate that dietary PyCs influence bioavailability of trace elements in the diet. This class of metal chelators, widely appreciated for their role in plant physiology, continues to confer effects on metal disposition in animals ingesting plant material. This impact was expected, considering the diverse interactions that endogenous GSH has with trace elements [8,45–47]. PyCs, in essence, are polymerized GSH which interact with metals and Se in much the same way as GSH, only with more thermodynamically favorable reactions and chelate complex formation. This study is not without limitations, which necessitate further study. PyC2-Gly is only one of the PyCs, and other PyCs could have different metal specificities. PyC2-Gly has the lowest metal-binding capacity of known PyCs. While use of this simple PyC form limited complexity of interpretation in the current study, follow up investigations of mixtures of PyCs will better reflect the natural diversity of PyCs present in the diet. Mouse food primarily consists of plant tissues, which already contain a small amount of PyCs. Future studies will be needed to see if the impacts of PyCs become even more apparent when compared with the use of a synthetic diet. Only male mice were tested; it will be important to determine if these hepatic effects also occur in female mice. Additionally, it would be valuable to investigate the impacts of PyC supplementation on mineral availability under deficient conditions, as the current study utilized mineral-sufficient diets. Lastly, examining the effects of PyC supplementation on the abundance and activity of liver metalloproteins could provide further insight.

These future investigations will elucidate the downstream effects of PyC supplementation on metal and redox homeostasis, and more broadly, the influence of plant-based foods on global metabolism. Ongoing research into the actions of PyCs after ingestion promises to reveal a more integrated view of metal homeostasis and may highlight novel ways to harness PyCs for enhancing human health and nutrition.
