On results of certain modules over untwisted affine Lie superalgebras
Asghar Daneshvar ∗, Hajar Kiamehr ⋆, Maryam Yazdanifar ⋆, Malihe Yousofzadeh ∗,⋆

Abstract
. Since 2020, finite weight modules have been studied over twisted affine Lie superalgebras. To complete the characterization of modules over affine Lie superalgebras, we need some information regarding modules over untwisted affine Lie superalgebras. There are several known results on representations of twisted affine Lie superalgebras that hold for untwisted cases, and their proofs are just a minor modification of the known ones. In this note, we gather these results for our further use.

Assume that
\mathfrak{L}
is an affine Lie superalgebra
with the standard Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{H}
. With respect to
the canonical bilinear form on
\mathfrak{H}^{*}
(the dual space of

into three kinds of roots:

•

•

imaginary roots: roots which are orthogonal to all roots,

•

The action of root vectors corresponding to real roots on a simple finite weight
\mathfrak{L}
-module

has a determining role in studying the representation theory of affine Lie superalgebras. These actions on
M
are performed in two types: injectively or locally nilpotently. Hence, we denote by

(resp.
R^{ln}
) the set of all (nonzero) real root
\alpha
whose corresponding nonzero root vectors act on

In Section 3, we show that (see Theorem
3.9
):

.

Suppose that
M
is a simple finite weight module over untwisted affine Lie superalgebra

. Then
for each

, one of the following will happen:

\beta

(ii)

(iii)

\displaystyle\gamma+\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 1}\delta\subseteq R^{in},~~\gamma+\mathbb{Z}^{\leq 0}\delta\subseteq R^{ln}

\displaystyle-\gamma+\mathbb{Z}^{\geq t}\delta\subseteq R^{in},~~-\gamma+\mathbb{Z}^{\leq t-1}\delta\subseteq R^{ln}

Affine Lie superalgebras

Affine Lie superalgebras introduced by Johan van de Leur in 1986, see

Affine Lie superalgebras are those contragradient Lie superalgebras corresponding to nonzero indecomposable symmetrizable generalized Cartan matrices of finite growth but not finite dimension. More precisely, suppose that

\gamma:=\beta+k\delta\in R^{ln},~~\gamma+\delta=\beta+(k+1)\delta\in R^{in}\quad\quad(*)

or

\gamma:=\beta+k\delta\in R^{in},~~\gamma+\delta=\beta+(k+1)\delta\in R^{ln}\quad\quad(**)

for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We claim that if
(*)
holds, then (iii) happens.
Before starting the main body of the proof of the claim, we show that the situation

-\gamma\in R^{ln},{\rm and}~~-\gamma-\delta\in R^{in}

cannot happen. Suppose on the contrary that

-\gamma\in R^{ln}
and
-\gamma-\delta\in R^{in}
. Then, according to
(*)
, we have
\pm\gamma\in R^{ln}
or
\pm(\gamma+\delta)\in R^{in}
.
Thus by Lemma
3.7
(ii)

\displaystyle r_{\gamma}(\gamma+\delta)=\gamma+\delta-\frac{2(\gamma,\gamma+\delta)}{(\gamma,\gamma)}\gamma=-\gamma+\delta\in R^{in},

\displaystyle r_{\gamma}(-\gamma-\delta)=-\gamma-\delta-\frac{2(\gamma,-\gamma-\delta)}{(\gamma,\gamma)}\gamma=\gamma-\delta\in R^{in}.

Let
\mu\in{\rm supp}(M)
. As
-\gamma+\delta\in R^{in}
, it follows that
\mu+(-\gamma+\delta)\in{\rm supp}(M)
.
On the other hand, since

\gamma-\delta\in R^{in}\subseteq\mathfrak{C}_{M}
, we have
\mu\in{\rm supp}(M)
. So, by similar arguments, we can easily show that

\mu+(-\gamma+\delta)\in{\rm supp}(M)\Leftrightarrow\mu\in{\rm supp}(M)\Leftrightarrow\mu+(\gamma+\delta)\in{\rm supp}(M).

Now, let
\eta\in{\rm supp}(M)
. Since
\gamma\in R^{ln}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}_{M}
, we can choose
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 1}
such that

\displaystyle\eta+2k\gamma\not\in{\rm supp}(M)\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\eta+2k\gamma+2k(-\gamma+\delta)\not\in{\rm supp}(M)\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\eta+2k\delta\not\in{\rm supp}(M)\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\eta+k(\gamma+\delta)+k(-\gamma+\delta)\not\in{\rm supp}(M)\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\eta\not\in{\rm supp}(M),

which is a contradiction. We now proceed with the remaining cases for
-\gamma
and
-\gamma-\delta
.

Case 1.

-\gamma\in R^{ln}
and
-\gamma-\delta\in R^{ln}
.

Assume that there is
p\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\gamma+p\delta\in R^{in}
. AS

\pm\gamma\in R^{ln}
, it follows from Lemma
3.7
(ii) that

r_{\gamma}(\gamma+p\delta)=\gamma+p\delta-\dfrac{2(\gamma,\gamma)}{(\gamma,\gamma)}\gamma=-\gamma+p\delta\in R^{in}.

Again by Lemma
3.7
(ii),

r_{\gamma}(-\gamma+p\delta)=-\gamma+p\delta-\dfrac{2(-\gamma,\gamma)}{(\gamma,\gamma)}\gamma=\gamma+p\delta\in R^{in}.

Thus, we can prove that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\gamma+p\delta\in R^{in}\Leftrightarrow-\gamma+p\delta\in R^{in},

for all
p\in\mathbb{Z}
. Particularly,

\gamma+\delta\in R^{in}\Leftrightarrow-\gamma+\delta\in R^{in}.

On the other hand,
if
\pm\gamma+2\delta\in R^{ln}
, then
by Theorem
3.8

\gamma+3\delta=\underbrace{-\gamma-\delta}_{\in R^{ln}}+\underbrace{2(\gamma+2\delta)}_{\in R^{ln}}\in R^{ln},

and so by Lemma
3.2
(ii)

-\gamma+3\delta=\underbrace{-\gamma+\delta}_{\in R^{in}}+\underbrace{\gamma+\delta}_{\in R^{in}}+\underbrace{(-\gamma+\delta)}_{\in R^{in}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{M}\cap R_{re}=R^{in}.

This contradicts (
3.1
). Hence,
\pm\gamma+2\delta\in R^{in}
.
Now, assume that
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 1}
. Then by Lemma
3.2
(ii)

\pm\gamma+2n\delta=\underbrace{\pm\gamma+2\delta}_{\in R^{in}}+\underbrace{(n-1)(\gamma+\delta)}_{\in R^{in}}+\underbrace{(n-1)(-\gamma+\delta)}_{\in R^{in}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{M}\cap R_{re}^{\times}=R^{in}.

Furthermore, by Lemma
3.2
(ii)

\pm\gamma+(1+2n)\delta=\underbrace{\pm\gamma+\delta}_{\in R^{in}}+\underbrace{n(\gamma+\delta)}_{\in R^{in}}+\underbrace{n(-\gamma+\delta)}_{\in R^{in}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{M}\cap R_{re}=R^{in}.

Now, assume that
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{\leq 0}
. If
\pm\gamma+2n\delta\in R^{in}
, then by Lemma
3.2
(ii)

\pm\gamma=\underbrace{\pm\gamma+2n\delta}_{\in R^{in}}+\underbrace{(-n(\gamma+\delta))}_{\in R^{in}}+\underbrace{(-n(-\gamma+\delta))}_{\in R^{in}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{M}\cap R_{re}=R^{in}.

This contradicts the assumption
\pm\gamma\in R^{ln}
. Hence,

\pm\gamma+2n\delta\in R^{ln}
. Therefore, we have showed that

(3.2)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\cdots,~\gamma-2\delta,~\gamma-\delta,~\gamma}_{\in R^{ln}}~,~\underbrace{\gamma+\delta,~\gamma+2\delta,~\cdots}_{\in R^{in}},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\cdots,~-\gamma-2\delta,~-\gamma-\delta,~-\gamma}_{\in R^{ln}}~,~\underbrace{-\gamma+\delta,~-\gamma+2\delta,~\cdots}_{\in R^{in}}\quad({\rm accutully}~t=1).

This means that (iii) holds.

Case 2.

-\gamma\in R^{in}
and
-\gamma-\delta\in R^{in}
.

Assume that
\gamma-n\delta\in R^{in}
for some
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{>0}
. Then by Lemma
3.2
(ii) and
(*)

\gamma=\underbrace{\gamma-n\delta}_{\in R^{in}}+\underbrace{n(-\gamma)}_{\in R^{in}}+\underbrace{n(\gamma+\delta)}_{\in R^{in}}\in\mathfrak{C}_{M}\cap R_{re}=R^{in}.

This contradicts the assumption
\gamma\in R^{ln}
, see (
*
). Thus

\gamma-n\delta\in R^{ln}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}
.
Based on
(*)
, we have
\gamma+\delta\in R^{in}
.
So by Lemma
3.7
,

r_{\gamma+\delta}(\gamma)=\gamma-\frac{2(\gamma,\gamma+\delta)}{(\gamma+\delta,\gamma+\delta)}(\gamma+\delta)=\gamma-2(\gamma+\delta)=-\gamma-2\delta

belongs to
R^{ln}
, because
\gamma\in R^{ln}
.
If
-\gamma-n\delta\in R^{in}
for
n\geq 3
, then consider the following equation

-\gamma-2\delta=-\gamma-n\delta+(n-2)(-\gamma)+(n-2)(\gamma+\delta).

By Lemma
3.2
(ii) and
(*)
, the right hand side of the equation belongs to
R^{in}
, while the left hand side of the equation belongs to
R^{ln}
, a contradiction. Thus,
-\gamma-n\delta\in R^{ln}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 3}
.

Now, assume that
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}
. Then by Lemma
3.2
(ii) and
(*)

\displaystyle-\gamma+n\delta=\underbrace{(n+1)(-\gamma)}_{\in R^{in}}+\underbrace{n(\gamma+\delta)}_{\in R^{in}}\in R^{in},

\displaystyle-\gamma+(n+2)\delta=\underbrace{(n+1)(-\gamma)}_{\in R^{in}}+\underbrace{(n+2)(\gamma+\delta)}_{\in R^{in}}\in R^{in}.

Note that by Lemma
3.2
(ii), we have

\gamma+n\delta=\underbrace{(n-1)(-\gamma)}_{\in R^{in}}+\underbrace{n(\gamma+\delta)}_{\in R^{in}}\in R^{in}

in which
n\geq 1
.
Therefore, we show that

(3.4)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\cdots,~\gamma-2\delta,~\gamma-\delta,~\gamma}_{\in R^{ln}}~,~\underbrace{\gamma+\delta,~\gamma+2\delta,~\cdots}_{\in R^{in}},

(3.5)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\cdots,~-\gamma-2\delta}_{\in R^{ln}}~,~\underbrace{-\gamma-\delta,~-\gamma,~-\gamma+\delta,~-\gamma+2\delta,~\cdots}_{\in R^{in}}\quad({\rm accutully}~~t=-1).

This means that (iii) holds.

Case 3.

-\gamma\in R^{in}
and
-\gamma-\delta\in R^{ln}
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}
. Then for
n\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle-\gamma+n\delta=\underbrace{(n+1)(-\gamma)}_{\in R^{in}}+\underbrace{n(\gamma+\delta)}_{\in R^{in}}\in R^{in},

\displaystyle\gamma+(n+1)\delta=\underbrace{n(-\gamma)}_{\in R^{in}}+\underbrace{(n+1)(\gamma+\delta)}_{\in R^{in}}\in R^{in}.

Assume that
\gamma-k\delta\in R^{in}
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 1}
. Since
-\gamma+k\delta=-(\gamma-k\delta)\in R^{in}
(above), we have

-\gamma+2k\delta=\gamma-2\gamma+2k\delta=\gamma-\frac{2(\gamma-k\delta,\gamma)}{(\gamma,\gamma)}(\gamma-k\delta)=r_{\gamma-k\delta}(\gamma)\in R^{ln}

by Lemma
3.7
.
However, according to the above, the left side belongs to
R^{in}
, which is a contradiction. Thus,
\gamma-n\delta\in R^{ln}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}
. On the other hand, by using the above and Theorem
3.8

-\gamma-(n+1)\delta=(\gamma-(n-1)\delta)+2(-\gamma-\delta)\in{R^{ln}}+2R^{ln}\subseteq R^{ln},

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 1}
. Therefore, we show that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\cdots,~\gamma-2\delta,~\gamma-\delta,~\gamma}_{\in R^{ln}}~,~\underbrace{\gamma+\delta,~\gamma+2\delta,~\cdots}_{\in R^{in}},

(3.7)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\cdots,~-\gamma-2\delta,~-\gamma-\delta}_{\in R^{ln}}~,~\underbrace{-\gamma,~-\gamma+\delta,~-\gamma+2\delta,~\cdots}_{\in R^{in}}\quad({\rm accutully}~~t=0).

This means that (iii) holds.

Regarding (iv), in
(**)
, we have

\eta:=\gamma+\delta\in R^{ln},~~\eta+(-\delta)=\gamma\in R^{in}.

This implies that
(**)
turns to
(*)
, and we get (iv). The proof is complete.
∎

-

\begin{cases}\zeta:{\rm span}_{\mathbb{R}}S\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\\
\quad\delta-\theta_{1}\mapsto 0\\
\quad\alpha_{j,1}\mapsto\dfrac{1}{t_{1}r_{j,1}},\quad 1\leq j\leq t_{1}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,1}\mapsto 0,\quad t_{1}+1\leq j\leq n_{1}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,i}\mapsto\dfrac{1}{t_{i}r_{j,i}},\quad 2\leq i\leq k,~1\leq j\leq t_{i}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,i}\mapsto 0,\quad 2\leq i\leq k,~t_{i}+1\leq j\leq n_{i}\end{cases}

Now, for
i\neq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\zeta(\delta-\theta_{i})=\zeta(\delta)-\zeta(\theta_{i})=\zeta(\delta-\theta_{1})+\zeta(\theta_{1})-\zeta(\theta_{i})=0+1-1=0.

Case 2.

\delta-\theta_{1},\cdots,\delta-\theta_{k}\in P\setminus-P
: Define

\begin{cases}\zeta:{\rm span}_{\mathbb{R}}S\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\\
\quad\delta-\theta_{1}\mapsto 1\\
\quad\alpha_{j,1}\mapsto\dfrac{1}{t_{1}r_{j,1}},\quad 1\leq j\leq t_{1}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,1}\mapsto 0,\quad t_{1}+1\leq j\leq n_{1}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,i}\mapsto\dfrac{1}{t_{i}r_{j,i}},\quad 2\leq i\leq k,~1\leq j\leq t_{i}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,i}\mapsto 0,\quad 2\leq i\leq k,~t_{i}+1\leq j\leq n_{i}\end{cases}

Now, for
i\neq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\zeta(\delta-\theta_{i})=\zeta(\delta)-\zeta(\theta_{i})=\zeta(\delta-\theta_{1})+\zeta(\theta_{1})-\zeta(\theta_{i})=1+1-1=0.

Case 3.

\delta-\theta_{1},\delta-\theta_{l_{1}},\cdots,\delta-\theta_{l_{p}}\in P\setminus-P
, and
\delta-\theta_{i}\in P\cap-P
for all
i\in\{1,\cdots,k\}\setminus\{1,l_{1},\cdots,l_{p}\}
: Define

\begin{cases}\zeta:{\rm span}_{\mathbb{R}}S\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\\
\quad\delta-\theta_{1}\mapsto 1\\
\quad\alpha_{j,1}\mapsto\dfrac{1}{t_{1}r_{j,1}},\quad 1\leq j\leq t_{1}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,1}\mapsto 0,\quad t_{1}+1\leq j\leq n_{1}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,i}\mapsto\dfrac{1}{t_{i}r_{j,i}},\quad i\in\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{p}\},~1\leq j\leq t_{i}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,i}\mapsto 0,\quad i\in\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{p}\},~t_{i}+1\leq j\leq n_{i}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,i}\mapsto\dfrac{2}{t_{i}r_{j,i}},\quad i\in\{1,\cdots,k\}\setminus\{1,l_{1},\cdots,l_{p}\},~1\leq j\leq t_{i}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,i}\mapsto 0,\quad i\in\{1,\cdots,k\}\setminus\{1,l_{1},\cdots,l_{p}\},~t_{i}+1\leq j\leq n_{i}\end{cases}

Now, for
i\in\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{p}\}
, we have

\displaystyle\zeta(\delta-\theta_{i})=\zeta(\delta)-\zeta(\theta_{i})=\zeta(\delta-\theta_{1})+\zeta(\theta_{1})-\zeta(\theta_{i})=1+1-1=1

and for

\displaystyle\zeta(\delta-\theta_{i})=\zeta(\delta)-\zeta(\theta_{i})=\zeta(\delta-\theta_{1})+\zeta(\theta_{1})-\zeta(\theta_{i})=1+1-2=0.

Case 4.

\delta-\theta_{1},\delta-\theta_{l_{1}},\cdots,\delta-\theta_{l_{p}}\in P\cap-P
, and
\delta-\theta_{i}\in P\setminus-P
for all
i\in\{1,\cdots,k\}\setminus\{1,l_{1},\cdots,l_{p}\}
: Define

\begin{cases}\zeta:{\rm span}_{\mathbb{R}}S\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\\
\quad\delta-\theta_{1}\mapsto 0\\
\quad\alpha_{j,1}\mapsto\dfrac{2}{t_{1}r_{j,1}},\quad 1\leq j\leq t_{1}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,1}\mapsto 0,\quad t_{1}+1\leq j\leq n_{1}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,i}\mapsto\dfrac{2}{t_{i}r_{j,i}},\quad i\in\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{p}\},~1\leq j\leq t_{i}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,i}\mapsto 0,\quad i\in\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{p}\},~t_{i}+1\leq j\leq n_{i}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,i}\mapsto\dfrac{1}{t_{i}r_{j,i}},\quad i\in\{1,\cdots,k\}\setminus\{1,l_{1},\cdots,l_{p}\},~1\leq j\leq t_{i}\\
\quad\alpha_{j,i}\mapsto 0,\quad i\in\{1,\cdots,k\}\setminus\{1,l_{1},\cdots,l_{p}\},~t_{i}+1\leq j\leq n_{i}\end{cases}

Now, for
i\in\{1,l_{1},\cdots,l_{p}\}
, we have

\displaystyle\zeta(\delta-\theta_{i})=\zeta(\delta)-\zeta(\theta_{i})=\zeta(\delta-\theta_{1})+\zeta(\theta_{1})-\zeta(\theta_{i})=0+2-2=0

and for
i\in\{1,\cdots,k\}\setminus\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{p}\}

\displaystyle\zeta(\delta-\theta_{i})=\zeta(\delta)-\zeta(\theta_{i})=\zeta(\delta-\theta_{1})+\zeta(\theta_{1})-\zeta(\theta_{i})=0+2-1=1

Now, we are ready to show that
P=S^{+}\cup S^{\circ}
. Let

\alpha\in S^{+}\cup S^{\circ}
. So, we can write
\alpha
by some elements of
\Pi
with positive integers coefficients, because
\Pi
is a base for
S
.
However, elements of
\Pi
belong to
P
, and
P
is closed, which implies that
\alpha\in P
. Thus
S^{+}\cup S^{\circ}\subseteq P
.

Conversely, to show
P\subseteq S^{+}\cup S^{\circ}
, it is enough to prove that
P\cap S^{-}=\emptyset
. To see this, assume on the contrary that
P\cap S^{-}\not=\emptyset
. Then, we pick

\alpha=\sum_{\epsilon\in\Pi}k_{\epsilon}\epsilon\in P\cap S^{-}
.
Since
\zeta(\alpha)<0
, we get that all
k_{\epsilon}
are negative integers. Thus,
-\sum k_{\epsilon}
is a positive integer. So, we may pick
\alpha
such that
-\sum k_{\epsilon}
is minimal, i.e. minimal height. Suppose that
-\alpha\in\Pi
. Then,
-\alpha=\epsilon
for some
\epsilon\in\Pi
. As
\zeta(\epsilon)=\zeta(-\alpha)>0
, we conclude from all the cases defined above for
\zeta
that

\epsilon\in P\setminus-P
. On the other hand,
-\epsilon=\alpha\in P
, and hence
\epsilon\in-P
. This is a contradiction; consequently,
-\alpha\not\in\Pi
.
This allows us choosing
\epsilon_{0}\in\Pi
such that
\alpha+\epsilon_{0}\in S
. It is clear that
\alpha+\epsilon_{0}\in S^{-}\cap P
and has a smaller height than
\alpha
, a contradiction.
Thus
P\cap S^{-}=\emptyset
and so

P=S^{+}\cup S^{\circ}=\{\alpha\in S~|~\zeta(\alpha)\geq 0\}.

A straightforward check proves the rest statements.
∎
