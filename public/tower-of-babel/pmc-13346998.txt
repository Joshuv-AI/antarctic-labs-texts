An analysis of the influence of perceptual dimensions in biomimetic packaging design on generation Z consumers’ flow experiences using PLS-SEM, ANN, and fsQCA
Authors: Xinyue Qi, Fan Wu, Lin Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysical packaging has evolved into a central medium for deep interaction between brands and consumers. While biomimetic design offers cross-disciplinary inspiration for product innovation, there remains a lack of systematic analysis—beyond linear reductionism—regarding how it triggers the immersive experience of “flow” through multidimensional sensory stimulation. Based on the “Function-Interaction-Sensory-Emotional-Value” theoretical framework, this study quantifies the five core perceptual dimensions of biomimetic packaging design (structural efficacy, immediate feedback, aesthetic appeal, narrative resonance, and sustainability value resonance). It innovatively employs a hybrid methodology integrating Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling, Artificial Neural Networks, and Fuzzy Set Qualitative Comparative Analysis to achieve triangulated validation. An analysis of 218 valid survey responses from Gen Z consumers indicates that, with the exception of immediate feedback, all other perceptual dimensions exert a significant linear influence on the flow experience, exposing the “net effect trap” inherent in traditional symmetric analysis; The ANN’s nonlinear sensitivity analysis precisely identified “aesthetic appeal” and “narrative resonance” as the predictors with the highest weights, while confirming that immediate feedback still holds extremely high predictive value in nonlinear mapping; fsQCA further unraveled causal asymmetry, identifying three categories (totaling four pathways) of equivalent configuration paths leading to high levels of flow: emotional and narrative-driven, value recognition-driven, and functional and sensory experience. This confirmed that immediate feedback must function as a boundary condition in synergy with other dimensions to be effective. This study deeply integrates and extends existing theoretical frameworks on design perception and flow, expanding their application from digital contexts to physical interactions. Introduction

In today’s highly competitive consumer market, packaging has evolved far beyond its traditional role of protecting products. It has become a vital medium for brands to engage directly with consumers, fostering experiential interactions. With the emergence of the experience economy, Generation Z—the primary consumer demographic—now seeks more than just the product itself; they desire a holistic experience that resonates emotionally and promotes deep engagement1–3. Consequently, packaging design has grown into a pivotal element in shaping brand perception, influencing purchase decisions, and nurturing brand loyalty. A well-crafted package can deliver an unforgettable “unboxing experience,” through its visual, tactile, and functional attributes, thereby significantly enhancing overall consumer satisfaction4. In this context, optimizing the user experience through innovative design strategies has become a critical focus for both designers and brand owners.

To address this challenge, the design community has increasingly turned to nature for inspiration, with biomimetic design emerging as an innovative solution. By emulating the forms, functions, colors, and structures of biological systems, biomimetic design provides designers with a fresh perspective 5). For instance, the shape of a whale has inspired the design of a chair that blends aesthetic appeal, playfulness, and multifunctionality, making it adaptable to a variety of contexts (see Fig. 1). Through billions of years of evolution, nature’s biological structures and functions have attained unparalleled efficiency and sophistication6. This not only opens up new possibilities for packaging sustainability and material innovation but also enhances user comfort and convenience from an ergonomic standpoint7,8. However, current research on biomimetic packaging predominantly focuses on case studies and practices related to material innovation, sustainability, or aesthetics9–12. There remains a notable gap in the systematic exploration of how these dimensions significantly influence consumers’ psychological perceptions and experiences, particularly in terms of quantifying such impacts.

Draw from Sunon’s portfolio: “SAMU”.

To accurately measure the depth of user immersion during product interactions, the theory of flow—derived from positive psychology—provides us with a key metric13. Flow is an optimal state of experience in which an individual is fully immersed in the present activity and feels immense pleasure and satisfaction in the process14. Although Flow theory has been widely applied in fields such as online user experience15, a significant research gap remains: How exactly do biomimetic packaging designs, derived from biological features, work together through multidimensional sensory stimuli to trigger Flow experiences in Gen Z users? More importantly, human cognitive and emotional responses to biomimetic designs constitute a highly complex, nonlinear psychological process. Traditional SEM has limitations in capturing the underlying nonlinear mapping relationships between variables,while single-factor configurational analysis (such as fsQCA can reveal multiple concurrent pathways, it struggles to precisely quantify the relative predictive importance of each independent perceptual dimension.

Therefore, the core objective of this study is to overcome the limitations of single-method approaches by innovatively constructing a mixed-method framework that integrates Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), and Fuzzy Set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA), in order to comprehensively decode the mechanisms through which the perceptual dimensions of biomimetic packaging design influence Generation Z’s flow experiences. Theoretically, this study does not aim to propose a completely new theory, but rather seeks to deeply integrate and extend existing theories to build an empirical bridge connecting “biomimetic design—consumer perception—flow experience”: first, PLS-SEM is employed to test the linear net effects of each perceptual dimension; second, ANN is introduced to capture complex nonlinear relationships and accurately assess the relative predictive importance of each dimension; and finally, fsQCA is used to explore, from a holistic perspective, how these dimensions stimulate high-level flow experiences through synergistic configuration. At the practical level, the findings will provide engineering and packaging designers with a scientific basis that transcends mere aesthetic considerations. This will not only help designers more precisely integrate various biomimetic design strategies but also create interactive experiences that better evoke deep immersion and emotional connections among young consumers, thereby fostering stronger user loyalty in the face of intense product competition.

Literature review

Biomimetic design and biomimetic packaging

Biomimetic design

Biomimetic design constitutes a methodological approach that entails observing, reflecting upon, learning from, imitating, and ultimately applying the models, processes, and elements of biological systems in nature to address human design challenges. At its core, this design philosophy emphasizes a return to fundamentals and the integration of interdisciplinary knowledge5. Thus, biomimetic design extends beyond the superficial replication of natural forms, striving instead for a profound understanding of the underlying principles and adaptive strategies embedded within biological systems. Scholars generally delineate three progressive levels of biomimetic practice (see Fig. 2): imitation of natural forms, imitation of natural processes and behaviors, and imitation of natural ecosystems16–18. This methodological framework not only provides an inexhaustible source of inspiration for technological innovation but also resonates strongly with contemporary aspirations for green design, owing to its focus on sustainability and resource efficiency. Within practical contexts, biomimicry functions as a “problem-solving tool,” guiding designers to adopt a fresh perspective in which nature is regarded as mentor, thereby fostering innovative design solutions rooted in biological wisdom.

The three levels of biomimetic design.

Biomimetic strategies in packaging design

When biomimetic principles are applied to packaging design, they open up entirely new avenues for addressing issues such as functionality, sustainability, and user experience. Biomimetic packaging design transcends mere visual imitation, instead systematically integrating biological principles throughout the entire lifecycle of packaging. Based on existing research and design practices, biomimetic strategies in packaging design can be categorized into texture biomimetic, structural biomimetic, color biomimetic, form biomimetic, and material biomimetic. These design touchpoints serve as external stimuli that ultimately influence consumers’ internal perceptions, providing the foundation and source for constructing the “design perception dimension” in this study.

Texture biomimetic design draws inspiration from the microscopic textures or patterns found on biological surfaces19, enabling packaging to acquire special functional properties such as waterproofing, self-cleaning, reduced friction, or anti-counterfeiting. Figure 3 depicts the Pentawards Gold Award-winning packaging design for “Hui Gan” bitter melon juice, created by a team from Hunan University of Technology in China. The design draws inspiration from the texture and colouration of bitter melon skin, mapping these elements onto the product’s glass bottle. This approach not only highlights the vegetable’s wholesome, natural attributes but also enhances the packaging’s slip resistance through increased friction. The tactile quality creates a distinctive sensory experience, ultimately aiming to elevate users’ “comfort” and “functional trust” while influencing consumers’ aesthetic perceptions.

Excellent structural biomimetic can directly enhance users’ perception of packaging structure, functionality, and interaction. Numerous baijiu and juice packaging designs exhibit pronounced color biomimetic characteristics, primarily targeting consumers’ visual senses, and serving as a core element in showcasing aesthetic value. The bottle features a sky-blue hue, while the cap is rendered in white (symbolising white clouds). Collectively, this design evokes a sense of freshness, naturalness, and refreshing comfort.

Form biomimetic is a strategy that uses abstract or concrete techniques to mimic the external form of natural organisms through formal selection and omission, either in part or as a whole21. This strategy employs intuitive, humorous, and anthropomorphic techniques to make packaging design more engaging, sparking users’ associations and imagination. Morphological biomimicry is frequently employed in packaging design, as exemplified by the “Leng Cui” series of fruit juice beverages (see Fig. 6). The designer geometrised the fruit forms while incorporating diamond-like faceted designs and highly translucent glass materials to create premium juice packaging that harmonises visual appeal with practicality. Through the careful orchestration of form, colour, texture, and patterns, the design further stimulates consumers’ appetite, rendering the juice appear more vibrant and alluring.

Material biomimetic involves mimicking the composition, synthesis process, or properties of biological materials to develop new, environmentally friendly packaging materials. The core of this strategy lies in drawing inspiration from nature’s material cycling patterns to replace traditional high-energy-consuming, non-biodegradable petrochemical-based materials. Material biomimetic not only influences packaging structure and interaction through its physical properties but, more importantly, embodies environmental sustainability principles, serving as the material foundation for users to develop a sense of “sustainability.” For example, researchers from the Indian Institute of Technology Madras developed fungal mycelium-based biocomposites using agricultural waste (such as sawdust and cardboard) and fungi (such as Ganoderma lucidum and Pleurotus ostreatus), providing scientific evidence for replacing traditional plastic foam22. Additionally, material biomimetic creates a realistic texture through the combined perception and interaction of sight and touch, prompting consumers to reflect on and associate with nature5. After the rice is fermented into wine, the husks continue to serve a purpose in another form, and the bottle can even be repurposed as a vase after the wine is consumed. This poetic continuation of life embodies the concept of yin-yang harmony and conveys a poetic philosophy of life.

In summary, existing research and practice comprehensively demonstrate the application strategies of biomimetic concepts in packaging design. Nevertheless, a unified theoretical framework that systematically translates these strategies into perceptual dimensions influencing consumers’ Flow experiences has yet to be established. Addressing this gap, the present study integrates and synthesizes previously fragmented design strategies, advancing them into a multi-layered theoretical model of user perception. Through this framework, the study further investigates the configurational effects of biomimetic packaging design, thereby contributing both conceptual clarity and empirical depth to the understanding of how biomimicry shapes immersive consumer experiences.

Flow experience theory

The definition and dimensions of flow

Flow, a psychological state first conceptualized by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi in 1975, is characterized by complete immersion and total engagement in an activity23,24. In this state, individuals become deeply absorbed, experiencing heightened concentration, intrinsic enjoyment, and a strong sense of control—often to the extent of losing awareness of time and external distractions. Under favorable conditions, Flow may even evolve into a hypnotic or ecstatic trance-like state, leading some scholars to interpret Flow itself as a form of trance25.

Csikszentmihalyi’s original Flow model emphasized the alignment between individual ability and task challenge as the central determinant of the Flow experience23. Massimini subsequently expanded the model by introducing the concept of indifference and, together with Carli, further refined the theoretical framework of Flow26. Building on this foundation, Jackson and Marsh provided explicit operational definitions for the nine dimensions summarized by Csikszentmihalyi27. Collectively, these dimensions describe an ordered state of consciousness in which individuals are liberated from the “mental entropy” of everyday life—namely, fragmented attention and psychological disorder—thus entering an optimal state of efficiency and pleasure24. Later, Novak and Hoffman classified these nine characteristics into three categories—conditional factors, experiential factors, and outcome factors—based on the generative process of Flow (see Fig. 8)28. These theoretical advancements provide the foundation for this study to identify and distill the key conditional variables underlying Flow in the context of biomimetic packaging design.

The process of generating flow experiences.

Flow in a consumer context

Within consumer behavior research, Flow theory has been extensively applied to elucidate the psychological mechanisms underlying consumer immersion during the shopping process. Hoffman and Novak29 first introduced the concept into the domain of online marketing, positing that the internet, as a hypermedia and computer-mediated environment, is particularly conducive to the emergence of Flow experiences. They further argued that the success of a website largely depends on its ability to create conditions for Flow, a claim that has been consistently supported by subsequent empirical studies. For instance, Liu30, drawing on the stimulus–organism–response (SOR) framework, demonstrated that Flow plays a significant mediating role between the characteristics of entertainment streamers and viewers’ willingness to purchase virtual gifts. Similarly, Song Yuke et al.31, building on Novak’s three-stage model, identified five key conditional factors for eliciting Flow experiences: clear structure, timely feedback, differentiated design, multi-sensory channels, and usability. In the realm of product design, Flow has been shown to arise from multi-sensory interactive stimulation, operational experience, and the embedding of core cultural elements32.

Collectively, these findings highlight the pivotal role of Flow in enhancing consumers’ purchase intentions and fostering brand loyalty. Moreover, for Generation Z consumers—who value immersive, interactive, and meaningful consumption experiences—Flow offers a particularly effective lens through which to investigate purchasing psychology and behavioral tendencies.

The consumption psychology and behavioral characteristics of generation Z

Generation Z (individuals born between 1995 and 2010), often referred to as “digital natives,” display distinctive generational traits in their consumption psychology and behavior, with emotional value emerging as a primary driver of their purchasing decisions2,3,33. Confronted with pressures from academics, work, and broader societal expectations, Gen Z frequently turns to consumption as a means of alleviating anxiety and seeking immediate comfort. Consequently, “emotional resonance” has increasingly supplanted traditional notions of “utility” as the prevailing logic of consumption34,35.

A defining characteristic of Gen Z’s consumer mindset is the coexistence of seemingly contradictory yet fundamentally complementary tendencies. On the one hand, they embrace multi-channel price comparisons and display skill in identifying affordable alternatives, reflecting a rational and pragmatic orientation. On the other hand, they are equally willing to invest in emotional value and experiential quality, often paying a premium to satisfy their desire for meaningful and emotionally rewarding experiences35. Beyond individual gratification, consumption for Gen Z is also an avenue for value expression. They exhibit a strong preference for brands and products that align with their ethical and social values, with particular concern for issues of sustainability36. For instance, the rise of the YONO (“You Only Need One”) movement reflects Gen Z’s inclination to reduce unnecessary consumption, prioritize essentials, and pursue a minimalist and sustainable lifestyle.

Research gaps

A review of the existing literature reveals that, although significant progress has been made in biomimetic design, flow theory, and research on Gen Z consumers, there is a clear theoretical disconnect among these three areas: First, existing research on biomimetic packaging tends to focus on design strategies themselves, lacking a systematic integration of user perception dimensions; second, the application of flow theory is largely concentrated on digital interfaces or virtual human–computer interaction, with insufficient attention paid to physical packaging interaction scenarios; Third, at the methodological level, existing research largely relies on single-dimensional linear statistical analysis; no study has yet comprehensively examined how the multiple perceptual dimensions of biomimetic packaging interact through linear, nonlinear, and complex configurations to influence Generation Z’s flow experience.

Therefore, this study will extract core design perceptual dimensions based on existing theories and innovatively introduce a hybrid methodology integrating PLS-SEM, ANN, and fsQCA to comprehensively explore the multi-dimensional causal mechanisms and concurrent pathways that trigger Generation Z’s flow experience.

It should be noted that this research framework is based on theoretical complementarity in the exploration of complex human cognitive mechanisms. PLS-SEM aims to assess the independent linear relationships between each perceptual dimension and the flow experience; however, since consumers’ evaluations of physical packaging often exhibit strong correlations and nonlinear characteristics, a single linear model has obvious limitations. Therefore, ANN is further introduced to overcome linear assumptions, leveraging its deep adaptive mechanisms to accurately extract the weights and rankings of each dimension. Although ANN offers extremely high predictive accuracy, it suffers from the “black box” limitation in terms of mechanism interpretation. Therefore, fsQCA, based on a set-theoretic perspective, is introduced to reveal how these nonlinear variables collectively trigger flow through asymmetric synergistic configurations. These three methods form a relatively rigorous logical chain, aiming to achieve triangulation through multiple methodologies.

Methodology

Variable setting

The variable system in this study is derived from the integration of interdisciplinary theories, including the antecedent triggering conditions of flow theory, biomimetic packaging design strategies, and the consumption characteristics of Generation Z. The antecedent conditions of flow experience provide the core psychological foundation for variable manipulation; the application strategies of biomimetic design in packaging are transformed into perceptible design attributes; and the tendency of Generation Z to pursue emotional value, immediate experiences, and sustainability further refines the selection and definition of variables; Additionally, the study integrates research on flow induction in product design. Ultimately, this study identifies five perceptual dimension condition variables that stimulate flow experiences among Gen Z consumers (the outcome variable) within the context of biomimetic packaging design: structural efficacy, feedback immediate, aesthetic appeal, narrative resonance, and sustainability value resonance (see Fig. 9). These are detailed below.

Perceptual dimensions of biomimetic packaging design.

This figure illustrates the conceptual mapping between the five design perception layers (Functional, Interactive, Sensory, Emotional, and Value) and the psychological antecedents of the Flow experience. It details how specific biomimetic strategies (texture, structure, color, form, and material) serve as external stimuli that are translated into perceived design attributes (Structural Efficiency, Immediate Feedback, Aesthetic Appeal, Narrative Resonance, and Sustainability Value Resonance). These dimensions are hypothesized to collectively trigger the immersive Flow state in Generation Z users, taking into account their unique cognitive and consumption traits.

Structural efficiency

Structural efficiency refers to users’ subjective perception of the functional superiority achieved through biomimetic structural design (such as honeycomb shock-absorbing structures and bamboo-joint reinforcing ribs), including operational convenience, protective reliability, and structural stability. This dimension is rooted in the “skill-challenge balance” condition from flow theory37, meaning that when users perceive their abilities as sufficient to handle the moderate challenges of opening or using the packaging, they are more likely to enter a state of immersion. This directly addresses the rational demand of Generation Z for efficient, practical, and zero-learning-curve design.

Immediate feedback

Immediate feedback refers to the degree to which users receive real-time multisensory responses during their interaction with packaging, including tactile, auditory, and visual feedback. This dimension directly corresponds to the core antecedent of flow theory, “immediate feedback,” and the principle of “multisensory interaction stimulation” in product design32,37. It aims to shorten the delay between action and perception through biomimetic mechanisms, thereby maintaining user engagement and a sense of control.

Aesthetic appeal

Aesthetic appeal refers to the emotional aesthetic experience evoked by packaging through biomimetic visual elements. This dimension integrates color and form biomimetic strategies in biomimetic design to enhance cognitive focus by reducing the risk of distraction through high visual appeal, while satisfying the emotional demands of Generation Z, who regard “appearance” as social currency and a key factor in decision-making.

Narrative resonance

Narrative resonance refers to the degree to which cultural symbols, natural stories, or values conveyed through biomimetic design in packaging evoke emotional resonance in users. For example, biomimetic forms or materials can be used to tell stories about environmental protection or culture. This dimension corresponds to the “core culture” factor in product design that stimulates flow32.

Sustainability value resonance

Sustainability refers not only to the environmental attributes of packaging at the physical level (such as biodegradable materials and recyclable structures); within the “Value Layer” framework of this study, it focuses more on measuring how physical products, as “green stimuli,” activate Gen Z consumers’ inner environmental values and sense of identity. This dimension responds to the eco-conscious choices Gen Z consumers make through their purchasing behavior, and it triggers a state of flow by aligning the sustainability concepts conveyed by products with consumers’ own values regarding green consumption.

Research methods

Partial least squares structural equation modeling

Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) is a variance-based multivariate statistical technique whose core algorithm aims to maximize the variance explained by endogenous latent variables38. Compared to traditional covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-SEM), which emphasizes rigorous theoretical validation, PLS-SEM demonstrates irreplaceable advantages in handling high-dimensional complex models, non-normally distributed data, and exploratory theory building39. In the fields of cognitive psychology and human–computer interaction, users’ perceptual experiences are often latent and highly subjective. PLS-SEM not only enables the precise measurement of these unobservable psychological constructs through multiple observational indicators but also effectively removes measurement error. This study employs PLS-SEM as the first component of a mixed-methods approach, aiming to preliminarily anchor the causal network between the physical and semantic stimuli of biomimetic packaging and user psychological immersion through rigorous linear mathematical logic.

Based on the five-dimensional perceptual theoretical framework of “Function-Interaction-Sensory-Emotional-Value” established earlier, the design attributes of biomimetic packaging constitute the external preconditions for triggering a user’s flow state. Specifically, structural efficacy and immediate feedback reduce the cognitive load of interaction from a human-factor perspective, directly addressing the triggering mechanisms of “challenge-skill balance” and “immediate feedback” in flow theory; aesthetic appeal and narrative resonance awaken users’ intrinsic motivation and emotional focus through visual stimuli and the transmission of cultural symbols; while sustainability, as a value alignment, endows the product experience with moral pleasure and a sense of meaning, further deepening the level of immersion.

Regarding the specific configuration of the structural model, given that in real-world human–computer interaction experiences, users’ multidimensional perceptions of physical products are often based on a holistic perspective, there is a potential risk of shared variance. therefore, the PLS-SEM model in this study did not include interaction terms between variables. Instead, it was configured as a first-order main effects model, aiming to assess the independent linear baselines of each dimension, while leaving the complex synergies and interactions among variables to be analyzed non-linearly in subsequent fsQCA.

Based on this, this study proposes the following fundamental hypotheses and constructs a theoretical research model (as shown in Fig. 10):

PLS-SEM theoretical model.

Artificial neural network

Artificial Neural Network (ANN) are computational models inspired by the structure and function of biological neural networks. Through the interconnection of a large number of neurons, they enable the processing of complex data and pattern recognition40. The core elements of an ANN model include the input layer, hidden layers, output layer, inter-connection weights, and the bias parameters of each neuron in the hidden layers. By leveraging its robust nonlinear mapping capabilities, an ANN can accurately simulate complex interaction patterns among variables, thereby enhancing the interpretability of empirical findings in a realistic manner41. Based on this, this study employs ANN as the second component of a mixed-methodology approach, aiming to build upon and surpass the linear baseline provided by PLS-SEM42.

By constructing a deep topological structure comprising input, hidden, and output layers, this study will precisely capture the latent nonlinear interactions among the five perceptual dimensions of biomimetic packaging when triggering a flow experience. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis will be employed to generate a ranking of predictive weights free from interference by artificial theoretical assumptions. This step not only serves as a robust cross-validation of traditional parameter estimation but also identifies the key design drivers with core predictive weights.

Fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis

Fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) is a methodological variant of Qualitative Comparative Analysis (QCA)43. QCA adopts a holistic perspective, positing that outcomes are shaped by complex causal relationships arising from combinations of multiple conditions44. Distinct from traditional dichotomies between qualitative and quantitative approaches, QCA integrates the strengths of case-oriented analysis and variable-based research. Among its variants, fsQCA—grounded in fuzzy set theory—offers a wider scope of application and is particularly well-suited for examining relationships among variables with gradational or fuzzy characteristics45.

This study employs fsQCA as the final component of a mixed-methods approach to explore the complex causal mechanisms by which biomimetic packaging elements influence Generation Z’s flow experiences, primarily based on three progressive methodological considerations:

First, it transcends the limitations of reductionism to reveal synergistic effects. While the aforementioned PLS-SEM and ANN models separately validated the net effects and predictive weights of variables, they essentially treated each perceptual dimension as an independently acting entity; fsQCA, however, adopts a holistic perspective, using set theory and Boolean algebra to analyze the configurational relationships among variables, thereby precisely revealing how multiple design perceptions trigger flow through interdependence and synergy.

Second, identifying multiple equivalent pathways. In real-world interactive scenarios, there is never just a single path leading to an optimal experience. fsQCA acknowledges and can precisely map out various combinations of antecedents that lead to the same high-flow outcome, directly providing brands with diverse, alternative references for differentiated design strategies.

Finally, fsQCA addresses causal asymmetry. It emphasizes that the combination of conditions leading to high flow is not necessarily the simple inverse of the causes leading to low flow. The introduction of this asymmetric perspective significantly expands the theoretical depth of biomimetic user experience research.

Questionnaire design and data collection

Stimuli and situational settings

Given that biomimetic packaging is a relatively specialized design concept, this study employed a Stimulus-Assisted Recall (SAR) paradigm to ensure that all Gen Z respondents had a consistent and clear understanding of the “perceptual dimensions of biomimetic packaging” prior to completing the questionnaire. Before the formal survey, respondents were presented with a set of carefully selected biomimetic packaging stimuli to serve as conceptual anchors.

Selection and order of stimuliThis study selected five representative biomimetic packaging cases mentioned in the literature review as stimuli (see Figures 3–7), covering the five biomimetic strategy dimensions of texture, structure, color, form, and material. These five products were presented in a random order to eliminate the primacy effect. It should be noted that this study was not a controlled experimental design; therefore, the biomimetic value of the products was not artificially manipulated. Instead, real-world commercial cases and high-fidelity design prototypes were used to measure users’ most natural overall perceptions.

Presentation of StimuliSince data collection employed a combination of online and offline methods, the presentation of stimuli was divided into two scenarios:The online scenario relied primarily on visual stimuli. For online respondents, the questionnaire presented the five packaging designs as high-resolution, multi-angle images accompanied by brief textual descriptions. Online respondents viewed the images without time constraints, triggering memories and resonances related to their past purchases or use of biomimetic packaging products.The offline scenario combines visual and tactile stimuli. Offline research was primarily conducted at university design exhibitions or design studios. The online scenario relied primarily on visual stimuli. Online respondents viewed the images without time constraints, triggering memories and resonances related to their past purchases or use of biomimetic packaging products.

The offline scenario combines visual and tactile stimuli. Offline research was primarily conducted at university design exhibitions or design studios. As shown in Table 4, the loadings of each item on its respective factor are greater than its cross-loadings on other dimensions, thereby satisfying the basic requirement for discriminant validity. However, the relatively high cross-loadings and the results of further multicollinearity tests indicate strong correlations among the latent variables, with VIF values in the internal model ranging from 5.317 to 6.990. This phenomenon has a solid theoretical foundation in consumer psychology and design evaluation research. Orth & Malkewitz60 argue that consumers’ perceptions of physical packaging often follow a “Gestalt”-like holistic approach, viewing packaging as a highly integrated, indivisible whole (holistic entity). At the same time, Bloch61 also pointed out that respondents’ positive evaluations of a certain dimension of product design (such as visual design) would drive positive ratings for other dimensions. Therefore, while the various perceptual dimensions of biomimetic design are conceptually independent, they inevitably exhibit high synergy and strong correlations in real consumer experiences.

Although this high multicollinearity, which aligns with real-world interaction patterns, challenges the strict statistical assumption of variable independence in traditional PLS-SEM, it provides the core methodological rationale and justification for the subsequent introduction of ANN—which is insensitive to multicollinearity—and fsQCA—which handles concurrent conditions based on set theory—in this study.

Structural model evaluation

Although the aforementioned high multicollinearity may, to some extent, reduce the accuracy of absolute estimates of independent net effects in linear structural equation modeling, in order to establish a traditional linear prediction benchmark for “triangulation” with subsequent nonlinear and configurational analyses, this study still employed SmartPLS 4.0 software and utilized the bootstrapping method (subsamples = 10,000) to conduct a preliminary assessment of the path coefficients in the structural model.

As shown in Fig. 11, the model’s coefficient of determination (R2) for the final endogenous latent variable “flow experience” reached 0.858. This indicates that the five biomimetic perception dimensions—structural efficacy, immediate feedback, aesthetic appeal, narrative resonance, and sustainability value resonance—collectively explain 85.8% of the variance in Gen Z consumers’ flow experience, demonstrating that the theoretical model possesses strong explanatory and predictive power. Regarding the hypothesis testing of specific pathways, H5 (β = 0.297, p < 0.001) and H4 (β = 0.237, p < 0.001) exhibited the highest path coefficients, indicating that the sustainable value identity and narrative emotional resonance conveyed by biomimetic packaging are the most critical linear factors directly driving Generation Z consumers’ flow experiences; simultaneously, H3 (β = 0.223, p = 0.001) and H1 (β = 0.165, p = 0.017) also exhibited significant positive effects, and hypotheses H1, H3, H4, and H5 were all effectively validated. However, H2 (β = 0.054, p = 0.383 > 0.05) did not reach statistical significance, meaning that Hypothesis H2 was not supported. This implies that, from the perspective of overall independent linear net effects, “immediate feedback” does not directly and independently trigger high levels of flow experiences.

Structural modeling results.

As discussed in the section on mixed methodologies, this lack of significant linear results is most likely due to the fact that, in Gestalt perception, the variance explained by this variable is masked by other highly correlated dimensions, thereby falling into the “net effect trap” of traditional symmetry-based statistical methods. Whether this dimension exerts a latent “catalytic” effect through nonlinear mappings or in combination with other dimensions within complex interactive scenarios will be subject to in-depth asymmetric analysis in subsequent studies using ANN and fsQCA.

ANN analysis process and results

To capture the potential complex nonlinear relationship between the perceptual dimensions of biomimetic packaging design and Generation Z’s flow experience, this study constructed a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) neural network model using SPSS software, building upon the PLS-SEM linear analysis. The data utilized the mean scores of the internal scales for each variable.

ANN model construction and training

The input layer of this MLP model consists of 5 neurons, corresponding to the five perceptual dimensions: structural efficacy, immediate feedback, aesthetic appeal, narrative resonance, and sustainability value resonance. The output layer contains 1 neuron, representing the flow experience. The number of nodes in the hidden layer is automatically optimized by the algorithm to avoid overfitting. Regarding the conversion mechanism for nonlinear signals, both the hidden layer and the output layer of this model employ the sigmoid activation function41. This function not only effectively handles continuous variables, but its smooth nonlinear mapping characteristics also enable precise fitting of the complex threshold effects between external physical stimuli and internal psychological immersion.

To ensure the model’s generalization ability and predictive robustness, this study employs the principle of tenfold cross-validation, randomly dividing the 218-sample data into a training set (80%) and a testing set (20%). Discussion

This study aims to transcend the linear causal limitations of traditional reductionism by employing a triangulated approach combining PLS-SEM, ANN and fsQCA to comprehensively decode the complex “black box” mechanism through which the perceptual dimensions of biomimetic packaging design trigger a state of flow in Generation Z. The findings not only confirm the necessity of multidimensional perceptual synergy but also reveal the asymmetric mechanisms and diverse evolutionary pathways underlying the surface-level interactions.

Asymmetric threshold effects of immediate feedback

The most theoretically significant finding of this study stems from the stark contrast exhibited by the three analytical methods on the “immediate feedback” dimension. In the symmetry statistics of PLS-SEM, immediate feedback failed to demonstrate a direct linear driving force on the flow experience (p = 0.386); however, the sensitivity analysis in the ANN revealed that it still possessed a non-linear predictive weight of 58.69%; more crucially, the configuration results of the fsQCA showed that, among the four equivalent pathways leading to high flow, three (configurations A1, A2, and C) all treated immediate feedback as an indispensable boundary condition.

This “contradiction” perfectly corroborates Woodside’s theory of causal asymmetry. For Generation Z68, as “digital natives”, the smooth opening and closing of physical biomimetic packaging and immediate sensory feedback are not “motivational factors” for creating ultimate immersion, but rather resemble “hygiene factors” or the foundation of the experience69. When fluid, immediate feedback is lacking, cognitive friction in the interaction instantly shatters the state of immersion,yet simply increasing the responsiveness of feedback, due to diminishing marginal returns, cannot independently induce flow. The immediate feedback must serve as a foundational medium, working in synergy with higher-order dimensions such as aesthetics and narrative, to become a catalyst for the emergence of flow. This serves as a warning that future interaction research must guard against the trap of the “linear net effect” and reject the binary mindset that “a single element is absolutely ineffective”.

The aesthetic compensation mechanism driven by emotion and narrative

The ANN results indicate that “aesthetic appeal (100%)” and “narrative resonance (93.07%)” dominate the predictive weights for flow. This finding is mechanistically illustrated in configuration A1 of the fsQCA: even though the biomimetic packaging has shortcomings in the core physical attribute of “structural efficacy”, strong aesthetic appeal and deep narrative resonance, combined with immediate feedback, can still successfully trigger flow.

This phenomenon is referred to in cognitive ergonomics as the “Aesthetic-Usability Effect”70. Don Norman71 notes in Emotional Design: Why We Love (or Hate) Everyday that a product’s visual impact at the “visceral level” and its construction of meaning at the “reflective level” can induce positive emotions in users, thereby broadening their cognitive bandwidth and fostering a high degree of tolerance for minor flaws in operation or structure. For Generation Z, consumption has evolved from the acquisition of functionality into a “ritual of self-expression”. When biomimetic design touches upon users’ spiritual sanctuary through mimetic colours or natural metaphors (such as the bitter gourd juice or whale-shaped chair mentioned in the text), emotional resonance successfully achieves an “immersive compensation” for the lack of physical functionality. This implies that designers must make the transition from “structural engineers” to “meaning-makers”.

Deep flow in the internalisation of values and moral alignment

In the PLS-SEM analysis, “sustainability value resonance” demonstrated the strongest direct linear explanatory power for flow (β = 0.297). Configuration B of the fsQCA further reveals a deeper psychological pathway that transcends traditional human-object interactions: in the absence of immediate feedback, a high state of flow can be reliably achieved solely through the combination of “sustainability value resonance” (the core condition) with aesthetics and narrative.

This pathway reveals the sublimation of the flow experience from “sensory pleasure” to “moral elevation”72. For some members of Generation Z, environmentalism serves as a self-identity marker reflecting personal moral values and a sense of social responsibility. Self-consistency theory posits that people actively choose behaviours or groups that align with their self-identity to reinforce it73,74. Bionic packaging, through the use of biodegradable materials or metaphors of material circulation (such as rice-husk wine bottle designs), gives tangible form to abstract environmental concepts. This “sustainability” is no longer a cold, impersonal material property, but rather a physical vehicle through which Generation Z constructs a “green social persona”. The core driving force behind the flow experienced by users in this interaction is no longer the low-level thrill of manipulation, but rather the deep focus and spiritual fulfilment derived from a shared sense of values. In this context, the brand essentially becomes a value-based ally for consumers in the pursuit of their moral ideals.

De-narrativisation and the spillover effect under a pragmatic aesthetic

Configuration C of the fsQCA model presents a pattern of de-narrativisation that differs from the emotional pathways described above. In this pathway, whilst “narrative resonance” is absent, structural efficacy, immediate feedback, aesthetic appeal and sustainability value resonance all converge to play a role. This independently explains approximately 41% of the high-flow cases.

This finding reveals the heterogeneity within the Generation Z consumer base—not all users require profound narrative depth. For some users, the combination of exceptional physical structural performance, fluid feedback mechanisms, intuitive biomimetic aesthetics, and responsible green credentials generates a powerful positive spillover effect within a functionalist framework. The pragmatic and hedonic aspects of a product are not mutually exclusive; rather, they can reinforce one another through interaction. When biomimetic design returns to the essence of “form follows function”, enveloping the user’s senses with a subtle, all-encompassing quality, it can likewise foster a pure state of flow driven by an exceptional mental model. This provides brands with a “less is more” paradigm for differentiated design.

Conclusion

Research contributions

This study systematically deconstructs the complex mapping relationship between the perceptual dimensions of biomimetic packaging design and the flow experience of Generation Z.

At the theoretical level, this study conducts an in-depth and systematic integration of existing flow theory and perceptual frameworks, extending their scope beyond traditional digital-virtual interactions to the realm of physical product packaging. Within this context, it validates and enriches a five-dimensional theoretical framework of user design perception encompassing “Function-Interaction-Sensory-Emotional-Value”. Building on this framework, the study avoids the limitations of traditional reductionism and simple linear statistics by introducing a triangulated methodology that integrates PLS-SEM, ANN and fsQCA. This hybrid paradigm not only establishes the linear baseline and non-linear predictive weights of each perceptual dimension on the flow experience but also profoundly reveals the causal asymmetry and co-evolutionary patterns among variables. The results of the study indicate that the flow experience is not a mechanical superposition of individual sensory stimuli, but rather a profound resonance between structural efficacy, aesthetic perception and moral identification within specific physical interaction contexts. Consequently, it provides a highly promising new perspective and explanatory model for future interdisciplinary research at the intersection of bionics, design engineering and consumer psychology.

At the practical level, this study offers a highly flexible strategic matrix for enterprises and design practitioners. Research shows that an exceptional user experience does not require blindly pursuing perfection across all design dimensions. Brands can flexibly choose from the following differentiated practical design strategies based on their own resource endowments:

For brands lacking core structural patents (corresponding to Emotion and Narrative-driven), the focus should be on enhancing the packaging’s “aesthetic appeal” and “narrative resonance.” By telling stories of nature through visual metaphors, they can compensate for mediocre physical functionality with emotional value;

Limitations of the study

Objectively speaking, this study inevitably faces certain limitations in its exploration of multidimensional perceptions and complex causal mechanisms.

First, regarding research design and causal inference, this study employed a single-group cross-sectional correlational design. It did not include “non-biomimetic conventional packaging” as a baseline control group and lacked longitudinal data to support its findings. Consequently, the study primarily reveals strong correlations and confounding factors among variables; it cannot definitively isolate the net causal incremental effect of biomimetic features. Therefore, caution should be exercised when drawing absolute causal inferences.

Second, regarding sample representativeness and generalizability, this study relies on 218 valid data points obtained through snowball sampling and is highly focused on the Generation Z demographic within a specific cultural context, without incorporating other age groups for intergenerational comparison. As digital natives, Generation Z exhibits distinct group-specific characteristics in their preference for instant gratification and sensitivity to environmental values, which to some extent limits the direct generalization of the study’s conclusions to a broader audience or cross-cultural contexts.

Furthermore, regarding the validity of the measurement methods, although this study employed multi-scenario stimuli, the final results still rely on retrospective self-report questionnaires. Physical packaging interaction is inherently a dynamic process involving multisensory feedback, including tactile, auditory, and even mechanical sensations. The questionnaire-based recall method not only struggles to fully replicate the authentic physical immersion but is also susceptible to interference from human memory simplification and subjective beautification biases.

Finally, regarding the temporal dynamics of the experience, the cross-sectional data structure focuses on static correlations and cannot effectively capture the patterns of diminishing novelty toward biomimetic features over a longer product lifecycle. It also struggles to dynamically demonstrate whether the moral pleasure derived from perceptions of sustainability will experience diminishing marginal utility over time.

Future research directions

Building upon the logical foundations established by this study, future academic exploration may extend into deeper cognitive mechanisms and broader technological frontiers.

In terms of research methodology, we advocate the incorporation of neurophysiological measurement techniques such as eye-tracking, electroencephalography (EEG) or electrodermal response (EDR). By capturing real-time biofeedback signals from users at the very moment they interact with physical packaging, we can overcome the subjective lag inherent in self-reports, thereby more precisely mapping the micro-mechanisms that trigger flow experiences amidst the interplay of multisensory channels.

In terms of research design, rigorous controlled experiments could be incorporated in the future. On the one hand, by establishing control groups comprising “biomimetic packaging” and “non-biomimetic conventional packaging,” it would be possible to more precisely isolate the net incremental effect of biomimetic features on the induction of flow; On the other hand, through longitudinal tracking experiments and cross-cultural, cross-generational comparative designs, researchers can systematically compare the differences in sensitivity to biomimetic design perceptions between Generation Z and other age groups, thereby examining the evolutionary trajectory and adaptability of biomimetic design experiences across different cultural contexts, age groups, and time spans.

Furthermore, in the face of the wave of technological iteration, future design research should keenly identify opportunities at the intersection of new materials and artificial intelligence. Investigating intelligent biomimetic materials with environmental responsiveness, or personalised adaptive packaging incorporating generative artificial intelligence (AIGC), will reveal how these innovations reshape individuals’ cognitive boundaries and the depth of their flow states. These cutting-edge topics are certain to inject a broader scope of exploration into the convergent evolution of biomimetic engineering and human–computer interaction.
