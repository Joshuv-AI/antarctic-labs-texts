Continuous frost causes a greater reduction in forest growth than isolated frost in the Northern Hemisphere
Authors: Hongjun Yang, Wenjing Tao, Juan Chen, Qin Yan, Yuchuan Yang, Haoyu Qiu, Junkai Ma, Josep Peñuelas, Lei Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrost events can cause severe and often irreversible damage to leaves and canopies. The negative impacts of isolated frost events, defined as discontinuous days of extreme cold, on forests are well documented. However, a critical aspect of frost damage—its prolonged duration—is largely overlooked, even though freezing temperatures often persist for multiple consecutive days in natural environments. The impact of continuous frost events, characterized by consecutive days of extreme cold, on forest growth remains poorly understood. Using multiple remote-sensing data sources, herein we demonstrate that continuous frost events result in significantly greater declines in forest growth compared to isolated frost events across the Northern Hemisphere. Our frost-controlled experiments using seven tree species further confirm that continuous frost causes more severe damage to cell integrity and photosynthetic rate. Using GPP and climate data simulated by CMIP6 models, we find that continuous frost events significantly reduce forest growth by the end of this century, with the largest reductions observed under high-emission scenarios. Our findings underscore the importance of accounting for the prolonged duration of frost events to fully capture their impacts on forest ecosystems, as failing to consider this factor may lead to underestimation of frost-induced effects on forest growth and carbon cycling under climate change.Subject terms: Forest ecology, Environmental impact

Introduction

Forests cover approximately 31% of the Earth’s land area and account for roughly 75% of the gross primary productivity of terrestrial ecosystems, which plays a critical role in mitigating climate change by absorbing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere1,2. Under climate change, forests are increasingly vulnerable to extreme climate events such as droughts, heatwaves, and frosts, which can cause severe, often irreversible damage to forest ecosystems3–5. The frequency, intensity, and spatial extent of these extreme events are projected to increase under future climate scenarios6–9. Understanding the impact of these extreme events on tree growth is therefore essential for accurately assessing and predicting forest productivity and terrestrial carbon cycling.

A frost event is defined as the occurrence of freezing temperatures capable of damaging the vegetative or reproductive parts of plants10. Freezing temperatures often cause irreversible cell damage, as the ice crystals forming inside the cells mechanically disrupt cell walls and membranes, leading to cell rupture and exudation of tissue fluid, ultimately hindering tree growth11–13. Furthermore, frost events can significantly reduce photosynthetic rate and carbon assimilation, leading to decreased carbon sequestration and impeding tree growth14–17. In extratropical forests, late spring frosts, which occur after the start of growing season, are of particular concern, as newly developed leaves and shoots are vulnerable to frost damage18–20. Under climate change, the risk of frost damage to forest productivity is also increasing in the Northern Hemisphere. The adverse effects of frost events on tree growth have been broadly reported, such as in Europe and North America, where frost damage causes great economic losses in forestry21,22.

Previous studies on frost events have primarily focused on temperatures falling below certain thresholds on isolated days19,23,24, despite the fact that freezing temperature typically persists over consecutive days in nature. However, sustained and repeated extreme climate events may exacerbate adverse impacts on forest productivity compared with isolated extreme climate events25–27. Frost damage may induce cell dehydration, membrane disruption, and chloroplast damage, disrupting the structure and function of leaves15,28,29. Prolonged frost exposure likely causes greater damage to leaves and more significant disruption of photosynthetic carbon uptake. Compared to isolated frost events, however, the impact of uninterrupted continuous days of frost events on forest growth remains uninvestigated. In addition, effects of frost damage on tree growth often vary spatially across different climate zones. Consequently, employing large-scale data to analyze how forest growth respond to continuous frost events is crucial for accurately predicting future changes in terrestrial carbon cycling and its associated climate feedbacks.

Combining remote-sensing datasets and frost-controlled experiments, herein we investigate and compare the effects of isolated frost events, defined as a single day of extreme cold temperatures, and continuous frost events, defined as uninterrupted consecutive days of extreme cold temperatures, on forest growth in the Northern Hemisphere. We first use multiple remote-sensing-derived vegetation indices to assess the overall difference in growth reduction caused by isolated and continuous frost events across the Northern Hemisphere. We then examine the spatial differences in frost-induced growth reduction across various climatic zones to assess the spatial heterogeneity in the impacts of isolated and continuous frost events on forest growth. Lastly, we conduct frost-controlled experiments using seedlings from seven tree species to further validate the impacts of continuous frost events on tree growth. We hypothesize that continuous frost, due to its prolonged duration, has more pronounced effects on forest growth than isolated frost events under climate change.

Results

We obtained multiple remote-sensing-derived vegetation indices from 2001 to 2020, including the Gross Primary Productivity (GPP), Net Primary Production (NPP), Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) from MODIS (GPPMODIS, NPPMODIS, and EVIMODIS), and Contiguous Solar-Induced Fluorescence (CSIF), to represent forest growth across the Northern Hemisphere (see “Methods”). A frost day was defined as a day when the daily minimum temperature fell below −4 °C of each pixel during the growing season from April to September based on climate data from the CRU JRA v2.4 datasets. To account for uncertainty in frost event classification, we tested four candidate definitions based on different durations of consecutive frost days, ranging from two to five days (see “Methods”). Continuous frost events were defined as those with two to five consecutive frost days, whereas isolated events referred to cases in which frost days occurred sporadically and did not form extended sequences (Fig. S1). As results were consistent across all definitions (Figs. 1 and S2–S4 and Supplementary Table 1), we presented the main findings using a standardized classification, where isolated frost events are defined as single-day occurrences (i.e., events lasting less than two consecutive days) and continuous frost events as those lasting two or more consecutive days. Results based on other alternative definitions are presented as a sensitivity analysis in the Supplementary Information (Fig. S2–S4 and Supplementary Table 1).

Based on this standardized definition, we then calculated the percentage changes in GPPMODIS (ΔGPPMODIS), NPPMODIS (ΔNPPMODIS), EVIMODIS (ΔEVIMODIS), and CSIF (ΔCSIF) when isolated and continuous frost events occurred and did not occur for each pixel across the Northern Hemisphere. All datasets consistently revealed that continuous frost exhibited a stronger negative impact on forest growth than isolated frost based on the four candidate definitions (Figs. 1 and S2–S4, P < 0.05; Supplementary Table 1). To ensure the robustness of our findings, we further accounted for the potential confounding effect of the number of frost days on forest growth using a linear mixed-effects model (see Methods). After controlling for the number of frost days, continuous frost events still exerted a significant negative impact on forest growth (Supplementary Table 2). These results underscore the independent role of frost duration in shaping tree growth responses and further strengthen the reliability of our conclusions.

We further compared the changes in forest growth due to isolated and continuous frost events across different climatic zones. In different climate zones, both continuous and isolated frost events significantly reduced forest growth (Fig. 2, P < 0.05; Supplementary Table 3). In temperate and boreal forests, we observed that both continuous and isolated frost events significantly reduced forest growth, with the negative effects of continuous frost being significantly greater than those of isolated frost in temperate and boreal forests across all datasets (Fig. 2, P < 0.05; Supplementary Table 3). However, no significant difference was observed between the effects of isolated and continuous frost on tree growth in subtropical forests (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 3).

To assess the interactive effects of frost and drought on forest growth, we compared the impacts of frost events across arid and humid regions. Across all datasets, continuous frost events consistently exerted a more pronounced negative effect on forest growth than isolated events in both climate regimes (Fig. S6 and Supplementary Table 5). Notably, the overall decline in growth was significantly greater in arid regions than in humid and sub-humid regions (Fig. S6, P < 0.05; Supplementary Table 5), suggesting that water-limited ecosystems are more susceptible to frost-related stress. We further analyzed the effects of frost using linear mixed-effects models, incorporating a drought index as a covariate (see Methods). Even after accounting for drought conditions, continuous frost events continued to cause significantly greater reductions in growth than isolated events (Supplementary Table 6). Moreover, a significant interaction between frost and drought was detected, with the negative effect of frost on tree growth diminishing as water availability increased (Supplementary Table 6).

To further validate our results, we conducted a frost-controlled experiment utilizing a total of 420 seedlings from seven tree species (see Methods). We investigated and compared the effects of isolated and continuous frost events on photosynthetic rate and relative electrolyte conductivity after frost treatments (see Methods). Based on the frost-controlled experiment, we observed significant differences in net photosynthetic rates and relative electrolyte conductivity of trees between continuous frost and isolated frost treatments (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Tables 7 and 8). Both isolated and continuous frost caused a decrease in net photosynthetic rates of trees. However, the mean net photosynthetic rate of trees was significantly lower under continuous frost treatment compared to isolated frost treatment across all tree species and within each species (Fig. 4a, b, P < 0.05; Supplementary Table 7). Additionally, compared to species with slower growth rates, species with faster growth rates (such as Pinus sylvestris and Quercus variabilis) exhibited a greater decline in photosynthetic rates under continuous frost events (Figs. 4b and S7a and Supplementary Table 7). Both isolated and continuous frost treatments increased relative electrolyte conductivity of trees. However, the mean relative electrolyte conductivity was significantly higher following continuous frost than that under isolated frost treatment across all tree species (Fig. 4c, d, P < 0.05; Supplementary Table 8). Furthermore, the electrical conductivity of fast-growing tree species increased more than that of slow-growing species (Fig. S7b).

Discussion

Under climate change, numerous studies have examined frost-induced damage to vegetation ecosystems in the Northern Hemisphere16,20,30,31. Most research has focused on the detrimental effects of isolated frost events, typically characterized by a single day of extreme cold temperatures, on vegetation growth23,32. The impacts of multiple isolated frost events or repeated frost occurrences across consecutive years on vegetation growth have also been explored15,32–34. In natural environments, frost events often persist for several consecutive days, a phenomenon referred to as continuous frost. This condition, characterized by uninterrupted extreme cold temperatures, differs significantly from the shorter, isolated frost events that have predominantly been the focus of prior studies16,35,36. Here, we show that continuous frost events, due to their prolonged duration, lead to significantly greater declines in forest growth than isolated frost events (Fig. S8). These findings highlight the need to account for frost duration when assessing and predicting the impacts of climate extremes on forest productivity and carbon cycling under future climate change.

Our study reveals that continuous frost, characterized by prolonged periods of extreme cold, exerts greater stress on forest growth compared to isolated frost events. Unlike isolated frost events, which often allow for rapid tree recovery, continuous frost frequently results in irreversible cellular damage37,38. During extended frost periods, ice crystals form within cells, mechanically disrupting cell walls and membranes39. This prolonged exposure to low temperatures typically leads to extensive cellular rupture and tissue necrosis40,41. Consequently, the cumulative cellular damage from continuous frost severely hinders recovery and significantly impairs tree growth, unlike the relatively minor effects of short, isolated frost events. High relative electrolyte conductivity is a well-established indicator of severe damage to plant cells42. In our frost experiment, we observed significantly higher relative electrolyte conductivity in trees exposed to continuous frost compared to those subjected to isolated frost events. This finding suggests that continuous frost inflicts more severe damage on leaf tissues, further highlighting its detrimental effects of tree growth. In addition to structural damage, continuous frost disrupts leaf photosynthesis, which is highly sensitive to temperature fluctuations. Prolonged cold conditions can cause irreversible chlorophyll degradation, thereby significantly lowering photosynthetic rate43,44. Similarly, our frost-controlled experiments revealed that the photosynthetic rate of trees declined more following continuous frost treatment compared to isolated frost events, especially for tree species with fast growth rate. Fast-growing tree species are generally more vulnerable to frost damage and exhibit greater growth reductions under freezing conditions, likely due to a combination of embolism-prone wide xylem conduits, limited investment in cold tolerance mechanisms, and a growth-defense trade-off that prioritizes rapid biomass accumulation over structural and physiological resilience45. This experimental result further underscores the heightened physiological stress imposed by prolonged frost conditions on tree growth and function.

Our study revealed that continuous frost events led to greater reductions in tree growth than isolated events across climatic zones, with temperate and boreal forests experiencing more pronounced declines than subtropical forests. We further found that continuous frost events caused greater energy loss in temperate and boreal forests than in subtropical forests, as reflected by larger reductions in evapotranspiration. These differences likely reflect the interaction between thermal energy availability and regional frost resistance46. In temperate and boreal regions, although trees tend to exhibit higher frost resistance, limited thermal energy and greater frost-induced energy loss may prolong recovery periods, ultimately constraining post-frost growth under continuous freezing conditions47. Conversely, in subtropical regions, trees exhibit lower frost resistance due to the infrequency of frost events. However, higher and relatively stable energy availability facilitates faster recovery, partially offsetting their lower frost tolerance48. Thus, the regional variation in tree responses to frost damage might be explained by the interplay between energy availability and frost resistance.

Our findings provide robust evidence that continuous frost events, owing to their prolonged duration, cause substantially greater reductions in forest growth than isolated events. This highlights the critical importance of incorporating frost duration into assessments of forest productivity and carbon cycling. Neglecting this dimension risks underestimating the ecological and functional impacts of frost damage, particularly under future climate scenarios where compound extremes are expected to intensify49. To improve predictive accuracy, dynamic vegetation models (DVMs) should integrate frost-duration metrics to better capture the cumulative effects of consecutive freezing days on carbon fluxes and biomass accumulation50,51. Such refinements are essential not only for forecasting vegetation responses but also for enabling early detection of frost-induced anomalies and informing timely, targeted interventions. These advances will play a central role in guiding climate-resilient forest management strategies, from selecting frost-tolerant species to optimizing planting schedules, ultimately enhancing ecosystem resilience in a warming world.

The observed interaction between continuous frost and drought–where increased water availability mitigates frost-induced growth reductions–further underscores the need to account for interacting stressors in both modeling frameworks and management practices52,53. As climate change accelerates, forests are increasingly exposed to concurrent disturbances, including droughts, heatwaves, and pest outbreaks49. Repeated freeze-arid cycles may compound physiological stress, compromise plant defenses, and increase susceptibility to biotic agents52,54. Addressing these synergistic effects through integrated risk assessments and adaptive management is essential for predicting ecological tipping points and maintaining ecosystem stability.

Although the integration of satellite remote sensing with field-based frost manipulation provides a robust framework for assessing the impacts of frost duration, several limitations warrant consideration. Satellite-derived vegetation indices may be subject to uncertainties caused by atmospheric interference, cloud contamination, and sensor limitations, which can obscure short-term frost responses55. Future experiments conducted across a broader range of regions and species will be essential for better capturing the variability in frost responses across different species and climatic regions. Despite these limitations, our findings lay a critical foundation for advancing the representation of compound climate stressors in ecological models and for guiding climate-resilient forest management under climate change.

Using GPP and climate data simulated by CMIP6 models, we projected the impact of continuous frost events on forest growth under four future emission scenarios and found the greatest reductions under the high-emission SSP5-8.5 scenario. Under such scenarios, trees may adapt by increasing their tolerance to heat and water stress, but this adaptation could come at the cost of reduced cold tolerance. As trees acclimate to progressively warmer conditions, they may gradually lose cold hardiness, increasing their vulnerability to infrequent yet potentially damaging frost events56. Although climate models, including IPCC (2023) projections, suggest a future decline in the frequency of extreme cold events and frost days, the physiological impact of rare frost episodes may remain substantial57,58. Therefore, even if frost events become less frequent, their potential to disrupt tree function and constrain forest growth remains critical for understanding and predicting forest responses to climate change.

Our study, integrating data analysis and controlled experiments, demonstrates that continuous frost events exert more pronounced negative impacts on forest growth. Using remote-sensing data, our study demonstrated that continuous frost exerts a stronger negative impact on forest growth compared to isolated frost events across the Northern Hemisphere. Frost-controlled experiments indicated significant reductions in net photosynthetic rates and increases in relative electrolyte conductivity under continuous frost conditions, especially for fast-growing species. While continuous frost had a stronger impact than isolated frost across all climatic zones, the extent of the effect varied among regions, likely driven by the interplay between energy availability and frost resistance. Under future warming scenarios, trees may adapt to increasing heat and drought stress by shifting their physiological strategies, potentially at the cost of reduced frost resistance. This trade-off may leave them more vulnerable to infrequent but severe frost events despite an overall decline in cold extremes. Therefore, reducing carbon emissions is critical to mitigate the adverse effects of frost damage on future forest growth. Our findings not only emphasize the importance of considering prolonged and repeated extreme events when assessing the long-term impacts of frost on forest ecosystems and carbon cycle feedbacks but also enhance our ability to predict forest carbon sequestration capacity.

Methods

Remote-sensing growth indices

We acquired monthly MODIS gross primary productivity (GPPMODIS) and net primary production (NPPMODIS) data from the MOD17A2H product with a spatial resolution of 500 m between 2001 and 2020 (https://lpdaac.usgs.gov/products/mod17a2hv006/)59. This dataset exhibits a strong correlation with the GPP records, which have been extensively employed in previous studies investigating the terrestrial carbon cycle60,61. Monthly MODIS Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVIMODIS) during 2001–2020 with a spatial resolution of 0.05° were obtained from the Global Land Surface Satellite (GLASS) product provided by the HKU Geography Group (https://www.glass.hku.hk/index.html)62. The global all-sky daily average spatially contiguous solar-induced fluorescence (CSIF) dataset with a spatial resolution of 0.05° between 2001 and 2020 was obtained from the study by Zhang et al. (10.6084/m9.figshare.6387494)63. This dataset effectively captures vegetation dynamics and interannual variability across diverse climatic conditions, providing valuable insights into global and regional GPP64,65. To unify the resolution of the remote-sensing and climate datasets, the GPPMODIS, NPPMODIS, CSIF, and EVIMODIS datasets were resampled to a spatial resolution of 0.5°. Additionally, we downloaded a global annual fractional tree cover dataset of 250 m resolution for the years 2001 to 2020 (10.5281/zenodo.10589730)66. GPP and NPP quantify carbon fixation and biomass accumulation, respectively. CSIF serves as a direct proxy for photosynthetic efficiency, while EVI reflects vegetation cover and canopy greenness63,67,68. Together, these metrics provide an integrated assessment of frost impacts on forest growth.

Climate data

Daily minimum temperature dataset was obtained from the Climatic Research Unit and Japanese reanalysis climate dataset (CRU JRA v2.4)69–71 between 2001 and 2020 with a spatial resolution of 0.5° (https://catalogue.ceda.ac.uk/). Monthly self-calibrating palmer drought severity index (PDSI) data during 2001–2020 at a spatial resolution of 0.5° were obtained from the Climatic Research Unit (https://crudata.uea.ac.uk/cru/)72. The global aridity index (AI) during 1970–2000 was obtained from the CGIAR-CSI database73 (https://cgiarcsi.community/data/global-aridity-and-pet-database/). Monthly MODIS evapotranspiration (ET) data during 2001–2020 with a spatial resolution of 0.5° were obtained from the GLASS product provided by the HKU Geography Group (https://www.glass.hku.hk/index.html)62. Bilinear interpolation offers a balance between computational efficiency and spatial continuity when resampling continuous environmental variables, making it widely applicable in ecological and climate research74,75. Thus, using the bilinear interpolation method in the raster package76 of R version 4.0.277, we extracted tree cover data for each remote-sensing pixel and calculated the annual average fraction of tree cover. We selected pixels with an average tree cover of more than 50% as forest ecosystems for study in the Northern Hemisphere. Following this, forests were categorized into subtropical, temperate, and boreal regions based on a map of terrestrial ecoregions78.

The Norwegian Earth System Model version 2-Medium resolution (NorESM2-MM) and Euro-Mediterranean Centre on Climate Change Earth System Model version 2 (CMCC-ESM2) have improved the representation of key physical processes and their comprehensive Earth system approach, allowing more reliable projections of future climate change, including carbon cycle feedbacks79,80. Thus, future GPP data for the period 2020–2079 were obtained from the NorESM2-MM and CMCC-ESM2 models at 1° spatial resolution under four CMIP6 Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs): SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5 (https://esgf-node.llnl.gov/projects/esgf-llnl/). Future daily minimum temperature from 2020 to 2079 was also obtained from the NorESM2-MM and CMCC-ESM2 models under four CMIP6 SSPs: SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5, at a 1° resolution. Future GPP and historical as well as future daily minimum temperature data for each forested pixel were extracted using bilinear interpolation in the raster package76 of R version 4.0.277.

Identification of isolated and continuous frost events

In this study, a frost day was defined as a day when the daily minimum temperature fell below −4 °C, a threshold at which trees can potentially be damaged by cold conditions19,81. Using this threshold, we identified all frost days for each year at each pixel during the growing season from April to September using historical and future daily minimum temperature data. We further defined paired isolated and continuous frost events based on consecutive days of frost occurrence. To address the uncertainty in frost event classification, we tested four candidate definitions based on different durations of consecutive frost days, ranging from two to five days (see Fig. S1). Continuous frost events were defined as those with two to five consecutive frost days, whereas isolated events referred to cases in which frost days occurred sporadically and did not form extended sequences.

Effects of continuous and isolated frost on forest growth

We calculated the percentage changes in GPPMODIS (ΔGPPMODIS), NPPMODIS (ΔNPPMODIS), CSIF (ΔCSIF), and EVIMODIS (ΔEVIMODIS) when isolated and continuous frost events occurred and when no frost events occurred for each pixel during the growing season, to examine the effects of continuous and isolated frosts on forest growth. The 95% confidence intervals of growth changes were used to determine the statistical significance of the effects of isolated and continuous frost events on forest growth. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s HSD test, was employed to compare the changes in growth due to isolated and continuous frost events. To ensure the robustness of the result, we further accounted for the potential confounding effect of the number of frost days on forest growth using a linear mixed-effects model. In this model, the response variable is NPPMODIS, with the predictors being the number of continuous frost events (i.e., frequency of prolonged frost events) and the number of frost days (i.e., frequency of frost days), and the PDSI as a covariate, and random intercepts specified for each pixel.

Additionally, we compared the differences in growth changes due to isolated and continuous frosts across subtropical, temperate, and boreal regions. We also analyzed the changes in evapotranspiration when frost occurred across subtropical, temperate, and boreal regions. The 95% confidence intervals were calculated for frost-induced changes in tree growth and ET across different climatic zones. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test was performed to compare forest growth changes and ET among different climatic zones. To assess the interactive effects of frost and drought on forest growth, we compared the impacts of frost events across arid and humid regions. We first divided the study region into three groups: the arid (AI: 0–0.4), the sub-humid (AI: 0.4–0.65), and the humid regions (AI > 0.65) based on the aridity index values82. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test was used to compare the difference in the forest growth when frost events occurred in arid and humid regions. We further analyzed the interactive effect between drought and frost on forest growth using linear mixed-effects models. In these models, the response variable was NPPMODIS, with fixed effects including the PDSI, the occurrence of frost events, monthly PDSI, and their interaction term, and random intercepts specified for each pixel.

We calculated the frequency of frost events for each year at each pixel. Linear regression analysis was then employed to assess the relationship between growth reduction and the increasing frequency of frost events across different climate zones. In these models, the response variable was the frost-induced change in forest growth (ΔNPPMODIS), while the predictor variable was the frequency of continuous frost events. We calculated the percentage change in future GPP (ΔGPP) during 2020–2049 and 2050–2079 from the CMIP models under four future climatic scenarios (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5) for each pixel when continuous frost events occurred and did not. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test was performed to compare forest growth changes among different climate scenarios.

Frost-controlled experiment

To further validate our results, we conducted a frost-controlled experiment in Pengzhou, Sichuan Province, China (31°10′ N, 104°00′ E) during the growing season from April to July in 2024. Utilizing a total of 420 seedlings from seven tree species (Supplementary Table 10), we investigated and compared the effects of isolated and continuous frost events on tree growth. The seven selected tree species are ecologically and functionally representative of broader tree functional groups, encompassing both broadleaf and coniferous types to capture potential differences in frost response strategies. Previous studies have demonstrated that temperatures below −4 °C can be lethal to new shoots and significantly impede tree growth83–85. Thus, we conducted the frost experiments at −4 °C. For the continuous frost treatment, 20 seedlings of each tree species were placed in an artificial climate chamber (Shanghai Sanfa model LGP-1000EA) set at −4 °C with 20% relative humidity for a continuous period of 48 h. For the isolated frost treatment, 20 seedlings of each species were placed in another artificial climate chamber set at −4 °C with 20% relative humidity for 12 h. The 12-h duration reflects a typical nocturnal frost event, corresponding to the natural period from sunset to sunrise when temperatures are lowest, and frost is most likely to occur. After this exposure, the trees were transferred to a controlled environment to recover for three to five days. This cycle of exposure followed by recovery was repeated four times, totaling 48 h of frost exposure. Additionally, a total of 20 trees of each species were placed under natural conditions, serving as the control group.

Following the frost treatment, we collected samples and measured relative electrolyte conductivity to quantify the extent of cell membrane damage in the leaves. One or two leaves from the middle of each tree, avoiding the main vein, were cut into small pieces and placed in 15 ml test tubes containing 10 ml of purified water. After standing for 24 h, the conductivity of the pure water (S0) was measured using a Benchtop Conductivity Meter (DDS-307A). The conductivity of the sample solution (S1) was also measured. After initial measurements, the samples were autoclaved at 120 °C for 20 min and cooled to room temperature, after which the conductivity (S2) was measured. The relative electrolyte conductivity was calculated as follows: (S1 − S0)/(S2 − S0) × 100%.

After the frost treatments, 420 potted tree seedlings from the seven species and three treatment groups were placed in an open field and watered regularly. We then randomly selected ten seedlings of each species from each treatment group to measure the net photosynthetic rate. Three leaves from different positions (top, middle, and bottom) on each tree were measured using a portable Li-6800 photosynthesis system (LI-COR, USA, Li-6800) equipped with a 2 × 2 cm leaf chamber, at a flow rate of 500 μmol s⁻¹, humidity controlled at 50%, and CO₂ concentration set at 400 μmol mol⁻¹. The light intensity was set between 600 and 1200 μmol m⁻² s⁻¹ to accommodate the varied points of light saturation of different tree species. A one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test was conducted to compare differences in average relative electrolyte conductivity and photosynthetic rates among frost treatments, both across all species and within each species. Based on their initial photosynthetic rates, the seven tree species were classified into two groups: fast-growth and slow-growth. A one-way ANOVA was then performed to analyze differences in changes in photosynthetic rate and relative electrolyte conductivity after frost treatments for these two groups. The overall workflow is illustrated in Fig. S9 to improve clarity and reproducibility.
