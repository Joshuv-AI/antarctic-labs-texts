Secure Data Reconstruction: A Direct Data-Driven Approach
Jiaqi Yan, Ivan Markovsky, John Lygeros

Abstract
This paper addresses the problem of secure data reconstruction for unknown systems, where data collected from the system are susceptible to malicious manipulation. We aim to recover the real trajectory without prior knowledge of the system model. To achieve this, a behavioral language is used to represent the system, describing it using input/output trajectories instead of state-space models. We consider two attack scenarios. In the first scenario, up to k k entries of the collected data are malicious. On the other hand, the second scenario assumes that at most k k channels from sensors or actuators can be compromised, implying that any data collected from these channels might be falsified. For both scenarios, we formulate the trajectory recovery problem as an optimization problem and introduce sufficient conditions to ensure successful recovery of the true data. Since finding exact solutions to these problems can be computationally inefficient, we further approximate them using an ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm and group Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO). We demonstrate that under certain conditions, these approximation problems also find the true trajectory while maintaining low computation complexity. Finally, we extend the proposed algorithms to noisy data. By reconstructing the secure trajectory, this work serves as a safeguard mechanism for subsequent data-driven control methods.



I
Introduction

The past decades have witnessed remarkable research interest in cyber-physical systems (CPSs). Application of these systems varies from aerospace, manufacturing, transportation, and power grids. Security stands as a top concern for CPS, since any failure or attack can lead to significant economic losses, or even pose threats to human lives
[
1
]
.
Nowadays, the extensive use of communication units (e.g., sensors and actuators) makes the system more vulnerable than before, where an adversary can easily break a communication channel and tamper with the transmitted data. Researchers, therefore, have recognized the importance of implementing secure detection and control algorithms.

Existing solutions typically rely on model-based approaches. One common strategy is detecting attacks by creating an observer based on the system model
[
2
,
3
]
,
where both linear and nonlinear systems are considered
. One estimates the process states using this observer, and
makes
a decision on possible misbehaviors by comparing these estimates with the received data. An application of this approach is bad data detection in power systems
[
4
]
.

Instead of detecting and isolating malicious components, another line of research focuses on developing resilient algorithms which guarantee an acceptable performance even in the presence of false data. This is usually achieved by deploying additional nodes or channels to “compensate” for fault signals. For instance,
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
solve the problem of secure state estimation in
linear systems
. According to their results, the system must be at least
2k
-sparse observable in order to tolerate
k
malicious sensors. This means that the removal of any
2k
sensors should not compromise the system’s observability. Similarly, for the resilient cooperative control problem, a
(2k+1)
-robust graph is necessary to handle
k
malicious agents, where redundant communication channels should be added to each normal agent
[
9
,
10
]
.

In these works, exact system models are integral to the algorithm design. However, deriving models from first principles can be challenging in practical scenarios. Often, only input/output trajectories collected from the system are available, which motivates the study of data-driven control. Typically, data-driven methods can be classified into
indirect
data-driven approaches, involving system identification followed by the model-based control, and
direct
data-driven approaches, where control policies are learned directly from data without identifying a model
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
.

Here we aim to enhance the CPS resiliency using a direct data-driven approach. We investigate two potential attack scenarios. In the first scenario, up to
k
entries of the collected data are malicious. On the other hand, the second scenario assumes that at most
k
channels of sensors or actuators are compromised, meaning that any input/output data collected from these channels can be manipulated. Our objective is to reconstruct the true trajectory for both scenarios without identifying a parametric representation of the system.

As shown in
[
14
,
15
]
, the behavioral approach is particularly suitable for solving direct data-driven problems and has advantages over classical model-based methods. The key result established in the behavioral setting that makes solution of the direct-data driven control problems possible is the so called fundamental lemma (
[
16
]
). In our paper, we build upon a more recent result from
[
17
]
, which extends the fundamental lemma by providing necessary and sufficient conditions—known as the generalized persistency of excitation condition—for establishing a nonparametric representation of the data-generating system (which is the unknown system under study), without requiring a predetermined input/output partitioning of the data
[
18
]
. Specifically, the nonparametric representation refers to an equivalent description of the system using input/output data, rather than the system matrices.
The key assumption on the data requires prior knowledge of the true system’s complexity (number of inputs and order). The result is applicable for data consisting of multiple trajectories, uncontrollable systems, and various classes of
nonlinear systems
, see
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
.

Note that the data used to construct the nonparametric representation is obtained
offline
. Building upon this nonparametric representation, we then perform
online
data reconstruction. That is, to detect whether the data received online is malicious.
The contributions of this paper are
two-fold
:

1) In both the entry-attacked and channel-attacked scenarios, we formulate the trajectory recovery problem as optimization problems utilizing
[
17
, Corollary 21]
. We introduce
sufficient conditions on the Hankel matrix for performing the online data reconstruction that ensures recovery of the true trajectory
. The conditions are demonstrated to be tight; violating them can result in unsuccessful recovery.

2) The proposed problems are known to be NP-hard. Finding exact solutions involves solving a combinatorial number of subproblems. To reduce the computational complexity, we approximate the problems by using the
\ell_{1}
-norm and group Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO). This transformation renders the problems convex, enabling tractable solutions with low computational complexity. We further establish conditions under which these approximation problems also yield the true trajectory. Extension to the noisy data is finally developed.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to consider security for unknown systems by using the behavioral language. The motivation for “recovering” the true data is to ensure a safe trajectory required for the subsequent data-driven control. Without this, malicious data could lead to incorrect or even unsafe control actions. Therefore, our data recovery method acts as a safeguard for data-driven approaches, regardless of the specific learning or control algorithms employed.

III-A

Entry-attacked model

In the context of data-driven control, trajectories collected from the system directly influence controller design. Hence, ensuring reliability of the data is paramount. This paper thus focuses on the problem of secure trajectory reconstruction under data manipulation.

Let
\bar{w}\in(\mathbb{R}^{q})^{L}

Remark 1

As defined,

Performance analysis

To this end, let us first define the

Definition 1

\begin{cases}\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathscr{H}),\text{ if }|\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathscr{H})|<k,\\
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathscr{H})\text{ with }|\mathcal{C}|=k,\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

(13)

Let us consider any
\mI^{(i)}
such that
\mI^{(i)}\supseteq\mathcal{C}
. Then

\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{C}^{(i)}}=\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{C}},

where
\mathcal{C}^{(i)}
is defined in (
8
).
With respect to the two cases in (

\mathcal{B}^{(i)}\subseteq[qL]\backslash\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathscr{H})
, or
|\mathcal{B}^{(i)}|=qL-2k
. Therefore, there must exist some set
\mI^{(i)}
such that

\rank\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{B}^{(i)}}<\rank\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}}.

As a result, it is not difficult to verify

\nul\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{B}^{(i)}}\supsetneq\nul\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}}.

Hence, the adversary can always choose some
\tilde{g}\in\{\nul\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{B}^{(i)}}\}\backslash\{\nul\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}}\}

\begin{split}\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{B}^{(i)}}\tilde{g}&=0,\;\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{C}}\tilde{g}\neq 0.\end{split}

\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{B}^{(i)}}g=\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{B}^{(i)}}(\bar{g}+\tilde{g})=w|_{\mathcal{B}^{(i)}}.

The adversary thus modifies data at the manipulated entries as

w|_{\mathcal{C}}=\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{C}}g=\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{C}}(\bar{g}+\tilde{g})=\bar{w}|_{\mathcal{C}}+\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{C}}\tilde{g}\neq\bar{w}|_{\mathcal{C}}.

Namely, it changes
\bar{w}|_{\mathcal{C}}

.
We show that by doing so, it can deceive the system operator into believing in some false data

with
\tilde{w}\neq\bar{w}
. To see this, without loss of generality, we assume

\mathcal{I}^{(i)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{B}^{(i)}\\
\mathcal{C}\end{bmatrix}.

(15)

Therefore,

w|_{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}}=\begin{bmatrix}w|_{\mathcal{B}^{(i)}}\\
w|_{\mathcal{C}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{B}^{(i)}}\\
\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{C}}\end{bmatrix}g=\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}}g

\tilde{w}=\mH g=\begin{bmatrix}\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{C}}\\
\mH|_{[qL]\backslash\mathcal{C}}\end{bmatrix}g=\begin{bmatrix}w|_{\mathcal{C}}\\
\bar{w}|_{[qL]\backslash\mathcal{C}}\end{bmatrix}\neq\bar{w}.

∎

Combining the above theorems with Lemma

IV-B

Performance analysis

In contrast to the entry-attacked scenario as discussed in Section
III
, in Algorithm
2
, the rows to be removed in
\mathscr{H}
are determined by channels rather than individual entries. Moreover, to reconstruct the original trajectory, it is crucial to ensure that the matrix formed by the remaining rows has sufficient rank to represent the full space of the system
\mathscr{B}
. To address this, we introduce the following definitions, quantifying the rank of a matrix after deleting rows corresponding to specific channels:

Definition 2

(
(q,L)
-periodical set)
.

Given an index set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,q\}
, we define
\mathscr{C}^{q,L}(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\{1,\ldots,qL\}
as the
(q,L)
-periodical set of
\mathcal{C}
, such that

\mathscr{C}^{q,L}(\mathcal{S})\triangleq\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\mathscr{C}_{i}^{q,L},

where

\mathscr{C}_{i}^{q,L}\triangleq\{i+q\ell|\ell\in\mathbb{N},0\leq\ell\leq L-1\}.

(19)

Clearly, for any
j
in the
(q,L)
-periodical set
\mathscr{C}^{q,L}(\mathcal{S})
, there exists
i\in\mathcal{S}
such that
j\mod q=i
. Furthermore, we introduce a
(q,L)
-critical row set
for any matrix
A
, if removing the rows specified by this set results in a loss of rank for
A
.

Definition 3

(Minimum
(q,L)
-critical row set)
.

Consider any matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Let us define
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}(A)
as a
(q,L)
-critical row set for
A
, if removing all the rows specified in its
(q,L)
-periodical set
\mathscr{C}^{q,L}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}(A))
causes a rank loss for
A
. That is:

\rank A|_{[mL]/\mathscr{C}^{q,L}(\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}(A))}=\rank A-1.

Furthermore, the minimum
(q,L)
-critical row set is defined as a
(q,L)
-critical row set with the least cardinality, and is denoted as
\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{*}(A)
.

Remark 3
.

From Definitions
1
–
3
, for any matrix
A
, if
|\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{*}(A)|=\beta
, there exist
\beta L
rows such that removing them causes

A
to lose rank. Therefore,
|\mathcal{S}^{*}(A)|\leq\beta L
.

Next, we introduce the following condition on the Hankel matrix
\mathscr{H}_{L}(w_{d})
.

Condition 2
.

The cardinality of the minimum
(q,L)
-critical row set for the Hankel matrix
\mathscr{H}_{L}(w_{d})
is at least
2k+1
, i.e.,
|\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{*}(\mathscr{H}_{L}(w_{d}))|\geq 2k+1
.

Recall that the total number of channels is
q
. For any channel
i\in\{1,\ldots,q\}
, rows corresponding to the data from this channel are indexed as
\{i,i+q,\ldots,i+(L-1)q\}
, consistent with the definition of
\mathcal{C}_{i}^{q,L}
in (
19
). Therefore, Condition
2
requires that the removal of all rows corresponding to the data from
any

2k
channels will not result in rank loss for
\mathscr{H}_{L}(w_{d})
. This key aspect ensures the resilience of Algorithm
2
with respect to an unknown set of manipulated channels, as proved below:

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that
no more than
k
channels are malicious
, (
1
) is satisfied, and Condition
2
holds. When Algorithm
2
finds a solution
\tilde{w}
, it follows that
\tilde{w}=\bar{w}
. That is, the real trajectory is recovered.

Proof.

Let us denote
\mI_{o}^{(i^{*})}\triangleq\idx(v_{o}^{(i^{*})})
, namely, the index set of the selected channels in the
i
-th
subproblem
that solves (
17
). It is clear that
|\mI_{o}^{(i^{*})}|=q-k
. We further define

\mathcal{C}_{o}^{(i^{*})}\triangleq\mathcal{I}_{o}^{(i^{*})}\cap\mathcal{C},\;\mathcal{B}_{o}^{(i^{*})}\triangleq\mathcal{I}_{o}^{(i^{*})}\backslash\mathcal{C}_{o}^{(i^{*})}.

As assumed,
|\mathcal{C}|\leq k
.
We therefore conclude that
|\mathcal{B}_{o}^{(i^{*})}|=|\mI_{o}^{(i^{*})}|-|\mathcal{C}_{o}^{(i^{*})}|\geq q-2k
.
That is, for any subsystem, the number of benign selected channels is no less than

. Let

\mathcal{B}^{(i^{*})}\triangleq\mathscr{C}^{q,L}(\mathcal{B}_{o}^{(i^{*})}).

Under Condition
2
,
we conclude

\rank\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{B}^{(i^{*})}}=\rank\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{I}^{(i^{*})}}=\rank\mH.

(20)

The remainder of this proof follows a similar structure to that of Theorem
1
.
∎

Finally, we can prove that Condition
2
is tight.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that
no more than
k
channels are malicious
, (
1
) is satisfied, but Condition
2
does not hold. Even if Algorithm
2
yields a solution
\tilde{w}
, it is possible that

Proof.

The proof of this result follows similar reasoning to that in Proposition

\begin{cases}\mathcal{C}=\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{*}(\mathscr{H}),\text{ if }|\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}^{*}(\mathscr{H})|<k,\\

\mI_{o}^{(i)}

\mI_{o}^{(i)}\supseteq\mathcal{C}

and

Based on the above results, Condition

2k

\mH

, this condition is essential in working against the worst-case attacks.

V
Computationally-Efficient Solutions

The previous sections presented brute force algorithms for solving (
4
) and (
16
). Although these algorithms successfully reconstruct the true trajectory, their complexity grows exponentially with the size of data and the number of malicious entries or channels. In this section, we introduce convex approximations of (
4
) and (
16
). Moreover, we show conditions under which these approximations also yield the real trajectory.

V-A

Approximation solutions

Since a non-convex cardinality constraint is involved in (
4
), a natural way to relax it is using an
\ell_{1}
-norm
:

\begin{split}g^{*}=\argmin_{g}\quad&||w-\mathscr{H}_{L}\left(w_{d}\right)g||_{1}.\end{split}

(22)

Moreover, let us denote the recovered data as

w^{*}=\mathscr{H}_{L}(w_{d})g^{*}.

(23)

Notice that by imposing the
\ell_{1}
-norm, we aim to achieve a convex relaxation for the
0
-norm, specifically
||w-w^{*}||_{0}
. This encourages sparsity in
w-w^{*}
, which finds the best fit of the collected data at the most entries.

While (
22
) is an approximation of the original problem (
4
), we prove that under certain conditions, it is also able to exactly exclude the malicious behaviors and recover the real trajectory.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that (
1
) is satisfied. Problem (
22
) recovers the true trajectory. i.e.,
w^{*}=\bar{w}
, if for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{T-L+1}
and
v\neq 0
, it holds

||\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}v||_{1}>||\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}v||_{1},

(24)

where

\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}\triangleq\mathscr{H}|_{[qL]\backslash\mathcal{C}},\;\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}\triangleq\mathscr{H}|_{\mathcal{C}}.

(25)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let us assume

\mH=\begin{bmatrix}\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}\\
\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}\end{bmatrix}.

(26)

Correspondingly,
w
is partitioned as

w=\begin{bmatrix}w_{\text{B}}\\
w_{\text{F}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{w}_{\text{B}}\\
w_{\text{F}}\end{bmatrix},

(27)

where
w_{\text{B}}\triangleq w|_{[qL]\backslash\mathcal{C}},\;w_{\text{F}}\triangleq w|_{\mathcal{C}}
, and
\bar{w}_{\text{B}}\triangleq\bar{w}|_{[qL]\backslash\mathcal{C}}
. To ensure
w^{*}=\bar{w}
, it is sufficient to show that
\bar{g}
is the optimal solution of (
22
), i.e.,
g^{*}=\bar{g}
, where
\bar{g}
generates the true trajectory as defined in (
12
). This holds if and only if for any
v\neq 0
,

||w-\mathscr{H}(\bar{g}+v)||_{1}>||w-\mathscr{H}\bar{g}||_{1}.

(28)

Since
\bar{w}_{\text{B}}=\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}\bar{g}
, the above inequality is equivalent to

\left|\left|\begin{bmatrix}\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}v\\
w_{\text{F}}-\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}(\bar{g}+v)\end{bmatrix}\right|\right|_{1}>||w_{\text{F}}-\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}\bar{g}||_{1}.

(29)

Or, equivalently,

||\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}v||_{1}>||w_{\text{F}}-\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}\bar{g}||_{1}-||w_{\text{F}}-\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}(\bar{g}+v)||_{1}.

(30)

The above inequality follows under (
24
), which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4
.

The main difficulty in using Theorem
3
is to validate (
24
) over all nonzero
v
’s. In this remark, we provide a way to verify the condition. Let us define the following epigraphs:

\mathcal{E}(B)\triangleq\left\{v:||\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}v||_{1}\leq 1\right\},\;\mathcal{E}(F)\triangleq\left\{v:||\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}v||_{1}\leq 1\right\}

We then propose the following lemma:

Lemma 3
.

If there exists
\delta>0
such that

\mathcal{E}(B)\subseteq(1-\delta)\mathcal{E}(F),

(31)

then (
24
) holds.

Proof.

Let us consider any
v\neq 0
. Since (
31
) holds, there must exist
\alpha\neq 0
and
v_{0}=\alpha v
such that
v_{0}\in\mathcal{E}(F)\backslash\mathcal{E}(B)
. That is,
||\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}v_{0}||_{1}>1
and
||\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}v_{0}||_{1}\leq 1
. Therefore, it follows
||\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}v_{0}||_{1}>||\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}v_{0}||_{1}
. Then it is easy to check
||\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}v||_{1}=\alpha||\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}v_{0}||_{1}>\alpha||\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}v_{0}||_{1}=||\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}v||_{1}
.
∎

Therefore, it is sufficient to verify (
31
). Since
\mathcal{E}(B)
and
\mathcal{E}(F)
are both convex polytopes
[
29
, Chapter 3]
, we only need to check
whether all the vertices of
\mathcal{E}(B)
lie in the interior of
\mathcal{E}(F)
. The vertices of
\mathcal{E}(B)
can be found by solving the equation
||\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}\nu||_{1}=1
, which can be done using linear programming. If for every vertex
\nu
,
||\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}\nu||_{1}\leq 1
holds, then (
31
) is verified. Alternatively, one can also solve the following optimization problem

\begin{split}\max_{\nu}\quad&||\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}\nu||_{1},\\
\text{s.t. }\quad&||\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}\nu||_{1}=1.\end{split}

If the optimal value of the above problem is less than
1
, we are also able to validate (
31
) and thus (
24
).

Instead of (
24
), the following condition can also guarantee the effectiveness of (
22
).

Condition 3
.

\rank\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}=\rank\mathscr{H}
, where
\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}
is defined in (

).

That is, removing “malicious rows” does not harm the rank of
\mathscr{H}
.
Note that if Condition

does not hold, the benign data itself is not persistently exciting and therefore cannot fully determine the true solution set. This gives the adversary some degree of freedom to arbitrarily modify the data without being detected.

Under Condition
3
, there exists a matrix
T
such that

\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}=T\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}.

(32)

Suppose that (

||T||_{1}<1

From (

||\mathscr{H}_{\text{F}}v||_{1}=||T\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}v||_{1}\leq||T||_{1}||\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}v||_{1}<||\mathscr{H}_{\text{B}}v||_{1}

∎

Note also that no prior knowledge on the number of either false entries or channels is required for solving (

Remark 5
.

Many algorithms are available in the literature to solve the

) and the group LASSO problem (

[

. The computational complexities of these algorithms are typically polynomial with respective to the number of channels, the size of the Hankel matrix, and the length of the trajectory. In contrast, brute-force algorithms show exponential complexity. Therefore, (

V-B

Extension to noisy data

In the presence of noisy data, we propose an approximation solution by solving a bi-level optimization problem.

1.

First, solving (
22
) yields the optimal solution
g^{*}
.

2.

For each
w_{i}
, namely, the
i
-th entry of
w
, calculate the residual
|w_{i}-\mathscr{H}|_{\{i\}}g^{*}|
. Denote
\mathcal{C}^{*}
as the index set of the largest
k
residuals.

3.

Let
\mI^{*}\triangleq[qL]/\mathcal{C}^{*}
. We solve the following least square problem to find the recovered trajectory
\tilde{w}
:

\begin{split}\min_{g,\tilde{w}}\quad&||w|_{\mI^{*}}-\tilde{w}|_{\mI^{*}}||_{2}\\
s.t.\quad&\tilde{w}=\mathscr{H}_{L}\left(w_{d}\right)g.\end{split}

(34)

The above procedure finds the recovered trajectory when up to
k
entries of
w
are subject to manipulation. For the channel-attacked scenario, we can have a similar procedure by leveraging (
33
). The details are omitted in the interest of space. In Section
VI
, we will test the
effectiveness of these approximations through numerical examples.

VII
Conclusion

This paper addresses the problem of trajectory reconstruction under malicious behaviors. The system is unknown, and we develop direct data-driven approaches without identifying the explicit model. Both entry-attacked and channel-attacked scenarios are investigated. With the behavioral language, we describe the system using trajectories and formulate the problem as optimization problems for both scenarios. Sufficient conditions are presented to guarantee successful data reconstruction. Moreover, we develop approximation solutions to mitigate computation costs and handle noisy data. We further demonstrate that under certain conditions, these approximation solutions also accurately yield the true trajectory.

For the future works, it is possible to extend the results presented in the paper to nonlinear systems or time-varying systems. Note that the fundamental lemma has already been generalized to various classes of nonlinear systems, such as Hammerstein–Wiener systems
[
19
]
, second-order discrete Volterra systems
[
20
]
, flat nonlinear systems
[
21
]
, bilinear systems
[
23
]
, and time-varying systems
[
22
]
. These advancements may enable the achievement of CPS resiliency for more complex systems through direct data-driven methods.
