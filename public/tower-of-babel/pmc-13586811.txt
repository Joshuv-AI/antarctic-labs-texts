Fatal multi-organ failure following delayed diagnosis of ruptured pulmonary hydatid cyst in an 11-year-Old boy: The airway management dilemma (‘intubate or not intubate')
Authors: Zohreh Shalchi, Nasibeh Ghalandari, Bahman Shams Shafigh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydatid disease (cystic echinococcosis), caused by Echinococcus granulosus, is a zoonotic infection that can involve the lungs, particularly in endemic regions. We report an 11-year-old boy with a 4-month history of chronic cough following an upper respiratory infection. He presented acutely with recurrent vomiting, hemoptysis, severe respiratory distress, and hypoxemia (SpO2 82% on oxygen). Blood pressure was preserved and there were no clinical features of anaphylaxis or sepsis at arrival. Imaging confirmed a ruptured left pulmonary hydatid cyst with bronchial fistula; right-sided radiographic opacities were also present and likely reflected aspiration of cyst contents contributing to the initial severity. Emergency thoracotomy, cyst resection, and chest-tube drainage of an associated pneumothorax (reflecting bronchopleural fistula) were performed Imaging revealed a ruptured pulmonary hydatid cyst. He underwent emergency left thoracotomy with cyst resection and chest tube placement. Postoperatively, he developed acute kidney injury with oliguria, managed conservatively, along with ongoing respiratory complications. Albendazole therapy was initiated. This case highlights the diagnostic delays, airway management challenges, and high morbidity of complicated pulmonary hydatid disease in children.

Introduction

Cystic echinococcosis remains a significant public health problem in endemic areas such as the Middle East, including Iran, where children are frequently affected, with lungs being the most common site of involvement in children (often more prevalent than hepatic cysts in the pediatric population). Pulmonary hydatid cysts may be asymptomatic or present with chronic cough, chest pain, or hemoptysis. Spontaneous or traumatic rupture into the bronchial tree can lead to life-threatening complications, including massive hemoptysis, aspiration of cyst contents, anaphylaxis, chemical pneumonitis, and secondary bacterial infection [[1], [2], [3]].

Once rupture and bronchial fistulization have occurred, the clinical priorities shift. Progressive hypoxia, chemical pneumonitis, secondary infection, and multi-organ involvement can rapidly ensue if definitive management is delayed. Airway management remains nuanced: while intact cysts carry a risk of iatrogenic rupture and dissemination with positive-pressure ventilation, an already fistulized cyst changes the risk–benefit calculus, often favoring controlled intubation to protect the contralateral lung and reverse life-threatening hypoxemia [4,5].

We present a fatal case that illustrates how diagnostic delay in the presence of classic clinical clues, combined with prolonged hypoxia, can culminate in irreversible multi-organ failure. The case highlights the critical importance of early recognition and urgent intervention in endemic regions.

Case presentation

An 11-year-old boy presented to the emergency department with a 4-month history of chronic cough. He had been evaluated multiple times outpatient without chest radiography and discharged. On the day of admission, he developed recurrent vomiting (starting that morning) and lethargy. Her mother later described (after his intubation) how he talked about sudden surge of “salty clear water” to his throat. In the Emergency Department (ED), he had two episodes of bloody vomiting; differentiation between hematemesis and hemoptysis was difficult due to concurrent coughing. He developed respiratory distress, received oxygen therapy, and a chest X-ray (Fig. 1) was obtained, prompting immediate transfer to a higher-level center. On arrival, he was conscious, afebrile, but in significant respiratory distress with SpO2 of 82% despite reservoir bag oxygen. Blood pressure was preserved and there were no cutaneous, gastrointestinal, or hemodynamic features suggestive of anaphylaxis; likewise, there were no signs of sepsis. A surgical team was present. Intubation code was called, but the surgeon advised against it due to the risk of rupturing a suspected hydatid cyst. The main challenge was the differential diagnosis and that whether the abnormality seen in the chest radiography is an air bulla or hydatic cyst. Attempts to contact a thoracic surgeon for tracheostomy were unsuccessful. The patient was transferred for urgent computed tomography (CT), which demonstrated the classic water-lily sign confirming a ruptured left pulmonary hydatid cyst with bronchial communication; right-sided alveolar opacities consistent with aspiration of cyst contents were also noted (see Fig. 2) then to the PICU. On hospital day 4, he developed oliguria (0.4 mL/kg/h), rising creatinine, and hyperkalemia. Nephrology consultation recommended avoiding nephrotoxic drugs, frequent monitoring of BUN/Cr/electrolytes, and strict input/output tracking. Medications were adjusted based on his glomerular filtration rate, The following day, urine output improved to 1300 cc/24h (∼1.35 mL/kg/h), with gradual normalization of creatinine and potassium in next 3-4 days. He also developed coffee-ground NG aspirate.

The chest tube drains ∼600 cc of bloody fluid with bubbling. Despite intubation, he has recurrent desaturations, palpable crepitations, and requires ongoing ventilator support (specific settings as per last record). He shows decorticate posturing with noxious stimuli and has a GCS of 5. Due to prolonged hypoxia of brain, he had decorticated movements. The next day on attempt of NG tube replacement his saturation dropped to 53% suggesting tracheal tube displacement and upon replacement of tracheal tube he developed bradycardia and despite 45-min CPR, was expired.

Discussion

Pulmonary hydatid cysts in children frequently present with chronic respiratory symptoms, and delayed imaging—as occurred here—can lead to rupture and catastrophic complications. Rupture is associated with higher rates of postoperative complications (4–6%) including prolonged air leak, empyema, and pneumonia [6].

The central dilemma in this case was the pre-operative airway management decision (“intubate or not intubate”). When a large cystic lesion with possible bronchial fistula is suspected on chest X-ray, positive pressure ventilation or intubation carries a significant risk of further cyst rupture, dissemination of scolices, anaphylaxis, or flooding of the contralateral lung. Literature highlights cases of iatrogenic rupture triggered by intubation, leading to acute difficult airway and severe desaturation. Strategies such as careful one-lung ventilation, endobronchial blockers, or delaying intubation until the patient is in the operating room with surgical readiness have been advocated. In this patient, the diagnostic uncertainty (hydatid cyst vs. giant bulla) and surgeon's strong recommendation against intubation were driven by these risks, but the delay ultimately contributed to progressive hypoxia and neurological injury. Multidisciplinary input (surgery, anesthesia, intensivist) is essential in such scenarios [4,5,7,8].

Modified airway strategies such as selective one-lung ventilation using double-lumen tubes (in children >6–8 years) or endobronchial blockers, combined with endobronchial intubation of the healthy side, can mitigate the risk of contralateral contamination in cases with bronchial fistula [7,9,10]. In this 11-year-old patient, such techniques (e.g., right-sided double-lumen tube or blocker) could have been considered to safely secure the airway while protecting the right lung. But due to the limited resources this could not be thought of or reached for In our patient the delay imposed by diagnostic uncertainty allowed progressive hypoxia that ultimately produced irreversible neurological injury.

A recently published CHEST Pearl described a 14-year-old boy who similarly expectorated bitter-tasting fluid and developed tension pneumothorax from a ruptured hydatid cyst; prompt recognition and intervention led to a favorable outcome [11]. Our patient shared the pathognomonic fluid expectoration yet experienced prolonged diagnostic uncertainty and hypoxia, culminating in multi-organ failure and death. The contrast underscores that early recognition and definitive management are decisive determinants of outcome.
