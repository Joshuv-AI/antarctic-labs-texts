Primal-Dual Direct Preference Optimization for Constrained LLM Alignment
Yihan Du, Seo Taek Kong, R. Srikant

Abstract
The widespread application of Large Language Models (LLMs) imposes increasing demands on safety, such as reducing harmful content and fake information, and avoiding certain forbidden tokens due to rules and laws. While there have been several recent works studying safe alignment of LLMs, these works either require the training of reward and cost models and incur high memory and computational costs, or need prior knowledge about the optimal solution. Motivated by this fact, we study the problem of constrained alignment in LLMs, i.e., maximizing the output reward while restricting the cost due to potentially unsafe content to stay below a threshold. For this problem, we propose a novel primal-dual DPO approach, which first trains a model using standard DPO on reward preference data to provide reward information, and then adopts a rearranged Lagrangian DPO objective utilizing the provided reward information to fine-tune LLMs on cost preference data. Our approach significantly reduces memory and computational costs, and does not require extra prior knowledge. Moreover, we establish rigorous theoretical guarantees on the suboptimality and constraint violation of the output policy. We also extend our approach to an online data setting by incorporating exploration bonuses, which enables our approach to explore uncovered prompt-response space, and then provide theoretical results that get rid of the dependence on preference data coverage. Experimental results on the widely-used preference dataset PKU-SafeRLHF demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach.

Related Work

In this section, we review the most related work and defer a more detailed description to Appendix

.

With the rapid development of LLMs, the alignment of LLMs has received extensive attention.
RLHF

]
are the two main algorithmic frameworks for LLM alignment. RLHF first trains a reward model, and then applies an RL algorithm with the learned reward model to fine-tune LLMs. DPO does not explicitly train a reward model, but instead directly fine-tunes LLMs using preference data.

Recently, to reduce the harmful content generation of LLMs, there are several works studying safety alignment.

[
Dai et al.(2024)Dai, Pan, Sun, Ji, Xu, Liu, Wang, and Yang
]
proposed a safe RLHF framework. This framework trains a reward model and a cost model on reward and cost preference data, respectively, and then applies an RL algorithm, PPO
[
Schulman et al.(2017)Schulman, Wolski, Dhariwal, Radford, and
Klimov
]
, to maximize the Lagrangian function using the learned reward and cost functions.

[
Liu et al.(2024b)Liu, Sun, and Zheng
,
Huang et al.(2024)Huang, Li, Dobriban, Bastani, Hassani, and
Ding
,
Zhang et al.(2025)Zhang, Li, Hounie, Bastani, Ding, and
Ribeiro
]
used trained reward and cost models to regenerate preference data according to the Lagrangian function, and then performed DPO on regenerated data.
[
Wachi et al.(2024)Wachi, Tran, Sato, Tanabe, and
Akimoto
]
observed a relationship between the optimal solution of maximizing the Lagrangian function and that of maximizing the reward function, and designed a stepwise DPO approach leveraging this observation. However, their approach requires prior knowledge of the optimal Lagrange multiplier, and their theoretical results depend on the deviation between the used and optimal Lagrange multipliers, which can be unbounded.
[
Kim et al.(2025)Kim, Jang, Kim, Kim, Lee, Bae, and
Lee
]
reordered preference data if the preferred response
is unsafe and the not-preferred response is safe,
and ran DPO on reordered data. Their approach is inefficient in cost information learning.

In contrast to the above works, our approach does not require trained reward and cost models, which saves memory and computational costs, or prior knowledge of the optimal Lagrange multiplier. Moreover, we investigate the online data setting, and develop an exploratory primal-dual DPO approach which achieves theoretical results without the dependence on preference data coverage.

3
Preliminaries

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF).

The RLHF framework
[
Christiano et al.(2017)Christiano, Leike, Brown, Martic, Legg, and
Amodei
,
Ouyang et al.(2022)Ouyang, Wu, Jiang, Almeida, Wainwright, Mishkin,
Zhang, Agarwal, Slama, Ray, et al.
]
consists of three phases: (i) supervised fine-tuning a pre-trained LLM on a high-quality dataset of downstream tasks, e.g., dialogue and summarization, (ii) reward model learning, and (iii) RL optimization with the learned reward model.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
denote the sets of all possible prompts and responses. We define a policy
\pi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\triangle_{\mathcal{Y}}
as a mapping from
\mathcal{X}
to a distribution on
\mathcal{Y}
, where
\triangle_{\mathcal{Y}}
denotes the set of all distributions on
\mathcal{Y}
. We formulate an LLM as a policy, and use
\pi_{\textup{ref}}
to denote the supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model.

In the reward model learning phase, we have access to a reward preference dataset
\mathcal{D}^{r}=\{x^{r}_{i},y^{rw}_{i},y^{r\ell}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N^{r}}
, where
x^{r}_{i}
is a prompt,
y^{rw}_{i},y^{r\ell}_{i}
are preferred and dispreferred responses under prompt
x^{r}_{i}
, and the superscript
r
refers to reward preference. The generation of preference data is as follows: We assume that there exists an
unknown
reward function
r^{*}(x,y)\in[-R_{\max},R_{\max}]
for some constant
R_{\max}
, which models the helpfulness of response
y
under prompt
x
. Human annotators compare a pair of responses
y^{rw},y^{r\ell}
under prompt
x
. Then, we assume that the probability that
y^{rw}
is preferred to
y^{r\ell}
under prompt
x
follows the Bradley-Terry model
[
Bradley & Terry(1952)Bradley and Terry
]
:

\displaystyle\Pr\left[y^{rw}\succ y^{r\ell}|x\right]=\frac{\exp(r^{*}(x,y^{rw}))}{\exp(r^{*}(x,y^{rw}))+\exp(r^{*}(x,y^{r\ell}))}=\sigma\left(r^{*}(x,y^{rw})-r^{*}(x,y^{r\ell})\right),

(1)

where
\sigma(z):=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-z)}
denotes the sigmoid function.
With the preference data, we train a reward model
r
via maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), i.e., minimizing the negative log-likelihood loss:

\displaystyle\min_{r}\ -\frac{1}{N^{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{r}}\log\sigma\left(r(x^{r}_{i},y^{rw}_{i})-r(x^{r}_{i},y^{r\ell}_{i})\right).

(2)

In the RL optimization phase, we apply RL algorithms, e.g., PPO
[
Schulman et al.(2017)Schulman, Wolski, Dhariwal, Radford, and
Klimov
]
, to fine-tune the SFT model under the learned reward model
r
:

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{p}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}\left[r(x,y)\right]-\beta\cdot\textup{KL}\left(\pi(\cdot|x)\|\pi_{\textup{ref}}(\cdot|x)\right)\right].

(3)

Here
\beta
is a parameter controlling the deviation between the trained model
\pi
and SFT model
\pi_{\textup{ref}}
, since we do not want the trained model to be too far away from the SFT model.
\mathcal{D}^{p}
is a distribution of prompts, and the optimal solution to Eq. (
3
) is independent of
\mathcal{D}^{p}
, which will be presented in Eq. (
4
).

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

Recently,
[
Rafailov et al.(2023)Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell, Manning, Ermon, and
Finn
]
designed an direct preference optimization (DPO) approach, which bypasses the reward model training phase in RLHF, and directly fine-tunes LLMs using preference data. The derivation idea of DPO is as follows.

First, the optimal solution to Eq. (
3
) is
[
Peters & Schaal(2007)Peters and Schaal
,
Peng et al.(2019)Peng, Kumar, Zhang, and Levine
]

\displaystyle\pi^{*}_{r}(y|x)=\frac{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y|x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)}{Z_{r}(x)},

(4)

where
Z_{r}(x):=\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y^{\prime}|x)\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y^{\prime}))
is the partition function. Then, we can rewrite Eq. (
4
) to express the reward function
r
by the optimal policy
\pi^{*}_{r}
as

\displaystyle r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{r}(y|x)}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y|x)}+\beta\log Z_{r}(x).

(5)

Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
) hold for any reward function
r
.
Hence, the Bradley-Terry model in Eq. (
1
) can be expressed by the optimal policy
\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}
:

\displaystyle\Pr\left[y^{rw}\succ y^{r\ell}|x\right]=\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}(y^{rw}|x)}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y^{rw}|x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}(y^{r\ell}|x)}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y^{r\ell}|x)}\right),

(6)

where the partition function
Z_{r^{*}}(x)
is cancelled out. Now, by expressing the probability that preference data happen by
\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}
, we can replace the likelihood in the MLE training objective in Eq. (
2
) by Eq. (
6
), and obtain a new objective with the optimization variable directly being the policy:

\displaystyle\min_{\pi}\ -\frac{1}{N^{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{r}}\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y^{rw}_{i}|x^{r}_{i})}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y^{rw}_{i}|x^{r}_{i})}-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y^{r\ell}_{i}|x^{r}_{i})}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y^{r\ell}_{i}|x^{r}_{i})}\right).

(7)

Eq. (
7
) is the training objective of DPO. Thus, DPO directly uses preference data to fine-tune LLMs without training a reward model, and enjoys lower memory and computational costs than RLHF.

Safe RLHF.
To enhance safety in LLM alignment,
[
Dai et al.(2024)Dai, Pan, Sun, Ji, Xu, Liu, Wang, and Yang
]
proposed a safe RLHF framework. In safe RLHF, we assume that there exists an
unknown
cost function
c^{*}(x,y)\in[-C_{\max},C_{\max}]
for some constant
C_{\max}
, which characterizes the harmfulness of response
y
under prompt
x
. We have access to a cost preference dataset
\mathcal{D}^{c}=\{x^{c}_{i},y^{cw}_{i},y^{c\ell}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N^{c}}
, where
y^{cw}_{i},y^{c\ell}_{i}
denote unsafer and safer responses under prompt
x^{c}_{i}
(
y^{cw}_{i}
has a higher cost than
y^{c\ell}_{i}
), and the superscript
c
stands for cost preference. We assume that cost preference is generated according to the Bradley-Terry model with cost function
c^{*}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Pr\left[y^{cw}\succ y^{c\ell}|x\right]=\frac{\exp(c^{*}(x,y^{cw}))}{\exp(c^{*}(x,y^{cw}))+\exp(c^{*}(x,y^{c\ell}))}=\sigma\left(c^{*}(x,y^{cw})-c^{*}(x,y^{c\ell})\right).

(8)

Similar to Eq. (
2
), we can also train a cost model
c
via MLE:

\displaystyle\min_{c}\ -\frac{1}{N^{c}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{c}}\log\sigma\left(c(x^{c}_{i},y^{cw}_{i})-c(x^{c}_{i},y^{c\ell}_{i})\right).

(9)

To restrict the costs of LLM outputs within a threshold, we consider the constrained optimization:

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{p}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}\left[r(x,y)\right]-\beta\cdot\textup{KL}\left(\pi(\cdot|x)\|\pi_{\textup{ref}}(\cdot|x)\right)\right]

s.t.

\displaystyle c(x,y)\leq 0,\quad\forall x\sim\mathcal{D}^{p},y\sim\pi(\cdot|x).

Here for simplicity, we set the threshold of harmfulness to
0
.
The above problem is hard to solve using neural networks, since it requires the cost of every possible response
y
to stay below
0
.

To feasibly perform safety alignment, many prior works, e.g.,
[
Dai et al.(2024)Dai, Pan, Sun, Ji, Xu, Liu, Wang, and Yang
,
Wachi et al.(2024)Wachi, Tran, Sato, Tanabe, and
Akimoto
,
Liu et al.(2024b)Liu, Sun, and Zheng
,
Kim et al.(2025)Kim, Jang, Kim, Kim, Lee, Bae, and
Lee
]
, consider a relaxed optimization problem with an expected cost constraint, which we called
constrained alignment problem
:

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}\quad

\displaystyle f(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{p}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}\left[r^{*}(x,y)\right]-\beta\cdot\textup{KL}\left(\pi(\cdot|x)\|\pi_{\textup{ref}}(\cdot|x)\right)\right]

s.t.

\displaystyle g(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{p},y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}[c^{*}(x,y)]\leq 0.

(10)

In this work, we also study this relaxed problem.
Then, it is natural to look into the Lagrangian dual problem of the above constrained optimization:

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda\geq 0}\ \max_{\pi}\ L(\pi;\lambda):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{p}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}\left[r^{*}(x,y)\!-\!\lambda\cdot c^{*}(x,y)\right]\!-\!\beta\!\cdot\!\textup{KL}\left(\pi(\cdot|x)\|\pi_{\textup{ref}}(\cdot|x)\right)\right],

(11)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is a Lagrange multiplier. Throughout the paper, we call
L(\pi;\lambda)
the
Lagrangian function
.

With the above unconstrained problem,
[
Dai et al.(2024)Dai, Pan, Sun, Ji, Xu, Liu, Wang, and Yang
]
regarded
r-\lambda\cdot c
as a new reward function and applied an RL algorithm PPO
[
Schulman et al.(2017)Schulman, Wolski, Dhariwal, Radford, and
Klimov
]
to maximize
L(\pi;\lambda)
, and performed subgradient descent
[
Beck(2017)
]
to update
\lambda
. This safe RLHF framework requires to train both reward and cost models, which incurs high memory and computational costs.

4
Primal-Dual DPO Utilizing Standard DPO

In this section, we propose a provably efficient primal-dual DPO approach for the constrained alignment problem (Eq. (
10
)), utilizing a model trained using standard DPO on reward preference data to provide reward information.
We first describe the key idea behind our approach, and present the specific algorithm
\mathtt{PD\mbox{-}DPO}
which has rigorous theoretical guarantees.

4.1
Our Approach

First, we have that the optimal solution to
\max_{\pi}L(\pi;\lambda)
in Eq. (
11
) is

\displaystyle r(x,y)-\lambda\cdot c(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{r-\lambda\cdot c}(y|x)}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y|x)}+\beta\log Z_{r-\lambda\cdot c}(x),

(12)

where
Z_{r-\lambda\cdot c}(x):=\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y^{\prime}|x)\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}\left(r(x,y^{\prime})-\lambda\cdot c(x,y^{\prime})\right))
is the partition function, and
r
and
c
can be any reward and cost functions.

When one wants to apply the derivation idea of DPO in Eqs. (
6
) and (
7
), a difficulty arises:
We do not have preference data generated according to
r-\lambda\cdot c
, but only have preference data generated according to
r
and
c
separately. Thus, we cannot use
\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{r-\lambda\cdot c}(y|x)}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y|x)}
to directly express data likelihood as in Eq. (
7
), which means that the DPO derivation idea cannot be directly applied here.

To overcome this difficulty,
we first rearrange Eq. (
12
) as

\displaystyle c(x,y)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(r(x,y)-\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{r-\lambda\cdot c}(y|x)}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y|x)}-\beta\log Z_{r-\lambda\cdot c}(x)\right).

Plugging the above equation with
r^{*}
and
c^{*}
into Eq. (
8
), the generation of cost preference data can be rewritten as
\Pr[y^{cw}\succ y^{c\ell}|x]=

\displaystyle\sigma\Bigg(\frac{1}{\lambda}\bigg(r^{*}(x,y^{cw})-\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}-\lambda\cdot c^{*}}(y^{cw}|x)}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y^{cw}|x)}-\Big(r^{*}(x,y^{c\ell})-\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}-\lambda\cdot c^{*}}(y^{c\ell}|x)}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y^{c\ell}|x)}\Big)\bigg)\Bigg),

where
Z_{r^{*}-\lambda\cdot c^{*}}(x)
is cancelled out.
Then, replacing the cost preference data likelihood in Eq. (
9
) by the above equation, we can obtain a training objective with the optimization variable directly being the policy which is supposed to get close to
\pi^{*}_{r^{*}-\lambda\cdot c^{*}}
during training:

\displaystyle\min_{c}\ -\frac{1}{N^{c}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{c}}\log\sigma\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(r^{*}(x^{c}_{i},y^{cw}_{i})-\beta L_{\pi}(y^{cw}_{i}|x^{c}_{i})-\left(r^{*}(x^{c}_{i},y^{c\ell}_{i})-\beta L_{\pi}(y^{c\ell}_{i}|x^{c}_{i})\right)\right)\right),

(13)

where
L_{\pi}(y|x):=\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y|x)}
is the logarithmic ratio of response
y
under
x
between
\pi
and
\pi_{\textup{ref}}
.

Now the main challenge lies in that
we do not know
r^{*}
, and meanwhile, we do not want to explicitly train a reward model in order to keep memory and computational efficiency. To handle this challenge, we make an observation that
r^{*}(x^{c}_{i},y^{cw}_{i})-r^{*}(x^{c}_{i},y^{c\ell}_{i})
can be expressed by
\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}(y^{cw}_{i}|x^{c}_{i})}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y^{cw}_{i}|x^{c}_{i})}-\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}(y^{c\ell}_{i}|x^{c}_{i})}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y^{c\ell}_{i}|x^{c}_{i})}
according to Eq. (
4
).
Then,
\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}
is what we can learn by training a model using standard DPO on reward preference data.

Therefore, using this observation, the training objective Eq. (
13
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\min_{c}\ -\frac{1}{N^{c}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{c}}\log\sigma\left(\frac{\beta}{\lambda}\left(L_{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}}(y^{cw}_{i}|x^{c}_{i})\!-\!L_{\pi}(y^{cw}_{i}|x^{c}_{i})-\left(L_{\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}}(y^{c\ell}_{i}|x^{c}_{i})\!-\!L_{\pi}(y^{c\ell}_{i}|x^{c}_{i})\right)\right)\right),

(14)

where
\pi^{*}_{r^{*}}
can be learned by first training a model using standard DPO on reward preference data.

Eq. (
14
) is the main idea of our primal-dual DPO approach utilizing standard DPO. This approach avoids training reward and cost models, which significantly reduces memory and computational costs compared to prior works
[
Dai et al.(2024)Dai, Pan, Sun, Ji, Xu, Liu, Wang, and Yang
,
Liu et al.(2024b)Liu, Sun, and Zheng
,
Huang et al.(2024)Huang, Li, Dobriban, Bastani, Hassani, and
Ding
,
Zhang et al.(2025)Zhang, Li, Hounie, Bastani, Ding, and
Ribeiro
]
. Our approach shows even more advantages when there already exists a trained model on reward preference data, which is often the case since there are many high-quality and open-source LLMs, e.g., LLaMA
[
Dubey et al.(2024)Dubey, Jauhri, Pandey, Kadian, Al-Dahle, Letman,
Mathur, Schelten, Yang, Fan, et al.
]
and Gemma
[
Team et al.(2024a)Team, Mesnard, Hardin, Dadashi,
Bhupatiraju, Pathak, Sifre, Rivière, Kale, Love, et al.
,
Team et al.(2024b)Team, Riviere, Pathak, Sessa, Hardin,
Bhupatiraju, Hussenot, Mesnard, Shahriari, Ramé, et al.
,
Team et al.(2025)Team, Kamath, Ferret, Pathak, Vieillard, Merhej,
Perrin, Matejovicova, Ramé, Rivière, et al.
]
.

4.2
A Provably Efficient Algorithm
\mathtt{PD\mbox{-}DPO}

While Eq. (
14
) has presented the main idea of our primal-dual DPO approach, to enable rigorous theoretical guarantees, we develop a specific provably efficient algorithm
\mathtt{PD\mbox{-}DPO}
, which imposes policy search constraints based on Eq. (
14
) and enjoys suboptimality and constraint violation guarantees.
Before describing the specific algorithm
\mathtt{PD\mbox{-}DPO}
, we first introduce several assumptions.

Assumption 1

(Slater’s Condition)
.

There exists a policy
\bar{\pi}
which satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{p},y\sim\bar{\pi}(\cdot|x)}[c^{*}(x,y)]<0
.
In addition, we know a constant
\rho\geq\frac{f(\pi^{*})-f(\bar{\pi})}{-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{p},y\sim\bar{\pi}(\cdot|x)}[c^{*}(x,y)]}
.

This assumption is common in the constrained optimization and learning literature
[
Beck(2017)
,
Efroni et al.(2020)Efroni, Mannor, and Pirotta
]
. In practice, it is reasonable that there exists a safe policy model, e.g., a language model which refuses to answer harmful questions albeit less helpful.

Following prior works
[
Dai et al.(2024)Dai, Pan, Sun, Ji, Xu, Liu, Wang, and Yang
,
Kim et al.(2025)Kim, Jang, Kim, Kim, Lee, Bae, and
Lee
]
, we also allow querying cost binary feedback from human annotators, which indicates whether a response
y
is safe under prompt
x
. Such cost binary feedback is generated according to

\displaystyle\Pr\left[Z(y)=1|x\right]=\sigma\left(c^{*}(x,y)\right)

and
\Pr[Z(y)=0|x]=1-\sigma\left(c^{*}(x,y)\right)
, where

5
Exploratory Primal-Dual DPO with Exploration Bonuses

The result of algorithm
\mathtt{PD\mbox{-}DPO}
(Theorem
1
) depends on the coverage of preference data, i.e.,
\left\|\phi(x,y)\right\|_{(\Sigma_{\mathcal{D}^{\diamond}}+\gamma I)^{-1}}
.
If the given preference data do not have sufficient coverage, the suboptimality and constraint violation of
\mathtt{PD\mbox{-}DPO}
can be unbounded.

To resolve this coverage issue, we further investigate an online setting where collecting preference data online is allowed. In this setting, we develop an exploratory primal-dual DPO algorithm
\mathtt{O\mbox{-}PD\mbox{-}DPO}
, which incorporates exploration bonuses
b^{c}_{k}(x,y)
and
b^{r}_{k}(x,y)
in the rearranged Lagrangian DPO and standard DPO objectives. In algorithm
\mathtt{O\mbox{-}PD\mbox{-}DPO}
, the trained policy has an incentive to explore uncovered prompt-response space, and
gradually expands the used preference data. We defer the pseudo-code and detailed description of
\mathtt{O\mbox{-}PD\mbox{-}DPO}
to Appendix
D
due to space limits.

We take the incorporation of
b^{r}_{k}
in standard DPO as an example to explain the
intuition behind why including exploration bonuses can encourage exploration
. For the standard DPO objective (Eq. (
15

\mathtt{O\mbox{-}PD\mbox{-}DPO}
will subtract a

term from the original
\beta\log\frac{\pi(y^{rw}|x)}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y^{rw}|x)}
term.
When preference data do not cover
(x,y^{rw})
well,
b^{r}(x,y^{rw})
will be large. Then, subtracting a large value from
\beta\log\frac{\pi(y^{rw}|x)}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y^{rw}|x)}
encourages
\pi
to put a higher probability on
y^{rw}
to maintain the original value of
\beta\log\frac{\pi(y^{rw}|x)}{\pi_{\textup{ref}}(y^{rw}|x)}
which achieves the optimal value of the MLE objective function.
Thus, by incorporating exploration bonuses in the training objective, the trained policy is incentivized to explore uncovered prompt-response space.

While there have been several recent works studying DPO with exploration
[
Liu et al.(2024a)Liu, Lu, Zhang, Liu, Guo, Yang,
Blanchet, and Wang
,
Xie et al.(2025)Xie, Foster, Krishnamurthy, Rosset, Awadallah, and
Rakhlin

Cen et al.(2025)Cen, Mei, Goshvadi, Dai, Yang, Yang, Schuurmans, Chi,
and Dai
]
, their ideas cannot be directly applied to our work, since their approaches perform optimistic exploration mainly for the optimal policy which maximizes a certain reward function, but cannot deal with the optimism for the optimal policy of a constrained optimization. To handle the exploration for the constrained alignment problem, we adopt exploration bonuses in our rearranged Lagrangian DPO training objective, and develop new analysis to get rid of offline dataset dependence. This design of DPO with bonuses and its analysis are novel to the RLHF literature.

Now we provide the suboptimality and constraint violation guarantees of algorithm

7
Conclusion

In this work, we study the constrained alignment problem for LLMs, which aims to maximize the reward while constraining the cost to stay below a threshold. We develop a novel primal-dual DPO approach for the offline and online data settings. Our approach adopts a rearranged Lagrangian DPO training objective, utilizing the reward information provided by a model trained using standard DPO. We establish suboptimality and constraint violation guarantees, and provide experimental results on the PKU-SafeRLHF dataset
[
Dai et al.(2024)Dai, Pan, Sun, Ji, Xu, Liu, Wang, and Yang
]
to validate the effectiveness of our approach.

There are several interesting directions for future work. One direction is to extend our theoretical results to the policy parameterization (large prompt-response space) setting. The challenge is that under popular policy parameterization settings such as the log-linear policy class, the constrained alignment problem can be non-convex with respect to the policy parameter. Another direction is to investigate stricter cost constraints, e.g., per-response constraints, which is challenging to tackle using neural networks.

This paper studies the alignment of LLMs to enhance safety or impose certain constraints. The data used in experiments may contain harmful or offensive content.

.

[
Dai et al.(2024)Dai, Pan, Sun, Ji, Xu, Liu, Wang, and Yang
]
proposed a safe RLHF framework, which considers maximizing the reward while restricting the cost to be no larger than a threshold. Their approach first trained a reward model and a cost model on reward and cost preference data, respectively, and then applied an RL algorithm, PPO
[
Schulman et al.(2017)Schulman, Wolski, Dhariwal, Radford, and
Klimov
]
, to maximize the Lagrangian function constituted by the learned reward and cost functions.

[
Liu et al.(2024b)Liu, Sun, and Zheng
]
regenerated preference data according to the Bradley-Terry model
[
Bradley & Terry(1952)Bradley and Terry
]
with the Lagrangian function using trained reward and cost models, and then performed the standard DPO algorithm
[
Rafailov et al.(2023)Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell, Manning, Ermon, and
Finn
]
on these regenerated data.
[
Wachi et al.(2024)Wachi, Tran, Sato, Tanabe, and
Akimoto
]
observed a relationship between the optimal solution of maximizing the Lagrangian function and that of maximizing the reward function, and then applied DPO combined with this observation. The algorithm in
[
Wachi et al.(2024)Wachi, Tran, Sato, Tanabe, and
Akimoto
]
requires prior knowledge of the optimal Lagrangian multiplier, and their theoretical results depend on the deviation between the used and optimal Lagrange multipliers, which can be unbounded.
[
Kim et al.(2025)Kim, Jang, Kim, Kim, Lee, Bae, and
Lee
]
reordered preference data if the preferred response (in terms of helpfulness) is unsafe and the dispreferred response is safe, and then ran DPO on these reordered data. Their algorithm is inefficient in cost information learning.
[
Huang et al.(2024)Huang, Li, Dobriban, Bastani, Hassani, and
Ding
,
Zhang et al.(2025)Zhang, Li, Hounie, Bastani, Ding, and
Ribeiro
]
first learned the optimal Lagrangian multiplier, and then maximized the Lagrangian function under the learned optimal Lagrangian multiplier. These two works also require trained reward and cost models.

There are also other works related to safety or constrained alignment of LLMs
[

,

,

,

]
.
[
Zhou et al.(2023)Zhou, Liu, Yang, Shao, Liu, Yue, Ouyang, and
Qiao
]
designed a multi-objective DPO approach.
[
Ji et al.(2024)Ji, Chen, Lou, Hong, Zhang, Pan, Qiu, Dai, and
Yang
,
Yang et al.(2024)Yang, Liu, Xie, Huang, Zhang, and
Ananiadou
]
developed simple single-objective and multi-objective alignment approaches, respectively, but their algorithms need corrected responses or a multi-objective reward model.

[
Qi et al.(2025)Qi, Lyu, Yang, Wang, Bai, and Cui
]
proposed a mixture of experts (MoE) framework for safety-helpfulness preference optimization.

Unlike prior works
[
Dai et al.(2024)Dai, Pan, Sun, Ji, Xu, Liu, Wang, and Yang
,
Liu et al.(2024b)Liu, Sun, and Zheng
,
Huang et al.(2024)Huang, Li, Dobriban, Bastani, Hassani, and

,
Zhou et al.(2023)Zhou, Liu, Yang, Shao, Liu, Yue, Ouyang, and

,
Yang et al.(2024)Yang, Liu, Xie, Huang, Zhang, and

,

\displaystyle=O\Bigg(\frac{C_{\max}}{\rho\sqrt{K}}\left(\frac{\left(\lambda_{1}-2\rho\right)^{2}}{\lambda_{1}}+\lambda_{1}\right)+C_{\max}\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(\frac{K}{\delta}\right)}{N^{\textup{CE}}}}+W\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(\frac{|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|N^{\textup{CE}}K}{\delta}\right)}{M^{\textup{CE}}}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\frac{\left(\exp\left(C_{\max}\right)+\exp\left(-C_{\max}\right)+2\right)^{2}}{N^{\textup{on}}}\left(|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|+\log\left(\frac{K}{\delta}\right)\right)+\gamma^{\textup{on}}(C_{\max})^{2}}\cdot

\displaystyle\quad\sqrt{|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|K\left(\log\left(\frac{\gamma^{\textup{on}}+|\mathcal{D}^{r}_{1}|+K}{|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|\gamma^{\textup{on}}}\right)+\frac{1}{C^{\textup{base}}}\log\left(\frac{\gamma^{\textup{on}}+|\mathcal{D}^{r}_{1}|+C^{\textup{base}}K}{|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|\gamma^{\textup{on}}}\right)\right)}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\rho}\sqrt{\frac{\left(\exp\left(R_{\max}\right)+\exp\left(-R_{\max}\right)+2\right)^{2}}{N^{\textup{on}}}\left(|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|+\log\left(\frac{K}{\delta}\right)\right)+\gamma^{\textup{on}}(R_{\max})^{2}}\cdot

\displaystyle\quad\sqrt{|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|K\left(\log\left(\frac{\gamma^{\textup{on}}+|\mathcal{D}^{r}_{1}|+K}{|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|\gamma^{\textup{on}}}\right)+\frac{1}{C^{\textup{base}}}\log\left(\frac{\gamma^{\textup{on}}+|\mathcal{D}^{r}_{1}|+C^{\textup{base}}K}{|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|\gamma^{\textup{on}}}\right)\right)}\Bigg).

∎

Appendix F
Technical Tools

In this section, we introduce several technical tools which are used in our analysis.

Lemma 8

(Theorem 8.42 in
[
Beck(2017)
]
)
.

For any
\lambda\geq 0
such that
q(\lambda)\leq u
,

\displaystyle\lambda\leq\frac{u-f(\bar{\pi})}{-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{p},y\sim\bar{\pi}(\cdot|x)}[c^{*}(x,y)]}.

Proof.

For any
\lambda\geq 0
such that
q(\lambda)\leq u
, we have

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\geq q(\lambda)\geq f(\bar{\pi})-\lambda\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{p},y\sim\bar{\pi}(\cdot|x)}[c^{*}(x,y)].

Hence,

\displaystyle-\lambda\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{p},y\sim\bar{\pi}(\cdot|x)}[c^{*}(x,y)]\leq u-f(\bar{\pi}).

Since
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{p},y\sim\bar{\pi}(\cdot|x)}[c^{*}(x,y)]<0
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda\leq\frac{u-f(\bar{\pi})}{-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{p},y\sim\bar{\pi}(\cdot|x)}[c^{*}(x,y)]}.

∎

Let
\Lambda^{*}
be the set of the optimal solutions to the dual problem
\min_{\lambda\geq 0}q(\lambda)
.

Corollary 1

(Corollary 8.43 in
[
Beck(2017)
]
)
.

For any
\lambda^{*}\in\Lambda^{*}
,

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}\leq\frac{f(\pi^{*})-f(\bar{\pi})}{-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{p},y\sim\bar{\pi}(\cdot|x)}[c^{*}(x,y)]}\leq\rho,

where the second inequality comes from the definition of
\rho
.

Proof.

This corollary can be obtained by setting
u
as the optimal value to the dual problem
\min_{\lambda\geq 0}q(\lambda)=f(\pi^{*})
in Lemma
8
.
∎

Define
g(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}^{p},y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}[c(x,y)]
.
Let

\displaystyle v(u)

\displaystyle:=\max_{\pi}\left\{f(\pi):g(\pi)\leq u\right\},

\displaystyle C(u)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\pi:g(\pi)\leq u\right\}.

Lemma 9

(Theorem 3.59 in
[
Beck(2017)
]
)
.

For any
\lambda^{*}\in\Lambda^{*}
,

\displaystyle v(0)+\lambda^{*}u\geq v(u).

Proof.

For any
\pi
, we have

\displaystyle f(\pi)-\lambda^{*}g(\pi)\leq\max_{\pi}\left(f(\pi)-\lambda^{*}g(\pi)\right)=q(\lambda^{*})=f(\pi^{*})=v(0).

Thus, for any
u\in\mathbb{R}
and
\pi\in C(u)
,

\displaystyle v(0)+\lambda^{*}u\geq f(\pi)-\lambda^{*}\left(g(\pi)-u\right)\geq f(\pi).

Since the above inequality holds for all
\pi\in C(u)
, by maximizing
f(\pi)
over
\pi\in C(u)
, we have that for any
u\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle v(0)+\lambda^{*}u\geq v(u).

∎

Lemma 10

(Theorem 3.60 in
[
Beck(2017)
]
)
.

If a policy
\tilde{\pi}
satisfies that

\displaystyle f(\pi^{*})-f(\tilde{\pi})+\rho^{\prime}[g(\tilde{\pi})]_{+}\leq L,

where
L>0
and
\rho^{\prime}\geq 2\lambda^{*}
, then

\displaystyle[g(\tilde{\pi})]_{+}\leq\frac{2L}{\rho^{\prime}}.

Proof.

From Lemma
9
, we have that for any
u\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle v(0)-v(u)\geq-\lambda^{*}u.

Let
\tilde{u}:=[g(\tilde{\pi})]_{+}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\left(\rho^{\prime}-\lambda^{*}\right)\tilde{u}

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{\prime}\tilde{u}+v(0)-v(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{(a)}}{\leq}f(\pi^{*})-f(\tilde{\pi})+\rho^{\prime}\tilde{u}

\displaystyle\leq L,

where inequality (a) uses the fact that
v(0)=f(\pi^{*})
and
v(\tilde{u})\geq f(\tilde{\pi})
.

Since
\rho^{\prime}\geq 2\lambda^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{u}\leq\frac{L}{\rho^{\prime}-\lambda^{*}}\leq\frac{L}{\rho^{\prime}-\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{2}}=\frac{2L}{\rho^{\prime}}.

∎

Lemma 11
.

Let
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{K}
be a sequence of
d
-dimensional random vectors following distributions
\mathcal{B}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{B}_{K}
, respectively, and we have
\|\psi_{k}\|\leq L
for any
k\geq 1
. Let
A_{0}
be a
d\times d
positive definite matrix such that
\sigma_{\min}(A_{0})\geq\{1,L^{2}\}
, and define
A_{k}=A_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{i}\sim\mathcal{B}_{i}}[\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{\top}]
for any
k\geq 1
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{k}\sim\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left[\|\psi_{k}\|^{2}_{(A_{k-1})^{-1}}\right]\leq 2\log\frac{\det(A_{K})}{\det(A_{0})}\leq 2\log\left(\frac{\left(\frac{\textup{trace}(A_{0})+KL^{2}}{d}\right)^{d}}{\det\left(A_{0}\right)}\right).

Proof.

This proof uses a similar analytical procedure as Lemma 11 in
[
Abbasi-Yadkori et al.(2011)Abbasi-Yadkori, Pál, and
Szepesvári
]
.

We have

\displaystyle\det\left(A_{K}\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left(A_{K-1}+\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{K}\sim\mathcal{B}_{K}}\left[\psi_{K}\psi_{K}^{\top}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left(A_{K-1}\right)\det\left(I+\left(A_{K-1}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{K}\sim\mathcal{B}_{K}}\left[\psi_{K}\psi_{K}^{\top}\right]\left(A_{K-1}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left(A_{K-1}\right)\det\left(I+\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{K}\sim\mathcal{B}_{K}}\left[\left(A_{K-1}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\psi_{K}\left(\left(A_{K-1}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\psi_{K}\right)^{\top}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left(A_{K-1}\right)\left(1+\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{K}\sim\mathcal{B}_{K}}\left[\|\psi_{K}\|^{2}_{(A_{K-1})^{-1}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\det\left(A_{0}\right)\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left(1+\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{k}\sim\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left[\|\psi_{k}\|^{2}_{(A_{k-1})^{-1}}\right]\right).

Taking logarithm on both sides, we have

\displaystyle\log\det\left(A_{K}\right)=\log\det\left(A_{0}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\left(1+\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{k}\sim\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left[\|\psi_{k}\|^{2}_{(A_{k-1})^{-1}}\right]\right).

Since
\sigma_{\min}(A_{0})\geq\{1,L^{2}\}
, we have
\|\psi_{k}\|^{2}_{(A_{k-1})^{-1}}\leq 1
for any
k\geq 1
. Using the fact that
x\leq 2\log(1+x)
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{k}\sim\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left[\|\psi_{k}\|^{2}_{(A_{k-1})^{-1}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\left(1+\mathbb{E}_{\psi_{k}\sim\mathcal{B}_{k}}\left[\|\psi_{k}\|^{2}_{(A_{k-1})^{-1}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=2\log\frac{\det\left(A_{K}\right)}{\det\left(A_{0}\right)}

\displaystyle\overset{\textup{(a)}}{\leq}2\log\left(\frac{\left(\frac{\textup{trace}(A_{0})+KL^{2}}{d}\right)^{d}}{\det\left(A_{0}\right)}\right),

where inequality (a) uses the AM-GM inequality.
∎

Lemma 12

(Lemma H.3 in
[
Agarwal et al.(2020)Agarwal, Henaff, Kakade, and Sun
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a distribution of
d
-dimensional vectors which satisfies that
\|\psi\|\leq L
if
\psi\sim\mathcal{B}
. Let
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{M}
be
M
i.i.d. samples from
\mathcal{B}
, and define
A=\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim\mathcal{B}}[\psi\psi^{\top}]
. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have that for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\left|v^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{\top}-A\right)v\right|\leq\frac{2L^{2}\ln\left(\frac{8\hat{d}}{\delta}\right)}{3M}+L^{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\ln\left(\frac{8\hat{d}}{\delta}\right)}{M}},

where
\hat{d}:=\frac{\textup{trace}(A)}{\|A\|}
is the intrinsic dimension of
A
.

Proof.

For any
i\geq 1
, let
D_{i}:=\psi_{i}\psi_{i}^{\top}-A
. Then, we have that
\mathbb{E}[D_{i}]=0
,
\|D_{i}\|\leq L^{2}
, and

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}[(D_{i})^{2}]\right\|\leq ML^{4}.

The intrinsic dimension of
\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}[(D_{i})^{2}]
is equal to that of
A
, which is
\hat{d}
by definition.

Using the Matrix Bernstein inequality (Theorem 7.7.1 in
[
Tropp et al.(2015)
]
), we have that for any
t\geq L^{2}\sqrt{M}+\frac{L^{2}}{3}
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\sigma_{\max}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}[(D_{i})^{2}]\right)\geq t\right]\leq 4\hat{d}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}{L^{4}M+\frac{L^{2}t}{3}}\right).

Setting
t^{\prime}=\frac{t}{M}
, we have that for any
t^{\prime}\geq\frac{L^{2}}{\sqrt{M}}+\frac{L^{2}}{3M}
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\sigma_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}[(D_{i})^{2}]\right)\geq t^{\prime}\right]\leq 4\hat{d}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{-\frac{M(t^{\prime})^{2}}{2}}{L^{4}+\frac{L^{2}t^{\prime}}{3}}\right).

When
t^{\prime}=\frac{2L^{2}\ln\left(\frac{4\hat{d}}{\delta}\right)}{3M}+L^{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\ln\left(\frac{4\hat{d}}{\delta}\right)}{M}}
, we have
4\hat{d}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{-\frac{M(t^{\prime})^{2}}{2}}{L^{4}+\frac{L^{2}t^{\prime}}{3}}\right)\leq\delta
.

Hence, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}[(D_{i})^{2}]\right)\leq\frac{2L^{2}\ln\left(\frac{4\hat{d}}{\delta}\right)}{3M}+L^{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\ln\left(\frac{4\hat{d}}{\delta}\right)}{M}}.

We can obtain this concentration inequality in the other direction with a similar argument. Therefore, we complete the proof of this lemma.
∎

Lemma 13

(Lemma H.4 in
[
Agarwal et al.(2020)Agarwal, Henaff, Kakade, and Sun
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{B}_{K}
be
K
distributions of
d
-dimensional vectors. For any
i\in[K]
, we draw
M
i.i.d. samples
\psi_{i,1},\dots,\psi_{i,M}
from
\mathcal{B}_{i}
, and form
\hat{A}_{i}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\psi_{i,j}\psi_{i,j}^{\top}
. Define
A_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim\mathcal{B}_{i}}[\psi\psi^{\top}]
,
A=\sum_{i=1}^{K}A_{i}+\gamma I
, and
\hat{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\hat{A}_{i}+\gamma I
. Setting
M:=\frac{32K^{2}\ln(\frac{8K\tilde{d}}{\delta})}{\gamma^{2}}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have that for any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}v^{\top}(A+\gamma I)^{-1}v\leq v^{\top}(\hat{A}+\gamma I)^{-1}v\leq 2v^{\top}(A+\gamma I)^{-1}v,

where
\tilde{d}:=\max_{i\in[K]}\frac{\textup{trace}(A_{i})}{\|A_{i}\|}
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha(M)=:\frac{2L^{2}\ln\left(\frac{8K\hat{d}}{\delta}\right)}{3M}+L^{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\ln\left(\frac{8K\hat{d}}{\delta}\right)}{M}}
. Using Lemma
12
, we have that with probability
1-\delta
, for any
i\in[K]
,

\displaystyle A_{i}+\alpha(M)I+\frac{\gamma}{K}I\succeq\hat{A}_{i}+\frac{\gamma}{K}I\succeq A_{i}-\alpha(M)I+\frac{\gamma}{K}I.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle A+K\alpha(M)I+\gamma I\succeq\hat{A}+\gamma I\succeq A-K\alpha(M)I+\gamma I.

When
\gamma\geq 2K\alpha(M)
, the above inequality implies

\displaystyle\left(A+K\alpha(M)I+\gamma I\right)^{-1}\preceq\left(\hat{A}+\gamma I\right)^{-1}\preceq\left(A-K\alpha(M)I+\gamma I\right)^{-1}.

Let
U\Lambda U^{T}
be the eigendecomposition of
A
, where
\Lambda=\textup{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{d})
and
U=[u_{1},\dots,u_{d}]
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle v^{\top}(\hat{A}+\gamma I)^{-1}v-v^{\top}(A+\gamma I)^{-1}v

\displaystyle\leq v^{\top}\left(\left(A-K\alpha(M)I+\gamma I\right)^{-1}-(A+\gamma I)^{-1}\right)v

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\left(\sigma_{i}+\gamma-K\alpha(M)\right)^{-1}-\left(\sigma_{i}+\gamma\right)^{-1}\right)(v^{\top}u_{i})^{2}.

For any
i\in[d]
, since
\sigma_{i}\geq 0
, we have
\sigma_{i}+\gamma\geq 2K\alpha(M)
, and then
2(\sigma_{i}+\gamma-K\alpha(M))\geq\sigma_{i}+\gamma
, which implies
(\sigma_{i}+\gamma-K\alpha(M))^{-1}\leq 2(\sigma_{i}+\gamma)^{-1}
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle v^{\top}(\hat{A}+\gamma I)^{-1}v-v^{\top}(A+\gamma I)^{-1}v\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sigma_{i}+\gamma\right)^{-1}(v^{\top}u_{i})^{2}=v^{\top}(A+\gamma I)^{-1}v.

Using a similar analysis, we can obtain the statement in the other direction.
∎
