Ayurvedic management of Non-Sjögren's Dry Eye (Shushkakshipaka) as a preparation for cataract surgery—a case report
Authors: Sushma Naranappa Salethoor, Anil Radhakrishnan, Shyamasundaran Kulangara, Ramya Shruthi Janardhanan, Rammanohar Puthiyedath, Reshma NS
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This case report describes Shushkakshipaka (Non-Sjögren's Aqueous Deficient Dry Eye – Non-Sjögren's ADDE) in a patient unfit for cataract surgery due to compromised ocular surface integrity. A 73-year-old woman presented with ocular irritation, foreign body sensation, and blurred distant vision for three months. She had a six-year history of type II diabetes mellitus and hypertension, controlled. Evaluation revealed Grade 2 Nuclear Sclerotic cataract (NS) and Posterior Subcapsular cataract (PSC) (Left > Right) with Punctate Keratopathy, making immediate bilateral cataract surgery unsuitable. Schirmer's test I showed right eye <1 mm and left eye <1 mm, and tear break-up time (TBUT) was right eye (4 s) and left eye (6 s). She was diagnosed with moderate-to-severe non-Sjögren's ADDE and cataract. During ophthalmic evaluation, the patient was diagnosed with punctate keratopathy and dry eye symptoms. She was prescribed artificial tears and omega-3 fatty acid supplements. Cataract surgery was deferred, as the patient was unfit for surgery. Owing to inconsistent treatment adherence, conditions showed minimal improvement at the time of presentation to the Ayurveda hospital. She was diagnosed as Vata-Pitta Pradhana Shushkakshipaka (predominance of Vata and Pitta Doshas; in Ayurveda, Vata, Pitta and Kapha are collectively called Tridosha, the three regulatory functional factors of the body). Treatment had three phases. The first focused on systemic intervention, with Deepana (metabolism-enhancing) and Pachana (digesting), followed by Mrdu Shodhana (mild therapeutic bio-cleansing) and Marsha Nasya (high-dose errhine therapy). Following systemic cleansing and attainment of Niramavastha (a state free from accumulated metabolites achieved by either therapeutic measures or the effect of time), the second phase focused on local ocular management through Netra Kriyakalpa (localised ocular therapeutic procedures). The third involved follow-up and maintenance therapy for long-term ocular stability. Oral Ayurvedic polyherbal formulations, dietary modifications, and lifestyle changes were administered throughout the two-month Ayurveda treatment period. After two months, parameters improved, with Schirmer's values of right eye 5 mm and left eye 7 mm and TBUT of right eye 8 s and left eye 14 s, making her fit for cataract surgery. Within the Ayurvedic framework, these changes may be viewed as consistent with improved Agni (digestive/metabolic factors) and supportive of subsequent Kriyakalpa; links with ocular surface integrity or the gut-eye connection remain hypothetical. This case warrants further investigation in selected cataract surgery candidates with severe, non-responsive dry eye.

Introduction

The Lancet Commission for Global Surgery estimates that about 11% of the global burden of diseases requires surgical management. Among them, cataract surgery is the most commonly performed operation, especially in the elderly population (1). Exacerbation of pre-existing dry eye symptoms is common in patients who undergo cataract surgery. Proper evaluation of dry eye to determine the severity and aggressive management are undertaken before surgery to deter complications like corneal ulcers. Yet, in severe dry eye, which is one of the ocular surface diseases, patients fail to respond to conventional lubricants due to multiple factors, and such patients are unable to undergo cataract surgery (2). Optimisation of the ocular surface before cataract surgery remains a clinical challenge, particularly when refractory cases respond inadequately to conventional lubricant-based care. Such scenarios highlight the need to document and evaluate individualised Ayurvedic management strategies, particularly in cases where systemic symptoms are addressed alongside ocular treatment. Dry eye manifests as aqueous-deficient type, caused by inadequate tear production due to conditions like Sjögren's syndrome, lacrimal gland dysfunction, obstruction, or systemic drugs. Evaporative dry eye, on the other hand, results from increased tear film evaporation, often due to meibomian gland dysfunction, despite normal tear production (3). Sjögren's disease (SjD; formerly Sjögren's syndrome) is an autoimmune disorder affecting the salivary and lacrimal glands, leading to dry mouth and dry eye symptoms (4). In contrast, non-Sjögren's (sicca syndrome) has a heterogeneous, non-autoimmune aetiology (5). This non-Sjögren's syndrome-related dry eye is an aqueous-deficient subtype characterised by decreased tear production and a Schirmer's test score of less than 5 mm, without the presence of Sjögren's syndrome (6).

This case report presents a case resulting from lacrimal dysfunction, the most common cause of which is age-related lacrimal gland deficiency. Emerging biomedical evidence suggests an association between inflammation, gut dysbiosis, and several disease conditions, and non-Sjögren's aqueous deficiency dry eye is no exception (7). There is also a prominent school of thought advocating the utilisation of omega-3 fatty acids in dry eye treatment, purportedly acting by modulating the gut microbiome (8). Dry eyes cause reduced tear film volume, hyperosmolarity and inflammation in the ocular surface and are among the most common reasons for a visit to an ophthalmology clinic (9). Around the world, it is estimated that between 5% and 34% of people have some form of dry eye, and prevalence significantly increases with age (10). Symptoms improve with treatment, but the condition tends to be long-standing. It is a chronic and potentially sight-threatening condition that occurs due to the dysfunction of the lacrimal glands (11). Emerging evidence suggests that Western dietary patterns, characterised by high intake of refined carbohydrates, saturated fats, and meat, may adversely influence gut microbiome diversity, gut dysbiosis, and inflammatory status (12).

An Ayurvedic approach addressing both digestive and metabolic factors and ocular manifestations may offer a potential management strategy for eye diseases and warrants further investigation. According to the classical Ayurveda text Ashtanga Hridayam, the Samprapti (pathogenesis) of Netra Roga (eye disease) is the consequence of deranged Dosha (regulatory functional factors of the body) from Koshta (viscera) reaching various parts of Akshi (eye) through Urdhwagata Siras (any tubular vessels of the body towards the head) (13). In Charaka Samhita, while discussing the condition of Udavarta [upward movement of Vayu (air element) and retention of stool and urine], it is mentioned that Netra Roga can manifest as one of its Upadrava (complications) (14), thereby indicating a potential gut-eye relationship. The Pitta Dosha (the Dosha responsible for regulating body temperature and metabolic activities) and Agni (digestive/metabolic factors) play an influential role in Netra Roga due to Guna Samanya Sambandha (similarity at the level of the qualities). The eyes are primarily governed by Alochaka Pitta (one of the five subtypes of Pitta responsible for vision and perception), while Pachaka Pitta (located between the stomach and large intestine) regulates the function of all other Pitta subtypes (15). Also, deranged digestive/metabolic regulation is the root cause of various diseases, especially of the gut. Thus, correcting the underlying factors of the pathogenesis, which takes place in the gut, and then/simultaneously treating the eye with local Kriyakalpa after this renders better relief in ocular conditions as well. This case report highlights an approach focusing on both the gut and the eye while managing non-Sjögren's ADDE (Aqueous Deficient Dry Eye), emphasising the relevance of addressing gut imbalance along with ocular treatments rather than only treating the eye. As a result of this, the patient was able to undergo cataract surgery after Ayurvedic intervention.

Patient information

De-identified patient data

A 73-year-old female, native of and living in Kerala, presented at our hospital in Kollam, Kerala, India, seeking Ayurvedic treatment for her dry eye complaints.

Patient concerns and symptoms

The patient presented with symptoms of ocular irritation, foreign body sensation, burning sensation and blurring of distant vision in both eyes and approached the Department of Ophthalmology of a biomedical hospital, where she was found to be suffering from bilateral grade 2 nuclear sclerotic cataract (NS) along with posterior subcapsular cataract (PSC), which had progressed more in the left eye than the right, and was advised to undergo cataract surgery. During the pre-operative examination, it was found that the patient was suffering from severe dry eye, with corneal staining revealing punctate keratopathies with mucus debris. Hence, the scheduled surgery was cancelled due to a poor surgical prognosis in ocular surface disease.

She sought Ayurveda treatment because of the persistence of ocular irritation, foreign body sensation, burning sensation and blurring of distant vision in both eyes associated with constipation and tiredness over the past three months despite the biomedical treatment. She also wanted to reduce her dependency on eye lubricants and reported that her cataract surgery was postponed due to the persistence of symptoms related to dry eye and reduced ocular surface index.

Medical, family, psychosocial history

The patient is a known case of type II diabetes mellitus and grade 1 diastolic dysfunction and has been hypertensive for six years. She was under Tab. Metformin 500 mg for diabetes mellitus and Tab. Losartan 50 mg for hypertension for the past six years. She occasionally used Tab. Neurobion for vitamin B-complex supplementation. The patient had a hysterectomy done at the age of 37.

Past interventions and outcomes

She was prescribed Optive® [Carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) sodium 0.5%] eye drops: 1 drop in each eye 4–6 times daily for 3 months and Omega-3 fatty acid: 1,000 mg/day oral supplements and was advised to revisit for an evaluation after the completed course. After three months, the patient's condition did not adequately improve due to inconsistent adherence to the advised treatment with the drops and oral supplements, and she approached Ayurvedic management of her condition.

Clinical findings

During the first consultation at the biomedical hospital (on 25th July, 2022)

The patient's visual acuity was measured as 6/60 in both eyes, with pinhole correction to 6/6. The prescription for glasses was +2.75 Ds for the right eye and +3.00 Ds for the left eye, both corrected to 6/6, with an additional +2.75 Ds for near vision in both eyes (N8). Non-contact tonometry recorded intraocular pressures of 19 mmHg in the right eye and 16 mmHg in the left eye. Keratometry readings were 43.25/43.50 at 80/170 with a cylindrical correction of −0.25*80 for the right eye and 43.50/44.25 at 85/175 with a cylindrical correction of −0.75*85 for the left eye. Lens examination indicated grade 2 NS with an immature senile cataract (IMSC) in both eyes. Meibography revealed tortuous but normal glands. Schirmer's test I showed the values in the right eye 2 mm and the left eye 3 mm. Slit lamp examination with corneal staining revealed punctate keratopathies with mucus debris. The diagnosis was based on these clinical observations, and a confirmatory meibography was conducted at the biomedical hospital to exclude meibomian gland dysfunction.

Clinical findings at ayurveda hospital (on 27th October, 2022)

A slit lamp examination showed a tear film break-up time of 4 s for the right eye and 6 s for the left eye. Schirmer's test I results were <1 mm for the right eye and <1 mm for the left eye. No clinically evident punctate keratopathy was observed on examination at our hospital. Mild congestion was observed in both bulbar conjunctivae, along with concretions in the palpebral conjunctivae of the lower lid of both eyes. Senile arcus was present. Diagnostic challenges

There were diagnostic challenges encountered, especially during the documentation for this case report, as only the required and routine tests for clinical evaluation were done and it was not rigorous research-level documentation. However, the biomedical diagnosis was already established by a biomedical ophthalmologist. From the symptoms of the patients, examination of the patient and diagnostic features, the Ayurveda diagnosis of Vata-Pitta Pradhana Shushkakshipaka (Shushkakshipaka with a predominance of Vata and Pitta Doshas) was arrived at.

Diagnosis

Biomedical diagnosis

Although tear film instability was evident from the reduced TBUT, there were no clinical findings suggestive of meibomian gland dysfunction or other evaporative mechanisms to support a diagnosis of evaporative or mixed dry eye. Furthermore, in the absence of clinical features of Sjögren's syndrome, the case was classified as non-Sjögren's aqueous-deficient dry eye. Old age and other systemic illnesses which are present in this case also affirm the diagnosis (7). Thus, this clinical presentation and the clinical findings pointed to the case as severe non-Sjögren's aqueous deficiency dry eye (16).

Ayurvedic diagnosis

Initial diagnostic considerations included Krichronmeelana (17), a Vartmagata Roga (eyelid disorder), based on the presence of symptoms consistent with Pamshu Purna Netra (foreign body sensation) and Krichronmeelana (difficulty in eyelid opening). However, a critical diagnostic symptom for Krichronmeelana, symptomatic and momentary relief upon ocular rubbing, was notably absent in this case. Conversely, Shushkakshipaka does not typically present with this feature. The absence of symptomatic relief upon ocular rubbing in our patient led to the exclusion of Krichronmeelana as a diagnosis.

Upon thorough evaluation and comparison with the aforementioned conditions, we have identified Gharsha (foreign body sensation), Toda (pricking pain), Upadeha (mucous debris), Avila Darshana (blurring of vision), Ruksha Daruna Vartma (crusting of lids), and Sheeteccha (desire for cold things) as prominent symptoms indicating aggravated Vata. Additionally, Paka (inflammation) and Daha (burning sensation) suggest aggravated Pitta. Consequently, the condition is considered Vata-Pittaja Sushkakshipaka (18), which is classified as a Bheshaja Sadya Vyadhi (classified in Ayurveda as a condition treatable with oral medications and localised treatments) (19).

Prognosis

Owing to the chronicity, ADDE can lead to ocular surface complications like punctate keratopathy, which can potentially threaten vision. Additionally, ageing, female sex, and diabetes form the most common risk factors for ADDE. In this case, the predisposition of risk factors and inadequate adherence might be a contributing factor to reduced treatment outcomes of the standard-of-care intervention. Judicious employment of topical lubricants, anti-inflammatory agents, secretagogues, and autologous serum are generally utilised along with systemic therapy, including immunomodulators, in the treatment of dry eye disease. Long-term treatment for ADDE is imperative to prevent keratopathy and blindness (20, 21).

Therapeutic intervention

Phases of therapeutic intervention

The therapeutic intervention was planned in three phases (22, 23), the first focusing on correcting the gut, which included the treatments starting from Deepana (metabolism-enhancing), Pachana (digesting), and Mrdu (mild bio-cleansing), both Urdhwa and Adho Shodhana (bio-cleansing from both upper and lower parts of the body).

The second phase of treatment focused on local ocular management, starting from Lekhana (cleansing) to Brmhana (nourishment), which was the line of treatment chosen for the management of this condition.

In the third phase, as a follow-up, maintenance and strengthening, Anulomana (mild purgation) was done. Specific eye treatments for Prasadana (quality-enhancing) and Rasayana (revitalising) were also administered for the maintenance and strengthening.

Considering the age, the digestive capacity and the tolerance capacity of the patient to take up Ayurvedic treatment, we intended to do only a Mrdu Shodhana and hence fixed a mild dose.

Changes in the therapeutic intervention

The treatment approach was dynamically adjusted based on the patient's response throughout the course. Initially, oral medications were administered to prepare the body by addressing Ama Pachana (therapeutic digestion of accumulated metabolites) before proceeding with Vicharana Snehapana (a method of co-administration of unctuous substances in various forms). Following Snehapana, the patient received Abhyanga for a day and was followed by Mrdu Virechana to correct the digestive/metabolic dysregulation. Subsequently, specific eye treatments, including Marsha Nasya, Anjana, Kshalana (irrigation), Seka, Ashchyotana (instillation of liquid medicaments drop by drop in the eye), Vidalaka (application of medicated paste on the outer part of eye), Tarpana (optimal soothing collyrium), and Talam (herbal paste or medicated oil to the bregma/crown of the head), were done.

Follow-up and outcomes

Clinician and patient-assessed outcomes

Clinician-assessed outcomes

During the patient's follow-up consultation after three months of outpatient treatment at the Department of Ophthalmology of the previously visited hospital, notable improvements were observed in dry eye condition without significant improvement in cataract-related visual acuity. Schirmer's test results showed increased tear production, with readings of 5 mm for the right eye and 7 mm for the left eye, which was previously noted to be 2 mm in the right eye and 3 mm in the left eye. Additionally, tear break-up time (TBUT) demonstrated improvement, measuring 6 s in the right eye and 8 s in the left eye, with no evidence of punctate keratopathy on ocular surface examination.

Patient-assessed outcomes

An immediate outcome that the patient could appreciate was a reduction in the irritation, foreign body sensation and burning sensation. The OSDI score after the Ayurvedic treatment reduced to 10 from 50, which comes within the normal reference range. The patient also reported clarity in vision post-cataract surgery.

Relevant follow-up investigations and diagnostic findings

At the biomedical hospital (22nd March 2023): The patient's visual acuity was evaluated, revealing corrected vision of 6/60 (blurred) in both the right eye and left eye, improving to 6/6 (blurred) with pinhole correction. The right eye had a prescription of +2.75 Ds, while the left eye had a prescription of +3.00 Ds. Near vision correction was set at +2.75 Ds N8 for both eyes. Non-contact tonometry readings indicated intraocular pressures of 15 mmHg for the right eye and 13 mmHg for the left eye.

Keratometry readings were 43.75/43.75 at 180/90 for the right eye and 44.25/45.00 at 85/175 with a cylindrical correction of −0.75*85 for the left eye. A slit lamp examination revealed grade 2 nuclear sclerosis with posterior subcapsular cataract in both eyes. Schirmer's test recorded 5 mm in the right eye and 7 mm in the left eye. Dilated fundus examination showed a Cup-to-Disc Ratio (CDR) of 0.4 for the right eye and 0.6 for the left eye, with the rest of the fundus within normal limits. Due to the improvement in the ocular surface integrity, the patient was scheduled for cataract surgery.

Ayurvedic intervention adherence and tolerability

Although the patient reported strict adherence to the treatment regimen, treatment tolerance was assessed throughout the intervention. The patient was interrogated several times to find out if she encountered any difficulty in tolerating the Ayurvedic treatment (both internal medications and external therapies). The patient reported experiencing no discomfort throughout the treatment and informed that she tolerated it well.

Adverse and unanticipated events

No adverse or unanticipated events were reported by the patient or identified by the treating physician throughout the course of the Ayurveda treatment.

Discussion

The TFOS Dry Eye Workshop II (DEWS II, 2017) (24) defines dry eye as a multifactorial disease of the ocular surface characterised by a loss of tear film homeostasis, in which tear film instability, hyperosmolarity, ocular surface inflammation and damage, and neurosensory abnormalities play central etiological roles. Building on this concept, the TFOS DEWS III (2025) report (25) redefines dry eye as a multifactorial, symptomatic disease resulting from symptomatic homeostatic failure of the tear film and/or ocular surface. The updated framework emphasises driver-based aetiologies, including eyelid abnormalities, lacrimal dysfunction, neural impairment, and disturbances in the ocular surface microenvironment, recognising that alterations in cellular metabolism, immune regulation, and nerve function collectively contribute to disease pathophysiology.

To improve the consistency of dye eye diagnosis, TFOS DEWS III (2025) recommends a standardised two-level diagnostic algorithm. Level 1 focuses on identifying dry eye through symptom assessment and evaluation of tear film and ocular surface function. In the present case, this framework was adopted to provide a comprehensive assessment of disease severity and its underlying drivers. Symptom screening was performed using the OSDI, which evaluates the functional impact of dry eye symptoms, their frequency, and environmental triggers. Tear film instability, a hallmark feature of dry eye irrespective of its aetiology, was assessed using the TBUT test. Ocular surface staining was performed to detect epithelial damage and devitalised tissue, providing objective evidence of ocular surface compromise. Schirmer I testing was used to quantify basal and reflex aqueous tear production and to identify aqueous tear deficiency. Together, these validated assessments enabled a comprehensive evaluation of symptoms, tear film homeostasis, ocular surface integrity, and lacrimal gland function in accordance with the TFOS DEWS III (2025) diagnostic recommendations (26).

The principal clinical observation in this case was an improvement in both patient-reported symptoms and selected ocular-surface parameters following the multimodal Ayurvedic treatment. The OSDI score decreased from 50 to 10, while improvements were also observed in Schirmer's test I and TBUT. Improvement in ocular-surface integrity subsequently allowed the patient to proceed with cataract surgery. Nevertheless, because the intervention consisted of several systemic and local treatments administered sequentially, the contribution of any individual treatment component cannot be determined. In the present patient, markedly reduced Schirmer's values, shortened TBUT, punctate keratopathy and the absence of substantial meibomian gland abnormality were consistent with a predominantly aqueous-deficient phenotype. This clinical pattern is also consistent with published guidance on the diagnosis of ADDE (21).

ADDE results from inadequate aqueous tear production by the lacrimal functional unit and may occur in association with Sjögren's syndrome or through non-Sjögren causes such as ageing, systemic disease, medication use, lacrimal gland dysfunction, or environmental factors (21). Older age, female sex and diabetes were relevant predisposing factors in this patient. Previous studies have also shown that patients with non-Sjögren sicca may experience a symptom burden comparable to that of Sjögren-associated disease, even when the clinical signs differ in severity (5, 6). This emphasises the importance of evaluating patient-reported outcomes together with objective ocular-surface findings.

A notable patient-reported outcome in this case was the reduction in OSDI score from 50 to 10, corresponding to a 40-point absolute reduction and a shift from severe symptoms to the normal range. The reported minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the OSDI is approximately 7.0–9.9 points across dry eye severity categories and 7.3–13.4 points among patients with severe symptoms at baseline (27). Thus, the improvement observed in this patient substantially exceeded the upper estimate of the MCID for severe dry eye. For context, the multicentre randomised DREAM trial reported mean OSDI reductions of 13.9 points in the omega-3 group and 12.5 points in the placebo group over 12 months (28), whereas a study of platelet-rich plasma eye drops reported a mean reduction of 8.6 points (29). Although the magnitude observed in the present case was numerically greater, these findings cannot be compared directly because of differences in study populations, interventions, follow-up periods, and study designs. Furthermore, as this is a single case involving multiple sequential interventions, the improvement should be interpreted as a marked and clinically meaningful patient-reported response temporally associated with the overall treatment protocol, including systemic and local ocular surface treatment, rather than evidence of efficacy or causality attributable to any individual component.

With regard to mechanistic studies, exploring the gut and eye connection, particularly in the context of Ayurveda, remains scarce. Until now, there is one case report and four clinical trials published on dry eye cases in Ayurveda but these papers have not evaluated the gut and ocular microbiome changes (30–35). This case report highlights the possibility of managing non-Sjögren's aqueous deficiency dry eye that is inadequately responsive to conventional biomedical interventions through an Ayurvedic treatment protocol focused on both systemic and ocular management, thereby improving ocular health. Experimental and observational studies suggest that intestinal dysbiosis may influence ocular-surface inflammation through systemic immune and metabolic pathways (18, 36–38) This provides biological plausibility for investigating a gut–eye relationship in dry eye. However, in this case, the gut microbiota composition, circulating inflammatory markers and ocular-surface inflammatory biomarkers were not evaluated. Consequently, this case cannot establish that the systemic treatment modified the gut microbiota or that such modification mediated the ocular improvement. The proposed gut–eye relationship should therefore remain a hypothesis for future investigation. The clinical presentation of lacrimal gland dysfunction-related non-Sjögren's aqueous deficiency dry eye matches the textual description of Vata-Pittaja Shukshakshipaka. The sequential treatment protocol comprised an initial systemic phase directed towards digestive and metabolic correction, followed by local ocular procedures and maintenance therapy. The classical rationale for the individual interventions and their sequence has been summarised in the intervention tables. These Ayurvedic constructs explain the clinical reasoning used to select the treatment; however, they should not be regarded as direct equivalents of lacrimal gland dysfunction, ocular-surface inflammation or other biomedical mechanisms. A clinically relevant observation from the sequential protocol-wise treatment administration was that ocular parameters remained largely unchanged during the initial gut-focused phase of treatment. Following the initiation of targeted ocular therapy in the subsequent phase, improvement was noted in both patient-reported symptoms and clinician-assessed findings. This temporal pattern, though, suggests an important contribution from the local treatment phase; however, it does not demonstrate that the systemic intervention restored the gut microbiota or facilitated the subsequent ocular response but only points to a possibility of an underlying mechanism working in the background. Loss of tear-film homeostasis, tear hyperosmolarity and ocular-surface inflammation are well-established mechanisms in dry eye disease according to TFOS DEWS II. By contrast, the gut–eye axis remains an emerging area of investigation.

Strengths

The biomedical diagnosis was established by a biomedical ophthalmologist. This case documents the potential role of Ayurvedic treatment in addressing non-Sjögren's ADDE, which was inadequately responsive to standard treatment in conventional medicine, and it is, to our knowledge, among the early documented case reports being treated, with an approach focusing on both the systemic and the local ocular intervention managing non-Sjögren's ADDE, emphasising the relevance of addressing gut imbalance along with ocular treatments rather than only treating the eye.

Future research directions

Future studies should test the hypothesis underlying this case report, based on the Ayurvedic principle of Roga Marga (pathways involved in disease manifestation and progression), wherein treatment targets both systemic and local factors implicated in dry eye.

There are preclinical studies and observational human studies that suggest possible associations between intestinal microbial composition, microbial metabolites and ocular-surface immune regulation, and from the observations of this particular case. However, it is not yet established that modification of the gut microbiota improves non-Sjögren's ADDE. Thus, studies in this direction will help in establishing these findings. Hence, future controlled studies should evaluate this sequential treatment approach using standardised OSDI assessment, tear osmolarity, ocular-surface staining, Schirmer's test I, TBUT and tear inflammatory biomarkers. If the gut–eye hypothesis is specifically investigated, paired gut and ocular microbiome assessments should be conducted before and after treatment.

The primary takeaway lessons of this case report

This case reports clinical improvement in severe, non-responsive dry eye following Mrdu Shodhana and subsequent Kriyakalpa in a cataract surgery candidate. Within the Ayurvedic framework, the observations may be viewed as consistent with favourable changes in Agni and Bhrmhana Karma; however, these interpretations remain hypothesis-generating and do not establish causality. The findings should therefore be understood as a single-case clinical observation rather than evidence of treatment efficacy.
