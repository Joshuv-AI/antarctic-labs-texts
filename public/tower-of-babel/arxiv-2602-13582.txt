Sean Eberhard, Luca Sabatini

Abstract
. We study how the spectral gap and diameter of Cayley graphs depend strongly on the choice of generating set. We answer a question of Pyber and Szabó (2013) by exhibiting a sequence of finite groups G n G_{n} with | G n | → ∞ |G_{n}|\to\infty admitting bounded generating sets X n , Y n X_{n},Y_{n} such that Cay ⁡ ( G n , X n ) \operatorname{Cay}(G_{n},X_{n}) is an expander while Cay ⁡ ( G n , Y n ) \operatorname{Cay}(G_{n},Y_{n}) has super-polylogarithmic diameter. The construction uses the semidirect product G n = C p n − 1 ⋊ S n G_{n}=C_{p}^{n-1}\rtimes S_{n} with p p exponentially large in n n , and the analysis reduces to bounding some exponential sums of permutational type.

1.
Introduction

Let
G
be an infinite group generated by a finite set
X
.
Several important properties of the Cayley graph
\operatorname{Cay}(G,X)
do not depend on the choice of
X
,
and are actually group properties:
the growth type, the number of ends, hyperbolicity, amenability (via the Følner condition), and property (T).
The key observation behind these facts is that, for any other finite subset
Y
of
G
,
there exists a constant
c
such that every element in
Y
can be written as the product of at most
c
elements in
X
.

The situation is different for a sequence of finite groups.
Answering a question of Lubotzky–Weiss
[
10
]
, Alon, Lubotzky, and Wigderson
[
1
]

constructed finite groups whose Cayley graphs are expanders with respect to one bounded-size generating set but not with respect to another.
Specifically, they showed that the groups
C_{2}^{p+1}\rtimes\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(p)
have this property.
In the seminal paper
[
9
]
, Kassabov showed that the symmetric groups also have this property,
and subsequently Kassabov, Lubotzky, and Nikolov
[
8
]
proved that almost all nonabelian finite simple groups are expanders with respect to suitable bounded-size generating sets.

However, all of the groups listed above likely have worst-case polylogarithmic diameter.
In the case of the finite simple groups, this is the content of the famous conjecture of Babai
[
2
]
*Conjecture 1.7.
This observation prompted Pyber and Szabó to ask the following question:

Question 1.1

(Pyber & Szabó
[
11
]
*Question 23)
.

Let
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of finite groups and suppose that

\operatorname{Cay}(G_{n},X_{n})
are expander graphs for some sets
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of bounded cardinality.
Is it true that for every sequence of generating sets
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
we have

\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{Cay}(G_{n},Y_{n}))\>\leq\>C(\log|G_{n}|)^{C}

for some constant
C
?

A similar question was asked by the second author at the 2024 Oberwolfach meeting
Growth and Expansion in Groups

[
3
]
*p. 1028, Question 5:
there it was asked whether it is enough to assume that
\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{Cay}(G_{n},X_{n}))
is polylogarithmic, i.e., whether polylogarithmic diameter is a group property.

In this note we answer both questions negatively. In fact we show that the diameter of
G
with respect to the second generating set can be as large as
\exp(c\sqrt{\log|G|})
.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exist constants
\delta,\varepsilon>0
and a sequence of finite groups
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
with
|G_{n}|\to\infty

with bounded-size generating sets
X_{n},Y_{n}\subseteq G_{n}
such that

(1)

\operatorname{Cay}(G_{n},X_{n})
are
\varepsilon
-expander graphs, and

(2)

\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{Cay}(G_{n},Y_{n}))\geq\exp(\delta\sqrt{\log|G_{n}|})
.

By contrast having polynomially large diameter
is
a group property,
and such groups are called
almost flat
: see
[
5
]
*Theorem 4.1, Corollary 4.16.
In particular, if (a) holds then
\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{Cay}(G_{n},Y_{n}))\leq|G_{n}|^{o(1)}
,
and it is an interesting open question to find the optimal bound in (b).

We now describe the construction.
Notably, there are elements in common with both Alon–Lubotzky–Wigderson and Kassabov.
From now on we suppress the subscript
n
, which will be clear from context.
The group is just

G\>=\>V_{0}\rtimes S_{n},

where
V_{0}<V=\mathbf{F}_{p}^{n}
is the deleted permutation module of dimension
n-1
,
and
p\sim e^{cn}
is a large prime.
Note that

\>|G|\>=\>p^{n-1}n!\>=\>\exp{\left(cn^{2}+O(n\log n)\right)}.

The two generating sets
X
and
Y
both have the form
\{v\}\cup T
where
v\in V_{0}
and
T\subset S_{n}
.
For
Y
we make the unimaginative choice

v=(1,-1,0,\dots,0),\qquad T=\{(1,2),(1,2,\dots,n)\}.

Clearly
\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{Cay}(G,Y))\geq\left\lfloor{pn/2}\right\rfloor\geq p\sim e^{cn}
, so (b) holds.

The nontrivial part of the proof is the demonstration that there is a choice of
v
and
T
such that
\operatorname{Cay}(G,X)
has a uniform spectral gap.
For
T
we take a bounded-size expanding generating set for
S_{n}
(which exists by the result of Kassabov).
By an analysis similar to that in
[
1
]
,
the problem of finding
v\in V_{0}
reduces to the following “permutational exponential sum” estimate.

For
v,w\in V=\mathbf{F}_{p}^{n}
, let
\langle{v,w}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}w_{i}
.
Let
\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1)
denote the all-one vector, so that
V_{0}=\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
,
and let
e_{p}(x):=\exp(2\pi ix/p)
.

Theorem 1.3
.

There exist constants
\delta,\varepsilon>0
such that the following holds.
For every integer
n\geq 1
and prime
p\leq e^{\delta n}
,
there exists
v\in V_{0}
such that, for all nonconstant
w\in V
,

\left|\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}e_{p}{\left(\langle{v,w^{\sigma}}\rangle\right)}\right|\>\leq\>1-\varepsilon.

In fact we show that a random
v\in V_{0}
works.
When
p
is bounded, this can be proved by a straightforward modification of the analysis in
[
1
]
.
The main novelty in our result is that
p
is allowed to be enormous:
in technical terms, the reason we can take
p
exponential in
n
is that we do not need a union bound over all vectors,
but only over support-one vectors, and we can then use a deterministic switching argument based on the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to deal with all other vectors.

Remark 1.4
.

We have chosen to present the simplest argument that answers Question
1.1
, but
a more elaborate analysis shows that Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
are true for any constant
\delta>0
and some suitable
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\delta)>0
.
Moreover,
\varepsilon(\delta)
can be taken to be a continuous monotonic function such that
\varepsilon(\delta)\to 1
as
\delta\to 0
.
On the other hand, a standard “rectification” argument in additive combinatorics (see
[
4
]
*Section 3 for example) shows that necessarily
\varepsilon(\delta)\to 0
as
\delta\to\infty
in Theorem
1.3
.

2.
Preliminaries

If
\Gamma
is a
d
-regular graph,
then
\operatorname{gap}(\Gamma)
denotes the spectral gap of the normalized adjacency matrix.
It is well-known that
\operatorname{gap}(\Gamma)>0
if and only if
\Gamma
is connected,
and we say
\Gamma
is an
(
\varepsilon
-)expander
if
\operatorname{gap}(\Gamma)\geq\varepsilon
(often
\varepsilon
is implicit).
For an expander graph, the diameter is logarithmic in the number of vertices.

If
G
is a finite group and
S\subseteq G
, the Cayley graph
\operatorname{Cay}(G,S)
is the undirected graph with vertex set
G
and edges
(g,gs^{\pm 1})
for
g\in G
and
s\in S
.
We allow multiple edges, so
\operatorname{Cay}(G,S)
is always a
d
-regular graph for
d=2|S|
.
We write
\operatorname{gap}(G,S)
for
\operatorname{gap}(\operatorname{Cay}(G,S))
, and if
\operatorname{Cay}(G,S)
is an expander then we say that
G
is an expander
with respect to
S
.

In practice, it is often technically convenient to argue in terms of the
Kazhdan constant
.
This is defined by

\kappa(G,S)\>:=\>\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\colon G\to U(V)\\
V^{G}=0\end{subarray}}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in V\\
\|\xi\|=1\end{subarray}}\max_{s\in S}\|\pi(s)\xi-\xi\|,

where the first infimum runs over all unitary representations
\pi\colon G\to U(V)
with no nonzero
G
-invariants,
and the second infimum runs over all unit vectors
\xi\in V
.
The following basic properties of
\kappa(G,S)
are mostly immediate from the definition.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group and let
S,T\subseteq G
.

(1)

\kappa(G,S)\leq\kappa(G,T)
if
S\subseteq T
;

(2)

\kappa(G,S)\leq 2
;

(3)

\kappa(G,G)\geq\sqrt{2}
;

(4)

\kappa(G,S)\geq\frac{1}{n}\kappa(G,S^{n})
.

Proof.

(1)–(2) are clear. For (3)–(4) see
[
9
]
*Propositions 1.3–1.4.
∎

It is well known that the spectral gap and the Kazhdan constant are related by the following inequalities.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G
be a finite group and
S\subseteq G
.
Then

\frac{\kappa(G,S)^{2}}{2|S|}\>\leq\>\operatorname{gap}(G,S)\>\leq\>\frac{\kappa(G,S)^{2}}{2}.

Proof.

See
[
6
]
*Proposition III.
∎

In particular, if
S
has bounded cardinality,
then
\operatorname{gap}(G,S)
is bounded away from zero if and only if
\kappa(G,S)
is so.

3.
Expansion in semidirect products

Alon–Lubotzky–Wigderson
[
1
]
analyze the expansion of semidirect products by identifying them as a special case of zig-zag products. Let us take the opportunity to explain a more direct approach based on Kazhdan constants.

3.1.
Kazhdan constant and semidirect products

Let
\pi\colon G\to U(V)
be a unitary
G
-representation,
S\subseteq G
, and
\varepsilon>0
.
We say that
\xi\in V
is
\varepsilon
-almost
S
-invariant
if

\|\pi(s)\xi-\xi\|\><\>\varepsilon\|\xi\|

for all
s\in S
.
Then
\kappa(G,S)
is the largest constant
\varepsilon\geq 0
with the property that every unitary
G
-representation with an
\varepsilon
-almost
S
-invariant vector has a nonzero
G
-invariant vector.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\pi\colon G\to U(V)
and let
\xi\in V
be
\varepsilon
-almost
S
-invariant.
Assume
\kappa(G,S)>0
and let
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/\kappa(G,S)
.
Then

(1)

there is a
G
-invariant vector
\xi_{1}\in V
such that
\|\xi-\xi_{1}\|<\varepsilon^{\prime}\|\xi\|
;

(2)

\xi
is
2\varepsilon^{\prime}
-almost
G
-invariant.

Proof.

(i)

Orthogonally decompose
\xi=\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}
where
\xi_{1}\in V^{G}
and
\xi_{2}\in(V^{G})^{\perp}
.
Since
((V^{G})^{\perp})^{G}=0
, there is some
s\in S
such that

\kappa(G,S)\|\xi_{2}\|\leq\|\pi(s)\xi_{2}-\xi_{2}\|=\|\pi(s)\xi-\xi\|<\varepsilon\|\xi\|.

Hence
\|\xi_{2}\|<\varepsilon^{\prime}\|\xi\|
.

(ii)

For
g\in G
,
\|\pi(g)\xi-\xi\|=\|\pi(g)\xi_{2}-\xi_{2}\|\leq 2\|\xi_{2}\|<2\varepsilon^{\prime}\|\xi\|.

∎

Lemma 3.2
.

If
H\leqslant G
then

\kappa(G,S)\geq\frac{1}{2}\kappa(G,S\cup H)\,\kappa(H,S\cap H).

Proof.

Let
\pi\colon G\to U(V)
be a unitary representation with
V^{G}=0
and suppose
\xi\in V
is
\varepsilon
-almost
S
-invariant vector
\xi
, where
\varepsilon>0
.
Then in particular
\xi
is
\varepsilon
-almost
(S\cap H)
-invariant.
By Lemma
3.1
(b),
\xi
is
2\varepsilon^{\prime}
-almost
H
-invariant, where
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/\kappa(H,S\cap H)
.
Since
\kappa(H,S\cap H)\leq 2
,
\varepsilon\leq 2\varepsilon^{\prime}
, so
\xi
is
2\varepsilon^{\prime}
-almost
(S\cup H)
-invariant.
Since
V^{G}=0
, it follows that
2\varepsilon^{\prime}\geq\kappa(G,S\cup H)
, i.e.,
\varepsilon\geq\frac{1}{2}\kappa(G,S\cup H)\kappa(H,S\cap H)
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

If
N\trianglelefteqslant G
, then

\kappa(G,S\cup N)\geq\frac{1}{4}\kappa(G/N,SN/N).

Proof.

Let
\pi\colon G\to U(V)
be a unitary representation with
V^{G}=0
and suppose
\xi\in V
is
\varepsilon
-almost
(S\cup N)
-invariant,
where
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{4}\kappa(G/N,SN/N)\leq 1/2
.
By Lemma
3.1
(a), there is some
\xi_{1}\in V^{N}
such that
\|\xi-\xi_{1}\|<\varepsilon^{\prime}\|\xi\|
, where
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/\kappa(N,N)\leq\varepsilon/\sqrt{2}
.
This implies
\|\xi_{1}\|>(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\|\xi\|
.
Now
V^{N}
is a
G/N
-representation, and if
s\in S
then

\displaystyle\|\pi(s)\xi_{1}-\xi_{1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\pi(s)\xi-\xi\|+2\|\xi-\xi_{1}\|

\displaystyle<(\varepsilon+2\varepsilon^{\prime})\|\xi\|<\frac{\varepsilon+2\varepsilon^{\prime}}{1-\varepsilon^{\prime}}\|\xi_{1}\|<4\varepsilon\|\xi_{1}\|.

Therefore
\xi_{1}
is
4\varepsilon
-almost
(SN/N)
-invariant.
Since
(V^{N})^{G/N}=V^{G}=0
and
4\varepsilon=\kappa(G/N,SN/N)
, this is a contradiction.
∎

The following result can be used to replace
[
12
]
*Theorem 6.4 in the
context of Cayley graphs.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
G=N\rtimes H
,
S\subseteq N
and
T\subseteq H
.
Let
R=S\cup T\subseteq G
.
Then

\kappa(G,R)\>\geq\>\frac{\sqrt{2}}{48}\,\kappa(N,S^{H})\,\kappa(H,T),

where
S^{H}=\{s^{h}:s\in S,h\in H\}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
applied to
H\leqslant G
we have

\kappa(G,R)\geq\frac{1}{2}\kappa(G,S\cup H)\kappa(H,T).

Now
S^{H}\cup H\subseteq(S\cup H)^{3}
, so by Lemma
2.1
(1,4) we have

\kappa(G,S\cup H)\geq\frac{1}{3}\kappa(G,(S\cup H)^{3})\geq\frac{1}{3}\kappa(G,S^{H}\cup H).

Applying Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
to
N\trianglelefteqslant G
,

\displaystyle\kappa(G,S^{H}\cup H)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\kappa(G,N\cup H)\kappa(N,S^{H})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{8}\kappa(G/N,G/N)\kappa(N,S^{H})\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{8}\kappa(N,S^{H}).

This concludes the proof.
∎

3.2.
Reduction to the exponential sum estimate

We now prove Theorem
1.2
(a) assuming Theorem
1.3
(which will be proved in the next section).
Let
V=\mathbf{F}_{p}^{n}
and let
V_{0}<V
be the deleted permutation module of dimension
n-1
.
Let
G=V_{0}\rtimes S_{n}
and
v\in V_{0}
.
By Kassabov
[
9
]
there is a bounded-size generating set
T\subseteq S_{n}
such that
\kappa(S_{n},T)
is bounded away from zero.
If
X=\{v\}\cup T
,
then by Proposition
3.4
and Lemma
2.2
we have

\displaystyle\kappa(G,X)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{48}\kappa(V_{0},v^{S_{n}})\kappa(S_{n},T)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{24}\operatorname{gap}(V_{0},v^{S_{n}})^{1/2}\kappa(S_{n},T).

We now focus on
\operatorname{gap}(V_{0},v^{S_{n}})
.
Observe that
\operatorname{Cay}(V,v^{S_{n}})
is the disjoint union of
p
graphs all isomorphic to
\operatorname{Cay}(V_{0},v^{S_{n}})
.
It follows that the eigenvalues of the adjacency operator of
\operatorname{Cay}(V_{0},v^{S_{n}})
are the same as for

\operatorname{Cay}(V,v^{S_{n}})
, the only difference being their multiplicities.
Since
V
is abelian, its regular representation is isomorphic to a direct sum of one-dimensional representations
indexed by the irreducible complex characters.
We can identify these characters with vectors
w\in V
, using the formula

\chi_{w}(u)=e_{p}(\langle{u,w}\rangle)\qquad(u,w\in V).

(Recall that
e_{p}(x):=\exp(2\pi ix/p)
.)
It follows that the eigenvalues of
\operatorname{Cay}(V,v^{S_{n}})
and so of
\operatorname{Cay}(V_{0},v^{S_{n}})
are given by

\frac{1}{2|S_{n}|}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}{\left(\chi_{w}(v^{\sigma})+\chi_{w}(-v^{\sigma})\right)}\hskip 28.45274pt(w\in V).

These are precisely the real values
\{\operatorname{Re}[\lambda_{v,w}]\}_{w\in V}
, where

\lambda_{v,w}\>:=\>\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}e_{p}(\langle{v,w^{\sigma}}\rangle).

The trivial eigenvalues are associated to the constant vectors, and so

\operatorname{gap}(V_{0},v^{S_{n}})=1-\max_{w\in V\setminus\langle{\mathbf{1}}\rangle}\operatorname{Re}[\lambda_{v,w}]
.
Since
\operatorname{Re}[\lambda_{v,w}]\leq|\lambda_{v,w}|
, the reduction follows.

4.
Permutational exponential sums

In this section we use a probabilistic argument to prove Theorem
1.3
, which asserts that there are constants
\delta,\varepsilon>0
with the following property. For every integer
n>0
and prime
p\leq e^{\delta n}
there is some vector
v\in V_{0}
such that

\max_{w\in V\setminus\langle{\mathbf{1}}\rangle}|\lambda_{v,w}|\>\leq\>1-\varepsilon.

The main challenge in proving this is that, if
p
is large, there are far too many vectors
w\in V\setminus\langle{\mathbf{1}}\rangle
for a naïve union bound to work (even if we just consider sorted vectors).

For
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\in V_{0}
and
u\in\mathbf{F}_{p}
,
let
\lambda_{v}(u)=\lambda_{v,(u,0,\ldots,0)}
and observe that

\lambda_{v}(u)\>=\>\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{p}(uv_{i}).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\varepsilon\geq 2/n
and assume
0\neq u\in\mathbf{F}_{p}
.
If
v\in V_{0}
is uniformly random, then

\Pr(|\lambda_{v}(u)|\geq\varepsilon)\>\leq\>4\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}n/8).

As a consequence,

\Pr{\left(\max_{0\neq u\in\mathbf{F}_{p}}|\lambda_{v}(u)|\geq\varepsilon\right)}\>\leq\>4p\exp{\left(-\varepsilon^{2}n/8\right)}.

Proof.

The random variables
(e_{p}(uv_{i}))_{i=1}^{n-1}
are independent uniformly random complex
p
th roots of unity.
If
\Sigma=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}e_{p}(uv_{i})
,
by Hoeffding’s inequality
[
7
]
*Theorem 2 we have

\Pr{\left(|\Sigma|\geq\varepsilon n/2\right)}\leq 4\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}/8(n-1))\leq 4\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}n/8).

On the other hand if
|\Sigma|\leq\varepsilon n/2
then by the triangle inequality

|\lambda_{v}(u)|\leq\varepsilon/2+1/n\leq\varepsilon.

The second bound follows by the union bound.
∎

We now deduce a deterministic bound for all nonconstant
w\in V
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
v\in V_{0}
. We have

\max_{w\in V\setminus\langle{\mathbf{1}}\rangle}|\lambda_{v,w}|^{2}\>\leq\>\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\max_{0\neq u\in\mathbf{F}_{p}}|\lambda_{v}(u)|^{2}.

Proof.

We apply a switching argument based on the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.

Let
w\in V\setminus\langle{\mathbf{1}}\rangle
and let us bound
\lambda_{v,w}
.
Obviously
\lambda_{v,w}=\lambda_{v,w^{\sigma}}
for all
\sigma\in S_{n}
, so we may assume
w_{1}\neq w_{2}
without loss of generality. Let
u=w_{1}-w_{2}
and let
\tau
be the transposition
(1,2)
. Then

\lambda_{v,w}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}e_{p}(\langle{v,w^{\sigma}}\rangle)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\frac{1}{2}{\left(e_{p}(\langle{v,w^{\sigma}}\rangle)+e_{p}(\langle{v,w^{\tau\sigma}}\rangle)\right)}.

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle|\lambda_{v,w}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\frac{1}{4}\left|e_{p}(\langle{v,w^{\sigma}}\rangle)+e_{p}(\langle{v,w^{\tau\sigma}}\rangle)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\frac{1}{4}{\left(2+2\operatorname{Re}\left[e_{p}(\langle{v,w^{\sigma}}\rangle-\langle{v,w^{\tau\sigma}}\rangle)\right]\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\left[\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}e_{p}(\langle v,(w-w^{\tau})^{\sigma}\rangle)\right].

Now
w-w^{\tau}=(u,-u,0,0,\dots)
, so

\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}e_{p}(\langle{v,(w-w^{\tau})^{\sigma}}\rangle)=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i,j\leq n\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}e_{p}(uv_{i}-uv_{j}),

and

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i,j\leq n\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}e_{p}(uv_{i}-uv_{j})=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}e_{p}(uv_{i}-uv_{j})-n=n^{2}|\lambda_{v}(u)|^{2}-n,

so we get

|\lambda_{v,w}|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{n|\lambda_{v}(u)|^{2}-1}{n-1}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\lambda_{v}(u)|^{2}.

This proves the lemma since
u\neq 0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
\varepsilon=1/2
and
\delta=1/100
.
Let
n
be an integer,
p\leq e^{\delta n}
a prime,
and note that
\varepsilon\geq 2/n
holds.
For
v\in V_{0}
uniformly random,
by Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
we have

\displaystyle\Pr{\left(\max_{w\in V\setminus\langle{\mathbf{1}}\rangle}|\lambda_{v,w}|^{2}\geq(1+\varepsilon^{2})/2\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\Pr{\left(\max_{0\neq u\in\mathbf{F}_{p}}|\lambda_{v}(u)|\geq\varepsilon\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 4pe^{-\varepsilon^{2}n/8}\leq e^{(3\delta-\varepsilon^{2}/8)n}<1.

This proves the theorem with
\sqrt{(1+\varepsilon^{2})/2}
in place of
1-\varepsilon
, which is sufficient.
∎
