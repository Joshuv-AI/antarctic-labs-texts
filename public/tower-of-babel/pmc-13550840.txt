Probiotic-Derived Extracellular Vesicles as Potential Nano-Immunotherapeutic Platforms in IBD: A Clinician’s Perspective
Authors: Hyeon-Jeong Jeon, A Reum Choe, Seong-Eun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory disorder driven by complex interactions. Despite therapeutic advances, achieving sustained remission remains difficult, underscoring the need for safer and more durable treatment strategies. Increasing evidence highlights the pivotal role of the gut microbiota in IBD pathogenesis, prompting growing interest in microbiome-based interventions. Probiotic-derived extracellular vesicles (probiotic-derived EVs), a subset of bacterial EVs (BEVs), have emerged as promising cell-free mediators of host–microbe communication. These nanoscale vesicles can deliver bioactive cargo, modulate immune responses, and influence epithelial barrier function, thereby offering a potential strategy to regulate intestinal homeostasis while potentially avoiding some limitations associated with live microbial therapies. This review provides a comprehensive overview of probiotic-derived EVs, including their biogenesis, classification, and molecular composition. It further examines their biological functions and mechanisms of action in IBD, with a focus on barrier regulation, immune modulation, and microbial homeostasis. Recent advances in engineering strategies to enhance therapeutic efficacy are also summarized. Key challenges for clinical translation, including standardization, safety concerns, and limited clinical validation despite promising preclinical findings, are discussed. Together, these mechanistic and translational perspectives help delineate the current evidence, remaining limitations, and future directions regarding the development of probiotic-derived EVs as nanotherapeutic platforms for IBD.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, is a chronic relapsing inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract that remains a significant clinical challenge despite advances in medical therapy.1,2 Recent studies have shown that gut microbiota dysbiosis is commonly observed in patients with IBD, and these alterations are associated with dysregulated immune responses and epithelial barrier dysfunction, contributing to disease onset and progression.3,4 Increasing evidence from experimental, genetic, and translational studies, as well as inflammatory biomarker analysis, further suggests that disrupted host–microbiota interactions play a central role in IBD pathogenesis and disease progression.5,6 Accordingly, restoration of intestinal homeostasis has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy that complements conventional immune suppression.7

Recent treatment guidelines emphasize a treat-to-target approach in IBD, with sustained clinical remission and endoscopic healing as key therapeutic goals.8,9 However, real-world evidence indicates that durable deep remission remains difficult to achieve and maintain in a substantial proportion of patients despite biologics and small-molecule therapies.10–13 This persistent treatment gap highlights the need for complementary therapeutic approaches that extend beyond conventional immune suppression and target additional pathogenic mechanisms, including disrupted host-microbiota interactions.7,14 Building on this rationale, microbiome-based therapeutic strategies have attracted increasing interest in IBD.15,16 However, current approaches relying on live microorganisms remain limited by safety concerns, variability in colonization, and limited pharmacological controllability. These limitations are particularly relevant for vulnerable or immunocompromised patients who may be at increased risk of microbial translocation or systemic infection. In addition, live microorganisms may replicate or persist in vivo, making treatment responses difficult to predict.

In this context, bacterial extracellular vesicles (BEVs) have recently emerged as a potential cell-free alternative capable of mediating host–microbe communication without the need for viable organisms.17–22 By avoiding bacterial replication and colonization, BEVs may offer a conceptually more controllable therapeutic approach than live microorganism-based therapies, although various issues such as dosing, stability, biodistribution, and manufacturing consistency remain unresolved. Among BEVs, probiotic-derived EVs represent a particularly relevant subset for IBD because they may deliver beneficial microbial signals directly to the intestinal mucosa while avoiding some limitations associated with live probiotics. Their nanoscale structure, microbial bioactivity, potential compatibility with oral administration, and ability to modulate epithelial and immune pathways support their investigation as gut-directed therapeutic candidates. Compared with mammalian cell-derived EVs, they may also offer a more gut-oriented therapeutic approach by directly engaging host–microbiota interactions at the intestinal mucosa. Although previous reviews have addressed EV biology, BEV biogenesis, probiotics, and microbiome-based interventions in IBD, the translational and clinical positioning of probiotic-derived EVs remains insufficiently defined. The novelty of this review lies in framing probiotic-derived EVs not only as biological mediators of host–microbiota communication, but also as emerging cell-free nano-immunotherapeutic platforms with potential relevance to IBD therapy. Accordingly, this review integrates current mechanistic evidence on probiotic-derived EVs with clinically relevant considerations, including safety, oral delivery potential, localized intestinal activity, standardization, pharmacokinetic characterization, scalable manufacturing, regulatory classification, and future clinical development.

We first summarize key aspects of BEV biology, including biogenesis, molecular composition, and functional roles, before reviewing the preclinical evidence supporting probiotic-derived EVs in experimental IBD. We further discuss recent engineering strategies together with the major translational challenges that must be addressed before these vesicles can be advanced toward clinical evaluation (Figure 1). By integrating mechanistic insights with translational and clinician-oriented perspectives, this review aims to clarify the current evidence, remaining limitations, and future directions for probiotic-derived EVs in IBD. Why Do We Need New Therapies in IBD?

Despite substantial therapeutic advances, achieving sustained remission and durable long-term disease control in IBD remains a major clinical challenge.23 IBD arises from complex interactions among genetic, environmental, microbial, and immunological factors, and this multifactorial pathogenesis continues to limit the ability of current approaches to achieve comprehensive disease control.24,25 The introduction of biologics and small-molecule inhibitors has markedly improved the management of moderate-to-severe IBD.26–28 However, a proportion of patients still exhibit primary non-response or develop secondary loss of response over time.29,30 Long-term treatment may also be associated with risks such as immunogenicity, infection, and malignancy, which should be considered in clinical practice.31–33 Consequently, treatment optimization, therapeutic switching, and maintenance of a durable response remain major challenges. Although biologics and small-molecule therapies effectively suppress inflammation and induce clinical or endoscopic remission, they do not necessarily restore a healthy intestinal microbial ecosystem. Recent longitudinal microbiome studies have shown that dysbiosis may persist even in patients who achieve remission, indicating that control of inflammation does not necessarily equate to restoration of a healthy intestinal microbial ecosystem.34,35 Collectively, these observations underscore the need for complementary therapeutic approaches that target additional pathogenic mechanisms beyond immune suppression.

Current therapies are primarily focused on suppressing inflammatory and immune-mediated pathways rather than directly correcting disturbances in host–microbiota interactions. Although highly effective in improving clinical outcomes, these therapies do not directly reverse intestinal dysbiosis or fully restore host–microbiota homeostasis, both of which are increasingly recognized as contributors to disease pathogenesis and recurrence.36 These limitations have prompted growing interest in microbiome-directed therapeutic strategies designed to complement, rather than replace, existing immunomodulatory therapies.37

The gut microbiota plays an important role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis and may contribute to the pathogenesis of IBD.38 Dysbiosis, characterized by reduced microbial diversity and depletion of beneficial commensals, has been associated with immune dysregulation and epithelial barrier dysfunction.39,40 These findings have encouraged the development of microbiome-based interventions aimed at restoring microbial balance and modulating host immune responses.4,41 The recent regulatory approval of live microbiome therapeutics, including fecal microbiota–based products for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection, demonstrates the clinical feasibility of this therapeutic concept.42,43 However, the broader use of live microbial therapeutics remains constrained by the risk of systemic infections, donor- or strain-dependent variability, inconsistent intestinal engraftment, manufacturing complexity, and limited pharmacological controllability.44,45 Collectively, these limitations highlight the need for more standardized, precisely engineered, and pharmacologically controllable platforms capable of modulating host–microbiota interactions in IBD.

EVs as a Therapeutic Platform (What are EVs?)

Types and Biogenesis of EVs

EVs are nanosized, lipid bilayer particles released by all cell types across all domains of life, including archaea, bacteria, and eukaryotes.46 Previously regarded as cellular byproducts, EVs are now widely recognized as key mediators of intercellular communication.47,48

EVs have traditionally been described using biogenesis-related terms, including exosomes, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies, although these terms should be used cautiously unless their cellular origin or biogenesis pathway has been experimentally demonstrated.49 Exosomes are nanoscale vesicles generated within the endosomal pathway. They arise from inward budding of multivesicular bodies via both endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT)-dependent and -independent mechanisms, typically measuring 30–150 nm in diameter.46,50,51 In contrast, microvesicles, also referred to as microparticles or ectosomes, are formed by outward budding of the plasma membrane and typically range in size from 100 to 1000 nm. Apoptotic bodies are larger vesicles (approximately 500–2000 nm) released during programmed cell death through membrane blebbing. These vesicles may contain cytoplasmic organelles and nuclear fragments.52,53

More recently, however, due to overlapping size distributions and the difficulty in clearly distinguishing their biogenesis pathways, the Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV) guidelines suggest an operational classification of EVs based on measurable characteristics, including size, with commonly used categories such as small EVs (<200 nm) and large EVs (>200 nm).54,55 Despite these distinctions, substantial overlap exists in physicochemical properties and molecular composition, and their precise classification remains an area of ongoing investigation (Table 1). EVs are widely distributed across diverse biological fluids, including blood, urine, and saliva.46,47 Their lipid bilayer membranes protect cargo from enzymatic degradation. In addition, their nanoscale size and origin as naturally secreted vesicles may contribute to high biocompatibility and relatively low immunogenicity.56,57 These features may also facilitate interaction with biological barriers. Moreover, surface-associated proteins and ligands can support selective interactions with recipient cells, suggesting an inherent targeting potential.58 Collectively, these properties highlight the biological relevance of EVs and underscore the need for further mechanistic investigation.

Functional Roles and Mechanisms of EVs

EVs function as carriers of diverse bioactive cargo, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, which can be delivered to or interact with recipient cells, thereby modulating gene expression and cellular behavior.57,59 EVs are taken up by recipient cells through multiple mechanisms, including endocytosis, phagocytosis, and macropinocytosis, or through direct membrane fusion.60 Surface molecules on EV membranes, such as integrins and tetraspanins, may contribute to preferential interactions with and uptake by specific cell types.58

The molecular composition of EVs reflects the physiological and pathological state of their cells of origin and is associated with their involvement in a wide range of biological processes, including immune regulation, inflammation, and tissue remodeling.59 Following internalization, EV cargo can modulate intracellular signaling pathways, thereby influencing cellular functions such as immune activation and epithelial barrier regulation.61 In addition, the lipid bilayer structure of EV membranes may protect their cargo from enzymatic degradation, facilitating relatively stable transport and efficient delivery in complex biological environments.56 Collectively, these features support the role of EVs as important mediators of intercellular communication and potential platforms for therapeutic applications. Building on these general functions, EVs play particularly important roles in the intestinal microenvironment.

EVs in the Gut Microenvironment

Within the gut, EVs derived from host and microbial sources are abundant and may link luminal microbial signals to epithelial and mucosal immune responses, thereby contributing to intestinal homeostasis.18,62,63 These vesicles can traverse the mucus layer and be internalized by intestinal epithelial cells. Following uptake, they may be transported across epithelial cells and access the underlying lamina propria, where they can interact with resident immune cells such as dendritic cells and macrophages.18 Under conditions of epithelial barrier disruption, such as in IBD, EVs may also pass through paracellular pathways, potentially contributing to increased intestinal permeability.64,65

Through these interactions, EVs may contribute to the regulation of epithelial barrier function, including the maintenance of tight junction integrity.57,66 They also modulate mucosal immune responses by influencing immune cell activity and cytokine signaling.67 Furthermore, EVs serve as mediators of microbiota–host communication by enabling the transfer of various bioactive components without requiring direct microbial contact.21,68

Collectively, these features highlight the potential relevance of EVs in the intestinal microenvironment and support a role in the pathogenesis of IBD. However, the functional heterogeneity of EVs remains incompletely understood, and further studies are required to better define their roles under both physiological and pathological conditions. These biological properties have led to growing interest in EVs as potential therapeutic platforms for IBD, in which intercellular communication and immune regulation play central roles.

EV-Based Therapies in IBD: From Stem Cells to Microbiota

Although diverse EV-based and cell-free platforms are being explored for intestinal inflammation, mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs (MSC-EVs) are discussed first because they represent one of the most extensively studied EV-based approaches in experimental IBD and provide a useful reference point for evaluating probiotic-derived EVs.69,70 Their therapeutic effects and translational limitations highlight key issues shared by EV-based therapies, including heterogeneity, manufacturing complexity, dose definition, and standardization, while also clarifying the distinct microbiota-related rationale for probiotic-derived EVs.71

MSC-EVs as Therapeutic Platforms

MSCs are multipotent stromal cells with well-established immunomodulatory and regenerative properties, and MSC-EVs have been increasingly investigated as cell-free therapeutic candidates for IBD.70,72–74 Previous studies have shown that MSC-EVs attenuate intestinal inflammation by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine production, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), inhibiting nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling, and promoting macrophage polarization toward an M2-like anti-inflammatory phenotype.75–77 In addition, MSC-EVs contribute to epithelial barrier restoration and mucosal healing by delivering bioactive molecules, including proteins, lipids, and regulatory RNAs.78 These findings support their anti-inflammatory efficacy across multiple experimental colitis models.

Despite these promising therapeutic effects, several critical limitations hinder their clinical translation. MSC-EVs exhibit substantial heterogeneity depending on the MSC source, culture conditions, and isolation methods, resulting in variability in composition and therapeutic potency.79–81 Moreover, large-scale production remains challenging due to low yield, complex purification processes, and high manufacturing costs. The absence of standardized protocols for isolation, characterization, and quality control further limits reproducibility and poses regulatory challenges.82–84 Additionally, MSC-EVs exert broad immunosuppressive effects but are not specifically tailored to target host–microbiota interactions, which are increasingly recognized as key contributors to IBD pathogenesis.22,85

Taken together, although MSC-EVs demonstrate preclinical efficacy, their limitations in standardization, scalability, and microbiota relevance underscore the need to explore alternative EV-based strategies in IBD.86–88 Compared with MSC-EVs, probiotic-derived EVs may offer relative advantages as a gut-oriented therapeutic platform for IBD, owing to their gut-associated microbial origin, potential oral deliverability, and microbe-associated cargo involved in host–microbiota signaling.67,70,89 To clarify these differences, Table 2 summarizes the comparative characteristics of MSC-EVs and probiotic-derived EVs as therapeutic platforms, highlighting both the potential relative advantages of probiotic-derived EVs and unresolved issues that require further translational validation. BEVs

As noted above, gut microbiota dysregulation contributes to IBD pathogenesis not only through direct host-microbe interactions but also through microbial products and vesicle-mediated signaling.38 In this context, BEVs have emerged as important mediators of host–microbe crosstalk by transferring bacteria-derived bioactive molecules to intestinal epithelial and immune cells.90–93 They carry enzymes, metabolites, glycoproteins, nucleic acids, and, in some cases, virulence factors.94,95 Accordingly, once regarded as waste disposal structures, BEVs are now recognized as active signaling entities involved in both pathological and physiological processes.96

In this review, the term BEVs refers to all bacterial extracellular vesicles, while “probiotic-derived EVs” specifically denotes vesicles derived from beneficial commensal or probiotic strains. Probiotic-derived EVs have attracted growing interest as a potential therapeutic platform and may represent an alternative to live microbial therapies and mammalian EV-based platforms.89,97 Compared with MSC-EVs, their microbial origin may offer closer relevance to the intestinal microenvironment and potential compatibility with oral administration; however, these proposed advantages remain to be clinically validated, particularly with respect to safety, dosing, biodistribution, manufacturing consistency, and therapeutic efficacy.89,98

In Gram-negative bacteria, these vesicles are commonly referred to as outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), which are formed by outward blebbing of the outer membrane and contain outer membrane and periplasmic components, including lipopolysaccharide (LPS).17 In addition to OMVs, Gram-negative bacteria can generate outer-inner membrane vesicles (OIMVs), which are thought to arise from membrane blebbing processes involving the encapsulation of inner membrane material.94 OIMVs may include both periplasmic and cytoplasmic contents.99 Explosive outer membrane vesicles (EOMVs) are produced through explosive cell lysis and can incorporate a broader range of intracellular components, including DNA and cytosolic proteins.94

In contrast, EV production in Gram-positive bacteria was long underappreciated; however, advances in imaging techniques have demonstrated that these bacteria also release EVs despite lacking an outer membrane.100,101 These vesicles, termed cytoplasmic membrane vesicles (CMVs), are generated through cytoplasmic membrane budding associated with localized weakening or remodeling of the peptidoglycan layer. CMVs primarily contain cytosolic components and lack LPS.102,103 Furthermore, Gram-positive bacteria can produce explosive cytoplasmic membrane vesicles (ECMVs), which are associated with cell lysis–related processes and may involve the release of intracellular contents.104–107 Collectively, these distinct biogenesis pathways contribute to the diversity in BEV structure, composition, and function.

Functional Roles of BEVs in the Gut: Focus on Probiotic-Derived EVs

The biological effects of BEVs are influenced by their microbial origin.19,108 For example, EVs derived from Gram-negative pathogens, such as Escherichia coli, can contain LPS and other virulence factors that activate Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated NF-κB signaling, leading to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-8 (IL-8).109 More broadly, pathogenic EVs are associated with epithelial injury and pro-inflammatory responses through the delivery of virulence factors and toxins.110–113

In contrast, EVs derived from commensal and probiotic bacteria exhibit immunomodulatory properties that may be beneficial to the host.18,114 Probiotics, defined as live microorganisms that provide beneficial effects to the host when administered in sufficient amounts, represent an important source of such BEVs.115,116 Probiotic-derived EVs have been proposed to contribute to the beneficial effects of their parent probiotic strains, with reported effects on epithelial barrier function, inflammatory responses, and intestinal homeostasis.114,117,118 Notably, Akkermansia muciniphila-derived EVs enhance epithelial barrier integrity by upregulating tight junction proteins, including zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and occludin, while reducing intestinal inflammation in dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis models.66

Collectively, these findings support the concept that selective utilization of beneficial EV populations may have therapeutic relevance. Accordingly, probiotic-derived EVs represent a biologically relevant and therapeutically promising subset of BEVs.98,119,120

Roles of Probiotic-Derived EVs in IBD: A Cell-Free Microbiome-Based Therapeutic Approach

Recent evidence from preclinical models of inflammation suggests that probiotic-derived EVs may have therapeutic potential for the prevention and treatment of IBD. We summarize findings from experimental models to outline the mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of probiotic-derived EVs in IBD, with a focus on immune modulation, intestinal barrier integrity, and regulation of gut microbiota homeostasis.

Potential Therapeutic Advantages of Probiotic-Derived EVs in IBD

Probiotic-derived EVs have emerged as a cell-free therapeutic platform that may address several limitations associated with conventional microbiome-based interventions in IBD.121 Their proposed advantages include their postbiotic nature, colonization-independent activity, compatibility with engineering approaches, and the potential for more controlled therapeutic applications.

Notably, conventional microbiome-based approaches are limited by the potential presence of antibiotic resistance genes and variability in efficacy arising from environmental and manufacturing factors such as temperature, pH, fermentation conditions, microbial composition, and product stability. Accordingly, there has been increasing interest in postbiotics, defined as preparations of inanimate microorganisms and/or their components that confer a health benefit on the host.122–124 Probiotic-derived EVs have therefore been proposed as postbiotic candidates that deliver bioactive microbial components without requiring viable microorganisms.125–127

Another potential advantage of probiotic-derived EVs is their ability to exert biological effects without requiring bacterial colonization.66 Because probiotic-derived EVs do not require bacterial engraftment, their therapeutic activity may be less susceptible to interindividual variability arising from differences in baseline microbiota composition.45,128 However, this colonization-independent property may also necessitate repeated administration to maintain sustained effects.19,129

In addition to these functional advantages, probiotic-derived EVs may reduce certain risks associated with live bacterial therapies, although their safety profile requires careful evaluation.130–132 Unlike live microbial therapies, probiotic-derived EVs are non-replicative cell-free structures that may reduce risks related to bacterial viability and uncontrolled colonization while retaining immunomodulatory properties derived from their parental bacteria.89,133,134 Nevertheless, these potential advantages should be interpreted cautiously because probiotic-derived EVs retain bacterial components that may introduce distinct safety concerns. In particular, EVs derived from Gram-negative probiotic or beneficial commensal strains may contain EV-associated LPS, whereas residual free endotoxin or bacterial debris may also be introduced during purification. Such components, together with other microbe-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs), may activate TLR- or NOD-like receptor-mediated pathways and induce dose-, strain-, and host-dependent inflammatory responses.135,136 These concerns may be particularly relevant in patients with impaired epithelial barrier function or those receiving immunosuppressive therapy, in whom mucosal uptake and systemic exposure may differ. Therefore, comprehensive safety assessment should include strain-specific characterization of LPS and other immunostimulatory components, endotoxin quantification, cytokine-response profiling, repeated-dose toxicity testing, and evaluation in immunocompromised or otherwise vulnerable host models.102,108,133,135

Compared with live microbial products, the cell-free and membrane-enclosed nature of probiotic-derived EVs may provide a conceptual basis for more controlled characterization and dosing. However, their composition remains strain- and process-dependent, and improved manufacturing consistency has not yet been demonstrated. Accordingly, the potential pharmacological controllability of probiotic-derived EVs should be regarded as a translational hypothesis that requires further validation rather than an established advantage.

In addition, probiotic-derived EVs have been proposed as candidates for oral administration because of their microbial origin and membrane-enclosed structure. However, their stability, bioactivity, and bioavailability throughout the gastrointestinal tract remain insufficiently characterized.123,137 Their physicochemical properties may facilitate interaction with intestinal epithelial and immune cells, potentially enhancing local bioavailability at sites of inflammation. Furthermore, compatibility with genetic and post-isolation engineering approaches expands their therapeutic versatility by enabling controlled modification of cargo composition and targeting.19

Collectively, probiotic-derived EVs represent a microbiome-based nano-therapeutic platform with distinct biological and pharmacological features, supporting their investigation as a potential next-generation therapeutic strategy for modulating host–microbiota interactions in IBD. These features have provided the basis for preclinical evaluation of probiotic-derived EVs in experimental models of intestinal inflammation. The following section summarizes the available evidence, focusing on barrier protection, immune modulation, and microbial homeostasis as the main mechanistic axes through which probiotic-derived EVs may exert therapeutic effects.

Enhancement of Intestinal Barrier Integrity

Accumulating evidence suggests that probiotic-derived EVs may support intestinal barrier integrity, in part by increasing expression of tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 and occludin, accompanied by reduced epithelial permeability.155,156 However, these effects appear to be strain- and cargo-dependent, and their magnitude may vary according to EV composition, dose, and host inflammatory status.157

Mechanistically, probiotic-derived EVs may influence epithelial barrier function through both direct effects on intestinal epithelial cells and indirect, immune-mediated pathways. Direct effects may include modulation of tight junction protein expression. Indirectly, EV-mediated crosstalk between immune and epithelial cells may contribute to barrier regulation during inflammation. Specifically, macrophage–epithelium interactions have been implicated in this process.97 These vesicles have been reported to promote macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, accompanied by increased levels of regulatory cytokines such as IL-10.

Consistent with these mechanisms, Lactobacillus murinus-derived EVs attenuated deoxynivalenol (DON)-induced epithelial damage and restored tight junction protein expression. In that study, the protective effects were associated with TLR2-dependent signaling, M2 macrophage polarization, and IL-10 production.158 Similarly, Lactobacillus kefirgranum-derived EVs improved epithelial integrity by upregulating tight junction protein expression, accompanied by alleviation of colitis and suppression of NF-κB–mediated inflammatory signaling.154 Likewise, Bacteroides acidifaciens-derived EVs attenuated DSS-induced colitis by reducing inflammatory responses and promoting intestinal mucosal barrier repair.159 Additionally, Bifidobacterium longum-derived EVs were reported to enhance barrier function and modulate immune responses in a DSS-induced colitis mouse model. Notably, these effects appeared to be partly independent of gut microbiota modulation and were associated with suppression of the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling pathway.138

Collectively, these findings suggest that probiotic-derived EVs may contribute to the maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity through coordinated epithelial and immune-related mechanisms. These barrier-protective and immunomodulatory effects support their potential role in restoring intestinal homeostasis.89,143

Immunomodulatory Mechanisms of Probiotic-Derived EVs

Probiotic-derived EVs have been reported to modulate both innate and adaptive immune responses in the context of intestinal inflammation.120,140 These vesicles interact with pattern recognition receptors, including TLRs, and may modulate downstream signaling pathways such as NF-κB, in a strain-, cargo-, and context-dependent manner.142,144 Rather than inducing excessive inflammation, however, this initial immune activation appears to promote balanced immune responses, ultimately contributing to increased IL-10 production, reduced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, and mucosal homeostasis.139,141

Beyond their effects on innate immunity, probiotic-derived EVs have also been implicated in regulating adaptive immune responses. In particular, modulation of the balance between T helper 17 (Th17) and regulatory T (Treg) cells (Th17/Treg balance) has been proposed as a potential mechanism underlying their immunoregulatory effects in IBD.

For example, Lactobacillus johnsonii-derived EVs have been reported to restore mucosal immune balance by regulating the Th17/Treg balance, accompanied by alterations in gut microbiota composition and amino acid metabolism. These effects were associated with improved epithelial barrier integrity and modulation of immunoglobulin transport via the polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR)- and neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn)-mediated pathways.140 Similarly, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum-derived EVs alleviated experimental colitis through multiple mechanisms, including promotion of M2 macrophage polarization, induction of anti-inflammatory cytokines, and modulation of gut microbiota and host metabolic pathways. In addition, Lactobacillus fermentum-derived EVs have been associated with reduced colonic inflammation, characterized by decreased TNF-α and IL-1β levels and increased IL-10 production, along with concurrent changes in epithelial barrier–related markers and gut microbial composition.141

Taken together, these findings suggest that probiotic-derived EVs may exert immunomodulatory effects through coordinated regulation of innate and adaptive immune pathways, supporting their potential role in restoring mucosal immune homeostasis.89,139

Modulation of Microbial Homeostasis

Probiotic-derived EVs have also been associated with alterations in gut microbiota composition; however, the extent and directness of these effects remain less clearly defined than their roles in barrier function and immune regulation. Reported changes include reductions in potentially pathogenic taxa and relative enrichment of beneficial microbial populations, although these findings are not consistent across strains and experimental models. For example, Lactobacillus plantarum Q7-derived EVs alleviated DSS-induced colitis and were associated with partial restoration of gut microbiota composition, including a reduction in Proteobacteria and an increase in beneficial taxa such as Bifidobacteria and Muribaculaceae.67 Similarly, Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG-derived EVs attenuated colitis and were associated with changes in gut microbial composition, alongside inhibition of the TLR4–NF-κB–NLRP3 signaling axis and modulation of host metabolic pathways.142 Likewise, Akkermansia muciniphila-derived EVs were associated with favorable shifts in microbial composition, characterized by increased abundance of beneficial taxa and a reduction in Proteobacteria.66 Collectively, current data suggest that probiotic-derived EVs may contribute to microbial homeostasis; however, whether these changes result from direct modulation of the microbiota or occur secondary to reduced inflammation and improved barrier integrity remains unclear. Accordingly, these effects should be interpreted with caution and require further mechanistic investigation.

More broadly, the preclinical evidence summarized in this section should be interpreted in light of the limitations of experimental colitis models. Many available studies of probiotic-derived EVs have relied on DSS-induced colitis models, which are useful for evaluating epithelial injury, barrier dysfunction, and acute inflammatory responses. However, the commonly used acute DSS-induced colitis is self-limiting after withdrawal of DSS exposure and does not fully recapitulate the chronic relapsing-remitting course, immune heterogeneity, and complex microbiota alterations observed in human IBD.160,161 Therefore, therapeutic effects observed in DSS-based studies should be regarded as important preclinical proof-of-concept rather than direct evidence of clinical efficacy. Future studies should validate probiotic-derived EVs across complementary models, including chronic or relapsing colitis models, T cell transfer models, IL-10-deficient models, and human intestinal organoid or ex vivo mucosal systems, to better assess their translational relevance.162,163

Engineered Probiotic-Derived EVs as Therapeutic Platforms for IBD

Despite their intrinsic therapeutic potential, natural probiotic-derived EVs may exhibit several limitations, including suboptimal targeting specificity and compositional heterogeneity.65,164,165 Furthermore, safety concerns may arise from immunogenic or toxic components, particularly LPS present in EVs derived from Gram-negative bacteria.108 These limitations highlight the need for engineering strategies to enhance targeting precision, functional control, and safety. Recent advances in the structural and mechanistic understanding of BEV biology have enabled the development of diverse engineering approaches.71,166

Engineering strategies for probiotic-derived EVs, a subset of BEVs, can be broadly classified into parental bacterial modification, primarily through genetic engineering, and post-isolation modification using physical or chemical methods.167–169 The applicability, advantages, limitations, and safety considerations of these strategies are summarized in Table 5. Engineered probiotic-derived EVs may offer advantages over conventional drug delivery systems and other EV platforms. In particular, compared with mammalian cell–derived EVs, they may provide practical benefits, including scalability, cost-effectiveness, and relative ease of genetic manipulation.19 These features may support large-scale production and clinical translation. Genetic engineering has been explored as a strategy for modifying parental bacterial strains. Recent advances in genetic engineering have enabled the development of EVs with enhanced and customizable functionalities.170 For example, genetic modification of parental bacteria using tools such as the CRISPR–Cas9 system and λ-red recombination can enable targeted modification of genes involved in EV biogenesis and cargo composition, thereby facilitating the generation of vesicles with optimized therapeutic properties.171,172 Such modifications may include the surface display of exogenous proteins or peptides to improve targeted delivery and functional activity.173,174 As a proof of concept from the broader BEV field, engineered Escherichia coli OMVs displaying tumor antigens have been used as vaccine platforms and induced antigen-specific antitumor immune responses in murine models.173 Moreover, editing genes associated with toxic components may reduce EV-associated toxicity while generally preserving membrane integrity and core biological functions.175–178 Beyond safety optimization, genetic modifications have also been reported to influence EV production efficiency, with certain alterations associated with increased vesicle release.179–181 In addition, optimization of production processes, including culture conditions and isolation methods, may further improve scalability and support clinical translation.

Another approach involves post-isolation engineering following EV extraction.182 EVs may be engineered to incorporate therapeutic cargo using active methods such as electroporation, sonication, and extrusion, as well as passive approaches including simple incubation.183,184 Surface modification with targeting moieties, including antibodies, peptides, and polysaccharides, has also been investigated as a means of enhancing tissue-specific delivery.185–187 These strategies may improve targeting efficiency and broaden the functional versatility of EV-based therapeutic platforms.

Despite these potential advantages, comprehensive characterization of engineered EVs remains essential for assessing batch-to-batch consistency, physicochemical stability, and biological safety, particularly in the context of large-scale production.188,189 Key considerations include product identity and purity, standardized manufacturing and characterization, targeting specificity, cargo-loading efficiency, and control of immunogenic or toxic bacterial components.184 Collectively, these approaches support the continued investigation of engineered probiotic-derived EVs as potential therapeutic platforms for IBD, although further preclinical and translational validation remains necessary.186

Translational Challenges

To date, no probiotic-derived EV-based therapies have advanced to late-phase clinical trials, highlighting the gap between promising preclinical findings and clinical application. Despite the therapeutic potential of probiotic-derived EVs, several key challenges remain for their successful clinical translation in IBD. These challenges are not merely technical issues, but directly affect product reproducibility, dose definition, safety assessment, regulatory evaluation, and ultimately the design of early-phase clinical trials and clinical implementation.

Standardization and Manufacturing

One of the primary limitations lies in the lack of standardized methods for EV isolation, purification, and characterization.19 Currently available techniques often yield heterogeneous vesicle populations with variable purity and composition.189 This variability may compromise reproducibility across studies and complicate the establishment of robust quality control criteria required for clinical-grade production.190 For probiotic-derived EVs, this issue is further complicated by their microbial origin, as vesicle yield, size distribution, surface components, and cargo composition may be influenced by the parental strain, bacterial growth phase, culture medium, fermentation conditions, and purification strategy.166

Although the MISEV guidelines provide a widely accepted framework for EV research, comparable standardized guidelines for probiotic-derived EVs remain limited.55,65,191,192 Accordingly, standardization efforts for probiotic-derived EVs should extend beyond general EV characterization and incorporate microbiology-specific variables related to bacterial strain, growth phase, and culture conditions that directly influence vesicle composition and function. Future studies should therefore define critical quality attributes, including particle number, size distribution, protein and lipid composition, nucleic acid cargo, purity, sterility, endotoxin levels and MAMP-related content, and biological potency. Establishing these parameters will be essential for comparing results across studies, defining release criteria, and supporting regulatory evaluation of clinical-grade EV formulations.

Beyond these methodological issues, scalable and reproducible manufacturing represents both an important translational challenge and a potential strength of probiotic-derived EVs. Probiotic-derived EVs may offer practical manufacturing advantages because bacterial cells can generally be expanded rapidly under comparatively simple culture conditions. However, EV yield and composition remain highly sensitive to strain- and process-dependent variables, and batch-to-batch consistency and process standardization have not yet been fully established.191,192 Engineering strategies may improve targeting, safety, or cargo loading, but their impact on large-scale and reproducible EV production remains unclear.188,191,192 Moreover, genetic or post-isolation engineering may introduce additional variability in vesicle composition, stability, and potency, which must be carefully controlled before clinical translation. Preservation and storage stability are also important determinants of product quality, as storage conditions may affect EV integrity, cargo stability, and biological activity.193 Standardized preservation protocols and stability testing will therefore be essential for clinical translation. The development of robust bioprocessing pipelines will also be necessary to translate the potential production advantages of probiotic-derived EVs into consistent vesicle composition, potency, and safety on an industrial scale. Such pipelines should integrate standardized upstream culture conditions, scalable purification methods, validated potency assays, batch-release criteria, and stability testing under clinically relevant storage and administration conditions.

Dosing and Pharmacokinetics

Once product identity and manufacturing consistency are established, rational clinical development requires clearer definition of dosing, biodistribution, and pharmacokinetic properties.189 These parameters remain insufficiently defined, despite their importance for route selection, dosing frequency, and dose-escalation design in future clinical trials of probiotic-derived EVs.174 Unlike conventional small-molecule drugs, probiotic-derived EVs are complex biological entities with heterogeneous cargo, which complicates the establishment of standardized dosing strategies.81,98 Current preclinical studies also show substantial heterogeneity in EV dose, dosing frequency, treatment duration, and route of administration. As summarized in Table 4, most studies have used oral gavage in experimental colitis models, but the administered doses vary widely across bacterial strains and disease models, and the rationale for dose selection is often not clearly described. This variability limits direct comparison across studies and makes it difficult to define a clinically relevant starting dose.

Another important issue is the lack of standardized dose metrics. Probiotic-derived EV doses may be reported as total protein amount, particle number, or vesicle concentration, but these parameters are not interchangeable and may not directly reflect biological potency.98,189 Therefore, future studies should establish standardized reporting criteria and include dose-response analyses to define minimum effective doses, therapeutic windows, and potential toxicity thresholds.

Beyond dose selection, key pharmacokinetic questions remain incompletely understood, particularly in human settings. These include stability in gastric and intestinal fluids, mucus penetration, uptake by intestinal epithelial and immune cells, retention at inflamed mucosal sites, local bioavailability, systemic translocation, and clearance.98 These parameters may be influenced by vesicle size, surface composition, parental bacterial strain, disease activity, and the degree of epithelial barrier disruption.189 When translating dosing regimens from preclinical models to human applications, several important limitations should be considered. For example, doses administered in murine models cannot be directly extrapolated to humans using conventional human equivalent dose (HED) scaling.81 Consequently, the optimal therapeutic dose for human application remains to be established and will require systematic evaluation in clinical studies, including standardized dose-response testing, biodistribution analyses, pharmacokinetic profiling, and repeated-dose safety assessments. These data will be critical for determining dose selection, administration schedules, and safety monitoring strategies in early-phase clinical trials.

Safety and Delivery

Safety considerations also require careful evaluation. EVs derived from Gram-negative bacteria may contain LPS, which can induce excessive inflammatory responses and limit therapeutic applicability.194 Although strategies such as genetic modification of parental strains and improved purification methods may reduce endotoxin-associated risks, ensuring a consistently favorable safety profile—especially under conditions of repeated administration—remains an important requirement.195 Beyond LPS contamination, unintended immune activation should also be considered. Although controlled activation of innate immune signaling may contribute to immune homeostasis, probiotic-derived EVs retain MAMPs that may activate pattern recognition receptors in a strain-, dose-, and context-dependent manner.18,196 This issue may be particularly relevant in immunocompromised patients or those receiving concomitant immunosuppressive therapies, in whom altered mucosal barrier function and impaired immune regulation could increase susceptibility to excessive inflammatory responses or systemic exposure. Therefore, future safety assessments should include endotoxin quantification, inflammatory cytokine profiling, repeated-dose toxicity testing, and evaluation in clinically relevant vulnerable-host conditions.

In addition, challenges related to oral delivery and in vivo stability should be considered. Although oral administration is an attractive route for probiotic-derived EVs in IBD, their lipid bilayer structure may confer relative structural stability.19 However, whether this stability is sufficient to preserve vesicle integrity and bioactivity throughout the gastrointestinal tract remains incompletely defined. After oral administration, probiotic-derived EVs may be exposed to gastric acid, digestive enzymes, bile salts, and variable intestinal pH, which could affect vesicle integrity, cargo stability, and biological activity.197,198 In addition, the intestinal mucus layer may limit mucosal penetration and epithelial uptake, contributing to variable local bioavailability. Therefore, future studies should directly evaluate gastrointestinal stability, mucus penetration, epithelial uptake, mucosal retention, and site-specific bioavailability before oral probiotic-derived EV therapy can be advanced toward clinical evaluation. Achieving efficient delivery to inflamed intestinal sites will likely require further optimization, such as encapsulation strategies or surface engineering approaches that enhance mucosal penetration and targeted uptake.98,166

Clinical Implementation

From a clinical perspective, microbiome-directed therapies may be particularly relevant for patients with primary non-response, secondary loss of response, or persistent alterations in the intestinal microbiota despite otherwise adequate inflammatory control.7 Currently, probiotic-derived EVs should be regarded as investigational platforms rather than established therapeutic agents.166 If clinically validated, their potential role would more likely be as gut-directed adjunctive therapies, rather than replacements for approved biologics or small-molecule therapies.199,200 However, optimal target populations, combination strategies, dosing regimens, and clinically meaningful endpoints remain to be defined.190 Although probiotic-derived EV-based therapies have not yet entered clinical evaluation in IBD, early-phase clinical trials have begun to explore other EV-based platforms in ulcerative colitis, including a Phase I study of ExoFlo, a bone marrow MSC-derived extracellular vesicle isolate product, and an early-phase trial of human umbilical cord MSC-derived exosomes.201,202 Recent systematic reviews indicate that EV therapeutics are gradually entering early-phase clinical development across multiple diseases, although important challenges remain regarding manufacturing, characterization, and clinical translation.190,203 Although these studies do not establish the clinical efficacy of probiotic-derived EVs, they provide valuable translational reference points for future trial design, including route of administration, dose selection, safety monitoring, and outcome assessment. Future studies should also determine whether probiotic-derived EVs provide additive benefit when used in combination with existing biologics or small-molecule therapies.

Finally, the regulatory classification and clinical positioning of probiotic-derived EVs remain to be clearly defined.98 These vesicles share features with biologics, microbiome-based therapeutics, and nanomedicines; however, they do not yet fit neatly within existing regulatory frameworks. This ambiguity may complicate their clinical development and underscores the need for clearer regulatory guidance regarding their classification, manufacturing standards, and clinical evaluation.190,204

Importantly, many of the proposed advantages of probiotic-derived EVs remain supported primarily by preclinical evidence, and their clinical efficacy has yet to be established. Addressing challenges related to manufacturing, safety, delivery, and regulatory standardization will be essential for translating these promising platforms into clinical applications for IBD (Figure 3). Conclusion and Future Perspectives

Probiotic-derived EVs have emerged as a cell-free platform for modulating host–microbiota interactions in IBD, with potential advantages in safety, stability, and functional versatility. Accumulating preclinical evidence suggests that these vesicles may contribute to the regulation of intestinal barrier integrity, immune responses, and microbial homeostasis, supporting their potential as a next-generation therapeutic strategy. However, several key challenges remain, including the standardization of isolation and characterization methods, scalable and reproducible manufacturing, pharmacokinetic characterization, safety optimization, and regulatory classification. While preclinical studies suggest multiple advantages, further validation is required to determine whether these benefits can be consistently translated into clinical settings.

Importantly, probiotic-derived EVs may exert their effects through microbiome-related mechanisms that are not fully captured by conventional or cell-derived approaches. As mediators of host–microbe communication, these vesicles may contribute to the modulation of immune responses at the gut interface, potentially through MAMP-associated signaling pathways. In addition, their compatibility with the gastrointestinal environment may support the potential for intestinal delivery and localized therapeutic effects. These features suggest that probiotic-derived EVs represent a mechanistically distinct platform for modulating intestinal homeostasis in IBD. Future efforts should focus on developing robust production platforms, optimizing engineering strategies to enhance targeting and functional specificity, and conducting clinically relevant translational studies to establish efficacy and safety. In particular, a deeper understanding of how probiotic-derived EVs interact with the gut microbiota and host immune system will be critical for their clinical translation.

Collectively, probiotic-derived EVs represent a potentially valuable therapeutic modality within microbiome-informed nanomedicine, with the potential to enable more precise and personalized approaches to IBD management.
