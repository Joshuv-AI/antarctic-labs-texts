Integrative phosphoproteomic analysis reveals co-regulatory phosphorylation networks of rhotekin in cancer progression
Authors: Akhina Palollathil, Althaf Mahin, Athira Perunelly Gopalakrishnan, Alimath Sambreena, Samseera Ummar, Prathik Basthikoppa Shivamurthy, R Ganesan, Manavalan Vijayakumar, Rajesh Raju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRhotekin (RTKN), a Rho GTPase effector, promotes the development of malignancies, including breast, gastric, and colon cancers, by enhancing cell proliferation and migration while inhibiting apoptosis. Despite its oncogenic role, the phosphoregulatory network of RTKN remains largely unexplored, with no experimental evidence on its upstream kinases and functional phosphosites. To characterize RTKN-associated phosphorylation dynamics, PubMed-indexed studies were systematically retrieved using predefined MeSH terms to compile large-scale cellular phosphoproteomics datasets. Analysis of 618 quantitative profiling and 179 differential abundance datasets identified 27 Class-I phosphosites in RTKN. Among these, five sites-Ser106, Ser220, Ser520, Ser529, and Ser543 were consistently observed across multiple datasets, suggesting them as predominant sites with potential functional significance. Structural mapping of predominant sites onto the AlphaFold2-predicted model indicated that these sites are located in accessible regions, highlighting their potential susceptibility to kinase-mediated regulation. As these sites represent understudied phosphosites, a robust strategy was employed to identify their functional role by assessing co-regulated phosphosites on other proteins (PsOPs). ACIN1_Ser243, CTNNA1_Ser641, and SHROOM2_Ser1036 were among the top positively co-regulated PsOPs, whereas MICALL1_Ser644, PRP4K_Ser366, and MYO18A_Ser1970 were negatively co-regulated. PsOPs were mainly involved in apoptosis, cell growth, motility, and cytoskeletal reorganization, suggesting potential functional convergence with RTKN. Additionally, phosphorylation at RTKN Ser520 and Ser543 co-occurred across multiple datasets. Moreover, TRPM7 and PAK4 were identified as predicted upstream kinases phosphorylating RTKN at Ser220 and Ser520. Pathway analysis showed involvement of co-regulated proteins in cancer-associated signaling pathways. These findings provide a foundation for future research to elucidate the phosphosite-specific role of RTKN in cancer.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04982-6.Keywords: Post-translational modifications, Cancer, Nervous system, Phosphorylation, Mass spectrometry, Phosphoproteomics

Introduction

Rhotekin (RTKN) is a Rho GTPase effector protein initially discovered using two-hybrid screening. The name Rhotekin is derived from the Japanese word “teki,” meaning “target,” reflecting its role as a target of the Rho GTPase. In humans, the RTKN gene is located on chromosome 2 and encodes a protein with a molecular weight of 61 kDa [1]. Three isoforms of RTKN (RTKN1/2/3) are produced through alternate splicing. Among these, RTKN1 is the canonical isoform, containing a Rho-binding domain (RBD), a pleckstrin homology (PH) domain, two proline-rich regions, and a C-terminal PDZ domain [2]. The RB domain of RTKN facilitates its interaction with GTP-bound Rho proteins and thereby inhibits the GTPase activity of Rho. Based on these features of the RTKN RB domain, the recombinant protein has been widely used to monitor the activated state of Rho GTPases [3]. Through altering Rho signaling, RTKN influences diverse cellular processes such as cytoskeletal organization, cytokinesis, cell differentiation, cell proliferation, cell adhesion, septin organization, and apoptosis [1, 2, 4, 5]. Protein kinase D2 (PKD2)-mediated phosphorylation of RTKN at Ser435 plays a critical role in the regulation of Rho signaling and actin stress fibre formation [6]. Additionally, upregulation of RTKN was found in the brain, mainly in hippocampal neurons, during developmental stages. It plays a role in promoting neuronal differentiation, survival, and axon growth [7–9]. Using an anti-Rhotekin antibody, Ito et al. observed a distinct developmental stage-specific localization pattern of RTKN in the rat brain [7].

RTKN functions as an oncogene and has been widely associated with cancer development and progression across multiple malignancies, including breast cancer [10], gastric cancer [11], colorectal cancer [12], hepatocellular carcinoma [13], and lung cancer [14–18]. Previous evidence indicates that the interaction of RTKN with the metastasis-associated protein S100A4 results in functional alterations in RhoA and promotes invasive growth in breast cancer [10]. Overexpression of RTKN has been shown to induce Rho-mediated activation of NF-κB signaling through the phosphorylation of IκB by IKKβ, resulting in inhibition of apoptosis and increased resistance to 5-fluorouracil and paclitaxel in gastric cancer [16, 19]. cGK-I has been reported to phosphorylate RTKN at Ser 93, thereby regulating nitrite outgrowth in neuroblastoma cells [9]. Elevated abundance of RTKN activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby enhancing cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while inhibiting apoptosis in breast cancer. This effect is mediated through the upregulation of key proteins, including Wnt3A, β-catenin, C-Myc, and Cyclin D1 [20]. In colon cancer, RTKN promotes tumour cell proliferation and metastatic potential by regulating critical cell cycle proteins such as MCM2, MCM3, MCM5, CDK1, CDK2, and PCNA [17]. Emerging evidence also highlights the post-transcriptional regulation of RTKN by several non-coding RNAs. MicroRNAs such as miR-145, miR-152, and the lncRNA let-7a have been reported to negatively regulate RTKN synthesis, thereby regulating RTKN-driven cancer cell growth, proliferation, and metastasis [14, 15, 21]. These findings underscore the significance of RTKN in the regulation of cancer biology and emphasize its potential as a therapeutic target.

Given its central role in cancer progression, a deeper understanding of the regulatory network of RTKN is essential for uncovering its potential as a molecular target in cancer therapy. Several studies have reported the involvement of RTKN overexpression in regulating the development and progression of various cancers. However, the functional roles of specific RTKN phosphosites in cancer remain largely unexplored. Currently, only a limited number of studies have explored the phosphorylation dynamics, upstream kinases, and interacting partners of RTKN. Phosphoproteomics data generated through mass spectrometry-based approaches provide a comprehensive resource for identifying and quantifying site-specific phosphorylation events. This enables the discovery of both previously known and novel phosphorylations in proteins, thereby offering critical insights into cellular signaling pathways and disease-related alterations [22, 23]. When combined with network analysis, structural modeling, and evolutionary conservation assessments, phosphoproteomics provides a powerful framework to delineate protein-centered signaling networks and uncover regulatory mechanisms underlying disease states [24]. Integrating the complementary omics datasets, including proteomics, microarray, and next-generation sequencing from the same biological system, enables cross-validation of phosphoproteins with corresponding gene- and protein-level expression data, thereby providing deeper insights into the underlying molecular mechanisms [25]. Balbin et al. integrated phosphoproteomics with transcriptomic and proteomic data to identify dysregulated genes, proteins, and pathways associated with mutant KRAS in non-small cell lung cancer cell lines. To address the low overlap among datasets, they developed a scoring metric that enabled the identification of transcripts, proteins, and phosphoproteins showing consistent or unique differential abundance across multiple data layers [26]. Such integrative strategies have been successfully applied to characterize phosphorylation-driven signaling in several oncogenic pathways [27]; however, their application to RTKN-associated phosphoregulatory networks remains limited. Hence, the present study systematically analyzed mass spectrometry-based phosphoproteomic datasets to identify RTKN phosphosites that are differentially regulated across various biological conditions. Furthermore, the co-regulation of these sites with phosphosites on other proteins, including known or predicted interactors and kinases, was investigated. This study provides the first comprehensive analysis of RTKN phosphorylation across multiple cancer types, integrating phosphoproteomic, transcriptomic, structural, and evolutionary approaches. By identifying conserved and functionally relevant phosphosites and linking them to gene, protein, and phosphosite-level expression in cancers, our work advances understanding of RTKN’s role in tumour progression.

Experimental identification of phosphosite-specific functions through independent studies often requires substantial resources and time. In this context, in silico and computational approaches that shortlist functionally relevant phosphosites from publicly available proteomics datasets provide a useful strategy for identifying biologically significant sites. Such candidate phosphosites can subsequently be validated through targeted experimental approaches. The data-driven findings from this study, including cancer-associated RTKN phosphosites, co-regulated binary and complex interactors, and predicted upstream kinases, provide a framework for future experimental studies to validate their functional roles in cancer.

Materials and methods

Screening of phosphoproteomics datasets to assemble RTKN phosphosites

Mass spectrometry-based phosphoproteomics data on human cell lines were retrieved from PubMed using the search query: “phosphoproteomics” OR “phosphoproteome” NOT “Plant” NOT “Review”. The phosphoproteomics studies, which reported Class-I phosphosites on RTKN with a localization probability ≥ 75% or an ambiguity score (A score) > 13, were manually curated. The datasets were categorized based on the protocol used for the enrichment of phosphopeptides (STY/ST/Y phosphosites). The datasets encompassed both phosphoproteomic profiling and differential abundance analyses across multiple experimental conditions. The differentially expressed phosphopeptides exhibiting a fold change of ≥ 1.3 were classified as upregulated, whereas those with a fold change of ≤ 0.76 were considered as downregulated. Statistical significance was determined using a p-value threshold of < 0.05. To ensure consistent mapping of phosphoproteins, protein names were standardized by converting them to their corresponding HGNC gene symbols [28] and UniProt accession numbers [29]. Each dataset was tagged with annotated biological and experimental conditions using a standardized format to enable efficient categorization and downstream analysis [30].

Identification of predominant phosphosites on RTKN across phosphoproteomics datasets

Class-I phosphosites on RTKN, identified from both profiling and differential abundance datasets, were further analyzed to determine the predominant phosphosites. To prioritise these sites, phosphorylation events on RTKN reported across diverse experimental conditions were ranked based on the frequency of their occurrence within specific experimental contexts. Phosphosites identified in over 50% of the profiling and differential abundance datasets were designated as predominant phosphorylation sites. Phosphosites on RTKN identified with the highest frequency were considered for subsequent analysis, and those that were not frequently observed or classified as Class I sites in the phosphoproteomics datasets were excluded [30, 31].

Identification of phosphosites on other proteins co-regulated with predominant sites on RTKN

The phosphosites in other proteins (PsOPs) that are positively or negatively co-regulated with predominant sites on RTKN were identified from the differential abundance datasets. Each predominant phosphorylation site of RTKN was analysed separately to assess its co-regulation with PsOPs. Phosphosites were categorized based on their regulation patterns in relation to the upregulation or downregulation of the predominant phosphosite of RTKN. When both the RTKN site and the associated phosphosites on other proteins were upregulated, they were classified as UU. Cases in which the RTKN site was upregulated, and the associated phosphosites were downregulated, were classified as UD. Conversely, when both the RTKN site and the associated phosphosites were downregulated, they were categorized as DD, while instances where the RTKN site was downregulated, and the associated sites were upregulated, were classified as DU. Further, we grouped the phosphosites exhibiting UU or DD patterns under UUDD, indicating positive co-regulation, while those showing UD or DU patterns were classified as UDDU, representing negative co-regulation. The occurrence of each phosphosite within these co-regulation categories was quantified across differential datasets, and the frequencies were subsequently ranked to identify the most consistently co-regulated sites. PsOPs showing positive and negative co-regulation with predominant sites on RTKN were filtered and compiled separately [30, 32].

To provide the significance of the co-regulation/co-occurrence pattern between the phosphosites on RTKN and PsOPs, a one-sided Fisher’s exact test (FET) was performed using a contingency table, as previously described [31, 33]. The positive (UUDD) and negative (UDDU) co-regulation patterns with a FET p-value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Subsequently, high-confidence co-regulation patterns among the significant (FET p-value) PsOPs were identified by calculating the co-regulation ratio. For positive coregulation, the ratio was defined as ∑(nUU + nDD)/∑(nUD + nDU), and for negative coregulation, it was ∑(nUD + nDU)/∑(nUU + nDD). A ratio exceeding 20% of the total differential frequency of predominant phosphosites was considered indicative of high-confidence coregulation. Further, to eliminate the potential bias arising from the overrepresentation of data from multi-temporal phosphoproteomics studies or repeated use of similar experimental conditions, we introduced additional filtering based on PMID and experimental code (studies involving the same stimuli were grouped under a single experimental code). For each phosphosite, the positive and negative experimental code confidence was calculated as UUDD EXP Code − UDDU EXP and UDDU EXP Code − UUDD EXP Code, respectively. Additionally, the PMID confidence value was determined based on the number of distinct studies (PMIDs) in which a given coregulation pattern was observed. The experimental code confidence threshold of ≥ 3 and PMID confidence of > 3 was applied to filter the highly significant positive and negative coregulation patterns [31, 34].

Analysis of co-occurring phosphosites within RTKN

A previous study by Li et al. (2017) reported that co-occurring phosphorylation sites within a protein are often functionally associated and share similar regulatory mechanisms [33]. To identify the co-occurring RTKN phosphosites, all RTKN phosphosites were retrieved from the differential abundance phosphoproteomics studies and categorized based on the experimental conditions. The frequencies of positive (n_UUDD) and negative (n_UDDU) coregulation among the co-occurring phosphosites were calculated as described above.

Compilation of established protein and phosphosite-specific interactors of RTKN

To understand the interaction landscape of RTKN phosphosites with other proteins, we compiled data from various databases, including IntAct [35], BioGRID [36], HPRD [37], BIND [38], SPIKE [39], and PhosphoPOINT [40].

Assembly of known and predicted kinases of RTKN phosphosites

Experimentally validated kinases for specific RTKN phosphosites were compiled by integrating data from multiple databases, including PhosphoSitePlus [41], Phospho.ELM 9.0 [42], and RegPhos 2.0 [43]. The predicted kinases for RTKN phosphosites were gathered from sources, such as NetworKIN (retrieved on 04.01.2023) [44], AKID (retrieved on 24.05.2023) [45], and iKiP-DB [46]. Additionally, we also included RTKN phosphosite-specific kinases identified by Johnson et al. (2023), with a confidence cutoff of 90%, based on systematic profiling of human kinase-substrate specificity using synthetic peptide libraries [47].

Functional enrichment analysis of co-regulated proteins

To evaluate the biological function of proteins positively and negatively co-regulated with predominant RTKN phosphosites, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) analysis using the Enrichr [48]. To provide a further layer of evidence, phosphosite-specific biological functions of co-regulated proteins were fetched from PhosphoSitePlus [41]. The pathway enrichment analysis was performed by using the Reactome database [49].

Data visualisation

The lollipop plot was generated in RStudio by using the Bioconductor package, trackViewer [50]. The Python package, Matplotlib, was used to display the frequencies of co-differentially regulated PsOPs across differential abundance datasets. A correlation matrix plot was generated using the Seaborn Python package to visualise the co-occurring phosphosites on RTKN. RAWGraph 2.0 was used to create the dendrogram (https://www.rawgraphs.io/). The interaction network of binary and complex interactors of RTKN predominant sites was visualised using Cytoscape [51].

Structural modelling and evolutionary conservation analysis of RTKN phosphorylation sites

The three-dimensional structure of RTKN was obtained using the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/), which provides high-confidence protein structure predictions generated by AlphaFold2. The human RTKN protein sequence (UniProt ID: Q5T2D2) was retrieved, and the corresponding AlphaFold-predicted structural model was downloaded directly from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database.

Multiple sequence alignment of RTKN protein sequences was performed using the Clustal Omega, Multiple Sequence Alignment (MSA) tool to assess evolutionary conservation across species. The RTKN amino acid sequences from human (Homo sapiens), mouse (Mus musculus), and rat (Rattus norvegicus) were retrieved from the UniProt database in FASTA format. These sequences were used as input for Clustal Omega with default alignment parameters. The resulting alignment was examined to identify conserved regions and to evaluate the conservation status of predominant phosphorylation sites identified in the study.

Transcriptomics analysis of RTKN co-regulated genes

To provide an additional layer of biological relevance to the RTKN-associated co-regulated proteins identified in this study, a comparative transcriptomic analysis was performed using publicly available RNA-seq data. The dataset GSE83540 was retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). This dataset was generated from HeLa cells overexpressing RTKN, with RNA sequencing conducted on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (Homo sapiens). Processed gene expression data were obtained from the GEO repository, and genes showing significant differential expression upon RTKN overexpression were extracted based on p-value > 0.05 and log₂ fold-change cutoff of ≥ 0.37 (upregulation) and ≤ − 0.39 (downregulation). These differentially expressed genes were then systematically compared with the list of RTKN co-regulated proteins identified from our phosphoproteomic and network-based analyses. The overlap between the proteomic-derived co-regulated proteins and transcriptomic gene expression data was determined using the InteractiVenn web-based tool (https://www.interactivenn.net/). This integrative analysis enabled the identification of RTKN-associated molecules exhibiting concordant regulation at both the transcriptomic and phosphoproteomic levels, thereby strengthening the functional relevance of the identified RTKN-centered regulatory network in cancer.

Evaluation of clinical significance of RTKN

The gene expression profile of RTKN across multiple cancers was analysed using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA2, version 2) (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/) database. The expression of RTKN in different stages of cancers was assessed by using the University of ALabama at Birmingham CANcer (UALCAN) data analysis Portal. The statistical significance was analysed by performing ANOVA, and a Pr (> F) < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The prognostic significance of modules was assessed by using the Survival Analysis in the GEPIA2 and represented by the Kaplan Meier plot [52]. To generate the overall survival (OS) map, the mRNA expression data in cancers were split into high and low expression groups by setting the group cutoff as the median (cutoff-high and low: 50%). The hazard ratio was calculated using the Cox PH Model with a 95% confidence interval (CI). The log-rank p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Compilation of RTKN phosphorylation sites from phosphoproteomics datasets

Phosphorylation is a pervasive post-translational modification (PTM) in proteins, which regulates protein activity, localization, and biological functions. The recent advances in mass spectrometry-based proteomics have generated many datasets, capturing diverse patterns of protein synthesis and PTMs. In this study, we focused on phosphoproteomics datasets retrieved from PubMed to identify the functionally significant RTKN phosphorylation across various biological and clinical contexts. The first-tier screening of over 3800 phosphoproteomics datasets from PubMed identified 797 datasets related to RTKN phosphorylation for further analysis. Of these, 618 datasets focused on qualitative profiling (Supplementary Table 1A-B), while 179 datasets provided quantitative differential abundance data on RTKN phosphosites (Supplementary Table 2 A-B). The majority of the profiling datasets were derived from HeLa cells, followed by EA.hy926, HCT116, A375, HepG2, and HEK293 cells. The differential datasets were primarily generated from A2058 cells, followed by HeLa, HT-144, H3122, HaCaT, HepG2, and THP-1 cells. The cell lines utilised in the profiling and differential abundance studies are presented in Supplementary Figs. 1 A and 1B. Through systematic analysis, 27 previously characterized phosphosites for RTKN were identified from the profiling datasets (Fig. 1A), of which 24 phosphosites showed differential abundance (Fig. 1B). Among the 27 RTKN phosphosites, four sites were not previously reported in PhosphoSitePlus, including Ser3, Ser497, Ser534, and Ser561. The additionally identified phosphosites were associated with various cancers, such as acute myeloid leukaemia (Ser3), renal cancer (Ser497), lung cancer (Ser497), and colorectal cancer (Ser534 and Ser561). Further studies are required to explore the potential role of these phosphosites in cancer progression. In contrast, two phosphosites on RTKN, at Thr101 and Thr465, which were reported in PhosphoSitePlus, were not detected in our data.

Further, the phosphosites on RTKN were ranked according to their frequency of identification across 618 profiling datasets and 179 differential abundance datasets. This ranking reflects the prevalence of these phosphorylation events across various experimental conditions and highlights the most consistently detected phosphosites, providing insights into their potential functional relevance. The analysis revealed substantial variation in the frequency of RTKN phosphosite identification, with Ser106, Ser220, Ser520, Ser529, and Ser543 emerging as the most frequently detected phosphosites (predominant sites). These predominant sites were identified in 282, 280, 338, 205, and 209 profiling datasets (Fig. 1A) and 49, 54, 63, 53, and 38 differential abundance datasets, respectively (Fig. 1B). One predominant site (Ser106) is found outside, and one site (Ser220) is found within the Anillin domain. Three predominant sites, including Ser520, Ser529, and Ser543, were found on the sequence preceding the PH domain. Hyperphosphorylation of RTKN at Ser529 was found in the highest number of differential datasets (n = 26), in response to stimuli such as hypoxia, metformin, trametinib, and Crizotinib (RTKi), among others. Likewise, hypophosphorylation of RTKN at Ser520 was observed in 41datasets, which was mainly induced by IL33, TNFA, Ang II, RSK1/2-RNAi, and ARS 1620 (KRas-G12Ci), to name a few.

Structural and evolutionary mapping of predominant RTKN phosphorylation sites

The three-dimensional structure of RTKN was generated using AlphaFold2 to gain structural insights into the spatial organization of the predominant phosphorylation sites identified in this study. The predicted model displayed a well-defined folding pattern consistent with the known domain architecture of RTKN. Mapping of the predominant phosphosites onto the AlphaFold2 structure revealed that key serine residues, including S106, S220, S520, S529, and S543, are positioned in structurally accessible regions, suggesting their potential susceptibility to kinase-mediated regulation. The structural localization of these predominant phosphosites may provide a framework for understanding how phosphorylation may influence RTKN conformation, interactions, and downstream signaling (Fig. 1C).

We performed multiple sequence alignment of RTKN protein sequences from human, mouse, and rat to assess the evolutionary conservation of phosphorylation sites identified in our analysis. The alignment revealed that the predominant serine residues, S106, S220, S520, S529, and S543, were fully conserved (100% sequence identity) across all three species. This high degree of conservation suggests that these residues are evolutionarily preserved and may play critical functional roles in RTKN regulation (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Proteins co-regulated with predominant phosphorylation on RTKN

The proteins whose phosphorylation patterns are synchronized with the predominant phosphosites on RTKN were identified from phosphoproteomics datasets by applying stringent quality control criteria. The phosphorylation of other proteins can occur in a coordinated manner with the key phosphorylation events on RTKN, either positively or negatively. This approach helps uncover potential interactions and regulatory pathways that may influence RTKN function and its cellular impact. Downstream analysis identified a total of 154 proteins that exhibit positive co-regulation with the predominant phosphosites on RTKN. These include 54, 44, 62, 3, and 14 phosphoproteins positively co-regulated with RTKN Ser106, Ser220, Ser520, Ser529, and Ser543, respectively (Supplementary Table 3 A-E). In contrast, 9 phosphosites on other proteins displayed negative co-regulation with RTKN phosphosites (Ser106: 1, Ser220: 1, Ser520: 4, Ser529: 1, and Ser543: 2) (Supplementary Table 4 A-E). For instance, the top positively co-regulated phosphoproteins associated with Ser106, Ser220, Ser520, Ser529, and Ser543 predominant sites of RTKN include ACIN1 (Ser243), KIF13B (Ser1381), CTNNA1 (Ser641), SHROOM2 (Ser1036), and MICALL1 (Ser578). Similarly, the top negatively co-regulated phosphoproteins were MICALL1 (Ser644), PRP4K (Ser366), MYO18A (Ser1970), and PPHLN1 (Ser205) (Fig. 2A-E).

Multi-layer validation of RTKN co-regulated proteins using transcriptomics Data

To further strengthen the biological relevance of the RTKN-associated co-regulated proteins, a comparative analysis was performed by integrating our proteomic findings with publicly available RNA-seq data (GSE83540) obtained from the GEO database. In the original transcriptomic study, RNA sequencing was conducted on HeLa cells overexpressing RTKN (test group) and compared against HeLa cells transfected with an empty vector (control group), enabling the identification of RTKN-driven transcriptional alterations. Differentially expressed genes were filtered using a log₂ fold-change cutoff of ≥ 0.37 for upregulated genes and ≤ − 0.39 for downregulated genes, as defined in the original dataset. These transcriptomic alterations were systematically compared with the RTKN co-regulated proteins identified in our analysis. Specifically, upregulated genes were matched with positively co-regulated proteins, while downregulated genes were compared with negatively co-regulated proteins, allowing assessment of concordant regulation across molecular layers. This integrative analysis revealed that 15 out of 154 positively co-regulated proteins from our data exhibited consistent regulatory trends at the transcriptomic level, supporting their potential transcriptional control downstream of RTKN signaling. The genes such as SHROOM2, SHROOM4, IQGAP1, MTBP, TNKS1BP1, TPR, EGFR, SLC6A15, SPTBN1, and SUN2, among others, demonstrated concordant regulation patterns at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2F; Supplementary Table 4 F). SHROOM2 was identified as one of the top positively co-regulated proteins corresponding to the RTKN-predominant phosphorylation site Ser529, underscoring its potential involvement in RTKN-mediated signaling. In addition, several other co-regulated genes identified in this analysis are well-established regulators of cancer-associated processes. For instance, EGFR is a major oncogene that drives cellular proliferation, survival, and invasion across multiple cancer types, including lung cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and glioma [53]. IQGAP1, a multifunctional scaffold protein, regulates MAPK signaling, Rho GTPase activity, and cytoskeletal dynamics, thereby promoting cancer cell migration, invasion, and metastasis [54]. Furthermore, RHBDF1 modulates EGFR ligand shedding and has been implicated in tumour growth and EGFR-driven oncogenic signalling [55]. The overlap between transcriptomic and phosphoproteomic datasets highlights a subset of RTKN-associated molecules that are consistently regulated across expression layers, thereby reinforcing their functional relevance in RTKN-mediated signaling and cancer-associated regulatory networks. In contrast, no overlap was observed between the negatively co-regulated proteins identified in our analysis and the genes that were downregulated upon RTKN overexpression in the RNA-seq dataset.

Co-occurrence of phosphosites within RTKN

Multiple PTMs can occur simultaneously (co-occurrence) within a protein, and such co-occurrence often suggests a functional association and potential regulation by the same kinases [33]. To identify co-occurring phosphosites within RTKN under similar experimental conditions, we performed a co-occurrence analysis. The differential abundance phosphoproteomics data of RTKN were assessed, and phosphosites that exhibit similar abundance (co-upregulated or co-downregulated) were classified as positive co-occurrence, while upregulation of one site when the other site is downregulated (opposite expression) was considered negative co-occurrence. Interestingly, we observed a high positive correlation between the two predominant phosphosites on RTKN, such as Ser520 and Ser543. The co-occurrence of these predominant phosphosites suggests that they may contribute to similar functional roles. These sites were found to be positively co-occurring in six studies and negatively co-occurring in one study, indicating a strong preference for positive co-occurrence. Additionally, the predominant phosphosite in Ser529 also showed a positive co-occurrence with other phosphosites on RTKN, including Ser239 and Ser242 (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 5).

Binary and complex interactors of RTKN

Proteins often execute their biological functions by physically interacting with other proteins, either through direct binding (binary interaction) or by associating with multiple protein partners to form complexes. These interactions are essential for coordinating a wide range of cellular processes, including signal transduction, enzymatic activity, structural organization, and the regulation of gene expression. To understand the interaction landscape of RTKN with other proteins, we compiled data from various databases. The compiled data consists of 86 distinct binary interactors (Supplementary Table 6) and 197 interacting proteins that are components of three separate multi-protein complexes involving RTKN (Supplementary Table 7). Further, we analysed the co-regulation of phosphosites on interacting partners with the RTKN predominant phosphosite. Among the 86 binary interactors, phosphosites on 6 proteins showed a positive regulation with RTKN Ser106, Ser220, and Ser520. Similarly, phosphosites on other proteins involved in 2 complexes showed a positive co-regulation with RTKN Ser106, Ser220, and Ser520 (Fig. 6). Negative co-regulation of RTKN phosphosites with phosphosites on binary and complex interacting partners was not found in the current study. Binary interactors of RTKN Ser520 with positively co-regulated phosphosites include FOXO3 (Ser253), which is involved in apoptosis and cell motility [56, 57], and PAK4 (Ser181), which regulates cell motility [58]. One of the binary interactors of RTKN Ser220 is KIF13B; its phosphorylation at Ser1381 triggers cytoskeletal reorganization [59]. RTKN is known to regulate cell motility [5], apoptosis [60], and cytoskeletal organization [10]; however, phosphosites involved in the regulation of these were not yet identified. The findings from this study suggest that the predominant phosphosites, including Ser520 and Ser220, may play a role in regulating these biological functions. This highlights the need for further studies to understand the phosphosite-specific functions of RTKN in apoptosis, cell motility, and cytoskeletal organization, as these functions are associated with the development and progression of various cancers [16, 61, 62].

Upstream kinases involved in the phosphorylation of RTKN predominant sites

Upstream kinases play a fundamental role in regulating the activity of target proteins through the process of phosphorylation. Phosphorylation by upstream kinases often results in conformational changes in target proteins, thereby altering their activity. Phosphorylation can create or disrupt protein-protein interactions, facilitate protein localization to specific cellular compartments, or enhance/decrease the enzymatic activity of the target protein. By catalysing the transfer of phosphate groups from ATP to specific amino acids, primarily serine, threonine, or tyrosine residues, upstream kinases modulate the conformation, stability, and functional properties of their target proteins. Currently, the upstream kinases responsible for regulating RTKN phosphorylation remain unidentified. From our analysis, we identified 10 kinases that are positively co-regulated with predominant phosphosites in RTKN, including TRPM7, EGFR, EEF2K, and PAK4, to name a few. Among the 10 kinases, two kinases, such as TRPM7 and PAK4, were predicted to be the upstream kinases of RTKN (Fig. 7A). Interestingly, both of these kinases exhibited a positive co-regulation with RTKN. TRPM7 (Ser1504) shows the potential to phosphorylate RTKN at Ser220, as evidenced by the data from Johnson et al. 2023 [47]. However, the functional role of Ser1504 phosphorylation in TRPM7 is not known. Likewise, the predicted upstream kinases reported in NetworKIN revealed that PAK4 with Ser181 phosphorylation is capable of phosphorylating RTKN at Ser520. Moreover, a previous study reported that the phosphorylation of PAK4 at Ser181 by PKCs or PKDs can induce its kinase activity [58]. Given that RTKN is positively co-regulated with PAK4 and the phosphorylation at Ser181 on PAK4 is known to activate its kinase activity, we propose that PAK4 may serve as a candidate upstream regulator of RTKN.

Potential upstream kinases of the predominant phosphosites of RTKN. A The information on upstream kinases obtained from Johnson et al. 2023 [37] and public databases. Phosphorylation level abundance correlation of PAK4 Ser181 and RTKN Ser520 in B breast cancer and C brain cancer

Further, we examined the expression correlation between phosphosites on predicted upstream kinases and RTKN phosphosites across various cancers using data from the cProSite database. Interestingly, both PAK4 Ser181 and RTKN Ser520 exhibited upregulation in breast cancer samples compared to normal tissue, suggesting a potential phospho-dependent regulatory relationship in oncogenic signaling (Fig. 7B). A modest level of correlation was also observed in other cancer types, such as brain cancer, although interpretation is limited due to insufficient expression data from corresponding normal tissues (Fig. 7C). Additionally, phosphosite-specific information for TRPM7 Ser1504 was not available in the cProSite database for any cancer condition, highlighting a gap in current phosphoproteomic data and the need for further experimental validation.

Inferring the pathways modulated by proteins co-regulated with RTKN

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed on both positively and negatively co-regulated proteins using the Reactome database. Analysis revealed the enrichment of positively co-regulated proteins in pathways associated with the RHOA GTPase cycle, apoptotic execution phase, mRNA 3’-end processing, nervous system development, signaling by ALK in cancer, and PI3K/AKT signaling in cancer. The negatively co-regulated proteins showed involvement in the RHOA GTPase cycle, mRNA 3’-end processing, mRNA splicing, and the apoptotic execution phase, among others (Fig. 8). The pathway analysis highlights those proteins co-regulated with predominant phosphosites on RTKN are also involved in similar functions as regulated RTKN, including Rho signaling, neuron development, and apoptosis [8, 10, 12]. Our analysis identified co-regulated phosphosites in proteins involved in these pathways. However, the direct regulatory information on these co-regulated phosphosites on proteins with RTKN is currently not known. Hence, future studies are essential to understand the functional implications of the co-regulated phosphosites in other proteins in the context of RTKN.

Reactome pathway analysis of proteins co-regulated with the predominant RTKN sites. Positively and negatively co-regulated PsOPs within each signaling pathway are highlighted in different colours

Gene, protein and phosphosite-level profiling of RTKN across human cancers

To assess the gene-level expression profile of RTKN across multiple cancer types, pan-cancer analysis was performed using the GEPIA2 database. This analysis revealed significant differences in RTKN expression between tumour tissues and their corresponding normal counterparts across diverse malignancies. RTKN expression was markedly elevated in Bladder Urothelial Carcinoma (BLCA), Colon Adenocarcinoma (COAD), Lymphoid Neoplasm Diffuse Large B-cell Lymphoma (DLBC), Esophageal Carcinoma (ESCA), Glioblastoma Multiforme (GBM), Head and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma (HNSC), Brain Lower Grade Glioma (LGG), Ovarian Serous Cystadenocarcinoma (OV), Rectum Adenocarcinoma (READ), Stomach Adenocarcinoma (STAD), Testicular Germ Cell Tumors (TGCT), Thymoma (THYM), Uterine Corpus Endometrial Carcinoma (UCEC), and Uterine Carcinosarcoma (UCS). In contrast, RTKN expression was significantly reduced in Kidney Chromophobe (KICH) when compared with normal kidney tissues (Fig. 9A).

RTKN Gene expression pattern in various tumour and normal tissue. A mRNA level expression profile of RTKN in pan-cancers. Cancer stage wise gene expression of RTKN in B BLCA, C HNSC, D UCEC, and E KICH

We utilised the cProSite database to systematically analyse the protein-level expression of RTKN across different cancer types [63]. This analysis revealed elevated RTKN protein abundance in breast cancer, colon cancer, head and neck cancer, lung cancer, and uterine cancer (Supplementary Fig. 3 A). In addition to total protein expression, we further explored the phosphosite-level expression of RTKN across cancers. The predominant RTKN phosphorylation sites, including Ser106, Ser520, and Ser543, exhibited increased abundance in breast cancer, lung cancer, and ovarian cancer (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Together, these findings indicate that both RTKN protein expression and site-specific phosphorylation are dysregulated in multiple malignancies, supporting a potential role for RTKN phosphorylation in cancer-associated signaling pathways.

Correlation of RTKN with clinical stages and survival

To further investigate the clinical relevance of RTKN, we evaluated its stage-wise gene expression patterns in cancer types where RTKN exhibited significant differential expression between tumour and normal tissues. Stage-wise analysis revealed a progressive and significant upregulation of RTKN expression across multiple clinical stages in BLCA, HNSC, and UCEC. These findings suggest a potential association between elevated RTKN expression and tumour progression in these malignancies. In contrast, KICH displayed a stage-wise negative regulation of RTKN expression, with reduced expression observed across advancing tumour stages (Fig. 9B-E). The statistical significance of the stage-wise comparisons is provided in Supplementary Table 8.

A Kaplan-Meier survival analysis for overall survival was performed using the GEPIA2 database to evaluate the prognostic significance of RTKN expression. Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on RTKN transcript levels. The analysis revealed that higher RTKN expression was significantly associated with improved overall survival in patients with LGG, suggesting a potential protective role of RTKN in this cancer type. Specifically, elevated RTKN expression correlated with a reduced risk of mortality, as reflected by a hazard ratio for the high-expression group of 0.63 (log-rank p = 0.0097; p(HR) = 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 4). These findings indicate that RTKN expression may serve as a favourable prognostic indicator in LGG. In contrast, no statistically significant association between RTKN expression and overall survival was detected in the other cancer types analysed.

Discussion

Phosphorylation is a well-established post-translational modification that plays a crucial role in regulating cellular signaling pathways and essential biological processes. To date, over 230,000 phosphorylation sites have been identified in humans, largely due to advancements in mass spectrometry-based phosphoproteomics techniques [23, 64]. Despite this progress, the functional characterization of individual phosphosites remains a significant challenge and continues to demand extensive research efforts. In this study, we aimed to uncover the co-regulatory phosphorylation patterns associated with RTKN using multiple phosphoproteomics datasets derived from diverse biological and experimental contexts. This integrative approach was adopted to capture reproducible and recurring associations in independent studies, which may reflect fundamental functionalities of RTKN. By focusing on phosphosites consistently observed in multiple datasets and applying stringent statistical thresholds, the present study identified several PsOPs that are co-regulated with RTKN. These patterns provide a comprehensive overview of potential functional interactions. However, their occurrence in specific physiological or pathological contexts will require targeted experimental validation.

RTKN is a GTPase inhibitor primarily involved in the regulation of Rho signaling by interacting specifically with the GTP-bound forms of RhoA and RhoC. RTKN is expressed across multiple tissues, including the brain, lung, kidney, and muscle, and has also been reported to exhibit elevated expression in gastric cancer cells, suggesting both tissue-specific physiological roles and potential involvement in tumour-associated processes [16]. RTKN has been implicated as an important regulator of neuronal development, with evidence supporting its involvement during both embryonic and postnatal stages of brain maturation [7, 65]. An in vitro study revealed that RTKN is necessary for neuronal survival and is also involved in neuronal differentiation and neurite outgrowth [8]. At present, no specific RTKN phosphorylation site has been experimentally validated as directly mediating its role in neuronal development, highlighting the need for future studies to delineate the contribution of site-specific phosphorylation to RTKN-dependent neuronal functions.

Recently, increased expression of RTKN has been implicated in multiple cancers, including lung cancer, gastric cancer, colon cancer, and breast cancer [15, 16, 18, 21]. RTKN promotes cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis by regulating NF-κB and Wnt signaling pathways [16, 20]. RTKN is a substrate of protein kinase D (PKD), which phosphorylates RTKN at S435. This phosphorylation promotes RhoA anchoring at the plasma membrane, enhancing stress fibre formation [6]. Currently, there are no studies directly investigating how phosphorylation on RTKN sites affects pathway-specific regulation, including potential impacts on protein interactions, subcellular localization, or downstream signaling, highlighting the need for future experimental validation. In the present study, we aimed to identify the phosphoregulatory network of RTKN in various biological contexts. For this purpose, we utilized mass spectrometry-based quantitative profiling and differential cellular phosphoproteomic data to identify PTM level alterations in RTKN. By analysing the 618 profiling and 179 differential abundance phosphoproteome datasets, we identified 27 phosphosites on RTKN that were previously characterized. Among these, five phosphosites, including Ser106, Ser220, Ser520, and Ser529, were predominantly expressed. These phosphorylation events may not occur simultaneously on a single RTKN molecule but instead in different RTKN variants (proteoforms), each potentially contributing to distinct regulatory roles. Currently, the biological functions regulated by these phosphosites are unknown.

To understand interactors and kinases of predominant RTKN phosphosites, we assessed the PsOPs that are positively and negatively co-differentially regulated with RTKN. From the analysis, we identified several PsOPs that are positively co-regulated (such as ACIN1 (Ser243), KIF13B (Ser1381), CTNNA1 (Ser641), SHROOM2 (Ser1036), and MICALL1 (Ser578)) and negatively co-regulated (such as MICALL1 (Ser644), PRP4K (Ser366), and MYO18A (Ser1970)) with RTKN predominant sites. The phosphosite-specific biological functions of these co-regulated proteins are poorly understood [66]; however, several studies have reported the vital cellular functions regulated by them. ACIN1 is a nuclear protein recognized to be involved in apoptosis and RNA splicing [67, 68], and also shows increased expression in platelets of lung cancer patients. KIF13B is a microtubule-associated motor protein involved in the trafficking of vesicles and organelles. Phosphorylation at Ser1381 of KIF13B plays a crucial role in regulating axon formation [59]. CTNNA1 is another positively co-regulated protein, which is a key component of the cadherin-catenin complex at adherens junctions. Par1b-mediated phosphorylation of CTNNA1 at Ser641 is essential to promote cell proliferation and migration during wound healing [69]. Additionally, the differential expression of CTNNA1 was also found to be involved in the invasion and metastasis in various cancers, including lung cancer, breast cancer, and gastric cancer [70, 71]. SHROOM2 acts as a critical mediator in RHOA signaling, contributing to actin filament organization and regulation of cell migration. While the functional role of SHROOM2 phosphorylation remains undefined, protein level downregulation has been associated with enhanced cell migration and invasion in nasopharyngeal carcinoma [72]. Phosphosites on MICALL1 showed both positive and negative co-regulation with RTKN. MICALL1 was identified to be involved in endocytic recycling [73]. The differential expression of MICALL1 promotes infiltration of macrophages and Th2 cells in hepatocellular carcinoma [74] and regulates Alzheimer’s disease pathology [75]. For the following negatively co-regulated proteins, the phosphosite-specific biological functions were not available in PhosphoSitePlus [66]. PRP4K is a tumour suppressor gene, and its reduced expression was known to be associated with the development of breast cancer, ovarian cancer, and cervical cancer [76]. Moreover, PRP4K regulates mRNA splicing by activating the spliceosomal B complex and also regulates cell division [76, 77]. MYO18A is an unconventional myosin involved in the organization of the cytoskeleton, cell adhesion, and cell migration [78, 79]. Altered expression of MYO18A is reported in cholangiocarcinoma [80], acute myeloid leukaemia [81], and colorectal cancer [82]. These findings suggest the potential role of RTKN in regulating key cellular processes such as apoptosis, cytoskeletal organization, vesicle trafficking, and gene regulation through site-specific phosphorylation events. Furthermore, pathway analysis of PsOPs revealed enrichment in pathways such as Rho signaling, neuronal development, cell motility, and apoptosis, which are consistent with the known functions of RTKN. Interestingly, the analysis also uncovered additional pathways not previously well-explored with RTKN, including mRNA 3’-end processing and mRNA splicing. Phosphosite-specific co-regulation of RTKN with other proteins underscores its potential role in cancer progression.

The positively co-regulated proteins were found to be associated with pathways related to RHOA GTPase cycle, nervous system development, and PI3K/AKT signaling in cancer. Given that RTKN is a well-established effector of RHOA, the strong association with the RHOA GTPase cycle is biologically coherent and supports the validity of the identified co-regulated network [10]. Pathways related to nervous system development further highlight the context-dependent functions of RTKN, particularly given the established roles of RHO GTPases in neuronal differentiation, axon guidance, and synaptic plasticity [8]. PI3K/AKT pathways has been reported to promote tumor cell survival and proliferation [83], hence, the crosstalk between RHOA–RTKN signaling and PI3K/AKT pathways may synergistically enhance oncogenic processes. The negatively co-regulated proteins were associated with pathways of mRNA 3’-end pocessing, aand mRNA splicing. mRNA splicing and 3’-end processing are critical for generating mature, translatable mRNAs and for maintaining transcriptome integrity, and dysregulation of these processes has been increasingly associated with various pathological conditions, including cancer [84, 85].

To explore the potential functional relevance of the predominant RTKN phosphosites, we mapped Ser220, Ser520, Ser529, and Ser543 onto the AlphaFold2-predicted three-dimensional structure of RTKN. These residues are located in structurally accessible regions, suggesting they may influence local conformational dynamics or modulate interaction surfaces. Multiple sequence alignment across human, mouse, and rat revealed that these sites are highly conserved, indicating potential functional importance.

The clinical analysis highlighted the cancer-specific and stage-dependent regulation of RTKN expression in several cancer types. Increased expression of RTKN was observed in stomach adenocarcinoma, colon adenocarcinoma, and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, among others. Previous reports have shown that RTKN is overexpressed in gastric cancer and its downregulation reduces cell proliferation and tumour growth while promoting apoptosis [11]. Likewise, elevated RTKN expression has been reported in colon and lung cancers, where RTKN silencing suppresses cell proliferation, migration, and invasion and induces apoptosis [17, 18]. Stage-wise evaluation revealed a progressive increase in RTKN expression across clinical stages in BLCA, HNSC, and UCEC, suggesting that elevated RTKN levels may be associated with tumour advancement in these malignancies. Consistent with our findings, mRNA expression analysis of RTKN in bladder cancer and adjacent non-cancerous tissue samples has demonstrated that RTKN is involved in the development and progression of Chinese bladder cancer patients [86]. In contrast, the stage-wise reduction in RTKN expression observed in KICH indicates that the RTKN regulation may be context-dependent across cancer types. The stage-specific analysis of RTKN expression in clinical samples from other cancers remains to be investigated. Survival analysis further indicated that higher RTKN expression is associated with improved overall survival in patients with LGG. In line with this, RTKN expression has been previously reported to be associated with poor prognosis in gastric cancer [11]. These findings highlight the potential clinical relevance of RTKN expression as a cancer marker, although the underlying molecular mechanisms of RTKN phosphorylation to disease progression remain to be further investigated. Collectively, these observations suggest that phosphorylation at these serine residues could contribute to pathway-specific regulation of RTKN by altering its conformation, accessibility, or ability to interact with downstream signaling partners.

Limitations and future outlook

The present study utilised previously published phosphoproteomics datasets to explore the phosphoregulatory network of RTKN, and therefore has certain limitations. As these datasets were compiled from multiple studies, variability exists in experimental protocols, cell types, biological contexts, sample sizes, and data quality. While our approach provides a global view of RTKN phosphorylation, it does not distinguish between proteoform-specific modifications. The findings of this study are entirely data-driven and therefore require further experimental validation to confirm the phosphoregulatory network. This study focused specifically on phosphorylation since it represents a major regulatory mechanism of RTKN activity and signaling. While other post-translational modifications, such as ubiquitination or acetylation, may also influence RTKN function, these were beyond the scope of the current analysis and warrant investigation in future studies. As phosphoproteomic datasets contain inherent cellular heterogeneity, the interpretation of RTKN phosphorylation patterns in specific cancers may be affected. Therefore, the predominant RTKN phosphosites identified in this study require further validation using clinical samples to establish their relevance across cancer types. Finally, the functional role of RTKN phosphosites discussed in this study are potential inference based on phosphosite co-regulation patterns rather than direct mechanistic evidence. Hence, these observations provide a framework for future experimental validation to determine the specific functional roles of RTKN phosphorylation and the identification of its upstream kinases using advanced approaches, including site-directed mutagenesis, kinase–substrate assays, single-cell phosphoproteomics, co-immunoprecipitation, and CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout.

Conclusions

While advances in mass spectrometry-based phosphoproteomics have enabled the large-scale identification of thousands of phosphorylation sites, understanding their biological relevance remains a significant challenge. Many phosphosites may be transient, context-dependent, or functionally redundant, making it difficult to distinguish regulatory modifications from non-functional ones. In the present study, we utilized phosphoproteomics data generated through mass spectrometry-based analysis to investigate context-specific phosphoregulation in RTKN. Our analysis identified five predominant phosphosites in RTKN, including Ser106, Ser220, Ser520, Ser529, and Ser543. The proteins co-regulated with RTKN exhibited involvement in apoptosis, cell growth, motility, and cytoskeletal reorganization. Additionally, we identified candidate upstream kinases capable of phosphorylating RTKN predominant sites. Pathway analysis of co-regulated proteins revealed their enrichment in cancer-associated pathways. In conclusion, the findings shed light on the phosphosite-specific functionalities of RTKN and its co-regulated partners in various biological contexts. However, future studies are essential to experimentally validate the functional significance of these RTKN phosphosites and their role as a therapeutic target in cancer treatment.
