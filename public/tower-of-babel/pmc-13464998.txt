A competency-based curriculum for geriatric dentistry in undergraduate and postgraduate education : a scoping review
Authors: Junhewk Kim, So Yeon Kim, Hoi-In Jung, Jeong-Hyun Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rapid rise in the number of older adults, including frail and dependent individuals, has increased demand for medical and dental care. This study aimed to develop a competency-based framework for undergraduate and postgraduate geriatric dental education by identifying key themes and corresponding competencies through a scoping review.MethodsA scoping review was conducted using Ovid-MEDLINE, Ovid-EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library, including studies published from January 1, 2010 to June 13, 2025, and following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Scoping Reviews. Eligible studies were screened in two stages, consisting of title/abstract screening and full-text review, and were analyzed using thematic synthesis to identify curricular domains and derive a structured competency framework.ResultsThe initial database search yielded 1403 articles, of which 42 met the inclusion criteria after full-text review. Thematic synthesis identified four main categories of geriatric dentistry curricula: (1) existing curricula worldwide; (2) educational themes and competencies; (3) teaching and learning methods; and (4) postgraduate and continuing education curricula. Across these areas, 8 major themes were identified, informing the development of a competency framework comprising 6 core competencies, 34 sub-competencies, and 72 enabling competencies. These competencies encompassed aging-related physical, cognitive, and psychosocial changes, oral health management, interdisciplinary communication, ethical and legal considerations, and clinical practice in outreach settings.ConclusionThis study proposes an integrated, staged competency-based framework for geriatric dental education that bridges foundational undergraduate learning and more advanced postgraduate training. By organizing the literature into major themes and corresponding competency domains, the framework provides a practical reference for curriculum planning and revision in response to population aging and the increasing complexity of geriatric oral healthcare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09625-6.

Introduction

As the global population ages at an unprecedented pace, numerous societal challenges have emerged, including the increasing prevalence of frailty and dependence among older adults, as well as growing demand for medical and dental care. Several countries, including Japan, South Korea, Italy, and Germany, already have entered super-aged societies, where over 20% of the total population is aged 65 or older [1]. Aging brings complex biological changes, such as physiological, neuromuscular, immunological, and endocrinological alterations. Promoting healthy aging and maintaining functional independence require a comprehensive understanding of the complex interplay among age-related physiological changes and identifying risk factors for frailty, dependence, and mortality.

Prior research has emphasized the pivotal role of oral health and function in shaping overall health outcomes for older adults [2–11]. Common oral health issues in older adults, including chewing and swallowing difficulties or oral dryness, can alter food choices and contribute to malnutrition and loss of bone and skeletal muscle mass [12–16]. In addition, reduced dentition and dry mouth are associated with physical frailty, increased dependence, and even mortality [12, 17]. Given the substantial impact of oral health-related factors on the quality of life and general well-being in older populations, integrating the principles of geriatric dentistry into dental education is imperative. Dental students should gain a comprehensive understanding of the multifaceted aspects of aging and the bidirectional relationship between oral and systemic health. Accordingly, efforts have been made to design dental curricula reflecting these needs [18, 19]. With the rapid growth of the older population and ongoing societal shifts, such as the rise of frail and dependent older adults with multicomorbidity, continuous revision of dental education and related competencies is essential in an aged society.

In competency-based education, emphasis is placed not only on knowledge acquisition but also on the progressive attainment of abilities required for professional practice [20]. The American Dental Education Association defines competency as a complex behavior or ability required for general dentists to begin independent and unsupervised dental practice [18]. In this framework, educational aims are organized around demonstrable abilities and measurable outcomes rather than time spent in the classroom alone, thereby improving the preparation of dental graduates [20]. Because competency encompasses not only knowledge acquisition but also students’ clinical experience, critical thinking, problem-solving, professionalism, and technical and procedural skills, it provides a more reliable measure of the quality and preparedness of dental graduates [21]. Within the broader landscape of competency-based education, several established frameworks provide conceptual foundations for structuring educational progression. Miller’s pyramid of clinical competence describes ascending levels from “knows” (factual knowledge) through “knows how” (applied knowledge), “shows how” (demonstration of competence), to “does” (independent performance in practice) [22]. Similarly, Bloom’s taxonomy organizes cognitive learning objectives in a hierarchy from knowledge recall and comprehension to analysis, synthesis, and evaluation. These frameworks are particularly relevant to geriatric dentistry education, as they provide a basis for differentiating competency expectations across educational stages: undergraduate programs may primarily target the foundational levels of knowing and applying knowledge, whereas postgraduate programs should aim for higher levels of demonstrated performance and independent clinical practice.

This perspective informed the present review by guiding both the identification of curricular themes and their translation into a structured hierarchy of competencies. In this study, competency refers to the integrated knowledge, skills, and professional attitudes required for effective geriatric dental practice. Curriculum refers to the organized structure and content of an educational program, including the subjects and learning experiences provided within a course of study. Learning outcomes, by contrast, are measurable and learner-centered statements that describe what learners are expected to know, understand, and demonstrate upon completion of an educational activity. Accordingly, the present review primarily aimed to propose themes and competency domains for geriatric dental education, while recognizing that specific learning outcomes may require further contextual refinement according to educational level, institutional setting, and assessment purpose.

Although previous studies have described geriatric dentistry curricula in individual countries or regions [23–28], existing reports remain heterogeneous in scope and are often descriptive rather than explicitly competency-based. In addition, few attempts have been made to integrate undergraduate and postgraduate educational needs into a single staged framework that reflects contemporary challenges such as frailty, multimorbidity, outreach care, and population aging. Therefore, this scoping review aimed to identify key themes and corresponding competencies in geriatric dentistry and to propose an integrated competency-based framework for undergraduate and postgraduate dental education in aged societies.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

The present scoping review was conducted through a comprehensive literature search using three electronic databases: Ovid-MEDLINE, Ovid-EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library. The review question was developed using the PCC (Population, Concept, Context) framework. The population included undergraduate dental students and postgraduate dental trainees; the concept concerned competency-based geriatric dentistry curricula and educational frameworks; and the context encompassed global dental education settings, including academic institutions and outreach care environments. Articles published between January 1, 2010 and June 13, 2025, were included. In addition, reference lists from relevant scoping, narrative, and systematic reviews, as well as original articles, were also manually screened to identify additional relevant literature.

The scoping review methodology was selected because the geriatric dentistry education literature is characterized by heterogeneous study designs and limited quantitative data, making it unsuitable for formal systematic review or meta-analysis. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) was used to guide reporting of the present study [29].

Multiple keywords and MeSH terms related to geriatric dentistry, students, aging, older adults, education, curriculum, and teaching were applied in the electronic search. Detailed search strategies for each electronic database are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Additionally, references of all relevant articles were manually reviewed to identify further eligible publications. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers for potential eligibility (JK and SYK). Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, with final adjudication by the corresponding author.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included in this review if they met the following criteria: (a) addressed curricula related to geriatric dentistry worldwide; (b) examined issues related to aging, oral health, and dental treatment; (c) covered relevant legal and policy issues concerning older adults in aged societies; (d) examined competencies required of general dentists in aged societies; (e) addressed educational content, as well as teaching and learning methods, in geriatric dentistry; and (f) focused on themes or competencies related to geriatric dentistry that are necessary for professional dental practice. Only peer-reviewed articles published in English were included. Studies were excluded if they were case reports or case series, not targeted toward dental students or postgraduate dental trainees, published in languages other than English, or duplicate publications.

Data extraction and evidence synthesis

A pilot data extraction was conducted to improve consistency and standardization in the data extraction process. Two independent reviewers extracted data using a predefined standardized form. The extracted information included: (1) study characteristics, including authors, publication year, country, and study design; (2) participant characteristics, such as faculty members, undergraduate students, or postgraduate trainees; (3) data collection methods; (4) study aims; (5) key findings, including reported themes, competencies, teaching methods, and curricular features; and (6) funding sources, when available. Any disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved by rechecking the extracted data and through further consultation with the corresponding author.

A thematic synthesis approach was used to analyze the included studies. Relevant data related to curricular content, educational themes, teaching methods, assessment-related descriptions, and competency-related statements were reviewed. Initial coding was conducted using a primarily inductive and iterative approach, whereby recurrent statements related to curricular topics, competencies, and educational approaches were identified, compared, and progressively grouped into broader thematic categories through discussion among the reviewers. Similar codes and themes were grouped into broader thematic categories through iterative comparison and discussion among the reviewers. Overlapping or outdated topics were merged or refined to improve conceptual clarity and educational relevance. The resulting themes were then mapped to corresponding competency domains, which were hierarchically organized into core, sub-, and enabling competencies. This process was intended to generate an integrated competency-based framework applicable to both undergraduate and postgraduate geriatric dental education.

Results

Literature review

Bibliographic search and literature selection

In total, 1403 articles were identified through the initial database search after duplicate removal. Following title and abstract screening, 162 articles were selected for full-text review. Of these, 120 were excluded based on eligibility criteria, leaving 42 studies for final qualitative analysis. Thematic synthesis identified four major categories related to geriatric dentistry curricula: (1) existing curricula worldwide; (2) educational themes and competencies; (3) teaching and learning methods; and (4) postgraduate and continuing education curricula (Fig. 1).

A total of 27 studies examined the geriatric dentistry curricula across countries including the USA, Canada, India, South Korea, Japan, Australia, Germany, Switzerland, the UK, Malaysia, and several Latin American nations. When multiple studies covered overlapping or consecutive periods within the same country, only the most recent publications were included to avoid redundancy and ensure up-to-date information. Consequently, 18 articles were selected for review, including 15 original research articles and 3 review articles. Regionally, studies were conducted in Europe (n = 4), North America (n = 3), South America (n = 2), Asia (n = 3), Australia (n = 4), and across multiple continents (n = 2). Among the original articles, 10 surveyed dental faculty members, 2 focused on dental students, and 1 included both groups. One study utilized bibliographical data and another relied on an online search for local information (Table 1).

Until the early 2010s, geriatric dentistry curricula were generally categorized under the broader field of special care dentistry. However, from the mid-2010s onward, studies increasingly recognized geriatric dentistry as a distinct domain, separate from special care dentistry. Collectively, these studies indicate that geriatric dentistry is increasingly recognized as a distinct educational domain rather than merely a component of special care dentistry. However, curricular implementation remains heterogeneous across regions, and nationally standardized competency-based frameworks are still limited. Notably, regional differences in curricular emphasis were observed. European dental schools, guided by the European College of Gerodontology curriculum [19], tended to incorporate structured didactic and clinical components, although implementation varied widely across countries [26, 27, 32]. North American programs, particularly in the USA, had the longest history of geriatric dentistry education and the widest variety of curricular formats, yet clinical training for frail older adults remained limited [28, 37]. In Asia, geriatric dentistry was emerging as a recognized discipline but generally lacked standardized national curricula, with the exception of Japan’s established postgraduate infrastructure [23, 38, 39]. South American and Australian programs were at earlier stages of development, with limited resources, workforce shortages, and the absence of dedicated faculty identified as major barriers [30, 35, 36].

Emerging themes and competencies in geriatric oral health care

Ten studies addressed emerging issues in oral health care for older adults and competencies required of general dentists or professional in an aged society. Of these, seven were original research articles and three were review articles. Two studies demonstrated the need for comprehensive knowledge of aging and dental treatment skills for geriatric care, whereas another two studies emphasized the interprofessional collaboration, including pharmacy and geriatric medicines. Notably, five studies underscored the importance of incorporating clinical training program, particularly in long-term care facilities setting, into undergraduate curricula (Table 2). These findings suggest that contemporary geriatric dental education is evolving beyond traditional restorative care in clinical setting toward a broader focus on frailty, oral function, multimorbidity, interprofessional collaboration, and care in long-term care settings.

Proposed curriculum themes and competencies for undergraduate and postgraduate education in geriatric dentistry

Identification of themes in geriatric dentistry curricula

A comprehensive review identified eight major thematic categories relevant to geriatric dentistry curricula: (1) public health issues; (2) aging and age-related changes; (3) geriatrics and geriatric dentistry; (4) aging-related oral and maxillofacial alterations; (5) pharmacy; (6) dental practice; (7) communication; and (8) ethical and legal issues (Table 5). Together, these categories reflect the broad range of knowledge, clinical skills, and professional attitudes required for the care of older adults. They encompass not only biological and functional changes associated with aging, but also oral disease management, pharmacologic considerations, care delivery in outreach settings, interprofessional communication, and ethical and legal decision-making.

Themes identified in Geriatric Dentistry Education and Curriculum

Proposed competencies in geriatric dentistry curricula

For educational application, these eight thematic categories were subsequently consolidated into six broader competency domains by grouping conceptually related themes and reducing overlap between content areas. Specifically, categories 1 (public health issues) and 2 (aging and aging-related changes) were merged into core competency 1, as both address foundational knowledge of demographic trends and age-related changes that form the contextual basis for all subsequent competencies. Category 3 (geriatrics and geriatric dentistry) formed core competency 2 as a standalone domain, given its distinct focus on geriatric syndromes, frailty, and assessment tools. Category 4 (aging and oral and maxillofacial alterations) constituted core competency 3, representing the clinical dental knowledge domain. Category 5 (pharmacy) was designated as core competency 4, reflecting its specialized pharmacological content. Category 6 (dental practice) became core competency 5, encompassing the practical organization and delivery of geriatric care. Finally, categories 7 (communication) and 8 (ethical and legal issues) were combined into core competency 6, as effective communication and ethical/legal reasoning are closely interrelated in the management of older patients, particularly those with cognitive impairment or diminished decision-making capacity. This theme-to-competency mapping was intended to improve the usability of the framework for curriculum design and assessment planning.

In total, 6 core competencies, 34 sub-competencies, and 72 enabling competencies, were identified. In this framework, core competencies represent general domains of knowledge, skills, and attitudes required in geriatric dentistry. Sub-competencies define more focused capabilities within each core competency. Enabling competencies describe detailed and measurable abilities or tasks that demonstrate mastery of the sub-competencies in practical settings. This three-level hierarchical structure was adopted to facilitate curriculum design at different levels of specificity: core competencies guide broad curricular goals and program-level planning, sub-competencies inform the design of individual courses or modules, and enabling competencies provide the basis for formulating specific, measurable learning outcomes and assessment criteria. This organization allows educators to translate the framework into actionable curricular content at each institutional level.

Postgraduate and continuing education curricula for geriatric dentistry

Seven original research articles examined postdoctoral curricula in geriatric dentistry. These programs predominantly focused on clinical competencies, particularly in the management of medically compromised frail older adults. One US study proposed 42 competencies for geriatric dental fellowship training, yet it did not establish clear continuity with undergraduate curricula.

Our proposed framework addressed this gap by integrating undergraduate and postgraduate training within a single staged structure. Undergraduate education is intended to establish foundational competencies—including knowledge of aging-related changes, common geriatric oral conditions, communication with older adults, and ethical awareness—expected of all future dentists. Postgraduate and continuing education are intended to develop advanced competencies for supervised practice with frail, dependent, or medically complex older adults in outreach settings. To reflect this staged differentiation in the competency framework, enabling competencies in Table 6 that are shown in bold text denote those designated for postgraduate and continuing education programs, whereas non-bold enabling competencies represent expectations at the undergraduate level (Table 6). The relationship among identified evidence, curricular themes, competency domains, staged educational levels, and assessment strategies in summarized in Fig. 2. For example, at the undergraduate level, students are expected to explain the concept of oral frailty and its clinical significance (knowledge), whereas at the postgraduate level, graduates are expected to assess oral frailty using appropriate evaluation tools (performance). This distinction aligns with the progression described in Miller’s pyramid, where undergraduate education primarily targets the “knows” and “knows how” levels and postgraduate training addresses the “shows how” and “does” levels of clinical competence.

Discussion

Dental curricula should prepare graduates to adapt their foundational clinical knowledge and decision-making skills to the complex needs of older adults, including altered physiology, multimorbidity, frailty, and functional decline. In this context, the competencies identified in the present study are important because geriatric dentistry increasingly requires dentists to respond not only to oral disease, but also to broader issues of oral function, dependency, interdisciplinary care, and complex care environments. Compared with previously reported curricula, which have often been descriptive, country-specific, or limited to selected educational contents, the present framework proposes an integrated, staged competency-based structure that links undergraduate and postgraduate education within a single developmental continuum. A key contribution of this study is the synthesis of contemporary educational needs, including oral function, outreach care, and interdisciplinary practice, into a unified framework for curriculum development. This continuity is important because fragmented educational pathways may lead to inconsistent competency levels among practicing dentists and missed opportunities for progressive skill development.

The proposed framework aligns with established educational theory. In terms of Miller’s pyramid of clinical competence, it positions undergraduate education primarily at the “knows” and “knows how” levels, where students acquire and apply foundational knowledge, and postgraduate education at the “shows how” and “does” levels, where trainees demonstrate competence in supervised settings and perform independently in authentic clinical environments. Similarly, the progression from enabling competencies through sub-competencies to core competencies reflects ascending levels of cognitive complexity consistent with Bloom’s taxonomy. By organizing the literature into this staged hierarchy, the framework may support the development of evidence-informed curricula, guide prioritization of educational content, and provide a basis for more consistent evaluation of student performance.

Assessment of non-technical competencies, including empathy, ethical judgment, and the ability to manage moral dilemmas, requires methods that extend beyond written examinations [66]. In geriatric dentistry, where care often emphasizes functional maintenance, comfort, and quality of life rather than cure alone, students are frequently confronted with ethical and communicative challenges, such as balancing patient autonomy with safety in individuals with cognitive impairment or making complex decisions related to end-of-life care. Accordingly, these competencies should be assessed as practical and observable aspects of professional performance. Within the staged competency framework proposed in this study, undergraduate assessment may emphasize foundational knowledge, communication, ethical awareness, and basic clinical reasoning through written examinations, case-based evaluations, reflective assignments, and objective structured clinical examinations. By contrast, postgraduate and continuing education may require more advanced assessment of supervised clinical performance, interdisciplinary collaboration, and decision-making in complex outreach care settings, using portfolios, supervised case documentation, and workplace-based assessment.

Many of the identified articles emphasized the importance of clinical practice in outreach settings, such as long-term care facilities, domiciliary care, or home visits, even at the undergraduate level [23, 26–28, 30, 33, 35–37, 41]. While active clinical training is essential across all dental specialties, geriatric dentistry is distinctive in that care frequently occurs the conventional dental clinic. However, several barriers limit the implementation of outreach-based education, including legal constraints and limited funding. In the USA, several dental schools have discontinued postgraduate geriatric dentistry programs following termination of Health Resources and Services Administration funding [64]. Given these constraints, extensive outreach practice may be difficult to implement within undergraduate programs. Instead, such experiences may be more appropriately incorporated into postgraduate or continuing education programs, where sufficient funding, supervision, and legal frameworks can be ensured. For undergraduate education, simulation-based training involving aged or disabled patient scenarios, as well as structured online programs, may serve as effective and feasible alternatives.

Beyond funding and legal constraints, implementation of geriatric dentistry curricula also depends on faculty preparedness, access to appropriate training sites, and the ability to incorporate new educational content into already crowded dental programs. These challenges may be further shaped by differences across healthcare systems, long-term care infrastructure, and available educational resources. In addition, accreditation requirements and policy support are likely to influence whether competency-based geriatric dentistry training can be sustainably implemented at undergraduate and postgraduate levels.

Despite widespread recognition of the need for outreach training, only a few countries currently recognize geriatric dentistry as a formal specialty or offer certified postgraduate programs [67]. The establishment of structured postgraduate pathways would support the development of dentists with the competencies required to provide comprehensive care in both clinical and outreach settings.

Standardized and validated curricula are essential to produce qualified dentists with minimal variation in competency. However, as detailed in the results of this review, few countries have implemented nationwide standardized curricula, and existing guidelines remain regionally focused [19, 68, 69]. Given the rapid aging of the global population and growing demand for geriatric dental care, developing and implementing standardized, competency-based curricula at both the undergraduate and postgraduate levels is an urgent priority.

Research on aging and rehabilitation is expanding at the basic, translational, and clinical levels. However, the core themes in geriatric dentistry curricula have remained largely unchanged over recent decades. Emerging research areas, including oral microbiomes, frailty, oral frailty, medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw, and updated legal issues such as long-term care insurance, should be incorporated into the competencies required of future dentists. These topics should not be viewed as isolated additions to the curriculum; rather, they emphasize the need for geriatric dentistry education to remain dynamic and responsive to ongoing scientific, clinical, and societal change. In terms of curricular placement, topics such as oral frailty assessment and microbiome-related research may be most appropriately emphasized at the postgraduate level, where the evidence base is more mature and clinical application more feasible, while foundational awareness of these concepts should be progressively introduced into undergraduate curricula as scientific understanding advances. Therefore, continuous curriculum updates and close monitoring are need to align scientific and societal advancements.

In the proposed staged framework, undergraduate education should focus on foundational competencies expected of all future dentists, including knowledge of aging-related changes, common geriatric oral conditions, communication with older adults, ethical awareness, and basic care planning. Appropriate teaching methods at this level may include didactic instruction, case-based discussion, simulation, and limited supervised clinical exposure. Assessment strategies may include written examinations, reflective assignments, case-based evaluations, and objective structured clinical examinations. By contrast, postgraduate and continuing education should emphasize advanced supervised practice for frail, dependent, and medically complex older adults, particularly in outreach settings such as long-term care facilities, domiciliary care, and home visits. Teaching at this level may require more intensive clinical immersion, interprofessional collaboration, and workplace-based training. Relevant assessment approaches may include portfolios, supervised case documentation, workplace-based assessment, and evaluation of decision-making in complex clinical scenarios. These staged recommendations also reflect workforce and public health realities in aging societies. Undergraduate education should prepare all general dentists to recognize and manage common geriatric dental presentations, communicate effectively with older adults and their caregivers, and make appropriate referral decisions when patients require specialized care. By contrast, postgraduate or specialist-oriented training is more appropriate for advanced management of frail, dependent, and medically complex older adults, particularly in outreach and interdisciplinary care settings.

The proposed framework may not be equally generalizable across all countries and regions. Differences in socio-cultural context, healthcare systems, economic resources, educational infrastructure, and long-term care environments may influence both the priorities and the feasibility of geriatric dentistry curricula. In addition, the restriction to English-language publications may have excluded relevant studies from non-English-speaking countries where geriatric dentistry education may be well-developed. As a scoping review, formal quality appraisal of individual studies was not performed, which is an inherent methodological limitation. Furthermore, while the framework defines competency domains and enabling competencies, detailed learning outcomes for non-technical domains, such as ethical reasoning, interprofessional collaboration, and attitudinal development, were not fully elaborated and require further research to develop validated, context-specific outcomes and assessment methods. Therefore, for further validation and broader applicability, the present framework should be interpreted as a structured reference to guide curriculum development rather than as a uniform model to be applied identically in all settings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this scoping review synthesized the global literature on geriatric dental education and developed an integrated, staged competency-based framework spanning undergraduate and postgraduate training. By organizing the evidence into 8 major themes and 6 core competency domains, the framework provides a practical basis for curriculum planning, revision, and educational prioritization in response to population aging and the increasing complexity of geriatric oral healthcare. Rather than serving as a uniform global template, it may function as a structured reference for institutions and professional bodies seeking to strengthen geriatric dentistry education across diverse settings.

The proposed curricula should incorporate up-to-date knowledge of aging, societal changes, as well as the empathetic and realistic responsibilities necessary to address moral dilemmas in geriatric care. Corresponding competencies and assessment should be clearly defined and regularly updated and monitored by authorized institutions. Furthermore, the establishment of structured and accredited postgraduate specialty training programs is important to cultivate dentists with advanced knowledge and practical clinical expertise in geriatric dentistry.
