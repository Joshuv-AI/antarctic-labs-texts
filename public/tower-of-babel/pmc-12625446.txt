Atherogenic index of plasma and subclinical vascular disease: predictive value for coronary and carotid atherosclerosis in a health screening population
Authors: Xiao-Wei Li, Ping Shuai, Xin-Cheng Huang, Yan Mou, Pei-Yuan He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the predictive value of the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) for coronary artery calcification (CAC), carotid atherosclerosis (CA), and their coexistence, and to develop a multidimensional risk prediction model to optimize early cardiovascular disease (CVD) screening.MethodsA total of 32,992 individuals who underwent carotid intima–media thickness (CIMT) measurement and coronary CT calcium scoring at Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital between January and December 2023 were enrolled. Participants were classified into healthy, CAC, CA, and combined groups. AIP quartiles were derived from the cohort distribution. Nonparametric tests, Spearman correlation, and multivariable logistic regression with sequential covariate adjustments (Models 1–4) were applied. Predictive performance was assessed by sROC curves, AUC, and validated using bootstrap resampling and ten-fold cross-validation.ResultsAIP levels differed significantly across groups, being lowest in healthy individuals (0.01) and highest in the CAC and combined groups (0.09 and 0.06, respectively). Spearman correlations between AIP and atherosclerotic burden were strongest in healthy participants (ρ = 0.89) and progressively attenuated in CAC (ρ = 0.64) and combined groups (ρ = 0.43). Higher AIP quartiles were associated with increased risk of CA, CAC, and combined lesions, with Model 4 showing the highest predictive performance (AUC = 0.91 for combined lesions). Bootstrap and cross-validation confirmed model stability.ConclusionAIP may help identify early subclinical coronary and carotid atherosclerosis, and sequential adjustment with metabolic indicators improves predictive performance. Introduction

Atherosclerosis is the primary pathological basis of cardiovascular disease (CVD), and its manifestations—such as coronary artery calcification (CAC) and carotid atherosclerosis (CA)—are well-established independent risk factors for myocardial infarction and stroke [1]. Globally, CVD accounts for approximately 18.6 million deaths annually, with atherosclerosis-related conditions contributing the majority [2]. Both CAC and carotid intima–media thickness (CIMT) have been widely recognized as early markers of subclinical atherosclerosis [3], with their severity showing a strong positive association with cardiovascular event risk [4]. However, traditional lipid parameters such as low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) demonstrate limited sensitivity in predicting complex lesions, particularly combined coronary and carotid atherosclerosis [5]. In apparently healthy populations, individuals with LDL-C within the “normal” range may still harbor dyslipidemia-related early risk, underscoring the need for more efficient biomarkers to improve risk stratification. This aligns with the global trend toward earlier screening; notably, the 2023 ESC/ESH hypertension guidelines identified subclinical organ damage—including CAC and increased carotid IMT—as a core component of cardiovascular risk assessment, emphasizing its value in detecting high-risk individuals who may be overlooked by traditional measures [6].

The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), a novel composite metabolic marker, is calculated as the logarithm of the triglyceride (TG) to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) ratio, reflecting the atherogenic potential of lipid metabolism [7]. AIP is closely linked to lipoprotein particle size, oxidative stress [8], and insulin resistance [9], and is independently associated with coronary artery disease [10] and carotid plaque formation [11]. Nevertheless, prior studies have primarily focused on single vascular beds, with limited evidence addressing multivessel involvement. Moreover, risk stratification in earlier research has frequently relied on fixed thresholds (e.g., AIP ≥ 0.21), the clinical applicability of which remains debated across different populations. In large-scale health screening cohorts, AIP stratification by quartiles may provide a more nuanced understanding of its value in detecting subclinical disease at an early stage.

Therefore, this study leveraged data from a large health examination cohort at Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital to systematically evaluate the predictive value of AIP for CAC, CA, and their coexistence. The specific aims were: (1) to examine the dose–response relationship between AIP stratification and different atherosclerotic phenotypes (CAC, CA, and combined lesions); (2) to validate the independent predictive role of AIP in early risk detection, particularly for individuals with “normal” LDL-C but underlying dyslipidemia; and (3) to assess whether incorporating additional metabolic indicators could further improve risk stratification models. The findings are expected to provide novel evidence to support early cardiovascular screening and individualized risk management.

Materials and methods

Study population

Participants were recruited from the Health Management Center of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital between January and December 2023. Eligible individuals underwent both carotid intima–media thickness (CIMT) measurement and chest CT examination.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; and (2) complete demographic, laboratory (including triglycerides [TG], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], LDL-C), carotid ultrasound, and chest CT data. Exclusion criteria were: (1) acute cardiovascular events, major trauma, or surgery within the past 3 months; and (2) missing data. A total of 32,992 subjects were finally included (men: 20,506; women: 12,486).

Based on disease status, participants were classified into four groups:

Healthy group (n = 20,608; 59.67%): 11,803 men and 8,805 women; mean age, 47.0 ± 7.5 years.

Coronary calcification group (n = 1,841): 1,348 men and 493 women; mean age, 60.0 ± 7.5 years.

Carotid atherosclerosis group (n = 6,987): 4,648 men and 2,339 women; mean age, 56.0 ± 7.5 years.

Combined group (n = 3,556): 2,707 men and 849 women; mean age, 66.0 ± 7.5 years.

This study divided the population into 4 groups based on the quartiles of AIP (G1: AIP <−0.18, n = 8,247; G2: −0.18 ≤ AIP < 0.03, n = 8,249; G3: 0.03 ≤ AIP < 0.25, n = 8,247; G4: AIP ≥ 0.25, n = 8,249). The quartile cutoffs were derived from the distribution of AIP within this cohort (P25 = − 0.18, P50 = 0.03, P75 = 0.25), rather than external references, to ensure internal consistency.

AIP was calculated as: AIP = log(TG/HDL-C​) [12].

All participants provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2024 − 364).

Data collection

General information

Trained medical staff collected demographic, lifestyle, and health information using standardized questionnaires. The questionnaire included smoking status, alcohol consumption, and Height and weight were measured using an HNH-318 scale (Shenzhen Shuangjia Electronics Co., Ltd., China), and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m)². Waist circumference (WC) was measured with a tape. Blood pressure was measured using a mercury sphygmomanometer (Omron HPB 9020, Japan), and the mean of two measurements was recorded for systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP).

Laboratory tests

After at least 8 h of fasting, 5 mL of venous blood was collected in the morning and analyzed in the hospital’s central laboratory. The following parameters were assessed:

Lipid metabolism: total cholesterol (TC), TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, and lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)]. Glucose metabolism: fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Renal function: uric acid (UA), serum creatinine (Scr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and cystatin C (CysC). Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR): calculated using the CKD-EPI equation [13].

Imaging examinations

Carotid ultrasound

Carotid IMT was measured using Philips EPIQ CVx and EPIQ 5 C color Doppler ultrasound systems (Philips, USA) with a 7.5–12 MHz linear probe. Measurements were obtained at the far wall of the common carotid artery (10–15 mm proximal to the bifurcation) and 1 cm above the origin of the internal carotid artery. The maximum CIMT value was recorded. Carotid atherosclerosis was defined as CIMT ≥ 0.1 cm or the presence of plaque [14]. All ultrasound examinations were performed by sonographers who had completed standardized training and certification, and quality control was implemented through periodic inter-observer reproducibility testing.

Chest CT

Coronary artery calcium scoring was performed using a 256-slice spiral CT scanner (Siemens, Germany). CAC was defined as an Agatston score > 0 [15]. CT image acquisition and calcium scoring were conducted according to the Agatston standard protocol, and scans were independently reviewed by two experienced radiologists blinded to clinical data to ensure consistency.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.0.5 and SPSS version 25.0. Data distribution was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Since AIP deviated significantly from normal distribution (P < 0.05), nonparametric tests were applied. Baseline characteristics were summarized as median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test. Spearman’s rank correlation was employed to examine associations between AIP and atherosclerotic outcomes. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) in linear regression models, and variables with VIF < 5 were retained for further analysis. Four multivariable logistic regression models were constructed with progressive covariate adjustment. These models were applied separately to the overall cohort and to subgroups defined by AIP risk stratification. Model discrimination was evaluated by summary receiver operating characteristic (sROC) curves and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals, and differences between models were compared using DeLong’s test. Model calibration was assessed using calibration curves, and model performance was further examined by ten-fold cross-validation. Internal validation was performed with 1,000 bootstrap resamples to evaluate model stability and generalizability. Sensitivity analyses were additionally performed in participants with hyperlipidemia. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the study population revealed that the distribution of AIP significantly deviated from normality (P < 0.05), necessitating the use of nonparametric statistical methods. AIP differed markedly across groups (H = 271.37, P < 0.01), being lowest in the healthy group (0.01) and highest in the CAC group (0.09), with intermediate values in the combined and CA groups (0.06 vs. 0.05). Compared with the healthy group, AIP levels in the combined group were approximately sixfold higher and accompanied by increased triglycerides (1.46 vs. 1.33 mmol/L), higher BMI and waist circumference (24.68 vs. 23.88 kg/m²; 87 vs. 81 cm), elevated blood pressure (SBP 134 vs. 118 mmHg), impaired glucose metabolism (FPG 5.31 vs. 4.78 mmol/L; HbA1c 5.94% vs. 5.52%), and reduced renal function (eGFR 80.8 vs. 94.9 mL/min/1.73 m²; CysC 1.00 vs. 0.85 mg/L). Moreover, participants with CAC or CA were more likely to have higher Lp(a) levels (16.0 vs. 15.0 nmol/L) and higher proportions of smoking (24.36% vs. 20.31%) and drinking (21.99% vs. 20.74%) behaviors compared with the healthy population (all P < 0.05). In addition, participants with a history of hyperlipidemia were more frequently distributed in the CAC, CA, and combined groups compared with those without hyperlipidemia. (Table 1). Given the substantial missing Lp(a) data, multiple imputation was performed for baseline analyses, but Lp(a) was not included in the multivariable logistic regression models.

Association between AIP and disease

Spearman correlation analyses revealed significant positive associations between AIP and both atherosclerosis and CAC (all P < 0.01). The strongest correlation was observed in the healthy population (ρ = 0.89), highlighting the high sensitivity of AIP at subclinical stages. In diseased populations, correlations were progressively attenuated (CA: ρ = 0.70; CAC: ρ = 0.64; combined: ρ = 0.43) but remained statistically significant (P < 0.01), suggesting differential roles of AIP across disease stages. These findings were further supported by scatter plots, showing consistent correlation patterns across subgroups (Figure S1).

Collinearity diagnosis and logistic regression

Univariate logistic regression indicated that all covariates were significantly associated with CAC and CA (all P < 0.01). Collinearity diagnostics across stratified models for CA, CAC, and combined phenotypes showed acceptable VIFs (< 5) for all variables, with minimal collinearity observed for AIP, LDL-C, age, smoking, and drinking, and slightly higher but still acceptable VIFs for BMI, waist circumference, SBP, HbA1c, and Cystatin C (Table S1).

Multivariable logistic regression was performed with sequential adjustments: Model 1 included age, BMI, and waist circumference; Model 2 further adjusted for smoking and drinking; Model 3 additionally included SBP and LDL-C; and Model 4 further incorporated HbA1c and cystatin C. Across all participants, AIP was positively associated with CA, CAC, and combined lesions. Overall, odds ratios (ORs) for AIP remained consistently > 1. For CA, ORs ranged from 1.25 to 1.45, reaching statistical significance across all models (all P < 0.01). Stronger associations were observed for CAC, with ORs ranging from 1.67 to 2.07 across all models (all P < 0.01). The highest risks were noted in combined lesions (Model 1 OR = 2.32; Model 4 OR = 1.56; both P < 0.01).

In subgroup analyses by AIP quartiles, risk elevations generally increased with higher quartiles. For CAC, the G3 group showed markedly increased risk in Models 3 and 4 (ORs 7.41 and 6.71, P < 0.05), though confidence intervals were wide, likely due to smaller subgroup size. The G4 group demonstrated more consistent risk increases: for CA, ORs ranged 1.73–1.95; for CAC, ORs ranged 1.72–2.09; and for combined lesions, ORs ranged 1.92–2.66, all statistically significant (P < 0.05), confirming that higher AIP is associated with elevated atherosclerosis and calcification risk across phenotypes. (Table 2). Additionally, sensitivity analysis in participants with a history of hyperlipidemia (Table S3) confirmed that the positive associations between AIP and CA, CAC, and combined lesions remained generally consistent across sequentially adjusted models. However, statistical significance was somewhat attenuated in certain strata due to the smaller sample size.

Model construction and validation

sROC analyses demonstrated significant improvements in predictive performance as covariates were sequentially added (all P < 0.01). In the CA group, AUC increased from 0.76 to 0.77 (Fig. 1); in the CAC group, from 0.83 to 0.84 (Fig. 2); and in the combined group, from 0.90 to 0.91 (Fig. 3). The addition of SBP, LDL-C, HbA1c and CysC contributed most substantially to model optimization, particularly for predicting combined lesions.

Bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations; Table S2), 10-fold cross-validation (Figure S3), and calibration curves (Figure S2) collectively confirmed the internal stability, discriminative ability, and calibration of the models. Across the CA, CAC, and combined lesion groups, progressively adjusted models (Models 1–4) showed modest improvements in C-index and Kappa values, with Model 4 generally performing slightly better than prior models. Overall, these results suggest that incremental covariate adjustment yields small but consistent gains in predictive performance, with the strongest consistency observed in the combined lesion group.

Discussion

This large-scale cross-sectional study systematically evaluated the predictive performance of the AIP across different vascular beds and developed an integrated metabolic risk model. Several key findings emerged.

First, AIP suggested potential predictive value for early risk identification in a health-screening population, with relatively high discriminative performance as reflected by the model AUCs. Stratification by quartiles revealed a dose–response relationship, with the strongest effects observed for CAC and combined vascular disease. In the fully adjusted models, individuals in the highest quartile of AIP showed markedly increased risks of CAC and combined lesions, underscoring its independent predictive utility. These results are consistent with recent findings by Hu et al. [16] and Feng et al. [17]. Across cardiovascular–kidney metabolic syndrome and postmenopausal patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), AIP remained an independent predictor of CVD risk even after adjustment for multiple metabolic factors. Our data suggest that AIP has stronger predictive value for CAC and multivessel involvement than for isolated carotid atherosclerosis, which may reflect heterogeneity in vascular pathophysiology and disease progression across different arterial beds.

Second, the addition of SBP, LDL-C, HbA1c, and cystatin C substantially improved model performance compared with single-indicator approaches. Internal Bootstrap validation confirmed model stability, with the highest C-index observed for the combined lesion model. Notably, after inclusion of HbA1c, cystatin C (Model 4), the odds ratio for AIP in the highest quartile decreased to 1.92 while the AUC increased to 0.91, suggesting a partial mediating effect of renal function in the relationship between AIP and vascular injury. This finding aligns with the hypothesis of Fernández et al. [18] that metabolic dysregulation and subclinical organ damage may accelerate atherosclerosis, even in the research of Gou et al. [19] about Chinese Han postmenopausal women, and supports the “lipid–renal–vascular” axis proposed by Verhave et al. [20].

Third, the correlation between AIP and disease risk was strongest in apparently healthy individuals and progressively attenuated in patients with established lesions. This pattern likely reflects differences in disease stage. During the subclinical phase, AIP appears to be a sensitive early warning marker, consistent with Budoff’s “window of intervention” concept [21]. Importantly, the median LDL-C level in the healthy group was only 1.31 mmol/L—well below the threshold defined in the Chinese Adult Dyslipidemia Prevention Guidelines [22]—yet elevated AIP still identified hidden high-risk individuals. This highlights its value in detecting metabolic risk overlooked by traditional lipid metrics and aligns with the 2023 ESC/ESH guidelines emphasizing subclinical markers for refined risk assessment [23]. Similarly, Liu et al. [24] reported that AIP independently predicted cardiovascular events in prediabetic patients with unstable angina, even after multivariable adjustment for traditional lipid and metabolic factors, underscoring its value in identifying residual risk beyond LDL-C. Mechanistically, AIP may be more predictive in the subclinical stage, as it captures early lipid abnormalities, including elevated small dense LDL, impaired HDL function, and disrupted reverse cholesterol transport. These changes reflect metabolic disturbances that precede overt vascular damage. As atherosclerosis advances, however, inflammation, oxidative stress, and vascular calcification become the dominant drivers of plaque progression, reducing the relative predictive value of lipid-based markers like AIP. By contrast, in advanced disease stages, where calcification and reduced metabolic activity dominate plaque biology, the predictive strength of AIP diminishes [25, 26].

Finally, this study highlights the clinical relevance of AIP for CAC and combined vascular lesions. Compared with conventional lipid indices, AIP more effectively identified individuals with multivessel involvement, including those with normal LDL-C but metabolic dysregulation. As a low-cost, readily available parameter, AIP may serve as a practical tool for large-scale early screening in asymptomatic populations. The threshold used in this study (AIP ≥ 0.25) was derived from internal quartile distribution rather than predefined clinical criteria. Although it showed good discriminative performance in our cohort, its applicability to other populations warrants external validation. Future studies should refine outcome-based AIP thresholds to facilitate clinical implementation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, the single-center health-screening population may limit generalizability; future validation in multicenter, diverse, and longitudinal cohorts is warranted. Third, residual confounding and incomplete medication data (e.g., statin use) may affect interpretation. To address this, a sensitivity analysis was conducted in participants with hyperlipidemia. Lp(a) was incorporated into baseline analyses using multiple imputation but excluded from multivariable models due to a high proportion of missing values in the raw data. Finally, refining lifestyle and metabolic variables in future prospective studies will help strengthen causal inference.

Conclusion

AIP was identified as an independent factor associated with CAC and combined coronary–carotid atherosclerosis. It appeared useful in detecting individuals with normal LDL-C but underlying metabolic disturbances, complementing traditional lipid indices. Stratification by AIP may help identify individuals at higher risk within health-screening populations, particularly for CAC and multivessel disease. The relatively high AUCs observed in our models highlight their discriminative strength, although external validation is still required to confirm generalizability.
