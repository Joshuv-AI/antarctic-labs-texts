Feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary impact of the WHO-Caregiver skill training (CST) in a low-income clinical setting: a mixed-methods evaluation
Authors: Surafel Worku Megersa, Sandra Dehning, Selamawit Alemayehu Tessema, Andrea Jobst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe intensive demands of caring for children with developmental disorders (DDs) result in diminished quality of life (QoL) and increased family burden, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), such as Ethiopia, where access to specialized healthcare and caregiver support is limited. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary impact of the World Health Organization-Caregiver skill training (WHO-CST) within a clinical setting.MethodsA convergent parallel mixed-methods study design was employed. The qualitative data included 19 caregiver interviews and three focus group discussions with 20 facilitators examining program acceptability and implementation barriers, and the data were analyzed thematically. The quantitative data included 127 caregivers who completed pre- and post-intervention assessments, which were analyzed using paired t-tests and regression models (p < .05).ResultsThe qualitative analysis revealed four main themes: program acceptability, implementation challenges, effective strategies and recommendations. After the intervention, the caregivers exhibited better self-care and reduced their reliance on physical punishment. They also reported improvements in their children’s communication skills and adaptive and challenging behaviors. Factors such as resource shortage and time constraints were identified as implementation barriers. Among those who completed the follow-up, 100% attended seven sessions or more. The quantitative analysis showed significant improvements in the psychological (Mdiff =3.01, t (126) =2.37, p = .019) and environmental (Mdiff =4.97, t (126) =4.19, p< .001) domains. However, the improvement in the overall quality of life (QoL) (Mdiff =1.74, t (126) =1.96, p= .052) and the reduction in the overall burden score (Mdiff = -.86, t (126) = -1.09, p= .277) were marginal and did not reach statistical significance.ConclusionThe WHO-CST is a feasible and acceptable intervention in Ethiopian clinical setting. It is associated with potential improvements in parenting style, self-care, and selected QoL domains among caregivers. By utilizing psychiatry residents as facilitators, the study implemented a service integration and capacity-building model to address gaps in a resource-limited setting. The findings may inform future implementation strategies and policy development in similar low-resource settings. Future evaluations should explore the cost-effectiveness and institutional support required to maintain the WHO-CST as standard care.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Developmental disorders, Caregiver skill training, WHO-CST, Clinical setting, Low-income setting, Ethiopia

Introduction

Developmental disorders (DDs) include conditions such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD), intellectual disability (ID), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and learning disorders [1, 2]. DDs can adversely affect a child’s physical, cognitive, and emotional development, leading to limitations in daily functioning and overall well-being [3, 4]. Approximately 95% of children with DDs reside in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [5]; these challenges are exacerbated by limited access to specialized services, particularly outside urban centers [6], placing caregivers in a critical role in supporting their children’s needs.

Although all DDs are considered to be in the same category, the challenges faced by caregivers vary substantially across specific diagnostic subtypes. The behavioral disruptions associated with ADHD and ASD can cause acute distress to caregivers, while intellectual and language disabilities can lead to a long-term caregiving burden due to the individuals ongoing dependence. Even within the same diagnostic category of ASD, caregivers needs and challenges may vary dynamically based on the child’s age, verbal abilities, cognitive profile and additional behavioral issues [7].

In the Ethiopian context, people with mental disorders and their caregivers, including those caring for children with DDs, face societal stigma and social ostracism within their communities. Moreover, limited awareness and misconceptions about DDs, often attributed to supernatural causes or poor parenting, may intensify blame and isolation [8–10]. As a result, caregivers are at increased risk of compromised QoL and overall well-being [11–15].

Although caregivers’ well-being, along with their effective engagement, is one of the key factors for a better outcome in treating children with DDs [16], it has rarely been taken into consideration when planning studies or intervention strategies [17, 18].

With the aim of narrowing the existing treatment need and service gap, the WHO developed a Caregiver skill training (CST). The program’s development was formally initiated in 2013–2014 following a recommendation from the WHO Mental Health Gap Action Program (mhGAP) in 2010 to provide parents with training in skills to address developmental delays [19]. The development process was highly collaborative and iterative and involved global experts. Input from LMICs was emphasized to ensure applicability, and clinicians and parent associations from all six WHO regions were included. The intervention uses a task-sharing model that allows it to be delivered by trained non-specialists, such as community health workers or experienced parents, rather than by highly specialized clinical staff [19].

The WHO-CST is a manualized, group-based intervention comprising multiple components: strategies for engaging children through play and daily routines, promoting communication skills, preventing and managing challenging behaviors, and fostering problem solving and self-care among caregivers [19]. It has been adopted and translated into the Ethiopian context and found to be feasible and acceptable and addresses local needs [20, 21].

Saint Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College (SPHMMC) in Addis Ababa is one of the very few institutions providing this training in Ethiopia. Historically, the training was delivered on ad-hoc basis by a very small number of senior consultants. This structure meant the training directly competed with the intensive clinical, academic and administrative duties of these consultants, creating a substantial limitation in service capacity. Consequently, only approximately 120 caregivers have received training in the last six years. This issue was exacerbated by a lack of resources and a two-year suspension of public gatherings due to the COVID-19 pandemic. At the time of the study, there were more than 280 caregivers on a waiting list expecting to receive training, and the list is growing. Had the training continued at the original pace, it would have taken more than five years to enroll everyone on the waiting list.

As an innovative approach and to ensure long-term implementation, the program was integrated into the postgraduate psychiatry residency curriculum. Following the initial delivery of the training by consultant psychiatrists, its promising outcomes, strong local demand, and the clear need to address an important clinical service gap became evident. A formal proposal was subsequently developed to integrate the training into the core psychiatry residency curriculum during the two- to three-month rotation in the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry (CAP) Unit. Following rigorous review and approval by the Department Council, it was officially approved by the Institutional Academic Committee. Under this formalized framework, every resident must now independently facilitate at least one complete training cycle, as documented in a verified supervisor logbook, in order to qualify as a psychiatrist.

Considering the low resource setting and scarcity of mental health provisions in Ethiopia, studies investigating the overall burden of DDs and suitable intervention strategies involving caregivers are lacking [8]. In addition, the preliminary impact and feasibility of integrating the WHO-CST into existing medical services requires more evidence.

Hence, the aim of this study is to address a critical service gap by optimizing a psychiatry residency program, increasing the enrollment of caregivers in training, explore the feasibility and acceptability of this approach in a clinical setting, and evaluate its preliminary impact on caregivers’ QoL and burden.

Methods

Study area and setting

The study was conducted from March 2024 to February 2025 at the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry (CAP) Unit of the SPHMMC in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. The unit comprises CAP subspecialists, general psychiatrists, clinical psychologists, and psychiatry residents. Its service operates on weekdays from 8:30 a.m. to 5:00 p.m., providing outpatient care to an average of 300 patients per month.

Study design

The study employed a convergent parallel mixed-methods study design, integrating qualitative data gathered through in-depth interviews and focus group discussions (FGDs) to explore its feasibility and acceptability within a clinical setting. Pre- and postintervention quantitative data to assess the preliminary impact of the WHO-CST on the QoL and burden among caregivers of children with DDs. Additionally, the acceptability of the program by caregivers was measured using a 7-item post-intervention Likert scale. Qualitative and quantitative data were collected concurrently and analyzed in parallel. Results were then integrated through data merging with joint display [22].

Following the implementation outcomes framework proposed by Proctor et al. (2011), ‘Feasibility’ as the extent to which the approach can be successfully used within the given setting and ‘Acceptability’ was defined as the perception among stakeholders that the approach is agreeable, palatable, and satisfactory [23].

Research team and reflexivity

The research team consisted of one male and three female, multidisciplinary investigators with over five years of experience in clinical psychiatry and public health. The interviews were conducted by two psychiatry residents who underwent two weeks of formal training as part of their training in research methodology. They also had experience of conducting semi-structured interviews in previous institutional clinical projects, including for their graduation theses, which had a qualitative element. The principal investigator briefed them on the specific semantic nuances of the interview guide used in this study, and they received further feedback during the pilot-testing of the questionnaire. To mitigate the social desirability bias resulting from the power imbalance between the residents and the caregivers in an institutional setting, where caregivers might overreport positive intervention outcomes due to the perceived risk of compromising their child’s subsequent clinical care, strict reflexivity and procedural safeguards were implemented. The residents who were not involved in delivering the training, and had no prior established relationship with the participants were selected to conduct the interviews. The interviews were conducted in neutral seminar rooms, away from the clinics and the caregivers were explicitly assured via a standardized script that critical feedback would not affect their routine medical care. During the recruitment and consent process, the participants were informed of the researchers’ affiliations, and the purpose of the study. The researchers maintained a reflexive stance and used a semi-structured interview guides to ensure their clinical backgrounds did not overshadow the participants’ authentic cultural and spiritual perspectives.

Study participants

This single-arm evaluation adopted a pragmatic sampling strategy, optimizing enrollment to clear the active outpatient CAP unit waiting list. This was based on the available cohort of trained psychiatry residents, as well as departmental room and resource allocations during the study period. A total of 150 caregivers were initially enrolled and divided into 15 groups of 10 caregivers each. Of those, 127 successfully completed the training and post-intervention assessment. The study population comprised primary caregivers of children aged 2 to 14 years with DDs who were recruited directly from the pre-existing waiting list at the SPHMMC Department of Psychiatry CAP Unit, based on their position on the list. Although the WHO-CST program is optimized for children aged 2 to 9 years, it was designed as flexible, parent-mediated intervention suitable for broad age rage and diverse developmental needs (WHO 2022). Due to substantial limitation in service capacity, many registered children crossed the age-9 threshold while remaining on the waiting list. We expanded our inclusion criteria to include children up to the age of 14 in order to address important ethical considerations. Artificially excluding children who crossed the age threshold while waiting enrollment would present significant ethical challenges and exacerbate local equity gaps [24, 25]. Furthermore, from our previous clinical observations, caregivers of these older children still benefited greatly from the core behavioral strategies such as managing challenging behaviors, implementing structured routines and self-care for the caregivers. Thus, expanding the age range helped to ensure equitable access to care for an underserved population. Caregivers of children with severe medical conditions (e.g., poorly controlled epilepsy), severe to profound hearing loss, severe visual impairment, or severe motor impairment were excluded.

For the in-depth interviews, participants were purposively selected, taking into account age, gender, the child’s diagnosis, and their relationship with the child. Recruitment continued until thematic saturation was achieved (n = 19).

Procedure

The WHO-CST master trainers (senior consultant psychiatrists) provided facilitator training for psychiatry residents. As part of their residency training, residents attend advanced didactic and clinical lectures on the biomedical, neurological and diagnostic and management components of DDs and common comorbidities. They are also assigned to the CAP for two to three months, during which time they evaluate and manage children and adolescents with DDs. The five-day manual-based facilitator training did not reiterate clinical features. Instead, the training focused on general aspects, such as manualized coaching methodologies and group facilitation dynamics. Intense role-play of parent-child interaction modules was also included, with a specific focus on the concepts behind the intended training outcomes, such as engagement, improving communication and adaptive skills, preventing and managing difficult behaviors, and self-care. The core modules of the training were delivered without modification to maintain strict fidelity to the validated manual. The theoretical sessions were followed by practical observation of the training, during which the residents observed a live session conducted by the master trainer. Caregivers who were attending follow-up appointments and were registered on the waiting list for the training were enrolled in the program.

The training was provided in groups using the WHO-CST, Amharic version, with each group comprising an average of ten caregivers. Each group was led by two facilitators. The program also included a coordinator nurse with B.Sc. level of training, who was responsible for preparing the training rooms, organizing training manuals and refreshments, and contacting caregivers and trainers to ensure their availability and active participation in each session.

The training was conducted over nine consecutive weeks, with one session per week lasting two and half to three hours. Owing to manpower and logistical shortages, three clinic visits were incorporated into the program, replacing the home visits recommended by the WHO. One visit was held prior to the start of the training to assess the children’s baseline developmental stage and parent- child interactions. Two additional visits were held: one between the fourth and fifth sessions and another after the final session. These clinical visits were intended to evaluate progress toward previously established goals, provide coaching to caregivers, and set subsequent goals. Caregivers who completed the ninth session graduated from the program and received certificates. Attendance was logged weekly on a paper registry managed by the project nurse coordinator. To be eligible for graduation, caregivers were required to attend at least seven of the nine sessions and they were informed about this, during their enrollment.

In addition, Telegram channels were used to maintain continuous engagement between trainees and facilitators, and to encourage participants to interact with each other outside of weekly sessions. Facilitators and program coordinators monitored the channels, sharing homework and training date reminders, digital copies of training manuals, questions and answers, and peer support resources. Although not all participants had smartphones, they used those belonging to their partners, family members or neighbors to actively engage with the channels. Social and mainstream media platforms were also utilized to raise community awareness about DDs, promote health-seeking behaviors, and showcase the WHO-CST program delivered at SPHMMC.

Qualitative assessment

Nineteen caregiver participants took part in a single interview session, each lasting between 45 min and one hour. In addition, three focus group discussions (FGDs) were held with twenty training facilitators within two weeks of the final training session. The facilitators were divided among three groups, with an average of one and a half hours per group. Two groups had seven facilitators each, and one group had six facilitators. No participant refused or dropped out of the interviews. Both the in-depth interviews and the FGDs were face-to-face and conducted at the CAP unit in the Department of Psychiatry at SPHMMC. To ensure confidentiality and open discussion, only the participants and researchers were present during the interviews. A semi-structured interview guide containing open-ended questions was developed and pilot tested with 5 participants who met the inclusion criteria. Based on their feedback, minor adjustments were made to the phrasing of some questions, to ensure they were clearly understood. All the interviews were audio recorded, transcribed into Amharic and then translated into English. Field notes were recorded during and after each session, to capture nonverbal cues and the environmental context.

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative data from the caregivers IDIs and facilitators FGDs were analyzed using the Braun and Clark six-phase thematic analysis framework, moving from initial coding to refined thematic development. SWM transcribed the audio recordings into Amharic and translated them into English. To preserve the unique nuances of the intervention recipients versus the providers, the transcripts from the caregiver IDIs and the facilitator FGDs were initially managed as separate datasets. SWM and SAT applied inductive and deductive line-by-line coding independently using MAXQDA (version 24). Initial deductive codes were derived from semi-structured interview guides, while inductive codes were dynamically generated from emerging patterns. Intercoder consistency was evaluated by comparing a sample of jointly coded transcripts, and any conceptual variation were resolved through a reconciliation process to establish a unified codebook. During a higher-level thematic synthesis phase, these independent code structures were cross-examined. Due to a high degree of conceptual convergence regarding systematic implementation barriers and program acceptability, the code set were integrated into a single, unified framework. Candidate themes were iteratively refined, defined, and checked against the raw textual extracts and entire dataset to ensure analytical validity. In the final reporting, data triangulation was made explicit by systematically identifying whether a specific finding emerged from caregiver perspective, facilitator dynamic or both.

Related concepts from the themes were grouped into organizing themes, which summarized the concepts in a structured format. Consequently, the description was completed according to these thematic areas.

Rigor

To ensure the rigor of the study, its credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability were evaluated. Credibility was established through prolonged engagement with participants and by ensuring a diverse sample across age, gender, DD type and relation to the child. To verify the findings further, transcripts and preliminary results were shared with participants to gather their feedback. To maintain dependability and confirmability, a thorough audit trail was created, comprising semi-structured interview guides, recordings and transcripts. Three additional researchers were involved in the decision-making and peer review processes, as well as methodological auditors. While these reviewers were not involved in primary coding, transcript analysis, or translation auditing, they participated in the post-analytical phase. In this phase, they independently reviewed a subset of English transcripts against the established codebook. Their evaluation determined the structural and logical alignment between raw codes, sub-themes, and final organizing themes. Transferability was ensured via purposive sampling, which allowed for a diverse sample of caregivers who experienced the intervention under different condition. Although this non-random sampling method is not representative, it maximizes the depth and diversity of the qualitative data, providing a comprehensive contextual background necessary for external investigators to determine how well these insights fit into their setting. Full reports written in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) and Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) guidelines are provided in Additional Files 1 and 2 respectively.

Quantitative assessment

After written informed consent was obtained from all caregiver participants, baseline demographic data were collected. The demographic data integrated both caregiver and child factors contributing to the QoL and burden of caregivers, which were assessed via the WHOQOL-BREF and Family Burden interview Schedule (FBIS) questionnaires. A post-intervention assessment was conducted one month after completion of the training program via the same tool administered during the pre-intervention assessment. Additionally, the acceptability of the program by caregivers was measured using a 7-item post-intervention Likert scale.

Facilitator competence

Three master trainers provided ongoing coaching and supervision to the facilitators. One-third of each group’s nine sessions were randomly selected for supervision via the Enhancing Assessment of Common Therapeutic Factors (ENACT) rating scale. The ENACT scale is an 18-item objective assessment tool designed to measure the facilitators helping behaviors across major domains, including active listening, verbal communication, rapport building and the normalization of client feelings. Each item is scored on a structured 4-point ordinal scale, where a score of 1 indicates the presence of unhelpful or potentially harmful behaviors; a score of 2 indicates that basic helping skills are partially demonstrated; a score of 3 reflects the successful and sustained execution of all basic helping skills; and a score of 4 denotes advanced therapeutic competencies [26].

Caregiver QoL

The QoL of caregivers was assessed via the WHO QOL-BREF questionnaire. The instrument consists of 26 items measuring physical health, psychological well-being, social relationships, and environmental factors. This tool is a cross-culturally validated instrument for measuring QoL in health care settings, demonstrating good sensitivity and specificity. In a previous validation study conducted in Ethiopia by Shumye et al., the instrument demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.88 [27]. The tool raw scores are transformed into a range between 0 and 100 and scaled in a positive direction.

Caregivers’ burden

Caregivers’ burden was assessed via the FBIS. It was developed by Pai and Kapur (1981). While it was initially validated and widely used in LMICs, including Ethiopia for families of adult psychiatric patients [28], it later became a valuable tool for evaluating caregivers of children with DDs, such as intellectual disability (ID) and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [29, 30]. This scale measures objective (24 items) and subjective (1 general standardized question) aspects of burden. Each item has three response categories, 0 (no burden), 1 (moderate burden), and 2 (severe burden), and the total scores range from 0 to 48 for objective burden [31]. The cutoff points for FBIS are 0 for no burden, 1–16 for mild burden, 17–32 for moderate burden, and 33–48 for severe burden [32]. It has been validated in various regions worldwide [33]. In a study conducted in Ethiopia, the instrument demonstrated excellent reliability, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.939 [28].

Quantitative program acceptability measure

To evaluate the intervention’s social validity and trainee’s satisfaction quantitatively, a seven-item Post-Intervention Feedback Questionnaire was administered to the 127 caregivers who completed the training sessions. The questionnaire was adopted from the standardized feedback and process-tracking modules in the WHO-CST monitoring and evaluation framework. Each item evaluates a district implementation outcome domain, focusing on program duration, perceived improvements in child behavior, efficiency in preventing/ minimizing and managing challenging behaviors, acquisition of practical caregiving skills, perceived coaching and therapeutic alliance and overall benefit of the training. Caregivers scored each item on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1(“Strongly Disagree/ Not at all Useful”) to 5 (“Strongly agree/ Very Useful”).

Quantitative analysis

The data were analyzed via SPSS software, version 26. The data were cleaned and checked for consistency. Descriptive analysis was performed by simple frequencies and proportions. The results are presented in tables. Simple and multiple regression analyses were performed to evaluate the strength of the associations between factors. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Both simple and multiple regression analyses were expressed as odd ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Paired t tests were performed for all analyses comparing pre- and post-intervention measures.

Mixed-methods integration

Qualitative results from caregiver IDIs and facilitators FGDs, and quantitative results from the WHOQOL-BREF, FBIS and the 7-item post-intervention feedback were integrated through data merging with joint display in a table which allows for side-by-side comparison to determine if they provide similar conclusions, expanded insights into the results, or provided contradictory findings [22].

In terms of professional level, 11 participants were third-year psychiatry residents, whereas nine were in their second year of residency.

Thematic presentation of the qualitative results

I. Acceptability of the program

Acceptability of the program

This section describes the changes in the caregiving approach, observed improvements, benefits of providing the WHO-CST in a clinical setting and overall perceived impact of the program as described by the participants.

Changes in caregivers’ perceptions of and approaches to managing their children

Most of the caregivers demonstrated a profound commitment to supporting their children and arrived at the clinic with intuitive approach to address their children’s needs. However, due to lack of structural access to formalized training, these proactive efforts relied on non-evidence-based strategies. This resulted in caregiving methods that felt like ‘trial and error’ to the caregivers themselves. The majority of caregivers reported that they were unsure how to manage their child’s condition. They stated that they often became easily irritated, felt overwhelmed, and resorted to physical punishment as a default response to their child’s challenging behaviors, describing caregiving for a child with DDs as highly demanding.

I especially developed one thing in this training: when I tried to feed her and she refused, I would get angry, hit, and pinch her, you know. CG5.

While most caregivers reported shifting to positive reinforcement, the qualitative analysis also revealed underlying linguistic and conceptual attitudes regarding discipline. For example, one caregiver stated “I know how to punish him-CG14”. This highlights a significant implementation barrier: the initial tendency of caregivers to filter behavioral de-escalation technique through traditional lens of discipline. “I was working on it from the beginning, but we Ethiopians have a culture of lashing out and punishing when we get angry. I was taught how to discipline and that you should not punish just because you’re angry. I have learned how to punish him. I got what I expected from the training.” CG14.

Following their participation in the, caregivers reported a marked improvement in their understanding of and strategies for managing their child’s challenging behaviors. They learned that challenging behaviors are more often a result of their child’s difficulty in expressing needs rather than intentional misbehavior. Additionally, they began using patience and positive approaches rather than physical punishment to manage challenging behaviors. Caregivers stated that the training helped them reflect on the strategies they used to support their children and become more organized by providing clear explanations and structured guidance for approaches they had already been using prior to enrollment in the program.

Before, I used to rush her to wash her face. She would not wash it well, and I would say, ‘That is it,’ and I would wash it for her. However, now, I have understood well in the training that I have to give her time and that she will develop it when I give her time, in different things. CG5.

Before participating in the training, I did not involve him in many things. I would tell him to sit quietly, and I did not give him a chance. I have corrected many things with the help of the training. CG10.

Observed improvements

The caregivers reported improvements in their children’s engagement, communication, daily activities, challenging behaviors, and academic performance.

Before, he used to communicate by pulling your hand and asking for what he wanted. Now, he uses a certain word… and if he cannot find the word, he will go and show you. CG2.

Now he participates in the chores at home. Previously, he focused more on materials. However, now, his desire to be with us and play with us has increased. His communication with us has increased. CG11.

The majority of caregiver participants reported a reduction in their stress and anxiety level following the training. They attributed this improvement to a deeper understanding of their children’s challenges, which enabled them to move beyond culturally shaped assumptions. Furthermore, the participants recognized the importance of self-care and acknowledged the need to critically reflect on and adjust their own behavior to better support their children.

It taught me what I should do. too take care of myself. To calm myself down. For example, when we first started the training, the first thing we were asked about was ourselves. They would ask us, “How was your health?” It makes you look inward, or you see worse things from other parents, and you realize it is not just yours. CG12.

Despite this, some participants reported that they had not observed any significant change in their children, but they acknowledged that they had learned additional skills to support their children.

Although I have not seen outstanding results, I have seen that he understands that he needs to know the things I tell him and teach him. CG17.

It is hard to say he has changed. You do not see much change in a month. This training is for me. I learned this training to know how to continue with him. CG15.

Benefits of providing training in a clinical setting

Implementing the training in a clinical setting offered structural and logistical advantages. The facilitators reported it was convenient for them as the training was provided in the same institution where they are practicing. Additionally, it facilitated referrals of children and caregivers to other medical services when necessary. This arrangement also gave credibility to the training, as it was regarded as an integral component of medical care. Additionally, it created a sense of ease for caregivers, enabling them to communicate more openly. The facilitators reported:

If the need arises, being here makes it possible to link them to appropriate medical service. In addition, providing training in the hospital setting makes the caregivers take the training seriously. F16.

The safety feeling they get from knowing that we are doctors makes them feel safe to some extent and speak openly. F9.

Perceived impact and overall acceptance of the

Most caregiver participants reported experiencing a shift from stagnant hopelessness to structured clarity. Ultimately, this change in perspective fostered deep-seated confidence in their ability to grow and navigate future challenges.

My perspective has changed. Before, I used to think nothing could change. However, now, I think, this is like reading a book, how many chapters is it? I go and do it, and I see it very clearly. I even hope that he will grow without any doubt. CG12.

As one of the facilitators mentioned, “Another thing they told us was that they were very happy and that they felt that their own feelings had changed a lot and that the stress and different feelings they had before had become lighter.” F13.

In light of the advantages accruing from the training, the participants articulated a desire for the program to be prolonged. Some caregivers tried to raise awareness about the training, encouraged others to seek assistance and shared copies of the WHO-CST manuals they had received with colleagues and neighbors who they thought might benefit from them. “Beyond getting trained, they were trying to create awareness for other caregivers and to get them to come and start the training.” F4.

II. Challenges experienced

Challenges experienced

This section presents the challenges experienced by both the facilitator and the trainee during the training.

Lack of awareness and stigma

While some caregivers reported that their neighborhood was compassionate and encouraging, others reported experiencing emotional difficulties in raising a child with DD, including but not limited to extreme stress and worry about their child’s condition, excessive guilt, stigma, and exclusion from social activities and responsibilities. As caregivers reported:

One day, we were in a church. My child was playing by his own with an umbrella and spinning it, a woman said, ‘Leave him, leave him, he is sick’ and everyone stared at him. Then, I said, ‘any child plays, what’s the problem?’ And left crying immediately. CG2.

I do not attend ‘edir’ (a traditional community-based insurance and mutual aid institution in Ethiopia) or any other social event, because it is a… it is something that makes you sick. CG3.

Why did this happen to me? What have I done differently? Why is God punishing me in this way?’ I used to have these kinds of self-reproach. CG5.

Difficulty in managing a dynamic group

The training was delivered in groups, with caregivers exhibiting a range of needs, difficulties, and expectations being enrolled together. The facilitators indicated While providing the training in groups has its own advantages, it sometimes also can present challenges, such as the difficulty of addressing everyone’s issues and the tendency for caregivers to compare their child to others, which can cause them to lose hope, feel inadequate, or even feel stigmatized.

Especially during the initial stages, there were challenges of parents comparing their child’s severity of condition and progress with others; this at times created a sense of failure and stigma within the groups. F2.

This was also observed by the caregivers. “It is true that there are parents whose children have worse problems, and when they express that, it is a heavy burden for them.” CG1.

Resource (space and equipment) inadequacy

The facilitators reported that the training room was shared with other activities, which occasionally required sessions to be relocated to smaller and less convenient rooms. In addition, owing to the unavailability of certain standardized play materials listed in the training manuals as ‘demonstration equipment’ such as plastic cups, blocks, balls and other toys were replaced by other plastic items, balloons or similar things.

There is a shortage of demonstration equipment, and we try to replace them with something similar. F10.

Sometimes the training rooms are also used for other purposes, and there are times we had to change places1. F8.

Furthermore, in the absence of childcare services during training sessions, some caregivers had to bring their children to the training room, as no designated play area or other suitable facilities were available. This phenomenon made it difficult for the entire group to maintain focus.

Time constraints and overall burden among participants

The facilitators reported that caregivers face a number of challenges during the training, including transportation issues and a lack of partner support.

There are people who come from far away, even outside of Addis. There are people who are at work or have to pick up their children from school, making schedules very tight. F2.

The facilitators, for their part, stated that they faced time constraints in aligning the training with their other clinical responsibilities. To mitigate this issue, the caregivers and facilitators agreed to schedule all training sessions between 11:00 AM and 2:00 PM. The mid-day slot optimized caregiver attendance by enabling them to drop their children off at school and still make it home in time for the afternoon pick-up. For facilitators, this time slot minimized disruption to core morning hospital rounds, though it did involve certain degree of professional compromise.

III. Effective strategies

Effective strategies

This section explores the factors that have been identified as beneficial in providing training, as described by the participants. These factors include strong group connections and persistent support from facilitators.

Group connection and shared experience

As the training progressed, the caregivers understood that the challenges associated with raising a child with special needs were not unique to them. Additionally, they expressed a strong desire to maintain good communication after the training program concluded.

We are people with similar problems; I have gained some understanding from them about where the different inclusive schools are. Beyond that, we have tried to discuss the problems we encounter and support each other. CG11.

The facilitators also discussed strategies such as encouraging caregivers to support one another and collaborate as a team, as well as the constant motivation to achieve success.

They also created a family-like environment among themselves. F12.

Supportive facilitators

The facilitators were lauded by the caregivers for their commitment to assisting them throughout the process, the manner in which they presented the material, their meticulous follow-up, and their endeavors to guarantee that each participant had an equal opportunity to engage in the process.

I thank the trainers very much. They gave their time, they worked hard, and they did a lot. They ask us to repeat things repeatedly and explain them repeatedly. Therefore, all I can say is ‘may God bless them’. CG17.

I was very happy with the patience of the trainers. The training was participatory, and everyone expressed their own experience. We were also given homework every week to work on with our children. CG18.

The facilitators asserted that they consistently endeavored to ensure the inclusion of all participants and focused on providing parents with the necessary tools to address their own issues and share their personal experiences.

Among the strategies, I found engaging the parents to come up with solutions for their questions/problems to be helpful. F6.

IV. Recommendations

Recommendations

This section describes the recommendations forwarded by the participants with respect to structure, training content and enhancing accessibility to others.

Structural recommendations

A series of structural recommendations were submitted by both the caregivers and facilitators with the objective of enhancing the program’s future implementation. The facilitators emphasized the importance of a dedicated training room, a nursery where the children may stay while their caregivers participate in the training, and equipment for demonstration purposes.

In the future, we should consider having a separate room.” with its own equipment. F4.

We also need a day care center because some parents come with their children, which can create a difficult situation. F10.

The majority of facilitators indicated that it would be advantageous for the program to consider enrolling individuals within the same age range, diagnosis, and severity of condition. This can foster a positive atmosphere for shared issues among the group. Additionally, the feasibility of providing financial compensation for transportation was discussed. Owing to the scheduling of training over the course of the week, some parents arrived late because of the necessity of utilizing multiple taxis to reach the hospital.

If parents of children in a comparable age group are enrolled in the same group, it will give them an opportunity to better share their experiences. F9.

The other recommendation was to conduct home visits in accordance with the WHO training guidelines, as this provides an opportunity to observe children and their caregivers in their natural environment.

Session content, order and method of delivery

The caregivers reported that training manuals were available and that they could take them home after each session. They found straightforward language, pictorial representations, and demonstrations to be positive aspects, as well as the translation and adaptation of the content to the local context. The facilitators also discussed the comments they receive from the caregivers.

Although we tried to let them know what we would address in the training, most caregivers were requesting toilet training. I think it would be good if the manual included this. We even tried to register some of them, as it was very common. F8.

They would repeatedly ask about food choices. It would be good if dietary recommendations are included in the manual. F5.

The sequence of the sessions was another topic of discussion. Some facilitators suggested addressing the management of challenging behaviors in earlier sessions, as this was a primary concern for many parents. Similarly, scheduling the self-care session at the end meant that complicated issues were brought up right before the last meeting. This was viewed as a potential weakness, as it could imply that the caregiver’s primary concern would be unaddressed.

Since the self-care session is at the end, and we might not see them after that, many things that affected them would remain unaddressed. Maybe it would be better if it had more sessions or was at the beginning. F8.

Improving accessibility

In light of the enormous disparity between access and demand, the caregivers suggested making the program multicentered to reach caregivers outside of Addis Ababa and using media to engage a wider demographic and enhance community awareness.

As nurses in health centers provide psychoeducation to people on lung problems, birth control methods and others, it would have been good to have a program for this as well. CG9.

I wish this training was accessible to everyone in need. Particularly, parents from the regions outside of Addis Ababa know that this opportunity exists, and they wish they could get it. CG18.

Quantitative results

Among the 150 caregivers who initially enrolled, 15.3% dropped out of the training, but 100% of those who completed the follow-up period participated in 7 or more sessions. Those who dropped out cited reasons such as residential relocation due to vast corridor development in Addis Ababa, a lack of social support and time constraints.

Facilitator competence

To ensure integrity, facilitator competence was monitored by random assessment of 34% (n = 43) of the total sessions. The ENACT mean (± SD) score of 3.6 (± 0.24) revealed that the facilitators maintained high standards of therapeutic rapport and participatory adult learning techniques throughout the training.

Discussion

This study presents a promising model for addressing the service gap in care for children with DDs in low-income settings. It involves integrating the WHO-CST into both clinical services and the psychiatry residency curriculum, using a service integration and capacity-building model to strengthen clinical care. The study evaluated the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary impact of implementing the WHO-CST program in a routine clinical setting. In-depth interviews with caregivers and focus group discussions with facilitators were conducted to explore program feasibility and implementation challenges. Additionally, caregiver QoL, burden and the program acceptability were assessed by the WHO-QoL-BREF, the FBIS and a 7-item Likert scale respectively.

The qualitative findings indicated that implementing the WHO-CST in a clinical setting is both a feasible and acceptable.

Intuitive culturally grounded caregiving practices that are deeply aligned with local familial norms often lack access to a structured, evidence-based neurodevelopmental training framework. One key finding was that the caregivers reported that the training provided them a structure and clarity to shift from a punitive disciplinary practice toward supportive, empathic communication. This is a very relevant domain of improvement that might have long and positive consequences for children, as positive, empathic and need-oriented parental practices/styles result in increased resilience and decreased vulnerability to additional mental health burdens for children [34]. The major contributors to this shift were caregivers’ enhanced skills and confidence in managing their child, as well as reduced self-blame and feelings of guilt. Caregivers also reported improvements in child development outcomes regarding communication, adaptive behaviors, and challenging behaviors. Similar findings have been reported in other studies conducted in LMICs that explored the feasibility and preliminary impact of the WHO-CST [21, 25, 35, 36].

The facilitators cited a number of challenges, such as a lack of a dedicated training room, an area for children to stay while their caregivers participate in training, inadequate ‘demonstration equipment’, and inconvenient timing with their other academic activities. Logistical challenges, stigma, and resource constraints are common barriers reported in other studies [21, 36, 37]. The caregivers reported difficulty affording travel-related costs and other expenses and a lack of partner support as factors hindering their participation in the training. Similar findings have been reported in other studies [36, 38].

Consistent with prior research, the study participants recommended providing more realistic examples of strategies for addressing challenging behaviors, expanding the training manual to cover essential topics such as toilet training and nutritional guidance [39–41]. Additionally, they discussed reordering the training sessions, such as moving “Management of Difficult Behaviors” and “Self-Care” to the earliest sessions. Finally, to increase accessibility and reach, the participants advocated expanding the program to different regions of the country and offering training virtually, mirroring other observations [41].

To further enhance the training provision, the study participants also suggested having home visits. While some studies reported that caregivers are skeptical of home visits, considering them an invasion of privacy that causes them more stress [38], other studies have reported that in-person or virtual home visits are mutually beneficial for caregivers and facilitators. These visits enable the evaluation of children’s development and their interactions with caregivers in a natural environment, allowing for the creation of individualized support strategies [36, 42]. The study participants emphasized that grouping caregivers by their child’s age and specific diagnosis promotes more relevant peer-to-peer experience sharing and reduces the stigma of comparison, which can discourage and stigmatize caregivers of children with more severe conditions when they observe quicker progress in others.

Both the qualitative findings and the post-intervention acceptability assessment revealed that majority of caregivers strongly agreed the training had an optimal length, the trainers were supportive, and the training taught them new skills. The caregivers also reported that the training helped them better prevent and manage their children’s difficult behaviors and brought positive changes to their children’s development. These findings mirror those of other studies conducted globally and in Ethiopia [20, 21, 43, 44]. Acknowledging the benefits they gained, the caregivers began acting as community advocates. They shared training manuals and encouraged other families in their neighborhood to seek support.

The significant gains observed in this study are reinforced by the high attendance rate of caregivers who completed the study and the high enhancing assessment of common therapeutic factors (ENACT) mean scores recorded during training. These results may be due to regular, supportive communication via the Telegram channel, as well as supervision and constructive feedback that the facilitators received throughout the training.

Given SPHMMC is a tertiary referral center located in Addis Ababa, the sample heavily reflected an urbanized (96.9%), educated (one-third have diploma-level education or above) population, providing a valuable opportunity to contrast these outcomes with the rural implementation of the WHO-CST. While the urban sample reflected a higher retention and a rapid adoption of behavioral concepts, Zerihun et al., (2023) emphasized the rural implementation encountered structural obstacles, such as low literacy rate, transportation limitation and a high local stigma [21]. Furthermore, structured child-led play was often perceived as a foreign concept in rural settings; the educated urban caregivers in this sample, however, integrated these modalities with fewer cultural barriers. Contrasting these two distinct socio-economic landscapes has important implications for strengthening health systems: the core behavioral principles of the training were helpful across both cohorts, but the delivery mechanism must match the context.

In contrast to findings reported in the global literature, which indicate that co-occurring conditions affect 70–90% of clinical cohorts with moderate-to-severe DDs [45], this study found an unusually low prevalence of documented medical and psychiatric comorbidities (see Table 4). Rather than representing the true figure, this lower rate of comorbidities is likely to be the result of diagnostic overshadowing and systematic constraints, such as a lack of routine screening infrastructures. This is also a common observation in other settings with limited resources, where comorbid conditions frequently go undiagnosed or are misdiagnosed [46].

The baseline mean QoL score in this study (47.93 ± 10.43) was substantially lower than reported by other studies of the general Ethiopian population, which ranged from 58.92 to 63.02 [47]. It also falls below the scores of caregivers of children with DDs attending public hospitals in Addis Ababa (62.61 ± 5.17) [48]. This difference may be due to the study site, which is a tertiary referral hospital that primarily treats more severe cases. Resulting in a greater burden and a substantially lower QoL among caregivers.

The study revealed statistically significant improvements in the psychological (p = .019) and environmental (p < .001) quality of life (QoL) domains of caregivers.

The significant improvement in the environmental domain may be due to the clinical setting. Caregivers reported feeling safer and more confident in receiving training from doctors in a hospital. This setting also facilitated referrals to other medical services as needed. Having a supportive facilitator and meeting other caregivers with shared concerns fostered collective problem-solving and mutual support. Additionally, the new skills acquired by the caregivers in adjusting the home setup to promote parent‒child engagement and the development of play routines may have contributed to this improvement.

Although the overall QoL and physical and social relationship domains did not reach statistical significance, the post hoc power analysis suggested that these results are likely underpowered. The small observed effect size indicates that a larger sample size or a more sensitive longitudinal design may be necessary to detect these changes. Future studies would require approximately 300 participants to achieve the conventional statistical power of 80%.

Despite caregivers’ qualitative reports of increased psychological ability, this study revealed a minimal, statistically non-significant reduction in the overall burden score. This may represent a limitation of skill-based training: although it can strengthen caregivers´ coping skills, it does not directly address the structural and contextual stressors faced by many caregivers in LMICs, including poverty, limited social support, stigma, and inadequate access to specialized educational services [8, 15, 49–51].

Although the WHO-CST was originally designed to be delivered in community setting by non-specialist facilitators [19], these findings demonstrate that it can also be feasibly and acceptably integrated into routine clinical care. Importantly, this implementation model also offers a scalable and sustainable approach by embedding the WHO-CST competencies into the mandatory postgraduate psychiatry curriculum, thereby creating a self-renewing, institutionalized pipeline of specialized professionals. Each successive cohort of residents completing their training will enter the workforce across different regions of the country, equipped to implement the intervention and to train and supervise other non-specialist providers. This created a sustainable mechanism for scaling up the intervention and could serve as a model for other academic medical centers in low-resource settings. In addition, this finding provides important evidence for policymakers and health system managers to support the integration of family-centered care into mental health services in resource-limited settings.

To achieve more comprehensive improvements from such interventions, future clinical implementations should prioritize the integration of concrete mechanisms to support the socioeconomic challenges faced by caregivers. While the qualitative component captured the lived experiences of both caregivers and facilitators, providing deeper insight into the program’s implementation, the quantitative measures assessed the preliminary impact of the training on caregivers’ QoL and burden. The study adapted WHO-recommended home visits to clinic-based visits, demonstrating how programmatic modifications can enhance feasibility within clinical settings. In addition, the program addressed key real-world challenges by increasing caregiver enrollment, strengthening residents’ capacity, and improving community awareness about DDs and available treatment options.

The main limitations of this study include its single-institution design; the lack of a randomized control group; a small, pragmatically expanded sample size; a wide, developmentally heterogenous age range of children; the omission of direct clinical outcomes for children alongside immediate behavioral micro-skill tracking for caregivers; and potential social desirability bias arising from institutional power dynamics.

Conducting this pilot evaluation exclusively within a single tertiary care facility (SPHMMC) means that the operational findings regarding feasibility, acceptability and structural barriers may not be applicable to other health care tiers in Ethiopia, where psychiatrists or psychiatry residents are not available. Using a single-arm pre-post design means that historical or maturational confounding variables cannot be definitively excluded. Therefore, the observed improvements should be interpreted as preliminary trends rather than definitive causal effects. The small sample size was strictly limited by local operational constraints. Furthermore, the pragmatic inclusion of children up to 14 years of age, implemented to avoid inequalities in access to care resulting from long waiting lists, introduced significant developmental heterogeneity into the study population. While prioritizing global outcome measures, such as the WHOQOL-BREF and FBIS, was essential to ensure that this high-intensity protocol did not inadvertently increase the assessment and logistical burden on families, the inclusion of more proximal measures—such as caregiver micro-skills (e.g., the quality of real-time caregiver–child interactions) and child-level clinical or functional outcomes—would have enabled a more precise examination of the behavioral mechanisms linking caregiver training to observed outcomes. Finally, although rigorous reflexivity measures were employed during the analysis, the inherent institutional power dynamics, collecting post-intervention data in the same tertiary care setting where participants received treatment may have influenced participants to over-report positive experiences or under-report program challenges.

Conclusion

The study concludes that implementing the WHO-CST in a routine clinical setting is a feasible, acceptable and scalable strategy with the potential to improve specific domains of QoL among caregivers of children with DD in low-resource settings. Through the thematic lens of program acceptability, the training encouraged caregivers to communicate more empathetically and in a way that was more attuned to their children’s needs. It also improved their self-care. Helpful strategies to bridge a specialized service gaps in a tertiary care setting were identified, notably the use of service integration and capacity-building model with psychiatry residents acting as facilitators to integrate the training into the clinical service. However, the intervention faced persistent implementation challenges, including socio-economic barriers for caregivers, inconvenient infrastructure and equipment shortages. To ensure sustainability, it is essential to mitigate the environmental and financial constraints that are inherent in low-resource settings. To enhance generalizability, future research must shift from single-institution, single-arm pilots to multi-site randomized controlled trials with larger sample size and longer follow-up period. Methodologically, future protocols should adopt a multi-level measurement approach that synchronizes proximal, behavior-specific checklists to track immediate caregiver micro-skills, alongside direct, child-level clinical and functional outcome tools. Finally, to mitigate institutional power dynamics and social desirability bias systematically, subsequent qualitative evaluations should utilize independent, external research teams that are entirely decoupled from the participants’ primary clinical care delivery.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at SPHMMC (PM 23/1001) and the CIHLMU Center for International Health at Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München in Munich, Germany (opinion no. 24–0362). After receiving ethical approval, initial caregiver cohort recruitment followed sequential, chronological screening of the pre-existing waiting list. The project coordinator nurse then contacted 186 eligible caregivers by telephone and invited them to an in-person intake at the unit. Those who agreed to enroll in the training were provided with a standardized participant information sheet. Literate participants read the document independently, while illiterate participants were read the document aloud in Amharic. They were assured that declining to participate would not impact their routine medical care. A separate written consent process was administered to the resident facilitators. This guaranteed that their focus group feedback, implementation performance and fidelity metrics would be completely anonymized and handled outside of academic channels. This ensured that their participation or critical evaluations would not affect their residency evaluations or their professional standing with senior consultants. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, including both caregivers and facilitators, before data collection. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.
