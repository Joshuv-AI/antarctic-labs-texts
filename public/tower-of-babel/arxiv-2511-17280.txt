Abstract
In [ 4 ] a family of processes that converge strongly towards Brownian motion, defined from renewal processes, are constructed. In this paper we prove that some of these processes can be utilized to build approximations of Gaussian processes such as fractional Brownian motion or multiple Stratonovich integrals and we provide sufficient conditions on renewal processes to ensure that the convergence holds. An illustrative example of such a Gaussian process is the fractional Brownian motion with any Hurst parameter.



Keywords.
weak convergence, renewal process, fractional Brownian motion, multiple Stratonovich integrals

1
Introduction

Let
\{N(t),\,t\geq 0\}
a standard Poisson process and, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the process:

h_{n}=\left\{h_{n}(t):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{nt}(-1)^{N(u)}du,\,t\in[0,T]\right\}.

In 1982, Stroock (see
[
10
]
) established that the processes
h_{n}
converge in law to a standard Brownian motion.
These processes were initially introduced by Kac in 1956 (see
[
7
]
) to derive a solution to the telegraph equation from a Poisson process.

Several extensions of the Kac–Stroock result have been investigated in the literature. These include modifying the processes
h_{n}

to approximate Gaussian processes other than Brownian motion, establishing stronger forms of convergence, and relaxing the structural assumptions on the approximating processes in order to obtain convergence to Brownian motion in more general settings.

An extension in the first direction, that is, modifying the processes
h_{n}
to obtain approximations of other Gaussian processes, is presented in
[
5
]
by Delgado and Jolis, who consider a class of Gaussian processes encompassing the fractional Brownian motion. For a stochastic process
Y
with representation:

Y=\{Y_{t}=\int_{0}^{1}K(t,r)dW_{r},\,t\in[0,1]\},

where
W
is a standard Brownian motion and the kernel
K:[0,1]\times[0,1]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies certain properties, they define the process

{Y}_{t}^{n}={\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{1}K(t,r)(-1)^{N(nr)}dr.

Their result proves that
{Y}_{t}^{n}
converges in law to
Y
in the space
\mathcal{C}([0,1])
.
Also in this direction, in
[
2
]
we find results of convergence to Stratonovich multiple integrals.

The second direction of extension concerns proving convergence in a stronger sense than convergence in distribution on the space of continuous functions. Several works obtain such stronger convergence results, in which the realizations of the processes converge almost surely and uniformly on the unit interval to Brownian motion. This is achieved by modifying the processes so that they do not start with a strictly increasing behaviour. The resulting modified processes, commonly referred to as uniform transport processes, are

X_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(-1)^{A}\int_{0}^{nt}(-1)^{N(u)}du,

where
A\sim\textrm{Bern}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)
is independent of the Poisson process
N
.
Griego, Heath and Ruiz-Moncayo
[
6
]
showed that these processes
converge strongly and uniformly on bounded time intervals to
Brownian motion.

The third direction is weakening the conditions of the approximating processes to find generalizations of the processes
(-1)^{N(u)}
that also converge to Brownian motion.
In
[
3
]
we prove that if instead of using the
Poisson process we consider a process with independent increments
which characteristic function satisfies some properties we obtain
also the convergence towards the law of a Brownian
motion. Between the examples of processes that satisfy these conditions
there are included the Lévy processes.

Finally, in the work of Bardina and Rovira
[
4
]
, the authors introduce a family of processes constructed from a renewal process, which converge almost surely to Brownian motion. The objective of the present paper is to study two results concerning the weak approximation of Gaussian processes arising from these realizations of Brownian motion. In this sense, our contribution extends the framework in two directions: firstly, by approximating Gaussian processes other than Brownian motion, and secondly, by establishing convergence results for more general processes than those originally considered by Kac and Stroock.

To achieve this, we follow an approach analogous to that of Delgado and Jolis
[
5
]
for our first main result (Theorem
3.1
), while the proof of our second result (Theorem
4.1
) is inspired by the ideas developed in Bardina and Jolis
[
2
]
. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, these are the first weak convergence results obtained from processes constructed using renewal processes available in the literature. They shed light on the conditions required to ensure convergence once the convenient properties of the exponential inter-arrival distribution of the Poisson process are no longer present.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the renewal processes and the kernels that will be used to construct our approximations. Section 3 is devoted to the convergence towards integrals with respect to a deterministic kernel, while Section 4 addresses the convergence towards multiple integrals. Finally, an appendix is included, where we discuss renewal processes with geometrically distributed inter-arrival times.

2
Renewal process

Consider
\left\{U_{m}\right\}_{m\geq 1}
a sequence of independent random variables which take only nonnegative values. Furthermore, assume that they are identically distributed with
\mathbb{P}\left(U_{1}=0\right)<1
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(U_{1}\right)^{l}\right]<\infty
, for
l
large enough (for our purposes,
l\geq 4
suffices).

For each
k\geq 1
, consider the renewal sequence
S_{k}=U_{1}+\cdots+U_{k}
and the counting renewal function

L(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]}(S_{k}).

This renewal function counts the number of renewals in
[0,t]
.

Set
\left\{\eta_{m}\right\}_{m\geq 0}
a sequence of independent identically distributed random variables following a
Bernoulli(\frac{1}{2})
distribution and independent of the sequence
\left\{U_{m}\right\}_{m\geq 1}
. Then, we can construct the following renewal reward process

T(t)=\eta_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\eta_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]}(S_{k})=\sum_{l=0}^{L(t)}\eta_{l}.

Given a strictly positive function
\beta
, we can define

T_{n}(t)=T_{\beta(n)}(t)=T\left(\frac{t}{\beta(n)}\right)=\eta_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\eta_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{\left[0,\frac{t}{\beta(n)}\right]}(S_{k}).

Notice that, setting
U_{m}^{n}=\beta(n)\times U_{m}
, for all
m\geq 1
, we have that

\beta(n)\times S_{k}=U_{1}^{n}+\cdots+U_{k}^{n}.

We will consider the kernels

\displaystyle\theta_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{G(n)}(-1)^{T\left(\frac{x}{\beta(n)}\right)},

(2.1)

where

G(n)=\left(\beta(n)\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(U_{1}\right)^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[U_{1}\right]}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

with

\sum_{n\geq 1}\beta(n)<\infty.

Let us recall that, under these hypotheses, in
[
4
]
we prove that the processes

x_{n}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\theta_{n}(u)du

(2.2)

converge almost surely to the Brownian motion.

If, in addition, we assume that
U_{1}
has a failure rate function bounded away from 0,
we can go a step further and show the convergence of processes constructed from the
x_{n}
such as stochastic integrals or iterated integrals with respect to these (see Theorems
3.1
and
4.1
).
More precisely, we will assume that the cumulative distribution function
F
of
U_{1}
satisfies the following hypothesis:

(G)

F
is given by

F(t)=1-\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{t}r(s)ds\right\},\hskip 9.24994ptt\geq 0,

(and
F(t)=0
otherwise) for some non-negative function
r
such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}r(s)ds=\infty
and for which there is a positive constant
\lambda>0
such that
r(s)\geq\lambda
for every
s\geq 0
.

3
Convergence to Gaussian processes represented by a stochastic integral of a deterministic kernel

Let
X=\left\{X_{t},~t\in[0,1]\right\}
be a centered Gaussian process with covariance function given by

Cov\left(X_{t},X_{s}\right)=\int_{0}^{1}K(t,x)K(s,x)dx,

where
K\colon[0,1]\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is a function satisfying the following assumptions:

(H)\left\{\begin{matrix}[l](i)&&K~\text{is measurable and }K(0,x)=0~\forall x\in[0,1].\\
\\
(ii)&&\text{There exists a continuous increasing function }G\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}\text{ and}\\
&&\text{a constant }\alpha>0~\text{such that for any }0\leq s<t\leq 1,\\
\\
&&\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(K(t,x)-K(s,x)\right)^{2}dx\leq\left(G(t)-G(s)\right)^{\alpha}.\end{matrix}\right.

(3.1)

In our first theorem, we establish that the law of a continuous Gaussian process, represented by a stochastic integral of a deterministic kernel with respect to a standard Wiener process, can be weakly approximated by the law of processes
\left\{Y^{n}\right\}
constructed using the kernels
\theta_{n}
.
More precisely, our first theorem states as follows:

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
K
satisfies hypothesis
(H)
given in (
3.1
) and that
the distribution function of
U_{1}
satisfies hypothesis
(G)
. Let
\left\{Q^{n}\right\}
be the family of laws in
\mathcal{C}([0,1])
of the processes
\left\{Y^{n}\right\}
given by

Y_{t}^{n}=\int_{0}^{1}K(t,x)\theta_{n}(x)dx,~~\text{for any }t\in[0,1],

where
\theta_{n}
are the kernels defined in (
2.1
). Then,
\left\{Q^{n}\right\}
converges weakly in
\mathcal{C}([0,1])
, as
n\to\infty
, to a law
Q
, that has the same finite-dimensional distributions as the Gaussian process
X
.

Remark 3.2
.

This family of processes includes the fractional Brownian motion (fBm) because if we consider the kernel

K(t,s)=K^{H}(t,r)\mathbbm{1}_{[0,t]}(r)
where

K^{H}(t,r)=C_{H}(t-r)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}+C_{H}\left(\frac{1}{2}-H\right)\int_{r}^{t}(u-r)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}\left(1-\left(\frac{r}{u}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-H}\right)du,

for a certain constant
C_{H}
, then the fBm admits the
representation

B^{H}=\{B_{t}^{H}=\int_{0}^{1}K(t,r)dW_{r},\,t\in[0,1]\},

and it is easy to see that the kernel
K
satisfies the two previous
hypothesis.

To prove Theorem
3.1
, we will show that the sequence
Y^{n}
is tight and that the finite dimensional distributions converge to those of
X
. In order to check both conditions, as discussed in
[
5
]
, it is sufficient to show that

\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\theta_{n}(x)dx\right|^{m}\right]\leq C_{m}\left(\int_{0}^{1}f^{2}(x)dx\right)^{\frac{m}{2}},

(3.2)

for some constants
m>2
,
C_{m}>0
and for every
f\in L^{2}([0,1])
and that the finite dimensional distributions of the sequence
x_{n}
defined in (
2.2
) converge to those of the Brownian motion. The latter is a consequence of the fact that the sequence
x_{n}
converges to the Brownian motion in the strong sense (in
\mathcal{C}([0,1])
), which in turn implies weak convergence and thus, weak convergence of the finite dimensional distributions. Hence, the only thing left to do is to show (
3.2
). A first step towards that direction is given by Lemma
3.3
below.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any even
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in L^{2}([0,1])
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\theta_{n}(x)dx\right)^{m}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{m!}{G^{m}(n)}\int_{[0,1]^{m}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}f(x_{j})\right)\mathbb{P}\left\{L\left(\frac{x_{2j}}{\beta(n)}\right)-L\left(\frac{x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)}\right)=0\colon j=1,...,\frac{m}{2}\right\}\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{1}\leq...\leq x_{m}\}}dx_{1}...dx_{m}.

(3.3)

Proof.

We first start by observing that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\theta_{n}(x)dx\right)^{m}\right]=\frac{1}{G^{m}(n)}\int_{[0,1]^{m}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}f(x_{j})\right)\mathbb{E}\left[(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{m}T_{n}(x_{j})}\right]dx_{1}...dx_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{m!}{G^{m}(n)}\int_{[0,1]^{m}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}f(x_{j})\right)\mathbb{E}\left[(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{m}T_{n}(x_{j})}\right]\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{1}\leq...\leq x_{m}\}}dx_{1}...dx_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{m!}{G^{m}(n)}\int_{[0,1]^{m}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}f(x_{j})\right)\mathbb{E}\left[(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(T_{n}(x_{2j})-T_{n}(x_{2j-1})\right)}\right]\mathbbm{1}_{\{x_{1}\leq...\leq x_{m}\}}dx_{1}...dx_{m},

where we have used that
T
is integer-valued.

For
0\leq x_{1}\leq...\leq x_{m}
, we can write

\sum_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(T_{n}(x_{2j})-T_{n}(x_{2j-1})\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\eta_{k}\mathbbm{1}_{\bigcup_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(\frac{x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)},\frac{x_{2j}}{\beta(n)}\right]}(S_{k}),

which (almost surely) contains a finite number of summands since
S_{k}\uparrow\infty
almost surely. Hence, by letting

A=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{\bigcup_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(\frac{x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)},\frac{x_{2j}}{\beta(n)}\right]}(S_{k})>0\right\},

we see that the events

A\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994ptA\cap\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{\bigcup_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(\frac{x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)},\frac{x_{2j}}{\beta(n)}\right]}(S_{k})<\infty\right\}

(3.4)

are equal up to a null set. Now let
\mathbb{P}_{A}(\cdot)=\mathbb{P}(\cdot|A)
, we then have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(T_{n}(x_{2j})-T_{n}(x_{2j-1})\right)}\Big|A\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{A}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(T_{n}(x_{2j})-T_{n}(x_{2j-1})\right)\text{ is even}\right\}-\mathbb{P}_{A}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(T_{n}(x_{2j})-T_{n}(x_{2j-1})\right)\text{ is odd}\right\}.

On one hand,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{A}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(T_{n}(x_{2j})-T_{n}(x_{2j-1})\right)\text{ is even}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{A}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(T_{n}(x_{2j})-T_{n}(x_{2j-1})\right)\text{ is even},\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{\bigcup_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(\frac{x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)},\frac{x_{2j}}{\beta(n)}\right]}(S_{k})=i\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k_{1}<...<k_{i}}\mathbb{P}_{A}\left\{\eta_{k_{1}}+...+\eta_{k_{i}}\text{ is even},S_{k_{1}},...,S_{k_{i}}\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(\frac{x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)},\frac{x_{2j}}{\beta(n)}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k_{1}<...<k_{i}}\mathbb{P}_{A}\left\{\eta_{k_{1}}+...+\eta_{k_{i}}\text{ is even}\right\}\mathbb{P}_{A}\left\{S_{k_{1}},...,S_{k_{i}}\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(\frac{x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)},\frac{x_{2j}}{\beta(n)}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k_{1}<...<k_{i}}\mathbb{P}_{A}\left\{S_{k_{1}},...,S_{k_{i}}\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(\frac{x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)},\frac{x_{2j}}{\beta(n)}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{A}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{\bigcup_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(\frac{x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)},\frac{x_{2j}}{\beta(n)}\right]}(S_{k})=i\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}.

In the second equality the fact that the sets in (
3.4
) are the same up to a null set is being used (so that only a finite number of
S_{k_{j}}
and hence, of
\eta_{k_{j}}
, appear), in the third one we have used that the sequences
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k}
and
\{U_{k}\}_{k}
are independent, in the fourth one the fact that the probability of a binomial random variable with probability of success
1/2
being even is
1/2
(no matter how many trials are performed) is being used and in the last step we use that

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{A}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{\bigcup_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(\frac{x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)},\frac{x_{2j}}{\beta(n)}\right]}(S_{k})=i\right\}=1.

Similarly, we see that

\mathbb{P}_{A}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(T_{n}(x_{2j})-T_{n}(x_{2j-1})\right)\text{ is odd}\right\}=\frac{1}{2},

so that
\mathbb{E}\left[(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(T_{n}(x_{2j})-T_{n}(x_{2j-1})\right)}\Big|A\right]=0
. Finally, using that the renewal process
L
is non-decreasing,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(T_{n}(x_{2j})-T_{n}(x_{2j-1})\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(T_{n}(x_{2j})-T_{n}(x_{2j-1})\right)}\Big|A^{c}\right]\mathbb{P}(A^{c})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}_{\bigcup_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(\frac{x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)},\frac{x_{2j}}{\beta(n)}\right]}(S_{k})=0\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m/2}\left(L\left(\frac{x_{2j}}{\beta(n)}\right)-L\left(\frac{x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)}\right)\right)=0\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left\{L\left(\frac{x_{2j}}{\beta(n)}\right)-L\left(\frac{x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)}\right)=0\colon j=1,...,\frac{m}{2}\right\},

which finishes the proof of the lemma.

\square

L
or on the sequence of interarrival times
\{U_{k}\}_{k}

[

]

3.2

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume that
U_{1}
has cumulative distribution function
F
satisfying hypothesis
(G)
. Then (
3.2
) holds for any even
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

By Theorem 2 in
[
8
]
, one can construct a Poisson process
N
with intensity
\lambda
whose arrival times are obtained by thinning
\{S_{0},S_{1},S_{2},...\}
. More precisely, there is a Poisson process of intensity
\lambda
,
N
, such that

\{T_{0},T_{1},...\}\subset\{S_{0},S_{1},...\},\hskip 9.24994ptT_{i}=\inf\{t>0\colon N(t)\geq i\},

almost surely. This in particular implies that
L
is bounded below by
N
with probability 1 and thus, that if
L
has not performed any jumps on
(s,t]
,
0\leq s\leq t
, then
N
has not performed any jumps on
(s,t]
as well. Bearing this in mind, the probability in (

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{N\left(\frac{x_{2j}}{\beta(n)}\right)-N\left(\frac{x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)}\right)=0\colon j=1,...,\frac{m}{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m/2}\mathbb{P}\left\{N\left(\frac{x_{2j}}{\beta(n)}\right)-N\left(\frac{x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)}\right)=0\right\}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{x_{2j}-x_{2j-1}}{\beta(n)}\right\},

where in the last line we have used that the Poisson process has independent increments. From here, the same computations as in
[

lead to the desired results.

\square

Remark 3.5
.

If, for instance,
U_{1}
has the same law as the absolute value of a standard Gaussian random variable, then its density and cumulative distribution functions are given by, respectively,
f(t)=2\phi(t)
and
F(t)=2\Phi(t)-1
for
t\geq 0
and where
\phi
and
\Phi
are the density and the cumulative distribution functions of a standard Gaussian variable, respectively. It follows from
[
1
, Formula 7.1.13, p.298]
that

r(t)\geq t+\sqrt{t^{2}+\frac{8}{\pi}}\geq 2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}>0.

Implying that such
U_{1}
verifies the hypotheses of Lemma
3.4
.

The same can be said if
U_{1}
is uniformly distributed over
[0,1]
since, in this case,

r(t)=\frac{1}{1-t},\hskip 9.24994ptt\in[0,1)

and
r(t)=+\infty
for
t\geq 1
. In this case, we have
r(t)\geq 1
for all
t\geq 0.

Another example can be given by assuming that
U_{1}
is distributed as a gamma random variable of parameters
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\lambda>0
. More precisely, if
U_{1}
is absolutely continuous with density function

f(x)=\frac{\lambda^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda x}\mathbbm{1}_{(0,\infty)}(x),

where
\Gamma
denotes the Euler gamma function. In this case we have that

r(t)=\frac{f(t)}{S(t)},\hskip 9.24994ptS(t)=\int_{t}^{\infty}f(x)dx,\hskip 9.24994ptt>0,

and
r(0)=+\infty
. It is easy to show that

\lim_{t\to\infty}r(t)=\lambda>0,

hence, if we show that
r(t)
is strictly decreasing in
(0,\infty)
, we will have that
r(t)>\lambda
for each
t\in[0,\infty)
. A direct computation shows that, for
t>0
,

r^{\prime}(t)=\frac{f(t)}{S^{2}(t)}\left[\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{t}-\lambda\right)S(t)+f(t)\right].

Thus,
r^{\prime}(t)<0
if, and only if,

G(t)=\frac{tf(t)}{\lambda t-\alpha+1}-S(t)<0

for all
t\geq 0
. But the latter is a consequence of the fact that
G(0)=-1<0
, that

G^{\prime}(t)=(1-\alpha)\frac{2f(t)}{(\lambda t-\alpha+1)^{2}}>0

and that
\lim_{t\to\infty}G(t)=0
.

4
Convergence to multiple integrals

In this section, we show that our approximating processes satisfy the conditions established in
[
2
]
to ensure weak convergence towards multiple Stratonovich integrals of functions of the form
f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})=f_{1}(t_{1})\cdots f_{n}(t_{n})1_{\{t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{n}\}}
, with
f_{i}\in L^{2}([0,T])
for any
i=1,\ldots,n
.

The theorem states as follows:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f_{i}\in L^{2}([0,1])
for all
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,l\right\}
and define

\begin{matrix}[l]\displaystyle Y_{1}^{n}(t)&=&\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}f_{1}(x)\theta_{n}(x)dx,~~\text{and for }k\in\{2,\ldots,l\},\\
\\
\displaystyle Y_{k}^{n}(t)&=&\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}f_{k}(x)Y_{k-1}^{n}(x)\theta_{n}(x)dx,\end{matrix}

where
\theta_{n}
are the kernels defined in (
2.1
) and
the distribution function of
U_{1}
satisfies hypothesis
(G)
.
Then,

\mathscr{L}\left(Y_{1}^{n},\ldots,Y_{l}^{n}\right)\xlongrightarrow{w}\mathscr{L}\left(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{l}\right)

in the space
\left(\mathcal{C}_{0}\left([0,1]\right)\right)^{l}
when
n\to\infty
, where

\begin{matrix}[l]\displaystyle Y_{1}(t)&=&\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}f_{1}(x)dW_{x},~~\text{and for }k\in\{2,\ldots,l\},\\
\\
\displaystyle Y_{k}(t)&=&\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}f_{k}(x)Y_{k-1}(x)\circ dW_{x},\end{matrix}

where the last integral is in the Stratonovich sense.

We now prove Theorem
4.1
. Similarly to Theorem
3.1
, the proof of this theorem relies on proving an inequality similar to (
3.2
), which is the content of the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Under the same hypothesis as in Lemma
3.4
, for any non-negative
g\in L^{2}\left([0,1]\right)
and any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a constant
K
, that only depends on
m
and on the
L^{2}\left([0,1]\right)
-norm of the function
g
, such that, for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{[s,t]^{4}\times[0,t]^{4(m-1)}}g(x_{1})\cdots g(x_{4m})\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\theta_{n}(x_{1})\cdots\theta_{n}(x_{4m})\right]\right|\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{x_{1}\leq\cdots\leq x_{4m}\right\}}dx_{1}\cdots dx_{4m}

\displaystyle\leq K\left(\int_{s}^{t}g^{2}(x)dx\right)^{2},

where
\{\theta_{n}\}_{n}
are our kernels.

Proof.

We want to show that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{G^{4m}(n)}

\displaystyle\int_{[s,t]^{4}\times[0,t]^{4(m-1)}}g(x_{1})\cdots g(x_{4m})\left|\mathbb{E}\left[(-1)^{T_{n}\left(x_{1}\right)}\cdots(-1)^{T_{n}\left(x_{4m}\right)}\right]\right|\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{x_{1}\leq\cdots\leq x_{4m}\right\}}dx_{1}\cdots dx_{4m}

\displaystyle\leq K\left(\int_{s}^{t}g^{2}(x)dx\right)^{2}.

To show this we can proceed as in Lemma
3.3
, use Theorem 2 in
[
8
]
and follow the same computations as in
[
5
]
again to obtain that left-hand side of the latter equation can be bounded by

C_{m}\left(\int_{s}^{t}g^{2}(x)dx\right)^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}g^{2}(x)dx\right)^{2(m-1)}\leq C_{m}\left(\int_{s}^{t}g^{2}(x)dx\right)^{2}\|g\|_{2}^{4(m-1)},

for some positive constant
C_{m}
which only depends on
m
. The result follows by setting
K=C_{m}||g||_{2}^{4(m-1)}
.

\square

With this result proved, we can proceed as in the paper of Bardina and Jolis
[
2
]
in order to achieve the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Appendix

The renewal process with geometric interarrival times

In this section we shall see some properties of the renewal process
L
when the random variables
\{U_{k}\}_{k}
are geometric with parameter
p\in(0,1)
. That is,

\mathbb{P}\{U_{1}=k\}=pq^{k-1},\hskip 9.24994ptk\in\mathbb{N},\hskip 9.24994ptq=1-p.

Since
S_{n}
takes values in
\{n,n+1,...\}
(in fact, it is well known that is distributed as a negative binomial random variable with parameters
n
and
p
), one has that
L(t)=L([t])
for any
t\geq 0
and that
L(t)-L(s)\leq[t]-[s]
for any
0\leq s\leq t
since the increment
L(t)-L(s)
is at most the number of integer numbers in
(s,t]
.

The following result determines completely the law of the increments of
L
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
0\leq s\leq t
and any
m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
with
m\leq[s]
and
n\leq[t]-[s]
, we have that

\mathbb{P}\{L(s)=m,L(t)-L(s)=n\}=\binom{[s]}{m}\binom{[t]-[s]}{n}q^{[t]}\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^{m+n}.

Proof.

One has to consider several cases depending on the values of
m
and
n
. More precisely, the cases where
(m,n)=(0,0)
,
(m,n)=(0,1)
,
(m,n)=(1,0)
,
(m,n)=(0,n)
with
n\geq 2
,
(m,n)=(m,0)
with
m\geq 1
,
(m,n)=(m,1)
with
m\geq 1
and, lastly, the case
m\geq 1
and
n\geq 2
have to be considered separately. We will only detail the computations for the latter, that is, we will be assuming that
m
and
n
are such that

1\leq m\leq[s],\hskip 9.24994pt2\leq n,\hskip 9.24994ptm+n\leq[t].

We have that

\mathbb{P}\{L(s)=m,L(t)-L(s)=n\}=\mathbb{P}\{L(s)=m,L(t)=n+m\}=\mathbb{P}\{S_{m}\leq s<S_{m+1}\leq S_{m+n}\leq t<S_{m+n+1}\}.

Let us define the random variables,

\displaystyle X_{1}

\displaystyle=S_{m}=Y_{1},

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle=S_{m+1}=Y_{1}+Y_{2},

\displaystyle X_{3}

\displaystyle=S_{m+n}=Y_{1}+Y_{2}+Y_{3},

\displaystyle X_{4}

\displaystyle=S_{m+n+1}=Y_{1}+Y_{2}+Y_{3}+Y_{4},

where the random variables
Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3},Y_{4}
are independent with laws

Y_{1}\sim\text{NB}(m,p),\hskip 9.24994ptY_{2}\sim\text{Geom}(p),\hskip 9.24994ptY_{3}\sim\text{NB}(n-1,p),\hskip 9.24994ptY_{4}\sim\text{Geom}(p),

(NB
(m,p)
and Geom
(p)
denote, respectively, a negative binomial random variable of parameters
m
and
p
and a geometric random variable of parameter
p
).

The next step is to write the set

D=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in\mathbb{N}^{4}\colon m\leq x_{1}\leq s<x_{2}<x_{3}\leq t<x_{4}\right\}

as the set of points
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in\mathbb{N}^{4}
such that

\hskip 9.24994ptm\leq x_{1}\leq[s],\hskip 9.24994pt[s]+1\leq x_{2}\leq x_{3}-1,\hskip 9.24994pt[s]+2\leq x_{3}\leq[t],\hskip 9.24994pt[t]+1\leq x_{4}.

With all this, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{S_{m}\leq s<S_{m+1}\leq S_{m+n}\leq t<S_{m+n+1}\}=\mathbb{P}\{(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})\in D\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x_{1},...,x_{4})\in D}\mathbb{P}\{X_{1}=x_{1},X_{2}=x_{2},X_{3}=x_{3},X_{4}=x_{4}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x_{1},...,x_{4})\in D}\mathbb{P}\{Y_{1}=x_{1},Y_{2}=x_{2}-x_{1},Y_{3}=x_{3}-x_{2},Y_{4}=x_{4}-x_{3}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x_{1},...,x_{4})\in D}\binom{x_{1}-1}{m-1}p^{m}q^{x_{1}-m}pq^{x_{2}-x_{1}-1}\binom{x_{3}-x_{2}-1}{n-2}p^{n-1}q^{x_{3}-x_{2}-n+1}pq^{x_{4}-x_{3}-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x_{1},...,x_{4})\in D}\binom{x_{1}-1}{m-1}\binom{x_{3}-x_{2}-1}{n-2}p^{m+n+1}q^{x_{4}-m-n-1},

where we use that
\binom{k}{m}=0
if
k<m
.
This last sum can be factored as a product of three sums, one depending only on
x_{1}
, another depending on
x_{2}
and
x_{3}
and another one depending only on
x_{4}
. The latter is a geometric sum, so we will not show how to compute it. For the former (the sum indexed by
x_{1}
), we will use the fact that

\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{n+k}{n}=\binom{m+n+1}{n+1}.

Bearing this in mind, we find that

\sum_{x_{1}=m}^{[s]}\binom{x_{1}-1}{m-1}=\sum_{x_{1}=0}^{[s]-m}\binom{m-1+x_{1}}{m-1}=\binom{[s]}{m}.

Thus, so far we got that

\mathbb{P}\{L(s)=m,L(t)-L(s)=n\}=p^{m+n}q^{[t]-m-n}\binom{[s]}{m}\sum_{x_{3}=[s]+2}^{[t]}\sum_{x_{2}=[s]+1}^{x_{3}-1}\binom{x_{3}-x_{2}-1}{n-2}.

This last sum can be treated by similar means as the sum indexed by
x_{1}
, but now using that

\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{j}{m}=\binom{n+1}{m+1},

and that
n\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{3}=[s]+2}^{[t]}\sum_{x_{2}=[s]+1}^{x_{3}-1}\binom{x_{3}-x_{2}-1}{n-2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{2}=[s]+1}^{[t]-1}\sum_{x_{3}=x_{2}+1}^{[t]}\binom{x_{3}-x_{2}-1}{n-2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{2}=[s]+1}^{[t]-1}\sum_{x_{3}=0}^{[t]-x_{2}-1}\binom{x_{3}}{n-2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{2}=[s]+1}^{[t]-1}\binom{[t]-x_{2}}{n-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{2}=1}^{[t]-[s]-1}\binom{x_{2}}{n-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{2}=0}^{[t]-[s]-1}\binom{x_{2}}{n-1}

\displaystyle=\binom{[t]-[s]}{n}.

\square

This result, in particular, shows that (by setting
s=0
and
m=0
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{L(t)=n\}=q^{[t]}\binom{[t]}{n}\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^{n}.

Moreover, one can easily see as well that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{L(t)-L(s)=n\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{[s]}\mathbb{P}\{L(s)=m,L(t)-L(s)=n\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{[s]}\binom{[s]}{m}\binom{[t]-[s]}{n}q^{[t]}\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^{m+n}

\displaystyle=\binom{[t]-[s]}{n}q^{[t]}\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^{n}\left(1+\frac{p}{q}\right)^{[s]}

\displaystyle=\binom{[t]-[s]}{n}q^{[t]-[s]-n}p^{n}.

Which shows that, for any
0\leq s\leq t
,
L(s)
and
L(t)-L(s)
are independent and that, when
0\leq s\leq t
with
s,t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the law of
L(t)-L(s)
is the same as the law of
L(t-s)
(observe that this need not be true when
s
and
t
are not in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
).

Finally, one can show that the increments of the process
L
are independent.

Proposition 4.4
.

The increments of
L
are independent.

Sketch of the proof.

Since
L(t)=L([t])
for all
t\geq 0
, we only need to show that the increments are independent when the process is considered in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. That is, we only need to show that, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and any
0\leq t_{0}\leq t_{2}\leq...\leq t_{k}
with
t_{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
for every
j\in\{0,...,k\}
, the random variables
L(t_{0}),L(t_{1})-L(t_{0}),...,L(t_{k})-L(t_{k-1})
are independent. The proof of this last fact is quite similar to the one for the case in which the random variables
\{U_{j}\}_{j}
are exponentially distributed (that is, when
L
is a Poisson process).

Let us define

U_{1}^{t}=S_{L(t)+1}-t,\hskip 9.24994ptU_{k}^{t}=U_{L(t)+k},\hskip 9.24994ptk\geq 2.

By the independence the sequence
\{U_{j}\}_{j}
, for any
y_{1},...,y_{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{L(t)=n,S_{n+1}-t>y_{1},U_{n+2}>y_{2},...,U_{n+j}>y_{j}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{S_{n}\leq t<S_{n+1},S_{n+1}-t>y_{1},U_{n+2}>y_{2},...,U_{n+j}>y_{j}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{S_{n}\leq t<S_{n+1},S_{n+1}-t>y_{1}\}q^{y_{2}}\cdot...\cdot q^{y_{j}}.

But

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{S_{n}\leq t<S_{n+1},S_{n+1}-t>y_{1}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{S_{n}\leq t,U_{n+1}>t-S_{n},U_{n+1}>y_{1}+t-S_{n}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{S_{n}\leq t,U_{n+1}>y_{1}+t-S_{n}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{t}\mathbb{P}\{U_{n+1}>y_{1}+t-x\}\mathbb{P}\{S_{n}=x\}

\displaystyle=q^{y_{1}}\sum_{x=0}^{t}\mathbb{P}\{U_{n+1}>t-x\}\mathbb{P}\{S_{n}=x\}

\displaystyle=q^{y_{1}}\mathbb{P}\{S_{n}\leq t,U_{n+1}>t-S_{n}\}

\displaystyle=q^{y_{1}}\mathbb{P}\{L(t)=n\}.

Hence,

\mathbb{P}\{L(t)=n,S_{n+1}-t>y_{1},U_{n+2}>y_{2},...,U_{n+j}>y_{j}\}=\mathbb{P}\{L(t)=n\}q^{y_{1}}\cdot...\cdot q^{y_{j}},

which, in turn, implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{L(t)=n,(U_{1}^{t},...,U_{j}^{t})\in H\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{L(t)=n,S_{n+1}-t>y_{1},U_{n+2}>y_{2},...,U_{n+j}>y_{j}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{L(t)=n\}\mathbb{P}\{(U_{1},...,U_{j})\in H\},

being
H=(y_{1},\infty)\times...\times(y_{j},\infty)
. Therefore, the law of
(U_{1}^{t},...,U_{j}^{t})
conditioned on
L(t)=n
is the same as the law of the vector
(U_{1},...,U_{j})
. From here, it follows that, for any
n_{0},...n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{L(t_{0})=n_{0},L(t_{1})-L(t_{0})=n_{1},...,L(t_{k})-L(t_{k-1})=n_{k}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{L(t_{0})=n_{0},L(t_{1})=n_{0}+n_{1},...,L(t_{k})=n_{0}+...+n_{k}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{L(t_{0})=n_{0},S_{n_{0}+...+n_{j}}\leq t_{j}<S_{n_{0}+...+n_{j+1}},j=1,...,k\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{L(t_{0})=n_{0},U_{1}^{t_{0}}+...+U_{n_{j}}^{t_{0}}\leq t_{j}-t_{0}<U_{1}^{t_{0}}+...+U_{n_{j+1}}^{t_{0}},j=1,...,k\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{L(t_{0})=n_{0},(U_{1}^{t_{0}},...,U_{n_{k}+1}^{t_{0}})\in\tilde{H}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{L(t_{0})=n_{0}\}\mathbb{P}\{(U_{1},...,U_{n_{k}+1})\in\tilde{H}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{L(t_{0})=n_{0}\}\mathbb{P}\{L(t_{1}-t_{0})=n_{1},...,L(t_{k}-t_{0})=n_{1}+...+n_{k}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{L(t_{0})=n_{0}\}\mathbb{P}\{L(t_{1}-t_{0})=n_{1},L(t_{2}-t_{0})-L(t_{1}-t_{0})=n_{2},...,L(t_{k}-t_{0})-L(t_{k-1}-t_{0})=n_{k}\},

where

\tilde{H}=\{(u_{1},...,u_{n_{k}+1})\colon u_{1}+...+u_{n_{1}+...+n_{j}}\leq t_{j}-t_{0}<u_{1}+...+u_{n_{1}+...+n_{j}+1},j=1,...,k\}.

Inductively, we can show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{L(t_{0})=n_{0},L(t_{1})-L(t_{0})=n_{1},...,L(t_{k})-L(t_{k-1})=n_{k}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{L(t_{0})=n_{0}\}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}\{L(t_{j}-t_{j-1})=n_{j}\}.

The only thing left to do is to recall that
L(t-s)\sim L(t)-L(s)
whenever
0\leq s\leq t
with
s,t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

\square
