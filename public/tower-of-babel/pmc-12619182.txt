On-line identification of chemical constituents in Polygoni Multiflori Radix and Polygoni Multiflori Radix Praeparata based on UHPLC-Q-Orbitrap-Linear Ion Trap-MS
Authors: Rui Yao, Hong Guo, Xiao-Shu Zhang, Ying Wang, Xiao-Han Guo, Bao-Zhong Duan, Wen-Guang Jing, Jian-Bo Yang, Xian-Long Cheng, Feng Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo rapidly characterize the chemical constituents of Polygoni Multiflori Radix and its processed form, Polygoni Multiflori Radix Preparata, utilizing ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole-orbitrap-linear ion trap high-resolution mass spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-Orbitrap-Linear Ion Trap-MS).MethodsChromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH phenyl column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) employing a gradient elution of methanol (A) and 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution (B). Mass spectrometric analysis was conducted using a heated electrospray ionization (H-ESI) source operated in negative ion mode. Data processing was performed using Thermo Scientific Compound Discoverer 3.0 software, integrating a self-constructed Polygoni Multiflori Radix MS and MS/MS spectral database. Compounds with a mass error tolerance of ≤ |5| ppm were tentatively identified via freestyle software and further validated by comparison with previously reported literature data.ResultsA total of 231 chemical constituents were tentatively identified through online analysis, comprising 64 stilbene glycosides, 28 anthraquinones, 38 flavonoids, 45 polyphenols, 18 amino acids, 7 naphthalenes, 6 chromones, 3 alkaloids, 5 fatty acids, and 17 other components. Following 24 h of processing, the levels of stilbene glycosides, free anthraquinones, torachrysone, and torachrysone-8-O-β-D-glucoside were observed to decrease, while the concentrations of dianthrones and bound anthraquinones exhibited a significant reduction. In contrast, the content of gallic acid increased markedly.ConclusionsThese findings provide a robust scientific foundation for further exploration of the active and toxic components in Polygoni Multiflori Radix and its processed form, Polygoni Multiflori Radix Preparata, contributing to a deeper understanding of their pharmacological and toxicological profiles.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-025-01672-2.

Introduction

Polygoni Multiflori Radix, derived from the dried tuberous roots of Polygonum multiflorum Thunb., is a traditional Chinese medicinal herb renowned for its bitter, sweet, and astringent taste, as well as its slightly warm properties. It is traditionally associated with the liver, heart, and kidney meridians and has been widely utilized for its detoxifying, anti-abscess, antimalarial, and laxative effects. The processed form of Polygoni Multiflori Radix, known as Polygoni Multiflori Radix Preparata, exhibits enhanced pharmacological activities, including tonifying the liver and kidneys, replenishing essence and blood, darkening hair, strengthening bones and tendons, and reducing turbid lipids [1]. However, with the expanding clinical application of Polygoni Multiflori Radix and Polygoni Multiflori Radix Preparata, increasing attention has been directed toward their potential hepatotoxicity. Studies have demonstrated that both Polygoni Multiflori Radix and its processed form may induce liver dysfunction and even drug-induced liver injury (DILI), raising significant concerns regarding their clinical safety and quality control [2, 3]. Given that the chemical constituents of Polygoni Multiflori Radix are the primary determinants of its pharmacological efficacy and toxicity, a comprehensive and systematic investigation into its chemical composition is of paramount importance.

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) is characterized by its multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway nature, underpinned by a highly complex chemical composition [4]. In the analysis of TCM constituents, liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) has demonstrated significant advantages, enabling the efficient detection and identification of trace components within complex matrices [5]. High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS), in particular, not only delivers precise molecular weight information but also generates extensive structural data through multi-stage mass spectrometry (MS/MS), facilitating the comprehensive characterization of chemical constituents [6]. Additionally, LC-MS requires minimal sample quantities and offers rapid analysis, effectively addressing challenges such as component loss or degradation often associated with traditional analytical methods. Consequently, LC-MS has emerged as a highly efficient, sensitive, and reliable analytical tool for the systematic investigation of chemical constituents in TCM [7].

In previous studies, our research team conducted a comprehensive chemical characterization of Polygoni Multiflori Radix, identifying a total of 152 compounds, including 50 anthraquinones, 33 stilbenes, 21 flavonoids, 7 naphthalenes, and 41 other compounds [8], utilizing a zebrafish model for toxicity evaluation, we further demonstrated that the toxicity of Polygoni Multiflori Radix could be reduced by more than 85% following pure steaming for 24 h, achieving the desired detoxification effect [9]. Given that the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 edition) specifies three processing methods for Polygoni Multiflori Radix Preparata—stewing with black bean juice, steaming, and steaming with black bean juice—this study employed ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole-orbitrap-linear ion trap mass spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-Orbitrap-Linear Ion Trap-MS) to systematically analyze the chemical profiles of Polygoni Multiflori Radix and Polygoni Multiflori Radix Preparata processed using these three methods. To comprehensively capture the diverse chemical constituents, extraction was performed using three solvents of varying polarities: water, 70% ethanol (v/v), and 95% ethanol (v/v). By constructing a chemical database for Polygoni Multiflori Radix Preparata and comparing the results with literature-reported compounds, we not only systematically tentatively identified the chemical constituents in Polygoni Multiflori Radix Preparata but also summarized the changes in the content of different types of compounds before and after processing. This study provides a robust scientific foundation for further research into the bioactive and toxic components of Polygoni Multiflori Radix and Polygoni Multiflori Radix Preparata, offering valuable insights for establishing quality standards and evaluating the quality of these medicinal materials.

Sample information

Reparation of sample solutions

Approximately 1g of Polygoni Multiflori Radix and Polygoni Multiflori Radix Praeparata powder (passed through a No. 4 sieve) was accurately weighed and transferred into a stoppered conical flask. A total of 20 mL of water, 70% ethanol (v/v), or 95% ethanol (v/v) was precisely added to each flask, and the total weight was recorded. The mixtures were then subjected to ultrasonication for 20 mins (Power: 300 W, frequency: 40 kHz). After extraction, the solutions were allowed to cool to room temperature, and the weight was re-measured. Any weight loss due to solvent evaporation was compensated by adding the respective solvent. The mixtures were thoroughly mixed, filtered, and the resulting filtrate was further passed through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane to obtain the final sample solution.

Liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry conditions

Liquid chromatography conditions: The separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Phenyl column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm). The mobile phase consisted of methanol (A) and 0.1% formic acid (v/v) aqueous solution (B) with the following gradient elution program: 0–5 min, 25–30% A; 5–15 min, 30–40% A; 15–18 min, 40–50% A; 18–23 min, 50–55% A; 23–30 min, 55–65% A; 30–35 min, 65–85% A; 35–40 min, 85% A; 40–50 min, 85–90% A; 50–60 min, 90% A; 60–61 min, 10–75% B; 61–70 min, 25% A. The column temperature was maintained at 35° C, and the detection wavelengths were set at 254 nm and 230 nm. The injection volume was 1 µL.

Mass spectrometry conditions: data acquisition was performed using a heated electrospray ionization (ESI) source in negative ion mode. For full-scan MS1 analysis, the mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) range was set to 100–1500 with a resolution of 120,000 (defined as the full width at half maximum (FWHM) at m/z 200), and the RF lens was maintained at 50%. The ion source parameters were optimized as follows: spray voltage, 3000 V; sheath gas pressure, 45 arbitrary units (Arb); auxiliary gas pressure, 15 Arb; sweep gas pressure, 0 Arb; ion transfer tube temperature, 350 °C; and vaporizer temperature, 320 °C. For MS/MS analysis, higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) was employed with stepped collision energies of 20, 40, 60, and 80 eV. collision energy mode: assisted; The detector was operated in Orbitrap mode with a resolution of 30,000 (defined as the full width at half maximum (FWHM) at m/z 200). Intensity threshold:2.5e4;Isolation Mode: Quadrupole; Isolation Window (m/z): 2; Data Dependent Mode: Number of Scans; Number of Dependent Scans: 5; Data dependent acquisition (DDA). Exclude after n times: 1; Exclusion duration(s): 8; AGC Target: Standard; Maximum Injection Time Mode: Auto. Sequence setup: The samples were injected in a randomized order, with a blank solvent wash inserted after every three sample injections.

Data analysis

The acquired mass spectrometry data were processed using Thermo Scientific Compound Discoverer 3.0 software. A comprehensive MS and MS/MS database was constructed by retrieving relevant literature and compound databases (including SciFinder, ChemSpider, mzVault, mzCloud, PubMed, CNKI, and ScienceDirect). This database encompassed chemical names, molecular formulas, m/z values, exact molecular weights, and fragment ions. The initial step involved interrogating the precursor ion signals from the high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) data to determine their exact molecular masses, with a mass accuracy threshold set within |5| parts per million (ppm). Subsequently, the characteristic fragment ions from the MS/MS spectra were analyzed and assigned by comparing them against the established dissociation pathways prevalent for the compound class, such as Retro-Diels-Alder (RDA) fragmentation, deglycosylation, dehydration, and demethylation. The acquired MS/MS spectra were then cross-referenced against public spectral databases (e.g., mzCloud, mzVault) and published literature data for further confirmation. For features sharing the same molecular formula but exhibiting different retention times, the identification workflow was conducted as follows: First, the retention time of each feature was assessed for its consistency with the expected chromatographic behavior for that class of compounds. Subsequently, the corresponding MS/MS spectra were interrogated for the presence of characteristic fragment ions reported in the literature for the compound of interest. Features satisfying both criteria—plausible retention time and matching characteristic fragments—were annotated as potential structural analogs or isomers of the putative compound. Owing to the lack of authentic standards for co-chromatographic confirmation and the inherent limitations in unambiguously defining precise structural variations based solely on MS/MS spectral differences, all such assignments were assigned a confidence level of 3 (Tentative identification). We acknowledge that these features may encompass positional isomers as well as other structural analogs that yield identical characteristic fragments upon mass spectrometric fragmentation, fragment ion information was also analyzed using Freestyle software, enabling manual comparison and verification of fragment patterns.

Fragmentation patterns and analysis of stilbene glycosides

In our preliminary laboratory studies, mass spectrometry analysis was conducted on reference standards of cis- and trans-stilbene glucosides. Despite their configurational differences [8], both compounds exhibited identical mass spectral characteristics. A quasi-molecular ion peak [M-H]⁻ was observed at m/z 405.1210. In the MS/MS spectrum, a fragment ion peak appeared at m/z 243.0670, resulting from the loss of a hexose moiety (162 Da, C6H10O5) from the quasi-molecular ion. These characteristic ions (m/z 405.1210 and 243.0670) serve as key markers for identifying stilbene glucosides, confirming that compounds S34 and S35 are trans- and cis-stilbene glucosides, respectively. The fragmentation pathway of trans-stilbene glucoside is illustrated in supplementary Fig. 5. Based on the structural features of stilbene glucosides, substituents on their derivatives may be located either on the glycosyl hydroxyl group or the phenolic hydroxyl group. By analyzing the fragmentation patterns, the position of the substituents can be inferred. If the parent ion initially loses a hexose moiety (162 Da), the substituent is likely attached to the phenolic hydroxyl group. Conversely, if the parent ion first loses the substituent and then generates the fragment ion at m/z 405, the substituent may be located on either the glycosyl or phenolic hydroxyl group. Additionally, if an ion peak corresponding to m/z (hexoside + substituent) is observed in the mass spectrum without the appearance of a hexose loss fragment, it can be deduced that the substituent is attached to the glycosyl hydroxyl group [11].

Discussion

The isomers discussed in this study primarily include geometric (cis/trans) and positional isomers. For geometric isomers, although they may generate identical fragment ions, their spatial configurations differ, leading to distinct retention behaviors in reversed-phase chromatography. Trans isomers typically exhibit higher planarity and lower polarity, resulting in stronger hydrophobic interactions with the stationary phase and generally longer retention times compared to cis isomers, as documented in several literature sources [8, 22]. Therefore, for compounds with previously reported geometric isomerism, our identification was based on retention time and fragment ion abundance. For other compounds potentially exhibiting geometric isomerism, further confirmation with reference standards will be required. For positional isomers, the distinct locations of substituents lead to significant differences in their MS/MS fragmentation patterns, allowing for effective differentiation.

Chemical constituents serve as the fundamental basis for the pharmacological efficacy and toxicological properties of medicinal substances. In recent years, there has been a notable increase in reports of hepatotoxicity associated with Polygoni Multiflori Radix. While processing techniques have been shown to mitigate its toxicity, instances of hepatotoxicity induced by processed Polygoni Multiflori Radix persist. This phenomenon may be attributed to non-standardized processing protocols, ambiguous processing criteria, and suboptimal processing durations, resulting in substantial quality variability among commercially available Polygoni Multiflori Radix Preparata products [23]. Previous investigations conducted by our research team demonstrated that the median lethal concentration (LC₅₀) of Polygoni Multiflori Radix in zebrafish was 27.73 mg·L− 1. Following a standardized 24-hour processing protocol, the toxicity was significantly reduced, yielding an LC₅₀ value of 79.20 mg·L− 1 [9], thereby establishing 24 h as the optimal processing duration. The observed toxicity variations are intrinsically linked to alterations in chemical composition, with the extraction efficiency of toxic constituents being influenced by solvent selection. For instance, Yang et al. [2] assessed the toxicity of various Polygoni Multiflori Radix extracts using zebrafish embryos and reported a toxicity hierarchy of 70% ethanol extract >95% ethanol extract >aqueous extract. Furthermore, the excipients and methodologies employed during processing are pivotal factors contributing to toxicity discrepancies. Consequently, this study focused on Polygoni Multiflori Radix and three distinct 24-hour processed Polygoni Multiflori Radix samples prepared using different processing techniques. Extractions were conducted using water, 70% ethanol (v/v), and 95% ethanol (v/v) to systematically identify and analyze the chemical constituents in both raw and processed Polygoni Multiflori Radix. The objective was to elucidate the material basis of Polygoni Multiflori Radix and provide scientific insights into the chemical underpinnings of its toxicity variations. The subsequent analysis is exemplified using Polygoni Multiflori Radix and Polygoni Multiflori Radix processed by steaming for 24 h.

Dianthrone components

In our prior investigations, dianthrone derivatives were successfully isolated for the first time. The C10-C10’ bond in dianthrones is inherently labile and can undergo cleavage under thermal conditions, yielding monoanthrones, which are subsequently oxidized to anthracenes. Anthracenes can further transform into anthraquinones, while anthraquinones can reversibly convert back to dianthrones under specific conditions. Notably, anthraquinones are metabolized into anthrones within the gastrointestinal tract prior to absorption [24, 25]. Li et al. [26] developed a zebrafish hepatotoxicity model to assess the hepatotoxic potential of constituents in Polygoni Multiflori Radix. Their findings revealed that both (cis)-emodin-emodin dianthrone and (trans)-emodin-emodin dianthrone exhibited pronounced hepatotoxicity. Furthermore, one of the adverse effects associated with Polygoni Multiflori Radix is the disruption of bilirubin metabolism. Uridine diphosphate (UDP)-glucuronosyltransferase 1A1 (UGT1A1) is the exclusive enzyme responsible for bilirubin metabolism. Wang [27] explored the inhibitory effects of Polygoni Multiflori Radix extracts and dianthrone derivatives on UGT1A1 using an in vitro rat liver microsome assay. The results demonstrated that (cis/trans)-emodin-emodin dianthrones significantly inhibited UGT1A1 activity, whereas polygonumnolide C3 exhibited moderate inhibition and polygonumnolide C4 showed weak inhibition. These observations suggest that dianthrone derivatives are likely among the principal toxic constituents.

Building on these findings, this study quantified three tentatively identified dianthrone compounds—(trans)-emodin-emodin dianthrone, (cis)-emodin-emodin dianthrone, and Polygonumnolide B1 by analyzing their peak areas. The results demonstrated that the extraction efficiency followed the order: 70% ethanol extract > 95% ethanol extract > aqueous extract. Post-processing, the content of dianthrone derivatives exhibited a significant decline. Specifically, the peak areas of (cis/trans)-emodin-emodin dianthrones were reduced by over 30%, while the peak area of Polygonumnolide B1 decreased by more than fivefold, as depicted in Fig. 8.

Anthraquinone components

Anthraquinone compounds, such as emodin, chrysophanol, rhein, aloe-emodin, physcion, and their respective glycosides, serve as both the bioactive and toxic constituents of Polygoni Multiflori Radix, demonstrating significant hepatotoxicity [26]. zhang et al. [28] revealed that emodin induces hepatocyte injury, exhibiting pronounced cytotoxicity on the human hepatocyte line L-02 in a concentration- and time-dependent manner. Moreover, the intracellular accumulation of emodin displayed time-dependent toxicokinetic behavior. Additional studies have indicated that Polygoni Multiflori Radix primarily disrupts phenylalanine and tyrosine metabolism, potentially contributing to liver injury accompanied by chronic kidney damage. Prolonged administration of Polygoni Multiflori Radix progressively affects vitamin B6, bile acid, and bilirubin metabolism, exacerbating liver injury, altering biochemical indicators, and inducing marked nephrotoxicity. Utilizing a zebrafish hepatotoxicity evaluation model, Li [26] identified emodin and emodin-8-O-β-D-glucoside as compounds with significant hepatotoxic potential.

Building on these findings, this study quantified six identified anthraquinone compounds—emodin, 2-acetyl emodin, emodin-8-methyl ether, emodin-O-(malonyl)-hexoside, emodin-8-O-β-D-glucoside, and physcion-8-O-β-D-glucoside by analyzing their peak areas. The results demonstrated that, with the exception of emodin-8-methyl ether, the extraction efficiency followed the order: 70% ethanol extract >95% ethanol extract >aqueous extract. Emodin-8-methyl ether was most effectively extracted using 95% ethanol (v/v). Post-processing, the content of emodin decreased by over 10%, 2-acetyl emodin by approximately 20%, and emodin-8-methyl ether by more than threefold. These free anthraquinones were extracted significantly more efficiently with ethanol than with water. In contrast, the peak areas of emodin-O-(malonyl)-hexoside decreased by more than 80%, while emodin-8-O-β-D-glucoside and physcion-8-O-β-D-glucoside decreased by over 60%. The introduction of glycosyl groups enhanced hydrophilicity and polarity, reducing intermolecular interactions and increasing the water solubility of these compounds compared to free anthraquinones. Detailed results are presented in Fig. 9. After 24 h of processing, the content of free anthraquinones, such as emodin, decreased primarily due to thermal sublimation, whereas the content of conjugated anthraquinones, such as emodin-8-O-β-D-glucoside, decreased due to the thermal cleavage of glycosidic bonds, leading to their decomposition into free anthraquinones [29].

Stilbene glycoside components

Stilbene glycosides represent the principal bioactive constituents in Polygoni Multiflori Radix. Among these, trans-stilbene glycosides exhibit a range of pharmacological properties, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-atherosclerotic activities [30], Following 24 h of processing, the content of trans-stilbene glycosides is reduced, with processed Polygoni Multiflori Radix containing significantly lower levels of stilbene glycosides compared to Polygoni Multiflori Radix [29]. Research has demonstrated that stilbene glycosides can mitigate the decline in renal function in diabetic nephropathy rats, potentially through the modulation of renal SUR2B/Kir6.1 subtype KATP channels [31]. Xing et al. [32] employed UHPLC/MS-MS to investigate the influence of trans-2,3,5,4’-tetrahydroxystilbene-2-O-β-D-glucoside (THSG) on the pharmacokinetics of emodin in rats. Their findings revealed that Polygoni Multiflori Radix extract containing THSG led to the accumulation of emodin in the body after 7 days of administration, whereas a single dose of THSG significantly enhanced emodin exposure over the same period. Further analysis indicated that THSG may accelerate emodin exposure and metabolism by upregulating cytochrome P450 enzyme activity. Consequently, it is hypothesized that hepatotoxicity may arise from the synergistic interaction between stilbene glycosides and emodin.

Based on these findings, this study quantified five tentatively identified stilbene glycosides—trans-stilbene glycoside, tetrahydroxystilbene-O-(acetyl)-hexoside, pentahydroxy-phenanthrene-O-hexoside, 2,3,5,4’-tetrahydroxystilbene-2-O-β-D-(2’’-O-monogalloyl ester)-glucoside, and multiflorumiside A—by analyzing their peak areas. The results demonstrated that the extraction efficiency followed the order: 70% ethanol extract > 95% ethanol extract > aqueous extract. Post-processing, the peak areas of all stilbene glycosides exhibited a decline. Specifically, the peak area of trans-stilbene glycoside decreased by over 10%, tetrahydroxystilbene-O-(acetyl)-hexoside by more than 5%, pentahydroxy-phenanthrene-O-hexoside by approximately 30%, 2,3,5,4’-tetrahydroxystilbene-2-O-β-D-(2’’-O-monogalloyl ester)-glucoside by over 50%, and multiflorumiside A by more than 18%. Detailed results are presented in Fig. 10.

Gallic acid

Gallic acid, a polyphenolic compound, exhibits a range of pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, but its potential hepatotoxicity has also garnered significant attention. Song et al. [33] compared the hepatotoxic effects of several components from Polygoni Multiflori Radix using the normal human L02 hepatocyte line as an evaluation model. Their findings revealed that gallic acid, rhein, and emodin exhibited high inhibition rates on hepatocytes at the same concentration, with gallic acid demonstrating significantly stronger hepatotoxicity than rhein and emodin, suggesting it may be a potential hepatotoxic component. However, other studies have shown that gallic acid can inhibit tert-butyl hydroperoxide (t-BHP)-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and glutathione (GSH) elevation in L02 cells, thereby exerting protective effects against t-BHP-induced hepatotoxicity [34]. During the thermal processing of Polygoni Multiflori Radix, gallic acid is generated as a degradation product of small-molecule phenolic compounds [29], This study found that the content of gallic acid increased by more than 80% after 24 h of processing, consistent with literature reports [29], The extraction efficiency followed the order: 70% ethanol extract >aqueous extract >95% ethanol extract, as detailed in Fig. 11.

Conclusion

In this study, a total of 231 chemical constituents were identified from Polygoni Multiflori Radix. The fragmentation patterns of three major categories of chemical constituents were systematically summarized and illustrated, providing valuable insights into the fragmentation mechanisms of Polygoni Multiflori Radix components. Quantitative analysis based on peak areas revealed that 70% ethanol (v/v) extraction demonstrated superior efficiency for most compounds compared to 95% ethanol (v/v) and aqueous extraction. Following 24 h of processing, the contents of various bioactive components, including stilbene glycosides, free anthraquinones, combined anthraquinones, torachrysone, torachrysone-8-O-β-D-glucoside, and dianthrones, exhibited varying degrees of reduction. The underlying reason is that high temperatures during processing lead to the cleavage of glycosidic bonds, resulting in the conversion of compounds into their aglycone forms [37]. This transformation aligns closely with the traditional objective of “detoxification,” which posits that processing can reduce the potential hepatotoxicity of Polygoni Multiflori Radix. Concurrently, the content of gallic acid exhibits a significant increase, likely due to the hydrolysis of complex compounds such as proanthocyanidins, gallic acid esters, and glycosides containing galloyl groups under high-temperature and high-pressure steaming conditions [38]. This hydrolysis generates free gallic acid monomers, suggesting that the processing procedure may not merely involve the degradation of constituents but rather represents a directional transformation characterized by “both decreases and increases” in specific components. This process may be associated with alterations in the pharmacological effects of the herb.

A limitation of this study lies in the employment of a single ionization mode for sample analysis, which may have resulted in incomplete identification of certain compounds that preferentially form hydroxide ions. To enhance the comprehensiveness and reliability of chemical profiling in future research, it is recommended to implement a comprehensive analytical approach incorporating multiple ionization modes (both positive and negative ion modes). This strategy would facilitate more thorough coverage of the chemical constituents present in Polygoni Multiflori Radix samples and improve the accuracy of identification results. Furthermore, the lack of systematic adoption of quantitative methodology utilizing isotope-labeled internal standards and calibration curves is also a limitation of this study.In follow-up studies, we will acquire corresponding reference standards (through commercial procurement or isolation) for the key candidate compounds identified in this work, followed by rigorous targeted verification analyses. This validation will implement a standardized protocol comprising stable isotope-labeled internal standards, multi-point calibration curves, and full methodological validation (including specificity, linearity, accuracy, precision, and stability) to achieve precise quantification and confirmation of these compounds.
