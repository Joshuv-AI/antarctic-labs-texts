From arcs to curves: Quadratic growth of 1-systems
Tarik Aougab, Jonah Gaster

Abstract
. We show that the largest size of a collection of simple closed curves pairwise intersecting at most once on an orientable surface of Euler characteristic χ \chi grows quadratically in | χ | |\chi| . This resolves a longstanding question of Farb-Leininger, up to multiplicative constants. Inspired by the work of Przytycki in the setting of arcs, we introduce the concepts of almost nibs , flowers , and stem systems in order to account for how certain polygons built from pairs of curves in the collection distribute their area over the surface.



1.
introduction

Let
\Gamma

S

\chi

[

Farb-Leininger.

How large may a
1

S

Constructions of size quadratic in
|\chi|
were known to Farb-Leininger, but upper bounds have been much harder to come by. Malestin-Rivin-Theran produced exponential upper bounds
[
MRT14
]
; Przytycki obtained cubic upper bounds
[
Prz15
]
; and Aougab-Biringer-Gaster obtained sub-cubic upper bounds
[
ABG19
]
. Finally, the best upper bounds prior to this work are due to Greene, who used a probabilistic argument to produce a bound of the form
O(|\chi|^{2}\log|\chi|)

[
Gre19
]
.

In this paper, we show:

Main Theorem
.

If
\Gamma
is a 1-system on the orientable surface
S
with Euler characteristic
\chi<0
, then the size
|\Gamma|
is at most the size of a pants decomposition on
S
plus
84|\chi|(|\chi|+1)
.

As a corollary, the size of the largest
1
-system on
S
grows quadratically in
\chi
. We strongly believe that, with some additional combinatorial book-keeping, the strategies we use to prove our main theorem will also produce upper bounds for a
k
-system of curves that are on the order of
|\chi|^{k+1}
. This will be explored in a forthcoming follow-up paper.

1.1.
Strategy

Przytycki’s approach to a cubic bound for the size of a 1-system relied on the analogous count for
arcs
on a punctured surface
S
.
Incredibly, Przytycki computed the maximum size of a 1-system of arcs on a punctured surface exactly. Since Przytycki’s remarkable paper, all subsequent improvements to the 1-systems bound relied on his work as a black box. Our central idea is to open up this box, and modify its components to achieve a similar level of control for curves on surfaces. We will briefly review Przytycki’s argument in §
2
.

For the reader familiar with Przytycki’s work, our strategy can be summed up as follows: Choose a maximal multicurve
\mathcal{C}\subset\Gamma
, and consider what happens when the curves in
\mathcal{C}
are pinched to cusps. The curves in
\Gamma\setminus\mathcal{C}
turn into arcs that give rise to “nibs”, as in Przytycki. However, a fundamental stumbling block is that a curve might pass through many curves in
\mathcal{C}
, and so may turn into many arcs, making estimation of
|\Gamma\setminus\mathcal{C}|
in terms of nibs impossible.
The solution is to not pinch
\mathcal{C}

completely
, but to stop just short.
Elements of
\Gamma\setminus\mathcal{C}
may pass through many curves in
\mathcal{C}
, but it turns out that by leveraging the hyperbolic geometry of the picture– in particular, by paying careful attention to when pairs of curves fellow-travel and when they diverge– there is a way to pick out “base curves” in
\mathcal{C}
for each of the curves in
\Gamma\setminus\mathcal{C}
, from which one may observe what seem to be
approximate
nibs and
approximate
slits. In our present terminology these become
almost nibs
,
flowers
, and
stems
. This terminology will be explained in §
2
and §
3
.

1.1
.

With
\Gamma
and
\mathcal{C}
as above, we have

(1.1)

|\Gamma|\;\leq\;|\mathcal{C}|\;+\;14|\chi|\,\sigma(S)\penalty\ ,

where
\sigma(S)

S
.

We refer to §
4

stems
) from a point
p\in S
to a nonempty multicurve
\mathcal{C}\subset S
(in context, a maximal multicurve drawn from
\Gamma
), with the property that distinct stems are non-homotopic as maps of pairs
(I,\partial I)\to(S,\{p\}\cup\mathcal{C})
, each stem intersects each

at most once, no stem is a prefix of another (up to homotopy of the pairs),
and one more technical condition, which the reader can think of for now as almost saying that distinct stems are disjoint–the latter needs to be interpreted carefully with respect to
\mathcal{C}
.
Suffice it to say, a stem system is what one sees when a point is in many different almost nibs satisfying a certain maximality condition.

.

We have
2(|\chi|+1)\leq\sigma(S)\leq 6(|\chi|+1)

The main theorem follows immediately from Theorem

and Proposition

The lower bound for

, and indicates limitations to improving the upper bounds we obtain for
|\Gamma|
. We are either unwilling or unable to determine where
\sigma(S)
lies between the two linear bounds, though it could be interesting to have a precise calculation. In fact, while it is not hard to determine
\sigma(S_{1})=2

and

1.2.
Outline.

In §
2
, we summarize Przytycki’s approach in the setting of arcs and introduce the concepts of
almost nibs
,
flowers
,
stems
, and
stalks
that we will need throughout the paper.

One complication that arises in our setting is that, in some sense, there are too many almost nibs. For our strategy to work, we have to focus on a subset of them which are “maximal” – we outline this in §
3
. This however introduces another complication, because we do not know that every curve in
\Gamma
arises as a side of a maximal almost nib, and so it becomes difficult to deduce a bound on
|\Gamma|
by only studying the maximal almost nibs. The solution is to categorize nibs into
types
which, loosely speaking, keep track of how the sides of an almost nib twist around a certain curve in a pants decomposition on the surface. At the end of §
3
, we show that every curve is a main curve of an almost nib that is maximal
amongst its type
; combining this with a bound on the number of possible types allows us to argue type-by-type.

In §
4
, we bound the size of a stem system and show that it can be at most linear in
|\chi|
. As mentioned below, a stem system is supposed to keep track of all of the almost nibs containing a given point. In §
5
, we demonstrate this control (modulo some details that will be spelled out later). Finally, in §
6
, we put everything together to prove the Main Theorem, and in §
7
we gather some remaining questions about the structure and size of
1
-systems.

2.
Almost nibs, flowers, stalks, and stems

2.1.
Przytycki’s approach

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is a set of simple proper geodesic arcs on a cusped hyperbolic surface
X
. Around a cusp, ends of elements of
\mathcal{A}
are cyclically ordered, and consecutive elements give rise to an immersion
\Delta\looparrowright X
of an ideal triangle
\Delta\subset\mathbb{H}
; this is called a
nib
for
\mathcal{A}\subset X
. Moreover, a nib has a preferred corner of the ideal triangle
\Delta
, and so each point
q\in\Delta
gives rise to a proper geodesic ray from
q
to
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}
, whose image under the nib map is called a
slit
.

Przytycki’s bounds are obtained by considering fibers of the local isometry

\nu:\bigsqcup_{nibs}\Delta\looparrowright X\penalty\ .

For
p\in X
, the fiber
\nu^{-1}(p)
gives rise to a collection of slits from
p
to the cusps of
X
. Przytycki’s count for
|\mathcal{A}|
is achieved with two observations:

(1)

The size
|\nu^{-1}(p)|
is at most
2(|\chi|+1)
, and the number of nibs is at most
4|\chi|(|\chi|+1)
.

(2)

The number of nibs is equal to
2|\mathcal{A}|
.

In Przytycki’s work, the deep realization is certainly (
1
), which is a consequence of the two beautiful facts that slits are embedded, and, provided
\mathcal{A}
is a 1-system, are pairwise disjoint– the nibs bound comes from considering hyperbolic area.
Item (
2
) is an obvious consequence of the construction, and provides the claimed bound for
|\mathcal{A}|
.
Przytycki’s cubic bound for 1-systems follows quickly as well: let
\mathcal{C}\subset\Gamma
be a maximal multicurve. The subset
\Gamma_{c}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma:\iota(\gamma,c)\neq 0\}
maps at worst 2-to-1 to a 1-system of arcs on the surface obtained by pinching
c
to a pair of cusps, so
|\Gamma_{c}|
is bounded quadratically in
|\chi|
. Because
|\mathcal{C}|\leq\frac{3}{2}|\chi|
, the size of
\Gamma=\bigcup_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\;\Gamma_{c}
is
\lesssim|\chi|^{3}
.

In order to adapt Przytycki’s work for closed curves, we must first construct the ‘nibs’, and then analyze fibers of the map from ‘nibs’ to the surface. These two steps pull against each other: in order to account for all the curves, we would like to flexibly allow many ‘nibs’. However, if there are too many ‘nibs’, the fibers will grow too large, complicating part (
1
). Conversely, if ‘nibs’ are defined restrictively so that the fibers are small, it will be difficult to ensure we are capturing enough of the curves to obtain a good estimate for
|\Gamma|
, interfering with part (
2
).

Before explaining the terminology, we highlight our modification of Przytycki’s strategy:

2.1
.

The number of almost nibs that are maximal amongst their type is at most
14|\chi|\sigma(S)
, where
\sigma(S)
is the size of the largest stem system on
S
.

2.2
.

The number of curves in
\Gamma\setminus\mathcal{C}
is at most the number of almost nibs that are maximal amongst their type.

2.3
.

Observe that in our adaptation of Przytycki’s arc bounds, we introduce a factor of
7\cdot 3
on the second summand on the righthand side of (
1.1
) (in replacing step (
1
) with Theorem
2.1
), and lose one factor of
2
on the lefthand side of (
1.1
) (in replacing step (
2
) with Theorem
2.2
).

Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
together prove Theorem
1.1
.

We turn now to introduce the construction of almost nibs and related terminology.

2.2.
Choosing a hyperbolic metric

Throughout the following,
S
is a closed orientable surface of genus
g\geq 2
, and
\Gamma
is a 1-system on
S
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be any maximal multicurve in
\Gamma
, and let
\epsilon>0

As
\mathcal{C}
is maximal, each curve in
\Gamma\setminus\mathcal{C}
intersects a curve in
\mathcal{C}
.
It will be useful as well to have a pants decomposition on

\mathcal{C}
to a pants decomposition
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}
is worthwhile to keep in mind.
We start by choosing a hyperbolic metric on
S
so that the lengths of the curves in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}
are smaller than
\epsilon

2.4
.

For every
\epsilon>0
there is a hyperbolic structure on
S
so that each curve in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}}
has length at most
\epsilon

Using this hyperbolic structure, we identify the universal covering with
\pi:\mathbb{H}\to S
, and we replace all curves in
\Gamma
with their geodesic representatives. Observe that we may endow the geodesics in
\Gamma
with orientations arbitrarily, according to convenience.

2.3.
Cyclic orders on annuli

Let
c\in\mathcal{C}
.
Choose an orientation for
c
, and a lift
\tilde{c}\subset\mathbb{H}
, and let
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{c}
indicate the collection of lifts of curves in
\Gamma_{c}
that intersect
\tilde{c}
. Endow all geodesics in
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{c}
with orientations so they interesect
\tilde{c}
positively, and observe that the endpoints of the curves in
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{c}
are now linearly ordered by
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}
. Because this linear order is preserved by the cyclic subgroup of
\pi_{1}S

\tilde{c}
, we obtain a cyclic order for the curves in
\Gamma_{c}
.

2.5
.

The reader should notice that the cyclic order we obtain on
\Gamma_{c}
is almost certainly different than the cyclic order on the intersection points
\{\gamma\cap c:\gamma\in\Gamma_{c}\}
induced by
c
. In particular, it is possible that curves from
\Gamma
form a triangle with
c
, in such a way that the order of their endpoints along
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}
is different than their order along
c
.
Philosophically speaking, it is especially pleasing that the order we use above is evidently independent of the chosen hyperbolic metric; indeed the order is determined by the positions of points on
\partial_{\infty}\Gamma\approx S^{1}
.

The literal translation to Przytycki’s setting is somewhat lost, as the slits get cut off. Thus our strategy is to not complete the geometric limit.
Nonetheless, there is enough here to carry through much of Przytycki’s argument, starting with

2.8

The difference in the roles of

Nonetheless, the main thrust of the argument can be appreciated in the case that

If the main sides of an almost nib project to sub-arcs of the main curves, then it is obvious that they (that is, their images) do not self-cross.
This is true in any case:

2.9

The main sides of an almost nib embed under
\pi|_{N}:N\to S
.

Proof.

If a main side

, there would be an element

so that

\widetilde{c_{-}}

2.11

Stems embed under the covering map

We point out two proofs of this fact.
Roughly speaking, they amount to: either adapt the proof of

For now we include both proofs, and we hope the reader will find it comforting to think of them as ‘embededness commutes with pinching’.

However, it will be essential in our strategy to later adapt

Proof 2.

3.
Maximal almost nibs

Observe that it is possible for two almost nibs

3.1
.

We caution the reader that we use

In particular, we allow one to slide the endpoint of

along

during the homotopy.

3.2
.

An almost nib
N
is
maximal
provided that no stem of
N
at a point
p
in its inner flower is a prefix of a stem in the inner flower of any other almost nib
N^{\prime}
.

3.3
.

The reader should note that the meaning of ‘maximal’ depends on the context of the curve system
\Gamma
. That is, an almost nib

might be maximal in a collection
\mathcal{N}
of almost nibs, but not maximal in a superset
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\supset\mathcal{N}
.
Note as well that it is tempting to interpret ‘maximality’ for almost nibs with respect to inclusion. However, the reader is warned that this is not literally true: it is possible for a stem of an almost nib
N
to be the prefix of the stem for almost nib
N^{\prime}
, and
N\nsubseteq N^{\prime}
, see Figure
4
.

3.4
.

The reader may also be wondering about the utility of this definition; in particular, one could ask what would go wrong if instead we worked with
all
almost nibs. As described in §
2.1
, our strategy proceeds by bounding the size of a fiber of the projection map from the set of almost nibs to the surface. So-called
stem systems
(see the beginning of §
4
for the formal definition) serve as a stand-in for measuring this fiber. The crux of the issue is that if one does not require a maximality condition as above, it is easy to construct an example of a stem system whose size is
quadratic
in
\chi
(see Remark
4.3
), as opposed to the desired linear-in-
\chi
bound expressed in Lemma
4.2
, upon which the main theorem heavily relies.

Given a nib
N
(maximal or otherwise) it will be of use to keep track of the various ways that its main sides behave near the tops of
N
.
Indeed, observe in Figure
4
a dichotomy for a non-maximal nib: in the order at the base, there are outside neighbors of its main curves which
track
its main sides, in that they determine identical trajectories in the dual graph
G(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{C}})
. On the left, these curves track the main sides all the way to the tops of the almost nib, while on the right the tracking fails at the very top.
It turns out that one of these – the left one– is easier to exploit, so we are lead to slightly refine our notion of almost nib, adding the notion of
type
.

We first observe an obvious consequence of non-maximality:
When one stem is a prefix of another, the main sides of the larger almost nib must track the stalk of the shorter one.

3.5
.

Suppose that

and

Proof.

The interiors of the main sides of an almost nib intersect the same lifts in

∎

3.6
.

It is possible that either one (but not both) of the main curves of

can be a main curve of

as well. That is, ‘to the left’ and ‘to the right’ in the conclusion of Lemma

should be interpreted with ‘or equal to’.

3.1.
The type of an almost nib

We now introduce the notion of

Before diving into the definition, we give an intuitive idea of type:

As mentioned in the introduction, to the best of our knowledge, all previous attempts at proving upper bounds for the size of a
1
-system relied on treating Przytycki’s bounds for arcs as a black box. The idea in all of these attempts is to start with a system of curves and somehow derive a system of arcs from it, perhaps by puncturing the surface somewhere and “dragging” each curve towards it. The key difficulty here is that some of the curves can obstruct this puncture from the other curves, in such a fashion that the only way one can drag all of the curves into it is to force additional intersections between curves. One might try to mitigate this by adding more than one puncture, but then of course Przytycki’s bound for arcs depends on the Euler characteristic of the underlying surface and will weaken as one adds punctures.

In any case, we will informally refer to this type of argument as
