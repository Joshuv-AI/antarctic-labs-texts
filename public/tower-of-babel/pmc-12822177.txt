DeepPNCC: reconstructing pseudo-spatial cell-cell interaction landscapes from single-cell data to decipher breast cancer pathogenesis
Authors: Xu-hua Li, Xiao-ling Gao, Deng-hui Guo, Jie Shen, Si-rui Li, Na Wang, Xiao-ling Wen, Jiang-ying Liang, Hong-jiu Wang, Zhen-zhen Wang
Published: 2025

Background

Cell-cell interactions orchestrate complex biological processes essential for tissue homeostasis and organismal function [1]. Disruptions in these interactions contribute to the initiation and progression of diseases, including cancer, where aberrant cellular crosstalk drives tumor growth, metastasis, immune evasion, and therapy resistance [2–6]. Importantly, spatial proximity of cells within the tissue microenvironment governs the likelihood and nature of these interactions, influencing cellular behavior and fate decisions [7]. Thus, decoding spatially resolved cell-cell communication networks is crucial for unraveling disease mechanisms, particularly in heterogeneous malignancies such as breast cancer.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has revolutionized transcriptomic profiling by enabling the dissection of cellular heterogeneity at unprecedented resolution. However, a critical limitation of scRNA-seq lies in the loss of spatial context during tissue dissociation, obscuring insights into the spatial architecture and cell-cell communication within tissues [4]. While spatial transcriptomics methods partly address this gap by providing gene expression alongside spatial coordinates, they often compromise cellular resolution and remain limited in throughput and availability [8, 9].

Advances in computational approaches have sought to infer cell-cell interactions based on known ligand-receptor pairs from scRNA-seq data [2, 10–14]. For example, iTALK [15] presents ligand-receptor relationship information of cell pairs in a simple computational approach; celltalker [16] generates directed cell network graphs; cellphoneDB [17] incorporates various ligand-receptor interaction information, such as complexes and integrins, into cell-cell interactions research; and the recently published NicheNet [18] refines interaction strength analysis, further considering the role of signal auxiliary factors in cell-cell interactions. These algorithms have advanced the study of cell-cell interactions, but their reliance on a priori knowledge, such as ligand-receptor pairs, may be incomplete, and they do not incorporate spatial information from tissues. Thus, further improvements are needed to enhance research in this area. Ghaddar et al. found that incomplete tissue dissociation during single-cell sequencing often generates undissociated cell aggregates, where multiple cells are captured in the same droplet [19].

Deep generative models, such as variational autoencoders (VAEs) and generative adversarial networks (GANs), have shown increasing utility across biological research. For instance, they have proven to be powerful tools for handling high-dimensional scRNA-seq data and performing various tasks utilizing latent features. These tasks include denoising [20], clustering [21], dimensionality reduction [22], and missing value interpolation [23]. Variational graph autoencoders (VGAE) [24, 25], which adapts the core of variational autoencoders (VAEs) [26] and extends them [27] for graph representation learning, is a recently developed machine learning algorithm. This algorithm provides strong support for exploring cell-cell interactions and elucidating pathogenic mechanisms in breast cancer. However, these methods typically lack incorporation of spatial information, potentially limiting accurate interpretation of functional intercellular signaling in situ. Intriguingly, recent studies revealed that incomplete tissue dissociation during scRNA-seq generates multiplets or undissociated aggregates that retain native spatial adjacency signals [19, 28]. Leveraging such intrinsic spatial hints embedded in scRNA-seq data presents a novel opportunity to reconstruct spatially informed cell-cell interaction landscapes.

Here, we developed DeepPNCC, a deep learning framework based on variational graph autoencoders (VGAE) enhanced by adversarial regularization, to infer pseudo-spatial cell-cell interaction networks (PSCCIs) from scRNA-seq datasets. By integrating local adjacency information from undissociated cells with single-cell transcriptomes, DeepPNCC overcomes the spatial information loss inherent in scRNA-seq. This integration enables the prediction of global spatial interaction patterns. Applying DeepPNCC to mouse brain and breast cancer datasets, we demonstrate its superior performance in recovering cell-cell interactions concordant with spatial transcriptomics data. Importantly, our analyses reveal that cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) orchestrate key intercellular signaling within the breast tumor microenvironment through a closed-loop pathway involving perivascular-like (PVL) cells. Specifically, in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), the ADM-CALCRL-STAT1-CD40/CCL2/ICAM1 signaling axis mediated by CAFs-PVL interactions drives tumor angiogenesis and vasculature development, thereby promoting tumor progression.

Collectively, DeepPNCC enables spatially informed exploration of cell-cell interactions at single-cell resolution. It advances understanding of cancer pathogenesis and provides a valuable resource for dissecting complex cellular ecosystems.

Methods

Data sources

Mouse brain scRNA-seq data generated with the Smart-seq2 protocol and spatial transcriptomics (ST) data generated with the 10x Genomics Visium platform were obtained as preprocessed Seurat objects from the Seurat website [29, 30]. Processed scRNA-seq data for human breast cancer were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE176078. Corresponding human breast cancer ST data were retrieved from the Zenodo Data Repository (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.4739739). Gene expression and clinical data for TCGA-BRCA patients, including mRNA expression quantified as FPKM-UQ, were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. In addition, three bulk RNA-seq datasets of breast cancer patients (GSE42568, GSE61304, and GSE31448), all based on the GPL570 platform, were downloaded from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/).

Identification and decomposition of undissociated cells

Undissociated cells were identified from scRNA-seq data of mouse brain and 26 breast cancer patients using the R package Neighbor-seq [19], with parameters set to 10 iterations (n = 10) and a maximum of two cells per multiplet. The mean proportion of undissociated cells across all patients was approximately 27% (Table S1). Each undissociated cell was computationally decomposed into two individual cells along with their corresponding gene expression profiles. The decomposition followed two criteria: (i) for multiplets composed of cells of the same type, gene expression was evenly split between the two cells [31, 32]; (ii) for multiplets containing different cell types, expression was apportioned according to the relative abundance of each cell type in the organism [33, 34]. Following decomposition, single-cell resolution expression atlases (denoted as E) were reconstructed. Based on E, a cell-cell adjacency matrix (X) was constructed to represent inferred spatial relationships. In this matrix, an entry of 1 indicates that two cells were derived from the same undissociated cell and are considered adjacent in the tissue context; an entry of 0 indicates that adjacency is unknown.

Construction of the DeepPNCC model

To infer interactions between single cells, we developed DeepPNCC, a deep learning framework combining a variational graph autoencoder (VGAE) with an adversarial network. The framework comprises three modules: a variational graph convolutional network (VGCN) encoder, an inner product decoder, and an adversarial regularization component.

The VGAE infers cell-cell interactions by learning embedding features from single-cell transcriptomic data. It consists of a variational graph convolutional network encoder and an inner product decoder. Given the single-cell expression profile E and the cell adjacency matrix X as inputs, VGAE learns the embedding features Z of cells. The encoder module of VGAE consists of two layers of graph convolutional networks (GCN).

VGAE performs well in learning the embedding features of graph elements [24]. However, its standard variational encoder-decoder structure does not impose additional constraints on the latent variables. This limitation can lead to underfitting when applied to sparse and noisy scRNA-seq data [35]. To address this issue, we introduce an adversarial network to regularize the latent distribution. This improves model performance and allows better adaptation to sparse and noisy data. Specifically, the adversarial module is built by building an adversarial module on top of a multilayer perceptron (MLP), similar to the discriminator of a generative adversarial network (GAN). The adversarial network contains two fully connected hidden layers with activation (256–276) and uses a function output for determining whether a potential sample is from a previous Gaussian distribution or VGAE.

The goal of the VGAE is to minimize its loss function by optimizing the variational lower bound (ELBO). The loss function of the adversarial module is the binary cross entropy.

Implementation of the DeepPNCC

The DeepPNCC framework was implemented in Python (v3.5.2, Anaconda3-4.2.0). It uses the following libraries: NumPy (v1.16.4), SciPy (v1.2.1), Pandas (v0.20.3), Matplotlib (v3.0.2), Seaborn (v0.8.1), NetworkX (v2.1), scikit-learn (v0.21.2), and TensorFlow (v1.4.0). The model takes two inputs: a scRNA-seq gene expression count matrix (cells × genes) and a predefined cell adjacency matrix. It outputs a predicted cell-cell interaction (CCI) matrix. Dropout regularization was applied to reduce overfitting.

During model training, multiple combinations of encoder and regularization layer dimensions were evaluated: [128, 128]/[148, 128], [128, 64]/[148, 128], [128, 64]/[84, 64], [256, 128]/[276, 256], [256, 128]/[148, 128], [256, 256]/[276, 256], [512, 256]/[532, 512], [512, 256]/[276, 256], and [512, 512]/[532, 512]. Dropout rates of 0, 0.03, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 were tested. The full source code is publicly available at: https://github.com/lxhbio/DeepPNCC.

Functional enrichment and survival analysis

To investigate the biological processes associated with CAFs related genes, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis using the enrichGO function from the clusterProfiler and org.hs.eg.db R packages. Enrichment results were visualized using the emapplot function from the enrichplot package. To assess survival differences among BRCA patient subtypes, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and log-rank tests were conducted using the R packages survival (v3.2-7) and survminer (v0.4.9). A log-rank p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Inference of cell-cell signaling pathways

To identify signaling pathways mediating cell–cell interactions involving CAFs, we applied the R package scMLnet [31] to infer ligand–receptor–transcription factor–target (L-R-TF-Target) signaling cascades between CAFs and other cell types. Analyses were conducted using default parameters, with thresholds set at logFC ≥ 0.15 and p ≤ 0.05.

Identification of CAFs signature gene sets

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the FindMarkers function from the R package Seurat, comparing cell populations across breast cancer subtypes as well as across cell types within the same subtype. Ligand–receptor (LR) gene pairs were obtained from the CellPhoneDB database and intersected with the DEGs to define three CAFs signature gene sets: (i) “ah”, consisting of four LRs highly expressed in CAFs from triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) patients and differentially expressed across CAFs from all three subtypes; (ii) “oh”, comprising six LRs specifically upregulated and differentially expressed in CAFs from TNBC patients only; and (iii) “al”, including 52 LRs downregulated in CAFs from TNBC patients and differentially expressed across all three subtypes.

Quantification of PSCCIs similarity across patients

To evaluate inter-patient similarity in predicted spatial cell–cell interactions (PSCCIs), we utilized outputs from the DeepPNCC model across different breast cancer subtypes. For each patient, the average interaction intensity between distinct cell types was calculated, excluding interactions among cells of the same type. These interaction profiles served as computational features to assess similarity between patients within the same subtype and across different subtypes. All similarity measures were subjected to min–max normalization. For each cell type, the mean interaction intensity with other cell types was computed, and sample variance was used to quantify dispersion in interaction strength.

Results

DeepPNCC model overview

Cell-cell interactions play a crucial role in the initiation and progression of diseases, including cancer. However, the inherent limitation of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data lies in the loss of spatial information during the tissue dissociation process. This loss of spatial context makes it challenging to accurately map the interactions between cells in their native tissue architecture. To address this challenge, we developed DeepPNCC (Deep learning framework for pseudo-spatial cell-cell interaction landscapes). It reconstructs pseudo-spatial cell-cell interactions (PSCCIs) from scRNA-seq data. The model effectively incorporates spatial information by leveraging undissociated cells, which retain spatial proximity information from the tissue microenvironment.

DeepPNCC combines variational graph autoencoders (VGAE) with adversarial learning strategies to infer global PSCCIs from local adjacency data. Specifically, the model uses the expression profile (E) of single cells and the cell adjacency matrix (X), derived from undissociated cells, to construct a global cell interaction landscape. A key advantage of DeepPNCC is its ability to use local adjacency information from undissociated cells. This information retains spatial proximity cues even though overall spatial context is lost during tissue preparation.

The process of constructing the PSCCIs begins with identifying and isolating undissociated cells from the scRNA-seq data using the Neighbor-seq package. These undissociated cells are then disassembled to generate high-resolution single-cell expression profiles (E) and adjacency matrices (X) for each individual cell. The local adjacency matrix (X) encodes information about the spatial relationships between cells, which is critical for accurate cell-cell interaction prediction. The DeepPNCC model leverages this adjacency information to train a variational graph autoencoder (VGAE) that embeds the spatial proximity of cells and learns the interactions between them.

To further enhance the robustness of the model, an adversarial network is incorporated into the VGAE framework. The adversarial module regularizes the latent variables by introducing additional constraints, addressing the sparsity and noise commonly encountered in single-cell transcriptomic data. This adversarial regularization improves the model’s performance, making it more effective at inferring cell-cell interactions from sparse data (Fig. 1B).

(A) Undissociated cells were identified and disassembled to construct a new single-cell expression profile (E) and a local adjacency matrix (X). (B) Basic structure of the DeepPNCC model. (C) Analysis of specific signaling pathways for the PSCCIs

The output of the DeepPNCC model is a comprehensive PSCCIs map that reveals the spatially-explicit interactions between different cell types (Fig. 1B-C). Using the PSCCIs derived from the DeepPNCC model, we applied the scMLnet package to identify specific signaling pathways. These include ligands of sending cells, receptors of receiving cells, transcription factors, and target genes (L-R-TF-Targets) in which the PSCCIs occur (Fig. 1C). These interactions reflect the underlying spatial organization of cells within the tissue, offering new insights into the mechanisms of disease progression.

Optimization and performance of DeepPNCC model parameters

To optimize the inference capacity of DeepPNCC, we systematically evaluated key hyperparameters, including encoder dimension (encoder_dim), regularization layer dimension (regularization_dim), and dropout rate. DeepPNCC was applied to mouse brain and breast cancer datasets. The edges reconstructed from undissociated cell multiplets were randomly divided into two groups for training (90%) and testing (10%). The negative set for testing, which was 100 times larger than the positive set, was composed of fake edges between two randomly picked non-neighboring cells in the dataset (Table S2). Encoder dimensions were varied from 64 to 512 nodes. The adversarial network’s input layer was set to 20 nodes above the encoder’s second layer, and its output layer size was scaled proportionally (64–512). Dropout rates ranged from 0 to 0.5 (0, 0.03, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5). Each parameter combination was trained for 100 iterations.

Performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and average precision (AP) metrics obtained from the test set. We observed that higher dropout rates universally reduced ROC and AP scores, while increasing encoder_dim and regularization_dim improved performance until reaching 256 nodes, beyond which performance declined. The optimal configuration was encoder_dim: [256, 256], regularization_dim: [276, 256], and dropout: 0.03, achieving highest stability (ROC: 0.92) and accuracy (AP: 0.87) (Fig. 2A). This parameter set balanced model complexity and regularization, effectively mitigating overfitting while preserving spatial interaction patterns inferred from sparse single-cell data.

We conducted control and ablation experiments to rigorously evaluate the robustness of DeepPNCC under multiple perturbation conditions. The original model achieved the highest performance, while both permutation-based negative controls (> 500 iterations; adjacency matrix shuffled) and removal of the adversarial regularization module caused significant decreases in ROC and AP (Fig. 2B), indicating that DeepPNCC’s predictive power relies on true edge information and the adversarial regularization module rather than spurious correlations or overfitting.

We further evaluated the stability of DeepPNCC using two complementary analyses (Fig. 2C-D). To assess resilience to incomplete interaction information, we randomly removed varying proportions of true edges and trained DeepPNCC on the remaining graph. The model reliably distinguished the withheld true edges from nonexistent edges, demonstrating robustness to missing interactions (Fig. 2D). Conversely, to test tolerance to noise, we added random fake edges at scales up to 10 times the size of the original network. DeepPNCC effectively filtered out these spurious edges and accurately recovered the underlying interaction structure (Fig. 2C).

Together, these results demonstrate that DeepPNCC maintains high accuracy and reliability across various experimental conditions.

Using these profiles, we employed the DeepPNCC model to infer pseudo-spatial cell-cell interactions (PSCCIs), revealing abundant intercellular communication networks in both the mouse brain and breast cancer samples (Figs. 3A and 4A).

To benchmark performance, we compared DeepPNCC against CellChat (ligand–receptor-based) and SPOTlight (spatial decomposition-based), generating cell–cell interaction maps from both scRNA-seq and matched spatial transcriptomics (ST) data (Figs. 3B-C and 4B-C). Across mouse brain and breast cancer tissues, DeepPNCC-inferred PSCCIs from scRNA-seq closely matched the interaction strengths identified by SPOTlight from spatial data, particularly within defined cell-type pairs. Furthermore, DeepPNCC-predicted strengths resembled an additive integration of the interaction signals inferred by CellChat from both scRNA-seq and ST data, indicating that DeepPNCC effectively captures both transcriptomic communication potential and spatial co-occurrence patterns (Figs. 3D and 4D). Specifically, in the mouse brain, the interaction strengths between Astro cells and other cell types identified from scRNA-seq data by DeepPNCC were largely consistent with results inferred from ST data by SPOTlight. These strengths also reflected an accumulation of the cell-cell interaction signals identified by CellChat from both scRNA-seq and ST data (Fig. 3D). Similar results were observed in breast cancer patients, for example, in interactions between B cells and other cell types (Fig. 4D).

Subsequently, to confirm that the PSCCIs identified by the DeepPNCC model contain spatial information of cells, we employed five mapping tools—Celltrek [30], CytoSPACE [36], Cell2location [37], Tangram [38], and scHolography [39] (Figs. S2, S3 and S4)—to project cells from scRNA-seq data onto tissue sections. The PSCCIs were then evaluated from three complementary perspectives to assess whether they retain cellular spatial information.

Firstly, the strength of PSCCIs correlates with inter-cellular spatial distances between cells: Cells inferred to have stronger PSCCIs by the DeepPNCC model tend to be spatially closer in tissue sections, whereas cells inferred to have weaker PSCCIs tend to be spatially farther apart in tissue sections (Figs. 3E-F and 4E-F, S3 and S4). For instance, in the mouse brain, the PSCCIs between L2/3 IT and L4 cells was stronger (0.85), and their distances in tissue sections were also relatively close, with distances enriched around at 150 μm. In contrast, the PSCCIs between L2/3 IT and L6b cells was weaker (0.52), and their distances in tissue sections were also relatively distant, with distance enrichment around at 1200 μm. And the difference in spatial distance distribution between the two groups of cells was significant (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, p < 2.2e-16) (Fig. 3F-H and S3). Simultaneously, based on multiple strength (0.3–0.8) of PSCCIs occurring between two cell types, cell groups were divided into strong and weak PSCCIs groups. Analysis of intercellular spatial distances revealed a significant difference between the two groups (cutoff = 0.5, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p = 0.012) (Fig. 3I and S5A). Specifically, cells with weaker PSCCIs showed larger spatial distances from each other, and vice versa. In breast cancer patients, CAFs exhibit stronger PSCCIs with Cancer Epithelial cells (0.58), and they are relatively close in tissue sections, with distance enrichment around at 200 μm. Conversely, the PSCCIs between CAFs and plasmablasts is weaker (0.31), and they are relatively distant in tissue sections, with distance enrichment at 625 μm. And the distribution of spatial distances between the two types of cells differs significantly (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, p < 2.2e-16) (Fig. 4F-G and S4). Additionally, based on multiple strength of PSCCIs occurring between two cell types (0.3–0.5), cell groups were categorized into groups with strong and weak PSCCIs. Statistical analysis of the spatial distances between these cell groups reveals that cells with weaker PSCCIs exhibit greater spatial distances, and vice versa (cutoff = 0.4, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p = 0.027) (Fig. 4H and S5B).

Secondly, the strength of PSCCIs between two clusters of cells distantly located on tissue sections exhibits significant differences both intra-/inter-cluster: Two distant cell clusters, C1 and C2, were randomly selected on tissue sections of mouse brain, and it was found that cells within the clusters (i.e., cells in close spatial proximity) underwent abundant PSCCIs (Fig. 3E). Computational analysis revealed that intra-cluster cells exhibited stronger PSCCIs (proximal, C1_intra: 0.54, C2_intra: 0.56), whereas inter-cluster cells displayed weaker PSCCIs (distal, C1_C2_inter: 0.51) (Fig. 3G).

Thirdly, abundant PSCCIs occurred among cells within individual spots in the ST data: Particularly, each spot in the spatial transcriptomics (ST) data encapsulates cells that are adjacent in the true spatial context of tissue sections, thereby containing genuine spatial proximity information of cells. Therefore, we utilized the Celltrek tool to map cells from the scRNA-seq data of breast cancer patients to spots in their ST data (Fig. 4E). Then the analysis was performed in terms of individual spot, and it was found that more PSCCIs occurred in the cells of the individual spot, which further proved that the PSCCIs deduced by DeepPNCC model contained the tissue spatial information of the cells. And the DeepPNCC model achieved the identification of cell-cell interactions at single-cell resolution from the individual spot of ST data (Fig. 4I).

Overall, the above results indicated that the DeepPNCC model can identify cell-cell interaction maps containing spatial information with good performance.

Specifically expressed genes of CAFs promote breast cancer progression

The patients of the same subtype exhibit certain similarities and specificities in pathogenesis and clinical phenotypes [40–42]. Therefore, we explored the differences in PSCCIs landscapes derived from the DeepPNCC model among patients with different subtypes of breast cancer (ER+, HER2+, TNBC) on a per-subtype basis. Using the strength of PSCCIs between different types of cells as a metric, we computed the similarity among patients with different subtypes. We observed that the strength of PSCCIs between different cell types exhibited intra-subtype similarity and inter-subtype differences across patients (e.g.: TNBC-TNBC: 1.00, TNBC-ER+: 0.76, TNBC-HER2+: 0.37) (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, by calculating the mean strength of PSCCIs occurring between different cell types, it was found that PSCCIs were significantly different in different subtypes of patients and were dominated by different cell types (Fig. 5B). In ER + subtype patients, PSCCIs predominantly occur within the interactions among CAFs, T-cells, B-cells, and other cell types. In HER2 + subtype patients, PSCCIs primarily manifest within the interactions among cancer epithelial cells, CAFs, PVL, and other cell types. For TNBC subtype patients, PSCCIs are chiefly observed within the interactions among cancer epithelial cells, CAFs, B-cells, and other cell types (Fig. 5C).

We found that the PSCCIs identified by the DeepPNCC model exhibit abundant interactions between CAFs and other cell types across three subtypes of breast cancer patients. Previous studies have indicated that CAFs are the major cellular component of the cancer stroma and play critical roles in the tumor microenvironment, promoting tumor growth and metastasis through various functions including extracellular matrix maintenance, angiogenesis, tumor metabolism regulation, and suppression of anti-tumor immunity [43, 44]. Interactions between CAFs and cancer cells affect treatment response and are closely associated with adverse prognosis in breast cancer patients, thus becoming a significant focus of cancer research [45]. Therefore, we identified differentially expressed signature genes of CAFs (“ah”, “oh”, “al”) respectively at the level of cell types and subtypes. Results revealed distinct signature genes in CAFs compared to other cell types, and the signature genes of CAFs varied among different subtypes of patients (Figure S6A).

Cell-cell interactions are based on complex reactions between ligands and their receptors, as well as the activation of specific cellular signaling pathways [17]. Therefore, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis on three signature gene sets of CAFs (“ah”, “oh”, “al”). The results revealed that the “ah” gene set mainly enriched in pathways such as angiogenesis, positive regulation of development, extracellular matrix, and second messenger-mediated signaling. The “oh” gene set primarily enriched in pathways including angiogenesis, positive regulation of development, response to hypoxia, regulation of lipid metabolism, receptor ligand activity, and signal receptor activator activity. The “al” gene set mainly enriched in pathways such as cell-matrix adhesion, negative regulation of cell motility, positive regulation of cytokine production, tumor necrosis factor production, epithelial cell proliferation, positive regulation of immune response, T cell activation, positive regulation of leukocyte activation, macrophage migration, regulation of receptor signaling pathway via JAK-STAT, and negative regulation of angiogenesis and development. All of these functional pathways are closely linked to breast cancer development. Up- or down-regulation of the corresponding CAFs signature genes disrupts these pathways, thereby promoting cancer progression (Fig. 5D).

CAFs and PVL in TNBC promote tumor angiogenesis and vasculature development through specific signaling pathways

Subsequently, the expression level of signature genes of CAFs in patients with different breast cancer subtypes was analyzed in TCGA-BRCA expression data and three datasets of breast cancer expression data (bulk RNA sequencing, bulk-seq) obtained from the GEO database. It was found that the majority (38/62) of signature genes of CAFs exhibited consistent expression trends between bulk-seq data and scRNA-seq data, i.e., signature genes that were highly expressed in scRNA-seq data of TNBC patients were also highly expressed in bulk-seq data of TNBC patients, indicating the expression stability of signature genes of CAFs across different subtypes of breast cancer patients (Fig. 6H, S8 and S9). To further investigate whether the expression of CAFs signature genes affects the survival prognosis of patients with subtypes, we divided breast cancer patients into high and low expression groups, respectively, based on the median expression of each CAFs signature gene. Survival analysis results showed that the expression changes of 4 (4/62) CAFs signature genes (ADM, IGF1, IFITM1, CD34) would impact the survival prognosis of patients. Significant differences in survival prognosis were observed between the two groups of patients grouped according to the median expression of these genes (Fig. 6I, log-rank test, p = 0.043, 0.00011, 0.019, 0.027, 0.095, 0.044).

Particularly, ADM and IGF1 were specifically highly expressed in CAFs from TNBC patients (Figure S6B). Dysregulated expression of these genes has been closely linked to breast cancer occurrence and progression. ADM is strongly upregulated under hypoxic conditions [46, 47], promoting tumor progression by controlling angiogenesis [48] and accelerating breast cancer bone metastasis [49, 50]. IGF1 serves as a convergence point for major signaling pathways associated with breast cancer growth [51–54]. Furthermore, ADM and IGF1 are key ligands mediating cell-cell interactions, and their dysregulated expression will affect their mediated cell-cell interactions, thereby affecting the progression of breast cancer. Therefore, we first explored the reasons leading to the specific high expression of the ligands ADM and IGF1 in CAFs from TNBC patients. Using scMLnet [55], we identified the signaling pathways (L-R-TF-Target) through which other cells interact with CAFs, thereby influencing the specific high expression of their target genes ADM and IGF1 in TNBC patients. The results indicated that the receptors of CAFs in TNBC patients were upregulated by the co-regulation of multiple ligands (cell type-specific ligands and co-expressed ligands) in other types of cells, which in turn promoted the upregulation of the expression of their TF-regulated target genes, ADM and IGF1 (Fig. 6A-B). Then, we further investigated the L-R-TF-Target signaling pathways through which cell-cell interactions occur after ADM and IGF1 are transcribed and translated into ligands in CAFs. The results revealed ADM-mediated interactions between CAFs and PVL cells. Specifically, ADM from CAFs binds to the highly expressed receptor CALCRL in PVL. This interaction activates the transcription factor STAT1 in PVL, which regulates the high expression of eight target genes, including CD40, CCL2, and ICAM1 (Fig. 6C). Notably, these results revealed a closed-loop inter-/intra-cellular signaling pathway mediating interactions between CAFs and PVL: CAFs (Ligand: ADM) → PVL (Receptor: CALCRL, Transcription factor: STAT1, Target gene/Ligand: APP) → CAFs (Receptor: CD74, Transcription factor: HIF1A, Target gene: ADM) (Fig. 6D). This pathway contributes to breast cancer progression by regulating tumor angiogenesis, vasculature development, and hemopoiesis (Table S3). In tissue sections from two TNBC breast cancer patients, this pathway was validated: CAFs and PVL cells were spatially co-localized (Fig. 6E-F, S7A-B), and the pathway-related genes were co-expressed within the corresponding cells (Fig. 6G, S7C).

Additionally, to further investigate the effects of eight highly expressed target genes (CD40, CCL2, ICAM1, APP, CTSL, IRF1, PLSCR1, SOCS3) in PVL on the progression of breast cancer, we performed GO functional enrichment analysis on these highly expressed target genes. The results revealed that they were primarily enriched in functions related to angiogenesis, vasculature development and regulation of immune response (p < 5.34E-04). The alteration of all these biological functions was closely related to the progression of breast cancer (Table S4). Particularly, the target genes, CD40, CCL2, and ICAM1, are mainly involved in the functions of positive regulation of angiogenesis and development, proliferation regulation of vascular endothelial cells, and positive regulation of blood circulation. Consequently, this reveals the pathogenic mechanism of ADM, the ligand of CAFs. ADM is highly expressed under regulation by other cells. It facilitates cell-cell interactions between CAFs and PVL via the ADM-CALCRL-STAT1-CD40/CCL2/ICAM1 signaling pathways. These interactions promote tumor angiogenesis and vasculature development, ultimately driving TNBC progression (Fig. 7). Furthermore, the elevated expression of target genes such as CD40, CCL2, ICAM1 has been confirmed to be closely associated with the occurrence and progression of breast cancer, potentially serving as therapeutic targets for breast cancer [56–59].

CAFs undergo PSCCIs with PVL to promote tumor angiogenesis and vasculature development, which in turn promotes breast cancer growth

Discussion

Cell-cell interactions are closely associated with the onset and progression of cancer, and neighboring cells in spatial proximity are often more prone to engage in cell-cell interactions, as confirmed by previous studies [60–63]. However, current research on cell-cell interactions is primarily based on known prior knowledge such as ligand-receptor [2, 10–14], and the spatial information of cells within tissues has not been incorporated into the study of cell-cell interactions. Therefore, there is still a need to expand research on cell-cell interactions to include spatial considerations.

Although single-cell sequencing technologies enable researchers to understand diseases at single-cell resolution, the dissociation of tissues results in the loss of spatial information of cells [4]. Spatial transcriptome technology can provide spatial location information, but it is difficult to achieve single-cell resolution [8, 9]. Nevertheless, scRNA-seq data contains some undissociated cells. Identifying and separating these cells provides partial spatial adjacency information [19], allowing us to explore PSCCIs with spatial information at single-cell resolution using only scRNA-seq data. Leveraging the powerful data representation of VGAE, we designed and optimized DeepPNCC to derive PSCCIs with spatial information without relying on prior knowledge. This approach expands cell-cell interaction studies to include spatial information and enhances understanding of breast cancer pathogenesis.

When deriving PSCCIs, the DeepPNCC model relies on the single-cell expression atlas and local adjacency matrix constructed after the dissociation of undissociated cells. Therefore, the identification and dissociation of undissociated cells are particularly important. We used Neighbor-seq proposed by Ghaddar [19] et al. to solve this problem. The setting of parameter values for the DeepPNCC model affects its inference capability. Accordingly, we trained the DeepPNCC model with different parameter values for the encoding layer, regularization layer, and dropout rate, and evaluated its performance using metrics such as ROC and AR. Finally, we determined the optimal DeepPNCC model. In addition, since DeepPNCC infers interactions based on the complete cell–cell graph within each sample, we did not stratify by patient or perform cross-sample validation, as doing so would fragment the graph and disrupt the topological context necessary for accurate and biologically valid inference.

DeepPNCC, a deep learning model, ingeniously leverages the local spatial information embedded in scRNA-seq data. Its identification of PSCCIs achieves spatial expansion of cell-cell interactions, thereby promoting and deepening our understanding of cell-cell interactions and the pathogenic mechanisms of breast cancer. DeepPNCC showed strong performance in inferring cell-cell interactions compared to other tools in both mouse brain and breast cancer scRNA-seq data. Its identified PSCCIs were confirmed to contain spatial information. Using the PSCCIs landscapes derived from DeepPNCC, we revealed the critical role of CAFs in breast cancer cell–cell interactions. We identified a closed-loop intra- and inter-cellular signaling pathway mediating interactions between CAFs and PVL in TNBC patients: CAFs (Ligand: ADM) → PVL (Receptor: CALCRL, Transcription factor: STAT1, Target gene/Ligand: APP) → CAFs (Receptor: CD74, Transcription factor: HIF1A, Target gene: ADM). In this pathway, ADM, a CAFs target gene, is highly expressed under regulation by multiple signaling pathways from other cells and interacts with PVL through ADM-CALCRL-STAT1-CD40/CCL2/ICAM1 signaling, which promotes tumor angiogenesis and vasculature development, ultimately driving TNBC progression.

In summary, we have developed a deep learning model, DeepPNCC, which can identify PSCCIs landscapes containing spatial information from scRNA-seq data. This spatially extends and deepens our understanding of cell-cell interactions, promoting the research on PSCCIs in the context of spatial information and facilitating the elucidation of cancer pathogenic mechanisms.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our developed deep learning model, DeepPNCC, can identify PSCCIs landscapes containing spatial information from scRNA-seq data, achieving the spatial expansion of cell-cell interactions. It also revealed a closed-loop intra-/inter-cellular signaling pathway mediating cell-cell interactions between CAFs and PVLs in TNBC patients. Meanwhile, we elucidated the pathogenic mechanism in TNBC patients, in which CAFs interact with PVLs via the ADM-CALCRL-STAT1-CD40/CCL2/ICAM1 signaling pathway. This interaction promotes tumor angiogenesis and vasculature development, ultimately facilitating tumor growth.
