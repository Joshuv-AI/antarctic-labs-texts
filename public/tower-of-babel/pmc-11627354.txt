A young lady with multiple lung nodules
Authors: Afshan Shaik, Mahismita Patro, Ranjan Patel, Aswathy Girija, Sourin Bhuniya
Published: 2024

A 26-year-old non-smoker lady presented with exertional dyspnoea for the past two years and dry cough for one month. Her family history was non-contributory. Her vitals were stable except for room air SpO2 of 68%. She had central cyanosis and pan digital clubbing. The systemic examination including upper and lower respiratory system was normal. The routine blood investigations including connective tissue disease and vasculitis workup were normal. Chest radiograph and high-resolution computed tomography (CT) revealed bilateral nodular opacities [Figure 1]. Contrast-enhanced CT abdomen and CT brain were normal. The bubble contrast echocardiography showed a delayed right to left shunt with the appearance of bubbles in left heart after four cardiac cycles. CT pulmonary angiography images are shown in Figure 2.

Chest radiograph in posterior-anterior projection (a) and high-resolution chest tomography scan axial view at various levels (b and c), and coronal view (d) showing multiple nodular opacities in bilateral lung fields (arrows). Some of the nodules are showing feeding vessel sign (arrowhead)

Computed tomography pulmonary angiography at various levels (a and b) showing multiple arteriovenous malformations in bilateral lower lobes with multiple feeding arteries and multiple draining veins suggestive of diffuse complex PAVM (arrows)

Q1. What is the diagnosis?

Answer

Idiopathic diffuse complex pulmonary AV malformation (PAVM).

There were total of 12 PAVMs predominantly in left upper lobe, right middle lobe and bilateral lower lobes. Four of them had multiple feeding arteries and multiple draining vessels suggestive of complex PAVM. As the patient had no secondary causes for PAVM, a final diagnosis of idiopathic diffuse complex PAVM was established. Our patient was managed non-invasively with percutaneous transcatheter embolotherapy [Figure 3]. Post-embolization, SpO2 improved to 96%.

Pulmonary angiograms prior to embolization show catheter tip in the distal pulmonary artery supplying the PAVM (white circle, a). Post-embolization pulmonary angiogram shows occlusion of the PAVM with coils (white circle, b). Chest radiograph post-embolization shows the presence of multiple coils (white arrows, c)

PAVM refers to abnormal communication between pulmonary artery and pulmonary vein in the absence of intervening pulmonary capillaries resulting in anatomic right-to-left shunt and hypoxemia depending on the degree of shunt. The PAVM also results in stasis of blood thereby increasing the risk of haemorrhage and paradoxical embolization leading to systemic complications. The PAVMs in 80–90% cases are manifestations of hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT).[1] HHT is characterised by dysplastic telangiectasia, multiple systemic AV malformations in lungs, brain, liver and gastrointestinal tract.[2] Acquired forms of PAVMs have also been described in association with liver cirrhosis, schistosomiasis, tuberculosis, mitral stenosis, prior surgery, trauma, actinomycosis, Fanconi’s syndrome, metastatic thyroid carcinoma and other cancers.[1] Rarely, when none of the associations are found, they are labelled as idiopathic PAVM. The clinical manifestations in PAVM vary from asymptomatic to severe dyspnoea related to hypoxemia or symptoms related to complications such as brain stroke, brain abscess, myocardial ischemia, haemothorax or haemoptysis. The most striking clinical finding is the relative paucity of symptoms when compared to the severity of hypoxemia.

PAVMs on imaging have been classified as simple vs complex and solitary vs diffuse PAVMs. Simple lesions (sometimes referred to as fistula-type PAVM) consist of an aneurysmal venous sac that appears on CT as a well-defined nodule communicating with a single dilated feeding artery and single/multiple draining veins. Complex PAVMs (sometimes referred to as plexiform PAVM) are associated with multiple feeding vessels arising from segmental or subsegmental pulmonary artery and multiple draining veins.[2] Solitary or focal PAVM is described for a single PAVM. Diffuse PAVMs have been defined as multiple PAVMs affecting a single segment, or multiple segments of one or more lobes.[2] Diffuse complex PAVMs are usually associated with HHT. Idiopathic diffuse complex PAVMs similar to our case have rarely been described in the literature.

The PAVMs need to be treated even when asymptomatic owing to their high rate of complications. The treatment of choice is endovascular treatment via percutaneous transcatheter embolization of the feeding artery when it is at least 2 mm in diameter. The embolization is commonly performed using plugs or coils or combination of both. The recent advancements in endovascular treatment options such as Amplatzer vascular plugs, microvascular plugs, microcatheters and stent grafts have made treatment of PAVM possible with minimal morbidity and no mortality.[3] Surgery is considered when embolization is not successful or couldn’t be performed or in the presence of complications.
