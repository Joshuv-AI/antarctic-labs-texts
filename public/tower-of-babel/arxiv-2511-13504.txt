Stable Self-Similar Blow-Up in Nonlinear Wave Equations with Quadratic Time-Derivative Nonlinearities
Jie Liu, Faiq Raees

Abstract
. We study singularity formation in two one-dimensional nonlinear wave models with quadratic time-derivative nonlinearities. The non-null model violates the null condition and typically develops finite-time blow-up; the null-form model is Lorentz-invariant and enjoys small-data global existence, yet still admits blow-up for large data. Building on our earlier work on spatial-derivative nonlinearities, we construct and classify a five-parameter family of generalized self-similar blow-up solutions that captures the observed dynamics. We prove that no smooth exact self-similar profiles exist, while the generalized self-similar solutions—exhibiting logarithmic growth—provide the correct blow-up description inside backward light cones. We further establish asymptotic stability for the relevant branches, including the ODE-type blow-up in both models. These results yield a coherent and unified picture of blow-up mechanisms in time-derivative nonlinear wave equations.



1. Introduction

We investigate the singularity formation for two classes of one-dimensional nonlinear wave equations (NLW) featuring quadratic nonlinearities in the first derivatives:

(1.1)

\partial_{tt}u-\partial_{xx}u=(\partial_{t}u)^{2}

and

(1.2)

\partial_{tt}u-\partial_{xx}u=(\partial_{t}u)^{2}-(\partial_{x}u)^{2}.

The study of blow-up dynamics for such equations is fundamental to the understanding nonlinear hyperbolic phenomena. This paper continues our systematic investigation into the self-similar blow-up mechanisms in one-dimensional NLW with quadratic derivative nonlinearities, extending our previous study
[
11
]
of the spatial-derivative case,
\Box u=(\partial_{x}u)^{2}
. Here, we focus on the equally important time-derivative counterparts.

1.1. Motivation and background

The study of singularity formation for nonlinear wave equations has a long and rich history. A foundational result
[
14
]
by Fritz John demonstrated that, for certain nonlinearities, solutions to NLW can develop singularities in finite time, even for arbitrarily small and smooth initial data. Equation (
1.1
) serves as a canonical example of such behavior: it violates the celebrated null condition
[
17
,
2
]
, and its nonlinear term
(\partial_{t}u)^{2}
is well known to induce finite-time blow-up. This makes it a prototypical model for investigating singularity formation in nonlinear hyperbolic dynamics.

In contrast, equation (
1.2
) represents the archetypal model satisfying the null condition. The quadratic form
(\partial_{t}u)^{2}-(\partial_{x}u)^{2}
(and its higher-dimensional counterpart
(\partial_{t}u)^{2}-|\nabla_{x}u|^{2}
) is Lorentz invariant—a structure of central importance in physics. It naturally arises in geometric and relativistic models such as the Wave Maps system
[
13
,
35
]
and the Yang-Mills equations (in the Lorentz gauge)
[
16
]
, where the null structure ensures the long-time stability of solutions.

While the local theory for these models is classical—smooth local well-posedness follows from standard energy methods—their long-time dynamics are markedly different. For (
1.1
), finite-time blow-up is expected even for small data. In contrast, for the null-form equation (
1.2
), the null structure yields small-data global existence (GWP), even in the one-dimensional setting where linear waves exhibit no decay
[
19
]
.

Nevertheless, despite small-data global existence, the null-form equation (
1.2
) also admits blow-up for large initial data. Indeed, both (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) possess the explicit ODE-type blow-up solution

(1.3)

\displaystyle u(t)=-\log(T-t),

indicating that finite-time singularities are a natural phenomenon for both systems. This raises a fundamental and largely unresolved question:
what are the precise blow-up dynamics and stability mechanisms underlying such singularities?

The objective of this paper is to provide a detailed characterization of these dynamics by constructing and analysing self-similar blow-up solutions. This work directly extends the methodology developed in our previous paper
[
11
]
, where we studied the stable self-similar blow-up for
\Box u=(\partial_{x}u)^{2}
. We now apply this framework to both (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), aiming to provide a unified understanding of singularity formation across this entire family of 1D NLW with quadratic derivative nonlinearities.

We note the recent, independent work of Gough
[
12
]
, which appeared as our present work was nearing completion. That work also investigates equation (
1.1
), adopting the spectral-theoretic framework introduced in our prior work
[
11
]
. Our analysis, however, is broader in scope and distinct in several key aspects. First, it covers both the non-null case (
1.1
) and, crucially, the null-form equation (
1.2
), which is not treated in
[
12
]
. Second, for the overlapping case (
1.1
), our results complete the stability analysis of the ODE-type blow-up solution (
1.3
) (corresponding to the
p=1
case in
[
12
]
), which was left open therein. Furthermore, we obtain an accurate spectral gap and improved convergence rates in the asymptotic stability of generalized self-similar blow-up profiles.

1.2. Main results

The self-similar ansatz for the both equations (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) takes the form

\displaystyle u(t,x)=U\left(y\right),\quad\ y=\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t},\quad T>0,\ x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.

Under this ansatz, exact self-similar solutions to (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) satisfy

\displaystyle 2y\partial_{y}U+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}U=y^{2}(\partial_{y}U)^{2}.

and

\displaystyle 2y\partial_{y}U+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}U=(y^{2}-1)(\partial_{y}U)^{2}.

respectively.

Our first result establishes the existence of a family of smooth generalized self-similar blow-up solutions.

Theorem 1.1

(Existence of smooth generalized self-similar blow-up solutions)
.

(1)

For any
T>0
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, there are no smooth exact self-similar blow-up solutions to (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) in the backward lightcone

\Gamma(T,x_{0}):=\left\{(t,x)\in\left[0,T\right)\times\mathbb{R}:\left|x-x_{0}\right|\leq T-t\right\}.

(2)

There exists a five-parameter family of smooth generalized self-similar blow-up solutions to (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) with logarithmic growth

\displaystyle u_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}=-\alpha\log\left(1-\frac{t}{T}\right)+U_{\alpha,\beta}\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t}\right)+\kappa

well defined in the backward lightcone
\Gamma(T,x_{0})
for all
T>0,\kappa,x_{0}\in\mathbb{R},

where for (
1.1
),
(\alpha,\beta)\in(0,1]\times\{0,\infty\}
and

\displaystyle U_{\alpha,0}(y)=-\alpha\ln(1-\sqrt{1-\alpha}y),\qquad U_{\alpha,\infty}(y)=-\alpha\ln(1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y);

and for (
1.2
),
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+},
and

\displaystyle U_{\alpha,\beta}(y)=-\log\left((1+y)^{\alpha}+\beta(1-y)^{\alpha}\right)+\log(1+\beta).

Remark 1.1

(Global-in-space smooth blow-up solutions)
.

By the finite propagation speed of wave equations and a cut-off of
u_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}
, we can also extend the solution out of the lightcone and obtain global in space smooth blow-up solutions to (
1.1
) and (
1.2
).

Remark 1.2
.

For
\alpha=1
,
u_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}
is reduced to the ODE blow-up solution (
1.3
) of (
1.1
) when
\beta=0,\infty
and of (
1.2
) when
\beta=1
, up to a constant.

The following two theorems establish the asymptotic stability of the generalized self-similar blow-up solutions
u_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa,T,x_{0}}
for (
1.1
),
(\alpha,\beta)\in(0,1]\times\{0,\infty\}
, and for (
1.2
),
(\alpha,\beta)\in\{1\}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Theorem 1.2

(Asymptotic stability of generalized self-similar blow-up solutions for (
1.1
))
.

Let
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1]
,
T_{0}>0,\kappa_{0},x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
k\geq 5
. For all
0<\delta<1
, there exists a constant
\epsilon>0
such that for any real-valued functions
(f,g)\in H^{k+1}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{k}(\mathbb{R})
with

\|(f,g)\|_{H^{k+1}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{k}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\epsilon

there exist parameters
\alpha^{*}\in(0,1],T^{*}>0,\kappa^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
, and a unique solution
u:\Gamma(T^{*},x_{0})\mapsto\mathbb{R}
of (
1.1
) with initial data

u(0,x)=u_{\alpha_{0},\infty,\kappa_{0},T_{0},x_{0}}(0,x)+f(x),\quad\partial_{t}u(0,x)=\partial_{t}u_{\alpha_{0},\infty,\kappa_{0},T_{0},x_{0}}(0,x)+g(x),\quad x\in B_{T^{*}}(x_{0}),

such that the bounds

\displaystyle(T^{*}-t)^{-\frac{1}{2}+s}\|u(t,\cdot)-u_{\alpha^{*},\infty,\kappa^{*},T^{*},x_{0}}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}(B_{T^{*}-t}(x_{0}))}\lesssim(T^{*}-t)^{1-\delta},

for
s=0,1,\ldots,k+1
, and

\displaystyle(T^{*}-t)^{-\frac{1}{2}+s}\|\partial_{t}u(t,\cdot)-\partial_{t}u_{\alpha^{*},\infty,\kappa^{*},T^{*},x_{0}}\|_{\dot{H}^{s-1}(B_{T^{*}-t}(x_{0}))}\lesssim(T^{*}-t)^{1-\delta},

for
s=1,\ldots,k+1
, hold for all
0\leq t<T^{*}.
Moreover,

\displaystyle|\alpha^{*}-\alpha_{0}|+|\kappa^{*}-\kappa_{0}|+\left|\frac{T^{*}}{T_{0}}-1\right|\lesssim\epsilon.

Remark 1.3
.

The same result also holds for
u_{\alpha,0,\kappa,T,x_{0}}
. Indeed, we have

u_{\alpha,0,\kappa,T,x_{0}}(t,x)=u_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa,T,-x_{0}}(t,-x),

and any solution
u(t,x)
to (
1.1
) implies that
u(t,-x)
also solves (
1.1
).

Theorem 1.3

(Asymptotic stability of generalized self-similar blow-up solutions for (
1.2
))
.

Let
\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
T_{0}>0,\kappa_{0},x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
k\geq k_{\beta_{0}}:=2+\beta_{0}^{-1}+\beta_{0}
. For all
0<\delta<1
, there exists a constant
\epsilon>0
such that for any real-valued functions
(f,g)\in H^{k+1}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{k}(\mathbb{R})
with

\|(f,g)\|_{H^{k+1}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{k}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\epsilon

there exist parameters
\beta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},T^{*}>0,\kappa^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
, and a unique solution
u:\Gamma(T^{*},x_{0})\mapsto\mathbb{R}
of (
1.2
) with initial data

u(0,x)=u_{1,\beta_{0},\kappa_{0},T_{0},x_{0}}(0,x)+f(x),\quad\partial_{t}u(0,x)=\partial_{t}u_{1,\beta_{0},\kappa_{0},T_{0},x_{0}}(0,x)+g(x),\quad x\in B_{T^{*}}(x_{0}),

such that the bounds

\displaystyle(T^{*}-t)^{-\frac{1}{2}+s}\|u(t,\cdot)-u_{1,\beta^{*},\kappa^{*},T^{*},x_{0}}\|_{\dot{H}^{s}(B_{T^{*}-t}(x_{0}))}\lesssim(T^{*}-t)^{1-\delta},

for
s=0,1,\ldots,k+1
, and

\displaystyle(T^{*}-t)^{-\frac{1}{2}+s}\|\partial_{t}u(t,\cdot)-\partial_{t}u_{1,\beta^{*},\kappa^{*},T^{*},x_{0}}\|_{\dot{H}^{s-1}(B_{T^{*}-t}(x_{0}))}\lesssim(T^{*}-t)^{1-\delta},

for
s=1,\ldots,k+1
, hold for all
0\leq t<T^{*}.
Moreover,

\displaystyle|\beta^{*}-\beta_{0}|+|\kappa^{*}-\kappa_{0}|+\left|\frac{T^{*}}{T_{0}}-1\right|\lesssim\epsilon.

Remark 1.4
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
and
0<\beta<\infty
, we conjecture that
u_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}
are only co-dimensionally stable. The spectral analysis of the corresponding linearized equation reveals the presence of some additional unstable eigenvalues, i.e., eigenvalues with positive real part, which not associated with any symmetries of the equation. Consequently, we expect that, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
and
0<\beta<\infty
, perturbations around
u_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}
would always converge to the ground state
u_{1,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}}
. In other words,
u_{1,\beta,\kappa,T,x_{0}},\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
serve as stable attractors for the dynamics.

1.3. Related work

The study of singularity formation in nonlinear wave equations is a broad field. One of its most significant branches focuses on characterizing the precise dynamics of blow-up, particularly the existence and stability of self-similar solutions. A comprehensive review of this specific subfield was provided in our prior work
[
11
, Section 1.2]
. As detailed therein, this line of research was pioneered by the foundational work of the Merle and Zaag
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
on power-type nonlinearities (e.g.,
\Box u=|u|^{p-1}u
). This was later developed into a robust spectral-theoretic framework by the Donninger group
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
3
,
36
,
28
]
for analysing the stability of the ODE blow-up profiles. We refer the interested reader to
[
18
,
11
]
for a detailed discussion of these developments, which form the context for the analytical approach adopted here.

For derivative-type nonlinearities, the literature on blow-up dynamics is far more limited. Most existing studies have focused on lifespan estimates and the geometric properties of singularities. We refer to the works of Rammaha
[
29
,
30
]
, Sasaki and coauthors
[
32
,
31
]
, and Shao–Takamura–Wang
[
33
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, the first rigorous characterization of type-I blow-up dynamics for a derivative nonlinear wave equation was achieved by Speck
[
34
]
, who established the stability of the ODE-type blow-up in the quasilinear model

-u_{tt}+\frac{\Delta u}{1+u_{t}}=-(u_{t})^{2},

using a delicate energy method. In contrast, for the semilinear equation (
1.1
), the blow-up mechanism remains largely unexplored; see, for instance, the discussion in
[
34
, Section 1]
.

We also highlight the recent contributions of Chen–McNulty–Schörkhuber
[
1
]
and McNulty
[
20
]
, who analysed the stable self-similar blow-up in Skyrme-type models. Their approach exploits a crucial structural transformation that removes the derivative nonlinearity, thereby allowing the application of the spectral-theoretic machinery originally developed for power-type nonlinearities.

In the proofs of Theorems 1.2 and 1.3, we build on the methods developed in our earlier work
[
11
]
. Several ingredients, however—most notably the spectral analysis—are problem-specific and substantially more delicate. For example, a key step in
[
11
]
is the observation that for all
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>-1
, the only eigenvalues of the linearized operator are
0
and
1
, yielding a spectral gap of size
1
between the stable and unstable spectra. By contrast, as noted in
[
12
]
, for equation (
1.1
) the author could only show that for
\operatorname{Re}\lambda\geq 0
the only eigenvalues are
0
and
1
, which led to an inaccurate conjecture of “instability” of the ODE blow-up solution. In this work, we recover the full spectral gap by a more refined Frobenius analysis.

A second difficulty arises from the degeneracy at
\alpha=1
: the first
\alpha
-derivative of the generalized self-similar profile
U_{\alpha,\beta}
is singular at
\alpha=1
, causing an apparent discontinuity in the
0
-generalized eigenfunction
\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}
as
\alpha\to 1
, which complicates the nonlinear modulation analysis (see Lemma
7.3
). We resolve this issue by constructing a more suitable choice of
\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}
and by performing a separate, carefully tailored analysis in the case
\alpha=1
.

2. Self-similar solutions and ODE blow-up

2.1. Self-similar solutions to (
1.1
).

For
T>0
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, we introduce the following self-similar variables

(2.1)

\begin{cases}u=U(s,y),\\
s=-\ln(T-t),\\
y=\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t}\end{cases}

The equation (
1.1
) can be transformed into

(2.2)

\partial_{ss}U+\partial_{s}U+2y\partial_{ys}U+2y\partial_{y}U+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}U=(\partial_{s}U+y\partial_{y}U)^{2}.

Some points are in order:

•

Self-similar solutions:
The self-similar solutions of (
1.1
) are always singular in the light cone
\lvert y\rvert\leq 1
. More precise, we have

U=\int\frac{4}{2y+(y^{2}-1)\ln\lvert\frac{1+y}{1-y}\rvert+d(y^{2}-1)},\quad d\in\mathbb{R}.

and note that for any
d\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a value
y_{\star}(d)\in(-1,1)
such that

2y_{\star}+(y_{\star}^{2}-1)\ln\left\lvert\frac{1+y_{\star}}{1-y_{\star}}\right\rvert+d(y_{\star}^{2}-1)=0.

Indeed, this follows from intermediate value theorem once one notes that denominator tends to
\pm 2
for
x=\pm 1
respectively.

•

ODE blow-up:
For this equation, we have a trivial ODE blow-up solution which is independent of the spatial variable i.e.

u=-\ln(T-t).

Moreover, by finite propagation speed, we can construct blow-up solutions for smooth and compact supported initial data. Unlike power nonlinearity
\lvert u\rvert^{p-1}u
, (
1.1
) is not Lorentz invariant so we can not obtain a family of type-I blow-up solutions by Lorentz transformation.

•

Generalized self-similar blow-up:
Nevertheless, for
0<\alpha<1,\beta\in[0,\infty],\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
, we find a new family of “generalized self-similar” blow-up solutions of the following form

U_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}(s,y)=\alpha s+\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}(y)+\kappa,

where
\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}(y)
satisfies the equation

(2.3)

\alpha+2y\partial_{y}\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}=(\alpha+y\partial_{y}\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta})^{2},

and the extra parameter
\beta
is to be specified.
We first note that

\tilde{U}_{\alpha,0}:=-\alpha\ln(1-\sqrt{1-\alpha}y)\quad\mbox{~and~}\quad\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\infty}:=-\alpha\ln(1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y)

are two exact smooth solutions to (
2.3
). Since (
2.3
) is a Riccati type equation (under the change of variable
z=\alpha+y\partial_{y}\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}
), we can solve the whole family of solutions to (
2.3
) by letting
\partial_{y}\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}=\partial_{y}\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\infty}+g_{\alpha,\beta}
where
g_{\alpha,\beta}
satisfies

(y^{2}-1)g_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}+\left(2(1-\alpha)y+\frac{2\alpha\sqrt{1-\alpha}y^{2}}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}\right)g_{\alpha,\beta}=y^{2}g^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}.

Then we can solve the above equation as

\displaystyle g_{\alpha,\beta}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\frac{1+y}{1-y})^{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}(y+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}})^{-2}}{\beta+\int_{-1}^{y}\frac{z^{2}}{1-z^{2}}(\frac{1+z}{1-z})^{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}(z+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}})^{-2}\mathrm{\,d}z}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\frac{1+y}{1-y})^{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}(y+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}})^{-2}}{\beta+\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}{2\alpha}\frac{1-\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}(\frac{1+y}{1-y})^{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}}

(2.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\alpha(1-\alpha)(1+y)^{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}}{2\alpha\beta(1-y)^{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}(1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y)^{2}+\sqrt{1-\alpha}(1+y)^{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}(1-(1-\alpha)y^{2})},

where
0<\alpha<1
and the parameter
\beta\geq 0
satisfies

\beta=\frac{1-\alpha}{g_{\alpha,\beta}(0)}-\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}{2\alpha}.

And we define for
0<\beta<\infty

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}(y)

\displaystyle:=\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\infty}(y)+\int_{0}^{y}g_{\alpha,\beta}(z)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{y}\frac{\alpha\sqrt{1-\alpha}\left(\sqrt{1-\alpha}(1+y)^{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}-2\alpha\beta(1-y)^{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}\right)}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}(1+y)^{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}(1-\sqrt{1-\alpha}y)+2\alpha\beta(1-y)^{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}(1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y)}

Note that by definition, for
0<\alpha<1
,
\tilde{U}_{\alpha,0}(y)
and
\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\infty}
(and thus
U_{\alpha,0,\kappa}(y)
and
U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}(y)
) are smooth in the light cone
|y|\leq 1
. However, for
0<\beta<\infty
,
\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}(y)
does not belong to
C^{k}[-1,1]
if
k>1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}
.

Remark 2.1
.

For
\alpha=1
, we have

g_{1,\beta}(y)=\frac{-2}{2y+\ln\lvert\frac{1-y}{1+y}\rvert+\beta},

which is always singular for
0\leq\beta<\infty.
Moreover,
\tilde{U}_{1,0}=\tilde{U}_{1,\infty}\equiv 0
, and
U_{1,0,\kappa}=U_{1,\infty,\kappa}=s+\kappa
which coincides with the ODE blow-up solution.

Remark 2.2
.

Similar to the non-linear wave equation with nonlinearity
u_{x}^{2}
, smooth generalized self-similar profiles
U_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}
only exist when
0<\alpha\leq 1
and
\beta=0,\infty
. Therefore, we may conjecture that
U_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}
with
0<\alpha\leq 1
and
\beta=0,\infty
are stable and determine the asymptotic behaviour of smooth blow-up solutions.

Remark 2.3

(Log blow-up)
.

An interesting phenomenon of the blow-up solutions to derivative nonlinear wave is that exact self-similar solutions are singular whereas the ODE blow-up solution has a log correction to the scaling. Log-blow up rate seems to be generic.

2.2. Self-similar solutions to (
1.2
).

For
T>0
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, we introduce the following self-similar variables

(2.5)

\begin{cases}u=U(s,y),\\
s=-\ln(T-t),\\
y=\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t}.\end{cases}

The (
1.2
) can be transformed into

(2.6)

\partial_{ss}U+\partial_{s}U+2y\partial_{ys}U+2y\partial_{y}U+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}U=(\partial_{s}U+y\partial_{y}U)^{2}-(\partial_{y}U)^{2}.

Few points are in order:

•

Self-similar solutions:
The exact self-similar solutions of (
1.2
) are always singular within the light cone
\lvert y\rvert\leq 1
. More precisely, we have

U(y):=-\int\frac{1}{d(y^{2}-1)+\frac{1}{2}(y^{2}-1)\ln\lvert\frac{1+y}{1-y}\rvert},\quad d\in\mathbb{R}

which is singular at
y=\pm 1
.

•

ODE blow-up:
For this equation, we also have a trivial ODE blow-up solution which is independent of the spatial variable, i.e.,

u=-\ln(T-t).

And by the finite propagation speed we can construct blow-up solutions for smooth and compactly supported initial data. By the Lorentz invariance of (

u(t,x)=-\ln(T-t+dx),\quad-1\leq d\leq 1.

•

Generalized self-similar blow-up:
Similarly, for
\alpha>0,\beta\in[0,\infty],\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
, there are other families of generalized self-similar blow-up solutions of the following form

(2.7)

U_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}=\alpha s+\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}(y)+\kappa

where

satisfies

\begin{cases}\alpha+2y\partial_{y}\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}+(y^{2}-1)\partial_{yy}\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}=(\alpha+y\partial_{y}\tilde{U})^{2}-(\partial_{y}\tilde{U})^{2},\\
\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}(0)=0,\end{cases}

and the extra parameter
\beta
is to be specified. Indeed, for each
\alpha>0
we can solve the above equation as

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}(y)

\displaystyle:=-\alpha\int_{0}^{y}\frac{(1+y^{\prime})^{\alpha-1}-\beta(1-y^{\prime})^{\alpha-1}}{(1+y^{\prime})^{\alpha}+\beta(1-y^{\prime})^{\alpha}}\mathrm{\,d}y^{\prime}

(2.8)

\displaystyle=-\log\left((1+y)^{\alpha}+\beta(1-y)^{\alpha}\right)+\log(1+\beta),\quad\beta>0.

In particular, for
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+},

\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\beta}(y)
is smooth for all
\lvert y\rvert\leq 1
. Moreover, for
\alpha=1
,

\tilde{U}_{1,\beta}(y)=-\log\left(1+\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}y\right),\quad\beta>0,

in which case we go back to the ODE-family blow-up solutions.

Remark 2.4
.

Compared to (
1.1
), there are countable many families of smooth generalized self-similar blow-up profiles to (
1.2
), i.e.,
{U}_{\alpha,\beta,\kappa}
with
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
. However, we only expect the ground state
\alpha=1,\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is stable, while for
\alpha\geq 2,\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, these families of blow-up solutions are only finite co-dimensional stable. Moreover, these smooth profiles together shall determine the asymptotic behaviour of general smooth blow-up solutions.

3.1. Unstable eigenvalues induced by symmetries

3.2. Eigen-equation for
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}

To study the mode stability of
U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}=\alpha s+\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\infty}+\kappa
with
0<\alpha\leq 1
, we need to solve the corresponding eigen-equation (
3.4
) which we recall:

(3.5)

(y^{2}-1)\varphi^{\prime\prime}+\left(2\lambda+2-\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}\right)y\varphi^{\prime}+\lambda\left(\lambda+1-\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}\right)\varphi=0.

This can be written as the Heun type ODE:

(3.6)

\varphi^{\prime\prime}+\left[\frac{\lambda-\sqrt{1-\alpha}}{y+1}+\frac{\lambda+\sqrt{1-\alpha}}{y-1}+\frac{2}{y+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}}\right]\varphi^{\prime}+\frac{\lambda(\lambda+1)y+\frac{\lambda(\lambda+1-2\alpha)}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}}{(y-1)(y+1)(y+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}})}\varphi=0.

Note that (
3.6
) has four regular singular points
\pm 1,-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}
and
\infty
. The standard form of a Heun type equation reads as

(3.7)

g^{\prime\prime}(z)+\left[\frac{\gamma}{z}+\frac{\delta}{z-1}+\frac{\varepsilon}{z-d}\right]g^{\prime}(z)+\frac{abz-c}{z(z-1)(z-d)}g(z)=0.

where
a,b,c,d,\gamma,\delta,\varepsilon\in\mathbb{C}
and
a+b+1=\gamma+\delta+\varepsilon
. Letting
z:=\frac{y+1}{2}
then (
3.6
) can be written in the standard form

(3.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\varphi^{\prime\prime}+\left[\frac{\lambda-\sqrt{1-\alpha}}{z}+\frac{\lambda+\sqrt{1-\alpha}}{z-1}+\frac{2}{z-\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}})}\right]\varphi^{\prime}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{\lambda(\lambda+1)z+\frac{\lambda(\lambda+1)}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}-1\right)-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}}{z(z-1)\left(z-\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}})\right)}\varphi=0.\end{split}

where we take
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda-\alpha+1-\frac{\alpha}{1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}}),\delta=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda-\alpha+1-\frac{\alpha}{1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}}),\varepsilon=-1,

d=\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}),

c=\frac{\lambda}{8}\left[(\lambda+1-4\alpha)-\frac{\lambda+1-2\alpha}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}\right]
and
a,b
are determined by solving

\begin{cases}a+b+1=\gamma+\delta+\varepsilon,\\
ab=\frac{\lambda}{4}(\lambda+1-4\alpha).\end{cases}

where we take
\gamma=\lambda-\sqrt{1-\alpha},\delta=\lambda+\sqrt{1-\alpha},\varepsilon=2,d=\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}),

c=-\frac{\lambda(\lambda+1)}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}-1\right)+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}
,
a=\lambda,
and
b=\lambda+1
.
The existence of smooth solutions to such type of ODEs remains largely open and is commonly known as the “connection problem” in the literature, see for instance
[
9
]
. However, we bypass this difficulty by using the Lorentz transformation in self-similar variables to convert Heun type ODE (

) into a new hypergeometric ODE.

3.2.1. Mode Stability of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}

The idea is motivated by the observation that the generalized self-similar blow-up solution
U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}
can be transformed to the ODE blow-up solution of a new derivative non-linear wave equation using the Lorentz transformation. More precisely, recall the Lorentz transformation and its inverse

(3.9)

\begin{cases}t^{\prime}=\frac{t-\gamma x}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}},\\
x^{\prime}=\frac{x-\gamma t}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}},\end{cases}\quad\text{and, }\quad\begin{cases}t=\frac{t^{\prime}+\gamma x^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}},\\
x=\frac{x^{\prime}+\gamma t^{\prime}}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}},\end{cases}\quad\text{ where }\gamma\in(-1,1).

Let
v(t^{\prime},x^{\prime}):=u(t,x)
and if
u
solves (
1.1

solves

(3.10)

\partial_{t^{\prime}t^{\prime}}v-\partial_{x^{\prime}x^{\prime}}v=\frac{1}{1-\gamma^{2}}\left(\partial_{t^{\prime}}v-\gamma\partial_{x^{\prime}}v\right)^{2}.

And under this transformation, the generalized self-similar blow-up solutions

U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}=-\alpha\ln(T-t)-\alpha\ln(1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}\frac{x-x_{0}}{T-t})=-\alpha\ln\left(T-t+\sqrt{1-\alpha}(x-x_{0})\right)+\kappa

corresponds to

V_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}:=-\alpha\ln\left(T+\frac{\gamma\sqrt{1-\alpha}-1}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}t^{\prime}+\frac{\sqrt{1-\alpha}-\gamma}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}x^{\prime}-\sqrt{1-\alpha}x_{0}\right)+\kappa.

In particular, if we take
\gamma=\sqrt{1-\alpha}
then

is independent of the spatial variable

and is thus an ODE blow-up solution to (
3.10
). However, it is not immediate that the stability of

directly corresponds to stability of

and

•

Stable evolution in

does not necessarily imply stable evolution in

.

Nevertheless, the advantage of such a transformation is that it allows us to study the spectrum of the linearised operator at

by analysing the linearised operator at

in self-similar variables, thereby facilitating a rigorous proof of the mode stability of

. To that end, we consider the Lorentz transformation.

For

A direct calculation implies the following:

(Lorentz Transform in self-similar variables)

Under the self-similar variables

. In particular, instead of studying the eigen-equation (
3.4
) which has a complicated Heun-type ODE structure (
3.6
), we can study (
3.14
) which has a hypergeometric-type structure which is more simpler as its connection problem is well-posed. Moreover, while previously, we had four singular points, now we only have three singular points at
\pm 1
and
\infty
.

More precisely, the eigen-equation for
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{\alpha}
is written as

(\lambda^{2}-\lambda)\varphi+(2\lambda y^{\prime}+2\sqrt{1-\alpha})\varphi^{\prime}+({y^{\prime}}^{2}-1)\varphi^{\prime\prime}=0.

This can be written as the hyper-geometric ODE:

(3.15)

(y^{\prime}-1)(y^{\prime}+1)\partial_{y^{\prime}y^{\prime}}\varphi+(2\sqrt{1-\alpha}+2\lambda y^{\prime})\partial_{y^{\prime}}\varphi+(\lambda^{2}-\lambda)\varphi=0.

Indeed, the standard form of a hypergeometric differential equation is given by

(3.16)

z^{\prime}(1-z^{\prime})\partial_{z^{\prime}z^{\prime}}\psi+(c-(a+b+1)z^{\prime})\partial_{z^{\prime}}\psi-ab\psi=0,\quad a,b,c\in\mathbb{C}.

Letting
y^{\prime}:=2z^{\prime}-1
and
\varphi(y^{\prime}):=\psi(z^{\prime})
, we can rewrite (
3.15
) as

(3.17)

z^{\prime}(1-z^{\prime})\partial_{z^{\prime}z^{\prime}}\psi+(\lambda-\sqrt{1-\alpha}-2\lambda z^{\prime})\partial_{z^{\prime}}\psi-\lambda(\lambda-1)\psi=0.

where we have set
a=\lambda-1,b=\lambda
and
c=\lambda-\sqrt{1-\alpha}
.
As a consequence, we will prove the following:

Proposition 3.8
.

For all
\alpha\in(0,1]
there exists no non-trivial
\psi\in C^{\infty}[-1,1]
solving (
3.17
) for
\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\lambda>-1
except for
\lambda=0
or 1. In particular, in light of Corollary
3.6
,
U_{\alpha,\infty,\kappa}
is mode-stable.

Remark 3.9
.

The result goes beyond mode stability, demonstrating that the spectral gap between the stable and unstable eigenvalues is at least 1.

To prove the above proposition, we will be needing the standard Frobenius analysis.

Lemma 3.10

(Frobenius Theory)
.

Consider the second-order differential equation

(3.18)

f^{\prime\prime}(z)+p(z)f^{\prime}(z)+q(z)f(z)=0

where
p,q\in H(\mathbb{D}_{R}^{\prime})
are holomorphic functions with at-most a first and second order pole at
0
respectively. Let
s_{\pm}
be the solutions to the
indicial equation

s^{2}+(p_{0}-1)s+q_{0}=0,\quad p_{0}:=\lim_{z\to 0}zp(z),\quad q_{0}:=\lim_{z\to 0}z^{2}q(z)

satisfying the ordering
\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}(s_{+})\geq\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}(s_{-})
. Then there exists
h_{\pm}\in H(\mathbb{D}_{R}):h_{\pm}(0)=1
such that the fundamental system of solutions of (
3.18
) on
\mathbb{D}_{R}\backslash\mathbb{R}_{-}
is given by

f_{+}(z):=z^{s_{+}}h_{+}(z),\quad f_{-}(z):=z^{s_{-}}h_{-}(z)+c_{s_{+}-s_{-}}f_{+}(z)\log(z)

where

c_{\omega}\in\begin{cases}\{0\}&\text{if}\quad\omega\not\in\mathbb{N},\\
\mathbb{C}&\text{if}\quad\omega\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\\
\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}&\text{if}\quad\omega=0\end{cases}

Dividing by
z^{\prime}(1-z^{\prime})
the equation (
3.17

\partial_{z^{\prime}z^{\prime}}\psi+\frac{\lambda-\sqrt{1-\alpha}-2\lambda z^{\prime}}{z^{\prime}(1-z^{\prime})}\partial_{z^{\prime}}\psi+\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)}{z^{\prime}(1-z^{\prime})}\psi=0

and thus

p(z)=\frac{\lambda-\sqrt{1-\alpha}-2\lambda z^{\prime}}{z^{\prime}(1-z^{\prime})},\quad q(z):=\frac{\lambda(1-\lambda)}{z^{\prime}(1-z^{\prime})}.

The Frobenius analysis allows us to construct a solution of the form

(3.20)

\psi(z^{\prime}):=(z^{\prime}-z_{0}^{\prime})^{s}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\psi_{k}(z^{\prime}-z^{\prime}_{0})^{n},

where the solution holds in a local (depending on the convergence radius of
p
and
q
) neighbourhood of the singularity
z_{0}\in\{0,1\}
. The idea now is to show that for
\lambda:\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\lambda>-1
, the solutions obtained via the Frobenius analysis will fail to be smooth on

except possibly when
\lambda=0
or

3.2.2. Frobenius analysis at

For the singularity

Moreover set

\lambda-\sqrt{1-\alpha}\end{matrix}\,;z^{\prime}\right)

c\end{matrix}\,;z^{\prime}\right)
is the Gauss hypergeometric function which is holomorphic in

•

If
-1<\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\lambda\leq 1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}
and

>

Proof of Proposition
3.8
.

For
\alpha\in(0,1]
, as per the above Frobenius analysis, we have shown that for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\lambda>-1
and any

\psi
to (
3.19
),

must also be analytic at
\{0,1\}
. We will show that
\psi
must fail to be analytic at
z^{\prime}=0
unless
\lambda=0
or
1
. Recall that Frobenius analysis at
z^{\prime}_{0}=1
showed that for
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
\lambda\neq 0,1
,
\tilde{\psi}(z^{\prime\prime}):=\psi(1-z^{\prime\prime})
must be a scalar multiple of either
h_{3},z^{\prime\prime}h_{3}
or
h_{4}
. Without loss of generality, let us fix

\tilde{\psi}(z^{\prime\prime})=h_{4}(z^{\prime\prime})=F\left(\begin{matrix}\lambda-1,\lambda\\
\lambda+\sqrt{1-\alpha}\end{matrix}\quad;z^{\prime\prime}\right).

We claim that it fails to be analytic at
z^{\prime\prime}=1
which then contradicts the results obtained via Frobenius analysis at
z^{\prime}_{0}=0
.
Since
h_{4}
is a power series centered at 0 that satisfies the hypergeometric equation (
3.21
), if it remains analytic at
1
, then its radius of convergence must exceed 1, because any solution of (
3.21
) is analytic everywhere except possibly at the regular singular points 0, 1, and

However, applying the ratio tests on the coefficients of the power series of

shows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(h_{4})_{n+1}}{(h_{4})_{n}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\frac{\Gamma(\lambda-1+n+1)\Gamma(\lambda+n+1)}{\Gamma(\lambda+\sqrt{1-\alpha}+n+1)\Gamma(n+1)}}{\frac{\Gamma(\lambda-1+n)\Gamma(\lambda+n)}{\Gamma(\lambda+\sqrt{1-\alpha}+n)\Gamma(n)}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(\lambda-1+n)(\lambda+n)}{(\lambda+\sqrt{1-\alpha}+n)n}\approx\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^{2}}{n^{2}}=1

unless
\lambda-1
or
\lambda
equals

in which case the radius of convergence is infinite. The same argument works in the case
\tilde{\psi}(z^{\prime\prime})=h_{3}(z^{\prime\prime})
or
\tilde{\psi}(z^{\prime\prime})=z^{\prime\prime}h_{3}(z^{\prime\prime})

. Then we have the following transformations

(4.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\begin{cases}U_{1,\beta,\kappa}\\
\eqref{eq:4.Linearised equation at Uαβκ}\\
\mathbf{L}^{2}_{1,\beta}\end{cases}\textup{gets transformed into}\\
&\begin{cases}V_{1,\beta,\kappa}:=s^{\prime}-\log 2\sqrt{\beta}+\kappa&\textup{(Self-similar blow-up family)},\\
\partial_{s^{\prime}s^{\prime}}\xi-\partial_{s^{\prime}}\xi+2y^{\prime}\partial_{y^{\prime}s^{\prime}}\xi+({y^{\prime}}^{2}-1)\partial_{y^{\prime}y^{\prime}}\xi=0&\textup{(Linearisation at }V_{1,\beta,\kappa}),\\
\mathbf{L}^{\prime\prime}_{1}\begin{pmatrix}r_{1}\\
r_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}r_{2}-y^{\prime}\partial_{y^{\prime}}r_{1}\\
r_{2}-y^{\prime}\partial_{y^{\prime}}r_{2}+\partial_{y^{\prime}y^{\prime}}r_{1}\\
\end{pmatrix}&\textup{(Linearised operator).}\end{cases}\end{split}

Proof.

Follows from direct calculation as done in the previous section.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Recall that we had shown

\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}r_{1}\\
r_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}r_{2}-y^{\prime}\partial_{y^{\prime}}r_{1}\\
r_{2}-y^{\prime}\partial_{y^{\prime}}r_{2}+\partial_{y^{\prime}y^{\prime}}r_{1}-2\sqrt{1-\alpha}\partial_{y^{\prime}}r_{1}\\
\end{pmatrix}

is mode stable for all
0<\alpha\leq 1.
Note that

\mathbf{L}^{\prime\prime}_{1}=\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{1}

and hence the mode-stability of
\mathbf{L}^{\prime\prime}_{1}
is reduced to showing the mode-stability of
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{1}
.

In light of Remark
4.1
and Remark
4.3
and Proposition
3.8
, we obtain

Proposition 4.4
.

For
\beta>0
, the operator
\mathbf{L}^{2}_{1,\beta}
admits no eigenvalues with
\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda>-1
except for
\lambda=0
and
\lambda=1
. As a result,
U_{1,\beta,\kappa}
is mode-stable for all
\beta>0,\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
.

5. Spectral Analysis of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}

In this section, we study the essential spectrum of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}:=\mathbf{L}^{1}_{\alpha,\infty}
. Here we aim to write
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
as a sum of a maximally dissipative operator and a compact perturbation. To that end, we present the functional setup.

5.1. Functional Setup

Per (
3.2
), the linearised equation can be written as

(5.1)

\partial_{s}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}:=\begin{pmatrix}q_{2}-y\partial_{y}q_{1}\\
-q_{2}-y\partial_{y}q_{2}+\partial_{yy}q_{1}+\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}q_{2}\end{pmatrix}

We now further decompose the operator into the
free wave operator
and the
potential
respectively:

\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}:=\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square}\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}:=\begin{pmatrix}q_{2}-y\partial_{y}q_{1}-q_{1}(-1)\\
-q_{2}-y\partial_{y}q_{2}+\partial_{yy}q_{1}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}(-1)\\
\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}q_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

We recursively define the following sesquilinear forms on
C^{k+1}[-1,1]\times C^{k}[-1,1]:

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle:=f_{1}(-1)\overline{g_{1}(-1)}+\langle f_{1},g_{1}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}(-1,1)}+\langle f_{2},g_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(-1,1)},

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}\rangle_{k}

\displaystyle:=\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}\rangle_{0}+\langle f_{1},g_{1}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k+1}(-1,1)}+\langle f_{2},g_{2}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k}(-1,1)},

and similarly, we define the induced norms on
C^{k+1}[-1,1]\times C^{k}[-1,1]
by

\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{k}^{2}:=\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}\rangle_{k}.

In fact, the
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{k}
norm is equivalent to the norm on the Sobolev space
H^{k+1}\times H^{k}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{f}\in C^{k+1}[-1,1]\times C^{k}[-1,1]
, we have

\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{k}\approx\lVert f_{1}\rVert_{H^{k+1}}+\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{H^{k}}.

Proof.

As per the definition of
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{k}
and
\langle\,,\,\rangle_{k}
, it suffices to show

\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{0}^{2}\geq\langle f_{1},f_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}},\quad\forall\mathbf{f}\in C^{1}[-1,1]\times C^{0}[-1,1].

Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\lVert f_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert f_{1}(-1)+\int_{-1}^{y}\partial_{y}f_{1}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lvert f_{1}(-1)\rvert+\int_{-1}^{y}\lVert\partial_{y}f_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lvert f_{1}(-1)\rvert+\lVert f_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}}\leq\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{0}.\qed

5.2. Generation of the semigroup.

For
\alpha>0
, fix
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\alpha})=\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{\tilde{L}})=(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
and denote
\mathcal{H}^{k}:=H^{k+1}(-1,1)\times H^{k}(-1,1)
. Then the free wave operator
\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square}:\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square})\subset\mathcal{H}^{k}\to\mathcal{H}^{k}
is well-defined for
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 5.2

(Dissipativity of
\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square}
)
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{f}\in C^{k+1}[-1,1]\times C^{k}[-1,1]:

\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square}\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}\rangle_{k}\leq-\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{k}^{2}.

Proof.

For
k=0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square}\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\left((f_{2}-y\partial_{y}f_{1}-f_{1}(-1))\bar{f}_{1}\right)|_{y=-1}+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle f_{2}-y\partial_{y}f_{1},f_{1}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}}+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle\partial_{yy}f_{1}-f_{2}-y\partial_{y}f_{2},f_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\lvert f_{1}(-1)\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lvert(f_{2}-y\partial_{y}f_{1})(-1)\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lvert f_{1}(-1)\rvert^{2}+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle f_{2},f_{1}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}}-\lVert\partial_{y}f_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle y,\partial_{y}\lvert\partial_{y}f_{1}\rvert^{2}\rangle+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle\partial_{yy}f_{1},f_{2}\rangle-\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle y,\partial_{y}\lvert f_{2}\rvert^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\lvert f_{1}(-1)\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lvert(f_{2}+\partial_{y}f_{1})(-1)\rvert^{2}-\lVert\partial_{y}f_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(\lvert\partial_{y}f_{1}(\pm 1)\rvert^{2}+\lvert f_{2}(\pm 1)\rvert^{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\lVert\partial_{y}f_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}(\partial_{y}f_{1}f_{2})|_{-1}^{1}-\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\lvert f_{1}(-1)\rvert^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\lVert\partial_{y}f_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{0}^{2}.

For
k\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square}\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}\rangle_{k}=\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle f_{2}-y\partial_{y}f_{1},f_{1}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle\partial_{yy}f_{1}-f_{2}-y\partial_{y}f_{2},f_{2}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k}}+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square}\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle f_{2},f_{1}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}-(k+1)\lVert\partial^{k+1}f_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle y,\partial_{y}\lvert\partial^{k+1}f_{1}\rvert^{2}\rangle+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle\partial_{yy}f_{1},f_{2}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\lVert\partial^{k}f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-k\lVert\partial^{k}f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle y,\partial_{y}\lvert\partial^{k}f_{2}\rvert^{2}\rangle+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square}\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}(\partial^{k+1}f_{1}\partial^{k}\bar{f}_{2})|_{-1}^{1}-(k+1-\frac{1}{2})(\lVert\partial^{k+1}f_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert\partial^{k}f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{1}{2}(\lvert\partial^{k+1}f_{1}(\pm 1)\rvert^{2}+\lvert\partial^{k}f_{2}(\pm 1)\rvert^{2})+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square}\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}\rangle_{0}\leq-\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{k}^{2}.\qed

We will now show that
\mathbf{\tilde{L}_{\square}}
has dense range in
\mathcal{H}^{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 5.3

(Dense range of
\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square}
)
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbf{g}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an
\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}:

\lVert\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square}\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{g}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}\leq\varepsilon.

Proof.

Since smooth functions are dense in
\mathcal{H}^{k}
, it suffices to assume
\mathbf{g}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
. We now show that there exists a
\mathbf{f}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
such that

\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square}\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{g}.

In particular, we show

\begin{cases}f_{2}-y\partial_{y}f_{1}-f_{1}(-1)=g_{1},\\
\partial_{yy}f_{1}-f_{2}-y\partial_{y}f_{2}=g_{2}.\end{cases}

(Here by
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, we mean a representative from the equivalence class denoted by
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
. Moreover, establishing the pointwise equality is allowed as we are only interested in the
L^{p}
-norms). Plugging the first equation in the second, we obtain the following second-order ODE:

(1-y^{2})\partial_{yy}f_{1}-2y\partial_{y}f_{1}=g_{1}-y\partial_{y}g_{1}+g_{2}+f_{1}(-1)

This can be written as

\partial_{y}\left((1-y^{2})\partial_{y}f_{1}\right)=g_{1}-y\partial_{y}g_{1}+g_{2}+f_{1}(-1)=:F(y)+f_{1}(-1)

and thus the solution is

f_{1}(y)=f_{1}(-1)+\int_{-1}^{y}\frac{\int_{-1}^{y^{\prime}}F(y^{\prime})+f_{1}(-1)}{1-{y^{\prime}}^{2}}\mathrm{\,d}y^{\prime}.

It remains to study the smoothness properties of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
. Since
f_{2}=y\partial_{y}f_{1}+f_{1}(-1)+g_{1}
where
g_{1}
is smooth so it suffices to show that
f_{1}
is smooth. As
g_{1},g_{2}
are smooth so then is
F
and thus the above formula suggests that
f
is smooth atleast on
(-1,1)
. At
y=-1
, we can express the integrand as

\frac{1}{1-y^{\prime}}\frac{1}{1+y^{\prime}}\int_{-1}^{y^{\prime}}(F(y^{\prime})+f_{1}(-1))

which is smooth at
-1
as it is a product of functions that are smooth near
-1
(recall that
\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}\varphi\in C^{k}[0,1]
if
\varphi\in C^{k-1}[0,1]
.) For
y=1
, we express the integrand similarly:

\frac{1}{1+y^{\prime}}\frac{1}{1-y^{\prime}}\int_{-1}^{y^{\prime}}\left(F+f_{1}(-1)\right)=\frac{1}{1+y^{\prime}}\frac{1}{1-y^{\prime}}\left(\int_{-1}^{1}(F+f_{1}(-1))-\int_{y^{\prime}}^{1}(F+f_{1}(-1))\right).

This is smooth iff
\int_{-1}^{1}\left(F+f_{1}(-1)\right)=0
. Thus fixing

f_{1}(-1)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}F

implies that
f_{1}\in C^{\infty}[-1,1]
and thus
\mathbf{f}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

For
\in\mathbb{N}
, the operator
\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square}:D(\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square})\subset\mathcal{H}^{k}\to\mathcal{H}^{k}
is a densely-defined closable operator whose closure
\mathbf{L}_{\square}
generates a strongly continuous one-parameter semigroup
\mathbf{S}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{k})
which satisfies

\lVert\mathbf{S}(s)\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{k})}\lesssim e^{-\frac{1}{2}s},\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{\tilde{L}}_{\square})=(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
so it is densely-defined in
\mathcal{H}^{k}
. Moreover, the previous lemmas show that it is a dissipative operator with dense range for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, the conclusion follows from the Lumer-Phillips Theorem.
∎

Next we deal with the potential operator
\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}
. It turns out that this potential operator is not a compact operator, not even relatively compact with respect to
\mathbf{L}_{\square}
. Nevertheless, we can prove that

Proposition 5.5
.

For all
\alpha\in(0,1],k\in\mathbb{N}
the operator
\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}
is bounded on
\mathcal{H}^{k}
. As such, the operator
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{L}_{\square}+\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}
generates a strongly continuous one-parameter semigroup
\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{k})
which satisfies

\lVert\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{k})}\lesssim e^{\left(\lVert\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{k})}-\frac{1}{2}\right)s},\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Proof.

Note that
\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}
is bounded on
\mathcal{H}^{k}
since

\lVert\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}\mathbf{f}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}={\lVert f_{1}(-1)\rVert_{H^{k+1}}}+\left\lVert\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}f_{2}\right\rVert_{H^{k}}\lesssim_{\alpha}\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}.

As such, by the bounded perturbation theorem,
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
generates a strongly continuous one-parameter semigroup.
∎

5.3. A new decomposition with a compact perturbation

Since
\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}
is merely a bounded perturbation without any compactness compared to the free wave operator, we can not obtain any information for the essential spectrum of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
and hence no uniform growth for the semigroup
\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}
. To overcome this difficulty, we introduce a new decomposition of the linearised operator
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
as a sum of a new dissipative operator plus a compact perturbation. More precisely, we recall that

\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}-y\partial_{y}&1\\
\partial_{yy}&\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}-1-y\partial_{y}\end{pmatrix}.

Here
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
is not necessarily dissipative:

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{f},

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}\times L^{2}}\leq\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle f_{2}-y\partial_{y}f_{1},f_{1}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}}+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\left\langle\partial_{yy}f_{1}-f_{2}-y\partial_{y}f_{2}+\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}f_{2},f_{2}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle f_{2},f_{1}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}}-\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle\partial_{y}f_{1}+y\partial_{yy}f_{1},\partial_{y}f_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle\partial_{yy}f_{1},f_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle y,\partial_{y}\lvert f_{2}\rvert^{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\left\langle\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}f_{2},f_{2}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle f_{2},f_{1}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}}-\lVert f_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle y,\partial_{y}(\lvert\partial_{y}f_{1}\rvert^{2}+\lvert f_{2}\rvert^{2})\rangle_{L^{2}}+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}(\partial_{y}f_{1}f_{2})_{-1}^{1}-\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\left\langle\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}f_{2},f_{2}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq-\lVert f_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}((\lvert\partial_{y}f_{1}\rvert^{2}+\lvert f_{2}\rvert^{2})(\pm 1)-\lVert f_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}-\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2})+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}(\partial_{y}f_{1}f_{2})_{-1}^{1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\left\langle\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}f_{2},f_{2}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\lVert f_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\left\langle\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}f_{2},f_{2}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\lVert f_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}+\left(-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}\right)\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

To gain more dissipation, we need to consider
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
in a Sobolev space with higher regularity, i.e.
\mathcal{H}^{k}:=H^{k+1}\times H^{k}
for large enough
k
.

Lemma 5.6

(Commutator with derivatives)
.

For
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\partial^{k}\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k}\partial^{k}+\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{\alpha,k}

where

\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k}=\begin{pmatrix}-k-y\partial_{y}&1\\
\partial_{yy}&\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}-1-k-y\partial_{y}\end{pmatrix}

and
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{\alpha,k}
satisfies the pointwise bound

\lvert\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{\alpha,k}\mathbf{f}\rvert\lesssim_{\alpha}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\lvert\partial^{j}f_{1}\rvert\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

Direct computation yields the following formulas:

[\partial^{k},y\partial_{y}]=k\partial^{k},\quad\left[\partial^{k},\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}\right]=\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}\partial^{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}C^{j}_{k}\partial^{j}\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}\partial^{k-j}

and the pointwise bound follows from

\left\lVert\partial^{j}\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(-1,1)}\lesssim_{\alpha,j}\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)^{\frac{j-1}{2}}}{(1-\sqrt{1-\alpha})^{j}}.\qed

For
k\geq 0
, we introduce the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{H^{k}}:=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\langle f,g\rangle_{\dot{H}^{j}}.

This is equivalent to the following representation which we will fixate from now onwards:

\langle f,g\rangle_{H^{k}}:=\langle f,g\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k}}+\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}}.

For tuples of functions
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},f_{2})
, we introduce the corresponding inner product on
\mathcal{H}^{k}
as

\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}:=\langle f_{1},g_{1}\rangle_{H^{k+1}}+\langle f_{2},g_{2}\rangle_{H^{k}}.

To control
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{\alpha,k}
, we prove the following subcoercivity estimate:

Lemma 5.7
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists positive
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
and positive
c_{n}
and subspaces
\{\Pi_{i}\}_{i\leq n}\subset H^{k+1}(-1,1):

(5.2)

\varepsilon_{n}\langle f,f\rangle_{H^{k+1}}\geq\lVert f\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}-c_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\langle f,\Pi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}}\rvert^{2},\quad\forall f\in H^{k+1}(-1,1),\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

Proof.

We first construct the subspaces
\{\Pi_{i}\}_{i\leq n}
. To that end, consider an element
T\in L^{2}(-1,1)
. This defines a anti-linear functional on
(H^{k+1},\langle\,,\,\rangle_{H^{k+1}})
via the mapping
h\mapsto\langle T,h\rangle_{L^{2}}
. As such, via Riesz representation theorem, we can find a unique element
l(T)\in H^{k+1}
satisfying

\langle l(T),h\rangle_{H^{k+1}}=\langle T,h\rangle_{L^{2}},\quad\forall h\in H^{k+1}(-1,1).

In particular, we have constructed a mapping
l:L^{2}(-1,1)\to H^{k+1}(-1,1)
. If we compose it with the imbedding
\iota:H^{k+1}(-1,1)\to L^{2}(-1,1)
, then
\iota\circ l
is compact (via Sobolev Embedding theorem) and self-adjoint as

\displaystyle\langle T_{1},\iota\circ l(T_{2})\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle T_{1},l(T_{2})\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle l(T_{1}),l(T_{2})\rangle_{H^{k+1}}=\overline{\langle l(T_{2}),l(T_{1})\rangle_{H^{k+1}}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\langle T_{2},l(T_{1})\rangle_{L^{2}}}=\langle\iota\circ l(T_{1}),T_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}.

We also find that it is positive-definite since

\langle i\circ l(T_{1}),T_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle l(T_{1}),l(T_{1})\rangle_{H^{k+1}}\geq 0.

Consequently,
\iota\circ l\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(-1,1))
is a positive-definite compact operator and so it admits a spectral decomposition characterised by the positive eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}
and an orthonormal basis
\{\Pi_{n,i}\}_{i\leq I(n)}
such that
0<\lambda_{n}\to 0.
With this, we have constructed the relevant collection of subspaces and we set
\{\Pi_{i}\}_{i\leq n}:=\{\Pi_{m,j}\}_{j\leq I(m),m\leq n}.

We now turn our attention towards establishing the inequality. To that end, first consider the minimisation problem

\displaystyle I_{n}:

\displaystyle=\inf_{\Psi\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\,\langle\Psi,\Psi\rangle_{H^{k+1}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{n}:

\displaystyle=\left\{\Psi\in H^{k+1}(-1,1):\lVert\Psi\rVert_{L^{2}}=1\text{ and }\forall j\in[1,n].\forall i\in[1,I(j)]:\langle\Psi,\Pi_{j,i}\rangle_{L^{2}}=0\right\}.

The infimum is attained on some
\Psi^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
as this problem can be seen as the projection of
0
onto the closed convex set
\mathcal{A}_{n}
in
(H^{k+1},\langle\,,\,\rangle_{H^{k+1}})
which always exist as per Hilbert space theory. Moreover, writing the Lagrangian of this minimisation problem and considering the first variation along any direction
h\in H^{k+1}(-1,1)
, we have

\langle\Psi^{\star},h\rangle_{H^{k+1}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq n\\
i\leq I(j)\end{subarray}}\beta_{j,i}\langle\Pi_{j,i},h\rangle_{L^{2}}+\beta\langle\Psi^{\star},h\rangle_{L^{2}},\quad\forall h\in H^{k+1}(-1,1).

Fixing
h=\Pi_{j,i}
gives us
\forall j\leq n,\forall i\leq I(j).

0=\lambda_{n}^{-1}\langle\Psi^{\star},\Pi_{j,i}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\lambda_{n}^{-1}\langle\Psi^{\star},l(\Pi_{j,i})\rangle_{H^{k+1}}=\langle\Psi^{\star},\Pi_{j,i}\rangle_{H^{k+1}}=\beta_{j,i}+\beta\langle\Psi^{\star},\Pi_{j,i}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\beta_{j,i}.

Thus we have

\langle\Psi^{\star},h\rangle_{H^{k+1}}=\beta\langle\Psi^{\star},h\rangle_{L^{2}}=\beta\langle l(\Psi^{\star}),h\rangle_{H^{k+1}},\quad\forall h\in H^{k+1}(-1,1).

Thus
(\beta,\Psi^{\star})
is an eigenpair for
(l,(H^{k+1},\langle\,,\,\rangle_{H^{k+1}}))
and thus even for
(l,L^{2})
. Since
\Psi^{\star}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
so

\beta^{-1}=\langle\Psi^{\star},l(\Psi^{\star})\rangle_{L^{2}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq n+1\\
i\leq I(j)\end{subarray}}\lambda_{j}\lvert\langle\Psi^{\star},\Pi_{j,i}\rangle_{L^{2}}\rvert^{2}\leq\lambda_{n+1}\lVert\Psi^{\star}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\lambda_{n+1}.

As such, we obtain

I_{n}=\langle\Psi^{\star},\Psi^{\star}\rangle_{H^{k+1}}=\beta\langle\Psi^{\star},\Psi^{\star}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\beta\geq\lambda_{n+1}^{-1}.

In other words,

(5.3)

\langle\Psi,\Psi\rangle_{L^{2}}\leq\lambda_{n+1}\langle\Psi,\Psi\rangle_{H^{k+1}},\quad\forall\Psi\in\mathcal{A}_{n}.

Finally, fix
\varepsilon>0,k\in\mathbb{N}
and for any
f\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
, by Gagliardo-Nirenberg and Young’s inequality and (
5.3
)

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon\lVert f\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}^{2}+c_{\varepsilon,k}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon\langle f,f\rangle_{H^{k+1}}+c_{\varepsilon,k}\lambda_{n+1}\langle f,f\rangle_{H^{k+1}}\leq(\varepsilon+c_{\varepsilon,k}\lambda_{n+1})\langle f,f\rangle_{H^{k+1}}.

Letting
n
large enough so that
c_{\varepsilon,k}\lambda_{n+1}\leq\varepsilon
, we obtain

\lVert f\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}\leq 2\varepsilon\langle f,f\rangle_{H^{k+1}},\quad\forall f\in H^{k+1}:\langle f,\Pi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}}=0,\forall i\leq n.

For a general
f
, we apply the above inequality with
f-\sum_{i\leq n}\langle f,\Pi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}}\Pi_{i}
.
∎

We will now be able to show the dissipativity of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
up to a compact perturbation.

Proposition 5.8

(Maximal Dissipativity of
\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha}
)
.

For
\alpha\in(0,1],k\geq 5
and
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
sufficiently small, there exists
(\mathbf{\Pi}_{\alpha,i})_{i\leq N}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
such that for the finite rank projection operator

(5.4)

\mathbf{\hat{P}}_{\alpha}=\sum_{i\leq N}\langle-,\mathbf{\Pi}_{\alpha,i}\rangle_{k}\mathbf{\Pi}_{\alpha,i}

the modified operator
\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha}:=\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{\hat{P}}_{\alpha}
is dissipative:

(5.5)

\forall\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}):\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle-\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}\geq(k-\frac{3}{2}-2\sqrt{1-\alpha}-\varepsilon)\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2}\geq(\frac{3}{2}-\varepsilon)\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2}\geq\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2}.

and is maximal:

\lambda-\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha}\text{ is surjective for some }\lambda>0.

Proof.

We first prove the dissipative estimate for smooth functions. Now for
\mathbf{q}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
using Lemma
5.6
, we have

\displaystyle\langle-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}=\langle-\partial^{k+1}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q})_{1},\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\langle-\partial^{k}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q})_{2},\partial^{k}q_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\langle-(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q})_{1},q_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\langle-(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q})_{2},q_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle-(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k+1}\partial^{k+1}\mathbf{q})_{1},\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\langle-(\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{\alpha,k+1}\mathbf{q})_{1},\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\langle-(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k}\partial^{k}\mathbf{q})_{2},\partial^{k}q_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\langle-(\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{\alpha,k}\mathbf{q})_{2},q_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\langle-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{L^{2}\times L^{2}}

For the terms involving
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,j}
we have the following equalities:

\displaystyle\langle-(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k+1}\partial^{k+1}\mathbf{q})_{1},\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle y\partial^{k+2}q_{1}+(k+1)\partial^{k+1}q_{1}-\partial^{k+1}q_{2},\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle y,\partial_{y}\lvert\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\rvert^{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}+(k+1)\lVert\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-\langle q_{2},q_{1}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k+1}},

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lvert\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\rvert^{2}(\pm 1)-\frac{1}{2}\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}^{2}+(k+1)\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}^{2}-\langle q_{2},q_{1}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}

\displaystyle\langle-(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k}\partial^{k}\mathbf{q})_{2},\partial^{k}q_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle-\partial_{yy}\partial^{k}q_{1}-(-1-k+\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y})\partial^{k}q_{2}+y\partial^{k+1}q_{2},\partial^{k}q_{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle-\partial^{k+2}q_{1},\partial^{k}q_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}+(1+k)\lVert\partial^{k}q_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\langle\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y},\lvert\partial^{k}q_{2}\rvert^{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\langle y,\partial_{y}\lvert\partial^{k}q_{2}\rvert^{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq-(\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\overline{\partial^{k}q_{2}})_{-1}^{1}+\frac{1}{2}\lvert\partial^{k}q_{2}\rvert^{2}(\pm 1)-\frac{1}{2}\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}+\langle q_{1},q_{2}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(1+k-\frac{2\alpha}{1-\sqrt{1-\alpha}})\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}.

Summing these up, we get

\displaystyle-\langle(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k+1}\partial^{k+1}\mathbf{q})_{1},\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle-\langle(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,k}\mathbf{q})_{2},\partial^{k}q_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq(k+\frac{1}{2})\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}^{2}+(k+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2\alpha}{1-\sqrt{1-\alpha}})\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(k+\frac{1}{2})\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}^{2}+(k-\frac{3}{2}-2\sqrt{1-\alpha})\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(k-\frac{3}{2}-2\sqrt{1-\alpha})\left(\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}^{2}+\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}\right).

For the remaining terms, we have via Young’s inequality and Lemma
5.6
,

\displaystyle\lvert\langle\mathbf{L}^{\prime}_{\alpha,k+1}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k+1}\times\dot{H}^{k}}\rvert+\lvert\langle\mathbf{L}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{L^{2}\times L^{2}}\rvert\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}+c_{\varepsilon,k,\alpha}\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k-1}}^{2}.

Finally, using Lemma
5.7
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}

\displaystyle\geq(k-\frac{3}{2}-2\sqrt{1-\alpha}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2}-c_{\varepsilon,k,\alpha}\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(k-\frac{3}{2}-2\sqrt{1-\alpha}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2})\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-c^{\prime}_{\varepsilon,k,\alpha}\left(\sum_{i\leq n_{1}}\lvert\langle q_{1},\Pi_{i}^{(1)}\rangle_{L^{2}}\rvert^{2}+\sum_{i\leq n_{2}}\lvert\langle q_{2},\Pi^{(2)}_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}}\rvert^{2}\right)

where
\Pi^{(j)}_{i}\in H^{k+2-j}
. Via Riesz theorem, the result is complete.

Now we claim that the inequality holds for all
\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
. Fix
\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}).
Recall that

\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}:=\mathbf{L}_{\square}\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}

and
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})=\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\square})\subset\mathcal{H}^{k}
where
\mathbf{L}_{\square}
is the closure of
\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\square}:\mathcal{D}(\tilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\square})\subset\mathcal{H}^{k}\to\mathcal{H}^{k}
. Thus
\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\square})
and hence
\mathbf{L}_{\square}\mathbf{q},\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
. As
(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}\subset\mathcal{H}^{k}
is dense, we can fix
\mathbf{f}_{n}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}:

\mathbf{f}_{n}\to-\mathbf{L}_{\square}\mathbf{q}.

As argued in Lemma
5.3
,
\mathbf{L}_{\square}:(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}\to(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}
is bijective; thus
\exists!\mathbf{q}_{n}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}:

\mathbf{f}_{n}=-\mathbf{L}_{\square}\mathbf{q}_{n}.

We claim that
\mathbf{q}_{n}\xrightarrow{\langle\,,\,\rangle_{k}}\mathbf{q}.
By Lemma
5.2
, we have

\lVert\mathbf{q}_{n}-\mathbf{q}_{m}\rVert_{k}^{2}\lesssim\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle-\mathbf{L}_{\square}(\mathbf{q}_{n}-\mathbf{q}_{m}),\mathbf{q}_{n}-\mathbf{q}_{m}\rangle_{k}\lesssim\lVert\mathbf{L}_{\square}\mathbf{q}_{n}-\mathbf{L}_{\square}\mathbf{q}_{m}\rVert_{k}\lVert\mathbf{q}_{n}-\mathbf{q}_{m}\rVert_{k}\lesssim\lVert\mathbf{f}_{n}-\mathbf{f}_{m}\rVert_{k}\lVert\mathbf{q}_{n}-\mathbf{q}_{m}\rVert_{k}.

As
\mathbf{f}_{n}
is convergent so it is Cauchy-convergent and thus so is
\mathbf{q}_{n}
. In particular,
\mathbf{q}_{n}\xrightarrow{\langle\,,\,\rangle_{k}}\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
. As such
[\mathbf{q}_{n},-\mathbf{L}_{\square}\mathbf{q}_{n}]\to[\mathbf{q}^{\prime},-\mathbf{L}_{\square}\mathbf{q}]
and since
\mathbf{L}_{\square}
is closed so we have
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\square})
and
\mathbf{L}_{\square}\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=\mathbf{L}_{\square}\mathbf{q}
. By Proposition
5.4
, we have
\lambda\in\rho(\mathbf{L}_{\square}),\forall\lambda:\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\lambda>-\frac{1}{2}
thus
\mathbf{L}_{\square}
is invertible implying
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=\mathbf{q}
. Finally, as
\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{\hat{P}}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{L}_{\square}+\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{\hat{P}}_{\alpha}
where
\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}
and
\mathbf{\hat{P}}_{\alpha}
are bounded operators so
\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}_{n}\to\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}
. As such, the dissipative inequality extends to all
\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
.

It remains to prove
\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha}
is maximal. Note that
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
generates a strongly continuous semigroup by Proposition
5.5
and so by bounded perturbation theorem so does
\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha}
as
\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{\hat{P}}_{\alpha}
where
\mathbf{\hat{P}}_{\alpha}
is a bounded perturbation. So by Hille-Yosida theorem, there exists a
\lambda
large enough such that
\lambda-\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha}
is invertible and hence surjective.
∎

5.4. Spectral Analysis of the generator.

Now we can give a sufficiently detailed description of the spectrum of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
.

Proposition 5.9
.

For
\alpha\in(0,1],k\geq 5
, we have

(5.6)

\sigma(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\subset\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}z\leq-1\}\cup\{0,1\}

and
\{0,1\}\subset\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
. Moreover, the geometric eigenspaces of eigenvalues
1
and
0
are spanned by the following functions respectively:

\mathbf{f}_{1,\alpha}(y):=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}\\
\frac{\alpha}{(1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y)^{2}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha}:=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Moreover for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there exists generalised eigenfunctions
\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}
for the eigenvalue
0

\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}-\sqrt{1-\alpha}\ln(1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y)+\frac{\alpha y}{2(1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y)}\\
\sqrt{1-\alpha}-\frac{(1-\alpha)y}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}+\frac{\alpha y}{2(1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y)^{2}}\end{pmatrix}

which satisfy

\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}=(1-\alpha)\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha},\quad\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}^{2}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}=0,

and for
\alpha=1,

\mathbf{g}_{0,1}
is reduced to an eigenfunction for eigenvalue
0
.

Proof.

By maximal dissipativity of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
, as shown in Proposition
5.8
, we have
\sigma(\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha})\subset\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}z\leq-1\}
for
k\geq 5
. Moreover,
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha}+\mathbf{\hat{P}}_{\alpha}
where
\mathbf{\hat{P}}_{\alpha}
is finite-rank and thus compact so the essential spectrum of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
remains unperturbed. Finally, the results in section 3 relating to the mode-stability of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
shows that all the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
must belong to the set
\{0,1\}
.

Moreover, a direct computation shows that
\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha}
and
\mathbf{f}_{1,\alpha}
are the eigenfunctions of
0
and
1
respectively, and
\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}
is a generalised eigenfunction for
0
. Moreover, Frobenius analysis shows that the local smooth solutions around
1
are always one-dimensional and thus the eigenspaces are one-dimensional. ∎

Now we define the usual Riesz projections associated to the eigenvalues
0
and
1
of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
. We set

\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma_{0}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,\infty,\lambda}},\quad\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma_{1}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha,\infty,\lambda}}

where we define the curves
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{C}
by

\gamma_{0}(s):=\frac{1}{4}e^{2\pi is},\quad\gamma_{1}(s):=1+\frac{1}{4}e^{2\pi is}.

We will now show that the algebraic multiplicity of
1
equals
0
whereas the algebraic multiplicity of
0
is strictly larger than its geometric multiplicity. As such, the generalised
0
-mode would induce a polynomial growth in time.

Lemma 5.10
.

The projections
\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
and
\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}
have rank
2
and
1
respectively.

Proof.

Note that it suffices to fix
\alpha:=1
. Indeed, these projections are finite-rank since if the rank was infinite then the respective eigenvalues would have infinite algebraic multiplicity and thus belong to the essential spectrum of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
. But
\sigma_{e}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})=\sigma_{e}(\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha})\subset\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}z\leq-1\right\}
, by maximal dissipativity of
1+\mathbf{\hat{L}}_{\alpha}
. Now since
\alpha\mapsto\mathbf{P}_{i,\alpha}
is a continuous map so by Lemma 4.10 in Kato 1.4.6, the rank of
\mathbf{P}_{i,\alpha}
remains constant as
\alpha
is varied.

Now for
\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
, note that Theorem 6.17
implies that
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}}
is a bounded operator and per the description of the spectrum of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
given in Proposition
5.9
, we have

\sigma(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}})=\{z:\lvert z\rvert<\tfrac{1}{4}\text{ and }z\in\sigma(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\}=\{0\}.

As
\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
is a finite-rank operator with spectrum equalling
0
so
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}}
is a nilpotent operator and thus there exists a minimal
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}})^{n}=0
. As the algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalue
0
equals
n
, it suffices to calculate this
n
to determine the rank of
\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
.

Recall that

\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}:\lim_{n\to\infty}\lVert(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})^{n}\mathbf{x}\rVert_{k}^{\frac{1}{n}}=0\right\}.

Thus the eigenspaces spanned by the generalised eigenvectors are contained in
\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
so
\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha},\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}\subset\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
and

(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}})^{2}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}=(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})^{2}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}=0.

This implies
n\geq 2
. If
n\geq 3
then there exists
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}:(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}})\mathbf{v}\in\ker(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}})^{2}\backslash\{0\}
. Since
\ker(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})^{2}
is spanned by
\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha}
and
\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}
, we can write

(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}})\mathbf{v}=c_{1}\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha}+c_{2}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}=c_{1}\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}+c_{2}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}.

As
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
we have
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{v}-c_{1}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha})=c_{2}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}
. This is only possible if
c_{2}=0
per Lemma
A.1
implying
\mathbf{v}-c_{1}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}\in\ker\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
and so
\mathbf{v}=c_{3}\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha}+c_{1}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}
contradicting
(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}})^{2}\mathbf{v}\neq 0.
A similar argument shows that
\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}
has rank
1
.
∎

5.5. The resolvent estimate

For some
0<w_{0}<1
, we define the rectangle

R_{m,n}:=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}z\in[-w_{0},m],\mathrm{Im\,}z\in[-n,n]\right\}

and

R^{\prime}_{m,n}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}z\geq-w_{0}\}\backslash R_{m,n}.

The following estimate confines the possible unstable eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
to the compact rectangle
R_{m,n}
.

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
k\geq 5,0<w_{0}<1,
and
I\subset(0,1]
be a compact region. Then there exists
m,n
such that
R_{m,n}^{\prime}\subset\rho(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
and

(5.7)

\sup_{\alpha\in I}\sup_{\lambda\in R_{m,n}^{\prime}}\,\lVert\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{k})}\leq C

where
C
is a uniform constant.

Proof.

Since
\sigma(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\subset\{\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}z\geq-1\}\cup\{0,1\}

by Proposition
5.9
we have
R_{m,n}^{\prime}\subset\{\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}z>-1\}\cap\{0,1\}^{\subset}\rho(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
. So to prove the resolvent estimate, we have to show

(5.8)

\lvert\langle(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k+1}\times\dot{H}^{k}}\rvert+\lvert\langle(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{0}\rvert\gtrsim\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2},\quad\forall\lambda\in R_{m,n}^{\prime},\ \alpha\in I,\ \mathbf{q}\in(C^{\infty}[-1,1])^{2}

since under an application of Cauchy-Schwartz, it is equivalent to the bound
\lVert(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}\gtrsim\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}
satisfied by smooth
\mathbf{q}
which are dense in
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})
.

To prove (
5.8
), we first subdivide
R_{m,n}^{\prime}
into the outer half
O_{m,n}^{\prime}
, and the middle half
M_{m,n}^{\prime}
as

O_{m,n}^{\prime}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}z\geq-w_{0},\lvert\mathrm{Im\,}z\rvert\geq n\},\quad M_{m,n}^{\prime}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}z\geq m,\lvert\mathrm{Im\,}z\rvert\leq n\}.

The first term in (
5.8
) can be expanded as

\displaystyle\langle(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k+1}\times\dot{H}^{k}}=\langle\partial^{k+1}((\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q})_{1},\partial^{k+1}\mathbf{q}_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\langle\partial^{k}((\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q})_{2},\partial^{k}\mathbf{q}_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle\partial^{k+1}(\lambda q_{1}+yq_{1}^{\prime}-q_{2}),\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\langle\partial^{k}(\lambda q_{2}-q_{1}^{\prime\prime}-\Upsilon_{\alpha}(y)q_{2}+q_{2}+yq_{2}^{\prime}),\partial^{k}q_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lambda\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}^{2}+\langle y\partial^{k+1}q_{1}^{\prime}+(k+1)\partial^{k+1}q_{1},\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle q_{2},q_{1}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\lambda\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}-\langle q_{1}^{\prime\prime},q_{2}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k}}-\langle\Upsilon_{\alpha}(y)q_{2},q_{2}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k}}+\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}+\langle y\partial^{k}q_{2}^{\prime}+k\partial^{k}q_{2},\partial^{k}q_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\lambda+k+\tfrac{1}{2})\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\lvert\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\partial^{k}q_{2}\rvert^{2})(\pm 1)+(\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\lambda+k+\tfrac{1}{2})\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\overline{\partial^{k}q_{2}}|^{+1}_{-1}-\langle\Upsilon_{\alpha}(y)q_{2},q_{2}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+i\mathrm{Im\,}\left(\lambda\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}^{2}+\lambda\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}+\langle y\partial^{k+2}q_{1},\partial^{k+1}q_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\langle y\partial^{k+1}q_{2},\partial^{k}q_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\langle q_{1},q_{2}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}\right)

where
\Upsilon_{\alpha}(y):=\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}
and we have used

\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\langle yf^{\prime},f\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle y,\tfrac{1}{2}(f^{\prime}\bar{f}+f\bar{f}^{\prime})\rangle=\langle y,\tfrac{1}{2}(\lvert f\rvert^{2})^{\prime}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}\lvert f\rvert^{2}(1)+\tfrac{1}{2}\lvert f\rvert^{2}(-1)=:\tfrac{1}{2}\lvert f\rvert^{2}(\pm 1).

For the term with
\Upsilon_{\alpha}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\Upsilon_{\alpha}q_{2},q_{2}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k}}

\displaystyle=\langle\Upsilon_{\alpha}\partial^{k}q_{2},\partial^{k}q_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}C^{k}_{j}\langle\partial^{j}\Upsilon_{\alpha}\partial^{k-j}q_{2},\partial^{k}q_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\alpha}{1-\sqrt{1-\alpha}}\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}+M_{\alpha_{0},\varepsilon^{\prime}}\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\varepsilon^{\prime}\lVert\partial^{k}q_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},\quad\forall\alpha\in B_{\varepsilon}(\alpha_{0})

where we have used Gagliardo-Nirenberg in bounded domain and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is sufficiently small. We have thus obtained

\displaystyle\lvert\langle(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{k+1}\times\dot{H}^{k}}\rvert

\displaystyle+M\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\lambda+k+1)\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}^{2}+\left(\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\lambda+k-\frac{3}{2}-2\sqrt{1-\alpha}-\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\lambda+\frac{3}{2}-\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2},

for
k\geq 5
. For
k=0
, we instead use the imaginary terms:

\displaystyle\lvert\langle yq_{1}^{\prime\prime},q_{1}^{\prime}\rangle_{L^{2}}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert q_{1}^{\prime\prime}\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert q_{1}^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k+1}}^{2}+M_{1}\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2},

\displaystyle\lvert\langle yq_{2}^{\prime},q_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert q_{2}^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}+M_{2}\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Thus we have

\displaystyle\lvert\langle(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}\times L^{2}}\rvert+\varepsilon^{\prime}\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{k+1}\times\dot{H}^{k}}^{2}\geq(\mathrm{Im\,}\lambda-M_{1}-M_{2})\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}\times L^{2}}^{2}.

Finally, it remains to bound the
L^{2}
-norm of the first term. We have

\displaystyle\langle((\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q})_{1},q_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle(\lambda q_{1}+yq_{1}^{\prime}-q_{2}),q_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\lambda\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lvert q_{1}\rvert^{2}(\pm 1)-\langle q_{2},q_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+i\left(\mathrm{Im\,}\lambda\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mathrm{Im\,}\langle yq_{1}^{\prime},q_{1}\rangle\right)

where

\langle yq_{1}^{\prime},q_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}\leq\lVert q_{1}^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}\lVert q_{1}^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+M_{3}\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

We obtain

\lvert\langle((\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q})_{1},q_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}}\rvert+\varepsilon^{\prime}\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}}^{2}\geq(\mathrm{Im\,}\lambda-M_{3})\lVert q_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Fixing
\mathrm{Im\,}\lambda\geq n:=M_{1}+M_{2}+M_{3}
and
\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\lambda>-w_{0}
, we obtain the resolvent bound for
\lambda\in O_{m,n}^{\prime}:

\lvert\langle(\lambda-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}\rvert\geq\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2}.

For
\lambda\in M_{m,n}^{\prime}
, we have, by Proposition
5.5
,

\lVert\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{k})}\lesssim e^{(\lVert\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}-\frac{1}{2})s}

where
S_{\alpha}
is the semigroup generated by
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
. This is equivalent to the following resolvent bound

\lVert\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{k})}\lesssim\frac{1}{\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\lambda-(\lVert\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}-\frac{1}{2})}.

Since

\lVert\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{k})}\leq 1+\left\lVert\frac{2\alpha}{y\sqrt{1-\alpha}+1}\right\rVert_{C^{k}}\lesssim C_{I},\quad\forall\alpha\in I

so fixing
m\geq C_{I}
completes the proof.
∎

6. Growth bound of the linearised flow

The above spectral analysis provides a sufficiently complete description of the linearised flow.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
I\subset(0,1]
be compact and
k\geq 5,0<w_{0}<1
. Then the flow
\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)
for
\alpha\in I
generated by
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
satisfies the following properties:

•

(Commutes with projections).

[\mathbf{S}_{\alpha},\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}]=[\mathbf{S}_{\alpha},\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}]=0
.

•

(Projection on unit eigenspace).

\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}=e^{s}\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}
,

•

(Projection on null eigenspace).

\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}=\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}+s\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
,

•

(Boundedness on remaining subspace).

\lVert\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{\tilde{P}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}\leq M_{I,w_{0}}e^{-w_{0}s}\lVert\mathbf{\tilde{P}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}
,

for all
s\geq 0,\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}
and where
\mathbf{\tilde{P}}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}-\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}
and
M_{I,w_{0}}
is a constant depending on
I,w_{0}
.

Moreover, we have the following description of the eigenspaces:

•

\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}=\mathbb{C}\mathbf{f}_{1,\alpha}
,

•

\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}=\mathbb{C}\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha}\oplus\mathbb{C}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}
,

where
(1,\mathbf{f}_{1,\alpha})
is the eigenpair and
(0,(\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha},\mathbf{f}_{1,\alpha}))
is the generalised eigen-cycle for
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
as shown in Proposition
5.9
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}
is strongly-continuous semigroup so it commutes with its generator and thus with the resolvent of the generator
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}}
. Since the projections can be expressed as an integral of the resolvent, so
\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}
commutes with
\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
and
\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}
. Moreover, Proposition
5.9
and Lemma
5.10
imply

\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}=\mathbb{C}\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha}\oplus\mathbb{C}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha},\quad\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}=\mathbb{C}\mathbf{f}_{1,\alpha}.

Thus we obtain

\partial_{s}\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}=\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}=\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}=\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}\implies\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}=e^{s}\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}.

Similarly, we also have

\partial_{ss}\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}=\partial_{s}\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}=\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}^{2}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}=0\implies\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}=s\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}+\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}.

Finally, for the restriction to the range of
\mathbf{\tilde{P}}_{\alpha}=1-\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}-\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}
, using the separation of spectrum theorem, we obtain

\sigma(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{\tilde{P}}_{\alpha}})=\sigma(\mathbf{L}_{\alpha})\backslash\{0,1\}\subset\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}z\leq-1\right\}.

This implies
R_{m,n}\subset\{\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}z>-1\}\subset\rho(\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{\tilde{P}}_{\alpha}}})
and so
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{\tilde{P}}_{\alpha}}}
is analytic in the compact set
R_{m,n}
so
\raisebox{2.15277pt}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\in R_{m,n}}\;$}}\,\lVert\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{\tilde{P}}_{\alpha}}}\rVert\lesssim_{I}1
and from Proposition
5.11
, we have

\sup_{\lambda\in R_{m,n}^{\prime}}\,\lVert\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{\tilde{P}}_{\alpha}}}\rVert\lesssim_{I}1.

Since
R_{m,n}\cup R_{m,n}^{\prime}=\{\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}z\geq-w_{0}\}
so

\sup_{\mathbb{R}{\rm\,}\lambda\geq-w_{0}}\,\lVert\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}|_{\mathrm{ran\,}\mathbf{\tilde{P}}_{\alpha}}}\rVert\lesssim_{I}1,\quad\forall\alpha\in I.

Then the uniform bound follows from the adaption of the Gearhart-Pruss-Grenier theorem.
∎

7. Nonlinear Stability

Now we derive the nonlinear stability. The full nonlinear equation can be written as

(7.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{s}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}),\\
\mathbf{q}(0,\cdot)=\mathbf{q}_{0}\end{cases}

where

(7.2)

\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}:=\begin{pmatrix}-y\partial_{y}&1\\
\partial_{yy}&\frac{2\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}y}-1-y\partial_{y}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}):=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
q_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

By Duhamel’s principle, we can write down the solution for (
7.1
)

(7.3)

\mathbf{q}(s,\cdot)=\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\mathbf{q}(0,\cdot)+\int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s-\tau)\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}(\tau,\cdot))\mathrm{\,d}\tau

where
\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}
is the semigroup generated by
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}
per Proposition
5.5
.

7.1. Stabilised nonlinear evolution

Definition
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N},0<w_{0}<1
. Define the Banach space
(\mathcal{X}^{k}(B),\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\mathcal{X}^{k}(B)})
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{k}(B)

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{q}\in C(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{H}^{k}(B)):\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}^{k}(B)}<\infty\},\quad\text{where}

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}^{k}(B)}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\,e^{w_{0}s}\lVert\mathbf{q}(s,\cdot)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}(B)}.

We also denote the
\varepsilon
-sized balls around 0 in a Banach space
X
as

\mathrm{B}^{X}_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{q\in X:\lVert q\rVert_{X}\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

We first consider a modified problem following the Lyapunov-Perron’s method from the dynamical systems theory to avoid encountering the unstable modes. To that end, set
\mathbf{P}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}+\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}
and denote the correction term

(7.4)

\mathbf{C}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{P}_{\alpha}\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}(\tau,\cdot))+\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}(-\tau)\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}(\tau,\cdot))+\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\tau}\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}(\tau,\cdot))\mathrm{\,d}\tau.

We now derive some basic estimates for the nonlinear term

Lemma 7.1

(Estimates for Nonlinear Term)
.

For all
k\geq 2

\lVert\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q})\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}\lesssim\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2}

\lVert\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q})-\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}\lesssim(\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}+\lVert\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}})\lVert\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}.

Proof.

Using the Sobolev Embedding, we have

\lVert\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q})\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}=\lVert q_{2}^{2}\rVert_{H^{k}}\leq\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{H^{k}}\lesssim\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{H^{k}}^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2}.

Similarly as above

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q})-\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}

\displaystyle=\lVert q_{2}^{2}-{q_{2}^{\prime}}^{2}\rVert_{H^{k}}\lesssim\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\,\lVert(tq_{2}+(1-t)q_{2}^{\prime})(q_{2}-q_{2}^{\prime})\rVert_{{H}^{k}}

\displaystyle\lesssim(\lVert q_{2}\rVert_{H^{k}}+\lVert q_{2}^{\prime}\rVert_{H^{k}})\lVert q_{2}-q_{2}^{\prime}\rVert_{H^{k}}\lesssim(\lVert\mathbf{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}+\lVert\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}})\lVert\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}.\qed

We can now produce a solution to stabilized version of (
7.1
) using a fixed-point argument.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1],k\geq 5,0<w_{0}<1
. Then there exists constants
\varepsilon>\varepsilon^{\prime}>0,
such that
\forall\alpha\in B_{\varepsilon}(\alpha_{0})\cap(0,1]
and
\forall\mathbf{f}\in\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{H}^{k}(B)}
there exists a unique
\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}\in\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{X}^{k}(B)}_{\varepsilon}
satisfying

(7.5)

\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}(s)=\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)(\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{C}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}))+\int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s-\tau)\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}(\tau))\mathrm{\,d}\tau.

Moreover, the solution-to-data map

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{H}^{k}(B)}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{X}^{k}(B)}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathbf{q}_{\alpha}

is Lipschitz-continuous.

Proof.

For
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1]
, let
I:=[\alpha_{0}-\varepsilon,1]
, for some
\varepsilon
to be chosen later, which is a compact subset of
(0,1]
. Then as
k\geq 5
and
0<w_{0}<1
so
\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}
is well-defined and satisfies Proposition
6.1
. Denote the solution operator for the corrected problem by

\mathbf{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{q})(s):=\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)(\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{C}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{q}))+\int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s-\tau)\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}(\tau))\mathrm{\,d}\tau.

We claim that there exists constants
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon
small enough such that

\mathbf{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},):\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{X}^{k}}_{\varepsilon}\longrightarrow\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{X}^{k}}_{\varepsilon}

is a contraction for any fixed
\mathrm{f}\in\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{H}^{k}}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
. Then uniqueness and existence for (
7.5
) follows from the Banach fixed-point theorem.

To that end, fix
\mathbf{f}\in\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{H}^{k}}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
and
\mathbf{q}\in\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{X}_{k}}_{\varepsilon}
. Expanding out the definition for
\mathbf{C}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{q})
, we can write

(7.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{q})(s)&=\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s)\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{f}+\int_{0}^{s}\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}(s-\tau)\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}(\tau))\mathrm{\,d}\tau-\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\int_{s}^{\infty}\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}(\tau))\mathrm{\,d}\tau\\
&\qquad-\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}\int_{s}^{\infty}(s-\tau)\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}(\tau))\mathrm{\,d}\tau-\mathbf{P}_{1,\alpha}\int_{s}^{\infty}e^{s-\tau}\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{q}(\tau))\mathrm{\,d}\tau\end{split}

where
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}=1-\mathbf{P}_{\alpha}
and we have used Proposition
6.1
. Using Minkowski inequality, Lemma
7.1
and
\mathbf{L}_{\alpha}\mathbf{P}_{0,\alpha}
is a linear finite operator and thus bounded, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in I}\,\lVert\mathbf{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{q})\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-w_{0}s}\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}\mathbf{f}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}+\int_{0}^{s}e^{-w_{0}(s-\tau)}\lVert\mathbf{q}(\tau)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2}\mathrm{\,d}\tau+\int_{s}^{\infty}\lVert\mathbf{q}(\tau)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2}\mathrm{\,d}\tau

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{s}^{\infty}(\tau-s)\lVert\mathbf{q}(\tau)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2}\mathrm{\,d}\tau+\int_{s}^{\infty}e^{s-\tau}\lVert\mathbf{q}(\tau)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{2}\mathrm{\,d}\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-w_{0}s}\varepsilon^{\prime}+\varepsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{s}e^{w_{0}(\tau-s)}e^{-2w_{0}\tau}+\varepsilon^{2}\int_{s}^{\infty}e^{-2w_{0}\tau}\mathrm{\,d}\tau

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\varepsilon^{2}\int_{s}^{\infty}(\tau-s)e^{-2w_{0}\tau}\mathrm{\,d}\tau+\varepsilon^{2}\int_{s}^{\infty}e^{s-\tau}e^{-2w_{0}\tau}\mathrm{\,d}\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{\prime}e^{-w_{0}s}+\varepsilon^{2}\frac{e^{-w_{0}s}}{w_{0}}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}e^{-w_{0}s}+\frac{e^{-w_{0}s}}{4w_{0}}+\frac{e^{-w_{0}s}}{w_{0}^{-1}+2}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{w_{0}}e^{-w_{0}s}(\varepsilon^{\prime}+\varepsilon^{2})\leq Ce^{-w_{0}s}(\varepsilon^{\prime}+\varepsilon^{2}).

Fixing
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon^{2}\leq C^{-2}
gives us
\sup_{\alpha\in I}\lVert\mathbf{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{q})\rVert_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}\leq\varepsilon
. Thus
\mathbf{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},-):\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{X}^{k}}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{X}^{k}}_{\varepsilon}
is well-defined for any
\mathbf{f}\in\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{H}^{k}}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
.

We now show that
\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{f},\cdot)
is a contraction on the same space. Using Proposition
6.1
and Lemma
7.1
, we have for
f\in\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{H}^{k}}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
and
\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{X}^{k}}_{\varepsilon}:

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle-\mathbf{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}\lesssim\int_{0}^{s}e^{-w_{0}(s-\tau)}\lVert(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime})(\tau)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}\lVert(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})(\tau)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{\infty}\lVert(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime})(\tau)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}\lVert(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})(\tau)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}+\int_{s}^{\infty}(\tau-s)\lVert(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime})(\tau)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}\lVert(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})(\tau)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{\infty}e^{s-\tau}\lVert(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime})(\tau)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}\lVert(\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime})(\tau)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon\lVert\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}\left(\int_{0}^{s}e^{-w_{0}(s-\tau)}e^{-2w_{0}\tau}+\int_{s}^{\infty}e^{-2w_{0}\tau}+(\tau-s)e^{-2w_{0}\tau}+e^{s-\tau}e^{-2w_{0}\tau}\mathrm{\,d}\tau\right)

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\varepsilon e^{-w_{0}s}\lVert\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}.

Fixing
\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{2}\min\{C^{-1},{C^{\prime}}^{-1}\}
we obtain

\lVert\mathbf{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{q})-\mathbf{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\rVert_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}^{k}},\quad\forall\mathbf{f}\in\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{H}^{k}}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}},\forall\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{X}^{k}}_{\varepsilon},\forall\alpha\in I.

Finally, we will show the continuous dependence on the initial data. For
\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}\in\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{H}^{k}}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
and
\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{B}^{\mathcal{X}^{k}}_{\varepsilon}
, the fixed point solution obtained via Banach fixed-point satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}(s)-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{q})(s)-\mathbf{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f}^{\prime},\mathbf{q}^{\prime})(s)=\mathbf{S}_{\alpha}\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{f}^{\prime})+\mathbf{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f}^{\prime},\mathbf{q})-\mathbf{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f}^{\prime},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}).

Using Proposition
6.1
and the contraction property of
\mathbf{K}_{\alpha}
, we obtain

\lVert\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}\leq C^{\prime\prime}\lVert\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{f}^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}+\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}.

In particular, we have

\lVert\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}^{k}}\lesssim\lVert\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{f}^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}

which implies the Lipschitz dependence of
\mathbf{q}
on the initial data
\mathbf{f}
.
∎

7.2. Stable flow near the blow-up solution

We will now carefully construct the initial data
\mathbf{f}
for our Cauchy problem so as to ensure that the correction term
\mathbf{C}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{q})
disappears. To that end, we define the following decomposition:

Definition

(Decomposition Map)
.

Let
(\alpha_{0},\alpha)\in(0,1]^{2},(T_{0},T)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
satisfying
\sqrt{1-\alpha_{0}}T<T_{0}
,
\kappa_{0},\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and
k\geq 5
. Define the decomposition operator

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{\alpha,\kappa,T}\colon\mathcal{H}^{k}(-1,1)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{k}(-1,1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\mathbf{f}^{T}+\mathbf{f}^{T}_{0}-\mathbf{f}_{\alpha,\kappa}.

where if we write
\mathbf{f}(y)=(f_{1}(y),f_{2}(y))
then

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{T}(y):=\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}(Ty)\\
Tf_{2}(Ty)\end{pmatrix},\,\mathbf{f}^{T}_{0}(y):=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{U}_{\alpha_{0},\infty}(\frac{T}{T_{0}}y)+\kappa_{0}\\
\frac{T}{T_{0}}\alpha_{0}+(\frac{T}{T_{0}})^{2}y\partial_{y}\tilde{U}_{\alpha_{0},\infty}(\frac{T}{T_{0}}y)\end{pmatrix},\,\mathbf{f}_{\alpha,\kappa}:=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\infty}(y)+\kappa\\
\alpha+y\partial_{y}\tilde{U}_{\alpha,\infty}(y)\end{pmatrix}

Lemma 7.3

(Taylor Expansion)
.

Let
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1],T,T_{0}>0
and
\kappa_{0},\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and
k\geq 5
. Then

\mathbf{U}_{\alpha,\kappa,T}(\mathbf{f})=\mathbf{f}^{T}+\left[(\kappa_{0}-\kappa)-\alpha\left(\frac{T}{T_{0}}-1\right)\right]\mathbf{f}_{0,\alpha}+\left(\frac{T}{T_{0}}-1\right)\mathbf{f}_{1,\alpha}+\frac{\alpha_{0}-\alpha}{\sqrt{1-\alpha}}\mathbf{g}_{0,\alpha}+\mathbf{r}\left(\alpha,\frac{T}{T_{0}}\right)

for any fixed
\mathbf{f}\in\mathcal{H}^{k}(-1,1)

1\end{pmatrix}.

Plugging the first equation into the second equation we obtain

v_{1}^{\prime\prime}+yv_{1}^{\prime}-\frac{y}{2}-y(v_{1}^{\prime}+yv_{1}^{\prime\prime})-\frac{1}{2}=-\frac{y}{2}\implies v_{1}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{2(1-y^{2})}

whose general solution is given by

v_{1}=c_{1}+c_{2}y+\frac{1}{4}(1+y)\log(1+y)+\frac{1}{4}(1-y)\log(1-y).

Then
v_{1}^{\prime}=c_{2}+\frac{1}{4}\ln\frac{1+y}{1-y}
which fails to be finite at
\pm 1
. Thus
\mathbf{v}\not\in\mathcal{H}^{4}
.
∎
