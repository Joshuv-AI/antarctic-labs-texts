Mechanism of interaction between ANXA6 and cGAS to regulate STING signaling pathway and promote ferroptosis in cervical cancer
Authors: Si Ouyang, Kaixin Xiong, Peijie Chen, Xiufeng Liu, Anzhou Li, Jiang Li, Yao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Annexin A6 (ANXA6), a calcium-dependent phospholipid protein, is closely related to the prognosis of a variety of tumors. However, the function and its regulatory mechanism in tumors is largely unknown. Here, we found that tumor microenvironment ANXA6 expression was associated with unfavorable prognosis of patients with cervical cancer. Downregulation of ANXA6 in cervical cancer cells suppressed tumor cell migration, colony formation and promoted tumor cell death. Furthermore, we identified ANXA6 co-localized with cGAS and function as an inhibitor of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway by promoting the ubiquitination of cGAS. Inhibiting ANXA6 expression resulted in increase of cGAS and activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, leading to elevated expression of phosphorylated TBK1, IRF3, and type I IFN stimulated gene MX1, ISG15, ISG56. In addition, downregulation of ANXA6 in cervical cancer cells impedes the K63-linked ubiquitination process of cGAS protein, resulting in an upregulation of cGAS expression. This subsequently facilitates the activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, leading to increased production of reactive oxygen species and lipid peroxides in cervical cancer cells. Ultimately, these events induce ferroptosis in tumor cells. Therefore, our findings revealed that inhibiting ANXA6 can promote tumor cell ferroptosis by activating the cGAS-STING pathway, thereby provide potential strategies that enhancing the efficacy of anti-tumor immunotherapy in cervical cancer.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is a malignancy that poses a significant threat to women’s health, ranking fourth in both incidence and mortality worldwide [1]. In 2020, there were approximately 604,000 new cases and 342,000 deaths globally, with significantly higher incidence and mortality rates in developing countries compared with developed countries, ranking second among female malignant tumors [2]. For early-stage cervical cancer patients, comprehensive treatment methods such as surgery and radiotherapy can significantly improve the survival rate. However, for recurrent, metastatic and advanced cervical cancer patients, the overall prognosis is poor due to limited treatment options [3]. In recent years, with the developed research on immunotherapy for cervical cancer, immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown good efficacy in patients with advanced cervical cancer, receiving consistent recommendations from domestic and international guidelines. However, the effective rate of PD-1 antibody monotherapy for treating metastatic cervical cancer is only 10%−25% [4–6]. Therefore, how to further improve the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors has become a focus of current clinical research.

Membrane-associated protein A6 (ANXA6), also known as Annexin A6, is a phospholipid protein that relies on calcium for its function. The levels of ANXA6 expression have been closely linked to the prognosis of different types of tumors. It influences tumor growth by controlling the progression of the cell cycle, the migration of tumors, and crucial signaling pathways like RAS/MAPK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR [7, 8]. Earlier investigations have revealed that during the transition from cervical intraepithelial neoplasia to microinvasive carcinoma, the cytoplasmic expression of ANXA6 gradually increased, with the highest level observed in invasive cervical squamous cell carcinoma [9, 10]. ANXA6 can selectively accumulate in oncogenic exosomes and be secreted in the form of extracellular vesicles (EVs), further inducing the occurrence of multidrug resistance in various tumors as well as regulating the tumor microenvironment [11–13]. In addition, ANXA6 can regulate the signal transduction and proliferation of T cells. Studies have found that knocking down ANXA6 can affect the lipid composition of T cells, and then interfere with the proliferation of CD4+ T cells through IL-2 reduction by disturbing mitotic spindle receptor signals [14]. Given the important role that ANXA6 plays in cancer formation, progression, treatment and immune regulation, it has the potential to become a target for potential therapeutic interventions.

The interferon signaling pathway is a major component of innate immunity, and studies have found that activation of the interferon signaling pathway can effectively enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors [15, 16]. cGAS-STING is an important component of the type I interferon signaling pathway, which can recognize DNA molecules derived from tumors, induce the expression of type I interferons, activate the host immune system, and monitor and eliminate tumor cells [17]. Recent research has found that the cGAS-STING pathway can inhibit the transport of iron and reduce the efficiency of iron utilization by regulating transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1), leading to iron accumulation and the generation of reactive oxygen species, ultimately inducing ferroptosis [18, 19]. Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is a major cause of cervical cancer [20], and studies have found that E7 from HPV16 can inhibit the cGAS-STING signaling pathway in cervical cancer cells [21]. Therefore, exploring therapeutic targets that can specifically regulate the cGAS-STING signaling pathway in cervical cancer is of great clinical significance.

Our initial research has discovered that ANXA6 and cGAS co-localize in cervical cancer cells. Thus, the main objective of this project is to conduct a comprehensive investigation into the impact of ANXA6 on the prognosis and its potential role in cervical cancer patients. The findings of this study will establish a foundation for the clinical development of immunotherapy targets for cervical cancer.

Materials & methods

Patients’ information and clinical characteristics

A total of 125 patients diagnosed with cervical cancer were treated at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center in Guangzhou, China and underwent medical follow-up until 2019. Partial tumor specimens were collected from these patients between 2008 and 2010 for this retrospective study. It is important to note that none of the patients had received any prior anti-tumor therapy before tissue collection, ensuring that all 125 cases included in this study met the classification criteria set by the World Health Organization and the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics as primary cervical cancer, the detailed clinical information was shown in Table 1. The tumor specimens used in this study along with relevant clinical data were provided by the Department of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center after obtaining written informed consent from all participating patients following approval from the Research Ethics Committee.

Cell culture and transfection

The cervical cancer cell lines Siha and Hela, as well as the human embryonic kidney 293 T (HEK293T) cells were purchased from American Type Cell Culture (Manassas, VA, USA). HEK293T, Siha, and Hela cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with L-glutamine, 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and penicillin-streptomycin solution (100 U/ml/100µg/ml). The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen; 11668-019) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The Siha-sh-ANXA6 stable cell line was constructed and provided by UBIGENE Company. Additionally, the company supplied three sh-ANXA6 viral solutions for transfecting other cell lines. Following verification of transfection efficiency, we chose the viral solution with the highest efficiency for all subsequent experiments. Seed Hela cells in optimal condition into a six-well plate at a density of 50–60%. Add 1 ml of the sh-ANXA6-RNA virus solution, which carries GFP fluorescence and is provided by the company, to each well. Subsequently, add 1 ml of fresh culture medium and 2 µL of Lipofectamine 3000 to facilitate cell transfection. Replace the culture medium with fresh medium 8–12 h post-transfection. At 48 h post-transfection, evaluate transfection efficiency using a fluorescence microscope and assess sh-ANXA6 knockdown efficiency via Western blot.

Cervical cancer xenograft model in nude mice

Balb/c nude mice (4-weeks-old, female) were obtained from Guangzhou Yaokang Co., Ltd. (animal production license number: SCXK Guangdong 2020-0054), and were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. Nude mice were randomly divided into two groups: the control group and the Siha-sh-ANXA6 group, each consisting of 6 mice. The cell density of both groups was adjusted to 1 × 107/mL using PBS, and a subcutaneous injection of 0.2 mL cell suspension was administered into the right axilla of each nude mouse. Tumor volumes were measured on day 6, 10, 14, 18 and 22 post injection. On day 22, euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation for the nude mice, followed by excision, photography, and weighing of the tumors.

Immunohistochemical

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were serially cut into 4 μm thick slices and subjected to immunohistochemical analysis using kits from Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the tissue sections were immersed in EDTA (pH 8.0) or ×citrate buffer (pH 6.0), then deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating in a pressure cooker at 95 °C for 22 min. To block nonspecific binding sites, goat serum was applied and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The primary antibody ANXA6 was blocked overnight, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody (Zhongshan Jinqiao Biological) for 30 min at room temperature. Visualization was achieved using DAB staining. Finally, the pathological sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and sealed with neutral glue for observation under a light microscope.

RNA isolation and real time-PCR

The total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (TaKaRa, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, cDNA synthesis was performed from 1 µg of total RNA using a reverse transcription kit (TaKaRa, Japan). To ensure sample normalization, GAPDH was utilized as an internal control. Gene expression levels were normalized to the transcript level of GAPDH based on ΔΔCt values and compared with controls. The sequence of primers used is shown in Table 2. Amplification reactions were carried out following the instructions for the 2 × SYBR Green Master Mix (BIO-RAD) utilizing a BIO-RAD C1000™ Thermal Cycler. PCR conditions included an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 30 s followed by denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s, and a total of 40 cycles. Relative expression levels were determined using the RT-PCR-based method known as the 2 − ΔΔCt method.

Western blot

Cells were lysed 48 h post-transfection in a lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 2 mM EDTA, and protease inhibitors with the addition of 0.1% SDS. After centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for ten minutes, protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. Lysates were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes prior to immunoblotting with specific antibodies. The lysates underwent overnight immunoprecipitation at four degrees Celsius using one microgram of desired antibodies and twenty-five microliters of protein A-Sepharose (88404, Invitrogen). Subsequently, precipitates were washed three times with cell lysis buffer before immune complexes were eluted with sample buffer containing 1% SDS for five minutes at ninety-five degrees Celsius and analyzed by SDS-PAGE. GAPDH was used as a loading control while an ECL Western blot detection kit from Thermo Fisher Scientific was employed for signal detection.

shRNA-Mediated ANXA6 knockdown

Three ANXA6-targeting shRNA sequences were designed using the BLOCK-iT™ RNAi Designer, avoiding off-target effects via BLAST alignment. Oligonucleotides were annealed and cloned into the pLKO. Puro vector using AgeI and EcoRI restriction sites. Plasmids were transformed into Stbl3 competent cells and validated by Sanger sequencing. 293 T cells were co-transfected with 1.5 µg shRNA plasmid, 1.0 µg psPAX2, and 0.5 µg pMD2.G using Lipofectamine 3000. Viral supernatants were harvested at 48 h, filtered (0.45 μm). Cells were seeded (2 × 10⁵cells/well) and transduced with lentivirus in medium containing 8 µg/mL Polybrene. Puromycin (1–2 µg/mL) was added 48 h post-transduction for 5–7 days. Knockdown efficiency was measured by qPCR and Werstern-blot.

si-RNA

The cells for our experiments were seeded in the experimental cells at a density of 3 to 5 × 105/well and incubated overnight to achieve a confluence rate of 50 to 70%. Transfection mixtures were prepared in strict accordance with the reagent manufacturer’s instructions. siRNA purchased from Guangzhou Ruibo Technology Co., Ltd. was carefully mixed with Lipofectamine 3000 in Opti-MEM low serum medium. After a 15–20 min incubation period, the transfection mixture was added drop by drop to the cell culture Wells and the plates were gently agitated. After 4–6 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete growth medium.Total RNA was extracted from the transfected and control cells 48 to 72 h after transfection to verify the knockdown of ANXA6. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was then performed using specific gene-specific primers and housekeeping genes as an internal control for relative quantification of mRNA levels. Western blot was used to detect and analyze the protein expression levels of specific genes. Protein extraction was performed by lysis of cells followed by protein quantification. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and probed by anti-specific gene antibody.

Colony formation assay and wound healing assay

Colony formation was measured by seeding stable cell lines into 6-well plates with complete medium (500 cells per well). After 12 days, visible colonies were stained with 0.1% crystal violet and numbers calculated. The well-cultured Siha and Hela cells with down-regulation of ANXA6 expression and the two groups of cells and the control group were spread in 6-well plates at a 5 × 105/well 2 ml system, respectively, shaking, standing for 10 min, and transferred to the incubator for overnight culture. On the second day, 10µL of crosshair was used to evenly scribe the crosshair vertically, and the samples were washed 3 times with PBS. That is, the 0 h image was taken and the width of the scratch was recorded in light field under a fluorescence microscope. The cells were placed in the incubator for continuous culture for 24, 48, and 72 h, and the distance of scratch reduction was observed under the microscope again. Image J was used to compare the area recovery rate (The ratio of the area covered by cell migration after the wound has healed to the initial wound area) of the corresponding groups of scratch and calculate the statistics.

Flow cytometry

The Siha and Hela cells were distinguished through FACS staining, followed by two PBS washes. Subsequently, the cells were labeled with the fixable reactive dye PI (eBioscience), data was collected using a Beckman Coulter instrument via flow cytometry, and ultimately analyzed utilizing FlowJo software.

Immunofluorescence

The Opal plorarisTM 7 color manual IHC kit (Akoya Biosciences) was utilized for immunofluorescence staining of cervical cancer cells, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Primary antibodies against human ANXA6and cGAS were employed, in conjunction with DAPI for nuclear staining and section mounting. Images were captured using an inverted confocal microscope (Nikon).

Detection of ROS and LPO

The intracellular total ROS level was quantified using a standard fluorescence probe, 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA). Specifically, Siha and Hela cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well. After incubation for 24 h, the cells were treated with Erastin (10 µM) for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with DMEM containing DCFH-DA (20 µM) at 37 °C for a duration of 30 min. Following the incubation period, the cells were washed again and analyzed by flow cytometry within two hours of staining. The detection of intracellular lipid peroxide levels relied on the utilization of a selective probe called Liperfluo, which was applied at a concentration of 10 µM (Ex = 532 nm, Em = 535–650 nm).For flow cytometry, excitation was performed at 488 nm and 565 nm, and the signal corresponding to 505–550 nm was measured in the FL1 channel, and the signal above 580 nm could be measured in the FL2 channel.

The maximum emission fluorescence of BODIPY 581/591 C11 was redshifted from 590 nm to 510 nm in the REDOX reaction with reactive oxygen species (ROS), which was proportional to the production of lipid ROS. To circumvent potential interference from the intrinsic fluorescence of the sh-RNA vector in flow cytometry experiments, we opted to quantify non-oxidized peroxides as an indirect measure of oxidized peroxide levels. This approach is based on the well-established inverse relationship between non-oxidized and oxidized peroxides, where a decrease in non-oxidized peroxides corresponds to an increase in oxidized peroxides due to redox equilibrium dynamics (cite relevant literature). By focusing on non-oxidized peroxides, we minimized technical artifacts and ensured more reliable detection of oxidative stress levels in our experimental system.

Ubiquitination assays

Transfect Ha-Ub and specific constructs into 293 T cells, followed by a 36-hour incubation period. Subsequently, expose the cells to a concentration of BafA1 at 10 µM for a duration of four hours. Following this treatment, disrupt cell membranes via sonication in buffer A (6 M guanidine-HCl, 0.1 M Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4, pH adjusted to 8.0 with imidazole). Proceed to incubate lysates overnight at a temperature of −20 °C utilizing Thermo magnetic beads for protein isolation purposes. Perform two rounds of washing on Ha pull-down products using Lysis Buffer before resolving them through SDS-PAGE for subsequent immunoblot analysis.

Co-immunoprecipitation

293 T cells were co-transfected with plasmids encoding HA-tagged ubiquitin (HA-Ub) and FLAG-tagged cGAS (FLAG-cGAS).Cells were treated with 10 µM MG132 (proteasome inhibitor) for 6 h prior to lysis to stabilize ubiquitinated proteins.Lysates were immunoprecipitated using anti-FLAG M2 magnetic beads. Ubiquitinated cGAS was detected via Western blot using anti-HA antibody.

Lysosomal degradation validation

Cells were treated with 100 nM Bafilomycin A1 (Bafa-1) for 12 h to inhibit lysosomal acidification. cGAS protein levels were analyzed by Western blot.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

For adherent Siha-Vector and Siha-sh-ANXA6 cells in the logarithmic growth phase, discard the DMEM complete medium and perform 2–3 washes with PBS to cleanse the cells. Employ trypsin for cell digestion, followed by termination using DMEM complete medium. Transfer the cells into 15 ml centrifuge tubes and centrifuge to remove the supernatant. Resuspend the cells in a PBS solution, conduct 1–3 additional washes with PBS, and eliminate the supernatant through centrifugation. Add 1 ml of TRIZOL reagent for every 107 cells, promptly freeze them in liquid nitrogen, and store at −80℃. Store both sets of samples on dry ice for transportation to Novolio Biotech for transcriptome sequencing utilizing Illumina sequencing platform.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 software (GraphPad Software, USA). All data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) of triplicate experiments, and the differences between the two groups were compared using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

High expression of ANXA6 in cervical cancer tissue is associated with unfavorable prognosis

The expression of ANXA6 in cervical cancer samples from 125 patients was detected by immunohistochemistry (Fig. 1A). The clinical characteristics of the 125 patients with cervical cancer are shown in Table 1. There were 105 cases (84%) in the low expression group and 20 cases (16%) in the high expression group. By drawing the Kaplan-Meier survival curve, the difference in survival rate between the two groups was statistically significant, and patients with high ANXA6 expression had a shorter overall survival (OS) time (P = 0.022, Fig. 1B). To investigate the specific functions of ANXA6, we constructed Siha-sh-ANXA6 cells with stable knockdown of ANXA6 by lentiviral transfection, and Vector were constructed with a non-targeting construct (Vector) as a control. In addition, ANXA6 was knocked down by transient transfection of the Hela cells with Hela-Vector as the control. The efficiency of viral stable transfection was validated through Western blot and real-time PCR analyses, and the sequence demonstrating the highest knockdown efficiency (sh1) was ultimately selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 1C-D). In our study utilizing animal experimentation, we observed that the downregulation of ANXA6 exhibited a suppressive impact on the rate of tumor growth. The tumor volume was assessed at regular intervals of 4 days subsequent to tumor formation, revealing a notably diminished tumor volume in the ANXA6 knockdown group compared to the negative control group. The tumor growth rate of the Siha-sh-ANXA6 group exhibited a significantly decelerated progression compared to that in the negative control group. Suppression of ANXA6 expression led to reduced tumorigenicity in nude mice. The average tumor weight of the Siha-sh-ANXA6 group was significantly lower than that of the Siha-Vector group (P < 0.001, Fig. 1E-G).

Downregulation of ANXA6 expression results in a decreased malignancy of cervical cancer cells in vitro

Knockdown of ANXA6 led to a significant inhibition in colony formation in Siha and Hela cervical cancer cells, with both the number of colonies formed and cell clones being significantly reduced compared to the negative control group. (P<0.05,Fig. 2A-B).The migration ability of Siha and Hela cervical cancer cells was also observed to be suppressed upon down-regulation of ANXA6. Cell scratch assay demonstrated a significant decrease in the migration ability of ANXA6 knockdown cells at 48 h and 72 h compared to the control group (P < 0.05, Fig. 2C-D). In addition, a flow cytometric measurement of cell death was conducted using PI staining. We collected cervical cancer cell lines, Siha and Hela, with varying levels of ANXA6 expression to evaluate the extent of cellular death. The results indicated that down-regulation of ANXA6 promotes tumor cell death, supporting its potential role as an oncogene (Fig. 2E-F). The result suggested that the depletion of ANXA6 impedes in vitro formation, migration, and viability of tumor cell colonies.

Knockdown of ANXA6 upregulates cGAS expression and actives cGAS-STING signaling pathway

To explore the role of ANXA6 in cervical cancer, we performed RNA-Seq on Siha-sh-ANXA6 cells and the control group Siha-Vector cells. The sequencing results showed significant differences in gene expression between the two groups, In particular, the expression of type I interferon pathway genes was significantly upregulated (Fig. 3A). Notably, no significant variation was detected in the expression of cGAS, corroborating the findings presented subsequently in our study. The expression of type I interferon key genes in Siha-sh-ANXA6 cells and Siha-Vector cells was further validated using real-time PCR. Our results revealed that there is no significant difference in cGAS expression at mRNA levels (Fig. 3B-C). Down-regulation of ANXA6 led to increased expressions of cGAS and STING in protein-level, crucial molecules for type I interferon signaling, as well as elevated phosphorylation levels of TBK1 and IRF3 (Fig. 3E). To further elucidate the impact of ANXA6 on the cGAS-STING pathway via cGAS, Siha cells were transfected with siRNA targeting the cGAS gene. Remarkably, simultaneous knockdown of cGAS reversed the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway induced by ANXA6 depletion (Fig. 3F). Therefore, it can be concluded that ANXA6 facilitates the activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway through its interaction with cGAS. The experimental findings suggest that ANXA6 can facilitate the activation of the type I cGAS-STING signaling pathway through cGAS. We also employed flow cytometry to examine the alterations in cell death after the simultaneous knockdown of ANXA6 and cGAS. The experimental results revealed that the increase in cell death induced by the Knockdown of ANXA6 was reversed when cGAS was simultaneously knocked down (Figs. 3G-H), further indicating that ANXA6 affects the process of cell death through cGAS.

ANXA6 interacts with cGAS and facilitates the ubiquitination of cGAS via the lysosomal pathway

To determine whether ANXA6 promotes cGAS ubiquitination via lysine 63 (K63) -linked polyubiquitination, we constructed an ubiquitin-deficient ANXA6 mutant (K63R) by replacing lysine 63 with arginine. Ha-tagged wild-type ubiquitin (WT-Ub), ubiquitin mimic mutant K63 and mutant K63R plasmids were transfected into 293 T cells, respectively. Western blot analysis using anti-HA immunoprecipitation revealed a significant increase in K63-linked cGAS ubiquitination in WT-ub and K63-expressing cells compared to vector controls. Strikingly, this effect was abolished by the K63R mutation (Fig. 4A). These data establish ANXA6 as a critical driver of K63-linked cGAS ubiquitination, likely facilitating lysosomal trafficking rather than proteasomal degradation. In order to investigate the specific correlation between ANXA6 and cGAS, we initially performed co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP) experiments and observed a direct interaction between ANXA6 and cGAS (Fig. 4B). The co-localization of ANXA6 and cGAS in cervical cancer cells was confirmed through immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 4C). The impact of ANXA6 expression on the degradation of cGAS protein was investigated in 293 T cells, utilizing cycloheximide (CHX) and bafilomycin A1 (Bafa-1), respectively. In ANXA6-overexpressing cells, Bafa-1 treatment rescued cGAS degradation (Fig. 4D-F), confirming that ANXA6 promotes cGAS turnover via the lysosomal pathway, not the proteasome. Therefore, ANXA6 effectively suppresses the cGAS-STING pathway by regulating the ubiquitination and degradation of cGAS.

Targeting ANXA6 indues ferroptosis sensibility of cervical cancer cells through cGAS-STING pathway

The RNA-Seq sequencing analysis of Siha-sh-ANXA6 and Siha-vector cells revealed a significant enrichment of iron channel activity and metal ion transport functions in Siha cells with Siha-sh-ANXA6 (Fig. 5A). Analysis of differential gene expression in the transcriptome demonstrated that the ANXA6 knockdown group exhibited high expression levels of ferroptosis-related molecules (Fig. 5B). To further validate the impact of ANXA6 on ferroptosis in tumor cells, we assessed the expression levels of ferroptosis-related genes in both experimental groups. The results indicated a significant upregulation of ferroptosis genes CTSB and ACSL4 in the Siha-sh-ANXA6 group (Fig. 5C-D). Subsequent analyses evaluated markers associated with ferroptosis, reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxides, which were found to be significantly elevated in the Siha-sh-ANXA6 group (Fig. 5E-F). These findings suggest that ANXA6 may regulate tumor cell ferroptosis through modulation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway. To confirm this hypothesis, siRNA targeting cGAS was transfected into Siha-sh-ANXA6 cells. As a result, we found a significant knockdown of ACSL4 and CTSB expression levels (Fig. 5G-H), indicating an effective reversal of ferroptotic effects by inhibiting cGAS expression when ANXA6 is downregulated in Siha cells. We quantified ROS and peroxide levels in Siha cells, revealed that when both ANXA6 and cGAS expressions were reduced, peroxide content decreased to control cell levels while ROS levels did not exhibit significant changes (Fig. 5I). Subsequently, we supplemented the ferroptosis inhibitor Liproxstatin-1 to ANXA6 - knocked - down Siha and Hela cells to investigate the alterations in ferroptosis markers. The findings indicated that the ferroptosis triggered by ANXA6 result knockdown was efficiently reversed upon the addition of the ferroptosis inhibitor(Fig. 5J). These results suggest potential involvement of cGAS in lipid peroxides-mediated ferroptosis induced by ANXA6 knockdown.

Discussion

This study elucidates a novel mechanistic insight into the role of ANXA6 in the modulation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway and the promotion of ferroptosis in cervical cancer. ANXA6 has been reported to act both as a tumor suppressor and oncogene [22], this dual role may result from its differential expression and post-translational modifications in different tumor microenvironments. In some cancers, ANXA6 may interact with tumor-suppressor pathways, such as regulating autophagy-related proteins to suppress tumor growth [23]. Our findings reveal that high ANXA6 expression in tumor correlates with an unfavorable prognosis in cervical cancer patients, underscoring the potential of ANXA6 as a prognostic biomarker. The downregulation of ANXA6 diminishes cervical cancer cell migration and colony formation, while promoting cell death, indicating that ANXA6 may serve as a critical facilitator of cervical cancer cell survival and proliferation.

The co-localization of ANXA6 with cGAS and its function as an inhibitor of the cGAS-STING pathway is a significant discovery. Previous studies have shown that ANXA6 functions by regulating cell cycle progression, tumor migration and signaling pathways such as RAS/MAPK, PI3K/AKT/mTOR [7, 8], etc. In our study, we found that ANXA6 overexpression in cervical cancer cells significantly promoted the K63-linked ubiquitination of cGAS protein, resulting in a significant decrease in cGAS expression. Knockdown of ANXA6 significantly upregulated cGAS, and this upregulation was a precursor to the subsequent activation of the cGAS-STING pathway, which has been implicated in innate immune responses to cytoplasmic DNA. There are studies showing that during the infection process of type 1 herpes simplex virus (HSV-1), the interaction between cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) and a series of cellular and viral proteins is significantly differentiated. By analyzing the interaction network diagram, we found that cGAS and Annexin A6 (ANXA6) exhibit interaction relationships in the functional context of apoptosis, autophagy and cellular stress response [22, 23]. This pathway also plays a vital role in tumor progression and distant metastasis [24]. In cervical cancer, the activated cGAS-STING signaling pathway can inhibit the in vitro and in vivo growth of cervical cancer cells, promote the expression of IFNβ and IL-6, and increase the number of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells and CD103+ dendritic cells [25, 26]. In our study, we observed an increase in the expression levels of cGAS and STING followed by ANXA6 knockdown. Furthermore, sequencing results suggested that downregulation of ANXA6 could significantly upregulate type I interferon production in cervical cancer cells. This regulatory mechanism highlights the delicate balance within the intracellular environment that determines cell fate decisions and immune signaling responses.

Our findings reveal that ANXA6 promotes K63-linked ubiquitination of cGAS, targeting it for lysosomal degradation—a mechanism distinct from canonical proteasomal turnover. This lysosomal dependency was confirmed by Bafilomycin A1 rescue experiments, suggesting ANXA6 may scaffold cGAS to lysosome-associated membranes via its phospholipid-binding domains. Given the emerging role of lysosomal degradation in immune checkpoint regulation, ANXA6-driven cGAS suppression could represent a novel immune evasion mechanism in ANXA6-high cancers. ANXA6 binding might induce structural changes in cGAS, exposing ubiquitination sites or SUMOylation access [27]– [28].

The activation of cGAS-STING signaling in the context of cervical cancer cells has far-reaching implications. Significantly, it culminates in the increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxides, both of which are critical mediators of oxidative stress [29]. While moderate levels of ROS are known to participate in cellular signaling processes, the excessive production within cancer cells can be detrimental [30]. The overabundance of ROS and lipid peroxides, as observed in our study, appears to be a driving force for the induction of ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of non-apoptotic cell death characterized by the accumulation of lipid peroxidation products and lethal cellular damage.

The link between the cGAS-STING pathway and ferroptosis is an emerging field of interest [31], with significant potential for therapeutic exploitation. The upregulation of cGAS, in the face of ANXA6 downregulation, suggests a vulnerability in cervical cancer cells that could be targeted by therapies aimed at modulating this pathway. The induction of ferroptosis has emerged as a promising strategy in cancer treatment, particularly for the eradication of cells resistant to conventional therapies like apoptosis-inducing agents [32].

Our study demonstrates ANXA6 suppresses the cGAS-STING axis via lysosomal degradation, yet its reliance on human cell lines and xenografts limits broader applicability. While resource constraints precluded validation in immunocompetent models or diverse cancers, our findings establish ANXA6’s tumor-intrinsic role in innate immune evasion. cGAS-STING pathway is involved in innate immune perception and is of great significance in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment. Its activation can produce cytokines such as type I interferon, which can attract and activate immune cell populations in the tumor microenvironment. For example, type I interferon can enhance the antigen presentation function of dendritic cells, activate tumor-specific T cells, and directly activate natural killer cells [33]. Under normal immunity, the alteration of tumor immune microenvironment and the activation of ANXA6 signaling pathway mediated by ANXA6 knockdown synergistically promote tumor cell death, but there is a complex feedback loop between the two. Tumors also develop escape mechanisms such as the secretion of immunosuppressive factors. The current study using Balb/c nude mice is of certain value, and the role of tumor immune microenvironment in this mechanism needs to be studied in immunocompetent models in the future.

In conclusion, our research provides compelling evidence that ANXA6 is a key regulator of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway and ferroptosis in cervical cancer. The interplay between ANXA6 and cGAS not only influences tumor cell survival but also shapes the immune landscape of the tumor microenvironment. Further studies are required to explore the therapeutic potential of modulating this interaction and to fully understand the implications of ANXA6 in cervical cancer pathophysiology and immunity.
