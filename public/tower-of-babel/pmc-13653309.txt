Dental Rehabilitation of the Scapular Tip in Maxillary Reconstruction
Authors: Leba M. Sarkis, John Lee, Karl Cuddy, Brian Rittenberg, Majd Al Mardini, Christopher MKL Yao, Sharon Tzelnick, David Goldstein, Ralph Gilbert, John R. de Almeida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe fibula‐free flap has traditionally been utilized to perform dental rehabilitation following maxillectomy. The purpose of this study is to present our experience with dental implantation of the scapula–tip‐free flap in midface reconstruction.MethodsFifteen adult patients underwent midface reconstruction with dental implantation using the scapula tip between January 2013 and December 2023. Implant success was defined as maintenance of the implant after 6 months of healing with associated functional prosthetic rehabilitation.ResultsA total of 35 implants were placed into 15 scapula‐free flaps. There were four implant failures (11.4%) that occurred in three patients (20%) during the study period resulting in an overall implant success rate of 88.6% with a median follow‐up period of 2.8 years. All patients were restored with a removable implant overdenture.ConclusionDental implantation can be achieved in the scapula tip following maxillectomy with a high degree of success.

Introduction

Reconstruction of the midface following surgical extirpation of the maxilla due to malignancy, benign neoplasms, congenital deformity or trauma poses one of the most significant challenges to the reconstructive surgeon. From an anatomical point of view, the maxilla consists of two horizontal (hard palate and orbital floor) and three vertical buttresses, the zygomaticomaxillary, nasomaxillary, and pterygomaxillary buttresses [1], all of which play a major role in mastication. Thus, defects in the maxilla may affect facial contour and result in functional impairment with loss of the ability to masticate. Reconstructive considerations should aim to restore anatomically important bony areas and support efforts to restore mastication through dental rehabilitation.

The reconstructive options of the midface when performing free tissue transfer for reconstruction include the fibula‐free flap, scapula flap, iliac crest, and the osteocutaneous radial forearm‐free flap. The fibula‐free flap has historically been utilized most commonly, particularly in mandibular reconstruction, given its bone stock for dental implantation, relatively thin and pliable skin paddle, which provides an ideal soft tissue envelope to accommodate implant retained prostheses. However, a number of patients are unsuitable for the fibula‐free flap harvest including those with significant peripheral vascular disease, patients with unsuitable anatomy (i.e., patients with a peroneal magnus artery or without three vessel run‐off to the foot), or in patients who have previously had harvested fibular flaps. In addition, elderly patients who are likely to have gait and ambulation issues may also be better served with an alternative donor site [2].

The subscapular system provides versatility allowing for multiple chimeric options to be harvested and provides an excellent donor site for complex midfacial defects such as the maxilla. Our group has previously described the utility of the scapular tip in midface reconstruction owing to its long pedicle spared of peripheral vascular disease, along with a relatively low morbidity donor site [3]. However, crucial to the rehabilitation of the midface and restoration of masticatory function is dental rehabilitation, for which there is a paucity in the literature. The purpose of this study is to present our experience with dental implantation of the scapula–tip‐free flap in midface reconstruction of the maxilla.

Materials and Methods

This is a retrospective review that included all patients who had undergone maxillary reconstruction using the scapula tip for reconstruction followed by either primary or delayed dental implant insertion between January 2009 and June 2023 at Toronto General Hospital, Toronto, Ontario. Research ethics board approval was obtained from the University Health Network prior to performing the study. Patients were included in this study if they were greater than or equal to 18 years of age, had primary or delayed dental implant insertion with or without bone grafting, and had undergone resection for either benign or malignant neoplasms. Implant failure was defined as the removal of a dental implant for any reason either during the primary period of osseointegration or at a later date. Patients were excluded if the dental implant failure occurred due to tumor recurrence or osteoradionecrosis (ORN). All dental implants were restored by a maxillofacial prosthodontist (M.A.M.).

“Implant success” was defined using a modified Albrektsson criteria [4]: analysis of cross‐sectional crestal bone levels on follow‐up imaging, gingival and periodontal health, patient symptoms, and prosthetic restorability with either a fixed or removable implant overdenture. For the purposes of this study, osteonecrosis following dental implant placement was considered a secondary adverse event. All patients underwent dental implant placement by an oral and maxillofacial surgeon (K.C, B.R.) using the 3.3 , 4.1 , or 4.8 mm diameter Straumann internal hexagon model dental implant system (Institute Straumann AG Basel Switzerland). Delayed failure was considered failure after > 2 years.

Time to implant failure was assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. Because most patients received more than one implant at the same time, survival was defined at the patient level rather than the implant level. The event of interest was defined as the occurrence of at least one implant failure. Time to event was calculated as the number of months from the date of implant placement to the earliest implant failure within that patient. Patients without implant failure during follow‐up were censored at their last recorded follow‐up date. A Kaplan–Meier curve was generated to illustrate implant survival at the patient level over time. Statistical analyses were performed using the R version 4.4.2 [R Core Team 2024].

Results

Fifteen patients (nine males, six females) with median age 52 years (range: 28–82) had a total of 35 implants placed. Five of 15 (33.3%) of the patients were either current or ex‐smokers and nine patients (66.7%) had never smoked. One patient's smoking status was unknown. The histopathology was osteosarcoma in five patients (33.3%), squamous cell carcinoma in three patients (20%), ameloblastoma in two patients (13.3%), mucoepidermoid carcinoma, mucosal melanoma, SNUC, adenocarcinoma, and fibrous dysplasia in the remaining patients. Two of the 15 (13.3%) of the patients received radiation, one of which was preop and the second patient adjuvant. Patient demographics can be found in Table 1. The median number of implants placed per scapula tip was two implants (range: 1–4) and the median time to implant placement was 20.1 months (range: 7.3–147.5).

Discussion

In this study we demonstrate the feasibility of dental implantation of scapula–tip‐free flaps used for midface reconstruction and all patients were dentally restored with an implant retained overdenture. Implant success was measured using a modified Albrektsson criteria [4]. Specifically, success was measured by well‐defined objective measures, namely, lack of implant mobility, peri‐implant bone loss and radiolucency, peri‐implant hard and soft tissue health and patient reported factors including pain and infection. Secondary stability on clinical assessment is controversial when assessing implants in the scapular‐free flap. At our institution, we do not torque implants greater than 15 N cm in the scapula, as we have historically noticed a higher rate of implant mobility at higher torque values. We have not found that this inhibits prosthodontic rehabilitation and therefore implants were not torque checked at high values. It should be noted that removable implant retained overdentures using locator attachments are funded by the ministry of health in our region, leading to this prosthetic choice for all patients in the study. However, extrapolating from the dental literature, implant retained overdentures, and implant supported fixed prosthesis appear to have equivalent patient satisfaction and quality of life outcomes with the added benefit of ease of oral hygiene [5]. To our knowledge, this is the first study demonstrating high success of implant retained overdentures utilizing the scapula tip in midface reconstruction.

The evolution of maxillomandibular reconstruction has led to the increased demand for occlusal based treatment planning with the ultimate goal of achieving dental implant rehabilitation. Historically the fibula‐free flap has been the preferred option when dental implantation is planned given the bicortical nature of bone allowing bicortical implant anchorage, ample cortical thickness to achieve primary stability, and the ability to perform a two‐team approach. Several studies have documented the success of dental implants in fibula bone to be > 90% [6, 7, 8, 9, 10] However, a number of patients may be deemed unsuitable for use of the fibula as a donor site, including those with a lack of three vessel run off, patients with significant arteriosclerotic disease, those with previous lower limb trauma, history of lower extremity thrombosis, or in patients whereby the fibula has been previously utilized. Furthermore, reconstruction in patients with a significant mucosal or external skin defect often requires significant soft tissue not offered by the fibular donor site. In addition, the three‐dimensional nature of the maxilla with large vertical dimension makes it difficult to reconstruct with a fibular graft without multiple complex osteotomies increasing complexity and risk. For certain defects or for certain patients, the scapula‐free flap provides a better reconstructive option.

The scapula–tip‐free flap provides several other advantages over other osseous flaps and specifically in midface reconstruction. First, it has been postulated that the scapula tip osteocutaneous flap produces less shoulder dysfunction than harvest of the upper lateral border of the circumflex scapular artery given less dissection of the rotator cuff musculature and specifically the teres major and minor muscles, the upper attachment of which can be preserved. It also provides the reconstructive surgeon with a long vascular pedicle, a significant amount of soft tissue when harvested in combination with either a thoracodorsal artery perforator skin paddle or latissimus dorsi muscle and also the ability to harvest in the supine position. The soft tissue options also provide a significant amount of bulk with maneuverability, particularly advantageous in the maxilla, in comparison to the thin pliable skin paddle of the fibula. The extended pedicle length of the scapula tip mitigates the need for vein grafts and has proven particularly reliable in maxillary reconstruction. The gentle curve of the distal tip of the scapula also provides a natural contour that mirrors the midface nasomaxillary and zygomaticomaxillary buttresses, providing excellent facial contour often precluding the need for an osteotomy, a distinct advantage over the fibula. Shoulder dysfunction following harvest of the scapula flap has been cited as a potential limitation; however, several studies have demonstrated near normal shoulder function following scapula harvest using validated objective parameters, and this has similarly been our own experience, even when harvested from the dominant side [11]. Lastly, in this study we have shown that the scapula provides sufficient bone stock to accommodate osseointegrated implants. We believe that the bone stock of the scapula provides highly vascular cancellous bone to surround the implant surface with significant height able to accommodate longer implants than a fibula. This is particularly advantageous in older patients whose fibulas may have predominantly thick sclerotic cortical bone that lacks the vascularity of the cancellous scapula.

Although several studies have documented the success of dental implants in mandibular reconstruction, we demonstrate the feasibility of implanting the scapula in both males and females. One of the criticisms of the utility of the scapula in dental implantation has been the ability to achieve primary stability on insertion. In our study, none of the patients were immediately loaded and all required a staged procedure for implant exposure. We feel this staged approach increases stability and allows the surrounding soft tissue envelope to achieve its final contour prior to providing an implant retained removable prosthesis. One unique advantage of the scapula flap is that muscle, as opposed to skin, is also commonly used to resurface the intraoral mucosal defect. Following contraction, the muscle becomes adherent to the peri‐implant tissue and undergoes mucosalization unlike the skin paddle of the fibula. This further provides a second vascularized barrier to plate exposure, oronasal fistula and potentially avoids subsequent ORN. However, the bulk of the muscle attached to the bone makes placement of implant or osteotomy cutting guides challenging and the addition of a setback during virtual surgical planning should be contemplated when incorporating cutting guides.

Peri‐implantitis was the major contributor of implant failure in this study. Preoperative assessment of gingival and periodontal health is a critical factor in determining longevity of dental implants. One patient in this study had active periodontal disease, although it was unclear if this was the case in the other two patients, which is the likely cause of subsequent implant failure, given the known association with periodontal disease and peri‐implantitis [12]. Periodontal care following ablative and reconstructive surgery is challenging for patients given the pain and swelling experienced in the oral cavity. This is subsequently a variable that is challenging to comprehensively address for anyone undergoing free tissue transfer for maxillary or mandibular reconstruction. This also supports the need for aggressive management of periodontal disease and maintenance of oral hygiene in patients considered for implants. Therefore, where possible, patients at our institution are screened preoperatively to identify active periodontal disease. Clearly given the urgency to manage patients with cancer, this is not always possible in the preoperative setting, but the reconstructive surgeon needs to remain cognizant of periodontal status and the ability of the patient to conduct diligent oral hygiene when contemplating dental implants.

One of the soft tissue complications in patients undergoing dental implantation is the development of hypergranulation. Previous studies have documented peri‐implant hyperplastic tissue in up to 20%–64% of patients with surviving implants [13]. In patients reconstructed with fibula‐free flaps, hyperplastic granulation is associated with subsequent peri‐implant bone loss and has a recurrence rate of 67% even after excision [14, 15]. It is likely that such hypergranulation tissue results from the lack of keratinized lining mucosa and submucosa around the implants with subsequent microbial overgrowth; however, the exact underlying cause is unclear. We do not have numerical data from our centre but have noted a high rate of peri‐implant granulation tissue in patients reconstructed with all forms of scapular flaps with or without grafting of keratinized tissue around the implants. Two out of three of the patients whose implants failed were also smokers, a well‐known risk factor for the development of peri‐implantitis in the dental literature [16] with the third patient also having diabetes mellitus. Although the median time to implant placement was more than 18 months in our study, timing was based on practicalities including healing and bone union on cross‐sectional imaging to prevent premature loading, ensuring no recurrence of disease, patient preference, and prosthodontic opinion on final soft issue contour stabilization prior to restoration.

In this cohort of patients, only one patient received adjuvant radiation treatment and one patient had preoperative radiation. The one patient who received adjuvant radiotherapy and had delayed implant placement developed ORN. Previous studies have documented success of dental implants in irradiated bone grafts [17], however, most recently Kim et al. demonstrated an implant failure rate of 71% in irradiated fibula, subsequently advocating for immediate dental implant placement which resulted in no failures [18]. We would certainly advocate exercising caution in irradiated bone given not only the higher rate of implant loss, but precipitating ORN.

In summary, the results of the paper are encouraging and provide the largest series to date of dental implants placed in the scapula tip for midface reconstruction with successful implant retained restoration. Clearly the generalizability of our data is limited by the highly selected patient population group and small numbers in the cohort. However, these results validate that patients who undergo scapula tip reconstruction for midface defects can be rehabilitated successfully with dental implants.
