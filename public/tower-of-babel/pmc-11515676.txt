Genetic framework sequencing analysis of Candida tropicalis in dairy cow mastitis and study of pathogenicity and drug resistance
Authors: Chenxi Jiang, Weile Fang, Shupeng Chen, Xiaoquan Guo, Xiaona Gao, Pei Liu, Guoliang Hu, Guyue Li, Wanrui Mai, Ping Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Candida tropicalis (C. tropicalis) is a zoonotic pathogen that is widespread in the environment and in recent years an increasing number of dairy cows have been infected with the fungus causing mastitis in cows.In this study, 37 milk samples from the udders of cows with clinical mastitis were collected from a dairy farm in Guangxi Province, China, from which C. tropicalis was isolated and identified, and then the isolated fungi were subjected to genome frame map sequencing, genome functional analysis as well as comparative genome analysis of the sequencing results, and combined with the virulence test of the fungi and drug sensitivity test of the fungi determined in infected mice, the resistance genes and pathogenicity of the fungi were Analysis of resistance genes and pathogenicity.Our study results revealed the isolation and characterisation of C. tropicalis from diseased cows, with a genome length of approximately 14.27 Mb. Functional annotation of the genome identified 4068 genes associated with C. tropicalis. The strain exhibited a chemoresistance mutation in the gene cyp51,a virulence-enhancing mutation in the gene VTC4, and mutations in genes linked to drug resistance. Pathogenicity tests demonstrated that C. tropicalis could induce damage to the internal organs of mice, leading to different levels of cyanosis in the abdominal cavity, white necrotic foci on the surface of internal organs, lung hemorrhage, and enlargement of the spleen and thymus.Histological sections also revealed varying degrees of hemorrhage and degenerative changes in the cells of different organs in the mice. Drug sensitivity tests showed that the fungus was highly sensitive to nystatin and ketoconazole, moderately sensitive to amphotericin B, and insensitive to antibiotics such as itraconazole, gentamicin, and penicillin. In conclusion, C. tropicalis isolated from dairy cows in the Guangxi region in this study was pathogenic and resistant to azoles such as itraconazole and fluconazole, and this study provides a theoretical basis for the further screening of novel resistance genes in C. tropicalis, as well as providing a certain reference for the drugs used for the treatment of fungal cow mastitis in this region.

Introduction

Mastitis, a common disease in dairy cows, is characterized by inflammation in the udder due to microbial invasion, physical, or chemical damage. This infectious disease not only negatively impacts milk quality and production performance but also leads to significant economic losses in the dairy industry [1–3]. While bacteria have traditionally been the main cause of mastitis, recent studies have shown an increase in fungal, viral, and mycoplasma infections [4, 5]. In the case of mammary gland fungal infection, yeasts of the genus Candida are the most frequently reported fungal pathogens in dairy cow mastitis [6].In an annual hospital-based survey of the prevalence of yeast mastitis, the prevalence of clinical mastitis in cattle in the region in 2018–2019 was 19.68%, with 5.51% of samples showing yeast accounting for 1.09% of the overall prevalence [7].Additionally, studies have shown thatmastitis caused by mycotic organisms can account for 2–13% of all cases of mastitis in cattle, and that yeast intramammary infections account for at least 10% veterinary clinical cases, suggesting that the prevalence of fungal infections in dairy mastitis is increasing [8–10]. Fungal mastitis, mostly caused by Candida yeasts, can result in clinical mastitis characterized by pain, prolonged fever, tenderness, inflammation of the mammary gland and associated lymph nodes, as well as decreased milk production and quality [11]. The incidence of fungal mastitis is on the rise, making effective prevention, control, and treatment of the disease a critical concern in dairy farming.

Candida spp. are considered to be opportunistic pathogens that can cause infection in both humans and animals, and are typically two-faced, yeast-like when not pathogenic, and mycelial growth when pathogenic, massive outgrowths of buds to form budding tubes or pseudohyphae, which enhances their ability to invade and adhere, and triggers the pathogenesis of the organism [12–15]. In dairy cows, Candida infections may be due to the use and abuse of antimicrobial drugs, contamination of antibiotic solutions and syringes and treatment with ducts or other materials in contact with the mammary gland leading to yeast colonization of the bovine dairy [14, 16, 17]. Although C. tropicalis infection is considered to be the most common cause of mastitis, there has also been a significant increase in the number of cases of mastitis in dairy cows due to infections with non-albicans Candida spp. (NAC), such as C. krusei, C. parapsilosis, C. glabrata, and C. tropicalis, in the last decade [18]. C. tropicalis has been reported to be the third or fourth most common cause of candidaemia globally and has been reported to have a relatively high rate of azole insensitivity among clinical C. tropicalis in China [19–21]. C. tropicalis, as an emerging pathogen of mastitis in dairy cows, has significantly lower antifungal susceptibility and produces more serious antimicrobial resistance, which seriously affects the development of dairy farming. In intensive livestock production, antibiotic treatment (local application of intramammary drugs and systemic administration of antibiotics) is the most common method of controlling mastitis, however, the widespread use of antibiotics for the treatment and prevention of intramammary infections can lead to the development of antimicrobial resistance, resulting in a range of adverse effects including drug resistance, drug residues, host microbial damage and environmental contamination, causing huge economic losses to the dairy farming industry [22–24].Therefore, knowledge of the pathogenicity and genomic profile of C. tropicalis is necessary for the treatment and prevention and control of fungal mastitis.

At present, among the available literature and studies related to C. tropicalis, there are more studies on human disease isolates and fewer studies on C. tropicalis isolates in dairy mastitis, mostly describing their clinical hazards and drug resistance, with few studies on their genomes. The Guangxi belt is in a subtropical monsoon climate with high ambient temperatures and humidity in summer, which significantly increases the number of mastitis pathogens or the risk of mastitis. This study analyzed the complete genome of C. tropicalis, focusing on its pathogenicity and drug resistance mechanisms. The aim was to understand the differences in pathogenicity and drug resistance between C. tropicalis strains causing dairy cow mastitis and those from other sources. The findings will serve as a foundation for future research on Candida infections in dairy cow udders and may also offer insights into tropical Candida infections in humans.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and ethics statement

In this study, 37 milk samples were randomly collected from cows suspected of having bovine mastitis from a dairy farm in Guangxi Province, China. The milk samples were collected by placing 10 mL of milk samples in sterile centrifuge tubes under aseptic conditions and stored at 4 °C for backup.

A total of 60 adult Kunming mice weighing approximately 20 g were used and randomly divided into six groups for the toxicity test. All the animals were acclimated under standard laboratory conditions (ventilated room, 25 ± 1 °C, 60 ± 5% humidity, 12 h light / dark cycle) and had free access to standard water and food. All procedures were conducted following the “Guiding Principles in the Care and Use of Animals” (China) and were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Jiangxi Agriculture University.

Isolation and identification

The milk samples were always aseptically collected and plated onto Sabouraud dextrose agar and modified Martin medium. The plates were incubated at 37 ℃ for 24～48 h, 0.5–1 mm in diameter, white or cream colored, uniformly bounded and that have distinctive yeast scent.In Gram staining, Gram positive. Then PCR and sequence analysis were performed.

The collected bacterial solution was extracted using the fungal genomic DNA extraction kit (Solarbio, China). PCR was performed on 1 µl of the Fungal liquid samples and according to standard procedures. Oligonucleotide primers were derived from rRNA genes of fungi and can be used for universal fungi PCR, forward primer ITS1 (5’-TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG-3’) and reverse primer ITS4(5’-TCC TCC GCT TAT TGA TAT GC-3’) of fungi.

Genome sequencing, assembly and quality assessment

Genomic DNA was extractedwiththe SDS method. The harvested DNA was detected by agarose gel electrophoresis and quantified by Qubit. Whole genome sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq PE150 platform. The six DNA samples were ligated to paired-end aptamers via A-tailed ligation and PCR-amplified into 350 bp insert fragments for library construction at Beijing Novozymes Bioinformatics (Beijing, China). After the library construction, Qubit 2.0 was used for preliminary quantification and the library was diluted to 2 ng/ul. Subsequently, the insert size of the library was detected using Agilent 2100, and after the insert size was as expected, the effective concentration of the library was accurately quantified using the Q-PCR method to ensure the quality of the library. After downstream raw data processing and sample assembly, genomic information was analysed.

Genome component predictions

Genome component prediction included the prediction of the coding genes, the repetitive sequences and the ncRNA. The strains were predicted to encode genes using GeneMarkS software (http://topaz.gatech.edu/) NcRNAs include transfer RNAs (tRNAs), ribosome RNAs (rRNAs), small RNAs (sRNAs), small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), and microRNAs (miRNAs). The rRNA was found by comparison with the rRNA database or predicted by rRNAmmer software for rRNA, by tRNAscan software for tRNA regions and secondary structure of tRNA, and by Rfam software for sRNA.

Genome functional annotation analysis

We utilized various databases including the Non-Redundant Protein Database databases(NR), Gene Ontology(GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes(KEGG), Clusters of Orthologous Groups(COG), Transporter Classification Database(TCDB), Pathogen Host Interactions(PHI), Pfam-A, Carbohydrate-Active enzymes Database(CAZy) to predict gene functions. Additionally, a whole-genome BLAST search was conducted. Secretory proteins were predicted using the Signal P database and TMHMM.

Comparative genomics analysis

Comparative genomics is the understanding of the intrinsic functions of genomes, expression mechanisms and species evolution through comparative analysis with known genomes [25]. The GCI genome of the strain was compared with the reference genome using MUMmer software to determine the covariance and relative sequence orientation between the two genomes, and a series of operations were performed using LASTZ and chainNet to find translocations and inverted regions between the genomes from which the SV regions between the comparison blocks were identified by the distance relationship between adjacent comparison blocks on the two genomes. Individual SNPs were also examined to annotate SNP functions according to the positional relationships and interactions between SNPs and genes.

Pathogenicity test

Healthy adult Kunming mice weighing 20 ± 5 g were used in this study.The bacterial solution concentration gradient was set according to the half lethal dose, and 60 mice were randomly divided into 6 groups (groups A, B, C, D, E and F), with 10 mice in each group. Mice in groups A, B, C, D and E were intraperitoneally injected with 1 mL of diluted C. tropicalis solution, with concentrations of 1 × 1010, 3.16 × 109, 1 × 109, 3.16 × 108 and 1 × 108 respectively. The control group F was injected with the same amount of normal saline. After injection, the activity, clinical symptoms and number of deaths of the mice were observed. We chose to euthanise mice by cervical dislocation (CD), and after death, organs were dissected and collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological observation.

Drug resistance analysis

The growth curve of the fungus was plotted and the fungal solution was selected during the logarithmic growth phase for drug susceptibility testing. The fungal solution was inoculated into modified Martin’s medium and cultured on a constant-temperature shaker at 37 °C. Tubes were taken out at hourly intervals for the first 24 h and then every 12 h thereafter, with OD values at 600 nm measured and recorded. The Kirby-Bauer test was used for drug susceptibility testing by inoculating the bacterial solution onto Sabouraud agar culture medium. Drug-sensitive paper was placed on the bacterial culture medium and cultured in a 5% CO2 constant temperature biochemical incubator. The growth of the fungus was observed after 24 h, and the diameter of the inhibition zone was measured and recorded.

Drug gradient susceptibility testing was conducted for fluconazole, itraconazole, amphotericin B, and ketoconazole. Initial drug concentrations were: fluconazole 128 µg/ml, itraconazole 32 µg/ml, amphotericin B 32 µg/ml, and ketoconazole 128 µg/ml. The drugs were diluted using gradient dilution into 11 concentration levels. Each tube received 0.1 mL of the diluted suspension. For instance, in the case of fluconazole, concentrations in test tubes A1-A10 post-dilution were 64 µg/mL, 32 µg/mL, 16 µg/mL, 8 µg/mL, 4 µg/mL, 2 µg/mL, 1 µg/mL, 0.5 µg/mL, 0.25 µg/mL, and 0.125 µg/mL. A11 served as the positive control, while A12 was the blank control. Triplicate replicates were performed for each concentration. The test tubes were placed on a constant temperature shaker at 220r/min and 37 °C for 24 h of shaking culture. Subsequently, the OD value of the cultured bacterial solution was measured at a wavelength of 600 nm in a 96-well plate for data recording and analysis.

Statistical analysis

All results were expressed in the format of Mean ± SE. The complete data set was submitted to the National Biotechnology Information Center (NCBI) short-reading archival database with the entry number SRR26490627.

Results

Isolation and identification of strains

In this study, C. tropicalis was detected in milk samples from mastitis. The suspected strain was purified and cultured to form round, smooth, shiny, creamy white colonies on plates. Gram staining of the strain was positive, revealing blue-purple pumpkin seed-like bacteria under a 40-fold oil microscope and bright bacteria under a 40-fold dark field of ink staining (Fig. 1-a, b,c). Gel electrophoresis bands were bright, with the PCR product size around 500 bp (Fig. 1-d). DNA star software was utilized to analyze the gene sequence of the sample strains, showing a 100% similarity among the three fungi CGI-1, CGI-2, and CGI-3 isolated in this experiment. When compared to the sequence of C. tropicalis published in NCBI, the homology was also 100% (Fig. 1-e). Therefore, the isolated strain was preliminarily identified as C. tropicalis.

Genome functional annotation analysis

Using Diamond software, the amino acid sequences of strain GCI were compared with various databases and the annotation results were obtained by combining the genes of strain GCI with the corresponding functional annotation information. Comparison with the NR database showed that C. tropicalis had a maximum of 4068 annotated genes Fig. 2-A). As shown in Fig. 2-B, the sample strains were annotated by the CAZy database and a total of 125 carbohydrate-active enzymes were obtained.

A comparative analysis of the KEGG databases identified a total of 4673 genes that were measured and annotated across six major functional pathways. Among these, 11 genes were associated with metabolism, while 36 genes were linked to drug resistance pathways such as Antifolate resistance, EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance, Endocrine resistance, Platinum drug resistance, β-Lactam resistance, and Endocrine resistance (Fig. 2-C). Examination of bacterial virulence and pathogenicity in the PHI database revealed a chemoresistant mutation at cyp51/erg11, a mutation at VTC4 associated with enhanced virulence, 36 mutations leading to reduced virulence, and four lethal mutations in the strain (Fig. 2-D). Furthermore, comparison of the TCDB database indicated a total of 365 transport proteins, including 155 electrochemical potential-driven transporters, 133 major active transporters, 40 ion channel proteins, and 11 cofactors involved in the transport process (Fig. 2-EF).

The KOG database comparison showed that a total of 2276 genes were predicted, covering 25 functions.There are 73 homologous sequences compared for post-translational modification, protein turnover and molecular chaperones; 242 sequences for general function prediction; 51 sequences for energy production and conversion; in addition, there are nucleotide sequences Transport and metabolism, carbohydrate transport and metabolism, signal transduction mechanism, etc.

Using IPRscan software, the amino acid sequences of strain GCI were compared with the GO database for classification statistics to obtain the overall distribution of the number of genes with different functions. The results showed that a total of 3921 genes were obtained, of which 2217 and 2067 were involved in cellular and metabolic processes, respectively, in the classification of biological processes. Among the cellular components, there were 1704 genes related to cells, 1704 to cellular parts, 809 to organelles and 606 to macromolecular complex objects. In the molecular function category, there are 1918 and 2096 genes related to catalytic activity and binding activity, respectively (Fig. 2-H).

A total of 197 secreted proteins and 14 cytochrome P450 proteins were obtained by annotation of the GCI genome using TMHMM software. These secreted proteins contain a signal skin structure but no transmembrane structure and need to be modified by the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus before being secreted outside the cell. Cytochrome P450 proteins play an important role in drug metabolism. Of the 14 predicted cytochrome P450s, nine are specific to C. tropicalis. However, the link between these proteins and drug resistance in C. tropicalis requires further in-depth analysis.

From the results of the covariance analysis between the sample strains and the reference genome (Fig. 3-AB), there were a large number of translocation regions in both, with 7,711 translocated genes and 280 translocations + inversions, while translocations or inversions usually cause rearrangement of genetic material and mutations. This result suggests that there are differences between the genomes of C. tropicalis isolated from dairy cows with mastitis and the reference strain in this experiment.

Pathogenicity test

Compared with the normal group, the attacked mice had a significant decrease in appetite, depression and overall emaciation. After 72 h, all mice in group A died, 80% in group B, 60% in group C, 20% in group D, and no death in the control group, as shown in theTable 4.

Pathological examination of infected mice had varying degrees of cyanosis in the abdominal cavity, white necrotic foci on the inner wall of the chest cavity and on the liver, fleshy lesions as well as haemorrhages in the lungs, enlarged spleens, a layer of white hyperplasia covering the surface of the kidneys, and enlarged thymus glands with haemorrhagic spots (Fig. 4). The pathological sections further revealed decreased alveoli, thickened alveolar septum, and stroma hyperemia in the disease group. Renal tubular stenosis and interstitial hyperemia were observed in the kidneys, while hepatocyte structure was blurred and boundaries between red pulp and white pulp of the spleen were indistinct. The thymic cortex and medulla showed blurred boundaries, reduced thymocytes, and signs of bleeding (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Cow mastitis is an inflammatory disease caused by the common infection of multiple pathogenic microorganisms.The occurrence of this disease will not only affect the health of dairy cows, but also reduce the milk production and quality of dairy cows, thus affecting the development of the dairy industry [27, 28]. In recent years, there are more and more reports about the isolation of fungi from cow mastitis, and the isolation rate of fungi is higher and higher [29]. Antibiotics are the most effective treatment for bovine mastitis; nevertheless, their therapeutic effect eventually diminishes due to antibiotic overuse, which causes drug resistance in pathogenic microorganisms [30]. In our experiments, we isolated the fungus from infected cows’ milk, identified it using a variety of methods, and determined that it was C. tropicalis. The fungus is an opportunistic yeast pathogen that causes mastitis infections that are well known in animals, and the use and abuse of antimicrobial agents, as well as the treatment of contaminated antibiotic solutions, and catheters or other materials in contact with the mammary glands also facilitate colonization in the breast of the cow [6, 31]. At present, the research on C. tropicalis mainly focuses on human diseases, and the research on bovine mastitis is rare. Furthermore, the majority of them describe clinical dangers and therapeutic resistance, with little research on the genome. Therefore, understanding the genomic structure and molecular biological function of C. tropicalis is of great importance to our study of fungal-type diseases of bovine mastitis.

Whole genome sequencing enables accurate identification of pathogens and also provides a comprehensive characterisation of AMR markers, plasmid replicon types and virulence factors, and the genomic information obtained from sequencing can also be used for WGS predictive antimicrobial susceptibility testing (WGS-AST) [32]. In this study, the whole genome of the isolated strain was sequenced and analyzed, and from the results, the genome length of the strain was14.27 Mb and the number of genes was 8,168. The NR database is characterized by its comprehensiveness, and the annotation results will contain species information that can be utilized to classify crop species [33]. Our results show the highest number of genes annotated to C. tropicalis, thus proving once again that the sample strains we isolated were C.tropicalis . Studies have shown that in higher animals, there are many different genomic rearrangements associated with sequence duplication and functionalization, all of which imperceptibly alter transcriptional activity and thus affect basic biological functions such as development and differentiation [34]. In our study, the sample strain had a repetitive sequence of 209,319 bp, representing 1.4659% of the genome.A large number of repetitive sequences mean that the genome is constantly self-replicating, which is an inevitable process of evolution. In addition, repetitive sequences protect certain structure-coding genes from damage. In addition, comparative genomic analysis showed that there were more translocations between the two, and the translocation was likely to cause structural changes of genetic material.

C. tropicalis has been widely considered the second most virulent Candida species, only preceded by Candida albicans [35]. Relevant literature shows that the infection rate of Candida tropicalis is higher than that of other Candida species. Candida tropicalis spreads faster in the body than other Candida species, and the mortality rate is also higher [36, 37]. In the pathogenicity analysis of C. tropicalis, this study compared the PHI database and found that the virulence-enhancing mutation gene VTC4 appeared in the annotated genes of the sample strains.VTC4 is the main enzyme that performs polyP synthesis in fungi, which is a part of the Vacuole Transporter Chaperone (VTC) complex, which plays an important role in vacuolar membrane fusion and has physical relations with vacuolar H+-ATPase (V-ATPase), influencing vacuolar H+ uptake [38, 39]. In yeast, polyPs are involved in the control of the cell cycle, stress response, and virulence, while Vtc4 is the main enzyme involved in the synthesis of polyPs in fungi [40]. This indicates that Vtc4 is involved in the change of fungal virulence mechanism, and also indicates that Candida tropicalis has Very toxic.In fact, our test results also confirmed this, the isolates can cause varying degrees of damage to the liver, kidneys, lungs and thymus of mice, and even cause the death of mice. The above results indicate that the fungal strains we isolated are highly pathogenic.

Antibiotics are currently effective drugs for the prevention and treatment of dairy cow mastitis. However, the increasingly serious problem of drug resistance, especially multidrug resistance (MDR), poses a huge threat to the control of the disease [41]. There are reports in the literature that long-term use of antibiotics may kill normal bacterial species in the body, break the balance of the flora, and provide favorable conditions for conditional pathogenic bacteria [42]. Therefore, the use of antibiotics can promote the growth and reproduction of Candida tropicalis.The structural characteristics of Candida tropicalis can produce a large number of biofilms and are highly adherent to epithelial cells and endothelial cells, making the bacteria highly resistant to drugs, so it often encounters difficulties in treatmenthemical resistance mutations. Cyp51 (sterol 14α-demethylase) is a member of the cytochrome P450 family and is the target of azole antifungal drugs [35, 43, 44]. Azole drugs inhibit ergosterol biosynthesis in fungal cells by binding to it, thereby producing resistance [45]. Our drug susceptibility results also confirmed this, Candida tropicalis is resistant to azole drugs.In subsequent experiments, we tested the sample strains for drug resistance genes through bacterial liquid PCR and found that the sample strains contained azole drug-related resistance genes ABC1, CDR1, CDR2, etc. And in the SNP site analysis data, azole drug-related resistance genes CDR1, CDR2, CDR3, CDR4 and the ABC protein family were also detected. The above results indicate that Candida tropicalis is resistant to azole drugs.In studies recently presented by Sasani, C. tropicalis strains isolated from patients with candidemia and characterized by increased metabolic activity and high biofilm mass were related to higher mortality rates, and biofilms formed by these strains showed increased resistance to azole drugs [46]. Studies have shown the predominant mechanism of azole resistance Candida species is mutation/ overexpression of different genes [45].The drug inducible overexpression of transporter genes like the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) and the major facilitator superfamily (MFS) can cause active efflux of cellular azole antifungal drugs there by contributing to antifungal resistance [46]. The CDR1, CDR2 and CaMDR1 genes are proposed to encode cell membrane-associated transporters, which act as possible mediators to enhance fluconazole efflux.Relevant evidence suggests that the efflux of azole drugs from fungal cells is due to the action of energy-dependent pumps, and some researchers have associated the increased expression of ABC superfamily member CDR genes (especially CDR1 and CDR2) and MFS superfamily CaMDR1 genes with fluoride [47]. The development of conazole resistance has been linked, which also verified our experimental results .

Conclusion

In summary, we isolated and characterized a strain of C. tropicalis from a cow with mastitis.Experimental results show that C.tropicalis is highly pathogenic. Through genome sequencing analysis, we predicted the drug resistance of the fungus, and screened and verified the resistance of related drugs through drug susceptibility testing. This study provides a theoretical basis for genomic analysis and drug resistance research of C. tropicalis, and also provides a reference for the prevention and treatment of dairy cow mastitis caused by C. tropicalis.
