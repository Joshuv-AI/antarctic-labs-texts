Research on the evolution and driving characteristics of cultural-tourism integration efficiency in urban clusters of the Yangtze River Delta
Authors: Duoxun Ba, Luxing He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The integration of culture-tourism is an important strategy for achieving economic circulation and sustainable development in global urban agglomerations. Utilizing the Epsilon-Based Measure (EBM) model, Malmquist index, and spatial analysis, this study investigated the efficiency across 27 central cities in the Yangtze River Delta (YRD) from 2015 to 2019. The results were as follows: (1) Significant spatial heterogeneity existed; certain developed cities initially exhibited input redundancies in tourism infrastructure and cultural facilities, yet overall efficiency reached high levels by the end of the study period. (2) Returns to scale showed multidimensional differentiation, manifesting as continuous growth, decline, and fluctuation, indicating unstable development paths. (3) Dynamic efficiency improvements were primarily driven by technological progress, with most cities approaching the production frontier over time. (4) High-efficiency clusters were concentrated along coastal and riverine corridors, though regional collaborative effects remained underdeveloped. Three distinct development models emerged: a resource-driven model that relies on distinctive local endowments to achieve high efficiency, a technology-led model that leverages balanced innovation and management, and a transitional optimization model that enhances efficiency by reducing redundancies. These results demonstrated that achieving high-quality integration requires both resource allocation optimization and technological empowerment. The findings provided a scientific basis for promoting deep integration of cultural-tourism in the YRD urban agglomeration and for the coordinated development of cultural-tourism in other urban agglomerations worldwide.

Introduction

Globally, cultural-tourism integration has become a key force for urban and regional regeneration, economic diversification, and enhanced international competitiveness1. In the experience economy era with intensifying inter-city competition, the capacity of metropolitan areas to efficiently integrate cultural resources into tourism services contributes to achieving lasting strategic advantages. The YRD region in China, as one of the world’s most dynamic megacity clusters, presents an ideal setting for studying this phenomenon. Its rapid economic growth, abundant tourism resources, and government-led integration strategy make it a typical case for examining culture-tourism integration efficiency and its driving factors. Since the 13th Five-Year Plan, the Chinese government has implemented a top-down institutional strategy to promote cultural-tourism industry integration. The 13th Five-Year Plan for Tourism Development in 2016 explicitly proposed facilitating this integration. The Ministry of Culture and Tourism’s establishment in 2018 institutionalized this concept, breaking down administrative barriers between sectors. The 20th National Congress of the Communist Party of China in 2022 further reinforced this strategic priority, encouraging positive interplay between culture and tourism. Subsequently, measures aimed at stimulating cultural-tourism consumption and revitalizing the industry underscored the urgency of integration in improving livelihoods and boosting consumption. However, inefficient resource allocation and structural contradictions arising from consumption upgrades have constrained its potential as a spiritual and cultural driver for a higher quality of life and domestic circulation. Although policies have enhanced tourism performance, integration efficiency faces challenges amid uncoordinated resource inputs. This aligns with research conclusions that China’s cultural-tourism integration policies boost tourism performance while facing efficiency bottlenecks absent coordinated resource allocation2,3. Therefore, measuring and improving cultural-tourism integration efficiency holds significant practical importance. Efficiency, as an indicator measuring resource allocation and utilization effectiveness, refers to achieving maximum social surplus through resource allocation4. During this critical integration period5, conducting efficiency measurements and differential analysis can sustain the dual-drive effect of cultural-tourism, respond to Chinese modernization requirements for coordinated material and spiritual civilization development, promote urban coordinated development and enhance support for common prosperity6.

While the progress of the cultural and tourism sectors has garnered extensive scholarly attention globally, distinct divergences characterize the specific research foci domestically and internationally. International scholarship has predominantly centered on specialized cultural-tourism niches, such as heritage tourism7, festival and experiential tourism8,9, creative tourism10,11, religious pilgrimage tourism12, and the interplay between activities like hiking and cultural preservation in ancient towns13, often revealing the complexities of commercializing traditional practices14. The methodology adopted primarily involves field investigations and in-depth case analyses, engaging in practice-oriented exploration at the micro level. In contrast, domestic scholars studying cultural-tourism integration have, amidst economic transformation and blurring industry boundaries, gradually shifted from early theoretical contemplations on culture-tourism relationships15 to interdisciplinary research from industrial integration perspectives. This research encompasses the logical framework of integration16, integration mechanisms17,18, and implementation pathways19,20, balancing mechanistic analysis at the theoretical level with empirical studies combining data and models21. As research deepens, the effectiveness of cultural-tourism integration has become a focal point22–25. Although the overall integration efficiency in China is improving, regional disparities persist4. Currently, the coupling coordination degree model is widely employed in industry assessments26–28. Scholars have constructed comprehensive evaluation indicator systems based on cultural-tourism resources, labor, and market dimensions5,29, and have gradually analyzed the complex symbiotic relationship, dynamic evolution process, and intrinsic mechanisms between cultural and tourism systems. Additionally, researchers have employed panel regression and geographical detector methods to examine spatiotemporal evolution characteristics of cultural-tourism integration development levels30–32.

Although research on cultural-tourism integration efficiency has attracted academic attention, methodological limitations remain. Regarding methodology, some studies construct evaluation indicators grounded in the homogeneity assumption of coordinated development between cultural-tourism industries and economic growth25, while others emphasize systematic analysis of cultural-tourism industries3,33. At the research scale, this field often exhibits a polarized pattern: some studies conduct macro-level assessments at national or provincial levels5,29,33, while others focus on micro-level case scholarship34. Consequently, empirical investigations at the meso-scale, specifically urban agglomerations as an integrated unit, remain relatively limited. Addressing this dimension would contribute to a more comprehensive research framework. Existing studies demonstrate limitations in theoretical hypothesis applicability, scale rationality, and explanatory power regarding regional heterogeneity, leading to conclusions whose objectivity and practical guidance warrant further consideration.

In summary, interpreting the spatiotemporal evolution and determinants of cultural-tourism integration in the YRD region is both an academic endeavor and a practical necessity. This helps avoid traditional extensive growth models and promotes high-quality development through regional cooperation. The accelerating regional integration process underscores this necessity, making the YRD an important testing ground for exploring high-quality cultural-tourism synergistic development35. Findings from this context are expected to provide valuable insights for other major city clusters worldwide. This study addresses the core areas of the YRD urban cluster, adopting a robust methodological framework combining the EBM model, Malmquist Productivity Index, and spatial analysis techniques. The objectives are threefold. First, assess the static efficiency and returns to scale of integration in 27 cities. Second, decompose dynamic sources of efficiency growth into technological progress and efficiency improvement. Third, reveal spatial patterns and associated characteristics, thereby identifying potential for coordinated development. Additionally, this study identifies three distinct development models, offering sights into integration mechanisms while pinpointing development paths and collaborative shortcomings of different cities. These findings provide policy implications and practical significance for enhancing regional cultural-tourism quality in the YRD region.

Methods

Study area

The Yangtze River Delta (YRD) urban agglomeration, situated at the confluence of rivers and sea in eastern China, serves as a growth engine driving the nation’s high-quality economic development in the new era. It bears the strategic tasks of jointly building a high-energy-level cultural and tourism industry development hub and world-class, high-quality cultural and tourism destinations. In 2024, its gross domestic product (GDP) exceeded 33 trillion yuan, accounting for approximately 24.58% of the national GDP. While this recent macroeconomic figure underscores the region’s sustained global significance and contemporary relevance, uncovering the intrinsic, endogenous evolutionary logic of its cultural-tourism integration necessitates a stable observation window. Therefore, to avoid the structural shocks and data anomalies caused by the post-2020 pandemic, this study deliberately focuses on the pre-pandemic baseline period (2015–2019). Since the proposal of the Regional Plan for the YRD Region in 2010, the integration process has continuously accelerated. It was elevated to a national strategy in 2018, and the Outline of the YRD Regional Integration Development Plan in 2019 further strengthened its development foundation. To ensure comparability and a uniform statistical framework, this study selects 27 cities within the central area, which exhibit stronger integration effects and radiating capabilities, as the research objects36. These include Shanghai, nine cities in Jiangsu Province, nine cities in Zhejiang Province and eight cities in Anhui Province.

EBM model

Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) is used to evaluate the relative efficiency of decision-making units (DMUs) with multiple inputs and outputs, including radial models like CCR and BCC, and the non-radial SBM model. The neglect of slack variables in the CCR model and the loss of proportional information in the SBM model both affect the accuracy of results. The Super-Efficiency EBM model proposed by Tone and Tsutsui37, combines the advantages of radial and non-radial models, has been recognized as a robust tool in recent tourism efficiency studies, capable of handling complex scenarios beyond traditional DEA models38. Unlike traditional DEA models that require orientation selection (input-oriented or output-oriented), the EBM model is inherently non-oriented, simultaneously optimizing both input reduction and output expansion through its hybrid structure. It relaxes the assumption of proportional reduction and is compatible with both radial proportional relationships and non-radial slack adjustments, effectively overcoming the defects of traditional models. This dual optimization mechanism is especially appropriate for cultural-tourism integration efficiency analysis, as it captures both the reduction of resource redundancies, a pressing issue in overdeveloped cities, and the enhancement of integration outputs, aligning with China’s high-quality development paradigm that advocates both waste minimization and value maximization. It has been widely applied in studies on tourism efficiency, such as dynamic DEA-based cultural tourism efficiency evaluation across Asian destinations39, medical institution service efficiency40, and energy efficiency41,42, among other fields. Accordingly, this model is employed to measure the cultural-tourism integration efficiency across the YRD urban agglomeration. Malmquist Index

The Malmquist Index was proposed by the Swedish economist Malmquist to evaluate the dynamic changes in DMU efficiency44. It can demonstrate the rate of change in DMU efficiency through distance functions and can be decomposed into the product of efficiency change (EC) and technological change (TC), i.e., MI = EC×TC. Compared to the DEA model, this index provides a more comprehensive calculation of efficiency values, overcoming the shortcomings of static efficiency in continuous time-series comparison. The Malmquist Index is used to reflect the changes in cultural-tourism integration efficiency during the study period. ArcGIS pro spatiotemporal analysis

ArcGIS Pro, as a professional GIS platform, integrates geographic spatial data management, analysis, and visualization, embedding cultural-tourism resources within its geographic space. Its powerful capabilities in spatiotemporal analysis have been fully validated in previous studies46–48. This study concentrates on the spatiotemporal evolution of cultural-tourism integration efficiency and uses the natural breaks method in ArcGIS Pro 3.0.2 to classify efficiency changes and technological progress decomposed from the MI index, thereby visualizing their spatial distribution.

Exploratory spatial data analysis

Exploratory Spatial Data Analysis (ESDA) is a method that reveals spatial clustering patterns and association manners based on the distribution patterns of spatial data, while identifying spatial heterogeneity. This technique has been widely applied in geographic research49–51. To investigate the spatiotemporal correlation of cultural-tourism integration efficiency in the YRD urban agglomeration, this study employs GeoDa software to construct a geographic distance-based spatial weight matrix and applies the Local Moran’s I for spatial autocorrelation analysis. Through LISA cluster maps, the study identifies the clustering patterns of efficiency evolution and types of spatial association, elucidating the spatial synergy between efficiency changes and technological progress.

Analytical framework and indicator system

Theoretical framework

To construct a robust conceptual foundation for evaluating cultural-tourism integration, this research is grounded in three interconnected theoretical streams that collectively inform the analytical approach and indicator selection.Industrial Integration Theory.Drawing from convergence economics, industrial integration occurs when previously distinct sectors merge production factors, technologies, and market channels to create synergistic value52. In the cultural-tourism context, integration transcends simple resource superimposition and entails systematic coordination across resource allocation, technology application, and market development. This theoretical lens provides a conceptual foundation for the evaluation system, guiding the selection of input dimensions such as cultural facilities and tourism capital and output metrics like integrated economic benefits.Production Frontier Theory.Efficiency evaluation based on Production Frontier Theory constitutes the principal methodological framework for operationalizing and measuring destination competitiveness. This theory emphasizes the dynamic capacity of a destination to transform multifaceted inputs into optimal outputs53. By assessing the extent to which a city approaches the production frontier, the research enables objective quantification of competitive advantage in cultural-tourism integration54. This theoretical perspective establishes the rationale for utilizing the Data Envelopment Analysis methodology to assess regional performance.High-Quality Development Economics.Aligned with the broader economic transformation paradigm, high-quality development prioritizes qualitative efficiency enhancement and sustainability over extensive scale expansion55,56. For cultural-tourism integration, this implies an imperative to optimize input-output relationships by maximizing economic and cultural outputs while minimizing resource redundancies. This orientation underpins the selection of two analytical tools. The first is the non-oriented EBM model, which simultaneously addresses input slacks and output shortfalls. The second is the Malmquist index, which decomposes growth into technological progress and managerial efficiency change.

Connotation of cultural-tourism integration development

Guided by the aforementioned theoretical framework, the integration of culture and tourism is an important development model for modern urban tourism industry. The acceleration of consumption upgrades and domestic circulation have led to a convergence of culture and tourism industries regarding resources, talent, and market aspects57. By exploring regional characteristics, traditional culture, and developing new tourism resources, diverse interactions and integration of factors are promoted, forming cultural-tourism products that enter the market and achieve the unity of value creation and synergistic development. The key to cultural-tourism integration lies in forging tight links between the two: it uses tourism to disseminate, engage with, and protect culture, while boosting travel experiences via cultural guidance to driving high-quality industrial progress. Against the backdrop of the new era, in-depth integration of cultural-tourism is not just stacking traditional resources; instead, it requires technological improvement and management optimization to realize coordinated growth in resource conversion efficiency and economies of scale. Specifically, the empowerment and support from cultural resources push forward the upgrading of the tourism sector, while the expansion of tourism industrial chain builds market-based channels to revitalize cultural resources58.

The YRD urban agglomeration encompasses different cultural-tourism resources, covering water towns to Jiangnan ecological and cultural landscapes, international urban culture and other innovative tourism formats. It also has a solid industrial basis and abundant innovation momentum. Under the background of digitalization and regional integration, high-quality development of cultural-tourism integration should break through traditional development models and explore cooperative paths between technological progress and efficiency improvement. Efficiency focuses on enhancing quality and effectiveness, emphasizing the reduction of disorderly development scale through higher input-output conversion rates, thereby achieving a high-efficiency state under multi-factor coordination4. Therefore, deepening the optimization of resource allocation mechanisms centered on technological innovation and aimed at efficiency enhancement not only effectively improves the efficiency of cultural-tourism integration but also conforms to the inherent requirements of economic transformation and upgrading. Through cross-regional factor flow and policy coordination, problems such as resource misallocation and input redundancy can be resolved, achieving a virtuous interaction between the cultural-tourism industry and the regional economy and high-quality development. This provides an anchor for research on the efficiency of cultural-tourism integration in urban agglomerations and injects new vitality of industrial synergistic development into Chinese modernization.

Indicator system and data sources

Resource endowment and multi-dimensional inputs are key elements for the development of cultural-tourism integration. Based on existing evaluation indicator systems for cultural-tourism integration efficiency4,24,29, and considering data availability and research practicality, panel data of 14 indicators for 27 cities in the central YRD from 2015 to 2019 were selected to measure the level of cultural-tourism integration development (Table 1). The selection of these specific indicators is theoretically grounded in the multifaceted nature of cultural-tourism integration. On the input side, cultural assessment includes the collection of public libraries, number of cultural and related industrial institutions, public budget expenditure on culture, sports, and media, and employees in culture, sports, and entertainment, comprehensively reflecting cultural resource stock, infrastructure level, policy support, and talent input. Tourism indicators include the number of A-grade attractions, travel agencies, star-rated hotels, and tourism employees, revealing tourism resource endowment, high-end accommodation scale, and the number of tourism service practitioners. The parallel inclusion of these diverse inputs forms the foundational structural basis for measuring cross-sectoral synergy. On the output side, cultural benefits are measured using the value-added of the tertiary industry and per capita consumption expenditure of urban residents on education, culture, and entertainment services. Tourism outputs are reflected in the number of domestic and international tourists and tourism revenue, comprehensively assessing consumption levels and international competitiveness. While direct tourism outputs capture immediate sector-specific gains, the value-added of the tertiary industry is deliberately employed as a macro-level proxy for integrated economic benefits. Under China’s current municipal statistical systems, a highly specific and independent accounting framework for the cultural-tourism integrated sector does not yet exist. More importantly, cultural-tourism integration functions as an economic catalyst, with its true value manifested in the extensive spillover effects it generates across the broader service economy—including accommodation, catering, transportation, and retail. Consequently, employing the value-added of the tertiary sector effectively captures the synergistic and multiplier effects produced by this integration, effects that narrow, sector-specific data would fail to reflect. The indicator system is designed rigorously to ensure data availability and horizontal comparability. It should be noted that input and output indicators in highly integrated urban agglomerations tend to exhibit internal correlation due to synchronized scale effects. However, as the Super-efficiency EBM model is a non-parametric linear programming method that constructs a production frontier without parameter estimation, such multicollinearity does not bias the resulting efficiency scores, a property well-documented in the DEA methodological literature59,60.

The data period for this study is strictly confined to 2015–2019. This specific timeframe was deliberately selected for three critical reasons. First, it covers the key implementation period of the 13th Five-Year Plan policies for cultural-tourism integration, capturing the formative phase of the industry. Second, it establishes a high-purity baseline by avoiding the severe structural disruptions and data contamination caused by the COVID-19 pandemic starting in 2020. Third, while the dataset concludes in 2019, the analytical framework and theoretical discussion are grounded in recent academic literature, enabling the study to interpret the stable historical patterns of cultural-tourism integration within contemporary debates on high-quality development. This approach offers a reliable historical reference for post-pandemic regional policy formulation by capturing the undisturbed evolutionary rules. Data were sourced from the China City Statistical Yearbook over the years, statistical yearbooks of the 27 cities, and statistical bulletins on national economic and social development. Relevant missing data were verified by contacting local statistics bureaus via email or telephone or were supplemented using linear interpolation. It should be specially pointed out that cultural and related industrial institutions include libraries, cultural centers, mass art centers, archives, museums, art education institutions, art performance venues, literary and artistic research institutions, and cultural relics protection institutions.

Observation reveals that among the 27 sample cities, only 25.93% had a mean cultural-tourism integration efficiency value exceeding 1, and significant spatial disparities and imbalances in dynamic changes were evident across cities. The top-ranked cities in terms of mean efficiency were Tongling, Chizhou, and Hangzhou.

Tongling, utilizing its position as a transportation hub along the Southern Anhui tourism corridor to accommodate the overflow of tourists from Anhui’s two mountains and one lake at a low cost; Chizhou revolves around Jiuhua Mountain’s Buddhist culture as its core attraction, vigorously developing religious and cultural tourism. These cities have low development levels and weak overall competitiveness, they have still established efficient development paths by capitalizing on ecological and cultural resources as well as industrial heritage. Hangzhou, with its systematic technical framework, achieves steady efficiency progress through the integration of digital technology and cultural-tourism. However, due to excessive investment in hotels and other infrastructure, as well as insufficient development of cultural resources, Yancheng’s efficiency score has remained below 0.5 for a long time, resulting in relatively slow development.

From a temporal perspective, the efficiency scores of a single city display unsteady variations across different years. Take Zhenjiang an example: its efficiency value stood at 0.2449 in 2015, yet climbed to 1.0021 in 2018, attributable to policy backing, innovation in cultural-tourism resources and eco-tourism products, and the optimization of excessive inputs. Meanwhile, the efficiency scores of Hefei and Wuhu rose steadily year on year, a trend that reflects the initial appearance of economy of scale, driven by factors like policy refinement, industrial upgrading and talent cultivation. Some cities experienced efficiency value fluctuations during the initial phase of integration into the urban agglomeration due to lagging resource integration but gradually recovered after adapting to regional development needs. Overall, the number of cities with efficiency values less than 1 decreased from 17 in 2015 to 3 in 2019, indicating an improvement in the synergistic level of culture and tourism in most cities and a resolution of the imbalance between inputs and outputs.

Macroscopically, the cultural-tourism integration efficiency of these cities demonstrated a development trend of overall improvement with local adjustments. During the study period, 96.30% of the cities had an average MI greater than 1, with only Tongling showing marginal decline. Efficiency growth shows characteristics of gradient differentiation: Zhenjiang, Xuancheng, and Taizhou (ZJ) are high-growth representative; Jiaxing and Suzhou set themselves apart with stable efficiency and slight fluctuations, and the standard deviation of the MI index even dropped below 0.0102. Regarding dynamic trends, 70.37% of cities saw efficiency gains between 2015 and 2016, increasing to 81.48% from 2016 to 2017 before stabilizing. This indicates that efficiency advancement has moved from being policy-guided to market-led. From a microscopic perspective, efficiency evolution across cities shows heterogeneity and policy relevance. For example, Yangzhou witnessed its efficiency plummet to 0.5414 from 2016 to 2017 because of challenges in investment implementation, subsequently stabilizing above 1 after adjustments; cities such as Nanjing and Hefei succeeded in steadily boosting efficiency through momentum transformation, exhibiting relatively steady variations. Taizhou’s (ZJ) MI value surged to 2.5337 from 2018 to 2019, possibly as technological embedded brought by the construction of the ‘Easten Zhejiang Tang Poetry Route’ digital cultural belt. Other cities experienced efficiency fluctuations due to overlapping resource allocation and external influences. For instance, Changzhou and Chuzhou had low efficiency due to resource redundancy in 2015–2016, while Taizhou (JS) achieved efficiency growth for four consecutive years.

Based on the above, for cities with an average MI greater than 1 and stable efficiency changes, introducing new technologies and high-level professionals, and exploring new cultural-tourism products like the cultural-tourism metaverse, can achieve long-term healthy development. Conversely, other cities should evaluate the inputs and outputs of cultural tourism to examine the rationality of the cultural-tourism industry scale, then optimize and adjust based on their own advantages and characteristics, laying a solid foundation for efficiency growth.

Given that the MI index can be decomposed into Efficiency Change (EC) and Technological Change (TC), the evolution of cultural-tourism integration development efficiency in the YRD urban agglomeration can be analyzed from the dynamic perspective of these two core elements. Efficiency change reflects the resource allocation management capability through the optimization and reorganization of cultural-tourism elements, while technological change reflects the innovative driving role of technology in enabling resource transformation and utilization. Research indicates that technological progress is the core engine for efficiency improvement, which is consistent with the finding that technological progress contributes 62% to tourism TFP growth in China25, and its performance highly depends on the degree of collaboration with efficiency change. As shown in Tables 5 and 96.30% of cities had an average annual TC value ≥ 1, significantly higher than the 73.07% of cities with an average annual EC value ≥ 1, indicating that technological progress is a common choice for cities. However, it is also necessary to prevent the aggravation of efficiency growth differentiation due to unbalanced management optimization. This study also identified the following driving characteristics: (1) Management and Technology Jointly Drive Efficiency Improvement. 70.37% of the cities showed simultaneous improvement in operational management efficiency and technological level. Taizhou (ZJ) and Xuancheng were particularly prominent, with both their efficiency growth rates and technological progress rates exceeding 20%, and their average MI values reaching 1.4568 and 1.3825, significantly leading cities driven by a single factor. Taizhou (ZJ) used digital technology to transform cultural-tourism resources and achieve intelligent tourist flow management, achieving a multiplier effect of resource optimization and technological innovation. Xuancheng similarly relied on technology application to enhance tourist experiences and stimulated resource vitality through the cluster effect of the cultural-tourism industry, forming a dual-core linkage of management and technology. (2) Divergence Between Management and Technology Constrains Efficiency Growth. Some cities failed to effectively coordinate the relationship between management optimization and technological innovation when developing the cultural-tourism industry. For example, although Zhenjiang’s MI value was 1.4959, the contribution of technological progress was significant while efficiency change was minimal, warranting caution against the limitations of single-factor technological driving. Although Wuxi’s technological improvements offset the deficiency in efficiency improvement, its efficiency change value was 0.9935, indicating a potential risk of management lag. Wuhu, with its model of integrating digital technology and culture, achieved a technological change value of 1.1252 and an efficiency change value also above 1, confirming that technology embedding needs to adapt to management optimization. (3) Overlapping Dilemma of Technological Regression and Management Lag. Tongling fell into a development bottleneck due to continuously declining EC and TC values. The traditional operation of scenic spots struggled to adapt to the upgrading of tourist demand, coupled with lagging technology updates and deficiencies in management efficiency, leading to slow monetization of cultural resources. Meanwhile, Shaoxing’s stable efficiency gained by addressing both efficiency refinement and technological innovation, capitalizing on the synergy between management reforms and proper technological integration.

Analytical results reveal that the differentiated growth of cultural-tourism integration efficiency amid cities can be categorized into three patterns. In the first, the Collaborative Advancement Type, marked by the mutual facilitation between efficiency advancement and technological enhancement, forming a positive development cycle, as exemplified by cities like Taizhou (ZJ) and Xuancheng. Second, the Single-Factor Dominant Type, where Nantong relied on management optimization for efficiency growth, while Taizhou (JS) and Zhenjiang relied on technological progress to drive development. Third, the Dual-Recession Type, represented by Tongling. The study also found that the dynamic coupling adjustment of resource management and technology application is an important logic for achieving deep integrated development of regional culture and tourism. Synergistically driven cities should continuously deepen their cultural-tourism digital integration systems; single-factor driven cities should improve management standardization or actively introduce and apply advanced technologies to compensate for shortcomings as much as possible; dual-recession cities urgently need to take proactive measures in technology introduction and application, management optimization, and rational resource allocation, supplemented by policy support, to reverse the trend of efficiency decline.

Spatiotemporal pattern of cultural-tourism integration development efficiency

Figure 1 shows the dynamic evolution characteristics of the spatial distribution of cultural-tourism integration development efficiency in the YRD urban agglomeration from 2015 to 2019. In terms of efficiency change, during the study period, Nantong and Xuancheng, leveraging management technology investment, transportation advantages, and abundant cultural-tourism resources, consistently maintained high-efficiency status, becoming typical cases of efficiency breakthroughs. Subsequently, the hotspot of efficiency change expanded to Taizhou (ZJ) on the eastern coast of Zhejiang, related to its cultural belt construction promoting digital cultural-tourism innovation and development. From the perspective of technological change, in the early stage of the study, cities around Hefei, as well as Zhenjiang, Yangzhou, Taizhou (JS), Huzhou, and Wenzhou, shaped local technological innovation effects through the transformation of industrial technology and the development of ecological resources, demonstrating the activating role of technological progress in cultural-tourism development. By 2018–2019, the achievements of technological progress were prominent across the entire region, with cities like Chuzhou and Jiaxing becoming high-value units for technological progress, reflecting the trend of technological resources diffusing from regional large cities to the periphery. Analysis of the cross-period averages reveals that Xuancheng and Nantong had long-term advantages in management efficiency change, particularly in resource allocation. Zhenjiang and Taizhou (ZJ) maintained stable development in technological progress, with Taizhou (ZJ) achieving optimal development through the coordination of both factors.

The spatial evolution of cultural-tourism integration efficiency in the YRD urban agglomeration is characterized by the rise of peripheral cities and the strengthening of axial belt functions. In the early stage of the research, cities such as Xuancheng and Nantong took the lead in management efficiency through differentiated strategies. Regional core cities such as Shanghai, Nanjing, Hangzhou, and Hefei maintained stable technological output relying on mature innovation systems. In the later stage, peripheral cities formed new growth points by leveraging technology spillovers. Overall, it shows an evolutionary trend from core leadership to local breakthroughs and then to multi-city linkage. However, the region still faces challenges such as homogeneous resource development, input redundancy, and insufficient technology transformation. Establishing regional technology sharing and optimizing factor management allocation can promote balanced improvement and high-quality development of cultural-tourism integration in the central area of the urban agglomeration, continuously leveraging the radiation and driving role of the central area.

Spatial correlation analysis of cultural-tourism integration development efficiency

Based on the local Moran’s index, spatial autocorrelation analysis was conducted on the efficiency change and technological change of cultural-tourism integration development in the YRD urban agglomeration from 2015 to 2019. The LISA cluster maps present the spatial patterns of efficiency change and technological change for the periods 2015–2016, 2018–2019, and their cross-period averages, respectively (Fig. 2). The spatial agglomeration patterns are categorized into four types: High-High (H-H), where high-value units are surrounded by similar high-value units; Low-Low (L-L), where low-value areas are encircled by other low-value areas; Low-High (L-H), where low-value units are surrounded by high-value areas; and High-Low (H-L), where high-value units are adjacent to low-value neighboring areas. This classification reveals the spatial distribution patterns and their significance characteristics.

The spatiotemporal distribution of efficiency evolution indicates that during 2015–2016, Chizhou and Anqing formed an L-L cluster, indicating insufficient integration of cultural-tourism resources and limited policy support in the southwestern Anhui region. The L-H agglomeration traits seen in Taizhou (JS) stem from surrounding cities devoting heavily and growing more rapidly in public budget spending on culture, sports, and entertainment, whereas its own efficiency gains fell behind the high-value environment of its neighbors. During 2018–2019, the L-L cluster found in Yangzhou and Chuzhou mirrored insufficient regional collaboration in cultural-tourism development between the Jianghuai and central Jiangsu regions, a situation generated homogeneous competition among tourism projects. The cross-period comprehensive average indicates that Nanjing’s efficiency improvement is impeded by inefficient resource conversion and inadequate regional cooperation, which in turn leads to L-L agglomeration. In contrast, Wenzhou, lacking distinctive urban resources and a clear positioning which weakened its differentiated competitive advantage, and being adjacent to high-efficiency cities like Ningbo and Taizhou (ZJ) rich in cultural heritage, became a buffer zone for technology spillover effects.

In the spatiotemporal pattern of technological change, during 2015–2016, Yangzhou’s H-H agglomeration represented a high-value cluster, relying on its profound cultural connotation and the business formats upgrading driven by the Grand Canal Cultural Belt construction. Huzhou was an H-L type, where its technological progress was relatively prominent but was affected by low-technology-efficiency cities in southern Anhui, making it difficult to exert technology spillover effects. Yancheng showed L-H agglomeration characteristics, influenced by the technology radiation from other cities, creating a technology lowland in the northern part of the YRD. In 2018–2019, Shaoxing, due to competitive pressure from similar attractions and limited application of tourism technology, showed L-H agglomeration, failing to improve expected benefits through technology. Observing the mean values of the study, it can be seen that the L-H type in Anqing indirectly reflects the city’s development focus leaning towards industry, making it difficult for tertiary industries like culture-tourism to advance. Wenzhou maintained the L-H state, revealing its predicament of sluggish technological progress and inadequate endogenous motivation.

In summary, the cultural-tourism integration efficiency in the YRD urban agglomeration has not yet formed a significant spatial linkage effect. Multiple factors hinder the evolution of geographical proximity among cities into development synergies. On the one hand, differences in economic and social development lead to unidirectional flow of resources, such as the various advantages of Shanghai, Suzhou, and Zhejiang continuously siphoning talent and other resources from surrounding areas. Moreover, disparities in development stages result in technological gaps, making it difficult for underdeveloped cities, constrained by their industrial base and technological conversion capacity, to effectively absorb technological expansion. On the other hand, differences in resource types solidify urban development patterns, and cities of varying development types suffer from a deficiency in cooperation and exchange mechanisms. Early studies show that cities initially developed independently, and subsequently, policies activated the organic combinations of technological progress and efficiency improvements. Nevertheless, disparities in urban tiers and weak synergy continue to restrict the balanced development of cultural-tourism integration across the entire regions.

Conclusion and discussion

Conclusions

Based on an empirical study of 27 cities in the YRD from 2015 to 2019, this research reveals the temporal and spatial evolution and driving mechanisms of cultural-tourism integration efficiency. The core findings are summarized as follows: First, static efficiency shows significant spatial heterogeneity; resource-driven models in less developed cities matching or exceeding the efficiency of more developed yet input-redundant cities. Second, most cities exhibit unstable returns to scale; due to unreasonable input-output relationships or homogeneous competition, only 18.52% of cities achieve increasing returns to scale year by year through technological leadership and cultural value transformation. Third, 70.37% of cities achieve improvements in both allocation efficiency and technology utilization, with technological progress identified as the primary driver of dynamic efficiency enhancement, far surpassing managerial efficiency. Finally, spatial spillover effects are relatively limited; high-efficiency zones are scattered along rivers and coasts, and overall spatial correlation has not yet been fully realized. Nevertheless, the overall development level of cultural-tourism integration exhibits an upward trajectory; spatial correlation is generally low and varies in type, suggesting that geographical proximity in this economically integrated area does not automatically lead to a synergistic cultural tourism ecosystem.

Discussion

Heterogeneous development models and their theoretical implications

The empirical findings suggest that the relationship between economic scale and cultural-tourism integration efficiency is more complex than conventionally assumed. Our analysis identifies three qualitatively distinct development models that emerge from divergent combinations of resource endowment, technological capability, and management efficiency, challenging the linear development paradigm implicit in regional tourism literature.

The resource-driven efficiency model, exemplified by cities such as Chizhou and Tongling, demonstrates that municipalities can achieve efficiency levels comparable to economically advanced centers despite lower GDP and infrastructure investment. This phenomenon reflects strategic niche specialization whereby cities capitalize on highly distinctive assets rather than competing across the full spectrum of cultural-tourism offerings. Chizhou leverages Jiuhua Mountain’s Buddhist heritage to create focused religious tourism products, while Tongling transforms its industrial legacy into heritage tourism experiences. The efficiency advantage stems from precise alignment between resource inputs and differentiated market positioning, thereby minimizing redundant investment while maximizing output relevance. For cities with limited economic resources but distinctive cultural endowments, optimal development involves deepening vertical specialization rather than pursuing horizontal diversification. This calls for institutional mechanisms that protect unique cultural assets against homogenizing market pressures while developing targeted service infrastructures that amplify distinctive attributes.

The technology-led balanced model, represented by cities such as Hangzhou and Shanghai, maintains efficiency leadership through coordinated advancement of both technological innovation and management optimization. These cities integrate digital platforms, smart tourism systems, and data-driven resource allocation mechanisms to sustain competitive advantage. However, the analysis reveals a critical vulnerability: even technologically advanced cities exhibit input redundancies, particularly in hotel infrastructure and cultural venue capacity, indicating that technological sophistication does not automatically translate into optimal resource allocation. This duality underscores the limits of technology-driven efficiency gains. While technological progress functions as the primary driver of dynamic efficiency improvement across the study period, its impact remains constrained by management effectiveness in identifying and eliminating structural inefficiencies. For high-performing cities, the central challenge shifts from expanding technological capabilities to achieving dynamic equilibrium between innovation adoption and operational efficiency. This necessitates institutional frameworks that embed efficiency audits within technology deployment processes, ensuring that digital transformation initiatives are accompanied by systematic evaluation of existing resource utilization patterns.

The fluctuating transition model, observable in cities such as Nanjing, Hefei, and Wuhu, exhibits unstable efficiency evolution marked by significant input redundancies in early years followed by gradual improvement through technological adoption and structural adjustment. This pattern reflects the complex interplay between legacy infrastructure burdens, ongoing industrial restructuring, and adaptive capacity development. Theoretically, this model illuminates the transition costs inherent in moving from traditional tourism development to integrated cultural-tourism systems. The initial efficiency decline observed in some cities corresponds to periods when integration policies were implemented but supporting institutional mechanisms remained underdeveloped, creating temporary misalignment between expanded inputs and effective output generation. The subsequent recovery indicates adaptive learning processes wherein cities progressively refine integration strategies through iterative policy experimentation. This requires proactively identifying sources of redundancy through comprehensive input-output audits, complemented by phased adjustment strategies to prevent simultaneous disruption across multiple resource categories.

Spatial dynamics and regional coordination imperatives

The spatial analysis reveals a paradoxical pattern: despite the YRD’s status as an economically integrated region with extensive infrastructure connectivity and policy coordination, cultural-tourism integration efficiency exhibits weak spatial correlation and limited spillover effects. This challenges the assumption in regional integration theory that geographic proximity and economic interdependence automatically generate positive externalities in sectoral development. The observed spatial fragmentation can be attributed to three interrelated mechanisms.

First, administrative fragmentation persists as a structural barrier to resource sharing and collaborative development. Although regional integration policies have reduced formal administrative barriers, informal competition for tourism revenue and cultural prestige remains intense. Cities continue to pursue parallel rather than complementary development strategies, resulting in homogeneous product offerings that intensify zero-sum competition rather than generating agglomeration economies. This competitive dynamic manifests in the proliferation of similar cultural venues and tourism attractions across multiple cities. Instead of developing specialized niches within a regional product portfolio, each city attempts to capture identical market segments.

Second, technological and managerial capability gaps create asymmetric absorption capacities that limit the diffusion of best practices from high-efficiency to low-efficiency cities. While technology spillovers theoretically occur through labor mobility, knowledge networks, and demonstration effects, our analysis suggests that technological progress in advanced cities does not automatically benefit neighboring municipalities. This asymmetry reflects differences in absorptive capacity. Specifically, cities that lack foundational digital infrastructure and skilled personnel cannot effectively adopt innovations developed in technologically advanced contexts. This leads to divergence patterns where technology-leading cities continue to enhance efficiency through innovation, while lagging cities remain trapped in lower-efficiency equilibria.

Third, the absence of coordinated specialization mechanisms prevents the emergence of complementary development patterns that could enhance overall regional efficiency. In mature tourism regions with effective coordination, different cities typically specialize in distinct market segments or product categories, creating a diversified regional portfolio that maximizes collective attractiveness while minimizing internal competition. The YRD currently lacks such coordination frameworks, leading to redundant development across multiple cities and underutilization of unique local assets.

Addressing these spatial coordination failures requires institutional innovations that transcend conventional regional governance approaches. A YRD Cultural-Tourism Efficiency Alliance could transform competitive dynamics into collaborative learning processes through systematic data sharing and best practice exchange. Beyond information exchange, effective regional coordination necessitates material mechanisms for technology transfer and capacity building through regionally funded facilities that pool resources from high-efficiency cities to finance technological upgrading in peripheral municipalities. More importantly, the region requires a Differentiated Development Compact that formally delineates specialized roles for different cities within the integrated cultural-tourism system. Under such an arrangement, cities would commit to developing their own distinctive strengths rather than blindly replicating the successful models of neighboring cities, while ensuring complementarity rather than competition through coordination mechanisms.

Model-specific development strategies and policy architecture

The identification of three distinct development models enables differentiated policy frameworks that align intervention strategies with structural characteristics and developmental challenges specific to each model type.

For cities following the resource-driven efficiency model, strategic priority lies in deepening niche specialization while avoiding premature infrastructure expansion that could generate input redundancies. Policy interventions should concentrate on heritage asset protection mechanisms to preserve cultural resource uniqueness against commercial pressures, targeted infrastructure development prioritizing facilities that directly amplify distinctive cultural experiences rather than generic tourism amenities, and strategic partnership frameworks connecting resource-driven cities with technologically advanced neighbors to enable access to digital platforms and marketing systems without requiring independent development of full technological infrastructure.

For cities demonstrating the technology-led balanced model, the central challenge involves maintaining innovation momentum while addressing persistent input redundancies and extending benefits to the broader regional system. Priority should be given to next-generation digital tourism platforms that integrate artificial intelligence, big data analytics, and personalized service delivery systems. Simultaneously, comprehensive resource utilization audits must identify specific sources of input redundancy, generating concrete adjustment plans potentially involving adaptive reuse of excess facilities or coordinated capacity management systems. Beyond internal efficiency enhancement, technology-leading cities should establish regional innovation hubs functioning as both research and development centers for new technologies and training platforms for peripheral cities, positioning their technological capabilities as regional public goods.

For cities characterized by fluctuating transition trajectories, the fundamental requirement is accelerated stabilization through systematic identification and elimination of redundancy sources combined with strategic technological catch-up. Comprehensive input-output audits should precisely map inefficiency sources across different resource categories, enabling targeted rather than blanket adjustment strategies. Based on audit findings, phased efficiency improvement plans should be implemented with clear annual benchmarking targets. Technological adoption strategies should prioritize cost-effective, high-impact innovations rather than comprehensive digitalization that could strain limited resources. Simultaneously, these cities must develop clear differentiated positioning within the regional cultural-tourism network to avoid homogeneous competition that perpetuates efficiency challenges. Nanjing should emphasize its republican-era historical heritage and contemporary cultural creativity, Hefei focus on science and innovation tourism linked to its research institutions, and Wuhu specialize in Yangtze River cultural tourism and ecological experiences. Such differentiation reduces direct competition with other cities while creating distinctive market positions that support efficiency enhancement.

Systemic integration

Beyond model-specific strategies, achieving comprehensive efficiency enhancement across the YRD urban agglomeration requires systemic interventions addressing cross-cutting barriers to integration. The development of integrated efficiency monitoring and early warning systems is essential for enabling proactive management of integration processes. Establishing a real-time digital monitoring platform that continuously tracks key efficiency indicators across cities would enable early identification of emerging inefficiencies and timely corrective interventions, incorporating predictive analytics that forecast efficiency trajectories under different policy scenarios to support evidence-based decision making.

Institutional frameworks for regional resource circulation must be strengthened to enable dynamic reallocation of inputs based on efficiency performance. Developing mechanisms for temporary resource sharing, such as cultural exhibition circulation systems wherein museums and cultural institutions share collections across cities, or skilled personnel exchange programs enabling knowledge transfer while optimizing talent deployment, could substantially enhance overall regional efficiency. Such circulation mechanisms require formal institutional agreements defining resource sharing protocols, benefit distribution arrangements, and quality assurance standards.

Innovation in cultural-tourism product development should emphasize cross-city collaborative offerings that leverage complementary assets across multiple municipalities. Rather than each city attempting to provide comprehensive cultural-tourism experiences independently, integrated products combining distinctive elements from multiple cities could enhance overall attractiveness while reducing redundant investment. A Grand Canal cultural tourism route integrating heritage sites across Yangzhou, Suzhou, and Hangzhou, for instance, would enable tourists to experience the full historical and cultural breadth of this World Heritage asset while distributing benefits across cities according to their respective contributions. Developing such integrated products requires new governance frameworks enabling cross-jurisdictional coordination, revenue sharing, and quality management.

Limitations and future research

While this study provides valuable insights into the efficiency of cultural and tourism integration in the YRD, several limitations should be acknowledged to outline productive directions for future research.

First, constrained by current municipal statistical categorizations in China, this study employed the value added of the tertiary industry as a macro proxy for integration outputs. This methodological choice is reasonable because the integration of culture and tourism, as a profound economic catalyst, truly lies in its extensive spillover effects on the broader service economy. However, it admittedly lacks industry specific granularity. Future studies could benefit from the ongoing development of municipal Tourism Satellite Accounts and big data analytics to obtain precise micro level metrics. Furthermore, expanding the analytical framework to incorporate environmental sustainability dimensions would provide a comprehensive assessment of integration quality. This recognition stems from the fact that pure economic efficiency might entail environmental costs that undermine sustainable development.

Second, the empirical observation window was strictly confined to the period between 2015 and 2019. This design was deliberately chosen to extract the pure endogenous evolutionary patterns of integration policies and to avoid the severe structural anomalies caused by the global pandemic. While this establishes a robust historical baseline, the subsequent sector recovery and digital transformation may have introduced novel evolutionary characteristics. Expanding the temporal scope to include subsequent years represents a valuable direction for future research. Such an expansion would enable scholars to examine how systemic shocks reshaped efficiency patterns, as well as whether these structural changes enhanced or degraded integration efficiency.

Finally, subsequent research should conduct comparative analyses extending beyond the YRD to other major Chinese urban agglomerations or international metropolitan regions to identify context specific versus generalizable efficiency determinants. A deeper investigation into the causal mechanisms linking specific policy interventions to efficiency outcomes, conducted through quasi-experimental designs, would also greatly strengthen the evidence base for policy recommendations. This would move the field beyond correlation-based insights toward robust causal inference regarding intervention effectiveness.
