Point Cloud Neural Operator for Parametric PDEs on Complex and Variable Geometries
Chenyu Zeng, Yanshu Zhang, Jiayi Zhou, Yuhan Wang, Zilin Wang, Yuhao Liu, Lei Wu, Daniel Zhengyu Huang

Abstract
Surrogate models are critical for accelerating computationally expensive simulations in science and engineering, particularly for solving parametric partial differential equations (PDEs). Developing practical surrogate models poses significant challenges, particularly in handling geometrically complex and variable domains, which are often discretized as point clouds. In this work, we systematically investigate the formulation of neural operators— maps between infinite-dimensional function spaces—on point clouds to better handle complex and variable geometries while mitigating discretization effects. We introduce the Point Cloud Neural Operator (PCNO), designed to efficiently approximate solution maps of parametric PDEs on such domains. We evaluate the performance of PCNO on a range of pedagogical PDE problems, focusing on aspects such as boundary layers, adaptively meshed point clouds, and variable domains with topological variations. Its practicality is further demonstrated through three-dimensional applications, such as predicting pressure loads on various vehicle types and simulating the inflation process of intricate parachute structures.

Neural networks hold great promise for developing efficient surrogate models for PDE problems, due to their ability to approximate high-dimensional functions effectively. \Omega

5.3

An alternative approach avoids the need for constructing parametric deformation maps by representing complex and variable computational domains

Point clouds inherently capture the geometry of

Furthermore, graph neural networks (GNNs)

2
Neural Operator

In this section, we provide a concise overview of neural operators, which are neural network-based surrogate models designed to approximate mappings where both inputs and outputs are functions.
The primary focus of this work is the mapping

\mathcal{G^{\dagger}}:(a,\Omega)\mapsto u.

(3)

Here
a
represents the parameter function (which includes source terms, boundary conditions, and other factors),
\Omega
denotes the computational domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
u
represents the solution function. Generally, the parameter function space is denoted as
\mathcal{A}=\{a:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}\}
, and the solution function space as
\mathcal{U}=\{u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}\}
; both are separable Banach spaces.
Neural operator approach aims to approximate
\mathcal{G^{\dagger}}
using a parametric neural network

G_{\theta}
, where
\theta\in\Theta
is assumed to be a finite dimensional parameter. To train neural operators, we consider the following standard data scenario. The input data
\{a_{i},\Omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is sampled from certain distribution
\mu
, and the corresponding outputs are given by
\{u_{i}=\mathcal{G}^{\dagger}(a_{i},\Omega_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
Given a cost function
c:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and the dataset
\{a_{i},\Omega_{i},u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the optimal
\theta^{*}
is obtained by solving the optimization problem:

\theta^{*}=\argmin\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(\mathcal{G}_{\theta}(a_{i},\Omega_{i}),u_{i}).

(4)

The goal is for

G_{\theta^{*}}
to approximate

\mathcal{G^{\dagger}}
effectively within the distribution
\mu
.

In practical applications, the computational domain
\Omega
is often represented by a point cloud
X
, with the connectivity between points defining the mesh structure.
The functions
a
and
u
are typically available only at the discrete points of
X
. Neural operators, however, are designed to approximate the operator (
3
) at the continuous level, as a mapping between function spaces, rather than at the discrete points. This framework grants neural operators with discretization invariant properties, allowing the learned operator to generalize robustly across different discretizations.

In general, to approximate the operator in (
3
), each layer of neural operator
\mathcal{L}
combines pointwise linear functions, integral operators and a nonlinear pointwise activation function, which maps the input function
f_{\rm in}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm in}}
to the output function
f_{\rm out}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}}
, such as

\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}:f_{\rm in}\mapsto f_{\rm out},\\
&f_{\rm out}(x)=\sigma\Bigl(W^{l}f_{\rm in}(x)+b+\int_{\Omega}\kappa\bigl(x,y,f_{\rm in}\bigr)W^{v}f_{\rm in}(y)dy\Bigr).\end{split}

(5)

Here,
W^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}\times d_{\rm in}}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}}
define a pointwise local linear function.
1
1

1

In general, the integral operator with continuous kernel between two infinite dimensional spaces (e.g., continuous function space
C(\Omega)
) is a compact operator, however identity mapping between two infinite dimensional spaces is not
[
86
, Section 2]
. The pointwise local linear function is able to represent such identity mappings and hence is necessary to improve the performance.

Note that
d_{\rm in}
and
d_{\rm out}
refer to the number of channels of the input and output functions, respectively. Such a linear function is also used to increase the number of channels
d_{a}
of the input function
a
in the initial lifting layer. This is crucial because a higher-dimensional channel space can capture more complex features and make the relationships more tractable
[
41
,
87
,
50
,
88
]
. In the final projection layer, a linear function projects the output back to the desired number of channels
d_{u}
.

W^{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}\times d_{\rm in}}
parameterizes the integral operator, which is defined by the kernel
\kappa(x,y,f_{\rm in}):\Omega\times\Omega\times(\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm in}})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
The integral operator in (
5
) can be seen as an analogy to matrix multiplication in the context of neural networks operating on finite-dimensional vector spaces.
The pointwise nonlinear activation function
\sigma
, such as the Gaussian Error Linear Unit (
GeLU
)
[
89
]
, acts on each point and each channel. It is worth noting that the operator
\mathcal{L}
described in (
5
) provides a nonlinear parametric mapping between function spaces.
The design of the neural operator is carried out at the continuous level and then discretized.

Next, we review several specific integral operators utilized in the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO), Graph Neural Operator (GNO), and Transformer.

2.1
Fourier Neural Operator

The Fourier Neural Operator
[
31
,
90
,
91
,
92
]
considers a series of Fourier kernel functions defined as

\kappa_{k}(x,y)=e^{2\pi ik\cdot(x-y)}\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
which can approximate positive semidefinite translationally invariant kernels
[
93
,
94
]
. This leads to the following Fourier integral operator:

2
2

2

The formula for the Fourier integral operator in the literature
[
31
,
95
]
is typically written as
f_{\rm out}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\Bigl(R\cdot\bigl(\mathcal{F}f_{\rm in}\bigr)\Bigr)
, where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Fourier transform for each channel. This maps the periodic function
f_{\rm in}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm in}}
to Fourier coefficients,
\bigl(\mathcal{F}f_{\rm in}\bigr)(k)\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rm in}}
, generally truncated at
k_{\rm max}
modes.
R
define
k_{\rm max}
complex-valued matrices
R(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}\times d_{\rm in}}
, which is applied on each of the Fourier modes as
R(k)\bigl(\mathcal{F}f_{\rm in}\bigr)(k)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}}
.
Finally,
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
denotes the inverse Fourier transform for each channel, resulting in
f_{\rm out}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}}
. In our equivalent formulation in (
6
), we separate each Fourier mode, denote
W_{k}^{v}=R(k)
, and use the fact that
W_{k}^{v}\bigl(\mathcal{F}f_{\rm in}\bigr)(k)=\bigr(\mathcal{F}(W_{k}^{v}f_{\rm in})\bigr)(k)
.

\displaystyle f_{\rm out}(x)=\sum_{k}\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{k}(x,y)W_{k}^{v}f_{\rm in}(y)dy,

(6)

where each frequency
k
corresponds to a complex parameter matrix
W_{k}^{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rm out}\times d_{\rm in}}
. The integral (
6
) can be efficiently evaluated using the Fast Fourier Transform, achieving quasi-linear complexity with respect to the number of mesh points when
\Omega
is a hypercube discretized on a structured grid. Using the fact that the Fourier kernel has a low-rank representation
\kappa_{k}(x,y)=\phi_{k}(x)\overline{\phi_{k}(y)}
, where
\phi_{k}(x)=e^{2\pi ik\cdot x}
, and focusing only on low-frequency modes
k
, the Fourier Neural Operator restricted to low frequencies can generalize beyond regular grids
[
96
]
with linear complexity using the following formulation

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\kappa_{k}(x,y)W_{k}^{v}f_{\rm in}(y)dy=\phi(x)\int_{\Omega}\overline{\phi(y)}W_{k}^{v}f_{\rm in}(y)dy=e^{2\pi ik\cdot x}\int_{\Omega}e^{-2\pi ik\cdot y}W_{k}^{v}f_{\rm in}(y)dy.

(7)

More generally, any serials of kernels
\kappa_{k}(x,y)=\phi_{k}(x)\phi_{k}(y)
with a low-rank representation can achieve linear computational complexity. For example, in
[
97
]
, the authors study
\phi_{k}(x)
as either the
k
-th eigenvector of the Laplacian operator on
\Omega
or the POD basis learned from the training data.

2.2
Graph Neural Operator

In the graph neural network framework
[
70
,
71
,
72
,
73
]
, information is propagated locally through the connectivity of the point cloud to learn the mapping in (
3
). This results in a discretization-dependent approach that requires careful tuning of the message-passing depth.
This mechanism is generalized to the graph neural operator
[
76
,
77
]
, by replacing the averaging aggregation as the integral operator with a local kernel, where
\kappa(x,y)=0
when
\lVert x-y\rVert>r
. The integral can be evaluated as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\kappa(x,y)W^{v}f_{\rm in}(y)dy=\int_{\Omega~\bigcap~\lVert x-y\rVert\leq r}\kappa(x,y)W^{v}f_{\rm in}(y)dy,

(8)

with
\mathcal{O}(m)
integration points sampled from the region
\Omega~\bigcap~\lVert x-y\rVert\leq r
. The complexity of evaluating the integral (
8
) becomes linear with respect to the number of mesh points. However, its performance might be sensitive to the hyperparameters
r
and
m
, which may vary depending on the mesh resolution.

2.3
Transformer

In the Transformer framework’s attention mechanism
[
23
,
98
]
, the function values
f_{\rm in}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm in}}
are interpreted as word embeddings.
Given a point cloud
\{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
representing the domain
\Omega
, the attention mechanism with a softmax operator computes the output at each point
x^{(i)}
as

\displaystyle f_{\rm out}(x^{(i)})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{e^{\langle W^{q}f_{\rm in}(x^{(i)}),W^{k}f_{\rm in}(x^{(j)})\rangle/\sqrt{d_{\rm out}}}}{\sum_{j^{{}^{\prime}}=1}^{N}e^{\langle W^{q}f_{\rm in}(x^{(i)}),W^{k}f_{\rm in}(x^{(j^{{}^{\prime}})})\rangle/\sqrt{d_{\rm out}}}}W^{v}f_{\rm in}(x^{(j)}),

(9)

where
W^{q},W^{k},W^{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}\times d_{\rm in}}
are the query, key, and value matrices, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}}
.
In the continuous limit, as the number of points approaches infinity and assuming uniform sampling from the domain
\Omega
, the attention mechanism leads to

\displaystyle f_{\rm out}(x)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{e^{\langle W^{q}f_{\rm in}(x),W^{k}f_{\rm in}(y)\rangle/\sqrt{d_{\rm out}}}}{\int_{\Omega}e^{\langle W^{q}f_{\rm in}(x),W^{k}f_{\rm in}(z)\rangle/\sqrt{d_{\rm out}}}dz}W^{v}f_{\rm in}(y)dy,

(10)

which gives rise to the following nonlinear parametric kernel:

\displaystyle\kappa(x,y,f_{\rm in})=\frac{e^{\langle W^{q}f_{\rm in}(x),W^{k}f_{\rm in}(y)\rangle/\sqrt{d_{\rm out}}}}{\int_{\Omega}e^{\langle W^{q}f_{\rm in}(x),W^{k}f_{\rm in}(z)\rangle/\sqrt{d_{\rm out}}}dz}.

(11)

Positional information can also be used in the kernel to develop neural operators
[
69
]
.
However, evaluating the integral operator (
5
) with this kernel (
11
) typically involves quadratic complexity with respect to the number of mesh points. Removing the softmax operation
[
68
,
99
]
simplifies the kernel to

\displaystyle\kappa(x,y,f_{\rm in})=\langle W^{q}f_{\rm in}(x),W^{k}f_{\rm in}(y)\rangle,

(12)

which enables a low-rank representation and reduces the computational cost to linear complexity.

3
Neural Operator on Point Cloud

In this section, we introduce a novel neural operator specifically designed to learn the mapping described in (
3
), with a particular focus on handling complex and variable geometries,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Notably,
\Omega
can represent a low-dimensional manifold within
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such as the surface of a vehicle in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(See
section

5.4
).
To account for both global and local effects, and inspired by the work of
[
33
]
, our approach separates integral and differential operators, and leverages traditional numerical methods for their discretizations.
This design ensures that the neural operator, initially defined in function space, can robustly handle complex and variable computational domains with arbitrary discretizations, and that the subsequent discretization results in small mesh-size-dependent errors.
Since the geometry
\Omega
is typically represented as a point cloud with connectivity information,
X=\{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where the number of points
N
may vary across datasets, we refer to our approach as the Point Cloud Neural Operator (PCNO), with further details provided in the following sections.

3.1
Integral Operator

We associate the geometry
\Omega
with a density function
\rho(x;\Omega)
defined on
\Omega
. The integral operator is defined as:

\begin{split}&f_{\rm out}(x)=\int_{\Omega}\kappa\bigl(x,y,f_{\rm in}\bigr)W^{v}f_{\rm in}(y)\rho(y;\Omega)dy,\end{split}

(13)

where
\kappa
is the kernel, and
W^{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}\times d_{\rm in}}
is a parameter matrix.
The density function
\rho(x;\Omega)
ensures that the integral does not inappropriately scale the function by the domain volume
|\Omega|
, which enhances stability during training and facilitates numerical integration.

The density function
\rho(x;\Omega)
can be set as a uniform density

\rho(x;\Omega)=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}
defined on
\Omega
. In this case,
\rho(x;\Omega)
acts as the indicator function of
\Omega
. Given a point cloud
\{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
representing the computational domain
\Omega
, the integral operator (
13
) can be numerically approximated as:

\begin{split}&f_{\rm out}(x)\approx\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\kappa\bigl(x,x^{(i)},f_{\rm in}\bigr)W^{v}f_{\rm in}(x^{(i)})\mathrm{d}\Omega_{i},\end{split}

(14)

where
\mathrm{d}\Omega_{i}
represents the estimated mesh size associated with point
x^{(i)}
, derived from the point cloud. Details on the computation of
\mathrm{d}\Omega_{i}
are provided in
A
.

In practice, the mesh is often adaptively generated, with mesh points more densely distributed in regions of higher importance (See
section

5.3
).
Accordingly,
\rho(y;\Omega)
can be set to represent the density from which the point cloud is sampled.
This allows the integral operator in (
13
) to assign greater weight to these critical regions through the term
\rho(y;\Omega)
.
Given the point cloud, the density can be estimated as
\rho(x^{(i)};\Omega)=\frac{1}{N\mathrm{d}\Omega_{i}}
, which satisfies the normalization condition

\int_{\Omega}\rho(x,\Omega)\mathrm{d}x:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho(x^{(i)};\Omega)\mathrm{d}\Omega_{i}=1
.
Then the integral operator in (
13
) can be numerically approximated as:

\begin{split}&f_{\rm out}(x)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N}\kappa\bigl(x,x^{(i)},f_{\rm in}\bigr)W^{v}f_{\rm in}(x^{(i)})\rho(x^{(i)};\Omega)\mathrm{d}\Omega_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\kappa\bigl(x,x^{(i)},f_{\rm in}\bigr)W^{v}f_{\rm in}(x^{(i)}).\end{split}

(15)

This expression is equivalent to the Monte Carlo method for numerical integration.

When
\rho(x;\Omega)
is defined as the point cloud density, it automatically incorporates mesh adaptivity information (if adaptivity is present), thereby improving the accuracy of the results (See
section

5.3
).
However, this approach requires the adaptive strategy to be consistent across all cases, as each
\Omega
is associated with a unique
\rho(x;\Omega)
. If the adaptive strategy is not universal, the uniform density should be used instead, as this approach is free from such limitations (See
section

5.1
).

3.2
Differential Operator

We further define a differential operator to capture local effects.
The gradient
\nabla f_{\rm in}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\rm in}}
can be computed independently for each of the
d_{\rm in}
channels. Without loss of generality, we assume
d_{\rm in}=1
for the following discussion. The gradient is estimated via a least-squares approach implemented as a single message-passing step.
Given the connectivity of the point cloud (See
A
for details on computing connectivity), let the neighbors of
x
be denoted as
x^{(1)},x^{(2)},\cdots x^{(m)}
. The gradient
\nabla f_{\rm in}(x)
is estimated by solving the least-squares problem:

\begin{split}&A(x)\nabla f_{\rm in}(x)=b_{f}(x)\\
&\textrm{ where }A(x)=\begin{pmatrix}x^{(1)}-x\\
x^{(2)}-x\\
\vdots\\
x^{(m)}-x\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}\quad\textrm{and}\quad b_{f}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}f_{\rm in}(x^{(1)})-f_{\rm in}(x)\\
f_{\rm in}(x^{(2)})-f_{\rm in}(x)\\
\vdots\\
f_{\rm in}(x^{(m)})-f_{\rm in}(x)\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.\end{split}

(16)

The least-squares problem can be solved by computing the pseudoinverse of the matrix
A(x)^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
. Special consideration is required when
\Omega
is a submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
d^{{}^{\prime}}\leq d
denote the intrinsic dimension of
\Omega
. For example, lines have dimension
d^{{}^{\prime}}=1
, and surfaces have dimension
d^{{}^{\prime}}=2
. The truncated singular value decomposition of
A(x)
with rank
d^{{}^{\prime}}
is given by

A(x)=U\Sigma V^{T},\textrm{ where }U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d^{{}^{\prime}}},\quad\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{{}^{\prime}}\times d^{{}^{\prime}}},\quad V^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{{}^{\prime}}\times d}.

(17)

The pseudoinverse is then computed as
A(x)^{\dagger}=V\Sigma^{-1}U^{T}
.
When
\Omega
is a submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the estimated gradient
\nabla f_{\rm in}(x)=A(x)^{\dagger}b_{f}(x)
satisfies

n(x)^{T}\cdot\nabla f_{\rm in}(x)=0

(18)

for any normal vector
n(x)
of
\Omega
at
x
, since
A(x)\cdot n(x)=0
. Consequently, the gradient
\nabla f_{\rm in}(x)
lies within the tangent space of
\Omega
at
x
.
For efficient implementation, we use the fact that
A(x)^{\dagger}
depends solely on the point cloud and its connectivity, allowing it to be preprocessed before neural operator training.
Additionally, the gradient
\nabla f_{\rm in}(x)=A(x)^{\dagger}b_{f}(x)
can be efficiently assembled using a message-passing procedure.
Specifically, the
i
-th column of
A(x)^{\dagger}
, denoted as
A_{i}(x)^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, can be stored at the edge connecting
x
and
x^{(i)}
. For any function
f_{\rm in}
, its gradient at
x
is computed by aggregating information from its neighbors, as follows

\nabla f_{\rm in}(x)=\sum_{i:x^{(i)}\textrm{ is a neighbor of }x}A_{i}(x)^{\dagger}\bigl(f_{\rm in}(x^{(i)})-f_{\rm in}(x)\bigr).

(19)

While the gradient is an intrinsic property of
f_{\rm in}
, and is independent of connectivity or mesh, discontinuities in
f_{\rm in}
result in infinite gradients. In such cases, the gradient estimation scales as
\mathcal{O}(1/\Delta x)
where
\Delta x
represents the local mesh size around
x
. To address this issue, we apply the
SoftSign
activation function to each gradient component:

\widetilde{\nabla}f_{\rm in}(x)=\texttt{SoftSign}(\nabla f_{\rm in}(x))\qquad\textrm{ where }\qquad\texttt{SoftSign}(x)=\frac{x}{1+|x|}.

(20)

The smoothed gradient
\widetilde{\nabla}
preserves gradient information while serving as an indicator for sharp or discontinuous local features in
f_{\rm in}
.

3.3
Point Cloud Neural Operator

Finally, by combining the aforementioned integral operator and differential operator, we introduce the following point cloud neural layer, which maps the input function
f_{\rm in}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm in}}
to the output function
f_{\rm out}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}}
:

\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}:f_{\rm in}\mapsto f_{\rm out},\\
&f_{\rm out}(x)=\sigma\Bigl(W^{l}f_{\rm in}(x)+b+\sum_{k}\int_{\Omega}e^{2\pi i\frac{k}{L}\cdot(x-y)}W_{k}^{v}f_{\rm in}(y)\rho(y;\Omega)dy+W^{g}\widetilde{\nabla}f_{\rm in}(x)\Bigr),\end{split}

(21)

where the components of the layer are defined as follows:

•

The first term represents a pointwise local linear function parameterized by
W^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}\times d_{\rm in}}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}}
.

•

The second term represents the integral operator.
In this work, we focus on the Fourier kernel discussed in
section

2.1
:
\kappa\bigl(x,y,f_{\rm in}\bigr)=e^{2\pi i\frac{k}{L}\cdot x}e^{-2\pi i\frac{k}{L}\cdot y}
. Here
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
denotes the frequency,
L\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
denotes the learnable length scales, and each frequency
k
is associated with a complex-valued parameter matrix
W_{k}^{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rm out}\times d_{\rm in}}
.

•

The third term represents the differential operator, parameterized by
W^{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}\times(d\cdot d_{\rm in})}
. Here, the smoothed gradient
\widetilde{\nabla}f_{\rm in}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\rm in}}
in (
20
) is flattened into a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d\cdot d_{\rm in}}
for computation.

The nonlinear activation function
\sigma
used in this work is
GeLU
function
[
89
]
.

As shown in
fig.

1
, the Point Cloud Neural Operator
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}
considered in this work consists of several point cloud neural layers (
21
).
The input function is defined as

\tilde{a}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1},~~\textrm{ where }\tilde{a}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}a(x)\\
x\\
\rho(x;\Omega)\end{bmatrix},

(22)

and consists of three components. The first component is the parameter function
a:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}
, which includes information such as source terms and boundary conditions. The latter two components are the identity function
\mathrm{id}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the density function
\rho(x;\Omega)
, both of which encode geometric information about the computational domain
\Omega
and the point cloud.
At the implementation level, the input
\bm{\tilde{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times(d_{a}+d+1)}
consists of evaluations of the input functions
\tilde{a}
at the point cloud
X=\{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

Figure 1:

Architecture of the Point Cloud Neural Operator (PCNO): Starting with input function
[a(x),x,\rho(x,\Omega)]
, it is first lifted to a higher-dimensional channel space using a pointwise neural network
P
. Several point cloud neural layers are then applied, followed by a projection back to the target dimensional space using a pointwise neural network
Q
. The final output is
u
.

The first layer is a lifting layer, which only contains a pointwise neural network:

\begin{split}f_{\rm out}(x)=\sigma\Bigl(W^{l}\tilde{a}(x)+b\Bigr).\end{split}

(23)

This is crucial for lifting the channel space from
d_{a}+d+1
to a higher-dimensional channel space
d_{\rm out}
, enabling the capture of more complex features and making the underlying relationships more tractable
[
41
,
87
,
50
]
.
In the final projection layer, the output function is projected back to the desired number of channels,

d_{u}
. In the present work, the projection layer consists of two consecutive pointwise neural network layers, given by:

\begin{split}&u(x)=W^{{}^{\prime}l}\sigma\Bigl(W^{l}f_{\rm in}(x)+b\Bigr)+b^{{}^{\prime}},\end{split}

(24)

where
W^{{}^{\prime}l}\in\mathbb{R}^{\cdot\times d_{u}}
and
b^{{}^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
.

4
Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we provide a theoretical analysis of the proposed Point Cloud Neural Operator, focusing on its invariance properties, computational complexity, and approximation capabilities. These aspects are studied through the following theorems.

Permutation invariance with respect to input points is a fundamental requirement for neural operators or networks that process point clouds, as emphasized in
[
64
]
. The proposed Point Cloud Neural Operator inherently satisfies this property.

Theorem 1

(Permutation invariance)
.

Assume the point cloud consists of
N
points, the Point Cloud Neural Operator
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}
maps

\Bigl[u(x^{(1)}),u(x^{(2)}),\cdots,u(x^{(N)})\Bigr]=\mathcal{G}_{\theta}\Bigl(\tilde{a}(x^{(1)}),\tilde{a}(x^{(2)}),\cdots,\tilde{a}(x^{(N)})\Bigr).

(25)

Given any permutation
\tau:(1,2,\cdots,N)\rightarrow(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\cdots,\tau_{N})
, the Point Cloud Neural Operator
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}
satisfies

\Bigl[u(x^{(\tau_{1})}),u(x^{(\tau_{2})}),\cdots,u(x^{(\tau_{N})})\Bigr]=\mathcal{G}_{\theta}\Bigl(\tilde{a}(x^{(\tau_{1})}),\tilde{a}(x^{(\tau_{2})}),\cdots,\tilde{a}(x^{(\tau_{N})})\Bigr).

(26)

Proof.

We only need to prove that the point cloud neural layer in (
21
) is permutation invariant. The point cloud neural layer consists of the local linear function, integral and differential operators at the discrete level (as given in (
14
), (
15
), and (
19
)), and the activation functions, given by the following expressions:

\displaystyle f_{\rm out}(x^{(j)})

\displaystyle:=W^{l}f_{\rm in}(x^{(j)})+b,

(27a)

\displaystyle f_{\rm out}(x^{(j)})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\kappa\bigl(x^{(j)},x^{(i)},f_{\rm in}\bigr)W^{v}f_{\rm in}(x^{(i)})\rho(x^{(i)};\Omega)\mathrm{d}\Omega_{i},

(27b)

\displaystyle f_{\rm out}(x^{(j)})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i:x^{(i)}\textrm{ is a neighbor of }x^{(j)}}A_{i}(x^{(j)})^{\dagger}\bigl(f_{\rm in}(x^{(i)})-f_{\rm in}(x^{(j)})\bigr),

(27c)

\displaystyle f_{\rm out}(x^{(j)})

\displaystyle:=\sigma\bigl(f_{\rm in}(x^{(j)})\bigr).

(27d)

For any permutation of the points
i\rightarrow\tau_{i}~(1\leq i\leq N)
, their connectivity remains unchanged. Specifically, when
x^{(i)}
is a neighbor of
x^{(j)}
, then
x^{(\tau_{i})}
is a neighbor of
x^{(\tau_{j})}
.
Additionally, the edge weights

A_{i}(x^{(j)})^{\dagger}
, density

\rho(x^{(i)};\Omega)
, and mesh size

\mathrm{d}\Omega_{i}
will be permuted accordingly. Thus, the functions in (
27
) remain unchanged under such a permutation, which implies that the point cloud neural layer and its composition are permutation invariant.
∎

Next, we analyze the computational complexity of the proposed Point Cloud Neural Operator. The following theorem demonstrates that the proposed operator achieves linear complexity during inference, ensuring scalability to large-scale problems.

Theorem 2

(Linear complexity)
.

Assume the point cloud consists of
N
points in
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
E=\mathcal{O}(N)
edges in the connectivity. The inference cost of the point cloud neural layer (
21
) from
f_{\rm in}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm in}}
to
f_{\rm out}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rm out}}
on the point cloud, with a truncated Fourier modes number
k_{\max}
is
\mathcal{O}\Bigl(Nd_{\rm in}d_{\rm out}(k_{\max}+d)+Ed_{\rm in}d\Bigr)
.

Proof.

For the point cloud neural layer described in (
21
), the inference complexity of the local linear function is
\mathcal{O}\bigl(Nd_{\rm in}d_{\rm out}\bigr)
, the inference complexity of the integral at the discrete level, as given in (
14
) and (
15
), is
\mathcal{O}\bigl(Nk_{\rm max}d_{\rm out}d_{\rm in}\bigr)
,
the complexity of the differential operator at the discrete level with precomputed edge weights
A_{i}(x^{(j)})^{\dagger}
is
\mathcal{O}\bigl(Edd_{\rm in}+Nd_{\rm out}dd_{\rm in}\bigr)
, where the first term is from the message-passing step in (
19
). The inference complexity of the activation function is
\mathcal{O}\bigl(Nd_{\rm out}\bigr)
. In total, the inference complexity is
\mathcal{O}\Bigl(Nd_{\rm in}d_{\rm out}(k_{\max}+d)+Ed_{\rm in}d\Bigr)
.
∎

Finally, we establish the universal approximation property, a fundamental criterion for neural network design. Our proposed Point Cloud Neural Operator satisfies this property, as a direct consequence of
[
100
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Theorem 3

(Universal approximation at continuous level)
.

Assume that
\Omega
, drawn from the data distribution
\mu
is uniformly bounded, full-dimensional, and has a Lipschitz boundary. And both
a
and
u
are
L_{p}(\Omega)
functions for a given
p\geq 1
. And
\inf_{\Omega,\,x\in\Omega}\rho(x;\Omega)=\rho_{\inf}>0
. We embed
\Omega
into a bounded hypercube
B
and extend the input function
\tilde{a}
defined in (
22
), and the solution function
u
as follows

\tilde{a}_{B}(x)=\begin{cases}\tilde{a}(x)&x\in\Omega\\
0&x\in B\backslash\Omega\end{cases}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad u_{B}(x)=\begin{cases}u(x)&x\in\Omega\\
0&x\in B\backslash\Omega\end{cases},

(28)

where
\tilde{a}_{B}(x)=[a_{B}(x),x\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(x),\rho_{B}(x)]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1}
, the support of
\rho_{B}
gives
\Omega
, and the restriction of
a_{B}
on
\Omega
recovers
a
. Consequently, both
\Omega
and
\tilde{a}
can be reconstructed from
\tilde{a}_{B}
.
We denote the extended operator as
\mathcal{G}^{\dagger}_{B}:L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1})\rightarrow L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
,
which satisfies

\mathcal{G}^{\dagger}_{B}(\tilde{a}_{B})=\mathcal{G}^{\dagger}(a,\Omega).

Assume further that
\mathcal{G}^{\dagger}_{B}
is a continuous operator defined on the compact set
\mathcal{K}_{B}\subset L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1})
, consisting of bounded functions satisfying
\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}\lVert\tilde{a}_{B}\rVert_{L_{\infty}}<\infty
.
Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a proposed Point Cloud Neural Operator
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}
, such that

\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}\lVert\mathcal{G}^{\dagger}(a,\Omega)-\mathcal{G}_{\theta}(\tilde{a})\rVert_{L_{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}\leq\epsilon.

(29)

We provide a proof of this theorem in
B
. The theorem highlights that a crucial aspect of the point cloud strategy (See the literature review in
section

1.1
) to construct neural operators to handle complex and variable geometries is the introduction of the bounded hypercube
B
and the embedding of the input and output functions within this hypercube.
However, a key feature of our Point Cloud Neural Operator is that our implementation operates exclusively on
\Omega
, without involving
B
or the embedding process.

5
Numerical Results

In this section, we present numerical studies of the proposed Point Cloud Neural Operator (PCNO), focusing on its performance across a diverse set of problems involving complex and variable geometries. The numerical tests span a wide range of scenarios, from pedagogical examples in one and two dimensions to real-world applications in three dimensions:

1.

One-dimensional advection-diffusion problem: A pedagogical example with multiscale features, used to investigate how the point cloud density
\rho(x;\Omega)
affects the accuracy.

2.

Two-dimensional Darcy flow problem: A pedagogical example with a varying computational domain, examining the effects of shape changes and mesh resolution.

3.

Two-dimensional flow over an airfoil: A pedagogical example involving an airfoil, which may either be a single airfoil or an airfoil with a flap, and an adaptively generated mesh. This problem explores topology changes in the computational domain and the effects of mesh adaptation.

4.

Three-dimensional vehicle application
[
79
]
: This problem involves learning the mapping from vehicle shapes to surface pressure loads. The dataset includes vehicles from ShapeNet and Ahmed body.

5.

Three-dimensional parachute dynamics: This problem studies the mapping from parachute design shapes to their inflation behavior under pressure loads.

All experiments involve variable computational domains, which may be discretized using a varying number of points.
To ensure consistent and parallelizable processing across all data samples, we pad the samples (including both inputs and outputs) with zeros so that all data samples reach the prescribed maximum length. A node mask vector is then used to distinguish between the original points and the padded ones. The implementation is designed such that garbage values on the padded points do not affect the original points.
For the network architecture, PCNO employs four point cloud neural layers in (
21
), each with a channel size of
128
. The widths of both the lifting and projection layers are also set to
128
, and the
GeLU
activation function is adopted. The truncated Fourier frequency
k
is fixed across all integral operators but may vary between experiments due to memory limitations. We observe that increasing the truncated Fourier frequency generally reduces test errors.
Training is performed using the Adam optimizer
[
101
]
with default hyperparameters (
\beta_{1}=0.9
,
\beta_{2}=0.999
) and a weight decay factor of
10^{-4}
over
500
epochs.
The base learning rate, denoted as
\gamma
, is tuned and varies across different experiments. The length scale vector
L
in the integral operator (
21
) is treated as a hyperparameter, and its corresponding base learning rate may differ from other parameters.
We utilize the OneCycleLR scheduler
[
102
]
to dynamically adjust the learning rate during training. Specifically, the learning rate starts at
\frac{\gamma}{2}
, increases linearly to the base learning rate
\gamma
over the first
20\%
of total epochs, and then decreases following a cosine annealing schedule to
\frac{\gamma}{100}
. The relative
L_{2}
error is used as the cost function for both training and testing. All training and testing are conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 80G GPU.
A comprehensive comparison with other state-of-the-art neural operators is provided in
C
, focusing on widely used benchmarks, but restricted to fixed unit cube computational domains.
Our code and datasets are publicly available at:
https://github.com/PKU-CMEGroup/NeuralOperator
.

Advection Diffusion Problem

In this subsection, we consider solving the steady state, one-dimensional advection diffusion boundary value problem

\begin{split}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}-D\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial^{2}x}=f(x)\quad\textrm{in}\quad\Omega,\\
u(0)=u_{l},u(L)=0,\end{split}

defined on a variable computational domain
\Omega=[0,L]
. Here

f(x)
represents the source field,
D
is the constant diffusivity, and
u
denotes the steady state solution. The left boundary condition is
u_{l}
, while the right boundary condition is fixed at
0
, resulting in a steep boundary layer at the right end (See

2

The computational domain length

is uniformly sampled from
U[10,15]
, the left boundary condition

0&x>L_{f},\end{cases}

where the support length
L_{f}
is uniformly sampled from
U[5,8]
.
The field
g(x)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,625(-\Delta+5^{2})^{-2})
is a Gaussian random field with zero boundary condition.

We generate the dataset by solving the boundary value problem using the finite difference method with three types of meshes.
The first is a uniform mesh with a mesh size of

(Uniform).
The second is an exponentially graded mesh, with the mesh size set to
10^{-4}
at the right end and

at the left end, employing a growth ratio of

(Exponential).
The third is a linearly graded mesh with mesh size set to

at the right end and
10^{-2}
at the left end (Linear).
The latter two meshes are adaptive to better resolve the boundary layer near the right end (See

).
For each mesh type, we randomly generate 2500 samples, which are then combined into a mixed mesh dataset (Mixed). The operator maps the source function
f
, diffusivity

, the left boundary condition

(treated as constant functions over
\Omega
), and the domain
\Omega
to the corresponding solution
u
:

\mathcal{G}^{\dagger}:(f,D,u_{l},\Omega)\mapsto u.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce the Point Cloud Neural Operator (PCNO), a neural network-based surrogate model framework for approximating solution maps of parametric PDEs on complex and variable geometries. By designing the operator at the continuous level, and implementing it with traditional numerical methods, PCNO effectively handles point clouds and mitigates the impacts of discretization. Its flexibility, effectiveness, and practicality are demonstrated through five PDE problems, including large-scale three-dimensional applications.
Several promising directions for future research remain.
On the algorithmic side, systematically extending PCNO to time-dependent PDE systems would significantly broaden its applicability to more complex real-world scenarios. Additionally, developing a unified geometric foundation model that incorporates diverse data types could enhance its generalization capabilities.
Improving the generalization capability from PDE solutions on simple geometries to complex geometry represents another promising research direction.
Furthermore, exploring PCNO’s potential for engineering design optimization, uncertainty quantification, and related applications is another valuable avenue.
On the theoretical side, a rigorous analysis of sample complexity and the optimization process is essential to provide deeper insights into PCNO’s efficiency in practical applications.

Appendix A
Mesh Size and Connectivity Computation

In this section, we provide details on computing mesh size
\mathrm{d}\Omega_{i}
and connectivity used in the integral and differential operators (See
sections

3.1
and
3.2
).
For most PDE-related problems, the point cloud is typically associated with a mesh, from which the mesh size
\mathrm{d}\Omega_{i}
and connectivity can be estimated.
For vertex-centered meshes, the quantities of interest are stored at each vertex, denoted as
\{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
The mesh size at node
i
is computed as:

\mathrm{d}\Omega_{i}=\sum_{j:e_{j}\textrm{ contains node }i}\frac{|e_{j}|}{n_{e_{j}}},

(33)

where

e_{j}
represents a cell containing node
i
,

|e_{j}|
is the volume of the cell, and
n_{e_{j}}
is the number of nodes in the cell. The volume of each cell is distributed equally among its nodes.
The connectivity of node
i
, defined as the set of its neighboring nodes, is given by:

\mathcal{N}(i)=\{j:i,j\textrm{ belong to the same cell}\}.

(34)

In other words,

\mathcal{N}(i)
consists of all nodes that share at least one cell with node
i
.
For cell-centered meshes, the quantities of interest are stored at each cell.
The mesh size at node
i
is computed as the volume of the cell, it represents.
The connectivity of node
i
, defined as the set of neighboring cells is given by:

\mathcal{N}(i)=\{j:\textrm{ cells }i,j\textrm{ share at least one node}\}.

(35)

In other words,

\mathcal{N}(i)
includes all cells that share at least one node with cell
i
.
When a mesh is unavailable, a Delaunay tessellation can be constructed from the point cloud, treating the resulting as a vertex-centered mesh.

)

Let

\mathcal{K}_{B}\subset L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1})

\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{J}:L_{1}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1})\to R

\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}\left\|\mathcal{G}_{B}^{\dagger}(\tilde{a}_{B})-\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{j}(\tilde{a}_{B})\eta_{j}\right\|_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}\leq\epsilon.

(36)

(Modified Lemma A.9 in

100
]
)
.

Let
\alpha:L_{1}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1})\to R
be a continuous nonlinear functional. Consider
\mathcal{K}_{B}\subset L_{1}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1})
as a compact set consisting of bounded functions, satisfying
\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}\lVert\tilde{a}_{B}\rVert_{L_{\infty}}<\infty
. There exists a Point Cloud Neural Operator
\alpha_{\theta}:L_{1}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1})\rightarrow L_{1}(B)
, whose output functions are constant and can therefore also be interpreted as a functional
\alpha_{\theta}:L_{1}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}|\alpha(\tilde{a}_{B})-\alpha_{\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})|\leq\epsilon,

for any given

.

Proof.

Following the proof of Lemma A.9 in
[
100
]
, there exists an orthogonal basis,
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
, of
L_{2}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1})
, which we may also assume to be in
C^{\infty}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1})
. Then we have uniform convergence

\lim_{J\to\infty}\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}\left\|\tilde{a}_{B}-\sum_{i=1}^{J}\langle\tilde{a}_{B},\xi_{j}\rangle_{L_{2}}\xi_{j}\right\|_{L_{1}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1})}=0.

(37)

The claim relies on the fact that
B
is bounded,
\mathcal{K}_{B}\subset L_{1}\cap L_{\infty}\subset L_{2}
, and
\mathcal{K}_{B}\subset L_{2}
is compact in
L_{2}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1})
.
The approximation of the functional can be decomposed into two mappings

\tilde{a}_{B}\rightarrow\bigl(\langle\tilde{a}_{B},\xi_{1}\rangle_{L_{2}},\cdots,\langle\tilde{a}_{B},\xi_{J}\rangle_{L_{2}}\bigr)\rightarrow\alpha\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{J}\langle\tilde{a}_{B},\xi_{j}\rangle_{L_{2}}\xi_{j}\bigr).

(38)

The first mapping is continuous and, can be approximated by the proposed Point Cloud Neural Operator for any given
\epsilon
, such that

\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}\Bigl|\langle\tilde{a}_{B},\xi_{j}\rangle_{L_{2}}-\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{P}(\tilde{a}_{B})\rho(x;\Omega)\mathrm{d}x\Bigr|=\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}\Bigl|\int_{\Omega}\tilde{a}_{B}\cdot\xi_{j}\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{P}(\tilde{a}_{B})\rho(x;\Omega)\mathrm{d}x\Bigr|\leq\epsilon.

(39)

Here
\mathcal{P}
represents the lift layer, consisting solely of local linear functions. The layer
\mathcal{P}
can be used to approximate the
L_{p}(B)
function
\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1}\rightarrow R
,
\tilde{a}_{B}\rightarrow\frac{1}{\max\{\rho_{B},\rho_{\inf}\}}\tilde{a}_{B}\cdot\xi_{j}(x)
with error
\frac{\epsilon}{\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}\lVert\rho_{B}\rVert_{L_{q}(B)}}
where
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
. Note that
\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}\lVert\rho_{B}\rVert_{L_{q}(B)}<\infty
, as
\tilde{a}_{B}
contains
\rho_{B}
and is uniformly upper bounded,
Furthermore, since
\rho_{B}
is lower bounded by
\rho_{\inf}
, we have the approximation

\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}\Bigl\lVert\frac{\tilde{a}_{B}\cdot\xi_{j}}{\rho_{B}}-\mathcal{P}(\tilde{a}_{B})\Bigr\rVert_{L_{p}(\Omega)}\leq\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}\Bigl\lVert\frac{\tilde{a}_{B}\cdot\xi_{j}}{\max\{\rho_{B},\rho_{\inf}\}}-\mathcal{P}(\tilde{a}_{B})\Bigr\rVert_{L_{p}(B)}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}\lVert\rho_{B}\rVert_{L_{q}(B)}}.

By applying Hölder’s inequality, we obtain
eq.

39
. The term
\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{P}(\tilde{a}_{B})\rho(x;\Omega)\mathrm{d}x
corresponds to the point cloud neural layer, which consists solely of a constant integral kernel
\kappa(x,y,f_{\rm in})=1
, a special case of the Fourier kernel. Therefore, combining the lift layer with the point cloud neural layer gives the approximation of the first mapping in (
38
).

As for the second mapping in (
38
), we note that since
\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}
is upper bounded, the image of the first mapping is compact in
[-M,M]^{J}
for sufficiently large
M>0
.
The second mapping defines a continuous function
\beta:\mathbb{R}^{J}\to R
,
(c_{1},\cdots,c_{J})\mapsto\alpha(\sum_{j=1}^{J}c_{j}\xi_{j})
, which can be approximated by the projection layer, on the compact domain
[-M,M]^{J}
. Here
\xi_{j}
can be approximated using the lifting layer and then directed towards the final projection. Finally, by using the continuity of
\alpha
and choosing sufficient large
J
, we prove the modified Lemma A.9.

∎

We are now prepared to prove Theorem
3
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

We demonstrate that the Point Cloud Neural Operator
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}
, comprising a lifting layer (with several sublayers), a single point cloud neural layer, and a final projection layer (with several sublayers), is sufficient for approximation

\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}\lVert\mathcal{G}^{\dagger}(\tilde{a}_{B})-\mathcal{G}_{\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})\rVert_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}\leq\epsilon.

(40)

And the restriction on
\Omega
gives (
29
).

Following Lemma
1
, it suffices to approximate any continuous functional
\alpha:L_{1}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}+d+1})\to\mathbb{R}
defined on
\mathcal{K}_{B}
and function
\eta\in L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
by the proposed Point Cloud Neural Operator. For any
\alpha
, there exists a
Point Cloud Neural Operator

\alpha_{\theta}
given by the Lemma
2
, which approximates
\alpha
uniformly. For
\eta
as a
L_{p}
function, the approximation is guaranteed by the classical approximation theory of neural networks.

Then, for any
j\in\{1,\cdots,J\}
we aim to construct a Point Cloud Neural Operator
\mathcal{G}_{j,\theta}:\mathcal{K}_{B}\to L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
such that the following holds:

\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}\Bigl\lVert\alpha_{j}(\tilde{a}_{B})\eta_{j}-\mathcal{G}_{j,\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})\Bigr\rVert_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}\leq\epsilon/(2J).

(41)

First, let
M_{\alpha}=\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}|\alpha_{j}(\tilde{a}_{B})|

and we can find a smoothed approximation
\tilde{\eta}_{j}\in C^{\infty}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
satisfying

\|\tilde{\eta}_{j}-\eta_{j}\|_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}\leq\epsilon/(8JM_{\alpha}).

Then we define
M_{\eta}=\|\tilde{\eta}_{j}\|_{L_{p}(B)}
. From Lemma
2
we know that there exists a Point Cloud Neural Operator
\alpha_{j,\theta}
such that

\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}|\alpha_{j}(\tilde{a}_{B})-\alpha_{j,\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})|\leq\epsilon/(8JM_{\eta}).

Also let
M_{\alpha}^{\prime}=\sup_{\tilde{a}_{B}\in\mathcal{K}_{B}}|\alpha_{j,\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})|
. Based on the approximation theory, there exists a neural network
\eta_{j,\theta}:B\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
such that

\|\tilde{\eta}_{j}-\eta_{j,\theta}\|_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}\leq\epsilon/(8JM_{\alpha}^{\prime}).

Note that
\tilde{\eta}_{j}
is in
C^{\infty}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
so that the output of the neural network can be bounded, which is denoted as
M_{\eta}^{\prime}=\|\eta_{j,\theta}\|_{L_{\infty}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}
. The Point Cloud Neural Operator is defined by combining
\alpha_{j,\theta}
and
\eta_{j,\theta}
. Specifically,
\eta_{j,\theta}
can be placed in the lifting layer, with its output passed directly to the final layer. Then, the two operators
\alpha_{j,\theta}
and
\eta_{j,\theta}
can be combined with an additional final projection layer which approximates the multiplication:

\sup_{x\in[-M_{\alpha}^{\prime},M_{\alpha}^{\prime}],y\in[-M_{\eta}^{\prime},M_{\eta}^{\prime}]^{d_{u}}}\|p_{\theta}(x,y)-xy\|\leq\delta.

By the definitions of
M_{\alpha}^{\prime}
and
M_{\eta}^{\prime}
, we know the output of
(\alpha_{j,\theta},\eta_{j,\theta})
locates in the cube
[-M_{\alpha}^{\prime},M_{\alpha}^{\prime}]\times[-M_{\eta}^{\prime},M_{\eta}^{\prime}]^{d_{u}}
. Therefore, by choosing
\delta
sufficiently small, we have

\|p_{\theta}(\alpha_{j,\theta},\eta_{j,\theta})-\alpha_{j,\theta}\eta_{j,\theta}\|_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}\leq\epsilon/(8J).

Finally, the Point Cloud Neural Operator is defined as
\mathcal{G}_{j,\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})=p_{\theta}(\alpha_{j,\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B}),\eta_{j,\theta})
, and we have

\displaystyle\|\alpha_{j}(\tilde{a}_{B})\eta_{j}-

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{j,\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})\|_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}

(42)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\alpha_{j}(\tilde{a}_{B})\eta_{j}-\alpha_{j}(\tilde{a}_{B})\tilde{\eta}_{j}\|_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}+\|\alpha_{j}(\tilde{a}_{B})\tilde{\eta}_{j}-\alpha_{j,\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})\tilde{\eta}_{j}\|_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}

\displaystyle+\|\alpha_{j,\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})\tilde{\eta}_{j}-\alpha_{j,\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})\eta_{j,\theta}\|_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}+\|\alpha_{j,\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})\eta_{j,\theta}-\mathcal{G}_{j,\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})\|_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M_{\alpha}\|\tilde{\eta}_{j}-\eta_{j}\|_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}+M_{\eta}|\alpha_{j}(\tilde{a}_{B})-\alpha_{j,\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})|

\displaystyle+M_{\alpha}^{\prime}\|\tilde{\eta}_{j}-\eta_{j,\theta}\|_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}+\|\alpha_{j,\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})\eta_{j,\theta}-p_{\theta}(\alpha_{j,\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B}),\eta_{j,\theta})\|_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon/(2J).

Therefore, we successfully construct a Point Cloud Neural Operator
\mathcal{G}_{j,\theta}:\mathcal{K}_{B}\to L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})
that satisfies (
41
), for any
j\in\{1,\cdots,J\}
. We then define a new Point Cloud Neural Operator
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}:=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\mathcal{G}_{j,\theta}
. Combining (
41
) and Lemma
1
leads to

\displaystyle\lVert\mathcal{G}_{B}^{\dagger}(\tilde{a}_{B})-\mathcal{G}_{\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})\rVert_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{G}_{B}^{\dagger}(\tilde{a}_{B})-\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{j}(\tilde{a}_{B})\eta_{j}\right\rVert_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}+\left\lVert\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{j}(\tilde{a}_{B})\eta_{j}-\mathcal{G}_{\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})\right\rVert_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}

(43)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{G}_{B}^{\dagger}(\tilde{a}_{B})-\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{j}(\tilde{a}_{B})\eta_{j}\right\rVert_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\lVert\alpha_{j}(\tilde{a}_{B})\eta_{j}-\mathcal{G}_{j,\theta}(\tilde{a}_{B})\right\rVert_{L_{p}(B,\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}})}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon/2+J\cdot\epsilon/(2J)

\displaystyle=\epsilon.

The one-dimensional periodic Burgers’ equation with viscosity
\nu=0.1
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(x,t)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}u^{2}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\nu\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}u(x,t),

\displaystyle x\in(0,1),t\in(0,1],

(44)

\displaystyle u(x,0)

\displaystyle=u_{0}(x),

\displaystyle x\in(0,1).

\mathcal{G}^{\dagger}:u_{0}(x)\mapsto u(x,1)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(a(x)\nabla u(x))

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle x\in\partial(0,1)^{2}.

\mathcal{G}^{\dagger}:a(x)\mapsto u(x)

a(x)
to the solution
u(x)
