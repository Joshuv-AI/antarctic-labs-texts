Emergency vascular shunting using IV tubing for brachial artery injury: a life-saving innovation in resource-limited settings
Authors: Mohamed Farah Gabobe Nor, Yonas Yilma Metaferia, Abdisalam Ismail Hassan, Ayoub Hussein Ahmed, Bukhari Nor Ahmed Barale, Abdirahman Mohamed Shire, Mohamed Mukhtar Kassim, Mohammed AM Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe brachial artery is the most commonly injured artery in the upper extremities because of its vulnerability. This case report presents our experience with managing brachial artery injury using an improvised vascular shunting technique. A 38-year-old man presented to the emergency department with a penetrating bullet wound on the forearm, resulting in a 10 cm transection of the brachial artery, brachial vein injury, and small medial epicondyle fracture. Emergency surgery was performed following resuscitation. Intraoperatively, a temporary vascular shunt was created using an intravenous drip to maintain distal perfusion before definitive repair. The brachial artery was reconstructed using a graft from the great saphenous vein harvested from the thigh. The brachial vein was ligated and two K-wires were used to stabilize the medial epicondyle fragment. Postoperatively, the patient showed good recovery, with proper wound healing, intact hand sensation, and distal blood supply, and was discharged after two weeks.Keywords: Brachial artery injury, Vascular trauma, Gunshot wound, Vein graft, Vascular shunting

Introduction

The brachial artery is the most commonly injured artery in the upper limb owing to its susceptibility. Damage to the upper limb arteries can have a major impact on the overall outcome of trauma patients [1]. Although penetrating trauma is the most common cause of upper limb artery injuries, blunt trauma poses a higher risk of amputation [2].

Bullet wounds can result in either single or multiple forearm injuries. Access to weapons by the general public is often heavily regulated, and their use is restricted or banned in many countries. Consequently, the risk of facing manslaughter or murder charges is heightened when bullet injuries occur. However, there may be exceptions to these regulations, as certain specialized shotguns are sometimes used in sporting events [4].

Injuries to the arteries in the upper limbs can significantly impact the outcome of trauma patients [3]. Arterial rupture usually occurs as a result of significant open injuries, such as those caused by traffic or industrial accidents. The edges of the wound are jagged, causing the artery’s inner lining to fold inward, which aids in the formation of a blood clot (thrombus) [1].

Brachial artery injuries can generally be treated successfully unless there is considerable nerve damage. The median nerve runs along the entire length of the brachial artery and parts of the artery run parallel to the radial and ulnar nerves. Consequently, injuries to the brachial artery often lead to nerve damage, similar to other vascular injuries of the upper extremities [5].

However, in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Somalia, trauma care is frequently delayed due to war, restricted transportation, and insufficient referral networks. Patients may present far after the optimal time for revascularization, increasing the risk of irreversible ischemic damage. In these situations, imaginative use of existing materials is critical. This case demonstrates the efficient use of intravenous (IV) drip tubing as a cost-saving solution for the vascular shunt to restore blood flow in a patient who presented 36 h after injury. Despite its unconventionality, this low-cost, readily available equipment saved lives and limbs by serving as a bridge to definitive surgical repair. The above study highlights two major issues in limited-resource settings: delayed referral and the requirement for innovative, improvised interventions when standard vascular tools are unavailable.

Discussion

The brachial artery is the most frequently injured vessel in the upper limb, accounting for approximately 28–52% of all vascular injuries reported in clinical settings. Multiple studies indicate that these injuries are most often caused by penetrating trauma [6].

Early diagnosis, enhanced surgical expertise, advancements in the treatment of hypovolemic Improved outcomes in patient survival have been attributed to advancements in shock management and antibiotic use, The severity and risk of death from brachial artery injuries are influenced by various factors, including the injury’s cause, involvement of nearby veins or tendons, and the presence of associated musculoskeletal or nerve damage. Penetrating trauma remains the most frequent cause of vascular injuries in the upper extremity [1].

The morbidity and mortality rates associated with brachial artery injuries vary depending on factors such as the cause of the injury, the specific vein or tendon affected, and the presence of additional musculoskeletal or nerve damage. For patients with clear clinical signs of brachial artery injury identified through physical examination or those showing a significant pressure difference between the right and left brachial arteries on Doppler ultrasonography, surgical repair should be performed without the need for further angiographic tests. Doppler ultrasonography of the upper extremity has been demonstrated to be as accurate and reliable as arteriography in detecting brachial artery injuries [7].

The timing of vascular repair in relation to fracture treatment has long been a topic of debate. As the main goal is to avoid extended tissue ischemia, vascular repair should be performed before addressing the fracture [8].

An initial closed reduction under sedation yielded near-anatomic alignment; however, subsequent loss of palpable pulses and pulse oximetry waveform with elbow flexion raised concern for arterial injury. Angiography confirmed a segmental occlusion of the proximal brachial artery. Surgical intervention was pursued, beginning with fracture stabilization via a modified 3.5-mm T-plate, carefully placed to avoid physeal injury.

Vascular exploration identified an intact adventitia but significant thrombus and an intimal defect at the fracture level. A 2-cm arteriotomy was performed, thrombus evacuated, and a vein patch angioplasty using autologous cephalic vein was completed. Restoration of arterial flow was confirmed intraoperatively. The patient recovered well postoperatively, with palpable radial pulses and resolution of neurologic symptoms. Surgical treatment of brachial artery injuries can be performed using several techniques, such as lateral repair, resection followed by end-to-end anastomosis, or interposition grafting, typically with a saphenous vein. End-to-end anastomosis is preferred when it can be performed without tension or damage to the major collateral vessels, but that end-to-end repair is uncommon because the wounding agent may result in a sizeable arterial defect that would require a bypass graft If this is not possible, a saphenous vein interposition graft is the next best option, as it offers better patency and greater resistance to infection than synthetic grafts. However, in one case, a ringed polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) graft was used because of insufficient saphenous vein availability. Endovascular methods are increasingly being employed to manage penetrating injuries and may offer benefits even in cases of blunt trauma. They are particularly useful for managing blunt axillary artery injuries, which can be anatomically challenging to repair [8].

Penetrating injuries to the upper extremities involving important vascular structures, such as the brachial artery, are limb-threatening and require immediate surgery. In this case, the patient received a gunshot wound to the forearm, causing total transection of the brachial artery over a 10 cm segment, brachial vein injury, and bone trauma to the medial epicondyle. Although immediate vascular repair is optimal during the “golden period” of 6–8 h to reduce ischemic problems, our patient arrived after a 36-hour delay due to transportation from a rural district (Guriel) to a tertiary care clinic in Mogadishu.

Conclusion

This case indicates that successful limb salvage and functional recovery are achievable even with delayed presentation after upper limb vascular damage. Timely surgical repair with autologous vein grafts, adequate orthopedic fixation, and early physiotherapy all contributed to a positive outcome. However, the delayed referral from Guriel to Mogadishu highlights the significance of establishing regional trauma networks and enhancing access to emergency surgical treatments in rural places. Continuous efforts are required to improve trauma treatment systems, reduce delays, and optimize outcomes for patients with complex injuries in resource-limited settings.
