Least-Squares Finite Element Methods for nonlinear problems: A unified framework Journal: arXiv
Fleurianne Bertrand, Maximilian Brodbeck, Tim Ricken, Henrik Schneider

Abstract
This paper presents a unified Least-Squares framework for solving nonlinear partial differential equations by recasting the governing system as a residual minimization problem. A Least-Squares functional is formulated and the corresponding Gauss–Newton iterative method derived, which approximates simultaneously primal and stress-like variables. We derive conditions under which the Least-Squares functional is coercive and continuous in an appropriate solution space, and establish convergence results while demonstrating that the functional serves as a reliable a posteriori error estimator. This inherent error estimation property is then exploited to drive adaptive mesh refinement across a variety of problems, including the stationary heat equation with either temperature-dependent or discontinuous conductivity or a ReLU-type nonlinearity, nonlinear elasticity based on the Saint Venant–Kirchhoff model and sea-ice dynamics.



1
Introduction

Nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) are ubiquitous in modeling complex physical phenomena across science and engineering.
Owing to their rigorous theoretical foundation, versatility, computational efficiency, and ability to handle irregular domains, Finite Element Methods (FEM) have proven to be an indispensable tool for approximating PDE solutions.
Within many of these finite element approaches, a primal variable
u
minimizes an energy potential over a bounded domain
\Omega
with Lipschitz boundary in
H^{1}(\Omega)=\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\ :\ \|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<\infty\}
.
However, these purely primal approaches often suffer from reduced robustness and fail to provide accurate approximations of certain physically relevant quantities.
To circumvent this so-called locking phenomena, mixed finite element methods introduce a dual quantity
\sigma
to be approximated in
H({\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}},\Omega)=\{\sigma\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}\ :\ \|\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<\infty\}
. A major challenge of these methods are the required inf-sup conditions, both at the continuous and discrete levels, to ensure well-posedness and stability. These conditions impose constraints on the choice of finite element spaces, requiring careful pairing of function spaces for primal- and dual variables. Failure to satisfy the inf-sup condition can lead to unstable or inaccurate solutions, making the selection of appropriate finite element spaces a nontrivial task, see
[
2
]
.

Least-Squares Finite Element Methods (LSFEM) (see e.g.
[
4
]
) provide an attractive alternative to classical mixed finite element methods by minimizing specifically designed Least-Squares functionals. Unlike mixed methods, LSFEM eliminates the need for inf-sup compatible function spaces and avoids the complexities associated with saddle point formulations. Another key advantage is the built-in a posteriori error estimate, which can directly be utilized in adaptive refinement strategies. LSFEM has been successfully applied to a wide range of linear partial differential equations, including the Poisson problem
[
14
]
, linear elasticity
[
6
,
18
]
or flow problems
[
5
]
.

Since the resulting system of linear equations is inherently symmetric and positive definite, LSFEM allows the use of efficient linear equation solvers, making it particularly well-suited for nonlinear problems. Gauss-Newton and multilevel methods, as developed in
[
17
]
, enable the extension of LSFEM to nonlinear problems, which is crucial in applications such as nonlinear elasticity
[
9
,
12
,
16
]
or general flow problems
[
17
,
13
]
.
Despite these advantages, a systematic analysis of LSFEM in the nonlinear setting remains largely absent in literature. In particular, while the Least-Squares functional in the linear case inherently provides an a posteriori error estimate, there has been limited exploration of how this property can be extended the nonlinear case.
The purpose of this paper is to bridge this gap by developing a general framework that extends this property to broader classes of problems.

In the following, section
2
introduces the Least-Squares framework for nonlinear PDEs, formulating the residual-based functional and deriving the corresponding Gauss-Newton iterative scheme. Section
3
provides a detailed analysis of the theoretical properties of the Least-Squares functional, establishing conditions for coercivity, continuity, and convergence. In particular, we prove that the Least-Squares functional serves as a reliable a posteriori error estimator in the nonlinear setting, thereby extending its well-known properties from the linear case.
Section
4
applies the developed framework to various nonlinear PDEs, including a stationary heat equation with temperature-dependent conductivity, nonlinear elasticity using the Saint Venant–Kirchhoff model, and a model for sea-ice dynamics. Additionally, we discuss extensions to problems with discontinuous coefficients and ReLU-type nonlinearities.

2
The Least-Squares approach

Let
\Omega
be an open, bounded Lipschitz domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,\,3\}
. Given a Hilbert Space
\mathbf{V}
, the nonlinear system of first-order partial differential equations under consideration reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})+\bm{f}={\bm{0}}

(1)

for
\bm{u}\in\mathbf{V}
. Throughout this work, the existence of a solution

\bm{u}^{*}\in\mathbf{V}
is assumed and the nonlinear functional

\mathcal{F}(\bm{u})=\|\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})+\bm{f}\|_{\mathbf{H}}^{2}
is minimized in a suitable Hilbert Space
\mathbf{H}
.
The corresponding Fréchet derivative
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
of the operator
\mathcal{R}
is given by

\displaystyle\lim_{\|\bm{h}\|_{\mathbf{V}}\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\|\bm{h}\|_{\mathbf{V}}}\|\mathcal{R}(\bm{u}+\bm{h})-\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})-\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(\bm{u})\,\bm{h}\|_{\mathbf{H}}\ ,

(2)

allowing the computations of gradient and hessian of the functional
\mathcal{F}
:

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{F}(\bm{u})=\left(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(\bm{u})\right)^{*}(\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})+\bm{f})\;,

\displaystyle{\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}(\bm{u})=\left(\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(\bm{u})\right)^{*}\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(\bm{u})+\left(\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}(\bm{u})\right)^{*}(\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})+\bm{f})}\;.

Using these quantities, the classical Newton method for minimizing
\mathcal{F}
is expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{n+1}=\bm{u}_{n}-\left(\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}(\bm{u}_{n})\right)^{-1}\nabla\mathcal{F}(\bm{u}_{n})\ .

To avoid its potentially expensive evaluation,
\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}(\bm{u}_{0})
can be neglected if
\bm{u}_{0}
is close to the solution
\bm{u}^{*}
.
This leads to the Gaus-Newton method, defined by the iterative process

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{n+1}=\bm{u}_{n}-\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\left(\bm{u}_{n}\right)^{-1}\left(\bm{f}+\mathcal{R}\left(\bm{u}_{n}\right)\right),

(3)

i.e.

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{n+1}=\bm{u}_{n}+\delta\bm{u}\;\;\text{with}\;\;\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\left(\bm{u}_{n}\right)\delta\bm{u}=-\bm{f}-\mathcal{R}\left(\bm{u}_{n}\right)\ .

(4)

Note that the Gauss-Newton method is equivalent to the Newton method for the residual
\mathcal{R}
, since

\mathcal{R}(\bm{u}^{*})=0
.
To fully exploit the advantages of the Least-Squares method, in particular its inherent error estimator property, the Newton step is reformulated as the minimization of a linear Least-Squares functional

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{lin}}(\bm{v};(\bm{u}_{n},{\bm{f}})):=\left\|\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\left(\bm{u}_{n}\right)\bm{v}+\bm{f}+\mathcal{R}\left(\bm{u}_{n}\right)\right\|^{2}_{\mathbf{H}}\ ,

to obtain

\displaystyle\delta\bm{u}=\argmin_{\bm{v}\in\mathbf{V}}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{lin}}(\bm{v};(\bm{u}_{n},{\bm{f}}))\ .

(5)

The Lax-Milgram Lemma implies the equivalence of the Least-Squares functional
and the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle C_{e}\|\bm{w}\|_{\mathbf{V}}\leq\left\|\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\left(\bm{u}_{n}\right)\bm{w}\right\|_{\mathbf{H}}\leq{C_{c}}\|\bm{w}\|_{\mathbf{V}}\text{ for all }\bm{w}\in\mathbf{V}\ ,

(6)

is a sufficient condition for the well-posedness of the Gauss-Newton step (
4
). In particular, the minimization problem (
5
) has a unique minimizer.
This result guarantees the practicability of the Gauss-Newton method outlined in Algorithm
1
.

Based on the results discussed above, the Least-Squares approach, combined with the Gauss-Newton method, offers a computationally efficient and theoretically robust framework for addressing nonlinear systems of PDEs. Subsequent sections build on these foundational results by refining the assumptions to align with the typical structure of nonlinear PDEs encountered in practical applications.

3
A unified framework

The theoretical and computational framework presented so far, relies on general assumptions about the residual operator

\mathcal{R}
and its Fréchet derivative. However, in many practical applications, the underlying PDE system exhibits additional structure and constraints that can be exploited to refine the convergence analysis.
This section introduces new assumptions designed to leverage these structural properties.
In many practical applications, the system

\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})+\bm{f}=\bm{0}
often consists of multiple coupled equations. These equations typically involve the primal variable

u
and additional flux or stress variables

\sigma
, introduced in Section
1
.
One of the equations then represents the a conservation law, such as the conservation of momentum. It is usually linear in
u
and
\sigma
and reads

L_{1}(u,\sigma)+f=0

with a linear function
L_{1}(u,\sigma)
. The second equation, commonly representing the constitutive equation, frequently exhibits a nonlinearity in
u
and reads

L_{2}(u,\sigma)+K(u)+g=0

with a linear function
L_{2}(u,\sigma)
and a nonlinear function
K(u)
. This can be cast into the system

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})=\bm{f}\quad\text{with}\quad\mathcal{R}(\bm{u})=\begin{pmatrix}L_{1}(u,\sigma)\\
L_{2}(u,\sigma)+K(u)\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{f}=\begin{pmatrix}f\\
g\end{pmatrix}\;.

The resulting Least-Squares functional reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(u,\sigma;f,g)=\|L_{1}(u,\sigma)+f\|^{2}_{H}+\|L_{2}(u,\sigma)+K(u)+g\|_{H}^{2}\ .

(10)

Recall that the norm equivalence (
6
) substantiates the convergence analysis of the Newton method. Consequently, the first assumption imposes an energy balance assumption on the linear part, i.e. the coercivity and continuity properties in the linear case.

Assumption 1
.

There exists positive constants
C_{1},...,C_{6}
such that the following norm-equivalence holds

\displaystyle C_{1}\|u\|_{U}^{2}+C_{2}\|\sigma\|_{\Sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|L_{1}(u,\sigma)\|^{2}_{H}+\|L_{2}(u,\sigma)\|^{2}_{H}

(11a)

\displaystyle\|L_{1}(u,\sigma)\|^{2}_{H}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\|u\|_{U}^{2}+C_{4}\|\sigma\|_{\Sigma}^{2}

(11b)

\displaystyle\|L_{2}(u,\sigma)\|^{2}_{H}

\displaystyle\leq C_{5}\|u\|_{U}^{2}+C_{6}\|\sigma\|_{\Sigma}^{2}

(11c)

for all
u,\sigma\in U\times\Sigma
.

Next, to control the nonlinear component of the PDE system, a Lipschitz continuity condition is imposed on the nonlinear term
K(u)
, restricting its growth and ensuring boundedness within a suitable set.

Assumption 2
.

There exists an open and non-empty set
S\subset U
such that

\displaystyle\|K(u)-K(v)\|^{2}_{H}\leq L\,\|u-v\|^{2}_{U}

(12)

holds for all
u,v\in S
with a Lipschitz constant
0<L<C_{1}
.

With the assumptions on the linear and nonlinear components in place, a crucial norm equivalence result for the linearized Least-Squares functional

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{lin}}
can now be established. This equivalence guarantees the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the associated minimization problem and forms the basis for analyzing convergence. The following theorem formalizes this result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold true. For any
u\in S
with
K(u)
Fréchet differentiable in
u
,
the norm-equivalence

\displaystyle\|v\|_{U}^{2}+\|\tau\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\lesssim\|L_{1}(v,\tau)\|^{2}_{H}+\|L_{2}(v,\tau)+K^{\prime}(u)v\|^{2}_{H}\lesssim\|v\|_{U}^{2}+\|\tau\|_{\Sigma}^{2}

(13)

holds true for all
(v,\tau)\in U\times\Sigma
.
In particular, the Least-Squares problem (
5
) has a unique solution.

Proof.

Since
K(u)
is Fréchet differentiable, the Fréchet derivative can be bounded by the Lipschitz constant, i.e. it holds
\|K^{\prime}(u)\|^{2}\leq L
.
The triangle inequality, together with (
11
), implies

\displaystyle\|L_{1}(v,\tau)\|^{2}_{H}+\|L_{2}(v,\tau)+K^{\prime}(u)v\|^{2}_{H}

\displaystyle\leq\|L_{1}(v,\tau)\|^{2}_{H}+2\|L_{2}(v,\tau)\|^{2}_{H}+2\|K^{\prime}(u)v\|^{2}_{H}

\displaystyle\leq(C_{3}+2C_{5})\|v\|^{2}_{U}+(C_{4}+2C_{6})\|\sigma\|_{\Sigma}^{2}+2\|K^{\prime}(u)\|^{2}\|v\|^{2}_{U}

\displaystyle\leq(C_{3}+2C_{5}+2L)\|v\|^{2}_{U}+(C_{4}+2C_{6})\|\sigma\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\ .

On the other hand, equation (
11
) implies

\displaystyle\|L_{1}(v,\tau)\|^{2}+\|L_{2}(v,\tau)+K^{\prime}(u)v\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|L_{1}(v,\tau)\|^{2}+\|L_{2}(v,\tau)\|^{2}-\|K^{\prime}(u)v\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq C_{1}\|v\|^{2}_{U}+C_{2}\|\tau\|_{\Sigma}^{2}-\|K^{\prime}(u)\|^{2}\|v\|_{U}^{2}\ ,

such that the crucial assumption
C_{1}-L>0
concludes the proof with

\displaystyle\|L_{1}(v,\tau)\|^{2}+\|L_{2}(v,\tau)+K^{\prime}(u)v\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(C_{1}-L)\|v\|^{2}_{U}+C_{2}\|\tau\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\ .

∎

The results so far demonstrate that the combination of norm equivalence, adaptive refinement through the AFEM-loop, and the inexact Newton framework ensures robust convergence of the Gauss-Newton method. One can now build on these previous results to focus on the use of the Least-Squares functional in posteriori error estimation. First, the assumptions
1
and
2
are are shown to imply the norm-equivalence of the nonlinear Least-Squares functional, which in turn forms the basis for deriving reliable and efficient a posteriori error estimates. It is important to note that, unlike the previous results, this analysis does not assume the Fréchet differentiability of
K(u)
, allowing the use of the error estimate for a broader class of nonlinear problems.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
(u,\sigma)\in U\times\Sigma
solve the underlying PDE
and the assumptions
1
and
2
hold true.
Then the Least-Squares functional (
10
) is a reliable and
efficient error estimator, i.e. it holds

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{rel}}\left(\|u-v\|_{U}^{2}+\|\sigma-\tau\|^{2}\right)\leq\mathcal{F}(v,\tau;f,g)\leq C_{\mathrm{eff}}\left(\|u-v\|_{U}^{2}+\|\sigma-\tau\|^{2}\right)

(15)

for all
(v,\tau)\in S\times\Sigma
.
The constants are given by

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{rel}}

\displaystyle=\min\{C_{1}(1-C)+L(1-C^{-1}),C_{2}(1-C)\}=C_{c},

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle=\max\{C_{3}+2(C_{5}+L),C_{4}+2C_{6}\}=C_{s}\ .

for any positive constant
C
satisfying
\frac{L}{C_{1}}<C<1
.

Proof.

Since
(u,\sigma)
is the solution of the minimization problem
\mathcal{F}(u,\sigma;f,g)=0
, it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(v,\tau;f,g)

\displaystyle=\|L_{1}(v,\tau)+f\|^{2}_{H}+\|L_{2}(v,\tau)+K(v)+g\|^{2}_{H}

\displaystyle=\|L_{1}(u-v,\sigma-\tau)\|^{2}_{H}+\|L_{2}(u-v,\sigma-\tau)+K(u)-K(v)\|^{2}_{H}\ .

On the one hand, this leads to the efficiency

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(v,\tau;f,g)

\displaystyle=\|L_{1}(v,\tau)+f\|^{2}_{H}+\|L_{2}(v,\tau)+K(v)+g\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|L_{1}(u-v,\sigma-\tau)\|^{2}_{H}+\|L_{2}(u-v,\sigma-\tau)+K(u)-K(v)\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|L_{1}(u-v,\sigma-\tau)\|^{2}_{H}+2\|L_{2}(u-v,\sigma-\tau)\|^{2}_{H}+2\|K(u)-K(v)\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(C_{3}+2(C_{5}+L))\|u-v\|^{2}_{U}+(C_{4}+2C_{6})\|\sigma-\tau\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\ .

On the other hand, Young’s inequality implies

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(v,\tau;f,g)

\displaystyle\geq\|L_{1}(u-v,\sigma-\tau)\|^{2}_{H}+(1-C)\|L_{2}(u-v,\sigma-\tau)\|_{H}^{2}-(C^{-1}-1)\|K(u)-K(v)\|^{2}_{H}

\displaystyle\geq C_{2}(1-C)\|\sigma-\tau\|_{\Sigma}^{2}+(C_{1}(1-C)-L(C^{-1}-1))\|u-v\|_{U}^{2}

and since
(C_{1}(1-C)+L(1-1/C))>0
and
C_{2}(1-C)>0
, the reliability estimate holds true.
∎

In conclusion, the Least-Squares functional can serve as an a posteriori error estimation under Assumptions
1
and
2
. The reliability and efficiency of the functional were shown to hold for a wide class of nonlinear problems, even without requiring the Fréchet differentiability of
K(u)
. To further illustrate these results, practical examples will be presented in the next section.

4
Application of the framework

Up to this point, it has been proven that nonlinear Least-Squares functionals of a specific form serve as a posteriori error estimators. In the following section this theory will be applied to a nonlinear Poisson problem with Lipschitz continuous coefficient as well as a source term with ReLU-type nonlinearity, the nonlinear Saint Venant–Kirchhoff model of elasticity and a model for sea-ice dynamics. Across the various applications, different boundary conditions are handled by decomposing the boundary
\partial\Omega
into
\Gamma_{D}
and
\Gamma_{N}
, with
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{D}\cup\Gamma_{N}
. The following usual Sobolev spaces

\displaystyle H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega):v_{|\partial\Omega}=0\},

\displaystyle H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}):v=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{D}\},

\displaystyle H_{\Gamma_{N}}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)

\displaystyle=\{\tau\in H({\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}},\Omega):\tau\cdot\nu=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{N}\}

are used.

4.1
Stationary heat equation with temperature-dependent thermal conductivity

The simplest case study for the nonlinear Least-Squares methodology is the quasilinear stationary heat equation with temperature-dependent thermal conductivity, as discussed e.g. in
[
1
]
. This equation seeks
u\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle-\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}(\kappa(u)\nabla u)=f\ \mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\quad u=0\ \mathrm{on}\ \Gamma_{D},\quad\nabla u\cdot\nu=0\ \mathrm{on}\ \Gamma_{N}

(16)

with
\kappa(u)
is Lipschitz continuous.
The corresponding Least-Squares functional reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}(u,\sigma;f)=\|f-\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma\|^{2}+\|\kappa(u)\nabla u+\sigma\|^{2}\ .

(17)

Recasting this first order system into the framework (
10
), requires the solution of the unconstrained minimization problem

\displaystyle\text{minimize }\mathcal{F}_{1}(u,\sigma;f)=\|L_{1}(u,\sigma)+f\|^{2}+\|L_{2}(u,\sigma)+K(u)\|^{2}\;,\

(18)

in
H_{\Gamma_{D}}^{1}(\Omega)\times H_{\Gamma_{N}}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)
with

\displaystyle K(u)

\displaystyle=(1+\kappa(u))\nabla u

\displaystyle L_{1}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma

\displaystyle L_{2}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sigma-\nabla u\ .

Clearly, the functional

\|\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma\|^{2}+\|\sigma-\nabla u\|^{2}

is elliptic (see e.g.
[
14
]
). However, to provide explicit constants and ensure quantitative estimates in the following analysis, recall the following technical lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and
C_{P}>0
.
For any
\alpha\in\left(\max\left(0,\frac{C_{P}^{2}-1}{C_{P}^{2}+1}\right),1\right)
there exists
\beta,\gamma>0
such that

1\;-\;\tfrac{(1-\alpha)^{2}\,\kappa^{2}}{\gamma}>0,\kappa^{2}\;-\;\beta\;-\;\gamma\,C_{P}^{2}>0,1\;-\;\tfrac{\alpha^{2}\,\kappa^{2}}{\beta}>0\ .

Proof.

In particular,

\gamma>(1-\alpha)^{2}\,\kappa^{2}
and

\quad\beta>\alpha^{2}\,\kappa^{2}
implies

\kappa^{2}>\beta+\gamma\,C_{P}^{2}>(1-\alpha)^{2}\,\kappa^{2}C_{P}^{2}+\alpha^{2}\,\kappa^{2}

and this is only possible if

\kappa^{2}>(1-\alpha)^{2}\,\kappa^{2}C_{P}^{2}+\alpha^{2}\,\kappa^{2}

i.e.

g(\alpha)>0
with
g(\alpha)=1-(1-\alpha)^{2}\,C_{P}^{2}-\alpha^{2}\ .

It holds

\displaystyle g(\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\alpha^{2}(C_{P}^{2}+1)+2\alpha C_{P}^{2}+(1-C_{P}^{2})=(C_{P}^{2}+1)\left(1-\alpha\right)\left(\alpha-\frac{C_{P}^{2}-1}{C_{P}^{2}+1}\right)

such that
g
admits two roots given by

\overline{\alpha}=1
and
\underline{\alpha}=\frac{C_{P}^{2}-1}{C_{P}^{2}+1}
and
g
is positive on
[\underline{\alpha},1]
.
Moreover, the derivative of
g
is given by

g^{\prime}(\alpha)=-2\alpha(C_{P}^{2}+1)+2C_{P}^{2}

with its maximum attained for

\alpha_{\text{max}}=\frac{C_{P}^{2}}{C_{P}^{2}+1}\in[\underline{\alpha},1]\ \text{ with }g(\alpha_{\text{max}})=(C_{P}^{2}+1)\left(1-\frac{C_{P}^{2}}{C_{P}^{2}+1}\right)\left(\frac{C_{P}^{2}}{C_{P}^{2}+1}-\frac{C_{P}^{2}-1}{C_{P}^{2}+1}\right)\frac{1}{C_{P}^{2}+1}\ .

Thus,
g:\left[\max\left(0,\frac{C_{P}^{2}-1}{C_{P}^{2}+1}\right),1\right]\rightarrow[0,\frac{1}{C_{P}^{2}+1}]
is bijective.
Moreover
\hat{\alpha}\in\left(\max\left(0,\frac{C_{P}^{2}-1}{C_{P}^{2}+1}\right),1\right)
implies

g(\hat{\alpha})>0
,
i.e.

\frac{1-\hat{\alpha}^{2}}{C_{P}^{2}}>(1-\hat{\alpha})^{2}

such that it is possible to choose

\hat{\gamma}\in\left((1-\hat{\alpha})^{2}\,\kappa^{2},\frac{\kappa^{2}}{C_{P}^{2}}(1-{\hat{\alpha}}^{2})\right),\ \hat{\beta}\in\left({\hat{\alpha}}^{2}\kappa^{2},\kappa^{2}-\hat{\gamma}{C_{P}^{2}}\right)\ .

∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\kappa
be constant and

\mathcal{F}_{1,l}(u,\sigma;0)\;=\;\|\mathrm{div}\,\sigma\|^{2}\;+\;\|\kappa\,\nabla u+\sigma\|^{2}.

There exists a constant
\lambda>0
such that

\mathcal{F}_{1,l}(u,\sigma;0)\;\geq\;\lambda\,\Bigl(\,\|\mathrm{div}\,\sigma\|^{2}\;+\;\|\nabla u\|^{2}\;+\;\|\sigma\|^{2}\Bigr)\quad\text{for all }(u,\sigma)\in H_{\Gamma_{D}}^{1}(\Omega)\times H_{\Gamma_{N}}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega).

Proof.

With a parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the functional reads

\mathcal{F}_{1,l}(u,\sigma;0)\;=\;\|\mathrm{div}\,\sigma\|^{2}\;+\;\kappa^{2}\,\|\nabla u\|^{2}\;+\;\|\sigma\|^{2}-\,2\,\alpha\,\kappa\,\langle\nabla u,\;\sigma\rangle\;-\;2\,(1-\alpha)\,\kappa\,\langle\nabla u,\;\sigma\rangle.

and integrating by parts leads to

\mathcal{F}_{1,l}(u,\sigma;0)\;=\;\|\mathrm{div}\,\sigma\|^{2}\;+\;\kappa^{2}\,\|\nabla u\|^{2}\;+\;\|\sigma\|^{2}\;-\;2\,\alpha\,\kappa\,\langle\nabla u,\;\sigma\rangle\;+\;2\,(1-\alpha)\,\kappa\,\langle u,\;\mathrm{div}\,\sigma\rangle.

For some
\beta>0
and
\gamma>0
it holds

-\,2\,\alpha\,\kappa\,\langle\nabla u,\;\sigma\rangle\;\geq\;-\Bigl[\beta\,\|\nabla u\|^{2}\;+\;\tfrac{\alpha^{2}\,\kappa^{2}}{\beta}\,\|\sigma\|^{2}\Bigr],

and

2\,(1-\alpha)\,\kappa\,\langle u,\;\mathrm{div}\,\sigma\rangle\;\geq\;-\,\Bigl[\gamma\,\|u\|^{2}\;+\;\tfrac{(1-\alpha)^{2}\,\kappa^{2}}{\gamma}\,\|\mathrm{div}\,\sigma\|^{2}\Bigr],

yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1,l}(u,\sigma;0)

\displaystyle\geq\;\Bigl[1\;-\;\tfrac{(1-\alpha)^{2}\,\kappa^{2}}{\gamma}\Bigr]\|\mathrm{div}\,\sigma\|^{2}\;+\;\Bigl[\kappa^{2}\;-\;\beta\Bigr]\|\nabla u\|^{2}\;+\;\Bigl[1\;-\;\tfrac{\alpha^{2}\,\kappa^{2}}{\beta}\Bigr]\|\sigma\|^{2}\;-\;\gamma\,\|u\|^{2}.

The Poincaré–Friedrichs inequality

\|u\|\;\leq\;C_{P}\,\|\nabla u\|

leads to

\mathcal{F}_{1,l}(u,\sigma;0)\;\geq\;\Bigl[1\;-\;\tfrac{(1-\alpha)^{2}\,\kappa^{2}}{\gamma}\Bigr]\|\mathrm{div}\,\sigma\|^{2}\;+\;\Bigl[\kappa^{2}\;-\;\beta\;-\;\gamma\,C_{P}^{2}\Bigr]\|\nabla u\|^{2}\;+\;\Bigl[1\;-\;\tfrac{\alpha^{2}\,\kappa^{2}}{\beta}\Bigr]\|\sigma\|^{2}.

According to Lemma
4.1
,
\alpha\in(0,1),\beta>0
and
\gamma>0
can be found such that

\displaystyle 1\;-\;\tfrac{(1-\alpha)^{2}\,\kappa^{2}}{\gamma}>0,\quad 1\;-\;\tfrac{\alpha^{2}\,\kappa^{2}}{\beta}>0,\quad\text{and}\quad 1\;-\;\tfrac{\alpha^{2}\,\kappa^{2}}{\beta}>0

(19)

holds,
and set

\lambda\;=\;\min\!\Bigl\{1-\tfrac{(1-\hat{\alpha})^{2}\,\kappa^{2}}{\gamma},\;\kappa^{2}-\beta-\gamma\,C_{P}^{2},\;1-\tfrac{\hat{\alpha}^{2}\,\kappa^{2}}{\beta}\Bigr\}\;.

∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume that the system (
16
) admits a solution
u^{*}\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega)
. Then, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
K(u)
satisfies Assumption
2
on
B(u^{*},\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

Since
\kappa
is Lipschitz-continuous,
(1-\kappa(u))\nabla u
is differentiable and for any constant
C_{1}>0
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle\|K(u)-K(v)\|^{2}\leq C_{1}\|u-v\|^{2}_{U}

(20)

holds for all
u,v\in B(u^{*},\varepsilon)
.
∎

4.2
Remarks on discontinuous coefficients

For completeness, note that extending the analysis to the case of discontinuous coefficients poses no additional difficulties. To this end, let
\Omega
be partitioned into two subdomains
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, on each of which the parameter is constant

\kappa(x)=\begin{cases}\kappa_{1},&x\in\Omega_{1}\\
\kappa_{2},&x\in\Omega_{2}\end{cases}\;.

Then, on each subdomain
\Omega_{i}
, integrating by parts yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1,l}(u,\sigma;0)\Big|_{\Omega_{i}}=

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{div}\,\sigma\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}^{2}+\kappa_{i}^{2}\,\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}^{2}+\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}^{2}

\displaystyle-2\alpha\,\kappa_{i}\,\langle\nabla u,\sigma\rangle_{\Omega_{i}}+2(1-\alpha)\,\kappa_{i}\,\langle u,\operatorname{div}\,\sigma\rangle_{\Omega_{i}}-2(1-\alpha)\,\kappa_{i}\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}}u\,(\sigma\cdot n)\,ds.

Since
u=0
on the external boundary, the only non-vanishing boundary contributions arise from the internal interface

\Gamma=\partial\Omega_{1}\cap\partial\Omega_{2}
and the interface term can be estimated using the duality between
H^{1/2}(\Gamma)
and
H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
. Indeed

\langle u,\sigma\cdot n\rangle_{\Gamma}\leq\|u\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}\,\|\sigma\cdot n\|_{H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)},

and the Young’s inequality with parameter
\delta>0
yields

-2(1-\alpha)(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})\langle u,\sigma\cdot n\rangle_{\Gamma}\geq-\delta\,\|u\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}^{2}-\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})^{2}}{\delta}\,\|\sigma\cdot n\|_{H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}^{2}.

Moreover, combining the standard trace inequality with the Poincaré–Friedrichs inequality leads to

\|u\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}^{2}\leq C_{tr,u}^{2}\Bigl(\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Bigr)\leq C_{tr,u}^{2}\,(1+C_{P}^{2})\,\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

\text{ and }\|\sigma\cdot n\|_{H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}^{2}\leq C_{tr,\sigma}^{2}\Bigl(\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\operatorname{div}\,\sigma\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Bigr).

Thus, the coercivity estimate reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1,l}(u,\sigma;0)\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\Biggl\{\Biggl[\left(1-\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}\,\kappa_{i}^{2}}{\gamma_{i}}\right)-\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})^{2}}{\delta}\,C_{tr,\sigma}^{2}\Biggr]\|\operatorname{div}\,\sigma\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}^{2}

\displaystyle+\Biggl[\left(1-\frac{\alpha^{2}\,\kappa_{i}^{2}}{\beta_{i}}\right)-\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})^{2}}{\delta}\,C_{tr,\sigma}^{2}\Biggr]\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}^{2}

\displaystyle+\Bigl[\kappa_{i}^{2}-\beta_{i}-\gamma_{i}\,C_{P}^{2}-\delta\,C_{tr,u}^{2}\,(1+C_{P}^{2})\Bigr]\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{i})}^{2}\Biggr\}.

In a first step, choose

\delta=\theta\frac{\min_{i=1,2}\left\{\kappa_{i}^{2}-\beta_{i}-\gamma_{i}C_{P}^{2}\right\}}{C_{tr,u}^{2}\left(1+C_{P}^{2}\right)}\ .

According to Lemma
4.1

1-\alpha
can now be chosen small enough to ensure that all the terms remains positive.

4.3
ReLU Activation

Equations involving ReLU-type nonlinearities appear frequently in control theory and physics-informed machine learning. A representative example is

-\Delta u+\max(u,0)=f\quad\text{in }\Omega\ .

To cast this second-order PDE into the presented framework, a flux variable

\sigma=\nabla u
is introduced.
Then the PDE is reformulated as first-order system

\displaystyle\sigma-\nabla u

\displaystyle=0,\quad\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma+\max(u,0)

\displaystyle=f\ .

(22)

Note that setting

\begin{array}[]{rcl}L_{1}(u,\sigma)&:=&\sigma-\nabla u,\\[2.84526pt]
L_{2}(u,\sigma)&:=&-\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma,\\[2.84526pt]
K(u)&:=&\max(u,0).\end{array}

with the corresponding Least-Squares functional

\|\sigma-\nabla u\|+\|-\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma+\max(u,0)\|.

would not satisfy assumptions
2
. In fact, the nonlinear term
K(u)=\max(u,0)
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
L=1
while in the analysis of the previous examples, one obtains

\|L_{1}(u,\sigma)\|+\|L_{2}(u,\sigma)\|\geq C_{1}||u||^{2}_{1}+C_{2}||\sigma||^{2}_{div}
with
C_{1}<1
.

K(u)
therefore needs to be shifted. To this end, note that the function
\max(u,0)-\delta u
for some
\delta>0
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
\max(|1-\delta|,|\delta|)
.
Weighting the first term with
\kappa
and choosing
\delta>1
it remains to prove

\mathcal{F}_{2}(u,\sigma;0)=\|L_{1}(u,\sigma)\|+\|L_{2}(u,\sigma)\|\geq C_{1}||u||^{2}_{1}+C_{2}||\sigma||^{2}_{div}

with
C_{1}>\delta

for

\begin{array}[]{rcl}L_{1}(u,\sigma):=\kappa(\sigma-\nabla u),\quad L_{2}(u,\sigma):=-\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma+\delta u\quad K(u):=\max(u,0)-\delta u\ .\end{array}

The corresponding linear Least-Squares functional reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2,l}(u,\sigma;f)

\displaystyle=\|\kappa(\sigma-\nabla u)\|^{2}+\|\delta u-\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma-f\|^{2}

and integration by parts leads to

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2,l}(u,\sigma;0)

\displaystyle=\|L_{1}(u,\sigma)\|^{2}+\|L_{2}(u,\sigma)\|^{2}=\kappa^{2}\|\sigma\|^{2}+\kappa^{2}\|\nabla u\|^{2}+\|\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma\|^{2}+\delta^{2}\|u\|^{2}-2(\kappa^{2}-\delta)\langle\sigma,\nabla u\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\left[\kappa^{2}-\varepsilon(\kappa^{2}-\delta)\right]\|\sigma\|^{2}+\left[\kappa^{2}-\frac{\kappa^{2}-\delta}{\varepsilon}\right]\|\nabla u\|^{2}+\|\operatorname{div}\sigma\|^{2}+\delta^{2}\|u\|^{2}.

For
1<\delta<\kappa^{2}
,

\varepsilon
can be chosen to satisfy

1<\varepsilon<\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{2}-\delta}.

and it holds

\kappa^{2}-\frac{\kappa^{2}-\delta}{\varepsilon}>\kappa^{2}-(\kappa^{2}-\delta)=\delta\ .

Since
\delta>1
,
\delta^{2}>\delta
this leads to

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2,l}(u,\sigma;0)>C_{2}\|\sigma\|^{2}_{\text{div}}+\delta\|u\|^{2}_{1}

with
C_{2}>0
.
Consequently, the assumptions
1
and
2
hold true and the theorems
3.1
and
3.3
apply. The Least-Squares functional
\mathcal{F}_{2,l}
can be used as an error indicator, as in the following numerical example based on the previously used L-shape
\Omega=[-1,\,1]^{2}\backslash[0,1]

4.4
Nonlinear elasticity

A fundamental component in structural analysis is the use of displacement-based finite element formulations, which form the basis of many mechanical simulations in engineering practice. In most scenarios, materials are modeled either using elastic or elastoplastic constitutive laws, with a significant portion of applications assuming elastic behavior under small deformations or, more generally, large deformations at small strains. While elasticity for small deformations leads to linear systems of equations, transition to the large deformation settings introduces nonlinearities. This section focuses on large deformations at small strains, for which the Saint Venant–Kirchhoff model provides a prototypical formulation.
Based on deformation gradient and Green-Lagrange strain

\bm{F}(u)=\bm{I}+\nabla u\quad\text{respectively}\quad\bm{E}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{F}^{\mathrm{T}}(u)\bm{F}(u)-\bm{I}\right)\;,

(23)

the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress reads

\sigma(u)=\bm{F}(u)\left(2\,\bm{E}(u)+\widetilde{\lambda}\,\mathrm{tr}\,\bm{E}(u)\,\bm{I}\right)=2\,\varepsilon(u)+\widetilde{\lambda}\mathrm{tr}\,\varepsilon(u)\,\mathbf{I}+k(u)\;,

(24)

with the nonlinearity

k(u)=\left[2\,\nabla^{\mathrm{T}}u+\nabla u+\nabla u\nabla^{\mathrm{T}}u+\widetilde{\lambda}\,\mathrm{tr}\,\bm{E}(u)\right]\,\nabla u+\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}}{2}\,\mathrm{tr}\left(\nabla^{\mathrm{T}}u\nabla u\right)\,\mathbf{I}\,.

The parameter
\widetilde{\lambda}
is thereby the ratio of the first- and the second Lamé parameter. Note that the classical notation from elasticity theory
\bm{P}
(see e.g.
[
10
]
) is not used here to highlight conformance with the presented framework.
Based on the compliance of linear elasticity

\mathcal{A}\tau=\frac{1}{2}\left[\tau-\frac{\widetilde{\lambda}}{2+d\widetilde{\lambda}}\,\mathrm{tr}\,\tau\,\mathbf{I}\right]\;,

(25)

and the conservation of linear momentum, weighted with a parameter
\kappa>0
, the Least-Squares functional of the considered problem reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{3}(u,\sigma;f)=\,\kappa^{2}\|\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma+f\|^{2}+\|\mathcal{A}\sigma-\varepsilon(u)-\mathcal{A}k(u)\|^{2}\;.

(26)

Within the notation of the presented framework, the unconstrained minimization problem

\displaystyle\text{minimize}\;\mathcal{F}_{3}(u,\sigma;f)=\|L_{1}(\sigma)+f\|^{2}+\|L_{2}(u,\sigma)+K(u)\|^{2}\

(27)

in
\left(H_{\Gamma_{D}}^{1}(\Omega)\right)^{d}\times\left(H_{\Gamma_{N}}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)\right)^{d}
, with

\displaystyle K(u)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}k(u)

\displaystyle L_{1}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\kappa\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma

\displaystyle L_{2}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}\sigma-\varepsilon(u)

has to be solved.

As
K(u)
is differentiable and the linear part of the Least-Squares functional elliptic, as is proven in the following theorem, the framework applies without further difficulties.

Theorem 4.4
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for

\mathcal{F}_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)=\|L_{1}(\sigma)\|^{2}+\|L_{2}(u,\sigma)\|^{2}=\|\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma\|^{2}+\|\mathcal{A}\sigma-\varepsilon(u)\|^{2}

it holds
\mathcal{F}_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)\simeq C\left(\|\sigma\|^{2}_{\text{div}}+\|u\|^{2}_{1}\right)

Proof.

The proof makes use of the equivalence

\displaystyle\|{\sigma}\|\simeq\left(\sqrt{(\mathcal{A}{\sigma},{\sigma})}+\|\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}{\sigma}\|\right)\ .

(28)

In fact, decomposing
\|\sigma\|^{2}=\|\operatorname{dev}\sigma\|^{2}+\frac{1}{d}\|\mathrm{tr}\,\sigma\|^{2}

leads to

(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}\|\operatorname{dev}\sigma\|^{2}+\frac{1}{d(2+d\widetilde{\lambda})}\|\mathrm{tr}\,\sigma\|^{2}

and invoking the trace inequality

\|\mathrm{tr}\,\sigma\|\leq C_{tr}\Bigl(\sqrt{(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)}+\|\nabla\cdot\sigma\|\Bigr)

yields

\|\sigma\|^{2}\leq 2\,(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)+\frac{C_{tr}^{2}}{d}\Bigl[(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)+\|\nabla\cdot\sigma\|^{2}\Bigr].

The Korn inequality allows to bound the cross term as follows

\displaystyle|(\sigma,\epsilon(u))|

\displaystyle=|(\sigma,\nabla u)-\left(\frac{\sigma-\sigma^{T}}{2},\nabla u\right)|\leq\bigl|(\operatorname{div}\sigma,u)\bigr|+\left|\left(\frac{\sigma-\sigma^{T}}{2},\nabla u\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\operatorname{div}\sigma\|\,\|u\|+\left\|\frac{\sigma-\sigma^{T}}{2}\right\|\,\|\nabla u\|\leq C_{K}\left(\|\operatorname{div}\sigma\|+\left\|\frac{\sigma-\sigma^{T}}{2}\right\|\right)\|\epsilon(u)\|\ .

Moreover,

\displaystyle\left\|\sigma-\sigma^{T}\right\|

\displaystyle=2\mu\left\|\left(\frac{1}{2\mu}\,\sigma-\epsilon(u)\right)-\left(\frac{1}{2\mu}\,\sigma-\epsilon(u)\right)^{T}\right\|=2\mu\left\|(\mathcal{A}\sigma-\epsilon(u))-(\mathcal{A}\sigma-\epsilon(u))^{T}\right\|\leq 4\mu\,\|\mathcal{A}\sigma-\epsilon(u)\|

leads to

|(\sigma,\epsilon(u))|\leq C_{K}\Bigl(\|\operatorname{div}\sigma\|+\|\mathcal{A}\sigma-\epsilon(u)\|\Bigr)\|\epsilon(u)\|\ .

Since
\mathcal{A}\sigma
is bounded by
{\sigma}
in the
L^{2}
norm, one can insert

\|\epsilon({u})\|\leq\|\epsilon({u})-\mathcal{A}{\sigma}\|+\|\mathcal{A}{\sigma}\|\leq\|\epsilon(u)-\mathcal{A}{\sigma}\|+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|{\sigma}\|

to obtain

|(\sigma,\epsilon(u))|\leq C_{K}\Bigl(\|\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma\|+\|\mathcal{A}\sigma-\epsilon(u)\|\Bigr)\left(\|\epsilon(u)-\mathcal{A}\sigma\|+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\sigma\|\right).

Therefore,

\begin{split}|(\sigma,\epsilon(u))|&\leq C_{K}\,\sqrt{F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)}\left(\sqrt{F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)}+\|\sigma\|\right)=C_{K}\Bigl(F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)+\sqrt{F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)}\,\|\sigma\|\Bigr)\end{split}

implies

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{A}\sigma-\epsilon(u),\sigma)+(\epsilon(u),\sigma)\leq\|\mathcal{A}\sigma-\epsilon(u)\|\,\|\sigma\|+C_{K}\Bigl(F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)+\sqrt{F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)}\,\|\sigma\|\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)+\left(\|\mathcal{A}\sigma-\epsilon(u)\|+C_{K}\sqrt{F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)}\right)\|\sigma\|\ .

The norm equivalence (
28
)

\|\sigma\|\leq\tilde{C}_{tr}\Bigl(\sqrt{(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)}+\|\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma\|\Bigr)

and
\|\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma\|^{2}\leq F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)

yields

\|\sigma\|\leq\tilde{C}_{tr}\left(\sqrt{(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)}+\sqrt{F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)}\right).

Thus,

\begin{split}\|\mathcal{A}\sigma-\varepsilon(u)\|\|\sigma\|&\leq\|\mathcal{A}\sigma-\varepsilon(u)\|\,\tilde{C}_{tr}\left(\sqrt{(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)}+\sqrt{F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)}\right)\\
&\leq\tilde{C}_{tr}\,\sqrt{F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)}\sqrt{(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)}+\tilde{C}_{tr}\,F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0).\end{split}

and

\begin{split}C_{K}\,\sqrt{F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)}\,\|\sigma\|&\leq C_{K}\,\sqrt{F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)}\,\tilde{C}_{tr}\left(\sqrt{(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)}+\sqrt{F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)}\right)\\
&=C_{K}\,\tilde{C}_{tr}\,\sqrt{F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)}\sqrt{(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)}+C_{K}\,\tilde{C}_{tr}\,F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0).\end{split}

implies

\begin{split}(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)&\leq\Bigl[\tilde{C}_{tr}\,\sqrt{F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)}\sqrt{(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)}+\tilde{C}_{tr}\,F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)\Bigr]+\Bigl[C_{K}\,\tilde{C}_{tr}\,\sqrt{F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)}\sqrt{(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)}+C_{K}\,\tilde{C}_{tr}\,F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)\Bigr]\\
&=\tilde{C}_{tr}(1+C_{K})\,F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)+\tilde{C}_{tr}(1+C_{K})\,\sqrt{F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)}\sqrt{(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)}.\end{split}

Young’s inequality yields

(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)\leq\tilde{C}_{tr}(1+C_{K})\,F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)+\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)+\frac{\tilde{C}_{tr}^{2}(1+C_{K})^{2}}{2}\,F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0)

i.e.

(\mathcal{A}\sigma,\sigma)\leq\Bigl(2\tilde{C}_{tr}(1+C_{K})+\tilde{C}_{tr}^{2}(1+C_{K})^{2}\Bigr)F_{3,l}(u,\sigma;0).

∎

4.5
Sea-Ice

The final example examines a model of sea-ice dynamics governed by the momentum balance
[
3
,
8
]
:

\rho h\,u_{t}=\nabla\cdot\sigma-\tau_{o}(u-v_{o})+F(u).

(29)

Therein,
u
is the velocity of the sea-ice,
\sigma
the stress tensor,
v_{o}
the water velocity,
F(u)
the external forces and
\tau_{o}(u)
the ocean current, involving the water drag coefficient
C_{o}
, and the sea-water density
\rho_{o}
as well as the velocity
u
:

\tau_{o}(u)=\rho_{o}C_{o}|u|u\;.

With an appropriate scaling and the introduction of a parameter
\eta
, the Least-Squares functional is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{4}(u,\sigma;v_{o}):=\|\beta^{-1/2}u+\tau_{o}(u)-\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sigma\|^{2}+\|({2\eta})^{-1/2}\sigma-({2\eta})^{1/2}\varepsilon(u)+({2\eta})^{1/2}\varepsilon(v_{o})\|^{2}\

(30)

for given parameters
\beta,\eta>0
.
Since
\tau_{o}
is differentiable the analysis reduces to the coercivity of the linear functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{4,l}(u,\sigma;0):=\|\beta^{-1/2}u-\text{div }\sigma\|^{2}+\|({2\eta})^{-1/2}\sigma-({2\eta})^{1/2}\varepsilon(u)\|^{2}\

(31)

in
\left(H_{\Gamma_{D}}^{1}(\Omega)\right)^{d}\times\left(H_{\Gamma_{N}}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)\right)^{d}
, shown in
[
6
]
. We refer to
[
3
]
for numerical examples on this problem.
