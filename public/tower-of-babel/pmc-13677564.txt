Catching Two Birds with One Stone: A Primary Open Revascularisation First Approach in a Case Report of Chronic Mesenteric Ischaemia
Authors: Nelson Oliveira, Manuel João Silva, Bruno Pereirinha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSymptomatic chronic mesenteric artery occlusive disease requires timely revascularisation. Although endovascular management is guideline endorsed as first line treatment, inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) revascularisation lacks evidence based guidance. This report presents a case in which an open repair first approach was selected with the IMA as the primary revascularisation target, aiming to provide long term benefit while enabling concomitant superior mesenteric artery (SMA) revascularisation.ReportA 60 year old woman, who was a smoker (40 pack/year), had a past medical history of hypertension and hypercholesterolemia, and a current body mass index of 17 kg/m2, was diagnosed with chronic mesenteric ischaemia (CMI). Anatomically, while both the coeliac and SMA were occluded, severe stenosis of the IMA ostium was also present. An open revascularisation addressing the IMA disease with venous patch and concomitant aorta to SMA bypass was performed through a limited midline transperitoneal approach without adverse events. The patient was discharged two days post-operatively. At one year follow up, an asymptomatic bypass stenosis just after the IMA take off (approximately 2 cm from the proximal anastomosis) was identified on duplex ultrasound (peak systolic velocity 396 cm/second, end diastolic velocity 35 cm/second) and subsequently treated with a paclitaxel eluting balloon (5 x 60 mm) with angiographic resolution. No signs of IMA re-stenosis were detected. At the six month post-angioplasty follow up, no signs of clinical or haemodynamic recurrence were noted in the SMA bypass or IMA ostium.ConclusionIn selected CMI patients, an open first revascularisation strategy may be performed with a low risk of adverse events.Keywords: Case report, Mesenteric artery bypass, Mesenteric artery occlusive disease, Open revascularisation, Vascular surgery

Introduction

Chronic mesenteric ischaemia (CMI) typically presents with postprandial abdominal pain and food aversion, leading to weight loss.1 Atypical presentations may include sustained abdominal discomfort, nausea, vomiting, or altered intestinal transit, which may delay the diagnosis of CMI. Revascularisation is warranted for patients with multivessel occlusive disease and chronic mesenteric ischaemia (class I, level B recommendation in the 2025 European Society for Vascular Surgery [ESVS] guidelines). The guidelines endorse the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) as the recommended revascularisation target (class I, level C). An endovascular first approach is preferred (class I, level B), with routine mesenteric artery stenting favoured over plain balloon angioplasty (class I, level C).1

This report presents a case of open revascularisation targeting the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA), which served as the inflow for a simultaneous SMA revascularisation. This choice of the well developed IMA as the primary revascularisation target was based on its calibre and patency, despite the presence of an ostial stenosis, easy accessibility through a limited midline transperitoneal approach, and the lack of evidence- based guidance on the management of IMA obstructive disease.

Report

A 60 year-old woman, who was a smoker (40 pack/year), had a past medical history of dyslipidaemia and hyperthyroidism, without any weight loss over the previous four years but with a long lasting modified diet pattern and body mass index (BMI) of 17 kg/m2, underwent abdominal computed tomography angiography (CTA) due to intermittent nausea and abdominal discomfort that was unrelated to meals. Worsening symptoms were perceived following a Covid-19 infection that occurred one year before. CTA confirmed a flush occlusion of both the coeliac artery (CA) and SMA (the CA was patent at its bifurcation, measuring 4.1 mm in diameter, while the SMA measured 3.1 mm in diameter at its origin, and was reconstituted three cm from its origin), while the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) had a > 70% ostial stenosis (Fig. 1A), which was corroborated on coloured duplex ultrasound (DUS) (peak systolic velocity [PSV] of 353 cm/second and end diastolic velocity [EDV] of 150 cm/second). Given the combination of the vascular occlusions (21 points on the ESVS prediction score1) and low BMI, CMI was suspected. From the remainder of the vascular assessment, the patient had intact upper and lower limb pulses, and bilateral atheromatous lesions (Carotid plaque RADS 2)2 was documented on carotid artery coloured DUS assessment.

Images showing: A) baseline computed tomography angiography evidencing inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) ostial stenosis, and superior mesenteric artery (SMA) reconstitution; B) intra-operative field with aortic–SMA bypass with IMA ostial plasty (overpassing the IMA take off, solid arrow) extending into the aorta (1.5 cm, asterisk) and a lazy C trajectory, mimicking a neo-Villemin arcade, towards the SMA.

From a limited midline transperitoneal approach (10 cm incision), IMA ostial arterioplasty was performed with a venous patch, which extended cranially along the aortic wall for a length of 1 cm (Fig. 1B, asterisk). The vein conduit (right great saphenous vein, harvested from the thigh) was fashioned as a patch overpassing the original IMA ostium (Fig. 1B, solid arrow) and used simultaneously as a bypass to the SMA, tunnelled with a short lazy C trajectory through the mesentery (Fig. 1B, dashed arrow), reproducing a neo-Villemin arcade. Intra-operative control imaging was not performed. The patient was discharged uneventfully two days post-operatively, maintaining her pre-operative dose of 100 mg of acetylsalicylic acid and 40 mg of atorvastatin, to which rivaroxaban (2.5 mg twice a day) was added for the first six months. Over the first six post-operative months, her BMI increased to 25 kg/m2 and abdominal complaints remained absent.

At one-year follow-up, despite no complaints of abdominal pain or changes in diet intake, DUS detected a stenosis (PSV 396 cm/second, EDV 35 cm/second) following the IMA take off (approximately two cm from the proximal anastomosis). While the IMA ostium was widely patent (Fig. 2A, asterisk), a conduit stenosis was confirmed on CTA and subsequent angiography (Fig. 2A and B, solid arrow). Following pre-dilation with a 4 mm plain old balloon, paclitaxel-eluting balloon angioplasty (5 x 60 mm, Pacific TM Plus, Medtronic. Minneapolis, MN) was successfully performed through a femoral steerable sheath access (Fig. 2C). The patient was discharged on a dual antiplatelet regimen (acetylsalicylic acid 100 mg and clopidogrel 75 mg per day) for three months. The patient remained asymptomatic and free of re-stenosis on follow up DUS at six months (PSV 216 cm/second, EDV 46 cm/second). Informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient.

Notice superior mesenteric artery patency (asterisk) and well developed meandering marginal artery (arrow).

Discussion

This report presents a case of CMI with open revascularisation targeting the IMA, which served as the inflow for simultaneous SMA revascularisation. Immediate relief of chronic mesenteric ischaemia was achieved, with rapid recovery of body weight.

This strategy may seem inconsistent with the recently published ESVS guidelines, which endorse an endovascular first approach for the invasive treatment of CMI. Accordingly, open surgery is reserved for fit patients with CMI deemed unfavourable for endovascular treatment or after failure of an endovascular strategy (class I, level C).1 This recommendation is supported by a systematic review reporting a reduced relative risk (RR) of complications compared with open revascularisation (RR 2.19, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.84 – 2.60). Importantly, although no differences were observed in the 30-day mortality (RR 1.57, 95% CI 0.84 – 2.93) and three-year survival rates, open surgery was associated with a lower risk of three-year symptom recurrence (RR 0.47, 95% CI 0.34 – 0.66).3 In another multicentre study, the endovascular group had a lower 30-day mortality rate than the open repair group (2.4% vs. 8.7%), despite a greater comorbidity burden.4 Similarly, Lehane et al. reported superior value (defined as life years/100k in charges) for endovascular repair over open repair, up to five-year follow up, driven by a lower rate of complications and shorter hospitalisation duration for the index revascularisation.5 However, the endovascular group required more re-interventions (p =.044). From a morphological perspective, both the CA and SMA presented with flush occlusions in this patient, with the CA reconstituting at its bifurcation and the SMA being a small calibre vessel (3 mm). These findings were considered limitations for an endovascular approach, both in terms of technical success and long-term durability, favouring open repair.

According to the guidelines, the SMA is the preferred target vessel for revascularisation in CMI. However, the IMA is also a valid alternative revascularisation target and can serve as an inflow source for renal and visceral revascularisation.1,6 In this patient, the IMA was widely patent and relatively spared from atherosclerotic involvement (except at its ostium). Importantly, IMA angioplasty could have been attempted, and although this approach has been reported in small case series, long-term outcomes are lacking.1,7, 8, 9 Furthermore, IMA ostial stenosis is frequently associated with post-stenotic dilation of the IMA, making stent sizing challenging and increasing the risk of excessive oversizing or stent malapposition. Moreover, an early left colic artery branch may also be a potential caveat for IMA stenting, particularly if covered stents are used like those recommended for the SMA.1 Thus, considering this patient’s age and low burden of comorbidities, and despite the investigator’s centre experience and preference for an endovascular first approach for SMA occlusive disease, an open approach was chosen. The risk of surgical site and graft infection was also considered. Bacterial translocation, due to increased intestinal permeability following surgical trauma, transient intestinal ischaemia, anaesthetic agents, and blood loss. may occur early after abdominal surgery.10 However, the availability of an autologous conduit carried a low risk of post-operative graft infection.

In this patient, a single vessel revascularisation approach could have been sufficient to clinically reverse CMI symptoms. However, because the primary target vessel was the IMA, concomitant SMA revascularisation was considered consistent with the ESVS guidelines, which endorse the SMA as the main revascularisation target in patients with CMI.1 Importantly, this choice did not add further intra-operative complexity, given the anatomic proximity and accessibility of the SMA and the planned conduit harvest. Furthermore, a reduced risk of graft compression or torsion was anticipated, given the relative proximity and consequent short tunnelling distance. Finally, a potential long term patency benefit was hypothesised, with incremental flow through the common IMA and bypass origin. However, early SMA bypass re-stenosis detected at the six month follow up, albeit corrected with a secondary intervention, may indicate limited longer term patency of this component of the revascularisation. Most importantly, there was no evidence of IMA re-stenosis.

Despite the insidious clinical presentation of this patient, the rapid post-operative BMI recovery provides diagnostic evidence of this patient’s symptomatic CMI and underscores the need for enhanced clinical attention, with careful consideration of subtle clinical manifestations before dismissing the patient from revascularisation. Additional investigations, such as visible light spectroscopy, oesophagogastroduodenoscopy, and colonoscopy, are useful in cases of diagnostic uncertainty.11,12 However, careful patient assessment that adequately values atypical symptoms and physical findings remains paramount for a correct diagnosis.
