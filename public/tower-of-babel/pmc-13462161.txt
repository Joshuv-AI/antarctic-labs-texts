False Friends in Fluorescence – Simple HPLC Evaluation of False Positive Fragment Hits in a DiFMUP Assay for Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase 1B
Authors: Martin Schwer, Sven R Aldea, Benedikt Masberg, Michael Laemmerhofer, Frank M Boeckler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDespite their essential role in the reversible protein phosphorylation cycle governing cellular signaling and pathogenesis, phosphatases are frequently overlooked in drug development compared to protein kinases. Protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B) is a prominent therapeutic target due to its crucial role in the etiology of diabetes, obesity, and cancer. Consequently, the goal of this study was to identify fragments inhibiting PTP1B via the fluorogenic DiFMUP assay. Furthermore, we wanted to develop a method to evaluate our primary hits and identify optical artifacts associated with these standard fluorogenic screening assays.MethodsUtilizing the DiFMUP assay, we screened four fragment libraries against PTP1B. To identify false positives caused by spectral interference, the preliminary hits were evaluated through a fluorescence control experiment. Furthermore, we developed and validated a rapid HPLC method to directly quantify the substrate (DiFMUP) and product (DiFMU) for orthogonal verification independent of optical artifacts.ResultsOur initial screening filtered 515 compounds down to 56 preliminary hits. The subsequent fluorescence control experiment revealed a high count of optically interfering fragments. Eventually, the direct quantification of DiFMUP and DiFMU by HPLC yielded three compounds demonstrating inhibition independent of optical interference.ConclusionOur fragment screening identified three verified hits. Furthermore, we developed and validated a simple and rapid HPLC-based evaluation method. Although this orthogonal approach does not account for non-specific mechanisms like protein aggregation or oxidation, it serves as a highly useful tool to verify hits from fluorogenic assays. We propose the described screening and verification workflow as an efficient bridge between high-throughput optical assays and resource-intensive downstream testing.Keywords: FBDD, protein phosphorylation, covalent inhibitors, enzyme inhibition

Introduction

Dysregulated phosphorylation disrupts signal transduction and contributes to numerous human diseases. Consequently, the selective control of signaling networks represents a major therapeutic frontier, evidenced by the clinical success of kinase inhibitors in oncology and immunology.1 However, drug discovery remains heavily focused on kinases, often neglecting the essential role of phosphatases in the reversible phosphorylation cycle. While signaling outcomes depend on the balance between both enzyme classes, protein phosphatases remain an underutilized resource for drug development. One prominent example is Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase 1B (PTP1B), a non-receptor phosphatase encoded by the PTPN1 gene. Since its discovery in 1988 by Tonks et al,2,3 PTP1B has become a validated drug target for diabetes and obesity due to its role in regulating insulin and leptin signaling.4,5 Additionally, PTP1B is implicated in the pathophysiology of other conditions, including cancer and neurodegenerative diseases, underscoring its broad therapeutic relevance.5–7 However, developing small-molecule PTP1B inhibitors remains challenging due to specific biochemical constraints. First, the active site is highly conserved across the PTP family, complicating the identification of selective ligands. Second, the positively charged binding pocket often necessitates anionic and highly polar inhibitors, which typically suffer from poor bioavailability and limited drug-likeness.6,8,9 Catalysis is mediated by a nucleophilic cysteine assisted by an essential arginine. Importantly, this cysteine undergoes reversible oxidation as a regulatory mechanism but is also susceptible to irreversible inactivation by various oxidizing agents.10–12 To overcome the inherent challenges associated with PTP1B, several non-classical drug design strategies have been applied. These include the use of prodrugs to bypass bioavailability barriers13,14 and the targeting of the inactive open-state conformation of the enzyme.15 Furthermore, allosteric inhibition16,17 and the covalent targeting of nucleophilic residues such as the catalytic Cys215 or the allosteric Cys121 have emerged as promising methods to achieve higher selectivity.18–21 Additionally, fragment-based drug discovery (FBDD) has been employed to identify novel scaffolds with optimized physicochemical properties that occupy previously unidentified binding sites, distant from the conserved catalytic center.22,23

The reliable monitoring of enzymatic activity is essential for developing potent inhibitors, particularly as the progress of drug discovery is often hindered by questionable data quality and significant variability across different biochemical assay formats.24 Various assays have been established to measure PTP activity. Early discontinuous methods relied on radioactive 32P-labeled phosphotyrosyl proteins or peptides,3 colorimetric phosphomolybdate detection,25,26 antiphosphotyrosine monoclonal antibodies,27,28 or HPLC-based separation of phosphorylated and dephosphorylated peptides.28–30 In contrast, modern continuous assays utilize the enzymatic hydrolysis of arylphosphate moieties from synthetic substrates to yield spectroscopically active products. These methods are preferred for their convenience in determining kinetic parameters and their ability to minimize sample manipulation.31,32 A widely used example is the colorless substrate para-nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP), which is hydrolyzed to the bright yellow para-nitrophenol and monitored via absorbance at 405 nm.31,33,34 More recently, fluorogenic substrates such as fluorescein diphosphate (FDP),35,36 3-O-methylfluorescein phosphate (OMFP),37 and 6,8-difluoro-4-methylumbelliferyl phosphate (DiFMUP)38,39 have gained popularity. These substrates are several orders of magnitude more sensitive than pNPP, significantly reducing the amount of recombinant enzyme required.40 Furthermore, they offer an excellent signal-to-noise ratio and a broad linear range for fluorescence emission. However, fluorogenic assays are susceptible to spectral interference from compound autofluorescence, absorption, fluorescence quenching, or light scattering.41–43 OMFP-based assays, yielding methylfluorescein, are more resistant to such interference due to their more red shifted excitation and emission wavelengths (λex=458 nm, λem=535 nm) relative to the DiFMUP assay (λex=360 nm, λem=450 nm). Nevertheless, OMFP has limited aqueous solubility and necessitates initial dissolution in DMSO at millimolar concentrations, which increases the total DMSO content in the final reaction mixture.40,44

In addition to optical interferences, compound-induced oxidation of Cys21530,45–48 or protein aggregation49–51 can lead to false positive results in the enzyme assay. However, these artefacts can be minimized by the presence of a detergent and a reducing agent in the measurement buffer.41

For this study, the fluorogenic DiFMUP assay was selected due to its minimal enzyme requirements and its established utility in PTP1B inhibitor investigation.23,52–56 We screened four fragment libraries comprising a total of 515 compounds. These libraries include electrophiles and compounds enriched with halogens or chalcogens, aiming to target nucleophilic residues covalently or utilize σ-hole interactions to address new binding sites and identify unconventional binding modes.57–61 The Halogen-Enriched Fragment Library (HEFLib) and the Covalent Fragment Library (CovLib) have been previously screened against different targets and yielded promising results, including halogen-bond-driven binding modes and the covalent rescue of proteins.57–59,61–63 The other two libraries, the Vmax-optimized Halogen-Enriched Fragment Library (VmaxLib)64 and the Chalcogen-Enriched Fragment Library (ChalcoLib),65 were recently developed by our group, and no screening results have been published so far.

We evaluated the preliminary hits using a two-batch fluorescence-based DiFMUP control experiment to identify false positives arising from spectral interference. Furthermore, we aimed to develop and establish an HPLC-based method to directly quantify the substrate, DiFMUP, and the product, DiFMU, to provide a rapid and cost-effective orthogonal verification tool independent of optical artifacts. Finally, this chromatographic approach was employed to further evaluate the preliminary hits.

Materials and Methods

Molecular Biology

Protein expression was performed using a custom-synthesized construct (BioCat, Heidelberg, Germany) containing the human PTP1B catalytic domain (residues 1–301) cloned into a pET24a(+)-HLT vector. This construct encodes a fusion protein comprising a His6-tag, a TEV cleavage site, and a Lipoyl domain to enhance solubility. The amino acid sequence of the cleaved protein is listed in Table S 1.1. The plasmid was transformed into E.coli BL 21 (DE3) pLysS cells (Novagen, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). A preculture (1 g tryptone, 0.5 g yeast extract, 2 g glucose per 100 mL) was grown overnight at 37 °C. Main cultures were grown in 2xYT (16 g tryptone, 10 g yeast extract, 15 g NaCl per L) at 37 °C until an OD600 of 0.6–0.8 was reached. Both cultures contained kanamycin (50 µg/mL) and chloramphenicol (34 µg/mL). Protein expression was induced with 0.8 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactoside and performed at 18 °C overnight. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4,000 rpm at 4 °C for 30 min. Harvested cell pellets were resuspended in buffer A (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100, 1mM TCEP, 5 mM imidazole) and cracked by sonification using Bandelin Sonopuls HD 3200 with KE76 sonicator (Bandelin, Berlin, Germany) after addition of Ribonuclease and Desoxyribonuclease. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 18,500 rpm for 1h at 4 °C, filtered through a 0.22 µM membrane and subsequently loaded onto 2×5 mL HisTrapTM FF Ni2+ columns (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) pre-equilibrated with buffer A. After washing the column to baseline with buffer A, the protein was eluted with buffer B (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100, 1mM TCEP, 300 mM imidazole). The HLT-Tag was removed by cleavage using TEV-protease while dialyzing against buffer C (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100, 1mM TCEP) overnight, yielding to an imidazole concentration of ~5 mM. The cleaved protein was then again loaded onto 2×5 mL HisTrapTM FF Ni2+ columns (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) pre-equilibrated with buffer A and subsequently washed with buffer A. Fractions containing protein were concentrated and loaded onto a size-exclusion HiLoadTM 26/60 SuperdexTM 75 prep grade column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) pre-equilibrated with buffer D (10 mM Tris pH 7.5, 25 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM TCEP). The sample was eluted with buffer D and peak fractions containing PTP1B were pooled, concentrated, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. The purity of the protein was monitored by SDS-PAGE. The identity of the protein was verified by UHPLC-ESI-MS. The resulting MS-spectrum is depicted in Figure S 1.1.

DiFMUP Assay

General Method

The DiFMUP enzyme activity assays were performed according to the procedure described by Montalibet et al32 with some modifications. A 10x stock of assay buffer was prepared (500 mM Bis-Tris pH 6.3 and 20 mM EDTA) and stored at 4 °C. Prior to use, the 10x stock was diluted to 1x, supplemented with 1 mM TCEP, and readjusted to pH 6.3. An enzyme dilution buffer was prepared by adding 20% (v/v) glycerol and 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100 to the 1x assay buffer. DiFMUP was prepared as a 1 mM stock solution in DMSO. Assays were performed in black PS low-binding 96-well plates F bottom (Greiner Bio-One, Kremsmünster, Austria) with a final reaction volume of 100 µL. Each well contained 85 µL assay buffer, 10 µL of PTP1B in enzyme dilution buffer, and 5 µL of DiFMUP in DMSO. The final reaction mixture contained 2% glycerol, 0.01% Triton X-100, and 5% DMSO. The buffer components were selected for specific functions: DMSO ensures compound solubility, whereas Triton X-100 and glycerol prevent protein aggregation and non-specific binding to the microplate. TCEP maintains the active-site cysteine in a reduced state, and EDTA chelates trace metal impurities that could otherwise cause PTP1B inhibition.32,41 Fluorescence intensity was monitored using a CLARIOstar plate reader (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany) with excitation at 360 nm, emission at 450 nm, and the dichroic filter set at 403.8 nm. Plates were shaken at 500 rpm for 20s prior to measurement.

To validate the enzymatic activity, the Michaelis constant (KM) was determined using 100 pM PTP1B and a serial dilution of DiFMUP starting at 100 µM. Fluorescence intensity was monitored for 20 min. Initial reaction rates were calculated from the slope of the linear portion of the fluorescence versus time plots. The reaction rates were plotted against substrate concentration and fitted to the Michaelis-Menten equation using OriginPro 2020 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA) to determine the Km value. This Plot is depicted in Figure S 1.2.

Primary Screening

Primary screening was performed using 100 nM PTP1B and 10 µM DiFMUP. Compounds were added from DMSO stocks to a final concentration of 2 mM, resulting in a final DMSO content of 5% (v/v). Compounds were tested in triplicate. Additionally, negative controls (lacking substrate or enzyme) and positive controls (containing enzyme and substrate but no compound) were included to calculate relative inhibition. Plates were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with shaking, followed by endpoint fluorescence intensity measurement. Relative residual enzyme activity (Arel) was calculated relative to the mean of fluorescence intensity of the positive controls (µp). Two-Batch Control Assay

To evaluate potential assay interference caused by intrinsic compound absorption, a control experiment was performed at a final compound concentration of 0.5 mM. Compounds were tested in triplicate. In parallel to the standard screening assay, a control set was prepared by incubating the reaction mixtures in the absence of compounds. The compounds were then added after the 1 h enzymatic reaction period, immediately prior to fluorescence measurement. A decrease in fluorescence intensity observed upon post-incubation addition of the compound was attributed to inner filter effects rather than enzymatic inhibition.

HPLC Evaluation Assay

To distinguish true enzymatic inhibition from fluorescence interference effects, reaction mixtures were separated and analyzed by HPLC. Assays were performed in a final volume of 200 µL containing 1 mM compound, 100 µM DiFMUP, and 200 nM PTP1B. For the concentration-dependent-measurements, compound concentrations of 5, 2.5, 1.25, 0.625, 0.313, and 0.156 mM were employed. After incubation for 1 h at room temperature with shaking, protein was precipitated by the addition of 100 µL of 35% (v/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA). The mixture was vortexed, incubated on ice for 15 min, and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to HPLC vials. Negative controls (lacking enzyme) and positive controls (lacking compound) were included to reference values for DiFMUP and DiFMU, respectively. Samples were analyzed using an Ultimate 3000 HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Dreieich, Germany). The system was equipped with a ReproSil-XR 120 C18, 5 μm, 150 mm x 4.6 mm column (Dr. Maisch GmbH, Ammerbuch-Entringen, Germany) with column temperature set to 25 °C. Mobile phase A was 10 mM phosphate buffer pH 2.3 and mobile phase B was MeOH. The flow rate was set to 0.5 mL per min with the following gradient: 0–9 min: 10–85% B, 9–13 min: 85% B, 13–14 min: 85–10% B, 14–18 min: 10% B. 5 µL of the reaction mixture was injected onto the column. Peak areas were integrated at 280 nm (DiFMUP) and 320 nm (DiFMU). The relative AUCs of remaining substrate (DiFMUP) and formed product (DiFMU) were calculated relative to the AUCs of the negative and positive control.

HPLC Method Validation

To evaluate system suitability, samples of DiFMUP and DiFMU were prepared in water containing 5% (v/v) DMSO at concentrations of 10, 50, and 100 µM. Each concentration was analyzed in six replicates. Mean retention times, peak areas, and their respective relative standard deviations (RSD) were calculated. Additionally, the symmetry factor according to the European Pharmacopoeia was determined for each injection using Chromeleon 7.2.10 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Dreieich, Germany) and reported as a mean value.

To evaluate method specificity, a positive control, a negative control, and a buffer blank were analyzed to ensure no interference from the sample matrix. Samples were prepared by mixing 170 µL assay buffer with 8 µL DMSO, followed by either 2 µL DiFMUP (positive control) or 2 µL DMSO (negative control and buffer blank). Subsequently, 20 µL PTP1B in enzyme dilution buffer (positive control) or plain enzyme dilution buffer (negative control and buffer blank) was added. The final assay concentrations were 5% DMSO (v/v), 100 µM DiFMUP, and 200 nM PTP1B, where applicable. Incubation and HPLC sample preparation were conducted as described above.

Calibration curves were constructed for DiFMUP and DiFMU from 0.1 to 500 µM in water with the addition of 5% DMSO (v/v). Measurements were performed in triplicate. The peak areas were integrated at 280 nm (DiFMUP) and 320 nm (DiFMU) and plotted versus the nominal concentrations to establish the regression equations. Calculation was performed with OriginPro2019b (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA) via weighted linear regression with a weighting scheme of 1/SD.2

To calculate the Limit of Detection (LOD) and Limit of Quantification (LOQ), additional calibration curves were constructed using concentrations ranging from 0.039 to 10 µM in water containing 5% (v/v) DMSO. Measurements were performed in triplicate. Signal-to-noise (S/N) ratios for the DiFMUP (280 nm) and DiFMU (320 nm) peaks were calculated in Chromeleon 7.2.10 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Dreieich, Germany) using a peak-to-peak noise evaluation over a time window of 20 times the peak width at half-height. The S/N ratios were plotted against the nominal concentrations, followed by linear regression using OriginPro 2019b. The LOD and LOQ were calculated from the resulting regression equations at S/N ratios of 3 and 10, respectively.

To evaluate intra-day and inter-day precision, samples of DiFMUP and DiFMU were prepared in water containing 5% (v/v) DMSO at concentrations of 10, 50, and 100 µM. Each concentration was analyzed in six replicates on two different days. Assay precision was expressed as the relative standard deviation (%RSD).

Method accuracy was evaluated through a spiking study. Samples were prepared by mixing 170 µL assay buffer with 8 µL DMSO, followed by 20 µL PTP1B in enzyme dilution buffer (protein samples) or plain enzyme dilution buffer (buffer samples). After incubation for 1 h at room temperature with shaking, the samples were spiked with 2 µL DiFMUP or DiFMU to yield final concentrations of 1, 10, and 100 µM. Subsequent protein precipitation and sample preparation were conducted as described above. All samples were measured in triplicate. Accuracy was expressed as percentage recovery, calculated by comparing the measured concentrations against the nominal concentrations.

Intact Protein MS

PTP1B at an approximately 10 µM concentration in buffer D (10 mM Tris pH 7.5, 25 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM TCEP) was filtered through a 0.22 µM polyvinylidenfluorid (PVDF) syringe filter into an HPLC vial. Intact protein mass analysis was performed using UHPLC-ESI-MS measurements using a C4-bonded 400 Å 2.7 μm superficially porous particle reversed-phase column and a time-of-flight mass analyzer. Data acquisition and data analysis were performed as previously described.57,59 The deconvoluted MS spectrum is depicted in Figure S 1.1.

The glutathione reactivity assessment was performed as previously described.57,61

Results and Discussion

Protein Expression and Purification

PTP1B catalytic domain (residues 1–301) was heterologous expressed in E.coli BL21 pLysS and purified using Ni2+-affinity and size exclusion chromatography. The respective mass was verified by intact protein ESI MS. The corresponding spectrum is depicted in Figure S 1.1. To validate the enzymatic activity, a kinetic DiFMUP assay was performed and a Michaelis constant (KM) of 6.7 ± 0.9 µM was calculated, matching to a KM of 5.5 µM determined by Montalibet et al.32

Fragment Screening

To identify potential PTP1B inhibitors, a primary screen of 515 fragments was conducted across four diverse libraries: a Covalent Fragment Library (CovLib),57 a Halogen-Enriched Fragment Library (HEFLib),60 a Vmax-optimized Halogen-Enriched Fragment Library (VmaxLib),64 and a Chalcogen-Enriched Fragment Library (ChalcoLib).65 Structural information for all screened compounds is provided as SMILES codes in Table S 2.1. An overview of the fragment screening and the following verification steps is illustrated in Figure 1.

Overview of fragment screening and verification steps.

The screening was performed at a compound concentration of 2 mM using a DiFMUP-based fluorescence assay. All assay components were integrated into a single-step mixing protocol, followed by the determination of endpoint fluorescence intensity after shaking for 1 h. Two sets of negative controls were employed: one omitting the enzyme to monitor spontaneous substrate hydrolysis, and another omitting the substrate to assess potential compound autofluorescence.

To evaluate the statistical robustness of the assay, the Z′-factor was determined using compound-free positive controls and negative controls containing 10 mM vanadate. The resulting Z′-factor of 0.980 demonstrates exceptional assay robustness and reliability.

Of the 515 compounds screened, 56 preliminary hits were identified. An overview of the hit counts and corresponding hit rates across the investigated fragment libraries is presented in Table 1. The complete hit profile, including standard deviations and specific inhibition values for all fragments, is provided in the Supporting Information (Table S 3.1). The overall hit rate was 10.9%, while the library-specific hit rates were relatively similar, ranging from 5.05% for HEFLib to 18.9% for ChalcoLib.

Hit Evaluation

To ensure the robustness of the preliminary hits, a control experiment was performed to identify potential false positives. Due to its relatively low excitation wavelength, the DiFMUP assay is particularly susceptible to inner filter effects, which occur when fragments absorb light at the operational wavelengths of the assay.32,40

This evaluation step involved a comparison of two distinct batches at a compound concentration of 0.5 mM. In the first batch, fragments were added prior to incubation to monitor their effect on the active enzymatic reaction. In the second batch, fragments were added only after the enzymatic reaction was complete to isolate their optical influence on the final fluorescent product, DiFMU. True inhibitors should exhibit reduced fluorescence pre-incubation but maximal relative fluorescence post-incubation. Conversely, fragments causing inner filter effects should demonstrate consistent signal suppression in both batches, as the interference is independent of enzymatic activity. The results of this control experiment and the corresponding chemical structures are presented in Figure 2. The results indicate that the majority of tested fragments exhibit significant inner filter effects. Only eleven compounds (SN050, 0467, 1244, 1264, 0468, 1222, VM008, VM025, CL002, CL020, and CL022) demonstrated minor optical interference under the assay conditions.

HPLC Assay Development and Validation

Given the high prevalence of interfering fragments, we sought to investigate whether the observed decrease in fluorescence intensity resulted from a genuine reduction in DiFMU concentration. To address this objective, an HPLC-based method was developed to directly quantify the enzymatic product, DiFMU, and the substrate, DiFMUP, independently of fluorescence interference. To ensure detectable signal levels for chromatographic evaluation, the concentrations of both the enzyme and substrate were increased to 200 nM PTP1B and 100 µM DiFMUP, compared to the fluorescence intensity-based assays. Following the enzymatic reaction, proteins were precipitated using trichloroacetic acid (TCA), and the resulting supernatant was analyzed. The concentrations of DiFMU and DiFMUP were determined relative to positive (no compound) and negative (no enzyme) controls by monitoring their characteristic absorbance at 320 nm and 280 nm, respectively. This dual-wavelength readout provides a robust secondary verification, particularly in cases where fragment retention times might overlap with those of the analyte or substrate. As summarized in Table 2, the relative standard deviations (RSD) for retention times and peak areas across all six replicates were below 2.0%. Results of the HPLC Assay Verification

The results of the HPLC-based verification are illustrated in Figure 2, with detailed numerical values provided in Table S 3.1. Only three compounds showed relevant levels of unconverted DiFMUP: VM025 (40%), 0467 (33%), and 1244 (22%). Several other compounds, including CA007, SN050, 1264, 0468, and 1217, exhibited only small amounts of unconverted substrate (<5%). Most of these compounds correspond to the hits detected with minor filter effects in the first evaluation procedure. Nevertheless, comparing inhibition levels remains difficult, as the concentrations of both substrate and test compounds were not uniform.

Furthermore, we conducted concentration-dependent measurements for the compounds VM025, 0467, and 1244. The results are illustrated in Figure 4. The four-parameter logistic fit yielded IC50 values of approximately 5 mM or higher for all o three investigated compounds. This reflects the inherently weak affinity typical of initial fragment hits. Additionally, these apparent values are likely inflated by substrate competition, given the high substrate concentrations necessary for reliable HPLC quantification.

Concentration-dependent HPLC assay of compounds VM025, 0467, and 1244. Data points represent the AUC of DiFMU relative to the positive control. The green lines show the four-parameter logistic fit (4PL). The calculated IC50 values for all compounds are approximately 5 mM or higher.

All three hit compounds originate from a Halogen Enriched Fragment Library (HEFLib/VmaxLib), featuring halogens bound to aromatic ring systems. To assess their potential reactivity toward nucleophiles via an SNAr mechanism, we evaluated their thiol reactivity in a standardized glutathione (GSH) assay. The resulting degradation curves are presented in Figure 5. A control measurement in PBS buffer was also conducted to monitor hydrolytic degradation. For comparison, the FDA-approved covalent inhibitor afatinib exhibited a GSH half-life of 1.1 ± 0.0 h under identical conditions.57

Structural formula and degradation curves of the glutathione (GSH) reactivity assessment and PBS buffer control for hit compounds. Pseudo-first-order kinetic fits yielded the following half-lives: VM025 (GSH and PBS > 100 h), 0467 (GSH = 11.2 ± 0.2 h; PBS = 4.4 h), and 1244 (GSH = 67.5 ± 2.2 h; PBS > 100 h).

Fragment 1244 proved stable in aqueous buffer (PBS t1/2 > 100 h) but exhibited a slow reaction with glutathione (GSH t1/2 = 67.5 ± 2.2 h). This reactivity is likely due to an SNAr reaction with chlorine acting as the leaving group. Additionally, the nitro group present in 1244 represents a potential liability, as such motifs are sometimes flagged as Pan-Assay Interference Compounds (PAINS) due to their redox properties. However, nitro compounds are highly abundant in our library since they serve as valuable electron-withdrawing groups. Compound 0467 demonstrated rapid degradation in both PBS (t1/2 = 4.4 h) and in the presence of thiol (GSH t1/2 = 11.2 ± 0.2 h). This molecule contains an isatin (1H-indole-2,3-dione) scaffold, which is known to undergo numerous reactions under diverse conditions.66 However, isatin is also an endogenous compound occurring at tissue concentrations of 0.1 to 10 µM67 and has been shown to covalently modify the SARS-CoV-2 3CL protease.68 It is highly likely that the isatin scaffold itself, rather than the halogen substitution, drives these degradation reactions. Due to this relatively high reactivity, 0467 must be handled with care if considered for further development. Finally, compound VM025 remained highly stable under both conditions (GSH and PBS t1/2 > 100 h). This observed stability is consistent with established reactivity trends, as VM025 features an iodine substituent, which typically exhibits the lowest SNAr reactivity among the halogens. Consequently, VM025 remains the most promising candidate for further development.

By directly utilizing the primary screening substrate (DiFMUP), this method avoids the need for distinct substrates and guarantees excellent assay comparability. While it provides a valuable tool to bypass optical interference, it cannot account for non-specific effects like protein aggregation or oxidative damage. Consequently, further independent evaluations for the hit compounds remain necessary to confirm specific enzyme inhibition.

Conclusion

In this study, we expressed and purified the catalytic domain of human PTP1B (residues 1–301) to screen four fragment libraries totaling 515 compounds. This collection, which included electrophiles as well as halogen- and chalcogen-enriched fragments, yielded 56 preliminary hits in a fluorescence-based DiFMUP assay. To address potential false positives caused by optical interference, we employed a fluorescence-based two-batch evaluation method. The results indicated that the majority of initial hits exhibited noticeable inner filter effects, with only 11 fragments exhibiting minor optical interference. To further investigate these findings, we developed a simple and rapid HPLC-based method that directly quantifies the substrate (DiFMUP) and the reaction product (DiFMU) via UV detection, bypassing fluorescence artifacts. This procedure confirmed that three compounds (VM025, 0467, and 1244) led to relevant levels of inhibited substrate conversion. By utilizing the primary screening substrate (DiFMUP), this HPLC approach facilitates direct assay comparability and avoids the need for alternative substrates. It bypasses optical interference to verify hits from fluorogenic assays. Although it cannot determine binding modes, address selectivity, or account for non-specific effects like aggregation or oxidative damage, it serves as an effective interface between high-throughput optical screenings and resource-intensive downstream evaluations. Consequently, further independent methods remain necessary to definitively confirm specific enzyme inhibition. Having utilized this approach to successfully isolate VM025, 0467, and 1244 from false positives caused by optical interference, follow-up studies are now required to determine their precise modes of binding and inhibition.
