Functional effects of rosmarinic acid on gut health and epigenetic regulation in antibiotic-free poultry diets
Authors: Mohamed E Abd El-Hack, Elwy A Ashour, Asmaa F Khafaga, Hadeel K Khaleel, Mahmoud Kamal, Ayman E Taha, Ahmed E Alfifi, Mohammed Al-Rasheed, Mohammad MH Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRosmarinic acid (RA), a bioactive polyphenol found in Salvia officinalis and other Lamiaceae herbs, has attracted attention for its functional feed application in animal nutrition. RA supplementation positively influences growth efficiency, liver antioxidant status, and serum biochemical indices in broilers. Its antimicrobial and immunomodulatory effects promote health and productivity. Studies suggest benefits for gut health and meat quality; however, epigenetic regulation of RA in poultry is considered a minor future perspective, being mostly based on mammalian studies. Hypothesized impacts of RA on chronic disease prevention and as a microbiome-engineering agent require further investigation. This review explores the regulatory effects of RA on DNA methylation, non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), and histone modifications, which influence gut microbiome structure, nutrient absorption, and immune function in poultry. It emphasizes RA's potential as a functional food for gastrointestinal health, metabolic regulation, and chronic disease prevention, alongside its use in antibiotic-free poultry feed for microbiome engineering. The review also discusses RA’s effects on lipid metabolism and oxidative stress, highlighting its role in maintaining intestinal barrier health. Nevertheless, certain limitations must be acknowledged, as successful nutritional interventions depend on understanding individual variability, including genetics, metabolism, age, and health status. Tailored methodologies, such as micronutrition and genomic nutrition, can improve diet quality, promote nutrient absorption, and enhance overall animal health. A tailored feeding regimen focuses on selecting nutrients with proven benefits for key health outcomes, such as omega-3 fatty acids, which scientific evidence shows promote brain and heart health while reducing inflammation.Keywords: Rosmarinic acid, Epigenetics, Gut microbiota, Poultry nutrition

Introduction

Chicken is the most widely consumed meat worldwide, accounting for approximately 70% of global meat production. Intensive poultry farming increases birds' susceptibility to environmental stressors such as feed dust, fecal contamination, and high stocking density, which can impair gut health and reduce growth efficiency (Gržinić et al., 2023; Okaiyeto et al., 2024). Antibiotics have historically been used as feed additives to enhance growth and prevent infections (Egila et al., 2023; Abou-Kassem et al., 2024); however, their use has contributed to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant microorganisms, raising public health concerns (Ferri et al., 2017; El-Ratel et al., 2024). Consequently, there is an urgent need for sustainable alternatives, including plant extracts, prebiotics, and organic acids, to maintain poultry health and productivity (Kamal et al., 2023a; Ashour et al., 2025a, b).

Rosmarinic acid (RA), a hydrophilic phenolic compound found abundantly in plants of the Cucurbitaceae, Lamiaceae, and Boraginaceae families, has gained attention due to its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties (del Pilar Sánchez-Camargo and Herrero, 2017; Azhar et al., 2023). RA modulates gut microbiota composition, reduces intestinal inflammation, and inhibits pathogenic bacteria such as E. coli and Salmonella (Rocha et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2018). In poultry, these mechanisms contribute to improved intestinal integrity, nutrient absorption, and overall growth performance.

Rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis) has been traditionally used for its medicinal properties, including antioxidant, antibacterial, and antifungal activities (Oluwatuyi et al., 2004; Guo et al., 2018). Its bioactive constituents, including RA, carnosic acid, and flavonoids, exhibit potent antioxidant effects, scavenging free radicals and reducing lipid peroxidation, thereby protecting cells from oxidative damage and supporting metabolic homeostasis (Govaris et al., 2007; Del Bano et al., 2003). The RA content in rosemary leaves ranges from 0.2% to 4.3%, with the highest antioxidant activity concentrated in this phenolic compound (de Macedo et al., 2020).

In poultry nutrition, rosemary and RA supplementation have been reported to enhance growth, feed conversion ratio, egg and meat quality, antioxidant status, and immune response (Petricevic et al., 2018; Shang et al., 2022). RA demonstrates stability during standard feed processing and storage, although extreme pH, high temperatures, light, and oxygen exposure can reduce its efficacy, emphasizing the importance of optimal feed preparation and packaging (Zorić et al., 2016; Al-Hijazeen et al., 2017; Bodalska et al., 2021). Despite the growing body of evidence, the application of RA in poultry remains limited, and further studies are required to optimize dosage, delivery strategies, and understand its molecular and epigenetic mechanisms.

This review critically evaluates the current knowledge on rosmarinic acid in poultry nutrition, focusing on its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and epigenetic effects. It highlights key advancements and knowledge gaps in the practical use of RA, providing insights for future research and potential implementation in poultry production systems.

Properties of rosmarinic acid (chemistry, bioavailability, and metabolism)

Rosmarinic acid (RA), a phytochemist's favorite for its versatile structure, is an ester of caffeic and 3,4-dihydroxyphenyllactic acids (Rossi et al., 2012). It has the formula C₁₈H₁₆O₈, molar mass 360.32 g mol⁻¹, and melts at 171–175°C (Petersen et al., 2009). Purified RA forms a reddish-orange powder with moderate polarity, soluble in water and organic solvents.

Interest in RA initially stemmed from its wide botanical distribution rather than from any single plant source. Sebranek et al. (2005) and later Tang et al. (2018) emphasized the prominence of RA in rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis), where it contributes substantially to the antioxidant profile of the extract (Fig. 1). Parallel botanical surveys extended these observations, documenting RA across multiple plant families, most notably Lamiaceae (particularly the subfamily Nepetoideae) and Boraginaceae, but also in less commonly cited taxa such as Blechnaceae, Zosteraceae, Cannaceae, and Potamogetonaceae (Azhar et al., 2023). This broad distribution suggests that RA biosynthesis represents a conserved secondary metabolic strategy rather than a species-specific anomaly. Beyond the parent compound, several research groups have focused on the remarkable structural diversity associated with RA-related metabolites. Bulgakov et al. (2012) highlighted that RA often co-occurs with higher-order caffeic acid oligomers, while Fedoreyev et al. (2005) and Jiang et al. (2005) provided some of the earliest characterizations of salvianolic acids, rabdosiin, and sagerinic acid isolated from Salvia species. More recently, Ijaz et al. (2023) revisited this chemical landscape, offering a detailed description of lithospermic acid A and the yunnaneic acid series, and proposing plausible biogenetic relationships among dimers, trimers, and tetramers derived from caffeic acid and RA precursors (Fig. 2). Collectively, these studies underscore that RA should be viewed not as an isolated molecule, but as part of a broader and chemically dynamic phenolic network.

Rosmarinic acid is accompanied by several structurally related derivatives, including salvianolic acids (A and B), lithospermic acid, melitric acid, rabdosiin, eritrichin, and sagerinic acid. These compounds share common phenolic and carboxylic acid functional groups and, in some cases, occur as dimeric structures. The presence of multiple hydroxyl moieties is associated with their pronounced antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, which may contribute to the modulation of gut microbiota and the maintenance of intestinal integrity reported in animal and poultry studies.

From a metabolic standpoint, Petersen (2013) and others clarified that RA biosynthesis reflects the convergence of phenylalanine- and tyrosine-derived pathways. Noor et al. (2022) and Trócsányi et al. (2020) provided mechanistic detail, demonstrating that tyrosine undergoes transamination to form 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvic acid, which is subsequently reduced to 4-hydroxyphenyllactic acid via hydroxyphenylpyruvate reductase using NADH or NADPH as co-substrates. Esterification with caffeic acid then yields RA, integrating aromatic amino acid metabolism with phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Fig. 3). These enzymatic steps have been consistently reported in plant systems, forming the biochemical basis for RA accumulation in RA-rich species.

Considerable effort has also been devoted to understanding the bioavailability of RA, particularly given its susceptibility to degradation during gastrointestinal transit. Madureira et al. (2016) explored lipid-based nanoformulations, demonstrating in rat and in vitro models that RA encapsulated in wax matrices retained antioxidant activity without detectable cytotoxic or genotoxic effects. In a different context, da Silva et al. (2016) examined chitosan-based nanoparticles for ocular delivery, further illustrating how formulation strategies may influence RA stability and tissue exposure. While these studies provide valuable proof-of-concept, they remain largely confined to non-poultry models, and extrapolation to avian metabolism should therefore be approached with caution.

Biological interest in RA has expanded alongside these chemical insights. Hossan et al. (2014) and Rocha et al. (2015) compiled evidence for antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial activities across diverse experimental systems, framing RA as a multifunctional phytochemical of potential nutritional relevance. However, as several authors have noted, including Alagawany et al. (2017), the translation of such properties into defined physiological mechanisms is highly context-dependent and influenced by species, dose, and matrix effects. Epigenetic regulation by rosmarinic acid in human and animal nutrition

Epigenetics refers to heritable, reversible changes in gene expression without altering the DNA sequence, as originally defined by Lin et al. (2009). This framework explains how environmental and nutritional cues regulate genes across the lifespan, with DNA methylation and histone modifications as primary mechanisms (Aggarwal et al., 2015; Lin and Gregory, 2015). These processes are responsible for important events such as X-chromosome inactivation, genomic imprinting, and development (Kaleem et al., 2021), while noncoding microRNAs (miRNAs) provide post-transcriptional control (Ducasse and Brown, 2006). Together, they form a dynamic network responsive to redox state, metabolism, and stressors (Fig. 4), setting the stage for dietary bioactives like rosmarinic acid to potentially influence regulation (Fig. 5, Fig. 6).

Mechanistically, RA exerts antioxidant effects by activating the KEAP1-Nrf2 pathway to produce enzymes like SOD and CAT, neutralizing ROS. Concurrently, it reduces inflammation by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway and associated pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6). This Figure illustrates how the natural polyphenol rosmarinic acid (RA), when incorporated into poultry feed, functions as an epigenetic regulator and functional food additive. In poultry, RA enhances growth performance by improving the feed conversion ratio (FCR) and nutrient absorption, while simultaneously improving meat quality through increased antioxidant activity and reduced lipid oxidation. In laying hens, RA improves egg quality metrics like shell thickness by modulating the intestinal barrier function. The resulting animal products provide safe food for human consumption because they contain beneficial RA metabolites, which transfer potent antioxidants and anti-inflammatory compounds into the human diet. This ultimately contributes to a healthy human by helping to prevent diseases linked to oxidative stress and chronic inflammation.

Studies in poultry and other species demonstrate RA's benefits on growth, antioxidant status, and inflammation without direct epigenetic evidence. For instance, Shang et al. (2022) reported improved broiler performance and hepatic antioxidants at 200 mg/kg RA, while Zhu et al. (2024) linked RA to reduced oxidative stress and inflammation in bullfrog intestines via microbiota shifts and gene downregulation. Poultry research, including Ding et al., 2025a, implicates Nrf2 pathways known to involve epigenetics in other models (Silva-Llanes et al., 2023) and Farag et al. (2023) showed toxicity protection in chicks but lacked chromatin or methylation analyses.

Current evidence positions RA's epigenetic influence as secondary, via redox homeostasis, microbiota alterations producing histone deacetylase-inhibiting short-chain fatty acids (Gok et al., 2015; Fawad et al., 2022), and inferential effects on methylation (Xin et al., 2024). Poultry studies remain limited to phenotypic and transcriptional outcomes. Future research must prioritize direct assays of DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin accessibility, and miRNAs to distinguish true epigenetic regulation from physiological modulation.

Absorption, metabolism, and bioavailability of rosmarinic acid in poultry

Rosmarinic acid (RA) demonstrates limited direct absorption in the upper gastrointestinal tract of poultry. Wang et al. (2025) observed that, due to minimal breakdown in the stomach, duodenum, jejunum, and ileum, RA concentrations remain relatively stable along the upper GI tract. This stability allows the compound to reach the lower gut, particularly the cecum, where microbial activity facilitates its biotransformation.

Microbial metabolism in the cecum generates several biologically active metabolites. RA is initially converted into dihydrosmarinic acid (DiRA), which is subsequently transformed into danshensu (DSS) and dihydrocaffeic acid (DiCA). The process culminates in the formation of 3-hydroxyphenyl propionic acid (3-HPPA), a metabolite with enhanced antioxidant properties compared to the parent compound (Ding et al., 2025b). The involvement of gut bacteria, including Clostridium and Alistipes species, underscores the pivotal role of the microbiota in mediating RA’s bioactivity.

Although intact RA exhibits low systemic absorption, its microbial metabolites appear to support its biological effects. Alagawany et al. (2017) demonstrated that DiRA and DiCA enhance antioxidant enzyme activities, including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), while reducing malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, a marker of lipid peroxidation. Dietary RA at 200 mg/kg has been shown to improve feed efficiency, body weight gain, and muscle quality in broilers (Shang et al., 2022), likely through the combined antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions of its metabolites. The stability of RA in the upper GI tract ensures that its delivery to the cecum is precise, maximizing microbial metabolism and subsequent biological activity (Ding et al., 2025b).

Emerging strategies in other species suggest potential approaches to enhance RA bioavailability. Blažević et al. (2021) reported that short-chain alkyl modifications increased RA absorption in mice by approximately sevenfold. While these findings have yet to be validated in poultry, they provide a theoretical basis for improving RA performance in feed formulations. Targeted delivery systems and microencapsulation techniques represent promising avenues for future research to optimize RA bioavailability and its functional effects.

Rosmarinic acid, a polyphenolic compound renowned for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, has been implicated in epigenetic regulation across mammalian and other animal models. Studies demonstrate that RA modulates DNA methylation by inhibiting DNA methyltransferases and ten-eleven translocation enzymes, particularly through reduction of oxidative stress that otherwise promotes hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes (such as in colorectal cancer models; Han et al., 2018). Similarly, RA influences histone modifications, such as enhancing histone acetylation via upregulation of histone acetyltransferases and suppression of histone deacetylases, which correlates with anti-inflammatory effects in neuronal and hepatic cells (Lu et al., 2022). RA also alters non-coding RNA expressions, including microRNAs like miR-21 and miR-155, by disrupting NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways linked to gene regulation (Han et al., 2015). These mechanisms, primarily evidenced in rodents and cell lines, underscore RA's potential to reshape the epigenome indirectly via lowered ROS and cytokine modulation. Although direct evidence in poultry remains limited, this broader mammalian context supports the biological plausibility of RA-mediated epigenetic effects in avian species, warranting targeted investigations.

Modulation of gut microbiota and intestinal integrity

Rosemary extract has been evaluated for its effects on immune modulation in poultry. Liu et al. (2022) reported that supplementation reduced allergic responses by lowering Th2 cytokines and increasing Th1 and Treg cytokines. These changes coincided with improved antioxidant status, as evidenced by decreased histamine levels and reduced mast cell protease-1 activity, highlighting the immunomodulatory potential of plant-derived compounds. Rosmarinic acid (RA) also influences gut microbial composition in a dose-dependent manner. Huang et al. (2025) observed an increase in Bacteroidetes and a decrease in Firmicutes in geese, which may support better nutrient absorption and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production. Liu et al. (2022) confirmed similar trends in broilers, linking microbial shifts with improved feed efficiency. Importantly, these studies measured physiological and microbial outcomes without implying direct epigenetic regulation.

RA supplementation positively affects intestinal morphology. Yang et al. (2024) demonstrated that jejunal crypt depth was reduced, villus height-to-crypt depth ratios increased, and the duodenal index improved. Concurrently, crude protein digestibility and nutrient absorption efficiency were enhanced, while meat quality remained stable, as indicated by consistent cooking weight and shearing strength. These results underscore RA's practical relevance for intestinal integrity and poultry production. Recent studies provide robust and well-supported safety data for RA supplementation in poultry. For instance, Liu et al. (2022) evaluated doses up to 500 mg/kg feed in broiler chickens over 42 days, reporting no adverse effects on growth performance, organ weights, or hematological parameters. Similarly, Huang et al. (2025) confirmed tolerability at 200–400 mg/kg in laying hens across a 12-week trial, with no impacts on egg quality, mortality, or clinical health markers. While long-term studies beyond 12 weeks remain limited, these findings affirm RA's safety profile across typical commercial dosages. Collectively, these findings support RA as a promising alternative to antimicrobial growth promoters. Future research should explore synergistic effects with prebiotics and investigate long-term impacts on gut microbiome stability and resilience under commercial production conditions. Mechanisms of RA in anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects

Adomako-Bonsu et al. (2017) highlighted that rosemary extract exhibits a combination of antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and antiviral properties, attracting considerable scientific interest. In broilers, Shang et al. (2022) reported that RA supplementation significantly enhanced performance metrics, including final body weight (BW), feed intake (FI), and body weight gain (BWG).

Dietary inclusion of RA at 30–500 mg/kg consistently improves growth performance, demonstrating a dose-dependent effect (Ghazalah and Ali, 2008; Li et al., 2017). Serum biochemical markers serve as important indicators for monitoring physiological responses to feed additives (Kamal et al., 2023b). Broilers receiving RA showed higher total protein and globulin levels, suggesting enhanced protein synthesis and improved immune status (Shang et al., 2022). Importantly, liver function remained unaffected, consistent with RA’s non-toxic profile, which supports stable blood glucose levels, normal carbohydrate metabolism, and overall health (Jayanthy et al., 2014).

The liver plays a pivotal role in growth efficiency, primarily through its antioxidant defense system (Lucarini et al., 2010). Enzymes such as total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and catalase (CAT) are essential for neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS). T-SOD scavenges superoxide radicals (O₂⁻), GSH-Px converts hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and lipid peroxides into less harmful molecules, and CAT further detoxifies H₂O₂ (David et al., 2008). Thus, the activity of these enzymes serves as a sensitive indicator of animal health and oxidative stress (Lei et al., 2007).

Shang et al. (2022) demonstrated that dietary supplementation with 200 mg/kg RA enhanced T-SOD and CAT activities in broiler livers. This effect may be explained by RA restoring GPx and SOD activity, reducing lipid peroxidation, and limiting nitric oxide synthesis, collectively lowering oxidative stress (Sadeghi et al., 2020). Malondialdehyde (MDA), a reactive oxygen species generated from lipid peroxidation, serves as an indirect marker of oxidative damage. RA supplementation effectively reduces hepatic MDA levels (Ghozlan et al., 2017), likely due to its phenolic (-OH) groups donating hydrogen atoms to neutralize free radicals, forming stable, non-reactive molecules and terminating free radical chain reactions (Zhang et al., 2015).

In summary, RA confers multiple benefits in broiler nutrition: it promotes growth, enhances immune function, and reduces oxidative stress via activation of antioxidant enzymes. Its safety profile, efficacy in improving biochemical markers, and protective effects on liver health underscore its potential as a functional feed ingredient capable of improving poultry welfare and production outcomes.

Rosmarinic acid in poultry nutrition

Supplementing broiler feed with 500 mg/kg of rosemary has been shown to enhance growth performance (Ghazaleh and Ali, 2008). Norouzi et al. (2016) further demonstrated that varying dietary doses of rosemary powder can influence growth, carcass characteristics, and gut microbial composition in broilers.

In heat-stressed laying hens, rosemary essential oil increased glutathione peroxidase activity and improved production efficiency (Torki et al., 2018). Similarly, Alagawany and Abd El-Hack (2015) reported that dietary inclusion of up to 6 g/kg of rosemary positively affects laying hen performance, antioxidant capacity, and immune responses. Hassanpour et al. (2025) highlighted rosemary’s potential as a natural feed additive, noting its beneficial effects on egg quality and overall productivity. Specifically, RA inclusion at 100–400 mg/kg improved broiler growth efficiency, with an optimal level around 200 mg/kg (Shang et al., 2022). This dose was associated with enhanced liver antioxidant enzyme activity, reduced oxidative stress, and improved meat quality. In ducks, high doses of rosemary extract (500–750 mg/kg) elevated antioxidant status and immune function without negative effects (Yao et al., 2023). Manafi et al. (2014) confirmed that even RA-rich extracts up to 5000 mg/kg did not adversely affect feed intake or feed conversion ratio, though such high levels are rarely applied in practice. Regulatory assessments suggest an acceptable level of about 64 mg/kg for related compounds; however, bird studies indicate that higher doses are often necessary to achieve meaningful antioxidant benefits (EFSA, 2025). In aquaculture, dietary supplementation with 0.5% rosemary improved tilapia immunity and countered aflatoxin B1 toxicity in feed (Naiel et al., 2019).

The stability of RA is influenced by pH, temperature, light, and oxygen exposure. It remains moderately stable but can degrade rapidly under acidic conditions (pH ∼2.5), with over 50% loss within 30 minutes (Zorić et al., 2016; Gonçalves et al., 2019). High temperatures, oxygen, and UV exposure during feed processing can also reduce RA content due to its sensitivity as a phenolic acid (Razboršek, 2011). Nevertheless, rosemary extracts containing RA maintain stability under proper storage, with minimal loss of active compounds such as carnosic acid (0–0.4%) (Al-Hijazeen et al., 2017). Long-term studies suggest that RA retains its functional properties for up to two years under controlled conditions (Bodalska et al., 2021).

To maximize RA efficacy in poultry feed, it is crucial to protect it from excessive acidity, heat, light, and oxygen during feed preparation and storage. Appropriate packaging and controlled storage conditions ensure the stability and bioactivity of RA. Critical evaluation, challenges, and knowledge gaps in using rosmarinic acids

Rosmarinic acid (RA, C18H16O8; molar mass ∼360.3 g/mol) is a bioactive phenolic compound derived from caffeic acid and 3,4-dihydroxyphenyllactic acid (Guan et al., 2022). It possesses both hydrophilic and lipophilic properties, appearing as a reddish-orange crystalline powder with a melting point of 171–175°C and limited solubility in water (Noor et al., 2022). Naturally occurring in rosemary and other Lamiaceae and Boraginaceae species, RA demonstrates stability in the upper gastrointestinal tract of poultry but low direct absorption, primarily reaching the lower intestine, where microbial metabolism by bacteria such as Clostridium and Alistipes generates bioactive metabolites, including dihydrosmarinic acid and dihydrocaffeic acid, which contribute to systemic antioxidant effects (Ding et al., 2025a; Abduh et al., 2023). Strategies to enhance RA bioavailability, such as nanoparticle delivery or chemical modifications, remain largely unexplored in poultry (Noor et al., 2022).

RA is chemically sensitive, prone to degradation under acidic conditions, heat, light, and oxygen exposure during feed processing and storage (Netala et al., 2024). Ensuring effective packaging and controlled storage is essential to preserve its activity (Noor et al., 2022). Its physiological benefits in poultry include antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, antibacterial, and growth-promoting effects, with an optimal dose of ∼200 mg/kg feed in broilers, though further safety verification is needed. Microbial metabolism plays a central role in converting RA into its bioactive forms, highlighting the importance of gut microbiota in mediating its effects (Guan et al., 2022; Ding et al., 2025a). Future research should focus on molecular pathways, optimized delivery systems, and avian pharmacokinetics to maximize RA's benefits.

Incorporating RA or feed by-products offers economic advantages by reducing feed costs and supporting sustainable agriculture (Van Zanten et al., 2018; Kamal et al., 2025). However, safe and effective use requires careful evaluation and processing, as poorly processed ingredients may contain anti-nutritional factors (Henchion et al., 2017). Consumer awareness, rigorous quality control, and accurate labeling are essential to ensure safety and efficacy of functional foods (Vlaicu et al., 2023). Excessive intake of bioactive compounds can cause adverse effects, including poisoning, nutritional imbalance, or interactions with other dietary or pharmaceutical components (Komaroff, 2009; Losurdo et al., 2018). Polyphenols such as catechins may exhibit cytotoxicity at high concentrations (Xia et al., 2010), while phytoestrogens like genistein can promote tumor growth under specific conditions (Marcelo et al., 2019). Limited bioavailability and potential interactions with feed components, digestive conditions, and metabolic factors further constrain RA's practical use as a functional feed additive (Azhar et al., 2023). Thus, an integrated approach is required, combining careful processing, balanced feed formulation, consumer education, and quality assurance to ensure that RA supplementation is safe, effective, and economically viable.

Limitations and future perspectives

The practical application of RA in poultry feed is constrained by several factors. Its content and composition vary with plant species, geographic origin, harvest time, and extraction methods, which complicates dosage standardization and reproducibility of biological effects (Zorić et al., 2016; Alagawany et al., 2017). RA is sensitive to heat, light, and oxidation, challenging its stability during feed processing, especially during pelleting or extrusion (Abdul Aziz et al., 2022). Protective approaches such as microencapsulation can improve stability but increase production costs.

Regulatory approval is another hurdle, as different regions have varying frameworks for feed additives. Approval requires comprehensive toxicity studies, residue analyses, and safety data for both animals and humans, which may delay market access (Hassanpour et al., 2025). Long-term safety data and studies on cumulative effects in poultry remain limited. The impact of RA supplementation on reproduction, immunity, and product quality has not been fully evaluated (Alagawany et al., 2017; Hassanpour et al., 2025).

Future research should focus on standardization of RA content, stabilization techniques, optimized delivery methods, and comprehensive safety assessments to enable consistent, effective, and safe use in commercial poultry production.

Conclusions

The rosmarinic acid offers robust antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory benefits for poultry nutrition, enhancing growth, gut health, and meat quality despite challenges in bioavailability, stability, and standardization. Future research should target advanced delivery systems, molecular/epigenetic mechanisms, and long-term safety trials to unlock RA's full potential as a sustainable antibiotic alternative, bolstering poultry productivity and industry resilience.
