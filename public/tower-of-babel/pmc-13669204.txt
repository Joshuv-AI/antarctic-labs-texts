Myeloid reprogramming generates myeloid-derived activating cells to potentiate radiotherapy and suppress metastasis
Authors: Fei Ji, Lei Zheng, Dapeng Chen, Peng Xia, Zhongyu Zou, Wei Liu, Jason Bugno, Emile Z. Naccasha, Andras Piffko, Chuangyu Wen, Kangdi Li, Liping Xu, Sean Z. Luo, Xiaona Huang, Jiaai Wang, Chengwei Ju, Bei Liu, Yiyi Ji, Han Li, Steven J. Chmura, Sean P. Pitroda, Hua Laura Liang, Chuan He, Ralph R Weichselbaum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary paragraphRadiation induces immunosuppressive myeloid cells that drive therapeutic resistance and metastasis. We identify TET2 as a radiation-induced regulator of myeloid-derived suppressor cells whose expression correlates with poor outcome in advanced lung cancer patients receiving radio-immunotherapy. Following radiotherapy, myeloid-specific Tet2 deletion suppresses tumor progression and redirects monocyte differentiation toward antigen-presenting myeloid-derived activating cells (MDACs), which augment antitumor T cell responses; spatiotemporal mapping shows this fate program initiates rapidly in the bone marrow. Mechanistically, a radiation-responsive p53–TET2 axis decreases m5C on chromatin-associated RNAs (caRNAs), enforcing immunosuppressive chromatin compaction and silencing interferon signaling, whereas Tet2 loss restores chromatin accessibility and enhances CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity. Pharmacological TET2 inhibition recapitulates this phenotype and augments radiotherapy and PD-L1 blockade to control primary and metastatic tumors. These findings define MDACs as an immunogenic myeloid subset and establish myeloid reprogramming as a strategy to improve radiotherapy–immunotherapy outcomes.

Introduction

Radiotherapy (RT), a mainstay of cancer treatment given to 50–60% of patients, elicits complex immunomodulatory effects1. While mediating tumor cell death via DNA damage, RT simultaneously expands immunosuppressive populations including myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs)2–5. These cells employ multiple mechanisms to suppress innate and adaptive immunity6–9 with radiation enhancing their recruitment and immunosuppressive activity within tumors10–13. Clinically RT-induced MDSC expansion correlates with reduced survival and increased metastatic progression14–17, highlighting the need to overcome this axis.

The role of TET2 in cancer immunity is complex and context-dependent. Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) caused by TET2 inactivation is associated with worse outcomes in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)18, yet paradoxically enhances immunotherapy response through increased IFN signaling in macrophages19,20. Beyond its DNA demethylase activity driving myeloid malignancies21–26and promoting immunosuppression via Arg127. TET2 also functions as an RNA demethylase, oxidizing m5C on chromatin-associated RNAs (caRNAs) to recruit MBD6–BAP1 complexes for chromatin remodeling28–36, marking a transition from DNA-centric to RNA-inclusive paradigms. However, how this dual-substrate specificity regulates myeloid plasticity in irradiated tumors remains unexplored.

Here, we uncover a spatiotemporal epigenetic program in which radiation activates a TET2–m5C switch. Radiation causes TET2-dependent systemic programming of myeloid cells, with the bone marrow as the earliest site of lineage bifurcation. Radiation-induced TET2 oxidizes m5C on caRNAs, establishing a repressive chromatin state that silences interferon responses and enforces immunosuppressive MDSC differentiation, whereas Tet2 ablation recruits MBD6–BAP1 complexes to erase these marks, restore chromatin accessibility, and drive differentiation toward immunostimulatory myeloid-derived activating cells (MDACs). These findings identify caRNA m5C as a direct regulator of chromatin topology and myeloid fate, establishing myeloid reprogramming as a strategy to overcome treatment resistance and metastasis.

Results

TET2 upregulation in myeloid cells impairs radiotherapy efficacy in tumors

Given previous reports of immune modulation by TET2, we examined its potential role in radiotherapy response using matched pre- and post-treatment peripheral blood mononuclear cells(PBMC) samples from patients with stage IV NSCLC enrolled in a phase I/II trial combining nivolumab and ipilimumab with sequential or concurrent multisite stereotactic body radiation therapy(SBRT)(NCT03223155; n = 33)37. M-MDSCs (Lineage−CD33+CD14+) exhibited the highest baseline TET2 expression relative to other immune cells (Figures 1A–1B). Radiation significantly increased TET2 in M-MDSCs (Figures 1C–1D), with a more pronounced increase in non-responders than responders (p = 0.0072 and p = 0.0532, respectively; Figure 1D). Similar trends were observed when patients were stratified by sequential or concurrent treatment, with significant increases in non-responders but not responders (Figures S1A–S1B). These findings suggest that radiotherapy-associated TET2 induction is not restricted to non-responders but more pronounced in this group, consistent with a general myeloid stress response whose magnitude may be associated with impaired therapeutic response.

We next evaluated whether myeloid Tet2 deletion affects radiation response. LyzCreTet2fl/fl (Tet2 cKO) mice and control Tet2fl/fl littermates (WT) bearing MC38 tumors received a single dose of 20 Gy irradiation (IR)10. With equal tumor cell inocula, Tet2 cKO mice exhibited slower post-IR tumor growth than WT controls (Figure S1C). After adjusting inoculation doses (Tet2 cKO: 2×106 vs WT: 1×106 cells) to achieve comparable tumor volumes at irradiation, Tet2 cKO mice showed superior radiation response and prolonged survival versus WT+IR controls, with Tet2 cKO+IR exhibiting significant additional tumor suppression over Tet2 deletion alone (Figures 1E and S1D). These findings were validated in B16-F1-OVA-ZsGreen melanoma and Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) models (Figures 1F–1G and S1E).

In the spontaneous LLC metastasis model, combined Tet2 deletion and radiotherapy further reduced pulmonary metastatic burden (nodule number and area) compared to either treatment alone (Figures 1H–1I and S1F). To exclude confounding by primary tumor burden, tumors were resected 7 days post-irradiation when no significant size differences were observed; Tet2 deficiency remained associated with reduced lung metastasis, an effect further enhanced in the Tet2 cKO+IR group (Figures S1G–S1H), demonstrating that myeloid Tet2 deletion enhances both local and distant anti-tumor effects of irradiation.

Flow cytometric profiling showed that irradiation significantly expanded CD11b+Ly6C+ monocytic MDSCs (mMDSCs) in WT tumors (Figures 1J and S1J–S1L), an effect abrogated by myeloid-specific Tet2 deletion, with similar findings in lung metastases (Figure S1I). CD11b+Ly6C+ cells sorted from Tet2 cKO tumors and co-cultured with CD8+ T cells supported higher T cell proliferation than WT controls, indicating attenuated immunosuppressive capacity (Figure 1K). These findings established TET2 as a regulator of mMDSC recruitment and function in the irradiated tumor microenvironment.

Cellular and molecular dissection of TET2-dependent immunosuppressive myeloid remodeling

To characterize myeloid dynamics at single-cell resolution, we performed scRNA-seq of CD45+ immune cells from MC38 tumors of WT or Tet2 cKO mice with or without irradiation, identifying 21 clusters across seven major immune lineages (Figures S2A–S2B). Myeloid-focused analysis (Figure 2A) revealed that irradiation increased monocyte abundance in WT but not Tet2 cKO tumors (Figure 2B), with sub-clustering identifying 21 unique myeloid populations (Figures 2C–2E). In WT control tumors, cluster 7 (Macro_Itga9), enriched for migration/differentiation genes (Itga9, Itga6, Cebpa) and the tolerance marker Ctla-2b (Figures S2C–S2D), predominated. Following irradiation, WT tumors instead showed expansion of two immunosuppressive populations: cluster 4_0 (Macro_Slfn4), marked by suppressive genes (Slfn4, Ly6c2, Cd274) and reduced antigen-presentation genes (H2-Aa, H2-Ab1) (Figure S2E); and cluster 3 (Macro_Hp), characterized by Hp, Gsr, Foxp1, and Cd244a (Figures S2J–S2K).

By contrast, Tet2 cKO tumors exhibited a distinct myeloid landscape with expansion of immune-activating populations, including cluster 0_0 (Macro_Itgax), cluster 6 (Macro_Pltp), cluster 5_0 (Mono_Chst11), cluster 5_1 (Mono_Inhba), and cluster 1_0 (Macro_Cxcl3) (Figures S2F–S2I,S2L). Cluster 0_0 was enriched for antigen-presentation and phagocytic genes (H2-Aa, H2-Ab1, H2-Eb1, H2-DMb1, and Itgax) (Figure S2F), while monocyte subsets displayed robust inflammatory signatures: cluster 5_0 enriched for Cxcl10, Ifi203, Il1b, and Cxcr4 (Figure S2G), cluster 5_1 for Inhba, Il1b, Cxcl3, Clec4d, and Clec4e (Figure S2H), and cluster 1_0 for Ccl3, Inhba, Nos2, and Clec4d (Figure S2L), further reinforcing the inflammatory program.

To define how Tet2 regulates myeloid differentiation under irradiation, we performed trajectory reconstruction using diffusion mapping, pseudotime ordering, and Slingshot (Figures S3A–S3B and 2F–2G). Pseudotime analysis revealed a radiation-induced differentiation arrest in WT monocytes, evidenced by predominant retention at early pseudotime following irradiation (Figures 2H and S3C), whereas Tet2-deficient monocytes displayed robust differentiation progression resembling unirradiated controls regardless of irradiation (Figure 2I), indicating that Tet2 loss overrides the radiation-imposed developmental blockade.

Cluster 3 occupied the earliest pseudotime position across all trajectory inference methods and served as the common transitional root state (Figure S3D), from which trajectory analysis revealed a clear bifurcation into two distinct fate programs (Figure 2J). Trajectory 1 progressed through clusters 3 → 5_0 → 5_1 → 1_0, culminating in an inflammatory terminal state marked by elevated Inhba, Clec4d, Il7r, and Tnfrsf1b (Figures 2J and S2L), while Trajectory 2 progressed from 3 → 0_1, corresponding to the radiation-associated suppressive program in WT tumors (Figures 2J and S2L). The distribution of these trajectories was genotype dependent: Tet2 cKO+IR samples were enriched along the proinflammatory branch (Trajectory 1), whereas WT+IR samples occupied the suppressive branch (Trajectory 2) (Figure 2K), indicating that Tet2 deficiency redirects monocyte differentiation toward proinflammatory fates under radiation. This was also confirmed by latent time and RNA velocity analysis (Figures S3E–S3I).

Comparative transcriptomic analysis revealed distinct gene expression programs between the two trajectories: Trajectory 1 showed increasing proinflammatory mediators (e.g., Inhba, Cxcl2, Il7r), whereas Trajectory 2 showed suppression of these genes and upregulation of immunosuppressive factors (e.g., Arg1, Il1r2) (Figures 2L and S3J), with pathway analysis confirming enrichment of IRF3-mediated type I interferon signaling in Trajectory 1 (Figure 2M). These data suggest a cytokine-dependent fate bifurcation model: elevated IFN-I signaling in Tet2 cKO+IR conditions maintains monocytes in a proinflammatory state, whereas low IFN-I signaling in WT+IR tumors promotes immunosuppressive differentiation (Figure 2N).

Radiation-activated TET2 establishes a caRNA m5C-gated chromatin switch to enforce myeloid immunosuppression

To investigate how TET2 regulates monocyte differentiation after radiation, we examined TET2 dynamics in irradiated tumors and myeloid cells. ScRNA-seq of CD45+ cells from MC38 tumors showed Tet2 upregulation in irradiated monocytes, particularly clusters 3 and 5_1 (Figure 3A), confirmed in tumor-derived MDSCs (CD11b+Ly6C+), beginning day 1 after irradiation (Figures 3B–3C). Radiation also directly induced TET2 in bone marrow(BM)-derived monocytes dose- and time-dependently, with 8 Gy producing the strongest induction (Figures 3D–3F). RNA-seq pathway analysis identified “transcriptional regulation by p53” as a top enriched pathway (Figure 3G): p53 increased in parallel with TET2 in both BM-derived and tumor-infiltrating MDSCs (Figures 3I–3J), and Trp53 knockdown abrogated radiation-induced TET2 expression (Figures 3K–3L). ChIP-qPCR confirmed p53 binding to the Tet2 promoter ~1.5–2.0 kb upstream of the transcription start site (Figures 3H and 3M). These results establish that radiation activates the p53-TET2 axis to modulate m-MDSC function.

Since previous research has reported that TET2 is a demethylase of DNA and RNA26–32, we investigated two potential mediators of TET2 function: (1) CXXC5, which directs TET2 to enhancer DNA 5mC oxidation, and (2) the m5C reader MBD6, which binds RNA m5C29. Using antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) to knock down Mbd6 and Cxxc5 in Tet2-deficient monocytes (Figures S4A–S4C), we found that the enhanced T cell proliferation and activation seen in Tet2 KO monocytes versus wild-type controls was completely abrogated by Mbd6 knockdown but not Cxxc5 knockdown (Figures S4D–S4F), indicating that TET2-mediated monocyte functional alteration relies primarily on MBD6-dependent RNA m5C recognition. Tet2-deficient monocytes also exhibited genome-wide increases in chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activity, as shown by EU incorporation, DNase-TUNEL, and ATAC-seq (Figures S4G–S4K), establishing TET2 as an epigenetic regulator that maintains chromatin compaction to restrain transcription—a phenotype consistent with TET2’s RNA m5C oxidation role rather than its canonical DNA demethylation function38,39. ASO-mediated knockdown of Mbd6 (RNA m5C reader), but not Cxxc5 (binding protein to DNA), reversed Tet2 deficiency-associated chromatin decompaction (Figures S4L–4M), revealing MBD6 and RNA m5C as critical downstream mediators of TET2 in an RNA-dependent regulatory axis governing chromatin dynamics and transcriptional output.

The RNA-dependent chromatin regulation by TET2 is known to be mediated through chromatin-associated RNAs (caRNAs)40–44. We performed caRNA-seq integrated with chromatin accessibility data in irradiated monocytes to test whether TET2 alters caRNA landscapes and observed a concomitant reduction in caRNA and caRNA m5C levels after radiation (Figures S5A–S5B and S5D–S5E). Genetic deletion of Tet2 reversed both the IR-mediated suppression of caRNA and loss of m5C modification (Figures S5C–S5E), indicating caRNA m5C oxidation by TET2 in WT cells after radiation.

It has been reported that radiation induces initial chromatin relaxation (for DNA repair) followed by compaction45–48. Image-based assays (EU, TUNEL) showed reduced radiation-induced chromatin compaction in Tet2-deficient cells (Figures 4A–4B), confirmed by ATAC-seq (Figures 4C–4D); these changes correlated with caRNA expression and transcriptional activity (Figures 4E–4F), and regions losing accessibility after IR were enriched for IFN-I response genes (Figures 4G–4I). IR downregulated IRF3 expression, whereas Tet2 deficiency restored IRF3 levels and reversed IR-mediated IFN-β suppression (Figures 4J–4M), and IFNAR1 blockade abrogated the enhanced antitumor response to IR in Tet2 cKO mice (Figure 4N), establishing IRF3-mediated IFN-I signaling as the critical link between Tet2 ablation and improved radiation response. Together, these data indicate that radiation induces p53-mediated TET2 upregulation, leading to m5C oxidation on caRNAs, chromatin compaction, and repression of interferon responses that enforce immunosuppressive M-MDSC differentiation (Figure 4O).

The caRNA-MBD6-H2AK119ub Axis Activates IRF3-mediated Interferon Signaling by Remodeling Chromatin

We next investigated whether caRNAs mediate the effects of TET2 on IFN signaling. Consistent with increased global chromatin accessibility in irradiated Tet2 KO monocytes (Figure 4D), ATAC-seq revealed increased accessibility at the Irf3 promoter (Figure 5A), and caRNA-seq identified elevated expression of the retrotransposon-derived caRNA Rmer21b at the Irf3 promoter under Tet2 cKO+IR conditions (Figure 5B), with increased m5C methylation on Rmer21b (m5C-MeRIP-qPCR; Figure 5C). Rmer21b-targeting ASOs reduced Irf3 chromatin accessibility and decreased IRF3 and IFN-β expression in irradiated Tet2 KO monocytes (Figures 5D–5E and S5F), indicating that caRNA Rmer21b promotes IRF3-mediated IFN-β production through local chromatin modulation.

To validate the functional role of Rmer21b in Tet2 KO-mediated antitumor efficacy, we adoptively transferred WT, Tet2 KO, or Rmer21b-silenced Tet2 KO BM-myeloid cells into LyzCreCSF1RCre-DTR mice following DT-mediated depletion of endogenous myeloid cells. Transfer of Tet2 KO myeloid cells enhanced radiation response, an effect abrogated by Rmer21b silencing (Figure 5F), confirming its functional importance in vivo. Given that MBD6 has been identified as a reader of caRNA m5C29, we further confirmed MBD6 binding to m5C-marked Rmer21b by MBD6-CLIP-PCR (Figures S5G and 5G). Mbd6 knockdown in irradiated Tet2 KO BM-monocytes phenocopied Rmer21b silencing, reducing Irf3 chromatin accessibility and IRF3/IFN-β expression (Figures 5D, 5H, S5H), and adoptive transfer of Mbd6-silenced Tet2 KO monocytes abolished the enhanced radiation response conferred by Tet2 KO cells (Figure 5I), identifying Rmer21b and MBD6 as critical effectors of this caRNA m5C-dependent epitranscriptomic–epigenetic axis.

Our analysis of histone profiles identified H2AK119ub, a canonical repressive histone marker that regulates chromatin accessibility, as the most dynamically altered modification in Tet2-deficient monocytes following irradiation (Figures 5J–5L): it accumulated in wild-type cells after radiation but was significantly reduced in irradiated Tet2 KO monocytes. In irradiated monocytes, H2AK119ub also correlated inversely with chromatin accessibility (Figure 5A) and global transcriptional activity (Figure 5M), suggesting it plays a pivotal role in the TET2-dependent epigenetic response to radiation by coordinately regulating chromatin architecture and gene expression.

H2AK119ub is reported to be removed by polycomb repressive deubiquitylase (PR-DUB) complexes49,50, with BAP1 being the catalytic component and MBD6 as one of the partner proteins29,51,52. We hypothesized that MBD6 is required for H2AK119ub removal. H2AK119ub cut&tag in Tet2 KO or Mbd6 knockdown Tet2 KO irradiated monocytes showed an increased H2AK119ub signal in Mbd6 KD Tet2 KO monocytes compared to Tet2 KO (Figure 5N), correlating with increased protein levels (Figure S5I); depletion of the caRNA Rmer21b similarly elevated H2AK119ub (Figures 5N and S5J). These results support a model in which radiation triggers p53-mediated TET2 upregulation, decreasing caRNA m5C modification to drive chromatin compaction and suppress type I interferon responses, whereas Tet2 deficiency activates the IRF3/IFN-I pathway through a caRNA-MBD6-H2AK119ub axis that orchestrates radiation-induced chromatin remodeling (Figure 5O), ultimately reprogramming tumor-associated myeloid cells toward a proinflammatory phenotype in the irradiated tumor microenvironment.

TET2-dependent divergent myeloid fates emerge from the bone marrow after irradiation

Having established the molecular mechanism by which TET2 regulates caRNA m5C oxidation, H2AK119ub deposition, chromatin accessibility, and downstream interferon signaling, we next sought to determine the spatio-temporal mechanism of myeloid fate programming induced by radiation in the context of Tet2 deficiency. To address this, we performed a compartment-specific time-course analysis across bone marrow, peripheral blood, and tumor tissues at 0, 1, 2, and 3 days post-irradiation of primary tumors in both WT and Tet2 cKO mice.

Following irradiation, WT and Tet2 cKO mice exhibited comparable early tumor damage and systemic DAMP release, reflected by similar kinetics of γH2AX accumulation and plasma HMGB1 elevation (Figure S6A), indicating that the upstream radiation-induced stress signal remains intact in both genotypes. Irradiated tumor-conditioned medium similarly induced an early p53 response in bone marrow monocytes from both hosts (Figure S6B), confirming that the initial DNA damage/repair response is TET2-independent.

The most rapid and pronounced divergence emerged in the bone marrow compartment. In WT mice, bone marrow monocytes displayed the fastest induction of p-p53 and TET2, detectable by day 1 post-irradiation and progressively increasing through day 3 (Figure S6C–S6D), accompanied by accumulation of the repressive H2AK119ub and progressive suppression of Irf3 and Ifnb1 (Figures S6E–S6G), consistent with a chromatin program favoring suppressive myeloid differentiation. In contrast, Tet2-deficient bone marrow monocytes preserved upstream p53 activation but lacked TET2 induction, showed impaired H2AK119ub accumulation, and maintained persistent IRF3–IFN-I signaling throughout (Figures S6D–S6G). These findings indicate that myeloid fate specification is determined in the bone marrow rapidly following radiation.

Peripheral blood monocytes exhibited a similar but delayed divergence, suggesting progressive propagation of this program during systemic trafficking, while tumor-infiltrating monocytes displayed the slowest, most fully executed differences, consistent with terminal differentiation into immunosuppressive MDSCs in WT hosts or pro-inflammatory monocytes in Tet2-deficient hosts.

Together, these data support a model in which local radiation triggers systemic stress signaling and p53 activation, with the bone marrow as the earliest bifurcation site for lineage commitment: under WT conditions, a p53-TET2-dependent trajectory drives suppressive MDSC differentiation, whereas under Tet2 deficiency the same stress is redirected into a pro-inflammatory monocyte lineage, with full functional execution occurring only after these primed cells migrate into the tumor microenvironment (Figure S6H).

Pro-inflammatory myeloid-derived activating cells (MDACs) prime CD8+ T cells through antigen presentation

We showed above that the IRF3/IFN-I pathway was activated in irradiated Tet2 KO monocytes, yielding enhanced proinflammatory gene signatures: a distinct immunostimulatory phenotype marked by upregulation of genes for antigen recognition (Clec4d, Clec4e) and antigen presentation (CD74), inflammatory cytokines (Il1a, Il1b, Tnf), and interferon-stimulated genes (Figures S2G–S2H and S7A). These inflammatory monocytes highly express the interferon-regulated surface marker Itgal53 (Figures S2G–S2H), and T cell chemoattractants Cxcl9 and Cxcl10 (Figures 6A–6B and S7B–S7D). ITGAL is a core component of the lymphocyte function-associated antigen-1 (LFA-1), mediating stable monocytes-T cells adhesion fundamental to antigen presentation and T cell activation. This adhesive interface supports immune synapse formation and downstream T cell responses54–56. Flow analysis confirmed that ITGAL+ monocyte frequency was significantly increased in irradiated Tet2 cKO tumors (Figures 6C–6D). Together, these immunostimulatory features and ITGAL expression define a proinflammatory myeloid-derived activating cell (MDAC, CD11b+Ly6C+ITGAL+) driven by IFN-I signaling - a fundamental reprogramming of myeloid function under combined Tet2 deficiency and radiation.

We next investigated T cell activation by immunoprofiling tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. Tet2 cKO tumors exhibited significantly higher CD8+ T cell proportion and activation than WT tumors (Figures 6E–6F and S7E–S7F), defining distinct microenvironmental states-immunologically active (Tet2 cKO+IR) versus immunosuppressive (WT+IR). CD8+ T cells were required for enhanced tumor control in Tet2 cKO hosts, as their depletion abolished the augmented response (Figure 6G), establishing CD8+ T cell reinvigoration as a key pathway underlying the synergistic anti-tumor effects of Tet2 ablation and radiotherapy.

To identify the cellular drivers of CD8+ T cell activation in irradiated tumors, we integrated spatial mapping with functional validation. Immunofluorescence showed MDACs positioned significantly closer to CD8+ T cells than dendritic cells (DCs), suggesting preferential MDAC-CD8+ T cell interactions over conventional DCs in irradiated Tet2 cKO tumors (Figures 6H–6I). Among inflammatory Ly6C+ monocytes in the Tet2 cKO+IR group, the ITGAL+ subset (MDACs) accounted for predominant antigen-presenting activity (Figure 6J), and isolated Ly6C+ITGAL+ cells robustly promoted naïve CD8+ T cell proliferation and IFN-γ production (Figures 6K–6M), supporting a direct role for MDACs in local antitumor T cell activation. Transfer of CD11b+Ly6C+ITGAL+ cells significantly suppressed tumor growth versus control and CD11b+Ly6C+ITGAL⁻ populations, providing direct in vivo evidence of antitumor activity (Figure 6N).

To further validate the role of inflammatory monocytes in CD8+ T cell priming, we generated LyzCreTet2fl/fl-CD11c-DTR mice, in which Tet2 is deleted in myeloid cells and CD11c+ dendritic cells can be selectively ablated by diphtheria toxin (Figure S7G). DC depletion did not alter CD8+ T cell activation (Figure S7H) or tumor response to irradiation (Figure S7I) versus the Tet2 cKO+IR group, indicating that intratumoral CD8+ T cell priming is mediated primarily by Tet2-deficient MDACs rather than conventional DCs, and establishing MDACs as necessary and sufficient for restoring antitumor CD8+ T cell immunity (Figure S7J).

To assess the clinical relevance of this program, we further analyzed patients with TET2-mutant clonal hematopoiesis (CH)20 and TCGA data, and found that the pro-inflammatory transcriptional program associated with Cluster 5_0, Cluster 5_1, and Trajectory 1 in Tet2-deficient myeloid cells was significantly enriched in TET2-mutant CH patients by GSEA (Figures S7K–S7M). The human MDAC signature (CD33, ITGAM, ITGAL) was likewise associated with improved overall survival across multiple cancer cohorts, including radiotherapy-treated patients (Figures 6O and S7N).

Pharmacological TET2 inhibition potentiates radiotherapy and immunotherapy

To test whether pharmacological TET2 inhibition phenocopies Tet2 genetic deletion, we treated tumor-bearing mice with a TET inhibitor (TET-inhibitor-C35) (Figure S8A)57,58. Consistent with Tet2 cKO mice, TET inhibition suppressed tumor growth in both MC38 and B16 models and further enhanced antitumor efficacy of IR over IR alone (Figures 7A–7B and S8B–S8C), and in the LLC spontaneous metastasis model, TET inhibition combined with IR reduced both number and size of lung metastases versus IR alone (Figures 7C–7E). These data suggest TET inhibition enhances the antitumor efficacy of irradiation, though this additional benefit was modest and the dominant therapeutic effect was driven by TET inhibition itself.

TET inhibitor treatment had no significant effect on tumor cell viability across tested concentrations (Figures S8D–S8F), suggesting host immunity was involved. Irradiation increased CD11b+Ly6C+Ly6G⁻ myeloid infiltration in WT tumors, an expansion abolished by TET inhibition (Figure 7F), which also markedly enhanced CD8+ T cell responses, with increased GzmB+ and IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells, and reduced exhaustion markers PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3 (Figures 7G–7H and S8G–S8J), consistent with irradiated Tet2 cKO mice. In MC38 model, TET inhibition improved tumor control and anti-PD-L1 efficacy; irradiation added further benefit, though TET inhibition remained the dominant effect (Figure 7J).

TET2 deficiency in hematopoietic stem cells is linked to myeloid malignancies. Although the Bana group59 demonstrated that pre-leukemic myeloproliferation arises exclusively in Tet2f/fVavCremice, but not in Tet2f/fLyzCre mice, consistent with the latter, TET inhibitor treatment did not increase CD11b+Gr1+ pre-leukemic myeloproliferation markers59 or splenic weights across all treated groups (Figures S8K–S8P). These findings indicate that short-term, low-dose TET inhibition does not induce leukemogenesis and instead enhances radio-immunotherapy efficacy synergistically.

Discussion

Increased TET2 expression in MDSCs from patients receiving radio–immunotherapy was associated with poor responses, implicating TET2 as a regulator of therapy resistance. Myeloid-specific Tet2 deletion synergized with irradiation to suppress primary tumor growth and metastasis in preclinical models by reprogramming monocyte toward a pro-inflammatory/immunostimulatory state - myeloid derived activating cells (MDACs) marked by co-expression of CD11b, Ly6C, and ITGAL. Mechanistically, Tet2 deficiency preserves caRNA m5C, enabling MBD6-(PR-DUB) complexes to erase repressive H2AK119ub marks, restore chromatin accessibility, and reinstates IFN-I responses. This epigenetic reprogramming enhanced radiation’s therapeutic effects by improving CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity and suppressing metastatic spread, revealing a targetable interface between RNA epitranscriptomics and chromatin regulation in cancer immunity.

Our findings place this mechanism within a spatiotemporal framework of radiation-induced myeloid reprogramming, in which the bone marrow is the earliest site of lineage bifurcation. Although upstream radiation-induced stress signals are comparable, divergence emerges in epigenetic execution: under WT conditions, activation of the p53–TET2 axis enforces a repressive chromatin program promoting immunosuppressive MDSC differentiation, whereas Tet2 deficiency redirects bone marrow monocytes toward an MDAC fate. This program is propagated during systemic trafficking and amplified within the tumor microenvironment, where MDACs acquire antigen-presenting capacity and drive CD8+ T cell activation.

These findings establish competing epitranscriptomic programs governing myeloid function: an immunosuppressive circuit maintained by radiation-TET2-m5C oxidation versus an immunostimulatory circuit activated by m5C preservation. This framework extends current models of stress-induced myelopoiesis60 by introducing an epigenetic bifurcation mechanism in which TET2 acts as a molecular switch between suppressive MDSC and immunostimulatory MDAC fates. Thus, tumor resistance is not static but a dynamic, reversible process governed by epigenetic lineage programming. Therapeutically, targeting this m5C-gated chromatin switch offers a strategy to reprogram myeloid fate and overcome radiotherapy/immunotherapy resistance. The efficacy of TET inhibition in combination with irradiation and immune checkpoint blockade in preclinical models highlights the potential to manipulate these epitranscriptomic checkpoints without inducing hematopoietic toxicity19,61. By redirecting myeloid differentiation toward MDACs, this approach enables simultaneous control of local tumor growth and metastasis.

In conclusion, our study defines a p53–TET2–caRNA epigenetic axis linking radiation-induced stress signaling to chromatin remodeling and systemic myeloid lineage differentiation, providing a mechanistic framework for radiotherapy resistance and a roadmap for restoring antitumor immunity through myeloid reprogramming.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be noted. First, our mechanistic studies relied on murine models and ex vivo or in vitro-cultured myeloid cells, which may not capture human tumor-associated MDSC heterogeneity. Although our CD3/CD28-based suppression assays support reduced suppressive activity of irradiated myeloid cells upon Tet2 loss, validation using patient-derived MDSCs and MDACs will be important. Second, spatial and functional imaging studies are needed to define MDAC interactions with T cells within tumors. Third, pharmacological TET inhibition improved tumor control and radiotherapy efficacy, but its additive benefit with irradiation was modest in the models tested. Future studies combining other modalities and humanized tumor models are warranted.

STAR METHODS

EXPERIMENT MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Patients samples

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from patients with metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) enrolled in our phase I COSINR trial (NCT03223155) receiving sequential or concurrent SBRT and immune checkpoint blockade therapy (Ipilimumab and Nivolumab). Paired samples were collected pre-treatment and post-SBRT completion under protocols approved by the University of Chicago Biological Sciences Division Institutional Review Board (IRB-17-0547), with written informed consent obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cancer cell lines

The MC38 murine colon adenocarcinoma, LLC Lewis lung carcinoma, and B16F10 melanoma cell lines were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA). The B16-SIINFEKL(OT-I)-ZsGreen (B16-OZ) cell line was generated and maintained in our laboratory. All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Corning, Manassas, VA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma, St Louis, MO) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (10,000 U/mL penicillin, 10 mg/mL streptomycin), and maintained at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed negative for mycoplasma contamination. ATCC-derived cell lines were authenticated by the provider and used within limited passages after thawing. The B16-SIINFEKL(OT-I)-ZsGreen cell line was generated and maintained in our laboratory and was validated by antigen/ZsGreen expression before use.

Mice

All mice were housed and used according to the animal experimental guidelines set by the Institute of Animal Care and Use Committee of The University of Chicago. All animals were maintained in pathogen-free conditions and cared for in accordance with the International Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care policies and certification. Tet2flox/flox mice, LyzCre Tet2flox/flox mice, C57BL/6 mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. Male and female mice aged eight to ten weeks were used in the experiments.

METHOD DETAILS

Tumor growth and treatment

MC38, LLC, and B16 tumor cells (1×106) were implanted subcutaneously into the right flanks of WT or Tet2 cKO mice. Once tumors reached ~100 mm3 (length × width × height × 0.5), mice received a single 20 Gy dose of localized ionizing radiation (IR) or sham treatment. Lung metastasis arising from LLC tumours was assessed at the end point, 30 days after tumor implantation.Tet2 cKO mice exhibited significantly delayed tumor growth compared to WT controls (Figure S1D). To ensure comparable baseline tumor volumes at the time of irradiation, Tet2 cKO mice received a higher initial tumor cell inoculum (2 × 106 cells) versus wild-type controls (1 × 106 cells) in designated experiments.

For anti–PD-L1 therapy, mice received 200μg of anti–PD-L1 antibody intraperitoneally, twice weekly for four doses. For TET inhibitor treatment, 10 μM of the compound was administered intravenously every other day for five doses, with appropriate vehicle controls. In DT-mediated monocytes depletion and adoptive transfer experiments, LyzcreCSF1R-DTR mice were injected intraperitoneally with DT (50 ng/mouse), followed the next day by tumor-localized IR and transfer of WT/Tet2 KO/Tet2 KO Rmer21b-ASO–treated/Tet2 KO Mbd6-ASO–treated monocytes. A second DT dose was given on day 5. For CD8+ T cell depletion, 200 μg of anti-CD8α antibody was administered intraperitoneally one day prior to treatment initiation and continued twice weekly thereafter.Tumor growth was monitored biweekly for 3–4 weeks. Each experiments were independently repeated 3 times with consistent results.

Flow cytometry

Tumors and lymph nodes were harvested and processed to generate single-cell suspensions. Tumor tissues were minced and enzymatically digested using 1 mg/ml collagenase I/IV (C4-22-1G, Sigma-Aldrich) and 200 μg/ml DNase I (04536282001, Millipore Sigma) at 37°C for 45 min, while lymph node cells were isolated by mechanical dissociation through 70 μm strainers. Cell suspensions were filtered (70 μm), washed twice in PBS-based staining buffer (2% FBS, 0.5 mM EDTA). Dead cells were stained with Zombie-NIR(BioLegend, 423105). Cells were blocked with anti-FcγR antibodies(2.4G2, CP025, BioXcell, 1:100) for 10 min. Surface staining was performed using fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (1:200 dilution) for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. For intracellular targets, cells were fixed/permeabilized (BD Cytofix/Cytoperm solution, 554722, Fisher Scientific) prior to antibody staining. For intracellular staining of IFN-γ, cells were stimulated with Cell Activation Cocktail with Brefeldin A(Biolegend,423303) for 6–8 h and followed by intracellular staining using BD Cytofix/Cytoperm intracellular staining kit. For TET2 intracellular staining, cells were fixed, washed twice with diluted Permeabilization Buffer, then incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-TET2 primary antibody. After washing, cells were stained with Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Life Technologies, 60 min, 4°C, dark). All samples were analyzed on a BD Fortessa flow cytometer, with data processed using FlowJo v10 software.

Histology

Tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for a minimum of 24 hours and sent to the university core facility for embedding and processing. The slides were stained using Leica Bond RX automated stainer. The protocol named as “No Post Primary 1h Bond DAB Refine” was used. Epitope retrieval solution I (Leica Biosystems, AR9961) was used for heat treatment for 20 minutes. Slides were scanned using CRi Panoramic SCAN 40x Whole Slide Scanner and analyzed by QuPath (v0.1.2). One section or two sections at a distance of 200 μm were analyzed per lung at experimental endpoint.

Volumetric microscopy of mouse tumors was performed as previously described17,69. Briefly, excised tumors were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at The University of Chicago HTRC core facility. Following deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through graded ethanol series, antigen retrieval was conducted in citrate buffer (90°C, 45 min). Tissue sections were blocked with 5% BSA and immunostained using anti-CD8 (BioLegend, 53–6.7; 1:100), anti-FSCN1 (Santa Cruz, 55-k2; 1:50), and anti-ITGAL (BioLegend, H155–78; 1:200) antibodies. Stained sections were mounted in Mowiol and imaged on a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope equipped with a 20×/0.75 NA objective. Whole-tumor 3D image stacks were acquired with 2 μm z-spacing (minimum 20 μm depth), processed using Leica LIGHTNING deconvolution (v3.5.7), and analyzed with Imaris 9.9 software for 3D surface reconstruction of individual cells.

ELISPOT assay

CD8+ T cells were isolated from MC38-OVA tumors three days post-irradiation (2–4×105 cells/condition) and stimulated ± 1 μg/ml SIINFEKL peptide for 48–72 hr. In parallel, naïve CD11c+ DCs were pulsed with irradiated tumor cells (6 hr), then co-cultured with CD8+ T cells (48–72 hr). IFN-γ production was quantified by ELISPOT (BDB551873, BD Biosciences) using manufacturer’s protocols, with spot analysis performed on an ELISPOT plate reader.

Cytokine detection

MC38 tumors were harvested from WT or Tet2 cKO mice three days post-IR, homogenized in PBS with protease inhibitors (1:100,78442, Thermo Fisher Scientific,), and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min) to obtain supernatant. Cytokine concentrations were quantified using the LEGENDplex Mouse Inflammation Panel (13-plex; 740446, BioLegend) on V-bottom plates, with acquisition on a BD Fortessa flow cytometer and data analysis using LEGENDplex v8.0 software (BioLegend).

BM-monocyte induction and isolation

Bone marrow was harvested from wild-type (WT) and Tet2 cKO mice to prepare single-cell suspensions, hereafter referred to as fresh bone marrow cells. These cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 20 ng/ml recombinant mouse GM-CSF (carrier-free,576306, BioLegend). On day 2, fresh medium containing GM-CSF was added to maintain cytokine levels. On day 4, CD11b+Ly6C+ bone marrow-derived monocytes were isolated from the cultured bone marrow cells through flow sorting.

T cell suppression assay

Tumor-infiltrating or bone marrow-derived MDSCs were co-cultured with naïve splenic CD8+ T cells isolated by EasySep Mouse CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit(19853, STEMCELL) at a 4:1 (T cell:MDSC) ratio. CD8+ T cells were pre-labeled with CellTrace CFSE (C34554, Invitrogen) and stimulated with anti-CD3/CD28 beads(11453D, Thermo Fisher) prior to co-culture. Proliferation was assessed by flow cytometric analysis of CFSE dilution.

Knockdown in BM-monocyte

SiRNA targeting mouse Trp53 (sc-29436, Santa Cruz,)was transfected into bone marrow derived MDSCs by Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (13778150, Invitrogen) according to manufacturer’s protocol. The sequences of AUMsilence ASOs(Antisense oligonucleotides, ASOs) targeting Mbd6(TCAGAGGGCACCAGGTTGGAT), CaRNA-LTR-Rmer21b(TGTAAGTGTTTGTGCTTGGAT) and Cxxc5(AAGAGTAGTGAGAGAGTGGTC) were transfected into BM-monocyte according to manufacturer’s(AUM BioTech, LLC) protocol. Two days after the transfection, the cells were collected. The knockdown efficiency was detected by qPCR.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted by using RNeasy Plus Mini Kit(Qiagen). cDNA was then synthesized by using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit(4368814, Applied Biosystems). RT-qPCR using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix(4309155, Applied Biosystems) was performed in QuantStudio 3 according to manufacturer’s instruction. The specific mRNA primers for RT-qPCR are as follows: Tet2 (Forward: AGAGAAGACAATCGAGAAGTCGG, Reverse:CCTTCCGTACTCCCAAACTCAT);Mbd6(Forward:ATGGAACGGTCCCCAAGAAG, Reverse:GCCACGACGAGACTTTCTGAC);IFNβ (Forward:GCACTGGGTGGAATGAGACT, Reverse:AGTGGAGAGCAGTTGAGGACA);Rmer21b(Forward:TTCTCCCTTCAGAGCTGCAT, Reverse:TGTAAGTGTTTGTGCTTGGATAGG); Cxxc5(Forward: GCCAAAAGGCAGGA GGAACAGA, Reverse:TGATACCGCTCTTGTTCCGACG); Cxcl9(Forward:CCT AGTGATAAGGAATGCACGATG, Reverse:CTAGGCAGGTTTGATCTCCGTTC); Cxcl10(Forward:ATCATCCCTGCGAGCCTATCCT, Reverse:GACCTTTTTTGGCTAAACGCTTTC-3). GAPDH was chosen as an endogenous control. Gene expression was calculated by using the 2-ΔΔCt method, fold difference = 2−(ΔCtA −ΔCtB), where Ct represents cycle threshold.

Bulk mRNA-seq

Ly6C+BM-monocytes were generated from WT and Tet2 cKO mice as described in ‘BM-monocyte induction and isolation’. Cells were either irradiated (8 Gy) or maintained as untreated controls. Two hours post-irradiation, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Poly(A)+ mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads, and stranded RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v2 - Pico Input Mammalian (634411, TaKaRa Bio) and quality-checked by Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) prior to paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina) with dual-indexing at the University of Chicago Genomics Core.

Raw sequencing reads were trimmed to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases using Cutadapt (v4.8)1. The cleaned reads were then aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome (mm10) and transcriptome, along with External RNA Control Consortium (ERCC) spike-in sequences (Thermo Fisher Scientific, when applicable), using STAR aligner (v2.7.9a)70. Gene annotation files (M23) were downloaded from the GENCODE database (https://www.gencodegenes.org/)71. For chromatin-associated RNA (caRNA) analysis, regulatory regions were defined according to criteria described in a previous study40. Gene-level quantification for both GENCODE-annotated genes and chromatin-associated RNAs (caRNAs) was performed using featureCounts (v2.0.3)72. Factors of unwanted variation estimated by RUVSeq73, using ERCC spike-ins as negative controls, were used to normalize the expression matrix. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package (v1.28.0)74. Genes with an P value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using GSEA75 and GSEApy76 for pathway enrichment and visualization.

Chromatin-associated RNA-seq(caRNA-seq)

Ly6C+ BM-monocytes were generated from WT and Tet2 cKO mice as described in ‘BM-monocyte induction and isolation’. Cells were either irradiated (8 Gy) or left untreated as controls. Two hours post-irradiation, chromatin-associated RNAs (caRNAs) were extracted using our previously established protocol29. RNA samples were then subjected to ribosomal RNA depletion using the RiboMinus Eukaryote System v2 (A15026, Invitrogen) followed by size selection with the RNA Clean & Concentrator-5 kit (R1013, Zymo Research) according to manufacturer’s instructions. High-quality caRNA libraries were prepared using the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v2 - Pico Input Mammalian (634411, TaKaRa Bio), with all steps performed as specified in the product manual. Finally, paired-end sequencing was carried out on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) at the University of Chicago Genomics Core.

EU-based nascent RNA labeling analysis

Nascent RNA was labeled by incubating cells with 1 mM 5-ethynyl uridine (EU) for 60 min at 37°C, followed by detection using Click-iT™ RNA Alexa Fluor™ 488 Imaging Kit according to manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were washed with 0.5% BSA/PBS (3,000 × g, 3 min, RT) between steps, except during the Click-iT® reaction rinse which used the specified buffer.

DNase I–TUNEL assay

To quantitatively assess chromatin accessibility, treated monocytes were subjected to DNase I-TUNEL assay following established protocols with modifications. Briefly, ASO-transfected cells were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and treated with 1 U/ml DNase I (EN0521, Thermo Fisher) for 5 min at 37°C before labeling with DeadEnd Fluorometric TUNEL System (G3250, Promega). propriate controls including DNase I-treated positive controls and rTdT-omitted negative controls were processed in parallel. High-resolution confocal imaging was performed on a Leica SP8 microscope (63× oil objective) with standardized acquisition parameters.

Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing(ATAC-seq)

ATAC-seq was performed on 100,000 Ly6C+ BM-monocytes isolated from wild-type (WT) and Tet2 conditional knockout (Tet2 cKO) mice, either irradiated (8 Gy) or left untreated as controls. Two hours post-irradiation, cells were processed using the ATAC-seq kit (53150, Active Motif) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Each replicate included an equal amount of Drosophila spike-in DNA (53154, Active Motif) for normalization.

Cells were permeabilized in a buffer containing 0.1% Tween-20 and 0.01% digitonin (supplied with the kit), and accessible chromatin regions were tagmented using a pre-assembled Tn5 transposome complex. Following genomic DNA extraction, libraries were prepared via PCR amplification and pooled for sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at the University of Chicago Genomics Core.

Sequencing reads were first trimmed using Cutadapt (v4.8) to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases. Cleaned reads were aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome (mm10) using Bowtie2 (v2.4.4)77, and only uniquely mapped reads were retained. Reads aligned to the mitochondrial genome and PCR duplicates were removed. MACS3 (v3.0.1)66 was used to identify ATAC-seq peaks. Peaks overlapping with ENCODE blacklist regions were excluded to minimize potential artifacts. Peaks consistently detected in at least two biological replicates were merged using BEDTools (v2.31.0)78 for downstream analyses. The chromatin accessibility (ATAC) were normalized by considering both sequencing depth and spike-in Drosophila melanogaster chromatin. To identify differentially accessible chromatin regions, read counts over merged peaks were quantified using featureCounts (v2.0.3)72. Differential accessibility analysis was then performed using DESeq2 (v1.28.0)74. Functional enrichment analysis of the differentially accessible regions was conducted using GSEA76.

Quantitative LC-MS/MS analysis of RNA modifications

For RNA modification profiling, ribodepleted RNA (75 ng) was sequentially digested with nuclease P1 (Sigma N8630; 20 mM ammonium acetate, pH 5.3, 42°C, 2 h) followed by FastAP thermosensitive alkaline phosphatase treatment (Thermo EF0651; 1× buffer, 37°C, 2 h). Digested samples were filtered (0.22 μm) and analyzed by LC-MS/MS (Agilent 6460) using a C18 reversed-phase column in positive electrospray ionization mode. RNA modifications were quantified via specific mass transitions (m5C: 258→126; hm5C: 274→142) against pure nucleoside standard curves, with three technical replicates per sample. Data were normalized to total nucleoside content and analyzed using Agilent MassHunter Quantitative Analysis software (v10.0).

m5C-RNA Immunoprecipitation and Sequencing

Spike-in RNA mixes (m5C-modified/unmodified) were prepared as previously described and added to chromatin-associated RNA prior to fragmentation. RNA was fragmented in 1× NEB fragmentation buffer (E6186A; 94°C, 4 min), with reactions halted by stop solution.

For immunoprecipitation, 4 μg of anti-m5C antibody (MAb-081-100, Diagenode) or control IgG (ab37355, Abcam) was conjugated to protein G beads (1003D, Invitrogen) in IP buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Triton X-100, 1 mM spermidine) for 2 h at 4°C. Denatured caRNA (10 μg) from BM-monocyte of four treatment groups(WT/Tet2 KO with/without IR) was incubated overnight at 4°C with antibody-bound beads in the presence of SUPERaseIn RNase Inhibitor (AM2694, Invitrogen). After stringent washing, bound RNAs were eluted in buffer (5 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA, 0.05% SDS, proteinase K; 50°C, 1 h), purified using Zymo RNA Clean & Concentrator-5(R1013, Zymo Research), and quantified by RT-qPCR.

For library preparation, immunoprecipitated RNAs were processed with the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v2 (634411, TaKaRa) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform at the University of Chicago Genomics Core.

Western blot

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (89900, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 1× Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (78441, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein concentrations were determined using Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kits (23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of total protein were denatured in 1× Laemmli loading buffer (1610747, Bio-Rad) at 90°C for 10 min, resolved on 4–12% NuPAGE Bis-Tris gels (NP0335BOX, Invitrogen), and transferred to PVDF membranes (88585, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Membranes were blocked with 3% BSA (A7030, Millipore-Sigma) in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) for 60 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing, membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using SuperSignal West Dura chemiluminescent substrate (34075, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged on a FluorChem R system (ProteinSimple).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) qPCR Assay

ChIP assays were performed using the Magna ChIP A/G Kit (17-10085, Millipore) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 1×107 Ly6c+ BM-monocytes were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature to crosslink protein-DNA complexes. Cells were lysed and chromatin was sheared by sonication to obtain DNA fragments averaging 200–500 bp. Chromatin extracts were immunoprecipitated overnight at 4°C with 5 μg of target-specific antibody or control IgG, coupled to protein A/G magnetic beads. After extensive washing, protein-DNA complexes were eluted and crosslinks were reversed by incubation at 65°C for 4 h. DNA was purified using the kit’s purification columns and analyzed by quantitative PCR with primers specific to the Tet2 promoter region.For quantification, 1% of the input chromatin was processed in parallel as an internal control. Enrichment was calculated using the percentage of input method: (100×2((Input Ct - 6.64) – Tet2 Ct)), where Ct represents threshold cycle values. All qPCR reactions were performed in technical triplicates.

CUT&Tag analysis

Cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag) analysis was conducted using the CUT&Tag-IT Assay Kit (53160, Active Motif) following the manufacturer’s protocol. 100,000 Ly6C+ BM-monocytes isolated from WT and Tet2 cKO mice, either irradiated (8 Gy) or left untreated as controls. Two hours post-irradiation, cells were washed with 1× wash buffer. The washed cells were conjugated to concanavalin A beads and permeabilized using a Digitonin-containing buffer before incubation with primary antibodies (anti-H2AK119ub or normal rabbit IgG). Preassembled protein A-Tn5 transposome complexes were employed for DNA tagmentation following secondary antibody conjugation. Equal amounts of Drosophila spike-in chromatin preps (53083, Active Motif) were added to each sample and subjected to the Tn5 tagmentation reaction. Tagged DNA was extracted by proteinase K digestion and amplified by PCR with indexed primers to construct DNA libraries. These DNA libraries were subjected to high-throughput sequencing using the NovaSeq 6000 platform at the University of Chicago Genomics Core.

High-quality reads were aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome (mm10) using Bowtie2 (v2.4.4)77 with the following parameters: --end-to-end --very-sensitive --no-mixed --no-discordant --phred33 --minins 10 --maxins 700 --dovetail −1. MACS3 (v3.0.1)66 was used for peak calling with the options: --nomodel --keep-dup auto -q 1e-4 --broad --broad-cutoff 0.01 -f BAMPE. Peak annotation was performed using the annotatePeaks.pl script from HOMER (v4.11)79. Signal visualization across genomic regions was carried out using IGV (v2.6.3)80. Signal intensities for H2AK119ub CUT&Tag were quantified using BEDTools78, and read counts were normalized to RPKM in 20-bp bins across defined genomic regions. The normalized signals were used to generate heatmaps with deepTools (v3.3.1)81, including those showing signal overlap between CUT&Tag and ATAC-seq datasets.

Quantitative analysis of RNA modification levels of Cross-linking and immunoprecipitation(CLIP) RNA

Radiated BM-monocytes from WT or Tet2 cKO mice were washed twice with DPBS before UV cross-linking at 254 nm using a Stratalinker (Stratagene). The cells were then resuspended in three volumes of ice-cold CLIP lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 2 mM EDTA, 0.5% (v/v) NP-40, 0.5 mM DTT, 1× Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (78442, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1× RNaseOUT recombinant ribonuclease inhibitor (10777019, Invitrogen). The pellets were lysed by rotating at 4 °C for 15 min. The cell suspensions were subsequently sonicated using the Bioruptor system (Diagenode) with 30s on/30s off for five cycles. Lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 21,000g for 15 min at 4 °C in a benchtop centrifuge. The supernatants were incubated with MBD6-antibody (gifted from Prof. Lu Wang82)-conjugated protein G beads (Invitrogen, 1001D) overnight at 4 °C on an end-to-end rotor. Beads were washed extensively with 1 ml wash buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 300 mM KCl, 0.05% (v/v) NP-40, 1× Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail, and 1× RNaseOUT recombinant ribonuclease inhibitor) at 4 °C five times. Input and IP samples were subsequently treated with proteinase K (25530049, Invitrogen) to release cross-linked RNA, which was recovered using TRIzol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen). Ribosomal RNA was then depleted using the RiboMinus Eukaryote System v2 (Invitrogen, A15026), followed by purification and size-selection with the RNA Clean & Concentrator-5 kit (R1013, Zymo Research). The recovered RNAs were then subjected to RT-PCR for downstream analysis.

Single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA) analysis

To profile the immune landscape of MC38 tumors, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on CD45+ immune cells isolated from wild-type (WT) and Tet2 conditional knockout (Tet2 cKO) mice, with or without prior irradiation (20 Gy). Tumors were pooled and dissociated into single-cell suspensions. Viable cells were labeled with Zombie NIR viability dye (30 min), followed by anti-mouse CD45 antibody staining (30 min). Zombie NIR−CD45+ single cells were FACS-sorted and processed for scRNA-seq library preparation using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ GEM, Library & Gel Bead Kit v3.1 (1000128), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform (University of Chicago Genomics Facility), with a target recovery of ~8,000 cells per library.

Raw single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were processed using Cell Ranger (v7.2.0, 10X Genomics). Briefly, Illumina base call files (BCL) were demultiplexed into FASTQ format using cellranger mkfastq. Sequencing reads were then aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10; GENCODE vM23/Ensembl 98, released July 2020 by 10X Genomics) and filtered for unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) using cellranger count. This pipeline generated a digital gene expression matrix, where each entry represents the UMI count for a given gene in an individual cell

Low-quality cells with UMIs beyond two standard deviation or having mitochondrial reads larger than 25% were discarded from further analyses.For scRNA-seq of mMDSC, as we applied Cell Hashing method using a series of oligo-tagged antibodies against ubiquitously expressed surface proteins with different barcodes to uniquely label cells from distinct samples, we demultiplex cells into different samples based on HTO enrichment by using HTODemux method in Seurat package (v4.4.0).

The processed whole gene expression matrix was then fed to Seurat (v4.4.0) for downstream analyses. Briefly, the UMI count matrix was normalized by using ‘SCTransformfunction’83. ‘Hormany’84 package was used to correct the batch effect among groups. Clustering analyses were performed using the first 30 principal components for constructing the shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph by using ‘FindNeighbors’ function, and then Louvain clustering algorithm was used to group the cells into different clusters. Next, we applied scClassify (v1.2.0)63 for cell type classification based on cell types hierarchies constructed from reference datasets (E-MTAB-8832, CD45+ immune cells sorted from MC38 tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice)85.

For lineage trajectory analyses, we specifically restricted the analysis to monocyte-derived populations. Trajectory inference was performed on CD45+CD11b+Ly6C+ cells and closely related monocyte-derived clusters, thereby excluding macrophage populations with transcriptional features consistent with tissue residency. In addition, clusters expressing canonical tissue-resident macrophage markers, including Timd4, Lyve1, and F13a1, were excluded from trajectory reconstruction to minimize lineage ambiguity and ensure accurate inference of radiation-induced monocyte fate transitions.

TCGA data analysis

TCGA data was acquired and analyzed in part using the Xena Platform86. For survival analysis, an MDAC signature score based on expression of ITGAM, ITGAL, CD33 was applied to the TCGA data. For Pan cancer, Lung cancer cohort’s gene expression was divided into high and low expression based on the median gene expression of all samples. Samples in the lowest and highest groups received a score of −1 and 1, respectively. Scores were added for each sample. Samples were filtered for patients receiving radiation therapy. Survival data from these groups were then compared and significance calculated using the ggsurvplot function in R.

Quantification and statistical analysis

To estimate the statistical significance of differences between two groups, we used an unpaired Student’s t-test to calculate two-tailed p-values. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA with multiple comparison tests was performed when more than two groups were compared. Survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan-Meier curves and evaluated with log-rank Mantel-Cox tests. Error bars represent the standard error of the mean (SEM), unless otherwise noted. p-values are indicated in the figures. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0).
