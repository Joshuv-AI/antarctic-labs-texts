Boundary-preserving weak Approximation for some semilinear stochastic partial differential equations
Johan Ulander

Abstract
. We propose and analyse a boundary-preserving numerical scheme for the weak approximation for some stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) with bounded state-space. We impose regularity assumptions on the drift and diffusion coefficients only locally on the state-space. In particular, the drift and diffusion coefficients may be non-globally Lipschitz continuous and superlinearly growing. The scheme consists of a finite difference discretisation in space and a Lie–Trotter time splitting followed by exact simulation and exact integration in time. The proposed scheme converges in the weak sense of order 1 / 4 1/4 in time and of order 1 / 2 1/2 in space, for globally Lipschitz continuous test functions. We prove the weak convergence order in time by proving strong convergence towards a strong solution driven by a different noise process. The convergence order in space follows from known results. The boundary-preserving property is ensured by the use of Lie–Trotter time splitting followed by exact simulation and exact integration. Numerical experiments confirm the theoretical results and demonstrate the practical advantages of the proposed Lie–Trotter-Exact (LTE) scheme compared to existing schemes for SPDEs.

1.
Introduction

Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) arise in modelling various phenomena across scientific disciplines
[
12
,
17
,
20
,
21
,
30
]
. Certain stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) take values only in a strict subset of the target-space, referred to as the invariant domain of the SPDE. For instance, the stochastic heat equation (SHE) with non-negative initial data should remain non-negative to be physically relevant. However, the SHEs with a globally Lipschitz source term and additive noise does not preserve non-negativity.

Many SPDEs of interest result from adding noise to partial differential equations (PDEs) that take values only in a half-bounded or bounded domain. For instance, the solutions of the classical Allen–Cahn PDE take values only in
[-1,1]
. Introducing noise of the form
(1-u^{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W
yields a stochastic Allen–Cahn equation whose solutions are also confined to
[-1,1]
.

Classical numerical schemes for SPDEs are known to leave the invariant domains of SPDEs
[
7
]
. In this work, we construct and analyse an implementable numerical scheme that preserves the invariant domain of the SPDE while converging in the weak sense to the mild solution.

We consider
(1+1)
-dimensional semilinear stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) of the form

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u(t)=\left(\Delta u(t)+f(u(t))\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+g(u(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(t),\ (t,x)\in(0,T]\times[0,1],\\
&u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D},\,x\in[0,1],\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

where
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}=[a,b]
denotes the closure of some open and bounded domain
\mathcal{D}=(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}
and where
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(t)
is space-time white noise, subject to constant Dirichlet boundary conditions equal to a value in
\mathcal{D}
. The coefficient functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are such that the mild solution
u(x,t)\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}
for every
x\in[0,1]
and
t\in[0,T]
, almost surely. Without loss of generality, we assume that
u
satisfies homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions and that
0\in\mathcal{D}
. See Section
2
for the precise definitions and assumptions. We remark that
f
and
g
are not assumed to be globally Lipschitz continuous. In fact, the numerical examples considered in this work (see Section
4
) violate the global Lipschitz property in both
f
and
g
. This lack of global Lipschitz continuity poses difficulties to approximate the solution of the SPDE in (
1
). Traditional SPDE theory requires
f
and
g
to be globally Lipschitz continuous to ensure well-posedness and convergence of classical numerical schemes. In fact, it was shown in
[
16
]
that classical schemes may diverge in moments for SDEs violating the global Lipschitz property, and such SDEs may arise after discretising an SPDE in space.

A numerical scheme
\hat{u}_{m,n}
, for some indexing
m
and
n
, for (
1
) is called boundary-preserving if
\hat{u}_{m,n}\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}
for all
m
and
n
, almost surely. In many applications, the invariant domain of a model has physical significance, and numerical approximations that leave the invariant domain can be interpreted as non-physical. For example, population dynamics models
[
12
,
18
]
and phase-separation dynamics models
[
1
]
are typically defined in
[0,1]
and temperature models (e.g, the classical heat equation) are only physical in
[0,\infty)
.

If a numerical approximation leaves the physical invariant domain of the model, then it can be influenced by forces that are not present in the model. To illustrate, considered the SPDE in (
1
) with solutions confined to
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}=[0,1]
. The solution remains unaffected by any modifications to the coefficient functions
f
and
g
outside
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}=[0,1]
. However, modifying
f
and
g
outside
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}=[0,1]
can influence a numerical approximation that leaves the invariant domain
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}=[0,1]
. Boundary-preserving numerical schemes are designed to avoid such discrepancies, ensuring that the numerical approximation remains in the relevant physical invariant domain.

The considered setting includes several important SPDE. For example, the stochastic Allen–Cahn equation
[
11
]
with
f(r)=r-r^{3}
and
g(r)=1-r^{2}
, the stochastic Nagumo equation
[
25
]
with
f(r)=r(1-r)(r-\gamma)
, for some
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
, and
g(r)=r(1-r)
, and the SIS SPDE with
f(r)=r(1-r)
and
g(r)=r(1-r)
. For more details on these SPDEs and their properties, see Section
4
, where we use them as test cases for the numerical experiments.

The proposed numerical scheme combines a finite difference discretisation in space with a Lie–Trotter splitting followed by exact integration and exact simulation in time. This ensures that the numerical scheme converges in the weak sense to the mild solution while preserving the invariant domain of the considered SPDE in (
1
). We abbreviate the scheme by
LTE
for
L
ie–
T
rotter and
E
xact integration and simulation.

The main results of the paper are the following:

•

We propose a boundary-preserving numerical scheme for the weak approximation for SPDEs of the form (
1
).

•

We prove the boundary-preserving property and weak convergence of order
1/4
in time for Lipschitz continuous test functions, see Proposition
6
and Theorem
9
.

•

We numerically verify the boundary-preserving property and weak convergence order of
1/4
in time.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first boundary-preserving numerical scheme for boundary-preserving SPDEs with coefficient functions that violate the global Lipschitz condition.

The proof of weak convergence is based on establishing strong convergence to the mild solution of the SPDE (
1
) with respect to a different driving noise. The change of the driving noise is necessary due to the use of exact simulation as part of the numerical time integration. For globally Lipschitz continuous test functions, this approach yields sharp order of weak convergence (see Section
4
). It is important to note, however, that the proposed scheme does not convergence in the strong sense to the mild solution of the SPDE (
1
). This issue arises because of the use of exact simulation.

Before closing the introduction, we compare the proposed scheme to existing numerical schemes for similar problems with a focus on weakly convergent schemes and boundary-preserving schemes. The fully discrete numerical schemes consisting of FD approximation in space and EM, SEM, or exponential integrator approximation in time is known to convergence in the weak sense (in fact, the stronger property of convergence in probability) to the mild solution of (
1
). We refer to
[
13
,
14
]
for the former and to
[
2
]
for the latter. Although classical numerical schemes to approximate the solution of (
1
) are known to convergence in the weak sense, such schemes are known to not be boundary-preserving
[
6
]
.

Boundary-preserving schemes for SDEs has been extensively studied over the last two decades, with approaches ranging from applying specific transforms (e.g., the Lamperti transform) to using truncation schemes. We mention the works
[
9
,
15
,
24
,
27
,
29
,
31
,
32
]
as references to works on boundary-preserving schemes for SDEs. In contrast, boundary preservation for SPDEs has received significantly less attention. The first positivity-preserving scheme for some semilinear stochastic heat equations with space-dependent noise was proposed and studied in previous work by the author and collaborators in
[
6
]
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the second work on boundary-preserving numerical schemes for SPDEs and the first to treat SPDEs with bounded invariant domains, and allowing
f
and
g
to be superlinearly growing.

This paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we specify the setting, assumptions, and the necessary preliminaries for later sections. Next, we describe the proposed numerical scheme, establish important properties of it, and prove convergence in Section
3
. Finally, we provide numerical experiments to support our theoretical findings in Section

.

{\mathbb{P}}\left(u(t,x)\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}=[a,b],\ \forall t\in[0,T],\ \forall x\in[0,1]\right)=1.

In this section, we provide the necessary background on finite difference approximations with uniform meshes for the specific case of the considered SPDE in (
1

The finite difference method approximates differentials with finite difference quotients. We let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and introduce the space grid size
\Delta x=1/N
and the corresponding space grid points
x_{n}=n\Delta x
, for

\kappa^{N}(1)=1

First, we discretise the initial value of the SPDE in (

\displaystyle=N^{2}\left(u^{N}(t,x_{n+1})-2u^{N}(t,x_{n})+u^{N}(t,x_{n-1})\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+f(u^{N}(t,x_{n}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+Ng(u^{N}(t,x_{n}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\left(W(t,x_{n+1})-W(t,x_{n})\right),

for

u^{N}(t,0)=u^{N}(t,1)=0.

We now re-write the above in a more compact form. Let us define the
\mathbb{R}^{N-1}

as the vector of size

u^{N}_{n}(t)=\left(u^{N}(t)\right)_{n}=u^{N}(t,x_{n})

2.3.
Lie–Trotter time splitting

The idea of a Lie–Trotter time splitting scheme for ordinary differential equations (ODEs) is to decompose an ODE of the form

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y(t)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}=f_{1}(y(t))+f_{2}(y(t)),\ t\in(0,\Delta t],\\
&y(0)=y_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

that we wish to solve on a small interval
[0,\Delta t]
, into two ODEs

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y_{1}(t)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}=f_{1}(y_{1}(t)),\ t\in(0,\Delta t],\\
&y_{1}(0)=y_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

and

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y_{2}(t)}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}=f_{2}(y_{2}(t)),\ t\in(0,\Delta t],\\
&y_{2}(0)=y_{1}(\Delta t),\end{aligned}\right.

that are solved in sequence. We refer the interested reader to
[
26
]
for a complete exposition of splitting schemes.

2.4.
Exact simulation of SDEs

We dedicate this section to a summary of exact simulation for one-dimensional time-homogeneous SDEs of the form

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X(t)=f(X(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\lambda g(X(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B(t),\ t\in(0,T],\\
&X(0)=x_{0}\in(a,b),\end{aligned}\right.

(16)

for some

and

It turns out to be convenient to first apply a deterministic time-change to the SDE in (

for more details on this time-change. We introduce

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{X}(t)=\lambda^{-2}f(\widetilde{X}(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+g(\widetilde{X}(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B(t),\ t\in(0,\lambda^{2}T],\\

The Lamperti tranform

for some choice of

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y(t)=\alpha(Y(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B(t),\ t\in(0,\lambda^{2}T],\\
&Y(0)=\Phi(x_{0})\in\mathbb{R},\end{aligned}\right.

where the drift coefficient

\alpha(r)=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\frac{f(\Phi^{-1}(r))}{g(\Phi^{-1}(r))}-\frac{1}{2}g^{\prime}(\Phi^{-1}(r)),\ r\in\mathbb{R}.

Recall that the function
\Phi^{-1}:\mathbb{R}\to(a,b)
is bounded, bijective, continuously differentiable and has bounded derivative by Proposition

is well-defined if

and observe that if we let

. In fact, we let

to comply with
[
4
]
. Let

We are now in a position to describe the exact simulation and we do so for the SDE in (

(i)

Use the Lamperti transform

defined in (

) to convert the SDE in (
17

) with drift
\alpha
and additive noise.

(ii)

Sample a skeleton
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{L}
(at certain random time points

, where
\omega_{L}\sim h
and

is the (deterministic) end point of the interval under consideration and where

h(r)\propto\exp\left(A(r)-\frac{r^{2}}{2T_{\lambda}}\right),\ r\in\mathbb{R},

(21)

Note that

(iii)

Compute a reject probability based on

at any (non-random) time point(s)

(iv)

Apply the inverse Lamperti transform
\Phi^{-1}
to the skeleton (re-enumerated to include additional points)

.

The above described algorithm produces sampled points
\Phi^{-1}(\omega_{0}),\ldots,\Phi^{-1}(\omega_{L})
corresponding to the time points
\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{L}
that have the same distribution as
\widetilde{X}(\tau_{0}),\ldots,\widetilde{X}(\tau_{L})
. To obtain sampled points from the original SDE (
16

\Theta^{-1}
. At this point, we would like to raise three points. Firstly, in order to use the exact simulation algorithm we have to be able to exactly sample from the above defined distribution function

depends on the choices of

[

[
4

(i) and (iv) are self-explanatory, it remains to consider (ii) and (iii) for the transformed SDE with additive noise in (

We now derive the accept-reject probability for (iii), this will also motivate the precise construction in (ii). First we compare the sample paths
\omega\in\mathcal{C}([0,T_{\lambda}])

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y(t)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B(t),\ t\in(0,T_{\lambda}],\\

to the sample paths of the SDE with additive noise

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y(t)=\alpha(Y(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B(t),\ t\in(0,T_{\lambda}],\\
&Y(0)=0,\end{aligned}\right.

(23)

using Girsanov’s theorem. To this end, we let

be the probability measure on

M_{t}(\omega)={\operatorname{exp}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\alpha\left(\omega(s)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B(s)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\alpha^{2}\left(\omega(s)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right),\ t\in[0,T_{\lambda}],\ \omega\in\mathcal{C}([0,T_{\lambda}]).

The Radon-Nikodym derivative is then given by

, by, e.g., Novikov’s condition (see, e.g., equation

]
) and Proposition

. The problem with this approach is that we, in that case, would have to evaluate (

By recalling the definition of

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{Q}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{Z}}}=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{Q}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{W}}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{W}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{Z}}}
where

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{W}}}(\omega)=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi T_{\lambda}}h(\omega(T_{\lambda}))}{\exp\left(-\frac{(\omega(T_{\lambda})-\omega(0))^{2}}{2T_{\lambda}}\right)}

and in turn that

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{Q}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{Z}}}(\omega)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T_{\lambda}}\alpha^{\prime}(\omega(s))+\alpha^{2}(\omega(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right),

where

for every

, which is possible by Proposition

[

]

\phi(r)=\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{\prime}(r)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}(r)-k_{1},\ r\in\mathbb{R},

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{Q}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{Z}}}(\omega)\propto\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T_{\lambda}}\phi(\omega(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)\leq 1.

We accept or reject an element

3.
The boundary-preserving scheme

In this core part of the paper, we give a complete description of the boundary-preserving scheme and state and prove that the scheme is boundary-preserving and sharp weak convergence order. First, we let
M\in\mathbb{N}
and introduce the time grid size
\Delta t=T/M
and the corresponding time grid points
t_{m}=m\Delta t
, for
m=0,\ldots,M
. Both
N
and
\Delta x
will be used for notational convenience. Associated with the introduced time grid, we let
\ell^{M}:[0,T]\to\{t_{0},\ldots,t_{M}\}
be the step function defined by
\ell^{M}(t)=t_{m}
for
t\in[t_{m},t_{m+1})
for

and
\ell^{M}(T)=T
. We first provide the definition of the scheme in Section

. We then state and provide the main results in Section

.

3.1.
Description of the integrator

The idea of the proposed
L
ie–
T
rotter with
E
xact simulation and integration (
LTE
) scheme is to first use a spatial finite difference discretisation and then for the temporal discretisation to apply a Lie–Trotter time splitting followed by exact simulation and exact integration, respectively, of the two resulting subproblems. More precisely, first we apply a standard finite difference discretisation in space (see Section
2.2
) to the SPDE in equation (
1
) to obtain the following system of stochastic ordinary differential equations (SDEs)

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u^{N}(t)=\left(N^{2}D^{N}u^{N}(t)+f(u^{N}(t))\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{N}g(u^{N}(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{N}(t).

(29)

The second step is to split the above system of SDEs in (
29
) into one diagonal system of SDEs given by

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v^{M,N,1}(t)=f(v^{M,N,1}(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{N}g(v^{M,N,1}(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{N}(t),

(30)

and one non-diagonal system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) given by

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v^{M,N,2}(t)=N^{2}D^{N}v^{M,N,2}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(31)

By the assumptions in Section
2.1
, the system (
30
) can be exactly simulated (see Section
2.4
), and the system (
31
) can be exactly integrated using the exponential map
e^{tN^{2}D^{N}}
. Recall that we exclude the first component
n=0
and the last component
n=N
by the Dirichlet boundary conditions. The third step is to, starting at time grid point
t_{m}
, sample according to the dynamics of equation (
30
) and integrate equation (
31
) in succession on a small time interval
[t_{m},t_{m}+\Delta t]
. More precisely, given the numerical approximation
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m}=\left(u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m,n}\right)_{1\leq n\leq N-1}
at some time grid point
t_{m}=m\Delta t
, where
0\leq m\leq M-1
, we compute the numerical approximation
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m+1}
at the next time grid point
t_{m+1}=t_{m}+\Delta t
as follows:

(1)

Sample according to the dynamics of the diagonal system of SDEs

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v^{M,N,1}_{m}(t)=f(v^{M,N,1}_{m}(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{N}g(v^{M,N,1}_{m}(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{N}(t),\\
&v^{M,N,1}_{m}(t_{m})=u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m},\end{aligned}\right.

(32)

at time
t_{m+1}
to obtain the intermediate state
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m+1/2}=v^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m+1}=v^{M,N,1}_{m}(t_{m+1})
. We sample according to (
32
) by applying exact simulation componentwise, see Section
2.4
for details.

(2)

Integrate the tridiagonal linear system of ODEs

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}v^{M,N,2}_{m}(t)=N^{2}D^{N}v^{M,N,2}_{m}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,\\
&v^{M,N,2}_{m}(t_{m})=u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m+1/2}=v^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m+1},\end{aligned}\right.

(33)

on
[t_{m},t_{m+1}]
to obtain the numerical approximation

u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m+1}=v^{M,N,2}_{m}(t_{m+1})=e^{\Delta tN^{2}D^{N}}v_{m}^{M,N,1}(t_{m+1})

(34)

at the next time grid point
t_{m+1}
.

A key property of the
LTE
scheme is that it is guaranteed to remain in the invariant domain
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}
. The following lemma will be used to prove that the LTE scheme is boundary-preserving.

Lemma 5
.

If
v\in[a,b]^{N-1}
, then
e^{tN^{2}D^{N}}v\in[a,b]^{N-1}
for every
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

It suffices to show the case
a=0
, since we can decompose
v=\max(v,0)-\max(-v,0)
, into its positive and negatives parts, and use the case
a=0
for each part separately. We set
v_{0}=v_{N}=0
and extend
v
to a function
\bar{v}:[0,1]\to[0,b]
by linear interpolation. By positivity of the heat kernel and by the estimate in equation
(3.5)
in
[
13
]
, we have that

\int_{0}^{1}G^{N}(t,x_{n},y)\bar{v}(\kappa^{N}(y))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq\max_{x\in[0,1]}|\bar{v}(x)|=\max_{k=0,\ldots,N}|v_{k}|\leq b.

We evaluate the integral

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}G^{N}(t,x_{n},y)\bar{v}(\kappa^{N}(y))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y&=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\Delta xG^{N}(t,x_{n},x_{k})\bar{v}(x_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}G^{N}_{nk}(t)v_{k}\\
&=\left(e^{tN^{2}D^{N}}v\right)_{n}\geq 0,\end{split}

by assumption, to conclude that
e^{tN^{2}D^{N}}v\in[0,b]^{N-1}
.
∎

Proposition 6
.

Let
M,N\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose the assumptions in Section
2.1
are satisfied. Let
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m,n}
, for
0\leq m\leq M
and for
0\leq n\leq N
, be constructed by the
LTE
scheme defined above with initial value
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{0,n}=u_{0}(x_{n})
. Then

{\mathbb{P}}\left(u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m,n}\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}=[a,b],\ \forall m=0,\ldots,M,\ \forall n=0,\ldots,N\right)=1.

Proof of Proposition
6
.

We use induction on the time index
m=0,\ldots,M
to obtain the result.

(1)

By Assumption
1
, the statement is true for the base case:
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{0,n}=u(0,x_{n})\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}
for every
n=0,\ldots,N
.

(2)

Suppose that
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m,n}\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}
, for every
n=0,\ldots,N
, for some
m=0,\ldots,M-1
. It suffices to show that both sub-problems in equations (
32
) and (
33
) preserve this property. By componentwise applying Feller’s boundary classification (see, e.g.,
[
17
]
) or the author’s related work
[
32
]
, it follows that the intermediate state
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m+1/2,n}=v^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m+1,n}=\left(v^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m+1}\right)_{n}
from the first sub-problem (
32
) satisfies
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m+1/2,n}\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}
for every
n=1,\ldots,N-1
. By Lemma
5
, we conclude that the solution to the second sub-problem (
33
) satisfies
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m+1,n}\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}
for every
n=1,\ldots,N-1
.

∎

3.1.1.
Auxiliary space-time continuous extension

We have so far constructed the
LTE
approximation on the discretised space-time grid and we now describe how to extend this numerical scheme to be defined for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times[0,1]
. The extended scheme plays a crucial role in the convergence proof. More precisely, it allows us to compare the mild integral equations for
u^{N}
in (
13
) to a similar mild integral representation of
u^{\textrm{LTE}}
. The space-time extension follows the same ideas as the space-time extension used by the author and collaborators in
[
6
]
.

As exact simulation is an accept-reject algorithm with re-sampling the noise path and we use it to treat sub-problem
1
in (
32
), we have to use a different driving noise for the mild integral equation for the space-time extension of
u^{\textrm{LTE}}
. More precisely, let
\widetilde{W}^{N}(t)
denote the
(N-1)
-dimensional Brownian motion that corresponds to the exact simulation in sub-problem
1
in (
32
) and let
\widetilde{W}
denote a space-time white noise with the property that
\widetilde{W}^{N}_{n}(t)=\sqrt{N}\left(\widetilde{W}(t,x_{n+1})-\widetilde{W}(t,x_{n})\right)
.

We first extend in time and then we extend in space. For
m\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\}
, we let

u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m}(t)=e^{(\ell^{M}(t)-t_{m})N^{2}D^{N}}v_{m}^{M,N,1}(t)

for
t\in[t_{m},t_{m+1}]
, where
v_{m}^{M,N,1}
is defined in (
32
). Note that
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m}(t)
is not the same object as
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m}
. Then the following properties hold:

(1)

if
t\in[t_{m},t_{m+1})
, then
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m}(t)=v_{m}^{M,N,1}(t)
.

(2)

u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m}(t_{m})=v_{m}^{M,N,1}(t_{m})=u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m}
.

(3)

u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m}(t_{m+1})=e^{\Delta tN^{2}D^{N}}v_{m}^{M,N,1}(t_{m+1})=u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m+1}
.

Let now
u_{n}^{\textrm{LTE}}(t)=\left(u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m}(t)\right)_{n}
, for every
n=1,\ldots,N-1
and every
t\in[0,T]
. Note that, as
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m}(t_{m})=u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m}
and
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m}(t_{m+1})=u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m+1}
, we may drop the dependence on
m\in\{0,\ldots,M\}
without any confusion. We now claim that

\begin{split}u_{n}^{\textrm{LTE}}(t)&=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}G_{nk}^{N}(\ell^{M}(t))u_{0}(x_{k})+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}G_{nk}^{N}(\ell^{M}(t)-\ell^{M}(s))f(u_{k}^{\textrm{LTE}}(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\\
&+\sqrt{N}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}G_{nk}^{N}(\ell^{M}(t)-\ell^{M}(s))g(u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{k}(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}^{N}_{k}(s),\end{split}

(35)

for every
t\in[0,T]
. Indeed, let
v_{m,k}^{M,N,1}(t)=\left(v_{m}^{M,N,1}(t)\right)_{k}
for every
k=1,\ldots,N-1
, then for
t\in[t_{m},t_{m+1}]
we have

\displaystyle u_{n}^{\textrm{LTE}}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m}(t)\right)_{n}=\left(e^{(\ell^{M}(t)-t_{m})N^{2}D^{N}}v_{m}^{M,N,1}(t)\right)_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}G_{nk}^{N}(\ell^{M}(t)-t_{m})v_{m,k}^{M,N,1}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}G_{nk}^{N}(\ell^{M}(t)-t_{m})u_{m,k}^{\textrm{LTE}}(t_{m})+\int_{t_{m}}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}G_{nk}^{N}(\ell^{M}(t)-t_{m})f(u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m,k}(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\sqrt{N}\int_{t_{m}}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}G_{nk}^{N}(\ell^{M}(t)-t_{m})g(u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m,k}(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}_{k}^{N}(s),

where we used that
v_{m}^{M,N,1}(t)
solves the diagonal system of SDEs in (
32
) with initial value
u_{m}^{\textrm{LTE}}=u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m}(t_{m})
and that
v_{m,k}^{M,N,1}(s)=u_{m,k}^{\textrm{LTE}}(s)
for
s\in[t_{m},t_{m+1})
. Repeating this argument and using that
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m,k}(s)=u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{k}(s)
, for
s\in[t_{m},t_{m+1})
, gives the desired expression in (
35
). Note that, by Feller’s boundary classificiation, the extension in time and Proposition
6
implies that
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{n}
only takes values in
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}=[a,b]
almost surely

{\mathbb{P}}\left(u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{n}(t)\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}=[a,b],\ \forall t\in[0,T],\ \forall n=1,\ldots,N-1\right)=1.

We now describe the extension in the spatial variable. We define
u^{\textrm{LTE}}
as the solution to the following integral equation

\begin{split}u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t,x)&=\int_{0}^{1}G^{N}(\ell^{M}(t),x,y)u_{0}(\kappa^{N}(y))dy\\
&+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}G^{N}(\ell^{M}(t)-\ell^{M}(s),x,y)f(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,\kappa^{N}(y)))dsdy\\
&+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}G^{N}(\ell^{M}(t)-\ell^{M}(s),x,y)g(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,\kappa^{N}(y)))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}(s,y)\end{split}

(36)

for every
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times[0,1]
, almost surely. Notice that (
36
) is similar to the mild equation (
13
) for the semi-discrete approximation
u^{N}
. As will be shown later on, this similarity will be important for the convergence proof. The following lemma gives us that
u^{\textrm{LTE}}
as defined in (
36
) is a spatial extension of
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{n}
. In fact, it follows from (
36
) that
u^{\textrm{LTE}}
is the piecewise linear extension of
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{n}
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
m\in\{0,\ldots,M\}
and
n\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
. Then
u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t_{m},x_{n})=u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{n}(t_{m})=u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m,n}
, almost surely.

Proof of Lemma
7
.

By using the property
NG_{nk}^{N}(t)=G^{N}(t,x_{n},x_{k})
, we insert
x=x_{n}
in (
36
) to see that

\displaystyle u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t,x_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\int_{x_{k}}^{x_{k+1}}G^{N}(\ell^{M}(t),x_{n},x_{k})u_{0}(x_{k})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\int_{x_{k}}^{x_{k+1}}G^{N}(\ell^{M}(t)-\ell^{M}(s),x_{n},x_{k})f(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,x_{k}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\int_{x_{k}}^{x_{k+1}}G^{N}(\ell^{M}(t)-\ell^{M}(s),x_{n},x_{k})g(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,x_{k}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}(s,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}G^{N}_{nk}(\ell^{M}(t))u_{0}(x_{k})+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}G^{N}_{nk}(\ell^{M}(t)-\ell^{M}(s))f(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,x_{k}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\sqrt{N}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}G^{N}_{nk}(\ell^{M}(t)-\ell^{M}(s))g(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,x_{k}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}_{k}^{N}(s),

where we decomposed the integrals as
\int_{0}^{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\int_{x_{k}}^{x_{k+1}}
and
where we for the third term on the right hand side also used that
\int_{x_{k}}^{x_{k+1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}(s,y)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}_{k}^{N}(s)
by definition. Thus,
u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t,x_{n})
and
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{n}(t)
satisfy the same equation and must therefore be equal. We obtain the desired property by inserting
t=t_{m}

u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t_{m},x_{n})=u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{n}(t_{m})=u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m,n}.

∎

Note that by Proposition
6
and Lemma
7
, we obtain boundary preservation of the full space-time extension
u^{\textrm{LTE}}
satisfies

{\mathbb{P}}\left(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t,x)\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}=[a,b],\ \forall t\in[0,T],\ \forall x\in[0,1]\right)=1.

Recall that
u^{LTE}
is the piecewise linear extension of
u^{LTE}_{n}
by (
36
).

Lemma 8
.

Let
T>0
and let
t\in[0,T]
. We have the following time increment estimate for the
LTE
scheme

\sup_{n=0,\ldots,N}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t,x_{n})-u^{\textrm{LTE}}(\ell^{M}(t),x_{n})\right|^{2}\right]\leq C(f,g)\left(\Delta t^{2}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\right).

Proof.

Recall that
u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t,x_{n})
satisfies

u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t,x_{n})=u^{\textrm{LTE}}(\ell^{M}(t),x_{n})+\int_{\ell^{M}(t)}^{t}f(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,x_{n}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{N}\int_{\ell^{M}(t)}^{t}g(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,x_{n}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}^{N}_{n}(s),

since
u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t,x_{n})
solves the SDE in (
32
) on
[\ell^{M}(t),\ell^{M}(t)+\Delta t)
starting from
\ell^{M}(t)
with initial value
u^{\textrm{LTE}}(\ell^{M}(t),x_{n})
. Thus,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t,x_{n})-u^{\textrm{LTE}}(\ell^{M}(t),x_{n})\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\int_{\ell^{M}(t)}^{t}f(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,x_{n}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right|^{2}\right]\right.

\displaystyle+\left.N{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\int_{\ell^{M}(t)}^{t}g(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,x_{n}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}^{N}_{n}(s)\right|^{2}\right]\right).

We bound the first term using Jensen’s inequality for integrals

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\int_{\ell^{M}(t)}^{t}f(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,x_{n}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right|^{2}\right]\leq C(f)\Delta t^{2}

and we bound the second using the Itô isometry

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\int_{\ell^{M}(t)}^{t}g(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,x_{n}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}^{N}_{n}(s)\right|^{2}\right]\leq C(g)\Delta t,

to obtain the desired estimate

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t,x_{n})-u^{\textrm{LTE}}(\ell^{M}(t),x_{n})\right|^{2}\right]\leq C(f,g)\left(\Delta t^{2}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\right).

Note that we also used that
u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t,x)\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}=[a,b]
for all
t\in[0,T]
and for all
x\in[0,1]
and that
f
and
g
satisfy Assumption
2
and Assumption
3
, respectively.
∎

3.2.
Convergence result

In the following, we prove that the proposed
LTE
scheme converges weakly to the exact solution of the considered SPDE in (
1
). This is the content of the following theorem.

Theorem 9
.

Let the assumptions in Section
2.1
be satisfied. Let
u^{\textrm{LTE}}
be the
LTE
approximation defined in Section
3
and let
u
be the exact solution to the SPDE in equation (
1
). Then for any globally Lipschitz continuous
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
there exists a constant
C(F,T,f,g)
such that

\sup_{m=0,\ldots,M}\sup_{n=0,\ldots,N}\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[F\left(u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m,n}\right)\right]-{\mathbb{E}}\left[F\left(u(t_{m},x_{n})\right)\right]\right|\leq C(F,T,f,g)\left(\Delta x^{1/2}+\sqrt{\Delta t^{2}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}}\right).

(37)

If
\Delta t=\Delta x^{2}
, then

\sup_{m=0,\ldots,M}\sup_{n=0,\ldots,N}\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[F\left(u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m,n}\right)\right]-{\mathbb{E}}\left[F\left(u(t_{m},x_{n})\right)\right]\right|\leq C(F,T,f,g)\Delta t^{1/4}.

(38)

As we use exact simulation as part of the proposed LTE scheme, we cannot expect to obtain strong convergence in Theorem
9
. We also remark that the order of convergence in Theorem
9
is what is expected for strong convergence. In other words, for globally Lipschitz continuous test functions
F
, the order of weak convergence is equal to the expected strong convergence rate. This is in agreement with, for example,
[
5
]
. The proof of Theorem
9
follows from Theorem
3.1
in
[
13
]
and from the following proposition where we compare the
LTE
approximation
u^{\textrm{LTE}}
to the finite difference approximation
u^{N}
with respect to a different driving noise than used in Section
2.2
.

Proposition 10
.

Let the assumptions in Section
2.1
be satisfied. Let
u^{\textrm{LTE}}
be the
LTE
approximation defined in Section
3
and let
u^{N}
be finite difference solution defined as the solution to (
13
) with respect to the same driving noise
\widetilde{W}
as used for the
LTE
scheme. Then

\sup_{m=0,\ldots,M}\sup_{n=0,\ldots,N}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[|u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m,n}-u^{N}_{n}(t_{m})|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}\leq C(T,f,g)\sqrt{\Delta t^{2}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}}.

We change the driving noise for
u^{N}
in Proposition
10
to be able to derive strong convergence. The proof uses the standard arguments in order to apply a (discrete) Grönwall’s lemma and is similar to the proof of strong convergence by the author and collaborators in
[
6
]
. One important difference is that the solution
u
, the semi-discrete approximation
u^{N}
, and the
LTE
approximation
u^{\textrm{LTE}}
are all confined to
[a,b]
in the current setting, implying that moment bounds are immediate.

Proof of Proposition
10
.

Recall that
u^{\textrm{LTE}}_{m,n}=u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t_{m},x_{n})
and that
u^{N}_{n}(t_{m})=u^{N}(t_{m},x_{n})
. We use the integral equations (
36
) and (
13
) with
t=t_{m}
. Note that, by definition,
\ell^{M}(t_{m})=t_{m}
and so the integrals with the initial values in (
36
) and in (
13
) coincide for
t=t_{m}
. We first split up the error into one term containing the deterministic integral and one term containing the stochastic integral:

|u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t_{m},x_{n})-u^{N}(t_{m},x_{n})|\leq\left|I_{1}\right|+\left|I_{2}\right|,

where

I_{1}=\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\int_{0}^{1}G^{N}(t_{m}-\ell^{M}(s),x_{n},y)f(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,\kappa^{N}(y)))-G^{N}(t_{m}-s,x_{n},y)f(u^{N}(s,\kappa^{N}(y)))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

and

I_{2}=\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\int_{0}^{1}G^{N}(t_{m}-\ell^{M}(s),x_{n},y)g(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,\kappa^{N}(y)))-G^{N}(t_{m}-s,x_{n},y)g(u^{N}(s,\kappa^{N}(y)))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}(s,y).

As the estimates for
I_{2}
containing the stochastic integral are the most interesting ones and as the estimates for
I_{1}
are very similar, we leave the analogous computations for
I_{1}
to the reader.

We first apply the triangle inequality to further split the error into one error term containing the time regularity of
G^{N}
and one error term comparing
g(u^{N})
to
g(u^{\textrm{LTE}})
.

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(G^{N}(t_{m}-\ell^{M}(s),x_{n},y)-G^{N}(t_{m}-s,x_{n},y)\right)g(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,\kappa^{N}(y)))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}(s,y)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\int_{0}^{1}G^{N}(t_{m}-s,x_{n},y)\left(g(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,\kappa^{N}(y)))-g(u^{N}(s,\kappa^{N}(y)))\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}(s,y).

Then, by Itô’s isometry, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|I_{2}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\int_{0}^{1}\left|G^{N}(t_{m}-\ell^{M}(s),x_{n},y)-G^{N}(t_{m}-s,x_{n},y)\right|^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|g(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,\kappa^{N}(y)))|^{2}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle+2\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\int_{0}^{1}\left|G^{N}(t_{m}-s,x_{n},y)\right|^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|g(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,\kappa^{N}(y)))-g(u^{N}(s,\kappa^{N}(y)))|^{2}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

Since
u^{\textrm{LTE}}
and
u^{N}
only take values in
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}=[a,b]
(see Proposition
6
and Proposition
4
, respectively) and since
g
satisfies Assumption
3
, we can further estimate the above as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|I_{2}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(g)\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\int_{0}^{1}|G^{N}(t_{m}-\ell^{M}(s),x_{n},y)-G^{N}(t_{m}-s,x_{n},y)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle+C(g)\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\int_{0}^{1}|G^{N}(t_{m}-s,x_{n},y)|^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,\kappa^{N}(y))-u^{N}(s,\kappa^{N}(y))|^{2}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

We use (
11
) and (
12
) to obtain the estimates

\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\int_{0}^{1}\left|G^{N}(t_{m}-\ell^{M}(s),x_{n},y)-G^{N}(t_{m}-s,x_{n},y)\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}

and

\int_{0}^{1}\left|G^{N}(t_{m}-s,x,y)\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq C(T)\frac{1}{\sqrt{t_{m}-s}}.

Thus,

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|I_{2}\right|^{2}\right]\leq C(g)\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}+C(T,g)\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t_{m}-s}}\sup_{k=0,\ldots,N}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,x_{k})-u^{N}(s,x_{k})|^{2}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

The above is not the correct form to apply a discrete Grönwall’s inequality, to resolve this we split up as

\begin{split}u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,x_{k})-u^{N}(s,x_{k})&=u^{\textrm{LTE}}(\ell^{M}(s),x_{k})-u^{N}(\ell^{M}(s),x_{k})\\
&+u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,x_{k})-u^{\textrm{LTE}}(\ell^{M}(s),x_{k})\\
&+u^{N}(\ell^{M}(s),x_{k})-u^{N}(s,x_{k}).\end{split}

(39)

We estimate the second term on the right hand side of (
39
) as

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|u^{\textrm{LTE}}(s,x_{k})-u^{\textrm{LTE}}(\ell^{M}(s),x_{k})\right|^{2}\right]\leq C(f,g)\left(\Delta t^{2}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\right),

by Lemma
8
, and we estimate the third term on the right hand side of (
39
) as

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|u^{N}(\ell^{M}(s),x_{k})-u^{N}(s,x_{k})\right|^{2}\right]\leq C(T,f,g)\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x},

by the time regularity estimate in (
14
). Thus, we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|I_{2}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(g)\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}+C(T,f,g)\left(\Delta t^{2}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\right)\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t_{m}-s}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+C(T,g)\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t_{m}-s}}\sup_{k=0,\ldots,N}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|u^{\textrm{LTE}}(\ell^{M}(s),x_{k})-u^{N}(\ell^{M}(s),x_{k})|^{2}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C(T,f,g)\left(\Delta t^{2}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\right)

\displaystyle+C(T,g)\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\sup_{k=0,\ldots,N}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t_{i},x_{k})-u^{N}(t_{i},x_{k})|^{2}\right]\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t_{m}-s}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,

where we also used the estimate

\int_{0}^{t_{m}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t_{m}-s}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq 2T.

Lastly, we estimate

\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t_{m}-s}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=2\sqrt{t_{m}-t_{i}}-2\sqrt{t_{m}-t_{i+1}}=2\sqrt{t_{m}-t_{i}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{\Delta t}{t_{m}-t_{i}}}\right)\leq\frac{2\Delta t}{\sqrt{t_{m}-t_{i}}},

where we also used that
1-\sqrt{1-z}\leq z
for all
z\in[0,1]
, to arrive at

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|I_{2}\right|^{2}\right]\leq C(T,f,g)\left(\Delta t^{2}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\right)+C(T,g)\Delta t\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t_{m}-t_{i}}}\sup_{k=0,\ldots,N}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t_{i},x_{k})-u^{N}(t_{i},x_{k})|^{2}\right].

Similar arguments for
I_{1}
, yields in total

{\mathbb{E}}\left[|u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t_{m},x_{n})-u^{N}(t_{m},x_{n})|^{2}\right]\leq C(T,f,g)\left(\Delta t^{2}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\right)\\
+C(T,f,g)\Delta t\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t_{m}-t_{i}}}\sup_{k=0,\ldots,N}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t_{i},x_{k})-u^{N}(t_{i},x_{k})|^{2}\right]

and an application of a Grönwall’s inequality yields

\sup_{n=0,\ldots,N}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t_{m},x_{n})-u^{N}(t_{m},x_{n})|^{2}\right]\leq C(T,f,g)\left(\Delta t^{2}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\right).

Taking the supremum over
m=0,\ldots,M
yields the desired result

\sup_{m=0,\ldots,M}\sup_{n=0,\ldots,N}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[|u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t_{m},x_{n})-u^{N}(t_{m},x_{n})|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}\leq C(T,f,g)\sqrt{\Delta t^{2}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}}.

∎

We are now in a position to prove the main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
9
.

We may, without loss of generality, assume that
\widetilde{W}
is the driving noise for
u
. We use Jensen’s inequality and Proposition
10
to obtain the first statement

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[F\left(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t_{m},x_{n})\right)\right]-{\mathbb{E}}\left[F\left(u(t_{m},x_{n})\right)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|F\left(u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t_{m},x_{n})\right)-F\left(u(t_{m},x_{n})\right)\right|^{2}\right]\right|^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C(F)\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|u^{\textrm{LTE}}(t_{m},x_{n})-u(t_{m},x_{n})\right|^{2}\right]\right|^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C(F,T,f,g)\left(\Delta x^{1/2}+\sqrt{\Delta t^{2}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}}\right),

where we also used the triangle inequality and the strong convergence estimate in (
15
). For the second statement we note that if
\Delta t=\Delta x^{2}
then
\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}=\sqrt{\Delta t}
.
∎

A.1.
About the spatial discretisation

Proof of Proposition
4
.

It suffices to show

{\mathbb{P}}\left(u^{N}_{n}(t)\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D},\ \forall t\in[0,T],\ \forall n=0,\ldots,N\right)=1,

since
x\mapsto u^{N}(t,x)
is piecewise linear and satisfies
u^{N}(t,x_{n})=u^{N}_{n}(t)
for
n=0,\ldots,N
.

The argument is based on extending and truncating the coefficient functions
f
and
g
as follows. Since
f(a)=f(b)=g(a)=g(b)=0
, we can extend
f
and
g
such that, for some
\epsilon>0
,
f\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon})
and
g\in\mathcal{C}^{3}(\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon})
where
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}=[l-\epsilon,r+\epsilon]
. Let
L\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large and define
f_{L}
and
g_{L}
as follows

(i)

f_{L}(r)=f(r)
and
g_{L}(r)=g(r)
for every
r\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{1/L}
.

(ii)

f_{L}(r)=0
and
g_{L}(r)=0
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{2/L}
.

(iii)

f_{L}
and
g_{L}
are linear on
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{2/L}\setminus\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{1/L}
.

Note that we take
L\in\mathbb{N}
large enough so that
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{1/L}\subset\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{\epsilon}
. Then,
f_{L}
and
g_{L}
are, for every
L\in\mathbb{N}
, globally Lipschitz continuous functions that coincide with
f
and
g
, respectively, on
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{1/L}
and are identically zero outside
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{2/L}
. We now consider the solution

of the following system of SDEs

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u^{N}_{L}(t)=\left(N^{2}D^{N}u^{N}_{L}(t)+f_{L}(u^{N}_{L}(t))\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{N}g_{L}(u^{N}_{L}(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{N}(t)

(44)

with initial vector
u^{N}_{L}(0)=u^{N}(0)
and with globally Lipschitz continuous coefficient functions. We next show that
{\mathbb{P}}\left(u^{N}_{L}\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}\right)=1
for every
L\in\mathbb{N}
. Let us denote by
\hat{u}^{\textrm{LTE}}_{L}
the

3.1

) with exact samples instead of exact simulations to solve the diagonal system of SDEs in (

. Note that, contrary to argument presented in the proof of Proposition

, there is no need to assume that
{\mathbb{P}}(u^{N}_{L}\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D})=1
, as the coefficient functions are now globally Lipschitz continuous for the truncated system of SDEs in (
44
). Since strong convergence implies almost sure convergence along a subsequence, we conclude that
{\mathbb{P}}(u^{N}_{L}\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D})=1
. Then, as modifying

and
g_{L}

\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}

, it follows that

. We conclude that

.

(i)

f_{L}(r)=f(r)
and
g_{L}(r)=g(r)
for every

.

(ii)

f_{L}(r)=0
and
g_{L}(r)=0
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{2/L}
.

(iii)

f_{L}
and
g_{L}
are linear on
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{2/L}\setminus\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{1/L}
.

Then,
f_{L}
and

are, for every

, globally Lipschitz continuous functions that coincide with
f
and
g
, respectively, on
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{1/L}
and are identically zero outside
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}_{2/L}
. We consider the following truncated SPDE

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{L}(t)=\left(\Delta u(t)+f_{L}(u_{L}(t))\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+g_{L}(u_{L}(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W(t)

with initial state
u_{L}(0)=u_{0}
and with globally Lipschitz continuous coefficient functions
f_{L}
and
g_{L}
. Existence and uniqueness of a continuous mild solution
u_{L}
to the truncated SPDE (
45
) follows from standard SPDE theory, since
f_{L}
and
g_{L}
are globally Lipschitz continuous.

We start with
(1)
. We first show that
{\mathbb{P}}(u_{L}\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D})=1
, for every
L\in\mathbb{N}
. It then follows that
u_{L}
solves the original SPDE in (
1
), as modifying
f_{L}
and
g_{L}
outside
\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}
does not affect the solution
u_{L}
, and establishes existence of a continuous mild solution to the original SPDE in (
1
). To this end, let us denote by

the solution of the following system of SDEs

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u^{N}_{L}(t)=\left(N^{2}D^{N}u^{N}_{L}(t)+f_{L}(u^{N}_{L}(t))\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{N}g_{L}(u^{N}_{L}(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{N}(t).

Then, as was shown in the proof of Propisition
4
,
{\mathbb{P}}\left(u^{N}_{L}\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}\right)=1
and Theorem
3.1
in
[
13
]
implies that
u^{N}_{L}\to u_{L}
strongly as
\Delta t\to 0
. Since strong convergence implies almost sure convergence along a subsequence, we conclude that
{\mathbb{P}}(u_{L}\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D})=1
. This, in turn, implies
(1)
.

We next prove
(2)
that any continuous mild solution
u
to (
1
) must satisfy
{\mathbb{P}}(u\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D})=1
. To this end, suppose that
u
is a continuous mild solution to (
1
). We define

\tau_{k}=\inf\{t>0:\ \exists x\in[0,1]\text{ such that }u(t,x)\not\in[l-1/k,l+1/k]\},

for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and we let

\tau=\inf\{t>0:\ \exists x\in[0,1]\text{ such that }u(t,x)\not\in[a,b]\}=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\tau_{k}.

We now claim that
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{k}=\infty)=1
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Observe that this would imply that
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau=\infty)=1
, and hence that
{\mathbb{P}}(u\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D})=1
. We prove the claim by contradiction; that is, suppose that
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{k}<\infty)=\epsilon>0
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
u_{k}
be the solution to the truncated SPDE in (
45
) with
L=k
and let
\omega\in\Omega
be a sample point such
\tau_{k}(\omega)<\infty
. We add
\omega
to indicate the chosen sample point
\omega\in\Omega
. Recall that
u_{k}\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D}
with probability
1
(see proof of Proposition
4
) and that
(t,x)\mapsto u(t,x)
is (jointly) continuous with probability
1
by assumption. Then, by definition of
\tau_{k}
, we have that

u(\omega;t,x)\in[l-1/k,r+1/k],

for every
t<\tau_{k}(\omega)
and for every
x\in[0,1]
. Then, by uniqueness for SPDEs with globally Lipschitz continuous coefficients, we have that

u(\omega;t,x)=u_{k}(\omega;t,x),

for
t<\tau_{k}(\omega)
and for
x\in[0,1]
. But then, since
{\mathbb{P}}(u_{k}\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D})=1
, we obtain that

u(\omega;t,x)\in[a,b]

for
t<\tau_{k}(\omega)
and for

. By continuity of the sample paths of

, we deduce that

u(\omega;t,x)\in[a,b]

for

and for

. This is a contradiction to the definition of

{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{k}<\infty)=0

that
{\mathbb{P}}(u\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D})=1
.

Lastly, let

and

be continuous mild solutions of the SPDE in (

,
{\mathbb{P}}(u\in\operatorname{cl}\mathcal{D})=1
and

. Therefore,

and

both solves the SPDE with globally Lipschitz continuous coefficients in (

\Phi(r)=\int_{x_{0}}^{r}\frac{1}{1-w^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w=\frac{1}{2}\left(\log(1+r)-\log(1-r)-\log(1+x_{0})+\log(1-x_{0})\right),\ r\in\mathcal{D},

We compute

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\Phi^{-1}(r)=\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)g\left(\Phi^{-1}(r)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)\frac{4(1-x_{0})(1+x_{0})e^{2r}}{\left((1+x_{0})e^{2r}+(1-x_{0})\right)^{2}},\ r\in\mathbb{R},

and we recall that
\phi
is defined to be

\phi(r)=\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{\prime}(r)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha(r)^{2}-k_{1},\ r\in\mathbb{R}.

In this case,
k_{1}=0
and

k_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)^{2},

since the above allows us to bound

0\leq\alpha^{\prime}(r)\leq 1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}},\ 0\leq\alpha^{2}(r)\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)^{2},

(49)

both for every

. The first estimate in (

0\leq\inf_{w\in[-1,1]}g(w)\leq g\left(\Phi^{-1}(r)\right)\leq\sup_{w\in[-1,1]}g(w),

for every
r\in\mathbb{R}
, and the second estimate in (
49
) follows from the property that
\Phi^{-1}(r)\in[-1,1]
for every
r\in\mathbb{R}
. We next compute

in (

A(r)=\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)\int_{0}^{r}\Phi^{-1}(w)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w=\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)\left(\log\left((1+x_{0})e^{2r}+(1-x_{0})\right)-r-\log(2)\right)

and use that the target density

in (

h(r)\propto\exp\left(A(r)-\frac{r^{2}}{2T_{\lambda}}\right)\propto\left((1+x_{0})e^{r}+(1-x_{0})e^{-r}\right)^{1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2T_{\lambda}}}.

(50)

As we cannot directly sample from
h
, we use an accept-reject strategy to sample for
h
. Keeping in mind that we let
\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\to 0
, we use the proposal density
H_{\nu}
given by

H_{\nu}(r)\propto\left((1+x_{0})e^{r}+(1-x_{0})e^{-r}\right)e^{-\frac{\nu r^{2}}{2T_{\lambda}}},

for some
\nu>0
to control the tails of
H_{\nu}
in relation to
h
. Owing to the factors
e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2T_{\lambda}}}
in
h
and
e^{-\frac{\nu r^{2}}{2T_{\lambda}}}
in

,
h
and
H_{\nu}
are probability densities and there exists normalising constants
c_{h}
and
c_{H_{\nu}}
such that

h(r)=\frac{1}{c_{h}}\left((1+x_{0})e^{r}+(1-x_{0})e^{-r}\right)^{1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2T_{\lambda}}},\ r\in\mathbb{R},

and

H_{\nu}(r)=\frac{1}{c_{H_{\nu}}}\left((1+x_{0})e^{r}+(1-x_{0})e^{-r}\right)e^{-\frac{\nu r^{2}}{2T_{\lambda}}},\ r\in\mathbb{R}.

Moreover, the constant
c_{H_{\nu}}
can be computed explicitly

c_{H_{\nu}}=2\sqrt{2\pi T_{\lambda}/\nu}e^{\frac{T_{\lambda}}{2\nu}},

using completion of squares. We next describe how to sample from
H_{\nu}
. Let us decompose
H_{\nu}
into a sum of two densities
H_{\nu}=\frac{1+x_{0}}{2}H_{\nu}^{1}+\frac{1-x_{0}}{2}H_{\nu}^{2}
with

H_{\nu}^{1}(r)=\frac{1}{c_{H_{\nu}^{1}}}e^{r}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2T_{\lambda}/\nu}}=\frac{1}{c_{H_{\nu}^{1}}}e^{\frac{T_{\lambda}}{2\nu}}e^{-\frac{1}{2T_{\lambda}/\nu}\left(r-\frac{T_{\lambda}}{\nu}\right)^{2}},\ r\in\mathbb{R},

and

H_{\nu}^{2}(r)=\frac{1}{c_{H_{\nu}^{2}}}e^{-r}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2T_{\lambda}/\nu}}=\frac{1}{c_{H_{\nu}^{2}}}e^{\frac{T_{\lambda}}{2\nu}}e^{-\frac{1}{2T_{\lambda}/\nu}\left(r+\frac{T_{\lambda}}{\nu}\right)^{2}},\ r\in\mathbb{R},

where

c_{H_{\nu}^{1}}=c_{H_{\nu}^{2}}=\sqrt{2\pi T_{\lambda}/\nu}e^{\frac{T_{\lambda}}{2\nu}}.

In other words,
H_{\nu}^{1}
is a normal distribution with mean
\mu=\frac{T_{\lambda}}{\nu}
and variance
\sigma^{2}=T_{\lambda}/\nu
and
H_{\nu}^{2}
is a normal distribution with mean
\mu=-\frac{T_{\lambda}}{\nu}
and variance
\sigma^{2}=T_{\lambda}/\nu
. Thus, we can sample from
H_{\nu}
by sampling from
H_{\nu}^{1}
with probability
\frac{1+x_{0}}{2}
and sampling from
H_{\nu}^{2}
with probability
\frac{1-x_{0}}{2}
. The next step is to compute an accept-reject sampling constant
K
(uniform bound) such that
\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{h(r)}{H_{\nu}(r)}\leq K
. Observe that this is one reason we introduce the parameter
\nu
to control the tails of
H_{\nu}
. We start with expressing

\frac{h(r)}{H_{\nu}(r)}=\frac{c_{H_{\nu}}}{c_{h}}\left(\widetilde{q}(r)\right)^{\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}},\ r\in\mathbb{R},

where

\widetilde{q}(r)=(1+x_{0})e^{r}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)r^{2}}{2T_{\lambda}}}+(1-x_{0})e^{-r}e^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)r^{2}}{2T_{\lambda}}}=(1+x_{0})\widetilde{q}_{1}(r)+(1-x_{0})\widetilde{q}_{2}(r),

for
r\in\mathbb{R}
. With the goal of computing the uniform bound
K
, we re-write and bound
\widetilde{q}_{1}
as

\widetilde{q}_{1}(r)=e^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}{2T_{\lambda}}\left(r-\frac{T_{\lambda}}{\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}\right)^{2}}e^{\frac{(1-\nu)T_{\lambda}}{2\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)^{2}}}\leq e^{\frac{T_{\lambda}}{2\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}},\ r\in\mathbb{R},

and, similarly, we can re-write and bound
\widetilde{q}_{2}
as

\widetilde{q}_{2}(r)=e^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}{2T_{\lambda}}\left(r+\frac{T_{\lambda}}{\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}\right)^{2}}e^{\frac{(1-\nu)T_{\lambda}}{2\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)^{2}}}\leq e^{\frac{T_{\lambda}}{2\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}},\ r\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus, we can estimate

\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{q}(r)\leq 2e^{\frac{T}{2(1-\nu)}}

and therefore also

\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{h(r)}{H_{\nu}(r)}\leq\frac{c_{H_{\nu}}}{c_{h}}2^{\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}}e^{\frac{T}{2\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}}.

To bound the quotient of the normalising constants
\frac{c_{H_{\nu}}}{c_{h}}
, we note that
h\to H_{\nu=1}
as
\lambda\to\infty
and we can therefore use the estimate

c_{h}\geq\frac{1}{2}c_{H_{\nu=1}}=\sqrt{2\pi T_{\lambda}}e^{\frac{T_{\lambda}}{2}},

which implies that

\frac{c_{H_{\nu}}}{c_{h}}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu}}e^{\frac{(1-\nu)T_{\lambda}}{2\nu}}.

We hence arrive at the following equality for an accept-reject sampling constant
K

\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{h(r)}{H_{\nu}(r)}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu}}e^{\frac{(1-\nu)T_{\lambda}}{2\nu}}2^{\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}}e^{\frac{T}{2\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}}=K.

\alpha^{\prime}

\alpha^{\prime}(r)=\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\Phi^{-1}(r)=\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)g\left(\Phi^{-1}(r)\right)=\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)\frac{4(1-x_{0})(1+x_{0})e^{2r}}{\left((1+x_{0})e^{2r}+(1-x_{0})\right)^{2}},

for
r\in\mathbb{R}
, where we used (
6
), and for
\phi

\phi(r)=\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{\prime}(r)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha(r)^{2}-k_{1},\ r\in\mathbb{R}.

In this case, we have that
k_{1}=0
and that

k_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\max\left(\left(1+\frac{2-2\gamma}{\lambda^{2}}\right)^{2},\left(1+\frac{2\gamma}{\lambda^{2}}\right)^{2}\right).

This follows from the estimates

0=\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)\min_{w\in[-1,1]}g(w)\leq\alpha^{\prime}(r)\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)\max_{w\in[-1,1]}g(w)=1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}},

for every
r\in\mathbb{R}
, and

0\leq\alpha^{2}(r)\leq\max\left(\left(1+\frac{2-2\gamma}{\lambda^{2}}\right)^{2},\left(1+\frac{2\gamma}{\lambda^{2}}\right)^{2}\right),

for every
r\in\mathbb{R}
. We next compute
A
in (
21
)

\displaystyle A(r)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)\int_{0}^{r}\Phi^{-1}(w)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w+\frac{r}{\lambda^{2}}(1-2\gamma)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right)\left(\log\left((1+x_{0})e^{2r}+(1-x_{0})\right)-r-\log(2)\right)+\frac{r}{\lambda^{2}}(1-2\gamma),

for
r\in\mathbb{R}
, and then use that the target density
h
in (
20

h(r)\propto\exp\left(A(r)-\frac{r^{2}}{2T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}\right)\propto\left((1+x_{0})e^{r}+(1-x_{0})e^{-r}\right)^{1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}}e^{\frac{r}{\lambda^{2}}(1-2\gamma)},

for
r\in\mathbb{R}
, where we recall that
T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}=\widetilde{\lambda}^{2}T
. As we cannot directly sample from
h
, we use an accept-reject strategy to sample for
h
. As we will let
\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\to 0
, we use the proposal density
H_{\nu}
given by

H_{\nu}(r)\propto\left((1+x_{0})e^{r}+(1-x_{0})e^{-r}\right)e^{-\frac{\nu r^{2}}{2T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}}e^{\frac{r}{\lambda^{2}}(1-2\gamma)},

for some
\nu>0
to control the tails of
H_{\nu}
in relation to the tails of
h
. Owing to the factors
e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}}
in

and
e^{-\frac{\nu r^{2}}{2T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}}
in

,
h
and
H_{\nu}
are probability densities and there exists normalising constants
c_{h}
and

such that

and

H_{\nu}(r)=\frac{1}{c_{H_{\nu}}}\left((1+x_{0})e^{r}+(1-x_{0})e^{-r}\right)e^{-\frac{\nu r^{2}}{2T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}}e^{\frac{r}{\lambda^{2}}(1-2\gamma)},\ r\in\mathbb{R},

Moreover, the constant
c_{H_{\nu}}
can be computed explicitly

c_{H_{\nu}}=(1+x_{0})c_{H_{\nu}^{1}}+(1-x_{0})c_{H_{\nu}^{2}},

where

c_{H_{\nu}^{1}}=\sqrt{\frac{T_{\lambda}\pi}{2\nu}}e^{\frac{T_{\lambda}}{8\nu}(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}(1-2\gamma))^{2}}

and

c_{H_{\nu}^{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{T_{\lambda}\pi}{2\nu}}e^{\frac{T_{\lambda}}{8\nu}(1-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}(1-2\gamma))^{2}}.

Note that
c_{H_{\nu}^{1}}
and
c_{H_{\nu}^{2}}
are the normalising constants for
H_{\nu}^{1}
and
H_{\nu}^{2}
, respectively, defined below. The next issue is to sample from
H_{\nu}
, which we do as follows. Let us decompose
H_{\nu}
into a sum of two densities
H_{\nu}=\frac{1+x_{0}}{2}H_{\nu}^{1}+\frac{1-x_{0}}{2}H_{\nu}^{2}
with

H_{\nu}^{1}(r)=\frac{1}{c_{H_{\nu}^{1}}}e^{r}e^{-\frac{\nu x^{2}}{2T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}}e^{\frac{r}{\lambda^{2}}(1-2\gamma)}=\frac{1}{c_{H_{\nu}^{1}}}e^{\frac{T_{\lambda}}{8\nu}\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\left(1-2\gamma\right)\right)^{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{T_{\lambda}/(2\nu)}\left(r-\frac{T_{\lambda}}{4\nu}\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}(1-2\gamma)\right)\right)^{2}},\ r\in\mathbb{R},

and

H_{\nu}^{2}(r)=\frac{1}{c_{H_{\nu}^{2}}}e^{-r}e^{-\frac{\nu r^{2}}{2T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}}e^{\frac{r}{\lambda^{2}}(1-2\gamma)}=\frac{1}{c_{H_{\nu}^{2}}}e^{\frac{T_{\lambda}}{8\nu}\left(1-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\left(1-2\gamma\right)\right)^{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{T_{\lambda}/(2\nu)}\left(r+\frac{T_{\lambda}}{4\nu}\left(1-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}(1-2\gamma)\right)\right)^{2}},\ r\in\mathbb{R}.

In other words,
H_{\nu}^{1}
is a normal distribution with mean
\mu=\frac{T_{\lambda}}{4\nu}\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}(1-2\gamma)\right)
and variance
\sigma^{2}=T_{\lambda}/(4\nu)
and
H_{\nu}^{2}
is a normal distribution with mean
\mu=-\frac{T_{\lambda}}{4\nu}\left(1-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}(1-2\gamma)\right)
and variance
\sigma^{2}=T_{\lambda}/(4\nu)
. Thus, we can sample from
H_{\nu}
by sampling from
H_{\nu}^{1}
with probability
\frac{1+x_{0}}{2}
and sampling from
H_{\nu}^{2}
with probability
\frac{1-x_{0}}{2}
. We next compute an accept-reject sampling constant
K
(uniform bound) such that
\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{h(r)}{H_{\nu}(r)}\leq K
. Note that this is one reason why we introduced the parameter
\nu>0
to control the tails of
H_{\nu}
. We start with expressing

\frac{h(r)}{H_{\nu}(r)}=\frac{c_{H_{\nu}}}{c_{h}}\left(\widetilde{q}(r)\right)^{\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}},\ r\in\mathbb{R},

where

\widetilde{q}(r)=(1+x_{0})e^{r}e^{-\frac{2(1-\nu)r^{2}}{T}}+(1-x_{0})e^{-r}e^{-\frac{2(1-\nu)r^{2}}{T}}=(1+x_{0})\widetilde{q}_{1}(r)+(1-x_{0})\widetilde{q}_{2}(r),\ r\in\mathbb{R},

We can re-write and bound
\widetilde{q}_{1}
as

\widetilde{q}_{1}(r)=e^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}{2T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}\left(r-\frac{T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}{\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}\right)^{2}}e^{\frac{(1-\nu)T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}{2\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)^{2}}}\leq e^{\frac{T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}{2\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}},\ r\in\mathbb{R},

and, similarly, we can re-write and bound
\widetilde{q}_{2}
as

\widetilde{q}_{2}(r)=e^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}{2T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}\left(r+\frac{T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}{\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}\right)^{2}}e^{\frac{(1-\nu)T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}{2\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)^{2}}}\leq e^{\frac{T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}{2\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}},\ r\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus,

\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{q}(r)\leq 2e^{\frac{T_{\widetilde{\lambda}}}{2\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}}=2e^{\frac{T}{8(1-\nu)}}

and therefore also

\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{h(r)}{H_{\nu}(r)}\leq\frac{c_{H_{\nu}}}{c_{h}}2^{\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}}e^{\frac{T}{8\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}}.

To bound the quotient of the constants
\frac{c_{H_{\nu}}}{c_{h}}
, we note that
h\to H_{\nu=1}
as
\lambda\to\infty
and we can estimate

c_{h}\geq\frac{1}{2}c_{H_{\nu=1}}=\frac{1+x_{0}}{2}c_{H_{\nu=1}^{1}}+\frac{1-x_{0}}{2}c_{H_{\nu=1}^{2}},

which, in turn, implies that

\frac{c_{H_{\nu}}}{c_{h}}\leq 2\frac{c_{H_{\nu}}}{c_{H_{\nu=1}}}.

Thus, finally, we arrive at

\sup_{r\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{h(r)}{H_{\nu}(r)}\leq\frac{c_{H_{\nu}}}{c_{H_{\nu=1}}}2^{1+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}}e^{\frac{T}{8\lambda^{2}(1-\nu)}}=K

and

\frac{c_{H_{\nu}}}{c_{H_{\nu=1}}}=\frac{(1+x_{0})c_{H_{\nu}^{1}}+(1-x_{0})c_{H_{\nu}^{2}}}{(1+x_{0})c_{H_{\nu=1}^{1}}+(1-x_{0})c_{H_{\nu=1}^{2}}}
