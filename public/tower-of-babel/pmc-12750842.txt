Effects of feeding infant formula rich in sn-2 palmitate for 6 months on fecal saponified fatty acids, calcium and stool characteristics: a cluster-randomized controlled trial
Authors: Qianqian Shen, Wei Wu, Ai Zhao, Sha Luo, Yanjie Hao, Hua Jiang, Tingchao He, Biao Liu, Wenhui Ye, Yumei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman milk palmitic acid (PA) is mainly esterified at the sn-2 position of triacylglycerols, while infant formula contains palmitate predominantly in the sn-1/3 positions. Current evidence on long-term health effects of increasing sn-2 palmitate in formula remains insufficient. This study investigated the effects of high sn-2 PA formula (> 40%) on fecal saponified fatty acid, calcium, magnesium and stool characteristics in healthy full-term infants.MethodsIn this cluster-randomized controlled trial, healthy infants < 14 d were assigned to breastfeeding (BF group, n = 66), high sn-2 palmitate formula (sn-2 group, n = 66, 46.3% sn-2 PA) or low sn-2 palmitate formula (control group, n = 67, 10.3% sn-2 PA). Infant demographics, feeding status, stool characteristics, physical exams, and stool samples were collected at 6, 16, and 24 weeks. Per-protocol analysis was used.ResultsThe sn-2 group exhibited a significant time-dependent decline in fecal saponified PA and calcium over time (Ph for Trend < 0.001). The BF group declined faster than the sn-2 group (Padjusted for Group*Time < 0.001). Fecal saponified PA proportion in sn-2 group was significantly lower than controls at all timepoints. At week 24, fecal calcium was lower in the sn-2 group vs. control (0.9 vs. 1.3 mg/g, P = 0.010). No significant difference was found in stool frequency, consistency or size between sn-2 and control groups at any point.ConclusionInfant formula enriched with > 40% sn-2 palmitate reduces fecal fatty acid and calcium excretion, supports efficient lipid and calcium absorption, shows a fecal magnesium pattern similar to breastfed infants, but does not alter stool characteristics relative to the control formula.Trial registrationThe trial is registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry: ChiCTR1800014479; 30/Jan./2018.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-025-01248-9.

Background

Human milk (HM) is recognized as the optimal source of nutrition for infants, providing both immediate and long-term benefits for growth and health [1]. Lipids, comprising 3% to 5% of HM, supply more than 50% of the energy intake, essential fatty acids (EFAs), and fat-soluble vitamins needed by infants [2].

Approximately 95% of these lipids are triacylglycerols (TAGs), consisting of three fatty acids (FAs) esterified to a glycerol backbone. Over 200 different FAs have been identified in HM [3], each esterified to one of the three stereospecific positions (sn) on TAGs, with unique distribution patterns. Previous studies have demonstrated that the composition of FAs and their positional distribution on TAG structures influence FA absorption and, consequently, the physiological response to dietary fat intake [4]. During lipid digestion, pancreatic lipase selectively hydrolyzes FAs at the sn-1 and sn-3 positions of TAG, resulting in the release of two free FAs and the formation of a corresponding sn-2 monoglyceride. This sn-2 monoglyceride can directly enter the intestinal mucosa, and its intestinal absorption and utilization are greatly improved regardless of the structure of its esterified FA [5]. The absorption characteristics of the free FAs are dependent on their structure. Of the free FAs, saturated fatty acids (SFAs), such as palmitic acid (PA, C16:0), are less readily absorbed than unsaturated FAs and can potentially form insoluble fatty acid soaps and calcium soaps with divalent cations (such as calcium), which are difficult to be absorbed by the intestine, resulting in in the loss of both calcium and energy in the feces [6–8].

PA is the predominant SFA in HM, accounting for roughly 25% of total FAs [2]. Importantly, about 70% of PA in HM is esterified at the sn-2 position [5, 9, 10]. This structural feature enhances the absorption of PA, total fat, and calcium in infants, and contributes to softer stool consistency [5, 11]. In contrast, most infant formulas are base on vegetable oils, which typically contain a much lower proportion of sn-2 PA (approximately 10%) [12]. As a result, their intestinal absorption is significantly lower than that of HM [5, 13]. Consequently, hydrolysis of infant formula triglycerides generates more free PA, which readily binds calcium to form insoluble PA soaps [14]. Several studies have demonstrated that increasing the proportion of sn-2 PA in infant formula reduces fecal excretion of both total and saponified PA in formula-fed infants [15, 16]. Bar-Yoseph et al. applied a modified Cohen utility model and found that having more than 40% of PA esterified at the sn-2 position facilitates the absorption of FAs and calcium [6, 17]. However, most existing studies were conducted in Western populations and often involved short intervention durations or limited outcome indicators. To date, high-quality evidence regarding the effects of infant formula containing more than 40% PA at the sn-2 position on the absorption of FA, calcium and magnesium, as well as its impact on infant stool characteristics, remains limited, particularly in China. Given potential differences in genetics, diet, and other factors between Asian and Western infants, locally generated evidence is needed to guide infant formula innovation and nutritional policy.

Therefore, given the observed benefits of the high proportion of sn-2 PA in HM, this cluster-randomized clinical trial aimed to evaluate whether long-term feeding of the high sn-2 PA infant formula (above 40%) improves fatty acid, calcium, and magnesium absorption, and contributes to better stool characteristics in infants compared to standard formula.

Methods

We conducted a multicenter, cluster-randomized, parallel-controlled trial to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of the high sn-2 palmitate formula on infant health outcomes, including nutrient absorption and stool characteristics. This study was carried out in four obstetric practices across three provinces in China (Hebei [two practices], Henan, and Shandong Province). Healthy, term formula-fed infants were randomized to receive one of two study formulas for 24 weeks. In addition, a nonrandomized breastfed group (BF group) was enrolled as a parallel reference. Clinic visits were scheduled at baseline, weeks 6, 16 and 24 for questionnaires data (e.g., infant demographics, feeding status, stool consistency, etc.), physical examination and infant stool samples. Baseline examinations began in December 2017, and all follow-up visits were completed by July 2019. All examinations were performed by the same study examiners using the same protocol and calibrated equipment, and study examiners were uniformly trained qualified obstetrics or pediatric care doctors and nurses.

The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Research Board of Peking University (No. IRB00001052-17081) and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (No. ChiCTR1800014479). This trial was conducted according to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Before participation, written informed consent was obtained from the infant’s parents or guardians. We report it according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guidelines.

Participants and randomization

Healthy full-term singleton neonates (gestational age 37–41 weeks) meeting the following criteria were enrolled: postnatal age < 14 days and birth weight between 2500 and 4000 g. Of these, infants whose mothers intended to exclusively breastfeed for more than four months were assigned to the BF group. Formula-fed infants, whose mothers had decided to forgo breastfeeding, were randomized to receive either the intervention or the control formula. Exclusion criteria for infants were [18]: (1) congenital or genetic diseases; (2) receiving antibiotic therapy or requiring hospitalization for longer than 7 days; (3) having a familial history of atopy and metabolic or chronic diseases. Participants were further excluded from final analysis if they: (1) additionally received antibiotics, prebiotics, or probiotics at any time and (2) failed to comply with the study protocol throughout the study period, such as receiving breast milk or other commercial formula in the formula groups, switching to mixed feeding in the breastfed group, or introducing complementary foods in any group.

Cluster randomization was performed by an independent statistician not involved in the study. Each hospital represented a unique cluster, with all 4 clusters equally allocated to the 2 study intervention arms (intervention or control group) in a 1:1 ratio. Infants enrolled at each hospital received the intervention corresponding to the hospital’s assigned group. The infants’ families, the clinical staff, and the investigators were aware of trial-group assignments, but outcome assessors and the statistician were kept blinded until the data were thoroughly analyzed.

Interventions

Infants in the BF group were fed breast milk exclusively for more than 4 months after birth. Formula-fed infants received a study formula for all feedings from randomization to 24 weeks of age. The intervention group (sn-2 PA group) received a modified infant formula containing 46.3% sn-2 esterified palmitic acid in total triglycerides, while the low sn-2 PA group (control group) received a standard formula with 10.3% sn-2 palmitate content in total triglycerides. The content of the sn-2 PA level in the high sn-2 palmitate infant formula was increased by adding refined vegetable fat, Betapol B-55 DMX (1,3-Dioleoyl-2-Palmitoy-TAG), produced by Bunge Loders Croklaan Oils (Pasir Gudang, Malaysia). All other nutrients were generally similar between the two groups (see Table 1). Both infant formulas met national food safety standards and adhered to dietary nutrition recommendations for infants. The two formulas, provided in dehydrated powder form to parents or caregivers (hereinafter referred to as parents), were reconstituted with warm water in infant bottles according to the preparation instructions on the packaging. Additionally, they were supplied in similar packaging. Parents were educated do not introduce complementary food before the age of 4 months, regardless of the study group.

Outcomes

Follow-up surveys and/or infant feces collection were performed at 4, 6, 8, 16, and 24 weeks after birth, and infant compliance with the study product feeding was assessed.

The primary outcome was the nutrients absorption. Specifically, the absorption of dietary fat and various fatty acids was reflected by measuring total fatty acids and saponified fatty acids in feces, and the absorption of calcium and magnesium was evaluated by detecting calcium and magnesium contents in feces. Stool samples were collected directly from the diaper into sterile tubes at home by parents the day before each follow-up visit. Parents stored the stool samples at −20 °C in the freezer before transferring them to a local medical laboratory within 24 h. Subsequently, all samples were then transferred under cold chain conditions to the central laboratory at Peking University and stored at −80 °C for later analysis. Briefly, saponified fatty acids in fecal samples were quantified via ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) [19]. Fecal calcium and magnesium concentrations were measured using microplate assay kits (JK-ZCA-1-W, JK-XM-1-W; 100 tubes/96 samples) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Secondary outcomes included infant growth and stool characteristics. Anthropometric measurements - weight, length, and head circumference - were obtained at baseline, week 16, and week 24. Infants were weighed without clothing or diapers on a calibrated digital scale to the nearest 10 g. Recumbent length was measured using a pediatric length board to the nearest 0.1 cm. Head circumference was measured thrice using a head circumference baby band to the nearest 0.1 cm. All measures were performed twice at each visit, and the mean value was calculated for analysis. Parents reported stool characteristics using the Amsterdam Infant Stool Scale and self-designed questionnaire [20] in the 3-day diary before each visit. Digestive tolerance and related behaviors were monitored throughout the study. A subset of symptoms relating to the digestive system was identified as of particular interest a priori: difficulty having a bowel movement, regurgitation, vomiting, bowel cramps, abdominal distension, acute diarrhea, and chronic diarrhea. The severity of symptoms and whether they were related to the study feedings were determined by the investigators. To ensure consistency in diagnosis, investigators were provided with standard definitions of these symptoms.

Adverse events (AEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs) were recorded throughout the study and monitored by an independent pediatrician. No code-break requests occurred for AEs or SAEs throughout the study and de-blinding did not need to occur.

Sample size

The stool saponified FA levels were used as the key outcome and for sample size calculation [21]. Assuming a multisite study and 20% dropout overhead correction, a sample size of 55 per group was determined to have 90% power to detect a difference with a 0.05 two-sided significance level. A block randomization procedure with blocks of 4 was used at all sites.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted according to an a priori written statistical analysis plan using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Two-sided P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. Continuous variables are presented as means (standard deviations, SDs) or medians (25th and 75th percentiles), and categorical variables as counts (percentages). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check the normality of the distribution of continuous data. A linear mixed effects model was used to examine whether continuous variables had a linear temporal trend, and to compare the differences between groups in temporal trends, and to include confounding variables in the model for adjustment. Continuous variables that do not satisfy the normal distribution are normalized using the natural logarithmic transformation method and then analyzed. Compliance was evaluated by counting the empty packs at each visit. All analyses were carried out according to the per protocol (PP) principle.

Results

Study population

Of 233 infant volunteers, 24 were excluded according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the remaining 199 were enrolled in the RCT: 66 in the sn-2 group, 67 in the control group and 66 in the breastfed reference group (BF group). Additionally, 18 voluntarily withdrew or were lost to follow-up. A total of 58 infants (87.9%) in the sn-2 group, 59 infants (88.1%) in the control group and 57 infants (86.4%) in the BF group completed follow-up at age 24 weeks and were included in the analysis. The follow-up rate for all infants was 87.4% (Fig. 1). Table 2 reports the baseline characteristics of all enrolled participants. Stool characteristics

No significant differences in defecation frequency distributions were observed between the sn-2 and control groups at weeks 6, 16, or 24. However, at week 6, infants in the sn-2 group exhibited a lower defecation frequency than BF infants, with 50.9% defecating 1–2 times/day compared to 49.1% of BF infants defecating 3–4 times/day (P < 0.05). By weeks 16 and 24, the defecation frequency between the sn-2 and BF groups were comparable, and the earlier difference was no longer apparent. No significant differences in stool consistency were observed between the sn-2 group and control group at any time point (weeks 6, 16, or 24). However, at weeks 6 and 16, the proportion of soft stools was 62.1% in the sn-2 group and 79.0% in the BF group, with a statistically significant difference in stool consistency distribution between the two groups. By week 24, this difference was no longer statistically significant. Stool size did not differ significantly between the sn-2 and control groups throughout the study. The sn-2 and BF groups showed similar stool size distributions at weeks 6 and 16. However, by week 24, a significant difference emerged: a lower proportion of infants in the sn-2 group passed stools covering more than 25% of the diaper area compared to the BF group (72.4% vs. 91.2%, P< 0.05) (Table S1-S3).

Anthropometric data

As previously reported [18], no significant differences were observed in the infants’ weights, lengths, or head circumferences at the baseline or any time point.

Adverse events (AEs)

Except for the frequencies of abdominal distension, hiccups and milk vomiting, which were not significantly different from those of breastfed infants, no vomiting or gastroesophageal reflux disease were reported in any groups. No serious AEs were observed in any group during the intervention period.

Discussion

In this multicenter RCT, for infants who could not be exclusively breastfed, increasing the proportion of sn-2 palmitic acid in formula supports adequate growth and leads to a time-dependent reduction in fecal saponified palmitic acid and calcium content over the first six months of life compared to standard formula. Notably, the decline in saponified palmitic acid was significantly slower than that observed in the breastfed group, while stool consistency in the sn-2 group was comparable to that in breastfed infants.

The present study demonstrates that infant formula containing more than 40% PA esterified at the sn-2 position effectively reduced the fecal PA levels. This finding aligns with previous reports, despite differences in study designs, infant age groups, and intervention durations. For example, a recent study conducted in Japan showed that high sn-2 PA milk (≥ 50% sn-2 PA esterified at sn-2 position) may reduce fecal PA levels in formula-fed newborns, but this was an observational study [17]. Similarly, a recent single-center RCT conducted in China [16] reported that infants in the high sn-2 PA group had lower fecal excretion of total fatty acids and saponified fatty acids. However, the intervention lasted only 12 weeks. Several studies have consistently shown that high sn-2 PA formula significantly lowers fecal saponified PA and total saponified fatty acid levels compared to low sn-2 PA formula [15, 16, 22–25], however, previous studies have not addressed how elevating the sn-2 palmitate ratio in infant formula influences fecal PA saponification dynamics. Our study demonstrates that increasing sn-2 palmitate in TAGs reduces saponified PA formation in infant feces and enhances PA absorption, with the proportion of saponified PA (relative to total fecal PA) serving as a potentially more sensitive indicator than its absolute value for evaluating fat absorption efficiency. Nevertheless, infants fed high sn-2 palmitate formula exhibited persistently higher fecal saponified PA levels and proportions compared to exclusively breastfed infants, which likely reflects the intrinsic differences in sn-2 palmitate content between formula and human milk. These results underscore the physiological superiority of human milk in fat digestion and absorption. In addition, our findings regarding other saturated fatty acids–such as C12:0 and C14:0–are consistent with earlier studies, showing improved absorption and reduced fecal excretion with high sn-2 PA formula [21, 23, 26]. In contrast, no significant differences were observed between the high sn-2 and control groups in the fecal contents of long-chain unsaturated fatty acids (LCUFAs) such as C18:1 and C18:2. This observation aligns with previous intervention studies conducted in China and Spain [23, 27] which also reported minimal impact of sn-2 PA enrichment on the fecal excretion of LCUFAs, suggesting that the absorption of unsaturated fatty acids is less influenced by TAG stereospecificity. Mechanistically, sn-2 PA metabolites have been shown to stimulate pancreatic secretion and enhance the activity of digestive lipases [28]. For example, carboxyl ester lipase activity has been reported to increase up to 11-fold in response to these metabolites [29]. These enzymes promote the esterification of long-chain fatty acids, thereby reducing their availability for saponification and facilitating absorption. This mechanistic insight may partly explain the reduced levels of unabsorbed and saponified long-chain fatty acids observed in infants receiving high sn-2 PA formula.

Our results do not support a similar intervention conducted in full-term infants enrolled at 25–45 days of age, which found no significant difference in fecal calcium concentration between the high sn-2 PA group and control group [30]. Notably, that study had a relatively short intervention period of only 28 days. In contrast, our findings are in keeping with a randomized, controlled single-center trial in full-term infants enrolled before 14 days of age [16], which demonstrated significantly lower fecal calcium concentrations in the high sn-2 PA group compared to those fed standard formula after 12 weeks of intervention. Additionally, two double-blind randomized cross-over trials also demonstrated [15] that increasing sn-2 PA in infant formula using bovine milk fat significantly reduced fecal excretion of palmitate soaps, total fatty acid soaps, and calcium in healthy, term infants. Our study provides further evidence that increasing the sn-2 PA ratio in infant formula can reduce fecal calcium content and promote calcium absorption. It should be acknowledged that the calcium content in the high sn-2 formula was lower than that in the standard formula, which might have affected the outcomes. Future studies are needed to validate this potential influence. Moreover, our results highlight the importance of evaluating the temporal trend of fecal calcium to assess calcium absorption in infants over time comprehensively. Interestingly, we found for the first time that fecal calcium levels in the high sn-2 PA group were lower than those in the breastfed group at week 6; however, the difference was no longer significant at weeks 16 and 24. This early difference, although transient, may reflect dynamic changes in calcium absorption and warrants further investigation in future studies. In contrast to calcium, magnesium excretion showed no significant reduction and no improvement in absorption in infants receiving high sn-2 PA formula. This result aligns with prior reports [27, 31], suggesting that a competitive interaction during intestinal absorption may exist between these two minerals.

Several studies on the effect of high sn-2 PA in infant formula on stool consistency or frequency have been published. Consistent with previous findings [30, 32], our results don’t demonstrate that infants fed more than 40% sn-2 palmitate formula had softer stools than controls. Interestingly, in a large multicenter RCT, formula enriched in sn-2 palmitate fed improve stool consistency from 2 weeks to 2 months of age, however, by 3 and 4 months, stool consistency scores no longer differed significantly [33]. It is important to note that different stool assessment scales were used across studies to evaluate the effects of varying sn-2 PA content on stool consistency. Yet, previous study conducted in the Philippines has also shown that, after 8 weeks intervention, infants in the sn-2 group had a higher proportion of soft stools compared to those in the control group, with 20% more mushy/soft stools and 50% fewer formed stools [26]. Formula-fed infants typically have harder stools than breastfed infants. Quinlan et al. reported that fatty acid soaps and calcium were the primary contributors to stool hardness in both formula and breastfed infants [14]. Although our study observed differences in fecal palmitate soaps and calcium content between the control and high sn-2 PA groups, no significant difference in stool softness was detected. This suggests that factors beyond the reduction of palmitate soaps may influence stool consistency [30]. Variability in findings across studies may be attributed to differences in stool evaluation methods and the subjective nature of parental reporting. The European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) [25] has also concluded that inclusion of high sn-2-palmitate fat blend in infant formulas may have short-term benefits on stool consistency by reducing the calcium soaps formation, but cannot be considered essential.

The two formula-fed groups exhibited comparable gains in body weight and other anthropometric parameters over the first 6 months of life and demonstrated normal growth trajectories when compared to the BF infants. These findings indicate that the both formulas provided sufficient caloric intake to support healthy infant growth. one possible explanation for the beneficial effects of sn-2 formula on infant weight gain is its high content sn-2 palmitic acid, which increased the level of fat absorption and thus enhanced the energy available from the sn-2 formula.

The incidence of AEs was similar across all groups. These results suggest that the formulas assessed in this study do not negatively affect gastrointestinal tolerance or increase the risk of allergic reactions in term infants.

The strengths of this study include its multicenter design involving four cities in China, multiple time points and a long intervention duration; a low drop-out rate (12.6%), and good participant compliance with comparable overall formula intake volume between the two study groups. Limitations that should be considered include: Although a cluster randomization method was adopted, there were still inter-group differences at baseline in some outcome indicators, such as fecal calcium content. In order to reduce their impact on inter-group differences in outcome indicators, baseline level variables were included in the statistical model. Despite adjustment for some potential confounders, we still cannot completely rule out the possible confounding caused by unknown variables. The inter-group comparison of outcome indicators uses PP analysis rather than intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis, which may have a certain impact on the study results.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that infant formula enriched with >40% sn-2 palmitate not only reduces fecal fatty acid and calcium excretion while supporting efficient lipid and calcium absorption, but also exhibits a temporal pattern of fecal magnesium excretion resembling that of breastfed infants. In particular, the investigational formula has a more similar effect to breast milk than the control formula, although it did not improve stool characteristics relative to the control group.
