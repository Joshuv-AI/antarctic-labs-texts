A target-responsive and signal-amplified DNA hydrogel biosensor for visual and quantitative detection of aflatoxin B1
Authors: Haojie Du, Haoyue Liu, Yongshuai Li, Shiyi Wei, Xinning Wang, Yunxuan Weng, Xiaoying Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAflatoxin B1 (AFB1) poses a severe threat to food safety due to its potent carcinogenicity. To address the need for rapid and on-site detection, this study developed a colorimetric biosensor based on an AFB1-responsive DNA aptamercross-linked sodium alginate hydrogel. Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) were encapsulated within the hydrogel as a visual signal reporter. The sensing mechanism relies on target-induced dissociation of aptamer crosslinkers, leading to hydrogel disintegration and release of AuNPs. The hydrogel formulation was optimized regarding sodium alginate concentration and crosslinking conditions. To enhance sensitivity, an Exonuclease I (Exo I)-triggered target recycling strategy was implemented, which amplifies hydrogel network disruption by cleaving the aptamer–target complex and recycling the aptamer. The sensor demonstrated a linear detection range of 0–50 μM, with a detection limit of 12.9 nM, and exhibited high selectivity towards AFB1. This work presents a sensitive, visually readable, and portable platform for mycotoxin detection in food safety applications.Keywords: Aflatoxin B1, Colorimetric detection, DNA-crosslinked hydrogel, Mycotoxin detection, Signal amplification

1. Introduction

A flatoxins are notorious mycotoxin contaminants in the global food supply. They are produced by the fungus Aspergillus flavus, and can be present at every stage of the food chain, from agricultural production and storage to final products such as animal feed, grains, peanuts, milk, and eggs, posing a severe threat to food safety [1,2]. Among them, aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) is the most hazardous, known for its potent carcinogenic and teratogenic effects [1,2]. Detection of aflatoxins in food is essential for ensuring food safety. Conventional methods, primarily based on high-performance liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry, offer high reliability and selectivity [3]. However, these techniques also have significant drawbacks, including high cost, lengthy detection times, and the inability to support real-time, onsite, or portable detection [3].

In recent years, aptamer-based biosensors have been developed and applied in mycotoxin detection [4]. A key component of these biosensors is DNA aptamer, a short single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) that can fold into secondary/tertiary structures upon binding to the target molecule [5–7]. This binding event can be converted into a measurable signal to enable target detection. Owing to their synthetic origin, DNA aptamers offer great modularity and are easy to produce compared to other biorecognition elements such as antibodies [8].

A recent development in biosensing involves the use of target-responsive hydrogels crosslinked by DNA aptamers [9,10]. Typically, polymers are grafted with ssDNAs which are partial complementary to the target-binding aptamer, to form modified polymer chains (PS-A and PS-B). The aptamers can hybridize with PS-A and PS-B to form a crosslinked network (hydrogel), providing both structural integrity and target specificity. Upon binding the target molecule, the aptamer crosslinkers dissociate, resulting in hydrogel disintegration and the consequent release of an encapsulated signaling agent [11]. Detection of the target is thus achieved by measuring this signal via readouts such as colorimetry, microfluidic chips, or common meters. Among these, colorimetric hydrogel sensors are particularly promising for on-site applications due to their visual readout and operational simplicity [3].

To enhance the sensitivity of the colorimetric hydrogel sensors, various signal amplification strategies have been employed, including rolling circle amplification (RCA), hybridization chain reaction (HCR), and the application of tool enzymes [12]. One such enzyme, Exonuclease I (Exo I) from E. coli, specifically degrades ssDNA from the 3′ to 5′ end, while leaving double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) largely intact. This property is exploited in a DNA-aptamer crosslinked hydrogel system [13–15]. In the initial state, the aptamer is hybridized to a complementary ssDNA grafted on a polymer chain, forming a dsDNA duplex that is resistant to Exo I. In the presence of the target, the target binds the aptamer, disrupting the crosslinked hydrogel structure, causing the aptamer to adopt a single-stranded conformation within the aptamer–target complex. When Exo I is present, it cleaves the exposed ssDNA aptamer in the aptamer–target complex, releasing the target back into the hydrogel. The liberated target can then bind to another intact aptamer crosslinker, initiating a new cycle of hydrogel displacement and cleavage. This recycling of the target amplifies the disruption of the hydrogel network and the release of the signal reporter, thereby improving detection sensitivity.

Therefore, this study developed an AFB1 aptamer-crosslinked hydrogel sensor for the visual and quantitative detection of AFB1 mycotoxin. An Exo I-triggered target recycling strategy was developed to enhance sensing sensitivity. The hydrogel matrix was fabricated from sodium alginate (SA), chosen for its good biocompatibility, abundance of functional groups, and ease of modification. Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) were employed as the signal reporter, leveraging their unique localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) property, which results in strong absorption within the visible light spectrum, enabling convenient visual detection.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Materials

NH2-mofidied DNA sequence (ssDNA-A:NH2-TTTTGTGGGCCTAGCGA, ssDNA-B:NH2-TTTACACGTGCCCAAC) and AFB1 aptamer (sequence: GTTGGGCACGTGTTGTCTCTCTGTGTCTCGTGCCCTTCGCTAGGCCCACA) [16] was purchased from Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Aflatoxin B1 (AFB1), deoxynivalenol (DON), zearalenone (ZEN), and ochratoxin A (OTA) were purchased from Baiyidi Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Sodium alginate (200 ± 20 mpa·s) and MES buffer (2-aminomethylsulfonic acid) were purchased from Beijing Inokai Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Trisodium citrate, HAuCl4·3H2O, 1-(3-dimethylamino propyl)-3-ethyl carbodiimide) (EDC) and N-hydroxysulfosuccinimide (Sulfo-NHS) were purchased from Beijing Mairuida Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Exonuclease I was purchased from the United States New England Biolabs Company (MA,USA).

2.2. Preparation of gold nanoparticles

Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) were prepared according to a previously reported method [11]. Citric acid trihydrate solution (38.8 mM, 4 mL) and HAuCl4·3H2O solution (50 mM, 1 mL) were mixed, diluted using deionized water, and placed in a three-necked flask, followed by heating, reflux for 20 min, cooling to room temperature, and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm using a freezing centrifuge (High speed refrigerated centrifuge, H2-16 KR, Hunan Kecheng Instrument Equipment Co., Ltd,China) to obtain AuNPs.

2.3. Preparation of sodium alginate-based DNA crosslinked hydrogel

Solution of sodium alginate (0.25%–1.5% W/V% in MES buffer, pH = 6), ssDNA-A (100 μM in deionized water), ssDNA-B (200 μM in deionized water), and AFB1 aptamer (200–450 μM in deionized water) were prepared respectively. EDC and Sulfo NHS were added to sodium alginate solution in a ratio of 5:1 to 1:2, respectively. The reaction was conducted at room temperature for 30 min, followed by addition of ssDNA A (3 μL, 100 μM), ssDNA B (3 μL, 100 μM), AFB1 aptamer (1 μL, 350 μM), AuNPs (5 μL, 30 nM), EDC (2.61 μL, 5 mM), and Sulfo NHS (0.65 μL, 5 mM). The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 min to obtain the aptamer crosslinked hydrogel. In the sensing test, the amount of the hydrogel and solution containing AFB1 (2.5 μM) toxin was 50 μL and 80 μL, respectively. The absorbance at 530 nm of the experimental group (deionized water containing AFB1) and blank (deionized water without AFB1) was determined. The difference between the absorbance of the experimental group and that of the blank group (A sample-A blank,ΔA) was recorded.

2.4. Exo I-mediated signal amplification

Solution of sodium alginate (0.25%–1.5% W/V% in MES buffer, pH = 6), ssDNA-A (100 μM in deionized water), ssDNA-B (200 μM in deionized water), and AFB1 aptamer (200–450 μM in deionized water) were prepared respectively. EDC and Sulfo-NHS were added to sodium alginate solution in a ratio of 5:1 to 1:2, respectively. The reaction was conducted at room temperature for 30 min, followed by addition of ssDNA A (3 μL, 100 μM), ssDNA B (3 μL, 100 μM), AFB1 aptamer (1 μL, 350 μM), AuNPs (5 μL, 30 nM), EDC (2.61 μL, 5 mM), and Sulfo-NHS (0.65 μL, 5 mM). The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 min to obtain the aptamer crosslinked hydrogel. Then AFB1 toxin (2.5 μM, 80 μL) was added to the hydrogel (50 μL), followed by incubation at 37°C for a period of time. Then Exo I (1.5 μL) was added and incubated at 37°C for a period of time. The supernatant above the hydrogel was collected and its ΔA at 530 nm was measured using a multifunctional microplate detector (TECAN, Spark, Switzerland). 3. Results and discussion

3.1. Sensing mechanism

The sensor was constructed by first designing two single-stranded DNA sequences (ssDNA A and ssDNA B) to be partially complementary to the AFB1 aptamer. The aptamer then acted as a cross-linker, bridging between sodium alginate polymer chains grafted with ssDNA-A (PS-A) and ssDNA-B (PS–B) to form a hydrogel network (Fig. 1a). AuNPs were encapsulated within this matrix as the signaling agent. In the presence of the AFB1, its binding to the aptamer competes with the aptamerss-DNA hybridization. This competitive binding disrupts the crosslinks, leading to hydrogel dissociation and the subsequent release of AuNPs into the supernatant. The released AuNPs can then be quantified spectroscopically, enabling both visual and quantitative detection of AFB1.

(A) Schematic sensing mechanism of AFB1 aptamer-crosslinked sodium alginate hydrogel sensor, (b) hydrogel morphology, (c) absorbance of the supernatant containing AuNPs at 530 nm above the hydrogel in response to AFB1 (2.5 μM), (d) TEM image of AuNPs, (e) size distribution of AuNPs.

The hydrogel had a porous honeycomb or sponge-like microstructure with a continuous three-dimensional network (Fig. 1b). The presence of AFB1 dissociated the hydrogel, releasing the encapsulated AuNPS (Fig. 1d and e), resulting in an absorption at 530 nm (Fig. 1c). While in the absence of AFB1, the hydrogel was intact and no significant absorption at 530 nm was observed (Fig. 1c).

3.2. Sensor optimization

To optimize the sensing performance, factors affecting the structure of the hydrogel, including SA concentration and ratio of EDC: Sulfo-NHS, were investigated (Fig. 2). Overall, the hydrogels exhibited a highly porous structure (Fig. 2a). With increasing sodium alginate (SA) concentration, the hydrogel became denser and the pore size decreased. The release of AuNPs from the hydrogel in the presence of AFB1, represented by the ΔA intensity at 530 nm, showed minimal change as the SA concentration increased from 0.25 to 0.75 wt.%, but then decreased with further increase in SA concentration from 0.75 to 1.5 wt.% (Fig. 2b). The reduction is likely attributable to the denser structure and smaller pores of the hydrogel, which hindered the release of AuNPs. Considering both the hydrogel structure and the release of the signaling agent (AuNPs), an SA concentration of 0.75 wt.% was selected for hydrogel construction.

(A) Morphology of the hydrogel prepared with sodium alginate at different concentrations (0.25–1.5 wt.% in MES); (b) ΔA at 530 nm of the supernatant above the hydrogel sensor prepared with SA at different concentrations in the presence of AFB1 (10 μM); (c) ΔA at 530 nm of the supernatant above the hydrogel sensor prepared at different ratios of EDC: Sulfo-NHS in the presence of AFB1 (SA concentration of 0.75wt.%, AFB1 concentration of 10 μM).

To graft ssDNA onto the polymer chains of sodium alginate, 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide (EDC) and Sulfo-NHS (N-hydroxysulfosuccinimide) were used to activate the sodium alginate. EDC activates the carboxyl groups on SA, which then react with Sulfo-NHS to form NHS esters, generating a more stable and reactive intermediate for coupling with primary amine groups on the ssDNA. The EDC: Sulfo-NHS ratio influences the SA-ssDNA grafting efficiency, the hydrogel structure, and consequently the sensing performance. Therefore, the effect of the EDC: Sulfo-NHS ratio on the sensing performance, specifically, the amount of AuNPs released from the hydrogel in the presence of AFB1, as indicated by the ΔA at 530 nm, was investigated (Fig. 2c). As the EDC: Sulfo-NHS ratio decreased, the ΔA intensity of the AuNPs at 530 nm initially increased, reached a maximum at ratios of 4:1 and 2:1, and then decreased. Based on these results, an EDC: Sulfo-NHS ratio of 2:1 was chosen for hydrogel fabrication. To summarize, optimal hydrogel formulation is SA concentration of 0.75 wt.% and EDC: Sulfo-NHS ratio of 2:1. This formulation was used for following study.

3.3. Signal amplification by Exo I-triggered target recycling

3.3.1. Signal amplification mechanism

Exo I specifically cleaves ssDNA from the ends and is insensitive to ssDNA. In the initial state, the aptamer is hybridized with a complementary ssDNA grafted onto the polymer chain, forming a dsDNA duplex that is resistant to Exo I cleavage. Upon introduction of the target (AFB1), the target binds the aptamer, disrupting the crosslinking and causing the aptamer to adopt a single-stranded conformation within the aptamer–target complex. This exposed ssDNA aptamer becomes susceptible to cleavage by Exo I. As a result, the target is released back into the hydrogel and can be reused to disrupt additional crosslinks (Fig. 3). This recycling of the target amplifies the dissociation of the hydrogel network and enhances detection sensitivity.

Scheme of signal amplification via target recycling using Exo I

3.3.2. Feasibility of signal amplification

The feasibility of signal amplification using Exo I was investigated (Fig. 4). In the absence of Exo I, the introduction of AFB1 induced hydrogel disintegration, releasing the encapsulated AuNPs, resulting in a ΔA of the supernatant at 530 nm. In the presence of Exo I, this ΔA intensity increased, indicating enhanced hydrogel breakdown and a more thorough release of AuNPs, attributable to the recycling of the AFB1 through Exo I-mediated cleavage of the AFB1-aptamer complex (Fig. 4a).

Next, the effect of the AFB1 and Exo I incubation time on the hydrogel sensing performance was examined (Fig. 4b). When AFB1 and Exo I were added sequentially, i.e., AFB1 was introduced first and incubated for 25 min, followed by the addition of Exo I with further incubation for 5, 10, or 15 min (25/5, 25/10, 25/15), the ΔA at 530 nm increased with Exo I incubation time, reached a maximum at 10 min, and then decreased. Meanwhile, an absorption peak at 730 nm was observed, the intensity of which increased with longer Exo I incubation, this could be attributed to the aggregation of the AuNPs [17–19]. In contrast, in the presence of the AFB1 alone (30/0), no significant AuNP aggregation was detected. These results suggest that prolonged Exo I incubation induced AuNP aggregation, likely due to enzyme-induced disruption of the AuNP surface charge.

When the Exo I incubation time was fixed at 15 min, longer AFB1 incubation with the hydrogel (25 min vs 15 min) led to an increased ΔA at both 530 nm and 730 nm. Furthermore, when AFB1 and Exo I were introduced simultaneously (30/-) and co-incubated for a total of 30 min, a much stronger absorption at 730 nm than at 530 nm was observed, indicating severe AuNP aggregation. Since the ΔA at 530 nm reflects the sensing performance of the hydrogel, a high ratio of A530/A730 indicating minimal Exo I-induced AuNP aggregation is desirable. Therefore, an AFB1 incubation time of 25 min followed by Exo I incubation for 5 min was selected.

Next, the effect of Exo I loading on the sensing performance was investigated (Fig. 4c). The ΔA of the supernatant at 530 nm in the presence of AFB1 increased as the Exo I loading was raised from 0 to 1.5 μL, which can be attributed to the more extensive hydrogel disintegration at higher enzyme loadings. Considering both the signal amplification effect and cost, an Exo I loading of 1.5 μL was selected for subsequent experiments.

We next tested the specificity of the two hydrogel sensors by detecting mycotoxins with chemical core-skeleton structures like AFB1, including zearalenone (ZEN), ochratoxin A (OTA) and deoxynivalenol (DON). The sensors both had a high ΔA at 530 nm in the presence of AFB1 and negligible ΔA with other mycotoxins, suggesting their high selectivity (Fig. 5b–d).

3.3.4. Food sample testing

The feasibility of the sensor in detecting AFB1 in food matrix (peanut oil) was evaluated (Table 2, Table 3). For the non-signal-amplification mode, the recovery rates ranged from 102% to 120%, with relative standard deviations (RSDs) below 2.44% (Table 2). For the signal-amplification mode, the recovery rates ranged from 117% to 137%, with RSDs below 2.63% (Table 3). These results demonstrate that the sensor exhibits good accuracy for AFB1 detection in real food matrices.

4. Conclusion

This study developed a sensitive and selective colorimetric biosensor for AFB1 detection based on a DNA aptamer-crosslinked sodium alginate hydrogel. The sensor utilizes an AFB1-specific aptamer as a crosslinker. Upon the aptamer binding the AFB1, the hydrogel network disintegrates, releasing pre-loaded AuNPs for visual and quantitative analysis. An Exo I-mediated target recycling strategy was incorporated, which cleaves the liberated aptamer-AFB1 complex, recycling the target to amplify signal generation and significantly enhance sensitivity. After optimization of the hydrogel composition, the sensor demonstrated a wide linear detection range and good specificity against other common mycotoxins (DON, OTA, ZEN). This work presents a promising, visually readable platform offering a potential for application in rapid, on-site food safety screening. Future efforts will focus on testing in real samples and further device integration.
