Determinants of pain, anxiety and patient satisfaction during intravenous injection: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Güzel Nur Yıldız, Hanım Duru Yüce Başaran, Burak Yavuz, Dilara Koçyiğit, Gamze Koç, Bahar Çiftçi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntravenous injection is one of the most frequently performed invasive nursing procedures in hospitals. However, patients may experience pain and anxiety during this procedure, which can affect patient satisfaction.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to determine the levels of pain, anxiety and satisfaction in patients undergoing intravenous injection and to examine the sociodemographic, clinical and procedural factors affecting these levels.MethodsA descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted between August and November 2025 in the emergency departments of Atatürk University Research Hospital, a large tertiary care university hospital. This single-centre study was carried out in a high-volume emergency department that provides 24-hour services to a diverse patient population. A total of 405 patients who received intravenous injections and agreed to participate in the study were included. Data were collected using an Information Form, Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for pain, VAS for anxiety and Post-Injection Satisfaction Scale. Descriptive statistics, t-tests, analysis of variance, correlation analyses and multiple regression analyses were used to analyse the data.ResultsThe mean pain VAS score for patients was 4.39±2.33, the mean anxiety VAS score was 4.42±2.14 and the mean Post-Injection Satisfaction Scale score was 3.51±0.77, indicating moderate levels of pain and anxiety on a 0–10 VAS. Women were found to have significantly higher levels of pain and anxiety than men (p<0.05). Low educational level, chronic disease, poorer perceived general health, negative intravenous experience, frequent injections, low pain tolerance and prolonged pain were associated with higher pain and anxiety levels and lower satisfaction levels (p<0.05). A strong positive correlation was found between pain and anxiety (r=0.656, p<0.05), while a negative correlation was found between pain, anxiety and satisfaction (p<0.05). Multiple regression analysis showed that lower pain tolerance and longer pain duration were among the most clinically relevant variables associated with higher pain and anxiety levels, particularly pain tolerance and pain duration, together with perceived general health status and procedure-related variables.ConclusionIt was found that patients experienced moderate pain and anxiety during intravenous injection and that this situation negatively affected patient satisfaction. The results indicate that individualised nursing approaches in intravenous injection practices and the implementation of pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions aimed at reducing pain and anxiety may increase patient satisfaction.Keywords: Patient Satisfaction, Nurses, Patient Care Management

Introduction

Intravenous injection is an invasive nursing practice that involves administering drugs directly into the venous lumen to facilitate their entry into the systemic circulation.1 This invasive procedure is widely used to administer highly irritant medications, regulate acid-base balance, enhance the effect of certain medications by delivering them directly into the circulation, perform blood or blood product transfusions, administer intravenous fluid replacement, provide nutritional products and generally maintain patient health.2,4 The international literature reports that nearly 80% of hospitalised individuals receive intravenous treatment at least once, and millions of patients experience this invasive procedure each year.5,7 This prevalence highlights the central role of intravenous procedures in nursing care and their impact on the patient experience.

In invasive procedures such as injections, multiple factors can increase pain levels, including differences in the angle at which the needle enters the tissue, deficiencies in the application technique, mechanical trauma to the tissue, the individual’s psychological state and previous experiences.8,10 Pain during intravenous injection may result from tissue irritation and stimulation of nerve endings, contributing to increased patient discomfort.11 12 Furthermore, factors that increase tissue irritation during injection, such as the administration rate, concentration and pH, have also been reported to exacerbate pain.13 14 All of these factors negatively impact the effectiveness of nursing interventions, patient–nurse interaction, patient satisfaction and overall quality of care.8,10 Pain experienced during intravenous procedures, in particular, is among the key factors directly affecting the success of the procedure and patient satisfaction.15 Therefore, the expectation of pain in individuals can trigger avoidance behaviours by causing procedure-related fear and physical discomfort.10

In this context, previous studies have examined various aspects of intravenous procedures related to pain, anxiety and patient experience. These findings provide important insights into the factors influencing patient experience during intravenous injection. The application of appropriate intravenous injection techniques, calming the patient, keeping them in a supine position during catheterisation, informing the patient beforehand and diverting their attention are effective interventions in reducing pain and anxiety.16

Rivera et al17 reported that invasive procedures in patients with visible or non-palpable veins increase pain due to unsuccessful cannulation attempts and complications. Ultrasound guidance and experienced staff improve success and patient satisfaction, whereas a lack of equipment and expertise worsens the patient experience. Additionally, ignoring previous cannulation experiences may lead to physical and emotional distress. All participants stated that ultrasound-guided cannulation by the difficult intravenous access (DIVA) team improved the experience and increased satisfaction.17 Özkan and Korkut18 reported that breathing exercises during peripheral venous catheterisation reduced pain and anxiety, lowered pulse and blood pressure, shortened procedure time and increased patient satisfaction. Therefore, they recommend using breathing exercises to improve patient comfort during intravenous procedures.18 A study by Rişvan and Su (2024) found that ball squeezing and virtual reality goggle interventions during intravenous injection procedures significantly reduced patients’ pain and anxiety levels.19 However, these studies primarily focused on specific interventions and did not comprehensively evaluate the combined effects of pain, anxiety and patient satisfaction.

Intravenous injections are common in clinical nursing practice but can cause pain and anxiety, affecting patient satisfaction and comfort. High levels of discomfort may reduce trust in healthcare professionals and treatment adherence. Existing literature suggests that pain during intravenous procedures is not only physiological but also directly influences patient satisfaction and quality of care.20,22 Although previous studies have examined pain or anxiety related to intravenous procedures, there is a clear and important gap in the literature regarding studies that simultaneously evaluate pain, anxiety and patient satisfaction alongside their associated sociodemographic, clinical and procedural factors, particularly within emergency department settings. This limitation in the literature highlights the need for in-depth research into the factors affecting pain and satisfaction during intravenous injections, particularly in clinical settings where these outcomes directly influence care quality and patient safety. Therefore, there is a need for a comprehensive assessment of patients’ experiences during intravenous injection.20,22 Accordingly, this study emphasises the need for a holistic approach to evaluating patients’ experiences during intravenous injections.

This study was conducted to determine pain, anxiety and satisfaction levels in patients undergoing intravenous injection and to examine the sociodemographic, clinical and procedural factors associated with these outcomes. Specifically, this study aimed to: (1) determine pain and anxiety levels during intravenous injection; (2) assess postprocedure satisfaction; (3) examine the relationships between these outcomes and sociodemographic, clinical and procedural factors; and (4) identify independent predictors of pain, anxiety and satisfaction.

Method

Sample and data collection

The study was designed as a descriptive, cross-sectional study. The study population consisted of adult patients who attended the emergency departments of a university hospital and a training and research hospital in northeastern Türkiye between August and November 2025 and underwent intravenous injection. This population was considered appropriate because intravenous injection is a common procedure in emergency care and is directly relevant to the study’s aim. Because no directly comparable study was identified to guide sample size estimation, the calculation was based on a conservative approach using assumptions that would yield the largest required sample. The sample size was determined using G*Power V.3.1.9.7 to ensure reliable and valid results. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed with a medium effect size (0.25) across five groups. Considering a 5% margin of error and a 1−β=95% power level, at least 305 patients were calculated to be sufficient. 20 participants were excluded from the study: 14 patients who agreed to participate were using painkillers and 6 patients developed complications. Data were collected from 405 patients in this study. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling to achieve the required sample size within the study timeframe, as no comprehensive sampling frame of all eligible patients was available in the emergency department setting. This sampling method may introduce selection bias and limit the generalisability of the findings; therefore, results should be interpreted with caution.

Research methodology and data collection tools

Patient information form

This form was developed by the researchers based on the literature and included items on age, gender, educational status, marital status, occupation, presence of chronic disease, previous intravenous experience, frequency of intravenous injections, perceived general health status, pain tolerance, pain duration, and selected procedure-related characteristics.

Visual Analogue Scale for pain

A 10 cm horizontal Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) was used to measure pain levels. On the scale, the left end marked 0 cm represented ‘No pain’, while the right end marked 10 cm represented ‘Intolerable/Most severe pain’. Participants were asked to indicate their perceived pain by placing a mark on the line. Measurements were taken in centimetres (cm); the distance of the mark from zero was recorded with millimetre precision (eg, 45 mm=4.5 cm). The resulting VAS pain score was evaluated as a continuous variable.23

VAS for anxiety

An independent 10 cm long VAS was also used to measure anxiety. On the scale, the left end was defined as 0 cm for ‘No anxiety/completely calm’, and the right end was defined as 10 cm for ‘Extremely anxious/unbearable anxiety’. Participants marked their momentary anxiety levels at the time of injection on this scale. Measurements were recorded in centimetres.24 (see online supplemental file 1).

Post-Injection Satisfaction Scale

The Post-Injection Satisfaction Scale (PISS), developed by Yıldız and ve Çiftçi25, was used to assess patients’ satisfaction with the injection procedure.25 The scale is a 5-point Likert-type structure rated from 1=Not at all satisfied to 5=Very satisfied. The scale consists of two dimensions: ‘Satisfaction with the Practitioner’ (Items 5–9) and ‘Satisfaction with the Procedure’ (Items 1–4). Scoring is based on the average of the items, with the lowest possible score being 1 and the highest possible score being 5. As the score increases, the patient’s satisfaction level also increases. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of the scale, consisting of a total of nine items, was reported as 0.895 in the study in which it was developed. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the ‘Satisfaction with the Practitioner’ dimension was found to be 0.823, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the ‘Satisfaction with the Procedure’ dimension was found to be 0.934 and Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale as a whole was found to be 0.925.25

Data collection

Data collection began after obtaining ethical approval and institutional permissions. In this context, data collection forms were duplicated and made ready for use. Eligible patients were informed about the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation and the data collection process, after which verbal informed consent was obtained. All intravenous injection procedures were performed by the same experienced clinical nurse to ensure procedural standardisation and reduce variability. Data were collected immediately (within 1 min) after the intraveous injection procedure using the Patient Information Form, the VAS for Pain, the VAS for Anxiety and the PISS. Data collection was conducted by the researcher through face-to-face interviews and completion of forms in an appropriate clinical setting.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS V.23.0. In the analysis, numbers, percentages, mean and SD values were used to determine the descriptive characteristics. The independent-samples t-test was used to evaluate the mean scores of two independent groups for measurements showing a normal distribution, while the ‘Mann-Whitney U Test’ was used for measurements not showing a normal distribution. One-way ANOVA was used to evaluate the mean scores of three or more independent groups for measurements showing a normal distribution, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for measurements not showing a normal distribution. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to identify variables associated with pain, anxiety and satisfaction. Kurtosis and skewness values were used to examine the normality of the data distribution, and Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was used to determine internal consistency. Pearson correlation analysis was used for normally distributed variables, whereas Spearman correlation analysis was used for non-normally distributed variables. Effect sizes were reported using Cohen’s d for two-group comparisons and partial eta squared for analyses involving more than two groups. Given the use of multiple statistical tests, there is a potential risk of type I error; therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. As no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons (eg, Bonferroni) was applied, the results should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines (see online supplemental file 2).

The mean VAS pain score was 4.39±2.33, mean VAS anxiety score was 4.42±2.14, mean score for the Satisfaction with the Practitioner subdimension was 3.55±0.90, mean score for the Satisfaction with the Procedure subdimension was 3.48±0.77 and mean total PISS score was 3.51±0.77 (table 1).

Women reported significantly higher pain and anxiety levels than men, whereas satisfaction levels did not differ by gender (p>0.05). Lower educational level and the presence of chronic disease were associated with higher pain and anxiety scores, while satisfaction tended to be higher among individuals with secondary education and those satisfied with healthcare services. Poor perceived health status was associated with increased pain and anxiety, whereas moderate health perception was linked to higher satisfaction levels. Procedural factors also played a role; patients with negative intravenous experiences and those undergoing frequent injections reported higher pain and anxiety and lower satisfaction. Individual characteristics were particularly influential. Lower pain tolerance and longer pain duration were strongly associated with increased pain and anxiety and decreased satisfaction. Similarly, individuals with previous negative injection experiences reported worse outcomes across all measures. In terms of procedural difficulties, patients who identified pain as the greatest challenge reported higher pain scores, while those reporting difficulty in locating a vein had lower anxiety levels compared with those reporting pain and fear. Additionally, patients’ preferred supportive interventions were associated with outcomes, suggesting that individual expectations may influence perceived pain and satisfaction. No significant associations were found between allergy-related variables and pain, anxiety or satisfaction outcomes (p>0.05). Detailed comparisons are presented in online supplemental file 3.

Multiple regression analysis was performed to determine the variables predicting pain, anxiety and satisfaction factors. For categorical variables, (k–1) dummy variables were created. Histograms and normal P–P plots were examined for each regression analysis, and it was found that the residuals satisfied the assumption of normality. In the multiple linear regression analysis for the pain variable, the model explained 43.9% of the variance (R²=0.439). The adjusted R² value of 0.400 indicates that the model has moderate explanatory power given the predictors included. The Durbin-Watson value (1.927) indicates that there is no autocorrelation among the residuals. When examining the multicollinearity assumption, tolerance values ranged from 0.378 to 0.863, and VIF values from 1.159 to 2.644, indicating no multicollinearity in the model. The regression model was statistically significant (F=11.380, p<0.001). Among the variables included in the model simultaneously, satisfaction with healthcare services, perceived general health status, intravenous injection frequency, pain tolerance, pain duration and measures desired prior to injection were significant predictors of pain perception (table 2).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the levels of pain, anxiety and satisfaction in patients receiving intravenous injections and the factors affecting these levels. Patients experienced moderate pain and anxiety, with moderate satisfaction levels, indicating that intravenous injection remains a clinically relevant source of discomfort.

Regression analyses indicated that pain tolerance, pain duration and perceived general health were the most relevant factors associated with pain and anxiety, while satisfaction was related to both individual and procedural characteristics. In addition to technical competencies, emotional intelligence may also influence patient–nurse interactions and patients’ perception of pain and anxiety during clinical procedures.26

The mean pain score observed in this study (4.39±2.33) reflects moderate pain and is consistent with findings reported in similar emergency department settings, supporting the findings and aligning with existing literature. These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that procedural difficulty, repeated attempts and unsuccessful first attempts increase pain, while non-pharmacological interventions may effectively reduce intravenous injection pain.27,30

The study found that women had significantly higher levels of both pain and anxiety than men. This finding is consistent with the literature indicating gender-related differences in the perception of pain and anxiety. A systematic review covering 119 studies reported that both needle fear and needle phobia are more prevalent in women than in men.31 Gender differences in anxiety prevalence have been found to be related to both biological and psychosocial factors.32 It has been reported that women experience higher anxiety than men due to hormonal fluctuations and greater focus on somatic sensations.33 Findings have been reported supporting that women have a lower pain threshold than men during electrophysiological procedures.34 A study conducted in adults in Mexico determined that gender is a significant risk factor for venipuncture anxiety.35 In this study, gender differences were also evident, reinforcing the need for targeted interventions. Similar findings have also been reported in studies conducted in Turkey and other Middle Eastern countries, where women tend to report higher levels of pain and anxiety during medical procedures. These findings may be influenced by cultural, social and gender-related factors shaping pain perception and expression in these regions.

When examining the relationship between educational level and anxiety, it has been observed that a low educational level may be a risk factor for procedural anxiety. It has been reported that patients with higher educational levels exhibit more accurate cognitive processing of the disease and the procedure, which reduces fear and anxiety.36 A study conducted using the Mendelian randomisation method found that educational level may have a protective effect on anxiety disorders.37 The effect of chronic illness on pain and anxiety is also noteworthy. It has been reported that the prevalence of needle phobia in adults with chronic illness varies widely depending on the type of disease and that this patient group requires special assessment.38 A study conducted in Saudi Arabia and Egypt found that needle phobia was associated with gender and certain demographic characteristics; it was also determined that this condition could lead to avoidance behaviour of injection-requiring healthcare services in a significant proportion of patients.39 These findings indicate that patients with chronic conditions requiring regular treatment should undergo individual risk assessment prior to intravenous injections.

An association between butterfly needle colour and pain/anxiety was observed; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously as the catheter gauge and insertion site were not directly assessed. Additionally, butterfly needle colour may act as a proxy for factors such as catheter gauge, insertion site or clinician preference, none of which were controlled in this study, which may limit the interpretation of this association. Although previous studies suggest that catheter characteristics and insertion site may influence procedural pain and patient satisfaction, the present study did not directly evaluate catheter gauge or insertion site. It has been noted that the back of the hand has tougher skin and a higher density of nociceptors, leading to a greater pain experience compared with the forearm.21 The updated ‘Infusion Therapy Standards of Practice’ guidelines emphasise that large peripheral catheters increase the risk of phlebitis and that the smallest gauge catheter appropriate for patient treatment and needs should be selected.40 The INS (Infusion Nurses Society) 2024 Standards recommend selecting the smallest catheter diameter required by the treatment plan and preferring the forearm region.41

The study found that individuals who rated their general health as poor had significantly higher pain and anxiety scores than those who rated their health as moderate or good, and that the satisfaction scores of the group reporting moderate general health were higher than those reporting poor health. This finding demonstrates that an individual’s perception of their general health is an important factor shaping their procedural experience. In this study, this effect was clearly observed across both pain and anxiety outcomes. The higher pain and anxiety experienced by individuals who perceive their general health status as poor can be explained by this patient group being in a more sensitive psychophysiological state. Chronic illnesses and poor perceived general health have been reported to increase procedural fear and anxiety.38 Perceived health status has been shown to influence individuals’ pain expectations and coping mechanisms.32 These findings suggest that perceived general health status is an important individual factor shaping the intravenous injection experience and that individuals who perceive their health as poor may represent a more vulnerable group in terms of procedural pain and anxiety.

The study found that patients with previous negative intravenous injection experiences and those who underwent frequent injections reported higher pain and anxiety levels and lower satisfaction. It was noted that needle phobia typically begins in childhood, but previous traumatic experiences and conditioning can also trigger anxiety in adulthood.31 It was found that fear levels increased in patients as the history of catheterisation and the number of needle insertions increased.36 It was stated that frequent exposure makes it easier to predict needle stick pain in advance and that this can increase anxiety through anticipation.33 Repeated intravenous catheterisation in oncology patients has been reported to negatively affect the patient experience and result in high catheter failure rates.42 Patients who experienced multiple needle insertions or complications were found to be more likely to lose trust in healthcare providers and express anxiety.43

The study determined that pain tolerance level was the variable with the greatest effect size on pain, anxiety and satisfaction. The fact that individuals with low pain tolerance had a significantly more negative experience of the procedure than other groups indicates that individual pain threshold is a critical factor determining the intravenous injection experience. It was emphasised that pain and anxiety are in a cyclical relationship where one influences the other, with the intensity of one increasing the other, and that the pain experienced by the patient during the procedure may increase anxiety, leading to vasoconstriction and complicating the procedure.19 A study evaluating the relationship between pre-intravenous cannulation anxiety and procedural pain in children in India found a strong positive correlation between preprocedural anxiety and procedural pain levels, with this relationship being particularly strong in patients undergoing intravenous cannulation for the first time.44 These findings support the need for nurses to assess the patient’s pain tolerance level before intravenous injection and to implement proactive pain management strategies for individuals with low tolerance. Moreover, previous studies have suggested that pain tolerance is not a fixed trait but can be modified through interventions such as cognitive-behavioural strategies, relaxation techniques and patient education, which may help reduce procedural pain and improve patient outcomes.

One of the important findings of the study was the detection of strong positive relationships between pain and anxiety, and negative relationships between these two variables and satisfaction. These findings reveal that as the pain experienced during intravenous injection increases, patients’ anxiety levels also rise in parallel, and both variables together negatively affect patient satisfaction. Similar relationships were found in an experimental study conducted with adult patients in Turkey, and it was reported that virtual reality goggles both reduced pain and anxiety and increased satisfaction.45 A study evaluating the effectiveness of the ShotBlocker device found a strong negative relationship between pain and comfort scores.18 Breathing exercises during peripheral venous catheterisation have been shown to reduce anxiety and increase satisfaction.46 These findings support the notion that pain and anxiety trigger each other, creating a vicious cycle and that both dimensions should be targeted simultaneously in nursing practice. Patients’ requests for psychological support, topical analgesia, distraction and hot/cold applications highlight the importance of considering patient preferences when planning supportive interventions. Accordingly, routine preprocedure assessment of these preferences may enhance individualised care and improve outcomes. Evidence also shows that non-pharmacological methods—such as the Valsalva manoeuvre, thermomechanical stimulation, virtual reality, coughing, spirometer use and stress balls—effectively reduce pain and anxiety, supporting their routine, cost-effective use in clinical practice.47,49

The study revealed that the variable ‘measures requested for IV injection’ was a significant predictor of both pain and satisfaction levels in regression analysis. It is noteworthy that pain scores were higher in patients requesting psychological support compared with those preferring other measures. Furthermore, patients requesting pain relief cream and psychological support reported significantly higher satisfaction scores than those who selected other options. This finding indicates that patients are aware of their own needs and that meeting these needs directly affects satisfaction. Questioning the patient’s preferred support method and planning interventions tailored to this preference whenever possible is a fundamental principle of patient-centred care. It has been reported that asking patients about their preferences before the procedure and implementing individualised interventions aligned with these preferences increases patient satisfaction.43 This finding further suggests that assessing patient preferences before the procedure and planning individualised interventions accordingly may improve patient satisfaction.

The study found that overall satisfaction was moderate and that satisfaction with the practitioner was higher than satisfaction with the procedure. This finding reveals that patients are more satisfied with nurses’ attitudes and behaviours than with the technical aspects of the procedure. Patient satisfaction is a multidimensional concept influenced not only by technical competence but also by communication, emotional support and the overall quality of nursing care.50 Furthermore, work environment and nurse-related factors such as stress and professional quality of life may indirectly influence patient satisfaction and clinical outcomes.51 A study conducted in Singapore and the Philippines determined that healthcare staff competence, infection prevention and pain management were the most important determinants of patient satisfaction, and that patients satisfied with their vascular access experience were more likely to express satisfaction with their overall hospital stay.43 Our study also supports this finding, as patients who were generally satisfied with healthcare services reported significantly higher satisfaction with intravenous injections. In this context, nurses’ work environment and professional well-being may also indirectly influence patient satisfaction and care quality.51

The study revealed that the most common difficulty patients experienced with intravenous injections was the inability to locate a vein. The fact that the inability to locate a vein was the most frequently reported difficulty highlights the importance of improving nurses’ vein assessment skills and early identification of patients with difficult vascular access (DIVA). It has been reported that the A-DIVA (Adult Difficult Intravenous Access) scale can be used to predetermine the risk of difficult venous access and that this assessment increases first-attempt success rates.30 Multiple attempts have been shown to significantly increase pain.51 A comprehensive meta-analysis examining peripheral intravenous catheter complications found that pain rates were significantly higher in developing economies compared with developed economies.[53] The INS 2024 Standards recommend a maximum of two attempts per clinician and referral to a more experienced nurse if the attempt fails.41 Overall, the intravenous injection experience is shaped by interacting individual and procedural factors, highlighting the need for integrated and patient-centred approaches in clinical practice.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. As the research used a cross-sectional design, the relationships between variables could be identified, but causal conclusions could not be drawn. The study was conducted only in the emergency departments of a university hospital and a teaching and research hospital located in the north-east of Turkey’s Eastern Anatolia Region and does not represent different clinical units or different regions. Furthermore, because the data were collected using self-report scales, response bias is possible. Furthermore, some potentially important confounding variables, such as clinician experience, catheter gauge size, insertion site and number of insertion attempts, were not controlled in this study and may have influenced the outcomes. In addition, patients receiving sedative or analgesic medications were excluded, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to real-world emergency department settings where such interventions are common. Additionally, convenience sampling was employed to facilitate recruitment during the study period, which may introduce selection bias and limit the sample’s representativeness. However, the large sample size and the fact that the study was conducted in two different hospitals support the generalisability of the findings to a certain extent for similar patient groups and emergency department settings. Future studies covering different clinical settings and broader geographical areas would increase the generalisability of the results. Moreover, multiple statistical comparisons were conducted without formal adjustment, which may increase the risk of Type I error; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Additionally, although not the focus of the main analysis, relatively low internal consistency in the pilot phase for some scales may be considered a limitation.

Clinical implications

These findings highlight the importance of individualised nursing care during intravenous injection. Assessing pain tolerance, previous experiences and patient preferences prior to the procedure may help reduce pain and anxiety. The integration of simple non-pharmacological interventions (eg, distraction, psychological support) into routine practice may improve patient comfort and satisfaction. Additionally, early identification of patients with difficult intravenous access and limiting repeated attempts may enhance procedural success and patient experience.

Conclusion

This study examined pain, anxiety and satisfaction levels in patients undergoing intravenous injection and the factors associated with these experiences. Patients reported moderate levels of pain and anxiety, along with moderate satisfaction, indicating that intravenous injection remains a clinically relevant source of discomfort. A strong positive relationship between pain and anxiety and a negative relationship between these variables and satisfaction highlight the need to address these dimensions together during clinical practice. Key factors, including pain tolerance, perceived general health, prior experiences and procedural characteristics, were associated with patient outcomes. Overall, intravenous injection is not merely a technical procedure but a multidimensional experience shaped by the interaction of individual and procedural factors. Therefore, adopting patient-centred and individualised nursing approaches is essential to reduce pain and anxiety and to improve patient satisfaction. Assessing patients’ needs and previous experiences before the procedure and integrating simple non-pharmacological interventions (eg, psychological support, distraction and breathing techniques) may enhance patient comfort. Strengthening nurses’ venipuncture skills and minimising the number of repeated attempts are also important for improving the overall patient experience. A limitation of this study is its cross-sectional design, which limits causal interpretation. Future studies should evaluate the effectiveness of targeted interventions, such as routine pain tolerance assessment, distraction techniques and psychological support, in reducing pain and anxiety during intravenous injection. In addition, policymakers should consider integrating non-pharmacological pain management protocols into emergency department nursing standards of care. Furthermore, nursing curricula should emphasise individualised pain assessment and patient preference-based interventions for common invasive procedures.
