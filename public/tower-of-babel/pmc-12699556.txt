Association of drinking water salinity with elevated blood pressure and risk of hypertension among coastal and other populations: a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies
Authors: Rajat Das Gupta, Setor K Kunutsor, David Eliecer Lopez-Salamanca, Fariha Tahsin Mercy, Nafisa Nawal, Carlos Espinal Tejada, Kunihiro Matsushita, Silvana Luciani, Anselm Hennis, Rajiv Chowdhury
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe link between drinking water salinity and increased blood pressure and hypertension risk among coastal and other populations remains unclear. To investigate this, we performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies on drinking water salinity and cardiovascular outcomes.MethodsWe systematically searched MEDLINE, Embase and Web of Science for relevant studies published until 10 May 2025. Observational studies reporting on the association between sodium in drinking water and systolic/diastolic blood pressure (SBP/DBP), hypertension, coronary heart disease (CHD), stroke and composite cardiovascular outcomes were prespecified to be included. We assessed study quality using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale and performed random effects meta-analysis.ResultsWe identified 27 observational studies (involving 74 063 unique participants from 7 countries), 15 of which included coastal populations. Comparing higher versus lower drinking water salinity, the mean differences were 3.22 mm Hg (95% CI 1.11 to 5.33) for SBP and 2.82 mm Hg (95% CI 1.44 to 4.20) for DBP. The pooled OR for hypertension, comparing higher versus lower water salinity, was 1.26 (95% CI 1.07 to 1.48). These associations were generally consistent across subgroups but were statistically significant for studies conducted in coastal populations and for those published after 2000. However, we found an insufficient number of studies with reliable data on CHD or stroke outcomes.ConclusionsHigher drinking water salinity is associated with an elevated risk of blood pressure and hypertension, especially among coastal populations. More research is needed to examine connections with CHD and stroke, and to create strategies to counter salinity’s effects, particularly in climate-vulnerable coastal areas.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Global Health, Environmental health, Epidemiology, Public Health

Introduction

Common cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), such as hypertension or high blood pressure (BP), continue to be the leading causes of death and disability in the world.1 A recent WHO report highlights that hypertension and its related complications impact more than a billion people worldwide.2 Additionally, only 54% of adults with hypertension are currently diagnosed, 42% receive treatment and just 21% have their hypertension controlled.2 3 While several behavioural factors (including unhealthy diet, physical inactivity and tobacco use) have been linked to increased risks of CVD and hypertension, recent evidence indicates important roles of environmental risk factors in CVD aetiology (such as air and drinking water pollutants).3,5 In this regard, global groundwater, which makes up nearly half of the world’s drinking water supply,6 is affected by varying mineral compositions due to infiltration from different sources, such as rain and surface water. Climate change is expected to further alter these mineral compositions, such as in coastal areas where rising sea levels are speeding up salinity intrusion into groundwater and soil.7 8 This increased salinity (defined as the dissolved salt content of a body of water)9 is a potentially significant global health challenge because: (a) over 3 billion people worldwide currently live in coastal or near-coastal areas10 and (b) most of these coastal populations are located in low-income and middle-income countries, where people drink water from sources with high salinity levels.

However, the extent to which drinking water salinity, a potential environmental risk factor, is associated with elevated BP and the risk of cardiovascular (including hypertension) outcomes remains unclear. Interpretation of findings from the available individual studies is complicated by: (a) inconsistent methods to measure salinity,11 (b) inconsistent results across studies (eg, some studies12,15) have found a significant positive association with BP outcomes, while the others did not16; (b) differences in the study design (cross-sectional vs cohort) or failure to explore a whole range of hypertension outcomes (eg, BP vs hypertension vs gestational hypertension) and (c) imprecise estimates due to low sample sizes. Furthermore, previous systematic reviews on this topic are significantly limited by: (a) their generally narrative nature, with no quantitative synthesis of findings,17 (b) a somewhat narrow focus on a single outcome (eg, gestational hypertension),11 (c) failure to conduct detailed analyses by stratifying risk estimates by clinically relevant subgroups (eg, by coastal vs non-coastal areas, age and sex grouping)11 and (d) excluding more comprehensive and larger epidemiological studies recently published on this topic.11

To address these key uncertainties, we conducted an updated systematic review and meta-analysis that aims to (a) provide detailed evidence on the associations between drinking water salinity and a wider range of cardiovascular outcomes in a single comprehensive study, (b) systematically evaluate risk estimates based on various study-level characteristics and (c) identify gaps in the existing evidence.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This study was conducted based on methods and reporting criteria described in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines for systematic reviews (detailed in online supplemental appendix 1).18 We comprehensively searched electronic databases including MEDLINE, Embase and Web of Science electronic databases from inception till 10 May 2025 (date of last search). First, we included observational studies (cross-sectional, case–control and cohort) where sodium in drinking water was used as the exposure factor and reported cardiovascular outcomes (including hypertension, fatal or non-fatal coronary heart disease (CHD) and stroke). Second, the included studies were required to provide quantitative measures that detail the association between sodium exposure and outcomes. Finally, we included only peer-reviewed publications and excluded experimental studies, reviews, books, conference abstracts and grey literature. Since our focus was solely on cardiovascular outcomes, we excluded studies that focused solely on all-cause mortality.

The computer-based searches combined search terms related to water salinity exposure (eg, “salinity”, “sodium”, “salt”, “sodium chloride”, “climate change” and “water”, “drinking water”, “potable water”), CVD outcomes of interest (eg, “blood pressure”, “high blood pressure”, “hypertension”, “coronary heart disease”, “myocardial infarction”, “stroke”, “cardiovascular diseases”), and study design (eg, “analytic”, “observational”, “cross-sectional”, “cohort”, “follow-up”, “prospective cohort”, “retrospective cohort”, “longitudinal”, “case-control study”), without any language restriction. Further studies were sought by manually searching for reference lists of the relevant articles. When relevant information was unavailable, efforts were made to contact corresponding authors. Details of the search strategy are presented in online supplemental appendix 2. Two independent reviewers independently screened the search results to assess conformity with selection criteria, with disagreements resolved with a third reviewer. In cases of multiple publications from a single study, we used the most up-to-date information.

Data extraction and quality of study assessment

Two investigators independently extracted data on the following: author, publication year, study design, country, study population, coastal and non-coastal location, sample size, age (years), sex, drinking water sodium level, method of salinity assessment, total sodium (food and liquid/water), type and number of CVD events, outcome assessment, measures of association (means and SD for continuous outcomes or fully adjusted ORs (with corresponding 95% CIs) for dichotomous variables), and covariates adjusted for. The quality of the studies included was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS).19 It uses a unique ’star system’ to evaluate studies across three domains: the method of selecting study groups, the comparability between these groups, and the determination of exposure or outcome variables in case–control and cohort studies, respectively.19 We also used a modified version of NOS for the cross-sectional study to ensure consistency.20 21 Quality rating was not a determining factor for a study’s inclusion in the systematic review.

Data synthesis and analysis

Summary measures of effect were presented as mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs for continuous outcomes (ie, systolic BP (SBP) and diastolic BP (DBP)) and ORs with 95% CIs for binary outcomes comparing the highest versus the lowest levels of water salinity. Studies that did not report levels of SBP and DBP or did not provide sufficient information to calculate the MDs were excluded from the meta-analysis. In cases where studies presented SBP, DBP and ORs with different levels of adjustment, we used the estimates with the most maximally adjusted adjustments. To combine the study-specific estimates, we used a random-effects meta-analysis that accounts for between-study variability. We assessed the extent of statistical heterogeneity across studies using standard χ² tests and the I2 statistic.22 The I2 values were interpreted by grouping these values into various heterogeneity categories, in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook guidelines: 0%–40% (‘low’), 30%–60% (‘moderate’), 50%–90% (‘high/substantial’) and 75%–100% (‘very high/considerable’) heterogeneity.23 Funnel plots and the Egger’s regression symmetry test were used to assess for publication bias.24 We explored prespecified study-level characteristics such as location, period of publication, study design, salinity assessment method, age and sex as potential sources of heterogeneity using stratified analysis and random-effects meta-regression.25 To ensure the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted focusing on specific aspects of the included studies. First, the study by Choi, which used secondary data from individuals in two distinct areas with differing sodium levels in the water, was included to test the consistency of the pooled estimates for SBP and DBP.26 Additionally, the study by Naser et al, which examined the influence of calcium and magnesium rather than sodium, was incorporated into the sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of broader exposure definitions on the association between salinity and hypertension.27 For studies investigating gestational hypertension, sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the association between high versus low water salinity and the risk of gestational hypertension or pre-eclampsia, considering the limited number of studies and variability in reported outcomes. Since all of the included studies reported results as ORs, no statistical transformation of HRs or risk ratios (RR) was needed. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p<0.05 was considered significant. All analyses were conducted by Stata V.17.0.

Association between salinity in drinking water and risk of BP

A total of 18 studies presented findings on salinity in drinking water and BP levels (table 1).12,1526 28 For the quantitative review, out of them, 11 of the studies provided relevant information to be included in the meta-analysis.1213 15 29,31 35 37 The pooled MD estimates (95% CIs) for BP, comparing higher versus lower water salinity, have been provided in figures2 3. Briefly, the pooled MD (95% CIs) was 3.22 mm Hg (1.11 to 5.33) for SBP, and was 2.82 mm Hg (1.44 to 4.20) for DBP (figures2 3). In the sensitivity analysis that involved the addition of the study by Choi,26 which had included secondary data based on individuals from two distinct areas with differing water sodium levels, the results were very comparable (online supplemental appendix 4; 5).

Association of salinity in drinking water with risks of hypertension

Six studies (two cohort and four cross-sectional studies) reported on the association between salinity in drinking water and the risk of hypertension (online supplemental appendix 6).2740 41 43,45 We did not include the study by Naser et al in the main analysis because it described the influence of calcium and magnesium on the odds of hypertension27; however, it was included in the sensitivity analysis. High versus low water salinity was associated with an increased risk of hypertension: pooled OR (95% CI) of 1.26 (1.07 to 1.48) (online supplemental appendix 7). In the sensitivity analysis by including the study by Naser et al, there was a modest decrease in the magnitude of the association observed: pooled OR (95% CI) of 1.15 (0.94 to 1.42) (online supplemental appendix 8). Additionally, three studies investigated the association between salinity in drinking water and the odds of gestational hypertension/pre-eclampsia.16 36 38 There was no strong evidence that high versus low water salinity increased the odds of gestational hypertension: pooled OR (95% CI) of 2.16 (0.63 to 7.40) (online supplemental appendix 7).

Salinity in drinking water and risk of other CVD outcomes

We found one study that reported on the association between salinity in drinking water and the risk of total CVD.42 The study ascertained only 10 CVD events based on participants’ self-report, thus preventing a more powerful and robust interpretation.42 The study examined the association between salinity exposure quartiles and hospital visits for CVD. compared with the lowest quartile of salinity exposure (Q1), the adjusted ORs for CVD-related hospital visits were 1.12 (95% CI 0.21 to 6.12) for Q2, 0.13 (95% CI 0.01 to 1.11) for Q3, and 1.64 (95% CI 0.25 to 10.99) for Q4, after adjusting for education and annual household income. Additionally, we found no study that had examined drinking water salinity in relation to CHD or stroke risk.

Meta-regression analyses

In the meta-regression analysis based on various study-level characteristics, the pooled MD estimates (95% CIs) for SBP levels, comparing higher versus lower water salinity, differed significantly by location, period of publication, area and study design, but not by age, gender or salinity assessment method (figure 4A). High versus low water salinity increased SBP levels in Asian populations, but not in other populations (p value for meta-regression=0.012), and in studies in the year 2000 and beyond, but not before the year 2000 (p value for meta-regression=0.002). By contrast, the pooled MD estimates (95% CIs) for DBP levels, comparing higher versus lower water salinity, differed significantly by study design and area, but not by location, period of publication, age, gender or salinity assessment method (figure 4B). Importantly, both SBP and DBP levels were significantly higher in coastal studies compared with non-coastal studies (p value for meta-regression <0.05). High versus low water salinity increased DBP levels in cross-sectional studies, with modest evidence for other study designs (p value for meta-regression <0.05). Comparing the studies published before and after 2000, the latter, more recent evidence showed significantly stronger positive associations between water salinity and risk of hypertension.

Assessment of study quality and publication bias

The quality of each included study, based on the systematic NOS assessment, is provided in online supplemental appendix 9. In this quality appraisal, the majority of the studies were in the moderate-to-high quality categories. Seven studies received the highest scores of 8–9, whereas 18 studies achieved a score of 7 stars. Only two studies scored 5 stars. Additionally, there was little or no evidence of publication bias in our study. The funnel plots for changes in SBP and DBP were symmetrical on visual inspection (online supplemental appendix 10). These observations were consistent with the Egger’s regression test (with p values of 0.63 and 0.44, respectively).

Discussion

Principal findings

We have conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis, using non-overlapping data from approximately 74 000 participants across 27 observational studies, to help clarify the available evidence on the associations of drinking water salinity with elevated BP and the risk of hypertension. Overall, our results suggest that elevated salinity in drinking water is associated with higher SBP and DBP levels, particularly among individuals residing in coastal areas. Additionally, drinking water salinity was associated with increased risk of hypertension, irrespective of age, sex and geographical location of the participants. By contrast, we found only one study on composite CVD and a lack of evidence on the association of drinking water salinity with risk of CHD and stroke—reflecting a key scientific need.

Mechanistic explanations and comparison with other studies

Several mechanistic pathways may explain the observed associations. First, it is well established that a higher sodium consumption through drinking water can cause changes in small resistant arteries, leading to increased systemic peripheral resistance and elevated blood pressure.46 Additionally, a higher salt intake can (a) reduce endothelial nitric oxide (a known vasodilator), (b) increase vascular stiffness in the large arteries47 and (c) modulate the autonomic neuronal supply and the sympathetic activity48—all of which can lead to increased BP.47 Finally, a higher sodium intake increases water retention in the body, which elevates circulating blood volume. This, in turn, leads to a higher cardiac output and a higher kidney perfusion pressure, triggering the pressure-natriuresis compensatory action.49 However, if a person has renal insufficiency, this sodium-excretion ability is impaired, leading to hypertension.49 50

These results generally align with earlier narrative reviews showing significantly higher average SBP and DBP levels in high salinity areas, such as coastal regions, compared with low salinity areas.11 Our findings are also in line with previous randomised controlled trials evidence, which shows an approximately linear relationship between sodium intake and both SBP and DBP across the entire range of dietary sodium exposure.51 52

Strengths and limitations of the study

Strengths and limitations of this work deserve careful consideration. This is the first comprehensive meta-analysis of drinking water salinity and its relation to the risk of CVD outcomes. Although the included studies were generally diverse, we examined and identified some potential sources of this heterogeneity, including location and study design. Most studies relied on a single assessment of exposure. To improve consistency in long-term exposure assessment, future research should consider using repeated measurements of drinking water salinity over time, which can account for any individual variation in levels (ie, regression dilution) studies.53 Additionally, in the included studies where hypertension was the outcome of interest, the definition of high BP was consistent across the studies (SBP ≥140 mm Hg or DBP ≥90 mm Hg). Therefore, the pooled effect estimates we calculated for hypertension based on these studies are unlikely to be affected by any subsequent change in the hypertension definition. We were also unable to standardise the study-level OR estimates (eg, by thirds or per SD increase in salinity level) to make a more consistent comparison because the majority of the relevant cohort studies did not provide all of the study-level data (eg, SD, SEs or number of outcomes) required for the risk conversion approach. Therefore, we used risk comparisons between the highest versus lowest level of drinking water salinity—an approach we have used in previous similar meta-analyses.54,57 This approach is considered reliable since it has been shown that pooled estimates from transformed and untransformed RR estimates are broadly similar.58 Finally, although this review is limited to published findings, using individual participant data from future large-scale primary studies would enable a more detailed and specific assessment of the relationship between increased salinity and CVD risk. This could include evaluating the roles of different exposure routes (eg, drinking, cooking, foods), applying standardised adjustments for confounders (such as concurrent traditional and environmental risk factors for CVD like environmental toxic metals), reducing heterogeneity in meta-analyses of diverse study populations (eg, by conducting studies in understudied regions like Latin America), and providing a more consistent characterisation of any potential dose-response relationship. These comprehensive assessments are currently in progress (eg, 5C cohort study in Colombia).

Scientific and policy implications

Our findings may have important policy and scientific implications. First, our findings highlight the potential importance of drinking water salinity in increasing the risk of hypertension, a major global disease and a leading risk factor for CHD, stroke and other non-communicable diseases (NCDs) beyond the roles of conventional behavioural risk factors such as tobacco use, unhealthy diet and physical inactivity. In this regard, sodium intake through drinking water salinity is not yet recognised as a potential risk factor for hypertension in any hypertension prevention efforts guideline.59,61 Additionally, current global NCD prevention strategies mainly focus on behavioural and metabolic factor determinants.62 63 Therefore, our results may highlight the critical importance of recognising environmental factors, such as increasing drinking water salinity, as additional priorities in future policy development. These efforts could also have co-benefits for communicable diseases, since elevated drinking water salinity has been linked to higher rates of acute respiratory infections, diarrhoeal diseases and the spread of mosquitoborne illnesses.64

Second, although we observed modest MDs (ie, 2–3 mm Hg in SBP/DBP), they have significant implications. Large-scale prospective cohorts show that every 2 mm Hg lower SBP is associated with a 7%–10% reduction in cardiovascular deaths,65 while randomised trials show near-linear risk reductions down to normotensive levels.66 Therefore, even small increases in BP attributable to drinking water salinity could significantly raise cardiovascular risk in affected populations. Given that tens of millions of people worldwide depend on saline-intruded groundwater, the overall burden could be considerable. Third, our findings underscore a significant scientific gap: a lack of population-based studies investigating drinking water salinity in relation to clinical CVD outcomes (such as CHD and stroke). Detailed, adequately powerful studies, in this regard, are needed to determine (a) the relative contributions of drinking water salinity to CVD outcomes in coastal regions, (b) the magnitude and shape of such associations, and (c) health-based guideline values for drinking water salinity. For the latter, the WHO currently recommends a guideline value of 200 mg/L for drinking water salinity.67 This guideline is, however, based on taste thresholds rather than health-based standards, further emphasising the need for relevant scientific evidence.

Fourth, we observed a somewhat greater magnitude of risk among the studies based in Asia compared with other settings. This discrepancy, however, can potentially be explained by differences in baseline salinity exposure levels across settings. For example, in coastal Bangladesh, sodium concentrations in groundwater have already reached 2600 mg/L27 38 40 41—that is, a 2–6 fold higher than maximal levels reported in studies from Europe, North America or Australia (where the average levels are typically below 400 mg/L level).28 31 33 34 Communities living in these Asian coastal areas might also face increased salinity exposure from (a) consuming locally produced rice and vegetables with high sodium content and (b) using salty water for cooking—adding to the overall risk exposure. Therefore, additional research involving non-Asian coastal regions (such as those in sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America and small island nations) is necessary for a more comprehensive assessment. Finally, our findings support considering future strategies that will be mutually beneficial for environmental resilience and disease prevention. If supported by future scientific studies, such efforts may include (a) targeted screening for drinking water quality and hypertension risk assessments in people exposed to saline water intrusion, and (b) context-specific adaptation strategies with co-benefits for cardiometabolic health such as health practices and awareness interventions to promote environmental sustainability and optimal BP control especially in climate-vulnerable coastal areas. Additional interventions to reduce drinking water salinity have been suggested, including nature-based solutions (eg, rainwater harvesting), pond sand filters, managed aquifer recharge and tube wells.6568,70 However, these potential solutions need further contextualisation and evaluation, especially to assess their health effectiveness and implementation outcomes, along with any unintended consequences.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that increased drinking water salinity is significantly associated with elevated levels of SBP and DBP, and a greater risk of hypertension, especially among people living in coastal areas. More studies are needed to measure the connection with clinical cardiovascular outcomes. Additionally, with rising sea levels caused by ongoing global climate change potentially speeding up saline water intrusion, tailored adaptation strategies with co-benefits for maintaining optimal BP, particularly for the most vulnerable coastal populations, are necessary.
