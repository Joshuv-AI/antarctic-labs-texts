Effective cefiderocol CSF penetration for treating Stenotrophomonas maltophilia ventriculitis
Authors: Clara Ribera, Ariadna Padullés, Fe Tubau, Luisa Corral, Maria Nuñez Garate, Carmen Cabellos, Alexandre Vilalta, Jordi Càmara, Sara Cobo-Sacristán, Gabriela Abelenda-Alonso, Fabio Di Paolo, Evelyn Shaw, Mireia Puig-Asensio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBacterial ventriculitis associated with CSF shunts or drains is a life-threatening infection often caused by MDR pathogens such as Stenotrophomonas maltophilia. Cefiderocol, an IV siderophore cephalosporin approved for drug-resistant Gram-negative infections, lacks extensive clinical data supporting its use in CNS infections.ObjectivesWe describe a case of S. maltophilia ventriculitis successfully treated with cefiderocol monotherapy at a standard dose of 2 g IV every 8 h. Data on cefiderocol CSF drug penetration are reported.MethodsCefiderocol trough plasma and CSF concentrations (Cmin) were determined at steady-state conditions, and S. maltophilia susceptibility testing was determined using Etest and disc diffusion.ResultsTherapeutic drug monitoring showed sustained CSF cefiderocol concentrations. CSF cefiderocol Cmin remained 116.0- and 131.8-fold above the isolate’s MIC throughout the dosing interval, with plasma-to-CSF penetration ratios of 16.6%–25.7%. Patient’s clinical cure was achieved after 26 days of therapy without adverse events or relapse at 1 year of follow-up.ConclusionsOur data show that cefiderocol at a standard dose of 2 g IV every 8 h achieved sustained therapeutic concentrations in the CSF. Cefiderocol monotherapy may be an option for CNS infections with MDR Gram-negative bacteria with low MICs such as S. maltophilia.

Introduction

Bacterial ventriculitis associated with CSF shunts or external ventricular drains (EVDs) is a severe, life-threatening infection that requires prompt diagnosis and initiation of appropriate antimicrobial therapy.1,2 Stenotrophomonas maltophilia is an uncommon cause of nosocomial CSF infections; however, its intrinsic MDR pattern—particularly to the first-line β-lactams employed in the treatment of bacterial ventriculitis—makes its management particularly challenging.3

In 2020, the EMA approved cefiderocol, an IV siderophore cephalosporin, for treating carbapenem-resistant aerobic Gram-negative bacteria and carbapenem-resistant non-fermenting Gram-negative bacilli, including Acinetobacter spp, Pseudomonas spp and Stenotrophomonas spp. This agent is currently recommended for complicated urinary tract infections, nosocomial pneumonia and bacteraemia in adult patients with limited therapeutic options.4

To date, cefiderocol has not been approved for treating CNS infections.5 Evidence regarding its capacity to penetrate the blood–brain barrier is scarce, and clinical experience with CNS infections is limited to case reports describing successful management of MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii meningitis or ventriculitis. Additional data are needed to better define optimal dosing regimens and CNS pharmacokinetics in these complex and difficult-to-treat infections. Previous reports have used heterogeneous dosing strategies (2 g every 4 or 6 h—particularly in patients with augmented renal clearance—or 2 g every 8 h) in 3 h extended infusion, whereas others did not describe the recommended 3 h extended infusion,6–11 which is essential to achieve pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) targets.4,12,13 Also, all reported cases of CNS infection have involved concomitant use of other intraventricular or systemic antibiotics, making it challenging to assess the microbiological efficacy of cefiderocol monotherapy.6–11,14

Here, we describe a case of Stenotrophomonas maltophilia ventriculitis successfully treated with cefiderocol monotherapy, including quantitative data on CSF drug penetration. This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (PR186/25) with a waiver of patient’s written informed consent.

Case report

We report the case of a 63-year-old woman admitted with a Fisher grade IV subarachnoid haemorrhage, who underwent emergency EVD placement upon hospital admission.

On hospital Day 6, there was an increase in C-reactive protein (CRP) and leucocytosis without fever. Empirical IV piperacillin/tazobactam was initiated. The CSF culture was negative. On hospital Days 8–9, the patient developed persistent fever, prompting repetition of the CSF analysis obtained through the EVD. Biochemical parameters were as follows: protein 0.36 g/L (reference range: 0.15–0.45), glucose 5.1 mmol/L (2.2–4.1), leucocytes 0.029 × 109/L (0.000–0.005) 51.8% neutrophils, and erythrocytes 0.0094 × 1012/L. A CSF infection was suspected and the EVD was replaced. CSF cultures were positive for S. maltophilia, which showed the following MICs: cefiderocol = 0.016 mg/L by Etest (cefiderocol 30 µg inhibition zone = 35 mm), levofloxacin ≤1 mg/L, colistin ≤2 mg/L and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole ≤2/38 mg/L.

Piperacillin/tazobactam was discontinued, and targeted treatment with IV levofloxacin 500 mg every 12 h and IV trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole 15 mg/kg (360 mg of trimethoprim component every 6 h) was initiated.

After 24 h of targeted antibiotic therapy the patient’s fever resolved. On hospital Day 12, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole was discontinued due to drug-induced metabolic acidosis, and levofloxacin was maintained in monotherapy. A CSF culture obtained the same day was negative, and the CRP levels decreased. At this point, the initial growth of S. maltophilia was reinterpreted as possible contamination and levofloxacin was discontinued on hospital Day 14.

Forty-eight hours after discontinuing antibiotics, a control CSF culture was performed. S. maltophilia was again detected and levofloxacin was restarted. Subsequent routine CSF cultures continued to show persistent growth of S. maltophilia. On hospital Day 19, levofloxacin was switched to cefiderocol 2 g every 8 h administered as 3 h extended infusion (estimated glomerular filtration rate CKD-EPI = 111 mL/min/1.73 m2).

Given the limited evidence for cefiderocol penetration into the CSF, simultaneous CSF and plasma samples were collected on treatment Days 2 and 7 after starting cefiderocol (corresponding to hospital Days 21 and 26, respectively) (Table 1). The CSF analysis showed negligible leucocyte counts on both days (<0.002 × 109/L and 0.004 × 109/L, respectively) and protein levels within the normal range (0.39 and 0.29 g/L). Cefiderocol trough plasma and CSF concentrations (Cmin) were determined at steady state, taking into account the reported plasma half-life of approximately 2–3 h as indicated in the product label. Detectable cefiderocol Cmin values were observed in both CSF samples: 2.1 mg/L on Day 2 and 1.8 mg/L on Day 7. The corresponding plasma-to-CSF penetration ratios were 16.6% and 25.7%, respectively. CSF cefiderocol Cmin remained 131.8- and 116.0-fold above the MIC of the isolate throughout the dosing interval.

CSF culture obtained 3 days after initiation of cefiderocol yielded S. maltophilia. The first negative CSF culture was documented 6 days after cefiderocol start (hospital Day 25), at which time the EVD was again replaced. Subsequent CSF cultures remained negative, with a second EVD exchange performed on hospital Day 58, when a definitive ventriculoperitoneal shunt was placed. Cefiderocol therapy was completed over a 26 day course (from hospital Days 19 to 45) achieving patient’s clinical cure without adverse events. Patient’s glomerular filtration rate always remained stable (CKD-EPI 106–117 mL/min/1.73 m2). No relapse was observed 1 year after the end of treatment.

Discussion

In this real-life experience, we observed sustained therapeutic penetration of cefiderocol into the CSF compartment, supporting its role for treating S. maltophilia ventriculitis. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of S. maltophilia CNS infection successfully managed with cefiderocol monotherapy, achieving documented microbiological cure. Previous case reports describing cefiderocol for CNS infections involved isolates with higher MIC values (i.e. P. aeruginosa or A. baumannii), often used high cefiderocol dosing (2 g IV every 6 h), and always in combination with other systemic or intraventricular antibiotics (Table 2).6–11,14

In our case, cefiderocol was administered at the standard dose of 2 g every 8 h as a 3 h extended infusion. Cefiderocol achieved sustained therapeutic CSF concentrations throughout treatment, with Cmin values >100-fold above the MIC of the isolate (0.016 mg/L). The established PK/PD targets for efficacy require a free drug time above the MIC (fT > MIC) of at least 75%.12,13 Our findings show that exposure consistently reached 100% fT > 4 times MIC, suggesting that standard dosing is sufficient for pathogens with low MICs, such as S. maltophilia.

Our data show a penetration rate ranging from 16% to 26%, within the range of the variability described in the published literature (4% to 68%).6–11 Cefiderocol’s structure is related to both ceftazidime and cefepime, but it has a larger molecular size, plasma-protein binding (53%–65%) and hydrophilicity, which could be unfavourable in terms of CSF penetration. Although there is high intra- and inter-individual variability, our results indicate cefiderocol’s penetration rate is higher than ceftazidime (2%–8%) and similar to cefepime (20%).15–17

Compared with previous reports, we administered cefiderocol as monotherapy, which proved sufficient to achieve sustained negative follow-up CSF cultures. The infection was caused by a strain with a low cefiderocol MIC, which may have contributed to favourable PK/PD target attainment and microbiological success. It is unclear if combination therapy, higher dosing regimens or even intraventricular administration might be needed for isolates with higher MIC values. Also, differences in ventricular size or variable CSF clearance as result of CSF drainage might cause variable cefiderocol CSF concentrations between patients and affect its efficacy. Although this might influence the generalizability of our findings, this limitation is common to other antibiotics and our results are consistent with the CSF variability described in previous reports of patients with EVDs. Moreover, the CSF profile was consistent with a minimally inflamed state throughout during Days 2 to 7 of cefiderocol treatment. Therefore, variations in CSF pleocytosis are unlikely to account for the differences in cefiderocol penetration ratios observed.

In all, we provide new insights into the PK/PD target attainment and microbiological efficacy of cefiderocol in CNS infections. The simultaneous measurement of plasma and CSF concentrations confirmed significant, although variable CNS penetration.

Conclusions

Our data show that cefiderocol at a standard dose of 2 g IV every 8 h in extended infusion achieves sustained therapeutic concentrations in the CSF, surpassing the PK/PD target against S. maltophilia. However, there is high interindividual variability in the penetration rate, which should be considered when designing treatments for infections caused by MDR bacteria. These findings support the role of cefiderocol as a therapeutic option for CNS infections caused by MDR Gram-negative pathogens. However, its use as monotherapy may be considered for organisms with low MICs where PK/PD target attainment is plausible, such as certain S. maltophilia strains.
