Estimation of Parameters of the Truncated Normal Distribution with Unknown Bounds
Dylan Borchert, Semhar Michael, Christopher Saunders

Abstract
Estimators of parameters of truncated distributions, namely the truncated normal distribution, have been widely studied for a known truncation region. There is also literature for estimating the unknown bounds for known parent distributions. In this work, we develop a novel algorithm under the expectation-solution (ES) framework, which is an iterative method of solving nonlinear estimating equations, to estimate both the bounds and the location and scale parameters of the parent normal distribution utilizing the theory of best linear unbiased estimates from location-scale families of distribution and unbiased minimum variance estimation of truncation regions. The conditions for the algorithm to converge to the solution of the estimating equations for a fixed sample size are discussed, and the asymptotic properties of the estimators are characterized using results on M- and Z-estimation from empirical process theory. The proposed method is then compared to methods utilizing the known truncation bounds via Monte Carlo simulation.

The motivation for estimating all four parameters of the truncated normal distribution stems from the identification of source problems within forensic science. In this problem, the task is to decide whether two sets of observations came from the same class or two different classes. This task can be seen as a non-nested model selection or an open set classification problem. Specifically within the forensic science community, there has been a push for making this assignment using a likelihood ratio or a Bayes factor
[
16
,
1

[
31
,
32

A potential drawback to this approach is if there are observations from two sources that are relatively far apart from each other, either in the tails of the between-source distribution or from a subpopulation that was under-represented in the training dataset, which is far away from the bulk of the training data sources. Though the lens of the data seen, it is very unlikely these two sets of observations are from the same source; it is even more unlikely that two rare sources would be selected from the between-source distribution. This is one consequence of Lindley’s paradox, where a ratio of small (marginal) likelihoods can still return a very small or very large likelihood ratio or Bayes factor.

There was a lively discussion about Lindley’s paradox following Shafer’s reply
[
37
]
, to Lindley’s paper
[

that proposed a Bayes factor for a problem in forensic science related to an open set classification problem applied to refractive index glass data. In this discussion, Degroot stated,

All good Bayesian statisticians reserve a little pinch of probability for the possibility that their model is wrong. Usually this possibility does not have to be acknowledged formally in the statistical analyses that are carried out, but every now and then, when an unusual value of Y is observed, it is important to reconsider the model and to evaluate that little pinch of probability
[
12

.

We propose truncating the within-source distribution as one way of incorporating this “pinch of probability.” This would allow for the likelihood that two sets of observations from rare sources become zero if the two sets of observations are far enough from each other in some sense. To begin developing this framework, we first consider studying the closed set classification problem under the frequentist setting, where classes are modeled using the truncated normal distribution. This allows for a new observation to have zero likelihood in all the known classes if it is too far from the exemplars in these classes. To do this, the parameters of the truncated normal distribution, both the mean and standard deviation of the parent normal distribution, as well as the truncation bounds.

In this paper, we propose estimators of the parameters of the truncated normal distribution, when the truncation bounds are unknown, as the solution to a system of equations set up from the theory of regression on the order statistics of a location-scale family of distributions and uniform minimum variance unbiased estimation of truncation bounds for a known parent distribution. The estimators are found using a numerical solution to the system of equations found with a proposed algorithm under the Expectation-Solution (ES) scheme, which is a generalization of the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm

Following notation from

3.2
Consistency of Parameter Estimates

The following results show that when a sample is from a truncated normal distribution, the proposed estimators of the truncated normal distribution provide consistent estimates, as convergence in probability, of the parameters of the truncated normal distribution that generated the data. Lemma 4

Theorem 2

Lemma 5

(Gnedenko as Presented in
[
11
]
)
.

Let
X_{n:n}
be the largest order statistic from a sample of size
n
from a population with distribution function
F
. There exists constants

and

such that
\frac{1}{a_{n}}(X_{n:n}-b_{n})\rightsquigarrow G_{\alpha}(x)=\exp\{-(-x)^{\alpha}\},x\leq 0,\alpha>0
, where

is the distribution function of the
\text{Weibull}(\alpha)
distribution if and only if the upper bound
\xi_{1}
is finite and

\lim_{x\rightarrow 0+}\frac{1-F(\xi_{1}-tx)}{1-F(\xi_{1}-x)}=t^{\alpha}

for every

Moreover, the standardizing constant
a_{n}
, making

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{Pr}(X_{n:n}\leq\xi_{1}+a_{n}x)=G_{\alpha}(x)

may be chosen as

a_{n}=\xi_{1}-F^{-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right).

Theorem 3
.

Define the random function
\Psi_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=U^{obs}((X_{n:1},\dots,X_{n:n})^{t},\boldsymbol{\theta}).
If

X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\sim\text{TN}(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0},\tau_{l0},\tau_{u0}),

and

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, where
W\sim\text{Weibull(1)}.

Proof.

By Theorem
2
, we have
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Now the truncated normal distribution is bounded above by
\tau_{u0}
. Consider

\displaystyle\frac{1-F(\tau_{u0}-tx)}{1-F(\tau_{u0}-x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\frac{\Phi(\frac{\tau_{u0}-tx-\mu_{0})}{\sigma_{0}})-\Phi(\tau_{l0}^{*})}{\Phi(\tau_{u0^{*}})-\Phi(\tau_{l0}^{*})}}{1-\frac{\Phi(\frac{\tau_{u0}-x-\mu_{0})}{\sigma_{0}})-\Phi(\tau_{l0}^{*})}{\Phi(\tau_{u0}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l0}^{*})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi(\tau_{u0}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{u0}^{*}-\frac{tx}{\sigma_{0}})}{\Phi(\tau_{u0}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{u0}^{*}-\frac{x}{\sigma_{0}})}

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow 0+}\frac{1-F(\tau_{u0}-tx)}{1-F(\tau_{u0}-x)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\rightarrow 0+}\frac{\frac{t}{\sigma_{0}}\phi(\tau_{u0}^{*}-\frac{tx}{\sigma_{0}})}{\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}}\phi(\tau_{u0}^{*}-\frac{x}{\sigma_{0}})}

n\rightarrow\infty
. Then, as

n\rightarrow\infty

5
Real Data Application

As discussed earlier, the motivation for want to estimate all four of the parameters of the truncated normal distribution lies in an application to forensic science in the form of an open set classification problem in hopes that incorporating truncation into the models will alleviate Lindley’s paradox seen in those applications. Here, a simplification of this problem will be shown in the form of a closed set classification problem, highlighting a desirable property using truncation. The idea is that truncation will allow for the likelihood to be zero when a new observation is too far from the exemplars for each class used to fit the models. This truncated normal discriminant analysis is closely related to quadratic discriminant analysis with atypicality scoring
[
27
]
, where the atypicality is built into the sampling models.

The example uses the iris data
[
2
,
18
]
focusing only on the variable petal length. The classes Setosa and Versicolor are split evenly into training and testing sets, with all of the Virginica added to the testing set. Using the training set, the parameters of the truncated normal distribution and the normal distribution are calculated for both classes. The parameters of the truncated normal distribution were estimated using the proposed algorithm with initial value
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}=(\bar{x},s,x_{n:1},x_{n:n})
for each class. Predictions are then made on the testing set using the truncated normal discriminant analysis and QDA with an atypicality cutoff of 0.05. This process is repeated 10,000 times with different training/testing splits on the Setosa and Versicolor classes.

6
Conclusion

The proposed algorithm provides numerical solutions to a system of equations to yield estimators of the parameters of the truncated normal distribution. These estimators are consistent. The marginal asymptotic distributions of the estimators of the truncation bounds can be characterized using the Weibull distribution and converge on order
n^{-1}
. The joint distribution of the estimators of the mean and standard deviation of the parent normal distribution is multivariate normal and converges on order
n^{-1/2}.
With these different rates of convergence, there is an open question about the joint distribution, and if
(nf_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}(\tau_{l0})(\hat{\tau}_{ln}-\tau_{u0}),nf_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}(\tau_{u0})(\hat{\tau}_{un}-\tau_{l0}))^{t}
and
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\mu}_{n}-\mu_{0},\hat{\sigma}_{n}-\sigma_{0})^{t}
are independent in the limit.

Fitting a truncated normal distribution to classes allows for a resulting discriminant analysis that is robust to the addition of observations from classes that have not yet been seen. Future work entails extending these methods to multivariate and hierarchical models to provide a likelihood ratio as one method to incorporate the “pinch of probability” discussed by Degroot.

Appendix A
Standard Truncated Normal Distributions

Some useful results from the standard truncated normal distributions are used for various results of the algorithm.

A.1
Quantile Function

The quantile function for the standard truncated normal distribution
\text{TN}(0,1,\tau_{l}^{*},\tau_{u}^{*})
takes the form

J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(p)=\Phi^{-1}\left(\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})+p(\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*}))\right),\;p\in[0,1],

(16)

where
\Phi
is the distribution function of the standard normal distribution. Now, some useful properties of
J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
are as follows.

Property 1
.

J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is Lipschitz.

Proof.

Consider
J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
as defined in (
16
). We have

\frac{\partial J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial p}=\frac{1}{\phi(\Phi^{-1}\left(\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})+p(\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*}))\right))}(\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})).

Now as the standard normal density function,
\phi
is continuous, positive, increasing on
(-\infty,0)
and decreasing on
(0,\infty)
and
\Phi^{-1}
is increasing we have

\frac{\partial J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial p}\leq\frac{1}{\phi(\max\{|\tau_{l}^{*}|,|\tau_{u}^{*}|\})}(\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*}))\equiv M(\boldsymbol{\theta}).

Now for
p_{1},p_{2}\in[0,1],
by the Mean Value Theorem, there is a
p^{*}\in(0,1)
such that

|J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(p_{1})-J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(p_{1})|=\frac{\partial J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{\partial p}\Bigg|_{p=p^{*}}|p_{1}-p_{2}|\leq M|p_{1}-p_{2}|.

Thus,
J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is Lipschitz.
∎

Property 2
.

The weights in (
8
) satisfy
\xi(n,h_{l}(n,\boldsymbol{\theta}),h_{u}(n,\boldsymbol{\theta}),k)=J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(p)
for some
p\in[\frac{i-1}{n},\frac{i}{n}].

Proof.

Recall that
\xi(n,h_{l}(n,\boldsymbol{\theta}),h_{u}(n,\boldsymbol{\theta}),k)
is the median of the
\text{Beta}(h_{l}(n,\boldsymbol{\theta})+k,h_{u}(n,\boldsymbol{\theta}),k)+n+1-k)\overset{d}{=}\text{Beta}(nc_{l}+k,nc_{u}+n+1-k)
distributions for
k=1,\dots,n
, where
c_{l}
and
c_{u}
are defined as in (
2
) and (
3
). Denote the mode and mean of the
k^{th}
beta distribution as
m(k)
and
\mu(k)
, respectively. Now

\frac{c_{l}+\frac{k-1}{n}}{c_{l}+c_{u}+1+\frac{1}{n}}<\frac{c_{l}+\frac{k-1}{n}}{c_{l}+c_{u}+1}<\frac{c_{l}+\frac{k-1}{n}}{c_{l}+c_{u}+1-\frac{1}{n}}

which implies

\mu(k-1)<\frac{c_{l}+\frac{k-1}{n}}{c_{l}+c_{u}+1}<M(k)

(17)

As
c_{u}+c_{l}+1-\frac{1}{n}>0
. Similarly, as
c_{u}>0,

\left(c_{l}+\frac{k-1}{n}\right)(c_{l}+c_{u}+1)+\frac{1}{n}\left(c_{l}+\frac{k-1}{n}\right)<\left(c_{l}+\frac{k-1}{n}\right)(c_{l}+c_{u}+1)+\frac{1}{n}\left(c_{l}+c_{u}+1\right),

yielding

\frac{c_{l}+\frac{k-1}{n}}{c_{l}+c_{u}+1}<\frac{c_{l}+\frac{k}{n}}{c_{l}+c_{u}+\frac{1}{n}}

which gives

\frac{c_{l}+\frac{k-1}{n}}{c_{l}+c_{u}+1}<\mu(k).

(18)

Finally, consider a third inequality

\left(c_{l}+\frac{k}{n}\right)(c_{l}+c_{u}+1)-\frac{1}{n}\left(c_{l}+c_{u}+1\right)<\left(c_{l}+\frac{k}{n}\right)(c_{l}+c_{u}+1)-\frac{1}{n}\left(c_{l}+\frac{k}{n}\right),

again as
c_{l}+c_{u}+1-\frac{1}{n}
, it follows that

\frac{c_{l}+\frac{k-1}{n}}{c_{l}+c_{u}+1-\frac{1}{n}}<\frac{c_{l}+\frac{k}{n}}{c_{l}+c_{u}+1}

giving

M(k)<\frac{c_{l}+\frac{k}{n}}{c_{l}+c_{u}+1}.

(19)

Now, combining (
17
) , (
18
) and (
19
) it has been shown that

\frac{c_{l}+\frac{k-1}{n}}{c_{l}+c_{u}+1}<\mu(k)<\frac{c_{l}+\frac{k}{n}}{c_{l}+c_{u}+1}

and

\frac{c_{l}+\frac{k-1}{n}}{c_{l}+c_{u}+1}<M(k)<\frac{c_{l}+\frac{k}{n}}{c_{l}+c_{u}+1}.

Now, for all values of
k,
both parameters of the Beta distributions are greater than one, so the distributions will exhibit a mode-median-mean inequality or a mean-median-mode inequality
[
19
,
4
]
. Then, after re-substituting
c_{l}
and
c_{u}
and simplifying we have that

J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)=\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{c_{l}+\frac{k}{n}}{c_{l}+c_{u}+1}\right).

Thus,

J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\frac{k-1}{n}\right)<\xi(n,h_{l}(n,\boldsymbol{\theta}),h_{u}(n,\boldsymbol{\theta}),k)<J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right).

∎

Note that property 2 also gives that the sequence of weights is an increasing sequence in
k
, and that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi(n,h_{l}(n,\boldsymbol{\theta}),h_{u}(n,\boldsymbol{\theta}),k)\rightarrow\int_{0}^{1}J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(p)dp,\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty

and

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi(n,h_{l}(n,\boldsymbol{\theta}),h_{u}(n,\boldsymbol{\theta}),k)^{2}\rightarrow\int_{0}^{1}J_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(p)^{2}dp,\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty

by the definition of the Riemann integral.

A.2
Moments

The moments of the standard truncated normal distributions are also of interest. Now, the moment generating function of the standard truncated normal distribution is

\varphi(t)=\exp\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{2}\right\}\frac{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*}-t)-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*}-t)}{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})},

(20)

where
s(t)=\frac{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*}-t)-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*}-t)}{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}.

Now, the first four moments of the standard truncated normal distribution will be of interest. Consider the first four derivatives of
\varphi
.

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=t\varphi(t)-\exp\{t^{2}/2\}s^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=(1-t^{2})\varphi(t)+2t\varphi^{\prime}(t)+\exp\{t^{2}/2\}s^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\varphi^{(3)}(t)

\displaystyle=(t^{3}-3t)\varphi(t)+(3-3t^{2})\varphi^{\prime}(t)+3t\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)+\exp\{t^{2}/2\}s^{(3)}(t)

\displaystyle\varphi^{(4)}(t)

\displaystyle=(6t-t^{4}-3)\varphi(t)+(4t^{3}-12t)\varphi^{\prime}(t)+(6-3t-3t^{2})\varphi^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.06749pt+4t\varphi^{(3)}(t)+\exp\{t^{2}/2\}s^{(4)}(t).

Now, the derivative of
s
evaluated at
0
are

\displaystyle s^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}

\displaystyle s^{\prime\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\tau_{u}^{*})-\phi^{\prime}(\tau_{l}^{*})}{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}

\displaystyle s^{(3)}(0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(\tau_{u}^{*})-\phi^{\prime\prime}(\tau_{l}^{*})}{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}

\displaystyle s^{(4)}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi^{(3)}(\tau_{u}^{*})-\phi^{(3)}(\tau_{l}^{*})}{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})},

which leads to the first four moments of the standard truncated normal distributions

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}\equiv\varphi^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{\phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\equiv\varphi^{\prime\prime}(0)=1+\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\tau_{u}^{*})-\phi^{\prime}(\tau_{l}^{*})}{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}

\displaystyle\alpha_{3}\equiv\varphi^{(3)}(0)=-3\frac{\phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}-\frac{\phi^{\prime\prime}(\tau_{u}^{*})-\phi^{\prime\prime}(\tau_{l}^{*})}{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}

\displaystyle\alpha_{4}\equiv\varphi^{(4)}(0)=3+6\frac{\phi^{\prime}(\tau_{u}^{*})-\phi^{\prime}(\tau_{l}^{*})}{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}+\frac{\phi^{(3)}(\tau_{u}^{*})-\phi^{(3)}(\tau_{l}^{*})}{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}.

Now this leads to property 3.

Property 3
.

The matrix
A=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}^{2}&\alpha_{3}-\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\\
\alpha_{3}-\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}&\alpha_{4}-\alpha_{2}^{2}\end{bmatrix}
is positive definite.

Proof.

Consider
Z\sim\text{TN}(0,1,\tau_{l}^{*},\tau_{u}^{*}).
The covariance matrix of
(Z,Z^{2})^{T}
is A, and therefore
A
is positive definite.
∎

Now, we refer to these distributions as the standard truncated normal distributions due to property 4.

Property 4
.

Let
Y\sim\text{TN}(\mu,\sigma,\tau_{l},\tau_{u})
and
Z\sim\text{TN}(0,1,\tau_{l}^{*},\tau_{u}^{*})
, where
\tau_{l}^{*}=\frac{\tau_{l}-\mu}{\sigma}
and
\tau_{u}^{*}=\frac{\tau_{u}-\mu}{\sigma}
. Then
Y\overset{d}{=}\mu+\sigma Z
.

Proof.

Consider the CDF of
Z

F_{Z}(t)=\frac{\Phi(t)-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}.
Then the CDF of
W=\mu+\sigma Z

F_{W}(t)=F_{Z}\left(\frac{t-\mu}{\sigma}\right)=\frac{\Phi\left(\frac{t-\mu}{\sigma}\right)-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}{\Phi(\tau_{u}^{*})-\Phi(\tau_{l}^{*})}

(21)

which is the CDF of the
\text{TN}(\mu,\sigma,\tau_{l},\tau_{u})

Y\overset{d}{=}\mu+\sigma Z.

∎
