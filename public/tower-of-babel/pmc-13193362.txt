Eating and Swallowing Issues in Students Enrolled in Special Education Schools for Intellectual Disabilities
Authors: Rena Hidaka, Ayako Nakane, Kazuharu Nakagawa, Haruka Tohara, Yuko Uchiyama, Shinya Wada, Chiyoko Hakuta, Koichiro Matsuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis study aimed to identify feeding‐related challenges among children attending special needs schools for students with intellectual disabilities.Methods and ResultsWe included 71 students in the first grade of elementary, junior high, and high schools at special‐needs schools for intellectual disabilities, along with their caregivers and teachers. A dental hygienist evaluated three feeding domains: pre‐feeding, mastication, and swallowing. Each item was scored on a five point scale, and the total scores were calculated. The presence of underlying medical conditions and hypersensitivity was also assessed. Caregivers and teachers also reported their perceived difficulties during students' mealtimes. Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) was the most common diagnosis (46.4%). Hypersensitivity was most prevalent among elementary school students (71%; p < 0.001). The mean total scores for three domains were 21.5, 16.6, and 21.3, for elementary; 25.2, 20.2, and 23.5, for junior high; and 26.1, 20.8, and 23.3, with significant differences observed between elementary students and the other groups. Common concerns reported by caregivers and teachers included unchewed swallowing, rapid eating, and selective eating.ConclusionStudents with intellectual disabilities in special needs schools experience challenges with pre‐feeding, mastication, and swallowing. These difficulties were especially prevalent among elementary level students compared to older age groups.

Introduction

Acquiring basic daily habits and fostering independence are central goals in the education of students with intellectual disabilities, and instructions related to eating are considered a particularly important component of this process [1]. Mealtime activities provide opportunities for the development of selfcare skills, as well as oral motor functions that support safe and efficient feeding.

Children with developmental and intellectual disabilities frequently experience feeding‐related difficulties. For example, children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) are known to exhibit strong food selectivity and may develop feeding disorders or malnutrition. [2, 3, 4, 5] In addition, children with Down syndrome have been reported to experience feeding difficulties across all phases of swallowing, including the oral, pharyngeal, and esophageal phases [6]. Feeding problems, such as food refusal, selective eating, and difficulties with chewing and swallowing, have also been widely reported among children with developmental disabilities [5]. Furthermore, delays in oral motor development and feeding skills have been documented in this population [7].

Although children with intellectual disabilities often experience delays in the development of feeding functions during early childhood, caregivers may fail to recognize these issues [8, 9]. Consequently, meals may be provided using inappropriate food textures or assistance methods that do not match the child's functional abilities. This mismatch can lead to further delays in oral motor development or the acquisition of maladaptive feeding patterns. However, such problems are often overlooked and misattributed solely to the child's disability, rather than to underlying developmental delays in oral function. However, it is also possible that this food selectivity stems from poorly developed oral functions, leading them to avoid foods that require advanced chewing or swallowing skills. These findings suggest the importance of evaluating the characteristics of disability and the child's actual feeding function when assessing feeding difficulties in children with intellectual disabilities.

In school settings, although it is often assumed that feeding difficulties diminish with age, distinguishing whether such challenges are individual differences or developmental issues is difficult [10]. Moreover, teachers frequently face difficulties such as a lack of training on feeding guidance, uncertainty regarding appropriate instructional methods, discrepancies between teacher and caregiver perceptions of a student's feeding abilities, and the challenge of providing individualized support within the limited time of school lunch periods [11]. Despite these concerns, the actual conditions in special‐needs schools for students with intellectual disabilities remain poorly understood.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate feeding‐related challenges by educational stage (elementary, junior high, and high school) among students with intellectual disabilities attending special‐needs schools. We hypothesized that the nature and extent of feeding difficulties would differ by age group and sought to provide data that could support teachers in delivering age appropriate feeding guidance.

Patients and Methods

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 2015) and the Ethical Guidelines for Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects (March 23, 2021, Japan). Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional ethics committee (approval number: D2021–014). An information sheet regarding this study was distributed to all participants, and informed consent was obtained. For child and student participants, consent was obtained from their legal representatives. Trial registration: UMIN‐CTR (UMIN000044176).

Study Design and Participants

This study was designed as a cross sectional observational study and was reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines [12]. A study was conducted between April and June 2022 to identify feeding‐related challenges among first‐year students of special‐needs schools for children with intellectual disabilities in Tokyo, Japan. Seventy‐one students (elementary, junior high, and high school), 71 caregivers, and 34 first year homeroom teachers (elementary, junior high, and high school) participated in the study. The inclusion criteria were students enrolled in the special education school for individuals with intellectual disabilities. The exclusion criteria included students with acute medical conditions that could interfere with feeding observation. Each student was observed by a trained investigator during multiple school lunch periods (approximately 2–3 sessions per student). The observations focused on feeding behaviors, including lip closure, food intake using the lips, mastication, bolus formation, and swallowing patterns during regular school lunches.

In addition, caregivers and teachers completed structured questionnaires regarding the students’ feeding and swallowing conditions. Data on the students’ disability types and severity were obtained from school health examination records. The level of intellectual disability was classified according to the records of the Ai‐no‐Techo (a disability certificate issued by the Tokyo Metropolitan Government), which categorizes intellectual disability into four levels, ranging from Level 4 (mild) to Level 1 (profound). Information regarding dentition status (mixed or permanent dentition) was obtained from school health records.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation in the study. For child participants, written informed consent was obtained from their legal representatives.

Assessment of Feeding Challenges

Two calibrated dental hygienists conducted direct meal observations (meal rounds) during lunchtime in each school level (elementary, junior high, and high school). A feeding evaluation sheet was developed with reference to existing assessment tools for children with physical disabilities,[13, 14, 15] incorporating expert input from dentists, dental hygienists, and school nurses. The sheet assessed three domains (pre‐feeding, mastication, and swallowing) using a 5 point Likert scale. A score of 1 indicated “poor, completely unable, or present,” whereas 5 indicated “good, no problem, or absent,” with higher scores reflecting fewer difficulties. Each student was observed over multiple lunch periods, and the final scores were determined through consensus between the two dental hygienists.

Additionally, oral hypersensitivity was assessed through intraoral palpation by the dental hygienists before lunch or during recess.

Questionnaire

The caregiver questionnaire included items regarding the student's demographic characteristics, caregivers’ interest in feeding support, and perceived feeding difficulties during mealtimes (e.g., rapid eating, swallowing without chewing, and selective eating). Teachers completed a similar questionnaire assessing their perceptions of students’ feeding behaviors and difficulties during school lunches. The questionnaires for caregivers and teachers were developed specifically for this study. In addition to items on respondents’ characteristics, such as family composition and years of teaching experience, respondents were asked to select applicable items describing the child's feeding difficulties. The list of feeding‐related issues was designed to correspond to the feeding observation items assessed by dental hygienists and was developed based on feeding problems commonly recognized in children with intellectual disabilities by caregivers, school staff, and healthcare professionals. The questionnaires are provided in the supplementary material.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. The total scores of each feeding domain and individual sub‐item scores from the feeding evaluation sheet were compared across school levels using the Kruskal‐Wallis test. The internal consistency of the feeding evaluation sheet was assessed using Cronbach's alpha coefficient to evaluate the reliability of the instrument. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted.

Results

Seventy one first year students participated in the study: 22 from elementary school, 19 from the junior high school, and 30 from the high school. The most common primary diagnosis was ASD, followed by Down syndrome (Table 1). Regarding the severity of intellectual disability, three students had no official classification, whereas the remainder were distributed across Levels 4 to 2 based on the Ai‐no‐Techo system. Regarding dentition status, most elementary school students had mixed dentition (85.7%), whereas most junior high school (84.2%) and high school students (77.7%) had permanent dentition.

In the mastication domain, elementary students showed widespread difficulties, and significant differences were observed between them and other school levels. In contrast, there was little variation between junior high and high school students across all mastication items. For swallowing, elementary students showed issues with “lip closure,” “unchewed swallowing,” and “food pooling.” Similar tendencies were observed in junior high and high school students, although the differences in mean scores were not statistically significant.

The prevalence of oral hypersensitivity was highest among elementary students (15 students, 71.4%), followed by junior high school (9 students, 47.4%), and high school (7 students, 24.1%).

Discussion

This study investigated feeding‐related challenges among students with intellectual disabilities across different school levels. Elementary school students showed greater difficulties in pre‐feeding and mastication than junior high and high school students. These findings suggest that some feeding functions improve with age and experience, whereas others may persist because of disability‐related characteristics. Early screening and intervention during the elementary school years may therefore be important.

Feeding Difficulties Attributable to Disability Characteristics

We found that feeding difficulties in students with intellectual disabilities are influenced by both developmental stage and disability specific characteristics. In this study, ASD was the most common diagnosis. Previous studies have shown that children with ASD often exhibit food selectivity, sensory related feeding difficulties, and limited dietary variety [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Because these characteristics may persist into adolescence, they may partly explain why feeding scores improved from elementary to junior high school but changed little thereafter in the present study. This finding suggests that age‐related maturation alone may be insufficient to resolve feeding difficulties in some students.

We also observed substantial individual variability, particularly among students with Down syndrome and those in higher school levels. Children with Down syndrome commonly present with hypotonia, reduced lip and tongue strength, malocclusion, and tongue thrust swallowing, all of which may interfere with efficient chewing and bolus control [5, 6, 21, 22, 23, 24]. These findings indicate that feeding support for older students should be individualized according to functional ability rather than chronological age alone. Multidisciplinary collaboration among educators, caregivers, and healthcare professionals may be especially beneficial.

Feeding Challenges by School Level

Insufficient lip function was observed across all school levels, whereas mastication related difficulties were more prominent in elementary school students. This suggests that lip motor problems may persist over time, while chewing ability may gradually improve through growth and experience. Developmental changes in dentition during childhood may also contribute to reduced mastication efficiency in younger students [10]. These findings further suggest that feeding problems become more individualized with age. If appropriate chewing habits are not established early, maladaptive eating behaviors may persist. Early guidance on mastication and mealtime behaviors may therefore help reduce later difficulties.

Perceptions of Feeding Challenges by Caregivers and Teachers

Caregivers and teachers most frequently cited “rapid eating,” “unchewed swallowing,” and “food selectivity” as feeding concerns across all levels. These behaviors appear to be persistent and clinically relevant challenges in students with intellectual disabilities. Feeding difficulties may also have broader health implications. Previous studies have linked feeding and swallowing disorders with increased support needs, problematic eating behaviors, and reduced quality of life [25, 26]. In addition, children with intellectual disabilities may be at greater risk of overweight and lifestyle‐related diseases [27, 28, 29, 30]. Early management of feeding difficulties may therefore contribute not only to improved oral function, but also to better long‐term health outcomes.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study has some limitations. First, participants were recruited from a single special‐needs school, which may limit generalizability. Second, students with different disabilities were analyzed together, which may have obscured diagnosis specific differences. Third, questionnaire responses were subjective and may have been affected by recall bias. Finally, the cross‐sectional design precluded assessment of developmental changes or causal relationships. Future longitudinal and multicenter studies are needed to clarify developmental trajectories and evaluate the effectiveness of early intervention strategies.

Conclusion

Students with intellectual disabilities in special‐needs schools experience feeding challenges across multiple domains, including pre‐feeding, mastication, and swallowing. These challenges were particularly evident among elementary level students and may reflect developmental delays in oral motor function rather than school level alone. Early identification and targeted feeding support may help prevent the persistence of maladaptive feeding behaviors in later school stages.
