Prognostic Utility of the Dicrotic Notch Index in Patients Undergoing Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement
Authors: Odette Iskandar, Shivabalan Kathavaryan Ramu, Maryam Majeed-Saidan, Nikolos Spilias, Habib Layoun, James Yun, Grant Reed, Rishi Puri, Amar Krishnaswamy, Samir R Kapadia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe dicrotic notch (DN) on the central aortic pressure waveform reflects aortic valve closure. We aimed to assess the clinical utility of the DN index (DNI) in patients with severe aortic stenosis undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR).MethodsIn this retrospective study, we analyzed 569 patients with aortic stenosis who underwent TAVR between January 2019 and December 2020. Aortic waveform data were used to measure systolic, diastolic, and DN pressures, whereas DNI was calculated as (systolic pressure − DN)/pulse pressure. Pre-TAVR and post-TAVR hemodynamic parameters were compared, and survival analysis was performed using Cox regression with post-TAVR/pre-TAVR DNI ratio as an independent covariate.ResultsCompared to pre-TAVR, DN pressure (92.18 to 95.37, p < 0.001) and DNI (0.45 to 0.58, p < 0.001) significantly increased post TAVR. Higher baseline DNI was predicted by carotid disease, diabetes mellitus, and a higher ejection fraction at baseline. Post-TAVR to pre-TAVR DNI ratio cut point >1.19 is an independent predictor of event-free survival at 2 years. Patients with higher post-DNI/pre-DNI ratio had a significantly lower risk of all-cause mortality in adjusted analyses (hazard ratio: 0.53, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.34–0.82).ConclusionsMore than 20% increase in DNI post-TAVR was associated with a lower long-term risk of all-cause mortality. Further studies are needed to understand the determinants of the increase in DNI and if this can be modulated by procedural modifications.Keywords: Aortic stenosis, Dicrotic notch index, Invasive hemodynamics, TAVR

Introduction

The dicrotic notch (DN) is a distinctive feature of the arterial pressure waveform that marks the end of systole, typically appearing about one-third of the way down the descending limb.1 It corresponds to the physiological closure of the aortic valve.1,2 In patients with aortic stenosis (AS), several hemodynamic features have been described, including delayed peak aortic pressure, narrow pulse pressure (PP), prolonged systolic ejection time, a smoothed or blunted DN, and a reduced slope of the ascending limb.1 The prominence and morphology of the DN in AS are influenced by factors such as age, ejection fraction, and, specific AS subtypes.1 A diminished DN has also been associated with increased arterial stiffness and correlates with all-cause mortality.3 Recently, the time between left ventricular (LV) and aortic systolic pressure peaks has been shown to be a reliable hemodynamic biomarker to identify true severe AS and has been demonstrated to offer incremental diagnostic value.4

Given the inconclusive evidence pertaining to the utility of echocardiographic and clinical parameters to quantify the afterload in severe AS, especially in cases of low-flow or high-afterload states, there is a pressing requirement for more precise diagnostic tools that can complement these modalities.5,6 Myocardial contractility, frequently diminished in patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), is not accurately reflected by the ejection fraction.7 The analysis and interpretation of the invasive central aortic waveform yield more dependable data, and by adopting indices extracted from the central waveforms, there is potential for improved risk stratification of patients and a more accurate evaluation of disease severity. Central pressures are more closely correlated with conventional cardiovascular risk factors such as carotid intima-media thickness and LV mass than brachial pressure.8, 9, 10

Despite these insights, the positional characteristics of the DN along the descending limb of the aortic waveform remain largely underexplored. Building on this concept, Mohananey et al.11 introduced the DN index (DNI)—a novel hemodynamic marker defined as the difference between aortic systolic blood pressure (BP) (SBP) and DN pressure, divided by PP. In patients with hemodynamically significant aortic regurgitation (AR) following TAVR, a lower DNI was observed compared to those without significant AR.11 Expanding this work, Kapadia et al. showed that DNI increases following mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, with a greater increase associated with improved clinical outcomes.12 In the present study, we aimed to further explore the prognostic utility of the DNI.

Methods

We studied all consecutive patients who underwent TAVR for severe AS in our institution between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2020. Severe AS criteria were met by 851 patients. After excluding 278 patients whose waveforms had artifacts, who converted to open surgery (n = 1), who failed to complete the procedure (n = 2), or who had missing pre-TAVR echocardiography data (n = 1), our final cohort consisted of 569 patients (Figure 1). Potential sources of waveform artifacts include overdamping or underdamping, incorrect transducer zeroing, and signal drift. TAVR was performed with Edwards SAPIEN XT and SAPIEN 3 (S3) valves (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, California), Medtronic CoreValve and Corevalve Evolut R (Medtronic, Minneapolis, Minnesota), and the Lotus Valve System (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Massachusetts). We obtained demographic and clinical data from electronic health records. The study protocol received approval from the Cleveland Clinic's Institutional Review Board.

Hemodynamic Measurements

The LV and aortic pressures were recorded simultaneously using two separate fluid-filled catheters. Invasive hemodynamic assessment was performed to obtain the SBP, diastolic BP (DBP), and DN pressures from the central aortic waveforms before and after the valve placement using the Syngo Dynamics software (Siemens Medical Solutions USA, Inc, Malvern, Pennsylvania; 2022). Each measurement was taken as an average of three consecutive waveforms. The DNI was calculated as: (SBP – DN)/PP (Figure 2). The index measures the relative position of the DN on the descending limb of the central aortic waveform with respect to the SBP taking the PP as the frame of reference. The central mean arterial pressure (MAP) was calculated as: 1/3 × (SBP) + 2/3 × (DBP).

Echocardiographic Evaluation

All patients underwent a comprehensive echocardiographic assessment. All echocardiographic measurements were reviewed by experienced clinicians blinded to patient information. Echocardiographic parameters measured included the following variables: peak aortic valve velocity, peak and mean transvalvular gradient, aortic valve area, LV ejection fraction (LVEF), LV outflow tract diameter, aortic valve and LV outflow tract velocity time integral, LV end-diastolic volume, and LV end-systolic volume. Forward stroke volume and cardiac output were obtained before TAVR at a baseline clinical visit and after TAVR at discharge.

Outcomes

The primary clinical outcomes included the rates of heart failure hospitalization (HFH), all-cause mortality, and a composite of HFH or all-cause mortality at 2 years, compared across the change in DNI strata after TAVR.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range. Categorical data are presented as absolute numbers and percentages. Comparisons between groups were conducted using the t tests or χ2 test, as appropriate. A multivariate linear regression model was developed to identify the predictors of DNI. The optimal cutoff for categorizing patients into higher and lower DNI change post TAVR was identified using the survminer package in R. Kaplan–Meier estimates were generated to compare event-free survival between the two DNI groups using the log-rank test. A complete case analysis approach was adopted, with no imputation for missing values. A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York) and R software (version 4.2.2; R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).13,14

Results

The study cohort was divided into two groups according to the post-/pre-TAVR DNI ratio. To determine a clinically meaningful threshold associated with improved event-free survival, maximally selected log-rank statistics were applied. This analysis identified a ratio of 1.19 as the optimal cutoff for predicting 2-year all-cause mortality (cutoff statistic = 3.08) (Supplemental Figure 1). Patients with a ratio ≤1.19 demonstrated significantly lower 2-year survival compared to those with a ratio >1.19 (p = 0.002, log-rank test), suggesting that a greater increase in DNI following TAVR was associated with better outcomes (Figure 3). The distribution of patients across these categories was as follows: ratio ≤1.19 (n = 211, 37.1%) and >1.19 (n = 358, 62.9%).

On echocardiography, the low DNI ratio group had a modestly higher baseline LVEF (59.3% ± 10.4% vs. 56.8% ± 11.1%, p = 0.01) and more favorable global longitudinal strain (–16.7 ± 3.5 vs. −15.7 ± 3.8, p = 0.02). LV end-systolic volume was smaller in this group (40.9 ± 21.1 ml vs. 46.6 ± 30.3 ml, p = 0.02), with a nonsignificant trend toward smaller LV dimensions.

Postdilatation was performed more frequently in the low DNI ratio group (41.7% vs. 27.7%, p < 0.001). Valve size distribution differed significantly between groups, with 23-mm valves used more frequently in the low DNI ratio group (38.9% vs. 29.3%, p = 0.02), whereas 29-mm valves were more commonly implanted in the high DNI ratio group (12.8% vs. 27.1%, p < 0.001). In addition, patients in the high DNI ratio group were less likely to have residual AR following valve deployment (no AR: 72.3% vs. 60.2%, p < 0.001).

Invasive hemodynamic assessment revealed that the low DNI ratio group had higher baseline SBP, MAP, and PP, but a lower pressure at the DN (86.5 ± 18.3 mmHg vs. 96.3 ± 17.9 mmHg, p < 0.001) and a higher baseline DNI (0.60 ± 0.16 vs. 0.37 ± 0.15, p < 0.001). Heart rate was slightly higher in this group (p = 0.02). Following TAVR, DNI increased markedly in this group (0.62 ± 0.12 vs. 0.58 ± 0.12, p < 0.001). In terms of relative changes, the high DNI ratio group exhibited greater increases in MAP, DBP, and heart rate, along with a pronounced reversal in DN pressure behavior (ΔDN: –1.8% ± 18.9% vs. +14.9% ± 25.0%, p < 0.001), consistent with a substantial rise in DNI (ΔDNI: +113.8% ± 195.8%, p < 0.001).

Table 2 summarizes echocardiographic and invasive hemodynamic changes before and after TAVR, stratified by DNI ratio (≤1.19 vs. > 1.19). TAVR led to marked reductions in transvalvular gradients and improvement in valve performance across both groups. In the low DNI ratio group, the transvalvular mean aortic gradient decreased from 40.1 ± 13.1 to 9.5 ± 4.4 mmHg (p < 0.001) and the peak gradient decreased from 68.3 ± 21.2 to 18.6 ± 8.3 mmHg (p < 0.001); similar findings were observed in the high DNI ratio group, where the mean gradient decreased from 40.3 ± 13.5 to 9.8 ± 4.3 mmHg (p < 0.001) and the peak gradient from 67.4 ± 20.7 to 18.8 ± 7.9 mmHg (p < 0.001). LV outflow tract stroke volume index (forward stroke volume index) increased from approximately 37–38 to 60 ml/m2 (p < 0.001).

SBP increased significantly in both groups following TAVR (≤1.19: 128.7 → 148.5 mmHg; >1.19: 121.8 → 146.4 mmHg; both p < 0.001) and was accompanied by modest but significant rises in DBP, MAP, and PP (p < 0.001 for all). Pressure at the DN increased in the low DNI ratio group but decreased slightly in the high DNI ratio group, with both changes reaching statistical significance (p < 0.001). Heart rate remained unchanged in the low DNI ratio group (p = 0.44), whereas a slight increase was observed in the high DNI ratio group (p < 0.001). DNI changed in opposite directions across strata: it decreased slightly in the low DNI ratio group (0.60 ± 0.16 → 0.58 ± 0.12, p = 0.02) but rose markedly in the high DNI ratio group (0.37 ± 0.15 → 0.62 ± 0.12, p < 0.001).

Predictors of Baseline DNI

A multivariate linear regression model (Table 3) demonstrated that diabetes mellitus (β = 0.037; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.004–0.069; p = 0.03) and carotid disease (β = 0.047; 95% CI: 0.007–0.086; p = 0.02) were significantly associated with modest increases in baseline DNI. In contrast, higher baseline LVEF showed a stronger positive association with baseline DNI (β = 0.024 per 1% increase in LVEF; 95% CI: 0.007–0.041; p < 0.001).

As shown in Supplemental Figure 2, Pearson correlation analysis revealed strong positive associations between SBP and PP (r = 0.87), DN pressure (r = 0.73), and MAP (r = 0.88) (all p < 0.001). DN pressure was also strongly correlated with DBP (r = 0.71) and MAP (r = 0.82). DNI showed moderate positive correlations with PP (r = 0.27), SBP (r = 0.20), heart rate (r = 0.15), and systolic ejection time (SET) (r = 0.21), and a moderate negative correlation with DN pressure (r = −0.45) (all p< 0.001). Heart rate was inversely correlated with SET (r = −0.51) and positively with DBP and MAP.

DNI Ratio and Clinical Outcomes

The high DNI ratio group had a shorter mean hospital stay postprocedure (1.63 ± 2.2 days vs. 2.04 ± 3.6 days; p < 0.02). The results of the Cox regression analysis for 2-year (median follow-up 730 days [588–730]) clinical outcomes in patients stratified by DNI ratio groups (≤1.19 vs. >1.19) are summarized in Table 4.

•All-cause mortality: During follow-up, patients in high DNI ratio group had approximately a 50% lower risk of all-cause mortality compared with those in low DNI ratio. This association was consistent in both unadjusted (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.53; 95% CI 0.35–0.80) and adjusted analyses (HR = 0.53; 95% CI 0.34–0.82). A total of 53 patients (9.3%) in the higher-ratio group and 28 patients (4.9%) in the lower-ratio group were lost to follow-up.

Discussion

In this study of patients with severe AS undergoing TAVR, we identified several key findings. First, baseline DNI was independently associated with a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus, carotid artery disease, and elevated baseline LVEF. Second, TAVR led to marked hemodynamic improvements, including increases in SBP, DBP, and DN pressure—resulting in an overall rise in DNI. Finally, a higher post-to-pre-TAVR DNI ratio (>1.19) was strongly associated with improved 2-year event-free survival and an approximately 50% reduction in all-cause mortality, irrespective of clinical features, surgical risk, valve type, size, and postprocedural AR.

The DNI is a hemodynamic index derived from invasive measurements during TAVR that reflects the position of the DN on the descending limb of the aortic waveform. Diabetes mellitus was associated with a modest increase in baseline DNI, with each unit increase in the standardized estimate corresponding to a 3.7% higher DNI. Similarly, carotid artery disease was significantly associated with a 4.7% higher DNI. These associations likely reflect a shared underlying pathophysiological process driven by atherosclerosis. Worsening glucose tolerance and chronic vascular inflammation—hallmarks of diabetes and atherosclerotic disease—lead to increased intimal-medial thickness in both peripheral and central arteries, contributing to progressive arterial stiffening.15,16 This vascular stiffness results in later return of the reflected arterial wave from peripheral bifurcations leading to increase in the DNI.17 This mechanism provides a unifying explanation for why diseases rooted in atherosclerotic burden—such as diabetes mellitus, carotid disease, and chronic kidney disease—are consistently associated with a trend toward higher DNI.18 The other factor that influences DNI is LV function responsible for more cardiac output (CO) and an enhanced rate of LV pressure rise (dP/dt). Baseline LVEF showed a strong association, with each unit increase in LVEF corresponding to a 2.4% higher DNI. We did not measure CO and dP/dt to address mechanism of LVEF influencing DNI. Advanced AS may decrease rate of rise of aortic pressure resulting in a shortened systolic ejection time, and a gradual decline in SET, ultimately explaining a relationship between DNI and LVEF.1 The correlation analysis demonstrated that DNI was positively associated with both systolic ejection time and central PP, suggesting that it serves as a composite biomarker integrating features of both arterial stiffness and rate of rise of aortic pressure which may be influenced by EF, severity of AS and CO.

After TAVR, there is an increase in forward flow across the aortic valve, as evidenced by a reduction in the transvalvular gradient, an increase in forward stroke volume and greater dP/dt of aortic wave form. The central hemodynamic changes we observed following TAVR are an increase in SBP, DBP, PP, MAP, and heart rate immediately after the procedure. We also found that after TAVR, the DN becomes prominent. In severe AS, much of the pressure energy is lost as blood accelerates through the narrowed valve, resulting in turbulence and reduced pressure transmission to the aorta. After TAVR, this energy loss is minimized due to more laminar flow across the prosthetic valve, allowing a greater proportion of kinetic energy to be retained as pressure in the proximal aorta. This results in an immediate increase in central aortic pressure. Although we did not specifically assess changes in aortic or peripheral arterial elasticity, prior work by Michail et al.19 has demonstrated that reservoir pressure—a surrogate for arterial compliance—remains largely unchanged immediately after TAVR. Instead, the observed rise in central pressure is attributed to an increase in excess pressure, reflecting enhanced transmission of energy to the proximal aorta.

Our results suggest that improvement in DNI has long-term prognostic implications. Male patients, with larger implanted valve size, with no evidence of postdeployment AR, and lower baseline LVEF had a higher DNI ratio (>1.19) following TAVR. This is consistent with theory that maximum positive changes in CO and aortic dP/dt occur in this population with lower baseline CO and aortic dP/dt with best hemodynamic result from large-size valve and no AR. After TAVR, a lower or declining DNI may reflect ongoing AR, as regurgitant flow widens PP.20

At 2 years, patients in the high DNI ratio group had a significantly lower risk of all-cause mortality compared to those in the low DNI ratio group. After adjustment for confounders, the high DNI ratio group demonstrated a 47% reduction in the hazard of all-cause mortality. This indicates that each unit increase in the DNI ratio is associated with an approximately 50% reduction in the hazard of all-cause mortality. The study by Perlman et al.21 identified postprocedural hypertension after TAVR as a predictor of better outcomes. In a cohort of 105 consecutive TAVR patients, 51% experienced sustained increases in BP, necessitating intensified antihypertensive treatment.21 These patients showed increased stroke volume and cardiac output, leading to improved prognosis.21 Although there are no established guidelines on the management of BP post TAVR, both low SBP and low DBP have been shown to correlate with poor outcomes.22 Patients in the low DNI ratio group evidently have a higher burden of advanced disease involving both the aortic valve and the arterial tree at baseline. As a result, the pulsatile component (denominator of the DNI) remained disproportionately elevated relative to the increase in arterial pressure despite both groups showing an overall increase in DNI following TAVR. This persistent state of heightened pulsatile load immediately after valve replacement may contribute to reduced forward flow and has been previously associated with adverse long-term outcomes.23,24 This mechanism may partly explain why patients in the low DNI ratio group tended to have worse follow-up results. In addition, according to our results, perfusion pressure improved to a greater extent in the high DNI ratio group, accompanied by a greater increase in diastolic BP. In the long run, this may help maintain better organ perfusion as well as preserve coronary perfusion pressure. Although, previously, an increase in DNI following transcatheter edge-to-edge repair has been shown to correlate with an increase in forward stroke volume, we did not observe similar changes in our analysis.12 This discrepancy is likely due to difference in disease pathology and the fact that dP/dt of aortic pressure changes with TAVR and not with the treatment of mitral valve.

The DNI can serve as a prognostic tool for stratifying long-term outcomes following TAVR, allowing for tailored patient management based on their response to the intervention. The novelty of DNI stems from its consideration of baseline disease severity and coexisting conditions, which play crucial roles in determining the response to TAVR. It essentially serves as a composite hemodynamic biomarker, reflecting the degree of ventricular–vascular coupling and offering insight into long-term outcomes by the end of the procedure. In addition, as previously described, DNI offers a straightforward method for detecting AR in patients during the TAVR itself.20

The study has known limitations due to its single-center, retrospective design. Although multivariate linear regression models were used to isolate possible confounders, there may be residual confounding from unmeasured factors that may affect the DNI, including the use of antihypertensives. Vast majority (93%) of patients in our cohort received balloon-expandable valves. Echocardiographic parameters were not measured simultaneously with central hemodynamic parameters.

Conclusions

DNI is a simple yet accurate hemodynamic index that can be derived from intraprocedural hemodynamic data in patients undergoing TAVR. A larger increase in DNI is associated with better long-term survival. Future prospective studies are warranted to validate the clinical utility of DNI across multiple centers and to explore its applicability in specific subtypes of AS.
