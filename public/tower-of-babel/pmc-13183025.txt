Assessment of dietary patterns and nutritional status among tuberculosis patients in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Rama M. Chandika, Fatima A. Elfaki, Husameldin E. Khalafalla, Maseer Khan, Osama H. Khalid, Yehya M. Hejri, Ibrahim Ahmed Bani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global health concern, although lifestyle and nutritional choices can significantly reduce its incidence. The aim of this study was to assess dietary patterns among patients with tuberculosis in Saudi Arabia and to examine their relationship with nutritional status, particularly body mass index (BMI). A cross-sectional design study was conducted among 600 patients with tuberculosis in the health sectors run by the directly observing mobile teams in Jazan Province, Saudi Arabia. Dietary patterns were identified using an exploratory factor analysis. In addition, associations between BMI and the explored dietary patterns were assessed. Contrary to global trends, a notably high prevalence of overweight/obesity among TB patients (40.59%) and relatively low proportion of underweight individuals (8.46%) were observed. Four distinct dietary patterns were identified that collectively explained 81% of the variance: prudent (fruits and vegetables, and legumes), high protein (milk and dairy products, meat and poultry, fish and seafood and traditional foods), grain-based (grains and cereals), and western (processed snacks, fast foods, and sugary foods and beverages). The prudent food pattern showed a highly significant negative linear trend with BMI (R2 = 0.098, P < .01), whereas the high-protein food pattern exhibited a highly significant positive trend (R2 = 0.017, P < .01). Grain-based and Western food patterns were not significantly associated with BMI. These findings highlight an evolving nutritional landscape among patients with tuberculosis in Saudi Arabia, where overweight and obesity are prevalent. Further research is required to explore the long-term implications of these dietary patterns on tuberculosis treatment outcomes and overall patient health.

1. Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a severe infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It often affects the lungs, although it can spread to other organs in the body.[1,2] TB remains a significant global health concern and one of the top 10 causes of death worldwide. An estimated 10.6 million people became ill with TB in 2021, and 1.6 million people died from the disease (including 187,000 individuals with human immunodeficiency virus).[3] In 2019, Saudi Arabia had 3004 new TB cases, and the incidence was 8.7 per 100,000 people.[2] Different regions of Saudi Arabia have different rates of TB infection. In Jeddah, the main port, the infection rate can reach up to 64 cases per 100,000, compared with 32 per 100,000 in Riyadh, which might be explained by the influx of pilgrims.[4–9] Jazan, located close to the border with Yemen, has similarly high rates of TB as well as higher rates of multidrug-resistant TB than other parts of the Kingdom; as many as 44% of cases are multidrug-resistant.[10] Although the association between TB and undernutrition is well established, less is known about the current dietary patterns of TB patients in specific local contexts such as Jazan. Moreover, nutritional risk in tb may no longer be limited to underweight alone, as shifts in dietary behaviors and the nutrition transition in Saudi Arabia may influence body composition and metabolic status among patients.[11] Research has shown that lifestyle risk factors, including eating habits, living conditions, education, overcrowding, smoking, alcohol use, and occupation, are strongly associated with the burden of TB.[12–14] Diet, a vital component of lifestyle, plays a particularly critical role, with undernutrition recognized as a key driver of TB epidemics.[15,16] Nutrition interventions have the potential not only to reduce disease prevalence but also to improve drug response and quality of life.[17] However, patients often face appetite suppression due to disease symptoms and medication side effects, compounded by the catabolic effects of TB and systemic inflammation, which together elevate the risk of undernutrition. Individuals diagnosed with TB face increased energy and nutrient requirements, yet meeting these demands can be difficult due to decreased food intake and other physiological challenges.[18] The disease further disrupts nutritional status by impairing nutrient and micronutrient absorption while causing metabolic alterations, ultimately leading to wasting and malnutrition.[19] However, data on the prevailing dietary patterns among TB patients in Jazan are limited, and it remains unclear whether current nutritional challenges are solely related to undernutrition or also reflect broader dietary imbalances. This study aims to assess dietary patterns among TB patients in Jazan and examine their relationship with nutritional status, particularly body mass index (BMI), to support future dietary and health interventions.

2. Methods

2.1. Study area

Jazan province lies along the coast of the Red Sea in the southern part of Saudi Arabia. It has the 6th-highest population in Saudi Arabia’s regions and benefits from comprehensive health, education, and communication facilities. However, Jazan has the 2nd-highest TB incidence rate in Saudi Arabia. This cross-sectional study was conducted during 2023 to 2024 in Jazan province, Saudi Arabia, using a 2-stage cluster sampling design among directly observing treatment mobile teams (DOTs) catchment areas. Research has shown that lifestyle and diet play a significant role in reducing the burden of TB. However, there is a lack of studies assessing dietary behaviors among patients with TB in Saudi Arabia. Therefore, the present study was undertaken to study nutritional behaviors and their associations with BMI among TB patients in the health sector (Jazan, Al Aridah, Abu Arish, Sabya, Damad, Baysh, Samtha, and Fayfa) that employs DOTs in Jazan province.

2.3. Sampling technique

This cross-sectional study employed a 2-stage cluster and systematic random sampling technique. In the 1st stage, all areas in total 8 that run DOTs program in Jazan Province (Jizan, Al Aridah, Abu Arish, Sabya, Damad, Baysh, Samtha, and Fayfa) were selected as clusters (Fig. 1). In the 2nd stage, systematic random sampling method was employed to select TB patients within each cluster. A total of 75 TB patients were selected from each cluster, resulting in an overall sample size of 600 participants across the 8 clusters. Each cluster TB patients list was obtained from the DOTs mobile teams. Using systematic random sampling, every kth patient was selected (where k = total number of patients in the cluster divided by 75). Patients under 20 years and over 65 years of age were excluded from the study. In addition, physically weak patients and those who did not wish to participate were also excluded. Older TB patients frequently experience frailty, comorbidities, and cognitive or recall limitations, which can reduce the reliability of self-reported dietary intake data. In addition, the strategy was restricted to patients with TB receiving anti-TB drugs from public DOTs mobile team. One health professional and paramedical staff member were recruited as enumerators from each cluster DOTs mobile team. A sensitization workshop was conducted for all enumerators to explain the importance of the study and provide training on data-collection methods. Enumerators were trained in neutral questioning, participants were assured of confidentiality, and answers were recorded without judgment to reduce the social desirability bias. When the DOT mobile team representatives visited TB patient house for follow-up examinations, the study enumerators collected the patient dietary information and sociodemographic data through questionnaires specifically designed for the study.

2.4. Statistical analysis

A validated and pre-tested questionnaire was used[20] with minor modifications to include the local common consumed foods (aseeda, marsah, mahshosh). Eexpert consultation taken from the Clinical Nutrition Department, Jazan University to ensure content validity and relevance to the local context. English version of the questionnaire was translated into Arabic through a standardized forward–backward translation process performed by independent bilingual translators. Pilot study conducted with 30 TB patients from Jizan city which is under the DOTs program. Patients were asked about the local language Arabic questionnaire simplification of item wording for clarity, reorganization of certain items for better flow and feasibility. Iinstrument reliability, yielding a Cronbach alpha coefficient of 0.78 indicating acceptable internal consistency. The questionnaire consisted of 2 parts. The first part collected sociodemographic and health characteristics (gender, age, nationality, residence, education level, marital status, occupation, monthly income, health condition, height [cm], and weight (kg) of the TB patients). The 2nd part assessed the dietary habits of the patients with TB. Exploratory factor analysis was performed over principal component analysis and confirmatory factor analysis to explore latent constructs (dietary patterns) that explain shared variance among observed food groups, rather than merely reduce dimensionality and no a priori dietary pattern model specific to our population was hypothesized. The Cronbach alpha index (0.856), Kaiser–Meyer–Olken values for individual items fruits and vegetables (0.89), milk and dairy products (0.88), grain and cereals (0.72), meat and poultry (0.64), fish and seafood (0.79), legumes (0.87), processed snacks (0.75), processed foods (0.75), sugary foods (0.86) and traditional foods (0.69). Overall Kaiser–Meyer–Olken (0.79), and P < .01 for Bartlett test of sphericity denoted the sample adequacy for the analysis. Four distant dietary patterns were decided based on the eigenvalue >1.1 through the scree plot. Varimax rotation was applied to improve the interpretability of the factor structure by maximizing the variance of factor loadings and producing a simpler structure in which each food group loads strongly on a single dietary pattern. This approach has been widely used in nutritional epidemiology studies to derive dietary patterns. Each dietary pattern was named based on the characteristics of food variables with absolute factor loadings over 0.30 for a increased efficient model. Further calculated variance inflation factors and tolerance values for all dietary pattern variables included in the regression models. The variance inflation values ranged from 1.02 to 1.18, and the tolerance values ranged from 0.85 to 0.98 indicating that no evidence of multicollinearity. Q–Q plots for normality, scatter plots for homoscedasticity, residual plots between BMI and the dietary patterns for linear relationship and study design ensure independence of observations. These diagnostic checks confirmed that the regression model assumptions were reasonably satisfied. The model was adjusted for confounders; age, sex, socioeconomic status, smoking, comorbidities, physical activity and BMI values were transformed into Z-scores. BMI was analyzed as a continuous outcome variable to preserve the full variability of the data and to maximize statistical power. The data were coded and analyzed through rao software version R-4.2.2 for windows using the psych and ggplot2 packages. frequencies and percentages were used for qualitative variables, chi-square tests for nominal variable associations, exploratory factor analysis for dietary patterns, and multivariable regression analysis was employed to assess the associations between the obtained dietary patterns and BMI. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

2.5. Ethical approval

This study obtained ethical approval from the Jazan Health Ethics Committee, Ministry of Health, Saudi Arabia, with reference No. 2344 on 23/05/2023. Permission was provided by the administrators of the primary health care centers, and written informed consent was obtained from the participating TB patients. Participants were told about the importance and objectives of the research, their right to decline participation/withdraw without consequences, and that their identities would remain confidential. The questionnaire was anonymous and did not record any personal information, thus ensuring the confidentiality of the participants.

Most patients reported consuming some food items only once a week, including fruits and vegetables (78%), milk and dairy products (80%), grains and cereals (79.67%), and fish and seafood (81.33%). 3.3. Dietary patterns by principal components analysis

As shown in Table 3, exploratory factor analysis among the TB patients identified 4 dietary patterns: prudent, high protein, grain-based, and western. A prudent food pattern in which the diet heavily features fruits, vegetables, and legumes accounted for 33% of the variability. The high-protein pattern, in which the diet largely consisted of a combination of milk and dairy products, meat and poultry, fish and seafood, and traditional foods, accounted for 24% of the variance. The grain-based food pattern, in which grains and cereals accounted for much of the patients’ food consumption, accounted for 14% of the variance. Finally, the western food pattern, in which the patient consumed a combination of processed snacks, fast foods, and sugary foods and beverages, accounted for 10% of the variance. Four major dietary patterns: prudent food pattern, high-protein pattern, grain-based foods pattern, and western food pattern accounted 33%, 24%, 14%, 10% of the total variance. Overall, the model explained 81% of the variance.

3.4. Association between dietary patterns and BMI

The association between BMI and the 4 dietary patterns among patients with TB is depicted in Figure 2. The prudent food patterns (fruits and vegetables, and legumes) exhibited a highly significant negative linear trend. This accounted for 9.8% of the variability in BMI. This finding suggests that higher consumption of fruits and vegetables, which are rich sources of micronutrients and dietary fiber, may contribute to maintaining healthier body weight and may protect against excessive weight gain among patients with TB (R2 = 0.098, P < .01; Fig. 2a).

4. Discussion

This study examined the dietary patterns of patients with TB in Jazan, Saudi Arabia, and identified 4 distinct patterns: prudent, high-protein, grain-based, and westernized, which collectively explained 81% of the variance. Unlike many studies focusing on undernutrition in TB populations,[21–25] our study revealed a notably high proportion of overweight patients (42%) and a relatively low proportion of underweight individuals (6.7%). While the low prevalence of underweight patients may favor better TB treatment outcomes, the high prevalence of overweight/obese patients raises concerns regarding the risk of other comorbidities. BMI had a positive linear trend with a high-protein diet; this association was significant. The finding that overweight and obesity are common among TB patients in this setting has important programmatic implications. TB services in Jazan may need to move beyond a sole focus on undernutrition and consider routine BMI screening and nutritional risk stratification at baseline. The participants’ sociodemographic characteristics aligned with typical TB risk factors, including rural residence, younger age, low income, unemployment, and smoking. However, the nutritional status of our cohort differed from other reports, with the prevalence of underweight patients (8.46%) being lower and that of overweight/obese patients (40.59%) being higher than global trends. PrayGod et al[21] reported a weight deficit of approximately 9 kg in Tanzanian patients with TB. Sinha et al[22] found that 54% of TB patients were malnourished, and Zheng et al[26] reported that 21.3% of TB patients had a BMI below 18.5 kg/m2. The differing results of this study can be attributed to the high prevalence of overweight and obese people in the general Saudi population: 45.1% and 23.1%, respectively.[27] The average daily intake of vegetables, fruits, animal food, dairy products, soy, and nuts was lower than that recommended by the Dietary Reference Intakes (P < .01). Our findings partially mirror a study among Jazan University students, which reported similar prudent and “Westernized” eating patterns.[28]

The consumption of fruits and vegetables was low, with more than 82% of the participants consuming them once a week or less. Fruit and vegetable consumption was similar to that reported by Zheng et al[26]; however, the same study also reported that patients’ protein intake was significantly below the recommended level. Legumes were never or rarely consumed by 82.17% of our study population. Zheng et al found a far higher rate of legume consumption, which was close to the national recommendations.[26] The prudent dietary pattern identified by the study included fruits, vegetables, and legumes, and was associated with a highly significant negative linear trend with BMI (R2 = 0.098, P < .01). This suggests that fruits and vegetables, which are rich in micronutrients and fiber, shield participants from excessive weight gain. Similar findings were reported by Bhargava et al,[29] who found that the consumption of vegetables and legumes was associated with good nutritional recovery among TB patients in India. Other studies have found that diets rich in fruits and vegetables are generally inversely associated with BMI and waist circumference.[19] From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that nutritional counseling for TB patients may benefit from differentiation by baseline BMI. Underweight patients may require targeted energy- and protein-dense diets to support recovery, whereas overweight or obese patients may benefit from emphasis on micronutrient-rich, high-fiber patterns such as the prudent diet identified in this study. TB patients face increased nutritional demands, particularly for proteins; thus, they will benefit from diets rich in animal proteins.[30,31] Protein consumption among study participants appeared reasonable (80% of respondents consumed high-protein foods 2–4 times a week); however, the high-protein dietary pattern, in which dairy products, meats, poultry, and seafood made up a large part of the diet, was positively associated with BMI (R2 = 0.017, P < .01), indicating that these foods may support weight maintenance or gain in undernourished TB patients.[31] However, in excess, this could result in undesirable weight gain. It should be noted that excessive intake of energy-dense animal products can lead to overweight or obesity. A Westernized dietary pattern is usually linked to increased BMI and metabolic disturbances,[32] and the increased consumption of ultra-processed food is a serious concern in low- and middle-income countries.[33,34] The participants in this study consumed fast foods, processed snacks, sugary foods, and beverages less frequently than other food items (78–90% never or rarely consumed these items). The Westernized pattern was positively associated with BMI; however, this association was not statistically significant.

5. Limitations

This study has certain limitations. Due to the cross-sectional design, temporality cannot be established; dietary patterns and BMI may both be consequences of the stage of disease progression and treatment, rather than causal factors. Therefore, causal relationships between dietary patterns and BMI cannot be inferred. As the sample came from a single region and was clinic-based (participants were recruited from Jazan TB services), this may have led to selection bias and limited the generalizability of the results to other Saudi regions or TB populations (e.g., hospitalized, migrant, or rural patients who do not access care). In addition, patients older than 65 years were excluded for practical reasons, which may further limit generalizability to older tb populations. Frequency-based reporting is prone to recall and social desirability bias, may not capture portion sizes or total energy, and does not account for seasonality. TB-specific Food Frequency Questionnaire validation was not performed although a design effect was applied during sample size estimation, clustering by dot area was not explicitly modeled in all analyses, which may have affected precision estimates. Despite these limitations, the findings provide important insight into the coexistence of TB with overweight/obesity in this setting, the modest inverse association between the prudent dietary pattern and BMI, and the generally low intake of fruits, vegetables, and legumes.

6. Implications for future research

Prospective, multicenter cohorts with repeated dietary measures (24-hour recalls or weighed records with portion sizes) validated TB-specific tools, seasonality assessment, body composition and biomarker panels, standardized disease severity metrics, food insecurity scales, and physical activity evaluation, along with robust multivariable modeling, multiple testing correction, and validation of dietary patterns, would enhance causal inference and external validity.

7. Conclusions

This study highlights the importance of BMI-stratified nutritional interventions in TB care, emphasizing the need for individualized plans based on initial and follow-up assessments. The findings revealed a high prevalence of overweight and obesity and a relatively low proportion of underweight patients, contrasting with many TB populations in which undernutrition predominates. Addressing both undernutrition and overnutrition is critical for optimizing TB outcomes and mitigating long-term health risks. In this context, promoting micronutrient-rich and diverse dietary patterns may support balanced nutritional management while reducing metabolic risk.
