Sam Yu-Te Lee, Chengyang Ji, Shicheng Wen, Lifu Huang, Dongyu Liu, Kwan-Liu Ma

Abstract
. Text analytics has traditionally required specialized knowledge in Natural Language Processing (NLP) or text analysis, which presents a barrier for entry-level analysts. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have changed the landscape of NLP by enabling more accessible and automated text analysis (e.g., topic detection, summarization, information extraction, etc.). We introduce VIDEE , a system that supports entry-level data analysts to conduct advanced text analytics with intelligent agents. VIDEE instantiates a human-agent collaroration workflow consisting of three stages: (1) Decomposition, which incorporates a human-in-the-loop Monte-Carlo Tree Search algorithm to support generative reasoning with human feedback, (2) Execution, which generates an executable text analytics pipeline, and (3) Evaluation, which integrates LLM-based evaluation and visualizations to support user validation of execution results. We conduct two quantitative experiments to evaluate VIDEE’s effectiveness and analyze common agent errors. A user study involving participants with varying levels of NLP and text analytics experience—from none to expert—demonstrates the system’s usability and reveals distinct user behavior patterns. The findings identify design implications for human-agent collaboration, validate the practical utility of VIDEE for non-expert users, and inform future improvements to intelligent text analytics systems.



1.
Introduction

To extract useful information and insights from a large collection of textual data,
the ability to choose and conduct a sequence of text analytics tasks, from
named entity recognition, sentiment analysis, to document clustering
(
Liu et al. 2019
;
Talib et al. 2016
)
,
is essential.
To carry out these tasks, however, one must acquire advanced Natural Language Processing (NLP) knowledge for a comprehensive understanding of the commonly deployed text analytics methods, creating significant barriers for entry-level data analysts.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) and the emergence of prompt engineering have brought significant changes to text analytics. Although LLMs are not yet ready to achieve complicated end-to-end goals reliably, many conventional text analytics methods can now be performed by prompting
(
Xu et al. 2024
;
Wan et al. 2024
)
.
For example, TnT-LLM
(
Wan et al. 2024
)
supports end-to-end taxonomy generation and annotation with a sequence of prompt templates, including summarization, taxonomy generation, taxonomy review, and LLM-augmented labeling.
These developments reduce the technical barrier for individual methods. However, they also introduce new challenges when combining multiple steps into a complete text analytics pipeline.

Large Decomposition Space.

The flexibility of prompts allows for many ways of decomposition to achieve a goal through different combinations of subtasks, which may vary in complexity. Analysts must trade off between the difficulty of subtasks and the overall robustness of the pipeline. Although LLMs tend to perform better on simpler subtasks, longer or more fragmented pipelines may also increase computational cost and
risk of errors
.

Technical Knowledge.

Analysts possess varying levels of technical knowledge, especially regarding LLMs.
LLM-related fields are undergoing rapid advancements
(
DAIR.AI [n. d.]
)
.
Analysts might not keep up with the state-of-the-art knowledge, such as Retrieval-Augmented-Generation (RAG)
(
Lewis et al. 2020
)
and generative reasoning
(
Xie et al. 2023
)
, or might not poss sufficient prompting skill
(
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
;
Kim et al. 2024
)
.

Implementation and Experimentation.

Constructing and implementing a text analytics pipeline requires a significant engineering effort.
The analyst needs to properly handle the input and output formats, intermediate data transformations, and the analysis parameters used in each step.
Analysts might find it difficult to quickly implement and experiment with alternative pipelines.

Evaluation.

It requires a unique set of evaluation methods to evaluate the effectiveness of an LLM-based text analytics pipeline.
While NLP researchers have been exploring new evaluation methods
(
Beigi et al. 2024
)
, these methods have not become widely popular and known to analysts. Analysts can find it difficult to integrate these state-of-the-art evaluation methods into their text analytics pipeline.

These challenges motivate the need for an agentic system to support text analysts. Given a user goal and a dataset, the agent, possessing sufficient technical knowledge, can automatically
decompose
the goal by searching the large decomposition space and generating a text analytics plan. It can then
implement
and
execute
the pipeline on the given dataset, and
evaluate
the results.

In this paper, we investigate the effectiveness of a human-AI collaboration workflow with three stages:
Decomposition, Execution
, and
Evaluation
, to achieve complex text analytics goals.
The human controls the agent to search the decomposition space and develop a text analytics plan.
Then, the agent generates implementations for the plan and executes them upon human request, while the human uses a set of criteria to evaluate the execution performance and refine the pipeline as needed.
Finally, the human derives insights from the pipeline results.

2.
Related Works

2.1.
Text Analytics with LLMs

We use “text analysis” to refer to individual analysis techniques, and “text analytics” to refer to a pipeline that combines multiple analysis techniques to accomplish an analytical goal.
In text analysis, researchers have found that LLMs can achieve state-of-the-art performance in many tasks.
Early investigations have found that LLMs excel in text classification and annotation
(
Gilardi et al. 2023
;
Törnberg 2023
)
, outperforming crowd-source workers.
Xu et al.
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
conducted a comprehensive survey to study the use of LLMs in information extraction (IE) based on a generative paradigm and found promising potential in universal and low-resource IE settings. Li et al.
(
Li et al. 2023
)
also found that LLMs have excellent performance in OpenIE settings.
Based on these findings, Zhang et al.
(
Zhang and Soh 2024
)
showed the efficacy of LLM-based automatic knowledge graph construction, which overcomes the limitations of predefined schema.
The findings of these works coincide with other large-scale evaluations of LLM in NLP
(
Chang et al. 2024
)
, suggesting that LLMs are ready to be text analysis task solvers in real-world projects.

Beyond individual analysis tasks, LLMs have been increasingly used to construct end-to-end text analytics pipelines.
Wan et al.
(
Wan et al. 2024
)
proposed TnT-LLM, a framework that supports LLM-based label generation and annotation for automatic text mining. Lam et al.
(
Lam et al. 2024
)
developed LLooM, which supports the induction of high-level concepts from unstructured text by chaining multiple prompt-based “operators” into a pipeline, such as synthesize, distill, or score. Dai et al.
(
Dai et al. 2023
)
proposed a human-LLM collaboration framework for thematic analysis. LLMs can generate initial codes, refine the codes from user feedback, annotate data with the codes, and then identify themes from the coded data. Rasheed et al.
(
Rasheed et al. 2024
)
showed the efficacy of automating qualitative analysis methods with LLMs, such as narrative analysis, content analysis, or discourse analysis.
A key shift introduced by LLMs in text analytics is the ability to define analysis tasks flexibly using natural language prompts, rather than adhering strictly to predefined analysis methods. This flexibility enables the construction of more context-aware and task-specific analytics pipelines. In this work, we aim to support the automatic generation of such pipelines through human-agent collaboration.

2.2.
LLM-assisted Data Analysis

Researchers have also found LLMs useful in automating or assisting data analysis processes.
Microsoft has integrated LLMs into Data Wrangler
(
Microsoft 2023
)
, enabling users to clean and transform data in natural language.
However, Kazemitabaar et al.
(
Kazemitabaar et al. 2024
)
found that users face serious challenges in verifying AI-generated results and steering AI in data analysis, calling for greater human involvement.
To this end, Xie et al.
(
Xie et al. 2024
)
transform LLM-generated data analysis code into an interactive visual representation to enhance user comprehension and control.
Zhao et al.
(
Zhao et al. 2024
)
proposed LightVA, a framework that supports visual data exploration with human-agent collaboration.
The user can control the agent to perform analysis and review results in static visualizations.
Chen et al.
(
Chen et al. 2025a
)
developed InterChat, a generative visual analytics system that generates visualizations from natural language. To enhance user control, it also supports hybrid interactions combining direct manipulation of visual elements with natural language inputs.

Compared to data analysis, text analytics presents unique challenges.
First, the structure of data could change multiple times throughout the text analytics pipeline, creating dependencies between consecutive steps.
Second,
LLM-based text analysis tasks are increasingly open-ended, as they are defined dynamically through prompts. Writing and refining these prompts, especially in sequence, requires significant cognitive effort.
These factors jointly cause the space of possible pipelines to grow rapidly, increasing the difficulty of pipeline design, evaluation, and optimization.

2.3.
Human-Agent Collaboration Design Studies

As GenAI systems and prompt engineering gain prominence, research on human-agent collaboration in HCI has grown significantly.
Early works have studied the challenges that users face when interacting with LLMs. Zamfirescu et al.
(
Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. 2023
)
found that non-AI experts explore prompt designs opportunistically and have inaccurate expectations in LLMs. Kim et al.
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
found four challenges in prompt evaluation: User evaluation of prompts is manual, multi-facted, dynamic, and not necessarily leading to actionable insights.
In addition to challenges, many works have also presented solutions from different perspectives. Kim et al.
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
overcome the evaluation challenges by supporting user-defined criteria with LLM judges. Lee et al.
(
Lee et al. 2025a
)
found that as LLM outputs reach human-level quality, feature metrics that characterize the outputs are more desirable over conventional quality metrics.
Tankelevitch et al.
(
Tankelevitch et al. 2024
)
propose using the metacognition framework to understand user challenges and guide future research in two directions: improving users’ metacognition, or reducing the metacognitive demands of GenAI systems.
These design studies show the necessity of supporting analysts in building LLM-based text analytics pipelines, and inform us of the design guidelines for agentic support.

2.4.
Applications of Agentic Interfaces

For advanced human-agent collaboration scenarios, bespoke interfaces that tailer to the specific usage requirements are needed.

For scenarios where intense information seeking is critical, Suh et al.
(
Suh et al. 2023
)
developed Sensecape, an interactive system that provides multilevel abstraction of complex information in a hierarchy view. Jiang et al.
(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
developed Graphologue, a system that converts long LLM responses into interactive flow diagrams.

For better control of agents, Cai et al.
(
Cai et al. 2024
)
introduce Low-code LLM, a framework integrating multiple types of visual programming to support users edit the agent’s plan before execution.
Masson et al.
(
Masson et al. 2024
)
demonstrate the benefit of supporting direct manipulation of LLM responses through DirectGPT, a user interface that presents ChatGPT responses in a continuous representation that is directly edited, as opposed to a sequence of conversations.

For better evaluation of agent actions, Cheng et al.
(
Cheng et al. 2024
)
designed RELIC, an interactive system that supports evaluation of factuality in agent responses based on the consistency of the responses when the same question is asked multiple times.
However, it remains technically challenging to reliably assess the uncertainty of agent responses in general
(
Beigi et al. 2024
)
, and therefore LLM judges
(
Chiang and Lee 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023a
)
, i.e., defining evaluation criteria as LLM instructions in prompt templates, are more widely adopted.
A concern of LLM judges is that LLM outputs may not align with human judgements.
To address this, Shankar et al.
(
Shankar et al. 2024
)
proposed EVALGEN, which enables users to craft and align LLM-judges by grading sampled outputs in a mixed-initiative approach.
These prior works collectively demonstrate the need for adaptable, user-centered interfaces in agentic systems. We build on these insights and design our system specifically for the unique requirements of LLM-based text analytics.

A three-stage human-agent collaboration workflow for text analytics with multiple agents. In the Decomposition stage, the human describes a goal and the decomposer agent searches for a plan under human monitor and control. The results are communicated back as semantic tasks (e.g., topic modeling). 3.
Design Rationale

3.1.
Motivating Scenario

Consider Alice, a data analyst with entry-level knowledge of text analytics. She is given a dataset of customer comments and is asked to analyze it to identify key themes in customer feedback.

C1
: Developing an analytics plan

Analyzing customer feedback is an open-ended goal with many possible approaches. Alice needs to break down the goal into an analytics plan, which requires her to have extensive prior knowledge in the available text analysis options, such as sentiment analysis or topic modeling, and combine them into a coherent text analytics pipeline.
Eventually, Alice comes up with a conceptual plan: first perform topic modeling on the dataset to group the comments into different themes, then perform sentiment analysis to understand the attitudes of the customers.

C2
: Implementing the analytics plan

Once the plan is defined, Alice must implement each step using a programming language and NLP libraries (e.g., NLTK
(
Bird and Loper 2004
)
). For example, she may choose BERTopic
(
Grootendorst 2022
)
for topic modeling, which requires learning how to install and use the package.
Implementation requires not only theoretical understanding of each method (e.g., the interpretation and tuning of hyperparameters), but also familiarity with library-specific documentation and sufficient programming skills.
Moreover, a typical text analytics pipeline involves multiple libraries or models, and ensuring compatibility and data flow between steps is often time-consuming and error-prone.

C3
: Evaluating the results

Before drawing conclusions, Alice must evaluate the quality of the results. However, each stage of the pipeline often requires different evaluation methods.
In an attempt to increase flexibility, Alice opts for prompt-based sentiment analysis, which allows querying specific attitudes using natural language.
Yet, because prompt-based methods are relatively new, there is limited consensus on appropriate evaluation techniques, leaving Alice uncertain about how to assess the reliability
of her findings
.

3.2.
Human-Agent Collaboration Workflow

Guided by literature review and close collaboration with senior NLP experts, we design a three-stage human-agent collaboration workflow
to overcome the aforementioned challenges.
To better explain the workflow, we first define some terms as follows:

•

Goal
: A practical action to be achieved by analyzing the dataset, such as exploring the HCI research topics in recent years or understanding sentiments of user comments.

•

Semantic tasks
: Conceptual descriptions of how the goal is to be achieved through text analytics. This level of description is appropriate for high-level planning and communication with non-technical people.

•

Decomposition
: The process of searching for a plan consisting of semantic tasks in order to achieve the goal.

•

Primitive task
: A concrete definition of a text analytics method. A primitive task should contain implementation information such as the algorithm and the data schema. This level of description is appropriate for analysts to implement and debug.

•

Pipeline
: A sequence of primitive tasks, including the definitions of each primitive task and their dependencies.

Next, we explain in detail the three-stage workflow of the system: Decomposition, Execution, and Evaluation, as shown in
Figure 1
. For each stage, we highlight the responsibility division between the human and the agent. Here, we focus on the high-level collaboration pattern, and leave the technical details in
Section 5
.

3.2.1.
Decomposition

In this stage, the human and the agent work together to come up with a pipeline of
semantic tasks
from the
goal
. Given the large decomposition space, the agent is responsible for searching semantic tasks that are meaningful to the human (
C1
).
We refer to the agent at this stage as the
decomposer agent
.
The decomposer agent takes the user’s goal as input and explores possible pipeline options following a generative reasoning approach
(
Xie et al. 2023
)
. To ensure reliability, a feedback-aware search algorithm governs the search direction that the agent chooses.
In our system, the decomposer agent uses a human-in-the-loop Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) algorithm
(
Coulom 2007
;
Kocsis and Szepesvári 2006
)
incorporating LLM judges.
The
human
user is assumed to lack sufficient technical knowledge to propose a reliable pipeline. By monitoring and controlling the agent’s search process, the human user learns the potential steps and guides the agent towards a desirable pipeline.
By design, this process improves the user’s metacognition of the text analytics tasks
(
Tankelevitch et al. 2024
)
.

Figure 2.
The interface for the decomposition stage. (a) Users can input their goal and dataset context in natural language. (b) The decomposer agent iteratively searches for text analytics plans to accomplish the goal using Monte-Carlo Tree Serch. Users can make various actions to intervene in the search process, such as choosing next expansion node or adjusting the scoring of a node. (c) Dataset Inspection View supports users to make sense of the dataset. (d) The scoring criteria of each node, including complexity, coherence, and importance. User feedback on the scoring is recorded by the system.

3.2.2.
Execution

The decomposition stage finalizes the
semantic tasks
that represent the planning of the text analytics pipeline.
In the execution stage, an
executor agent
, drawing from a comprehensive set of technical knowledge in text analytics,
generates an executable pipeline composed of
primitive tasks
(
C2
).
The
human
user is responsible for inspecting the generated pipeline and executing each step.
The results are presented to the human for further evaluation and refinement.
In our system, each execution step can be accomplished via a prompt template or a “tool”, which is essentially a pre-defined code snippet for a function.

3.2.3.
Evaluation

To ensure the reliability of the insights generated, the evaluation of each step is necessary but often overlooked by human users
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
. To compensate, the
evaluator agent
recommends some evaluation criteria, and the
human
user picks the most suitable ones, adds new ones, and makes decisions based on the evaluation (
C3
).
The evaluation results are presented with visualizations for human inspection.
In our system, each evaluation criterion is instantiated as an LLM judge that can be generated from a user-defined criterion expressed in natural language.

4.
Interface Design

The user interface in the system instantiates the three-stage workflow, supporting a no-code environment to plan, execute, and evaluate a text analytics pipeline.
Searching View
streams the MCTS process in a tree diagram that supports various user controls (
Figure 2
-b).

Dataset Inspection View
supports visual sensemaking of the dataset with a radial topic visualization (
Figure 4
).
After search is completed, users can switch to a second page of the interace with four views (
Figure 3
).
The Plan View shows the semantic tasks selected from the decomposition stage.
The Execution View shows the primitive tasks generated by the system.
The Evaluation View shows the evaluation criteria for each primitive task.
Each view instantiates a computation graph that can be interactively adjusted and executed.

Inspection View
(
Figure 3
-d) shows the details of a user-selected node and the corresponding execution or evaluation results.
Next, we introduce each view in more detail and discuss their design considerations.

4.1.
Searching View

At the top of the Searching View, user can input their goal in natural language, and then clicks “Decompose” to start the search process (
Figure 2
-a).
As the MCTS algorithm proceeds, semantic tasks are incrementally added to the canvas, with each node representing one task (
Figure 2
-b).
For each node, the agent generates: (1) a task label and description, (2) an explanation for why this task is needed, and (3) scores for three criteria: task complexity, coherence with the parent node, and importance for achieving the overall goal. Each criterion is scored on a discrete Likert scale from 1 to 5, where higher values are more desirable. These scores are generated using LLM judges. On a high level, the MCTS algorithm uses the aggregation of these scores as reward values to guide the selection of expansion nodes.
More technical details are introduced in
Subsection 5.2
.

4.1.1.
Layout

We employ the Sugiyama graph layout method
(
Misue et al. 1995
)
to visualize the search tree. Each expansion adds
k
candidate semantic tasks, shown as child nodes. In this layout, any path from the root to a leaf represents a complete analytics plan, which users can select to proceed to the Execution stage. The layout avoids node overlaps and supports zooming and panning for easier exploration.

4.1.2.
Visual Encodings

To help users monitor and steer the search process, we provide both textual and visual encodings. Each node displays three discrete scores (complexity, coherence, importance), as well as a continuous reward value computed from their aggregation. All scores use a shared red-to-green color scale, with green indicating more desirable values, to ensure visual consistency. Each scoring criterion is represented by a unique icon and rendered as a Likert scale. Users can hover over icons to view explanations or click to manually adjust the scores. The reward value is displayed as a horizontal bar at the top of the node, enabling users to quickly assess node quality and identify low-scoring branches.

We also distinguish three types of nodes in the visual design: the node selected for expansions, newly generated nodes, and the nodes on the highest-value path.
These encodings support effective user control over the search direction and help users focus on promising decomposition plans.

(b) Based on the plan, the system generates an executable pipeline. Each node is a primitive task with label, description, and execution parameters. Users can click the “Execute” button to execute a node.

4.1.3.
User Interactions

The Searching View supports several kinds of user interaction.
(1) To
control
of the search process, users can toggle the “Streaming Mode” to switch between streaming or step-by-step generation. They can also change the search strategy between “Balanced”, which uses the UCT selection strategy
(
Kocsis and Szepesvári 2006
)
, or “Exploit”, which uses the greedy strategy.
(2) To
make sense of
the search results, users can expand a node to inspect its description, or hover over a score icon to inspect an AI-generated explanation.
(3) To
edit
the tree, users can regenerate or delete a branch, and add children to a node.
(4) To
provide feedback
on the reward, users can change the scores by clicking the scoring criterion icon and entering an optional explanation. These changes are recorded and taken into account in future scoring. Users can also directly edit the definition of a criterion (
Figure 3
-d).
(5) Finally, users can
select a path
as the analytics plan, and move on to the execution stage by clicking the “Execution” button at the top.

4.2.
Dataset Inspection View

Sensemaking of the dataset is a critical auxiliary step in text analytics. Previous works on supporting sensemaking in text data
(
Grootendorst 2022
;
Lam et al. 2024
)
have found that topics provide the most helpful information.
VIDEE provides sensemaking support with the Dataset Inspection View, which consists of a paged list of documents in the dataset, and a topic radial chart (
Figure 4
).

The topic radial chart adapts the uncertainty chart introduced by Lee et al.
(
Lee et al. 2025b
)
, which visualizes the distribution of a numerical attribute and the distribution of the topics in a compact way.
The chart divides the canvas into regions that represent topics.
Each document is represented as a circle and placed in the corresponding region. The distance from the document to the center of the canvas encodes a numerical value, which can be uncertainty or any evaluation scores.

This design allows users to quickly get a sense of the distribution of the encoded value compared to the distribution of topics.
The relative size of each topic can be visually estimated by the area or the angle of the topic region, and the absolute size can be estimated by the number of circles in the region.
The system also supports interaction on the documents: Clicking on a circle triggers the list view to automatically navigate to the clicked document.

In Dataset Inspection View, the topic radial chart is simplified to encode only the topic of each document. In Evaluation View, we reuse the same topic layout for each document and further add the encoding of the evaluation value. This setup ensures visual continuity for users from sensemaking to evaluation.

Figure 4.
Demonstration of the Data Inspection View using a UIST paper abstract dataset. Left: a simple list view showing the raw content of each document. Right: the topic radial chart that plots the topic distribution of the documents. Each node is a document and each fan region is a topic, such as “Shape-Changing Interfaces and Interaction Techniques”. Clicking a node in the topic radial chart will highlight the corresponding document in the list view.
The topic radial chart faciliates the sensemaking process of text analytics.

4.3.
Plan View

The Plan View shows the semantic task plans selected from the Searching View.
Here, the system also shows dependencies between semantic task plans, which are hidden in the Searching View for visual clarity.
Users have the opportunity to make final adjustments to the semantic tasks before execution.
Once the semantic tasks are finalized, clicking the “Convert” button triggers the executor agent to generate a pipeline of
primitive tasks
.
Each semantic task is linked to the corresponding primitive task in the Execution View.

4.4.
Execution View

Similarly, each primitive task has a label, description, and explanation.
Each primitive task is selected from a predefined list of tasks that the system supports, consisting of well-established text analysis tasks, such as entity extraction, sentiment analysis, embedding generation, clustering, or dimensionality reduction.

4.4.1.
Compilation

As the user inspects the primitive tasks, the executor agent automatically starts the
compilation
process, which generates technical details such as input/output schema, algorithms, and hyperparameters (details are introduced in
Subsection 5.3
).
Compilation typically takes less than 10 seconds for the whole pipeline.
Once the pipeline is compiled, users can see the input output schema and parameters by clicking the “Inspect” button.
Then, users can make any adjustments on the information shown in the inspection view (
Figure 3
-d).

4.4.2.
Execution and Results

Once a node is compiled, it can be executed using the “Execute” button. The executor agent will automatically fetch the data, run the specified functions, and collect the results.
The results can be inspected in their raw form in the Inspection View (
Figure 3
-e).
This inspection of results allows users to perform a quick sanity check to identify obvious errors.
Users can edit and re-execute each node to refine the pipeline.

4.5.
Evaluation View

Evaluation of a node can be performed once execution is complete.
Currently, the system only supports LLM judges as evaluations, which only applies to execution nodes that generate textual responses.
Methods such as dimensionality reduction or cluster analysis require specific sets of evaluation metrics and are not supported.
Due to concerns that LLM judges may not accurately estimate numbers
(
Chiang and Lee 2023
)
, we restrict LLM judges to always output categorical scores.

4.5.1.
Creating an Evaluator

Users can click the “plus” button on the top right of the Evaluation view, select an execution node as the target, and describe the evaluation criterion they want to use in natural language.
The evaluator agent will automatically generate a prompt template using the description. Users can inspect the prompt template and make necessary changes.
Additionally, the evaluator agent automatically recommends
k
evaluation criteria for each execution node that generates textual responses. We empirically set
k=3
to cover a reasonable amount of criteria while not
overwhelming the user
.

4.5.2.
Result Visualization

The evaluation results are visualized in two ways (
Figure 5
).
Since the outputs of LLM judges are always categorical, we design a simple bar chart that shows the distribution of the frequency of the scores.
In addition, if the evaluation is run on documents, we use an extended version of the radial topic chart (introduced in
Subsection 4.2
) to visualize the results.
The extension divides each topic region into subregions, each representing a category. The documents assigned a category would fall into the corresponding subregion.
Since regions closer to the center have smaller areas and are more likely to be cluttered, the system tries to assign regions by category frequencies, where higher frequency categories are assigned with larger region, while maintaining the semantic order of the category (e.g., Low, Medium and High).
This extension of topic radial chart in evaluation inherits most of the visual designs when users are making sense of the datasets, ensuring the visual continuity between evaluation and sensemaking.

Each bar is a category generated by the LLM judges, and the height encodes how many units (e.g., documents) are assigned this category (Long or Short). Right: The topic radial chart extended to encode the categories. Each topic region is further subdivided by the categories. 5.
Backend Architecture

In this section, we first introduce an underlying specification language that is critical for connecting the system modules and user interactions.
We then introduce three system modules—Decomposer, Executor, and Evaluator—that supports the corresponding agents. In addition, we propose a conceptual framework for analysis units to guide the manipulation of execution result structures.
We discuss the associated algorithmic choices, technical considerations, and implementations.

5.1.
Specification Language

Similar to the declarative specification language (DSL) in AutoGen Studio
(
Dibia et al. 2024
)
, we have defined a JSON-based specification language tailored to text analytics to bridge agent generations and user interactions.
The specification language describes the format for three types of objects: semantic tasks, primitive tasks, and evaluator nodes.
The agents are instructed to generate responses based on the format of the specification language, while
user interactions in the interface trigger modifications of the specifications, enabling the system to support a wide range of user interventions.

5.2.
Decomposer

To efficiently and comprehensively search the decomposition space, the decomposer agent follows a generative reasoning approach
(
Xie et al. 2023
)
to iteratively search for alternative next steps of the plan.

5.2.1.
Choice of Search Algorithm

The decomposer module currently implements a Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) algorithm with several modifications to adapt to the generative reasoning approach and human intervention.
A previous work by Xie et al.
(
Xie et al. 2023
)
has shown the efficacy of generative reasoning with beam search and self-evaluation, where each reasoning step is considered a token in a sequence and rewards are given by LLM judges.
However, beam search maximizes the accumulated reward greedily, which is not ideal for text analytics pipeline,
since the value of certain steps might not be apparent until later steps are introduced.
This suggests that the planning of an analytics pipeline is not linear, and backward feedback is necessary.
The backpropagation of MCTS provides such a backward feedback, as shown in
Figure 6
.
At every iteration, the algorithm expands a node with
k=2
children as alternative next steps.
For each new child, we calculate the reward using LLM judges and backpropagate the reward until the root node.
With backpropagation, the reward of previous nodes is adjusted by the reward of later nodes, effectively providing a backward feedback.
The full detail of the MCTS algorithm is presented in the Supplemental Materials.

5.2.2.
Reward Function

We use LLMs as judges to evaluate each step in the search process.
Compared to self-consistency-based evaluation methods
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
, which aggregate model outputs based on agreement levels, LLM judges provide a more context-aware and adaptable mechanism
(
Beigi et al. 2024
)
.
We define three explicit evaluation criteria: complexity, coherence, and importance, and adopt an ensemble of LLM agents to ensure robust and interpretable scoring.
The criteria are defined as follows:

•

Complexity
assesses whether a task can be executed as a single step in a text analytics pipeline. During decomposition, it is essential to ensure that each step cannot be further divided and can be directly mapped to an executable operation. This promotes simplicity and facilitates efficient execution.

•

Coherence
evaluates the logical consistency between consecutive steps. A coherent sequence ensures that each subtask logically follows from its predecessor, avoiding contradictions, redundancies, or unrelated operations. This helps maintain a meaningful and interpretable analytical flow.

•

Importance
measures the contribution of each subtask to the overall analytical goal. Each node should be critical, essential, and significantly beneficial to achieving the final outcome. Subtasks deemed optional or peripheral are excluded to maintain focus and efficiency in execution.

Ensembled LLM Judges

Building on prior works that show the effectiveness of LLM judges using Likert-scale scores
(
Chiang and Lee 2023
)
,
we adopt an ensemble of LLM judges to evaluate each reasoning step. Each judge independently scores the three criteria on a 1–5 Likert scale. The scores are averaged across agents per criterion and then combined into a final node-level score. The overall reward of a reasoning path is computed using the geometric mean of the node-level scores, balancing both local and global quality while penalizing weak links in the chain. This approach ensures a reliable and interpretable evaluation framework.

Taking user feedback

The system supports human-in-the-loop customization during the search. Users can manually revise scores and optionally provide explanations for their decisions. These explanations are stored as few-shot prompts to fine-tune the LLM judges’ behavior in subsequent evaluations, thereby adapting to the user’s preferences over the course of searching. Additionally, users can redefine the scoring criteria themselves, enabling dynamic and domain-specific evaluation strategies.

5.2.3.
Implementation

The decomposer is implemented using the AutoGen
(
Wu et al. 2023
)
framework, which simplifies coordination among multiple LLM agents. Each scoring criterion is evaluated by a dedicated set of LLMs (namely GPT-4o
(
OpenAI 2024
)
, Claude-3.5-Sonnet
(
Anthropic 2024
)
, and Gemini-2.0
(
Google 2025
)
in our implementation), forming an ensemble of nine evaluators. Each agent receives the criterion definition, scale explanation, relevant few-shot examples (including user feedback), the parent step for context, and the overall task goal.

Figure 6.
The process of Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) algorithm. Each node is a semantic task and each path represents an analytics plan. At each iteration, a node is selected with a seleection strategy (e.g., UCT selection or Greedy selection). Then, the selected node is expanded with
k=2
children. For each child, we calculate a reward value using an ensemble of LLM judges, each ensemble has three scoring criteria: complexity, coherence, and importance. The reward is back-propagated until the root node is reached.

5.3.
Executor

After the semantic tasks are finalized with the decomposer, the Executor creates an executable pipeline in which each step is defined as a primitive task.
The executor creates the pipeline in two steps:
Conversion
and
Compilation
.
In conversion, the semantic tasks are translated into primitive tasks with only task names and descriptions, allowing human intervention. Then in compilation, the input/output schema, tools to use, and parameters are generated to make the pipeline executable.
The results of conversion and compilation are defined in the specification language.
Next, we introduce the technical considerations in each step.

5.3.1.
Conversion

The executor agent generates primitive tasks from semantic tasks in a step-by-step process.
For each semantic task, the agent selects the primitive tasks from a predefined list, which we summarize from existing literature on text analytics
(
Liu et al. 2019
)
.
Each item in the list contains information such as definition and input/output data (e.g., text or embeddings), which helps ensure data structure compatability between consecutive primitive tasks.
The full list of primitive tasks is in the Supplemental Materials.

5.3.2.
Compilation

Based on the conversion result, the compilation agent generates a complete execution specification in the following procedure:

1. Generate input/output schema:

First, the executor generates the input/output schema to specify how to manage the data structure of the results, which specifies the types of input and output in Python.
The compilation agent follows a conceptual framework centered around
Analysis Unit
to infer the correct input/output schema, which is introduced in more detail in
Subsection 5.4
.

2. Create Tools and Parameters:

Give the input/output schema, the compilation agent generates the tools (i.e., function calls) and parameters needed to execute the primitive task.
The system supported tools cover most common text analytics needs, such as prompt tool, clustering tool, or data transformation tool.
For prompt tool, we generate a prompt template tailored to the primitive task and user goals.
For primitive tasks requiring local execution tools (e.g., clustering tool), we develop specialized agents tailor to the tool to pick a specific algorithm (e.g., K-Means), and hyperparameters.

3. Construct Execution Graph:

The results of compilation are stored using the specification language to allow human intervention.
Before executing a primitive task, the executor gathers all the compiled information to create an executable node in the LangGraph computation graph.

5.3.3.
Human Intervention

The Executor supports a core design principle of enabling human-in-the-loop refinement of the text analytics pipeline.
We support users to (1)
Modify task definition
, including label and descriptions, (2)
Adjust input/output schema
, and (3)
Tune execution parameters
, such as the prompt templates or the hyperparameters of clustering algorithms.

5.3.4.
Implementation

We implement the core executor functionalities with LangGraph.
This includes constructing computation graphs, managing the data structure, supporting human intervention, and supporting persistence of results and resumability of the execution process.
LangGraph also supports paralleized execution, which is critical to improve the response time for prompt tools.

Figure 7.
The conceptual framework that defines how each primitive task handles data structure changes. First, data is read from a data structure that stores all the results. Each analysis unit (e.g., document) is mapped to an intermediate object that are the direct inputs of an execution function. The Reduce step writes the execution results back to each input unit, and additionally creates a new analysis unit adds it back to the data structure.
This framework ensures all later steps can access the new units created by previous steps.

5.4.
Conceptual Framework for Analysis Units

Text analytics typically starts with text data in the form of unstructured documents, and the data structure is enriched after each primitive task.
When automatically generating a pipeline, it is critical to explicitly manage the data structure so that later primitive tasks can access new structures.
To this end,
the compilation agent follows a conceptual framework centered around
Analysis Unit
, as shown in
Figure 7
.

Analysis Unit Definition

The analysis unit specifies
what
is the input of a primitive task.
For example, when extracting entities from a list of documents, each
document
is the analysis unit.
As the execution pipeline progresses, more units can be created.
Entity extraction creates a new unit,
entity
, that later steps can utilize.
In order to support a wide variety of primitive task combinations that the user might need, we assume that every primitive task creates a new analysis unit and use the
MapReduce Paradigm
to handle how each primitive task can access the units.

The MapReduce Paradigm

The execution process of each primitive task is generated with the following paradigm: (1)
Map
: Given a list of analysis units, create a list of intermediate objects that one-to-one maps to the units. For example, from a list of documents, create a list of objects that contains the content of each document. (2)
Execute
: On the mapped result, execute a function. The function can be run on each object or the list of objects. For example, it can be extracting entities for each document or grouping the documents by topics. (3)
Reduce
: Store the execution result back to the data structure with any aggregation operations.

Automatic Unit Creation

Combining the MapReduce paradigm and the analysis unit, we automatically create new units after executing each primitive task.
In the reduce step, in addition to adding the execution results back to the input unit, we assume a new unit is created and aggregate the results to represent the new unit.
For example, after entity extraction on documents, we not only store the extracted entities to the documents, but also create a new unit named “entities”, in which each unit is an object of entity. As a result, the new unit is detached from the original unit (document), creating new options for future primitive tasks to operate on.

5.5.
Evaluator

The evaluator module implements the functionalities needed to support the evaluation of execution results.
The evaluator module is also constructed around the concept of analysis units: the evaluation is restricted to be conducted on a selected unit.
Currently, the system only supports LLM judges as evaluation methods, and thus evaluations on primitive tasks that use the prompt tool are supported, such as Summarization or Entity Extraction, while others, such as dimensionality reduction, are not supported.

5.5.1.
The No-Ground-Truth Assumption

One critical consideration when designing the evaluation is the “No-Ground-Truth” assumption, i.e., no human annotations or labels are available.
Due to the flexibility of prompts, ground-truth labels need to be recollected every time the prompt is changed, which is unrealistic.
Instead, prompt engineering with optional few-shot examples has replaced the conventional way of collecting training samples to improve model performance.

As a result of no ground-truths, conventional metrics such as F1 score or accuracy are not applicable in evaluation.
Rsearchers are still exploring new evaluation methods
(
Beigi et al. 2024
)
, and LLM judges are currently the most widely adopted way of evaluation in NLP and prompt engineering
(
Shankar et al. 2024
)
.
Compared to conventional metrics, LLM judges are typically used to evaluate
local
characteristics, i.e., evaluations are conducted per analysis unit.
For example, the textual features of summarizations
(
Lee et al. 2025a
)
, which are calculated per generated summary, or the language simplicity and scientific accuracy of scientific explanations
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
.
Although limited, these local characteristics evaluated by LLM judges facilitate users to quickly make sense of a large collection of responses.

5.5.2.
Evalautor Generation

Given a primitive task and a user description of evaluation criterion, we use a zero-shot prompt to generate an LLM judge, including the evaluator name, description, and prompt template. We restrict the LLM judges to only output categorical scores. To help users jumpstart the evaluation, we automatically generate three criteria recommendations per primitive task (if applicable).

5.5.3.
Evalautor Result Visualization

Combining the restrictions on analysis unit and categorical output, we can reliably generate visualizations for each evaluation result
Figure 5
.
As an overview, we use a bar chart to show the distribution of outputs.
Each bar is a categorical output, and height of the bar represents the number of units that are scored with the category.
In addition to bar charts, we can also extend the topic radial chart to visualize evaluation results, as introduced in
Subsection 4.5
.

6.
Quantitative Experiments

In this section, we perform technical evaluations on the decomposer module and the execution module.
For decomposer, we investigate its effectiveness by comparing the generated pipelines against human-crafted pipelines.
For execution, we use a human-crafted pipeline with generated execution parameters and compare the results on a dataset with ground truths.

6.1.
Decomposer Evaluation

The first experiment evaluates the effectiveness of the decomposer agent without human intervention.
We use two text analytics pipelines as the basis: LLooM
(
Lam et al. 2024
)
and TnT-LLM
(
Wan et al. 2024
)
.

6.1.1.
Goals

We express the respective goals of the pipelines and a description of the dataset in natural lanaugage.
For LLooM, the goal is to “
extract high-level concepts to understand research topics from a dataset of UIST paper abstracts
”.
For TnT-LLM, the goal is to “
understand the user’s intents when interacting with the chatbot from a dataset of user conversation with the Microsoft’s Bing Consumer Copilot system
”.
The description of goals and datasets are extracted from the papers.
Note that the decomposer does not have access to the content of the dataset, and relies solely on the description to receive information of the dataset.

6.1.2.
Creating Pipelines

For each paper, we first manually create a pipeline with the specification language from the pipeline introduced in the paper.
For example, the TnT-LLM pipeline is specified in four steps: Summarization (produce summaries), Data Transformation (pick a subset of summaries), Label Generation (produce a label taxonomy from the subset of summaries), and Document Classification (label the whole dataset).
We refer to this pipeline as the
manual pipeline
.
For the
generated pipeline
, we run the MCTS algorithm until a complete tree is reached, i.e., all the leaf nodes are “END” nodes.
We pick the path with the highest value as the final semantic tasks, and convert it to primitive tasks.

6.1.3.
Evaluation Method

Our requirement for generated pipelines is to be
on par with
manual pipelines, not necessarily exceeding manual pipelines.
To fairly compare the pipelines, we utilize a reasoning model (o3-mini
(
OpenAI 2025
)
) to compare two pipelines and pick a better one.
If the reasoning model does not consistently pick the manual pipeline as the better one, it means that the generated pipelines are arguably
on par with
to the manual pipelines.
To avoid position bias
(
Shi et al. 2024
)
, we randomize the order of the two pipelines in the prompt.
This process is repeated five times for each pipeline comparison, resulting in five different pipelines for each paper.
This gives us ten comparisons to evaluate the effectiveness of the decomposer module.
The prompts and comparison results generated by o3-mini can be found in the Supplemental Materials.

6.1.4.
Results and Analysis

Out of ten comparisons, o3-mini selected the generated pipeline as better in 6 cases (2 for LLooM and 4 for TnT-LLM).
In general, the model recognizes that both pipelines have strengths and align with the goal, showing that our generated pipelines are of comparable quality to human-crafted ones.
To provide a deeper understanding of the selection, we summarize the overarching pros and cons of the generated pipelines.

Direct and Streamlined Solutions

The generated pipelines are praised for offering a direct and streamlined solution to the goal, while manual pipelines generally have some extra technical decisions. For example, the manual pipeline for LLooM first summarizes each document into a list of bullet points, then conduct cluster analysis on the bullet points. This technical decision potentially provides some benefits, but they are not apparent. We speculate that the generated pipelines are more direct and streamlined because of the coherence and importance scoring on the path selection.

Failure to Consider Long Data

The manual pipelines are often in favor because of their consideration for handling long data. Due to the context window limitations of LLMs, both of the manual pipelines have an extra summarization step to reduce the input length. The generated pipelines often fail to consider such limitations, which we speculate is due to the lack of knowledge on the context window limitations.

6.1.5.
Cost Analysis

On average, the MCTS algorithm takes 5 seconds per expansion, including one LLM request to generate two children and 18 (
2\ children\times 3\ criteria\times 3\ judges
) parallel LLM requests.
The average cost of API usage per expansion is 0.005$.
A full tree has 80 nodes on average, which takes about 7 minutes to generate a complete tree. Note that with human intervention, users do not need to wait for the complete tree to finish.

6.2.
Executor Evaluation

For executor, we investigate the reliability of the generated prompts and execution parameters compared to human-crafted ones.
The experiment design is based on the technical evaluations of LLooM
(
Lam et al. 2024
)
.

6.2.1.
Execution pipeline

First, we recreate the pipeline of LLooM using our system with a five step pipeline:
(1) Summarization, where abstractive summaries are generated by a prompt template in the form of a bullet list, (2) Embedding Generation, where each bullet point in the list is encoded with an embedding, (3) Cluster analysis, where we run K-Means clustering on the embeddings of the bullet points, (4) Data Transformation, where the bullet points are grouped by the cluster numbers, (5) Label Generation, where the bullet points in each cluster are inserted into a prompt template to generate labels that represent the high-level concepts in each cluster.
The prompt templates and parameters for each step are automatically generated using the compilation procedure.

6.2.2.
Dataset and Metric

We use the Wikipedia dataset
(
Pham et al. 2024
)
, which includes ground-truth labels as topics. For evaluation metric, we use
concept coverage
as defined in LLooM, which measures the proportion of ground truth topics that are covered by the generated concepts. The metric uses a prompt template to identify covered concepts. In our experiment, we use the same prompt as LLooM, but run the prompt on GPT-4o to keep up with the advance in foundation models.

6.2.3.
Baselines

BERTopic
(
Grootendorst 2022
)
and GPT-4o are baselines to compare performance. Since GPT-4o has a limited context window, the documents are randomly sampled to fit the context window.

6.2.4.
Procedure

Following the technical evaluation in LLooM, we first use random samples of the dataset documents (n=210) stratifed across topics to accommodate context window limits.
Then, we execute the baseline methods and the pipeline on the sampled dataset for ten iterations.

6.2.5.
Results

As shown in
Figure 8
, the pipeline exceeds baseline coverage rate by 30% (BERTopic = 52.6%, GPT-4o = 53%, ours = 83%). This result is line with the results reported in LLooM, achieving comparable results with the human-crafted pipeline of LLooM.
This shows the reliability of using LLM generated prompts and execution parameters to replace human-crafted ones.

Figure 8.
Results of the executor experiment. On the Wikipedia dataset, our pipeline with generated parameters exceeds baseline performance achieving a 83% coverage rate (52% for BERTopic, 53% for GPT-4o).

6.2.6.
Execution Errors

During the experiment, we find that the executor sometimes picks the wrong analysis unit for a primitive task during compilation.
For example, consider a pipeline with two steps: Clustering Analysis and Label Generation (for the clusters).
For the second task, Label Generation, the correct analysis unit is the
cluster
, but the executor might pick
document
as the analysis unit.
These errors are difficult to detect and recover, and currently can only rely on human intervention to address.
This error is taken into account during the user study.

7.
User Study

To evaluate the usability and utility of VIDEEand understand how data analysts might use it to conduct text analytics, we conducted a qualitative user study on participants with varying levels of experience in text analytics.

7.1.
Study Design

Recruitment and Participants

We recruited six graduate students from research labs in the field of NLP or data science as participants.
All participants have experience in data science with a passing knowledge of data analysis, such as common cluster analysis or dimensionality reduction methods.
The participants are divided into three groups according to their experience in text analytics:

•

No experience
with NLP models or text analytics (
P1, P2
).

•

Some experience
using NLP models or building text analytics pipelines (
P3, P4
).

•

Expert-level experience
, including prior involvement in research projects using NLP models or text analytics (
P5, P6
).

Task Design

Participants are given the
Concept Induction
task as defined in LLooM
(
Lam et al. 2024
)
:
Generate high-level concepts from a corpus
, on a dataset with 100 UIST paper abstracts
(
Cao et al. 2023
)
.
No participants have prior knowledge of the dataset.
Considering that conducting text analytics from start to finish is cognitively demanding, we divide the task into two subtasks with an optional break.
In the first subtask, participants complete the
Decomposition
stage using the Searching View.
In the second subtask, participants complete the
Execution
and
Evaluation
stage using the Execution and Evaluation View.
To test whether the interactions support users in identifying errors in the pipeline, we picked common errors occurred during quantitative experiments and placed them into a pipeline.
The participants are given the same pipeline in the second subtask and are instructed to find and fix the errors while executing and evaluating the pipeline.

Procedure

The user study began with an introduction session that explains the background and motivation of the system, and asked for consent to participation and recording.
Then, participants went through a training session to learn about the three-stage workflow, the UI components, and interactions in the interface.
The introduction and training session typically took 30 minutes.
After that, we introduce concept induction and the UIST dataset, and let participants use the interface freely to complete the two subtasks.
Participants were encouraged to think out loud while using the interface.
This session typically took 30 to 45 minutes.
After the tasks were completed, we conducted a semi-structured interview to gain a deeper understanding of the user experience.
After the study, participants were rewarded 20$ as compensation.
On average, the user study took 1 hour and 45 minutes in total.
We recorded the screen and audio while participants were completing the task and during the interview, and transcribed them for further analysis.

7.2.
Usability and Utility Results

Drawing from our observations and feedback from the participants, we present insights into the usability and utility of VIDEE.

Clear and straightforward workflow

Overall, the user study received positive results. All participants were able to complete the given task in a reasonable amount of time.
The clear and straightforward workflow is highly praised for significantly simplifying a complicated task, especially for participants with no experience in text analytics.
The gradual introduction of semantic tasks and primitive tasks helps participants get a full picture of possible tasks and build up expectations.
P1 commented that “
(the searching tree) allows me to explore more tasks that I would not think of myself, but not in a way that deviates from my expectation, and I feel prepared before moving on to execution.
”
The positive results show the benefit and necessity of the workflow for entry-level data analysts.

Significance of automation

Participants all agree that conducting text analytics using VIDEE is more efficient than coding, even for expert-level participants.
The system is helpful in automatically handling some tedious and error-prone implementation actions, such as integrating execution functions in a script, writing prompt templates, or ensuring consistent input/output schema. Although all of this can be done manually, it is more efficient and robust to systematically connect code snippets into a coherent pipeline.

Learning Process

Participants reported a high cognitive demand to learn how to use the interface, including expert-level participants.
P6 commented that “
I would certainly not be able to use it without you going through the interface and introducing everything (the training session), and I imagine other users will be lost if the interface is thrown at them without any tutorial.
”
This is because the interface integrates many features and functionalities to cover as many actions in text analytics as possible.
Still, the interface is learnable within a 30-minute introduction and training session for participants with no experience in text analytics.
P1 commented that “
It’s not too hard, but it’s not too easy, I mean it’s like the middle part. Once you get the logic, it will become easier and natural to use
".

7.3.
Findings

During the user study, we uncovered several intriguing behavior patterns among the participants, offering valuable insights into the dynamics of human-agent collaboration in text analytics.

Searching strategy

The typical MCTS algorithm balances between exploitation (favoring nodes with high rewards) and exploration (trying less-visited nodes to discover potentially better options).
Although the balanced strategy may be suitable for automatic searching, it is not necessarily desirable in the human-in-the-loop setting.
Most participants prefer the “exploit first, explore later” strategy, i.e., first complete searching on one best path, then optionally explore the alternatives based on the best path.
This strategy avoids overwhelming the users with too many options.

Alignment vs. Suggestion

Many participants expressed the same mixed feelings about LLM judges and feedback mechanisms in the Decomposition stage.
While many participants expressed concerns about fully trusting the LLM judges, they generally did not feel it necessary to provide feedback, even though feedback mechanisms were supported and appreciated.
Participants reported that knowing that LLM judges follow explicit criteria improved their confidence in reward values.
As a result, they did not find it necessary to actively
align
their judgments with the LLM judges
(
Shankar et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2023b
)
.
Participants took the scores of LLM judges as
suggestions
rather than
ground truths
and use them only as references.
This observation reveals subtle differences in perception of LLM judges in different usage contexts.

Analysis units help understanding the pipeline

Understanding a pipeline is a cognitively demanding process, and making the analysis units explicit helped.
By making sense of the changes of analysis units, participants can easily keep track of the execution progress, anticipate results, and identify errors. Many participants started debugging by mentally comparing the expected analysis unit of the input and output for each primitive task, and eventually found the cause of errors without external support.

Gaining trust through procedural generation

Prior works have reported the challenge in gaining human trust in AI systems
(
Lee et al. 2025a
;
Degachi et al. 2024
)
.
We find that participants reported more trust in the results generated by VIDEE than other GenAI systems such as ChatGPT.
In comparison, most GenAI systems generate answers from a black-box decoding process, while participants prefer results generated from an explicit procedure, i.e., the text analytics pipeline.
As commented by P2: “
because it has some step-by-step process, it looks more convincing… and I am more confident in the results.
”
Note that procedural generation is not the same as
transparency
, which requires systems to reveal the inner workings of a model, e.g., revealing the decoding process of ChatGPT.
Instead, it is the
visibility of procedure
that gains the user’s trust.

7.4.
Suggestions For Improvement

Participants raised several constructive suggestions for improvements, as summarized below.

Better visual distinctions in searching

The highly repetitive names in the search tree create an undesired cognitive load, forcing the participants to distinguish nodes with descriptions.
As P4 commented, “
there’re so many terms that have similar names, and you do have to be more attentive to see the description, or the explanation by AI
”.
P4 also recommended adding the analysis unit of each task to the tree, to facilitate visual distinctions.
This highlights how adding contextual information—like the analysis unit—can help ease the cognitive effort required to interpret the search results.

Comparison of execution results

Both expert-level participants (P5 and P6) expressed the desire to compare searched paths based on execution results.
This suggests an extension of the linear three-stage workflow with backward feedback, in which the results from Execution and Evaluation inform the search process in the Decomposition stage.
By incorporating this feedback loop, users could refine the decomposition using the outcomes as evidence,
as opposed to using the purely deductive scores generated by LLM judges.

Support for exploratory analysis

P2 and P3 expressed interest in conducting an exploratory analysis before finalizing the execution parameters.
For example, users can perform dimensionality reduction methods (e.g., PCA) before setting hyperparameters in clustering analysis (e.g., number of clusters).
Exploratory analysis is a common preliminary step in text analytics for real-world usage, and it would be helpful if exploratory analysis is also automated with a dedidated agent.

8.
Discussion

VIDEE is specifically designed for text analytics, but it also addresses technical challenges common to agentic systems more broadly. In this section, we discuss the implications for text analytics, considerations for general agentic system developers, limitations of VIDEE, and directions for future work.

8.1.
Implications for Text Analytics

8.1.1.
Democratizing Text Analytics

Text data is arguably the most accessible data for the general public, as reading and writing are essential parts of education, yet the ability to analyze text data remains technically advanced even for data analysts
(
Liu et al. 2019
)
. This gap between familiarity and analytical capability highlights a critical barrier to participation in modern data practices: While individuals may feel confident expressing opinions, crafting arguments, or summarizing information in writing, they often lack the tools or knowledge required to systematically explore patterns, trends, or insights in textual data at scale. The complexity of text analytics stems in part from its reliance on NLP techniques, which traditionally require highly specialized knowledge.
Replacing conventional analysis methods with LLM prompting significantly reduces the technical barriers
(
Subramonyam et al. 2024
)
, indicating strong potential for democratizing text analytics
(
Lefebvre et al. 2021
)
.
We believe VIDEE is a first step towards this future, offering a user-friendly interface that simplifies the complexities of text analytics while ensuring the reliability and validity of results.
While VIDEE exhibits a steep learning curve that is not suitable for the general public, it can benefit a wide range of professionals who have strong desire to analyze text data, such as journalists and social science researchers
(
Drápal et al. 2023
)
.
By democratizing text analytics, we envision a future where more people can leverage this valuable resource to explore, understand, and act upon the vast amounts of information embedded in written content.

8.1.2.
Balancing Rigor and Usability

While democratizing text analytics seems to be a desirable future, it is also critical to avoid issues such as the overreliance
(
Passi and Vorvoreanu 2022
)
on AI. Relying too heavily on automated tools without understanding the underlying processes can lead to oversimplification and erroneous conclusions. Therefore, non-experts must also be aware of alternative methods, properly handling the coherence between methods, and avoiding false findings that may arise from incorrect assumptions or uncritical reliance on a single technique.
To support such a process, the system must bridge the gap between technical sophistication and human interpretability.
This creates a dilemma for the system design: On the one hand, the system needs to enforce certain engineering practices to ensure the reliability of the results. On the other hand, the system must remain user-friendly, supporting intuitive interaction, minimizing cognitive overhead, and enabling users to focus on reasoning and insight generation rather than the complexity of the underlying tools.
The design of VIDEE successfully navigates these trade-offs by incorporating design considerations such as the gradual introduction of semantic and primitive tasks, the conceptual framework for analysis units, and effective visualization design.

8.2.
Implications for Agentic Systems

8.2.1.
Improving Reliability of Agentic Systems

Current agents are implemented by non-deterministic foundation models.
Consequently, ensuring the reliability of the agentic system is a longstanding challenge for developers.
The design of VIDEE breaks down text analytics into multiple stages with a clear separation of human and agent responsibilities.
This ensures that the agents can respond in a relatively controlled manner.
Another benefit of the design is that the user and the agent collaborate on an explicitly specified
plan
. The plan ensures that the user is aware of the
actions
that the agents might take, e.g., the primitive tasks to complete in a text analytics pipeline.
Although such a collaboration paradigm imposes cognitive and metacognitive demand
(
Tankelevitch et al. 2024
)
on users, the human monitoring and control of agents would drastically improve the reliability of the system, outweighing the introduced demand.

8.2.2.
LLM Alignment and Trust

In most cases, practitioners still have concerns about LLM judges
(
Shankar et al. 2024
)
even when alternative approaches perform worse.
In VIDEE, LLM judges are heavily used, but participants did not feel the need to perform alignment.
We argue that this is due to the dynamic between human and agent as designed by the three-stage workflow:
The users see themselves as managers and perceive the LLM judges as consultants, i.e., the comments of consultants are helpful, but the manager makes the final decision.
Contrary to other usage contexts, where LLM judges are treated as replacements for ground truths,
our human-agent dynamic facilitates the metacognition
(
Tankelevitch et al. 2024
)
process in GenAI systems.
Our findings reveal that simply changing the human-agent collaboration dynamic can reduce the necessity of LLM alignment and foster trust in agentic systems.

8.3.
Limitations and future work

8.3.1.
User study limitations

There are some limitations in the study design. Six participants is not a large sample size and the recruitment could be considered biased: all participants are graduate students. Despite these limitations, the participants are representative of our target users, entry-level data analysts in text analytics, and we were able to discover interesting findings regarding the human-agent collaboration workflow.

8.3.2.
Limitations of the system

First, the system relies heavily on parallelized cloud-based LLM queries to maintain acceptable response time and interactivity, which is not transferable to local LLM queries.
Second, the agents might produce erronous responses, and the system lacks a comprehensive self-correction mechanism.
Third, the system does not incorporate any metadata such as dates or sources of documents.
As a result, the system does not support time-series analysis or network analysis on text data.
Finally, the system does not connect to any external knowledge base, which could be critical for some analysis contexts that require domain-specific knowledge.
We consider them to be out of the scope of our research objective.

8.3.3.
Future work

For future work, we plan to incorporate conversational agents that can translate user commands to changes on the text analytics pipeline using the specification language. This will significantly reduce the learning barrier and improve user experience.
The system could also be improved by connecting the results of execution and evaluation to the decomposition stage.
For the evaluation stage, we plan to significantly improve the applicability of evaluator nodes and visualizations.
Evaluation methods beyond LLM judges should be supported for tasks like cluster analysis or dimensionality reduction.
Visualization researchers have been exploring ways to generate charts
(
Chen et al. 2025a
;
Chen et al. 2025b
;
Tian et al. 2025
)
.
Under the analysis unit framework, it is feasible to incorporate these methods to automatically generate visualizations for evaluation.
Moreover, VIDEE can significantly improve the reproducibility of existing bespoke visual text analytics systems
(
Jänicke et al. 2017
;
Liu et al. 2019
)
.
To use these bespoke sytems, one can recreate the text analytics pipeline proposed in respective papers and importing the results to the visualization systems.
Finally, VIDEE is highly extensible, as it is implemented with common open-source frameworks.
We plan to integrate the system with tools in the open-source ecosystem, e.g., LangSmith
(
LangSmith 2025
)
, which provides profiling support on LLM usages.

9.
Conclusion

In this work, we build upon recent advances in agentic systems and develop VIDEE, which reduces the technical barrier of text analytics.
The system sheds light on promising directions for democratizing text analytics, and
we discover interesting insights regarding the impact of human-agent dynamics in the reliability of agentic systems and LLM alignment.
In the future, we plan to extend VIDEE to enhance its impact, including incorporating conversational agents, supporting more evaluation methods and visualizations, and integrating with the open-source community of agentic systems.

Appendix A
Monte-Carlo Tree Search Algorithm for Generative Reasoning

In this section, we present the full technical modications made to the Monte-Carolo Tree Search algorithm (MCTS).

A.0.1.
Modifications of MCTS

To adapt MCTS to generative reasoning, we make several modifications to the original MCTS algorithm.

LLM judges as rewards

To improve the reliability of LLM judges, we explicitly define three categorical scoring criteria (complexity, coherence, and importance) and use an ensemble of LLM judges.
The scores are aggregated to produce a continuous reward value. More details are introduced in
Subsubsection 5.2.2

Replacing random playouts

Second, we calculate the reward for all children after each expansion.
The original MCTS algorithm runs playout on a randomly selected child to improve efficiency. This is not necessary when the reward function is implemented by LLM judges, since we can efficiently calculate the rewards for all children. This also simplifies the backpropagation step for the user to understand, because all the rewards of the children are backpropagated after each iteration, instead of a randomly selected child.

Human Intervention

By integrating the MCTS results in the specification language, the search process can be paused for human intervention after each iteration. When resumed, the search continues on the current tree as defined in the specification language, which allows human intervention to be incorporated in subsequent iterations.
