Penetrating brain injury with a nail gun: technical considerations and the role of cerebral angiography. Illustrative case

The popularization of nail guns in the late 20th century led to a rapid increase in injuries associated with their use.1,2 Between 2001 and 2005, approximately 37,000 patients were treated annually in emergency departments for nail gun–related injuries, reflecting a threefold rise from the early 1990s. Many of these injuries were work related, particularly among construction workers.3,4 Most injuries were accidental from unintentional discharge or misfire and predominantly occurred in males with a mean age of 27 years.3,5–7

Nail gun injuries are relatively rare compared with other forms of traumatic brain injuries.2,8 The clinical presentation varies widely for nail gun injuries, ranging from asymptomatic to fatal, and depends on structures transgressed and injured.9,10 CT of the head (CTH) and CT angiography (CTA) are essential for detecting early intracranial hemorrhage and vascular injuries as the incidence of vascular injury associated with penetrating trauma is up to 20%.11–14 Treatment involves surgical intervention with angiography, but there is significant variability regarding the order of operations as well as surgical timing, prophylactic antibiotic regimen, and operative approach.8,10,13

Here, we describe the case of a patient presenting with a work-related left parietal lobe nail gun injury and discuss critical management considerations including the use of intraoperative angiography to assess for vascular injury.

Illustrative Case

Case Presentation

A right-handed male in his 40s with no significant past medical or surgical history presented to the emergency department of a nearby trauma hospital after sustaining a nail gun injury to the head while at work. He was kneeling on the ground while installing a door when a coworker working above him accidentally fired a nail gun toward the patient’s head. The patient lost consciousness for an unknown period until emergency medical services arrived. On arrival to the emergency department, the patient had stable vital signs and a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15. He was administered a combined tetanus, diphtheria, and pertussis vaccination in the emergency department. Routine laboratory values including infectious and coagulation markers were normal. On neurosurgical evaluation, his only complaints were a mild headache and dizziness. Review of systems was otherwise negative. On physical examination, the nail entry site was identified overlying the left parietal region, but the nail head was not clearly visualized. A complete neurological examination was performed with no focal neurological deficits, including no cranial nerve, motor/sensory, or visual field deficits.

Imaging

Noncontrast CTH was performed that demonstrated the retained nail entering the skull 1.5 cm to the left of midline and traversing through the left parietal lobe in a cranial to caudal direction (Fig. 1A). The entire length of the nail (4.5 cm) was embedded with the nail head in the outer table of the skull and the tip of the nail terminating 4.2 cm deep into the brain parenchyma. There was trace intraparenchymal hemorrhage and associated pneumocephalus (Fig. 1A). CTA and CT venography (CTV) of the head were also performed and did not demonstrate any vascular abnormalities (Fig. 1B).

A: Sagittal (left) and coronal (right) noncontrast CTH scans demonstrating the 4.5-cm nail traversing the left parietal lobe with associated trace intraparenchymal hemorrhage along the nail tract. B: Three-dimensional CTA reconstructions demonstrating no vascular injury.

Operative and Hospital Course

Given his stable and nonfocal neurological examination, the patient was urgently transferred overnight to our academic hospital with neuroendovascular capabilities. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit for close neurological monitoring. He was started on levetiracetam for seizure prophylaxis and broad-spectrum triple antibiotics with cefepime, metronidazole, and vancomycin.

A diagnostic cerebral angiogram was performed preoperatively in our neuroendovascular suite in order to obtain high-quality biplane angiography images. The angiograms did not identify any pseudoaneurysm, aneurysm, or any other vascular injury (Fig. 2). The patient was then taken to the hybrid operating room/angiography suite for burr hole craniotomy under general anesthesia. Intraoperative monoplane angiography was used in case of vascular injury during nail removal requiring an expanded craniotomy.

Preoperative diagnostic cerebral angiograms. Lateral (A) and anteroposterior (B) left common carotid artery injection demonstrated no vascular abnormality. Arrows indicate the nail seen on digitally subtracted images.

The patient was positioned supine with his head turned to the right on a gel doughnut to expose the left parietal boss. The puncture site of the nail was identified and sterilized. The right groin was separately sterilized and draped for intraoperative angiography. A 2-cm incision was made centered around the head of the nail. Bovie electrocautery was used to dissect the subcutaneous and galea layers down to the skull. The nail head was identified and circumferential dissection of the subcutaneous tissue was performed. Significant care was taken to ensure monopolar cauterization was not performed on the nail head to prevent transmission of current down the shaft of the nail into surrounding brain parenchyma. A high-speed drill was then used to create a small burr hole around the nail. Once the entry point of the nail into the dura mater was clearly visualized, bipolar cautery was used to coagulate the surrounding dura for hemostasis. Systolic blood pressure was maintained below 140 mm Hg during the entirety of the case and strictly during nail removal. A hemostat was used to slowly grasp the nail head and remove the nail with minimal oscillatory movements. No active bleeding was noted from the exit site. A titanium burr hole cover was placed, and the incision was copiously irrigated with antibiotic saline. Immediately after removal, an intraoperative angiogram selective of the left internal carotid artery did not identify active extravasation (Fig. 3A).

A: Intraoperative lateral cerebral angiogram showing selective left internal carotid artery injection with no extravasation. B: Immediate postoperative sagittal CTH scan demonstrating expected trace surrounding hemorrhage and pneumocephalus along nail tract. C: Two-week follow-up sagittal CTH scan showing no residual intracranial abnormalities.

The patient was extubated in the operating room with no focal neurological deficits. Postoperative CTH was performed that demonstrated trace hemorrhage and expected pneumocephalus along the nail tract (Fig. 3B). He was then transported to the intensive care unit for strict neurological and blood pressure monitoring. He remained at his neurological baseline and was discharged home on postoperative day 1 with a 2-week course of doxycycline and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for prophylaxis.

Follow-Up

The patient was seen in clinic for routine follow-up 2 weeks after being discharged from the hospital. He reported no fevers, had no focal neurological deficits, and his incision was healing well. CTA of the head was performed which demonstrated resolution of the previously seen small-volume hemorrhage and no delayed pseudoaneurysm (Fig. 3C). A delayed catheter-based diagnostic cerebral angiogram was planned, but the patient was lost to follow-up.

Discussion

Observations

Nail gun injuries are a relatively uncommon cause of penetrating brain injuries, but they can be associated with significant morbidity and mortality. The overall mortality for both intentional self-inflicted and unintentional nail gun injuries is approximately 10%.15 The rate of postoperative complication is up to 23.8%.15 Several complications have been previously reported, including intracranial hemorrhage, sinus injury, infection, and seizures.16 Given the rarity of nail gun injuries, management strategies differ greatly and there is a paucity of agreed on standards.

While operative intervention for nail removal remains the standard of care, there is significant variation in perioperative management of vascular injury. Some reported cases either did not include catheter-based angiography if noninvasive imaging was negative or only utilized preoperative catheter-based angiography if there was confirmed vascular injury on noninvasive imaging.7,8 Other authors performed catheter-based angiography in patients with negative noninvasive imaging if the nail was near the skull base, close to major arterial and/or venous structures, or associated with intracranial hematomas.13 Most authors agree that follow-up vessel imaging is important to assess for traumatic aneurysms and delayed vascular injury. There remains equipoise, however, on timing for follow-up vascular studies and the most appropriate imaging modality. CTA, magnetic resonance angiography, and catheter-based cerebral angiography are all reported methods of assessing for delayed vascular injury.10,13,17–19 There is a paucity of data on intraoperative angiography after intracranial nail removal.

In this case, we demonstrate our methodology for safe removal of a penetrating object and critical perioperative management considerations for mitigation of complications.

Lessons

First, patients presenting with penetrating nail gun injuries to the brain require rapid, multidisciplinary evaluation. On arrival to the emergency department, standard trauma protocols should be followed, including initial assessment and stabilization by the emergency department/trauma surgery team. Once the patient has been deemed stable from a neurological and cardiopulmonary standpoint, cranial imaging should be performed, including both noncontrast CTH and CTA/CTV, to assess for both arterial and venous vascular injuries. Simultaneously, medical management including blood pressure control and prophylactic medications should be initiated. For seizure prophylaxis, we prescribed 7 days of levetiracetam 1000 mg twice daily based on traumatic brain injury guidelines.20,21 Tetanus prophylaxis should also be universally administered.22 Data regarding antibiotic prophylaxis for penetrating brain injuries remain limited, and retrospective studies have variable recommendations regarding the need for antibiotics, duration of therapy, and antibiotic regimen.23–25 Given the significant morbidity associated with CNS infections, we opted for a broad-spectrum triple antibiotic regimen during the perioperative period and continued the patient on oral antibiotics at discharge until he was seen at his follow-up visit.

Next, it is critical to develop a comprehensive, multidisciplinary surgical plan including preoperative diagnostic cerebral angiography to evaluate for vascular lesions such as pseudoaneurysms. Rates of vascular injury in penetrating injuries to the brain can range from 12% to 20%.8,26 Traumatic pseudoaneurysms are rare and have a high rupture risk with significant morbidity. Their clinical presentation is often in a delayed manner, which makes diagnosis challenging.18,19,27–29 Additionally, we elected to perform preoperative angiography as the presence of a pseudoaneurysm could significantly affect our surgical approach.17 Treatment options for intracranial pseudoaneurysms vary depending on morphology, location, and surgeon preference. Broadly, internal carotid artery pseudoaneurysms near the skull base may be more amenable to endovascular techniques, while distal lesions may be treated with either parent vessel sacrifice or surgical clipping.28 Separately, some previously reported cases have involved a significantly larger craniotomy in case of substantial intraoperative hemorrhage.8,30 By thoroughly understanding the surrounding vessels and ruling out any major vascular injury, we were able to confidently remove the nail and confirm that there was no extravasation using intraoperative angiography. This strategy allowed us to avoid a large craniotomy and instead remove the entire nail through a small burr hole just larger than the diameter of the nail head. While the patient in this case was lost to follow-up, we recommend interval follow-up with diagnostic cerebral angiography to assess for delayed pseudoaneurysm formation.

In summary, nail gun injuries are rare forms of penetrating brain injuries, and optimal management requires comprehensive, multidisciplinary care. One of the most critical considerations is the real-time application of intraoperative angiography to facilitate safe, efficient nail removal. While a tailored approach to each patient is required, we hope this case provides a framework to aid other clinicians in developing a comprehensive surgical plan that is both safe and effective.
