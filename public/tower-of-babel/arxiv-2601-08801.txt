Extinction in Reaction Network Models
Pranav Agarwal, Gheorghe Craciun, Abhishek Deshpande, Jiaxin Jin

Abstract
In this paper, we study extinction in dynamical systems generated by reaction networks. We introduce two notions: weak extinction and strong extinction , and relate them to the structure of the underlying network through Lyapunov functions and LaSalle’s invariance principle. In particular, for all deficiency-zero networks that are not weakly reversible, we provide a geometric construction of linear Lyapunov functions. Using these functions, we establish that if these networks have bounded invariant subspaces, then they must exhibit weak extinction within every such subspace. Also, for linear networks that are not weakly reversible, we show that every species outside a terminal strongly connected component undergoes strong extinction. Moreover, in order to further emphasize the difference between weak and strong extinction, we construct an example of a reaction system (based on the Ivanova network) that exhibits weak extinction for all the species, but does not exhibit strong extinction in any species.

Introduction

Systems of polynomial equations frequently arise in models of biological interaction networks. A fundamental question in this context is whether a particular species can
persist
or go
extinct
.
Persistence, defined as the property that no species goes extinct, has been studied extensively.
Starting from the work of Feinberg
[
19
]
, who conjectured that weakly reversible reaction networks are persistent, subsequent studies have established persistence in specific network classes. In particular, two-dimensional endotactic networks and weakly reversible networks with two-dimensional stoichiometric subspaces have been shown to be persistent
[
11
,
34
]
, and strongly endotactic networks were proven to be persistent in
[
24
]
.

Our focus here is on
extinction
, which has biological significance. In epidemiology, it can be used to identify conditions for eradicating a certain type of infection, which is important for therapeutic design
[
18
,
26
]
. In the pharmaceutical industry, it can be used to selectively flush out a particular toxic species
[
2
,
23
,
36
]
. This highlights that extinction is not merely a theoretical outcome of a dynamical system, but also a clinically meaningful endpoint.
Determining when and how extinction occurs provides key insights into the long-term behavior of biological systems.

The study of extinction is closely linked to the dynamics and stability of these networks. Papers by May
[
33
]
showed that stability depends on the structure of interactions among species rather than on system complexity.
Despite its relevance, the notion of extinction in reaction networks remains relatively underdeveloped. One line of work
[
29
]
introduces an
extinction event
for networks with a discrete state space, such as those modeled by discrete-state Markov chains. The approach constructs a modified network and verifies structural properties of this auxiliary network to determine whether the original system exhibits an extinction event. A computational package implementing these criteria was later introduced in
[
30
]
.

In this paper, we introduce the notions of
weak extinction
and
strong extinction
. We say that a mass-action system exhibits
weak extinction
if for some initial condition, there exists a species
X_{i}
such that the corresponding solution
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
satisfies

\liminf\limits_{t\to\infty}x_{i}(t)=0.

Further, a mass-action system exhibits
strong extinction
if for some initial condition, there exists a species
X_{i}
such that the corresponding solution
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
satisfies

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}x_{i}(t)=0.

A detailed formulation of these two notions is provided in Definition
3.1
.

By definition, strong extinction in species
X_{i}
implies weak extinction in species
X_{i}
. However, the converse does not hold. In Example
3.9
, for a system with initial condition
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
, the corresponding solution
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
satisfies

\liminf_{t\to\infty}x_{i}(t)=0,\ \text{ but }\ \lim_{t\to\infty}x_{i}(t)\neq 0,

illustrating that weak extinction can occur without strong extinction.

Structure of the paper.

In Section
2
, we review fundamental definitions and basic properties of reaction networks.
In Section
3
, we introduce the notions of weak extinction and strong extinction.
Sections
3.1
and
3.2
focus on Lyapunov functions for reaction networks. In Theorem

, we apply LaSalle’s invariance principle to show that if a mass-action system admitting a strict Lyapunov function has a stoichiometric subspace containing only finitely many equilibria, then every trajectory converges to a unique nonnegative equilibrium.

Section

3
Extinction

In this section, we focus on the concept of extinction, in which the concentrations of certain species converge to zero. We introduce two notions of extinction in reaction networks:
weak extinction
and
strong extinction
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
be a mass-action system.

(a)

(G,\boldsymbol{k})
is said to exhibit
weak extinction
if for some initial condition
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
, there exists a species
X_{i}
, such that the solution
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
satisfies

\liminf\limits_{t\to\infty}x_{i}(t)=0.

In this case, we will say that the mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
exhibits weak extinction in species
X_{i}
.

(b)

(G,\boldsymbol{k})
is said to exhibit
strong extinction
if for some initial condition
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
, there exists a species
X_{i}
such that the solution
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
satisfies

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}x_{i}(t)=0.

In this case, we will say that the mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
exhibits strong extinction in species
X_{i}
.

In what follows, we show that certain families of reaction networks exhibit weak or strong extinction. Our analysis relies on the properties of Lyapunov functions together with LaSalle’s invariance principle.

3.1
Lyapunov Functions for Reaction Networks

In this section, we introduce the notion of a
Lyapunov function
.
Using LaSalle’s invariance principle, we show that, under certain additional assumptions, systems with strict Lyapunov functions possess robust dynamical properties.

Definition 3.2

(
[
31
]
)
.

Let
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=f(\boldsymbol{x})
be a dynamical system, where
\boldsymbol{x}\in D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a continuous function.

(a)

A continuously differentiable non-constant function
\mathcal{V}\colon D\to\mathbb{R}
is a
Lyapunov function
for the system
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=f(\boldsymbol{x})
on
D
if it satisfies

\dot{\mathcal{V}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\nabla{\mathcal{V}}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot f(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 0\ \text{ for all $\boldsymbol{x}\in D$}.

(b)

Moreover, let
E=\big\{\boldsymbol{x}\in D\mid f(\boldsymbol{x})=0\big\}
. A Lyapunov function
\mathcal{V}\colon D\to\mathbb{R}
is called a
strict Lyapunov function
on
D
if it further satisfies that
\dot{\mathcal{V}}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
if and only if
\boldsymbol{x}\in E
.

Remark 3.3
.

(a)

Lyapunov functions are fundamental tools for analyzing the qualitative behavior of dynamical systems. In particular, the existence of a strict Lyapunov function ensures that the system admits no periodic trajectories. Moreover, if a Lyapunov function is locally positive definite, it can be used to establish the stability of an equilibrium.

(b)

In reaction network theory, the Horn–Jackson Lyapunov function

\sum\limits^{n}_{i=1}x_{i}\ln(x_{i})-x_{i}-x_{i}\ln(x^{*}_{i}),

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=(x^{*}_{1},\ldots,x^{*}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
denotes a positive complex-balanced equilibrium, characterizes the stability of complex-balanced systems. This strictly convex Lyapunov function ensures that every complex-balanced equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable
[
27
]
.
In fact, complex-balanced equilibria are conjectured to be
globally
asymptotically stable, a statement known as the
Global Attractor Conjecture

[
9
,
28
]
. Craciun has proposed a proof of this conjecture in full generality
[
12
]
using the idea of toric differential inclusions
[
13
,
4
,
17
,
16
]
.

(c)

There is a direct connection between extinction and the Global Attractor Conjecture. Specifically, it would suffice to prove the conjecture by showing that complex-balanced systems cannot exhibit weak extinction for any positive initial condition
[
11
]
. Using LaSalle’s invariance principle, the absence of extinction prevents trajectories from approaching the boundary and thereby ensures convergence to the unique complex-balanced equilibrium within each stoichiometric compatibility class.

We next recall
LaSalle’s invariance principle
, a powerful tool that characterizes the asymptotic behavior of trajectories.
Note that LaSalle’s invariance principle appears under multiple formulations. In this work, we adopt the version stated below, which is well-suited for analyzing the asymptotic behavior of trajectories in mass-action systems.

Theorem 3.4

(LaSalle’s invariance principle
[

)

Suppose a dynamical system

\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{dt}=f(\boldsymbol{x})\ \text{ with }\ \boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}\ \text{ and }\ f:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(3)

Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
be a compact forward-invariant set with respect to (
3
), and let
\mathcal{V}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuously differentiable function such that

\dot{\mathcal{V}}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 0\ \text{ for all $\boldsymbol{x}\in\Theta$.}

Define the set
\Phi=\big\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Theta\mid\dot{\mathcal{V}}(\boldsymbol{x})=0\big\}
and let
\varphi
denote the largest invariant set
1

.
Then, for every solution
\boldsymbol{x}(t)

We now present two results that apply LaSalle’s invariance principle to characterize the long-term behavior of dynamical systems admitting a (strict) Lyapunov function.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
be a mass-action system, and let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
be a compact forward-invariant set of
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
.
Suppose that
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
admits a strict Lyapunov function on

If there exists a stoichiometric compatibility class
\mathcal{S}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}
such that
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
admits only finitely many non-negative equilibria in
\cl(\mathcal{S}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}})

\boldsymbol{x}(t)
of
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
with initial condition
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\cap D
satisfies

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\boldsymbol{x}(t)=\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\ \text{ for some non-negative equilibrium $\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\cl(\mathcal{S}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}})$}.

Proof.

For the system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
, there exists a strict Lyapunov function
\mathcal{V}(\boldsymbol{x})
on a compact forward-invariant set
D
such that

\dot{\mathcal{V}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\nabla{\mathcal{V}}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot f(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 0\ \text{ for all $\boldsymbol{x}\in D$}.

Moreover,
\dot{\mathcal{V}}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
if and only if
\boldsymbol{x}\in\big\{\boldsymbol{x}\in D\mid f(\boldsymbol{x})=0\big\}
.
Since
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\cap D
, LaSalle’s invariance principle implies that

\omega(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\subseteq\big\{\boldsymbol{x}\in D\mid f(\boldsymbol{x})=0\big\}.

(4)

Recall that
D
is a compact forward-invariant set of
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
.
Note that omega-limit sets of bounded trajectories are connected and
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
admits only finitely many non-negative equilibria in
\cl(\mathcal{S}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}})
.
Thus, we conclude that
\omega(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})

\boldsymbol{x}^{*}

\omega(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\{\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\}.

Together with (

\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\cl(\mathcal{S}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}})

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\boldsymbol{x}(t)=\boldsymbol{x}^{*}.

3.2
Lyapunov Functions for Deficiency-Zero, Non-Weakly Reversible Networks

In this section, we analyze deficiency-zero networks that are not weakly reversible and present an explicit construction of Lyapunov functions for the associated systems.

We begin by recalling Stiemke’s Theorem, which will be used later in the subsequent analysis.

Theorem 3.11

(Stiemke’s Theorem
[
35
]
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2},...,\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then exactly one of the following statements holds:

(a)

There exists constants
c_{1},c_{2},....,c_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}=\mathbf{0}.

(b)

There exists a vector
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\leq 0\ \text{ for all }\ 1\leq i\leq k,

with strict inequality holding for at least one
i
.

Theorem 3.12
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a reaction network. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

G
is not consistent.

(b)

There exists a vector
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\boldsymbol{w}\cdot(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y})\leq 0
for every reaction
\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E
, and the inequality is strict for at least one reaction in
E
.

(c)

For all choices of rate constants
\boldsymbol{k}
, there exists a linear Lyapunov function
\mathcal{V}
for the mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
such that
\dot{\mathcal{V}}(\boldsymbol{x}(t))<0
for every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
.

(d)

For all choices of rate constants
\boldsymbol{k}
, the mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
does not admit a positive equilibrium.

Proof.

We show the equivalence of the four statements by establishing the following chain of implications:

(a)\Rightarrow(b)\Rightarrow(c)\Rightarrow(d)\Rightarrow(a).

(a)\Rightarrow(b)
:
This follows directly from Theorem
3.11
.

(b)\Rightarrow(c)
:
Define the linear function
\mathcal{V}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}x_{i}
.
We claim that, for any choices of rate constants
\boldsymbol{k}
,
\mathcal{V}
is a Lyapunov function for the mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
.
Differentiating
\mathcal{V}
with respect to time along trajectories of
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
, we obtain

\dot{\mathcal{V}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{d\mathcal{V}}{d\boldsymbol{x}}\cdot\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{dt}=\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E}k_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y})=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E}k_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{y}}\big(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y})\big).

From part
(b)
,
\boldsymbol{w}\cdot(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y})\leq 0
for every reaction
\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E
, and there exists at least one reaction for which the inequality is strict.
Hence,

\dot{\mathcal{V}}(\boldsymbol{x})<0\ \text{ for all }\ \boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n},

and we prove the claim.

(c)\Rightarrow(d)
:
Given rate constants
\boldsymbol{k}
, let
\mathcal{V}
be a Lyapunov function for the dynamical system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
, such that

\dot{\mathcal{V}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{d\mathcal{V}}{d\boldsymbol{x}}\cdot\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{dt}<0\ \text{ for all }\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}.

This implies that the system has no positive equilibrium, since
\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{dt}\neq 0
for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
.

(d)\Rightarrow(a)
:
We prove the contrapositive: if
G
is consistent, then there exists a choice of rate constants
\boldsymbol{k}
such that the mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
admits a positive equilibrium.

Since
G
is consistent, there exists positive scalars
\big\{\lambda_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}>0\mid\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E\big\}
such that

\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E}\lambda_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y})=0.

Define the rate constants
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}})_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}_{>0}
by

k_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}=\lambda_{\boldsymbol{y}\to\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}>0.

Consider the mass-action system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
evaluated at the point
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=(1,\ldots,1)
. We have

\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{dt}\mid_{\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=(1,\ldots,1)}=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in E}k_{\boldsymbol{y}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{1})^{\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y})=0.

Therefore,
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=(1,\ldots,1)
is a positive equilibrium of the system
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
.
∎

3.3
Extinction for Two Classes of Reaction Networks

In this section, we identify two classes of reaction networks in which at least one species goes extinct by using Lyapunov functions and LaSalle’s invariance principle.

First, we show that every deficiency-zero, non–weakly reversible network admits a species exhibiting weak extinction.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Since
G
is a deficiency-zero reaction network that is not weakly reversible, Proposition

shows that
(G,\boldsymbol{k})
admits a linear Lyapunov function
\mathcal{V}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}

\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\bf(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{y})\leq 0

This, together with (

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{V}(\boldsymbol{x}(t))=0\ \text{ only if }\ \boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}.

Lemma 3.17

(Barbalat’s Lemma

(b)

4
Discussion

This work establishes a connection between the existence of Lyapunov functions and extinction in reaction networks.
In contrast to the
persistence conjecture

[
1
,
11
,
21
,
24
]
, the concept of extinction has received comparatively less attention.
We introduce two types of extinction:
weak extinction
and
strong extinction
, and relate them to the existence of Lyapunov functions via LaSalle’s invariance principle.
Our results show that trajectories of mass-action systems admitting strict Lyapunov functions converge to a unique nonnegative equilibrium. Moreover, we construct a linear Lyapunov function for deficiency-zero, non–weakly reversible networks and show that such systems exhibit weak extinction in at least one species when the stoichiometric compatibility class is bounded.
Finally, for a non–weakly reversible reaction network, we prove that strong extinction occurs in any species not belonging to a terminal strongly connected component when the network is first-order.

While beyond the scope of the present work, the framework developed here suggests several directions for future research.
For deficiency-zero systems that are not weakly reversible, it remains to determine conditions under which one can identify the species that undergo weak extinction.
This involves understanding how features of the network structure or stoichiometric constraints influence which species approach extinction along trajectories.
A closely related question is to characterize additional structural or dynamical assumptions under which such systems exhibit strong extinction in a specified species.

A second direction is to obtain a more complete characterization of the capacity for weak or strong extinction in reaction networks with low-dimensional stoichiometric subspaces, particularly in one or two dimensions. For example, in the two-dimensional setting, one may ask whether there exists a “bad” reaction that inevitably induces weak or strong extinction for some or all choices of rate constants and for some or all initial conditions.
