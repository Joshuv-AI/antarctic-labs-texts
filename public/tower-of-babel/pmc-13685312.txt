Integrative microbiome and transcriptome analyses identify parity-associated alterations in rectal fecal microbiota and peripheral immune transcriptional signatures in dairy cows
Authors: Xin Li, Caixia Shi, Wenguang Zhang, Risu Na, Mingjuan Gu, Lin Zhu, Shuhan Zhao, Liye Xiong, Tiankai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncreasing parity in dairy cows is accompanied by cumulative physiological, reproductive, lactational, and management exposures that may coincide with changes in metabolic and immune status. However, the relationships among parity group, distal hindgut microbial communities, peripheral immune-related transcription, and their coordinated variation remain incompletely understood. In this cross-sectional observational study, 40 nonpregnant Holstein cows in mid-lactation without recorded disease were randomly selected from an eligible population within a commercial dairy herd and classified into naturally occurring parity groups: primiparous cows (PrC; parity = 1; n = 20) and multiparous cows (MuC; parities = 2–5; n = 20). Rectal fecal 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) gene sequencing and peripheral whole-blood RNA sequencing were integrated to characterize microbial and immune-related transcriptional differences between the two groups.ResultsMultiparous cows had lower Shannon diversity, Chao1 richness, and observed amplicon sequence variant (ASV) richness than primiparous cows, but a higher Simpson dominance index (D), consistently indicating lower α-diversity and greater community dominance in multiparous cows. A one-factor permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) based on Bray–Curtis distances indicated that parity-group membership was associated with a modest proportion of the variation in rectal fecal microbial composition (R² = 0.0581, permutation p = 0.004). Using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe), random forest, and analysis of compositions of microbiomes with bias correction 2 (ANCOM-BC2), 13 candidate genera were identified by at least two analytical approaches. Prevotellaceae_UCG-003 was more abundant in PrC, whereas Turicibacter, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Acetitomaculum, and Lachnospiraceae_NK3A20_group were more abundant in MuC. Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2 (PICRUSt2)-based inference indicated group differences in predicted functional potential related to protein synthesis, amino acid and carbohydrate metabolism, bacterial motility, chemotaxis, and signal transduction; these results represent predicted rather than directly measured microbial functions. Whole-blood RNA sequencing identified 189 differentially expressed genes in the comparison between MuC and PrC, including 116 genes with higher transcript abundance in MuC and 73 with higher transcript abundance in PrC. Overall KEGG analysis of the complete DEG set identified 38 significantly enriched pathways, many of which were related to immune and inflammatory processes. Direction-stratified KEGG analysis further indicated that broad significant immune-related enrichment was mainly associated with genes showing higher transcript abundance in PrC cows, whereas only one KEGG term remained significantly enriched among the MuC-higher genes after Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Integration of differential expression, weighted gene co-expression network analysis, and protein–protein interaction analysis prioritized matrix metallopeptidase 9 (MMP9), mitogen-activated protein kinase 13 (MAPK13), and WD repeat domain, phosphoinositide interacting 1 (WIPI1) as candidate hub genes, all of which showed higher transcript abundance in MuC. Sparse partial least squares integration further identified a multivariate association structure involving selected candidate fecal genera and immune-related genes.ConclusionsThis study provides exploratory multi-omics evidence of differences in rectal fecal microbial composition, predicted microbial functional potential, and peripheral blood immune-related transcription between primiparous and multiparous cows. The coordinated microbiome–transcriptome patterns identify candidate microbial and transcriptional features for future investigation. Because this was a cross-sectional observational study, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects of parity. Introduction

Maintaining dairy cow health, stable production performance, and milk quality is a central objective of modern dairy production. As parity increases, dairy cows accumulate repeated experiences of pregnancy, lactation, and dry periods. These cumulative physiological and reproductive experiences are associated with differences in metabolic and immune status that may also coincide with variation in health and production performance [1–4].

Dairy cows rely on plant fiber as their primary nutritional source; however, they lack endogenous enzymes capable of degrading plant polymers. Consequently, feed digestion and utilization largely depend on fermentation by the gastrointestinal microbiota. Although the rumen serves as the main site of feed fermentation and energy supply, it cannot fully digest all dietary substrates. The distal gastrointestinal tract therefore also participates in further fermentation of undigested components and may be related to host health [5]. Accumulating evidence suggests complex bidirectional crosstalk between the gut microbiome and the host immune system, and the structural and functional stability of microbial communities is important for sustaining immune homeostasis and intestinal integrity [6–8]. To date, many studies of the ruminant microbiome have focused on the rumen, particularly in relation to enteric methane production and metabolic health [9–11], whereas the distal hindgut microbiome of dairy cows remains comparatively less studied. Recent studies have reported associations between fecal or hindgut microbial profiles and production performance, mammary gland health, and peripheral immune function in dairy cows [12–14]. However, systematic investigations of parity-associated variation in rectal fecal microbial profiles and peripheral blood immune-related transcription remain limited. Because rectal fecal samples can be collected with minimal animal stress and are used here as a proxy for distal hindgut microbial communities, they provide an accessible window into microbial variation at the distal end of the gastrointestinal tract. Microbial communities in this region are positioned near the intestinal mucosal immune system and may be related to mucosal immune tone and intestinal integrity. Disruptions of microbial structure have been associated with dysregulated host immune responses and inflammation [15, 16]. Previous studies have indicated that the gastrointestinal microbial community is jointly shaped by environmental factors and dietary structure [17].

In the present study, Holstein cows without recorded disease from naturally occurring parity groups were enrolled. Rectal fecal samples, used here as a proxy for distal hindgut microbial communities, were collected to minimize animal stress, and 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) gene sequencing of rectal fecal microbiota and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of peripheral blood were performed to characterize rectal fecal microbial profiles and host immune-related gene expression profiles, respectively. Multi-omics data were integrated using sparse partial least squares (sPLS) to address the following scientific questions: (1) Characterize parity-associated differences in rectal fecal microbial diversity and composition; (2) Identify peripheral blood immune transcriptional signatures associated with parity; and (3) Explore coordinated variation between candidate microbial genera and immune-related genes.

This study identifies candidate microbial and transcriptional features associated with parity-group membership and provides a basis for future longitudinal and functional studies of microbiome–immune relationships in dairy cows.

Materials and methods

Animals and sample collection

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted on a commercial Holstein dairy farm in Hohhot, China. Farm health and production records were screened to identify 237 eligible mid-lactation, nonpregnant cows, including 125 primiparous cows (PrC; parity = 1) and 112 multiparous cows (MuC; parities = 2–5). Eligible cows had no recorded ketosis, mastitis, elevated somatic cell count, metritis, or other diagnosed disease according to routine farm health-monitoring records. No additional research-specific diagnostic screening was performed. Twenty cows were randomly selected from each parity group, resulting in 20 PrC and 20 MuC cows. The mean parity of the MuC group was 3.95 ± 0.83. Body weight, days in milk (DIM), body condition score (BCS), milk yield, and milk composition were not used as selection or matching criteria. The descriptive characteristics of the two groups are presented in Supplementary Table S1. All cows were maintained under the routine group-housing conditions of the commercial farm, without study-specific individual housing or experimental pen assignment. The selected cows may have originated from the same or different routine group pens, and individual pen identity was not retained in the research records. The total mixed ration (TMR) was formulated according to the recommendations of the National Research Council (NRC, 2001) [18] and was offered ad libitum to both parity groups. The ingredient composition and analyzed nutrient levels of the TMR are presented in Supplementary Table S2. Representative TMR samples were collected and dried at 65 °C to a constant weight for chemical composition analysis. Crude protein, neutral detergent fiber, acid detergent fiber, ether extract, ash, calcium, and phosphorus were determined according to standard feed-analysis procedures [19]. The specific target-cow assumptions used for ration formulation, including body weight, milk yield, and expected dry matter intake, were not retained in the research records. Individual and pen-level dry matter intake was not measured because the cows were managed under routine commercial group-housing conditions, the farm was not equipped with an individual feed-intake monitoring system, and pen-level feed delivery and refusals were not recorded specifically for this study. Fecal and blood samples from all cows were collected on the same day before the morning feed delivery under routine feeding conditions, without experimental fasting. Fresh fecal samples were collected directly from the rectum into sterile, nuclease-free cryovials, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction. The exact sampling time relative to morning milking was not recorded. Jugular whole blood was collected into plain red-top blood-collection tubes without anticoagulant or clot activator. Immediately after collection, one volume of whole blood was mixed with three volumes of TRIzol reagent, thoroughly homogenized by repeated pipetting and vortexing, and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. The homogenates were then transferred into sterile, RNase-free cryovials, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for no longer than one week before RNA extraction. This study was approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Inner Mongolia Agricultural University (Approval No. NND2025098), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the relevant institutional animal-welfare regulations and applicable ethical standards.

16S rRNA sequencing and data processing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from approximately 200 mg of each fecal sample using a cetyltrimethylammonium bromide/sodium dodecyl sulfate (CTAB/SDS)-based procedure. Briefly, the fecal samples were suspended in 1 mL of preheated extraction buffer containing 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 100 mM EDTA, 1.5 M NaCl, 2% CTAB, and 1% SDS. The samples were mechanically homogenized by bead beating twice for 60 s, with cooling between cycles, and subsequently incubated at 65 °C for 30 min with intermittent mixing. After centrifugation, the supernatant was extracted with an equal volume of chloroform–isoamyl alcohol (24:1, v/v) and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min. The aqueous phase was transferred to a new sterile tube, mixed with 0.7 volumes of isopropanol, incubated at − 20 °C for 30 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min to precipitate the DNA. The DNA pellet was washed with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and dissolved in sterile water. An extraction blank without fecal material was processed in parallel to monitor potential contamination. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA), and DNA integrity was evaluated by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Based on the measured DNA concentrations, qualified DNA samples were diluted with sterile water to a nominal concentration of 1 ng/µL before PCR amplification. The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primers 341 F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). Each PCR reaction contained 15 µL of Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs), 0.2 µM of each primer, and 10 ng of template DNA. The amplification program consisted of an initial denaturation at 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Biolabs; Cat. No. E7645). Library concentration and fragment-size distribution were assessed using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, respectively. Qualified libraries were subjected to paired-end sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Approximately 2.43 million valid amplicon sequences were obtained, averaging approximately 60,800 sequences per sample, with a mean merged sequence length of approximately 411 bp (Supplementary Table S3). Bioinformatic analysis of the 16S rRNA gene sequencing data was performed using the standardized EasyAmplicon2 pipeline [20]. Raw paired-end reads were merged and quality-filtered using VSEARCH (v2.21.1) according to the expected-error criterion (maxEE = 0.01) [21]. After dereplication of the quality-filtered sequences using USEARCH (v11), the UNOISE3 algorithm was applied for denoising and generation of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) with single-nucleotide resolution [22]. Chimeric and erroneous sequences were removed during the UNOISE3 denoising procedure; therefore, no additional post-denoising chimera-removal step was performed. Quality-filtered reads were mapped back to the representative ASV sequences at 100% sequence identity to generate the ASV abundance table. Taxonomic assignment was performed using the SINTAX algorithm with a confidence threshold of 0.8 against the preformatted SILVA v123 reference database provided in the EasyAmplicon2 workflow [23]. Sequences assigned to chloroplasts, mitochondria, or nonbacterial sources were removed before downstream analysis. To reduce the influence of unequal sequencing depth on microbial community diversity estimates, the ASV feature table was rarefied to 10,000 sequences per sample for alpha- and beta-diversity analyses. Alpha-diversity indices were calculated using the rarefied ASV table, and differences between the PrC and MuC groups were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The Simpson dominance index was calculated as D = ∑pi², where pi represents the relative abundance of the ith ASV; higher values indicate greater community dominance and lower diversity. p-values for the alpha-diversity comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Beta-diversity was assessed using Bray–Curtis dissimilarities, and differences in microbial community structure between parity groups were tested using a one-factor PERMANOVA with 999 permutations. Parity group was the sole explanatory variable in this analysis. Homogeneity of multivariate dispersion was evaluated using the betadisper function in the vegan package before PERMANOVA interpretation. Genus-level microbial features were evaluated using LEfSe (LDA score > 3.0), random forest, and ANCOM-BC2, and the overlap among the three analytical approaches was assessed using UpSet analysis [24–26]. Genus-level abundance profiles were centered log-ratio (CLR) transformed before machine-learning analysis. Boruta was used to select informative microbial features, which were subsequently used to construct a random forest classifier. Model performance was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation implemented in the caret package. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was calculated from the cross-validated predictions using the pROC package and was interpreted as within-cohort discriminatory performance [27]. Genera identified by at least two of the three analytical approaches were defined as parity-group-associated candidate genera (PACGs) and retained for subsequent multi-omics association analyses. Microbial functional potential was inferred using PICRUSt2 (v2.4.1), and predicted KEGG level 3 pathway abundances were generated from the ASV sequence data. Differences in predicted pathway abundances between the PrC and MuC groups were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Visualizations were generated using ggplot2 [28, 29].

Whole blood transcriptome sequencing and differential gene analysis

Total RNA was extracted and evaluated from the TRIzol-preserved whole-blood homogenates of all 40 cows using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA) [30], and RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 5300 Fragment Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and expressed as the RNA quality number (RQN). All 40 RNA samples had RQN values ≥ 7.0 and were used for transcriptome library construction and paired-end 150-bp sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. A total of 261.96 Gb of clean data was obtained from the 40 samples, with per-sample clean-data yields ranging from approximately 5.88 to 8.24 Gb and Q30 base percentages exceeding 92.98%. Sample-level sequencing quality metrics for all 40 samples are provided in Supplementary Table S4. Clean reads were aligned to the Bos taurus reference genome (GCF_002263795.3) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1), with alignment rates ranging from 89.55% to 96.55%, indicating good alignment quality [31]. Gene-level raw count matrices and transcripts per million (TPM) values were estimated using RSEM (v1.3.3) [32]. The raw count matrix was used for differential expression analysis with DESeq2 (v1.46.0), with parity group as the sole explanatory variable in the design formula (design = ~ Group), corresponding to a two-group comparison between PrC and MuC cows. p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Genes with |log₂FC| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs) [33]. TPM values generated by RSEM were used only for visualization, WGCNA, and downstream microbe–gene association analyses. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.6.0) [34] for the complete set of 189 DEGs and separately for genes with higher transcript abundance in MuC cows (log₂FC > 0; n = 116) and PrC cows (log₂FC < 0; n = 73). Genes tested by DESeq2 and successfully mapped to KEGG identifiers were used as the common background. p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and adjusted p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis and protein-protein interaction network analysis

To identify gene co-expression modules closely associated with parity, weighted gene co-expression network construction was performed using the WGCNA package in R based on the standardized TPM expression matrix [35]. Pearson correlations between genes across samples were calculated, and a soft-thresholding power of β = 8 was selected according to the scale-free topology criterion to construct the adjacency matrix. The minimum number of module genes was set to 50, and the module merging threshold was set to 0.25. The correlation between modules and parity phenotypes was obtained by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient between module eigengenes (ME) and phenotype values [36]. Modules with an absolute correlation coefficient |r| > 0.4 and p < 0.05 were defined as “parity-associated significant modules” for subsequent functional analysis. All genes in the significant modules were further subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment (implemented by clusterProfiler), and the enrichment results were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Genes were ranked according to intramodular connectivity, and the top 15 genes were selected as candidate hub genes for subsequent analysis. All genes in the significant modules identified by WGCNA were uploaded to the STRING database (v12.0, https://string-db.org/) to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, with the minimum interaction confidence threshold set to 0.7 [37]. The constructed PPI network was imported into Cytoscape (v3.10.3, http://www.cytoscape.org/), and the MCC (Maximal Clique Centrality) algorithm in the cytoHubba plugin was used to score network nodes. The top 15 genes with the highest scores were retained as candidate hub genes [38]. To prioritize hub genes with differential expression support, the top 15 PPI hub genes identified by cytoHubba were intersected with the 189 DEGs. The overlap between the top 15 WGCNA high-connectivity genes and DEGs was also assessed for comparison.

Microbe–host gene association analysis

To characterize the association structure between parity-associated microbial features and host immune-related transcriptional signatures, the 13 parity-group-associated candidate genera (PACGs) and the expression matrix of selected candidate immune-related genes were used as input data for sparse partial least squares regression (sPLS) using the mixOmics package [39]. The candidate gene set was derived from the PPI-prioritized hub genes and immune- and autophagy-related genes retained for downstream microbe–gene association analysis. Microbial abundance profiles were transformed using centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation to reduce the influence of compositional effects, whereas gene-expression values were transformed as log₂(TPM + 1) before association analysis [40]. Pairwise Spearman rank correlations were calculated between the CLR-transformed abundances of the 13 PACGs and the log₂(TPM + 1)-transformed expression levels of the 15 PPI-prioritized candidate hub genes using all 40 cows with paired microbiome and transcriptome data. p-values were adjusted across all 195 genus–gene comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, and correlations with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Both data matrices were centered and scaled within the mixOmics workflow before sPLS model fitting. The sPLS model was used to identify coordinated multivariate association patterns between PACGs and candidate immune-related genes based on variable loading weights and the model-derived association structure. A clustered image map (CIM) derived from the sPLS model was used to visualize the association structure and variable-clustering patterns between the two omics layers. All sPLS analyses and associated visualizations were performed in the mixOmics environment.

Results

Differences in fecal microbial diversity, community structure, and predicted functional potential between parity groups

Significant differences in the Shannon, Simpson dominance, Chao1, and Sobs indices were detected between the PrC and MuC groups (Adjusted p-value < 0.05; Fig. 1A). The Shannon, Chao1, and Sobs indices were higher in the PrC group, whereas the Simpson dominance index (D) was higher in the MuC group. Because higher Simpson D values indicate greater community dominance and lower diversity, these results consistently indicated lower fecal microbial richness and diversity in MuC cows. No significant difference in multivariate dispersion was detected between the PrC and MuC groups (betadisper, p > 0.05). A one-factor PERMANOVA based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities indicated an association between parity-group membership and rectal fecal microbial community composition (R² = 0.0581, permutation p = 0.004; Fig. 1B).

After characterizing community diversity and overall structural profiles, the taxonomic composition of the rectal fecal microbiota at the phylum and genus levels was further analyzed (Fig. 1C). The dominant bacterial phyla included Firmicutes (63.13 ± 0.11%), Bacteroidetes (27.25 ± 0.15%) and Actinobacteria (1.96 ± 0.06%). The predominant bacterial genera were Ruminococcaceae_UCG-005 (13.27 ± 0.07%), Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group (7.64 ± 0.05%), [Eubacterium]_coprostanoligenes_group (5.39 ± 0.05%), and Ruminococcaceae_UCG-010 (4.78 ± 0.03%). Although the PrC and MuC groups shared similar dominant phyla, the relative abundance of core genera displayed distinct intergroup specificity, which provided a basis for subsequent screening of parity-associated candidate microbial genera.

PICRUSt2 was used to infer microbial functional potential at KEGG level 3, and the top 20 predicted pathways with significant intergroup differences were identified (Fig. 1D). These predicted pathways were mainly related to ribosome biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, flagellar assembly, bacterial chemotaxis, nucleotide repair, and signal transduction. Collectively, these results indicated differences in the predicted functional potential, rather than directly measured microbial functions, of the rectal fecal microbial community between the two parity groups.

Multi-method identification of parity-group-associated candidate genera

Based on the differential analysis of microbial diversity and community structure, genus-level screening was further performed to identify candidate bacterial taxa associated with parity. First, LEfSe analysis (LDA > 3.0) was used to compare the PrC and MuC groups, and a total of 14 genera with significant intergroup differences were identified (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, a random forest classification model was constructed using Boruta-selected CLR-transformed genus-level features. According to the ranking of feature importance (Mean Decrease Accuracy, MDA), 11 genera with high contribution to the model were obtained (Fig. 2B). The random forest model based on Boruta-selected genera showed good within-cohort cross-validated discriminatory performance for parity grouping, with an AUC of 0.89 (Fig. 2C). To further evaluate intergroup microbial differences, ANCOM-BC2 was applied for genus-level differential analysis, and 36 significantly differentially abundant genera were detected (Supplementary Table S5).

The intersection analysis of the three analytical methods was conducted to screen candidate genera consistently identified by multiple approaches (Fig. 2D). Each approach identified a partially distinct set of parity-related taxa, and several genera were simultaneously detected by two or three algorithms. Finally, a total of 13 genera jointly identified by at least two methods were defined as parity-associated candidate genera (PACGs) for subsequent integrated analysis. To characterize the distribution of these candidate microbes, the relative abundance of the 13 PACGs was compared between the two groups (Fig. 2E). Distinct distribution patterns of the candidate genera were observed between the two parity groups. Prevotellaceae_UCG-003 and other related genera were enriched in the PrC group, while Turicibacter, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Acetitomaculum and Lachnospiraceae_NK3A20_group were significantly more abundant in the MuC group.

Differential expression analysis of the peripheral blood transcriptome

To further investigate the differences between the PrC and MuC groups, we compared the peripheral blood transcriptomes of the same cohort of dairy cows. A total of 189 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified from a two-group DESeq2 comparison between MuC and PrC cows, including 116 genes with higher transcript abundance in the MuC group and 73 genes with higher transcript abundance in the PrC group (Fig. 3A). Among these, genes related to immune regulation, inflammatory responses, and metabolism, including matrix metallopeptidase 9 (MMP9), solute carrier family 11 member 1 (SLC11A1), interleukin 2 receptor subunit alpha (IL2RA), CCAAT enhancer binding protein delta (CEBPD), very low density lipoprotein receptor (VLDLR), carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A), and pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4), showed significantly higher transcript abundance in the MuC group. In contrast, kelch domain containing 8 A (KLHDC8A) and the annotated locus LOC786796 showed significantly higher transcript abundance in the PrC group, suggesting parity-associated differences in peripheral immune-related transcriptional profiles.

To characterize the overall functional categories represented among these DEGs, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was first performed using the complete set of 189 DEGs. A total of 38 significantly enriched pathways were identified (Adjusted p-value < 0.05; Fig. 3B). These pathways were mainly related to immune and inflammatory processes, including primary immunodeficiency, the NF-κB signaling pathway, the FcεRI signaling pathway, the B cell receptor signaling pathway, FcγR-mediated phagocytosis, the intestinal immune network for IgA production, and the hematopoietic cell lineage. Multiple autoimmune and infection-related KEGG terms were also enriched, including autoimmune thyroid disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, asthma, and rheumatoid arthritis. In addition, pathways related to immune effector processes, including Th17 cell differentiation, leukocyte transendothelial migration, and complement and coagulation cascades, were significantly enriched. Because this analysis included genes with higher transcript abundance in both parity groups, it characterized the overall functional categories represented among the DEGs but did not distinguish the direction contributing to the enrichment.

To further clarify the direction underlying the overall enrichment pattern, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed separately for the 116 genes with higher transcript abundance in MuC cows and the 73 genes with higher transcript abundance in PrC cows. Among the MuC-higher genes, Amoebiasis was the only KEGG term that reached significance after Benjamini–Hochberg correction (Adjusted p-value < 0.05; Fig. 3C). In contrast, 34 KEGG pathways were significantly enriched among the PrC-higher genes (Adjusted p-value < 0.05; Fig. 3D). These pathways were mainly associated with immune-cell development, antigen processing and presentation, immune-receptor signaling, and immune effector functions, including primary immunodeficiency, hematopoietic cell lineage, antigen processing and presentation, the T cell receptor signaling pathway, the B cell receptor signaling pathway, the FcεRI signaling pathway, FcγR-mediated phagocytosis, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, the NF-κB signaling pathway, and the intestinal immune network for IgA production. Collectively, the combined analysis identified the overall immune-related functional landscape of the complete DEG set, whereas the direction-stratified analyses indicated that the broader pattern of significant immune-related enrichment was mainly associated with genes showing higher transcript abundance in PrC cows.

Parity-associated gene co-expression modules and immune functional characteristics

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed based on the transcripts per million (TPM) expression matrix of peripheral blood transcriptomes, and 6 co-expression modules were identified (Fig. 4A). Using parity group (MuC vs. PrC) as the trait, the correlation between module eigengenes (ME) and phenotype was calculated. The MEbrown module (463 genes) was positively correlated with the MuC group (r = 0.542, p = 0.0003), and the MEgreen module (82 genes) was also significantly positively correlated with the MuC group (r = 0.481, p = 0.00168); both were negatively correlated with the PrC group. Based on correlation strength and module size, MEbrown and MEgreen were defined as parity-associated modules.

To evaluate the overlap between parity-associated modules and the above DEGs, Venn diagrams were constructed for MEbrown, MEgreen, and DEGs (Fig. 4B). In total, 44 overlapping genes were found between MEbrown and DEGs, whereas no overlapping genes were detected between MEgreen and DEGs, suggesting that parity-related differential expression signals were mainly concentrated in MEbrown. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that no significant pathways were enriched in MEgreen (Adjusted p-value ≥ 0.05), whereas 97 significant pathways were enriched in MEbrown (Adjusted p-value < 0.05), with the top 20 shown in Fig. 4C. (Supplementary Table S6 and Supplementary Table S7). These pathways were mainly annotated to immune and inflammatory processes, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, IL-17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, JAK-STAT signaling pathway, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, complement and coagulation cascades, and B cell receptor signaling pathway, as well as multiple infection- and immune-related disease pathways (e.g., tuberculosis, Staphylococcus aureus infection, and pathways in cancer). The top 15 highly connected genes in MEbrown were selected according to intramodular connectivity to construct a co-expression network (Fig. 4D), including NLRX1, SIRPB2, CSF2RA, XDH, ARHGEF11, and PFKFB4, which have reported links to immune regulation, signal transduction, and energy metabolism.

PPI network analysis and prioritization of candidate immune hub genes

A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the 463 genes in the MEbrown module via the STRING database (confidence score > 0.70). The cytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape, with the MCC algorithm, was then applied to prioritize the top 15 candidate PPI hub genes with the highest topological centrality in the network. These 15 candidate PPI hub genes were subsequently subjected to intersection analysis with the 15 WGCNA candidate hub genes and the 189 DEGs, respectively (Fig. 5A). No overlap was observed between the WGCNA candidate hub genes and DEGs, whereas three genes were shared between the candidate PPI hub genes and DEGs: MMP9, mitogen-activated protein kinase 13 (MAPK13), and WD repeat domain, phosphoinositide interacting 1 (WIPI1). All three genes showed significantly higher transcript abundance in the MuC group (Fig. 5B), suggesting a distinct immune-related transcriptional signature in the peripheral blood of multiparous cows.

Among the 195 genus–gene pairs tested, 16 remained significant after Benjamini–Hochberg correction (Fig. 5C). Among the three DEG-supported candidate hub genes, the strongest significant correlations were observed between [Eubacterium]_oxidoreducens_group and MMP9 (Spearman’s ρ = −0.647, Adjusted p-value = 0.0013), [Eubacterium]_coprostanoligenes_group and MAPK13 (ρ = −0.553, Adjusted p-value = 0.014), and [Eubacterium]_coprostanoligenes_group and WIPI1 (ρ = −0.498, Adjusted p-value = 0.026). Prevotellaceae_UCG-003 was also negatively correlated with all three hub genes (ρ = −0.487 to − 0.450, Adjusted p-value = 0.031–0.047). Complete correlation results are provided in Supplementary Table S8.

sPLS identifies coordinated associations between parity-associated microbiota and host gene expression

We further employed sparse partial least squares (sPLS) to construct a joint analysis model for the microbiome and host gene expression. The joint sPLS latent component captured coordinated variation between parity-associated fecal genera and peripheral blood immune-related genes, leading to partial separation of PrC and MuC samples along Component 1 (Fig. 6A).

sPLS-based integration of parity-associated candidate genera and selected immune-related genes. A Sample score plot based on the first two sPLS components, showing partial separation between PrC and MuC cows. B Top microbial contributors to Component 1 ranked by absolute loading weight. C Top immune-related gene contributors to Component 1 ranked by absolute loading weight. D Clustered image map derived from the sPLS model, showing the association structure between selected parity-associated candidate genera and immune-related genes

Based on the loading weights output from the sPLS model, we identified features with high contributions to component separation. At the microbial dimension, genera including [Eubacterium]_coprostanoligenes_group, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Turicibacter, Acetitomaculum, Prevotellaceae_UCG-003, and Lachnospiraceae_NK3A20_group exhibited high contributions to Component 1 (Fig. 6B). At the gene dimension, immune- and autophagy-related genes, including WIPI1, MAPK13, gamma-glutamyltransferase 5 (GGT5), autophagy related 3 (ATG3), unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1 (ULK1), and GTP cyclohydrolase 1 (GCH1), displayed substantial loading weights (Fig. 6C), indicating that they contributed to the multivariate association pattern between rectal fecal microbiota and host immune-related transcription. We further generated a clustered image map derived from the sPLS model to visualize the association structure between selected microbial genera and immune-related genes (Fig. 6D). Genera including Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Turicibacter, Acetitomaculum, and Lachnospiraceae_NK3A20_group showed similar positive association patterns with genes including WIPI1, MAPK13, ATG3, and ULK1, whereas Prevotellaceae_UCG-003 and certain Eubacterium-related taxa showed contrasting association patterns with these genes.

Overall, the sPLS model suggested coordinated association patterns between parity-associated candidate genera and host immune-related genes, providing association-based insight into putative cross-omics relationships across different parities. These findings provide a basis for future investigations into the biological functions of selected microbial taxa and their potential relationships with host immune-related transcription.

Discussion

In this study, we integrated 16S rRNA gene sequencing data from rectal fecal samples and whole-blood RNA-seq data from dairy cows in naturally occurring parity groups. We characterized parity-group-associated differences in rectal fecal microbial diversity and composition, predicted microbial functional potential, and peripheral blood transcriptional profiles. By combining differential analysis, gene-network analysis, and cross-omics integration, we further identified candidate microbial genera and candidate hub genes and characterized their coordinated association patterns. These analyses extend previous parity-related microbiome studies by linking hindgut microbial features with peripheral immune-related transcription in the same cohort of mid-lactation dairy cows.

Previous studies of the rumen microbiota have reported parity-associated differences in microbial diversity [41, 42]. In a previous study comparing the hindgut microbiota of dairy cows at different reproductive and parity stages, open multiparous cows showed lower observed richness, Chao1 richness, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity than some younger groups, with no significant differences in the Shannon or Simpson indices; Prevotellaceae_UCG-003 was also differentially represented among physiological groups, whereas Turicibacter, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Acetitomaculum, and Lachnospiraceae_NK3A20_group were not among the principal taxa highlighted [43]. In the present study, MuC cows showed lower Chao1 richness, Sobs richness, and Shannon diversity, together with higher Simpson dominance, indicating lower fecal microbial richness and diversity and greater taxon dominance than PrC cows. The lower richness observed in MuC cows was partially consistent with the previous hindgut findings, whereas the differences in Shannon diversity, Simpson dominance, and the candidate genera identified were not entirely concordant. These differences across studies may reflect variation in reproductive and lactational stage, age, diet, geographic environment, 16S rRNA target region, bioinformatic workflow, and statistical methods. More broadly, age-related differences in gut microbial composition and intestinal homeostasis have been reported in other host species [44–46], providing additional context for the variation observed across physiological groups.

Prevotellaceae are capable of degrading plant polysaccharides and producing short-chain fatty acids, including succinate and propionate [47]. Propionate is a major gluconeogenic precursor in ruminants and may also participate in the regulation of intestinal barrier and immune homeostasis [48]. The higher abundance of Prevotellaceae_UCG-003 in PrC cows may therefore reflect differences in fermentative and host-interactive potential between the two parity groups. Turicibacter has been linked to intestinal 5-hydroxytryptamine signaling, barrier status, and inflammatory contexts [49–51], while gut microorganisms more broadly can influence host serotonin biosynthesis [52]. Specific pathogenic Clostridium species, such as Clostridium perfringens, have been associated with intestinal disease and physiological stress, while environmental stress can alter rumen and hindgut microbial communities in dairy cows [53–55]. However, the genus-level Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 identified in the present study should not be equated with a specific pathogenic species. A rumen Lachnospiraceae NK3A20 isolate has shown substrate-dependent metabolic flexibility [56], suggesting that members of this lineage may participate in diverse fermentation processes, although the role of the fecal Lachnospiraceae_NK3A20_group remains to be clarified. The biological role of Acetitomaculum in the bovine hindgut is also less well defined. Together, these genera represent candidate microbial features characterizing parity-group-associated differences in hindgut microbial ecology.

PICRUSt2-based inference indicated that pathways related to bacterial chemotaxis and flagellar assembly were predicted to be more abundant in MuC cows. Bacterial chemotaxis may facilitate the localization of dietary substrates and contribute to microbial ecological adaptation [57], whereas flagellar motility may influence interactions between microorganisms and the intestinal mucus layer. Flagellin can also participate in mucosal immune recognition through Toll-like receptor 5 [58, 59]. These results suggest that the two parity groups differed in predicted microbial motility-related functional potential and in microbial features potentially relevant to the host–microbe interface. Because these functions were inferred from 16S rRNA profiles, these predictions require validation by direct metagenomic or functional measurements.

Multiparous and primiparous cows also showed distinct peripheral immune-related transcriptional profiles. Overall KEGG analysis of the complete DEG set highlighted broad immune- and inflammation-related functional categories, including the NF-κB signaling pathway, FcεRI signaling pathway, B cell receptor signaling pathway, Th17 cell differentiation, and complement and coagulation cascades. Direction-stratified analysis further showed that most significantly enriched immune-related pathways were identified among genes with higher transcript abundance in PrC cows, particularly pathways related to antigen processing and presentation, T- and B-cell receptor signaling, Fc receptor-mediated functions, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, hematopoietic cell lineage, and the intestinal immune network for IgA production. In contrast, only one pathway reached significance among the MuC-higher genes. This directional distinction refined the functional interpretation of the combined DEG analysis.

At the gene-network level, integration of weighted gene co-expression network analysis, protein–protein interaction analysis, and differential expression prioritized matrix metallopeptidase 9 (MMP9), mitogen-activated protein kinase 13 (MAPK13), and WD repeat domain, phosphoinositide interacting 1 (WIPI1) as candidate hub genes, all of which showed higher transcript abundance in MuC cows. Previous dairy-cow studies have reported parity- and reproductive-stage-associated differences in metabolic, redox, and immune status and peripheral leukocyte profiles [2, 4]. However, to our knowledge, MMP9, MAPK13, and WIPI1 have not previously been jointly identified as a parity-group-associated candidate hub-gene set in whole-blood transcriptomes from mid-lactation dairy cows. Their joint prioritization therefore represents a potentially novel finding and provides specific candidates for further investigation. MAPK13, a component of the p38 MAPK signaling cascade, participates in cellular responses to bacterial toxins and osmotic stress [60, 61]. MMP9 is expressed by neutrophils and monocytes and contributes to extracellular-matrix remodeling and leukocyte-associated inflammatory processes [62, 63]. More broadly, matrix metalloproteinases participate in tissue remodeling in the bovine mammary gland during development and involution [64], while MMP-9 has been implicated in trophoblast invasion and uteroplacental remodeling during pregnancy [65]. WIPI1 participates in autophagy-related cellular processes, including intracellular recycling and the removal of damaged cellular components [66–68]. The higher transcript abundance of these genes in MuC cows highlights specific transcriptional features related to immune function, stress responses, and autophagy that differed between the parity groups. Because the transcriptome was generated from whole blood, variation in circulating leukocyte composition may also have contributed to these expression patterns.

Finally, pairwise Spearman correlation analysis and sparse partial least squares integration identified coordinated associations between parity-group-associated fecal genera and peripheral blood immune-related genes. Pairwise analysis showed that [Eubacterium]_coprostanoligenes_group and Prevotellaceae_UCG-003 were negatively associated with MMP9, MAPK13, and WIPI1, whereas [Eubacterium]_oxidoreducens_group was negatively associated with MMP9 and MAPK13. The sPLS model further identified a multivariate association structure involving selected microbial genera and immune-related genes. The convergence of pairwise and multivariate analyses indicates that the microbial and transcriptional features distinguishing the two parity groups varied in a coordinated manner within this cohort. These coordinated patterns identify specific candidate microbe–gene associations that can be prioritized in future longitudinal and functional studies.

Overall, the present study identified a coherent set of parity-group-associated candidate microbial genera, direction-specific immune transcriptional features, and candidate hub genes. The combination of direction-stratified pathway enrichment, gene-network analysis, and microbiome–transcriptome integration provides a more detailed view of the biological differences between naturally occurring parity groups and generates testable candidates for further investigation.

Limitations

This study was a cross-sectional observational comparison conducted within a single commercial dairy herd, and parity group was not experimentally assigned. Therefore, the observed differences may partly reflect age, cumulative reproductive and lactational history, and other herd-related factors. Individual pen identity and dry matter intake were unavailable for inclusion in the analyses. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as parity-group-associated patterns, and longitudinal validation in independent herds is warranted.

Conclusion

Within this single commercial dairy herd, primiparous and multiparous mid-lactation, nonpregnant cows differed in rectal fecal microbial diversity and composition, predicted microbial functional potential, and peripheral blood transcriptional profiles. Integrative analyses identified candidate genera and candidate hub genes, including MMP9, MAPK13, and WIPI1, that showed coordinated cross-omics associations. Because the study was cross-sectional and parity was not experimentally assigned, these findings should be interpreted as group-associated patterns rather than causal effects of parity. Longitudinal studies in independent herds, together with directly measured microbial functions and cell- or protein-level immune validation, are required.
