The rise of non-vdW moiré superlattices
Authors: Zicheng Li, Xinyu Duan, Zehui Zhou, Linjun Li, Beibei Xu
Published: 2024

Main text

The formation of a moiré superlattice is induced by the stacking of van der Waals (vdW) 2D materials with a twist angle or small lattice mismatch. This superlattice functions as a periodic potential for electrons, forming moiré flat bands with emerging quantum phenomena or highly correlated states.1 It has emerged as one of the most significant advancements in condensed matter physics in recent decades. However, the vdW interactions between adjacent layers result in weak interlayer coupling and narrow energy splitting of the flat bands at a range of tens of millielectron-volt (meV). Consequently, electrons are weakly localized and easily disturbed by thermal fluctuations, hindering the development of strongly correlated phenomena at room temperature.2

In contrast, non-vdW chemical bonding exhibits a more robust interlayer interaction, resulting in significant energy modulation (Figure 1A). Hence, an energy splitting of a few hundred meV is expected (Figure 1B),3 which significantly enhances electron localization and facilitates the observation of exotic quantum phenomena or correlated states, including Wigner crystallinity at elevated temperatures. Consequently, this opens opportunities for the exploration of new fields. However, non-vdW superlattices inherently resides within 3D crystallized structures due to the chemical bonding at the interface,2 posing a significant challenge to their fabrication via traditional methods, such as mechanical exfoliation of layered materials. Therefore, most of the prior investigations were confined to theoretical domains. Zhang et al. reported a significant breakthrough, which includes the fabrication of ion-bonding moiré superlattices in inorganic 2D halide perovskite.4 Traditional methodologies cannot achieve the atomically smooth, ultrathin, and readily transferable inorganic 2D halide perovskite. Conversely, exfoliation or drop-casting can prepare the layered structure of organic-inorganic hybrid Ruddlesden-Popper (RP)-phase perovskite featuring an interlayer vdW interaction. Based on this foundation, an in situ solid-liquid interfacial reaction involving an ultrathin layer was employed to generate ion-bonding inorganic 2D perovskite. This reaction involves a 2D RP-phase layered perovskite template (L2An-1PbnX3n+1, where L+ represents a long-chain alkyl or aromatic ammonium cation, A+ is a small cation, X– is a halide, and n is an integer) and a PbX2/AX equilibrated isopropanol (IPA) solution. The concentration gradient induces the release of L+ cations and excess X– into the IPA solution, promoting the fusion of inorganic layers by sharing the remnant X– bridge (Figure 1C). Simultaneously, A+ cations diffuse into the interlayer spaces, developing a non-layered APbX3 2D perovskite. The small amount of [PbX6]4– in the solution is crucial (Equation 1), as it prevents the dissolution of [An-1PbnX3n+1]2–. However, the Pb2+ bonding with AX is essential to facilitate dissolution, as PbX2 is intrinsically difficult to dissolve in IPA. The AX/PbX2 must be carefully controlled; a low ratio fails to generate sufficient [PbX6]4– concentrations necessary for stabilizing the inorganic framework, resulting in the dissolution and decomposition of precursors into [PbX6]4– (Equation 2). Conversely, a high ratio introduces excess X–, disrupting the X− bridge between octahedral units and causing the dissolution of the 2D PR-phase template with the formation of free [PbX6]4– (Equation 3). The optimal ratio of AX/PbX2 is around 3:1. The ligand and n value of the RP phase also influence the phase transition. Uniform 2D MAPbI3, MAPbBr3, and FAPbI3 films with 2- to 100-nm thickness and controllable domain sizes were obtained at a surface roughness below 0.2 nm. All films inherited the [001] stacking orientation of the original 2D RP-phase perovskite. These films exhibit quantum confinement with larger exciton binding energy than their 3D bulk counterparts; for example, ∼70 meV for a 6 nm 2D MAPbI3 sheet compared to 16 meV for a 3D film.

The mechanical stacking of these 2D inorganic perovskites by polydimethylsiloxane transfer generates a moiré superlattice with angle-dependent moiré excitons. The exciton motion is substantially impeded at a 9° twist angle. The exciton diffusion constant is 2–3 times smaller than that of 2° and 29° twist angles, and the charge carrier mobility is also the lowest, thereby validating the presence of a flat band feature. Remarkably, the exciton localization can be observed up to 9-unit-cell thickness, indicating a broader range interlayer interaction that surpasses that of a vdW moiré superlattice. Furthermore, the photoluminescence decay component exhibits a faster rate as the pump intensity escalates, which is discernible for twist angles of 2° and 29°, whereas this variation remains negligible at 9°. This reinforced localization phenomenon at 9° results in an exciton-exciton annihilation (EEA) rate of 4.5 × 10−5 cm−2 s−1, which is two orders of magnitude lower than that at 2° and 29° as well as in other 2D hybrid perovskites. This localization enhances PL intensity more than 10-fold within a twist angle of 8°–12° and probably arises from the confinement of bright moiré excitons. This enhancement starkly contrasts with the weak emission of dark excitons in TMDC bilayers (Figures 1D–1G). Other non-vdW superlattices involve metavalent and metallic bonding. Large splitting of flat bands and robust metavalent coupling are observed in slightly angular twisting PbS nanosheets. This process leads to a series of evenly spaced localized quantum states, accompanied by substantial electronic renormalization and structural reconstruction. Consequently, it holds potential for quantum information applications.2,3 The ultrathin PbS nanosheets are obtained using water-soluble ligands, which are subsequently purged via dilute basic and acidic aqueous solutions. The resulting bare nanosheets are subsequently employed to assemble moiré superlattices through drop-casting and solvent evaporation. Additionally, Swain et al. achieved optical chirality in large-scale moiré chiral superlattices of metallic gold (Au) through the thermal tape transfer printing method.5

However, the exploration of non-vdW moiré superlattices is hindered by several pivotal challenges. First, the non-layered architecture inherent to these systems precludes the direct application of conventional exfoliation techniques. The current in situ chemical phase transition from vdW 2D organic-inorganic perovskite to non-vdW inorganic perovskite may introduce defects, influencing the interfacial quality, interlayer interactions, and related physical properties. Hence, new preparation methods must be designed for non-vdW moiré superlattices, such as direct growth using the chemical vapor deposition method. Second, the stringent lattice-matching conditions governing twisted non-vdW superlattices restrict current research to a narrow range of material systems with strict twist angles required. There is an urgent demand for developing lattice-mismatch hetero-stacking moiré superlattices that are insensitive to twist-angle disorders (Figure 1H). This process will enrich the materials system and offer great flexibility to tune the properties of non-vdW moiré superlattices, which promises to exhibit deep moiré potential, rich moiré exciton physics, and a strongly correlated state. Last, no in situ measurement method is available to identify twist angles during stacking. The scalable fabrication of non-vdW 2D materials and moiré superlattices, along with in situ characterization technologies capable of resolving local structures, visualizing rotation angles, mapping stacking regions, and identifying domain walls, are urgently needed.

Halide perovskite moiré superlattices have the potential to be an alluring platform for investigating light emission and light-matter interactions due to their distinctive properties, including bright moiré excitons, low EEA rates, deep moiré potentials with strong localization, and the confinement of, at most, one exciton per moiré site. These properties enable broad applications in lasers, light-emitting diodes, topological exciton polaritons, chiral optics, optical nonlinearity, and room-temperature coherent quantum emitters. In addition, the heightened oscillator strengths of these excitons provide new opportunities for tailoring energy and charge transfer functionalities. Leveraging small twist angles or lattice mismatches to boost charge transport holds promise for advancing solar cell technologies. Moreover, the on-device phase engineering via in situ phase transition, as well as the moiré exciton engineering by external stimuli, composition tuning, and doping, enable the realization of multi-function, such as the development of moiré transistors, next-generation thermoelectrics, ferroelectrics, ferromagnetics, and multiferroics.

Overall, the emergence of non-vdW superlattices significantly enriches the landscape of moiré materials. Their unique advantages over vdW counterparts open new avenues for fundamental science and technological advancements in both 2D and 1D homo- and hetero-structures as well as beyond bilayers for more different periodic moiré patterns (Figure 1H).
