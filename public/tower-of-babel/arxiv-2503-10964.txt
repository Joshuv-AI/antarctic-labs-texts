Abstract
The Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) is a cornerstone of optimal control theory, widely studied in both model-based and model-free approaches. Despite its well-established nature, certain foundational aspects remain subtle. In this paper, we revisit three key properties of policy optimization in LQR: (i) strong duality in the nonconvex policy optimization formulation, (ii) the gradient dominance property, examining when it holds and when it fails, and (iii) the global optimality of linear static policies. Using primal-dual analysis and convex reformulation, we refine and clarify existing results by leveraging Riccati equations/inequalities, semidefinite programming (SDP) duality, and a recent framework of Extended Convex Lifting ( ECL ). Our analysis confirms that LQR 1) behaves almost like a convex problem (e.g., strong duality) under the standard assumptions of stabilizability and detectability and 2) exhibits strong convexity-like properties (e.g., gradient dominance) under slightly stronger conditions. In particular, we establish a broader characterization under which gradient dominance holds using ECL and the notion of Cauchy directions. By clarifying and refining these theoretical insights, we hope this work contributes to a deeper understanding of LQR and may inspire further developments beyond LQR.

Introduction

The Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) is one of the most fundamental problems in optimal control, with both theoretical significance and widespread practical applications. This classical problem seeks to design a linear control system by minimizing the integral of a quadratic cost function along the system trajectories. It is well established that, for linear time-invariant (LTI) systems in the infinite-horizon case, the optimal solution is a linear static constant state feedback controller
[
1

Since Kalman’s seminal work

For discrete-time systems, the finite-horizon LQR can be conveniently expressed as a convex quadratic program, especially in MPC for online control

1.1
Our results

In this paper, we revisit three fundamental properties of policy optimization in LQR:
1) strong duality in the nonconvex policy optimization, 2) the intriguing gradient dominance property (examining both
when it holds and when it fails
), and 3) the global optimality of linear static policies.

Many results have been established in the literature through various techniques and settings. Our approach is rooted in
primal-dual analysis
and
convex reformulation
.
We
revisit the strong duality and global optimality of linear static policies from a primal and dual lens
and leverage the convex reformulation of LQR to establish the gradient dominance property under broader assumptions than the existing studies
[
19
,
25
,
24
]
.
Our technical results are summarized below (see
Table

1
for an overview).

1)

Strong duality in LQR.
While the policy optimization for LQR is known to be nonconvex,
we demonstrate that strong duality holds under stabilizability and detectability.
This result is not surprising, but
a self-contained and explicit proof is not easily accessible in the literature, as existing discussions often rely on different formulations or advanced mathematical tools.
Early discussions in
[
26
]
are informal and technical details are missing. The recent result in
[
27
]
only focuses on the finite-horizon case and lacks the characterization on the uniqueness of the solution.
Our approach follows the standard Lagrange duality theory, leveraging well-established Riccati equations/inequalities and careful KKT analysis. While these techniques are standard, we carefully fill in the missing details. One subtle point is that the optimal LQR feedback gain may not be unique when the covariance matrix
W

is only positive semidefinite (
W\succeq 0
); see
Figure

1
(c) and
Example

1
. In this case, the standard solution derived from the Riccati equation is just one optimal solution. If
W
is strictly positive definite (
W\succ 0
), the optimal LQR feedback gain is unique. In this setting, we further reveal a favorable
strict complementarity
property
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
in the associated SDPs for LQR.

2)

Gradient dominance via Extended Convex Lifting.
We revisit the elegant property of
gradient dominance
in LQR using a recent framework of Extended Convex Lifting (
ECL
)
[
17
,
31
]
. Our analysis reveals two key insights: 1) gradient dominance requires stronger conditions than those for strong duality; with the usual stabilizability and detectability, gradient dominance may fail (see
Example

2
); 2) gradient dominance does not hold globally for continuous-time LQR even with strong assumptions of controllability and observability (see
Example

3
). Accordingly, we establish the most general known conditions for ensuring gradient dominance in LQR: (i) compact sublevel sets and (ii) a positive definite solution to a Lyapunov equation. This setting extends beyond the existing results in
[
24
,
32
,
25
]
. Our proof leverages the
ECL
framework while refining its analysis using the notion of Cauchy directions
[
33
]
. A key insight in our proof is that the existence of gradient dominance is closely tied to a convex reformulation of LQR
that satisfies
quadratic growth

[
34
,
35
]
, a similar but weaker condition than strong convexity.

3)

Global optimality of static state feedback policies.
We also revisit the global optimality of static state feedback policies among all (potentially nonlinear) stabilizing policies. Similar to
[
26
,
27
]
, we adopt the primal and dual analysis for LQR. A key ingredient in our analysis is the Gramian (or covariance) representation
[
36
]
, which captures all relevant quadratic information of system trajectories. Leveraging this representation, we reformulate LQR over all stabilizing policies, including nonlinear ones, as a finite-dimensional optimization problem. We then construct
inner
and
outer
approximations: the inner approximation restricts the policies to be static state feedback, while the outer approximation relaxes the feasible domain to an SDP. A careful KKT analysis reveals that these approximations preserve the same optimal value as the original LQR, which confirms that an optimal LQR policy is linear static state feedback.

1.2
Related works

Primal and dual analysis in control.

Optimization has long been an indispensable tool in control, and the use of (convex) duality in control theory has a rich history.
Early primal-dual results can be traced back to the 1970s, when Rockafellar
[
37
]
conducted a primal-dual analysis for optimal control with convex costs and constraints.
For
\ell_{1}
optimal control, Dahleh and Pearson exploited a dual problem to compute an optimal closed-loop transfer matrix
[
38
]
. Later, Rantzer introduced a new duality-based proof for the Kalman-Yakubovich-Popov (KYP) lemma in
[
39
]
.
Since the 2000s, driven by advancements in LMIs and SDPs, duality has been increasingly utilized in key control problems. The seminal work
[
26
]
provides an elegant interpretation of several fundamental control problems, including the KYP lemma and LQR, through duality analysis.
Additionally, Scherer
[
40
]
discovered that
the relaxation process of robust LMIs by S-lemma was exactly the Lagrangian relaxation procedure, and the connection was further examined in
[
41
]
. Recent studies
[
42
,
43
,
44
]
have introduced alternative methods for computing
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
norm using the SDP duality and the
Gramian/covariance representation
.
Very recently, Bamieh
[
27
]
revisited the
finite-horizon
linear quadratic control and established the global optimality of linear static policies through KKT analysis and a
differential Riccati equation
arising from a differential LMI in the dual.

It is important to distinguish the
Lagrange duality
in optimization from the duality between
control
and
estimation
.
Still, a fundamental similarity exists:
adjoint operators
play a crucial role in both contexts. A key connection between control and estimation lies in the
2
-norm equivalence between linear operators and their adjoints
[
4
, Chapter 3.3.2]
, where adjoint operators of dynamical systems have an interpretation of observers.
Similarly, adjoint operators emerge naturally in Lagrange dual problems in optimization
[
45
]
.

Benign nonconvex landscape of policy optimization in control.

Motivated by the remarkable success of RL,
many recent studies have revisited control costs as functions of policy parameters, examining their analytical and geometrical properties.
A key breakthrough is the discovery of gradient dominance for the discrete-time LQR in
[
15
]
. Since then, gradient dominance has been further explored in discrete-time variants

[
32
,
46
]

and
their continuous-time counterparts
[
19
,
25
,
24
,
47
,
48
]
. These studies often differ in their settings and involve nuanced, often case-by-case analyses of gradient dominance.
Beyond the classical LQR, the landscape of other fundamental control problems has also been investigated, including
filtering
[
49
]
, LQG
[
23
,
17
]
, LQ differential game
[
50
]
,
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
control
[
51
,
52
]
, and distributed control
[
53
]
; see two recent surveys
[
16
,
22
]
for a broader overview.
Very recently,
several works
[
54
,
49
,
31
,
52
,
17
]

have sought to identify the underlying sources that enable benign nonconvex landscapes. A common idea is to connect the original nonconvex problems to their well-established convex reformulations.
Among these, the Extended Convex Lifting (
\mathtt{ECL}
)
[
31
,
17
]
is the most generic framework, which naturally accommodates dynamic output feedback and nonsmooth cost functions.

In this paper, we utilize the
ECL
framework and refine its analysis using the notion of Cauchy directions
[
33
]
for the LQR problem. This provides an alternative streamlined approach to establish gradient dominance for LQR under the most general known setting.

Gramian/covariance representation in control.

The covariance representation
is a powerful technique that reformulates standard control problems for linear systems into SDPs by replacing
the state, input, and disturbances with their covariance matrices.
Its deterministic counterpart is known as Gramian representation.
The use of covariance matrices in control was pioneered by Hotz and Skelton
[
36
]
and later extensively discussed in the book
[
55
]
.
This approach has been useful for handling power constraints in controller synthesis in a convex manner.
In
[
56
]
, Rantzer applied this idea to a distributed control problem, and Gattami proposed an SDP-based controller synthesis method for linear quadratic control with power constraints
[
57
]
.
Gattami and Bamieh proposed an alternative computation method of
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
norm via the covariance representation and duality
[
42
]
.
The covariance technique was also applied to an online learning scheme for LQR with unknown performance weights in
[
58
]
.
Very recently, Bamieh utilized this covariance representation technique to derive the optimal controller for finite-horizon linear quadratic control
[
27
]
.
Our recent work
[
59
]
also dealt with LQ differential games and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
control in the same spirit.

In this paper, we leverage the Gramian representation to revisit the global optimality of static state feedback policies among all (potentially nonlinear) stabilizing policies for the infinite-horizon LQR problem.

Three fundamental aspects in LQR

The LQR problem

Theoretical guarantees of policy optimization in LQR often rely on proper characterizations of its nonconvex geometry. In recent years, many favorable properties have been revealed for LQR

Strong Duality and Hidden Convexity in LQR

In this section, we investigate the strong duality and hidden convexity in LQR. In particular,
we rewrite
5
into a nonconvex matrix optimization problem, derive its Lagrange dual formulation, and establish strong duality between the primal and dual formulations. The celebrated algebraic Riccati equation for LQR naturally comes from solving the dual SDP and facilitates the KKT optimality analysis.

3.1
A Lagrange dual formulation

Deriving a Lagrange dual formulation requires an algebraic characterization of both the cost and the stability constraint. It is well-known that for any
K\in\mathcal{K}
, the LQR cost
J_{\mathtt{LQR}}(K)
can be evaluated as

J_{\mathtt{LQR}}(K)=\mathrm{tr}(Q+K^{{\mathsf{T}}}RK)X,

(6a)

where

(A+BK)X+X(A+BK)^{{\mathsf{T}}}+W=0.

(6b)

If
W\succ 0
,
we know that

if and only if the solution

to

\displaystyle p^{*}=\min_{K\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle(A+BK)X+X(A+BK)^{{\mathsf{T}}}+W=0,X\succeq 0.

4.1

Consider a differentiable function

(26)

is an optimal solution of

. Note that

for different regularity conditions.

For functions satisfying gradient dominance, it is well-known that basic gradient descent achieves linear convergence.
We summarize a version of this result below.
Recall that a function

is

f(x)\to\infty\text{ whenever }\|x\|\to\infty\text{ or }x\to x_{\infty}\in\partial\mathcal{D},

where

represents the boundary of

.

Lemma 5

(
[
24
, Theorem 4.2]
)
.

Consider
f^{*}=\min_{x\in\mathcal{D}}f(x)
, where f is twice continuously differentiable and coercive, and
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open.
Suppose that
the gradient dominance

holds with

over a sublevel set
\mathcal{D}_{\nu}

Then,

is

-smooth over
\mathcal{D}_{\nu}
,
and the gradient descent algorithm

satisfies

(27)

where

This lemma guarantees the feasibility of all iterates, as well as the linear convergence of the cost value to the global minimum

We provide a proof in
Section

C.1
for self-completeness.
Our proof is inspired by
[

.
The range of step size

\mathcal{D}_{\nu}
.

Remark 3

The problem in

A different approach showing the feasibility can be found in

Remark 7

4.3
Extended Convex Lifting (
\mathtt{ECL}
)
and gradient dominance

In this section, we introduce the
\mathtt{ECL}
framework
[
31
]

and present a general result of gradient dominance (
Theorem

3
) for differentiable functions. The LQR result in
Theorem

2
will be a special case.

4.3.1
Gradient dominance in
\mathtt{ECL}

The idea of
ECL
is intuitive and closely related to convex re-parameterization. Let
f
be a potentially
nonconvex function, and suppose there exists a
C^{1}
diffeomorphism
y=\Phi(x)
such that
h(y):=f(\Phi^{-1}(y))
is convex. In other words,
h
is a convex parameterization of
f
under the new coordinates
y=\Phi(x)
. Then, it is clear that 1) minimizing
f(x)
is equivalent to minimizing
h(y)
, which is a convex problem; 2) all stationary points of
f
are globally optimal. If
both
f
and
h
are differentiable and

h
further
satisfies gradient dominance

\mu\left(h(y)-h(y^{\star})\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla h(y)\|^{2},

we have

\displaystyle f(x)-f(x^{\star})

\displaystyle=f(\Phi^{-1}(y))-f(\Phi^{-1}(y^{\star}))

(30)

\displaystyle=h(y)-h(y^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\nabla h(y)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\nabla\Phi^{-1}(y)^{{\mathsf{T}}}\nabla f(\Phi^{-1}(y))\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\nabla\Phi^{-1}(y)\|_{2}^{2}\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2},

where the third and fourth lines follow from the gradient dominance of
h
and the chain rule, respectively.
If the quantity
\|\nabla\Phi^{-1}(y)\|_{2}
is bounded, then we have established the gradient dominance of
f(x)
despite its nonconvexity. In particular, the parameter
\mu
in
30
plays the same role of
\mu_{\mathrm{qg}}
in
28b
, and the bound on
\|\nabla\Phi^{-1}(y)\|_{2}
is the same as
1/c_{\mathtt{LQR}}
in
28b
.

The analysis strategy in
30
has good intuition and simplicity, which was recently explored in
[
65
]
.
However, we cannot directly apply it to general control problems for two main reasons: 1) the bijection
\Phi
may not exist due to the natural appearance of Lyapunov variables; 2) the gradient may not be a good tool to connect the original function and its convex re-parameterization when their domains are not
full dimensional
(e.g., constraints are involved). The
ECL

[
31
]
is a general framework to resolve the first difficulty, and we will refine
ECL
via the notion of Cauchy direction
[
33
]
to address the second difficulty.

For convenience, we introduce a simplified
ECL
that is sufficient for the LQR; see
[
31
]
for a more general setup. Recall that the epigraph of a function
f:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as follows

\operatorname{epi}_{\geq}(f):=\{(x,\gamma)\mid x\in\mathcal{D},f(x)\leq\gamma\}.

Definition 1

(Extended Convex Lifting)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function, where
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an open set. We say that the tuple
\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{lft}},\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cvx}},\Phi\right)
is an
\mathtt{ECL}
of
f
, if the following conditions hold:

1)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{lft}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}
is a lifted set with an extra variable
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\xi}}
, such that the canonical projection of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{lft}}
onto the first
d+1
coordinates
satisfies
\operatorname{epi}_{\geq}(f)=\pi_{x,\gamma}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{lft}}\right)
.
3
3

3

Let
\mathcal{S}\!\subseteq\!\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\!+\!n_{2}}
.
The canonical projection onto the first

Conclusion

In this paper, we have revisited three fundamental properties of policy optimization in LQR: strong duality in the nonconvex policy optimization formulation, the gradient dominance property, and the global optimality of linear static policies. Through primal-dual analysis and the
ECL
framework, we have provided a clearer derivation of strong duality and established a broader and more precise characterization of gradient dominance. We hope these findings contribute to a deeper understanding of optimization landscapes in the classical LQR problem. More broadly, we believe that the primal-dual perspective can offer valuable insights into other fundamental control problems, including robust control and beyond. We are also interested in the principled design of first-order algorithms for both smooth and nonsmooth policy optimization in control problems.

A.1
Strong duality in SDPs

We here review the primal and dual SDPs and their strong duality results (see
[
63
]
for more detail).
The standard primal SDP is a problem of the form:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} p^{*}=\min_{X}&\hskip 9.24994pt\langle C,X\rangle\\
\text{subject to }&\hskip 9.24994pt\mathcal{A}(X)=b,\\
&\hskip 9.24994ptX\in\mathbb{S}^{r}_{+},\end{aligned}

(A.1)

where
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\,C\in\mathbb{S}^{r}
and

\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{S}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a linear map.
Its Lagrange dual problem is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} d^{*}=\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}&\hskip 9.24994ptb^{{\mathsf{T}}}y\\
\text{subject to }&\hskip 9.24994ptC-\mathcal{A}^{*}(y)\in\mathbb{S}^{r}_{+},\end{aligned}

(A.2)

where
A^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{S}^{r}
is the adjoint of
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
identified by

\left\langle y,\mathcal{A}(X)\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathcal{A}^{*}(y),X\right\rangle,\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{m},X\in\mathbb{S}^{r}.

For this primal-dual pair, the KKT optimality condition can be written as

•

Primal feasibility:

X\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{r}
and

\mathcal{A}(X)=b
.

•

Dual feasibility:
C-\mathcal{A}^{*}(y)\in\mathbb{S}^{r}_{+}
.

•

Complementary slackness:
\left\langle X,C-\mathcal{A}^{*}(y)\right\rangle=0
.

Here,
A.1
is said to be
strictly feasible
if there exists
X\in\mathbb{S}^{r}_{++}
such that
\mathcal{A}(X)=b
, and
A.2
is
strictly feasible
if there exists
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
C-\mathcal{A}^{*}(y)\in\mathbb{S}^{r}_{++}
. We have the following result
[
63
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Lemma A.1
.

Consider the primal-dual pair
A.1
and
A.2
.
The following statements hold.

1)

Weak duality:
d^{*}\leq p^{*}
.

2)

Strong duality-1: if
d^{*}
is finite and the dual problem
A.2
is strictly feasible,
then the primal problem
A.1
is solvable, i.e., its minimum is attained.
Moreover, the duality gap is zero, i.e.,
d^{*}=p^{*}
.

3)

Strong duality-2: if
p^{*}
is finite and the primal problem
A.1
is strictly feasible,
then the dual problem
A.2
is solvable, i.e., its maximum is attained.
Moreover, the duality gap is zero, i.e.,
d^{*}=p^{*}
.

4)

Complementary slackness:
Assume the strong duality holds and both problems are solvable.
Then,

\left\langle X^{\star},C-\mathcal{A}^{*}(y^{\star})\right\rangle=0
holds for any primal-dual pair of solutions

(X^{\star},y^{\star})
.

The complementary slackness is one key condition in the strong duality.
One sufficient condition for strong duality is
the existence of a strictly feasible solution.

A.2

Here, we give a quick review of gradient dominance and its relevant properties. Consider an
L
-smooth function

Our discussion follows from

Let

1)

Strong convexity
(
\mu

For all

For all

\mu

For all

For all

, we have

f(x)-f^{*}\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\times\mathrm{dist}^{2}(x,S).

.

Assume that

w
be a normalized eigenvector of
X
associated with
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(X)
.
Then, the Lyapunov equation
6b

implies that

\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(X)=\frac{w^{{\mathsf{T}}}Ww}{-w^{{\mathsf{T}}}\left((A+BK)+(A+BK)^{{\mathsf{T}}}\right)w}\geq\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(W)}{2\|A+BK\|}\geq\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(W)}{2\|A\|+2\|B\|\|K\|_{F}}>0,

where we have used
w^{{\mathsf{T}}}Ww\geq\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(W)
, and the fact that the spectral norm of a symmetric matrix is always no less than the absolute value of its smallest eigenvalue. So we have

\displaystyle-w^{{\mathsf{T}}}\left((A+BK)+(A+BK)^{{\mathsf{T}}}\right)w

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}\left((A+BK)+(A+BK)^{{\mathsf{T}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\|(A+BK)+(A+BK)^{{\mathsf{T}}}\|\leq 2\|A+BK\|.

Thus,
K
is bounded since

\nu\geq J_{\mathtt{LQR}}(K)\geq{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(W)\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(R)\|K\|_{F}^{2}}/(2\|A\|+2\|B\|\|K\|_{F}).

We note that this lower bound of
J_{\mathtt{LQR}}(K)
also appeared in
[
24
, Lemma 3.8]
.

C.4.2
Proof with the sufficient condition (b)

First, we check the second statement (
K\in\mathcal{K}
iff

).
By the assumption of
\operatorname{Im}B\subseteq\operatorname{Im}W
, for all
K\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
(including non-stabilizing gains), we have the controllability of
(A+BK,W)
from the controllability of
(A,B)
(recall the PBH test
[
1
]
).
Thus, the Lyapunov equation
6b
has a positive definite solution
X\succ 0
if and only if

(A+BK)
is stable, i.e.,
K
is stabilizing
(see
[
1
, Lemma 3.18]
).

We now establish the first statement: the compactness of
\mathcal{K}_{\nu}

for any
\nu\geq J_{\mathtt{LQR}}(K^{\star})
.
Here, since
Assumption

2
(1) is true from the discussion above, we can similarly construct the
\mathtt{ECL}
in
Section

4.3
.
Recalling the
invertibility of the affine map
\mathcal{A}_{\star}(\cdot)
,
we have

\mathcal{K}_{\nu}=\Xi\left(\mathcal{C}_{\nu}\right)

(C.13)

with the continuous map
\Xi:\pi_{Y}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cvx}})\to\mathcal{K}
, given by

\Xi(Y)=YX_{Y}^{-1}+K^{\star}

with
X_{Y}=-\left(\mathcal{A}_{\star}^{-1}(\mathcal{B}(Y)+W)\right)\succ 0
,
and

\mathcal{C}_{\nu}:=\left\{Y\in\pi_{Y}(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{cvx}})|f_{\mathrm{cvx}}(Y,X_{Y})\leq\nu\right\}.

Thus,
we show the compactness of
\mathcal{C}_{\nu}
instead.

First, we show that the set
{\mathcal{C}}_{\nu}
is bounded.
By
Q\succ 0
, we see that

\nu\geq{f}_{\mathrm{cvx}}(Y)\geq\mathrm{tr}(QX_{Y})\geq\lambda_{\min}(Q)\mathrm{tr}(X_{Y}){\geq\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(Q)\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(X_{Y}),}

which gives

\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(X_{Y}^{-1})=\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(X_{Y})}\geq\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(Q)}{\nu}>0.

Thus, we observe that
from the proof of
Section

C.3.2
,

Y
is bounded as

\displaystyle\nu-J_{\mathtt{LQR}}(K^{\star})\geq\mathrm{tr}(X_{Y}^{-1}Y^{{\mathsf{T}}}RY)\geq\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(X_{Y}^{-1})\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(R)\|Y\|_{F}^{2}\geq\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(R)\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(Q)\|Y\|_{F}^{2}}{\nu}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle\|Y\|_{F}\leq\frac{\nu}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(R)\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(Q)}}.

Finally, we prove that the set
\mathcal{C}_{\nu}
is closed by showing that
for any sequence
\{Y_{i}\}\subset\mathcal{C}_{\nu}
such that
Y_{i}\to Y^{*}
,
we have
Y^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{\nu}
.
To establish this, we consider
X_{Y^{*}}=-\lim_{Y_{i}\to Y^{*}}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\star}^{-1}(\mathcal{B}(Y_{i})+W)\right)
and show that
X_{Y^{*}}
is positive definite; this yields
Y^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{\nu}
by the continuity of
f_{\mathrm{cvx}}(Y)
for
X_{Y}\succ 0
.
(Since the mapping
X_{Y}=-\left(\mathcal{A}_{\star}^{-1}(\mathcal{B}(Y)+W)\right)
is affine,
X_{Y^{*}}
exists and is positive semidefinite.)
The proof is as follows.
By
f_{\mathrm{cvx}}(Y_{i})-J^{\star}=\operatorname{tr}(YX_{Y_{i}}^{-1}Y^{{\mathsf{T}}}R)\geq\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(R)\operatorname{tr}(X_{Y_{i}}^{-1/2}Y^{{\mathsf{T}}}YX_{Y_{i}}^{-1/2})
,
we obtain

\|Y_{i}v\|^{2}\leq\frac{\nu}{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(R)}v^{{\mathsf{T}}}X_{Y_{i}}v.

for any real vector
v.

If
X_{Y^{*}}v=0
, this inequality yields
Y^{*}v=0
.
From the Lyapunov equation

A^{\star}X_{Y^{*}}+X_{Y^{*}}(A^{\star})^{{\mathsf{T}}}+BY^{*}+(Y^{*})^{{\mathsf{T}}}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}+W=0,

we then obtain
v^{{\mathsf{T}}}Wv=0,
i.e.,
Wv=0
,
Consequently, we obtain
B^{{\mathsf{T}}}v=0
due to the assumption
\operatorname{Im}B\subseteq\operatorname{Im}W
,
which implies
\ker X_{Y^{*}}\subseteq\ker B^{{\mathsf{T}}}.

Now, by multiplying the Lyapunov equation by
v
, we obtain
X_{Y^{*}}(A^{\star})^{{\mathsf{T}}}v=0
, which implies

(A^{\star})^{{\mathsf{T}}}v\in\ker X_{Y^{*}}\subseteq\ker B^{{\mathsf{T}}}.

Therefore,

v^{{\mathsf{T}}}\begin{bmatrix}B,A^{\star}B,\ldots,(A^{\star})^{n-1}B\end{bmatrix}=0.

By the controllability of
(A^{\star},B)
,
we obtain
v=0
and
\ker X_{Y^{*}}=\{0\}
.
Since
X_{Y^{*}}
is positive semidefinite,
we conclude
X_{Y^{*}}\succ 0
,
and this completes the proof.

C.4.3
Proof with the sufficient condition (c)

Since
(A+BK,W)
for any

is controllable
by the controllability of
(A,B)
and
\operatorname{Im}B\subseteq\operatorname{Im}W
, we have the second statement
from the same argument as
Proposition

3
(b).

Thus, we here prove the first statement, the compactness of
\mathcal{K}_{\nu}
.
Now, it suffices to see the coercivity of
J_{\mathtt{LQR}}(K)
over
\mathcal{K}

J_{\mathtt{LQR}}(K)\to\infty
as

and

J_{\mathtt{LQR}}(K)\to\infty

.
The first property follows from the proof of

3
(a)
because
(A+BK,W)
is controllable.
The second property can also be verified as follows.
With
P
in
29
,
we have

.
Thus, it is not difficult to see that

Appendix D
Discussions on (non)-unique optimal LQR feedback gains

Here, we provide some details regarding the (non)-uniqueness of optimal control gain
K^{\star}
for the LQR problem.
While the optimal gain is unique under
Assumptions

1
and
2
, we may have an infinite number of solutions in some other cases as
Example

1
.

In our discussions below, the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{ctrb}}:=

\displaystyle\{K\in\mathcal{K}|(A+BK,W^{1/2})\text{ is controllable}\}

plays a pivotal role.
As seen in our main results in
Section

4
,
if
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{ctrb}}

(as
Proposition

3
(a,b)),
we can formulate
\mathtt{ECL}
s and have the gradient dominance
beyond the uniqueness.
Having
\mathcal{K}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{ctrb}}\neq\emptyset
, however, can cause the non-uniqueness.
Recall that
the matrix
W
is the covariance matrix of the initial point
x_{0}
, i.e.,
W=\mathbb{E}[x_{0}x_{0}^{{\mathsf{T}}}]
(see
Section

2
).

First, we summarize
two important properties of
the set
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{ctrb}}
.
Our main results in
Section

4

and
Assumption

2

heavily rely on the following properties.

Proposition D.1

(Properties of matrix
X
and
J_{\mathtt{LQR}}
on
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{ctrb}}
)
.

With
Assumption

1
, the following statements hold:

1)

(Positive definiteness of
X
).
The solution
X
to
6b
is positive definite if and only if

K\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{ctrb}}
.

2)

(Uniqueness of the stationary point and its optimality). If

\nabla J_{\mathtt{LQR}}(K)=0
holds for
K\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{ctrb}}
,
we uniquely have
K=-R^{-1}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}P^{\star}

with the unique solution
P^{\star}
to the ARE in
11
, and
K
is an optimal solution.

Proof.

The first statement is straightforward from
[
1
, Lemma 3.18]
.
The second statement follows from
X\succ 0
. Indeed,
when
X\succ 0
for
K
,

\nabla J_{\mathtt{LQR}}(K)=2(RK+B^{{\mathsf{T}}}P)X=0\hskip 9.24994pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 9.24994ptRK+B^{{\mathsf{T}}}P=0\hskip 9.24994pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 9.24994ptK=-R^{-1}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}P

where
P
is from
29
.
For the derivation of
\nabla J_{\mathtt{LQR}}(K)
, see for example

[
70
, Section IV]
.
Now, by substituting
K=-R^{-1}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}P
into
29
, we obtain the ARE
11
.
The uniqueness of
K
is guaranteed by the uniqueness of
P^{\star}
in
Lemma

3
.
Thus, we have
P=P^{\star}
, and
K^{\star}
is clearly optimal (see e.g.,
Appendix

E
).
∎

This proposition implies that the first part of
Assumption

2
is crucial for our main results based on
the
\mathtt{ECL}
(i.e., the exact convex reformulation).
For
K\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{ctrb}}
, we have
X\succeq 0
, under which we cannot formulate
\mathtt{ECL}
s.
Moreover, we also observe that
the non-uniqueness of optimal gains cannot happen on

\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{ctrb}}

under
Assumption

1
.
Indeed, the following proposition shows that
if we see the non-uniqueness,
the solutions lie in
\mathcal{K}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{ctrb}}
.
This is consistent with
Example

1
because
(A+BK,W^{1/2})
is uncontrollable and
X
becomes singular for
K=[k,k]\in\mathcal{K}
where we have the uncountable optimal solutions.
Note that the second part is a restatement of
Corollary

1
.

Proposition D.2

(Properties of non-unique optimal gains)
.

Suppose
Assumption

1
.
Let
K^{\star}=-R^{-1}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}P^{\star}

with
P^{\star}
from the ARE
11
.
Then,
the following statements hold.

1)

If we have another optimal solution

satisfying
K_{1}^{\star}\neq K^{\star}
,
we have
K^{\star}_{1}\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{ctrb}}
.

2)

If we have two optimal solutions
K^{\star}_{1},\,K^{\star}_{2}\in\mathcal{K}
, then

K^{\star}_{\eta}:=K^{\star}_{1}+\eta(K^{\star}_{1}-K^{\star}_{2})

is also optimal
for any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}

as long as
K^{\star}_{\eta}\in\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

The first part is shown as follows.
Leveraging the proof of the lower bound of

in
[
25

,
we have

0=J_{\mathtt{LQR}}(K^{\star}_{1})-J_{\mathtt{LQR}}(K^{\star})=\mathrm{tr}(K_{1}^{\star}-K^{\star})^{{\mathsf{T}}}R(K_{1}^{\star}-K^{\star})X^{\star}_{1},

where
X^{\star}_{1}
is the solution to the Lyapunov equation
6b
associated with
K_{1}^{\star}
.
Then, by

, we observe that
\left\|(K^{\star}_{1}-K^{\star})(X_{1}^{\star})^{1/2}\right\|_{F}=0
.
Since

must be singular and thus

.
The second part follows from

1
.
∎

Without controllability,
we may have many optimal LQR gains.
On the other hand, if we only have stabilizability, then the unboundedness of

on the boundary still holds.
This shows that
the stabilizability of
(A+BK,W^{1/2})
for

is sufficient for confirming

as

.

.

With

,

The proof of this result follows the first part of

Appendix E
Completion of squares in the classical LQR proof

Here, we review a classical proof of the global optimality of a static state feedback policy in LQR using a completion-of-squares technique. This approach can be found
in classical textbooks such as

[
1
, Theorem 14.2]
and
[
71
, Theorem 21.1]
.
In the following proof, we assume
Assumption

1
holds.

Consider the LQR problem
42
, and let
P
be a
n\times n
symmetric matrix.
Then, for any input
u\in\mathcal{L}_{2}^{m}[0,\infty)
that stabilizes the system, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dt}(x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(t))dt=

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{d}{dt}(x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(t))dt

(E.1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}x(T)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(T)-x(0)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(0)=-x(0)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(0).

We also have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(t))

\displaystyle=\dot{x}(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(t)+x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}P\dot{x}(t)

(E.2)

\displaystyle=\left(Ax(t)+Bu(t)\right)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(t)+x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}P\left(Ax(t)+Bu(t)\right),

where we have applied the dynamics
\dot{x}=Ax+Bu
.
Then, we can reformulate the LQR cost function as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Qx(t)+u(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Ru(t)\right)dt

(E.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Qx(t)+u(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Ru(t)\right)dt+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dt}(x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(t))dt-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dt}(x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(t))dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Qx(t)+u(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Ru(t)+\left(Ax(t)+Bu(t)\right)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(t)+x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}P\left(Ax(t)+Bu(t)\right)\right)dt+x_{0}^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}\left(A^{{\mathsf{T}}}P+PA+Q\right)x(t)+u(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Ru(t)+2u(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(t)\right)dt+x_{0}^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px_{0},

where the second equality applies
E.1
and
E.2
. We observe that the last equality is a quadratic functional of
x(t)
and
u(t)
, and moreover, we can perform a
completion of squares
as follows

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}\left(A^{{\mathsf{T}}}P+PA+Q\right)x(t)+u(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Ru(t)+2u(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(t)\right)dt+x_{0}^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px_{0}

(E.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(u(t)+R^{-1}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(t))^{{\mathsf{T}}}R(u(t)+R^{-1}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(t))dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}\left(A^{{\mathsf{T}}}P+PA+Q-PBR^{-1}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}P\right)x(t)dt+x_{0}^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px_{0}.

By completing the square in
E.4
, we clearly see a nonnegative square term
(u(t)+R^{-1}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(t))^{{\mathsf{T}}}R(u(t)+R^{-1}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}Px(t))
and the familiar Riccati equation.
We then select
P
as the unique stabilizing solution
P^{\star}
to the ARE (
11
), which is guaranteed to exist by
Lemma

3
. From
E.3
and
E.4
, it is clear that we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\left(x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Qx(t)\!+\!u(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Ru(t)\right)dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\left(u(t)+R^{-1}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}P^{\star}x(t)\right)^{{\mathsf{T}}}R\left(u(t)+R^{-1}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}P^{\star}x(t)\right)dt+x_{0}^{{\mathsf{T}}}P^{\star}x_{0}

(E.5)

for any input
u\in\mathcal{L}_{2}^{m}[0,\infty)
that stabilizes the system.

From
E.4
and
E.5
, we can see that the optimal control input to the LQR problem
42
is a linear static feedback
u(t)=-R^{-1}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}P^{\star}x(t)
. Indeed, if we use any stabilizing input
\tilde{u}(t)=-R^{-1}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}P^{\star}x(t)+v(t)
in
E.4
, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(x(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Qx(t)+u(t)^{{\mathsf{T}}}Ru(t)\right)dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}v^{{\mathsf{T}}}(t)Rv(t)dt+x_{0}^{{\mathsf{T}}}P^{\star}x_{0}\geq x_{0}^{{\mathsf{T}}}P^{\star}x_{0}.

Since
R\succ 0
, the optimal LQR cost value is achieved only when
v(t)\equiv 0
.
Therefore,
{u}(t)=-R^{-1}B^{{\mathsf{T}}}P^{\star}x(t)
is the
unique
optimal control input to the LQR problem.

Remark E.1

(Unique optimal control input vs unique optimal feedback gain)
.

The completion of squares in
E.4
plays a key role in the proof above. It confirms that the optimal control input is in the form of
linear static state feedback
, and this optimal input signal
u(t)
is
unique
. However, it does not guarantee the uniqueness of the optimal feedback gain
K
.
In fact, as demonstrated in
Example

1
, the LQR instance under
Assumption

1
admits non-unique optimal feedback gains, even though the optimal input signal
u(t)
remains unique. If the covariance matrix
W
is strictly positive definite, the optimal LQR feedback gain is then unique (
Proposition

1
).
While the completion-of-squares proof is relatively straightforward, the technique of adding and subtracting
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dt}x^{{\mathsf{T}}}Pxdt
in
E.3
is non-trivial.
Our alternative proof strategy, based on primal-dual analysis (
Theorem

4
), provides additional insights — specifically, it shows that the linear static feedback policy naturally emerges from the KKT conditions of a standard convex optimization problem.
\square
