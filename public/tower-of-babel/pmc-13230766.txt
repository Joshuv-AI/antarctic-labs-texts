Depth of rainfall induced landslides revealed by DEM of difference analysis using airborne LiDAR data in igneous terrains
Authors: Yuki Kudo, Taro Uchida, Misa Tsushima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Landslide areas and depths were investigated using a digital elevation model (DEM) of difference (DoD) analysis based on pre- and post-rainfall airborne laser survey data. Three regions, consisting predominantly of granite or granodiorite bedrock, were selected as study sites: (A) the Noborikawa River Basin, (B) the Serizawa and Iriyamazawa Basins, and (C) the Uchikawa River Basin. Site C features gentler slopes than A and B. Although, assuming scale-invariant geometry, landslide depth is theoretically expected to depend on area, this was not observed, especially for shallow landslides. However, in all three regions, the landslide area was not a dominant determining factor for the depth of particularly shallow landslides. At site C, the landslide depth did not correlate with area, while at sites A and B it showed only a weak dependence. In both regions, gentle slopes showed a decreasing lower envelope of the landslide depth with increasing slope angle, while steep slopes showed a decreasing upper envelope. The results align with infinite slope theory, suggesting that landslide depth is controlled by mechanical equilibrium and maximum soil depth on steep slopes, rather than landslide area.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46714-4.

Introduction

Climate change has driven an increase in the frequency of severe rainfall events worldwide in recent years1. Based on historical rainfall data in Japan, the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) reported such an increase occurring gradually over the past 120 years2. Although the precise impact of climate change on the risk of landslide occurrence remains difficult to assess and currently cannot be accurately estimated, the potential risk to human lives posed by landslides triggered by intense rainfall clearly cannot be ignored3,4.

The large volume of sediment carried by flowing rainwater is a key contributing factor for landslides, debris flows, and other life-threatening phenomena associated with sediment transport. The scale of transported sediment correlates closely with landslide magnitude. Predicting likely landslide locations and estimating their potential scale is crucial for damage mitigation and for implementing effective prevention measures. Numerous landslide prediction studies have examined the effects of topography, geology, and vegetation on landslide occurrence5,6. However, the development of techniques for predicting landslide magnitudes (in terms of their area, volume, and other characteristics) remains a significant challenge.

An accurate prediction of landslide magnitude is essential for identifying slopes that require priority countermeasures and for designing rigorous disaster prevention strategies. While considerable research has been conducted on landslide areas using aerial photographs and other data sources7,8, landslide depth has received less attention and requires further data.

Studies of geometrical factors have shown that the landslide volume increases exponentially with the landslide area, independently of the terrain mechanical or geomorphological properties9. For example, Malamud et al.8 calculated landslide volumes using the power-law relationship between the landslide area A and the volume V initially proposed by Simonett10 and Hovius et al.11. Various studies have used the power law V = αAβ9,12–17. The empirical coefficients α and β are influenced by site-specific conditions, such as the lithology, hillslope geometry, landslide type, and triggering factors12,13. Notably, many studies have found β to exceed 1.0, indicating that landslide depth increases with area.

Conversely, the slip surface of shallow landslides is often observed to form near the soil–bedrock interface18,19, suggesting that the landslide depth generally approximates soil thickness. Soil thickness therefore plays a significant role in controlling the volume–area relationship of shallow landslides13. In some cases, however, the slip surface may develop within the transition zone, particularly where the bedrock is highly fractured or weathered. When this transition zone is thin, the soil thickness is likely to exert primary control on landslide depth. Furthermore, a sufficiently thin soil layer can make the landslide depth independent of the area. Indeed, some of previous observations of β being close to 1.0 indicate situations where the landslide depth was not area-dependent13,15. Prancevic et al.20 recently observed that landslides on steep slopes tend to be smaller and shallower owing to the thinness of the soil layers. This agrees with previous modeling studies that indicated a theoretical instability where, for slopes exceeding the soil’s internal friction angle, the soil thickness exceeded a certain threshold. An upper theoretical limit to the soil thickness was therefore postulated21–23.

Several studies in recent decades have examined the spatial soil thickness distribution to investigate the relationship between topography and the soil thickness on hill slopes21,24–26. Heimsath et al.24 demonstrated that the soil thickness is related to the slope curvature, suggesting that it is determined by an equilibrium between the soil production and erosion rates. On steep hill slopes, the soil layer thickness distribution is highly variable and cannot be treated uniformly26–28. After these pioneer studies, various methods have been developed to predict the spatial distribution of soil thickness29,30. For example, Catani et al.29 and Gupta et al.30 proposed a numerical model to evaluate the distribution of soil thickness, taking into account slope curvature, slope gradient, and position along the hillslope profile. Despite these advances, measuring or predicting soil thickness distributions over large areas remains challenging. Consequently, few studies have examined the influence of soil thickness on the occurrence of numerous landslides within a given region.

In the last two decades, the use of Digital Elevation Model of Difference (DoD) analysis with airborne laser survey data for disaster assessment has increased significantly in recent years. This approach has been effectively applied to the estimation of landslide depth and volumes, and it has facilitated the rapid accumulation of data on landslide depths, enabling comparisons with various factors such as topography, geology and vegetation31,32. However, as noted by Bernard et al.31, many DoD-based studies have focused on specific or relatively few landslides33,34, and systematic analyses of hundreds of landslides remain limited to only a few studies, such as those by Bernard et al.31 and Ju et al.32. Therefore, we can conclude that a systematic analysis of hundreds of landslides can provide new insights into landslide depth.

The objective of this study is therefore to systematically analyse depth information from 1253 soil-based landslides induced by heavy rainfall using three LiDAR datasets acquired before and after landslide occurrence. Then, we also examine the roles of the landslide area and slope gradient. Accordingly, we hypothesized thatif the landslide slip surface is formed within the soil layer, the landslide depth depends on the landslide area;on the other hand, if the slip surface forms at thin soil layer, its depth is limited;on steep slopes, the soil thickness is considered relatively thin, making the landslide depth more sensitive to variations in the soil thickness.

To minimize the influence of geological variability, we focused on rainfall-induced shallow landslides occurring in areas with underlying igneous rock.

Noborikawa river basin (site A)

The entire watershed area considered was 79.9 km2. The watershed is surrounded by a steep hillslope and deeply incised valleys. The highest and lowest elevations in site A are 1964.3 and 257.2 m, respectively. The average riverbed gradient of the Noborikawa River is 1/16. The mean slope gradient is 35.4°. According to the High-Resolution Land-Use and Land-Cover Map of Japan (2024JPN_ver. 25.04) provided by the Earth Observation Research Center (EORC) of the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA)39, site A features a mixture of evergreen conifers and deciduous broad-leafed trees. The geology of site A consists predominantly of granite and granodiorite (Fig. 2). Borehole surveys conducted in 2009 revealed weakly weathered granite at an elevation of approximately 870 m with a thickness of about 0.5–1.0 m. A subsequent borehole survey in 2013 found a topsoil layer about 0.1–0.5 m thick between elevations of approximately 690 and 725 m. Beneath this layer, a thick deposit of debris-flow sediment was identified. At a similar elevation, a partially weathered granite layer about 4 m thick was also observed. Borehole surveys conducted in 2014 at an elevation of around 570 m identified a strongly weathered granite layer about 2.5 m thick38. The average annual precipitation over 2002–2011 was 2655 mm.Fig. 2Lithological map of site A.

Uchikawa river basin (site C)

The total Uchikawa River Basin watershed covers 106 km2. The Gofukuya and Shinkawa Rivers flow as tributaries of the Uchikawa River, with basin areas of 24 and 17 km2, respectively. The highest and lowest elevations in site C are 690.2 and 12.9 m, respectively. The variation of elevation in site C was less than at sites A and B. The average riverbed gradient is 1/20. The geology of site C consists predominantly of granodiorite and basalt (Fig. 4). At this site C, borehole survey results identified a sandy silt layer above the bedrock layer. According to the results of a borehole survey conducted in 2022 at an elevation of approximately 40 m, a weathered to strongly weathered granodiorite layer about 0.1–1.5 m thick was identified beneath a sandy soil layer approximately 3 m thick. Below this layer, a hard and thick granodiorite bed was observed. The results of borehole surveys conducted in 2021 within an elevation range from 230 to 250 m revealed a silt layer approximately 2–5 m deep below the surface and a weathered granite layer of approximately 1–2 m thickness below the silt layer. The deeper layers consisted of fresh granodiorite38. The average annual precipitation over 2000–2023 was 1468 mm.Fig. 4Lithological map of site C.

In the borehole surveys, the strongly weathered granite and granodiorite layers were reported as sandy to clayey materials that could be indented by finger pressure. In contrast, the weathered granite and granodiorite layers were described as strata that could be crushed by hammer blows. These descriptions are based on the records of each borehole survey report. In this study, the strongly weathered layers are regarded as soil layers, whereas the weathered layers are defined as a transition zone and are not treated in the same manner as the soil layer.

In July 27–30, 2011, numerous landslides were induced by heavy rain in site A. Niigata prefecture recorded four fatalities and thirteen injuries (serious or minor); forty one houses were completely destroyed. A total of 227 sediment-related disasters, including dosekiryu, jisuberi and gakekuzure, were reported in Niigata Prefecture40. The Shimizu rain gauge43 recorded the maximum 24-h rainfall over a four-day period as 328 mm, occurring on July 30, and the maximum 3-h rainfall as 130 mm on July 30, with a cumulative rainfall of 505 mm over the four days. The torrential rainfall event considered in this study had a greater magnitude than the three previous heavy rainfall events witnessed in the region. In addition, most of the landslides associated with the disaster under the present study were shallow landslides46.

A typhoon of significant intensity occurred in site B on September 7–11, 2015, resulting in a series of sediment-related disasters. The Nakamiyori rain gauge44 recorded the maximum 24-h rainfall over a five-day period as 502 mm, occurring on September 10, the maximum 3-h rainfall as 141 mm, also occurring on September 10, and a cumulative rainfall of 594 mm over those five days. A total of 57 sediment-related disasters were recorded, including dosekiryu, jisuberi and gakekuzure in Tochigi Prefecture41. In the Serizawa basin, where the debris flow occurred, no deep erosion capable of scouring the base rock was observed, except in the upper part of the slope where the debris flow occurred. Instead, small rocks with a grain size of 10–40 cm and driftwood were deposited and washed away. The deposits contained few gravels, suggesting that the sediment mixed with the rainwater and the surface soil47,48. The field survey conducted on the slope at the source of the debris flow identified a layer of collapsed soil, which consisted of past debris flow deposits, and a layer of volcanic ash in the area just below the surface. The results suggested that, during heavy rainfall, a large amount of groundwater may have spread along the volcanic ash layer and mixed with rainwater to form an aquifer within the volcanic ash layer49.

Typhoon Hagibis occurred in Japan on October 11–13, 2019, causing significant damage in the Kanto and Tohoku regions, including site C. The maximum 24-h rainfall recorded at the Hippo rainfall gauge45 over a three-day period was 588 mm, the maximum 3-h rainfall was 212 mm on October 12, and the cumulative rainfall over the three days was 608 mm50. This heavy rainfall caused a total of 145 recorded sediment-related disasters, including dosekiryu, jisuberi and gakekuzure42. There were ten casualties and seventy eight houses were completely destroyed in Marumori-cho51. The National Institute for Land and Infrastructure Management (NILIM) (2020)52 showed the landslide distribution, which was mapped by the Geographical Survey Institute. It also reported that 490 landslides were mapped in the Uchikawa River Basin. The field survey by Irasawa et al.53 considered several landslides in Uchikawa, reporting that most of them could be categorized as shallow landslides and that the soil thickness was generally shallow (0.2–0.4 m). According to Wakatsuki et al.54, the soil layers on the granodiorite slopes consist mainly of weathered soils, and the upper soil layers on the slip surfaces of the landslides identified in the field survey had little parent rock tissue.

In the borehole investigations, the strongly weathered granite and granodiorite layers were reported as sandy to clayey materials that could be indented by finger pressure. In contrast, the weathered granite and granodiorite layers were described as strata that could be crushed by hammer blows. These descriptions are based on the records of each borehole investigation report. It has been reported in numerous studies of various granitic mountain ranges within Japan that the permeability of this weathered bedrock layer is sufficiently low55. Consequently, a temporary saturation zone has been observed to form above it during periods of heavy rainfall56,57. Consequently, it is generally the case that the slip surfaces of shallow landslides often occur above this weathered bedrock layer26. Consequently, in this study too, the weathered bedrock layer confirmed by boreholes can be regarded as a bedrock layer that does not collapse in shallow landslides.

Methods

Landslide mapping

A DEM was used for analysis. Data were collected via airborne LiDAR. The DEM cell size was 1 m × 1 m (Table 3). Aerial photographs (with a resolution of 0.5 m for Sites A and B, and 0.25 m for Site C), an elevation difference map, and a CS topographical map developed by Toda58 were used to identify landslide areas, except for sediment deposition areas. The CS topographical map combines curvature and slope maps created from the DEM and was developed to facilitate topographical interpretation. The threshold drainage area used for classifying hill slopes and channels was set to 10,000 m2 based on the preliminary analysis by Tsushima and Uchida59. Following Montgomery60, Tsushima and Uchida59 examined the relationship between the drainage area and the slope angle. Then, the threshold drainage area was defined as the drainage area where the relationship changes. The study included only landslides that occurred on hill slopes. The landslide area is identical to the polygon area. Figure 5 shows examples of landslide polygons on the CS topographical map. Landslides were identified from pre- and post-disaster elevation differences, CS topographical maps, and aerial photographs to create polygons that included only the collapsed area. When interpreting landslides, no elevation-difference threshold was set. Instead, each landslide was visually identified individually, primarily using the direction of contour lines and, on the CS topographical map, the variations in color that indicate terrain undulations and slope angles. For further details on landslide interpretation, please refer to supplementary materials. Topographic data analysis

The landslide depth was calculated from the differential elevation data pre- and post-disaster within the landslide polygons. The slope angle of the pre-disaster slope was calculated from the DEM, resampled to 10 m using the Surface Parameters tool in ArcGIS Pro (ver. 3.2.2). The reference grid spacing is 10 m. Scale effects of the landslide depth

Various studies have used the power law V = αAβ, described above, to evaluate the relationship between the landslide volume and area9,12,13. Therefore, since the landslide mean depth D obeys D = V/A, it follows that the landslide depth becomes D = αAγ where γ is an empirical parameter. We fitted the observed relationship between the area and the mean depth using this power law and hence determined α and γ.

Results

The slope topography of each site

The pre-disaster slope angle for each site is characterized in Fig. 7. Although the soil thickness and the landslide scale are plausible determinants of the landslide depth, the study sites provide no adequate information about spatial patterns of soil thickness to confirm this hypothesis. Based on an infinite slope stability analysis, previous studies classified slopes into three categories in terms of shallow landslide occurrence: (1) always unstable, (2) slope stability controlled by groundwater conditions, and (3) always stable22,23,71. Those framework studies considered two criteria: on the one hand, the depth of the soil layer and, on the other hand, a slope safety factor, which equals 1 in cases where the soil layer is either completely unsaturated (hmax) or saturated (hcr):4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${h}_{cr}= \frac{c}{{ \gamma }_{w} tan\phi cos\theta + {\gamma }_{sat} cos\theta (tan\theta -tan\phi )}$$\end{document}5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${h}_{max}= \frac{c}{{\gamma }_{sat} cos\theta (tan\theta -tan\phi )}$$\end{document}

where γsat and γw are the specific weights of the saturated soil and water, respectively, φ is the soil frictional angle, θ is the slope angle, and c is the soil cohesion.

The slope is always stable when the soil depth is less than hcr but always unstable when it exceeds hmax. In the latter case, the slope is unsustainable and has already collapsed, indicating that the depth of the existing soil layer should be less than hmax. This means that the upper theoretical limit of the soil thickness on steep slopes where the slope gradient exceeds the frictional angle of the soil depends on the slope gradient. In the intermediate range between hcr and hmax, landslides are possible. Within this range, thicker soil layers require higher groundwater levels to trigger landslides.

We propose the following hypotheses. The depth of soil-based landslides is limited by the thickness of the soil layer present on the slope and by the stability of the soil layer. Thus, firstly, the thickness of the soil layer on a slope varies in its development with the internal friction angle as a boundary. On slopes are gentler than the internal friction angle, it is theoretically considered that landslide is less likely to occur because the groundwater level required to trigger a collapse becomes extremely high (pink area in Fig. 15). A collapse is less likely to occur even if the soil layer on the slope is thick. Secondly, the consistently stable soil layer (blue area in Fig. 15) that exists on gentle slopes may also determine the lower limit of the landslide depth. In other words, the soil layer thickness that guarantees stability decreases as the slope steepens. Consequently, the lower limit of the potential landslide depth decreases in proportion to the increase in the slope gradient. Moreover, the decreasing trend of the lower limit of the landslide depth should be weak as the slope gradient increases (blue line in Fig. 15). Thirdly, on slopes steeper than the internal friction angle, the upper limit of the landslide depth becomes more distinct. This is because there are areas where the soil layer cannot thicken (gray area in Fig. 15). This may be attributed to the thinning of the area that can collapse as a result of this limitation. Consequently, the depth of the soil-based landslides is determined by these three constraints owing to the soil layer stability. It should be noted that our model is based on the theoretical framework for rainfall-induced shallow translational landslides, which consistent with the infinite slope model. Thus, our model is well suited to hillslopes underlain by granular soils deposited on rigid bedrock where a clear “soil depth” limit exists. Therefore, it does not apply to deep-seated landslides or to failures occurring within mechanically homogeneous soft rock formations. Furthermore, to provide additional support for this hypothesis, future studies need to include a survey of soil physical parameter heterogeneity in the field. Conclusion

To conclude, this study analyzed the depth of relatively small landslides triggered by rainfall. By utilizing airborne laser survey data, comprehensive information on landslides, including the pre-disaster topography, was obtained.

We analyzed the determining factors of the landslide depth, focusing on the slope angle and the landslide area. The results showed that the lower limit of the landslide depth tends to decrease with increasing slope angle on gentle slopes, while on steep slopes, the upper limit of the landslide depth tends to decrease as the slope steepens. These findings are consistent with the theoretical framework of soil layer stability based on the infinite slope stability model. Therefore, this study suggests that the soil layer thickness is a critical factor in determining the landslide depth and that this thickness is likely to be largely governed by mechanical balance.

The findings of this study question the general validity of the widely used power-law scaling relationship between landslide area and depth. In situations where soil-based landslides are predominant, and thus the upper limit of landslide depth is determined by soil depth, the potential for deep landslides on steep slopes may be diminished due to the shallow soil depth. Conversely, on gentler slopes, the requirement of a significantly elevated pore water pressure to initiate a landslide may preclude the occurrence of deep landslides. Consequently, it is hypothesized that the phenomenon of landslide depth increasing with landslide area is confined to the range of smaller landslide areas. Indeed, Larsen’s13 research also reports γ values close to 1 when soil-based landslides are predominant, which is consistent with this perspective.

These results improve our understanding of the distribution characteristics of the soil layers that initiate landslides. They are expected to contribute to the development of technologies for predicting landslide occurrence and magnitude. The measured landslide depths in this study could be explained by the theoretical framework of the infinite slope model. However, it should be noted that the infinite slope model considerably simplifies the complex geometry of actual slopes. It is evident that the stability of real slopes is influenced by a combination of longitudinal and transverse factors, thereby rendering it a three-dimensional phenomenon. Additionally, the thickness of soil layers is not uniform. Furthermore, while the present study assumed a uniform spatial distribution of soil strength, in reality, this should vary considerably. It should also be noted that factors that can influence the depth of shallow landslides—such as the presence of a very shallow groundwater table, root systems, and vegetation—were not taken into account in the present analysis. It is considered that further consideration should be given to the development of a more accurate mechanism for determining landslide depth, with these influences being given due consideration.
