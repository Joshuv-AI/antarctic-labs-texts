Long-term stability of moisture-capturing hydrogels by preventing metal-mediated degradation
Authors: Carlos D Díaz-Marín, Chad T Wilson, Won Jun Song, Xiao-Yun Yan, Yuran Shi, Shucong Li, Chang Liu, Emily Lin, Yang Zhong, Lorenzo Masetti, Xuanhe Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSorbent-based atmospheric water harvesting promises passive, geography-independent, and economical freshwater production. Achieving low water cost through moisture harvesting demands inexpensive, high-performance, and durable sorbents. Among all sorbents, hydrogel salt-composites have exceptional performance at a low cost. However, hydrogel durability has so far been overlooked, ultimately preventing moisture capture from realizing reliable, inexpensive water production. In this work, we systematically study hydrogel-salt composite degradation under different conditions. We demonstrate that commonly used polyacrylamide-lithium chloride (PAM-LiCl) are intrinsically durable, with a limited decrease (~50%) in their elastic moduli, even at elevated temperatures (75 °C) and for prolonged durations (>8 months). In contrast, degradation quickly occurs (<3 weeks) when these hydrogels interface with copper and copper oxides, as is common practice in moisture-harvesting devices or for polyvinyl alcohol-lithium chloride hydrogels (in <50 days). We rationalize these results through a proposed metal-mediated degradation mechanism involving hydroxyl radical generation, which is consistent with our PAM-LiCl observations, including ion concentration measurements and experiments with other metals. With the insights from our experiments and proposed mechanism, we implement coatings which prevent hydrogel degradation. This enables stable cyclic moisture absorption-desorption (>190 cycles) and provides a path towards <0.01 $/L water from moisture.Subject terms: Gels and hydrogels, Mechanical engineering, Polymers

Introduction

Earth’s atmosphere contains 13,000 trillion liters of freshwater which can be utilized irrespective of geography to mitigate the water scarcity challenge1,2. In particular, sorption-based atmospheric water harvesting (SAWH) has unique advantages as a moisture-harvesting technology, such as the potential to be passive, viable even in arid regions, decentralized, and low-cost3. Due to its potential, it has been estimated that SAWH can produce water for over a billion people2. Sorbent materials capable of controllably capturing and releasing humidity are key to achieving the high performance demanded of SAWH. This has motivated the development and study of numerous sorbents including metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)4,5, covalent organic frameworks (COFs)6, zeolites7,8, hygroscopic salts9,10, and hydrogel-salt composites11–14. The latter have recently attracted significant interest due to their low cost, scalability, and ability to prevent practical problems of salts, such as water leakage10,14. These promising properties have inspired significant efforts to enhance the performance of hydrogel composites. For instance, previous works have achieved extreme moisture capture even in low humidity14,15, fast capture and release kinetics12,16,17, and high energy efficiency for moisture release18,19 with hydrogel-salt composites. These factors make hydrogels promising candidates for inexpensive, liter-scale SAWH.

Still, the durability of these materials, which is necessary for long-term, inexpensive water production, has commonly been overlooked. For instance, previous works have demonstrated material-level cyclic sorption-desorption stability for <30 cycles13,14,17,19,20. Although these measurements have shown consistent moisture uptake, the limited number of cycles demonstrated is typically insufficient for economic water production1. Furthermore, due to the nature of these experiments, these measurements do not capture critical features of realistic device-level operation, such as the possible degradation due to interaction with device components, nor the response to the increased temperatures needed for desorption (Fig. 1, center)3,21–23. On a material-level, the mechanical stability of hydrogels in SAWH conditions, which is critical for water and salt retention14, has not been systematically studied. For instance, Li et al., found a significant increase in the elastic modulus of hydrogel-salt composites after >1 month-long SAWH24. However, the mechanisms and factors causing these changes have not been explored, preventing the implementation of strategies that enable long-term, stable operation. Similarly, previous work observed a dynamic increase in the rigidity of polyacrylamide (PAM) hydrogels in calcium chloride solutions due to hydrolysis and ion-polymer interactions, leading to deswelling25. These two works suggest a stiffening of the hydrogel-salt composite over time, which can critically cause salt leakage into the drinking water due to a reduced hydrogel swelling ability (Fig. 1, right)14,26,27. On the other hand, studies on degradation of PAM and polyvinyl alcohol, commonly used polymers in hydrogel-salt composites13,14,18,25,28–30, have found degradation in the form of chain scission in response to ultraviolet light, shear flow, iron ions, and elevated temperatures31–38. This chain scission reduces the hydrogel's mechanical integrity, potentially causing material loss during SAWH (Fig. 1, left). For PAM, this could potentially lead to acrylamide, which is carcinogenic, contaminating the condensed drinking water32,33,39. Degradation of other polymers commonly used in hydrogel-salt composites like polyvinyl alcohol18,40, polyacrylic acid41, and poly(N-isopropylacrylamide)19 could similarly lead to material loss over time and performance decay. All these works highlight the critical knowledge gap in the degradation mechanisms of hydrogel-salt composites under SAWH conditions, a crucial step towards safe, reliable, and low-cost water production from air.

Schematic of hydrogel-salt composite during SAWH and possible degradation mechanisms.

During SAWH, a hydrogel-salt composite typically experiences high temperatures during desorption and is interfaced to a metal surface as part of the water harvesting device3,12,21,22,51. These conditions can lead to unique degradation processes, which can limit the hydrogel lifetime and compromise the produced water quality. For instance, the hydrogel polymer chains or crosslinks can break, causing loss of the hydrogel mechanical integrity and potential material loss over time33,39,60. Alternatively, the hydrogel can stiffen over time24,25, limiting its ability to retain salt solution and potentially leading to leakage of salt into the drinking water.

In this work, we study the critical property of durability in moisture-capturing hydrogels by performing long-term and systematic studies of hydrogel-salt composite stability and degradation under different SAWH-relevant conditions. Using polyacrylamide-lithium chloride (PAM-LiCl) as a model system, we first demonstrate that PAM-LiCl hydrogels are intrinsically durable, with a limited decrease (~50%) in their elastic modulus, even at high temperatures representative of desorption (75 °C), and for prolonged durations (>8 months), in contrast with polyvinyl alcohol-lithium chloride (PVA-LiCl) hydrogels, which suffer a similar modulus decrease in <45 days. On the other hand, PAM-LiCl stability is drastically reduced when in contact with copper and copper oxides, as is common in moisture-harvesting devices, leading to degradation in less than 3 weeks. This degradation does not occur with other metals and metal oxides such as iron, iron oxides, and aluminum oxides. We rationalize these results through a proposed metal-mediated degradation mechanism involving the generation of metal ions, followed by a hydroxyl radical production step. Through systematic experiments, we find that this mechanism is consistent with all our observations, including ion concentration measurements and stability with various metals. Leveraging the findings from our experiments and proposed mechanism, we implement anti-corrosion coatings that prevent hydrogel-salt composite degradation. This simple strategy enables stable cyclic moisture absorption and desorption for >190 cycles over 96 days. The findings from this work provide a crucial path towards <0.01 $/L water production from moisture and yield novel insights into hydrogel stability for a wide range of applications that interface hydrogels with metals.

Results

Hydrogel synthesis and characterization of intrinsic stability

We synthesized PAM-LiCl and PVA-LiCl hydrogels using a one-pot synthesis process in which monomer/polymer, salt, and crosslinker are all dissolved in water and mixed (see Methods for details)29,30,42. This simple synthesis allowed us to fabricate a large number of hydrogels per batch (>100, Figs. S1, S3) for long-term degradation studies.

We first studied the intrinsic stability of the hydrogel-salt composites, that is, their stability without any additional chemical component that is not present in the as-synthesized hydrogel (see Methods for a detailed experimental description). This allowed us to study the hydrogel stability in a simple and controlled setting, removing the effect of device-level components, such as the metals to which hydrogels are typically interfaced3,21,22,24,28. For this first study, we left the hydrogels in a closed container with aqueous LiCl solution at the same concentration as that used during the synthesis (~0.25 g LiCl/g solution). This allowed us to have a constant hydrogel composition, in terms of polymer, water, and salt, in the absence of changes in the polymer or polymer network, even for prolonged periods and elevated temperatures14. Such a constant composition would be challenging to achieve if the hydrogels were placed in an air environment, as they can transiently desorb moisture, especially at high temperatures30,43. The container with the hydrogels in the aqueous LiCl solution was placed in an oven at 75 °C, a temperature representative of SAWH desorption12,20,22,44, which was expected to accelerate degradation. We let the hydrogels reach swelling equilibrium in the salt solution at this temperature, to ensure a constant hydrogel composition (Fig. S2). After swelling equilibrium, we periodically removed three hydrogels, measured their elastic modulus in compression and volume, and finally discarded the tested hydrogels. The measured elastic moduli and volume provided a simple way to probe hydrogel degradation since the breaking of polymer chains or crosslinkers due to degradation reduces the modulus, while volume loss might indicate polymer degradation45.

Figure 2a shows the long-term, hydrogel elastic moduli, where the time is measured after reaching swelling equilibrium. For PAM-LiCl, the elastic moduli had an initial value of ~ 6 kPa. Across 8 months of testing, the moduli exhibited random variations, which may be attributed to sample variability due to network imperfections46,47, partial hydrolysis25, and varying salt concentration14. After >180 days of being in a 75 °C environment, the moduli had only a limited decrease to ~3 kPa. On the other hand, the PVA-LiCl moduli quickly decreased to half its initial value in less than 45 days, indicating less stability compared to PAM-LiCl. The PAM-LiCl stability and PVA-LiCl degradation are further confirmed by their volume changes over time (Fig. 2b). While the volume of PAM-LiCl samples remained relatively constant over >180 days, the PVA-LiCl volume quickly decreased by ~40% in 40 days. As a consequence, PAM-LiCl hydrogels visibly looked similar to their initial state even after >8 months, in contrast to PVA-LiCl, which showed a visible shrinkage and a yellowing after 40 days (insets Fig. 2b).

These results suggest that PAM-LiCl hydrogels have high stability even at 75 °C, while PVA-LiCl hydrogels suffer from faster chemical degradation in these conditions. We hypothesize that the observed PVA-LiCl changes arise from thermal degradation. Previous work has shown that PVA can quickly dehydrate and thermally degrade (~minutes) at elevated temperatures (≥100 °C), leading to chain rupture and sample yellowing, consistent with our observations34,37,48. Similar processes occur only at ≥200 °C for PAM. This difference highlights the relevance of choosing polymers with high stability at typical desorption temperatures (~100 °C) for durable SAWH with hydrogel-salt composites. Beyond polymer chemistry, the salt type can also influence the degradation. For instance, previous work observed dramatic deswelling and crosslinking over time by PAM in calcium chloride solutions25. Our results (Fig. 2a) suggest that PAM-LiCl is more stable than PAM with calcium chloride, consistent with previous observations of considerable ion-polymer crosslinking with multivalent ions (e.g., Ca2+), but not as significant with monovalent ions (e.g., Li+)49. Therefore, based on both polymer and salt stability, we focused on PAM-LiCl hydrogels throughout the rest of this work.

PAM-LiCl hydrogels also exhibited relatively stable moisture capture, stability at temperatures <200 °C, and relatively unchanged absorption spectra (Fig. S4). The moisture uptake was reduced by only 13%, 10%, and 10% at 30%, 50%, and 70% relative humidity, respectively, from its value after reaching swelling equilibrium in the solution to after seven months at 75 °C (Fig. 2c, see Methods for details). Based on previous works, which have shown that the uptake is mostly determined by the hydrogel salt content14,16,42, the decreased uptake after > 7 months indicates a lower lithium chloride content in the hydrogel, consistent also with the slight volume decrease observed (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, we characterized the temperature-dependent hydrogel stability through a thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, see Methods for details) where the as-synthesized hydrogel-salt composite mass is measured as the sample is heated up to 1100 °C and different hydrogel-salt composite components are removed. Figure 2d shows the hydrogel-salt composite mass as a function of temperature, showing a decrease in mass as temperature increases. We interpret our TGA results in terms of a three-stage process with sequential removal of the a) water, b) polymer, and c) salt, respectively (see Note S2 for details). At lower temperatures (<200 °C), the mass loss corresponded to water desorption from the sample, while the mass of the polymer and the salt remained essentially constant35,36,50. In the second stage, at intermediate temperature (~200–600 °C), the polymer degrades, while the salt remains stable35,36,50. In the last step, the salt degrades beyond 600 °C. Based on this description, PAM-LiCl hydrogels are stable at temperatures below 200 °C, as observed in our long-term experiments (Fig. 2a, b) and in agreement with previous studies of PAM35,36.

Metal-mediated hydrogel degradation

In a SAWH device, a hydrogel composite is typically interfaced with additional components, such as metal surfaces, which can dramatically change its stability. Although not all SAWH devices use metallic parts, the use of metals can enable compactness, device robustness, and high-performance3,12,21,22. Therefore, we investigate the potential for hydrogel degradation caused by these metal components. Figure 3a shows a PAM-LiCl hydrogel over a copper plate (101 copper, McMaster-Carr), which is representative of common high thermal conductivity metal components used in SAWH3,22,51. Initially, the hydrogel was transparent and preserved the as-fabricated shape. The hydrogel-coated copper plate was left in a hermetically sealed petri dish at room temperature. After 2 months of being in contact with the copper, the hydrogel had noticeably changed color. After > 9 months, the color change was even greater, and the hydrogel exhibited some tears, which led to the copper plate coming out of the hydrogel. These tears, which can be extremely damaging for practical use, contrast sharply with the observed PAM-LiCl stability in the absence of copper (Fig. 2a).

We systematically studied the effect of copper in PAM-LiCl degradation through experiments following a similar procedure to that of Fig. 2a. We placed hydrogel-salt composites in aqueous LiCl solutions (~0.25 g LiCl/g solution), added powders of copper and copper oxides, left them in closed containers, and periodically removed samples to test their moduli (see Methods for details of the experimental procedure). Figure 3b shows the elastic moduli over time for hydrogels left with different metals/metal oxides and at different temperatures. Initially, all hydrogels had moduli ~ 3–6 kPa. However, as time passed, the hydrogels left with metal powders at 75 °C exhibited a dramatic decrease in their modulus. The PAM-LiCl hydrogels left with copper (Cu) powders had a modulus of ~0 kPa after two weeks, while the hydrogels left with copper oxides (CuO and Cu2O) had a ~0 kPa modulus after three weeks. This fast loss of rigidity is in stark contrast with the observed long-term durability in the absence of copper and copper oxides (Fig. 2a). Additionally, for PVA-LiCl hydrogels, copper further reduced their stability (Fig. S5). At room temperature (~23 °C), the PAM-LiCl moduli with Cu did not considerably decrease during three weeks.

Figure 3c–j shows the PAM-LiCl composites after 1 day in their respective solution with metal/metal oxides and after 2-3 weeks. After 1 day, all hydrogels looked similar regardless of the metal/metal oxide (Fig. 3c–f). However, after 2–3 weeks all hydrogels left at 75 °C lost their mechanical rigidity, becoming a liquid-like solution. This agrees with the observed decay of elastic moduli (Fig. 3b). PAM-LiCl with copper at room temperature retained their mechanical integrity after three weeks, in agreement with their stable moduli observed in Fig. 3b. We also note that all hydrogels had a slight change of color towards a blue color after 2-3 weeks, which can be indicative of the presence copper ions52. To quantify this, we measured the copper ion concentration in the solutions at the end of the degradation experiments using Inductively Coupled Plasma - Optical Emission Spectrometer (ICP-OES, see Methods for details). Figure 3k shows a measurable copper ion content for all the samples left with metal powders. The solutions with Cu2O, CuO, and Cu at 75 °C all had copper ion concentrations between 10–100 ppm. Interestingly, these concentrations were similar despite the Cu release only lasting 13 days at 75 °C compared to 21 days for CuO and Cu2O. This suggests fast Cu+ release for Cu powders, in agreement with the observed fast hydrogel degradation with Cu powder. On the other hand, the solution with Cu at room temperature had a lower ion content of ~ 4 ppm. Lastly, if no copper metal powders are added, as we did in the experiments corresponding to Fig. 2a, then <1 ppm of copper was measured, as expected.

Altogether, the results of Fig. 3 point to critical changes due to the presence of copper/copper oxides, including the generation of copper ions and the 2 - 3 week hydrogel degradation for the intrinsically stable PAM-LiCl. We propose a possible degradation pathway to elucidate these observations. First, we hypothesize that the color change observed in the hydrogels is linked to the generation of copper ions from the copper/copper oxides (e.g. for pure copper,Cu→Cu++e−, corresponding to the anodic reaction of copper corrosion)52. This is consistent with previous thermodynamic studies showing the generation of copper ions (Cu+) at low concentrations of dissolved species and high temperatures53. Secondly, we note that previous works have reported that metal ions can oxidize to produce hydroxyl radicals, *OH. These hydroxyl radicals, in turn, can cause degradation of organic matter54–56. For instance, previous works reported that the Fenton reaction, which involves the oxidation of iron ions to produce hydroxyl radicals, degrades polyacrylamide31,57. Similarly, the oxidation of copper ions can produce hydroxyl radicals58,59. Based on these observations, we hypothesize a second degradation step given by 3Cu++O2+2H+→3Cu2++OH−+*OH. This step also implies the consumption of dissolved oxygen, as observed in previous studies of degradation of linear PAM by iron ions31,60. Finally, we propose that the generated radicals break the polymer chains, as also reported in literature31,54,57–59,61. This polymer chain scission translates into the observed decrease of moduli45. Taken together, these three steps provide a possible mechanism for the observed metal-mediated degradation of PAM-LiCl hydrogels. In our proposed mechanism, we have not considered the role of LiCl salt, as we have also observed similar hydrogel degradation in the absence of LiCl (Fig. S7).

Our experimental observations support this degradation mechanism. Firstly, our ion concentration measurements show that copper ions are generated as proposed. Secondly, we observe a correlation between the generation of copper ions and hydrogel degradation. This also follows from our proposed mechanism, in which chain scission requires prior copper-mediated generation of hydroxyl radicals. In the absence of copper ions, the hydrogels exhibit significantly enhanced stability, as there is no significant radical generation and thus no chain scission. For copper at room temperature, the smaller copper ion content is consistent with the thermodynamics of copper and water, which leads to lower ion content at lower temperatures53. The slow degradation at room temperature is consistent with a slower generation of hydroxyl radicals due to, partially, the lower concentration of copper ions (~4 ppm) and slower kinetics of ion oxidation and chain scission (steps 2 and 3 of our proposed degradation mechanism), ultimately leading to slower degradation. Additionally, we observed lower degradation when using a radical scavenger (Fig. S8), supporting the role of radicals in the degradation. Lastly, our proposed mechanism is further corroborated by experiments with polyacrylamide solutions (i.e., not chemically crosslinked), which suggest a fast molecular weight decrease due to copper-mediated chain scission as reflected by the 100X decrease over 22 days in the viscosity of solutions with copper relative to samples without copper (Fig. S10, see Methods for details).

Beyond copper, we also studied the degradation of PAM-LiCl hydrogels in contact with other metals commonly used in SAWH devices, such as aluminum and iron. Figure 4a shows the elastic modulus measurements for hydrogels left in solutions with Al2O3, Fe2O3, Fe, and FeO powders at 75 °C. The moduli had initial values of ~ 6 kPa. During three weeks, the moduli fluctuated, as seen also in our experiments without any metals (Fig. 2a). However, no considerable decay occurred, in strong contrast with our results with copper/copper oxides. Figure 4b–e show the hydrogels left in the different metal solutions after one day. After 3-4 weeks, the hydrogels preserved their shape and mechanical rigidity, looking similar to their initial state (Fig. 4f–i). These results (Fig. 4a–i) suggest hydrogel stability in these conditions, like that observed in our experiments without metals (Fig. 2a) and in marked contrast with the degradation with copper (Fig. 3).

We measured the ion content of all solutions that the hydrogels were left in for three weeks after swelling equilibrium using ICP-OES. Across all solutions, the copper and the aluminum concentrations were small, <10 ppm and <1 ppm, respectively. The iron concentration was <1 ppm in all solutions except the bath containing pure iron powder, which exhibited a ~10 ppm concentration. Even this highest ion concentration is lower than the highest concentrations measured when adding copper powders to the solutions (Fig. 3k). Altogether, these results show significantly smaller generation of metal ions for iron, iron oxides, and aluminum oxide under the conditions studied, which is consistent with the expected thermodynamic equilibrium of aluminum-water62 and iron-water63. This higher thermodynamic stability of the iron and aluminum oxides under the conditions studied leads to the key difference with the copper experiments. For stable oxides, a smaller number of ions is generated. According to the proposed mechanism, hydroxyl radical generation and subsequent chain scission are not expected to be as significant under these conditions, leading to the observed higher stability. These observations highlight again the correlation between metal ions and degradation, as also observed in our experiments with copper. Based on previous literature, we hypothesize that if iron ion generation is more significant (>20 ppm), some degradation could occur via an iron autoxidation mechanism31. On the other hand, Fenton-like degradation is likely not occurring as no hydrogen peroxide was used in our experiments31,54.

Long-term stability by preventing metal-mediated degradation

We leverage our experimental insights to prevent degradation and ensure long-term cyclic operation of PAM-LiCl hydrogel-salt composites. We focus on preventing the first degradation step, which involves the copper/copper oxide corrosion and subsequent ion generation. For this, and motivated by its simplicity and low cost, we apply a commercial anti-corrosion coating (Anti-Seize Technology™ Red Insulating Varnish) on a copper plate where we then place a PAM-LiCl hydrogel disk (Fig. 5a). The copper plate serves as a heater that can drive moisture desorption and is representative of heating elements used in sorption devices, such as electrically or photothermally heated surfaces3,21,22,51,64. To test the stability in SAWH-like conditions, we cycled the hydrogel between absorption and desorption in a custom-built setup (see Methods for details of the test and Fig. S11). We heated the copper plate to 80 °C for 2 h, causing moisture desorption from the hydrogel. The heater was then turned off, and the hydrogel was allowed to recapture moisture for 10 h under room temperature and humidity, which varied according to ambient conditions in the laboratory (Fig. S13). This 12-h cycle was repeated 192 times for 96 consecutive days and the hydrogel mass was measured continuously (see Fig. S12). Figure 5a shows the PAM-LiCl hydrogel over the anti-corrosion coated copper plate after 60 days, showing no major changes after 120 cycles. As a comparison, we also placed an identical PAM-LiCl hydrogel on an uncoated copper heater. After 60 days, the hydrogel exhibited signs of degradation, with a color change and some visible salt leakage (Fig. 5b). From a microscopic perspective, we hypothesize that the anti-corrosion coating serves as a protective barrier, stopping the ion generation at the hydrogel-metal interface (Fig. 5c). In contrast, without the anti-corrosion coating, copper ions are generated, as indicated by the hydrogel color change. These copper ions can then lead to polymer chain scission and degradation over time (Fig. 5d), as shown in the previous section. These results and observations highlight the ability of anti-corrosion coatings in preventing metal-mediated hydrogel degradation, ensuring >190 absorption-desorption cycles. Our systematic experiments and mechanism also suggest other possible approaches to prevent degradation, such as using hydroxyl radical scavengers65,66 or alternative device materials that do not lead to degradation. However, we have focused on using anti-corrosion coatings, as they are simple, low-cost, and do not require modifying the material choices of SAWH components. Additionally, metals are commonly used as multi-element alloys, complicating the prediction of durability in different alloys. As an example, we observed degradation of PAM-LiCl on an aluminum alloy fin (Fig. S14) despite our results (Fig. 4) showing no degradation with aluminum oxide.

Anti-corrosion coatings for long cyclic stability of hydrogel-salt composites.

The hydrogel shows a color change and salt leakage after 120 absorption-desorption cycles. c Anti-corrosion coating serves as a protective barrier blocking copper ion generation into the hydrogel. d Without the coating, copper ions are released, which can then break the hydrogel polymer chains. e Cumulative water desorbed by the hydrogel on the anti-corrosion coated copper. The hydrogel steadily captures and releases water over 96 days. f Preliminary technoeconomic analysis for water production cost using SAWH with PAM-LiCl. Our analysis, based on that by Zhong et al.1, shows that by increasing material lifetime from days to > 1 month, the cost of water can be reduced by an order of magnitude to be potentially <0.01 $/L.

Because of its cyclic stability, the PAM-LiCl sorbent can repeatably capture and release a significant amount of water over time. Figure 5e shows the cumulative water desorbed, as calculated from the hydrogel mass before and after heating, over the 96-day period of cycling for the hydrogel on the anti-corrosion coating. This cumulative water desorbed corresponds to the drinking water produced in a SAWH device, assuming perfect moisture transport and condensation21. In total, the hydrogel demonstrates a consistent ability to harvest and desorb moisture, leading to a cumulative water desorbed of almost 500 kg per m2 of hydrogel-salt composite after 192 cycles. This represents a major water production from moisture due to the combined large material lifetime, high uptake, and fast cycling.

Ultimately, long material durability translates into a reduced water cost through SAWH. To illustrate this, we performed a preliminary technoeconomic analysis (TEA) of SAWH using hydrogel-salt composites, based on the study by Zhong et al.1. In our analysis we considered a device cost between $50–$250 per footprint area, a 20-year device lifetime, a water production rate of 2 L per device m2 per day2, and a 1-mm thick PAM-LiCl hydrogel with cost between $0.4 - $1.2 per kilogram. Further details and justifications of these assumptions are provided in Note S10.

Figure 5f shows the TEA results, where the gray area shows the water cost ranges predicted. Our analysis reveals a pronounced impact of the hydrogel lifetime on the water cost. For a ~1 day hydrogel lifetime, the water cost is between $0.1 - $1/L, comparable to the cost of bottled water1. However, for a > 1-month hydrogel lifetime, as we have demonstrated in this work, the water cost can be significantly reduced to <$0.01/L. This low cost starts approaching the tap water cost in different cities in the United States, such as Charleston, West Virginia67 and Boston, Massachusetts68. This potential for low cost, which hinges on high material durability, highlights the critical importance of the insights from this work for achieving low-cost water with SAWH for large populations.

Discussion

In this work, we systematically studied the degradation of hydrogel-salt composites under different SAWH-relevant conditions. We first studied the impact of polymer chemistry on stability. We observed that PAM-LiCl hydrogels are intrinsically stable, with a limited decrease (~50%) in their mechanical modulus after 8 months, even at elevated temperatures representative of desorption. In contrast, PVA-LiCl hydrogels exhibited a similar decrease in their mechanical properties in 40 days. Then, we show that PAM-LiCl hydrogels quickly degrade in <3 weeks, losing their mechanical integrity, when in contact with copper and copper oxides, commonly used metals in SAWH devices. We find that this degradation does not occur with other common materials in SAWH devices, such as iron, iron oxides, and aluminum oxides. We rationalize these results using a proposed metal-mediated degradation mechanism that involves the generation of metal ions, followed by the production of hydroxyl radicals. This mechanism is consistent with all our experimental observations, including measurements of ion concentration and the correlation between ion presence and degradation across all experiments. Lastly, we used our experimental findings and the proposed mechanism to implement anti-corrosion coatings that prevent hydrogel-salt composite degradation. This simple strategy enables stable cyclic moisture absorption-desorption for >190 cycles, providing a path towards <0.01 $/L water production using hydrogel-salt composites. This represents a major step towards low-cost water production to address the water scarcity problem of large populations across the world.

Methods

Hydrogel synthesis

We synthesized hydrogels based on a one-pot approach, as reported in our previous works14,42. For the PAM-LiCl hydrogels, we first dissolved the salt (16.72 g per 50 mL of solution) in deionized water with a magnetic stirring bar. The solution was then covered to prevent evaporation and left to cool down to room temperature. We added sequentially 4.18 g of acrylamide, the N,N’-methylenebisacrylamide (MBA) as crosslinker (50 mg per 50 mL of water), ammonium persulfate (APS) as initiator (14.2 mg per 50 mL of water), and N,N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylendiamin (TEMED) as accelerator (12 μL per 50 mL of water). After adding the TEMED, we quickly poured ~ 1.6 mL of solution per hydrogel sample into circular silicone molds of 23 mm diameter (Fig. S1). The hydrogels in the silicone molds were covered and left to gel overnight at room temperature. For the PVA-LiCl hydrogels, we first added salt (16.72 g per 50 mL of solution) and 99% hydrolyzed PVA (4.18 g per 50 mL of solution) to deionized water, then mixed the solution at 90 °C for 48 h. The solution was covered to prevent evaporation and left to cool down to room temperature. We added sequentially 1.2 M hydrochloric acid as acid catalyst (250 μL per 50 mL of solution) and 25% glutaraldehyde as crosslinker (375 μL per 50 mL of solution). Then we quickly poured ~1.6 mL of solution per hydrogel sample into circular silicone molds of 23 mm diameter and cured it in a 75 °C oven for 24 h.

Long-term experiments of intrinsic hydrogel stability and elastic modulus measurements

After the hydrogels had gelated, we removed them from the molds and placed them in a borosilicate glass container. We covered the hydrogels with aqueous lithium chloride solution of the same concentration as that used in synthesis (16.72 g of LiCl per 50 mL of water). By using the same concentration, we ensured that the salt-to-water concentration in the as-synthesized state and in the fully swollen state was the same14,25. We closed the glass container and placed it in an oven at 75 °C for two days. During these two days we tracked the mass of two hydrogel samples in these conditions and verified that after these two days the hydrogels reached steady state swelling (Fig. S2). After the hydrogels reached steady state swelling, we removed all the aqueous solution from the glass container and replaced it with a fresh solution with the same concentration. This removed unreacted chemical from the polymerization. We placed the glass container filled with salt solution and hydrogels again in the oven at 75 °C. This corresponded to time zero in our measurements. After this point, we periodically removed three different hydrogels, measured their elastic modulus in compression using a TA.XT plus Texture Analyser (with the moduli obtained from the linear section of the stress-strain curve), and finally discarded the used hydrogels. The measurements reported in Fig. 2a correspond to the average of the three moduli measured on a given day. We measured three samples per day to minimize the effects of sample variability across the batch. The error bars correspond to the standard error of the mean. Different batches synthesized with the same recipe showed similar mechanical properties and physical appearance, as corroborated by the initial moduli of PAM-LiCl in Figs. 2a, 3b, 4a. We monitored the water level in the glass container in between measurements to assess the rate of water evaporation from the closed container, finding negligible changes in the water level. However, due to rapid evaporation when the container was open during sample removal, the water concentration could change over time. To minimize the impact of this evaporation, we replaced the salt solution 3 times during the 8-month experiments.

Dynamic Vapor Sorption (DVS) characterization

We characterized the sorption isotherms using a DVS instrument (Surface Measurement Systems Ltd.) as reported in our previous work42. Initially, we dried the samples at 80 °C for 5 h, followed by a thermal equilibration step at 25 °C for 3 h. Next, we recorded the sample mass at a series of RH steps (from 0 to 70%) by regulating the partial pressure of water vapor at 25 °C. For sample equilibrium at each step of RH, we used an equilibrium criterion of 0.005%sample min−1, i.e., a percentage change in sample mass per minute, to ensure precise measurements. The samples used weighed >1 mg, and the DVS resolution was 0.0001 mg, leading to a negligible error due to the measurement resolution.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

We characterized the temperature-dependent decomposition of the PAM-LiCl hydrogels using a Thermal Analysis System TGA/DSC 3+ by Mettler Toledo. We placed an as-synthesized PAM-LiCl hydrogel sample weighing ~10 mg into an inert alumina pan under dry nitrogen gas. We heated the sample from room temperature up to 1100 °C with a heating rate of 1 °C/min and measured the mass as a function of temperature as the different components of the hydrogel decomposed.

Hydrogel stability experiments with metals

We studied the hydrogel stability when in contact with different metals and metal oxides. We followed a process identical to that described before (Long-term experiments of intrinsic hydrogel stability), with the only modification that, after swelling equilibrium and while replacing the solution for the hydrogels, we added metal/metal oxides powders (1mg of powder/mL of water in the solution) in the replacement solution. This corresponded to time zero and for the rest of the experiments, the hydrogels were in this solution with powders. The powder concentration was chosen to be higher than the iron ion concentrations in previous studies of polyacrylamide degradation31,60. Additionally, the metal powder concentrations used led to copper ion concentrations (Fig. 3k) comparable to measured ion concentrations during Fenton-mediated degradation of linear PAM60. The metal powders used were all purchased from Sigma Aldrich: Cu (266086), Cu2O (208825), CuO (241741), Fe (413054), FeO (400866), Fe2O3 (310050), and Al2O3 (199974).

Ion concentrations with Inductively Coupled Plasma - Optical Emission Spectrometry

We measured the ion concentrations using an Agilent ICP-OES 5100 VDV instrument. Each solution at 75 °C was removed from the oven after the last modulus measurement for that solution was taken. Before measurements, we filtered the solutions with a 0.22 µm syringe filter. This filter size is smaller than the particle sizes for the powders used. This should result in a filtered solution where the powders are removed and the metal content is due to ions. This filtering is verified by the strong change of solution color before and after filtering (Fig. S17) and by the <1 ppm concentrations measured for FeO and Fe2O3. We calibrated the ICP-OES instrument with standards for Al (0 ppm, 1 ppm, 40 ppm, 1000 ppm), Cu (0 ppm, 1 ppm, 20 ppm, 1000 ppm), and Fe (0 ppm, 1ppm, 100 ppm, 1000 ppm). All concentrations measured falled between these calibration values, guaranteeing the accuracy of our calibration. For each ion tested, three replicate measurements were taken. The result shown is the average, and the error bars are calculated from the standard deviation.

Steady-shear Viscosity Measurement

We prepared polyacrylamide (molecular weight of 5,000,000–6,000,000 g/mol) solutions with 4 g of salt/g of polymer (identical to the salt/polymer mass ratio of the hydrogels synthesized throughout this work). We dissolved these in 30 g water/g polymer overnight on a hot plate at ~70 °C to ensure proper dissolution. The solutions were left to cool down to ambient temperature. For the solution with copper, we added 1 mg Cu/mL solution. The solutions with and without copper were left in a covered Falcon tube at room temperature for 22 days, after which their viscosity was measured.

The viscosity of the polymer solution was measured using a TA Instruments Ares-G2 rheometer. A steady shear rate of 0.05–100 1/s was applied on the solution while using a 40-mm 2-degree cone geometry. The viscosity was measured during the steady-state at each flow rate using the built-in steady-state sensing algorithm with a 60 s equilibrium time. Samples were kept at 25 °C during measurement, and each was measured twice to demonstrate consistency.

Cyclic absorption and desorption tests

We tested the cyclic moisture absorption and desorption of hydrogel-salt composites with a custom-built setup (Fig. S11). We fabricated two PAM-LiCl hydrogels with a volume of 4 mL and a diameter of 35 mm. Then, we manufactured two custom heating stages out of copper, with dimensions 40 mm by 76 mm by 13 mm. We coated the top surface of one heating stage with a layer of commercial anti-corrosion coating (Anti-Seize Technology™). We installed two parallel 1/8” cartridge heaters with built-in K-type thermocouples (24 V, 96 W, OEM Heaters, Inc.) inside each heater block. Both heating stages were placed on separate 1-inch-thick fiberglass beds serving as thermal insulation, and the beds were subsequently placed in a 3D-printed bed (high-temp resin, Formlabs Inc.) to confine all components. One hydrogel sample was placed atop each heating stage, and the coated sample assembly was placed atop a mass balance to continuously record sample mass throughout testing (A&D Weighing, EK600-i). An Arduino Nano controller was used to switch each stage from absorption to desorption, alongside a Max 66675 thermocouple reader and an OLED display to record temperature and current setpoint configuration throughout testing. A PID controller was used to maintain each heating stage at 80 °C during desorption. Ambient temperature and humidity of the testing chamber were recorded every minute via an Arduino using a temperature and humidity sensor (Grove Temp & Humi Sensor), while mass was recorded every 10 s using A&D Weighing commercial software.
