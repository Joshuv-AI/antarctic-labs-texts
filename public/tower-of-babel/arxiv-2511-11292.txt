The Jasmin Compiler Preserves Cryptographic Security
Santiago Arranz-Olmos, Gilles Barthe, Lionel Blatter, Benjamin Grégoire, Vincent Laporte, Paolo Torrini

Abstract
. Jasmin is a programming and verification framework for developing efficient, formally verified, cryptographic implementations. A main component of the framework is the Jasmin compiler, which empowers programmers to write efficient implementations of state-of-the-art cryptographic primitives, including post-quantum cryptographic standards. The Jasmin compiler is proven functionally correct in the Rocq prover. However, this functional correctness statement does not apply to nonterminating or probabilistic computations, which are essential features in cryptography. In this paper, we significantly enhance the guarantees of the compiler by showing, in the Rocq prover, that its front-end (25 out of 30 passes) preserves cryptographic security. To this end, we first define a Relational Hoare Logic tailored for compiler correctness proofs. We prove the soundness of our logic w.r.t. a new denotational semantics of Jasmin programs based on interaction trees. Secondly, we use our program logic to prove the functional correctness of the (unmodified) Jasmin compiler w.r.t. said semantics. Lastly, we formalize cryptographic security—focusing on IND-CCA—with interaction trees and prove that the Jasmin compiler preserves cryptographic security.



Formalizing Cryptographic Security with ITrees and Proving Compiler
Preservation with RHL

1.
Introduction

Security proofs are at the heart of modern cryptography: (almost)
every new cryptographic scheme comes with a mathematical proof of
security. However, these cryptographic schemes often eschew security
critical components to focus on idealized schemes, sometimes known as
cryptographic cores. The emphasis on cryptographic cores is primarily
justified by the need to ensure that the overall analysis remains
tractable with pen-and-paper proofs. However, this emphasis leads to
dangerous gaps between security proofs and cryptographic schemes as well
as between cryptographic schemes and their implementations.
Both gaps can (and have been) exploited to launch devastating attacks on
deployed cryptographic
implementations
(
Degabriele et al. 2011
)
.

Computer-aided cryptography
(
Barbosa et al. 2021
)
aims
to address these gaps by developing and applying methods and tools for
reasoning about cryptographic implementations, rather than
cryptographic cores. To date, computer-aided cryptography has been
used successfully to deploy high-assurance code in mainstream
cryptographic libraries and applications. While these deployments
avoid the pitfalls of reasoning on cryptographic cores, they still
rely on the assumption that compilers preserve cryptographic security:
(almost) all verifications are performed at source-level
implementations, and implicitly assume that the security guarantees
hold for assembly-level implementations. While reasonable, the
assumption has never been established formally.

This paper addresses this gap by proving that the Jasmin
(
Almeida et al. 2017
;
Almeida et al. 2020
)

compiler front-end
preserves cryptographic security. The proof is mechanized in the

Rocq
prover
(
The Rocq Development Team 2025
)
, and provided as an artifact. Our
choice of the Jasmin compiler is motivated by two reasons. First,
Jasmin is a state-of-the-art framework for high-assurance
high-speed cryptography. It has been used to write and verify many
cryptographic implementations, and in particular a highly optimized,
vectorized implementation of the post-quantum key encapsulation
mechanism ML-KEM
(
of Standards et al. 2024
)
, which was recently standardized by
the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)—a KEM is a
special form of public-key encryption that is used by two parties to
share a secret key. Secondly, the Jasmin compiler is formally
specified and verified in the
Rocq
prover. This
formalization provides an excellent starting point for our work.

Proving that a compiler preserves cryptographic security requires two
ingredients that have not been considered so far in the context of the
Jasmin compiler, namely:

•

Probabilistic Semantics.
Randomness is ubiquitous in
cryptography; therefore, one must consider randomized programs, and
equip them with a probabilistic semantics. Furthermore, many
cryptographic implementations, and in particular post-quantum
implementations, only achieve a probabilistic notion of termination,
known as almost sure termination—meaning that the probability that
execution diverges is zero. Therefore, one must extend the usual
compiler correctness statements to account for such probabilistic
behaviors.

•

Security Experiments.
Most notions of cryptographic
security are defined by security experiments, in which an adversary
interacts with a challenger with access to the cryptographic
system. Every security experiment also defines winning conditions,
which determine when the adversary successfully broke the
cryptographic system. Therefore, one must extend the usual compiler
correctness statements to reason about winning probabilities in an
interactive setting.

We extend the Jasmin framework to support both ingredients, formalize
them, and use the
Rocq
prover to show that the Jasmin compiler
preserves cryptographic security.
We elaborate on our main contributions below.

Contribution 1:
Relational Hoare Logic for Compiler Correctness

In
Section

4
, we define two Relational Hoare Logics (RHLs) for
Jasmin programs.
Our first logic is a core RHL akin to
(
Benton 2004
)
.
Our second logic elaborates on the first to be tailored for compiler
correctness proofs: for instance, we update the notion of validity and
provide specialized rules for common compiler patterns.
This second logic forms the basis of our
new proof of the Jasmin compiler. Both logics are proved sound
w.r.t. a denotational semantics of programs. Concretely, our
denotational semantics is based on the general framework of
interaction trees
(
Xia et al. 2020
)
. However,
many of the ideas of our tailored RHL are independent of interaction
trees and of Jasmin.

Contribution 2:
New Semantics and New Proofs for the Old Jasmin Compiler

We leverage the framework of interaction trees to define a new semantics
of Jasmin programs that accounts for infinite and probabilistic
executions. Then, in
Section

5
, we use our Relational Hoare
Logic to lift the compiler correctness theorem of Jasmin to this new
semantics. Critically, we
do not modify
the Jasmin compiler,
and we largely
reuse
existing correctness proofs.
Pleasingly, we employ the same generic RHL implementation to verify the
entire front-end (spanning four intermediate languages), and our proofs
involve no probabilistic reasoning (as interaction trees allow us to
treat random sampling abstractly).
A further benefit of our RHL is that, in spite of the more
complex semantics, the correctness proofs are significantly shorter.

Contribution 3:
Security Experiments and Preservation of Cryptographic Security

In
Section

6
, we formalize security experiments with
interaction trees, and we provide instantiations of security experiments
where the adversary interacts with either a Jasmin implementation or
its compilation. We
apply the compiler correctness theorem to prove that the adversary
cannot distinguish between a security experiment based on a source
Jasmin implementation and the same experiment based on the compiled
implementation. For concreteness, our theorem is stated for
KEMs. Interestingly, our theorem underpins all assumptions that
guarantee preservation of cryptographic security, including a new
assumption about the well-initialization of return values. While
retrospectively self-evident, this new assumption highlights the
benefits of formally verified end-to-end security theorems in a unified
framework.

Limitations

Our formalization does not yet cover the Jasmin compiler back-end,
which comprises five passes, and is left for future
work. Furthermore, preservation of cryptographic security is proven
assuming the same adversary model for source and target programs—in
contrast to robust compilation
(
Abate et al. 2020
)
,
which explicitly provisions for different adversary models between
source and targets. However, one can connect with more concrete
assembly-level adversarial models, as briefly discussed in the
conclusion and will be reported separately.

Artifact

We provide a modified version of the Jasmin compiler with the new
proofs for its front-end as an artifact in

https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17604303
.

2.
Interaction Trees

Interaction Trees
(
Xia et al. 2020
)
(ITrees)
provide a convenient framework to reason about interactive and
nonterminating computations. Informally, ITrees represent
computations as potentially infinite trees, where each non-leaf node
is either a silent step or a visible event. Interaction trees
are an attractive option for us because, on the one hand, unlike most
operational semantics, they offer an executable semantics (as a
functional definition) and compositional (we can reason inductively on
the language syntax rather than on the more complex semantic datatype).
On the other hand, unlike old-style denotational semantics, ITrees
support modularity (we can deal with different types of events
separately), and they allow for a comparatively simple style of
coinductive reasoning, supported by a rich equational theory and relying
on parameterized coinduction
(
Zakowski et al. 2020
)
.

2.1.
Background on Interaction Trees

Formally, ITrees are modeled in
Rocq
as a coinductive type
parameterized by a family of events

Interpretation

Interaction trees can be interpreted modularly with respect to the
disjoint union of event families (written

{{\mathsf{E}}_{1}\mathop{+^{\prime}}{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}

subevent relation (written
{{{\mathsf{E}}_{1}}\sqsubseteq{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}}

ITrees are iterative monads, and

{\mathsf{A}}

Mappings from ITrees into a monad

When the event handler itself is recursive, we can use the operator

{{\mathsf{interp\_mrec}}:\left({\forall{{\mathsf{A}}}.\,{{\mathsf{E}}^{\prime}~{\mathsf{A}}\to{\mathsf{itree}~{\left({\mathsf{E}}^{\prime}+^{\prime}{\mathsf{E}}\right)}~{{\mathsf{A}}}}}}\right)\to{\forall{{\mathsf{A}}}.\,{{\mathsf{itree}~{\left({\mathsf{E}}^{\prime}+^{\prime}{\mathsf{E}}\right)}~{{\mathsf{A}}}}\to{\mathsf{itree}~{{\mathsf{E}}}~{{\mathsf{A}}}}}}}

Since ITrees can be themselves interpretation targets, we can write
modular interpreters, each for a subfamily of events, and compose them
sequentially.

, triggers a
{\mathsf{Rnd}}(n)
event that expects

n
uniformly sampled bytes, and writes them to
{\ell}
. The
cases of sequencing and conditionals use the standard ITree bind
operator.
While loops use the
{\mathsf{iter}}
operator to iteratively execute the
body, potentially infinitely many times. Finally, function calls
evaluate their argument

{\mathsf{Call}}
event, write the

{\ell}
, and update the memory.
Each
{\mathsf{Call}}

The
interprocedural semantics
is obtained by interpreting

{\mathsf{Call}}
events, which we handle by executing the body of the callee.
We define the handler for
{\mathsf{Call}}
events as follows:

{{{H}^{\text{\tiny{$\hskip 0.73616pt\mathsf{c}$}}}}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({\mathsf{Call}}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({f},{{m}},{v}\right)\right)}\triangleq{{{\sigma}}\leftarrow{{\llparenthesis\hskip 1.00006pt{{f}_{\text{\tiny{{body}}}}}\hskip 1.00006pt\rrparenthesis^{\text{\tiny{$\mathsf{cer}$}}}_{{{\sigma}_{{m}}},{\{{{{f}_{\text{\tiny{{arg}}}}}\mapsto v}\}}}}}~;{{{\mathsf{Ret}}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({{{\sigma}_{{m}}},{{\sigma}_{{r}}}({{f}_{\text{\tiny{{res}}}}})}\right)}}}\text{,}

where
{\{{{{f}_{\text{\tiny{{arg}}}}}\mapsto v}\}}
is the variable map that contains

as the argument of
f

{{f}_{\text{\tiny{{body}}}}}
and
{{f}_{\text{\tiny{{res}}}}}

are the body and the results of
f

With this handler and the mutual recursion operator
{\mathsf{interp\_mrec}}
,
we can define the interprocedural semantics of a command
c

as follows:

{\llparenthesis\hskip 1.00006ptc\hskip 1.00006pt\rrparenthesis^{\text{\tiny{$\mathsf{er}$}}}_{{\sigma}}}:{\mathsf{itree}~{\left({\mathsf{Err}}\mathop{+^{\prime}}{\mathsf{Rnd}}\right)}~{{\mathsf{State}}}}\triangleq{{\mathsf{interp\_mrec}}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({{{H}^{\text{\tiny{$\hskip 0.73616pt\mathsf{c}$}}}}},{{\llparenthesis\hskip 1.00006ptc\hskip 1.00006pt\rrparenthesis^{\text{\tiny{$\mathsf{cer}$}}}_{{\sigma}}}}\right)}\text{,}

The

4.
Relational Hoare Logic for an Interaction Trees Semantics

In this section, we present a Relational Hoare Logic for ITree-based
program semantics. We first present a vanilla logic and then discuss
a specialized version for compiler correctness proofs, which we later
use to verify the Jasmin compiler.

4.3.
Specializing the Logic for Compiler Proofs

Verifying a realistic compiler with RHL can benefit significantly from
using a specialized notion of validity and specialized rules
that capture common compiler patterns.
This section presents extensions to RHL that proved particularly helpful
in proving the Jasmin compiler.
We distill four key ingredients of our specialized logic, which are not
specific to Jasmin or to its ITree semantics.

Equivalence up-to-Cutoff

Compiler correctness typically relates the semantics of a source program
and that of its compilation
as long as the source program is
safe
.
Assuming safety is often essential in compiler proofs, as it allows us
to disregard invalid behaviors that need not be preserved.
Additionally, safety also encodes important invariants of the compiler
passes (e.g., if the compiler introduces a memory access, that access
must be in-bounds) that subsequent passes rely upon.

We internalize this kind of “up to source safety” reasoning in our
logic by redefining the validity of RHL tuples with equivalence
up-to-cutoff
{\overset{\text{\tiny{$u$}}}{\approx}}
, instantiating the left cutoff to be

{\mathsf{Err}}
events and no cutoffs on the right-hand side.
Changing the relation in RHL tuples impacts the interpretation of the
judgments for commands, expressions, and left-values.
For instance, the
Assign
rule now states that if either

e_{1}
or
{\ell_{1}}
trigger an error, we can choose an arbitrary
postcondition—including a contradiction—and relate the assignments
vacuously.
All rules admit this line of reasoning.
The soundness of the proof system extends directly to this new setting.

Parameterization with Respect to Semantics

Compiler passes frequently have different input and output languages
(e.g., transforming a program with abstract arrays into one with a
concrete memory layout)—indeed, the Jasmin front-end contains four
such passes.
To verify these passes, our
Rocq
formalization of the semantics and
of RHL is substantially more general that what was presented in the
previous section,
being parametric on the semantics of either
side
.
This generalization allows us to verify the front-end of Jasmin with

a single implementation
of RHL, and is essential in the proof of
the Inlining pass, as we discuss in
Section

5.2
.
We omit this more general form for brevity and refer the reader to the

Rocq
development for details.

Specialized One-Sided Rules

Compiler passes often introduce or remove instructions from the program.
In such cases, the source and target programs are not in lock-step,
and cannot be related using two-sided rules.
One-sided rules allow us to relate such programs: for example, in a
compiler pass that removes dead code, we can relate an assignment to a
dead variable with the
skip
command using the rule

Assign
L
, since the postcondition of that pass requires that
the states coincide on live variables, and said assignment satisfies it
trivially.

Unfortunately, the one-sided rules in
Figure

4
are too generic and
become cumbersome to use directly for more complex transformations.
Thus, we introduce new one-sided rules tailored for common compiler
transformations. These rules pattern-match on the command on the
left-hand side (i.e., the source) and set the command on the
right-hand side (i.e., the target) to the transformation of the
former. The rules have non-relational proof obligations to prove
that the right-hand side correctly implements the source. Such
obligations enjoy good automation in the
Rocq
prover, thanks to our
defining a partial evaluator for commands that can reduce loop- and
event-free commands to their results; for instance, it establishes that

{{{\llparenthesis\hskip 1.00006pt{{x}~{\coloneq}~{0}}\hskip 1.00006pt\rrparenthesis^{\text{\tiny{$\mathsf{cer}$}}}_{{\sigma}}}}\mathrel{{\approx}}{{{\mathsf{Ret}}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({{{{\sigma}}\!\left[{x}\mapsto{0}\right]}}\right)}}}
.

The first four rules in
Figure

5
are specialized one-sided rules.
The
Assign
L
rule relates an assignment to an arbitrary
command.
This rule is well-suited for passes that expand an assignment into
several ones (e.g., mapping a complex load
{{x}~{\coloneq}~{{{a}\!\left[{y+z}\right]}}}
into two
instructions
{{{t}~{\coloneq}~{y+z}}\mathop{;}{{x}~{\coloneq}~{{{a}\!\left[{t}\right]}}}}
) since our
partial evaluator can readily reduce the assignments to their result.

The
Case
L
rule allows us to consume a “prologue” command on
the right-hand side before relating two conditionals.
The first premise ensures that the guards
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are
synchronized and that the prelude
c
is event-free, safe, and
terminating.
This pattern applies to passes that introduce a prologue before a
conditional to compute a complex guard; for instance, the Instruction
Selection pass in Jasmin maps conditionals to a comparison and the
appropriate combination of flags.
For instance, it transforms

{{\text{{if}}}~({x\leq y})~\{{\dots}\}~\{{\dots}\}}

into

{{{o,s,c,z}~{\coloneq}~{\mathtt{\#CMP}(x,y)}}\mathop{;}{{\text{{if}}}~({c\lor z})~\{{\dots}\}~\{{\dots}\}}}

since the disjunction of the carry and zero flags represent

Lastly, the

Leveraging Static Analyses in RHL

Many compiler passes leverage the results of program analyses to modify
programs in a structure-preserving way. At an abstract level, such
passes replace expressions (resp. left-values) with new expressions
(resp. left-values) that are equivalent given the results of the
analysis.
Consequently, we have developed a general approach to validate such
passes using simplified RHL rules.

Our approach entails an abstract set
{\mathcal{A}}

interpretation

and
{{{\mathsf{chk}}_{{\ell}}}:{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathsf{LVal}}\times{\mathsf{LVal}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathsf{Prop}}}

information about the two program executions, and the interpretation
maps elements of the abstract domain to a logical representation. The
checkers, on the other hand, verify whether an abstract value
validates a desired relation
{\mathcal{S}}
(for example, that two given
expressions evaluate to the same value) and establish an updated
abstract value. We identify three properties needed to incorporate
checkers in the logic, namely:

(

The first property ensures that no information is lost after analyzing

Using instances of a general syntax and semantics as internal languages

(1)

alpha-equivalent.
The checker for this pass is implemented and verified in

(2)

The Constant Propagation pass propagates the values of constants,

(3)

The Inlining pass replaces function calls to functions annotated

Compiler Correctness

The proof of each pass in the Jasmin front-end establishes an RHL
program tuple of the form

{{=,=}~{{\models}~{{P}\mathrel{\sim}{Q}\mathrel{:}{{\Phi}_{{\preccurlyeq}}}\mathrel{\Rightarrow}{{\Psi}_{{\preccurlyeq}}}}}}
, where
P
is
the input program and
Q
its compilation, the handler contract is
equality, and the relational specification

{\Phi_{{\preccurlyeq}}}
and
{\Psi_{{\preccurlyeq}}}
requires that each function
and its compilation preserve the compiler simulation
{\preccurlyeq}
.

The simulation relation
{\preccurlyeq}
involves a number of well-formedness
conditions (e.g., that array values in the source correspond to pointers
in the target) and relates values with the usual
more defined
than
relation.
Specifically, a target value is more defined than a source value if
either they are equal or the latter is undefined.
In the case of arrays, the simulation requires that a pointer in the
target points to a region that is more defined than the corresponding
array in the source.
In the remainder of the section, we will omit this relation for
simplicity and say that target and source values are equal.

The correctness of the Jasmin compiler front-end is states as
follows.
Note that the judgment is interpreted with equivalence up to source
safety
{\overset{\text{\tiny{$u$}}}{\approx}}
, which means that the simulation relation holds
until source errors occur.

Theorem 1 (Compiler Correctness).

Given a source program
P
and its compilation
Q
, we have

{{=,=}~{{\models}~{{P}\mathrel{\sim}{Q}\mathrel{:}{{\Phi}_{{\preccurlyeq}}}\mathrel{\Rightarrow}{{\Psi}_{{\preccurlyeq}}}}}}

under the conditions mentioned below.

Proof.

As usual, the proof repeatedly applies the

chain the proofs of each pass.
It is formalized as
it_compiler_front_endP
in

proofs/compiler/it_compiler_proof.v
.
∎

The theorem relies on several assumptions about the initial states
(e.g., that there is enough stack space in the target state) and about
the arguments (e.g., that writable pointers are disjoint).
These assumptions are similar to those in

Theorem

Register Renaming and Static Analyses

The checker in the Register Renaming pass checks that two programs are
alpha-equivalent.
It analyzes the code to compute a
renaming
, a map from source
variables to target registers, at each program point.
For example, the instruction
{{x}~{\coloneq}~{y+3}}
is alpha-equivalent to

{{{\mathsf{rdi}}}~{\coloneq}~{{\mathsf{rdi}}+3}}
if the initial renaming (before the
assignment) is
{\{{y\mapsto{\mathsf{rdi}}}\}}
and the resulting one (after the
assignment) is
{\{{x\mapsto{\mathsf{rdi}},y\mapsto\bot}\}}
.

The correctness of the checker states that the source and target states
are equal up to renaming of register variables.
The simulation relation is parameterized by a renaming

{{\alpha}:{\mathsf{Var}}\rightharpoonup{\mathsf{Var}}}
, which is a
partial function from variables to variables.
The simulation relation for this pass is as follows:

{{{\sigma}}\mathrel{{\preccurlyeq_{{\alpha}}}}{{\sigma}^{\prime}}}\triangleq{{\sigma}_{{m}}}={{\sigma}^{\prime}_{{m}}}\land{\forall{x\in\dom{{\alpha}}}.\,{{\sigma}(x)={\sigma}^{\prime}({\alpha}(x))}}\text{,}

which means that memories should be identical, and if the renaming is
defined on
x

Register Renaming is a natural fit for the simplified static analysis
rules, since the pass itself is a checker; nevertheless,

For example, checking two assignments
{{{\ell}_{1}}~{\coloneq}~{e_{1}}}
and

{{{\ell}_{2}}~{\coloneq}~{e_{2}}}

{\alpha}

such that

is an renaming of
e_{1}

left-values.
Thus, the premises of the

In this setting, the

and

{\alpha}

Most passes of the compiler benefit from the simplified static analysis
rules.

Constant Branch Elimination and One-Sided Rules

This transformation optimizes control flow constructs whose guards are
constant—i.e., it removes constant branches. For example, the
command
{{\text{{if}}}~({x=x})~\{{c}\}~\{{c^{\prime}}\}}

c

skip

We prove it correct by showing that the source and target states are

We use specialized one-sided rules deal with constant branches, as this

All passes in Jasmin that modify the program structure use one-sided

This pass replaces function calls with the body of the callee if the
latter is annotated as
#inline

The proof for the Inlining pass is the most complex in the compiler
after our changes, since a critical step in the argument requires a
generic result on ITrees that is not provable in RHL directly.
Proving the correctness of this pass is challenging because it requires

Our solution builds on a new handler for

Using this new semantics, we proceed in two steps: first, we show
that the semantics of the source program is equivalent to its one-step
inlining semantics; and secondly, we use RHL to prove that the one-step
inlining semantics of the source program is equivalent to the semantics
of the target program.

The new handler and semantics are as follows:

This handler interprets a call to an

{{H}^{\text{\tiny{$\hskip 0.73616pt\mathsf{c}$}}}}

other calls by re-triggering the original event.
Based on this handler, the one-step inlining semantics interprets calls
to

instead of
{\mathsf{Call}}

Note that the bodies of

{\mathsf{Call}}

Trade-Offs

We identify two potential improvements in our formalization of RHL,
both of which relate to the checker abstractions.
These improvements have limited use in the current Jasmin
infrastructure and, thus, have not been implemented. However, we
believe that they present no fundamental difficulty and
could be of interest outside Jasmin.

First, the Stack Allocation pass would benefit for a more general type
for
{\mathcal{S}}
that involves states as well as values.
This is because it places local variables—including arrays—in the
stack frame of their function.
For example, it transforms

{{{{{a}\!\left[{1}\right]}}~{\coloneq}~{1}}\mathop{;}{{x}~{\coloneq}~{{{a}\!\left[{1}\right]}}}}
into

{{{{\left[{{\mathsf{rsp}}+8}\right]}}~{\coloneq}~{1}}\mathop{;}{{x}~{\coloneq}~{{\left[{{\mathsf{rsp}}+8}\right]}}}}
, where the array
a
now resides in memory, starting at address

{\mathsf{rsp}}
.
The simulation relation is more subtle than for previous passes, as we
need to relate a source-level expression (e.g.,
{{a}\!\left[{1}\right]}
) with a
target-level machine word (e.g.,
{{\mathsf{rsp}}{}+8}
) if the latter
points to a memory region containing the value of the former.
Thus, this pass would benefit from a more general type for
{\mathcal{S}}
that
takes the state into account, rather than just the value of the
expressions—naturally, we can define
{\mathcal{S}}
relative to a memory and
fix the memory outside the RHL tuple, but this quickly becomes tedious.

Secondly, the SLH Instruction Selection pass would benefit from improved
support of flow-sensitive analyses.
This pass replaces selective speculative load hardening (SLH) operators
with architecture-specific instructions.
Selective speculative load hardening is a mitigation for Spectre attacks
that has been recently adopted in Jasmin
(
Shivakumar et al. 2023
;
Arranz-Olmos et al. 2025b
)

to protect against
Spectre-v1
(
Kocher et al. 2020
)
and

5.3.
Comparison with Previous Work

Before our changes, the meaning of Jasmin programs was given by a
big-step operational semantics of the form
{\langle{c},{{\sigma}}\rangle\Downarrow{{\sigma}^{\prime}}}
, stating
that running a command
c
on a state
{\sigma}
terminates without
errors and reaches a state
{\sigma}^{\prime}
.
The semantics of random sampling commands was given by an infinite
random tape.
Compiler correctness was stated as a forward simulation—that was later
flipped backward—between a source command
c
and its
compilation
{\bar{c}}
, stating that for every pair of source states

{\sigma}
and
{\sigma^{\prime}}
and target state
{\tau}

{\langle{c},{{\sigma}}\rangle\Downarrow{{\sigma}^{\prime}}}\land{{{\sigma}}\mathrel{{\preccurlyeq}}{{\tau}}}\implies\exists{\tau}^{\prime}.\,{\langle{{\bar{c}}},{{\tau}}\rangle\Downarrow{{\tau}^{\prime}}}\land{{{\sigma}^{\prime}}\mathrel{{\preccurlyeq}}{{\tau}^{\prime}}}\text{,}

where

is
the simulation relation presented in
Section

5.2

with an additional clause ensuring equal random tapes.

Consequently, the compiler correctness statement above ensured that safe
and terminating Jasmin programs were compiled to safe and terminating
programs with equal results.
However, it did not give any guarantee on executions that diverge or
on the probabilistic behavior (i.e., as distributions) of programs.

6.
Preservation of Cryptographic Security

This section formalizes and proves preservation of cryptographic
security. For concreteness, we focus on a single class of
primitives,
key encapsulation mechanisms
(KEMs), and a single
security property,
indistinguishability under chosen-ciphertext
attacks
(IND-CCA). We show that the Jasmin compiler preserves
IND-CCA for KEMs.

Preservation of cryptographic security is especially interesting for
Jasmin, as a number of cryptographic primitives have been implemented
in Jasmin and their security formally verified in EasyCrypt.
At the same time, cryptographic security is particularly challenging to
formalize and reason about, as it requires an interactive,
probabilistic, almost surely terminating setting.

6.1.
Cryptographic Background

A KEM is a public-key cryptographic primitive which allows two parties
to share a secret key. Formally,
KEMs are specified by three probabilistic algorithms:

{{\mathrm{GenKey}}:()\to{\mathcal{PK}}\times{\mathcal{SK}}}
to generate a
public and a secret key,
{{\mathrm{Encap}}:{\mathcal{PK}}\to{\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{C}}}
to generate a message and its encapsulation given
a public key, and

{{\mathrm{Decap}}:{\mathcal{SK}}\times{\mathcal{C}}\to{\mathcal{M}}}
to
decapsulate a message given a secret key, where
{\mathcal{PK}}
,
{\mathcal{SK}}
,

{\mathcal{C}}
, and
{\mathcal{M}}
denote the sets of public keys, secret
keys, encapsulations, and messages, respectively. Note that, in our
setting, decapsulation never fails; this corresponds to the case of KEMs
with implicit rejection, of which ML-KEM is an instance. Key
encapsulation mechanisms are required to be correct, in the sense that
decapsulating a ciphertext recovers the shared key (message) with high
probability.

The security of KEMs, IND-CCA, is modeled by a probabilistic experiment
shown in

access to

{{\mathrm{Decap}}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({{\mathit{sk}}},{\cdot}\right)}

6.2.
Formalizing IND-CCA with Interaction Trees

There are three challenges in formalizing IND-CCA security:
the oracle queries are interactive, the algorithms are probabilistic,
and they are not certainly terminating.
All three are readily addressed using ITrees, as discussed in the
previous sections.
Oracle queries are represented as
{\mathsf{Orac}}
events, which are similar to

{\mathsf{Call}}
events but only for the three KEM algorithm.
Random sampling is represented as
{\mathsf{Rnd}}
events, and nontermination
follows from the coinductive nature of ITrees.

We model the three components of the challenger as functions that return
in the
{\mathsf{itree}~{{\mathsf{Rnd}}}~{}}
monad.
This choice allows us to derive a challenger from a Jasmin program
that implements a KEM, each component corresponding to the semantics of
the respective function in the program, as we discuss in the next
section.

Similarly, the components of an adversary return in the

{\mathsf{itree}~{{\left({\mathsf{Orac}}\mathop{+^{\prime}}{\mathsf{Rnd}}\right)}}~{}}
monad, as follows:

{\mathscr{A}_{1}}:{\mathcal{PK}}\to{\mathsf{itree}~{{\left({\mathsf{Orac}}\mathop{+^{\prime}}{\mathsf{Rnd}}\right)}}~{{\mathcal{S}}}}\text{,}\hskip 40.00006pt{\mathscr{A}_{2}}:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathsf{itree}~{{\left({\mathsf{Orac}}\mathop{+^{\prime}}{\mathsf{Rnd}}\right)}}~{{\{{0,1}\}}}}\text{.}

We model the internal state of the adversary explicitly as
{\mathcal{S}}
.

Finally, the IND-CCA experiment combines these parts in a tree of
type
{\mathsf{itree}~{{\left({\mathsf{Orac}}\mathop{+^{\prime}}{\mathsf{Rnd}}\right)}}~{{\{{0,1}\}}}}
using the monadic structure of ITrees.
Since oracle queries from the adversary are
{\mathsf{Orac}}
events, we deal
with them defining a handler that uses
{\mathrm{Decap}}
.

To define the advantage of an adversary we need to interpret ITrees as
distributions: given a experiment
{E:{\mathsf{itree}~{{\mathsf{Rnd}}}~{{\{{0,1}\}}}}}
,
we need to compute the probability that the output of the experiment is
one.
We do so generically, defining a probabilistic semantics
{\left\llbracket\cdot\right\rrbracket}
for
ITrees that will allow us to define the adversary’s advantage as

{{\left|{{\mathrm{Pr}\!\left[{{\left\llbracket E\right\rrbracket}=1}\right]}-\frac{1}{2}}\right|}}
.

Figure

7(b)
presents the probabilistic interpretation of ITrees.
Given a tree
{t:{\mathsf{itree}~{{\mathsf{Rnd}}}~{{\mathsf{R}}}}}
, we first
define its
n
-step approximation semantics

{{\left\llbracket t\right\rrbracket^{{n}}}:{{\mathcal{D}}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({{\mathsf{R}}}\right)}}
, and then take the limit as

n
tends to infinity (we write
{{\mathcal{D}}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({{\mathsf{R}}}\right)}
for distributions
over
{\mathsf{R}}
).
All zero-step approximations are the null distribution

{\mathbb{0}}
, which assigns zero probability to all results. All positive
approximations of
{{\mathsf{Ret}}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({r}\right)}
are the unit distribution on the
result
r
, denoted
{\mathds{1}_{r}}
, which assigns probability one
to
r
. The
(n+1)
-step approximation of
{{\mathsf{Tau}}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({t}\right)}
is the
n
-step
approximation of
t
—note that this means that, as expected,
diverging executions have probability zero. Finally, the
(n+1)
-step
approximation of
{{\mathsf{Vis}}\mathchoice{\!}{\!}{}{}\left({{\mathsf{Rnd}}(v)},{k}\right)}
accumulates the probabilities
over all possible random samples

and divides by the number of such
possible samples—there are
2^{8v}
vectors of
v

Finally, the probabilistic interpretation of

is denoted
{\left\llbracket t\right\rrbracket}

and defined as the limit over

{\left\llbracket t\right\rrbracket^{{n}}}

{\left\llbracket t\right\rrbracket^{{n}}}

This semantics respects weak bisimilarity in the following sense.

Theorem 1.

Weakly bisimilar ITrees
{{t}\mathrel{{\approx}}{t^{\prime}}}
give the same distribution,
i.e.,
{{\left\llbracket t\right\rrbracket}={\left\llbracket t^{\prime}\right\rrbracket}}
.

This theorem can be extended to allow trees that are weakly bisimilar
w.r.t. a relation
{\phi}
, as presented in

Appendix

dinterp_eutt
in
proofs/compiler/distr.v

Nevertheless, the simplified version stated above suffices to prove the
preservation of IND-CCA.

7.
Related Work

We structure this section along the different lines of work related to
our discussion.

Program Semantics

There is a long line of work that formalizes semantics for
nonterminating and probabilistic computations in proof assistants.
Coinductive program semantics and definitions of recursive functions are
mechanized in
(
Leroy 2006a
;
Nakata and Uustalu 2009
;
McBride 2015
)
.
Semantics of probabilistic programs
are mechanized in
(
Hurd et al. 2004
)
(discrete
setting),
(
Affeldt et al. 2023
;
Hirata et al. 2023
)

(continuous setting), and
(
Gregersen et al. 2024
)

(tape-based). We refer
the reader to
(
Barthe et al. 2020b
)
for further information
about (mechanized and non-mechanized) approaches to semantics of
probabilistic programs.

Verified Compilers

There is a long history of using proof assistants for proving
functional correctness and for security properties of compilers;
see
(
Blanchette and editors
)
.
Other works, e.g.,
(
Ebresafe et al. 2025
)
, extend
proofs assistants to ease compiler verification tasks.
Here, we focus on key related works.

CompCert
(
Leroy 2006b
)
is a compiler for C, written and
proven correct
Rocq
. Early
versions of CompCert used a big-step semantics; later
versions are based on a small-step coinductive semantics. All proofs
are based on simulation diagrams. Unlike CompCert, we benefit from
ITrees and Relational Hoare Logic in our switch from big-step
semantics to coinductive denotational semantics.

CakeML
(
Kumar et al. 2014
)
is a compiler for Standard ML,
written and verified in HOL. The semantics is defined in a functional
big-step style with clocks to deal with divergence, and proofs are based
on simulation diagrams and equational reasoning.
Pancake
(
Zhao et al. 2025
)
is an offspring of CakeML
that uses an ITree semantics, proven equivalent to the
functional big-step one, to reason about the
interactions between programs and the external world.

Vellvm
(
Zhao et al. 2012
)
is a compiler for the LLVM
framework written and verified in
Rocq
.
Vellvm uses the Single Static Assignment (SSA) representation
and leverages SSA-specific proof techniques for proving correctness of
program transformations. It uses ITrees to formalize LLVM-IR as
a representation
(
Zakowski et al. 2021
)
, to verify
compiler optimizations and a Helix compiler to LLVM, and more
recently relying on a two-phase memory
model
(
Beck et al. 2024
)
.
Unlike Jasmin, the semantics of LLVM is nondeterministic.
Equivalence up-to-cutoff
{\overset{\text{\tiny{$u$}}}{\approx}}
generalizes the specific
extension of
{\overset{\text{\tiny{$e$}}}{\approx}}
used in
(
Beck et al. 2024
)

to deal with out-of-memory errors in the target, which intuitively
correspond to right cutoffs.

ProbCompCert
(
Tassarotti and Tristan 2023
)
is an extension
of CompCert for a fragment of the Stan language, supporting
sampling from continuous distributions but no almost-sure termination.
Zar
(
Bagnall et al. 2023
)
is a formally verified
compiler from discrete probability programs to ITree samplers based on
weakest preexpectations.

While the aforementioned works focus on the classic compiler correctness
in terms of execution traces, stronger
notions have also been considered. One such notion is
robust compilation
(
Abate et al. 2020
;
Patrignani et al. 2024
)
, which asks
that program behaviors are preserved under adversarial contexts. A main
difference between robust compilation and our work is that it allows
for source and target contexts to differ, so that the latter are able
to capture low-level attacks. We plan to address low-level attacks in
a separate (but compatible) effort, leveraging recent developments in
hardware-software contracts
(
Guarnieri et al. 2021
)
. Yet a
different line of work studies preservation of information flow
properties: Silver et al.
(
Silver et al. 2023a
)

use the framework of ITrees to prove preservation of
noninterference for an
Imp
-to-
Asm
compiler. Barthe
et al.
(
Barthe et al. 2018
;
Barthe et al. 2020a
;
Barthe et al. 2021
)

prove preservation of constant-time for a core imperative language,
CompCert, and an older version of the Jasmin compiler—the proof
is not maintained but it could be of interest to restore it in the
future.
Constant-time has also been considered in the context of
nondeterminism
(
Conoly et al. 2025
)
and
speculative side-channels,
e.g.,
(
Fabian et al. 2025
;
Arranz-Olmos et al. 2025a
)
.

(Relational) Programs Logics

Relational program logics provide a general framework for reasoning
about 2-properties, i.e., properties of two programs. Relational
Hoare Logic
(
Benton 2004
)
is a program logic
for reasoning about executions of two programs or two executions of
the same program. Relational Separation Logic
(RSL)
(
Yang 2007
)
is a program logic for reasoning
about execution of two heap-manipulating programs; it was developed
concurrently with RHL. Iris
(
Jung et al. 2015
)
is a
mechanized separation logic formalized in the
Rocq
prover,
with several extensions that support relational reasoning.
One of the primary applications of relational program logics is
compiler correctness; for instance, Iris has been used to prove
correctness of
program transformations
(
Gäher et al. 2022
)
.
Recently, RSL has been used to prove correctness of (an idealization
of) the tail-modulo constructor optimization used by the OCaml
compiler
(
Allain et al. 2025
)
.

Several works specifically develop program logics and related
formalisms on top of ITrees. Vistrup et al.
(
Vistrup et al. 2025
)
develop a
modular method for defining program logics within the Iris framework,
relying on a generic definition of weakest precondition for ITrees,
alongside effect libraries that can be combined together to deal with
complex languages. Silver and Zdancewic
(
Silver and Zdancewic 2021
)
formalize the
Dijkstra monad for weakest precondition reasoning with ITrees.
Silver et al.

Cryptographic Security

There is a large body of work that formalizes cryptographic security
in proof assistants
(
Barbosa et al. 2021
)
. In
particular, the EasyCrypt proof
assistant
(
Barthe et al. 2011
;
Barthe et al. 2013
)

has been used to mechanize the security proof of many constructions,
including MLKEM
(
Almeida et al. 2024
)
.
EasyCrypt is based on pRHL (probabilistic Relational Hoare
Logic)
(
Barthe et al. 2009
)
. Other mechanized
frameworks for cryptographic security include
FCF
(
Petcher and Morrisett 2015
)
,
CryptHOL
(
Basin et al. 2020
)
,
SSProve
(
Haselwarter et al. 2023
)
, and
IDPL
(
Gancher et al. 2023
)
. Some works are more
specifically focused on formalizing oracle systems. For
instance,
(
Tuma and Hopper 2024
)
presents a formalization of
oracle systems in Lean. Their formalization has been further extended
in
(
Dziembowski et al. 2025
)
to reason about

Compiler Preservation of Cryptographic Security

Almeida et al.
(
Almeida et al. 2016
, Theorem 2)

present a general method for proving preservation of cryptographic
security against adversaries with acccess to side-channel
leakage. However, their proof is carried on pen-and-paper, at a high
level of abstraction, and elides many of the important details
addressed by our work.
We discuss side-channels in the context of our work in the conclusion.

8.
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper proves that the Jasmin compiler front-end preserves
IND-CCA security of KEMs. Our
Rocq
proof lays the foundations to be
“combined” with the existing proofs of functional correctness and
cryptographic security of the optimized Jasmin implementation of
the post-quantum standard ML-KEM to conclude that the compiled code is
also cryptographically secure. Note that the
combination is necessarily informal (hence the quotes), as both proofs
of functional correctness
(
Almeida et al. 2023
)

and security
(
Almeida et al. 2024
)
are
carried using the EasyCrypt proof
assistant
(
Barthe et al. 2011
;
Barthe et al. 2013
)
.

Our work assumes that the adversary models for source and target
levels are equivalent. It would be possible to accomodate stronger
target-level adversaries using techniques from hardware-software
contracts
(
Guarnieri et al. 2021
)
, in particular to
consider adversaries with access to side-channel leakage. On the
programming language side, it will require proving that target
programs are protected against side-channels. This can be done in two
different ways: either by proving that the compiler also preserves
protections againt side-channels or by proving directly that the
assembly program is protected against side-channels, e.g., using a
type system in the spirit of
(
Song et al. 2025
)
. In the first case, it
would be interesting to explore the potential benefits of ITrees for
secure compilation.

Appendix B
Probabilistic Liftings

Lifting a relation between two sets
X
and
Y
to one between
distributions over
X
and
Y
is necessary to reason about the
probabilistic semantics of programs.
In this section, we review the standard approach to achieve this
lifting.

A crucial advantage of building the semantics of our language with
ITrees—and using equivalence up-to-tau as our compiler
correctness notion—is that
Theorem

1
theorem is independent
of the language and compiler, and that the compiler correctness theorem

Theorem

1
is independent of the probabilistic
semantics.
Specifically, we can elide all probability details when proving the
compiler correct, and, afterward and separately, use

Theorem

1
to achieve strong guarantees about the
probabilistic behavior of programs.

Unfortunately, the statement of
Theorem

1
is too restrictive
for most compilers, as the compiler correctness result is expressed
relative to a simulation relation
{\preccurlyeq}
.
We need to refine the theorem to account for such a relation, which
means that we must relate the probability of returning a source state
with that of returning a target state related by the simulation.
Moreover, since simulation relations are seldom bijective, we need to
further relax this statement to consider the probability of sets of
states, where one is the image of the other under the simulation
relation.
Let us now reformulate the theorem with these distinctions in mind.
Recall that the probability under a distribution
\mu
of a
subset
X
is
{{\mathrm{Pr}_{\mu}\!\left[{X}\right]}\triangleq\sum_{x\in X}\mu(x)}
.
In our case, we are concerned with subsets of source and target states,
for the source and target semantics, respectively.

Theorem 1.

Given two ITrees that are in simulation up-to-tau

{{t}\mathrel{{\approx}_{{\preccurlyeq}}}{t^{\prime}}}
, and a subset
S
of source states and
a subset
T
of target states such that

\displaystyle\forall{\sigma}{\tau}.\,{{{\sigma}}\mathrel{{\preccurlyeq}}{{\tau}}}\implies\left({\sigma}\in S\iff{\tau}\in T\right)

then

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Pr}_{{\left\llbracket t\right\rrbracket}}\!\left[{S}\right]}={\mathrm{Pr}_{{\left\llbracket t^{\prime}\right\rrbracket}}\!\left[{T}\right]}\text{.}
