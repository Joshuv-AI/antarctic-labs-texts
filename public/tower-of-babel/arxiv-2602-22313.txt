Quantum simulation of massive Thirring and Gross–Neveu models for arbitrary number of flavors
Bojko N. Bakalov, João C. Getelina, Raghav G. Jha, Alexander F. Kemper, Yuan Liu

Abstract
The study of fermionic quantum field theories is an important problem for realizing the standard model of particle physics on a quantum computer. As a step towards this goal, we consider the massive Thirring and Gross–Neveu models with arbitrary number of fermion flavors, N f N_{f} , discretized on a spatial one-dimensional lattice of size L L in the Hamiltonian formulation. We compute the gate complexity using the higher-order product formula and using block-encoding/qubitization and quantum singular value transformations in the limit of large N f N_{f} and L L . We also prepare the ground states of both models with excellent fidelity for system sizes up to 20 qubits with N f = 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 N_{f}=1,2,3,4 using the adaptive-variational quantum imaginary time algorithm. In addition, we also classify the dynamical Lie algebras of these relativistic fermionic models and show that they belong to the same isomorphism class. Our work is a concrete step towards the quantum simulation of real-time dynamics of large N f N_{f} fermionic quantum field theories models relevant for chiral symmetry breaking, understanding dimensional transmutation, and exploring the conformal window of field theories on near-term and early fault-tolerant quantum computers.

Introduction

The theory of strong interactions, known as quantum chromodynamics (QCD), has achieved great success in explaining the structure of matter in the last six decades. A proper understanding of QCD in the non-perturbative regime is essential for wide ranges of phenomena that occur in nature, such as nuclear structure, confinement of quarks, and the composition and microscopic behavior of neutron stars. In particular, many of the most pressing questions involve real-time dynamics and matter at finite baryon density.
Lattice methods are powerful tools for studying non-perturbative physics, but are restricted to Euclidean (imaginary) time and real lattice actions. In lower dimensions, four-fermion interaction models have been studied using classical methods, including tensor networks
Pannullo:2019bfn
;
Banuls:2019hzc
;
Roose:2020znu
;

One reason why the real-time dynamics of a lattice Hamiltonian with a large number of flavors

can be important is for understanding chiral symmetry breaking (CSB)

;
Wellegehausen:2017goy
, which is the
mechanism that explains how quarks (e.g., protons and neutrons) get almost all of their mass. Depending on whether one is in the chirally broken or symmetric phase, the time-evolved vector current after a global quench for different numbers of fermion flavors will behave differently, allowing, in principle, the critical flavor number to be computed

For a wide range of models relevant to the theory of strong interactions and other quantum field theories, the main hurdle is the lack of a suitable method for studying real-time dynamics on a discretized space-time lattice. One approach is to go beyond the paradigm of classical computing and instead use quantum computing to attack this problem. Quantum computers will likely offer the possibility to study real-time dynamics of quantum field theories in higher dimensions, and thus improve our understanding of fundamental interactions, including nuclear processes in QCD. However, this problem is still beyond the reach of current capabilities and requires several more orders of magnitude of resources than currently available

Contrary to the Lagrangian approach to QCD, the Hamiltonian approach requires more effort, because one has to impose the gauge constraints and locality explicitly by building the physical Hilbert space and operators that act on it, while this is naturally embedded in the path integral formulation.

Four-fermion models and qubit formulation

The Lagrangian for single flavor massive Thirring model is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Thirring}}=i\overline{\psi}(\not{\partial}-m)\psi-g(\overline{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\psi)(\overline{\psi}\gamma_{\mu}\psi),

(1)

and for single flavor massive Gross–Neveu by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GN}}=i\overline{\psi}(\not{\partial}-m)\psi+g(\overline{\psi}\psi)^{2},

(2)

where we denote
\not{\partial}=\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}
, according to the standard Feynman’s slash notation, and
\overline{\psi}=\psi^{\dagger}\gamma^{0}
is the Dirac adjoint of two-component spinor
\psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{0}\\
\psi_{1}\end{pmatrix}
. For the gamma matrices
3
3

3

We use the mostly minus/West-coast metric i.e., metric signature
(+-)
where
\gamma^{0}
is Hermitian and
\gamma^{1}
is anti-Hermitian.
, we choose the standard representation (Dirac representation) where
\gamma^{0}=\sigma_{z}=Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}
and
\gamma^{1}=i\sigma_{y}=iY=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}
.
In this paper, our focus is to initiate a quantum computing study of the massive Thirring/GN model with
N_{f}
flavors, where the interaction term is
(\overline{\psi}_{\alpha}\gamma^{\mu}\psi_{\alpha})^{2}
for the Thirring model, and
(\overline{\psi}_{\alpha}\psi_{\alpha})^{2}
for the Gross–Neveu model. The sum over flavors
\alpha
is always implied if not explicitly written, i.e.,
\overline{\psi}_{\alpha}\gamma^{\mu}\psi_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{f}}\overline{\psi}_{\alpha}\gamma^{\mu}\psi_{\alpha}
. We choose the same mass for all flavors, i.e.,
m_{\alpha}=m
.

Starting with the generalization
of the Lagrangian given by Eq. (
1
) to the multi-flavor case, we can write down the Hamiltonian of the massive Thirring model for
N_{f}
flavors with open boundary conditions as:

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{Thirring}}=

\displaystyle-i\sum_{n=0}^{L-2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{f}}\left(\overline{\psi}_{n,\alpha}\gamma^{1}\partial_{1}\psi_{n+1,\alpha}\right)+m\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{f}}\overline{\psi}_{n,\alpha}\psi_{n,\alpha}

\displaystyle+g\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{f}}(\overline{\psi}_{n,\alpha}\gamma^{\mu}\psi_{n,\alpha})\cdot(\overline{\psi}_{n,\alpha}\gamma_{\mu}\psi_{n,\alpha}).

(3)

In order to consider the lattice Hamiltonian on discretized spatial lattice, we rewrite the two-component fermion field
\psi(x)
at site
x
as:

\psi(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{0}\\
\psi_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}\\
c_{1}\end{pmatrix},

(4)

with the lattice spacing being set to
a=1
. For the kinetic term, we need the derivative on the spatial lattice
\partial_{1}
for which we use the standard (symmetric) finite difference
Susskind:1976jm
:

\partial_{1}\psi_{n}=\frac{1}{2a}(\psi_{n+1}-\psi_{n-1}).

(5)

Given a four-fermion Hamiltonian defined on
L
sites, one requires
2N_{f}
qubits per site, giving a total of
n=2N_{f}L
qubits. The Hamiltonian is a sum of three terms: the kinetic term
H_{k}
, the mass term
H_{m}
, and the four-Fermi term,
H_{\text{FF}}
. Only the four-Fermi term is different between the two models. The kinetic term is:

\displaystyle H_{k}

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{n=0}^{L-2}\sum_{f=0}^{N_{f}-1}\overline{\psi}_{n,f}\,\gamma^{1}\partial_{1}\,\psi_{n,f},

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{L-2}\sum_{f=0}^{N_{f}-1}\Big[\begin{pmatrix}c_{n,f,0}^{\dagger}&c_{n,f,1}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}}_{\gamma^{0}\gamma^{1}=X}\begin{pmatrix}c_{n+1,f,0}\\
c_{n+1,f,1}\end{pmatrix}\,-\begin{pmatrix}c_{n+1,f,0}^{\dagger}&c_{n+1,f,1}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\,X\,\begin{pmatrix}c_{n,f,0}\\
c_{n,f,1}\end{pmatrix}\Big],

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{L-2}\sum_{f=0}^{N_{f}-1}\Big[c_{n,f,0}^{\dagger}c_{n+1,f,1}+c_{n,f,1}^{\dagger}c_{n+1,f,0}-c_{n+1,f,0}^{\dagger}c_{n,f,1}-c_{n+1,f,1}^{\dagger}c_{n,f,0}\Big].

(6)

We now consider the mass term which, like the kinetic term, is the same for both models and given by:

H_{m}=m\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}\sum_{f=0}^{N_{f}-1}\left[\left(c_{n,f,0}^{\dagger}c_{n,f,0}-c_{n,f,1}^{\dagger}c_{n,f,1}\right)\right].

(7)

The two models differ in the interaction term. For the Thirring model, we have:

\displaystyle H_{\text{FF, Thirring}}

\displaystyle=g\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}\Bigg[\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{f}}\psi^{\dagger}_{n,\alpha}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{\mu}\psi_{n,\alpha}\Bigg]\cdot\Bigg[\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{f}}\psi^{\dagger}_{n,\alpha}\gamma^{0}\gamma_{\mu}\psi_{n,\alpha}\Bigg],

\displaystyle=g\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}\Bigg(\Bigg[\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{f}}\psi^{\dagger}_{n,\alpha}\,\mathbb{I}\,\psi_{n,\alpha}\Bigg]^{2}-\Bigg[\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{f}}\psi^{\dagger}_{n,\alpha}\,X\,\psi_{n,\alpha}\Bigg]^{2}\Bigg),

\displaystyle=g\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}\Bigg(\Bigg[\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{f}}\Big(c^{\dagger}_{n,\alpha,0}c_{n,\alpha,0}+c^{\dagger}_{n,\alpha,1}c_{n,\alpha,1}\Big)\Bigg]^{2}-\Bigg[\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{f}}\Big(c^{\dagger}_{n,\alpha,0}c_{n,\alpha,1}c^{\dagger}_{n,\alpha,1}c_{n,\alpha,0}\Big)\Bigg]^{2}\Bigg),

(8)

while for the Gross–Neveu (GN) model it is:

\displaystyle H_{\text{FF, Gross--Neveu}}

\displaystyle=-g\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}\Bigg[\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{f}}\Big(\psi^{\dagger}_{n,\alpha}\gamma^{0}\psi_{n,\alpha}\Big)\Bigg]^{2},

\displaystyle=-g\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}\Bigg[\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{f}}\Big(c^{\dagger}_{n,\alpha,0}c_{n,\alpha,0}-c^{\dagger}_{n,\alpha,1}c_{n,\alpha,1}\Big)\Bigg]^{2}.

(9)

The total Hamiltonian is the sum of Eqs. (
6
), (
7
), (
8
) for the Thirring model, and Eqs. (
6

7

9
) for the Gross–Neveu model. In order to make this problem amenable to quantum computing, we encode the Dirac spinors using the Jordan–Wigner (JW) transformation. Using this fermion-qubit mapping, we can rewrite the full Hamiltonian completely in terms of Pauli matrices for any number of flavors utilizing a total of
2N_{f}L
qubits for a spatial lattice of
L

Ground state preparation using AVQITE and static fermion correlator

3.1
AVQITE preparation

The state-preparation method that we have chosen is the adaptive-variational quantum imaginary-time evolution (AVQITE)
Gomes:2021ckn
;
Getelina:2023nwx
;
Getelina:2023yrf
. AVQITE builds on the quantum imaginary-time evolution
Motta:2020qite
and its variational counterpart
McArdle:2019vqite
, by allowing the underlying parameterized circuit to expand iteratively as needed, thus saving quantum resources and producing shallower circuits than other imaginary-time approaches, making it more suitable for near-term quantum simulations. In addition, after using AVQITE to prepare the ground state, one can employ the same adaptive-variational principles to perform the real-time dynamics
Yao:2021avqds
. Additional details about AVQITE can be found in Appendix
B
.

McLachlan:1964
;

\exp(-\tau H)

\tau
being the imaginary time and
H
the system Hamiltonian. The adaptive part of this procedure stems from iteratively appending operators to the parameterized unitary
U_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, until the minimization criterion is satisfied. These unitary operators are drawn from a predefined operator pool, which is in general composed of Pauli strings
with maximum weight
k

4.1
Complexity using product formulas

One popular method for Hamiltonian simulation is the product formula, also referred to as the ‘Suzuki-Trotter’ or ‘Lie-Trotter-Suzuki’ formula
Hatano:2005gh
.
Following the works of Refs.
Childs2019
;
Childs:2019hts
;
Zhuk:2023zeh
, it is known that given a local Hamiltonian with nearest-neighbor interactions, we need
O(n^{2}t^{2})
gates using the first-order product formula for some fixed accuracy
\epsilon
. This can be improved to
O(n^{1+1/p}t^{1+1/p})
where
n
is the number of qubits,
t
is the total evolution time, and
p
is the order of the product formula used. This result is optimal and consistent with those obtained using the Lieb-Robinson bound
Haah:2018ekc
. For
lattice Hamiltonians with a two-component Dirac spinor with
O(N_{f})
geometric locality and maximum Pauli weight, we are
not aware whether such tight bounds exist.

Once we have the Hamiltonian in the second-quantized form, we use the Jordan–Wigner mapping to express it in terms of Pauli strings of length
2N_{f}L
where the maximum weight is
2N_{f}+2
. In addition to the Jordan–Wigner mapping, we have also tried using Bravyi–Kitaev mapping; however, the difference was not significant for the range of qubits considered. Then, we can use a graph-coloring algorithm to collect the Pauli strings into clusters consisting of commuting Pauli string. For the Gross–Neveu model and the Thirring model with
N_{f}=1
, we have
\Gamma=3
clusters, while for the Thirring model with
N_{f}>1
, we have
\Gamma=4
. Once we have these clusters, we can simultaneously diagonalize each cluster and find its unitary circuit
van_den_Berg_2020
;
Murairi:2022zdg
;
Asaduzzaman:2023wtd
;
Jha:2024vcw
. For each cluster, finding the diagonalizing unitary circuit amounts to finding
V
such that
V^{\dagger}HV=H_{d}
, where
H_{d}
consists of Pauli strings with either
Z
or
\mathbb{I}
(diagonal).
The Trotter error comes from the terms in different clusters that do not commute with each other. The sum of spectral norms for each pair using the first-order product formula is bounded by
O(LN_{f}^{2})
. The number of steps
n
required to achieve error
\epsilon
is (
j,k=1,\dots,\Gamma
):

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\geq\frac{t^{2}}{2\epsilon}\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq\Gamma}\Big|\Big|[H_{j},H_{k}]\Big|\Big|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{t^{2}}{2\epsilon}\widetilde{\alpha},

(12)

where
\widetilde{\alpha}=\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq\Gamma}\Big|\Big|[H_{j},H_{k}]\Big|\Big|
is the commutator norm, which is
O(LN_{f}^{2})
. Hence, we find that for each first-order Trotter step, we need
O(LN_{f}^{2})
Clifford+T-gates leading to overall complexity of

O(L^{2}N_{f}^{4}t^{2}/\epsilon)
. We collect the total Clifford+T-gate costs using the first-order Trotter approach in Table
AI
and
AII
in
Appendix
D
.

Instead of using the first-order product formula, we can use the higher-order product formulas. We now estimate the costs of the Hamiltonian simulation based on higher-order (order
p
) product formulas. The general bound for the number of required Trotter steps
n_{p}
is:

\displaystyle n_{p}

\displaystyle\geq\Gamma t\left(\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}_{p+1}\,t}{\epsilon}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\geq\Gamma t\left(\frac{t}{\epsilon}\right)^{1/p}\widetilde{\alpha}^{\frac{p+1}{p}},

(13)

where
\widetilde{\alpha}_{p+1}
is the
p+1
-nested commutator sum:

\widetilde{\alpha}_{p+1}=\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{p+1}=1}^{\Gamma}\Big|\Big|\big[H_{j_{p+1}},\big[H_{j_{p}},\dots,\big[H_{j_{2}},H_{j_{1}}\big]\dots\big]\big]\Big|\Big|\leq 2^{p}\alpha^{p+1},

(14)

the inequality follows from repeated use of
||[A,B]||\leq 2||A||\,||B||
, and
||\cdot||
denotes the spectral norm. We need (in the worst-case)
n\geq O(LN_{f}^{2}t^{1+\frac{1}{p}}\epsilon^{-1/p})
steps, leading to overall complexity of
O(L^{2}N_{f}^{4}\,t^{1+\frac{1}{p}}\epsilon^{-1/p})

for order
p
product formula. For local Hamiltonians, there exists a tighter result of
O(N_{f}Lt)
due to the Lieb-Robinson bound
Childs2019
. However, for our case of relativistic fermionic models with large
N_{f}
mapped to qubits, it is not clear whether such a tight bound can be attained. We leave this question for future work.

4.2.1
Cost of block-encodings

In order to estimate the cost using quantum singular value transformations (QSVT)/ qubitization for the Hamiltonian simulation, the first step is to block-encode the Hamiltonian (Hermitian matrix)
H
, or more accurately,
H/\alpha
where
\alpha
is a rescaling factor that keeps the spectrum of
H/\alpha
in the interval
[-1,1]

A unitary

is said to be an
\varepsilon
-block-encoding of
\widetilde{H}=\frac{H}{\alpha}
provided that:

\Big|\Big|\widetilde{H}-\big(\left\langle{0^{a}}\right|\otimes I_{s}\big)\,U\,\big(\left|{0^{a}}\right\rangle\otimes I_{s}\big)\Big|\Big|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha},

(15)

where
\left|{0^{a}}\right\rangle
denotes the ancilla qubits and the subscript
s
denotes the system.
i.e., the top-left block of
U
approximates
H/\alpha
within operator-norm error

\varepsilon/\alpha
as:

U=\begin{pmatrix}H/\alpha&\,\,*\\
*&\,\,*\end{pmatrix}.

(16)

The Hamiltonian for the Thirring and Gross–Neveu models in the limit of large
L
and
N_{f}
has
O(LN_{f}^{2})
terms (see Eq. (
30
)), and can be written schematically as:

\widetilde{H}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{\gamma}\alpha_{j}P_{j},

(17)

where
\gamma=5N_{f}L+2LN_{f}^{2}-4N_{f}
for the GN model and
\gamma=3N_{f}L+4LN_{f}^{2}-4N_{f}

\text{PREP}(\left|{0}\right\rangle^{\otimes\log(O(LN_{f}^{2}))})=\left|{A}\right\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{O(LN_{f}^{2})}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha}}\left|{j}\right\rangle,

(18)

which prepares the coefficient, and then use another unitary

\text{SELECT}(H)=\sum_{j=1}^{O(LN_{f}^{2})}\left|{j}\right\rangle\left\langle{j}\right|\otimes P_{j}.

Then the (normalized) Hamiltonian

U:=\Big(\mathrm{PREP}^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbb{I}\Big)\cdot\mathrm{SELECT}(H)\cdot\Big(\mathrm{PREP}\otimes\mathbb{I}\Big).

Similarly to

Using the definition of
\mathrm{PREP}
and

\mathrm{PREP}

H

\alpha_{j}=c

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{m}}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m}-1}\left|{j}\right\rangle,

(21)

leading to a total Clifford+T-gate cost of
O(1)

gates. The other extreme case is where all terms are different, leading to a cost of
O(LN_{f}^{2})

K=3

|\alpha_{j}|

O(\log K+\log LN_{f}^{2})

O(\log LN_{f}^{2})
for the PREP circuit
since

\mathrm{SELECT}
circuit.
For this, we require an index register of size
m=\lceil\log(LN_{f}^{2})\rceil
to compute a 1-bit equality flag and apply a single flag-controlled Pauli string. For our Hamiltonian, the maximum Pauli weight is
2N_{f}+2

O(N_{f}+\log(LN_{f}^{2})\big)

6
Summary and future directions

We have provided the quantum-computational framework to simulate the 1+1-dimensional Thirring and Gross–Neveu models with arbitrary number of fermionic flavors,
N_{f}
. Using our formulation, we then constructed the ground state of both the Thirring and Gross–Neveu models for
N_{f}=1,2,3,4
up to 20 qubits using AVQITE methods, and found excellent agreement with the exact results. We then considered the problem of complexity of Hamiltonian simulation using both higher-order product formulas and block encoding/QSVT methods and found that the required resources scale polynomially in both system size and number of flavors and linear in simulation time, which suggests that our quantum simulation is efficient. Finally, we identified the dynamical Lie algebra (DLA) of both models and found that they belong to the same isomorphism class of Ref.
Aguilar:2024dnz
.

Our results for both ground state preparation and time evolution provide concrete evidence that quantum computing methods are efficient for understanding non-perturbative aspects of four-fermionic models through real-time evolution and for exploring interesting questions that are of interest to high-energy theorists at finite and large
N_{f}
. In the coming years, we believe that this work will be extended to a full-scale hardware implementation on near-term and fault-tolerant devices, paving the way to understanding relativistic fermionic field theories with properties such as asymptotic freedom similar to four-dimensional QCD. In addition, it would be interesting to apply other quantum algorithms for state preparation and see how they compare with the results presented here using AVQITE. Future computations of these relativistic field theories on quantum hardware in 1+1-dimensions and eventually in higher dimensions will help us probe and better understand various aspects of chiral symmetry breaking, flavor physics, and eventually lead to complete understanding of real-time and non-perturbative processes in QCD.

B.1
Convergence of QITE with non-zero overlap

Consider a Hamiltonian
H
that has a ground state
|g\rangle
with energy
E_{0}
and
excited states
\{|k\rangle\}_{k\geq 1}
with energies
E_{k}\geq E_{0}+\Delta
, where
\Delta>0
is the spectral gap defined as
E_{1}-E_{0}
. For an initial state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
that has a finite overlap
\delta
with the target ground state, i.e.,
\delta\equiv|\langle\Psi_{0}|g\rangle|\neq 0
, one can write the following expansion:

|\Psi_{0}\rangle=\delta|g\rangle+\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}|k\rangle.

Applying the imaginary time evolution operator to the above expression gives

|\phi_{\tau}\rangle=e^{-\tau(H-E_{0})}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=\delta|g\rangle+\sum_{k\geq 1}c_{k}e^{-\tau(E_{k}-E_{0})}|k\rangle.

Now, let us define the quantity

r_{\tau}\equiv\sum_{k\geq 1}|c_{k}|^{2}e^{-2\tau(E_{k}-E_{0})}.

(31)

Note that
e^{-2\tau(E_{k}-E_{0})}\leq e^{-2\tau\Delta}
for
k\geq 1
, because
every excited states is at least one gap above the ground state. Hence, we can bound Eq. (
31
) as:

r_{\tau}\leq(1-\delta^{2})e^{-2\Delta\tau}.

(32)

The normalized state is

|\Psi_{\tau}\rangle=\frac{|\phi_{\tau}\rangle}{||\phi_{\tau}||},\qquad||\phi_{\tau}||^{2}=\delta^{2}+r_{\tau}.

(33)

Thus, the fidelity with the true ground state after evolving for time
\tau
is:

F(\tau)=|\langle g|\Psi_{\tau}\rangle|^{2}=\frac{\delta^{2}}{\delta^{2}+r_{\tau}}=\frac{1}{1+r_{\tau}/\delta^{2}}.

(34)

Since
(1+x)^{-1}\geq 1-x
for all
x\geq 0
, we obtain the lower bound

F(\tau)\geq 1-\frac{r_{\tau}}{\delta^{2}}.

(35)

Using
r_{\tau}\leq(1-\delta^{2})e^{-2\Delta\tau}
, this yields

F(\tau)\geq 1-\Big(\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}-1\Big)e^{-2\Delta\tau}.

(36)

To attain
\epsilon
infidelity,
F(\tau)\geq 1-\epsilon
, we need to evolve to imaginary time
\tau
given by:

\tau\geq\frac{1}{2\Delta}\log\left(\frac{\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}-1}{\epsilon}\right).

(37)

B.2
AVQITE optimization procedure

In this subsection, we give the basic formalism of AVQITE approach used in the paper. We refer the reader to Refs.
Yuan:2018jdl
for additional details. Let us start from the real-time Schrödinger evolution equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left|{\psi(t)}\right\rangle=-\,i\hat{H}\left|{\psi(t)}\right\rangle.

(38)

Performing a Wick rotation
t\to-i\tau
, we obtain
\partial_{\tau}\left|{\psi(\tau)}\right\rangle=-\hat{H}\left|{\psi(\tau)}\right\rangle
which is the
imaginary-time Schrödinger equation. The solution is

\displaystyle\left|{\psi(\tau)}\right\rangle=e^{-\tau\hat{H}}\left|{\psi(0)}\right\rangle,

(39)

which projects onto the ground state as

\tau\to\infty
assuming nonzero ground-state overlap
\delta^{2}
. We will use AVQITE to approximate this solution. One of the problems associated with this approach of finding ground states is that the operator
\exp(-\tau\hat{H})
is not unitary. AVQITE aims to follow the (normalized) imaginary-time flow:

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\left|{\psi(\tau)}\right\rangle=-\bigl(\hat{H}-\langle\hat{H}\rangle_{\tau}\bigr)\left|{\psi(\tau)}\right\rangle,\qquad\langle\hat{H}\rangle_{\tau}\equiv\left\langle{\psi(\tau)}\right|\hat{H}\left|{\psi(\tau)}\right\rangle.

(40)

Instead of representing the non-unitary operator
e^{-\tau\hat{H}}
directly, AVQITE restricts to a unitary variational family

\displaystyle\left|{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right\rangle=U(\boldsymbol{\theta})\left|{\psi(0)}\right\rangle,\qquad U(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\prod_{k}e^{-i\theta_{k}A_{k}},

(41)

where each generator
A_{k}
is Hermitian and selected from a predetermined pool to find the closest unitary to
e^{-\tau\hat{H}}
. To understand the cost function used in AVQITE, we start by recasting the flow equation in terms of density matrices. We can write the density matrix,
\widetilde{\rho}=\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\left\langle{\psi}\right|
, where
\widetilde{\rho}
denotes the unnormalized density matrix. Then

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{\rho}

\displaystyle=\left|{\partial_{\tau}\psi}\right\rangle\left\langle{\psi}\right|+\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\left\langle{\partial_{\tau}\psi}\right|

(42)

\displaystyle=-\,\hat{H}\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\left\langle{\psi}\right|-\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\left\langle{\psi}\right|\,\hat{H}

(43)

\displaystyle=-\,\{\hat{H},\widetilde{\rho}\}.

(44)

To preserve normalization, we define

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\left|{\psi}\right\rangle=-\bigl(\hat{H}-\langle\hat{H}\rangle\bigr)\left|{\psi}\right\rangle,\qquad\langle\hat{H}\rangle=\left\langle{\psi}\right|\hat{H}\left|{\psi}\right\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{H}\rho),

(45)

where
\rho=\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\left\langle{\psi}\right|
is the normalized density matrix. Then

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\rho

\displaystyle=-(\hat{H}-\langle\hat{H}\rangle)\rho-\rho(\hat{H}-\langle\hat{H}\rangle)

(46)

\displaystyle=-\{\hat{H},\rho\}+2\langle\hat{H}\rangle\rho.

(47)

If we now define the Liouvillian superoperator:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\rho)\equiv-\{\hat{H},\rho\}+2\langle\hat{H}\rangle\rho,

(48)

we find:

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\rho=\mathcal{L}(\rho).

(49)

In AVQITE simulations of Eq. (
49
), the variational state
\rho
is a function of parameters, i.e.,
\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})
but we will simply write it as
\rho
and we have approximately
\partial_{\tau}\rho=\sum_{\mu=1}^{N_{\theta}}(\partial_{\mu}\rho)\,\dot{\theta}_{\mu}
using the shorthand
\partial_{\mu}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{\mu}}
.
We can now define the residual, which measures the deviation from the actual imaginary flow, as:

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\sum_{\mu=1}^{N_{\theta}}\partial_{\mu}\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\dot{\theta}_{\mu}-\mathcal{L}\bigl(\rho(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr).

(50)

The variational principle due to McLachlan aims to minimizes the quadratic cost:

\displaystyle L^{2}=\Big|\Big|R(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\Big|\Big|_{2}^{2},

(51)

where
||\cdot||_{2}
is the Schatten-2 norm (also known as the Frobenius norm) defined as
||R||_{2}^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}(R^{\dagger}R)
. If we now expand Eq. (
51
) using the definition in Eq. (
50
), we obtain:

\displaystyle L^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\Bigl(\sum_{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\rho\,\dot{\theta}_{\mu}-\mathcal{L}(\rho)\Bigr)^{\dagger}\Bigl(\sum_{\nu}\partial_{\nu}\rho\,\dot{\theta}_{\nu}-\mathcal{L}(\rho)\Bigr)\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu\nu}\dot{\theta}_{\mu}\dot{\theta}_{\nu}\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigl[(\partial_{\mu}\rho)^{\dagger}(\partial_{\nu}\rho)\bigr]-\sum_{\mu}\dot{\theta}_{\mu}\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigl[(\partial_{\mu}\rho)^{\dagger}\mathcal{L}(\rho)\bigr]-\sum_{\nu}\dot{\theta}_{\nu}\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigl[\mathcal{L}(\rho)^{\dagger}(\partial_{\nu}\rho)\bigr]+\mathrm{Tr}\bigl[\mathcal{L}(\rho)^{\dagger}\mathcal{L}(\rho)\bigr].

(52)

For real parameters
\dot{\theta}_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}
, we can define:

\displaystyle M_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\Big[(\partial_{\mu}\rho)^{\dagger}(\partial_{\nu}\rho)\Big],

(53)

\displaystyle V_{\mu}

\displaystyle\equiv\Re\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big[(\partial_{\mu}\rho)^{\dagger}\mathcal{L}(\rho)\Big],

(54)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\mathcal{L}(\rho)^{\dagger}\mathcal{L}(\rho)\Big]=||\mathcal{L}(\rho)||_{2}^{2}.

(55)

Here
M_{\mu\nu}
is an
N_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\times N_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
real symmetric matrix also referred to as the quantum Fisher information matrix
Koczor:2019pvr
;
Gomes:2021ckn
. For a pure state, i.e.,
\rho=\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\left\langle{\psi}\right|
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\rho

\displaystyle=\left|{\partial_{\mu}\psi}\right\rangle\left\langle{\psi}\right|+\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\left\langle{\partial_{\mu}\psi}\right|.

(56)

Using Eq. (
56
) in Eq. (
53
) with
\mathrm{Tr}(\left|{a}\right\rangle\left\langle{b}\right|)=\langle b|a\rangle
, we obtain:

\displaystyle M_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=2\mathfrak{R}\Big[\langle\partial_{\mu}\psi|\partial_{\nu}\psi\rangle+\langle\partial_{\mu}\psi|\psi\rangle\,\langle\psi|\partial_{\nu}\psi\rangle\Big],

(57)

where we denote the parametrized pure state
\left|{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right\rangle
simply by
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle
.
Now, consider Eq. (
54
). We can express
V_{\mu}
using Eq. (
46
) i.e.,
\mathcal{L}(\rho)=-(\hat{H}-\langle\hat{H}\rangle)\rho-\rho(\hat{H}-\langle\hat{H}\rangle)
as:

\displaystyle V_{\mu}

\displaystyle=-2\mathfrak{R}\Big[\langle\partial_{\mu}\psi|\hat{H}|\psi\rangle\Big],

(58)

where we have again used the shorthand
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle=\left|{\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right\rangle
.
Now consider the last term of Eq. (
52
). Setting
A\equiv\hat{H}-\langle\hat{H}\rangle\mathbb{I}
, we can write Eq. (
48
) as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\rho)=-\{A,\rho\}.

(59)

As
\rho
is Hermitian, we obtain:

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\mathcal{L}(\rho)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\{A,\rho\}^{2}\Big],

\displaystyle=2\,\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^{2}A^{2})+2\,\mathrm{Tr}(\rho A\rho A)

(60)

after expanding
\{A,\rho\}^{2}=(A\rho+\rho A)^{2}
and using cyclicity of the trace. For a pure state
\rho=\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\left\langle{\psi}\right|
, we have
\rho^{2}=\rho
and
\rho A\rho=\langle A\rangle\rho=0
.
Hence,
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho A\rho A)=0
and

\displaystyle C=2\langle A^{2}\rangle=2\bigl(\langle\hat{H}^{2}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\langle\hat{H}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\bigr)=2\,\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\hat{H}).

(61)

Then, the objective cost function given by Eq. (
52
) becomes:

\displaystyle L^{2}=\sum_{\mu\nu}M_{\mu\nu}\dot{\theta}_{\mu}\dot{\theta}_{\nu}-2\sum_{\mu}V_{\mu}\dot{\theta}_{\mu}+C.

(62)

where
M_{\mu\nu}
,
V_{\mu}
and
C
are defined in
Eq. (
57
), Eq. (
58
), and Eq. (
61
), respectively. Differentiating Eq. (
62
) with respect to
\dot{\theta}_{\mu}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L^{2}}{\partial\dot{\theta}_{\mu}}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\sum_{\nu}M_{\mu\nu}\dot{\theta}_{\nu}=V_{\mu}.

(63)

In vector form, we have
\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\,*}=M^{-1}V
,
and plugging back into Eq. (
63
), we find the minimum/optimal value as:

\displaystyle L_{2,\min}^{2}=C-V^{T}M^{-1}V.

(64)

In practical calculations, for a given time step, one should append operators to the ansatz whenever the measured McLachlan distance
L^{2}
is below a certain threshold
L_{\text{cut}}^{2}
, which we set to
L_{\text{cut}}^{2}=10^{-2}
. We also limit the number of operators that can be added to the ansatz to five at each time step. If
L^{2}
is above the threshold even after adding five operators, we simply move on to the next time step. The imaginary-time evolution proceeds until the maximum energy gradient
\max|V|
is below an empirically determined threshold of
V_{\textrm{cut}}=10^{-4}
, which has been observed to consistently yield a final state with excellent fidelity with respect to the exact ground state for various spin models
Gomes:2021ckn
;
Getelina:2023nwx
;
Getelina:2023yrf
.

Note that inverting the matrix
M
defined in Eq. (
53
) may cause numerical problems due to its condition number being too large. To circumvent this issue, we apply a ridge regression (also called Tikhonov regularization), which consists of shifting the diagonal values of
M
by a relatively small constant
Hoerl:1970ridge
. Here, for all of our calculations we have considered
M\rightarrow M+\lambda\mathbb{I}
with
\lambda=10^{-6}
.

Finally, as discussed in the previous subsection, having a finite overlap with the target ground state is essential for the success of any imaginary-time-evolution method. Fig.
B.1
shows the initial state overlap as a function of the number of qubits for various system sizes and number of flavors for both the Thirring and Gross–Neveu models. For the Gross–Neveu model we see that the overlap improves with increasing
N_{f}
, while for the Thirring model it appears to be independent of
N_{f}>1
. In both the cases, they seem to follow
O(\text{polylog}(n))
reduction, which is better than the estimate of exponential decrease from naive counting.

Appendix C
Brief review of dynamical Lie algebras

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra. A subalgebra
\mathfrak{s}
of
\mathfrak{g}
is defined as a subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
that is closed
under the bracket operation, i.e.,
[\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{s}]\subseteq\mathfrak{s}
.
If a subalgebra
\mathfrak{s}
satisfies
[\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{g}]\subseteq\mathfrak{s}
, it is called an ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
.
A simple Lie algebra is a non-abelian Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
(i.e.,
[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]\neq\{0\}
)
whose only ideals are
\{0\}
and
\mathfrak{g}
. A Lie algebra is called semi-simple if it
can be written as a direct sum of simple (commuting) ideals.
For a semi-simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, we can define its Cartan decomposition as a split

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{m}

that satisfies:

[\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{k}]\subseteq\mathfrak{k},\qquad[\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}]\subseteq\mathfrak{k},\qquad[\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{m}]\subseteq\mathfrak{m}.

In this paper, our focus is only on the dimension of
\mathfrak{g}
rather than on finding the Cartan pair and the applications associated with it. In control theory
DAlessandro2021
, occurrence of barren plateaus
Ragone:2023qbn
and other problems, the size of the DLA, i.e.,
\text{dim}(\mathfrak{g}(H))
is often crucial. In a recent study
Wiersema:2023txu
, the DLAs of two-local spin models were classified, while the general case of a Pauli generator set was considered in Ref.
Aguilar:2024dnz
. Here, we determine the DLA for the fermionic Hamiltonians that correspond to four-fermion quantum field theories in the continuum limit.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\{A,B,\ldots\}
be a set of Pauli strings (Hermitian) on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space. The Lie bracket (also known as commutator) is defined as:

[A,B]=AB-BA.

(65)

Since the commutator of Hermitian operators is anti-Hermitian, it is convenient to work in the real vector space of anti-Hermitian operators. The
dynamical Lie algebra
generated by
\mathcal{G}
is defined as

\mathfrak{g}=\mathrm{Lie}_{\mathbb{R}}\big(i\mathcal{G}\big)=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\Big\langle iA,\,iB,\,\ldots\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{Lie}}

(66)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{Lie}}
denotes the
Lie closure
. The Lie closure
\langle i\mathcal{G}\rangle_{\mathrm{Lie}}
is the smallest set that contains

i\mathcal{G}
and is closed under the Lie bracket, i.e., it is the set obtained by iterating commutators:

X_{1},X_{2}\in\langle i\mathcal{G}\rangle_{\mathrm{Lie}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad[X_{1},X_{2}]\in\langle i\mathcal{G}\rangle_{\mathrm{Lie}},

(67)

until no new elements are added.
Let us define such a basis of
Hermitian
elements as
\{L_{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{\dim(\mathfrak{g})}
so that:

\mathfrak{g}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{iL_{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{\dim(\mathfrak{g})},\qquad(L_{\mu})^{\dagger}=L_{\mu}.

(68)

Here
\dim(\mathfrak{g})
is the dimension of the dynamical Lie algebra as a real vector space,
and
\{L_{\mu}\}
is a set of linearly independent generators whose span determines
\mathfrak{g}
. For our case, the generating set
\mathcal{G}
is simply the individual Pauli strings of the Hamiltonian
H
.
