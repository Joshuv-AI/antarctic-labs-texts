Identification of aging-related signature genes in temporomandibular joint degeneration based on bioinformatics
Authors: Xinyi Wang, Ziyang Xia, Yanzhang Zhou, Minda Wang, Ting Jiang, Jingwen Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDegenerative temporomandibular joint diseases (TMJ-DJD) are increasingly affecting elderly populations, with aging being a significant risk factor driving the TMJ degenerative changes. This study aims to explore the shared characteristics between aging and TMJ degenerative diseases at the tissue level, and to identify aging-related signature genes in TMJ degenerations using bioinformatics approaches.MethodsCondyles from young (2 months, n = 7) and aged (18 months, n = 6) mice were used to construct an aging dataset. Overlapping genes were identified as the intersection of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the aging and TMJOA datasets, with exclusion of DEGs from the mechanical stimulation dataset. Temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis-associated and aging-related differentially expressed genes (TMJOA-ARDEGs) were identified through Weighted gene co-expression network analysis. Functional enrichment analysis and Protein-Protein Interaction networks were employed. Machine learning algorithms were subsequently applied to further screen the TMJOA-ARDEGs, yielding eight Hub TMJOA-ARDEGs. An age-related TMJOA mouse model (18 months of age) was established and grouped based on the micro-CT and the modified Mankin scores. The expression changes of key Hub TMJOA-ARDEGs were verified by immunohistochemical staining. The Mann-Whitney U test was used in non-normally distributed data, presented as [median (interquartile range)].ResultsFifty-two TMJOA-ARDEGs were identified; functional enrichment analysis revealed that these genes are involved in circadian rhythm regulation and apoptosis. Eight Hub TMJOA-ARDEGs were identified: ANK1, MELTF, FERMT3, MDFI, CXCL14, EPHA3, SMOC2, and NR1D1. Based on the micro-CT, 12 condyles of aged mice were divided into the TMJOA group (n = 6) and the healthy group (n = 6). Micro-CT revealed bone resorption in the TMJOA group compared to the healthy group, with a decrease in BV/TV (p < 0.05) and an increase in Tb.Sp (p < 0.05). The modified Mankin scores showed that the TMJOA group had a score of 5.61 (2.28), higher than the healthy group’s score of 0.83 (1.28), p < 0.01. Among the Hub TMJOA-ARDEGs, NR1D1 is a key gene involved in regulating circadian rhythm. Immunohistochemical staining confirmed that NR1D1 expression was significantly elevated in the TMJOA group compared to the healthy group (p < 0.01).ConclusionNR1D1, a negative transcriptional regulator within the circadian feedback loop, is identified as a candidate gene shared between aging and TMJ degenerative diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08775-9.

Introduction

The health of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is crucial for essential functions including chewing and speaking. With the accelerating trend of global population aging, degenerative diseases of the TMJ have emerged as a significant concern for the quality of life in middle-aged and elderly populations.

Degenerative temporomandibular joint disease (TMJ-DJD), primarily involves a histological degenerative process in the temporomandibular joint, encompassing cartilage degradation and subchondral bone remodeling [1]. TMJ-DJD encompasses TMJ osteoarthritis (TMJ-OArth) and TMJ osteoarthrosis (TMJ-OAro). Since TMJ-OArth and TMJ-OAro are nearly identical in terms of etiology, pathological manifestations, and treatment methods, differing only in the presence of pain, many studies use the term TMJOA to broadly refer to both osteoarthritis and osteoarthrosis [2].

The pathogenic mechanisms of joint degeneration have long been a subject of extensive research.

Previous epidemiological studies have shown that, as observed in other joints [3], the incidence of degenerative diseases of the temporomandibular joint increases with advancing age [4, 5]. Additionally, imaging data [6, 7], histological samples [8, 9], as well as clinical symptoms [10], demonstrate that degenerative changes in the TMJ condyle also escalate with age. However, there is currently no clear evidence that aging is a direct causative factor for TMJ degeneration. By contrast, in large joints such as the knee and hip, aging is widely regarded as one of the most significant risk factors for degeneration [11].

However, the TMJ, as the only bilateral coupled joint, differs fundamentally from large joints in its structural composition, movement, and internal architecture, as shown in Table 1. In terms of structure, as a modified hinge joint, the TMJ allows for bilateral coordinated multi-axis movement. In terms of composition, unlike the hyaline cartilage of the knee or hip joint that only contain type II collagen, the fibrocartilage of the TMJ contains both type I and type II collagen. Therefore, the TMJ is more susceptible to mechanical stress and degeneration, while also exhibiting stronger remodeling and repair capabilities [12–14]. These unique characteristics highlight the distinct research value of the TMJ.

Furthermore, aging inevitably leads to the accumulation of mechanical stress, which is also a key factor in TMJOA. Investigating the relationship between age-related degenerative changes becomes especially challenging due to the complex interplay of factors.

Therefore, this study focuses on aging as a key risk factor, using bioinformatics to explore the molecular commonalities between aging organisms and TMJ degenerative changes, and to identify candidate genes shared between the two conditions.

Materials and methods

Study design

Samples of TMJ condylar process were collected from young and aged groups of mice and subjected to RNA sequencing. The resulting transcriptomic data are hereafter referred to as the Aging Dataset. Transcriptomic datasets GSE182969 and GSE158106 were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Dataset GSE182969, comprising TMJOA samples and healthy samples, is hereafter referred to as the TMJOA dataset. Dataset GSE158106, comprising mechanical stimulation samples and non-mechanical stimulation samples, is hereafter referred to as the Mechanical Stimulation Dataset. Additionally, the intersection of the DEGs from the aging dataset and the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the TMJOA dataset was taken, excluding the intersection with the mechanical stimulation dataset DEGs, to obtain the overlapping DEGs. This allowed for the identification of DEGs associated with both aging and osteoarthritis, while excluding the interference caused by mechanical stimulation as a risk factor for TMJOA.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed on the aging dataset to identify core genes of aging-related modules, and functional enrichment analysis was conducted for the module most strongly associated with aging. The intersection of the WGCNA aging-related module core genes and the overlapping DEGs was subsequently identified as temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis-associated and aging-related differentially expressed genes (TMJOA-ARDEGs). Protein-protein interaction analysis and functional enrichment analyses were further conducted for TMJOA-ARDEGs. Machine learning methods were applied to further screen the Hub temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis-associated and aging-related differentially expressed genes (Hub TMJOA-ARDEGs). Finally, the Age-related TMJOA mouse model was established and evaluated. The expression levels of key Hub TMJOA-ARDEGs in the Age-related TMJOA mouse model were validated using immunohistochemistry.

This study is divided into three parts: First, the collection of transcriptomic datasets related to aging, TMJOA, and mechanical stimulation, and the identification of overlapping DEGs (the Aging dataset + the TMJOA dataset – the Mechanical stimulation dataset). Second, the identification of TMJOA-ARDEGs. Third, the identification of Hub TMJOA-ARDEGs and experimental validation. The workflow of this study is shown in Fig. 1.

The identification of overlapping DEGs

Construction of aged mouse model, collection of TMJ samples, RNA extraction and sequencing

A total of 13 C57BL/6 male mice (18–20 g; Vital River, Beijing, China) were used in our study to establish the aging dataset. The mice were divided into two groups: 7 young mice (2 months of age, male) and 6 aged mice (18 months of age, male). They were housed under a 12-hour light-dark cycle with free access to water and food. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Peking University and performed in accordance with ethical guidelines (Approval number: LA2022044).

The euthanasia of mice was performed using CO2 overdose at a concentration of 70–100%, with a flow rate of 30–70% of the chamber volume per minute. Condylar cartilage tissue samples were collected from both the young and aged groups. Cartilage tissue samples were cut, frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen. Tissue samples were homogenized, ground, and lysed using the Trizol reagent method to extract purified total RNA samples.

The isolated RNA was sent to BGI® (Beijing Genomics Institute, Shenzhen, China) for RNA-seq, which was performed on the DNBSEQ high-throughput platform. The mRNA library was prepared using the BGI Optimal series dual-module mRNA library construction kit (BGI-Shenzhen, China). PE100/PE150 sequencing was conducted on the G400/T7/T10 sequencer (BGI-Shenzhen, China).

The datasets were retrieved directly from the NCBI GEO database using a keyword search, with the search query: (temporomandibular joint OR TMJ) AND (degeneration OR osteoarthritis OR arthritis OR TMJOA) AND (Mus musculus [Organism]). A total of 13 datasets were retrieved, and the basic information of these datasets was reviewed for selection.

Data preprocessing and the identification of Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) and overlapping DEGs

To identify and validate aging-related signature genes in TMJOA, transcriptomic data were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database using GEOquery (v2.62.2) in R, including GSE182969 as and GSE158106 as the mechanical stimulation dataset.

For the TMJOA dataset (GSE182969), we directly utilized the pre-calculated differential analysis results provided by the original authors to maintain consistency with the published evidence. For the mechanical stimulation dataset GSE158106 (GPL23038 platform), we obtained the processed gene expression matrix (Series Matrix File), which had undergone background correction and normalization by the original depositors. To ensure data consistency, multiple probes corresponding to the same Gene Symbol were averaged. We applied a log2(expression value + 1) transformation to stabilize variance. Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package (v3.50.0). To control the false discovery rate (FDR), P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method, with an adjusted P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Batch effect correction was not required as GSE158106 and GSE182969 were analyzed independently for discovery and validation without cross-platform integration.

DESeq2 (v1.34.0) [15] was used to identify DEGs in the aging dataset. Across all three datasets, DEGs were defined by the criteria of |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05. A volcano plot for the DEGs was generated using the ggplot2 package (v3.5.7) in R. DEGs with log2FC ≤ -1 were considered downregulated, while those with log2FC ≥ 1 were considered upregulated.

First, we selected the common DEGs between the TMJOA dataset and the aging dataset, whose expression levels are associated with aging and TMJOA. Then, we excluded genes that were also differentially expressed in the mechanical stimulation dataset. Ultimately, we identified these genes as Overlapping DEGs, as they are associated with both aging and TMJOA but not with mechanical stimulation. A Venn diagram was created using Venny [15].

The identification of TMJOA-ARDEGs

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)

For the expression matrix in the aging dataset, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was constructed based on the expression matrix of the aging dataset using the WGCNA package in R. The soft threshold power was determined to be 22, where R² > 0.8, thus building a scale-free network. Genes were clustered into multiple modules, each represented by different color labels, and the correlation between these modules and aging was analyzed and discussed.

The module most strongly associated with aging was considered the key module for further enrichment analysis. For the key module, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.2.2) in R. GO analysis, including cellular components (CC), biological processes (BP), and molecular functions (MF), was conducted to define and describe gene functions from three major functional categories. KEGG analysis identified various biological pathways. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.2.2) in R. GO or KEGG terms with a q-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched in DEGs. Visualization of the enrichment plots was carried out using the enrichplot package (v1.14.2) and ggplot2 (v3.5.1) in R.

For all modules related to aging, genes with both a module membership > 0.5 and a gene significance > 0.5 were selected as core genes in aging-related modules.

The identification, functional enrichment analysis and protein-protein interaction analysis of TMJOA- ARDEGs

The intersection of overlapping DEGs and core genes from the aging-related modules in WGCNA was defined as the temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis-associated and aging-related differentially expressed genes (TMJOA-ARDEGs). A Venn diagram was created using Venny.

GO and KEGG analyses were performed on TMJOA-ARDEGs, and a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed. Visualization of the GO and KEGG enrichment plots was carried out using the enrichplot package (v1.14.2) and ggplot2 (v3.5.1) in R.

Based on information from the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) [16], a PPI network was constructed using stringi (v1.7.12) and stringr (v1.5.0) packages in R to present the relationships between proteins. Cytoscape software (v3.9.0; https://www.cytoscape.org/) was used for visualization and beautification of the PPI network.

The identification of hub TMJOA-ARDEGs and experimental validation

Selection of Hub TMJOA-ARDEGs based on Machine learning

To identify high-performance diagnostic biomarkers, we employed two distinct machine learning algorithms: Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression and Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE).

LASSO was used to perform variable selection and regularization while fitting a generalized linear model. For LASSO analysis, a binomial logistic regression model was constructed using the glmnet package. To ensure the robustness of feature selection, we performed 10-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal regularization parameter. Specifically, lambda.1se value was selected as the penalty parameter to achieve a balance between predictive accuracy and model parsimony, thereby enhancing the generalization capability of the feature subset. Genes with non-zero coefficients at lambda.1se were identified as candidate Hub genes.

SVM-RFE is a feature selection method aimed at selecting the optimal subset of features by recursively building models and eliminating unimportant features. SVM-RFE was applied to rank and filter the 52 TMJOA-related DEGs using the caret package. We utilized a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel and conducted a fine-grained recursive search (step size = 1, range 1–52) to avoid missing the optimal feature subset. The final Hub gene set was determined based on the maximum cross-validation accuracy.

To reduce the risk of overfitting and improve model stability, ten-fold cross-validation was applied in the LASSO regression analysis and SVM-RFE (Supplementary Figs. 3 & 4).

Overlapping aging-related feature genes filtered by the LASSO and SVM-RFE algorithms were obtained using Venny and defined as Hub temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis-associated and aging-related genes (Hub TMJOA-ARDEGs).

Evaluate the expression levels of Hub TMJOA-ARDEGs in healthy and TMJOA mice in GSE182969, and visualize the data using a bar plot created in SRplot [17].

Construction of the age-related TMJOA mouse model based on imaging and histological evaluation

Twelve bilateral TMJ condyles were collected from six 18-month-old C57BL/6 mice (18–20 g; Vital River, Beijing, China) after 70%-100% CO2 inhalation, including 3 male mice and 3 female mice. Micro-CT, Safranin O-fast green staining, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and modified Mankin score were performed. Based on the degree of bone resorption in the mouse condyle observed in the three-dimensional micro-CT reconstructions, the 12 TMJ condyles were classified into the TMJOA group and the healthy group. Bone-related parameters were quantitatively analyzed using micro-CT. The modified Mankin score was assessed on Safranin O-Fast Green (SO/FG) stained sections and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained sections to evaluate the severity of TMJ degeneration.

A total of six C57 BL/6 aged mice (18 months of age) were euthanized, and bilateral TMJs were isolated. The TMJs were then fixed overnight in 10% Neutral Buffered Formalin (NBF). Using the SkyScan 1276 micro-CT system (Bruker, China), the condyles were scanned at 40 kV, 200 µA with a pixel size of 8.06 μm, and three-dimensional datasets were collected. The CTAn software (v1.20.8.0) was used for the reconstruction and analysis of the X-ray images. Parameters including Bone volume/Total volume (BV/TV), Trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), bone mineral density (BMD), Trabecular Thickness (Tb.Th), Trabecular Number (Tb.N) were measured.

The condylar cartilage samples were decalcified in 10% EDTA and embedded in paraffin after dehydration. Parallel 5 μm thick sagittal sections were prepared from the condylar process. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through an ethanol gradient. For histological examination, Safranin O/Fast Green (SO/FG) staining (Servicebio, China) and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Servicebio, China) was applied to the condylar cartilage samples. The modified Mankin scoring system [18] was used to semi-quantitatively assess cartilage degeneration in the SO/FG stained sections and H&E stained sections, including cartilage structure, chondrocyte count, Safranin O-stained cartilage matrix, and tide-mark structure.

Three experienced graders (Y.X., M.W., and Y.Z.) evaluated the sections of all joints. The slides were randomized and assigned a temporary identification code in a blinded manner. Subsequently, the modified Mankin score was applied to assess the cartilage degeneration. In each section, three fields at 20x magnification were selected (anterior, middle, and posterior regions of the condyle). Inter-rater agreement among the three graders was evaluated using Kendall’s W Test.

Histological validation of hub TMJOA-ARDEGs expression levels

The tissue sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by antigen retrieval using a microwave in citrate buffer solution. After serum blocking for 1 h, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with the primary antibody anti-NR1D1 Rabbit pAb (Servicebio, China). Following PBS washes, HRP was used to label goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Servicebio, China) and it was incubated for 1 h. All histological images were acquired under standardized microscope settings to ensure consistent exposure, illumination, and color balance across all groups. Immunoreactive positive cells were visualized using the DAB method, and cell nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Three graders (Y.X., M.W., and Y.Z.) independently calculated the proportion of NR1D1-positive cells in three fields (anterior, middle, and posterior regions of the condyle) of each section using ImageJ (NIH, USA). The mean values of the three fields were recorded as the result for each section.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables with a normal distribution and homogeneity of variance were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent-samples t-test. Variables that did not follow a normal distribution were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The identification of overlapping DEGs

By applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, we obtain high-quality and relevant data, ultimately including dataset GSE182969 as the TMJOA dataset, GSE158106 as the mechanical stimulation dataset.

The TMJOA dataset, from the GPL19057 platform, included data from 6 mice, with 3 mice in the experimental group subjected to partial discectomy surgery and 3 mice in the control group receiving sham surgery.

The mechanical stimulation dataset, based on the GPL23038 platform, included data from 8 mice, with 4 subjected to mechanical stress and 4 in the non-mechanical stress group.

In aging dataset, a total of 16,159 genes were detected between the young and aged group, leading to the identification of 2014 DEGs. Among these, 1536 genes exhibited upregulation, while 478 genes showed downregulation.

In the TMJOA dataset, 3298 DEGs were identified from a total of 23,170 detected genes in TMJOA mice and healthy mice, of which 457 genes were upregulated and 192 genes were downregulated.

In the mechanical stimulation dataset, 186 DEGs were identified from a total of 12,946 detected genes in mice with mechanical stimulation and without mechanical stimulation, with 115 genes exhibiting upregulation and 71 genes showing downregulation.

The correlation between the modules and aging was calculated and plotted (Fig. 3B). The results indicated that the green, blue, brown, and turquoise modules were significantly associated with the aging (p < 0.05). A total of 804 genes were found in these four modules. Based on the criteria of module membership > 0.5 and gene significance > 0.5, 599 core aging-related genes were selected from these modules (Figs. 3 C-F).

GO and KEGG analyses were performed for the turquoise module (-0.99, p < 0.0001, Fig. 3G-H) and green module (0.99, p < 0.0001, Fig. 3I-J), which showed the strongest negative and positive correlations with aging, respectively.

A total of 1,265 GO terms were enriched in the turquoise module, including 1,042 BP terms, 107 CC terms, and 116 MF terms. The top 10 terms in BP, CC, and MF categories are shown in Fig. 3G. These enriched terms were primarily associated with extracellular matrix organization, membrane microdomain structure, bone mineralization and development, molecular binding involved in ECM–cell interactions, and circadian rhythm. These terms include such as extracellular matrix organization, bone mineralization, collagen fibril organization, cell adhesion molecule binding, and circadian regulation of gene expression.

In the turquoise module, 36 KEGG pathways were significantly enriched, and the top 10 pathways are presented in Fig. 3H. These pathways were primarily associated with cytoskeleton, ECM–receptor interaction, protein processing and digestion/absorption and circadian rhythm, for example ECM–receptor interaction, Protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, and Circadian rhythm.

In total, 576 GO terms were enriched in the green module, including 508 BP terms, 22 CC terms, and 48 MF terms. The top 10 terms in BP, CC, and MF are shown in Fig. 3I. The significantly enriched biological processes were mainly related to extracellular matrix organization and structural components, cartilage development, mineralization and tissue repair, molecular binding and cell adhesion, material transport, and acidification. These terms include such as external encapsulating structure, wound healing, proton-transporting V-type ATPase, and cell integrin binding.

In the green module, 31 KEGG pathways were significantly enriched. The top 10 are shown in Fig. 3J, primarily associated with cell signaling, bone development, and metabolic processes. Representative pathways include PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, Proteoglycans in cancer, and Osteoclast differentiation.

Further literature review revealed that NR1D1 has been reported as a crucial regulator of the cartilage circadian clock and is closely associated with aging and osteoarthritis [19–21]. Given its pivotal role in the circadian clock and its distinct expression pattern, NR1D1 was selected for further experimental validation in an age-related TMJOA mouse model.

Discussion

Degenerative changes in the temporomandibular joint represent histological degenerative alterations, with an increasing incidence as age progresses. Under the influence of various factors, these changes can develop into temporomandibular osteoarthritis, leading to impairments in chewing, speech, and other functions.

Aging may contribute to the pathogenesis of temporomandibular joint degenerative changes through multiple pathways. Growing evidence suggests that aging, as one of the most significant risk factors, plays a key role in the pathogenesis of degenerative changes in the TMJ. It is believed that the aging process provides a “platform” for other risk factors (such as mechanical stress) to interact with age-related cartilage changes, thereby triggering the disease [22].

It is also widely accepted that with advancing age, the number of aging-related chondrocytes and synovial fibroblasts in the joint increases, accompanied by the upregulation of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). This involves the secretion of various cytokines, chemokines, and proteases [23]. The release of pro-inflammatory factors leads to alterations in the local microenvironment, resulting in a chronic low-grade inflammatory state within the joint. Additionally, an increase in the secretion of proteases, such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), can cause extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation and remodeling, which disrupts the balance between cartilage destruction and repair and leads to histological degenerative changes [24]. However, there is currently a lack of research focusing on the specific mechanisms by which aging induces degenerative changes in the TMJ.

To address this, the present study established a transcriptomic database for both aged and young mice. To control the confounding factor of sex on TMJOA pathogenesis, both groups consisted of male mice. A series of bioinformatics analyses were conducted to explore the commonalities between the aging process and degenerative changes in the temporomandibular joint, identify key genes, and validate them at the histological level.

First, WGCNA was performed on the aging dataset to obtain aging-related modules. GO and KEGG analyses were then conducted on the turquoise and green modules, which showed the strongest negative and positive correlations with aging, respectively. The results revealed that both the turquoise and green modules were enriched in processes, such as extracellular matrix (ECM) structure, ECM-receptor interaction, bone development and mineralization, and cellular biological membrane systems. These enrichment results are consistent with findings from related studies, which suggest that ECM degradation plays a crucial role in age-related degenerative changes in the temporomandibular joint [25]. Bone remodeling and catabolic-synthetic metabolism are particularly active during the aging process of the TMJ [26], while the cellular biological membrane system likely serves as a key hub in material transport, information transmission, and maintaining homeostasis within the aging TMJ.

The turquoise module, which was most strongly negatively associated with aging, was also enriched in pathways associated with circadian rhythms and apoptosis. This supports previous research linking circadian rhythm disruption to aging [21, 27]. Previous studies have suggested that diminished circadian rhythms in aged joint cartilage may lead to increased chondrocyte senescence and apoptosis [23], thereby increasing the susceptibility of joints to age-related osteoarthritis.

Notably, we also calculated the intersection of DEGs from the aging and osteoarthritis datasets, while excluding DEGs from the mechanical stimulation dataset, to obtain the overlapping DEGs. This exclusion was made because, in recent years, osteoarthritis has increasingly been regarded as a result of active biological dysregulation in cartilage and other joint tissues rather than merely a consequence of mechanical ‘wear and tear’ [28]. Therefore, to eliminate the confounding effects of cumulative mechanical loading that inevitably accompany aging and to identify genes specifically related to aging, we excluded the DEGs from the mechanical stimulation dataset. Next, the core genes from the aging-related modules identified through WGCNA were intersected with overlapping genes from three datasets, resulting in 52 TMJOA-ARDEGs. Functional enrichment analysis and PPI network analysis were then performed on the TMJOA-ARDEGs.

The functional enrichment analysis revealed that TMJOA-ARDEGs were primarily involved in processes related to inflammation and immunity, antioxidant stress, the cytoskeleton, and extracellular matrix (ECM) functions. Age-related oxidative stress stimuli continuously increase, exacerbating the accumulation of senescent cells in tissues [29]. This process is accompanied by an increase in the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which includes the upregulation of various pro-inflammatory factors and inflammation-related pathways, thereby contributing to a low-grade inflammatory environment in the joints and driving cartilage degradation. Meanwhile, immune cells are activated, participating in the regulation of the inflammatory response. The cytoskeleton not only participates in transmitting mechanical stimuli from the ECM to the cell interior but also mediates cell-environment communication and cell migration, making the integrity of the chondrocyte cytoskeleton critical for maintaining chondrocyte homeostasis. We further screened eight hub TMJOA-ARDEGs using two machine learning algorithms, LASSO and SVM-RFE. Nevertheless, given the limited number of RNA-seq datasets available for murine TMJOA, the overall sample size remains relatively small. Consequently, machine learning analyses may still be susceptible to overfitting and feature selection instability. Future studies should address these limitations by increasing the sample size or integrating multiple emerging independent datasets to further validate and refine the identification of hub TMJOA-associated DEGs. These signature genes were ANK1, MELTF, FERMT3, MDFI, CXCL14, EPHA3, SMOC2, and NR1D1.

Ankyrin 1 (ANK1) is an anchoring protein that connects integrin membrane proteins to the cytoskeleton, maintaining the shape, stability, and movement of red blood cells.

Melanotransferrin (MELTF) is a glycoprotein on melanoma cells, involved in cellular iron absorption.

FERM Domain Containing Kindlin 3 ( FERMT3 ) is strongly expressed only in the hematopoietic system. Studies have reported that mutations in Kindlin-3 lead to decreased adhesion ability and increased proliferation of mesenchymal stem cells from bone marrow, resulting in enhanced cartilage formation [30] .

MyoD Family Inhibitory Factor (MDFI) is a negative regulator of the transcription factors of other myogenic family proteins.

C-X-C Motif Chemokine Ligand 14 ( CXCL14 ) is a chemokine that participates in immune surveillance by promoting the chemotaxis and migration of monocytes and NK cells. CXCL14 plays an important role in tissue repair and fibrosis. Previous studies have demonstrated a close relationship between CXCL14 and fibrosis in various tissues, including the heart, lung, and liver, particularly through the recruitment of M2-type macrophages to promote the fibrotic process [31–33]. Moreover, under external stimuli or pathological signaling, the secretion level of CXCL14 is upregulated, facilitating the accumulation of M2 macrophages to adapt to environmental changes, maintain multicellular homeostasis, or accelerate tissue fibrosis [34–36].

We hypothesize that CXCL14 may exert a similar function within the articular cartilage microenvironment. Under moderate aging-related stimulation, CXCL14 expression is upregulated, promoting macrophage chemotaxis and M2 macrophage polarization, thereby facilitating controlled fibrosis to repair articular cartilage. However, persistent and excessive external stimuli may lead to advanced over-fibrosis of hyaline cartilage, ultimately resulting in degenerative changes in joint structure. This aligns with our findings that CXCL14 expression is upregulated in both the TMJOA dataset and the aging dataset.

Ephrin Type-A Receptor 3 ( EPHA3 ), a transmembrane glycoprotein, is involved in cell–cell adhesion, signal transduction, and the regulation of cell morphology and motility. EPHA3 mediates neural system development and is associated with the proliferation and migration of various tumors. Multiple studies have shown that EPHA3 expression levels are significantly higher in patients with osteoarthritis than in control groups [37–39]. It may serve as a shared biomarker associated with both cellular senescence and osteoarthritis, with its expression positively correlated with immune-related genes—findings that are consistent with the results of the bioinformatics analysis in this study. However, the precise relationship and underlying mechanisms linking EPHA3 to aging and osteoarthritis remain to be further elucidated.

Secreted Modular Calcium-Binding Protein 2 (SMOC2) is a calcium-binding protein secreted into the extracellular matrix by chondrocytes.

Nuclear Receptor Subfamily 1 Group D Member 1 ( NR1D1 ) is a nuclear receptor transcription factor that regulates circadian rhythm and multiple metabolic processes within cells. Previous studies have shown that circadian rhythm disorders (CRD) can disrupt the internal environment and metabolic homeostasis of the body, and are considered one of the factors that induce and accelerate osteoarthritis (OA) [40]. Enhancing the circadian rhythm through mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-based therapy has been reported to mitigate the severity of age-related TMJOA [27] .

NR1D1 functions as a negative feedback regulator in the circadian rhythm loop by binding to ROREs (REV-ERB response elements) and repressing BMAL1 transcription, thereby fine-tuning rhythmic regulation [21]. Through this mechanism, NR1D1 controls chondrocyte metabolism and inflammation, maintaining cartilage homeostasis. Disruption of this regulatory circuit could therefore lead to chondrocyte dysfunction and cartilage degradation, key hallmarks of OA.

We systematically examined NR1D1 expression across three datasets (the TMJOA dataset, the aging dataset, and the validation experiment) to explore the possibility of NR1D1 acting as a candidate molecular marker, driven by its differential expression patterns in joint degeneration.

In the TMJOA dataset, NR1D1 expression was downregulated in the partial discectomy surgery-induced TMJOA mice compared to healthy mice. This result is consistent with previous studies conducted in the partial discectomy surgery-induced TMJOA mice model. Gossan et al. found that NR1D1 and BMAL1 expression levels were downregulated in surgical partial discectomy OA mice compared to healthy mice [41]. Akagi et al. also observed that NR1D1 expression was primarily concentrated in the superficial and upper-middle layers of the cartilage [42]. This downregulation may reflect the acute mechanical injury sustained by the TMJOA model following partial discectomy, manifesting as the destabilization of the chondrocyte molecular clock and the simultaneous transcriptional repression of core circadian genes, such as NR1D1 and BMAL1.

In the aging dataset, NR1D1 expression was upregulated in aging mice compared to healthy controls. Studies have shown that with increasing age, the amplitude of the circadian rhythm clock generally diminishes, and phase shifts occur. The expression and rhythmicity of core clock genes, such as BMAL1, which is negatively regulated by NR1D1, may weaken with age [43]. Notably, in aging organisms, the expression of NR1D1 is modulated not only by the circadian rhythm but also potentially by processes such as chronic inflammation, mechanical stimulation, and metabolic stress that accumulate during senescence [44]. Consequently, the elevation of NR1D1 in aging mice may represent an adaptive upregulation driven by multiple aging-related physiological stressors.

To validate these findings within a clinically relevant context, we employed an age-related TMJOA model in our validation experiment that recapitulates the natural progression of the human disease [45]. By comparing condylar samples from the OA group and the healthy control group in naturally aged mice, we were able to account for the systemic effects of aging and determine whether the alterations in NR1D1 are OA-specific. The validation results demonstrated that NR1D1 expression was significantly upregulated in age-related TMJOA mice compared to their healthy aged counterparts. This result is consistent with previous studies, where NR1D1 expression was upregulated in human aging TMJOA (age-related TMJOA) samples. Chaturvedi et al. found that NR1D1 expression was higher in human OA cartilage samples [46]. Liu H et al. observed weak positive staining of NR1D1 in human OA cartilage samples, and its expression was found to inhibit M1 macrophage activation, alleviating cartilage and bone damage [47]. Xu X et al. found that NR1D1 expression was upregulated in human OA samples, and its expression was mainly concentrated in the cartilage damage areas [48]. In summary, these findings demonstrate that the upregulation of NR1D1 represents a specific expression pattern characteristic of age-related TMJOA.

As previously established, circadian rhythm disruption is a critical risk factor for the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis. Various stressors, including aging and mechanical overload, can trigger peripheral circadian disturbances. These are characterized by the downregulation of BMAL1, the upregulation of NR1D1, and the dysregulation of other core clock genes, which collectively compromise articular cartilage homeostasis, accelerate matrix degradation, exacerbate inflammatory responses, and ultimately drive the progression of temporomandibular joint degeneration.

Previous research suggests that NR1D1 may contribute to circadian-driven OA through the following mechanisms: external stimuli activate the MAPK-MYC signaling pathway, inducing NR1D1 expression. This, in turn, disrupts the cartilage molecular clock and triggers chondrocyte dysfunction, thereby propelling the OA process [49]. Furthermore, while circadian disruption is typically manifested by BMAL1 deficiency, the aberrant upregulation of NR1D1 may further reinforce the repression of BMAL1, dismantling the NR1D1–BMAL1 negative feedback loop. The subsequent decline in BMAL1 levels activates the MAPK-ERK cascade, leading to elevated levels of p-ERK and IL-6, which induce OA-like pathological alterations [50].

Although the framework discussed above offers a theoretical foundation for NR1D1’s role in TMJOA, our study only demonstrates an association between its expression and the disease; it does not prove causality. It remains unclear whether NR1D1 upregulation is a signature of circadian rhythm disturbance, a driver of OA, or an adaptive response aimed at preserving cartilage stability. We intend to address this in future work using NR1D1 knockout models or pharmacological tools to pinpoint its precise function.

If NR1D1 is indeed found to drive TMJOA, targeting the circadian clock via NR1D1 could become a promising strategy to halt or reverse the disease [51]. This would provide a new direction for managing TMJ degeneration.

Methodologically, this study has limitations, particularly regarding the influence of sex-related factors. To minimize the interference of fluctuating estrogen levels on gene expression, the aging RNA-seq dataset was restricted to male mice. Although our validation experiment included both sexes with a balanced distribution between the OA and non-OA groups—thereby partially mitigating confounding effects—the established role of estrogen in OA pathogenesis cannot be ignored [50]. Future studies with larger sample sizes and sex-stratified analyses are warranted to determine whether sex differences exist in NR1D1 expression patterns and to explore the potential interactions between sex hormones, circadian rhythms, and cartilage degeneration.

This study utilized bioinformatics analysis and machine learning methods to identify characteristic genes related to age-associated degenerative changes in the temporomandibular joint. CXCL14, FERMT3, ANK1, MDFI, MELTF, SMOC2, NR1D1, and EPHA3 were identified as candidate genes associated with both aging and TMJ degenerative changes.

Among these, as a negative feedback regulator in the maintenance and balance of the cartilage clock, NR1D1’s expression was significantly upregulated in the condyles of aged osteoarthritis mice compared to healthy aged mice (p < 0.01), making it a candidate gene related to both aging and TMJ degenerative changes.
