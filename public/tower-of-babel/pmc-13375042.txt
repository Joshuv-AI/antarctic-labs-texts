Erectile dysfunction severity and penile Doppler findings in pelvic fracture urethral injury: A descriptive cohort study
Authors: Pankaj Mangalkumar Joshi, Wattanachai Ratanapornsompong, Maria Antonia Ocampo, Jimena Navarro, Saket S. Patwardhan, Gunter DeWin, Filip Thiessen, Dan Wood, Sanjay B. Kulkarni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Erectile dysfunction (ED) is a common and multifactorial complication after pelvic fracture urethral injury (PFUI), yet objective characterization of ED severity and penile Doppler findings remains limited. We aimed to describe ED severity categories and penile Doppler findings in men with PFUI undergoing anastomotic urethroplasty.Methods:We performed a retrospective analysis of a prospectively maintained database. Among 110 men who underwent anastomotic urethroplasty for PFUI between September 2022 and September 2023, 97 with complete preoperative penile Doppler ultrasound and standardized Sexual Health Inventory for Men assessment were included. Pelvic fractures were classified using the Young–Burgess system. All patients received tadalafil 5 mg daily for 6 months. Postoperative Doppler was not performed.Results:The median age was 29.5 years with a median follow-up of 11 months. Urethroplasty success rate was 85%(83/97). Preoperatively, 56 out of 97 patients had ED (42 mild/moderate; 14 severe). Penile Doppler abnormalities were nearly universal: 93 had cavernosal, and 83 had dorsal artery insufficiency. Postoperatively, 13 out of 41 men developed de novo ED, while 13 out of 56 improved by at least one severity category. Among 76 patients with complete fracture data, greater fracture severity was associated with worse postoperative ED, and worsening erectile function was more frequent among patients with urethroplasty failure.Conclusions:ED and penile Doppler abnormalities are highly prevalent after PFUI. ED severity correlated with pelvic injury severity, supporting the concept that ED reflects global trauma burden rather than isolated vascular pathology. Given the retrospective design and routine postoperative use of PDE5 inhibitors, these findings should be considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

INTRODUCTION

Pelvic fracture urethral injury (PFUI) occurs in approximately 5%–10% of pelvic fractures and typically involves disruption at the bulbomembranous junction of the urethra.[12] Although delayed anastomotic urethroplasty provides excellent urethral patency rates in experienced centers,[34] sexual dysfunction – particularly erectile dysfunction (ED) – remains a major long-term concern for patients.

ED has been reported in approximately 5%–28% of patients after pelvic fracture alone and is substantially more common after PFUI, with reported rates ranging from approximately 26%–76%.[5678] The mechanisms underlying ED in this setting are complex and likely multifactorial, involving varying contributions from neurogenic injury, arteriogenic compromise, veno-occlusive dysfunction, corporal tissue damage, and psychogenic factors.[567] The cavernous nerves and branches of the internal pudendal artery traverse the pelvis in close proximity to the posterior urethra and pelvic ring and are therefore particularly vulnerable during high-energy pelvic trauma.[12]

From a reconstructive perspective, an additional anatomic consideration is the vascular supply of the bulbar urethra. The bulbar urethra receives antegrade blood supply from the bulbar arteries and retrograde flow from the dorsal penile arteries via the glans.[9] During anastomotic urethroplasty, transection of the bulbar urethra interrupts antegrade blood supply, rendering the distal urethra dependent on retrograde flow from the dorsal penile circulation.[10] This anatomic principle has generated interest in penile vascular status in patients with PFUI, although the true clinical significance of specific arterial abnormalities remains uncertain.

Penile duplex Doppler ultrasound is widely used to evaluate arteriogenic and venogenic causes of ED.[11] However, the applicability of standard Doppler thresholds in young trauma populations and their clinical meaning in PFUI remain unclear.[56] In particular, it is unknown how frequently Doppler abnormalities are present in PFUI when assessed systematically and how these findings relate to ED severity or the severity of pelvic injury.

The primary aim of this study was to describe ED severity categories (no ED, mild/moderate ED, severe ED) and penile Doppler findings in men with PFUI undergoing anastomotic urethroplasty. Secondary aims were to explore associations between pelvic fracture severity (Young–Burgess classification),[12] penile Doppler patterns, and postoperative ED severity categories. Given the observational design and routine postoperative tadalafil use, analyses were performed as descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and population

We performed a retrospective analysis of a prospectively maintained institutional PFUI database. Between September 2022 and September 2023, 110 men underwent anastomotic urethroplasty for PFUI at our center. Among these, 97 patients had complete preoperative penile Doppler ultrasound assessment and standardized erectile function categorization and were included.

Approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (UROKUL/22/003). Informed consent was obtained from all patients recruited to the study, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The authors confirm the availability of, and access to, all the original data reported in this study.

Erectile function assessment

Erectile function was assessed using the Sexual Health Inventory for Men (SHIM)[13], a validated 5-item questionnaire with scores ranging from 1 to 25, with lower scores indicating worse erectile function. For the purpose of this study, SHIM scores were categorized into three clinically relevant groups: No ED (SHIM 22–25), mild/moderate ED (SHIM 8–21), and severe ED (SHIM ≤7). Preoperative SHIM assessment was performed prior to surgery, and postoperative SHIM assessment was obtained at the most recent follow-up visit (median follow-up 11 months [Interquartile Range (IQR) 7–18]) while patients were receiving tadalafil as part of the institutional penile rehabilitation protocol. These categories were used for descriptive and exploratory analyses rather than as continuous variables.

Penile Doppler ultrasound

Color penile duplex Doppler ultrasound was performed in all patients prior to surgery according to a standardized protocol.[11] After intracavernosal injection of 1 mL papaverine (equivalent to 30 mg), peak systolic velocity (PSV) of the cavernosal and dorsal penile arteries was measured bilaterally. Based on established ED criteria, cavernosal arterial inflow was classified as normal or abnormal using a PSV cutoff of 25 cm/s, with values <25 cm/s considered indicative of arterial insufficiency.[11] Dorsal artery flow was similarly classified as normal or abnormal using the same PSV threshold. End-diastolic velocity (EDV) was recorded, and venous leak was assessed using standard criteria. Venous leak was defined as EDV >5 cm/s and recorded as binary (present or absent).

Pelvic fracture classification

Pelvic fractures were classified using the Young–Burgess system.[12]

Surgical technique

All patients underwent anastomotic urethroplasty using a progressive perineal approach as described by Webster and Ramon and later refined by Joshi et al.[49] Surgical success was defined as no need for secondary intervention.

Postoperative management

All patients were discharged on tadalafil 5 mg daily for 6 months as part of a standardized penile rehabilitation protocol.[14]

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for distribution by visual inspection of histograms and are reported as median (IQR) or mean ± standard deviation, as appropriate. Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. ED status was categorized using SHIM-based groups and analyzed primarily descriptively.

Because of the incomplete availability of pelvic fracture classification and/or postoperative erectile function data, only 76 of the 97 patients with complete data for both variables were included in the fracture association analysis. Cases with missing data were excluded from this sub-analysis, and no imputation was performed.

Pre- to postoperative changes in ED status were summarized using a 3 × 3 transition table. Exploratory comparisons across pelvic fracture patterns (Young–Burgess classification) were performed using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. No multivariable or predictive analyses were planned because of the retrospective design, limited subgroup sizes, and potential confounding (including routine postoperative tadalafil use and lack of postoperative Doppler assessment); therefore, all P values should be interpreted as exploratory. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed using R.

The erectile function category changed substantially over the follow-up period. Among 41 patients with normal erectile function preoperatively, 28 (68.3%) remained in the no-ED category, while 13 (31.7%) developed de novo ED, including 3 (7.3%) who developed mild/moderate ED and 10 (24.4%) who developed severe ED. Among 42 patients with preoperative mild/moderate ED, 11 (26.2%) improved to no ED, 10 (23.8%) remained in the same category, and 21 (50.0%) worsened to severe ED. Among 14 patients with preoperative severe ED, 4 (28.6%) improved by at least one category (2 to no ED and 2 to mild/moderate ED), whereas 10 (71.4%) remained in the severe ED category.

Overall, improvement in ED severity category occurred in 15 of 97 patients (15.5%), worsening occurred in 34 of 97 patients (35.1%), and 48 of 97 patients (49.5%) remained in the same severity category. When categories were collapsed into ED versus no ED, 13 patients with normal preoperative erectile function developed de novo ED, while 13 patients with preoperative ED improved to the no-ED category, resulting in no net change in the overall prevalence of ED despite substantial individual-level transitions. DISCUSSION

This study provides a detailed descriptive overview of ED severity patterns and penile Doppler findings in a relatively large single-center cohort of men with PFUI.

ED was highly prevalent and dynamic in this cohort. Approximately one-third of men with normal preoperative erectile function developed de novo ED, while nearly one-quarter of those with preoperative ED improved by at least one severity category. These findings are consistent with prior reports demonstrating wide variability in erectile outcomes after PFUI.[5678] Because all patients in the present study received tadalafil as part of a standardized penile rehabilitation protocol, the observed postoperative erectile function categories represent erectile function under pharmacologic support rather than the untreated natural history.

Penile Doppler abnormalities were almost universal in this cohort. This contrasts with the findings of El-Assmy et al., who reported a predominance of veno-occlusive dysfunction with relatively fewer cases of isolated arterial insufficiency,[5] and Guan et al., who observed abnormal Doppler findings in approximately 30% of their selected cohort.[6] The much higher prevalence of Doppler abnormalities in the present study likely reflects differences in patient selection, as Doppler ultrasound was performed routinely in all patients rather than selectively in those with abnormal screening tests.

In general ED populations, penile Doppler ultrasound is interpreted using established hemodynamic thresholds: PSV values ≥30–35 cm/s indicate adequate arterial inflow, borderline values are approximately 25–30 cm/s, and PSV <25 cm/s suggests arterial insufficiency; EDV <5 cm/s typically excludes significant venous leak, and resistive index values >0.8–0.9 indicate intact veno-occlusive function.[11] Because arterial abnormalities were nearly universal in this cohort, we analyzed Doppler findings using a binary abnormal-versus-normal classification rather than PSV severity grading. In contrast, in the present PFUI cohort, nearly all patients were classified as abnormal using these criteria, suggesting that standard Doppler thresholds derived from nontrauma ED populations may not effectively discriminate vascular status in trauma-related ED. It is plausible that these thresholds overclassify vascular insufficiency in PFUI, where microvascular injury, corporal tissue damage, and neurogenic factors coexist.[567] Although venous leak was present in approximately one-quarter of patients, arterial abnormalities were nearly universal and dominated the Doppler findings, limiting the discriminatory value of Doppler for stratifying ED severity in this cohort. Therefore, Doppler findings in this cohort should be interpreted as physiologic descriptors rather than diagnostic or prognostic markers.

From an anatomic and reconstructive standpoint, the interest in penile vascular status in PFUI is understandable. The bulbar urethra becomes dependent on retrograde flow from the dorsal penile circulation after transection during anastomotic urethroplasty.[10] However, the present data suggest that vascular abnormalities are so prevalent in PFUI that it is unlikely that any single arterial parameter can adequately explain the wide spectrum of ED observed. Rather, these findings support the concept that ED after PFUI is fundamentally multifactorial, reflecting combined neurogenic, vascular, and corporal tissue injury.[1567]

In our cohort, successful reconstruction rarely led to meaningful recovery of erectile function, whereas worsening of erectile function was more commonly seen in patients who experienced urethroplasty failure. This fits with prior work showing that ED after PFUI is usually multifactorial, reflecting a combination of neurogenic, vascular, and corporal tissue injury rather than a single correctable anatomic problem.[15] It is plausible that persistent or recurrent obstruction further stresses an already injured neurovascular system and contributes to additional functional decline. That said, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as our analysis is unadjusted and exploratory, and the higher rate of deterioration in the failure group may simply reflect greater baseline injury severity and surgical complexity rather than a direct effect of surgical outcome itself.

We also observed that ED severity appeared to correlate with overall pelvic injury severity as classified by the Young–Burgess system. This supports the concept that ED in PFUI is primarily a marker of the global burden of pelvic trauma rather than a complication attributable to a single anatomic or surgical factor.[12] More severe fracture patterns are likely to result in more extensive combined neurogenic, microvascular, and corporal tissue damage, leading to more severe and persistent ED. Importantly, this observation should be interpreted cautiously, as it is based on unadjusted, exploratory analysis.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design limits control over confounding and precludes causal inference. Erectile function was analyzed using severity categories rather than continuous SHIM scores, and all patients received tadalafil, so postoperative SHIM reflects erectile function under pharmacologic support rather than untreated function. Postoperative Doppler was not performed. Although EDV was recorded and venous leak was assessed in a binary fashion, detailed veno-occlusive and neurogenic evaluation was not performed. No multivariable adjustment was made for potential confounders such as age, smoking, comorbidities, pelvic trauma severity, surgical complexity, or prior urethral interventions. Finally, the median follow-up of 11 months is relatively short for PFUI and mainly reflects short-term outcomes.

Accordingly, these results should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating. Future prospective studies with longer follow-up and comprehensive neurovascular assessment are required. Further work is needed to determine whether preoperative penile Doppler parameters can meaningfully predict functional and reconstructive outcomes after PFUI repair, stratify patients by risk, guide surgical planning, and identify patients at increased risk of ischemic complications, such as bulbar urethral necrosis.

CONCLUSIONS

In this cohort of 97 men with PFUI, ED and penile Doppler abnormalities were highly prevalent, and ED severity appeared to track with overall pelvic injury severity, supporting the concept that ED after PFUI reflects global trauma burden rather than isolated vascular pathology. These descriptive findings reinforce the multifactorial nature of ED after PFUI.
