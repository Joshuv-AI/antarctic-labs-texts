Introduction

The structure and richness of distance sets arising from subsets of metric spaces has been a focal point of investigation in geometric measure theory and harmonic analysis. Understanding distance sets has broad implications in analyzing geometric configurations, understanding patterns in large data sets, and studying the structure of fractals. A fundamental question, referred to as Falconer’s distance problem, asks: for what
s
must a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
of Hausdorff dimension
s
ensure that the set
\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(E)
, of all Euclidean distances between points in
E
, has positive Lebesgue measure? This problem remains largely open, with known partial results improving incrementally over time. The behavior of analogous distance set problems in non-Euclidean settings remains comparatively underexplored (see for example,
[
4
,
17
,
13
,
16
]
and references within for non-Euclidean set up other than Heisenberg group). It is quite interesting to ask for an analogue of Falconer distance set conjecture in the Heisenberg group set-up, given its foundational importance and the rich interplay with the geometry of sets.

The basic tools to address distance set problems in Euclidean setup are, primarily, that the energy integral (see
[
20
]
for further details) of the sparse sets can be written in terms of the Fourier transform of the measures supported on these sets, and secondarily, the pointwise Fourier decay of the surface measure of the Euclidean sphere. It does not seem to be easy to observe any analogue of the energy integral in terms of the group Fourier transform of generic measures in the Heisenberg group and it is of future interest to the authors. Nevertheless in this article we attempt to state an analogue of the secondary tool, that is to find the right “decay” of the coefficients in the spectral decomposition of the group Fourier transfrom of the surface measure on the Korányi sphere in the Heisenberg group (see
Lemma

1.4
).

In line with earlier studies focusing on Kakeya-type or Besicovitch-type problems or other geometric measure theoretic problems in the Heisenberg group (see
[
3
,
15
,
2
,
7
,
9
]
and references within), this article aims to initiate the study of distance set problems in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. We believe this will open pathways for further quantitative and structural investigations in the Heisenberg group set-up.

The classical theorem of Steinhaus
[
26
]
on the Euclidean space,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, states that if
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
has positive Lebesgue measure, then the set
\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(E)
has non-empty interior. Steinhaus’ theorem has led to a substantial development in a vast array of subsequent work. In particular, analyzing the distance sets
\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(E)
and other configuration sets of a similar nature involving generalized distance functions in the literature (see
[
21
,
20
,
18
]
and references within). The generalized version of the Steinhaus’ Theorem for any locally compact group

with identity

and a left Haar measure

in
[
27
]
states that
“If

, then the set

has

In the Euclidean case, the quantitative version of Steinhaus’ theorem was not evident from the proof in
[

and was studied in
[

The existence of sufficiently large distances in the Euclidean sets of positive upper density was studied independently by Bourgain
[
6
]
, Falconer et al

]
and Furstenberg, et al

Theorem A

(
[

,
8
,

]
)
.

Suppose

, and that
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
has positive upper density, that is,

(1.1)

\limsup_{R\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\lambda_{d}(A\cap B(x,R))}{\lambda_{d}(B(x,R))}>0,

where
B(x,R)
denotes a ball of radius
R
centred at
x
and
\lambda_{d}
denotes the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. Then the set
A
contains all sufficiently large distances; namely, there exists
R_{0}=R_{0}(A)>0
with the property that for every
R\geq R_{0}
, one can find
x,y\in A
with
|x-y|=R
. Here,
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm.

In this article, our main goal is to find an anologue of
A
in the Heisenberg group set-up. Recall that the Heisenberg group,
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, is the two step nilpotent Lie group with underlying manifold
\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
associated with the group law

(1.2)

(z,t)\cdot(w,s):=\left(z+w,t+s+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}(z.\overline{w})\right),\ \ \text{for all}\ \ (z,t),(w,s)\in\mathbb{H}^{n},

where
z.\overline{w}=z_{1}\overline{w_{1}}+\cdots+z_{n}\overline{w_{n}}
, for any
z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We have a family of non-isotropic dilations defined by
\delta_{r}(z,t):=(rz,r^{2}t)
, for all
(z,t)\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
, for every
r>0
. The Korányi norm of
(z,t)
in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is given by

(1.3)

|(z,t)|_{K}:=\left(|z|^{4}+t^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}.

The Korányi norm is homogeneous of degree 1, that is
|\delta_{r}(z,t)|_{K}=r\,|(z,t)|_{K}
. The distance between two points
x
and
y
in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
will be given by the left invariant metric defined by
d_{K}(x,y)=|y^{-1}\cdot x|_{K}
.
The Haar measure on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is given by the Lebesgue measure
dz\ dt
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}
. The Korányi ball,
B(a,r)
, of radius

centered at
a\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
is
a\cdot B(0,r)=\{x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}:|a^{-1}\cdot x|_{K}<r\}
. One has its measure
|B(a,r)|=C_{Q}\,r^{Q}
, where
Q=(2n+2)
is known as the homogeneous dimension of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Let
\Delta(E)=\{d_{K}(x,y):x,y\in E\}
denote the distance set of
E\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

We define
Korányi upper density
of a set
A\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
as

(1.4)

\limsup_{R\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}}\frac{|A\cap B(x,R)|}{|B(x,R)|}.

The main result of this paper is to show that, in the Heisenberg group, the set of positive Korányi upper density admits large enough distances:

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
n\geq 1
, and that
A\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}
has positive Korányi upper density. Then the set
A
contains all sufficiently large distances; namely, there exists
R_{0}=R_{0}(A)>0
with the property that for every
R\geq R_{0}
, one can find
x,y\in A
with
d_{K}(x,y)=|x\cdot y^{-1}|_{K}=R
.

Finally, as a form of sharpness of the main
Theorem

1.1
, we establish that the Korányi upper density in the above theorem cannot be replaced by a slower decaying density by constructing an example (see
Section

6.2
). This example is similar to the Euclidean one constructed in
[
24
]
for the Theorem
A
.

Proposition 1.2
.

Suppose
f:(0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{[}0,1]
such that
\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}f(R)=0
. There exists
A\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
and a sequence of
R_{m}\rightarrow\infty
such that

(1)

\frac{|A\cap B(0,R_{m})|}{|B(0,R_{m})|}\geq f(R_{m}),
for all
m=1,2,3,\dots

(2)

|x-y|_{K}\neq R_{m},
for all

and for all
m=1,2,3,\dots
.

1.1.
On Proof techniques

The study of spherical averaging operators and their associated Fourier transforms has played a central role in Euclidean harmonic analysis and geometric measure theory. A foundational object in this context is the surface measure on the Euclidean sphere, whose Fourier transform exhibits pointwise asymptotic decay that gives a wide range of results, including restriction estimates, distance set problems, and dispersive PDE estimates. The pointwise asymptotics of Fourier transform of the surface measure on the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are fundamental (see
[
25
]
) for understanding the fine-scale structure of convolution operators, oscillatory integrals, and in particular, is the main ingredient of the Fourier analytic proof of Theorem
A
by Bourgain in
[
6
]
.

A natural analogue of the Euclidean spherical surface measure in the Heisenberg group set up is the surface measure supported on the unit Korányi Sphere,
S_{K}(0,1)
, which is defined as

(1.5)

S_{K}(0,1):=\{(z,t)\in\mathbb{H}^{n}:|(z,t)|_{K}=1\}.

Let
\sigma
denote the surface measure on
S_{K}(0,1)
, normalised to have unit mass, and let
R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)
be the coefficients in the spectral decomposition of the group Fourier transform of
\sigma
on the Korányi sphere in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
:
\hat{\sigma}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)\mathcal{P}_{k}(\lambda)
. We refer the reader to
Section

2
for a precise definition and reminder of their basic properties. While previous works have explored various important analytic tools in Heisenberg group, the precise “decay” of the coefficients
R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)
in the spectral decomposition of the group Fourier transform of the surface measure on the Korányi sphere—that is, the analogue of the Euclidean stationary phase analysis—has not been fully characterized to the authors’ best knowledge. Hence it is also of an independent interest to study
R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)
. The novelty of the present article lies in providing the right “decay” of these coefficients
R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)
(see Lemma
1.4
), thus mirroring the Euclidean setup.

1.2.
Sketch of the proof of the main theorem

Following Bourgain
[
6
]
, the proof of our main
Theorem

1.1
is obtained by first establishing the following quantitative analogue in the Heisenberg group.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
0<\epsilon<\frac{1}{2}
, let
E\subset B(0,1)\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
have measure
|E|\geq\epsilon
, and let
J=J(\epsilon)
be a sufficiently large natural number depending on
\epsilon
. Suppose that
0<r_{J}<\cdots<r_{1}\leq 1
are a sequence of scales with
r_{j+1}\leq r_{j}/2
for all
1\leq j<J
. Then for at least one
1\leq j\leq J
, one has

(1.6)

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\int_{S_{K}(0,1)}1_{E}(x)1_{E}(x\cdot\delta_{r_{j}}y)d\sigma(y)\,dx\gtrsim\epsilon^{2},

where,
d\sigma
is the surface measure on the unit Korányi sphere
S_{K}(0,1)
, normalised to have unit mass.
2
2

2

See
Section

1.4
for our conventions on asymptotic notation.

Intuitively, the reason for this quantitative result, as in the Euclidean case
[
6
]
, is based on Fourier analytic techniques. Thus, from Fourier inversion, we write left side of (
1.6
) in terms of group Fourier transforms
\widehat{1_{E}}
of the function
f:=1_{E}
, the indicator function of the set

, and
\widehat{\sigma}
that of the Korányi surface measure
\sigma
as

(1.7)

(2\pi)^{-n-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}R_{k}(\lambda r_{j}^{2},\sigma)\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda,

which we decompose into “low”, “middle” and “high frequencies” determined by the size of
2(2k+n)\,|\lambda|r_{j}^{2}
. Here,
R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)
are the coefficients in the spectral decomposition

\widehat{\sigma}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)\mathcal{P}_{k}(\lambda),

(see Subsection
2.3

R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma):=c_{n}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}L_{k}^{n-1}\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}|\lambda|\cos{\theta}\right)\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}|\lambda|\cos{\theta}}e^{i\frac{1}{4}\lambda\sin{\theta}}(\cos{\theta})^{n-1}d\theta.

For the contribution of the low frequencies to (
1.7
), the factor
R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)
being close to
1
(See
Lemma

4.1
) so by relating the expression
\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle
to the
L^{2}
-norm of the twisted convolution of
f^{\lambda}
(the Euclidean Fourier transform of
f
in the last variable, see (
2.1
)) with the Laguerre functions, we will obtain the lower bound to this quantity that is
\gtrsim|E|^{2}\geq\epsilon^{2}
if

for some small enough
\delta
, see
Lemma

4.2
. The contribution of the middle frequencies (that is, when

To show that the contribution of high frequencies to the integral (
1.7

R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)
, for high frequencies, that is when
2(2k+n)|\lambda|
large. The key novelty of this paper is that we could manage to find the right “decay” of these coefficient of

that works for
\mathbb{H}^{n}

\mu=2(2k+n)

Lemma 1.4
.

Let
n\geq 1
. For
\mu|\lambda|\gg 1
, one has

(1.8)

|R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)|\lesssim(|\lambda|\mu)^{-(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{4})}.

This “decay” of these coefficients,

, seems to be new in the literature. Roughly speaking, our approach to achieve it involves first splitting the integral for

into the integral over the region where the phase

one can discard the factor

and estimate the rest part of the integral by employing the delicate asymptotics of the Laguerre polynomials to deal with the further oscillations present due to the factor

Section

5

2.
Preliminaries

As our approach is to use Fourier analytic tools (especially in proving the quantitative version of our main theorem) so we gather here all the material relevant for analysis on the Heisenberg group. In particular, we need to recall the facts about Schrödinger representations, Weyl transform, Hermite and special Hermite operators and Laguerre functions.

2.1.
Fourier transform on the Heisenberg group

Let us first briefly recall the unitary representation theory of the Heisenberg group,
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. For our purposes, it will be more convenient to work
with Schrödinger representations. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{*}:=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, the
Schrödinger representation

\pi_{\lambda}
acts on the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as follows:

\pi_{\lambda}(z,t)\varphi(\xi)=e^{i\lambda t}e^{i(x\cdot\xi+\frac{1}{2}x\cdot y)}\varphi(\xi+y),\,\,\,

where
z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. One can easily check that these representations are irreducible and unitary. Ignoring the finite dimensional representations of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
(as they do not contribute to the Plancherel measure) one has that, up to unitary equivalence, these
\pi_{\lambda}
accounts for all irreducible unitary representations of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
which are nontrivial at the center of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, thanks to a theorem of Stone-von Neumann. For a proof we refer to the monograph of Folland
[
11
]
. In accordance with general theory, these unitary representations
\pi_{\lambda}
give rise to a group Fourier transform on
L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
. Given
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
its group Fourier transform is an operator valued function on
\mathbb{R}^{*}
obtained by integrating
f
against
\pi_{\lambda}
:

\hat{f}(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}f(z,t)\pi_{\lambda}(z,t)dzdt.

Clearly,
\hat{f}(\lambda)
is a bounded linear operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
Let
f^{\lambda}(z)
stands for Euclidean inverse Fourier transform of
f
in the last variable
t
:

(2.1)

f^{\lambda}(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(z,t)e^{i\lambda t}dt,

then one can easily see that

(2.2)

\widehat{f}(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}f^{\lambda}(z)\pi_{\lambda}(z,0)dz.

The operator which takes a function
g
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
into the operator

\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}g(z)\pi_{\lambda}(z,0)dz

is called the
Weyl transform
of
g
and is denoted by
W_{\lambda}(g)
. Thus,

(2.3)

\widehat{f}(\lambda)=W_{\lambda}(f^{\lambda}).

From the explicit formula for the kernel of the Weyl tranform and the Plancherel theorem over
\mathbb{R}
with respect to the last variable, one has that for
g\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
its Weyl tranform
W_{\lambda}(g)
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator satisfying

(2.4)

\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}|g(z)|^{2}dz=(2\pi)^{-n}|\lambda|^{n}\,\|W_{\lambda}(g)\|_{\text{HS}}^{2}.

This immediately leads to the the following Plancherel formula:
for
f\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
its Fourier transform is in fact a Hilbert-Schmidt operator and one has

(2.5)

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|f(z,t)|^{2}dzdt=(2\pi)^{-n-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|\hat{f}(\lambda)\|_{HS}^{2}|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda.

Thus, the Fourier transform extends to be a unitary operator between
L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
and the Hilbert space of Hilbert-Schmidt operator valued functions on
\mathbb{R}
which are square integrable with respect to the Plancherel measure,
(2\pi)^{-n-1}|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda.

Taking the inverse Fourier transform in the central variable, that is to say (
2.1
), is an important tool which is quite often employed in studying problems on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. For instance, it converts the group convolution on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
into the so-called
twisted convolution
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Let us recall that the convolution of two absolutely integrable functions
f,g
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is defined in the usual manner as

f*g(x):=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}f(xy^{-1})g(y)dy,\quad x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}.

Writing in coordinates
(z,t)\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
and taking inverse Fourier transform with respect to the central variable
t
, it can be shown that

(f*g)^{\lambda}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}f^{\lambda}(z-w)g^{\lambda}(w)e^{i\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}(z.\overline{w})}dw.

Motivated by this, one defines the
\lambda
-twisted convolution of two absolutely integrable functions
f,g
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
to be

f\ast_{\lambda}g(z):=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}f(z-w)g(w)e^{i\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}(z.\overline{w})}dw.

From the definitions above, it follows that the identity
\widehat{f*g}(\lambda)=\widehat{f}(\lambda)\widehat{g}(\lambda)
yields the relation

(2.6)

W_{\lambda}(f^{\lambda}*_{\lambda}g^{\lambda})=W_{\lambda}(f^{\lambda})W_{\lambda}(g^{\lambda}).

2.2.
Hermite functions and the Heisenberg group

Let
\mathbb{N}_{0}
denotes the set of all nonnegative integers. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the Hermite polynomial
H_{k}(t)
on
\mathbb{R}
is defined by Rodrigues’ formula
H_{k}(t)=(-1)^{k}e^{t^{2}}{d^{k}\over dt^{k}}\big(e^{-t^{2}}\big)
, and the
L^{2}
-normalized Hermite functions

h_{k}(t):=(2^{k}k!\sqrt{\pi})^{-1/2}H_{k}(t)e^{-t^{2}/2}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
form an orthonormal basis
of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Whereas, in higher dimensions
the
n
-dimensional Hermite functions are given by the tensor products of
h_{k}
:
\Phi_{\alpha}(x):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}}(x_{i}),\quad\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})\ {\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}.}

The set
\{\Phi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}}
forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and the functions
\Phi_{\alpha}
are eigenfunctions for the Hermite operator with eigenvalue
2|\alpha|+n
where
|\alpha|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}
.
Thus, for every
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we have the Hermite expansion

\varphi=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}}\langle\varphi,\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle\Phi_{\alpha}=\sum_{k\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}+d}\mathcal{P}_{k}\varphi,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the inner product in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mathcal{P}_{k}
denotes the Hermite spectral projection given by
\mathcal{P}_{k}f:=\sum_{2|\alpha|+n=k}\langle f,\Phi_{\alpha}\rangle\Phi_{\alpha}
, see for instance
[
29
, Chapter 1.4]
.

Since we will be working on the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}\equiv\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
where we employ the trick of taking inverse Fourier transform in the central variable to reduce the matters to
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, so for us it more suited to consider the scaled Hermite functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. So, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
and each
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
, we introduce the family of scaled Hermite functions

\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}(x):=|\lambda|^{\frac{n}{4}}\Phi_{\alpha}(\sqrt{|\lambda|}\,x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

As these
\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}
also forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, so expressing Hilbert Schmidt norm of
\widehat{f}(\lambda)
in this basis one gets

\|\widehat{f}(\lambda)\|_{\operatorname{HS}}^{2}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\|\widehat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}

and hence, by (
2.5
), the Plancherel formula can be written as

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}|f(z,w)|^{2}\,dz\,dw=\frac{2^{n-1}}{\pi^{n+1}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Big(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\|\widehat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}\Big)|\lambda|^{n}\,d\lambda.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{k}(\lambda)
denotes the orthogonal projection
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
onto the finite dimensional subspace spanned by
\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}
, with
|\alpha|=k
:
\mathcal{P}_{k}(\lambda)\varphi=\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\varphi,\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}
. Then
\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}
are eigen functions of the scaled Hermite operators
H(\lambda):=-\Delta+|\lambda|^{2}|x|^{2}
, indexed by
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
, whose spectral decomposition is given by
H(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2k+n)|\lambda|\mathcal{P}_{k}(\lambda)
.

If a function
f
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is radial , that is,
f(z,t)=f_{0}(|z|,t)
, for some
f_{0}
defined on
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
, then its Fourier transform
\widehat{f}(\lambda)
is a function of the Hermite operator
H(\lambda)
. To see this one need to bring in the Laguerre functions. Consider the Laguerre functions of type
n-1
defined on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
as

(2.7)

\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}(z):=L_{k}^{n-1}\Big({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}|\lambda||z|^{2}\Big)e^{-\frac{1}{4}|\lambda||z|^{2}}.

Here,
L_{k}^{n-1}
is the
k
-th Laguerre polynomials of type
(n-1)
. In general, for
\beta>-1
the Laguerre polynomials of type
\beta
are given by the Rodrigues’ formula

e^{-r}r^{-\beta}L_{k}^{\beta}(r):=\frac{1}{k!}\frac{d^{k}}{dr^{k}}(e^{-r}r^{k+\beta}),

where,
r>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The collection
\{\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
forms an orthogonal basis for the subspace consisting of the (usual) radial functions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n}).

As can be seen from
[
30
]
, for any
L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
-function
g
one has the following compact form of the so-called special Hermite expansion
g(z)=(2\pi)^{-n}|\lambda|^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}g*_{\lambda}\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}(z).
The connection between the Hermite projections
\mathcal{P}_{k}(\lambda)
and the Laguerre functions
\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}
, via the Weyl transform, is given by the following important formula

(2.8)

W_{\lambda}(\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1})=(2\pi)^{n}|\lambda|^{-n}\mathcal{P}_{k}(\lambda).

For any function
f
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, we have the expansion
f^{\lambda}(z)=(2\pi)^{-n}|\lambda|^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f^{\lambda}*_{\lambda}\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}(z)

which, when specialised to radial
f
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, reduces to simply a Laguerre expansion.

f^{\lambda}(z)=(2\pi)^{-n}|\lambda|^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{c}_{k}(\lambda,f)\,\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}(z).

Here,
\mathfrak{c}_{k}(\lambda,f)
are the Laguerre coefficients of the radial function
f^{\lambda}
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
given by

\mathfrak{c}_{k}(\lambda,f)=|\lambda|^{n/2}\,\frac{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}f^{\lambda}(z)\,\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}(z)dz.

Thus, for radial
f
on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, the above Weyl transform argument and the identity (
2.8
) yields

\widehat{f}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathfrak{c}_{k}(\lambda,f)\,\mathcal{P}_{k}(\lambda).

We record the following useful identity we will need for lower bounding the low frequency part of (
1.7
) : For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})
one has

(2.9)

\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\widehat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\widehat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle=(2\pi)^{-n}|\lambda|^{n}\,\|f^{\lambda}\ast_{\lambda}\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}\|_{2}^{2},

which is a consequence of (
2.6
), (
2.4
) and (
2.8
). We refer the reader to
[
29
]
or
[
30
]
for a more detailed treatment of these topics.

2.3.
Fourier transforms of radial measures

Let
\nu
be a finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. The Fourier transform of such
\nu
are defined similarly:

\widehat{\nu}(\lambda):=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\pi_{\lambda}(z,t)\,d\nu(z,t),

which is again a bounded linear operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. If the measure
\nu
has more symmetry then one can give good description of its Fourier transform. Consider the natural action of the unitary group,
U(n)
, on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
given by
\bm{k}\cdot(z,t):=(\bm{k}\cdot z,t)
,
\bm{k}\in U(n)
. This induces an action on functions and measures on the Heisenberg group. We say that a measure
\nu
is radial if it is invariant under the action of
U(n)
. Similar to the case of radial functions, if a finite Borel measure
\nu
is also radial then it turns out that its Fourier transform
\hat{\nu}(\lambda)
is a function of scaled Hermite operator
H(\lambda)=-\Delta+\lambda^{2}|x|^{2}
. If
H(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2k+n)|\lambda|\mathcal{P}_{k}(\lambda)
stands for the spectral decomposition of this operator, then for a radial measure
\nu
we have

\widehat{\nu}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}R_{k}(\lambda,\nu)\mathcal{P}_{k}(\lambda).

Here,
\mathcal{P}_{k}(\lambda)
are the orthogonal projection of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
onto the
k^{th}
eigenspace spanned by scaled Hermite functions
\Phi^{\lambda}_{\alpha}
for
|\alpha|=k
. The coefficients
R_{k}(\lambda,\nu)
are explicitly given by

R_{k}(\lambda,\nu)=\frac{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}e^{i\lambda t}\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}(z)\,d\nu(z,t).

Recall that there exist a unique Radon measure
\sigma
, the surface measure, on
S_{K}(0,1)
such that for every integrable function on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
we have

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}f(z,t)dz\,dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{S_{K}(0,1)}f(\delta_{r}y)r^{Q-1}d\sigma(y)dr.

For
r>0
, one defines the
r
-dilate,
\sigma_{r}
, of
\sigma
by

\langle\sigma_{r},\phi\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}\phi(\delta_{r}(x))dx,\ \ \ \phi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

Then we have
\widehat{\sigma_{r}}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma_{r})\mathcal{P}_{k}(\lambda)
, where

(2.10)

\begin{split}R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma_{r})&=\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{n+1}{2}\big)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\big(\frac{n}{2}\big)}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}(r\sqrt{\cos\theta})e^{i\lambda\frac{1}{4}r^{2}\sin\theta}\,\,\,(\cos\theta)^{n-1}d\theta\\
&=c_{n}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}L_{k}^{n-1}\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}|\lambda|r^{2}\cos{\theta}\right)\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}|\lambda|r^{2}\cos{\theta}}e^{i\frac{1}{4}\lambda r^{2}\sin{\theta}}(\cos{\theta})^{n-1}d\theta.\end{split}

The above integral cannot be evaluated in a closed form. Nevertheless, this representation of
R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma_{r})
will be used extensively throughout the paper. In some earlier work of Fischer
[
10
]
, this is used to study the spherical means
f\ast\sigma_{r}
and corresponding spherical maximal function.

2.4.
Asymptotic of Laguerre polynomials

To study the behavior of the coefficients
R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma_{r})
given by (
2.10
) one require the precise asymptotic expression of Laguerre polynomials
L_{k}^{n-1}
. In fact, there is a large body of literature concerning the asymptotic behavior of the Laguerre polynomials. However, for our purpose we will repeatedly use the asymptotic given below in
Lemma

2.2
which is based on the following relatively simple asymptotic formula for Laguerre polynomial due to Erdélyi.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
1
]
, p. 697)
.

Let
\alpha\geq 0
.

(
i
)

Given
0<b<1
, there exists
k_{0}
such that if
k\geq k_{0}
and
0\leq x\leq b\,\nu
, then

(2.11)

\begin{split}L^{\alpha}_{k}(x)&=\frac{\Gamma(k+\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(k+1)}2^{\alpha-1/2}\nu^{(1-\alpha)/2}\,x^{-(\alpha+1)/2}\\
&\cdot e^{x/2}\left(\frac{\psi(\frac{x}{\nu})}{\psi^{\prime}(\frac{x}{\nu})}\right)^{1/2}\left[J_{\alpha}\left(\nu\,\psi(\textstyle\frac{x}{\nu})\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{x}{\nu-x}}\,\widetilde{J_{\alpha}}\left(\nu\,\psi(\textstyle\frac{x}{\nu})\right)\right)\right].\end{split}

(
ii
)

Given
a>0
, there exists
k_{0}
such that if
k\geq k_{0}
and
x\geq a\,\nu
, then

(2.12)

\begin{split}L^{\alpha}_{k}(x)&=\frac{(-1)^{k}\pi^{1/2}2^{5/6}N^{N+1/6}e^{x/2}}{\Gamma(k+1)\left(-\phi^{\prime}(\textstyle\frac{x}{\nu})\right)^{1/2}x^{(\alpha+1)/2}e^{N}}\left[\emph{Ai}\left(-\nu^{2/3}\,\phi(\textstyle\frac{x}{\nu})\right)+O\left({\frac{1}{x}}\,\widetilde{\emph{Ai}}\left(-\nu^{2/3}\,\phi({\textstyle\frac{x}{\nu}})\right)\right)\right],\end{split}

where,
\nu=4N=2(2k+\alpha+1)
,

(2.13)

\psi(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left[(t-t^{2})^{1/2}+\sin^{-1}(t^{1/2})\right],\ \ \ 0\leq t<1,

(2.14)

\phi(t)=\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{2/3}\,\begin{cases}\left[\cos^{-1}{t^{1/2}}-(t-t^{2})^{1/2}\right]^{2/3},&0<t\leq 1,\\
-\left[(t^{2}-t)^{1/2}-\cosh^{-1}{t^{1/2}}\right]^{2/3},&t>1,\end{cases}

where,

\widetilde{J_{\alpha}}(u)=\begin{cases}J_{\alpha}(u),&0\leq u\ll 1,\\
\left(|J_{\alpha}(u)|^{2}+|Y_{\alpha}(u)|^{2}\right)^{1/2},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and

\widetilde{\emph{Ai}}(u)=\begin{cases}\emph{Ai}(u),&u\geq 0,\\
\left(|\emph{Ai}(u)|^{2}+|\emph{Bi}(u)|^{2}\right)^{1/2},&u<0.\end{cases}

Here,
J_{\alpha}
and
Y_{\alpha}
are Bessel functions of order
\alpha\geq 0
and
\emph{Ai}
,
\emph{Bi}
are Airy functions.

In view of the asymptotic of Bessel and Airy functions (see
[
19
]
), and the Stirling formula, the above theorem when specialized to our set up leads to

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Let
0<a<b<1
. Set
\mu:=2(2k+n)
. Then, there exists
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, large enough, such that for
k\geq k_{0}
, the following hold:

(1)

In the Bessel regime, that is
0\leq x\leq b\mu
, one has

(
a
)

If
x\,\leq 1/\mu
, then

(2.15)

\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\,L^{n-1}_{k}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}\,x^{n-1}=C\,x^{n-1}\left[1+O\left((x\mu)^{1/2}\right)\right].

(
b
)

If
1/\mu\leq x\,\leq b\mu
, then

(2.16)

\begin{split}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\,L^{n-1}_{k}(x)&\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}\,x^{n-1}=C\,x^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}+1}\left(\frac{\psi(\frac{x}{\mu})}{\psi^{\prime}(\frac{x}{\mu})}\right)^{1/2}\\
&\times\left[\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\mu\psi({\textstyle\frac{x}{\mu}})}}\,\cos\left(\mu\psi(\textstyle\frac{x}{\mu})-\frac{\pi}{2}(n-1)-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)+O\left((x\mu)^{-3/4}\right)\right]\\
&\hskip 50.00008pt=x^{n-1}O\left((x\mu)^{-\frac{2n-1}{4}}\right).\end{split}

(2)

In the Airy regime, that is
a\mu\leq x\leq\frac{1}{a}\mu
, one has

(
a
)

If
a\mu\leq x\leq\mu-c\,\mu^{1/3}
,
0<c\ll 1
, then

(2.17)

\begin{split}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\,&L^{n-1}_{k}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}\,x^{n-1}\\
&=(-1)^{k}\mu^{-\frac{4}{3}}\mu^{\frac{1}{12}}(\mu-x)^{-\frac{1}{4}}\left[\cos\left({\textstyle\frac{2}{3}|\xi(x)|^{3/2}-\frac{\pi}{4}}\right)+O\left({\textstyle\mu^{\frac{1}{6}}\left(1-\frac{x}{\mu}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}}\right)\right],\end{split}

Here,
\xi(x):=-\mu^{2/3}\phi({\textstyle\frac{x}{\mu}})
. Moreover,
\xi(x)\sim-\mu^{2/3}\left(1-{\textstyle\frac{x}{\mu}}\right)
, if
a\mu\leq x\leq\mu
and
\xi(x)\sim\mu^{2/3}\left({\textstyle\frac{x}{\mu}}-1\right)
, if
x>\mu
.

(
b
)

If
\mu-c\,\mu^{1/3}\leq x\leq\mu+c\,\mu^{1/3}
,
0<c\ll 1
, then

(2.18)

\begin{split}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\,&L^{n-1}_{k}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}\,x^{n-1}\\
&=\,(-1)^{k}\mu^{{-\textstyle\frac{n}{2}}-\frac{7}{12}}x^{{\textstyle\frac{n}{2}-\frac{3}{4}}}\left[\emph{Ai}(\xi(x))+O\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{x}}\,\emph{Ai}(\xi(x))\right)\right]\\
&=O\big(\mu^{{-4/3}}\big).\end{split}

(
c
)

If
\mu+c\,\mu^{1/3}\leq x\,\leq{\textstyle\frac{3}{2}}\mu
, then

(2.19)

\begin{split}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\,&L^{n-1}_{k}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}\,x^{n-1}\\
&=\,(-1)^{k}\mu^{{-\textstyle\frac{4}{3}}}\,\mu^{{\textstyle\frac{1}{12}}}(x-\mu)^{-{\textstyle\frac{1}{4}}}\,e^{-{\frac{2}{3}}\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}}(x-\mu)^{3/2}}\left[1+O\left(\mu^{-\frac{1}{4}}(x-\mu)^{-\frac{3}{4}}\right)\right].\end{split}

(3)

In the exponential regime, that is
x\geq\frac{3}{2}\mu
, one has

(2.20)

\begin{split}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\,&L^{n-1}_{k}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}\,x^{n-1}=O\left(\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{n}{2}-1}e^{-\frac{2}{3}x}\right).\end{split}

Here, the implied constants in the asymptotic notation may depend on
n,k_{0}
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
: Reduction to quantitative formulation

In this section, we deduce
Theorem

1.1
from
Theorem

1.3
. This deduction is straightforward extension of the corresponding Euclidean argument by Bourgain. Assume for the sake of contradiction that
Theorem

1.1
failed, then one can find a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}
of positive upper density
\delta>0
, and a sequence of scales
l_{1}<l_{2}<\cdots
going to infinity such that for each
l_{j}
there are no
x,y\in A
with
|x\cdot y^{-1}|_{K}=l_{j}
. Sparsifying this sequence of scales so that we have
l_{j+1}\geq 2l_{j}
for all
j
. Let
\epsilon>0
be sufficiently small (depending only on
\delta
) and let
J
be as in
Theorem

1.3
. As
A
has density
\delta
, one can find
x_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
and a radius
R>l_{J}
so that

\frac{|A\cap B(x_{0},R)|}{|B(x_{0},R)|}\geq\frac{1}{2}\delta,

or equivalently

|A\cap B(x_{0},R)|\gtrsim\delta\,R^{Q},\ \ \ Q=2n+2.

If we consider the rescaling
E=\{x\in B(0,1):\delta_{R}x\cdot x_{0}\in A\}=B(0,1)\cap\delta_{1/R}\left(x_{0}^{-1}\cdot A\right)
, and define
0<r_{J}<\cdots<r_{1}\leq 1
by

r_{j}:=l_{J+1-j}\Big/R,

for
j=1,\cdots,J
, then
|E|\gtrsim\delta\ (\gg\epsilon)
and
r_{j+1}=\frac{1}{R}l_{J-j}\leq\frac{1}{R}\frac{1}{2}l_{J-j+1}=\frac{1}{2}r_{j}
, for all
1\leq j\leq J
; and for any
1\leq j\leq J
there are no points
x,y\in E
so that
d_{K}(x,y)=r_{j}
. In particular, the left hand side of (
1.6
) vanishes for all
1\leq j\leq J
, contradicting
Theorem

1.3
.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Fix
0<\epsilon<1/2
. Let us also fix
E
to be a Lebesgue measurable set inside
B(0,1)\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
such that
|E|\geq\epsilon
. Set
f=1_{E}
, the indicator function of the set
E
. A short Fourier-analytic calculation reveals that the integral in (
1.6
), at the radius
r
, may be written as

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}&\int_{S_{K}(0,1)}1_{E}(x)1_{E}(x\cdot\delta_{r}y)\,d\sigma(y)\,dx=(2\pi)^{-n-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}R_{k}(\lambda r^{2},\sigma)\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\,|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda,\end{split}

where
R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)
are the coefficients of the group Fourier transform of the surface measure on the Korányi sphere :
\hat{\sigma}(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)P_{k}(\lambda)
, which from (
2.10
) are given by

R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)=c_{n}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}L_{k}^{n-1}\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}|\lambda|\cos{\theta}\right)\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}|\lambda|\cos{\theta}}e^{i\frac{1}{4}\lambda\sin{\theta}}(\cos{\theta})^{n-1}d\theta.

Let
J=J(\epsilon)
be a large positive integer depending only on
\epsilon
. Given any sequence of sufficiently small scales
0<r_{J}<\cdots<r_{1}\leq 1
depending on all previous ones and
\epsilon
as
r_{j+1}\leq r_{j}/2
for all
1\leq j<J
, we need to ensure that for at least one
1\leq j\leq J
, one has

(4.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}R_{k}(\lambda r_{j}^{2},\sigma)\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda\gtrsim\epsilon^{2}.

As in the 1986 paper of Bourgain,
[
6
]
, we will follow the energy pigeonholing argument. Thus, for
r_{j}^{2}<\delta\ll 1
to be specified later, we can split the the integral in (
4.1
) into the contribution of the “low frequencies”

(4.2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\,:\,2(2k+n)|\lambda|r_{j}^{2}\leq\delta}R_{k}(\lambda r_{j}^{2},\sigma)\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda,

the “medium frequencies”

(4.3)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\,:\,\delta/r_{j}^{2}\leq 2(2k+n)|\lambda|\leq 1/(\delta r_{j}^{2})}R_{k}(\lambda r_{j}^{2},\sigma)\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda,

and the “high frequencies”

(4.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\,:\,2(2k+n)|\lambda|>1/(\delta r_{j}^{2})}R_{k}(\lambda r_{j}^{2},\sigma)\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda.

4.1.
Low frequency part

In this subsection we tackle the low frequency part of the expression in the left hand side of (
4.1
), namely, the region where
\mu|\lambda|r_{j}^{2}\leq\delta\ll 1
, where
\mu=2(2k+n)
. We begin with observing the behavior of the coefficients of the surface measure in this low frequency case.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\delta\ll 1
. If
\mu|\lambda|\leq\delta\ll 1
then one has

(4.5)

R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)=C_{n}+O\big((\mu|\lambda|)^{1/2}\big),

for some
C_{n}>0
, where the implied constant in the asymptotic notation depends on
n
.

Proof.

By symmetry, we can assume
\lambda>0
and re-write
R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)
as

\begin{split}R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)&=c_{n}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}L_{k}^{n-1}\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\cos{\theta}\right)\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}\lambda\cos{\theta}}\cos{\big({\textstyle\frac{1}{4}}\lambda\sin{\theta}\big)}(\cos{\theta})^{n-1}d\theta.\end{split}

Since here
0<\lambda\ll 1
so it suffices to show

(4.6)

\begin{split}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}L_{k}^{n-1}\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\cos{\theta}\right)\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}\lambda\cos{\theta}}(\cos{\theta})^{n-1}d\theta=C_{n}+O\big((\mu|\lambda|)^{1/2}\big),\end{split}

where the implied constants in the asymptotic notation are allowed to depend on
n
.

Fix
k_{0}
to be the large natural number as in the
Lemma

2.2
. Making a change of variable
x=\frac{1}{2}\lambda\cos{\theta}
, the left side of (
4.6
) becomes

(4.7)

\begin{split}\lambda^{-n+1}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{0}^{\lambda/2}L_{k}^{n-1}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{(\lambda/2)^{2}-x^{2}}},\end{split}

which, being in the Bessel regime
0\leq x\leq\lambda\ll 1/\mu
, using the asymptotics (
2.15
) for
k\geq k_{0}
, can be written as

\begin{split}C_{n}\,\lambda^{-n+1}\int_{0}^{\lambda/2}x^{n-1}\left[1+O_{k_{0}}\left((x\mu)^{1/2}\right)\right]\frac{dx}{\sqrt{(\lambda/2)^{2}-x^{2}}}.\end{split}

By rescaling, the last expression equals a positive constant (depending only on
n
) times the expression

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{n-1}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx+O_{k_{0}}((\lambda\mu)^{1/2}),

which leads the desired estimate (
4.6
) but for
k\geq k_{0}
.

The case of bounded
k
’s easily follows from the behavior (near
0
) of the explicit expression of Laguerre polynomials. Indeed, if
k\leq k_{0}
, then we are essentially in the region
0<\lambda\ll_{k_{0}}\delta
. Thus, for Laguerre polynomials,
L_{k}^{n-1}
, with their degrees bounded by
k_{0}
, one has
L_{k}^{n-1}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(k+n)}{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(n)}+O_{k_{0}}(x)
valid for small
x
uniformly for all
0\leq k\leq k_{0}
. Substituting this into
(
4.7
) one has that for
\lambda
sufficiently small (
4.7
) becomes a positive constant (depending on
n,k_{0}
) times the expression

(4.8)

\begin{split}\lambda^{-n+1}\int_{0}^{\lambda/2}[1+O_{k_{0}}(x)]\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{(\lambda/2)^{2}-x^{2}}},\end{split}

which yields (
4.6
) for all
k\leq k_{0}
.

Altogether, these gives the claimed estimate (
4.6
) and hence (
4.5
), and the lemma is proved.
∎

We now return to the low frequency estimate

Lemma 4.2
.

For
1\gg\delta\geq r^{2}
, there exists an absolute constant
C_{n}
such that

(4.9)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\,:\,2(2k+n)|\lambda|r^{2}\leq\delta}R_{k}(\lambda r^{2},\sigma)\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda\geq C_{n}|E|^{2}.

Proof.

Let us abbreviate
\mu=2(2k+n)
. We recall that
f=1_{E}
as well as the identity

(4.10)

\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\widehat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\widehat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle=(2\pi)^{-n}|\lambda|^{n}\,\|f^{\lambda}\ast_{\lambda}\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}\|_{2}^{2},

which is just (
2.9
).

Note that, in view of
Lemma

4.1
(applied at
\lambda r^{2}
instead of
\lambda
) and the identity (
4.10
), there exists a positive constant
C_{n}
so that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\,:\,2(2k+n)|\lambda|r^{2}\leq\delta}\left(R_{k}(\lambda r^{2},\sigma)-C_{n}\right)\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda\right|

(4.11)

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\sqrt{\delta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\,:\,2(2k+n)|\lambda|r^{2}\leq\delta}\|f^{\lambda}\ast_{\lambda}\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}\|_{2}^{2}\ |\lambda|^{2n}d\lambda

Hence, with an appropriate choice of
\delta<\frac{1}{4}
, we see from (
4.10
) and (
4.11
) that the left-hand side of (
4.9
) is bounded below by

(4.12)

\frac{1}{2}C_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\,:\,\mu|\lambda|r^{2}\leq\delta}\,\|f^{\lambda}\ast_{\lambda}\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}\|_{2}^{2}\ |\lambda|^{2n}d\lambda.

Let
\lambda>0
. We now concentrate on the factor
\|f^{\lambda}\ast_{\lambda}\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}\|_{2}^{2}
in the above expression. For this we shall prove that if
\mu\lambda\ll 1
for
k\geq k_{0}
, with
k_{0}
being a large natural number as in
Lemma

2.2
, and
|z|\leq\rho_{k}
(for some
\rho_{k}\ll 1
to be optimized later) we have

(4.13)

f^{\lambda}\ast_{\lambda}\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}\,(z)=C_{n}^{\prime}\,\mu^{n-1}|E|+O(\mu^{n-1}(\lambda\mu)^{1/2}|E|).

Note that for
\delta\geq r^{2}
the approximation (
4.13
) gives the lower bound for (
4.12
). Indeed, for appropriate positive constants
a_{n},b_{n}
, the expression (
4.12
) is

\begin{split}&\geq\frac{1}{2}C_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\geq k_{0}\,:\,\mu\lambda\ll 1}\,\int_{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:|z|\leq\rho_{k}}|f^{\lambda}\ast_{\lambda}\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}\,(z)|^{2}dz\,\lambda^{2n}d\lambda\\
&\geq\frac{1}{8}C_{n}{C_{n}^{\prime}}^{2}|E|^{2}\,\int_{0<\lambda\,\leq b_{n}}\sum_{k_{0}\leq k\leq a_{n}\lambda^{-1}}\,\rho_{k}^{2n}\mu^{2n-2}\ \lambda^{2n}d\lambda.\end{split}

Now
\rho_{k}
can be chosen to be
\mu^{-M}
, for some
M>0
, and the last expression then becomes

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{8}C_{n}{C_{n}^{\prime}}^{2}|E|^{2}\,\int_{0<\lambda\,\leq b_{n}}\sum_{k_{0}\leq k\leq a_{n}\lambda^{-1}}\,\mu^{-2nM+2n-2}\ \lambda^{2n}d\lambda,\end{split}

which is
\simeq_{n,k_{0}}|E|^{2}
. This gives the asserted lower bound for (
4.12
), and hence (
4.9
) is proved.

It remains to establish (
4.13
). Here we use asymptotic of Laguerre polynomials in the Bessel regime and write

\begin{split}f^{\lambda}\ast_{\lambda}\varphi_{k,\lambda}^{n-1}\,(z)&=\int_{\mathbb{H}^{n}}1_{E}(z-w,-t)\,L_{k}^{n-1}\big({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\lambda|w|^{2}\big)\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}\lambda|w|^{2}}e^{i\lambda\left(-t+\frac{1}{2}\Im(z\cdot\overline{w})\right)}dwdt\\
&=\int_{(z,0)-E\,\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}}L_{k}^{n-1}\big({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\lambda|w|^{2}\big)\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}\lambda|w|^{2}}e^{i\lambda\left(-t+\frac{1}{2}\Im(z\cdot\overline{w})\right)}dwdt\end{split}

where, we have used that
1_{E}(z-w,-t)=1
if and only if
(w,t)\in(z,0)-E
. Since
E\subseteq B(0,1)\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
, the Korányi norm
|(w-z,t)|_{K}<1
, whence
\frac{\lambda}{4}|w|^{2}\leq\lambda
and
|\lambda\left(-t+\frac{1}{2}\Im(z\cdot\overline{w})\right)|\leq 2\lambda
. Since
\lambda\ll 1
, so
\left|\exp{i\lambda\left(-t+\frac{1}{2}\Im(z\cdot\overline{w})\right)}-1\right|\leq 4\lambda
which together with the estimate
\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(n)\Gamma(k+n)}|L_{k}^{n-1}(x)e^{-x/2}|\leq 1
, and the Stirling approximation, gives that the last integral equals

(4.14)

\begin{split}\int_{(z,0)-E\,\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}}L_{k}^{n-1}\big({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\lambda|w|^{2}\big)\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}\lambda|w|^{2}}dwdt\ +\ O\left(\lambda\mu^{n-1}|E|\right).\end{split}

Since,
{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\lambda|w|^{2}\leq 2\lambda\ll 1/\mu
in the integral in (
4.14
), using Laguerre polynomial asymptotic (
2.15
) in conjugation with the Stirling approximation the last expression (
4.14
) becomes

\begin{split}&C\int_{(z,0)-E\,\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}}\frac{\Gamma(k+n)}{\Gamma(k+1)}\left[1+O\left(\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\lambda|w|^{2}\,\mu\right)^{1/2}\right)\right]dwdt\ +\ O\left(\lambda\mu^{n-1}|E|\right)\\
&\simeq\mu^{n-1}|E|+O(\mu^{n-1}(\lambda\mu)^{1/2}|E|),\end{split}

which is what we were aiming for in (
4.13
). Thus, we have completed the proof of
Lemma

4.2
.
∎

4.2.
Middle frequency part

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
0<\epsilon<\frac{1}{2}
, let
E\subset B(0,1)\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
have measure
|E|\geq\epsilon
, and let
J=J(\epsilon)
be a sufficiently large natural number depending on
\epsilon
. Suppose that
0<r_{J}<\cdots<r_{1}\leq 1
are a sequence of scales with
r_{j+1}\leq r_{j}/2
for all
1\leq j<J
. Let
r_{j}^{2}<\delta\ll 1
. Then for at least one
1\leq j\leq J
, one has

(4.15)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\,:\,\delta/r_{j}^{2}\leq(2k+1)|\lambda|\leq 1/(\delta r_{j}^{2})}R_{k}(\lambda r^{2},\sigma)\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda\lesssim\epsilon|E|.

Proof.

We simply observe that since
\textstyle\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(n)\Gamma(k+n)}\,|\varphi^{n-1}_{k,\lambda}(x)|\leq 1
so
|R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)|\lesssim 1
, for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus, of course, we can estimate left hand side of (
4.15
) by

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\,:\,\delta/r_{j}^{2}\leq(2k+1)|\lambda|\leq 1/(\delta r_{j}^{2})}\,\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda,

and by an application of Plancherel’s theorem (
2.5
) this in turn is crudely estimated by

(4.16)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\geq 0}\,\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda=C_{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\|\hat{f}(\lambda)\|^{2}_{\text{HS}}\,|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda=C_{n}\,|E|.

However, because of the lacunarity hypothesis,
r_{j+1}\leq r_{j}/2
, the annulii
A^{\delta}_{j}:=\{(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{R}\times(2\mathbb{N}+n):\delta/r_{j}^{2}\leq\lambda\mu\leq 1/\delta r_{j}^{2}\}
only overlap with multiplicity
O\left(\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
, hence the Plancherel bound, in fact, yields

\sum_{j=1}^{J}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\,:\,\delta/r_{j}^{2}\leq(2k+1)|\lambda|\leq 1/(\delta r_{j}^{2})}\,\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda\lesssim\log\frac{1}{\delta}\ |E|,

and hence by the pigeonhole principle we can choose a scale
r_{j}
, for some
1\leq j\leq J
, for which

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\,:\,\delta/r_{j}^{2}\leq(2k+1)|\lambda|\leq 1/(\delta r_{j}^{2})}\,\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda\lesssim\frac{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{J}\,|E|.

We will now compare it with the contribution of the low frequencies, (
4.9
). Choose
J=J(\delta,\epsilon)
large enough so that
\textstyle\frac{\log 1/\delta}{J}\,|E|\lesssim\epsilon|E|
, or equivalently
\frac{1}{{\displaystyle\epsilon}}\log 1/\delta\lesssim J
, that is for
J=J(\delta,\epsilon)
large enough,
J=O\big(\frac{1}{{\displaystyle\epsilon}}\log 1/\delta\big)
, one can capitalize this “good” scale,
r_{j}
, to make the contribution due to the medium frequencies small compared to that the low frequencies (which was
|E|^{2}
), and one can conclude the desired estimate, (
4.15
).
∎

Remark 4.1
.

We remark that the bound on the number
J
of scales is
J\gtrsim\frac{1}{{\displaystyle\epsilon}}\log\frac{1}{{\delta}}\gtrsim\frac{1}{{\displaystyle\epsilon}}\log\frac{1}{{\displaystyle\epsilon}}
. So, we can take
j=O\big(\frac{1}{{\displaystyle\epsilon}}\log\frac{1}{{\displaystyle\epsilon}}\big)
, which is the same dependence on
\epsilon
as in the Euclidean case.

4.3.
High frequency part

After locating the scale
r_{j}
which takes care of the contribution (
4.3
) of the medium frequencies as in
Lemma

4.3
, we proceed to address high frequencies. We use the precise “decay” of the coefficients
R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)
, for
\mu|\lambda|
large from
Lemma

1.4
to show that the contribution (
4.4
) of the high frequencies is negligible.

Using Plancherel’s theorem and the
Lemma

1.4
, one can make contribution (
4.4
) of the high frequencies small as well:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\,:\,2(2k+n)\lambda|>1/(\delta r_{j}^{2})}R_{k}(\lambda r_{j}^{2},\sigma)\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\,:\,2(2k+n)|\lambda|>1/(\delta r_{j}^{2})}\left(2(2k+n)|\lambda|r_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\alpha(n)}\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{\alpha(n)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k\,:\,2(2k+n)|\lambda|>1/(\delta r_{j}^{2})}\,\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{\alpha(n)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left|\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\langle\hat{f}(\lambda)^{*}\hat{f}(\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle\right|\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda

\displaystyle\simeq\delta^{\alpha(n)}(2\pi)^{-n-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}\|\hat{f}(\lambda)\|^{2}_{\text{HS}}\ |\lambda|^{n}d\lambda

\displaystyle=\delta^{\alpha(n)}\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n})}

(4.17)

\displaystyle=\delta^{\alpha(n)}|E|,\ \ \ (\text{recall}\,f=1_{E}).

Altogether, we can now quickly prove the quantitative version of our main theorem, that is
Theorem

1.3
.

4.4.
Proof of
Theorem

1.3

We continue the discussion before Subsection
4.1
. For
\delta\ll 1
in view of
Lemma

4.3
we get a single scale
r_{j}
such that

4.3

“Decay” of coefficients in the spectral decomposition of
\widehat{\sigma}

, unless specified otherwise. Fix

large enough, so that we can use the asymptotic for Laguerre polynomials in

2.2
for

. Recall that

(5.1)

\begin{split}R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)&=c_{n}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}L_{k}^{n-1}\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}|\lambda|\cos{\theta}\right)\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}|\lambda|\cos{\theta}}e^{i\frac{1}{4}\lambda\sin{\theta}}(\cos{\theta})^{n-1}d\theta.\end{split}

By symmetry, we can assume
\lambda>0
. Also, recall the notation

introduced in the notation subsection of the paper; this will be convenient to use in presentation of the argument that follows. To show Lemma
1.4

\frac{1}{\mu}\leq\lambda\lesssim 1
and
\lambda\gg 1

When
\frac{1}{\mu}\leq\lambda\lesssim 1
.
Since
\lambda\lesssim 1
, so the the factor
e^{i\frac{1}{4}\lambda\sin{\theta}}
, being
\sim 1
, produces no significant extra oscillations. By abuse of notation we continue denoting integral in (
5.1
) without
e^{i\frac{1}{4}\lambda\sin{\theta}}
by
R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)
only:

(5.2)

\begin{split}R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)&=c_{n}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}L_{k}^{n-1}\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\cos{\theta}\right)\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}\lambda\cos{\theta}}(\cos{\theta})^{n-1}d\theta.\end{split}

After change of variable
x=\frac{1}{2}\lambda\cos{\theta}
, we see that

(5.3)

\begin{split}R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)&=C_{n}\lambda^{-n+1}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{0}^{\lambda/2}L_{k}^{n-1}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{(\lambda/2)^{2}-x^{2}}},\end{split}

for some

. Since,
\lambda\mu\gg 1

where the second equivalence follows from the fact that
\lambda/2\leq\lambda/2+x\leq\lambda

\begin{split}|J_{2}|&\lesssim\lambda^{-n+1}\int_{1/\mu}^{\lambda/2}x^{n-1}(x\mu)^{-\frac{2n-1}{4}}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{(\lambda/2)^{2}-x^{2}}}\\

Thus, we obtain that

\begin{split}|J_{2}|&\lesssim\lambda^{-n+1}\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}\lambda^{-1/2}\lambda^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}\lambda^{1/4}\\
&=\lambda^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}.\end{split}

Combining the bounds for

When
\lambda\gg 1
.
In view of symmetry, we will abuse the notation and write

for integral in (

[0,\pi/2]

\lambda\gg 1

5.1

(5.6)

\begin{split}R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)&=c_{n}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\left\{\int_{|\theta-\pi/2|\gtrsim\lambda^{-1/2}}+\int_{|\theta-\pi/2|\lesssim\lambda^{-1/2}}\right\}L_{k}^{n-1}\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\cos{\theta}\right)\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}\lambda\cos{\theta}}e^{i\frac{1}{4}\lambda\sin{\theta}}(\cos{\theta})^{n-1}d\theta\\

\begin{split}R_{k}^{II}(\lambda,\sigma)&=c_{n}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\,e^{i\frac{1}{4}\lambda}\int_{|\theta-\pi/2|\lesssim\lambda^{-1/2}}L_{k}^{n-1}\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\cos{\theta}\right)\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}\lambda\cos{\theta}}(\cos{\theta})^{n-1}d\theta.\end{split}

Treatment of the base of oscillatory integral:
R_{k}^{II}(\lambda,\sigma)
,
\lambda\gg 1

Let us fix a change of variable in above integral:

Then we have

whence we get

\begin{split}R_{k}^{II}(\lambda,\sigma)&=C_{n}\lambda^{-n+1}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\,e^{i\frac{1}{4}\lambda}\int_{0}^{c\lambda^{1/2}}L_{k}^{n-1}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4x^{2}}}\\
\end{split}

,

.

To avoid repetition of similar calculations, set

\begin{split}S_{k}(y)&:=\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{0}^{y}L_{k}^{n-1}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4x^{2}}},\end{split}

Further analysis of

is dictated by the behavior of the Laguerre polynomial. We distinguish the following three cases:

\begin{split}&\text{A}:1\leq c\lambda^{1/2}\leq b\mu\ \ \ (\text{``Bessel regime"}),\\
&\text{B}:a\mu\leq c\lambda^{1/2}\leq\frac{1}{a}\mu,\ \ 0<a<b<1.\ \ \ (\text{ up to the ``Airy regime"}),\\
&\text{C}:c\lambda^{1/2}\gg\mu\ \ \ (\text{ up to ``exponential regime"}).\\
\end{split}

Case A.
In view of the asymptotic of Laguerre polynomial in this region, we break

\begin{split}S_{k}(c\lambda^{1/2})&=\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{0}^{c\lambda^{1/2}}L_{k}^{n-1}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4x^{2}}}\\
&=\int_{0}^{c1/\mu}+\int_{1/\mu}^{c\lambda^{1/2}}=:{\mbox{I}}+{\mbox{II}}.\end{split}

For
I
,

\begin{split}{\mbox{I}}&=\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{0}^{1/\mu}L_{k}^{n-1}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4x^{2}}}\\
&\sim\int_{0}^{1/\mu}x^{n-1}\left[1+O\left((x\mu)^{1/2}\right)\right]\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4x^{2}}}\sim\int_{0}^{1/\mu}x^{n-1}\frac{dx}{\lambda}\\
&\sim\lambda^{-1}\frac{1}{\mu^{n}},\end{split}

whereas for
II
, one has

\begin{split}{\mbox{ II }}&=\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{1/\mu}^{c\lambda^{1/2}}L_{k}^{n-1}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4x^{2}}}\\
&\sim\int_{1/\mu}^{c\lambda^{1/2}}x^{n-1}O\left((x\mu)^{-\frac{2n-1}{4}}\right)\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4x^{2}}}.\end{split}

Thus,

\begin{split}|{\mbox{II}}|&\lesssim\int_{1/\mu}^{c\lambda^{1/2}}x^{n-1}\,(x\mu)^{-\frac{2n-1}{4}}\frac{dx}{\lambda}\simeq\frac{1}{\lambda}\mu^{-n/2+1/4}(\lambda^{1/2})^{n/2+1/4}.\end{split}

Altogether,

(5.11)

\begin{split}|S_{k}(c\lambda^{1/2})|&\lesssim\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{1}{\mu^{n/2-1/4}}\left[\frac{1}{\mu^{n/2+1/4}}+(\lambda^{1/2})^{n/2+1/4}\right]\\
&\simeq\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{1}{\mu^{n/2-1/4}}(\lambda^{1/2})^{n/2+1/4}.\end{split}

Therefore,

(5.12)

\begin{split}|R_{k}^{II}(\lambda,\sigma)|&\simeq\lambda^{-n+1}\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{1}{\mu^{n/2-1/4}}(\lambda^{1/2})^{n/2+1/4}\\
&=\frac{1}{(\lambda\mu)^{n/2-1/4}}\,\frac{1}{(\lambda)^{n/4+1/8}}.\end{split}

Case B.
If
a\mu\leq y\leq\mu-c\mu^{1/3}
then

S_{k}(y)=S_{k}(a\mu)+\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{a\mu}^{y}L_{k}^{n-1}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4x^{2}}}.

From (
5.11
),
S_{k}(a\mu)\simeq\lambda^{-1}\mu^{1/2}
. For the remaining piece we use asymptotic (
2.17
) to obtain

(5.13)

\begin{split}&\left|\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{a\mu}^{y}L_{k}^{n-1}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4x^{2}}}\right|\\
&\simeq\int_{a\mu}^{y}\mu^{-\frac{4}{3}}\mu^{\frac{1}{12}}(\mu-x)^{-1/4}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4x^{2}}}\\
&\simeq\lambda^{-1}\mu^{-\frac{4}{3}}\mu^{\frac{1}{12}}\int_{a\mu}^{y}\frac{dx}{(\mu-x)^{1/4}}\\
&\overset{x\rightarrow\mu x}{=}\lambda^{-1}\mu^{-\frac{4}{3}}\mu^{\frac{1}{12}}\mu^{3/4}\int_{a}^{y\mu^{-1}}\frac{dx}{(1-x)^{1/4}}\\
&\simeq\lambda^{-1}\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

If
\mu-c\mu^{1/3}\leq y\leq\mu+c\mu^{1/3}
,
0<c\ll 1
, then

S_{k}(y)=S_{k}(\mu-c\mu^{1/3})+\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{\mu-c\mu^{1/3}}^{y}L_{k}^{n-1}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4x^{2}}},

so using the the Airy regime asymptotic of Laguerre polynomial, the contribution due to the second term above is

(5.14)

\begin{split}&\simeq\int_{\mu-c\mu^{1/3}}^{y}\mu^{-\frac{4}{3}}\frac{dx}{\lambda}\simeq\lambda^{-1}\mu^{-4/3}\mu^{1/3}=\lambda^{-1}\mu^{-1},\end{split}

which combined with previous estimates give

(5.15)

\begin{split}|S_{k}(y)|&\simeq\lambda^{-1}\mu^{1/2}+\lambda^{-1}\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda^{-1}\mu^{-1}\\
&\simeq\lambda^{-1}\mu^{1/2}.\end{split}

Finally, if
\mu+c\mu^{1/3}\leq y\leq\frac{3}{2}\mu
, then we write

S_{k}(y)=S_{k}(\mu+c\mu^{1/3})+\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{\mu+c\mu^{1/3}}^{y}L_{k}^{n-1}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4x^{2}}},

wherein for the second part we can use asymptotic (
2.19
) and thus, in modulus, it is

\begin{split}&\lesssim\mu^{-\frac{4}{3}}\mu^{\frac{1}{12}}\int_{\mu+c\mu^{1/3}}^{y}(x-\mu)^{-\frac{1}{4}}e^{\frac{2}{3}\mu^{-1/2}(x-\mu)^{3/2}}\frac{dx}{\lambda}\\
&\overset{x\rightarrow\mu x}{=}\lambda^{-1}\mu^{-\frac{4}{3}}\mu^{\frac{1}{12}}\mu^{-\frac{1}{4}}\int_{1+c\mu^{-2/3}}^{y/\mu}(x-1)^{-\frac{1}{4}}e^{\frac{2}{3}\mu(x-1)^{3/2}}dx.\end{split}

A short calculation yields that the last integral is
\simeq\mu^{-1/2}
so the second part is bounded by
\lambda^{-1}\mu^{-\frac{5}{3}}
. On the other hand bound for the first part is given by (
5.15
). So, in the region
\mu+c\mu^{1/3}\leq y\leq\frac{3}{2}\mu
, we obtain total bound of

(5.16)

\begin{split}|S_{k}(y)|&\simeq\lambda^{-1}\mu^{1/2}+\lambda^{-1}\mu^{-\frac{5}{3}}\simeq\lambda^{-1}\mu^{1/2}.\end{split}

Putting all this together, and using
\lambda^{1/2}\simeq\mu
in this Case B, we obtain here that

(5.17)

\begin{split}|R_{k}^{II}(\lambda,\sigma)|&\simeq\lambda^{-n+1}\lambda^{-1}\mu^{1/2}\simeq(\lambda\mu)^{-\left(\frac{2n}{3}-\frac{1}{6}\right)}.\end{split}

Case C.
In view of Case B, it suffices to bound the portion of
S_{k}(y)
after Airy regime, that is to say
\mu\ll x\leq y=c\lambda^{1/2}
:

\left|\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{\frac{1}{a}\mu}^{y}L_{k}^{n-1}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4x^{2}}}\right|\ll\lambda^{-1}\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-N},

for all
N>0
. Proving this estimate would ensure in Case C also we have

(5.18)

\begin{split}|R_{k}^{II}(\lambda,\sigma)|&\simeq\lambda^{-n+1}\lambda^{-1}\mu^{1/2}\simeq(\lambda\mu)^{-\left(\frac{2n}{3}-\frac{1}{6}\right)}.\end{split}

But from the asymptotic (
2.20
) of
L_{k}^{n-1}
in this region the above integral expression is majorized by

\lambda^{-1}\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\frac{1}{a}\mu}^{c\lambda^{1/2}}x^{\frac{n}{2}-1}e^{-\frac{2}{3}x}dx=\lambda^{-1}\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}\left[\Gamma({\textstyle\frac{n}{2}})-O(\lambda^{-N/2})-\left(\Gamma({\textstyle\frac{n}{2}})-O(\mu^{-N})\right)\right]

whence the claim follows.

Putting all the cases
A
,
B
and
C
together, we conclude that the second term in (
5.6
) has the bound

(5.19)

|R_{k}^{II}(\lambda,\sigma)|\simeq(\lambda\mu)^{-\left(\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)}.

Treatment of the auxiliary part of oscillatory integral:
R_{k}^{I}(\lambda,\sigma)
,
\lambda\gg 1

Next, we show that the first part, that is
R_{k}^{I}(\lambda,\sigma)
in (
5.6
), is relatively smaller than the second. Here, extra oscillations given by
e^{i\frac{1}{4}\lambda\sin{\theta}}
will play the crucial role and will be exploited using the following version (to avoid differentiating Laguerre polynomials) of van der Corput lemma about oscillatory integral, for example see
[
25

.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\varPhi
be a smooth function on an interval
I=[a,b]
and
\varPsi
be a smooth positive decreasing function on
[a,b]
. Suppose that
|\varPhi^{\prime}|\geq L
on
I
and suppose additionally that
\varPhi^{\prime}
is monotonic on
I
. Then, there exist a universal constant
C>0
such that

\left|\int_{a}^{b}e^{i\varPhi(s)}\varPsi(s)ds\right|\leq C\frac{\varPsi(a)}{L}.

In case when amplitude function
\varPsi
is instead increasing then the above oscillatory integral is bounded by
C\frac{2\varPsi(b)-\varPsi(a)}{L}.

This time we will work on the original coordinate
\theta
when integrating to avoid working with singular amplitudes:

(5.20)

\begin{split}R_{k}^{I}(\lambda,\sigma)&=c_{n}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\int_{0}^{\pi/2-c\lambda^{-1/2}}L_{k}^{n-1}\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\cos{\theta}\right)\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}\lambda\cos{\theta}}e^{i\frac{1}{4}\lambda\sin{\theta}}(\cos{\theta})^{n-1}d\theta.\end{split}

However, to decide the case study and compact presentation, it will be helpful to read integrand in the notation
x=x(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\,\lambda\cos{\theta}
as

c_{n}\lambda^{-n+1}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}L_{k}^{n-1}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}e^{i\frac{1}{4}\lambda\sin{\theta}}

In view of the behavior of Laguerre polynomials we need to consider the following three exhaustive cases:

\begin{split}&\mathcal{A}:1\leq c\lambda^{1/2}\leq b\mu\ \ \ (\text{``Bessel regime" and after}),\\
&\mathcal{B}:a\mu\leq c\lambda^{1/2}\leq{\textstyle\frac{1}{a}}\mu,\ \ 0<a<b<1,\ \ \ (\text{ ``Airy regime" and after}),\\
&\mathcal{C}:{\textstyle\frac{1}{a}}\mu\leq c\lambda^{1/2}\ \ \ (\text{ only ``exponential regime"}).\\
\end{split}

Case
\mathcal{C}
.
We begin with the case when oscillatory integral lives only inside the exponential regime. From the asymptotic (
2.20
) of
L_{k}^{n-1}
in this region, it suffices to bound

\left|\lambda^{-n+1}\int_{0\leq\theta\lesssim\frac{\pi}{2}-c\lambda^{-1/2}}e^{i\frac{1}{4}\lambda\sin{\theta}}\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\,e^{-\frac{2}{3}x}d\theta\right|

Here,
x=x(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\,\lambda\cos{\theta}
.

Since
\lambda\gg 1
, so for all
n\geq 1
the amplitude (off the factors constants for integration)
\theta\mapsto x(\theta)^{\frac{n}{2}-1}e^{-\frac{2}{3}x(\theta)}
increases on
[0,\frac{\pi}{2}-c\lambda^{-1/2}]
. Also, on this interval phase function
\varPhi(\theta):=\frac{1}{4}\lambda\sin{\theta}
has its derivative bounded below as :
|\varPhi^{\prime}(\theta)|\gtrsim\lambda^{1/2}
. Thus, using van der Corput lemma, the above integral is

(5.21)

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{-n+1}\left(\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\left(\lambda^{1/2}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}-1}e^{-\frac{2}{3}\lambda^{1/2}}}{\lambda^{1/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=(\lambda\mu)^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}\,\mu^{-3/4}\lambda^{-(n+1)/4}e^{-\frac{2}{3}\lambda^{1/2}},

which is acceptable.

Case
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.

a\mu\leq c\lambda^{1/2}\leq\mu-\mu^{1/3}
. In view of the case study Case

\frac{\lambda}{2\mu}\leq b/2\ll 1
so
\cos{\theta_{0}}\sim 1-\frac{\lambda}{4\mu}
which forces the stationary point
\theta_{0}
to lie inside the region of integration
[0,\frac{\pi}{2}-c\lambda^{-1/2}]
.
Moreover, using this approximation of
\cos{\theta_{0}}
, we can compute the
\Psi_{+}^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{0})
to get an idea of the size of stationary region:

(5.27)

\begin{split}-\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}\left(\Psi_{+}(\theta_{0})\right)&=\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left[\left(\frac{2\mu}{\lambda\cos{\theta_{0}}}-1\right)^{-1/2}\left\{\frac{1}{2\cos{\theta_{0}}}+\frac{2\mu}{\lambda}-\cos{\theta_{0}}\right\}+\sin{\theta_{0}}\right]\\
&\simeq\frac{1}{2}\lambda\left(\frac{\lambda}{2\mu}\cos{\theta_{0}}\right)^{1/2}\left[\frac{1}{2\cos{\theta_{0}}}+\frac{2\mu}{\lambda}-\cos{\theta_{0}}+1\right]\\
&\simeq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\frac{\lambda^{3/2}}{\mu^{1/2}}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{4\mu}\right)^{1/2}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{4\mu}\right)^{-1}+\frac{2\mu}{\lambda}+\frac{\lambda}{2\mu}\right]\\
&\simeq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\frac{\lambda^{3/2}}{\mu^{1/2}}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{8\mu}\right)\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{4\mu}\right)+\frac{2\mu}{\lambda}+\frac{\lambda}{2\mu}\right]\\
&\simeq\frac{\lambda^{3/2}}{\mu^{1/2}}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{8\mu}\right)\frac{\mu}{\lambda}\simeq\lambda^{1/2}\mu^{1/2}.\end{split}

Whence, the main contribution to (
5.25
) is given by the stationary region

\begin{split}&\left|\lambda^{-n+1}\int_{|\theta-\theta_{0}|\lesssim(\lambda\mu)^{-1/4}}\,x^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}\,\psi^{\prime}({\textstyle\frac{x}{\mu}})^{-1/2}d\theta\right|\\
&\simeq\left|\lambda^{-n+1}\int_{|\theta-\theta_{0}|\lesssim(\lambda\mu)^{-1/4}}\,x^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}\,({\textstyle\frac{x}{\mu}})^{1/4}d\theta\right|\\
&\simeq\lambda^{-n+1}\lambda^{\frac{n}{2}-1+\frac{1}{4}}\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}\int_{|\theta-\theta_{0}|\lesssim(\lambda\mu)^{-1/4}}\,(\cos{\theta})^{\frac{n}{2}-1+\frac{1}{4}}d\theta\\
&\simeq\lambda^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}(\lambda\mu)^{-1/4},\end{split}

where in the second equivalence we have used
\psi^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{t}-1\right)^{1/2}\simeq t^{-1/2}
, for
t\ll 1
and
x=x(\theta)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\cos{\theta}
, and
\cos{\theta_{0}}\simeq 1-\frac{\lambda}{2\mu}\simeq 1
.

For the remaining non-stationary region
|\theta-\theta_{0}|\gtrsim(\lambda\mu)^{-1/4}
inside
[0,\frac{\pi}{2}-c\lambda^{-1/2}]
we can use van der Corput lemma to bound

(5.28)

\begin{split}&\left|\lambda^{-n+1}\int_{|\theta-\theta_{0}|\gtrsim(\lambda\mu)^{-1/4}}\,x^{\frac{n}{2}-1}\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}\,\psi^{\prime}({\textstyle\frac{x}{\mu}})^{-1/2}\,e^{i\Psi_{\pm}(\theta)}d\theta\right|\\
&\simeq\left|\lambda^{-n+1}\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}\int_{|\theta-\theta_{0}|\gtrsim(\lambda\mu)^{-1/4}}\,x^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{3}{4}}\,e^{i\Psi_{\pm}(\theta)}d\theta\right|.\end{split}

To this end, since
\frac{d}{d\theta}(\Psi_{+})
is decreasing in
\theta
and
\frac{d}{d\theta}(\Psi_{+})(\theta_{0}\mp(\lambda\mu)^{-1/4})\sim\frac{d^{2}}{d\theta^{2}}(\Psi_{+})(\theta_{0})(\lambda\mu)^{-1/4}
so, in view of (
5.27
), in the non-stationary region
|\theta-\theta_{0}|\gtrsim(\lambda\mu)^{-1/4}
, we have

\left|\frac{d}{d\theta}(\Psi_{+})(\theta_{0}\mp(\lambda\mu)^{-1/4})\right|\gtrsim(\lambda\mu)^{1/4}.

We distinguish two cases. If
n\geq 2
, then amplitude
\theta\mapsto x(\theta)^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{3}{4}}
is decreasing in
\theta
, so from van der Corput lemma, (
5.28
) is bounded by

\lambda^{-n+1}\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}\frac{(\lambda/2)^{\frac{n}{2}-\frac{3}{4}}}{(\lambda\mu)^{1/4}}\simeq\lambda^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}\mu^{-\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{4}}(\lambda\mu)^{-\frac{1}{4}},

which is acceptable. If
n=1
, then the amplitude
\theta\mapsto x(\theta)^{-\frac{1}{4}}
is increasing function of
\theta
so this time (
5.28
) is

\begin{split}&\left|\mu^{-\frac{1}{4}}\int_{|\theta-\theta_{0}|\gtrsim(\lambda\mu)^{-1/4}}\,x^{-\frac{1}{4}}\,e^{i\Psi_{\pm}(\theta)}d\theta\right|\\
&\lesssim\mu^{-\frac{1}{4}}\left[\frac{x(\theta_{0}-(\lambda\mu)^{-1/4})^{-\frac{1}{4}}}{(\lambda\mu)^{1/4}}+\frac{x(\theta_{0}-c\lambda^{-1/2})^{-\frac{1}{4}}}{(\lambda\mu)^{1/4}}\right]\\
&\simeq(\lambda\mu)^{-\frac{1}{4}}\mu^{-\frac{1}{4}}\left[(\lambda)^{-\frac{1}{4}}+(\lambda^{1/2})^{-\frac{1}{4}}\right]\\
&\simeq(\lambda\mu)^{-\frac{1}{4}}\mu^{-\frac{1}{4}}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{8}},\end{split}

which is again acceptable.

It remains to give the desired bound on
R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)
, for
0\leq k\leq k_{0}
. Because
k
are now bounded so
\mu=2(2k+n)\simeq_{k_{0}}1
, and therefore
|\lambda|\mu\gg 1
is equivalent to
|\lambda|\gg 1
. Again, by symmetry, we can assume
\lambda>0
. We decompose
R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)
as in (
5.6
):
R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)=R_{k}^{I}(\lambda,\sigma)+R_{k}^{II}(\lambda,\sigma)
. As before the region of integration in
R_{k}^{II}(\lambda,\sigma)
being the stationary region for the factor
\exp i\frac{1}{4}\lambda\sin{\theta}
so

\begin{split}R_{k}^{II}(\lambda,\sigma)&\sim c_{n}\lambda^{-n+1}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}e^{i\frac{1}{4}\lambda}\int_{0}^{c\lambda^{1/2}}L_{k}^{n-1}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4x^{2}}}\\
&\sim c_{n}\lambda^{-n}\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{\Gamma(k+n)}\,e^{i\frac{1}{4}\lambda}\int_{0}^{c\lambda^{1/2}}L_{k}^{n-1}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}x}x^{n-1}dx\\
&\lesssim_{k_{0},n}\lambda^{-n},\end{split}

for large
\lambda
, where the last inequality is a consequence of
k\simeq_{k_{0}}1
and the decay of
e^{-x/2}
for large
x
. On the other hand, for the piece
R_{k}^{I}(\lambda,\sigma)
, one can substitute the expression of Laguerre polynomial in it and, after swapping summation (in the polynomial) and integration, then applying van der Corput lemma for each term one can easily see
|R_{k}^{I}(\lambda,\sigma)|\lesssim_{k_{0}}\lambda^{-n-N/2}
, for all
N>0
. Combining these two bounds, one obtains
|R_{k}(\lambda,\sigma)|\lesssim_{k_{0}}\lambda^{-n}\simeq_{k_{0}}(\lambda\mu)^{-n}
, in the case
k\leq k_{0}
.

Altogether, this completes the proof of the lemma.

6.
Appendix

The
Theorem

1.1
describes the distances of all unbounded sets with a positive upper density. In this section, we initially prove that the sets with higher upper density admit all distances. This is an easy consequence of the quantitative version of the generalized Steinhaus’ theorem, which states that all the subsets of the unit ball whose measure is at least
1/2
admit uniform interval distances. As mentioned in the Introduction, we state and prove these observations in
Theorem

6.1
. In addition, we provide an example that explains the necessity of the hypothesis on the upper density by proving
Proposition

1.2
. The proofs are trivial analogues from the Euclidean case observed in
[
22
, Section 10.1]
and we write them out for completion.

6.1.
Uniform distances of small sets

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
\rho<\frac{1}{2}
.
There exists a small
c_{\rho}>0
, depending only on
\rho
and
n
, such that the following holds: for all Borel sets
E\subseteq B(0,1)\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
with
|E|\geq 1-\rho
, we have

(6.1)

E\cdot E^{-1}\supseteq B(0,c_{\rho});\quad\text{ as a result }\quad\Delta(E)\supseteq[0,c_{\rho}].

As an application, if
C_{Q}
denotes the volume of the unit ball
B(0,1)\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
, the sets in Heisenberg group with Korányi upper density at least
C_{Q}^{-1}(1-\rho)
that is,

(6.2)

\limsup_{R\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}}\frac{|A\cap B(x,R)|}{|B(x,R)|}>C_{Q}^{-1}(1-\rho),

has all large distances:

(6.3)

\Delta(A)=[0,\infty).

Proof.

Similar to the argument in
[
5
, Lemma II]
, we prove (
6.1
). Suppose
E\subseteq B(0,1)
,
|E|\geq 1-\rho>\frac{1}{2}
. Since
2(1-\rho)>1
, we can choose a constant
c_{\rho}>0
small enough such that

(6.4)

(1+c_{\rho}\sqrt{2n})^{2n}(1+c_{\rho}^{2}+c_{\rho}\sqrt{n/2})<2(1-\rho).

Indeed, such a choice is possible since
\rho<\frac{1}{2}
.Then for any
x=(z,t)\in[0,c_{\rho}]^{2n}\times[0,c_{\rho}^{2}]
, we have

(x\cdot E)\cup E\subset Q_{\rho,n}:=[0,1+c_{\rho}\sqrt{2n}]^{2n}\times\left[0,1+c_{\rho}^{2}+c_{\rho}\sqrt{n/2}\right].

Note that the Haar measure of
Q_{\rho,n}
is at most
2(1-\rho)
, for the above choice of
c_{\rho}
in (
6.4
). Since
(x\cdot E)\cup E\subseteq Q_{\rho,n}
, we have

\displaystyle\bigl|(x\cdot E)\cup E\bigr|\leq|Q_{\rho,n}|<2(1-\rho)\leq|x\cdot E|+|E|.

But this implies
\bigl|(x\cdot E)\cap E\bigr|>0
, and hence
(x\cdot E)\cap E
is non-empty, that is,
x\in E\cdot E^{-1}
. Since this is true for all
x\in[0,c_{\rho}]^{2n}\times[0,c_{\rho}^{2}]
, we conclude that
\Delta(E)\supseteq[0,c_{\rho}]
.

The proof of (
6.3
) follows from (
6.1
) similar to the proof found in
[
22
, Section 10.1]
.

Given a set
A
satisfying (
6.2
), one can find a sequence
R_{k}\nearrow\infty
and
x_{k}=(z_{k},t_{k})\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
so that

(6.5)

\frac{|(A\cap B(x_{k},R_{k})|}{|B(x_{k},R_{k})|}\geq C_{Q}^{-1}(1-\rho),\quad\text{ for all $k$.}

Let us define the transformation
\mathbb{T}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}
by

\mathbb{T}_{k}(w,u):=\delta_{R_{k}^{-1}}\left(x_{k}^{-1}\cdot(w,u)\right),

where
``\cdot"
is the group law of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
.
Note that
E_{k}:=\mathbb{T}_{k}(A\cap B(x_{k},R_{k}))
and
(z,t)\in E_{k}
if and only if
x_{k}\cdot\delta_{R_{k}}(z,t)\in A\cap B(x_{k},R_{k})
. We have
E_{k}\subset B(0,1)
and
|E_{k}|\geq 1-\rho
. Invoking (
6.1
), we obtain
\Delta(E_{k})\supseteq[0,c_{\rho}]
, and hence

\Delta(A)\supseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Delta\bigl[\mathbb{T}_{k}^{-1}(E_{k})\bigr]\supseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}R_{k}\Delta(E_{k})\supseteq\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigl[0,c_{\rho}R_{k}\bigr]=[0,\infty),

as claimed.
∎

6.2.
Example

We shall now turn to the example mentioned in the introduction and show why the positive upper Korányi density cannot be qualitatively improved. Thus strengthening the hypothesis in our main theorem. This is an easy variant, in the Heisenberg group context, of a parallel example by A. Rice,
[

]
, formulated for general metric on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

[

]
and
[

Proof of
Proposition

1.2
.
The set
\mathbb{Z}^{2n}\times\mathbb{Z}

R_{m}\nearrow\infty

\epsilon_{m}\searrow 0
in the following order of choice

\displaystyle\bigl||(z,t)|_{K}-R_{j}\bigr|>4\epsilon_{m}\text{ for all $(z,t)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2n}\times\mathbb{Z}$ and }1\leq j\leq m,

(6.7)

\displaystyle R_{m}\geq 100R_{n-1}\quad\text{ and }\quad f(R_{m})\leq\left(\frac{\epsilon_{m-1}}{16}\right)^{2}.

By (

), there exists
(z_{m},t_{m})
such that

L_{m}:=\{(z_{m},t_{m})\cdot(z,t)^{-1}:(z,t)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2n}_{\lfloor\frac{R_{m}}{4}\rfloor}\times Z_{\lfloor\frac{R_{m}}{4}\rfloor}\}\subset B\left(0,{\textstyle\frac{R_{m}}{2}}\right)\backslash B(0,10R_{m-1})

Consider

Suppose
A\cap B(0,R_{m-1})
has no
R_{j}
distances for all
j
. By the definition of
P_{m}
we have

\displaystyle d_{K}((z^{\prime},t^{\prime}),(w,u))\geq 9R_{m-1},\ \forall(z^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in A\cap B(0,R_{m-1})\text{ and }(w,u)\in P_{m}

If there exists
(w_{1},u_{1}),(w_{2},u_{2})\in P_{m}
such that
|(w_{1},u_{1})\cdot(w_{2},u_{2})^{-1}|_{K}=R_{j}
