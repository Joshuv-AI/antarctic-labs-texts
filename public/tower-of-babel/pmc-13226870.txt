How Pregnancy Affects Asthma in Korea: Risk Factors and Adherence Decline
Authors: Myoung-Nam Lim, Jin-Sung Park, Jae-Woo Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePregnancy is a well-known trigger for asthma exacerbations, but risk prediction remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of pregnancy on asthma control, identify risk factors for exacerbations, and assess adherence to asthma management during and after pregnancy in Korea.MethodsWomen with asthma who gave birth between 2012 and 2015 in Korea were identified using the Korean National Health Insurance claims database. Asthma was defined as having at least three asthma diagnoses and at least one prescription of medication for asthma within 4 years before pregnancy. Asthma exacerbations during pregnancy were defined as the requirement for oral corticosteroid treatment or hospitalization, or an emergency department visit. A case-control study was conducted to evaluate risk factors for these exacerbations within the overall cohort and, specifically, among women who discontinued asthma medication during pregnancy. Postpartum adherence to asthma medication was also assessed.ResultsAmong 123,355 women with asthma, 14.8% experienced asthma exacerbation during pregnancy, while 82.9% discontinued asthma medication during pregnancy. Long-term asthma control, spanning the 4 years before conception was more strongly associated with pregnancy outcomes than the 1-year assessment. During pregnancy, poor adherence to asthma treatment was strongly associated with exacerbations, and women who discontinued medication were particularly vulnerable if they had a history of pre-pregnancy exacerbations, moderate to severe rhinitis, multiple gestation, neuropsychiatric disorders, or prior inhaled corticosteroid use. Even after delivery, only 27.4% of asthmatics continued taking asthma medication.ConclusionsThis study emphasizes that long-term asthma control and medication maintenance throughout pregnancy are crucial for preventing exacerbations. High-risk women were characterized by comorbidities such as rhinitis, neuropsychiatric disorders, and multiple gestation. These findings underscore the importance of sustained asthma management and patient education not only before and during pregnancy but also throughout the postpartum period.

INTRODUCTION

Pregnancy is a well-known trigger for asthma exacerbations, and asthma is one of the most serious comorbid conditions during pregnancy.123 Asthma affects approximately 8.8% of pregnant women in the United States and 1%–4% of pregnancies are complicated by poorly controlled asthma.4 One in 500 expectant mothers experiences serious health consequences from uncontrolled asthma during pregnancy, including maternal and/or perinatal death from asphyxia, preeclampsia, intrauterine growth restriction, premature birth, and low birth weight.34 Adequate asthma control may reduce the risk of exacerbations and prevent obstetric and neonatal complications.5 Thus, current guidelines emphasize continued asthma treatment throughout pregnancy.6

Approximately a third of women with asthma experience worsening of their asthma during pregnancy.7 Although identifying and closely managing high-risk groups is highly desirable, it remains difficult to predict the course of asthma during pregnancy, and the mechanisms underlying asthma exacerbations during pregnancy are still poorly understood.8 To date, only pre-pregnancy asthma severity and medication adherence have been consistently reported as risk factors for asthma flare-ups during pregnancy.2359 Therefore, preventing asthma exacerbations during pregnancy requires identifying risk factors and understanding pregnancy-induced behavioral changes among patients regarding their adherence to asthma treatment.

This study aimed to evaluate the impact of pregnancy on asthma control, identify risk factors for exacerbations, and assess adherence to asthma management during pregnancy in Korea.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source

We constructed a study cohort of women who gave birth between January 2012 and December 2015, using data obtained from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) claims database (M20190910959). The NHIS provides mandatory health insurance to approximately 97% of the Korean population, enabling near-complete coverage of medical utilization across the country. This nationwide database enabled the capture of data on all deliveries nationwide during the study period. The database includes extensive information on patient demographics, diagnostic codes (International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th edition [ICD-10]), healthcare services provided, and prescriptions dispensed from January 2007 to December 2018.10

Study design

The inclusion criteria were women with asthma who gave birth between January 2012 and December 2015 and were aged ≥ 20 years at delivery. Pre-pregnancy asthma was defined as having a diagnosis of asthma (ICD-10 codes J45–J46) on three or more occasions with at least one prescription of medication for asthma, or a history of hospitalization or an emergency department (ED) visit for asthma within the 4 years prior to conception. Asthma medications included inhaled corticosteroids (ICSs), ICSs combined with long-acting β2-agonists (LABAs), oral leukotriene antagonists, short-acting β2-agonists, systemic LABAs, xanthine derivatives, and systemic corticosteroids.11 Severe asthma exacerbations were defined as hospitalization or an ED visit for asthma. For women who gave birth 2 or more times during the study period, only the first pregnancy was included.

Pregnancy was defined based on procedure codes related to delivery. The pregnancy period was dened as 280 days (40 weeks) preceding the date of delivery, and the start of pregnancy was defined as the first day of this period. Asthma exacerbations during pregnancy were defined as ≥ 3 asthma diagnoses with at least one oral corticosteroid (OCS) prescription, or any hospitalization or ER visit for asthma during pregnancy. Medication discontinuation was defined as the complete absence of prescription for any of the above-listed asthma medications during the entire pregnancy period or until the occurrence of an exacerbation event. In addition to complete discontinuation, reduction in ICS use was also evaluated as a separate variable. ICS reduction was defined as a mean daily ICS dose during pregnancy that was < 50% of the mean daily dose received during the 1-year pre-pregnancy period. Loss to follow-up for asthma was defined as a decrease in follow-up frequency for asthma during pregnancy to < 10% of the frequency before pregnancy. Comorbidities including rhinitis (J30), urticaria (L50), gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) (K21), and neuropsychiatric problems, such as depression (F32 and F33) and anxiety (F40 and F41), were defined as ≥ 3 diagnoses during the 4-year period before pregnancy.10 Rhinitis severity was determined by the number of annual healthcare visits for rhinitis during the 4-year period, and individuals with ≥ 3 visits per year were classified as having moderate-to-severe rhinitis.10 The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kangwon National University Hospital (KNUH-2022-02-008).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics are presented as numbers and percentages and were analyzed using the χ2 test. Logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the risk of asthma exacerbations during pregnancy. Crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) were calculated. In a nested case-control study with 1:2 matching for age, season, and year of delivery, conditional logistic regression was performed to estimate the crude ORs. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.3; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

A total of 123,355 women with asthma became pregnant, among whom 14.8% (n = 18,257) experienced asthma exacerbations during pregnancy (Fig. 1). Table 1 shows that 14.2% of patients were lost to follow-up during pregnancy, with rates of 30.8% in the exacerbation group and 11.3% in the non-exacerbation group. Moreover, 82.8% of patients discontinued asthma medication during pregnancy, with corresponding rates of 87.2% and 82.1% in the exacerbation and the non-exacerbation groups, respectively. The frequency of hospital visits for rhinitis was higher in the exacerbation group, whereas maternal age, multiple gestation, parity, and other comorbidities showed no significant differences between the 2 groups. No differences in exacerbation rates were observed across trimesters. The age-stratified analysis showed similar trends across all age groups (Supplementary Table S1), and multiple gestation and multiparity were associated with a higher frequency of exacerbations among women in their 30s (OR, 1.18; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.03–1.28 and OR 1.26; 95% CI, 1.20–1.36, respectively).

In the matched analysis, several risk factors for asthma exacerbation during pregnancy were identified (Table 2). The frequency of severe exacerbations during the 4 years prior to pregnancy was more strongly associated with exacerbations during pregnancy than the 1-year assessment (OR, 3.82; 95% CI 3.16–4.53 vs. OR, 2.49; 95% CI, 2.21–2.65). During pregnancy, loss to follow-up for asthma showed a strong association with exacerbations (OR, 3.08; 95% CI, 2.78–3.39), exceeding the impact of medication discontinuation or reduction in ICS prescriptions. Women in the exacerbation group were also more likely to have received ICS prescriptions before pregnancy, but the rate of ICS discontinuation during pregnancy was high. In addition, moderate-to-severe rhinitis before pregnancy was significantly more frequent in the exacerbation group (OR, 5.96; 95% CI, 4.81–6.14).

Among the 82.8% (n = 102,178) of pregnant women who discontinued asthma medication, a history of OCS use (OR, 1.45; 95% CI, 1.35–1.56) and severe asthma exacerbations prior to pregnancy (OR, 1.24; 95% CI, 1.13–1.29) were significantly associated with asthma exacerbations after adjustment for maternal age, season of delivery, and year of delivery (Table 3). Discontinuation of asthma medication within 1 year prior to pregnancy was not significantly associated with asthma exacerbations during pregnancy. However, among women who had used ICS in the year before pregnancy, discontinuation of ICS was associated with an increased risk of exacerbation (OR, 1.44; 95% CI, 1.37–1.54), comparable to that observed in women with a history of OCS use in the year prior to pregnancy (OR, 1.45; 95% CI, 1.35–1.56). For pregnancy-related factors, multiple gestation was significantly associated with exacerbation (OR, 1.83; 95% CI, 1.46–2.01). Regarding comorbidities, neuropsychiatric problems showed a strong association with asthma exacerbations during pregnancy (OR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.53–1.73), while urticaria, GERD, and rhinitis were also significantly associated.

Fig. 2 shows asthma medication adherence and exacerbation events up to 1 year after delivery. Only 27.4% of asthmatics were taking their asthma medication, with a greater reduction observed in the non-exacerbation group (22.4% vs. 49.0%). A marked increase in the frequency of severe exacerbations (3.34 episodes/year) was observed during the first postpartum year among those who experienced an asthma exacerbation during pregnancy. In contrast, the non-exacerbation group maintained a low and stable exacerbation frequency (0.41 episodes/year) after delivery.

DISCUSSION

This study reinforces that pre-pregnancy asthma control is the cornerstone of prevention, not only during the 1 year prior to pregnancy but also over a longer period of at least 4 years. Moderate-to-severe rhinitis before pregnancy was also a significant risk factor for asthma exacerbations during pregnancy. In addition, poor adherence to asthma treatment during pregnancy was strongly associated with exacerbations. Women who discontinued asthma medication were particularly vulnerable if they had a history of pre-pregnancy exacerbations, moderate-to-severe rhinitis, multiple gestation, coexisting neuropsychiatric disorders, or prior ICS use. Importantly, poor adherence to asthma management triggered by pregnancy persisted for up to 1 year postpartum, contributing to an increased risk of severe exacerbations even after delivery.

Our findings are consistent with known determinants of asthma exacerbations, such as prior severe exacerbations, OCS use, and poor medication adherence or discontinuation, underscoring that the fundamental principles of asthma management remain paramount during pregnancy. However, this study provides the following pregnancy-specific insights that strengthen its clinical and scientific relevance. First, long-term asthma control status over a 4-year period was a stronger predictor of exacerbations during pregnancy than the conventional 1-year assessment. Second, the finding that pre-pregnancy moderate-to-severe rhinitis was a significant independent risk factor for exacerbation in this cohort highlights the critical need for appropriate management of allergic rhinitis in pregnant women with asthma. Third, the high rate of medication discontinuation during pregnancy was quantified, and this poor adherence was identified as a major predictor of exacerbation in this cohort. This represents a key management challenge specific to the care of pregnant women with asthma.

Severity and control status of asthma before pregnancy are the strongest risk factors for asthma exacerbation during pregnancy. Most previous studies evaluated asthma control only during the year before pregnancy,111213 and current guidelines also emphasize the preceding 1-year period as a risk factor for future exacerbations. In contrast, our study shows that long-term asthma control is a stronger predictor of exacerbations during pregnancy, as the frequency of exacerbations over a 4-year pre-pregnancy period was more strongly associated with pregnancy outcomes than that observed during the 1-year assessment. This is in line with previous findings that longitudinal asthma trajectories better reflect underlying disease stability and treatment adherence.1415 Thus, it should be emphasized in asthma patient education and preconception counseling that maintaining stable asthma control before pregnancy is important for safe pregnancy. Clinicians should also evaluate asthma control status over several years before pregnancy, not just focusing on the immediate pre-pregnancy period to prevent asthma exacerbations.

In this study, 82.8% of women did not use asthma medication during pregnancy, and 15.6% experienced exacerbations. Discontinuation of asthma medication was a serious problem, but lack of physician visits posed an even greater risk in this study. It has been known that a large proportion of pregnant women with poorly controlled asthma do not fill a prescription during their pregnancy.1617 A recent Korean study has also reported that only a small percentage of asthmatic patients sought healthcare services during pregnancy,18 and the prescription rates of asthma medication were markedly low in pregnant women with asthma compared to general asthmatic patients.11

In this study, discontinuation of ICS was clearly linked to an increased risk of exacerbations during pregnancy. This finding underscores that ICS therapy should be more actively prescribed in clinical practice and maintained throughout pregnancy. Indeed, lack of appropriate ICS treatment or discontinuation during pregnancy is a major predictor of exacerbations,16 and a previous study suggests that up to 29% of severe exacerbations are attributable to non-adherence to ICS.17 A Korean study reported that only 19.4% of women who had used ICS before pregnancy continued its use during pregnancy.18 Since maintaining optimal asthma control reduces the risk of exacerbations and mitigates adverse obstetric and neonatal outcomes, such as preterm birth and low birth weight,17 current guidelines strongly recommend continued asthma treatment, particularly ICS, throughout pregnancy. Moreover, ICS use in early pregnancy has been reported to confer a protective effect against the development of asthma in the offspring.19

It is well known that one-third of pregnant women with asthma experience worsening symptoms during pregnancy. A previous study using Korean claims data has suggested the presence of a subset of asthma patients who experience a marked increase in exacerbations during pregnancy.20 In our analysis, the comparison between those who discontinued medication with and without exacerbations showed characteristics of such vulnerable subset of patients with asthma. Besides pre-pregnancy asthma status, twin or multiple gestation, comorbidities such as neuropsychiatric problems and moderate-to-severe rhinitis before pregnancy were risk factors for asthma exacerbation during pregnancy.

Moderate-to-severe rhinitis is a risk factor not only for asthma exacerbations in pregnant women with the condition, but also for new-onset asthma in those without it.10 Actually, rhinitis usually gets worse during pregnancy and shows clinical concordance between rhinitis and asthma during pregnancy.8 These findings suggest that clinicians should monitor asthma flare-ups more carefully for pregnant asthmatics with a history of moderate-to-severe rhinitis, and underscore the importance of pre-pregnancy screening for asthma in women with rhinitis. Previous cohort studies have suggested that factors such as advanced maternal age, smoking, obesity, chronic conditions including diabetes, psychological problems, and the use of non-asthma medications may increase the risk of asthma exacerbations during pregnancy.1317 In line with these findings, our study also confirmed the importance of pre-pregnancy asthma severity and psychological problems, although maternal age was not a significant factor in the current study.

Asthma medication adherence declined markedly during pregnancy and did not fully recover after delivery. Previous studies reported a marked reduction in asthma medication use during pregnancy, followed by a gradual increase over the 2 years after delivery.1120 Our findings demonstrate that women who experienced asthma exacerbations during pregnancy continued to show poor adherence to asthma medications after delivery, which was associated with a sustained increase in severe exacerbations. In contrast, women without pregnancy-related exacerbations, although also demonstrating low medication use postpartum, did not exhibit an increase in exacerbations for at least the first year after delivery. This highlights that inadequate adherence in the high-risk group has particularly detrimental consequences, underscoring the importance of maintaining regular treatment and follow-up even beyond pregnancy.

While previous international studies reported approximately 20% of asthma exacerbation rates requiring medical intervention during pregnancy,21617 the exacerbation rate in our study, 14.8%, was slightly lower. Similar trends have been observed in prior studies using Korean claims data, which reported a marked decrease in OCS use for asthma during pregnancy as well as no significant increase in asthma-related hospitalizations or ED visits for asthma.20 This finding raises concerns about potential underestimation or undertreatment of asthma exacerbations even during pregnancy in Korea. Some studies reported that a marked decrease in OCS use for asthma during pregnancy appears to be associated with previously reported high OCS prescription rates in Korea during the usual (non-pregnant) period, before pregnancy2122 and the general tendency to minimize medication use, especially OCS, during pregnancy. On the other hand, this study also directly reflects a major issue in asthma management in Korea, namely the remarkably low prescription rate of ICS. Only 13% of patients with asthma received ICS prescriptions before pregnancy, which is consistent with findings from a previous Korean study.18

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, we were unable to include certain important variables, such as smoking status and obesity (body mass index, BMI), and detailed socioeconomic or environmental factors (e.g., air pollution exposure), due to the nature and lack of available data in the NHIS claims database. Smoking is a well-established risk factor for asthma exacerbations during pregnancy,13 and obesity is also a significant predictor of poor asthma control. Lack of these factors may have introduced residual confounding in our results. Specifically, if individuals with higher exposure to these risk factors (e.g., smokers, high BMI) also have poorer pre-pregnancy asthma control or adherence, the estimated effect sizes (ORs) for the observed risk factors (e.g., pre-pregnancy exacerbation history, rhinitis) might be overestimated, or their true associations may be obscured. Although we included multiple gestation and neuropsychiatric problems as comorbidities, incomplete control of confounding warrants caution in interpreting the absolute magnitude of the associations. Second, while our study focused on asthma exacerbations, we were unable to assess perinatal outcomes, which are clinically important endpoints in this population. Third, distinguishing asthma exacerbations from other causes of respiratory symptoms such as cough, dyspnea, wheezing, and chest tightness during pregnancy was challenging; to address this, we applied a stricter definition of exacerbation (at least 3 asthma diagnoses and at least one OCS prescription). However, this definition, which relies on OCS use or severe events, likely results in an underestimation of the overall burden of asthma exacerbations, particularly milder cases that may not have required OCS treatment or an ED visit. Similarly, the application of the definition of comorbidities—requiring 3 or more diagnoses—increased the specificity of our cohort definition, but may have reduced the sensitivity for identifying mild cases of asthma or comorbid diseases with fewer healthcare visits. Finally, the external validity of our findings should be interpreted with caution because ICS prescription rates in Korea are substantially lower than in many Western healthcare systems where guideline-based controller therapy is more consistently implemented. This pattern—reflecting differences in prescribing practices, patient adherence, and the degree to which clinical guidelines are applied—may influence both the baseline risk profile and the observed rates of exacerbations in our cohort. Thus, although the identified risk factors (such as pre-pregnancy exacerbations and rhinitis) are likely to be universally relevant, the absolute incidence of exacerbations (14.8%) and patterns of medication adherence may not be directly generalizable to countries with higher ICS utilization or different healthcare delivery models. Future multinational studies are warranted to determine whether these associations are consistent across diverse healthcare systems.

In conclusion, this study highlights that long-term asthma control extending over several years before conception is essential for preventing exacerbations during pregnancy. Management of comorbidities, particularly rhinitis, also plays a critical role. Moreover, poor adherence to asthma medications, often initiated during pregnancy, may persist into the postpartum period and increase the risk of severe exacerbations. These findings underscore the importance of sustained asthma management and patient education not only before and during pregnancy, but also throughout the postpartum period.
