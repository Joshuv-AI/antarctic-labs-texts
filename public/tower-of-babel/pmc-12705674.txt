Hygiene status and carcass contamination with Escherichia coli 0157H7 at meat handling sites in Eastern Uganda
Authors: Igeme Thomas, Herbert Itabangi, Rebecca Nekaka, Paul Oboth, Julius Nteziyaremye, David Okia, Joel Bazira, Jacob Stanley Iramiot, Iramiot Jacob Stanley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In developing countries, foodborne infections, particularly those linked to Escherichia coli (E. coli) 0157:H7 contamination, pose significant public health risks. These infections often result from poor hygiene practices at meat-handling locations such as abattoirs and butcher shops. Despite interventions aimed at improving sanitation, the prevalence of meat contamination remains high, especially in rural and peri-urban areas like Iganga Municipality, Uganda. This study aimed to assess the sanitation and hygiene status of meat handling sites in Iganga Municipality, the level of carcass contamination with Escherichia coli 0157:H7, and the antibiotic susceptibility profile of Escherichia coli 0157:H7. This descriptive cross-sectional study involved meat handlers at meat handling sites in Iganga Municipality. Data were collected through structured interviews and observational checklists to assess hygiene practices. Carcass swabs were taken from meat samples and analyzed for the presence of Escherichia coli 0157:H7. The antibiotic susceptibility of the isolates was determined using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method. The study revealed that 86.6% of meat handlers had received personal hygiene training; however, improper practices, such as low usage of personal protective equipment (PPE) and inadequate handwashing, persisted. The prevalence of Escherichia coli 0157:H7 contamination in carcass samples was 16%, indicating significant health risks. Antibiotic resistance was high for commonly used antibiotics like ampicillin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, tetracycline, nalidixic acid, and nitrofurantoin, although susceptibility to gentamicin and ciprofloxacin remained robust. Study respondents who did not undergo any personal hygiene training [AOR = 2.48, CI = 2.06–3.88, P = 0.01] were 2.48 times more likely to have their carcasses contaminated with E. coli 0157:H7 compared to those who received training. Furthermore, the odds of carcass contamination with E. coli 0157:H7 were 3.11 times higher [AOR = 3.11, CI = 2.02–5.51, P = 0.04] among study respondents who reported experiencing diarrhea in the last two weeks compared to those who did not. Additionally, respondents who reported using soap/detergent during handwashing [AOR = 0.06, CI = 0.02–0.22, P = 0.03] were 0.94 times less likely to have their carcasses contaminated with E. coli 0157:H7 compared to those who did not use soap/detergent. Poor hygiene practices and the high prevalence of multidrug-resistant E. coli 0157:H7 in meat handling sites in Iganga Municipality underscore the necessity for stricter enforcement of hygiene standards and antibiotic stewardship. Ongoing training for meat handlers and enhanced sanitation protocols are essential for reducing contamination and protecting public health.

Background

The primary cause of disease and mortality in developing nations, along with associated medical and societal costs amounting to billions of dollars, is food-borne infections1. While the burden of foodborne disease is a global public health concern, developing countries experience the highest incidence and death rates2. Studies indicate that each year, 1 in 10 people become ill due to microbial food contamination, resulting in 600 million illnesses, 420,000 deaths, and a loss of 33 million healthy years of life worldwide3. This often occurs after consuming contaminated food, particularly from animal products such as meat from infected animals or carcasses tainted with pathogenic bacteria, including Escherichia coli.

Escherichia coli is a group of bacteria residing in the gastrointestinal tracts of mammals commensally, often without pathogenic effects to the animals. Most E. coli strains are also nonpathogenic to humans, but the detection of Escherichia coli 0157:H7 in foods intended for human consumption shows poor hygiene in production, processing, or preparation. Some E. coli are directly pathogenic to humans, and the best example is the Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli 0157:H7, which can cause severe enteric infections1,2. However, it is acknowledged that the importance of some serotypes may vary by geographic area3. And that there are various Enterohemorrhagic Escherichia Coli serotypes, some of which could be more significant than Escherichia Coli 0157:H7 in some countries4.

Globally, Escherichia Coli 0157: H7 causes 2,801,000 acute illnesses annually with an incidence rate of 43.1 cases per 100,000 persons per year. Among those, a total of 10,200,000 cases of Escherichia Coli 0157:H7 infections occur in Africa with an incidence rate of 1.4 cases per 100,000 people per year5.

In sub-Saharan Africa, meat handling and hygiene practices by butcher operators were rated at 54%, which explained the high prevalence of 5–55% of food-borne infections per year in different countries of Africa6. Most Sub-Saharan countries are reported to have introduced regulations governing meat hygiene practices. Still, the level of adherence is very low, especially in the rural areas where hygiene facilities such as hand washing sinks, toilets, clean serving environments, taps, and other necessities are unavailable7. In East Africa, adherence to meat hygiene practices by beef vendors has been reported8 to be 51%, which is very low. This means 49% of butcheries in East Africa do not adhere to beef processing hygiene practices, which results in outbreaks of infections related to poor hygiene.

In Uganda, several studies9,10 have reported that 14% of all diseases treated in Uganda each year were due to consumption of contaminated meat, which gets contaminated at the points of sale. Whereas 31.5% of the butchery operators in Kampala District were reported to adhere to the use of personal protective wear, 68.5% did not observe proper meat hygiene practices. More recently, it was established that 75% to 80% of butcheries in rural Uganda lacked cooling facilities and were associated with unhygienic handling practices of meat10. Most human E. coli cases have been linked to direct or indirect contact with cattle, but some have been linked to other species, including sheep, goats (unpasteurized goat’s milk), dry-fermented pork salami, deer, horses, rabbits, and birds. However, epidemiological studies have found that cattle manure is the most common source of human E. coli infection11–13. Beef is the most common vehicle among foodborne outbreaks of Escherichia coli 157: H714. These foodborne outbreaks are usually challenging to control, especially if the bacteria involved are resistant to the commonly used antibiotics. Moreover, the resistant clones of bacteria have gained a global distribution, with over 90% resistant to the commonly prescribed antibiotics15.

This study aimed to assess the sanitation, hygiene status, and Carcass contamination with Escherichia coli 0157:H7 at meat handling sites in Iganga municipality. This study sought to assess the sanitation and hygiene status of meat handling sites in Iganga Municipality, the level of carcass contamination with E. coli 0157:H7, and the antibiotic susceptibility profile of E. coli 0157: H7 to highlight the magnitude of the problem in our setting.

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

A descriptive cross-sectional study carried out between July 2024 and September 2024 involved meat handlers at abattoirs and butcher shops in Iganga Municipality.

Study population and sampling

The study population consisted of 260 human subjects who handled the meat carcasses and workers at the selected butcheries in Iganga municipality, and 430 carcasses. This sample size was arrived at using Kish and Leslie’s formula for cross-sectional studies. This study area was selected due to rampant cases of diarrheal illness reported in the Uganda Health Management Information System, most of which could be attributed to food contamination. The study units were carcasses from selected slaughtered animals and workers who operated in the selected meat-handling sites. A systematic random sampling method was employed to select the study unit at each abattoir and butcher shop. On each sampling day, usually once a week, 7–10 animals were selected.

Data collection methods

A questionnaire was used to collect data from abattoir workers, supervisors, and meat-handling sites. The observational checklist was employed during a guided interview with butcher sellers and meat transporters. These questionnaires and observation checklists were designed to reflect the minimum hygiene requirements for meat handling sites, and they include both multiple-choice questions and open-ended questions, and adopted and modified questions from different studies9.

Culture and Identification of E. coli 0157:H7

The types of samples collected from meat handling sites were stool swabs from slaughtered animals. All the samples for the study were collected under strict aseptic procedures and then transported in an icebox to the Microbiology laboratory of Busitema University for microbiological culture to detect E. coli 0157:H7 and sensitivity testing. The stool samples were cultured on Sorbitol MacConkey agar and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Presumptive identification was done using colony characteristics and Biochemical identification methods, including Indole, Voges-Proskauer, and citrate tests. Further identification was done by serotyping with antisera.

Serotyping

The translucent E. coli colonies from sorbitol MacConkey, after biochemical identification, were sub-cultured on Mueller Hinton agar and subjected to serotyping using rapid diagnostic E. coli 0157: H7 antisera (Difco Laboratories, Detroit, USA). One drop of sterile normal saline was added to a slide, followed by a drop of free-falling antisera containing monoclonal antibodies to the E. coli 0157: H7 antigens. A pure colony of each test isolate from Mueller-Hinton agar was emulsified into the mixture. The slides containing the mixture were then rocked in a circular motion for 60 s and observed for visible agglutination.

Antimicrobial resistance profiling

The Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method was used following the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Pure colonies of E. coli 0157:H7 (18–24-hour growth) were emulsified in 0.85% physiological saline, and a 0.5 McFarland concentration of inoculum was prepared. This was spread on Mueller-Hinton agar and antibiotic discs, which were applied, followed by incubation at 35 °C for 18–24 h. Isolates were regarded as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant according to the inhibition zone around the disc. The antibiotics were tested including; Ampicillin (10 µg), Amoxicillin/Clavulanate (10 µg), Ceftazidime (30 µg), Ceftriaxone (30 µg), Cefotaxime (30 µg), Cefepime (30 µg), Imipenem (10 µg), Ertapenem (10 µg), Amikacin (30 µg), Nalidixic acid (30 µg), Gentamicin (10 µg), Ciprofloxacin (5 µg), Tetracycline (30 µg), Nitrofurantoin (300 µg) and Trimethoprim Sulfamethoxazole (23.5 µg). The selection of the antibiotic panel was guided by the treatment guidelines of the Uganda Ministry of Health.

Inclusion criteria

Adults aged 18 years and above who worked at the selected meat handling sites for at least one month participated in the study.

Exclusion criteria

New workers who had joined within one month at the meat handling site never participated in the study, and Workers who never consented to the study.

Data analysis and presentation

STATA (version 15.1) was used for analysis. Descriptive statistics of percentages, frequencies, and multivariate logistic regression were calculated.

Ethical considerations

Ethical clearance was obtained from Busitema University Faculty of Health Sciences Research and Ethics Committee no. BUFHS-2024-172. Permission was obtained from Iganga municipal authority for data collection, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants in the study during data collection in line with guidelines set by good clinical practice and the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology (UNCST). The participants had complete right to withdraw from the study at any time without any explanation and without affecting their right to the standard of the Community and Public Health Department. No person identifiers were used during analysis to ensure confidentiality. The study had minimal potential risks to participants, and all possible measures were taken to prevent any harm to participants during this investigation. The study had no direct benefits to participants, but the data collected will be used for better management of meat handling sites in Iganga Municipal Council.

Level of sanitation and hygiene status of meat handling sites

The majority of the study participants, 86.6% (225/260), reported having ever been trained on personal hygiene, 82.7% (215/260) conducted regular checkups for foodborne diseases, and 93.8% (244/260) had ever suffered from a diarrheal disease. About 80.4% reported washing their hands before starting work, 57.7% after visiting the toilet, and 43.8% after touching dirty objects. No one washed their hands when passing on work from one seller to another. Use of soap/detergent for washing was reported at 83.5% (217/260). Only 2.7% of the study participants reported using gloves, and 14.2% had hair covered.

About 88.5% (230/260) reported having a separate storage for clean and dirty clothes, 62.3% (162/260) wore jewelry and, wristwatch during working, and 73.8% (192/260) reported that there was a regular cleaning procedure at the shop. Over three-quarters, 64.2% (167/260) reported that their equipment was placed in the hot water to have a disinfecting effect (Table 2).

Prevalence of carcass contamination with E.coli 0157:H7

A total of 16.0% (69/432) of the tested samples were confirmed to be contaminated with Escherichia coli 0157: H7.

Antibiotic susceptibility profile of E. coli 0157:H7 associated with carcass contamination

The highest resistance of Escherichia coli 0157:H7 to the antibiotics was noted in Ampicillin (62/69) and Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (Co-trimoxazole) (56/69). The resistance rates were lowest in cefotaxime (4/69), and Imipenem (5/69), whereas no resistance was registered with amikacin (69/69) (Fig. 1). Antibiotic resistance assessment was done in accordance with the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines following the essential medicines list of Uganda.

Upon multivariate logistic regression analysis, suffering from diarrheal diseases, and not using soap/detergent for hand washing were significantly associated with carcass contamination at slaughterhouses. Study respondents who did not undergo any training regarding personal hygiene [AOR = 2.48, CI = 2.06–3.88, P = 0.01] were 2.48 times more likely to have their carcasses contaminated with Escherichia coli 0157:H7 compared to those who underwent training. Also, the odds of carcass contamination with E.coli 0157:H7 were 3.11 higher [AOR = 3.11, CI = 2.02–5.51, P = 0.04] among study respondents who reported to have suffered from diarrhea in the last 2 weeks compared to those who didn’t at 95% confidence interval. Those who reported using soap/detergent during hand washing [AOR = 0.06, CI = 0.02–0.22, P = 0.03] were 0.94 times less likely to have their carcasses contaminated with E.coli 0157:H7 compared to those who didn’t use soap/detergent (Table 3).

Discussion, conclusion, and recommendations

Level of sanitation and hygiene status of meat handling sites

This section of discussion focuses on the sanitation practices, hygiene measures and associated risks at meat handling sites, shedding light on the progress made and the areas that still require significant improvement to ensure public safety.

The study reported that 86.6% of participants had received personal hygiene training, and it was significantly associated with carcass contamination. Those who were trained were more likely to have their meat contaminated with Escherichia coli 0157:H7, which is similar to findings in a study by Amoah (2021) that found that over 80% of food handlers had formal hygiene training; unfortunately, they were not implementing the preventive measures at the meat handling sites. Yet training is critical in reducing foodborne diseases, as handlers trained in hygiene are better equipped to implement safe practices at the meat-handling site. The high percentage suggests good knowledge dissemination but requires assessment of how effectively this knowledge translates into practice, given that other behaviors (like hand washing) remain suboptimal.

A high proportion (82.7%) of participants conducted regular checkups for foodborne diseases compared to those in a study by16, which showed only 54.2% of the participants conducted checkups. Regular checkups are essential for identifying carriers of foodborne pathogens. Studies such as those by Todd (2019) indicate that routine checkups reduce the risk of food contamination. However, in low-resource settings, the availability of regular health screenings for food handlers may be limited. While the percentage is high, the effectiveness of these checkups depends on the thoroughness and frequency of the medical evaluations, especially in detecting asymptomatic carriers of pathogens.

The findings further revealed that almost all participants had suffered from a diarrheal disease. This finding was significantly associated with carcass contamination, where those who reported having suffered from a diarrheal disease in the last 2 weeks were more likely to have their meat contaminated with E. coli, similar to findings by Luis (2017). This is a critical finding, as diarrheal diseases are often linked to poor hygiene practices. According to the WHO (2020), foodborne diarrheal diseases are a major global concern, especially in areas with compromised sanitation standards. Studies show that meat handlers who have experienced foodborne diseases are more likely to adopt protective measures afterward16. The high prevalence suggests ongoing hygiene gaps, possibly in the handling and processing environments. It underlines the need for continuous hygiene training and stricter regulatory oversight at meat-handling sites.

Whereas 80.4% reported washing hands before starting work, only 57.7% did so after visiting the toilet, and 43.8% after touching dirty objects. No one washed their hands when passing work between sellers. Hand washing is one of the most effective means of preventing the spread of foodborne pathogens.

According to Luis (2017), inadequate hand washing among food handlers significantly increases contamination risks. The low percentage of hand hygiene after toilet use and after touching dirty objects mirrors findings from other studies that highlight inconsistencies in hand hygiene practices. The inconsistency in hand hygiene practices poses a significant risk for cross-contamination, particularly in environments like abattoirs where there is frequent contact with raw meat and potential pathogens.

The majority of the meat handlers (83.5%) reported using soap or detergent for hand washing. Those who washed their hands with soap/detergent were less likely to have their meat contaminated with E. coli 0157:H7. This finding is in agreement with a study conducted by17, which showed that over 80% of the study participants reported using soap/detergent for hand washing and had a significant association with meat contamination. A cross-sectional study by Osaili noted that hand washing with soap can reduce the bacterial load on hands significantly, which is critical in preventing pathogen transmission. However, effectiveness depends on adherence to recommended procedures. While soap use was common, ensuring that handlers wash their hands at critical points (for example, after restroom use) is essential for preventing contamination.

Only 2.7% used gloves and 14.2% used hair cover, similar to a cross-sectional study conducted by13 in Ethiopia. The use of PPE like gloves and hair covers is crucial for preventing contamination of food products, on hygiene compliance in food handling18. The low use suggests a significant gap in the sanitation and hygiene measures to prevent contamination of food with disease-causing organisms. This gap exposes meat to contaminants from food handlers’ hands and hair, contributing to a higher risk of foodborne diseases. Increasing awareness and mandating PPE use can mitigate contamination risks.

About 88.5% reported having separate storage for clean and dirty clothes, while 62.3% wore jewelry or wristwatches during work. This is slightly higher than findings in a separate study by19, which showed that only 57% had separate storage for clean and dirty clothes. The difference in study findings could be attributed to strict policies regarding storage for clean and dirty clothes in the later study. Proper storage of work attire minimizes cross-contamination risks, and this practice is commendable.

However, jewelry, as noted in research by the World Health Organization (2020), can harbor bacteria and contribute to contamination. Jewelry should be prohibited at meat-handling sites to reduce contamination risk. Encouraging separate clothing storage is a positive finding, but it must be complemented by discouraging jewelry use.

Additionally, 73.8% reported regular cleaning procedures at the shop. The finding is similar to a cross-sectional study conducted by20, which showed that 75% had regular cleaning of meat-handling environments reduces bacterial load on surfaces. Consistent cleaning protocols are essential for maintaining hygiene standards in food preparation areas (2017). While cleaning procedures are in place, it is paramount to ensure strict adherence to protocols, especially during high-risk activities like handling raw meat.

Over three-quarters (75.9%) placed equipment in hot water for disinfection. This is in agreement with a cross-sectional study conducted in Kenya by21, which showed that three-quarters of the study respondents reported always placing equipment in hot water for disinfection. Heat disinfection is a well-documented method for killing bacteria like E. coli, which showed that hot water reduces bacterial contamination effectively. The high rate of equipment disinfection was a positive trend to ensure good hygiene. However, ensuring that the water temperature is consistently high enough for proper disinfection is crucial.

Prevalence of carcass contamination with E. coli 0157:H7

The finding that 16% of the carcass samples tested were contaminated with E. coli 0157:H7 is significant and highlights potential public health risks associated with the meat-handling practices in the abattoirs and meat-handling sites in Iganga municipality. The low awareness of zoonotic diseases, Lack of training, cultural practices, inconsistent enforcement of meat safety regulations, limited veterinary presence, and inadequate microbial testing capacity directly affects hygiene and sanitation. Studies on E. coli 0157:H7 contaminations in abattoirs vary by region. For instance, a study in Ethiopia22 reported a contamination rate of 7.2% in abattoir meat, which is lower than these findings. However, higher rates have been found in other parts of Africa, with a study in Nigeria showing up to 20% contamination in beef samples in Africa. This is largely because meat is mostly handled by the informal sector with inadequate infection prevention and control knowledge. Whereas a study done in Uganda recovered E. coli from cattle stool samples, it reported a similar prevalence to the one reported in this study (2020). The global prevalence of Escherichia coli 0157:H7 in carcasses ranges between 1 and 20%, depending on hygiene practices and regional factors. Therefore, these findings are on the higher end of this spectrum. The relatively high contamination rate in Iganga indicates potential lapses in sanitation and hygiene practices during slaughtering, processing, and handling. These lapses could include improper cleaning of carcasses, cross-contamination from equipment or handlers, or failure to adhere to safety protocols.

Escherichia coli 0157:H7 is typically found in the intestines of animals, particularly cattle, and can contaminate meat during slaughter if there is improper handling or inadequate cleaning of carcasses. Studies suggest that fecal contamination during the evisceration process is the most common route of contamination in Africa. This study’s contamination rate aligns with findings from other studies that show a direct link between carcass contamination and poor hygiene during slaughter and meat processing. A similar study in Kenya23 highlighted that poor hygiene in abattoirs led to contamination of up to 16.7% of beef samples with E. coli 0157:H7, which is close to the results of this study. This highlights the importance of strict hygiene and sanitation controls at every stage of meat processing, from slaughter to handling and storage. Improving personal hygiene practices among workers, such as frequent hand washing and the use of protective gear like gloves, can significantly reduce contamination.

Escherichia coli 0157:H7 is a serious public health hazard due to its potential to cause severe illness. According to the CDC, Escherichia coli 0157:H7 is responsible for severe gastrointestinal infections, including hemorrhagic colitis and hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS), which can be fatal, especially in children, the elderly, and immune-compromised individuals (CDC, 2017). Outbreaks associated with contaminated meat have been documented globally, emphasizing the public health risk posed by even relatively low levels of contamination24. Given the high contamination rate, there is a significant risk to consumers, particularly if the meat is consumed undercooked. This calls for immediate public health interventions, including stricter regulatory controls in abattoirs, enhanced food safety awareness for meat consumers and measures to ensure meat is cooked thoroughly.

Antibiotic susceptibility profile of Escherichia coli 0157:H7 associated with carcass contamination

The study reported a high resistance to ampicillin, a widely used β-lactam antibiotic, is concerning but consistent with other studies globally. Resistance to ampicillin among E. coli strains has been reported in several studies. For instance, in a study from Nigeria, resistance rates as high as 88.7% to ampicillin in Escherichia coli isolates were found in Nigeria. The wide use and often inappropriate administration of β-lactam antibiotics, such as ampicillin, in both human and veterinary settings contribute to this resistance.

The significant resistance observed in Trimethoprim /sulfamethoxazole (46/69) could be due to the extensive use of this combination as a first-line treatment for a variety of bacterial infections. A study in neighboring Kenya showed similar resistance patterns25, where E. coli showed high resistance to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Resistance to this drug combination is associated with the acquisition of mobile genetic elements like plasmids and integrons26.

Tetracycline resistance has been frequently reported in both human and animal isolates of Escherichia coli 0157:H7, largely due to its broad use in livestock production as a growth promoter and therapeutic agent. A study from India reported tetracycline resistance in 62.5% of E. coli 0157:H7 isolates from meat in India. The overuse of tetracycline in agriculture is a major driver of this resistance to this drug.

Resistance to Nalidixic acid is concerning as it can be a precursor to fluoroquinolone resistance. Nalidixic acid is often used in laboratories to test for fluoroquinolone susceptibility, and the development of resistance could signal future challenges in treating Escherichia coli 0157:H7 infections with this drug class. Another separate cross-sectional study27 also revealed resistance of Escherichia Coli to nalidixic acid.

Gentamicin is an aminoglycoside antibiotic and its high susceptibility indicates it remains effective against Escherichia coli 0157:H7. This aligns with findings from other studies, such as in Egypt, where susceptibility to gentamicin was observed in 90% of Escherichia coli 0157: H7 isolates in Egypt. Its use in treating serious Gram-negative infections remains relevant. Amikacin, another aminoglycoside, showed maximum susceptibility (100%). This antibiotic is often reserved for severe infections, and the lack of resistance may suggest limited overuse in both human and animal health, preserving its efficacy. A cross-sectional study conducted by28 also shows maximum susceptibility of Escherichia coli 0157:H7 to Amikacin.

Ciprofloxacin, a fluoroquinolone, was highly effective against Escherichia coli, and the susceptibility observed in this study aligns with global trends where resistance to ciprofloxacin remains lower compared to other antibiotics29. However, resistance to nalidixic acid could indicate emerging fluoroquinolone resistance, warranting careful monitoring30.

The β-lactam antibiotics and carbapenems showed good susceptibility. Carbapenems like imipenem are often the last resort for treating multidrug-resistant bacterial infections, and the susceptibility seen here is encouraging. The susceptibility to cephalosporins tested indicates that these antibiotics are still useful against Escherichia coli 0157:H7, although ESBL production could potentially compromise their future efficacy. The findings from this study are in agreement with those in a cross-sectional study conducted in Nigeria31, which showed similar susceptibility patterns.

Conclusions

The findings of this study reveal significant public health concerns regarding sanitation and hygiene practices in meat-handling sites in the Iganga municipality. Despite a high percentage of workers having received personal hygiene training and the majority conducting regular health checkups, lapses in critical hygiene practices, such as hand washing after touching dirty objects or when passing work from one seller to another, were evident. The low usage of personal protective equipment (PPE) further emphasizes the need for strengthened enforcement of hygiene protocols. These shortcomings in hygiene practices likely contribute to the high rates of diarrheal diseases reported by participants.

This study reported a high level of carcass contamination with Escherichia coli 0157:H7, posing a significant health risk to consumers. This contamination level underscores the need for improved hygiene and sanitation measures at meat-handling sites to minimize bacterial transmission through the food supply chain. Furthermore, the widespread use of improper practices, such as wearing jewelry and inadequate cleaning of equipment, contributes to the persistence of pathogens like E. coli on meat products.

Recommendations

Strengthening hygiene training and enforcement

Although the majority of participants reported receiving hygiene training, there are significant gaps in practice. It is essential to implement continuous, practical hygiene training for workers in abattoirs and meat handling sites, emphasizing critical points like hand washing after touching dirty objects, after using the toilet, and between handling different products. Supervisors and regulatory bodies should consider strictly monitoring and enforcing compliance with these hygiene practices.

Improving the use of personal protective equipment

Given the low use of PPE, it is recommended that all abattoir workers and meat handlers be mandated to wear appropriate PPE, including gloves, hair nets, and aprons, to reduce the risk of cross-contamination. Providing regular access to PPE and training on its correct use can significantly improve food safety.

Enhancing sanitation and regular cleaning procedures

To reduce carcass contamination with pathogens like Escherichia coli 0157:H7, there is a need to implement stricter sanitation protocols. This includes the regular cleaning of equipment and surfaces using disinfectants, as well as ensuring that all tools and machinery are sterilized effectively. Government agencies and local authorities should enforce the adoption of standard operating procedures (SOPs) for cleaning and disinfecting abattoir facilities.

Antimicrobial stewardship programs

The high resistance to common antibiotics, such as ampicillin and tetracycline, calls for stricter regulation and monitoring of antibiotic use in both animal husbandry and human medicine. Individuals and authorities should be aware of the magnitude of the problem through regulation by authorities. Antimicrobial stewardship programs should be introduced to promote the responsible use of antibiotics and limit the misuse of broad-spectrum antibiotics in livestock farming. Regular antibiotic resistance surveillance should be conducted to guide appropriate treatment regimens.

Limitations

This study used only phenotypic detection methods; therefore, we recommend a larger study using molecular methods.
