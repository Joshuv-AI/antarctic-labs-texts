Deubiquitination of NICD1 by USP10 Restrains Esophageal Cancer: A Link Between NOTCH1 Stabilization, Oxidative Stress, and Ferroptosis
Authors: Xiufeng Tang, Zhiqin Zhang, Xinyi Wei, Yanshuang Yang, Zongxin Shi, Haonan Fan, Minxuan Xu, Chengxin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of new drugs and focused treatment strategies is significantly hampered by the inadequate understanding of the complex pathophysiology of esophageal cancer (EC). Significant risk factors for the development of EC include imbalances in the oxidative stress and anti-oxidative response pathways. It has been discovered that therapeutic interventions that target the ubiquitin-specific protease 10 (USP10) and its targeted response element in response to oxidative stress can inhibit tumour growth and provide notable clinical benefits for cancer patients. To determine if USP10 is a ubiquitin-specific protease that controls oxidative stress, which in turn stabilises NOTCH1 and deactivates the anti-oxidative transcription-related pathway, and to investigate the impact of USP10 on the development of EC. As a major endogenous stabiliser of NOTCH1 intracellular domain (NICD1) function, we recognized USP10 as a protein that interacts with NICD1 and catalyses the deubiquitination of NICD1 in EC cells. Loss of USP10 specific to epithelial cells interferes with NICD1 stabilisation, which in turn stimulates the production of antioxidant genes to prevent ferroptosis caused by lipid peroxidation and significantly accelerates the development of esophageal cancer. On the other hand, in animal models treated with 4-NQO, transgenic overexpression-mediated USP10 gene therapy reduces the development of esophageal cancer. Mechanistically, USP10 binds to NICD1 in response to oxidative stress, deconjugates ubiquitination chains, and increases NICD1 abundance and the activation of its downstream signalling cascade. In vivo investigations have also shown that inhibiting USP10 expression significantly reduces the impacts that cause cancer. Importantly, a bad prognosis is indicated by reduced expression of USP10, which is linked to the severity of EC. According to these results, USP10 might be a suitable therapeutic target for the treatment of esophageal cancer.

Introduction

As the most common type of cancer in the population, esophageal cancer (EC) is widely acknowledged as a serious global public health issue. If not identified early, esophageal cancer can spread from the surface of the gullet to other organs such the stomach, liver, prostate gland, seminal vesicle gland, and uterus [1]. Even though the molecular mechanism behind the development of esophageal cancer has been extensively studied, further research is necessary to find novel and effective treatment strategies. Recent epidemiological research has found a link between esophageal diseases and oxidative stress [2,3]. It is important to note that the prevalence of esophageal cancer is rising dramatically on a global scale, primarily as a result of widespread dietary issues and the consequent development of metabolic syndrome linked to irregular schedules [4,5]. The genetic alterations in esophageal cancer, such as mutations in TP53, KRAS, and EGFR, have been thoroughly described in recent reports [[6], [7], [8]]. Esophageal cancer is a very variable cancer that has a high death rate and a poor response to treatment. Disruption of immunological, metabolic, and genetic homeostasis is believed to be a major contributing factor to oesophageal cancer, even if the precise origin of the disease and the tumours it causes is yet unknown [9,10].

Tissue homeostasis, embryonic development, and the aetiology of specific diseases, including cancer, chronic metabolic disorders, and cardiovascular disease, rely on the NOTCH signalling pathway, an evolutionarily conserved mechanism. Upon the binding of a donor cell's NOTCH ligand to a recipient cell's NOTCH receptor, ADAM and γ-secretase cleave the receptor, resulting in the release of the NOTCH intracellular domain (NICD) [11]. After being released, NICD1 binds to target genes like Hes1 to activate their expression, which in turn causes associated disorders [12]. Among the NOTCH signalling components, NOTCH1 is crucial in mediating the development of metabolic diseases by controlling a variety of biological processes, including apoptosis, fibrosis, inflammatory response, and oxidative stress [13]. Of note, in response to reactive oxygen species (ROS) production during tumor progression, NOTCH1 loss promotes cancer development by directly increasing antioxidant gene expression, ultimately suppressing ferroptosis [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. However, recent studies have indicated that increasing abundance of NOTCH1 significantly facilitates cancer progression by promoting Nrf2-associated anti-oxidative stress response [18,19]. Inimitably, NOTCH1 signaling can suppress Nrf2 activity, or vice versa, depending on the context. Notch activation can lead to increased Nrf2 expression, but also potentially inhibit its activity through the Keap1 protein [20,21]. Conversely, Nrf2 can regulate Notch activity, impacting various cellular processes. Therefore, these investigations show that NOTCH1 has a unique role in the pathophysiology of various cancer types, albeit the exact mechanism behind oesophageal cancer is yet unknown.

By modifying the ubiquitination of particular substrates to promote signal transmission, ubiquitin-specific protease 10 (USP10), a prominent member of the deubiquitinating enzyme (DUB) superfamily, has been identified as a "multi-faceted" regulator in innate immune responses [22,23]. Prior research has demonstrated that USP10 contributes to carcinogenesis through deubiquitinating alteration in conjunction with p53, a significant tumour suppressor [24]. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that USP10 deubiquitinates, stabilises PTEN, and then initiates a downstream signalling cascade [25]. Recent research has demonstrated that USP10 is essential for the development of cancer associated with NOTCH1 [26]. By controlling USP10-NOTCH1 oncogenic signalling, NOTCH1 degradation-associated regulatory polypeptide (N1DARP) functions as a tumour suppressor and chemosensitizer [27]. It also suggests a potential treatment approach that targets the N1DARP-NICD1 relationship in NOTCH1-activated pancreatic cancer [27,28]. These earlier discoveries demonstrated that USP10 did, in fact, carry out unique roles in many disease processes. Furthermore, the function of USP10, particularly in esophageal cancer pathologies, is still unclear; therefore, our investigations have led us to thoroughly identify USP10′s molecular mechanism.

In this study, we found that oesophageal cancer samples from a 4-NQO-induced mice model have dramatically reduced USP10 activity. Notably, we found that USP10 was significantly downregulated in oesophageal tissues, which is inversely connected with the expression of cancer markers in human patients. In response to oxidative stress, pharmacological approaches targeting the USP10 and its targeted response element can limit tumour growth and improve cancer patients' clinical outcomes. To discover if USP10 is a ubiquitin-specific protease that controls oxidative stress, stabilises NOTCH1, and deactivates the anti-oxidative transcription-related pathway, and to examine how USP10 affects EC development. USP10, a significant endogenous stabiliser of NICD1 function, interacts with NICD1 and deubiquitinates it in EC cells. Loss of epithelial USP10 disrupts NICD1 stabilisation, which drives antioxidant gene expression to counteract ferroptosis induced by lipid peroxidation and accelerates esophageal cancer growth. However, transgenic overexpression-mediated USP10 gene therapy decreases esophageal cancer in 4-NQO-treated animal models. In response to oxidative stress, USP10 binds to NICD1, deconjugates ubiquitination chains, and increases NICD1 abundance and downstream signalling cascade activation. In vivo studies suggest that decreasing USP10 expression dramatically lowers cancer-causing effects. USP10 expression, which is connected to EC severity, is low and indicates a poor prognosis. These results imply that USP10 could be a useful target for oesophageal cancer therapy.

Materials and Methods

Research consent and ethical statement

The animal experiments in this study followed the guidelines outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (1996, in Chinese). The Shandong First Medical University, and the Institutional Animal Use and Care Committee all approved the procedures. Patients with oesophageal carcinoma (EC) phenotypes were recruited from Shandong Province's Key Traditional Chinese Medical Discipline of Clinical Chinese Pharmacy. The specific characteristics are presented in Supplemental Table 1.

Cell Culture

The present study employed the following human esophageal cancer cell lines: TE-11 (Cellosaurus, RRID: CVCL_1761), TE-14 (Cellosaurus, RRID: CVCL_3336), KYSE450 (Cellosaurus, RRID: CVCL_1353), EC9706 (Cellosaurus, RRID: CVCL_E307), KYSE50 (Cellosaurus, RRID: CVCL_1360), HKESC-1 (Cellosaurus, based on prior reports), AKR1 (ATCC Cat# TIB-232, RRID: CVCL_6565), human normal cell lines BAR-T (Cellosaurus, RRID: CVCL_4M95), HET-1A (Cellosaurus, RRID: CVCL_3702), mouse normal cell lines E13161-7 (Cellosaurus, RRID: CVCL_9172), and CDR2 (Cellosaurus, RRID:CVCL_H172). It was found that the entire cell line was free of mycoplasma. The culture of EC cell lines was conducted in RPMI1640 medium (Cat#: SLM-240, Sigma-Aldrich, China) supplemented with EmbryoMax® 1×penicillin-streptomycin solution (Cat#: TMS-AB2, Sigma-Aldrich, China) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Cat#: 12103C, Sigma-Aldrich, China). The cells were cultivated at 37°C in a humidified environment within culture plates containing 5% CO2. The standard cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 media augmented with 10% foetal bovine serum, as per the product and instruction manuals. Some cells were cultivated on 6-well, 12-well, or 24-well plates with 60% fusion for a whole day prior to cell transfection. As directed in the product description, the cells were then transfected using transfection reagent (#790310P, Sigma-Aldrich, China). To put it briefly, the transfection mixture contained 2 μg of vector, 5 μl of FuGENE® 6 DNA Transfection Reagent, and 130 μl of TheraPEAKTM, T-VIVOTM.

Knockout Cell and Knockdown Lines Preparation

The method for constructing knockout cell lines with certain gene ablation was built up in relation to the current investigation. Essentially, an EC cell line was created by deleting the USP10 or NOTCH1 genes using the CRISPR-Cas9 technology. The CRISPR-Cas9 sgRNA-bearing lentivirus was created by inserting a pre-made sgRNA that targets the human USP10 or NOTCH1 gene into pLentiCRISPRV2 vectors (Addgene). Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc. provided the pre-made CRISPR/Cas9 gene ablation kit for the human USP10 vector (#sc-404281) and NOTCH1 vector (#sc-400167). The BsmBI restriction enzyme cleaved the sgRNA oligonucleotides, which were then introduced into pLentiCRISPRV2 vectors. Western blotting was used to extract and identify the clones with gene deletions.

Transwell Assay

Transwell assay kit was used to quantify cell invasion and migration. The Matrigel (#356234, BD) was used to cultivate the Corning® Transwell® (#CLS3422, Corning, USA). The detected tumour cells were recultured in a serum-free cell culture media (#12-725F, LONZA) and subsequently positioned in the higher chamber at a abundance of 6.0 × 104 cells per well. Following this, the bottom chamber was mixed with the medium containing 10% foetal bovine serum, which was then given for 48 hours. A cotton swab was then used to completely remove the cells in the upper chamber. After being cleaned and preserved using a 4% formaldehyde solution, the cells that had moved into the lower chamber were stained with a 0.65% crystal violet stain. Lastly, a microscope was used to view the cells. The same methods were used for the migration calculation experiment, except that Matrigel was not used for pre-coating the upper chamber.

Animals and studies

The mouse (5-10 mice per cage) was housed in a SPF environment at a temperature of 22–25°C and under conventional laboratory settings, which included a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle. They were given either the usual laboratory normal diet (NCD, #1016706476803973120, Beijing Keaoxieli Feed Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) or unlimited access to water. Before being used, water bottles and cages were autoclaved. Three times a week, cages with regular corncob bedding were replaced. Littermate control mice were used in every in vivo experiment. There were no discernible variations in starting weight, health, or immune function based on the mouse genotype. For these investigations, only male mice were used because female mice are known to be resistant to cancer. During the surgical procedures and result evaluations, the researchers were blinded to the assignment of the several groups, which were assigned in a randomised fashion.

Esophagus epithelial-specific USP10 knockout mice (USP10CKO) with EC progression

Mice with a loss of USP10 specific to the oesophagus (USP10CKO) were created using ED-L2-Cre+/− recombinase, which expresses CRE recombinase only in epithelial cells. Jackson Laboratory provided the ED-L2-Cre mice (B6.Cg-Tg(ED-L2-cre)267Jkat, MGI:4462391) (Bar Harbour, ME, USA). Two loxP sites were introduced at exon 3 of the USP10 gene using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing to produce USP10flox/flox mice with a C57BL/6 background. Double heterozygous floxed USP10 mice (USP10flox/wildCre+/−) were created by crossing homozygous floxed USP10 mice (USP10flox/flox) with ED-L2-Cre+/− mice. After that, USP10flox/wildCre+/− mice were backcrossed to produce USP10 knockout mice (USP10CKO), which are specific to epithelial cells. The control mice (USP10flox/flox) had Cre expression and broad type alleles. Four groups of six-week-old male mice were randomly assigned: USP10flox/flox mice with saline (Saline/USP10flox/flox), USP10CKO mice with saline (Saline/USP10CKO), USP10flox/flox mice with 100 μg/mL 4-NQO (4-NQO/USP10flox/flox), and USP10CKO mice with 100 μg/mL 4-NQO (4-NQO/USP10CKO). 4-NQO model construction procedures were carried out as previously stated. Samples from the oesophagus were taken for histopathological computation and biochemical examination at the conclusion of 16 weeks. The esophageal tissues were gathered for a later investigation of protein or RNA extraction.

Esophagus epithelial-specific USP10 overexpression mice (USP10AAV) with EC progression

The adeno-associated virus (AAV2) technique was utilised to create high-specific expression of USP10 in esophageal tissues in male USP10CKO mice (5–6 weeks old). As a control, recombinant AAV2 containing empty vector (EV) cDNA was employed. The full-length USP10-containing recombinant AAV2 vector was also used to promote NOTCH1 expression, primarily in esophageal cells. Two sets of AAV-control (AAV-Control) and AAV-USP10 mice were randomly selected from among the USP10CKO animals. Via the tail vein, these mice were given 2 × 1011 vg genomic particles of either AAV2-USP10 (AAV-USP10) or AAV2-GFP (AAV2-Control) (Hanbio Biotechnology Inc., China).

Esophagus epithelial-specific NOTCH1 knockout mice (NOTCH1CKO) with EC progression

ED-L2-Cre+/− recombinase was used to create mice with an oesophageal epithelium-specific deletion of NOTCH1 (NOTCH1CKO). Jackson Laboratory provided the ED-L2-Cre mice (B6.Cg-Tg(ED-L2-cre)267Jkat, MGI:4462391) (Bar Harbour, ME, USA). Two loxP sites were placed into exons 3 & 4 of the NOTCH1 gene to create NOTCH1flox/flox mice using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated editing. Double heterozygous floxed NOTCH1 mice (NOTCH1flox/wildCre+/−) were produced by crossing homozygous floxed NOTCH1 mice (NOTCH1flox/flox) with ED-L2-Cre+/− mice. After that, NOTCH1flox/wild Cre+/−mice were backcrossed to produce NOTCH1 knockout mice (NOTCH1CKO) that are specific to epithelial cells. The controls were mice (NOTCH1flox/flox) with broad type alleles and Cre expression. The 6-week-old male mice were split into four groups at random: NOTCH1flox/flox mice receiving saline (Saline/NOTCH1flox/flox), NOTCH1CKO mice receiving saline (Saline/NOTCH1CKO), NOTCH1flox/flox mice receiving 100 μg/mL 4-NQO (4-NQO/NOTCH1flox/flox), and NOTCH1CKO mice receiving 100 μg/mL 4-NQO/NOTCH1CKO). 4-NQO model construction procedures were carried out as previously stated. Samples from the oesophagus were taken for histopathological computation and biochemical examination at the conclusion of 16 weeks. The oesophageal tissues were gathered for a later examination of protein or RNA extraction.

Esophagus epithelial-specific NOTCH1 overexpression mice (NOTCH1AAV) with EC progression

The male NOTCH1CKO mice (5-6 weeks old) were used to construct high-specific expression of NOTCH1 in esophagus tissues by using the adeno-associated virus (AAV2) method. Recombinant AAV2 carrying empty vector (EV) cDNA was used as a control. Recombinant AAV2 vector containing the full-length NOTCH1 was also utilized to facilitate the expression of NOTCH1 mainly in esophagus epithelial. The NOTCH1CKO mice were randomly divided into two groups: the AAV-control (AAV-Control) group and AAV-NOTCH1 group. These mice received 2 × 1011 vg genomic particles of AAV2-GFP (AAV2-Control) or AAV2-NOTCH1 (AAV-NOTCH1) (Hanbio Biotechnology Inc., China) via the tail vein.

Ferroptosis-related indexes determination and ROS production measurements

ChemicalBook provided the iNOS (#CSB-E08326m), Malondialdehyde (MDA) colorimetric assay kit (#E0597Ge), and GSH and GSSG test kits (#CB14698413, UK). In order to determine the levels of NO (#A012-1-2), H2O2 (#A064-5-1), XO (#A002-1-1), XDH (#H520-1), superoxide anion (#A052-1-1), and 8-OHdG (#H165-1-1), assay kit obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng BioEngineering Institute (Jiangsu, China) was used. The Fe2+ concentrations in the treated EC cells were measured using the QuantiChromTM Iron Assay Kit (#DIFE-048, BioAssay Systems) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. In short, the specified cells (1 × 106) were lysed by sonication after being resuspended in 100 μl of iron assay buffer. After centrifugation, the iron probe was incubated with the supernatants for 30 minutes at 37°C. Each sample's absorbance readings were then determined at a wavelength of 593 nm. The ATP levels in EC cells were determined using an ATP Assay Kit (#KTB1019-S, Abbkine) by generating an ATP standard curve and assessing the supernatant concentration in compliance with the manufacturer's guidelines. In the meantime, a BCA protein assay kit was used to quantify the protein concentrations of the cellular supernatants based on the manufacturer's instructions, and the amount of ATP in each µg of protein was finally computed. Following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer, tissues or cells were homogenised and centrifuged. ELISA kits for serum mouse IL-6 (ab222503), IL-18 (ab216165), TNF alpha (ab208348), IL-1beta (ab242234), and MCP1 (ab208979) were acquired from Abcam. The ROS generation was detected using dihydroethidium (DHE) (#T2021, TargetMol Chemicals Inc) or ROS assay kit (#C0177, TargetMol Chemicals Inc). The corresponding images were captured and evaluated by fluorescence microscope (KEYENCE).

RNA Sequencing

RNA was extracted from the pertinent EC cells utilising the TRNzol universal reagent in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer system assessed the RNA integrity of the samples, while the BioPhotometer Plus (Eppendorf) quantified the RNA samples. In accordance with the vendor's guidelines, RNA sequencing libraries were constructed with Thermo Fisher Scientific's Collibri Stranded RNA Library Prep Kit. GENEWIZ, LLC got 35 million reads per sample, and the Illumina HiSeq equipment was used for the sequencing process using a 2 × 150 Paired End configuration. Using whole RNA as the starting material, sequencing libraries were constructed using the Small RNA-Seq Library Prep Kit. In conclusion, Sera-Mag SpeedBead particles were used to separate the messenger RNA (mRNA) from the total RNA, and the mRNA was then chemically fragmented. Reverse transcription was used to convert the RNA fragments into complementary DNA (cDNA) using random primers that had a tagging sequence on their 3′ ends. The KAPA high-fidelity DNA polymerase was then used to amp up the cDNA libraries. The Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer was used to validate the quality of the libraries. After that, an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 was used to perform a high-throughput sequencing process. The biological pathway analysis was carried out with Cluster-Profiler after the sequences were mapped against either Homo sapiens or Mus musculus using the STAR or HISAT2 software. The technical staff of Nanjing Biomed Sciences Research Bio-Lab performed the RNA sequencing and library construction.

Real time RT-qPCR assay and Western blot analysis

The detection of mRNA expression levels and protein expression levels was carried out using commercial kits (#RR014A, PrimeScript™ RT-PCR Kit, TaKaRa; #926-32081, Western Blotting Kit I RD (PBS/PVDF), LICORbio), and the procedures were conducted according to our previous reports [[29], [30], [31], [32]].

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

As for IF staining, HEY1 (#ab154077), HES1 (#ab108937), HO1 (#ab189491), NQO1 (#ab80588), and 4-HNE (#ab48506) were conducted on paraffin-embedded EC samples sections (10 μm thick). The samples were boiled in pH 9.0 EDTA buffer for 20 minutes in a pressure cooker in order to extract antigens. To remove endogenous peroxide activity, the samples were cooled and then treated with 3% H2O2 for 20 minutes. The slides were blocked with 10% BSA (#V900933, Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C for one hour after being cleaned with PBS. After that, the sections were incubated with the designated primary antibodies for an entire night at 4°C. The following day, the sections underwent successive washes with PBS buffer for 5 min before being incubated with corresponding second antibodies at 37°C for 1 h. Images were finally captured using a light microscope (Leica, Germany). Histological images were quantified utilizing Image-J software (v1.8.0, Microsoft Windows, USA).

Cell Proliferation Detection

For the cell proliferation assay, exponentially proliferating oesophageal cancer cells were collected using a 0.30% trypsin buffer (#HY-129047, MedChemExpress) and subsequently re-cultured in complete media to create a single-cell suspension with an ultimate concentration of 5 × 10³ cells/mL. Subsequently, 120 μL of the cell suspension was dispensed into each well of a 96-well cell culture plate, with three replicate wells established for each experimental group and a blank control group (containing just full media without cells). The plate was maintained in 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 hours to facilitate cell adherence. After initial adhesion, 10 μL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) kit (#K1018, APExBIO Technology) was added to each well, and the plate was further incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 2 h in the dark to avoid reagent photodegradation. The absorbance (A) value of each well at a wavelength of 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Cat. No. Infinite M200 Pro, Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland), with the blank control group used to calibrate the microplate reader and eliminate background interference. The relative cell proliferation rate was detected based on the measured A values, and each experiment was independently repeated three times to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the experimental results.

Immunoprecipitation and S-Protein pull down assay

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and S-Protein pull-down assays were detected following modified protocols based on previous works [33,34]. For IP, cells were lysed in E1A lysis buffer on ice for 30 minutes. The USP10-specific primary antibody (Cat# ab188814, Abcam, UK) was pre-conjugated to Protein A/G Beads (#C0132, TargetMol) at 4°C for 2 hours, and the antibody-bead complex was then incubated with the cell lysates overnight at 4°C with gentle rotation. For the S-Protein pull-down (SFB) assay, TE-11 cells transfected with SFB-tagged recombinant plasmids were lysed in NETN buffer at 4°C for 25 minutes. The crude lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 20 minutes at 4°C to remove insoluble debris, and the resulting supernatants were incubated with S-Protein Agarose Resin (Cat# 69704, Merck Millipore, Germany) for 3.5 hours at 4°C with constant mixing. After incubation, both IP and pull-down resin complexes were washed four times with ice-cold NETN buffer to eliminate non-specific bindings. To identify target protein interactions, bound proteins were eluted by boiling the resin in 2× SDS loading buffer (Cat# P0015, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) for 10 minutes at 95°C. The eluted samples were then subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.

GST pull-down assay

The GST pull-down was performed to verify the factors binding between USP10 and NOTCH1. First, the full-length sequences of USP10 and NOTCH1 were respectively cloned into pGEX-4T-1 (GST-tagged, Cat. No. 28-9545-49, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) and pET-28a (+) (His-tagged, Cat. No. 69864-3, Novagen, Madison, WI, USA) prokaryotic expression vectors. The recombinant plasmids pGEX-4T-1-USP10, pET-28a-NOTCH1, and empty vectors (as negative controls) were separately transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells (Cat. No. C2527I, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). The protocol of GST pull-down assay was performed using GST Protein Interaction Pull-Down Kit (#EA-IP-K008, Elabscience) in accordance with previous works [[29], [30], [31], [32]].

Tissue Preparation of Single-cell (scRNA-seq) Analysis

Following the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, the Shandong First Medical University Ethics Committee granted local approval for the procurement of human esophagus tissue for scRNA-seq and histological analysis (Ethics approval number: 2024XJ001). Written informed consent was given by each participant. The healthy donors (n = 2), esophagus cancer stage 1 (n = 2), esophagus cancer stage 2 (n = 2), esophagus cancer stage 3 (n = 2), and esophagus cancer stage 4 (n = 2) patients were included in the scRNA-seq study. In brief, the freshly prepared human esophagus tissue were placed on ice in RIPM-1640-contaning 15% FBS medium. Then, the samples were ground to achieve a single-cell suspension, which was then digested in a 37°C pre-warmed medium containing 15 mM HEPES (#15630080, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 500 μg/mL Liberase TM (S#5401127001, Roche). The samples were then placed on ice with EDTA added to a final concentration of 15 mM to stop Liberase digestion. The cells were then rinsed twice in Hanks solution-containing 3% FBS and filtered through a 40 μm mesh filter. Before being used in scRNA-seq assay, the samples that were taken at various groups were frozen and kept in liquid nitrogen. Next, the prepared single cell suspension of esophagus was washed twice using Hanks solution-containing 0.4% BSA. The concentration was adjusted to 700–1200 cells/μl prior to single-cell analysis, and the Countess II Automated Cell Counter was used to count the single-cell suspensions. Trypan blue exclusion verified that the overall cell viability was over 85%.

Library Construction and Sequencing of 10× Genomics Chromium

Single-cell libraries were constructed using the 10× Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3.1 (Cat# 1000121, 10× Genomics, USA) strictly following the manufacturer’s standard protocols, with single-cell suspensions loaded onto the 10× Genomics Chromium Controller to capture approximately 10,000 individual cells. Subsequent steps including reverse transcription, cDNA amplification, and library preparation were executed based on the optimized guidelines provided by the kit. The assembled libraries underwent high-throughput sequencing at Novogene Bioinformatics utilising an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with a paired-end 150 bp sequencing approach, guaranteeing a minimum sequencing depth of 25,000 reads per cell for dependable data collection. Raw sequencing data were processed with CellRanger software to produce single-cell gene expression matrices, with reads aligned to the reference genome utilising the STAR aligner (v2.7.11b, https://github.com/alexdobin/STAR) for precise splicing-aware mapping. The CellRanger-derived output files were imported into Seurat software (v4.3.0) for comprehensive scRNA-seq data analysis, starting with quality control filtering that retained 128,742 high-quality cells-excluding genes expressed in fewer than 5 cells, cells with 600–9500 expressed genes, unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts below 600, and mitochondrial gene expression ratios exceeding 20%. To minimise technical artefacts, doublet cells were removed using DoubletFinder (v2.0.4), and Seurat's "LogNormalize" approach was used to normalise gene expression levels. The top 3000 highly variable genes were subjected to Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and the top 1500 variable genes were used for dimensionality reduction for cell subsets. Batch effects were corrected using the R package Harmony (v0.2.0) with default parameters, which iteratively adjusted PCA embeddings based on the top 30 PCA components. Lastly, the first 30 harmony-corrected components were used to visualise and analyse cell populations using cell clustering and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for non-linear dimensionality reduction.

Gene Ontology and KEGG Pathway Analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted to identify biologically meaningful terms significantly overrepresented in differentially expressed genes (DEGs) relative to the entire genomic background, with a focus on filtering DEGs linked to distinct molecular functions, cellular components, and biological processes. First, all target genes were annotated to corresponding GO terms by querying the latest release of the GO database (http://www.geneontology.org/, Release 2025-03-16), followed by counting the number of genes assigned to each GO term. A hypergeometric distribution test was used to evaluate the statistical significance of GO term enrichment in DEGs compared to the genomic background, with FDR < 0.05 set as the threshold for significant enrichment. For pathway-level analysis to gain insights into the biological functions and interaction networks of DEGs, the KEGG Mapper database was used to conduct KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, which identified metabolic pathways, signal transduction cascades, and other biological pathways notably enriched in DEGs when contrasted with the whole-genome background. Additionally, GSEA for both GO and KEGG terms was implemented using the R packages with RNA-seq datasets, and the resulting enrichment results were visualized as bubble plots, bar charts, and enrichment maps using the "ggplot2" (v3.5.0) and "enrichplot" (v1.22.0) R packages to facilitate intuitive interpretation of the functional relevance of DEGs.

Statistical analysis

All experiments in this study were conducted in triplicate with independent biological replicates to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the results. The raw data underwent rigors statistical evaluation using appropriate methods, as detailed in the corresponding figure legends. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analyses were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (v10.0.0 for Microsoft Windows; GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA) and R statistical software (Version 4.4.0), supplemented with packages including "stats" and "ggplot2". For comparisons between two independent groups, a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was employed, whereas one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was utilised for multiple group comparisons to ascertain significant differences among experimental groups. The normality of data distribution was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was evaluated by Levene’s test; in cases of unequal variances, Welch’s ANOVA with Dunnett’s T3 post-hoc test was utilised as an alternative. A p-value below 0.05 (p < 0.05) was deemed indicative of a statistically significant difference, and all statistical visualisations were produced via GraphPad Prism and R tools to provide clear and standardised data display.

Discovery of putative genes that are closely linked to the development and outcome of esophagus cancer

We examined the amounts of deubiquitin ligases in 5 μM 4-NQO- or 2 μM NMBA-treated in vitro, esophageal tissue taken from 4-NQO- or NMBA-induced esophageal cancer mice model, and human patients with esophageal cancer pathological phenotype in order to understand their role in esophageal cancer. As illustrated in Figure 2A and B, the analysis of the experimental groups in relation to the period of time of 4-NQO- or NMBA-treated human or mouse EC cells, as well as the 16-week 4-NQO-fed EC mouse model and patient samples, revealed the five most prominently expressed deubiquitin-associated ligase candidates: USP10, OTUD3, USP11, USP17, and OTUD7B. Subsequently, the differential expression genes (DEGs) in the two species of 4-NQO-induced ECs were examined throughout time. Five deubiquitin ligase markers-USP10, OTUD3, USP11, USP17, and OTUD7B-met the intersection screening selection criteria as expected; USP10, in particular, demonstrated a significant downregulation across the entire analysis (Figure 2C). Due to the possible association between USP10 and the aetiology of EC, we conducted a quantitative proteomic analysis utilising SILAC and mass spectrometry, following established methodologies from prior research [[29], [35]]. Notably, we also verified that USP10 was significantly downregulated in response to the 10-hour 4-NQO challenge and illustrated its potential substrate in vitro (Figure 2D-F). We also examined the expression matrix of oesophageal cancer and adjacent healthy tissues in public datasets that included high-coverage gene profiling data from the TCGA, GEO, and ICGC databases (GEO: GSE290209, GSE248518, GSE273848, GSE273847, GSE157804, GSE231969, GSE172159, and GSE153096) to identify intriguing genes that regulate the progression of oesophageal cancer (Figure 2G). In the EC tissues, events linked to tumours and metabolic processes were noticeably more prevalent (Figure 2H). In the ten datasets we analysed, we discovered 43 upregulated DEGs and 57 downregulated DEGs (Figure 2I). Clinical data from the TCGA and ICGC databases identified six upregulated and twelve downregulated DEGs that exhibited a significant connection with survival rates (Supplementary figure 2A). Esophageal epithelium-specific USP10 ablation facilitates esophageal cancer progression in 4-NQO-induced mouse model

Along with the significant decline in USP10 seen in the ICGC and TCGA datasets (Supplementary figure 2A), We acquired 98 pairs of EC samples and the neighbouring samples that matched them in order to verify the reduced expression of USP10 in EC. Comparing EC tissues to adjacent normal tissues, our research showed a significant drop in USP10 mRNA expression levels (Figure 4A). Furthermore, the protein analysis showed that USP10 expression was downregulated in mouse-associated esophageal cancer cell lines (AKR1, mEC525M, and mEC586F), human EC tumour tissues, and EC-related cell lines (TE-11, TE-14, KYSE450, EC9706, and KYSE50). Western blotting and immunofluorescence detection were used to further validate this discovery (Figure 4B-E). Therefore, we looked at possible mechanisms that might lower USP10 in esophageal cancer. As seen in Figure 4F and G, exposure to a concentration gradient of Wu-5 dramatically reduced the expression of the USP10 protein in human EC cell lines in a dose-dependent manner. We then created esophageal epithelium-specific USP10 deficient mice (USP10CKO) (Figure 6A, B; Supplementary figure 3A, B) and treated them with either saline or 100 μg/mL 4-NQO for 16 weeks, respectively, to fully examine the functional role of USP10 in the context of EC in vivo. As anticipated, the 4-NQO treatment resulted in a significant decrease in body weight in the USP10CKO group as compared to the USP10flox/flox group. Similarly, the 4-NQO/USP10CKO mice group showed a similar reduction in food consumption (Figure 6C, D). Furthermore, following the 4-NQO challenge, USP10CKO mice displayed more esophageal lesions and greater sizes than USP10flox/flox mice (Figure 6E). When comparing the tumour area of 4-NQO/USP10CKO mice to that of 4-NQO/USP10flox/flox mice, we observed a down-regulation in cell apoptosis (active Caspase3) and an up-regulation in cell proliferation (Ki67) (Figure 6F). Furthermore, 4-NQO treatment significantly enhanced the production of high-grade EC in USP10-deficient animals (Figure 6G). Compared to 4-NQO/USP10flox/flox mice, 4-NQO/USP10CKO mice showed a substantial elevation of cytokines linked to inflammation, such as IL-6, IL-18, TNF-α, IL-1β, and CCL2 (Figure 6H). As demonstrated by the immunofluorescence experiment, the consistent results were further confirmed by the decreased expression of HEY1 and HES1, which are downstream signalling of the NOTCH1 pathway in 4-NQO/USP10CKO mice (Figure 6I). Our work offers more evidence that USP10 may contribute to the pathogenesis of EC. Discussion

Oxidative stress and ferroptosis are intricately linked to cancer progression, playing pivotal roles in tumor development, survival, and therapeutic resistance [36,37]. Oxidative stress, characterized by an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant defenses, has dual effects in cancer: it can drive tumorigenesis by inducing DNA damage, activating pro-survival signaling pathways, and promoting angiogenesis, while also exerting anti-tumor effects by triggering cell death and sensitizing cancer cells to therapy [38,39]. Ferroptosis, a unique form of regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, further complicates this dynamic [40,41]. Cancer cells often develop resistance to ferroptosis by upregulating antioxidant mechanisms like GPX4 and altering iron and lipid metabolism, thereby promoting survival and progression [42]. Targeting ferroptosis can selectively kill cancer cells, especially those with high iron dependency or metabolic vulnerabilities, and enhance the efficacy of conventional therapies [43,44]. The interplay between oxidative stress and ferroptosis is critical, as ROS amplify lipid peroxidation, a key driver of ferroptosis, while dysregulated iron metabolism exacerbates ROS production. Therapeutic strategies exploiting these mechanisms, such as ferroptosis inducers or pro-oxidant therapies, hold promise for overcoming treatment resistance in aggressive cancers. However, challenges remain, including the context-dependent nature of these processes, resistance mechanisms, and the influence of the tumor microenvironment. Understanding the intricate relationship between oxidative stress and ferroptosis offers novel avenues for developing effective cancer therapies. In our current work, the complex pathophysiology of esophageal cancer hinders drug development, with oxidative stress imbalances being a key risk factor. Targeting ubiquitin-specific protease 10 (USP10), which stabilizes NOTCH1 and modulates oxidative stress, shows promise in inhibiting tumor growth. USP10 interacts with the NOTCH1 intracellular domain (NICD1), catalyzing its deubiquitination and enhancing downstream signaling. Loss of USP10 disrupts NICD1 stabilization, triggering antioxidant gene production to prevent ferroptosis and accelerating EC progression. Conversely, USP10 overexpression in animal models reduces EC development by increasing NICD1 abundance and activating its signaling cascade. Reduced USP10 expression correlates with poor EC prognosis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target. These findings suggest USP10 modulation could offer significant clinical benefits in EC treatment.

Compelling evidence has identified oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammatory response, ECM deposition and cell death significantly implicated in the EC progression, as the key mediators in the pathogenesis of EC [45,46]. NOTCH signaling, as a developmental pathway, is involved in numerous cellular processes and promotes liver, kidney, heart, skin injuries and tumorigenesis [47,48]. Several studies have suggested that NOTCH1 signaling is a highly conserved pathway that plays a critical role in cell fate determination, proliferation, and differentiation. However, its role in cancer is complex and context-dependent, often described as having “two faces” due to its dual role as both an oncogene and a tumor suppressor in different cancer types or stages [47,48]. Of note, the role of NOTCH1 in oxidative stress and ferroptosis is complex and context-dependent, with studies revealing seemingly contradictory effects on tumor progression [49,50]. On one hand, NOTCH1 has been shown to promote oxidative stress and ferroptosis, which can enhance tumor development by driving cancer cell survival, proliferation, and metastasis. This occurs through mechanisms such as disrupting redox balance and amplifying lipid peroxidation, which are hallmarks of ferroptosis. On the other hand, high levels of NOTCH1 have also been found to suppress cancer development by inhibiting the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant response. This suppression increases oxidative stress and ferroptosis, ultimately leading to cancer cell apoptosis. These dual roles highlight the intricate interplay between NOTCH1 signaling, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis, which can either promote or inhibit tumor progression depending on the cellular context, cancer type, and stage. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that USP10, a prominent member of the deubiquitinating enzyme (DUB) superfamily with a variety of roles, is an essential regulator of several cellular processes. Our results showed that USP10 activity was significantly reduced in both in vitro and in vivo experiments using patient samples and EC cancer cells. According to a previous study, USP10 can deubiquitinate NEMO and so prevent the synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines. By increasing oxidative stress, inflammatory infiltration, and cell apoptosis in a variety of disease models, including sepsis and neuroinflammation in ischemic stroke, dysfunctional USP10 exacerbates chronic organ or tissue injury by altering the signalling pathways of S-phase kinase-associated protein 2 (SKP2), NF-kappa-B (NF-κB), NACHT, LRR and PYD domains-containing protein 3 (NLRP3), or TNF Alpha Induced Protein 3 (A20) [[51], [52], [53], [54]]. Importantly, our current study provides compelling evidence that the interaction between USP10 and the ANK segment of NICD1, as revealed by our pull-down experiments, is a novel finding that expands upon previous research. Also, the deubiquitination of NOTCH1 by USP10 has profound implications for oxidative stress responses. Our results align with those of previous report, which demonstrated that NOTCH1 activation can mitigate oxidative stress [55]. However, our study extends this by showing that USP10-mediated deubiquitination of NICD1 may enhance its stability and activity, thereby potentially amplifying NOTCH1’s protective effects against oxidative stress.

Lipid peroxidation reliant on iron is a characteristic feature of ferroptosis, a form of cellular demise. It is distinct from necrosis and apoptosis since it primarily entails iron accumulation and subsequent lipid peroxidation within cells. This procedure is essential for preventing excessive oxidative stress and maintaining cellular integrity. According to earlier research, ferroptosis is closely linked to oesophageal cancer, suggesting that it could be a promising target for treatment [42,56]. Ferroptosis plays a significant role in esophageal cancer, with elevated ferroptosis markers observed in esophageal cancer tissues relative to normal tissues. Our findings expand on this by showing that USP10 knockout in human esophageal cancer cell lines KYSE450 and TE-14 significantly alters the expression of ferroptosis markers, such as GPX4 and SLC7A11. This suggests that USP10 may be a critical regulator of ferroptosis in esophageal cancer, a finding that has not been previously reported. Meanwhile, USP10 overexpression reduces esophageal cancer development by increasing NICD1 abundance and activating its signaling cascade. Also, in the context of ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death associated with iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation. Previous studies have implicated NOTCH1 in the regulation of ferroptosis [57,58]. Our work suggests that USP10 may modulate this pathway by affecting NOTCH1 stability and function. This is a significant departure from the traditional view of ferroptosis as solely dependent on iron and lipid metabolism, introducing a new layer of regulation via protein ubiquitination and deubiquitination. Comparing our results with the studies previous reported, which focused on the role of USP10 in another signaling pathway, we observe a stark contrast [[51], [52], [53], [54]]. While previous reports did not report any interaction between USP10 and NOTCH1, our study clearly demonstrates a strong interaction, highlighting the specificity of USP10’s role in different cellular contexts.

Conclusion

Our study significantly advances the field by elucidating the role of USP10 in NOTCH1 deubiquitination and its implications for oxidative stress and ferroptosis (Figure 12). Our findings not only highlight the complexity of cellular signaling pathways but also offer new perspectives on the regulation of these processes.
