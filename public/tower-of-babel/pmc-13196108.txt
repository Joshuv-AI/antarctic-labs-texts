Evaluation of a nurse-led pneumococcal vaccination program in a day hospital
Authors: Olivier Bory, Manon Verstraeten, Elisabeth Aslangul, Morgane Michel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPneumococcal vaccination coverage among at-risk adults remains low in France. Our objectives were to assess pneumococcal vaccination coverage before and after implementation of a nurse-led vaccination program in a French university hospital day-hospital, to describe reasons for refusal, and to assess the duration of the nurse intervention as an indicator of feasibility in routine practice.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective observational study using health records from the day-hospital unit where pneumococcal vaccination was systematically offered to unvaccinated patients during a three-week period in 2023. The nurse-led program encompassed identification of vaccination indications, patient counseling on vaccination, and immediate on-site vaccine administration when accepted. Multivariable penalized logistic regression was used to study factors associated with vaccine acceptance.ResultsA total of 102 patients were analyzed (median age, 68 years [56–75]; 66% women). At baseline, pneumococcal vaccination coverage was 39%. Among unvaccinated patients (n = 62), two-thirds accepted vaccination, increasing overall coverage to 79%. Patients who accepted vaccination were older (aOR per 10-year increase, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.15–3.87) and more frequently male (aOR, 15.4; 95% CI, 1.6–501). Counseling duration was shorter among patients who accepted vaccination, with 61% completing the interview in less than 5 min. The most common reasons for refusal were insufficient knowledge and concerns about side effects, nearly half explicitly referring to COVID-19.ConclusionA nurse-led program for pneumococcal vaccination in a day-hospital substantially increased vaccination coverage, with most accepted vaccinations completed within 5 min, supporting the feasibility of this strategy in routine care.

Background

Streptococcus pneumoniae (S pneumoniae) causes illnesses ranging from mild respiratory infections to severe invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD), including bacteraemia, pneumonia, and meningitis. It is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality from lower respiratory tract infections [1, 2]. The introduction of conjugate pneumococcal vaccines has substantially reduced the incidence of IPD. Following the switch from the 7-valent to the 13-valent formulation in 2011, the annual incidence of IPD declined from 13.0 to 8.3 cases per 100,000 population between 2010 and 2019 in France [3].

The risk of pneumococcal infection is increased fourfold in individuals with chronic conditions (e.g., diabetes, pulmonary or cardiac disease, alcohol use disorder) and 23–48-fold in immunocompromised patients with cancer or HIV infection [4]. Despite established recommendations for them to be vaccinated against S pneumoniae [5–8], pneumococcal vaccination coverage rate (PVCR) remains far below targets, varying between 4% and 27% depending on national context and comorbidity profile [9–11].

In France, the pneumococcal vaccination schedule introduced in 2017 recommends one dose of the pneumococcal 13-valent conjugate vaccine (PCV13), followed two months later by a dose of the pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23), with a PPSV23 booster five years thereafter [4]. However, vaccination coverage among at-risk adults has remained very low. In France, national estimates indicate that in 2020, among 7.3 million adults identified as at risk, pneumococcal vaccination coverage (PCV13 + PPSV23) was 9.9%, compared with 51.1% for influenza vaccination [12]. More specifically, PVCR ranged from 5.6% in diabetes, 14.9% in malignancy, 16.2% in chronic respiratory disease, 24.1% in HIV infection, and 27.5% in autoimmune or inflammatory disorders treated with immunosuppressants or biologics.

These persistent gaps in vaccination coverage underscore the need to identify strategies to increase vaccine uptake and reduce missed opportunities for pneumococcal vaccination [13]. One approach is to use every patient encounter within the healthcare system as an opportunity for vaccination, through proactive communication and on-site vaccine availability [14]. Previous studies have shown the effectiveness of nurse-led vaccination programs among patients with rheumatoid arthritis [15] and other autoimmune inflammatory diseases [16]. In our own day-hospital, a nurse-led program to vaccinate eligible patients was implemented over a 1-month period in 2018, and PVCR increased from 32% to 85.5% [17]. However, the program was implemented only temporarily and could not be maintained as a systematic vaccination process in routine practice. The program was reintroduced in 2023, providing an opportunity to reassess its impact in the day-hospital setting.

The aim of the present article is to study pneumococcal vaccination coverage before and after the offer of vaccination in our day-hospital along with reasons for refusal, and to determine the duration of the interview so as to estimate its feasibility in routine practice.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective observational study using electronic health records in the day-hospital of a French university hospital. The unit encompasses six specialties: surgery, oncology, pulmonology, internal medicine, geriatrics, and gerontology.

Description of the nurse-led vaccination program

Between March 20 and April 7, 2023, pneumococcal vaccination was systematically offered to eligible unvaccinated patients attending the day-hospital. The program was carried out by nurses with a standardized approach in routine care and the information collected was recorded in patients’ electronic health records.

The program followed a standardized three-step approach: First, patients answered two questions to determine whether they had an indication for pneumococcal vaccination and, if so, their current vaccination status. Second, for patients identified as unvaccinated, a structured interview was conducted to (1) provide information on pneumococcal disease and vaccination, (2) assess the patient’s knowledge and concerns regarding vaccination, and (3) address potential barriers or misconceptions. Third, if the patient agreed, vaccination was performed immediately by the nurse within the day-hospital, where the vaccine was readily available, and the patient was given a vaccination record along with a prescription for a booster dose of 23-valent vaccine (Pneumo23® or Pneumovax®) two months later.

All nurses received standardized training in vaccination counselling and motivational interviewing during the week preceding the implementation of the program. The training consisted of a single session delivered within the department by an advanced practice nurse [18].

Population

All patients with an indication for pneumococcal vaccination attending the day-hospital were considered eligible for the program, with the exception of patients admitted to the day hospital for surgery or for intravenous iron therapy (excluded for practical reasons) and patients who could not consent to the vaccination (unable to communicate in French, major cognitive impairment).

According to French national vaccination recommendations at the time of the study, pneumococcal vaccination was recommended for adults aged ≥ 65 years as well as for younger adults with specific risk factors or comorbidities (e.g., immunocompromised conditions, malignancies, immunosuppressive therapies, and chronic diseases such as respiratory, cardiac, renal, liver disease, or diabetes).

Outcomes of interest

The primary outcome was pneumococcal vaccination coverage rate (PVCR) at baseline and after the nurse-led offer of vaccination. Secondary outcomes included (1) vaccine acceptance rate among previously unvaccinated patients, (2) reported reasons for vaccine refusal, and (3) the duration of the nurse-led procedure as a proxy indicator of feasibility in routine practice (time elapsed between the first question on eligibility to vaccination and the conclusion of the procedure, i.e. either vaccination or a definitive refusal).

Data sources

All data were extracted from the patients’ electronic health records, including: age, sex, reason for day-hospital attendance, specialty, indication for pneumococcal vaccination, and vaccination status before and after the nurse interview, and the number of prior day-hospital visits. Baseline vaccination status was assessed based on patient self-report during the nurse interview, as vaccination records were not systematically available for verification. The number of prior visits refers to the number of day-hospital visits recorded before the visit during which the patient was included in the vaccination program. Regarding the reason(s) for vaccine refusal, nurses offering the vaccination were asked to record it in the medical records during the interview using predefined categories based on the most frequently reported reasons for vaccine hesitancy. An additional “other” category allowed patients to report alternative reasons. Interview duration was documented by nurses in four categories: <5 min, 5–10 min, 10–15 min, and > 15 min. For analysis, these were subsequently grouped into three categories: <5 min, 5–10 min, and > 10 min.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis was performed to summarize the characteristics of the study population. Analyses were conducted overall and by vaccination status (vaccinated vs. unvaccinated). Patients were considered unvaccinated if they had never received pneumococcal vaccination or were not up to date according to national recommendations. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as medians and first and third quartiles [Q1-Q3]. Comparisons between groups were made using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, as appropriate. All tests were two-sided and the statistical significance level was set at α = 0.05.

To identify factors associated with vaccine acceptance, a multivariable logistic regression was carried out. Explanatory variables were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and univariate significance (age, sex), as well as interview duration, which was used as a proxy indicator of vaccine hesitancy, under the assumption that patients with higher vaccine hesitancy would require longer discussions, while patients with no hesitancy who were not vaccinated due to lack of knowledge for example would agree faster and have shorter interview duration.

Given the small sample size and sparse data that could lead to separation issues, we used Firth’s penalized maximum-likelihood logistic regression as the primary estimator to reduce bias in parameter estimation. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by carrying out parsimonious maximum-likelihood models.

When patients refused vaccination, nurses were asked to document the reason(s) for refusal in the medical records during the interview. When documented, these reasons were subsequently reviewed and grouped into thematic categories (e.g., lack of knowledge, fear of side effects, media influence). The number and proportion of patients in each category were then calculated.

Program duration was analyzed overall, by vaccination acceptance or refusal, as well as by reasons for refusal using appropriate statistical tests.

Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics and regulation

This study was based on a retrospective analysis of anonymized data collected as part of routine patient care within a day hospital setting. Because an anonymized hospital database was used, informed consent from individual patients was not obtained. According to French regulations, this type of study does not require approval from a research ethics committee, as it falls outside the scope of the French Public Health Code governing research involving human participants (Loi n°2012 − 300 of 5 March 2012, known as the Loi Jardé). Data processing was conducted in compliance with the methodology of reference MR-004 established by the French Data Protection Authority (Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés, CNIL). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Study population

Between March 20 and April 7, 2023, a total of 289 day-hospital admissions were recorded, corresponding to 164 individual patients of whom 102 were seen by a nurse as part of the program and included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Median age was 68 years [56–75], and 66% were women (Table 1). Overall, 34% of patients were seen in oncology, 32% in pulmonology, and 30% in internal medicine. The majority of patients (57%) were attending for chemotherapy, and 4.9% were receiving immunosuppressive therapy. The duration of the nurse interview ranged from approximately 5 to 20 min. Overall, 44% of nurse-led interviews lasted less than 5 min, 40% between 5 and 10 min, and only 16% exceeded 10 min.

Pneumococcal vaccination coverage rate at baseline

PVCR prior to any offer of vaccination was 39% (40/102 patients) in our population. No significant difference was found between vaccinated and unvaccinated patients in terms of age andsex. However, the distribution by specialty differed (p < 0.001), with unvaccinated patients more frequently seen in oncology, whereas vaccinated patients were more often from pulmonology (Table 1).

Vaccination acceptance

Among the 62 unvaccinated patients (Table 2), 41/62 (66%) accepted pneumococcal vaccination following the nurse’s offer. This led to a PVCR post-interview of 79% (81/102 patients). There were significant differences between the characteristics of patients who did and did not accept vaccination: those who accepted were older (median, 72 vs. 61 years; p = 0.019) and more frequently male (41% vs. 4.8%; p = 0.007). The number of previous day-hospital visits was not found to be associated with vaccine acceptance (p = 0.46).

The duration of the procedure differed significantly between patients who accepted and those who refused vaccination (p < 0.001). Among those who accepted, 61% received counselling lasting less than 5 min, whereas only 9.5% of those who refused were counselled within this timeframe. Conversely, longer interviews (> 10 min) were more frequent among patients who refused vaccination than among those who accepted vaccination (38% vs. 49%). However, no association was found between the duration of the interview and the number of previous day-hospital visits. All patients who had previously received at least one pneumococcal vaccine accepted revaccination in less than 5 min.

Factors associated with vaccine acceptance

In the multivariable regression analysis using Firth’s penalized logistic regression, age, sex, and interview duration were independently associated with higher vaccine acceptance. Acceptance increased with age (aOR per 10-year increase, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.15–3.87) and was higher among men than women (aOR, 15.4; 95% CI, 1.6–501). Compared with interviews lasting less than five minutes, the odds of accepting vaccination were significantly lower when the interview lasted 5–10 min (aOR, 0.09; 95% CI, 0.01–0.38) or more than 10 min (aOR, 0.004; 95% CI, 0.0001–0.058). Results were consistent in sensitivity analyses using standard maximum-likelihood logistic regression. The likelihood-ratio test for the Firth model was highly significant (p < 0.001), supporting the overall robustness of the associations.

Reasons for refusing vaccination

Among the 21 of 62 patients (34%) who declined vaccination, the most frequently reported reasons were insufficient knowledge about the vaccine (n = 4) and fear of side effects combined with media-driven distrust (n = 4). Other less frequent reasons are reported in Table 3. Three patients had no documented reason. In addition, nearly half (48%) explicitly referred to COVID-19, mainly referring to vaccine fatigue or a broader distrust toward vaccination after the pandemic.

The distribution of reasons varied with interview times. Patients counselled for less than 5 min (n = 2) mainly mentioned fear related to media information or side effects. Among those with 5–10-minute interviews (n = 11), reasons were more diverse, including lack of knowledge (n = 2), fear of adverse effects (n = 1), or combined fears linked to media influence (n = 2). For patients with interviews longer than 10 min (n = 8), the main reasons were vaccine ignorance (n = 2) combined fear of side effects and media influence (n = 2), or a general opposition to vaccination (n = 2).

Discussion

In this university hospital study, only 39% of at-risk patients were vaccinated against S. pneumoniae at baseline. Systematic nurse-led vaccination increased coverage to 79%, with two thirds of previously unvaccinated patients accepting vaccination. Acceptance was independently associated with older age, male sex, and shorter interviews; however, these associations should not be interpreted as causal. Refusal was mainly driven by lack of knowledge, fear of adverse effects, media influence, and vaccine opposition, with nearly half citing the COVID-19 pandemic.

Vaccination rates observed before and after the nurse-led program were higher than those reported in the literature [12] but were consistent with rates from other single-centre hospital studies [15, 16]. This discrepancy likely reflects under-recording of hospital-administered vaccinations in national claims databases [12], and the absence of a dedicated adult vaccination registry in France [19]. Selection bias is also possible, as hospital-based patients may have more healthcare contact and vaccination opportunities [10]. Multicentre day-hospital studies, followed by population-based analyses, are needed to better estimate coverage and enable comparisons with at-risk individuals outside hospital settings.

A key finding of this study is that most successful vaccinations were completed within less than five minutes, suggesting that lack of information or prompting, rather than active opposition, may represent a major barrier to adult vaccination [16, 17, 20]. The absolute increase in vaccination coverage was 40 percentage points (from 39% to 79%), which was at the upper end of reported effects and similar to the increase observed during our 2018 nurse-led program (32% to 85.5%) [17]. We did not have information on whether pneumococcal vaccination had previously been offered to these patients. Therefore, we cannot distinguish the specific effect of nurse-led counselling from the simple fact of offering vaccination during the day-hospital visit. The low baseline vaccination coverage may partly reflect missed opportunities for vaccination if patients had not previously been offered the vaccine. This interpretation is further supported by a recent systematic review and meta-analysis showing that hospital-based multi-component strategies, particularly those involving nursing staff and patient reminders, significantly increase vaccine uptake threefold among older and high-risk adults [21]. These findings reinforce the hospital as a pivotal setting for health promotion and preventive care, extending its role beyond curative care toward broader public health action [22, 23].

The high end-of-program coverage suggests that vaccine hesitancy affects only a minority of patients, although this subgroup warrants targeted attention. Prior studies indicate that adult vaccine reluctance is driven less by general opposition than by concerns about personal benefit, safety, and trust in health authorities, even among high-risk populations [24, 25]. All previously vaccinated patients agreed to revaccination, indicating strong trust once vaccination is initiated and substantial potential for reminder-based reinforcement. Conducted in 2023, the study also reflects post–COVID-19 attitudes, with nearly half of refusers citing the pandemic, including perceptions of “vaccine saturation” [26]. Follow-up sessions could be offered to patients who initially declined vaccination, and for those refusing a second time, a structured motivational interview could be proposed [24].

In our cohort, pneumococcal vaccine acceptance was higher among men than women, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size. Although counterintuitive, evidence on sex-related differences in oncology populations is inconsistent. Some studies found a better PVCR in patients with gynaecologic cancers than with thoracic cancers [27], whereas other studies in older adults have shown that men have higher influenza and pneumococcal vaccination coverage than women [28]. In our study, men were older and more often followed for pulmonary disease (data not shown), which may have increased perceived susceptibility to pneumococcal infection. Prior studies indicate that patients in clinical high-risk groups (i.e. particularly those with chronic respiratory disease) report more favorable attitudes toward pneumococcal vaccination and greater intent to be vaccinated [29]. Moreover, pneumococcal vaccination uptake is higher when patients are followed by pulmonologists, underscoring the importance of specialty care and organ-focused risk communication in promoting vaccine acceptance [30]. Across studies, pneumococcal vaccination coverage in patients with malignancies remains markedly suboptimal, often below 10% in cohorts with solid or mixed tumors and similarly low among cancer survivors [12]. Interestingly, although one might hypothesize that oncology patients would have higher baseline vaccination rates due to repeated contact with the healthcare system, or greater acceptance due to disease severity, this was not observed in our study.

The similarly low baseline vaccination coverage before the nurse offer, compared with the end of our 2018 program, indicates that vaccination was not naturally sustained in the day hospital despite unchanged staff. This suggests that awareness alone is insufficient and that the absence of a stable nurse coordinator likely hindered integration into routine workflows. Our findings show that nurses can integrate systematic vaccine assessment and counselling into routine workflows without extending day-hospital time constraints. Successful vaccinations were achieved in less time than consultations with patients who ultimately refused vaccination. This relatively limited time investment supports the integration of nurse-facilitated vaccination into routine day-hospital practice. Although this finding should be interpreted with caution, it suggests that such interventions may be both effective and time-efficient. Simple implementation tools, such as checklists or point-of-care reminder forms (whether paper-based or digital) could support sustainability [31]. In rheumatology practices, for instance, a quality improvement strategy using a simple paper reminder form significantly increased pneumococcal vaccination coverage among patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy [32].

This study also has several strengths. It was conducted in a real-world hospital setting, with nurses trained in motivational interviewing and vaccination counselling. The immediate availability of the pneumococcal vaccine minimized logistic barriers and allowed direct translation of counselling into vaccination.

This study has several limitations. Its single-center design and limited sample size restrict generalizability, and vaccination status partly relied on patient report, introducing potential recall bias. The analysis focused on short-term acceptance and did not assess durability or transferability to other settings. Finally, the intervention addressed pneumococcal vaccination only, whereas at-risk adults are eligible for multiple recommended vaccines. Information on patients’ vaccination status for other recommended adult vaccines was not collected. Because vaccines are often coadministered and may influence attitudes toward pneumococcal vaccination, this limits interpretation of overall vaccine behavior and whether similar acceptance would occur if multiple vaccines were offered. We hypothesise that multi-vaccine strategies may require tailored communication to sustain high overall coverage but further studies are needed on this topic. Another limit of this study is that we did not assess inter-nurse variability, such as differences in time spent per vaccination or level of engagement, which could influence effectiveness. Finally, interview duration may have been overestimated among patients who accepted vaccination, as the recorded time included both counselling and vaccine administration. As a result, the actual time dedicated to vaccination counselling alone could not be precisely identified. This suggests that the time required for counselling alone may be shorter than reported, which further supports the feasibility of integrating this nurse-led program into routine practice.

From a population health perspective, pneumococcal vaccination in at-risk adults is both effective and efficient. Nurse-led delivery represents a cost-efficient strategy, as structured task-shifting improves immunization coverage while reducing administrative costs [33]. By preventing severe infections and hospitalizations, systematic vaccination can improve health system performance and reduce costs. Given the €10.1 billion annual economic burden of adult community-acquired pneumonia in Europe, streamlined nurse-led programs are both clinically and economically justified [34]. Although single-center, this pragmatic intervention provides a potentially transferable model adaptable to other hospital pathways, although further multicentre studies are needed, and integration with primary care. At scale, nurse-led programs may reduce persistent gaps in adult vaccination and improve equity in preventive care.

Conclusion

In this retrospective study, a brief and structured nurse-led program in a day-hospital was associated with a marked increase in pneumococcal vaccination coverage among at-risk adults. These findings suggest that systematic, opportunistic vaccination assessment and counselling by nurses, combined with on-site vaccine availability, may substantially reduce missed opportunities for adult vaccination within routine care. Systematically offering vaccination during care encounters, strengthening confidence in vaccines, and improving communication by healthcare professionals are key strategies to overcome these persistent barriers.
