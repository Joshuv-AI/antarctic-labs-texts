Integrated molecular, microRNA, and biochemical profiling reveals oxidative stress–driven degeneration of the ligamentum flavum in lumbar spinal stenosis
Authors: Damian Strojny, Klaudia Skóra, Tomasz Kulpok, Zygmunt Wnuk, Wojciech Niedziałek, Dawid Sobański, Rafał Staszkiewicz, Beniamin Oskar Grabarek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDegenerative lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is driven by fibrosis and hypertrophy of the ligamentum flavum (LF). Oxidative stress—an excess of reactive oxygen species (ROS) over antioxidant defenses—may underlie LF remodeling, but tissue-level evidence is limited. LF from patients with LSS (n = 180) and controls (n = 102) underwent transcriptomic microarray and microRNA (miRNA) profiling, reverse-transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and biochemical assays of thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances (TBARS), total glutathione (GSH), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) and functional enrichment analyses were performed. Among 14,130 oxidative-stress–related transcripts, 8,197 were differentially expressed (p < 0.05). Redox-sensitive inflammatory mediators were upregulated, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin-1β (IL-1B), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8 (CXCL8), alongside mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK8/JNK1, MAPK14/p38α, MAPK1/ERK2) and superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) and peroxiredoxin-1 (PRDX1). Antioxidant defenses—catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutases SOD1/SOD3, glutathione peroxidase-1 (GPX1), and sirtuin-3 (SIRT3)—were downregulated. RT-qPCR confirmed these trends. ELISA demonstrated higher MAPK8 and IL-6 and lower SIRT3 and GPX1 in hypertrophic LF (all p < 0.05). Biochemically, LSS tissue showed increased TBARS and GPx activity and decreased GSH versus controls; oxidative imbalance intensified with greater pain and higher body mass index. Selected miRNAs (e.g., hsa-miR-3163→MAPK8, hsa-miR-4291→SIRT3) were reduced. PPI networks were highly enriched (p < 1 × 10⁻¹⁶). Notably, MAPK8, SIRT3, GPX1, and IL-6 dysregulation was independent of pain category. LF in LSS exhibits a persistent ROS-driven, MAPK-amplified inflammatory program with mitochondrial vulnerability (SIRT3 loss) and impaired peroxide detoxification (GPX1 decline). This oxidative signature likely sustains fibrosis and LF thickening and nominates MAPK8–SIRT3–GPX1–IL-6 as candidate biomarkers and therapeutic targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56686-0.Keywords: Lumbar spinal stenosis, Ligamentum flavum, Oxidative stress, Thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances, GlutathioneSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biomarkers, Diseases, Medical research, Molecular biology

Introduction

Degenerative lumbosacral spinal stenosis (LSS) is a common and disabling condition, particularly among older adults, that leads to chronic back pain, neurogenic claudication, and reduced quality of life1,2. The disorder arises from a complex interplay of structural, cellular, and biochemical changes within the spinal canal. Among these, hypertrophy and fibrosis of the ligamentum flavum (LF) play a central role in narrowing the spinal canal and compressing neural elements3,4. Although the macroscopic and histopathological manifestations of LF hypertrophy are well recognized, the molecular mechanisms driving its degeneration and remodeling remain insufficiently understood5,6.

In recent years, oxidative stress has emerged as a fundamental pathophysiological process contributing to a wide range of degenerative disorders of the nervous and musculoskeletal systems. The imbalance between the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the tissue’s antioxidant defense mechanisms can lead to oxidative damage of lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, ultimately triggering inflammation, apoptosis, and extracellular matrix degradation. Within the context of LSS, these processes may promote fibroblast proliferation, collagen cross-linking, and elastin fragmentation — all of which contribute to the stiffening and thickening of the LF7,8. Nevertheless, despite these conceptual links, direct evidence connecting oxidative stress markers with molecular and biochemical alterations in LF tissue remains scarce.

Most studies investigating oxidative imbalance in LSS have relied on serum-based or systemic indicators, such as malondialdehyde, superoxide dismutase, or total antioxidant capacity. While informative, these peripheral markers do not necessarily reflect the local oxidative microenvironment within the LF, where the pathological process actually occurs. Similarly, transcriptomic and proteomic studies on LF tissue are limited in number and often address inflammation or fibrosis rather than oxidative pathways per se. There is therefore a significant gap in our understanding of how oxidative stress manifests and interacts with molecular signaling networks directly within the degenerating LF5,7–9.

To bridge this gap, the present study establishes a comprehensive molecular and biochemical methodology designed to evaluate oxidative stress in LF tissue obtained from patients with degenerative LSS. The approach integrates several complementary layers of analysis, encompassing transcriptomic and microRNA microarray profiling, RT-qPCR validation, and protein quantification by ELISA, as well as biochemical assays measuring lipid peroxidation (TBARs), total glutathione (GSH), and GPx activity. This multidimensional strategy allows for a robust characterization of both the molecular determinants and the biochemical consequences of oxidative imbalance in LF degeneration.

The main aim of this study was to evaluate oxidative stress in LF tissue from patients with degenerative lumbosacral spinal stenosis. To achieve this, we used a combination of molecular and biochemical methods to measure both gene and protein expression, as well as the levels of key oxidative markers. The applied approach included microarray analysis of oxidative stress–related genes and microRNAs, RT-qPCR and ELISA validation, and biochemical assays for TBARs, total GSH, and GPx activity. This comprehensive analysis was designed to provide a more complete picture of oxidative processes occurring directly in the LF, and to help identify potential molecular markers linked to tissue degeneration in LSS.

Materials and methods

Study design

The study group comprised 180 patients (87 women and 93 men) with a mean age of 51.12 ± 2.98 years, all presenting with degenerative LSS. Surgical qualification and hemilaminectomy procedures were conducted by a neurosurgeon following standard clinical criteria. Each patient underwent a detailed neurological examination, including both objective and subjective assessments, supported by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Imaging was performed using a 1.5 Tesla (T) or higher scanner with sequences such as SE T1, SE T1 FLAIR, FSE T2, and STIR, obtained in transverse and sagittal planes with slice thicknesses of 3 mm and 4 mm. Anthropometric parameters—body weight and height—were recorded for body mass index (BMI) calculation. Pain severity prior to surgery was evaluated using a visual analog scale (VAS) ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst possible pain). Participants confirmed that, within the six months preceding inclusion, they had not followed any special diet, nor had they taken vitamin or mineral preparations classified as medicinal supplements. None of the patients reported occupational or accidental exposure to heavy metal contaminants.

The selection of target genes and proteins was guided by their recognized roles in redox regulation, inflammatory signaling, and apoptosis.

To assess whether transcriptional alterations translated into corresponding protein-level changes, quantitative ELISA assays were performed for selected proteins representing distinct aspects of oxidative stress and inflammation, These proteins were chosen because they occupy central positions at the interface between oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammatory signaling.

The biochemical component of the study was designed to validate and functionally complement the molecular analyses. Lipid peroxidation was quantified by measuring TBARs, representing the accumulation of malondialdehyde as a marker of oxidative membrane damage. The concentration of total reduced GSH was determined to assess intracellular redox buffering capacity, while the activity of GPx was measured as an indicator of enzymatic peroxide detoxification. These three biochemical markers—TBARs, GSH, and GPx—were selected to provide a direct functional correlate of the transcriptional and proteomic findings. By combining transcriptomic, protein, and biochemical assessments, the study design allowed for a multidimensional evaluation of oxidative stress and its contribution to ligamentum flavum hypertrophy and degeneration in LSS.

Study group characteristics

The study group comprised 180 patients (87 women and 93 men) with a mean age of 51.12 ± 2.98 years, all presenting with degenerative LSS. Surgical qualification and hemilaminectomy procedures were conducted by a neurosurgeon (D.S.) following standard clinical criteria. Each patient underwent a detailed neurological examination, including both objective and subjective assessments, supported by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Imaging was performed using a 1.5 Tesla (T) or higher scanner with sequences such as SE T1, SE T1 FLAIR, FSE T2, and STIR, obtained in transverse and sagittal planes with slice thicknesses of 3 mm and 4 mm. Anthropometric parameters—body weight and height—were recorded for body mass index (BMI) calculation. Pain severity prior to surgery was evaluated using a visual analog scale (VAS) ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst possible pain). A total of 180 patients reported VAS scores between 4 and 10, whereas no patients reported VAS values between 0 and 3. The distribution showed that 24 patients had a VAS score of 4, 42 patients had a score of 5, 51 patients had a score of 6, 27 patients had a score of 7, and 12 patients each reported VAS scores of 8, 9, and 10. Participants confirmed that, within the six months preceding inclusion, they had not followed any special diet, nor had they taken vitamin or mineral preparations classified as medicinal supplements. None of the patients reported occupational or accidental exposure to heavy metal contaminants.

Control group

The control cohort consisted of 102 healthy individuals (49 women and 53 men) with a mean age of 48.18 ± 1.87 years. These participants reported no symptoms suggestive of degenerative spinal disease, chronic back pain, osteoarthritis, or other musculoskeletal disorders. They also denied any known contact with heavy metal pollutants. As with the study group, none of the controls adhered to specific dietary restrictions or used therapeutic vitamin or mineral supplements within six months prior to participation.

Ligamentum flavum samples for the control group were obtained post-mortem from human cadavers during organ procurement procedures. Tissue collection was performed in accordance with the Polish Act of July 1, 2005, on the Collection, Storage, and Transplantation of Cells, Tissues, and Organs (Journal of Laws 2020, item 2134)10, which operates under an opt-out consent system. All procedures were conducted following institutional ethical approval.

Post-mortem tissue retrieval was performed within 48 h of confirmed death. Only samples without macroscopic signs of degeneration were included. Tissue integrity and absence of degenerative changes were verified by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, and final sample qualification was independently confirmed by two experienced neurosurgeons (D.S. and R.S.).

Preparation of LF tissue homogenates

LF specimens were rinsed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to remove blood residues and immediately frozen at − 80 °C. Before biochemical analysis, tissues were thawed on ice and homogenized in PBS (1:10, w/v) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) using a using a hand-held homogenizer (T18 Digital Ultra-Turrax, IKA Polska Sp. z o.o., Warsaw, Poland) until no solid fragments were visible. The homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for oxidative stress assays. All analyses were performed in triplicate.

Isolation of total ribonucleic acid (RNA)

Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA; Cat. No. 15596026), following the manufacturer’s instructions. To ensure high purity and remove residual organic contaminants, the isolated RNA was further purified with the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany; Cat. No. 74104). In addition, DNase I treatment (Fermentas International Inc., Burlington, ON, Canada; Cat. No. 18047019) was applied to eliminate potential genomic DNA contamination.

RNA integrity was verified by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel containing ethidium bromide (0.5 mg/mL), allowing visualization of the 28 S and 18 S rRNA bands. RNA concentration and purity were determined spectrophotometrically by measuring absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm, and calculating the A260/A280 ratio. Samples with ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 were considered suitable for downstream applications.

To provide a more objective assessment of RNA quality, RNA integrity was additionally evaluated using the RNA Integrity Number (RIN) determined with capillary electrophoresis (Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). RIN values were assessed in both the study and control groups to ensure comparable RNA quality and to minimize potential bias related to post-mortem degradation. Only samples with RIN ≥ 7.0 were included in subsequent transcriptomic and RT-qPCR analyses.

Messenger RNA and microRNA microarray analysis

Comparative transcriptomic profiling was performed to evaluate oxidative stress–related gene expression in hypertophy LF, relative to controls. The experiment utilized the HG-U133_A2 microarray platform (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and the GeneChip 3′ IVT PLUS Reagent Kit (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA; Cat. No. 902416), following validated manufacturer protocols. A reference list of 14,130 genes associated with oxidative stress was retrieved from the GeneCards human gene database (https://www.genecards.org; accessed on 1 August 2025) by performing a keyword search using the term “oxidative stress pathway” in the database search field. The resulting gene list, comprising genes annotated as related to oxidative stress based on GeneCards annotations and integrated evidence, was exported and used as a reference set. Differentially expressed oxidative stress–related genes were defined as the intersection between this reference gene set and genes identified in the microarray dataset. Double-stranded complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the GeneChip 3′ IVT Express Kit, followed by in vitro transcription for complementary RNA (aRNA) amplification and fragmentation. The labeled aRNAs were hybridized to the microarray, and fluorescence signals were detected using the Gene Array Scanner 3000 7G. Image data were processed and normalized with GeneChip Command Console Software.

Quality control of microarray data was performed to ensure reliability and reproducibility of transcriptomic measurements. Raw intensity data were evaluated using standard quality assessment procedures, including background signal distribution, probe intensity distribution, and array-to-array variation. Data normalization was performed using the robust multi-array average (RMA) algorithm implemented in the Transcriptome Analysis Console software (Thermo Fisher Scientific), including background correction, quantile normalization, and log2 transformation.

To assess sample comparability and detect potential outliers, principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering were conducted. The analyses demonstrated consistent clustering of samples within study groups and no significant technical outliers. Additionally, internal control probes and hybridization controls provided by the manufacturer met recommended quality thresholds, confirming appropriate labeling efficiency and hybridization performance. Only samples meeting predefined quality criteria were included in downstream analyses.

To explore post-transcriptional regulation, microRNA expression was analyzed using the GeneChipmiRNA 2.0 Array (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s standardized procedure. Differentially expressed miRNAs between treated and control H-RPE cells were identified and annotated. Functional target prediction was performed using TargetScan (http://www.targetscan.org; accessed on 1 September 202511 and miRanda (http://mirdb.org; accessed on 1 September 202512. Predicted miRNA–mRNA interactions with confidence scores above 90 were considered highly reliable, whereas interactions scoring below 60 were treated as low-confidence and reserved for potential experimental verification12,13.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

Quantitative RT-PCR was conducted to validate key gene expression changes identified in the microarray analysis. Complementary DNA was synthesized using the same RNA samples, and amplification was performed following the 2–ΔΔCt method. β-actin (ACTB) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as endogenous reference genes for normalization. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for protein quantification

To evaluate protein-level changes corresponding to transcriptional alterations within the oxidative stress pathway, quantitative ELISA assays were conducted according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The following commercial kits were used (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA): Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase 8 (MAPK8) ELISA Kit (#MBS1603534), Sirtuin 3 (SIRT3) ELISA Kit (#MBS091264), and Glutathione Peroxidase 1 (GPX1; Cat. No. MBS765363) from MyBioSource (San Diego, CA, USA), and Interleukin 6 (IL6) ELISA Kit (#MBS2021124).

Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Varioskan Flash Multimode Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and protein concentrations were calculated from standard calibration curves. All assays were performed in triplicate to ensure analytical precision, reproducibility, and data reliability.

Measurement of lipid peroxidation products

Lipid peroxidation was evaluated by quantifying TBARs according to the fluorometric method developed by Asakawa and Matsushita14. Briefly, 500 µL of the tissue homogenate was diluted to a final volume of 2 mL with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Sequentially, 100 µL of 100 mM butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), 1 mL of 10% trichloroacetic acid (TCA), 500 µL of 5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and 250 µL of 8% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) were added. Subsequently, 750 µL of 0.6% thiobarbituric acid (TBA) solution was introduced, and the mixture was incubated at 90 °C for 60 min. After cooling to ambient temperature, the samples were centrifuged at 1690 × g for 10 min using a TOMY LC-200 benchtop centrifuge (TOMY Digital Biology Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Fluorescence of the supernatant was measured at excitation and emission wavelengths of 515 nm and 553 nm, respectively, using a Varioskan Flash Multimode Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Calibration curves were prepared using 1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane as the standard.

Assessment of GSH levels

Total GSH content was determined using the DTNB–glutathione reductase recycling method described by Anderson15. This assay is based on the reaction of 5,5′-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) with thiol groups, producing a yellow-colored chromophore. For each measurement, 50 µL of supernatant was combined with 200 µL of reaction mixture containing 0.4 mM nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) and 1.2 U/mL glutathione reductase in 0.01 M sodium phosphate buffer supplemented with 0.05 mM EDTA. After incubation at 30 °C for 2 min, 20 µL of DTNB was added to initiate the reaction. Absorbance was monitored at 412 nm every 30 s for 3 min using a Varioskan Flash Multimode Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). GSH concentrations were calculated from the slopes of absorbance changes relative to a standard curve prepared with known GSH concentrations and expressed as nmol per mg of protein.

Measurement of glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity

GPx activity was determined spectrophotometrically according to the method of Takahashi et al.16. A 20 µL aliquot of the sample supernatant was mixed with 185 µL of reaction buffer containing 25 mM reduced GSH, 0.2 mM NADPH, and 1.25 mM EDTA in 0.25 M Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4). Subsequently, 25 µL of glutathione reductase (10 U/mL) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 2 min. The enzymatic reaction was initiated by the addition of 20 µL of 1.75 mM tert-butyl hydroperoxide (t-BuOOH). The decrease in absorbance associated with NADPH oxidation was recorded at 340 nm every 15 s for 10 min using a Varioskan Flash Multimode Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). GPx activity was calculated using the extinction coefficient for NADPH (ε = 2.57 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹) and expressed as µmol of NADPH oxidized per minute per mg of protein.

STRING network and functional enrichment analysis

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) and functional enrichment analyses were performed using the STRING database (version 12.0; https://string-db.org; accessed on 1 August 2025). Differentially expressed oxidative stress–related genes identified by microarray analysis were used as input. The interaction network was generated using a high-confidence interaction score threshold (> 0.7), integrating evidence from experimental data, co-expression, curated databases, and text mining.

The enrichment strength parameter was calculated as log₁₀(observed/expected), reflecting the ratio between observed and expected functional associations within the network. Statistical significance of enrichment was assessed using false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple testing, with FDR-adjusted p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Functional annotation of differentially expressed genes was further explored using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses to identify overrepresented biological processes and signalling pathways (accessed on 1 September 2025).

Visualization of the top-ranked GO and KEGG terms was generated using bubble plots, in which bubble size represents gene count and color intensity corresponds to FDR-adjusted p-values.

The resulting PPI network was imported into Cytoscape Web 1.0 (https://cytoscape.org; accessed on 12 February 2026) for visualization and topological analysis. To identify key regulatory nodes, network centrality parameters, including degree centrality and betweenness centrality, were calculated using the NetworkAnalyzer tool. Nodes were ranked according to degree centrality, and the top-ranked genes were defined as hub nodes. The top 10 hub genes and their corresponding centrality values are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Transcriptome Analysis Console (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and StatPlus Professional (AnalystSoft Inc., Walnut, CA, USA). These programs were applied to microarray, RT-qPCR, ELISA, and biochemical datasets.

Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normally distributed variables were compared between two groups using Student’s t-test, whereas comparisons involving more than two groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Scheffé’s post hoc test. For non-normally distributed variables, appropriate nonparametric alternatives were applied when necessary. Categorical variables, including sex distribution and body mass index (BMI) categories, were analyzed using the chi-square test.

Baseline demographic characteristics between the study and control groups, including age, sex, and BMI distribution, were compared to assess group comparability and potential confounding effects.

Microarray differential expression analysis was conducted using the robust multi-array average (RMA) normalization method, including background correction, quantile normalization, and log₂ transformation. Genes were considered differentially expressed when meeting predefined criteria of absolute fold change (|FC|) ≥ 5.0 and false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p-value < 0.05, applying the Benjamini–Hochberg correction to control for multiple testing.

To evaluate the potential confounding effect of age on oxidative stress–related molecular markers, additional analyses were performed. Participants were stratified into two age groups (< 60 years and ≥ 60 years), and expression levels of MAPK8, IL-6, SIRT3, and GPX1 were compared between hypertrophic and control ligamentum flavum samples within each subgroup.

Furthermore, age-adjusted linear regression models were constructed to assess the association between ligamentum flavum status (hypertrophic vs. control) and marker expression while controlling for age as a continuous variable. Regression coefficients and corresponding p-values were calculated to determine the independent effect of tissue status on marker levels.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sample size determination

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power version 3.117, based on effect sizes derived from earlier investigations assessing elemental imbalances in degenerative spinal pathologies. To achieve a statistical power of 80% (β = 0.20) with a significance threshold of α = 0.05, a minimum of 150 patients and 100 control participants was required. The final inclusion of 180 patients and 102 controls exceeded this minimum, thereby enhancing the robustness and validity of the results. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were also calculated for key intergroup differences to evaluate their clinical importance in addition to statistical significance.

Notably, the upregulated genes were predominantly involved in redox-sensitive inflammatory signaling, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin 1 beta (IL1B), interleukin 6 (IL6), and C–X–C motif chemokine ligand 8 (CXCL8). Increased expression was also observed in genes encoding components of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family, such as MAPK8 (JNK1), MAPK14 (p38α), MAPK1 (ERK2), and MAPK10 (JNK3), as well as oxidative defense regulators including nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (NFE2L2/NRF2), oxidation resistance 1 (OXR1), peroxiredoxin 1 (PRDX1), and superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2).

Several extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and adhesion molecules, such as matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9), collagen type IV alpha 1 chain (COL4A1), and intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1), were also significantly overexpressed, indicating structural reorganization of connective tissue under chronic oxidative stress conditions.

In contrast, the expression of key antioxidant enzymes, including catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), superoxide dismutase 3 (SOD3), and glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPX1), was markedly decreased, suggesting insufficient compensatory antioxidant activity in degenerative LF. Downregulation of B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2) and sirtuin 3 (SIRT3), accompanied by increased transcription of caspase 3 (CASP3) and caspase 9 (CASP9), further supports an oxidative stress–induced apoptotic response.

Importantly, comparative stratification according to pain severity (VAS score) revealed that several transcripts — notably MAPK8, SIRT3, GPX1, and IL6 — consistently differentiated hypertrophic from normal ligamentum flavum independently of pain intensity, indicating that their dysregulation represents a stable molecular hallmark of ligamentum flavum degeneration rather than a pain-dependent effect.

The PPI network constructed using the STRING database comprised 32 nodes and 224 edges, with an average node degree of 14 and an average local clustering coefficient of 0.774 (Fig. 6). The expected number of edges in a random network of comparable size was 100, whereas the observed number was more than twice as high, indicating significantly increased connectivity among the analyzed proteins (PPI enrichment p < 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁶). These findings suggest that oxidative stress–related differentially expressed genes form a highly interconnected functional module rather than acting as isolated components. Several inflammatory and stress-response mediators, including TNF, IL6, IL1B, MAPK8, MAPK14, SOD2, CAT, PRDX1, and TP53, formed densely connected regions characterized by multiple high-confidence interactions. The observed network architecture reflects strong functional associations between oxidative stress regulation, inflammatory signalling, and apoptotic pathways in hypertrophic ligamentum flavum tissue.

Age-stratified and age-adjusted analysis of key molecular markers

Age-stratified analysis demonstrated that the expression patterns of MAPK8, IL-6, SIRT3, and GPX1 remained significantly different between hypertrophic and control ligamentum flavum samples in both age groups (< 60 years and ≥ 60 years). Specifically, MAPK8 and IL-6 levels were consistently elevated in hypertrophic tissue, whereas SIRT3 and GPX1 levels were reduced, regardless of age category (p < 0.05 for all comparisons).

To further evaluate the independent effect of age, linear regression models adjusted for age were performed. Ligamentum flavum status remained a significant predictor of MAPK8 and IL-6 upregulation and SIRT3 and GPX1 downregulation after controlling for age (all p < 0.05), indicating that the observed molecular alterations were not solely attributable to age-related changes.

Discussion

The present study provides the first comprehensive molecular and biochemical characterization of oxidative stress directly within the LF of patients with degenerative LSS. By integrating transcriptomic, microRNA, and proteomic data with biochemical indices of lipid peroxidation, redox buffering, and enzymatic antioxidant activity, we reveal a convergent network of oxidative, inflammatory, and apoptotic pathways contributing to ligament hypertrophy and degeneration. The results collectively depict LF as a metabolically active tissue undergoing progressive oxidative remodeling, where mitochondrial dysfunction, dysregulated redox signaling, and impaired cellular defense mechanisms cooperate to promote fibrosis and structural thickening.

At the transcriptional level, we observed profound dysregulation of oxidative stress–related genes. The upregulation of TNF, IL6, IL1B, and CXCL8 indicates the presence of a strong proinflammatory milieu sustained by redox-sensitive transcription factors such as NF-κB and AP-1. These cytokines are known to induce ROS generation through NADPH oxidase activation, further amplifying oxidative injury and leading to a positive feedback loop between inflammation and oxidative stress18,19. Parallel overexpression of MAPK8 (JNK1), MAPK14 (p38α), and MAPK1 (ERK2) highlights activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase cascade, a canonical redox sensor linking extracellular stimuli to nuclear responses. MAPKs regulate the transcription of genes involved in fibrosis, collagen cross-linking, and matrix metalloproteinase production, all key features of LF thickening20,21. The concurrent elevation of TP53 and CASP3/9 points toward a transition from protective adaptation to apoptotic remodeling, suggesting that persistent oxidative stress pushes cells beyond the threshold of recovery22.

In contrast, several crucial antioxidant defense genes were markedly suppressed. The downregulation of CAT, SOD1, SOD3, GPX1, and SIRT3 suggests that the intrinsic antioxidant capacity of the LF is severely compromised. Catalase and glutathione peroxidase are essential for detoxifying hydrogen peroxide, while superoxide dismutases dismutate superoxide radicals; their depletion creates a permissive environment for ROS accumulation. SIRT323,24, a mitochondrial deacetylase that activates antioxidant enzymes and maintains mitochondrial integrity, was significantly downregulated, emphasizing mitochondrial vulnerability as a central driver of oxidative imbalance25. Decreased BCL2 expression, combined with enhanced CASP3 and CASP9, supports the notion that apoptosis is a downstream consequence of prolonged redox disruption26. Collectively, these transcriptional findings delineate a shift from a balanced antioxidant–prooxidant state toward one dominated by oxidative damage, inflammation, and programmed cell death27,28.

Importantly, stratification of LF samples according to pain severity (VAS score) revealed that the transcriptional profiles of MAPK8, SIRT3, GPX1, and IL6 remained significantly different between hypertrophic and control tissues, irrespective of VAS category. This observation suggests that the dysregulation of these four genes represents a stable molecular hallmark of LF degeneration rather than a mere reflection of clinical pain intensity. In other words, while oxidative and inflammatory processes may contribute to nociceptive sensitization, the MAPK8–SIRT3–GPX1–IL6 axis appears to define a persistent redox-inflammatory state intrinsic to degenerative remodeling5. This independence from subjective pain gradation underscores the notion that oxidative imbalance and mitochondrial dysfunction precede and sustain structural pathology even before maximal symptom manifestation6,8.

From a mechanistic perspective, MAPK8 and IL6 act as central mediators of redox-sensitive inflammatory amplification, whereas SIRT3 and GPX1 constitute critical defensive counter-balances25. The concurrent up-regulation of MAPK8/IL6 alongside the suppression of SIRT3/GPX1 implies a breakdown in the compensatory equilibrium between oxidative stress signalling and mitochondrial protection29. This pattern suggests a self-sustaining molecular programme: MAPK-driven inflammation and cytokine release continuously stimulate ROS production, while impaired SIRT3-dependent deacetylation and GPX1-mediated peroxide detoxification fail to mitigate oxidative load30. Consequently, even after resolution of acute pain or inflammation, ligamentum flavum tissue may remain locked in a pro-oxidant state that drives persistent fibrosis and hypertrophy.

The integration of miRNA data adds a critical post-transcriptional layer to this imbalance31. Several miRNAs that normally restrain oxidative and inflammatory gene expression were markedly down-regulated. Among them, hsa-miR-3163, hsa-miR-4291, hsa-miR-665-3p, and hsa-miR-4256 were suppressed, inversely correlating with their target genes MAPK8, SIRT3, GPX1 and IL6, respectively. This loss of miRNA-mediated inhibition likely results in the derepression of transcriptional programs that sustain oxidative and inflammatory signaling. By releasing redox-sensitive transcripts such as MAPK8, IL6, and related downstream effectors from post-transcriptional silencing, the diminished miRNA activity facilitates persistent overexpression of pro-oxidant and pro-inflammatory molecules, thereby reinforcing a chronic redox imbalance32. The miR-4291→SIRT3 and miR-665→GPX1 interactions, in particular, illustrate how mitochondrial metabolism and enzymatic detoxification are compromised not only by direct gene down-regulation, but also by impaired miRNA control33. Such dual-layer deregulation ensures that oxidative and inflammatory signalling remains persistently active, even in the absence of acute external stressors. The crosstalk between miRNAs and their targets therefore appears central to the chronicity of redox imbalance within ligamentum flavum tissue34–36.

At the protein level, these molecular changes were confirmed by ELISA quantification. Elevated levels of MAPK8 and IL-6 reflect hyperactivation of stress-responsive and cytokine-mediated signaling pathways, consistent with the transcriptional upregulation observed. In contrast, SIRT3 and GPX1 protein concentrations were significantly reduced, reinforcing the observation that both mitochondrial homeostasis and peroxide detoxification capacity are diminished37,38. SIRT3, as a master regulator of mitochondrial antioxidative responses, modulates the activity of MnSOD (SOD2) and the electron transport chain; its reduction compromises mitochondrial efficiency and increases ROS leakage39,40. Similarly, diminished GPX1 impairs the neutralization of hydroperoxides, thereby amplifying lipid and protein oxidation41,42. The concomitant increase in TBARs—a marker of lipid peroxidation—provides direct biochemical evidence that membrane components undergo oxidative injury, while the reduction in GSH further confirms depletion of the intracellular redox buffer43,44. The partial elevation of GPx activity in the early stages, followed by decline in severe pain categories, may reflect an adaptive enzymatic upregulation that ultimately fails under chronic oxidative load43,44.

The biochemical findings integrate coherently with the molecular data to illustrate a tissue environment dominated by oxidative stress45. Elevated TBARs indicate enhanced peroxidation of membrane phospholipids, which can lead to altered membrane fluidity, cross-linking of collagen and elastin, and activation of pro-fibrotic signaling46. Reduced GSH levels signify impaired capacity for neutralizing free radicals and maintaining thiol redox status, essential for protein folding and enzymatic activity47. The progressive reduction in GPx activity with increasing pain intensity suggests that the antioxidant defense system becomes overwhelmed as oxidative burden and tissue damage accumulate48,49. Notably, patients with higher BMI displayed more pronounced oxidative disturbances, consistent with systemic metabolic dysregulation promoting ROS overproduction50. Obesity-driven oxidative stress is well-documented to exacerbate local tissue hypoxia, mitochondrial dysfunction51, and inflammation, all of which may synergistically accelerate LF fibrosis and stiffness52. The association between oxidative markers and pain intensity also supports the hypothesis that ROS and cytokine-mediated inflammation contribute to nociceptive sensitization and chronic pain in LSS5. Although GPX1 protein levels did not demonstrate statistically significant differences across all VAS categories (p = 0.120), the observed directional trends, together with consistent transcriptomic downregulation and reduced antioxidant capacity in hypertrophic tissue, suggest biological relevance. Variability in GPX1 levels may reflect dynamic compensatory responses within the glutathione system under chronic oxidative stress. Therefore, the interpretation of these findings should consider effect magnitude and biological plausibility rather than strict adherence to an arbitrary statistical threshold.

Our PPI network analysis revealed a highly interconnected structure (32 nodes, 224 edges, enrichment p < 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁶), indicating that oxidative stress–related genes in ligamentum flavum tissue form a coordinated functional module rather than acting independently. The network contained densely connected regions composed of key inflammatory and stress-response mediators, including TNF, IL6, IL1B, MAPK8, MAPK14, SOD2, TP53, CAT, and PRDX1, reflecting strong functional links between oxidative stress regulation, inflammatory signalling, and apoptotic pathways.

Topological analysis further identified several central hub genes, including CASP3, IL6, JUN, IL1B, TP53, TNF, MMP9, BCL2, SOD2, and STAT3, suggesting their critical regulatory roles in maintaining network integrity and coordinating cellular stress responses. This network architecture supports the concept of tight integration between oxidative, inflammatory, and apoptotic mechanisms in hypertrophic ligamentum flavum tissue and is consistent with previous mechanistic studies demonstrating that oxidative stress promotes activation of Akt/MAPK signalling pathways, enhances pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and contributes to fibrotic remodelling in ligamentum flavum degeneration52. The enrichment of MAPK, FOXO, TNF and HIF-1 signalling pathways further underscores a cooperative role among redox regulation, cell survival and fibrogenesis — such as HIF-1α promoting IL-6 under hypoxia/oxidative stress53,54 and FOXO factors driving SOD2/CAT expression in oxidative contexts55.

Despite the comprehensive design integrating transcriptomic, microRNA, proteomic, and biochemical analyses, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, this study was cross-sectional in nature, which precludes establishing causal relationships between oxidative stress and ligamentum flavum hypertrophy. Longitudinal studies or experimental models would be necessary to determine whether the observed molecular alterations directly drive fibrotic remodeling or arise secondarily from degenerative changes.

Second, although the study included a relatively large cohort, interindividual variability—particularly in metabolic status, diet, and lifestyle factors—may have influenced oxidative stress markers. While participants with known confounders were excluded, subtle variations in systemic redox balance cannot be entirely ruled out.

Third, control samples were obtained from individuals without spinal pathology and may not have been fully matched for all comorbid conditions, such as diabetes or mild obesity, which could potentially influence oxidative stress parameters. Third, although the present study identified consistent dysregulation of the MAPK8–SIRT3–GPX1–IL-6 axis in degenerative ligamentum flavum tissue, the findings are based primarily on correlative transcriptomic, proteomic, and biochemical analyses of human samples. Therefore, direct causal relationships and regulatory interactions within this signaling network cannot be definitively established. Specifically, no quantitative analyses were performed to evaluate associations between molecular expression levels and clinical indicators of disease severity, postoperative outcomes, or long-term prognosis. Furthermore, the diagnostic performance of these markers, including sensitivity, specificity, and predictive value, was not assessed. Therefore, the proposed biomarkers should be interpreted as preliminary candidates, and future studies involving longitudinal clinical follow-up and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses are required to establish their diagnostic and prognostic utility in degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis. Functional validation using experimental models, such as primary ligamentum flavum fibroblast cultures, gene silencing or overexpression approaches, or pathway inhibition studies, would be required to confirm the mechanistic role of this axis in ligamentum flavum degeneration. Such experiments require dedicated long-term experimental models and were beyond the scope of the present clinical study; thus, future investigations should address the causal regulatory mechanisms underlying this pathway.

An important methodological limitation relates to the use of postmortem ligamentum flavum tissue as a control source. The reliance on postmortem material, although unavoidable due to the lack of access to non-degenerated surgical tissue, introduces potential variability. Postmortem changes, including RNA degradation, hypoxia-induced oxidative stress, early autolysis, and disturbances in water–electrolyte balance, could theoretically affect molecular and biochemical measurements. To minimize these effects, control tissues were collected within 48 h of death and verified histologically to exclude degenerative changes. Storage at low temperatures served as a protective factor for biological material and was applied to preserve molecular integrity. Furthermore, RNA quality was assessed using electrophoretic evaluation of ribosomal RNA bands and RNA Integrity Number (RIN) analysis, and only samples meeting predefined quality criteria were included in downstream analyses. Gene expression data were additionally normalized using endogenous reference genes to control for potential variability related to RNA degradation. Nevertheless, residual postmortem effects cannot be completely excluded. Moreover, as highlighted by Zhang et al.56, the degenerative cascade itself leads to depletion of viable ligamentum flavum cells, thereby limiting the pool of cells available for protein extraction. From a methodological perspective, postmortem sampling remains the only feasible way to obtain non-degenerated ligamentum flavum tissue, and similar approaches have been adopted in previous studies57,58. Future investigations should consider alternative control strategies, including the use of hypertrophic ligamentum flavum samples obtained from age-matched deceased donors, to further reduce potential confounding related to tissue origin.

Another limitation concerns the subjective nature of pain assessment using the visual analog scale (VAS)59. Individual differences in pain thresholds and the declarative nature of self-reported data may reduce the precision of pain quantification.

Finally, while microarray and ELISA assays provided robust coverage of oxidative stress–related targets, certain post-translational modifications, enzymatic isoforms, and non-coding regulatory elements were not captured. Complementary proteomic and metabolomic approaches could provide additional insight into the downstream biochemical landscape. Moreover, ligamentum flavum tissue represents a heterogeneous mixture of fibroblasts, macrophages, and endothelial cells; single-cell or spatial transcriptomic methods may further clarify cell-type–specific oxidative responses in future studies.

Conclusion

Taken together, the combined transcriptomic, miRNA, proteomic, and biochemical data converge on a unifying mechanistic model in which chronic oxidative stress functions as both a trigger and amplifier of LF degeneration. Mitochondrial dysfunction—exacerbated by SIRT3 depletion—leads to excessive ROS generation, which activates MAPK8 and TP53 signaling cascades. These, in turn, induce transcription of inflammatory mediators such as IL-6, TNF, and IL-1β, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of oxidative inflammation. The persistence of this dysregulation independently of VAS-defined pain categories indicates that oxidative remodeling constitutes an intrinsic pathological process that precedes or sustains clinical manifestations. The persistent imbalance between ROS production and antioxidant defense not only damages cellular macromolecules but also alters extracellular matrix dynamics through MMP activation and collagen remodeling. Over time, these processes culminate in ligament thickening, fibrosis, and reduced elasticity, hallmarks of spinal canal stenosis.
