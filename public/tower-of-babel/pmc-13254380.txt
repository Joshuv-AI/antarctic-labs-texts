Anaesthetic gas emissions in a tertiary hospital in Pakistan: Behavioural drivers versus technological solutions
Authors: Samie Asghar Dogar, Muhammad Sohaib, Ausaf Khan, Waleed Bin Ghaffar, Khalid Samad, Asad Latif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnaesthetic gases contribute disproportionately to healthcare-related greenhouse gas emissions. End-tidal control (ETC), originally developed to reduce cognitive load and improve dosing accuracy, has been promoted as a potential strategy to lower emissions. However, its benefit must be considered alongside established drivers such as agent choice and flow. This study examined predictors of emissions, focusing on clinician behaviour versus technological modifications such as ETC.MethodsWe conducted a six-month prospective audit at Aga Khan University Hospital, Karachi. Data were extracted from Carestation™ Insights. The outcome was emissions (kgCO₂e/min), with predictors including ETC use, fresh gas flow, case duration, and flow-normalised consumption of isoflurane, sevoflurane, and nitrous oxide. Multivariable regression models were run for all cases and stratified by agent.ResultsA total of 7231 cases were analysed. In the combined regression, nitrous oxide usage was the strongest predictor of emissions (β=1.56, p < 0.001), followed by isoflurane (β=2.76, p = 0.040), sevoflurane (β=1.54, p = 0.009), and mean fresh gas flow (β=0.37, p < 0.001). ETC and case duration were not significant. In agent-specific models, gas usage and flow rate were the dominant predictors. ETC had a small but significant protective effect in isoflurane models, the opposite effect in sevoflurane models, and no effect in nitrous oxide models.ConclusionsAnaesthetic gas emissions are driven mainly by clinician behaviours, agent choice, concentration, and flow, rather than by end-tidal control. Nitrous oxide is the largest contributor and should be targeted for elimination. Low-cost behavioural interventions offer the most effective and scalable pathway for sustainable anaesthesia in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Anaesthesia gases, Low-flow anaesthesia, Nitrous oxide, Sustainability, End-tidal control

Introduction

Climate change poses an escalating global health threat, manifesting through rising disease burdens, food and water insecurity, and more frequent extreme weather events [1]. These impacts disproportionately affect low- and middle-income countries (LMICs], where fragile health systems are less resilient and often lack the resources to adapt effectively [2]. Healthcare systems occupy a dual role in this crisis: they are vulnerable to climate-related disruption and also contribute significantly to greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, accounting for an estimated 4–5 % of global totals [3]. Within hospitals, operating theatres are among the most resource- and energy-intensive areas [4]. Although administered in small volumes, anaesthetic gases contribute disproportionately to emissions because of their high global warming potentials (GWPs) and long atmospheric lifespans [5].

Anaesthetic gases are delivered through anaesthesia machines, with fresh gas flow (FGF) rates determined by the anaesthetist. Historically, high flows were used to minimise rebreathing and reduce the risk of circuit depressurisation [6]. Advances in delivery systems and monitoring have made low-flow anaesthesia safe and practical, with well-documented environmental and economic benefits [7]. Nevertheless, adoption remains inconsistent, sustained by entrenched habits, perceived safety concerns, and institutional inertia [8]. Many anaesthetists also face a cultural tension between earlier training, which emphasised high flows, and contemporary guidance advocating flow reduction for environmental reasons [9].

Among technological innovations, end-tidal control (ETC) has received increasing attention. ETC is an automated gas-delivery mode in which the workstation continuously adjusts fresh-gas flow and vaporiser output to achieve a clinician-defined end-tidal concentration. The software models uptake kinetics, monitors inspired–end-tidal gradients, and applies short, high-flow wash-in bursts followed by rapid down-regulation once equilibrium is achieved. Although conceptually attractive, ETC is still not widely available. It received U.S. FDA approval only in 2022, and many regions, including most LMICs, lack access to compatible workstations. ETC was initially developed to reduce cognitive workload and improve precision [10,11]. More recently, ETC has been promoted as an environmentally sustainable tool, with a scoping review reporting reduced volatile agent use [12]. However, ETC does not eliminate clinician responsibility; the anaesthetist still determines the desired concentration and minimum flow rate, meaning consumption remains strongly influenced by user behaviour rather than the technology itself [13]. Its environmental benefit can only be meaningfully assessed alongside established drivers of emissions such as high FGFs, volatile concentration, and agent choice [5,14]. Randomised trials comparing ETC with manual control under mandated low-flow conditions may suggest superiority, but the effect largely reflects enforced low-flow anaesthesia rather than ETC itself.

The technology requires additional infrastructure and considerable cost, which may be prohibitive in resource-limited systems [12]. Framing ETC as a “green” solution risks oversimplification, particularly in LMICs. If investment in ETC is prioritised over lower-cost, higher-impact interventions such as clinician education, low-flow adoption, and rational agent selection, health systems may face higher financial burdens without proportional environmental benefit.

Against this background, we undertook a six-month prospective audit at a tertiary LMIC hospital to quantify anaesthetic gas emissions and identify their independent predictors. The primary objective was to evaluate combined regression models to determine the overarching drivers of emissions across agents and patient groups. Secondary objectives were to examine stratified models by agent (isoflurane, sevoflurane, nitrous oxide) and by patient population (adult vs paediatric) to identify specific patterns and assess potential technological effects.

Methodology

Study design and setting

We conducted a six-month prospective observational audit between 1 July and 31 December 2024 in the main operating rooms of Aga Khan University Hospital (AKUH), a 750-bed tertiary academic centre in Karachi, Pakistan. The project was approved by the AKUH Ethical Review Committee (ERC Number; 2024–10,255-29,704), and undertaken as part of an institutional quality-improvement initiative. The report adheres to STROBE guidelines for observational studies.

Study population

All surgical cases performed under general anaesthesia with volatile agents during the study period were eligible. Procedures conducted without volatile use, such as regional anaesthesia, sedation, or monitored anaesthesia care, were excluded.

Anaesthesia was delivered using GE Aisys CS² workstations (GE Healthcare, USA), equipped with both manual fresh-gas flow controls or automated end-tidal control (ETC) systems. In ETC mode, fresh gas flow is dynamically adjusted to maintain a clinician-defined end-tidal concentration, whereas in manual mode the anaesthesiologist directly manages flow throughout the case. The operating rooms included in this study were staffed by approximately 35 consultant anaesthesiologists during the audit period. Individual provider identity was not captured in the dataset.

Data collection

Minute-by-minute operational data were extracted from GE Carestation™ Insights, a cloud-based analytics platform. For each case, the following parameters were obtained: proportion of case time delivered under ETC, mean fresh-gas flow rate (L/min), total case duration (minutes), and per-minute emissions of isoflurane, sevoflurane, and nitrous oxide (kgCO₂e/min). ETC utilisation was analysed as a continuous variable representing the proportion of case time under ETC, rather than a binary ETC vs manual classification, to avoid misclassification in cases with intermittent use.

Direct vaporiser settings and end-tidal concentrations were not available in the Carestation™ Insights dataset. To account for concentration-related behaviour, a flow-normalised usage metric (mL per L FGF) was incorporated into all models as a surrogate for the clinician’s effective concentration preference. This was calculated as the total amount of each agent consumed (mL) divided by the product of case duration (minutes) and mean fresh-gas flow rate (L/min).

Emissions estimation

Anaesthetic gas–related emissions were estimated using the Aga Khan Development Network (AKDN) Carbon Management Tool [15]. This tool applies agent-specific global warming potential (GWP) conversion factors consistent with international GHG accounting standards (Isoflurane: 0.76 kgCO₂e/mL, Sevoflurane: 0.187 kgCO₂e/mL, Nitrous oxide: 0.000524 kgCO₂e/mL). Volumes were derived from time-stamped concentration and flow data recorded by the anaesthesia machines, ensuring case-level precision in emission estimates.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables and reported as means with standard deviations and ranges. Multiple linear regression was used to identify independent predictors of greenhouse gas emissions, expressed as kilograms of CO₂ equivalent per minute (kgCO₂e/min). The combined model included all anaesthetic cases and assessed the effect of flow-normalised usage of isoflurane, sevoflurane, and nitrous oxide, mean fresh gas flow (L/min), case duration (minutes), and proportion of case time delivered under end-tidal control (ETC, %). Interaction terms between volatile agent usage and nitrous oxide usage (isoflurane × nitrous oxide, sevoflurane × nitrous oxide) were also tested. To explore heterogeneity, models were stratified by patient population (adults vs paediatrics) and by individual agent (isoflurane, sevoflurane, nitrous oxide), allowing evaluation of agent-specific and population-specific predictors, including whether ETC or duration contributed differently across groups. Model assumptions were verified through inspection of residuals and Q–Q plots to confirm linearity, normality, and homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (all <3.0). Model fit was summarised using R² and adjusted R², and statistical significance was defined as two-tailed p < 0.05. All analyses were performed on complete cases (<2 % excluded due to missingness) using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Data integrity

<2 % of cases were excluded due to incomplete data capture. No identifiable patient information was collected; all analyses were based on machine-generated operational data, ensuring compliance with institutional and international data protection standards.

Results

Overall cohort

A total of 7231 anaesthetic cases were analysed between July 2024 and December 2025, comprising 7007 adults (96.9 %) and 224 paediatric patients (3.1 %). Adults had longer mean case durations than paediatrics (156.8 ± 145.2 vs. 110.5 ± 89.4 min). Paediatric cases demonstrated higher mean emissions per minute (0.160 ± 0.186 vs. 0.119 ± 0.134 kgCO₂e/min), driven by greater agent concentrations (0.65 ± 0.71 vs. 0.26 ± 0.41 mL/min) and higher fresh gas flow rates (3.89 ± 1.85 vs. 3.06 ± 1.64 L/min). The proportion of case time delivered under end-tidal control (ETC) was similar across groups (adult: 67.4 ± 42.1 %; paediatric: 69.6 ± 41.8 %).

Combined regression models

In the primary regression including all cases, emissions were overwhelmingly determined by anaesthetic gas usage and flow variables. Nitrous oxide was the strongest predictor (β = 1.56, p < 0.001), followed by isoflurane (β = 2.76, p = 0.040), sevoflurane (β = 1.54, p = 0.009), and mean flow rate (β = 0.37, p < 0.001). In contrast, case duration and ETC usage were not statistically significant. The model explained 89 % of the variance (R² = 0.89). (Table 1)

Interaction terms

When interaction effects were included, model fit improved further (R² = 0.93). Isoflurane × nitrous oxide interaction was associated with a modest negative effect (β = –1.73, p < 0.001), while sevoflurane × nitrous oxide showed a strong positive effect (β = 10.39, p < 0.001), indicating that concurrent sevoflurane and nitrous oxide amplified emissions beyond additive contributions. Stratified analyses

In adults, results mirrored the combined model: nitrous oxide, isoflurane, sevoflurane, and mean flow were all significant, while ETC and duration remained non-significant (R² = 0.89).

In paediatrics, all predictors except ETC were significant. Case duration contributed meaningfully (β = 0.0009, p = 0.006) alongside nitrous oxide (β = 1.64, p < 0.001), isoflurane (β = 3.53, p < 0.001), sevoflurane (β = 1.83, p < 0.001), and flow rate (β = 0.33, p < 0.001). Model performance was near-perfect (R² = 0.997), reflecting high predictability in this subgroup.

Agent-Specific models

Nitrous oxide: In both adults and paediatrics, emissions were almost entirely explained by nitrous oxide usage and flow rate, with no role for ETC or duration. Model fits were excellent (R² = 0.89 in adults; R² = 0.997 in paediatrics).

Isoflurane: In adults, isoflurane usage and flow were strong predictors, with ETC showing a small but statistically significant protective effect. In paediatrics, usage and flow remained dominant, with ETC again reducing emissions modestly. Model fits were moderate to strong (R² = 0.65 adults, R² = 0.90 paediatrics).

Sevoflurane: In adults, sevoflurane usage, flow, and case duration were significant predictors, with ETC paradoxically associated with slightly higher emissions. In paediatrics, usage, flow, and duration were significant, while ETC was not. Model fits were strong (R² = 0.90 adults; R² = 0.94 paediatrics).

Discussion

In this large prospective audit of >7200 anaesthetic cases, GHG emissions were overwhelmingly determined by anaesthetic agent use and FGF, with nitrous oxide the dominant contributor. Although adults comprised most cases, paediatric patients showed higher per-minute emissions due to greater concentrations and higher flows. Regression models consistently demonstrated that nitrous oxide, followed by isoflurane and sevoflurane, explained most of the variance, whereas case duration and end-tidal control (ETC) had only minor or context-specific effects. These findings confirm that emissions are primarily driven by clinician-controlled behaviours rather than by technology.

Nitrous oxide’s impact was striking. Its exceptionally high GWP and long atmospheric lifetime mean that once used, neither low-flow anaesthesia nor ETC meaningfully reduce its footprint [5]. Moreover, emissions extend beyond direct clinical use: our earlier work showed a five-fold discrepancy between procurement and recorded use, implicating pipeline leakage, as also reported in high-income settings [16,17]. Addressing this dual burden requires decisive action. Transitioning to portable cylinders or phasing out nitrous oxide entirely should be prioritised in line with international recommendations [18].

Even with volatile agents such as isoflurane and sevoflurane, behaviour, particularly agent concentration and FGF, had a far greater influence on emissions than technological modifications. ETC, although promoted as an environmentally friendly innovation, showed modest, agent-specific effects in our study. Randomised trials supporting ETC largely standardised flows [12,19,20], conflating the benefit of ETC with that of enforced low-flow anaesthesia. In real practice, where flows are clinician-determined, our results show that ETC offers little advantage. ETC was originally designed to reduce cognitive burden and improve dosing precision [11,21], not as a sustainability tool. Observational studies comparing ETC with manual control similarly report little environmental benefit [11,20,22].

The paradoxical increase in sevoflurane emissions with ETC is physiologically and behaviourally plausible. ETC algorithms use short, high-flow wash-in surges to reach the clinician-set end-tidal target, and sevoflurane’s rapid uptake amplifies vapour delivery during these surges. In addition, clinicians often select higher minimum flows for sevoflurane because of persistent concerns regarding Compound A, limiting ETC’s ability to operate at very low flows. Slightly higher target concentrations and transient overshoot during equilibration may further increase consumption. Notably, two of the ten studies included in the recent scoping review of ETC also reported higher sevoflurane use with ETC compared with manual control, despite similar flow settings, and the same pattern was observed in the authors’ previous observational study [12,23]. These combined factors may explain why sevoflurane emissions increased in this dataset despite automated flow control.

Reluctance to use low-flow anaesthesia persists despite strong evidence for safety. Much of this hesitation originates from earlier training that emphasised high flows to avoid circuit leaks, rebreathing, or inadequate anaesthetic depth. Concerns about Compound A formation during low-flow sevoflurane, based on early animal studies, also continue to influence clinician behaviour, even though human studies have not demonstrated clinically relevant nephrotoxicity [24]. These historical and cultural perceptions contribute to persistent high-flow habits across institutions.

For LMICs, this distinction is crucial. ETC capable machines are costly, and investment risks diverting resources away from more effective strategies. Behavioural interventions, such as audit feedback loops, dashboards, and education, have consistently reduced volatile use and FGF across diverse settings [[25], [26], [27]]. In addition to these behavioural tools, institutions can strengthen cultural change by integrating low-flow targets into departmental policies, orienting new staff with sustainability-focused anaesthesia practices, and providing regular performance feedback at team or individual level [[28], [29]]. Visible leadership endorsement, recognition of good practice, and inclusion of environmental metrics in quality-improvement audits further reinforce sustained low-flow adoption. These organisational levers complement individual clinician behaviour and help embed sustainability as a routine norm. These interventions are low-cost, scalable, and adaptable, delivering larger and more durable gains than technology alone. Embedding them into quality-improvement frameworks may represent the most effective sustainability pathway, particularly in LMICs.

Our study has several limitations. Unmeasured clinical factors such as provider experience or intraoperative complications may have influenced gas use, but their effects are likely captured indirectly through flow settings and agent choice. Adjunct intravenous infusions (e.g., propofol, ketamine, dexmedetomidine) were not systematically captured in the dataset. However, such infusions are used extremely rarely at our institution, mainly during scoliosis surgery to facilitate neuromonitoring, and represent fewer than ten cases annually. Their minimal frequency makes any influence on overall volatile consumption or regression estimates unlikely. We did not include patient-level variables such as age, sex, or ASA status, which are not known to independently affect emissions. MAC decreases only modestly with age (∼6 % per decade) compared with the large variation created by flow and agent choice [30,31], sex differences are inconsistent with prospective studies showing no effect on desflurane MAC [32], and ASA is a risk stratification tool rather than a determinant of gas uptake, with any effect mediated through case duration or clinician practice [30]. Consistent with this, carbon-footprint and life-cycle assessments consistently identify agent, nitrous oxide use, FGF, and duration as the main determinants of emissions, without incorporating age, sex, or ASA class [33]. Direct dialled and end-tidal volatile concentrations were not recorded by the analytics platform. However, inclusion of the flow-normalised usage metric in all regression models provides a robust surrogate for concentration-related behaviour, allowing this factor to be analytically captured despite the absence of direct measurements. Finally, our single-centre design may limit generalisability, and residual confounding cannot be excluded despite strong model performance.

In summary, anaesthetic gas emissions in this LMIC setting were primarily driven by clinician behaviour, with agent selection and FGF the dominant predictors. Sustainable reductions cannot be achieved through technology alone but require consistent behavioural change, reinforced by audit, education, and policy reform. Nitrous oxide remains uniquely resistant to mitigation and should be prioritised for elimination from piped supply systems. For LMICs, the most pragmatic pathway lies in low-cost, scalable interventions that optimise clinician practice while progressively reducing reliance on high-emission agents.
