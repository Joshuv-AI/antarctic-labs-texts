On the Irrationality Exponents of Mahler Numbers
Andrew Rajchert Supervisor: Dmitry Badziahin

Abstract




Chapter 1
Introduction

An irrationality exponent, sometimes called an irrationality measure, is a way of measuring how well an irrational number can be approximated by rational numbers. As
\mathbb{Q}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}
, it is certainly possible to find arbitrarily accurate rational approximations to any given real number, however the irrationality exponent also takes into account how ”complicated” the rational approximation needs to be through the size of the denominator. Additionally, the irrationality exponent takes into account how well an irrational number may be consistently approximated, so infrequent accurate approximations are discarded.

Definition 1.1
.

For a fixed
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, Consider the inequality

\displaystyle\Big|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}\Big|<\frac{1}{q^{\mu}},

(1.2)

where
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. Consider the set of
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
such that there are infinitely many integer solutions for
p
and
q
to (
1.2
). The irrationality exponent of
\alpha
, denoted by
\mu(\alpha)
, is defined as the supremum of this set.

It is already known, by Dirichlet’s approximation theorem, that for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
\mu(\alpha)\geq 2

[
5
]
. Furthermore, for any
\mu\in[2,\infty]
, there exists an
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mu(\alpha)=\mu

[
7
]
.

The irrationality exponent is a powerful and interesting tool in number theory and the study of transcendental numbers. One early application of the irrationality exponent is the definition of the Liouville numbers, the set of irrational numbers
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
such that
\mu(\alpha)=\infty
. Not only was it shown that these numbers exist, but they are also relatively easy to construct and are transcendental, thus becoming the first known examples of transcendental numbers
[
6
]
. Over 100 years later, Roth’s theorem was proven, which states that that all irrational algebraic numbers have an irrationality exponent of 2, providing a clear way to prove some numbers are transcendental, a result for which Klaus Roth won the 1958 Fields medal for discovering
[
4
]
.

While a interesting and unique tool, the irrationality exponent of any given irrational number can be extremely difficult to compute, and almost immediately leads to open problems in mathematics. For example, as of the time of writing, the best known bounds on
\mu(\pi)
are
2\leq\mu(\pi)\leq 7.1032...

[
9
]
. In this thesis, we aim to study the irrationality exponents of a certain class of numbers, called
Mahler Numbers
, as these numbers have several properties that makes it feasible to compute the irrationality exponent, despite the fact that the Mahler numbers capture a great variety of real numbers. Before we define this, we give some more notation useful for the rest of the report.

Definition 1.3
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and let
(c_{k})_{k\geq-n}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{Q}
. We say

\displaystyle f(z)=\sum_{k=-n}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{-k},

is a formal Laurent series in
\mathbb{Q}
. The set of all formal Laurent series in
\mathbb{Q}
is denoted
\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
.

For our purposes, we will only be dealing with formal Laurent series over
\mathbb{Q}
as opposed to any other set. If treated as a function, this is a power series of
1/z
, with some added polynomial part, hence it has some radius of convergence
\rho\in[0,\infty]
where
f(z)
converges in
\mathbb{C}
for any
|z|>\rho
and diverges for
|z|<\rho
. Although there are choices of the
c_{k}
coefficients such that the function does not converge anywhere in
\mathbb{C}
, this only presents an issue when evaluating
f(z)
, but we may still treat it as a power series.

Definition 1.4
.

For
f\in\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
, we say
f
is a Mahler Function if there exists some integers
n\geq 1,d\geq 2
and polynomials
P_{0},P_{1},...,P_{n},Q\in\mathbb{Q}[z]
such that
P_{0}(z)P_{n}(z)\neq 0
and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}P_{i}(z)f(z^{d^{i}})=Q(z),

(1.5)

for any
z
inside the disc of convergence of
f
. For any integer
b
in the disc of convergence of
f
,
f(b)
is called a Mahler number.

We aim to study a subset of the Mahler functions. More specifically, given
f\in\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
satisfies the functional equation,

\displaystyle f(z)=\frac{A(z)f(z^{d})+C(z)}{B(z)},

(1.6)

where
A(z),B(z),C(z)\in\mathbb{Q}[z],A(z)B(z)\neq 0
, what can we say about the irrationality exponents of the Mahler numbers associated with
f
? Can they be computed in a (relatively) fast manner, and what values do they take? This question is motivated Adamczewski and Rivoal, who ask if the irrationality exponent of an automatic number is always rational
[
1
]
. We will not define what an automatic number is, but simply state that it forms a subset of the Mahler numbers
[
2
]
, and thus a significant step into solving their proposed problem is achieved by investigating Mahler numbers.

For simplicity, unless explicitly stated otherwise, the term Mahler function is used to describe any Mahler function satisfying (
1.6
) for the rest of this thesis, and similarly with Mahler numbers. Note that it is not guaranteed that a Mahler number is irrational. It is possible that a function of the form
P(z)/Q(z),P,Q\in\mathbb{Q}[z]
satisfies (
1.6
), leading to all associated Mahler numbers being rational. As these are of little interest in terms of the irrationality exponent, we will only focus on irrational Mahler numbers, and thus avoid situations where a rational function may satisfy (
1.6
).

The case of
C(z)=0
is studied in depth by Badziahin, who finds the irrationality exponent of many Mahler numbers originating from (
1.6
) are rational, partially answering the question posed by Adamczewski and Rivoal. Furthermore, given a Mahler function with
C(z)=0
and some additional minor assumptions, there is a (relatively) simple method to calculate the irrationality exponent of these Mahler numbers exactly
[
2
]
.

The purpose of this report is to produce results that are analogous to that of Badziahin in this slightly more generalised form, where
C(z)
may be non-zero. Furthermore, we cover this in a more approachable manner, including relevant background and introductory information so the work can be understood by a wider range of readers.

Chapter 2
Continued Fractions for Formal Laurent Series

An essential tool for this essay is the theory of continued fractions for formal Laurent series. Given a function
f\in\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
, it is possible to produce a sequence of rational functions that approximate
f
very well. If
f
is a Mahler function, we can
evaluate the rational function approximations of
f
at an integer, which will produce a sequence of rational approximations for the associated Mahler number. This provides a way to get a lower bound on what the irrationality exponent of the Mahler number may be.

The study of continued fractions for formal Laurent series is based on the theory of continued fractions for real numbers, a more well known area with many parallels. Traditionally continued fractions for real numbers is used to study irrationality exponents, however the functional equation (
1.6
) provides a helpful path when using continued fractions for formal Laurent series instead, especially when the Mahler number itself may be challenging to compute.

It may be helpful to have some background knowledge on continued fractions for real numbers to contextualise the results of this chapter, however it is certainly not required to understand them. Before we cover continued fractions, we first study the space of formal Laurent series and introduce it as a metric space.

We can equip the set
\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
with a metric
d
to form a metric space, where the metric is defined as

\displaystyle d(f(z),g(z)):=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if $f(z)=g(z)$}\\
2^{\deg(f(z)-g(z))}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

All required conditions for a metric are simple to verify with the possible exception of the triangle inequality, which we will check. If
f(z)=g(z),f(z)=h(z)\text{ or }g(z)=h(z)
, then clearly
d(f(z),h(z))\leq d(f(z),g(z))+d(g(z),h(z))
. Otherwise,

\displaystyle d(f(z),h(z))

\displaystyle=2^{\deg(f(z)-g(z)+g(z)-h(z))}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\max(\deg(f(z)-g(z)),\deg(g(z)-h(z)))}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\deg(f(z)-g(z))}+2^{\deg(g(z)-h(z))}=d(f(z),g(z))+d(g(z),h(z)).

It can also be shown that this metric space is complete. Indeed, let
f_{n}(z)
be a Cauchy sequence in
\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
, and consider the
z^{k}
terms for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
as
n
varies. As
f_{n}
is a Cauchy sequence, there exists a
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
where for all
m,n\geq n_{0}
,
d(f_{n}(z),f_{m}(z))<2^{k}
. This implies
\deg(f_{n_{0}}(z)-f_{n}(z))<k
for all
n\geq n_{0}
, and hence the
x^{k}
coefficient is eventually constant.

Set
f(z)
to be the formal Laurent series such that for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, the
x^{k}
coefficient is the constant eventually reached by the
x^{k}
terms in the
f_{n}(z)
sequence. For any
\epsilon>0
, fix an integer
k
such that
k<\log_{2}(\epsilon)
. By construction there exists a
n_{0}
where
\deg(f_{n}(z)-f(z))<k
for all
n\geq n_{0}
as the coefficients of
f_{n}
will eventually match those of
f
, hence
d(f_{n}(x),f(x))<\epsilon
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. This immediately implies
f_{n}\to f
with respect to this metric, and as
f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
, we have completeness of the space.

We can now introduce continued fractions. The rest of this chapter makes use of the proofs and concepts given by Alf van der Poorten
[
8
]
, however we present it much more rigorously with added structure similar to that of Hardy and Wright’s discussion of continued fractions for real numbers
[
5
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(a_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of functions in
\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
such that
\deg(a_{n}(z))\geq 1
for
n\geq 1
. Then for any
N\geq 0
,

\displaystyle[a_{0}(z),a_{1}(z),a_{2}(z),...,a_{N}(z)]:=a_{0}(z)+\frac{1}{a_{1}(z)+\frac{1}{a_{2}(z)+\frac{1}{...+\frac{1}{a_{N}(z)}}}}.

This is called a finite continued fraction. Assuming the right hand side converges in
\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
as
N\to\infty
, we may have an infinite continued fraction:

\displaystyle[a_{0}(z),a_{1}(z),a_{2}(z),...]

\displaystyle:=\lim_{N\to\infty}a_{0}(z)+\frac{1}{a_{1}(z)+\frac{1}{a_{2}(z)+\frac{1}{...+\frac{1}{a_{N}(z)}}}}

\displaystyle=a_{0}(z)+\frac{1}{a_{1}(z)+\frac{1}{a_{2}(z)+\frac{1}{...}}}.

This expression is a little cumbersome, but thankfully we can simplify it with the following proposition.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
(a_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
such that
\deg(a_{n}(z))\geq 1
for
n\geq 1
. Let
p_{0}(z)=a_{0}(z)
,
q_{0}(z)=1
,
p_{1}(z)=a_{1}(z)a_{0}(z)+1
,
q_{1}(z)=a_{1}(z)
and for
n\geq 2
,
p_{n}(z)=a_{n}(z)p_{n-1}(z)+p_{n-2}(z)
,
q_{n}(z)=a_{n}(z)q_{n-1}(z)+q_{n-2}(z)
. Then for
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle[a_{0}(z),a_{1}(z),...,a_{n}(z)]=\frac{p_{n}(z)}{q_{n}(z)}

Proof.

We verify this by induction. The result is obvious for
n=0,1
and 2 by basic algebraic manipulations, so assume it holds for some fixed
n\geq 2
. We now try to prove it for
n+1
.

Consider a slightly different sequence
(a^{\prime}_{k}(z))_{k\geq 0}
where
a^{\prime}_{k}(z)=a_{k}(z)
for
k\neq n
, and
a^{\prime}_{n}(z)=a_{n}(z)+\frac{1}{a_{n+1}(z)}
. As
\deg(a_{k}(z))\geq 1
for
k\geq 1
, we have
\deg(a^{\prime}_{k}(z))\geq 1
for all
k\geq 1
as well, thus the continued fraction involving these terms is well defined.

Let
p^{\prime}_{k}(z)
and
q^{\prime}_{k}(z)
be defined in a similar manner to
p_{k}(z)
and
q_{k}(z)
, except based off the sequence
(a^{\prime}_{k}(z))_{k\geq 0}
. As
p^{\prime}_{n-1}(z),p^{\prime}_{n-2}(z),q^{\prime}_{n-1}(z)
and
q^{\prime}_{n-2}(z)
depend only on
a^{\prime}_{k}(z)
for
k\leq n-1
, which are identical to the
a_{k}(z)
for
k\leq n-1
, we must have
p^{\prime}_{n-1}(z)=p_{n-1}(z)
and similarly for
p^{\prime}_{n-2}(z),q^{\prime}_{n-1}(z)
and
q^{\prime}_{n-2}(z)
. We can apply our inductive hypothesis on the continued fraction of
a^{\prime}_{k}(z)
to find

\displaystyle[a_{0}(z),a_{1}(z),...,a_{n}(z),a_{n+1}(z)]

\displaystyle=[a_{0}(z),a_{1}(z),...,a_{n}(z)+\frac{1}{a_{n+1}(z)}]

\displaystyle=[a^{\prime}_{0}(z),a^{\prime}_{1}(z),...,a^{\prime}_{n}(z)]

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{\prime}_{n}(z)}{q^{\prime}_{n}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{\prime}_{n}(z)p^{\prime}_{n-1}(z)+p^{\prime}_{n-2}(z)}{a^{\prime}_{n}(z)p^{\prime}_{n-1}(z)+p^{\prime}_{n-2}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(a_{n}(z)+\frac{1}{a_{n+1}(z)})p_{n-1}(z)+p_{n-2}(z)}{(a_{n}(z)+\frac{1}{a_{n+1}(z)})q_{n-1}(z)+q_{n-2}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{n+1}(z)(a_{n}(z)p_{n-1}(z)+p_{n-2}(z))+p_{n-1}(z)}{a_{n+1}(z)(a_{n}(z)q_{n-1}(z)+q_{n-2}(z))+q_{n-1}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{n+1}(z)p_{n}(z)+p_{n-1}(z)}{a_{n+1}(z)q_{n}(z)+q_{n-1}(z)}=\frac{p_{n+1}(z)}{q_{n+1}(z)}.

This completes the inductive step, hence the proposition is true for all
n\geq 0
.

∎

The elements of the sequence
(p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}
are called
convergents
, and are much easier to deal with than the continued fraction itself. As the name suggests,
p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z)
converges in
\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
as
n\to\infty
, which we will show momentarily.

Also note that if
(a_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}
are all polynomials in
\mathbb{Q}[z]
, we have
p_{n}(z)
and
q_{n}(z)
being polynomials and hence
p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z)
is a rational function, thus we have a sequence of rational functions converging to some function in
\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
, exactly what we desire in our overarching plan of approximating Mahler functions.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
(a_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of functions in
\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
such that
\deg(a_{n}(z))\geq 1
for
n\geq 1
, and let
(p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z))_{n\geq 1}
be the convergents formed by the
(a_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}
sequence. Then
p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z)
converges to some
f\in\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

As
\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
is a complete metric space, it is enough to show that the sequence of convergents is a Cauchy sequence. First note,

\displaystyle p_{n}(z)q_{n-1}(z)-p_{n-1}(z)q_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=(a_{n}(z)p_{n-1}(z)+p_{n-2}(z))q_{n-1}(z)-p_{n-1}(z)(a_{n}(z)q_{n-1}(z)+q_{n-2}(z))

\displaystyle=(-1)(p_{n-1}(z)q_{n-2}(z)-p_{n-2}(z)q_{n-1}(z)).

Also observe
p_{1}(z)q_{0}(z)-p_{0}(z)q_{1}(z)=(a_{0}(z)a_{1}(z)+1)-a_{0}(z)a_{1}(z)=1
, thus with a simple induction it follows that

\displaystyle p_{n}(z)q_{n-1}(z)-p_{n-1}(z)q_{n}(z)=(-1)^{n-1}.

(2.4)

Using this, for any fixed
n>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{p_{n}(z)}{q_{n}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{n-1}(z)}{q_{n-1}(z)}+\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{q_{n-1}(z)q_{n}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{n-2}(z)}{q_{n-2}(z)}+\frac{(-1)^{n-2}}{q_{n-2}(z)q_{n-1}(z)}+\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{q_{n-1}(z)q_{n}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{0}(z)}{q_{0}(z)}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{q_{k}(z)q_{k+1}(z)}

\displaystyle=a_{0}(z)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{q_{k}(z)q_{k+1}(z)}.

Also observe that the degrees of the
q_{n}(z)
are strictly increasing. Indeed,
\deg(q_{1}(z))\geq 1>0=\deg(q_{0}(z))
. Assuming
\deg(q_{k}(z))>\deg(q_{k-1}(z))
for some
k\geq 1
, we also have

\displaystyle\deg(q_{k+1}(z))

\displaystyle=\deg(a_{k+1}(z)q_{k}(z)+q_{k-1}(z))

\displaystyle=\deg(a_{k+1}(z)q_{k}(z))>\deg(q_{k}(z)).

It follows by induction that the degree of
q_{n}(z)
is strictly increasing over
n
, and thus the degrees of the terms of the sum

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{q_{k}(z)q_{k+1}(z)},

are strictly decreasing over
k
. Now showing that
p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z)
is a Cauchy sequence, fix
\epsilon>0
. For any
1\leq m<n
,

\displaystyle\frac{p_{n}(z)}{q_{n}(z)}-\frac{p_{m}(z)}{q_{m}(z)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=m}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{q_{k}(z)q_{k+1}(z)}.

As the degrees of the terms are strictly decreasing, it follows that the degree of this sum is
-\deg(q_{m}(z))-\deg(q_{m+1}(z))<-2\deg(q_{m}(z))
, hence by definition of the metric,

\displaystyle d\Big(\frac{p_{n}(z)}{q_{n}(z)},\frac{p_{m}(z)}{q_{m}(z)}\Big)<2^{-2\deg(q_{m}(z))}.

As
\deg(q_{m}(z))
is strictly increasing, simply set
n_{0}
such that
-2\deg(q_{n_{0}}(z))\leq\log_{2}(\epsilon)
, then for all
m,n\geq n_{0}
,

\displaystyle d\Big(\frac{p_{n}(z)}{q_{n}(z)},\frac{p_{m}(z)}{q_{m}(z)}\Big)<\epsilon.

As this can be done for every
\epsilon>0
, it follows the sequence of convergents is a Cauchy sequence and hence must converge in
\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
by completeness, with the limiting function expressed as

\displaystyle f(z):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{n}(z)}{q_{n}(z)}=a_{0}(z)+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{q_{k}(z)q_{k+1}(z)}.

(2.5)

∎

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z)
be a convergent of
f(z)\in\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
such that
p_{n}(z),q_{n}(z)\in\mathbb{Q}[z]
. Then
p_{n}(z)
and
q_{n}(z)
are coprime. That is,
\gcd(p_{n}(z),q_{n}(z))
is a constant.

Proof.

From equation (
2.4
),
p_{n}(z)q_{n-1}(z)-p_{n-1}(z)q_{n}(z)=(-1)^{n-1}
. Note however, for any pair of polynomials
P(z),Q(z)\in\mathbb{Q}[z]
,
p_{n}(z)P(z)+q_{n}(z)Q(z)
is always a multiple of
\gcd(p_{n}(z),q_{n}(z))
. We immediately conclude that
\gcd(p_{n}(z),q_{n}(z))
divides 1, hence it must be some constant.
∎

While this establishes that all continued fractions converge in
\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
, the power of continued fractions is that given
f\in\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
, it is very simple to find a sequence
(a_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}\in\mathbb{Q}[z]
such that
f=[a_{0}(z),a_{1}(z),...]
.

For
f\in\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
, let
\lfloor f\rfloor
be the polynomial part of
f
. That is, if
f(z)=\sum_{k=-n}^{\infty}c_{-k}z^{-k}
, then
\lfloor f(z)\rfloor=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}z^{k}
if
n\geq 0
, and
\lfloor f(z)\rfloor=0
otherwise.

Theorem 2.7

(Continued Fraction Algorithm for Laurent Series)
.

Assume
f(z)\in\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
is not a rational function, and define
a_{0}(z)=\lfloor f(z)\rfloor
,
f_{1}(z)=(f(z)-a_{0}(z))^{-1}
. For
n\geq 1
, inductively define the sequences,

\displaystyle a_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\lfloor f_{n}(z)\rfloor

\displaystyle f_{n+1}(z)

\displaystyle=(f_{n}(z)-a_{n}(z))^{-1}.

Then
f_{n}(z)
is well defined for all
n\geq 1
, and

\displaystyle[a_{0}(z),a_{1}(z),a_{2}(z),...]=f(z).

Proof.

First, we need to check
f_{n}(z)
is well defined for all
n\geq 1
. This will be true if
f(z)\neq a_{0}(z)
and
f_{n}(z)\neq a_{n}(z)
for all
n\geq 1
, as then we never have the issue of dividing by zero. Clearly this first condition is satisfied as
f(z)=a_{0}(z)=\lfloor f(z)\rfloor
immediately implies
f(z)
is a rational function, a contradiction. Suppose there exists a
n\geq 1
where
f_{n}(z)=a_{n}(z)
. Then by construction,

\displaystyle f(z)

\displaystyle=a_{0}(z)+\frac{1}{f_{1}(z)}

\displaystyle=a_{0}(z)+\frac{1}{a_{1}(z)+\frac{1}{f_{2}(z)}}

\displaystyle=[a_{0}(z),a_{1}(z),...,f_{n}(z)]

\displaystyle=[a_{0}(z),a_{1}(z),...,a_{n}(z)].

By definition,
a_{k}(z)
is a polynomial for all
k\geq 1
, hence this implies
f(z)
is a rational function, a contradiction. It follows that the
f_{n}(z)
sequence is well defined for all
n\geq 1
. Additionally note that for
n\geq 1
,
\deg(a_{n}(z))\geq\deg(f_{n}(z))=-\deg(f_{n-1}(z)-\lfloor f_{n-1}(z)\rfloor)\geq 1
, hence the continued fraction is well defined.

Now showing that the continued fraction converges to
f(z)
, let
(p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}
be the convergents of the continued fraction
[a_{0}(z),a_{1}(z),a_{2}(z),...]
. We have just shown that for any
n\geq 0
,
f(z)=[a_{0}(z),a_{1}(z),...,a_{n}(z),f_{n+1}(z)]
. Let
(p_{k}^{\prime}(z)/q_{k}^{\prime}(z))_{0\leq k\leq n+1}
be the convergents of this finite continued fraction. By the recursive definition from Proposition
2.2
,
p_{k}(z)=p_{k}^{\prime}(z)
and
q_{k}(z)=q_{k}^{\prime}(z)
for
1\leq k\leq n
, hence

\displaystyle f(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{n+1}^{\prime}(z)}{q_{n+1}^{\prime}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{n+1}(z)p_{n}(z)+p_{n-1}(z)}{f_{n+1}(z)q_{n}(z)+q_{n-1}(z)}.

It follows that

\displaystyle q_{n}(z)f(z)-p_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=q_{n}(z)\frac{f_{n+1}(z)p_{n}(z)+p_{n-1}(z)}{f_{n+1}(z)q_{n}(z)+q_{n-1}(z)}-p_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{n-1}(z)q_{n}(z)-p_{n}(z)q_{n-1}(z)}{f_{n+1}(z)q_{n}(z)+q_{n-1}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{f_{n+1}(z)q_{n}(z)+q_{n-1}(z)},

where for the last equality, we have applied (
2.4
). Note that,
\deg(f_{n-1}(z)-\lfloor f_{n-1}(z)\rfloor)<0
, hence by definition of the
a_{n}(z)
and
f_{n}(z)
sequences,
\deg(f_{n}(z))\geq 1
for
n\geq 1
. Recalling the degrees of
q_{n}(z)
were strictly increasing, we must have

\displaystyle\deg(q_{n}(z)f(z)-p_{n}(z))

\displaystyle=-\deg(f_{n+1}(z)q_{n}(z)+q_{n-1}(z))

\displaystyle<-\deg(q_{n}(z)).

This implies that the degree of
f(z)-p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z)
must converge to
-\infty
as
n\to\infty
, hence by definition of the metric on
\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
, as
n\to\infty

\displaystyle d\Big(f(z),\frac{p_{n}(z)}{q_{n}(z)}\Big)\to 0.

It follows the convergents converge to
f(z)
, and hence

\displaystyle f(z)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{n}(z)}{q_{n}(z)}=[a_{0}(z),a_{1}(z),a_{2}(z),...].

∎

We say
(p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}
is the sequence of convergents of
f\in\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
if it is the sequence of convergents for the continued fraction given by the continued fraction algorithm applied to
f
. It should be noted that, although we assumed that
f(z)
was not a rational function to guarantee that the algorithm will not terminate, this is not strictly required. If
f(z)
is a rational function, then it can be shown that the algorithm will terminate once an exact representation of
f
is reached, leading to a finite continued fraction expression of
f
. As rational functions are not very interesting in the context of Mahler numbers, we don’t place as much emphasis on cases such as these.

We conclude this chapter with two very useful properties about continued fractions for Laurent series which allows us to very quickly check if a rational function is a convergent of a given Laurent series, and also show that the convergents are very good approximations of
f(z)
in regards to how large the degree of the denominator of the convergent is.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
(p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}
be the sequence of convergents for a non-rational formal Laurent series
f(z)\in\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
. Then
\deg(q_{n}(z)f(z)-p_{n}(z))=-\deg(q_{n+1}(z))
.

Proof.

Using the series expansion from (
2.5
),

\displaystyle f(z)-\frac{p_{n}(z)}{q_{n}(z)}

\displaystyle=a_{0}(z)+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{q_{k}(z)q_{k+1}(z)}-a_{0}(z)-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{q_{k}(z)q_{k+1}(z)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{q_{k}(z)q_{k+1}(z)}.

Thus by multiplying by
q_{n}(z)
and using the fact that the degrees of
q_{n}(z)
are strictly increasing,

\displaystyle\deg(q_{n}(z)f(z)-p_{n}(z))

\displaystyle=\deg\Big(\frac{(-1)^{n}}{q_{n+1}(z)}+\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}q_{n}(z)}{q_{k}(z)q_{k+1}(z)}\Big)

\displaystyle=\deg\Big(\frac{(-1)^{n}}{q_{n+1}(z)}\Big)=-\deg(q_{n+1}(z)).

∎

An immediate corollary of this is that
\deg(q_{n}(z)f(z)-p_{n}(z))
is strictly decreasing over
n
, and so the quality of the rational approximation
p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z)
is consistently improving in our metric space.

Theorem 2.9

(Criterion for Convergents of Laurent Series)
.

Let
p(z),q(z)\in\mathbb{Q}[z]
be coprime and
f(z)\in\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
. Then
\deg(q(z)f(z)-p(z))<-\deg(q(z))
if and only if
p(z)/q(z)
is a convergent of
f
.

Proof.

If
p(z)/q(z)=p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z)
is a convergent of
f
, the statement is clear from Lemma
2.8
, as
\deg(q(z)f(z)-p(z))=-\deg(q_{n+1}(z))<-\deg(q(z))
. Now suppose that
p(z)/q(z)
is not a convergent of
f
. Fix
n
such that
\deg(q_{n-1}(z))\leq\deg(q(z))<\deg(q_{n}(z))
, and consider the system of equations for
a(z)
and
b(z)
,

\displaystyle q(z)=a(z)q_{n-1}(z)+b(z)q_{n}(z)

\displaystyle p(z)=a(z)p_{n-1}(z)+b(z)p_{n}(z).

From (
2.4
) the corresponding matrix has determinant
(-1)^{n-1}
, hence there exists unique polynomial solutions for
a(z)
and
b(z)
. If either
a(z)
or
b(z)
are zero, then
p(z)/q(z)
is a convergent, so this cannot be the case. Furthermore, if
\deg(a(z)q_{n-1}(z))\neq\deg(b(z)q_{n}(z))
, we will then find
\deg(q(z))\geq\deg(q_{n}(z))
, a contradiction to our choice of
n
. Thus,

\displaystyle\deg(a(z))

\displaystyle=\deg(b(z))+\deg(q_{n}(z))-\deg(q_{n-1}(z))

\displaystyle\geq\deg(q_{n}(z))-\deg(q(z)).

In addition, by using Lemma
2.8
,

\displaystyle\deg(a(z)(q_{n-1}(z)f(z)-p_{n-1}(z)))

\displaystyle=\deg(a(z))-\deg(q_{n}(z))

\displaystyle=\deg(b(z))-\deg(q_{n-1}(z))

\displaystyle>\deg(b(z))-\deg(q_{n+1}(z))

\displaystyle=\deg(b(z)(q_{n}(z)f(z)-p_{n}(z))).

Putting this all together,

\displaystyle\deg(q(z)f(z)-p(z))

\displaystyle=\deg(a(z)(q_{n-1}(z)f(z)-p_{n-1}(z))+b(z)(q_{n}(z)f(z)-p_{n}(z)))

\displaystyle=\deg(a(z)(q_{n-1}(z)f(z)-p_{n-1}(z)))

\displaystyle=\deg(a(z))-\deg(q_{n}(z))

\displaystyle\geq-\deg(q(z)).

∎

This criterion gives
\deg(f(z)-\frac{p(z)}{q(z)})<-2\deg(q(z))
if and only if
p(z)/q(z)
is a convergent of
f(z)
, suggesting that only the convergents achieve such a high quality of approximation relative to the degree of the denominator. This is somewhat analogous to the irrationality exponent where we seek good rational approximations relative to the size of the denominators of the approximations. It is this suggestion that makes looking at the convergents of formal Laurent series a powerful candidate when discussing irrationality exponents.

Chapter 3
Approximation Properties of Convergents

Recall, we are investigating formal Laurent series
f\in\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
that satisfy

\displaystyle f(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{A(z)f(z^{d})+C(z)}{B(z)},

(3.1)

where
A,B,C\in\mathbb{Q}[z]
,
A(z)B(z)\neq 0
. If
C(z)=0
, then the equation reduces down to the case of Badziahin which has already been analysed in detail, so for the rest of this essay we make the assumption
C(z)\neq 0
. Although
A,B
and
C
can have non-integer coefficients, we will assume they have integer coefficients as we can simply multiply the numerator and denominator of (
3.1
) by a rational constant to adjust the coefficients.

The goal of this chapter is to find an expression for the irrationality exponent of a Mahler number in terms of the sequence of convergents of
f
. While continued fractions for Laurent series give the best approximations of
f
in
\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
, this does not mean the approximation is accurate when evaluating them at integers, despite this holding intuitively. In the special case of functions satisfying (
3.1
) however, it is possible to produce countably many rational approximations for a Mahler number
f(b)
from any given convergent of
f
. This allows us to form an upper and lower bound of the irrationality exponent
\mu(f(b))
, which happen to be equal, giving an exact expression for the irrationality exponent. As we are working to the same goal as Badziahin but in a slightly more generalised context, much of the working in this chapter follows the same line of logic as his, but applied to (
3.1
).

To simplify the working in this section, we make use of some asymptotic notation.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0},(y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be sequences in
\mathbb{R}
. We say:

•

x_{n}\ll y_{n}
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that
|x_{n}|\leq C|y_{n}|
for all
n\geq 0
. Similarly,
x_{n}\gg y_{n}
if there exists a
c>0
such that
|x_{n}|\geq c|y_{n}|
for all
n\geq 0
.
1
1

1

This is the same as Big-O and Big-
\Omega
notation, however we denote the relationship with inequalities here since we repeatedly make use of the transitivity of this property.

•

x_{n}=\Theta(y_{n})
if
x_{n}\ll y_{n}
and
x_{n}\gg y_{n}
.

•

x_{n}=o(y_{n})
if for all
\epsilon>0
, there exists a
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|x_{n}|<\epsilon|y_{n}|
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.

1
Lower bound for
\mu(f(b))

As before, let
(p_{n}(z)/q_{n}(z))_{n\geq 0}
be the sequence of convergents for
f
. We can define another strictly increasing sequence
(d_{n})_{n\geq 0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
d_{n}=\deg(q_{n}(z))
. With this, we can give a lower bound on the irrationality exponent of some Mahler numbers.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f(z)\in\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
be a Mahler function that satisfies (
3.1
) and is not a rational function. Let
b\in\mathbb{Z},|b|\geq 2
be inside the disc of convergence of
f
such that
A(b^{d^{t}})B(b^{d^{t}})\neq 0
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the irrationality exponent of
f(b)
is bounded from below by

\displaystyle\mu(f(b))\geq 1+\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{d_{k+1}}{d_{k}}

Proof.

By Lemma
2.8
, for any fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
we can write

\displaystyle q_{k}(z)f(z)-p_{k}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=d_{k+1}}^{\infty}c_{k,i}z^{-i}

(3.4)

where
c_{k,i}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
k\geq 0
and
i\geq d_{k+1}
, and
c_{k,d_{k+1}}\neq 0
for all
k\geq 0
. Replacing
z
with
z^{d}
and using the Mahler functional equation (
3.1
),

\displaystyle q_{k}(z^{d})B(z)f(z)-(p_{k}(z^{d})A(z)+q_{k}(z^{d})C(z))=A(z)\sum_{i=d_{k+1}}^{\infty}c_{k,i}z^{-di}

(3.5)

we can repeat this procedure an additional
m-1
times to find

\displaystyle q_{k,m}(z)f(z)-p_{k,m}(z)=U_{m}(z)\sum_{i=d_{k+1}}^{\infty}c_{k,i}z^{-d^{m}i}

(3.6)

where

\displaystyle q_{k,m}(z):=q_{k}(z^{d^{m}})\prod_{t=0}^{m-1}B(z^{d^{t}}),\quad U_{m}(z):=\prod_{t=0}^{m-1}A(z^{d^{t}}).

(3.7)

and
p_{k,m}(z)
satisfies a recursive formula given by

\displaystyle p_{k,m}(z)=p_{k,m-1}(z^{d})A(z)+q_{k,m-1}(z^{d})C(z),\quad p_{k,1}(z)=p_{k}(z^{d})A(z)+q_{k}(z^{d})C(z).

Although not obvious, it can be checked that the closed formula

\displaystyle p_{k,m}(z)=p_{k}(z^{d^{m}})\prod_{t=0}^{m-1}A(z^{d^{t}})+q_{k}(z^{d^{m}})\sum_{u=0}^{m-1}\Big[\Big(\prod_{t=0}^{u-1}A(z^{d^{t}})\Big)C(z^{d^{u}})\Big(\prod_{t=u+1}^{m-1}B(z^{d^{t}})\Big)\Big],

(3.8)

satisfies this recursive relationship. Indeed, by relabeling the summation and product indexes, a loose inductive proof is as follows:

\displaystyle p_{k,m}(z)=p_{k,m-1}(z^{d})A(z)+q_{k,m-1}(z^{d})C(z)

\displaystyle=A(z)p_{k}(z^{d^{m-1}d})\prod_{t=1}^{m-1}A(z^{d^{t}})+A(z)q_{k}(z^{d^{m-1}d})\sum_{u=0}^{m-2}\Big[\Big(\prod_{t=1}^{u}A(z^{d^{t}})\Big)C(z^{d^{u+1}})\Big(\prod_{t=u+2}^{m-1}B(z^{d^{t}})\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle+C(z)q_{k}(z^{d^{m-1}d})\prod_{t=1}^{m-1}B(z^{d^{t}})

\displaystyle=p_{k}(z^{d^{m}})\prod_{t=0}^{m-1}A(z^{d^{t}})+q_{k}(z^{d^{m}})\sum_{u=0}^{m-1}\Big[\Big(\prod_{t=0}^{u-1}A(z^{d^{t}})\Big)C(z^{d^{u}})\Big(\prod_{t=u+1}^{m-1}B(z^{d^{t}})\Big)\Big].

Strictly speaking, the above expression has products that are not well defined as the lower index is larger than the upper index, however as a convention for the rest of this essay, we take their value to be 1. By evaluating
q_{k,m}(z)
and
p_{k,m}(z)
at
z=b
, (
3.6
) suggests that
p_{k,m}(b)/q_{k,m}(b)
may be a reasonable approximation of
f(b)
for large
m
. Furthermore, as
A,B
and
C
have integer coefficients,
q_{k,m}(b)
and
p_{k,m}(b)
will be rational. We must now find out how large the right hand side of (
3.6
) and
q_{k,m}(b)
grows over
m
to find an expression for the irrationality exponent. This is achieved with the following Lemma.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
b\in\mathbb{R},|b|>1
be inside the disc of convergence of
f
. Assume that for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
A(b^{d^{t}})B(b^{d^{t}})\neq 0
. Then for each
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle|q_{k,m}(b)|=\Theta(\beta^{m}|b|^{d^{m}(\frac{r_{b}}{d-1}+d_{k})}),\quad\text{and}\quad|q_{k,m}(b)f(b)-p_{k,m}(b)|=\Theta(\alpha^{m}|b|^{d^{m}(\frac{r_{a}}{d-1}-d_{k+1})})

with asymptotic dependence on
m
, where
\deg(A(z))=r_{a}
,
\deg(B(z))=r_{b}
and the leading coefficients of
A(z)
and
B(z)
given by
\alpha
and
\beta
respectively.

Proof of Lemma
3.9
.

Using (
3.4
), for any
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle z^{d_{k+1}}(q_{k}(z)f(z)-p_{k}(z))=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_{k,i+d_{k+1}}z^{-i}.

As we let
z\to\infty
, the RHS tends to the non-zero constant
c_{k,d_{k+1}}
. As
|b|>1
and
d\geq 2
,
|b|^{d^{m}}
tends to
\infty
as
m\to\infty
. We substitute this for our value of
z
to give

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{i=d_{k+1}}^{\infty}c_{k,i}b^{-d^{m}i}\Big|=\Theta(|b|^{-d^{m}d_{k+1}}),

with asymptotic dependence on
m
. Now note we can write

\displaystyle\prod_{t=0}^{\infty}\frac{A(z^{d^{t}})}{\alpha z^{r_{a}d^{t}}}=\prod_{t=0}^{\infty}P(z^{-d^{t}}),

where
P(z)
is a polynomial with
P(0)=1
. We claim this infinite product converges absolutely if
|z|>1
. This is obvious if
P(z)=1
, otherwise we write
P(z)=1+z^{i}Q(z)
where
i\geq 1
and
Q
is a polynomial with a non-zero constant term. Using relatively standard properties of infinite products, the infinite product converges absolutely if

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}|z^{-id^{t}}Q(z^{-d^{t}})|<\infty.

Applying the ratio test will immediately show this series converges when
|z|>1
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\frac{z^{-id^{t+1}}Q(z^{-d^{t+1}})}{z^{-id^{t}}Q(z^{-d^{t}})}

\displaystyle=z^{id^{t}(1-d)}\frac{Q(z^{-d^{t+1}})}{Q(z^{-d^{t}})}\longrightarrow 0\times\frac{Q(0)}{Q(0)}=0.

We also know
A(b^{d^{t}})\neq 0
for all integers
t\geq 0
and
|b|>1
, hence

\displaystyle\prod_{t=0}^{m-1}\frac{A(b^{d^{t}})}{\alpha b^{r_{a}d^{t}}},

converges to a non-zero number as
m\to\infty
. It follows

\displaystyle\Big|\prod_{t=0}^{m-1}\frac{A(b^{d^{t}})}{\alpha b^{r_{a}d^{t}}}\Big|=\Theta(1),

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\Big|\prod_{t=0}^{m-1}A(b^{d^{t}})\Big|=\Theta(|\alpha|^{m}|b|^{(1+d+...+d^{m-1})r_{a}})=\Theta(|\alpha|^{m}|b|^{\frac{d^{m}}{d-1}r_{a}}).

An analogous argument applies to a similar product over
B(b^{d^{t}})
. Putting this together,

\displaystyle|q_{k,m}(b)|=\Big|q_{k}(b^{d^{m}})\prod_{t=0}^{m-1}B(b^{d^{t}})\Big|

\displaystyle=\Theta(|\beta|^{m}|b|^{d^{m}(\frac{r_{b}}{d-1}+d_{k})})

\displaystyle|q_{k,m}(b)f(b)-p_{k,m}(b)|

\displaystyle=\Big|\prod_{t=0}^{m-1}A(b^{d^{t}})\sum_{i=d_{k+1}}^{\infty}c_{k,i}b^{-d^{m}i}\Big|

\displaystyle=\Theta(|\alpha|^{m}|b|^{d^{m}(\frac{r_{a}}{d-1}-d_{k+1})})

∎

Applying this Lemma and continuing the proof of Lemma
3.3
, we can say there exists a sequence
(x_{m})_{m\geq 1}
and constants
c,C\in\mathbb{R}
such that
0<c<x_{m}<C
for all
m
and,

\displaystyle|q_{k,m}(b)|

\displaystyle=x_{m}|\beta|^{m}|b|^{d^{m}(\frac{r_{b}}{d-1}+d_{k})}

\displaystyle=|b|^{d^{m}(\frac{r_{b}}{d-1}+d_{k})+m\ln|\beta|/\ln|b|+\ln(x_{m})/\ln|b|}.

A similar manipulation can be done for
|q_{k,m}(b)f(b)-p_{k,m}(b)|
. Noting that the
d^{m}
term grows much faster than all other terms in the exponent, we can write

\displaystyle|q_{k,m}(b)|

\displaystyle=b^{d^{m}(d_{k}+\frac{r_{b}}{d-1}+o(1))}

(3.10)

\displaystyle|q_{k,m}(b)f(b)-p_{k,m}(b)|

\displaystyle=b^{-d^{m}(d_{k+1}-\frac{r_{a}}{d-1}+o(1))},

(3.11)

where the
o(1)
terms converge to zero as
m\to\infty
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
and set
k=k(\epsilon)
such that

\displaystyle d_{k}>\frac{1}{\epsilon}\max\Big(\frac{r_{a}}{d-1}+1,\frac{r_{b}}{d-1}+1\Big).

This is possible as
d_{k}
is monotone increasing to
\infty
. Now choose
m_{0}=m_{0}(k)
such that absolute value of the
o(1)
terms are smaller than
1/2
for all
m\geq m_{0}
.

Claim:

\displaystyle|q_{k,m}(b)|^{-\frac{d_{k+1}(1+\epsilon)}{d_{k}(1-\epsilon)}}<|q_{k,m}(b)f(b)-p_{k,m}(b)|<|q_{k,m}(b)|^{-\frac{d_{k+1}(1-\epsilon)}{d_{k}(1+\epsilon)}}.

Supposing this claim is true, then by increasing
m
, we gain an infinite sequence of rational approximations to
f(b)
with the quality of approximation in terms of the size of the denominator, exactly as desired for the irrationality exponent. As
\epsilon
tends to zero,
k
tends to
\infty
as a function of
\epsilon
and the claim suggests that

\displaystyle\mu(f(b))\geq 1+\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{d_{k+1}}{d_{k}}.

This has not been completely justified, as we have only verified that
p_{k,m}(b)
and
q_{k,m}(b)
are rational rather than integers, as the definition of the irrationality exponent requires. Note however that by (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), we can verify that the denominator of
p_{k,m}(b)
and
q_{k,m}(b)
are independent of
m
, as
A,B
and
C
were assumed to have integer coefficients, and the denominators of
p_{k}(b^{d^{m}})
and
q_{k}(b^{d^{m}})
do not increase over
m
.

It follows that for each
k
, there exists a constant
N\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\displaystyle|q_{k,m}(b)|^{-\frac{d_{k+1}(1+\epsilon)}{d_{k}(1-\epsilon)}+\ln(N)/\ln(|q_{k,m}(b)|)}

\displaystyle<|Nq_{k,m}(b)f(b)-Np_{k,m}(b)|

\displaystyle<|q_{k,m}(b)|^{-\frac{d_{k+1}(1-\epsilon)}{d_{k}(1+\epsilon)}+\ln(N)/\ln(|q_{k,m}(b)|)},

where
Nq_{k,m}(b)
and
Np_{k,m}(b)
are integers for all
m
. As
m
increases, so does
|q_{k,m}(b)|
by Lemma
3.9
, hence when we take the supremum over all exponents, the additional
\ln(N)/\ln(|q_{k,m}(b)|)
factor tends to zero and will not affect the irrationality exponent. This resolves our problem, and hence shows

\displaystyle\mu(f(b))\geq 1+\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{d_{k+1}}{d_{k}}.

We are left to prove the previous claim.

Proof of Claim:

From (
3.10
), (
3.11
) and our assumptions on the
o(1)
terms, the claim quickly reduces down to showing the following two inequalities:

\displaystyle(d_{k}+\frac{r_{b}}{d-1}-\frac{1}{2})\frac{d_{k+1}(1+\epsilon)}{d_{k}(1-\epsilon)}>d_{k+1}-\frac{r_{a}}{d-1}+1/2

\displaystyle d_{k+1}-\frac{r_{a}}{d-1}-1/2>(d_{k}+\frac{r_{b}}{d-1}+\frac{1}{2})\frac{d_{k+1}(1-\epsilon)}{d_{k}(1+\epsilon)}.

We show both in order. First,

\displaystyle(d_{k}+\frac{r_{b}}{d-1}-\frac{1}{2})\frac{d_{k+1}(1+\epsilon)}{d_{k}(1-\epsilon)}-(d_{k+1}-\frac{r_{a}}{d-1}+1/2)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\epsilon d_{k+1}d_{k}-d_{k+1}(1+\epsilon)}{2d_{k}(1-\epsilon)}+\frac{r_{a}+r_{b}}{d-1}-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle>\frac{d_{k+1}(\frac{4r_{a}}{d-1}+4-1-\epsilon)}{2d_{k}(1-\epsilon)}+\frac{r_{a}+r_{b}}{d-1}-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle>\frac{3r_{a}}{d-1}+\frac{r_{b}}{d-1}+1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}>0.

Simply rearranging terms gives the first inequality. For the second inequality,

\displaystyle(d_{k+1}-\frac{r_{a}}{d-1}-1/2)-(d_{k}+\frac{r_{b}}{d-1}+\frac{1}{2})\frac{d_{k+1}(1-\epsilon)}{d_{k}(1+\epsilon)}

\displaystyle>d_{k+1}\frac{2\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}-\frac{r_{a}}{d-1}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{d_{k+1}(1-\epsilon)}{d_{k}(1+\epsilon)}(\epsilon d_{k}-\frac{1}{2})

\displaystyle=d_{k+1}\epsilon-\frac{r_{a}}{d-1}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{d_{k+1}(1-\epsilon)}{2d_{k}(1+\epsilon)}

\displaystyle>\frac{r_{a}}{d-1}+1-\frac{r_{a}}{d-1}-\frac{1}{2}>0.

This proves the claim.

∎

2
Upper bound for
\mu(f(b))

Before we evaluate the upper bound, we first require a lemma about irrationality exponents in general. In the previous section, we found a series of rational approximations to
f(b)
which we were able to use to get a lower bound on the irrationality exponent, however, as far as we know at this point, there may exist better rational approximations which give a larger irrationality exponent. Although it may not seem intuitive, it is possible to show that if there are too many ”good” rational approximations to a number, then there cannot exist many ”great” rational approximations. This concept will allow us to bound the irrationality exponent from above, which happens to intersect our lower bound found previously. We formalise this idea in the following Lemma.

Lemma 3.12
.

Fix
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Assume there exists two sequences,
\big(\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}\big)_{n\geq 0}\in\mathbb{Q}
and
\big(\frac{p_{n}^{\prime}}{q_{n}^{\prime}}\big)_{n\geq 0}\in\mathbb{Q}
forming rational approximations for
\alpha
. Furthermore, suppose there exists three real valued sequences
(\theta_{n})_{n\geq 0},(\delta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 0}
with
\theta_{n}
,
\delta_{n},\tau_{n}>0
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

1.

q_{n}^{\prime}\ll q_{n}^{\theta_{n}}
.

2.

Chapter 4
Computing the Irrationality Exponent

While the previous chapter successfully finds an expression for the irrationality exponent in terms of the degrees of the denominator of the convergents of
f
, this expression is not easy to compute. In general, there is no closed formula for what the convergents of a formal Laurent series may be and usually they can only be computed sequentially. This makes any limit involving the convergents of a function very difficult to compute.

The aim of this chapter is to investigate the convergents of a Mahler function satisfying (

Under the conditions of Theorem

\displaystyle\mu(f(b))=1+\limsup_{k\to\infty}\frac{d_{k+1}}{d_{k}},

\displaystyle\Phi=\Phi(f):=\{d_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}.

We say the tuple

The power of this Lemma is that, given one convergent of

Definition 4.2

\displaystyle\frac{p_{u^{\prime}}(z)}{q_{u^{\prime}}(z)}

This is most challenging part of showing the

Lemma 6.5
.

Suppose that there exists some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

Sadly, the conditions that induce the assumption of this Lemma are not clear other than trivial ones such as

This assumption is certainly not expected to hold in general, however restricting to this case allows us to apply the Euclidean algorithm as we have done before, reducing to an expression similar to that of the non-cyclotomic case.

\displaystyle G_{n,c}(z)

\displaystyle=\gcd(A(z)p_{u_{n}}(z^{d})+C(z)q_{u_{n}}(z^{d}),B_{c}(z)q_{u_{n},c}(z^{d}))

denotes the cyclotomic factors of

. As there is no addition term, we can apply an argument similar to that of Badziahin.

One definition of a cyclotomic polynomial is an irreducible monic factor of

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq n\\
\gcd(i,n)=1\end{subarray}}(z-e^{2i\pi\frac{k}{n}}).

6.5

[
2
]
. For any two positive integers

For any positive integers

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(z^{d})

\displaystyle=\prod_{r|r(d,n)}\Phi_{rns(d,n)}(z).

\displaystyle\xi=\xi_{nd}^{k+nt}\quad 0\leq t\leq d-1\text{ and }1\leq k\leq d-1.

\displaystyle N_{t}

\displaystyle=\{x\in N|\gcd(x,d)=t\}.

The

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(z^{d})=\prod_{t|r(d,n)}\Phi_{rns(d,n)}(z)=\prod_{r|r(d,n)}\Phi_{rns(d,n)}(z).

\displaystyle S=p_{1}^{\beta_{1}}p_{2}^{\beta_{2}}...p_{k}^{\beta_{k}}p_{k+1}^{\beta_{k+1}}...p_{k+l}^{\beta_{k+l}}\quad d=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}...p_{k+l}^{\alpha_{k+l}}

where the
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}
are integers such that
0\leq\beta_{i}<\alpha_{i}
for

and
0<\alpha_{i}\leq\beta_{i}
for

, which is certainly less than
S
. We also find
r(d,s)
is a multiple of
p_{1}^{\beta_{1}}...p_{k}^{\beta_{k}}
, thus

\displaystyle p_{1}^{\beta_{1}}...p_{k}^{\beta_{k}}ss(d,s)

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{\beta_{1}}...p_{k}^{\beta_{k}}\times p_{k+1}^{\beta_{k+1}-\alpha_{k+1}}...p_{k+l}^{\beta_{k+l}-\alpha_{k+l}}\times p_{k+1}^{\alpha_{k+1}}...p_{k+l}^{\alpha_{k+l}}

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{\beta_{1}}...p_{k+l}^{\beta_{k+1}}=S.

It immediately follows by Lemma
6.6
that
\Phi_{S}(z)
is a factor of
\Phi_{s}(z^{d})
. By the inductive hypothesis,
\Phi_{s}(z)|\Phi_{r}(z^{d^{m}})
for some

\Phi_{S}(z)|\Phi_{r}(z^{d^{m+1}})
. This completes the inductive step for
S
, and hence the result holds true regardless of what

is, and thus for all
n
.

∎

For any
P(z),Q(z)\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
with
P(z)
non-constant, let
\sigma(P(z),Q(z))
be the largest integer
n
such that
P(z)^{n}|Q(z)
. With this, we have the last required Lemma before the main proof.

Lemma 6.8
.

Fix
P(z)\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\geq 0
. Then
\sigma(\Phi_{k}(z),P(z^{d^{m}}))
is bounded from above for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Write
k=rs
where
r=r(k,d)
and
s=s(k,d)
. By definition,
r
is coprime with
d
and
\text{rad}(s)|\text{rad}(d)
. Now write
P(z)=P_{r}(z)Q(z)
where
P_{r}(z)
contains all the roots of
P(z)
that are roots of unity with some degree
k^{\prime}
where
r(k^{\prime},d)=r
. As the roots of the cyclotomic polynomial
\Phi_{n}(z)
are the roots of unity of degree
n
, we can write
P_{r}(z)
in the form

\displaystyle P_{r}(z)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\text{rad}(p)|\text{rad}(d)}\Phi_{rp}(z)^{\alpha(p)}.

Note that for any cyclotomic factor
\Phi_{t}(z)
of
\Phi_{n}(z^{d})
, Lemma
6.6
suggests
r(t,d)=r(ns(d,n),d)=r(n,d)
i.e. the
r
-value is preserved. As the cyclotomic factors
\Phi_{t}(z)
of
Q(z)
have

, it follows that this also holds for the factors of
Q(z^{d^{m}})
, and hence
Q(z^{d^{m}})
is always coprime with
\Phi_{k}(z)
. This means
\sigma(\Phi_{k}(z),P(z^{d^{m}}))=\sigma(\Phi_{k}(z),P_{r}(z^{d^{m}}))
.

We will now induct on the values of
s
in a similar manner to the proof of the previous Lemma. If
s=1
, applying Lemma
6.6
gives

\displaystyle P_{r}(z^{d^{m}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{\text{rad}(p)|\text{rad}(d)}\Phi_{rp}(z^{d^{m}})^{\alpha(p)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\text{rad}(p)|\text{rad}(d)}\prod_{t|r(d,rp)^{m}}\Phi_{trps(d,rp)^{m}}(z)^{\alpha(p)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\text{rad}(p)|\text{rad}(d)}\prod_{t|r(d,p)^{m}}\Phi_{trps(d,p)^{m}}(z)^{\alpha(p)}.

As
k=r
, the only factor to possibly have an effect is when
t=p=1
so
s(d,p)=1
, hence

\displaystyle\sigma(\Phi_{k}(z),P_{r}(z^{d^{m}}))=\sigma(\Phi_{r}(z),\Phi_{r}(z)^{\alpha(1)})=\alpha(1),

which is clearly bounded independently of
m
. Now fix
S\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\text{rad}(S)|\text{rad}(d)
, and assume the lemma holds for all
s<S
for our value of
k
. Using Lemma
6.6
we note the degree of any factor of
\Phi_{n}(z^{d})
is larger than
n
, thus by simply considering the
\Phi_{rs}(z)
with
s<S
, we have the upper bound

\displaystyle\sigma(\Phi_{rS}(z),P_{r}(z^{d^{m}}))\leq\sigma(\Phi_{rS}(z),\Phi_{rS}(z^{d^{m}})^{\alpha(S)})+\sum_{s|S,s<S}\sigma(\Phi_{rs}(z),P_{r}(z^{d^{m-1}})).

The first term is simply
\alpha(S)
, however the summation term sums over
s<S
, hence by the inductive hypothesis, each term is bounded by some constant independent of
m
, and so must the sum. This forms an upper bound independent of
m
, and finishes the inductive step and the proof.
∎

With these tools, we are now ready to prove Lemma
6.5
which will conclude this final chapter.

Proof of Lemma
6.5
.

Similar to the justification of the cyclotomic case, it is safe for us to assume
A(z)p_{u_{n}}(z^{d})+C(z)q_{u_{n}}(z^{d})
is always periodic to
B_{c}(z)
and

\displaystyle r_{g,n,c}=\deg(\gcd(A_{c}(z),q_{u_{n},c}(z^{d})).

We note from Lemma
6.6
, any divisor
\Phi_{k}(z)
of
\Phi_{n}(z^{d^{m}})
satisfies
r(k,d)=r(ns(d,n),d)=r(n,d)
. By definition,
q_{u_{n},c}(z)|q_{u_{0},c}(z^{d^{m}})\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}B_{c}(z^{d^{k}})
, hence we can split
q_{u_{n},c}(z^{d})
into a product of cyclotomic factors
\Phi_{k}(z)
grouped by the value of
r(k,d)
. These factors will not interact later as the r-value is preserved, hence we can split
r_{g,n,c}
into a sum and
G_{n,c}(z)
into a product,

\displaystyle r_{g,n,c}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in\mathbb{N},\gcd(d,r)=1}r_{g,n,c,r}

\displaystyle G_{n,c}(z)

\displaystyle=\prod_{r\in\mathbb{N},\gcd(d,r)=1}G_{n,c,r}(z).

Furthermore, as
A_{c}(z)
has finitely many cyclotomic factors, we have a finite number of these sequences having non-zero terms, hence it is enough for us to show
r_{g,n,c,r}
is eventually periodic for each
r\in\mathbb{N},\gcd(d,r)=1
. We will do this across three cases.

Case 1:
Both
B_{c,r}(z)
, and
q_{u_{0},c,r}(z)
do not contain the factor
\Phi_{r}(z)
. Let
\Phi_{rp}(z)
be some factor of
B_{c,r}(z)
or
q_{u_{0},c,r}(z)
where
\text{rad}(p)|\text{rad}(d)
and
p>1
. Now note by Lemma
6.6
, any factors
\Phi_{k}(z)
of
\Phi_{rp}(z^{d^{n}})
have
rps(d,rp)^{n}|k
. Note that
s(d,rp)>1
as
\text{rad}(p)|\text{rad}(d)
and
p>1
. This means that as
n\to\infty
, we also need
k\to\infty
for
\Phi_{k}(z)
to divide
\Phi_{rp}(z^{d^{n}})
. It follows that there exists some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
n\geq n_{0}
,

\displaystyle\gcd(A_{c,r}(z),B_{c,r}(z^{d^{n}}))=\gcd(A_{c,r}(z),q_{u_{0},c,r}(z^{d^{n}}))=1.

This is analogous to the case of non-cyclotomic polynomials, as once we write
q_{u_{n},c,r}(z)
in the form

\displaystyle q_{u_{n},c,r}(z^{d})=q^{\prime}_{n}(z)\prod_{k=1}^{n}B^{\prime}_{k,n}(z),

where
q^{\prime}_{n}(z)|q_{u_{0},c,r}(z^{d^{n+1}})
and
B^{\prime}_{k,n}(z)|B_{c,r}(z^{d^{k}})
, we find there exists a
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\displaystyle r_{g,n,c,r}

\displaystyle=\gcd(A_{c,r}(z),\prod_{k=1}^{n_{0}}B^{\prime}_{k,n}(z)).

Applying a very similar combinatorial argument to that of the non-cyclotomic case will result in this sequence being eventually periodic.

Case 2
:
\Phi_{r}(z)
has a factor of
q_{u_{0},c,r}(z)
, but not
B_{c,r}(z)
. We may then write

\displaystyle A_{r,c}(z)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Phi_{rp_{i}}(z).

where the
p_{i}
are some collection of integers. Let
S
be the set of divisors of the
p_{i}
. That is,
S=\{s\in\mathbb{N}:\,s|p_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq m\}
. Finally, let the elements of
S
be enumerated as
s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{N}
and

\displaystyle\sigma_{s_{i},n}:=\sigma(\Phi_{rs_{i}}(z),q_{u_{n},c,r}(z^{d})),\quad\Sigma_{n}:=(\sigma_{s_{1},n},...,\sigma_{s_{N},n}).

As each
p_{i}\in S
by definition, we see by definition that
\gcd(A_{r,c}(z),q_{u_{n},c,r}(z^{d}))
can be computed exactly given
\Sigma_{n}
, and thus so can
q_{u_{n+1},c,r}(z^{d})
. Additionally, noting that
\Phi_{k}(z)
can only be a factor of
\Phi_{n}(z^{d})
if
k
is a multiple of
n
, we have
\Sigma_{n+1}
entirely dependent on
\Sigma_{n}
,
A_{c,r}(z)
and
B_{c,r}(z)
. If we assume that each element of
\Sigma_{n}
is bounded independently of
n
, then clearly we must have
\Sigma_{n_{0}}=\Sigma_{n_{1}}
for some
n_{0}\neq n_{1}
as there are only finitely many values
\Sigma_{n}
could take. Due to the dependence on the previous element only, we have
\Sigma_{n_{0}+1}=\Sigma_{n_{1}+1}
, and so on, implying
\Sigma_{n}
is eventually periodic, and hence so must
\gcd(A_{r,c}(z),q_{u_{n},c,r}(z^{d}))
. It remains to show each element of
\sigma(\Phi_{rs_{i}}(z),q_{u_{n},c,r}(z^{d}))
is bounded independently of
n
.

We recall

\displaystyle q_{u_{n},c,r}(z^{d})|q_{u_{0},c,r}(z^{d^{n+1}})\prod_{t=1}^{n}B_{c,r}(z^{d^{t}}).

By applying Lemma
6.8
, we have
\sigma(\Phi_{rs_{i}}(z),q_{u_{0},c,r}(z^{d^{n+1}}))
bounded from above by some constant independent of
n
. Furthermore, by applying the ideas of Case 1 here, as
\Phi_{r}(z)
does not divide

, there exists a

such that
\Phi_{rs_{i}}(z)
is coprime with

. Thus,

\displaystyle\sigma(\Phi_{rs_{i}}(z),q_{u_{n},c,r}(z^{d}))

\displaystyle\leq\sigma(\Phi_{rs_{i}}(z),q_{u_{0},c,r}(z^{d^{n+1}}))+\sum_{t=1}^{n_{0}}\sigma(\Phi_{rs_{i}}(z),B_{c,r}(z^{d^{t}})),

which is bounded from above independently of
n
for each

. This finishes this case.

Case 3:

\Phi_{r}(z)
divides

but not
A_{c,r}(z)
. We perform a similar set up to Case 2, with the same

, set of divisors

and

vector where

can be determined using

and

can be computed from the

\displaystyle q_{u_{n+1},c,r}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{B_{c,r}(z)q_{u_{n},c,r}(z^{d})}{\gcd(A_{c,r}(z),q_{u_{n},c,r}(z^{d}))},

the

will not eliminate the

as

does not have any of these factors. As
\Phi_{r}(z)|\Phi_{r}(z^{d})
, we must have
\sigma(\Phi_{r}(z),q_{u_{n},c,r}(z^{d}))\geq n
. When computing
\Sigma_{n+1}
given

, we imagine this as removing the cyclotomic factors of

from each of the corresponding elements in

where possible, adding the cyclotomic factors of

then performing a ”redistribution” when computing

, the redistribution will cause many elements of

to increase larger than the maximum removal of the cyclotomic factors of

to eventually monotonically increase over

\gcd(A_{c,r}(z),q_{u_{n},c,r}(z^{d}))=A_{c,r}(z)

One final case we have left out is

and

both contain a factor of

, however it turns out that this will form a contradiction to our original assumption. Indeed, we have assumed

. This implies

, however we have

\displaystyle q_{u_{n_{0}+1},c,r}(z^{d})

\displaystyle=B_{c,r}(z^{d})\frac{q_{u_{n_{0}},c,r}(z^{d^{2}})}{\gcd(A_{c,r}(z^{d}),q_{u_{n_{0}},c,r}(z^{d^{2}}))}.

,

∎

Although this proof seemingly implies the assumptions of the Lemma will never hold if there exists a

and

, this can be avoided via a transformation similar to that of the powers of

\displaystyle f(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{A(z)\Phi_{n}(z)f(z^{d})+C(z)}{B(z)\Phi_{n}(z)}

\displaystyle\implies f(z)\Phi_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{A(z)\Phi_{n}(z)f(z^{d})+C(z)}{B(z)}.

Note that by Lemma
6.6

\Phi_{n}(z^{d})=\prod_{r|d}\Phi_{rn}(z)
, hence

\displaystyle f(z)\Phi_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{A(z)f(z^{d})\Phi_{n}(z^{d})+C(z)\prod_{r|d,r\neq 1}\Phi_{rn}(z)}{B(z)\prod_{r|d,r\neq 1}\Phi_{rn}(z)}.

It follows

is a Mahler function with its associated

and

polynomials containing one fewer factor of
\Phi_{n}(z)
each. This may be repeated until either
A(z)
or

does not contain
\Phi_{n}(z)
for any
n
coprime to
d
. Furthermore, as
\Phi_{n}(z)
has integer coefficients, it’s easy to see any Mahler number associated with
f(z)
has the same irrationality exponent as a Mahler number from
g(z)
, thus it’s enough to examine
g(z)
. Of course it needs to be checked if the conditions of Lemma
6.5

g(z)
, however this does not mean the case is lost entirely as our proof of the Lemma suggests. Collating the work in this chapter, we form the following Theorem.

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
f(z)\in\mathbb{Q}((z^{-1}))
be a Mahler function with an associated Mahler number
f(b)
that satisfies Theorem
3.13
and is irrational. Without loss of generality, we may assume
A(z)
contains no powers of
z
and there does not exist a

coprime to

such that
\Phi_{n}(z)
divides

and

.

If

contains non-cyclotomic factors, assume the assumptions of Lemma

Chapter 7
Conclusion and Future Work

In this essay, we have managed the problems of computing the irrationality exponent of Mahler numbers that originate from Mahler functions satisfying (
3.1
) and investigating the rationality of these irrationality exponents, extending the work of Badziahin to a slightly more general form.

On the topic of computation, the work of Badziahin extends very well once the new
C(z)
polynomial is accounted for, and due to this, there only a minimal number of new issues introduced with computing the irrationality exponent of these Mahler numbers. Perhaps the greatest barrier in being able to compute these irrationality exponents is that the method we have currently developed relies on the fact that it is possible to find a big gap in
\Phi(f)
, the set of degrees of denominators of
f(z)
. This task is not always achievable, as there may be no big gaps in
\Phi
resulting in the irrationality exponent of
f(b)
being 2, however we have not developed the tools to prove if a certain
\Phi
has no big gaps. This means in practice, if we cannot find any big gaps in
\Phi
, we cannot conclude what the irrationality exponent of any associated Mahler number may be with any rigor.

One way to manage this is to apply Lemma
4.4
and Lemma
4.9
to form an upper bound on the irrationality exponent which approaches 2 as more elements of
\Phi(f)
are computed. This is done by assuming that a primitive gap of maximal size occurs immediately after the last computed elements of
\Phi(f)
. While this can quickly produce an approximation of the irrationality exponent of a Mahler number as accurate as one desires, which may be sufficient for some purposes, an exact value still eludes us. Investigations into analogs problem have been attempted
[
3
]
, however none have succeeded yet as this turns out to be an extremely challenging problem. Regardless, further investigation would certainly be worthwhile.

Another path of further investigation is generalising what Mahler functions we are studying beyond that of (
3.1
). Recall the original definition of a Mahler function from (
1.5
). Finding a method to compute the irrationality exponent of all Mahler numbers rather than the subclass we have focused on would be highly valuable, however it is not likely that the work of Badziahin will extend nearly as well when compared to our work here as we have at least quadratic-style relationships for
f(z)
instead of the much more simple linear style equations we have restricted to in (
3.1
).

In studying if the irrationality exponent is rational, we succeed in finding several conditions for which the irrationality exponent of an irrational Mahler number is rational, however the
C(z)\neq 0
assumption does not allow us to solve the problem in full generality, and our assumptions are necessary to make any significant progress. While an obvious area of future work is to work to discard these assumptions or loosen them as much as possible, it should be noted that this may not be possible.

There is very little evidence behind the motivation of Adamczewski and Rivoal’s claim that the irrationality exponent of all automatic numbers being rational, other than the fact that no irrational irrationality exponents have been observed across all examples investigated. For our purposes, given the work by Badziahin and our work, we do expect the irrationality exponent of an irrational Mahler number satisfying (
3.1
) to be rational, but it would not be too surprising should this not be the case for general Mahler numbers from (
1.5
).
