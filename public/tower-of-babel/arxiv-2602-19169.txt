Virtual Parameter Sharpening: Dynamic Low-Rank Perturbations for Inference-Time Reasoning Enhancement
Saba Kublashvili

Abstract
We introduce Virtual Parameter Sharpening (VPS) , an inference-time technique that augments frozen transformer linear layers with dynamic, activation-conditioned low-rank perturbations. Unlike parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods such as LoRA, which learn static low-rank adapters, VPS constructs its perturbation factors on-the-fly from batch activation statistics and optional gradient signals, enabling test-time adaptation without persistent parameter updates. The perturbation takes the form Δ ​ W = γ ⋅ W ⊤ ​ V ​ U ⊤ ​ W \Delta W=\gamma\cdot W^{\top}VU^{\top}W , where selector matrices U U and V V are constructed via sparse activation-guided selection or Sylvester-coupled regression. We provide theoretical analysis of the perturbation’s spectral properties and describe an adaptive policy system that modulates perturbation magnitude based on activation energy and token-level entropy. The system incorporates multi-objective verification with iterative refinement for tasks with ground-truth supervision. We present the complete algorithmic framework, analyze its mathematical foundations, and discuss the mechanisms by which activation-conditioned computation may enhance reasoning capabilities in large language models. Implementation and experimental code are available at https://github.com/Saba-Kublashvili/vps-virtual-parameter-synthesis .



Keywords:
inference-time adaptation, low-rank perturbation, reasoning enhancement, large language models, dynamic computation

1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across diverse reasoning tasks
(
Brown et al. 2020
;
Wei et al. 2022
;
Kojima et al. 2022
)
. However, deploying these models effectively often requires task-specific adaptation, traditionally achieved through fine-tuning or prompt engineering. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods such as LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2021
)
and adapters
(
Houlsby et al. 2019
)
have emerged as practical alternatives to full fine-tuning, learning compact modifications to frozen base weights. These approaches, while effective, still require a training phase with task-specific data and produce static parameter modifications.

A complementary research direction explores
inference-time
adaptation, where model behavior is modified dynamically during generation without persistent parameter changes. Test-time training
(
Sun et al. 2020
)
, in-context learning
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
, and self-consistency decoding
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
represent various approaches to this paradigm. The appeal of inference-time methods lies in their flexibility: the same base model can exhibit different behaviors for different inputs without maintaining separate parameter checkpoints.

In this work, we introduce
Virtual Parameter Sharpening (VPS)
, an inference-time technique that augments transformer linear layers with dynamic low-rank perturbations constructed from activation statistics. The key distinguishing features of VPS are:

1.

Weight-dependent perturbation structure
: Unlike additive LoRA adapters (
W+BA
), VPS constructs perturbations of the form
\Delta W\propto WUV^{\top}W
, ensuring the modification respects the geometry of the existing weight space.

2.

Activation-guided sparsity
: Selector matrices
U
and
V
are constructed by identifying high-activation input and output dimensions, focusing perturbation capacity on task-relevant feature subspaces.

3.

Adaptive policy system
: Perturbation magnitude and rank are modulated based on activation energy and token-level entropy, providing input-dependent computation depth.

4.

Iterative verification refinement
: For tasks with ground-truth supervision, a multi-objective verifier provides feedback to the policy system, enabling iterative improvement across inference passes.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
reviews related work on parameter-efficient adaptation and inference-time methods. Section
3
presents the complete VPS framework, including mathematical formulation, builder algorithms, and the adaptive policy system. Section
4
provides theoretical analysis of spectral properties and perturbation bounds. Section
5
describes implementation details and integration with transformer architectures. Section
6
presents the experimental framework. Section
7
discusses mechanisms and limitations. Section
8
concludes.

2
Related Work

2.1
Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods modify a small subset of model parameters while keeping the majority frozen.
Adapters

(
Houlsby et al. 2019
;
Pfeiffer et al. 2020
)
insert small trainable modules between transformer layers.
LoRA

(
Hu et al. 2021
)
parameterizes weight updates as low-rank matrices
\Delta W=BA
, where
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times r}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{in}}
with
r\ll\min(d_{in},d_{out})
. Subsequent work has extended LoRA with adaptive rank allocation
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
, quantization-aware training
(
Dettmers et al. 2023
)
, and dynamic composition
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
.

VPS differs from these methods fundamentally: PEFT methods learn static parameter modifications during a training phase, while VPS constructs perturbations dynamically at inference time without learned parameters. The perturbation structure also differs—VPS uses weight-dependent construction rather than independent additive factors.

2.2
Test-Time Adaptation

Test-time adaptation (TTA) modifies model behavior during inference using information from the test input itself.
Sun et al. 2020
propose updating batch normalization statistics and minimizing entropy on test samples.
Wang et al. 2021
further develop entropy minimization for domain shift. In the context of language models,
Bansal et al. 2023
explore test-time training on the test input itself.

VPS shares the test-time paradigm but operates through activation-conditioned perturbations rather than explicit parameter updates. This avoids the computational cost of backpropagation through the full model for each input.

2.3
Dynamic and Conditional Computation

Dynamic networks modulate computation based on input characteristics. Mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures
(
Shazeer et al. 2017
;
Fedus et al. 2022
)
route tokens to different expert subnetworks. Early exit mechanisms
(
Schwartz et al. 2020
;
Schuster et al. 2022
)
allow variable computation depth. Hypernetworks
(
Ha et al. 2016
)
generate network weights conditioned on auxiliary inputs.

VPS implements a form of conditional computation where the perturbation applied to each linear layer depends on the activation statistics of the current batch. This provides per-input adaptation without the discrete routing decisions of MoE or the computational overhead of hypernetworks.

2.4
Self-Consistency and Verification

Wang et al. 2023
demonstrate that sampling multiple reasoning paths and aggregating via majority voting improves performance on reasoning tasks. Subsequent work has explored more sophisticated aggregation
(
Li et al. 2023
)
and iterative refinement
(
Madaan et al. 2023
;
Shinn et al. 2023
)
. VPS incorporates a verification mechanism that can provide feedback across inference iterations, implementing a form of iterative refinement through perturbation adjustment rather than explicit re-prompting.

3
Method

3.1
Problem Setting

Consider a pre-trained transformer model with frozen parameters. For a linear layer with weight matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times d_{in}}
, the standard forward pass computes
y=Wx
for input
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{in}}
. Our goal is to construct a dynamic perturbation
\Delta:\mathbb{R}^{d_{in}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{out}}
such that the modified computation
\tilde{y}=Wx+\Delta(x)
improves task performance while:

1.

Maintaining computational efficiency (sublinear in
d_{in}\cdot d_{out}
)

2.

Preserving stability (bounded perturbation magnitude)

3.

Adapting to input characteristics (activation-conditioned)

3.2
VPS Forward Pass

The VPS-augmented forward pass for a linear layer is:

\tilde{y}=Wx+\gamma\cdot(xA)B^{\top}

(1)

where:

•

A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{in}\times r}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times r}
are dynamically constructed low-rank factors

•

\gamma\in[0,1]
is a scaling coefficient (adaptive or fixed)

•

r\ll\min(d_{in},d_{out})
is the perturbation rank

The factors
A
and
B
are derived from selector matrices
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{in}\times r}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times r}
via:

A=W^{\top}V,\quad B=WU

(2)

Substituting (
2
) into (
1
) and rearranging:

\displaystyle\tilde{y}

\displaystyle=Wx+\gamma\cdot x(W^{\top}V)(WU)^{\top}

\displaystyle=Wx+\gamma\cdot xW^{\top}VU^{\top}W^{\top}

\displaystyle=\left(W+\gamma\cdot(WUV^{\top}W)^{\top}\right)x

\displaystyle=\left(W+\gamma\cdot W^{\top}VU^{\top}W\right)^{\top}x

(3)

Remark 3.1

(Weight-Dependent Structure)
.

The effective perturbation
\Delta W=\gamma\cdot W^{\top}VU^{\top}W
is a function of the frozen weights
W
. This differs fundamentally from LoRA’s additive structure
\Delta W=BA
where
B,A
are independent of
W
. The VPS perturbation operates within the column and row spaces of
W
, modifying existing weight structure rather than adding orthogonal components.

3.3
Selector Construction: Builder Algorithms

The core question is how to construct
U
and
V
. We present three builder algorithms with increasing sophistication.

: By selecting dimensions with high activation magnitude, the SK builder focuses perturbation capacity on the feature subspace most active for the current input. Dimensions with near-zero activation contribute minimally to the output; perturbing them would waste capacity.

3.3.2
SC Builder: Sylvester-Coupled Refinement

The SC (Sylvester-Coupled) builder refines the SK selection by solving a ridge regression that couples input activations to output activations within the selected subspaces.

Algorithm 2

SC Builder

0:

Batch activations
X
, linear layer
W

,

1:

Obtain initial

: The ridge regression finds a linear transformation

Complexity
: The additional cost is
O(Nr^{2}+r^{3})
for forming the Gram matrix and solving the

r
(typically 2-8), this is negligible.

3.3.3
Hybrid Builder

The Hybrid builder adaptively selects between SK and SC based on available information:

\text{Builder}(X,H,W,g)=\begin{cases}\text{SC}(X,H,W)&\text{if }g\neq\emptyset\\
\text{SK}(X,H,W)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(5)

where
g
denotes gradient information (if available from a verification loss). When gradient signals indicate an optimization context, the more sophisticated SC coupling is employed.

3.4
Spectral Norm Control

To prevent the perturbation from destabilizing the forward pass, we apply per-component spectral clipping to the constructed factors.

Definition 3.2

(Per-Component Clipping)
.

For factors
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{in}\times r}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times r}
and threshold
\tau>0
, define the clipping operation:

\text{Clip}_{\tau}(A,B)=(\tilde{A},\tilde{B})

(6)

where for each column
c\in[r]
:

\sigma_{c}=\|A_{:,c}\|_{2}\cdot\|B_{:,c}\|_{2},\quad s_{c}=\max\left(1,\frac{\sigma_{c}}{\tau}\right)

(7)

\tilde{A}_{:,c}=\frac{A_{:,c}}{\sqrt{s_{c}}},\quad\tilde{B}_{:,c}=\frac{B_{:,c}}{\sqrt{s_{c}}}

(8)

This ensures
\|\tilde{A}_{:,c}\|_{2}\cdot\|\tilde{B}_{:,c}\|_{2}\leq\tau
for all
c
, bounding the spectral norm of each rank-1 component of the perturbation.

3.5
Adaptive Policy System

The policy system modulates perturbation hyperparameters based on input-dependent statistics.

3.5.1
Activation Energy

For hidden activations
H=XW^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_{out}}
, define the batch energy:

E=\frac{1}{N\cdot d_{out}}\sum_{i,j}H_{ij}^{2}=\frac{\|H\|_{F}^{2}}{N\cdot d_{out}}

(9)

The energy is mapped to a scale factor via a saturating nonlinearity:

\sigma=1-e^{-E}

(10)

This maps
E\in[0,\infty)
to
\sigma\in[0,1)
, with low energy producing small
\sigma
(minimal perturbation) and high energy producing
\sigma\approx 1
(full perturbation).

3.5.2
Token Entropy

For the output logits
z\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
at a generation step (where
V
is vocabulary size), define the token entropy:

\mathcal{H}(z)=-\sum_{v=1}^{V}p_{v}\log p_{v},\quad p=\operatorname{softmax}(z)

(11)

The scale factor is adjusted to incorporate entropy:

\sigma\leftarrow\max\left(\sigma,\min\left(1,\frac{\mathcal{H}(z)}{3}\right)\right)

(12)

Rationale
: High entropy indicates model uncertainty—the probability mass is spread across many tokens. In uncertain states, increasing perturbation magnitude may help the model differentiate between candidates, analogous to raising temperature in simulated annealing to escape local optima.

3.5.3
Adaptive Hyperparameters

Given bounds
[r_{lo},r_{hi}]
,
[\gamma_{lo},\gamma_{hi}]
, etc., hyperparameters are interpolated:

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=r_{lo}+\lfloor(r_{hi}-r_{lo})\cdot\sigma\rfloor

(13)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\gamma_{lo}+(\gamma_{hi}-\gamma_{lo})\cdot\sigma

(14)

This provides smooth, input-dependent hyperparameter scheduling without discrete mode switching.

3.5.4
Improvement History

The policy tracks whether recent perturbations improved a verification metric:

\text{improved}_{t}=\mathbf{1}[\mathcal{L}_{t}<\mathcal{L}_{t-1}]

(15)

The proportion of recent improvements influences future scaling:

\rho=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{W}}\text{improved}_{t}

(16)

where
\mathcal{W}
is a sliding window of recent iterations. High
\rho
increases the scale factor; low
\rho
decreases it, implementing a simple form of online adaptation.

3.6
Multi-Objective Verification

For tasks with ground-truth supervision, we employ a composite verifier that computes a weighted loss across multiple objectives.

Definition 3.3

(Composite Verification Loss)
.

Given predicted text
\hat{y}
, ground truth
y^{*}
, and weights
\{w_{i}\}
:

\mathcal{L}(\hat{y},y^{*})=\sum_{i}w_{i}\cdot\ell_{i}(\hat{y},y^{*})

(17)

The implemented objectives are:

Numeric Loss
: Extract numeric values from
\hat{y}
and
y^{*}
using pattern matching, compute squared error:

\ell_{num}=(\text{extract}(\hat{y})-\text{extract}(y^{*}))^{2}

(18)

Unit Consistency Loss
: Parse quantities with units using dimensional analysis, penalize inconsistent dimensions:

\ell_{unit}=\mathbf{1}[\text{dim}(\hat{y})\neq\text{dim}(y^{*})]

(19)

Algebraic Form Loss
: Parse expressions symbolically, check structural equivalence:

\ell_{alg}=\mathbf{1}[\text{simplify}(\hat{y}-y^{*})\neq 0]

(20)

Self-Consistency Loss
: Sample
n
responses at temperature
T>0
, compute variance:

\ell_{sc}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{y}_{i}-\bar{y})^{2}/(\bar{y}^{2}+\epsilon)

(21)

The verification loss provides a signal for policy updates and enables iterative refinement across multiple inference passes.

3.8
Q/K Coupling in Attention

In self-attention layers, query and key projections interact via the attention score computation:

\text{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)V

(22)

where
Q=XW_{Q}
and
K=XW_{K}
.

Independent perturbations to
W_{Q}
and
W_{K}
may disrupt the alignment between query and key representations. VPS optionally pairs query and key projection layers:

\text{VPS}_{W_{Q}}.\text{peer}=\text{VPS}_{W_{K}},\quad\text{VPS}_{W_{K}}.\text{peer}=\text{VPS}_{W_{Q}}

(23)

This pairing allows coordinated selector construction, ensuring that perturbations to
Q
and
K
preserve their geometric relationship.

4
Theoretical Analysis

4.1
Perturbation Bound

Proposition 4.1

(Spectral Norm Bound)
.

Let
\Delta=AB^{\top}
where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{in}\times r}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times r}
, and
\text{Clip}_{\tau}(A,B)=(\tilde{A},\tilde{B})
with the clipping operation from Section
3.4
. Then:

\|\tilde{A}\tilde{B}^{\top}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{r}\cdot\tau

(24)

Proof.

The perturbation can be written as a sum of rank-1 terms:

\tilde{A}\tilde{B}^{\top}=\sum_{c=1}^{r}\tilde{A}_{:,c}\tilde{B}_{:,c}^{\top}

(25)

By the triangle inequality for the spectral norm:

\|\tilde{A}\tilde{B}^{\top}\|_{2}\leq\sum_{c=1}^{r}\|\tilde{A}_{:,c}\|_{2}\|\tilde{B}_{:,c}\|_{2}\leq r\cdot\tau

(26)

A tighter bound uses the Frobenius norm relationship:

\|\tilde{A}\tilde{B}^{\top}\|_{2}\leq\|\tilde{A}\tilde{B}^{\top}\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{c,c^{\prime}}(\tilde{A}_{:,c}^{\top}\tilde{A}_{:,c^{\prime}})(\tilde{B}_{:,c}^{\top}\tilde{B}_{:,c^{\prime}})}

(27)

For orthogonal or near-orthogonal columns (which the sparse SK construction encourages), the cross-terms vanish, yielding:

\|\tilde{A}\tilde{B}^{\top}\|_{F}\approx\sqrt{\sum_{c=1}^{r}(\|\tilde{A}_{:,c}\|_{2}\|\tilde{B}_{:,c}\|_{2})^{2}}\leq\sqrt{r}\cdot\tau

(28)

∎

Corollary 4.2
.

The perturbed output satisfies:

\|\tilde{y}-y\|_{2}=\gamma\|(xA)B^{\top}\|_{2}\leq\gamma\|x\|_{2}\|A\|_{2}\|B\|_{2}\leq\gamma\sqrt{r}\tau\|x\|_{2}

(29)

This establishes that the perturbation magnitude is controlled by
\gamma
,
r
, and
\tau
, all of which are tunable hyperparameters.

4.2
Effective Rank and Expressiveness

Proposition 4.3

(Selector Rank)
.

Let
U
,
V
be constructed by the SK builder with top-
k
selection and target rank
r\leq k
. If the selected indices are distinct (no repeated activations), then
\text{rank}(U)=\text{rank}(V)=r
.

Proof.

By construction,
U
and
V
have exactly
r
non-zero entries, each equal to 1, placed in distinct rows. The columns are thus linearly independent, giving rank
r
.
∎

The effective perturbation
\Delta W=\gamma W^{\top}VU^{\top}W
has rank at most
r
, but its action on the input space depends on the alignment between the selected dimensions and the weight matrix structure.

4.3
Connection to Projection Operators

When
U
and
V
are sparse one-hot selectors, define the projection matrices:

\Pi_{U}=UU^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{in}\times d_{in}},\quad\Pi_{V}=VV^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times d_{out}}

(30)

These are diagonal matrices with 1s at the selected indices. The perturbation can be rewritten as:

\Delta W=\gamma W^{\top}\Pi_{V}\Pi_{U}W=\gamma(P_{V}W)(P_{U}W)^{\top}

(31)

where
P_{V},P_{U}
are the corresponding selection operators. This reveals that VPS selectively amplifies interactions between specific input and output feature dimensions, mediated by the existing weight structure.

7
Discussion

7.1
Why Might VPS Improve Reasoning?

We hypothesize several mechanisms by which VPS may enhance reasoning performance:

Activation-Conditioned Computation
: Unlike static weights, VPS modulates computation based on the current input’s activation pattern. Different reasoning problems activate different feature subspaces; VPS’s activation-guided selection focuses perturbation on the currently-relevant subspace, potentially amplifying task-specific signal pathways while suppressing irrelevant noise.

Implicit Ensemble Effect
: The dynamic nature of the perturbation means each input sees a slightly different effective model. This provides implicit ensembling without multiple forward passes, potentially improving robustness on out-of-distribution reasoning patterns.

Iterative Refinement as Search
: The verification loop implements a form of search in output space. By adjusting perturbation policy based on verification feedback, VPS explores the space of possible outputs through continuous perturbation adjustment rather than discrete sampling.

Entropy-Aware Adaptation
: Increasing perturbation magnitude in high-entropy (uncertain) states may help the model differentiate between candidates. This is analogous to exploration-exploitation trade-offs in reinforcement learning.

7.2
Limitations

No Theoretical Guarantees
: While the mechanisms are principled, there is no formal proof that VPS improves reasoning. Empirical validation on specific benchmarks does not guarantee generalization.

Compute Overhead
: The dynamic construction of selectors and spectral clipping adds per-layer overhead. For latency-sensitive applications, this may be prohibitive.

Hyperparameter Sensitivity
: The system has many interacting hyperparameters (
\gamma
,
\tau
,
r
,
k
, builder choice). Optimal settings may be task-dependent and require tuning.

Verification Dependency
: The full iterative refinement requires ground-truth labels for feedback. For open-ended generation, only activation-based mechanisms operate.

L-BFGS Application
: The ephemeral L-BFGS component is applied heuristically outside a proper optimization loop. Its contribution to performance is unclear and may be minimal given the small scale factor.

8
Conclusion

We have presented Virtual Parameter Sharpening (VPS), an inference-time technique for augmenting transformer linear layers with dynamic, activation-conditioned low-rank perturbations. The key contributions are:

1.

A novel perturbation structure
\Delta W=\gamma W^{\top}VU^{\top}W
that respects the geometry of frozen weights

2.

Activation-guided builder algorithms (SK, SC, Hybrid) for constructing selector matrices

3.

An adaptive policy system that modulates perturbation based on activation energy and token entropy

4.

Multi-objective verification with iterative refinement for supervised tasks

5.

Theoretical analysis of spectral bounds and perturbation structure

VPS represents a step toward dynamic, input-conditioned computation in large language models without the need for task-specific training. The complete implementation and experimental code are available at
https://github.com/Saba-Kublashvili/vps-virtual-parameter-synthesis
.

Future work may explore: (1) theoretical analysis of when activation-conditioned perturbations improve generalization, (2) integration with other inference-time methods such as self-consistency, (3) application to multimodal models, and (4) more sophisticated policy learning through meta-optimization.
