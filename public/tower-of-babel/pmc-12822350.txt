Meningoencephalitis as a presentation of primary Sjögren’s syndrome: a case report
Authors: Ali Kadim Karim, Faiq I Gorial, Israa Ali Badi, Farah J Mahdi, Nabaa Ihsan Awadh, Ahmed Moaffak Hassan, Mohammad Khazaal Juma’a
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSjögren’s syndrome is an autoimmune, chronic inflammatory disease characterized by its impact on salivary and lacrimal glands, along with potential involvement of internal organs. Sicca symptoms, such as dry eyes and mouth, are typical features.Case presentationWe present the case of a 30-year-old Iraqi woman who presented with meningoencephalitis and acute motor axonal neuropathy, which were unusual as initial manifestations of Sjögren’s syndrome. Initial investigations included a complete blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, renal and liver function tests, and cerebrospinal fluid analysis. Brain magnetic resonance imaging showed hyperintense lesions in the brainstem and periventricular regions, and magnetic resonance venography was performed to rule out venous thrombosis. An aquaporin 4 antibody screening test was sent for suspected neuromyelitis optica and returned positive. However, subsequent magnetic resonance imaging scans of the spinal cord and optic nerve found no abnormalities. Serological tests confirmed the presence of antinuclear antibodies, rheumatoid factor, and antibodies against SS-A and SS-B, consistent with Sjögren’s syndrome. The patient was treated with methylprednisolone and intravenous immunoglobulin. Given the suspected diagnosis of Bickerstaff encephalitis, intravenous immunoglobulin was included in the regimen, although it was later ruled out. After 4 weeks of treatment, the patient showed significant improvement, regaining consciousness and partial motor function. Several months after symptom onset, the patient achieved independent mobility despite experiencing mild weakness in the right lower limb, and plans were made to initiate either cyclophosphamide or rituximab therapy.ConclusionsThis case underscores the diverse clinical presentations of Sjögren’s syndrome, highlighting the importance of considering autoimmune etiologies in patients presenting with neurological manifestations such as meningoencephalitis and acute motor axonal neuropathy. Early recognition and management of such atypical presentations are crucial for improving patient outcomes.Keywords: Primary Sjögren’s syndrome, Meningoencephalitis, Acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN)

Background

Sjögren’s syndrome (SS) is a common, underdiagnosed autoimmune disease that exhibits a wide diversity of organ-specific and systemic manifestations. The most common complaint is reduced lacrimal and salivary gland function (xerophthalmia and xerostomia, respectively), as well as parotid gland enlargement [1]. Twenty-five percent of cases develop internal organ involvement; these disease features can occasionally predominate and become the presenting manifestations of SS. The development of non-Hodgkin’s B-cell lymphomas is the major complication of the disease [2]. One of the extraglandular manifestations is neurological involvement; its prevalence is approximately 20% in patients with primary Sjögren’s syndrome [3]. It can be categorized into peripheral and central nervous system involvement, the most common of which is peripheral neuropathy. A review of the literature reveals that Sjögren’s syndrome is an important but poorly recognized cause of peripheral neuropathy. Distal sensory and sensorimotor neuropathies are the most common manifestations of peripheral nerve disease in primary SS, while motor axonal neuropathy occurs only occasionally [4].

The wide variety of neurologic manifestations in primary SS complicates its treatment approach. Each of these various neuropathic manifestations responds differently to corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) [5]. Retrospective case reports and case series provide limited evidence on the optimal treatment for Sjögren’s peripheral neuropathy. Most patients treated with IVIG for peripheral neuropathies and SS showed clinical improvement, and some patients also benefited from additional treatments such as corticosteroids, rituximab, and cyclophosphamide [6].

This case report showed meningoencephalitis as an unusual presenting feature of primary Sjögren’s syndrome.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old woman presented with a 1-week history of fever that responded to antipyretics without associated sweating or chills, along with a generalized headache that interfered with her daily activities and was accompanied by nausea, but without vomiting, visual blurring, or ocular pain. One day prior to admission, the patient developed numbness over her tongue, which spread to the whole body. After that, she was unable to stand or walk without support. Several hours later, she was found unresponsive to her surroundings, snoring, with frothy mouth secretions, and with a loss of sphincter control. She was transferred to the emergency department, where she received management and regained consciousness but remained sleepy. Her past medical history, from 7 years ago, is significant for meningoencephalitis. The patient mentioned that she previously experienced a complaint of dry eye, a sensation similar to sand in her eyes. She also had a prolonged history of multiple unexplained dental caries at a young age with a dry mouth. On examination, she was found to be stuporous and to have a slight left-sided facial asymmetry, as well as bilaterally and medially deviated eyes. The pupil was symmetrical and midsize, and had a sluggish reaction to light bilaterally. A funduscopic examination revealed an erythematous disc with clear margins. Both horizontally and vertically, the vestibulo-ocular reflex was impaired. The gag reflex was absent. Meningeal signs were negative. Upon motor examination, she was localizing pain in her right upper limb and withdrawing from pain in her left upper limb. She had normal reflexes in the upper and lower limbs. Plantar reflexes were bilaterally down-going. The impaired consciousness prevented the evaluation of sensory and cerebellar examinations. The Schirmer test was positive at 2 mm bilaterally.

A complete blood count showed a low lymphocyte count. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was 129 mm per hour, and C-reactive protein (CRP) was 205 mg/dL. Renal and liver function tests, as well as serum electrolytes, were all within normal limits. The thyroid function test was normal, and the antithyroid peroxidase TPO was negative. Urinalysis revealed turbid, alkaline urine with six to eight pus cells. The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was negative. A virology screen was negative. The cerebrospinal fluid test showed that there were 2850 red blood cells, 26 white blood cells (80% of which were lymphocytes), and 69 mg/dL of protein. The Gram stain was negative. The brain computed tomography (CT) scan was normal. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic resonance venography (MRV) revealed extensive abnormal signal intensity within the upper anterior medulla, entire pons, middle cerebellar peduncles, and midbrain, as well as a confluent abnormal signal in the basal ganglia, lateral thalami, and external capsule. The lesions showed hyperintense signals on T2 and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), hypointense signals on T1 with patchy diffusion restriction, and a mild mass effect, accompanied by features of increased intracranial pressure (Fig. 1).

The signal abnormality and lesion extension is more clearly depicted in coronal FLAIR images (D, E). Similar high signal intensity on FLAIR is also seen involving the basal ganglia, lateral thalami, and external capsule (F). The brainstem lesions show high signal intensity on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) (G) and decreased apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) value (not shown) consistent with restricted diffusion. No enhancement was noted on post-contrast images (H). Axial T2-weighted images showed dilated, tortuous optic nerve sheaths with a partially empty sella, findings consistent with secondary pseudotumor cerebri (I)

Initially, the patient was suspected to have meningoencephalitis and was treated accordingly. However, after 3 days of antibiotic therapy, there was no clinical improvement, and cerebrospinal fluid analysis did not support infection as the cause of her symptoms. Cerebral venous thrombosis was considered to be an alternative diagnosis, but MRI/MRV findings (involvement of the brainstem) ruled out this possibility and suggested an inflammatory cause rather than venous thrombosis. Therefore, investigations were ordered to evaluate for potential autoimmune diseases presenting with neurological symptoms.

While awaiting the possible autoimmune screen results, the patient developed weakness in her lower and upper limbs, could not turn her head to either side, and could not shrug her shoulders. The tongue was central, with no atrophy. The power assessment was grade 0, proximal and distal, both upper and lower limbs, with hypotonia. Reflexes were absent in both the upper and lower limbs, along with mute plantar reflexes. Cervical, dorsal, and lumbosacral spine MRIs were normal. Nerve conduction studies revealed acute symmetrical motor polyneuropathy affecting both the upper and lower limbs, sparing sensory fibers, consistent with acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN), a subtype of Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS). The serology revealed positive antinuclear antibodies of 4.86 (> 1.2) and rheumatoid factor of 142.11 IU/mL (> 25 IU/mL), as well as positive SS-A and SS-B, while the rest of the panel showed negative results. There was concern regarding neuromyelitis optica (NMO) as a differential diagnosis, given its similar clinical presentation and frequent association with Sjögren’s syndrome. An aquaporin-4 (AQP4) antibody screening test was performed and returned positive, with a titer of 1:160. We performed subsequent MRI scans of the spinal cord and optic nerve to check for NMO lesions, but found no abnormalities.

Therefore, we diagnosed the patient with primary Sjögren’s syndrome and meningoencephalitis. Treatment involved methylprednisolone 1 g for 5 days, alternating every other day with IVIG 30 g for 5 days, while waiting for the CSF culture result. Bickerstaff encephalitis was considered among the differential diagnoses and was the main reason for the addition of IVIG to the treatment regimen (since it is known that Bickerstaff encephalitis does not typically respond to steroid therapy). However, screening for anti-GQ1-b immunoglobulin (Ig)M and IgG returned negative results. Therefore, we ruled out Bickerstaff encephalitis as a differential diagnosis.

After 4 weeks, the patient regained consciousness, and her cranial nerve function, including the lower cranial nerves, returned to normal. Following removal of the nasogastric (NG) tube, she was able to eat normally. According to the power assessment, the upper limbs were graded 2 both proximally and distally, while the lower limbs were graded 1 proximally, 1 for dorsiflexion, and 2 for plantarflexion. Both the upper and lower limbs showed hypotonia and had reflexes of +1. Plantar reflexes were down bilaterally. The sensory examination revealed intact pinprick, proprioception, and vibration. The combination of encephalitis and Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), along with the investigation results, narrowed the differential diagnosis to neuromyelitis optica (NMO) and Sjögren’s syndrome. Both conditions have the potential to lead to encephalitis. However, with respect to GBS, neuromyelitis optica (NMO) can present as acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (AIDP), which is not a typical GBS variant (Continuum 2022). By contrast, Sjögren’s syndrome is associated with the acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN) variant of GBS.

Despite the initial exacerbation of symptoms after antibiotic administration and the following deterioration in her motor symptoms, treatment with steroids and IVIG led to a marked improvement. Several months after symptom onset, the patient achieved independent mobility despite experiencing mild weakness in the right lower limb. A follow-up brain MRI obtained after treatment showed near-complete resolution of the previously observed signal abnormalities on FLAIR images in the brainstem, basal ganglia, and thalamus. However, a small persistent residual signal abnormality was noted at the lateral aspect of the pons (Fig. 2). Therefore, the patient was closely monitored, and plans were made to initiate therapy with either cyclophosphamide or rituximab.

Discussion

The main sign of Sjögren’s syndrome (SS), an autoimmune disease that causes chronic inflammation, is lymphocytic infiltration of the exocrine glands, which are the salivary and lacrimal glands. This exocrinopathy can develop either on its own (primary Sjögren’s syndrome) or in conjunction with another autoimmune disorder, such as rheumatoid arthritis, systemic sclerosis, or systemic lupus erythematosus (secondary Sjögren’s syndrome). In the majority of cases, this condition develops insidiously over a period of months or years. It can involve a wide variety of organs, including the lung, heart, skin, kidney, and nervous system. Arthralgia and non-erosive arthritis are the most common extraglandular manifestations. The next most common is pulmonary involvement, which typically manifests as interstitial lung disease [7].

The American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) classification criteria for SS [8] assign a score of 3 to the labial gland biopsy and the serological test for anti-SSA/Ro antibodies, and a score of 1 to the ocular staining test, Schirmer’s test, and salivary flow rate test, respectively. An important complication of Sjögren’s syndrome is inflammation of the central and peripheral nervous systems. Primary Sjögren’s syndrome occasionally involves the central nervous system, and aseptic meningoencephalitis occurs in exceptional cases [9]. In 40–93% of cases with neurological manifestations, these symptoms may precede the onset of sicca symptoms [10].

The involvement of the central nervous system (CNS) includes myelopathy, multiple sclerosis-like syndrome, aseptic meningitis, and meningoencephalitis. Most of the reported cases of meningoencephalitis in SS refer to an acute, sometimes recurrent disorder with variable outcomes, which can be a rare presenting manifestation [9, 11]. Alexander et al. reported in their study that 20% of 25 patients with Sjögren’s-associated central nervous system involvement had recurrent aseptic meningitis or meningoencephalitis [12]. The clinical range of Sjögren’s-related neuropathy is wide and includes sensory ataxic neuropathy, multiple mononeuropathy, painful sensory neuropathy, multiple cranial neuropathy, autonomic neuropathy, ganglionopathy, and radiculoneuropathy [13]. Symmetric sensorimotor polyneuropathy (PN) and symmetric pure-sensory peripheral neuropathy are the most prevalent of these. Motor neuropathy has not been commonly recognized. However, only a few case reports have described this severe complication of Sjögren’s syndrome with fulminant limb weakness [14].

The treatment approach is variable depending on the severity of the presentation; in cases of aggressive presentation and progression of lesions, immunosuppressive treatment is used, with corticosteroids and intravenous cyclophosphamide being the most common. The literature has also used cyclosporine, azathioprine, and methotrexate [15]. According to the 2020 EULAR recommendations, the treatment of neuro-Sjögren in patients with CNS vasculitis or neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder involves first-line therapy with glucocorticoids, second-line therapy with cyclophosphamide, and third-line therapy with rituximab, with or without plasma exchange [16].

Conclusion

This case report highlights the rare and atypical neurological presentation of meningoencephalitis and acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN) in a patient with primary Sjögren’s syndrome. It emphasizes the importance of considering Sjögren’s syndrome in the differential diagnosis of unexplained neurological symptoms, even in the absence of its classical features. Early diagnosis and prompt immunosuppressive therapy can significantly improve outcomes, as demonstrated by the patient’s recovery following treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin. This case serves as a reminder of the diverse clinical manifestations of Sjögren’s syndrome and highlights the need for a high index of suspicion for autoimmune pathology, particularly in patients presenting with unexplained neurological symptoms.
