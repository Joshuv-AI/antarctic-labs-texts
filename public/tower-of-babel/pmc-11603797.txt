Higher complication and readmission rates after total knee arthroplasty with discharge to inpatient facility vs. home: a propensity score matched analysis
Authors: Sarah Whitaker, Sarah Cole, Maria Peri, Brady Ernst, Conor O’Neill, James Satalich, Jibanananda Satpathy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe purpose of this retrospective cohort study was to assess differences in complication rates, early readmission rates, and reasons for readmission following TKA based on discharge destination. Secondarily, we aimed to identify independent risk factors for developing any adverse event (AAE) in the 30-day postoperative period.MethodsThe American College of Surgeons (ACS) National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) was filtered using current procedural terminology (CPT) codes to identify patients undergoing TKA from 2015 to 2020. Patients were divided into three cohorts based on discharge destination: home, skilled nursing facility (SNF), or inpatient rehabilitation facility (IRF). Propensity score matching was used to account for confounding variables. Statistical analysis was conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Chi-square tests, and multivariable logistic regression.Results352,824 patients were initially identified with 303,375 discharged home, 31,635 discharged to SNF, and 17,814 discharged to IRF. Following propensity score matching, there were 5,000 patients in each cohort. Regarding postoperative complications, the home cohort had significantly a lower readmission rate (p = 0.01) and rate of any adverse event (p < 0.001) when compared to the IRF and SNF cohorts. The IRF cohort had a significantly higher rate of AAE than the SNF cohort or the home cohort. On multivariable analysis, increasing age, increasing BMI, increasing length of hospital stay, male sex, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification four, and history of COPD were all found to be independent risk factors for developing AAE.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that patients who are discharged to a rehabilitation facility or SNF following TKA experienced significantly higher rates of readmission and postoperative complications than patients discharged home, even after controlling for baseline demographic differences and comorbidities. Given the high financial burden associated with these facilities, it is important for physicians to consider these potential impacts on outcomes when determining patient disposition following TKA.Keywords: TKA, Arthroplasty, SNF, IRF, NSQIP

Background

Total joint arthroplasties (TJA) of the lower limb have continued to expand in demand and prevalence given their immense success in managing debilitating and disabling chronic pain and have become some of the most commonly performed surgeries in the United States [1–3]. While total knee arthroplasty (TKA) has been shown to be both safe and effective in the management of osteoarthritis (OA) [4], complications still occur and providers should be aware of any patient factor that may increase the risk of unfavorable outcomes in this population. In addition to safety profile information, there is growing interest in developing patient care plans postoperatively that also aim to minimize costs associated with their medical care [5, 6].

One variable of particular interest in the research to optimize both safety profile and cost effectiveness in TKA patients is discharge destination. Discharges to non-home facilities can significantly increase the cost of care after a TJA and current literature lacks the data to corroborate this increased cost with a better safety profile for patients with more comorbidities and risk factors [2]. Patients discharged to a skilled nursing facility (SNF) or inpatient rehabilitation facility (IRF) are more likely to be older, have long lengths of stay (LOS), use private insurance, and have greater severity of illness [2, 7–10]. Current studies have also begun to identify significantly higher rates of complications with discharge to a facility over home. Researchers have found longer LOS, greater rates of unplanned readmissions, and higher risk of surgical complications such as surgical site infection (SSI) and reoperation in patients sent to a non-home destination postoperatively [11–18].

Given the association of inpatient discharges with more complicated patients, there is an important need in this field of research to identify whether these greater risks are solely associated with the medical complexity of the case or if there may be further considerations about the safety of facility discharge destinations even in sicker patients. This study seeks to fill this gap in literature by comparing rates of readmission and complications within the initial 30-day window after a TKA based on patient discharge destination with a matched analysis to try to eliminate demographic variability.

This study aims to (1) assess differences in postoperative complication rates, (2) look at differences in readmission rates and reasons, and (3) account for demographic differences that contribute to readmissions based on discharge destination within the first 30 days after a TKA.

Methods

The American College of Surgeons (ACS) National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) was searched to identify patients receiving TKA from 2015 to 2020. The NSQIP database is a nationally used and de-identified registry of surgical data reported annually from nearly 700 hospitals across the United States [19, 20]. Reported variables include patient demographics and comorbidities, surgical procedure, intraoperative variables, and 30-day postoperative complications. Data is collected at each participating site by a certified Surgical Clinical Reviewer with experience in quality improvement work [21]. Audits of the database assessing inter-rater reliability (IRR) are routinely done at randomly selected sites to ensure reliable data reporting; IRR disagreement rates have been found to be around 2% in recent audits, suggesting that the NSQIP data collection protocol is effective [20, 22].

Patients undergoing TKA were identified with a query of the NSQIP database using current procedural terminology (CPT) code 27,447. Cases were excluded if they lacked relevant demographic and intraoperative data, including age, operative time, functional status, biological sex, total length of hospital stay (LOS), body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, and various comorbidities to ensure cases had sufficient demographic data for propensity score matching. Additionally, cases were excluded if they did not have a designated discharge destination, as this was the primary variable of interest for the study. After identifying and excluding relevant cases, patients were then divided into three cohorts determined by discharge destination: home or permanent residence, skilled nursing facility (SNF), or rehabilitation center. Preoperative and intraoperative characteristics as well as short-term complications were recorded for each patient in each cohort. Reasons for readmission within 30-days were also recorded for those patients requiring readmission.

The rehabilitation center cohort was the smallest of the three groups (N = 17,814), so 5,000 random cases were selected as a subset of this group using a random group generator algorithm in R Studio to allow for maximal strength of propensity score matching. Specific RStudio packages utilized include dplyr, data.table, MatchIt, ggplot2, gridExtra, epiR, and purrr. The “matchit” function was utilized in RStudio to perform 1:1 propensity score matching of the three cohorts using the nearest neighbor method. Matching was based on age, BMI, sex, race, ASA classification, functional status, smoking status, preoperative steroid use, and history of comorbid diabetes, congestive heart failure (CHF), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder (COPD). The inclusion of these variables in our matching algorithm was determined to achieve the best match between cohorts. Recent studies have shown that utilizing coarsened exact matching and propensity score matching on the same dataset produces similarly matched cohorts with similar levels of differences between groups [23]. Therefore, propensity score matching was employed in this study, followed by statistical analysis on matched cohorts’ demographics (as described below) to ensure we had achieved a sufficiently strong match for the purpose of this study.

Statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio software version 2023.06.0 + 421 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Baseline demographics, complications, and readmission rates were compared across cohorts using one-way ANOVA (for continuous variables) and chi-square tests (for categorical and dichotomous variables). One-way ANOVA and chi-square tests were performed using code written in RStudio, specifically using the “chisq.test” and “aov” functions, which can be found in the aforementioned RStudio packages. Multivariable logistic regression was performed using the “logistic.model” function in RStudio to determine if any demographics, operative characteristics, and comorbidities were independent risk or protective factors for readmission within 30 days, regardless of discharge destination. Statistical significance for all analyses was achieved if p < 0.05.

Results

Demographics

358,329 patients were initially identified who underwent TKA from 2015 to 2020. 3,489 patients were excluded due to insufficient demographic data. Of the remaining 354,840 patients, 352,824 patients were discharged to home, a SNF, or a rehabilitation facility. 303,375 patients were discharged home, 31,635 were discharged to SNF, and 17,814 were discharged to IRF. After matching, 5,000 patients were included in each cohort to maximize the strength of the match for a total of 1,500 patients in the final matched analysis.

Prior to matching, there were statistically significant differences in all measured baseline characteristics and rates of comorbid conditions. Patients discharged home were generally younger (p < 0.001), of a lower ASA classification (p < 0.001), more commonly male (p < 0.001), had lower BMIs (p < 0.001), and had lower rates of comorbid conditions when compared to the SNF and IRF cohorts. While these demographic differences were statistically significant, the magnitude of these differences was minimal for most differences aside from operative time, length of stay, and percentage of patients billed as outpatient (Table 1). After matching, the IRF cohort had a longer mean operative time (103.8 min, p < 0.001) compared to the SNF (98.0 min) and home cohorts (88.4 min) as well as longer mean LOS (3.61 days, p < 0.001) compared to the SNF (3.40 days) and home (2.01 days) cohorts. These longer hospital stays may be explained by the Medicare requirement for a three night hospital stay after TKA. Additionally, the home cohort had a higher percentage of outpatient procedures (21.0%, p < 0.001) compared to the SNF (3.80%) and IRF (4.06%) cohorts. After matching, the vast majority of baseline demographics did not vary significantly between cohorts, indicating that we had achieved a sufficiently strong match between our groups. Complications

In the unmatched analysis, rates of all short-term complications, including any adverse event (AAE) were found to be significantly lower in the home cohort when compared to the SNF and IRF cohorts. After matching, the home cohort experienced AAE at a significantly lower rate (5.04%, p < 0.001) when compared to the SNF (9.60%) and the IRF (11.8%) cohorts. Specifically, the home cohort experienced lower rates of sepsis (0.240% vs. 0.340% vs. 0.520% for home, SNF, and IRF respectively, p = 0.0636), pulmonary embolism (0.380% vs. 0.820% vs. 0.960%, p = 1.65e− 3), renal complication (0.120% vs. 0.40% vs. 0.420%, p = 0.0111), stroke (0.060% vs. 0.012% vs. 0.50%, p = 3.41e− 3), DVT (0.70% vs. 0.940% vs. 1.84%, p < 0.001), pneumonia (0.20% vs. 0.560% vs. 0.560%, p = 7.23e− 3), intubation issues (0.020% vs. 0.180% vs. 0.460%, p < 0.001), return to the OR (1.20% vs. 1.72% vs. 1.92%, p = 0.0129), extended LOS (4.16% vs. 12.5% vs. 17.1%, p < 0.001), and readmissions (3.70% vs. 4.56% vs. 4.90%, p = 0.0105) when compared to the SNF And IRF cohorts. The IRF cohort had a lower rate of postoperative blood loss requiring transfusion (0.120%, p < 0.001) when compared to the home (0.980%) and SNF (3.72%) cohorts. Readmissions

Prior to matching, patients discharged to SNF or IRF had significantly higher readmission rates when compared to the home cohort. Reasons for readmission differed significantly between the three unmatched groups. After matching, patients discharged to IRF or SNF continued to have significantly higher 30-day readmission rates (4.90% and 4.56% respectively) when compared to patients discharged home (3.70%, p = 0.0105). The only readmission reason which differed significantly between the matched cohorts was anemia. The most common readmission reason amongst all matched cohorts was surgical site infection (SSI) with 0.460% of all patients in the matched analysis being readmitted within 30 days of surgery due to SSI. Discussion

This study found that patients discharged to non-home destinations following TKA tended to be older, female, have a higher BMI, have a higher ASA class, have higher rates of dependent functional status, have longer operative time and length of hospital stay, and higher rates of several comorbid conditions. This is in line with findings by recent studies which report that patients discharged to an inpatient facility following total joint replacement are more likely to be older, female, functionally dependent, of a higher ASA classification, and to have higher rates of many comorbidities [7, 9–11, 24, 25]. Even after controlling for these differences in baseline characteristics, patients discharged to an inpatient rehabilitation facility or a skilled nursing facility had higher rates of many early postoperative complications when compared to patients discharged home.

After matching, patients discharged home following TKA had a significantly lower rate of AAE when compared to patients discharged to a SNF or to an IRF. This is in line with findings by Keswani et al. in their 2016 study which revealed higher rates of any postoperative complication, specifically organ/space infection, wound dehiscence, reoperation, unplanned intubation, thromboembolic events, cardiac arrest, sepsis, and death in patients discharged to non-home locations following total joint arthroplasty [11]. Similarly, other studies have shown that patients discharged to SNF after total joint arthroplasty had increased rates of inpatient medical complications, 30- and 90-day readmissions, reoperation, and increased postoperative pain [2, 17, 18, 24]. Many of these studies explain these increased complication rates in non-home discharges as the consequence of older, sicker, less functional patients requiring inpatient facilities for more extensive postoperative care [2, 7, 9, 11, 26, 27]. However, a recent study investigating reasons for non-home discharges after THA or TKA found that most patients discharged to SNF cite social concerns regarding their health as their primary motivator rather than a medical need [28]. Similarly, Issa et al. in their 2021 study found that 25% of patients who discharge to an inpatient facility after TKA do so due to patient preference, and 20% for family/caretaker preference [29]. Therefore, despite the fact that patients discharged to non-home destinations after TKA tend to be older and have more medical comorbidities, the majority of these non-home discharges may not be due to a true medical need for inpatient care. Further, this study found that the increased AAE rate in non-home discharges persists even after controlling for demographics and comorbidities, indicating that the higher rate of postoperative complications in patients discharged to SNF or IRF cannot be entirely explained by baseline cohort differences.

Regarding readmission rates, this study found an increased rate in 30-day readmissions in patients discharged to SNF or IRF compared to home after TKA. This supports findings by many recent studies which report higher readmission rates in patients discharged to non-home destinations following joint replacement [12–14, 16, 27]. Some studies attribute this higher readmission rate to the medical complexity of patients who require inpatient care following total joint replacement [15], however this study controlled for demographics and comorbidities, indicating that the reason for this increased readmission rate goes beyond individual patient characteristics. Similarly, a recent study by Johnson et al. found that patients discharged to SNF after TKA had higher 90-day readmission rates compared to patients discharged with home health care, even after controlling for mobility and access to caregivers. Therefore, there are likely other factors at play informing these increased readmission rates in patients discharged to non-home destinations. In their recent study investigating discharges to SNF following total joint arthroplasty, Kaidi et al. found that patients discharged to a preferred SNF (meaning the facility maintains a partnership with the surgical center) had shorter lengths of stay and decreased readmission rates when compared to patients discharged to a non-preferred SNF [30]. Additionally, Rahman et al. found the rehospitalization rate of the specific facility to be a significant risk factor for readmission for patients discharged to SNF following hospitalization [31]. Therefore, the findings of these studies and our current study suggest that intrinsic factors related to inpatient facilities may, at least partly, contribute to the increased readmission rates in patients discharged to non-home destinations following TKA.

Given the demonstrated complications associated with non-home discharges, it is crucial for physicians to balance the risks of discharge to SNF or IRF with the expected benefit of inpatient care following TKA. Not only are non-home discharges associated with higher complication rates, but they also carry significant costs to the patient. Recent studies have demonstrated that post-acute care costs for total joint arthroplasty may be up to 6.1x higher for patients discharged to non-home destinations [32, 33]. This represents not only a significant cost to patients, but also a significant financial burden on the U.S. healthcare system. In fact, one study estimated the annual cost spent on postoperative stays at rehabilitation facilities following joint replacement surgery in the U.S. to be around $2 billion [9]. Despite perceptions regarding the rehabilitative benefits of inpatient facilities, there is some evidence that patients discharged to IRF or SNF may not have any difference in functional outcomes postoperatively compared to patients discharged home [27, 34]. Given the increased complication and readmission rates, higher costs, and potential lack of functional benefit associated with non-home discharges, this study suggests that physicians should strive for home discharges following TKA unless there is a demonstrated medical benefit to be gained through discharge to SNF or IRF.

This study identified several independent risk factors for unplanned readmission following TKA, including increasing age, increasing BMI, increasing LOS, male sex, ASA classification of 4, and history of COPD. These findings corroborate recent studies which identify patient demographics, such as male sex and older age, as well as comorbidities, namely higher ASA classification, to be independent risk factors for readmissions following joint replacement surgery [12, 26]. Additionally, Mesko et al. similarly demonstrated that higher ASA classification was an independent predictor of unplanned readmissions following THA and TKA [15]. These findings provide important evidence to help physicians tailor preoperative counseling to individual patients based on characteristics and comorbidities.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, this study only reported on certain complications and did not address functional outcomes or mechanical complications following TKA. While these are important outcomes to consider in the context of postoperative complications, they are beyond the scope of this study which sought to focus on early, life-threatening complications. Second, reasons for readmission were elucidated based on ICD codes and, therefore, rely on the accuracy of the codes entered by billing departments. However, the NSQIP database is subjected to random audits to ensure data accuracy and inter-rater disagreement rates are as low as 2% [35, 36], therefore we believe the risk of inaccurate coding to be minimal. Lastly, given the limitations of the NSQIP database, we are only able to report on complications occurring in the 30-day postoperative period. However, a recent study investigating readmissions following THA or TKA in nearly 1.5 million patients found that readmissions peaked on the fourth postoperative day, and over 75% of readmissions occurring in the first 90 days occurred in the 30-day postoperative period [37]. Therefore, we believe that the 30-day postoperative period is sufficient to capture the majority of unplanned readmissions and life-threatening complications.

Conclusions

In this study, study patients who were discharged to a rehabilitation facility or SNF following TKA experienced higher rates of readmission and postoperative complications than patients discharged home, even after controlling for baseline demographic differences and comorbidities, indicating that the difference in complication rates cannot be entirely explained by individual, patient-level differences. Given the high financial burden associated with these facilities, it is important for physicians to consider these potential impacts on outcomes when determining patient disposition following TKA.
