Association of lifestyle and dietary preferences with precancerous serrated polyps: a hospital-based case-control study in Guangzhou, China
Authors: Shaoyu Cheng, Liji Chen, Shujun Liu, Haiyan Zhang, Cailing Zhong, Tianwen Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrecancerous serrated polyps (SPs) are significant precursor lesions of colorectal cancer (CRC), whose development is associated with lifestyle and dietary preferences. However, the relationship between lifestyle, dietary preferences with precancerous SPs remains underexplored in regionally specific cohorts. This study aimed to investigate the risk associations between lifestyle and dietary preferences and precancerous SPs in a hospital-based cohort from Guangzhou, China.MethodsWe conducted a hospital-based case-control study between 2021 and 2023 in Guangzhou, China, including 62 patients with histologically confirmed precancerous SP and 1247 controls. Before colonoscopy, participants completed validated questionnaires assessing demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and dietary preferences. Relative risk (RR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for precancerous SPs were estimated using multivariable modified Poisson regression models.ResultsSmoking was significantly associated with precancerous SP risk (RR 4.85, 95% CI: 2.64–8.89), with a dose-response relationship for smoking duration and daily cigarette consumption (P-trend < 0.001). Beer preference (RR 2.68, 95% CI: 1.31–5.49), frequent red meat (RR 7.32, 95% CI: 1.03–52.13), and freshwater fish consumption (RR 2.01, 95% CI: 1.08–3.75) were positively associations with precancerous SP risk. Conversely, daily water intake of 2–4 cups (RR 0.21, 95% CI: 0.07–0.63) or 4–8 cups (RR 0.27, 95% CI: 0.10–0.75), and chili peppers consumption (RR 0.47, 95% CI: 0.24–0.94) were significantly inversely.ConclusionThis study highlights the critical role of lifestyle and dietary factors in precancerous SP, suggesting that regional prevention strategies targeting lifestyle and dietary modifications may help alleviate the CRC burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04388-8.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Serrated polyps, Lifestyle, Dietary, Factors, Risk

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most prevalent cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with its disease burden on the rise. Recent epidemiological projections indicate that by 2040, there will be an estimated 3.2 million new cases of CRC and 1.6 million deaths annually across the globe [1]. Approximately one-third of CRC cases arise through the serrated pathway. According to the 2019 World Health Organization (WHO) classification criteria, serrated polyps (SPs) are categorized into three primary subtypes: hyperplastic polyps (HPs), sessile serrated lesions (SSLs), and traditional serrated adenomas (TSAs) [2, 3]. It is noteworthy that while HPs constitute about two-thirds of all SPs, their risk of malignant transformation is minimal [4]. In contrast, SSLs and TSAs, as precancerous SPs, exhibit a significant potential for malignant transformation, advancing rapidly to invasive carcinoma and being closely linked to the emergence of interval colorectal cancer [4–6].

Research indicates that a substantial proportion of CRC cases are associated with modifiable risk factors, such as lifestyle choices, dietary preferences, and environmental exposures [7–9]. Consequently, identifying these modifiable risk factors and implementing targeted interventions has become a fundamental aspect of primary CRC prevention [10]. Although existing studies have established associations between smoking, alcohol consumption, and red meat consumption with the risk of SPs, several critical knowledge gaps hinder the development of targeted prevention strategies for region-specific cohorts [11–13]. First, most of these investigations treat SPs as a homogeneous group, overlooking the risk heterogeneity among different subtypes. For instance, one study found that alcohol consumption was positively correlated with the risk of HPs but did not show a significant association with SSLs [14]. Second, there are notable racial differences in the development of SPs. White populations exhibit a higher risk of developing SPs compared to other racial groups, a disparity that may stem from a combination of genetic susceptibility, lifestyle factors, and dietary patterns [12, 13, 15, 16]. Third, substantial variations exist in dietary structures across geographical regions. Unlike Western dietary habits, the typical diet in East Asia is predominantly plant-based, supplemented by white meats (such as fish and poultry) and soy products, with an emphasis on poultry, freshwater fish, and seafood, particularly in southern China [17]. However, the impact of lifestyle and dietary preferences in the geographic region on the development of precancerous SPs remains inadequately understood.

Therefore, we conducted a hospital-based case-control study at a tertiary hospital in Guangzhou, China to investigate the association between lifestyle and dietary preferences and the risk of precancerous SPs, aiming to inform targeted CRC prevention strategies.

Methods

Study design and population

This study utilized a hospital-based case-control design. Participants were recruited from the Gastrointestinal Endoscopy Center at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, a provincial tertiary hospital located in Guangzhou, China. Out of the 78,124 individuals who underwent colonoscopy between November 1, 2021, and September 30, 2023, 5,030 met the initial inclusion criteria: (1) completion of a full colonoscopy; (2) completion of a survey questionnaire; and (3) provision of signed written informed consent. We excluded 3,721 participants due to: (1) inadequate bowel preparation quality, defined as a Boston Bowel Preparation Score (BBPS) of less than 6; (2) the presence of other bowel pathologies, including colorectal adenomas, colorectal HPs, inflammatory bowel disease, CRC, etc.; and (3) unreasonable or inconsistent questionnaire information. Cases were defined as patients with at least one pathologically confirmed sessile serrated lesion (SSL) or traditional serrated adenoma (TSA). Patients with only hyperplastic polyps (HPs) were excluded from the case group because, despite being a common SP subtype, HPs are associated with minimal risk of malignant transformation, whereas SSLs and TSA are established precancerous lesions [4, 6]. Control group participants were defined as individuals with normal findings on complete colonoscopy. Finally, 1,309 participants were included in the study, comprising 62 patients in the case group and 1,247 individuals in the control group. The study flow chart is presented in Fig. 1.

Data collection and variable definition

All participants provided written informed consent and completed a face-to-face questionnaire before undergoing colonoscopy, and colonoscopy was performed on the same day the questionnaire was completed.The questionnaire gathered information on: (1) demographic characteristics and anthropometric measures, including age, gender, height, and weight; (2) medical history, encompassing conditions such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, a personal history of malignant tumors, and history of colorectal surgeries; (3) family history, including first-degree relatives with CRC and a familial history of adenomatous polyps; and (4) personal history, covering smoking status, alcohol consumption, lifestyle, dietary habits, and preferences. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by height in meters squared and categorized according to WHO standards as underweight (< 18.50 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m²), and obesity (≥ 30.0 kg/m²). Smoking status was classified as never smoked, current smoker (smoking more than 1 cigarette per day for over 6 months), and former smoker (abstinent for 2 years). Smokers were queried about their smoking duration and daily cigarette consumption, categorized as less than 10 years, 10–20 years, and over 20 years, and consumption as less than ½ pack, ½ to 1 pack, and more than 1 pack per day. Alcohol consumption was categorized as never drank, current drinker (consuming alcohol more than once per week for over 6 months), and former drinker (abstinent for 2 years). Alcohol consumers were asked about their daily consumption and preferred types of alcohol, with consumption categorized as less than 50 g, 50–100 g, 100–200 g, and more than 200 g daily; preferred types included red wine, beer, and liquor. Physical activity was assessed based on frequency (≥ 3 times per week over the past year) and intensity, categorized as sedentary (e.g., desk work), light (e.g., brisk walking), moderate (e.g., bicycling, ping-pong), and vigorous (e.g., weight training, swimming). Daily water consumption was categorized as 1 cup or less, 2–4 cups, 4–8 cups, and more than 8 cups. Lifestyle and dietary habits, such as staying up late or taking naps, were defined as occurring at least 3 times per week over the past year. Food preferences were evaluated based on the frequency of consumption (≥ 3 times per week over the past year) across various food groups, including red meat, poultry, seafood, freshwater fish, soy products, dairy products, eggs, chili peppers, vegetables, and fruits.

Histopathologic evaluation

All colonoscopies were conducted by experienced endoscopists, who meticulously documented bowel preparation quality, procedural integrity, and lesion characteristics (including location, morphology, and size) using a standardized reporting system. Histopathologic diagnoses were independently reviewed by two senior pathologists. Precancerous SPs were classified as SSLs and TSAs by the fifth edition of the WHO criteria. Polyps were categorized based on their location: those situated from the cecum to the splenic flexure of the colon were designated as proximal polyps; those located in the descending and sigmoid colon were classified as distal polyps; and those found at the rectoethmoidal junction and rectum were identified as rectal polyps. Additionally, polyps were stratified by size, with those measuring less than 10 mm in diameter classified as small polyps, and those measuring 10 mm or more classified as large polyps.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages), and group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, with group comparisons performed using the t-test. For non-normally distributed data, the median and interquartile range (IQR) were reported, and the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for comparisons. Given the low incidence rate of precancerous SPs in this study, a modified Poisson regression model was applied to evaluate the association between exposure factors and the risk of precancerous SPs, with the presence of precancerous SPs as the dependent variable. Relative risk (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. The regression model was constructed in three stages: Model 1: Unadjusted; Model 2: Adjusted for age (≤ 44, 45–59, ≥ 60) and gender (male vs. female); Model 3: Further adjusted for body mass index (BMI; <24.9, 25.0–29.9, ≥ 30) and history of chronic disease, including hypertension, diabetes, and cancer (yes vs. no). A linear trend test (P-trend) was conducted to assess the risk of developing precancerous SPs for ordered categorical variables, such as smoking duration, daily cigarette consumption, and daily alcohol consumption. As part of the exploratory analyses, stratified analyses were performed by age (≤ 44, 45–59, ≥ 60) and gender (male vs. female). Subgroup analyses were conducted based on anatomical site (proximal vs. distal polyps) and polyp size (< 10 mm vs. ≥10 mm). For these stratified and subgroup analyses, modified Poisson regression models were used, adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and history of chronic disease. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.3). All P-values were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Comparison of characteristics between case and control groups

Table 2 displays the comparison of demographic characteristics, lifestyle, and dietary habits between the case and control groups. Significant differences were identified between the two groups regarding gender, age, smoking behavior, drinking behavior, and the consumption of freshwater fish, soy products, and chili peppers (all P < 0.05). Conversely, there were no statistically significant differences concerning BMI, previous medical history, frequency of physical activity, and the consumption of red meat, dairy products, vegetables, and fruits (all P > 0.05). Compared to the control group, the case group exhibited a higher proportion of males, a greater number of individuals over the age of 60, and a higher percentage of current smokers and alcohol consumers. In terms of dietary factors, the case group consumed freshwater fish significantly more frequently than the control group, while their consumption frequency of soy products and chili peppers was notably lower.

Multivariable regression analysis of precancerous SPs risk

Table 3 presents the results of the multivariable modified Poisson regression analysis examining the relationship between lifestyle, dietary preferences, and the presence of SPs. Current smokers exhibited a significantly increased risk of developing precancerous SPs (RR 4.846, 95% CI: (2.641–8.892). This risk demonstrated a dose-response relationship with both the duration of smoking and the number of cigarettes smoked per day (P-trend < 0.001). Although overall alcohol consumption did not show a statistically significant association, individuals who preferred beer had a notably higher risk of precancerous SPs (RR 2.677, 95% CI: 1.305–5.490).

Furthermore, compared to individuals who drank only one glass of water per day, those who drank 2–4 glasses had a significantly lower risk of precancerous SPs (RR 0.213, 95% CI: 0.072–0.631), as did those who drank 4–8 glasses (RR 0.271, 95% CI: 0.099–0.745). The analysis of dietary preferences revealed that a high-frequency consumption of red meat (RR 7.315, 95% CI: 1.026–52.129) and freshwater fish (RR 2.009, 95% CI: 1.076–3.749) was associated with a significantly increased risk of precancerous SPs. Conversely, frequent consumption of chili peppers (RR 0.473, 95% CI: 0.238–0.938) was linked to a significantly lower risk, indicating a protective effect against the development of precancerous SPs.

Subgroup analysis of risk factors based on pathology, location, and size

The subgroup analyses presented in Table 4 highlight the heterogeneity of risk factors based on pathology type, location, and lesion size. Smoking behavior significantly elevated the risk of disease across all subgroups (all P < 0.05), which aligns with the findings in Table 3. Alcohol consumption was linked to an increased risk of TSAs, distal polyps, and small polyps (all P < 0.05). Interestingly, drinking two or more glasses of alcohol per day significantly reduced the risk of TSAs.

Discussion

In this case-control study, we explored not only the common lifestyle factors associated with precancerous SPs, such as smoking and alcohol consumption, but also examined the impact of typical dietary patterns prevalent in the region, including the consumption of poultry, seafood, and freshwater fish. This approach offers fresh insights into how lifestyle and dietary preferences may influence the risk of developing precancerous SPs, contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of the factors affecting colorectal health in this specific demographic.

The findings of this study confirm that smoking is an independent risk factor for the development of precancerous SPs, aligning with the conclusions of previous research [18]. Nicotine derivatives in tobacco may contribute to the formation of precancerous SPs by directly inducing mutations in the BRAF V600E and KRAS genes, as well as by exacerbating disturbances in the intestinal microbiota [18–22]. Importantly, our results indicate that the risk of precancerous SPs in individuals who quit smoking tends to approach that of nonsmokers, highlighting the potential value of smoking cessation as a strategy for primary prevention. Furthermore, a sex-stratified analysis revealed that smoking was significantly associated with the risk of colorectal precancerous SPs in males. However, no significant association was observed in females. This may be attributable to the relatively small sample size of female smokers, resulting in insufficient statistical power to detect a potential association; furthermore, the possibility of a true sex difference in smoking effects cannot be completely excluded. A study in Taiwan, China, showed that the effect of smoking on death was higher in male CRC patients than in female CRC patients [23]. This difference may be explained by the protective effects of estrogen receptor β, which enhances DNA mismatch repair and cell cycle regulation. Additionally, estrogen’s modulation of gut microbial metabolites may reduce the likelihood of chronic inflammation, further lowering the risk [24–26]. Future studies should incorporate larger sample sizes with a specific focus on female smokers to further elucidate these findings, thereby providing evidence for developing sex-specific smoking cessation health promotion strategies and optimizing preventive measures.

Previous studies have demonstrated that alcohol consumption significantly increases the risk of precancerous SPs [11]; but there are limited evidence exists regarding the differential risk associations between specific alcoholic types and precancerous SPs. Our more detailed analysis of alcohol types revealed that individuals who predominantly consume beer have a significantly higher risk of developing precancerous SPs. This finding suggests that different types of alcohol may have varying effects on the risk of precancerous SPs. A research by Chen et al., demonstrated that long-term heavy consumption of beer and liquor is linked to an increased risk of colorectal cancer, whereas wine did not show this association [27]. This discrepancy may originate from mycotoxins produced by fungal contamination of brewing ingredients, such as barley and corn, along with polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) carcinogens produced during the roasting and smoking process of the raw materials and during brewing [28, 29]. Current public health guidelines typically treat alcohol as a single risk factor. However, our study suggests that the impact of different types of alcohol on the development of precancerous SPs may vary. Thus, further research into the relationship between specific types of alcohol consumption and the risk of precancerous SPs is warranted in the future.

To date, there has been no study that has definitively clarified the biological connection between water consumption and the development of precancerous SPs. However, our study is the first to demonstrate that drinking more than one glass of water per day is associated with a reduced risk of developing precancerous SPs. One study found that drinking more than four glasses of water per day decreased the risk of CRC [30]. Additionally, a Mendelian randomization analysis suggested a causal relationship between genetically predicted increased water consumption and a lower risk of CRC [31]. The underlying mechanism for this protective effect may involve improvements in gut microbiota composition. Significant differences have been observed in the gut flora of individuals with low versus high water consumption [32]. Adequate water consumption may reduce the colonization of oncogenic bacteria by diluting gut contents or altering pH levels, while simultaneously promoting the growth of beneficial bacteria. This shift in microbial balance could contribute to a decreased risk of CRC [33].

This study indicates that red meat consumption is linked to an increased risk of developing precancerous SPs, aligning with the carcinogenic effects reported in previous research [34]. For example, a large case-control study by Mosley et al. demonstrated that high consumption of red meat was strongly associated with an increased risk of SSLs [35]. The underlying pathogenic mechanism may involve heme iron-mediated lipid peroxidation. Red meat, particularly processed varieties, is rich in heme iron and fat, which can facilitate lipid peroxidation and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the gut during digestion. This process can lead to oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, creating an environment that may compromise the integrity of the colonic epithelial barrier and promote the development of serrated polyps [36, 37].

While white meat is often regarded as a healthier protein source compared to red meat, evidence supporting a direct protective effect against the development of precancerous SPs remains insufficient. In this study, white meat was categorized into poultry, seafood, and freshwater fish, reflecting the dietary structure characteristics of the East Asian population. We observed that individuals who frequently consumed freshwater fish had a significantly increased risk of developing precancerous SPs. This increased risk may be linked to environmental pollution-mediated carcinogenesis. Research has identified cadmium (Cd) accumulation in freshwater fish from the Pearl River Basin in Southern China. Furthermore, a case-control study conducted in Southern China revealed significantly higher median blood Cd concentrations in CRC patients compared to healthy controls. Multivariable logistics regression analysis confirmed a significant association between blood Cd levels and CRC risk [38, 39]. This suggests that although white meat is generally regarded as a healthy choice, freshwater fish consumption may be a potential risk factor for colorectal precancerous lesions in populations in specific regions due to the accumulation of environmental heavy metal contamination.

Regarding plant-based dietary factors, the present study found a negative trend between high-frequency consumption of soy products and the risk of precancerous SPs. This protective effect was more pronounced in women and individuals aged 40–59 years. The anticancer properties of soy products are largely attributed to the biological activity of soy isoflavones. These compounds have been shown to regulate inflammatory pathways, such as NF-κB, mitigate oxidative stress imbalances, and remodel the intestinal microbiota, all of which contribute to their protective effects [40–44]. A dose-effect analysis revealed that consuming 54 g of soy products daily could lower the risk of cancer by 11%, and this protective effect was more pronounced in the Chinese population [45]. A cross-sectional study in a Chinese population suggests that the positive association between total soy intake and colorectal polyp risk is primarily driven by the consumption of fried soy products, whereas boiled or fermented soy products showed no significant association [46].This suggests that opting for healthier cooking methods, like steaming or fermentation, rather than frying, can enhance the protective efficacy of soy products and potentially reduce disease risk.

In this study, we found that chili pepper consumption is significantly negatively associated with the risk of precancerous SPs. While previous clinical studies have reported no significant association between chili pepper consumption and CRC risk, those studies primarily focused on populations with confirmed CRC diagnoses [47–49]. This distinction between precancerous stages and diagnosed tumors suggests that cayenne pepper may exert a protective effect by modulating the early tumor microenvironment. Mechanistic investigations have demonstrated that capsaicin, the principal active component of chili peppers, can exert synergistic anti-tumor effects through multiple targets and pathways. Specifically, capsaicin reduces the release of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6 by inhibiting the TLR4/NF-κB inflammatory pathway, thereby alleviating the inflammatory response within the tumor microenvironment [50–53]. Furthermore, capsaicin, in conjunction with resveratrol, has been shown to produce radiopotentiating effects on colorectal tumor cells in vitro, influencing both the cell cycle and apoptosis [54]. However, there is currently insufficient evidence regarding the capacity of cayenne pepper to inhibit the development of precancerous SPs, highlighting the need for further studies to validate its potential protective effects.

This study demonstrates significant strengths and clinical relevance. Firstly, it is the first study to investigate the relationship between lifestyle habits and dietary preferences in a hospital-based southern Chinese cohort and the risk of developing precancerous SPs. Notably, we identified for the first time the potential influence of daily water consumption, freshwater fish consumption, and chili pepper on the risk of precancerous SPs. These findings offer valuable insights for formulating localized primary prevention strategies for CRC in specific regions. Secondly, the study encompassed participants across all age groups, providing data that support the optimization of early screening strategies. Current research reveals a trend of CRC onset at increasingly younger ages, with precancerous SPs in younger individuals exhibiting higher incidence rates and malignancy potential [55, 56]. This highlights the necessity of extending CRC screening to include individuals younger than 45 years of age. Furthermore, the study utilized a combination of stratified and subgroup analyses, facilitating a more precise evaluation of the risk differences among SPs across demographic subgroups. This risk stratification model is essential for enhancing the efficiency of screening programs.

The study also had the following limitations: First, as a hospital-based case-control study, both cases and controls originated from the same medical center, which may be subject to selection bias. Since the control group consisted of clinic attendees with negative colorectaloscopy, it was not fully representative of the general population, which may affect the extrapolation of the study results. And the study failed to effectively exclude participants with a history of colorectal polyps, which may have resulted in the effect estimates conflating the risk of initial polyp development with the risk of recurrence, thereby interfering with the assessment of the association between risk factors and the incidence of precancerous SPs. Second, information on exposure factors relied heavily on participants’ self-reports, which may be subjective and subject to recall bias. To minimize these biases, a structured questionnaire was administered before the examination to improve the accuracy of the information collected. In addition, the study design did not allow for direct determination of causal relationships between risk factors and the risk of developing precancerous SPs. Future prospective cohort studies with multicenter long-term follow-up are needed to confirm our findings by dynamically tracking risk factors and disease progression.

Conclusions

In summary, we explored the association of lifestyle and dietary preferences with the risk of developing precancerous SPs. To our knowledge, this is the first study to find a correlation between daily water consumption, beer, chili peppers, and freshwater fish and the potential risk of precancerous SPs, and to confirm that smoking and red meat are strongly associated with precancerous SPs. This suggests that primary prevention and early screening strategies can be further optimized based on lifestyle and dietary preferences to reduce the disease burden of CRC. However, this study was limited by its sample size, and it is recommended that future research include prospective cohort studies with multicenter involvement and long-term follow-up to facilitate a more comprehensive analysis.
