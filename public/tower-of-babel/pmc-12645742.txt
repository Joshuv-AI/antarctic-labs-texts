AI-ding peer feedback: a randomized study of self-generated vs. ai-assisted peer feedback
Authors: Zachary R Noel, Heewoo Lee, Christina H Sherrill, Ian B Hollis, Jacqueline E McLaughlin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeProviding effective peer feedback plays an important role in collaborative learning and is an essential professional skill for students to develop. This study investigates whether generative AI can improve the quality of peer feedback, and whether it influences students’ perceptions of the feedback received.MethodsAn experimental design was employed involving 129 third-year Doctor of Pharmacy (PharmD) students. Participants were randomized into two groups: self-generated (SG) feedback and AI-assisted (AI) feedback. The SG group provided feedback independently (i.e., the feedback was crafted on their own, without the use of an AI prompt) using the task, gap, action (TGAP) framework. The AI group utilized generative AI to create feedback based on a given prompt that aligned with the TGAP framework. The prompt was partially completed, requiring students to add behaviors, professional skills (e.g., communication, interpersonal skills), or knowledge related to each student they were evaluating. Feedback was coded and analyzed, and students’ perceptions were measured using the Feedback Perceptions Questionnaire.ResultsA total of 353 peer feedback comments were analyzed (162 self-generated, 191 AI-assisted). The AI-assisted group achieved significantly higher median scores across all TGAP criteria (Task: H(1) = 27.32, p < 0.001; Gap: H(1) = 89.32, p < 0.001; Action: H(1) = 86.86, p < 0.001). Specifically, 37.0% of students in the SG group compared to 61.3% of students in the AI group provided specific feedback on what their peers did well. For areas of improvement, 13.6% of students in the SG group provided specific areas to improve upon compared to 55.0% of students in the AI group. Only 22.8% of students in the SG provided feedback on how their peers could improve moving forward, compared to 72.8% in AI group. Student perceptions of feedback were positive, with only 3% of the AI group reporting negative feelings about the feedback they received compared to 12.7% in the SG group; this difference was not statistically significant (χ²(1) = 2.97, p = 0.085).ConclusionsThe study demonstrates how the strategic use of AI can be used to improve the quality of peer feedback. Future research should explore the long-term effects of AI-assisted feedback on students’ independent feedback skills and investigate behavioral changes resulting from improved feedback quality.Keywords: Peer evaluation, Peer feedback, Collaborative learning, Artificial intelligence

Introduction

Peer evaluation, also known as peer assessment or peer review, is a widely used strategy to enhance student learning and collaboration. Defined as an arrangement where individuals assess the quality and success of their peers’ learning outcomes [1], peer evaluation is integral to many collaborative learning approaches, such as team-based learning [2]. A key component of peer evaluation is peer feedback, which involves providing qualitative comments on peers’ performance contributions (e.g., skilled behaviors, contributions to team performance, communication, etc.). [1, 2]

Research has shown that well-structured peer feedback can significantly enhance student learning, communication, and teamwork [3–7]. This is further supported by the fact that feedback serves as a critical driver of the processes that contribute to self-regulated learning (SRL). [8, 9] Though various models of SRL exist, there are three common assumptions that they each share: SRL involves being behaviorally, cognitively, metacognitively, and motivationally involved with one’s own learning; SRL is cyclic and dynamic; and goal-setting triggers SRL [9]. Effective peer feedback provides the receiver an opportunity to self-reflect on their behaviors and contributions and, most importantly, help with goal-setting and behavior change in the future. Altogether, peer feedback can serve as a way of promoting SRL in the classroom.

Despite the merits of peer feedback, decades of research have demonstrated that humans are inherently poor feedback “givers” – largely because our instinct is to rely on human intuition, or judgment, rather than on behaviors [10]. Rater training has emerged as one of the most essential elements to maximize the usefulness of feedback [4]. Rater training involves coaching students on what effective feedback is and how to communicate it and then providing students feedback on their feedback. This is inherently time-consuming and difficult to implement with large class sizes [4].

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) provides a unique opportunity to reduce mental effort and time necessary to craft peer feedback comments while simultaneously enhancing the quality of peer feedback comments. The effectiveness of using generative AI for this – both in terms of how well students’ feedback conforms to best practices and how the feedback is perceived by the receiver – has not been studied. Thus, we designed a study to address two key research questions:

Research Question #1 (RQ1): Does strategic use of an AI prompt to craft qualitative comments improve the quality of peer feedback?

Methods

Context

This study used convenience sampling of all third-year Doctor of Pharmacy (PharmD) students enrolled in Pharmacotherapy II (PHCY 732). PHCY 732 is positioned in the Fall and is the second-to-last didactic semester that students complete before their experiential year. PHCY 732 uses a collaborative learning strategy that incorporates many elements of team-based learning (e.g., permanent teams of 4–6 students, readiness assurance process, and team application activities). PHCY 732 is designed to develop students’ capacity to research, analyze, and solve complex patient medication problems. In the course, students work in their teams for in- and out-of-class assignments. At the beginning of the semester, students create a team contract to ensure that all team members share similar expectations for group work and team performance. During the course, students complete two peer evaluations – one at the midpoint and one at the final – to provide each of their teammates feedback on their contributions to the team’s performance, professional behaviors (e.g., communication, being engaged and prepared for class, etc.), and skills.

Of note, several courses in the curriculum use a similar collaborative learning strategy, so students are familiar with the course structure, the teamwork requirements, and the importance of peer evaluations.

Research design

Quality of peer feedback comments (RQ1)

To determine the impact of generative AI on the quality of peer feedback, an experimental study design was used (Fig. 1). Student teams were randomized into either a self-generated (SG) feedback group or an AI-assisted (AI) feedback group. Students in the SG feedback group were provided with explicit instructions about how to complete peer feedback (Appendix A). In short, students were told to focus on behaviors, and instructions were adapted from the task, gap, action (TGAP) framework (Table 1) [11, 12]. The TGAP framework, based on Ericsson’s deliberate practice theory, was chosen based on evidence that it can be used to assess feedback quality in medical education [11–13].

Students in the AI feedback group received similar instructions as the SG feedback group; however, a key difference was that the instructions included a partially completed prompt that students were instructed to place into a generative AI software of their choosing (Appendix A). Students were told to complete the prompt and then modify the output however they felt was appropriate. Prior to using the prompt, it was tested and revised by the research team to maximize the quality of the output.

To reduce the risk of contamination from one study group to the other (e.g., students in the SG group using AI when they otherwise would not have), we deliberately withheld information about the different group instructions. In other words, students were not initially aware that two different sets of peer feedback instructions were being used. InteDashboard™, which is an all-in-one online team-based learning platform, was used for teams to complete their peer evaluations and review the peer feedback comments that they received.

Student perception of feedback received (RQ2)

Following completion of the peer evaluation, students were instructed to log in to InteDashboard and review the feedback they received (Fig. 1). After reviewing the feedback from their peers, they were then instructed to complete the Feedback Perceptions Questionnaire (FPQ) using Qualtrics [14, 15]. The FPQ is an 18-item questionnaire that measures five scales: usefulness of feedback, fairness of feedback, acceptance of feedback, willingness to improve, and affect (Appendix B). The FPQ was chosen for this study because it can be used as a state-specific questionnaire, meaning that it can be adapted and used for a specific task within a course.

The original FPQ was modified for this study. Instead of containing six different items to measure affect, we used a single item and asked students to select the descriptor that best represented their feelings toward the feedback they received. Items were aggregated to simplify and shorten the instrument in hopes of improving the response rate. Positive descriptors included satisfied, confident, and successful whereas negative descriptors included offended, angry, and frustrated. All other items were answered on a Likert scale ranging from the 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

This study was funded as part of a Center for Faculty Excellence Lenovo grant at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. The study was IRB approved. All students were asked to complete the survey as a required part of the course. Students were notified via email and through the learning management system to complete the survey. The survey contained a consent form, and only data from students who completed the consent form were included in the analysis.

Data collection and analysis

Completion of peer evaluations was a required element in the course and was completed at the midpoint and final evaluation. For purposes of this study, only peer feedback comments from the midpoint evaluations were included. Peer feedback comments were anonymized and de-identified, and coders were blinded to feedback groups. Each peer feedback comment underwent a deductive coding process against the three TGAP criteria. The scoring criteria ranged from 0 (no description provided) to 3 (specific description provided) as outlined in Table 1. The consensus building process involved two coders independently scoring a total of 150 peer feedback comments. Through an iterative process, the coders collaboratively identified discrepancies, clarified behavioral anchors, and refined the rubric after coding each set of 50 peer feedback comments. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion to ensure consistency in scoring for the remainder of the dataset. After coding 150 peer feedback comments, interrater agreement of > 80% was achieved. Given high agreement, one coder scored half of the remaining comments, and the other scored the other half.

We estimated that approximately 10% of students in the self-generated (SG) feedback group would provide specific descriptions for each of the TGAP criteria, corresponding to a score of 3. This projection was based on historical data in the course from peer evaluations. In contrast, the anticipated proportion for the AI-assisted (AI) feedback group was set at 20%. To detect this difference between groups, and with statistical parameters set at alpha = 0.05 and beta = 80%, we calculated that a sample size of around 350 peer feedback comments would be required.

In total, 760 peer- and self-feedback comments were submitted by 129 students in the course. Based on the power analysis, a random sample of 353 peer feedback comments was used. All peer evaluation data were stored and coded in REDCap at the University of North Carolina. REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies [2].

Completion of the FPQ was required as part of the course. Students were emailed with the link to complete the FPQ after the peer evaluation comments were released to feedback receivers. Though completion of the FPQ was a requirement of the course, students were asked to consent to have their survey data used, and only survey data from students who consented were included in the analysis. All data on the FPQ were collected using Qualtrics (Qualtrics, Provo, UT). SPSS (v29.0.1.0) was used to conduct data analysis.

Median scores and histograms were used to describe TGAP scores. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to analyze differences in TGAP scores, with level of significance set at p < 0.05. The Chi Square Test using an alpha of 0.05 and beta of 0.8 were used to compare differences in student responses for the affective scale of the FPQ. The reliability of the FPQ was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and demonstrated acceptable reliability (α = 0.78).

Results

Baseline characteristics of AI use

Peer evaluations were completed by all students in the course (n = 129) and 112 students (86.8%) completed the consent (SG = 55; AI = 57). In the AI feedback group, all students indicated that they used AI (Table 2). In the SG feedback group, four students (7.3%) reported using AI to formulate their feedback, independent of the instructions they received. ChatGPT was used by 51 students (87.8%) in the AI feedback group. The majority of students (n = 43; 75.5%) in the AI feedback group indicated that they used AI and the provided prompt to create an initial draft of the feedback and then modified the output. Ten students (17.5%) reported crafting their own peer feedback and then using AI to improve the quality of it. Six students (10.5%) indicated that they used the AI prompt and did not modify the output at all.

Results of RQ1 and RQ2

A total of 353 peer feedback comments were analyzed (162 from the SG group and 191 from the AI group). As shown in Table 3, the AI feedback group achieved higher median scores in all three TGAP criteria compared to the SG feedback group (Task; H(1) = 27.32, p < 0.001; Gap; H(1) = 89.32, p < 0.001; Action; H(1) = 86.86, p < 0.001). The score distribution (Fig. 2) shows that a significantly higher proportion of students in the AI feedback group provided specific feedback (i.e., score of 3) on their peers’ strengths (37.0% vs. 61.3%). Similarly, a greater percentage of students in the AI feedback group identified specific areas for improvement moving forward (13.6% vs. 55.0%). When it came to identifying gaps or areas that were not done well, only 22.8% of students in the SG feedback group provided feedback, compared to 72.8% of students in the AI feedback group. Despite the AI feedback group providing significantly more feedback on gaps and areas for improvement, only two students (3%) reported negative feelings towards the feedback received from their peers. In the SG group, 7 (12.7%) reported negative feelings toward the feedback they received (Fig. 3). This difference did not reach statistical significance (χ2(1) = 2.97, p = 0.085).

Overall, in the SG group, only 23 peer evaluation comments (14.2%) scored 2 or higher in all three TGAP criteria, and just 4 comments (2.5%) achieved a score of 3 or higher in all three TGAP criteria. In contrast, the AI group had 125 peer evaluation comments (65%) scoring 2 or higher in all three TGAP criteria, with 36 comments (18.8%) scoring 3 in each of the TGAP criteria.

Discussion

Effective feedback requires students to (1) craft and deliver appropriate messages, (2) employ evaluative judgments, and (3) consider socio-emotional factors [16]. However, most students receive minimal instruction, practice, and feedback on this process. In our study, we found that the use of AI significantly influenced the quality of peer feedback within a collaborative learning classroom. Students in the AI feedback group produced higher quality feedback comments across all three components of the TGAP framework. The AI group provided more detailed and specific feedback regarding peers’ strengths, areas needing improvement, and actionable suggestions for future performance. Despite utilizing an AI prompt, students were still required to supply necessary background information concerning specific behaviors, professional skills, and learning tasks.

Carefully crafting effective peer feedback can be time consuming and effortful. We theorize that the use of generative AI significantly reduced the mental effort necessary to craft and deliver messaging. In addition, the partially completed prompt effectively served as a completion task, altogether reducing the cognitive load and mental effort required for feedback givers [17]. The prompt we used included instructions on the tone of the message (i.e., “Make the tone of the evaluation positive and friendly…”) which may have helped to offset student concerns about socio-emotional factors (e.g., how students will perceive the feedback and the social implications of it). This is one plausible explanation for why so few students identified areas for improvement in the SG feedback group. Taken altogether, we theorize taht students in the AI feedback group were able to spend more time reflecting on specific professional behaviors for which to make evaluative judgments, and less time and effort crafting the message [17–20]. Interestingly, students in the AI peer feedback group received the feedback quite positively, despite receiving far more specific feedback on what was not done well. Only two students in the AI feedback group indicated negative feelings (e.g., frustration) with the feedback they received, compared to seven in the SG feedback group. These differences were not statistically significant; however, given the low number of students reporting negative feelings toward the feedback, the study was underpowered to detect a difference in this outcome.

One of the most significant enhancements in the peer feedback comments was the increase in students offering specific suggestions for future improvements. Peer feedback serves as an essential facilitator for goal setting and reflection, both of which are crucial to self-regulated learning and enhancing team performance [8, 9]. Furthermore, working effectively in teams and providing feedback to team members is a point of emphasis in health professions education programs [21].

The use of generative AI for peer feedback presents a scalable solution for large classes and diverse educational settings [22–24]. By automating parts of the feedback process, AI can significantly reduce the workload for instructors, making it feasible to implement higher-quality peer feedback even in courses with large enrollments. This scalability is particularly beneficial in health professions education, where class sizes can often exceed 100 students. Additionally, the practicality of AI tools lies in their ability to provide consistent and structured feedback, which can help maintain a high standard of peer evaluations across different courses and institutions [24].

This study raises important questions about how students learn when using AI to help formulate peer feedback. Although the quality of feedback that students receive is better, what are the implications for the feedback giver? How does the use of AI influence students’ understanding of, and ability to give, good feedback independently? These are important areas of future research.

While this study demonstrates significant improvements in the quality of peer feedback through the use of generative AI, several limitations should be considered. First, although the feedback comments in the AI feedback group were more specific and actionable, the study did not measure whether these improvements translated into actual behavior change among students. Future research should explore the impact of AI-assisted feedback on performance and professional development over time. Additionally, the study was underpowered to detect differences in students’ perceptions of feedback, particularly regarding trust in AI-generated comments—an area warranting further investigation.

Second, while AI may enhance the quality of peer feedback, it does not replace the need for teaching students effective feedback practices. All students in this study were concurrently enrolled in a professional leadership course that included training on effective feedback giving. Both groups also received similar instructions about what to focus on with their feedback based on the TGAP framework (Appendix A). This consistency reduces the likelihood that instructions alone account for the significant difference in feedback quality. Nonetheless, there were minor differences in wording, such as the inclusion of tone (“positive and friendly”) in the AI group that may have influenced the how receivers perceived the feedback.

Third, the AI prompt used in this study was carefully crafted and vetted by faculty. The results may have differed had students used AI tools without structured guidance or relied on different platforms (e.g., chatbots or agents). This underscores the importance of faculty oversight in AI integration.

Fourth, teams—not individual students—were randomized, which may have introduced intra-team correlation and violated assumptions of statistical independence in the Kruskal-Wallis test. However, given the magnitude of observed differences and the randomized design, we believe this had minimal impact on the findings.

Finally, although students were not told about the study interventions, crosstalk could have led to the introduction of bias. Only four students in the SG group used AI, which would suggest that the impact of student crosstalk on the results was minimal. Furthermore, to minimize the potential of crosstalk later in the semester, only the midpoint evaluation was used (as opposed to the midpoint and the final evaluation).

Conclusion

In our study, generative AI significantly enhanced the quality of peer feedback in a third year Doctor of Pharmacy course. Students who were provided with a carefully designed prompt based on the TGAP framework were more likely to provide specific and actionable comments to their peers compared to those who generated feedback on their own without the help of generative AI. Furthermore, AI-assisted peer feedback was received positively from students.

AI is a powerful tool, but one that requires careful human guidance to maximize its educational value. The use of a pre-vetted prompt in our study demonstrates one potential way in which AI can be integrated as an educational tool to support constructive peer feedback. As AI continues to be integrated into educational practice, future research should explore how best to balance AI integration with peer feedback, and how these tools can be leveraged to foster independent feedback skills and behavioral change.
