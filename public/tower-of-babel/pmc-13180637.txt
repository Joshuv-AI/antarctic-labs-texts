Insulinoma after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass successfully treated by endoscopic ultrasound-guided radiofrequency ablation
Authors: Constance Herrebout, Michiel Bronswijk, Toon Maes, Mathieu Lefere, Claire Bourgain, Veerle Ide
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-bariatric hypoglycemia (PBH) can significantly impact patient quality of life and is generally referred to as postprandial hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia or late dumping. In this report, we present the case of a 55-year-old man with a recent history of Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) surgery and multiple admissions owing to hypoglycemic coma postoperatively. Initially, the hypoglycemic episodes were interpreted as being caused by PBH. Due to symptom severity, a fasting test was performed, revealing hypoglycemia and inappropriate hyperinsulinemia, suggestive of an insulinoma. Further computed tomography evaluation revealed a lesion in the pancreatic neck, which could not be demonstrated on gallium-68 (68Ga)-labeled dodecanetetraacetic acid-tyrosine-3-octreotate (DOTATATE) positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT). Anatomopathological confirmation was pursued using endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided fine needle aspiration through EUS-directed gastrogastrostomy and showed presence of a grade 1 neuroendocrine tumor. We decided to perform EUS-guided radiofrequency ablation (RFA), which resulted in complete resolution of the hypoglycemic episodes. This case report offers an important contribution by demonstrating successful treatment of an insulinoma by RFA via an EUS-guided gastrogastrostomy approach after RYGB, which has not been previously well established. It also underlines the importance of considering alternative causes of recurrent hypoglycemia after bariatric surgery beyond PBH.

Introduction

Bariatric surgery (BS) has become increasingly popular based on its ability to induce a sustained weight loss, decrease overall mortality, and improve or even reverse comorbidities in obese patients. Post-BS hypoglycemic complications are well-known and can significantly impact quality of life [1]. Post-bariatric hypoglycemia (PBH) is usually referred to as postprandial hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia or late dumping, occurring typically 2 to 4 hours after ingestion of food. Although the pathophysiology of PBH is complex and multifactorial, a key mechanism involves an exaggerated insulin release triggered by the rapid delivery of carbohydrates to the small intestine. The hypoglycemia is accompanied by typical adrenergic and/or neuroglycopenic symptoms (sweating, tachycardia, hunger, fatigue/weakness, confusion, dizziness, blurred vision, irritability and in severe cases coma) [2]. Symptoms resolve quickly once normoglycemia is restored, a pattern commonly referred to as the Whipple triad.

In the diagnostic workup for patients with PBH, it is crucial to distinguish it from other causes of hypoglycemia, including the use of antidiabetic drugs such as insulin and insulin secretagogues, alcohol intake, malnutrition, liver and kidney failure, sepsis, hypoadrenalism, growth hormone deficiency, and insulin autoimmune hypoglycemia, as well as causes of endogenous hyperinsulinism such as nesidioblastosis or (pro)insulin-secreting tumors [3]. Our patient presented with an insulinoma following Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) surgery, which was successfully treated through endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided radiofrequency ablation (RFA) via a EUS-guided gastrogastrostomy approach. Although sporadic cases of insulinoma have been described following BS [Table 1], to our knowledge no other cases in the post-RYGB setting have been described employing endoscopic treatment modalities only.

Case presentation

We report the case of a 55-year-old man with a medical history of morbid obesity and obstructive sleep apnea syndrome who underwent RYGB surgery in February 2024. The patient's preoperative weight was 95 kg, corresponding to a body mass index (BMI) of 36 kg/m2. The immediate postoperative course was complicated by a stenosis of the enteroenteric anastomosis, resulting in secondary bowel obstruction approximately 10 days postoperatively. The patient subsequently underwent revision surgery to reconstruct the anastomosis, followed by an uneventful recovery. In the subsequent 2 months, the patient was admitted twice to the emergency department with episodes of severe hypoglycemic coma. On both occasions, prompt administration of intravenous glucose led to full neurological recovery. At that time, the patient had lost approximately 10 kg since surgery. Based on the clinical presentation and timing of symptoms, a diagnosis of late dumping syndrome was considered. The patient was advised on dietary modifications and started on subcutaneous octreotide at a dosage of 0.1 mg twice daily (off-label use). A continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) device was used to monitor his blood glucose levels.

Despite these interventions, he was readmitted approximately 1 month later following another episode of hypoglycemic coma. He was found unconscious in bed, with a venous blood glucose level of 35 mg/dL (1.94 mmol/L) (reference range: 74-106 mg/dL [4.108-5.884 mmol/L]). Intravenous glucose administration again resulted in rapid clinical improvement.

Diagnostic assessment

The patient was admitted to the endocrinology ward for further diagnostic evaluation. A standardized 72-hour fasting test was initiated [Table 2]. Hypoglycemia was observed after 33 hours of fasting, with a measured blood glucose concentration of 40 mg/dL [2.22 mmol/L], prompting discontinuation of the fasting test. Notably, the patient remained asymptomatic during the hypoglycemic episode. Inappropriate hyperinsulinemia was confirmed by inadequate suppression of serum insulin, C-peptide, and pro-insulin concentrations in the setting of hypoglycemia [Table 2]. The patient was subsequently initiated on diazoxide (100 mg, 3 times daily) in combination with octreotide (0.1 mg, 3 times daily), under which glycemic stability was achieved. Comprehensive biochemical evaluation showed no evidence of liver disease, ingestion of sulfonylurea (based on negative urinary screening), or insulin autoimmune syndrome. Serum growth hormone, insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), cortisol levels and an adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) stimulation test were within normal reference ranges. Radiological assessment via contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed a 16-mm contrast-enhanced nodule located in the pancreatic neck (Fig. 1A), exhibiting slight exophytic growth consistent with characteristics of a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor (pNET). However, subsequent gallium-68 (68Ga)-labeled dodecanetetraacetic acid-tyrosine-3-octreotate (DOTATATE) positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) demonstrated no increased tracer uptake in the region of the CT-identified lesion or elsewhere in the body. Additional imaging using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed the presence of an 11-mm thick-walled cystic nodule in the pancreatic neck, likely corresponding to a small pNET (Fig. 1B and 1C). Following multidisciplinary discussion and patient-informed decision, the patient was referred to the gastroenterology department for further diagnostic workup, aimed at performing EUS-guided fine needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) of the lesion using a stentfix-assisted single-session EUS-guided gastrogastrostomy with a lumen-apposing metal stent (LAMS). After insertion of the EUS-scope into the gastric pouch and visualization of the gastric remnant, the “sand-dollar sign” was punctured with an FNA needle to allow for saline instillation. Following adequate distention of the excluded stomach, a successful EUS-guided anastomosis was created using a dumbbell-shaped LAMS (Hot Axios stent, Boston Scientific). Stent fixation was achieved using the Ovesco Stentfix System, after which the gastric remnant could safely be accessed through the LAMS. The partially cystic pancreatic lesion (measuring 13.6 × 11.7 mm) was identified and targeted for FNA with the same 19G-needle.

Treatment

Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of a grade 1 pNET (Ki67 3%), staged as cT1N0M0, with immunohistochemistry confirming the endocrine nature of the tumor (Figs. 2 and 3). The patient was readmitted for EUS-RFA after multidisciplinary presentation in our institutional tumor board, where a preference was given for endoscopic treatment given the complicated postoperative course following RYGB. In a single session, protective through-the-LAMS main pancreatic duct stenting was performed by EUS-directed transgastric endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) (EDGE procedure), given the close proximity of the pancreatic duct and subsequent risk of ductal injury. After securing adequate pancreatic duct drainage, EUS-RFA was performed through the previously placed LAMS. Asymptomatic hyperamylasemia occurred the day after, without any postinterventional complications. Octreotide and diazoxide were successfully discontinued after a few days.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient received outpatient follow-up from both the gastroenterology and endocrinology departments. At the 2-month follow-up assessment after the EUS-RFA, glycemic profiles remained stable without the need for medication. No episodes of hypoglycemia were observed, even during physical activity. MRI revealed a residual cystic lesion measuring approximately 4 mm, that could not be distinguished on follow-up EUS. The pancreatic duct stent and LAMS were subsequently extracted endoscopically, followed by closure of the gastrogastrostomy using a 12/6t over-the-scope clip (OTSC; Ovesco).

At the 6-month follow-up visit, the patient had experienced significant weight gain (from 68.5 to 72.5 kg), likely due to increased nutritional intake facilitated by the gastrogastrostomy. EUS demonstrated the presence of a gastrogastric fistula, consistent with the previously performed EDGE procedure. MRI at that time revealed a mildly increased arterial hyperenhancement at the ablation site, measuring approximately 4 mm (Fig. 4). In the absence of clinical signs of recurrence, reflected by the lack of hypoglycemic episodes, no additional therapeutic intervention was deemed necessary. The gastrogastric fistula was closed using argon plasma coagulation (APC), followed by OTSC placement using a 12/6t Ovesco clip.

Discussion

We present a novel case of insulinoma following RYGB surgery, which was successfully treated by EUS-RFA through an EDGE procedure. Combinations of the search terms “insulinoma” or “pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor” and “bariatric surgery” or “Roux-en-Y gastric bypass” or “sleeve gastrectomy” in the PubMed database yielded a total of 15 case reports describing insulinomas or pNET that were diagnosed following BS [Table 1]. We included only case reports involving patients who had undergone BS, excluding those in which the patient had upper gastrointestinal surgery for other reasons, such as peptic ulcer disease or oncologic indications. We also excluded pNET that were an incidental finding on imaging and thus asymptomatic (1 case).

This case highlights 2 important aspects. First, it underscores the diagnostic challenge associated with hypoglycemia following BS.

In nearly all reviewed cases, patients were initially misdiagnosed with PBH and managed conservatively through dietary modifications and pharmacologic therapy. A review of the 15 case reports showed that patients exhibited similar symptoms and clinical features, predominantly in the fasting state. The pattern of hypoglycemia in PBH and insulinoma is indeed different. PBH typically occurs 2 to 4 hours after eating with an onset relatively late after surgery (1 to 3 years postoperatively) [3, 9]. In contrast, our patient experienced a relatively rapid onset of hypoglycemic episodes after surgery, mainly in the fasting state.

Second, in all reported cases, insulinomas were managed surgically, typically via enucleation or partial pancreatic resection, performed either via laparotomy or laparoscopy. One patient underwent a pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure) due to tumor localization in the pancreatic head adjacent to the main pancreatic duct, rendering the lesion unsuitable for enucleation [10]. Poku et al described a patient with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) syndrome who developed metastatic insulinoma after sleeve gastrectomy. This patient underwent partial hepatectomy, distal pancreatectomy, splenectomy, and duodenal mass resection [11]. Surgical resection historically has been the first-line treatment for insulinoma and this remains the case for recurrent lesions or lesions >20 mm. However, given the significant risk of postoperative morbidity and mortality, an “endoscopy-first approach” is gaining popularity in many referral centers throughout the world for lesions <20 mm, fueled by the excellent efficacy (90% curation) and safety of EUS-RFA. Randomized confirmation of these outcomes is currently underway. Similar to surgery, recurrence of insulinoma after EUS-RFA occurs in approximately 7% according to the systematic review by Mehrabi et al. Therefore, regular postoperative follow-up is necessary (3, 6, and 12 months and then annually) [19]. Several case series of EUS-RFA for insulinomas have already been published over the last 10 years, all in non-bariatric patients [20]. These show that EUS-RFA is an effective and safe treatment option for pNET. However, reported follow-up periods are relatively short, ranging from a few months to approximately 3.5 years in one reported case by Yao et al [21]. Studies with a larger series of patients with longer follow-up periods are therefore needed. Our patient had already experienced a complicated postoperative course after his RYGB surgery (obstruction). He was therefore deemed a suboptimal candidate for surgical resection and was referred for EUS-RFA through an EDGE procedure. This approach proved successful, with resolution of hypoglycemic episodes and no evidence of recurrence up to date.

Learning points

Hypoglycemia after BS is frequently caused by PBH. However, it is crucial to consider alternative causes and rule them out when indicated based on history and physical examination in order to avoid diagnostic and therapeutic delay.
