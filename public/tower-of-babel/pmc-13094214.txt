Angiopep-2-decorated bacterial outer membrane vesicles penetrate the blood-brain barrier for glioblastoma chemo-immunotherapy
Authors: Pir Tariq Shah, Zhangrong Lou, Xiaojing Zhu, Wenrui Jin, Chen Chen, Tao Hu, Peng Guo, Xuanzhu Zhao, Fan Li, Yin Xiaoliang, Zhenyong Wu, Chengjun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGlioblastoma (GBM) therapy remains one of the most formidable challenges in oncology because of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and drug resistance. To overcome these challenges, we designed engineered angiopep-2-decorated bacterial outer membrane vesicles for targeted drug delivery across the BBB. Using a novel autotransporter-based (AIDA-I) genetic engineering approach, we developed a novel platform via straightforward genetic modification of E. coli with our designed pAIDA1-ANG plasmid to stably display Angiopep-2 on OMVs (OMV-ANG) for targeted BBB penetration via LDL receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) receptor-mediated transcytosis. Our in vitro BBB model and real-time in vivo imaging confirmed the BBB penetration and transcytosis of OMV-ANG and doxorubicin-loaded OMV-ANG, with accelerated brain accumulation within 2–4 h post-injection and sustained retention for 6 h. In orthotopic GBM models, systemic DOX-OMV-ANG administration extended survival, induced potent tumor suppression via DOX-induced apoptosis and OMV-ANG-mediated immunomodulation, and triggered sustained IFN-γ elevation with macrophage and CD8 + T-cell recruitment. This engineered OMV-ANG platform shows promise in overcoming critical therapeutic barriers in glioblastoma and warrants further investigation as a versatile delivery system for diverse CNS therapeutics.KEYWORDS: Glioblastoma, blood-brain barrier, outer membrane vesicles, Angiopep-2, drug carriers, receptor-mediated transcytosis

Introduction

Brain tumors represent a devastating category of primary and metastatic neoplasms affecting the central nervous system (CNS). Their significant mortality, morbidity, and recurrence rates stem largely from their intricate location within the brain and characteristically invasive growth patterns (worldlifeexpectancy.com 2017; Siegel et al. 2019; Sung et al. 2021). Glioblastoma (GBM), a Grade IV glioma, stands as the most prevalent and aggressive primary brain malignancy in adults. Despite intensive standard-of-care interventions, including surgical resection followed by adjuvant chemoradiation, patient prognosis remains bleak, with a median survival typically ranging from 15 to 16 months (Chinot et al. 2014; Gilbert et al. 2014). Pediatric brain tumors also impose a substantial burden, constituting approximately 26% of childhood cancers and ranking as the second leading cause of solid-tumor-related death in children (Siegel et al. 2019). Furthermore, brain metastases, arising from primary cancers such as those of the breast, lung, colon, kidney, and melanoma, occur in 9%–17% of adult cancer patients (Eichler et al. 2011; Nayak et al. 2012). Collectively, these malignancies underscore an urgent need for more effective and novel therapeutic strategies.

Chemotherapy remains a leading approach to glioma clinical management. However, its efficacy is greatly limited by two major biological hurdles, e.g. the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and the brain-tumor barrier (BTB). These highly selective barriers restrict the passage of most therapeutic agents into brain tumor tissue (Qiao et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2021). Compounding this challenge, glioma cells frequently develop multidrug resistance (MDR), often mediated by the upregulation of efflux pumps, such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp), which actively pumps out chemotherapeutic drugs even after successful intracellular delivery (Jin et al. 2012). Consequently, treatment failure is common.

To overcome these barriers, considerable research has focused on developing multi-functional nanomedicines featuring sophisticated surface modifications (Khan et al. 2021; Ni et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2022). These nanocarriers aim to enhance transport across the BBB/BTB, improve deep tumor penetration, and potentially reverse MDR mechanisms (Ruan et al. 2018, 2021; Wang et al. 2022). Recent advances in integrated nano-oncology, including the exploration of innovative material platforms like copper-chalcogenide nanoparticles for brain diseases, highlight dynamic progress in this field (Jiang et al. 2024; Xie et al. 2025). Despite advances in nanoparticle design, achieving high drug loading capacities, versatile functionalization, and smart drug release, their therapeutic impact against gliomas remains largely unsatisfactory (Lin et al. 2016; Feng et al. 2019). A key limitation is the diminished enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect within the tightly structured brain parenchyma, which hinders nanoparticle accumulation in glioma tissue compared to peripheral tumors (Matsumura 2011; Wu et al. 2020). Furthermore, while active targeting strategies designed to traverse the BBB/BTB and specifically bind tumor cells hold promise, their in vivo performance is often compromised by the formation of a protein corona on the nanoparticle surface, which can mask targeting ligands and alter biodistribution (Wang et al. 2021; Farshbaf et al. 2022).

Concurrently, cancer immunotherapy, including approaches like immune checkpoint inhibitors and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, has emerged as a transformative paradigm for treating various malignancies (Sadelain 2017; Miliotou and Papadopoulou 2018; Wilky 2019; Sharma et al. 2021). Nanoparticles possess inherent immunomodulatory potential due to their ability to traffic through the vasculature, interact with immune cells, and deliver immunostimulatory cargo (Bachmann and Jennings 2010; Lizotte et al. 2016). Bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), naturally released nano-sized lipid bilayers derived from Gram-negative bacteria, represent a unique class of nanoparticles rich in pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) (Furuyama and Sircili 2021; Gilmore et al. 2021). These acellular bacterial OMVs are one of the cutting-edge immunostimulatory agents recognized by many scientists as candidate vaccines and delivery vehicles (Kim et al. 2015; Schwechheimer and Kuehn 2015). Recent advances have further demonstrated the potential of OMVs in cancer immunotherapy, including hybrid vesicle platforms for enhanced immune activation (Ijaz et al. 2024, 2024; Yu et al. 2025; Zhao et al. 2025), including NIR‑II fluorescence‑labeled hybrid vesicles for imaging‑guided therapy (Yu et al. 2025), comprehensive reviews on bacterial vesicle‑based drug delivery (Ijaz et al. 2024) and bacteria‑nanomaterial hybrids for theranostics (Ijaz et al. 2024), as well as hybrid OMV/tumor‑cell‑membrane platforms for chemo‑immunotherapy of liver cancer (Zhao et al. 2025). Critically, OMVs offer significant safety advantages over live or attenuated bacteria, as they are acellular; they eliminate risks associated with bacterial replication while retaining potent immunogenicity at low doses. This safety profile is validated by the clinical use of an OMV-based meningococcal group B vaccine in children under the trade name Bexsero (Nøkleby et al. 2007; Acevedo et al. 2014). Recently, the application of OMVs has been expanded to treat complex diseases like sepsis through immunomodulation (Du et al. 2025).

Building upon this foundation, we hypothesized that engineered OMVs could serve as a multi-functional platform for targeted glioma therapy. While OMVs and Angiopep-2 have been explored separately, a genetically engineered platform integrating them for glioblastoma chemo-immunotherapy remains less common. To fill this gap, we aimed to develop bacterial-derived OMVs decorated with the BBB-targeting peptide Angiopep-2 (ANG) and loaded with the chemotherapeutic agent (doxorubicin: DOX). Angiopep-2 facilitates receptor-mediated transcytosis across the BBB via interaction with low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1), which is highly expressed on brain endothelial cells (Demeule et al. 2008; di Polidoro et al. 2022). We engineered E. coli DH5α using a specialized plasmid (pAIDA1-ANG) to express angiopep-2 on the bacterial surface, thereby ensuring its display on subsequently harvested OMVs (OMV-ANG). We anticipated that OMV-ANG would efficiently transport DOX across the BBB, target tumor cells, and simultaneously activate the host immune system, leveraging the intrinsic adjuvant properties of OMVs to stimulate anti-tumor immunity and potentially counteract the immunosuppressive glioma microenvironment.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

(Shanghai, China). DMEM (Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium, Catalog No. C11995500BT), MEM (Minimum Essential Medium, Catalog No. C11995500BT), and FBS (Fetal Bovine Serum) were purchased from Gibco Life Technologies, Inc. (Grand Island, NY, USA). PFA (Paraformaldehyde, Product Code: BL539A) was purchased from Biosharp (Hefei, China). DAPI (4ʹ,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, Product Code: C0065), trypsin-EDTA solution (Product Code: T1300), and BCA Protein Assay kit (Product Code: PC0020) were purchased from Solarbio Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). LB (Luria-Bertani) broth or agar media were freshly prepared using Tryptone (Product Code: BS212, Biosharp, China), Yeast extract (Product Code: BS211, Biosharp, China), Sodium chloride (NaCl: Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd.), and Agar (Product Code: BS195, Biosharp, China). Chloramphenicol (Product Code: 56-75-7) was purchased from Thermo Scientific, the c-Myc-tagged antibody (Catalog No. 100828-1-AP) from Proteintech (Wuhan, China), and the pAIDA1 plasmid (Plasmid No. 79180) from Addgene (Beijing Zhongyuan, Ltd., Beijing, China). Mouse IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ primers were acquired from Qinke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Animals

Female C57BL/6 mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were acquired from Jinan Pengyue Laboratory Animal Breeding Co. (Jinan, China) and maintained at the Shandong Laboratory of Yantai Drug Discovery, Bohai Rim Advanced Research Institute for Drug Discovery (Yantai, China) for use in this study. The mice were housed in a pathogen-free room with a temperature of 22–25 °C, 50%–60% relative humidity, and a diet under a 12 h dark/12h light cycle. The animals were allowed to acclimatize for at least a week before the experiments began. Mice were anaesthetized with isoflurane (2%–3% in oxygen) for each experimental procedure.

For all surgical and imaging procedures, mice were anesthetized using isoflurane (induction: 3%–4% in 100% oxygen at 1 L/min flow rate; maintenance: 1.5%–2% via a precision vaporizer and nose cone). Depth of anesthesia was monitored by the absence of pedal reflex and regular respiration. At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep isoflurane anesthesia (5% for > 2 min), followed by exsanguination. Death was confirmed by the absence of respiration, heartbeat, and corneal reflex. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (Underwood and Anthony 2020). All in vivo experiments, including tumor model establishment, biodistribution studies, therapeutic efficacy, and safety assessments, were conducted from June 2025 to September 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Dalian University of Technology (Approval No. DUTFM240306-02) and performed in compliance with the Shanghai Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee facility license (License No. SYXK-2020-0042).

Plasmid construction, bacterial transfection, OMV-ANG isolation, and purification

For surface expression of Angiopep-2, the pAIDA1 plasmid (Plasmid No. 79180 Addgene) was modified by inserting the Angiopept-2 sequence (ACCTTCTTTTACGGCGGAAGCAGGGGCAAGAGAAACAACTTCAAGACAGAGGAGTAC) between the KpnI and SacI sites and removing the His6-tag to expose the angiopep-2 on the outer surface (pAIDA1-ANG). The surface expression vector pAIDA1-ANG is based on the AIDA-I autotransporter. Expression is under the control of the lacUV5 promoter. The detection tag Myc with adjacent protease cleavage recognition sites (for 3C and TEV) flanks the Angiopep-2 protein (Supplementary Figure 1). The E coli DH5α was transfected with plasmid pAIDA1-ANG to express the Angiopep-2 on its outer surface and consequently on the outer surface of OMVs, e.g. OMV-ANG. Next, the OMV-ANG was isolated according to the protocol described previously for preparing OMVs derived from E. coli, with some modifications (Kuerban et al. 2020; Mi et al. 2023). Briefly, the bacteria were cultured in LB broth and incubated in a shaking incubator at 200 rpm at 37 °C overnight. The cultured bacteria were pelleted twice at 5000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm pore-size bottle top filter using a vacuum pump. The filtered supernatant was then concentrated using 100 kDa ultrafiltration tubes (BIOFIL, Guangzhou Jet Bio-Filtration Co., Ltd) and washed twice with PBS to obtain crude OMV-ANG. The purified OMV-ANG was obtained by ultracentrifugation, where it was subjected to ultracentrifugation at 150,000 × g for 3 h at 4 °C, resuspended in PBS, and stored at −80 °C until use. The yield of OMV-ANG was typically in the range of 500–800 μg of total protein per liter of bacterial culture, as determined by BCA protein assay.

OMV-ANG/DOX-OMV-ANG preparation, characterization, and drug release profile

An appropriate mass ratio of OMV-ANG and drug (DOX) was gently mixed in PBS and incubated under gentle agitation for 4–6 h at 37 °C to facilitate drug loading. Following incubation, the unloaded DOX was removed using the ultrafiltration tube with a 100 kDa molecular weight cutoff membrane (5000 × g, 10 min). The resulting DOX-loaded OMV-ANG (DOX-OMV-ANG) was subsequently washed three times with PBS to eliminate residual free drug. The concentration of DOX entrapped within OMV-ANG was quantified using the fluorescent microplate reader (Ex/Em 480/590 nm) after OMV-ANG particles were cracked using a methanol emulsion method, involving the addition of a 10-fold volume excess of methanol, 30 minutes of ultrasonication, and subsequent centrifugation (10,000 × g, 10 min). Drug loading efficiency (LE) and encapsulation efficiency (EE) were calculated according to the equations:

EE (%) = (The mass of Drug encapsulated in OMV-ANG)/(Total mass of Drug used to prepare the OMV-ANG) × 100

EE (%) = (The mass of Drug encapsulated in OMV-ANG)/(Total mass of Drug used to prepare the OMV-ANG) × 100

LE (%) = (The mass of Drug encapsulated in OMV-ANG)/(Total mass of OMV-ANG) × 100

LE (%) = (The mass of Drug encapsulated in OMV-ANG)/(Total mass of OMV-ANG) × 100

The optimized drug:carrier mass ratio for loading was 1:1 (DOX:OMV-ANG). Across three independent preparations, this ratio consistently yielded encapsulation efficiency of 72.3% ± 3.2% and loading efficiency of 71.8% ± 2.9%, demonstrating excellent batch-to-batch reproducibility.

The OMV, OMV-ANG, or DOX-OMV-ANG particle size, zeta potential, and polydispersity index (PDI) were determined using the Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS), while the morphology was assessed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) using the standard protocols. The release kinetics of DOX from OMV-ANG were evaluated under simulated physiological conditions (PBS, pH 7.4). DOX-OMV-ANG suspensions were loaded into dialysis bags (MWCO: 3500 Da) and immersed in release medium under sink conditions. At predetermined time intervals, 1 mL aliquots of the external medium were withdrawn and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh pre-warmed PBS. The concentration of the released drug in the collected aliquots was quantified using a fluorescent microplate reader at an excitation/emission wavelength of 480/590 nm to determine the cumulative release profile.

In vitro uptake of DOX-OMV-ANG by tumor cells

To detect cellular uptake, GL-261 cells were seeded in 12-well plates (4 × 104 cells/well) with confocal dishes in each well and cultured for 24 h. Then, the cells were washed and incubated with DOX, DOX-OMV, or DOX-OMV-ANG for 2–4 h. The cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. The nuclei were stained with DAPI for 10 min, followed by visualization with a confocal microscopy image system (FV1000, Olympus, USA), which allowed for the observation of fluorescence from DAPI and the intrinsic fluorescence of DOX (Ex/Em 480/590 nm).

In vitro tumor targeting by OMV-ANG

To evaluate the tumor targeting by OMV-ANG, the GL-261, and U87 MG cells (5000 cells/well) were incubated in DMEM and MEM, respectively, in 96-well plates overnight. Then, the cells were incubated with PBS, OMV-ANG, or DOX-OMV-ANG for 24 h, respectively. Then, the cells were treated with 100 mL CCK8 solution (0.5 mg/mL) for 1-2 h. The cells were observed under the ZEISS Axio Widefield Microscope (Germany), and the absorbance (450 nm) was measured using a SpectraMax iD3 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader.

In vitro blood-brain barrier penetration assay

The in vitro/artificial BBB model was developed as reported previously (Aday et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2024). Briefly, 2 × 104 brain microvascular endothelial cells (bEnd.3) were seeded in each well of the upper chamber of the transwell plate. Following the cell confluence and barrier maturation, confirmed by transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurements exceeding 200 Ω·cm², test compounds were introduced. PBS control, DOX-OMV, and DOX-OMV-ANG (10µg/ml each) were administered to the apical compartment and incubated for 2 or 4 h, respectively. Following incubation, fluorescence intensity within both apical and basolateral chambers was quantitatively assessed using the SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader (USA) at Ex/Em wavelengths of 785/825 nm. The transportation ratio, reflecting BBB penetration, was calculated as the ratio of basolateral to apical fluorescence intensity post-incubation.

In vivo blood-brain barrier penetration assay

Following the preliminary confirmation of penetration efficacy in the in vitro BBB model, the healthy female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks) were used to further validate the BBB penetration capacity. The indocyanine green (ICG) was used to label the OMV/OMV-ANG, and mice received intravenous injections of PBS (vehicle control), free ICG, OMV-ICG, OMV-ANG-ICG, or DOX-OMV-ANG-ICG (30µg/ml each combination). At designated intervals, the mice were anaesthetized, and live mice as well as organs (e.g. brain, heart, liver, lungs, spleen, and kidneys) were harvested for ex vivo imaging using a Newton 7.0 FT-500 fluorescence imaging system (IVIS, Vilber Lourmat, France) and H&E (Hematoxylin and Eosin) staining. Blood serum was collected to check the cytokine profiling of IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ via RT-qPCR.

Development of orthotopic GBM mice model

The orthotopic glioblastoma (GMB) model was generated in 6- to 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice through stereotactic implantation of GL261-Luc cells. Briefly, the GL-261-Luc cells were enzymatically detached, washed, and resuspended in sterile PBS. Mice were anaesthetized with isoflurane (2%–3% in oxygen) and secured in a stereotaxic frame. Following a midline scalp incision to expose the skull, a burr hole was drilled at the predetermined coordinates relative to bregma, e.g. +1.0 mm anteroposterior (AP), +2.0 mm mediolateral (ML). A total of 5 × 10⁴ cells in 2 µl PBS were injected into the right striatum at a depth of −3.0 mm dorsoventral (DV) using a Hamilton micro-syringe at a rate of 0.5 µl/min, with the needle left in place for an additional 2 min post-injection to minimize reflux. Tumor engraftment was verified non-invasively for 7 days post-implantation via bioluminescence imaging. Mice received an intraperitoneal injection of D-luciferin potassium salt (200 µl, 15 mg/ml in PBS) and were imaged under sustained isoflurane anesthesia 10 min post-injection using the Newton 7.0 FT-500 system (IVIS, Vilber Lourmat, France). The bioluminescent signal localized to the right hemisphere confirmed successful tumor establishment.

In vivo tumor targeting by OMV-ANG

To evaluate the tumor-targeting capability of Angiopep-2-functionalized OMVs, OMV-ANG, and DOX-OMV-ANG were fluorescently labeled with indocyanine green (ICG). Orthotopic GBM models were established in mice as mentioned above. After 10 days, a strong spontaneous luminescent signal was observed from the tumor cells using the Newton 7.0 FT-500 system (IVIS, Vilber Lourmat, France), confirming the successful establishment of the model. At this point, these tumor-bearing mice were randomized into four treatment cohorts (n = 5/group), e.g. PBS control, free ICG, ICG-labeled OMV-ANG, and ICG-labeled DOX-OMV-ANG. All formulations were administered via tail vein injection on days 1, 4, and 7 (10 µg/dose) post-randomization. The dose of OMV-ANG (10 µg/dose, three doses) was selected based on the standard doxorubicin dose of 2 mg/kg in mice (approximately 36–40 µg per 18–20 g mouse) and the drug loading efficiency of the platform, ensuring a comparable total chemotherapeutic dose while allowing assessment of repeated administration. Fluorescence intensity at the tumor site was longitudinally monitored at predetermined intervals using the IVIS imaging system. At the end of the experiment, mice were anaesthetized with isoflurane, followed by brain extraction for H&E, Ki67, and TUNEL histological analysis.

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from blood serum using the Trizol method (RNAiso Plus, Takara, Japan). The cDNA was acquired by reverse transcription of 1 μg of total RNA using the HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The RT-qPCR was performed using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. All experiments were performed in triplicate. The relative expression levels of transcripts were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method (Schmittgen and Livak 2008) by normalizing to GAPDH transcripts.

Histopathological analysis via H&E staining

Following euthanasia, the collected major organs, including brain, liver, lungs, spleen, kidneys, and heart, were immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sliced. The sections were stained with H&E (Hematoxylin and Eosin), and histopathological assessment was conducted under bright-field microscopy (Nikon Eclipse, Japan) at 100× to 400× magnification. Tissue architecture, cellular morphology, and pathological alterations were evaluated by two independent pathologists blinded to treatment groups.

TUNEL assay

Brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by dehydration through a graded ethanol series and paraffin embedding. Sections were rehydrated, underwent proteinase K digestion, followed by incubation with TUNEL reaction mixture for 60 min at 37 °C in a humidified chamber, using the TUNEL assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min at room temperature, and slides were mounted with anti-fade medium. Whole-slide digital imaging was performed using a netScope Viewer (Germany) slide scanner.

Ki67 immunohistochemistry

Tumor-bearing brains were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 5-μm thickness. After deparaffinization, antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Sections were blocked with 5% normal goat serum, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit monoclonal anti-Ki67 antibody (Abcam, ab16667; 1:200 dilution). Detection utilized a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody and 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Ki67-positive nuclei were quantified in three representative fields per section (40× magnification) using netScope Viewer and ImageJ (NIH). The immunopositive area (%) was calculated as the ratio of Ki67⁺ nuclei to total nuclei in viable tumor regions, excluding necrosis.

Bioinformatics validation of immuno-chemotherapeutic mechanisms

The bioinformatics analysis was conducted to validate the therapeutic mechanisms of OMV-ANG in glioblastoma (GBM). To validate the role of IFN-γ in glioblastoma survival, we performed an analysis of the Cancer Genome Atlas Glioblastoma (TCGA-GBM) cohort. Survival and tumor grade analysis for IFN-γ expression were performed using data from the TCGA-GBM project. The analysis was conducted in R (version 4.4.1) with the following packages: survival (v3.5-7) for survival modeling, survminer (v0.4.9) for Kaplan-Meier visualization, ggplot2 (v3.4.4) for graphical representations, and dplyr (v1.1.3) for data manipulation. Code reproducibility was ensured through the use of fixed random seeds (set.seed(123)).

The tumor microenvironment correlations were assessed using the TIMER2.0 (http://timer.cistrome.org/), with immune cell associations, e.g. CD8+ T cells and macrophages, quantified via Pearson correlation (R-values) and statistical significance (p-values) for IFN-γ expression. The drug- sensitivity predictions were derived from GSCALite (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/web/GSCALite/) using TCGA GBM data, in which negative correlation coefficients indicated enhanced doxorubicin susceptibility.

Statistical analyses

All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, statistical significance was determined using unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests, while multiple group comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA when data met the assumption of normality; otherwise, non-parametric tests were applied. Survival data were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank (Mantel-Cox) testing. RT-qPCR results were analyzed via the 2−ΔΔCT method with GAPDH normalization. A threshold of p < 0.05 defined statistical significance for all the analyses.

Results

Preparation and characterization of engineered OMVANG and OMVANG-DOX

We performed a comprehensive physicochemical characterization to confirm the successful functionalization and drug loading of the engineered OMVs. We transfected the E. coli DH5α strain with our designed plasmid, pAIDA1-ANG (Supplementary Figure 1), to express angiopep-2 on the outer surface of the bacterium. Initial validation using selective medium, antibiotic resistance, and a Myc-tag (Figure 1H, Supplementary Figure 2) confirmed the successful insertion and expression of angiopep-2 on the outer surface of the bacterium/OMVs. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that unmodified OMVs, angiopep-2-decorated OMVs (OMV-ANG), and doxorubicin-loaded OMV-ANG (DOX-OMV-ANG) all exhibited spherical, membrane-enclosed nanostructures with uniform morphology (Figure 1A–1G). The surface modification with angiopep-2 did not alter the intrinsic vesicular architecture, though OMV-ANG displayed slight surface roughness, likely attributable to peptide display. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis indicated that OMV-ANG (Figure 1E) maintained a monodisperse size distribution comparable to wild OMVs (Figure 1D), with hydrodynamic diameters averaging approximately 100–105 nm. However, the TEM results showed that the average diameters of OMV, OMV-ANG, and DOX-OMV-ANG were approximately 21.4, 22.9, and 28 nm, respectively (Figure 1E). The observed discrepancy between the larger hydrodynamic diameter (DLS) and the smaller core size (TEM) is a well-known phenomenon for nanoparticle characterization (Wilson and Prud'homme 2021). It is primarily attributed to the DLS measurement, including the hydration shell and surface polymers around the particles in solution, whereas TEM reports the dehydrated, core dimensions of the same particles under high vacuum (Wilson and Prud'homme 2021). This, combined with the inherent tendency of DLS to be more sensitive to larger particles and aggregates, accounts for the differences observed and confirms the nanoscale, monodisperse nature of our preparations.

(D–G) DLS showed a monodisperse hydrodynamic diameter of ~100 nm, while TEM revealed a smaller core size; this known difference arises from DLS measuring the hydrated particle size. (F) The zeta potential shift confirmed successful surface conjugation of angiopep-2. (H) Myc-tag immunofluorescence validated surface display on engineered E. coli. (I) OMV-ANG showed a high doxorubicin loading capacity. Optimized loading conditions (300 RPM, 37°C, 4 h vs 1:1 mass ratio, 200 RPM, 37°C, 3 h vs 1:1 mass ratio) yielded high doxorubicin encapsulation efficiency. (J) The drug release profile exhibited a biphasic pattern with sustained release, ideal for therapy.

Following DOX encapsulation, DOX-OMV-ANG (Figure 1F) retained low polydispersity (PDI < 0.2), confirming colloidal stability. Zeta potential measurements demonstrated that surface conjugation of the cationic angiopep-2 peptide shifted the OMV-ANG surface charge toward negative values relative to wild-type OMVs (Figure 1G), consistent with successful ligand display. These characteristics are similar to the typical OMVs isolated in previous studies (Chen et al. 2010; Kuerban et al. 2020; Mi et al. 2023). We subsequently loaded the isolated OMV-ANG with DOX by co-incubating appropriate proportions of OMV-ANG and DOX, followed by the elimination of free DOX through ultrafiltration. Drug loading efficiency assessments confirmed the effective encapsulation of DOX within OMV-ANG, with encapsulation and loading efficiencies exceeding 70% at a 1:1 ratio after repeated combinations and final optimization at a shaking speed of 300 RPM and 37 °C for 5 h (Figure 1I). The drug release profile (Figure 1J) exhibited biphasic kinetics, with an initial burst release (~45% within 10 h) followed by sustained release, achieving ~70% cumulative release by 72 h. This controlled release pattern supports prolonged therapeutic exposure while mitigating premature drug leakage during systemic transit. Collectively, these results validate the successful engineering of stable, angiopep-2-functionalized OMVs with high drug-loading capacity suitable for subsequent biological evaluation.

In vitro uptake and tumor targeting by OMV-ANG

The cellular internalization of doxorubicin (DOX) delivered via angiopep-2-decorated OMV-ANG was evaluated in GL-261 glioma cells. The cells were incubated with DOX alone, DOX loaded into wild-type OMVs, or OMV-ANG. Our results showed significantly enhanced intracellular accumulation of DOX in the DOX-OMV-ANG group compared to free DOX and wild-type DOX-OMV (Figure 2A). The quantitative analysis confirmed a 2.8-fold increase in fluorescence intensity for DOX-OMV-ANG relative to free DOX and DOX-OMV (p < 0.0001) (Figure 2B), demonstrating the critical role of angiopep-2 in facilitating receptor-mediated uptake.

Cellular uptake and cytotoxicity of OMV-ANG in GBM cell lines. (A) Confocal microscopy, showing enhanced intracellular DOX (red) from DOX-OMV-ANG after 4 h incubation. Nuclei are stained with DAPI (blue). (B) Quantification confirmed a 2.8-fold increase in DOX fluorescence for DOX-OMV-ANG versus free DOX (p < 0.0001 vs. free DOX; p < 0.0001 vs. DOX-OMV; n = 5 fields/group) post 4 h incubation. (C–H) Cytotoxicity assays, revealing that while OMV-ANG alone is biocompatible (C, F), DOX-OMV-ANG exhibits significantly enhanced cell killing over free DOX in both GL-261 (C–E) and U87 MG (F–H) cells, demonstrating superior drug delivery. Cytotoxicity assays were performed across a concentration gradient (3–50 µg/ml; n = 6). Viability was assessed via CCK-8 assay. Data was analyzed using ImageJ Software. Data represent mean ± SD (n = 6).

Furthermore, we systematically assessed the therapeutic efficacy of OMV-ANG in GL-261 (Figure 2C–2E) and U87 MG (Figure 2F–2H) cell lines. Both the cell lines were incubated with DOX alone, DOX-OMVs, or DOX-OMV-ANG. The results indicated that OMV-ANG alone exhibited minimal cytotoxicity across all tested concentrations (3–50 µg/ml), confirming carrier biocompatibility (Figure 2C, F). Free DOX induced moderate dose-dependent cell death in both cell lines, achieving 45%–60% viability reduction at 50 µg/ml concentration (Figure 2D, G). Remarkably, DOX-OMV-ANG (Figure 2E, H) demonstrated substantially higher cytotoxicity, reducing viability to <40% at 50 µg/ml in GL-261 cells and ~50% in U87 MG cells. While DOX-OMV-ANG demonstrated potent cytotoxicity in both models, its efficacy was more pronounced in GL-261 cells than in U87 MG. Critically, DOX-OMV-ANG still outperformed free DOX, confirming that the OMV-ANG platform enhances drug delivery and overcomes baseline chemoresistance. The amplified response of DOX-OMV-ANG compared to free DOX highlights the advantage of the OMV platform for enhanced drug delivery. The enhanced cytotoxicity of DOX-OMV-ANG in vitro is attributed to improved cellular uptake and delivery of the chemotherapeutic payload. We note that the full therapeutic potential, particularly the immunostimulatory component of the platform, is realized in the context of an intact immune system, as demonstrated in our subsequent in vivo studies.

BBB penetration, biodistribution, immune-sensitization, and safety validation

Following the successful preparation and characterization, as well as the demonstrated efficacy of our engineered OMV-ANG with enhanced cellular uptake and cytotoxicity, we further evaluated its critical ability to penetrate the blood-brain barrier (BBB), a fundamental prerequisite for effective glioblastoma therapy. We first assessed the in vitro BBB penetration using an in vitro BBB model with bEnd.3 brain endothelial cells, where DOX-OMV-ANG demonstrated significantly enhanced transcytosis compared to wild-type DOX-OMV and free DOX controls (Figures 3A and 4B). Our quantitative analysis revealed a ~10% increase in the basolateral/apical fluorescence ratio for DOX-OMV-ANG versus DOX-OMV at 4 h (p < 0.0001) (Figure 3B). While the specific role of LRP1 was not directly validated through receptor blockade, this enhanced transcytosis of OMV-ANG, which is decorated with the known LRP1 ligand Angiopep-2, is consistent with a receptor-mediated process and suggests successful functionalization. These results were further supported in vivo following intravenous administration in healthy mice, where ICG-labeled OMV-ANG and DOX-OMV-ANG exhibited rapid brain accumulation, with peak fluorescence intensity in the brain at 2-4 h (Figure 3C–3J, Supplementary Figures 3 and 4). Notably, OMV-ANG brain retention persisted for 6 h, demonstrating sustained BBB penetration. Minor brain accumulation was also observed for free ICG and unmodified OMV-ICG. This could be attributed to the slight passive diffusion of the small molecule ICG and potential non-specific interactions of the OMVs with the BBB. Crucially, OMV-ANG-ICG showed significantly higher and more sustained brain accumulation, underscoring the benefit of Angiopep-2 functionalization.

(C–J) In vivo and ex vivo imaging, showing rapid brain accumulation of OMV-ANG within 2–4 h, with sustained retention and reduced off-target sequestration compared to controls. (H) Spleen enlargement indicates systemic immune activation. (K–P) Cytokine profiling, revealing a controlled immune response, e.g. an early, transient peak of TNF-α and IL-6, followed by a sustained elevation of IFN-γ, which persists on day 10. Data represent mean ± SD (n = 3/group).

Histopathological safety assessment of OMV-ANG and DOX-OMV-ANG. Our biodistribution analysis revealed targeted organ tropism, where OMV-ANG showed preferential accumulation in the brain with reduced off-target sequestration in the liver/spleen (Figure 3G). Additionally, spleen enlargement was observed in both OMV and OMV-ANG groups (Figure 3H), consistent with innate immune recognition of bacterial components. Moreover, the cytokine profiling revealed controlled immune sensitization, characterized by transient upregulation of TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ. Specifically, TNF-α and IL-6 peaked at 4–24 h and 4–8 h post-injection, respectively, returning to baseline by 72 h, while IFN-γ remained suppressed at 1–72 h (Figure 3K–3M). By the endpoint at day 10, TNF-α and IL-6 returned to physiological levels, while IFN-γ dominated the cytokine profile (Figure 3N–3P). The early phase of TNF-α/IL-6 upregulation, which may mediate innate immune activation and facilitate the enhancement of vascular permeability and antigen-presenting cell recruitment, is both critical for initiating anti-tumor responses. Similarly, the late-phase IFN-γ may drive adaptive immunity by activating cytotoxic T cells and macrophages while countering immunosuppressive Tregs in the glioma microenvironment.

To further assess the biocompatibility of OMV-ANG formulations, healthy mice were randomized into four groups. Each group received intravenous injections of PBS control, three fractional doses of OMV-ANG (10 µg/dose on days 1, 4, and 7), a single high-dose OMV-ANG (30 µg on day 1), or DOX-OMV-ANG (30 µg on day 1) (Figure 3). Major organs, including the liver, kidney, spleen, heart, and lungs, from mice treated with the formulations, were analyzed using H&E staining (Figure 3). All treatment groups exhibited preserved tissue architecture and cellular morphology indistinguishable from those of the PBS controls. The organs showed no obvious evidence of hepatotoxicity, e.g. intact liver lobules, no steatosis or necrosis; nephrotoxicity, e.g. normal glomeruli and tubules; splenic hyperplasia, e.g. maintained red/white pulp boundaries; and cardiopulmonary damage, e.g. unaltered myocardium/alveoli. This absence of pathological alterations, even under aggressive dosing regimens, confirms the excellent in vivo biosafety of both empty and drug-loaded OMV-ANG. Combined with transient cytokine responses (Figure 3K–3P) and rapid blood clearance of off-target particles (Figure 3I–3J), these results suggest that OMV-ANG is a safe therapeutic for repeated systemic administration.

Therapeutic efficacy and survival benefit of DOX-OMV-ANG in orthotopic glioblastoma

Building on the validated BBB penetration, tumor-targeting capability, and biosafety of OMV-ANG formulations, we next assessed their therapeutic efficacy in an orthotopic glioblastoma model. In orthotopic GL261-Luc glioblastoma-bearing mice, systemic administration of DOX-OMV-ANG elicited potent anti-tumor efficacy. Longitudinal bioluminescence imaging revealed significant suppression of tumor growth in the DOX-OMV-ANG group compared to controls, e.g. vehicle control (ICG), free DOX, and OMV-ANG (Figure 5A). By day 28, DOX-OMV-ANG significantly reduced tumor bioluminescence, while control tumors exhibited exponential progression. Notably, OMV-ANG monotherapy also demonstrated moderate tumor control, validating its immunostimulatory activity (Figure 5D).

OMV-ANG eradicates orthotopic glioblastoma and extends survival. (A) Bioluminescence imaging, showing potent suppression of tumor growth in the DOX-OMV-ANG group. (B) Body weight remains stable in treatment groups, indicating tolerability. (C) DOX-OMV-ANG confers a significant survival advantage compared to vehicle control (ICG), with a 40% long-term survival rate (>30 days). (D) Ex vivo bioluminescence, confirming reduced tumor burden.

Critically, OMV-ANG and DOX-OMV-ANG conferred a substantial survival advantage, e.g. median survival extended to 42 days versus 25 days in vehicle controls and free DOX-treated mice (Figure 5C). The combinatorial group achieved 40% long-term survivors (>30 days) with no tumor recurrence, whereas all control mice succumbed by day 30. Furthermore, body weight remained stable in OMV-ANG and DOX-OMV-ANG groups, while the control groups reduced to 14–15 g (Figure 5B), confirming the treatment tolerability of OMV-ANG and DOX-OMV-ANG without systemic toxicity.

Our histopathological analysis (H&E) of brain sections at Day 30 revealed a persistent tumor mass across all groups (Figure 6A). This is a recognized characteristic of glioblastoma, where functional control, such as the induction of apoptosis and suppression of proliferation at the invasive front, often precedes observable gross morphological regression (Seker-Polat et al. 2022; Coy et al. 2023). In line with this, high-magnification analysis of the tumor-brain interface suggested a more defined border and reduced invasive protrusions in OMV-ANG and DOX-OMV-ANG treated groups (Figure 6B). To directly assess this functional anti-tumor activity, we performed TUNEL and Ki67 staining. Firstly, the TUNEL assays were performed on brain tumor sections to quantify apoptosis (Figure 6C, 6D). The control group exhibited minimal FITC signal (green), confirming baseline apoptosis levels in untreated tumors. Free DOX induced moderate apoptosis, with sparse TUNEL-positive cells dispersed throughout the tumor tissue. The OMV-ANG alone group demonstrated increased apoptosis compared to the control, attributable to the intrinsic immunostimulatory properties of OMVs that activate anti-tumor immunity. Critically, the DOX-OMV-ANG group exhibited extensive and densely concentrated TUNEL-positive cells, reflecting robust apoptosis induction. This response significantly surpassed all other groups, with apoptotic nuclei colocalizing throughout tumor regions. The amplified cell death aligns with the near-complete tumor regression observed in bioluminescence imaging (Figure 5) and confirms the synergistic potency of combined chemo-immunotherapy. Our Ki67 immunohistochemistry further revealed marked suppression of proliferation, with DOX-OMV-ANG significantly reducing Ki67⁺ nuclei compared to the controls (Figure 6E, F).

(B) High-magnification H&E analysis of the tumor infiltration margin, where control and DOX groups show a poorly defined, highly invasive tumor margin with dense sheets of tumor cells infiltrating the brain parenchyma, while OMV-ANG and DOX-OMV-ANG treatment result in a more demarcated tumor border and reduced density of invasive cells at the interface. While H&E showed no significant reduction in gross tumor size, the complete survival of OMV-ANG and DOX-OMV-ANG-treated mice, supported by ex vivo imaging, TUNEL, and Ki67 data, demonstrates that these platforms achieve potent functional control of glioma progression by suppressing proliferation, inducing apoptosis, and targeting invasive disease before manifesting as morphological regression. The agreement between functional biomarkers (TUNEL apoptosis, Ki67 proliferation arrest), bioluminescent viability reduction, and survival extension robustly validates therapeutic efficacy, even in the absence of H&E morphometric changes. These findings establish DOX-OMV-ANG as a promising strategy for glioma therapy, leveraging angiopep-2-mediated targeting and OMV-driven immunomodulation to overcome BBB constraints and induce synergistic tumor regression.

Bioinformatics validation of therapeutic mechanisms

Based on our results observed in the survival benefit of OMV-ANG and the apoptotic synergy of DOX-OMV-ANG, we performed bioinformatics analyses to further validate the molecular mechanisms underlying the efficacy of OMV-ANG. We utilized the clinical glioma datasets and investigated IFN-γ, a key cytokine elevated by OMV-ANG treatment (Figure 3P), and a central regulator of immunochemotherapeutic response. This integrated approach bridges our preclinical findings with clinical biomarkers of glioma progression and treatment resistance. IFN-γ expression predicted survival outcomes in the TCGA GBM cohort. Patients with high IFN-γ expression exhibited significantly prolonged overall survival (Figure 7A), with a >40% survival advantage at 24 months versus low expressers. Critically, IFN-γ levels decreased progressively with increasing tumor grade, e.g. highest in Grade II gliomas and lowest in Grade IV GBMs (Figure 7B), explaining its role as an independent prognostic factor for the survival advantage observed in high expressers at 24 months.

Bioinformatics validation of IFN-γ as a therapeutic biomarker. (A) Analysis of TCGA data, validating the mechanism of OMV-ANG. High IFN-γ expression correlates with (A) prolonged patient survival and (B) lower tumor grade. IFN-γ levels are positively associated with infiltration of (C) CD8+ T cells and (D, E) M1 macrophages. (F) Critically, high IFN-γ expression predicts enhanced doxorubicin susceptibility, providing a clinical rationale for the synergy observed with DOX-OMV-ANG.

Our tumor microenvironment analysis revealed the immunomodulatory networks of IFN-γ (Figure 7C–7E). Its expression showed strong positive correlations with cytotoxic CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration (Rho = 0.319, p = 1.44e−04) (Figure 7C). In addition, IFN-γ expression showed a significant positive correlation with macrophages (Rho = 0.197; p = 2.10e−02) (Figure 7D), specifically with the pro-inflammatory, anti-tumor M1-polarized macrophages (Rho = 0.206, p = 1.57e−02) (Figure 7E). These results suggest that IFN-γ promotes macrophage recruitment and polarization toward anti-tumor M1 phenotypes. The results also indicate that IFN-γ may reprogram the immunosuppressive glioma microenvironment toward anti-tumor activity. Critically, IFN-γ expression sensitized gliomas to doxorubicin (Figure 7F). High IFN-γ levels correlated with enhanced doxorubicin susceptibility, providing a mechanistic basis for the superior efficacy of DOX-OMV-ANG, which induced sustained IFN-γ elevation (Figure 3P) in overcoming chemoresistance.

Discussion

Glioblastoma remains one of the most formidable challenges in oncology due to its therapeutic resistance, which stems from three interconnected barriers, e.g. the blood-brain barrier (BBB) that restricts a significant quantity of therapeutics from reaching tumors (Qiao et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2021), intrinsic tumor resistance mechanisms like efflux pumps (Jin et al. 2012), DNA repair activation (Lozinski et al. 2021), and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment characterized by M2 macrophage polarization (Zhu et al. 2020), T-reg infiltration (Jacobs et al. 2010), and checkpoint molecule overexpression (Singh et al. 2025). Current glioblastoma therapies, such as surgery, radiation, and temozolomide, fail to adequately address these barriers, yielding median survivals of merely 15–16 months (Chinot et al. 2014; Gilbert et al. 2014). While emerging immunotherapies, e.g. checkpoint inhibitors and CAR-T cells, show promise in peripheral cancers, their efficacy in GBM is hampered by poor BBB penetration and the brain’s unique immunosuppressive landscape (Sadelain 2017; Miliotou and Papadopoulou 2018; Wilky 2019; Shen et al. 2020; Sharma et al. 2021). Similarly, synthetic nanomedicines designed for BBB penetration often suffer from manufacturing complexity, limited drug loading, rapid clearance, and protein corona effects that mask targeting ligands (Lin et al. 2016; Feng et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2021; Farshbaf et al. 2022).

Herein, we introduce a promising engineered nanoarchitectural platform, the angiopep-2-decorated bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMV-ANG), that may overcome these barriers while actively stimulating the anti-tumor immunity. Recently, OMVs have emerged as versatile platforms for immunotherapy and targeted drug delivery. For instance, Du et al. developed immunomodulatory nanozymes (PdIr@OMVs) that effectively eradicate intracellular multidrug-resistant bacterial infections and rescue immunoparalysis in sepsis models (Du et al. 2025). While their work demonstrates the potent adjuvant properties and macrophage-targeting capabilities of OMVs in systemic infections, our study extends this paradigm to the central nervous system by engineering OMVs with the BBB-penetrating peptide angiopep-2 for glioblastoma therapy. This approach not only leverages the inherent immunogenicity of OMVs but also enables precise brain tumor targeting via LRP1-mediated transcytosis, offering a dual chemo-immunotherapeutic strategy against glioblastoma. Our engineered OMV-ANG synergistically merges targeted drug delivery with intrinsic immunomodulation, achieving significant survival extension in orthotopic GBM models.

The utility of this platform lies in its multifaceted functionality, which combines several advantageous features into a single system. First, angiopep-2 decoration enables high-efficiency BBB penetration via LRP1 receptor-mediated transcytosis. While our in vitro BBB model confirmed barrier integrity prior to treatment, we did not monitor TEER after administration; thus, the potential contribution of transient barrier modulation to the enhanced transcytosis cannot be fully excluded and warrants further investigation. Additionally, although the enhanced brain accumulation of OMV-ANG is consistent with LRP1-mediated transport, direct validation through receptor blockade experiments represents an important direction for future mechanistic studies. Our OMV-ANG maintained robust brain accumulation in vivo. We speculate that the dense native protein landscape of the OMV surface may confer a ‘self-shielding’ effect that helps mitigate the protein corona’s masking of angiopep‑2, a common limitation for synthetic nanoparticles (Wang et al. 2021; Farshbaf et al. 2022). This mechanism, however, requires direct experimental validation in future work. We selected a genetic engineering approach for Angiopep-2 display to ensure stable, covalent attachment of the ligand during the biogenesis of the OMVs, which may offer advantages in batch-to-batch consistency over post-production chemical conjugation methods. Secondly, the high drug loading capacity (70% encapsulation efficiency) and biphasic release kinetics ensure sustained intra-tumoral doxorubicin exposure, countering P-gp-mediated efflux and hypoxia-driven chemoresistance (Jin et al. 2012). Thirdly, the OMV backbone provides intrinsic immunostimulation. OMVs are rich in pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs, e.g. LPS, lipoproteins) that activate Toll-like receptors (TLRs) on innate immune cells (Kim et al. 2015; Schwechheimer and Kuehn 2015, Furuyama and Sircili 2021; Gilmore et al. 2021). This triggered a controlled cytokine cascade in our study, where early TNF-α/IL-6 peaks may enhance vascular permeability and dendritic cell recruitment, while sustained IFN-γ production may polarize macrophages toward M1 phenotypes and activate cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells, effects that were corroborated by our bioinformatics analysis linking IFN-γ to improved survival and immune cell infiltration in human GBM. Critically, this immune reprogramming counteracts the GBM TME’s immunosuppressive signals, e.g. TGF-β and IL-10, converting ‘cold’ tumors into ‘hot’ lesions amenable to immunotherapy (Sharma et al. 2021; Singh et al. 2025).

Our data revealed a controlled systemic immune response, characterized by transient spleen enlargement and a defined cytokine cascade, which may signify robust immune activation. The observed splenomegaly following OMV-ANG administration is a recognized phenomenon in response to potent pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and is thought to be multifactorial (Janeway et al. 2001; Gilmore et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2022). It may signify robust immune activation, involving the expansion of leukocyte populations within the spleen, a key secondary lymphoid organ. Furthermore, in the context of significant immune stimulation, transient splenomegaly can be associated with extramedullary hematopoiesis, where the spleen supports the increased demand for innate and adaptive immune cells (Bronte and Pittet 2013). This systemic priming is consistent with the subsequent recruitment of CD8⁺ T cells and macrophages to the brain tumor site, effectively bridging the innate immune response with adaptive anti-tumor immunity. The cytokine profile, an early, self-resolving peak of TNF-α and IL-6 followed by a sustained elevation of IFN-γ, further supports this narrative. The initial pro-inflammatory phase may enhance vascular permeability and antigen presentation, while the sustained IFN-γ, a key driver of anti-tumor immunity, promotes cytotoxic T-cell function and M1 macrophage polarization. Critically, this immune activation was transient and coupled with an excellent safety profile, as confirmed by histopathology. The absence of chronic inflammation or organ damage suggests that OMV-ANG achieves a favorable balance, effectively training the immune system for an anti-tumor response without inducing harmful autoimmunity or cytokine release syndrome. It is worth mentioning that we observed transient splenomegaly and a defined cytokine cascade, consistent with systemic immune activation by OMV-associated PAMPs. While this response was self-resolving in our acute study and correlated with anti-tumor efficacy, the long-term effects of repeated dosing and the potential for hyperactivation represent important topics for future safety pharmacokinetics studies.

Our OMV-ANG offers distinct advantages over current nanocarriers. Firstly, biocompatibility and safety. Despite potent immune activation, histopathology revealed no organ damage even at high doses, aligning with clinical safety data from OMV-based vaccines like Bexsero® (Nøkleby et al. 2007; Acevedo et al. 2014). This contrasts with cationic synthetic nanoparticles that may cause hepatotoxicity (Cornu et al. 2020; Vilas-Boas and Vinken 2021). Secondly, the manufacturing scalability. OMV production via bacterial culturing is cost-effective and scalable compared to exosome isolation or complex nanoparticle synthesis. Engineered E. coli strains can yield high OMV titers, e.g. 5–10 mg/l culture, facilitating clinical translation. Lastly, the single-step surface functionalization via autotransporter expression (using our pAIDA1-ANG plasmid; Supplementary Figure 1) avoids the multi-layer conjugations required for polymeric NPs, preserving vesicle integrity.

A key insight that emerged from our histopathological analysis is that while H&E staining showed no significant reduction in gross tumor size at day 30, functional assays revealed potent anti-tumor activity. Our TUNEL assays demonstrated extensive apoptosis, and Ki67 staining confirmed suppression of proliferation in DOX-OMV-ANG-treated tumors. This apparent paradox reflects the infiltrative nature of GBM, where therapy preferentially eliminates disseminated tumor cells invading normal brain parenchyma, e.g. cells not captured in 2D sectional analysis, while the tumor core’s structural framework persists temporarily (Seker-Polat et al. 2022; Coy et al. 2023). The survival extension and bioluminescence reduction validate that functional tumor control precedes morphological regression, suggesting the limitations of H&E alone for assessing efficacy in invasive gliomas.

The clinical promise of OMV-ANG extends beyond the delivery of doxorubicin, offering a versatile platform for next-generation GBM therapeutics. The modular nature of our system allows for the encapsulation of diverse cargo. For instance, delivering siRNA against the DNA repair enzyme MGMT could sensitize a majority of GBM patients to temozolomide, while loading STING agonists, e.g. cGAMP, could directly ignite a potent type I interferon response within the tumor microenvironment, potentially overcoming the ‘cold’ tumor phenotype. Furthermore, the autotransporter system can be adapted to display tumor-specific targeting ligands beyond angiopep-2. We hypothesize that co-displaying an EGFRvIII-specific scFv antibody fragment would enable dual targeting, e.g. LRP1-mediated BBB transcytosis followed by precise binding to the constitutively active EGFRvIII mutant present in ~30% of GBM, thereby concentrating the therapeutic payload within the most aggressive tumor cell populations.

Most compelling is the rational combination of OMV-ANG with immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as anti-PD-1. Our data provides a clear mechanistic hypothesis for this synergy, e.g. OMV-ANG acts as an in-situ vaccine, priming and recruiting a population of tumor-specific CD8⁺ T cells (as evidenced by IFN-γ elevation and T-cell infiltration), while anti-PD-1 would prevent the exhaustion of these newly recruited T cells within the immunosuppressive niche (Lowther et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2019). This combination strategy directly addresses the two major failures of checkpoint monotherapy in GBM, e.g. inadequate T-cell priming and profound T-cell exhaustion, by simultaneously providing the signal 3 (inflammatory milieu) and blocking the brakes on adaptive immunity. Future work will rigorously test this hypothesis in immunocompetent, checkpoint-resistant models.
