Metastatic Papillary Thyroid Carcinoma Without Intrathyroidal Primary: Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Mohammed Sulaiman Alsayyari, Homood Mohammad Almutairi, Hassan Alshurafa, Yazeed Abdulrahman Alkhalifah, Abdulaziz Muhayya Alotaibi, Alyaa Shojaa Al Mutairy, Rawan Mosleh Alahmadi, Semonti Nandi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPapillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) usually has an intrathyroidal primary lesion and a good prognosis. In rare cases, however, individuals may also present with metastatic disease in the absence of a palpable thyroid primary, so‐called occult PTC. Such cases are often difficult to diagnose and manage, especially when serial monitoring of serum thyroglobulin (Tg) levels and imaging studies demonstrate the opposite. We report the case of a 56‐year‐old woman who underwent total thyroidectomy for suspected multinodular goiter in 2019. The results of a histological examination revealed no intrathyroidal tumor in 78 blocks, but one central node was found to be positive for metastatic PTC. Follow‐up examination proved that Tg had increased even though the results from U/S, PET/CT, and I‐123 WB scans were always negative. Laboratory studies suggested potential heterophile antibody interference, but a fine needle aspiration (FNA) of a cervical node in 2023 and 2024 reported recurrence of metastatic PTC. She underwent left selective lateral neck dissection (levels II–V) with central compartment (level VI) exploration in January 2025, and a number of metastatic nodes without extranodal extension were found. Tg levels were undetectable throughout follow‐up with no evidence of residual disease on US postoperatively. The patient remains well on long‐term follow‐up. We present a rare case of metastatic PTC with no identifiable thyroid primary, which is combined with the diagnostic difficulties of Tg assay interference and limitations of imaging in the follow‐up setting. It highlights the importance of multimodal surveillance (including cytology, imaging, and biochemical testing) to ensure true recurrences are accurately detected, often many years after apparently curative surgery.

1. Introduction

Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), the most prevalent form of thyroid malignancy, is generally associated with an identifiable intrathyroidal primary lesion and has an excellent outcome when treated adequately. Cervical lymph node metastases are common in PTC but virtually always occur with a detectable primary in the thyroid gland [1].

In rare instances, metastatic PTC may be identified in regional lymph nodes without an identifiable intrathyroidal primary, even after extensive histopathological examination of the thyroid gland. This entity, often referred to as occult PTC or metastatic PTC of unknown primary, remains poorly understood and presents significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [1–3]. Proposed mechanisms include spontaneous regression of the primary tumor, the presence of an undetected microscopic focus, or carcinoma arising from ectopic thyroid tissue [1–3].

The clinical complexity of such cases is further compounded during postoperative surveillance. Serum thyroglobulin (Tg), a key tumor marker in differentiated thyroid cancer, may demonstrate discordant behavior relative to imaging findings. In addition, limitations in imaging modalities for detecting low‐volume or micrometastatic disease, as well as potential assay interference, can obscure early detection of recurrence [4, 5].

Here, we report a rare case of metastatic PTC without an identifiable intrathyroidal primary, complicated by discordant Tg trends, limitations of conventional imaging, and delayed cytological confirmation of recurrence. This case highlights the importance of a multimodal approach to surveillance and underscores the diagnostic challenges associated with occult PTC.

2. Case Presentation

An otherwise healthy 56‐year‐old woman had previously undergone a total thyroidectomy in 2019 for suspected multinodular goiter. Intraoperatively, the presence of suspicious features in intraoperative central compartment lymph nodes, leading to a limited central compartment lymph node dissection by the surgeon. The final histopathology demonstrated benign nodular hyperplasia of the entire thyroid gland and no evidence of malignancy after 78 carefully examined histologic blocks. But 1 of 6 central neck (level VI) lymph nodes was positive for metastatic PTC. No intrathyroidal primary lesion was found, and three unremarkable parathyroid glands were identified. Expert pathology review confirmed the diagnosis, which was metastatic PTC of unknown primary, a rare but documented phenomenon.

Her immediate postoperative course was complicated by dyspnea, chest pain, and bradycardia. A pulmonary embolism was suspected based on CT angiography, and she was discharged on anticoagulation, which she later discontinued on her own.

Routine surveillance imaging was initiated in September 2020 with neck ultrasound, which demonstrated an unremarkable thyroid bed and benign‐appearing cervical lymph nodes. However, because of serum Tg levels remained high, PET/CT was carried out. This showed mild metabolic activity in the left thyroid bed, possible residual tissue, low‐ to mild‐FDG avid bilateral lung nodules suspicious for micrometastases benign bilateral axillary lymph nodes.

She underwent radioactive iodine (RAI) ablation (100 mCi) in March 2021. A post‐ablation scan demonstrated uptake in the thyroid bed and thyroglossal duct cyst but no distant iodine‐avid lesions (Figure 1).

Neck ultrasound in December 2021 and September 2022 demonstrated small bilateral residual thyroid tissue and a left cervical lymph node with an irregular contour but preserved fatty hilum with a size of 0.5 cm (Figure 2). Thyroid function tests done postoperatively (2022) showed euthyroid status with thyroid hormone replacement therapy. The patient was continued on levothyroxine 200 mcg daily. Laboratory results were notable for TSH 0.618 mIU/L and free T4 20.8 pmol/L; serum Tg was low at 1.4 µg/L, anti‐thyroglobulin antibodies were recorded as 22 kU/L. These findings were consistent with an appropriate biochemical profile following total thyroidectomy, with biochemical findings of euthyroid state.

By early 2023, there was a 6‐month history of a progressive increase in serum Tg to 4.8 ng/mL. However, FDG‐PET, I‐123 whole‐body scintigraphy, and neck ultrasound did not show any structural disease. In light of this discordance, assay interference, including heterophile antibody interference, was considered.

Additional workup revealed an increased rheumatoid factor (54 IU/mL). Serial Tg dilution testing resulted in linearity and confirmed polyethylene glycol (PEG) precipitation produced undetectable Tg levels. While these results were not definitive they raised suspicion for possible assay interference.

Due to limited access to confirmatory testing (e.g., mass spectrometry), samples were planned for external testing. The patient agreed to defer additional imaging, and multidisciplinary consensus supported observation.

MRI of the neck in December 2023 showed multiple non‐necrotic cervical lymph nodes (levels IB and IIA/IIB) and focal enhancement in bilateral thyroidectomy beds. Clinical correlation was advised.

October 2023 work‐up revealed malignant cells consistent with metastatic PTC on fine needle aspiration (FNA) of a left cervical lymph node, which was confirmed again during repeat FNA in August 2024. Neck ultrasound in February 2024 revealed a hypoechoic suspicious left lymph node (0.6 × 0.5 cm). Finally, MRI performed in October 2024 revealed subtle progression of bilateral paratracheal signal abnormalities, consistent with recurrent disease.

Following a comprehensive review of her Tg trend, imaging, and cytology, the rising Tg was now attributed to true disease recurrence. A left selective lateral neck dissection (levels II–V) with central compartment (level VI) exploration was recommended.

2.1. Surgical Pathology and Postoperative Course

A left selective lateral neck dissection (levels II–V) with central compartment (level VI) exploration was performed on the patient in January 2025. Histopathology findings showed multilevel nodal metastatic disease. Level IIA: one lymph node positive, metastatic deposit (0.1 cm), no extranodal extension In the central compartment (level VI), one 0.6 cm positive lymph node with a 0.3 cm metastatic deposit without extranodal extension was identified. Collectively, these results are compatible with multilevel nodal metastatic disease, absence of extranodal extension.

After left selective lateral neck dissection (levels II–V) with central compartment (level VI) exploration in January 2025, the patient had an uneventful recovery. She had no postoperative complications, including vocal cord dysfunction, hypocalcemia, and wound‐related problems. We did not find alterations in the calcium level that required supplementation. Postoperative laryngoscopy confirmed the preservation of vocal cord mobility, the surgical wound was well healed with no infectious signs, seroma, or hematoma. Her therapy with levothyroxine was reinstituted and she was discharged in stable condition, with continued TSH suppression.

During the patient’s 6‐week clinic follow‐up, the patient was asymptomatic clinically. A serum Tg level checked during TSH suppression was undetectable, and anti‐Tg antibodies continued to be negative. A complete neck ultrasound was performed at the time, which showed no evidence of residual or recurrent disease in the thyroid bed or cervical lymph node compartments, indicating a very good response to surgery.

Further follow‐up in April and May 2025 confirmed clinical stability. There were no other accompanying symptoms such as neck swelling, dysphagia, or fatigue. Sustained undetectable Tg levels, and interval imaging revealed no abnormalities. The multidisciplinary team elected not to pursue further RAI therapy at this time, as the disease had been no evidence of iodine‐avid distant disease on prior post‐ablation imaging and her post‐surgical response was characterized as excellent.

The patient was followed in a long‐term surveillance program by endocrinology and head and neck surgery. The follow‐up program involves serial monitoring of Tg every 3–6 months, under both suppression and stimulation (if necessary), as well as a neck ultrasound to identify any structural recurrence. Cross‐sectional imaging including PET/CT should be provided for cases with Tg elevation that not attributed to an ultrasound detectable lesion.

3. Literature Review

Papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) is the most common thyroid malignancy and generally has a favorable prognosis. In rare cases, however, patients have been reported to develop metastatic PTC without a primary focus in the thyroid gland. This rare manifestation, referred to frequently as occult PTC, presents challenges in diagnosis and management, particularly in the context of a total thyroidectomy patient who is thought to be cured of the disease.

[2] reported a PTC case with concomitant IgG4‐related thyroiditis, where fibrosis was so profound that it may have masked microscopic foci of PTC. Xu et al. These cases demonstrate different diagnostic possibilities for the patients without a primary tumor: these are spontaneous regression, carcinoma from ectopic thyroid, microscopic lesions missed at pathology, and obscuration of the main lesion by fibrosis or inflammation. They also underscore the importance of continued rigorous surveillance (both imaging and biochemical) in patients considered to be free from disease.

4. Discussion

PTC usually has a discernable site of intrathyroidal disease and is associated with a favorable prognosis when adequately managed. Nonetheless, a tiny fraction of patients appears with lymph node metastasis without a primary thyroid tumor, which is called occult PTC or metastatic PTC of unknown primary [1–3].

In the present case, the patient had undergone a total thyroidectomy for MNG, but a micropapillary thyroid carcinoma macrometastasis (tumor metastasis >2 mm) was found in one of the central lymph nodes at histopathologic examination, and no intrathyroidal primary lesion was identified after extensive histologic examination in 78 blocks studied. Although we used ultrasound, CT, PET/CT, and MRI for serial imaging, a primary focus was not found, consistent with previous reports [1–3].

Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have limited sensitivity for the detection and characterization of small lymph nodes and micrometastatic disease. In differentiated thyroid carcinoma, micrometastases may also lack radioiodine avidity, further reducing the sensitivity of functional imaging. In this case, the absence of radiologically suspicious lymphadenopathy on CT, MRI, and radioiodine imaging did not exclude the presence of microscopic or low‐volume metastatic disease. The eventual confirmation of recurrence by fine‐needle aspiration cytology highlights the limitations of imaging modalities in detecting early nodal disease.

There may be several reasons of such phenomenon. It is thought to regressed spontaneously, perhaps under immune surveillance [1]. A carcinoma in ectopic thyroid rests, for example, in thyroglossal duct tissue, is another consideration [5]. Harach et al. [6] showed that PTC microscopic foci may exist in up to 10% of the normal thyroid glands (4%), so even with exhaustive biopsies, a microfocus is missed may be nonetheless. Invasive foci of carcinoma may also be masked or destroyed by inflammatory process of the carcinoma, as exemplified by the case of Papadimitriou et al. [2].

A characteristic of our case was difficulty in diagnosis during serum Tg follow‐up. The rising Tg levels were suspicious for recurrence, but initial studies were consistent with heterophile antibody interference. The presence of elevated rheumatoid factor, linear Tg dilution, and undetectable Tg after PEG precipitation were supportive but not definitive for assay interference, reflecting previously reported limitations of Tg measurement [5,7]. The final cytology of the 2 FNAs finally reconfirmed the appearance of true metastatic PTC recurrence, highlighting the necessity for a multimodal evaluation and not relying on Tg measurements alone.

This case illustrates several important clinical points:

1.Occult PTC is a diagnostic conundrum, and when nodal metastases are found in the absence of an intrathyroid lesion, occult PTC should be considered.
