Respiratory Syncytial Virus Symptomatic Fraction, Shedding and Detection in Children Aged 6–23 Months in South Africa in 2022

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAccurate detection of RSV infections is important to understand epidemiology. We estimated symptomatic fraction and shedding duration of reverse‐transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR)‐confirmed cases and compared RSV detection through molecular and serologic techniques.MethodsWe enrolled healthy children aged 6–23 months in a prospective cohort study, from May to October 2022, collecting thrice‐weekly mid‐turbinate nasal swabs for RSV RT‐PCR testing. We collected serum at baseline and end of follow‐up and detected anti‐RSV IgG using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and multipathogen bead‐based immunoassay (MIA). We ascertained symptoms by twice‐weekly nurse visits, and daily caregiver symptom diaries. We calculated sensitivity, specificity, and positive (PPV) and negative (NPV) predictive value of RT‐PCR, ELISA, and MIA compared to a standard (two or more assays‐positive) among children with paired sera and baseline blood preceding an RT‐PCR–confirmed episode.ResultsThe RSV season started mid‐February 2022. Serology had a high sensitivity and specificity for infection identification.Keywords: burden, respiratory syncytial virus, South Africa, symptoms, transmission, vaccine

Background

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) causes an estimated 3.6 million episodes of acute lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) hospitalizations and approximately 100,000 deaths annually in children aged < 5 years globally. More than 95% of these hospitalizations and deaths occur in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMIC) [1]. In South Africa, RSV is estimated to cause almost 100,000 LRTI episodes annually among children aged < 5 years, with the highest incidence in children aged < 1 year [2]. The RSV season in South Africa (southern hemisphere [SH]) occurs from February through May and typically precedes the SH winter influenza season [3].

Young children have been shown to be the key drivers of RSV transmission in communities [4, 5]. Interventions to prevent severe RSV among infants (long‐acting monoclonal antibodies and maternal vaccination) have been introduced in several high‐ and middle‐income countries, but are not yet available in South Africa [6, 7]. Live‐attenuated vaccines targeting children aged > 6 months are also under development [8]. Vaccination of children aged > 6 months has the potential to reduce RSV transmission as well as protect against illness beyond the first 6 months of life. Understanding symptomatic fraction, viral shedding and best approaches for RSV detection in this age group might help guide decisions about the benefits of potential future transmission‐blocking vaccines.

We aimed to estimate the symptomatic fraction, and shedding duration of infections caused by RSV in a cohort of children aged 6–23 months during the first winter after the SARS‐CoV‐2 pandemic in South Africa, 2022. We evaluated factors associated with symptomatic infection and duration of shedding. We also evaluated the concordance of infection ascertainment by serology (using a commercial enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay [ELISA] and in‐house multipathogen bead‐based immunoassay [MIA]) and reverse‐transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) to better understand which testing modalities might be most useful for future clinical trials aiming to evaluate to what extent new toddler RSV vaccines in development can prevent infection and reduce viral shedding in breakthrough infections.

Methods

We conducted a prospective cohort study in Jouberton, a township outside Klerksdorp in North West Province of South Africa, the site of several previous studies of community influenza, RSV, and SARS‐CoV‐2 burden and transmission [9, 10, 11]. The objective of the main study was to evaluate influenza burden. Findings of the influenza study, together with a detailed description of study methods, have been previously published [12]. The study was timed to coincide with the winter influenza season with follow‐up from May 2 through October 30, 2022. This means that the study was not well timed for RSV incidence estimation and RSV PCR‐confirmed infections may have been missed as the peak of the RSV season was over at the time of starting follow‐up (Figure 1a). For this reason we focused on symptomatic fraction and shedding duration as the main epidemiologic outcomes. As a secondary analysis, we estimated the minimum RSV incidence.

We enrolled healthy HIV‐uninfected children aged 6–23 months who were available for 6 months of follow‐up. We focused on this age group as studies suggest that this age group is important for influenza and RSV transmission [4, 10]. We aimed to enroll 100 children to achieve a goal of 50 evaluable children. This sample size was pragmatically chosen given available resources.

We identified potentially eligible children by prescreening attendees at child vaccination and well‐baby clinics, or those children accompanying ill mothers or siblings during clinic visits from January through March 2022. After obtaining parental written informed consent, we collected baseline data including demographic data and screened for underlying illnesses, including HIV infection. HIV infection status is routinely established in infancy for all children born in South Africa. We collected serum samples from all participants at enrolment and again at the time of participant withdrawal or the end of study follow‐up. At enrolment, we trained caregivers on study procedures including collection and packaging of mid‐turbinate nasal swab samples, daily measurement of temperature, heart rate and oxygen saturation (Oxypalm pulse oximeter, KTMed Inc., Seoul, Korea) and completion of daily electronic symptom questionnaires. We provided refresher training to caregivers if irregular measurements were submitted during the follow‐up period.

Follow‐up involved the following components: twice‐weekly nurse household visits (Monday and Thursday or Tuesday and Friday) with nurse collection of mid‐turbinate nasal swabs, measurement of tympanic temperature, and administration of a structured questionnaire to assess presence of symptoms and healthcare‐seeking behavior; and once weekly (Saturday or Sunday depending on visit schedule), the parent/caregiver collected a mid‐turbinate nasal swab placed in universal transport medium from their child. The swab was packaged with ice packs in a cooler box and collected on the same day by a motorcycle courier who transported the sample on ice packs to the laboratory. Once daily, the parent/caregiver was requested to complete an electronic symptom questionnaire on a study‐provided tablet and to record the child's tympanic temperature, heart rate, and oxygen saturation. Participants with signs and symptoms needing medical attention such as respiratory distress were referred to their local clinic for further care at any time during the study.

Laboratory Testing

All specimens were refrigerated (2°C–8°C) and transported within 24 h to the National Institute for Communicable Disease (NICD). Total nucleic acids were extracted from swabs using the Roche MagNA Pure 96 instrument (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) and MP96 DNA and Viral NA SV Kit (Roche Diagnostics). Extracts were tested for influenza A, influenza B, RSV, and SARS‐CoV‐2 by RT‐PCR using the Allplex SARS‐CoV‐2/FluA/FluB/RSV multiplex commercial kit (Seegene, Seoul, South Korea). A specimen was considered positive if RSV was detected with cycle threshold (Ct) values ≤ 40 according to manufacturer instructions. RSV‐positive samples were subgrouped using the Unites States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) RSV‐A and B typing kits, respectively, available through the International Reagent Resource (IRR).

Anti‐RSV IgG was detected using two different assays. Specimens were tested with a commercial ELISA kit (EUROIMMUN, Medizinische Labordiagnostika AG, Lübeck, Germany) as well as an in‐house MIA based on a previously described assay (Bio‐plex 200 System: Bio‐Rad Laboratories Ltd., USA; RSV prefusion F (pre‐F) antigen: Sino Biological, China) [14]. The WHO first international standard for RSV (16/284) was used to determine pre‐F IgG antibody concentrations (arbitrary units/mL [AU/mL]) for the MIA [15]. For the ELISA, baseline seropositivity was defined as ELISA titer ≥ 22 relative units [RU]/mL according to the manufacturer's instructions. The MIA does not have established cutoffs for seropositivity; therefore, we arbitrarily defined this as the preseason titer where a 50% protection against RT‐PCR– or serological‐confirmed infection was conveyed, assuming that all children in the cohort were exposed to RSV. This threshold (7.5 AU/mL) was identified using unadjusted logistic regression models assessing the association between baseline MIA titers and subsequent infection, with the selected cutoff corresponding to an odds ratio of approximately 0.5.

Data Management and Analysis

All data, including laboratory results, were collected electronically in real time using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) hosted at the University of the Witwatersrand [16, 17]. Forms were accessed by study staff through password‐secured REDCap accounts on an electronic tablet. Parents accessed daily symptom diaries through a survey link emailed to a study‐specific email address on a study‐provided tablet using CubiLock (Cubilock Denmark, Birkerød, Denmark) device management software to reduce data wastage.

An episode of RT‐PCR–confirmed RSV infection was defined as ≥ 1 RT‐PCR positive nasal swab for RSV. The duration of shedding was the difference in days between the first and the last days of RT‐PCR positivity. If an individual tested positive for a different RSV subgroup, or the same subgroup ≥ 2 weeks after the last day of a prior episode, it was considered a separate infection episode. An RT‐PCR–confirmed illness episode was defined as an RT‐PCR–confirmed episode with ≥ 1 nurse‐ or caregiver‐reported symptom documented in the period from one nurse visit before to one visit after the infection episode. An episode of serology‐confirmed infection was defined as a fourfold rise in the ELISA or MIA antibody concentrations on paired serology. For MIA, a four‐fold rise was only considered if the final blood draw titer was ≥ 1 AU/mL to avoid false positives arising from small absolute changes at low baseline titers.

The symptomatic fraction was computed as the number of RT‐PCR–confirmed infections reporting one symptom or more divided by the total number of RT‐PCR–confirmed infection episodes. We assessed factors associated with serology or RT‐PCR–confirmed infection and RT‐PCR–confirmed symptomatic illness using logistic regression and factors associated with shedding duration using Weibull accelerated time failure regression, for which a hazard ratio of < 1 corresponds to prolonged duration of shedding. The selection of variables in the models was based on previous studies of factors associated with outcomes of interest to address potential confounding. Multivariable analysis was not conducted as the sample size was too low. p < 0.05 was considered significant. As a secondary analysis we estimated minimum incidence of serology and RT‐PCR–confirmed infection and symptomatic RT‐PCR–confirmed illness as the number of infection and illness episodes divided by the person time of follow‐up.

We compared the detection of RSV infections using RT‐PCR, ELISA, and MIA. For this subanalysis we excluded children without paired sera and where baseline bloods were collected during/after RT‐PCR–confirmed RSV infection (n = 13). We calculated sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and Cohen's kappa statistic (κ) of RT‐PCR (≥ 1 positive swab), ELISA (fourfold antibody titer rise), and MIA (fourfold antibody rise plus exit concentration ≥ 1 arbitrary units [AU]/mL) to detect recent RSV infection (irrespective of baseline seropositivity) compared to positivity on two or more assays (reference standard). The threshold for MIA positivity was determined within the current study population potentially limiting generalizability; therefore, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using different test combinations as the gold standard [1] RT‐PCR positive and fourfold rise on ELISA [2] RT‐PCR positive or fourfold rise on ELISA [3] Fourfold rise on ELISA only.

Ethical Review

The ISiT protocol was approved by the University of Witwatersrand Human Research Ethics Committee (Medical) (Reference 220104). The CDC relied on the University of Witwatersrand Human Research Ethics Committee review. Written informed consent was obtained from all caregivers prior to data collection. Participants received grocery store vouchers of USD 3 per nurse visit and an additional USD 3 following the weekly parent‐collected swab to compensate for time required for specimen and data collection. After 28 August, caregivers were incentivized with an additional USD ~1.5 or USD ~3 if more than 50% or 80% of weekly symptom diaries were completed, respectively. Caregivers were issued with a study electronic tablet, which was retained by caregivers at the end of the study. Study mobile data were provided through direct credit to mobile numbers.

Results

We screened 230 children for study eligibility and enrolled 100 as participants (Figure S1). Of them, 93 completed their first follow‐up visit and were included in the analysis of incidence and risk factors and 80 children were included in the comparison of detection on RT‐PCR and serology.

The 2022 RSV season began in mid‐February and ended in mid‐June (Figure 1a). Intensive participant follow‐up started in week 19 (week starting May 8, 2022), with some participants starting a week or two later. Baseline blood draws were collected from week 15 (April 13, 2022) to week 23 (June 6, 2022) and final blood draws from week 39 (September 26, 2022) to week 43 (October 24, 2022), with a small number of early withdrawals having final blood collected from 31 August 2022 (Figure 1a,b).

Forty‐seven percent (44/93) of included children were aged 6–11 months and 53% (49/93) were male (Table 1). The median caregiver age was 30 years (range 24–35 years); only 26% (28/93) had completed secondary education and 28% (26/93) of mothers were living with HIV.

Of 93 participants, 87 (94%) were followed to the final study visit. Of 4369 scheduled nurse swabs during follow‐up, a swab was collected for 4245 (97%), and of 2203 scheduled caregiver swabs, 2045 (93%) were collected. The human RNase P gene was detected in > 99% (4239/4245) of nurse‐ and 99% (2032/2045) of caregiver‐collected swabs. RSV was identified on RT‐PCR from 2.6% (166/6290) of collected swabs (Figure 1a,b). Symptom data were available for 97% (4247/4369) of scheduled nurse visits and symptom diaries were completed by caregivers for 67% (9245/13768) of scheduled instances.

Overall, 51 individuals had ≥ 1 episode of RSV infection on RT‐PCR or serology. There were 40 first episodes of infection identified on RT‐PCR (with or without serology) and 11 episodes identified using serology only. Six individuals had a repeat episode of RT‐PCR–confirmed RSV during follow‐up (6/46, 13%) (Figure S2 and Table S1). The median interval between repeat episodes was 26 days (mean 28 days, range 18–46 days). Of 46 episodes of RT‐PCR–confirmed RSV infection, 33 (71.7%) were RSV‐A, 10 (21.7%) were RSV‐B, and subgroup was not identified for 3 (6.5%) due to low viral load (Ct > 35). Four repeat episodes were RSV‐A for both episodes, one was RSV‐B and RSV unsubgrouped and one was RSV‐A and RSV unsubgrouped (Table S1 and Figure S2). The third weekly swab, collected by caregivers on the weekend, added only one additional episode of RT‐PCR–confirmed infection (nurse‐collected twice weekly swabs: 39 episodes versus combined nurse and caregiver swabs: 40 episodes).

Symptomatic Fraction

Combining all data from symptom diaries and nurse visits, one symptom or more was reported from 54% (25/46) of RT‐PCR–confirmed RSV episodes overall (Table 2). The most frequently reported symptoms were blocked or runny nose (22/46, 48%), cough (17/46, 37%), and measured or reported fever (12/46, 26%). A total of 20% (9/46) reported both fever and cough. There were four episodes of medically attended RSV‐associated illness (4/46, 9%), all with cough (meeting definitions of RSV‐associated medically attended respiratory illness [MARI]). One episode was attended at a pharmacy, two at a general practitioner, and one at a hospital outpatient department. In univariate analyses, there were no factors significantly associated with presence of one symptom or more among these children (Table S2).

Duration of Shedding

The median duration of shedding was 7 days (IQR = 4–11 days). Shedding duration was not significantly different for RSV‐A (7 days, IQR = 4–11) and RSV‐B (11 days, IQR = 9–16; hazard ratio (HR) = 0.7, 95% CI = 0.3–1.4) and in individuals with (9 days, IQR = 6–15) and without (7 days, IQR = 3–10) one or more symptoms (HR = 0.5, 95% CI = 0.3–1.1; Table S3 and Figure S3). On bivariate analysis, a minimum PCR cycle threshold value < 25 was associated with shedding duration > 7 days for RSV (HR = 0.5, 95% CI = 0.2–0.8).

Minimum Incidence

The minimum estimated incidence of RSV infection on RT‐PCR or serology in children aged 6–23 months was 60.5 per 100 person seasons (95% CI = 45.1–79.6). Estimated incidence was highest in children aged 18–23 months (66.5, 95% CI = 30.4–126.3), although confidence intervals overlapped (Table S4).

Correspondence of Serology and RT‐PCR

We included 80/93 (86%) enrolled children (excluded four without paired sera and nine where baseline blood was collected during/after RT‐PCR–confirmed RSV infection) in the comparison of detection by RT‐PCR and serology. We detected 29/80 (36%) infections by RT‐PCR, 33/80 (41%) by ELISA, 35/80 (44%) by MIA, and 40/80 (50%) by all assays combined (Figures 2 and 3).

At baseline, 22/80 (28%) children were RSV seropositive on ELISA, six of them (27%) had subsequent RSV infections (baseline ELISA concentration range = 28–143 relative units [RU]/mL): two each positive on RT‐PCR–only, MIA‐only, and RT‐PCR and MIA. Of 29 RT‐PCR detections, 23 (79%) were also detected on both serological assays, two by ELISA only, two by MIA only, and two on neither.

Serology detected 11/80 (14%) infections not identified by RT‐PCR: one by ELISA, three by MIA, and seven by both. Of participants positive on RT‐PCR and MIA, the lowest exit concentration was 23 AU/mL, and of those positive on RT‐PCR and ELISA, the lowest exit concentration was 7 RU/mL. MIA showed the highest sensitivity (97%) with specificity of 93%, followed by ELISA (92% sensitivity, 100% specificity) and RT‐PCR (78% sensitivity, 98% specificity) (Table 3). On sensitivity analysis, exploring different gold standards excluding MIA, MIA sensitivity remained ≥ 95%, but specificity dropped particularly when the gold standard was both ELISA and RT‐PCR positivity (supplementary Table S5).

Conclusions

We found that among healthy children aged 6–23 months in South Africa, approximately half of all RT‐PCR–confirmed infection episodes were symptomatic, but cough was reported in < 40% and fever in only a quarter of episodes. The median shedding duration was 7 days (IQR = 4–11 days). Paired serology performed well compared to RT‐PCR for RSV infection detection.

In our study, approximately half of all RT‐PCR–confirmed infections were associated with one or more symptoms, similar to previous cohort studies of similar design in South Africa and Kenya [4, 18]. Cough was reported in just over a third of infections and fever in approximately a quarter. Only one fifth of episodes met the influenza‐like illness case definition of fever and cough, highlighting that in this age group, a large proportion of cases would be missed with traditional ILI surveillance [19]. Only 16% of symptomatic cases sought medical care. This is higher than in a South African cohort study among individuals of all ages, highlighting the fact that young children are more likely to seek care, likely in part because of increased severity of illness.

Longer shedding duration is associated with increased risk of onward transmission of infection [4]. The median duration of RSV shedding was 7 days, similar to findings from previous studies [4, 20, 21, 22, 23]. The only identified predictor of prolonged shedding duration was a low cycle threshold value (proxy for a high viral load). Previous studies have found symptomatic illness to be associated with prolonged RSV shedding [4, 21, 24], in our study the shedding duration was longer among symptomatic individuals but this did not reach statistical significance, possibly because of the small sample size. Interestingly, age was not associated with symptomatic fraction or shedding duration even though the cohort likely included a mixed group of children with first and second episodes of RSV infection. While a third weekly swab did not identify a substantial number of additional infection episodes, it may be useful for more precise estimation of shedding duration. We assumed that shedding duration was from the first to the last day of RT‐PCR–positive swabs, but individuals could have been shedding the day before or the day after swab collection. In addition, while completeness of swabbing was high, it was not 100%. If missed swabs occurred the visit before or after an episode, this could have led to underestimation of episode duration. Therefore, shedding duration may be an underestimate.

Studies of children presenting for clinical care have found that paired serology can increase RSV detection by approximately 10% [25]. We found that approximately a quarter of infections were identified on serologic testing only, with the MIA identifying more cases than the ELISA. Our finding of a higher proportion of infections detected on serology only could be, in part, because of the study timing, as intensive follow‐up began around the peak of transmission. For this reason, infections may have been missed on RT‐PCR but could possibly have been detected on serology, due to the time taken to mount a serologic response. Encouragingly, there was only a single episode identified on RT‐PCR, which was missed on serology, suggesting that if resources are limited for frequent RT‐PCR follow‐up, serology (particularly MIA) may be a useful approach to identify infections when estimating RSV incidence. MIAs offer the benefits of using very small volumes of sample, allowing simultaneous detection of antibodies to multiple pathogens and the higher surface area on beads allows better antigen/antibody binding [14, 26, 27]. The high sensitivity and specificity of serology and lower cost, compared to twice‐weekly swabbing, suggests that it may be a useful adjunct for identification of infections, for example, in household members where resources do not allow frequent RT‐PCR testing. However, our analysis might have been biased by missed RT‐PCR confirmed infections before the start of follow‐up. The youngest children enrolled were 6 months of age by which time maternal antibodies would be expected to have waned or be waning; therefore, it is unlikely that serologically identified infections reflected maternal antibodies.

Although our analysis of incidence was limited by the fact that cohort follow‐up began at approximately the peak of the RSV season, we found a minimum incidence of RSV infection of over 60 infection episodes per 100 person seasons. This is similar to rates identified in a large South African cohort study in two communities before the SARS‐CoV‐2 pandemic as well as an earlier Kenyan study with a similar study design, but lower than a US study, which did not use PCR for diagnosis [4, 13, 20]. The rate of MARI in our cohort was 4.7 (95% CI = 1.3–12.2) per 100 person seasons, similar to the rate of medically‐attended mild illness from a previous study of RSV burden in South Africa [2]. The high observed incidence of infection in children aged 6–23 months suggests that vaccines in this age group could potentially have a large impact, particularly if they reduce onward transmission.

A major limitation of our study was the late start to follow‐up because the main study was focused on influenza and the RSV season in South Africa precedes the influenza season [3]. This meant that the timing of serum sample collection was not optimal and the ability to identify infections on RT‐PCR was limited as it is likely that several infections may have occurred prior to the start of follow‐up. Our estimates of incidence are therefore a minimum incidence, and this may also have affected our comparison of serology and RT‐PCR as serology may have been able to identify some infections, which occurred before the start of follow‐up. Caregivers may have varied in their ability to identify symptoms. We excluded children with self‐limiting respiratory illness at baseline, which could have biased our results. Our study population may not have been representative of the general population given greater likelihood of health‐seeking given the recruitment strategy. Our study was conducted in the year after the COVID‐19 pandemic restrictions ended, which could have been an atypical RSV season, but according to surveillance data, in 2022, RSV epidemiology had returned to prepandemic patterns in South Africa. We did not perform virus culture and therefore were only able to examine duration of RT‐PCR detection rather than shedding of viable virus. In our cohort, six individuals experienced repeat infections; only one of these was confirmed to be due to a different subgroup. We could not perform sequencing of repeat infections due to high Ct values; therefore, we cannot exclude that some of these might represent prolonged shedding of the same virus, although this is unlikely because in all instances there were several weeks of negative swabs on RT‐PCR between episodes.

Approximately half of all identified RSV infections were symptomatic and median shedding duration was 7 days. Despite limitations, the minimum RSV attack rate was high. This supports a likely important role of this group in onward transmission and suggests that vaccination in this age group could have important public health benefits. We identified high correspondence RT‐PCR and paired serology for identification of infections. Platforms suitable for the evaluation of the efficacy of current and new RSV vaccines against infection versus illness and duration of shedding for breakthrough and repeat infections in vaccinated individuals are needed to support the effort to develop improved vaccines [28, 29]. Such platforms could also provide important information on correlates of protection against infection and illness. In this cohort study we achieved over 90% follow‐up and compliance with repeated nasal swabs, suggesting feasibility to take this platform forward for clinical trials of RSV vaccine efficacy against infection and illness. Given the mismatch of our study with the RSV season, further studies, which are optimally timed for the RSV season, are needed to confirm our findings. Future studies could also explore the use of repeated serologic testing in household members as a more cost‐effective approach to ascertaining infections as well as expanded use of caregiver‐administered swabs.

Ethics Statement

The ISIT protocol was approved by the University of Witwatersrand Human Research Ethics Committee (Medical) (Reference 220104). Written informed consent was obtained from all caregivers prior to data collection. Participants received grocery store vouchers of USD 3 per nurse visit and an additional USD 3 following the weekly parent‐collected swab to compensate for time required for specimen and data collection. After August 28, caregivers were incentivized with an additional USD ~1.5 or USD ~3 if more than 50% or 80% of weekly symptom diaries were completed, respectively. Caregivers were issued with a study electronic tablet, which was retained by caregivers at the end of the study. Study mobile data were provided through direct credit to mobile numbers.
