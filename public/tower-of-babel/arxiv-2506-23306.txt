GATSim: Urban Mobility Simulation with Generative Agents
Qi Liu, Can Li, Wanjing Ma

Abstract
Traditional agent-based urban mobility simulations rely on rigid rule-based systems that fail to capture the complexity, adaptability, and behavioral diversity characteristic of human travel decision-making. Recent advances in large language models and AI agent technology offer opportunities to create agents with reasoning capabilities, persistent memory, and adaptive learning mechanisms. We propose GATSim (Generative-Agent Transport Simulation), a novel framework that leverages these advances to create generative agents with rich behavioral characteristics for urban mobility simulation. Unlike conventional approaches, GATSim agents possess diverse socioeconomic attributes, individual lifestyles, and evolving preferences that shape their mobility decisions through psychologically-informed memory systems, tool usage capabilities, and lifelong learning mechanisms. The main contributions of this study include: (1) a comprehensive architecture combining an urban mobility foundation model with agent cognitive systems and transport simulation environment, (2) a fully functional prototype implementation, and (3) systematic validation demonstrating that generative agents produce believable travel behaviors. Through designed reflection processes, generative agents in this study can transform specific travel experiences into generalized insights, enabling realistic behavioral adaptation over time with specialized mechanisms for activity planning and real-time reactive behaviors tailored to urban mobility contexts. Experiments show that generative agents perform competitively with human annotators in mobility scenarios while naturally producing macroscopic traffic evolution patterns. The code for the prototype system is shared at https://github.com/qiliuchn/gatsim .



1
Introduction

Agent-based modeling and simulation is a powerful approach for modeling complex systems, including urban traffic, epidemic spread, and market dynamics. In the domain of urban planning and traffic management, transportation simulation systems play a crucial role by enabling researchers and policymakers to model complex mobility patterns and evaluate infrastructure changes. While existing agent-based urban traffic simulation systems like
MATSim
have demonstrated significant capabilities in modeling large-scale traffic flows, they face fundamental limitations in capturing the complexity and adaptability of human travel behavior.

Traditional simulation approaches rely on predefined expert rules for agent behavior, creating several critical limitations. First, this mechanistic approach lacks the flexibility and diversity characteristic of real human decision-making — when agents follow rigid, predetermined rules, they cannot accurately reflect the complex reasoning processes of actual travelers, particularly in response to unexpected events or dynamic conditions. Second, these systems struggle to model social interactions and coordination behaviors among multiple agents, as complex social dynamics resist simple rule-based formulations. Third, rule-based systems suffer from poor generalization capability — creating new scenarios requires extensive scenario-specific programming, demanding substantial expert effort and limiting widespread adoption.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) offer a transformative opportunity to address these limitations. The pioneering work of Park et al.
[
POC+23
]
introduced “generative agents” with memory, planning, and social capabilities, demonstrating the feasibility of using LLMs to create more realistic simulation agents. These breakthroughs enable a paradigm shift from “rule-based agents” to “intelligence-based agents” in transportation modeling, where agents can adaptively reason about complex situations, learn from experiences, and coordinate naturally with others.

Building on this foundation, we present
GATSim (Generative-Agent Transport Simulation)
, a novel framework that leverages large language models to create autonomous agents with rich behavioral characteristics for urban mobility simulation. Unlike conventional approaches, GATSim agents possess diverse socioeconomic attributes, individual lifestyles, and evolving preferences that shape their mobility decisions through psychologically-informed memory systems, tool usage capabilities, and lifelong learning mechanisms. This paper makes three primary contributions to the field of transportation simulation:

1.

A comprehensive framework for generative-agent-based traffic simulation
: We present GATSim, a novel architecture that integrates LLM-based agents with urban mobility simulation environments. The framework includes an urban mobility foundation model for population synthesis and behavior generation, agent cognitive architectures with hierarchical memory systems, and transport simulation environment.

2.

A working prototype implementation
: We develop and release a fully functional prototype system that demonstrates the framework’s capabilities. The implementation includes population synthesis using off-the-shelf LLMs, multi-modal memory management, activity planning and reactive behavior generation, and a graphical user interface for visualization and interaction.

3.

Validation of generative agent behavior
: We conduct systematic experiments to validate both microscopic and macroscopic simulation outcomes. Our validation includes human-agent behavioral comparison studies using LLM-as-judge evaluation, demonstration of emergent traffic patterns including realistic peak spreading and user equilibrium-like states, and analysis of agent learning and adaptation mechanisms through experience-based reflection.

Through this comprehensive methodology, GATSim contributes to the paradigm shift from rule-based to intelligence-based traffic simulation, providing a more realistic, flexible, and user-friendly framework for urban transportation modeling and analysis. Our results demonstrate that generative agents can produce believable travel behaviors comparable to human decision-makers while naturally exhibiting emergent system-level patterns without explicit behavior coding.

Traditional Traffic Simulation Methods

Traffic simulation methodologies have evolved from macroscopic to increasingly microscopic approaches over several decades. The Four-Step Model

However, these traditional methods face fundamental limitations that restrict their ability to capture realistic human behavior:

•

Limited behavioral diversity
: Conventional methods struggle to represent the heterogeneous socioeconomic attributes and travel preferences that drive individual mobility decisions.

•

Rigid decision-making
: Rule-based agents lack adaptability to novel situations and cannot exhibit the flexible reasoning characteristic of human travelers.

•

Poor generalization
: Behaviors are typically scenario-specific and require extensive reprogramming for new contexts or geographic areas.

•

Absence of social dynamics
: Traditional agents operate independently without mechanisms for coordination, negotiation, or social learning.

2.4
Research Gaps and Opportunities

Despite significant progress, existing approaches face critical limitations that prevent comprehensive urban mobility simulation:

Fragmented Focus
: Current studies predominantly address isolated aspects such as prediction accuracy or simple behavior generation, lacking integrated frameworks that combine realistic population synthesis, sophisticated behavioral modeling, and emergent system dynamics.

Limited Behavioral Complexity
: Existing systems fail to capture the full spectrum of human decision-making processes, including spatial-temporal memory utilization, adaptive learning from experience.

Inadequate Social Dynamics
: Most approaches treat agents as independent entities, missing the collaborative coordination, information sharing, and social influence that characterize real urban mobility systems.

Our work addresses these gaps by presenting a comprehensive framework that integrates sophisticated agent cognition with scalable simulation architecture, enabling realistic behavioral diversity while maintaining computational tractability for practical applications.

Model

This section presents the GATSim framework and prototype system. The GATSim framework integrates three core components: an urban mobility foundation model for population synthesis and behavior generation (“LLM”), generative agents with cognitive architectures (“Agent”), and a simulation environment that manages system dynamics and user interactions (“Environemnt”), as illustrated in Figure
1
. This modular design enables flexible integration of different LLM and simulation environment backends while maintaining a consistent simulation interface, designed to support large-scale simulations while preserving individual agent behavioral richness. The urban mobility foundation model serves dual purposes: generating synthetic population data and functioning as the cognitive engine for agent behavior generation. It should integrate multiple data modalities and domain knowledge to produce realistic population distributions and mobility patterns.

For rapid prototyping and framework verification, we employ off-the-shelf LLMs without domain-specific fine-tuning. The prototype uses GPT-4o for population synthesis and Qwen3 for agent behavioral modeling, representing state-of-the-art closed-source and open-source models respectively at the time of writing. Future versions will incorporate fine-tuned models optimized for urban mobility simulation tasks.

The

3.3
Agent Memory

The memory system represents a critical component of generative agents, enabling learning from experience, preference formation, and adaptive decision-making over time. Our implementation goes beyond simple information storage to create a cognitive architecture that mirrors human memory processes while addressing the unique requirements of urban mobility simulation.

3.3.1
Memory Structure and Organization

The prototype implements a hierarchical memory structure inspired by cognitive psychology, comprising short-term and long-term memory components with distinct but interconnected functions. Short-term memory serves as the agent’s working cognitive space, maintaining dynamically updated information for the current simulation day including activity plans, ongoing states, travel paths, and immediate perceptions. This component operates with limited capacity following Miller’s cognitive constraints
[
Mil56
]
, forcing agents to prioritize relevant information and creating realistic bounded rationality in decision-making.

Long-term memory stores accumulated information across extended time horizons, forming the foundation for behavioral learning and adaptation. To unify representation of diverse experience types—perceived events, social conversations, and internal reflections—we employ a ConceptNode class structure where each node encapsulates comprehensive attributes including textual content, semantic embeddings, extracted keywords, spatial coverage, temporal scope, importance scores, creation timestamps, and access histories.

Unlike previous implementations maintaining separate spatial memory structures
[
POC+23
]
, our unified approach integrates spatial-temporal information directly into long-term memory. This architectural decision enables holistic reasoning across information types, allowing agents to seamlessly correlate traffic patterns with social interactions and personal events. For instance, an agent reflecting on metro delays can connect congestion patterns with conversations about service disruptions or memories of alternative routes discovered during similar situations. The unified structure simplifies retrieval mechanisms by enabling comprehensive memory search without requiring separate processing pipelines for different memory types. Most importantly, storing spatial-temporal events alongside personal experiences and social information enables sophisticated cross-modal reasoning for adaptive behavior, allowing agents to develop richer behavioral patterns that reflect real-world decision-making complexity.

3.3.2
Memory Generation and Content Creation

The memory system generates content through multiple pathways that capture comprehensive aspects of the urban mobility experience. Travel experience recording automatically captures significant mobility events including encountered congestion levels, trip durations compared to expectations, mode-specific experiences such as transit crowding, and notable incidents or delays. These episodic memories form the foundation for route learning and mode preference evolution through intelligent filtering that records only meaningful experiences while avoiding memory saturation with routine events.

Social interaction documentation generates memory traces from communications between agents, capturing information exchange, coordination outcomes, and social learning processes. The memoroy system can tracks relationship dynamics, trust building, and influence patterns that affect future coordination decisions, enabling realistic social influence propagation in mobility choices.

Reflective insight generation synthesizes multiple experiences into higher-level understanding through periodic reflection processes. These create semantic memories about travel patterns, reliability assessments, and behavioral strategies that guide future decision-making more efficiently than raw experiences. Reflective insights represent emergent knowledge that arises from experience accumulation rather than direct observation, enabling agents to develop sophisticated behavioral heuristics over time.

Planning decision recording documents the reasoning process during activity plan creation and revision, capturing rationales behind choices, considered alternatives, and constraint trade-offs. This decision history enables agents to learn from past planning successes and failures, developing personalized planning strategies that reflect individual preferences and environmental constraints.

The comprehensive memory generation ensures agents develop rich, multifaceted understanding of their urban environment, supporting both immediate reactive behaviors and long-term behavioral adaptation.

3.3.3
Multi-Modal Memory Retrieval

Effective retrieval mechanisms are fundamental to memory system functionality, enabling agents to access relevant past experiences for informed decision-making. The prototype implements a multi-modal retrieval framework combining three complementary strategies that address different aspects of memory relevance.

Keyword-based retrieval employs set-theoretic matching using the
Jaccard similarity coefficient
to identify memories with relevant keywords, supporting precise recall of specific events or locations. The Jaccard coefficient balances precision and recall by considering both intersection and union of keyword sets:

\text{keyword\_score}(K_{q},K_{m})=\frac{|K_{q}\cap K_{m}|}{|K_{q}\cup K_{m}|}

(1)

where
K_{q}
and
K_{m}
represent the keyword sets of the query and memory respectively. This approach penalizes memories with excessive irrelevant keywords while rewarding those with focused, relevant content.

Semantic similarity retrieval leverages embedding-based search using FAISS (Facebook AI Similarity Search) for efficient vector-based operations. Content embeddings are generated using pre-trained language models (
SentenceTransformer
with the model
all-MiniLM-L6-v2
), with semantic similarity computed as normalized cosine distance:

\text{semantic\_score}(q,m)=1.0-\frac{d(e_{q},e_{m})}{d_{\max}}

(2)

where
e_{q}
and
e_{m}
are the embeddings of the query and memory content respectively, enabling agents to generalize from past experiences through conceptual relationships.

Spatial-temporal relevance retrieval addresses the inherently spatial-temporal nature of urban mobility decisions through specialized mechanisms. We adopt the
Overlap Coefficient (Szymkiewicz–Simpson coefficient)
for its desirable property that subset relationships yield perfect scores:

\text{Overlap}(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{\min(|A|,|B|)}

(3)

This metric applies separately to spatial and temporal dimensions, computing spatial relevance for memories with spatial scopes
\mathcal{S}_{q}
and
\mathcal{S}_{m}
and temporal relevance for time intervals
\mathcal{T}_{q}
and
\mathcal{T}_{m}
. The combined spatial-temporal relevance multiplies these scores, ensuring memories relevant to both location and timing receive highest priority.

\text{spatiotemporal\_score}(q,m)=\text{spatial\_score}(q,m)\times\text{temporal\_score}(q,m)

(4)

The retrieval system also incorporates recency scoring through exponential decay modeling, recognizing that recent experiences often carry greater relevance for immediate decisions:

\text{recency\_score}(m)=\lambda^{t_{m}}

(5)

where
t_{m}
represents time elapsed since memory creation or access, and
\lambda\in(0,1)
controls decay rate. The system defaults to exponential decay as it naturally models the rapid fading of routine events while preserving significant experiences, with recommended values of 0.90 for recent pattern emphasis or 0.95 for medium-term habit preservation. Table

3.3.4
Psychologically-Informed Forgetting Mechanisms

Realistic memory decay modeling prevents unbounded memory growth while reflecting psychological principles governing human memory retention. Each memory entry receives an expiration attribute determining its lifespan based on non-linear relationships with importance scores, reflecting the well-established principle that salient experiences persist longer than mundane ones
[
AS91
,
WE91
]
. The importance score criteria is shared at
gatsim/technical_highlights
. The lifespan calculation employs power functions aligning with empirical findings on human memory retention:

\text{lifespan}_{\text{init}}=\text{lifespan}_{\text{min}}+(\text{lifespan}_{\text{max}}-\text{lifespan}_{\text{min}})\times\text{importance}^{\text{power}}

(7)

Parameters are tuned to each memory type’s distinct characteristics. Event memories use minimum lifespans of 2 hours and maximum of 4 days with power 2.4, allowing transient irrelevant events to fade quickly while enabling important ones to persist for pattern learning. Conversational memories employ 4-hour minimums and 2-day maximums with power 3.2, reflecting that most coordination content are relevant but only pertains to immediate needs, while highly important exchanges still warrant extended retention. Reflective insights use 8-hour minimums and 8-day maximums with power 1.6, enabling learned knowledge to guide behavior over longer timescales (see Figure
3
.

This parameterization ensures behaviorally appropriate retention where mundane memories expire within hours, routine experiences persist for 1-2 days, and critical insights remain accessible for extended planning horizons. This approach mirrors power-law forgetting curves observed in psychological studies
[
WE91
]
where retention probability follows
P(t)\propto t^{-\beta}
. Memory retrieval during reasoning or reflection extends expiration by the initial lifespan, implementing the spacing effect observed in human memory
[
CPV+06
]
. This ensures frequently accessed memories persist longer, mirroring how rehearsal strengthens retention while maintaining computational efficiency through periodic pruning when memory stores exceed capacity thresholds.

3.3.5
Memory-Driven Behavioral Adaptation

This memory architecture enables several forms of behavioral adaptation that distinguish generative agents from traditional rule-based systems. Preference evolution occurs as agents accumulate experiences with different travel modes, routes, and activity patterns, leading to gradual shifts in behavioral tendencies that reflect individual learning rather than fixed parameters. Route knowledge develops through repeated exposure to network conditions, enabling agents to build mental models of typical congestion patterns, reliability assessments, and alternative options that inform future travel decisions. Social learning emerges through memory integration of information shared by other agents, creating realistic diffusion of travel strategies, route discoveries, and mode preferences through social networks. The memory system enables agents to develop relationships that influence future coordination decisions. The memory architecture thus serves not merely as information storage but as the foundation for realistic behavioral learning that enables agents to develop sophisticated, personalized travel strategies through experience accumulation. This capability represents a fundamental advance over traditional simulation approaches and demonstrates the potential for generative agents to capture the adaptive nature of human mobility behavior in urban environments.

3.4
Agent Activity Planning and Reactive Behavior

The activity planning and reactive behavior represent core capabilities that distinguish generative agents from traditional rule-based approaches. These interconnected systems enable agents to routinely develop daily plans while maintaining the flexibility to adapt to dynamic conditions, mirroring the complex decision-making processes observed in human mobility behavior.

3.4.1
Activity Planning Process

The activity planning process operates through a hierarchical cognitive architecture that integrates multiple information sources and reasoning stages. Each generative agent initiates daily planning at the start of a day by synthesizing contextual inputs including long-term reflections summarizing personal experiences over the past week, daily reflections from recent experiences, and perceived broadcast events representing shared external occurrences that may influence decisions. This contextual foundation ensures planning decisions reflect both individual learning history and current environmental conditions.

Long-term reflections encapsulate key personal experiences encompassing work-related tasks, social interactions, and family affairs while containing anticipations of upcoming events. These reflections serve as persistent behavioral context that evolves through experience accumulation, enabling agents to develop increasingly sophisticated planning strategies over time. Broadcast events represent shared external occurrences such as museum exhibitions, weather alerts, or infrastructure disruptions that create common awareness across the agent population, enabling realistic collective responses to major events. Long-term reflection generate concepts like: “I haven’t spent time with my family for a week; maybe I should plan a visit with them to an amusement park this weekend”; “St_1 is always congested during Monday morning rush hour; I should avoid it”.

The planning process incorporates social coordination through collaborative decision-making where agents may initiate conversations with relevant parties before finalizing plans. For instance, household members might discuss school pickup responsibilities or friends might coordinate post-work activities. These interactions model the cooperative decision-making processes important to real-world mobility planning, where individual choices often depend on coordination with others. Conversation summaries are added to the memories of both parties, ensuring social agreements influence final plan generation.

The reasoning and planning stage employs LLM-based processing that synthesizes all contextual inputs to generate comprehensive activity plans. This approach enables agents to consider complex trade-offs between competing objectives, temporal constraints, social obligations, and personal preferences in ways that traditional optimization-based methods cannot capture. The LLM-based planner can handle novel situations and unexpected combinations of constraints without requiring pre-programmed rules for every possible scenario. The initial activity plan consists of a chronologically ordered sequence of activities the agent intends to perform throughout the day, with each activity represented through a structured tuple containing facility destination, departure time, reflection frequency, travel mode, preferred path, and descriptive content. The departure time specification enables flexible temporal coordination where activities may have explicit times or conditional timing based on previous activity completion. This flexibility accommodates the reality that many travel decisions depend on variable activity durations and cannot be precisely scheduled in advance. Activities with uncertain departure times begin immediately upon arrival at the previous facility, enabling realistic chaining of related activities such as dropping off children before proceeding to work. The reflection frequency parameter
reflect_every
governs the temporal granularity of plan reconsideration during ongoing activities, balancing behavioral realism with computational efficiency. This mechanism recognizes that humans periodically reassess their plans while acknowledging that different activities warrant different levels of cognitive monitoring. The system implements activity-dependent reflection frequencies with global minimum intervals of 30 minutes for general activities and extended intervals of 150 minutes for work activities, reflecting the longer duration and lower interruption tolerance typical of professional tasks. Path specification allows agents to express routing preferences ranging from explicit route choices reflecting local knowledge to shortest-path delegation emulating navigation application usage. This flexibility enables agents to exhibit realistic routing behavior that varies based on familiarity, urgency, and personal preferences while maintaining computational efficiency for routine decisions.

Concept nodes generated during planning represent knowledge, intentions, or preferences that emerge from the reasoning process, providing valuable additions to long-term memory that support future decision-making. These concepts capture the intermediate reasoning steps and insights that inform plan generation, enabling agents to learn from their planning processes and develop increasingly sophisticated strategies over time.

Updated long-term reflections capture how current plans build upon or respond to prior experiences, enabling cumulative behavioral modeling throughout the simulation. The planning and reflection processes are intertwined in the prototype. This joint design mirrors natural human cognition — updating one’s agenda often goes hand-in-hand with evaluating past outcomes. Coupling reflection and planning also reduces computational cost.

3.4.2
Dynamic Plan Revision and Adaptation

Agents possess capabilities for plan revision during simulation execution, enabling realistic responses to changing conditions while maintaining computational tractability. The revision process operates through carefully designed trigger mechanisms that balance responsiveness with efficiency, recognizing that excessive plan revision would be both computationally expensive and behaviorally unrealistic.

Plan revision opportunities occur at strategically selected moments including: 1) simulation day initiation; 2) waiting periods at network nodes such as traffic intersections or transit stops; 3) periodic intervals during ongoing activities based on reflection frequencies; and 4) natural transition points when activities conclude. These revision opportunities reflect realistic decision-making patterns where people reconsider their plans during natural breaks rather than continuously monitoring and adjusting their schedules. The revision process operates through a four-stage cognitive pipeline that mirrors human decision-making processes while leveraging the reasoning capabilities of large language models. The perception stage

Memory retrieval stage

The retrieval mechanisms described in the memory architecture section enable agents to identify analogous situations from their experience history, supporting decision-making through case-based reasoning and learned heuristics. This capability allows agents to benefit from accumulated experience when facing novel situations, leading to increasingly sophisticated behavioral responses over time.

Interaction stage

Behavioral responses ranging from plan continuation when no changes are needed to comprehensive plan replacement when major disruptions occur. Intermediate options include path updates to reflect real-time routing preferences, departure time adjustments to accommodate changed conditions, and partial plan modifications to address specific constraints while maintaining overall daily objectives.

3.4.3
Perception and Environmental Awareness

The perception system serves as the sensory interface between agents and their environment, gathering diverse information types that inform both planning and reactive decisions. The system operates through continuous monitoring of multiple information channels while employing intelligent filtering to manage cognitive load and focus attention on relevant stimuli.

Spatial perception monitors nearby network events and conditions within the agent’s immediate vicinity, enabling awareness of local traffic incidents, facility conditions, and other agents’ activities. This localized awareness supports realistic behavioral responses to immediate environmental conditions while avoiding the computational overhead of global monitoring.

Social perception maintains awareness of family member states, friend activities, and coworker conditions, enabling realistic social coordination and information sharing. This social awareness operates through relationship networks that determine information sharing patterns and trust levels, creating realistic information propagation dynamics that affect collective mobility behavior patterns.

Broadcast perception receives system-wide announcements including weather alerts, major events, infrastructure disruptions, and public information campaigns. This information channel enables coordinated responses to large-scale events while modeling the realistic information dissemination patterns that occur through official communication channels.

The perception system integrates seamlessly with the memory architecture to avoid redundant storage while ensuring novel information receives appropriate attention. This integration enables agents to distinguish between familiar and novel stimuli, focusing cognitive resources on information that genuinely warrants consideration for plan revision or behavioral adaptation.

3.4.4
Multi-Scale Reflection and Learning

Here we review on the reflection system to emphasis that it operates at multiple temporal scales to support both immediate adaptation and long-term learning, creating a comprehensive framework for behavioral development over time. This multi-scale approach mirrors human cognitive processes where immediate experiences inform short-term decisions while accumulated patterns shape long-term behavioral strategies.

Immediate reflection
occurs during reactive decision-making, enabling agents to synthesize current conditions with past experiences to generate contextually appropriate responses. This reflection process creates concept nodes that capture the reasoning behind specific decisions, building a detailed history of decision-making contexts and outcomes that support future learning.

Daily reflection
processes synthesize experiences from completed simulation days, identifying patterns, successful strategies, and areas for improvement. These reflections create higher-level insights that transcend specific incidents, enabling agents to develop generalized knowledge about traffic patterns, route reliability, and mode effectiveness that inform future planning decisions.

Long-term reflection
maintains evolving summaries of behavioral patterns and preferences that persist across multiple simulation days. This reflection level enables the development of stable behavioral tendencies while maintaining flexibility for adaptation to changing conditions.

4.1.3
Simulation Constraints and Simplifications

To maintain experimental control while demonstrating core framework capabilities, several simplifications are implemented in this prototype evaluation:

Travel Mode Consistency
: Travel mode is determined at the beginning of each simulation day and remains fixed throughout, reflecting common daily patterns where individuals typically commit to driving or using transit for their primary activities.

Children Mobility Modeling
: Children’s independent mobility is not explicitly simulated; instead, their activities are implicitly represented through parental coordination behaviors such as school drop-off and pick-up activities. This approach captures the essential mobility impacts while reducing computational complexity.

Network Scale
: The 13-node network, while smaller than real urban systems, provides sufficient complexity for demonstrating agent learning, social coordination, and emergent traffic patterns while enabling detailed behavioral observation and validation.

These simplifications do not fundamentally limit the framework’s applicability to larger, more complex scenarios but enable focused evaluation of core generative agent capabilities in urban mobility contexts.

4.2
Evaluation Framework

Agent-based simulation evaluation requires assessment at multiple scales to capture both individual behavioral realism and system-level dynamics. Our evaluation framework addresses two complementary levels:

Microscopic Evaluation
: Assesses individual agent decision-making quality, behavioral consistency, and human-like reasoning patterns through direct comparison with human annotators in role-playing scenarios.

Macroscopic Evaluation
: Examines emergent system-level patterns including traffic flow evolution, peak spreading phenomena, and equilibrium-seeking behaviors that arise from individual agent learning and adaptation.

This dual-level approach ensures that the framework produces both believable individual behaviors and realistic aggregate patterns, addressing the primary requirements for practical transportation simulation applications.

4.3
Microscopic Behavior Validation

This section evaluates the realism of individual agent decision-making by comparing generative agent behaviors with human responses in controlled mobility scenarios. The evaluation focuses on two key capabilities: reactive decision-making in dynamic conditions and behavioral learning through experience accumulation.

4.3.1
Experimental Design

We conducted a systematic comparison between generative agents and human annotators using a role-playing experimental framework. Five human participants were recruited as annotators, each presented with 10 mobility scenarios carefully selected from the simulation for a total of 50 evaluation cases. Each scenario included comprehensive contextual information: simulation environment description, agent role profile, real-time traffic conditions, environmental perceptions, and retrieved memory content.

Both generative agents and human annotators were asked to provide two outputs for each scenario: (1) a reflection capturing their reasoning process and situational assessment, and (2) a concrete action plan for immediate next steps. To ensure unbiased evaluation, we employed GPT-o1 as an independent judge
[
ZCS+23b
]
, randomly permuting the order of AI and human responses to eliminate positional bias effects. The LLM judge outputs whether the first response, second response, is more realistic, or a “tie”.

Note that we are comparing generative agents with human annotators under role-playing tasks rather than comparing humans with their actual profiles and observed real-world behaviors. This choice reflects practical constraints in transportation research. Recruiting diverse populations to participate in extended mobility simulations using their actual profiles is challenging and raises privacy concerns. Role-playing by trained annotators provides a controlled, replicable benchmark that captures human reasoning patterns while maintaining experimental validity.

4.3.3
Comparative Performance Results

The quantitative evaluation revealed competitive performance between generative agents and human annotators. Of the 50 scenarios evaluated, generative agents were judged more realistic in 23 cases (46.0%), human annotators prevailed in 20 cases (40.0%), and 7 cases (14.0%) resulted in ties. This translates to generative agents performing at least as well as human annotators in 60.0% of comparisons.

The slight advantage for generative agents can be attributed to their superior ability to process and synthesize extensive contextual information. Human annotators frequently struggled with the volume of information presented, including detailed activity histories, event logs, social interaction records, and environmental conditions. Generative agents demonstrated consistent attention to relevant details while generating responses in seconds compared to the several minutes required by human participants.

4.3.5
Identified Limitations and Error Analysis

Detailed examination of agent responses revealed six categories of systematic errors that highlight current limitations and areas for improvement:

•

Hallucination Problem
: Generative agents may generate reflections on non-existent traffic conditions. As shown in List
4
, an agent reflects that “traffic on Ave_3 has been consistently congested during the morning commute” on the first day of simulation, despite having no prior experience with this route.

•

Instruction Following Problem
: Generative agents may fail to follow instructions properly. For example, in List
5
, an agent repeatedly chatted about the same event with the same person despite explicit instructions to avoid repetitive conversations.

•

Commonsense Violations
: Generative agents may generate actions that violate basic commonsense, such as planning to eat lunch at 19:00 or scheduling activities for time periods that have already passed (see List
6
).

•

Reasoning Deficits
: Generative agents sometimes fail to make reasonable judgments and decisions. Examples in List
7
include an agent attempting to avoid congestion unrelated to their planned route, another agent allocating insufficient time for a round trip to the Museum during lunch break, and frequent generation of invalid paths. This suggests limited spatial understanding capabilities in the underlying LLM.

•

Multi-agent Coordination Failures
: We experimented with multi-agent coordination scenarios, such as parents coordinating school drop-off and pick-up activities for their children. Multi-agent coordination frequently failed. As shown in List
8
, two parents schedule duplicate pick-up activities for their only child at different times.

•

Over-responsiveness and Submissiveness Behavior
: In our experiments, generative agents are often overly responsive to network events (such as exhibitions or entertainment events), despite being assigned different personality traits and preferences. Additionally, generative agents tend to be excessively accommodating, rarely rejecting proposals from family members or friends. This behavior likely stems from the fact that most LLMs are pre-trained to be helpful assistants, and this characteristic persists even when instructed to adopt different personas.

While these limitations stem from fundamental challenges in contemporary LLM architectures and training paradigms rather than inherent framework deficiencies, they nonetheless indicate promising avenues for future model development and specialized fine-tuning methodologies. Despite these constraints, we propose several practical interventions below that can substantially alleviate these issues within current technological bounds.

4.3.6
Proposed Remedies

To address the identified limitations, we propose a comprehensive set of practical strategies that can be implemented within current technological constraints:

•

Leveraging Advanced Base Models
: Employing LLMs with enhanced reasoning capabilities represents the most direct and convenient approach to improving generative agent performance. We experimented with different versions of Qwen models and found that agents using the
qwen_max
model generate more reasonable activity plans and exhibit significantly better coordinated behavior compared to the smaller
qwen_plus
model, albeit at the cost of increased latency. Concurrent API calls and batched processing can effectively mitigate latency concerns while preserving quality improvements.

•

Iterative Activity Plan Validation
: Implementing systematic plan review processes enables agents to self-correct errors through multiple validation rounds. For example, an agent initially generated a plan to use transit for the entire day except for one midday activity; after multiple checks, the agent successfully identified and corrected this inconsistency. Generative agents may occasionally forget to include the “return home” activity at the end of their plan, but they typically remedy this oversight before day’s end. Since agents naturally adapt plans throughout the day, perfect initial planning is unnecessary—continuous refinement proves more effective and realistic.

•

Task Decomposition
: Generative agents using smaller LLMs (such as 7B parameter models) struggle to simultaneously generate reflections and daily activity plans with consistent vehicle usage and task coordination in a single step. When practical constraints necessitate using smaller LLMs, we recommend decomposing the task into smaller components, such as reflection, activity facility decision, travel mode decision, and departure time decision etc. Even for agents with larger LLMs, task decomposition remains beneficial, as demonstrated by the success of Chain-of-Thought reasoning
[
WWS+22
]
.

•

Prompt Optimization
: Long contextual inputs can overwhelm agent attention mechanisms, degrading instruction adherence despite expanded context windows in modern LLMs. Prompt engineering techniques can improve generative agent performance, such as placing important and frequently “forgotten” instructions at the end of prompts and incorporating redundancy for critical instructions.

•

Fine-tuning for Behavioral Alignment
: Hallucination presents a dual challenge—enabling creative behavioral diversity while maintaining behavior grounded in actual experiences. Fine-tuning offers a promising approach to reducing unwanted hallucination while simultaneously enhancing behavioral diversity.

•

Rule-based Validation
: Implementing automated validation for critical domain constraints (e.g. vehicle usage consistency) provides immediate error detection without requiring model modifications. When violations occur, agents receive specific feedback prompting plan regeneration. This lightweight approach offers immediate practical benefits with minimal implementation overhead.

•

Appropriate Abstraction Level Selection
: Effective simulation requires strategic decisions about modeling granularity based on research objectives and computational resources. Key considerations include: vehicle sharing mechanisms within households, agent perception radius and frequency, social network complexity and evolution, and temporal resolution for activity scheduling. These choices fundamentally shape both computational requirements and behavioral realism, requiring careful calibration for specific application domains.

4.4
Macroscopic Pattern Validation

This experiment examines emergent system-level traffic patterns arising from individual agent learning and adaptation over multiple simulation days. The analysis focuses on how distributed decision-making by autonomous agents produces realistic aggregate traffic dynamics without centralized coordination.

4.4.1
Experimental Design

The simulation spans three consecutive days beginning Monday, March 10, 2025, using the complete synthetic population of 70 individuals. Employment patterns create natural commuting demand with 37 workers concentrated at Factory and Office locations, predominantly following 8:00–17:00 schedules. This temporal concentration of trip demand establishes conditions for morning and evening peak periods, enabling observation of traffic pattern evolution.

Analysis Methodology

traffic_state_animator

traffic_state_snapshot
for discrete-time network state analysis. These tools enable systematic observation of congestion patterns, peak spreading phenomena, and mode choice evolution across the simulation period. Figure

5
Conclusion

This work presents GATSim, a novel framework that introduces generative agents to transportation simulation, contributing to the fundamental paradigm shift from rule-based to intelligence-based modeling. Unlike traditional approaches that rely on rigid expert rules, generative agents bring in-depth understanding of mobility intent and user profiles through robust comprehension capabilities for commonsense reasoning and high-quality contextual decision-making. The framework enables agents to continuously learn and adapt through sophisticated memory systems and reflection mechanisms, creating realistic behavioral evolution over time.

GATSim demonstrates the potential to mimic emergent properties of human societies, revealing underlying mechanisms of urban mobility that are difficult to capture through conventional modeling approaches. The distributed decision-making of autonomous agents naturally produces system-level patterns such as peak spreading and user equilibrium-like states without explicit programming, suggesting that generative agents can unlock new insights into transportation system dynamics.

Our research makes three primary contributions to the field of transportation simulation. First, we present a comprehensive framework for generative agent-based traffic simulation that integrates LLM-based agents with urban mobility environments through sophisticated cognitive architectures and memory systems. Second, we develop and release a fully functional prototype system demonstrating the framework’s capabilities, including population synthesis, multi-modal memory management, activity planning, and reactive behavior generation. Third, we conduct systematic validation at both microscopic and macroscopic levels, demonstrating that generative agents can produce believable travel behaviors comparable to human decision-makers while exhibiting emergent system-level patterns.

The microscopic validation reveals that generative agents perform competitively with human annotators in role-playing scenarios, with agents judged more realistic in 46% of cases compared to 40% for humans. More importantly, Bayesian analysis suggests a 92% posterior probability that generative agents perform at least as well as human annotators. The macroscopic validation demonstrates realistic traffic evolution over multiple simulation days, with agents adaptively adjusting departure times, routes, and mode choices to achieve system states comparable to traditional user equilibrium models.

The GATSim framework opens numerous avenues for practical applications in transportation planning and policy analysis. It can enable planners to easily specify scenarios and analyze outcomes without extensive programming expertise. The behavioral richness of generative agents supports detailed analysis of policy interventions, infrastructure changes, and service disruptions with greater realism than traditional approaches. The framework’s ability to model social coordination and information sharing makes it particularly valuable for studying ride-sharing, transit coordination, and crisis response scenarios. Furthermore, the adaptive learning capabilities enable longitudinal studies of behavioral change in response to new transportation services, pricing policies, or urban development patterns. The framework’s modular design facilitates integration with existing transportation models and data sources, supporting practical deployment in real-world planning contexts.

Limitations

Despite promising results, several limitations constrain current framework capabilities. LLM agents demonstrate excessive cooperativeness compared to human behavior, reflecting their training as helpful assistants rather than realistic individual personas. This characteristic affects coordination scenarios where agents rarely reject proposals from family members or friends, potentially overestimating cooperation levels in transportation systems.

Multi-modal urban data processing remains a significant challenge. While our framework includes tools for spatial queries, LLMs still struggle with complex spatial reasoning. Enhanced integration of spatial computation tools and specialized training on geographic reasoning tasks could improve performance. Additionally, there is a strong need to enhance capabilities for processing diverse urban data modalities to improve credibility and realism.

Memory mechanism improvements pose ongoing challenges in retrieving the most relevant information and better handling spatial and temporal attributes. As noted by Park et al.
[
POC+23
]
, while LLMs can generate believable responses in short interactions, maintaining consistent behavior over extended simulation periods remains challenging.

Validation challenges persist as developing benchmarks for quantitative and qualitative evaluation of LLM-driven agent-based simulation remains a largely open problem. Current evaluation methods, while systematic, rely heavily on role-playing scenarios rather than direct comparison with real-world behavior data, limiting our ability to assess absolute realism levels.

Finally, simulating city-scale populations with LLM-based agents remains computationally intensive. Current implementation costs and latency constraints limit practical deployment to relatively small populations, though advances in model efficiency and specialized hardware may address these limitations over time.

Future Directions

Several promising research directions emerge from this work. Generic LLM reasoning may not capture transportation-specific decision heuristics that real travelers employ, such as ”always add 10 minutes to Google Maps estimates” or ”never trust the 7:45 bus.” Incorporating these domain-specific heuristics requires careful prompt engineering or specialized training on transportation behavior datasets.

The current implementation employs static, hand-crafted social structures including family relationships, friendship networks, and occupational roles that remain fixed throughout simulation. While generative agents could model dynamic social structure evolution—allowing relationships to form, strengthen, or dissolve based on agent interactions—this extension would require substantially longer simulation horizons and introduces complexity beyond transportation-focused validation. Future work could explore this extension for studies specifically examining the interplay between social dynamics and mobility patterns.

Future work should develop specialized memory architectures that maintain coherence while managing computational costs. This includes more sophisticated forgetting mechanisms, hierarchical memory organization, and efficient retrieval systems that scale with population size and simulation duration.

Advances in model compression, specialized hardware, and more efficient architectures will be crucial for practical deployment at city scale. Research into domain-specific fine-tuning for transportation applications could reduce computational requirements while improving behavioral realism and reducing unwanted hallucination.

Finally, developing comprehensive validation frameworks that incorporate real-world behavioral data, longitudinal studies, and cross-cultural comparisons will be essential for establishing the framework’s practical utility and identifying areas for continued improvement.
