Dutch euthanasia law does not support claims of a gradual broadening of eligibility
Authors: Theo J. Matthijssen, Willie J.C. Swildens-Rozendaal, Martina S. Vos
Published: 2026

18 June 2026

The Editorial ‘A new path for assisted dying in Europe: France's middle way’ in The Lancet Regional Health – Europe 2026; 61: 101617 contains the following passage:

‘Furthermore, experience from countries such as the Netherlands and Belgium has complicated the debate, with ongoing discussion about how eligibility for assisted dying has evolved beyond terminal illness to include some chronic non-terminal and psychiatric conditions. These developments have renewed long-standing concerns about the potential for gradual broadening of eligibility once physician-assisted dying is legalised.’1

This passage may lead to misunderstandings about Dutch euthanasia law. In Dutch euthanasia law, the judge and the legislator have never made a fundamental distinction between terminal and (chronic) non-terminal conditions, nor between somatic and psychiatric conditions.

The following is a brief description of the Dutch situation. In the Netherlands, euthanasia (termination of life on request) and assisted suicide are criminal offences under articles 293 and 294 of the Dutch Criminal Code. The only exception is when the procedure is performed by a physician who has satisfied six statutory due care criteria.

The physician must: (1) be satisfied that the patient's request is voluntary and well-considered, (2) be satisfied that the patient's suffering is unbearable, with no prospect of improvement, (3) have informed the patient about their situation and prognosis, (4) have come to the conclusion, together with the patient, that there is no reasonable alternative in the patient's situation, (5) have consulted at least one other, independent physician, and (6) have exercised due medical care and attention in terminating the patient's life or assisting in the patient's suicide.

These due care criteria were developed in Dutch case law in a series of landmark court cases between 1973 and 1994. The Royal Dutch Medical Association (KNMG) actively contributed to the development of the due care criteria in a series of reports. The KNMG also made no distinction between terminal and (chronic) non-terminal conditions.

The Dutch Psychiatric Association (NVvP) issued a guideline in 1998 explicitly accepting the possibility of euthanasia for suffering due to a psychiatric disorder, as allowed by Dutch case law.2 This guideline has been updated several times since then.

In April 2002, a law (also called: Euthanasia Act) entered into force that codified the standards developed in case law. During the parliamentary debate on the bill, the government explicitly confirmed its earlier position that euthanasia would also remain possible in cases of non-terminal suffering caused by a psychiatric disorder.

Since the introduction of the Euthanasia Act in 2002, the legal conditions under which a physician may perform euthanasia have remained unchanged. However, over time, more physicians have become willing to actually perform euthanasia in cases of suffering due to a psychiatric disorder, in line with the legal scope already provided by case law and professional guidelines before the Euthanasia Act was enacted.3

In the Netherlands, therefore, there is no basis for the notion of ‘gradual broadening of eligibility once physician-assisted dying is legalised’. On the contrary, the legal conditions under which a physician may perform euthanasia have remained unchanged since the introduction of the Euthanasia Act in 2002.
