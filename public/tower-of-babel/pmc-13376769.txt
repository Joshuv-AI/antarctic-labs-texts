Tunability of third-order nonlinear response in nematic liquid crystal E7 via SnO2 nanoparticle doping: a structured temperature-concentration analysis
Authors: Mahsa Khadem Sadigh, A H Moayednia, A Ranjkesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the temperature- and concentration-dependent linear and third-order nonlinear optical properties of SnO2-doped nematic liquid crystal E7. Refractive index measurements confirm that nanoparticle incorporation modifies both ordinary and extraordinary indices while preserving positive birefringence throughout the nematic phase. Kerr-effect analysis shows a pronounced enhancement of nonlinear response at 0.1 wt% SnO2, where the Kerr constant increases from 2.73 × 10–10 m V-2 (pure E7) to 6.94 × 10–10 m V-2, corresponding to an enhancement of approximately 154%. The third-order nonlinear optical susceptibility reaches its maximum value of 7.62 × 10–18 m2 V-2 at the same concentration near the nematic-isotropic transition temperature. At higher nanoparticle loadings, both the Kerr constant and χ(3) decrease, which may be attributed to nanoparticle aggregation and partial disruption of molecular ordering. Inverse Kerr analysis indicates a shift in pre-transition temperature from 332.2 to 332.7 K (ΔT⁓0.5 K), suggesting subtle modifications in the critical behavior near the phase transition. Therefore, these results demonstrate the potential of SnO2-E7 nanocomposites in nonlinear optical applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53517-0.Keywords: Liquid crystals, Kerr constant, Nanoparticles, Nonlinear optical response, Refractive indexSubject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

In condensed-matter physics, materials are generally classified as either isotropic, with identical properties in all directions, or anisotropic, where directionality influences their behavior. Liquid crystals (LCs) belong to the anisotropic class, yet they occupy a special middle ground: they flow like liquids but retain orientational and, in some phases, partially positional order similar to solids. This hybrid structure makes LCs a valuable model for studying how order develops and transforms in soft matter. Their diverse mesophases, defect patterns, and field- or temperature-driven transitions have long attracted attention in fundamental research. At the same time, the strong optical and dielectric anisotropy of liquid crystals gives them exceptional technological relevance1–4. They are widely used in display technologies and increasingly in adaptive optical components such as smart windows, sensors, and fast optical shutters. Their tunable optical response also supports emerging photonic and optoelectronic applications, while their biocompatibility has made them promising materials for biophotonics and advanced biosensing5–9.

In isotropic materials, it is common to tailor physical properties by introducing external dopants, and a similar strategy can be applied to anisotropic systems such as liquid crystals10,11. In nematic liquid crystals (NLCs), the addition of non-mesogenic molecules, organic dyes, or a wide range of nanoparticles can significantly reshape their optical and optoelectronic behavior by perturbing the local director configuration and modifying dielectric and refractive characteristics. These effects become even more pronounced when such doped systems are subjected to external electric or magnetic fields, which interact with the altered molecular environment and lead to enriched, tunable optical response. Recent studies have shown that metal-oxide nanostructures12–17, carbon nanomaterials18–20, and various chromophoric dopants can each influence threshold voltages, birefringence, ionic conductivity, and field-induced reorientation dynamics, providing a versatile approach for engineering the optical properties of nematic phases.

The nonlinear optical response of both pristine and doped liquid crystals can be investigated using several experimental techniques. Commonly employed methods include the Z-scan21, the electro-optic Kerr effect22, degenerate four-wave mixing, pump-probe spectroscopy23, and time-resolved optical measurements. These approaches allow researchers to probe changes in nonlinear absorption, refractive index, and field-induced birefringence, as well as to evaluate the influence of dopants and external stimuli on the dynamic optical behavior of nematic and other liquid-crystalline phases.

Despite substantial progress, most studies have been concentrated on isolated observables, typically within restricted temperature or concentration regimes. Systematic experimental correlations between linear optical anisotropy, pre-transitional behavior, and nonlinear electro-optical response remain comparatively limited. Theoretical treatments further suggest that nanoparticle inclusions may generate competing mechanisms yet comprehensive experimental validation is scarce. SnO2 nanoparticles were selected as dopants for the selected liquid crystal because their physical and optical characteristics are well suited for optically responsive liquid crystal composites. SnO2 is a wide-bandgap, n-type semiconductor with strong transparency in the visible region, which allows adjustment of optical properties while avoiding significant absorption losses. When structured at the nanoscale, SnO2 can exhibit modified electronic states and pronounced third-order nonlinear optical behavior. These attributes enable tuning of parameters such as refractive index and nonlinear absorption without substantially disturbing the liquid crystal phase. Relative to other metal oxide nanoparticles, SnO2 presents several advantages24–28. TiO2 particles may introduce stronger light scattering and photo-related effects, and ZnO nanoparticles often exhibit aggregation tendencies that complicate uniform dispersion29–33. SnO2 nanoparticles, in contrast, offer a combination of high optical clarity, stable semiconductor response, and reduced impact on liquid crystal alignment, making them more suitable for forming homogenous liquid crystal nanocomposites. The choice is also consistent with previous observations in liquid crystal nanocomposite research, where semiconducting nanomaterials have been shown to enhance nonlinear optical responses. Semiconductor quantum dots, including CdSe and ZnS, have demonstrated significant improvements in nonlinear absorption and refraction due to quantum confinement and increased polarizability34. These results emphasize the effectiveness of nanoscale semiconductors in tailoring light-matter interactions, supporting the use of SnO2 nanoparticles as a viable dopant for controlling the optical and nonlinear optical behavior of liquid crystal systems.

In this work, we focus on a detailed characterization of the linear optical properties of the nematic liquid crystal E7 doped with SnO2 nanoparticles, specifically examining the ordinary and extraordinary polarizabilities as well as the linear refractive indices across varying temperatures and nanoparticle concentrations. Building upon this foundation, we employ the electro-optic Kerr effect to probe the third-order nonlinear optical response, aiming to uncover how subtle adjustments in thermal conditions and dopant loading can be used to finely tune the nonlinear behavior. By systematically correlating temperature-dependent and concentration-dependent variations in linear and nonlinear optical parameters, this study provides a comprehensive framework for understanding and controlling the interplay between nanoparticle-induced perturbations and the intrinsic molecular order of nematic phases, offering new avenues for the design of advanced optoelectronic materials.

Experimental section

Materials

The nematic liquid crystal E7 and SnO2 nanoparticles employed as dopants were supplied by Merck. The nematic liquid crystal E7 exhibits a phase transition from nematic to isotropic phase upon heating, with a transition temperature of approximately 333.15 K, in accordance with the product specification. This phase transition makes it suitable for investigating temperature-dependent optical and nonlinear properties. The SnO2 nanoparticles employed as dopants were commercially obtained from Merck. According to the manufacturer’s specifications, the average size of the nanoparticle is 20–30 nm. This is in agreement with the SEM image (Fig. 1). The SEM image indicates that average size of the nanoparticles is 28.27 nm. Since the particles were used as received, no additional chemical analysis or surface modification procedures were performed. Pure E7 and a series of E7-based nanocomposites containing 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, and 0.7 wt% SnO2 were prepared for subsequent measurements. Nanoparticles were dispersed in ethanol as the solvent with a specific ratio (1:20) to avoid agglomeration. The mixture was subjected to sonication for six hours to ensure thorough and homogeneous dispersion. To obtain the target weight concentration, precisely measured amounts of the prepared mixture were incorporated into the liquid crystal phase containing the predetermined nanoparticle weight fraction. Due to the volatility of ethanol, controlled heating was applied and maintained until further nanoparticle addition no longer induced aggregation.

SEM image of NP.

Experimental setup

The refractive indices of the samples in their isotropic and anisotropic states were determined using an Abbe refractometer (Bellingham Stanley Abbe 60ED) fitted with a high-precision temperature control system providing an accuracy of ± 0.1 °C, which utilizes the sodium D-line at 589 nm as the standard light source.

Electro-optical measurements

The electro-optical response of both pristine and SnO2-doped liquid crystal samples was examined in the isotropic phase using the nulled-intensity technique35–37, as schematically shown in Fig. 2. Below the nematic-isotropic transition temperature, the liquid crystal cell remained optically dark, preventing light transmission in the absence of an external field. A continuous-wave solid- state laser (Coherent Varid V5) operating at the wavelength of 532 nm with an output power of 5 mW was used as probe beam. This technique enables accurate evolution of field-induced birefringence through monitoring variations in the transmitted optical intensity under an applied electric field. Electro-optical measurements were conducted using a quartz cell with an optical path length of 10 mm, following the Haltner-Konski configuration38. Stainless-steel electrodes with a 2 mm × 1.8 mm × 10 mm Teflon spacer were incorporated into the cell to generate an alternating electric field at a frequency of 1 kHz. A rectangular voltage waveform was supplied by a high-voltage power source. In this case, an alternating voltage supplied by an external source was applied to the electrode pair, creating an electric field across the sample. The electrode arrangement, separated by a Teflon spacer, effectively establishes a parallel-plate capacitor geometry that ensures a nearly uniform field distribution within the liquid crystal. This field was introduced to generate Kerr-induced birefringence and to examine the electro-optical behavior, while the chosen AC frequency helps suppress charge accumulation and ionic contributions.

Schematic illustration of the experimental setup employed for electro-optical Kerr effect measurements.

Temperature control during measurements was ensured using a PT-100 sensor with an accuracy of ± 0.1 °C. The polarizer and analyzer were positioned in a crossed geometry and oriented at 45° relative to the direction of the applied electric field. After transmission through a quarter-wave plate, the optical signal was detected using a photodetector and subsequently recorded with a digital oscilloscope (GPS1202XE digital oscilloscope). This arrangement is physically justified since, in the isotropic phase, the application of an electric field establishes an induced optical axis parallel to the field direction. Orienting the incident polarization at an angle of 45º relative to the field enhances the measurement sensitivity to the induced birefringence, thereby enabling the detection of field-dependent phase retardation via variations in the transmitted intensity.

All measurements were repeated under the same conditions to ensure consistency. Representative data for pure E7 and the 0.1 wt% SnO2 sample are included in the Supplementary Data and show good agreement. The same cell was used for all measurements, and the experimental conditions were carefully controlled throughout.

Results and discussions

Linear refractive indices of SnO2 NPs-liquid crystal composites

The ordinary (no) and extraordinary (ne) refractive indices of pure E7 and SnO2-doped samples were measured as a function of temperature to investigate the effect of nanoparticle incorporation on the optical response of the nematic phase (Fig. 3). The figures for each condition are presented separately in the Supplementary Data (Fig. S1). In all compositions, the condition ne > no was maintained throughout the nematic regime, indicating that the positive birefringence of E7 is preserved upon doping. This indicates that the director orientation remains the principal optical axis after the addition of SnO2, and the dopant does not invert the optical anisotropy of the host matrix. For the undoped system, ne exhibited its maximum value at room temperature and decreased progressively with increasing temperature. This temperature dependence is attributed to the reduction of long-range orientational order as the system approaches the nematic-isotropic transition. Near the clearing temperature, ne converged toward a constant value, coinciding with the isotropic refractive index (niso). In contrast, no showed a slight increase with temperature before reaching the same limiting value at the phase transition. The opposite thermal trends of the two indices reflect the gradual loss of optical uniaxiality as the orientational order parameter (S) diminishes. From a theoretical perspective, in the isotropic phase of liquid crystal-nanoparticle composites, a weak decrease in refractive index with temperature is expected due to thermal expansion and reduced short-range orientatonal order. The experimental data are consistent with this behavior39. Incorporation of SnO2 nanoparticles modified the absolute values of both refractive indices in a concentration-dependent manner. At low nanoparticle content (0.1 wt%), ne remained close to that of pure E7, suggesting that the particles are well dispersed and induce minimal disruption in molecular alignment. Increasing the dopant concentration to 0.3 wt% resulted in the most pronounced reduction in ne, and an increase in no. This behavior implies that, at this concentration, possible nanoparticle aggregation or surface-induced disorder reduces the local nematic coherence, effectively lowering the average polarizability along the director while enhancing the optical response perpendicular to it. At higher loadings (0.5–0.7 wt%), partial recovery of ne suggests a balance between disordering effects and increased effective polarizability. In the isotropic phase, the refractive index shows a nearly constant or weakly decreasing trend with temperature. The slight decrease in isotropic refractive index with increasing nanoparticle concentration can be explained using an effective medium approach40. Although used nanoparticle has a high intrinsic refractive index, the optical response of the nanoparticle-liquid crystal composite is governed by the effective polarizability of the system. In the isotropic phase, where long-range order is absent, local density and short-range molecular correlation dominate the optical response. Nanoparticle incorporation can slightly modify molecular packing and reduced effective density, leading to a small decrease in the measured refractive index. Interfacial interactions may also contribute redistribution of local polarizability. The magnitude of this effect remains small (⁓0.04), indicating optical stability of the system.

All liquid crystal samples were prepared under identical experimental conditions. Care was taken to ensure homogenous nanoparticle dispersion and to minimize aggregation effects. Selected experiments (pure E7 and 0.1 wt% SnO2) were repeated under identical conditions. The corresponding results, provided in Fig. S2 of Supplementary Data, show strong agreement and confirm the reproducibility of the observed trends.

According to Fig. 4, doping nanoparticles into nematic liquid crystals affects the overall birefringence through a balance of two competing mechanisms. At low concentrations, well-dispersed nanoparticles can enhance local polarizability and promote alignment of nearby liquid crystal molecules along the director, slightly increasing or maintaining the birefringence. At higher concentrations, however, nanoparticles can disrupt the uniform molecular order due to possible aggregation or surface interactions, leading to a reduction in the effective birefringence. Essentially, the interaction between nanoparticles and liquid crystal molecules modifies the orientational order parameter: attractive or ordering interactions can stabilize the nematic alignment, while steric hindrance and disordering effects reduce long-range molecular coherence, thereby modifying the optical anisotropy of the system. Overall, the results indicate that controlled incorporation of SnO2 can be used as an effective route to tailor the optical properties of E7 without altering the sign of its birefringence.

Kerr constant of SnO2 NPs-liquid crystal composites

The Kerr constant (B) quantifies the nonlinear electro-optical response of liquid crystals by describing the electric-field-induced birefringence within the medium. In the present work, B was determined for pure and SnO2-doped liquid crystal samples in the isotropic phase through the nulled-intensity electro-optical method, in which the analyzer is rotationally adjusted to suppress the transmitted optical signal upon application of an external field. This approach enables direct extraction of the field-induced phase retardation (δ), and consequently the Kerr constant, with signal-to-noise reliability. The results indicate (Fig. 6(a)) that the Kerr constant exhibits a strong dependence on both nanoparticle concentration and temperature. Near the nematic-isotropic transition, the samples doped with 0.1 wt% SnO2 showed the most pronounced enhancement in B, followed by 0.5 wt%, 0.3 wt%, 0.7 wt%, and finally the undoped liquid crystal (Fig. 6(b)). This non-linear concentration dependence highlights a competitive mechanism between two effects: (i) local enhancement of polarizability and local field enhancement effects in the vicinity of nanoparticles, which can enhance molecular reorientation at low concentrations; and (ii) possible disruption of cooperative orientational correlations at higher concentrations, which limits collective alignment and therefore reduces the Kerr response.

(a) Variation of the Kerr constant with temperature and (b) dependence of the Kerr constant on SnO2 nanoparticle weight fraction for the investigated liquid crystal samples.

Temperature further modulates this balance. With increasing thermal energy, the nematic-like short-range order in the isotropic phase weakens, diminishing the capacity of the medium to reorient under an electric field. Consequently, the maximum Kerr response occurs near the transition temperature for systems with low nanoparticle doping, where the microenvironment retains sufficient pre-transitional coherence to amplify the external field coupling while avoiding excessive structural perturbation. The measurements shown in Fig. 6 (b) were performed in the vicinity of the nematic-isotropic phase transition, a temperature region where liquid crystal properties are strongly affected by pre-transitional effects, enhanced order parameter fluctuations, and increased molecular reorientation dynamics. In this regime, optical and nonlinear optical parameters are highly sensitive to small perturbations, and deviations from simple monotonic behavior are therefore physically expected. Near the transition temperature, nanoparticle inclusions can locally modify orientational order and internal field distributions, which may lead to nonlinear or irregular variations in measured quantities. Such behavior is consistent with the known critical behavior of liquid crystals. Importantly, at temperatures significantly higher than the phase transition, where fluctuation effects are reduced and the system becomes more thermodynamically stable, the behavior becomes noticeably more systematic and reproducible.

Pre-transition temperature of SnO2 NPs-liquid crystal composites

The temperature dependence of the inverse Kerr constant (B− 1) was analyzed to extract the pre-transition temperature (T*) for pure and SnO2-doped E7 systems. In accordance with the Landau-de Gennes framework, a linear extrapolation of B− 1 (T) towards zero was employed to determine T*, which represents the hypothetical temperature at which the Kerr response would tend to diverge. The obtained values (Fig. 7), equal to 333.2 K for both pure and 0.1 wt% doped samples, 333.5 K for 0.3 wt%, and 0.7 wt%, and 333.7 K for 0.5 wt%, indicate that nanoparticle incorporation induces measurable shifts in the pre-transitional behavior of the isotropic phase.

These variations reflect the sensitivity of short-range orientational correlations to nanoparticle concentration. The invariant T* observed for the pure and 0.1 wt% samples suggest that, at low loading, SnO2 nanoparticles are sufficiently dispersed to avoid perturbing the local pre-transitional fluctuations. In contrast, the upward shift of T* at 0.3–0.7 wt% implies that increased dopant density may induce local disturbances in the elastic environment, which may be associated with enhanced pre-transitional correlations. This behavior suggests a concentration-dependent modification of the susceptibility of the isotropic phase to external fields and reflects a balance between enhanced local polarizability (at low concentrations) and disruption of correlation coherence (at higher concentrations).

Although the extracted values corroborate the predictions of Landau-de Gennes theory, they suggest that the evolution of the electro-optic Kerr response in these hybrid systems is influenced by the interplay between nanoparticle-induced perturbations and intrinsic criticality near the isotropic-nematic transition. The observed shifts in pre-transition temperature therefore provide a useful indicator of how dopant concentration modulates fluctuation-driven optical nonlinearities, offering a complementary metric for optimizing electro-optical performance in SnO2 NPs-liquid crystal composites.

The obtained results in Fig. 8 (a) reveal that χ(3) is strongly influenced by both temperature and nanoparticle concentration. Measurements conducted in the isotropic phase show that near the nematic-isotropic transition, the highest value of χ(3) corresponds to the sample doped with 0.1 wt% SnO2, followed by 0.5 wt%, 0.3 wt%, 0.7 wt%, and finally the pure liquid crystal (Fig. 8 (b)). This non-linear concentration-dependent behavior suggests an optimal doping level where the interaction between nanoparticles and host matrix leads to maximum enhancement of the nonlinear response. At low concentrations, local field enhancement effects and improved polarizability in the vicinity of nanoparticles may contribute to enhanced molecular orientation of the molecules under the applied field, increasing χ(3). Conversely, at higher concentrations, spatial inhomogeneity, disruption of molecular alignment, and increased dielectric disorder may reduce the collective reorientation efficiency, resulting in lower susceptibility values. A clear temperature dependence is also observed. With increasing temperature, χ(3) generally decreases, which can be attributed to the diminished short-range molecular ordering in the isotropic phase and reduced susceptibility of the system to field-induced reorientation. Hence, the region just above the phase transition represents a favorable temperature region where residual orientational correlations are still present, allowing the hybrid system to exhibit stronger nonlinear optical responses.

To situate the present findings within the broader literature on nanoparticle-doped nematic liquid crystals, several recent studies have reported dopant-dependent modifications of optical properties. As summarized in Table 1, Brouckaert et al. demonstrated that ferroelectric and plasmonic nanoparticles in nematic hosts induce non-monotonic changes in elastic constants and response times due to aggregation and local effects, but did not integrate Kerr nonlinearities in a temperature-concentration framework. Enhanced third-order optical nonlinearity was also observed in CdSe quantum dot doped 7CB liquid crystals, with peak behavior at specific loadings, although systematic temperature effects were not examined. Studies on oxide nanoparticles such as ZnO and Fe2O3 have shown improved birefringence and order parameter changes with concentration and particle shape, and recent work on SnO2 in ML-0648 liquid crystals reported enhanced dielectric anisotropy for moderate dopant levels. In contrast, the present study aims to provide a more comprehensive analysis that not only is consistent with these enhancement and suppression mechanisms, but further correlates them with Kerr constant behavior, third-order susceptibility, and shifts in the pre-transition temperature across both temperature and concentration, thereby offering a more complete picture of nanoparticle-induced nonlinear optical tuning13,42–48.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that the incorporation of SnO2 nanoparticles provides an effective route for tailoring both the linear and nonlinear optical responses of the nematic liquid crystal E7. The experimental results indicate a clear balance between two competing effects: the possible enhancement of local field-induced polarization at low nanoparticle loadings and disruption of orientational order at higher concentrations. At the optimal concentration of SnO2 (0.1 wt%), the Kerr constant increases from 2.73 × 10–10 to 6.94 × 10− 9 m V-2, and the third-order nonlinear optical susceptibility reaches 7.62 × 10–18 m2 V-2. The enhanced nonlinear response observed near the nematic-isotropic transition can be related to stronger pre-transitional molecular fluctuations under the applied electric field. The slight shift in pre-transition temperature suggests that nanoparticle incorporation influences short-range orientational correlations while preserving the overall nematic phase stability. Overall, the study highlights that SnO2 doping enables controlled modulation of important optical parameters through a competition between ordering and disordering mechanisms.
