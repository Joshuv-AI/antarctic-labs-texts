Urban and Rural Hospital Closures in Georgia, 2022-2024
Authors: Sara D. Turbow, Kajal Patel, Young-Rock Hong, Jennifer Lom, Mohammed K. Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study examines changes in discharge volume, total charges, and length of stay at hospitals near urban vs rural hospital closures in the state of Georgia.

Introduction

Hospital closures are a growing concern with a marked increase in both urban and rural hospital closures seen in recent years.1 These closures, often due to financial strain, decline in patient volumes, and inequities in health care funding, can result in loss of access to critical medical services.2,3,4,5 The downstream effects on nearby institutions, especially when it comes to patient volume, resource availability, and care delivery, remain poorly understood. The purpose of this study was to examine postclosure changes in discharge volume, charges, and length of stay at hospitals near urban vs rural hospital closures in the state of Georgia.

Methods

The data source for this cross-sectional study was administrative data from the Georgia Hospital Association (GHA) for 2022 to 2024, linked to the 2021 American Hospital Association Annual Survey.6 GHA data include a deidentified record of every discharge in the state over the course of a calendar year. The Emory University Institutional Review Board deemed this study exempt from institutional review board review due to use of deidentified data in accordance with the Common Rule. Reporting follows the STROBE guideline.7

This analysis examined discharges from acute care hospitals within 35 miles of acute care hospitals that closed between 2022 and 2024. The 35-mile threshold was selected based on the distance criterion used to define a rural Critical Access Hospital by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services.8 Hospital closures were identified when the number of monthly discharges decreased to 0 and remained at 0 through 2024. The primary exposure was a time-varying variable that equaled 0 prior to hospital closure and equaled 1 following hospital closure. The outcomes examined were the preclosure and postclosure differences in the number of discharges per month, the mean charges per admission, and the mean length of stay (LOS) per admission before and after hospital closure.

Analyses were adjusted for hospital and patient characteristics. Hospital characteristics included urban vs rural location, number of beds, hospital ownership, and the mean number of discharges each month. Patient characteristics included age, sex, race and ethnicity, and insurance payer. Information about how each covariate was defined is available in the eMethods in Supplement 1. Information on admission diagnoses, procedures, and comorbidities was not available in the data.

All analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute). We used unadjusted and adjusted negative binomial (hospital volume) or gamma (charges and LOS) regressions with a log link using generalized estimating equations with an exchangeable correlation matrix, accounting for clustering within hospitals.9 Significance was defined as a 2-sided P < .05.

Results

Four hospital closures were identified; 1 urban hospital closed in 2022, 2 rural hospitals closed in 2023, and 1 rural hospital closed in 2024. There were 1 637 164 discharges within 35 miles of a hospital that closed (675 006 female [41.2%]; mean [SD] age, 51.2 [23.4] years); of these, 1 184 201 were after hospital closure (Table 1).

In the fully adjusted model, hospital closure was associated with a 0.60% (95% CI, 0.52%-0.67%) decrease in urban discharges and a 5.01% increase (95% CI, 4.51%-5.52%) in rural discharges.

Hospital closure was associated with a decrease of 2.63% (95% CI, 1.99%-3.27%) mean total charges among urban hospitals. There was no statistically significant difference in the mean total charges for rural hospitals. Urban hospitals had a 1.40% (95% CI, 0.82%-1.97%) decrease, while rural hospitals had a 4.96% (95% CI, 3.01%-6.86%) decrease in average LOS associated with hospital closure (Table 2).

Discussion

Overall, this cross-sectional study found different associations of hospital closure in urban vs rural settings with changes in nearby hospitals’ monthly discharges, charges, and LOS. Among urban hospitals, hospital closure was associated with reductions in the number of discharges, LOS, and charges. Possible reasons for this finding are that perhaps nearby hospitals anticipated an influx of patients from the hospital that closed and created systems to prevent low-risk admissions, or that patients avoided the open hospitals due to fear of overcrowding. Among rural hospitals, the hospital closure was associated with a higher volume of discharges, lower LOS, and no change in mean charges. In contrast with urban hospitals, rural hospitals may be the only emergency or inpatient health care facility for a community or geographic area and therefore may have fewer options but to manage an increased number of patients when a nearby hospital closes. Key limitations include that we were unable to adjust for a patient’s clinical characteristics and that, as a pre-post analysis without a concurrent control group, the observed associations may partly reflect secular trends in hospital utilization.

Urban hospital closures are an emerging area that will only become more relevant as policy changes are expected to lead to more urban closures in the coming years. It is critical that stakeholders understand the downstream effects that occur when an urban hospital closes to prevent them or lessen their impact on patient and hospital outcomes.
