MYB gene from Endocarpon pusillum regulates growth and development and enhances drought tolerance in creeping bentgrass (Agrostis stolonifera L.)
Authors: Ye Ai, Jielin Liu, Yinglong Chen, Xu Qian, Jiabao Chen, Jiaxing Li, Lixin Xu, Xinbo Sun, Fu Ran, Liebao Han, Yuehui Chao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMYB transcription factors play a crucial regulatory role in plant growth and stress response. The gene EpMYB, obtained from Endocarpon pusillum, a dominant lichen in the Tengger Desert, was transferred to creeping bentgrass to explore its effects on plant growth and response to abiotic stress.ResultsCompared to wild-type (WT), transgenic (TG) plants exhibited a faster growth rate, a significantly higher number of leaves per tiller, increased internode length, and longer maximum leaf length. However, some of the leaves were severely twisted. Additionally, the antioxidant enzyme content, lignin content and drought tolerance of the TG plants was significantly enhanced. RNA-seq analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the TG-vs-WT were primarily associated with pathways such as photosynthesis, wax biosynthesis, lipid metabolism, and flavonoid biosynthesis. By comparing the TG (Drought treatment)-vs-TG and WT (Drought treatment)-vs-WT groups, numerous DEGs related to growth, development, and stress tolerance were identified, including aldehyde decarbonylase gene(CER1), lignin synthesis gene (HCT), adenylate dimethylallyltransferase gene (IPT), peroxin-10 gene (PEX10), among others. These results suggest that the EpMYB gene enhances the drought tolerance of transgenic creeping bentgrass by regulating photosynthesis, antioxidant enzyme activity, lignin synthesis, wax synthesis, and lipid metabolism.ConclusionThese findings suggest that the EpMYB gene functions as a positive regulator of plant growth and development, while also playing a crucial role in the plant’s response to drought stress. Furthermore, this study demonstrates the feasibility of selecting specific functional genes from stress-tolerant microorganisms and applying them to plants to enhance stress resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07740-y.

Background

MYB is one of the largest families of transcription factors. The N-terminal of MYB transcription factors contains a highly conserved MYB domain, which typically consists of 1 to 3 tandemly repeated, incomplete repeat sequences (R). Each repeat, consisting of 51–52 amino acids, folds into a helix-turn-helix (HTH) structure. This structure is a recognition helix that directly interacts with DNA, participating in the binding of large trenches within the DNA molecule. The MYB domain typically contains three hydrophobic residues, forming a hydrophobic core within the HTH three-dimensional structure. This core is crucial for maintaining the configuration of the HTH structure [1, 2]. The C-terminal of MYB transcription factors usually contains a transcriptional activation region rich in acidic amino acids, which typically folds into a bipartite helix and exhibits functional plasticity [3].

MYB transcription factors can be classified into four types based on the number of MYB domain repeats: (1) Protein containing a single MYB domain (R1/R2-MYB) may play crucial roles in maintaining chromosome structural integrity, regulating gene transcription, controlling cell morphogenesis, and regulating the formation of the plant biological clock [4]. Examples include OsPTBP1 [5] and CmREVEILLE2 [6]. (2) Proteins with two MYB domains (R2R3-MYB) constitute the majority of MYB proteins in plant species. These proteins are involved in various aspects of plant growth and development, including the regulation of secondary metabolism, cell differentiation, response to exogenous stress, and defense against diseases and pests [7, 8]. Examples include GhMYB109 [9] and AtMYB75 [10]. (3) Proteins with three MYB domains (R1R2R3-MYB) primarily regulate the cell cycle and cell differentiation [11]. Examples include AtREP-MYB [12] and NtmybA1 [13]. (4) Proteins with four MYB domains (4R-MYB) belong to a subfamily that was discovered relatively recently and their specific functions remain insufficiently studied [14]. In this study, EpMYB was identified as a protein containing only a single MYB domain, belonging to the R1/R2-MYB structural type.

Creeping bentgrass (Agrostis stolonifera) is a cold-season turfgrass known for its ability to produce new roots and stems on stolons. It is valued for its soft color, rapid growth, salt tolerance, cold resistance, and ability to withstand low pruning and trampling. However, creeping bentgrass has relatively weak drought tolerance [15–17]. Due to difficulties in seed production and its genetic self-incompatibility, traditional breeding methods have struggled to improve creeping bentgrass genetically [18]. As a result, genetic engineering has become a promising approach to enhance its traits.

This study is the first to explore the impact of a fungal MYB gene on abiotic stress in plants. The gene was originally discovered in response to drought stress in Endocarpon pusillum [19], a dominant lichen in the Tengger Desert that can survive up to 7 months under drought and starvation stress, and more than 8 months under starvation stress alone [20]. Genome-wide and transcriptome analysis revealed that Endocarpon pusillum possesses a range of specific drought adaptation mechanisms, including damage repair, energy supply, and adjustments in carbon and nitrogen metabolism [19, 21]. In this study, the EpMYB gene was overexpressed in creeping bentgrass to examine its role in plant growth, development, and stress response. Compared to wild-type (WT) plants, the transgenic (TG) plants with high expression of EpMYB showed some phenotypic changes, including partial leaf curl and altered cell shape. These plants also had an increased number of leaves per tiller, greater lignin content, and significantly enhanced drought tolerance. The results suggest that the EpMYB gene not only influences the growth and development of creeping bentgrass but also markedly increases its drought tolerance. Furthermore, we explored the regulatory mechanisms of the EpMYB gene on plant growth and stress response at the transcriptional level, providing a novel approach for improving plant stress resistance by selecting functional genes from stress-tolerant microorganisms and applying them to plants.

Results

EpMYB bioinformatics analysis

The EpMYB (XM_007804716.1) gene has an 831 bp open reading frame and encodes 276 amino acids. The conserved structure and properties of the EpMYB protein were analyzed and predicted using the NCBI website. It was found that a MYB-DNA-binding domain spans amino acids 92 to 134 (Fig. 1A), which corresponds to the MYB binding site. The molecular formula of EpMYB is C1347H2118N410O411S9, with a total molecular mass of 30.92 kDa and a theoretical isoelectric point of 9.65. The number of positively charged residues is 39, and the number of negatively charged residues is 29. The instability index (II) is 79.98, and the grand average of hydropathy (GRAVY) is −0.924. These results suggest that EpMYB is an unstable, hydrophilic protein. Secondary and tertiary structure predictions indicate that the EpMYB protein is predominantly composed of alpha helices (27.17%) and random coils (57.97%), with extended strands (10.51%) and beta turns (4.35%) also present (Fig. 1B, C). Additionally, SignalP 6.0 predictions indicate that the EpMYB protein does not contain signal peptides (Fig. 1D).

Subcellular localization

The 3302Y vector control and the 3302Y-EpMYB were transferred into tobacco leaves through Agrobacterium-mediated method, respectively. After a two-day dark culture, the tobacco leaves were observed under a confocal fluorescence microscope. The results showed that there was a strong fluorescence signal in the cell nucleus. Furthermore, the staining results of 4’, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) on the cell nucleus further illustrate that the EpMYB protein was located in the cell nucleus (Fig. 2).

The acquisition of transgenic EpMYB plants

PCR results confirmed the presence of the EpMYB gene in the genome of transgenic creeping bentgrass (Fig. S1). Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) results showed that the EpMYB gene was expressed normally in TG plants (Fig. S2). Western blotting demonstrated that EpMYB protein was produced in TG plants (Fig. S3).

Phenotype and internal structure characteristics of wild-type and transgenic creeping bentgrass

WT and TG plants were cultured for 12 weeks under identical conditions. Compared to WT plants, TG plants exhibited a faster growth rate (Fig. 3A, B, C), with stem fresh weight and root fresh weight increased by 44.8% and 41.6% (Fig. 3G, H), respectively, increased maximum leaf length (25.6%) (Fig. 3D, I), and significantly more leaves per tiller (3.0-fold) (Fig. 3F, J), along with longer internodes (35.1%) (Fig. 3K). Some leaves showed severe distortion (Fig. 3E). Additionally, TG plants had significantly higher (9.4%) chlorophyll content than WT plants (Fig. 3L). In addition, scanning electron microscopy revealed increased wax content around the stomata of TG plant leaves, with altered morphology (Fig. 4A, B). The expression level of the wax synthesis gene MAH1 in the TG was also significantly higher than that in the WT (2.5-fold), indicating that the EpMYB gene significantly increased the wax synthesis in the TG plants (Fig. 4C).

Transgenic plants increased lignin content

The results showed that compared to WT plants, the total lignin content in the ground part of the two transgenic lines increased by 25.2% and 9.1% (Fig. 7A), respectively. In addition, compared to WT plants, the expression level of lignin synthesis gene HCT4 in TG plants was significantly higher (4.2-fold) (Fig. 7B), indicating that the EpMYB gene promotes lignin synthesis in TG plants.

Hormonal changes of transgenic creeping bentgrass

Compared to WT plants, the levels of IAA (31.9%) (Fig. 8A), IPA (9.6%) (Fig. 8B), GA3 (13.7%) (Fig. 8D), and BR (40.1%) (Fig. 8E) were up-regulated in TG plants, with the increases in IAA and BR being particularly significant. Conversely, ZR content was lower (6.1%) in TG plants (Fig. 8C). These results suggest that the EpMYB transcription factor affects the co-regulation of multiple hormones, which influences both plant growth and response to abiotic stress.

Results of transcriptome sequencing

After transcriptome sequencing of 12 samples generated 91.90 Gb of clean data, with each sample yielding 6.37 Gb of clean data. Q30 indicates that the quality value of the base is 30, and the corresponding error rate is 0.1%, which means the accuracy rate is 99.99%. This indicator is used to measure the accuracy and reliability of sequencing data. The result shows that the range of Q30 for 12 samples is 94.19%−95.04%, indicating that both the reliability and accuracy of the transcriptome data are relatively high (Table 1). The correlation of biological repetitions not only tests the reproducibility of biological experimental operations, it can also evaluate the reliability of differentially expressed genes. The Pearson correlation coefficient r is used as an evaluation indicator for the correlation between samples. The closer the r^2 value is to 1, the stronger the correlation between the two samples is. As shown in Fig. 9A, the correlation between repeated samples is relatively high, indicating that the reliability of the identified differentially expressed genes is also high. Discussion

In this study, the gene EpMYB was obtained from stress-tolerant microorganisms of Endocarpon pusillum. This protein contains only one MYB domain, classifying it as the first R1/R2-MYB structural type. This structure may be related to its multifunctionality. The results showed that the growth rate of transgenic creeping bentgrass was accelerated, the number of leaves per tiller significantly increased, and some leaves showed severe curling, suggesting that EpMYB affected the morphogenesis of transgenic creeping bentgrass. The hormone assay results showed that, compared with the WT, the content of cytokinin in the transgenic plants significantly increased. In the TG-vs-WT group, the cytokinin signal transduction-related genes CRE1 and B-ARR were significantly up-regulated. In addition, the expression level of the CRE1 gene remained almost unchanged in the TG (Drought treatment)-vs-TG group, while it significantly decreased in the WT (Drought treatment)-vs-WT group. This indicates that the EpMYB gene positively regulates the cytokinin signal transduction in transgenic plants. It suggests that the EpMYB gene, as a powerful positive regulatory factor, effectively enhances the endogenous cytokinin signal, thereby driving a comprehensive enhancement of the nutritional growth of plant.

In the TG-vs-WT group, KEGG analysis revealed that a large number of DEGs were enriched in the photosynthetic pathway (Fig. 13), with significantly up-regulated expression, indicating that overexpression of the EpMYB gene could promote photosynthesis and facilitate plant growth in TG plants. ERECTA proteins, a class of leucine-rich receptor-like kinases (RLKs), are involved in the regulation of cell growth and tissue development. In Arabidopsis thaliana erecta mutants, leaf area was reduced and petiole length shortened [22]. Overexpression of the Populus PdERECTA gene in Arabidopsis thaliana resulted in earlier seedling morphogenesis, increased initial root length, larger leaf area, enhanced photosynthetic rate, and greater dry matter accumulation [23]. In this study, the expression of three ERECTA genes was significantly up-regulated, suggesting that the EpMYB gene affected the morphogenesis of transgenic creeping bentgrass by regulating ERECTA expression. The results of this study are consistent with those of the aforementioned studies. Additionally, the Oryza sativa URL1 gene, encoding the homeodomain-leucine zipper (HD-Zip) IV family member RICE OUTERMOST CELL-SPECIFIC 8 (ROC8), has been shown to play an important role in leaf curling [24]. In this study, compared to WT plants, the ROC8 gene in TG plants was significantly up-regulated, indicating that the EpMYB gene may cause severe curling of transgenic creeping bentgrass leaves by regulating the expression of the ROC8 gene. The results of this study support the existing research findings and also provide strong evidence for the research in this area.

Drought stress increases the levels of O2− and H2O2 in plant tissues, inducing rapid accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative damage to proteins and lipids [25, 26]. Plants have developed defense mechanisms to regulate ROS levels, including antioxidant enzymes like POD, SOD, and CAT [27], as well as non-enzymatic antioxidants such as flavonoids [28, 29]. In this study, in the TG-vs-WT group, the expressions of antioxidant enzyme genes (APX6, PER13 and SOD1) and the flavonoid synthesis gene (DFR) were significantly up-regulated. And the expression level of PEX10 gene was significantly increased in the TG (drought treatment)-vs-TG group, while it remained almost unchanged in the WT (drought treatment)-vs-WT group. Furthermore, after the drought treatment, the activities of antioxidant enzymes (POD and CAT) in the TG plants were higher, while the content of MDA was lower. These results indicate that the EpMYB gene positively regulates the peroxisome pathway in the transgenic plants, enhancing the drought tolerance of the TG plants by improving their antioxidant capacity.

Lignin is a key component of the cell wall of plants, which can enhance the mechanical strength of plants, maintain the water transport and osmotic balance in cells, and participate in plant stress response. Some studies have shown that the increase of lignin content positively regulated the drought tolerance of plants. Overexpression of DcDREB1A in Daucus carota improves drought resistance to a certain extent by up-regulating the expression level of lignin biosynthesis-related genes and increasing the lignin content in stems [30]. MeRAV5 gene promotes drought resistance of Manihot esculenta by regulating hydrogen peroxide and lignin accumulation [31]. Overexpression of CtCYP71A1 in Arabidopsis thaliana upregulates proline content and the expressions of PAL and HCT genes related to lignin biosynthesis, and reduces the accumulation of ROS, thus promoting drought tolerance and lignin accumulation [32]. In this study, compared to WT plants, lignin content in above-ground parts of TG plants was significantly increased. The results of high-throughput sequencing showed that in the TG-vs-WT group, the expressions of HCT and CCR1 genes related to lignin synthesis were significantly up-regulated. In addition, the expression level of HCT4 gene in the TG (Drought treatment)-vs-TG group remained almost unchanged, while it was significantly down-regulated in the WT (Drought treatment)-vs-WT group. This indicates that the EpMYB gene positively regulates the lignin synthesis pathway, enhancing the drought resistance of transgenic creeping bentgrass by promoting lignin synthesis. However, further research is needed on how lignin improves drought tolerance of TG plants.

Lipids, as membrane stabilizers, signaling molecules and energy reserves, play a crucial role in stress responses [33]. Glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase (GPAT) uses various aliphatic acyl groups or their derivatives as substrates to catalyze the acylation of glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P) to form lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), a rate-limiting enzyme in the lipid anabolic pathway [34]. Arabidopsis thaliana GPAT family members are involved in the synthesis and metabolism of membrane phospholipids, triglycerides, keratins, and suberin. In this study, in the TG-vs-WT group, multiple Arabidopsis homologous genes GPAT were upregulated, indicating that the EpMYB gene may regulate lipid synthesis by promoting the expression of the GPAT gene, thereby affecting the growth and development of TG plants.

Epidermal wax is a hydrophobic layer formed in the epidermis of plants to prevent non-stomatal water loss. It helps maintain water balance and plays a significant role in drought stress response [35, 36]. In this study, we observed that the content and morphology of wax crystals around stomata in TG plant leaves have undergone significant changes. In the TG-vs-WT group, the wax synthesis-related gene MAH1 was significantly up-regulated. Additionally, the key wax synthesis gene CER1 showed a significantly increased expression level in the TG (Drought treatment)-vs-TG group, while its expression level decreased significantly in the WT (Drought treatment)-vs-WT group. This indicates that the EpMYB gene positively regulates the wax synthesis in transgenic plants, which may help the transgenic plants reduce water loss during the drought treatment. Moreover, This finding may be a key factor contributing to the increased drought resistance in TG plants.

Alpha-linolenic acid (18:3) and linoleic acid (18:2) can act as endogenous antioxidants, directly reacting with ROS to reduce the damage caused by oxidative stress [37, 38]. In this study, the KEGG analysis results of both the TG-vs-WT and TG (Drought treatment)-vs-TG groups showed that many DEGs were enriched in the alpha-linolenic acid and linoleic acid metabolism pathways, which might be one of the reasons for the improved drought tolerance of TG plants. In higher plants, LOXs mainly use linoleic acid and linolenic acid as catalytic substrates to mediate lipid metabolism, with LOX activity often serving as an important indicator of seed vitality and plant stress resistance [39, 40]. The research has found that both the DkLOX3 gene of Diospyros kaki and the CaLOX1 gene of Capsicum annuum can positively regulate the drought stress response of plants [41, 42]. In this study, the LOX genes in the TG-vs-WT group were significantly upregulated, providing evidence that the EpMYB gene promotes unsaturated fatty acid metabolism by regulating LOX gene expression, enabling transgenic creeping bentgrass to actively respond to drought stress and reduce drought-induced damage. This study supports the aforementioned research findings. Overall, the EpMYB gene regulates the synthesis and metabolism of various lipids, positively affecting the growth, development, and stress response of transgenic creeping bentgrass.

Although this study has demonstrated that the EpMYB gene can significantly enhance the growth rate and drought tolerance of transgenic creeping bentgrass under laboratory conditions, several limitations warrant further investigation. Firstly, as the current research was primarily conducted in controlled growth chambers, field trials are needed to validate its performance under natural drought conditions, including assessments of water-use efficiency, long-term survival rates, and potential yield trade-offs. Secondly, the lichen-derived nature of this gene may raise biosafety concerns, necessitating ecological risk evaluations or the development of non-transgenic application strategies. Nevertheless, this study provides important theoretical foundations and technological resources for crop drought resistance improvement. The EpMYB gene and its regulatory network can serve as molecular markers for drought-resistant breeding, while its compact size makes it suitable for constructing smart response systems. Additionally, the genetic resources of the extremely resilient Endocarpon pusillum deserve in-depth exploration. These findings can open up innovative interdisciplinary approaches for the development of new drought-resistant crop varieties.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study mainly outlines the molecular mechanisms by which EpMYB regulates plant growth, development, and drought stress response. Overexpression of this gene in creeping bentgrass resulted in a higher growth rate of both shoots and roots, increased internode elongation, and more leaves on a single tiller compared to WT plants. Additionally, the content of antioxidant enzyme, lignin, wax crystals around the stomata and drought tolerance of the TG plants was significantly enhanced. High-throughput sequencing revealed a number of DEGs related to growth, development, and the response to drought stress, such as genes involved in leaf curling, photosynthesis, peroxisome function, lignin synthesis, cytokinin synthesis and signal transduction. Notably, our study also found that the EpMYB gene regulates the expression of various DEGs related to lipid synthesis and metabolism, suggesting that this gene plays a crucial regulatory role in the lipid metabolism pathway. In summary, the EpMYB gene improves the drought tolerance of transgenic creeping bentgrass primarily by influencing photosynthesis, antioxidant synthesis (including both antioxidant enzymes and abiotic antioxidants), lignin synthesis, wax synthesis, and lipid metabolism. These findings provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms of EpMYB in plant growth, development, and abiotic stress response. Furthermore, they offer a novel approach for improving plant stress resistance by selecting specific functional genes from stress-tolerant microorganisms and applying them to plants.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

Based on the EpMYB (XM_007804716.1) sequence in the NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) database, the open reading frame (ORF) and encoded amino acid sequence of EpMYB were analyzed using DNAMAN software, and the conserved domain of the EpMYB protein was analyzed on the NCBI website. The physicochemical properties of the EpMYB protein were predicted using the ExPASy-ProtParam tool (Expasy online). The secondary structure of the EpMYB protein was analyzed using SOPMA (https://npsa-pbil.ibcp.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl?page=/NPSA/npsa_sopma.html) software [43]. The tertiary structure of the EpMYB protein was analyzed using SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/interactive). SignalP 6.0 (SignalP 6.0 - DTU Health Tech - Bioinformatic Services) was used for protein signal peptide prediction.

Scbcellular localization of EpMYB protein

Tobacco seeds were planted in soil (vermiculite: perlite: peat = 1:1:1) and cultured in a 25 °C light incubator for 4 weeks to obtain mature tobacco plants. The EpMYB gene was linked to the 3302Y vector containing the YFP gene by single enzyme digestion with NcoⅠ and transferred to Agrobacterium EHA105. The primers (F: 5’tgacgcacaatcccactatcctt 3’, R: 5’ccgtccagctcgaccaggat 3’) were designed based on the sequence of 3302Y vector. PCR was performed on the Agrobacterium containing constructed plant expression vector 3303Y-EpMYB using this primer. After gel electrophoresis, the size of the bands was observed. The PCR products with the correct band size were sent to a biological company for sequencing. The sequencing results were compared with the original sequence.

The 3302Y control and 3302Y-EpMYB were transferred into tobacco leaves by Agrobacterium transformation, respectively. After two days of dark culture, the fluorescence signals were captured by laser confocal microscopy following our previous method [44], and the location of the EpMYB gene in plant cells was identified. The cell nucleus was stained with DAPI for further verification. DAPI is a blue fluorescent dye that can penetrate the intact cell membrane and bind to the double-stranded DNA in the cell nucleus, thereby generating a strong fluorescence signal. A few drops of diluted DAPI staining solution were added to the prepared slides, and the slides were stained for 10 min. The staining solution was rinsed off with running water, and the excess water was absorbed by filter paper. The slides were observed under a laser confocal microscope.

Identification of transgenic creeping bentgrass

The transgenic creeping bentgrass was transferred from the culture medium into the soil (vermiculite: nutrient soil: perlite = 1:1:1) and grew into mature plants after 12 weeks of growth. 0.1 g of leaf samples were taken from each transgenic plant for DNA extraction. And PCR was used to detect whether the EpMYB gene was integrated into the genome of creeping bentgrass. 0.1 g of leaf samples were extracted from each transgenic plant for RNA extraction and reverse transcription into cDNA. Then, using cDNA as the material, RT-qPCR was used to determine the expression of the EpMYB gene in TG plants. In addition, the proteins of the transgenic plants were extracted using PBS (pH 7.4), and western blotting was used to assess the translation status of EpMYB in TG plants.

Plant materials, propagation and abiotic stress treatment

WT and TG plants overexpressing EpMYB were transplanted from the growth medium into 15 cm × 15 cm pots filled with soil (vermiculite: nutrient soil: perlite = 1:1:1) and placed under a 16 h/8 h light/dark cycle at 23 °C. After 12 weeks of growth, WT and TG plants were carefully removed from the pots and thoroughly cleaned. Observations were made on the growth of the aboveground.

parts and roots, and measurements were taken for plant fresh weight, number of leaves per tiller, internode length, maximum leaf length, and chlorophyll content. Samples from WT, TG5, and TG11 plants were selected for measurement of these parameters, each with three biological replicates.

After 12 weeks of growth, WT and TG plants with similar growth statuses were selected for natural drought treatment, and the growth states of the plants were observed and photographed daily. On days 0, 10, and 19, leaves from WT and TG plants were collected. These samples were rapidly frozen with liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis of chlorophyll content, soluble protein content, MDA content, and antioxidant enzyme activities including POD and CAT. The physiological indicator measurement experiment involved three repetitions. WT, TG5, and TG11 plants were selected as samples for measuring physiological parameters, each with three biological replicates.

Scanning electron microscopy

Fresh plant leaves were fixed using an electron microscope fixative solution (Servicebiop, G1102-100ML), followed by post-fixation, dehydration, drying, and conductive treatment. The epidermal structure of plant leaves was observed, and images were captured using scanning electron microscopy [45]. Samples from WT, TG5, and TG11 plants were selected for scanning electron microscopy, with each sample having three biological replicates.

Determination of chlorophyll content, soluble protein, lignin content, antioxidant enzyme content and malondialdehyde content

Frozen leaf samples (0.08 g) were finely chopped and incubated in 8 mL of 95% ethanol for 48 h in darkness. The chlorophyll extraction was then quantified by measuring absorbance at 665 nm, 649 nm, and 470 nm using a microplate reader, with subsequent calculations of chlorophyll content according to standard protocols [46].

Frozen leaf tissue (0.15 g) was ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in 2 mL of ice-cold PBS (pH 7.0). The homogenate was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant (enzyme extracting solution) was collected and stored at 4 °C for subsequent assays, including POD, CAT, soluble protein content, and MDA determination. For the determination of POD, the reaction mixture contained 1 mL of 50 mM PBS (pH 7.0), 0.95 mL of 0.2% guaiacol, 50 µL of enzyme extracting solution (PBS for blank), and 1 mL of 0.3% H₂O₂. The increase in absorbance at 470 nm was recorded for 1 min using a ultraviolet spectrophotometer [47]. For the determination of CAT, the assay mixture consisted of 2.84 mL of 50 mM PBS (pH 7.0), 100 µL of enzyme extracting solution (PBS for blank), and 30 µL of 1.5 M H₂O₂. The decrease in absorbance at 240 nm was monitored for 1 min [48]. For the determination of soluble protein content, a standard curve was prepared with bovine serum albumin (BSA) (0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8 mL) mixed with Coomassie Brilliant Blue reagent, and the volume was adjusted with distilled water. Enzyme extracting solution (100 µL) was mixed with 5 mL of Coomassie Brilliant Blue, incubated for 5 min, and measured absorbance value at 595 nm using a microplate reader [50]. For the determination of MDA content, 1 mL of enzyme extracting solution was mixed with 2 mL of 0.25% thiobarbituric acid (TBA), heated in a boiling water bath for 30 min, and then cooled on ice. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant absorbance was measured at 532 nm and 600 nm using a microplate reader.

For the determination of lignin content, sample preparation was initiated by drying and grinding above-ground tissue samples, followed by sieving through a 40-mesh screen. Approximately 10 mg of the sieved powder was transferred to a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube and mixed with 1.5 mL of 80% ethanol. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly and incubated in a water bath at 50 °C for 20 min. After cooling to room temperature under running water, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was retained. This extraction procedure was repeated once with 1 mL of 80% ethanol. The final pellet was dried at 95 °C for subsequent analysis. Lignin extraction was performed according to the protocol of a lignin assay kit (Geruisi, G0708W). Briefly, Reagent 1 was added to the prepared samples, followed by incubation at 70 °C for 30 min. After addition of Reagent 2 and acetic acid, the mixture was vortexed and centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 5 min at room temperature. For quantification, 100 µL of supernatant was mixed with 100 µL acetic acid, and the absorbance was measured at 280 nm using a microplate reader. The lignin content was calculated based on the standard curve equation provided in the kit instructions. Samples from WT, TG5, and TG11 plants were selected to determine the lignin content, with each sample having three biological replicates.

Determination of hormone content in leaves

Phytohormone quantification was performed using competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Fresh plant leaf tissue (500 mg) was homogenized in 2 mL of ice-cold extraction buffer (80% methanol containing 1 mM tert-butyl-p-cresol) using a pre-chilled mortar. The homogenate was transferred to centrifuge tubes and incubated at 4 °C for 4 h for complete hormone extraction, followed by centrifugation at 3,500 × g for 8 min at 4°C. The pellet was re-extracted with 1 mL of fresh buffer for 1 h at 4 °C and centrifuged again. Combined supernatants were purified through C-18 solid-phase extraction columns preconditioned with 1 mL 80% methanol, followed by sequential washing with 5 mL 100% methanol, 5 mL 100% diethyl ether, and 5 mL 100% methanol (two cycles). Eluates were concentrated by vacuum evaporation, reconstituted in 10 mL PBS-T (0.01 M phosphate buffer with 0.1% Tween-20, pH 7.4), and filtered (0.22 µm). For ELISA, standard curves were generated using hormone standards, with samples and standards processed in triplicate: primary antibody incubation (37°C, 1 h), PBS-T washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody addition, 3,3’,5,5”-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate reaction. The absorbance values of the samples were measured at 490 nm using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent spectrophotometer [49]. The calculation method followed the protocols described previously [50]. The hormone determination experiment involves three repetitions. Samples from WT, TG5 and TG11 plants were selected for the determination of hormone content, with each sample having three biological replicates.

Statistical tests

The statistical data were visualized using Prism 9 software, and significance between the WT and TG samples was analyzed using the unpaired t-test.

RNA extraction and gene expression assay

Total RNA was extracted from 100 mg of fresh leaf samples using RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (TIANGEN, DP432) following the method of [17]. The RNA was treated with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, RR047A) to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. The RNA purity and concentration were determined using the Thermo Fisher Nanodrop 2000 instrument, and the integrity of the RNA was detected by the LabChip GX (model LabChip GX Touch HT) tool [44]. Using Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR Ver. 2 (ACCURATE BIOLOGY, AG11728), RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA as the reaction template for RT-qPCR. Through the design of primers for the RT-qPCR reaction, the length of the template sequence in the actual reaction was controlled to be less than 200 base pairs (Table 4).

RT-qPCR was conducted using SYBR® Green Premix Pro Tag HS qPCR Kit (ACCURATE BIOLOGY, AG11701). The ubiquitin gene of creeping bentgrass, AsUBQ (JX570760), served as the reference gene [51, 52]. Reaction system: 2X SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix 10 µL, forward primer 1 µL, reverse primer 1 µL, supplemented with RNase-free water to a total volume of 20 µL. The relative expression level of each gene was calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method [53, 54].

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was extracted from untreated WT, untreated TG5 single line, drought-treated WT, and drought-treated TG5 single line (each treatment consisting of three biological replicates, resulting in a total of 12 samples). The RNA template material for transcriptome sequencing is the same as that for qRT-PCR. The RNA was treated with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, RR047A) to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. The detection of RNA concentration, purity and integrity is the same as described above. Sequencing libraries were generated using the VAHTSTM Universal DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® V2 (NuoWeiZan, ND627-01), following the provided instructions, and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. The quality of Illumina libraries was assessed using the Agilent Biological Analyzer 2100 system, and library sequencing was performed on a HiSeq4000 instrument.

After removing reads containing adapter sequences and filtering low-quality reads from raw data, clean data were obtained and used for transcriptome analysis [55]. Expression level were estimated using RSEM (version 1.2.31), and the expression abundance of unigenes was expressed by the fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments (FPKM) values. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 software with the following parameters: adjusted p-value < 0.01 and |log2FC| >= 1 [56, 57]. The original data obtained from transcriptome sequencing have been uploaded to NCBI database at the login number PRJNA906343: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/PRJNA906343.
