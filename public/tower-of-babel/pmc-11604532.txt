Anti-CD19 CAR-T cells are effective in severe idiopathic Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome
Authors: Jonathan Wickel, Ulf Schnetzke, Anne Sayer-Klink, Jenny Rinke, Dominic Borie, Diana Dudziak, Andreas Hochhaus, Lukas Heger, Christian Geis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryLambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS) is an autoantibody-mediated disease of the neuromuscular junction characterized by muscular weakness. Autoantibodies to presynaptic P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs) induce defective neuromuscular function. In severe cases, current immunosuppressive and immunomodulatory treatment strategies are often insufficient. First reports show beneficial effects of anti-CD19 chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy in patients with autoantibody-mediated myasthenia gravis. We report a patient with isolated idiopathic LEMS treated with autologous anti-CD19-CAR-T cells. In this patient, CAR-T infusion leads to expansion of predominantly CD4+ CAR-T cells with a terminally differentiated effector memory cells re-expressing CD45RA (TEMRA)-like phenotype indicating cytotoxic capabilities and subsequent B cell depletion. VGCC antibody titers decrease, resulting in a clinical improvement of LEMS symptoms, e.g., 8-fold increase in walking distance. The patient does not show relevant side effects except for cytokine release syndrome grade 2 and intermittent neutropenia suggesting that anti-CD19 CAR-T cell therapy may be a treatment option in patients with LEMS.

Introduction

Firstly described in 1956,1 Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS) is a rare and often tumor-associated autoimmune disorder of the neuromuscular junction. Typical clinical symptoms include progressive weakness of trunk muscles, ptosis and double vision, dysarthria, and dysphagia. Typically, pathogenic autoantibodies (Abs) targeting voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs) can be detected.2 These VGCC Abs are predominantly directed against channels of the P/Q-type. In contrast to Abs causing myasthenia gravis (MG), which are directed to postsynaptic targets at the neuromuscular junction, VGCC Abs bind to presynaptic VGCC at the motor neuron synaptic terminals. P/Q-type VGCC Abs are directly pathogenic as they cause a reduction of effective presynaptic calcium channels and therefore lead to diminished presynaptic vesicle release and defective neuromuscular transmission, in particular at resting conditions and upon short-term activation.3,4,5 Sustained stimulation of presynaptic calcium channels, e.g., by prolonged muscle activation, results in increased amplitudes of compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs) in electrophysiological recordings, which also reflects a clinically relevant diagnostic criterion for LEMS.1

The reversible potassium channel blocker amifampridine is used to symptomatically treat LEMS6,7 as it prolongs the action potential so that calcium channels stay open longer with a subsequent increase of acetylcholine release. Causal treatment involves tumor therapy (if applicable) and immunosuppressive therapy or immunomodulatory therapy with prednisone, azathioprine, intravenous immunoglobulins, and/or plasma exchange.8 Direct depletion of antibody-producing B cells with rituximab has been attempted in cases refractory to broadly immunosuppressive approaches.9 Neither of these approaches has proven effective in severe cases and furthermore is exposing patients to the infectious risks of long-term immunosuppressive therapy.

Recently, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells targeting CD19 or B cell maturation antigen (BCMA) have been reported to be effective in patients with MG.10,11 Furthermore, Motte et al. very recently described two patients with typical MG and Abs to the acetylcholine receptor and additional low-titer N-type VGCC Abs who had a striking treatment response after anti-CD19 CAR-T cell therapy.12

We here present a case of idiopathic LEMS with isolated high-titer P/Q-type Abs to presynaptic VGCC who significantly improved upon treatment with anti-CD19 CAR-T cells.

Results

Patient history and disease course of severe LEMS

A 54-year-old male patient presented with a gradually progressive, activity-dependent, symmetric tetraparesis and a severe gait disorder. Additional symptoms included tremor, dysarthria, dysphagia, and double vision. Electrodiagnostic studies revealed a reproducible increase in CMAP following postexercise facilitation (+390%) (Figure 1A) suggestive for LEMS. 4 weeks later, a diagnosis of idiopathic LEMS was made after high-titer P/Q-type VGCC Abs were detected in serum (348 pmol/L; normal range <40 pmol/L), with no tumor detected on repeated positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) scans (Figure 1B). The patient's medical history was remarkable for a prior diagnosis of Graves' disease and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) B receptor Ab-positive autoimmune encephalitis, which presented one year earlier with a first epileptic seizure, indicating a general predisposition for autoimmune disorders. When LEMS was diagnosed, the patient still showed GABA B receptor Abs and hippocampal MRI hyperintensities, so immunosuppressive therapy, which was initially started for autoimmune encephalitis, was continued and intensified due to occurrence and worsening of LEMS symptoms. Immunosuppressive therapy included multiple courses of high-dose steroids followed by tapering; continuous low-dose steroids (4 mg), which had been discontinued before CAR-T cell therapy; plasma exchange/immunoadsorption; rituximab (1,000 mg intravenous day 1 and 15 as well as 6 months apart; terminated 14 months before CAR-T cell therapy); and participation in a randomized, placebo-controlled trial (NCT03993262) investigating the proteasome inhibitor bortezomib for the treatment of autoimmune encephalitis.13 The use of bortezomib/placebo consisted of three cycles with four subcutaneous and weight-adapted applications over 9 weeks and was terminated 34 weeks before CAR-T cell therapy.

As a symptomatic treatment for LEMS, amifampridine was introduced and subsequently increased to the maximum dose. Following immunosuppressive therapy, symptoms of autoimmune encephalitis completely resolved without further epileptic seizures or behavior and memory abnormalities, and Abs to the GABA B receptor were no longer detectable. However, LEMS symptoms, especially tetraparesis, dysarthria, and double vision, showed only minimal improvement. At baseline, the quantitative myasthenia gravis (QMG) score (range 0 [normal] to 39 [most severe]) was 18 and the Besinger score (range 0 [normal] to 3 [most severe]) was 1.5. MG activities of daily living (MG-ADL) (range 0 [normal] to 24 [most severe]) were scored at 13. The patient was able to walk 11 m using a support walker. He could hold his legs for maximum 10 s and his arms for 204 s.

CAR-T cell expansion and B cell depletion after autologous anti-CD19 CAR-T cell infusion

Given the severity of the patient’s LEMS with insufficient response to immunomodulatory and immunosuppressive therapy and symptomatic treatment, we evaluated the patient for treatment with anti-CD19 CAR-T cell therapy. The treatment was administered in a compassionate use setting after approval from state authorities and the institutional ethics committee (Reg. No. 2024-3206-iH). Informed consent was obtained from the patient. Following leukapheresis, the patient’s CD3+ T cells were transduced with a second-generation anti-CD19 CAR construct (KYV-101, Kyverna Therapeutics, Inc., CA, USA) as previously described12 (Figure 2A), resulting in in vitro CAR/KYV-101 expression in 58% of CD3+ T lymphocytes. Expansion of CAR-T cells was observed starting on day +5 after infusion with a peak expansion on day +7 (192 cells/mL; 50% of total CD3+ T cells) (Figure 2B). Subsequently, CAR-T cells were detectable until day +29 (Figures 2B and S1). Similar to two previously reported clinical cases using the same CAR construct,12 CAR-T cell expansion was largely predominated by CD4+ CAR-T cells (Figure 2C), which is different from the first report of CD19 CAR-T cells in MG with expansion driven by CD8+ CAR-T cells.10 A decline in CD25 expression among CD4+ and CD8+ CAR-T cells following transfusion was noted, which correlated with the decrease in CAR-T cell count over time (Figures 2D and 2E). Treatment with anti-CD19 CAR-T cells resulted in subsequent depletion of CD19+ B cells in peripheral blood (Figure 2F). As part of the high-affinity interleukin (IL)-2 receptor,14 CD25 is essential for expansion and survival of T cells. Thus, the decline in CD25 expression might correspond to reduced activation of the CAR-T cells as deep elimination of the CD19+ B cell pool (Figure 2F) removed the targets for the CAR construct. Two weeks after CAR-T cells were no longer detectable in peripheral blood, B cells started to repopulate (Figure 2F).

CD4+ CD19 CAR-T cells show a Th1-like and a high-effector TEMRA-like phenotype

In order to monitor the induced immune response by transfused CAR-T cells, the phenotype of the CAR-T cells was analyzed by multicolor flow cytometry. For an efficient CAR-T cell-induced CD19+ B cell depletion, Th1 as well as cytotoxic CD8+ T cells are needed.15 Therefore, CXCR3 expression was monitored on CAR-T cells, which is preferentially expressed on Th1-polarized CD4+ T cells as well as on CD8+ cytotoxic effector T cells.16 During peak expansion of CAR-T cells, CXCR3 expression was increased on both CD4+ and CD8+ CAR-T cells (Figures 3A and S2). Further, based on their CD45RA and CCR7 expression, T cells can be distinguished into naive (CD45RA+CCR7+), effector memory (CD45RA−CCR7-), central memory (CD45RA−CCR7+), and terminally differentiated effector memory cells re-expressing CD45RA (TEMRA) (CD45RA+CCR7-). Both CD4+ as well as CD8+ CAR-T cells showed a predominant TEMRA-like phenotype (Figures 3B, 3C, and S3). In contrast, very few TEMRA-like T cells were observed among the CAR− CD4+ T cells which repopulated after lymphodepletion (Figure 3D). LEMS symptoms improve upon anti-CD19 CAR-T cell treatment

Due to cytokine release syndrome (CRS) with fever and general sickness, LEMS symptoms initially deteriorated but returned to baseline level until hospital discharge on day +14. In the subsequent weeks, neuromuscular weakness as measured by the QMG/Besinger scores showed a continuous improvement (Figures 4A and 4B). General improvement of activities of daily living following CAR-T cell therapy was also reflected in a reduction in the MG-ADL score (Figure 4C). Despite being still restricted to a support walker, the walking distance steadily increased from 11 to 90 m (Figure 4D), which was also reflected in a pronounced longer leg-holding time and arm-holding time (legs: baseline: 10 s, day 85: 306 s; arms: baseline: 204 s, day 85: 287 s) (Figures 4E and 4F). On day +29 after CAR-T cell infusion, the patient was able to change his position in the bed without help, and on day +41 he was able to shave himself without assistance. Independent mobilization in bed and shaving had not been possible for the preceding two years. This clinical improvement was paralleled by a profound reduction of pathogenic P/Q-type VGCC Abs to approximately 40% at day 43 (Figure 4G), which might be attributed to the CAR-T cell-mediated depletion of CD19-positive plasmablasts. Pathogenic GABA B receptor Abs were no longer detectable at baseline (data not shown) indicating that neurologic symptoms were exclusively caused by VGCC Abs-driven LEMS. Acceptable safety profile of anti-CD19 CAR-T cell treatment

Due to the lymphodepleting chemotherapy, the patient developed grade 1 nausea on day −2, which was managed with antiemetic therapy. Starting from day +2 after CAR-T cell infusion, he developed fever and hypotension consistent with CRS. Correspondingly, IL-6, C-reactive protein (CRP), and ferritin levels increased (Figures S4A–S4C). Vasopressor therapy was required for less than 24 h to stabilize blood pressure, classifying the CRS as grade 217. The patient received 3 doses of tocilizumab (600 mg), with CRS symptoms resolving on day +7. No signs of immune effector cell-associated neurological syndrome or epileptic seizures were observed. Non-infectious diarrhea on day +5 was treated with fluids and was most likely due to lymphodepletion. Prolonged CAR-T-associated neutropenia was treated with repetitive injections of granulocyte growth factor (Figure S4D), with no infections occurring during this period. During neutropenia, the patient received anti-infective prophylaxis with trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and acyclovir. Blood transfusions were not required (neither erythrocytes nor platelets). Hemoglobin levels remained above 5.5 mmol/L (Figure S4E), and platelet counts maintained levels exceeding 100/nL throughout the observation period (Figure S4F). Notably, immunoglobulin (Ig)G levels were reduced following CAR-T infusion but consistently remained above 4.5 g/L, obviating the need for immunoglobulin substitution (Figure S4G). IgA and IgM levels also declined but largely remained in the normal range throughout the observation period (Figures S4H and S4I).

Discussion

CAR-T cell therapy is currently emerging as a new, potentially revolutionizing approach in neuroimmunology. First reports of individual patients with severe neurological diseases have shown favorable treatment response, e.g., in MG,10,11,12,18 neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder,19 and stiff person syndrome (SPS).20 So far, CD19 and BCMA CAR constructs have been applied, targeting either a broad range of B cell subsets, i.e., pro-B cells to late plasmablasts (CD19 constructs), or plasma cells and plasma blasts (BCMA constructs) with promising clinical results (reviewed by Chung et al.21). Here, we add to this emerging field by reporting clinical success and acceptable tolerability as well as T cell phenotyping in a patient with isolated idiopathic LEMS with exclusive pathogenic Abs against a presynaptic VGCC treated with CD19 CAR-T cells.

Treatment of LEMS refractory to standard immunosuppressive therapy and symptomatic treatment remains a challenge. Motte et al. reported two cases treated with anti-CD19 CAR-T cells who had typical acetylcholine receptor Ab-positive MG and concomitant positive N-type VGCC Abs possibly contributing to disease symptoms.12 Both patients and another MG patient with anti-CD19 CAR-T cells10 demonstrated substantial treatment responses and even returned to normal daily activities within two months.

The patient reported here received a flat dose of 1.2 × 108 cells of the same CD19 CAR-T construct, which, in this 80 kg patient, amounts to an approximate dose of 1.5 × 106 CAR-T cells/kg. The rationale for selecting this dose was based on considering that, in the hematology setting, KYV-101 CAR-T cells showed comparable efficacy and safety profiles across 3 dose levels, including 0.66, 2, and 6 × 106 CAR+ T cells/kg.22 No dose-dependent effects were observed with this weight-based dose range, which translates to approximately 0.5, 1.4, and 4.2 × 108 total CAR+ T cells (assuming 70 kg body weight). Thus, the dose used in this patient and in other patients with neurological autoimmune diseases treated with KYV-10110,12,20,23,24 was set in the lower part of the dose range already tested in the hematology setting and is being further characterized in clinical trials (NCT06400303, NCT06342960). Second, this flat dose of 1.2 × 108 CAR-T cells is also consistent with the range of doses approved for the anti-CD19 CAR-T cell commercial products (approximately 0.4–5.2 × 108 CAR+ T cells, if standardized to total CAR+ T cell dose based on 70 kg body weight). Last, other CD19 CAR-T cells have been successfully administered in autoimmune diseases at doses of 1.0 × 106 CAR-T cells/kg, corresponding to absolute cell doses ranging from 0.44 × 108 to 0.91 × 108 CAR-T cells.25,26,27,28 While it is important to consider the number of cells infused, it should be remembered that with this new concept of “living drug,” the level of CAR-T cell expansion in the body after injection is a significant driver of efficacy.29 As reported in few individual patients with neuroimmunological diseases treated with KYV-101, CD19 CAR-T cells rapidly expanded preferentially as CD4+ CAR-T cells.12 The expanded CAR-T cells in our patient showed primarily an effector cell/TEMRA phenotype, whereas only few cells had a central memory phenotype.30,31 Deep B cell depletion by terminally differentiated effector cells with high cytotoxic functions, although of shorter duration, might be effective to induce a naive B cell repertoire in patients with non-oncological, immune-mediated disorders.32 The effector memory/TEMRA phenotype of the CAR-T cells in our patient might be at least partially attributed to the CD28 costimulatory domain of KYV-101. This domain is known to induce faster signaling compared to 4-1BB-based CAR constructs and may lead to the generation of effector memory rather than central memory T cells.33,34 We observed that the CAR-T cell level in the blood decreased below the detection limit after 30 days, which is in accordance with other neuroimmunological patients treated with KYV-101.10,12,20 4-1BB-based CAR-T cells may decline with a similar time course as reported in autoimmune diseases treated with CAR-T cells targeting CD1927,28,35,36 whereas some reports show persistence of anti-BMCA CAR-T cells for several months in neuroimmunological disorders.19,37 Whether this difference in CAR-T cell persistence is due to the targeted antigen, patient characteristics, or influence of the costimulatory domains is unclear. The empirical belief that 4-1BB-based constructs could be associated with higher CAR-T cell persistence is still under debate (reviewed by Cappell et al.38). The role of the costimulatory domains as well as the influence of the target antigen will therefore need to be firmly established through clinical studies.

The superior efficacy and possibly more sustained treatment effect of anti-CD19 CAR-T cell therapy for autoimmune diseases compared to anti-CD20 antibody-based therapies (e.g., rituximab) are attributed to both the capacity of CAR-T cells to enter tissues and the extensive depletion of several additional B cell subsets (including pro-B cells, plasmablasts, and early plasma cells, which do not express CD20). In contrast to the temporary depletion of circulating B cells by anti-CD20 antibody-based therapies, anti-CD19 CAR-T cells lead to deep depletion of the B cell lineage also in tissue.21,32 Interestingly, and possibly paradoxically, given that B cell depletion with rituximab in tissue is not as extensive as that achieved with CD19 CAR-T approaches (reviewed by Schett et al.39), the time to B cell repopulation after anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies ranges from 6 to 9 months40,41 and may be longer than that reported for CD19 CAR-T cell approaches. The underlying mechanism of this observation is so far unclear and may be due to increased levels of available B cell growth factors (e.g., B cell-activating factor [BAFF]) after CD19 CAR-T cell therapy and will need to be solidified by further characterization of the impact of CAR-T cells in autoimmunity. We further hypothesize that the extensive B (precursor) cell depletion may facilitate repopulation of naive B cells, thus leading to a reset of the B cell repertoire.25,32 During in vivo CAR-T cell expansion, increased expression of CXCR3 on CD4+ and CD8+ CAR-T cells was observed, indicating that expanded CAR-T cells are rather Th1-polarized T cells. Increased CXCR3 expression may potentially also contribute to better tissue infiltration, which might be important for effective CAR-T cell therapy in autoimmune diseases.19

In our patient, B cell reemergence starting from day 50 appeared short compared to other reports, but it was in line with what has been observed in autoimmune diseases. In a series of 15 patients with rheumatologic conditions treated with a CD19 CAR-T cells, B cells returned after 72 to 153 days (median 100 days), contrasting with the reported 2–59 months (median 12 months) of B cell recovery time for hematology patients.42 The reasons for these differences across diseases remain unclear and may be related to variations in bone marrow reserve and function. Unlike patients with B cell neoplasms, those with autoimmune diseases have not been subjected to multiple prior cytotoxic therapies, including various chemotherapy regimens that affect stem cells and the immune cell repertoire. Another plausible explanation could be the rapid elimination of cells expressing the target antigen (CD19), as the B cell load is significantly lower in autoimmune diseases than in patients with B cell neoplasms. Consequently, the loss of the target antigen may trigger an early reduction of the CAR-T cell population,32 which might also be reflected in the loss of CAR-T cell CD25 expression, as reported here.

At this time, it does not appear that the time to B cell reconstitution is a key driver of the long-term therapeutic response, as B cell reemergence after CAR-T cell therapy was not associated with disease recurrence in systemic lupus erythematosus, and all patients remained in treatment-free remission and did not produce double-stranded DNA Abs.35 Rather, it appears that a predictor of long-term success might reside in the initially demonstrated disappearance of memory B cells and plasmablasts, followed by the reappearance of newly emerging B cells in the circulation that display IgM and IgD heavy chains indicating a pre-class switched naive B cell phenotype.32 This early B cell repopulation in autoimmune diseases may also reduce the risk for hypogammaglobulinemia and severe infections.

In the patient reported here, we observed a significant improvement of LEMS symptoms within the three months following CAR-T cell infusion including marked amelioration in walking distance and leg-holding time. During the observation period, clinical improvement correlates to a reduction of pathogenic VGCC Ab titers indicating a relevant reduction of CD19-expressing antibody-producing plasmablasts.43 It is important to note that, similar to MG, Ab levels in LEMS only serve as a paraclinical surrogate parameter, which often does not directly correspond to the clinical status at a specific time point. Moreover, B cell contribution to disease pathogenesis goes well beyond their role as antibody-producing cells and may explain why notable clinical improvement can be observed in the absence of reduction of Ab titers.24 B cells may also contribute to disease by producing detrimental cytokines or by assisting T cells through antigen-presenting cell functions.21 The transient deterioration of the patient’s condition in the early phase after CAR-T cell infusion may be explained by the clinical impact of CRS, followed by a slight improvement over baseline levels prior to a decrease in Ab levels.

In contrast to prior reports using the same CAR construct in MG10,12 patients, treatment response was less pronounced in the first two months in our LEMS patient, who remained reliant on walking support, and the reduction of Ab titer was less pronounced. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in patient age (our patient being older), gender (our patient being male), and/or different pathophysiology of LEMS and MG.44 The faster reemergence of B cells in peripheral blood as compared to prior cases, potentially indicating an incomplete depletion of tissue-based B cells, may have also contributed to observed clinical differences. In line with previous reports, clinical improvement and decrease of pathogenic Abs continued after B cell repopulation during the observation time. This is in accordance with the hypothesis that the extensive B (precursor) cell depletion may facilitate repopulation of naive B cells.

As another indication of the mechanistic impact of anti-CD19 CAR-T cells, Abs against TPO normalized in our patient and treatment for Graves' disease could be stopped. This underlines the potential efficacy of anti-CD19 CAR-T cells in treating multiple autoimmune disorders within a single patient.

While there exist only few reports on the treatment of patients with LEMS with cyclophosphamide45,46 but none with fludarabine or tocilizumab, we cannot exclude a possible beneficial effect of the lymphodepleting regimen administered prior to CAR-T cell therapy or of tocilizumab applied for the management of CRS. Nevertheless, given the selective reset of B cells that has been described in other reports of autoimmune diseases,35 the mid-term therapeutic effect reported here is more likely attributable to the action of CD19 CAR-T cells. Controlled studies in neuroimmunological diseases are needed to evaluate the intrinsic effect of CD19 CAR-T cells independent from the lymphodepleting regimen.

In terms of adverse events, our patient experienced a grade 2 CRS and prolonged neutropenia without signs of infections. Acute cytopenias, including neutropenia, observed within the 28 days that follow CAR-T infusion are an anticipated side effect of the lymphodepleting regimen.21,29 Grade 3 febrile neutropenia has been reported in 17%–31% of hematology subjects.47,48 Not all lymphodepleting regimen are equal as bendamustine-based lymphodepletion was associated with significantly less hematopoietic toxicities and earlier granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF) use than the cyclophosphamide and fludarabine regimen used in our patient.49 Late neutropenia is less frequently observed, reported in 11% of subjects with hematological disease after 3 months50 and possibly caused by the CAR-T product. However, its mechanism is still poorly understood. In hematology patients, baseline cytopenia, inflammatory state, higher-grade CRS, and multiple previous line of therapies have been identified as possible risk factors.51,52 Potential underlying pathophysiologic mechanisms include systemic immune dysregulation at baseline, resulting in impaired CAR-T expansion and myeloid-related inflammatory changes in cases of prolonged neutropenia.53 While the risk of cytopenia appears less prominent in patients with autoimmune diseases,21,35 its prevalence and possible relationship with CAR-T persistence or other risk factors will need to be addressed through accruing clinical experience. It is noteworthy that, during the observation period, the patient did not show clinically relevant hypogammaglobulinemia. Of note, although the patient had epileptic seizures during the preceding period of encephalitis, no epileptic seizures occurred after CAR-T cell therapy (no prophylactic antiepileptic therapy was given). As CAR-T-related adverse events17 and their mitigations approaches are now well established, this led our patient to a favorable treatment response, ultimately resulting in an acceptable and manageable safety profile of anti-CD19 CAR-T cells.

Limitations of the study

Data presented here are based on a single case report without an adequate control due to the treatment in a compassionate use setting. Furthermore, due to the relatively short observation period, we cannot report long-term data on efficacy or safety. Future randomized controlled studies are needed to evaluate the use and safety profile of anti-CD19 CAR-T cells as a highly effective cellular immunomodulatory therapy approach in refractory neuroimmunological disorders such as LEMS, MG, SPS, or multiple sclerosis.

Experimental model and study participant details

Patient

The patient was a 54-year-old Caucasian man who was working as a warehouseman prior to disease. Pre-existing conditions included a depressive episode (treated with escitalopram), Graves' disease, and GABA B receptor Ab positive autoimmune encephalitis. 7 years prior to CAR-T cell treatment, the patient stopped smoking (30 pack years). The patient had no further cardiovascular or renal diseases. The patient provided informed consent for CAR-T cell treatment and publication. Treatment with autologous CAR-T cells received approval by state authorities in a compassionate use setting. Under German law, individualized treatment approaches can be made in the case of life-threatening diseases and failure of approved therapies.

Method details

CAR-T cell therapy

Leukapheresis was performed to collect autologous lymphocytes. CD3+ T cells were selected and enriched. Subsequently, CD3+ T cells were transduced with a lentiviral vector encoding a second-generation anti-CD19 CAR construct (KYV-101, Kyverna Therapeutics, Inc., CA, USA) and expanded in vitro. Following lymphodepletion (cyclophosphamide, 300 mg/m2, fludarabine, 30 mg/m2; days −5 to −3) and 2 days of rest, the patient was infused with 1.2 x 108 CAR+ T cells autologous anti-CD19 CAR-T cells (day 0).

Clinical assessments and autoantibody testing

Baseline and follow-up clinical assessments were performed by the same neurologist experienced in the treatment of myasthenic syndromes. Care was taken to carry out the assessments at the same time of day. MG-ADL was completed by the patient.

Anti-P/Q-type VGCC antibodies were tested using a commercial radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA; cutoff 40 pmol/L; MVZ Labor PD Dr. Volkmann & Kollegen GbR, Germany).

Flow cytometry

Mononuclear cells (MNCs) were freshly isolated from peripheral blood by gradient density centrifugation at regular intervals before and after CAR-T cell infusion. Initially, peripheral blood samples were mixed with serum-free medium (RPMI 1640, ThermoFisher) at a ratio of 1:2. Subsequently, 25 mL of the diluted blood was carefully layered onto 15 mL of Biocoll (Bio&SELL) in a 50 mL Falcon tube. The mixture was centrifuged at room temperature for 40 min at 1500 rpm (Eppendorf 5810 R) with minimum acceleration and without applying the brake. Following centrifugation, the upper plasma phase was removed to facilitate access to the mononuclear cell ring, which was carefully transferred into a new 50 mL Falcon tube and washed twice with RPMI medium, centrifuging each wash for 10 min at 1500 rpm. After counting the cells, the pellet was resuspended in the appropriate amount of freezing medium (60% IMDM, 30% FBS, 10% DMSO), and 1 mL aliquots were dispensed into labeled cryo-vials. Samples were frozen overnight at −80°C and subsequently transferred to −159°C until further processing.

For flow cytometric analysis, PBMCs were thawed in a water bath at 37°C, transferred to 50mL Falcons containing PBS +0.5% BSA (FACS buffer) to dilute DMSO used for freezing, and centrifuged for 5min with 520 × g at 4°C. Subsequently, cells were filtered using 70μm cell strainers and centrifuged for 5min with 520 × g at 4°C. 10μL Precision Count Beads (corresponding to a total of 10,300 beads) were added to the cells and directly transferred to a 96-well V-bottom plate. After centrifugation for 5min with 520 × g at 4°C, supernatant was removed and the pellets were stained in 50μL of antibody staining mix for 30min on ice. The antibody staining mix consisted of the following antibodies/reagents diluted in FACS buffer: anti-human CCR6 BV605 (1:50, clone: G034E3, BioLegend, Cat# 353420), anti-human CD4 BV510 (1:50, clone: SK3, BioLegend, Cat# 344634, anti-human CD8 PE/Cy7 (1.100, clone: RPA-T8, BD Biosciences, Cat#557746), anti-human CD25 PerCP/Cy5.5 (1:100, clone: M-A251, BioLegend, #Cat 356112), anti-human CCR7 PE (1:100, clone: G043H7, BioLegend, #Cat 353204), anti-human CD3 FITC (1:100, clone: SK7, BioLegend, #Cat 344804), anti-human CXCR3 APC/Cy7 (1:50, clone: G025H7, BioLegend, #Cat 353722), anti-human CD45RA Alexa Fluor 700 (1:50, clone: HI100, BioLegend, #Cat 304120), and CD19 CAR Detection Reagent Biotin (1:50, Miltenyi Biotec, #Cat 130-129-550). After addition of 100μL FACS buffer, cells were centrifuged for 5min with 520 × g at 4°C and the supernatant was removed. Cells were resuspended in 150μL FACS buffer and centrifuged for 5min with 520 × g at 4°C. After removal of the supernatant, cells were stained with 50μL of APC-coupled anti-Biotin antibody (diluted 1:50 in FACS buffer, clone: REA746, Miltenyi Biotec, #Cat 130-110-952) for 15min on ice. After two rounds of washing, cells were diluted with DAPI-containing FACS buffer (1:1,000), filtered over 30μm Cell Strainer Snap Caps, and acquired using a BDFACS Canto (laser lines: 405nm, 488nm, 635nm). Data analysis was performed with FlowJo (BD Bioscience, V10).

Quantification and statistical analysis

As we report a single case without a control patient, no statistical analyses were performed. Graphs were created using OriginPro (v. 2020b) and Inkscape.
