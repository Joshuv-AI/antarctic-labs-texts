Investigating the alleviatory ability of bio-synthesized zinc oxide nanoparticles from Sargassum ilicifolium (Turner) C. Agardh on the tomato plants exposed to whitefly infestation
Authors: Jabraeil Razmjou, Mozhgan Mardani-Talaee, Perumal Vivekanandhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGreen nanoparticle synthesis presents a sustainable agricultural approach that reduces dependence on chemical pesticides while promoting environmental sustainability. The whitefly (Trialeurodes vaporariorum), a major sap-sucking pest of tomato plants, causes significant yield losses worldwide. In this study, we developed an eco-friendly management strategy using zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) biosynthesized from the brown seaweed Sargassum ilicifolium, a rich source of bioactive compounds. The nanosized ZnO-NPs (5–65 nm) were applied as a foliar spray and effectively mitigated whitefly-induced oxidative stress in tomato plants. Foliar application of ZnO-NPs at 50 ppm significantly increased chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoid contents compared to the control. This treatment also enhanced the activities of antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase, catalase, and phenylalanine ammonia-lyase. Additionally, ZnO-NPs at 50 and 100 ppm significantly reduced malondialdehyde and hydrogen peroxide levels. ZnO-NPs exhibited strong insecticidal activity against T. vaporariorum, achieving 96.66% mortality at 1000 ppm (LC₅₀ = 407.4 ppm after 96 h). ZnO-NPs synthesized using S. ilicifolium demonstrated a dual role: priming plant defense mechanisms and acting as an effective nanopesticide. These findings highlight their potential as a sustainable strategy for integrated whitefly management in greenhouse environments. Future research should focus on field-scale validation, environmental safety assessments, and formulation optimization.Keywords: Biosafety, Defense mechanism, Sap-sucking pest, Nanotechnology, Nano-pesticide, OxidativeSubject terms: Physiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Zinc (Zn) is essential micronutrient for plant health. It plays a vital role in managing reactive oxygen species, protects cells from oxidative stress, and stabilizes RNA and DNA structures1,2. Zn is also involved in key processes such as auxin synthesis, protein formation, and nitrogen assimilation1,2. Furthermore, it is crucial for maintaining cell membrane integrity, promoting plant growth, and regulating growth hormones2. In tomatoes, Zn enhances yield and quality, contributing to health benefits for consumers due to the fruit’s antioxidant-rich composition and essential nutrients3. Despite their high nutritional value, tomatoes, the world’s second most consumed vegetable- require adequate micronutrients like Zn to maintain optimal growth, development, and defense against pests4,5.

The whitefly, Trialeurodes vaporariorum (Westwood), is a significant agricultural pest that inflicts considerable physiological damage on plants. Its feeding can lead to yield losses of up to 50%, and it acts as a vector for harmful plant viruses6,7. Due to its rapid proliferation, extensive damage, and adaptability to favorable climates, pesticide-based control remains a common strategy7,8. However, the increasing resistance of insects to chemical and the detrimental effects of these pesticides on natural predators highlight the urgent need for ecosystem-compatible pest management strategies9. According to the FAO (2023), global pesticide usage nearly doubled between 1990 and 2021, underscoring the pressing demand for sustainable and environmentally friendly alternatives in agriculture8,10,11.

Nanotechnology has emerged as a promising tool for integrated pest management. It provides safe and efficient solutions that minimize chemical inputs while supporting higher crop productivity without harming the environment12–14. This technology is increasingly utilized to enhance plant tolerance to stress, improve photosynthetic efficiency, and boost overall plant quality14–17. Nanoparticles (1 to 100 nm in size) have demonstrated significant potential in medicine, industry, and agriculture due to their unique physicochemical properties18. Despite this promise, nanotechnology-based fertilizers still face challenges related to production scalability, purification processes, and the use of surfactants. Ongoing research aims to address these barriers and enhance the efficiency of nanoparticle production for sustainable agricultural applications19.

Green synthesis (GS) methods utilize plant extracts to produce nanoparticles, offering a simple, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly approach for various industries20,21. Notably, nanoparticles (NPs) produced through GS hold significant potential in agriculture, where they can enhance fertilization, serve as plant growth regulators and pesticides, and reduce reliance on conventional chemical inputs, thereby minimizing environmental pollution22,23. Seaweeds, particularly brown algae from the Persian Gulf and the Oman Sea, provide a sustainable source for nanoparticle biosynthesis. Their abundance of bioactive compounds often surpasses the capabilities of many terrestrial plants24.

Among various nanoparticles, zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) are notable for their exceptional physical, optical, antimicrobial, and insecticidal properties, making them highly versatile for applications in agriculture, medicine, and materials science25,26. GS methods for producing ZnO-NPs are particularly appealing due to their simplicity, sustainability, and environmental compatibility27. Although research on the use of marine algae and microalgae for ZnO-NP biosynthesis remains limited28,29, several genera including Sargassum, Caulerpa, Gracilaria, Pterocladia, and Arthrospira have demonstrated high potential due to their rich content of reducing agents and bioactive compounds28–30. Studies have demonstrated the broad agricultural benefits of ZnO-NPs26. For instance, they can improve nutrient uptake, promote growth, and enhance pest resilience in tomato plants31. Abdel Latef et al.32 reported that 21.3 nm ZnO-NPs enhanced the growth of Lupinus albus L., improved stress tolerance, increased zinc uptake, and reduced sodium accumulation. Sargassum sp. is known for its rich bioactive compounds, which can enhance the efficacy of ZnO-NPs in pest control while being environmentally friendly33. Similarly, Murugan et al.34 showed that low doses of Sargassum wightii extract and its green-synthesized ZnO-NPs exhibited strong larvicidal and pupicidal activity against Anopheles stephensi Liston and Helicoverpa armigera Hübner. ZnO-NPs have also been found to boost antioxidant enzyme activities and the production of secondary metabolites in crops such as tomato, cotton, and wheat, thereby enhancing their resistance to abiotic stress35–37. Foliar application of biosynthesized ZnO-NPs on tomato plants has been found to induce systemic acquired resistance, reduce disease severity, and improve growth38. Furthermore, studies on ZnO nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) derived from Turbinaria triquetra and T. ornata suggest their potential use in developing nano-fertilizers for crops such as date palm and rice39,40. These nanoparticles can enhance protein expression and alter carbohydrate metabolism, resulting in increased levels of soluble proteins and antioxidants, which may improve stress resistance41. These nanoparticles also influence vital processes like nitrogen fixation and carbon metabolism, which are crucial for plant health and environmental stress tolerance42. Ultimately, the integration of biopesticides with nanotechnology, as exemplified by GS ZnO-NPs, represents a critical shift towards more sustainable agricultural practices by addressing the limitations of synthetic pesticides43.

However, despite these promising advancements, potential risks such as environmental toxicity and the long-term effects of nanoparticles on ecosystems, which remain underexplored, must be carefully considered44. Although several studies have reported the GSof ZnO nanoparticles using seaweed extracts and demonstrated their pesticidal activity, there is a significant lack of information regarding their role in enhancing plant tolerance to biotic stresses. Previous research has primarily focused on nanoparticle characterization or their direct bioactivity against pathogens and insects, with limited investigation into their physiological and biochemical effects on plants under biotic stress. Moreover, most studies on seaweed-mediated ZnO synthesis have not systematically compared the effects of nanoparticle treatments with those of conventional bulk zinc sources.

Therefore, this study aimed to address these critical research gaps by evaluating the impact of Sargassum ilicifolium-derived ZnO-NPs on tomato plants exposed to whitefly-induced biotic stress. The investigation specifically focused on growth traits, antioxidant responses, osmolyte accumulation, and key biochemical markers. This approach was designed to elucidate the unique advantages of nanoparticle-based treatments over conventional bulk zinc salts. By utilizing ZnO-NPs as both a biostimulant and a nanopesticide, this research seeks to develop a sustainable dual-strategy for whitefly management in greenhouse environments, thereby promoting crop health and productivity while reducing reliance on chemical interventions45,46.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

All chemicals were of analytical grade or higher. Zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(OAc)₂·2 H₂O, ≥ 98%), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂, 30%), sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃), potassium iodide (KI, ≥ 99%), Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, quercetin (≥ 95%), gallic acid (≥ 98%), trichloroacetic acid (TCA), thiobarbituric acid (TBA), and Bradford reagent were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA), Merck (Germany), or Bio-Rad (USA).

Seaweed collection and preparation

The collection of species of the brown seaweed, Sargassum ilicifolium (Turner) C. Agardh, was carried out from the coastal areas of the Persian Gulf and the Sea of Oman in Iran. The taxonomic identification was confirmed morphologically using diagnostic keys from the Agricultural Research, Education, and Extension Organization (AREEO), the Iranian Fisheries Science Research Institute (IFSRI), and the Offshore Research Center in Chabahar, with particular emphasis on key morphological traits47. The materials were first washed with tap water and then with distilled water, after which they were dried in the shade. The dried seaweed was finely ground for further studies. Approximately 2 g of the seaweed powder was added to 100 mL of deionized water, heated to 100 °C, and then filtered through Whatman 41 filter paper. The obtained extract was stored in dark containers in a refrigerator at -4 °C and subsequently used for the preparation of ZnO-NPs48.

Synthesis of ZnO-NPs using S. ilicifolium

Zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(OAc)2·2H2O) at a concentration of 0.1 M was added to 100 mL of aqueous seaweed extract in a reaction vessel. The mixture was placed in a shaking incubator maintained at 60 ± 5 °C, and stirred for 6 h. The appearance of white precipitates indicated the successful formation of ZnO nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs). Zinc oxide reaction mixture was then transferred into 15 mL centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min using a Kendra D-37,520 (Osterode, Germany). D-37,520 centrifuge resulting pellet was washed three times with deionized water to remove any unreacted residues and impurities, followed by centrifugation at the same speed after each wash.

The collected precipitate was transferred to a porcelain mortar, dried in an oven at 100 °C overnight to eliminate moisture, and then subjected to calcination in a muffle furnace at 450 °C for 4 h to obtain crystalline ZnO-NP powder48. This high-temperature treatment enhances the removal of organic residues from the GS process. The synthesized ZnO-NPs were characterized using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, MIRA3 FEG-SEM, Tescan, Czech Republic) to analyze their surface morphology, shape, and particle size distribution. FE-SEM analysis provides high-resolution images that confirm the nanoparticle size range and surface features, which are critical for evaluating the efficiency and potential applications of the synthesized nanoparticles.

Preparation of ZnO-Nanopriming solution and tomato seed priming and culture plant

Tomato seeds (Solanum lycopersicum L.) were purchased from Mazra Danesh Company, Shiraz, Iran. A herbarium voucher specimen of the tomato cultivar S. lycopersicum L. cv. 4129 was deposited in the herbarium of the Department of Plant Protection at the University of Mohaghegh Ardabili, Ardabil, Iran, for future reference and traceability. For seed treatment of tomato seeds were surface sterilized with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) solution and then rinsed thoroughly with deionized water. To assess the effects of seed priming, various concentrations of green-synthesized zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) and bulk zinc oxide (B-ZnO) were prepared at 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 ppm. Each concentration was sonicated in distilled water using an ultrasonic processor (Hielscher Ultrasonics UP50H, Germany) for 30 min to ensure uniform dispersion. Deionized water alone served as the control for hydropriming.

Tomato seeds were immersed in the respective ZnO nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) and B-ZnO solutions for 12 h with gentle agitation. Following the priming process, the seeds were air-dried at room temperature and rinsed with distilled water to eliminate any unabsorbed residues. The treated seeds were then sown in germination trays filled with a sterile substrate. After germination, the seedlings were watered three times a week.

After priming, tomato seeds were sown in germination trays filled with sterilized substrate under greenhouse conditions (25 ± 5 °C, 65 ± 5% RH, 14:10 h L: D photoperiod). Seedlings were irrigated three times per week with deionized water. At 25 days after germination, the most vigorous seedlings were transplanted into 7-L plastic pots (21.5 × 23 cm) containing 5 kg of sterilized soil mixture (loam: sand: peat moss; 2:1:1). Plants were maintained under controlled greenhouse conditions until the 4–5 leaf stage, when foliar applications of ZnO-NPs and B-ZnO were performed.

Irrigation requirements for tomato plants were determined using a Time Domain Reflectometer (TDR, Trase/ 6050 × 1, Soil Moisture, United stated) to monitor the soil volumetric water content. Soil moisture was maintained at approximately 70–75% of field capacity for all treatments. Irrigation was scheduled when the soil water content dropped below 60% of field capacity, and the required volume of water was applied to bring the soil back to field capacity. This approach ensured uniform water availability across all treatments and minimized drought-induced variability during the experiment.

At the 5–6 leaf stage, plants were artificially infested with T. vaporariorum. Seventy-two hours after infestation, leaves were sampled for biochemical assays. Approximately 20–30 adult whiteflies per plant were used. For harvesting, representative leaves were excised from four plants per treatment, immediately wrapped in aluminum foil, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis (Table 1).

Biochemical assay

To evaluate the effects of different concentrations of bulk zinc oxide (B-ZnO) and green-synthesized zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) on the levels of secondary metabolites and biochemical responses in tomato leaves infested with whiteflies (T. vaporariorum), individual tomato plants at the five- to six-leaf growth stage were utilized (Table 1).

Pigments content

To extract total soluble proteins, 200 µL of the buffer mixed with ground leaf tissue was transferred into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 800 µL of cold acetone. The mixture was briefly vortexed and incubated at − 20 °C for one hour to facilitate protein precipitation. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for five minutes using a microcentrifuge (Model 1–14 K, D-37520 Osterode am Harz, Germany). The concentrations of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and total carotenoids were determined spectrophotometrically according to the method of Arnon49. Absorbance readings were taken at 663 nm, 645 nm, and 470 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (Labomed UV-3200) to quantify pigment concentrations.

Preparation of ethanolic extracts for TPC and TFC

Leaf samples from each treatment were ground using liquid nitrogen in a pre-chilled mortar and pestle to prevent enzymatic degradation. Subsequently, 5 g of the pulverized tissue was transferred into an Erlenmeyer flask containing 50 mL of 80% ethanol. The mixture was allowed to extract bioactive compounds through Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The filtrate was centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 5 min using a centrifuge (Kendra, D-37520 Osterode, Germany). The resulting supernatants were collected and used for subsequent biochemical analyses.

Total phenolic content (TPC)

The total phenolic content (TPC) in the plant extracts was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method50. Briefly, 0.5 mL of appropriately diluted plant extract (prepared by diluting 1 mL of the original extract with 80% methanol to a final volume of 10 mL) was mixed with 2.5 mL of 10% (v/v) Folin–Ciocalteu reagent. After a 5-minute incubation at room temperature, 2 mL of 7.5% (w/v) sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) solution was added to the mixture. The solution was then incubated for 30 min at room temperature to allow color development. The absorbance was measured at 720 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (UNICO S-2100 SUV, USA), with 80% methanol used as the blank. The total phenolic content was quantified by comparison with a standard curve prepared using gallic acid and expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per gram of fresh weight (mg GAE g− 1 FW).

Total flavonoid content (TFC)

The total flavonoid content (TFC) was determined using the aluminum chloride (AlCl3) colorimetric method51. Briefly, 0.5 mL of plant extract was mixed with 1.5 mL of methanol, 0.1 mL of 10% AlCl3 solution, 0.1 mL of 1 M potassium acetate, and 2.8 mL of distilled water. The mixture was thoroughly homogenized and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After incubation, the absorbance was measured at 415 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (UNICO S-2100 SUV, USA). The flavonoid content was quantified using a standard calibration curve constructed with quercetin and expressed as mg quercetin equivalents per gram of fresh weight (mg QE g− 1 FW).

Total anthocyanin content (TAC)

The anthocyanin content in tomato leaves was quantified using the method described by Wagner52. Briefly, 0.05 g of fresh plant tissue was finely ground in a chilled porcelain mortar using acidic methanol (methanol containing 1% HCl, v/v) as the extraction solvent. The homogenized mixture was transferred to screw-cap test tubes and incubated in complete darkness at 25 °C for 24 h to ensure thorough extraction. After incubation, the absorbance of the extract was measured at 550 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer. The anthocyanin concentration was expressed as absorbance units per in of fresh weight (A550 g− 1 FW).

Antioxidative enzymes and non-enzymes assay

To assess antioxidant enzyme activities, 1 g of leaf tissue stored at − 80 °C was transferred to a pre-chilled mortar and ground with liquid nitrogen. After crushing, 0.5 g of the tissue was homogenized in 3 mL of phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.0) containing 1% polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) to prevent protein degradation. The mixture was further supplemented with 3 mL of extraction buffer consisting of potassium phosphate and 1 mM sodium metabisulfite to stabilize the enzymes. The samples were thoroughly mixed and then centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected and used for the quantification of catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POX), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), polyphenol oxidase (PPO), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities. Enzyme activities were expressed on a fresh weight basis (U mg− 1 FW), where one unit (U) of activity was defined as the amount of enzyme causing a 50% inhibition (for SOD) or a change of 0.01 in absorbance per minute (for CAT and POD) (Table 1).

Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay

The MDA concentration was determined by measuring lipid peroxidation. To do this, 250 µL of TCA (20%) was mixed with 500 µL of leaf extract. The mixture was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Next, 250 µL of 8% thiobarbituric acid (TBA) was added to the top layer of the supernatant, and the mixture was incubated for 60 min in a water bath set at 80 °C. The absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 535 nm using a spectrophotometer60.

Total soluble protein assay

Total soluble protein was extracted from 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue using 2 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein content was quantified using the Bradford method61, with BSA as the standard. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm, and results were expressed as mg protein per g fresh weight (mg g− 1 FW).

Measurement of starch content

The ethanol solution was used to purify the contents of the plant sample, which were then used for starch content analysis. The remaining precipitate was employed to measure the starch present in the plant organ. First, the sediment was dried and weighed. Then, 10 mL of distilled water was added to the sediment, and the mixture was placed in a boiling water bath for 15 min. The volume of the filtered solution was adjusted to 25 mL with distilled water. Finally, 2 mL of this solution was taken, and the insoluble sugars were quantified using the phenol and sulfuric acid62.

Cultivation and collection of whitefly colonies

The initial population of the greenhouse whitefly, T. vaporariorum, was obtained in January 2024 from infested tomato plants in a commercial greenhouse located in Ardabil, northwestern Iran (38.2498°N, 48.2933°E). Both adult and immature stages were carefully transferred from infested plants to healthy tomato seedlings (S. lycopersicum L., cv. 4129) using a fine brush and an aspirator.

To facilitate natural colonization, infested and healthy plants were placed together in insect-proof cages. The colony was maintained under controlled greenhouse conditions at 25 ± 2 °C, 60 ± 10% relative humidity, and a photoperiod of 16:8 (light: dark) hours. The whiteflies were reared for at least three successive generations on healthy tomato plants prior to use in experiments to ensure colony stability and uniformity (Table 1).

For the bioassay, twenty adult whiteflies were released onto each tomato plant at the 4–6 leaf stage. The insects were allowed to feed directly on the tomato leaves, with no additional food source provided.

Performance of a bioassay test

The bioassay of various concentrations of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) (0, 250, 330, 436, 575, 760, and 1000 ppm) was conducted on T. vaporariorum using a completely randomized design. Prior to the bioassay, the target concentrations were selected based on a review of literature related to seaweed-derived brown algae species, to determine appropriate dosage levels. Glass Petri dishes served as experimental units, each treated with a specific nanoparticle concentration. Twenty adult whiteflies were individually introduced into each Petri dish. In the control group, deionized water was used. The Petri dishes were sealed and maintained under controlled environmental conditions at 25 ± 2 °C, 60 ± 5% relative humidity, and a 16:8 h light-dark photoperiod. Treatments were applied every four days, and the number of dead insects was recorded daily for each nanoparticle concentration.

Statistics analyses

The experiment was conducted in a Completely Randomized Design (CRD). Each treatment was replicated four times (n = 4 biological replicates), and data from independent plants were used for analysis. For biochemical assays (pigments, TPC, TFC, TAC, enzyme activities, H₂O₂, MDA, protein, and starch contents), each replicate consisted of samples obtained from four independent plants per treatment. The data were tested for the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance. Specifically, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the residuals and the Levene test was employed to confirm the homogeneity of variances across treatments. Subsequently, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to determine significant differences among treatments, followed by Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc test at a significance level of P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using MINITAB software version 16.

For the insect bioassay, each treatment included four replicates, with 20 adult whiteflies per replicate. Graphical representations of the data were prepared using Microsoft Excel. Lethal concentration (LC50) was estimated using probit analysis in SAS software version 9.4.

Results

FE- SEM analysis

The formation of ZnO-NPs was confirmed through visual evaluation. The color of the reaction mixture changed from dark brown to pale white during the synthesis, indicating the successful production of ZnO-NPs. Photosynthetic pigments contents

Whitefly infestation significantly altered the content of photosynthetic pigments in tomato leaves, with the response depending on the zinc treatment and its form (B-ZnO or ZnO-NPs) (Table 2). For chlorophyll a, the most effective concentrations were 50 ppm for B-ZnO and 25 ppm for ZnO-NPs, both resulting in significantly higher levels than the corresponding 5 ppm treatments. A similar pattern was observed for chlorophyll b, where the 50 ppm B-ZnO and ZnO-NP treatments yielded higher values than the 10 and 5 ppm B-ZnO and ZnO-NP treatments respectively. Consequently, total chlorophyll content was highest at 50 ppm for B-ZnO and 25 ppm for ZnO-NPs compared to 5 ppm (Table 2).

Discussion

Plants are increasingly producing defense compounds, such as phenolic compounds, salicylic acid, and hydrogen peroxide, to counteract abiotic and biotic stresses, including temperature fluctuations and diseases48,63. The infestation of plants by sap-sucking insects such as T. vaporariorum triggers a complex array of defense responses, often mediated by the activation of antioxidant systems and the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, including phenolic compounds and flavonoids56,64. Our study demonstrates that foliar application of S. ilicifolium-derived ZnO-NPs significantly enhance these innate defense mechanisms in tomato plants under whitefly stress. The observed improvements are not merely correlative but can be explained by several interconnected physiological and biochemical mechanisms.

Green-synthesized ZnO nanoparticles derived from Lobelia leschenaultiana exhibited both spherical and hexagonal shapes, with sizes ranging from 20 to 65 nm65. Brown macroalgae, Sargassum wightii, and brown seaweed, Turbinaria ornata, produced spherical nanoparticles of varying sizes, some of which displayed rod-shaped morphology, with an average size ranging from 15 to 62 nm 34,39. Nanoparticles synthesized from plant sources typically exhibit a spherical shape, which is consistent with previous research65–68.

The present study provides solid biochemical evidence supporting the role of S. ilicifolium-mediated ZnO-NPs in enhancing tomato plant resistance. Our previous comprehensive characterization confirmed their crystalline nature (as indicated by XRD), negative surface charge (zeta potential of approximately − 5.3 mV), and functionalization with bioactive compounds derived from the S. ilicifolium extract. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) analysis specifically revealed the presence of hydroxyl groups (-OH) from polyphenols and carbohydrates, carbonyl groups (-C = O) from proteins and flavonoids, and carboxyl groups (-COOH) from algal polysaccharides, all of which played essential roles in the reduction of zinc ions and stabilization of the formed nanoparticles48. While FT-IR provides valuable information on functional groups, the specific bioactive compounds involved require further investigation. The antioxidant and insecticidal properties of our biosynthesized ZnO-NPs are undoubtedly influenced by these capping agents, which may include phlorotannins, fucoidans, and carotenoids characteristic of brown seaweeds24.

Nanoparticles (NPs) influence plant photosynthetic efficiency, chlorophyll fluorescence, and quantum yield, primarily through their effects on NP-induced oxidative stress and the plant’s antioxidant defense system. Understanding these interactions is essential for evaluating the overall impact of NPs on plant health69,70. ZnO-NPs demonstrated superior uptake compared to traditional zinc sources, leading to significant improvements in biomass and zinc accumulation in tomatoes71. Combining seed priming with foliar application of Zn-NPs (0.3%) on spinach effectively mitigated salt stress, leading to improved growth and biochemical traits, such as increased antioxidant production72. Foliar application of ZnO and ZnO-NPs on safflower significantly increased biomass yield and grain production, with optimal concentrations identified at 6000–10,000 ppm73. The application of T-ZnO-NPs on wheat through foliar and soil methods resulted in enhanced germination rates, root and shoot development, and chlorophyll content, indicating a positive influence on growth parameters74. A foliar application of 200 ppm ZnO-NPs on strawberry improved net assimilation rates and leaf area, although higher concentrations negatively impacted photosystem II efficiency75.

The application of ZnO-NPs, particularly at 25 ppm, significantly mitigated the loss of photosynthetic pigments typically caused by whitefly herbivory. This preservation of chlorophyll a, b, and total carotenoids can be attributed to two primary mechanisms. First, zinc is a vital co-factor for enzymes involved in chlorophyll biosynthesis and carbonic anhydrase activity69,70. The nano-form of ZnO, characterized by its high surface area and enhanced bioavailability, likely facilitates more efficient zinc uptake and utilization, thereby supporting the integrity of the photosynthetic apparatus. Additionally, by alleviating oxidative stress (as evidenced by reduced MDA levels), ZnO-NPs protect chloroplasts from ROS-induced damage, particularly to the photosystem II complex and lipid membranes, thus preserving chlorophyll structure and function69,70. Both abiotic and biotic factors significantly alter the levels of chlorophyll a and b, indicating a close physiological relationship between these important pigments76. Chlorophyll a, the primary photosynthetic pigment, is particularly sensitive to light-induced degradation and serves as a valuable biomarker for assessing the toxicity of nanomaterials. Fluctuations in chlorophyll a and b levels under normal and stress conditions may reflect the extent of nanoparticle-induced stress77,78. In the present study, the observed increase in chlorophyll content after ZnO-NP treatment suggests enhanced photosynthetic efficiency, which is likely due to the role of zinc as a co-factor for key enzymes involved in chlorophyll biosynthesis, such as carbonic anhydrase and porphobilinogen deaminase1,79. Tomato leaves treated with various concentrations of B-ZnO and ZnO-NPs exhibited higher total carotenoid levels than the control group. Both chlorophylls and carotenoids are essential components of the photosynthetic machinery, and zinc plays a critical role in chlorophyll biosynthesis and overall plant growth, even though it is required in trace amounts1,79.

Due to their small size and high surface area-to-volume ratio, nanoparticles exhibit enhanced uptake efficiency and faster translocation within plant tissues80. For instance, ZnO-NPs have been shown to improve plant biomass, chlorophyll and protein synthesis, and enhance tolerance to abiotic stress81,82. In Lupinus albus, ZnO-NPs alleviated oxidative stress by enhancing pigment stability and reducing MDA levels32. Salama et al.83 reported a dose-dependent increase in chlorophyll a and b content in Phaseolus vulgaris fallowing ZnO-NPs treatment. In date palms (Phoenix dactylifera), application of 50 and 75 ppm ZnO-NPs significantly increased chlorophyll a and b content, whereas higher concentrations resulted in a decrease84. This reduction in chlorophyll content is often associated with heightened physiological stress, increased production of ROS, lipid peroxidation, and disruption of the integrity of photosynthetic membranes85.

Our results outline a proposed mechanistic pathway by which S. ilicifolium-derived ZnO-NPs mitigate whitefly-induced biotic stress in tomato plants. We hypothesize that these nanoparticles act as priming agents, initiating an early, low-level activation of defense signaling. This hypothesis is supported by the significant upregulation of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), the key enzyme in the phenylpropanoid pathway. The resulting increase in total phenolic compounds (TPC) and flavonoid content (TFC) serves a dual function: first, these metabolites act as potent non-enzymatic antioxidants, directly scavenging the reactive oxygen species generated by whitefly feeding; second, they serve as antifeedants and toxins, reducing the host plant’s palatability and nutritional value to the insect. Concurrently, ZnO-NPs appear to activate the phenylpropanoid pathway, a crucial route for producing defense-related secondary metabolites. The significant increase in PAL activity, particularly at 25 and 50 ppm ZnO-NPs, is a critical finding. PAL is the gateway enzyme of this pathway79,86. It’s up regulation leads to the enhanced synthesis of a wide array of phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and lignins87. Flavonoids are sensitive indicators of environmental stress and play a pivotal role in the defense of plant against adverse conditions88,89. Longer wavelengths of light have been found to enhance PAL activity, which correlates with increased flavonoid accumulation. García-López et al.90 demonstrated that spraying Capsicum annuum plants with a ZnO-NPs solution significantly increased their total flavonoid content90. The increased TPC and TFC levels in the treated plants are the direct outcomes of this activation. The total phenolic compounds (TPCs) are recognized for their antioxidant properties that help prevent oxidative stress in plant cells by scavenging ROS. These compounds are synthesized under optimal conditions; however, their levels can be influenced by various environmental stresses85,87. These metabolites fulfil several protective functions. They act as powerful non-enzymatic antioxidants that directly scavenge ROS73, and they act as pesticides and toxins that reduce the palatability and nutritional quality of the plant91,92. Furthermore, the observed increase in polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity is crucial. PPO catalyzes the oxidation of phenolics to quinones, which are highly reactive compounds that can cross-link proteins, reducing the digestibility of plant tissue for insects and forming physical barriers against pathogen ingress91–93.

This study demonstrates that tomato leaves nano-primed with biogenic ZnO-NPs exhibited an increased total anthocyanin content (TAC), which is likely attributable the antioxidant activity of the nanoparticles in scavenging reactive oxygen species94. This finding is consistent with a study on tobacco plants, where ZnO-NPs treatment also increased TAC compared to the control95. Anthocyanins, water-soluble flavonoids, contribute directly to TAC levels, and their accumulation often signifies enhanced flavonoid biosynthesis. These compounds protect plants from harmful photodynamic reactions by suppressing ROS94. ZnO nanoparticles have been shown to stimulate secondary metabolism, assist in protecting plants from stress96, and enhance plant defense against pathogens and insects97. The content of anthocyanins generally increases in response to external stimuli, aiding plants in adapting to changing environmental conditions98. For instance, the highest dose of ZnO-NPs significantly elevated anthocyanin content in in vitro olive cultures99. However, Bustos-Torres et al.100 observed that plants increased anthocyanin content at 18 ppm ZnO-NPs as part of their protective mechanism, which inhibits photo-oxidative processes by promoting valence electrons100. Similarly, Baskar et al.101 found that nanoparticle treatments modulated anthocyanin content in eggplant seedlings, with production increasing at lower doses but decreasing at higher concentrations101.

The primary mechanism by which ZnO-NPs bolster plant resilience is likely through the modulation of the antioxidant defense system. Our results showed a significant increase in the activities of key enzymes such as SOD, APX, and POX in plants treated with 50 ppm ZnO-NPs, suggesting a targeted elicitation of the plant’s enzymatic antioxidant machinery. ZnO-NPs may act as priming agents, sensitizing the plant’s defense pathways41. This priming effect induces a state of preparedness, enabling a more robust and rapid detoxification response upon whitefly infestation and the associated burst of reactive oxygen species as a secondary stress signal102. Specifically, the enhanced SOD activity facilitates the swift dismutation of O₂•⁻ into H₂O₂. Subsequently, a coordinated increase in APX and catalase (CAT) activities efficiently detoxifies H₂O₂ into water77,103, preventing the accumulation of highly reactive hydroxyl radicals. This efficient scavenging cascade mitigates oxidative damage to cellular membranes and organelles, as evidenced by significantly lower malondialdehyde levels in the treated plants. The production of MDA, a reliable biomarker of stress, increases when oxidative stress disrupts cellular homeostasis104.

Another enzyme that scavenges H2O2 in plants, alongside SOD, is CAT. The activity of SOD leads to the production of H2O2. In the current study, the activity of CAT, a key enzyme in the oxidative stress response, increased in tomato plants treated with 5 and 10 ppm concentrations of ZnO-NPs, indicating the activation of oxidative stress mechanisms. At these concentrations, the enzyme effectively degraded and eliminated hydrogen peroxide radicals, resulting in a reduction of H2O2 levels via the Fenton reaction105. Elevated concentrations of H2O2 and free radicals in plant cells can inhibit metabolic processes, inactivate enzymes, decrease enzyme synthesis, and alter enzyme subunit structures, ultimately leading to decreased catalase activity106. The observed increase in catalase activity in the leaves treated with ZnO-NPs under whitefly feeding suggests that this biotic stress imposed additional strain on the plant. Furthermore, the increase in soluble protein content in leaves treated with 100 ppm ZnO-NPs compared to the control group may be attributed to the upregulation of stress-modifying enzymes.

The activity of POX increased with higher concentrations of ZnO-NPs in response to whitefly feeding. POX plays a crucial role in defending against the harmful effects of superoxide ions by converting H2O2 into water, while also promoting auxin morphogenesis and oxidation. This function may serve as a protective response to delay senescence and mitigate free radical damage107. Additionally, POX contributes to increased leaf resistance in plants by promoting lignification, which helps prevent herbivore feeding108. The oxidative activity on phenolic compounds generates phenoxy radicals, which reduce plant digestibility and deter herbivores92. Plants infested with whiteflies exhibited significantly lower activities of PAL, POX, and PPO in the control group compared to those treated with varying concentrations of ZnO-NPs. Furthermore, the observed increase in PPO activity is a crucial component of this defense strategy. PPO catalyzes the oxidation of accumulated phenolics to quinones, which are highly reactive compounds capable of cross-linking proteins. This process reduces the digestibility of plant tissue for phloem-feeding insects such as whiteflies and forms physical barriers that hinder pest establishment91–93. Thus, the dual mode of action comprising the direct insecticidal activity of the NPs and this primed, multi-layered biochemical defense response in the plant collectively enhances tomato resilience to T. vaporariorum infestation. Helaly et al.109 found that ZnO-NPs, when combined with MS medium, enhanced proline synthesis and increased the activities of SOD, CAT, and POX, thereby improving the plants’ tolerance to biotic stress. This finding corroborates the results of the present study, which demonstrates that ZnO-NPs enhance stress tolerance by promoting proline synthesis and boosting the activities of key antioxidant enzymes.

Whitefly infestation induced stress in tomato plants, leading to an increase in leaf anthocyanin content as a defensive response. In contrast, the control group exhibited lower activity of antioxidant enzymes, POX, SOD, TPC, TAC, and carotenoids. This compromised antioxidant system was associated with heightened oxidative stress, evidenced by significantly higher MDA content, a marker of lipid peroxidation, compared to ZnO-NP-treated plants. The absence of ZnO-NPs thus left the plants more vulnerable, resulting in greater damage from whitefly infection. Nanotechnology in crop protection utilizes nanometal oxides, such as ZnO, to formulate and deliver nano-sized pesticides, thereby enhancing their efficiency and effectiveness110–112. ZnO-NPs derived from S. ilicifolium exhibit promising insecticidal activity against whiteflies, attributed to their unique characteristics, including small size and large surface area. In the present study, ZnO-NPs demonstrated a lethal concentration (LC50) of 407.4 ppm, effectively eliminating whiteflies within 96 h. At a concentration of 1000 ppm, they achieved a 96.66% mortality rate in T. vaporariorum. These findings align with those of Khooshe-Bast et al.113, who reported a 91.6% mortality rate at the highest tested concentration, further confirming the potency of ZnO-NPs. Therefore, the concentration of ZnO-NPs is a critical factor, with higher concentrations consistently correlating with increased pest mortality114,115.

The primary insecticidal mechanism of ZnO-NPs is likely the induction of oxidative stress. Following ingestion or contact, ZnO-NPs can penetrate the insect cuticle and the epithelial linings of the digestive tract. Within the insect’s body, these nanoparticles may release Zn²⁺ and/or generate ROS, including O₂•⁻ and H₂O₂114,115. The insect’s antioxidant defense systems are often overwhelmed by this rapid onset of oxidative stress, resulting in lipid peroxidation of cell membranes, enzyme inactivation, protein oxidation, and ultimately, cellular apoptosis and death114,115. This mode of action is consistent with our bioassay results, which demonstrated a clear dose-dependent increase in mortality. A secondary, physical mode of action involves the adhesion and abrasion of nanoparticles. ZnO-NPs can adhere to the insect’s cuticle, causing physical disruption of the protective wax layer. This damage leads to uncontrolled desiccation (water loss) and mortality. Furthermore, their nanoscale size may enable them to obstruct spiracles, interfering with respiration and potentially causing suffocation106.

Lade et al.116 emphasized the potential of ZnO-NPs as a sustainable alternative to conventional pesticides, calling for further research into their interactions with insect pests, specifically for whitefly control. These nanoparticles have exhibited significant insecticidal activity, markedly reducing whitefly populations116. The efficacy of ZnO-NPs is further enhanced when combined with NeemAzal, leading to improved control of whiteflies114. Integrating ZnO-NPs, kaolin, and NeemAzal-T/S into Integrated Pest Management (IPM) programs has been recommended for effective management of B. tabaci, at optimal concentrations of 20, 30, and 15 ppm, respectively114. Moreover, a synergistic effect between ZnO-NPs and neonicotinoid insecticides has been documented, resulting in a mortality rate of up to 93.51% in Spodoptera litura (Fabricius) and Aphis gossypii Glover115. Nanoformulations, including those based on ZnO-NPs, have proven to be as effective as standard insecticides in suppressing whitefly populations117. ZnO-NPs typically range in size from 5 to 35 nm, which may influence their insecticidal properties118. However, further investigation is necessary to assess the environmental and health risks, long-term efficacy, and potential for the development of pest resistance114.

Conclusion

The study suggests that treatments modifying the production of antioxidants a crucial mechanism for stress reduction in plants may have beneficial effects on infected plants. The activation of defense enzymes like POX, SOD, CAT, phenol, and flavonoids enhances the host’s tolerance to biological stress119. The results demonstrated that biosynthesized ZnO-NPs function both as plant defense primers and as direct insecticidal agents. They enhance plant defense by up regulating antioxidant enzymes- SOD, APX, and POX and activating the phenylpropanoid pathway, including PAL, PPO, TPC, and TFC. This establishes a primed physiological state that mitigates whitefly-induced oxidative damage and reduces host suitability. Simultaneously, the nanoscale properties of ZnO-NPs enable direct insecticidal action by inducing oxidative stress and causing physical damage to the pest. This dual functionality positions ZnO-NPs as a promising strategy for sustainable crop protection. Future studies should aim to elucidate the molecular signaling networks underlying nanoparticle-induced priming, assess potential non-target and ecological impacts, and evaluate the long-term environmental fate and biosafety of ZnO-NPs under field conditions.
