Emergence of a Bundibugyo virus variant in the 2026 outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda
Authors: Adrienne Amuri-Aziza, Gradi Luakanda-Ndelemo, Alisen Ayitewala, Daan Jansen, Pascal Adroba-Tandele, Andrew Nsawotebba, Eddy Kinganda-Lusamaki, Stephen Kanyerezi, Pontiano Kaleebu, Christian Ngandu, Allan Muruta, Daudi Jjingo, Susan Nabadda, Raphael Lumembe-Numbi, Olga Ntumba-Tshitenge, Misaki Wayengera, Ibrahimm Mugerwa, John O Otshudiema, Judith Kyokushaba, Daniel J Kyabayinze, Prince Akil-Bandali, Ebola outbreak response consortium, NHLDS consortium, UVRI consortium, Uganda Ministry of Health consortium, ACE-Uganda consortium, Sentinel consortium, Henry Kyobe Bosa, Daniel Mukadi-Bamuleka, Charles Olaro, Princesse Paku-Tshambu, Ahidjo Ayouba, Sabue Mulangu, Aloysious Ssemaganda, Nick Loman, Anne W Rimoin, Atek Kagiria, Samuel Wilkinson, Benard Lubwama, Kevin K Ariën
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn May 2026, an outbreak of Ebola disease caused by Bundibugyo virus (BDBV, species Orthoebolavirus bundibugyoense) was declared in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), with cases originating from the DRC and locally transmitted cases reported in Uganda. Bundibugyo virus disease (BVD) outbreaks were previously recorded in 2007–2008 in Bundibugyo District, Uganda, and in 2012 in Isiro, DRC. Here we generated 22 genomes from samples obtained from individuals with BVD in the DRC and Uganda. These genomes form a well-supported phylogenetic cluster separate from BDBV variants associated with the 2007 and 2012 outbreaks, together with evidence for sustained human transmission. This is consistent with the emergence of a new zoonotic spillover event rather than resurgence of previously reported variants. Alongside ongoing efforts to strengthen surveillance systems and community engagement, establish Ebola treatment centers and develop targeted medical countermeasures, our report advocates for specifically increasing decentralized laboratory-diagnostics capacity, with pan-Orthoebolavirus assays, including genomic sequencing capacity, to limit further outbreak expansion and enable timely detection and control of future outbreaks.Subject terms: Molecular biology, Viral infection, Viral epidemiology

Main

Ebola disease is a severe and often fatal illness caused by viruses that belong to the genus Orthoebolavirus. Among the known orthoebolaviruses, three species are known as highly pathogenic in humans and have been responsible for multiple outbreaks with high case-fatality rates in sub-Saharan Africa: Orthoebolavirus zairense (EBOV), Orthoebolavirus sudanense (SUDV) and Orthoebolavirus bundibugyoense (BDBV)1. The DRC has recorded the highest number (n = 17) of Ebola disease outbreaks since the first recorded one in 1976 in Yambuku, DRC2; in Uganda, eight outbreaks have been recorded to date3. These recurrent epidemics underscore the persistent threat posed by orthoebolaviruses in sub-Saharan Africa. The 2013–2016 West Africa Ebola virus disease (EVD) outbreak, which resulted in 28,646 cases and 11,323 deaths, demonstrated that a high-consequence pathogen can overwhelm fragile health systems, disrupt essential services and rapidly expand to neighboring regions in the absence of field-deployable molecular point-of-care (POC) testing and targeted medical countermeasures4.

On 13 May 2026, local health authorities in the Bunia health zone, Ituri province, DRC, notified national authorities of a cluster of suspected EVD cases (based on the DRC Ministry of Health case definition for EVD). The affected individuals were admitted to the Centre Medical Evangélique (CME) Bunia Hospital, Rwampara health zone. Clinical specimens, mainly blood and oropharyngeal swabs, were collected from 19 people who were suspected or likely to have the disease. All tested negative for EBOV by the GeneXpert Ebola assay, conducted at the provincial public-health laboratory in Bunia. These samples were sent to the Institut National de Recherche Biomédicale (INRB), the national reference laboratory for emerging and re-emerging pathogens, in Kinshasa, DRC, for PCR-based testing. Rapid identification of Orthoebolavirus (non-EBOV) with species confirmation resulting from viral-enriched metagenomic sequencing at INRB provided evidence and confirmation of BDBV infection (Table 1).

On the basis of these results, the Ministry of Public Health, Hygiene and Social Welfare, DRC, officially declared a BVD outbreak in Ituri province on 15 May 2026 (ref. 5). This represents the seventeenth Ebola disease epidemic documented in the DRC, and the third documented BVD outbreak overall3.

Concurrently, a BVD outbreak was declared on the same day by the Ugandan Ministry of Health following a post-mortem diagnostic confirmation of the disease in an individual who traveled from Bunia, DRC, through Hoima, Uganda, to Kampala, Uganda; the diagnosis was performed at Central Public Health Laboratories (CPHL), Kampala (Table 1)5,6. In Uganda, further BDBV infections were confirmed that mostly involved individuals linked to the initial infected person, including contacts involved in transportation and health care. A Public Health Emergency of International Concern7 and a Public Health Emergency of Continental Security8 were declared by the World Health Organization and Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC) on 17 May 2026 and 18 May 2026, respectively. As of 9 July 2026, 20 PCR-confirmed cases (15 cases originating from the DRC and 5 locally acquired cases) of BVD have been reported in Uganda; 1,830 PCR-confirmed cases, including 648 deaths, have been reported in the DRC (https://insp.cd/sitrep-n32-mvb_15-06-2026/).

We generated viral genomes from the new outbreak to reconstruct its early events and to determine whether it resulted from a new zoonotic spillover event or was a resurgence linked to previous Bundibugyo outbreaks in the region.

Although all samples tested negative by GeneXpert Ebola assay, the pan-Orthoebolavirus diagnostic assays (POC RADIONE Ebola detection kit RP017 and Altona Filovirus Screen RT-PCR kit v1.0) tested positive. Notably, the Altona Filovirus Screen RT–PCR test failed to detect Orthoebolavirus in three samples (regarded as invalid results), whereas whole-genome sequencing with a viral probe-based enrichment approach did still successfully recover complete or partial BDBV genomes, ultimately confirming BVD in these three individuals (Table 1).

High-quality viral genomes from 21 people (14 from DRC and seven from Uganda), with a minimum mean depth coverage of 45× and genome coverage of >80% (Extended Data Table 1), were used to infer the phylogenetic tree. Phylogenetic analysis, including contextual BDBV full genomes from previous outbreaks (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Table 2), demonstrated that the 2026 viral genome sequences form a distinct (Extended Data Fig. 1), well-supported cluster that was different than the clusters of viruses associated with the 2007 and 2012 outbreaks (Fig. 1b). Given this phylogenetic placement, these findings are consistent with a zoonotic spillover event rather than a resurgence of previously known BDBV variants.

In the 2026 outbreak, phylogenetic analysis confirmed cross-border transmission between the DRC and Uganda (Fig. 1c). Notably, available epidemiological investigations indicate that all included Ugandan cases belong to a single transmission chain, highlighting that the apparent separation of sequences in the phylogeny should not be overinterpreted as evidence for independent introductions. Additional genomic data could help resolve these relationships in the future. Mutational analysis identified 25 mutations in total in the 2026 BDBV genome cluster, of which 9/25 (36%) showed patterns consistent with adenosine deaminase acting on RNA (ADAR)-mediated editing9. ADAR mutations are a signature of ebolavirus transmission into the human population10. Of these 25 mutations, 5 resulted in non-synonymous substitutions (further detailed in Extended Data Table 3).

The ongoing 2026 Ebola outbreak in the DRC and Uganda is of particular concern owing to the absence of approved Bundibugyo-specific or pan-Ebola vaccines or therapeutics. Bundibugyo virus disease has a high fatality rate11. The risk of regional and international spread is further increased by high population mobility and insecurity (the presence of armed groups and militias) in affected mining regions. In addition, weakened health systems and poor community resilience hamper outbreak-control efforts. Important diagnostic gaps remain, including the limited availability of POC tests5,12. The outbreak highlights the continued vulnerability of Central and East Africa to recurrent zoonotic emergence of filoviruses and underscores the importance of sustained regional diagnostic and genomic-surveillance capacity. Although centralized laboratory molecular diagnostic testing and genomic sequencing enabled case confirmation and virus characterization, increasing similar decentralized capacity might help to detect outbreaks at even earlier stages. Thus, rapid deployment of sustainable decentralized molecular diagnostic testing and genomic sequencing capacity should further decrease turnaround times for pathogen detection, improve patient management and outbreak containment, and lead to better preparation for future outbreaks. A key limitation of this study is the relatively small number of genomes, which precluded a robust estimation of the time to the most recent common ancestor of the virus behind the outbreak. Decentralized sequencing capacity will enable the generation of more genomes to accurately estimate the date of the virus’s most recent common ancestor. In addition, near-real-time genomic surveillance will assist in early identification of viral-spread dynamics and viral evolution affecting outbreak measures.

Rapid genomic data generation strengthened cross-border situational awareness and supported real-time epidemiological investigations during the evolving outbreak response13,14. These findings emphasize that sustained regional preparedness, molecular-diagnostic capacity, real-time genomic sequencing capacity to support molecular epidemiology15 and open international data sharing are critical components of the response to a filovirus outbreak and global health security. Timely release of sequences of the BDBV variant behind the 2026 epidemic in the DRC and Uganda is relevant to public health, providing an important resource for the design or adaptation of specific medical countermeasures (vaccines and therapeutics), as well as molecular diagnostic tests.

Methods

Ethics committee approval

Specimens were collected as part of a routine country-wide surveillance of emerging and re-emerging infectious diseases and were therefore exempt from ethical approval. However, in the DRC, permission to use anonymous data for publication was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Kinshasa School of Public Health (ESP-UNIKIN, no. ESP/CE/81/2026). In Uganda, for public-health surveillance and outbreak-response activities, a waiver of informed consent was granted through the Uganda National Health Laboratory Services Research and Ethics Committee (UNHLS-2025-133).

Direct molecular testing of clinical specimens

Blood samples were collected by venipuncture into EDTA tubes, except for one individual in Uganda, for whom posthumous EDTA whole blood was available. Oral-swab specimens were collected from deceased individuals by gently rubbing sterile dry swabs against the buccal mucosa (the inner surfaces of the cheeks) to collect oral fluids and cellular material. We analyzed clinical samples that were shipped to INRB, Kinshasa, in two batches (n = 21) on 14 and 17 May 2026, and to CPHL, Kampala, on 15 and 26 May 2026. At the INRB, clinical specimens were tested for the presence of EBOV RNA using the GeneXpert Ebola assay and the RADIONE Ebola detection kit RP017 (KH Medical). Direct molecular testing was not performed at the CPHL.

Viral RNA extraction and real-time qPCR amplification

In brief, both at the INRB and the CPHL, viral RNA was extracted from 140 µl of inactivated blood using the QIAamp viral RNA Mini kit (Qiagen), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time PCR was performed using the RealStar Filovirus Screen RT-PCR Kit 1.0 (Altona Diagnostics) at INRB and the RealStar Filovirus Type RT–PCR assay (Altona Diagnostics) at the CPHL, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Additional negative and positive controls were included to ensure the absence of carryover contamination and efficiency of the assay.

Whole-genome sequencing

At the INRB, RNA extracts were reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using LunaScript RT SuperMix kit (New England Biolabs). The resulting cDNAs were subsequently used to prepare sequencing libraries with either the Illumina DNA Prep with enrichment (Illumina) or the Twist Comprehensive Viral Research Panel probe-enrichment (Twist Bioscience), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing libraries were loaded on Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) GridION and on the Illumina NextSeq 1000/2000 sequencer. At the CPHL, whole-genome sequencing was performed using a targeted viral metagenomics approach on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina) with the Illumina Viral Surveillance Panel (Illumina), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Phylogenetic inference

We generated consensus genomes using the Metatropics v0.1.0 (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20430617)16 for ONT Long-reads and Viralrecon 2.6.0 (https://github.com/nf-core/viralrecon) for Illumina short-reads. Both are Nextflow-driven pipelines that include trimming steps to generate high-quality sequencing data, which are subsequently used to construct consensus BDBV genomes (reference: KC545393.1) using default settings. The reference genome was selected on the basis of two criteria: (1) the availability of a complete genome sequence from the 2012 Bundibugyo outbreak, and (2) its close genetic similarity to the initial dataset, making KC545393.1 the appropriate reference genome. At the CPHL, BDBV consensus genomes from human samples were generated as previously described by Nsawotebba et al.6. The newly generated BDBV genome sequences from Uganda and the DRC were analyzed in the context of 34 BDBV genomes from the previous outbreaks in 2007 and 2012, downloaded from Pathoplexus (Extended Data Table 1). RefSeq genomes from other orthoebolaviruses were used as an outgroup to confirm the position of the root. Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analyses were performed using the RACCOON pipeline (https://github.com/artic-network/raccoon), which utilizes MAFFT to generate a multiple sequence alignment and IQ-TREE 2 to construct a maximum likelihood tree under the +gamma substitution model, and 1,000 ultra-fast bootstrap iterations. The alignment was trimmed from position 18903 (coordinates relative to the reference genome used) due to evident sequencing noise in one genome (26FHV054; Pathoplexus accession: PP_006XHL9).

Statistics and reproducibility

This descriptive study included individuals with PCR-confirmed Bundibugyo virus infection. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. The sample size was determined by the number of samples received and tested on a first-come, first-served basis during the early phase of the 2026 Bundibugyo virus disease outbreak. One newly generated genome was excluded from the phylogenetic analysis because its genome coverage did not meet the predefined threshold. Reproducibility of the phylogenetic analyses was ensured through the use of common bioinformatics tools. All genome sequences are publicly available in the Pathoplexus database to enable independent reproduction of the phylogenetic analyses. The experiments were not randomized because the study did not involve treatment allocation or experimental groups. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment because there were no intervention groups.

Ethics and inclusion statement

In this study, sex was determined on the basis of physical examination and extracted from the national viral hemorrhagic fever case investigation form; information on gender was not collected. This research was conducted in close collaboration with local partners throughout all stages of the study, including conceptualization and study design, intellectual property, data ownership and authorship of resulting publications. The inclusion of the national, provincial and local researchers was central to the study, ensuring that their expertise and contextual knowledge guided the multidisciplinary investigation team and supported the implementation of medical countermeasures.

The study addressed a public-health problem of regional and international significance, extending beyond the DRC and Uganda. Contributions of partners and collaborators were clearly defined. In addition, capacity-building activities were implemented, including open real-time PCR and sequencing activities at the Laboratoire Provincial de Santé Publique (Provincial Public Health Laboratory) in Bunia, Ituri, the epicenter of the 2026 Bundibugyo virus disease outbreak.

Ethical approval was obtained in both countries, ensuring adherence to ethical requirements. All laboratory activities were performed in compliance with recommended standards of biosafety and environmental protection. The research posed no risk of stigmatization, discrimination or incrimination of participants, and appropriate measures were in place to safeguard their confidentiality and well-being.

No transfer of biological materials or related knowledge out of the country involved benefit-sharing considerations. Finally, the findings were shared with the national health authorities, and all relevant contributors were appropriately acknowledged to recognize the existing body of work and strengthen the contextual understanding of our results.
