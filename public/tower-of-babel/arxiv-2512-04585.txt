SAM3-I: Segment Anything with Instructions
Jingjing Li, Yue Feng, Yuchen Guo, Jincai Huang, Yongri Piao, Qi Bi, Miao Zhang, Xiaoqi Zhao, Qiang Chen, Shihao Zou, Wei Ji, Huchuan Lu, Li Cheng

Abstract
Segment Anything Model 3 (SAM3) has advanced open-vocabulary segmentation through promptable concept segmentation, allowing users to segment all instances corresponding to a given concept, typically specified with short noun-phrase (NP) prompts. While this marks the first integration of language-level concepts within the SAM family, real-world usage typically requires far richer expressions that include attributes, spatial relations, functionalities, actions, states, and even implicit reasoning over instances. Currently, SAM3 relies on external multi-modal agents to convert complex instructions into NPs and then conduct iterative mask filtering. However, these NP-level concepts remain overly coarse, often failing to precisely represent a specific instance. In this work, we present SAM3-I , an enhanced framework that unifies concept-level understanding and instruction-level reasoning within the SAM family. SAM3-I introduces an instruction-aware cascaded adaptation mechanism that progressively aligns expressive instruction semantics with SAM3’s existing vision–language representations, enabling direct instruction-following segmentation without sacrificing its original concept-driven capabilities. Furthermore, we design a structured instruction taxonomy spanning concept, simple, and complex levels, and develop a scalable data engine to construct a dataset with diverse instruction–mask pairs. Experiments show that SAM3-I delivers appealing performance, demonstrating that SAM3 can be effectively extended to follow natural-language instructions while preserving its strong concept grounding. We open-source SAM3-I and provide practical fine-tuning workflows, enabling researchers to adapt it to domain-specific applications. The source code is available here .

Introduction

Semantic segmentation
9
;
21
;
7
is a fundamental task in visual understanding. Traditional methods
14
;
8
have primarily targeted explicit instance segmentation, where each pixel is assigned to one of a fixed set of predefined categories. However, such closed vocabularies remain considerably narrower than the diverse range of instances present in real-world scenarios. To this end, SAM3
3
represents an important step forward by introducing PCS, a concept-driven paradigm that enables users to segment all related instances using short NP prompts. This formulation naturally aligns with open-vocabulary segmentation. As an example in Fig.
1
b, a concept prompt such as “soccer player” can be used to segment all associated instances in an image.

However, real-world interactions are typically not conveyed through short NPs alone; users often describe instances using far richer expressions that may incorporate attributes, spatial relations, functions, actions, states, or even implicit reasoning over instances. Such descriptions often specify a concept together with multiple conditions,
e.g
.
, “
the soccer player on the far right, wearing a blue jersey, is sliding on the grass to tackle the ball
”, as shown in Fig.
1
c. Handling such inputs requires the model to parse structured semantics and accurately ground them in the scene. This capability is essential for many open-world applications, including home robotics
12
;
17
, autonomous driving
5
, and augmented reality
10
;
20
, where systems must follow complex instructions to guide segmentation and decision-making.

This leads to a natural question:
Can we retain SAM3’s strong concept recall ability gained from large-scale training while enabling it to interpret more complex instructions and ground the corresponding instances?
To approximate this capability, SAM3 currently relies on external multi-modal agents,
e.g
.
, Qwen3-VL
1
, to convert long instructions into short NPs and then perform multiple rounds of mask filtering process. Although workable, this agent-driven pipeline separates linguistic reasoning from visual segmentation, adds notable computational overhead, and limits the model’s ability to directly align complex instructions with visual concepts. More importantly, the PCS formulation constrains the agent’s final output to short NPs. These NP-level concepts are overly coarse and often insufficient for precisely grounding specific instances described by detailed instructions, as shown in Fig.
1
c and d.

To overcome these limitations, we present
SAM3-I
, an enhanced framework that unifies concept-level understanding and instruction-level reasoning within the SAM family. SAM3-I directly interprets complex natural-language instructions and grounds the corresponding instances that satisfy the specified conditions, while retaining SAM3’s strong concept recall ability. At its core, SAM3-I introduces an instruction-aware cascaded adaptation mechanism that progressively aligns expressive instruction semantics with SAM3’s existing vision–language representations, enabling instruction-following segmentation without sacrificing its original concept-driven capabilities.

To enable scalable instruction alignment, we further develop a data construction pipeline that converts existing open-vocabulary segmentation datasets into a large instruction-centric corpus. The pipeline is guided by a structured instruction taxonomy, covering concept, simple, and complex levels of instruction, and synthesizes diverse descriptions accordingly.
Each generated instruction is then validated through a three-stage annotation–inspection–correction procedure to guarantee faithful grounding to its corresponding image. This process yields a high-quality dataset that more accurately reflects the richness and diversity of natural-language expressions encountered in real-world applications.

Empirical experiments show that SAM3-I achieves appealing performance on both referring and reasoning segmentation, bringing robust instruction-following capability to the SAM family. We release SAM3-I together with lightweight fine-tuning workflows to support future research on instruction-based segmentation. Finally, we highlight several promising directions for further exploration.

Preliminary: SAM Family

SAM represents a major milestone in segmentation foundation models, showcasing strong cross-domain generalization with compelling performance in diverse downstream applications, such as medical imaging and industrial inspection
21
;
16
.
Central to this success is the promptable segmentation paradigm introduced by the SAM1 and SAM2 series
9
;
19
, where a user supplies points, boxes, or masks to guide the model in segmenting a single target per prompt, a setting known as Promptable Visual Segmentation (PVS), as illustrated in Fig.
1
a. When combined with the large-scale, high-quality SA-1B and SA-V datasets that provide rich supervision signals, this paradigm forms the foundation of the SAM family’s remarkable segmentation capability.

SAM3 extends this paradigm from single-object prompting to Promptable Concept Segmentation (PCS), allowing the model to segment all instances of a given concept within an image or video, as illustrated in Fig.
2
a. SAM3 advances open-vocabulary segmentation by supporting short noun-phrase (NP) such as “soccer player” or “yellow bus”. However, SAM3 is fundamentally designed around atomic concept prompts, restricting text inputs to simple NPs rather than long referring expressions or multi-condition instructions. When presented with complex instructions, SAM3 must rely on the agentic pipeline shown in Fig.
2
b, where a multi-modal large language model (MLLM) converts long instructions into short NPs and then conducts iterative mask filtering to produce the final output. This multi-step process depends heavily on external reasoning and often collapses rich natural-language descriptions into over-simplified NP prompts. As a result, the PCS interface inherently limits SAM3’s ability to segment specific instances that satisfy detailed natural-language conditions.

Real-world usage, however, involves expressions that go well beyond simple NPs. This motivates us to extend SAM3 with the ability to follow rich, complex instructions while preserving its strong concept-level grounding capability.

3
Promptable Instruction Segmentation

We extend the PCS paradigm to a more expressive instruction-following setting, which we term Promptable Instruction Segmentation (PIS). We formulate the instructions into three progressively finer hierarchical levels:

•

i) Concept instruction
represents the most atomic form, expressed as short noun phrases (NPs) consistent with SAM3.

•

ii) Simple instruction
retains explicit NP mentions but augments them with additional referring conditions such as attributes, spatial cues, or local context.

•

iii) Complex instruction
removes explicit NP references and instead requires the model to identify targets by interpreting functions, actions, affordance, or broader contextual semantics.

Together, these three levels establish a unified hierarchy that enables SAM-I to unify concept-level understanding and instruction-level reasoning within the SAM family.

SAM3-I Model

Overview.
As shown in Fig.
3
, SAM3-I builds upon the SAM3 architecture and extends it to the PIS task, while remaining fully compatible with PCS and PVS. Briefly, SAM3 comprises a dual vision–language encoder and a detector-based segmentation head, along with a tracker and a memory module for video segmentation. Both the detector and tracker operate on representations produced by the Perception Encoder (PE
2
), which jointly embeds visual and text inputs.

To equip SAM3 with the ability to interpret and execute natural language instructions, from simple referring expressions to complex reasoning instructions, we introduce a lightweight, instruction-aware adaptation mechanism while keeping the entire SAM3 backbone frozen. This design preserves SAM3’s original capabilities, prevents catastrophic forgetting, and injects new instruction-following abilities in a parameter-efficient manner.

Instruction-Aware Cascaded Adapter.
A key challenge in instruction understanding is the wide variability in linguistic expressions.
Simple referring expressions typically hinge on recognizing the target concept and integrating perceptual attributes, positional cues, or local relations, which requires the model to detect explicit linguistic anchors and align them with visual evidence.
Complex reasoning instructions demand substantially richer linguistic competence: the model must interpret functional descriptions, resolve implicit references, or perform multi-step reasoning to link abstract semantics to concrete visual instances.
Mastering this spectrum of linguistic complexity is essential for achieving robust and accurate instruction-following segmentation.
To this end, we introduce an instruction-aware cascaded adapter, a hierarchical module inserted into each layer of SAM3’s text encoder.
The design contains two adapters:

•

i) S-Adapter
learns attribute, position, and relation semantics, enabling effective grounding for simple referring expression where the target instance’s NP remains explicitly available.

•

ii) C-Adapter
builds upon S-Adapter and is responsible for handling complex instructions that lack explicit target NP mentions, requiring multi-hop or contextual reasoning.

Both adapters employ a lightweight bottleneck structure (down-projection, ReLU activation, and up-projection) to enhance alignment with visual features, and a multi-head self attention (MHSA) layer is incorporated to capture long-range textural dependencies. During training, S-Adapter is used exclusively for simple instructions, whereas complex instructions activate the full cascade so that C-Adapter can refine and enrich the representation produced by S-Adapter. This progressive cascaded learning mechanism mirrors the natural hierarchy of linguistic difficulty, stabilizes optimization, mitigates catastrophic interference, and yields more structured and expressive linguistic embeddings that generalize well to both simple and complex instruction forms.

To propagate instruction understanding into the segmentation stage, we further integrate the cascaded adapter into SAM3’s detector. The detector receives the adapted instructional features and visual features, and produces instruction-conditioned mask predictions for both simple and complex branches, denoted as
p_{\text{simple}}
and
p_{\text{complex}}
, respectively.

Instruct-Mask Distribution Alignment Loss.

Allowing the two branches to decode instructions independently risks
semantic drift
between them. To maintain coherent and stable grounding across different levels of instruction, we introduce a pair of alignment losses that regularize their predictions beyond the segmentation loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{seg}}
used in SAM3, enforcing consistency in both instruction semantics and mask distributions:

•

i) Distribution Alignment.
To ensure that the simple- and complex-instruction branches produce compatible predictions for the same target, we align their mask distributions using a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}=\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{simple}}\;\|\;p_{\text{complex}}).

This encourages the two branches to share a consistent semantic space and stabilizes optimization by anchoring them to SAM3’s original concept-level detector.

•

ii) Uncertainty-Aware Hard-Region Supervision.
Discrepancies between the two branches often arise in semantically difficult regions,
e.g
.
, occlusions, relational cues, or context-dependent areas. To emphasize learning on these challenging pixels, we compute an uncertainty map
w_{\text{uncertainty}}
based on the disagreement between the simple- and complex-instruction predictions, measured using the Jensen–Shannon Divergence
13
. This uncertainty score is then used as an adaptive weight in an auxiliary cross-entropy (
\mathrm{CE}
) loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{hard}}=w_{\text{uncertainty}}\cdot\mathrm{CE}(p_{\text{simple}},p_{\text{complex}}).

By guiding the model to focus on ambiguous or reasoning-intensive regions, this mechanism enhances robustness to complex linguistic instructions.

Multi-Stage Training.
SAM3-I is trained through a three-stage curriculum designed to ensure stable convergence and progressively acquire increasingly complex instruction-following capabilities:

•

i) Stage 1: Simple-instruction learning.
The SAM3 backbone is frozen. The S-Adapter is trained on simple instructional data to establish category-, attribute-, and relation-level grounding ability.

•

ii) Stage 2: Complex-instruction reasoning.
The S-Adapter is kept fine-tuned.
The C-Adapter is inherited from it and trained using complex instructions to enable the model with functional, and reasoning-level grounding ability.

•

iii) Stage 3: Joint alignment refinement.
All adapters are activated and jointly fine-tuned using the alignment objectives, harmonizing the two branches and ensuring consistent instruction-conditioned predictions.

This progressive curriculum reflects the inherent hierarchy of linguistic complexity and allows SAM3-I to integrate new instruction-following capabilities without disrupting SAM3’s original concept-driven strengths. The overall training objective is
\mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{seg}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{hard}}
.

Scalable Instructional Data Construction

To enable PIS at scale, SAM3-I requires a large and diverse collection of high-quality instruction–mask pairs. To achieve this, we develop a semi-automatic data construction pipeline that enriches existing open-vocabulary segmentation datasets with rich, compositional natural-language instructions. The pipeline operates in three stages: i) MLLM-assisted automatic instruction annotation, ii) agentic quality inspection using a second MLLM, and iii) human-assisted correction to resolve challenging or ambiguous cases. An overview of the full workflow is shown in Fig.
4
.

Instruction Taxonomy.
For each target instance, we generate multiple levels of instructions to reflect the diversity of natural-language grounding:

•

Positive instructions
include two levels.
i) Simple instructions
explicitly include the target’s NP and correspond to referring-style descriptions that focus on the target’s perceptual attributes,
e.g
.
, color, shape, states, and spatial relationships alongside the target’s NP.
ii) Complex instructions
exclude the NP and instead rely on cognitive reasoning to identify the target,
e.g
.
, action, functions, and affordance, forcing the model to interpret the underlying intent and deduce the correct object to segment. Both instruction types are produced in declarative and question formats, aiming to enrich linguistic diversity.

•

Negative instructions
are synthesized by modifying or contradicting the visual semantics while preserving natural-language fluency. They act as contrastive distractors, enabling the inspector MLLM to reliably distinguish correct instructions from incorrect ones during quality filtering.

•

Concept NPs
are generated for open-vocabulary datasets that provide only raw class labels, ensuring compatibility with SAM3’s PCS-style inputs, which expect short concept NPs rather than long class labels.

Through this process, our pipeline yields four positive instructions, four negative instructions, and one short concept NP for each target instance, all paired with the original class label. The detailed pipeline is described below.

Stage 1: Automatic Instruction Annotation.

Given an image, its ground-truth masks, and the corresponding class labels, we first construct a rich visual prompt that includes the original image, an overlaid image with masks, and cropped object region with masks. Combined with a carefully designed system prompt and a set of few-shot examples, this visual input is fed into an MLLM to produce instruction candidates. For each instance, the annotator MLLM outputs: i) four positive instructions, covering both simple and complex instructions in declarative and question formats, ii) four negative instructions with intentionally contradicted semantics, and iii) one short concept NP compatible with SAM3’s PCS-style prompting.

Stage 2: Agentic Quality Inspection.

All generated positive and negative instructions are then evaluated by a second MLLM through a multiple-choice verification task. The inspector must correctly identify all instructions that align with the visual content,
i.e
.
, image with target mask, while rejecting those that do not. An instruction set is accepted only when the inspector achieves 100% accuracy across all candidates. If any misalignment is detected, the entire set is rejected and returned to Stage 1 for regeneration. This annotation–inspection loop is repeated for up to ten rounds, ensuring both broad coverage and high semantic precision.

Stage 3: Human-in-the-Loop Correction.

Samples that repeatedly fail automatic verification are forwarded to human annotators for final review. Three annotators collaboratively revise or rewrite problematic instructions to ensure correctness, clarity, and precise grounding; extremely ambiguous or impractical cases are discarded. This stage resolves long-tail failures that automated agents struggle to correct.

Through these three stages, our pipeline produces a scalable, high-quality instructional dataset as the foundation for training SAM3-I’s instruction-following capabilities.

Implementation Details.

For data engine construction, we use two separate Qwen3-VL-8B (Instruct) models
1
to perform the annotation–inspection loop described in Sec.
5
. The entire process is executed on 32 H100 GPUs to support large-scale instruction generation and verification. The afterwards model training are carried out with a batch size of 32 on 16 H100 GPUs. During training, we use only the PACO-LVIS-Instruct train split and update the simple- and complex-instruction branches, while keeping the original concept branch frozen to preserve SAM3’s original concept segmentation capability. For hyper-parameters, we follow the original SAM3 settings without modification. For the SAM3 plus multimodal-agent, we set the maximum number of iterative checking rounds to 3.

Discussion and Outlook

While SAM3-I demonstrates the feasibility of extending the SAM family toward instruction-following segmentation, several limitations and opportunities for further improvement remain:

i) Scaling training data for richer instruction knowledge.
Our current data engine offers an effective prototype for generating rich instructional corpus, yet scaling to larger and more diverse instruction–mask datasets would further strengthen the model’s ability to understand and reason over natural-language instructions. Similar to how SA-1B, SA-V, and SA-Co
9
;
19
;
3
enabled the evolution of the SAM family, expanding the scale and diversity of PIS training data across both object- and part-level annotations is a crucial step toward building a more comprehensive instruction-grounded segmentation foundation model.

ii) Potential bias of instruction-instance pairs.
SAM3-I is primarily trained on settings where multiple instructions correspond to a single instance, and all instances are associated with a single concept under original SAM3’s PCS formulation,
i.e
.
,
N-to-1 instance segmentation
. However, real-world scenarios often require interpreting instructions that refer to multiple instances simultaneously,
i.e
.
, group reasoning or
1-to-N instance segmentation
. Overcoming this limitation calls for greater data diversity and explicit mitigation of dataset bias, enabling SAM3-I to generalize more effectively to multi-instance and group-level grounding.

iii) More flexible and scalable architecture.
The current design employs separate branches or adapters for concepts, simple, and complex instructions. A promising direction for scaling SAM3-I is to move beyond heuristic branch switching and adopt adaptive routing mechanisms. For example, consolidating the instruction-guided branches or introducing a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)
4
;
15
router could dynamically choose the appropriate reasoning path while preserving SAM3’s original concept-level capabilities.

iv) Broader evaluations.
A natural next step is to fine-tune SAM3-I on task-specific instruction datasets, such as training on ReasonSeg
11
and evaluating its instruction-level generalization under domain-specific conditions.
In addition, as larger-scale instructional data becomes available, we expect SAM3-I to progressively evolve toward a more foundational PIS model.
To this end, we will also conduct broader zero-shot generalization studies, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of SAM3-I’s adaptability and robustness across different tasks and linguistic distributions.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced SAM3-I, an enhanced framework that unifies concept-level understanding and instruction-level reasoning within the SAM family. Enabled by a scalable instructional data construction pipeline, SAM3-I provides a preliminary demonstration that SAM3 can be effectively extended to follow natural-language instructions while preserving its strong concept grounding. We further analyzed the current limitations of SAM3-I and outlined several directions for future improvement. Moving forward, we aim to scale the instruction corpus, explore finer-grained part-aware segmentation, and develop more flexible and adaptive architectures that better accommodate diverse real-world scenarios. We hope that SAM3-I represents an early step toward more capable instruction-driven segmentation foundation models.
