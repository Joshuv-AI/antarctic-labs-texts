Differentiating pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma from solid pseudopapillary neoplasm using contrast-enhanced computed tomography
Authors: Jie Yu, Yingying Cao, Anna Jie, Xiaolong He, Zhongqiu Wang, Shuai Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSolid pseudopapillary neoplasm (SPN) and pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma (PACC) represent two uncommon pancreatic tumors with distinct biological behaviors and management strategies. Both entities can present with overlapping appearances on contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT), posing a diagnostic challenge preoperatively. Therefore, reliable imaging differentiation between these tumors is clinically important. This study aimed to evaluate computed tomography (CT) imaging features for differentiating SPN from PACC and to develop a combined model to improve preoperative diagnostic performance.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 68 patients (51 SPN, 17 PACC) who underwent preoperative CECT. Clinical and CT imaging features were compared between groups. Variables that were significant in univariate analysis were entered into a forward stepwise multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and differences between ROC curves were compared using the DeLong test.ResultsPatients with PACC were significantly older and more likely to be male than those with SPN (both P<0.001). The tumor-to-pancreas enhancement ratio in the pancreatic parenchymal phase (PPP) was significantly higher in PACC (P=0.004). The “floating cloud” sign was observed more frequently in SPN (P=0.010), whereas pancreatic duct dilation occurred more frequently in PACC (P=0.004). Multivariate analysis identified the tumor-to-pancreas enhancement ratio in the PPP [odds ratio (OR) =1.10; P=0.002] and pancreatic duct dilation (OR =4.65; P=0.036) as independent predictors of PACC, whereas the “floating cloud” sign was independently associated with SPN (OR =0.12;P=0.010). The combined model achieved the highest area under the curve (AUC) among all evaluated diagnostic models in this cohort (AUC =0.865).ConclusionsA combined assessment incorporating the tumor-to-pancreas enhancement ratio in the PPP, the “floating cloud” sign, and pancreatic duct dilation may improve preoperative differentiation between PACC and SPN.

Introduction

Solid pseudopapillary neoplasm (SPN) is a rare pancreatic tumor of uncertain histogenesis, accounting for roughly 1–2% of exocrine pancreatic neoplasms and around 5% of cystic pancreatic neoplasms (1). SPN predominantly affects young women, most commonly between the second and fourth decades of life, and carries a favorable prognosis following surgical resection (2). In contrast, pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma (PACC) originates from acinar cells of the exocrine pancreas and accounts for approximately 1–2% of all pancreatic tumors (3). Unlike SPN, PACC tends to occur in older male patients and is associated with a more aggressive clinical course (4).

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) remains the cornerstone of preoperative pancreatic tumor evaluation (5). However, differentiation between SPN and PACC remains challenging due to substantial overlap in imaging features. Both tumors may present as well-circumscribed masses with heterogeneous internal architecture, including necrosis, hemorrhage, or cystic degeneration. Although SPN is classically described as a cystic–solid lesion and PACC as a predominantly solid tumor, this distinction is frequently unreliable in clinical practice, as SPN may appear nearly solid whereas PACC can exhibit cystic changes (6,7). In addition, both entities may demonstrate heterogeneous enhancement patterns on CECT, and commonly used imaging features such as tumor size, internal composition, and lesion margins lack sufficient specificity for accurate differentiation. Moreover, atypical presentations, such as predominantly solid SPN or well-circumscribed PACC, may further complicate differentiation (8,9).

Consequently, SPN and PACC may be misclassified preoperatively. Accurate preoperative differentiation between these entities is clinically important because they differ substantially in biological behavior, prognosis, and treatment strategies. SPN is generally associated with an excellent outcome following surgical resection, whereas PACC often requires more aggressive management and is associated with a poorer prognosis (2,3).

Despite prior work describing the individual imaging appearances of each tumor (10,11), direct comparative studies integrating both qualitative and quantitative computed tomography (CT) parameters remain limited. The present study aimed to identify CT features distinguishing SPN from PACC and to evaluate whether a combined imaging model could improve preoperative differentiation. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1280/rc).

Methods

Study population

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (No. 2022NL-060-02), and the requirement for individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Between January 2021 and March 2026, patients with pathologically confirmed SPN or PACC based on surgical resection or biopsy were identified from the database of the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine. Of 83 patients initially screened for SPN, 32 were excluded (19 lacked adequate CECT and 13 had undergone prior local treatment), leaving 51 for analysis. Of 21 PACC patients identified, 4 were excluded (3 without CECT, 1 with prior local treatment), yielding 17 eligible cases. The final cohort therefore comprised 68 patients (51 SPN, 17 PACC). CT protocol

CECT was performed using multidetector scanners (GE Optima 670, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA; Philips Brilliance 64, Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, Netherlands). All patients were scanned in the supine position from the diaphragm to the inferior margin of the kidneys. Patients fasted for at least 6 hours and received 800–1,000 mL of water as a negative oral contrast agent before scanning.

The protocol included an unenhanced scan followed by triphasic contrast-enhanced imaging. A nonionic contrast agent (iohexol, 350 mg/mL) was administered via an antecubital vein at a dose of 1.5 mL/kg with an injection rate of 3.0–4.0 mL/s. The pancreatic parenchymal, portal venous, and delayed phases (DPs) were acquired at fixed delays of 40, 60, and 120 seconds, respectively, after contrast injection. Scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage, 120 kV; automatic tube current modulation; rotation time, 0.5 seconds; pitch, 0.984–1.375; slice thickness, 1.0–3.0 mm; and reconstruction interval, 1.25 mm.

Image analysis

Qualitative features

Two experienced abdominal radiologists independently reviewed all CT examinations while blinded to pathological results. Disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third senior radiologist. Interobserver agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa for qualitative variables and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for quantitative variables.

Evaluated features included tumor location (head/neck vs. body/tail), shape (round/oval vs. lobulated/irregular), and margin (well-defined vs. ill-defined). A well-defined margin was defined as a smooth, clearly demarcated boundary, whereas an ill-defined margin was characterized by spiculation or infiltration involving more than one-quarter of the tumor perimeter (12). Tumor composition was classified as predominantly solid, mixed solid-cystic, or predominantly cystic according to the proportion of enhancing solid components (13). On account of the limited sample size, tumor composition was subsequently dichotomized as solid versus non-solid for regression analysis. The “floating cloud” sign refers to enhancing solid components suspended within non-enhancing cystic regions (13). Calcification was assessed on unenhanced images. Secondary pancreatic changes included parenchymal atrophy (parenchymal thickness <2 cm) and main pancreatic duct dilation (diameter >3 mm) (14). Peripancreatic lymph node enlargement was defined as a lymph node with a short axis diameter greater than 10 mm on CECT (15).

Quantitative measurements

Quantitative analysis included tumor size, CT attenuation values [Hounsfield units (HU)], and tumor-to-pancreas enhancement ratios across different phases.

Maximum tumor diameter was measured on axial images at the largest cross-section. CT attenuation values (HU) were measured on unenhanced and all contrast-enhanced images by placing a region of interest (ROI) within viable solid tumor tissue, while avoiding necrotic, cystic, calcified, or vascular regions. Each ROI covered approximately one-third to one-half of the tumor area and was maintained at a similar position and size across phases. Measurements were repeated three times and averaged for analysis. The tumor-to-pancreas enhancement ratio was derived by dividing tumor attenuation by that of adjacent normal pancreatic parenchyma, expressed as a percentage (16).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using the software SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R 4.5.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Normality was evaluated with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared by independent-samples t-test; non-normally distributed variables were reported as median [interquartile range (IQR)] and compared by Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were presented as n (%) and assessed by chi-square or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

Univariate analysis was conducted to explore associations between candidate variables and tumor type, and those with P<0.05 were carried forward for further evaluation. Multivariable logistic regression using a forward stepwise approach was subsequently performed to identify independent predictors, with tumor type coded as PACC =1 and SPN =0. To reduce the risk of overfitting given the limited sample size, a forward stepwise approach was applied. The number of variables retained in the final model was considered acceptable in relation to the number of events, in accordance with the events per variable (EPV) principle (17). Variables with potential collinearity were not entered simultaneously.

Diagnostic performance was assessed based on receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. In addition to the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were also calculated. The cutoff value was selected using the Youden index. AUCs were compared by the DeLong test, and a two-sided P<0.05 indicated statistical significance.

CT imaging features

Qualitative findings

Qualitative CT features of PACC and SPN are summarized in Table 1. Tumors in the SPN group were more frequently located in the pancreatic body and tail, whereas PACC lesions were more commonly located in the head and neck (P=0.034). A well-defined margin was more frequently observed in SPN; however, the difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.088).

In terms of internal characteristics, SPN lesions were more often mixed cystic-solid or predominantly cystic, whereas PACC lesions were more frequently solid (P=0.003). The “floating cloud” sign was more frequently observed in SPN (70.59% vs. 35.29%, P=0.010), whereas calcification was identified only in SPN (49.02% vs. 0%, P<0.001).

Secondary pancreatic changes were more prevalent in PACC. Pancreatic duct dilation (58.82% vs. 21.57%, P=0.004) was significantly more common in PACC. The remaining qualitative features did not differ significantly between groups (all P>0.05).

Pairwise comparisons based on the DeLong test indicated that the combined model performed significantly better than pancreatic duct dilation (P=0.001) and the “floating cloud” sign (P=0.005). Discussion

PACC and SPN are both rare pancreatic neoplasms that may demonstrate overlapping imaging manifestations, including mixed cystic–solid appearance, relatively well-defined margins, and heterogeneous enhancement patterns, making preoperative differentiation challenging. Nevertheless, accurate distinction between these two entities is clinically important because they differ substantially in biological behavior, prognosis, and therapeutic strategy. SPN predominantly affects young women and generally carries a favorable prognosis after surgical resection, whereas PACC more commonly occurs in older men and demonstrates more aggressive biological behavior and poorer clinical outcomes (7,18). Our findings suggest that integrating the “floating cloud” sign, pancreatic duct dilation, and the tumor-to-pancreas enhancement ratio in the PPP may improve CT-based differentiation between PACC and SPN. These findings further support the complementary role of qualitative and quantitative imaging features in pancreatic tumor characterization.

Certain variables were intentionally excluded from the final multivariable model. Although age and sex demonstrated significant differences between groups, these clinical factors were excluded to focus on an imaging-based diagnostic model rather than a general clinical prediction tool. In clinical practice, demographic features alone may not reliably distinguish SPN from PACC, as both entities can present outside their typical demographic distributions. SPN may occur in middle-aged or older male patients, whereas PACC can occasionally present in women, which may lead to diagnostic uncertainty in cases with overlapping clinical characteristics (10,11). A previous study also reported that atypical SPN in male patients are more likely to be misdiagnosed on CT imaging (10). Therefore, CECT-based imaging features may provide more objective and reproducible diagnostic information in such situations. This model may be particularly useful in diagnostically challenging cases with overlapping clinical or imaging features, where demographic information alone is insufficient for confident differentiation.

Calcification was not retained in the final multivariable model despite its recognized diagnostic relevance. Previous studies have reported that calcification is relatively common in SPN but uncommon in PACC (11,19), making it a useful discriminative feature. However, in the present cohort, calcification showed marked imbalance between groups and resulted in quasi-complete separation in logistic regression, leading to unstable parameter estimation. Therefore, it was excluded from the multivariable model to ensure model stability and reliable coefficient estimation. Nevertheless, calcification remains a meaningful qualitative imaging feature in clinical practice. Its presence strongly favors SPN over PACC, whereas its absence should not be used to exclude SPN because calcification was not observed in all SPN cases in this cohort. Therefore, calcification should be interpreted together with the tumor-to-pancreas enhancement ratio in the PPP, pancreatic duct dilation, and the “floating cloud” sign rather than in isolation. Peripancreatic lymph node enlargement was also evaluated as a potentially relevant CT feature in pancreatic neoplasms. However, no significant difference was observed between PACC and SPN in the present cohort. Consequently, this variable was not included in the multivariable analysis. Although peripancreatic lymph node enlargement may reflect tumor biology in certain settings, its value for differentiating PACC from SPN remains uncertain and warrants further investigation in larger cohorts.

Among qualitative CT features, the “floating cloud” sign was observed in 70.59% of SPN cases in our study, consistent with previously reported rates ranging from 56.1% to 84.4% (13,20). Ren et al. (13) further demonstrated that this sign was significantly more frequent in SPN than in PDAC with cystic changes (84.4% vs. 42.9%, P<0.001), supporting its role in differential diagnosis. This imaging appearance likely reflects the characteristic histopathological composition of SPN, which typically presents as an encapsulated mass containing varying proportions of solid, cystic, hemorrhagic, and necrotic components due to hemorrhagic and cystic degeneration (21). Nevertheless, this sign should not be interpreted in isolation, as Li et al. (20) reported no significant difference in its prevalence between invasive and non-invasive SPN subgroups (P=0.847), suggesting limited value in reflecting tumor biological behavior. Therefore, although the “floating cloud” sign provides useful qualitative diagnostic information, it should be interpreted in conjunction with other imaging and clinical findings.

Pancreatic duct dilation was identified as an independent predictor of PACC. Unlike pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), which arises from the pancreatic ductal epithelium, PACC originates from pancreatic acinar cells (4). Therefore, duct dilation in PACC is unlikely to reflect a primary ductal involvement and is more likely related to secondary effects such as ductal compression or expansile tumor growth, particularly in larger lesions or tumors located adjacent to the pancreatic ductal system. In larger tumors, mass effect may also result in upstream dilation of the main pancreatic duct or its branches, although such secondary ductal dilation remains relatively uncommon in PACC. In some cases, PACC may additionally demonstrate focal intraductal or polypoid extension toward the pancreatic duct, which may further contribute to ductal obstruction (22). Previous studies have similarly shown that although pancreatic ductal dilation is less frequent in PACC than in PDAC, it may still occur in a subset of patients (7,22). In contrast, SPN is typically characterized by indolent circumscribed growth and the presence of a fibrous pseudocapsule, tending to displace rather than obstruct adjacent pancreatic ducts, thereby resulting in a lower frequency of pancreatic ductal dilation (23). Consequently, the presence of duct dilation on CT may still provide important discriminatory value in differentiating PACC from SPN despite the acinar origin of PACC. The diagnostic value of pancreatic duct dilation may be more pronounced in relatively small tumors located in the pancreatic head or body, where even subtle ductal obstruction can be more readily detected. Clinically, this feature may be particularly helpful when evaluating lesions in the pancreatic head, where ductal obstruction is more readily appreciable.

Regarding quantitative imaging parameters, absolute CT attenuation values did not differ significantly between PACC and SPN across any phase. However, the tumor-to-pancreas enhancement ratio during the PPP was significantly higher in PACC than SPN (64.00% vs. 50.32%, P=0.004), whereas no significant differences were observed during the portal venous or DPs. This finding highlights the importance of normalizing tumor enhancement to the pancreatic parenchyma, which may reduce interpatient variability related to contrast administration and scan timing (24). The more pronounced differences in the PPP suggest higher sensitivity for detecting early enhancement differences, whereas contrast equilibration in later phases may reduce contrast between tumors. In this context, the optimal cutoff value of 63.53% derived from ROC analysis should be regarded as an exploratory threshold rather than a definitive clinical cutoff and requires external validation.

Previous studies have reported heterogeneous enhancement characteristics in PACC. Although PACC has traditionally been described as a hypoattenuating and relatively hypovascular tumor, growing evidence suggests considerable variability in its enhancement pattern. Notably, Barat et al. (25) reported that hypoattenuation in only 45% of PACC compared with 85% of PDAC (P=0.009), with variable peak enhancement timing across tumors (11,25), suggesting heterogeneity in tumor vascularity. In the present study, the higher tumor-to-pancreas enhancement ratio observed in PACC during the PPP may reflect the presence of viable acinar tumor components with relatively preserved vascularity, despite frequent internal necrosis or hemorrhage (11). In contrast, SPN frequently contains varying degrees of degenerative changes, including cystic and hemorrhagic components, which may reduce measurable enhancement on CT (21,26). These differences may partially explain the observed quantitative findings and support the value of enhancement ratios in pancreatic tumor characterization. Future integration of quantitative imaging with radiomics, molecular profiling, and artificial intelligence may further improve comprehensive tumor characterization (27-29).

In the comparison of diagnostic models, the combined model incorporating the “floating cloud” sign, pancreatic duct dilation, and the tumor-to-pancreas enhancement ratio in the PPP showed superior performance compared with individual imaging features in this cohort. Although the enhancement ratio alone already showed strong discriminatory ability, the addition of complementary qualitative imaging features further improved model performance and more closely reflected the multiparametric approach used in routine radiologic assessment. The combined model significantly outperformed the individual qualitative imaging features, whereas no statistically significant difference was observed compared with the enhancement ratio in the PPP alone. This finding suggests that quantitative enhancement parameters may contribute substantially to the differentiation of PACC and SPN, whereas qualitative imaging findings provide complementary and incremental diagnostic information. Given the limited number of PACC cases and the use of forward stepwise variable selection, the proposed model should be considered exploratory and may be susceptible to overfitting. Although the combined model achieved an AUC of 0.865, it did not significantly outperform the tumor-to-pancreas enhancement ratio in the PPP alone, suggesting limited incremental value of qualitative imaging features in this cohort. Therefore, validation in larger independent datasets will be necessary to confirm both the reproducibility of the model and the clinical utility of the exploratory cutoff identified in this study. Beyond external validation, future studies may explore multimodality imaging and advanced imaging biomarkers to further improve pancreatic tumor characterization and support imaging-guided clinical decision-making (30,31).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, which may have introduced selection bias, and the relatively small number of PACC cases further limited statistical power. The wide confidence intervals observed for certain variables, particularly pancreatic duct dilation, reflect the limited sample size and the uneven distribution of imaging features between groups. Second, the proposed model was not externally validated, and future multicenter prospective studies are needed to confirm its generalizability. Third, a fixed-delay scanning protocol rather than bolus tracking was used. Although image acquisition was standardized, this protocol may have introduced variability in enhancement timing among patients, potentially affecting quantitative attenuation values. Nevertheless, the tumor-to-pancreas enhancement ratio may have partially reduced the impact of interpatient hemodynamic variability on quantitative analysis. Finally, although the proposed model showed encouraging diagnostic performance in this cohort, further validation in larger multicenter cohorts will be necessary before clinical implementation.

Conclusions

The tumor-to-pancreas enhancement ratio in the PPP, together with the “floating cloud” sign and pancreatic duct dilation, may improve CECT-based differentiation between PACC and SPN. Although individual imaging features alone are insufficient for reliable diagnosis, a multiparametric approach combining quantitative and qualitative imaging features may improve diagnostic confidence in selected cases. Further external validation in larger cohorts is required to confirm these findings.
