RUNX1 upregulation and RUNX1-ETO depletion inhibits survival of t(8;21)-positive leukemia cells
Authors: Junshi Zhang, Ruixia Sun, Yafang Chen, Xinxiao Lu, Qiuqiu Zhang, Xue Wang, Xiaosi Jiang, Zhichao Liu, Dongmei Li, Guang Yang, Yuan Meng, Kefei Wang, Shuang Yang, Xingli Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundt(8;21) acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is one of the most extensively studied AML subtypes, with the RUNX1-ETO fusion gene playing a crucial role in its pathogenesis. However, targeting this fusion gene alone has not yielded positive outcomes, likely because it fails to eliminate leukemia cells without restoring normal RUNX1 function.ObjectiveTo enhance the therapeutic efficacy for this leukemia, we propose a novel strategy based on the pathogenic mechanism. We deliver RUNX1 small activating RNA (RUNX1‑saRNA) and RUNX1‑ETO small interfering RNA (RUNX1‑ETO‑siRNA) to RUNX1‑ETO‑positive AML cells, aiming to simultaneously silence the fusion gene and restore wild‑type RUNX1 expression.MethodsKasumi‑1 cells were transfected with RUNX1‑saRNA and/or RUNX1‑ETO‑siRNA. The effects on apoptosis, autophagy, cell proliferation, and cell cycle progression were evaluated by Western blotting, CCK‑8 assays, and real‑time PCR.ResultsUpregulation of RUNX1 promoted apoptosis and autophagy, inhibited proliferation, and blocked cell cycle progression. Similarly, downregulation of RUNX1-ETO produced comparable effects. The combined treatment resulted in enhanced effects compared with either intervention alone, with increased apoptosis and autophagy, and more pronounced inhibition of proliferation and cell cycle arrest.ConclusionsThis combination therapy exerts potent cytotoxic effects on RUNX1-ETO-positive AML cells in vitro. These findings provide a preliminary basis for further investigation of nucleic acid-based combination strategies for fusion gene-driven leukemias.Keywords: RUNX1, RUNX1-ETO, AML, SaRNA, SiRNA

Introduction

RUNX1-ETO-positive AML is the most common and best studied of the various AML subtypes. This form of AML is typically caused by the t (8;21) chromosomal translocation, which results in the fusion of a portion of the RUNX1 gene with the ETO gene to generate the RUNX1-ETO fusion gene. This fusion gene plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of AML [1]. RUNX1 is an essential transcription factor that is involved in normal hematopoiesis by regulating the differentiation and self-renewal of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells [2]. In contrast, RUNX1-ETO disrupts the normal transcriptional activation function of RUNX1 by binding to its target genes, thereby inhibiting the expression of genes that promote the growth of hematopoietic cells [3], [4], [5]. This inhibition leads to the over-accumulation of immature myeloid cells, ultimately leading to the development of leukemia.

Research has indicated that the expression level of RUNX1-ETO is an independent prognostic factor for t(8;21) AML. The results of the MRC AML−15 clinical trial in the UK showed that a decrease of more than 3 log values in RUNX1-ETO transcripts after one course of chemotherapy indicates a good prognosis [6]. Additionally, two studies have indicated that after two courses of consolidation therapy with intermediate- to high-dose cytarabine, a reduction of less than 3 logs in RUNX1-ETO transcript levels is significantly associated with the absence of relapse [7], [8]. These findings highlight that RUNX1-ETO plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of leukemia and serves as a specific therapeutic target for t(8;21) AML.

Currently, there is a lack of specific targeted therapeutic drugs for treating RUNX1-ETO-positive AML, and the efficacy of some non-specific targeted therapies is limited [9], [10], [11], [12]. High-dose cytarabine remains the primary treatment method for RUNX1-ETO-positive AML. However, this high therapeutic dose can cause severe toxic side effects, such as bone marrow suppression, fever, and vomiting [13], [14]. Although the prognosis for patients with this subtype of AML is relatively better compared to other types, a significant number of patients still relapse [15]. Therefore, there is an urgent clinical need for an effective and safe therapy to further improve the prognosis of RUNX1-ETO-positive AML.

Previous efforts to restore RUNX1 function have included pharmacological modulation of its interacting partners and genetic overexpression systems [16], [17]. However, direct pharmacological reactivation of RUNX1 at the transcriptional level remains challenging, and constitutive overexpression systems lack dynamic regulation and may pose safety concerns. In contrast, small activating RNA (saRNA) technology offers a post-transcriptional, sequence-specific approach to endogenously upregulate RUNX1 expression, potentially providing a more physiologically relevant and controllable strategy. Accordingly, we sought to leverage this saRNA-based approach to restore RUNX1 function in the context of t(8;21) AML, and further combine it with RUNX1-ETO silencing to address both sides of the pathogenic imbalance. Our findings indicate that saRNA-mediated upregulation of RUNX1 expression induces apoptosis and autophagy, inhibits proliferation, and arrests cell cycle progression in RUNX1-ETO-positive AML cells. Similarly, downregulation of RUNX1-ETO expression using siRNA exhibits analogous effects. Simultaneous silencing of RUNX1-ETO and overexpression of RUNX1 in Kasumi−1 cells significantly increased apoptosis and autophagy, attenuated cell proliferation, and enhanced cell cycle arrest. These preliminary results suggest that dual targeting of RUNX1-ETO and RUNX1 is feasible for improving the therapeutic effect of t(8;21) AML. This combined oligonucleotide approach demonstrates the potential of saRNA and siRNA technologies in gene therapy for fusion gene-driven leukemias.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and transfection

Kasumi−1 cells were purchased from Fenghui Biotechnology (Hunan, China) and cultured in RPMI−1640 medium (Gibco-Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 20% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Hyclone, South Logan, UT, USA) at 37℃ under 5% CO2. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Oligonucleotide sequences

Four small activating RNAs (saRNAs) targeting the promoter region of RUNX1 and one small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting the junction sequence of RUNX1-ETO fusion transcript were designed and synthesized. The sequences of the four RUNX1-targeting saRNAs are listed below: saRUNX1#1: sense, 5′−UUCCCUUUCCCAGGCUUUATT−3′; antisense, 5′−UAAAGC CUGGGAAAGGGAATT−3′; saRUNX1#2: sense, 5′− GGCAUUGCUCAGAAAAGAATT−3′; antisense, 5′−UUCUUUUCUGAGCAAUGCCTT−3′; saRUNX1#3: sense, 5′−GUGGAAAG GGGAACAGUUATT−3′; antisense, 5′−UAACUGUU CCCCUUUCCACTT−3′; saRUNX1#4: sense, 5′−GGGUCAGACUCUCACCAAATT−3′; antisense, 5′−UUUGGUGAGAGUCUGACC CTT−3′. The siRNA sequence targeting RUNX1-ETO was as follows: 5′−CCUCGAAA UCGUACUGAGAAGTT−3′; antisense, 5′−CUUCUCAGUACGAUUUCGA GGTT−3′.

Antibodies and reagents

We purchased antibodies against RUNX1 from Proteintech (Hubei, China), antibodies against tubulin from Affinity Biosciences (Jiangsu, China), and antibodies against CyclinD1 from SAB (Jiangsu, China). Antibodies against Bax, Bcl2, P62, BECN1, LC3II, P21, P27, HRP-conjugated Goat anti-mouse IgG, and HRP-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG were purchased from BBI Life Sciences (Shanghai, China). RIPA Lysis Buffer purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China); Cell Counting Kit−8 purchased from Biosharp (Anhui, China); HiFiScript cDNA Synthesis Kit purchased from cwbio (Jiangsu, China).

Real-time PCR

Cell RNA was extracted and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using HiFiScript cDNA Synthesis Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. Real-time PCR was performed on the Roche LightCycler® 480 System (Basel, Switzerland). The primer sequences were as follows: GAPDH, forward, 5′−AACAGCGACACCCATCCTC−3′ and reverse, 5′−CATACCAGGAAA TGAGCTTGACAA−3′; RUNX1, forward, 5′−ACCGACAGCCCCAACTTCCT−3′ and reverse, 5′−GCTTTTCCCTCTTCCACTTC−3′; RUNX1-ETO, forward, 5′−AATCACAGTGGATGG GCCC−3′ and reverse, 5′−TGCGTCTTCACATCCACAGG−3′. Data were analyzed by the ΔΔCt method.

Cell proliferation assay

After transfection, the cells (1 ×104 cells) were seeded in a 96-well culture plate, and 10 μl of CCK−8 solution was added to each well. The plate was then incubated at 37°C in the dark for 3 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using the BioTek Synergy LX multi-mode reader, and the proliferation rate of each group was calculated.

Western blotting

Cells were harvested and lysed with RIPA Lysis Buffer at 4°C for 30 min. The lysate was then centrifuged at 4°C at 12,000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected and mixed with loading buffer. The cell lysates were subsequently subjected to SDS-PAGE, and the proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. After blocking the membranes with 5% nonfat milk at room temperature for 45 min, primary antibodies were incubated for 1.5 h, followed by a 45-min incubation with the secondary antibody. Finally, the membranes were treated with chemiluminescent reagent for 2 min and visualized using the Tanon 5200 system (Tanon, Shanghai, China). Densitometric quantification of Western blot bands was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Protein expression levels were normalized to the corresponding Tubulin loading controls and expressed as fold change relative to the control group.

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of the results of three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed using the GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). * stands for P < 0.05, ** stands for P < 0.01, *** stands for P < 0.001, and **** stands for P < 0.0001 in comparison.

Results

saRNA upregulates RUNX1 expression in Kasumi−1 cells

To investigate the effects of RUNX1 upregulation and RUNX1-ETO downregulation on the biological function of leukemia cells, we initially upregulated the expression of RUNX1 and downregulated the expression of RUNX1-ETO in cells. Four small activating RNAs targeting RUNX1 were designed and transfected into Kasumi−1 cells to assess their effects on RUNX1 expression. As shown in Fig. 1 A, Western blotting showed that all four saRNAs significantly upregulated RUNX1 expression compared to the control group. However, both saRNA#1 and saRNA#2 also led to the upregulation of RUNX1-ETO expression. Protein quantification results are presented in Fig. 1B. Real-time PCR further demonstrated that saRNA#1 and saRNA#2 not only upregulated the mRNA levels of RUNX1 but also those of RUNX1-ETO, whereas saRNA#3 and saRNA#4 specifically upregulated the expression of RUNX1 (Fig. 1 C and D).

(C) Kasumi−1 cells (5 ×105) were transfected with saRNA (30 pmol), 48 h post-transfection, cells were harvested, and total RNA was extracted and reverse transcribed into cDNA. The biological function of RUNX1 upregulation in Kasumi−1 cells

After confirming that saRNA#3 and saRNA#4 specifically upregulate RUNX1 expression, we investigated the impact of this upregulation on cellular functions. Kasumi−1 cells were transfected with saRNA#3, saRNA#4, and control saRNA, respectively. After 72 h, samples were collected and Western blotting was performed to detect the effect of RUNX1 upregulation on cell apoptosis. The results are shown in Fig. 2A, compared to the control group, upregulation of RUNX1 decreased the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl−2 and increased the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax, indicating the promotion of apoptosis in t(8;21) AML cells. Similarly, the effect of RUNX1 upregulation on cell autophagy was examined. As shown in Fig. 2 B, upregulation of RUNX1 decreased P62 expression and increased BECN1 and LC3II expression in Kasumi−1 cells, suggesting that upregulation of RUNX1 promotes cell autophagy. The concurrent decrease in P62 together with increases in BECN1 and LC3II supports enhanced autophagic activity, although LC3II accumulation alone can also reflect impaired flux. Next, we examined the effect of RUNX1 upregulation on the proliferation of Kasumi−1 cells. Cell proliferation was measured at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h after transfection with saRNA. The results in Fig. 2C showed that upregulation of RUNX1 inhibited AML cell proliferation. Furthermore, we investigated the effect of RUNX1 upregulation on the cell cycle of Kasumi−1 cells and found that it led to an increase in the cell cycle arrest proteins P21 and P27, indicating that RUNX1 upregulation induces cell cycle arrest (Fig. 2D). These results indicate that upregulation of RUNX1 can inhibit the growth of human AML cells by promoting apoptosis, autophagy and blocking cell cycle progression.

siRNA downregulates RUNX1-ETO expression in Kasumi−1 cells

After observing the impact of saRNA-induced upregulation of RUNX1 expression on certain cellular functions in Kasumi−1 cells, we subsequently employed siRNA to downregulate endogenous RUNX1-ETO expression to assess its influence on cellular functions. Kasumi−1 cells were transfected with siRNA and control RNA, and the cells were harvested for Western blot analysis 48 h post-transfection. As shown in Fig. 3 A and B, siRNA effectively reduced the expression of endogenous RUNX1-ETO protein in the cells. Real-time PCR also showed that siRNA reduced the mRNA levels of RUNX1-ETO, but did not affect the mRNA levels of RUNX1 (Fig. 3 C and D).

The biological function of RUNX1-ETO downregulation in Kasumi−1 cells

Having demonstrated that siRNA can specifically downregulate RUNX1-ETO expression in cells, we investigated the effect of this downregulation on cellular functions. The results are shown in Fig. 4A, downregulation of RUNX1-ETO decreased Bcl−2 levels and increased Bax levels, indicating that RUNX1-ETO downregulation promotes cell apoptosis. Autophagy related genes P62 decreased, while BECN1 and LC3II increased, suggesting enhanced cell autophagy (Fig. 4B). Cell proliferation was detected at 0, 24, 48 and 72 h after transfection with siRUNX1-ETO, and the results showed that RUNX1-ETO knockdown reduced cell proliferation (Fig. 4C). Additionally, Western blot analysis of cell cycle related proteins revealed upregulation of P27 and downregulation of CyclinD1 and CDK2, indicating that RUNX1-ETO knockdown induced cell cycle arrest in the G1 phase (Fig. 4D).

Enhanced effects of saRUNX1 and siRUNX1-ETO in Kasumi−1 cells

After investigating the effects of upregulating RUNX1 or downregulating RUNX1-ETO on the biological function of Kasumi−1 cells, we conducted co-transfection experiments using saRNA and siRNA to explore the combined effect of these two small RNAs on Kasumi−1 cell functions. As shown in Fig. 5A and B, the combination of upregulating RUNX1 and downregulating RUNX1-ETO expression led to a increase in apoptosis and autophagy of Kasumi−1 cells compared to single treatment. Moreover, cell proliferation experiments revealed that either upregulating RUNX1 or downregulating RUNX1-ETO can inhibit cell proliferation, and the combined treatment resulted in greater inhibition. (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, Western blot analysis of cell cycle-related proteins demonstrated that combined treatment led to enhanced cell cycle arrest (Fig. 5D). These findings imply that the combined treatment may be a more effective approach for targeting RUNX1-ETO-positive AML cells.

Discussion

RUNX1-ETO is the product of the t(8;21) chromosomal translocation and represents a defining genetic lesion in one of the most common AML subtypes. Its pathogenic role has been extensively characterized: the fusion protein disrupts normal RUNX1-mediated transcriptional programs by competing for shared DNA binding sites, thereby impairing hematopoietic differentiation and promoting the accumulation of immature myeloid progenitors [1], [3], [4], [18]. Beyond this dominant-negative interference, RUNX1-ETO actively remodels the epigenetic landscape and corrupts cell cycle regulation, reinforcing its central role in leukemogenesis [18], [19], [20]. Consistent with earlier reports using siRNA electroporation or nanoparticle delivery [21], [22], our study demonstrated that siRNA-mediated RUNX1-ETO knockdown in t(8;21)-positive AML cells resulted in reduced proliferation and G1-phase cell cycle arrest. Notably, we also observed increased apoptosis and autophagy following RUNX1-ETO knockdown. These observations extend the current understanding of RUNX1-ETO biology, suggesting that its pathogenic role extends beyond cell cycle regulation to the suppression of programmed cell death and autophagic pathways.

The role of wild-type RUNX1 in myeloid malignancies is context-dependent. In t(8;21) AML, RUNX1 has been proposed to function as a tumor suppressor: its overexpression induces P57 via KLF4, inhibits proliferation, and promotes apoptosis [16], and cytotoxic drugs can upregulate RUNX1 to enhance the DNA damage response [23]. Our saRNA-mediated RUNX1 upregulation recapitulated these anti-leukemic effects, driving apoptosis, autophagy, cell cycle arrest, and growth inhibition. However, in other AML contexts, such as MLL-AF9-driven models, RUNX1 can exert pro-survival functions, and its expression has been linked to leukemic cell proliferation through targets like CENPE [24], [25]. This functional duality underscores that the net effect of RUNX1 modulation is highly dependent on cellular genotype, mutational landscape, and the presence of fusion-induced transcriptional rewiring. In our specific setting, transient restoration of RUNX1 activity likely counterbalances the dominant-negative interference of RUNX1-ETO, tilting the balance toward growth suppression. Importantly, these suppressive effects were observed specifically in the RUNX1-ETO-positive context. Given the context-dependent functions of RUNX1 across genetically distinct AML subtypes, further validation in additional experimental models will be required before these findings can be generalized to other forms of AML.

The interplay between RUNX1 and RUNX1-ETO within leukemia cells is not merely one of passive repression versus activation. While RUNX1-ETO was long viewed as a competitive inhibitor of RUNX1 DNA binding, recent chromatin studies reveal that it actively establishes a unique epigenetic state, and its depletion generates novel RUNX1 occupancy sites that were previously inaccessible [20], [26]. This suggests that restoring wild-type RUNX1 function while simultaneously removing the fusion protein may be more efficacious than targeting either node alone. Previous studies have predominantly focused on RUNX1-ETO silencing as a single modality; our approach distinguishes itself by simultaneously restoring wild-type RUNX1 expression, addressing both the gain-of-function and loss-of-function components of the pathogenic mechanism. Our co-transfection experiments support this hypothesis: combined RUNX1-saRNA and RUNX1-ETO-siRNA treatment produced greater apoptosis, autophagy, cell cycle arrest, and proliferation inhibition compared to either monotherapy. By simultaneously silencing RUNX1-ETO and restoring wild-type RUNX1 expression, this dual strategy may provide a complementary approach to fusion gene silencing alone and warrants further validation in additional experimental models.

An unexpected but informative finding was the differential selectivity of the four RUNX1-targeting saRNAs. While saRNA#1 and #2 upregulated both RUNX1 and RUNX1-ETO, saRNA#3 and #4 selectively enhanced only the wild-type transcript. Given that the fusion allele retains the 5′ regulatory region of RUNX1, saRNAs targeting shared promoter elements may activate both alleles, whereas those targeting regions unique to the intact locus or regions where the fusion allele has undergone epigenetic alteration could exhibit selectivity. saRNAs can modulate transcription through chromatin remodeling, recruitment of transcriptional co-activators, or enhancer-promoter interactions [27], [28]; thus, positional differences in target sites may dictate allele-specific outcomes. Although we cannot exclude off-target effects, the consistent functional differences between saRNA pairs suggest genuine locus-selective activity. Formal validation via chromatin immunoprecipitation and chromosome conformation capture will be required to delineate the underlying allele-specific regulatory architecture.

Current minimal residual disease (MRD)-guided strategies for t(8;21) AML rely on serial monitoring of RUNX1-ETO transcript levels, with a > 3-log reduction after induction conferring favorable prognosis [6], [7], [8]. Patients with inadequate molecular responses remain at high risk of relapse and have limited therapeutic options. Our dual nucleic acid approach, if validated in vivo, could serve as a mechanism-based adjunct to standard chemotherapy in this MRD-positive subset, potentially reducing residual leukemic burden without the off-target toxicity associated with high-dose cytarabine. More broadly, the principle of simultaneously correcting a fusion-driven oncogene and its wild-type counterpart may be applicable to other leukemia subtypes driven by recurrent chromosomal translocations.

While the present study provides proof-of-concept evidence supporting the combined modulation of RUNX1 and RUNX1-ETO in t(8;21) AML cells, several aspects merit further investigation. Validation in additional cellular models, primary patient specimens, and in vivo systems will be valuable for determining the generalizability of these findings. Furthermore, quantitative assessment of potential treatment synergy and evaluation of optimized delivery platforms may provide deeper mechanistic and translational insights. Future studies addressing these aspects will further clarify the therapeutic potential of this dual-targeting strategy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that the combined delivery of RUNX1-saRNA and RUNX1-ETO-siRNA exerts enhanced cytotoxic effects in t(8;21) AML cells in vitro. By restoring wild-type RUNX1 expression while silencing the oncogenic RUNX1-ETO fusion gene, this dual-targeting strategy simultaneously modulates two key molecular drivers implicated in leukemogenesis. Compared with either treatment alone, the combination approach resulted in greater induction of apoptosis and autophagy, stronger cell-cycle arrest, and more inhibition of cell proliferation. These findings provide initial experimental evidence for developing nucleic acid-based combination therapies for RUNX1-ETO-positive AML. Further studies in primary patient samples and in vivo models are warranted to validate these findings and evaluate their translational potential.
