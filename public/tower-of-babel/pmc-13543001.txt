Assessing the effect of dentin pre-treatment techniques on the longevity of Non-Carious Cervical Lesions (NCCLs) restorations: A scoping review☆
Authors: Darianna Masih, Lamia Sami Mokeem, Qoot Alkhubaizi, Mary Anne S. Melo, Guilherme Anziliero Arossi
Published: 2026

Introduction

Non-carious cervical lesions (NCCLs) present a unique challenge in restorative dentistry given their location at the tooth's cervical margin, where enamel is thin or absent, and dentin is often exposed. Caries do not cause these lesions, as they result from a combination of factors, including abrasion, erosion, and occlusal stress, leading to the loss of hard dental tissue [1]. The restoration of NCCLs is crucial for preventing further tissue loss, reducing sensitivity, and restoring aesthetics and function. However, the longevity of these restorations is influenced by the restorative material used, the application technique, and notably, the bonding protocols [2], [3]. Consequently, the choice of adhesive system, its application method, and the pre-treatment of the tooth surface are all critical considerations that can significantly impact the restoration’s outcome [4], [5].

Despite the advancements in adhesive systems, hybrid layer degradation and microleakage over time jeopardize the longevity of the restorations. Fluid infiltration along the cavity walls has harmful effects, such as disruption of marginal integrity, discoloration, and postoperative sensitivity. Hybrid layer degradation is a combination of hydrolysis and percolation of adhesive from the demineralized dentin matrix, which makes it vulnerable to the attack of host-proteolytic enzymes, the metalloproteinases (MMPs) and cysteine cathepsins (CPs) [6], [7]. Over the years, surface pre-treatment techniques have been developed to improve the bonding of restorative materials to the tooth structure. These techniques include mechanical roughening, using fine-grit diamond burs or sandblasting devices [8], and/or chemical dentin pre-treatment. The literature shows different ways to perform this chemical priming of dentin other than the traditional use of phosphoric acid. There are studies advising the use of protease inhibitors, such as Chlorhexidine (CHX), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), and proanthocyanidins (PA); the use sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), and the ethanol wet bonding technique (EWBT) [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14].

Chlorhexidine inhibits the action of MMP-2 (gelatinase A), MMP-8 (collagenase 2), and MMP-9 (gelatinase B) by different mechanisms. A cation-chelating mechanism impedes MMP-2 and MMP-9 by binding to the active site's zinc or calcium metal ions. On the other hand, MMP-8 is inhibited by interacting with the sulfhydryl and/or cysteine group. In the literature, it has been reported that CHX has inactivated MMPs at concentrations as low as 0.02% [10], [15]. Similarly, EDTA inhibits MMPs by chelating the calcium ions and removing the smear layer. Furthermore, it is a weak acid that produces a gentle and shallow demineralization of the dentin, which is considered accountable for the strong bonding to sound and sclerotic dentin in self-etch adhesives [9], [16].

Natural enzymatic inhibitors found in green tea, such as the Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), reduces MMP-2, MMP-9 activity. It also shows collagen cross-linking property, contributing to its maintenance against enzymatic degradation [17]. Another natural product that presents collagen cross-linking enhancement and enzymatic inhibition is proanthocyanidins (PA) from grape-seed extracts [7].

Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) is a non-specific proteolytic agent that removes acid-etching exposed collagen, not affecting carbonates and phosphates, preventing the formation of a non- adhesive-infiltrated layer of collagen, which could undergo hydrolysis. This technique is called deproteinization, and it is often used within a 10% aqueous solution, passively applied for 60 s, followed by rinsing, drying, and adhesive system application [18], [19]. The ethanol wet bonding technique (EWBT) uses alcohol to dehydrate the collagen exposed network from acid etching, increasing the hydrophobicity of the hybrid layer. A simplified protocol of EWBT includes a 1-minute application of 100% ethanol [20].

Despite the availability of multiple restorative techniques, the clinical performance of noncarious cervical lesion (NCCL) restorations remains inconsistent. Long-term studies have reported survival rates ranging from approximately 50% to over 90% after five years, depending on factors such as the adhesive strategy, restorative material, lesion geometry, and occlusal stress distribution [21], [22], [23]. This variability highlights the importance of understanding how material properties, tooth substrate, and clinical technique collectively influence the durability of NCCL restorations. Furthermore, patient-related outcomes such as postoperative sensitivity, satisfaction with the aesthetic results, and the incidence of secondary caries or periodontal responses also merit consideration, as they contribute to the overall success of the treatment [2], [24], [25]. Given the evolving landscape of dental materials and adhesive technologies, alongside the ongoing debate regarding the optimal strategies for NCCL restoration, a systematic exploration of the current evidence is both timely and necessary. Restorative dentistry continues to seek improvements in clinical protocols to enhance the durability, functionality, and aesthetics of NCCL restorations, making it imperative to assess the impact of various factors on restoration longevity [26], [27].

Therefore, this scoping review aims to systematically assess the existing literature on the impact of dentin pre-treatment techniques on the longevity and clinical performance of NCCLs restorations. By examining evidence related to clinical performance, patient satisfaction, and the incidence of complications, this review seeks to identify knowledge gaps that can be further investigated to establish best practices for the management of NCCLs.

Material and methods

Study design

This paper has applied the Arksey and O'Malley framework [28] and has been enhanced by Levac, Colquhoun, and O′Brien's recommendations [29]. The methodological framework comprises determining the research question and relevant studies, selecting the type of study, mapping the data, outlining and reporting the results.

Stage I: Research question

The research question proposed to be answered in this scoping review is: Do dentin chemical pre-treatments influence the clinical longevity outcome of NCCLs restorations?

Stage II: relevant studies search

Two independent reviewers (D. M. and G.A.) searched literature to identify papers in three databases that included references in one language. English publications in Ovid Medline®, EMBASE (Elsevier), and SCOPUS (Elsevier) were searched using the keywords and subject heading methods and were logically connected with the Boolean operators "AND" and "OR". Publication-year limit was set from 1946 to January 12, 2024. Stage III: study selection

The overall inclusion criteria for study selection were based on stages I and II methodologies. Studies eligible for inclusion encompass randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, case-control studies, and cross-sectional studies. The population of interest included patients undergoing restorative treatment for NCCLs with no age restrictions.

The review included studies that evaluated chemical dentin surface pre-treatments prior to the adhesive system application as a restorative protocol for NCCLs. Studies were eligible regardless of the restorative material used, including composite resin and glass ionomer cement (GIC/RMGIC) restorations. The primary outcome of interest is the longevity of restorations, measured through survival rate, failure rate, or the need for re-intervention. Secondary outcomes were postoperative sensitivity, esthetic outcomes, and patient satisfaction.

Finally, the review included studies with a follow-up period of at least 6 months to assess restoration's longevity objectively. The language criterion stipulates that studies were published in English or had available English translations to ensure data accessibility for analysis.

The excluded studies were classified as case reports, editorials, opinion pieces, and animal studies. In vitro studies were also excluded. Regarding the study population, the review excluded studies focusing on carious lesions or other dental conditions unrelated to NCCLs. Regarding intervention focus, studies that did not specifically assess dentin pre-treatment restorative protocols relevant to NCCLs, such as studies focused on the type of bonding system, composite or glass ionomers used as restorative materials, were also excluded. Excluded studies also comprised those with incomplete data or lacking sufficient detail, language limitations without available translations. Results

Regarding sample size distribution (Fig. 2B), numbers ranged from 12 to 148 patients among the studies. When considering “restoration” as the sample, data ranged from 36 to 225 restorations. Most of the studies had a sample size of less than 100 restorations, constituting 40% of the total. This was followed by sample sizes ranging from 100 to 150 restorations, making up 32% of the studies. A smaller proportion of studies had sample sizes over 150 (28%). In terms of follow-up duration (Fig. 2 C), the most common were 12- and 24-months follow-up, reported in 22% of the studies for each period. The next most frequent follow-up periods were 18 and 36 months, observed in 18% of the investigations for each period. Follow-ups of 48 months or greater were observed in only two studies. This demonstrates variability in follow-up periods across studies, indicating inconsistency in long-term outcome assessment.

The mean age of patients in the studies was most reported to be between 50 and 60 years old. Age groups varied from 18 to 92 years old. There was no prevalent gender among the study participants considering all research evaluated. Some studies had an equal distribution between male and female, others had mostly men, while some presented mostly women.

Chlorhexidine (2%) was assessed in five studies involving 20–42 patients with follow-up periods ranging from 6 to 36 months and showed no effect on clinical behavior. EDTA was examined in four studies with 32–50 patients over 12–36 months; while most findings indicated no significant benefit, one study reported higher retention rates, and another noted delayed marginal defects. Ethanol (100%) was tested in three studies with sample sizes between 12 and 148 patients and follow-ups of 6–12 months, with one study reporting a lower success rate. Proanthocyanidins were investigated in two studies involving 33–45 patients over 24 months, showing no impact on clinical outcomes. Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) was evaluated in three studies with 14–30 patients and follow-ups of 24–60 months, also demonstrating no effect on clinical behavior. Finally, oxalic acid–based treatments were analyzed in five studies with 20–40 patients over 12–48 months, with one study reporting reduced postoperative sensitivity. Overall, the included studies show heterogeneity in study designs, sample sizes, follow-up periods, and surface pre-treatment protocols.

Discussion

Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) is used to produce deproteinization, removing acid-etching exposed collagen, preventing the formation of a non-adhesive-infiltrated layer of collagen, which could undergo hydrolysis. Three clinical studies were conducted to evaluate this technique, and the results from all three showed no statistical significance in clinical behavior between the application or not of NaOCl after etching, before adhesive placement and subsequently resin composite restorations at any given evaluation time period [18], [19], [30].

Dentin pre-treatment with chlorhexidine (CHX), usually at a 2% concentration, aims to avoid the metalloproteinases enzymatic action over the exposed collagen present at dentin while adhesive procedures are being done. A total of five clinical studies were identified in the literature. One study evaluated the incorporation of CHX into self-etch adhesives, and found no differences compared to the traditional formulation [31]. All other 4 studies evaluated the use of CHX after phosphoric acid etch& rinse, before adhesive application. No statistical differences were found in marginal adaptation [10], retention [32], [33], failure rates [32], and success rates [34] between the experimental and the control groups. There is an agreement in the literature that the addition of CHX as a step to adhesive procedures did not relate to better clinical outcomes.

Similarly to CHX, EDTA inhibits MMPs by chelating the calcium ions and removing the smear layer, which may improve bonding outcomes. This literature review identified four clinical studies that investigated the use of EDTA as a dentin pre-treatment for adhesive procedures. One study identified increased retention rates when EDTA was used prior to one-step self-etch adhesives at 18 months evaluation [11]. However, when the use of EDTA was evaluated along with two-step self-etch bonding agent, no differences in clinical outcomes were found at 18-month assessments [9]. The use of EDTA before glass-ionomer cements also did not show a statistical difference at a 12-month evaluation [35]. When this strategy was evaluated after 36 months, data showed delayed marginal defects compared to non-EDTA conditioned glass-ionomer restoration, as well as compared to one-bottle self-etch protocol [36].

The ethanol wet-bonding technique (EWBT) is described to use 100% alcohol to remove extra water content from dentin, increasing the interaction between bonding agents and dentin, thus improving bond strength and longevity. Three clinical studies investigated this technique as a dentin pre-treatment for adhesive procedures. Comparing the use of EWBT with self-etch, total etch and a hydrophobic bonding agent, the results indicated that the hydrophobic adhesive system had the highest failure rate, while no significant differences were observed among the other groups [20]. A similar evaluation identified no statistical differences among the groups [31]. Another study investigated the EWBT associated to gold-standard adhesive systems on total etch (OptiBond FL) and self-etch (Clearfil SE Bond) technique. The retention rate was 100% at baseline and 6 months for all groups, and 91.67% at 9 and 12 months for the ethanol wet bonding group. No significant differences were observed among the groups [37]. These studies collectively suggest that the addition of an extra-step into the bonding protocol does not find support in the literature regarding clinical outcomes in one-year evaluation.

Also, dentin pre-treatment with epigallocatechin-3-gallate provided no benefit in the clinical performance of the adhesive applied, regardless of the adhesive technique utilized. The retention rate for ER was 97% and SE 94.2% [17]. Another enzymatic agent, the proanthocyanidins, may be even harmful to the bonding performance, producing significantly lower retention rates at higher concentrations [7].

Another attempt to improve clinical outcomes of adhesive procedures is to apply an oxalic acid based desensitizing agent during the bonding technique. Five clinical studies investigated the effect of this approach, treating among 20 patients (90 restorations) and 40 patients (140 restorations), during a evaluation time period between 12 months and 48 months. Only one study found significant reduction in dentin hypersensitive after the use of oxalic-acid agent [38]. All other studies [39], [40], [41], [42] agree that clinical performance of restorations was not improved by the use of oxalic based agents during the bonding protocol.

This scoping review outlines the current understanding of NCCL restorative protocols while pinpointing areas that warrant deeper investigation. By identifying evidence gaps, this study lays the groundwork for refining clinical strategies and improving patient outcomes. Firstly, it highlights the variability in study designs, treatment protocols, and outcome assessments. Surprisingly, patient complaints and satisfaction remained unexplored in the reviewed studies. Secondly, the majority of investigations had relatively short follow-up periods, typically up to one year. Thirdly, some studies lacked clear diagnoses of NCCLs, particularly concerning their extent and severity, which compromise the assessment of complexity of the cavity and the longevity. Lastly, the majority of participants were under 60 years old, raising questions about the generalizability of findings to older populations. Given these concerns regarding evidence heterogeneity, future research should aim to elucidate the factors influencing the effectiveness of NCCL restorations and refine treatment approaches accordingly.Top of Form

Another important consideration when interpreting the findings of this review is the heterogeneity of restorative materials used across the included studies. While this review focus was the effect of dentin pre-treatment protocols, the studies evaluated restorations performed with different materials, which rely on distinct adhesion mechanisms. Consequently, the effect of a given dentin pre-treatment may vary according to the restorative material employed. For example, EDTA conditioning was investigated in studies using both self-etch adhesive systems and glass ionomer-based restorations, with some reports suggesting improved retention or delayed marginal deterioration, whereas other studies found no significant differences [9], [11], [35], [36]. Because the number of studies for each pre-treatment/material combination was limited, and because the objective of a scoping review is to map available evidence rather than quantitatively compare interventions, results were synthesized descriptively according to the pre-treatment strategy. Therefore, conclusions regarding the effectiveness of dentin pre-treatments should be interpreted with caution, as the observed outcomes may have been influenced not only by the surface treatment itself but also by the restorative material and adhesive strategy used in each study.

Even though this scoping review was conducted according to the scoping review methodology, some limitations are worth noting. First, scoping reviews aim to provide a broad overview of the available evidence on a specific topic, which may lead to a superficial analysis of individual studies. Second, instead of conducting a meta-analysis or quantitative data synthesis, it was emphasizing descriptive analysis and mapping of the available evidence. This may limit the ability to draw solid conclusions or make conclusive recommendations based on the review's findings. A final limitation is that this field is advancing rapidly, so it is important to acknowledge that this scoping review is a snapshot at a particular time.

Conclusions

Taking into consideration the limitations of this study, according to the data, this scoping review found that while various dentin pre-treatments have been proposed to enhance the longevity of NCCL restorations, the available evidence is limited and highly heterogeneous, and does not allow for definitive conclusions regarding their clinical effectiveness in a consistent or generalizable manner across studies. Standardized protocols and longer-term multicenter RCTs are needed to clarify their clinical relevance and to strengthen the evidence base by addressing existing gaps in study design, outcomes, and follow-up duration.
