AutoCumulus: an automated mammographic density measure created using artificial intelligence
Authors: Osamah Al-qershi, Tuong L Nguyen, Michael S Elliott, Daniel F Schmidt, Enes Makalic, Shuai Li, Samantha K Fox, James G Dowty, Carlos A Peña-Solorzano, Chun Fung Kwok, Yuanhong Chen, Chong Wang, Jocelyn Lippey, Peter Brotchie, Gustavo Carneiro, Davis J McCarthy, Yeojin Jeong, Joohon Sung, Helen M L Frazer, John L Hopper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMammographic (or breast) density is an established risk factor for breast cancer, previously measured using a variety of quantitative, semi-automated and automated approaches. We present a new automated measure, AutoCumulus, learned from applying deep learning to semi-automated measures.MethodsWe studied the mammograms of 9,057 population-screened women in the BRAIx program for which semi-automated measurements of mammographic density had been made by experienced readers using the CUMULUS software. The dataset was split into training, testing, and validation sets (80%, 10%, and 10%, respectively). We applied a deep learning regression model (fine-tuned ConvNeXtSmall) to estimate percentage density and assessed performance by the correlation between estimated and measured percent density using the testing dataset. The automated measure was independently tested using the CSAW-CC dataset in which density was measured using the LIBRA software by comparing measures for the left and right breasts, and the specificity for high sensitivity and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for interval cancers.ResultsThe correlation in percent density between the automated and human measures was 0.95. Based on the CSAW-CC dataset, AutoCumulus outperformed LIBRA in terms of the correlation between the left and right breast (0.95 versus 0.79; P < 0.001), specificity for 95% sensitivity (13% versus 10%; P < 0.001 using McNemar’s test), and AUC (0.638 versus 0.597; P < 0.01 using DeLong’s test) for interval cancers.ConclusionWe have created an automated measure of mammographic density that is highly accurate and allows rapid measurement of large numbers of mammograms. Compared with a well-established automated measure, AutoCumulus showed higher within-woman repeatability and modestly better prediction of interval cancers. These findings support its potential value as a scalable risk indicator, although further validation is required before clinical implementation.Keywords: Breast cancer, Breast density, Deep learning, Machine learning

Background

Breast cancer is a prevalent and potentially life-threatening disease that affects millions of women worldwide [1]. It is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women [2]. Timely detection and treatment can improve outcomes [3], and many developed countries have implemented large-scale mammography screening programs at least for women over the age of 50 years [4]. Digital mammography is now commonly used for early detection. Mammographic (or breast) density is defined in terms of the regions on a mammogram that are “white or bright”, as distinct from dark, that are considered to represent glandular and connective tissue rather than fatty tissue. Mammographic density is measured from mammograms and cannot be measured by a physical exam.

Mammographic density poses challenges for breast cancer screening programs because the dense breast tissues are sometimes similar to cancerous tissue, making it more challenging to detect tumors during routine screening [5]. Women with greater dense area on their mammograms are at greater risk of having breast cancers diagnosed before their next regular screen (‘interval’ cancers). They might benefit from additional imaging tests, such as ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), to improve early detection and accurate diagnosis [6]. Fortunately, mammographic density decreases with increasing age and with increasing body size (such as body mass index (BMI) or breast size), making screening more effective for older women.

Many studies have found that mammographic density also predicts the future risk of being diagnosed with breast cancer at future regular screens. This appears to be paradoxical given that both age and BMI are associated with increasing breast cancer risk for screening-aged women. The strengths of these associations increase after adjusting for age and BMI, irrespective of how density was measured [7–9]. Either way, the risk association is stronger for ‘interval’ cancers diagnosed in the next two or three years than for screen-detected cancers [10], which is thought to be at least in part due to the role of masking existing breast cancers mentioned above. There are a variety of quantitative, semi-automated and automated approaches to measuring mammographic density. One qualitative approach is the BI-RADS density categorization, in which the radiologist records mammographic density to one of four groups: fatty (A), scattered fibroglandular (B), heterogeneously dense (C), or extremely dense (D).

Boyd and Yaffe developed the computer program CUMULUS as a semi-automated way to segment regions and measure the area covered by dense regions (dense area) as well as the total breast area [11]. The human operator uses a slider to delineate what they consider dense regions, as well as the outline of the breast. The percent mammographic density is then calculated as the dense area/total breast area as a percentage. More recently, automated methods for measuring mammographic density have been developed using artificial intelligence (AI) approaches. For example, the freely available Laboratory for Individualized Breast Biodensity Assessment (LIBRA) software package [12] used an adaptive multiclass fuzzy c-means algorithm to identify and partition the mammographic breast tissue area into multiple regions of similar intensity, which were then aggregated by a support-vector machine classifier to obtain a measure of percent density. The VOLPARA software, a commercially available software based on a physics-based model [13], employs segmentation algorithms and then uses segmented images to calculate breast density metrics based on the proportion of dense tissue relative to the total breast area. It provides quantitative measurements such as volumetric breast density (VBD) and percentage of breast density (PD). The LIBRA measure had a correlation of 0.77–0.84 with the CUMULUS measures and 0.85–0.90 with the VOLPARA measures [13].

Kallenberg and colleagues used a convolutional sparse autoencoder to learn features from manually segmented dense areas created by a radiologist [14]. The correlation in percent density between their algorithm and the manual measure was 0.85. The algorithm’s segmentation performance was deemed suboptimal given that the Dice coefficient, averaged across images, was only 0.63.m Lee and Nishikawa used the VGG16 network to segment the breast and dense fibroglandular areas [15]. The correlations between these measures and the LIBRA measurements ranged from 0.6 to 0.7. Other approaches to measuring density used applied conditional generative adversarial networks (cGANs) [16] and simple pulse coupled neural networks (SPCNNs) [17].

Most recently, Jeong and colleagues studied Korean women and applied a deep learning approach for estimating percent breast density from semi-automated measures using the CUMULUS software by using an enhanced attention U-Net architecture for segmentation [18]. The correlation between the automated and semi-automated measures was 0.96. After adjusting for age and total breast area, the AUC for automated percent breast density as a predictor of breast cancer for Korean women was 0.63. Here we report on how we created AutoCumulus, a novel and fully automated approach for estimating mammographic density, as measured using the CUMULUS software, by applying deep learning to a dataset of CUMULUS measures. We evaluated its performance against a publicly available algorithm using a large independent case–control dataset.

Methods

Dataset

The dataset consists of 9,057 mammograms selected from the BRAIx program’s ADMANI dataset [19], which contains more than 4 million mammograms from 630,000 women. This population-based collection supports AI development for improved breast cancer detection and risk-based screening in Australia. The selected mammograms were obtained from 6,781 women, comprising of 1,381 women with breast cancer and 5,400 without breast cancer frequency matched for 4:1 for age. For cases, only the mammogram contralateral to the affected breast was used. For controls, some women are represented by a single CC-view image, whereas others are represented by two CC-view images. Following convention, mammograms were craniocaudal (CC) views to avoid the problem of the pectoral muscle in mediolateral oblique (MLO) views. In MLO images the pectoral muscle often appears as a high-density region overlapping with breast fibroglandular tissue [20]. Its inclusion can bias estimates of breast density (total & percent density) by artificially increasing the “dense” area and altering breast boundaries, which may reduce accuracy in quantitative measurements and risk prediction. All images were generated using machines from the same manufacturer.

Semi-automatic mammographic density measures

Mammographic density was measured for all images by an experienced measurer, who was blinded to all identifying details, using the computer-assisted thresholding technique Cumulus (Imaging Research Program, Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre, University of Toronto, Toronto, Canada) (CUMULUS) software, as in previous studies [21]. By moving a slider to create a threshold that defines the outer limits of the breast image, or the pixel brightness region, the CUMULUS software automatically identifies the areas above and below that threshold by drawing a contour line; for example, the green line in Fig. 1 outlines the dense regions.

An example of a dense area (the area within the green regions) and PD measured using the CUMULUS software, with the breast area indicated by the red line (the white areas within the green region are regions of higher density)

CUMULUS therefore provides direct measurements of the total breast area and the area occupied by mammographically dense tissue, as assessed by the observer. The percentage density is calculated by dividing the dense area by the total breast area.

To construct and assess the AI-trained model, we partitioned the 9,057 images by assigning 7,278, 888, and 891 mammograms exclusively to the training, validation, and testing subsets, representing 80%, 10%, and 10% of the complete dataset, respectively. The validation subset is used during training to tune hyperparameters and monitor the model’s performance, helping to prevent overfitting, while the testing is used after training and validation to evaluate the model’s final performance and generalization to new, unseen data. The three subsets were created randomly while maintaining the same proportion of age group and affected-to-unaffected ratios in each dataset, using a subject-level split to ensure that all images from the same woman were assigned exclusively to a single subset.

Deep learning regression model

We used a deep learning regression model to generate automated estimates of percent density from semi-automated measures obtained using the CUMULUS software, with the input being the images and the output being percent density. We used the pretrained ConvNeXtSmall [22] network as the backbone of our model. ConvNeXtSmall is a CNN architecture that boasts state-of-the-art performance in image classification, surpassing the accuracy of established models such as ResNets. After the convolutional layers, a global average pooling layer was applied, followed by a densely connected layer (32 units) with rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation and L1/L2 regularization for capturing intricate features. The final regression neuron used a sigmoid activation, constraining predictions to the range [0,1]. This approach learns hierarchical features from images while mitigating overfitting through regularization techniques and leveraging knowledge from ImageNet.

Training

The mammographic images typically had dimensions of approximately 4000 × 3000 pixels and were of 16-bit depth, which would necessitate substantial computational resources for model training. To address such computational demands and dimensionality challenges, we employed a preprocessing step in which all images were resized to a standardized dimension of 1024 × 768 pixels while maintaining the original aspect ratio. Additionally, the pixel intensity values were normalized to the [0,1] range, facilitating more efficient model training while preserving the essential information encapsulated in the images.

Because all images were acquired from the same manufacturer and followed a consistent acquisition protocol, a lightweight and reproducible pre-processing pipeline was used. First, images were converted to floating point and intensity normalised to the range [0,1] by dividing each image by its maximum pixel value (i.e., ). Breast masking was then performed using global intensity thresholding to obtain a binary foreground mask. To remove background regions and non-breast structures (e.g., labels/markers/artifacts), connected-component analysis was applied to the binary mask and only the largest connected component was retained as the breast region; all remaining components were discarded. The final breast mask was applied to the original image to suppress non-breast pixels, and the image was cropped to the tight bounding box of the retained component to minimise background. Any remaining non-breast areas outside the mask were set to zero and excluded from subsequent density estimation.

The proposed model was trained using the adaptive moment estimation (Adam) optimizer, employing a learning rate of 0.0001, with learning rate schedule followed exponential decay rate of 0.90, and decay steps at 200 intervals. The training process utilized a batch size of 8, incorporating image augmentation techniques such as random horizontal and vertical flipping, along with a 5% brightness adjustment. Vertical flipping was included to improve robustness to small variations in positioning and minor differences in acquisition orientation; importantly, our primary objective is breast density estimation (a global tissue characteristic) rather than lesion localisation, and all augmentations were applied only during training. The mean squared error (MSE) was employed as the loss function, and the training process was iterated for a maximum of 30 epochs to fine-tune the model’s parameters. Early stopping was applied with a patience of 5 epochs based on validation loss, and the model checkpoint with the lowest validation loss was retained as the final model used for prediction.

The model was constructed using Python 3.10.4 and TensorFlow 2.11.0, leveraging the computational power of two Nvidia A100 GPUs for all training and testing processes. We call the measure based on the final model AutoCumulus.

Statistical testing and model performance

We compared the AutoCumulus measures (predicted measures) with the original Cumulus measures (actual measures) by plotting them against each other and calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient for the testing subset (standard deviations were < 0.03). Additionally, we created a Bland–Altman plot, also known as a Tukey mean difference plot. In this plot, we plotted the difference between the two measures as a function of their mean and included the limits of agreement, defined as the mean difference plus and minus 2 standard deviations of the difference in the mean.

We conducted analyses that compared measures for the left and right breast of the same woman using the independent CSAW-CC dataset risk [23], which includes four images (MLO and CC views for both the left and right breasts) for a total of 24,694 women, comprising 1,868 women with cancer (screen detected and interval cancer) and 22,868 women without breast cancer. We exclusively utilized CC view images. These images had independently been measured for percent density using LIBRA software. Both our internal dataset and the CSAW-CC dataset were acquired using mammography systems from the same manufacturer. We could not use VOLPARA, as it requires raw images that were not available to us.

Using the CSAW-CC dataset, we assessed the relative performances of the AutoCumulus and LIBRA measures by comparing the percent density of left and right breast images for 1,826 women with and 22,868 women without breast cancer. We compared predictions of interval breast cancer risk using AUC for 267 women with and 22,868 women without an interval breast cancer. Results

The correlation between the model’s estimated and automated percent density and the actual percent density determined by humans using a semi-automated approach was 0.95 for the testing subset, as illustrated in Fig. 3A. The Bland–Altman plot shown in Fig. 3B demonstrates minimal average differences between the measures, with a narrow limit of agreement (mean ± 1.96 standard deviation) of ± 0.04 (± 4 percentage points). The mean bias was 0.003 (95% CI: 0.001 to 0.005), indicating a slight systematic overestimation by the predicted values. The 95% limits of agreement ranged from −0.033 (95% CI: −0.035 to −0.032) to + 0.039 (95% CI: 0.038 to 0.042), suggesting that 95% of predictions fall within approximately 3 to 4 percentage points of the actual values. Additionally, the relationship between the mean and the limit of agreement remained constant, except in the upper tail with percent densities higher than 0.3 (30%).

A regression of the differences on the mean showed evidence of proportional bias (Difference = − 0.0077 + 0.0480 × Mean; slope 0.0480, standard error 0.0106, p < 0.001), although the effect size was small (R2 = 0.0226). This density-dependence was further summarised by deciles of the mean percent density as shown Fig. 3C. Mean differences were near zero across most deciles, with a tendency toward higher positive differences in the upper deciles and wider dispersion (± 1.96 SD) at higher density levels, consistent with slightly increased error and variability at the upper end of the density range. This indicates that the model is well-calibrated on average but exhibits a small density-dependent drift and greater variability at the upper end.

Given that we did not conduct the labour-intensive semi-automated CUMULUS measurements for the CSAW-CC dataset, we evaluated the repeatability of AutoCumulus by comparing the percent density of the right and left breasts of the same woman. Based on the literature and our own experiences, at least for controls the measurements for the left and right breasts should be highly correlated. A high correlation between the left and right breasts would demonstrates the model’s insensitivity to differences between the mammograms of the left and right breasts due to positioning.

Applied to the CSAW-CC dataset, the correlation between the percent density values for the left and right breasts was 0.946 (95% CI 0.945 to 0.948) when using AutoCumulus, greater than 0.79 (95% CI 0.789 to 0.799) when using LIBRA software (P < 0.001); see Fig. 4A and B.

Discussion

We have demonstrated how a highly accurate automated algorithm for measuring mammographic density can be learnt by applying AI to human-derived measures made using a semi-automated approach. This allows for rapid measurement of thousands of mammograms (1,000 per minute) and makes it possible to create measures for population screening programs and for large-scale research studies not previously possible [24]. Our approach was replicated using an independent dataset from which we found that our automated measure, AutoCumulus, was more repeatable (i.e. more strongly correlated between a woman’s left and right breasts) and better predicted interval cancers than the freely available and widely used automated tool LIBRA.

There is a well-established association between mammographic density and the risk of interval cancers that are diagnosed between regular mammographic screens [25, 26], and there is a wide concern about ‘dense breasts’ per se [27]. However, the measurement of density has been problematic, and clinical practice has generally relied on the pathologists’ interpretation without any formal quality control; the risk-predicting performance of the BI-RADS categorisations when used in practice by multiple readers across a period of time is far less than that found by controlled research studies utilizing one or a few radiologists [28]. The specialist semi-automated measure, CUMULUS, is used in many research papers but does not appear to be used in clinical practice. Automated measures such as VOLPARA and LIBRA have been developed and are being used in clinical and research settings.

The strengths of this study include its replication on both internal and external datasets, comparison with an established tool, and large sample sizes and highly significant findings. A potential limitation is the use of one experienced measurer, so using a range of measurers could further enhance accuracy and broaden applicability. Another is the focus on CC views, so use of MLO views could be beneficial. Other machines should also be studied to broaden clinical reach.

In summary, AutoCumulus provides a fast, scalable, and objective approach for measuring mammographic density. In the present study, it showed higher repeatability and better interval cancer prediction than LIBRA. However, the improvement in interval cancer prediction was modest in absolute terms, and these findings should therefore be interpreted as evidence of comparative utility rather than evidence that AutoCumulus is ready to be used as a standalone clinical decision tool. AutoCumulus may be useful as a scalable risk indicator for research and for future risk-stratified screening models, particularly when combined with other clinical, imaging, and epidemiological risk factors. Further validation across different imaging systems, mammographic views, populations, and prospective screening settings is required before its role in clinical practice or population screening programmes can be fully established.

Conclusion

We developed AutoCumulus, a deep learning–based automated measure of mammographic density trained on semi-automated CUMULUS measures. AutoCumulus demonstrated high agreement with human-derived measures, while providing substantially faster processing than semi-automated CUMULUS, which requires reader interaction. It also showed higher reproducibility than the widely used LIBRA software and stronger prediction of interval cancers than LIBRA, although the absolute predictive performance was modest.

These findings suggest that AutoCumulus is a promising, rapid, and scalable mammographic density measure that may support large population-based studies and contribute to future risk-stratified screening approaches. However, it should not be considered a standalone clinical tool at this stage. Further validation across imaging platforms, mammographic views, and diverse screening populations, as well as evaluation in combination with other risk factors, is needed before its clinical utility and impact on screening programmes can be determined.
