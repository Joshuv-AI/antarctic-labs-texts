The impact of emotional labor on the mental health of vocational college teachers in China: the mediating role of occupational identity and the moderating role of teacher-student relationships
Authors: Mengyuan Sui, Mingxing Zhou, Yang Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUsing a sample of 912 vocational college teachers in China, this study empirically examines the impact of emotional labor, such as surface acting, expression of naturally felt emotion, and deep acting, on the mental health of Chinese vocational college teachers. Robustness tests were conducted by replacing explanatory variables and research methods to explore the mediating effect of vocational identity on the relationship between emotional labor and mental health among Chinese vocational college teachers, as well as the moderating role of teacher-student relationships in this relationship. Research findings indicate that surface acting exhibits a significant positive correlation with mental health issues among vocational school teachers, while expression of naturally felt emotion and deep acting demonstrate a significant negative correlation. Heterogeneity analysis reveals that surface acting positively impacts the mental health of female teachers, those aged 35 and above, teachers with over 10 years of teaching experience, and high-income vocational school teachers. Conversely, natural acting and deep acting exert a positive influence on the mental health of male teachers, those aged 35 and above, teachers with over 10 years of experience, and low-income teachers. Mediation analysis indicates that occupational identity exerts a significant mediating effect on the relationship between emotional labor and the mental health of vocational school teachers. Moderation analysis reveals that teacher-student relationships exert a significant inhibitory effect on the relationship between surface acting, expression of naturally felt emotion, and deep acting and the mental health issues of vocational school teachers. These findings provide robust evidence and important practical implications for effectively alleviating mental health challenges among vocational college faculty.Keywords: Emotional labour, Vocational college teachers, Mental health, Occupational identity, Teacher-student relationship

Introduction

Vocational education, a crucial component of China’s higher education system, is responsible for producing qualified workers to meet shifts in social and economic development. To encourage the integration of vocational education into the general public, the fusion of industry and education, the fusion of science and education, and the optimization of the positioning of the various types of vocational education, the Twentieth Party Congress report suggests coordinating the collaborative innovation of vocational education, higher education, and much-needed education. According to the Twentieth Party Congress report, China plans to significantly raise the standards of vocational education. High-talent development must be the foundation for the high-quality advancement of vocational education. The teacher is the primary determinant of the caliber of talent development. Teachers have high emotional demands, must put in a lot of emotional labor, and are prone to psychological issues. Their mental health issues have a significant effect on their motivation for their work, and improving the quality of instruction in vocational colleges has become crucial to the advancement of high-quality vocational education. Improving the mental health of instructors in technical colleges has become essential to advancing vocational education. As a review of the literature reveals, emotional labor directly or indirectly impacts teachers’ psychological issues. Exploring the effects of emotional labor on the mental health of vocational college teachers in China not only disrupts the normal teaching activities of these educators but also compromises teaching quality and may even adversely affect the mental health of vocational college faculty.

Therefore, this study empirically examined the relationship between emotional labor and mental health among vocational college teachers in China. It validated the heterogeneity among teachers of different ages, genders, monthly incomes, and years of teaching experience. It explored the mediating effect of occupational identity and the mediation influence of teacher-student relationships. The specific layout is as described below: the second part is the literature review and theoretical foundation of this study; the third part is the material and methodology of this study; the fourth part is the baseline regression analysis, heterogeneity analysis, mediation analysis and moderation analysis of the effect of emotional labour on the mental health of teachers in vocational colleges; the fifth part is the discussion related to the study; and the sixth part is the conclusions of the study.

Literature review and theoretical foundations

Research on emotional labour and mental health among vocational college teachers

Emotional labour could be essential in influencing and mitigating teachers’ mental health. Emotional labour is the quiet work of eliciting and suppressing emotions in oneself and others. The management of emotions to create a facial and physical performance that meets the public’s expectations (service users, organisations, systems, etc [4]. According to individual performance characteristics, some researchers classify emotional labour as a specific type of emotional regulation, i.e., an emotional labour strategy, which can be divided into two categories: surface acting and deep acting [12]. The surface acting refers to the individual’s feelings, and public expectations are inconsistent; its show of emotions by public expectations, but the individual’s internal emotions did not change, is a kind of ‘pretend’ emotional expression; deep acting refers to the individual’s internal feelings and public expectations of emotional expression is inconsistent, the individual through internal adjustment to make their real emotions and the need to show emotions in line with the public expectations [12]. Deep acting means that when the individual’s inner feelings are not consistent with the public’s expected emotional expression, the individual adjusts internally to make his/her real emotion consistent with the emotion that needs to be expressed [12].

In recent years, Diefendorff and other scholars have combined the original theoretical framework [9] to propose a third type of emotional labour strategy, which is the expression of naturally felt emotion, meaning that individuals do not need to feel deliberate, and the emotions displayed are the same as the emotions expected by the public, which is a natural emotional expression strategy [47, 3], and also the ‘passive depth play’ of real emotions [21]. As scholars have classified the types of emotional labour differently, the empirical research models for measuring emotional labour are also diverse. Based on the classical classification of emotional labour strategies, Grundy reorganised and integrated previous research models and proposed a research model of emotional labour with situational cues as antecedents and teachers’ work behaviours and emotional experiences as consequences, which mainly includes sub-dimensions such as job satisfaction [1], job stress and tendency to leave the company [12]. Other scholars, on the other hand, have drawn on the three-dimensional theory of emotional labour proposed by Dieffendorf and constructed an analytical framework [50]. Since then, more researchers have examined the impact of emotional labour on mental health through its three dimensions. Studies show significant differences in mental health among those using various emotional labour strategies [5, 37, 2, 3]. Some have found that factors like organisational caring [22, 21],organisational commitment [50], work-family conflict [47], and psychological capital [15] can mediate the relationship between teachers’ emotional labour strategies and mental health.

Research on the relationship between emotional labour and occupational identity among vocational college teachers

There is a strong correlation between teachers’ occupational identity and emotional labour. Teachers’ emotional labour shapes their occupational identity. First, teachers’ emotional labour affects teachers’ occupational identity. Teachers experience different emotions at different stages of their development, and positive emotions such as happiness, pride, and love enhance their occupational identity [6, 28] while negative emotions such as sadness, fear, and anger reduce their occupational identity [6, 48] or push them to revise their original occupational identity [28]. However, the above studies are qualitative analyses or cross-sectional surveys and do not provide causal evidence. Although Jiang et al. introduced longitudinal tracking, it was limited to case analysis and did not reveal the causal association between teachers’ emotions and occupational identity [18].

Secondly, teachers’ occupational identity may also influence their emotions reciprocally. First, teachers with high occupational identity choose this career primarily out of a genuine passion for education [10], potentially investing greater enthusiasm in teaching and learning [33], which in turn affects their emotional experiences. Teachers with strong occupational identity perceive themselves as professionals with an educational mission rather than mere employees executing others’ ideas. Consequently, they experience less intense emotional fluctuations when facing challenges such as educational reforms [43]. Finally, occupational identity influences teachers’ mental health. Teachers with high occupational identity regulate their emotions more effectively, aligning their outward emotional expressions with their inner feelings. This leads to more positive emotional experiences and improved mental health. Conversely, some teachers may question their career choice during the uncertainty of early professional stages. Negative feedback encountered during this period can lead to feelings of disappointment [36]. The conflict between reality and expectations experienced early in their careers can also undermine their occupational identity, causing tension and adversely affecting their mental health [30].

Teacher-student relationships and emotional labour: relevant research on the mental health of vocational college teachers

The teacher-student relationship is a psychological relationship between teachers and students in schools that takes the form of emotional, cognitive, and behavioural interactions and influences the implementation and conduct of many aspects of education. Good teacher-student relationships are a prerequisite for effective teaching and learning and help teachers motivate and improve students’ learning behaviours and attitudes, which, in turn, indirectly affect teachers’ mental health [14]. In addition, good teacher-student relationships can influence teachers’ emotional labour, helping them perceive and express their emotions more accurately, thus avoiding the emotional internal conflict that occurs when they maintain relationships, and helping ensure they are enthusiastic about their work [29]. According to Côté’s [7] social interaction model of emotion regulation, negative emotional interactions lead individuals to feel relational conflict, which increases emotional stress and can lead to psychological problems; conversely, positive emotional interactions provide social support, enhancing self-worth and reducing emotional stress. The teacher-student relationship also directly or indirectly mediates the relationship between emotional labor and mental health. Research indicates that surface acting, as a negative emotion regulation and interaction style, significantly predicts negative teacher-student relationships. In contrast, deep expression of naturally felt emotion, as well as positive emotion regulation and interaction styles, predicts positive teacher-student relationships [38]. As recipients of teachers’ emotional labor, students’ perceptions of the authenticity of teachers’ emotional expressions influence their feedback to teachers. This, in turn, affects teachers’ perceptions of the teacher-student relationship, subsequently impacting their subsequent work behaviors and mental health [41].

According to the research findings, while existing studies have examined the relationship between emotional labor and mental health, research on vocational school teachers remains scarce. Limitations in sample size, diversity, geographical distribution, measurement tools, and cultural context have led to inconsistent results and incomplete explanations for the same issue. Furthermore, relevant studies have yet to incorporate the mediating role of occupational identity or the moderating effect of teacher-student relationships. This study concludes that emotional labor significantly impacts the mental health of vocational school teachers, with occupational identity serving as a mediating factor and teacher-student relationships as a moderating factor. Specifically, occupational identity mediates the relationship between emotional labor and teachers’ mental health across surface acting, expression of naturally felt emotion, and deep acting.

Furthermore, teacher-student relationships significantly mitigate the effect of emotional labor on teachers’ mental health. Accordingly, this study focuses on vocational college teachers in China and addresses four questions: First, does emotional labor affect the mental health of vocational college teachers? Second, does emotional labor exhibit heterogeneity in its effects on the mental health of vocational college teachers with different characteristics? Third, does occupational identity mediate the relationship between emotional labor and the mental health of vocational college teachers? Finally, does the teacher-student relationship moderate the relationship between emotional labor and the mental health of vocational college teachers? Materials and methods

Data sources

This study employed a cluster sampling method to survey teachers from 10 vocational colleges across three provinces in China: Hunan, Jiangxi, and Chongqing. The survey was divided into three points (each two months apart) to reduce the impact of common methodological bias and ensure the study’s validity. Demographic information and Emotional Labour Scale data were collected for the first time (September 2023), Mental Health Scale data were collected for the second time (November 2023), and Occupational identity Scale data were collected for the third time along with Teacher-Student Relationship Scale data (January 2024), resulting in the acquisition of 912 valid questionnaires, with an overall questionnaire validity rate of 91.2%.

Among them, there are 419 male teachers, accounting for 45.9 per cent, and 493 female teachers, accounting for 54 per cent; 403 teachers of science and engineering, accounting for 44.2 per cent, and 509 teachers of literature and history, accounting for 55.8 per cent; 189 teachers of high grade, accounting for 20.7 per cent, 236 teachers of deputy grade, accounting for 25.9 per cent, 312 teachers of intermediate grade, accounting for 34.2 per cent, and 175 teachers of junior grade, accounting for 19.2 per cent; 198 teachers with less than 5 years of teaching experience, accounting for 21.7 per cent, 6–10 years of teaching experience 287 people, accounting for 31.5 per cent, 11–15 years of teaching experience 239 people, accounting for 26.2 per cent, 16–20 years of teaching experience 188 people, accounting for 20.6 per cent; 173 people earned less than 5,000 yuan, accounting for 19%, 336 people earned between 5,000 and 8,000 yuan, accounting for 36.8%, 274 people earned between 8,000 and 11,000 yuan, accounting for 30%, 129 people earned more than 11,000 yuan, accounting for 14.2%. Under 30 years old there were 132 people, accounting for 14.5%, 30–35 years old there were 179 people, accounting for 19.6%; 225 individuals aged 36–40, accounting for 24.7%, 203 aged 41–45, accounting for 22.3%, 101 aged 46–50, accounting for 11.1%, and 72 aged 51 and above, accounting for 7.8%.

Variable design

Explanatory variable

The explanatory variable in this study is emotional labor. In this research, the Emotional Labour Questionnaire was adopted from the Emotional Labour Questionnaire for Primary and Secondary School Teachers developed by Yin Hong [46], which consists of 15 questions divided into three dimensions: surface acting, deep acting, and expression of naturally felt emotion. A 5-point Likert scale was employed, with responses falling between ‘never’ and ‘always’ on a range of 1–5. Higher scores indicate that vocational school teachers engage in this type of emotional labor more frequently. The internal consistency coefficients for the three dimensions were 0.852, 0.896, and 0.912, and the F-test results were significant (P < 0.01), indicating that the questionnaire is highly reliable. The total Cronbach’s alpha for the scale was 0.889, representing a satisfactory level of validity for the questionnaire.

Dependent variable

The dependent variable of interest in this study is mental health. The mental health scale adopts the three dimensions of anxiety and depression somatization in the revised Clinical Symptom Self-Assessment Scale (CSAS) introduced by Bian Cuidong [8]. Relevant items were optimized to better align with the practice of vocational college teachers, resulting in a 25-item scale. A five-point Likert scale is used, ranging from ‘no’ to ‘serious’, scored as 1 to 5 points. Higher scores indicate poorer mental health among vocational college teachers. The three dimensions exhibited satisfactory internal consistency, with coefficients of 0.910, 0.952, and 0.923, respectively. The overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale was 0.902, indicating good reliability and validity.

Mediating variable

The study’s mediating variable is teachers’ occupational identity, measured using the Occupational Identity Scale developed by Sun Mengmeng [26], comprising 12 items across five dimensions: professional will and expectation, career will, career value, and career effectiveness. More professional identification was indicated by higher scores on a five-point Likert scale, which ranged from 1 to 5, from ‘disagree’ to ‘totally agree’. The Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient for the Occupational Identity Scale is 0.889, indicating good validity, and the internal consistency coefficient is 0.872, indicating reliability.

Moderate variables

The Pianta Teacher-Student Relationship Questionnaire, revised by Wang [42], was used in this study. Only the teacher-student intimacy dimension of the original questionnaire was used in this study, which consisted of 11 items and was scored on a five-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 1 to 5, from ‘not at all’ to ‘completely’, and the higher the score, the more intimate the teacher-student relationship. The higher the score, the closer the teacher-student relationship. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of this dimension scale was 0.856, which demonstrated that the reliability of the questionnaire was good, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.871, which demonstrated that the validity of the questionnaire was good.

Control variables

Considering that teachers in vocational colleges can be affected by other kinds of statistics, some control variables are chosen in this study to ensure the reliability of the research results. In conjunction with previous studies, demographic variables represented by gender, age, marital status, education background, and monthly income were selected [27, 31, 51]and work situation represented by years of teaching experience, professional category, and title were selected as control variables [25, 39]. Robustness tests

The current study employed two techniques, measure substitution and explanatory variable substitution, to test the robustness of the baseline regression results. Its purpose was to investigate further the impact of emotional labour on teachers’ mental health in vocational colleges [35]. Firstly, the explanatory variables in the present study can be treated as multicategorical. Therefore, the probit model was used as a substitution measure to test the robustness of the baseline regression results. Second, the explanatory variable of this study, teachers’ mental health, can be classified into three dimensions: anxiety, depression, and somatization. This study replaces these three dimensions with the variable “mental health” for the robustness test. Table 3 shows that surface acting significantly increases vocational college teachers’ anxiety, depression, and somatization (at the 1% level). In contrast, expression of naturally felt emotion and deep acting significantly reduce these issues (also at the 1% level). Frequent surface acting undermines teachers’ mental health, while frequent expression of naturally felt emotion and deep acting enhance it. This matches the benchmark regression results, confirming the robustness of the research.

Heterogeneity analysis

The above studies discussed the impact of emotional labour on teachers’ mental health in vocational colleges. However, they did explore differences in the impact of teachers’ personal and work characteristics, including heterogeneity by age and gender. Therefore, this study will delve into the heterogeneity of dimensions [45, 17] of the impact of emotional labour on teachers’ mental health in vocational colleges in terms of age, gender, monthly income, and years of teaching experience.

As Table 4 demonstrates, surface acting exerts a significant positive influence across all age groups. However, its positive impact on the mental health of vocational college teachers aged 35 and above is greater than that observed in teachers under 35, indicating that surface acting poses a greater threat to the mental health of those aged 35 and above. Both the expression of naturally felt emotion and deep acting exert a significant negative influence across all age groups. The negative impact coefficients for vocational college teachers aged 35 and above are greater than those for younger teachers, indicating that expression of naturally felt emotion and deep acting pose a greater threat to the mental health of younger vocational college teachers [49].

At the level of teaching experience, expression of naturally felt emotion and deep acting have significant effects on the mental health of vocational college teachers of different teaching ages. Surface acting exhibits a higher positive coefficient for teachers with over 10 years’ tenure, indicating that surface acting poses a greater threat to their mental health. Both the expression of naturally felt emotion and deep acting exert significant negative effects on teachers’ mental health, with the expression of naturally felt emotion showing a stronger negative coefficient among those with over 10 years’ tenure. Deep acting exhibits a stronger negative impact coefficient for vocational college teachers with less than 10 years of teaching experience. This indicates that the more frequently expressed naturally felt emotion, the better the mental health of teachers with over 10 years’ experience compared to those with less than 10 years. Conversely, the more frequent deep acting occurs, the better the mental health levels of teachers with less than 10 years’ experience compared to those with over 10 years. The explanation is that the more extended teachers in Chinese vocational colleges are, the more frequently they engage in emotional labour at work. The frequent surface acting is more likely to produce anxiety and depression, which in turn jeopardise their mental health. In contrast, the frequent expression of naturally felt emotions will help them to adjust their state of mind and improve their mental health [23, 19].

Discusion

Conclusions of the study

According to the baseline regression analysis, surface acting significantly and positively influenced vocational college teachers’ mental health scores. At the same time, expression of naturally felt emotion and deep acting significantly negatively influenced these scores. Specifically, higher surface acting scores correlated with poorer mental health, whereas higher expression of naturally felt emotion and deep acting scores correlated with better mental health. This conclusion validates the relationship between surface acting, expression of naturally felt emotion, and deep acting within emotional labor and vocational college teachers, consistent with findings among university faculty and healthcare practitioners, aligning with previous research outcomes [52, 34].

According to the heterogeneity analysis, emotional labour significantly affects the mental health of vocational college teachers of different age groups, genders, and monthly incomes, and teaching ages. In terms of gender, the coefficient of the positive influence of surface actings on females is greater than that of males, which indicates that surface actings are more likely to make females suffer from mental health problems; the coefficient of the negative influence of expression of naturally felt emotions on males is greater than that of females, which indicates that expression of naturally felt emotions are more likely to enhance men’s mental health; the negative influence coefficient of deep acting on men is more significant than that on women, indicating that deep acting are more likely to cause mental health problems in women and that female vocational college teachers are more likely to have mental health problems than male vocational college teachers [13]. Regarding age, surface acting poses a greater risk to the mental health of vocational college teachers aged 35 and above, while expression of naturally felt emotion and deep acting are more likely to harm the mental health of those under 35. This finding aligns with research by Wang Shang and colleagues [40].

Regarding the teaching age, surface acting adversely affects the mental health of teachers with over 10 years’ experience, whereas the more frequently teachers express naturally felt emotion, the better their mental health. Conversely, the more frequent the deep acting, the better the mental health of vocational college teachers with less than 10 years’ experience. This suggests that there is a close relationship between the mental health of teachers in vocational colleges and their years of teaching; the longer the years of teaching, the more frequent the emotional labour and the more likely they are to develop mental health problems [11]. In terms of income, the more frequent the surface actings, the lower the mental health level of high-income teachers, and the more frequent the expression of naturally felt emotions and deep acting, the better the mental health level of low and middle-income teachers, which is also consistent with the study that higher income means more pressure on vocational college teachers, and the more pronounced the surface actings, the more likely to have mental health problems. In contrast, expression of naturally felt emotions and deep acting are conducive to improving the mental health level of low-income teachers [32, 24]. The mediation results show that there is a significant mediation effect of occupational identity in emotional labour and mental health of teachers in Chinese vocational colleges, in the way that the higher the scores of surface actings in emotional labour, the lower the scores of occupational identity of teachers in vocational colleges, the higher the scores of expression of naturally felt emotions and deep acting, the higher the scores of occupational identity of teachers in vocational colleges, and the lower the scores of mental health problems. The higher the scores for expression of naturally felt emotion and deep acting, the higher the occupational identity score for teachers in vocational colleges, and the lower the score for mental health problems. The moderated results showed that surface acting, the expression of naturally felt emotion, and deep acting were positively associated with teachers’ mental health in vocational colleges. Concurrently, the correlation between emotional labour and the teacher-student relationship was found to negatively affect teachers’ mental health in vocational colleges. This suggests that the quality of the teacher-student connection has a notable influence on the relationships among surface acting, the expression of naturally felt emotion, deep acting, and teachers’ mental health in vocational colleges. The teacher-student connection exerts a significant inhibitory effect on the relationships among surface acting, the expression of naturally felt emotion, and deep acting, as well as on the mental health issues faced by educators in vocational colleges.

Policy recommendations

This study suggests the following implications: First, in conjunction with the findings on the effects of emotional labour on the mental health of teachers in vocational colleges, schools should pay attention to the adverse outcomes of emotional labour on the mental health of teachers in vocational colleges, should provide humanistic care and psychological assistance to vocational college, and should incorporate emotional labour into the personnel management of the school, for example, by reflecting emotional labour in the performance appraisal and remuneration [16] Secondly, schools should focus on the mediating role of occupational identity, whereby they should strengthen occupational identity education for teachers in vocational colleges, enhance their professionalism and strengthen their professional ideals and beliefs. The achievements and contributions of teachers in vocational colleges should be valued and recognised. The social value and sense of mission of teachers in vocational colleges should be emphasised to raise their awareness of the social impact of their work and enhance their pride in and love for their work in special education.

Research limitations and perspectives

The following limitations are inherent to this study: First, the study was conducted on vocational college teachers, and the survey sample did not include teachers from vocational colleges in different regions of China but was limited to teachers from 10 vocational colleges in central China, which could subsequently be used to compare the effects of emotional labour on the mental health of teachers from vocational colleges in central, eastern, and western China. Second, emotional labor may exert distinct effects on the mental health of vocational school teachers through different mechanisms. Future research could explore the mediating or moderate roles of various mediating variables or moderating variables in the relationship between emotional labor and the mental health of vocational school teachers, thereby further clarifying the underlying mechanisms of influence.

Conclusion

This study empirically examined the relationship between emotional labor and mental health among vocational college teachers in China. It validated the heterogeneity of this relationship across teachers with different characteristics, ages, genders, monthly incomes, and years of teaching experience. Furthermore, the research explored the mediating effect of occupational identity and the moderate effect of teacher-student relationships.

The study found that surface acting are positively correlated with the mental health of vocational school teachers, while both surface and deep acting are negatively correlated with their mental health. This suggests that vocational school teachers who engage in emotional labor, such as passive surface acting, experience harm to their mental health. In contrast, expressing naturally felt emotions positively and engaging in deep acting can enhance their mental health. Heterogeneity analysis reveals that surface acting positively impacts the mental health of female teachers who are over 35 years old, have over 10 years of teaching experience, and are high-income vocational school teachers. Expression of naturally felt emotion and deep acting have a positive impact on the mental health of male teachers, particularly those over 35 years old with over 10 years of teaching experience and low-income backgrounds. Mediating analysis indicates that emotional labor has a significant mediating effect on the relationship between surface acting, expression of naturally felt emotion, deep acting, and the mental health of vocational school teachers. Moderation analysis indicates that teacher-student relationships exert a significant inhibitory effect on the relationship between surface acting, expression of naturally felt emotion, deep acting, and mental health issues among vocational school teachers.
