Note: Part of the results in this paper were presented at the 2018 EUSIPCO conference [ 1 ] .

Abstract
We study a blind deconvolution problem on graphs, which arises in the context of localizing a few sources that diffuse over networks. While the observations are bilinear functions of the unknown graph filter coefficients and sparse input signals, a mild requirement on invertibility of the diffusion filter enables an efficient convex relaxation leading to a linear programming formulation that can be tackled with off-the-shelf solvers. Under the Bernoulli-Gaussian model for the inputs, we derive sufficient exact recovery conditions in the noise-free setting. A stable recovery result is then established, ensuring the estimation error remains manageable even when the observations are corrupted by a small amount of noise. Numerical tests with synthetic and real-world network data illustrate the merits of the proposed algorithm, its robustness to noise as well as the benefits of leveraging multiple signals to aid the (blind) localization of sources of diffusion. At a fundamental level, the results presented here broaden the scope of classical blind deconvolution of (spatio-)temporal signals to irregular graph domains.

2
Preliminaries and Problem Statement

We briefly review the necessary GSP background to introduce the observation model and formally state the problem.

2.1
Graph signal processing background

Consider a weighted and undirected graph denoted as
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathbf{A}})
, where
{\mathcal{V}}:=\{1,\ldots,N\}
comprises the vertex set. The symmetric graph adjacency matrix
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N\times N}
has entries
A_{ij}=A_{ji}\geq 0
, that represent the weight of the edge
(i,j)
between nodes
i
and
j
. Naturally, if such edge
(i,j)
is not present in
{\mathcal{G}}
then
A_{ij}=0
. We do not allow for self-loops, hence
A_{ii}=0
for all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
. Directed graphs are important
[
32
]
, but beyond the scope of this work.

As a general algebraic descriptor of graph connectivity, we henceforth rely on a symmetric GSO
{\mathbf{S}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
, inheriting and encoding the sparsity pattern of
{\mathcal{G}}

[
33
,
12
]
. That is, the only requirement for an admissible GSO
{\mathbf{S}}
is that
S_{ij}=0
when there is no edge connecting
i
and
j
. Typical choices are
{\mathbf{A}}
itself, the combinatorial graph Laplacian
{\mathbf{L}}={\mathbf{A}}-\textrm{diag}({\mathbf{A}}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N})
, or, their various degree-normalized forms
[
34
,
10
]
that we will adopt for our experiments in Section
5
. Given that
{\mathbf{S}}
is real and symmetric, it is always diagonalizable and we can decompose it as
{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{V}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\textrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})
collects the eigenvalues and
{\mathbf{V}}
’s columns comprise an orthonormal basis of GSO eigenvectors.

Lastly, we define a graph signal
{\mathbf{x}}:{\mathcal{V}}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
as an
N
-dimensional vector, where
x_{i}
denotes the signal value at node
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
. This value could for instance represent the rating user
i
assigns to a particular item (e.g., an article as in the experiments in Section
5.3
), the measurement collected by sensor
i
, or, the neural activity recorded in the
i
-th cortical region-of-interest as defined by some brain parcellation. In GSP, the prevalent view is that graph signal
{\mathbf{x}}
should not be viewed in isolation, but rather as a tuple along with the GSO
{\mathbf{S}}
– the later provides useful contextual (or prior) information about pairwise relationships among individual signal elements in
{\mathbf{x}}
. Next, we elaborate on the operator viewpoint of
{\mathbf{S}}
as a map between graph signals.

2.2
Graph-filter models of network diffusion

Let
{\mathbf{y}}
represent a graph signal supported on
{\mathcal{G}}
, which is assumed to be generated from an initial state
{\mathbf{x}}
through linear network dynamics of the form

\displaystyle\textstyle{\mathbf{y}}\ =\ \alpha_{0}\prod_{l=1}^{\infty}({\mathbf{I}}_{N}-\alpha_{l}{\mathbf{S}}){\mathbf{x}}\ =\ \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\beta_{l}{\mathbf{S}}^{l}{\mathbf{x}}.

(1)

The linear transformation
{\mathbf{S}}
represents one-hop network aggregation (or averaging), so repeated (
l=1,2,\ldots
) applications of the GSO in (
1
) diffuse
{\mathbf{x}}
across
{\mathcal{G}}
. Accordingly,
\{\alpha_{l}\}_{l=0}^{\infty}
and
\{\beta_{l}\}_{l=0}^{\infty}
can be viewed as diffusion coefficients for the multiplicative and additive signal model parametrizations in (
1
), respectively. This generative model for
{\mathbf{y}}
is admittedly simple, but it nonetheless encompasses a broad class of linear network processes such as heat diffusion, average consensus, PageRank, and the DeGroot model of opinion dynamics
[
35
,
36
]
.

The Cayley-Hamilton theorem
[
37
, p.109]
ensures that the infinite series in the right-hand-side of (
1
) can always be equivalently reparametrized using
bounded-degree
polynomialss of
{\mathbf{S}}
. Introducing the coefficient vector
{\mathbf{h}}:=[h_{0},\ldots,h_{L-1}]^{\top}
and the shift-invariant graph filter
[
10
]

\mathbf{H}:=h_{0}{\mathbf{I}}_{N}+h_{1}\mathbf{S}+h_{2}\mathbf{S}^{2}+\ldots+h_{L-1}\mathbf{S}^{L-1}=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}h_{l}\mathbf{S}^{l},

(2)

the signal model (
1
) becomes

{\mathbf{y}}=\left(\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}h_{l}{\mathbf{S}}^{l}\right){\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}},

(3)

for some
{\mathbf{h}}
and
L\leq N
. While graph filters are leveraged here as simple generative mechanisms to describe diffusion processes on networks, these convolutional operators play a central role in GSP and machine learning on graphs; see e.g.,
[
13
]
for a recent tutorial treatment.

Frequency representation.
Graph filters and signals admit representations in the frequency (i.e., graph spectral) domain
[
15
,
10
]
. To this end, recall the GSO eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}
and introduce the
N\times L
Vandermonde matrix
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}
, where
\Psi_{ij}:=\lambda_{i}^{j-1}
and
L
is the order of the graph filter (
2
). The graph Fourier transform (GFT) of a signal
{\mathbf{x}}
and the frequency response of filter
{\mathbf{h}}
are
{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}:={\mathbf{V}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}:=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}{\mathbf{h}}
, respectively. This follows by evaluating the GFT of the filter’s output
{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}
and using the spectral decomposition
{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{V}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}
, to yield

{\tilde{\mathbf{y}}}=\text{diag}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}{\mathbf{h}}\big){\mathbf{V}}^{\top}{\mathbf{x}}=\text{diag}\big({\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}\big){\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}={\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}\circ{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}.

(4)

Apparently, the graph filter
{\mathbf{H}}
is diagonalized by the graph’s spectral basis
{\mathbf{V}}
. As a result
{\tilde{\mathbf{y}}}
is given by the element-wise product
(\circ)
of
{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}
and the filter’s frequency response
{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}:=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}{\mathbf{h}}
, analogous to the convolution theorem for temporal signals.

2.3
Problem formulation

Suppose we observe
P
graph signals that we collect in a matrix
{\mathbf{Y}}=[{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{y}}_{P}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times P}
. For given shift operator
{\mathbf{S}}
and filter order
L
, observations adhere to the (graph convolutional) diffusion model
{\mathbf{Y}}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{X}}
in (
3
), where
{\mathbf{X}}=[{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}_{P}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times P}
is sparse having at most
S\ll N
non-zero entries per column. We do not require that the columns of
{\mathbf{X}}
share a common support. The pragmatic setting whereby observations are corrupted by additive noise will be considered in Section
4.2
.

The goal is to perform blind deconvolution on the graph
{\mathcal{G}}
, which amounts to estimating sparse
{\mathbf{X}}
and the filter coefficients
{\mathbf{h}}
up to scaling and (possibly) permutation ambiguities
[
1
, Sec. IV-A]
. All we are given is
{\mathbf{Y}}
, a forward model in terms of the parameterized filter family in (
2
), and a structural assumption on
{\mathbf{X}}
. Sparsity is well motivated when the signals in
{\mathbf{Y}}
represent diffused versions of a
few
localized sources in
{\mathcal{G}}
, here indexed by
{\mathcal{S}}:=\textrm{supp}({\mathbf{X}})=\{(i,j)\mid X_{ij}\neq 0\}
. From this vantage point, the blind deconvolution task can be viewed as a source localization one, where recovering
{\mathbf{X}}
is of primary interest and
{\mathbf{h}}
becomes a nuisance parameter. In any case, the non-sparse formulation is ill-posed, since the number of unknowns
NP+L
in
\{{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{h}}\}
exceeds the
NP
observations in
{\mathbf{Y}}
.

All in all, using (
4
) the diffused source localization task can be stated as a non-convex feasibility problem of the form

\text{find }\{{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{h}}\}\>\>\text{s. to }\>{\mathbf{Y}}={\mathbf{V}}\text{diag}\big(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{L}{\mathbf{h}}\big){\mathbf{V}}^{\top}{\mathbf{X}},\>\|{\mathbf{X}}\|_{0}\leq PS,

(5)

where the
\ell_{0}
-(pseudo) norm
\|{\mathbf{X}}\|_{0}:=|\textrm{supp}({\mathbf{X}})|
counts the non-zero entries in
{\mathbf{X}}
. In other words, the goal is to find the solution to a system of bilinear equations subject to a sparsity constraint in
{\mathbf{X}}
; a hard problem due to the cardinality function as well as the bilinear equality constraints. To deal with the latter, building on
[
21
]
we will henceforth assume that the filter
{\mathbf{H}}
is invertible.

3
Convex Relaxation for Invertible Graph Filters

In this section we propose a convex relaxation of (
5
), which is feasible under the additional mild assumption of diffusion filter invertibility. We wrap up with a brief discussion about algorithms to solve the resulting linear program.

3.1
A linear programming problem reformulation

From the filter’s input-output relationship in (
4
), it follows that
{\mathbf{H}}
will be invertible if
{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}
does not vanish at any of the graph frequencies
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. That is, no frequency component of the input should be completely annihilated by the filter. We make the following assumption on the signal model.

Assumption 1

(Invertible graph filter)
.

Recall the observation model
{\mathbf{Y}}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{X}}
in Section
2.2
, where
{\mathbf{H}}=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}h_{l}{\mathbf{S}}^{l}
is a graph filter. We assume
{\mathbf{H}}
is invertible, meaning
\tilde{h}_{i}=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}h_{l}\lambda_{i}^{l}\neq 0
holds for all
i=1,\ldots,N
.

The inverse operator
{\mathbf{G}}:={\mathbf{H}}^{-1}
is also a graph filter on
{\mathcal{G}}
, which can be uniquely represented as a polynomial in the shift
{\mathbf{S}}
of degree at most
N-1

[
12
, Theorem 4]
. Specifically, let
{\mathbf{g}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
be the vector of inverse-filter coefficients, i.e.,
{\mathbf{G}}=\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}g_{l}{\mathbf{S}}^{l}
. Then one can equivalently rewrite the observation model
{\mathbf{Y}}={\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{X}}
as

{\mathbf{X}}={\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{Y}}={\mathbf{V}}\text{diag}({\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}){\mathbf{V}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}},

(6)

where
\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}:=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{N}{\mathbf{g}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is the inverse filter’s frequency response and
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{N}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
is a square Vandermonde matrix. Naturally,
{\mathbf{G}}={\mathbf{H}}^{-1}
implies the condition
{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}\circ{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}=\mathbf{1}_{N}
on the frequency responses, where
\mathbf{1}_{N}
denotes the
N\times 1
vector of all ones.

The fundamental implication of Assumption
1
is that, leveraging (
6
), one can recast (
5
) as a
linear
inverse problem

\min_{\{{\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{g}}\}}\>\|{\mathbf{X}}\|_{0},\>\>\text{s. to }\>{\mathbf{X}}={\mathbf{V}}\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{N}{\mathbf{g}}){\mathbf{V}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}},\>{\mathbf{X}}\neq\mathbf{0}_{N\times P}.

(7)

This approach to handle the bilinear equations (
5
) is markedly different from the matrix lifting technique in
[
18
]
. We were inspired by the blind deconvolution method in
[
21
]
, but in the GSP setting dealt with here the convolution kernel
{\mathbf{H}}
is not circulant, and so
{\mathbf{V}}
is no longer the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) matrix.

The
\ell_{0}
pseudo-norm in (
7
) renders the problem NP-hard to optimize. Convex-relaxation
approaches to tackle sparse recovery problems have enjoyed
remarkable success, since they often entail no loss of optimality. An important contribution of this work is to establish this holds for (
7
) as well; see Section
4
. Accordingly, we instead: (i) seek to minimize the
\ell_{1}
-norm convex surrogate of the cardinality function, that is
\|{\mathbf{X}}\|_{1,1}=\sum_{i,j}|X_{ij}|
; and (ii) express the filter in the graph spectral domain as in (
6
). This way, we arrive at the convex cost function

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{X}}\|_{1,1}={}

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{G}}{\mathbf{Y}}\|_{1,1}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{V}}\text{diag}(\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}){\mathbf{V}}^{\top}{\mathbf{Y}}\|_{1,1}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\|({\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{V}}\odot{\mathbf{V}})\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}\|_{1},

where the last equality is obtained after vectorizing the norm’s matrix argument, and
\odot
denotes the Khatri-Rao (i.e., columnwise Kronecker) product. Our idea is to solve the convex
\ell_{1}
-synthesis problem (in this case a linear program), e.g.,
[
38
]
, namely

\hat{\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\>\|({\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{V}}\odot{\mathbf{V}})\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}\|_{1},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{s. to }\>\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}=N.

(8)

While the linear constraint in (
8
) avoids
\hat{\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}}=\mathbf{0}_{N}
, it also serves to fix the scale of the solution. An insightful discussion on the role of the constraint will emerge as an upshot of the exact recovery guarantees derived in Section
4.1
.

3.2
Algorithms

From an algorithmic point of view, under the mild assumption that the diffusion filter is invertible (cf. Assumption
1
), one can readily use e.g., an off-the-shelf interior-point method or a specialized sparsity-minimization algorithm to solve the linear programming formulation (
8
) efficiently. Different from the solvers in
[
18
,
39
]
, the aforementioned algorithmic alternatives are free of expensive singular-value decompositions per iteration.
Regardless of the particular algorithm chosen, we have found that overall performance can be improved via the iteratively-reweighted
\ell_{1}
-norm minimization procedure tabulated under Algorithm
1
; see also
[
40
]
for a justification of such refinement that effectively zeroes out small residual entries in intermediate estimates of
{\mathbf{X}}
. In practice, a couple refinement iterations suffice so the additional overhead is minimal.

Once the frequency response
\hat{{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}}
of the inverse filter is recovered, one can readily reconstruct the sparse sources via

{\hat{\mathbf{X}}}=\text{unvec}\left(({\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{V}}\odot{\mathbf{V}})\hat{{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}}\right),

(9)

where here the
\text{unvec}(\cdot)
operator reshapes its vector argument to an
N\times P
matrix. If so desired, one can likewise form the diffusion filter estimate
{\hat{\mathbf{H}}}={\mathbf{V}}\text{diag}(\mathbf{1}_{N}/\hat{{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}}){\mathbf{V}}^{\top}
, where the division is to be conducted entrywise.

Algorithm 1

Iteratively-reweighted
\ell_{1}
minimization for (
8
)

1:

Input:
Matrix
{\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{V}}\odot{\mathbf{V}}
,
\delta>0
and
\epsilon>0
.

2:

Initialize

t=0
,
{\mathbf{w}}^{(0)}=\mathbf{1}_{NP}
,
{\mathbf{X}}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}_{N\times p}
.

3:

repeat

4:

Solve

\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}^{(t+1)}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}}\left\|{\mathbf{w}}^{(t)}\circ[({\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{V}}\odot{\mathbf{V}})\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}]\right\|_{1},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{s. to }\>\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}=N.

5:

Form
\textrm{vec}({\mathbf{X}}^{(t+1)})=({\mathbf{Y}}^{\top}{\mathbf{V}}\odot{\mathbf{V}})\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}^{(t+1)}
.

6:

Update
w_{i}^{(t+1)}=\frac{1}{[\textrm{vec}({\mathbf{X}}^{(t+1)})]_{i}+\delta},\>\>i=1,2,\ldots,NP.

7:

t\leftarrow t+1
.

8:

until

\|{\mathbf{X}}^{(t)}-{\mathbf{X}}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
.

9:

return

\hat{\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}}:=\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}^{(t)}
and
\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}:={\mathbf{X}}^{(t)}
.

In the next section we will discuss the exact recovery condition for (
8
) and its robustness to noise corrupted observation.

4
Recovery Guarantees

Here we conduct a theoretical analysis of the proposed convex estimator in (
8
), which is a relaxation of the blind deconvolution problem on the graph
{\mathcal{G}}
. We first derive exact recovery guarantees, which hold with high probability under a Bernoulli-Gaussian model for the sparse inputs. A stable recovery result is then established, ensuring the estimation error on the inverse filter’s frequency response can be kept in check when the observations are corrupted by a small amount of additive noise.

Because of its analytical tractability, the Bernoulli-Gaussian model is widely adopted to describe and generate random sparse matrices such as the unknown sources
{\mathbf{X}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times P}
in Section
2.3
. We henceforth adopt the following model specification that is consistent with the definition in
[
31
]
.

Definition 1

(Bernoulli-Gaussian model)
.

We say a random matrix
{\mathbf{X}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times P}
adheres to a Bernoulli-Gaussian model with parameter
\theta\in(0,1)
, if its entries are
X_{ip}=\Omega_{ip}\gamma_{ip}/\sqrt{\theta}
, where
\Omega_{ip}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\theta)
and
\gamma_{ip}\sim\text{Normal}(0,1)
are i.i.d. for all
i,p
.

In the context of Definition
1
we say that the matrix entries
X_{ip}
are Bernoulli-Gaussian random variables, with
{\mathbb{E}}\left[X_{ip}\right]=0
and
\text{var}\left[X_{ip}\right]=1

4.1
Exact recovery conditions

Suppose that (
7
) is identifiable (see e.g.,
[
1
, Remark 1]
for sufficient conditions under the Bernoulli-Gaussian model), and let
\{{\mathbf{X}}_{0},\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}_{0}\}
be the solution. For the ensuing discussion and to state our exact recovery result, some preliminary notation is in order. We compactly denote polynomials of the given graph-shift operator
{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{V}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}{\mathbf{V}}^{\top}
as
{\mathcal{P}}({\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}):={\mathbf{V}}\text{diag}({\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}){\mathbf{V}}^{\top}
, where
{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}
is the corresponding filter’s frequency response. So given observations
{\mathbf{Y}}={\mathcal{P}}({\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_{0}){\mathbf{X}}_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times P}
(implying
{\mathbf{X}}_{0}={\mathcal{P}}({\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}_{0}){\mathbf{Y}}
under Assumption
1
, for
{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}_{0}\circ{\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_{0}=\mathbf{1}_{N}
), we will study the following problem [cf. (
8
)]

\hat{{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}}=\text{arg}\min\limits_{{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}}\|\mathcal{P}({\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}){\mathbf{Y}}\|_{1,1},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{s. to }\>{\mathbf{r}}^{\top}{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}=c,

(10)

where
{\mathbf{r}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
is a generic constraint vector, and
c={\mathbf{r}}^{\top}{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}_{0}\neq 0
is a constant to control the (inherently ambiguous) scale of the solution
\hat{{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}}
. In general we do not have any prior knowledge about the ground-truth filter, so we can just select
{\mathbf{r}}=\mathbf{1}_{\text{N}}
as in (
8
). Our main result will offer some insights on how the recovery performance is affected by the choice of
{\mathbf{r}}
.

We let
{\mathbf{P}}_{1}^{\perp}:={\mathbf{I}}_{N}-\frac{\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}}{N}
denote the projection operator onto the orthogonal complement of the subspace
\textrm{span}(\mathbf{1}_{N})
. Finally, consider the matrix
{\tilde{\mathbf{U}}}:=({\mathbf{V}}\circ{\mathbf{V}}){\mathbf{P}}_{1}^{\perp}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
, which one can show has a maximum singular value
\sigma_{\max}({\tilde{\mathbf{U}}})\leq 1
. With these definitions in place, we have all the elements to state the main theorem in this section. The result provides sufficient conditions to guarantee exact recovery of the inverse filter
{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}_{0}
, and hence the sparse sources
{\mathbf{X}}_{0}
via (
9
), with high probability.

Theorem 1

(Exact recovery)
.

Consider graph signal observations
{\mathbf{Y}}={\mathcal{P}}({\tilde{\mathbf{h}}}_{0}){\mathbf{X}}_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times P}
, where
{\mathbf{X}}_{0}
adheres to the Bernoulli-Gaussian model with
\theta\in\left(0,0.324\right]
. Recall that under Assumption
1
, we can write
{\mathbf{X}}_{0}={\mathcal{P}}({\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}_{0}){\mathbf{Y}}
. Let
P\geq C^{\prime}\sigma_{m}^{-2}\log{\frac{4}{\delta}}
, where
\sigma_{m}=\min(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3},\sigma_{4})
and
\sigma_{1}\in\left(0,\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\theta^{3/2}}{2}\right]
,
\sigma_{2}\in\left(0,\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\theta}{2}\right]
,
\sigma_{3}>0
,
\sigma_{4}\in(0,1)
,
\delta\in(0,1)
are parameters, while
C^{\prime}
is a constant that does not depend on
P
,
\sigma_{m}
, or

\left\|\mathbf{P}^{\perp}_{1}\text{diag}({\mathbf{r}})\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}_{0}\right\|_{2}\leq cd_{0},

(11)

where
d_{0}:=\frac{\sqrt{1-\sigma_{\max}^{2}(\tilde{{\mathbf{U}}})}\left[(1-\sigma_{1})-2\theta(1+\sigma_{2})\right](1-\sigma_{4})}{(1+\sigma_{3})\sqrt{\theta}}
.

Notice that when

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{P}^{\perp}_{1}\tilde{{\mathbf{g}}}_{0}\right\|_{2}\leq Nd_{0}.

(12)

Condition (

is closer to the all-ones vector

{\tilde{\mathbf{g}}}_{0}

Additionally, increasing
\theta

will decrease

5.3
Experiments on real social network data

In this section we test the proposed approach on the Epinions dataset

6
Concluding Summary and Future Work

We studied the problem of blind graph filter identification from multiple sparse inputs, which extends blind deconvolution of time (or spatial) domain signals to graphs. By introducing a mild assumption on invertibility of the graph filter, we obtained a computationally simpler convex relaxation for (diffused) source localization in the multi-signal case. In terms of theoretical analyses, we first derived sufficient conditions for exact recovery, which hold with high probability under a Bernoulli-Gaussian model for the sparse inputs. A stable recovery result is then established, ensuring the estimation error on the inverse filter’s frequency response is manageable when the observations are corrupted by a small amount of noise.

Ongoing work includes additional analyses on the
robustness
of the proposed approach to imperfections in the observed graph, as well as when measurements are collected only in a fraction of nodes. On the algorithmic side, developing an
online
network source localization method capable of processing streaming graph signal observations is also of interest.

Lemma 5

(Bounded energy)

Let

\Big|\sum_{i,p}a_{i}|X_{ip}|\Big|\leq(1+\sigma_{3})\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i,p}a_{i}|X_{ip}|\right]\right|

Proof of Lemma
5
. Note that for
\theta\in\left(0,e^{-1}\right]
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}\left[|X_{ip}|\right]=\sqrt{\theta}\beta_{0},

{\mathbb{E}}\left[|X_{ip}|^{2}\right]=1,
and
\text{var}\left[|X_{ip}|\right]=1-\theta\beta_{0}^{2}>0
, for

|X_{ip}|
has an upper sub-Gaussian tail since it has the folded Normal distribution and,

\text{Pr}\left[|X_{ip}|\geq t\right]\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2}t^{2}},

Similar to
[
44

{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{u|X_{ip}|}\right]\leq e^{\sqrt{\theta}\beta_{0}+Cu^{2}}

\breve{X}_{i}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{P}}\sum_{p=1}^{P}(|X_{ip}|-\sqrt{\theta}\beta_{0})

\text{var}\left[\breve{X}_{i}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\breve{X}_{i}^{2}\right]=1

An analogous calculation also yields

Let

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\bar{a}_{i}}{\sqrt{P}}\sum_{p=1}^{P}(|X_{ip}|-\sqrt{\theta}\beta_{0})

\displaystyle<t

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{i,p}a_{i}(|X_{ip}|-\sqrt{\theta}\beta_{0})

\displaystyle<\|{\mathbf{a}}\|_{2}^{2}\sqrt{P}t

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{i,p}\left(a_{i}|X_{ip}|-{\mathbb{E}}\left[a_{i}|X_{ip}|\right]\right)

\displaystyle<\sigma_{3}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i,p}a_{i}|X_{ip}|\right],

where

Appendix C
Epinions Data Sampling and Pre-Processing

C.1
Sampling

Here describe the implemented sampling design applied to
{\mathbf{Y}}_{\textrm{obs}}
. The goals of data sampling are: (i) the resulting observation density should be as high as possible; and (ii) all of the users in the sampled dataset should be connected. To achieve those goals, for
k=1,2,\ldots
we repeat the following three steps.
Step 1:
From the previous item set
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
we pick all items that have been rated by at least
N_{\min}=150
users from
{\mathcal{U}}_{k}
, and let the new item set be
{\mathcal{I}}_{k+1}
.
Step 2:
From the previous user set
{\mathcal{U}}_{k}
we pick all users who have rated at least
N_{\min}
items in
{\mathcal{I}}_{k+1}
and let the new user set be the user set candidate
{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}_{k+1}
.
Step 3.
To maintain the connectivity of the user network, we randomly select a user from
{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}_{k+1}
and collect all of the users (within
{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}_{k+1}
) that are accessible from this user (including the selected user) to generate the new user set
{\mathcal{U}}_{k+1}
. The above three steps are repeated until there is no feasible update for
\{{\mathcal{I}}_{k},{\mathcal{U}}_{k}\}
.

C.2
Pre-processing

As hinted by the results in Theorem
1
, the unbiasedness of the graph signals is crucial to satisfactory performance of the proposed approach. Hence we centered the rating matrix by adjusting the range from
[1,5]
to
[-2,2]
.
