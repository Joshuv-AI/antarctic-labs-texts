Good vision without peripheries: behavioral and fMRI evidence
Authors: M Ninghetto, A Kozak, T Gałecki, K Szulborski, J P Szaflik, M Ołdak, A Marchewka, K Burnat
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn healthy vision, bright slow-motion stimuli are processed primarily by the regions of the visual system that receive input from the central part of the scene, whereas processing of dark fast-motion stimuli is more dependent on peripheral visual input. We tested 31 retinitis pigmentosa (RP) patients with long-term loss of peripheral photoreceptors and healthy controls with temporarily limited peripheral vision. We measured motion-based acuity using random-dot kinematograms, establishing individual thresholds for differentiating a circle from an ellipse. Participants subsequently performed a functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) task set at a constant level of difficulty. The results showed that limiting vision did not affect motion-acuity thresholds in control participants but did cause different brain activations than those in RP patients, indicating prompt implementation of the strategy that would be perceptually successful. Compared with controls with both full and limited vision, impaired motion acuity in RP patients led to decreased brain activation, particularly in the primary peripheral visual areas V1-3. Importantly, compared with controls in full vision, matched decreased activation in MT+/V5, salience-processing cortices and the superior temporal cortex were detected in RP patients and in controls with limited peripheral vision, revealing brain networks that compensate for the loss of peripheral vision.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76879-9.Subject terms: Visual system, Extrastriate cortex, Motion detection, Pattern vision, Retina, Striate cortex, Retinal diseases, Psychophysics, Magnetic resonance imaging, Cortex

Introduction

Visual processing, object recognition, and reading depend on sharp vision and the ability to distinguish dark from light elements in the scenery. This separation between the negative contrast and positive contrast parts of the visual scene starts at the eye and is maintained at the level of the primary visual cortex1. For example, in bird watching, the dark birds against the brighter sky are separated by luminance contrast. Most natural visual scenes have a similar luminance structure: the large bright region (i.e., the sky) at the top and darker regions with complicated textures and details below2. In natural environments, detailed information about objects is carried predominantly by the dark parts of the visual scene, which is reflected by the visual system neuronal activity from the retina to the primary visual cortex (V1), which responds preferentially to dark regions when examined within the central 10 degrees of the visual field3,4. The processing of light and dark areas is segregated not only at the retina but also at the cortical level, where the processing of dark regions dominates the processing of light regions. However, these assumptions are based on the central V1 stationary stimuli stimulation5–8.

Less is known about responsiveness to the specific visual stimulation of peripheral vision outside the sharp central 10 degrees of the visual field9. So far, it is accepted that low acuity and sensitivity to fast-motion velocities clearly differentiate peripheral from central visual processing10. In visually deprived animals, which can be used to model congenital cataracts, visual deprivation halts the maturation only of the cortical representation of the peripheral visual field in V111,12. Furthermore, when tested in adulthood, animals with early visual impairment show deficiencies in discriminating motion signals at high velocities or motion signals carried by dark dots on a bright background13. Surprisingly, these deficiencies are similar to those of retinitis pigmentosa (RP) patients, who suffer from peripheral vision malfunction due to photoreceptor degeneration in the peripheral regions of the retina. Therefore, we aimed to explore the behavioral and cortical functions of the central and peripheral visual fields. We modeled the loss of the visual periphery in the control group by mechanically limiting the peripheral visual field using narrowing goggles developed in-house. Using this novel approach, we aimed to differentiate the behavioral and cortical responses to short-term limiting of the peripheral visual field in comparison with the long-term loss of peripheral input in RP patients with preserved tunnel vision (central 10 degrees of the visual field).

Retinitis pigmentosa affects approximately 1 in 5000 individuals worldwide and is associated with mutations in approximately 90 different genes; long-term retinal peripheral vision loss results in tunnel vision, impairing the patient’s daily life due to various visual dysfunctions that have not yet been fully described14. Retinal thinning due to photoreceptor degeneration has been shown to be significantly correlated with lower visual acuity scores15 and a lower ability to distinguish shapes and details even when wearing corrective lenses16. At the visual cortex level, fMRI studies have shown reduced cortical activity in V1, V2 and V3 in RP patients when presented with bright flashes and checkerboard patterns17. Masuda and colleagues18,19, exploring cortical responses to visual stimuli consisting of checkerboards and scrambled or intact faces, reported that when RP patients were asked to look at the stimuli passively, they exhibited in V1 either large unresponsive zones or responsive zones but with reduced brain activity. Importantly, fMRI analysis revealed activation within the visual cortex even in fully blind RP patients at the final stages of photoreceptor degeneration20. Studies aimed at clinically assessing visual field loss in RP patients have used automated static perimetry tests21,22 combined with a wide range of clinical tests that examine central and peripheral vision separately23. In the clinical context, ophthalmological tests of central and peripheral vision deficiencies are separate, and acuity assessment is based exclusively on the perception of stationary black stimuli on a white board, such as the Snellen letter chart.

We aimed to explore motion and shape perception in central and peripheral vision simultaneously in healthy controls and in RP patients using a motion-acuity task developed in-house24,25. The motion-acuity test is based on the discrimination of centrally located shapes, an ellipse and a circle with matching surfaces, built from random dot kinematograms (RDKs) in negative or positive contrast (dark or light moving dots, respectively) separated from the same RDK background by velocity. We measured acuity according to shape-from-motion discrimination, exploring how acuity thresholds depend on the velocity and luminance contrast of motion signals. The behavioral assessment of individual motion-acuity thresholds was followed by an fMRI session using the same stimuli at the individual threshold level. We investigated the functions of the central and peripheral visual fields in RP patients and healthy controls with controlled peripheral stimulation of the retina by transiently limiting the peripheral visual field with goggles. The control group was tested twice, with full unrestricted vision and with a visual field limited to the central 10 degrees. We also tested a genetically described group of 37 patients with long-term loss of the peripheral retina and preserved central tunnel vision. The motion-acuity thresholds and cortical activations differed between RP patients and controls with full vision and limited peripheral vision.

Materials and methods

Participants

We tested thirty-seven RP patients (16 males, 21 females) aged 28–62 years (mean age = 43.83 ± 9.66) and forty-six healthy participants (control group; 21 males, 25 females) aged 20–63 years (mean age = 36.67 ± 12.38). In the cohort of RP patients, we analyzed only data from participants who completed both behavioral and neuroimaging sessions. After the data were collected, we matched the controls with the RP patients according to sex and age. The final analyses were conducted on 31 RP patients (13 males, 18 females; age 43.13 ± 9.75) and 31 controls (14 males, 17 females; age 41.77 ± 10.91). The genetic probands of all the RP patients were sequenced with the RP-LCA smMIPs platform. RP patients with tunnel vision had a central residual visual field limited to a 10-degree diameter (Humphrey field analyzer) and best-corrected visual acuity equal to or better than 20/40 (Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study [ETDRS]; see Supplementary Table 2 for uncorrected acuity). Clinical examination of RP patients revealed optic disc pallor, pigmentary deposits extended throughout the retina and narrowed blood vessels. In full-field flash electroretinography (ERG; RETIscan, Roland Consult, Germany), the rod responses were severely diminished, more than the cone responses, similar to rod‒cone dystrophy. The multifocal ERG (mfERG) was abnormal. Patient clinical and genetic data are listed in Supplementary Table 2. The control group had normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity. All the participants reported no history of psychiatric or neurological disorders. Written informed consent was obtained and signed from all participants or their legal guardians, ensuring that they understood the general purpose of the experiment and the potential risks associated with the MRI procedures. All procedures were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, and approval was obtained from the Bioethical Committee at the Medical University of Warsaw (granted to J. Szaflik, KB/157/2017).

Procedure

The experiment conducted at the Laboratory of Brain Imaging (Neurobiology Center, Nencki Institute of Experimental Biology, Warsaw) consisted of two parts performed on the same day: behavioral measurement of motion-acuity thresholds conducted outside the MRI scanner and a subsequent neuroimaging session (Fig. 1A, B). In controls, to transiently limit the peripheral visual field, we developed a narrowing system using swimming goggles, with transparent lenses replaced with white opaque lenses25, which had an aperture of 1.4 mm that limited the visual field to the central 10 degrees. To ensure that the goggles were suitable for every subject and to account for the natural individual interocular distance, we made 14 pairs of goggles with holes spaced from 58 mm to 72 mm (with a step of 1 mm between each pair of goggles). After the behavioral assessment was in the control performed with full vision and repeated while wearing goggles, the participants underwent an fMRI procedure (Fig. 1B); similar to the behavioral assessment, the control group first underwent scanning in an unrestricted vision condition. Afterward, for 15 min before the second scan in goggles, the subjects walked freely while wearing the goggles, and the procedure was then repeated. The RP patients underwent fMRI only once without goggles. The results presented in this manuscript are part of a larger study in which DTI and population receptive field (pRF) mapping protocols were performed as a result prolonging the total time inside the MRI per person to a maximum of 90 min.

Motion-acuity task

First, individual thresholds were measured using stationary, solid gray figures (Fig. 1C). The subjects were asked to choose the circle from the surface-matched ellipse. The target stimuli (S + and S-) were simultaneously displayed centrally within 10 degrees of the visual field. To have direct feedback, we asked patients to inform us if they were not able to see the shapes. Depending on the position of S + relative to the central fixation, the participants had to press the left or right button on the response pad. Each subsequent trial started after the subject responded or after 10 s if no response was given. The ellipse aspect ratio changed according to individual performance from elongated to circular. The individual threshold assessment was performed with an adaptive staircase procedure described previously24,25. The difficulty of the test increased as the ellipse became more circular until reaching the individual minimal perceived difference between shapes, i.e., the acuity threshold. The individual thresholds for stationary shape discrimination were used to set the individual baseline difficulty for the subsequent motion-acuity measurements and fMRI sessions. In Fig. 2, the acuity thresholds are reported as the minimal perceived difference in visual degrees. The smaller the difference is, the better the acuity is; better acuity means that the subject can still determine which shape the circle is, even when it is almost identical to the ellipse.

We used a motion-acuity task24,25 in which participants had to discriminate between a circle (S+) and a vertically oriented ellipse (S-), composed of an RDK separated from the RDK background by the velocity of the dots. S+, S- and the background consisted of dots moving coherently upward; dots within S+/- moved slower than the background dots did. We performed the test under two velocity conditions: fast, 10/20 deg/s, and slow, 1/5 deg/s. The motion-acuity test was performed in two contrast conditions: positive, bright dots on a dark background, and negative, dark dots on a bright background. During the subsequent fMRI procedure, the motion-acuity tasks were performed at a constant level of individual difficulty, which was based on the baseline threshold acquired at the beginning of the procedure (Fig. 1C). The fMRI motion-acuity task task consisted of six blocks of 10 trials per task (10 s stimulation, 20 s interstimulus interval) with dots moving coherently upward at slow and fast velocities in negative or positive contrast. During the 20 s interstimulus interval, a plain gray screen was displayed. The participants were requested to use the response pad to indicate the position of the circle (S+), while the ellipse length/width ratio remained constant.

We set the motion-acuity threshold for each participant based on the final level of the stationary baseline task. For this reason, the starting point for the constant task in fMRI was different between the control group and the RP group, possibly leading to different extents of cortical activation.

fMRI acquisition and preprocessing

Acquisition was performed using a 3-Tesla MRI scanner (Siemens Magnetom Trio TIM, Erlangen, Germany) with a 12-channel phased-array head coil. A structural T1-weighted image (repetition time = 2530 ms, echo time = 3.32 ms) was acquired for each participant. Evoked blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) responses were obtained using a T2*-weighted gradient echo‒planar imaging sequence with the following parameters: repetition time = 2500 ms, echo time = 28 ms, flip angle = 80°, field of view = 504 × 504, and 72 axial slices with 3 mm slice thickness and no gap between slices. Each functional run comprised 92 volumes. Motion acuity stimuli were presented on a 32’’ LCD rear-projection screen with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels, an active area of 69.8 × 39.3 cm, and a refresh rate of 120 Hz (BOLD Screen 32, Cambridge Research Systems).

Standard preprocessing was performed using SPM12 (Wellcome Trust Center for Neuroimaging, London, UK), including distortion correction for magnetic field inhomogeneities, motion correction through realignment to the first acquired image, coregistration of the anatomical image to the mean functional image, segmentation of the coregistered structural image using default tissue probability maps, and normalization to MNI space with a voxel size of 2 × 2 × 2. Movement regressors were incorporated into the design matrix using the ART toolbox to identify and exclude motion-affected volumes exceeding a threshold of 3 mm and a rotation threshold of 0.05 radians.

Statistical analysis

To investigate the differences between individual motion-acuity thresholds obtained in the behavioral assessment, we performed three separate full factorial GLM analyses for the comparisons: (1) controls with full vision vs. controls with limited vision, (2) RP patients vs. controls with full vision and (3) RP patients vs. controls with limited vision. We included the tasks (4 levels: fast negative contrast, slow negative contrast, fast positive contrast, slow positive contrast) and the group (control with full and limited vision; control with full vision and RP; control with limited vision and RP) as factors. We examined post hoc results using Bonferroni correction. Using the same GLM design, we also investigated the percentage of correct responses to the constant motion-acuity task performed during fMRI.

To investigate the differences in whole-brain cortical responses to the motion-acuity tasks as a main factor, we performed three separate t tests using the SPM12 toolbox for (1) controls with full vision versus controls with limited vision, (2) controls with full vision versus RP patients and (3) controls with limited vision versus RP patients. Further separate t tests for each motion-acuity task (fast velocity in negative contrast, slow velocity in negative contrast, fast velocity in positive contrast and slow velocity in positive contrast) were performed. The t tests were performed using t contrasts to investigate differences in both directions.

To investigate the responses in the motion-processing MT+/V5 area, we used the SPM toolbox MarsBaR26 and the retinotopy-defined MT+/V5 ROI from the Wang and colleagues atlas27. We performed three full factorial GLM analyses for the following pairs: (1) controls with full vision versus controls with limited vision; (2) controls with full vision versus RP patients; and (3) controls with limited vision versus RP patients. The GLM included the following factors: group (2 levels), hemisphere (2 levels) and task parameter (velocity: fast or slow; contrast: positive or negative). We found no significant differences in BOLD responses between the groups. The significance level for all analyses was set at p < 0.05. Familywise error (FWE) correction was applied to address multiple comparisons.

To identify the brain regions, we used the Julich Brain Atlas28 and the atlas developed by Wang and colleagues27. To delineate the cortical representation of the central and peripheral visual fields within V1-3 areas (Fig. 4A-C), we utilized our study in preparation, that investigated population receptive field (pRF) mapping on the same participants .

Results

Motion-acuity assessment

The stationary baseline threshold (Fig. 1C) did not differ between the controls with full vision and the RP patients (Fig. 2A). For controls, the stationary baseline threshold did not differ from the final motion-acuity thresholds. In contrast, for the RP patients, the baseline threshold (mean ± standard deviation; 0.159 ± 0.09) was significantly lower than that in all tasks: under negative contrast at fast velocity (p < 0.001; 0.969 ± 0.47) and slow velocity (p < 0.001; 0.879 ± 0.53) and under positive contrast at fast velocity (p = 0.001; 0.600 ± 0.43) and slow velocity (p = 0.002; 0.582 ± 0.40).

The motion-acuity thresholds determined outside the scanner and the accuracy of the motion-acuity task performed during the fMRI procedure were analyzed using the same statistical approach. Figure 2B-C shows the significant main effects of the Bonferroni-corrected post hoc tests for separate motion-acuity tasks (Fig. 1D).

Individual motion-acuity thresholds differentiated RP patients

For the comparison between controls in full- versus limited-vision conditions, we found a main effect of the task (F(3,239) = 8.4032, p = 0.00002) but not of the vision condition. Motion-acuity thresholds between tasks were significantly greater for the negative contrast at fast velocity (0.36 ± 0.22) than for the positive contrast at slow velocity (0.18 ± 0.13; p = 0.00006).

The comparison between controls with full vision and RP patients was significant for the task (F(3,224) = 5.8143, p = 0.0007) and for the group (F(1,224) = 71.3216, p < 0.001). For the tasks, the motion-acuity thresholds were significantly higher for the RP patients under negative contrast (Fig. 2B) at fast velocity (p = 0.00009; controls 0.37 ± 0.25, RP 0.96 ± 0.61) and slow velocity (p = 0.0001; controls 0.30 ± 0.30, RP 0.88 ± 0.68). Motion-acuity thresholds were also significantly higher for the RP patients under positive contrast, but only for the slow velocity (Fig. 2C; p = 0.009, controls 0.17 ± 0.15, RP 0.58 ± 0.53).

The comparison between controls with limited vision and RP patients revealed significant differences for the task (F(3,223) = 4.7058, p = 0.003) and the group (F(1,223) = 74.3877, p < 0.001). Similar to the comparison between controls with full vision and RP patients the motion-acuity thresholds were significantly higher for the RP patients under negative contrast (Fig. 2B) at fast velocity (p < 0.001; limited 0.35 ± 0.19, RP 0.96 ± 0.61) and slow velocity (p = 0.0001; limited 0.25 ± 0.13, RP 0.87 ± 0.68) and under positive contrast at slow velocity (Fig. 2C; p = 0.017; limited 0.20 ± 0.12, RP 0.58 ± 0.53).

Behavior during the fMRI session—decreased accuracy in RP patients

The accuracy of the motion-acuity task performed at the constant stimulus level during the fMRI procedure, calculated as the percentage of correct responses, did not differ between controls with full and limited vision.

The comparison between controls with full vision and RP patients revealed significant differences for the task (F(3,240) = 2.918, p = 0.034) and the group (F(1,240) = 111.388, p < 0.001). The RP patients had a lower percentage of correct responses in all motion-acuity tasks: negative contrast at fast velocity (p < 0.001: controls 99.03% ± 3.96, RP 48.06% ± 46.50) and slow velocity (p < 0.001: controls 97.41% ± 5.14, RP 46.77% ± 43.15) and positive contrast at fast velocity (p = 0.003: controls 96.45% ± 6.60, RP 67.09% ± 39.42); and slow velocity (p = 0.005: controls 99.67% ± 1.79, RP 71.29% ± 37.39). Similarly, between the controls with limited vision and RP patients, we found significant differences for the task (F(3,240) = 3.009, p = 0.030) and the group (F(1,240) = 104.905, p < 0.001). The RP patients also had a lower percentage of correct responses in all motion-acuity tasks: negative contrast at fast velocity (p < 0.001: controls 98.06% ± 5.42; RP 48.06% ± 46.50) and slow velocity (p < 0.001: controls 96.12% ± 13.08; RP 46.77% ± 43.15) and positive contrast at fast velocity (p = 0.003: controls 96.77% ± 5.99; RP 67.09% ± 39.42) and slow velocity (p = 0.011: controls 98.70% ± 4.27; RP 71.29% ± 37.39).

Discussion

We aimed to explore the consequences of limiting peripheral visual field input, either because of the long-term progression of photoreceptor degeneration in RP or because of the transient limitation of peripheral vision in healthy controls. Transient limiting of peripheral vision in controls did not affect motion-acuity thresholds but still significantly affected brain activation, revealing prompt implementation of the perceptually successful strategy. Compared with controls with full vision, RP patients presented impaired motion-acuity thresholds and distinct patterns of brain activation, with vastly decreased responses within the peripheral primary visual areas V1-3. Surprisingly, RP patients and controls with limited vision showed decreased activation in the bilateral motion-sensitive area MT + compared with controls with full vision29–31. MT + activation may be suppressed by central visual processing in both RP patients and controls with limited peripheral vision. Previously, this interpretation was based on fMRI results obtained from seven blind RP patients with retinal prostheses stimulating the retinal ganglion cells. After stimulation, the V1-3 areas but not the MT + area presented an increase in the BOLD signal32.

Here, we used the motion-acuity task performed under two contrasts engaging both stationary and motion perception24,25. Consistent with facilitation for positive contrast slow motion33, we showed that the easiest task for healthy controls was the slow-velocity positive contrast motion-acuity task compared with the high-velocity negative contrast motion-acuity task, irrespective of full or limited peripheral vision. The processing of lights and darks is segregated at the retina and maintained at the cortical level, where the processing of darks dominates the processing of lights. These assumptions are based on stationary stimuli stimulation and examining the representation of the central visual field within V15–8. Less information is known about contrast-dependent motion stimulation: previously, the behavioral best accuracy was observed for positive contrast slow velocity bar detection33. The impaired motion-acuity negative contrast processing in RP patients is consistent with their retinal deficiencies. Patients with RP suffer from dysregulation of the central and peripheral retina due to photoreceptor degeneration. Photoreceptor degeneration affects not only the whole peripheral retina, which is dominated by rod photoreceptors that are highly sensitive to contrast change34,35 but also the rods within the central retina36. RP patients also suffer from night blindness and deficits in dark adaptation37. Indeed, RP patients had significantly higher thresholds for negative contrast motion-acuity tasks. The deteriorating effect of peripheral stimulation on the acuity of RP patients was also shown with high-contrast black letter discrimination38.

RP patients exhibit lower activation within the cortical representation of the peripheral visual field in V1-3, as shown in this study and previously in V139. For RP patients, the fast-velocity negative contrast motion-acuity task was most demanding, likely reflecting peripheral vision loss. Sensitivity to fast velocity is known to be a feature that differentiates peripheral visual processing from central processing10.

In RP patients, exploratory motor behavior and saccadic movements are impaired40,41, which may be reflected by the lower activation in the preCG than that in controls with full vision. Higher preCG gray matter volume was found to be correlated with efficient antisaccade behavior and maintenance of the saccadic motor behavior plan in healthy participants3. In contrast, RP patients, as well as controls with full vision, had greater activation of the bilateral putamen than did controls with limited vision. The basal ganglia, including the putamen, are part of the cortico-basal ganglia-thalamocortical circuit42 and play pivotal roles in motor43 and oculomotor functions44–47. Owing to the disorganized oculomotor behavior of RP patients, their putamen likely requires greater activation to achieve satisfactory exploratory control. The complementary roles of the putamen and dACC during learning behavior in a visual classification task were observed in macaques with single-unit recordings. During the implementation of a new strategy, the dACC is first activated, and after a series of successful trials, the putamen is engaged48. We found higher activation for the dACC and lower activation for the putamen in controls with limited vision than in controls with full vision. The high engagement of the right dACC in the controls with limited vision possibly reflects the adaptation to new strategies, whereas the lower activation of the putamen possibly reflects a lack of confidence in solving the task during the transient loss of peripheral vision48. In contrast, for RP patients, the lower activation of the bilateral dACC than that in controls with full and limited vision possibly reflects their adjustment to the high-conflict cognitively demanding environment of progressive low vision. The dACC is also engaged during tasks involving high-conflict trials such as the Stroop test49 and tasks with cognitive interference, such as identifying the position of a target digit in an array of three numbers50. This decrease in activation during highly demanding motion-acuity tasks may be similar to the decrease reported for procrastinating subjects under pressure of punishment, in contrast to the increase in dACC activity in low procrastinating subjects51.

Furthermore, the increased activation of the dACC and postCG was specific to the right hemisphere in controls with limited vision, likely linked to a general mobilization of the right hemisphere attention network in the demanding visual condition52. Similarly, we observed greater cortical responses in the right V1-3 in the control group than in the RP patients. Additionally, in both RP patients and controls with limited vision, we observed decreased activation in the right spatial processing supTG area compared with that in controls with full vision. Lesions in the right supTG have been shown to affect spatial processing and awareness in patients suffering from hemispatial neglect53,54. Moreover, transcranial magnetic stimulation of the right supTG impairs stimulus-centered spatial processing, leading to decreased accuracy55. It is likely that narrowing the visual field leads to impaired spatial processing, as reflected by the lower activation of the right supTG in RP patients and controls with limited vision.

A similar pattern of decreased activation under the loss of peripheral vision in RP patients and controls was also observed in the FO and aIC; both cortical regions are involved in goal-directed salient tasks. The aIC and dACC are the major nodes involved in processing salient stimuli, and they exhibit connectivity with limbic structures involved in reward and motivation56–58. The FO is likely to have a role in goal-directed tasks that overlap between task modalities59,60.

We know that introducing rehabilitation procedures is challenging, as RP patients become aware of the presence of blind spots in the peripheral visual field only at the late stages of disease progression61. To date, successful rehabilitation procedures have been directed toward parts of the retina unaffected by RP, such as diode micropulse laser monocular treatment applied at the foveal region of the retina62. The V1 cortical representation of the peripheral retina is the target of the multimodal response described in blindness63–65. In RP patients, the multimodal response can be detrimental to the peripheral visual functions; therefore, strengthening the healthy strategies observed in controls with limited vision may help to halt this response, leading to better functional compensation for visual impairments.
