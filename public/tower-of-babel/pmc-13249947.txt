Preserving spectral selectivity of radiative cooling textile under sweating conditions in hot urban environments
Authors: Bin Gu, Shuangjiang Feng, Qiang Zhang, Ruifeng Lu, Guifang Xiahou, Siying Huang, Guo Li, Huajie Tang, Wenqi Zhong, Dongliang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Atmospheric window (8-13 μm) spectrally selective radiative-cooling textiles can minimize heat gain from the surroundings, especially under the urban heat-island conditions. However, perspiration can substantially degrade this selectivity because sweat has broadband emissive properties. Here, we design an integrated spectrally selective radiative cooling textile with directional sweating capability. Embedded conical microstructures drive rapid sweat transport and shedding, maintaining a dry radiative surface and thereby preserving spectral selectivity. By incorporating 20 wt% silicon nitride particles into polyvinyl butyral fibers, the textile achieves 83.8% emissivity within atmospheric window, 43.3% emissivity within non-atmospheric window (2.5-8 µm and 13-20 µm), and 92.8% solar reflectivity. After sweating, it still possesses 84.4% atmospheric-window emissivity and 45.4% non-atmospheric window emissivity, demonstrating robust spectral preserving capabilities. Outdoor experiments demonstrate that the spectrally selective textile is 2.8 °C and 7.3 °C cooler than broadband textile and cotton, respectively, highlighting its potential for personal radiative cooling in hot urban environments.

Introduction

Rapid urbanization is replacing vegetated land with impervious materials such as concrete and asphalt1. These surfaces can efficiently absorb solar radiation and reradiate it as heat, driving excess surface warming and elevating near-surface air temperatures—hallmarks of the urban heat-island effect2–4. During hot periods, facade and pavement temperatures may exceed 60 °C, well above human skin temperature5, creating persistent radiant heat sources that increase the thermal load on pedestrians6. Sustained exposure undermines thermal comfort and can precipitate heat stress, amplifying the risk of heat-related morbidity and mortality7,8. As continued urbanization and climate change are expected to intensify urban heat islands9,10, strategies that provide effective personal thermal and moisture management in dense cities are increasingly needed.

Over the past decade, passive radiative cooling textiles have attracted considerable interest for their ability to achieve sub-ambient cooling11–15. In open space scenarios, such cooling performance is realized by minimizing solar absorption while maximizing thermal emission through the atmospheric window to outer space16–18. However, only about 3% of human body surface is oriented directly toward the sky, whereas most surface is oriented toward surrounding structures19. In this case, the cooling performance of broadband emissive textiles can become counterproductive under high-temperature urban environments, as they absorb long-wave radiation from heated buildings and pavements20. Notably, recent advances in spectrally selective textiles offer a promising alternative: by combining high emissivity within the atmospheric window with low emissivity elsewhere21, they can preserve radiative cooling, suppressing parasitic heating from the built environment19,22–24.

In high-temperature environments, the human body increases sweat gland activity to maintain thermal homeostasis25,26. In humid climates, however, evaporation is impeded, and perspiration readily accumulates on clothing27. As a result, the hydrophilic regions of earlier sweat-wicking textiles, even those engineered with wettability gradients, can become fully saturated, sharply diminishing capillary transport. The textiles then become heavier and clammy28,29, thereby impairing the body’s cooling process and degrading thermal comfort30. Critically, the spectral selectivity of the textiles is lost because sweat has broadband emissive properties. Enhancing sweat transport is therefore essential for coupled thermal and moisture management in hot and humid environments31–33. Unfortunately, inadequate sweat-wicking in current spectrally selective textiles leads to moisture accumulation that reduces solar reflectivity and elevates emissivity within the non-atmospheric window (2.5–8 μm and 13–20 μm), thereby diminishing spectral selectivity34,35. Preventing sweat-induced deterioration of spectral selectivity, therefore, emerges as a key design requirement and a promising route toward maintaining optimal spectral control in radiative cooling textiles.

By combining spectrally engineered multilayer architecture with directional sweat transport design, we develop a spectrally selective radiative cooling (SSRC) textile that preserves atmospheric-window selectivity under sweating conditions. The hierarchical SSRC textile is comprised of an outer layer of silicon nitride (Si3N4)-doped polyvinyl butyral (PVB/Si3N4) fibers, an intermediate silver nanowire (AgNWs) layer, and an inner layer of poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF-HFP) fibers (Fig. 1a). To enhance directional sweat transport, hydrophilic conical structures, engineered based on capillary action, are embedded within the middle and outer layers of the textile. The cones are oriented with their tips pointing outward, while their bases are in intimate contact with the hydrophobic inner layer, establishing a dual-gradient wettability architecture. The hydrophobic outer surface further promotes rapid shedding of sweat droplets, thereby minimizing moisture-induced disruption of spectral selectivity. Incorporating 20 wt% Si3N4 particles into the outer PVB fibers enables the SSRC textile to suppress solar absorption via optical resonance, while maintaining strong thermal emission (83.8%) within the atmospheric window and reduced emission elsewhere. Meanwhile, the AgNWs middle layer possesses high mid-infrared reflectivity (99.1%), further blocking thermal radiation transmitted through the outer layer from heated urban infrastructure. In addition, the PVDF-HFP inner layer absorbs thermal radiation emitted by the skin and transfers it through the AgNWs layer to the outer surface, where it is radiated to the outer space. Results

Significance of the SSRC textile

In hot urban environments, heat transfer among the human body, clothing, and the surroundings is intrinsically more complex. Compared with open space scenarios, the textiles in urban scenarios undergo additional radiative exchange with adjacent structures (Fig. 1b). When surrounding building facades are hotter than the textile surface, their long-wave thermal emission becomes a net radiative heat input to the textile (Fig. S1). Previous study has shown that, under a solar irradiance of 300 W/m2 and an ambient temperature of 303.15 K, ground and facade temperatures can readily exceed the mean human skin temperature21. Assuming ground and wall surface temperature of 50 °C, we theoretically evaluated the impact of building facades radiation on the thermal management performance of ideal selective textile and ideal broadband textile (Fig. S2). Relative to open space scenarios, both textiles showed reduced cooling power in urban scenarios (Fig. 1c), primarily due to the additional radiative loading imposed by surrounding structures. Evidently, when ambient temperature exceeded the textile surface, the selective textile maintained better cooling performance, consistent with the trends discussed above. When textile surface temperature approached ambient, the superior cooling performance of the selective textile arose from its reduced absorption of thermal radiation from the ground and walls. Although cooling power decreased with increasing ambient temperature for both textiles (Fig. S3a), the selective textile retained a cooling advantage under high-temperature conditions. In addition, the height and width of surrounding buildings markedly affect textile cooling. Cooling performance declined as the building–body angle increased in both elevation and azimuth, consistent with a higher building view factor that increased the textile’s radiative heat load. Notably, as the vertical and horizontal view angles increased, the cooling advantage of selective textile over broadband textile became more pronounced (Figs. 1d and S3b). These results underscore the pivotal role of selective textiles in enabling effective personal radiative cooling in dense, hot urban environments.

Structure and optical performances

To mitigate perspiration-induced degradation of spectral selectivity, we engineered a fibrous textile that combines spectrally selective emission with rapid and directional sweat transport capability. Firstly, conical pores were laser-engraved through the PVB/Si3N4-AgNWs bilayer. Next, cones of matching geometry were fabricated on a plasma-treated PVB/cellulose acetate (PVB/CA) fibrous membrane and inserted into these pores to enhance wicking efficiency. Finally, a hydrophobic PVDF-HFP fibrous membrane was electrospun onto the AgNWs surface to encapsulate the embedded PVB/CA cones (see “Methods” section for more details).

PVB fibers exhibit spectrally selective emission within the atmospheric window, making them attractive candidates for selective radiative cooling (Fig. S4). However, further gains in solar reflectivity and atmospheric-window emissivity are needed to maximize net cooling. In this regard, Si3N4 particles offer a compelling optical complement, providing high solar reflectivity while preserving favorable atmospheric-window spectral selectivity. To elucidate the optical properties of PVB fibers and Si3N4 particles, their complex refractive indices across the visible and mid-infrared regions were extracted from spectroscopic ellipsometry and transmission spectroscopy data. The fitted real (n) and imaginary (k) parts were then incorporated into effective medium theory to quantify solar scattering and mid-infrared absorption efficiencies (Figs. 2a, b and S5). The calculations demonstrate that the incorporation of Si3N4 particles can substantially enhance the solar reflectivity and atmospheric-window spectral selectivity of PVB fibers. Additionally, the thickness of the PVB/Si3N4 fibrous membrane strongly modulated both atmospheric-window spectral selectivity and solar reflectivity. The results prove that a thickness of 200 μm yielded the optimal spectral selective ratio (Figs. S6b, S8, and S9). At this thickness, the PVB/Si3N4 layer reached an atmospheric window emissivity of 85.3%, while maintaining a comparatively low non-atmospheric window emissivity of 45.7% (Fig. 2c). This response arises primarily from vibrational absorptions of Si–N, C–O, and C–H bonds within the atmospheric window (Fig. 2d). Incorporating Si3N4 particles also markedly increased the solar reflectivity of the PVB fibrous membrane. This enhancement is primarily attributed to fiber diameter distribution that more closely matches solar wavelengths, thereby strengthening Mie scattering. (Fig. 2e, inset in Figs. 2f and S10a). As a result, the PVB/Si3N4 layer achieved a solar reflectivity as high as 92.8%.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) and energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) images confirmed that Si3N4 particles were uniformly embedded within the PVB fiber matrix (Figs. 2f and S10b), with an estimated loading of approximately 20 wt% (Fig. S11). Particle incorporation also noticeably increased fiber surface roughness (Fig. S12), which is expected to enhance surface hydrophobicity. Moreover, highly dispersed AgNWs with ultrahigh aspect ratios formed a dense, interconnected network that supported strong plasmonic resonances (Figs. S13 and S14), thereby boosting mid-infrared reflectivity. The AgNWs layer reached an average mid-infrared reflectivity of up to 99.1% (Fig. S15), effectively reflecting ambient thermal radiation that penetrated the outer layer of the SSRC textile. The results indicate that, compared with SSRC textile without the AgNWs layer, the PVB/Si3N4 side of the SSRC textile retained a high emissivity within the atmospheric window, while exhibiting suppressed emissivity outside this range, owing to the high reflectance of the AgNWs layer (Figs. 2g, S16, and S17), thereby reinforcing the spectral selectivity of the SSRC textile. Although the emissivity of the PVDF-HFP fibrous membrane increased with thickness (Fig. S18), the PVDF-HFP inner layer was kept below 30 µm to maintain rapid, directional sweat transport. Compared with a freestanding PVDF-HFP membrane, the PVDF-HFP side of the SSRC textile exhibited higher absorptivity within the atmospheric window and lower emissivity outside it (Fig. S19). This behavior arises from the low transmittance of the textile combined with the intrinsically strong vibrational absorption bands of PVDF-HFP in the atmospheric-window region, as well as the high reflectance of the AgNW layer in the non-atmospheric window region (Fig. S20). Such spectral characteristics are advantageous for efficiently capturing thermal radiation emitted by the human body. In addition, ultralong PVDF-HFP fibers formed a dense network that increased tensile strength while preserving flexibility and foldability (Figs. S21–S23), making the SSRC textile well suited for wearable applications.

Directional sweating performance

Nature-inspired conical structures, exemplified by the periodic spindle knots of spider silk and the tapered spines of cacti, enable directional liquid transport through curvature-driven pressure gradients. Guided by this principle, we fabricated a fiber-based textile that integrates conical microstructures within the fibrous membrane to achieve rapid directional sweat transport. The hydrophobic inner layer, coupled with superhydrophilic conical structures, establishes a surface energy gradient that drove directional sweat transport. Capillary pressure gradients along the tapered geometry propelled liquid from the base toward the tip, whereas reverse transport failed to sustain continuous flow. The progressive decrease in curvature radius and contact angle along the taper established a capillary pressure gradient, with higher pressure at the tip than at the base, thereby driving spontaneous transport from base to tip (Fig. 3a). The dynamic behavior of sweat droplets was further investigated after they passed through the hydrophobic inner layer and entered the superhydrophilic conical structures, ultimately leading to droplet shedding. The process proceeded through four stages: infiltration, absorption, transport, and shedding (Fig. 3b). Upon contacting the hydrophobic inner surface, the droplet infiltrated the cone base, driven by the wettability gradient and capillary forces. It was then rapidly imbibed and transported through the conical channels toward the outer surface by capillarity coupled with the surface energy gradient. Finally, the hydrophobic outer surface enabled rapid droplet shedding under gravity and interfacial repulsion. Experimental observations show that sweat droplets penetrated the thin hydrophobic inner layer, traversed the superhydrophilic conical structures, and were shed from the hydrophobic outer surface within 22 s (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Movie 1). In contrast, droplets placed on the outer conical tips did not infiltrate inward and instead shed rapidly (Fig. S24 and Supplementary Movie 2). These findings highlight that combining conical architectures with an asymmetric surface energy gradient can provide an effective textile design strategy for rapid, directional sweat transport. The conical pores were uniformly distributed (Figs. 3e and S25a), with opening diameters of about 1000 µm at the base and about 600 µm at the tip (Figs. 3f and S25b). Their rims exhibited well-defined concentric, step-like terraces. Color contrast mapped the depth profile, and the consistent hue within each ring corroborated the coaxial alignment of the conical channels. Three-dimensional imaging further revealed camel-hump-like conical sidewall profiles and a uniform distribution of channels throughout the structure (Fig. 3g). Notably, size-matched PVB/CA conical microstructures fit snugly into the conical channels without discernible gaps (Figs. 3h and S26). The SSRC textile retained atmospheric-window spectral selectivity under sweating. After perspiration, atmospheric window emissivity increased slightly from 83.8 to 84.4%, whereas non-atmospheric window rose from 43.3 to 45.4%, corresponding to only a 0.08 decrease in the spectral selectivity ratio (Fig. 3i). In contrast, an otherwise identical textile lacking the conical architecture showed a much larger post-sweating reduction of 0.308 (Fig. S27), demonstrating that the conical structure is essential for preserving spectral selectivity during perspiration. Moreover, the outer surface of the SSRC textile exhibited a high water contact angle of 130.8° (Fig. 3d), attributable to the increased micro-nanoscale surface roughness of fibers induced by particle incorporation. Compared with a conventional waterproof textile, it showed markedly stronger repellency to common liquids, including dyed water, tea, cola, milk, and coffee (Fig. S28), indicating robust resistance to liquid fouling. Despite its pronounced hydrophobicity, the porous fibrous network maintained high breathability, with air permeability and water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) comparable to cotton and low airflow resistance (Fig. S29 and Supplementary Movie 3). The textile also exhibited strong resistance to UV aging and laundering, with mid-infrared emissivity and solar reflectivity remaining essentially unchanged after prolonged UV exposure and washing, respectively (Figs. S230 and S31). Consistently, extended UV irradiation produced no discernible changes in surface chemical bonds or color (Fig. S32), indicating high chemical stability and sustained performance under environmental stress. Together, these attributes improve comfort and durability, supporting the SSRC textile’s suitability for personal thermal management.

Outdoor cooling performance

The outdoor cooling performance of the SSRC textile was tested on the rooftop of Building 4 in the Southeast University Science and Technology Park, Nanjing, China (118.82 °E, 31.88 °N). To mimic human skin and metabolic heat generation, a silicone pad with skin-like color was supplied with an electric power density of 100 W m−2. Textile temperature was recorded in real time using three thermocouples attached to the surface of the simulated skin. During testing, the simulated skin and textile were placed together in a recessed cavity at the center of a well-insulated polyethylene foam box, whose outer surface was covered with aluminum foil (Fig. 4a). The textiles were positioned vertically adjacent to a simulated wall constructed from a waterproof textile with high infrared emissivity (94.0%) (Figs. 4b and S33). Under elevated daytime solar irradiance and ambient temperature (10:00–16:00) (Fig. S34), the SSRC textile maintained a surface temperature slightly below ambient, whereas the broadband textile remained slightly above ambient (Fig. 4c). This is primarily attributable to the absorption of thermal radiation by the broadband textile from surrounding buildings and the ground. The SSRC textile surface was 2.8 and 7.3 °C cooler than the broadband textile and cotton, respectively. The influence of realistic built environments on textile radiative cooling was further evaluated on the second-floor platform of the School of Energy and Environment at Southeast University, Nanjing (118.82 °E, 31.88 °N). Textiles were vertically aligned facing the north facade of a building, with a fixed distance to avoid shading from the structure. Under these conditions, the SSRC textile exhibited a slightly lower surface temperature than the broadband textile (Fig. 4e). In the Northern Hemisphere, north-facing walls receive minimal direct solar irradiance and thus remain cooler than south-facing facades (Fig. S36). Consequently, radiative heat gain from the north wall is limited and is instead dominated by radiation from the heated ground. The surface temperature of the SSRC textile was 0.9 and 6.7 °C lower than that of the broadband textile and cotton, respectively (Fig. S37), with the larger contrast relative to cotton arising primarily from the lower solar absorptivity of the SSRC textile. When the textiles faced away from the sun, solar heat gain was minimized, and the radiative load was dominated by emission from the ground and nearby walls, leading to relatively small surface temperature differences among the textiles. When oriented toward the east-, south-, and west-facing walls, the SSRC textile exhibited temperature reductions of 0.5, 1.0, and 3.4 °C, respectively, compared with the broadband textile (Figs. S38–S44). The pronounced temperature difference observed the west-facing wall is driven by the wall’s elevated surface temperature under intense afternoon solar irradiance (Fig. S45). These results indicate that the building facade temperature can serious influence the thermal gain of the textile in the absence of direct solar input. This phenomenon was further corroborated by theoretical calculations (Fig. 4f). Solar irradiation is also a key determinant of textile cooling performance. Under intense solar irradiation, the SSRC textile delivers markedly stronger cooling than low-reflectivity cotton (Figs. 4g and S46). In addition, infrared thermography of the SSRC textile and cotton attached to the human back outdoors further corroborated cooling performance. Under steady-state conditions, the cotton surface appeared markedly hotter than the SSRC textile (Fig. S47). These results show that, in hot urban environments, the SSRC textile outperforms broadband textiles and cotton in enhancing radiative heat dissipation.

Discussion

In this work, we developed a hierarchically structured SSRC textile that integrates spectrally selective radiative cooling with efficient and directional moisture transport for preserving spectral selectivity under sweating. Driven by a wettability gradient and capillary forces, the conical structure enabled rapid directional sweat transport within 22 s. Incorporating 20 wt% Si3N4 particles into the PVB fibers delivered an optimal spectral selectivity ratio (1.83) and a high solar reflectivity of 92.8%, enabled by enhanced Mie scattering. After sweating, the emissivity of the SSRC textile increased by only 0.6% in the atmospheric window and by 2.1% in the non-atmospheric window, resulting in a minimal 0.08 decrease in spectral selectivity ratio. Outdoor tests demonstrate that, when vertically oriented a simulated wall surface, the SSRC textile was 2.8 and 7.3 °C cooler than the broadband textile and cotton, respectively. Building facades orientation strongly influenced the cooling performance. Compared with the broadband textile, the SSRC textile achieved temperature reductions of 0.5, 1.0, 3.4, and 0.9 °C when facing east-, south-, west-, and north-facing walls, respectively. Additionally, the SSRC textile exhibited excellent moisture permeability, breathability, mechanical robustness, resistance to UV aging, flexibility, foldability, and washability. Overall, the directional sweat-wicking architecture mitigates perspiration-induced degradation of spectral selectivity, enabling sustained personal thermos-moisture management and providing a design basis for next-generation cooling textiles in hot urban environments.

Methods

Materials

Polyvinyl butyral (PVB) (M.W.90000–120000) and Poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF-HFP) (average Mw ~400000) were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cellulose acetate (CA) (acetyl content: 39.8 wt%; hydroxyl content: 3.5 wt%) was obtained from Aladdin Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Silicon nitride (Si3N4) particles were supplied by Suzhou Yuante New Materials Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). AgNWs were purchased from Jiangsu Xianfeng Nanomaterials Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China), they have an average diameter of approximately 50 nm and were dispersed in an aqueous solvent at a concentration of 20 mg/mL. Anhydrous ethanol and acetone were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Regent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of the SSRC textile

The SSRC textile was fabricated by combining electrospinning with CO2 laser engraving. First, Si3N4 and PVB powders were separately dispersed in anhydrous ethanol and magnetically stirred in a 30 °C water bath for 4 h to obtain a 15 wt% PVB/Si3N4 spinning solution. The mass ratio of Si3N4 to PVB was adjusted at 10, 20, 30, and 40%. The spinning solution was then loaded into a syringe and electrospun at an applied voltage of 10 kV using a no. 19 gauge needle, with a tip-to-collector distance of 12 cm and a flow rate of 0.6 mL h−1. The resulting white PVB/Si3N4 fibrous membrane was dried in a vacuum oven at 45 °C for 6 h to remove residual ethanol. A layer of AgNWs was subsequently spray-coated onto the PVB/Si3N4 membrane, and the resulting PVB/Si3N4-AgNWs bilayer textile was dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for 8 h to eliminate excess moisture. Conical pores were fabricated on the bilayer textile using a CO2 laser engraving system, with aperture diameters of 600 μm on the PVB/Si3N4 side and 1000 μm apertures on the AgNWs side. The laser engraving parameters were set to a speed of 500 mm s−1, a power of 35%, and a frequency of 5 kHz. Using the same CO2 laser engraving system, conical fibrous structures of identical dimensions (top diameter 600 μm, bottom diameter 1000 μm) were cut from the plasma-treated PVB/CA fibrous membrane and subsequently inserted into the conical pores of the PVB/Si3N4-AgNWs bilayer. The thickness of the plasma-treated PVB/CA membrane was matched to that of the PVB/Si3N4-AgNWs bilayer. The PVB/CA membrane itself was prepared by electrospinning a 12 wt% solution of PVB and CA (mass ratio 1:1) dissolved in anhydrous ethanol. Finally, a 10 wt% PVDF-HFP solution in acetone was electrospun onto the AgNWs surface at an applied voltage of 18 kV to anchor the PVB/CA conical structures and prevent their detachment. The assembled SSRC textile was dried in a vacuum oven at 45 °C for 6 h to remove residual solvent.

Characterization

SEM images and EDX mapping images were captured using a field emission scanning electron microscopy (Nova NanoSEM450, America). Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FT-IR) spectra were collected by an FT-IR spectrometer (Nicolet iS10) equipped with ATR accessory, and the scanning range was 4000–400 cm−1. The refractive index and extinction coefficient were obtained through a spectral ellipsometer (J. A. Woollam IR-VASE). The solar reflectance spectra were measured using a UV–Vis-NIR spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-3600i-plus) with an integrating sphere accessory. The spectra for mid-infrared band (2.5–20 μm) were collected by an FT-IR spectrometer (Nicolet iS50) equipped with diffuse gold integrating sphere. WCA was measured using a high-speed camera (NAC HX-7S). The tensile strength was measured through a tensile testing tester (QB-8106) at a tensile speed of 1 mm·min−1. Flexibility was verified by twisting and bending. The foldability was verified through complex folding. Infrared thermal images of the objects were captured using an infrared camera (ZLV-FLIRE6390).

Ultraviolet resistance test

UV aging test was conducted using a 330 W/m² ultraviolet lamp (D480-CM). The textile was exposed to continuous UV irradiation for up to ten days, during which spectral measurements in the solar and mid-infrared wavelength ranges were performed at five-day intervals. Assuming a solar radiation intensity of 1000 W/m2 during the summer, and considering that ultraviolet radiation accounts for approximately 4% of the total solar irradiance, the UV intensity during summer is estimated to be around 40 W/m2. Exposure to the ultraviolet lamp for 10 days is equivalent to approximately 248 days of direct sunlight exposure, assuming an 8-h daytime period. Therefore, 10 days of UV lamp exposure corresponds to roughly two summer seasons of natural sunlight exposure.

Air permeability test

The moisture permeability of the SSRC textile is tested by the weighing method. First, 50 × g of anhydrous calcium chloride desiccant was accurately weighed and placed into an Erlenmeyer flask. The sample was then transferred to a vacuum drying oven and dried at 120 °C for 12 h to remove any residual moisture. Finally, the mouth of the Erlenmeyer flask was immediately sealed with a piece of textile, and the flask was weighed to determine the initial mass. The mass was subsequently recorded at regular time intervals. The increase in mass corresponds to the moisture absorbed by the desiccant from the air through the textile, which can be used to evaluate the moisture permeability of the textile.

Water vapor transmission rate test

The water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) of the textile was evaluated using the cup method, in accordance with the standard GB/T 12704.2-2009, Textiles—Test methods for water vapour permeability of fabrics—Part 2: Evaporation method. A volume of 50 mL of distilled water was added to the bottle, which was then sealed with the textile sample (the inner diameter of the bottle mouth is 5 cm). The sealed bottle was placed in a constant temperature and humidity chamber for 48 h, where the temperature was maintained at 38 °C and the relative humidity at 50%. The water loss was expressed as the percentage of the total amount of water evaporated through the fabric. Washability test

The washability of the SSRC textile was evaluated by simulating the rotational speed of a conventional washing machine. A washing solution was prepared by adding 3 mL of detergent to 250 mL of distilled water. The SSRC textile was washed in this solution at a rotational speed of 500 rpm for 200 min, followed by a 10-min rinse, and then air-dried at room temperature. Finally, the spectral properties of the washed SSRC textile were measured in both the solar and mid-infrared wavelength ranges.

Directional sweating performance test

For individuals engaged in outdoor activities, the majority of the textile surface worn is oriented vertically relative to the ground. Therefore, the textile was positioned vertically for the directional sweat-wicking test. The SSRC textile was vertically secured using a retort stand, and a rubber-tipped dropper was used to apply artificial sweat to the inner surface of the SSRC textile. The entire process, from the moment the sweat contacted the inner surface to when it permeated through to the outer surface and eventually dripped off, was recorded using a camera.

Outdoor cooling test

The cooling performance of the textile were conducted to test at two locations: the second-floor terrace of the Energy Building on the Jiulonghu Campus of Southeast University in Nanjing, and the rooftop of Building 4 in the Science and Technology Park (118.8°, 31.9°). The apparatus was constructed from polystyrene foam (20 × 20 × 6 cm), with its surface covered in aluminum foil to minimize radiant heat exchange. A cylindrical cavity (Φ6 × 2 cm) was cut into the center of the surface to accommodate the simulated skin, thermocouple, and textile. An electrical power input of 100 W/m2 was supplied to the simulated skin to mimic human metabolic heat generation. During the test, the textile was positioned vertically to the ground. The temperature of the textile was measured using three T-type thermocouples attached to the surface of the simulated skin. The ambient temperature was recorded using three T-type thermocouples placed inside a louvered radiation shield to prevent direct exposure to sunlight. In addition, a weather station continuously monitored solar radiation, ambient relative humidity, and wind speed. Experiments simulating wall surfaces using waterproof fabric were conducted on the rooftop of Building 4 in the Science and Technology Park. The cooling performance of the textile facing actual buildings was tested on the second-floor terrace of the Energy Building. Cooling performance tests were carried out with the textile oriented toward the south, north, and west walls of the building, respectively.
