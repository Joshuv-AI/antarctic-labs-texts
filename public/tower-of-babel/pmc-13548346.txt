Comparison of Thiotepa, Busulfan, and Cyclophosphamide Versus Busulfan and Thiotepa Conditioning Regimens in Autologous Stem Cell Transplantation for Primary CNS Lymphoma: A Multicenter Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Eun‐Jeong Jeong, Sang Eun Yoon, Sang‐A Kim, Ji Yun Lee, Seok Jin Kim, Soo‐Mee Bang, Won Seog Kim, Jeong‐Ok Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAutologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) with thiotepa‐based conditioning has replaced whole‐brain radiotherapy as consolidation for primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL), providing improved efficacy and reduced neurotoxicity. However, the optimal thiotepa‐containing regimen remains undefined. We retrospectively analyzed patients aged ≤ 60 years who underwent ASCT with thiotepa–busulfan–cyclophosphamide (TBC) or busulfan–thiotepa (BuTT) conditioning after high‐dose methotrexate–based induction at two Korean institutions between 2015 and 2023. Propensity score matching (PSM; n = 20 per group) was performed to adjust for baseline differences. Sixty‐seven patients were included (TBC, n = 30; BuTT, n = 37). TBC was associated with significantly longer progression‐free survival (PFS) from induction (3‐year: 89.9% vs. 72.1%, p = 0.034) and from transplantation (89.9% vs. 69.2%, p = 0.032); overall survival did not differ significantly. In multivariable Cox regression adjusted for pre‐transplant response and cumulative MTX exposure, BuTT was independently associated with inferior PFS (HR 4.09, 95% CI 1.27–13.18, p = 0.019). After PSM, TBC retained superior PFS (84.7% vs. 73.3%, p = 0.035), with a non‐significant trend in Cox analysis (HR 0.22, 95% CI 0.05–1.03, p = 0.054). TBC was associated with delayed engraftment and more grade ≥ 3 non‐hematologic toxicities, but no treatment‐related mortality occurred. In younger patients with PCNSL, TBC demonstrated a trend toward more durable disease control than BuTT, with manageable toxicity. As the thiotepa dose represents the major distinction between regimens, dose‐intensified thiotepa strategies with adjusted partner agents merit further prospective investigation.

Introduction

Primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL) is a rare, aggressive extranodal diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma [1]. High‐dose methotrexate (MTX)‐based chemotherapy is standard, but consolidation is required for durable disease control [2, 3]. Autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) has largely supplanted whole‐brain radiotherapy (WBRT) as consolidation, offering comparable or superior progression‐free survival (PFS) with markedly reduced neurotoxicity [4, 5]. Nonetheless, the optimal conditioning regimen remains unclear. Carmustine, etoposide, cytarabine, and melphalan (BEAM) has limited efficacy owing to poor CNS penetration [6, 7]. Thiotepa‐based regimens are preferred for achieving effective CNS concentrations [8, 9, 10, 11], although the optimal thiotepa dose and partner agents remain undefined. Common strategies combine thiotepa with carmustine (TT‐BCNU) or busulfan (BuTT), or add cyclophosphamide (thiotepa‐busulfan‐cyclophosphamide, TBC). TBC shows potent antitumor activity but higher risks of mucositis and infectious complications [12], and TT‐BCNU was associated with lower non‐relapse mortality and improved survival in older patients [13]. In Korea, limited BCNU availability and high cost have led most centers to adopt BuTT or TBC, with the choice largely determined by patient characteristics and institutional practice [14, 15, 16]. TBC is often selected for younger patients, raising concerns regarding age‐related selection bias. However, data directly comparing conditioning regimens in younger medically fit patients remain scarce. We therefore conducted a multicenter retrospective study of patients aged ≤ 60 years, comparing TBC and BuTT as consolidation for PCNSL undergoing ASCT.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

This retrospective multicenter cohort study was conducted at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (SNUBH) and Samsung Medical Center (SMC). Consecutive patients with PCNSL who underwent high‐dose chemotherapy followed by ASCT between 2015 and 2023, were identified from electronic medical records. Inclusion criteria were: (1) histologically confirmed diagnosis of PCNSL, or, if biopsy was not feasible, a diagnosis strongly suspected by characteristic imaging; (2) age ≤ 60 years at the time of diagnosis; (3) receipt of consolidative ASCT after high‐dose MTX‐based induction; (4) conditioning with either TBC or BuTT; and (5) availability of sufficient clinical data with a minimum follow‐up duration. Patients with secondary CNS involvement of systemic lymphoma, those receiving consolidation without ASCT or other conditioning regimens, or those with incomplete data were excluded. A total of 67 patients met these criteria (30 from SNUBH and 37 from SMC). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (SNUBH IRB number: B‐2506‐980‐106; SMC IRB number: 2016‐11‐040‐025), informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design, and the study followed the Declaration of Helsinki.

Treatment Protocols

All patients received rituximab, MTX, vincristine, and procarbazine (R‐MVP) induction, except two who received MVP without rituximab. Cycles were repeated every 2 weeks and comprised rituximab 375 mg/m2, vincristine 1.4 mg/m2 (maximum 2 mg), procarbazine 100 mg/m2 on days 1–7 of odd‐numbered cycles, and high‐dose MTX at 3.0 g/m2 for six cycles (SNUBH) or 3.5 g/m2 for five cycles (SMC) per institutional protocol. Patients who achieved complete or partial remission underwent high‐dose chemotherapy conditioning, followed by ASCT. Conditioning consisted of either TBC or BuTT. At SNUBH, TBC was preferentially administered to patients aged ≤ 60 years and BuTT to those > 60 years, whereas SMC used BuTT irrespective of age. The TBC regimen included thiotepa 5 mg/kg on days −9 to −7, busulfan 3.2 mg/kg intravenously on days −6 to −4, and cyclophosphamide 50–60 mg/kg intravenously on days −3 to −2. The BuTT regimen comprised busulfan 3.2 mg/kg intravenously on days −8 to −5, followed by thiotepa (5 mg/kg) intravenously on days −4 to −3.

Clinical Assessments and Definitions

Responses were evaluated per International Primary CNS Lymphoma Collaborative Group criteria using brain MRI and, when available, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cytology at interim (after 2–3 cycles) and end of induction, with follow‐up MRI 3‐monthly for 2 years, then 6‐monthly to 5 years [17]. Primary endpoints were overall survival (OS) and PFS, each measured from induction initiation (OS, PFS) and from ASCT (OS2, PFS2). OS was defined as time to death from any cause; PFS as time to disease progression, relapse, or death. Neutrophil engraftment was defined as the first of three consecutive days with an absolute neutrophil count exceeding 500/μL, and platelet engraftment as the first of three consecutive days with a platelet count ≥ 20,000/μL without transfusion. The length of hospitalization was defined as the interval from the date of stem cell infusion to the date of discharge. Treatment‐related toxicities were graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared using tests appropriate for variable type and distribution. OS/OS2 and PFS/PFS2 were estimated by Kaplan‐Meier method and compared by log‐rank test. Multivariable Cox models used a small number of covariates specified a priori rather than by univariate screening, because the limited number of events precluded a more extensive model: conditioning regimen as the exposure of interest, plus pre‐transplant response status and cumulative body surface area (BSA)‐adjusted MTX dose to account for institutional differences in induction. Induction cycle number was not included, being determined by institutional protocol and therefore collinear with the conditioning regimen (concordance 65/67, 97.0%). With only seven deaths, multivariable modeling was not performed for OS, and univariate estimates are reported. Propensity score matching (PSM) addressed baseline imbalances between groups. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression including age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) at ASCT, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level, Hans classification, Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation‐specific Comorbidity Index (HCT‐CI), ocular involvement, deep structure involvement, and multiple lesion involvement. Although CSF protein level is a validated prognostic component of the International Extranodal Lymphoma Study Group (IELSG) score, it was excluded from the PSM model because CSF protein data were available for only 43 of 67 patients (64.2%); a covariate with ∼36% missing data would have substantially reduced the matched sample and risked bias. Patients were matched 1:1 using optimal matching without replacement, with age as an exact covariate (10‐year intervals), yielding 40 matched patients (20 per group). Covariate balance was confirmed (all Standardized Mean Differences (SMD) < 0.1). Matched‐cohort survival was re‐estimated by Kaplan‐Meier method and compared with stratified log‐rank tests. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained from Cox models stratified by matched subclass, with conditioning as the sole exposure. Post hoc power was calculated using Schoenfeld's approximation from observed hazard ratios and event counts. All tests were two‐sided (p < 0.05). Analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Sixty‐seven patients were analyzed (TBC, n = 30; BuTT, n = 37; Table 1). TBC patients were older (median 55 years, range 20–60) than BuTT patients (median 50 years, range 34–60, p = 0.011). Most had a favorable PS at the time of ASCT (ECOG 0–1 in 98.5%). Hans classification distribution was similar between groups. Median LDH was higher in BuTT, though the proportion with abnormal LDH did not differ significantly (23.3% vs. 45.9%, p = 0.096). Other disease features did not differ between groups. The TBC group had a higher median HCT‐CI score (median 1, range 0–6) than the BuTT group (median 0, range 0–3; p < 0.001), though the proportion with HCT‐CI ≥ 3 did not differ significantly (16.7% vs. 5.4%, p = 0.229).

Table 2 summarizes induction details. Conditioning Regimen and Transplantation Outcomes

Table 3 summarizes the conditioning details and transplantation outcomes. Disease status at ASCT did not differ, with complete response (CR)/CR unconfirmed in 66.7% and 81.1% of TBC and BuTT patients, respectively (p = 0.286). The conditioning agent doses varied by regimen: TBC delivered higher thiotepa (mean 15.2 vs. 9.9 mg/kg, p < 0.001) but lower busulfan exposure (mean 9.0 vs. 12.4 mg/kg, p < 0.001) than BuTT, with cyclophosphamide included only in TBC (mean 93.9 mg/kg). Both groups engrafted favorably; however, median time to neutrophil recovery (11 vs. 9 days, p < 0.001) and platelet recovery (16.5 vs. 8 days, p < 0.001) were significantly longer in the TBC group. Post‐transplant hospitalization did not differ between groups (mean 12.9 vs. 14.1 days, p = 0.396). Serious infection‐related adverse events occurred in 26.7% and 13.5% of TBC and BuTT patients, respectively (p = 0.297). Grade ≥ 3 non‐hematologic adverse events were more frequent with TBC (46.7% vs. 13.5%, p = 0.007), with no treatment‐related mortality in either group.

Survival Outcomes

At a median follow‐up of 63.7 months, the 3‐year PFS from induction was 89.9% (95% CI, 79.6%–100.0%) with TBC versus 72.1% (95% CI, 58.8%–88.4%) with BuTT (p = 0.034; Figure 1A); OS did not differ significantly (100% vs. 91.5% (95% CI, 82.8%–100.0%), p = 0.42; Figure 1B). When analyzed from ASCT, results were consistent: 3‐year PFS2 was 89.9% (95% CI, 79.6%–100.0%) versus 69.2% (95% CI, 55.5%–86.2%) (p = 0.032; Figure 2A) and OS2 did not differ significantly (100% vs. 88.6% (95% CI, 78.6%–99.8%), p = 0.38; Figure 2B). In multivariable Cox regression adjusted for pre‐transplant response status and cumulative MTX/BSA dose, BuTT was independently associated with inferior PFS (HR 4.09, 95% CI 1.27–13.18, p = 0.019; Table 4).

PSM Analysis

After PSM, 20 patients per group were well‐balanced (SMD < 0.1 for all covariates). In the matched cohort, TBC was associated with improved PFS compared with BuTT. Three‐year PFS rates were 84.7% (95% CI, 70.2%–100.0%) with TBC versus 73.3% (95% CI, 55.7%–96.4%) with BuTT (stratified log‐rank, p = 0.035; Figure 3A). Corresponding 3‐year OS rates were 100.0% (95% CI, 100.0%–100.0%) versus 89.2% (95% CI, 76.0%–100.0%), respectively (stratified log‐rank, p = 0.18; Figure 3B). Cox regression revealed a non‐significant trend toward inferior PFS with BuTT compared with TBC (HR 0.22, 95% CI 0.05–1.03, p = 0.054; Table 5). No significant difference in OS was observed between groups (HR 0.25, 95% CI 0.03–2.24, p = 0.215; Table 5). Post hoc power calculations using Schoenfeld's approximation revealed that the matched cohort was substantially underpowered: the estimated statistical power was 57% for PFS (HR 0.22; 8 events) and 17% for OS (HR 0.25; 2 events).

Treatments Post‐Relapse

At SNUBH, four patients experienced relapse: two received WBRT combined with high‐dose MTX‐based regimens, one received WBRT plus ibrutinib and intrathecal triple therapy, and one was lost to follow‐up. At SMC, 14 relapsed patients received heterogeneous salvage approaches, including immunomodulatory regimens (rituximab‐lenalidomide, n = 4; lenalidomide monotherapy, n = 2), cytotoxic chemotherapy (MVP, n = 2; ifosfamide, carboplatin, and etoposide [ICE]/rituximab‐ICE, n = 3; cytarabine, n = 1), and other approaches such as bevacizumab (n = 1) or intrathecal triple therapy alone (n = 1). Subsequently, several patients received novel targeted or immune‐based therapies, including an ibrutinib‐everolimus combination, nivolumab, tirabrutinib, and chimeric antigen receptor T‐cell therapy (tisagenlecleucel).

Discussion

Registry studies have established thiotepa‐based conditioning in ASCT for PCNSL. A Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research (CIBMTR) analysis of 603 patients showed TBC and TT‐BCNU achieved superior disease control over BEAM (3‐year PFS 75%–76% vs. 58%), primarily from lower relapse rates [11]. In patients aged ≤ 59 years, TBC achieved superior PFS (86% vs. 79%) and OS over TT‐BCNU, with comparable non‐relapse mortality (NRM); conversely, in patients ≥ 65 years, TT‐BCNU was associated with lower NRM and improved survival, underscoring the need to tailor regimen intensity to patient age and physiologic reserve [13].

Because BCNU is unavailable in Korea, our cohort used busulfan as the partner agent. BCNU carries pulmonary toxicity in up to 26% of patients receiving BCNU‐containing regimens [18, 19], whereas busulfan predominantly causes mucositis, gastrointestinal toxicity, and sinusoidal obstruction syndrome [20]. These differences may differentially affect patient tolerability and post‐transplant recovery. Direct BuTT‐versus‐TT‐BCNU data are lacking, but an indirect comparison with the CIBMTR analysis by Scordo et al. [11] is informative. In patients aged ≤ 59 years, 3‐year PFS was 79% with TT‐BCNU (thiotepa median 20 mg/kg, range 10–20) in the Scordo et al. cohort, versus 72.1% in our BuTT cohort (thiotepa mean 9.9 mg/kg, range 8.7–10.0; ≤ 60 years). This difference may partly reflect the substantially lower thiotepa dose intensity in our BuTT cohort, although the distinct pharmacological profiles of busulfan versus BCNU may also contribute; the relative contribution cannot be determined from indirect comparison alone.

Thiotepa dosing varies considerably across regimens and institutions, and the relationship between dose and outcome remains incompletely defined. In the CIBMTR registry, TBC used a median of 18 mg/kg (range 16–19) and TT‐BCNU 20 mg/kg (range 10–20). A CIBMTR dataset analysis comparing 10 versus 20 mg/kg within the TT‐BCNU platform found no significant outcome differences, though a non‐significant trend favored higher doses without substantially increased toxicity [21]. On the other hand, favorable outcomes have been reported at higher thiotepa exposure with both BuTT (≈ 15–17 mg/kg; 3‐year OS and PFS > 90%, no treatment‐related mortality [TRM]) [22] and TBC (≈ 18–20 mg/kg; 2‐year PFS 84.7%), albeit with increased gastrointestinal toxicities in the latter [23]. Together, these data suggest that the optimal thiotepa dose and partner agent combination—balancing efficacy and toxicity—warrants prospective investigation. These cross‐study comparisons are limited by differences in patient selection, induction, and supportive care; furthermore, limited BCNU availability in Korea restricts generalizability to settings where TT‐BCNU is standard.

MATRix (methotrexate, cytarabine, thiotepa, and rituximab) is widely adopted as induction in fit patients across European centers; within IELSG32, MATRix achieved significantly higher CR rates than thiotepa‐free arms, and patients receiving MATRix followed by consolidation achieved a 7‐year OS of 70% [24], suggesting that thiotepa in induction contributes to deeper pre‐ASCT disease control. Furthermore, although the induction thiotepa dose itself (120 mg/m2 total over four cycles) is modest, the induction regimen may influence cumulative thiotepa exposure, potentially affecting the tolerability and relative benefit of subsequent conditioning. Because our patients received R‐MVP induction without thiotepa, direct comparison with MATRix‐based programs is not appropriate, and our findings should not be generalized to MATRix‐standard settings.

Direct comparisons between TBC and BuTT have not been reported previously. Here, TBC achieved superior PFS (89.9% vs. 72.1%, p = 0.034), consistent across both time points. Despite less favorable baseline characteristics (older age, higher HCT‐CI, more partial responses), TBC retained superior PFS after PSM (84.7% vs. 73.3%, p = 0.035), with a non‐significant trend in Cox analysis (HR 0.22, 95% CI 0.05–1.03, p = 0.054). OS did not differ significantly, likely reflecting effective salvage therapies; nevertheless, similar OS rates do not necessarily offset the clinical burden associated with inferior disease control and the need for salvage treatment. TBC was associated with delayed engraftment and more grade ≥ 3 non‐hematologic events, yet nearly all patients engrafted by day +30 with no TRM. Notably, despite delayed platelet recovery, hospitalization was comparable (mean 12.9 vs. 14.1 days, p = 0.396), suggesting this toxicity did not increase inpatient burden.

The mechanism underlying these efficacy differences merits consideration. In our cohort, the PFS advantage of TBC is likely driven primarily by the higher thiotepa dose (mean 15.2 vs. 9.9 mg/kg); notably, busulfan exposure was higher in BuTT (mean 12.4 vs. 9.0 mg/kg), yet BuTT remained inferior, suggesting busulfan dose alone does not explain the gap. Thiotepa intensification thus appears the primary driver, while the optimal busulfan dose within thiotepa‐based platforms remains undefined. Indirect evidence from the CIBMTR registry, where TBC achieved superior PFS over TT‐BCNU despite comparable thiotepa doses (median 18 vs. 20 mg/kg) [11], raises the additional possibility that the partner agent—busulfan versus BCNU—may differentially influence CNS disease control. Busulfan achieves CSF concentrations of approximately 80% of plasma levels [25], potentially exceeding BCNU, though this requires prospective confirmation. Cyclophosphamide penetrates the CNS only modestly (∼20%–30% of plasma) and contributes primarily through systemic myeloablation [26, 27], and its risk‐benefit profile in this context warrants further evaluation.

Our findings must be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, the retrospective design carries inherent risks of selection bias and residual confounding that PSM cannot fully eliminate. Unmeasured factors—physiologic reserve not fully captured by HCT‐CI, institutional differences in supportive care, and center‐level variability in regimen selection—may have influenced outcomes and could account for some or all of the PFS difference. Second, the modest sample size—particularly after PSM (n = 20 per group)—limits statistical power; post hoc analysis confirmed the study was underpowered for PFS (57%) and markedly for OS (17%), so results are hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive. Third, toxicity assessment was restricted to acute events; long‐term neurocognitive outcomes and quality‐of‐life data were not collected, limiting characterization of tolerability. Fourth, CSF protein—a validated IELSG component—was excluded from the PSM model owing to incompleteness (64.2% available), potentially introducing residual prognostic imbalance. Fifth, the number of induction cycles was set by institutional protocol and thus inseparable from the conditioning regimen; no BuTT patient received six cycles and only two TBC patients received five, precluding stratified comparison. Although cumulative MTX/BSA exposure was balanced and non‐prognostic after adjustment, and pre‐transplant disease status did not differ significantly, residual confounding related to institutional practice cannot be excluded. Sixth, with only seven deaths, multivariable analysis of OS was not statistically supportable; OS comparisons should be regarded as exploratory. The events‐per‐variable ratio for the PFS model likewise falls below conventional thresholds, so these estimates are hypothesis‐generating.

Conclusion

In younger patients with PCNSL, TBC demonstrated a trend toward more durable disease control compared with BuTT, with clinically manageable toxicities under modern supportive care. However, given the retrospective design, modest sample size, and lack of long‐term toxicity data, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than practice‐defining. As the thiotepa dose is the major distinction between regimens, dose‐intensified thiotepa with adjusted partner agents merits further study. Prospective trials of dose‐intensified thiotepa with optimized partner agents—incorporating systematic neurocognitive and quality‐of‐life assessment—are warranted to define the optimal conditioning strategy for younger PCNSL patients.
