Adoption of intraoperative mechanomyography as a spinal decompression endpoint: A single-surgeon comparison by era
Authors: Ross Jones, David Nay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntraoperative adequacy of spinal decompression is typically judged by visual inspection and tactile assessment rather than objective physiologic confirmation, leaving no objective endpoint for completion. We compared outcomes before and after a single surgeon adopted routine intraoperative mechanomyography (MMG).MethodsIn this retrospective single-surgeon, single-institution, era-based cohort, 600 consecutive adult spinal decompression patients were analyzed: 300 before adoption and 300 after stable 100% MMG utilization. The preadoption era used visual and tactile assessment; the postadoption era used MMG threshold-guided decompression targeted at ≤2.0 mA. The primary endpoint was 90-day unplanned return to the operating room (RTOR); confirmatory endpoints were incidental durotomy and 3-month pain. Adjusted regression controlled for demographic and procedural covariates; a post hoc classification distinguished decompression-related from nondecompression RTOR, with sparse subcategories analyzed by Fisher exact tests and a multinomial interaction test.ResultsRTOR decreased from 29/300 (9.7%) to 8/300 (2.7%; adjusted OR 0.245, 95% CI 0.106–0.566, p=.001). Decompression-related RTOR decreased from 4.7% to 1.0% (p=.012) and nondecompression RTOR from 5.0% to 1.7% (p=.038); the differential reduction was not significant (interaction p=.590). Durotomy decreased from 9.7% to 2.3% (adjusted OR 0.246, p=.002), mean 3-month pain from 4.91 to 1.93 (adjusted β −3.09, p<.001), and treatment nonresponse from 45.7% to 7.3%.ConclusionsRoutine MMG adoption was associated with lower RTOR, durotomy, pain, and treatment nonresponse. Because nondecompression RTOR also decreased, and case volume doubled between eras, causal attribution to MMG alone is not possible. These findings are hypothesis-generating and support prospective multicenter validation with patient-level threshold capture.Keywords: Mechanomyography, Spinal decompression, Nerve root, Reoperation, Durotomy, Treatment nonresponse, Minimal clinically important difference, Revision surgery

Introduction

A central challenge in spinal decompression surgery is determining, before closure, whether neural decompression is sufficient. Although decompression aims to relieve symptomatic nerve-root compression while avoiding unnecessary tissue disruption, adequacy is still judged predominantly through anatomical exposure, visual inspection, and tactile assessment rather than through objective physiologic confirmation. That intraoperative information gap is clinically important because the surgeon faces 2 opposing risks. In an underdecompressed case, the surgeon stops before the nerve root is physiologically free, and patients may experience persistent symptoms, recurrent radiculopathy, or early reoperation. In an overdecompressed case, the surgeon removes more tissue than necessary—the nerve is free, but the aggressive dissection puts adjacent structures at risk, including the dura, facet joints, and stabilizing ligaments. Spine surgery has increasingly effective tools for confirming anatomical decompression, but no standard method for confirming physiologic decompression—the nerve may look free without functioning as free.

A diagnostic system that tells the surgeon when to stop could address both failure modes. If thresholds remain elevated, the nerve root is still compressed, and further decompression is warranted. If thresholds have normalized, the nerve is physiologically free, and the surgeon has an objective basis for concluding the procedure—rather than continuing exploratory tissue removal based on subjective judgment alone. The original study protocol was designed to test whether such feedback could standardize technique across 2 surgeons with different baseline practice patterns; however, the final cohort includes only 1 surgeon and cannot test that intersurgeon hypothesis.

The physiologic basis for this approach is well established. Chronically compressed nerve roots require higher electrical stimulus intensities for activation than noncompressed roots [1]. Wessell et al. [2] demonstrated that pre and postdecompression threshold changes measured by mechanomyography (MMG) can verify decompression intraoperatively. More recently, Sommer et al. [3] reported implementation feasibility across minimally invasive spine procedures and described a case in which persistently elevated thresholds prompted further exploration, revealing a residual disc fragment whose removal reduced the stimulation threshold.

A prior 42-patient prospective pilot study linked intraoperative MMG threshold metrics to early postoperative pain outcomes, finding that threshold-anchored measures aligned more closely with pain improvement than percentage-change measures [4]. These studies provide a mechanistic and technical foundation for MMG as a decompression confirmation tool, but they do not establish whether routine MMG adoption is associated with improved clinical outcomes at scale.

The purpose of the present study was to compare outcomes before and after the routine adoption of intraoperative MMG by a single high-volume spine surgeon. The prespecified primary endpoint was 90-day unplanned return to the operating room (RTOR). Because MMG is expected to influence decompression adequacy rather than all surgical complications, the study also includes a post hoc classification of RTOR by mechanism, distinguishing decompression-related from nondecompression-related returns. The study is observational and intended to provide effect-size estimates and design guidance for prospective multicenter validation.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a single-center retrospective era-based cohort study conducted at a tertiary referral institution. The study was approved by the institutional review board with waiver of informed consent under 45 CFR 46.116(f). The manuscript was prepared with reference to the STROBE guidelines for observational cohort studies and the RECORD statement for routinely collected health data [5,6].

The analytic comparison was restricted to 2 clean exposure eras: a preadoption period with 0% MMG utilization and a postadoption period with 100% MMG utilization. The design, therefore, estimates outcomes associated with routine MMG adoption, not the causal effect of MMG independent of era, surgeon maturation, case volume, or care pathway changes.

Study population

A consecutive cohort of adult spinal decompression patients was retrospectively identified from institutional records of a single surgeon. Eligible patients were aged 18 years or older and underwent elective spinal decompression surgery, including cervical or lumbar foraminotomy, discectomy or microdiscectomy, laminectomy or hemilaminectomy, or spinal fusion with a decompression component. Patients with fewer than 90 days of follow-up were excluded; no patient meeting procedural criteria was excluded for insufficient follow-up.

The preadoption era comprised cases from January 2018 through October 2019. The postadoption era comprised cases from January 2022 through November 2022. The intervening period was excluded because it encompassed the COVID-19 pandemic disruption and early MMG adoption, during which MMG utilization was inconsistent, and the surgeon was integrating threshold-guided decision-making into the operative workflow. This exclusion preserved a clean 0%-vs-100% exposure contrast, but it creates an important risk of temporal confounding because other practice changes—including changes in instruments, anesthesia, staffing, discharge pathways, referral patterns, and patient selection—may have occurred during the gap.

Neuromonitoring comparator and MMG workflow

During the preadoption era, decompression adequacy was assessed by visual and tactile judgment alone. No MMG technology was available or used. Based on surgeon's review, no neuromonitoring modality was used as an intraoperative endpoint for determining whether decompression was complete. Neuromonitoring used for other indications in individual instrumented cases, such as triggered electromyography (EMG) for pedicle screw assessment, was not used to guide or confirm decompression adequacy.

During postadoption cases, the surgeon used a commercially available MMG device (Sentio MMG, DePuy Synthes) to measure nerve-root stimulation thresholds, defined as the minimum direct electrical stimulation intensity required to elicit a mechanical response in a muscle innervated by the target nerve root. Noninvasive accelerometer sensors were placed on relevant muscle groups. After exposure, target roots were stimulated, and thresholds were reassessed during decompression. Decompression was considered physiologically adequate when target thresholds reached ≤2.0 mA, consistent with published normal-function thresholds [2]. Persistently elevated thresholds prompted reinspection and, when clinically appropriate, additional decompression; threshold normalization provided a physiologic basis for concluding the procedure.

Although baseline and final thresholds were assessed intraoperatively as part of the surgical workflow, individual threshold trajectories were not stored in structured fields retrievable for this retrospective analysis. The study, therefore, cannot determine how many roots began at or below the 2.0 mA target, quantify threshold change, identify cases in which thresholds prompted additional decompression, or test whether outcome differences were mediated by threshold normalization.

Outcomes and testing hierarchy

The primary outcome was 90-day unplanned RTOR, defined as any unplanned spine-related operative procedure within 90 days of the index surgery. Planned staged procedures documented in the original operative plan were excluded. Reoperation decisions were not protocol-directed; they reflected clinical findings, patient-reported symptoms, imaging when available, and the operating surgeon's judgment. The same clinical decision framework was applied in both eras. Early decompression-related RTOR is therefore heterogeneous and may include acute neurologic deterioration as well as elective revision for persistent symptoms. RTOR and index-level revision were tracked independently. Most RTOR events involved acute perioperative complications or early neurologic deterioration, whereas most revision events occurred after the 90-day RTOR window.

RTOR indications were classified post hoc from charted reoperation reasons into 2 categories. Decompression-related RTOR included persistent or recurrent radiculopathy, residual or recurrent stenosis, neurogenic claudication, cauda equina syndrome, foot drop, paralysis, weakness, or advancing neurologic deficit. Nondecompression RTOR included hematoma or seroma evacuation, wound complications, infection or debridement, hardware or mechanical failure, cerebrospinal fluid collection, esophageal tear, or other indications without a direct relationship to decompression adequacy. Classification was applied in 2 steps: the reoperation indication was abstracted verbatim from the operative record by a medical assistant with chart access who was not involved in the analysis, and each indication was then mapped to 1 of the 2 predefined categories during analysis, unblinded to era and to the study hypothesis. The robustness of this classification to misclassification was examined in a sensitivity analysis (Results).

A sequential confirmatory testing hierarchy was applied after the primary endpoint: (1) incidental durotomy and (2) pain at approximately 3 months measured on a 0–10 Numeric Rating Scale (NRS). Testing proceeded to the next endpoint only if the preceding endpoint reached a two-sided alpha=0.05. Incidental durotomy was included as a confirmatory endpoint because it relates to the overdecompression side of the problem. MMG does not directly detect or prevent dural injury. The hypothesized mechanism is indirect: when MMG confirms that the nerve root is physiologically free, the surgeon has an objective reason to stop––rather than continuing bone, ligamentous, or neural-adjacent dissection in pursuit of subjective adequacy. That additional tissue work, performed after physiologic decompression has already been achieved, is what increases durotomy risk. Because individual threshold trajectories were unavailable, this mechanism remains inferential.

Exploratory endpoints included hematoma requiring evacuation; infection; any surgical complication (durotomy, hematoma, or infection during the index surgical episode); 6-week pain; 3-month minimal clinically important difference (MCID) achievement (≥2-point NRS improvement from baseline); treatment nonresponse (failure to achieve MCID); same-day discharge; length of stay; index-level revision at 12 and 24 months; and subsequent fusion within 24 months. A ≥2-point NRS improvement was selected as a clinically interpretable pain-response threshold based on prior pain-scale literature [7]. Infection was treated as a specificity comparator because it lacks a mechanistic relationship to decompression adequacy, but the very low event rate limits its inferential value.

Missing data and follow-up

Pain scores at 6 weeks and 3 months were available for all 600 patients from structured electronic health record (EHR) fields. Revision and fusion status at 12 and 24 months was ascertained for all patients through institutional operative records, including the affiliated ambulatory surgery center. Data were abstracted in 2026, providing at least 40 months of follow-up for every postadoption patient, so the 24-month revision and fusion endpoints were fully observable in both eras. Events occurring outside the institutional system were not systematically captured. No imputation was performed; analyses used complete-case data for each endpoint.

Statistical analysis

Two-sided tests at alpha=0.05 were used throughout. Fisher exact tests were used for unadjusted binary comparisons, independent-samples t tests for continuous comparisons, and Mann-Whitney U testing for ordinal pain distributions. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated for baseline balance.

Adjusted analyses used 2 covariate specifications. The base model included era, age, sex, procedure type, and number of spinal levels. The full model added smoking status, diabetes, anatomical region, preoperative pain, and preoperative motor strength. Logistic regression with Huber–White HC3 robust standard errors was used for binary outcomes; ordinary least squares regression with HC3 standard errors for continuous pain scores; and Poisson regression with HC3 standard errors for length of stay. Full-model results are reported as the primary adjusted estimates, with base-model results used to assess stability across covariate specification.

Because decompression-related and nondecompression-related RTOR were sparse subcategories, they were compared with Fisher exact tests rather than full multivariable models. A multinomial logistic model with no RTOR as the base category tested whether the era association differed between decompression-related and nondecompression RTOR categories. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied to exploratory endpoints; those findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. De-identified data and analysis code are provided in Supplementary Materials.

Protocol deviations

The IRB-approved study protocol specified a 2-surgeon, tiered-accrual design with a 4-step confirmatory hierarchy (RTOR, durotomy, patient satisfaction, pain at 3 months). This manuscript reports a single-surgeon Tier-1 cohort. The second surgeon was excluded before data abstraction because complete medical record access was unavailable. Patient satisfaction was excluded because structured data were available for fewer than 17% of patients, below the protocol’s prespecified completeness threshold. The confirmatory hierarchy was therefore narrowed to durotomy and 3-month pain. These modifications were determined before outcome data abstraction.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

Six hundred consecutive patients met the inclusion criteria: 300 in the preadoption era and 300 in the postadoption era. The preadoption cohort spanned 22 calendar months, with approximately 13.6 cases per month; the postadoption cohort spanned 11 calendar months, with approximately 27.3 cases per month.

Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. Groups were well matched on age, sex, diabetes, region, instrumentation, and preoperative foot drop. Meaningful imbalances were present for smoking, number of spinal levels, preoperative pain, and preoperative strength. The postadoption era had fewer smokers (20.7% vs 9.0%; SMD −0.33) and fewer treated levels (2.66 vs 2.34; SMD −0.34), both of which could bias toward better postadoption outcomes. The postadoption era also had higher preoperative pain (6.93 vs 7.72; SMD 0.40). Higher baseline pain biases against finding lower absolute postoperative scores but provides more room for improvement, which can inflate change-based MCID achievement (addressed in Discussion).

Primary outcome: 90-day unplanned RTOR

The primary endpoint occurred in 29/300 preadoption patients (9.7%) and 8/300 postadoption patients (2.7%), yielding an absolute risk reduction of 7.0% (95% CI 3.2%–10.8%), number needed to treat of 14.3, and Fisher exact p=.0005. The base adjusted model yielded OR 0.269 (95% CI 0.118–0.613, p=.002). The full adjusted model yielded OR 0.245 (95% CI 0.106–0.566, p=.001).

Mechanism-specific RTOR classification is shown in Table 2. Decompression-related RTOR decreased from 14/300 (4.7%) to 3/300 (1.0%; Fisher p=.012). Nondecompression RTOR also decreased from 15/300 (5.0%) to 5/300 (1.7%; Fisher p=.038). Although the relative reduction was numerically larger for decompression-related RTOR, a formal multinomial interaction test did not demonstrate a significant difference in the magnitude of reduction between categories (p=.590). The RTOR pattern is therefore compatible with a decompression-specific MMG signal, but it does not prove decompression-specific causality because nondecompression events also improved.

Overlap between incidental durotomy and RTOR was limited. In the preadoption era, 2 of 29 RTOR patients also had an incidental durotomy, and only 1 returned for a likely durotomy-related indication (cerebrospinal fluid collection). In the postadoption era, none of the 8 RTOR patients had a durotomy. The RTOR reduction was therefore not driven by fewer durotomy-related returns.

Among the 3 decompression-related postadoption RTOR cases, indications were severe spinal stenosis with neurogenic claudication, cauda equina syndrome, and paralysis of the left leg. These cases represent clinically heterogeneous early returns, and individual MMG threshold data were unavailable to determine whether threshold normalization occurred or whether the event represented a false-negative MMG assessment. None of the 8 postadoption RTOR patients required later revision or subsequent fusion within 24 months, and their mean 3-month pain score was 1.9/10.

Because postadoption RTOR counts were small, 2 analyses examined the classification's robustness. Of the 37 RTOR events, only 2 charted indications required a judgment beyond the documented reason (“cauda equina and seroma” and “spondylolisthesis,” both preadoption); the 3 postadoption decompression-related events were clinically unambiguous. Reclassifying either ambiguous preadoption case left the decompression-related reduction significant (Fisher p=.007–0.020), and reassigning a single postadoption event in either direction also left it significant (p=.004–0.028), whereas the nondecompression reduction lost significance when a postadoption event was reassigned into that category (p=.073). The decompression-related signal was therefore robust to misclassification; the nondecompression reduction was the more fragile.

Confirmatory endpoints

Because the primary endpoint was significant, confirmatory testing proceeded per the sequential hierarchy.

Incidental durotomy

Durotomy occurred in 29/300 preadoption patients (9.7%) and 7/300 postadoption patients (2.3%), with an absolute risk reduction of 7.3% (95% CI 3.6%–11.1%), number needed to treat of 13.6, and Fisher exact p=.0002. The base adjusted model yielded OR 0.234 (95% CI 0.098–0.556, p=.001), and the full adjusted model yielded OR 0.246 (95% CI 0.103–0.587, p=.002).

Pain at 3 months

Mean 3-month NRS pain decreased from 4.91±2.72 to 1.93±2.03. The full adjusted model showed a 3.09-point lower mean pain score in the postadoption era (beta −3.092, 95% CI −3.508 to −2.677, p<.001; model R-squared=0.304). Median pain decreased from 5 to 2, and the overall ordinal distribution differed significantly between eras (Mann-Whitney U=72,334.5, p<.001). Pain-free status increased from 26/300 (8.7%) to 103/300 (34.3%), whereas severe pain (NRS 7–10) decreased from 89/300 (29.7%) to 12/300 (4.0%) (Figure 1).

Specificity comparator: infection

Surgical site infection occurred in 4/300 preadoption patients (1.3%) and 1/300 postadoption patients (0.3%; Fisher p=.373). With only 5 total infection events, this comparison is substantially underpowered. The absence of a significant change should not be interpreted as evidence of decompression specificity; it only fails to contradict that hypothesis.

Same-day discharge increased and length of stay decreased substantially, but these operational endpoints are especially vulnerable to secular outpatient trends and should not be attributed to MMG.

Smoking sensitivity analysis

Because smoking prevalence differed between eras, a stratified sensitivity analysis was performed for the primary endpoint. In the preadoption era, RTOR rates were identical among current smokers and nonsmokers: 6/62 (9.7%) versus 23/238 (9.7%). In the postadoption era, RTOR occurred in 0/27 smokers and 8/273 nonsmokers (2.9%). These sparse subgroup counts do not establish the absence of confounding, but they make smoking imbalance alone an unlikely explanation for the observed RTOR reduction.

Subgroup analysis by procedure type

Results stratified by procedure type are shown in Table 4. RTOR decreased significantly in instrumented cases (12.9%–2.0%, p<.001) but did not reach statistical significance in noninstrumented cases (6.9%–3.4%, p=.205). Durotomy, any complication, cumulative revision, pain, MCID achievement, and treatment nonresponse improved significantly in both strata. Subsequent fusion decreased significantly among noninstrumented cases (20.0%–8.8%, p=.006).

Temporal sensitivity analysis

Monthly preadoption RTOR rates showed no evidence of a downward trend (slope approximately 0, p=.982). Linear extrapolation of the pre-era RTOR trend to the beginning of the postadoption era projected an RTOR rate of approximately 10.3%, similar to the observed preadoption mean and substantially above the observed postadoption monthly mean of 2.5%. postadoption RTOR rates were stable across the post-era (slope p=.152).

Durotomy and pain had borderline preadoption improvement trends (durotomy slope p=.050; pain slope p=.053). These trends create greater temporal vulnerability for those endpoints than for RTOR. However, postadoption changes were larger than the observed pre-era drift, and postadoption durotomy and pain values were stable during the 2022 era. The temporal analyses should therefore be interpreted as supportive for the primary endpoint and partially supportive, but not definitive, for confirmatory pain and durotomy outcomes.

Discussion

In this 600-patient retrospective single-surgeon era-based cohort, routine adoption of intraoperative MMG was associated with significantly lower 90-day RTOR, lower incidental durotomy, and lower 3-month pain. Decompression-related RTOR decreased from 4.7% to 1.0%. However, nondecompression RTOR also decreased significantly, and the formal interaction test was not significant. The findings are best interpreted as a clinically important adoption-era association that is compatible with a decompression-specific MMG contribution but not sufficient to establish MMG causality independent of secular practice changes.

Physiologic decompression window: underdecompression and overdecompression

Intraoperative decompression can fail in 2 directions. If decompression stops before the nerve root is physiologically free, patients may experience persistent symptoms, recurrent radiculopathy, or early decompression-related reoperation—this is underdecompression. If the surgeon continues bone, ligamentous, or neural-adjacent dissection after the nerve is already physiologically free, the additional tissue work puts the dura, facet joints, and stabilizing structures at unnecessary risk—this is overdecompression. MMG may address both failure modes through a single mechanism: the surgeon has an objective signal for when to stop. Elevated thresholds support continued decompression; normalized thresholds support stopping.

This framework explains why incidental durotomy was included as a confirmatory endpoint. MMG would not be expected to prevent durotomy by detecting dural compromise directly. Rather, threshold normalization gives the surgeon an objective reason to conclude the procedure, reducing the exploratory dissection that creates durotomy risk. The observed durotomy reduction is consistent with this mechanism, but it does not prove mediation because patient-level threshold data were unavailable.

The data support treating durotomy and decompression-related RTOR as separate event streams. In the preadoption era, only 2 of 29 RTOR patients also had a durotomy, and only 1 return appeared directly attributable to a dural injury. In the postadoption era, none of the 8 RTOR patients had a durotomy. These are different failure modes—underdecompression and overdecompression—that objective threshold-guided stopping could theoretically reduce through a single mechanism.

This same framework clarifies the limits of interpretation. Decompression-related RTOR, pain response, treatment nonresponse, and durotomy are all plausibly connected to whether the surgeon stopped at the right point. Infection, wound dehiscence, hardware failure, and other nondecompression complications are not. Their improvement in this cohort likely reflects secular practice changes, higher case volume, or other unmeasured factors.

Decompression-specific RTOR and its limits

The most directly relevant RTOR endpoint for evaluating MMG is not all-cause RTOR but return for persistent or recurrent neurologic symptoms attributable to decompression adequacy. In this analysis, decompression-related RTOR decreased by 79% (14/300 to 3/300, p=.012).

At the same time, nondecompression RTOR also decreased significantly (15/300 to 5/300, p=.038), and the formal interaction test was not significant (p=.590). This limits any strong specificity claim. Hematoma, wound complications, hardware failure, and debridement have no direct physiologic link to nerve-root threshold normalization. Their improvement suggests that factors beyond MMG—including higher surgical volume, technical maturation, and perioperative workflow changes—likely contributed to the broader outcome pattern. The appropriate conclusion is not that MMG explains all improved outcomes, but that the decompression-related findings provide a plausible signal warranting prospective validation.

Treatment nonresponse and clinical significance

Although RTOR was the prespecified primary endpoint, the reduction in treatment nonresponse may be a clinically meaningful patient-centered finding. In the preadoption cohort, 45.7% of patients failed to achieve a 2-point NRS improvement by 3 months. After MMG adoption, treatment nonresponse fell to 7.3%—a shift from nearly 1 in 2 patients failing to achieve meaningful pain relief to fewer than 1 in 13.

This preadoption nonresponse rate is consistent with published benchmarks. In the Michigan Spine Surgery Improvement Collaborative, Hamilton et al. [8] reported that 40.8% of 10,922 elective lumbar surgery patients did not reach MCID in PROMIS Physical Function at 90 days. Sunderland et al. [9] reported that 61% of primary lumbar decompression patients achieved MCID by 3 months, with lower early response in redo patients. Nie et al. [10] similarly found that many lumbar decompression patients do not achieve MCID until months after surgery.

The postadoption MCID rate of 92.7% exceeds these external benchmarks, although direct comparison is limited by differences in instruments, MCID definitions, case mix, and follow-up. Mechanistically, improved treatment response could occur if threshold-guided decompression reduces subclinical residual compression that might otherwise manifest as persistent postoperative pain. This interpretation is plausible but exploratory. TNR was exploratory, was not corrected for multiplicity, and relies on an absolute-change MCID threshold that is sensitive to baseline severity—patients with worse preoperative scores have more room to reach it [11]. Because the postadoption era had higher baseline pain (7.72 vs 6.93), part of the apparent MCID and TNR advantage may reflect this headroom rather than a treatment effect of MMG, and attribution requires prospective, baseline-stratified analysis.

Preadoption-to-3-month pain improvement was 2.03 points and postadoption improvement was 5.79 points, though this raw change-score comparison is influenced by the higher postadoption baseline.

Temporal confounding and case-volume effects

The doubled monthly case volume between eras is a major competing explanation. In a dose-response meta-analysis of 11 studies including 1,986,545 patients, Li et al. [12] found that higher surgeon volume was associated with lower postoperative morbidity (pooled OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.52–0.75), though heterogeneity was substantial. Malik et al. [13] similarly summarized literature linking higher surgeon volume with improved spine outcomes.

The adjusted ORs in the present study (0.245 for RTOR, 0.246 for durotomy) are larger than the pooled morbidity effect reported in the literature, but cross-study comparisons cannot rule out confounding. More importantly, this was a within-surgeon comparison across different eras. Higher volume may reflect not only repetition but also referral changes, team maturation, workflow standardization, and patient selection. The volume increase must be considered a central limitation.

The temporal analysis provides partial reassurance for the primary endpoint: preadoption RTOR showed no downward trend (p=.982), and extrapolation projects a rate of 10.3% at the start of the post-era—far above the actual postadoption rate of 2.5%. By contrast, durotomy and pain had borderline pre-era trends, making those endpoints more vulnerable to temporal maturation.

The 2-year exclusion interval is also an important limitation. It was excluded to preserve a clean exposure contrast, but changes in instruments, anesthesia, staffing, discharge pathways, referral patterns, and patient selection may have occurred during the gap. MMG does not change the core decompressive operation––it changes the decision framework at the point of determining whether decompression is physiologically complete. This distinction supports the plausibility of an MMG contribution but does not remove the possibility of broader systemic improvement.

Downstream revision and fusion

Cumulative revision at 24 months decreased from 18.0% to 3.7%, and subsequent fusion decreased from 11.3% to 4.3%. The postadoption revision rate compares favorably with published estimates, including approximately 7% by 2 years in a systematic review [14] and 11.3% by 3 years in a prospective lumbar stenosis cohort [15]. Postadoption fusion rates fall within or below published 2-year estimates of 3.5%–8.9% after decompression alone [16].

These downstream outcomes should remain exploratory. Revision and fusion events were identified from institutional records only; the study was not randomized, and the lower postadoption revision burden could reflect case selection, practice maturation, or care pathway changes.

Economic and operational considerations

If confirmed prospectively, reductions in RTOR, durotomy, and revision could have meaningful health-system implications. Dural tears have been associated with higher length of stay, readmission risk, and costs [[17], [18], [19]]. However, the present study did not capture claims-based costs or operative time. MMG requires sensor placement and threshold measurements, and persistently elevated thresholds may prompt additional decompression––but threshold normalization may reduce further exploratory tissue removal and partially offset measurement time. The net time and cost effect cannot be determined from this dataset.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, the retrospective era-based design cannot establish causation. MMG adoption coincided with a 2-year excluded interval, doubled monthly case volume, and possible unmeasured changes in patient selection, instruments, team workflow, anesthesia, discharge pathways, referral patterns, and surgeon maturation. Second, the study reflects a single surgeon at a single institution, limiting generalizability. The original study protocol included 2 surgeons to test whether MMG feedback could standardize decompression technique across different practice patterns, but complete data were available for only 1 surgeon; the intersurgeon question remains unanswered.

Third, baseline imbalances were present. The postadoption group had fewer smokers and fewer treated levels, both of which could be biased toward better outcomes; these were adjusted in multivariable models and explored with smoking sensitivity analysis, but residual confounding remains possible. Fourth, decompression-related RTOR classification was post hoc and applied unblinded to era; a sensitivity analysis (Results) showed the decompression-related reduction was robust to the chart-based ambiguities identified and to single-event reclassification, but category-specific inferences remain limited by sparse counts. The 3 postadoption decompression-related RTOR cases could not be adjudicated against intraoperative threshold trajectories because patient-level threshold data were unavailable.

Fifth, individual baseline and final MMG thresholds were not available in structured data. The study cannot evaluate threshold-response relationships, determine how many patients began at or below the 2.0 mA target, identify false-negative MMG cases, or test mediation through threshold normalization. Sixth, the operative time was not available. Seventh, revision and fusion events were captured from institutional records only; events at outside facilities may have been missed. Eighth, several adjusted models involve low event counts relative to covariates, and exploratory endpoints were not corrected for multiplicity.

Future directions

The next step should be a prospective multicenter evaluation, ideally using a stepped-wedge or pragmatic adoption design with each surgeon’s preadoption outcomes as a historical control. Such a study should capture individual baseline and postdecompression thresholds, cases in which MMG prompted additional decompression, cases in which thresholds normalized without additional work, operative time, device-related costs, and standardized patient-reported outcomes, including Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Physical Function, and Analogue (VAS) back and leg pain.

Prospective adjudication of RTOR should be built into the design, with decompression-related and nondecompression-related returns analyzed separately. This would allow the field to determine whether the association observed here reproduces across surgeons and whether MMG is most beneficial for specific pathologies, symptom chronicity profiles, or baseline threshold patterns.

Conclusions

In this 600-patient single-surgeon retrospective era-based cohort, routine intraoperative MMG adoption was associated with lower 90-day RTOR, lower incidental durotomy, lower 3-month pain, and substantially lower treatment nonresponse. Decompression-related RTOR decreased from 4.7% to 1.0% (p=.012), supporting a mechanism-compatible signal; however, nondecompression RTOR also decreased, and the observational design cannot distinguish MMG-specific effects from secular practice changes, increased case volume, or surgeon maturation. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and support prospective multicenter validation with patient-level threshold capture, adjudicated decompression-specific endpoints, and standardized patient-reported outcomes.
