Independent and joint associations of cardiorespiratory fitness and BMI with dementia risk: the Cooper Center Longitudinal Study
Authors: Tal Gafni, Galit Weinstein, David Leonard, Carolyn E Barlow, Laura F DeFina, Kelley Pettee Gabriel, Jarett D Berry, Kerem Shuval
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine the association of midlife fitness and body mass index (BMI) with incident dementia later in life.Design and participantsA cohort study of 6428 individuals (mean age 50.9±7.6 years) from the Cooper Center Longitudinal Study.MeasuresCardiorespiratory fitness and BMI were assessed twice (1970–1999) during visits to the Cooper Clinic, a preventive medicine clinic in Dallas, Texas. These measures were examined as continuous and categorical variables. As continuous variables, fitness and BMI were examined at baseline (averaged of two examinations) and as absolute change between exams (mean time 2.1±1.8 years). Variables were categorised: unfit versus fit and normal versus overweight/obese. Medicare claims data were used to obtain all-cause dementia incidence (1999–2009). Mean follow-up between midlife examinations and Medicare surveillance was 15.7 ((SD=6.2) years. Multivariable models were used to assess the associations between fitness, BMI and dementia.ResultsDuring 40 773 person years of Medicare surveillance, 632 cases of dementia were identified. After controlling for BMI and covariates, each 1-metabolic equivalent increment in fitness was associated with 5% lower (HR 0.95; 95% CI 0.90 to 0.99) dementia risk. In comparison, after controlling for fitness and covariates, each 1 kg/m2 increment in BMI was associated with a 3.0% (HR 1.03; 95% CI 1.00 to 1.07) higher risk for dementia, yet without significance (p=0.051). Similar findings were observed when the exposures were categorised. Changes in fitness and BMI between examinations were not related to dementia. Jointly, participants who were unfit and overweight/obese had the highest (HR 2.28 95% CI 1.57 to 3.32) dementia risk compared with their fit and normal weight counterparts.ConclusionLower midlife fitness is a risk marker for dementia irrespective of weight status. Being unfit coupled with overweight/obese status might increase one’s risk for dementia even further.

Introduction

Dementia is a clinical diagnosis characterised by a decline in cognitive performance that impairs activities of daily living and social function.1 There were over 57 million dementia cases worldwide in 2019 with an excess of 152 million cases expected by 2050.2 Currently, many dementia cases have no effective treatment, therefore, identifying modifiable risk factors for primary prevention, such as physical inactivity and obesity, is an important public health priority.1 3

Cardiorespiratory fitness (henceforth ‘fitness’) is a direct consequence of habitual physical activity, also influenced by non-modifiable factors (eg, genetics).4 5 Previous findings indicate that higher fitness in midlife is related to decreased dementia risk later in life.6–9 More specifically, individuals with low fitness had nearly three times the risk for dementia compared with individuals with high fitness.10 Another important dementia risk factor is weight status, commonly assessed by body mass index (BMI).11 Higher BMI in midlife has been associated with dementia risk,3 12–14 yet results have not always been consistent and have rarely taken into account the potential protective effects of fitness.12 14 Other potential confounders, such as socioeconomic status, smoking and alcohol intake, which are often linked to BMI and fitness as well as dementia, have been accounted for in some, but not all the research to date.7 15

In addition, fitness and weight status are interrelated, and since they are modifiable risk factors with important health implications,16 17 their independent and joint effects on morbidity (eg, type 2 diabetes) and premature mortality have been explored in numerous studies.18–24 For example, a study by Lee et al observed that when fitness and obesity were examined jointly, obesity was predictive of type 2 diabetes irrespective of participants’ fitness levels.22 Moreover, a meta-analysis by Barry et al found that being unfit was related to a higher risk for premature mortality irrespective of weight status.23 However, scant evidence exists pertaining to the joint relationship of these two risk factors with dementia incidence. Consequently, in the current study, we examined the independent and joint association of midlife fitness and BMI with the incidence of dementia. We hypothesised that fitness and BMI would each be independently associated with the risk of dementia. To this end, we used data from the Cooper Center Longitudinal Study (CCLS) linked to Medicare claims data. These data include objectively measured fitness and weight status as well as data on all-cause dementia incidence.25

Methods

Study design and population

The study sample consisted of participants from the CCLS, who opted into the study and provided informed consent at the Cooper Clinic (Dallas, Texas). The CCLS explores the effects of lifestyle behaviours and fitness on health and longevity.26 CCLS participants are generally healthy community-dwelling men and women who are primarily white with access to medical care.5 27 Participants are referred by their employer or self-referred to the Cooper Clinic for preventive medicine examinations.6 28 Participants undergo an extensive medical examination, anthropometric measurements, laboratory blood testing and a fitness assessment via a maximal, graded treadmill exercise test.29 The average age of CCLS participants appears to have increased over the years. For example, Willis et al reported that the mean age of CCLS participants was 42.8 years in the 1970s and 1980s compared with an average age of 45.5 years and 46.9 years from 1990 to 1999 and 2000 to 2009, respectively.30

In the current study, the initial sample included 19 652 CCLS participants with information on the primary study variables who later enrolled in fee-for-service Medicare (parts A and B) with coverage at any time during the Medicare surveillance period (1999–2009).6 25 A total of 326 participants were excluded due to prevalent dementia based on the Medicare Chronic Condition Warehouse before entering the Medicare surveillance period.31 Furthermore, 11 402 participants were excluded since they did not have information on the primary study variables assessed at two clinical examinations. We additionally excluded 242 participants with >10 years between examinations to minimise the effects of ageing. Furthermore, we excluded 548 participants with a history of myocardial infarction or stroke, and 676 participants who did not reach 85% of their maximal heart rate on the treadmill test to achieve an apparently healthy sample.27 Moreover, 30 underweight participants (BMI<18.5 kg/m2) were excluded since this could be indicative of an underlying medical condition.32 This resulted in a final analytical sample of 6428 apparently healthy participants.

Cardiorespiratory fitness and BMI ascertainment

Fitness was determined based on a maximal treadmill test while adhering to a modified Balke protocol.33 34 Briefly, the assessment began with a walk at 88 m/min pace and 0% grade in the first minute, 2% grade in the second minute and an increase of 1% grade per minute until the 25th min.25 After completion of 25 min, elevation remained unchanged while speed increased by 5.4 m/min for each additional minute until volitional exhaustion.25 35 Maximal metabolic equivalents (METs) were estimated from the final treadmill speed and grade where 1 MET=3.5 mL O2/kg/min.36 Evidence indicates that the treadmill duration using this protocol is highly correlated with measured maximal oxygen uptake, in both men (r=0.92),37 and women (r=0.94).38 BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared; weight and height were measured at the clinic using a standard clinical scale and stadiometer, respectively.25 39

Fitness and BMI were treated as continuous and categorical independent variables based on averaged values of the first and second examination (ie, baseline) as well as absolute change (second examination minus the first examination). To investigate joint associations, we further categorised participants into four groups: fit and normal weight (reference group), unfit and normal weight, fit and overweight/obese, and unfit and overweight/obese. The unfit and fit categories were based on the lower 20% and the remaining 80% of the age-standardised and sex-standardised fitness distribution, respectively.39 Participants who had 18.5≤BMI<25 kg/m2 were classified as normal weight whereas participants with BMI≥25 kg/m2 were regarded as having overweight/obesity status.

Ascertainment of dementia

For participants aged ≥65 years, health service claims information was extracted from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS). CMS contains 100% of claims paid by Medicare for inpatient and outpatient health services.6 33 Medicare claims data were analysed to identify all-cause dementia cases using the International Classification of Diseases, ninth edition (ie, ICD-9) and Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System codes (ICD-9).29 All-cause dementia was determined if the individual had at least one Medicare claim from an inpatient, skilled-nursing facility, home health agency, hospital outpatient, or physician or supplier related to Alzheimer’s disease, senile dementia, presenile dementia or vascular dementia during the past 3 years.6 31 More specifically, the following ICD-9 codes were used: 331.0, 331.1, 331.11, 331.19, 331.2, 331.7, 290.0, 290.10, 290.11, 290.12, 290.13, 290.20, 290.21, 290.3, 290.40, 290.41, 290.42, 290.43, 294.0, 294.1, 294.10, 294.11, 294.8 and 797. The linkage of the CCLS with Medicare data enabled a valid examination of the exposure to dementia risk as an outcome measure over a long follow-up period.

Covariates

Covariates, selected based on the literature,3 included age at the first examination, sex, and averaged values across the two examinations of systolic blood pressure, fasting glucose, total cholesterol levels and education. Education was either self-reported (39%) or estimated due to missing (61%); see the Statistical analysis section. Education was operationalised as college educated (yes/no). Participants were regarded as college educated if indicating they had a bachelor’s degree (or above) or at least 16 years of education. In addition, blood pressure was measured at rest at the Cooper Clinic via standard auscultatory, and values of fasting glucose and total cholesterol were analysed from venous blood samples.29

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the sample at midlife were summarised. Later life dementia HRs for fitness and BMI were estimated by maximising the likelihood of a Royston-Parmar model.40 The maximum likelihood approach allowed us to include data with missing education (61% missing), assuming data were missing at random.41 42 We estimated multivariable adjusted HR models first controlling for age, sex and education (ie, partially adjusted) and then additionally controlling for variables that are potentially on the causal pathway between the exposures and outcomes; namely, fasting glucose, total cholesterol and resting systolic blood pressure (ie, fully adjusted).43 Model 1 included only fitness as the primary exposure to examine its relationship with dementia risk irrespective of BMI. Similarly, model 2 included BMI as the primary exposure in relation to dementia without fitness. Additionally, model 3 included both fitness and BMI as primary exposures as they related to dementia. These models were estimated with the exposures as continuous variables and then re-estimated as categorical variables, while computing 95% (CIs) alongside the HRs. All models were re-estimated adjusting for alcohol intake and current smoking,44 instead of the education variable. Due to missing observations in these covariates, these models were also fit by maximising the Royston-Parmar likelihood40–42 (see online supplemental table). In addition, to examine the relationship between changes in fitness and BMI and the outcome, we repeated the models with changes in the primary independent variables and added time between clinical examinations and the baseline values of covariates. For joint association analysis, fitness and BMI groups were used as categorical independent variables, while adjusting for covariates. All statistical analyses were performed by using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute).

Patients and public involvement

There was no patient or public involvement in any aspect of the current study.

Results

Participants’ mean age was 50.9 years (SD=7.6) and 85% were men. At baseline, the mean BMI was 25.4 kg/m2 (SD=3.2) and average fitness was 11.6 METs (SD=2.3) (see table 1). The time between exams ranged from 0.8 to 10 years (mean 2.1 (SD=1.8) years; median 1.2 (IQR 1.0–2.4) years). Changes in fitness and BMI between examinations ranged from −5.9 to 10.5 METs (mean 0.3 (SD=1.2) years; median 0.0 (IQR −0.5 to 0.9) METs), and from −18.3 to 13.0 kg/m2 (mean 0.0 (SD=1.4) kg/m2; median 0.1 (IQR −0.6 to 0.7) kg/m2), respectively. On average, participants entered the Medicare surveillance period 15.7 years (SD=6.2) after their baseline fitness and BMI values were determined (median follow-up time 16.4 years (IQR 11.9–20.0)). During 6.3 years (SD=3.6) or 40 773 person years of Medicare surveillance, 632 cases of all-cause dementia were identified. In addition, the cumulative incidence of dementia by age during follow-up was 2.4% for 70 years, 7.1% for 75 years, 18.6% for 80 years and 34.3% for 85 years.

Table 2 describes the associations of baseline exposures (fitness, BMI) independent of each other and combined in relation to the incidence of dementia adjusted for covariates. When examining the relationship of fitness continuously without BMI (ie, model 1), each 1-MET increment in fitness was significantly related to an 8.0% (HR 0.92; 95% CI 0.89 to 0.96) and 7.0% (HR 0.93; 95% CI 0.89 to 0.97) lower risk for dementia in the partially and fully adjusted models, respectfully. When examining fitness categorically, independent of weight status, fit participants had, on average, a 50.0% (HR 0.50; 95% CI 0.36 to 0.69) and 48% (HR 0.52; 95% CI 0.37 to 0.72) lower risk for dementia in comparison to their unfit counterparts in the partially and fully adjusted models, respectfully. When examining fitness while accounting for BMI and weight status in the same model (ie, model 3), the relation between fitness and dementia remained significant yet slightly less pronounced (see table 2). For example, fit participants had, on average, a 49.0% (HR 0.51; 95% CI 0.37 to 0.72) and 46.0% (HR 0.54; 95% CI 0.39 to 0.76) lower risk for dementia than participants who were unfit.

When examining the BMI–dementia association, each 1 kg/m2 increment in BMI was significantly associated with a 5.0% (HR 1.05; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.08) higher risk for dementia, in the partially and fully adjusted models that did not account for fitness (ie, model 2). When BMI was categorised in model 2, participants who were overweight/obese exhibited, on average, a higher risk for dementia (partially adjusted: HR 1.18; 95% CI 1.00 to 1.39; fully adjusted: HR 1.16; 95% CI 0.99 to 1.37) than participants who were of normal weight, yet without reaching statistical significance (partially adjusted: p=0.054; fully adjusted: p=0.066). In addition, when BMI or weight status was examined alongside fitness (ie, model 3), they were not significantly related to dementia risk in both the partially and fully adjusted models (see table 2). For example, in model 3, a 1 kg/m2 increment in BMI was related to a 3.0% (HR 1.03; 95% CI 1.00 to 1.07) higher risk for dementia yet without reaching statistical significance (p=0.051). In addition, sensitivity analysis, which added smoking and alcohol as covariates in the models (instead of education), did not change results materially (see online supplemental table).

Moreover, when an interaction term between baseline exposures variables was added, it did not reach significance (p>0.10), and therefore was not included in the models. In addition, the between-examination changes in exposures (fitness and BMI) was not significantly associated with dementia risk (HR 1.07; 95% CI 0.99 to 1.15; and HR 1.01; 95% CI 0.95 to 1.09, respectively). The joint association analysis (figure 1) revealed that compared with the reference group (ie, fit and normal weight), participants who were unfit and overweight/obese exhibited ~2.3 times higher risk for dementia (HR 2.28; 95% CI 1.57 to 3.32). Other groups did not significantly differ from the reference group.

Discussion

Findings suggest that lower fitness at midlife was related to higher dementia risk later in life, regardless of weight status and covariates. In comparison, higher BMI was significantly related to dementia risk independent of potential confounders but not fitness. Moreover, when fitness and BMI were examined jointly, unfit and overweight/obese participants were, on average, at the highest risk for dementia. Overall study findings suggest that fitness might play a more prominent role as a risk marker for dementia than weight status. Hence, our hypothesis that the primary exposures (fitness and BMI) would be independently associated with dementia risk was met. However, when both exposures were in the same model, fitness predicted dementia while weight status did not reach statistical significance. This finding is consistent with previous research on this topic with other outcomes, such as depression, cardiovascular disease and mortality.20 22 24 45 Other research, however, such as a study by Lee et al, found that both fitness and obesity were significantly related to type 2 diabetes.

Plausible underlying mechanisms between fitness and dementia risk include improved cerebral blood flow,46 47 elevated level of circulating brain-derived neurotrophic factor, which improves neuroplasticity,48 thereby reducing dementia risk.49 This study bolsters previous research by relying on fitness and BMI assessment at two time points rather than only one assessment. In particular, Laukkanen and Kunutsor posited that the use of one fitness measurement at baseline could underestimate the true relationship due to high within-person variability between examinations.50 While two assessments has methodological merits, no significant relationships were observed between changes in the exposures and dementia. One likely explanation is the short time period (~2 years, on average) between clinical exams. Nonetheless, the fact that the clinical examinations (including fitness and weight status assessment) preceded the Medicare surveillance (when dementia was determined) by close to 16 years, on average, suggests that reverse causality is very unlikely.51

With regard to the weight status–dementia relationship, overweight/obese status was associated with a 16% higher risk to incur dementia compared with normal weight when controlling for covariates but not accounting for fitness. When adjusting for fitness (and covariates) the risk was reduced to 14%, yet in both cases the associations were not statistically significant. Comparing findings to the literature is somewhat difficult since previous research did not account for the protective effects of fitness. For example, a meta-analysis by Albanese et al found that obesity at midlife (in comparison to normal weight status), was related to 33% higher dementia risk.12 While the studies included in the meta-analysis adjusted for potential confounders, such as age and education, fitness which is a robust predictor of morbid outcomes,29 35 was not included in any of the studies.

In addition, the joint analysis underscores that the unfit and overweight/obese group are at the highest risk for dementia. Other combined categories did not reach statistical significance and a significant interaction was not found. There is a dearth of evidence examining the association of both modifiable risk factors with dementia. When exploring these exposures independently (continuously) in the current study, both appear to be associated with dementia, which is consistent with previous research.6 52 However, when combined in the same model fitness appears as a more robust predictor of dementia. Previous research using the same exposures as in this study found that fitness was a predictor of other outcomes (eg, cardiovascular disease, heart failure, depression and mortality) independent of weight status.20 24 39 53–55

Of note, fitness and adiposity are interrelated and fitness has been suggested as a means to offset obesity-related pathophysiology, such as vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction,56 insulin resistance,57 hypoperfusion and oxidative stress; these have been found to impact cognitive function.21 58–62 In fact, Frith and Loprinzi combined fitness and adiposity into one measure, finding it predictive of dementia morality.63 Thus, since higher BMI is related to lower fitness,64 and due to the detrimental relationship between obesity and dementia risk factors (eg, hypertension, diabetes),65 our findings should not be interpreted as weight status not contributing to dementia risk.

This study has methodological strengths, such as the cohort study design, the large number of participants and objective measurement of the exposures. Yet, there are several limitations to consider when interpreting the findings. Specifically, while a cohort study design provides a higher level of evidence than cross-sectional designs, causality cannot be inferred and residual confounding cannot be ruled out.66 Moreover, BMI has been used extensively in epidemiological studies to measure obesity and its relationship to chronic diseases (eg, type 2 diabetes, heart disease) and mortality67–69; however, it might overestimate obesity in athletes and underestimate obesity in older adults. Additionally, while we controlled for various confounders in multivariable analyses, we did not account for other potential confounders, such as genetic factors (eg, APOE4 gene) or dietary intake, which should be examined in future research. Additionally, some covariates (eg, education) had missing responses, hence we employed an analytic strategy (maximum likelihood method),42 which allowed for estimating missing information.

Additionally, dementia diagnosis was based on Medicare claims files rather than clinical examination, and therefore, some dementia cases might have been misclassified. That said, Medicare data have been shown to be a valid and cost-effective resource.70 Previous research has shown that in comparison to clinical diagnosis, Medicare claims have a sensitivity of 85% in detection of dementia and specificity of 76% among individuals with normal cognition.71 Nonetheless, merging individual-level data with Medicare claims files has been used extensively to provide insight into the contribution of risk factors to health outcomes.70 Further, while we excluded participants with prevalent dementia prior to the Medicare surveillance, it is possible that we missed certain cases because of incomplete surveillance from baseline to Medicare enrolment. This is not likely, however, since participants’ average age at baseline was 50.9 years and the prevalence of young onset dementia has been estimated at 119 cases per 10 000 population.72 In addition, the CCLS cohort consists of participants who are primarily white with access to preventive medicine healthcare and thus not representative of the US population.70 The homogeneity of the sample increases the internal validity of the study,34 yet this might limit the generalisability of findings and necessitates future research among more diverse and representative samples.28 When comparing the incidence rate of dementia in our sample to data from the CHAP study, the annual incidence rate was very similar. For example, the annual incidence of dementia for 75–84 years in this study was 3.4% compared with 3.2% in CHAP.73 Finally, while fitness generally increases by performing endurance exercise it is also determined by genetic and environmental factors,74 which are not accounted for in the current study. Other variables, such as age, sex, smoking and alcohol intake, are determinants of fitness as well.75 In conclusion, in this sample of apparently healthy adults, fitness at midlife appears to be a strong risk marker for dementia irrespective of weight status. Hence, our findings suggest that high fitness levels are beneficial when it comes to brain health, thereby illuminating the importance of health-promoting physical activity as a means to improve fitness. Future research should examine the effects of changes in fitness and adiposity on dementia risk over a longer period and among more diverse samples.
