Systemic administration of Photobacterium angustum promotes antitumor immunity and direct tumor lysis in murine models of colorectal cancer
Authors: Mikako Miyahara, Teruhisa Takizawa, Matomo Sakari, Eijiro Miyako
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite remarkable advances in cancer immunotherapy, including immune checkpoint inhibitors and adoptive cell therapies, many patients fail to achieve durable responses due to factors such as immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments, poor tumor antigenicity, and systemic toxicity. These limitations underscore the need for alternative platforms that can elicit robust and localized antitumor immune responses. Bacteria have emerged as promising candidates owing to their natural ability to selectively colonize tumors, stimulate innate and adaptive immunity, and be engineered for therapeutic delivery. However, the therapeutic potential of naturally occurring marine bacterial strains remains largely unexplored in colorectal cancer.MethodsWe screened a panel of marine bacterial strains for antitumor activity in syngeneic and orthotopic mouse models of colorectal cancer. Photobacterium angustum was selected based on its potent antitumor effects. We evaluated tumor colonization, systemic toxicity, and immune responses following intravenous administration. Biosafety was assessed via serum cytokine profiling and hematological analysis. Immune activation was examined by quantitative PCR and immunohistochemistry.ResultsP. angustum therapy significantly prolonged survival in tumor-bearing mice and exhibited strong tumor tropism with minimal colonization of healthy tissues. Systemic administration was well tolerated, with low levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and no hematological toxicity. Treatment enhanced infiltration of T cells, B cells, and neutrophils in the tumor microenvironment, accompanied by elevated intratumoral tumor necrosis factor-α and interferon-γ levels. Additionally, P. angustum demonstrated intrinsic oncolytic activity, contributing to direct tumor cell lysis.ConclusionsOur findings support P. angustum as a promising platform for bacterial immunotherapy in colorectal cancer, combining tumor-specific colonization, immune activation, and direct tumoricidal effects with an excellent safety profile.Keywords: Colorectal Cancer, Colon Cancer, Complete response - CR, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, and despite substantial advancements in treatment, many challenges persist. One of the most pressing needs in oncology is the development of therapeutic modalities that can selectively and precisely target tumor cells while minimizing systemic toxicity. Although cancer immunotherapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors and chimeric antigen receptor T cells have revolutionized treatment paradigms, they are often limited by high cost, immune-related adverse events, restricted efficacy in solid tumors, and resistance mechanisms within the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

In this context, bacterial therapy has emerged as a promising alternative to conventional approaches such as surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, and even current immunotherapies. Certain bacterial species exhibit a unique ability to selectively colonize and proliferate within the immunosuppressive and hypoxic tumor microenvironment, enabling them to act as tumor-selective vectors for both direct cytotoxicity and immune activation.1,9

While genetically engineered bacteria have shown preclinical promise, they raise several translational concerns, including biosafety risks, potential for virulence reversion, complex manufacturing processes, and strict regulatory oversight (eg, Cartagena Protocol).10,13 Moreover, many synthetic systems require extensive genetic modifications and fail to achieve robust therapeutic efficacy. As such, there is a critical need to identify naturally occurring, non-engineered bacterial strains that combine strong innate tumor selectivity with a favorable safety profile.

Although the concept of bacterial anticancer therapy dates back over a century, the exploration of biocompatible, naturally oncolytic strains remains underdeveloped. In this study, we report the use of marine bacteria as a novel therapeutic platform, capitalizing on their intrinsic tumor-homing capabilities and immunomodulatory potential. Specifically, Photobacterium angustum demonstrated robust proliferation within colorectal tumors, selective tumor colonization, and minimal impact on healthy tissues, suggesting a wide therapeutic window.

To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating the use of naturally marine bacteria for cancer therapy, beyond previous applications of engineered Escherichia coli or Salmonella typhimurium strains for bioimaging or photodynamic therapy.14 15 Our findings position P. angustum as a unique and promising vector for bacterial immunotherapy, offering a foundation for future non-genetically modified organism (GMO) anticancer strategies that combine precision targeting with low systemic toxicity.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and growth and characterization

P. angustum (NBRC104593), Photobacterium phosphoreum (NBRC104104), Photobacterium aquimaris (NBRC104633), Aliivibrio logei (NBRC103152), Photobacterium indicum (NBRC14233), and Bifidobacterium longum (NBRC114370) were obtained from the Biological Resource Center, NITE (Chiba, Japan). Clostridium novyi (ATCC19402) was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) (Manassas, Virginia, USA). Bacteria except for B. longum and C. novyi were grown in BD DIFCO Marine Broth 2216 (Becton, Dickinson and Company, New Jersey, USA) medium at 20–25°C. B. longum and C. novyi were cultured anaerobically at 37°C in NBRC1534 and ATCC2107 media, respectively, using an incubator (MIR-154-PJ; PHCbi, Tokyo, Japan). The cell number and viability of the bacteria were measured using a bacterial counter (CASY Cell Counter and Analyzer; OMNI Life Science, Basel, Switzerland) in addition to an active colony assay. Transcriptome analyses of the bacteria were performed at the Bioengineering Lab (Kanagawa, Japan). The bacterial virulence factors were identified using a type strain genome database.16

Cell culture and viability assay

Murine colorectal cancer (Colon-26) cells were obtained from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresource Cell Bank (Tokyo, Japan), and cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 IU/mL penicillin-streptomycin. Drug-resistant mouse mammary tumor (EMT6/AR1) cell line was obtained from KAC (Tokyo, Japan). EM6/AR1 cell line was cultured in cell growth medium No.104 (KAC) containing doxorubicin hydrochloride (1 µg/mL, Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan). Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2, cryopreserved in multiple vials, and stored in liquid nitrogen. Cell stocks were regularly revived to avoid the genetic instability associated with high passage numbers.

Cell viability was assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Colon-26 (1×105 cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The cells were then exposed to bacteria at various concentrations for 24 hours in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2. After washing with fresh media, the cells were incubated with the CCK-8 solution for 1 hour in a 37°C incubator. Absorbance at 450 nm was determined using a microplate reader (Infinite 200 PRO M Plex; Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

Tumor spheroids

Colon-26 or HT-29 cells (1×104 cells/well) were seeded in a three-dimensional (3D) culture spheroid plate (Cell-able BP-96-R800; Toyo Gosei, Tokyo, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s instructions provided with the plate. The cells were cultured for 5 days at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. The medium was replaced after 3 days. Prepared spheroids were then exposed to 1.0×107 CFU (colony-forming units) of P. angustum or Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium for 0, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 hours at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Spheroids were observed using a fluorescence microscopy system (BZ-X810, Keyence, Tokyo, Japan) at 20°C.

Therapeutic agent preparation

Conventional therapeutic agent was prepared according to clinical protocols to enable meaningful comparative studies. Trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhertu) was obtained as a clinical formulation (100 mg powder for concentrate for solution for infusion, Daiichi Sankyo, Tokyo, Japan) and stored at 4°C to maintain stability. Prior to administration, both agents were diluted in sterile saline to achieve desired dosing concentrations according to established protocols.

Antitumor therapy

The Colon-26 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice (female; approximately 8 weeks; n=5; average weight=20 g; average tumor size=200 mm3; BALB/cCrSIc; Japan SLC) were intravenously (i.v.) injected in the tail vein with saline (Chemix, Kanagawa, Japan) (200 µL) containing P. angustum (6.5×109 CFU/mL). The bacterial dose (6.5×109 CFU/mL) was selected based on preliminary optimization studies, which demonstrated this concentration provided the most robust therapeutic efficacy while maintaining acceptable tolerability in mice. Higher inoculation levels did not confer additional benefit and were associated with adverse effects, and thus were not pursued. Control experiments without bacterial injection were also performed. Tumor formation and overall health (viability and body weight) were monitored daily. The tumor volume was calculated as V=L × W2/2, where L and W denote the length and width of the tumor, respectively. The survival ratio of Colon-26 tumor-bearing mice (n=10 biologically independent mice) was also measured during treatment for 40 days. When the tumor volume reached>1500 mm3, the mice were euthanized according to the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Japan Advanced Institute of Science and Technology.

To evaluate the induction of long-term antitumor immunity, tumor-free mice that had achieved complete regression following P. angustum treatment were rechallenged on day 120 post-treatment by subcutaneous injection of 1×106 Colon-26 cells into the contralateral flank. Age-matched BALB/c mice were used as controls. Tumor growth was monitored for an additional 30 days.

To compare the therapeutic efficacy of different delivery routes, intratumoral injection experiments were conducted. Female BALB/c mice (approximately 8 weeks old, average weight 20 g, n=5 per group; BALB/cCrSIc; Japan SLC) bearing subcutaneous Colon26 tumors (average tumor volume 200 mm³) were anesthetized with isoflurane and positioned for direct tumor access. Using a 29-gage needle, P. angustum suspension (1.3×10⁹ CFU in 50 µL saline) was slowly injected directly into the tumor mass under sterile conditions. Care was taken to ensure complete injection without leakage. Following injection, mice were monitored for immediate adverse reactions and returned to their cages for recovery. Tumor growth, survival, and overall health status were assessed as described in the survival analysis section. This intratumoral delivery approach was compared with the standard i.v. administration to evaluate route-dependent therapeutic differences.

An orthotopic colorectal cancer model was prepared as follows. 5-week-old female C57BL/6 mice (n=5; average weight, 20 g; C57BL/6CrSIc; Japan SLC) were intraperitoneally injected with azoxymethane (AOM) (10 mg/kg, Fujifilm Wako, Tokyo, Japan). After 1 week, 2% dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) solution (Cosmo Bio, Tokyo, Japan) was administered to the mice in their drinking water for 1 week and regular water for the following 12 weeks. The model was i.v. injected in the tail vein with saline (Chemix) (200 µL) containing P. angustum (6.5×109 CFU/mL). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was used as the placebo control against P. angustum. The untreated group was also tested as a negative control, without AOM/DSS or any administration of samples. The animals were euthanized on day 7, and their colons were harvested for weighing and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. The weight of the colon of each mouse was measured on the same day.

To investigate the efficacy of bacteria against drug-resistant triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) model, 100 µL of the culture medium/Matrigel (Dow Corning, Corning, New York, USA) mixture (v/v=1:1) containing 1×106 cells of EMT6/AR1 were subcutaneously inoculated into the right side of the backs of the mice (female; 6 weeks; n=5; average weight=18 g; BALB/cCrSIc; Japan SLC). After approximately 10 days, when the tumor volumes reached ∼ 200 mm3, the mice were i.v. injected with 200 µL culture medium containing P. angustum (6.5×109 CFU/mL). The control experiments were also performed using PBS (200 µL). The tumor volume and overall health (viability and body weight) of mice were also studied.

Comparative therapeutic efficacy studies

To assess the therapeutic potential of P. angustum relative to established cancer treatments, comparative efficacy studies were performed under identical experimental conditions. Female BALB/c mice bearing Colon-26 tumors (~200 mm³) were treated with a single i.v. injection of B. longum (200 µL, 5.0×10⁹ CFU/mL), a single i.v. injection of C. novyi (200 µL, 5.0×10⁹ CFU/mL), or conventional therapy administered according to standard clinical protocols. The bacterial concentration (5.0×109 CFU/mL) was determined from preliminary optimization experiments, which indicated that this dose achieved the greatest therapeutic benefit while remaining well tolerated in mice. Increasing the inoculum beyond this level failed to enhance efficacy and instead led to adverse effects, so higher doses were not further evaluated.

The conventional therapy group received trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhertu, 2.5 mg/kg body weight) via i.v. injection every other day for a total of four doses, representing clinically relevant regimens. All treatments were prepared in sterile saline to achieve the desired concentrations and administered via tail vein injection under aseptic conditions.

Neutrophil depletion study

To investigate the role of neutrophils in P. angustum-mediated anticancer efficacy, neutrophil depletion experiments were performed using anti-Ly6G antibody. Female BALB/c mice (approximately 8 weeks old, average weight 20 g, n=5 per group; BALB/cCrSIc; Japan SLC) bearing subcutaneous Colon-26 tumors (average tumor volume 200 mm³) received intraperitoneal injection of either anti-Ly6G antibody (Anti-mouse Ly6G-InVivo; Selleck, Houston, Texas, USA; 0.2 mg per mouse in 200 µL saline) or saline control (200 µL). 24 hours after antibody administration, mice were i.v. injected with P. angustum (200 µL, 4×10⁹ CFU/mL). Tumor growth and survival were monitored as described above.

Immunohistochemistry staining of tumor

The Colon-26 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice (female; approximately 8 weeks; n=3; average weight=20 g; average tumor size=200 mm3; BALB/cCrSIc; Japan SLC) were euthanized 12 hours after i.v. injection of P. angustum (200 µL, 6.5×109 CFU/mL). Tumors were harvested for IHC staining. Tumor tissue specimens were prepared by the Biopathology Institute (Oita, Japan) using standard protocols. Primary tumors were surgically removed, fixed in 10% formalin, processed for paraffin embedding, and cut into 3–4 µm-thick sections. After incubation with primary antibodies (online supplemental table S1), the sections were stained with hematoxylin and examined using light microscopy (BZ-X810). Areas showing positive staining in the tumor tissues were analyzed using a light microscopy system (BZ-X810), hybrid cell count, and microcell count software (Keyence).

Quantitative PCR

The Colon-26 tumor-bearing mice (female; approximately 8 weeks; n=3; average weight=20 g; average tumor size=200 mm3; BALB/cCrSIc; Japan SLC) were euthanized 3, 6, and 12 hours after i.v. administration of P. angustum (200 µL, 6.5×109 CFU/mL). To analyze the immune cells and cytokines in tumors, tumors were collected from mice in different groups 3, 6, and 12 hours after i.v. injection of the sample and then homogenized using a homogenizer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) before quantitative PCR (qPCR). qPCR was performed using QuantStudio 1 PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to study the relative gene expression of CD3, CD19, Ly6G, F4/80, NKp46, interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), using gene specific primer-probe combinations (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (online supplemental table S2) with Taqman chemistry. The endogenous control was determined using the TaqMan Array Mouse Endogenous Control Plate 96 well (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The reactions were run in triplicate using ACT-β as an endogenous control. The thermal cycling parameters, 50°C for 2 min for AmpErase UNG activation, 95°C for 2 min for AmpliTaq Gold DNA Pol. activation, 40 cycles each at 95°C for 1 s and 60°C for 20 s for denaturation/melting, yielded the optimum amplification. For the test genes and endogenous control standards, 10-fold serial dilutions were run to estimate the efficiency of PCR and the percentage efficiency ranged from 90% to 100%. The results were analyzed and shown as fold change (log10 relative quantification) relative to the control group.

Flow cytometry

The Colon-26 tumor-bearing mice (female; about 8 weeks old; average weight=20 g; average tumor size=200 mm3; n=3; genetic background: BALB/c; obtained from Japan SLC) were euthanized the day after i.v. injection of PBS (200 µL) or P. angustum (200 µL, 6.5×109 CFU/mL). To analyze the immune cells in tumors, tumors were collected from mice in different groups at 6 hours after i.v. injection of sample and homogenized into single-cell suspensions (gentleMACS Octo Dissociator with Heaters, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Then, red blood cells were removed by ACK lysing buffer (Miltenyi Biotec), and debris was removed by cell strainer (mesh size=100 µm, Miltenyi Biotec). Samples containing 1×106 cells were stained with antibodies conjugated to fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), phycoerythrin (PE), peridinin-chlorophyll-protein complex-Vio 700 (PerCP-Vio 700), phycoerythrin-Vio 770 (PE-Vio 770), allophycocyanin (APC), or allophycocyanin-Vio 770 (APC-Vio 770), together with Viobility 405/520 Fixable Dye (used to discriminate live cells from apoptotic or dead cells; see online supplemental table S1), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Stained cells were subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry using a MACSQuant Analyzer 16 (Miltenyi Biotec) and MACSQuantify software (V.2.13.3; Miltenyi Biotec).

Blood tests

The complete blood counts (CBCs) were measured using the Celltac α blood cell counting machine (MEK6558; Nihon Kohden, Tokyo, Japan) and biochemical parameters were investigated by Oriental Yeast (Tokyo, Japan). Cytokine levels were measured using the Bio-Plex Multiplex immunoassay system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA) and Bio-Plex Pro Mouse Th17 Panel 6-Plex (Bio-Rad). BALB/cCrSlc mice (female; 6 weeks; n=5; average weight=18 g; Japan SLC) were injected in the tail vein with saline (Chemix) containing P. angustum (200 µL, 6.5×109 CFU/mL) or P. indicum (200 µL, 6.5×109 CFU/mL). Blood samples were collected from the inferior vena cava of mice after 3, 6, 24, 48, 72, and 168 hours. Control experiments without bacterial administration were also performed as the 0 hour treatment.

Safety analysis

A blood cell counting machine (MEK6558, Celltac α) was used to measure the CBC of the mice and the biochemical parameters were investigated by Japan SLC and Oriental Yeast (Tokyo, Japan). Female BALB/c mice (6 weeks; n=5 in each group; average weight=18 g; Japan SLC) were injected with P. angustum (6.5×109 CFU/mL; 200 µL) or 200 µL of PBS in the tail vein. Blood samples were collected from the inferior vena cava of mice after 3, 6, 24, 72, and 168 hours. Additionally, the major organs, including the heart, liver, kidneys, spleen, and lungs, were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde before being sent to a specialized facility for H&E staining to evaluate the safety profile of P. angustum.

Statistical analysis

All experiments, except those described in the Supporting Information, were performed in triplicates and repeated three or more times. Quantitative values are expressed as the mean±SEM of at least two independent experiments. Statistical differences were identified using Student’s two-sided t-test, two-way analysis of variance, or the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test using GraphPad Prism, V.9.4.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA). Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Results

Exploring effective oncolytic marine bacteria

We verified the anticancer efficacies of various marine bacteria, including P. angustum, P. phosphoreum, P. aquimaris, A. logei, and P. indicum for the development of effective cancer therapies. While many archaeal and bacterial species require special culture methods,17 these bacteria can be easily cultured under aerobic and static conditions using conventional marine broth at ambient temperature from 20°C to 25°C (figure 1a). Although the unique luminescent features of these bacteria were completely lost during the culture process, this simple production method has a substantial advantage in good practice-graded quality assurance for future clinical trials. By confirming with optical microscopy, the bacteria showed spherical and/or rod-shaped structures, and they were approximately 2–20, 2, 10, 2.5, and 2.5 µm in length (online supplemental figure S1).

The in vivo antitumor therapeutic effects of these bacteria were investigated using a syngeneic model of BALB/c mice with Colon-26 (figure 1b). The Colon-26 tumor model, a widely used transplantable mouse tumor model, is a valuable tool in colorectal cancer research.18 19 In fact, this model effectively reproduces tumors through the transplantation of Colon-26 cancer cells into murine subjects, allowing for the assessment of tumor growth, immune responses, and treatment efficacy. Such models are useful for evaluating potential therapeutic interventions and understanding the underlying mechanisms of tumor biology using various marine bacteria. Each bacterium strain was i.v. administered once when the volume of tumors reached approximately 200 mm3. Notably, only P. angustum showed dramatic anticancer effects and significantly extended the survival rates (figure 1c–e). Meanwhile, all mice died within 2 days after i.v. administration of other bacterial strains, such as P. phosphoreum, P. aquimaris, A. logei, and P. indicum, due to their high toxicity (figure 1e and online supplemental figure S2). The untreated group showed obvious tumor growth over time, and all mice died within 16 days (figure 1c). The fact that no significant weight loss (> 20%) with P. angustum confirmed that there were no side effects associated with bacterial administration (figure 1f). To determine whether P. angustum therapy could elicit durable antitumor immunity, tumor-free mice were rechallenged with Colon-26 cells 120 days after the initial treatment. Strikingly, all previously cured mice completely rejected secondary tumor growth, whereas all control mice developed progressive tumors (online supplemental figure S3). These results demonstrate that P. angustum–based therapy establishes potent and long-lasting immunological memory.

P. angustum exhibited superior antitumor efficacy when administered i.v. compared with intratumoral injection, suggesting that systemic delivery not only enhances tumor colonization but also provides therapeutic benefit for disseminated or metastatic lesions (online supplemental figure S4).

P. angustum demonstrated markedly superior therapeutic efficacy in the BALB/c Colon-26 tumor model compared with conventional bacterial cancer therapy strains such as B. longum and C. novyi (online supplemental figure S5). While both reference strains exhibited partial antitumor activity, P. angustum achieved more pronounced tumor regression and higher rates of complete response, highlighting its distinct advantage over previously established bacterial therapeutic approaches.

To further study the potential anticancer activity of P. angustum, colitis-associated cancer cells were examined (figure 1g). We used AOM, an alkylating agent that induces DNA-reactive adducts, in combination with the inflammatory agent, DSS, to obtain a mouse model of inflammation-driven sporadic colorectal cancer.20 PBS was used as the placebo control against P. angustum. The untreated group was also tested as a negative control without AOM/DSS treatment or sample administration. Notably, P. angustum was markedly effective against the inflammation-driven sporadic colorectal cancer model (figure 1h,i). IHC staining of cytokeratin 2021 as a biomarker, predictor, and promoter of colorectal cancer also showed that the tumor tissues of mice administered a single dose of P. angustum looked similar to those of the non-treatment group, whereas color changes were observed in the groups administered a single dose of PBS (figure 1j,k).

To further assess the generalizability of our approach, we evaluated P. angustum in a drug-resistant TNBC model, in which EMT6/AR1 cells were implanted into syngeneic BALB/c mice. Notably, i.v. administration of P. angustum induced pronounced tumor regression in this refractory setting, whereas treatment with trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhertu), a clinically approved antibody–drug conjugate, exhibited minimal efficacy (online supplemental figure S6). These findings highlight the broad applicability of P. angustum-based therapy beyond colorectal cancer models, including highly drug-resistant TNBC.

Biocompatibility of P. angustum

Next, we performed various biocompatibility tests in mice treated with P. angustum (figure 2). P. indicum was used as a representative control toxic bacteria to compare with the effective oncolytic P. angustum strain. The mice in the P. indicum-treated group died within 6 hours. Transcriptome analyses of these bacteria indicated that P. angustum did not express as many virulence factors as P. indicum (figure 2a). CBCs (white blood cells (WBCs), platelets (PLTs), hematocrit, hemoglobin, mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), MCH concentration, mean corpuscular volume, red blood cells), inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α, interleukin (IL)-6, IL-1β, IL-17A), and anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10), were measured from blood samples 3, 6, and 24 hours after either of P. angustum or fatal P. indicum treatment (figure 2b‒i and online supplemental figure S7). Chronological WBC reduction was observed with both bacterial strains, presumably because neutrophils, which play a central role in early host defense following infection, were evaded or their death was induced by the foreign bacteria in the blood vessels.22 Additionally, PLT concentrations significantly decreased, especially after i.v. injection of P. indicum, possibly because PLTs inactivate toxic bacteria.23 No significant differences were observed in the other CBCs between the untreated and P. angustum groups (online supplemental figure S7). However, WBC and PLT concentrations returned to normal values 72 hours after P. angustum treatment (online supplemental figure S8). We also conducted comprehensive biochemical analyses, including alanine transaminase, aspartate aminotransferase, and creatinine, at 3, 6, 24, and 72 hours post-injection (online supplemental table S3-6). Transient fluctuations were observed at earlier time points; however, by 24 hours the values had stabilized and closely approximated those of the control group, indicating no sustained elevation in liver enzymes or creatinine and supporting the favorable acute safety profile of P. angustum. The expression of inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines after treatment with P. angustum was lower than after P. indicum administration (figure 2d‒i). The potent therapeutic immune responses in the tumor microenvironment may be caused by the high tumor selectivity of P. angustum. However, we believe that it will not cause an immunological crisis in healthy organs. In fact, 24 hours after administration of P. angustum to Colon-26-bearing mice, no bacterial colonies were detected in the vital organs, except for the liver (figure 2j and k), whereas numerous colonies were detected in the tumors, indicating high selectivity of the bacteria against colorectal tumors and no severe side effects. To further confirm the safety of P. angustum, blood tests indicated no significant physiological alterations in mice following P. angustum treatment for at least 7 days (table 1). H&E analysis of the tissues of the harvested vital organs, heart, liver, lungs, kidneys, and spleen also revealed no significant abnormalities or evidence of toxicity in the P. angustum-treated group (figure 3). In addition to the hematological and histopathological evaluations showing no systemic toxicity, we further investigated the biodistribution and clearance kinetics of i.v. administered P. angustum. Colony assays conducted at 48 hours and 72 hours confirmed complete bacterial elimination from major organs, including the liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and heart, with no evidence of colonization in sterile tissues (online supplemental figure S9). These findings, consistent with the absence of hematological or histological abnormalities, suggest that the risk of systemic infection or endocarditis is minimal under our experimental conditions. Nevertheless, we recognize the limitation that only short-term kinetics were evaluated in a murine model. Future studies with extended monitoring and in larger or immunocompromised models will be important to comprehensively validate the translational safety of this approach.

Antitumor mechanism

Finally, we investigated the mechanism underlying the antitumor effects of P. angustum by studying cancer cell elimination and intratumoral immune responses (figure 4). A 3D spheroid model composed of Colon-26 cells was prepared and the oncolytic ability of P. angustum was examined using an optical microscope (figure 4a). Robust spheroid constructs were dramatically destroyed over time after co-culture with P. angustum. Control spheroids without bacterial co-culture were not destroyed after 120 hours (figure 4a). In addition, killing assays using human colorectal cancer HT-29 spheroids were performed, confirming their direct cytotoxic activity (online supplemental figure S10). The cytotoxicity of P. angustum against Colon-26 cells increased with increasing bacterial concentration (figure 4b). The toxins and enzymes produced by bacteria can directly destroy cancer cells and cut-off the supply of nutrients to tumors by attacking tumor blood vessels.24,26 This suppresses tumor growth and promotes immune cell activation, which is expected to have a broader antitumor effect.17 27 28 The anticancer efficacy of P. angustum was confirmed by its innate oncolytic bacterial exotoxins.29 30 Transcriptome analysis indicated that P. angustum expressed hemolysins (gene-C0W36_RS20660 and gene-C0W36_RS01100) and toxins (gene-C0W36_RS15860) (onlinesupplemental data 1 2).

qPCR assay and IHC staining revealed that T cells, B cells, and neutrophils were activated and the inflammatory cytokine TNF-α and IFN-γ were increased in solid tumors 12 hours after tail vein administration of P. angustum (figure 4c,d and online supplemental figure S11). We further performed comprehensive flow cytometry analysis using specific antibodies to characterize tumor-infiltrating immune cell populations (online supplemental figure S12). The flow cytometry analysis complements and confirms our qPCR and IHC findings, showing similar trends of T cell, B cell, and neutrophil activation and infiltration. These results indicate that bacteria substantially evoke immune responses and inflammation, which affect therapeutic performance. H&E staining indicated that the antitumor effects of P. angustum facilitated the destruction of tumor tissues compared with those in the PBS group (figure 4d). Apoptosis markers (caspase-3 and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated 2́-deoxyuridine, 5́-triphosphate nick end labeling) and TNF-α in tumor sections also exhibited substantial apoptosis and induced a strong inflammatory response after P. angustum administration (figure 4d).

Taken together, the oncolytic ability and cytotoxic immune responses of P. angustum play important roles in the therapeutic outcome of tumor cell destruction and could effectively destroy targeted cancerous tumors (figure 4e). We believe that the tumor-targeting mechanism of the aerobic strain, P. angustum, highlights the involvement of passive intratumoral entrapment of bacteria as the starting point.31 Namely, P. angustum initially enters the tumor via passive entrapment in the chaotic tumor vasculature, and oncolytic bacterial exotoxins then destruct intratumor blood vessels with the help of immune cells lured by inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IFN-γ from the bacterial damaged intratumor blood vessels.32 Finally, the targeted tumor is completely destroyed because P. angustum gradually invades deeper tumor tissues using bacterial exotoxins, with the assistance of cytotoxic immune cells and cancer immune evasion systems.33

Moreover, depletion of neutrophils with an anti-Ly6G antibody marked enhanced tumor clearance, even following a single low-dose bacterial administration (online supplemental figure S13). These findings suggest that neutrophils may restrict bacterial dissemination within the tumor and thereby hinder complete oncolysis.34

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that the naturally marine bacterium P. angustum exerts potent antitumor activity in both syngeneic and inflammation-driven models of colorectal cancer. Treatment with P. angustum led to significant tumor regression and prolonged survival, suggesting its strong therapeutic potential.

A key contributor to this antitumor efficacy is the activation of robust intratumoral immune responses. We observed marked infiltration and activation of T cells, B cells, and neutrophils, alongside elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IFN-γ. These immune factors likely synergize with P. angustum’s direct cytotoxic effects to induce apoptosis and necrosis of tumor cells. In parallel, the bacterium’s natural exotoxin production contributes to its intrinsic oncolytic activity, offering a dual mechanism of tumor elimination: immune-mediated and bacteriolytic.

Notably, P. angustum exhibited high tumor selectivity with minimal dissemination to or colonization of vital organs. This localized targeting capacity is crucial for minimizing systemic toxicity and improving the therapeutic index. Unlike genetically engineered bacterial vectors, P. angustum requires no synthetic modifications, which simplifies production and reduces biosafety risks—key considerations for clinical translation.

These findings suggest that P. angustum may serve as a promising microbial-based immunotherapy for solid tumors, particularly in treatment-resistant colorectal cancer. While further investigation is required to elucidate its specific mechanisms of tumor tropism and immune activation, this study lays the foundation for the development of a novel class of naturally derived, non-GMO bacterial therapies.

Future work will focus on expanding the range of tumor models tested, evaluating potential combinatory effects with immune checkpoint inhibitors, and assessing long-term immunological memory. Ultimately, this research supports the broader application of biocompatible marine bacteria as next-generation immunotherapeutic platforms.
