Dipeptidase-1 Contributes to Sepsis-Associated Endothelial Dysfunction via the EGR1/NF-κB Signaling Axis
Authors: Bing Lin, Xinhua Xian, Rong Huang, Zhaohai Wang, Xinju Jia, Ting Su, Haiqiu Liang, Yanjiao Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSepsis is characterized by endothelial dysfunction that contributes to vascular leakage, inflammation, and organ failure. Although metabolic enzymes are increasingly being recognized as regulators of inflammatory signaling, the role of Dipeptidase-1 (DPEP1) in endothelial dysfunction remains unclear.MethodsEA.hy926 cells were exposed to serum from 10 patients with septic shock and 6 healthy controls, while LPS treatment was used to establish an in vitro model of sepsis-associated endothelial injury. DPEP1 expression was assessed by qRT-PCR and Western blotting. Gain- and loss-of-function approaches were used to evaluate the effects of DPEP1 on cell viability, apoptosis, permeability, angiogenic capacity, and production of inflammatory cytokines. Transcriptomic analysis was performed to identify downstream regulators and signaling pathways, followed by validation using immunofluorescence and Western blotting.ResultsDPEP1 expression increased in EA.hy926 cells under septic conditions. Overexpression of DPEP1 reduced cell viability, increased apoptosis and permeability, impaired tube formation, and enhanced the production of inflammatory cytokines, whereas DPEP1 knockdown had the opposite effects. Transcriptomic analysis revealed the enrichment of genes involved in transcriptional regulation and inflammatory signaling. Among these, EGR1 expression was reduced following DPEP1 knockdown. Functional experiments showed that DPEP1 modulated the nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 and phosphorylation of IκBα, while restoration of EGR1 partially rescued NF-κB activation.ConclusionDPEP1 contributes to endothelial dysfunction under septic conditions and is associated with enhanced inflammatory response. These findings suggest that DPEP1 may promote NF-κB activation in an EGR1-dependent manner, thereby enhancing inflammatory signaling in endothelial cells.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome caused by dysregulated host response to infection.1 A central feature of sepsis is endothelial dysfunction, which contributes to vascular leakage, inflammation, and organ failure.2,3 As the interface between circulating blood and tissues, endothelial cells not only maintain barrier integrity, but also actively regulate vascular tone, hemostasis, and immune responses.4 During sepsis, endothelial cells are among the first cells to encounter pathogens and inflammatory mediators. These signals trigger endothelial activation, leading to increased vascular permeability, enhanced production of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, and upregulation of adhesion molecules.5,6 In addition, microbial products and endogenous mediators engage in intracellular signaling pathways, including NF-κB and MAPK, promoting a shift toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype.7,8 This process is accompanied by the release of cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, CCL2, and CXCL1, which further disrupt endothelial barrier integrity and drive vascular leakage and tissue edema,6 highlighting the need to define the molecular pathways that regulate endothelial activation during sepsis.

Recent evidence suggests that metabolic enzymes can influence inflammatory signaling beyond their conventional metabolic functions.9,10 DPEP1 is a membrane-associated metabolic enzyme involved in leukotriene metabolism and has also been linked to leukocyte recruitment and inflammatory tissue injury.11 Leukotrienes are potent mediators that promote vascular permeability, leukocyte recruitment, and endothelial injury, which are key features of sepsis.12 DPEP1 is also involved in the regulation of inflammatory responses and host–pathogen interactions.13 It has been reported to modulate neutrophil recruitment and contribute to inflammatory tissue injury in acute settings, suggesting a pivotal role in shaping endothelial responses during inflammation.14 However, studies on DPEP1 remain relatively limited, with most focusing on its role in cancer, where dysregulated DPEP1 expression has been associated with tumor progression and poor prognosis.15 In addition, emerging evidence suggests a role for DPEP1 in inflammatory injury. In models of acute kidney injury and ischemia–reperfusion, DPEP1 has been shown to facilitate leukocyte recruitment, and its inhibition reduces inflammatory cell infiltration and tissue damage.13,14 Similarly, in experimental acute lung injury, genetic deletion of DPEP1 attenuated LPS-induced inflammation, reduced cellular apoptosis, and improves survival.14,16 These observations raise the possibility that DPEP1 may influence endothelial activation and vascular dysfunction under septic conditions. However, whether DPEP1 directly regulates endothelial inflammatory signaling during sepsis and the downstream mechanisms involved remain unclear.

In this study, endothelial cells exposed to septic serum or LPS were used to examine the expression and functional role of DPEP1. We assessed its effects on endothelial cell survival, barrier integrity, angiogenic capacity, and inflammatory responses. To further define the underlying mechanisms, transcriptomic analysis was integrated with functional validation to explore the link between DPEP1 and transcriptional regulation, with a particular focus on EGR1 and NF-κB signaling, thereby providing experimental support for how DPEP1 contributes to endothelial inflammation and dysfunction in sepsis.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Approval of Participants

Blood samples were collected between March and September 2025 from 10 patients diagnosed with septic shock and 6 age-matched healthy controls from the Department of Intensive Care Unit of the Nanning Second People’s Hospital. Healthy control samples were obtained from individuals without an infection or underlying inflammatory diseases. Serum was separated from whole blood by centrifugation and used in subsequent experiments. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Nanning Second People’s Hospital (No. Y2025010).

Cell Culture

EA.hy926 endothelial cells (ATCC CRL-2922) were provided by the Fuheng Cell Center (catalog no. FH0279; Shanghai, China) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The authenticity of the cell lines was confirmed by short tandem repeat profiling. The cells were maintained as monolayers under standard culture conditions and passaged routinely. To evaluate the effects of serum from patients with septic shock on endothelial cells, EA.hy926 cells were incubated with 10% septic serum in DMEM for 4 h. Cells treated with 10% FBS or serum from healthy individuals at the same concentration were used as controls. For the in vitro sepsis model induced by LPS, EA.hy926 cells were stimulated with LPS at a concentration of 1 μg/mL, whereas untreated cells served as controls. All cells were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2. After treatment, the cells were collected for subsequent analyses.

Cell Transfection

Cell transfection was performed using the jetPRIME transfection reagent (Polyplus, Illkirch, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. EA.hy926 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1×105 cells/mL and cultured until they reached 60–80% confluence. For transfection, 2 μg of plasmid DNA was mixed with 200 µL of jetPRIME buffer and 4 µL of jetPRIME reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol and added to each well. The shRNA sequence targeting DPEP1 was 5′-GATCGGCCTGGTGATGGTGAACTTCTCTCGAGAGAAGTTCACCATCACCAGGCTTTTTT-3′. After 6 h, the medium was replaced, and the cells were cultured for an additional 48 h. Transfection efficiency was evaluated by qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis.

CCK-8 Assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, EA.hy926 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells/mL. After cell attachment, the cells were divided into different groups and cultured for 48 h. Subsequently, 10% CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37°C for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Flow Cytometry

Cell apoptosis was analyzed using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the cells were collected by trypsinization without EDTA, washed with cold 1× binding buffer, and resuspended at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL. A total of 1×105 cells were incubated with 5 μL Annexin V-FITC and 10 μL propidium iodide at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. Subsequently, 400 μL of 1× binding buffer was added and the samples were immediately analyzed by flow cytometry. Unstained cells were used as negative controls.

ELISA

The levels of IL-18, IL-6, and IL-1β in the cell culture supernatants were measured using ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Tube Formation Assay

Matrigel was thawed on ice and added to pre-chilled ibidi angiogenesis slides followed by incubation at 37°C for 1h. EA.hy926 cells were resuspended in DMEM at a density of 6×105 cells/mL, and 50 μL of the cell suspension was seeded into each well. The cells were then incubated for 16–18 h at 37°C. Images were captured using a phase-contrast microscope. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and four random fields per well were selected for imaging. Tube formation was quantified using the ImageJ software.

RNA-Seq Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from EA.hy926 cells (shRNA_NC and shRNA_DPEP1 groups; n = 3 per group). RNA libraries were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform with a paired-end 150-bp strategy, generating at least 6 Gb of data per sample. Adapter sequences were trimmed, and low-quality reads were removed before alignment to the human reference genome using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1). Gene expression levels were quantified and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between groups were identified using DESeq2 (version 1.32.0; |log2FC| ≥ 1, adjusted P < 0.05). Functional enrichment analysis was performed using Reactome pathways. Data visualization was conducted using ggplot2 (version v3.5.0) and TBtools (version v2.096).

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from EA.hy926 cells using RNAiso Plus reagent, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA was synthesized using a PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Japan). Reverse transcription was performed at 37°C for 15 min, followed by 85°C for 5s. qRT-PCR was performed using PerfectStart® Green qPCR SuperMix on a qTOWER3G Real-Time PCR System (Analytik Jena AG, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. GAPDH was used as an internal control for normalization of mRNA expression. The primer sequences were as follows: DPEP1 forward: 5′-CCTGC-GACACCCAGAACAA-3′; DPEP1 reverse: 5′-CGCCCAAACTGCTGTCAAT-3′; FOSB forward: 5′-GAGCTGACCGACCGACTCC-3′; FOSB reverse: 5′-CGGCAAATCTCTCACCTCCG-3′; FOS forward: 5′-CAGACTACGAGGCGTCATCC-3’, FOS reverse: 5′-TCTGCGGGTGAGTGGTAGTA-3′; EGR1 forward: 5′-CACCTGACCGCAGAGTCTTT-3’, EGR1 reverse: 5′-CTGACC-AAGCTGAAGAGGGG-3′; GAPDH forward: 5′-ACGGATTTGGTCGTATTGGG-3′; GAPDH reverse: 5′-CGCTCCTGGAAGATGGTGAT-3’. The relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western Blot

Cells were lysed on ice using RIPA lysis buffer. The total protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Protein samples were denatured at 95°C and separated by SDS-PAGE using 4–20% gradient precast gels, followed by transfer onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST at room temperature for 1 h. After blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against DPEP1 (Proteintech, 68081-1-Ig), IκBα (Proteintech, 10268-1-AP), phosphorylated IκBα (p-IκBα; Affinity Biosciences, AF2002), GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig), and β-actin (Affinity Biosciences, T0022). Following washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Beyotime, A0208) or HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Proteintech, SA00001-1) at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system and imaged with a protein imaging system. GAPDH was used as the internal control for DPEP1, whereas β-actin was used as the internal control for IκBα and p-IκBα. Band intensities were quantified using the ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence Staining

EA.hy926 cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 5 min, and blocked with 5% normal goat serum for 30 min at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with a Cy3-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at 37°C in the dark. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Coverslips were mounted using a fluorescence quenching-resistant mounting medium and images were acquired using an Olympus BX53 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Endothelial Permeability Assay

Endothelial permeability was measured using a transwell system. EA.hy926 cells were seeded into 0.4 μm inserts at a density of 2×105 cells/mL and cultured until a confluent monolayer was formed. The cells were then treated with LPS for 24 h. FITC-dextran was added to the upper chamber and incubated for 1 h at 37°C in the dark. The medium from the lower chamber was collected and the fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader (excitation 492 nm, emission 520 nm). Permeability was evaluated based on FITC-dextran fluorescence in the lower chamber.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10. Normality was evaluated with the Shapiro–Wilk test, and no significant departures from normality were detected in the residuals of the parametric models (all P > 0.05; range, 0.111–0.946). Homogeneity of variance was examined using the Brown-Forsythe test. Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for comparisons among multiple groups. Welch’s ANOVA was applied when variances were unequal. For qRT-PCR data, statistical comparisons were based on ΔCt values, while relative expression levels are presented as 2−ΔΔCt. Experiments were independently repeated at least three times, with each independent experiment treated as a biological replicate. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

To explore the potential involvement of DPEP1 in this process, we assessed its expression in endothelial cells under septic conditions. Following treatment with septic serum, EA.hy926 endothelial cells showed clear morphological changes indicative of cellular injury (Figure 1A). At the same time, DPEP1 expression was markedly increased compared to that in the control cells (Figure 1B and C). To further confirm this observation, cells were exposed to increasing concentrations of LPS (0, 0.1, and 1 μg/mL) for 24 h. DPEP1 expression increased in a dose-dependent manner at both mRNA and protein levels (Figure 1D and E). DPEP1 Aggravates LPS-Induced Endothelial Dysfunction in vitro

To test whether the increase in DPEP1 expression has functional consequences, we examined endothelial cell behavior following DPEP1 overexpression. Overexpression was confirmed at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figure 2A and B). LPS treatment significantly reduced cell viability compared to control cells, and this effect was more pronounced in cells overexpressing DPEP1 (Figure 2C). Consistently, LPS increased apoptosis, which was further enhanced by DPEP1 overexpression (Figure 2D). A similar pattern was observed for barrier function. LPS increased endothelial permeability, and this effect was further aggravated by DPEP1 overexpression, indicating impaired barrier integrity (Figure 2E). In addition, LPS impaired the angiogenic capacity, as reflected by a reduction in the number of nodes and meshes, which was further increased by DPEP1 overexpression (Figure 2F). DPEP1 Knockdown Attenuates LPS-Induced Endothelial Dysfunction in vitro

Next, we examined whether reducing DPEP1 expression could alleviate endothelial injury. The efficiency of DPEP1 knockdown was confirmed at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figure 3A and B). Upon LPS stimulation, cell viability was reduced, whereas DPEP1 knockdown partially restored cell viability (Figure 3C). Consistently, LPS increased apoptosis, which was attenuated by DPEP1 silencing (Figure 3D). A similar pattern was observed for barrier function. LPS increased endothelial permeability, which was partially reversed following DPEP1 knockdown, indicating improved barrier integrity (Figure 3E). In parallel, angiogenic capacity also improved. LPS impaired tube formation, whereas DPEP1 silencing partially rescued this effect, as reflected by the increased numbers of nodes and meshes (Figure 3F). DPEP1 Regulates NF-κB Activation Through EGR1 in Endothelial Cells

To explore the underlying mechanisms, we performed a transcriptomic analysis following DPEP1 knockdown. Reactome pathway enrichment revealed that the DEGs were primarily associated with transcriptional regulation and inflammatory signaling (Figure 5A), suggesting that DPEP1 may influence gene expression programs linked to endothelial activation. Among the DEGs, EGR1, along with members of the FOS family, were downregulated following DPEP1 knockdown (Figure 5B). qRT-PCR analysis confirmed a reduction in EGR1, FOS, and FOSB expression (Figure 5C). EGR1 has been implicated in endothelial dysfunction and inflammatory responses, suggesting its potential role in downstream signaling. Therefore, we examined NF-κB activation. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that LPS induced the nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65, whereas DPEP1 knockdown reduced p65 nuclear localization; this effect was partially restored by EGR1 overexpression (Figure 5D). Consistently, Western blot analysis showed that LPS increased p-IκBα levels, which were attenuated by DPEP1 knockdown, whereas restoration of EGR1 partially rescued NF-κB activation (Figure 5E). Discussion

Endothelial dysfunction plays a central role in sepsis pathophysiology of sepsis.17 Disruption of endothelial barrier integrity and activation of inflammatory signaling pathways are key features of this process.6,18 In this study, DPEP1 expression was markedly increased in endothelial cells under septic conditions, and functional experiments showed that DPEP1 aggravates endothelial injury. Overexpression of DPEP1 enhances apoptosis, increases permeability, impairs angiogenic capacity, and promotes inflammatory cytokine production, whereas DPEP1 knockdown produces the opposite effects. These findings indicated that DPEP1 contributes to endothelial injury under inflammatory conditions.

Previous studies have linked DPEP1 to inflammatory injury, particularly its role in leukocyte recruitment and tissue inflammation.13,16 DPEP1 facilitates neutrophil adhesion and migration, and its inhibition reduces inflammatory cell infiltration and tissue damage in acute injury models.14 Although these studies have primarily focused on immune cell-mediated injury, they suggest that DPEP1 may play a broader role at the vascular–immune interface. Our findings extend these observations by showing that DPEP1 directly affects endothelial cell behavior, suggesting that its contribution to inflammatory injury may also involve mechanisms within endothelial cells and influence the progression of endothelial injury.

Transcriptomic analysis provided further insight into the downstream pathways affected by DPEP1. DEGs were enriched in pathways related to transcriptional regulation and inflammatory signaling, suggesting that DPEP1 may influence endothelial responses at the transcriptional level. Among these genes, EGR1 showed consistent changes across both the transcriptomic and validation analyses. As an immediate early transcription factor, EGR1 is rapidly induced by stress and inflammatory stimuli, and has been implicated in vascular inflammation and endothelial activation.19 Previous studies have shown that EGR1 is upregulated in endothelial cells under inflammatory and hypoxic conditions and promotes the expression of proinflammatory genes, adhesion molecules, and cytokines.20,21 In addition, EGR1 has also been reported to participate in vascular permeability regulation and leukocyte–endothelial interactions.22 The reduction in EGR1 expression following DPEP1 knockdown suggested that DPEP1 may regulate early transcriptional responses during endothelial injury.

NF-κB is a central regulator of inflammatory responses and plays a key role in endothelial activation and barrier dysfunction.4,7 In this study, the LPS-induced nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65 and phosphorylation of IκBα were attenuated by DPEP1 knockdown, indicating reduced NF-κB activity. Restoration of EGR1 expression partially rescued NF-κB activation, suggesting that EGR1 contributes to the DPEP1-mediated regulation of inflammatory signaling. Previous studies have shown that EGR1 acts as an upstream regulator of inflammatory gene expression and functionally interacts with NF-κB signaling in various inflammatory conditions, including through the regulation of cytokine transcription and endothelial activation.23 In addition, EGR1 may promote inflammatory signaling by modulating transcriptional programs that govern NF-κB–dependent responses.24 Taken together, these findings suggest that DPEP1 modulates inflammatory responses in endothelial cells, at least in part, through the EGR1–NF-κB signaling axis.

In summary, our data highlight the role of DPEP1 in regulating inflammatory responses in endothelial cells and provide new insights into the mechanisms underlying endothelial dysfunction in sepsis. However, several limitations should be considered. First, this study was primarily based on a single endothelial cell line and in vitro models, and the role of DPEP1 in other endothelial cell types and in vivo remains to be validated. Second, the relatively small sample size may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Third, although EGR1 appears to be involved in mediating DPEP1-dependent NF-κB activation, additional regulatory pathways may also be involved. Future studies should clarify the upstream regulation of DPEP1 and evaluate whether targeting the DPEP1–EGR1–NF-κB axis may offer therapeutic benefits in sepsis.
