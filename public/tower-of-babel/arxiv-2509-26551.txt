Pretrain–Test Task Alignment Governs Generalization in In-Context Learning
Mary I. Letey, Jacob A. Zavatone-Veth, Yue M. Lu, Cengiz Pehlevan

Abstract
In-context learning (ICL) is a central capability of Transformer models, but the structures in data that enable its emergence and govern its robustness remain poorly understood. In this work, we study how the structure of pretraining tasks governs generalization in ICL. Using a solvable model for ICL of linear regression by linear attention, we derive an exact expression for ICL generalization error in high dimensions under arbitrary pretraining–testing task covariance mismatch. This leads to a new alignment measure that quantifies how much information about the pretraining task distribution is useful for inference at test time. We show that this measure directly predicts ICL performance not only in the solvable model but also in nonlinear Transformers. Our analysis further reveals a tradeoff between specialization and generalization in ICL: depending on task distribution alignment, increasing pretraining task diversity can either improve or harm test performance. Together, these results identify train-test task alignment as a key determinant of generalization in ICL.

I
Introduction

Pre-training on simple next-token prediction enables Transformer models to acquire a remarkably broad array of capabilities, from language translation to code generation and mathematical reasoning
Achiam et al. 2023
;
Anthropic 2024
;
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
;
Vaswani et al. 2017
. Among the emergent abilities that enable Transformers to flexibly execute a myriad of tasks, their capacity for in-context learning (ICL) is particularly striking, as it allows for test-time task execution without task-specific pretraining
Von Oswald et al. 2023
;
Wei et al. 2022
. In other words, ICL reflects the ability to emergently meta-learn a learning algorithm during pretraining, which is then applied to learn from data within a context at test time
Akyürek et al. 2023
;
Raventós et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
.

For ICL to be effective, the tasks encountered at test time must not be totally unrelated to those encountered during pretraining, as there is no free lunch. Though substantial theoretical attention has been devoted to the question of why and how ICL emerges and how well the resulting algorithms perform, this key issue of how pretraining tasks should be selected to enable ICL in the real, test-time world remains underexplored
Lu et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
. This motivates the central question of our work:

Central Question

How does mismatch between the structure of tasks seen in pretraining and the structure of tasks faced at test time affect the ability of in-context learners to generalize?

Here, we investigate how pretrain-test task alignment affects generalization in a simple model setting: ICL of linear regression. Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We give a precise mathematical analysis of the performance of a simplified linear Attention module learning to do linear regression in-context. We model pretraining and test task distributions with arbitrary covariance structure, generalizing previous works on this model that assumed identical task distributions
Lu et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
. In this solvable model, ICL generalization is governed by a particular alignment metric between pretraining and test task distributions.

•

Though derived for a simplified linear model, we show that this alignment measure predicts the generalization performance of nonlinear Transformers trained to do linear regression in-context.

•

Finally we show in the solvable model that it is not always optimal from a generalization standpoint to pretrain on the same distribution of tasks that the model will encounter at test time, as echoed in kernel regression
Canatar et al. 2022
.

In all, our work sheds light on how pretrain-test task alignment shapes the performance of in-context algorithms. It reveals how the inductive biases of Transformers can result in optimal task
misalignment
: rather than teaching to the test, a different curriculum of pretraining tasks may better enable a Transformer to learn the algorithm that enables it to generalize well at test time.

I.1
Related Work

Empirical studies of ICL.

Empirical work has shown that LLMs can learn diverse tasks from examples alone, with performance improving predictably with scale
Achiam et al. 2023
;
Anthropic 2024
;
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
;
Vaswani et al. 2017
;
Wei et al. 2022
;
Von Oswald et al. 2023
. Several studies document how architectural components, such as attention heads or MLP layers, are recruited during training to implement ICL
Reddy 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
;
Tong and Pehlevan 2025
;
Kratsios and Furuya 2025
. Various works have also focused on understanding what algorithms transformers can learn to perform, including gradient descent, Bayesian inference, and compression.
Ahn et al. 2023
;
Mahankali et al. 2023
;
Shen et al. 2025
;
Cole et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Singh et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Wurgaft et al. 2025
;
McCracken et al. 2025
;
Elmoznino et al. 2025
;
Lee et al. 2025
. Others have explored the role of task diversity in shaping generalization, showing that diverse pretraining induces transitions from memorization to generalization
Raventós et al. 2023
. The specific effect of data structure and its role in ICL emergence has also been studied empirically, notably by
Chan et al. 2022
highlighting the importance of
anisotropic
data for the emergence of ICL abilities. Our work provides a theoretical counterpart to these empirical investigations, as we model the generalization effects of structured task distributions.

Theoretical studies of ICL.

Theoretical work has flourished in simplified Transformer models, particularly with linear or kernelized attention. A number of studies show that these architectures can implement classical learning algorithms, including kernel regression, ridge regression, or gradient descent, purely from in-context tokens
Bai et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Akyürek et al. 2023
;
Von Oswald et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
. These insights have advanced our mechanistic understanding of ICL as algorithm emulation. However, most of these works make restrictive assumptions. Commonly, data is drawn from isotropic Gaussians, train and test distributions are matched, and generalization is studied only in the infinite-sample or population limit. Even recent studies of finite-sample ICL retain these simplifying assumptions
i.e.
without full generality of training and testing task distribution
Zhang et al. 2024b
;
Fu et al. 2023
;
Lu et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2024
. A notable exception is the work of
Goddard et al. 2025
that studies tasks sampled from separate portions of a spherical task manifold. Our work advances this “task generalization” frontier by deriving an exact expression for the ICL generalization error in the presence of arbitrary task covariances and finite-sample regimes. This allows us to explore how task-structure mismatch affects generalization, a setting largely absent from prior theoretical models.

Train-Test task alignment in other settings.

Outside of ICL, the idea of train-test task alignment in linear regression has been studied in the context of out-of-distribution generalization under target vector and covariate shifts. In particular, past works have studied which measures of alignment between train and test feature covariance matrices determine out-of-distribution generalization in high-dimensional ridge regression
Canatar et al. 2021a
;
Tripuraneni et al. 2021
;
Patil et al. 2024
;
Atanasov et al. 2025
. These works build on a substantial body of results showing how the generalization performance of ridge regression is determined by the structure of the training feature covariance—principal directions with larger population variance are learned first—and the alignment of the task vector with those high-variance directions
Canatar et al. 2021b
;
Hastie et al. 2022
;
Dobriban and Wager 2015
;
Atanasov et al. 2024
;
Advani et al. 2020
. Our work brings this perspective into the ICL setting by analyzing the effects of the pretraining covariance, and pretrain-test misalignment on generalization. We show that even in simple linear regression settings, structure mismatch induces rich, nontrivial behavior, reinforcing the broader principle that distributional alignment between pretraining and test-time data is a key driver of generalization.

II
Model Setup

We begin by setting up the solvable model used to derive the results of this work. This setup builds on previous works that apply a reduced-parameter version of linear attention to linear regression ICL
Lu et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Wu et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2020
.

ICL of linear regression.

We consider an in-context regression task: the input to the model is a sequence of the form
\{\bm{x}_{1},y_{1},\bm{x}_{2},y_{2},\ldots,\bm{x}_{\ell},y_{\ell},\bm{x}_{\ell+1}\}
, and the required output is the matching
y_{\ell+1}
corresponding to
\bm{x}_{\ell+1}
. This input is called a context, and
\ell
the context length. We consider the case that the relationship between
\bm{x}
and
y
is approximately linear:

y_{i}=\langle\bm{x}_{i},\bm{w}\rangle+\epsilon_{i}\,

(1)

for noise
\epsilon_{i}
and task vector
\bm{w}
. Thus, the model needs to form an estimate of
\bm{w}
using
\{\bm{x}_{1},y_{1},\bm{x}_{2},y_{2},\ldots,\bm{x}_{\ell},y_{\ell}\}
and then apply it to
\bm{x}_{\ell+1}
to estimate
y_{\ell+1}
.

Pretraining data.

The pretraining data batch will contain
n
sample sequences of the above form,
i.e.
, for
\mu=1,...,n
, the
\mu
th sample sequence
\{\bm{x}_{1},y_{1},\bm{x}_{2},y_{2},\ldots,\bm{x}_{\ell},y_{\ell},\bm{x}_{\ell+1}\}
related by the approximate linear mapping from (
1
),
y_{i}^{\mu}=\langle\bm{x}_{i}^{\mu},\bm{w}^{\mu}\rangle+\epsilon_{i}^{\mu}
, where now
\bm{w}^{\mu}
is the task vector corresponding to the
\mu
-th sample context.

We will sample the pretraining batch as follows: For
i=1,\dots,\ell
and
\mu=1,\dots,n
,

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{i}^{\mu}

\displaystyle\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}/d)\,,\qquad\epsilon_{i}^{\mu}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,\rho)\,,\qquad

(2)

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{\mu}

\displaystyle\sim_{\text{unif}}\{\bm{t}_{1}\,,\cdots\,,\bm{t}_{k}\}\qquad\text{where }\bm{t}_{j}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,C_{\mathrm{train}})\,\text{ for $j=1,\dots,k$}\,.

The parameter
k
here is called
task diversity
. Note that if
k<n
, the pretraining batch contains some tasks repeated across the contexts. In this way, we control both the amount
k
of actually unique tasks seen during pretraining, as well as the structure of the task distribution using
C_{\mathrm{train}}
. This distinguishes our setup from previous studies which typically focus on isotropic or matched tasks
Lu et al. 2025
;
Raventós et al. 2023
;
Fu et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
.

Linear attention.

We will study the performance of the linear self-attention block
Wang et al. 2020
on this in-context regression task. The input to the linear self-attention model is an embedding matrix
Z
made up of our context sequence. Here, following the convention of
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Wu et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2020
, we chose to embed
\{\bm{x}_{1},y_{1},\bm{x}_{2},y_{2},\ldots,\bm{x}_{\ell},y_{\ell},\bm{x}_{\ell+1}\}
as

\displaystyle Z=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\bm{x}_{1}&\bm{x}_{2}&\ldots&\bm{x}_{\ell}&\bm{x}_{\ell+1}\\
y_{1}&y_{2}&\ldots&y_{\ell}&0\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(\ell+1)},

where
0
in the lower-right corner is a placeholder token for the
y_{\ell+1}
we wish to predict. The model’s output
Shen et al. 2021
;
Katharopoulos et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2020
is given by

\displaystyle A=Z+VZ(KZ)^{\top}(QZ)/\ell

(5)

for value matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}
and key, query matrices
K,Q
such that
K^{\top}Q\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}
.
Following the positional encoding in (
II
), the linear attention model’s prediction for
y_{\ell+1}
is

\displaystyle\hat{y}=A_{d+1,\ell+1}.

(6)

Previous works from
Zhang et al. 2024a
and
Lu et al. 2025
have shown that the output
\hat{y}=A_{d+1,\ell+1}
of the model can be reduced to give a simpler, analytically-tractable model. Writing the attention and value matrices as

\displaystyle V=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}V_{11}&\bm{v}_{12}\\
\bm{v}_{21}^{\top}&v_{22}\end{array}\right],\quad M=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}M_{11}&\bm{m}_{12}\\
\bm{m}_{21}^{\top}&m_{22}\end{array}\right]\equiv K^{\top}Q\,,

the predictor expands as

\displaystyle\hat{y}=\frac{1}{\ell}\bm{x}_{\ell+1}^{\top}\bigg(v_{22}M_{11}^{\top}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}+v_{22}\bm{m}_{21}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}y_{i}^{2}+M_{11}^{\top}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell+1}\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{v}_{21}+\bm{m}_{21}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{v}_{21}\bigg).

(11)

Zhang et al. 2024a
and
Lu et al. 2025
argued that that the final two terms depending on
\bm{v}_{21}
can be removed without affecting the performance of the estimator: the first, depending on
\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{v}_{21}
, does not contain any task information, and thus does not help us estimate
\bm{w}
. The second, depending on
y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{v}_{21}
provides only a one-dimensional projection of
\bm{x}
and
\bm{w}
, and so for large-dimensional tokens, does not effectively contribute to good estimate of
\bm{w}
either. For this reason, we set
\bm{v}_{21}=\bm{0}
.
Zhang et al. 2024a
showed that this choice of parameter initialization is stable under SGD, further validating this assumption. With this simplification, we can rewrite the simplified model’s output as

\hat{y}_{\ell+1}=\tr(\Gamma H_{Z}^{\top})

(12)

for a parameter matrix

\displaystyle\Gamma\equiv v_{22}\begin{bmatrix}M_{11}^{\top}/d&\bm{m}_{21}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d+1)}\,.

(13)

and a data matrix

\displaystyle H_{Z}\equiv\bm{x}_{\ell+1}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{d}{\ell}\sum_{i\leq\ell}y_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}&\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i\leq\ell}y_{i}^{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d+1)}.

(14)

Reduced model optimization.

Given a batch
\{\bm{x}_{1}^{\mu},y_{1}^{\mu},\ldots,\bm{x}_{\ell+1}^{\mu},y_{\ell+1}^{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{n}
of pretraining data (as explained above), we can find finite-sample optimal parameters by minimizing MSE loss on next-output prediction with ridge regularization, giving

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\underset{\Gamma}{\arg\,\min}\,\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\left(y_{\ell+1}^{\mu}-\tr(\Gamma(H^{\mu})^{\top})\right)^{2}+\frac{n}{d}\lambda\tr(\Gamma\Gamma^{\top})\,,

(15)

where
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter, and
H^{\mu}
is defined by (
14
) for the
\mu
th context. We will focus on the minimum-norm predictor,
i.e.
, on the limit where
\lambda\to 0

Hastie et al. 2022
.

Test error.

We finally wish to test the pretrained model on a general task to see if the model can genuinely perform in-context regression. The test distribution
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}
is then

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{i}^{\text{test}}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}/d)\,,\qquad\epsilon_{i}^{\text{test}}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,\rho)\,,\qquad\bm{\bm{w}}^{\text{test}}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,C_{\mathrm{test}})\,.

(16)

We will measure ICL performance by the average MSE error of our optimal estimator
\hat{y}^{*}_{\ell+1}=\tr(\Gamma^{*}H^{\top})
over the test distribution,

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{ICL}}(\Gamma^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}}[(y_{\ell+1}-\tr(\Gamma^{*}H^{\top}))^{2}]\,.

(17)

We highlight here the generality of the test task distribution through the new matrix
C_{\mathrm{test}}
, allowing us to study the interaction of training and testing task structure.

Data parameters in the high-dimensional limit.

We have introduced four data parameters: token dimension
d
, context length
\ell
, pretraining batch size
n
, and task diversity
k
. As is standard in the theory of high-dimensional regression
Hastie et al. 2022
;
Advani et al. 2020
;
Atanasov et al. 2024
;
Lu et al. 2025
, we will consider a scaling limit where all four of these parameters are taken to infinity in a way such that the following ratios remain constant:

\displaystyle\frac{\ell}{d}\equiv\alpha,\qquad\frac{n}{d^{2}}\equiv\tau,\qquad\textrm{and}\quad\frac{k}{d}\equiv\kappa.

(18)

Considering this limit makes the model analytically tractable, but preserves interesting phenomena that are present at finite size. Going forward, we will refer to
\alpha
,
\tau
, and
\kappa
as the context length, batch size, and task diversity parameters, respectively.

Pretraining task quantities.

Before presenting our formula for this ICL test error, it will be helpful to first define some task-distribution quantities, which depend on the pretraining task covariance
C_{\mathrm{train}}
and task diversity parameter
\kappa
. These quantities effectively tell us how well we can reconstruct
C_{\mathrm{train}}
from the
k
-sample task covariance that the model sees during pretraining,

R_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in[k]}\bm{t}_{j}\bm{t}_{j}^{\top}\,.

(19)

Because the pretraining tasks
\{\bm{t}_{1},...,\bm{t}_{k}\}
are random,
R_{k}
is a random matrix. However, in high dimensions, the following
deterministic
quantities capture the relevant information about
R_{k}
: let the deterministic matrix
F_{\kappa}(z)
and deterministic scalar
\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(z)
be defined through the implicit equations

\displaystyle F_{\kappa}(z)

\displaystyle=\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{z}{\kappa}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(z)\right)C_{\mathrm{train}}+zI_{d}\right)^{-1}

(20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\tr(F_{\kappa}(z))\,.

(21)

Then, we have the high-dimensional equivalence

\displaystyle\left(R_{k}+zI_{d}\right)^{-1}\simeq F_{\kappa}(z).

(22)

As we define formally in Definition
1
of Appendix
B
, this equivalence holds in the sense that the (normalized) traces of
(R_{k}+zI_{d})^{-1}
and
F_{\kappa}(z)
multiplied against test matrices coincide in the high-dimensional limit. Here,
z\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a noise threshold parameter suppressing smaller eigenvalues of
R_{k}
.

Intuitively,
F_{\kappa}(z)
and
\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(z)
give us information about how much signal in
C_{\mathrm{train}}
can be recovered after a finite number
k
of pretraining samples, filtered by noise level
z
. As
\kappa\to\infty
,
R_{k}
approaches
C_{\mathrm{train}}
, and we therefore fully recover the original distribution of tasks. We will use
F^{\prime}_{\kappa}(z)
to refer to the derivative of
F_{\kappa}(z)
with respect to
z
; this gives a measure of the sensitivity of this covariance recovery matrix to the noise level
z
. These quantities will play a key role in our discussion of task alignment.

ICL Test Error Depends on Task Misalignment

III.1
Task alignment determines generalization in the solvable model

We state the main result of our analysis of the simplified linear attention model, which is an analytical formula for the ICL error (
17
) in high dimensions. Here we give only an informal statement of this result and focus on its implications; the formal statement is given by (

Result:
High-dimensional formula for the ICL error
\mathcal{E}(\Gamma^{*})

The ICL test error (

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{ICL}}(\Gamma^{*})\simeq e_{\text{scalar}}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathrm{train}},c_{\mathrm{test}})+e_{\mathrm{misalign}}(C_{\mathrm{train}},C_{\mathrm{test}})\equiv e_{\mathrm{ICL}}(C_{\mathrm{train}},C_{\mathrm{test}})

in the high-dimensional limit, where

\displaystyle e_{\mathrm{misalign}}(C_{\mathrm{train}},C_{\mathrm{test}})=\left\langle C_{\mathrm{test}},\,\,\mathcal{K}\right\rangle\,

(24)

measures the alignment between
C_{\mathrm{test}}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}\equiv qF_{\kappa}(\sigma)+(q\tilde{\lambda}-\sigma^{2})F^{\prime}_{\kappa}(\sigma).

(25)

Appearing in this formula are an effective ridge variable

and effective noise variable

given by the solution of the joint equations

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}\left(\sigma\right)=1-\tau\quad\text{for $\tau<1$}\,,\qquad\tilde{\lambda}=0\quad\text{for $\tau>1$}

(26)

\displaystyle\sigma=(\rho+c_{\mathrm{train}})/{\alpha}+\tilde{\lambda}\,.

(27)

where
\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\cdot)
is determined self-consistently as in (

). Finally

is a pretraining-error term given by (

) and

is given by (

); note that both

only depend on

through its trace

, and

.

To gain intuition for this misalignment error (
24
), a useful analogy is to consider the simplest matrix “misalignment” measure
\langle C_{\mathrm{test}}C_{\mathrm{train}}^{-1}\rangle.
Firstly, this measure captures misalignment that can occur from misalignment of the
eigenvectors
of
C_{\mathrm{test}}
and
C_{\mathrm{train}}
; see Corollary
IV.1
and its proof for further discussion. Going beyond eigenvectors, because the eigenvalues of
C_{\mathrm{train}}^{-1}
are ordered opposite to the eigenvalues of
C_{\mathrm{train}}
, this measure effectively captures the relative strength of signal directions between
C_{\mathrm{test}}
and
C_{\mathrm{train}}
: if
C_{\mathrm{train}}
and
C_{\mathrm{test}}
share the same eigenvalues, alignment will be maximized when these eigenvalues appear in the same order, and minimized when these eigenvalues appear in opposing orders. This is precisely why
\langle C_{\mathrm{test}}C_{\mathrm{train}}^{-1}\rangle
can be interpreted as a misalignment measure, as even this very simple
Ansatz
shows how misalignment arises from mismatches in the relative weighting of signal directions between train and test tasks. This relative strength argument holds for our alignment measure, where instead of
\langle C_{\mathrm{test}}C_{\mathrm{train}}^{-1}\rangle
we use
\langle C_{\mathrm{test}}\mathcal{K}\rangle
. Here
\mathcal{K}
obviously depends on
C_{\mathrm{train}}
—they share the same eigenvectors—and importantly, has the same property as
C_{\mathrm{train}}^{-1}
that its eigenvalues are oppositely ordered to the eigenvalues of
C_{\mathrm{train}}
(we prove this claim in Appendix
D
for
\tau>1
and study it numerically for
\tau<1
; we conjecture that it holds for all
\tau
).

However, this eigenvalue-ordering argument is incomplete for the ICL setting. It assumes that
C_{\mathrm{train}}
can be fully learned, but with finite context length, finite task diversity, and label noise, this cannot be the case. Thus, the model cannot access the full covariance structure, only a partial version, whose resolution depends on both sampling and noise. This is precisely what the resolvent terms
F_{\kappa}(\cdot)
and
F^{\prime}_{\kappa}(\cdot)
in
\mathcal{K}
can capture, as explained in the setup. This is analogous to the alignment measures that emerge in ordinary ridge regression under covariate shift, which capture how much of the training feature covariance can be resolved from finitely many samples
Canatar et al. 2021a
;
Patil et al. 2024
;
Atanasov et al. 2025
;
Tripuraneni et al. 2021
. Furthermore, the effective noise
\sigma
must play a key role in alignment, as the model does not know
a priori
that this is an “in-context” learning problem, and must learn to decouple the tokens from the task for each context in order to extract the task information implicit in that context. The ability of the model to do this depends on both the label noise
(\rho)
, the context length
(\alpha)
, and a sufficient number of contexts
(\tilde{\lambda})
. In fact, the form of
\sigma=(\rho+\tr[C_{\mathrm{train}}])/\alpha+\tilde{\lambda}
is also familiar from ordinary ridge regression as an effective noise-to-signal term: the optimal ridge regularization parameter balances the variance due to label noise (
\rho
) with the estimation error from having finite data
Hastie et al. 2022
;
Patil et al. 2024
;
Canatar et al. 2021b
;
Atanasov et al. 2024
. At infinite sample size, the optimal ridge is simply
\rho
, the label noise. However, at finite sample size, the effective regularization is increased due to the finite-sample estimation error, and becomes
(\rho+\sigma^{2}_{w})/\alpha
, where
\sigma^{2}_{w}
characterizes variability or complexity of the regression task. In the same way, our model has to resolve the statistics of the tokens
\bm{x}
over samples (
\ell
, measured by
\alpha
), and so we expect terms familiar from linear regression to appear in our formula.

IV
Mismatched Task Distributions Are Often Optimal

We have identified the relevant measure of alignment between the pretraining and testing task distributions for linear transformers learning to perform ICL. It is then natural to ask whether for a fixed test distribution
C_{\mathrm{test}}
, is it always optimal to pretrain on the test distribution,
i.e.
, is
e_{\mathrm{ICL}}(C_{\mathrm{test}},C_{\mathrm{test}})\leq e_{\mathrm{ICL}}(C_{\mathrm{train}},C_{\mathrm{test}})
? In this section, we show that the answer to this question is in general no, it is
not
always optimal to pretrain on the test distribution.

IV.1
Covariance Alignment and Eigenspace Structure

We first show that it suffices to consider the case in which
C_{\mathrm{train}}
and
C_{\mathrm{test}}
are co-diagonalizable, as the ICL error is extremal in this case. Note that we can focus on
e_{\mathrm{misalign}}
, as other terms in the ICL error (
23
) contained in
e_{\mathrm{scalar}}
(
C.16
) are independent of the eigenvectors of these two matrices.

Corollary IV.1:
Eigenspace alignment extremizes misalignment error

The misalignment error
e_{\mathrm{misalign}}(C_{\mathrm{train}},C_{\mathrm{test}})=\langle C_{\mathrm{test}},\mathcal{K}\rangle
is extremized when
C_{\mathrm{test}}
and
C_{\mathrm{train}}
are co-diagonalizable. Concretely, letting
\lambda_{1}(C_{\mathrm{test}})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}(C_{\mathrm{test}})
and
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{K})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}(\mathcal{K})
be the ordered eigenvalues of these two real symmetric matrices, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(C_{\mathrm{test}})\lambda_{d-j+1}(\mathcal{K})\leq e_{\mathrm{misalign}}\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(C_{\mathrm{test}})\lambda_{j}(\mathcal{K}),

(28)

with equality in either the upper or lower bound if and only if
C_{\mathrm{train}}
and
C_{\mathrm{test}}
are co-diagonalizable.

To show that this result holds, we observe that, as
C_{\mathrm{test}}
and
\mathcal{K}
are real symmetric matrices, the desired bound (
28
) is simply a restatement of Ruhe’s trace inequality normalized by
1/d
, where equality holds if and only if
C_{\mathrm{test}}
and
\mathcal{K}
are co-diagonalizable. We give a self-contained proof of this inequality in Appendix
E
(see also
Marshall et al. 2010
or
Li 2020
’s expository blog post). By its definition (
25
),
\mathcal{K}
and
C_{\mathrm{train}}
have the same eigenvectors, so the claim follows.

Therefore, the misalignment error can be minimized
or
maximized if we assume that
C_{\mathrm{train}}
and
C_{\mathrm{test}}
are simultaneously diagonalizable. We therefore restrict our attention to the co-diagonalizable setting, and write the ordered eigenvalues of
C_{\mathrm{train}}
and
C_{\mathrm{test}}
as
\lambda_{1}(C_{\mathrm{train}})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}(C_{\mathrm{train}})
and
\lambda_{1}(C_{\mathrm{test}})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}(C_{\mathrm{test}})
, respectively.

IV.2
Optimal Test Covariance for Fixed Pretraining Distribution

Before presenting results on optimal pretraining structure for fixed task structure, which is a more practical question to answer, we begin first with a simpler question: For fixed training distribution
C_{\mathrm{train}}
, is it always optimal to test on the pretraining distribution,
i.e.
, is
e_{\mathrm{ICL}}(C_{\mathrm{train}},C_{\mathrm{train}})\leq e_{\mathrm{ICL}}(C_{\mathrm{train}},C_{\mathrm{test}})
? The following result answers this question in the negative:

Corollary IV.2:
Trace-constrained optimal test covariance

For fixed
C_{\mathrm{train}}
and fixed
c_{\mathrm{train}}=c_{\mathrm{test}}
, and
\tau>1
, we have that
e_{\mathrm{ICL}}(C_{\mathrm{train}},C_{\mathrm{test}})
is minimized by the single-index spike covariance with eigenvalues

\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}(C_{\mathrm{test}})&\cdots&\lambda_{i}(C_{\mathrm{test}})&\cdots&\lambda_{d}(C_{\mathrm{test}})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}dc_{\mathrm{train}}&\cdots&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}

i.e.
, all test signal is aligned with the largest eigenvector of
C_{\mathrm{train}}
.

To see that this holds, note that
e_{\mathrm{ICL}}(C_{\mathrm{train}},C_{\mathrm{test}})
is a linear function of the eigenvalues of
C_{\mathrm{test}}
as
e_{\mathrm{misalign}}(C_{\mathrm{train}},C_{\mathrm{test}})
is linear in
C_{\mathrm{test}}
and
e_{\mathrm{scalar}}(\bm{\lambda}_{\mathrm{train}},c_{\mathrm{test}})
does not depend on
C_{\mathrm{test}}
when
c_{\mathrm{test}}=c_{\mathrm{train}}
. Furthermore, the constraint
c_{\mathrm{test}}=c_{\mathrm{train}}
is a simplex in the space of
C_{\mathrm{test}}
eigenvalues. The minimum will thus be attained along the simplex, at the vertex corresponding to the lowest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{K}
. As argued in Appendix
D
, this corresponds to the largest eigenvalue of
C_{\mathrm{train}}
when
\tau>1
. As further discussed in Appendix
D
, we conjecture this to be true for all
\tau
.

This result illustrates a bias towards exploiting low-dimensional structure seen in pretraining to optimize performance at test time. In these cases, where we are optimizing test structure
C_{\mathrm{test}}
for a fixed pretraining structure
C_{\mathrm{train}}
, we observe that the best generalization is achieved by concentrating all signal into a single shared direction, collapsing the task manifold into a single dimension. In other words, it is easier to learn to generalize over a degenerate low-rank test structure highly aligned to the pretraining structure than to generalize over the entire pretraining structure learned from limited samples. This is familiar from spectral bias results of ordinary ridge regression
Canatar et al. 2021b
;
Canatar et al. 2021a
. Note that, had our training distribution been isotropic (
C_{\mathrm{train}}=I_{d}
) then all trace-fixed test covariances will perform equally; utilizing anisotropy is crucial.

IV.3
Non-Optimality of Pretraining on Test Structure

We now investigate the original question of the optimal pretraining task covariance
C_{\mathrm{train}}
, given a particular test task covariance
C_{\mathrm{test}}
. We will provide an example showing it is in fact
not optimal
in general to have
C_{\mathrm{train}}=C_{\mathrm{test}}
. We particularly emphasize that whether or not a particular task structure is better for pretraining depends strongly on the task diversity
\kappa
.

Consider the case of power-law task distributions at both training and test time,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{train}}\propto\text{diag}\begin{bmatrix}1^{-p_{\mathrm{train}}}&\cdots&d^{-p_{\mathrm{train}}}\end{bmatrix}\,,\qquad C_{\mathrm{test}}\propto\text{diag}\begin{bmatrix}1^{-p_{\mathrm{test}}}&\cdots&d^{-p_{\mathrm{test}}}\end{bmatrix},

(29)

with the normalization constant chosen such that
\tr[C_{\mathrm{train}}]=\tr[C_{\mathrm{test}}]
. We can study the effect of changing
C_{\mathrm{train}}
relative to
C_{\mathrm{test}}
on
e_{\mathrm{ICL}}
by changing the exponent
p_{\mathrm{train}}
relative to fixed
p_{\mathrm{test}}
.

Figure
4
shows that, for low
\kappa
, pretraining on
C_{\mathrm{train}}
with higher spectral power relative to the test power can improve ICL error. We see that focusing pretraining on a low-dimensional subspace, effectively creating a strong inductive bias, can improve in-context learning performance when training data is scarce. The model generalizes better by overfitting to fewer directions, rather than learning more directions weakly. However, increasing the pretraining power too much relative to the test power will worsen ICL performance, as the pretraining task set is now too low-dimensional to cover enough variation at test time. We highlight that the potential advantage coming from increasing training spectral power weakens as soon as
\kappa
is large enough for the model to be able to resolve enough task directions during pretraining.

V
Conclusions

We have developed a framework of in-context task alignment, showing that our derived measure of
error from task misalignment
is a robust predictor of ICL performance, even in nonlinear architectures. This derivation builds on a model of in-context learning of linear regression, which we extend to
fully general
task covariates. Previous works have shown that the performance of ICL models can suffer under task shift, particularly in cases of low task diversity
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Garg et al. 2022
;
Goddard et al. 2025
. We highlight that, while task misalignment is provably a driver of ICL error, our fully-general covariance framework can be used to show that task misalignment can indeed be utilized to
improve
ICL performance in cases of limited data.

Looking forward, our analysis provides a rich model of ICL with many further avenues of investigation. A more detailed analysis of the interaction between pretraining-specific error
e_{\text{scalar}}
and misalignment error
e_{\mathrm{misalign}}
could be used to derive a general heuristic for optimal pretraining, with potential implications for practical settings. Beyond task alignment, additional highly relevant phenomena can be investigated: we highlight (1) a generalized learning transition in task diversity (empirically studied by
Raventós et al. 2023
; see Appendix
F
) and (2) test-time scaling (see
Gozeten et al. 2025
and Appendix
G
). We leave exploration of these phenomena to future work.

Appendix A
General Error Formulas

Here we will set up the definition of various test errors more generally than in the main document. As this is an in-context learning setup, there are different types of “generalization” that can be studied: generalization over the tokens and generalization over the tasks. We will define two test errors to capture these separately.

In general, we wish to understand the performance and behavior of this estimator
\Gamma^{*}
, which is pretrained on data from the pretraining distribution
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{train}}
, when tested on new data. Namely, we will analyze the average performance of an estimator
\hat{y}=\tr(\Gamma,H_{Z})
as a function of given fixed
\Gamma
under the MSE loss, which is the natural loss for a regression test. The most general this expression can be is

\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}(\Gamma)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}}\left[\left(y_{\ell+1}-\tr(\Gamma,H_{Z})\right)^{2}\right]\,

(A.1)

for a general test distribution
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{test}}
detailing how to sample tokens
x
, tasks
w
, and noise
\epsilon
at test time.

We will consider two different testing regimes: the
in-context learning (ICL)
test, where the model sees new tasks
w
, and the
in-distribution generalization (IDG)
(or
in-weights
) test, where the model sees the
exact same tasks
used in pretraining
\{\bm{t}_{1}\,,\cdots\,,\bm{t}_{k}\}
, where each
\bm{t}_{j}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,C_{\mathrm{train}})
. Explicitly, we define these test distributions and corresponding error functions as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{IDG}}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}_{\text{IDG}}}\left[\left(y_{\ell+1}-\tr(\Gamma,H_{Z})\right)^{2}\right]

(A.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\text{IDG}}

\displaystyle:=\bm{x}_{i}^{\mu}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}/d)\,,\quad\bm{w}^{\mu}\sim_{\text{unif}}\{\bm{t}_{1}\,,\cdots\,,\bm{t}_{k}\}\,,\quad\epsilon_{i}^{\mu}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,\rho)\,,

where
i\in[\ell]
and
\mu\in[n]
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{ICL}}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}_{\text{ICL}}}\left[\left(y_{\ell+1}-\tr(\Gamma,H_{Z})\right)^{2}\right]

(A.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\text{ICL}}

\displaystyle:=\bm{x}_{i}^{\mu}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}/d)\,,\quad\bm{w}^{\mu}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,C_{\mathrm{test}})\,,\quad\epsilon_{i}^{\mu}\sim_{\text{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,\rho)\,,

where
i\in[\ell_{\mathrm{test}}]
and
\mu\in[n]
.

Notice here that we’ve introduced two different context lengths:
\ell
for the IDG distribution, which is the same context length as the pretraining setup
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{train}}
given by (
2
), and
\ell_{\text{test}}
which is the context length at testing time. This allows us to later explore the effect of pretraining and testing on sequences of different context lengths.

We assume that both task covariance matrices
C_{\mathrm{train}}
and
C_{\mathrm{test}}
are well-behaved in high dimensions, specifically that
\tr[C_{\mathrm{train}}],\tr[C_{\mathrm{test}}]=\Theta(1).
This ensures the task signals are not over or under amplified as
d\to\infty
.

Lemma 1
.

Simplified test losses.
Consider IDG and ICL test distribution and corresponding error functions as given by (
A.2
) and (
A.3
). For fixed parameters
\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d+1)}
and data sampled according to test distribution
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{IDG}}
or
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ICL}}
, the corresponding errors
e_{\mathrm{IDG}}
and
e_{\mathrm{ICL}}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{IDG}}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle\simeq\rho+\tr[\Sigma R_{k}]-2\tr[\Gamma A_{\mathrm{IDG}}^{\top}]+\tr[\Sigma\Gamma B_{\mathrm{IDG}}\Gamma^{\top}]

(A.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{ICL}}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle\simeq\rho+\tr[\Sigma C_{\mathrm{test}}]-2\tr[\Gamma A_{\mathrm{ICL}}^{\top}]+\tr[\Sigma\Gamma B_{\mathrm{ICL}}\Gamma^{\top}]

(A.5)

where

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{IDG}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma R_{k}\Sigma&\left(\tr[\Sigma R_{k}]+\rho\right)\Sigma\bm{b}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(A.6)

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{ICL}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma C_{\mathrm{test}}\Sigma&\quad 0\end{bmatrix}\,,

(A.7)

\displaystyle B_{\mathrm{IDG}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma R_{k}\Sigma+\frac{d}{\ell}\left(\tr[\Sigma R_{k}]+\rho\right)\Sigma&\left(\tr[\Sigma R_{k}]+\rho\right)\Sigma\bm{b}_{k}\\
\left(\tr[\Sigma R_{k}]+\rho\right)(\Sigma\bm{b}_{k})^{\top}&\left(\tr[\Sigma R_{k}]+\rho\right)^{2}\end{bmatrix}

(A.8)

\displaystyle B_{\mathrm{ICL}}

Appendix B
Random Matrix Theory calculation

Note that while Result
1
is offered for general token covariance
\Sigma
, all steps and results in this section and the following section assume
\Sigma=I_{d}
.

The setup and structure of this formalism and corresponding results will follow Section SI.3 and SI.4 of
Lu et al. 2025
. There will be key differences caused by the different task statistics
\mathcal{N}(0,C_{\mathrm{train}}),\mathcal{N}(0,C_{\mathrm{test}})
that we use in this work compared to the isotropic tasks used in
Lu et al. 2025
. Since the token structure is the same between the work of
Lu et al. 2025
and our work here, we will not prove various error bounding claims or approximations rigorously here, as such bounds would be identical to what can be found in
Lu et al. 2025
. However, care will be taken to highlight the distinction and differences required for handling the additional complexity introduced by our consideration of non-isotropic tasks.

We will use
\approx
to denote two scalar quantities which converge in probability in the high-dimensional limit, or for two matrices to represent deterministic equivalence.

Definition 1
.

For two
d\times d
(possibly random) matrices
A_{d}
and
B_{d}
, we write
A_{d}\simeq B_{d}
if
\tr[M_{d}(A_{d}-B_{d})]\to 0
in probability as
d\to\infty
for any sequence of bounded spectral norm test matrices
M_{d}

Lu et al. 2025
;
Atanasov et al. 2024
.

As noted above, we will not attempt to control rates of convergence.

We begin by considering the closed-form optimized parameters given by (
15
). This optimization problem can be solved explicitly as

\displaystyle\text{vec}(\Gamma^{\ast})=\left(\frac{n}{d}\lambda I+\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}\text{vec}(H_{Z^{\mu}})\text{vec}(H_{Z^{\mu}})^{\top}\right)^{-1}\sum_{\mu=1}^{n}y_{\ell+1}^{\mu}\text{vec}(H_{Z^{\mu}})\,.

Ultimately we wish to characterize the ICL and IDG error of these parameters, which are given by Result

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{ICL}}(\Gamma^{*})

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{IDG}}(\Gamma^{*})

\displaystyle=\rho+\tr[R_{k}]-2\tr[\Gamma^{\ast}A_{\mathrm{IDG}}^{\top}]+\tr[\Gamma^{\ast}B_{\mathrm{IDG}}(\Gamma^{\ast})^{\top}]

\chi^{\mu}(\bm{w}_{\mu})
gives

\sum_{\mu\in[n]}\frac{1}{1+\chi^{\mu}(\bm{w}_{\mu})}G_{\mathrm{ext}}^{[\mu]}\bm{z}_{\mu}\bm{z}_{\mu}^{\top}+G_{\mathrm{ext}}(\pi B_{\mathrm{ext}}+\tau\lambda I)\simeq I\,.

(B.25)

Next, we will also average the term
\bm{z}_{\mu}\bm{z}_{\mu}^{\top}
on on the left-hand side of (
B.25
) over the remaining disorder in
\bm{x},\epsilon
, holding
\bm{w}
fixed, and replace
\bm{z}_{\mu}\bm{z}_{\mu}^{\top}
with this conditional expectation. Doing so introduces some small error which is bounded in
Lu et al. 2025
. From this we have

\sum_{\mu\in[n]}\frac{1}{1+\chi^{\mu}(\bm{w}_{\mu})}G_{\mathrm{ext}}^{[\mu]}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x},\epsilon}[{\bm{z}_{\mu}\bm{z}_{\mu}^{\top}}]+G_{\mathrm{ext}}(\pi B_{\mathrm{ext}}+\tau\lambda I)\simeq I\,.

(B.26)

We already have enough to compute
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x},\epsilon}[{\bm{z}_{\mu}\bm{z}_{\mu}^{\top}}]
using Lemma
2
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x},\epsilon}[{\bm{z}_{\mu}\bm{z}_{\mu}^{\top}}]

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{d^{2}}\Upsilon,

(B.27)

where because it will appear in subsequent equations we define the matrix

\displaystyle\Upsilon\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\rho+\tr[\bm{w}\bm{w}^{\top}]&\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\vecop\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{w}\bm{w}^{\top}&(\rho+\tr[\bm{w}\bm{w}^{\top}])\bm{w}\end{bmatrix}\right)^{\top}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\vecop\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{w}\bm{w}^{\top}&(\rho+\tr[\bm{w}\bm{w}^{\top}])\bm{w}\end{bmatrix}\right)&I_{d}\otimes E(\bm{w})\end{bmatrix}.

(B.28)

Now substitute this expression into (
B.26
) for the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x},\epsilon}\left[\bm{z}_{\mu}\bm{z}_{\mu}^{\top}\right]
, giving

\displaystyle\frac{\tau}{n}\sum_{\mu\in[n]}\frac{1}{1+\chi^{\mu}(\bm{w}_{\mu})}G_{\mathrm{ext}}^{[\mu]}\Upsilon+G_{\mathrm{ext}}(\pi B_{\mathrm{ext}}+\tau\lambda I)\simeq I

(B.29)

where we recall
\tau=n/d^{2}
.

“Leave-one-out” terms behave like their full-sum equivalents.
In high dimensions and for large
n
, there is negligible difference between
\sum_{\nu\neq\mu}
and
\sum_{\mu}
. Thus, we replace
G_{\mathrm{ext}}^{\mu}
by
G_{\mathrm{ext}}
, and
\chi^{\mu}(\bm{w}_{\mu})
by

\chi(\bm{w}_{\mu})\equiv\frac{1}{d^{2}}\tr\left(\left[G_{\mathrm{ext}}\right]_{\setminus 0}\cdot\left[I\otimes E(\bm{w}_{\mu})\right]\right).

(B.30)

So finally we have the expression for
G_{\mathrm{ext}}

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{ext}}\left(\frac{\tau}{n}\sum_{\mu\in[n]}\frac{1}{1+\chi(\bm{w}_{\mu})}\Upsilon+\pi B_{\mathrm{ext}}+\tau\lambda I\right)\simeq I\,.

(B.31)

Exploit finiteness of training task set.
So far we are summing over
n
task vectors, but really only
n/k
of these are unique. Thus, we can simplify (
B.31
) as

G_{\mathrm{ext}}\left(\frac{\tau}{k}\sum_{j\in[k]}\frac{1}{1+\chi(\bm{t}_{j})}\Upsilon+\pi B_{\mathrm{ext}}+\tau\lambda I\right)\simeq I\,.

(B.32)

Indeed
\chi(\bm{t}_{j})
is also self-averaging in
\bm{t}_{j}
, and as argued by
Lu et al. 2025
, concentrates to its mean

\widehat{\chi}_{\mathrm{ave}}\equiv\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in[k]}\chi(\bm{t}_{j}).

(B.33)

We can thus simplify our expressions by substituting (
B.30
) into (
B.33
) and performing the sum over
w_{1},...,w_{k}
. Here we will use the that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in[k]}\left(\rho+\tr[\bm{t}_{j}\bm{t}_{j}^{\top}]\right)

\displaystyle=\rho+\tr[R_{k}]

(B.34)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in[k]}\left(\rho+\tr[\bm{t}_{j}\bm{t}_{j}^{\top}]\right)\bm{t}_{j}

\displaystyle\simeq\left(\rho+\tr[R_{k}]\right)\bm{b}_{k}

(B.35)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in[k]}\left(\rho+\tr[\bm{t}_{j}\bm{t}_{j}^{\top}]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\simeq\left(\rho+\tr[R_{k}]\right)^{2}

(B.36)

where for (
B.35
) and (
B.36
) we have used that
\tr[\bm{t}_{j}\bm{t}_{j}^{\top}]\simeq\tr[R_{k}]
as
\tr[R_{k}]-\tr[\bm{t}_{j}\bm{t}_{j}^{\top}]
has mean 0 and variance
\Theta(1/d)
. We thus have that

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in[k]}E(\bm{t}_{j})\simeq B_{\mathrm{IDG}}

(B.37)

and so

\widehat{\chi}_{\mathrm{ave}}=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\tr\left(\left[G_{\text{ext}}\right]_{\setminus 0}\cdot\left[I\otimes B_{\mathrm{IDG}}\right]\right).

(B.38)

Final formula for extended resolvent in terms of training task sample.
The extended resolvent
G_{\mathrm{ext}}(\pi)
is asymptotically equivalent to

\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{e}(\pi)\equiv\left(\frac{\tau}{1+\widehat{\chi}_{\mathrm{ave}}}\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{\mathrm{train}}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\vecop\left(\begin{bmatrix}R_{k}&\rho_{\mathrm{train}}\bm{b}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\right)^{\top}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\vecop\left(\begin{bmatrix}R_{k}&\rho_{\mathrm{train}}\bm{b}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\right)&I_{d}\otimes B_{\text{IDG}}\end{bmatrix}+\pi B_{\mathrm{ext}}+\tau\lambda I\right)^{-1}

(B.39)

with
\widehat{\chi}_{\text{ave}}
defined self-consistently by

\widehat{\chi}_{\mathrm{ave}}\equiv\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in[k]}\chi(\bm{t}_{j})=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[{\tr\left(\left[G_{\mathrm{ext}}\right]_{\setminus 0}\cdot\left[I_{d}\otimes B_{\mathrm{IDG}}\right]\right)}\right]

(B.40)

with

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{k}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in[k]}\bm{t}_{j}\,,\qquad R_{k}\equiv\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in[k]}\bm{t}_{j}\bm{t}_{j}^{\top}\,,\qquad\rho_{\mathrm{train}}\equiv\rho+\tr[R_{k}]\,,

(B.41)

\displaystyle B_{\text{IDG}}

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{bmatrix}R_{k}+\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{train}}}{\alpha}I_{d}&\rho_{\mathrm{train}}\bm{b}_{k}\\
\rho_{\mathrm{train}}\bm{b}_{k}^{\top}&\rho_{\mathrm{train}}^{2}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(B.42)

Simplifying self-consistency variable
\chi
.

\chi
is defined in terms of the dominant component
[G_{\mathrm{ext}}]_{\backslash 0}
of
G_{\mathrm{ext}}
. To simplify this, choose ansatz for
B_{\mathrm{ext}}
as

B_{\mathrm{ext}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&I_{d}\otimes B_{\mathrm{test}}\end{bmatrix}

where
B_{\mathrm{test}}
will later be
B_{\text{ICL}}
or
B_{\text{IDG}}
depending on which error we compute. Then
[G_{\mathrm{ext}}]_{\backslash 0}
can be expanded as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\tau}{1+\chi_{\pi}}I_{d}\otimes B_{\text{IDG}}+\pi I_{d}\otimes B_{\mathrm{test}}+\tau\lambda I_{d}\otimes I_{d+1}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\quad=I_{d}\otimes\left(\frac{\tau}{1+\chi_{\pi}}B_{\text{IDG}}+\pi B_{\mathrm{test}}+\tau\lambda I_{d+1}\right)^{-1}

(B.43)

\displaystyle\quad=I_{d}\otimes F_{E}(\pi)

(B.44)

where

F_{E}(\pi)\equiv\left(\frac{\tau}{1+\chi_{\pi}}B_{\mathrm{IDG}}+\pi B_{\mathrm{test}}+\tau\lambda I_{d+1}\right)^{-1}.

We can use this to replace
\widehat{\chi}_{\text{ave}}
with
\chi_{\pi}
defined self-consistently by

\chi_{\pi}\simeq\frac{1}{d}\tr\left[\Big(\frac{\tau}{1+\chi_{\pi}}B_{\text{IDG}}+\pi B_{\mathrm{test}}+\lambda\tau I_{d+1}\Big)^{-1}B_{\text{IDG}}\right]

(B.45)

Relating
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{e}(\pi)
to a deterministic equivalent for
\Gamma^{*}
.

Lu et al. 2025
shows that two key quantities
\tr[\Gamma^{*}A^{\top}]
and
\tr[\Gamma^{*}B(\Gamma^{*})^{\top}]
in
\mathcal{E}(\Gamma^{*})
can also be computed from the extended resolvent
G_{\text{ext}}(\pi)
. As this is purely a matrix algebra claim, and independent of the particular task structure hidden within
\Gamma^{*}
, it generalizes immediately to our case. We thus include this result here without proof and refer to reader to
Lu et al. 2025
for a derivation.

Lemma 3
.

[From
Lu et al. 2025
]

For any matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d+1)}
,

\tr[\Gamma^{\ast}A^{\top}]=\frac{-1}{c(0)\sqrt{d}}\begin{bmatrix}0&\vecop(A)^{T}\end{bmatrix}G_{\mathrm{ext}}(0)\bm{e}_{1},

(B.46)

where
\bm{e}_{1}
denotes the first natural basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}+d+1}
. For any symmetric and positive semidefinite matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}
, if we set

B_{\mathrm{placeholder}}=I_{d}\otimes B

(B.47)

in (
B.13
), then

\tr[\Gamma^{\ast}B(\Gamma^{\ast})^{\top}]=\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\pi}\left(\frac{1}{c(\pi)}\right)\biggr|_{\pi=0}.

(B.48)

Using Lemma
3
, we can find the deterministic equivalent for
\Gamma^{*}
from

\displaystyle\tr[{\Gamma^{\ast}}A^{\top}]

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{-1}{c(0)\sqrt{d}}\begin{bmatrix}0&\vecop(A)^{T}\end{bmatrix}\mathcal{G}_{e}(0)\bm{e}_{1}

(B.49)

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{\ast}(0)}{c(0)}\cdot\frac{1}{d}\tr\left(\begin{bmatrix}R_{k}&\rho_{\mathrm{train}}\bm{b}_{k}\end{bmatrix}(B_{\text{IDG}}+\lambda(1+\chi_{0})I)^{-1}A^{\top}\right)

(B.50)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{d}\tr\left({\begin{bmatrix}R_{k}&\rho_{\mathrm{train}}\bm{b}_{k}\end{bmatrix}(B_{\text{IDG}}+\lambda(1+\chi_{0})I)^{-1}}A^{\top}\right).

(B.51)

and thus

\displaystyle\Gamma^{*}_{\mathrm{eq}}\simeq{\begin{bmatrix}R_{k}&\rho_{\mathrm{train}}\bm{b}_{k}\end{bmatrix}(B_{\text{IDG}}+\lambda(1+\chi_{0})I)^{-1}}

(B.52)

Self-consistency equations and finite task sample averages

So far, everything is left in terms of the quantities
\bm{b}_{k},R_{k},B_{\text{IDG}}
that depend on a typical sample of
k
task vectors
w_{1}\,,\cdots\,,w_{k}
. What remains to be analyzed is the behavior of this sample and its sample statistics,
i.e.
, how does the finiteness of
k
affect
\Gamma^{*}
. The following steps will deal with this.

Characterization of
\chi_{0}
We see that we almost have a characterization for the deterministic equivalent of
\Gamma^{*}
but we need to understand what
\lambda(1+\chi_{0})
term is doing. From (
B.45
) we know
\chi_{0}
is defined self-consistently from

\displaystyle\chi_{0}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{d}\tr\left[\Big(\frac{\tau}{1+\chi_{0}}B_{\text{IDG}}+\lambda\tau I_{d+1}\Big)^{-1}B_{\text{IDG}}\right]

(B.53)

\displaystyle\frac{\tau\chi_{0}}{1+\chi_{0}}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{d}\tr\left[\Big(B_{\text{IDG}}+\lambda(1+\chi_{0})I_{d+1}\Big)^{-1}B_{\text{IDG}}\right]

(B.54)

\displaystyle=\frac{d+1}{d}-\lambda(1+\chi_{0})\frac{1}{d}\tr\left[\Big(B_{\text{IDG}}+\lambda(1+\chi_{0})I_{d+1}\Big)^{-1}\right]

(B.55)

Remember
B_{\text{IDG}}
is a
(d+1)\times(d+1)
block matrix with upper
d\times d
block given by
R_{k}+\rho_{\mathrm{train}}/\alpha
. We wish to express the above resolvent just in terms of
R_{k}
.

Working heuristically and block-inverting the
B_{\text{IDG}}+\lambda(1+\chi)
resolvent we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\tr\left[\Big(B_{\text{IDG}}+\lambda(1+\chi_{0})I_{d+1}\Big)^{-1}\right]\simeq\frac{1}{d}\tr(F_{R}(\sigma))\,

(B.56)

where

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{train}}}{\alpha}+\lambda(1+\chi_{0})

(B.57)

\displaystyle F_{R}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\equiv(R_{k}+\sigma I_{d})^{-1}

(B.58)

Sample covariance
R_{k}
and the Stieltjes transform.
We thus have

\frac{\tau\chi_{0}}{1+\chi_{0}}\simeq\frac{d+1}{d}-\lambda(1+\chi_{0})\frac{1}{d}\tr[F_{R}(\sigma_{0})]\simeq 1-\lambda(1+\chi_{0})\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}\left(\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{train}}}{\alpha}+\lambda(1+\chi_{0})\right)

where
\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)
is the asymptotic equivalent of the Stieltjies transform of the Wishart resolvent

F_{R}(\sigma)=(R_{k}+\sigma I_{d})^{-1}.

Following
Atanasov et al. 2024
for correlated feature wisharts, we have

\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{d}s\tr((C_{\mathrm{train}}+s\sigma\mathbb{I})^{-1})

(B.59)

where
s
is defined by the self consistency equation

\frac{1}{s}=1-\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{1}{d}\tr(C_{\mathrm{train}}(C_{\mathrm{train}}+s\sigma\mathbb{I})^{-1})=1-\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{\sigma}{\kappa}{\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)}

(B.60)

and also only depends on
\sigma,\kappa,
and
C_{\mathrm{train}}
. In other words, we have a self-consistency equation for
\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)
as

\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)=\tr\left[\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{\sigma}{\kappa}{\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)}\right)C_{\mathrm{train}}+\sigma I_{d}\right)^{-1}\right]\,.

(B.61)

Similarly, we can express
F_{R}
in deterministic form as

\displaystyle(R_{k}+\sigma I_{d})^{-1}\simeq F_{\kappa}(\sigma)\equiv\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{\sigma}{\kappa}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)\right)C_{\mathrm{train}}+\sigma I_{d}\right)^{-1}\,.

(B.62)

Note that

(R_{k}+\sigma I_{d})^{-2}\simeq-\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\sigma}F_{\kappa}(\sigma)=-F_{\kappa}(\sigma)\left(\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)+\frac{\sigma}{\kappa}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\sigma}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)\right)C_{\mathrm{train}}+I_{d}\right)F_{\kappa}(\sigma)\,.

(B.63)

Given a particular problem with a particular
C_{\mathrm{train}}
, each of these can be computed.

Self-Consistency equation for
\chi_{0}

Recall to characterize
\Gamma^{*}_{\mathrm{eq}}
we need to understand
\lambda(1+\chi_{0})\equiv\tilde{\lambda}
. We now know this variable is defined by

\tilde{\lambda}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}\left(\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{train}}}{\alpha}+\tilde{\lambda}\right)-\frac{\lambda\tau}{\tilde{\lambda}}=1-\tau

(B.64)

In the ridgeless
\lambda\to 0
limit this equation becomes

\tilde{\lambda}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}\left(\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{train}}}{\alpha}+\tilde{\lambda}\right)=1-\tau\,.

(B.65)

While this equation has nontrivial solutions for
\tau<1
, its only solution is
\tilde{\lambda}=0
when
\tau>1
.

Final deterministic equivalence for
\Gamma^{*}
in terms of Wishart resolvent.
Recall from above that

\Gamma^{\ast}_{\mathrm{eq}}=(B_{\text{IDG}}+\hat{\lambda}\,I_{d+1})^{-1}{\begin{bmatrix}R_{k}&\rho_{\mathrm{train}}\bm{b}_{k}\end{bmatrix}}

We can use the trick that

\begin{bmatrix}R_{k}&\rho_{\mathrm{train}}\bm{b}_{k}\end{bmatrix}=S\left(B_{\text{IDG}}+\tilde{\lambda}_{C}I_{d+1}-\Big(\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{train}}}{\alpha}+\tilde{\lambda}_{C}\Big)I_{d+1}\right)

(B.66)

where
S
is an almost-identity matrix that selects top
d\times(d+1)
of a
(d+1)\times(d+1)
matrix. We can then simplify

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\ast}_{\mathrm{eq}}

\displaystyle=S\left(I-\Big(\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{train}}}{\alpha}+\tilde{\lambda}\Big)(B_{\text{IDG}}+\tilde{\lambda}I)^{-1}\right)

(B.67)

\displaystyle\simeq\begin{bmatrix}I-\sigma F_{R}(\sigma)&0\end{bmatrix}\,.

(B.68)

We stress that this is an approximation that comes from the full block inversion of the resolvent of
B_{\text{IDG}}
, but is robustly handled in
Lu et al. 2025
.

Characterization of Quadratic term in error.

The only remaining error term left to approximate is those with the form
\Gamma B\Gamma^{\top}
. This is what we introduced
\pi
and
B_{\mathrm{ext}}
for.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\tr\left(I_{d}\Gamma B_{\mathrm{test}}\Gamma^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\vecop(\Gamma)^{\top}\Pi\vecop(\Gamma)=\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\pi}\frac{1}{c(\pi)}(\pi=0)

(B.69)

for
B_{\mathrm{placeholder}}=I_{d}\otimes B_{\mathrm{test}}.

The definition of
c(\pi)
comes from the scalar term in the block inversion of
G_{\mathrm{ext}}(\pi)
,

{\mathcal{G}}_{e}(\pi)=\begin{bmatrix}c^{\ast}(\pi)&-c^{\ast}(\pi)\,(\bm{q}^{\ast}(\pi))^{\top}\\
-c^{\ast}(\pi)\,\bm{q}^{\ast}(\pi)&I\otimes F_{E}(\chi_{\pi})+c^{\ast}(\pi)\bm{q}^{\ast}(\pi)(\bm{q}^{\ast}(\pi))^{\top}\end{bmatrix},

(B.70)

with

\frac{1}{c^{\ast}(\pi)}=\frac{\tau\rho_{\mathrm{train}}}{1+\chi_{\pi}}+\lambda\tau-\frac{\tau^{2}}{(1+\chi_{\pi})^{2}}\frac{1}{d}\tr\left(\begin{bmatrix}R_{k}&\rho_{\mathrm{train}}\bm{b}_{k}\end{bmatrix}F_{E}(\chi_{\pi})\begin{bmatrix}R_{k}&\rho_{\mathrm{train}}\bm{b}_{k}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\right).

(B.71)

with

\displaystyle\chi_{\pi}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{d}\tr\left[\Big(\frac{\tau}{1+\chi_{\pi}}B_{\text{IDG}}+\pi B+\lambda\tau I_{d+1}\Big)^{-1}B_{\mathrm{IDG}}\right]

(B.72)

\displaystyle F_{E}(\chi_{\pi})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\tau}{1+\chi_{\pi}}B_{\text{IDG}}+\pi B_{\mathrm{test}}+\tau\lambda I_{d+1}\right)^{-1}

(B.73)

Now following through the calculus, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\pi}F_{E}(\chi_{\pi})

\displaystyle=-F_{E}(\chi_{\pi})\left(B_{\mathrm{test}}-\frac{\tau}{(1+\chi_{\pi})^{2}}\left(\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\pi}\chi_{\pi}\right)B_{\mathrm{IDG}}\right)F_{E}(\chi_{\pi})

(B.74)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\pi}\chi_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\tr\left[\left(\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\pi}F_{E}(\chi_{\pi})\right)B_{\text{IDG}}\right]

(B.75)

Plugging (
B.74
) into (
B.75
) and simplifying for
\chi_{0}^{\prime}
gives

\frac{\tau\chi^{\prime}_{0}}{(1+\chi_{0})^{2}}=\frac{\frac{1}{d}\tr\left(B_{\mathrm{test}}F_{0}B_{\text{IDG}}F_{0}\right)}{\frac{1}{d}\tr(F_{0}B_{\text{IDG}}F_{0}B_{\text{IDG}})-\tau}=\frac{\frac{1}{d}\tr(B_{\mathrm{test}}F_{0})-\tilde{\lambda}\frac{1}{d}\tr(B_{\mathrm{test}}F_{0}^{2})}{1-2\tilde{\lambda}\frac{1}{d}\tr(F_{0})+\tilde{\lambda}^{2}\frac{1}{d}\tr(F_{0}^{2})-\tau}

(B.76)

for

F_{0}\equiv\left(B_{\text{IDG}}+\tilde{\lambda}I_{d+1}\right)^{-1}\,,\qquad\tilde{\lambda}\equiv\lambda(1+\chi_{0})\,.

We can finally simplify the quadratic term as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\pi}\frac{1}{c(\pi)}\right)(\pi=0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\tr(\Gamma^{\ast}_{\mathrm{eq}}B_{\mathrm{test}}\Gamma^{\ast}_{\mathrm{eq}})

\displaystyle\quad-\tau\frac{\chi_{0}^{\prime}}{(1+\chi_{0})^{2}}\left(\rho_{\mathrm{train}}-\frac{1}{d}\tr(\Gamma^{\ast}_{\mathrm{eq}}A_{\text{IDG}}^{\top})-\tilde{\lambda}\frac{1}{d}\tr(\Gamma^{\ast}_{\mathrm{eq}}(\Gamma^{\ast}_{\mathrm{eq}})^{\top})\right)

(B.77)

Appendix D
Ordering of Eigenvalues

Here we analyze the spectrum of the
C_{\mathrm{train}}
-dependent component of the misalignment error term (
24

\mathcal{K}\equiv qF_{\kappa}(\sigma)+(q\tilde{\lambda}-\sigma^{2})F^{\prime}_{\kappa}(\sigma)\,.

Given the self-consistent definitions of

and

in (

for

\displaystyle A_{\kappa}

\displaystyle\equiv 1-\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{\sigma}{\kappa}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)

(D.1)

\displaystyle B_{\kappa}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\kappa}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)+\frac{\sigma}{\kappa}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\sigma)\,.

(D.2)

Conjecture:
Eigenvalue Ordering

We wish to show that

\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{K})\leq\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{K})\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{d}(\mathcal{K})

\lambda_{1}(C_{\mathrm{train}})>\lambda_{2}(C_{\mathrm{train}})>\cdots>\lambda_{d}(C_{\mathrm{train}})\,,

C_{\mathrm{train}}
.

We will separate this into two cases. For
\tau>1
we provide a rigorous proof. For
\tau<1

we cannot currently provide a rigorous proof with the same methodology. Instead, we provide numerical plots supporting our claim, with the goal of proving this formally for the
\tau<1

Lemma
.

We have that
1-\kappa\leq\sigma\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)\leq 1
and
\sigma^{2}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\sigma)\leq\kappa-1.

Proof.

R_{k}
is a positive semi-definite matrix and so each eigenvalue of
\sigma(R_{k}+\sigma I_{d})^{-1}
is a non-negative increasing function of
\sigma\geq 0
. Therefore
\sigma\tr[(R_{k}+\sigma I_{d})^{-1}]
is increasing in
\sigma
and so

\lim_{d\to\infty}\sigma\tr[(R_{k}+\sigma I_{d})^{-1}]=\sigma\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)

is an increasing non-negative function of
\sigma
. Thus we have

0\leq\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\sigma\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)\leq\sigma\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma).

Also as
R_{k}
is positive definite it is obvious that

\sigma\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)=\lim_{d\to\infty}\sigma\tr[(R_{k}+\sigma I_{d})^{-1}]\leq 1.

By an equivalent argument,

\sigma^{2}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\sigma)\leq\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\sigma^{2}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\sigma)\leq 0\,.

It’s helpful to first write the self-consistency equation

\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)=\tr\left[\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{\sigma}{\kappa}{\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)}\right)C_{\mathrm{train}}+\sigma I_{d}\right)^{-1}\right]

(D.3)

for
\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)
equivalently as a self-consistency equation for the “renormalized ridge”
\tilde{\sigma}
of this Wishart resolvent, defined by

\tilde{\sigma}\tr[(C_{\mathrm{train}}+\tilde{\sigma})^{-1}]=\sigma\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)

or equivalently

\tilde{\sigma}=\frac{\sigma}{1-\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{\kappa}\tr\left[\left(C_{\mathrm{train}}+\tilde{\sigma}\right)^{-1}\right]}\,.

(D.4)

In the
\sigma\to 0
limit,
\tilde{\sigma}
limits to 0 when
\kappa>1
, and limits to the unique solution of

1-\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{\kappa}\tr\left[\left(C_{\mathrm{train}}+\tilde{\sigma}\right)^{-1}\right]=0

(D.5)

for
\kappa<1
. For our purposes, this means that

\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\sigma\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)=\begin{cases}1-\kappa\,,&\kappa<1\\
0\,,&\kappa>1\end{cases}\,.

(D.6)

Differentiating with respect to
\sigma
gives

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\sigma^{2}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\kappa-1\,,&\kappa<1\\
0\,,&\kappa>1\end{cases}\,

(D.7)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa-1

and so
1-\kappa\leq\sigma\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)
and
\sigma^{2}\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\sigma)\leq\kappa-1
.
∎

With this lemma in hand, we can prove the main claim. For
\tau>1
, we have
\tilde{\lambda}=0
and so
\sigma=(\rho+c_{\mathrm{train}})/\alpha
and

\lambda_{i}(\mathcal{K})=\frac{1}{A_{\kappa}\lambda_{i}(C_{\mathrm{train}})+\sigma}\left(q+\sigma^{2}\frac{B_{\kappa}\lambda_{i}(C_{\mathrm{train}})+1}{A_{\kappa}\lambda_{i}(C_{\mathrm{train}})+\sigma}\right)\,.

Without loss of generality, let’s compare
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{K})
and
\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{K})
. First, we can see from the Lemma above that

, and thus we have that

\frac{1}{A_{\kappa}\lambda_{1}(C_{\mathrm{train}})+\sigma}\leq\frac{1}{A_{\kappa}\lambda_{2}(C_{\mathrm{train}})+\sigma}

.

Furthermore, have

We reason about this quantity as follows. We have that

For

Appendix E
Trace Inequalities for Misalignment

In this appendix, we give a self-contained expository proof of Ruhe’s trace inequality in the form we require for Corollary
IV.1
. We actually present two lines of analysis: first a direct proof of the inequality, and then an alternative analysis based on Riemannian optimization.

We begin by recalling the general setup: let
A
and
B
be
d\times d
real symmetric matrices. By the spectral theorem, they are orthogonally diagonalizable, with non-increasingly ordered real eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(A)\geq\lambda_{2}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}(A)
and
\lambda_{1}(B)\geq\lambda_{2}(B)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}(B)
, respectively. We want to show that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(A)\lambda_{d-j+1}(B)\leq\tr(AB)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(A)\lambda_{j}(B),

(E.1)

with equality attained in either bound if and only if
A
and
B
are co-diagonalizable.

Writing the eigendecompositions of
A
and
B
as

\displaystyle A=O_{A}\Lambda_{A}O_{A}^{\top}\quad\textrm{and}\quad B=O_{B}\Lambda_{B}O_{B}^{\top},

(E.2)

respectively, where
O_{A}O_{A}^{\top}=I_{d}
,
O_{B}O_{B}^{\top}=I_{d}
,
[\Lambda_{A}]_{ij}=\lambda_{i}(A)\delta_{ij}
, and
[\Lambda_{B}]_{ij}=\lambda_{i}(B)\delta_{ij}
, we see immediately that it suffices to consider the case in which
A
is diagonal, with non-decreasing elements, as

\displaystyle\tr(AB)=\tr(\Lambda_{A}O\Lambda_{B}O^{\top})\quad\textrm{where}\quad O\equiv O_{A}^{\top}O_{B},

(E.3)

and the matrix
O\Lambda_{B}O^{\top}
has the same eigenvalues as
B
. We can see that
A
and
B
are co-diagonalizable if and only if
O\Lambda_{B}O^{\top}
is diagonal. Moreover, we can see that the claimed bounds can be attained only if
O\Lambda_{B}O^{\top}
is in fact equal to
\Lambda_{B}
up to permutation of its diagonal elements. What remains is to show that they are in fact bounds.

E.1
Direct proof of Ruhe’s inequality

We now give a direct proof of the claimed inequality. We remark that the version of Ruhe’s inequality proved in
Marshall et al. 2010
is not sufficient for our purposes, as it assumes that both
A
and
B
are positive semi-definite,
i.e.
, that
\lambda_{d}(A)\geq 0
and
\lambda_{d}(B)\geq 0
. We instead follow the proof for general Hermitian matrices outlined in
Li 2020
’s blog post.

The strategy of this proof is to proceed by induction on the matrix dimension
d
. The claim clearly holds for
d=1
, as then
A
and
B
are scalars equal to their only eigenvalue, and both bounds collapse. Now consider
d>1
. Assuming—as noted before—that
A
is diagonal with elements given by its ordered eigenvalues, we have

\displaystyle\tr(AB)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(A)B_{jj}

(E.4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}[\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{d}(A)+\lambda_{d}(A)]B_{jj}

(E.5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}[\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{d}(A)]B_{jj}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{d}(A)B_{jj}.

(E.6)

We now observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}[\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{d}(A)]B_{jj}=\tr(\Delta\tilde{B}),

(E.7)

where
\Delta
is a
(d-1)\times(d-1)
diagonal matrix with
\Delta_{jj}=\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{d}(A)
and
\tilde{B}
is the
(d-1)\times(d-1)
principal submatrix of
B
given by discarding its last row and column. The ordering of the eigenvalues of
A
implies the ordering

\lambda_{1}(A)-\lambda_{d}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d-1}(A)-\lambda_{d}(A)\geq 0

(E.8)

of the eigenvalues of
\Delta
.

Thus,
\tr(\Delta\tilde{B})
is the trace of a product of
(d-1)\times(d-1)
real symmetric matrices with
\Delta
having its eigenvalues in non-decreasing order along the diagonal, so on the induction hypothesis we have the bound

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}[\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{d}(A)]\lambda_{d-j}(\tilde{B})\leq\tr(\Delta\tilde{B})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}[\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{d}(A)]\lambda_{j}(\tilde{B}).

(E.9)

By the Poincaré separation theorem (also known as the Cauchy interlacing theorem), as
\tilde{B}
is a principal submatrix of the real symmetric matrix
B
, we have the inequality

\displaystyle\lambda_{j+1}(B)\leq\lambda_{j}(\tilde{B})\leq\lambda_{j}(B)

(E.10)

for all
j\in[d-1]
.

Combining these results and using the fact that
\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{d}(A)\geq 0
by our ordering assumption, we thus have the upper bound

\displaystyle\tr(AB)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}[\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{d}(A)]\lambda_{j}(\tilde{B})+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{d}(A)B_{jj}

(E.11)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}[\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{d}(A)]\lambda_{j}(B)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{d}(A)B_{jj}

(E.12)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\lambda_{j}(A)\lambda_{j}(B)-\lambda_{d}(A)\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\lambda_{j}(B)+\lambda_{d}(A)\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{jj}

(E.13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\lambda_{j}(A)\lambda_{j}(B)-\lambda_{d}(A)\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\lambda_{j}(B)+\lambda_{d}(A)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(B)

(E.14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(A)\lambda_{j}(B),

(E.15)

as
\tr(B)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{jj}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(B)
. Here, the first line uses the induction hypothesis in the form re-stated above, the second line uses the upper bound from the Poincaré separation theorem, and the remaining three lines are just algebraic simplification.

Similarly, we have the lower bound

\displaystyle\tr(AB)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}[\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{d}(A)]\lambda_{d-j}(\tilde{B})+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{d}(A)B_{jj}

(E.16)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}[\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{d}(A)]\lambda_{d-j+1}(B)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{d}(A)B_{jj}

(E.17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\lambda_{j}(A)\lambda_{d-j+1}(B)-\lambda_{d}(A)\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\lambda_{d-j+1}(B)+\lambda_{d}(A)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(B)

(E.18)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\lambda_{j}(A)\lambda_{d-j+1}(B)-\lambda_{d}(A)\sum_{j=2}^{d}\lambda_{j}(B)+\lambda_{d}(A)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(B)

(E.19)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(A)\lambda_{d-j+1}(B).

(E.20)

By induction, we therefore conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(A)\lambda_{d-j+1}(B)\leq\tr(AB)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}(A)\lambda_{j}(B),

(E.21)

as desired.

E.2
Riemannian optimization

We now give an alternative perspective on this result based on the properties of orthogonal matrices. Consider

\displaystyle f(O)=\tr(\Lambda_{A}O\tilde{\Lambda}_{B}O^{\top})

(E.22)

as a function on the space of orthogonal matrices, where
\tilde{\Lambda}_{B}
is a diagonal matrix with non-zero elements given by any permutation of the eigenvalues of
B
. Near the identity, we can write any orthogonal matrix as

\displaystyle O=I+tS+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}S^{2}+\mathcal{O}(t^{3}),

(E.23)

where
S
is a skew-symmetric matrix (
S^{\top}=-S
) and
t
is a small parameter. Substituting this expansion into
f(O)
, we have

\displaystyle f(O)=\tr(\Lambda_{A}\tilde{\Lambda}_{B})-t\tr([\Lambda_{A},\tilde{\Lambda}_{B}]S)+\frac{1}{2}t^{2}\tr(\Lambda_{A}\tilde{\Lambda}_{B}S^{2}-2\Lambda_{A}S\tilde{\Lambda}_{B}S+\Lambda_{A}S^{2}\tilde{\Lambda}_{B})+\mathcal{O}(t^{3}),

(E.24)

where
[X,Y]=XY-YX
is the matrix commutator. Irrespective of the permutation we have chosen to define
\tilde{\Lambda}_{B}
,
[\Lambda_{A},\tilde{\Lambda}_{B}]=0
, so
f(O)
is stationary at the identity.

We therefore consider the quadratic term

\displaystyle H=\frac{1}{2}\tr(\Lambda_{A}\tilde{\Lambda}_{B}S^{2}-2\Lambda_{A}S\tilde{\Lambda}_{B}S+\Lambda_{A}S^{2}\tilde{\Lambda}_{B}),

(E.25)

as it will determine whether this stationary point is a local minimum, local maximum, or saddle. As
[\Lambda_{A},\tilde{\Lambda}_{B}]=0
, we can immediately simplify this to

\displaystyle H=\tr(\Lambda_{A}\tilde{\Lambda}_{B}S^{2}-\Lambda_{A}S\tilde{\Lambda}_{B}S).

(E.26)

For brevity, define
a_{i}=(\Lambda_{A})_{ii}
and
b_{i}=(\tilde{\Lambda}_{B})_{ii}
. Then, expanding in components,

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}(a_{i}b_{i}-a_{i}b_{j})S_{ij}S_{ji}

(E.27)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{i}(b_{i}-b_{j})S_{ij}^{2},

(E.28)

as by skew-symmetry
S_{ji}=-S_{ij}
. Using the fact that the
i=j
terms clearly vanish, we have

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i<j}a_{i}(b_{i}-b_{j})S_{ij}^{2}-\sum_{i>j}a_{i}(b_{i}-b_{j})S_{ij}^{2}.

(E.29)

By re-labeling indices
i\leftrightarrow j
and using the fact that
S_{ji}^{2}=S_{ij}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i>j}a_{i}(b_{i}-b_{j})S_{ij}^{2}=-\sum_{i<j}a_{j}(b_{i}-b_{j})S_{ij}^{2}

(E.30)

so upon re-combining terms

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i<j}(a_{i}-a_{j})(b_{i}-b_{j})S_{ij}^{2}.

(E.31)

This now depends on the ordering of the eigenvalues, and thus on the permutation of indices. If the eigenvalues of both
A
and
B
are in non-increasing order such that
a_{i}\geq a_{j}
and
b_{i}\geq b_{j}
whenever
i<j
, we have
H<0
, and the identity is a local maximum of
f(O)
. In contrast, if they are oppositely ordered, then
H>0
and the identity is a local minimum. Otherwise, the stationary point is a saddle. These results are consistent with Ruhe’s inequality as proved above.

Appendix F
Phase transition with increasing pretraining task diversity

As we have seen throughout, the task diversity parameter is crucial because it quantifies the quality of the pretraining data: higher
\kappa
implies a richer span of task variations, enabling the in-context learner to infer the structure shared between pretrain and test sets more accurately. Understanding
\kappa
thus sheds light on the implicit algorithm performed by the model. Previous work from
Raventós et al. 2023
;
Lu et al. 2025
in particular have investigated the performance of ICL as
\kappa
increases. In particular,
Lu et al. 2025
showed, in the isotropic task case, that there is a phase transition at
\kappa=1
: for
\kappa<1
, the model has insufficient task diversity to be able to generalize within tasks, and is forced to memorize the training tasks, while for
\kappa>1
, the model has seen sufficient tasks to generalize in-context efficiently.

Corollary F.1:
Phase transition in task diversity

In the proportional limit of
\alpha,\tau\to\infty
such that
\tau=\alpha/\gamma
for
\gamma=\Theta(1)
fixed, there is a phase transition in
e_{\mathrm{ICL}}
at
\kappa=\mathrm{rank}(C_{\mathrm{train}})/d
. Specifically, for
r\equiv\mathrm{rank}(C_{\mathrm{train}})/d
then

\lim_{\tau,\alpha\to\infty}e_{\mathrm{ICL}}(C_{\mathrm{train}},C_{\mathrm{test}})=\begin{cases}\rho+\left(1+\frac{\rho}{\rho+c_{\mathrm{train}}}\gamma\right)\tr\left[C_{\mathrm{test}}\left(x_{1}C_{\mathrm{train}}+I_{d}\right)^{-1}\right]&\,\kappa<r\\
\rho+\left(1+\frac{\rho}{\rho+c_{\mathrm{train}}}\gamma\right)\tr\left[C_{\mathrm{test}}\left(x_{2}C_{\mathrm{train}}+I_{d}\right)^{-1}\right]&\,\kappa>r\end{cases}

(F.1)

where
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are defined self-consistently as solutions of

\displaystyle\tr\left[\left(x_{1}C_{\mathrm{train}}+I_{d}\right)^{-1}\right]

\displaystyle=1-\kappa

(F.2)

\displaystyle\tr\left[\left(x_{2}C_{\mathrm{train}}+I_{d}\right)^{-1}\right]

\displaystyle=1-r.

(F.3)

To prove this result, we first suppose that
C_{\mathrm{train}}
is invertible. We start by writing the self-consistency equation

\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{d}\tr\left(\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{\sigma}{\kappa}{\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)}\right)C_{\mathrm{train}}+\sigma I_{d}\right)^{-1}\right)\,.

(F.4)

for
\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)
equivalently as a self-consistency equation for the renormalized ridge
\tilde{\sigma}
, defined by

\tilde{\sigma}\tr[(C_{\mathrm{train}}+\tilde{\sigma})^{-1}]=\sigma\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)

or equivalently

\tilde{\sigma}=\frac{\sigma}{1-\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{\kappa}\tr\left[\left(C_{\mathrm{train}}+\tilde{\sigma}\right)^{-1}\right]}\,.

(F.5)

In the
\sigma\to 0
limit,
\tilde{\sigma}
limits to 0 when
\kappa>1
, and limits to the unique solution of

1-\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{\kappa}\tr\left[\left(C_{\mathrm{train}}+\tilde{\sigma}\right)^{-1}\right]=0

(F.6)

for
\kappa<1
. For our purposes, this means that

\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\sigma\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)=\begin{cases}1-\kappa\,,&\kappa<1\\
0\,,&\kappa>1\end{cases}

(F.7)

as well as

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\,\sigma F_{\kappa}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\,\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(1-\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{\sigma\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)}{\kappa}\right)C_{\mathrm{train}}+I_{d}\right)^{-1}

(F.8)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\left(xC_{\mathrm{train}}+I_{d}\right)^{-1}\,,&\kappa<1\\
0\,,&\kappa>1\end{cases}

(F.9)

and finally

\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\,\sigma^{2}F_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\sigma)=\begin{cases}-\left(xC_{\mathrm{train}}+I_{d}\right)^{-1}\,,&\kappa<1\\
0\,,&\kappa>1\end{cases}

(F.10)

for
x
defined by
\tr\left[\left(x_{1}C_{\mathrm{train}}+I_{d}\right)^{-1}\right]=1-\kappa
.

Using these characterizations of the
\sigma\to 0
limit, and noticing for our problem that if
\tau\to\infty
(so
\tilde{\lambda}=0
), then we must take the
\sigma\to 0
limit of
e_{\mathrm{ICL}}
, being careful to use

\tau=\frac{\rho+c_{\mathrm{train}}}{\gamma}\frac{1}{\sigma}

(F.11)

to enforce the proportional
\alpha/\tau
limit. Doing so gives the required answer.

The above proof requires explicitly that
C_{\mathrm{train}}
is invertible. If it is not, we have to be more careful with handling the
\sigma\to 0
limit of (
D.4
). One potential branch of this solution is

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{\kappa}+\frac{1}{\kappa}\tilde{\sigma}\tr[(C_{\mathrm{train}}+\tilde{\sigma})^{-1}]\to 0

(F.12)

or equivalently

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{\lambda_{i}+\tilde{\sigma}}+\frac{1}{d}(d-r)\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{0+\tilde{\sigma}}\to 1-\kappa\quad\implies\quad\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{\lambda_{i}+\tilde{\sigma}}\to\frac{r}{d}-\kappa

(F.13)

where
r
is the rank of
C_{\mathrm{train}}
. This is what causes the split in behavior of the solution at
\kappa=r/d
(which was previously 1). For
\kappa<r/d
, this is solvable at nonzero
\tilde{\sigma}
and we end up with the familiar solution branch of

\displaystyle\sigma\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)=\tilde{\sigma}\tr[(C_{\mathrm{train}}+\tilde{\sigma})^{-1}]\to 1-\kappa.

(F.14)

For
\kappa>r/d
, there is no longer a sensible solution at nonzero
\tilde{\sigma}
(since
\tilde{\sigma}
cannot be negative) and so we’re forced to take
\tilde{\sigma}\to 0
. This gives

\displaystyle\sigma\mathcal{M}_{\kappa}(\sigma)=\tilde{\sigma}\tr[(C_{\mathrm{train}}+\tilde{\sigma})^{-1}]=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{\lambda_{i}+\tilde{\sigma}}+\frac{1}{d}(d-r)\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{0+\tilde{\sigma}}\to 1-\frac{r}{d}\,.

(F.15)

This concludes the proof of the claimed result, and recovers the previous result for invertible
C_{\mathrm{train}}
.

Context length shifts

:

We have thus far assumed that the pretraining- and test-time context lengths are the same. Our general result in (
C.6
) allows for an
\alpha_{\mathrm{test}}
that differs from the training context length
\alpha
, but shows that this shift only affects a single scaling factor in
e_{\mathrm{scalar}}
, where a factor of
1/\alpha
is replaced by
1/\alpha_{\mathrm{test}}
. Despite
e_{\mathrm{ICL}}
not being monotonic in training context length, as demonstrated by
Lu et al. 2025
, it is monotonically decreasing in test context length: testing on larger-context prompts can only decrease error, and testing on shorter-context prompts can only increase error. This is demonstrated in Figure
7
for a variety of different task structures. Recall that
\alpha_{\text{test}}
only appears in
e_{\mathrm{ICL}}
through
e_{\text{scalar}}
, as part of an effective noise term: having longer test-time contexts will give the model a better estimate of the token distribution, leading to a better predictor for the task corresponding to that context.

H.1

Linear simulations are done by sampling pretraining and testing distributions as described by (
2
) and (
16
), and computing numerical
\Gamma^{*}
by (

). We simulate the ridgeless
\lambda\to 0

.

H.2
Nonlinear Architecture

The architecture used for Figure
3
is a transformer formed of two transformer-blocks, constructed as follows. The input to the architecture is the
Z
matrix (
II
). We apply a causal mask to ensure that each token attends to itself and prior tokens in the sequence. Each transformer-block is made of first applying single-head softmax attention to this masked input, with residual connection, and then normalised; this is then passed to a single hidden layer MLP with GELU activation, followed by a final residual connection and layer norm application. The final logit is computed by a dense layer projecting the output of the two transformer-blocks into a scalar.

The model is pretrained with data sampled by (
2
). We form
n

Z
-matrices from these samples, which are again the inputs to the architecture, and the model is pretrained to predict the corresponding
y_{\ell+1}
value by minimising MSE loss between the final logit and
y_{\ell+1}
. Training is done using AdamW for 1000 epochs with batch size 16 and learning rate 0.0001.

Testing is done by sampling a batch of
n
contexts from (
16
), and tracking MSE between the true
y_{\ell+1}
and the model output. This test sampling is repeated 500 times to average out noise.
