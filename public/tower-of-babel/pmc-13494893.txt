Phase angle and ultrasonographic muscle thickness are potential methods to assess 30-day mortality risk in older adults with hip fractures
Authors: Thais Caroline Silva Piccoli, Victoria Moralez Soares, Juli Thomaz de Souza, Jéssica Caroline Ferreira, David do Nascimento Pereira, Filipe Welson Leal Pereira, Sergio Alberto Rupp de Paiva, David Nicoletti Gumieiro, Daniel Dinhane, Daniela Biagioni Vulcano, Bethan E. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Phase Angle (PhA), quadriceps thickness, even when adjusted for knee height, was associated with 30-day mortality.•The composite index (PhA and MT) has the potential to outperform individual measures in discriminating 30-day mortality.•For hospitalized, bedridden patients, the combination of MT and PhA may be useful to assess muscle health.

Introduction

A hip fracture resulting from low-energy trauma constitutes a clinical diagnosis of osteoporosis, regardless of bone mineral density [1]. Therefore, patients presenting with both hip fracture and sarcopenia may be classified as having osteosarcopenia, which increases the risk of falls, complications and death. The reported prevalence of osteosarcopenia in hip fractures is variable, but commonly accepted to be higher than 50% [2]. One observational study found that 86% of the 342 patients who underwent surgery for hip fractures exhibited osteosarcopenia [3]. In addition, a review article exploring a future path for osteosarcopenia research and treatment reported that older adults with this condition had an odds ratio of 2.83 and 3.86 for falls and fractures, respectively [4].

Diagnosing sarcopenia in patients in the acute phase of hip fracture is challenging due to them being bedridden, in pain, and often delirious or cognitively impaired, limiting strength testing and access to DXA [5]. Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) and ultrasound (US) are each portable and can be performed in bedridden patients [6].

Phase angle (PhA), measured by BIA reflects tissue electrical properties related to cell quality, membrane integrity and fluid distribution, and has prognostic value in several stress conditions [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. The US can quantify quadriceps thickness with less influence of hydration, but standardization and clinical validation in hip fractures are limited [10,11]. We tested whether BIA and quadriceps US thickness, alone or in a composite index of a combination of MT and PhA, predict 30‑day mortality after hip fracture. Identifying reduced muscle mass and quality would help with risk stratification and to assess sarcopenia, for bedridden and cognitively impaired patients not able to complete the standard tests.

Subjects and methods

Study design, settings, and participants

Conducted as an observational, prospective cohort study in a quaternary University Hospital in São Paulo State, Brazil, this study was approved by an institutional ethics board (CAAE:55916422.4.0000.5411) in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and suitable screening was initiated after written informed consent. Study conducted in 2022–2023 at the Clinical Hospital of Botucatu Medical School, Botucatu, SP, Brazil.

Eligible participants were those hospitalized for a low-impact trauma and hip fracture with intended surgery. Inclusion criteria were: (i) age over 60 years, (ii) low-impact trauma from <1 meter, (iii) fracture of the proximal femur (femur neck, trans-trochanteric, or subtrochanteric), and (iv) a fracture duration of less than 72 h prior. Exclusion criteria were hemodynamic instability and failure to undergo surgery for any reason.

Procedures and variables

Clinical assessment

The Nottingham Hip Fracture Score (NHFS) was used to define the risk of 30-day mortality, and the abbreviated mental test score (AMTS) was used to assess cognitive impairment, as previously described [12,13]. NHFS is a widely used risk screening tool that includes age, gender, presence of 2 or more comorbidities, AMTS, hemoglobin, cancer history, and residency in care homes [13,14]. Additionally, a translated and transculturally adapted (for Brazil) version of Parker and Palmer's score, known as the New Mobility Score (NMS), previously developed for the hip fracture population, was used to assess pre-fracture mobility [15,16]. Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) [17], Charlson index [14], and Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) [18] were also evaluated.

Anthropometry and Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis (BIA)

Height and weight were estimated from knee height (Kh) as previously described [19,20]. BIA was performed, before the surgical procedure, with participants in the supine position, using SECA mBCA525® BIA multifrequency instrument (Hamburg, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and calibrated annually [21]. Phase angle (PhA) was measured directly, and fat-free mass (FFM), fat mass (FM), total body water (TBW), extracellular water (EW), and intracellular water (IW) were estimated using previously validated equations for the SECA equipment across different ethnicities and assuming constant hydration status [21].

Ultrasound (US)

A Philips Lumify® portable US device (Eindhoven, NE), equipped with a linear probe operating at 4−12 MHz, was used to obtain images of the thigh in the non-fractured leg, as previously reported [22,23]. Muscle thickness (MT), such as rectus femoris thickness (RFMT) and vastus lateralis thickness (VLMT), was measured in the distal third of the thigh. Acquired images were analyzed using ImageJ® by a blinded and trained assessor; sums including vastus intermedius (VIMT) were also derived. To account for body size, thickness was standardized by the square of knee height (Kh²). Composite indices of MTs (RFMT, (RF + VI)MT, VLMT, (VL + VI)MT, RF/Kh2MT, (RF + VI)MT/Kh2, VLMT/Kh2, (VL + VI)MT/Kh2, and PhA were standardized as the sum of the Z-score of each MT and the Z-score of PhA.

Handgrip strength

HGS: JAMAR® dynamometer (Sammons Preston, Bolingbrook, IL, USA) (supine, elbow 90 °), when cognition allowed [24,25].

Outcomes and definitions

The primary outcome was mortality up to 30 days after fracture, collected from medical records or via phone call. Low mobility was considered when the NMS was five or less; mental status was considered compromised by previous impaired cognition or by current delirium when the AMTS was at six or lower; and high-risk patients were defined as an NHFS of five or more [12,13,26].

Sample size and statistical analysis

The sample size was determined by comparing PhA in older people with hip fractures between survivors (PhA 5.18 ± 1.13) and non-survivors (PhA 4.5 ± 0.94), with 80% power, a 5% alpha level, and a medium effect size, resulting in 76 participants [27]. Survivors’ vs non‑survivors were compared with t‑test/Mann–Whitney/χ2 as appropriate. Discrimination was evaluated by Receiver Operating Curve and Area under the curve (ROC/AUC) with Youden cut‑offs. Variables identified as significant in the univariate analysis of muscle ultrasound and BIA data were considered for ROC curve analysis and Cox regression. For the ROC curve analysis, each variable was standardized (Z-score transformation), and a weighted composite index was calculated by multiplying each Z-score by its respective hazard ratio (HR) obtained from the univariate Cox proportional hazards model, as follows: zMT*HR + zPhA*HR. Cox regression (unadjusted; model 1: adjusted for NHFS, which indicates disease severity by including age, sex, comorbidities, cancer, cognitive impairment (AMTS), hemoglobin, and place of residency, and for previous mobility (NMS); and model 2: adjusted for CFS and CCI, indicating Frailty and comorbidities assessment. A non-weighted composite index was generated as the simple sum of the standardized variables Z-score of MT and Z-score of PhA (zMT + zPhA) and evaluated in Cox regression. Significance levels were set at 5%. Software: GPower (3.1.9.7) and Jamovi (version 2.6.26) [28].

Discussion

PhA, extracellular fluid ratio, and quadriceps muscle thickness were associated with poor outcomes. In addition, the composite index of MT and PhA, an indicator of muscle mass and quality, improved the discriminative power of each measure to predict 30-day mortality. Overall, this study reinforces the potential of BIA and US as reliable methods for assessing sarcopenia in a high-risk population, where standard assessments, such as DXA and handgrip strength, are challenging to obtain. Further, these assessments can enable early intervention to achieve better outcomes.

PhA directly reflects fluid distribution, body cell mass, and cell membrane integrity, and is influenced by inflammation, catabolism, and nutritional status [9]. Since muscle is the principal conductor of electrical current, PhA shows a positive association with muscle mass and function, partly because myocytes contain high levels of protein, glycogen, and intracellular water. An increase in extracellular water or a decrease in muscle mass reduces the PhA and is associated with a poor prognosis in several clinical situations, including critical care, surgical care, and cancer [8,9]. In this study, it is possible that higher extracellular fluid, loss of membrane integrity, and lower muscle mass were each key factors to explain mortality in older people with hip fractures. Also, a previous study reported a cut-off value of PhA for predicting 12-month mortality in hip fracture of 4.05 ° for women and 4.65 °, with 35% below the cut-off [27]. As in the present study, where the PhA cut-off for mortality was 4.55, and 31% of the participants were below this level, this shows the external validation of our data.

Regarding US measurements, one previous study reported that the average VLMT in moderately active older adults was 1.97 cm, compared with 1.25 cm in older adults with mobility limitations, consistent with age-associated muscle loss. The muscle thickness measures in this study were markedly lower than those previously reported in younger adults [23,29]. Considering older adults who have suffered a fracture, in individuals with various non-weight-bearing fragility fractures, the average VLMT was 1.37 cm [30]. In hip fracture patients with a history of mobility impairment [31], the mean VLMT was 1.02 cm, and the RFMT was 0.64 cm. Across the quadriceps muscle group, our finding is consistent with the literature (averages VLMT = 1.00 cm and RFMT = 0.70 cm) and notably lower than that of healthy older adults, indicating the severity of muscle compromise in older adults with hip fractures [23,30,31]. Yet normalizing thickness by knee height squared offers a simple approach to reduce body‑size bias when standing height or weight are difficult to measure in bedridden patients [32]. Another option for normalizing thickness is to measure the ratio of fascicle length to VLMT [23,31]. However, in the present study, fibers were more parallel to the deep fascia, making it challenging to locate the fascicle.

Finally, completing the diagnosis of sarcopenia by measuring muscle strength is even harder because it is a volitional test, and 47% of our participants presented cognitive compromise as observed by AMTS, with scores less than 6. To obtain a measurement indicative of muscle mass and quality, we combined muscle US thickness, which indicates muscle quantity, with PhA, which reflects cellularity, membrane integrity, and cellular function [7,33]. In one previous study, the product of rectus femoris cross-sectional area and PhA predicted sarcopenia in hospitalized older patients, with AUCs of 0.900 for women and 0.969 for men. The composite index MT and PhA, adjusted or not for knee height, yielded a higher AUC for discriminating 30-day mortality than the individual analyses and maintained the association with the outcome in Cox regression, even after adjustment for confounders.

Since the PhA has been explored as a predictor of poor outcomes in previous studies, the novel contribution of our research is the observation that reduced MT and the composite index of MT and PhA, even after adjustment for confounders, were associated with 30-day mortality. Confounders, included in model 1 (NHFS and NMS) and model 2 (CCI and CFS), adjust the assessments by the burden of age, sex, comorbidities, frailty, and functionality. Importantly, skeletal muscles are essential for locomotion and physical function, but they are also metabolic organs that confer bodily resilience to physical stress, such as surgical procedures [34]. Therefore, MT and PhA may reflect overall physiological reserve associated with osteosacopenia, which contributes to a higher 30-day mortality ratio, rather than being a direct determinant of mortality.

The clinical implications of this study underlies on the facts that for hospitalized patients who are bedridden and unable to perform volitional strength tests, such as HGS, the combination of muscle thickness and PhA may help complete an assessment of osteosarcopenia, considering the clinical diagnosis of osteoporosis in the presence of hip fracture and by estimating muscle mass and function, thereby increasing the ability to predict poor outcomes [33]. Moreover, these tools are portable, quick, and may help identify patients at the highest short‑term risk, supporting early multidisciplinary interventions (nutrition, mobilization, delirium prevention, and osteoporosis care) while awaiting broader validation.

Limitations include a single‑center design; incomplete BIA due to contraindications and clinical constraints; lack of gold‑standard imaging (e.g., MRI/DXA) and larger population for external validation of the Kh2 adjustment and of the composite index; serum biomarkers were not assessed; seven participants who underwent bioimpedance assessment presented previous femur fracture with metal prothesis or fixation, hydration status were not considered for bioimpedance assessment, and a lower number of positive events, limited statistical adjustments and might be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

PhA, EW/TBW, and quadriceps US thickness predicted 30‑day mortality after hip fracture. Standardization by knee height was used to reduce BMI bias, while the composite index (PhA and MT), which indicates muscle mass and quality, improved discrimination to predict 30-day mortality. These bedside measures may support risk stratification and identification of osteosarcopenia in the acute phase of hip fracture, warranting validation in larger, multi‑center cohorts.
