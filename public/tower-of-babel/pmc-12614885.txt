Exploration of Yeast Species Suitable for Preparation of Stable Isotope–Labeled Internal Standards Extracts (SILIS)
Authors: Taisuke Seike, Kahar Prihardi, Chiaki Ogino, Fumio Matsuda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Isotope dilution mass spectrometry is a widely used method for measuring intracellular metabolite concentrations, relying on the ratio of peak areas between the target compound and its stable isotope–labeled internal standard. For metabolome analysis of microorganisms, comprehensive concentration measurements have been achieved through the preparation of stable isotope–labeled internal standard extracts (SILIS). Methods have been developed to prepare SILIS by extracting crude metabolites from fully 13C-labeled bacteria Escherichia coli and yeasts Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Pichia pastoris (Komagataella phaffii). For cost-effective preparation of SILIS, ideal characteristics of host yeasts include rapid cell growth, high biomass production, and significant metabolite accumulation. In this study, suitable yeast species for SILIS production were investigated from diverse candidates. Batch cultures of 15 yeast species from 12 genera were performed in synthetic defined medium, with cells harvested at different growth phases and metabolites extracted using the methanol/chloroform/water method. Metabolomic analysis by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry revealed the relative concentrations of 65 metabolites. The results demonstrated that S. cerevisiae and Kluyveromyces marxianus in the stationary phase were the most effective for SILIS production of central metabolic intermediates. SILIS production using S. cerevisiae and K. marxianus can be widely applied in standard laboratories because these species are safe, the media are commercially available, and the extraction methods are easily implementable.

INTRODUCTION

Metabolome analysis using mass spectrometry (MS) is widely applied in biotechnology and biology to comprehensively quantify intracellular and extracellular metabolites.1,2) Specifically, this technique plays an essential role in metabolic engineering studies. Metabolic bottlenecks in bacteria and yeasts have been estimated by measuring the intracellular abundances of sugar phosphates, organic acids, amino acids, and nucleic acids.3–5) Moreover, metabolome analysis has been applied for various purposes, such as quality assessment through food component quantification6) and optimization of medium components.7)

Conventional quantitative analysis using MS relies on calibration with standard compounds. For instance, the stable isotope dilution method employing stable isotope–labeled internal standards is crucial for liquid chromatography–MS (LC–MS) quantification of intracellular metabolites, as it mitigates ionization suppression.8–10) Mixtures of stable isotope–labeled amino acids, nucleic acids, lipids, and organic acids are commercially available. However, preparing stable isotope–labeled standards for many target compounds in metabolome analysis remains challenging.5) Consequently, relative quantification, which assesses the ratio of metabolite content between samples, has been employed for widely targeted metabolome analyses.11,12) Nevertheless, comprehensive quantification of metabolite concentrations is essential to advance the application of metabolomics in biotechnology and biology.

One approach to achieve comprehensive concentration measurements is the preparation of stable isotope–labeled internal standard extracts (SILIS) using isotopically labeled microorganisms.13) Methods have been developed to prepare SILIS by extracting crude metabolites from fully 13C-labeled bacteria or yeasts.14) In addition to the prokaryote Escherichia coli,13,15) eukaryotic microorganisms such as the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae have been used for SILIS preparation because of their advantages in high metabolite abundance, reduced odor, and safety as GRAS (generally recognized as safe) microorganisms.16) SILIS prepared from S. cerevisiae has been applied for the absolute quantification of intracellular metabolites in S. cerevisiae and human cells.17–20) Furthermore, the nonconventional yeast Pichia pastoris (Komagataella phaffii) has been employed as a suitable host for SILIS production due to its high cell yield.21–23)

For cost-effective SILIS preparation, ideal host characteristics include rapid cell growth, high biomass production, and significant metabolite accumulation. While S. cerevisiae and K. phaffii have been used for SILIS preparation, the potential of other nonconventional yeasts remains largely unexplored. It is expected that fermentative (Crabtree-positive) yeasts, such as S. cerevisiae, convert most consumed glucose to ethanol,24,25) whereas Crabtree-negative yeasts accumulate more metabolites without producing ethanol. Moreover, it is desirable that SILIS can be prepared under simple and widely applicable cultivation and extraction conditions. In this study, we evaluated 15 yeast species to identify suitable hosts for SILIS production. The results demonstrated that S. cerevisiae is among the most effective hosts for SILIS preparation.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Species and culture conditions

The 15 yeast species used in this study are listed in Table S1. Yeast cells were plated on Yeast Peptone Dextrose (YPD) agar (1% Bacto yeast extract [YE], 2% Bacto peptone, 2% d-glucose, and 2% agar) to obtain single colonies. However, for Schizosaccharomyces pombe (L972), YE agar medium (0.5% YE, 3% d-glucose, and 1.5% agar) was used. Three single colonies were inoculated into YPD liquid medium (or YE liquid medium for S. pombe) and cultured overnight at 30°C and 150 rpm in test tubes containing 5 mL medium to prepare a pre-preculture (BR-43FL; TAITEC, Saitama, Japan). The preculture was then prepared in a synthetic defined (SD) medium (0.67% yeast nitrogen base without amino acids and 2% glucose). Cells were transferred to a 200-mL baffled Erlenmeyer flask containing 50 mL medium to achieve an initial optical density (OD) at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.05 and cultured for 16 h at 30°C and 120 rpm (G-BR-200; TAITEC). OD600 was measured using a spectrophotometer (UV-1700; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The cells were then transferred to fresh SD medium at an initial OD600 of 0.05 to establish the main culture. Cells were cultured at 30°C and 120 rpm, and samples were collected at fixed time points. During batch cultivation in SD medium, 2 mL of culture (1 mL × 2) was collected into a 1.5-mL tube at 10, 24, and 48 h. Approximately 1 mL of the sample was used to measure OD600 of the culture suspension using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (UV-1280; Shimadzu). Means ± standard deviation were determined for n = 3 replicates. For fully 13C-labeling culture, both precultures and main cultures were performed in SD medium containing 1% [U-13C6]glucose.

Phylogenetic tree construction

The phylogenetic tree of the 15 yeast species was constructed based on the nucleotide sequences of the D1/D2 domain of the large-subunit ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene. Genomic DNA was extracted from yeast cells cultured overnight in YPD or YE liquid medium using the Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The D1/D2 domain was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using KOD FX Neo DNA polymerase (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) with primers NL1 (5′-GCATATCAATAAGCGGAGGAAAAG-3′) and NL4 (5′-GGTCCGTGTTTCAAGACGG-3′).26) The PCR conditions were as follows: 94°C for 2 min, followed by 30 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 52°C for 30 s, and 68°C for 30 s, with a final extension at 68°C for 5 min. PCR products were purified using a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Nucleotide sequences of the D1/D2 domain were determined by Sanger sequencing with the NL1 primer (Eurofins Genomics, Tokyo, Japan). The resulting sequences were aligned using the Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis (MEGA) X software (version 10.1.8).27) Phylogenetic analysis based on the D1/D2 domain was performed using the neighbor-joining method with Kimura 2-parameter distance correction in MEGA X. The confidence of the clades was assessed through bootstrap analysis with 1000 replicates.28)

Metabolome analysis of intracellular metabolites

After batch culture in SD medium, cells were harvested at the exponential growth phase (OD600 = 0.5–1.0, 10 h), early stationary phase (24 h), and late stationary phase (48 h). Yeast cultures corresponding to OD600 × mL = 10 were collected by filtration.20) Quenching methanol containing 20 mM d-camphor sulfonic acid as an internal standard was aliquoted into 1.6-mL portions in 15-mL Falcon tubes and placed on ice. The filtration apparatus was assembled, and a filter (polytetrafluoroethylene membrane filter; pore size, 0.45 μm; diameter, 47 mm [Omnipore, Merck Millipore, Belize, MA, USA]) was placed on a vacuum filtration holder. The yeast culture was poured onto the filter and collected by vacuum filtration using a membrane vacuum pump (EYELA TOKYO RIKAKIKAI, Tokyo, Japan). The cells were immediately washed with 5 mL of chilled sterile water, and the filter was immersed in methanol for quenching. The samples were stored at −80°C until analysis.

Metabolite extraction was performed using the methanol/chloroform/water method.20) To the quenching methanol (1.6 mL), 640 μL sterile water and 1.6 mL chloroform were added, followed by vortexing and sonication for 1 min each. The mixture was resuspended and vortexed again. After centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C, the mixture was allowed to stand for 10 min. The supernatant was then transferred to six 1.5-mL tubes (250 μL each) and dried overnight under reduced pressure at room temperature using a SpeedVac concentrator (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For quantification, 1 of the 6 tubes was used to measure metabolite concentrations.

The dried residue of the upper phase was dissolved in 50 μL of Milli-Q water and centrifuged at 6000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was analyzed using ion-pairing liquid chromatography–triple quadrupole MS in multiple reaction monitoring mode.29) A Nexera X2 system (Shimadzu) equipped with a MASTRO C18 column (150 × 2.1 mm, 3 μm; Shimadzu GLC, Tokyo, Japan) was coupled to an LCMS-8050 mass spectrometer (Shimadzu). The instrument settings were as follows: solvent A, Milli-Q water containing 15 mM tributylamine and 10 mM acetic acid; solvent B, methanol; gradient, 0 min, 0% B; 0.5 min, 0% B; 8 min, 25% B; 12 min, 98% B; 15 min, 98% B; 15.1 min, 0% B; 20 min, 0% B; flow rate, 0.3 mL/min; injection volume, 1 μL; polarity, negative; ionization, electrospray ionization; nebulizer gas flow, 3 L/min; heating gas flow, 10 L/min; interface temperature, 300°C; desolvation line (DL) temperature, 250°C; heat-block temperature, 400°C; and drying gas flow, 10 L/min. Multiple reaction monitoring conditions for each metabolite were obtained from a previous study (Table S2).20)

Data analysis

All chromatogram data were processed using LCMS-Solution (Shimadzu) to obtain raw peak area values. The sum of relative peak area (SRPA) levels was determined as follows.

The peak area of each metabolite in sample j (Pi,j) was calculated from the raw peak area (RP) as:

Pi,j=RPi, j/RPd-camphor sulfonate,j

Here, i and j represent the metabolite and the sample, respectively. For normalization, Pi,j was divided by the mean peak area value of metabolite i among all samples:

NPi,j=Pi,j/∑jn[Pi,j/n]

Here, n represents the total number of samples (3 × 15 = 45 samples in this study). The SRPA per unit cell mass and per flask of sample j was determined as follows:

SRPAcell mass, j=∑imNPi,j/10

SRPAflask, j=SRPAcell mass, j[OD600]

Here, m represents the total number of measured metabolites (65 metabolites in this study), and OD600 represents the measured optical density at 600 nm.

RESULTS

Growth profiles of 15 yeast species

In this study, we used 15 yeast species from 12 genera to identify the optimal candidates for producing isotopically labeled compounds (Table S1). These species included the model yeasts S. cerevisiae (BY4947) and S. pombe (L972), which are Crabtree-positive (fermentative) yeasts. Others were Crabtree-negative yeasts, including industrially relevant species such as the thermotolerant yeast Kluyveromyces marxianus (NBRC1777), the halotolerant yeast Zygosaccharomyces bailii (J39), the oleaginous yeast Yarrowia lipolytica (CLIB89), and the methanol-assimilating yeasts K. phaffii (CBS7435) and Ogataea polymorpha (NBRC1476). Phylogenetic analysis based on D1/D2 rRNA sequences revealed that the yeast species were genetically diverse (Fig. S1A). Microscopic observations revealed differences in cell size among the 15 species (Fig. S1B). All selected species were prototrophic for either amino acids or vitamins.

To compare cell growth speed and final cell turbidity, all yeast species were cultured in flask-scale batch cultures under aerobic conditions. SD medium was used for all cultivations. The initial cell turbidity was set to OD600 = 0.05. This condition was employed because it is easily reproducible in most laboratories. The growth curves are shown in Fig. S2, and Fig. 1 shows the cell turbidity at 10, 24, and 48 h after the start of cultivation. All species exhibited exponential growth between 4 and 10 h, and reached nearly maximum OD600 at 24 h (Fig. S2). The results also indicated that cells proliferated significantly between 10 and 24 h. Thereafter, almost no further changes in cell numbers were observed up to 48 h, indicating that the period from 24 to 48 h corresponded to the stationary phase. Comparison of turbidity among yeast species revealed a large variation in final cell turbidity. For instance, the OD600 levels at 48 h were 5.3 ± 0.1 and 2.2 ± 0.1 for S. cerevisiae and K. phaffii, respectively, both of which have been used for SILIS production.17,18,21–23) Among the yeast species examined, the highest turbidity at 48 h was observed for O. polymorpha (OD600 = 9.4 ± 0.1), followed by K. marxianus (OD600 = 8.9 ± 0.2).

Fig. 1. Growth of 15 yeast species. Yeast species were cultured in 50 mL of SD medium, and cell turbidity was measured at the log phase (10 h), early stationary phase (24 h), and late stationary phase (48 h). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). SD, synthetic defined.

Comparison of intracellular metabolite levels among 15 yeast species

All yeasts were cultured again under identical conditions for metabolite analysis. Cells were harvested during the exponential growth (log) phase (10 h), early stationary phase (24 h), and late stationary phase (48 h). Culture broths were collected to a liquid volume corresponding to OD600 × mL = 10. Metabolites were extracted using the methanol–chloroform method and subjected to targeted metabolome analysis using LC–tandem MS (LC–MS/MS). A dataset including the relative amounts of 65 metabolites was obtained. Figure 2 shows a heatmap representation of relative metabolite levels. During the log phase (10 h), S. cerevisiae accumulated higher amounts of nucleotides and amino acids, such as nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide and valine. S. pombe exhibited higher levels of glycolytic intermediates, such as phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), glucose-6-phosphate (G6P), and fructose 6-phosphate (F6P).

Fig. 2. Heatmap of intracellular metabolites in 15 yeast species at 3 different growth stages. Yeast species were cultured in 50 mL of SD medium, and cells were harvested at 3 stages: (A) log phase (10 h), (B) early stationary phase (24 h), and (C) late stationary phase (48 h). Metabolites were extracted using the methanol–chloroform–water method and subjected to targeted metabolome analysis using LC–MS/MS. The heatmap data, including 65 measured metabolites, were normalized for each metabolite to a mean of 1, shown as the mean of n = 3 replicates, and categorized into 6 groups: amino acids, glycolysis, TCA cycle, pentose phosphate pathway, nucleotides, and coenzymes. LC–MS/MS, liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry; SD, synthetic defined; TCA, tricarboxylic acid.

The levels of most metabolites decreased at 24 h because of the metabolic shift from the log phase to the stationary phase (Fig. 2).30,31) During the late stationary phase (48 h), most metabolite levels were further reduced compared with those at 24 h. These results indicate that even within the same species, significant variations in metabolite abundance were observed depending on the culture time. Moreover, the accumulation patterns of metabolites also differed among the yeast species.

Evaluation of intermediate compound production

Next, we evaluated the optimal species for the production of central metabolic intermediates. We hypothesized that species accumulating higher levels of central metabolic intermediates would be advantageous. For this purpose, the SRPA was calculated as follows (see “Experimental Procedures” for details). First, the raw peak area value of a metabolite was divided by that of the internal standard (d-camphor sulfonate) in the same sample. Second, a normalized peak area was obtained by dividing by the mean peak area value across all samples. Finally, the SRPA was calculated for each sample. The SRPA value was considered an indicator of the total abundance of 65 metabolites. Figure 3A compares SRPA values per unit cell mass (OD600) among yeast species. The results showed that all yeast species tended to exhibit higher SRPA per unit cell mass levels (SRPA/OD600) during the logarithmic phase. In the stationary phase, SRPA per unit cell mass decreased by approximately half compared with the logarithmic phase. Variability was also observed among yeast species. For example, during the logarithmic phase, Debaryomyces polymorphus showed the highest SRPA per unit cell mass, whereas the level in Wynnella silvicola was the lowest—only 35% of that in D. polymorphus.

Fig. 3. SRPA of 15 yeast species across different growth phases. (A) SRPA per unit cell mass (OD600). The SRPA value was considered an indicator of the total abundance of 65 metabolites. Raw peak areas were divided by the peak area of the internal standard (d-camphor sulfonate). Relative peak areas were then normalized by dividing by the mean peak area of each metabolite across all samples. Finally, the SRPA per unit cell mass was obtained for each sample. (B) SRPA per flask. These values were calculated by multiplying the SRPA per unit cell mass by the OD600 at the time of sampling. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). OD, optical density; SRPA, sum of relative peak area.

Next, SRPA per flask levels (SRPA per flask) were calculated by multiplying the SRPA per unit cell mass by the OD600 at the time of sampling (Fig. 1). Figure 3B shows that all yeast species tended to exhibit higher SRPA per flask in the stationary phase than in the logarithmic phase. This was attributed to the higher OD600 values in the stationary phase compared with the logarithmic phase, as shown in Fig. 1. Furthermore, comparison among yeasts indicated that S. cerevisiae at 48 h had the highest SRPA per flask, followed by K. marxianus at 48 and 24 h. These findings suggest that cells in the stationary phase are more cost-effective for achieving higher SRPA per flask and that S. cerevisiae and K. marxianus are the most suitable yeast species for SILIS preparation among the 15 species examined.

Comparison of isotopically labeled compounds using [U-13C6]glucose

The previous section indicated that S. cerevisiae and K. marxianus were identified as potentially suitable species for the efficient production of isotopically labeled compounds. To confirm the intracellular metabolite levels of these species, we performed batch cultures of S. cerevisiae and K. marxianus in SD medium containing [U-13C6]glucose. In this experiment, 25 mL of culture medium with 1% [U-13C6]glucose was used for pre- and main cultivations, and intracellular metabolites were extracted at 24, 36, and 48 h after cultivation. These time points were selected because higher SRPA per flask levels were observed at 24 and 48 h (Fig. 3). All detected metabolites showed stable isotope–labeling purities >99% (data not shown).17) The relative quantification results of 58 fully 13C-labeled compounds are shown in Fig. 4. The total number of metabolites detected was smaller than that shown in Fig. 2 due to the concentration of LC–MS/MS. In S. cerevisiae, metabolites from glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, and coenzymes were abundant, whereas nucleotides and amino acids were more abundant in K. marxianus. Mixing the extracted metabolites from each species and analyzing them by LC–MS/MS confirmed that the bias in metabolite abundances was reduced compared with the individual species (Fig. 4).

Relative quantification results of 58 hydrophilic compounds in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Kluyveromyces marxianus, and the mixture of S. cerevisiae and K. marxianus. DISCUSSION

In this study, we performed a comprehensive metabolomic analysis of 15 genetically diverse yeast species to identify those optimal for the efficient production of SILIS. Our results revealed that, in all yeast species, most metabolites were more abundant in the logarithmic growth phase than in the stationary phase (Figs. 2 and 3A). This is likely due to the consumption of glucose in the medium and the overall activation of metabolism required to synthesize the building blocks essential for growth.30,31) In contrast, cell density in flasks was markedly higher in the stationary phase than in the logarithmic phase (Fig. 1). Consequently, the total metabolite abundance per flask tended to be greater in the stationary phase than in the logarithmic phase (Fig. 3B). These findings indicate that the efficient production of SILIS requires consideration not only of intracellular metabolite levels but also of cell density across cultivation phases.

Among the 15 yeast species analyzed, S. cerevisiae and K. marxianus proved to be the most effective for SILIS production of a wide range of central metabolic intermediates. Although their SRPA per unit cell mass was not particularly high compared with other species (Fig. 3A), their high cell densities in the stationary phase (Fig. 1) resulted in greater total metabolite abundances per flask (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, cultivation of S. cerevisiae and K. marxianus with [U-13C6]glucose confirmed their ability to synthesize fully labeled intermediates (Fig. 4). These results suggest that S. cerevisiae and K. marxianus are suitable species for efficient SILIS production. Similar to S. cerevisiae, K. marxianus is a GRAS yeast and can be safely cultivated in standard laboratories.32) The usefulness of SILIS prepared from S. cerevisiae has already been demonstrated in many studies for the absolute quantification of intracellular metabolites in S. cerevisiae and human cells.17–20) It is expected that its usefulness will be further increased by mixing SILIS derived from K. marxianus or by co-culturing S. cerevisiae and K. marxianus.

The usual metabolome analyses of S. cerevisiae are conducted by sampling culture broth in which the product of the culture volume and OD600 is approximately 5–10. This means that SILIS for 12–25 samples can be obtained from a single flask-scale culture containing 25 mL of medium with a final OD600 of 5.0. Moreover, SILIS should be consumed within a short period of time because the stability of metabolic intermediates is not guaranteed even when stored at −80°C.

The results of this study also showed that other yeast species exhibited high accumulation of specific metabolites. For instance, the accumulation levels of certain metabolites, including PEP, G6P, and F6P, were significantly higher in S. pombe than in other yeasts (Fig. 2). In targeted analyses focusing on these metabolites, more efficient production of SILIS may be achieved by incorporating such species into SILIS preparation and mixing them with SILIS derived from other yeasts.

For SILIS preparation, a synthetic medium composed of fully 13C-labeled carbon sources with multiple inorganic salts and trace elements is required. In this study, we used SD medium, a synthetic medium commonly employed for S. cerevisiae culture. Since commercial mixtures of inorganic salts and trace elements (yeast nitrogen base; see “Experimental Procedures”) are readily available, preparation of SD medium with consistent quality is feasible in standard laboratories. The results shown in Fig. 1 demonstrated that the maximum cell turbidity levels varied among yeast species. For example, the levels of OD600 at 48 h were 9.4 ± 0.1 for O. polymorpha and 8.9 ± 0.2 for K. marxianus, respectively. These variations are partly due to the differences in the dependence on trace elements in the medium. Thus, custom optimization of synthetic media may enable more efficient SILIS production using S. cerevisiae and other yeast species.33,34)

It should also be noted that metabolite extraction in this study was performed using the chloroform–methanol–water method optimized for S. cerevisiae.35) This method was chosen because it is more accessible for standard laboratories compared with extraction protocols requiring extremely high or low temperatures.36–38) However, more suitable yeast species may be identified from a wider range of yeasts by applying other extraction methods, since factors such as cell size and cell wall composition strongly influence extraction efficiency (Fig. S2).21–23)

Overall, this study demonstrated that S. cerevisiae and K. marxianus were the most effective species for SILIS production of a wide range of central metabolic intermediates. SILIS production using S. cerevisiae and K. marxianus can be readily applied in standard laboratories because these yeasts are safe, widely available, and compatible with commercially available media and easily implementable extraction methods. However, further improvements in SILIS production efficiency are likely to be achieved through the exploration of additional yeast species, as well as the optimization of cultivation media and extraction protocols. Continued refinement will be necessary for the broader application of the SILIS method in biotechnology and biological studies.
