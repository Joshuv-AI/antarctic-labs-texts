Neurofilaments in cerebrospinal fluid and/or serum as a possible biomarker of pediatric spinal muscular atrophy
Authors: Markéta Kumhera, Eva Vrsčaj, Tanja Loboda, Damjan Osredkar, Petra Holotová, Marie Rohlenová, Barbora Lauerová, Vanesa Jurášová, Jitka Hanzalová, Markéta Pavlíková, Jana Haberlová
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is a severe, genetically determined neuromuscular disorder characterised by progressive muscle weakness resulting from motor neuron degeneration. Although therapies such as nusinersen have altered disease trajectories, objective biomarkers for treatment monitoring remain lacking. Neurofilament proteins, particularly phosphorylated neurofilament heavy chain (pNFH), are promising candidates for this role.ObjectivesTo evaluate pNFH levels in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and serum as potential biomarkers for monitoring treatment response to nusinersen in paediatric patients with SMA.MethodsThis two-center observational study involved 56 SMA patients (types I–III) treated with nusinersen and 30 age-matched controls. pNFH concentrations in CSF and serum were measured at predefined intervals using ELISA kits. Motor function was assessed with CHOP INTEND, HFMSE, or MFM scales at baseline and after 3, 6, and 12 months. Mixed linear regression modeling evaluated changes in neurofilament levels and motor function.ResultsBaseline pNFH levels in both CSF and serum were significantly higher in SMA patients compared to controls. Following nusinersen therapy, pNFH levels decreased significantly in both compartments, especially in the first 6 months. Younger patients had higher initial serum pNFH levels. A significant correlation was found between CSF and serum pNFH concentration. An association between pNFH levels and motor scores was observed.ConclusionspNFH levels in CSF and serum show potential as objective biomarkers for monitoring the initial treatment response in paediatric SMA. Longitudinal changes are more informative than single measurements. Further validation in larger cohorts and using more sensitive methods is warranted.

1. Introduction

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is a rare autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disease affecting peripheral motor neurons and belongs to the group of hereditary motor neuropathies. 1 The reported incidence is 1 in 10,000 live births. 2 The cause in 95% of cases is a homozygous deletion of the SMN1 gene (survival motor neuron).3,4 As a result, production of the SMN protein is reduced, leading to the degeneration of alpha motor neurons in the anterior horns of the spinal cord.1,5 The disease manifests itself by progressive muscle weakness. Neurological findings in the most frequent form, SMA type 1, are dominated by hypotonia and hypo- or areflexia. The disease can be classified into five basic phenotypes based on the age of onset and the motor maximum reached.6,7 Nowadays, in the era of disease-modifying therapies, preferably functional classification based on maximal motor ability is used: non-sitters, sitters and walkers. Among other less defined factors, the phenotypic severity is influenced by the copy number of the SMN2 gene, which differs from the SMN1 gene in five nucleotides 8 ; in general, a higher number of SMN2 copies is associated with a milder clinical phenotype. 5 SMA is a very serious disease, being the most common lethal congenital disease of infancy and early childhood before the introduction of causal treatment. 2

Increasing knowledge about the pathogenesis and course of SMA has led to the development of several innovative therapeutic options in recent years. Previously, management was limited to supportive and symptomatic care aimed at preventing respiratory failure, joint contractures and spinal deformities.7,9 The first approved therapy, nusinersen (Spinraza®) became available in Europe in May 2017. Nusinersen is a synthetic antisense oligonucleotide that modulates RNA splicing of the SMN2 gene, resulting in increased production of functional SMN protein. 10 Nusinersen is administered intrathecally using initial 4 loading doses followed by maintenance doses every 4 months. Other available medications are risdiplam (Evrysdi®) and onasemnogene abeparvovec (Zolgensma®). Onasemnogene abeparvovec is the first systemically administered gene therapy. It uses a vector in the form of a synthetic adeno-associated virus 9 capsid, which carries recombinant DNA of the SMN1 gene. The synthetic DNA does not incorporate into the patient’s DNA, it creates an episome, which remains in the neuronal cells and is expressed, leading to the production of the SMN protein. A single intravenous administration of this therapy is expected to resolve the cause of the disease. 11 Risdiplam has a mechanism of action similar to nusinersen, it is a small molecule modifying the splicing of the SMN2 gene. It is administered daily as an oral solution; the dose is based on the patient’s weight. 12

Currently, treatment efficacy is assessed using validated motor function scales performed by trained physiotherapists. The most commonly used scales include the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia Infant Test of Neuromuscular Disorders (CHOP INTEND) or the Hammersmith Infant Neuromuscular Examination (HINE) for SMA type 1, the Extended Hammersmith Functional Motor Scale (HFMSE), the Revised Upper Limb Module (RULM) and the 6-minute walk test (6MWT) for SMA types II and III respectively or Motor Function Measurement (MFM). 13 However, their interpretation may be influenced by factors such as scoliosis, joint contractures, weight gain, fatigue, or limited cooperation.

With evolving therapeutic strategies, the need for stratification of patients regarding treatment eligibility and monitoring treatment efficacy has increased. A necessary condition for the therapy optimisation is the availability of a high-quality, objective biomarker. According to recently published data, the determination of neurofilament levels in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or serum appears to be a promising biomarker for monitoring disease activity and response to treatment.14,15

Neurofilaments are a group of cytoskeletal proteins consisting of three subunits: light, medium and heavy, depending on their molecular weight. 16 While all neurofilament isoforms are released following axonal injury, NF-H contains an extensive C-terminal tail rich in lysine–serine–proline (KSP) repeats that undergo heavy phosphorylation. This phosphorylation increases stability, slows axonal transport and results in a prolonged half-life in extracellular fluids. Unlike total NF-H, the phosphorylated form (pNFH) is more resistant to proteolytic degradation and therefore accumulates more readily in CSF and serum, making it a sensitive but nonspecific indicator of ongoing axonal degeneration.16,17

They have already been described as biomarkers in several neurodegenerative and neuroinflammatory diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, multiple sclerosis or Parkinson’s disease.18–20 Elevated levels of neurofilaments, particularly phosphorylated neurofilament heavy chain (pNFH), have also been observed in various neurodegenerative conditions, including SMA, making them a candidate for monitoring disease progression and treatment response. Published data are showing elevated levels of neurofilaments especially in patients with SMA type 1 and 2, as well as a decrease of the level with ongoing treatment.14,21,22 Recently reference ranges of neurofilament light chain in the pediatric population have been published and European Medicines Agency (EMA) issued a Letter of Support of Neurofilament light in childhood Neurological diseases.23,24 At the time this study was initiated, pNFH was supported by published evidence as a promising pharmacodynamic biomarker in SMA, including data from nusinersen clinical trials. 14 Based on the available evidence and practical considerations regarding assay availability and cost at the time, we therefore focused our subsequent analyses on pNFH as a potentially sensitive biomarker of therapeutic response in nusinersen-treated pediatric SMA patients.

The aim of our study is to investigate phosphorylated heavy chains of neurofilaments as a possible biomarker of treatment effectiveness in children and young adults with SMA type I, II and III who are treated with nusinersen.

2. Methods

This was a two-centre observational study conducted at the Department of Paediatric Neurology, Motol and Homolka University Hospital, Prague, Czech Republic, and the Department of Child, Adolescent and Developmental Neurology, University Children’s Hospital, Ljubljana, Slovenia. The study was approved by local ethics committees at both centres, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. Both centres are members of the European Reference Network for Rare Neuromuscular Diseases (ERN EURO-NMD).

2.1. Study samples and patients

In this study, we examined CSF samples from 56 patients with a diagnosis of spinal muscular atrophy who were treated intrathecally with nusinersen. We also analysed serum samples of a subset of 28 Czech SMA patients. All included SMA patients had genetically confirmed 5q-associated SMA, defined as a homozygous deletion of exon 7 in the SMN1 gene. The number of SMN2 copies was determined by multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) in accredited molecular genetics laboratories at the respective centres. SMN2 copy number was used for phenotypic correlation and included as a covariate in subsequent statistical models. SMA type was classified according to established clinical criteria, based on age at symptom onset and the highest motor milestone achieved.

Nusinersen was administered intrathecally according to the standard dosing regimen, consisting of four loading doses followed by maintenance doses every 4 months. The recommended dose was 12 mg per administration. The first dose was followed by three additional loading doses at approximately 2, 4 and 9 weeks, followed by maintenance treatment every 4 months. In the Czech patients, samples were collected prior to the first administration of nusinersen and then at each administration. Only samples from the 1st, 3rd, 4th, 5th and 6th administrations were analysed, if applicable. In patients of the Slovenian centre, the samples were collected prior to the first administration and before the 7th administration.

2.2. Control group

The control group consisted of 30 age-matched controls from whom CSF and paired serum samples were obtained as part of the diagnostic process to exclude neuroinflammatory disease at the Department of Paediatric Neurology, Motol University Hospital, Prague, Czech Republic. The legal representatives of the patients signed their consent to perform this examination, sample collection, and its use for potential research purposes. We selected patients without a proven neurodegenerative disease from the stored samples.

2.3. Samples analysis

The CSF was collected via lumbar puncture and immediately analysed for routine CSF parameters (cell count and protein level) at a local laboratory. The remaining CSF was frozen at -78°C and stored until analysis. Serum samples were collected before administration for routine purposes and the residues were frozen until analysis. All neurofilament analysis were performed in the Cerebrospinal fluid laboratory of the Institute of Immunology, Motol University Hospital, Prague.

During an initial pilot phase, NfL and pNFH were assessed in paired CSF and serum samples in a subset of patients. Based on the available evidence at the time and practical considerations regarding assay availability, cost and feasibility, pNFH was selected for the subsequent analyses.

Phosphorylated neurofilament heavy chain was determined in CSF using the EUROIMMUN® ELISA kit EQ 6561-9601. In serum, pNFH concentrations were measured using the high-sensitivity EUROIMMUN® ELISA kit EQ 6562-9601. Both assays are CE-IVD certified and were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Results are reported in the original units specified for each assay (ng/mL for the standard CSF ELISA and pg/mL for the high-sensitivity serum ELISA).

2.4. Clinical assessment

To evaluate the clinical symptoms, we used the following motor scales: CHOP INTEND, HFMSE or MFM, according to the age and capabilities of the patients. Testing was performed by a trained physiotherapist before the treatment and then at follow-ups at 3, 6 and 12 months after the initiation of therapy. Because different SMA types require different validated motor scales, CHOP INTEND, HFMSE and MFM were analysed separately and subsequently transformed to percentage values of the maximum score to allow modelling of longitudinal trajectories as in our previously published study. 25 We acknowledge that these scales measure partially distinct motor domains; therefore, the percentage transformation was used exclusively for statistical modelling and not for direct comparison across SMA types.

2.5. Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviation and/or median with interquartile ranges as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarised using absolute and relative frequencies.

Longitudinal changes in motor function scores were modelled using mixed-effect linear regression models, with visit time points as a fixed factor and with a random intercepts to account for a very large inter-individual variability at baseline. Various fixed factors such as age at first application, type of SMA, and number of SMN2 gene copies were included in the base model to check for factors potentially modifying the Motor score development. Interactions between the fixed factors and measurement timing were explored as well. To explore changes in CSF and serum neurofilament levels, the same type of modelling was used but the measurements were log-transformed to improve model fit.

All analyses were performed using the statistical language and environment R, version 4.1.2. The level of statistical significance was set to 0.05.

The mean age at the time of examination was 96.2 months (range 15.5-174.1). There was no significant difference in the mean age in the control group compared to SMA patients (t-test, p=0.651). Detailed age distribution of SMA patients and controls is shown in Supplementary Figure S1. No child in the control group had evidence of CNS infection, inflammatory disease or structural pathology that could affect neurofilament levels.

3.3. Clinical assessment

In one patient (patient 24), physical abilities could not be assessed using any validated methods due to severe disability. At baseline, the mean motor function score across the cohort was 54.3% of the maximum possible score. The higher baseline score was in patients with type 3 and those with 4 copies of the SMN2 gene. The highest baseline levels were observed in the SMA type 1 patients. In 17 patients the baseline level was the maximal peak, in 21 patients the peak occured within the first month. CSF pNFH concentrations declined significantly with ongoing therapy. The most pronounced decrease was observed between week 4 and 9 (estimate -1.478, p<0.0001) and between weeks 9 and 26 (estimate -1.288, p<0.0001). After 6 months of treatment, the pNFH concentration decreased to almost the same level in all types and then stabilised. 3.8. Correlation between CSF levels of pNFH and motor scales

A statistically significant correlation was observed between CSF pNFH levels and motor scores over time (estimate = −3.808, SE = 0.772, p<0.0001). Motor scores increased over time, whereas individual pNFH concentrations were not directly associated with motor scores. The observed correlation reflects parallel longitudinal trends during nusinersen therapy rather than a direct point-to-point or predictive relationship between pNFH levels and motor function.

4. Discussion

The need for valid biomarkers capable of assessing both disease activity and treatment efficacy has become increasingly evident with the expanding range of therapeutic options available to SMA patients as well as the heterogeneity of clinical responses. In recent years, neurofilaments (both light and heavy chains) have emerged as potential biomarkers in SMA.14,15,21,26,27 28 However, as our study and others have demonstrated, results across studies have been inconsistent, with neurofilament levels influenced by factors such as age,22,29 disease severity, and the method of detection.

The choice of pNFH in our study also reflected the state of the field at the time the study was initiated, when pNFH had already shown promise as a pharmacodynamic biomarker in SMA and was supported by clinical trial data.14,21,26 Practical considerations, including assay availability, cost and feasibility, also contributed to its selection. Since then, NfL has become increasingly prominent in SMA biomarker research, particularly with the development of highly sensitive SIMOA-based assay.

In the present study, we determined pNFH levels both in CSF and serum by using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Although ELISA was the available method at the time of analysis, we acknowledge that more sensitive methods, such as Single Molecule Array (SIMOA), would be preferable for future studies. SIMOA has shown particular promise for detecting light neurofilament chains and may offer greater sensitivity in assessing disease activity and therapeutic response.

We observed a significant decline in pNFH levels following the initiation of nusinersen therapy in all types and age groups. Although we observed higher median baseline levels in patients younger than 5 years of age. These median baseline levels were notably elevated in both serum and CSF in paediatric SMA patients compared with control group. In our cohort, we observed overlap in a range of levels with healthy individuals. This may reflect the inequality of the groups (control group versus SMA patients); no statistically significant distinguishing factor was identified within this overlap. The observation of significantly elevated median baseline pNFH levels provides a valuable insight into the disease pathophysiology. Neurofilaments are structural proteins found in nerve cells, and their phosphorylated form, pNFH, is released into the extracellular fluids during axonal damage or degeneration.16,29 This age-related difference supports the concept that neurodegeneration is more pronounced in the early stages of the disease, emphasising the importance of early therapeutic intervention.30–32

The decrease in neurofilament levels during therapy likely reflects the effect of treatment. Cerebrospinal fluid levels approached near-to-normal values after approximately 6 months on therapy. In contrast, we observed higher median serum pNFH levels in patients compared to the control group even after therapy; this persistent elevation may be attributable to ongoing damage to the axons of the peripheral nervous system or delayed onset of therapeutic effect.

An important aspect of our study was the positive correlation between serum and CSF pNFH levels, which is consistent with some of the previous research.15,27,29,33 This finding supports the hypothesis that peripheral blood measurements of pNFH might offer a less invasive tool for monitoring disease progression and treatment response. This may be particularly valuable in those patients for whom lumbar puncture is not feasible or in those receiving alternative treatment, such as risdiplam or onasemnogene abeparvovec. However, the observed correlation does not imply that individual CSF pNFH concentrations can be directly predicted from serum concentrations.

Our study also showed elevated serum levels of pNFH in SMA type 3 patients when compared to healthy individuals. While these findings may contribute to clinical discussions regarding the effectiveness of therapy in these subgroup our data indicates that longitudinal follow-up data—rather than a single measurement—are necessary for accurate interpretation.

Furthermore, we observed an association between CSF pNFH levels and motor scores over time. Motor scores continued to improve during nusinersen treatment, while pNFH levels showed the most pronounced decrease during the first six months of therapy. This suggests that the observed association may reflect parallel longitudinal changes in pNFH and motor function during nusinersen therapy rather than a direct relationship between individual pNFH concentrations and motor performance. Notably, motor scale scores continued to improve even after the stabilization of pNFH levels during the subsequent six months. This is consistent with our previous study in a larger cohort of nusinersen-treated patients. 25 Monitoring neurofilament dynamics may therefore be particularly useful in clinical situations where motor assessments are not feasible or may be unreliable, such as following surgery, during intercurrent illness, fatigue or limited patient cooperation. However, whether pNFH provides additional clinically actionable information beyond standard motor assessments remains to be established.

Although neurofilaments have gained increasing attention as biomarkers in SMA, it is important to emphasise that pNFH is not disease-specific. Elevated levels have been reported in a variety of neurological conditions, including ALS, multiple sclerosis, traumatic brain injury and other neurodegenerative or neuroinflammatory disorders.16–20,29 Therefore, pNFH should be interpreted as a marker of axonal damage rather than a diagnostic tool. In SMA, its utility lies primarily in monitoring disease activity and treatment response rather than establishing the diagnosis.

Several recent studies have proposed additional potential biomarkers predictive of treatment response during nusinersen therapy such as inflammatory cytokines or circulating MyomiRs.34–36 These findings highlight the growing interest in multimodal biomarker approaches and support the need to validate pNFH within this broader biomarker landscape.

Although our study provides favourable results, several important limitations must be acknowledged. Our cohort was small, and the follow-up period was relatively short. The wide age range at treatment initiation reflects differences in national reimbursement policies and the availability of treatment in earlier years. For some patients with SMA type 1, treatment was started substantially later than currently recommended international standards. These cases represent long-term survivors, some with ventilatory and nutritional support who were still eligible for nusinersen therapy. This explains the higher-than-expected treatment initiation age in certain SMA type 1 patients and should be considered when interpreting baseline neurofilament concentrations.

In addition, data from two centres with partially different sampling schedules were combined. Although all neurofilament analyses were performed in a single laboratory and mixed-effects modelling was used to address the unbalanced design, differences in sampling density may have influenced the precision of longitudinal estimates. Future studies should aim for harmonised sampling protocols across centres. Larger, longer-term studies with more diverse patient populations are needed to fully validate neurofilament levels as a reliable biomarker for SMA.

5. Conclusion

The documented elevation of baseline pNFH levels in paediatric SMA patients, their subsequent decline following nusinersen therapy, and the correlation between serum and CSF levels highlight the potential of pNFH as a valuable biomarker for treatment monitoring. Although an correlation between pNFH levels and motor scores was observed, our findings do not establish a direct or predictive relationship between individual pNFH concentrations and motor function.

Although overlap between pNFH concentrations in SMA patients and healthy controls represents an important limitation, our findings suggests that longitudinal follow-up on pNFH levels within an individual patient may serve as a surrogate marker of disease activity and treatment response. Longitudinal studies involving larger cohorts are required to further validate these findings and to clarify the relationship between pNFH dynamics and disease progression.

Additional research is also necessary to identify the most effective methodologies for measuring neurofilament levels, particularly as newer and more sensitive analytical techniques become available. As the therapeutic landscape of SMA continues to evolve with the introduction of novel disease-modifying therapies, biomarkers such as neurofilaments are likely to play an increasingly important role in guiding treatment decisions and monitoring outcomes. A better understanding of the relationship between neurofilament levels, disease activity, and therapeutic responses may ultimately enhance patient care and support the development of more personalized treatment strategies for SMA.
