The Relationship between Social Media and Schizophrenia and Other Psychotic Disorders: A Systematic Review
Authors: Maddalena Cipriani, Haruka Notsu, Shrutangi Vaidya, Juan Castillo, Christine Hau, Sunnie Li, Winna Xia, Dina Yacoub, Selina (Zhitong) Zhou, Michael L Birnbaum, John Torous
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and HypothesisThe impact of social media on individuals with schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders is poorly understood. We conducted a systematic review to summarize the existing literature on the association between social media use and symptoms in this population.Study DesignA systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Embase and Medline, from January 2010 to May 2025. We included studies which reported an association between social media use and symptoms of schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders, except for reviews. Search results were screened using Rayyan, and data was extracted according to predefined categories.Study ResultsThe included studies were largely cross-sectional (64%), and had moderate-high risk of bias. However, some key findings emerged. Firstly, social media was linked with fewer negative symptoms and better functioning. Secondly, there was no association between increase in positive symptoms and social media use. Thirdly, the impact of social media on broad psychological distress and symptoms was dependent on engagement type. Finally, qualitative insights confirmed that this population are aware of the risks surrounding social media use; however, they also acknowledged many benefits from its use, including social connection.ConclusionsThis review has clinical and public health implications, such as the need for digital literacy interventions promoting healthy social media use. However, implications are limited by the high risk of bias in findings. Future research should investigate this relationship further through randomized trials and use digital phenotyping to understand social media use patterns and their consequences based on type of engagement and platform.

Introduction

Social media has become a ubiquitous aspect of daily life worldwide. A 2023 Pew Research survey found that 83% of US adults use YouTube, 68% Facebook and 47% Instagram. YouTube (95%) and TikTok (67%) were the most frequently used platforms among teens.1,2 As a result, the impact of social media on mental health, especially on youth depression and anxiety, has garnered significant attention.

However, studies show that individuals with schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders (ISOPS), are equally engaged with social media, with usage patterns similar to the general population.3–6 Despite this prevalence, research on ISOPS has focused almost entirely on the offline world, missing the abundance of interactions that take place online.7 As a result, the impact of social media use among ISOPS is poorly understood.8,9 This paper aims to review and summarize the current research, identifying emerging trends regarding how social media may impact mental health in ISOPS.

Research has hypothesized potential harms from the use of social media in ISOPS, including privacy risks,10 relapse related to decline in incoming and outgoing calls and message, pathological usage pattern,11 and negative impact on mood.12 One potential mechanism for these negative effects may be that ISOPSs find it more challenging to understand and interpret social interactions.13 This difficulty may be exacerbated in an online environment, where non-verbal cues such as facial expressions and verbal tones are unavailable. As a result, ISOPS may experience heightened feelings of anxiety, paranoia and even delusions.

On the other hand, social media can serve as a valuable tool to mitigate feelings of loneliness often experienced by ISOPS. Furthermore, research also suggests that social media may improve psychosocial functioning by providing an accessible platform for social interactions when in-person interactions feel intimidating or difficult.14 The flexibility allowed by social media may be particularly important for ISOPS, given that social support is known to reduce symptom burden and increase medication adherence and quality of life in this population.15 Finally, social media platforms can provide timely psychoeducation, information regarding local care resources, and even serve as a treatment platform (eg., online peer support groups).3,4,16 The accessibility of social media is particularly salient in a world where digital health is increasingly common, and a lack of social media connection may limit access to adequate information, for example through the digital divide, which affects ISOPS and other individuals with mental health conditions.5,17 This divide results in low digital literacy which is incompatible with today’s digital world, meaning individuals which lack access to digital resources may have limited access to resources available online, such as psychoeducation materials or groups.

Overall, the association between social media use and ISOPS remains poorly understood.17 To our knowledge, the existing literature on this relationship has not been systematically reviewed, summarized or quantified.17–22 Understanding the potential for both risks and benefits is especially important for ISOPS. Their complex and burdensome symptom presentation (eg., paranoia, hallucinations) intersecting with psychosocial challenges (eg., isolation, limited resources) may render the population highly susceptible to social media harm, yet uniquely position them to benefit from the accessible connections allowed by social media. Therefore, there is a need for clinical guidance on the possible risks and benefits of social media on symptoms and broad psychosocial functioning of ISOPS, to inform its safe use by individuals in this population while limiting harm. This review aims to be an initial step towards systematically organizing any available evidence on the relationship between social media use and schizophrenia symptoms, drawing any available conclusions to suggest reasonable implications from existing evidence, but also where the greatest needs for future research lie. We aim to address this relevant topic by providing a synthesis of what is a fragmented and inconsistent literature.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted based on a full protocol, also published in the PROSPERO register (CRD4202510-51374).23 We followed the PRISMA guidelines to develop the protocol and to report our systematic review.24

Search Strategy

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Ovid Medline and Embase. The following terms were included in the search strategy “schizophrenia”, “psychosis”, “psychotic”, “schizo”, “social media”. The search was conducted on May 22, 2025.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they investigated the relationship between social media use and symptoms in individuals with schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders. Diagnoses could be self-reported or clinically established. While the primary outcome of interest was any association between social media and symptoms of schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders assessed using validated scales, given the limited research in this area, studies reporting all outcomes were included to ensure that relevant data were not missed. For this review, social media was defined as “an Internet-based application that allows the creation and exchange of user-generated content, and mass personal communication”.25 Consequently, studies assessing messaging-only platforms, or simulated social media environments (eg., an app simulating social-media features) were not included. All comparator groups were eligible, including other diagnoses controls, healthy controls, non-social media users and no comparison group. Eligible designs included quantitative (cross-sectional and longitudinal) and qualitative studies. Review papers and unpublished manuscripts (eg., dissertations and presentations) were excluded. Inclusion was limited to publications published after January 1, 2010.3,4

Identification of Studies and Screening

The study selection process was conducted on the Rayyan platform. Initial title and abstract screening was conducted by three independent reviewers, with any conflicts resolved through discussion and review of eligibility criteria. Subsequently, fifty-six articles selected for full text screening were screened by 4 reviewers, so that each article would be screened twice. Therefore, each reviewer screened 28 references. Articles were screened independently, and in a blinded fashion on the Rayyan platform. This was to reduce contamination of judgment. Any disagreement which could not be resolved within reviewers was discussed with the first author until a decisive agreement was reached.

Data Extraction and Management

Data was extracted on Microsoft Excel by the same 4 reviewers, so each article was extracted twice and cross-checked. Extracted variables included study design, sample demographics and size, diagnostic information, social media platforms assessed, outcome assessed, scale used, and magnitude and direction of use on the outcome. The first author reviewed the extracted information and resolved any discrepancies by discussing with the reviewers and reviewing inclusion criteria.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias for quantitative studies was assessed using Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.26 Each article was independently reviewed by two independent reviewers who resolved conflicts through discussion (with the first author if needed). Based on this assessment, studies were stratified into low, moderate, or high risk of bias. For qualitative studies, MC reviewed all studies (n = 4) according to Cochrane guidance, based on the principles of credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability.27 The first author conducted the qualitative assessment, as Cochrane guidance states the benefit of content expertise for evaluation of quality, due to the higher likelihood of consistent assessment.27

Results

Study Selection and Characteristics

Fourteen studies met the inclusion criteria for this systematic review (see Figure 1 for a PRISMA flowchart). The characteristics of these studies are outlined in detail in Supplementary Material 1. Of the 14 studies, nine were cross-sectional in design, three were longitudinal, one was qualitative and one was mixed methods. The cross-sectional studies varied in their temporal focus. Three studies3,4,28 asked participants to recall behaviors and symptoms from a prior period (eg., during symptom emergence). The other six studies focused on assessing current technology usage, symptoms, and perceptions. The longitudinal studies varied in duration, from an intensive 6-day Experience Sampling Method (ESM) study12 to a 12-month follow-up study with assessments every three months.29

PRISMA flowchart showing the screening process from search to final inclusion. *The total number of excluded records will be inferior to the sum of its breakdown, as some records were excluded for multiple reasons.

Eight studies recruited participants from clinical settings, such as outpatient units, community mental health centers, or specialized early psychosis programs.3,4,29–34 The remaining six studies recruited participants directly online, either through social media platforms like Twitter10 or by also using online advertisements.32

The total sample size across the 14 studies was 1957 participants. Individual study sample sizes ranged from 28 to 409.32,35 Two studies included a healthy or non-clinical control group for comparison.12,28 Three studies utilized a clinical comparison group, comparing findings in schizophrenia-spectrum populations to those with bipolar disorder or other mood disorders.4,32,33 Participant ages varied widely, with studies including cohorts of adolescents and young adults (mean age 18.3-25.47 years), samples of middle-aged adults (mean age 40.38-49.36 years), and much wider ranging (aged 18-65). Many studies did not report racial data, but studies were comprised of predominantly White/European or Asian (Chinese) participants, as well as mixed-ethnicity US-based samples. Where education information was available, the majority of participants included across the studies had completed high school.

Conceptualization and Measurement of Social Media Use

Five studies specifically explored general social media use.7,10,12,29,34 Nine studies investigated a combination of internet use, general social media use, and other digital media use (eg., messaging apps, video games).3,4,28,30–33,35 One study focused on Facebook use in specific.36

Measurement of social media usage varied across studies. Two studies relied solely on binary classification (use vs. non-use) to establish prevalence rates.30,32 Other studies additionally collected more detailed metrics on frequency and duration, such as hours per day or times checked per day.3,4,28,31,34

Moving beyond frequency and duration metrics, several studies examined the qualitative nature of engagement, including the type of use, purpose of use, and the content engaged with. One study distinguished between active (eg., posting about feelings, venting) and passive (eg., scrolling newsfeeds) behaviors, also further examining the differential impact of posting different types of contents (eg., “posting about feelings” vs. “daily activities”).12 Tran and colleagues assessed the setting of social experiences, asking whether positive, negative, or victimizing interactions were more likely to occur online versus offline.7 Finally, two studies specifically investigated patterns of problematic or addictive use.29,36

Social media usage was assessed almost exclusively through self-report. The majority of studies used author-developed questionnaires or interview items created specifically for the study. Only two studies used standardized scales to measure addictive use: Fekih-Romdhane and colleagues36 used the Facebook Addiction Scale (BFAS)37 and the Internet Addiction Test (IAT), and Saffari et al. (2023) used the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale.38

Association of Social Media Use with Clinical and Functional Outcomes

Negative Symptoms and Psychosocial Functioning

Five cross-sectional studies examined the link between social media use and negative symptoms or psychosocial functioning. The studies used a variety of validated, clinician-rated or self-report scales to assess symptoms and functioning (see Supplementary Material 1). These results consistently demonstrated that greater social media use was linked to lower symptom severity and higher functioning.

Specifically, correlational analyzes revealed that greater social media use was associated with lower global negative symptoms,34 and lower asociality during the COVID-19 pandemic (r = -0.44).28 Three studies compared ISOPS who self-reported social media use and non-use.30,31,34 They found that social media users had significantly higher psychosocial functioning scores and lower negative symptom scores compared to non-social media users. However, authors noted that as the cross-sectional associations cannot establish causality; it is unclear if social media use improves functioning or if individuals with better functioning are more likely to use social media.

Positive Symptoms and Paranoia

Eight studies assessed the relationship between social media use and positive symptoms, particularly paranoia, using a range of self-report and clinician-rated scales (see Supplementary Material 1), as well as ecological momentary assessment. Collectively, the findings do not support the hypothesis that social media use exacerbates positive symptoms. Five cross-sectional studies found no significant association between the use of social media and the severity of positive symptoms or paranoia.7,28,30,33,34 Participants in two studies disagreed with statements that social media made them more paranoid or made their voices worse.31,33

Interestingly, two longitudinal studies suggested a potential protective effect of social media.12,32 The studies assessed paranoia in relation to social media use using intensive momentary assessments and found that social media use was associated with a modest decrease in paranoia in the subsequent hours. More specifically, Paquin and colleagues32 reported that social media use was followed by a ~ 10% reduction in paranoia in the schizophrenia group (fold-change = 0.900), with a 5.5% reduction being measured in the overall sample (schizophrenia and bipolar disorder), and Berry and colleagues found that passively viewing newsfeeds predicted a reduction in paranoia.12

Other Psychiatric Symptoms

Six studies examined outcomes related to affective symptoms (ie, depression and anxiety) and general psychological functioning (ie, stress, mood, self-esteem, and quality of life). These studies used scales such as the DASS-21,39 WHOQOL-BREF,40 PANSS depression factor,41 and author-developed measures. Overall, findings do not support a consistent unidirectional relationship between social media and the symptoms. Instead, outcomes appeared to be influenced by moderators such as the quality of connection and specific usage behaviors.

The findings for the simple frequency of social media usage were mixed. Rekhi and colleagues found that lower social media use was associated with more severe depressive symptoms on the PANSS.34 Zhang and Strauss did not find a connection between social media use and depression, but higher usage was linked to higher anxiety.28 On the other hand, Miller and colleagues found a majority of users disagreed that social media made them feel depressed or anxious.31

However, when analyzing usage behaviors, a clearer pattern emerged. Zhang and Strauss found that the perceived quality of connection (“feeling closer” to others via electronic media) was associated with decreased depression.28 Berry and colleagues found that posting about daily activities predicted higher positive affect and self-esteem, whereas “venting” predicted lower mood and self-esteem.12

Finally, problematic use and social media addiction consistently predicted poorer outcomes. In a group of high schizotypal students (at-risk group for transitioning to psychosis), internet addiction, but not adaptive usage, specifically predicted higher psychological distress.36 Saffari and colleagues found that problematic social media use predicted lower quality of life indirectly, by worsening sleep quality and increasing self-stigma.29

These findings must be interpreted with caution. Some studies were limited by a focus on specific contexts (eg., the pandemic) or constructs (eg., addiction).28,29,36 Additionally, some studies used non-validated questionnaires.28,31

Patient Perceptions and Help-Seeking Behaviors

Six studies used qualitative methods to investigate patients’ subjective experiences, attitudes, and behaviors related to social media (Supplementary Material 2). Overall, participants expressed a nuanced but predominantly positive view of social media. A consistent theme was the utility of these platforms for social connection, psychoeducation, and clinical support. Two studies by Birnbaum and colleagues that the internet was a primary resource for help-seeking (eg., information gathering and treatment initiation) during the duration of untreated psychosis.3,4 Furthermore, a large majority of youth with first episode psychosis (76%-78%) were open to being proactively contacted by a mental health professional via social media.3,4 This was echoed by Caponnetto and colleagues and Tanrıverdi Oluğ and colleagues, where the majority of participants viewed technology as helpful for psychological support and were interested in receiving clinical communications (eg., reminders) from their doctors.33,35

Despite the perceived benefits, patients also articulated concerns and risks. A qualitative study by Caponnetto and colleagues asked ISOPS to reflect on their perceptions of digital tools, social networks and their use in the mental health field.35 The participants identified themes of concern around internet addiction, detachment from real-life relationships, exposure to misinformation (“fake news”), and risks of online deception or “scams.” Another study found that even among patients who publicly self-disclosed their illness, 36% reported concerns with data privacy, with the main themes being threats to employment and fear of stigma from others.10 See Table 1 for a summary of the findings outlining an association between social media use and illness outcomes, and Supplementary Material 2 for insights from qualitative findings.

Risk of Bias

Quantitative Studies

Risk of bias was assessed with the Newcastle-Ottawa scale on the 12 purely quantitative studies. Eight of the 12 studies were judged to have moderate risk of bias, with 3 having high risk, and only 1 with low risk (Figure 2).

Cochrane “General” Risk of Bias visualization based on the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, showing 1 low risk, 8 moderate risk, and 3 high risk studies.

Some trends can be seen in the risk of bias visualization. Namely, sample-related criteria (D1 and D2) tend to have low risk of bias, whereas confounder criteria (D5 and D6) tend to have high risk of bias. Outcome and exposure assessment criteria (D3 and D4) are very mixed, probably explaining the prevalence of “moderate risk of bias” in the overall judgment. This is an interesting finding; the assessment of exposure and outcome, which in the Newcastle-Ottawa scale assigns a low risk of bias if using gold-standard assessment tools, and high risk of bias if using inappropriate or insufficiently described assessment tools.26 The bulk of our results, and any implications drawn from these, relies on the accurate and reliable measurement of outcomes. The mixed quality of the assessments used across these 12 papers may therefore be revelatory of potential methodological limitations which may limit our ability to draw reliable conclusions from the results presented.

Qualitative and Mixed Methods Studies

There was one fully qualitative and one mixed methods study which were not fit for risk of bias assessment using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale.10,35 Using similar criteria to the assessment of quantitative findings, Cochrane guidance suggests using the 4 criteria of credibility (whether the representation of the data fits participant views), transferability (whether findings are generalizable to other settings), dependability (whether the research process is clearly documented and logical) and confirmability (whether findings are objective) (Figure 3).27

Risk of Bias in qualitative studies assessed according to cochrane collaboration qualitative methods group.

In terms of credibility, both studies had 2 independent researchers look over the data and develop codes for thematic analysis, and then discuss together to reach a consensus, and both studies also reported verbatim quotes. In terms of transferability, both studies provided detailed research and interview questions adopted in the study, and detailed demographic information on participants. For dependability there was moderate risk of bias for both studies, with Naslund and Aschbrenner not providing any specific details of their thematic analysis approach or the rationale behind it, and both studies not showing any evidence of reflexivity or peer review throughout the critical appraisal of results.10,35 However, they both laid out their methods clearly and logically. In terms of confirmability, there was little to no information on researchers or their backgrounds and biases in both studies. Overall, these two studies seem to be most biased when it comes to researcher bias (including their reflexivity, and how any of their biases may affect the interpretation of results); their procedures and methods are, however, thoroughly reported and conducted, thus increasing the reliability of any implications drawn from their findings.

Discussion

The rapid integration of social media into daily life has resulted in significant clinical and research interest in its impact on individuals with serious mental illness, especially schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders (ISOPS). Despite evidence that individuals with schizophrenia are active on social media platforms,21 a systematic synthesis of the clinical risks and benefits of social media use in schizophrenia is lacking. This systematic review synthesized evidence from 14 studies that broadly investigated the relationship between social media use and symptoms in ISOPS. Among the included studies, nine were cross-sectional, highlighting the need for future research using more rigorous, longitudinal designs to establish causal conclusions about the effects of social media use on ISOPS.

Despite the limitations, a few key findings emerged. First, of the 5 cross sectional studies that explored the link between social media use and negative symptoms, all found that higher social media use was linked to lower symptom severity and higher functioning. It is impossible to know the direction of this relationship, due to the cross-sectional nature of the findings; it is therefore possible that any such association is due to the fact that higher-functioning individuals may have more motivation to engage with social platforms, not necessarily that engaging with the platforms improves symptoms. Secondly, of the eight studies that assess the relationship between social media use and positive symptoms, none explicitly states that social media exacerbated positive symptoms, with two longitudinal studies even suggesting a potential protective effect of social media use. The other six studies found no significant association between social media use and the severity of positive symptoms, with qualitative findings showing that participants disagreed with statements that social media increased their paranoia or hallucinations. However, it must be noted that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, and more longitudinal research is needed to establish causality. Indeed, selection biases should be considered when interpreting these results; it is unlikely that individuals with high prevalence of positive symptoms would engage with social media at all, due to low functioning and generally lower digital literacy. Thus, this association should also be explored in non-users. Thirdly, results for social media’s impact on broader psychological distress and mood, while appearing largely influenced by moderators such as quality and type of use, were complex, dynamic and mixed. Indeed, the quality of these studies was very poor, and results should thus be interpreted with caution. Finally, qualitative studies revealed that ISOPS acknowledge concerns and risks associated with social media. On the other hand, many individuals also discussed social media as a platform for social connection, psychoeducation, and clinical support.

The lack of longitudinal data should be of primary concern for the available evidence base. The field is in need of more research to examine the longitudinal associations between social media use and psychosis symptoms, for example using repeat measures and EMA. Additionally, research needs to assess the separate effects of various types of social media use on symptoms. Indeed, from the current study, it is unclear whether interactions with social media that result in short-term distress reduction may offer a longer term benefit. This significant gap outlines that social media use and its effects is a very complex and heterogeneous phenomenon, on which we require additional data before making decisive conclusions on its safety for ISOPS. Consequently, findings from this review which are framed as potentially beneficial should be interpreted with the possibility that higher-functioning individuals may have greater access, digital literacy, motivation and social capacity to engage with online resources, and that this is a key shortcoming of cross-sectional data.

A prominent issue with interpreting results from this review is the consistently high risk of bias of studies and their findings. Therefore, the reliability of results identified may be limited. Biases found in the assessment of exposure and outcome could be limited in future research by the use of gold-standard assessment tools, instead of author-generated scales, which are prevalent in current social media research, perhaps as it is a relatively novel field. Additionally, our findings showed high risk of bias in confounding; to mitigate this risk, future studies could endorse randomized controlled designs, reducing the impact of confounder variation, and clarifying directionality of findings, while assessing underlying mechanisms.

The challenges in assessing the impact of social media on ISOPS are paralleled by the broader literature on the impact of social media on mental health more generally. While there is public concern about social media, there is an evidence base which suggests this relationship might be more nuanced, a picture that mirrors the findings in our study.42,43 While aspects of schizophrenia and related disorders, including cognitive impairment, stigma, and paranoia, may create additional risk, our results do not bear this out. For example, in two studies,29,31 a majority of participants disagreed that social media led to increased paranoia. Rather, our findings that ISOPS identify benefits of social media align with broader health technology trends of increasing feasibility, interest, and active adoption. This review also highlights that the quality of social media use acts as a stronger determinant of psychological outcomes than the quantity of use. We identified a few specific problematic use patterns, such as addictive patterns and venting. Combined with other research,43 this review provides strong evidence that the risk is likely not social media use itself, but rather the specific cognitions and behaviors (eg, venting, privacy precautions).

This review has clinical, empirical and public health implications. Beyond emphasizing the need for new research, the potential benefits and harms identified in our study highlight the potential of digital literacy interventions. These interventions could be used to teach individuals practical skills, such as navigating misinformation and managing privacy settings, as well as healthy usage habits, such as mitigating social comparison, limiting screen time, and disengaging from negative online content. This could help to mitigate any harms resulting from dysfunctional or untrained use of social media. Additionally, these interventions would address the digital divide, whereby digital exclusion may exacerbate existing inequalities for ISOPS.44 Initial work in this area is already demonstrating a positive effect for serious mental illness,45 providing a foundation for future adaptations tailored to the specific needs and vulnerabilities of those with schizophrenia.

Second, this review identified preliminary signs of between- and within-person association connecting social media use patterns and schizophrenia symptoms. This suggests that changes in individuals’ social media usage could serve as an early warning sign for clinical relapse. For example, objective indicators such as a sharp increase in screen time or shifts in linguistic patterns may flag opportunities for early intervention.46,47 Future research may investigate these markers captured through digital phenotyping and processed with machine learning, thereby improving the granularity and objectivity of exposure measurement relative to self-report. We note, however, that these methods do not address the sampling and confounding biases identified in our risk-of-bias assessment, as those biases require longitudinal, prospectively designed studies. As a pragmatic first step, clinicians should integrate questions about individuals’ social media patterns into standard assessments.

Finally, the high reported willingness to engage with health professionals via social media indicates that ISOPS are receptive to intervention in digital spaces. While public health campaigns have already leveraged social media to reach individuals,48 our findings highlight their potential for more interactive interventions, such as peer-to-peer support.49

This review has several limitations. The included studies were predominantly cross-sectional in design with small, convenience samples. The risk of bias was moderate to high. The reliance on correlational data limits the review’s conclusion about the longer-term outcomes of social media use, or the potential causal pathways between specific online behaviors and clinical outcomes. Additionally, the lack of an expert qualitative researcher to check qualitative risk of bias assessment could reduce the thoroughness of the assessment. Furthermore, synthesis of findings based on platform or engagement type was not possible, due to inconsistent reporting. Future studies should clearly specify platform type and engagement style when assessing the relationship between use and outcome. As the papers included in this review are inconsistent with reporting of engagement type, any conclusions may not apply uniformly across these types of use. Future research should also shift from measuring general social media use to a more nuanced understanding of specific social media behaviors. The qualitative studies included in this review provide a rich picture of both the perceived benefits and the concerns of this population. These in-depth insights should guide the design of robust quantitative studies, including longitudinal data collections or Ecological Momentary Assessment.
