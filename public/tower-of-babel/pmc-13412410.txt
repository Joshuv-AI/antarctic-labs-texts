Lenvatinib Added to Nivolumab and Ipilimumab Improves Tumor Control and Progression-Free Survival in Unresectable Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Kuan-Chang Lai, Chien-An Liu, Hao-Jan Lei, San-Chi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn CheckMate-9DW, the suboptimal early tumor control of nivolumab-ipilimumab resulted in higher early mortality compared with the sorafenib/lenvatinib arm during the first 12 months. We evaluated whether adding lenvatinib to nivolumab-ipilimumab (NIL) improves outcomes versus nivolumab-ipilimumab (NI).MethodsPatients with unresectable HCC treated with NI with or without lenvatinib were retrospectively analyzed. Responses were evaluated by RECIST v1.1, survival outcomes by Kaplan-Meier and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) adjustment, and multiple sensitivity analyses. Liver function changes were evaluated using the albumin–bilirubin (ALBI) score.ResultsAmong 104 patients, 82 received NI and 22 received NIL. The NIL group demonstrated a higher disease control rate than the NI group (81.8% vs. 39.0%; p < 0.01), with similar duration of response (21.4 vs. 21.8 months). Median progression-free survival (PFS) was longer with NIL (10.2 vs. 3.2 months), but not for overall survival (OS). In the first-line setting, NIL showed a trend toward longer PFS and fewer early deaths within 12 months. After IPTW adjustment and sensitivity analyses, the NIL regimen consistently demonstrated improved PFS (HR 0.44; p < 0.01) without a statistically significant OS benefit. Hypertension, proteinuria and stomatitis were more frequent in the NIL group, while ALBI scores remained stable. Development of immune-related adverse events was associated with improved outcomes.ConclusionThis real-world study provides proof-of-concept evidence that incorporating a VEGF inhibitor into dual ICIs therapy is associated with improved tumor control and prolonged PFS, with a potential reduction in early mortality. Prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings.

Introduction

Over the past 5 years, the treatment landscape for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (uHCC) has evolved from tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) monotherapy to immune-based combination regimens, including atezolizumab plus bevacizumab (IMbrave150), durvalumab plus tremelimumab (HIMALAYA), and nivolumab plus ipilimumab (CheckMate-9DW) [1–3]. Each of these regimens has achieved meaningful improvements in survival and response compared with TKI monotherapy, establishing immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based therapy as the standard first-line treatment for advanced HCC. Among these, atezolizumab plus bevacizumab demonstrated the highest disease control rate, attributable to the synergistic effects of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) blockade and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibition. In contrast, ICI-only regimens, while capable of inducing durable responses, appear less effective for early tumor control.

In the phase III CheckMate-9DW trial, nivolumab plus ipilimumab was compared with single-agent TKI (lenvatinib or sorafenib) as first-line therapy [3]. Although the dual ICI regimen yielded superior long-term overall survival (OS), both the progression-free survival (PFS) and OS curves exhibited a cross-over pattern within the first year, reflecting suboptimal early disease control relative to VEGF-targeted agents. This finding suggests that VEGF blockade plays a role in preventing early disease progression and mortality.

Recent efforts to enhance efficacy through additional immune modulation have been less successful. The phase III IMbrave152/SKYSCRAPER-14 trial, which combined atezolizumab and bevacizumab (ate/bev) with the anti-TIGIT antibody tiragolumab, failed to demonstrate superior outcomes compared with the standard ate/bev regimen [4]. Collectively, these findings underscore an unmet clinical need for novel combination strategies capable of achieving both better disease control and durable outcomes.

Evidence from other malignancies supports the potential value of this approach. For example, in clear-cell renal cell carcinoma, first-line triplet therapy with cabozantinib, nivolumab, and ipilimumab improved PFS and objective response rate (ORR) compared with dual ICIs, with survival curves that did not cross – indicating consistent benefit throughout treatment [5]. These findings provide a rationale for evaluating lenvatinib, a potent VEGFR/FGFR inhibitor, in combination with nivolumab and ipilimumab to enhance early disease control in advanced HCC.

Method

Patient Selection

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in the Department of Oncology, Taipei Veterans General Hospital, Taiwan. Between January 2018 and April 2025, patients aged 18 years or older with histologically or clinically confirmed unresectable HCC were identified through electronic medical records. Unresectable HCC was defined as tumors not amenable to curative resection or locoregional therapy as determined by a multidisciplinary team. Macrovascular invasion was defined as the presence of a radiologically confirmed tumor thrombus within the main portal vein or its major branches, the hepatic veins, or the inferior vena cava. All included patients had received systemic therapy containing nivolumab and ipilimumab with or without lenvatinib, in any line of treatment. Patients had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–3 and baseline liver function ranging from Child-Pugh class A to C. Pre-treatment esophagogastroduodenoscopy was not mandatory for study inclusion. Comprehensive clinical information was collected, including demographics (age, sex), viral infection status, baseline biochemistry and hematology, underlying comorbidities, tumor markers, tumor stage, imaging findings, prior local and systemic therapies, treatment outcomes, and treatment-related toxicities.

Treatment

In clinical practice, prior treatment exposure influenced regimen selection. Patients with prior lenvatinib exposure were more likely to receive nivolumab-ipilimumab (NI) sequentially, whereas those without prior lenvatinib exposure were more frequently considered for the off-label nivolumab-ipilimumab-lenvatinib (NIL) combination through shared decision-making. For patients receiving the NIL regimen, lenvatinib was initiated at a weight-based starting dose of 12 mg once daily for patients weighing ≥60 kg and 8 mg once daily for those weighing <60 kg, administered continuously. Nivolumab (3 mg/kg) plus ipilimumab (1 mg/kg) was administered every 3 weeks for four doses, followed by nivolumab (3 mg/kg) every 2 weeks as maintenance therapy. In patients receiving NIL, immunotherapy was initiated within 1 week of starting lenvatinib, provided no high-grade adverse events had occurred.

Dose modifications of lenvatinib were performed according to toxicity, with stepwise reductions to 8 mg or 4 mg daily as clinically indicated. Temporary interruption was allowed for intolerable adverse events. When immune-related adverse events (irAEs) occurred, management and decisions regarding treatment interruption, discontinuation, or rechallenge were conducted in accordance with the Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer (SITC) guidelines [6].

Outcome Measures

The imaging assessment schedule followed a 2- to 3-month cycle, with radiological response evaluated via either computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. Treatment response was evaluated according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 and modified RECIST (mRECIST) criteria by two independent specialists. Definitions were as follows: complete response (CR), disappearance of all viable tumors; partial response (PR), a ≥30% reduction in the sum of diameters of target lesions; stable disease (SD), a change in tumor size between −30% and +20%; and progressive disease (PD), a ≥20% increase in the sum of target-lesion diameters. The ORR was defined as the proportion of patients achieving CR or PR, while the disease control rate (DCR) included CR, PR, and SD. The OS was measured from initiation of nivolumab-ipilimumab-based therapy to death from any cause. The PFS was defined as the interval from treatment initiation (specifically, the first dose of lenvatinib in the NIL group) to the date of disease progression or death. Duration of response (DoR) was calculated from the date of the first documented response (CR/PR) until disease progression or death, whichever occurred first.

Treatment-Related Adverse Events

Treatment-related adverse events were recorded and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. Immune-related adverse events (irAEs) were defined as adverse events with a suspected immune-mediated etiology that required close monitoring, systemic corticosteroids, or other immunosuppressive treatment, as determined by the treating physician. For survival analyses, all irAEs occurring from treatment initiation until the end of follow-up were included.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics and treatment-related adverse events were summarized descriptively (mean, median, standard deviation, interquartile range, and proportions). Group differences in baseline features, adverse events, and associations between treatment response and clinical variables were evaluated using the chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. DoR, PFS, and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between survival curves were evaluated with the log-rank test. The Cox proportional hazards model was applied to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and to identify prognostic factors for disease progression and death in both univariate and multivariate analyses. Variables with a p value <0.1 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. Liver-function dynamics were evaluated based on longitudinal changes in the albumin–bilirubin (ALBI) score, and comparisons during therapy were performed using paired t-tests. Patients with missing laboratory values at either time point were excluded. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To adjust for baseline differences between treatment groups, propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression incorporating sex, age, HBV infection, HCV infection, Child-Pugh class, extrahepatic metastasis, MVI, AFP level, prior lenvatinib exposure, prior ICI exposure, and treatment line. Stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to balance baseline covariates, with balance assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs). To evaluate the robustness of survival outcomes under different modeling assumptions, several sensitivity analyses were performed, including 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM), doubly robust estimation, multivariable Cox regression. Early mortality was assessed using restricted mean survival time (RMST) analysis, landmark analysis, and piecewise hazard modeling. To account for potential immortal time bias in the evaluation of irAE, time-dependent Cox regression and a 3-month landmark analysis were also performed. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 21.0 and R software.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 104 patients with HCC treated with nivolumab-ipilimumab-based therapy were included. Of these, 82 received dual immunotherapy with nivolumab and ipilimumab (NI), and 22 received a triple-combination regimen incorporating lenvatinib (NIL).

In the overall study population, the median age was 60.9 years (interquartile range, 48.6–69.4). Hepatitis B virus infection was present in 70.2% of patients. ALBI grade 1 and grade 2 each accounted for 44.2% of cases. Extrahepatic metastasis was observed in 71.2% of patients, and MVI was present in 40.4%. Most patients (79.8%) were classified as Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage C. Regarding prior targeted therapies, 59.6% had previously received lenvatinib, 28.8% sorafenib, and 33.7% bevacizumab. When comparing the NIL and NI groups, no significant differences were observed in age, sex, HBV/HCV infection, baseline liver function (including Child-Pugh classification and ALBI grade), presence of extrahepatic metastasis, MVI, BCLC classification, or history of prior locoregional therapy. However, patients in the NIL group tended to have less prior exposure to immunotherapy and lenvatinib and were more frequently treated in earlier lines of systemic therapy. Efficacy

In the NIL group, the ORR was numerically higher than that in the NI group (27.3% vs. 18.3%), as was the CR rate (18.2% vs. 2.4%). The DCR was significantly higher in the NIL group compared with the NI group (81.8% vs. 39.0%, p < 0.01). The DoR was comparable between the two groups (21.4 vs. 21.8 months). In the first-line setting, the ORR reached 66.7% in the NIL group, higher than 33.3% in the NI group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance due to the limited number of cases. The DCRs in both groups were high (100% and 60%, respectively) (Table 2). Among the 10 patients (12.2%) in the NI group with missing response assessments, 9 patients died within 1.5 months of treatment initiation before undergoing their first scheduled follow-up imaging evaluation, and the remaining patient had just initiated therapy and had not yet undergone the first response assessment at the time of data cutoff. Evaluable-response analysis restricted to patients with at least one post-baseline imaging assessment (n = 72). In this subset, the ORR was 20.8% and the DCR was 44.4% (Table S1). Using mRECIST criteria, a similar trend was observed, with a numerically higher ORR (31.8% vs. 18.3%) and significantly higher DCR (81.8% vs. 39.0%, p < 0.01) in the NIL group compared with the NI group (Table S2). The treatment outcomes under NIL therapy are illustrated in the swimmer plot (Fig. 1).

Progression-Free Survival and Overall Survival

At the data cutoff, 63 OS events and 85 PFS events were recorded. The median follow-up duration for the entire cohort, estimated via the reverse Kaplan-Meier method, was 30.5 months (95% CI, 22.8–38.3). The NIL group demonstrated significantly longer PFS and OS compared with the NI group. The median PFS in the NIL group was 10.2 months (95% CI, 3.6–16.9), significantly longer than 3.2 months (95% CI, 2.6–3.8) observed in the NI group. Likewise, the median OS was not reached in the NIL group, whereas it was 9.4 months (95% CI, 5.3–13.5) in the NI group (Fig. 2a, b). The primary reasons for overall regimen discontinuation were disease progression (63.6% in the NIL group vs. 56.1% in the NI group) and death (9.1% vs. 26.8%, respectively). Regarding treatment adherence, 81.8% (NIL) and 80.5% (NI) of patients failed to complete the four cycles of nivolumab/ipilimumab induction. Maintenance nivolumab was interrupted in 54.5% of the NIL group compared to 25.6% of the NI group, while lenvatinib dose reduction or interruption occurred in 86.4% of patients in the NIL group. Propensity Score Weighting and Sensitivity Analyses

Following stabilized IPTW, covariate balance improved across the cohort (Fig. S1). Stabilized weights were well-distributed without extreme values, with a maximum weight below 2. SMDs were reduced across most variables, as illustrated in the Love plot (Fig. S2) and summarized in Table S4. In IPTW-adjusted analyses, the NIL regimen was associated with significantly prolonged PFS (HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.24–0.80, p < 0.01), whereas the OS difference did not reach statistical significance (HR 0.54, 95% CI 0.26–1.10, p = 0.09). Sensitivity analyses yielded consistent findings for PFS across multiple approaches, including propensity score matching (HR 0.40, p = 0.02), doubly robust estimation (HR 0.37, p < 0.01), and multivariable Cox regression (HR 0.47, p = 0.03). For OS, results were directionally similar but statistically non-significant in most models (Table S5).

Early Mortality Analysis

To further explore the potential early survival benefit suggested by the Kaplan-Meier curves, several additional analyses were performed. A 12-month RMST analysis showed a numerically longer survival in the NIL group compared with the NI group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (RMST difference 1.8 months, 95% CI −0.94 to 4.54; p = 0.20) (Table S6). Consistently, landmark analyses at 3 and 6 months demonstrated numerically higher survival rates in the NIL group during early follow-up, although these differences were not statistically significant (Table S7). Piecewise hazard modeling within the first 12 months also did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference between groups (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.16–4.28; p = 0.82). These analyses suggest a potential early survival benefit with the NIL regimen, although the statistical power may have been limited by the small sample size.

Prognostic Factors for Progression and Death

Univariate analysis identified treatment group, Child-Pugh class, MVI, prior exposure to ICIs, prior lenvatinib exposure, and treatment beyond the first line as factors associated with disease progression. In multivariate analysis, the NIL regimen remained independently associated with improved PFS, while Child-Pugh classes B or C and the presence of MVI were risk factors (Table 3). Regarding OS, multivariate analysis demonstrated that Child-Pugh classes B or C and the presence of MVI were independent adverse prognostic factors for death, while the NIL regimen showed a non-significant trend toward improved survival (Table 3).

Treatment-Related Adverse Events

Adverse events are summarized in Table 4. In the NIL group, the most common adverse events of any grade were hypertension (54.5%), transaminase elevation (31.8%), pruritus (27.3%), fatigue (27.3%), proteinuria (22.7%), rash (18.2%), and diarrhea (18.2%). Compared with the NI group, hypertension, proteinuria and stomatitis occurred significantly more frequently in the NIL group (54.5% vs. 3.7%, 22.7% vs. 1.2% and 13.6% vs. 1.2%, respectively; both p < 0.05). Most other adverse events occurred at comparable frequencies between the two groups. Grade 3–4 adverse events were uncommon in both groups, suggesting that the addition of lenvatinib did not substantially increase severe toxicities. Overall, the NIL regimen was associated with higher rates of hypertension, proteinuria and stomatitis but showed a generally tolerable safety profile.

Association between irAEs and Clinical Outcomes

To address potential immortal time bias, the association between irAE development and survival outcomes was evaluated using time-dependent Cox regression and 3-month landmark analyses. In the time-dependent Cox model, irAE occurrence was treated as a time-varying covariate. Development of an irAE was significantly associated with improved PFS (HR 0.41, 95% CI 0.27–0.64; p < 0.001) and OS (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.33–0.84; p = 0.01) (Table S8). A 3-month landmark analysis was subsequently performed. Among patients who remained progression-free at 3 months (n = 57), early irAE development was associated with significantly longer subsequent PFS (HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.24–0.82; log-rank p = 0.01), with clear separation of the landmark PFS curves (Fig. 3c). Similarly, early irAE development was associated favorable OS (HR 0.36, 95% CI 0.16–0.80; p = 0.01) (Table S9, Fig. 3c, d).

Liver Function Monitoring

Changes in liver function were evaluated using ALBI scores at baseline, 4 weeks, and 8 weeks after treatment initiation. After excluding patients with missing laboratory values, 19 patients in the NIL group and 62 patients in the NI group were included in the paired t-test analysis. In the NIL group, the mean ALBI score remained largely stable throughout treatment, showing no significant change from baseline to week 4 or week 8 (both p > 0.05). In contrast, the NI group exhibited a mild but significant deterioration in ALBI score at both week 4 and week 8 compared with baseline (both p < 0.05). The magnitude of ALBI decline was smaller in the NIL group, suggesting that the addition of lenvatinib to nivolumab and ipilimumab did not lead to significant hepatic functional decline (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The major findings of this study include: (1) the addition of lenvatinib to nivolumab and ipilimumab demonstrated, higher DCR, and prolonged PFS, but without a clear survival advantage; (2) in the first-line setting, the triple combination also enhanced DCR and reduced early progression within the first 12 months; (3) the triple combination of lenvatinib, nivolumab, and ipilimumab did not increase the incidence of high-grade adverse effects (AEs). The increase in low-grade AEs was mainly attributable to lenvatinib-related events, and liver function remained overall stable during treatment.

In the CheckMate-9DW trial, dual ICIs therapy with nivolumab and ipilimumab was compared against single-agent multi-kinase inhibitors (lenvatinib or sorafenib) as first-line treatment for unresectable HCC. The Kaplan-Meier curves for PFS and OS demonstrated a distinct cross-over at approximately 12 months, reflecting superior early disease control with lenvatinib or sorafenib during the first year of therapy [3]. This finding suggests that the presence of VEGF inhibition may decrease early disease progression and mortality. While direct cross-trial comparisons are inherently limited by differences in patient populations and study designs, they provide indirect insight into this observation. Among the three approved first-line combination regimens, the atezolizumab-bevacizumab regimen – which incorporates VEGF blockade – achieved a numerically higher disease control rate (74%), compared with nivolumab-ipilimumab (68%) and the STRIDE regimen (durvalumab plus tremelimumab, 60%) [1–3]. Direct comparison of these figures is limited by inter-trial variability. However, in IMbrave150, the ate/bev arm maintained persistent PFS superiority over sorafenib throughout the follow-up period. In contrast, both the STRIDE and sorafenib arms in HIMALAYA showed a rapid early decline in PFS, with the progression-free rate dropping below 50% at 6 months. Collectively, these data underscore the contribution of VEGF inhibition to sustain tumor control in the early treatment phase.

Dr. Kudo noted that, although cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted with caution, due to potential selection bias and heterogeneity across study cohorts, the control arms across major studies showed nearly identical OS curves. He observed a consistent trend that regimens incorporating VEGF inhibition – such as lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab or atezolizumab plus bevacizumab – demonstrated superior OS during the first year, particularly within the initial 6 months, compared with ICI-only regimens [7]. While these observations are speculative and subject to the biases of cross-study synthesis, our findings support the same proof-of-concept: adding lenvatinib, a potent VEGF inhibitor, to nivolumab and ipilimumab improved tumor control and reduced early disease progression, underscoring the potential of VEGF inhibition in preventing early mortality during first-line therapy.

Lenvatinib is a multi-kinase inhibitor targeting VEGFR, FGFR, PDGFR, KIT, and RET [8]. In the phase III REFLECT trial, it achieved non-inferior OS versus sorafenib but demonstrated superior tumor control, with a DCR of 75.5% and PFS of 7.4 months [9]. Similar efficacy was observed in LEAP-002 (DCR 78%, PFS 8.0 months) and in the CheckMate-9DW control arm, in which 85% were treated with lenvatinib (DCR 75% and PFS 9.2 months) [10]. In real-world data from our multicenter cohort of 205 patients, lenvatinib yielded a DCR of 80.5% and PFS of 7.3 months [11]. Collectively, these results demonstrate the consistent and reproducible efficacy of lenvatinib across clinical and real-world settings, supporting its integration with immunotherapy to enhance tumor control.

Tumor angiogenesis, driven by VEGF-mediated vascular abnormalities, impairs immune-cell infiltration and limits the efficacy of ICIs [12]. Anti-VEGF therapy can normalize the tumor microenvironment and enhance antitumor immunity [13]. In preclinical HCC models, lenvatinib combined with anti-PD-1 therapy reduced regulatory T cells and activated CD8+ T cells and dendritic cells, demonstrating its immunomodulatory potential [14]. Although lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab did not show a survival advantage in phase III trials, a recent meta-analysis and our previous studies have demonstrated meaningful clinical benefits from combining lenvatinib with anti-PD-(L)1 therapy [10, 15, 16]. Ongoing trials, including SLIDE-HCC (NCT06880523) and TREMENDOUS-2 (NCT07081633), are further evaluating this strategy. In the present study, adding lenvatinib to nivolumab-ipilimumab improved response and PFS, but did not enhance long-term survival durability, suggesting that prolonged outcomes are likely limited by resistance to VEGF inhibition and ultimately depend on the sustained efficacy of dual ICI therapy.

The main concern when combining lenvatinib with dual ICIs therapy (nivolumab and ipilimumab) is potential liver toxicity. In the REFLECT trial, lenvatinib was associated with elevated liver enzymes, though high-grade events occurred in fewer than 7% of patients [9]. In contrast, the LEAP-002 study showed that lenvatinib combined with immunotherapy did not increase hepatic toxicity [10]. Previous reports have noted ALBI-grade deterioration within 4 weeks of lenvatinib treatment [17]. In our study, however, the NIL group did not show increased hepatic adverse events, and liver function deterioration was less pronounced than in the NI group, supporting the hepatic safety of this triple-combination regimen.

Lenvatinib commonly induces VEGF-related toxicities such as hypertension, proteinuria, and bleeding, along with other adverse events including hand-foot skin reaction (HFSR), fatigue, diarrhea, and hypothyroidism [18]. In the present study, most adverse events were low-grade, and the addition of lenvatinib to nivolumab and ipilimumab did not increase the incidence of irAEs compared with dual ICI therapy alone. Toxicities that were significantly more frequent in the NIL group-hypertension, proteinuria, and HFSR-were primarily lenvatinib-related and generally manageable. Both immune-related and lenvatinib-associated toxicities can be effectively managed according to established guidelines and expert consensus [6, 19]. However, overlapping toxicities remain the most challenging aspect, requiring clinical experience and tailored management strategies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single-center analysis with a limited sample size, especially for patients receiving the NIL regimen, which may have reduced power for subgroup and survival analyses. Second, because treatment selection was based on clinical judgment rather than random assignment, residual selection bias and unmeasured confounding cannot be fully excluded, even though multiple adjustment and sensitivity analyses yielded generally consistent results. Third, while the addition of lenvatinib was consistently associated with improved disease control and longer PFS, the adjusted OS analyses did not demonstrate a clear statistically significant survival advantage. Therefore, any survival signal should be interpreted with caution. Finally, several supportive analyses, including the first-line subgroup, early mortality analyses, and exploratory survival modeling, were based on small numbers of patients or events and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

This study validates the rationale for combining lenvatinib with nivolumab and ipilimumab to improve early tumor control and prevent the initial survival curve cross-over typical of dual immunotherapy. Although lenvatinib potentially reduces early mortality, long-term outcomes likely depend on the durable response of the immune checkpoint inhibitors. These results warrant further investigation in prospective trials to refine the strategic integration of these agents.
