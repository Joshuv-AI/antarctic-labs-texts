Unusual Infections following Haploidentical Hematopoietic Stem cell Transplant in a Case with severe Aplastic Anaemia'
Authors: Manaswinee Mallik, Sarthak Wadhera, Sucheta Guleria, Harsimran Kaur, Valliappan Muthu, Sumeeta Khurana, Charanpreet Singh, Arihant Jain, Gaurav Prakash, Pankaj Malhotra, Alka Khadwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a case of very severe aplastic anemia who developed from an uncommon bacterial, recalcitrant parasitic infection and viral reactivation after haploidentical hematopoietic stem cell transplant. The risk factors for multiple infections in this setting included immunocompromised state due to pretransplant prolonged immunosuppression, viral reactivation and graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) requiring multiple immunosuppressive agents. The index case was successfully treated for pulmonary nocardiosis, refractory giardiasis, cytomegalovirus, and adenovirus reactivation highlighting significant challenges during the management of underlying GVHD and these infections.

Introduction

The curative option for very severe aplastic anemia is matched related donor hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT). However, in the absence of such donors, HSCT from haploidentical donors is being increasingly performed worldwide. Although encouraging outcomes are being reported with haploidentical transplants, these are associated with a higher incidence of post-transplant complications, mainly infections, delayed engraftment, graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), and primary or secondary graft rejection. Apart from the common bacterial, viral and fungal infections, the recipients of HSCT can develop life threatening and sometimes refractory opportunistic infections, which are illustrated by the case being reported here.

Case Report

A 17-year-old male presented with bleeding and history of blood transfusions. Examination revealed marked pallor without lymphadenopathy or hepatosplenomegaly. His complete blood counts were hemoglobin 6.0 g/dL, total white blood cell counts 1.9×109/L, and platelet counts 18×109/L. Bone marrow studies revealed < 5 % cellularity with reduction in all three hematopoietic lineage. Flow cytometry ruled out paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria and stress cytogenetics as well as next generation sequencing were not suggestive of inherited bone marrow failure syndrome which led to the diagnosis of very severe aplastic anemia. He was treated with triplet therapy comprising horse anti thymocyte globulin (hATG), cyclosporine and eltrombopag for 7 months without any appreciable response. He remained transfusion dependent and suffered from two episodes of febrile neutropenia without any clinical localization. The family was counselled for allogeneic HSCT. Due to lack of a matched donor, he underwent sex-matched, cytomegalovirus (CMV)-matched, and blood group matched haploidentical HSCT with cousin brother as donor. The pre-transplant infective work up, including stool routine and microscopy was negative. Prior to conditioning, he had received albendazole and ivermectin as a part of antiparasitic prophylaxis. He was conditioned with rabbit ATG, fludarabine, cyclophosphamide, and total body irradiation (4 Gray), and post-transplant cyclophosphamide, cyclosporine, and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) were given for GVHD prophylaxis. He was started on acyclovir and posaconazole prophylaxis from Day (D) +1, and bactrim prophylaxis from D +30 onwards. Engraftment of neutrophils and platelets occurred on D +18 and +23, respectively. On D +30, he had transient adenovirus (ADV) reactivation resolving spontaneously but had rising cytomegalovirus (CMV) DNAemia requiring treatment with ganciclovir and CMV DNA copies became undetectable by D +50. He developed biopsy proven acute liver GVHD grade 4 (CIBMTR) on D +62, while he was on cyclosporin prophylaxis. He responded to steroids, and MMF was restarted as a steroid sparing agent. He was admitted on D +119, with fever of 101°F, malaise, cough with mild expectoration, and yellowish semi loose stools two to three episode per day for a week. On examination he had pallor, cachexia and bilateral coarse crepitation on chest auscultation. He was non neutropenic did not have any infective episodes prior to this admission, however he was pancytopenic at hospitalization. Investigations are summarized in Table 1. A computed tomography (CT) scan of chest revealed multiple lung nodules (Figure 1A), for which he underwent bronchoscopy, and Nocardia spp. was grown on a culture of bronchoscopic alveolar lavage (BAL) fluid. Nervous system involvement with nocardiosis was ruled out on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain. He was found to be noncompliant with bactrim prophylaxis as per mother's information. Stool routine and microscopy showed cysts and trophozoites of Giardia (Figure 1C), and PCR identified it as Giardia duodenalis (Figure 1D). The upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy was planned to rule out concomitant exitance of other etiology of loose stools like gut GVHD, for which patient refused. The possibility of infection associated secondary hemophagocytic lymphocytosis (HLH) was kept due to ongoing infection, cytopenia, raised serum ferritin (13,534 ng/mL), fever spikes, low fibrinogen (1.15 g/dL), and transaminases > 5× of ULN however H score of 174 did not strongly supported this possibility. He was given intravenous imipenem for three weeks for the treatment of nocardiosis along with oral therapeutic dose of bactrim. On follow up, the CT scan of his chest revealed resolution of lesions (Figure 1B). For giardiasis, nitazoxanide was given for 7 days and stool became normal in frequency and consistency and was negative for Giardia. Considering his severely immunocompromised and malnourished state, stool sample was regularly monitored microscopically which showed reappearance of trophozoites of Giardia; hence he was given a combination of metronidazole and nitazoxanide for another two weeks; stool examination became negative again for Giardia and treatment for giardiasis was discontinued. However, there was no weight gain in the absence of gut GVHD despite good appetite. A repeat stool examination again showed presence of the cysts and trophozoites of Giardia suggesting refractory giardiasis. He was put on weekly tinidazole along with weekly stool examination. In view of multiple infections, and no active GVHD, steroids were tapered and stopped. Due to worsening of cytopenias, MMF had to be discontinued and only cyclosporine was continued. Intravenous immunoglobulin prophylaxis once in two weeks was initiated. For cytopenias, he was supported with irradiated blood products, granulocyte colony stimulating factors (GCSF), and thrombopoietin agonist (romiplostim). On follow-up at D +162, his liver enzymes were raised (Table 1), which were attributed to resurgence of liver GVHD, as in the past, he had biopsy proven liver GVHD and a repeat biopsy was refused by the patient. The elevated liver enzymes responded to addition of cyclosporin and ruxolitinib. He remained on regular follow-up and was off immunosuppression by D +455. On D +485, he had herpes zoster reactivation requiring treatment with therapeutic doses of valacyclovir for 2 weeks followed by valacyclovir prophylaxis. On his last follow-up at two years post transplant, he has gained weight, is off transfusion support and maintaining 100% donor chimerism with no signs of GVHD. He received giardiasis prophylaxis with tinidazole weekly for 1 year and stopped after stool routine and microscopy were negative for Giardia on multiple occasions. The bactrim prophylaxis was given for 18 months then stopped, and he is off prophylaxis for last 6 months. Currently, he is receiving post-transplant vaccination as per schedule.

Discussion

Nocardiosis infection is not very common in HSCT recipients and has been observed in 1.7% of allogeneic cases1. In post allogeneic hematopoietic transplantation, patient undergoing immunosuppression for GVHD prophylaxis as well as treatment can contract opportunistic infections like nocardiosis. The risk factors for nocardiosis include acute or chronic GVHD, multiple immunosuppressants, high dose prednisone, lymphopenia and CMV infection2. Our patient was on prednisone, cyclosporine, and MMF for liver GVHD and was recently treated for CMV reactivation, predisposing him to multiple infections. The reactivation of CMV is common among haploidentical HSCT and the incidence varies from 30-60 % and significantly increases non relapse mortality (NRM)3. Cantoni et al. have reported that the presence of GVHD increases the risk of CMV reactivation and vice versa. Both conditions delay immune reconstitution rendering patient immunocompromised, which predisposes to multiple infections4.

The spectrum of involvement in nocardiosis varies from isolated pulmonary nocardiosis to disseminated infections involving the brain. In patients who get infected with nocardia, brain imaging should be done to rule out brain involvement as duration of therapy varies depending on area of involvement5. Nocardiosis is associated with significant mortality (approximately 30%) within 1 year. The primary site of involvement is pulmonary, and early diagnosis and management could prevent the dissemination of infection. A systematic review reporting 109 cases (irrespective of their immune status) from India found the highest incidences in north India ranging from 17.4-21.1 % and the incidence of isolated pulmonary nocardiosis in India is 92%, neurological and skin involvement in 2% and 5% respectively6. In their study, Yetmar et al. found isolated pulmonary nocardiosis in 74.1%, disseminated in 14.8% and 11.1% had localized skin infection1. The median time of transplant to systemic nocardiosis was 198 days2. Our patient developed breakthrough nocardial infection on bactrim prophylaxis. Similar situation has been reported in a case series by Kurosawa et al. where patients developed nocardiosis while being on cotrimoxazole(bactrim) or pentamidine prophylaxis and all of the patients had chronic GVHD at the time of onset of nocardiosis, and were receiving either cyclosporine alone with or without MMF, and steroid7.

Our patient also had concomitant refractory giardiasis. The presence of both organisms at same time is quite uncommon. Immunocompromised individuals with refractory giardiasis can have varied presentation, from being an asymptomatic carrier to life threatening infection. A case reported by Ma et al. in severe aplastic anemia where severe diarrhea on D +69 by Giardia was mimicked acute gut GVHD, he was treated with metronidazole without recurrence of symptoms. It occurred due to consumption of unsanitary foods8. Another case report by Ajumobi et al. was suspected gut GVHD, on endoscopic biopsy of duodenum revealed presence of giardia trophozoites, patient responded to metronidazole single course without occurrence of similar symptoms9. In a study compromising of cardiac transplant recipients, Snyder et al. reported one patient, who had pre transplant Giardia, had recurrence twice after two course of metronidazole therapy and required quinacrine for clearing the infection10. This raises the importance of pre transplant screening. The treatment of refractory giardiasis warrants combination of antiparasitic drugs and if the combinations therapy fails, the development of resistance or sequestration of organism in other body organs is common. When conventional treatment like metronidazole, nitazoxanide, or tinidazole is ineffective, the alternate combination of metronidazole and quinacrine for 3-4 weeks course has been tried as per previous case report; however, the data and studies are limited11,12.

Our patient initially had gastrointestinal symptoms, which resolved on single agent nitazoxanide. However, he continued to have frequent recurrences of Giardia in stool while off therapy and had a malnourished health state which required treatment with a combination of metronidazole and nitazoxanide for prolonged duration. After recovery, he was put on prophylactic tinidazole throughout the duration of immunosuppression.

Protozoal infections are common in low- and middle-income countries like India. The spores can remain in the environment for months and unsafe water acts as source of infection13. In India, 85.11% of immunocompromised individuals get infected with Giardia14. The retrospective study from north India showed prevalence of 5.2 % by Tejan et al.15.

The basic preventive measures are safe water and sanitation which is still lacking in the remote parts of India. So, considering these conditions, patients should undergo screening stool examination prior to HSCT and also, they should receive either albendazole or ivermectin prophylaxis as in the post-transplant setting, they are at high risk of developing protozoal infections like giardiasis given their immunocompromised status. Our index case might have acquired this infection from intake of unsafe food or water. As compared to immunocompetent individuals, these individuals develop severe disease-causing diarrhea, dehydration and malnutrition8. Immunocompromised individuals develop persistent infections and become chronic carriers causing malnutrition6. Managing multiple Infections in post-transplant HSCT patient is challenging and can be associated with high morbidity as well as mortality. To the best of our knowledge, simultaneous occurrence of nocardiosis and refractory giardiasis in the same patient which was complicated by ongoing liver GVHD has not been reported in the available literature. Parasitic infections are still understudied in HSCT patients16. Even though these are rare infections, the high index of suspicion can prevent misdiagnosis and delay in initiation of therapy in immunocompromised people as these infections require prolong treatment.
