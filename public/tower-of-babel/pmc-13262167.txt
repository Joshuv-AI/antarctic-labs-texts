Crankshaft Phenomenon After Definitive Spinal Fusion in Pre-teens: Should We Be Concerned with Pedicle Screw Constructs?
Authors: Jake H. Goldfarb, Eshan S. Sane, Christian E. Wright, Nicholas C. Speller, Ling Chen, Simon Y. Tang, Keith H. Bridwell, Blake K. Montgomery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBecause of remaining growth, early spinal fusion can lead to the crankshaft phenomenon, which occurs when continued growth of the anterior spine after posterior fusion causes curve progression. This project aims to explore the impact of early fusion on crankshaft.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted of idiopathic scoliosis patients aged 8-12 treated with posterior spinal fusion. Inclusion criteria were idiopathic scoliosis diagnosis, thoracic fusion, and ≥2 years of follow-up. Thoracic curve magnitude and Rib Vertebral Angle Difference (RVAD) were measured preoperatively and at the first and the final postoperative follow-ups, with crankshaft defined as a ≥10° change in thoracic angle and ≥20° change in the RVAD. Patient-Reported Outcome Measurement Information System (PROMIS) scores, screw density, and screw length were recorded. Significance was set at P ​< ​.05.ResultsOne hundred thirty-two patients were included, with a mean age of 12.0 years (range: 8–12.9) and mean follow-up of 4.7 years. The mean preoperative thoracic curve was 62° ​± ​17, with 70% of Risser 0 patients ​and 30% having open triradiate cartilage. 95.9% of the screws reached the anterior 50% of the vertebral body. No patients underwent anterior release. The thoracic curve minimally increased from the first to the final follow-up (mean difference: 1.54°, P ​< ​.01), as did the RVAD (mean difference: 4.08°, P ​< ​.01). Eight Risser 0 patients experienced crankshaft under the thoracic criteria and eight under the RVAD criteria. There was no significant association between RVAD and self-image, as measured by PROMIS Peer Relations.ConclusionsPre-teen idiopathic scoliosis patients undergoing early spinal fusion may experience minimal crankshaft exhibited by worsening rib rotation. Surgeons may consider counseling families about small changes to the rib hump, with reassurance of no differences in outcome measures.Key Concepts(1)The crankshaft phenomenon occurs at a low rate after posterior spinal fusion in pre-teens with idiopathic scoliosis.(2)Measuring crankshaft using the thoracic curve magnitude and Rib Vertebral Angle Difference (RVAD) produced similar results with respect to the crankshaft phenomenon.(3)Patients who met criteria for crankshaft did not demonstrate worsening Patient-Reported Outcome Measurement Information System scores.Level of EvidenceIII

Introduction

Young patients undergoing spinal fusion grow after surgery, which may increase their risk for complications such as the crankshaft phenomenon. The crankshaft phenomenon describes the continued anterior spinal growth after a posterior spinal fusion, resulting in curve progression over time [1]. The reported incidence of the crankshaft phenomenon ranges dramatically from 0 to 70% of cases of posterior fusion in the growing young person [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Furthermore, a significant amount of our understanding of crankshaft originates from the previous-generation surgical technique (Cotrel-Dubousset) of hook-based fixation, and hooks do not reach the anterior aspects of the spine. Currently, the most widespread technique is pedicle screw-based fixation, which incorporates screws that can extend to the most anterior aspect of the spine. The risk of crankshaft phenomenon with the current surgical technique is not well defined. Furthermore, it is unknown if screw length may be associated with crankshaft.

As modern trends have shifted toward ​fusing younger patients and disillusionment with growth friendly instrumentation increases ​[11], this analysis aims to provide insights into the crankshaft phenomenon and characterize screw lengths in young spinal fusion. Our goal is to better understand the crankshaft phenomenon in young patients who have undergone spinal fusion with a pedicle screw fusion construct.

Materials and methods

After obtaining institutional review board approval, a retrospective analysis was conducted. Patients were included if they had idiopathic scoliosis, were aged ≤12 ​years at time of surgery, and were treated with posterior spinal fusion that included the thoracic spine using pedicle screw technique from 2005 to 2022 with at least 2 years of follow-up. Patients were excluded if they had previous growing rod surgery or nonidiopathic scoliosis.

Radiographs were assessed at three different time points: preoperatively, at the first postoperative visit, and at the final follow-up visit. The main thoracic and lumbar Cobb angles were collected at all three time points. Preoperatively, the chronological age, Risser score, and triradiate cartilage status were assessed to determine skeletal maturity. Rib Vertebral Angle Difference (RVAD) was calculated at the first and the final follow-up visits. The apex of the thoracic curve was determined using the preoperative radiograph to allow for identification of the periapical spine postoperatively. The periapical spine was defined as the apex, two levels above the apex, and two levels below the apex. The number of screws and total fixation points was then determined for the entire construct as well as for the periapical spine. The fixation density was defined as the number of fixation points per level in the fusion ​and was determined for the total construct as well as for the periapical spine. Utilizing previously described methods, the crankshaft phenomenon was defined as an increase in the thoracic curve of at least 10° from the first to the final follow-up. Due to the increased margin of error for the RVAD [12], crankshaft was defined as an increase of at least 20° in the RVAD from the first to the final follow-up ​[5,13]. As prior studies have analyzed both 10° and 20° RVAD thresholds for crankshaft, the 10° threshold was also included in our analysis ​[6]. Data were collected by trained medical students, and all were trained to collect RVAD data using the same methodology.

Screw lengths

Using a standing sagittal radiograph, screw lengths were determined by dividing the vertebral body into quartiles. Screw lengths were given a score from 1 to 4 based on their depth. A screw reaching the most posterior quartile (0-24%) of the vertebral body was given a screw length of 1, a screw reaching the second quartile (25-49%) was given a screw length of 2, a screw reaching the third quartile (50-74%) was given a screw length of 3, and a screw reaching the most anterior quartile (75-100%) was given a screw length of 4, as shown in Figure 1. Screw lengths were calculated for the 5 levels in the periapical spine. For instances where the apex was a disc (i.e., the T8/T9 disc), screw lengths were calculated for two levels above and two levels below the apical disc. Patient-Reported Outcome Measurement Information System (PROMIS) scores for Mobility, Pain Interference, Upper Extremity, and Peer Relations were collected at preoperative, first follow-up, and final follow-up time points for all patients when available. Mean and standard deviation were reported for continuous variables. All statistical tests were two sided with a significance threshold of 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

A total of 132 consecutive patients met the inclusion criteria. The mean age at surgery was 12.0 years (range: 8-12.9), and patients were followed up for a mean of 4.7 years (range: 2.0–12.2). The 6 patients aged under 10 years had a mean age of 8.9 years (range: 8.0-9.8) and a mean follow-up up of 6.1 years (range: 3.1-10.1), while the remaining 126 patients had a mean age of 12.1 years (range: 10.1-12.9) and were followed up for a mean of 4.6 years (range: 2.0-12.2). Patients were 94% female (125 patients). Eighty-eight patients were Risser 0 (70%), and 40 patients (30%) had open triradiate cartilage. All patients aged under 10 years were Risser 0 preoperatively, and 83% (5/6) were Risser 4 ​at the final follow-up, as shown in Table 1. The mean number of levels fused was 12, ranging from 8 to 15 levels. Preoperatively, the mean thoracic curve angle was 62° ​± ​17, and the mean lumbar angle was 42° ​± ​16. Heights were available at the first and the final follow-up visits for 79 patients. Mean height change from first postoperative follow-up to the final follow-up was 6.1 ​cm ​± ​4.7 (P ​< ​.01). Crankshaft phenomenon

The RVAD minimally increased from the first to the final follow-up (4°, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.9-6.2°, P ​< ​.01). Thirty-six patients (27%) experienced an increase of at least 10° (range: 10°–42°) in the RVAD from the first to the final follow-up, with 11 (8%) experiencing an increase of at least 20°. Twenty-six of the patients who experienced a 10° increase (72%), and eight of the patients who experienced a 20° increase (73%) were Risser 0. Fifteen of the patients who experienced a 10° increase (42%) and six of the patients who experienced a 20° increase (55%) had open triradiate cartilage. The thoracic Cobb angle minimally increased from the first to the final follow-up (22° ​± ​12 ​at the first follow-up and 23 ​± ​13 ​at the final follow-up, 1.5°, 95% CI: 0.5-2.6, P ​< ​.01), as shown in Figure 2 and Figure 3. Only eight patients (6%) had an increase in their thoracic curve angle of more than 10°. All of these patients were Risser 0, and four had open triradiate cartilage preoperatively. Two patients who experienced a 20° increase in the RVAD ​and one patient who experienced a 10° increase in the thoracic angle ​were under 10 year ​of age. Risser 0 patients

Among the 88 Risser 0 patients, the mean follow-up was 4.5 years and the mean preoperative curve was 65 ​± ​18°. The RVAD increased by an average of 4.06° from the first to the final follow-up (P ​< ​.01). The thoracic curve minimally progressed, averaging 21° ​± ​12 ​at the first follow-up compared to 23° ​± ​13 ​at the final follow-up (P ​< ​.01). The lumbar curve remained stable, averaging 16° ​± ​11 ​at the first follow-up compared to 16° ​± ​12 ​at the final follow-up (P ​= ​.60). With an increase in Risser score of one, there was a nonsignificant 25.3% decrease in the risk of thoracic angle progression (P ​= ​.13, OR ​= ​0.75, 95% CI: 0.51-1.09). Eight (9%) Risser 0 patients met the 20° RVAD criteria for the crankshaft phenomenon, and eight patients (9%) met the thoracic angle criteria.

Open triradiate cartilage patients

For the 40 patients with open triradiate cartilage, the RVAD increased by an average of 5.8° from the first to the final follow-up (P ​= ​.02). The thoracic Cobb angle averaged 18° ​± ​10 ​at the first follow-up and 21° ​± ​12 ​at the final follow-up (P ​= ​.01). Compared to patients with closed triradiate cartilage, we did not find that patients with open triradiate cartilage were any more likely to have thoracic angle progression (P ​= ​.26, OR ​= ​1.56, 95% CI ​= ​0.72-3.36). Six (15%) of these patients met the RVAD criteria for the crankshaft phenomenon, while four (10%) met the thoracic angle criteria.

Screw placement

The mean implant density was 1.6 (range: 0.9–2.0) for the total construct and 1.5 (range: 0.6–2) for the periapical region. Neither total construct screw density nor periapical screw density played a significant role in the occurrence of the crankshaft phenomenon under thoracic curve criteria (P ​= ​.27 for periapical region; P ​= ​.90 for the whole construct) or RVAD criteria (P ​= ​.28 for periapical region; P ​= ​.29 for the whole construct). We assessed 940 periapical screws at the first follow-up visit. In total, 95.9% of the screws were scored either a 3 or a 4 (reached anterior half of vertebral body) at the first follow-up (33.7% scored 3 and 62.1% scored 4). The eleven patients experiencing the crankshaft phenomenon according to the RVAD criteria had 90.0% of screws extending more than halfway across the vertebral body (31.3% scored 3 and 58.8% scored 4), while the remaining 121 patients had 95.4% of screws extending to the anterior half of the vertebral body. Additionally, the eight patients experiencing the crankshaft phenomenon according to the thoracic criteria had 87.9% of screws extending to the anterior half. The differences in percentage of screws scoring 3 or 4 were not statistically significant for crankshaft through the RVAD criteria (P ​= ​.65) or the thoracic criteria (P ​= ​.53). The 88 Risser 0 patients had 95.5% of periapical screws extend more than half-way across the vertebral body.

Patient-Reported Outcome Measurement Information System

PROMIS Mobility, Pain Interference, Upper Extremity, and Peer Relationship scores were compared between preoperative, first follow-up, and final follow-up time points for 51 patients. No significant differences were found between preoperative and final follow-up PROMIS scores in any of the four categories. However, as expected, Mobility scores significantly increased from the first to the final follow-up (39.0 ​at the first follow-up vs 51.9 ​at the final follow-up, P ​< ​.01), Pain Interference scores significantly decreased from 49.8 ​at the first follow-up to 42.9 ​at the final follow-up (P ​< ​.01), and Upper Extremity scores significantly increased between the first and the final follow-up (41.6 ​at the first follow-up vs 53.4 ​at the final follow-up, P ​< ​.01). Lastly, Peer Relationship scores, which capture self-appearance, did not significantly change from the first to the final follow-up (51.9 ​at the first follow-up vs 53.8 ​at the final follow-up, P ​= ​.21). This was also unchanged in the 11 patients with PROMIS data who experienced the crankshaft phenomenon under the 10° RVAD criteria (56.9 preoperatively vs 54.1 ​at the final follow-up, P ​= ​.43; and 58.2 ​at the first follow-up vs 54.1 ​at the final follow-up, P ​= ​.56).

Relationships between RVAD and PROMIS data were also analyzed. We did not find a significant correlation between crankshaft according to RVAD or thoracic criteria and Peer Relationship score at the final follow-up (P ​= ​.75 and P ​= ​.56, respectively). Thoracic progression was found to significantly correlate with an increased Upper Extremity score at the final follow-up (P ​= ​.01). No significant associations were found for Pain Interference or Mobility PROMIS scores.

Discussion

The current study analyzed 132 patients aged 12 years and younger, 88 of which were Risser 0, finding that the crankshaft phenomenon occurs at a low rate after posterior spine fusion using modern pedicle screw fixation techniques. While both the thoracic Cobb angle and RVAD slightly increased over time for Risser 0 patients, the crankshaft phenomenon was observed in only 8 patients (9%) using the 20° RVAD criteria and 8 patients (9%) using the 10° thoracic angle criteria. Screw lengths consistently measured into the anterior half of the vertebral body, with 95.9% of screws located in the anterior half. No significant associations were found between crankshaft phenomenon and screw length.

Early analyses of the crankshaft phenomenon demonstrated an almost certain crankshaft phenomenon occurrence, with one study reporting a 98% crankshaft rate ​[1]. However, subsequent analyses have provided different results about its prevalence. Some studies report no instances of crankshaft occurring [[2], [3], [4]], while others report rates ranging from 15% to 35% [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. The crankshaft phenomenon is often described as an increase in the thoracic curve. However, as an increase in the RVAD has also been used to assess crankshaft and may be more sensitive in capturing the rotation of the ribs during crankshaft [5,6,8], the current analysis utilized both crankshaft criteria. Skeletal maturity and preoperative curve angles have been shown to be factors that may predict the occurrence of crankshaft ​[14]. Due to the anatomy of the immature spine and the role of remaining growth, these potential risk factors were considered in the current analysis, with Risser 0 patients and patients with open triradiate cartilage being individually grouped for analyses. Furthermore, due to the inclusion criteria limiting patients to the age of 12 years and younger, the potential for skew by including patients under 10 years of age was considered. The mean follow-up for the six patients aged under 10 years was 6.1 years, and 5 out of 6 were Risser 4 ​at the final follow-up, demonstrating that much of the growth was captured for these younger patients.

The results from Risser 0 patients provide key takeaways from the current study due to a Risser score of 0 corresponding to skeletal immaturity with increased remaining growth. While the rates of crankshaft in Risser 0 patients were 9% according to both RVAD and thoracic angle criteria, higher than the overall cohort average of 8% for the RVAD criteria, and 6% for the thoracic angle criteria, the rates remained low in a large sample of skeletally immature patients. Preoperative curve angles were large for the cohort more broadly, with a mean preoperative angle of 62° for the entire cohort and 65° for Risser 0 patients, demonstrating that crankshaft occurs at a low rate even in spines with moderate to severe preoperative thoracic Cobb angles. The decreased incidence of the crankshaft phenomenon since Dubousset’s classification in 1973 may be explained by the use of pedicle screws, which are strong and may hold against crankshaft ​[13].

While several studies have examined the crankshaft phenomenon in young patients, the current analysis expands upon these prior works. No study to our knowledge has examined screw length in the context of the crankshaft phenomenon. Quantifying screw length by measuring its distance through the vertebral body provides insights into fusion techniques and the relationship between increased screw length and curve progression. Rates of the crankshaft phenomenon were low in our patient cohort. Notably, one recent study analyzing the role of bracing in skeletally immature adolescent idiopathic scoliosis patients with curves greater than 40° found that while bracing serves as a holding strategy, it ultimately lengthens fusions distally ​[15]. In the context of our low rates of crankshaft and screw lengths consistently reaching the anterior half of the vertebral body, our findings suggest that concern for crankshaft may not be a reason to delay definitive fusion in the pre-teen years for appropriate patients.

While PROMIS data were limited overall due to lack of institutional availability until more recently, the current data indicate ​no significant differences between preoperative PROMIS data and final follow-up data years after spinal fusion. The Peer Relationship scores were of interest ​as the presence of the crankshaft phenomenon may be posited to impact interpersonal relationships and internal feelings for patients. While no significant relationships were found using the current data, the small number for patients experiencing the crankshaft phenomenon combined with 39% of patients having PROMIS data makes it challenging to identify clear trends. However, the low rates of crankshaft even in skeletally immature patients combined with stable PROMIS data may suggest patient comfort and satisfaction. Additionally, the statistical significance found between upper extremity and thoracic progression is difficult to explain ​as an increased upper extremity score suggests increased function, contradicting what would be expected with thoracic progression.

Several limitations exist for this study. First, our minimum follow-up was two years. While longer follow-up would have been desired, we felt that two years provided an adequate threshold to view spinal changes over time. We feel that the average follow-up period of over 4.7 years captures much of the growing period for these patients, exemplified by 85% of patients being Risser 4 ​at the final follow-up. Additionally, since most screws included in this study were long, with 95.9% scoring a 3 or 4 ​at the first follow-up, it is unknown if shorter-length screw constructs have similar crankshaft rates. When determining screw lengths, the sagittal view radiograph was utilized. While there is some inherent rotation built into this radiographic view, we believe it provided us with an adequate interpretation of the screw lengths. Furthermore, screws are typically angled and not inserted in a straight line, meaning the 1- to 4-point screw length scoring system may underestimate the screw length. Additionally, we were unable to describe the impact of a formal derotation maneuver on the crankshaft phenomenon ​as this information was not widely available for the patients included in this study. Lastly, PROMIS data were limited for the overall cohort due to an institutional transition to collecting PROMIS scores after many patients had already completed their operative course. The sample size of patients with PROMIS data and RVAD progression is consequently small. Furthermore, PROMIS data may not be the best way to capture patient self-image. However, this is an accepted outcome measure, and SRS-22 scores were not available. We feel that there is value in the PROMIS data for these patients, and the trends over time can still be revealing.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the crankshaft phenomenon occurs at a low rate for Risser 0 pre-teen patients after posterior spinal fusion using the modern pedicle screw technique in pre-teen patients with idiopathic scoliosis. Few patients met the threshold for the crankshaft phenomenon using either the thoracic angle criteria or RVAD criteria. Furthermore, the clinical impact of the crankshaft phenomenon is the age group is questionable ​as it was not associated with worsening PROMIS outcome scores.
