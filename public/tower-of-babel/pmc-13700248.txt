Comparative diagnostic accuracy of PSMA-PET versus conventional imaging for Initial T/N/M staging of intermediate- to high-risk prostate cancer: a patient-level paired bivariate meta-analysis
Authors: Haowen Wu, Wei Zhang, Luchen Yang, Xin Yan, Pan Song, Qiang Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo compare the patient-level diagnostic accuracy of PSMA-PET with multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI), computed tomography (CT), and bone scintigraphy (BS) for initial T, N, and M staging of newly diagnosed intermediate- to high-risk prostate cancer using paired studies.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus from inception to December 1, 2025, for paired studies in which the same patients underwent PSMA-PET and conventional imaging, with histopathology or predefined composite criteria as the reference standard. Risk of bias was assessed with QUADAS-2 and QUADAS-C. Pooled sensitivity and specificity with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using bivariate composite-likelihood models. Modality effects were tested by meta-regression and likelihood ratio tests, and absolute between-modality differences with 95% CIs were estimated using the delta method.ResultsForty-three studies including 4,023 patients were analyzed. For local T staging, PSMA-PET did not show uniform superiority over mpMRI. For extraprostatic extension, PSMA-PET/CT did not differ significantly from mpMRI in sensitivity (absolute difference, -7.6 percentage points; 95% CI, -16.1 to 0.9; p = 0.08) or specificity (-7.0 percentage points; 95% CI, -14.7 to 0.8; p = 0.08); no significant differences were observed for seminal vesicle invasion. In an exploratory six-study comparison, PSMA-PET/MRI had higher sensitivity than mpMRI for extraprostatic extension (+ 12.1 percentage points; 95% CI, 1.9 to 22.2; p = 0.02). For regional lymph node staging, PSMA-PET/CT sensitivity was 69.3% (95% CI, 56.6–79.7) versus 45.3% (95% CI, 33.0–58.3) for mpMRI (difference, + 24.1 percentage points; 95% CI, 6.0–40.3; p = 0.009), whereas specificity did not differ (difference, + 3.2 percentage points; 95% CI, -4.6 to 11.8; p = 0.41). For bone metastasis staging, PSMA-PET had greater sensitivity than BS (95.8% [95% CI, 92.8–98.7] versus 76.2% [95% CI, 68.7–83.8]) and greater specificity (92.8% [95% CI, 89.9–95.7] versus 77.4% [95% CI, 71.3–83.5]; both p < 0.001).ConclusionsPSMA-PET/CT was more sensitive than mpMRI for regional lymph node staging, while specificity was comparable. PSMA-PET had greater sensitivity and specificity than bone scintigraphy for bone metastasis staging. For local T staging, PSMA-PET did not show uniform superiority over mpMRI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16639-2.Keywords: Prostate cancer staging, Intermediate- to high-risk prostate cancer, PSMA-PET/CT, PSMA-PET/MRI, Multiparametric MRI, Computed tomography, Bone scintigraphy, T/N/M staging, Diagnostic accuracy, Meta-analysis, 1⁸F-PSMA, ⁶⁸Ga-PSMA

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most common malignancies among men worldwide [1], with a disease spectrum ranging from indolent lesions to highly aggressive tumors. Early detection and curative intervention are significantly associated with a reduction in prostate cancer mortality among men with at least some unfavorable risk features [2].

Conventional imaging modalities, including computed tomography (CT), multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI), and bone scintigraphy (BS), demonstrate limited sensitivity and specificity in detecting lymph node and distant metastases [3–5], frequently resulting in understaging or overstaging that directly compromises the precision of therapeutic decision-making.

Positron emission tomography targeting prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA-PET), as a highly specific molecular imaging technique, has substantially transformed the clinical management paradigm of prostate cancer [6]. Currently, authoritative nuclear medicine guidelines, represented by the European Association of Nuclear Medicine (EANM) and the Society of Nuclear Medicine and Molecular Imaging (SNMMI), recommend PSMA-PET as a first-line imaging modality for metastasis detection in the initial staging of high-risk prostate cancer patients [7, 8]. Nevertheless, controversies persist in clinical practice: whereas the value of PSMA-PET in metastasis staging (N/M staging) has been widely established, mpMRI remains the current clinical standard for local staging of primary prostatic tumors (T staging), with insufficient direct comparative evidence between PSMA-PET and mpMRI in this specific domain [9–11]. Furthermore, the diagnostic efficacy differences between PSMA-PET/CT and PSMA-PET/MRI warrant further systematic clarification because these hybrid platforms combine the same molecular imaging principle with different anatomical components.

Additionally, although studies have suggested potential health economic advantages of PSMA-PET [12], it remains a resource-intensive examination. High-quality paired comparative studies are therefore needed before its comprehensive integration into routine initial staging pathways for prostate cancer. For these reasons, our review aims to systematically synthesize existing literature evidence to provide clinicians with optimal evidence-based data regarding the diagnostic accuracy of PSMA-PET ligands in the initial staging of prostate cancer.

Materials and methods

Evidence acquisition

This study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Diagnostic Test Accuracy Studies (PRISMA-DTA) guidelines [13], employing the Population, Index test, and Target condition (PIT) framework to define study eligibility. Furthermore, the study protocol was registered with PROSPERO, an international prospective register for systematic reviews. Protocol registration details are accessible at CRD420261299700.

Search strategy and selection criteria

A systematic literature review was conducted using PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and Web of Science databases for articles published from inception to December 1, 2025, with the involvement of Sichuan University Library in developing the search strategy. Specifically, we constructed structured search queries using Boolean logic operators, connecting synonyms within the same concept with OR (e.g., "prostate-specific membrane antigen"/"PSMA"; "prostate cancer"/"prostate neoplasm"), and subsequently combining different conceptual term groups (imaging techniques and target disease) with AND to comprehensively retrieve relevant literature (Supplementary Table 1). Two independent reviewers (W. H. W, Z. W) screened all titles and abstracts and performed full-text review of potentially eligible studies; discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer (Y. X). Reasons for exclusion at this stage were documented, including case reports, conference papers, editorials, non-English publications, commentaries, and studies with fewer than 20 patients.

Studies were eligible for inclusion based on the following criteria: (1) Study population: patients with newly diagnosed intermediate- to high-risk prostate cancer confirmed by biopsy, scheduled to undergo curative-intent treatment; (2) Imaging examinations: all patients must have undergone PSMA-PET, defined broadly as PSMA-ligand PET performed as either PSMA-PET/CT or PSMA-PET/MRI, with no restriction on radioligands or isotopes, and at least one conventional imaging modality before treatment initiation; (3) Reference standard: studies were required to use histopathology or composite clinical criteria as the reference for disease staging. Radical prostatectomy (RP) combined with pelvic lymph node dissection (PLND) pathological results were preferred; when unavailable, composite reference standards based on clinical parameters, serial imaging findings, and pathological evidence obtained during follow-up were accepted; (4) Data reporting: studies must have directly reported or allowed indirect calculation of complete data for diagnostic accuracy analysis, namely the numbers of true positives, false positives, false negatives, and true negatives.

Paired or head-to-head comparison refers to studies in which the same patients underwent PSMA-PET and at least one conventional imaging modality in the pretreatment setting. This terminology indicates within-study comparative diagnostic accuracy assessment and does not imply that all imaging examinations were performed simultaneously or under identical technical conditions.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of all included studies was systematically assessed using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) tool and its comparative review extension (QUADAS-C) (Supplementary Appendix 1), independently conducted by two review authors (W. H. W and Z. W), with disagreements resolved through consultation with a third author (Y. X). The assessment encompassed four core domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. Within each domain, we specifically judged the risk of bias and assessed applicability for the first three domains. Regarding patient selection, studies were judged to be at high risk of bias if they did not explicitly employ consecutive or random enrollment; for the index test, if interpretation of PSMA-PET or conventional imaging was not blinded to the results of the other modality, this was also considered high risk; the reference standard domain was assessed according to the appropriateness, independence, and blinding of histopathological or composite reference standards, rather than being automatically judged as low risk solely because a reference standard was reported; for flow and timing, if the time interval between PSMA-PET and conventional imaging examinations was not explicitly reported, this was judged as high risk. For any domain, if insufficient information in the original literature precluded definitive judgment, the risk for the corresponding item was marked as "unclear."

Outcomes

This meta-analysis aimed to quantify the differences in diagnostic performance between PSMA-PET and conventional imaging modalities in the initial staging of prostate cancer through head-to-head comparisons. To standardize terminology, PSMA-PET is used in this manuscript as a modality-level term for PSMA-ligand positron emission tomography regardless of the anatomical component. When scanner-platform-specific evidence is reported, the terms PSMA-PET/CT and PSMA-PET/MRI are used. The term mpMRI is used consistently for multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging performed for prostate cancer staging, whereas CT and BS refer to computed tomography and bone scintigraphy, respectively. Outcome measures were analyzed at the patient level, with absolute differences in sensitivity and specificity between the two methods used for quantification. The primary outcome measures specifically included: (1) PSMA-PET versus mpMRI for local staging of the primary tumor, with PSMA-PET/CT and PSMA-PET/MRI summarized separately where appropriate; (2) PSMA-PET versus mpMRI for regional lymph node staging, with PSMA-PET/CT- and PSMA-PET/MRI-based cohorts separated according to scanner platform; and (3) PSMA-PET, including PSMA-PET/CT or PSMA-PET/MRI as reported in the included studies, versus BS for bone metastasis staging. Accordingly, M-staging results are reported using the modality-level term PSMA-PET rather than a PET/CT-specific label. Additionally, the comparison between PSMA-PET and abdominopelvic CT for lymph node staging was analyzed as a secondary outcome measure.

Data extraction and analysis

The following information was extracted: (1) demographic and clinical baseline characteristics of the study population; (2) technical details of imaging examinations, including radioligands used for PSMA-PET; (3) specific study design types; (4) details of reference standards used for disease staging, including histopathological results or explicit definitions of composite reference standards; and (5) time interval between PSMA-PET and conventional imaging examinations.

For meta-analysis, the bivariate model [14] was primarily employed to estimate pooled sensitivity and specificity with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Given the complexity of the bivariate model, and in contrast to the standard bivariate regression typically employed in prior systematic reviews, we adopted the bivariate composite likelihood (CL) method as the primary estimation approach for eligible studies meeting the predefined minimum sample-size criterion of 20 patients [15]. In analyses with sparse event counts or unstable variance-component estimation, model simplification, continuity correction, or fixed-effect specifications were applied as appropriate to improve model convergence and stability [16]. Furthermore, building upon the basic bivariate model, we incorporated "imaging modality" as a covariate to construct a meta-regression model. For lymph node staging, PSMA-PET/CT- and PSMA-PET/MRI-based cohorts were separated according to scanner platform. The primary pooled comparison against mpMRI was therefore based on PSMA-PET/CT cohorts. Because only three PSMA-PET/MRI studies were available, the pooled estimates for this subgroup were interpreted as exploratory. The likelihood ratio test was used to compare models with and without this covariate to examine whether differences in diagnostic performance between modalities were statistically significant [17]. Absolute differences in sensitivity and specificity between different imaging modalities were calculated, and their corresponding 95% CIs were estimated using the delta method to quantify the magnitude and precision of these differences. During model fitting, we also considered the potential influence of imaging modality on outcome heterogeneity and estimated variance components separately for each diagnostic test when necessary.

Coupled forest plots were generated in STATA and R

Between-study heterogeneity was evaluated using complementary approaches. Heterogeneity was examined visually by inspecting forest plots, SROC plots, and prediction regions. Where data and model convergence permitted, heterogeneity was quantified separately for sensitivity and specificity using endpoint-specific I2 values on the logit scale and between-study variance estimates from the bivariate model. Because conventional I2 statistics are less straightforward in bivariate diagnostic accuracy meta-analysis than in intervention meta-analysis, these values were interpreted as descriptive indicators of variability rather than as the sole basis for inference.

Exploratory subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses were conducted according to prespecified and clinically relevant sources of heterogeneity, including PSMA radioligand class, scanner platform, reference standard type, conventional comparator modality, time interval between PSMA-PET and conventional imaging, and study design. Radioligands were stratified into ⁶⁸Ga-labeled tracers, including ⁶⁸Ga-PSMA-11 and ⁶⁸Ga-PSMA-I&T, and 1⁸F-labeled tracers, including 1⁸F-PSMA-1007, 1⁸F-rhPSMA-7, and 1⁸F-DCFPyL. Reference standards were categorized as histopathology or composite reference standards, and conventional comparators were considered separately as mpMRI, CT, or BS according to the relevant staging domain. Imaging interval was dichotomized at one month (≤ 1 month versus > 1 month), and study design was categorized as prospective or retrospective. Subgroup pooling was performed only when a sufficient number of studies was available within a stratum; otherwise, results were summarized descriptively to avoid unstable estimates.

Due to the observed heterogeneity, assessment of publication bias using Deeks' test [17] was not performed, as this method has limited power in detecting funnel plot asymmetry in diagnostic test accuracy meta-analyses.

Data synthesis

Study selection

A total of 6,588 titles were retrieved. After deduplication and rigorous screening, 43 studies were ultimately deemed suitable for subsequent qualitative and quantitative analyses, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Characteristics of included studies

This study included 43 studies for systematic analysis, encompassing local tumor invasion (31 studies), lymph node staging (22 studies), and bone metastasis assessment (10 studies) in prostate cancer, with some studies covering multiple staging dimensions. All studies on local tumor staging used radical prostatectomy pathology as the reference standard; the majority of lymph node staging studies utilized extended pelvic lymph node dissection pathology as the reference, with a minority employing composite reference standards; all bone metastasis studies adopted composite reference standards, among which 2 included histopathological confirmation, 7 combined clinical, biochemical, and radiological findings, and 1 was based solely on follow-up imaging results. The PSMA-PET platform was predominantly PSMA-PET/CT, with a minority being PSMA-PET/MRI. Because the bone metastasis cohorts were not restricted exclusively to PSMA-PET/CT hardware, the M-staging comparison was reported as PSMA-PET versus BS. The tracers used included ⁶⁸Ga-PSMA-11 (31 studies), ⁶⁸Ga-PSMA-I&T (1 study), 1⁸F-PSMA-1007 (6 studies), 1⁸F-DCFPyL (3 studies), 1⁸F-rhPSMA-7 (1 study), and one study using both.1⁸F-PSMA-1007 and ⁶⁸Ga-PSMA-11 concomitantly. Methodological quality assessment: risk of bias and applicability

Supplementary materials (Supplementary Appendix 2 and Supplementary Table 8) summarize the QUADAS-2 and QUADAS-C assessment results, demonstrating variable methodological quality across studies with notable risk of bias. Potential risks were most commonly observed in patient selection and flow and timing domains, although information on index-test interpretation and reference-standard procedures was incomplete in some studies: retrospective studies predominantly failed to employ consecutive or random enrollment approaches, and exhibited deficiencies in test execution procedures and time intervals, thereby compromising the reliability of the results.

Assessment of local tumor invasion extent

Coupled forest plots (Figs. 2– 3) were generated for patient-level comparisons of PSMA-PET/CT versus mpMRI for extraprostatic extension (EPE; 11 studies) and seminal vesicle invasion (SVI; 10 studies); PSMA-PET/MRI versus mpMRI for EPE (6 studies) and SVI (5 studies) are presented in Supplementary Figs. 1–2.

Assessment of bone metastasis

Because the bone metastasis cohorts were not limited exclusively to PSMA-PET/CT hardware and included hybrid PSMA-PET examinations, the M-staging comparison is reported as PSMA-PET versus bone scintigraphy. Bivariate regression analysis demonstrated that PSMA-PET had higher sensitivity (0.958 [95.8%; 95% CI 0.928–0.987] versus 0.762 [76.2%; 95% CI 0.687–0.838]) and specificity (0.928 [92.8%; 95% CI 0.899–0.957] versus 0.774 [77.4%; 95% CI 0.713–0.835]) than bone scintigraphy, with both p-values < 0.001. Detailed visualizations are presented in Supplementary Figs. 5–6.

Heterogeneity assessment and subgroup analyses

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across staging domains and endpoints, particularly for local T-staging comparisons. Endpoint-specific heterogeneity was evaluated separately for sensitivity and specificity, with I2 values on the logit scale interpreted descriptively together with forest plots, SROC plots, prediction regions, and between-study variance estimates. For EPE detection, the I2 values for sensitivity/specificity were 87.1%/76.9% for PSMA-PET/CT and 83.6%/79.0% for mpMRI. For SVI detection, the corresponding values were 56.4%/83.7% for PSMA-PET/CT and 78.7%/69.0% for mpMRI. For the primary nodal comparison after separating PSMA-PET/MRI-based cohorts, I2 values for sensitivity/specificity were 67.6%/64.7% for PSMA-PET/CT and 70.8%/83.7% for mpMRI. These findings indicate moderate-to-marked endpoint-specific variability and support interpretation by staging domain and scanner platform rather than reliance on a single global heterogeneity measure. For bone metastasis staging, all 10 studies used composite reference standards; where stable endpoint-specific I2 quantification was not available, heterogeneity was assessed through forest-plot dispersion, SROC patterns, and prediction regions, with variability particularly evident in specificity estimates (Supplementary Table 7).

Subgroup analyses further explored radioligand class, scanner platform, imaging interval, study design, reference standard, and conventional comparator modality. For radioligand class, the available nodal-staging data showed numerically higher pooled PSMA-PET sensitivity for 1⁸F-labeled tracers (81.4%, 95% CI 68.9–89.6; 4 studies) than for ⁶⁸Ga-labeled tracers (61.0%, 95% CI 49.9–71.1; 15 studies), whereas no consistent tracer-class pattern was observed for local EPE or SVI analyses. Because no formal between-tracer interaction test was used as the basis for inference and tracer class was partially confounded by scanner platform, protocol, study design, patient population, and reference standard, these estimates should be interpreted as exploratory rather than as evidence of intrinsic superiority of one tracer class (Supplementary Table 5). For nodal staging, separation by scanner platform showed that PSMA-PET/CT retained higher sensitivity than mpMRI with comparable specificity, whereas the three PSMA-PET/MRI cohorts were summarized separately because of limited evidence (Supplementary Table 6). Analyses by imaging interval showed a sensitivity advantage for PSMA-PET over mpMRI in both the ≤ 1-month and > 1-month strata (Supplementary Fig. 7), while study-design stratification yielded similar comparative patterns in prospective and retrospective studies, with no evidence of effect modification by study design (Supplementary Tables 3–4). Reference-standard stratification was constrained by domain-specific use: all local T-staging studies used histopathology, nodal studies predominantly used pelvic lymph node dissection pathology with a minority using composite standards, and all bone-metastasis studies used composite reference standards. Comparator-specific analyses therefore remained domain based: PSMA-PET did not show uniform superiority over mpMRI for local T staging, PSMA-PET/CT was more sensitive than mpMRI for nodal staging, and PSMA-PET outperformed bone scintigraphy for bone metastasis staging. Collectively, these subgroup findings support a platform-, reference-standard-, and staging-domain-specific clinical interpretation rather than assuming a uniform diagnostic advantage of PSMA-PET across all settings (Supplementary Tables 2–7).

Discussion

This meta-analysis integrates one of the most comprehensive collections of direct comparative evidence currently available for initial prostate cancer staging. The principal clinical findings are that PSMA-PET/CT provides higher sensitivity than mpMRI for lymph node staging, PSMA-PET demonstrates higher diagnostic performance than bone scintigraphy for bone metastasis staging, and PSMA-PET does not show uniform superiority over mpMRI for local T staging. An exploratory six-study comparison found higher sensitivity with PSMA-PET/MRI than mpMRI for detecting extraprostatic extension. PSMA-PET also outperformed CT for lymph node staging in both sensitivity and specificity. To our knowledge, this represents the largest head-to-head meta-analysis in this field, incorporating 43 studies and 4,023 patients, and expands on previous reviews by evaluating multiple staging domains using direct paired comparisons. By applying histopathology or composite clinical reference standards and bivariate paired meta-analytic methods, this study provides a structured assessment of the relative diagnostic accuracy of PSMA-PET versus conventional imaging modalities, including mpMRI, CT, and bone scintigraphy, across local tumor invasion, lymph node, and bone metastasis staging.

Previous systematic reviews predominantly employed indirect comparison approaches or limited PSMA-PET versus conventional imaging modality (CIM) comparisons to single staging dimensions [11, 59, 60]. Such analyses frequently introduced bias due to inconsistencies in study populations, examination protocols, and reference standards. Guidelines for diagnostic accuracy reviews recommend that indirect comparison results be interpreted with caution [16]. In contrast, this review included only paired or head-to-head studies in which the same patients underwent PSMA-PET and at least one conventional imaging modality before treatment. This design reduces cross-study population bias and provides more directly comparable evidence regarding the relative diagnostic accuracy of PSMA-PET versus CIM across local tumor invasion, lymph node, and bone metastasis staging, although it does not imply that all imaging examinations were performed simultaneously or under identical technical conditions.

In local staging of PCa, the overarching comparison indicated that PSMA-PET did not demonstrate consistent superiority over mpMRI across both EPE and SVI. For EPE detection, the performance of PSMA-PET varied according to scanner platform: PSMA-PET/CT showed numerically lower but statistically non-significant sensitivity and specificity compared with mpMRI, whereas PSMA-PET/MRI demonstrated significantly higher sensitivity. For SVI assessment, no substantial difference was observed between PSMA-PET and mpMRI. These findings suggest that the advantage of PSMA-PET in local staging should not be generalized across all PSMA-PET configurations or all local invasion endpoints. The more favorable performance of PSMA-PET/MRI for EPE detection may reflect the complementary value of integrating molecular information from PSMA uptake with high-resolution pelvic mpMRI anatomy. In contrast, PSMA-PET/CT showed relatively modest performance for both EPE and SVI, consistent with the conclusions drawn by Kivikallio et al. [61]. A more appropriate explanation is that local T staging, particularly the assessment of capsular extension and seminal vesicle involvement, depends heavily on detailed soft-tissue delineation of the prostatic capsule, prostate base, and seminal vesicles. Compared with mpMRI, the CT component of PSMA-PET/CT provides limited soft-tissue anatomical contrast for defining these local boundaries, whereas mpMRI provides superior anatomical delineation of the prostate, prostatic capsule, and adjacent seminal vesicles [62]. Therefore, PSMA-PET/MRI may benefit from combining PSMA uptake information with high-resolution pelvic mpMRI anatomy.

For lymph node staging of newly diagnosed prostate cancer, PSMA-PET/MRI-based cohorts were separated from the primary pooled comparison to avoid methodological inconsistency caused by combining technically distinct PSMA-PET platforms. After excluding PSMA-PET/MRI-based cohorts, PSMA-PET/CT retained significantly higher sensitivity than mpMRI (absolute difference + 24.1 percentage points, 95% CI: 6.0 to 40.3; p = 0.009), whereas specificity remained comparable (absolute difference + 3.2 percentage points, 95% CI: −4.6 to 11.8; p = 0.41). This finding indicates that the sensitivity advantage of PSMA-PET for nodal staging was not driven solely by the inclusion of PSMA-PET/MRI-based cohorts. However, the comparable specificity suggests that mpMRI retains important value in accurately identifying metastasis-negative lymph nodes—a finding consistent with the recent report by Yang et al. [63]. The underlying mechanism for this comparable specificity performance lies in the fact that pathologically confirmed negative lymph nodes lack both significant tumor biological activity capturable by PSMA probes and structural destruction detectable by mpMRI, thus presenting negative findings under both imaging modalities and resulting in convergent efficacy in the diagnostic dimension of "correctly ruling out negatives." The three PSMA-PET/MRI-based cohorts were summarized separately, and the resulting pooled estimates should be interpreted as exploratory because only three studies were available. In contrast, PSMA-PET showed higher sensitivity and specificity than CT for lymph node staging, which aligns with current academic consensus [5].

In bone metastasis staging, the included evidence was therefore described using the modality-level term PSMA-PET rather than PSMA-PET/CT. PSMA-PET demonstrated higher diagnostic accuracy than conventional bone scintigraphy. Our quantitative analysis showed higher sensitivity (95.8% versus 76.2%, absolute difference + 19.6%) and specificity (92.8% versus 77.4%, absolute difference + 15.4%). This was associated with a lower false-positive rate (PSMA-PET: 7.2% versus bone scintigraphy: 22.6%). These findings suggest that PSMA-PET may reduce false-positive interpretation compared with bone scintigraphy and may better identify bone involvement beyond osteoblastic remodeling alone [64].

These findings have direct clinical implications for initial staging. For intermediate- to high-risk prostate cancer, PSMA-PET should be considered preferentially for nodal and distant metastatic staging when imaging results may alter treatment planning, because it showed higher sensitivity than mpMRI or CT for lymph node assessment and better diagnostic performance than bone scintigraphy for bone metastasis detection. In contrast, mpMRI should remain essential for local T staging and anatomical planning, as assessment of extraprostatic extension, seminal vesicle invasion, surgical margins, and radiotherapy targets depends on high-resolution pelvic soft-tissue delineation. Clinically, positive PSMA-PET findings may influence the extent of lymph node dissection, radiotherapy field design, nodal boost planning, or systemic treatment intensification. However, because PSMA-PET/CT sensitivity for nodal disease remains imperfect, a negative PSMA-PET result should not be used alone to omit PLND; this decision should be integrated with clinical risk, nomograms, PSA level, Gleason grade group, and multidisciplinary judgment.

Several design and analytic features should be considered when interpreting this review. First, the review followed PRISMA-DTA guidance and was prospectively registered. Second, eligibility was restricted to paired head-to-head diagnostic studies in which the same patients underwent PSMA-PET and at least one conventional imaging modality before treatment, thereby reducing bias from indirect cross-study comparisons. Third, study screening, data extraction, and quality assessment were performed independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer. Fourth, diagnostic accuracy was evaluated using histopathology or predefined composite clinical reference standards, and complete patient-level 2 × 2 data were extracted whenever available. Finally, pooled sensitivity and specificity were estimated using bivariate paired meta-analytic models, with imaging modality incorporated as a covariate and likelihood ratio tests used for formal comparisons between modalities. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses further examined the influence of scanner platform, tracer type, study design, reference standard, and time interval between imaging examinations.

This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, although primarily including intermediate- to high-risk patients, the admixture of a small number of low-risk patients may have exerted a dilution effect on the results. Second, due to insufficient stratified data on clinical parameters, we were unable to perform subgroup analyses according to prespecified risk groups and PSA levels. Third, the observed heterogeneity primarily stemmed from differences in design, population, and methodology among PSMA-PET/CT studies, which may affect the precision and generalizability of the results. Fourth, evidence for PSMA-PET/MRI was limited: the comparison with mpMRI for EPE included six studies, and only three PSMA-PET/MRI-based cohorts were available for lymph node staging. Accordingly, these pooled estimates should be interpreted as exploratory, and the small number of studies limits their stability and generalizability. Fifth, owing to the lack of lesion-level data, this analysis was restricted to the patient level, limiting in-depth exploration of lesion detection performance. Finally, the conclusions were predominantly based on [⁶⁸Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 and [1⁸F]F-PSMA-1007 tracers, with scant evidence for other ligands, restricting the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should employ consecutive or random enrollment, comprehensively report the time interval between PSMA-PET and conventional imaging, and provide PI-RADS quantitative details to enhance evidence quality. In addition, endpoint-specific I2 estimates were used descriptively because heterogeneity metrics are less straightforward in bivariate diagnostic accuracy meta-analysis, and several subgroup analyses were constrained by small numbers of studies within individual strata.

Conclusions

In this patient-level paired diagnostic accuracy meta-analysis of intermediate- to high-risk prostate cancer, PSMA-PET/CT was more sensitive than mpMRI for regional lymph node staging, while specificity was comparable. PSMA-PET had greater sensitivity and specificity than bone scintigraphy for bone metastasis staging. For local T staging, PSMA-PET did not show uniform superiority over mpMRI. These conclusions are limited to the evaluated patient-level endpoints and direct imaging comparisons.
