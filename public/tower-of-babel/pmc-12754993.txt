Mental health and beliefs about abortion access following changes to reproductive healthcare laws in 2022: findings from Oklahoma
Authors: Munjireen S Sifat, Laili K Boozary, Tina N Le, Julia McQuoid, Brittany Zaring-Hinkle, Sarah Tonkin, Lizbeth Benson, Bingjing Mao, Motolani Ogunsanya, Ruosi Shao, Amy M Cohn, Allyson Shortle, Darla E Kendzor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionA secondary analysis of cross-sectional survey data was conducted to characterize beliefs about abortion access in Oklahoma, measure stress associated with the overturn of Roe vs. Wade (2022), and evaluate the potential relationship between post-Roe stress (PR stress) and mental health.MethodsFrom August through September 2022, adult Oklahomans (N = 1793) were surveyed about their sociodemographic characteristics, beliefs about abortion access, frequency of past-month stress related to the overturn of Roe vs. Wade, and mental health. Beliefs about abortion access were characterized overall and within sociodemographic groups. The relationship between PR stress and mental health was evaluated, and inductive thematic analysis of open-ended responses regarding reasons for stress was conducted.ResultsParticipants identified as predominantly female (54.3%), 23.3% racially minoritized, 7.5% Hispanic, and 11.7% sexual and/or gender minoritized. Regarding socioeconomic status, 30.9% reported having ≤ high school education, and 67.9% reported an annual income < $60,000. Overall, the greatest proportions of participants believed that abortion should be legal with some exceptions (34.5%) or illegal with exceptions (32.8%), while smaller proportions believed that abortion should be legal in all cases (25.0%) or illegal in all cases (7.6%). PR stress was reported by the majority of participants (56.4%) and was associated with screening positive for Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-2 score > 3) and Major Depressive Disorder (Patient Health Questionnaire [PHQ-2] score > 3). The most common themes derived from participants’ reasons for PR stress included “loss of rights” (36.2%), “concerns about social equity” (13.9%), and “perceiving abortion as healthcare” (11.9%).ConclusionsA plurality of participants favored having access to abortion. PR stress was common and was associated with mental health concerns.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25623-7.Keywords: Stress, Reproductive rights, Anxiety, Depression, Abortion

Introduction

The June 2022 Dobbs v. Jackson decision overturned Roe v. Wade, allowing states to regulate abortion [1]. This decision triggered Oklahoma’s 1910 law [2] which bans most abortions [3, 4], with limited exceptions for life-saving procedures [4]. Although the Oklahoma Governor signed other bills into law to make abortion illegal following the detection of a heartbeat and to allow citizens to sue physicians who performed abortion, these laws were found to be unconstitutional by the Oklahoma supreme court [5].

Importantly, limits on abortion access can adversely impact patients with pregnancy complications and/or medical comorbidities who are seeking abortion as potentially lifesaving healthcare [6]. Stevenson et al. estimated a > 20% increase in pregnancy-related mortality after the first year following a total abortion ban in the U.S., with an even greater increase among non-Hispanic Black people [7]. Actual pregnancy-related deaths in states such as Texas have exceeded these projections [8]. International health and human rights organizations [9–11] and others [12] have argued that the right to abortion should be considered a fundamental human right, internationally, to ensure that therapeutic abortion and women’s health are universally protected and equally guaranteed in every country. The American Association of Obstetrics and Gynecology (ACOG) identifies abortion as “an essential component of comprehensive, evidence-based health care” and “strongly opposes any effort that impedes access to abortion care and interferes in the relationship between a person and their health care professional” [13].

Nevertheless, abortion restrictions are spreading despite public support for legal abortion access. Recent national surveys [14, 15] have indicated that > 60% of respondents believed that abortion should be legal in all or most cases, while < 40% believed it should be illegal in all or most cases. In Oklahoma, a 2023 survey indicated that 56% of residents supported abortion access in all or most cases, whereas only 12% opposed abortion in all cases [15]. Notably, beliefs about abortion legality vary across subgroups; for example, younger people, women, those who identify politically as a Democrat, people with greater education, certain racial groups (e.g., people identifying as Asian or Black) and those who are less religious tend to support greater abortion access [14, 16–18].

Previous research indicates that people denied access to abortion experience poorer mental health than those with access to abortion services [19, 20]. Studies have also shown that implementing state abortion restrictions is associated with worsening mental health in the broader population [21–23]. Likewise, living in a place with restricted access to abortion is associated with poorer mental health, at least among women [24, 25]. Restricted access to legal abortions has disproportionate impacts on the mental health of socioeconomically disadvantaged and other populations who may not have the resources to support a child or the ability to access out of state abortion services [21, 24, 26]. While no previous studies have examined the relationship between abortion access and mental health specifically in Oklahoma, Lee et al. [27] reported that females living in neighboring Texas showed a greater increase in mental distress following the passing of Texas Heartbeat Act Senate Bill 8 in 2021 (which prohibits physicians from performing abortions once a fetal heartbeat is detected) compared with males in Texas and females in several states that had not yet passed severe restrictions on abortion access. Increases in distress were especially pronounced among women of reproductive age (18–39 years).

The current study surveyed adult Oklahomans following the overturn of Roe v. Wade in 2022. The goals of the study were to characterize: 1) beliefs about abortion access, 2) self-reported stress following the overturn of Roe v. Wade (which we refer to as post-Roe [PR stress]), 3) the relationship between PR stress and mental health indicators, and 4) self-reported reasons for PR stress among adult Oklahomans. It was hypothesized that 1) PR stress and beliefs about abortion access would vary by sociodemographic group, and 2) PR stress would be associated with greater odds of mental health concerns. Findings will provide additional insight into the beliefs of Oklahomans regarding abortion access and evaluate the relationship between stress related to restricted access to abortion with mental health concerns.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedures

This ancillary study utilized data from a cross-sectional survey of Oklahomans from August to September 2022. A multi-methods approach [28, 29] was employed where closed-ended survey responses assessed the degree of participants’ stress related to the overturn of Roe v. Wade in the full sample. Participants who reported any stress related to the overturn of Roe v. Wade in their closed-ended responses were prompted to provide open-ended, write-in responses explaining their reasons for feeling stress. Qualitative themes regarding reasons for stress were synthesized overall and within sociodemographic subgroups.

Oklahoma residents (verified by zip code of residence) aged 18 or older who could read and respond to English surveys were recruited through a professional panel vendor and compensated according to the vendor's agreement (Cint) [30]. Although responses were not anonymous so that participants could receive incentives; participants were informed during the consenting process that their responses would be kept confidential and that the study had a certificate of confidentiality from the National Institutes of Health. Panel study methods and validation are described elsewhere [30]. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences (protocol #11994), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Measures

Sociodemographic information

Sociodemographic characteristics were assessed, including natal sex, sexual/gender identity, ethnicity, race, years of age, education, annual household income, political identity, partner status, and rural residence. Rurality of participants’ residence was determined based on zip codes, using the Rural–Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes (4 to 10 were considered urban) [31].

Mental health

Possible Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) was indicated by a 2-item GAD-2 score ≥ 3 [32]. Possible Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) was indicated by a 2-item Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) score of ≥ 3 [33]. Both measures have demonstrated reliability and validity [34].

Beliefs about abortion access

Beliefs concerning whether or not abortion should be legal were assessed using the following item adapted from the Pew Research Center [14]: “Abortion should be …”: 1 = Legal in all cases, no exceptions, 2 = Legal in all/most cases with exceptions, 3 = Illegal in all/most cases with exceptions, or 4 = Illegal in all cases, no exceptions.

Post-Roe (PR) stress

Participants were asked, “In the past month, how often has the Supreme Court decision to reverse Roe v. Wade (declaring that the constitutional right to abortion no longer exists) caused stress for you?” Answer choices included: 0 = Never, 1 = Almost never, 2 = Sometimes, 3 = Fairly often, 4 = Very often, 5 = I did not know about this decision. Participants who did not know about the decision were excluded from analyses (n = 151/2010, 7.5% of participants). Responses were dichotomized to any PR stress (1 = Almost never, Sometimes, Fairly often, Very often) vs. no PR stress (0 = Never). The “almost never” category was included in any PR stress because participants wrote meaningful responses when describing their stress in the open-ended follow-up question: “Please explain, to the best of your ability, why this decision was distressing for you.”

Quantitative and qualitative data analyses

Quantitative analyses

Complete case analysis was conducted using R/Rstudio (Version 4.3.0). Using the package tableone, [35] chi-square or Fisher tests (in cases with ≤ 10) examined differences in demographic variables by PR stress and by beliefs about abortion legality. Post-hoc tests were conducted for variables that had a significant family-wise test at α = 0.05. In post-hoc tests of beliefs about abortion legality, tests were conducted with Bonferroni corrections to account for Type I error from multiple tests (α = 0.083). Next, bidirectional stepwise logistic regression analyses were conducted to predict possible MDD and GAD as a function of PR stress, beliefs about abortion legality and the following sociodemographic variables: sex, sexual/gender minority (SGM) identity, ethnicity, race, age, education, income, political ideology, partner status, and rurality. The analytic model retained the following predictors for both models (PR stress, sex, SGM identity, age, and income); and beliefs about abortion legality was retained for the GAD model only.

Qualitative analyses

An inductive thematic team approach [36, 37] was employed to analyze the open-ended responses regarding reasons for PR stress. Our guiding research question was: “What explains participants’ stress-related reactions to the Roe vs. Wade decision, drawing from their own narrative responses?” Responses were coded in NVivo [38]. The team (MSS, TNL, and JM) consisted of one PhD-level faculty member with qualitative methods expertise and two postdoctoral fellows, all self-identified cisgender women with diverse racial, ethnic, and sexual identities and varying views on abortion legality. Of 1,002 open-ended responses, 858 were analyzed after removing meaningless entries (e.g., “non-applicable”). Responses ranged from 13 to 971 characters (average 83 characters). Open-ended responses were also grouped according to participants’ political ideology, sex, age, SGM identity, and race to compare the frequency of themes by participant characteristics, given known relationships between sociodemographic characteristics and beliefs about abortion access.

The team held weekly meetings via videoconference throughout the completion of analyses. We first conducted tandem readings of about one-quarter of responses, as we have done in prior studies [39–41]. This consisted of independently reviewing 10 responses at a time (noting patterns) and then discussing patterns as a group to create codes. Codes were adjusted until saturation was reached, with no new patterns emerging after five consecutive rounds. MSS revised the codebook, finalized it with the team, and organized it into an overarching taxonomy [42] of 12 parent codes (Table 4). MSS applied the final codebook to all responses; all relevant codes were applied to each response. TNL independently coded 26% of responses (n = 180) and discussed and reconciled coding discrepancies with MSS. JM was consulted as a tiebreaker. Frequencies of code application across responses are described in Table 4, along with the Kappa (ĸ range from 0.71–1.00) and percent agreement between the two independent coders (ranged from 93%—100%).

Results

Participant characteristics

The analytic samples ranged from 1790–1793 participants due to missing data. Participants were 54.3% female (n = 973), 23.3% racially minoritized (n = 418), 7.5% Hispanic (n = 135), 63.2% < 50 years of age (n = 1133), and 95.6% had at least a high school education (n = 1715). See Table 1. For comparison, according to the U.S. Census Bureau [43], Oklahomans overall were 50.3% female, 16.6% > 65 years of age; 72.9% White, 9.5% American Indian/Alaska Native, 7.9% Black, 6.8% multi-racial, and 2.6% Asian (27.1% racially minoritized); 12.9% Hispanic; 88.9% had at least a high school education; and the median income was $61,364. Overall, the study sample demographics were similar to the demographics of the state overall with several minor descriptive differences including a slightly higher proportion of participants with at least a high school education (95.6% vs. 88.9%), fewer Hispanic participants (7.5% vs. 12.9%), and more females (54.3% vs. 50.3%).

Qualitative findings

Table 4 displays the 12 themes that characterize the participants’ experiences of stress following the overturn of Roe v. Wade, along with coding frequency and detailed definitions for each theme. Because all relevant codes applied to each response, percentage totals may be greater than 100%. The top three most common themes were rights (36.2% of responses), followed by social equity (13.9% of responses) and abortion as healthcare and a life-saving measure (11.9% of responses). Below, we describe these common themes with supporting participant quotes and then compare themes by sociodemographic characteristics.

Rights

Responses regarding rights alluded to autonomy and sovereignty over one’s body: “We as women should be able to make choices about our own bodies”(7815). This included the idea that autonomy over one’s own body is a right bestowed by a higher power: “[I]t is taking away my God given right to do whatever I want with my own body” (6931). The notion of citizenship was mentioned, with the right to abortion as a component of U.S. citizenship: “I feel the U.S. government has no business on making moral personal decisions for its citizens,” (7971) as were concerns about criminalization resulting from having or aiding an abortion: “As a woman, it is absolutely terrifying to think that I don't have complete control over the outcome of my body. The government has control over my and my daughter’s uterus. Doctors are scared to perform necessary medical treatments on women for fear that they will lose [their] license. That has caused women fear to seek necessary medical treatment. Women have already been indicted on criminal charges due to miscarriages. That is terrifying. Absolutely terrifying.”(7177).

Social equity

The social equity theme encompassed justice and freedom from persecution, especially regarding gender, sexual orientation, and race: “As someone who is biologically female, trans, and living in an area where "corrective rape" is not unheard of, it definitely made me a lot more anxious thinking about things that might happen, and how limited my options would be if something did. And we all know that Roe v. Wade is just the beginning,” (7642).

Abortion as healthcare and a life-saving measure

This theme included the notion that abortion can be necessary to save lives. Some responses characterized abortion as undesirable but medically necessary and sometimes due to contraceptive failure: “I know so many people that contraceptives have failed them or mistakes happened and they ended up pregnant, not all of them were in positions to keep the babies and one found out her pregnancy was nonviable and was threatening her life, so she had an abortion. Under this ruling, my friend would have lost her life for something that never lived or had a chance to live at all.”(7744).

One quote from a participant illustrated all three themes: “Both of my pregnancies have been nearly fatal due to my weight and health issues and after having two children I got pregnant while using prevention and would have died before my third trimester if I didn’t get an abortion [healthcare and lifesaving measure]. Choosing between dying which would result in the death of the fetus and mean leaving my children without their mother or carrying out a pregnancy knowing the risks because I had no choice wasn't even a decision. Every woman should be able to choose what she wants and needs to do with her own body [rights]. Bodily autonomy is extremely important but is practically nonexistent for women [social equity]” (6586).

Qualitative findings by demographic subgroups

The three most common themes regarding reasons for stress (i.e., rights, social equity, abortion as healthcare and a lifesaving measure) following the overturn of Roe v. Wade were consistent across SGM identity, sex, and race, but differed slightly by political ideology (Fig. 1). Participants who self-identified as moderate, liberal, and very liberal most frequently explained their stress in alignment with the most common overall themes. Conservative-identifying participants similarly explained their stress as related to abortion being lifesaving and social equity; and related to having a personal connection to someone who needed an abortion. In contrast, those who self-identified as very conservative explained their stress as focused on sanctity of life, rights, and discomfort from debate. Differences were also observed by age. Reasons for PR stress among people over 55 years of age most commonly included rights, social equity, and barriers to safe healthcare, whereas those under 55 years most frequently reported rights, social equity, and abortion as healthcare and a lifesaving measure as key reasons for stress.

Discussion

This study characterized PR stress and beliefs about abortion access; and examined the relationship between PR stress and mental health among adult Oklahomans. While most participants (67.3%) held moderate views on abortion access (i.e., abortion should be legal or illegal with some exceptions), overall attitudes leaned towards favoring legal abortion access in all or most cases (59.5%). A majority of participants (56.4%) reported at least some PR stress, and PR stress was more common among females, SGM people, those without a partner, those living in urban areas, Hispanics, Asians, Native Hawaiians/Pacific Islanders, younger people (18–49 years), and people identifying as politically moderate or liberal. Reporting any PR stress was associated with greater odds of screening positive for possible GAD and MDD. Qualitative analyses revealed that the most common reasons for PR stress were concerns about loss of rights, social equity, and abortion as a healthcare issue.

Study findings can be understood in the context of the reproductive justice framework [44], which has been defined as the human right to personal bodily autonomy and the belief that people have the right to have children, the right to not have children, and the right to parent children in safe environments. Within this framework, reproductive autonomy is influenced by social and political factors which may have varying impacts across sociodemographic groups. For example, individuals with low socioeconomic status may experience a greater negative impact of abortion bans because they are less financially able to travel out-of-state to seek abortion services, and they may not have the necessary resources to support a child. More broadly, impediments to reproductive autonomy and adverse pregnancy outcomes can lead to increased stress and poor mental health. For example, individuals may experience stress and worry given the associations of abortion bans with increased pregnancy-related mortality, adverse birth outcomes, and poorer socioeconomic outcomes (e.g., financial instability, lower educational attainment).

Importantly, findings highlight a mismatch between beliefs about abortion access among surveyed Oklahomans and current abortion policy in Oklahoma, which restricts all abortions, with the only exception being as a lifesaving procedure for the mother. Most respondents were politically moderate (45%) or conservative (34.1%), with fewer identifying as liberal (20.4%). Thus, even in a sample of individuals who were predominantly moderate or conservative in their political identities, the majority believed that abortion should be legal in all or most cases (59.5%). Among the remainder who believed that abortion should be illegal (40.4%), most believed that there should be exceptions when abortion is allowable (32.8%). These study findings among Oklahomans align with other national [14, 15] and Oklahoma [15] state surveys indicating that most adults (e.g., 56–64%) believe abortion should be legal in all/most cases.

The discrepancies between state abortion laws and public preferences may be indirectly attributable to low voter turnout in Oklahoma [45]. Specifically, low voter turnout can lead to the election of legislators whose views do not align well with the preferences of the public. In turn, this can lead to misaligned laws and policies. Notably, certain groups have especially low voter turnout rates, including young adults [46] who tend to favor legal abortion [47]. Barriers to voting include many factors (including those of daily life) [48, 49], such as limited access to polling stations and inadequate voter education [50]. Plausibly, reducing barriers, educating the public, and increasing awareness of local elections may increase voter turnout, and ultimately increase alignment between Oklahoma legislators and preferred public policies. More broadly, less common viewpoints about abortion access can be amplified through structural factors including a lack of diversity among supreme court appointees (e.g., limited racial, gender, and viewpoint diversity) [51, 52], reproductive gerrymandering [53], low representation of women in state legislatures [54], and restrictive voting laws. [55]

Consistent with the reproductive justice framework [44], beliefs about abortion access vary by race and ethnicity and socioeconomic status. Findings from national surveys have indicated that Asian and Black people were most likely to believe that abortion should be legal in most or all cases, whereas Hispanic and White people have been least likely to believe that abortion should be legal in all or most cases [14, 15]. Findings from the current study of Oklahomans indicated that those who identified as multi-racial (76.5%), Hispanic (72.6%), and Black (72.0%) were most likely to believe that abortion should be legal in all or most cases, whereas those who identify as White (56.6%) were less likely to believe that abortion should be legal. Socioeconomic disadvantage may also impact beliefs about abortion and abortion access, given limited financial support for childcare and/or structural barriers that influence healthcare access. In both the U.S. overall and Oklahoma specifically, racially and ethnically minoritized groups are more likely to experience socioeconomic disparities [56, 57]. In addition, religious and other contextual differences may vary by race to influence abortion-related beliefs. [58, 59]

Most participants reported some PR stress, particularly females and younger people, who are disproportionately affected by abortion restrictions. Although PR stress was associated with possible GAD and MDD, the cross-sectional design limits inferences about causality. Nevertheless, the observed associations are consistent with possible pathways through which abortion restrictions may exert psychophysical effects. Plausibly, concerns about unintended pregnancy in a restrictive legal environment can lead to stress and worsen mental health. Alternatively, preexisting mental health issues may have heightened stress from the Roe v. Wade overturn. Longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate the longer-term impact of restrictive reproductive environments on mental health over time.

People with moderate, liberal, and very liberal political ideology reported higher rates of PR stress (63–86%) compared with conservative or very conservative respondents (25–36%). Qualitative analysis revealed that the most commonly reported reasons for PR stress were the same across moderate, liberal, and very liberal political ideologies, and focused on concerns about rights, social equity, and abortion as healthcare. Similarly, participants with conservative identity were most likely to report that PR stress was due to concerns about social equity, abortion as healthcare, and because of a personal connection to abortion. In contrast, among those with very conservative political ideology, PR stress was most commonly related to concerns about the sanctity of life, rights, and discomfort from the debate.

Finally, the reasons for PR stress differed by age. Compared with younger participants, older participants more frequently reported concerns about rights and were less likely to report concerns about abortion as healthcare. Differing themes among older people may have emerged because they are less directly affected by pregnancy and may be more concerned about the impact of abortion access on others.

Limitations

Although the study sample was generally representative of the state [43], the use of a panel recruitment platform may have introduced demographic and self-selection biases in the study sample relative to the general population of Oklahoma [30]. Study participants exhibited slightly higher educational attainment (95.6% with at least a high school education) compared to state census data (88.9%) [30, 43], which may reflect the digital literacy required for online survey participation. The sample also skewed toward lower income levels, with 67.9% earning less than $60,000 annually ($61,364 was the median income for Oklahoma [43]), potentially due to the incentive-driven nature of online panels. Hispanic individuals were underrepresented (7.5% vs. 12.9% in Oklahoma overall) [30, 43], possibly due to language barriers and disparities in digital access. Women were slightly overrepresented (54.3% vs. 50.3% in Oklahoma overall) [30, 43], consistent with broader trends in survey participation. Importantly, the non-random sample and cross-sectional design restrict causal inferences.

The brevity of open-ended responses limited in-depth analysis. While participants could express their thoughts in their own words, many responses were succinct, which constrained our ability to explore the complexity of participants’ emotional and cognitive reactions to the prompt. Furthermore, because responses were not elicited through interviews, we were unable to probe for clarification or elaboration, which may have resulted in the underrepresentation of more subtle or context-dependent themes. As such, while the thematic analysis provided valuable insights, the findings should be interpreted with caution given the limitations inherent in the data collection method. Notably, there are several key variables that may relate to beliefs about abortion access and PR stress which were not collected, including political party affiliation, voting history, and religiosity. Notably, the authors created the face valid PR stress items and thus there is no psychometric data to support the reliability or validity of the measure.

Finally, although the GAD-2 and PHQ-2 instruments are widely validated and efficient for screening, they do not provide a clinical diagnostic assessment, which may underestimate or oversimplify the complexity of mental health responses to abortion restrictions. Future research should incorporate comprehensive measures or structured clinical interviews to capture the full spectrum of psychological and somatic effects associated with restrictive abortion policy environments.

Public health implications

Future research is needed to prospectively examine the mental health impact of abortion bans, especially during the transition from legalized to restricted abortion access. Understanding the sociopolitical factors influencing abortion policy in Oklahoma can inform future policy initiatives. Ideally, the findings of this research will help to motivate efforts to address low voter turnout and structural factors that contribute to policies that are mismatched with the preferences of the public [14, 15] and misaligned with evidence-based health care practice [60, 61]. Finally, reproductive healthcare providers must be aware of the potential for mental health concerns among their patients in the context of restricted access to abortion services. Mental health referrals or counseling may be needed in reproductive health care settings. Ultimately, strategic political and policy advocacy will be needed to align reproductive health care policies with public preferences and evidence-based health care practice.
