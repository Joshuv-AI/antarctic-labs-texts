Hardwired differences: Sexually dimorphic neural connectomes in the brain-lung axis
Authors: Tao Wang, Fancheng Meng, Wenting Dong, Mengyang Jiang, Qian Xue, Yong Han, Hui Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySexual dimorphism in lung physiology and disease is well established; yet, the underlying central circuits remain unexplored. Using retrograde trans-synaptic tracing with pseudorabies virus from the lung combined with whole-brain mapping, we uncovered fundamental sex differences in brain-lung connectivity. Male mice exhibited anterior, cognition-associated networks clustering and hemispheric lateralization, whereas female mice displayed posterior, brainstem-predominant circuits specialized for automatic respiratory control, along with more integrated and modular network architectures. These findings reveal a hardwired neural framework for sex-specific respiratory control, with implications for understanding differential susceptibility to respiratory disease between males and females.Subject areas: biological sciences

Introduction

Normal pulmonary function is essential for survival and is regulated by the brain-lung axis, defined as neural circuits connecting the central nervous system (CNS) to the lungs. These pathways modulate respiration, inflammation, and microbiome signaling via hierarchical sympathetic and parasympathetic efferents along with sensory feedback mechanisms.1,2 The brainstem, particularly the medulla, forms the core respiratory center, dynamically adjusting breathing patterns in response to metabolic demand. Sensory input from peripheral neurons such as those in the vagus nerve checks the condition of the airway. This information is relayed to the CNS to maintain stable breathing and trigger protective reflexes including coughing, which play critical roles in pneumonia, neurogenic pulmonary edema, and acute respiratory distress syndrome.3,4,5,6 The cerebellum further acts as an integration hub, coordinating brainstem pattern generators to synchronize respiration with cardiovascular function, emotion, and motor activity, thereby preventing respiratory-postural conflict.7 This integrated mechanism enables dynamic regulation of tidal volume and expiratory timing in response to internal or external stimuli.8

Substantial evidence indicates that lung physiology and respiratory disease susceptibility differ significantly between males and females. These differences originate during embryonic development and persist across the lifespan.9,10,11 For example, post-pubertal female mice exhibit higher incidence of asthma,12 chronic obstructive pulmonary disease10 and lung adenocarcinoma13 due to both sex hormones and chromosomal factors.11,14 Nevertheless, the central neural mechanisms underlying these disparities have not been directly compared between males and females, due to technical limitations and a lack of sex-dimorphic models.15 Although pseudorabies virus (PRV) tracing has been widely used to map peripheral-CNS pathways, most studies have focused exclusively on male mice, and female-specific circuitry has not been systematically examined.16,17

In this study, we combined PRV tracing via tracheal intubation with whole-brain imaging and computational modeling to map the brain-lung connectome in both sexes. This study aimed to compare the spatiotemporal progression of PRV labeling in the lung-efferent brain regions of male and female mice, identify sex-specific hub regions in the brain-lung axes using graph theory, and characterize the hemispheric lateralization and modular organization of lung-related neural connectomes. Our results reveal a pronounced sexual dimorphism in the distribution, connectivity, and functional topology of pulmonary regulatory brain networks.

Results

Experimental designs and workflow

To systematically map central neural circuits innervating the lungs, we administered retrograde transsynaptic PRV into the bilateral pulmonary tissue via tracheal intubation. While previous reports indicate that neurological signs typically onset in PRV-infected mice within approximately 5 days post-inoculation (dpi),18,19 mortality occurred at approximately 5.5 days under our experimental conditions. To maximize transneuronal viral dissemination, mice were perfused during the agonal phase. Additional perfusion time points were included to capture spatiotemporal infection dynamics and enable subsequent analysis of potential loop projections (Figures 1A and 1B). Serial coronal brain sections were imaged by confocal microscopy and processed through automated signal detection, registration, and segmentation pipelines. This workflow enabled whole-brain reconstruction and quantification of PRV-positive neurons across anatomical subregions (Figures 1C–1E). Functional neural networks were constructed and analyzed within a graph-theoretical framework (Figure 1F). Due to the virus toxicity and the uncertainty in the time of death of the mice, at least three mice that met the criteria were included at each time point.

Sexual dimorphisms in the brain-lung axis

Although sexual dimorphism in lung physiology is well-documented, its manifestation within central neural circuits remains poorly characterized. To address this gap, we systematically compared the spatial distribution of lung-projecting PRV-positive neurons in the brain between male and female mice (Figures 2A, 3A, and S1, Data S1). Flow cytometric analysis was conducted on day 2 post-PRV infection to assess enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) intensity in lung tissues. This analysis revealed sex-comparable infection patterns following intratracheal delivery (Figure 2B, Data S1), indicating uniform viral targeting of the initial cell populations within the organ. This consistency ensured uniform viral targeting of initiation cells within the organ. Animals were perfused at the predefined endpoint of 5.5 dpi upon manifestation of severe infection symptoms. The infected brain regions were annotated according to the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas (Figures 2C, 2D and 3B–3D, Data S1). PRV-positive cells were predominantly distributed across six major brain regions: cerebral cortex (CTX), cerebral nuclei (CNU), thalamus (TH), hypothalamus (HY), pons (P), and medulla (MY). Male mice exhibited anterior-dominant labeling (CTX, CNU, TH, and HY), whereas female mice showed posterior-dominant enrichment (P, MY) (Figure 2E). Subsequently, a comprehensive quantitative analysis of PRV-positive cells across brain subregions was performed based on one representative male mouse (Figure 3E). We identified approximately 30 PRV-positive subregions in total, with 27 detected in male mice and 23 in female mice. Among these, ten subregions were commonly infected bilaterally in males and females. Notably, infection bias differed substantially between sexes: 17 subregions displayed male-biased infection, compared to 13 with female-biased infection, indicating divergent connectivity patterns. Furthermore, the lateral hypothalamic area (LHA) displayed notable inter-sex asymmetry (Figure 2F), underscoring the sexually dimorphic organization of lung-related central circuits.

Modularity and connectivity differences in lung-innervating neural connections between male and female mice

To characterize the functional network architecture underlying lung innervation, we employed graph theory to identify critical hub regions within the brain-wide connectome.20,21 Interregional correlation analysis based on PRV-positive cell counts revealed significantly stronger functional connectivity in female mice than in male mice (Figures 4A–4C), indicating enhanced network integration in female mice.

Hierarchical clustering demonstrated fundamental sex differences in modular organization, with male mouse brains segregating into two discrete modules and female mouse brains partitioning into four distinct clusters (Figures 4D–4F). In male mice, cluster 1 primarily comprised anterior regions (the CTX and the HY), while cluster 2 encompassed CNU, TH, and P regions (Figure 4D, Data S2), reflecting anatomical aggregation. Cluster 1 exhibited stronger internal correlations (Figure 4G), consistent with elevated PRV labeling in the CTX and the HY observed via fMOST (Figure 3). Female mouse networks showed more distributed organization, with forebrain regions (the CNU, the TH, and the HY) concentrated in cluster 1, while posterior areas (the P and the MY) were distributed across multiple clusters (Figure 4E). Cluster 1 maintained the highest mean correlation strength among female mice modules (Figure 4H).

These findings reveal that while PRV-infected regions in the brain form anatomically coherent clusters in both sexes, female mice exhibit more refined modular parcellation alongside stronger interregional synchronization. This integrated network architecture suggests enhanced functional coordination in female mouse brain-lung regulatory system, whereas male mouse networks display more segregated organizational principles.

Spatiotemporal dynamics and sex-specific trajectories of the brain-lung connectome revealed by transsynaptic tracing

The brain-lung axis comprises a polysynaptic circuitry-termed the brain-lung connectome that coordinates pulmonary function through central neural pathways.22,23 To delineate this connectome, we performed PRV-based transsynaptic tracing to map multisynaptic circuits linking the CNS to the pulmonary system. Temporal analysis of viral propagation revealed hierarchical connectivity patterns, with PRV-positive neurons increasing progressively across infection time points (Figure 1A). No cerebral labeling was detected within 1–3 days post-inoculation, indicating delayed transneuronal passage beyond spinal circuits. By day 4, PRV-positive neurons emerged in key brainstem nuclei, including the parabrachial nucleus (PB), the facial motor nucleus (FMN), and the intermediate reticular nucleus (IRN) in male mice, alongside the vestibular nuclei (VNC) in both sexes (Figures 5A and 5B). These regions constitute core components of the respiratory-motor integration system, with established roles in respiratory rhythm generation and modulation.24,25,26,27,28 While the PB, the IRN, and the FMN directly influence respiratory patterning, the VNC likely modulates breathing through shared brainstem networks including the reticular formation.24,28 In male mice, viral labeling extended to the MY, the P, and the HY by day 4.5, confirming brainstem dominance in pulmonary innervation. By day 5, infection involved >20 subregions across five major brain divisions (excluding the CTX), and by day 5.5, all six major regions showed labeling, though earlier-infected areas exhibited a decreased intensity, suggesting PRV-induced neuronal compromise.

Hierarchical clustering revealed progressive simplification of network modularity over time, with the minimal module count (n = 2) attained at 5.5 dpi coinciding with PRV labeling stabilization (Figure 5C). Based on correlation and clustering results, we constructed a neural network to predict critical hub regions defined by their intramodule and intermodule connectivity.20,21 In line with previous methodologies,21 a Pearson’s correlation coefficient threshold of ≥0.82 was applied to define significant network connections. Given the observed modular connectivity differences across infected brain regions in mice, we focused on intramodule connectivity to identify hub regions. We computed three graph-theoretical centrality metrics for all brain regions within each network: degree (number of links connected to a node), normalized betweenness centrality (the fraction of all shortest paths passing through a node), and eigenvector centrality (a measure of a node’s influence based on the connectivity of its neighbors) (Data S2). Brain regions ranking highly across all three metrics were identified as hub nodes.

Analysis of the top 10% PRV-infected regions revealed dynamically shifting hub distributions in male mice across the infection time course (Figure 5F). At 4.5 dpi, hub regions included the parvicellular reticular nucleus and the paragigantocellular reticular nucleus in the MY, along with the pontine reticular nucleus, caudal part (PRNc) and the supratrigeminal nucleus (SUT) in the P. By day 5, hub organization shifted to the IRN in the MY and the subthalamic nucleus (STN) in the HY. At the terminal phase (5.5 dpi), key hubs comprised the pontine central gray (PCG) in the P, globus pallidus internal segment (GPi) in the CNU, and visceral area in the CTX. This spatiotemporal progression followed a consistent trajectory: MY-P, MY-HY, and P-CNU-CTX (Figure 6A). Notably, these hubs were distributed across distinct functional clusters rather than forming a single anatomical group. In male mice, circuits were organized in a step-by-step order from the brainstem to the forebrain. Then they support a top-down control system for lung function, and different brain levels are used in a timed sequence.

Possible brain-lung axes in male and female mice

(A and B) The top-down process of the brain-lung axis through which the brain regulates lung activity in male (A) and female (B) mice. The different colored circles denoted the top 10% hub brain regions at different days of PRV infection.

Female mice exhibited significantly more constrained viral propagation patterns compared to the males (Figure 5B). Initial infection at 4.5 dpi primarily involved brainstem regions (MY, P, and HY), mirroring male mice distribution at this early timepoint and suggesting conserved direct pulmonary innervation pathways. By day 5, labeling expanded to include the CNU, and by 5.5 dpi, it involved five major brain regions (excluding the CTX). Throughout the time course, female mouse brains demonstrated minimal spatial expansion of infected areas, indicating a compact, stable network architecture consistent with their stronger interregional connectivity observed previously (Figure 4B). This anatomical conservation was further supported by cluster analysis, which revealed minimal modular reorganization (Figure 5D) and only a marginal reduction in cluster numbers over time (Figure 5E).

Across all time points, female mice demonstrated a spatially stable and anatomically conserved hub organization, which contrasted sharply with the progressively dispersed pattern observed in the males (Figure 5G). On day 4.5, the identified hub regions included the nucleus raphe magnus (RM) and the parapyramidal nucleus in the MY, as well as the PB in the P. By day 5, hubs comprised the IRN in the MY, the SUT in the P, and the substantia innominata in the CNU. On day 5.5, key hubs included the VNC in the MY, the motor nucleus of the trigeminal nerve (Vmo) and the PRNc in the P, the zona incerta (ZI), the parasubthalamic nucleus in the HY and the CEA in the CNU. Analysis of trajectory shifts revealed that female mice, similarly to males, displayed a continuous hierarchical descending circuit: MY-P, MY-P-CNU, and MY-P-HY-CNU (Figure 6B). Unlike the distributed male mouse hubs, female mouse hubs predominantly clustered within similar functional modules, aligning with their enhanced interregional correlations. These findings indicate that although both sexes employ descending hierarchical circuits for pulmonary control, female mice maintain tighter functional integration within a more compact network architecture.

Discussion

In this study, we constructed a comprehensive brain-lung connectome and performed a multi-faceted analysis of the neural circuits regulating pulmonary function. We systematically delineated the descending neural pathways from the brain to the lung. In contrast to previous studies,29,30,31 our approach emphasized whole-brain projections, including bilateral hemispheric comparison, exploration of sexual dimorphisms, and identification of potential hub regions and their innervation patterns. Our tracing results encompassed over 50 brain regions, revealing moderate sexual differences in the distribution of lung-projecting neurons and limited left-right asymmetry. Furthermore, using graph-theory approaches, we identified distinct projection patterns between male and female mice.

Advancements in techniques such as functional magnetic resonance imaging, optogenetics, and chemogenetics have significantly accelerated the exploration of the brain-lung axis by enabling precise circuit-level investigations.27 Brain regulation of pulmonary function is primarily mediated through the cortical-brainstem-medullary respiratory pathway, with additional involvement of indirect modulation by forebrain and thalamic regions.23,32 Our comprehensive mapping reveals a broadly consistent distribution of PRV labeling across these canonical pathways, while further identifying pronounced sex-specific divergences. The LHA, a region established as central to energy balance and motivated behaviors,33,34,35 exhibits notable sexual dimorphism in structure and function.36,37 A higher density of PRV-positive neurons was shown in the LHA among male mice, suggesting its potential role in generating sex-specific respiratory patterns. A hypothesis requires future mechanistic validation.

The mammalian brain exhibits inherent anatomical and functional asymmetry, a dynamic network property often overlooked in preclinical research dynamic network property often overlooked in preclinical research.38,39 Specific brain regions can modulate pulmonary inflammation via neuroimmune pathways, for instance, manipulation of the anterolateral motor cortex can attenuate acute lung injury.40 We identified a right-hemispheric dominance in the AI and the CEA of male mice, a lateralization absent in female mice. This finding underscores that the development of respiratory-related neural circuits may be governed by sex-dependent mechanisms, which may contribute to differential neuroinflammatory responses.

Utilizing graph theory analysis, we identified sex-specific differences in the top 10% of hub connections. While central nodes in both sexes were concentrated in brainstem structures (the TH, the HY, the P, and the MY), the male mice exhibited a greater proportion of cortical (the CTX) hubs. This aligns with the established role of corticobulbar circuits in integrating autonomic, neuroendocrine, and rhythmic signals for bidirectional brain-lung communication,41 and highlights the need to investigate these circuits to explain sex-based differences in pulmonary disorders. Temporal analysis confirmed that brain-lung control operateed via a descending hierarchical circuit. In male mice, early-infected hub regions like the PB are positioned for interoceptive integration and neuroimmune modulation,42 while other hubs like the STN and the PCG process visceral signals and regulate respiratory rhythm.43,44 In female mice, hub regions such as the RM and the ZI are embedded within networks that concurrently support pulmonary homeostasis and integrate emotional or sensory processing via serotonergic or GABAergic pathways.45,46,47 The distinct neurophysiological organization, characterized by discrete modulation in male mice and integrated processing in female mice, may account for the higher prevalence of emotional comorbidities in female patients with pulmonary diseases.9,10,15,48 Further studies into the mediating role of sex hormones among these diseases need investigating.

In summary, our study provides a comprehensive wiring diagram of the brain-lung control network, encompassing descending neural pathways, hemispheric lateralization, sexual dimorphisms, and temporal dynamics. These findings establish a structural and functional framework for understanding the brain-mediated mechanisms underlying pulmonary physiology and sex-specific disease vulnerabilities, paving the way for future targeted investigations into neuro-respiratory disorders.

Limitations of the study

Our study should be considered in light of certain methodological and biological constraints. First, potential PRV cytotoxicity over extended tracing periods may influence the integrity of the mapped functional connectome, particularly at later time points.49,50 Second, this study used sexually mature young adult mice (6–8 weeks old). While this model captures the post-pubertal phase where sex differences in pulmonary diseases become apparent, the brain-lung connectome may further evolve with full somatic maturity (≥12 weeks).51,52 Mechanistic insights suggest that more mature mice exhibit a stabilized microbiome with potentially stronger immunomodulatory effects via the microbiota-brain-gut-lung axis,53,54 a shift toward chronic neuropathological interactions mediated by factors like hypoxia55,56,57 and distinct molecular profiles marked by increased senescence versus acute apoptotic responses.58 Thus, the potential dynamic nature of the brain-lung circuitry across the lifespan remains an open question and a valuable direction for future research.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Male and female C57BL/6J mice (wild-type genotype) were obtained from the specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal facility of the Fourth Military Medical University (FMMU). Mice were 6–8 weeks old (young adult stage) and weighed 18–20 g at the start of experiments. Both sexes were included as a biological variable, and male and female mice were directly compared throughout the study. Animals were group-housed (5–6 per cage) under a 12/12-h light/dark cycle at a controlled temperature of 24°C. Food and water were available ad libitum. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the “Principles of Medical Laboratory Animal Care” (National Ministry of Health, China) and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of FMMU (IACUC20230150). Every effort was made to minimize animal suffering and to use the minimum number of animals required for robust statistical analysis.

Influence of sex on study results: The primary objective of this study was to investigate sexual dimorphism in the brain-lung connectome. Our results revealed pronounced sex differences in the distribution, connectivity, and functional topology of pulmonary regulatory brain networks (see results and discussion). Therefore, sex is a major influencing factor on the outcomes of this study. No cell lines or human subjects/samples were used in this work; hence, statements regarding cell line authentication, mycoplasma testing, or human sample allocation are not applicable.

Method details

PRV inhalation through a tracheal intubation

The recombinant PRV strain PRV-CAG-EGFP (BrainVTA, Cat# P03001; titer 5.0 × 109 PFU/mL) was diluted 1:40 in sterile saline to obtain the working PRV dilution (final titer: 1.25 × 108 PFU/mL).59 Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital at a dose of 10 μL/g based on body weight. They were then positioned in a stereotaxic holder with the head elevated and jaw extended. Under transillumination-guided visualization of the neck region, the oral cavity was gently retracted to expose the trachea. With direct visual access to the glottis, a 24-gauge intravenous catheter needle (Jiangxi Fenglin Medical Appliance Co. Ltd., model IV-Y24II) with its stylet removed was used as a tracheal cannula. The cannula was then advanced approximately 3 mm into the trachea along the anterior tracheal wall.59 A 50-μL microsyringe (Shanghai Gaoge) was used to aspirate 50 μL of the PRV dilution. The syringe needle was then slowly inserted into the indwelling tracheal cannula. The virus was injected slowly and steadily via the intratracheal route. Throughout the injection, the mouse’s respiratory rate and depth were continuously monitored. The infusion speed was adjusted dynamically based on respiratory status to prevent gagging or respiratory distress. Following the procedure, the animal was placed on a 37°C heating pad until full recovery of ambulation.

Tissue preparation

Under deep anesthesia (1% pentobarbital) mice were transcardially perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, 40 mL, pH 7.4). Brains and lungs were carefully dissected and post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4°C overnight. After cryoprotection in 30% sucrose, tissues were sectioned on a freezing microtome (Leica VT-1000): brains into 35 μm coronal sections and lungs into 10 μm sections. For complete and anatomically aligned sampling, coronal brain sections were collected in 4 series (140 μm intervals; 50 ± 3 sections/series). The first series was imaged for all mice. Systematic series of whole-brain sections (sampled at 140 μm intervals) were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Abcam, ab228551; 1:1000) and initially scanned using a fully automated slide scanner. Selected regions were then imaged at higher resolution with an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope.

Quantification of EGFP in lung tissue via flow cytometry

The study comprised four groups: saline-treated controls and PRV-infected mice, each consisting of six female and six male animals. The infected groups received 50 μL of PRV inoculum (1.25 × 108 PFU/mL) via intratracheal delivery. Following perfusion, lung tissues were collected, digested (Xinchao Pengpai, XCPP-TD-100A), and subjected to RBC lysis (Tiangen, RT122-02) to prepare single-cell suspensions from different lung regions. EGFP expression was analyzed by flow cytometry on a BD LSRFortessa instrument. Target cell populations were gated to exclude debris and doublets, and the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was quantified to assess relative EGFP levels. Statistical analysis was performed using two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test; data were presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was denoted as ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001 and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001 for the specific comparisons indicated above.

Quantification of PRV-positive cells

To mitigate bias associated with manual counting, we employed a fully automated, high-throughput pipeline for cell quantification. This pipeline, executed on whole-slide imaging data, encompassed signal detection, image registration to the Allen CCFv3 atlas via the Brain Registration, Identification, and Encoding Framework, and subsequent CNN-based 3D cell detection, morphological classification, and spatial annotation of PRV+ neurons across the entire brain (Panolyzerpro, Meca Scientific).60

Fluorescence micro-optical sectioning tomography (fMOST)

At 5.5 days post-inoculation, a male PRV-infected mouse was transcardially perfused, and the brain was harvested and fixed in 4% PFA at 4°C for 24 h. The fixed brain was embedded in 3–5% agarose and allowed to polymerize for 8–10 min fMOST-compatible sample preparation followed established protocols.61 The imaging system provided a theoretical resolution of ∼0.6 μm laterally and ∼3.8 μm axially. Prior to imaging, whole-brain specimens were mounted in a 3D translation stage immersed in 0.01 M PBS to enhance EGFP signal stability and maintain refractive index uniformity. Tomographic acquisition was performed at 35 μm thickness using a BioMapping 9,000 system (Oebio Biological) equipped with a 20× objective (Oebio Biological Co., Ltd, Wuhan, China), equipped with a 20× objective (Olympus LUMPLFLN, NA 0.5), 473/561 nm lasers, and an sCMOS camera (Hamamatsu ORCA-Flash 4.0). The imaging cycle consisted of four stages: (1) vibratome sectioning at 77 Hz (0.2 mm/s feed), (2) stage repositioning, (3) light-sheet acquisition, and (4) cutting plane reset, ensuring that each imaged optical plane corresponded precisely to the physical sectioning depth.

Raw fMOST data underwent illumination correction and multi-tile stitching. Images were downsampled and registered to the Allen CCFv3 template via the Brain Registration, Identification, and Encoding Framework. A CNN-based classification pipeline enabled automated 3D cell detection, morphological classification, and spatial annotation of PRV+ neurons across brain regions.

Graph theory and hub network identification

Functional networks of PRV-infected brain regions were characterized using graph theory approaches to identify hub regions.20,21 Regional connectivity was quantified using three standard network metrics: degree (number of connections per node), normalized betweenness centrality (proportion of shortest paths traversing a node), and eigenvector centrality (influence weighted by connectedness of neighbors). Hub nodes were defined as regions ranking in the top tier across all three metrics, computed via the Brain Connectivity Toolbox (https://sites.google.com/site/bctnet/).

Functional networks were visualized in Cytoscape,62 with nodes representing brain regions, edges reflecting correlation coefficients, and module assignment informed by hierarchical clustering.

Correlation analysis

Prior to analysis, raw PRV-positive cell counts were log10 transformed to reduce variance. Interregional Pearson correlation analysis was then performed to assess connectivity patterns between all pairs of brain regions. For each group, correlation coefficients (R-values) were calculated as the mean pairwise correlation across animals. Functional networks were constructed by thresholding the group-averaged correlation matrix at R ≥ 0.82,21 with retained connections defining the edges of the brain network.

Hierarchical clustering

Functional modules were identified through hierarchical clustering based on interregional co-activation profiles,20,63 Pairwise Euclidean distances were computed from Pearson correlation matrices, and dendrograms were generated to delineate modules. A splitting threshold was set to 50% of the maximum tree height was applied to partition regions into distinct clusters.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SEM. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (v21.0). Normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Parametric data were analyzed using two-tailed unpaired t-tests or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc comparisons. Non-parametric datasets were evaluated with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Kruskal–Wallis test, or Dunn’s test, as appropriate. Differences were considered statistically significant when p values were represented as follows: ∗< 0.05, ∗∗< 0.01, ∗∗∗< 0.001, ∗∗∗∗< 0.0001.
