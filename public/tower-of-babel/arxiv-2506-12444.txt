Adjusted Shuffling SARAH: Advancing Complexity Analysis via Dynamic Gradient Weighting
Duc Toan Nguyen, Trang H. Tran, Lam M. Nguyen

Abstract
In this paper, we propose Adjusted Shuffling SARAH, a novel algorithm that integrates shuffling techniques with the well-known variance-reduced algorithm SARAH while dynamically adjusting the stochastic gradient weights in each update to enhance exploration. Our method achieves the best-known gradient complexity for shuffling variance reduction methods in a strongly convex setting. This result applies to any shuffling technique, which narrows the gap in the complexity analysis of variance reduction methods between uniform sampling and shuffling data. Furthermore, we introduce Inexact Adjusted Reshuffling SARAH, an inexact variant of Adjusted Shuffling SARAH that eliminates the need for full-batch gradient computations. This algorithm retains the same linear convergence rate as Adjusted Shuffling SARAH while showing an advantage in total complexity when the sample size is very large.

Introduction

In this paper, we investigate the following finite sum minimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{P(w)\coloneq\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(w)\right\},

(1)

where each
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is smooth, for
i\in[n]:=\{1,\cdots,n\}
. Problem (
1
) appears in many convex and nonconvex problems in machine learning, such as logistic regression
(
Cox 1958
)
, multi-kernel learning
(
Bach et al. 2004
)
, conditional random fields
(
Lafferty et al. 2001
)
, and neural networks
(
Hastie 2009
)
. For example, given a training set
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}^{n}_{i=1}
with
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},y_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
, we formalize the
l_{2}-
regularized logistic regression for the binary classification problem as (
1
) with each individual function
f_{i}(w):=\log(1+\exp(-y_{i}x_{i}^{T}w))+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert w\rVert^{2}
.

One of the most popular methods for solving (
1
) is gradient descent (GD). At each iteration, GD updates the variable
w_{t}
in the opposite direction of the gradient
\nabla P(w_{t})
as

w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\eta\nabla P(w_{t}),

where
\eta
is the learning rate that determines the size of each update step. In practice, the dimension of the problem
d
and the number of components
n
in (
1
) are usually very large, which makes computing full gradients very expensive. Thus, deterministic optimization methods, which require exact gradient computations like GD, are inefficient in solving the problem (
1
)
(
Bottou et al. 2018
;
Sra et al. 2011
)
. Alternatively, stochastic gradient descent (SGD)
(
Robbins and Monro 1951
)
becomes a widely used method for solving (
1
). At each iteration, SGD chooses an index
i\in[n]
uniformly at random and updates the new element as

w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\eta\nabla f_{i}(w_{t}).

Intuitively, the component gradient
\nabla f_{i}(w_{t})
is a stochastic approximation for the full gradient
\nabla P(w_{t})
in GD. Another version of SGD called mini-batch gradient descent
(
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2007
;
Cotter et al. 2011
;
Takác et al. 2013
)
replaces the stochastic gradient with
\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}\nabla f_{i}(w_{s}),
with
S\subset[n]
. This method combines multiple component gradients
\nabla f_{i}(w_{t})
to better approximate
\nabla P(w_{t})
.

Variance reduction.
Despite the inexpensive computation for a sampled gradient at one iteration, SGD requires more iterations to approach the optimal point than GD. This is because the variance of the individual gradient
\nabla f_{i}(w_{t})
is usually high.
Therefore, many variance reduction methods emerge to address this problem, where examples are SAG/SAGA
(
Roux et al. 2012
;
Defazio et al. 2014
;
Schmidt et al. 2017
;
Qian et al. 2019
)
, SVRG
(
Johnson and Zhang 2013
;
Allen-Zhu and Yuan 2016
)
, SDCA
(
Shalev-Shwartz and Zhang 2013
)
, MISO
(
Mairal 2015
)
, SARAH
(
Nguyen et al. 2017
;
Nguyen et al. 2021a
;
Nguyen et al. 2022
;
Pham et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2020
)
, SPIDER
(
Fang et al. 2018
)
, and STORM
(
Cutkosky and Orabona 2019
)
.
Among these methods, perhaps the most popular algorithm is SVRG
(
Johnson and Zhang 2013
)
.
SVRG has a double-loop structure where it updates the weights as

w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\eta v_{t},

and the estimator
v_{t}
is computed using the true gradient and two stochastic gradients:

v_{t}=\nabla f_{i_{t}}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{i_{t}}(\tilde{w}_{k})+\nabla P(\tilde{w}_{k}),

where
i_{t}
is drawn randomly from
[n]
,
\tilde{w}_{k}
is the starting weight inside the loop and
\eta
is the learning rate. Hence,
v_{t}
is an unbiased stochastic gradient estimator of
\nabla P(\tilde{w}_{k})
. Another popular method applying variance reduction is SARAH
(
Nguyen et al. 2017
)
, which also utilizes the double-loop structure and weight update. But unlike the SVRG, the estimator
v_{t}
in SARAH is computed recursively:

v_{t}=\nabla f_{i_{t}}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{i_{t}}(w_{t-1})+v_{t-1},

where the initial estimator
v_{0}=\nabla P(\tilde{w}_{k})
.

In addition, iSARAH
(
Nguyen et al. 2021a
)
is the inexact version of SARAH that replaces the initial full gradient
v_{0}=\nabla P(\tilde{w}_{k})
by a mini-batch gradient estimator

v_{0}=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}\nabla f_{i}(\tilde{w}_{k}),

where
S\subset[n]
. This method is more efficient for large-scale problems and can also be used to solve the expectation minimization problem where the function
f
has a continuous Lipschitz gradient.

In general, variance-reduced algorithms are useful since they can achieve better computational complexities than SGD in terms of
\epsilon
.

Shuffling-based methods.
Another type of SGD variant, which has been actively studied and developed by researchers, is shuffling SGD. Original SGD samples function
f_{i}
randomly at each iteration, i.e., uses
sampling with replacement
strategy, while shuffling SGD uses the
sampling without replacement
strategy. With shuffling techniques, we sample each training data exactly once at each epoch. In practice, shuffling strategies are easier to implement, and they serve as the default sampling mechanism in many optimization methods for deep learning
(
Bengio 2012
;
Sun 2020
)
. In addition, the training loss from shuffling-type methods practically decreases faster than that from standard SGD
(
Bottou 2009
;
Recht and Ré 2013
)
.

There are three basic approaches to sampling without replacement.

1.

Incremental Gradient (Cyclic).
The training data is processed cyclically with natural order over the given dataset. In other words, a deterministic permutation is fixed throughout all epochs during the training process.

2.

Shuffle-Once (SO).
The training data is shuffled randomly only once before starting the training process.

3.

Random Reshuffling (RR).
The training data is randomly reshuffled before starting any epoch.

Although implementing shuffling strategies is more efficient in practice than standard SGD, analyzing those shuffling methods is more challenging due to the lack of statistical independence. Recently, there have been a few studies about theoretical analyses
(
Nguyen et al. 2021b
;
Mishchenko et al. 2020
;
Ahn et al. 2020
;
Haochen and Sra 2019
;
Safran and Shamir 2020
;
Rajput et al. 2020
;
Cai et al. 2024
)
showing that shuffling techniques can improve the convergence rates of SGD. Moreover, shuffling SGD can be combined with accelerating techniques such as momentum update
(
Tran et al. 2021
;
Tran et al. 2022
;
Tran et al. 2024
;
Hu et al. 2024
)
to improve the convergence rates of shuffling SGD.

Variance reduction with shuffling.
As discussed above, variance reduction and shuffling are two paradigms that can improve the performance of SGD. The original variance-reduced methods, like SVRG or SARAH, sample with replacement the index for the stochastic gradients of each update. In practice, it is easier to sample the list of indices without replacement, so it is natural to combine shuffling and variance reduction techniques. Some algorithms using this idea include IAG
(
Gurbuzbalaban et al. 2017
)
, DIAG
(
Mokhtari et al. 2018
)
, cyclic-SAGA
(
Park and Ryu 2020
)
, AVRG
(
Ying et al. 2020
)
, Prox-DFinito
(
Huang et al. 2021
)
, Shuffling SVRG
(
Malinovsky et al. 2023
)
, and Shuffling SARAH
(
Beznosikov and Takáč 2024
)
. The shuffling variance-reduced paradigm can also be applied in the Minimax Problem in
(
Jiang et al. 2024
)
.

On the other hand, theoretically, analyzing these shuffling variance-reduced methods is difficult. Recent complexity analysis results of these methods cannot match the complexities of some original algorithms samples with replacement. In particular, the complexities of most of these methods are even worse than the complexity of GD under the strongly convex setting. DIAG
(
Mokhtari et al. 2018
)
and Prox-DFinito
(
Huang et al. 2021
)
have the same complexity as GD, but they require the assumption of strong convexity for each function
f_{i}
. PIAG
(
Vanli et al. 2018
)
also can achieve that complexity, but the analysis is just involved with the cyclic technique. PIAG also requires storing
n
past gradients for updates, while other methods like SVRG and SARAH do not. Shuffling SVRG
(
Malinovsky et al. 2023
)
can achieve the same complexity as GD without the assumption of strong convexity for each
f_{i}
, but it requires the Big data regime, which needs a large sample size
n
. Table
1
summarizes the computational complexities of those shuffling variance-reduced algorithms introduced above. Eventually, without additional conditions, no algorithm can accomplish the same complexity as GD for all shuffling techniques. This background motivates us to answer the central question of the paper:

Can we design a shuffling variance-reduced algorithm that can achieve the best-known complexity of same-type algorithms for all shuffling techniques without any special condition?

Our Contributions

•

We introduce Adjusted Shuffling SARAH, a novel shuffling variance-reduced algorithm that integrates shuffling techniques with the SARAH algorithm. Our method dynamically adjusts the weights of the stochastic gradients within each inner loop, increasing their impact as the algorithm approaches the end of the loop. This adjustment promotes better exploration.

•

We perform a comprehensive convergence analysis of Adjusted Shuffling SARAH under the strongly convex setting with general assumptions. Our results demonstrate that the algorithm achieves the best-known gradient complexity for shuffling variance-reduced algorithms, matching the gradient complexity of GD,
\mathcal{O}(n\kappa\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right))
, when
P(w)
is strongly convex. This outperforms all prior results for similar algorithms.

•

We propose an inexact variant of Adjusted Shuffling SARAH, which eliminates the need for full-batch gradient computations. This algorithm not only has the same linear convergence rate as Adjusted Shuffling SARAH but also achieves a complexity of
\mathcal{O}\left(\min\left\{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\mu\varepsilon},n\right\}\kappa\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)
that makes this inexact method more efficient when
n
is very large.

•

We validate the performance of Adjusted Shuffling SARAH through empirical evaluation on a strongly convex binary classification task using logistic regression. Our algorithm demonstrates competitive performance relative to other variance-reduced algorithms in terms of loss residuals and gradient norms.

Related work.
Let us briefly review the most related works to our results in this paper.

There are various variance-reduced methods proposed in the last decade, such as SAG/SAGA
(
Roux et al. 2012
;
Defazio et al. 2014
)
, SVRG
(
Johnson and Zhang 2013
)
, and SARAH
(
Nguyen et al. 2017
)
, that can improve the convergence rate of the original SGD. These methods achieve the total complexity of
\mathcal{O}((n+\kappa)\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right))
in the case of strong convexity. In the case of general convex function, SVRG achieves the complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n+\sqrt{n}/\varepsilon)
, and SARAH achieves the complexity of
\mathcal{O}((n+1/\varepsilon)\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right))
. These results are better than the complexity of SGD, which is
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon^{2})
. In the case of smooth nonconvexity, SVRG and SARAH can improve SGD with complexities
\mathcal{O}(\frac{n}{\varepsilon})
and
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\varepsilon}\lor n)
, respectively. There are some proximal variants of these variance-reduced algorithms, such as ProxSVRG
(
J Reddi et al. 2016
)
, ProxSAGA
(
J Reddi et al. 2016
)
, and ProxSARAH
(
Pham et al. 2020
)
, that can solve the nonsmooth nonconvex finite-sum problem. In general, algorithms with variance reduction and their variants have been widely investigated, which shows the efficiency of this class of algorithms in both theory and practice.

Recently, researchers have made progress in analyzing the convergence rates of shuffling-type algorithms. In the case of strong convexity,
(
Gurbuzbalaban et al. 2019
;
Haochen and Sra 2019
;
Rajput et al. 2020
)
improved the convergence rate of SGD to the best-known result of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{n^{2}K^{2}}+\frac{1}{nK^{3}})
, for
K
is the number of epochs, when the sum of the functions is a quadratic. However, these methods require either the assumption of gradient boundedness and/or Lipschitz Hessian assumptions. Without those assumptions,
(
Ahn et al. 2020
;
Mishchenko et al. 2020
;
Nguyen et al. 2021b
)
can prove the upper bounds of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{nK^{2}})
. Most recently,
(
Cha et al. 2023
;
Liu and Zhou 2024
;
Cai and Diakonikolas 2025
)
slightly pushed the frontier of the last-iterate convergence rates of shuffling SGD. In the case of general convexity,
(
Shamir 2016
;
Mishchenko et al. 2020
;
Nguyen et al. 2021b
;
Cha et al. 2023
;
Liu and Zhou 2024
)
proves the convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{n^{1/3}K^{2/3}})
.
(
Cai et al. 2024
)
introduces a unified primal-dual framework that yields improved, fine-grained convergence bounds for shuffled SGD. In addition, there are some algorithms applying shuffling techniques to some variants of the original SGD, such as Minibatch and Local SGD in federated learning
(
Yun et al. 2021
)
and Nesterov Accelerated Shuffling Gradient (NASG)
(
Tran et al. 2022
)
. NASG can improve the convergence rate to
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{K})
, the best result within the class of shuffling algorithms in the convex regime. In the case of nonconvex,
(
Nguyen et al. 2021b
;
Mishchenko et al. 2020
)
proves the convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{K^{2/3}})
for shuffling SGD under standard assumptions. This rate can also be achieved with a shuffling momentum method in
(
Tran et al. 2021
)
. Most recently, this rate improved in the case of the incremental gradient in
(
Koloskova et al. 2024
)
.

Combining both shuffling techniques and variance reduction, various algorithms have been proposed and investigated. However, convergence analysis of this type of algorithm is difficult. Some cyclic algorithms, including Incremental Average Gradient (IAG)
(
Gurbuzbalaban et al. 2017
)
, Doubly Incremental Average Gradient (DIAG)
(
Mokhtari et al. 2018
)
, Proximal Incremental Average Gradient (PIAG)
(
Vanli et al. 2018
)
, and Cyclic-SAGA
(
Park and Ryu 2020
)
, were proposed and proved to have a linear convergence rate. The first analysis with algorithms using the RR technique is investigated by
(
Ying et al. 2020
)
. Later, Prox-DFinito in
(
Huang et al. 2021
)
improves the complexity to
\mathcal{O}(n\kappa\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}))
, but it requires each
f_{i}
strongly convex. In
(
Malinovsky et al. 2023
)
, Malinovsky et al. investigate the shuffling version of SVRG with all shuffling techniques. Most recently,
(
Beznosikov and Takáč 2024
)
proposed a shuffling version of SARAH that does not need full gradient computations. The total complexities of these algorithms are summarized in Table

There are three differences between our algorithm and SARAH. The first one is inside the inner loop. SARAH samples a random index
i_{t}
from
[n]
and uses that for the update

v_{t}=\nabla f_{i_{t}}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{i_{t}}(w_{t-1})+v_{t-1}.

Instead, in our algorithm, the indices of the gradients inside the inner loop are already determined, which follow a permutation of
[n]
. Note the permutation
\pi_{s}
can be chosen from three shuffling methods:

•

For the Cyclic method,
\pi_{s}
is the same as the natural order over the given set, which does not require any further step inside the outer loop.

•

For the Shuffle-Once method,
\pi_{s}
is determined by shuffling once at the beginning of the algorithm and outside the outer loop. No extra step is needed for the permutation inside the outer loop.

•

For the Random Reshuffling method,
\pi_{s}
is randomly shuffled in each iteration of the outer loop.

All three shuffling methods are easier to implement than sampling a random index in each inner-loop iteration. Another difference between our algorithm and SARAH is the number of inner-loop iterations, where our method has exactly
n
steps, and SARAH has a flexible number. The last difference is the coefficient
\frac{n+1}{n+1-t}
in the inner-loop update:

v_{t}=\left(\frac{n+1}{n+1-t}\right)(\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t-1}))+v_{t-1}.

Intuitively, when the algorithm runs toward the end of an inner loop, i.e.,
t
is close to
n
, the difference between
w_{t}
and
w_{t-1}
is close to
0
. Then, the difference between two gradients
(\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t-1}))
is also close to 0. The coefficient
\left(\frac{n+1}{n+1-t}\right)
becomes bigger when
t
tends to
n
, which can leverage the term
(\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t-1}))
. Then, the algorithm can explore better solutions by updating
v_{t}
and
w_{t+1}
.

One concern about the term
\left(\frac{n+1}{n+1-t}\right)
as
t
tends to
n
is that it can get very large, which can lead to a significant update
v_{t}
. This concern can be solved through a sufficient choice learning rate
\eta
.

.

\displaystyle=\lVert v_{t-1}\rVert^{2}-\frac{2}{\eta}\cdot\frac{(n+1)}{n+1-t}\left\langle\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t-1})-\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t}),w_{t-1}-w_{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\,\,+\left\lVert\left(\frac{n+1}{n+1-t}\right)(\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t-1})-\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t}))\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\lVert v_{t}\rVert^{2}

From that, take

n\geq 1
). Then, for all
t\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
,

\displaystyle 1-\frac{2}{\eta}\cdot\frac{(n+1-t)}{n+1}\cdot\frac{1}{L}\leq 1-\frac{2}{2/((n+1)L)}\cdot\frac{(n+1-t)}{n+1}\cdot\leq 1-(n+1-n)=0.

Therefore, for
0<\eta\leq\dfrac{1}{2nL}
, we have

\lVert v_{t}\rVert^{2}\leq\lVert v_{t-1}\rVert^{2}.

∎

This lemma ensures that the update cannot lead to an increase in an update
v_{t}
.

Another advantage of the coefficient
\left(\frac{n+1}{n+1-t}\right)
is enhancing the consistency among all three shuffling techniques, which allows Algorithm
1
to achieve the same convergence rate with any shuffling method. In detail, let
\tilde{w}_{s}
and
\tilde{w}_{s-1}
be two final points of two consecutive outer cycles. Then, we have

\tilde{w}_{s}-\tilde{w}_{s-1}=\eta\sum_{t=0}^{n}v_{t}.

Let
\Delta:=\sum_{t=0}^{n}v_{t}
. We present the following lemma to analyze the term
\Delta
.

Lemma 2
.

Consider
v_{t}
at iteration
s
defined from Algorithm
1
. Denote
\Delta=\sum_{t=0}^{n}v_{t}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=(n+1)\sum_{t=1}^{n}(\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t-1}))+(n+1)\nabla P(\tilde{w}_{s-1}).

The proof for this lemma is given in the Appendix. This lemma shows the role of the adjusted weight in our algorithm, which indicates that each term
(\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t-1}))
has the same weight of
(n+1)
in
\Delta
, for each
\pi_{s}^{t}\in\pi_{s}
. Then, with any shuffling technique, each term
(\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t-1}))
contributes equally to the updates of Algorithm
1
.

To further clarify this, we may consider the ordinary shuffling version of SARAH (without adjusted weight), which is analyzed in
(
Beznosikov and Takáč 2024
)
:

v_{t}=\left(\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t-1})\right)+v_{t-1},

(
Beznosikov and Takáč 2024
)
further shows that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n}v_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{n}(n+1-i)(\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t-1}))+(n+1)\nabla P(\tilde{w}_{s-1}).

In this ordinary version, each term
(\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t-1}))
has a different weight. Thus, for different permutations, the contribution of each term
(\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t-1}))
is not equal. For this reason, Adjusted Shuffling SARAH allows an update of
v_{t}
with more balanced weights of each individual function, regardless of the shuffling data scheme.

3
Theoretical Analysis for Adjusted Shuffling SARAH

In this section, we will investigate the convergence rate and the total complexity of Algorithm
1
in the case of strong convexity under standard assumptions. Our first assumption is that all component functions
f_{i}
are
L
-smooth.

Assumption 1

(
L-
smooth)
.

Each function
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, for
i\in[n]
, is
L-
smooth, i.e., there exists a constant
L>0
such that for all
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\lVert\nabla f_{i}(w)-\nabla f_{i}(w^{\prime})\rVert\leq L\lVert w-w^{\prime}\rVert

Assumption
1
can imply that the average function
P
is also
L
-smooth. This assumption is widely used in the literature for gradient-type methods in stochastic and deterministic settings. Next, let us define the assumption of strong convexity for the average function
P(w)
.

Assumption 2

(
\mu-
strongly convex)
.

The function
P:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mu-
strongly convex, i.e., there exists a constant
\mu>0
such that for all
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

P(w)\geq P(w^{\prime})+\langle\nabla P(w^{\prime}),w-w^{\prime}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\lVert w-w^{\prime}\rVert^{2}.

Note that with this assumption, there exists a minimizer
w_{\ast}
for the function
P
. Now, we establish the main lemma for proving the convergence rate of Algorithm
1
.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds. Consider Algorithm
1
with the choice of
\eta
such that
0<\eta\leq\frac{1}{2nL}
, we have

\displaystyle P(\tilde{w}_{s})\leq P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-\dfrac{\eta(n+1)}{2}(1-\eta^{2}n^{2}L^{2})\lVert\nabla P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})\rVert^{2}.

The proof for this lemma is provided in the Appendix. Based on this lemma, we prove our main theorem, which indicates the algorithm’s convergence rate in the case of strong convexity.

Theorem 1

(Strongly convex case)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Consider Algorithm
1
with the choice of
\eta
such that
0<\eta\leq\frac{1}{2nL}
. Then, we have

P(\tilde{w}_{s})-P_{\ast}\leq\left(1-\dfrac{\eta(n+1)\mu}{2}\right)^{s}(P(\tilde{w}_{0})-P_{\ast}).

Proof.

Since
P
is
\mu-
strongly convex, then

\lVert\nabla P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})\rVert^{2}\geq 2\mu(P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-P_{\ast})

From Lemma
3
, take
\eta\leq\dfrac{1}{2nL}
, we have

\displaystyle P(\tilde{w}_{s})

\displaystyle\leq P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-\dfrac{\eta(n+1)}{2}(1-\eta^{2}n^{2}L^{2})\lVert\nabla P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-\eta(n+1)\mu(1-\eta^{2}n^{2}L^{2})(P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-P_{\ast}).

Thus, for
0<\eta\leq\dfrac{1}{2nL}\leq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}nL}
, we have

1-\eta^{2}n^{2}L^{2}\geq 1-\frac{n^{2}L^{2}}{2n^{2}L^{2}}=1-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{2}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle P(\tilde{w}_{s})

\displaystyle\leq P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-\eta(n+1)\mu\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot(P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-P_{\ast})

\displaystyle=P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-\frac{\eta(n+1)\mu}{2}(P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-P_{\ast}).

Thus, by subtracting
P_{\ast}
from both sides, we have

P(\tilde{w}_{s})-P_{\ast}\leq\left(1-\dfrac{\eta(n+1)\mu}{2}\right)(P(\tilde{w}_{s-1}-P_{\ast}).

Therefore, after
s
iterations, we have

P(\tilde{w}_{s})-P_{\ast}\leq\left(1-\dfrac{\eta(n+1)\mu}{2}\right)^{s}(P(\tilde{w}_{0})-P_{\ast}).

∎

From this theorem, we derive the following corollary with linear convergence rate and total complexity of Algorithm
1
.

Corollary 1
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and let us run Algorithm
1
with
\eta=\dfrac{1}{2nL}
. Then, we can obtain an
\varepsilon-
approximate solution (in terms of
P(\tilde{w})-P_{\ast}\leq\varepsilon
) after

\mathcal{O}\left(\kappa\log\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)
iterations. Also, we can achieve the total complexity of Algorithm
1
as
\mathcal{O}\left(n\kappa\log\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)
.

Proof.

For
\eta=\dfrac{1}{2nL}
, from Theorem
1
, we have

\displaystyle P(\tilde{w}_{s})-P_{\ast}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\dfrac{(n+1)\mu}{4nL}\right)^{s}(P(\tilde{w}_{0})-P_{\ast})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\dfrac{n\mu}{4nL}\right)^{s}(P(\tilde{w}_{0})-P_{\ast})

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{4\kappa}\right)^{s}(P(\tilde{w}_{0})-P_{\ast}).\quad\left(\kappa=\frac{L}{\mu}\right)

Now, fix any
\varepsilon>0
, we will show that if
s\geq 4\kappa\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
, then

P(\tilde{w}_{s})-P_{\ast}\leq\varepsilon(P(\tilde{w}_{0})-P_{\ast}).

Indeed, assume that
s\geq 4\kappa\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
. We have
\kappa=\frac{L}{\mu}>1>0
. Then,
s\cdot\dfrac{1}{4\kappa}\geq\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)=-\log(\varepsilon).
This implies

\log(\varepsilon)\geq s\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{4\kappa}\right).

Then, since the exponential function is increasing, we have

\varepsilon\geq\left(e^{-1/4\kappa}\right)^{s}

We have
e^{x}\geq x+1
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, take
x:=-\frac{1}{4\kappa}
in the inequality, we have

\varepsilon\geq\left(e^{-1/4\kappa}\right)^{s}\geq\left(1+\left(-\frac{1}{4\kappa}\right)\right)^{s}=\left(1-\frac{1}{4\kappa}\right)^{s}.

Therefore, for
s\geq 4\kappa\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
, or
s\in\mathcal{O}\left(\kappa\log\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)
, we have

P(\tilde{w}_{s})-P_{\ast}\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{4\kappa}\right)^{s}(P(\tilde{w}_{0})-P_{\ast})\leq\varepsilon(P(\tilde{w}_{0})-P_{\ast}).

∎

In the next section, we will develop an inexact version for Algorithm
1
that does not require computing the full gradient in each outer iteration.

4
Inexact Adjusted Reshuffling SARAH Algorithm

In large-scale optimization, it is desirable to replace the exact gradient computation with averaged gradients from a smaller portion of the dataset, as the number of samples
n
can be big.
In this section, we introduce an
inexact
version of Algorithm
1
, named Inexact Adjusted Reshuffling SARAH, in Algorithm
2
.

Algorithm 2

Inexact Adjusted Reshuffling SARAH

1:

Initialization
: Initial point
\tilde{w}_{0}
, learning rate
\eta
, the inner loop size
m
, number of epochs
S

2:

Iterate
:

3:

for

s=1,2,\cdots,S

do

4:

Sample an
m
-element permutation
\pi_{s}=(\pi_{s}^{1},\cdots,\pi_{s}^{m})
of
[n]

5:

w_{0}=\tilde{w}_{s-1}

6:

v_{0}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla f_{\pi^{i}_{s}}(w_{0})\approx\nabla P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})

7:

w_{1}=w_{0}-\eta v_{0}

8:

Iterate:

9:

for

t=1,\cdots,m

do

10:

v_{t}=(\frac{m+1}{m+1-t})\left(\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t-1})\right)+v_{t-1}

w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\eta v_{t}

end

for

\tilde{w}_{s}=w_{m+1}

end

for

The most related work to Inexact Adjusted SARAH is probably iSARAH algorithm
(
Nguyen et al. 2021a
)
, an inexact version of SARAH originally designed for the expected risk minimization (ERM).
While iSARAH also uses mini-batch gradients to replace the full gradient computation in the outer loop, our method is fundamentally different from iSARAH as our inner loop uses a shuffling data scheme, which cannot be applied to the ERM problem.

Let us describe Algorithm
2
in more detail. In each outer iteration
s
, Algorithm
2
only uses the random reshuffling method to sample an
m
-element permutation
\pi_{s}=(\pi_{s}^{1},\cdots,\pi_{s}^{m})
of
[n]
. Then, the algorithm computes
v_{0}
as the average gradient of
m
functions from the sampled permutation
\pi_{s}
, which approximates the full gradient
\nabla P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})
. Next, it proceeds
m
inner updates
w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\eta v_{t}
, where
v_{t}
is defined as

v_{t}=\left(\frac{m+1}{m+1-t}\right)\left(\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t})-\nabla f_{\pi_{s}^{t}}(w_{t-1})\right)+v_{t-1},

and
\eta
is the learning rate. One key difference of Algorithm
2
compared to Algorithm
1
is the ability to choose the inner loop size
m
. In the convergence analysis below, we will show that Algorithm
2
has the same linear convergence rate as Algorithm
1
. Therefore, the total complexity of Algorithm
2
is
\mathcal{O}\left(m\kappa\log\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)
, which is better than Algorithm
1
when
m\ll n
.

Now, we show the theoretical analysis for Algorithm
2
in more detail. First, we need the following standard bounded variance assumption.

Assumption 3

(Standard bounded variance)
.

There exists
\sigma\in(0,+\infty)
such that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert\nabla f_{i}(w)-\nabla F(w)\rVert^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}.

This assumption is often used in stochastic optimization
(
Bottou et al. 2018
)
. Next, we present the main lemma for proving the convergence rate of our algorithm.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
3
holds. Consider Algorithm
2
with the choice of
0<\eta\leq\dfrac{1}{4mL}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[P(\tilde{w}_{s})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})]-\frac{\eta}{2}(m+1)(1-4m^{2}L^{2}\eta^{2})\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\nabla P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})\rVert^{2}\right]+\frac{3\sigma^{2}}{Lm}.

The proof for this lemma is included in the Appendix. From this lemma, we derive the main theorem for the convergence rate of Algorithm
2
in the strongly convex setting.

Theorem 2

(Strongly convex case)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
hold. Consider Algorithm
2
with the choice of
\eta
such that
0<\eta\leq\frac{1}{4mL}
. Then, we have that
\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{s})-P_{\ast})]
is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\eta(m+1)\mu}{2}\right)\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-P_{\ast})]+\frac{3\sigma^{2}}{Lm}.

Proof.

Since
P
is
\mu-
strongly convex, then

\lVert\nabla P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})\rVert^{2}\geq 2\mu(P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-P_{\ast})

From Lemma
4
, take
\eta\leq\dfrac{1}{4mL}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[P(\tilde{w}_{s})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})]-\frac{\eta}{2}(m+1)(1-4m^{2}L^{2}\eta^{2})\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\nabla P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})\rVert^{2}\right]+\frac{3\sigma^{2}}{Lm}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})]-\frac{\eta}{2}(m+1)(1-4m^{2}L^{2}\eta^{2})\mathbb{E}\left[2\mu(P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-P_{\ast})\right]+\frac{3\sigma^{2}}{Lm}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})]-\eta(m+1)(1-4m^{2}L^{2}\eta^{2})\mu\mathbb{E}\left[(P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-P_{\ast})\right]+\frac{3\sigma^{2}}{Lm}.

Take
0<\eta\leq\dfrac{1}{4mL}\leq\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{2}mL}
, we have

1-4\eta^{2}m^{2}L^{2}\geq 1-\frac{4m^{2}L^{2}}{8m^{2}L^{2}}=1-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{2}.

Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[P(\tilde{w}_{s})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})]-\eta(m+1)\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mu\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-P_{\ast})]+\frac{3\sigma^{2}}{Lm}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})]-\frac{\eta(m+1)\mu}{2}\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-P_{\ast})]+\frac{3\sigma^{2}}{Lm}

Thus, by subtracting
P_{\ast}
from in both sides, we have

\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{s})-P_{\ast})]\leq\left(1-\frac{\eta(m+1)\mu}{2}\right)\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-P_{\ast})]+\frac{3\sigma^{2}}{Lm}.

∎

Finally, we present a direct corollary indicating the convergence rate and the total complexity of Algorithm
2
.

Corollary 2
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and let us run Algorithm
2
with
\eta=\dfrac{1}{4mL}
,
m=\mathcal{O}\left(\min\left\{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\mu\varepsilon},n\right\}\right)
. Then, we can obtain an
\varepsilon-
approximate solution (in terms of
\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{s})-P_{\ast})]\leq\varepsilon
) after with
\mathcal{O}\left(\kappa\log\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)
iterations and total complexity of

\mathcal{O}\left(\min\left\{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\mu\varepsilon},n\right\}\kappa\log\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)
.

Proof.

First, if
n\leq\frac{96\sigma^{2}}{\mu\varepsilon}-1
, then we set
m=n
. Then, Algorithm
2
is the same as Algorithm
1
. Thus, based on Theorem
1
, Algorithm
2
can achieve
\varepsilon
-accuracy after
\mathcal{O}\left(\kappa\log\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)
iterations and have total complexity of
\mathcal{O}\left(n\kappa\log\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)
.

Now, consider
n>\frac{96\sigma^{2}}{\mu\varepsilon}-1
. We will choose
m=\frac{96\sigma^{2}}{\mu\varepsilon}-1
later. Let
\Psi=\dfrac{3\sigma^{2}}{Lm}
and
\alpha=\left(1-\frac{\eta(m+1)\mu}{2}\right)
.

From
Theorem

2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{s})-P_{\ast})]

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{s-1})-P_{\ast})]+\Psi

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{2}\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{s-2})-P_{\ast})]+(1+\alpha)\Psi

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{s}\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{0})-P_{\ast})]+\left(1+\alpha+\cdots+\alpha^{s-1}\right)\Psi

\displaystyle=\alpha^{s}\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{0})-P_{\ast})]+\dfrac{1-\alpha^{s}}{1-\alpha}\Psi

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{s}\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{0})-P_{\ast})]+\dfrac{\Psi}{1-\alpha}\quad\text{(Since $0<\alpha^{s}<1$)}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{s}\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{0})-P_{\ast})]+\frac{2}{\eta(m+1)\mu}\cdot\dfrac{3\sigma^{2}}{Lm}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{s}\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{0})-P_{\ast})]+\dfrac{6\sigma^{2}}{\eta(m+1)\mu Lm}.

We set
\dfrac{6\sigma^{2}}{\eta(m+1)\mu Lm}=\dfrac{\varepsilon}{4}
and
\alpha^{s}\mathbb{E}[(P(\tilde{w}_{0})-P_{\ast})]\leq\dfrac{3\varepsilon}{4}
. Also, choose
\eta=\dfrac{1}{4mL}
. Then we have
m=\dfrac{96\sigma^{2}}{\mu\varepsilon}-1=\mathcal{O}\left(\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{\mu\varepsilon}\right).
Also, we can achieve
\varepsilon-
accuracy after
\mathcal{O}\left(\kappa\log\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)
iterations. The proof is similar to that of Corollary
1
.

Therefore, from both cases, the total complexity is

\mathcal{O}\left(m\cdot\kappa\log\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\min\left\{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\mu\varepsilon},n\right\}\kappa\log\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right).

∎

Corollary
2
shows that the total complexity of Algorithm
2

improves the complexity of Algorithm
1
. In a large-scale optimization setting where
n>\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
, Algorithm
2
is much more efficient.
Therefore, Algorithm
2
is more suitable for solving large-scale problems than Algorithm

.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we propose a new shuffling variance-reduced method, named Adjusted Shuffling SARAH, by incorporating all three shuffling techniques into SARAH with an adjusted coefficient for the inner-loop stochastic gradient update. Our algorithm requires a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n\kappa\log(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}))
individual gradient computations in the strongly convex case, matching the best-known complexity of other shuffling variance-reduced methods. Notably, this result applies to any shuffling technique. Additionally, we introduce an inexact version of the Adjusted Shuffling SARAH algorithm that has a better total complexity when the number of samples
n
is very large. Furthermore, Adjusted Shuffling SARAH empirically demonstrates performance comparable to other recent shuffling variance-reduced methods.

For future work, while our algorithm achieves the best-known complexity for all shuffling techniques in the strongly convex case, it still falls short of matching the complexities of uniform-iid-sampling variance-reduced algorithms such as SVRG or SARAH. This discrepancy raises an important question: Can shuffling variance-reduced algorithms obtain the same complexities as the ones with uniform-iid-sampling? The question motivates us to find the upper bound for the complexity of algorithms applying both shuffling and variance-reduced techniques.

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{n}v_{t}

∎
