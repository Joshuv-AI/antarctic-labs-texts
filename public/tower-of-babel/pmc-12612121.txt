Nitrogen-doped ordered mesoporous carbons based on covalent triazine framework as the dopant for biodiesel production
Authors: Zahra Taherinia, Arash Ghorbani-Choghamarani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
N-Doped mesoporous carbons, NMCs, have attracted the growing interest of many scientists in various scientific fields, including catalysis and energy conversion/storage. The NMCs were prepared using the template method, SBA-15 as the template, and covalent triazine framework as carbon and nitrogen precursors. These NMC materials exhibit high catalytic activity in the metal-free catalytic esterification of methanol and biodiesel with a total yield of up to 94% at 80˚C in 5 h. The recycled N-doped mesoporous carbons had no obvious change in catalytic performance after four consecutive cycles, indicating their durability and recyclability. Additionally, all the evaluated physicochemical characteristics adhere to ASTM standards. Consequently, it has been determined that eucalyptus is a suitable crop for the production of biodiesel.

Introduction

Carbon materials with ordered mesoporous structures are frequently produced by soft1–3 and hard templating methods4–7. In the hard template method, zeolites and mesoporous silica such as Mobil Composition of Matter (MCM), Santa Barbara Amorphous(SBA), Fudan University (FDU)-series, Korea Institute of Technology (KIT)-series, and Hexagonal mesoporous silica (HMS) species are used as the hard template. The carbon precursors are inserted into the hard template’s channel, and the carbonization process causes it to be ablated. The soft template method results in mesopores by removing the surfactant (acting as the soft template) through high-temperature treatment8,9. Replicating the templates’ structures can be done with ease and reliability using the hard-templating method10–12. For numerous energy-related applications, carbon nanomaterials have been extensively studied for doping, substitution, modification, or combination of heteroatoms13–19. Nitrogen doping in porous carbon materials has attracted the most research interest among various dopants. The metal-free N-doped carbon materials have sprung up rapidly in the last few years, benefiting from the low cost and particularly high stability of carbon active sites without similar deactivation and fuel crossover problems. Mesoporous N-doped carbons significantly improved heat transfer and energy storage capacity with a high phase change materials (PCMs) loading ratio19. The price of petroleum and environmental concerns about car gas pollution are leading to a constant increase in the importance of biodiesel. It is usually produced by transesterifying oils with short-chain alcohols or by the esterification of fatty acids20–23. Producing biofuel from natural and renewable materials offers numerous benefits. Vegetable oil or animal fat are the sources of these oils, which make them biodegradable and non-toxic. To improve biodiesel yield and economic and environmental sustainability, numerous catalytic systems have been studied for this purpose24,25. Moreover, the homogeneous catalysts are inefficient as they require effluent treatment after the transesterification process. The main disadvantage of homogeneous catalysis is the difficulty associated with separating the catalyst from the product and solvent26,27. Compared to homogeneous catalysts, heterogeneous catalysts are a more suitable option due to easy separation and reusability, with better catalytic activity and stability28. Various heterogeneous catalyst systems have been employed for biodiesel production29–34. Most of the recent research has focused on the development of N-doped carbons as active species for various catalytic reactions for biodiesel production35–37. Herein, we reported a practical synthetic method for N-doped ordered mesoporous carbon (N-OMC) by SBA-15 as the template, and covalent triazine framework as carbon and nitrogen precursors, and their performance in the production of biodiesel from eucalyptus oil.

Experimental section

Catalyst synthesis

Production of SBA-15

An acidic solution was used to generate SBA-15 by employing Pluronic P123 and tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS) as structural directing agents. 126 mL of deionized (DI) water was used for a typical process that involved dissolving and stirring Pluronic P123 (4.0 g). Afterward, 20 mL of hydrochloric acid (37 wt%) and TEOS (9.2 mL) were added to it and stirred for 20 h at 35 ˚C. The product was treated with hydrothermal processes (12 h), filtered, dried, and calcined at 550 ˚C for six hours in air to remove the template38.

Production of covalent triazine framework

Figure 1 illustrates the synthesis process for generating a covalent triazine framework by reacting 1,4-dicyanobenzene and ZnCl2 with a mol ratio of 1:1. Then, the product was heated at 400 °C for 40 h. After cooling to room temperature, the reaction mixture was collected and washed with dilute hydrochloric acid under stirring to remove ZnCl239.

Production of N-doped mesoporous carbon

To make the N-doped mesoporous carbon, a covalent triazine framework (2 g) and SBA-15 (1 g) were mixed with 30 mL of acetone solvent. Following that, the mixture was subjected to reflux stirring for 3 h. The resulting N-doped mesoporous carbon powder was then centrifuged and allowed to drain at 50 °C. Next, the mixture was placed into two porcelain boats, and the temperature was increased by 5 °C per minute from room temperature to 800 °C for a duration of 2 h. After the mesoporous silica-carbon nanocomposite material was made, it was treated with 1.0 M NaOH solution in a 100 ˚C autoclave for 6 h. The silica template was removed completely from the composite material by performing this procedure twice (Fig. 2).

Oil extraction

A Soxhlet extractor was used for 24-hour reflux with n-hexane to extract oil at 70 °C. After extracting hexane using Soxhlet, it was removed via rotary evaporation at 50 °C to obtain oil (Fig. 3).

Biodiesel production

The reaction was conducted by combining methanol with eucalyptus oil (with a ratio of 10 mol: 1 g) and 800 mg of catalyst at 80 ˚C. Once the reaction concluded, the catalyst was removed, and methanol was obtained through rotary evaporation. Finally, the oil was dissolved in 20 mL of ethyl acetate, and a 5% solution of NaHCO3 was utilized to neutralize acidic byproducts and eliminate residual catalyst materials. The biodiesel composition was analyzed using GC-MS method (Fig. 4; Table 1).

Results and discussion

The morphology of the as-synthesized SBA-15 and the nitrogen-doped mesoporous carbon (NMC) materials were characterized by the FE-SEM technique (Figs. 5 and 6). FE-SEM images were captured using an FE-SEM ZEISS Sigma 300 and an FE-SEM TESCAN MIRA3, with an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. FE-SEM images revealed that the particles of silica SBA-15 are connected to form a well-organized mesoporous fiber-like structure, which is a characteristic morphology of such type of material (Fig. 5).

Fig. 5FE-SEM images of SBA-15.

FE-SEM images of SBA-15.

FE-SEM was utilized to examine both the morphology and porous structure of N-doped mesoporous carbons (Fig. 6). A change in the morphology has taken place in the mesoporous ordered structure, which could be due to the collapse of mesoporous channels and the graphitization of carbon at elevated temperatures. Some fibrous structures are also observed on the surface of the irregular carbon (the red mark on Fig. 6b). Excess covalent triazine framework is polymerized on the surface of the silica template instead of entering mesoporous silica, as suggested by Fig. 6b.

Fig. 6FE-SEM images of N-doped mesoporous carbons (a, b before recovery), and (c, d after recovery).

FE-SEM images of N-doped mesoporous carbons (a, b before recovery), and (c, d after recovery).

To investigate the surface composition of NMC material, EDX and CHN analyses were performed. The EDX analysis manifested the presence of C, O, and N (Fig. 7). Also, CHN analysis with quantification of each element was performed to further support the EDX results, and the result of CHN was 64.2% C, 28.3% O, and 7.5% N.

Fig. 7The EDS analyses for N-doped mesoporous carbon.

The EDS analyses for N-doped mesoporous carbon.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed on a Philips PW1730 diffractometer, which features a Ni filter and a graphite monochromator. This apparatus uses Cu kα radiation (wavelength = 1.15 Å) as its X-ray source. The scanning range was established at 2θ = 10°−80°, operating at 45 kV and 50 mA, with a step size of 2θ = 0.06° and a measurement time of 1 s for each step. The XRD pattern for N-doped mesoporous carbons before and after recovery is displayed in Fig. 8. The diffraction peaks at around 24.7 ° and 43.7° correspond to the planes (002) and (100) of the sp2-hybridized graphitic carbon network40. With the increase in nitrogen content, there is a gradual decrease in the intensity of the (100) diffraction line that refers to interlayer organization.

Fig. 8XRD patterns of N-doped mesoporous carbon (before recovery (a), and after recovery (b)).

XRD patterns of N-doped mesoporous carbon (before recovery (a), and after recovery (b)).

Raman spectra of the nitrogen-doped mesoporous carbon (NMC) were performed using Teksan Takram P50C0R10 Raman spectrometer (laser wavelength: 532 nm, laser power: 0.5–70 mW) (Fig. 9). Two prominent peaks were detected. The D band, located at about 1389 cm−1, is induced by disorder and is linked to structural imperfections and other disordered structures within the graphitic plane41. The G band, found at roughly 1594 cm−1, is typically seen in all graphitic materials and is associated with the E2g vibrational mode of sp2-bonded graphitic carbons41. The ratio of the intensity of the D band to that of the G band, referred to as the ID/IG ratio, serves as an indicator of the extent of structural defects and offers a quantitative assessment of edge plane exposure41. The ID/IG ratio calculated for NMC is 0.87.

Fig. 9Raman patterns of N-doped mesoporous carbon (before recovery (a), and after recovery (b)).

Raman patterns of N-doped mesoporous carbon (before recovery (a), and after recovery (b)).

To check the thermal stability of the NMC, a thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) is carried out using an N2 atmosphere and a heating rate of 10 °C per minute from room temperature to 800 °C (Fig. 10). Nitrogen-doped mesoporous carbon (NMC) decomposes into two steps within the temperature range of 200–600 °C, as shown by its TGA curve. At around 250 °C, moisture and surface hydroxyl groups are removed, which leads to the first stage of the weight loss (Fig. 10). The second step, with an estimated mass loss of 12% at temperatures up to 600 ˚C, corresponded to the removal of more stable oxygen functionalities42. According to the DTG curve of NMS, the first endothermic reaction takes place at approximately 247 °C, which means that adsorbed water or other volatile impurities are dehydrated. The decomposition of more stable oxygen-containing functional groups within the material is represented by the second endothermic peak at 587 °C, which indicates energy consumption.

Fig. 10TGA and DTG analyses of nitrogen-doped mesoporous carbon (NMC).

TGA and DTG analyses of nitrogen-doped mesoporous carbon (NMC).

The catalyst activity is greatly influenced by the products’ surface characteristics and pore structures. N2 sorption analyses were conducted using the BELSORP-mini II instrument. Before the sorption measurements, the sample underwent a degassing process at 150 °C under vacuum for 3 h. The N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms and pore size distributions of the NMC sample are depicted in Fig. 11. The results show that the NMC sample has Type IV isotherms, with hysteresis at p/p0 = 0.4–0.9, indicating the mesoporous character of the materials. Based on the material’s porous size distributions, it is clear that the NMC sample’s pore diameters are closely distributed at 9.26 nm. The adsorption-desorption data for N2 are presented in Table 1, and the results show that the NMC sample has a surface area of 89.18 m2g−1. The pore size distribution curve was calculated from an analysis of the adsorption branch using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method. The obtained average pore size is about 3.12 nm (Tables 2 and 3).

Material performance

The catalytic activity of the NMC was evaluated in the esterification of eucalyptus oil with methanol. The optimization of conditions for oil biodiesel production was carried out using the nitrogen-doped mesoporous carbon (300–800 mg) and various ratios of methanol: oil at various temperatures. The highest biodiesel yield was obtained using 800 mg of catalyst. Reaction with a temperature of 80˚C and a time of 5 h led to the maximum percentage yield. The ratio of.

the methanol-to-oil ratio was found to be 10:1 in this study, which was sufficient to complete the esterification process (Table 4).

Recyclability performance of nitrogen-doped mesoporous carbon (NMC)

The esterification of methanol with eucalyptus oil was investigated to assess the catalytic activity and recyclability of NMC. Once the reaction was over, high-speed centrifugation was utilized to filter the catalyst, and ethyl acetate was used to wash it. After multiple cycles with the catalyst, the product yield reached 94%, 90%, 89%, and 87% for the fourth cycles. A decrease in the catalytic efficiency of the nanoparticles is due to the aggregation during the catalysis process. XRD, Raman, and FE-SEM techniques were used to characterize the recovered catalyst to examine its structural stability after recycling (Figs. 6 and 8, and 9). An overview and in-depth examination of the NMS sample after recovery (6c-6d) reveals particles agglomerated together in contrast to the initial sample (6a-6b). Additionally, no significant changes were observed following the reaction, indicating that the catalyst’s structure is unchanged by the reaction. The recovered nitrogen-doped mesoporous carbon (NMC) is capable of being recycled based on the XRD, Raman pattern and FE-SEM techniques.

Conclusion

In summary, we have presented the synthesis and characterization of NMC using the template method, SBA-15 as the template, and covalent triazine framework as carbon and nitrogen precursors. The resulting NMC, featuring an amount of nitrogen doping (7.5%), exhibited high catalytic activity for biodiesel production with a total yield of 94% at 80 ˚C in 5 h.
