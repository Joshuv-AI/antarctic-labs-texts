Introduction

The connections between statistical physics and dynamical systems have long been
fruitful, with ideas initially flowing from physics into the study of dynamical systems.
The foundational developments in ergodic theory and chaotic dynamics were deeply
influenced by statistical mechanics, particularly through the notion of Gibbs states.
These measures, introduced by the pioneering work of Boltzmann and Gibbs, became a
crucial tool in understanding long-term statistical behavior in dynamical systems,
notably in the study of hyperbolic systems and Anosov flows. The development of
thermodynamic formalism, initiated by Sinai, Ruelle, and Bowen, brought a statistical
physics perspective to dynamical systems, using concepts such as entropy, pressure,
Gibbs and equilibrium states to describe the statistical properties of chaotic
systems
[
12
,
14
]
.

However, in recent years, this flow of ideas has started to reverse in certain contexts.
Techniques originating in ergodic theory and symbolic dynamics – historically developed
for understanding dynamical systems – are now being applied back to lattice models in
statistical physics.
Lattice models have played a pivotal role in the study of phase transitions, providing a
mathematical framework to understand how systems change between distinct states of
matter (e.g., from a disordered phase to an ordered phase). These models are defined on
a discrete lattice (
e.g.
,
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
) and describe interactions between variables
(
e.g.
, spins, particles, or other degrees of freedom) located at the lattice
sites; see for instance
[
23
,
27
]
.

Recent studies on low-temperature limits and ground states of lattice models have
increasingly leveraged techniques from symbolic dynamics
[
3
,
10
,
11
,
13
,
15
,
26
]
. In this paper, we focus on
freezing phase transitions, where the set of equilibrium states can collapse at low
temperatures – not just at zero temperature. While our results on freezing phase
transitions are abstract, they were motivated by understanding how equilibrium states
collapse in a way that they become concentrated on subshifts without any periodic
configuration, which serve as models for quasicrystals in the context of lattice systems.

Despite the close connections between statistical physics and dynamical systems through
thermodynamic formalism
[
46
]
, these two fields remain largely distinct communities. This
separation arises not only from differences in the types of questions they address but
also from fundamental differences in their core objects and frameworks. In statistical
physics, the primary object is an interaction, sometimes called an “interaction potential”, which is a family of functions
indexed by finite subsets of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. This object describes how components of a
system interact locally and provides a natural, flexible foundation for building models
from the ground up. In contrast, in dynamical systems, the central object is a function
on the phase space – often called a potential as well.

Schematically (we will revisit this point in more detail later), interactions
are used to define Gibbs states as well as equilibrium states

In the context of
statistical physics, one uses the term “state” to mean a probability measure, especially for
Gibbs and equilibrium measures which describe the states of the system.
. The construction of
these states relies on an averaged version of the interactions over finite subsets of

containing the origin. These averaged functions correspond to
the potentials used in dynamical systems, where the natural objects are equilibrium

mapping that transforms an interaction into a potential lacks good properties.
As a result, it is generally not
possible to automatically translate results obtained in the context of dynamical systems
to apply them directly to interactions. This effect will show up in this
article: although our results are stated in the spirit of dynamical systems, we will
show how to adapt and translate them into the framework of statistical physics.

1.2
Subshifts and quasicrystals

We can approach the problem in reverse: starting with a subshift that models either a
crystal or a quasicrystal, we can investigate how to construct an interaction for which this subshift will serve as the maximizing one. Here a crystal is a
subshift consisting of finitely many configurations, each being a shift of another.
A quasicrystal, by contrast, is a uniquely ergodic, zero-entropy subshift with
infinitely many configurations, and thus no periodic configurations.
We emphasize that there is no universally accepted definition of a quasicrystal, even within the framework of lattice models.
One could for instance refine the previous definition by additionally requiring the subshift to be minimal in the ergodic-theoretic sense, which informally means that all configurations share the same patterns. On the other hand, one could simply require a subshift that contains uncountably many configurations, with none of them being periodic.

There is a striking difference between
d=1
and
d\geq 2
. To model a quasicrystal for
d\geq 2
,
one can consider subshifts of finite type without periodic configurations, which correspond to
aperiodic Wang tilings (we will mention examples and references below).
For
d=1
, however, subshifts of finite type are either reduced to finitely many
periodic configurations (crystal), or are of positive topological entropy which is
interpreted as chaos/disorder.
Consequently, in dimension 1, natural zero-entropy uniquely ergodic subshifts such
as the Thue-Morse substitution have been commonly used, and infinite-range
interactions are required
[
24
]
.
For further discussions and background, we refer the reader to
[
17
,
20
]
.

1.3
Freezing at non-zero temperature.

For
0<\beta<\infty
, the variational principle described above indicates that equilibrium states exhibit a balance between “order” and “disorder”, with the potential
\varphi
acting as the driving force behind ordering. However, phase transitions can still occur.
From this perspective, we can reconsider the previous discussion by examining the behavior at nonzero temperature and addressing the following
questions:

Given a subshift modeling, for example, a quasicrystal, can we construct a function
\varphi
whose equilibrium states coincide, for all
\beta>\beta_{\mathrm{c}}
, with the unique invariant measure supported by this subshift? Is it possible that for
\beta<\beta_{c}
, none of the equilibrium states for
\varphi
are supported on the given subshift?

For a more general subshift, one might wish for the equilibrium states to correspond, for all
\beta>\beta_{\mathrm{c}}
, to the measures of
maximal entropy associated with this subshift.

If this scenario occurs, the phase transition corresponds to an abrupt contraction of the support of the equilibrium states, which become concentrated on a significantly smaller set of maximizing configurations for the given potential.
This type of phase transition differs from the
classical phase transitions typically encountered in statistical physics

[
23
,
27
]
.

In this article, given any proper subshift
X_{0}
of
X=\mathcal{A}^{{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}}
, we are able to construct a continuous potential, with controlled modulus of continuity, that “freezes” on
X_{0}
. Specifically, for
\beta>\beta_{\mathrm{c}}
, the equilibrium states coincide with the measures of maximal entropy supported on
X_{0}
, whereas for
\beta<\beta_{\mathrm{c}}
, none of the equilibrium states for the given potential are supported on
X_{0}
.
Additionally, we establish a no-go theorem, demonstrating that potentials satisfying a classical summability condition cannot exhibit such phase transitions.

Statistical physics

Recall that
X=\mathcal{A}^{{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}}
. The primary objects here are interactions.
An interaction

Interactions are the building blocks of Gibbs states that we will not describe

For a shift-invariant interaction, one can construct a function

This function appears in the definition of equilibrium states (variational principle)

It is commonly referred to as a potential in dynamical systems theory, and is

A fundamental question is to understand the structure of the set of Gibbs and

\beta\Phi
where
\beta\geq 0
. Let us emphasize

Notice that sign conventions used in dynamical systems are different from those used in statistical physics, basically because physicists prefer to minimize energy.

2.1
Freezing on any proper subshift and a no-go result

We first define what we precisely mean by a

2.2
Some remarks about Theorem A

One may wonder about what happens for
\beta=\beta_{\mathrm{c}}
.
As demonstrated already by the classical example of Hofbauer (see below),
\mathrm{ES}(\beta_{c}\varphi)
may or
may not coincide with
\mathrm{ZTA}(\varphi)
, and the pressure function may or may not be differentiable at
\beta_{\mathrm{c}}
.

Mathematically, one can consider negative inverse temperatures.
Associated with the limit
\beta\to-\infty
, one can consider minimizing measures. The corresponding freezing question relates to whether it is possible to reach the associated slant asymptote, which would occur to the left of a certain inverse temperature
\beta^{\prime}_{c}
(which can be
\neq\beta_{\mathrm{c}}
). Clearly, this is equivalent to analyzing the problem for
\beta^{\prime}(-\varphi)
with
\beta^{\prime}\to\infty
since
\beta\varphi=(-\beta)(-\varphi)
.

The last remark is related to the measure of maximal entropy on
X
.
If
\varphi=u\circ\sigma-u+c
where
u:X\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous,
c\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\sigma:X\to X
is the shift map,
then
p_{\varphi}(\beta)=c\beta+\log|\mathcal{A}|
. Moreover, these potentials correspond to the
same equilibrium state which is the measure of maximal entropy of
X
whose value is

\log|\mathcal{A}|=\ent_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\rm top}}(X)
, the topological entropy of
X
(see next section for more details).
All shift-invariant measures are maximizing measures for this
\varphi
.

2.3
Intermezzo on statistical physics

One may naturally ask what happens when starting from an interaction

\Phi=(\Phi_{\Lambda})_{\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
. Unfortunately, the correspondence between
\Phi

and
\varphi
is rather intricate in general. For instance, the mapping
\Phi\mapsto\varphi
is
not injective, and there is no general method to construct preimages of
\varphi
;
see
[
46
, pp. 34-35]
). Hence, some
information is inevitably lost when comparing the regularity of
\Phi
with that of
\varphi

For finite-range interactions – which encompass most models in dimension

– the situation is straightforward, as this implies that
\var_{n}\varphi
vanishes for
all sufficiently large

. Hence, Theorem B implies that finite-range
interactions cannot have a freezing phase transition. But of course the condition

\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{d-1}\var_{n}\varphi<\infty

can be satisfied by long-range interactions, provided they decay fast enough.
(If there exists a finite
R>0
such that
\Phi_{\Lambda}=0
for all
sets
\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
whose diameter is larger than

finite range. Otherwise, it has infinite range.)
The issue is that one cannot directly derive the natural assumption on
\Phi
from that on

\varphi
, which separates interactions based on whether they can induce a freezing phase
transition or not. Therefore, we state a theorem specifically for interactions.

Theorem B’
.

Let
X
be a full
d
-dimensional shift on a finite alphabet and
\Phi=(\Phi_{\Lambda})_{\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
be an interaction.
If
\sum_{\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{d},\,\Lambda\ni 0}\|\Phi_{\Lambda}\|_{\infty}<\infty
then
\Phi
cannot have a freezing phase transition.

The summability condition in the previous statement is satisfactory because it holds precisely for interactions belonging to the classical space of interactions. We provide more information
in Section
4.2
.
We do not claim that Theorem B’ is entirely new, but we were unable to find a suitable reference.

We end this section with the following remark regarding the analog of Theorem A in terms of interactions.

Remark 2.5
.

Theorem

below provides a more precise formulation of Theorem A by explicitly expressing the potentials that exhibit a freezing phase transition in terms of the target subshift
X_{0}
. For each such potential, we derive a formula for the corresponding interaction, which, as expected, is not summable in the sense of Theorem B’ (see Remark

2.4
Previously known results

Previous studies on freezing phase transitions have focused exclusively on the case
d=1
and specific examples. The constructions
were carried out in one-sided settings, i.e.,
X=\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
, and the advantage is two-fold. First, Ruelle’s Perron-Frobenius operator
can be used to evaluate the pressure. Second, a potential defined on a one-sided shift can be readily extended to the corresponding two-sided shift without altering the pressure function. Hence, the results concerning phase transitions
immediately carry over to the two-sided settings. We note that the converse is not true. Walters
[
47
]
provides necessary and sufficient
conditions for a continuous two-sided potential to be cohomologous to a one-sided potential, and these conditions cannot be
satisfied by any potential with a freezing phase transition.

In the context of shift dynamical systems, the first example of a freezing phase
transition is due to Hofbauer
[
30
]
, though his motivation was to exhibit a continuous
potential
\varphi
with two distinct (ergodic) equilibrium states (there is no inverse temperature in his paper). He works with
the one-sided full shift
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
and defines the potential
\varphi
by

\varphi(0^{\infty})=0
and
\varphi(x)=a_{n}
if
\dist(x,0^{\infty})=2^{-n}
, where
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}

is a sequence of negative real numbers such that
\sum_{n\geq 0}\e^{a_{0}+\cdots+a_{n}}=1
.
If
\sum_{n\geq 0}(n+1)\e^{a_{0}+\cdots+a_{n}}<\infty
, there are exactly two ergodic equilibrium states. Conversely, if
\sum_{n\geq 0}(n+1)\e^{a_{0}+\cdots+a_{n}}=\infty
, there is a unique equilibrium state.
Reformulated in our context, he proves that this potential has a freezing phase transition at
\beta_{\mathrm{c}}=1
with

X_{0}=\{0^{\infty}\}
, hence
s_{\varphi}=\ent_{\varphi}=0
and
\mathrm{ES}(\beta\varphi)=\mathrm{MM}(\varphi)=\mathrm{ZTA}(\varphi)=\{\delta_{0^{\infty}}\}
for
all
\beta>1
. In these examples, prior to the freezing phase transition, the unique equilibrium state is
fully supported, whereas after the phase transition, the unique equilibrium state is supported on a single point.
When the previous series is finite, the pressure function is not differentiable at
\beta_{\mathrm{c}}
,
while it is differentiable when it is infinite.
Hofbauer was inspired by the Fisher-Felderhof droplet model
[
22
]
, of
which it is a crude simplification.
We refer to
[
40
]
for a shorter, alternative proof of the nonuniqueness part of Hofbauer’s results.
A systematic study of a family of Hofbauer potentials exhibiting a phase transition
at some value
\beta_{\mathrm{c}}
was done by Lopes
[
42
]
.

Another example in the same spirit as Hofbauer’s example is given in
[
44
]
to exhibit
non-analyticity of multifractal spectra for pointwise dimension. The author considers

X=\{0,1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
and, reformulated in our context, he has
X_{0}=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\beta_{\mathrm{c}}=1
. A general case where
X=\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
for a finite alphabet
\mathcal{A}
and
X_{0}\subset X
is any subshift of finite type is studied in
[
36
]
, where an explicit construction of a potential with a freezing phase transition on
X_{0}
is carried out.

In the context of one-dimensional, one-sided shifts, with potentials of the same form as the ones
used in this paper, references
[
5
,
6
]
assert that they have established a freezing phase
transition with
X_{0}
defined by the subshift generated by either the Thue-Morse or Fibonacci
substitutions. Unfortunately, the proofs presented in these articles are flawed.
Work in progress
[
4
]
establishes a freezing phase transition in the Thue-Morse subshift,
building on the techniques of
[
5
]
, but requiring significant combinatorial refinements.
In particular, since these papers use Ruelle’s operator, this approach cannot be extended to
higher dimensions.

Recent work
[
7
]
broadly extends
[
5
,
6
,
4
]
by proving existence of freezing
phase transitions for all zero-entropy (one-sided and two-sided) subshifts, instead of
very specific ones.
However, the proof is not constructive, since the potentials are ultimately obtained
through the Hahn-Banach theorem. Recently, leveraging the same underlying techniques – which are inherently non-constructive – it was demonstrated in

[
29
]
, within a context encompassing ours, that for any nonempty collection of ergodic measures with a constant entropy
function, there exists a continuous
\varphi
such that, for all
\beta\geq 1
, its closed convex hull coincides with

\mathrm{ES}(\beta\varphi)
.

There is an abstract result in
[
37
]
that guarantees the existence of
\varphi
leading to a pressure
function as in Figure
1
. Precisely, given a compact
topological dynamical system
(X,T)
with positive entropy and upper semi-continuous entropy map, and any closed invariant subset

X_{0}\subset X
with positive topological entropy, there exists a continuous potential
\varphi:X\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying (
1
), (
2
) and (
3
) (see Lemma
3.5
below).
The proof is based on the fact that equilibrium states can be viewed as tangent
functionals to the pressure and relies on a theorem by Israel about approximations of shift-
invariant measures by equilibrium states; see
[
31
]
or
[
27
, Proposition 17.7, p. 341 ]
.
It does not provide any information on the type of potentials
which would generate a freezing phase transition.

To conclude this section, we observe that in Hofbauer’s example, as well as in
[
4
]
, there exists a unique equilibrium state with full support for all
0\leq\beta<\beta_{c}
. In the construction provided in the proof of Theorem A (detailed in Theorem
3.6
), it remains unclear whether fully supported measures
\mu\in\mathrm{ES}(\beta\varphi)
exist for all
0\leq\beta<\beta_{c}
. We leave this as an open question.

2.5
Some illustrative examples

Theorem A is applicable to any subshift; however, we highlight specific examples of particular interest, especially in light of the motivations outlined in the introduction.

We note that our results have interesting implications already in one dimension (
d=1
). For example, the objective of
[
5
]
and
[
6
]
was
to determine for which sequences
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
the Hofbauer-type potential
\varphi(x)=a_{n}
if
\dist(x,X_{0})=2^{-n}
has a freezing phase
transition given that
X_{0}
is the Thue-Morse shift or the Fibonacci subshift respectively. Their conclusion is that
\varphi
freezes when
a_{n}\approx-\frac{1}{n^{\alpha}},\alpha\in(0,\,1)
for the former and when
a_{n}\approx-\frac{1}{n}
for the latter. Their techniques rely
heavily on the specific properties of the Thue-Morse subshift and Fibonacci shift, which are very well understood, and the use of the transfer operator on
a certain induced system. The proofs of
[
5
,
6
]
appear to be incomplete, and the ongoing effort
[
4
]
aims to justify them in the Thue-Morse case as well as establish freezing when
a_{n}\approx-\frac{1}{n}
. Meanwhile, a direct application of Theorem
3.6
(for the two-sided Thue-Morse and Fibonacci subshifts)
gives that
\varphi
freezes when
a_{n}\approx-\frac{\log n}{n}
. Also, Theorem B shows that
\varphi
does not freeze for
a_{n}\leq-\frac{1}{n^{1+\epsilon}}
for all
n
and for some
\epsilon>0
. We
discuss further freezing phase transitions for one-dimensional subshifts in Section
5
. In particular, up to a logarithmic factor, we recover the result of
[
4
]
on the Thue-Morse subshift using a completely different approach. Moreover, Theorem
5.3
applies to any subshift.

In
[
19
]
, the authors consider Sturmian subshifts (which can be used for instance to code irrational
rotations on the unit circle). Here
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}
and these subshifts are minimal, uniquely ergodic, and have zero topological entropy. They construct an infinite-range potential, decaying as fast as one wants, whose maximizing subshift is a Sturmian subshift. Using Theorem A (see Theorem
3.6
for a more precise statement), we know how to construct infinite-range
potentials, decaying slowly enough, such that a freezing phase transition occurs.
However, using Theorem B, we know that the potential in
[
19
]
cannot have a freezing phase transition.

We now turn to higher-dimensional subshifts, focusing on subshifts of finite type (SFTs) which exhibit significantly greater diversity compared to their one-dimensional counterparts
[
41
]
,
and are relevant to model quasicrystals, as mentioned in the introduction. Informally,
X_{0}\subset\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is an SFT if no configuration in
X_{0}
contains a pattern from a prescribed collection
of patterns
F
, where
F\subset\mathcal{A}^{\Lambda}
and
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is finite.

The first example we mention is taken from
[
9
, Example 1.5]
. Let
M\in\mathbb{N}
and define
\mathcal{A}=\{-M,-M+1,\ldots,-2,-1,1,2,\ldots,M-1,M\}
. Then,
x\in X_{0}
if and only if, whenever
\|{\boldsymbol{i}}-{\boldsymbol{j}}\|_{1}=1
, we have
(x_{\boldsymbol{i}},x_{\boldsymbol{j}})\notin\{(a,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}:aa^{\prime}\leq-2\}
. In other words, the only way for a negative number to have a positive neighbor is through the pair
\pm 1
. This defines a (strongly) irreducible subshift of finite type (SFT), which consequently has positive topological entropy. Theorem 1.17 in
[
9
]
then asserts that if
M>4\e 28^{d}
, there are exactly two extremal (ergodic) measures of maximal entropy.

In dimension
d\geq 2
, there exist subshifts of finite type (SFTs) that have uncountably many configurations but no periodic configurations – a phenomenon that is impossible for
d=1
. Let us focus on the case
d=2
. Such SFTs are typically constructed using aperiodic Wang tilings. Given a finite collection of
1\times 1
square tiles with colored edges, copies of the tiles are placed (without rotation or reflection) at the points of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, arranged so that adjacent edges match in color. For further details, see
[
41
]
.
A notable example is the Kari-Culik shift, which is constructed in a fundamentally different way from classical examples and, moreover, has positive topological entropy
[
16
]
. According to a folk theorem, this SFT cannot be uniquely ergodic. Recently, a family of minimal, uniquely ergodic, aperiodic Wang shifts was constructed in
[
38
,
39
]
. These shifts are self-similar in the sense that they can also be obtained using two-dimensional substitutions, where the inflation factors are the
n
-th metallic means. This provides an elegant family of quasicrystal models for which we have explicit constructions of potentials that freeze on them.

Freezing on any proper subshift

3.1
Notation and definitions

Let

,
i.e.

is the space of all maps

We will call them configurations.
We put the discrete topology on

and the product topology on

(which is thus compact).
For

is the projection of

(or the restriction of

to
\Lambda

For

for

.
The shift map is the natural action of
{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
on

by translations.
We take

, where

(compatible with

The space of shift-invariant probability measures is equipped with the vague topology, under which it is compact.

A subshift
X_{0}
of

is a closed subset

which is

\sigma^{{\boldsymbol{n}}}
-invariant for every
{\boldsymbol{n}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Given
\Lambda\Subset{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}

(that is,
\Lambda
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
),
a pattern

appears in a configuration

{\boldsymbol{m}}_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
such that

for all

{\boldsymbol{m}}\in\Lambda
. We recall the definition of the language of

.
For
\Lambda\Subset{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, define
{\mathscr{L}}_{\Lambda}(X_{0})
as the
set of patterns supported on
\Lambda
that appear in some configuration

x\in X_{0}
. The language of
X_{0}
is defined as

{\mathscr{L}}(X_{0})=\bigcup_{\Lambda\Subset{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}}{\mathscr{L}}_{\Lambda}(X_{0})
.

Throughout the paper, we define

\Lambda^{\!\lx@scalerel@obj{+}}_{n}=[0,n-1]^{d}\cap{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
and
\Lambda_{n}=[-n,n]^{d}\cap{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
; these
appear more frequently in ergodic theory and statistical physics respectively.
For
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we denote by

its volume, that is, the number of sites
in
\Lambda
.
The topological entropy of
X_{0}
is

\ent_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\rm top}}(X_{0})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Lambda^{\!\lx@scalerel@obj{+}}_{n}|}\log|{\mathscr{L}}_{\Lambda^{\!\lx@scalerel@obj{+}}_{n}}(X_{0})|.

(4)

A function
\varphi:X\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous if and only if

\var_{n}(\varphi):=\sup\{|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|:x|_{\Lambda_{n}}=y|_{\Lambda_{n}}\}
goes to
0
.
(Note that
\var_{n}(\varphi)=\sup\{|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|:x,y\in X,\dist(x,y)\leq 2^{-n}\}
, the modulus of continuity of
\varphi
.)
Given
\varphi:X\to\mathbb{R}
continuous, recall that

means that

\ent(\nu)+\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu=\sup\left\{\ent(\nu)+\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\nu:\nu\;\text{shift-invariant}\right\},

where
\ent(\nu)
is the (measure-theoretic) entropy of
\nu
, that is,

\ent(\nu)=\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{|\Lambda^{\!\lx@scalerel@obj{+}}_{n}|}\sum_{w\,\in\mathcal{A}^{\Lambda^{\!\lx@scalerel@obj{+}}_{n}}}\nu([w])\log\nu([w])\,

with

.
By compactness in vague topology, there exists at

\varphi
, that is, at least one
\mu

\nu
; this is the variational principle.
By definition, the above supremum is the topological pressure of
\varphi
, denoted

. If
\varphi\equiv 0
then
P(\varphi)=P(0)=\ent_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\rm top}}(X)=\log|\mathcal{A}|
.
(More generally, this is true if
\varphi=u\circ\sigma-u+c
where

is continuous,

, see
[
46
, p. 61]
.)
Given
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, we can define, as done in the introduction, the equilibrium states of
\beta\varphi
in the obvious way,
as well as the pressure function
\beta\mapsto p_{\varphi}(\beta):=P(\beta\varphi)
(which is convex and Lipschitz continuous).
Given a subshift
X_{0}
of
X
, its measures of maximal entropy are the measures whose measure-theoretic entropy is the same as
\ent_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\rm top}}(X_{0})
.

3.2
Dyadic tiling lemma

The following lemma plays a key role in our construction of potentials that freeze on a subshift.
To show that equilibrium states are supported on the given subshift, we will show that ergodic measures
not supported on the subshift do not achieve the supremum in the variational principle. Our
approach to this is based on efficiently partitioning the orbit of a point lying outside
the subshift into blocks that do lie inside the subshift.

Definition 3.1
.

A
tiling

t
of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is a partition of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
into a disjoint union of finite non-empty subsets:
\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}
.
Given
{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we let
t({\boldsymbol{j}})
denote the set
S_{i}
containing
{\boldsymbol{j}}
.

We let
\mathcal{T}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
denote the collection of all tilings of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. If
t=\{S_{i}\colon i\in I\}
is a tiling
and
{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we define the
translation of
t
by
{\boldsymbol{j}}
to be
\mathsf{Tr}_{{\boldsymbol{j}}}(t)=\{S_{i}-{\boldsymbol{j}}\colon i\in I\}
, where

S_{i}-{\boldsymbol{j}}
denotes the translate of
S_{i}
by the vector
{\boldsymbol{j}}
, that is,

S_{i}-{\boldsymbol{j}}=\{{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{j}}\colon{\boldsymbol{k}}\in S_{i}\}
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
T
be a measurable
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-action on a measure space
(Z,\rho)
. An
equivariant tiling

is a map
\tau
from

to
\mathcal{T}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
with the property that
\tau(T^{{\boldsymbol{j}}}z)=\mathsf{Tr}_{{\boldsymbol{j}}}(\tau(z))
.

Rather than writing
\tau(z)
for the tiling, we will write
\tau^{z}

\tau^{z}({\boldsymbol{j}})
is the tile
in
\tau(z)
containing
{\boldsymbol{j}}
.

We describe the standard dyadic odometer as the space
\Sigma=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, where

as a left-infinite sequence
\ldots y_{2}y_{1}y_{0}
and define the homeomorphism
\iota
of

\iota(y)=\begin{cases}\ldots y_{n+1}10\ldots 0&\text{if $y_{n}\ldots y_{0}=01\ldots 1$ for some $n\geq 0$};\\
\ldots 0000&\text{if $y=\ldots 1111$}.\end{cases}

That is, in our presentation of the standard odometer, the map
\iota
is binary addition of 1 with carry to the left.
Given

, we define a family of equivalence relations on
\mathbb{Z}
by
i\mathrel{\mathop{\lx@scalerel@obj{\sim}}\limits_{\vbox to0.0pt{\kern-6.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle n,y$}\vss}}}j

That is, the

Definition 3.3
.

The

Lemma 3.4

(Dyadic Tiling Lemma)
.

Let
X_{0}
be a proper subshift of

and let
\mu
be an ergodic shift-invariant measure

. Let

be the dyadic
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-odometer with unique invariant

\nu
and let
\bar{\mu}
be an ergodic joining of
\mu
and
\nu
, where

\mathbb{Z}^{d}

Then there exists an equivariant tiling
\tau\colon X\times Y\to\mathcal{T}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
with the following properties:

1.

For
\bar{\mu}
-a.e.
(x,y)
and each
{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}

such that

. That is, all tiles appearing in

are dyadic tiles defined by

2.

For
\bar{\mu}
-a.e.
(x,y)
and each

, then

. That is, the

-tile that is larger than

;

3.

For
\bar{\mu}
-a.e.
(x,y)
and each

.

Taken as a whole, the lemma states that the tiling

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be as in the statement and let
\bar{\mu}
be an ergodic joining of them.
Define

\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\colon x|_{\tau_{n}^{y}({\boldsymbol{j}})}\in{\mathscr{L}}(X_{0})\}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(5)

Note that since
\mu

\mu

,

is
almost surely finite.
We then define the tiling
\tau
by

That is,
\tau^{(x,y)}({\boldsymbol{j}})
is the largest dyadic tile (as determined by

) for
which the corresponding

-block belongs to
{\mathscr{L}}(X_{0})
.
To see that this is a tiling, let

and let

.
First notice that

for each

,
so that the tiles cover
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Next note that if

\tau_{m}^{y}({\boldsymbol{j}})=\tau_{m}^{y}({\boldsymbol{k}})
for all

From the definition of

so that

.
∎

3.3
Potentials that freeze on a given subshift

We will prove Theorem A, which, as stated above, follows as a consequence of a more detailed theorem.
The following proposition provides sufficient conditions for a potential to freeze on a given subshift.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let

4

4.1
Proof of Theorem B

In this section we show that the equilibrium states for potentials with summable variation are
fully supported, and then use that to prove Theorem B.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X
be the full shift
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
and let
\varphi\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous potential satisfying

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{d-1}\var_{n}\varphi<\infty.

(8)

Then any equilibrium state for
\varphi
is fully supported (every cylinder set has a strictly positive measure).

For a sketch of the proof, if
\mu
is a shift-invariant probability measure on
X
which
is not fully supported, we use
\mu
to construct another measure
\tilde{\mu}
on
X
by occasionally
inserting blocks that lie outside the support of
\mu
to ensure that

\ent(\tilde{\mu})+\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\mu}>\ent(\mu)+\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu
.
This implies that
\mu
is not an equilibrium state of the potential
\varphi
.

Proof.

Let
\mu
be an ergodic invariant measure that is not fully supported.
We let
W
be a block of symbols such that
\mu([W])=0
, where
[W]
denotes
the cylinder set
\{x\colon x|_{\Lambda}=W\}
and
\Lambda
is the
set of coordinates of symbols appearing in
W
. By extending
W
if necessary,
we may assume
\Lambda=\Lambda^{\!\lx@scalerel@obj{+}}_{n}
for some
n
.
We show that
\mu

is not an equilibrium state for
\varphi
by constructing a shift-invariant measure
\tilde{\mu}

such that

\ent(\tilde{\mu})+\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\mu}>\ent(\mu)+\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu.

Informally,
\tilde{\mu}
will be constructed by starting from a realization,
x
, of
\mu

and overwriting blocks in
x
with copies of the pattern
W
at random and with very low frequency
to obtain a new point
z
. Since
W
’s do not occur in
x
, any
W
that occurs in
z
occurs as a result
of this overwriting process. This allows one to recover from
z
the original point
x
(up to regions that were
overwritten with
W
’s), as well as the locations where the overwriting took place.
There may be some ambiguity in identifying the overwriting locations because when a
W
is inserted
it is possible that some of the symbols of the inserted
W
together with some of the existing symbols in
x

may make additional copies of
W
.
We show that the entropy of
\tilde{\mu}
is bounded below by the entropy of
\mu

plus
the entropy of
the overwriting process minus the entropy of the parts of
x
that are overwritten and with a correction
for the ambiguity in identifying the overwrite locations. If the overwriting process has frequency
\delta
,
the entropy of
\tilde{\mu}
will be
\ent(\mu)+\delta|\log\delta|-O(\delta)
. Similarly we will show

\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\mu}\geq\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu-O(\delta)
. Combining these, we will see that for small
\delta>0
,
the desired inequality holds.

More precisely, let
\delta>0
be a parameter and let
\nu_{0}
be the Bernoulli measure on
Y_{0}=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}

where 1’s occur with frequency
\delta
. We define a continuous shift-commuting map
\Psi\colon Y_{0}\to Y_{0}
by

\Psi(y)_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $y_{\boldsymbol{v}}=1$; and $y_{\boldsymbol{u}}=0$ for all ${\boldsymbol{u}}$ with $0<\|{\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{\infty}<n$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

That is,
\Psi
removes 1’s that are closer than
n
apart, this distance being chosen to ensure that overwritten

W
’s don’t overlap. We then define
\nu
to be
\Psi_{*}(\nu_{0})
. Since
\Psi
is shift-commuting,

\nu
is another invariant measure on
Y_{0}
, and is still mixing as it is a factor of a mixing measure. We claim that
\overline{\mathrm{d}}(\nu,\nu_{0})\leq(2n)^{d}\delta^{2}
, which is intuitively unsurprising since this is an upper bound for
the probability that
\Psi
changes a 1 to a 0.

Recall that the Ornstein’s
\overline{\mathrm{d}}
-distance between measures
\nu
and
\nu_{0}
is given by

\overline{\mathrm{d}}(\nu,\nu_{0})=\inf_{\bar{\nu}}\int\1_{\{y_{\mathbf{0}}\neq y_{\mathbf{0}}^{\prime}\}}{\,\mathrm{d}}\bar{\nu}(y,y^{\prime})
, where
\bar{\nu}
runs over all joinings of
\nu
and
\nu_{0}
; see

[
28
]
.
To estimate

Remark 4.3
.

We established that if
\varphi
satisfies (

[
33
, Chapter 5]
, as we now outline. Notice that our proof takes a completely different approach, relying on ergodic theoretic techniques and notably avoiding any use of Gibbs states.

Keller first defines Gibbs states associated with a potential
\varphi
. (It is worth noting that the classical definition of a Gibbs state is based on an interaction
\Phi
, as in
[
46
]
and
[
27
]
.) He then shows that these Gibbs states – which are not necessarily shift-invariant – assign a strictly positive measure to all cylinder sets. This result follows from a precise estimate involving the Birkhoff sum of
\varphi
and its pressure. Finally, he demonstrates that under condition (
8
), the shift-invariant Gibbs states coincide with the equilibrium states – the key point being to establish that the equilibrium states of

\beta\varphi

.

The one-dimensional case

Theorem A gives an explicit construction of a potential
\varphi
.
An interesting question concerns the optimality of the potential in (

)
in a sense that defining
\varphi
using a sequence which converges to zero slower than

generates a freezing phase transition, but using a faster converging sequence does not.
For a subshift
X_{0}\subsetneq\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
we know that

{\varphi(x)\approx\left(\ent_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\rm top}}(X_{0})-\frac{\log(\#j\text{-words in }X_{0})}{j}\right)-\frac{\log\log j}{j}}\text{ if }\,{\dist(x,X_{0})=\frac{1}{2^{j}}}

has a freezing phase transition and the zero-temperature accumulation measures for

are the measures of maximal entropy of

.

below that when

is an SFT, the term
{\frac{\log\log j}{j}}
can be replaced by
{\frac{1}{j}}
. Moreover, we establish this fact in the one-sided settings.

Theorem 5.1

(One-sided SFT’s)
.

Suppose
X^{+}=\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
is a full shift and
X_{0}^{+}\subset X^{+}
is a one-step subshift of finite type.
Then the potential
\varphi^{+}:X^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\varphi^{+}(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\quad x\in X_{0}^{+},\\
-a_{j}&\text{if}\quad\dist(x,X_{0}^{+})=2^{-j},\end{cases}

(16)

has a freezing phase transition provided
(a_{j})
is a decreasing sequence converging to 0, such that

a_{j}\geq\frac{1}{j}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. In this case,
\mathrm{ZTA}(\varphi)
coincides with the set of maximal entropy measures of
X_{0}^{+}
. In particular, if all transitive components of
X_{0}^{+}
have the same entropy then
\varphi
freezes on
X_{0}^{+}
.

Proof.

Let
\mu
be an ergodic measure which is not supported on
X_{0}^{+}
. Then
\mu
assigns positive measure to the set
E=\{x\colon x_{0}x_{1}\not\in{\mathscr{L}}(X_{0}^{+})\}
. We induce on
E
. Denote by
r(x)
the first return time to
E
, i.e.,

r(x)=\min\{j\geq 1:\sigma^{j}(x)\in E\}
. We obtain the ergodic system

(E,{\mu}_{E},{\sigma}_{E})
with corresponding potential
{\varphi}_{E}
where

{\mu}_{E}=\frac{{\mu}|_{E}}{{\mu}(E)},\quad\sigma_{E}(x,z)=\sigma^{r(x)}(x),\quad{\varphi}_{E}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{r(x)-1}{\varphi^{+}}({\sigma}^{i}(x)).

Then Abramov’s formula and Kac’s theorem imply that

\ent({\mu})+\int{\varphi^{+}}{\,\mathrm{d}}{\mu}={\mu}(E)\left(\ent({\mu}_{E})+\int{\varphi}_{E}{\,\mathrm{d}}{\mu}_{E}\right).

Let
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be the first return time partition of
E
, i.e.,
Q_{j}=\{x\in E:r(x)=j\}
, and
let
\mathcal{P}
be its further refinement into cylinder sets. Denote by
q_{j}={\mu}_{E}(Q_{j})
and by
n_{j}
the
number of words of length
j
which are admissible in
X_{0}
. We have

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}q_{j}=1,\quad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}jq_{j}=\frac{1}{{\mu}(E)},\quad\card\{A\in\mathcal{P}\colon A\subset Q_{j}\}\leq n_{j}.

We estimate the entropy of measure
{\mu}_{E}
by the
{\mu}_{E}
-entropy of the partition
\mathcal{P}
:

\ent({\mu}_{E})\leq H(\mathcal{P})=H(\mathcal{Q})+H(\mathcal{P}|\mathcal{Q})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}-q_{j}\log q_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}q_{j}\log n_{j},

where in the last step we used the fact that each
\mathcal{Q}_{j}
contains at most
n_{j}
elements of
\mathcal{P}
.
Note that when
q_{j}>\frac{1}{j^{2}}
we have
-\log q_{j}<2\log j
. Therefore,

\begin{split}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}-q_{j}\log q_{j}&=\sum_{q_{j}>\frac{1}{j^{\lx@scalerel@obj{2}}}}-q_{j}\log q_{j}+\sum_{q_{j}\leq\frac{1}{j^{\lx@scalerel@obj{2}}}}-q_{j}\log q_{j}\\
&\leq\sum_{q_{j}>\frac{1}{j^{\lx@scalerel@obj{2}}}}2q_{j}\log j+\sum_{q_{j}\leq\frac{1}{j^{\lx@scalerel@obj{2}}}}-q_{j}\log q_{j}\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2q_{j}\log j+\sum_{q_{j}\leq\frac{1}{j^{\lx@scalerel@obj{2}}}}(-q_{j}2\log j-q_{j}\log q_{j})\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2q_{j}\log j+\sum_{q_{j}\leq\frac{1}{j^{\lx@scalerel@obj{2}}}}\frac{1}{j^{2}}\left(j^{2}q_{j}\log\frac{1}{q_{j}j^{2}}\right)\,.\end{split}

(17)

Since the function
x\log\frac{1}{x}
is bounded above by
\frac{1}{\e}
and

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{\lx@scalerel@obj{2}}}{6}
, we get

\ent({\mu}_{E})<\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}q_{j}(2\log j+\log n_{j})+1.

(18)

We now turn our attention to
\int{\varphi}_{E}{\,\mathrm{d}}{\mu}_{E}
. Recall that for
x\in Q_{j}

we have
{\varphi}_{E}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\varphi^{+}(\sigma^{i}x)
. We know that
x_{1}\dots x_{j}
is
admissible in
X_{0}^{+}
, but
x_{1}\cdots x_{j+1}
is not admissible in
X_{0}^{+}
since
x_{j}x_{j-}\notin{\mathscr{L}}(X_{0}^{+})
.
Therefore,
\dist(x,X_{0}^{+})=2^{-1}
and
\dist(\sigma^{i}p(x),X_{0}^{+})\geq 2^{-j+i}
for
0<i<j
. Hence
\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\varphi(\sigma^{i}x)\leq-a_{1}-a_{j-1}-\dots-a_{2}=-\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{i}
.
This gives

\int{\varphi}_{E}{\,\mathrm{d}}{\mu}_{E}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{Q_{j}}{\varphi}_{E}{\,\mathrm{d}}{\mu}_{E}\leq-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(q_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{i}\right).

(19)

Combining the last inequality with the entropy estimate (
18
), and using that
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}q_{j}=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\ent({\mu}_{E})+\int{\varphi}_{E}{\,\mathrm{d}}{\mu}_{E}

\displaystyle<\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}q_{j}\Big(2\log j+\log n_{j}+1-\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}jq_{j}\left(\frac{2\log j}{j}+\frac{\log n_{j}}{j}+\frac{1}{j}-\frac{1}{j}\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{i}\right)\,.

Since
n_{j}
is the number of words of length
j
in
X_{0}^{+}
, it follows from Perron-Frobenius theorem that
\ent_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\rm top}}(X_{0}^{+})\leq\frac{\log n_{j}}{j}\leq\ent_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\rm top}}(X_{0}^{+})+\frac{c}{j}
for some constant
c>0
.
Hence, taking
a_{i}=\frac{2(c+2)}{i}
and using that
\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{1}{i}>\frac{1}{2}+\log j
, we obtain

\displaystyle\ent({\mu}_{E})+\int{\varphi}_{E}{\,\mathrm{d}}{\mu}_{E}

\displaystyle<\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}jq_{j}\left(\frac{2\log j}{j}+\ent_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\rm top}}(X_{0}^{+})+\frac{c+1}{j}-\frac{2(c+2)}{j}\Big(\frac{1}{2}+\log j\Big)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}jq_{j}\left(\ent_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\rm top}}(X_{0}^{+})-\frac{2(c+1)\log j+1}{j}\right)

\displaystyle<\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}jq_{j}\,\ent_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\rm top}}(X_{0}^{+})=\frac{\ent_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\rm top}}(X_{0}^{+})}{{\mu}(E)},

where equality comes from Kac’s Theorem.

Remark 6.1
.

It can be readily checked that Theorem

6.1
Properties of accumulation points of
(\mu_{\beta_{n}\varphi})_{n}

as
\beta_{n}\to\infty

Let
\varphi:\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function.
Recall that a shift-invariant measure
\mu
is maximizing for
\varphi
if

\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu\geq\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\eta
for all shift-invariant
measures
\eta
. By weak
∗
-compactness of the set of shift invariant measures and continuity of the mapping
\eta\mapsto\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\eta
,
the maximum is attained, so that
\mathrm{MM}(\varphi)
, the set of maximizing measures, is non-empty.
Let

s_{\varphi}=\sup_{\nu}\lx@scalerel@obj{\int}\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\nu\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\ent_{\varphi}=\sup\big\{\ent(\eta):\eta\in\mathrm{MM}(\varphi)\big\}.

In statistical physics,
\ent_{\varphi}
, known as the residual entropy, can also be determined as the exponential growth rate of the number of ground configurations in increasingly large boxes.
[
1
]
.

Recall also that the entropy function
\eta\mapsto\ent(\eta)
, where
\eta
runs over the set of shift-invariant measures, is
bounded between
0
and
\log|\mathcal{A}|
, and is upper semi-continuous
[
33
, Theorem 4.5.6]
.

Using the variational principle, we define the pressure function
p_{\varphi}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\beta\mapsto p_{\varphi}(\beta):=\sup_{\nu}\Big\{\ent(\nu)+\beta\lx@scalerel@obj{\int}\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\nu\Big\},

where the supremum is taken over all shift-invariant measures. Since the functional
\nu\mapsto\ent(\nu)+\beta\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\nu

is upper semi-continuous, the supremum is attained again. The measures
for which the supremum is attained are the equilibrium states for
\beta\varphi
.
As a convex function,
p_{\varphi}
has left and right derivatives
p^{\prime}_{\varphi,-}(\beta)
and

p^{\prime}_{\varphi,+}(\beta)
at each
\beta>0
, and

p^{\prime}_{\varphi,-}(\beta)\leq\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\beta\varphi}\leq p^{\prime}_{\varphi,-}(\beta)\text{ for any $\mu_{\beta\varphi}\in\mathrm{ES}(\beta\varphi)$}.

(22)

(When
p^{\prime}_{\varphi,-}(\beta)=p^{\prime}_{\varphi,+}(\beta)
, that is, when the pressure function is differentiable at
\beta
, we thus have

p^{\prime}_{\varphi}(\beta)=\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\beta\varphi}
.)
These facts follow from a combination of
[
27
, Chapter 16]
and
[
46
, Theorems 3.4 and 3.12]
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\varphi:\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function. We have the following facts:

(i)

\mathrm{ZTA}(\varphi)\subseteq\mathrm{MM}(\varphi)
, and if
\mu\in\mathrm{ZTA}(\varphi)
then

\ent(\mu)=\sup\{\ent(\nu):\nu\in\mathrm{MM}(\varphi)\}
.

(ii)

The pressure function has a slant asymptote. More precisely,

\lim_{\beta\to+\infty}\big(\,p_{\varphi}(\beta)-(s_{\varphi}\beta+\ent_{\varphi})\big)=0.

Proof.

Firstly, if
\eta\in\mathrm{MM}(\varphi)
, the variational principle yields the bounds

h(\eta)+\beta s_{\varphi}\leq p(\beta\varphi)\leq\log|\mathcal{A}|+\beta s_{\varphi}.

(23)

Now let
\mu^{*}\in\mathrm{ZTA}(\varphi)
and let
\beta_{n}\to\infty
and
(\mu_{n})
be such that

\mu_{n}
is an equilibrium state for
\beta_{n}\varphi
and
\mu_{n}\leadsto\mu^{*}
.
By (
23
),
\int\beta_{n}\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu_{n}+\ent(\mu_{n})\geq\beta_{n}s_{\varphi}+\ent(\eta)

for all
n
. Dividing by
\beta_{n}
and taking the limit, we obtain
\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu^{*}\geq s_{\varphi}
,
so that
\mu^{*}\in\mathrm{MM}(\varphi)
.
That is, we have shown
\mathrm{ZTA}(\varphi)\subseteq\mathrm{MM}(\varphi)
.

Now we prove that, if
\mu^{*}\in\mathrm{ZTA}(\varphi)
then it has the maximal entropy over the set of
maximizing measures for
\varphi
. Along the way, we will
also prove that
\lim_{\beta\to+\infty}\big(\,p_{\varphi}(\beta)-s_{\varphi}\beta\big)=\ent_{\varphi}
, which will complete the proof of statement (ii).
Define the auxiliary function
g_{\varphi}(\beta):=p_{\varphi}(\beta)-s_{\varphi}\beta
(for
\beta>0
). From
(
23
), we see

g_{\varphi}(\beta)\geq\ent(\eta),\;\forall\beta>0.

(24)

We can compute the left derivative of
g_{\varphi}
to show that it is a decreasing function. Indeed,

g^{\prime}_{\varphi,-}(\beta)=p^{\prime}_{\varphi,-}-s_{\varphi}\leq\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\beta\varphi}-s_{\varphi}\leq 0.

Thus, the limit of
g_{\varphi}(\beta)
as
\beta\to\infty
exists, so using (
24
), we see

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}g_{\varphi}(\beta)\geq\ent(\eta).

(25)

Taking a supremum of (
25
) over
\eta\in\mathrm{MM}(\varphi)
, we see

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}g_{\varphi}(\beta)\geq\sup\{\ent(\eta)\colon\eta\in\mathrm{MM}(\varphi)\}.

(26)

Let
\alpha>0
and let
\mu_{\alpha\varphi}
be any equilibrium state for
\alpha\varphi
,
We see from the variational principle and the definition of
s_{\varphi}
that

g_{\varphi}(\alpha)=\ent(\mu_{\alpha\varphi})+\alpha\Big(\,\lx@scalerel@obj{\int}\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\alpha\varphi}-s_{\varphi}\Big)\leq\ent(\mu_{\alpha\varphi}),

so that since
g_{\varphi}
is decreasing,

\ent(\mu_{\alpha\varphi})\geq\lim_{\beta\to\infty}g_{\varphi}(\beta)\text{ for all $\alpha$.}

Let
\mu^{*}\in\mathrm{ZTA}(\varphi)
and let
\beta_{n}\to\infty
and
(\mu_{n})
be a sequence of equilibrium states for
\beta_{n}\varphi

such that
\mu_{n}\leadsto\mu^{*}
.
By upper semi-continuity of the entropy function we deduce that

\ent(\mu^{*})\geq\limsup_{n}\ent(\mu_{\beta_{n}\varphi})\geq\lim_{\beta\to\infty}g_{\varphi}(\beta)

(27)

Since we have shown
\mu^{*}\in\mathrm{MM}(\varphi)
, combining (
26
) with (
27
), we see

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}g_{\varphi}(\beta)\geq\sup\{\ent(\eta)\colon\eta\in\mathrm{MM}(\varphi)\}\geq\ent(\mu^{*})\geq\lim_{\beta\to\infty}g_{\varphi}(\beta).

This finishes the proof of statement (ii) and the second part of statement (i).
∎

6.2
When the pressure function is affine on a segment

The following proposition provides insight into the affine parts of the pressure function graph.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\varphi\colon\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function. Then, for any
\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}
, the following assertions are equivalent:

1.

\mathrm{ES}(\beta_{1}\varphi)\cap\mathrm{ES}(\beta_{2}\varphi)\neq\emptyset
;

2.

p_{\varphi}
is affine on
\left[\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\right]
;

3.

\mathrm{ES}(\beta\varphi)=\mathrm{ES}(\beta_{1}\varphi)\cap\mathrm{ES}(\beta_{2}\varphi)
for all
\beta\in(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
.

Proof.

Suppose (
1
) holds and let
\mu\in\mathrm{ES}(\beta_{1}\varphi)\cap\mathrm{ES}(\beta_{2}\varphi)

and
s\in(0,1)
so that
(1-s)\beta_{1}+s\beta_{2}\in(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
.
Then since
p_{\varphi}
is convex,
p_{\varphi}((1-s)\beta_{1}+s\beta_{2})\leq(1-s)p_{\varphi}(\beta_{1})+sp_{\varphi}(\beta_{2})
.
On the other hand,
p_{\varphi}((1-s)\beta_{1}+s\beta_{2})\geq\ent(\mu)+((1-s)\beta_{1}+s\beta_{2})\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu=(1-s)p_{\varphi}(\beta_{1})+sp_{\varphi}(\beta_{2})
, so that
p_{\varphi}
is affine on
[\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]
as claimed
and
\mu\in ES(((1-s)\beta_{1}+s\beta_{2})\varphi)
.

Now suppose that
p_{\varphi}
is affine on
[\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]
and let
\beta=(1-s)\beta_{1}+s\beta_{2}

with
0<s<1
and
\mu\in\mathrm{ES}(\beta\varphi)
. Then
p_{\varphi}^{\prime}(\beta)=\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu
, so that

\ent(\mu)+t\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu=\ent(\mu)+\beta\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu+(t-\beta)\int\varphi{\,\mathrm{d}}\mu=p_{\varphi}(\beta)+(t-\beta)p_{\varphi}^{\prime}(\beta)=p_{\varphi}(t)
for all
t\in[\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]
. It follows that

\mu\in\mathrm{ES}(\beta_{1}\varphi)\cap\mathrm{ES}(\beta_{2}\varphi)
. Conversely, if
\mu\in\mathrm{ES}(\beta_{1}\varphi)\cap\mathrm{ES}(\beta_{2}\varphi)

the first part implies that
\mu\in\mathrm{ES}(\beta\varphi)
.

The implication (
3
) implies (
1
) is trivial.
∎

6.3
Proof of Proposition
2.3

By Proposition
6.3
,

p_{\varphi}
is affine on
[\,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}]

if and only if
\mathrm{ES}(\beta\varphi)
is equal for all
\beta\in(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
.
If
\varphi
freezes at
\beta_{\mathrm{c}}
, by
continuity of
p_{\varphi}
, we see
p_{\varphi}
is affine on
[\,\beta_{\mathrm{c}},\infty)

but no larger interval.

For the converse, using Proposition
6.3
, if
p_{\varphi}
is affine on
[\,\beta_{\mathrm{c}},\infty)
,
then
\mathrm{ES}(\beta\varphi)=\mathrm{ES}(\beta^{\prime}\varphi)
for all
\beta,\beta^{\prime}>\beta_{\mathrm{c}}
. Meanwhile, if
\beta<\beta_{\mathrm{c}}<\beta^{\prime}
,
since
p_{\varphi}
is not affine on
[\,\beta,\beta^{\prime}]
, we see
\mathrm{ES}(\beta\varphi)\cap\mathrm{ES}(\beta^{\prime}\varphi)=\emptyset
.
Hence
\varphi
has a freezing phase transition at
\beta_{\mathrm{c}}
.

By Theorem
6.2
,
p_{\varphi}(\beta)
approaches the slant asymptote
s_{\varphi}\beta+\ent_{\varphi}

as
\beta\to\infty
, so if
p_{\varphi}
is affine
on
[\beta_{\mathrm{c}},\infty)
, in fact
p_{\varphi}(\beta)=s_{\varphi}\beta+\ent_{\varphi}
on that range.

6.4
Gibbs states have full support

For completeness, and since we were unable to find a suitable reference, we provide a proof of the following well-known result invoked in Section
4.2
.

We begin by quickly recalling the definition of a Gibbs state associated with a given interaction
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}
(see (
14
)). For a comprehensive treatment, we refer to
[
27
]
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}
denote the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
X
, which coincides with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra generated by cylinder sets. Given a subset
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we define
\mathfrak{B}_{\Lambda}
as the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the coordinate maps
x\mapsto x_{\boldsymbol{i}}
for
{\boldsymbol{i}}\in\Lambda
.

The Gibbsian specification for
\Phi
is given by

\gamma^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(x|y):=\frac{\e^{U^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(x|y)}}{Z^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(y)}

where

U^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(x|y):=\sum_{\lx@scalerel@obj{\begin{subarray}{c}\Lambda'\Subset\mathbb{Z}^d \\
\Lambda'\cap\Lambda\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}}\Phi_{\Lambda^{\prime}}(x|_{\Lambda}y|_{\Lambda^{\mathrm{c}}}),

and
Z^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(y)
is the normalization factor,
y|_{\Lambda^{\mathrm{c}}}
represents the boundary condition, and
\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
(For each
y
,
\big(\gamma^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(\cdot|y)\big)_{\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is a family of probability
kernels on
(X,\mathfrak{B})
subject to natural conditions we don’t list here.)

A probability measure
\mu
on
(X,\mathfrak{B})
is said to be a Gibbs state for
\Phi
if, for every finite subset
\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the conditional probability
\gamma^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(x|\cdot)
provides a version of

\mu\big(x|_{\Lambda}\big|\mathfrak{B}_{\Lambda^{\mathrm{c}}}\big).

Equivalently, this means that for all
B\in\mathfrak{B}
and all finite
\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we have

\mu(B)=\int\sum_{x\,\in\mathcal{A}^{\Lambda}}\gamma^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(x|y)\,\mathds{1}_{B}(x|_{\Lambda}y|_{\Lambda^{\mathrm{c}}}){\,\mathrm{d}}\mu(y).

(28)

(This is called the DLR equation – after Dobrushin, Lanford and Ruelle.) It is well known that at least one Gibbs state for
\Phi
always exists.

Finally, for
x\in X
and
n\geq 1
, we define the cylinder set centered at
x
with support on
\Lambda_{n}
as

C_{n}(x):=\big\{y\in X:y|_{\Lambda_{n}}=x|_{\Lambda_{n}}\big\}.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}
and
\mu
be a Gibbs state for
\Phi
.
Then there exists
\rho=\rho(\Phi,\mathcal{A})\in\left(0,1\right)
such that, for all

x\in X,n\geq 1
,

\mu(C_{n}(x))\geq\rho^{|\Lambda_{n}|}.

(29)

Proof.

Since
\Phi\in\mathcal{S}
, we have the bound

\big|U^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(x|y)\big|\leq|\Lambda|\|\Phi\|_{\mathcal{S}},

which is true for all
x,y\in X
, hence

\e^{-|\Lambda|\|\Phi\|_{\mathcal{S}}}\leq\e^{U^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(x|y)}\leq\e^{|\Lambda|\|\Phi\|_{\mathcal{S}}}\quad\text{and}\quad Z^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(y)\leq|\mathcal{A}|^{|\Lambda|}\e^{|\Lambda|\|\Phi\|_{\mathcal{S}}}.

Therefore

\gamma^{\Phi}_{\Lambda}(x|y)\geq|\mathcal{A}|^{-|\Lambda|}\e^{-2|\Lambda|\|\Phi\|_{\mathcal{S}}},

which by (
28
) yields

\mu(C_{n}(x))=\int\gamma^{\Phi}_{\Lambda_{n}}(x|y){\,\mathrm{d}}\mu(y)\geq|\mathcal{A}|^{-|\Lambda_{n}|}\e^{-2|\Lambda_{n}|\|\Phi\|_{\mathcal{S}}}

since
\mu(X)=1
. Letting
\rho:=|\mathcal{A}|^{-1}\e^{-2\|\Phi\|_{\mathcal{S}}}
, this concludes the proof.
∎
