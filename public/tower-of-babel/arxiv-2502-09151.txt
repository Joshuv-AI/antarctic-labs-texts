Regularization can make diffusion models more efficient
Mahsa Taheri, Johannes Lederer

Abstract
Diffusion models are one of the key architectures of generative AI. Their main drawback, however, is the computational costs. This study indicates that the concept of sparsity, well known especially in statistics, can provide a pathway to more efficient diffusion pipelines. Our mathematical guarantees prove that sparsity can reduce the input dimension’s influence on the computational complexity to that of a much smaller intrinsic dimension of the data. Our empirical findings confirm that inducing sparsity can indeed lead to better samples at a lower cost.

Introduction

Diffusion models are probabilistic generative models that generate new data similar to those they are trained on
[
50
,
24
,
52
]
.
These models have recently gained significant attention due to their impressive performance at image generation, video synthesis, text-to-image translation, and molecular design
[
12
,
25
,
46
,
64
]
.

A diffusion generative model is based on two stochastic processes:

1.

A forward process

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}_{0}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}_{1}\to\dots\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}}
that starts from a sample
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}_{0}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}
from a target data distribution
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}_{0}
(
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}_{0}\sim{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}_{0}
) and then diffuses this sample in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}
steps into pure noise
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}
(
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}},\mathbb{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}})}
).

2.

A reverse process
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widetilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widetilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}-1}\to\dots\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widetilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}}_{0}
that starts from pure noise
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widetilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}
(
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widetilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}},\mathbb{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}})}
) and then converts this noise in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}
steps into a new sample
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widetilde{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}}_{0}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}
that is similar in distribution to the target distribution.

Making the data noisy is easy.
Therefore, fitting a good reverse process is the key to successful diffusion modeling.

The three predominant formulations of diffusion models are denoising diffusion probabilistic models
(DDPM)
[
24
]
, score-based generative models (SGM)
[
50
]
, and score-based stochastic differential equations (SDE)
[
52
,
49
]
.
DDPM include two Markov chains:
a forward process that transforms data into noise, and a reverse process that recovers the data from the noise.
The objective is to train a function (usually a deep neural network) for denoising the data over time.
Sample generation then takes random Gaussian noise through the trained denoising function.
SGM, which is the setting adopted in this paper, also perturb data with a sequence of Gaussian noise but then try to estimate the score functions, the gradient of the log probability density, for the noisy data.
Sampling combines the trained scores with score-based sampling approaches like Langevin dynamics.
While DDPM and SGM focus on discrete time steps,
Score SDEs
consider infinitely many time steps or unbounded noise levels. In
Score SDEs
, the desired score functions are solutions of stochastic differential equations.
Once the desired score functions are trained, sampling can be reached using stochastic or ordinary differential equations.

Much research efforts are geared toward non-asymptotic rates of convergence, particularly in the number of steps
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T}
needed to achieve a desired level of reconstruction accuracy.
Typical measures of accuracy are Kullback–Leibler
divergence, total variation, and Wasserstein distance between the true distribution
\mathbb{Q}_{0}
and the approximated counterpart
P_{0}
.
For example, one tries to ensure
\operatorname{TV}(\mathbb{Q}_{0},P_{0})\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}
for a fixed error level
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}\in(0,\infty)
, where
\operatorname{TV}(\mathbb{Q}_{0},P_{0})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sup_{A\subset{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\mathbb{Q}_{0}(A)-P_{0}(A)|
is called the total variation
[
57
]
.
The many very recent papers on this topic highlight the large interest in this topic
[
4
,
10
,
11
,
36
,
7
,
5
,
39
,
9
,
40
]
.
Results like
[
4
, Theorem 13]
provide rates of convergence for diffusion models in terms of Wasserstein distance employing Langevin dynamics,
but they suffer from the curse of dimensionality in that the rates depend exponentially on the dimensions of the data
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}
, that is, the number of input features.
Improved convergence rates in terms of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}
are proposed by
[
7
,
39
]
, showing polynomial growth in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}
.
Recently
[
40
]
proposed a new Hessian-based accelerated sampler for the stochastic diffusion processes.
They achieve accelerated rate for the total variation convergence for
DDPMs
of the order
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}^{1.5}/{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}
for any target distributions having finite variance and assuming a uniform bound over the accuracy of the estimated score function (see our Section
5
for an overview of recent works).
While these results are a major step forward, they involve a strong dependence on the dimensionality of the data, which is problematic as images, text, and so forth are typically high dimensional. The key question is whether improving these rates with respect to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}
is possible at all.

Contribution

This paper aims to enhance the efficiency of diffusion models through the incorporation of regularization techniques commonly used in high-dimensional statistics

Thus, our research is a step forward in the whole field’s journey of improving our understanding of diffusion models and of making diffusion modeling more efficient.

2
Preliminaries of score matching and discrete-time diffusion process

In this section, we provide a brief introduction to score matching and discrete-time diffusion process.

Notations

For a vector
\bm{z}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}
, we use the notation
|\!|\bm{z}|\!|_{1}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}|z_{i}|
,
|\!|\bm{z}|\!|^{2}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}(z_{i})^{2}
,
|\!|\bm{z}|\!|_{\infty}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sup_{i\in\{1,\dots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}\}}|z_{i}|
, and
|\!|\bm{z}|\!|_{0}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}\mathbf{1}(z_{i}\neq 0)
.

2.1
Score matching

Assume a dataset
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}^{1},\dots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}\}
of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}
training data samples
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}^{i}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}
with an unknown target distribution
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}_{0}
(
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}^{i}\sim{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}_{0}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}\}
).
The goal of probabilistic generative modeling is to use the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}
to learn a model that can sample from
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}_{0}
.
The score of a probability density
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})
,
the gradient of the log-density with respect to
\bm{x}

denoted as
\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}\log{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})
,
are the key components for generating new samples from
q
.
The score network
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}
is then a neural network parameterized by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{B}}
, which will be trained to approximate the unknown score
\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}\log{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}_{0}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})
.
The corresponding objective functions for learning scores in
SGMs

[
50
]
is then based on
[
27
,
28
]

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}^{*}}\in\arg\min_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{B}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}_{0}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})}\bigl[|\!|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})-\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}\log{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}_{0}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})|\!|^{2}\bigr]\,,

(1)

which yields the parameters of a neural network

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}^{*}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})
that approximates the unknown score function
\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}\log{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}_{0}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})
.
Of course,
the objective function in (
1
) entails (i) an expectation over
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}_{0}
and (ii) the true score
\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}\log{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}_{0}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})
,
which are both not accessible in practice.
The expectation can readily be approximated by an average over the data samples
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}_{n}}
;
replacing the score needs more care
[
60
,
51
]
.
We come back to this point later in Section
2.2
by representing a time dependent form of denoising score matching
[
60
]
.
Once the score function is trained, there are various approaches to generate new samples from the target distribution
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}_{0}
employing the approximated score.
These include deterministic and stochastic samplers (see
[
39
]
for an overview), Langevin dynamics among the most popular one
[
50
]
.

2.2
Discrete-time diffusion process

Let
\bm{x}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}
be an initial data sample and
\bm{x}_{t}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}
for a discrete time step
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
be the latent variable in the diffusion process.
Let
\mathbb{Q}_{0}
be the initial data distribution, that is, the distribution belonging to the data’s density
q_{0}
, and let
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
be the marginal latent distribution in time
t
in the forward process.
We also use the notation
\mathbb{Q}_{t,t+1}
as the joint distribution over the time
t
to
t+1
and
\mathbb{Q}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathbb{Q}_{0,\dots,T}
as the overall joint distribution over the time
T
.
In the forward process, white Gaussian noise is gradually added to the data with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}=\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\bm{w}_{t}
, where
\bm{w}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}})
and
\beta_{t}\in(0,1)
captures the “amount of noise” that is injected at time step
t
and are called the noise schedule.
This can be written as the conditional distribution

\mathbb{Q}_{t|t-1}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t};\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t-1},\beta_{t}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}})\,.

An immediate result is that

\mathbb{Q}_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})=\mathcal{N}\bigr({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t};\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}},(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}}\bigl)\,,

for
\alpha_{t}=1-\beta_{t}
and
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}
.
For large enough
T
we have
\mathbb{Q}_{T}\approx\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}})
.
We also denote
q_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})
as the corresponding density of
\mathbb{Q}_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})
and that

q_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})=\int q_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})q_{0}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}})d{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}
, in which,
q_{0}
is the unknown target density for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}
.
We also assume that
\mathbb{Q}_{0}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure and so the absolute continuity is preserved for all

t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
due to the Gaussian nature of the noise.
The goal of the reverse process in diffusion models is then to generate samples (approximately) from the distribution
\mathbb{Q}_{0}

starting from the Gaussian distribution
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}})=\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}P_{T}
.
Let’s first define

\bm{u}_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\bigl({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}}\log q_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})\bigr)\,.

At each time step, we then consider the reverse process (for sampling), specifically Langevin dynamics, which can generate samples from a probability density using the true score function, as follows:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\bigl({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t})\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}}\!\log q_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})\bigr)+\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}}\bm{z}_{t}=\bm{u}_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})+\sigma_{t}\bm{z}_{t}\,,

for
\bm{z}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}})
and
\sigma_{t}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sqrt{1-\alpha_{t}/\alpha_{t}}
.
Let
P_{t}
be the marginal distribution of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}
in the true reverse process, which is the reverse process by employing the true scores
\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}}\log q_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})
, and
p_{t}
be the corresponding density.
Then, the above statement can be written as

P_{t-1|t}=\mathcal{N}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t-1};\bm{u}_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}),\sigma_{t}^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}})
.
But in practice, one does not have access to the true scores
\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}}\log q_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})
,
instead, an estimate of it namely
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}}(\cdot,t)
, which corresponds to a neural network parameterized with a tuple
\Theta\in\mathcal{B}
(tuple of weight matrices; we consider ReLU feedforward neural networks with

L
hidden layers and a total of

p
parameters), implying

\hat{\bm{u}}_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\bigl({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}+(1-\alpha_{t}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t},t)\bigr)\,.

Let
\widehat{P}_{t}
be the marginal distribution of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}
in the estimated reverse process implying
\widehat{P}_{t-1|t}=\mathcal{N}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t-1};\hat{\bm{u}}_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}),\sigma_{t}^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{I}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}})
.
For
\mathbb{Q}_{0}
absolutely continuous, we are then interested in measuring the mismatch between
\mathbb{Q}_{0}
and
\widehat{P}_{0}
through the
Kullback–Leibler divergence

\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{Q}_{0}||P_{0})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim\mathbb{Q}_{0}}\biggl[\log\frac{q_{0}(X)}{p_{0}(X)}\biggr]\geq 0\,.

3
Regularizing denoising score matching

A promising avenue for accelerating sampling in diffusion models is high-dimensional statistics
[
34
]
.
High-dimensional statistics is a branch of statistics that deals with many variables.
A key idea in high-dimensional statistics is the concept of sparsity;
broadly speaking,
it means that among those many variables, only few are relevant to a problem at hand.
There are different sparsity-related approaches in deep learning,
such as dropout
[
23
,
44
,
31
,
19
]
or explicit regularization
[
1
,
15
,
22
]
.
The latter approach adds prior functions (“penalties”, “regularization”) to the objective functions of the estimators.
These penalties push the estimators toward specific parts of the parameter space that correspond to certain assumptions, for example, sparsity in
\ell_{0}
-norm and/or
\ell_{1}
-norm
[
34
]
.
The benefits of sparsity are well-documented in regression, deep learning, and beyond
[
56
,
14
,
21
,
45
,
18
,
48
,
22
,
53
,
43
,
17
,
55
]
.
However, sparsity-inducing prior functions are abundant in statistics and machine learning,
they are rarely employed for generative models
[
42
]
.
In this paper, we examine the advantages of incorporating regularization into the objective functions of score-based diffusion models.
Additionally, we leverage techniques from empirical process theory
[
57
,
59
]
to analyze regularized objectives and to calibrate the tuning parameter.

3.1
Denoising score matching under
\ell_{1}
-regularization

Here we propose an
\ell_{1}
-regularized estimator for diffusion models, inspired by the concept of “scale regularization” in deep learning
[
54
]
. Consider the parameter space

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{B}_{1}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\bigl\{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{p}:|\!|\!|\Theta|\!|\!|_{1}\leq 1,~|\!|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t},t)|\!|_{1}~\leq~1~~~\forall{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}},t\in\{1,\dots,T\}\bigr\}\,,

(2)

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}}(\cdot,\cdot):({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}},\mathbb{N})\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}
is modeled as a neural network with two inputs, parameterized by a tuple
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}=(W_{0},\dots,W_{L})
. Here,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}
collects all the weight matrices of the network, which has
L
hidden layers and input and output dimensions in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{R}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}
.
We consider
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}}(\cdot,\cdot)
as a time-dependent score-based model approximating
\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}}\log q_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})
, which is crucial for sample generation in the backward process of diffusion models.
The parameter space
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{B}_{1}}
corresponds to sparse networks and sparse score functions, meaning that both the network outputs and the parameters are sparse.
Motivated by the denoising score matching objective, a scalable alternative to the objective function in (
1
)
[
60
]
, we define a regularized denoising score-matching estimator as

({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}_{\ell_{1}}},\hat{\kappa})\in\arg\,\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{B}_{1}}\\
\kappa\in(0,\infty)\end{subarray}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\sim\mathcal{U}_{[0,T]}\\
X_{t}\sim\mathbb{Q}_{t}\end{subarray}}|\!|\kappa{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}}(X_{t},t)-\nabla_{X_{t}}\!\log q_{t}(X_{t})|\!|^{2}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}\kappa^{2}\,,

(3)

where
\kappa\in(0,\infty)
represents the scale of the score function,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}\in[0,\infty)
is a tuning parameter that balances the penalty between scale and score matching,
and
\mathcal{U}_{[0,T]}
denotes the uniform distribution over
[0,T]
.
The fixed constraint
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{B}_{1}}
enforces
\ell_{1}
-norm regularization, while the actual regularization concerns only on the scale
\kappa\in(0,\infty)
.
Additionally, we regularize
\kappa^{2}
to simplify our proofs.
We will further elaborate in Section
A.4
on how the objective function in (
3
) can be computed in practice in terms of expectation and score functions.

Our main contribution in this paper is to theoretically and numerically demonstrate that our proposed regularized estimator in (
3
) can accelerate the sampling process of diffusion models, specifically increasing the rate of convergence in Kullback–Leibler divergence from
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}^{2}/{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}

[
39
]
or
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}^{1.5}/{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}

[
40
]
to
s^{2}/{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}
, where
s\ll{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}
.

We are now ready to introduce some assumptions and present our main theorem for our proposed estimator in (
3
).

We first set the learning rates to be used for our theory and analyses.
For sufficiently large
T
, we set the step size
\alpha_{t}
as

\displaystyle 1-\alpha_{t}\leq c\frac{\log T}{T}\,,~~\forall t\in\{1,\dots,T\}\,,

(4)

for a universal constant
c\in(0,\infty)
, which we omit in the remainder of the paper to simplify notation.
We then impose some standard assumptions on the true density function.

Assumption 3.1

(Finite second moment)

There exists a constant
M<\infty

such that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{0}\sim\mathbb{Q}_{0}}|\!|X_{0}|\!|^{2}\leq M
.

Assumption
3.1
simply states that the distribution is not excessively heavy-tailed; it is applied in the proof of our main theorem.
The assumption is standard;
see
[
5
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
40
]
, and many others.

Assumption 3.2

(Absolute continuity)

We assume that
\mathbb{Q}_{0}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure, and thus
q_{0}

exists.

We then assume that the derivatives of true log densities are regular, that is, they are bounded by a constant
B\in(0,\infty)
.

Assumption 3.3

(Regular derivatives)

For all
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
and
l\in\{1,2,\dots\}
and
\bm{a}\in[d]^{p}
such that
|\bm{a}|_{1}=p\in\{1,2,\dots\}
, it holds that

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t}\sim\mathbb{Q}_{t}}|\partial^{p}_{\bm{a}}\log q_{t}(X_{t})|^{\ell}~\leq~B~~~\text{and}~~~{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t}\sim\mathbb{Q}_{t}}|\partial^{p}_{\bm{a}}\log q_{t}\bigl(\bm{u}_{t}(X_{t})\bigr)|^{\ell}~\leq~B\,,

for a constant
B\in(0,\infty)
.

The regularity Assumption
3.3
is required for our analysis in Lemma
4.1
and also is utilized in previous works like
[
26
]
. As discussed extensively in
[
40
, Section 5]
, this assumption is relatively mild, for example for distributions with finite variance or Gaussian mixtures.
We then push an assumption over the true gradient vectors
\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}}\log q_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})
for
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, that is, assuming they can be well approximated by some sparse versions.
More precisely, we assume that only a small subset of features or directions in the high-dimensional space contributes significantly to the score functions.
Assumption
3.4
is well-motivated in high-dimensional statistics and is central to our main Theorem
3.1
.

Assumption 3.4

(Sparsity)

There is a sparsity level
s\in\{1,2,\dots\}
and an accuracy
\epsilon\in(0,\infty)
,
such that for all
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, there is an analytic auxiliary function
q^{s}_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})
and the corresponding score

\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}}\log q_{t}^{s}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})
that is
s
-sparse and
\epsilon
-accurate:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t}\sim\mathbb{Q}_{t}}|\!|\nabla_{X_{t}}\log q_{t}^{s}(X_{t})|\!|_{0}~\leq~s~~\text{and}\\
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t}\sim\mathbb{Q}_{t}}|\!|\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}}\log q_{t}(X_{t})-\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}}\!\log q^{s}_{t}(X_{t})|\!|^{2}}~\leq~\epsilon\,.

Our sparsity assumption above is formulated as an
average
over all time steps
t
.
It states that, among the many features of the space, only a small subset has a major impact on the score vectors on average, while the majority contribute only marginally (
s<{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}
).
Naturally, the sparsity level
s
may vary depending on the dataset and could grow at different rates in different contexts.
We refer the reader to the detailed discussion following Theorem
3.1
and in Section
A.3
, where we analyze the worst-case scenario for the scaling behavior of
\epsilon
relative to the rate of the tuning parameter
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}
, some simple examples that sparsity holds in practice, and empirically validation of sparsity on MNIST and CIFAR10 data.
Also, we refer to Assumption
A.1
in the supplementary material for a more relaxed version of our sparsity assumption, which can be used in place of Assumption
3.4
.

Theorem 3.1

(Non-asymptotic rates of convergence for regularized diffusion models)
.

Under the Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
,
3.3
, and
3.4
and
for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r^{*}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=C_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}\sqrt{\log(np)/n}
, our (in-sample) estimator proposed in (
3
) generates samples with

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{Q}_{0}||\widehat{P}_{0})

\displaystyle~\leq~\frac{M}{T^{2}}+\frac{1}{T}\max\{1,9(sB)^{2}\}+C_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}s^{2}B^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\log(nTp)}{n}}+\Delta_{T}(\log q,\log q^{s})

\displaystyle~~~~~~+\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{B}_{1}}\\
\kappa\in(0,\infty)\end{subarray}}\biggl\{\frac{\log T}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\!|\kappa{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}^{i},t)-\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}}\!\log q_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}^{i})|\!|^{2}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}\kappa^{2}\biggr\}\,,

where
\Delta_{T}(\log q,\log q^{s})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t}\sim\mathbb{Q}_{t}}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t-1}\sim\mathbb{Q}_{t-1|t}}[\log q_{t-1}(X_{t-1})-\log q^{s}_{t-1}(X_{t-1})]-\allowbreak{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t-1}\sim P^{s}_{t-1|t}}\allowbreak[\log q_{t-1}(X_{t-1})-\log q^{s}_{t-1}(X_{t-1})]])
,
C_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}
a constant depending on the input distribution, and

p
denotes the total number of network parameters with probability at least
1-1/n
.

Corollary 3.2

(Parametric setting)
.

Assume that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r^{*}}
and that there exists a pair
(\Theta^{*},\kappa^{*})\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{B}_{1}}\times(0,\infty)
such that
\kappa^{*}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}^{*}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}^{i},t)=\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}}\log q_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}^{i})
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
. Then, under the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
, our (in sample) estimator proposed in (
3
) generates samples with

\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{Q}_{0}||\widehat{P}_{0})~\leq~\frac{1}{T}\max\{1,9(sB)^{2}\}+C_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(nTp)}{n}}\max\{(sB)^{2},(\kappa^{*})^{2}\}+\Delta_{T}(\log q,\log q^{s})

with probability at least
1-1/n
.

Our Theorem
3.1
reveals that if the true gradient vectors
\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}}\log q_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})
can be well approximated by
s
-sparse vectors
\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}}\log q^{s}_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})
and for sufficiently large tuning parameter, the rates of convergence of diffusion models scale with
s
, where potentially
s\ll{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}
.
The term
\Delta_{T}(\log q,\log q^{s})
in the bound of Theorem
3.1
measures how close the log density
\log q_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})
is to the auxiliary log density
\log q^{s}_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})
across the entire sample space and time steps.
Note that we use the notation
P^{s}_{t}
for the distribution of the latent steps in the reverse process, utilizing the sparse scores of Assumption
3.4
(see also Section
B.1
for more details).
Following the intuition behind score matching, we argue that Assumption
3.4
also promotes closeness between these log densities.
While one might argue that the sparsity assumption for the score functions at large time steps
t
and in
3.4
may not always hold, our detailed discussion in Section
A.3
demonstrates that, due to the carefully chosen order of the tuning parameter, our estimator performs comparably to standard score matching even in the worst-case scenario—namely, when the score functions exhibit no sparsity on average. However, when some degree of sparsity is present, our method can lead to significant improvements by promoting sparse representations while still keeping the score estimation error small.
Put differently, our estimator strikes a balance between the average score estimation error and the level of sparsity. As a result, it not only achieves low estimation error but also identifies and leverages sparsity level (or scale) when it exists.
Furthermore, our empirical observations in Sections
6
and
A.3
support the practical validity of our assumptions.
Following the work of
[
29
]
, we also conjecture that the
\ell_{1}
-regularizer may serve as a useful tool for improving the training dynamics of diffusion models, although a thorough investigation is still needed.
Theorem
3.1
also directly implies a bound on the the total-variation distance between
\mathbb{Q}_{0}
and
\widehat{P}_{0}
in view of Pinsker’s inequality.
Detailed proof of Theorem
3.1
is provided in Appendix
B.2
.
Corollary
3.2
follows directly from Theorem
3.1
, so we omit its proof.

[
39
,
40
]

show that the reverse diffusion process produces a sample with error roughly at the order of
\varsigma
if
\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t}\sim\mathbb{Q}_{t}}|\!|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}(X_{t},t)-\nabla_{X_{t}}\log q_{t}(X_{t})|\!|^{2}/T\leq\varsigma
. But whether such accurate estimators are available in practice remains unclear.
For example,

[
65
, Theorem 3.5, Corollary 3.7]
upper bounds the estimation error of the score (using
n
training samples) at the order of
n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d})}
assuming the true data distribution
q_{0}
is 1.
\sigma_{0}
-sub-Gaussian and 2. in the Sobolev class of density functions with the order of smoothness
\beta\leq 2
, see also
[
61
,
13
]
.
This result highlights that the original score matching method suffers from the curse of dimensionality (note that
\beta\leq 2\ll{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}
).
Thus, regularization not only accelerate the reverse process but also help in the estimation of the scores directly—compare to
[
33
]
.

[
4
]
and
[
20
]
studied the sample complexity of score matching, providing bounds that scale as
O(d^{5/2}(B^{\prime 2}p)^{L}\sqrt{L}/\tau^{2})
and
O(d^{2}(Lp)\log B^{\prime}/\tau^{3})
, respectively. Here,
d
denotes the data dimensionality,
p
the total number of network parameters (each bounded by
B^{\prime}
), and
L
the number of hidden layers of the network. In contrast, our result in Corollary
3.2
(see also Remark
3.3
) shows a sample complexity scaling as
O(\max\{(sB)^{4},(\kappa^{*})^{4}\}\log(pn)/\tau^{2})
. This demonstrates a substantial improvement for our regularized estimator: the bounds now depend on the effective sparsity
s\ll d
and grow only logarithmically with the total number of network parameters, rather than polynomially.
More precisely, the bound can even decrease exponentially with the number of hidden layers of the network, which we omit here for simplicity; see
[
54
]
.
In fact, regularization can reduce the effective complexity of the network space under consideration, thereby improving the sample complexity of score estimation. This is consistent with the results of
[
66
]
, who establish convergence rates of order
O(d\log\mathcal{N}(\cdot,\mathcal{F})/\tau^{2})
, where
\mathcal{N}(\cdot,\mathcal{F})
quantifies the complexity of the network function class.

Remark 3.3

(Sample complexity)
.

Corollary
3.2
states that once the network space is sufficiently large (that is, there exists a pair
(\Theta^{*},\kappa^{*})\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{B}_{1}}\times(0,\infty)
such that
\kappa^{*}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}^{*}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}^{i},t)=\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}}\log q_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}^{i})
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
), the sample complexity of the regularized diffusion model increases by

O\!\left(C_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}}^{2}\frac{\log(nTp)}{\tau^{2}}\max\big\{(sB)^{4},(\kappa^{*})^{4}\big\}\right)

in order to achieve

\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{Q}_{0}\,\|\,\widehat{P}_{0})\leq\tau.

We highlight that our regularization technique is motivated by two main considerations:
First, it allows for a more focused search during sample generation by concentrating on the features
that have the greatest influence on the generated samples (see Figure
1
).
Second, as noted in
[
47
, Example 4.1]
, one of the dominant sources of error in the convergence of diffusion models
is the estimation error

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{est}}(\Theta)=\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{0}\sim q_{0}}\!\left[\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}\sim q_{t|0}}\bigl[\|s_{\Theta}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t},t)-\nabla\log q_{t|0}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}|x_{0})\|^{2}\bigr]\,dt\right],

which, in the finite-sample regime, can lead to overfitting.
Thus, regularization—being a classical and effective remedy for overfitting—naturally plays a crucial role to reduce estimation error.

Related work

The non-asymptotic rates of convergence for diffusion models established very recently
[
4
,
10
,
11
,
36
,
7
,
5
,
39
,
9
,

,

]
show the large interest in this topic and are an important step forward but do not fully explain the success of generative models either.
For example, results like
[
4
, Theorem 13]
provide rates of convergence for diffusion models in terms of Wasserstein distance employing the tools from empirical-process theory,
but they suffer from the curse of dimensionality in that the rates depend exponentially on the dimensions of the data, that is, the number of input features.
Recent works then concentrate on improving convergence guarantees to grow polynomially in the number of input features under different assumptions on the original and estimated scores (
L_{2}
-accurate score estimates, Lipschitz or smooth scores, scores with bounded moments)
[
36
,
62
,
8
,
7
,
5
,
9
,
37
,
26
]
.
For example,
[
36
]
prove a convergence guarantee in terms of total variation for
SGMs
,
which has a polynomial dependence on the number of input features if the score estimate is
L_{2}
-accurate for any smooth distribution satisfying the log-Sobelev inequality.
A very recent work by
[
39
]
proposes improved convergence rates in terms of total variation for
DDPMs
with ordinary differential equations and stochastic differential equations samplers that are proportional to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}^{2}/{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}
,
where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}
is the number of input features and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}
the error in the measure under consideration.
They assumed 1. finite support assumption, 2.
L_{2}
-accurate score estimates, and 3. accurate Jacobian matrices.

[
39
, Theorem 3]
also provides rates growing by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}^{3}/\sqrt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}}
for an accelerated ordinary differential equations samplers.

While works like
[
39
]
and
[
38
]
concentrate more on improving the rates in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}
,
[
7
]
focus on improving the rates in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}
for denoising diffusion implicit models.

[
7
]
use a specially chosen corrector step based on the underdamped Langevin diffusion to achieve their improvements, namely rates proportional to
L^{2}\sqrt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}}/{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tau}

by assuming: 1. the score function along the forward process is
L
-Lipschitz, 2. finite second moments of the data distribution, and 3.
L_{2}
-accurate score estimates.

[
5
,
3
]
then relaxed the assumptions over the data distribution and proposed the rates of convergence for DDPM proportional to
\sqrt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}^{2}/\tau}
and
\sqrt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}/\tau}
under 1. finite second moments of the data distribution, and 2.
L_{2}
-accurate score estimates.
Further research directions may also build upon
[

, who assume that the data lie on a low-dimensional linear subspace. They demonstrate that, in this setting, the convergence rates depend on the dimension of the subspace.

7
Conclusion

Our mathematical proofs (Section
3
) and empirical illustrations (Section
6
) demonstrate that regularization can reduce the computational complexity of diffusion models considerably.
Broadly speaking,
regularization replaces the dependence on the input dimension by a dependence on a much smaller intrinsic dimension.
But our findings might just be the beginning: we believe that types of regularization beyond the sparsity-inducing
\ell_{1}
-regularization applied here, such as total variation, could lead to further improvements.
Finally, exploring sparsity in more structured domains—such as wavelet or Fourier bases, where many signals are naturally sparse—offers a promising and interpretable direction for future work.

A.3
Discussion over Assumption
3.4

We consider a worst-case scenario for our estimator and examine the validity of Assumption
3.4
.
According to the statement of Theorem
3.1
, let set the tuning parameter as
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r}=r^{*}=\max(1/T,\sqrt{\log(np)/n})
, where we omit constants for simplicity here. Then, the objective function seeks a pair
(\hat{\kappa},\hat{\Theta})
such that

\displaystyle(\hat{\kappa},\hat{\Theta})\in\arg\min_{\kappa,\Theta\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{B}_{1}}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\kappa s_{\Theta}(x_{t}^{i},t)-\nabla\log q_{t}(x_{t}^{i})\right|^{2}\right)+r^{*}\kappa^{2}.

Suppose
(\kappa_{o},\Theta_{o})
is the estimator from the original score matching, i.e., it perfectly fits the true score function.
Then,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\kappa_{o}s_{\Theta_{o}}(x_{t}^{i},t)-\nabla\log q_{t}(x_{t}^{i})\right|^{2}\right)+r^{*}\kappa_{o}^{2}\approx 0.0+r^{*}\kappa_{o}^{2}.

While our estimator may still yield a smaller objective value compared to the original score matching implying

\displaystyle\inf_{\kappa,\Theta\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{B}_{1}}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\kappa s_{\theta}(x_{t}^{i},t)-\nabla\log q_{t}(x_{t}^{i})\right|^{2}\right)+r^{*}\kappa^{2}\leq r^{*}\kappa_{o}^{2}\,.

Therefore, for our estimator
({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}_{\ell_{1}}},\hat{\kappa})
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\hat{\kappa}s_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}_{\ell_{1}}}}(x_{t}^{i},t)-\nabla\log q_{t}(x_{t}^{i})\right|^{2}\right)+r^{*}\hat{\kappa}^{2}\leq r^{*}\kappa_{o}^{2}

that implies
\hat{\kappa}<\kappa_{o}
.
Note that the scale term reflects the sparsity level since it determines the radius of the optimal unit ball. Despite this reduced scale, the estimator still achieves small score estimation error:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\hat{\kappa}s_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}_{\ell_{1}}}}(x_{t}^{i},t)-\nabla\log q_{t}(x_{t}^{i})\right|^{2}\right)<r^{*}\kappa_{o}^{2}~\leq~\max\Bigl(\frac{1}{T},\frac{\sqrt{\log(np)}}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigr)\kappa_{o}^{2}\,.

These last two points show that our regularized estimator either performs comparably to the original score matching estimator (if no better
(\hat{\kappa},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}_{\ell_{1}}})
exists), or it finds a better pair with smaller scale and only a small estimation error for score functions.
In such cases, we may interpret
\hat{\kappa}s_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}_{\ell_{1}}}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}^{i},t)
as a sparse estimator for
\nabla\log q_{t}
in Assumption
3.4
, since
\hat{\kappa}<\kappa_{o}
, indicating a smaller radius for the unit ball, which gives a small estimation error for scores. Although Assumption
3.4
is stated for the expected error, not the in-sample one, this still gives a meaningful interpretation in practice. We would also like to highlight the expressive power of the network space
\kappa s_{\Theta}(\cdot)
with
\Theta\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
, which, following the original work by
[
54
]
, can approximate the entire network space
s_{\Omega}
under some assumptions, where
\Omega
denotes an unconstrained parameter space. This is related to how interpret
\hat{\kappa}s_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}_{\ell_{1}}}}(.)
as
\nabla\log q^{s}(.)
.
Our discussion above shows that our estimator may behave, in the worst case, similarly to the original score matching. However, it can perform significantly better in the sense that it induces sparsity (by finding a smaller scale
\hat{\kappa}\leq\kappa_{o}
) while still achieving a small estimation error for the score of the order of
\max(1/T,1/\sqrt{n})\hat{\kappa}^{2}
(note that
\hat{\kappa}\approx sB
).

Also, as an extreme and simple case of sparse scores one can consider a two-dimensional dataset, where the distribution along the
x
-axis is Gaussian, while along the
y
-axis it is uniform and
x
and
y
are independent. The score function is sparse, given by
\nabla\log p(x,y)=(\partial_{x}\log p(x),0)
, indicating no contribution from the
y
-direction.
This toy example can be naturally extended to more complex datasets, such as images, where includes local regularity like certain regions (e.g., background pixels) exhibit near-uniform distributions and thus contribute negligibly to the score. Such structure leads to sparsity in the score function, which is consistent with our assumption, and in fact our empirical observations already well support that.

Assumption
3.4
on toy data

We conducted an experiment to train a diffusion model for generating mixed Gaussian–Uniform data in a
d
-dimensional space.
We varied the number of Gaussian features
s
, with the remaining
d-s
dimensions drawn from a uniform distribution independent of the other features.
For each setting, we trained a diffusion model and evaluated the quality of the generated samples by computing the KL divergence between the generated and original distributions.
Results show that our regularized approach, using a fixed tuning parameter
\lambda=0.001
, not only outperforms standard score matching when the sparsity level is high (i.e., many features are uniform), but also consistently dominates standard score matching even when the sparsity level is low.

Assumption
3.4
on MNIST

We conducted an experiment on MNIST to validate the sparsity assumption for a diffusion model trained with standard score matching.
We measured score approximation error across all time steps for sparsity levels
\{600,400,200,100,20\}
, obtaining overall errors of approximately
\{0.05,0.09,0.18,0.30,1.0\}
.
Errors were also computed separately for early and late time steps: early steps gave
\{0.01,0.05,0.10,0.17,0.61\}
, while late steps gave
\{0.32,0.46,0.89,2.9,5.1\}
.
Since early steps dominate in diffusion models, these results indicate that the sparsity assumption holds well in practice, with negligible bias up to moderate sparsity levels.
Furthermore, MNIST image generation with small
T
shows even smaller bias than suggested by the above errors, likely due to score estimation and optimization effects, and our method performs well even for high sparsity.

We would note that Assumption
3.4
can also be relaxed as:

There is a sparsity level

and an accuracy
\epsilon\in(0,\infty)
,

\epsilon\leq 1/s^{a}T^{a^{\prime}}
(
a^{\prime},a^{\prime}\in(0,\infty)

such that for all

, there is an analytic auxiliary function
q^{s}_{t}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})
and the corresponding score

\nabla_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t}}\log q_{t}^{s}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{x}}_{t})

-accurate:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t}\sim\mathbb{Q}_{t}}|\!|\nabla_{X_{t}}\log q_{t}^{s}(X_{t})|\!|_{0}~\leq~s~~\text{and}\\

Collecting the arguments above, we conclude that by assuming sparsity in the score functions, we incur a bias term of order

By selecting the tuning parameter appropriately, we ensure that the trade-off between bias and variance is well balanced.

A.5
Network architecture and training settings

Our model is a U-Net architecture with 4 downsampling and 4 upsampling layers, each comprising residual blocks. The network starts with a base width of 32 channels, doubling at each downsampling step to a maximum of 256 channels, and mirrors this in the decoder. A bottleneck layer with 256 channels connects the encoder and decoder. Time information is encoded using Gaussian Fourier projections and injected into each residual block via dense layers. Group normalization is applied within the residual blocks, and channel attention mechanisms are included selectively to enhance feature representations.
For training, we used the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001, and for sampling, we employed a signal-to-noise ratio of 0.1.
We used a batch size of
128
and trained for
2000
epochs on the Butterflies dataset and less than
1000
epochs on the other datasets.

A.6
Comparison with other sparsity-inducing regularizers

We conducted additional simulations to compare the perfomance of our method vs using other sparsity-inducing regularizers.

1.

\ell_{2}
-regularization:
Although the
\ell_{2}
-norm helps control the growth of magnitudes, it does not promote sparsity by design. Our complementary simulations show that using
\ell_{2}
-regularization does not significantly speed up inference time in our experiments.

2.

Group lasso:
Our simulations show that applying group lasso on images—considering groups as
p\times p
pixel blocks (
4\times 4
)—can also improve inference time and, for some datasets like MNIST, even outperform
\ell_{1}
-regularization.

3.

Combination of norms:
Employing a combination of norms, namely group lasso plus
\ell_{1}
-norm, performs comparably to individual regularizations for the MNIST family, but we do not observe any considerable improvement over using either group lasso or
\ell_{1}
-norm alone.

A.7
Runtimes

We measured the optimization time for training the regularized and the original diffusion models on MNIST over
50
epochs using Adam on the same device. The training time for the original DFM is approximately
20
minutes in total, while the regularized version takes about
21
minutes.
Both objectives converge after about
20
epochs. This shows that adding regularization has only a minor effect on the training time. Similar behavior also observed for other datasets.

\displaystyle~~~~~+\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2(1-\alpha_{t})}|\!|\bm{u}^{s}_{t}(X_{t})-\hat{\bm{u}}_{t}(X_{t})|\!|^{2}\biggr]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha_{t})\sqrt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t}}|\!|\nabla\log q_{t}(X_{t})-\nabla\log q^{s}_{t}(X_{t})|\!|^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t}}|\!|\nabla\log q^{s}_{t}(X_{t})-\hat{\kappa}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}_{\ell_{1}}}}}(X_{t},t)|\!|^{2}}

\displaystyle~~~+\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t}}|\!|\hat{\kappa}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}_{\ell_{1}}}}}(X_{t},t)-\nabla\log q^{s}_{t}(X_{t})|\!|^{2}\,.

That implies

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}~

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t},X_{t-1}\sim\mathbb{Q}_{t,t-1}}\biggl[\log\frac{p^{s}_{t-1|t}(X_{t-1}|X_{t})}{\hat{p}_{t-1|t}(X_{t-1}|X_{t})}\biggr]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}(1-\alpha_{t})\sqrt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t}}|\!|\nabla\log q_{t}(X_{t})-\nabla\log q^{s}_{t}(X_{t})|\!|^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t}}|\!|\nabla\log q^{s}_{t}(X_{t})-\hat{\kappa}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}_{\ell_{1}}}}}(X_{t},t)|\!|^{2}}

\displaystyle~~~+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}}_{X_{t}}|\!|\hat{\kappa}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{s}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta}}_{\ell_{1}}}}}(X_{t},t)-\nabla\log q^{s}_{t}(X_{t})|\!|^{2}\,,

as desired.
