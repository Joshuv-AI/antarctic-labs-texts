Phytase modulates amino acid and mineral ion release kinetics: Ingredient-specific responses in plant protein ingredients
Authors: Javaria Waheed, Aftab Qamar, Jiaxuan Zhang, Rujie Liu, Zewei Sun, Qingzhen Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOne of the most important antinutritional factors in plant-derived feeds is phytic acid (myo-inositol hexakisphosphate, IP6), which inhibits nutrient absorption in monogastric animals through a potent association with minerals, proteins, and digestive enzymes. Although exogenous phytase is widely used to improve phosphorus availability, its role in regulating the dynamic release of amino acids and mineral ions remains poorly understood. This study investigated the effects of phytase supplementation on IP6 hydrolysis, mineral ion release, enzyme activity, and amino acid release kinetics across different plant protein ingredients using a controlled in vitro digestion model. The results showed that phytase supplementation significantly enhanced apparent IP6 hydrolysis across all ingredients, with apparent hydrolysis approaching maximal levels at 500 U/kg. This was accompanied by increased solubilization of phosphorus and calcium, with clear dose-dependent responses in canola meal and plateau effects in soybean meal and corn gluten meal. Amino acid release exhibited different ingredient-dependent kinetic patterns, characterized by rapid-plateau responses in soybean and canola meals and a bimodal release pattern in corn gluten meal. Notably, kinetic parameters of amino acid release (Dmax and k) showed weak association with IP6 hydrolysis, indicating that protein digestion is governed mainly by ingredient matrix characteristics rather than phytate degradation alone. Phytase primarily accelerated the timing of nutrient release without substantially increasing the total extent of protein hydrolysis. These results provide a mechanistic understanding of the kinetics of in vitro digestion and indicate ingredient-specific variations in nutrient release kinetics.Keywords: Phytase, IP6 hydrolysis, Amino acid release kinetics, Matrix-dependent digestion, Mineral solubility, In vitro digestion

Introduction

Optimizing protein utilization in monogastric animal nutrition is critical because of rising feed costs, environmental sustainability challenges, and the demand for improved nutrient efficiency (Costa and Prates, 2024; Sonea et al., 2023). Plant-derived protein ingredients, such as soybean meal, canola meal, and corn gluten meal, form the backbone of global feed formulations; however, their efficient use is limited due to the presence of antinutritional factors. Among these, phytic acid (myo-inositol hexakisphosphate, IP6) is the most prominent, accounting for the majority of phosphorus stored in plant tissues (EL Houssni et al., 2024). Because monogastric animals lack adequate endogenous phytase, phytic acid is poorly hydrolyzed, leading to low phosphorus availability, increased excretion, and nutritional imbalance (Sung et al., 2024; Velayudhan et al., 2025).

While traditionally considered problematic due to its strong chelating capacity for minerals such as phosphorus, calcium, zinc, and iron, recent research has demonstrated that the antinutritional effects of phytic acid extend well beyond mineral binding (Qamar et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2024). Phytic acid interacts with dietary proteins through electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding, often mediated by multivalent cations, leading to the formation of stable protein-phytate complexes. This reduces protein solubility and obstructs enzymatic hydrolysis (Sarkhel and Roy, 2022). These complexes particularly interfere with gastric digestion, where pepsin activity is essential for initiating protein denaturation and fragmentation. Consequently, phytic acid not only decreases the extent of protein digestion but also interrupts the temporal patterns of amino acid release during digestion, thereby compromising nutrient utilization efficiency (Pei et al., 2024). This dynamic disruption of protein digestion and amino acid release kinetics has emerged as an underexplored but critical challenge in monogastric nutrition.

Microbial phytases are widely used in animal nutrition because of their proven ability to improve phosphorus digestibility, enhance bone mineralization, and reduce the excretion of phosphorus (Philippi et al., 2024; Muhammad et al., 2025). These benefits have made phytase one of the most important enzymes in monogastric feeding systems, where it contributes to improved nutrient efficiency and greater sustainability. Although the nutritional benefits of phytase supplementation are well established (Derakhshan et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024), the dynamic regulation of nutrient release, particularly the temporal pattern of amino acid and mineral ion release during digestion, is not yet fully understood in phytase research. Moreover, most studies rely on endpoint measurements (Ali and Irfan, 2025; Gulizia et al., 2023), which obscure time-dependent digestion dynamics and limit understanding of how phytase influences nutrient release kinetics. Given that digestion is inherently a time-dependent process, the rate and timing of nutrient release are critical determinants of nutrient availability. However, the effects of phytase supplementation on these temporal release patterns during digestion remain unclear.

This question is particularly relevant in the context of different plant protein feed ingredients. Soybean meal, canola meal, and corn gluten meal are major protein sources in monogastric diets; however, they differ significantly in terms of phytate content, protein composition, and structural constraints affecting enzymatic digestion. Soybean meal, with intermediate phytate levels and a predominance of globular storage proteins, is generally highly digestible but often exhibits a higher amino acid release because of rapid initial hydrolysis (Wang et al., 2024). Canola meal is characterized by high phytate content and substantial lignified fiber fractions. This imposes greater barriers to proteolysis by reducing protein accessibility, increasing digesta buffering capacity, and limiting effective protease activity under gastric conditions. In contrast, corn gluten meal is dominated by hydrophobic, disulfide-rich zein proteins that form dense protein matrices, resulting in slower proteolysis and a more complex amino acid release pattern over time (Yu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023). These differences in composition and structure suggest that digestion behavior may vary among ingredients, which could impact the effects of phytase on nutrient release during digestion.

This study hypothesized that phytase-mediated IP6 hydrolysis differentially regulates dynamic release of amino acids and mineral ions during digestion. To test this, a controlled in vitro digestion model was used to quantify IP6 hydrolysis, mineral ion release (phosphorus and calcium), enzyme activities (phytase and pepsin), and amino acid release kinetics across different plant protein ingredients and phytase doses. The aim was to evaluate the effects of phytase on nutrient release kinetics and to determine whether these responses differ among protein sources with distinct compositional and structural characteristics.

Materials and methods

Feed ingredient processing

Soybean meal, canola meal, and corn gluten meal were obtained from Shandong Liuhe Feed Co., Ltd. (Jiyuan, China). A laboratory hammer mill was used for grinding, and the samples were passed through a 1 mm mesh sieve to achieve a uniform particle size. Samples were stored in airtight containers at room temperature until analysis.

Nutrient composition

AOAC (2012) procedures were used for nutrient composition analysis of soybean meal, canola meal, and corn gluten meal. Dry matter (DM), ash, crude protein, crude lipid, crude fiber, neutral detergent fiber, and acid detergent fiber were analyzed using methods 934.01, 942.05, 990.03, 945.16, 978.10, 2002.04, and 973.18, respectively. Phytate phosphorus was quantified through enzymatic hydrolysis using a commercial assay kit (Phytic Acid Assay Kit, K-PHYT; Megazyme International, Bray, Ireland) following the manufacturer's validated protocol. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and values are reported on a dry matter basis.

In vitro model for protein digestion

The method of Minekus et al. (2014), with minor modifications, was used to evaluate the in vitro digestion of soybean meal, canola meal, and corn gluten meal. The phytase used in this study was a commercial feed-grade, thermostable microbial phytase (Escherichia coli-derived 6-phytase) produced by genetically engineered microorganisms (Yinong Biotech, Co., Ltd., Suqian, Jiangsu, China). The enzyme was dissolved in 100 mmol/L sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.5) to prepare a phytase stock solution with a final concentration of 100 U/mL. One unit was defined as the release of 1 μmol inorganic phosphate per minute from 5 mmol/L sodium phytate at pH 5.5 and 37 °C. To achieve supplementation levels of 500, 1000, and 1500 U/kg, the stock solution was added to the digestion mixture, while a phytase-free control (0 U/kg) received an equal volume of buffer to ensure that observed effects were attributable to the enzyme.

Approximately 1000 mg of ground sample was incubated with pepsin (5 mg/mL; P-7000, Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) in 0.05 mol/L HCl containing guar gum (0.05 g) at pH 2.0 and 37 °C for 30 min under continuous agitation to simulate gastric digestion. Subsequently, samples were incubated in 10 mL of 0.25 mol/L sodium acetate buffer containing pancreatin (0.7 g; P-7545, Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany), amyloglucosidase (0.05 mL; A-7095, Sigma-Aldrich, Milan, Italy), invertase (3 mg; I-4504, Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany), and bile salts (10 mM; B-8631, Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) to simulate small intestinal digestion at pH 6.86 and 37 °C. Enzymatic reactions were terminated by heating the samples in boiling water for 4 min, followed by rapid cooling on ice. Samples were stored at −20 °C until analysis (Fig. 1). Free amino acids were measured in 0.5 mL aliquots using a colorimetric assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China), and results were expressed as μmol/mL. Net amino acid release was calculated by subtracting the concentration at 0 min from each time point. Using the final digestion time point (180 min) as a reference, cumulative percentage release and percentage increments between adjacent time points were calculated. Apparent IP6 hydrolysis in feed ingredients

IP6 in feed ingredients and digesta supernatants was analyzed using a commercial assay kit (K-PHYT, Megazyme, Ireland) following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. Ground samples were extracted with 0.66 M HCl (20 mL) under continuous stirring for 3 h at room temperature. The extracts were centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected for inorganic phosphate analysis after enzymatic hydrolysis. Digesta samples obtained after 180 min of in vitro digestion were centrifuged (12000 × g, 15 min), and the supernatant was used directly for analysis. Released phosphate was calculated colorimetrically at 655 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy HTX, Agilent, USA). Determination of mineral ions concentration

Samples were centrifuged at 12000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C after 180 min of in vitro digestion. The resulting digesta supernatants were diluted appropriately for analysis and used to determine soluble calcium (Ca2+) and inorganic phosphate (Pi) concentrations using commercial colorimetric assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). Absorbance was measured at 610 nm for Ca2+ and 660 nm for Pi. The concentrations were measured from standard curves and reported as mmol/L.

Enzyme activity assays

Digesta supernatants collected after the gastric (30 min) and intestinal (180 min) phases were used to determine pepsin and residual phytase activity, respectively. Pepsin activity was measured using a commercial assay kit (G1210W, Geruisi Bio, China), whereas phytase activity was determined by quantifying inorganic phosphate released from a phytic acid substrate according to the method of Engelen et al. (2001). Appropriately diluted supernatants were incubated with the respective substrates, and reactions were terminated using specific colorimetric reagents. Absorbance was recorded at 680 nm for pepsin and 655 nm for phytase. Activities were calculated from standard curves and expressed as U/mL.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data were first tested for normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (Levene's test). Differences among treatments were analyzed using two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. All in vitro digestion experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are reported as mean ± standard deviation. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess relationships among kinetic parameters of amino acid release (Dmax and k), mineral ion concentrations, IP6 hydrolysis, and enzyme activities. Correlation matrices were visualized as heatmaps generated using OriginPro software (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

IP6 hydrolysis

Apparent IP6 hydrolysis was significantly influenced by ingredient, phytase dose, and their interaction (p < 0.05). In the absence of exogenous phytase, IP6 hydrolysis differed among ingredients, reflecting their distinct phytate contents and matrix characteristics (Table 1). Phytase supplementation significantly increased IP6 hydrolysis across all ingredients compared to the control (P < 0.05; Fig. 2), with apparent hydrolysis approaching maximal levels at 500 U/kg. Increasing phytase inclusion to 1000 or 1500 U/kg did not further enhance hydrolysis, indicating a clear plateau response. The observed plateau suggests that substrate availability or enzyme-substrate interactions limit further hydrolysis at higher phytase doses under the experimental conditions. Furthermore, the significant ingredient × phytase interaction indicates that the magnitude of the phytase response varied among ingredients, consistent with their differences in phytate content and structural accessibility.

Mineral ions concentration

Both soluble phosphate (Pi) and calcium (Ca2+) concentrations were significantly affected by ingredient type and phytase inclusion level (p < 0.05), reflecting differences in phytate-mineral complex composition among ingredients. The effects of phytase supplementation on soluble phosphate (Pi) and calcium (Ca2+) concentrations are shown in Fig. 3. Phytase addition significantly increased soluble phosphate and calcium concentration across all test ingredients (p < 0.05). In soybean meal, both phosphate and calcium concentrations increased significantly with phytase supplementation, but no further improvement was observed beyond 1000 U/kg. In canola meal, a clear dose-dependent response was observed, with both ions increasing progressively across all supplementation levels and reaching maximum values at 1500 U/kg. In corn gluten meal, phosphate concentrations increased significantly across all supplementation levels up to 1500 U/kg, whereas calcium concentration was maximized at 1000 U/kg and plateaued thereafter. The ingredient × phytase interaction was significant (p < 0.05), confirming that mineral release responses to phytase supplementation were ingredient-dependent.

In vitro free amino acid release kinetics

The main effects of ingredient, phytase dose, and their interaction on cumulative amino acid release were all statistically significant (p < 0.05). Canola meal showed higher release than soybean meal and corn gluten meal (Fig. 4). Phytase increased amino acid release, but the response differed among the ingredients. These patterns were supported by incremental amino acid release trends (Fig. 4B–D, and F).

Total free amino acid release kinetics of soybean meal (A and B), corn gluten meal (C and D), and canola meal (E and F) during in vitro digestion under varying phytase levels (n = 3). Panels A, C, and E show cumulative amino acid release, whereas panels B, D, and F show incremental amino acid release between adjacent digestion time points.

In soybean meal (Fig. 4A), amino acid release increased rapidly during the first 30 min and then stabilized. Phytase accelerated early release, with the highest rates at 1500 U/kg. The differences among the doses were minimal at later stages. This pattern reflects a rapid-plateau digestion phenotype. Corn gluten meal (Fig. 4C) exhibited a bimodal release pattern without a clear plateau. Release increased gradually and remained variable over time as reflected by incremental release patterns (Fig. 4D). Phytase improved early release, but the overall pattern remained irregular. Canola meal (Fig. 4E) showed rapid early release followed by a gradual decline. Phytase increased early release, with a slightly more extended release phase than soybean meal. This indicates a rapid-plateau phenotype with an extended early phase. Overall, soybean meal and canola meal exhibited rapid-plateau kinetics, whereas corn gluten meal displayed a bimodal digestion phenotype.

Significant main effects of ingredient and phytase dose were detected for all kinetic parameters (p < 0.05), while the interaction was significant for ΔD and k (p < 0.05) but not for Dmax and D0 (p > 0.05). Canola meal showed the highest Dmax, whereas corn gluten meal had the lowest k values, indicating slower digestion. Soybean meal showed intermediate behavior (Table 2). Within soybean meal, increasing phytase reduced ΔD but increased k, indicating faster but smaller incremental release. In canola meal, Dmax remained stable, whereas k increased slightly at higher doses. In corn gluten meal, ΔD was highest at 500 U/kg, whereas k peaked at 1000 U/kg. Overall, phytase primarily influenced the rate of amino acid release (k) rather than the final extent of digestion (Dmax), and the significant interaction for ΔD and k confirms that the kinetic response to phytase differed among ingredients.

Across all ingredients, 500 U/kg resulted in significantly higher pepsin activity than 1000 and 1500 U/kg (p < 0.05). At higher phytase inclusion levels (≥1000 U/kg), pepsin activity was reduced. Correlation analysis of nutrient release, phytate hydrolysis, and enzyme activities

Pearson's correlation analysis revealed a strong positive relationship between phytase activity and IP6 hydrolysis (r = 0.86, p < 0.001), confirming that phytase effectively degrades phytic acid (Fig. 7). Calcium release was also positively correlated with phytase activity (r = 0.64, p < 0.05) and IP6 hydrolysis (r = 0.53). In contrast, Dmax showed weak associations with phytase activity and IP6 hydrolysis, whereas k was strongly correlated with calcium release (r = 0.74, p < 0.01) and moderately correlated with phytase activity (r = 0.58, p < 0.05). Together with the kinetic results, these findings show that phytase modulates digestion dynamics rather than total protein hydrolysis, with responses largely driven by ingredient-specific matrix characteristics and digestion phenotype.

Discussion

Phytic acid is the major storage form of phosphorus in plant-based feed ingredients and can limit mineral and protein utilization in monogastric animals. Its concentration varies widely among ingredients depending on botanical origin and processing (Chen and Xu, 2023). In this study, canola meal contained the highest phytic acid (2.52 g/100 g) and phytate-P (0.71 g/100 g), corn gluten meal showed intermediate levels (1.68 g/100 g; 0.47 g/100 g), and soybean meal had the lowest (1.30 g/100 g; 0.36 g/100 g). These differences (Table 1) provide the basis for the stronger phytase responses observed in subsequent mineral and digestion results. These findings are in agreement with earlier reports that oilseed meals, particularly canola, typically accumulate more phosphorus in the form of phytate compared with cereal by-products such as corn gluten meal (Grootswagers et al., 2024; Song et al., 2023).

Phytic acid hydrolysis was significantly enhanced by phytase supplementation, with a high degree of apparent hydrolysis observed at 500 U/kg across soybean meal, canola meal, and corn gluten meal, and no further benefits were detected at higher inclusion levels. This plateau indicates that substrate accessibility, not enzyme dose, limits further hydrolysis. The lack of additional IP6 disappearance at 1000 and 1500 U/kg suggests that the readily soluble phytate fraction was largely depleted at 500 U/kg, while any residual phytate was likely associated with insoluble protein-phytate or mineral-phytate complexes that are less susceptible to enzymatic attack, consistent with previous in vitro observations (Riviere et al., 2021). Phytase efficacy is influenced by pH, substrate accessibility, and feed matrix characteristics, and continued stepwise dephosphorylation to lower inositol phosphate esters may occur without further reductions in measurable phytic acid, as shown in detailed in vitro studies tracking inositol phosphate intermediates during digestion (Hirvonen et al., 2019). Similar matrix-related constraints on phytase efficacy have been reported for soybean and rapeseed meals, where phytate localization and protein binding limit complete dephosphorylation despite increasing enzyme doses (Riviere et al., 2021). From a practical perspective, these results show that moderate enzyme levels are sufficient to maximize soluble IP6 hydrolysis under controlled laboratory conditions, whereas higher doses mainly promote downstream dephosphorylation rather than additional reductions. This aligns with industry practices that favor moderate phytase inclusion to balance efficacy and cost-effectiveness (Giannenas et al., 2017; Imranuzzaman et al., 2025).

Phytase supplementation significantly increased soluble phosphorus and calcium concentrations across all ingredients (Fig. 3), confirming the effective breakdown of phytate-mineral complexes. The dose-dependent response was most prominent in canola meal, likely due to its higher phytate content and greater mineral-binding capacity. In soybean meal and corn gluten meal, mineral release plateaued at higher phytase levels, consistent with the observed plateau in IP6 hydrolysis. This suggests that once the accessible phytate fraction is degraded, the remaining mineral-bound complexes are less responsive to the addition of further enzymes. These findings are supported by Osunbami and Adeola (2024), who reported substantial improvements in standardized ileal phosphorus digestibility in phytase-supplemented soybean meal-based diets fed to broiler chickens. Furthermore, Walker et al. (2025) recently demonstrated that enzyme supplementation containing phytase significantly improved both phosphorus retention and ileal digestibility in laying hens. This supports our observation that phytase effectively releases bound phosphorus from the feed matrix, transforming it into a soluble form available for absorption. This interaction highlights the importance of balanced mineral formulations in phytase supplementation strategies. Collectively, mineral ion release data reveal that phytase-mediated solubilization of calcium and phosphate modifies digestive pH and osmotic environment, enhancing protein unfolding and protease accessibility.

The present study demonstrates that phytase supplementation significantly modulates protein digestion dynamics among different protein ingredients, providing novel mechanistic understandings into the patterns of amino acid release during in vitro digestion. This is supported by the increased early-phase release rates and steeper initial curves (Fig. 4). Our findings align with recent advances in understanding the multifaceted role of phytase beyond traditional phosphorus release, extending to direct effects on protein digestibility and enzyme efficiency (Passos et al., 2023; Sens et al., 2021). The dose-dependent improvements observed in this study support the findings of Peñuela-Sierra et al. (2024) and Ali and Irfan (2025), confirming that enzyme supplementation can enhance the efficiency of protein utilization in broiler diets.

In soybean meal, phytase supplementation resulted in a pronounced increase in early-phase amino acid release, with the highest rate observed at 1500 U/kg within 15 min. This rapid response is consistent with the structural characteristics of soybean proteins, which are relatively soluble and accessible to enzymatic hydrolysis. Phytase-mediated disruption of protein-phytate complexes likely enhances protein unfolding and enzyme accessibility, leading to increased hydrolysis. Similar effects were reported by Han et al. (2024), who showed that phytase treatment increased protein solubility and shifted the isoelectric point, resulting in faster digestion. These findings provide a mechanistic explanation for the higher amino acid release kinetics observed in our soybean meal treatments. This rapid burst profile reflects the patterns reported by Wang et al. (2024) and is supported by modeling from Cargo-Froom et al. (2024), confirming that leguminous globulins (glycinin) are highly susceptible to phytase-mediated unlocking. Furthermore, Yao et al. (2024) also found faster amino acid release in soybean meal than in other protein sources using computer-controlled digestion systems, consistent with the present findings. The rapid-plateau kinetics observed in this study indicate that readily digestible protein fractions were quickly depleted, after which further hydrolysis was limited by the availability of substrate.

Canola meal exhibited a similar rapid-plateau pattern, although the decline in amino acid release occurred more gradually than in soybean meal. The higher initial phytate content and more complex fiber-rich matrix likely restricted protein accessibility, resulting in a slightly extended early digestion phase. Phytase significantly increased initial release rates, indicating improved accessibility of protein fractions bound within the matrix. However, the persistence of structural barriers, including lignified fiber components and protein-phytate interactions, likely limited sustained digestion, leading to convergence of release across treatments at later time points. These findings highlight the importance of matrix structure in modulating phytase efficacy. This supports the findings of Li et al. (2024), who emphasized the importance of protein-source-specific factors in predicting amino acid digestibility.

In contrast, corn gluten meal displayed a distinct bimodal release pattern, characterized by an initial increase followed by a secondary phase of amino acid release. This behavior reflects the structural properties of zein proteins, which form dense, hydrophobic aggregates that limit enzyme accessibility. The initial phase likely represents the hydrolysis of more accessible protein fractions, whereas the later phase corresponds to delayed proteolysis of the resistant zein-rich core. Phytase supplementation enhanced early amino acid release, suggesting partial disruption of phytate-associated barriers; however, the overall digestion pattern remained constrained by matrix structure. Similar slow and multi-phase digestion profiles have been reported for corn-derived proteins (Bryan et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2025). This response is also consistent with the weak gastric and strong intestinal digestion profiles reported by Fan et al. (2025) and suggests that phytase accelerates the onset of protein release in corn gluten meal. This two-stage release pattern fundamentally differs from the rapid-plateau kinetics observed in soybean and canola meals, reflecting the more resistant protein matrix of corn gluten meal, which requires sustained enzymatic exposure.

Particle size is an important determinant of enzymatic digestion because it governs the effective surface area available for protease and phytase access to substrate binding sites. In this study, all ingredients were standardized to pass through a 1 mm sieve, which is commonly used in controlled in vitro digestion systems (Minekus et al., 2014). However, a 1 mm particle size may still impose physical restrictions on enzyme penetration, particularly in structurally dense matrices such as corn gluten meal. Reducing particle size generally increases the surface-area-to-volume ratio, enhances enzyme accessibility to internal phytate-protein complexes, and may accelerate digestion kinetics, reflected as higher rate constants (k). Thus, the comparatively low k values observed for corn gluten meal may partly reflect restricted enzymatic penetration into zein-rich particles in addition to the intrinsic resistance of the protein matrix. Similarly, the plateau in apparent IP6 hydrolysis observed at 500 U/kg may not solely indicate enzyme-substrate saturation, but also a substrate accessibility ceiling imposed by particle size. In contrast, finer grinding of fiber-rich matrices such as canola meal could expose additional phytate-protein complexes and potentially amplify phytase-mediated effects on mineral release and amino acid digestion kinetics. Although particle size effects were not directly evaluated in the present study, these observations suggest that physical matrix accessibility contributes to ingredient-specific differences in phytase responsiveness and digestion rate constants.

The non-linear response of residual pepsin activity to phytase supplementation observed in the present study is consistent with earlier mechanistic evidence showing that phytase influences gastric proteolysis primarily through matrix-mediated effects rather than direct enzyme inhibition. Selle et al. (2023) demonstrated that intact phytic acid and higher inositol phosphates markedly suppress pepsin activity by forming insoluble phytate-protein complexes under acidic conditions, whereas progressive phytate hydrolysis alleviates this inhibition. Similarly, Soares et al. (2024) reported that phytase supplementation improved protein digestibility and reduced phytate-induced interference with gastric protease activity in poultry diets, although responses varied with enzyme dose and dietary matrix. In vivo studies by Rehman et al. (2025) further showed that phytate reduced gastric pepsin activity, whereas phytase effects depended on substrate availability and mineral balance rather than direct modulation of pepsin catalytic function.

Higher phytase doses (≥1000 U/kg) were associated with increased phytate degradation and changes in digestion behavior. These conditions can enhance protein solubility and speed up the hydrolysis of substrates. Consequently, the reduced residual pepsin activity could be due to the depletion of the substrate at the point of sampling rather than the loss of enzyme productivity. This pattern is consistent with the amino acid release kinetics, in which increased doses of phytase enhanced the release in the initial stages, but not at later stages. Increased calcium and phosphate release at higher doses may also alter the gastric environment and influence protein structure and enzyme access. Overall, the moderate phytase dosage was linked with higher measurable pepsin activity, whereas the higher doses seem to alter the digestion kinetics by indirect and matrix-based mechanisms.

Phytase supplementation was associated with parallel increases in protein digestion and mineral solubilization, rather than strict temporal synchronization between amino acid and mineral release. Such modifications can probably be connected with the interference of phytate complexes and greater accessibility of substrates. The decrease in soluble phytate, as well as the increase in phosphate and calcium levels, can change the physicochemical characteristics of the digesta and facilitate the unfolding of proteins and accessibility of enzymes. The correlation analysis further describes these relationships. Phytase activity was strongly associated with IP6 hydrolysis and calcium release, but showed a weak relationship with Dmax, indicating limited linkage with the total extent of protein hydrolysis. In contrast, the rate constant (k) was moderately associated with calcium release and phytase activity, suggesting that mineral dynamics contribute to the rate of digestion.

The study has some limitations related to the analytical approach and digestion model. The measurement of free amino acids does not differentiate between individual amino acid release patterns. As amino acids differ in their digestion behavior, the results represent overall trends rather than specific amino acid responses. The other limitation is the comparatively short length of digestion (180 min). This time is an indication of early digestion and does not necessarily indicate full hydrolysis of proteins. The restriction would be more appropriate with the structurally resistant ingredients like corn gluten meal. Thus, the results are mainly a description of the kinetics of digestion during the measured time intervals, not overall digestibility. These limitations can be overcome in future studies by using amino acid-specific analyses, long-term digestion, and in vivo validation to assess nutrient interactions further and to develop ingredient-specific enzyme strategies.

Conclusion

The phytase supplementation enhanced IP6 hydrolysis and mineral solubilization among ingredients. The parameters of amino acid release were not strongly correlated with the phytate degradation, which demonstrates that the process of protein digestion is strongly dependent on the properties of the ingredient matrix. Inclusion of moderate levels of phytase was adequate to attain effective phytate degradation, while ingredient-specific responses highlight the need for targeted enzyme application strategies. Overall, the study offers a mechanistic understanding of in vitro digestion kinetics and ingredient-specific nutrient release patterns.
