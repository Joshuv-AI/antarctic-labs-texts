Fabrication of synergistic calix[4]arene–PVC–MWCNT–graphite films for fast and sustainable adsorption of dye pollution
Authors: Reza Dadashi, Mojtaba Nouri, Morteza Bahram, Karim Akbari Dilmaghani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The increasing discharge of dye pollutants, particularly methylene blue (MB), into aquatic systems represents a critical environmental challenge due to their persistence, toxicity, and resistance to biodegradation. In this study, we report the synthesis and characterization of a novel flexible adsorbent film composed of tetraformyl calix[4]arene, polyvinyl chloride (PVC), multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), and graphite (TfCPMG). The calixarene moieties introduce highly active formyl functionalities and aromatic cavities, enabling strong electrostatic and π–π interactions with cationic dyes, while the MWCNT and graphite components provide structural reinforcement, conductivity, and additional adsorption sites. Structural characterization using FT-IR, Raman, BET, and FE-SEM confirmed the successful integration of these components and revealed a heterogeneous, porous surface favorable for adsorption. Adsorption performance was systematically evaluated by examining the effects of pH, adsorbent dosage, contact time, and initial MB concentration. The TfCPMG film exhibited an optimal removal efficiency of 80% for a 5 ppm MB solution at pH 9, with rapid kinetics reaching equilibrium within 10 min. Notably, the adsorbent maintained > 78.5% removal efficiency after ten adsorption–desorption cycles, demonstrating excellent reusability and stability. Comparative analysis against previously reported adsorbents highlights the superior efficiency, low cost, and operational simplicity of the fabricated film. These findings establish the TfCPMG flexible film as a high-performance, scalable, and sustainable adsorbent, offering a promising solution for practical wastewater treatment and dye pollution remediation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28147-7.

Introduction

The increasing presence of dye pollutants in water systems due to industrial discharges poses a significant environmental and health challenge1. Dyes, commonly used in textiles, paper, and plastics industries, are often resistant to natural degradation processes, resulting in persistent contamination of aquatic environments2,3. The toxic and carcinogenic nature of many dyes further amplifies the need for effective removal strategies. Traditional dye removal methods, such as chemical coagulation, and biological treatment, often suffer from limitations, including high operational costs, incomplete removal, and secondary pollution4,5. As a result, there has been growing interest in the development of advanced adsorbent materials that are not only efficient but also sustainable and easy to implement6.

The textile industry is a major contributor to environmental pollution, discharging substantial volumes of untreated wastewater containing synthetic dyes into ecosystems. These dyes, used in processes such as dyeing, finishing, and washing, are integral to the industry, which consumes large amounts of water and produces an estimated 700,000 to 800,000 tons of dyes annually, encompassing over 100,000 distinct compounds7–10. Unfortunately, about 15% of these dyes are lost during manufacturing and end up contaminating water bodies, posing severe environmental and health risks11. Commonly used dyes, such as methyl orange, methylene blue (MB), rhodamine B, and congo red, exhibit complex chemical structures that resist natural degradation12–15. Methylene blue, in particular, is widely employed across industries as a colorant but is also a persistent and toxic cationic dye. Its discharge into water systems can disrupt aquatic ecosystems, impair photosynthesis, and lead to long-term health issues, including cancer, anemia, and neurological disorders16–18. The environmental impact of dye-laden wastewater includes aesthetic pollution, eutrophication, and interference with aquatic food chains, underscoring the urgent need for effective remediation technologies16,17. Conventional wastewater treatment methods, while widely used, are often ineffective in removing dyes like MB due to their high chemical stability and resistance to biodegradation19,20. Advanced treatment techniques, such as reverse osmosis, nanofiltration, electrocoagulation, and photocatalysis, have shown improved efficiency but face challenges like high energy demands, operational complexity, and significant costs21. Adsorption has emerged as a preferred approach for dye removal due to its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and eco-friendly nature22–24. This process relies on the interaction between the dye molecules (adsorbate) and the surface of the adsorbent. The ideal adsorbent should be abundant, non-toxic, cost-effective, and highly efficient, with properties like a high surface area, porosity, and functional group diversity25,26. However, the development of such adsorbents remains a significant challenge. Exploring advanced materials with enhanced adsorption capabilities is critical to addressing the limitations of current technologies and achieving sustainable wastewater treatment27. Recently, compounds such as MOFs modified with various metals28,29 as well as tricarboxylate-based coordination polymers30,31 have been reported as suitable adsorbents for the removal of colored pollutants.

Calix[4]arenes, a class of macrocyclic compounds, have garnered attention in material science due to their unique structural versatility and high functionalization potential32. They exist in a ‘cup’ like shape with a defined upper and lower rim and a central annulus. Their rigid conformation enables calixarenes to act as host molecules because of their preformed hydrophobic cavities33. Due to this ability to form host–guest type complexes with a variety of organic and inorganic compounds the calixarenes have received increasing attention during the last two decades. These synthetic macrocyclic compounds that can be modified to exhibit selectivity for specific targets, making them widely applicable in pollution remediation efforts34,35. The functionalization of calix[4]arene with reactive groups, such as formyl groups, further enhances its potential by introducing specific interaction sites for targeted applications. Tetraformyl calix[4]arenes are anticipated to exhibit exceptional adsorption capabilities32,33. These properties are attributed to their optimized chemical functionalities and enhanced surface area, resulting from the presence of active functional groups on both their upper and lower rims. Although calixarenes exhibit promising adsorption properties, their practical application is hindered by high synthesis costs, the risk of secondary pollution, complex and costly recovery procedures, and limited reusability, especially after adsorption of persistent pollutants5,36. By incorporating these compounds into flexible PVC-MWCNT-Graphite films, we address the challenges associated with their use in powdered form and transform them into high-performance adsorbents. This innovative approach not only leverages the superior adsorption capabilities of tetraformyl calix[4]arenes but also enhances their practicality and efficiency in real-world applications. The integration into flexible films eliminates the need for complex and costly separation processes, as the films can be easily retrieved after use.

Moreover, embedding these compounds into a durable PVC-graphite matrix mitigates the risk of secondary pollution, ensuring environmental compatibility during wastewater treatment. The flexibility of the films allows for convenient handling and scalability, making them suitable for large-scale operations. Additionally, the robust nature of the composite material enhances the reusability of the adsorbent, enabling multiple cycles of dye removal without significant loss of efficiency. This development not only addresses cost-effectiveness but also promotes sustainable and environmentally friendly practices in pollutant removal from wastewater. In this study, we present the fabrication and characterization of tetraformyl calix[4]arene flexible films as innovative adsorbent materials. These films were carefully assessed for their adsorption efficiency, kinetics, and reusability in the removal of dye pollutants. Designed to function as a robust physical dye absorbent, the films offer several advantages, including ultra-fast, high-performance absorbtion, reusability, environmental compatibility, and ease of application.

The novelty of the present work lies in the combined use of tetraformyl-calix[4]arene chemically anchored within a flexible PVC matrix together with conductive carbon phases (MWCNT and graphite). This architecture (i) converts a highly functionalized calixarene from a powder into a retrievable, flexible film, (ii) couples high-affinity formyl/aromatic binding sites with rapid mass transport afforded by hierarchical porosity and conductive networks, and (iii) delivers fast kinetics with good reusability. These features distinguish TfCPMG from previously reported calixarene-based adsorbents (which were mostly powder form or lacked conductive/carbon reinforcement).

Experimental

Chemicals and apparatus

For this work, industrial graphite powder (98% purity), multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT) with diameters of 20–30 nm, polyvinyl chloride (PVC, 99% purity), N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP), and methylene blue dye (purity ≥ 70%) were obtained from Sigma Aldrich. Surface morphology and elemental features were examined using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Tescan MIRA). Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded with a Frontier spectrophotometer. Raman analysis of the synthesise samples were conducted using a Thermo Nicolet Almega dispersive Raman spectrometer, and UV–Vis absorption spectra were measured on a PG-T60 spectrophotometer equipped with a 1 cm path length quartz cuvette.

Fabrication of calix-PVC-MWCNT-graphite film (CPMG film)

For the fabrication of flexible CPMG film, first, synthesized tetraformyl calix[4]arene compound. To synthesis the tetraformyl calix[4]arene, we first synthesized tetrakis-para-formylcalix[4]arene (compound (3) in Fig. 1) based on our previous work32,33. Initially, tetra-para-tert-butylcalix[4]arene (compound (1) in Fig. 1) was prepared following the method developed by Gutsche37. This compound was obtained as white crystals in good yield via a one-step condensation reaction between para-tert-butylphenol and formaldehyde in the presence of sodium hydroxide, using diphenyl ether as the solvent under reflux conditions. Subsequently, the tert-butyl groups were removed through a retro-Friedel-Crafts reaction by treating compound (1) with aluminum chloride and phenol, resulting in the formation of tetrahydroxycalix[4]arene (compound (2) in Fig. 1). This intermediate was then subjected to a Duff formylation reaction by reacting it with hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA) in trifluoroacetic acid, affording tetrakis-para-formylcalix[4]arene (compound (3) in Fig. 1). The obtained compound (3) was then utilized for the preparation of the final tetraformyl calix[4]arene based flexible film. To prepare the flexible CPMG film for dye adsorption (Fig. 2), 0.24 g of MWCNTs, 0.06 g of graphite, 0.06 g of PVC, and 0.1 g of calix were dispersed in 15 mL of NMP by sonication for 4 h. The sonication temperature was carefully controlled to avoid agglomeration of MWCNT. Specifically, aggregation of carbon nanotubes typically occurs when the temperature of the sonication bath exceeds 30 °C; therefore, we maintained the bath temperature at approximately 25 °C using an ice-water mixture during sonication of the composite solution. This procedure effectively prevented MWCNT aggregation, which was previously reported38,39.

The homogeneous suspension was cast into a Petri dish and dried in an oven at 70 °C for 24 h. The resulting film was thoroughly washed with ethanol and deionized water, then dried again to obtain the CPMG adsorbent. The average thickness of the fabricated TfCPMG film was measured to be approximately 100 μm. The unit mass of the film was determined as 0.026 g per cm², enabling straightforward normalization of adsorption capacity based on mass and surface area.

Methylene blue adsorption experiments

The adsorption efficiency of the synthesized adsorbent was evaluated using methylene blue (MB) as a model dye pollutant. Batch adsorption tests were conducted at room temperature under controlled conditions. In a typical experiment, a known concentration of MB solution was prepared and mixed with a predetermined dose of the adsorbent. The suspensions were agitated for a fixed contact time to ensure equilibrium between the solid and liquid phases. Following adsorption, the residual MB concentration in the supernatant was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (T60, PG Instruments) at the maximum absorption wavelength of 666 nm. Adsorption isotherm studies

The equilibrium adsorption behavior of MB onto TfCPMG film was examined by contacting 40 mg of the adsorbent with 10 mL of MB solutions of various initial concentrations. The mixtures were agitated for 10 min under ambient conditions to reach equilibrium. Afterward, the residual MB concentration in the solution was quantified using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Adsorption isotherms describe how adsorbate molecules distribute between the liquid phase and the solid surface at equilibrium. These models are used to infer the surface characteristics and adsorption mechanisms, depending on whether the adsorbent surface is homogeneous or heterogeneous42. In the present study, three well-known equilibrium models Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherms were employed to analyze the experimental data. The Langmuir model assumes monolayer adsorption on a homogeneous surface with identical binding sites and no interactions between adsorbed molecules43,44. Results and discussion

FE-SEM, FT-IR&raman studies

Figure 3a and b present the FT-IR spectrum of the CPMG and TfCPMG film adsorbent, respectively. Figure 3b provides critical information about the functional groups and chemical interactions within the composite structure. The broad absorption band observed in the range of 3400–3450 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the stretching vibrations of hydroxyl (–OH) groups, which are characteristic of the calixarene moieties incorporated into the film32. These hydroxyl groups are of particular importance, as they can serve as active sites for hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with dye molecules, thereby enhancing adsorption capacity. The distinct peak at 1687 cm⁻¹ is assigned to the stretching vibrations of carbonyl (C = O) groups present in the tetraformyl calixarene framework32,33. The introduction of these formyl functionalities increases the polarity and density of electron-rich sites, favoring stronger interactions with cationic pollutants such as methylene blue. The absorption band at approximately 2859 cm⁻¹ arises from the symmetric stretching vibrations of aliphatic C–H bonds, primarily attributed to the MWCNTs embedded in the matrix47. The presence of this peak highlights the successful incorporation of MWCNTs, which are known to improve surface area, mechanical stability, and provide additional π–π interaction sites for dye molecules. Furthermore, characteristic peaks at 1270 and 1380 cm⁻¹ are associated with the stretching vibrations of C–H and C–Cl bonds in the PVC compound, confirming the role of PVC as a supporting matrix. Additional peaks at 933 and 745 cm⁻¹ are linked to the out-of-plane bending vibrations of trans configured C–H bonds and C–Cl stretching modes of PVC, respectively48,49. The FTIR spectra show noticeable shifts and changes in the intensity of characteristic peaks corresponding to –CHO (around 1687 cm⁻¹), C–O–C (around 1100 cm⁻¹), and aromatic C = C bands after the incorporation of calix[4]arene and MWCNTs into the PVC matrix, compared to the CPMG film adsorbent. These spectral modifications indicate possible hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking between the aromatic rings of calix[4]arene/MWCNTs and PVC chains. The coexistence of all these characteristic peaks demonstrates the successful integration of calixarene, MWCNTs, graphite, and PVC within a single composite framework. This structural confirmation supports the effective fabrication of the TfCPMG film adsorbent and justifies its expected multifunctional adsorption performance.

To further confirm the chemical structure and functional groups present in the fabricated adsorbent, Raman spectroscopy was employed. The Raman spectrum of the TfCPMG film adsorbent is presented in Fig. 3c. The band observed at 1356 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the D band of MWCNTs, which originates from the breathing modes of sp² carbon atoms in disordered or defective graphitic domains50. The prominent band at 1588 cm⁻¹ is assigned to the G band, associated with the in-plane vibrational stretching of sp² hybridized C = C bonds in graphitic structures. In this composite, the G band also overlaps with the stretching vibrations of the C = C functional groups present in the calix[4]arene moieties, reflecting the hybrid contribution of both organic and carbonaceous phases50–52. A distinct band at approximately 2705 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the 2D (or G′) band, which is a second-order overtone of the D band and is a characteristic fingerprint of sp² carbon systems such as MWCNTs and graphite53,54. The presence of this band provides further evidence of the graphitic domains within the composite and confirms the effective incorporation of both MWCNT and graphite into the TfCPMG structure. Additionally, a broad signal in the range of 3000–3500 cm⁻¹ is observed, corresponding to the stretching vibrations of aromatic C–H groups within the calix[4]arene structure, highlighting the organic component of the hybrid film51,52. Furthermore, characteristic Raman peaks of the PVC matrix are detected at 757, 1038, and 1356 cm⁻¹, which can be assigned to the symmetric stretching of C–Cl bonds, C–O stretching vibrations, and out-of-plane bending vibrations of C–H groups, respectively48,50. These PVC related bands confirm the successful integration of the polymer phase with the calixarene, graphite, and MWCNT components. Collectively, the Raman analysis provides strong evidence for the coexistence and interaction of polymeric, graphitic, and calixarene domains in the synthesized TfCPMG film adsorbent.

To gain deeper insight into the morphological characteristics of the fabricated composite, FE-SEM was employed. Figure 4a presents FE-SEM micrographs of the TfCPMG film adsorbent at various magnifications. The images clearly demonstrate that the surface of the synthesized film exhibits a heterogeneous, rough, and highly textured morphology, accompanied by a well developed three-dimensional porous framework. Such microstructural features are critical for adsorption applications, as they provide a large number of accessible sites and facilitate efficient mass transfer of dye molecules toward the active domains of the adsorbent. The observed porosity and surface roughness are attributed to the synergistic contribution of the calix[4]arene moieties, graphite particles, and MWCNTs within the PVC matrix. Specifically, the calixarene units introduce structural irregularities and create binding cavities with functional groups that enhance host guest interactions with MB dye. Meanwhile, the MWCNTs and graphite improve the film’s structural integrity, impart electrical conductivity, and contribute additional adsorption sites through their extended sp² carbon networks. The combination of these components results in a hierarchical surface morphology where micro and mesoporous regions coexist, enabling enhanced dye uptake. The roughened morphology observed in the SEM images suggests that the TfCPMG film provides not only high surface area exposure but also favorable adsorption pathways, which together facilitate strong interaction and entrapment of MB dye molecules. Such a morphology is particularly advantageous for fast adsorption kinetics, as dye molecules can readily diffuse through the interconnected porous channels and interact with the functional groups distributed across the film surface. In addition to their structural and surface characteristics, the fabricated composite film exhibited excellent mechanical flexibility and handling durability throughout the preparation and adsorption experiments. The presence of PVC in the matrix provides cohesive support, while the incorporation of MWCNTs and graphite contributes to enhanced mechanical reinforcement, preventing fracture during bending and repeated manipulation. As shown in Fig. 4b, the TfCPMG film can be manually bent into a tight curvature without cracking, peeling, or structural deformation, demonstrating its high flexibility and integrity. During the adsorption–desorption cycles, the film maintained its shape and strength while being easily handled and recovered, confirming its suitability for practical wastewater treatment applications where repeated use and mechanical stability are essential.

Furthermore, to evaluate the porosity and surface characteristics of the fabricated TfCPMG film adsorbent, BET and BJH analyses were performed. The nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm of the TfCPMG film adsorbent (Fig. 5a) exhibits a characteristic type IV behavior according to the IUPAC classification, indicating the presence of a mesoporous structure. The distinct hysteresis loop observed at relative pressures between approximately 0.4 and 0.9 further supports the existence of capillary condensation within mesopores. Such a pattern is commonly associated with slit-like pores formed by the stacking or aggregation of PVC-carbon-Calix nanostructures, suggesting a well-developed porous network that facilitates efficient diffusion of adsorbate molecules. The corresponding BJH pore size distribution curve (Fig. 5b) reveals a narrow distribution centered within the mesoporous range, confirming the uniformity of the pore structure. The predominance of mesopores implies that the adsorbent possesses a high accessible surface area and interconnected pore channels, both of which enhance the mass transfer and adsorption efficiency of MB molecules. Overall, the nitrogen sorption and BJH analyses demonstrate that the fabricated TfCPMG film adsorbent features a hierarchically porous framework favorable for rapid adsorption kinetics and high adsorption capacity.

Evaluation of the influence of operational parameters on MB removal using synthetic adsorbents

To better understand the adsorption behavior of the synthesized films, the effect of different operational parameters on MB removal was investigated. Since adsorption efficiency can vary significantly with experimental conditions, factors such as adsorbent dosage, contact time, solution pH, temperature, and initial dye concentration were systematically examined. Each parameter plays a distinct role: adsorbent dosage determines the number of available active sites, contact time influences the rate at which equilibrium is established, pH controls the surface charge of the adsorbent and the ionic state of MB, and initial dye concentration provides the driving force for mass transfer. By evaluating these parameters individually, the optimum conditions for MB removal can be identified, enabling a clearer understanding of the adsorption mechanism and the potential practical applications of the synthesized adsorbents.

Adsorption performance of fabricated films for MB removal

Following the preparation of the adsorbent films, their efficiency in removing MB dye was examined. A 5 ppm MB solution was first prepared, and 0.02 g of each adsorbent was separately introduced into 10 mL of the solution at its natural pH (~ 6). The mixture of dye and adsorbent were stirred for 10 min using a magnetic stirrer to ensure proper mixing. Finally, the spectrophotometric measurements were recorded before and after adsorption, and the removal efficiency of each adsorbent was calculated using Eq. (1). Figure 6a,b presents the UV–Vis absorption spectra and percentage removal of MB solution before and after treatment with the different flexible adsorbent films. A clear reduction in absorbance intensity is observed upon the introduction of adsorbents, reflecting the progressive removal of MB dye. Among the fabricated materials, the TfCPMG film exhibited the highest adsorption efficiency, confirming its superior ability to capture cationic dye molecules. The reference CPMG film, prepared without the calix compound, achieved only 29.55% dye removal, which can be attributed mainly to the adsorption contribution of graphite and MWCNTs. Incorporation of calix into the adsorbent film significantly enhanced MB uptake, as evidenced by the increased removal efficiencies of 34% for the TbCPMG film and 46.5% for the TfCPMG film. This improvement is related to the functional groups present in the calix structure, which introduce additional active sites for electrostatic and π–π interactions with the aromatic cationic dye. Notably, the higher performance of the TfCPMG film compared with TbCPMG is ascribed to the formylation of tert-butyl groups within the calix framework. This chemical modification increases the density of negatively charged and electron-donating sites, thereby facilitating stronger binding and higher affinity toward MB molecules. These findings demonstrate that structural modification of calix-based adsorbents can play a crucial role in tuning their adsorption capacity and selectivity for organic dye pollutants. Finally, based on the comparative performance of the synthesized adsorbents, the TfCPMG film demonstrated the highest and most efficient dye removal capacity. Therefore, this adsorbent was selected for further studies to evaluate the influence of different operational parameters on its adsorption performance toward MB.

Effect of solution pH on MB removal using TfCPMG film adsorbent

The pH of the aqueous solution is a critical factor influencing the adsorption behavior of dye pollutants, as it affects both the ionization state of the dye molecules and the surface charge of the adsorbent. To evaluate the impact of pH on the removal efficiency of MB by the TfCPMG film, five 10 mL solutions of MB (5 ppm) were prepared with pH values adjusted to 3, 6, 7, 9, and 10.5. Subsequently, 0.02 g of the TfCPMG film adsorbent was added to each solution, and the mixtures were continuously stirred for 10 min to ensure uniform contact between the adsorbent and dye molecules. Upon completion of the adsorption period, the residual concentration of MB in each solution was measured using UV–Vis spectrophotometry, and the removal efficiency was calculated accordingly. As depicted in Fig. 7a,b, the TfCPMG film exhibited significant variation in adsorption performance across different pH values. This behavior can be attributed to changes in electrostatic interactions: at lower pH, the protonation of the adsorbent surface reduces cationic dye adsorption, while near-neutral pH optimizes the availability of adsorption sites. At higher pH, partial deprotonation of the functional groups on TfCPMG may influence dye-binding affinity, slightly altering the removal efficiency. These results underscore the importance of optimizing solution pH to maximize adsorption efficiency and provide insight into the interaction mechanisms between cationic dyes and functionalized adsorbent films. As illustrated in Fig. 7b, the maximum adsorption efficiency of the TfCPMG film was observed at pH 9. At strongly acidic (pH 3) conditions, the dye removal efficiency decreased considerably. This trend can be explained by the surface chemistry of the adsorbent and the ionization behavior of MB4,55. Under acidic conditions, protonation of the functional groups on the tetraformyl-calix framework leads to a reduction in negatively charged active sites, thereby weakening electrostatic attraction with the cationic MB molecules. Conversely, at excessively alkaline pH, although deprotonation of surface groups increases, the competition between hydroxyl ions (OH⁻) and MB cations for adsorption sites, as well as potential structural changes in the calixarene moieties, reduces the overall adsorption efficiency (pH = 10.5)55,56. The highest performance at pH 9 can be attributed to the optimal balance between deprotonated functional groups on the TfCPMG surface and the cationic nature of MB, which enhances electrostatic interactions and π–π stacking between the aromatic structures of MB and the calixarene/graphitic domains of the composite film. These combined mechanisms maximize dye uptake under mildly alkaline conditions, confirming that pH is a crucial factor governing adsorption efficiency. Based on the experimental findings, pH 9 was identified as the optimum condition and was therefore selected for subsequent parameter evaluations.

Effect of TfCPMG film adsorbent dosage on MB removal efficiency

To evaluate the influence of adsorbent dosage on the removal of MB, different quantities of the TfCPMG film were tested to determine the optimum mass for maximum dye uptake. Precisely weighed portions of the adsorbent (0.0025, 0.005, 0.02, 0.03, 0.04, and 0.05 g) were separately introduced into 10 mL MB solutions (5 ppm, pH = 9) and stirred for 10 min to ensure effective contact between dye molecules and the active sites of the adsorbent. After the adsorption period, UV–Vis spectra were recorded, and the percentage removal was calculated using Eq. (1). The spectra presented in Fig. 8a clearly illustrate the reduction in MB absorbance with increasing adsorbent dosage. Figure 8b further highlights this trend, showing a progressive rise in removal efficiency as the amount of TfCPMG film increases. The improvement can be explained by the availability of more active sites and a larger surface area at higher dosages, which enhances interaction with MB molecules. However, beyond a certain threshold (0.04–0.05 g), the increase in removal efficiency becomes negligible. This plateau effect may be attributed to particle agglomeration or incomplete dispersion of the adsorbent within the solution, which restricts the accessibility of some adsorption sites. Considering the minimal difference between the efficiencies at 0.04 and 0.05 g, the dosage of 0.04 g was selected as the optimum value for subsequent experiments. Furthermore, based on Eq. (2), the adsorption capacity of the TfCPMG film was determined to be 3.14 mg/g, indicating strong performance and confirming the suitability of the flexible film as an efficient adsorbent for MB removal.

Effect of contact time on MB adsorption by TfCPMG film

The influence of adsorption time on the removal efficiency of MB using the TfCPMG film was systematically examined under optimized conditions (pH = 9, 0.04 g adsorbent dosage, 10 mL MB solution with an initial concentration of 5 ppm). To evaluate the kinetics of the adsorption process, batch experiments were conducted at different contact times ranging from 1 to 20 min (1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 12, and 20 min). Following each contact time, the residual MB concentration was determined by UV–Vis spectrophotometry, and the corresponding percentage removal was calculated. As presented in Fig. 9a,b, the adsorption efficiency of the TfCPMG film increased rapidly with increasing contact time, reaching a maximum removal at approximately 10 min (80%). Beyond this equilibrium point, the removal percentage exhibited little to no further improvement, indicating that the available adsorption sites on the film surface had become nearly saturated. The fast initial uptake can be attributed to the abundance of unoccupied active sites and strong electrostatic interactions between the cationic MB molecules and the negatively charged functional groups of the tetraformyl-calix framework. The subsequent plateau phase suggests that the remaining active sites are either inaccessible or exhibit lower affinity, which limits further adsorption despite prolonged contact. Therefore, a contact time of 10 min was identified as the optimum duration for MB removal and was selected for subsequent parameter optimization studies. These findings highlight the rapid adsorption kinetics of the TfCPMG film, confirming its potential for efficient dye removal in short operational times compared to many conventional adsorbents. Table 1 provides a comparative overview of MB removal efficiencies achieved by previously reported adsorbents alongside the performance of the newly developed TfCPMG film. As evident from the data, the TfCPMG film exhibits superior adsorption capacity and removal efficiency when benchmarked against many conventional adsorbents. This enhanced performance can be attributed to the synergistic effect of multi-walled carbon nanotubes, graphite, and the functionalized calix[4]arene units, which together create a large number of accessible active sites and facilitate strong electrostatic interactions with the cationic MB molecules. Furthermore, the flexible film morphology ensures good dispersion of the active components in the aqueous medium, promoting rapid dye diffusion and accelerated adsorption kinetics. In addition to its high efficiency, the TfCPMG film stands out as an economical and sustainable material since its synthesis relies on widely available precursors, and its reusability over several cycles further reduces operational costs. These features position the TfCPMG film as a promising alternative to more expensive or less stable adsorbents, making it suitable for practical wastewater treatment applications where both cost-effectiveness and performance are critical.

In this study, to identify the most suitable kinetic model describing MB dye uptake, the experimental kinetic data were analyzed using three commonly applied models: pseudo-first-order (PFO), pseudo-second-order (PSO), and Elovich. The corresponding plots are shown in Fig. 10a−c, and the calculated kinetic parameters are summarized in Table 2.

Based on the correlation coefficients, the PSO model provided the best fit for MB dye adsorption onto TfCPMG film, indicating that the adsorption rate is mainly governed by the available active sites of the adsorbent and the concentration of MB in the solution3,40,41,57. In addition, electrostatic interactions between the oppositely charged adsorbent surface and MB molecules are likely to enhance adsorption.

Effect of initial MB concentration on its removal by TfCPMG film

The initial concentration of dye contaminants plays a crucial role in determining the adsorption performance of any adsorbent, as it directly affects the driving force for mass transfer between the aqueous phase and the adsorbent surface67. To evaluate this factor, the TfCPMG film was tested for MB removal under varying initial concentrations (5, 7, 10, 12, and 15 ppm at pH 9) using 0.04 g of adsorbent and a contact time of 10 min. The UV–Vis spectra shown in Fig. 11a illustrate the progressive decrease in MB absorbance after treatment with the TfCPMG film at different initial concentrations. The corresponding removal efficiencies are presented in Fig. 11b. The data indicate removal percentages of 80%, 74.6%, 69.5%, 54.8%, and 45.9% for solutions with initial concentrations of 5, 7, 10, 12, and 15 ppm, respectively. These results demonstrate a clear inverse relationship between initial dye concentration and removal percentage. At lower concentrations, the relatively high ratio of available active sites to dye molecules promotes rapid and efficient adsorption. However, as the dye concentration increases, the fixed number of adsorption sites on the TfCPMG surface becomes progressively saturated, leading to competition among MB molecules and thus lowering the percentage removal. Despite this decline, the adsorbent still exhibited remarkable performance, achieving nearly 30% removal even at 20 ppm, which highlights its high adsorption capacity and robustness. The observed trend aligns with adsorption theory, where higher pollutant concentrations enhance the driving force for diffusion but simultaneously cause crowding at the adsorbent surface68,69. This balance results in higher absolute uptake (mg/g), even if the percentage removal decreases at elevated concentrations56,69. The ability of the TfCPMG film to function effectively across a wide concentration range underscores its potential applicability for real wastewater treatment, where pollutant levels often vary significantly.

Adsorption thermodynamics

Thermodynamic evaluation helps clarify whether the adsorption process occurs naturally and how heat energy influences the interaction between the dye molecules and the adsorbent surface. In this work, the temperature-dependent behavior of MB adsorption onto TfCPMG was examined using the Van’t Hoff relationship, which links changes in equilibrium constant with temperature. Values for the standard Gibbs free energy (ΔG°), standard enthalpy (ΔH°), and standard entropy (ΔS°) were determined at three different temperatures: 298, 313, and 333 K. By plotting ln Kd against 1/T (Fig. 13), the slope of the resulting line corresponds to ΔH° and the intercept provides ΔS°. These parameters offer insight into the energy effects and molecular ordering that occur during adsorption.

The thermodynamic parameters for the adsorption of MB onto the TfCPMG adsorbent were obtained as ΔH = − 2.0471 kJ/mol and ΔS = + 541.09 J/mol·K.

The negative value of ΔH indicates that the adsorption process is exothermic in nature. Since the enthalpy change is relatively small (less than 20 kJ/mol), the adsorption can be classified as physisorption, suggesting that the main driving forces are weak interactions such as van der Waals forces or electrostatic attractions rather than strong chemical bonding.

The large positive entropy change (ΔS = + 541.09 J/mol·K) signifies a significant increase in randomness or disorder at the solid-liquid interface during the adsorption process. This increase may be attributed to the release of water molecules or structural rearrangements on the adsorbent surface, which enhance the degree of freedom of the system. The ΔG values, calculated at different temperatures, are all negative (–163.29, − 166.00, and − 171.41 kJ/mol at 298, 303, and 313 K, respectively), confirming that the adsorption of methylene blue is spontaneous at all studied temperatures. Moreover, as the temperature increases, the absolute value of ΔG becomes slightly more negative, implying that the process remains spontaneous, though the exothermic nature may lead to a minor decrease in adsorption capacity at higher temperatures.

Reusability assessment of the TfCPMG film for methylene blue removal

An essential factor in determining the practical feasibility of any adsorbent is its reusability, since materials that cannot be efficiently regenerated and reused are neither cost-effective nor environmentally sustainable. To examine this property, the TfCPMG film was subjected to multiple adsorption–desorption cycles under optimized conditions. Following the initial adsorption experiment, in which approximately 80% of MB was removed, the spent film was regenerated by rinsing several times with a dilute acidic solution (0.1 mM HCl) to desorb the retained dye molecules. This acidic wash efficiently disrupted electrostatic and π–π interactions between the cationic MB dye and the functional groups of the film. Subsequently, the material was thoroughly washed with deionized water to restore neutral pH, and dried at 60 °C for 24 h in order to prepare the adsorbent for reuse. The results, illustrated in Fig. 14, demonstrate that the regenerated TfCPMG film maintained excellent stability and adsorption capacity over ten successive cycles, achieving dye removal efficiencies of 79.9% and 78.9% in cycles two through ten, respectively. The negligible decline in performance confirms the strong structural integrity of the film and the robustness of its tetraformyl-calix functional groups, which provide reversible and stable adsorption sites. The sustained adsorption efficiency can be attributed to several factors, including (1) reversible dye adsorbent interactions: The interaction of MB with the calixarene functional groups is primarily electrostatic and hydrogen-bond driven, which can be disrupted under mild acidic conditions, enabling effective desorption. (2) stability of the composite matrix: The combination of MWCNTs, graphite, and PVC provides a mechanically robust framework that resists structural degradation during multiple adsorption-desorption cycles. (3) resistance to pore blockage: The film’s porous and flexible structure prevents significant clogging by dye molecules, ensuring that active sites remain accessible during regeneration. Collectively, these findings highlight that the TfCPMG film is not only an efficient adsorbent but also a reusable material with strong potential for real-world wastewater treatment applications. Its ability to sustain > 78.5% removal efficiency over ten cycles underscores its economic and environmental advantages compared with conventional single-use adsorbents.

Conclusion

In summary, a flexible composite film based on tetraformyl calix[4]arene, PVC, MWCNTs, and graphite was successfully fabricated and demonstrated as an efficient adsorbent for methylene blue removal. The incorporation of calixarene moieties introduced abundant formyl groups and aromatic cavities, while MWCNTs and graphite provided structural robustness and additional adsorption domains. Comprehensive spectroscopic and microscopic analyses confirmed the synergistic integration of the components, yielding a porous and heterogeneous surface with excellent adsorption potential. Systematic evaluation of operational parameters revealed optimal performance at pH 9, with 0.04 g of adsorbent achieving 80% MB removal within 10 min. Furthermore, the TfCPMG film retained its adsorption capacity over five reuse cycles, underscoring its structural stability and cost-effectiveness. Compared with many reported adsorbents, the TfCPMG film offers rapid kinetics, high efficiency, and good reusability, positioning it as a practical candidate for wastewater treatment. This work highlights the potential of functionalized calixarenes embedded in flexible carbon-based films as next generation materials for environmental remediation.
