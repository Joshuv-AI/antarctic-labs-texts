Clinical features analysis of inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors of the retroperitoneal and genitourinary system
Authors: Qianhao Huang, Meixuan Ding, Yifan Huang, Xin Lian, Yijie Xie, Tao Wang, Bin Chen, Yuedong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the clinical features of inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors (IMT) of the retroperitoneal and genitourinary system.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of the clinical data of 23 patients with pathologically confirmed retroperitoneal and urogenital inflammatory myofibroblastic tumours who were admitted to the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University between January 2010 and December 2024.ResultsThere were 10 male patients and 13 female patients, with an average age of (42.1 ± 4.1) years. The tumors were located in the retroperitoneum in 8 cases, in the bladder in 5 cases, in the kidney in 5 cases, in the spermatic cord in 2 cases, in the testis in 1 case, in the epididymis in 1 case, and in the prostate in 1 case. All 23 patients were diagnosed with space-occupying lesions before surgery.Among the 23 cases, 19 underwent surgical treatment and 4 received conservative treatment.Seven patients received postoperative adjuvant treatment. Among them, 4 patients received non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug treatment, 3 patients had no recurrence and are currently alive, and 1 patient had recurrence and died; 1 patient received treatment with epirubicin and cisplatin, had no recurrence and is currently alive; 1 patient was treated with ifosfamide combined with epirubicin and pirarubicin, had no recurrence and is currently alive; 1 patient was treated with azathioprine and crizotinib, had no recurrence and is currently alive. As of the last follow-up, 2 patients had died, and the rest were all alive.ConclusionIMT can originate from any part of the genitourinary system, with pain being the most common symptom. Its clinical management is similar to that of malignant tumours of the genitourinary system, and complete surgical resection is the standard treatment strategy for all genitourinary IMT.Keywords: Genitourinary system tumors, Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT), Retroperitoneal tumors, Clinical and pathological characteristics

Introduction

Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour (IMT) is a rare benign tumour with a low rate of recurrence and distant metastasis [1]. This disease can occur in a variety of anatomical sites, but is most commonly found in the lung, mesentery and omentum. Genitourinary IMT (GU-IMT) is relatively rare and most commonly occurs in the bladder [2]. Of the urological IMT, renal IMT are usually asymptomatic or present with abdominal pain and discomfort [3]; clinical symptoms of bladder or prostate IMT include urinary frequency, urgency and dysuria [4, 5]; and testicular, spermatic cord and retroperitoneal IMT usually present with abdominal and lumbar swelling [6–8].

Histopathologically, IMT consists of proliferating spindle fibroblasts, myofibroblasts and inflammatory cells. The characteristic immunohistochemical marker is Anaplastic lymphoma kinase(ALK), which is statistically present in about half of patients [9]. The overall prognosis of genitourinary IMT is good, with few recurrences or distant metastases [8]. Disease recurrence mainly occurs after non-radical surgery or initial surgery, and postoperative follow-up remains important [10]. However, most of the current studies on urological inflammatory myofibroblastic tumours are case reports or retrospective analyses of small samples, and standard diagnostic and treatment protocols have not yet been developed. In view of this, this study conducted a retrospective analysis of the clinical data of 23 patients with inflammatory myofibroblastic tumours of the urinary tract and retroperitoneum at our hospital, with the aim of summarising the clinical characteristics and prognosis of this disease.

Methods

This study is a single-centre retrospective study. We were able to collect and analyse the clinical data of 23 patients diagnosed with urological inflammatory myofibroblastic tumours. This was based on preoperative or postoperative pathological diagnosis at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University from January 2010 to December 2024. The data included general information (such as gender, age, clinical manifestations, tumour location, and preoperative examinations), details of the treatment process (surgery and adjuvant therapy), and follow-up outcomes. The specific study research flowchart is illustrated in Fig. 1. It is our understanding that the procedures followed in this study comply with the requirements of the 2013 revised Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical and pathological information of patients

Result

General information

Among 23 patients diagnosed with IMT, comprising 10 males and 13 females, with a median age of 42.1 years (± 4.1). Two cases of bladder IMT presented with gross haematuria, urinary frequency, urgency and dysuria. Two further cases presented with abdominal pain and gross haematuria, and one case was asymptomatic and was detected by health check-up. In the case of renal IMT, four cases presented with symptoms including low back and abdominal pain, with or without dysuria. One case was asymptomatic and was detected incidentally. In the cases of spermatic cord and testicular IMT, the presentation was of a mass with or without pain. Prostate IMT primarily manifested as difficulty in urination, akin to the clinical manifestations of prostatic hyperplasia. Six cases of retroperitoneal IMT were characterised by the presence of abdominal or pelvic masses, often accompanied by discomfort, while two cases remained asymptomatic.

Prior to surgery, CT imaging is typically used to assess the condition of the tumour by examining the bladder, kidneys, and retroperitoneal IMT. Most bladder and kidney IMT present as solitary lesions, while retroperitoneal IMT are primarily solitary or may be multiple. Bladder IMT often manifest as posterior wall masses, typically presenting as endophytic or exophytic polypoid or cauliflower-like soft tissue masses. In our cases, 4 involved posterior wall masses, and 1 involved an anterior wall mass. The tumour margins were smooth, with clear boundaries (Fig. 2). Renal IMT often presents as multiple masses, appearing on CT scans as low-density soft tissue masses or renal cystic lesions. Enhanced CT scans show uneven enhancement, with or without stones (Figs. 3-A, B, D). To differentiate between benign and malignant tumours and rule out other renal tumours, further pelvic PET-CT scans can be performed. Our results showed a high metabolic lesion in the left kidney (Fig. 3-C). Abdominal CT and MRI are not specific for diagnosing retroperitoneal IMT, with MRI being more commonly used. Typically, retroperitoneal IMT appears on abdominal MRI as a heterogeneous mass with high signal intensity, demonstrating well-defined or ill-defined borders and an irregular morphology. Our abdominal computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a complex mass in the retroperitoneal cavity adjacent to the left diaphragm, demonstrating extensive invasion of adjacent structures. The lesion exhibits irregular morphology with internal areas of low attenuation/density, suggesting possible necrosis or cystic change. The mass shows well-defined margins (Fig. 4). Preoperative pelvic MRI is a routine preoperative examination for spermatic cord and testicular IMT, often showing a high-signal mass with clear boundaries. Our case presented with a well-defined, high-signal elliptical mass in the left scrotum, clearly demarcated from surrounding tissues (Fig. 5). Pelvic MRI is also a routine preoperative examination for prostate IMT patients to distinguish it from benign prostatic hyperplasia. Our case showed prostate enlargement characterised by multiple heterogeneous signal nodular lesions (Fig. 6).

Treatment

Preoperative diagnosis revealed a mass, and depending on the patient’s preference, a pathological biopsy was performed or not. Additionally, we conducted intraoperative rapid pathological biopsy to comprehensively assess the selection and decision-making of surgical procedures. In bladder IMT, we performed partial cystectomy in 4 cases and radical cystectomy in 1 case (suspected involvement of the ureter); in renal IMT, we performed partial nephrectomy in 2 cases, radical nephrectomy in 2 cases, and only pathological needle biopsy in 1 case without surgical intervention. In cases of IMT in the spermatic cord or testis, 3 patients underwent mass resection, and 1 patient underwent radical resection; in cases of IMT in the prostate, 1 patient underwent transurethral resection of the prostate; in cases of IMT in retroperitoneal masses, 5 patients underwent mass resection and resection of involved organs, and 3 patients did not undergo surgical treatment but only underwent pathological needle biopsy.

Pathological characteristics

Pathological manifestations

All patients underwent pathological biopsy of the tumour and were diagnosed by histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses. The gross pathology of IMT was not specific and often showed specimens with tumour sections that were greyish-red, greyish-yellow or greyish-white in colour, with a soft to medium texture (Fig. 7A). Immunohistochemical staining for anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), S-100, smooth muscle actin (SMA), thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1), CD34, CD68, CD117, Ki-67, Vimentin, and desmin was used to distinguish IMT from other tumours. Histopathologically, IMT can be classified into three distinct histopathological subtypes based on pathological patterns: mucinous/vascular, dense spindle cell and cytopenic fibrous [11]– [12].

Postoperative treatment and follow-up

The vast majority of patients underwent surgical resection, with a small subset receiving additional adjuvant therapy. For instance, one patient with retroperitoneal IMT received three cycles of chemotherapy consisting of ifosfamide combined with epirubicin and piroxicam. No recurrence was observed during the 3-month follow-up, and the patient remains alive. Another patient with bladder IMT was treated with azatinib combined with crizotinib for six cycles. No recurrence was observed at the 6-month follow-up, and the patient is also alive. We also recommend that appropriate adjuvant therapy be considered for aggressive, unresectable, or metastatic IMT.

We conducted regular follow-ups primarily through outpatient visits, telephone interviews, or readmissions, with the starting point defined as the time of pathological diagnosis and the endpoint as either patient death or the last follow-up. The specific follow-up protocol was as follows: for patients with renal or retroperitoneal IMT, imaging studies (CT or MRI) were performed at postoperative months 3, 6, and 12, and annually thereafter; for patients with bladder IMT, cystoscopy served as the primary follow-up method, conducted at postoperative months 3, 6, and 12, and every 6–12 months thereafter based on clinical assessment; for patients with renal, testicular, or retroperitoneal IMT, ultrasonography was the main follow-up modality, performed at postoperative months 3, 6, and 12, and annually thereafter. Additionally, laboratory tests (including urinalysis, renal function, and inflammatory markers) were mandatory for all IMT patients at each follow-up visit. As a result, 4 out of 23 patients were lost to follow-up. Among the remaining patients, the median follow-up time was 3 months (range: 3–24). At the last follow-up, two patients had died: one with bladder IMT succumbed to disease-related cachexia and natural aging, unrelated to IMT; the other with retroperitoneal IMT died due to renal failure. All other patients survived. Treatment outcomes and subsequent follow-up details are summarized in Table 1.

Despite the detailed follow-up protocol established, practical challenges were encountered during implementation, reflecting to some extent the broader difficulties in conducting long-term, high-intensity follow-up for rare diseases within our healthcare system. The relatively short median follow-up time (3 months) and high loss-to-follow-up rate in this study are partly attributable to these challenges.

Discussion

It is acknowledged that preceding studies have appraised the clinical and pathological data of IMT in the urogenital system, encompassing the bladder, kidneys, and prostate [13]. It is unfortunate that the majority of studies included a considerable number of cases, with some reaching up to 40 or more, yet none exceeded 100 cases [8, 10]. It is acknowledged that, due to the rarity of IMT, it is usually almost impossible to conduct large-scale retrospective studies or prospective clinical trials. In contrast, the present study provides a more comprehensive review of IMT in common sites of the urogenital system, including the bladder, kidneys, prostate, perineum, spermatic cord, testicles, epididymis, and retroperitoneum. In summary, the treatment and prognosis of the aforementioned cases are described and compared.

The aetiology and pathogenesis of IMT remain to be elucidated, with existing hypotheses including the role of chronic inflammatory stimuli caused by bacterial and viral microorganisms (Mycobacterium, Hepatitis B Virus, Corynebacterium, EBV, EBV, and human oncoviruses), a history of trauma to the bladder, a history of prolonged use of hormone therapy, and the fact that the anaplastic lymphoma kinase rearrangement (ALK) gene is located on chromosome 2p23 [13–15].

Histopathological analysis reveals the presence of proliferating spindle fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, and inflammatory cells [13]. The predominant histological subtypes observed are mucinous/vascular, compact spindle cell, and cytopenic fibroblastic. It is noteworthy that tumours can manifest as a single entity or as a combination of two or three types [17]– [18]. Immunophenotypically, almost all IMT cells are strongly positive for waveform protein (vimentin). Furthermore, the majority of cases express smooth muscle actin (SMA) or desmin (desmin), which are also variable; The broad-spectrum epithelial marker CK is expressed in approximately 30% of cases, and the histiocyte marker CD68 (+) in about 25% of cases. The characteristic immunohistochemical marker is the mesenchymal lymphoma kinase ALK, which is present in approximately 50% of patients and involves the presence of an ALK (+) located on chromosome 2p23, where the ALK receptor tyrosine kinase gene is activated [18–20].

The initial presentation of GU-IMT is typically characterised by painless gross haematuria, with the clinical manifestations of GU-IMT exhibiting variability across different sites [22]. The predominant symptom among patients with bladder IMT is hematuria, which is visible to the naked eye. Additionally, some patients may experience bladder irritation and difficulty in urination. In the present study, three cases were observed where patients presented with hematuria of the naked eye in combination with bladder irritation symptoms, including urinary frequency, urgency, and pain. These patients also exhibited symptoms of lower urinary tract discomfort, abdominal or pelvic pain, and urinary retention [22]. It is noteworthy that one patient did not present with any clinical symptoms. The principal signs and symptoms exhibited by patients with renal IMT encompass renal mass, pain, haematuria, lower urinary tract symptoms and incidental findings on imaging [23]. The present case exhibited primarily renal mass, pain and bladder irritation signs. In contrast, patients with prostate IMT frequently present with urinary symptoms, including dysuria, obstructive LUTS and acute urinary retention [6]. In the case of spermatic cord IMT, the predominant symptom is painless swelling of the scrotum and groin [24]. The initial symptom in all patients with testicular IMT is a palpable mass [25]. In the case of epididymal IMT, patients primarily present with a palpable scrotal mass [26]. In cases of IMT in patients with retroperitoneal masses, the predominant symptoms are abdominal and lumbar pain, a palpable mass, bowel habits, and dysuria [27]. In conclusion, the vast majority of IMT become symptomatic, and in only a small percentage of cases does IMT present as an incidental finding or asymptomatic.

The presence of IMT is not clearly characterised in diagnostic imaging studies (CT, MRI, ultrasound), and it usually suggests occupying lesions that lack specificity.The diagnosis of IMT relies on a pathological-histological assessment and requires endoscopic resection or open complete resection or excisional biopsy to remove the tumour before a final diagnosis can be made, depending on the organ site. Furthermore, the histology of IMT can be easily confused with that of sarcoma, particularly mucinous smooth muscle sarcoma and sarcomatoid carcinoma [27–30].

The standard treatment strategy for the majority of IMT cases is complete surgical resection, with the preservation of organ function where possible. However, in cases where surgical intervention is contraindicated, systemic or targeted therapies may be employed [32]– [33]. The specific treatment is based on tumour site, size and depth of infiltration. In cases of IMT without bladder muscle layer infiltration, transurethral resection of bladder tumour is typically performed, while partial cystectomy is employed in cases with muscle layer infiltration, large tumours, and those that cannot be effectively removed by transurethral resection of bladder tumour [34]– [35]. Extended resection and lymph node dissection are not recommended. In the event of recurrence, reoperation is undertaken in accordance with the same surgical principles as previously outlined. For renal IMT, partial nephrectomy is the preferred surgical approach, and lymph node dissection is not recommended [21]. In the event of recurrence, reoperation or anti-inflammatory treatment may be considered. The most common surgical procedure for prostate IMT is transurethral resection of the prostate, and postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy may be considered for those with residual tumour or positive margins after surgery. For patients with ALK(+) sex and no indication for surgery, targeted therapy may be considered [36]. The management of epididymis, testicular and spermatic cord IMT typically involves mass resection or partial organ removal, or radical surgery.

In cases where the disease has spread beyond the scope of surgery or has returned after treatment, systemic therapy has been employed. The prevailing protocol in these instances involves the administration of anthracycline-based therapy. However, the efficacy of anthracycline-based regimens for IMT remains unproven due to the absence of clinical data from prospective trials [36]– [37]. Rearrangements of ALK or other fusion genes observed in IMT play a significant role in tumourigenesis and progression, for example, by activating cell proliferation signalling pathways, and these genes are considered to be cancer driver genes and potential therapeutic targets [37]. A 2010 study of the tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) crizotinib, which targets ALK, ROS1, and other oncoproteins, for use in IMT, revealed that the clinical response of IMT to ALK rearrangements. As an ALK inhibitor, crizotinib was the most commonly prescribed agent in these cases, but other newer generation ALK inhibitors, such as ceritinib, alectinib, loratinib, and buxtitinib, were also prescribed and resulted in objective responses [36]. Despite the ineffectiveness of neoadjuvant or concomitant use of available systemic therapies, the recent approval of crizotinib by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for use in adult and paediatric patients is promising [37]. It is approved for unresectable, recurrent or refractory ALK-positive IMT. IMT is characterised by a benign course, non-metastatic tumours, and a low risk of local recurrence and distant metastasis. Furthermore, the presence of anthracyclines has been shown to result in a more favourable prognosis for ALK-positive patients when compared with negative patients, although it should be noted that the available data is limited to case reports and small sample sizes. The requirement for postoperative follow-up is paramount, despite the fact that complete surgical resection of the tumour is sufficient.

The IMT of the urogenital system is challenging to differentiate from tumours with higher malignant potential, as evidenced by clinical, radiological, and histopathological findings. The optimal treatment strategy is surgical complete resection of the tumour; however, if the tumour’s location and size permit, partial resection methods (e.g., partial cystectomy and partial nephrectomy) are reasonable surgical treatment options. The mechanisms underlying ALK inhibitor resistance appear to be incompletely understood. The present study is subject to certain limitations, primarily due to its retrospective, single-centre, and small-sample size design. The characteristics of these patients may not be representative of all adult IMT patients. The sample size of this study is relatively small.
