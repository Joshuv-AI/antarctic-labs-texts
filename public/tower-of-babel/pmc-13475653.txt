Body weight rather than unfractionated heparin dose predicts periprocedural bleeding during elective PCI
Authors: Nobuhiro Honda, Kenji Sadamatsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOptimal anticoagulation during elective percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) remains uncertain, particularly in East Asian populations with lower body weight and increased bleeding susceptibility. We investigated the association of body weight and unfractionated heparin (UFH) dose with periprocedural bleeding during elective PCI.Methods and ResultsThis retrospective single-center study included 348 Japanese patients undergoing elective PCI. Patients received either fixed-dose UFH (10,000 U) or weight-adjusted UFH (100 U/kg). Within the fixed-dose group, patients were stratified according to weight-adjusted UFH exposure (≥150 vs. <150 U/kg). The primary endpoint was Bleeding Academic Research Consortium (BARC) type 1 or 2 bleeding. Bleeding events were less frequent in the low-dose than in the high-dose group (24% vs. 40%, P < 0.001). No significant differences in major cardiovascular events were observed during follow-up. In multivariable analysis, lower body weight was associated with increased bleeding risk (OR 0.948, 95% CI 0.905–0.994, P = 0.027), whereas UFH dose was not independently associated with bleeding.ConclusionsLower body weight may be more closely associated with periprocedural bleeding than UFH dose during elective PCI. Careful bleeding risk assessment may therefore be warranted in patients with low body weight undergoing PCI.

Introduction

Anticoagulation in addition to antiplatelet therapy remains essential during elective percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) to prevent procedure-related thrombin generation and catheter-associated thrombosis. Unfractionated heparin (UFH) continues to be the most widely used intraprocedural anticoagulant. Current European and Japanese guidelines recommend UFH at a dose of 70–100 U/kg during PCI [1,2]. However, these recommendations are largely derived from historical data, and the optimal anticoagulation intensity during contemporary elective PCI—particularly in East Asian populations—has not been fully established.

In the current era of optimized medical therapy, the role of PCI in patients with chronic coronary syndrome (CCS) has become more selective, with clinical benefit driven primarily by symptom relief rather than reduction in major cardiovascular events [3,4]. Accordingly, minimizing even minor procedural complications has become increasingly important when considering invasive treatment in stable patients. Even minor periprocedural bleeding may impair patient comfort, prolong hemostasis, and potentially affect subsequent antithrombotic management. Therefore, strategies that reduce bleeding without compromising ischemic protection are highly relevant in modern PCI practice.

Ethnic differences in thrombotic and bleeding risk, often referred to as the “East Asian paradox,” further support the need for individualized anticoagulation strategies [5,6]. East Asian patients have consistently demonstrated lower ischemic risk but greater susceptibility to bleeding during antithrombotic therapy. In Japan, the approved maintenance dose of prasugrel is lower than that used in Western countries, reflecting this narrower therapeutic window [7]. Regarding intraprocedural anticoagulation, previous studies have suggested that Asian patients may require approximately 10 U/kg less UFH than Western patients to achieve comparable activated clotting time (ACT) levels [8]. In addition, racial differences have been reported to influence the risk of anticoagulation-related intracranial hemorrhage [9].

Low body weight has also been reported to be associated with an increased risk of bleeding complications after PCI, particularly in East Asian populations where body size tends to be smaller [10]. These observations suggest that both anticoagulant exposure and patient body size may influence bleeding risk during PCI.

Despite guideline recommendations for weight-adjusted UFH administration, uncertainty remains as to whether bleeding during elective PCI is primarily determined by anticoagulation intensity or by intrinsic patient characteristics such as body weight [11]. This clinical question is particularly relevant in East Asian populations, where lower body weight is common and bleeding susceptibility is increased. However, contemporary clinical data evaluating the relative contribution of UFH dose and body weight to bleeding risk during elective PCI remain limited. Therefore, the present study aimed to determine whether body weight or UFH dose is more strongly associated with periprocedural bleeding in Japanese patients undergoing elective PCI.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

Between January 2010 and August 2011, 361 consecutive patients underwent elective PCI for stable or unstable angina pectoris at Saga-ken Medical Centre Koseikan (Saga, Japan).

During the early study period (January–October 2010), a fixed high dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH; 10,000 U) was routinely administered at the start of the procedure. From late October 2010 onward, the institutional protocol was modified to a weight-adjusted UFH regimen of 100 U/kg.

Accordingly, 181 patients who underwent elective PCI with the fixed-dose regimen were classified as the fixed-dose group (FD), and 180 patients treated after the protocol change were classified as the adjusted-dose group (AD). Patients with known intolerance, hypersensitivity, or contraindications to the study medications were excluded. Thirteen patients who underwent thrombus aspiration or experienced failed PCI were also excluded. The final study population consisted of 348 patients.

Because the UFH dosing strategy changed during the study period rather than being randomized, the potential for temporal bias cannot be completely excluded. However, no substantial changes in PCI devices, vascular access strategies, operator staffing, or standard antiplatelet therapy protocols occurred during the study period.

Clinical data were obtained from electronic medical records. The study protocol complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Japanese Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects and was approved by the institutional ethics committee. Informed consent was obtained using an opt-out approach.

Procedural characteristics

All procedures were performed using standard PCI techniques. UFH was administered immediately after sheath insertion either as a fixed dose (10,000 U) or as a weight-adjusted dose (100 U/kg), according to the institutional protocol during the respective study periods. Additional UFH was administered at the operator's discretion when considered necessary.

ACT was routinely measured at the end of the procedure only after implementation of the weight-adjusted dosing protocol, reflecting institutional practice at that time. During the earlier fixed-dose period, ACT monitoring was not routinely performed because a standardized UFH bolus was used. Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors were not used because they are not approved for clinical use in Japan. Dual antiplatelet therapy consisting of aspirin (100 mg/day) and clopidogrel (75 mg/day) was recommended for at least 12 months following PCI.

Follow-up and endpoint definitions

Patients were routinely followed six months after the index procedure and annually thereafter, unless earlier evaluation was required because of symptoms or clinical events. Angiographic follow-up at approximately eight months was encouraged.

The primary endpoint was the incidence of periprocedural bleeding events, including minor bleeding, defined as Bleeding Academic Research Consortium (BARC) class 1 or 2 bleeding.

The secondary endpoint was a composite of cardiac death, myocardial infarction (MI), urgent or emergent target vessel revascularization for MI, definite or probable stent thrombosis, or major bleeding (BARC class ≥3) within 30 days.

Stent thrombosis was defined according to the Academic Research Consortium criteria. MI was defined as the development of new pathological Q waves (≥30 ms in duration and ≥0.1 mV in depth) in at least two contiguous leads or elevation of creatine kinase–MB levels to ≥2 times the upper limit of normal.

Major adverse cardiac events (MACE) were defined as a composite of target lesion failure (TLF), target lesion revascularization (TLR), non-fatal MI, non-fatal stroke, cardiac death, and all-cause death. TLR was defined as repeat PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting of the target lesion. TLF was defined as a composite of clinically driven TLR and target vessel MI.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages, whereas continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate.

Initial comparisons were performed between the FD and AD groups. Because a uniform UFH dose of 10,000 U may result in variable weight-adjusted exposure depending on body weight, patients in the FD group were further stratified according to weight-adjusted UFH dose.

The threshold of 150 U/kg was selected to represent relative overdosing beyond the guideline-recommended range (70–100 U/kg) and corresponded approximately to the upper quartile of weight-adjusted exposure within the fixed-dose group. Accordingly, patients were categorized into a high-dose group (HD; ≥150 U/kg) and a low-dose group (LD; <150 U/kg).

Group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate, and Student's t-test for continuous variables.

To identify independent predictors of periprocedural bleeding, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed. Variables entered into the model were selected based on clinical relevance and previous reports of bleeding risk after PCI in Japanese populations [12], including age, body weight, body mass index, unfractionated heparin dose (U/kg), prior brain infarction, serum creatinine level, diabetes mellitus, and femoral access. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the ability of weight-adjusted UFH dose and body weight to predict periprocedural bleeding. The optimal cutoff value was determined using the Youden index, which identifies the point maximizing the sum of sensitivity and specificity. The discriminative ability of each variable was assessed using the area under the ROC curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 3.6.1 with the EZR graphical user interface [13]. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Logistic regression analysis

Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with periprocedural bleeding events.

In the univariate analysis, lower body weight (OR 0.935, 95% CI 0.897–0.973, P = 0.001) and femoral access (OR 4.22, 95% CI 1.72–10.3, P = 0.002) were significantly associated with bleeding events. Age showed a significant association in univariate analysis but was not significant in the multivariate model.

In the multivariate analysis, lower body weight remained an independent predictor of periprocedural bleeding (OR 0.948, 95% CI 0.905–0.994, P = 0.027). ROC analysis

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive ability of body weight and weight-adjusted UFH dose for periprocedural bleeding.

Weight-adjusted UFH dose demonstrated limited discriminatory ability (AUC 0.572, 95% CI 0.429–0.715). In contrast, body weight showed a significantly better predictive performance (AUC 0.726, 95% CI 0.645–0.808) (Fig. S1).

ROC analysis identified an exploratory optimal cutoff value of 57 kg for body weight (sensitivity 78%, specificity 62%). Patients with body weight <57 kg had a significantly higher incidence of bleeding compared with those ≥57 kg (12.7% vs. 2.4%, P = 0.00025) (Table S1).

Discussion

In this retrospective single-center study of Japanese patients undergoing elective PCI, higher weight-adjusted UFH exposure (≥150 U/kg) was associated with an increased incidence of periprocedural bleeding. However, after adjustment for clinical covariates, lower body weight—but not UFH dose—remained independently associated with bleeding. In addition, body weight demonstrated better discriminatory performance than UFH dose in ROC analysis. Collectively, these findings suggest that lower body weight is an important factor associated with periprocedural bleeding during contemporary elective PCI. However, given the retrospective before-and-after design and the close relationship between body weight and weight-adjusted UFH exposure, these findings do not establish that body weight is causally more important than UFH dose.

In the contemporary era, particularly among patients with CCS, the primary benefit of PCI lies in symptom relief rather than reduction in hard cardiovascular endpoints [14]. Accordingly, procedural safety has become a key determinant of overall treatment value. In this context, minimizing periprocedural complications, including even minor bleeding, is essential when considering invasive strategies in otherwise stable patients.

Previous studies have shown that lower UFH dosing regimens (e.g., 100 U/kg) provide net clinical benefit compared with higher historical doses, mainly through reductions in bleeding without increasing ischemic events [15]. In the present study, although major bleeding and early ischemic events were similar between groups, higher weight-adjusted exposure (≥150 U/kg) was associated with an increased incidence of minor bleeding. Given that more than 80% of procedures were performed via the transradial approach—already associated with lower access-site bleeding [[16], [17], [18], [19]]—these findings suggest that anticoagulation intensity remains a modifiable determinant of bleeding risk even in contemporary radial-dominant practice.

Importantly, minor bleeding events should not be considered clinically negligible. Most events in this study were access-site hematomas, which may prolong compression time and increase the risk of radial artery occlusion. Radial artery occlusion may limit future transradial access and preclude the use of the radial artery as a conduit for coronary artery bypass grafting or arteriovenous fistula creation in patients requiring hemodialysis [20]. In addition, nuisance bleeding after drug-eluting stent implantation has been associated with premature discontinuation of antiplatelet therapy, potentially increasing the risk of stent thrombosis [21]. Thus, even minor bleeding may adversely affect both procedural quality and long-term treatment adherence.

The optimal intensity of anticoagulation during elective PCI remains uncertain. Although ACT monitoring is widely used, the ideal target range has not been clearly established. In our cohort, most patients in the adjusted-dose group achieved guideline-recommended ACT levels [2]. However, prior studies have demonstrated only modest correlations between ACT and bleeding risk, with limited association with ischemic outcomes [22]. Furthermore, ethnic differences in antithrombotic risk—often referred to as the “East Asian paradox”—suggest greater bleeding susceptibility relative to ischemic risk in East Asian populations compared with Western cohorts [5,23]. These differences raise the possibility that standard anticoagulation strategies may result in relatively higher effective exposure in this population.

ROC analysis further supported the importance of patient body habitus. Body weight demonstrated moderate discriminatory ability for predicting periprocedural bleeding, whereas weight-adjusted UFH dose showed limited predictive performance. These findings suggest that body weight may provide clinically relevant information for bleeding risk assessment during elective PCI, whereas weight-adjusted UFH dose showed limited discriminatory performance in this cohort. However, the relative causal contributions of body weight and UFH exposure cannot be determined from this observational study. This observation is consistent with the results of our multivariable analysis and supports individualized bleeding risk assessment when selecting intraprocedural anticoagulation strategies.

Finally, despite differences in anticoagulation intensity, no increase in long-term cardiovascular events was observed, consistent with prior studies in contemporary PCI practice [14,24]. Taken together, these findings suggest that careful assessment of patient body weight may be more important than further escalation of UFH dose when balancing bleeding and ischemic risk during elective PCI.

From a practical perspective, our findings should not be interpreted as supporting routine reduction of UFH below guideline-recommended doses solely on the basis of low body weight. Rather, in patients with low body weight, careful attention to modifiable procedural factors may be particularly important, including accurate vascular puncture and appropriate application and management of radial compression devices. Such measures may help reduce access-site bleeding while maintaining adequate anticoagulation during PCI. However, the generalizability of these findings to contemporary PCI practice outside Japan remains uncertain. Differences in patient body habitus, UFH dosing practices, antithrombotic regimens, vascular access strategies, and procedural techniques may influence the relationship between body weight, UFH exposure, and bleeding risk. Therefore, prospective studies in diverse contemporary PCI populations are needed to determine whether these findings are applicable beyond Japanese patients and historical treatment settings.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study with a relatively small sample size, limiting statistical power to detect differences in rare ischemic or mortality outcomes. Second, the change in UFH dosing strategy during the study period introduces potential temporal bias. Changes in operator experience and other unmeasured factors over time may also have influenced periprocedural bleeding independently of the UFH dosing strategy and could not be fully accounted for in this retrospective study. Third, ACT monitoring was not routinely available during the fixed-dose period and was introduced after implementation of the weight-adjusted dosing protocol. Therefore, direct comparison of anticoagulation intensity based on ACT between the two dosing periods was not possible. Fourth, procedural techniques and intraprocedural management were left to operator discretion, which may have influenced bleeding risk [25,26]. Fifth, residual confounding cannot be excluded despite multivariable adjustment. In particular, low body weight may represent a surrogate marker of frailty or reduced physiological reserve, which was not directly assessed in this study. Additionally, the same UFH dosing strategy was applied regardless of concomitant oral anticoagulation, although optimal dosing may differ in patients receiving combination antithrombotic therapy.

Finally, given the historical nature of this cohort, contemporary PCI devices, pharmacotherapy, and bleeding avoidance strategies have evolved, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to current clinical practice.

Conclusion

Lower body weight was independently associated with periprocedural bleeding during elective PCI, whereas UFH dose was not independently associated with bleeding after multivariable adjustment. These findings suggest that patient body weight may provide clinically relevant information for bleeding risk assessment during elective PCI. Further prospective multicenter studies are needed to clarify the relative contributions of body weight and UFH exposure and to define optimal population-specific anticoagulation strategies.
