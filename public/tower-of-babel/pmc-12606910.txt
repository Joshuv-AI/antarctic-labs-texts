Short- and long-term neurobehavioural impairments in mice offspring following chronic perinatal caffeine exposure and withdrawal
Authors: Funmilayo Eniola Olopade, Oluwabusayo Racheal Folarin, Taidinda Tashara Gilbert, James Olukayode Olopade
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explored neurodevelopmental and adult neurobehavioural outcomes in mouse offspring following chronic perinatal caffeine exposure and subsequent withdrawal. Healthy adult female BALB/c mice were mated and, at gestational day (GD) 10, were randomly assigned to one of three groups (n = 4): control, low-dose caffeine, and high-dose caffeine. The caffeine-exposed groups received either a low (50 mg/kg) or high (100 mg/kg) dose of caffeine daily via oral gavage from GD 10 until the end of lactation at postnatal day (PND) 21, whereas the control group received sterile water. At PND 21, a subset of offspring from the caffeine-exposed and control groups underwent neurodevelopmental behavioural assessments, including negative geotaxis, cliff avoidance, and surface righting tests, to evaluate the effects of chronic perinatal caffeine exposure on sensorimotor reflex functions. The remaining offspring were allowed to grow into adulthood and, at three months of age, underwent neurobehavioural assessments to evaluate motor function, anxiety-like behaviour, and cognition via the pole test, open field test, and Morris water maze (MWM) test. Compared with those in the high-dose caffeine group, the mice in the low-dose caffeine group had accelerated attainment of neurological milestones, such as the righting reflex and cliff avoidance. Conversely, compared with the control and low-dose caffeine treated offspring, the high-dose caffeine-treated offspring presented delayed eye opening and impaired negative geotaxis. However, there were no significant differences between the caffeine-exposed and control groups in the adult neurobehavioural assessments following caffeine withdrawal. These findings suggest that perinatal caffeine exposure can influence early neurodevelopmental processes but does not seem to have persistent effects on motor function, cognition, or anxiety in adulthood. This study underscores the transient nature of caffeine’s impact on brain development and highlights the need for further research to explore the long-term consequences of prenatal caffeine exposure.Keywords: Learning and memory, Cognition, Caffeine, Morris water maze, Perinatal

Introduction

Caffeine, a naturally occurring methylxanthine, ranks among the most widely consumed psychoactive substances across various age groups globally [1–4]. Owing to its known pharmacological properties and widespread use in beverages, more focus has been placed on its toxicity than its beneficial effects, especially in vulnerable populations, such as neonates and pregnant women, where caffeine consumption could be relatively unsafe [5–7]. Due to the history of long-term consumption of caffeine, it was initially regarded as safe for drinking; however, after the discovery of its fetotoxicity, the consumption of caffeine was cautioned in lactating mothers and pregnant women [6, 8, 9].

Caffeine is an alkaloid of plant origin that is structurally analogous to adenosine, an extracellular signalling molecule that functions through selective adenosine receptors (namely, A1, A2a, A2b, and A3) located in the extracellular space of the brain [8, 10–12]. These receptors are also found in moderate amounts in other organs, such as the lungs, heart, and kidneys [13–15]. Adenosine functions primarily as a neuromodulator and regulates neuronal signalling. Additionally, it plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis in eukaryotic organisms [16–18]. As a central nervous system stimulant, caffeine exerts its effects by blocking adenosine receptors, particularly at nontoxic concentrations, influencing brain development. [19, 20]. The brain contains a high density of A1 and A2a adenosine receptors, which are closely associated with caffeine’s stimulatory effects [5, 13, 21, 22].

Research indicates that the mechanisms of action of caffeine differ across animal species [11] and are significantly influenced by dosage [8, 12]. Owing to its lipophilic properties, caffeine easily crosses biological membranes, including the blood‒brain barrier, where it can reach comparable concentrations in both serum and cerebrospinal fluid [23–25]. It easily crosses the placenta, is widely distributed in foetal tissue, and is excreted in the breast milk of lactating mothers [26, 27]. Numerous studies have shown neurodevelopmental benefits of caffeine at therapeutic doses [8, 28–30]. The neuroprotective potential of caffeine has been demonstrated in the prevention and treatment of apnoea of prematurity without any neurodevelopmental disability [8, 30–32]. Additionally, studies using animal models of preterm brain injury have indicated that low-dose caffeine treatment has antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antiapoptotic effects [8, 12]. In neonatal rats subjected to hypoxia, low-dose caffeine treatment during the early postnatal stages mitigated white matter hypomyelination, preserved the oligodendrocyte population, and supported normal oligodendrocyte maturation [33].

In addition, exposure to caffeine in utero in rats during the first week of life increased their vulnerability to seizures. Hippocampal neuron migration is delayed, which is aggravated by hippocampal neuronal loss and cognitive impairment in both juvenile and adult offspring. Caffeine was found to be teratogenic, and administration to pregnant rats affected the development of the foetus’s brain even at extremely low doses. Compared with control rats, neonatal rats exposed to low doses (1 or 2 mg/100 g body weight) presented significantly reduced brain weight [34]. However, postnatal behaviour and functional alterations identified in the offspring of dams exposed to caffeine during gestation need to be investigated; fewer studies have reported the effects of in utero caffeine exposure on the behavioural development of offspring. Marked variation in emotional reactivity and reduced open field exploration were reported in adult male offspring exposed to caffeine during gestation [35, 36], and hypersensitivity to pain perception and reduced anxiety in offspring were also observed [36]. Furthermore, hyperactivity in open field tests, which persisted into adulthood, was also detected in juvenile rat offspring following gestational exposure to caffeine [37]. Research has indicated that when mothers are exposed to caffeine, it can have lasting effects on their offspring, affecting their sleep patterns, movement, learning capabilities, emotional responses, and anxiety levels and resulting in permanent behavioural issues [38]. A recent study on the effects of chronic maternal caffeine exposure on cognitive behaviour in rats revealed that it disrupted novel object recognition as well as radial arm maze performance in both adult male and female offspring [34]. Additionally, exposing rat pups to caffeine doses of either 1 or 9 mg/kg during their first week of life resulted in the hypoactivity observed at 2 weeks of age, along with spatial learning impairment when they matured [36]. Passive avoidance retention was notably affected in both male and female juvenile rats [39]. However, more studies are needed to determine the lasting behavioural impacts of caffeine exposure during pregnancy on newborns.

This study sought to investigate whether chronic maternal exposure to two different doses of caffeine, i.e., a low dose of 50 mg/kg and a high dose of 100 mg/kg, during pregnancy from gestation day 10 to lactation (postnatal day 21), could induce behavioural impairment in offspring and whether long-term withdrawal from caffeine exposure after lactation would ameliorate the behavioural impairments initially observed in offspring or continue to lead to severe brain damage in adulthood. The hypothesis of this study is “maternal caffeine ingestion will induce short- and long-term behavioural impairment in the offspring following perinatal exposure”.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The study was approved and carried out in accordance with the guidelines by the University of Ibadan Animal Care and Use Research Ethics Committee (UIACUREC), under ethical code number UI-ACUREC/App/2016/011. The study followed NIH Guide for the Use and Care of Laboratory Animals, in accordance with the European Communities Council Directives (86/609/EEC) and ARRIVE guidelines, minimising the number of animals used and avoiding their suffering.

Perinatal caffeine exposure

Healthy adult female BalB/mice were obtained and mated in the experimental animal house at the Faculty of Basic Medical Sciences, University of Ibadan, Ibadan. The female mice were mated, and the day a vaginal plug was observed was designated Day 0 of the gestation period. From gestational day 10, the dams were separated into three main groups: group 1 (control), group 2 (low-dose caffeine), and group 3 (high-dose caffeine). Dams (n = 4) in group 1 (control) were administered tap water; Dams (n = 4) in group 2 (low-dose caffeine) were administered 50 mg/kg caffeine [40] dissolved in tap water; and Dams (n = 4) in group 3 (high-dose caffeine) were administered 100 mg/kg caffeine [41] in tap water. The selection of 50 and 100 mg/kg doses of caffeine was based on previous studies, where 50 mg/kg was shown to produce mild to moderate central nervous system stimulation without toxicity [40], while 100 mg/kg elicited neurochemical effects [41]. These doses have been reported in rodent studies to represent low and high caffeine exposure levels, respectively. Caffeine was administered to the pregnant dams daily by oral gavage (maintaining relevance to humans) and continued from gestation day 10 until the end of lactation - day 21. The rationale for restricting caffeine exposure to gestational day 10 through postnatal day 21 (PN21) was to model the effects of maternal caffeine consumption during critical periods of neurodevelopment, specifically, late gestation and early postnatal life (lactational caffeine exposure). This design allows us to assess the indirect developmental impact of maternal caffeine ingestion on the offspring, rather than lifelong or direct intake by the offspring. By focusing on this window, the study evaluates how prenatal and early postnatal exposure may have lasting neurobehavioral consequences, even after withdrawal. The 70-day withdrawal period (post-PN21 to adulthood) was chosen to allow the offspring to reach young adulthood, enabling us to examine the persistent or delayed effects of early-life caffeine exposure. Power analysis was not conducted prior to the study. After weaning, the mice were fed pelleted food and water ad libitum. They were kept at room temperature and subjected to natural light and dark cycles throughout until adulthood.

Behavioural tests

During the first three weeks of life, a subset of mouse pups from both the perinatal exposure and control groups underwent a series of neurodevelopmental behavioural tests conducted during the light phase of the day. For each experimental group, four dams were mated, and three male pups per dam (total of 12 pups per group) were selected for behavioural testing in order to minimize the risk of pseudo replication. This strategy was adopted to avoid over-representation of any single litter within a treatment group. Twelve male offspring from each experimental group (control: n = 12; low-dose caffeine: n = 12; high-dose caffeine: n = 12) [42, 43] were subjected to three neurodevelopmental assessments; negative geotaxis, cliff avoidance, and surface righting tests, to evaluate the impact of chronic perinatal caffeine exposure on sensory, motor, and reflex development. The remaining pups were allowed to mature into adulthood.

At postnatal day 21 (PND 21), caffeine exposure was stopped and the mice allowed to grow until three months (adulthood). These adult mice were then subjected to a series of behavioural assessments. On Day 1, the Open Field Test was performed first, followed by the Pole Test, with a one-hour interval between them to reduce stress and fatigue. From Days 2 to 5, the Morris Water Maze test was conducted for four consecutive days. These tests aimed to assess whether behavioural impairments caused by caffeine exposure during development persisted or were ameliorated in adulthood.

To facilitate acclimatisation to the testing environment, the mice were placed in the testing room 30 minutes before each behavioural test. All tests were conducted between noon and 4 p.m. under dim lighting conditions. The experimenter remained outside the testing room except during the trials. Behavioural scoring and evaluation were performed by at least two observers who were blinded to the treatment groups.

Neurodevelopmental behavioural tests

Negative geotaxis test

The negative geotaxis test assesses neurodevelopment, vestibular function and motor activity. The test was carried out as previously described by Olopade et al. [44]. Each mouse was positioned at the centre of a wooden slab (30 cm × 45 cm) with an incline of 30° for assessments conducted in the first week of life (D2, D4) and 45° for those in the second week (D12, D14). The mice were placed head-down, and the latency to turn 180° to a head-up position was recorded. Three replicates were performed for each animal, with successive trials performed after all the mice had completed previous trials so that enough rest was ensured. The average time was calculated for each mouse pup.

Surface righting reflex

The righting reflex test evaluates truncal motor function, which is regulated by vestibular pathways, spinal interneurons, proprioceptive afferents, and motor neurons. During the test, each mouse was placed on its back and then released, and the time required to return to a prone position with all four paws on the ground was recorded [45]. This test was performed in triplicate after the completion of each successive trial. This test was conducted at PND 5.

Cliff avoidance test

The cliff avoidance test assesses labyrinth reflexes, muscle strength, and coordination. Each mouse was positioned at the edge of a surface with its forepaws and snout touching the edge before being released. The latency to turn and initiate movement away from the edge was measured in seconds [46]. This test was conducted at PND 5.

Age at eye opening

The age at which the eyes of the mouse pups in each group first opened was observed and documented.

Adult neurobehavioural tests

At 3 months of age, another set of 12 male offspring from each of the experimental groups (control n = 12; caffeine: low-dose n = 12; high-dose n = 12) underwent three adult neurobehavioural tests, including the open field test, pole tests, and Morris water maze to assess the lasting effects of prolonged prenatal caffeine exposure on the neurobehavioural functions of mouse offspring in adulthood.

Open Field Test

Each mouse was positioned at the centre of a white wooden box measuring 120 × 120 cm. The floor was marked with black ink, dividing it into 20 cm2 squares, including a central 20 cm2 square. The mice were given five minutes to move freely and explore the open field environment. Between each test, the box was thoroughly dried and cleaned with 70% ethanol to remove any odour cues that might affect mouse behaviour in subsequent trials. The movements of the mice were recorded via a digital camera attached to a makeshift stand as described previously [47]. The set of observations listed below was obtained and manually analysed.

Line crossing: The frequency with which the mouse crossed a grid line with all four paws.

Centre square entries: The number of times the mouse fully entered the central square by crossing a marked boundary with all four paws.

Centre square duration: The total time the mouse spent in the central square.

Rearing: The frequency with which the mouse stood on their hind legs in the box.

Grooming: The frequency and duration of licking or scratching while remaining stationary.

Freezing: The total time the mouse remained completely motionless.

Defecation: The number of faecal pellets expelled.

Pole test

This test evaluates motor coordination and basal ganglia function. The pole test was conducted following the method described by [48], with slight modifications. Each mouse was positioned at the top of a vertically oriented rough-surfaced pole (8 mm in diameter, 55 cm in height), with its head facing upwards. The time required to turn downwards and descend to the floor was recorded, with a maximum duration of 60 seconds allowed. Each mouse completed 5 trials, and the average time was calculated.

Modified Morris water maze test

The test utilised a circular water pool containing a hidden platform and a submerged spherical escape stage positioned approximately 1.5 centimetres below the water surface. The animal relied on contextual and visual cues to learn the location of the platform. The principle of the test is based on the idea that rodents are extremely encouraged to abscond from the surrounding water through the quickest, most precise course [49].

The circular water pool was marked with north, south, east, and west designations, with the escape platform positioned at a fixed location. Each animal was released into the pool and required to locate the platform. The time taken to find the platform was recorded. If an animal failed to locate it within one minute, it was guided to the platform and allowed to remain there for 15 seconds to familiarise itself with the location.

All the experimental groups underwent three tests per day for three consecutive days, constituting the acquisition phase, which assessed learning ability. On the 4th day, a single probe test was conducted to evaluate spatial memory in the absence of the platform. The memory of the platform’s previous location was determined by measuring the time spent in the target zone and the number of crossings over the former platform site, indicating spatial memory capacity. Two observers manually recorded the test results using a timer. This test is commonly used to detect hippocampal based memory impairment in rodents [50].

Statistical analysis

The quantitative data from the behavioural test scores are expressed as the means ± SEMs after normality was assessed via the D’Agostino‒Pearson omnibus test. The group means were compared via analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Body weight

At birth, there was no variation in average body weight between the control (group 1) and caffeine-treated (low-dose; group 2) and high-dose (group 3) mice offspring. The pups in all the groups gained weight steadily from birth until sacrifice at PND 21. No significant differences were observed in the rate of weight gain among the groups, with F = 2.948, DFn = 2, and DFd = 18. p = 0.0781 (Fig. 1A). However, our current findings are limited to the early developmental window because long-term body weight data in adulthood were not collected in this study.

Neurodevelopmental behavioural tests

Eye opening

The mean age at which the mouse pups achieved eye opening was significantly different across the groups. Compared with those in the control and low-dose groups, the eyes of the pups in the high-caffeine group opened later; chi-square = 13.78, df = 4, p = 0.0080 (Fig. 1 B).

Surface righting reflex

The mice pups in low dose and high caffeine groups achieved the rightening reflex significantly faster than those in the control group: 3.9 ± 0.6179 s, 4.591 ± 0.5913 s, and 7.14 ± 0.6052 s, respectively, F (2, 27) = 7.959, p = 0.0019 (Fig. 2A).

Bar charts representing the neurodevelopmental tests conducted on the neonatal mice pups from birth till PND21. Bar chart showing the Time taken by the mice pups to turn to the prone position (righting reflex) in (A), Time taken to turn away from the edge in cliff avoidance test in (B), latency to turn 180° from the head-down position on the 30° in (C), and on the 45° in (D) negative geotaxis tests respectively. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ****p < 0.0001

Cliff avoidance test

In the cliff avoidance test, the pups in the low-dose caffeine group turned away from the edge significantly faster than those in the control group did. In contrast, those in the high-dose group took significantly longer: 6.335 ± 0.386 s, 13.53 ± 3.658 s and 23.39 ± 2.615 s, respectively; F (2, 34) = 16.79, p < 0.0001 (Fig. 2B).

Negative geotaxis test

Negative geotaxis tests conducted during the first week (Days 2 and 4) and the second week (Days 12 and 14) revealed that mouse pups in the high-dose caffeine group had a significantly longer latency to turn than those in the low-dose and control groups did; F = 7.83, DFn = 2, DFd = 54, p = 0.001 and F = 12.6, DFn = 2, DFd = 72, p < 0.0001, respectively (Figs. 2C and 2D).

Adult neurobehavioural tests

Adult neurobehavioural tests were carried out when the mice were 3 months old, following the discontinuation of caffeine administration at PND 21.

Open Field Test

In the open field test, there was no significant difference among the groups regarding the frequency of horizontal movements or line crossings, vertical movements or rearings, or grooming behaviours: F = 0.9849, p = 0.3887; F = 2.091, p = 0.1369; and F = 1.355, p = 0.2709, respectively (Fig. 3).

Open field test conducted at 3 months of age. Bar charts showing the number of line crossings in (A), the number of rearings in (B), and the number of groomings in (C) among the experimental groups of mice

Pole test

Similarly, in the pole test, there was no significant difference among the adult mouse groups in the time taken by the mice to turn their heads downwards on the pole, and the duration of the descent down the pole (0.4422, p = 0.6470 and F (2, 28) = 1.301, p = 0.2882, respectively) (Fig. 4).

Pole test conducted at 3 months of age. The bar charts show the time it takes for the mice to change their heads downwards in (A) and the duration of the descent down the Pole in (B)

Morris water maze test

In the Morris water maze test, a steady reduction in the time taken to locate the hidden platform (escape latency) during subsequent trials was recorded for the mice in all three groups. However, there was no significant difference in the rate of reduction in escape latency among the groups; F = 1.439, DFn = 2, DFd = 21, p = 0.2596 (Fig. 5A). Similarly, when the platform was removed in the probe trial, there was no significant difference among the three groups in the time spent in the correct quadrant (F = 3.212, p = 0.0555) or in the frequency at which the mice passed the area in which the platform had initially been placed, i.e., the number of platform crossings (F = 0.3778, p = 0.6888) (Figs. 5B and 5C).

Discussion

This study investigated neurodevelopmental and adult neurobehavioural tests conducted on offspring following chronic maternal perinatal caffeine exposure and withdrawal. In the present study, we focused on male offspring to reduce variability associated with hormonal cycles and to establish a consistent baseline for assessing the effects of maternal (prenatal and lactational) caffeine exposure.

We observed that chronic maternal administration of low-dose caffeine resulted in earlier development of neurological milestones such as the righting reflex and cliff avoidance. In contrast, the high dose caused a delay in these events, as well as in negative geotaxis. However, in the long term, by early adulthood (3 months of age), neurological functions did not appear to be adversely affected following caffeine withdrawal at PND 21.

All groups of mouse pups showed steady weight gain from birth until sacrifice at postnatal day 21 (PND 21). There was no significant difference in the rate of weight gain among the groups, indicating that caffeine exposure did not influence this aspect of development. This is interesting because previous studies have provided varying reports on the effects of caffeine administration on body weight. There are no conclusive clinical studies showing, especially, the long-term effect of caffeine on body weight, mainly because of the development of tolerance to caffeine over time; the smaller studies that have been carried out have variable outcomes [51]. While some studies have reported a reduction in weight gain following, usually higher doses of caffeine in rodents, others have reported either no change or a slight increase in weight [52–57].

Concerning the various neurodevelopmental parameters studied, mouse pups in the high caffeine group achieved eye opening later than those in the control and low-dose groups did. These findings suggest a potential delay in neurodevelopmental milestones with higher doses of caffeine exposure during early life. Ajarem et al. [58], however, reported that offspring born to dams exposed to both low and high doses of green tea (containing caffeine) opened their eyes earlier than did control offspring. Compared with those in the control group, the righting reflex of the pups in the low- and high-dose caffeine groups was significantly shorter. Sawyer et al. [59] reported that lower concentrations of caffeine increase locomotor activity. This finding could indicate accelerated motor development in caffeine-exposed pups. The results of cliff aversion varied between groups, with the low-dose group showing a shorter time to turn away from the edge than the control group did, whereas the high-dose group took significantly longer. These findings suggest a dose-dependent effect of caffeine on behaviour in this test.

Research by Warburton [60] and McLellan et al. [61] have shown that low doses of caffeine can enhance cognitive function in habitual users following a period of abstinence. Additionally, some studies suggest that caffeine administration after training can improve memory consolidation in various tasks, including habituation in rats [62], inhibitory avoidance in mice [63] and rats [62], and active cliff avoidance in rats [64]. In the negative geotaxis test, mouse pups in the high-dose caffeine group demonstrated longer latencies to turn than those in the low-dose and control groups did, indicating potential disruptions in vestibular and motor function. Caffeine exposure had a significant, dose-dependent effect on the righting reflex, rotation reflex, and cliff avoidance behaviour, with more pronounced effects observed in offspring from dams exposed to the higher dose. These findings suggest that chronic perinatal caffeine exposure, particularly at higher doses, may negatively impact early neurodevelopmental milestones in mice.

Furthermore, adult neurobehavioural tests were conducted when the mice reached three months of age, following the discontinuation of caffeine administration at three weeks. In the Morris water maze (MWM) test, there was no significant difference in the escape latency or performance in the probe trial between the low-dose and high-dose caffeine groups compared with the control group. This finding seems to indicate that caffeine exposure during early life did not affect spatial learning or memory in adulthood. However, Mahdi et al. [65] reported that the latency to reach the platform in a group of mice treated with moderate doses of caffeine in the MWM test was significantly greater than that in a group treated with high doses. These variations may be due to the different doses of caffeine used; in this study, 50 mg/kg (low dose) and 100 mg/kg (high dose) were used, whereas Mahdi and his colleagues used 20 mg/kg (low dose) and 200 mg/kg (high dose) caffeine. Angelucci et al. [66] reported similar findings, with the most pronounced benefits observed at lower caffeine doses (0.3, 3, and 10 mg/kg). The results of this study align with those of previous studies, indicating that caffeine enhances learning and memory in animal models. Molinengo et al. [67] reported that chronic caffeine administration following the cessation of training reduced memory decay in rats. In the open field test, no significant differences were observed among the groups in horizontal movements, vertical movements, or grooming behaviours, suggesting comparable levels of exploratory activity and anxiety-related behaviours. These findings agree with our previous study [68], where there was no significant difference in the number of horizontal and vertical movements, or grooming behaviours among control adult mice and those exposed to varying doses of caffeine. No significant differences were detected among the adult groups in terms of motor coordination or balance in the pole test.

Overall, these findings suggest that caffeine exposure during early life can significantly affect neurodevelopmental milestones and some aspects of behaviour in mice. However, these effects may not persist into adulthood or manifest in all behavioural domains. Further studies may be needed to elucidate the long-term effects of caffeine exposure and its potential implications for human development and behaviour.

While this study provides valuable insights into the short- and long-term effects of perinatal caffeine exposure on neurobehavioural development, several limitations are acknowledged. These involve the use of a rodent model, which may not fully reflect human neurodevelopment, and the focus on specific behavioural end points without assessing underlying neurobiological mechanisms. Future studies could explore additional endpoints, such as molecular and cellular changes in the brain, to better understand the mechanisms underlying caffeine-induced neurobehavioural alterations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study enhances our comprehension of the neurodevelopmental effects of chronic perinatal caffeine exposure and withdrawal. While alteration in neurodevelopmental responses was observed during early development, these effects did not persist into adulthood, suggesting a degree of plasticity in the brain as it develops. However, further research is needed to elucidate the lasting effects of caffeine exposure on brain activity and to inform public health recommendations regarding caffeine consumption during pregnancy and early life. Furthermore, we also recommend that future studies should include both sexes to explore potential sex-specific effects of caffeine on brain development and behaviour.
