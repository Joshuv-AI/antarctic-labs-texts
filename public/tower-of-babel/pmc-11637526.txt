The Study of the Epidemiology of Pediatric Hypertension Registry (SUPERHERO): rationale and methods
Authors: Andrew M South, Victoria C Giammattei, Kiri W Bagley, Christine Y Bakhoum, William H Beasley, Morgan B Bily, Shupti Biswas, Aaron M Bridges, Rushelle L Byfield, Jessica Fallon Campbell, Rahul Chanchlani, Ashton Chen, Lucy D'Agostino McGowan, Stephen M Downs, Gina M Fergeson, Jason H Greenberg, Taylor A Hill-Horowitz, Elizabeth T Jensen, Mahmoud Kallash, Margret Kamel, Stefan G Kiessling, David M Kline, John R Laisure, Gang Liu, Jackson Londeree, Caroline B Lucas, Sai Sudha Mannemuddhu, Kuo-Rei Mao, Jason M Misurac, Margaret O Murphy, James T Nugent, Elizabeth A Onugha, Ashna Pudupakkam, Kathy M Redmond, Sandeep Riar, Christine B Sethna, Sahar Siddiqui, Ashley L Thumann, Stephen R Uss, Carol L Vincent

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite increasing prevalence of hypertension in youth and high adult cardiovascular mortality rates, the long-term consequences of youth-onset hypertension remain unknown. This is due to limitations of prior research, such as small sample sizes, reliance on manual record review, and limited analytic methods, that did not address major biases. The Study of the Epidemiology of Pediatric Hypertension (SUPERHERO) is a multisite, retrospective registry of youth evaluated by subspecialists for hypertension disorders. Sites obtain harmonized electronic health record data using standardized biomedical informatics scripts validated with randomized manual record review. Inclusion criteria are index visit for International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) code–defined hypertension disorder on or after January 1, 2015, and age < 19 years. We exclude patients with ICD-10 code–defined pregnancy, kidney failure on dialysis, or kidney transplantation. Data include demographics, anthropomorphics, US Census Bureau tract, histories, blood pressure, ICD-10 codes, medications, laboratory and imaging results, and ambulatory blood pressure. SUPERHERO leverages expertise in epidemiology, statistics, clinical care, and biomedical informatics to create the largest and most diverse registry of youth with newly diagnosed hypertension disorders. SUPERHERO’s goals are to reduce CVD burden across the life course and establish gold-standard biomedical informatics methods for youth with hypertension disorders.Keywords: biomedical informatics, blood pressure, cardiovascular health, causal inference, child health, electronic health record, life course, youth

Introduction

Youth and adults have increasing prevalence of hypertension and related disorders, including elevated blood pressure (BP; high BP not meeting hypertension criteria) and white coat hypertension (BP in the hypertension range when assessed in the clinical setting but normal when assessed outside of the clinical setting).1,2 Hypertension occurs in 46% of adults, though it is unknown if hypertension onset begins in childhood or adolescence.3 In youth, global hypertension prevalence increased from 1.3% in the 1990s to 6.0% in the 2010s,4 and elevated BP prevalence, which may increase the risk of overt hypertension in youth, increased from 11.8% in 1999 to 14.2% in 2014.2 Youth with obesity and those from marginalized communities have double the hypertension risk.5–10 Other modifiable cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors common in adults—obesity, dyslipidemia, and diabetes mellitus—are increasingly common in youth, especially among marginalized groups.2,11–13 Among youth with hypertension, 46% have at least 1 additional CVD risk factor, including 77% with obesity.14–19

Despite this increased modifiable CVD risk factor burden in youth, we do not know the long-term health consequences of youth-onset (pediatric) hypertension. Although BP values during childhood and adolescence predict adult-onset hypertension and death due to CVD, these data are from large cohorts from general populations without diagnosed hypertension and have limited applicability.20–23 No data exist that routinely screening BP, diagnosing hypertension, or treating hypertension in youth prevents adult CVD.24–26 Because hypertension-attributable CVD remains a leading cause of death in adults, it is critically important to improve our understanding of the epidemiology of youth-onset hypertension disorders as they transition across the life course.10,21,27–31

These knowledge gaps hinder clinical practice and public health efforts10,21,26,32–36 and exist due to limitations of scientific rigor (Table 1). Well-described cohorts enrolled participants from general populations with limited applicability to youth with diagnosed hypertension disorders,37–41 and most studies of youth with confirmed hypertension disorders were cross-sectional with small sample sizes from 1 or a few sites.5,16,31,42,43 Most studies relied on manual review of health records, which is labor intensive and susceptible to unrecognized biases.42 Although important prospective studies have attempted to address these gaps, sample size remains a major barrier. Studies have not applied rigorous epidemiologic and statistical best practices, further increasing susceptibility to biases that preclude meaningful causal inferences.30,31,43 Biomedical informatics offers a substantial opportunity to advance the field of youth-onset hypertension disorders through electronic health record (EHR) data use, but these methods have not been widely applied to this population due to a lack of rigorous methods, lack of validation, and heterogeneous EHR systems. Thus, there is an urgent need for large-scale, rigorous studies of youth with hypertension disorders from diverse populations with long-term follow-up and that use epidemiologic, statistical, and biomedical informatics best practices to appropriately leverage EHR data.38,44

Goals and objectives

To address these knowledge gaps and transform the field, our diverse, transdisciplinary team leveraged expertise in epidemiology, statistics, clinical care, and biomedical informatics and applied counterfactual, causal diagram, and target trial frameworks to create the Study of the Epidemiology of Pediatric Hypertension Registry (SUPERHERO). The registry contains data on a large, diverse population of youth referred to subspecialty care for hypertension disorders across North America that were assessed retrospectively. Our ultimate goals for SUPERHERO are to reduce CVD burden across the life course in youth with hypertension disorders and establish gold-standard biomedical informatics methods to improve EHR data use for this population.36 Our objectives are as follows: (1) Understand how epidemiology and practice patterns vary across time and geography; (2) determine contributions of social determinants to health disparities, including the health care system’s role; (3) define CVD risk, including due to target organ injury; and (4) determine which medications and other interventions are most effective to inform future clinical trials.

Methods

SUPERHERO is a multisite registry of youth referred to subspecialty care for hypertension disorders that retrospectively obtains patient-level data from each site’s EHR. We launched SUPERHERO in June 2021 at the coordinating site and Atrium Health Levine Children’s Hospital, and we began enrolling the first 13 additional sites across North America in October 2021 (Figure 1); site enrollment is ongoing. Our philosophical approach, conceptual framework, approach to data validity and missingness, and team science design are reported in Appendix S1. Our study website URL is https://school.wakehealth.edu/superhero.

Data management and sharing plan

We will share all appropriate data acquisition, management, and analysis code and de-identified aggregate and patient-level data in our GitHub repository, in line with national and international recommendations. We will publish interactive de-identified aggregate data on our website. We will make each data set publicly available once the corresponding analysis has been published.

Ethical approval

The institutional review boards of each site approved the study and granted the study waivers from requiring written informed consent and assent, due to the retrospective collection of limited protected health information data and because the study could not be practicable without a waiver. The coordinating site serves as a single institutional review board for select sites. We obtained data use agreements with each site as indicated.

Study population

The target (theoretical) population is all youth (defined as those <19 years of age) who have a new diagnosis of a hypertension disorder and receive care from a hypertension subspecialist. We define our study (empirical) population as youth who are referred to and receive care from a hypertension subspecialist for evaluation or management of a hypertension disorder, defined as all conditions of high BP, including but not limited to confirmed hypertension, elevated BP, or white coat hypertension (Table 2). We are interested in youth with all causes of hypertension disorders, excepting a few specific sub-populations (see “Inclusion and exclusion criteria”). Planned phases include refining our study population definition’s precision and accuracy to improve sensitivity and specificity, and broadening the study population to include youth with hypertension disorders who are treated by their primary care provider only.45

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Sites are eligible if they use an EHR (Table 3). We identify potentially eligible patients from among youth referred and seen for evaluation or management of hypertension disorders (new patient or consultation visit) at each site’s qualifying subspecialty clinics (eg, hypertension, nephrology, cardiology). We work with each site to determine which providers and clinics provide care for patients with hypertension disorders. Subspecialist providers include but are not limited to nephrologists and cardiologists, the subspecialties most likely to provide this care in most health care systems. Providers include both physicians and advanced practice providers.

A patient’s initial visit to the qualifying subspecialist clinic must occur within the study time period. The initial study period is January 1, 2015, through December 31, 2022; planned phases will extend the end of the study period every 6 to 12 months. The start date is variable according to the availability and validity of each site’s EHR data, with the goal for each site’s start date to be before September 2017 whenever possible. We chose this date because it was when the American Academy of Pediatrics published its clinical practice guideline, which changed the definitions of hypertension disorders and management recommendations.21 It is important to account for how epidemiology and practice patterns changed.46 We identify hypertension disorders by International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes at the first qualifying clinic visit encounter (index visit). Participants must be <19 years of age at the index visit.

We exclude patients who were pregnant, had kidney failure and received dialysis, or who had undergone kidney transplantation before the index visit, as identified by ICD-10 codes. We also exclude patients who received care in the qualifying clinics prior to the index visit for a different condition (eg, chronic kidney disease) and subsequently developed a hypertension disorder. Planned phases will apply additional criteria to further refine the study population to those participants who were primarily seen for a hypertension disorder. Planned phases will also develop and validate methods to track participants across sites and time (eg, if they move) and as they age into adulthood.

Biomedical informatics methods

The coordinating site develops and standardizes phase-specific, biomedical informatics Structured Query Language (SQL) scripts in the Epic EHR with corresponding data dictionaries in collaboration with the Data Harmonization Committee (which comprises at least 1 clinical investigator and 1 biomedical informatics specialist from each site). In the pilot phase 1.1, the SQL scripts were first validated externally at Atrium Health Levine Children’s Hospital and then were implemented at each site in an iterative process with the coordinating site to ensure standardization and harmonization. Most sites use Epic and execute SQL scripts directly in their Clarity database. However, the study is designed to be implemented across all EHR systems and data warehouses. Sites that do not use Epic adapt and validate the standardized scripts to their local systems.

Initially, each site applies the inclusion and exclusion criteria to define their study population and assigns a de-identified study identification number for each participant that is prefixed with their site’s unique code; only the coordinating site can link codes to sites. Each site maintains a fully encrypted, password-protected local database that remains behind their firewall to store limited protected health information linking participants to their study identification number. Each site’s biomedical informatics team works with the coordinating site’s biomedical informatics team to apply phase-specific SQL or equivalent scripts and data dictionaries to structure and format the local biomedical informatics data sets in an iterative process (Figure 2). Each site calculates and shares de-identified time variables (eg, duration between date of the index visit and date of birth). Each site records individual clinic location, department name, and provider name and dummy codes these data prior to sharing the data set with the coordinating site. Each site runs simple quality control checks to confirm formatting, missingness, and biological plausibility and then securely submits its biomedical informatics data set to the coordinating site.

The coordinating site then executes a battery of quality control tests on each site’s submitted biomedical informatics data sets to check for completeness, validate formatting, and to assess for biological plausibility to ensure normalization. The coordinating site provides each site with a quality check report detailing whether each data element has passed, failed, or needs further examination. Each site then refines its biomedical informatics data set in an iterative process before final export to the coordinating site in preparation for manual review validation. In phase 1.1, sites underwent, on average, 2 quality checks prior to passing; 2.3% of records had at least 1 biologically implausible flagged value for height, weight, or body mass index; on review, 100% were true values.

Manual record review methods

The coordinating site’s biomedical informatics team randomly selects a subset of records for local manual review validation (~2%) for each site; the proportion varies according to each site’s sample size and capabilities (eg, manual review team size). This was chosen as a trade-off between optimizing rigor and ensuring that the manual review was not overly burdensome at each site. The coordinating site’s manual review team develops and validates standardized review protocols and trains personnel at each site (Appendix S2). The protocols are designed to abstract time and encounter-specific discrete data elements to mimic the biomedical informatics scripts and to be adapted to any EHR. Each site enters data from the manual review into phase-specific data collection instruments in a Research Electronic Data Capture database hosted at the coordinating site, independent of the biomedical informatics data set.47 A second manual review team member cross-validates the initial manual review data to verify accuracy; discrepancies are adjudicated by the site principal investigator in discussion with the coordinating site.

Once the initial manual review data entry is complete, the coordinating site exports each site’s manual review data set and validates it against the biomedical informatics data set to ensure at least 95% agreement for all variables. The coordinating site sends each site a quality check report highlighting data disagreements. Each site’s principal investigator adjudicates discrepancies in discussion with the coordinating site. The biomedical informatics and manual review protocols are updated and refined in an iterative process until appropriate agreement is met. In phase 1.1, sites underwent, on average, 2 quality checks prior to passing; initial missingness was 13% for BP data and 2% for height and weight data. Once the site and phase-specific data set passes all biomedical informatics and manual review validation steps, the data set is uploaded into the secure, relational registry for analysis. All centralized data are stored securely at the coordinating site.

Data

We collect data in well-defined phases and sub-phases according to our conceptual model of aligning study data elements (empirical estimands) with target data elements (theoretical estimands) (Figures 3–5).48 Phase 1 covers the evaluation period, which is defined as the day of the index visit and up to 6 months after the index visit, because this is typically the time frame when patient tests are obtained or medications are prescribed. Phase 2 covers the follow-in period (ie, data that occurred prior to the index visit); by default, this includes data from up to 2 years before, but planned sub-phases will expand this period. Phase 3 covers the follow-up period (ie, data obtained from >6 months after the index visit). Planned analyses will evaluate follow-up data at regular intervals (eg, 1 year, 2 years), and the phase 3 period is open ended to allow us to investigate long-term outcomes as the study population transitions through childhood, adolescence, and adulthood. We ensure all data are collected specific to a particular encounter to ensure data are time-varying.

Phase 1.1 data included the index date month and year; calculated age (index visit date minus birth date); sex; gender; race and ethnicity (as recorded in the EHR); insurance status (government-sponsored, commercially provided, self-pay, other); dummy-coded clinic and provider names; provider type (physician, advanced practice provider); provider subspecialty (nephrology, cardiology, other); height; weight; BP; and ICD-10 codes that were linked as visit diagnoses to the index visit encounter. We classify race and ethnicity per Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) definitions.49 Our approach allows us to collect data on self-identified, nonbinary gender based on what was recorded in the EHR. In phase 1.1, we also collected the day (order date minus index visit date) when specific orders were placed relative to the index visit for echocardiograms, measurements of urine albumin or urine protein, antihypertensive medication prescriptions, and referrals to dietitian or metabolic or weight management clinics within 6 months of the index visit. We recorded antihypertensive medication prescription class in phase 1.1.

Data elements to be collected in planned sub-phases include medication name, dosage, pharmacy filled prescription status, and indication; echocardiogram and other imaging results; laboratory results; US Census Bureau tract (or equivalent data element for non-US sites); birth, medical, surgical, family, and social histories; and raw data from ambulatory BP monitoring studies, which are stored in our linked ambulatory BP monitoring database.

In addition to analysis on the continuous scale, we categorized age groups as < 1 year (infants), 1–12 years (children), 13–17 years (adolescents), 18–24 years (young adults), and ≥ 25 years (adults) for descriptive purposes to distinguish among established age criteria per BP guidelines for youth and adults.21,27 In phase 1.1, we categorized participants’ home addresses according to Federal Information Processing System state codes and US Census Bureau 9-level division and 4-level region and equivalent categorizations for non-US sites. We calculated weight for length (age < 2 years) and body mass index (age ≥ 2 years). We calculated age and sex-specific percentiles and z scores for length, weight, and weight-for-length z scores for participants using World Health Organization normative values for non-US sites and age < 2 years for US sites, as well as height, weight, and body mass index for ages 2–20 years for US sites using CDC normative data.50,51 We classified participants’ weight status by age according to World Health Organization and CDC guidelines.52,53

We collect all available discrete BP values for a given encounter. Planned sub-phases will record in which extremity the measurement was taken, if obtained manually or via an automated device, cuff size, and whether the participant moved during measurement. If multiple BP measurements are available for a given encounter, we record the average of the measurements; planned sub-phases will interrogate the best approach to summarize BP for a given encounter. We calculate age, sex, and height-specific BP z scores and percentiles and classify BP severity per relevant guidelines (Figure 6).10,21,27,54–61

Analytical approach

The latest versions of Python (Python Software Foundation) and SAS (SAS Institute) are used to visualize and check the data, and generate reports, and the latest versions of R (R Foundation), SAS, and SAS Enterprise Guide are used for all analyses. Additional software may be used for specific research questions as appropriate. Unless otherwise prespecified, we consider a 2-sided α < 0.05 as statistically significant. As indicated, we estimate power and sample size a priori for each hypothesis. We define the goals and type of each planned analysis as descriptive, predictive, or inferential and cross-sectional, retrospective cohort, case-control, nested case-control, interrupted time series, or as appropriate.

We report descriptive statistics such as measures of central tendency, dispersion, and association and ensure that all underlying assumptions are met for the statistical tests we use. We improve distributional characteristics with natural log transformation or other methods as appropriate. For values that are below the lower limit of detection, we divide the lower limit value by the square root of 2 or by similar methods as appropriate.62 We primarily analyze data on the continuous or ordinal scales and only dichotomize or classify those data according to clinically meaningful and standardized definitions.

We apply a causal inference approach using life course, counterfactual, causal diagram, and target trial frameworks for all analyses whose goal is to infer causality. We develop graphical causal models for each relevant research question a priori to relate our variables of interest based on our clinical and epidemiologic knowledge and the independent literature, and with the goal to create a living master causal model that is updated continuously and shared publically on the SUPERHERO website.43 We develop directed acyclic graphs for each exposure-outcome relationship to identify potential sources and types of bias. The directed acyclic graphs inform the minimally sufficient adjustment sets that we include in each multivariable model to close noncausal, biasing pathways, particularly those due to confounding bias and informed presence bias.43,63,64 We publish the directed acyclic graphs and underlying code. We report effect size estimates for the exposure with corresponding 95% confidence limits (CL) for both the unadjusted and adjusted models. We report estimated effect sizes and corresponding 95% CLs within each stratum of a potential effect-modifying variable on the additive and multiplicative scales.65 As appropriate, we test for effect modification by sex.

For each research question, we evaluate missingness patterns visually and by descriptive statistics to inform our analytical approach to missing data (Appendix S1).66 These reports examine missing data patterns for each data element and combinations of data elements in aggregate and by site, clinic, and provider by generating indicator variables. If valid, we consider using multiple imputation and determine the total number of imputations by examining the variable with the maximum percent missing. We will consider several methodological approaches to mitigating misclassification bias and other types of bias (Appendix S1).67–69 As a sensitivity analysis, we examine the impact of other techniques (eg, inverse probability censoring weighting) on the final results. For each research question, we test our assumptions via several sensitivity analyses.43,70–74

Discussion

Novelty and innovation

Our novel, innovative, and rigorous SUPERHERO registry contributes to the largest North American study population of youth with new hypertension disorders ever assembled and has enormous potential to address critical knowledge gaps and transform the field of youth-onset hypertension (Table 4). Our application of a causal inference–based approach—with life course, counterfactual, causal diagram, and target trial frameworks—to an academic learning health system model in this population is unique and offers us the opportunity to directly inform the design of planned prospective observational studies and clinical trials, both double-blind and pragmatic. Our validated methods of collecting standardized and harmonized EHR data across diverse systems and populations will create a foundation for other investigators to take advantage of EHR data and are broadly applicable to other health conditions and young and adult populations.

We will continue to refine our case definition’s accuracy and precision to improve sensitivity and specificity, including our inclusion and exclusion criteria and primary exposures and outcomes. We can follow our study population over a long period, well into adulthood, allowing us to investigate long-term cardiovascular outcomes in youth with hypertension disorders. Our study has created the largest database of echocardiogram and ambulatory BP monitoring data in this population. Our approach allows us to easily recruit additional sites into SUPERHERO throughout the course of the study; planned phases will enroll international sites. Site recruitment is ongoing and includes leveraging resources through several national and international organizations, such as the American Society of Pediatric Nephrology, the Pediatric Nephrology Research Consortium, and the International Pediatric Hypertension Association. Site recruitment will continue to ensure diversity and inclusivity of our study population and our study team.

Limitations

Our approach naturally has important trade-offs that may limit the study. Given the retrospective study design, we are not able to measure variables directly from the participants in real time, which has important implications for measurement error, missing data, misclassification bias, time-dependent bias, and confounding bias, among others. Our intentional focus on youth who receive subspecialty care magnifies the potential risk for selection bias and informed presence bias, because among the entire population of youth with hypertension, youth with more severe hypertension or those who are more likely to have target organ injury or secondary hypertension may be more likely to be referred to a subspecialist rather than receive care from their primary care provider. However, this is of primary scientific interest to hypertension subspecialists. In addition, planned phases of the study will expand our methods to include EHR data from primary care clinics to broaden the study population to all youth with new hypertension disorders. Our use of best practices in epidemiology, statistics, and biomedical informatics directly addresses many of these limitations. Finally, we are intentional in how we frame our research questions, aims, and hypotheses and how we present and interpret our findings.

We have developed robust and rigorous alternative strategies to mitigate anticipated and unanticipated potential problems. Our methods provide sufficient flexibility and adaptability for diverse EHR systems. Our centralized study design reduces each site’s workload. We can use additional, well-validated biomedical informatics methods and expand our manual EHR review to mitigate selection and misclassification bias and missing data. All sites’ regulatory approvals provide for manual review of echocardiogram and imaging reports and allow study sites to provide the coordinating site with actual images and other raw data. We propose alternative measures of the exposures, outcomes, and other variables for all research questions. We can expand use of ICD-10 codes and other data elements if there is a high proportion of missing data.

Perspectives

The registry will include the largest, most diverse population of youth with hypertension disorders and allows us to follow participants across the transitionary phases of childhood and well into later adulthood. Our diverse, transdisciplinary collaborative team leverages expertise in clinical care, epidemiology, statistics, and biomedical informatics to address critical knowledge gaps, using intentional and rigorous methods to transform the field of youth-onset hypertension disorders. The registry will be updated regularly with data from new patients and new sites; in the future, we will expand to other countries. Our approach allows us to study historically marginalized patient populations with robust sample sizes, including by race, ethnicity, and nonbinary gender, and positions us to be a leader in the field in rigorously using social determinants of health EHR data from this population, including both neighborhood-level and individual-level social risk factors and unmet social needs, as these data become increasingly available due to government mandates. Our results will inform future studies in an academic learning health system model, including population health interventions to address the effects of social and health disparities on cardiovascular health; development of cost-effective, value-based, and risk-based patient management and population health strategies, particularly aimed at primordial and primary prevention based on modifiable cardiovascular risk factors; and comparative-effectiveness clinical trials with long-term follow-up in an integrated research network that leverages the EHR. SUPERHERO will transform the health care provided to youth with hypertension disorders to address the burden of hypertension and CVD across the life course to ultimately reduce deaths due to CVD, as well as establish rigorous methods in the field to best use EHR data.36
