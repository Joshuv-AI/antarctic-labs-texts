ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThis study examined the latent cognitive structure of the 10/66 Dementia Research Group protocol and assessed its psychometric properties in culturally diverse older adults in Latin America and the Caribbean.METHODSData from 10,838 adults aged 65+ across five countries were analyzed. Confirmatory factor analyses tested unidimensional and multidimensional models representing major cognitive domains. Model fit, reliability, and a hierarchical second‐order structure were examined. Measurement invariance was assessed across normative and non‐normative subsamples and gender.RESULTSA multidimensional structure emerged, comprising memory, orientation, executive functioning, semantic/language, and visuospatial domains, along with a higher‐order global cognition factor. Most domains demonstrated adequate fit and acceptable reliability. The second‐order model provided the best overall fit. Invariance was supported across all groups tested.DISCUSSIONThis study provides the first empirical validation of the 10/66 protocol's latent structure, enabling the derivation of standardized cognitive scores and strengthening its utility for harmonized dementia research in diverse cultural settings.

BACKGROUND

Population aging has become a defining demographic shift worldwide, with the fastest growth occurring in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs; 1 Latin America and the Caribbean are experiencing a rapid increase in the proportion of older adults and marked gains in life expectancy, 2 creating an urgent need for culturally valid and scalable tools to assess cognitive health and identify early stages of cognitive impairment. Dementia and related neurocognitive disorders are major contributors of disability and dependence in the region; however, accurate detection remains constrained by the scarcity of neuropsychological instruments with demonstrated cross‐cultural validity and by limited availability of empirically validated diagnostic algorithms. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Equally important, there is a lack of regionally derived normative data (but see Sosa et al 7 ) capable of supporting valid interpretation of cognitive performance across diverse demographic and educational backgrounds in Latin America. 5 , 8

The 10/66 Dementia Research Group was established as a collaborative network of researchers, aiming to generate robust, comparable data on dementia in LMICs and to develop standardized, education‐ and culture‐fair diagnostic tools. 4 , 9 The protocol has been widely implemented across more than 20 countries, and its diagnostic algorithm has been validated in culturally diverse samples. 5 , 6 , 10 , 11 Although this work has had influence on global dementia research, the cognitive battery itself has never undergone a psychometric evaluation to establish its factorial structure, measurement properties, and suitability for generating domain‐specific scores. 12 , 13 Without such validation, the derivation of demographically adjusted normative values is essential for clinical interpretation and cross‐population comparisons across Latin America and high‐income countries. 14 , 15

Recent efforts such as the Harmonized Cognitive Assessment Protocol (HCAP); 16 have emphasized the importance of empirically validating latent cognitive domains to facilitate cross‐national comparability and harmonization. 17 , 18 Factor analytic approaches applied to the HCAP have demonstrated that multidimensional models of cognitive performance—encompassing domains such as memory, language, executive function, orientation, and visuospatial skills—provide a robust framework for summarizing neuropsychological performance and operationalizing diagnostic categories such as mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia. 17 , 19 In contrast, the 10/66 protocol was designed primarily for binary diagnosis (normal aging vs. dementia) and has not yet been formally evaluated through factor analysis methods to determine its latent domain structure or measurement validity.

Validating the factorial structure of the 10/66 cognitive protocol is essential for several reasons. First, it allows for the generation of domain‐specific factor scores that can capture gradations of cognitive performance. Second, it facilitates harmonization with other large‐scale studies of aging and cognition, such as the United States Health and Retirement Study (HRS), and other HCAP cohorts, thus supporting regional and global comparative analyses. Third and particularly relevant for the region, a validated latent structure forms the foundation for generating robust normative data that are demographically calibrated to the region's unique educational and sociocultural context. Norms derived from an empirically supported factor structure offer a scientifically rigorous basis for interpreting cognitive performance and improving early identification of impairment across diverse Latin American populations.

The present study aims to examine the psychometric and factorial validity of the 10/66 cognitive protocol using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). First, we tested whether defined cognitive domains (single factor)—memory, language/semantic fluency, orientation, executive functioning, and visuospatial ability—form reliable and well‐fitting latent constructs that accurately summarize performance on their respective indicators. Building on this foundation, we then evaluate multidimensional models to assess domain distinctiveness and interrelations, followed by higher‐order structures to explore a general cognitive factor. Then we evaluated measurement invariance of the derived models between included (cognitively healthy) and excluded (cognitively at‐risk and impaired) participants, as well as across gender, to determine whether the cognitive constructs were measured equivalently across groups and key demographic characteristics. Finally, we derived standardized factor scores to generate demographically adjusted cognitive metrics suitable for normative interpretation and cross‐group comparability. Drawing from advances in cultural neuropsychology and cross‐national data harmonization, this work provides the first CFA‐based validation of the 10/66 protocol, establishing empirically derived, domain‐specific metrics of cognitive performance suitable for use across diverse Latin American and Caribbean populations and enabling future harmonized analyses with global aging cohorts.

METHODS

Participants

Details of the 10/66 Dementia Research Group study design have been published elsewhere. 4 , 9 Briefly, the 10/66 study is a longitudinal multicenter, population‐based study originally conceived to estimate the prevalence, incidence, and determinants of dementia in LMICs. The study began with pilot validation work between 1999 and 2001 6 and was followed by the first cross‐sectional population surveys conducted from 2004 to 2006 across 11 sites in seven countries.

For the present analyses, we used baseline data from 10.838 community‐dwelling adults aged 65 years and older recruited from five countries in Latin America and the Caribbean: Cuba, the Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico, Mexico, and Peru. Participants were selected through one‐phase, door‐to‐door household surveys that invited all age‐eligible residents (≥65 years) to complete an in‐person cognitive and health assessment. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. The study was approved by local ethics committees and the King's College London Research Ethics Committee.

Trained interviewers conducted structured, in‐home assessments lasting 2–3 hours using standardized protocols. All interviewers and field examiners (four to nine per country) received uniform and standardized training, including (1) study protocol and procedures, (2) standard structured interview techniques, and (3) a two‐day specific training for structured clinical assessment and neurological/physical examination. Interviewers and field examiners were regularly monitored and supervised to ensure uniform data collection and procedures. In addition to direct cognitive testing/interview, informant interviews were obtained using the Community Screening Instrument for Dementia (CSI‐D; Hall et al., 1993) informant section, which collects collateral information on functional and behavioral changes. All assessments were adapted and validated in local languages to ensure cross‐site comparability.

Included sample

To develop normative cognitive reference data and evaluate the factorial structure of cognitive domains, a robust “cognitively unimpaired” sample was selected using procedures analogous to those described in the HRS–HCAP. 17 , 19 Individuals were excluded if they met any of the following: (1) evidence of dementia (based on the 10/66 dementia diagnostic algorithm; 23.7%); (2) cognitive impairment (probable MCI 2.3%); (3) reported or diagnosed stroke, Parkinson's disease, dementia, or other conditions such as depression that can impair cognitive performance (43.3%); (4) functional decline (assessed via informant reports and self‐reported activities of daily living; 7.1%). The resulting included subsample was intended to represent older adults free from known or probable cognitive impairment, representing expected cognitive performance in this population. This sample formed the basis for latent variable estimation and subsequent normative modeling. Although sample sizes differed between the included and excluded sample, the relative distributions of gender and age range were similar.

Measures

The 10/66 cognitive protocol includes: The CSI‐D, a cognitive test assessing orientation, attention, comprehension, and memory. 13 The Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease (CERAD) modified 10‐word list, an immediate and delayed recall test, evaluating episodic memory. 12 The NEUROEX, a concise, structured neurological examination designed to provide quantifiable assessments of frontal functioning, lateralizing signs, parkinsonism, ataxia, apraxia, and primitive reflexes. 20 Basic neuropsychological and functional measures embedded within the protocol, such as short constructional praxis, semantic verbal fluency, and verbal comprehension. 4 The Geriatric Mental State (GMS), a structured clinical interview, which includes assessments of orientation and mental status. 21 The WHO‐DASII, World Health Organzization Disability Assessment Schedule II. 22 Initial assignments of individual cognitive tests to theoretical domains were proposed by an expert committee of investigators based on prior work and neuropsychological theory, following procedures similar to those in the Indianapolis‐Ibadan Dementia Project. 17 , 23 Domains were intended to represent separable but correlated constructs of cognitive performance typically affected in aging and dementia: memory, language/semantic, orientation, executive functioning, and visuospatial ability. Analytic approach

The primary objective of the analyses was to construct and assess latent variable models that capture key domains of cognitive performance as measured by the 10/66 protocol. This involved a stepwise process: (1) preliminary descriptive examinations to guide sample selection and indicator definitions (as summarized in Table 1), (2) unidimensional CFA models for each of the five predefined domains, (3) multidimensional CFA models factors to evaluate domain distinctiveness, (4) higher‐order (hierarchical) CFA models incorporating a general factor overlying the specific domains, and (5) evaluate measurement invariance between the included and excluded sample.

Sociodemographic characteristics of the included and excluded sample

Unidimensional models (single factor) of memory, language/semantic fluency, orientation, executive functioning, and visuospatial ability, were fitted to determine if the hypothesized domains could be adequately represented as a single cognitive domain. These models serve as foundational elements, providing domain‐specific summaries suitable for operationalizing cognitive abilities.

Multidimensional correlated factors models were then used to test whether the domains operate as distinct yet interrelated constructs within the 10/66 protocol. Finally, second‐order (hierarchical) models were explored to assess whether a single overarching general cognitive factor could explain the intercorrelations among domain‐specific factors, potentially aiding in cross‐study harmonization and overall cognitive summaries.

Factor analysis methods

All CFA models were estimated in R (version 4.5) using the lavaan package. Overall, there was only 0.5% missing data across the variables of interest, which were imputed using the mice package in R when needed. The imputation process involved applying multiple imputation with predictive mean matching (pmm) for binary/numeric variables and polytomous logistic regression (polr) for ordinal ones, using a custom predictor matrix of demographic (age, gender, education), cognitive, and related variables across 20 iterations. The distributions of imputed variables were examined to ensure plausibility and alignment with original patterns. We employed weighted least squares mean and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimation, given its suitability for mixed continuous and categorical indicators. Indicators were modeled as continuous or categorical based on their scale of measurement (Table 1). To account for the nested structure of the data—participants clustered within countries—models were estimated incorporating the sampling design, which adjusts standard errors and fit statistics for cluster dependence without altering the underlying parameter estimates. 24 These scores represent each participant's expected position on the latent construct given their observed responses and model parameters.

Model fit was evaluated using multiple complementary indices: the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). Following conventional thresholds, 25 models were considered to exhibit satisfactory fit when SRMR ≤ 0.08, CFI ≥ 0.95, and RMSEA ≤ 0.05. In cases of misfit, models were iteratively refined by removing poorly performing indicators (e.g., those with low loadings or high residual correlations violating local independence). Internal consistency reliability for each latent domain was assessed using McDonald's omega (ωt), computed from the standardized factor loadings and residual variances. 26

Measurement invariance

To assess measurement equivalence of the latent cognitive constructs between the included and excluded samples, multigroup measurement invariance analyses were conducted using the best‐fitting confirmatory factor models. For each single‐domain factor (memory, orientation, executive, semantic/language, and visuospatial) and for the winning global model, a sequence of increasingly constrained models was tested using the WLSMV estimator in the lavaan package 27 with semTools utilities. 28 The baseline model assessed configural invariance, testing whether the same pattern of factor‐indicator relationships held across groups. Next, metric invariance constrained factor loadings to equality across groups, followed by scalar invariance, which additionally constrained item intercepts. Finally, strict invariance constrained both loadings, intercepts, and residual variances to equality. Changes in fit indices (ΔCFI, ΔRMSEA, ΔSRMR) were used to evaluate invariance, with ΔCFI ≤ 0.01 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015 considered evidence of adequate invariance. 29

To further evaluate measurement equivalence across demographic characteristics, the same invariance sequence was additionally conducted by gender. Following Bauer's recommendation 30 against discretizing continuous variables, age was incorporated as a continuous covariate predicting each latent factor within the multigroup model, rather than defining age‐based subgroups. This approach allows detection of age‐related differences at the construct level while preserving the full invariance testing sequence across gender groups.

Derivation of standardized cognitive scores

For each cognitive domain, factor scores were extracted from the normative sample using the Empirical Bayes Modal (EBM) method. Since factor scores are not normally distributed, a rank‐based normalization (Blom transformation) was applied to the normative sample scores prior to demographic adjustment. To map factor scores from the excluded sample onto the same normative scale, a penalized spline GAM was fitted on the normative Blom‐transformed scores and subsequently applied to the excluded sample, avoiding direct rank‐normalization in a non‐normative population. Demographic adjustment followed the procedure described by Jones et al., 17 whereby Blom‐transformed scores were regressed on age, gender, and education level, including all two‐way interactions. The residual from this regression —representing performance after removing age, gender, and education effects—was divided by the standard error of the estimate (SEE = SD × √(1 − R 2)), yielding a standardized adjusted score with mean ≈ 0 and SD ≈ 1 in the normative sample. T‐scores were computed as T = (standardized score × 10) + 50. The visuospatial domain followed the same pipeline applied directly to the composite raw score (sum of three items) rather than a CFA‐derived factor score.

RESULTS

Sample characteristics

Table 2 summarizes the characteristics of the included sample (n = 2,421) and the excluded group (n = 8,417). The two groups were broadly similar in age distribution and sex composition, indicating that the normative subsample is demographically representative of the overall 10/66 cohort. Educational levels and indicators of socioeconomic vulnerability were also comparable, although the normative group showed slightly higher educational attainment, consistent with the exclusion of individuals with cognitive or functional impairment.

As expected, clear differences emerged in cognitive and functional measures. The normative group demonstrated substantially higher cognitive summary scores (Cogscore = 2.6 ± 4.1 vs. 0.4 ± 0.6) and fewer informant‐reported difficulties (Relscore = 30.6 ± 1.9 vs. 28.5 ± 5.6), reflecting greater functional independence and better cognitive performance. Country distribution was similar across groups (Table S1). Overall, the included sample was demographically similar but cognitively and functionally healthier, aligning with its intended role as a reference group for psychometric calibration.

Single factor models

For the construction of each cognitive domain, different factorial models were implemented that differed in the number of items, complexity, and structure (Table 3). This was an iterative process aimed at finding the winning model (based on fit indices) for each cognitive domain.

Summary of model fit indices of single factor models

Memory domain

The memory factor included four indicators representing immediate and delayed recall processes (CERAD) and long‐term memory tasks (CSID). The model demonstrated excellent overall fit (χ 2 2 = 4.03, p = 0.13, CFI = 0.99, RMSEA = 0.02, SRMR = 0.01, Table 3). Average standardized loadings were moderate to high (mean λ ≈ 0.70), suggesting a coherent underlying construct. The internal consistency coefficient (ω = 0.77) indicated acceptable reliability. Several redundant indicators (e.g., nrecall, trialno, imprec, wordimm, learn1, learn2) were excluded due to conceptual and statistical overlap with the retained items. The two‐factor model differentiating short‐term from long‐term memory showed inadequate fit indices and was consequently discarded.

Semantic / language domain

The language/semantic domain initially included object naming and expressive language items from the CSI‐D. After excluding low‐informative indicators (e.g watch), the final model incorporated nine indicators encompassing naming (e.g., pencil, shoes, elbow, etc.), language use (bridge, hammer), and pragmatic/semantic comprehension (pray, chemist). The model achieved excellent fit (χ 2 25 = 14.11, p = 0.92, CFI = 0.975, RMSEA = 0.040, SRMR = 0.02, Table 3) and strong internal consistency (ω = 0.80). Average loadings were high (mean λ ≈ 0.75). Minor residual correlations were included between semantically related pairs (elbow‐knuckle; bridge‐pray) to capture shared cultural or lexical variance. An alternative second‐order structure separating expressive and comprehension subdomains did not improve fit and was therefore rejected.

Orientation domain

The orientation factor included seven indicators covering both temporal (month, day, year) and spatial orientation (town, street, store, chief). The final model demonstrated excellent global fit (χ 2 25 = 8.16, p = 1.00, CFI = 0.99, RMSEA = 0.030, SRMR = 0.020, Table 3). Despite the high RMSEA value, the overall pattern indicated satisfactory performance according to CFI, TLI and SRMR. Mean standardized loadings were moderate (λ ≈ 0.65), and internal consistency was acceptable (ω = 0.86). The season variable was excluded due to low variance and poor contribution to model stability. A second‐order model distinguishing temporal and spatial subdomains failed to converge adequately and was discarded.

Executive functioning domain

The executive domain presented the greatest modeling challenge due to the limited number of performance‐based indicators available in the 10/66 protocol. After excluding several redundant items (neo7a–neo7f), the final model retained two NEUROEX tasks which corresponded to the Luria sequences task (neo8a, neo8b), semantic fluency (animals), repetition fluency (repetition), a story task, and following instructions (nod, point). The model achieved excellent fit (χ 2 7 = 8.78, p = 0.312, CFI = 0.986, RMSEA = 0.020, SRMR = 0.014, Table 3). Average factor loadings were moderate (λ ≈ 0.55). Reliability was modest (ω = 0.65). Notably, the inclusion of nod and point—despite their weaker loadings—improved the conceptual coverage of executive functioning. Spliting the executive domain into two subfactors—praxis and motor functioning—decreased the model efficiency by not showing improvement from the baseline model and was disregarded.

Visuospatial domain

Due to the limited number of visuospatial indicators (only three candidate items), the tested combinations of items consistently resulted in models with inadequate fit indices or convergence issues, preventing a reliable confirmatory factor modeling solution (Table 3). Instead, this domain was represented by a composite observed variable derived from three tasks—copying a pentagon / circle, and the folded paper task—intended to summarize visuospatial constructional ability. Model‐based fit statistics were not computed for this domain, but the composite measure was retained for downstream analyses to ensure full cognitive domain representation.

Across the CFA results demonstrated excellent to adequate model fit and acceptable internal consistency for group‐level analyses. The language/semantic and memory domains showed the strongest psychometric properties, with high loadings and excellent model fit indices, while executive and orientation domains achieved acceptable reliability despite structural limitations. The visuospatial construct was retained as an observed composite variable due to measurement constraints. These results support a dimensional latent structure of cognitive performance within the 10/66 protocol, broadly consistent with theoretical expectations and prior harmonization frameworks such as HCAP. 17 , 18

Multidimensional models

To assess the global factor structure underlying the 10/66 cognitive protocol, we employed a broad analytic strategy including several theoretically grounded model specifications. These comprised a unifactorial model (Model I), a correlated (oblique) multidimensional model (Model II), an alternative estimation approach using maximum likelihood (Model III), a second‐order hierarchical model (Model IV), and a bifactor S‐1 model (Model V), following recent recommendations for bifactor specification (Table 4).

The unifactorial model (Model I, Table 4) specified all indicators loading on a single general cognitive domain, assuming a unitary underlying construct explaining all observed variance. This model is summarized in Table 4 and showed poor fit overall, as expected for a cognitive protocol designed to capture multiple domains, with inadequate CFI and TLI, and elevated RMSEA and SRMR, indicating that a multidimensional structure better represents the data.

The correlated factors model (oblique, Model II, Table 4) included the original four latent domains (memory, orientation, semantic/language, visuospatial, and executive), allowing free correlations between factors to reflect shared variance among distinct constructs. This model had adequate fit by RMSEA, CFI and TLI, suggesting reasonable but not optimal representation. A variant using maximum likelihood estimation (Model III, Table 4) yielded similar results to the correlated WLSMV model, confirming robustness to estimator choice but with slightly higher chi‐squared due to assuming continuous indicators.

To evaluate whether the domain‐specific factors could be represented within a broader hierarchical structure (Global Domain), we estimated a second‐order factor model (Model IV, Table 4) in which the four latent domains (memory, language/semantic, orientation, and executive functioning) loaded on a higher‐order general cognitive factor. Visuospatial ability was included as an observed composite indicator in the global factor.

The second‐order model (Model IV) showed strong overall performance (χ 2 [202] = 738.248, CFI = 0.958, RMSEA = 0.026, SRMR = 0.122), reflecting good fit across all major indices. The model was parsimonious, demonstrated coherent loadings from each domain onto the general factor, and yielded strong reliability for the higher‐order factor (ωh = 0.923) with an H index of 0.887, indicating high construct replicability. The proportion of variance in item covariances attributable solely to the general factor (ECV = 0.42) and the percentage of unique covariances explained by G (PUC = 0.706) provided further evidence of a well‐defined global cognitive dimension.

Following Eid et al. 31 and Mansolf and Reise, 32 we additionally estimated a bifactor S‐1 model (Model V, Table 4) as the appropriate modern specification for bifactor structures. In this model, memory items served as the reference domain loading exclusively on the general factor G, while items from all other domains loaded simultaneously on G and on their respective orthogonal specific factors (S‐orientation, S‐semantic, S‐visuospatial, S‐executive). All factors were constrained to be mutually orthogonal per the S‐1 specification. This model yielded acceptable fit (CFI = 0.930, RMSEA = 0.035, SRMR = 0.130), with bifactor indices indicating a strong general factor (ωh = 0.719, H index = 0.910, ECV = 0.352, PUC = 0.789).

To formally compare the second‐order and bifactor S‐1 models, we followed the procedure recommended by Mansolf and Reise, 32 computing the correlation between the G factor loadings from the bifactor S‐1 model and the indirect loadings on the general factor from the second‐order model. This differentiability index (r = 0.80) fell below the 0.90 threshold, indicating that the two models are empirically distinguishable rather than functionally equivalent. Notwithstanding, the second‐order model outperformed the bifactor S‐1 on global fit indices (ΔCFI = 0.028, ΔRMSEA = −0.0011) while yielding comparable bifactor indices (ECV, PUC), and offered a more parsimonious and theoretically interpretable representation of the data.

Taken together, these results indicate that the second‐order model provides the most parsimonious, stable, and well‐fitting representation of the cognitive structure assessed in the 10/66 protocol, while the bifactor S‐1 indices corroborate the presence of a strong general cognitive dimension that accounts for substantial common variance across domains.

Convergent and discriminant validity

The latent factor correlations derived from the second‐order model supported both convergent and discriminant validity of the cognitive domains (Table S2). Moderate positive correlations were observed among domains (r = 0.36–0.57), reflecting expected shared cognitive processes within the 10/66 battery, while maintaining clear differentiation across constructs. Memory showed the strongest associations with other domains, whereas visuospatial ability presented the weakest—consistent with the domain's more limited measurement coverage.

Discriminant validity was further supported by the Fornell–Larcker criterion: for each domain, the square root of the Average Variance Extracted (√AVE) exceeded its correlations with the remaining latent factors, indicating that each construct captures more variance from its own indicators than from other domains. Finally, all domains showed substantial correlations with the higher‐order general cognitive factor (r = 0.42–0.86), confirming that while domains contribute meaningfully to global cognition, they retain unique and non‐overlapping variance. Overall, the pattern of correlations is consistent with a multidimensional but integrated structure and supports the validity of the derived cognitive domains within the 10/66 protocol.

Measurement invariance between included and excluded samples

To evaluate whether the latent cognitive constructs were measured equivalently across the included (cognitively unimpaired) and excluded samples (cognitively at risk), a series of multigroup CFAs were conducted using each winning model (single and multi domains, Table S3 and Table S4). Configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance models were tested sequentially for each final single‐domain factor and for the higher‐order global model (Model V).

All single‐factor models (memory, orientation, executive, and semantic) demonstrated excellent model fit across groups, with negligible changes in CFI (ΔCFI ≤ 0.003), RMSEA (ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.006), and SRMR (ΔSRMR ≤ 0.02) across increasing levels of constraint (Table S3). These results support at least scalar invariance, indicating that the latent constructs had equivalent meaning and measurement properties in both groups.

Similarly, the hierarchical global model, comprising memory, orientation, semantic, executive, and visuospatial domains, showed strong evidence of configural, metric, and scalar invariance (ΔCFI ≤ 0.001; ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.001; ΔSRMR ≤ 0.002). Strict invariance constraints slightly reduced fit indices but remained within acceptable limits (CFI = 0.997, RMSEA = 0.0642, SRMR = 0.0302). This pattern indicates that the factor loadings and intercepts were invariant, while some residual variances differed between groups. Here, the absence of strict invariance is theoretically expected, given that the excluded group included individuals with MCI and dementia, in whom increased intraindividual variability and reduced test reliability typically lead to greater residual variance. Importantly, achieving scalar invariance supports the comparability of latent means across groups, justifying the use of parameters from the original sample to compute standardized T‐scores for the excluded participants.

Supplementary analyses further examined measurement invariance by gender, with age modeled as a continuous covariate following Bauer 30 The full invariance sequence was supported across all domain‐level models and the global model, with negligible changes in fit indices at each step (all |ΔCFI| ≤ 0.003, all ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.007), indicating that factor loadings, item thresholds, and residual variances were equivalent for men and women after controlling for age. Full results are presented in Table S5 and Table S6.

Derivation and distribution of standardized cognitive scores

Standardized cognitive scores were derived from the normative sample using factor score estimates followed by rank‐based normalization and demographic adjustment. The resulting scores showed the expected distribution in the normative sample (mean ≈ 0, SD ≈ 1 across age strata), supporting the adequacy of the standardization procedure. The same transformation functions were applied to the excluded group, allowing scores from both samples to be expressed on a common standardized scale. As expected, the excluded group showed lower scores and greater variability across domains, consistent with the inclusion of individuals with MCI and dementia.

Age‐referenced percentile distributions and corresponding T‐score equivalents for both samples are presented in Table S7. In the normative sample, percentile values approximated those expected under a standard normal distribution, whereas the excluded group exhibited lower median performance and wider dispersion, particularly in the oldest age groups. These findings indicate that the standardization procedure produced stable and clinically interpretable cognitive scores across the range of cognitive ability.

DISCUSSION

The present study provides the first comprehensive psychometric validation of the 10/66 cognitive protocol using factor analysis methods across five Latin American and Caribbean countries. Guided by neuropsychological theory and prior harmonization work in the HCAP and related studies, 17 , 18 , 19 we identified a multidimensional factor structure reflecting separable yet correlated domains of cognitive functioning—memory, orientation, executive functioning, semantic/language, and visuoconstructional ability—and a higher‐order global factor summarizing overall cognitive performance. Importantly, complementary analyses supported this interpretation. A bifactor S‐1 model did not meaningfully improve model fit relative to the second‐order structure and yielded bifactor indices inconsistent with essential unidimensionality (e.g., low explained common variance). In addition, the differentiability index indicated that the bifactor S‐1 and second‐order representations were empirically distinguishable rather than equivalent. Taken together, these findings suggest that general cognitive performance in the 10/66 protocol is best conceptualized as arising from a hierarchical organization of partially independent domains rather than a single dominant latent trait, reinforcing the interpretability and theoretical coherence of the second‐order model. Furthermore, the second‐order structure resonates with the clinical trajectory of neurodegenerative disease as it typically unfolds in older Latin American populations. MCI and dementia commonly manifest first as circumscribed deficits in specific domains—most notably episodic memory—while other cognitive functions remain relatively preserved. It is only as disease progresses that impairment generalizes across domains, ultimately converging into global cognitive decline. This pattern of differential domain vulnerability followed by eventual generalization is precisely what a hierarchical second‐order model captures: separable, clinically meaningful latent domains that nonetheless share a common higher‐order factor reflecting overall cognitive burden.

Consistent with theoretical proposals and prior findings from large‐scale studies such as the HRS–HCAP, the memory and executive domains showed strong factorial coherence and high reliability, while the language/semantic and orientation domains also demonstrated adequate psychometric properties. The visuospatial composite contributed to a broader hierarchical model that captured both specific and general variance in cognitive performance. Importantly, measurement invariance analyses confirmed that these latent constructs operated equivalently across the included and excluded groups, supporting configural, metric, and scalar invariance. This indicates that the same cognitive constructs were measured in both cognitively healthy and impaired participants, providing a robust foundation for deriving standardized, demographically adjusted factor scores and for extending the protocol's use to identify cognitive impairment and dementia. The lack of strict invariance—manifested as minor differences in residual variances—is theoretically expected given the inclusion of individuals with MCI and dementia in the excluded group, where greater intraindividual variability and reduced reliability are typical of neurodegenerative processes. Complementary analyses further demonstrated that measurement equivalence held across gender after accounting for age, suggesting that the constructs are measured with comparable precision regardless of sex and across the age range of the sample. Together, these findings support the generalizability of the measurement model across key demographic characteristics.

The derivation of demographically adjusted standardized scores represents a key translational output of this psychometric validation. When these normative parameters were applied to the excluded sample, scores were systematically lower and showed a progressive age‐related decline across most cognitive domains, providing validity criterion evidence for the derived scores.

This study advances the psychometric characterization of the 10/66 cognitive protocol in several ways. First, it provides empirical support for a multidimensional, hierarchically organized model of cognition that parallels frameworks validated in high‐income contexts, extending their applicability to Latin American populations. Second, it establishes the feasibility of generating domain‐specific and global standardized scores (e.g., T‐scores) adjusted for demographic factors, enabling harmonized cognitive metrics that can be compared across countries and linked with other international studies. Third, it lays the groundwork for future work toward algorithmic diagnosis of MCI and dementia based on empirically defined cognitive domains rather than categorical test cut‐offs, enhancing diagnostic precision and cultural fairness.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The 10/66 protocol was not originally designed for detailed psychometric modeling, and some domains—particularly visuospatial and executive functioning—are represented by a limited number of indicators, which may constrain measurement precision. Moreover, while our models were tested in a large, cross‐national sample, additional replication using independent or longitudinal data would strengthen generalizability and allow examination of measurement stability over time. Although the multidimensional CFA models showed acceptable to excellent fit on global indices such as CFI, TLI, and RMSEA, the SRMR values were higher than ideal. This pattern was observed across all multidimensional structures, including the correlated factors and second‐order models. Importantly, this systematic elevation of SRMR is a well‐documented artifact in complex CFA models with large numbers of indicators, mixed item formats, and unbalanced domain density. The 10/66 protocol includes both categorical and continuous measures, residual correlations added for substantive reasons, and several domains represented by relatively few indicators. Each of these characteristics increases the average absolute residual correlation, which SRMR directly reflects, even when overall model fit is strong. Moreover, with a very large sample, small discrepancies between the observed and model‐implied correlation matrices become statistically magnified.

The validated domains could enable several applications such as standardized domain and global scores for epidemiological modeling, MCI case‐finding, and dementia progression studies in the 10/66 protocol. Integrating informant data (CSI‐D) with performance‐based latent scores could further refine impairment detection. Cross‐validation in longitudinal 10/66 waves will assess predictive validity for incident dementia, while harmonization with HCAP will support global comparative research.

Overall, our work provides a robust, replicable measurement model for the 10/66 protocol—one that transforms a diagnostic screening tool into a precision instrument for cognitive aging research in LMICs. In conclusion, our findings provide strong evidence that the 10/66 cognitive protocol captures core neurocognitive domains relevant to aging and dementia in Latin America and the Caribbean. The validated multidimensional structure and demonstrated measurement equivalence across cognitively healthy and impaired groups support its use as a foundation for developing demographically standardized scores and diagnostic algorithms for MCI and dementia. These results position the 10/66 dataset as a valuable resource for harmonized cognitive aging research and for advancing equitable dementia assessment in global contexts.
