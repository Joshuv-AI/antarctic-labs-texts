A sensorimotor function construct to assess mild cognitive impairment risk
Authors: Amal A Wanigatunga, Hang Wang, Di Huang, Anis Davoudi, Ryan J Dougherty, Qu Tian, Jennifer A Deal, Alison G Abraham, Eleanor M Simonsick, Alden L Gross, Susan M Resnick, Luigi Ferrucci, Jennifer A Schrack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONIdentifying precursors to mild cognitive impairment (MCI)—the transitional stage between unimpaired cognition and dementia—is a public health priority. While sensory and motor impairments are each linked to MCI, their integrated contribution as a sensorimotor construct remains underexplored.METHODSWe analyzed cross‐sectional data from two US cohorts: Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC; n = 880) and Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging (BLSA; n = 681), excluding individuals with stroke, Parkinson's disease, or dementia. A composite sensorimotor score—based on hearing, vision, olfaction, balance, gait speed, and grip strength—was created using factor analysis. Structural equation modeling assessed associations with MCI, adjusting for key covariates.RESULTSHigher sensorimotor function was associated with lower odds of MCI: ARIC odds ratio (OR) = 0.53 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.40–0.71); BLSA OR = 0.59 (95% CI: 0.43–0.81).DISCUSSIONSensorimotor function appears robustly related with MCI in a large sample of older adults. These findings highlight the potential value of incorporating sensorimotor assessments in early detection for cognitive decline. Longitudinal research is needed to understand temporality.Keywords: dementia risk, motor performance, older adults, sensory integration, structural equation modeling

BACKGROUND

Early detection of persons at risk for, or living with, mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is an important public health priority. MCI is an emerging state of cognitive impairment, beyond normal aging, that frequently precedes a dementia diagnosis. Diagnosing MCI relies on clinical judgement using data from informant information, neuropsychological testing, 1 and sometimes neuroimaging. 2 Sensory and motor functioning are often measured clinically, but not formally used in helping to diagnose MCI. Yet, a growing number of studies have demonstrated a link between sensory and motor functioning and cognitive impairment. 3

Substantial evidence suggests that slowed gait speed and impaired motor function precede declines in memory, executive function, processing speed, and global cognition, and also precede the onset of MCI, by as many as 10 to 15 years. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Yet, a limitation of slowed physical functioning as an early marker is that it commonly occurs with aging and is not highly specific to dementia progression. 3 , 9 , 10 Sensory impairments, specifically in hearing, 11 , 12 , 13 vision, 14 , 15 and olfaction, 16 , 17 have also been linked to incident cognitive decline, MCI, and dementia. Yet, to date, most studies investigating the link between sensory and cognitive functioning have typically considered the independent effects of each type of sensory impairment or motor impairment or dual effects, 18 as opposed to their cumulative effect.

Sensorimotor functioning—the integration of sensory information with motor function signaling to perform tasks and interact with the environment—is a novel domain that reflects complex thinking and mental ability. 19 Recent studies support the notion that sensorimotor function is heavily intwined with cognitive processes (e.g., spatial coding, 20 kinesthetic imagery, 21 semantic processing 22 ) that impact how individuals function in daily life. A recent study found that the addition of separate mid‐life sensory and motor functioning variables to traditional parameters improved the prediction of cognitive decline and impairment. 23 To this end, impairment resulting from degraded information and integration from across multiple sensory and motor systems may augment and differ from established associations between single sensory impairment or motor impairment and cognitive decline, and gaps remain about how to best operationalize sensorimotor function. Defining which measures of sensorimotor function convey the most information about MCI risk may help identify the most pertinent measures for screening and intervention efforts.

This study aimed to create a parsimonious sensorimotor assessment encompassing several commonly measured sensory and motor function indices and explore its relationship with MCI. First, we constructed a sensorimotor score comprising commonly administered sensory and motor function tests included in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study, a study of older community‐resident adults with an average age of ≈ 80 years. Second, we evaluated the association between this algorithmically derived sensorimotor function score with concurrent MCI. Third, we examined the external validity of the sensorimotor construct in relation to MCI in a well‐functioning sample of older adults enrolled in the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging (BLSA). We hypothesized that lower/poorer sensorimotor function would be associated with higher MCI risk in both cohorts.

METHODS

Study design and participants

The ARIC study

The ARIC study is a prospective cohort study conducted across four US communities, including Forsyth County, North Carolina; Washington County, Maryland; Minneapolis, Minnesota; and Jackson, Mississippi, where only Black participants were enrolled. The study enrolled 15,792 men and women aged 45 to 64 years at baseline (1987–1989) and was initiated to explore the mechanisms of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular risk factors. Evaluation was conducted twice per participant, 3 years apart. 24

The present analysis used data collected during the sixth ARIC examination (conducted in 2016–2017). At the sixth visit, 3699 individuals completed assessments of motor and physical function. Among this group, 957 participants also underwent vision testing as part of the Eye Determinants of Cognition (EyeDOC) ancillary study from the Washington County and Jackson sites. They also completed hearing and smell evaluations. Participants who identified as neither White nor Black were excluded (n = 4 Asian participants). Individuals with a prior history of stroke (n = 38), Parkinson's disease (n = 4), or Alzheimer's disease (n = 21) were also excluded. Further, nine participants without measures on body mass index (BMI) and one participant without reported education were also excluded. The sample for this analysis was 880 men and women who were evaluated between June 2016 and December 2017 (Figure S1 in supporting information). This study adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Institutional review board approval was obtained, and each participant provided written informed consent. All measures undergo quality control procedures and only valid data for this study were shared for analyses. 25

The BLSA

Established in 1958, the BLSA is conducted through the National Institute on Aging's Intramural Research Program. This ongoing cohort study enrolls community‐dwelling adults to investigate how aging and disease interact and affect physical and cognitive function. Participants must be free of major chronic illnesses and cognitive and functional impairments at enrollment. Participants are followed for life through clinical or home visits as needed, scheduled every 4 years for those < 60 years, every 2 years for those aged 60 to 79 years, and annually for those ≥ 80 years. 26 In 2003, the study was redesigned to use clear criteria for selecting healthy participants and to collect more detailed information on different aspects of their health. 27 All measures undergo quality control procedures and only valid data for this study were shared for analyses. 28

Longitudinal data were drawn from motor and physical function tests conducted between October 2012 and December 2018, comprising 858 participants and 1491 observations. Additionally, 1091 participants completed sensory testing, including hearing, vision, and olfaction, resulting in 2704 observations between January 2012 and July 2019. For cross‐sectional analysis, only data from each participant's most recent visit were used when participants had all available data on sensorimotor functioning. Participants were excluded if they were under age 50 years (n = 95) or had a prior history of stroke, Parkinson's disease, or non‐amnestic cognitive impairment (n = 39). After further excluding participants with dementia (n = 8), the final analysis comprised 681 participants (Figure S2 in supporting information). The institutional review board of the intramural research program of the National Institutes of Health approved the study protocol. At each visit, each participant provided written informed consent.

Sensory function

Hearing

In ARIC, pure tone air conduction audiometry was performed in a sound‐treated booth at visit 6 (2016–2017). 29 Trained technicians measured air conduction thresholds for each ear at standard octave frequencies ranging from 0.5 to 8 kHz, using insert earphones and an Interacoustics AD629 audiometer. 30 Pure tone average (PTA) was derived by averaging four frequencies at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz in the better‐hearing ear. Hearing loss was defined as PTA > 25 dB hearing loss in the better‐hearing ear based on those commonly used in population research. 31

PTA measured in BLSA was conducted in the same manner, except the standard octaves ranged from 0.5 kHz to 4 kHz. 32

Vision

In ARIC, vision was assessed using a combination of visual acuity and contrast sensitivity measures. 33 Distance visual acuity was assessed by having participants read letters from a backlit ETDRS (Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study) chart while wearing their usual corrective lenses, if applicable. An autorefractor was also used to measure refraction and corrected visual acuity. 34 , 35 , 36 Impaired presenting visual acuity was defined as a visual acuity < 20/40, which is equivalent to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) values > 0.30. Contrast sensitivity was assessed using the Mars letter contrast sensitivity test while participants wore their usual corrective lenses. 37 Impaired contrast sensitivity was defined as a score more than one standard deviation below the sample mean, with logContrast < 1.48 measured unilaterally using the Mars chart, in accordance with established thresholds. 38

In BLSA, composite visual impairment was defined as having impairment in any one of the following: contrast sensitivity, visual fields, visual acuity, and stereo acuity. 33 Beginning in January 2015, the BLSA vision protocol was updated to measure visual acuity unilaterally at a 3 meter distance using ETDRS eye charts (Precision Vision), with participants wearing their usual corrective lenses. 14 Better‐eye presenting visual acuity was assessed using the ETDRS chart, which determined whether the participant can correctly identify at least three of five letters. Impairment was defined as visual acuity < 20/40, consistent with commonly used clinical and research criteria. 39 Contrast sensitivity was assessed bilaterally using the Pelli–Robson chart, reported in logContrast units, with participants wearing their corrective lenses when applicable. 14 , 40 Contrast sensitivity impairment was defined as a logContrast score < 1.55, representing one standard deviation below the average reported in previous population‐based studies of adults aged ≥ 60 years. 39 , 41 Visual fields were assessed using the Humphrey Field Analyzer (Carl Zeiss Meditec) with an 81‐point, single‐intensity (24 dB) full field test covering 60°. Monocular visual fields were assessed directly, while binocular visual fields were estimated using a previously established method. 42 Composite binocular visual fields were scored based on the total number of missed points out of 96, comprising 56 points in the central field, 18 in the upper peripheral field, and 22 in the lower peripheral field. Impairment was defined as a score exceeding one standard deviation above the cohort mean for total points missed. Stereo acuity, assessed using the Randot Stereo Vision Test, was considered impaired if participants were unable to discern a depth differential of more than 85 seconds of arc. 43

Olfaction

In ARIC, olfactory function was assessed at visit 5 using the 12‐item Sniffin’ Sticks Odor Identification Test, with 1 point awarded for each correctly identified odorant for a maximum score of 12. 44 Olfactory impairment was defined as a total score of ≤ 6, in accordance with established thresholds of the 10th percentile. 45

In BLSA, olfaction was assessed using the 16‐item Sniffin’ Sticks Odor Identification Test. 46 Two versions of the test were randomly used at the initial assessment and then alternated at follow‐up visits. Due to the absence of established impairment thresholds in the literature, impaired olfaction was defined as a score of ≤ 8 on test A and ≤ 7 on test B, corresponding to the 11th percentile of each test version within the current sample. 39

Motor function

In both ARIC and BLSA, physical functioning was assessed using the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), 47 , 48 which includes full tandem stance (FTS) balance tests (held for 10 seconds in ARIC and 30 seconds for BLSA), five repeated chair stands performed as quickly as possible, and usual gait speed (UGS). Notably, gait speed was measured over a 4‐meter course in ARIC, whereas a 6‐meter course was used in BLSA. UGS was classified as fast if it exceeded 0.8 m/s in ARIC, based on the sample's data distribution, and 1.0 m/s in BLSA. 47 The ability and duration to maintain each standing balance pose were recorded. For the FTS, the binary indicator reflected the ability to hold each pose for the full 10 or 30 seconds. Chair stand performance was quantified as the rate of stands per second. The cutoff for chair stand pace (CSP) was defined as > 0.28 stands per second in ARIC and > 0.44 stands per second (representing the lowest quintile) in BLSA, based on the current sample data distribution. 49 , 50

In both ARIC and BLSA, hand grip strength was assessed in kilograms using a Jamar hydraulic hand dynamometer. 51 Three trials were performed on each hand, and the highest measurement from either the left or right hand was used for analysis. Cutoffs for normal grip strength were set at 32 kg for men and 20 kg for women, consistent with thresholds used previously. 52

MCI

MCI was determined through an evaluation of neuropsychological test results. In the ARIC study, the diagnosis of MCI was determined using criteria based on the National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) workgroups and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5). A classification committee evaluated participants identified with MCI by algorithm. The diagnosis was confirmed by two independent reviewers, a neurologist and a neuropsychologist, while a third reviewer resolved any discrepancies when needed. 44

In BLSA, consensus diagnoses of cognitive impairment are made collaboratively by a multidisciplinary research team that includes neurologists, neuropsychologists, research nurses, and research assistants, 53 using neuropsychological testing and clinical data. MCI is diagnosed using the Petersen criteria, which require evidence of cognitive impairment in one or more domains without significant decline in the ability to perform activities of daily living. 53 , 54 , 55

Covariates

Sociodemographic characteristics, including age, race, sex, and education level (college or higher), were self‐reported during the health interview. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). Self‐reported chronic conditions included cardiovascular disease, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and cancer. The number of comorbidities were classified as less than two versus two or more conditions. Depressive symptoms were measured as ≥ 9 on the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CES‐D) 11‐item questionnaire for the ARIC study 56 and ≥ 20 on the CES‐D 30‐item questionnaire for BLSA. 57

Statistical approach

Means and standard deviations (SD) or frequencies and percentages were calculated for demographic characteristics and sensorimotor function measures.

Structural equation modeling (SEM) allows constructs to be represented as latent variables by capturing the shared covariation among indicators within a measurement model. This method typically results in less measurement error compared to using algebraic composites or sum scores. 58

A unidimensional sensorimotor functioning construct was derived using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) in ARIC. Model fit was assessed using absolute fit indices, including the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Criteria for excellent model fit were defined as CFI ≥ 0.95, TLI ≥ 0.95, RMSEA ≤ 0.05, and SRMR ≤ 0.08. 59 The initial CFA model included seven binary indicators of sensory and motor function: hearing, vision, olfaction, FTS balance, five‐repeat CSP, UGS, and grip strength, with higher values reflecting better functioning. To achieve a more parsimonious model, a leave‐one‐out approach was applied. This final CFA model retained six binary indicators: hearing, vision, olfaction, FTS balance, UGS, and usual grip strength. This final model was selected based on improved model fit indices and more evenly distributed factor loadings across indicators.

Cross‐cohort measurement invariance of the latent construct sensorimotor score was evaluated using multi‐group confirmatory factor analysis with weighted least squares mean and variance (WLSMV) adjusted estimation for binary indicators. Models were fit sequentially starting with configural invariance (same factor structure across cohorts) and then scalar invariance (equality constraints appropriate for categorical indicators). Model fit was assessed using RMSEA, CFI, TLI, and SRMR. Because WLSMV chi‐squared values are not differenced in the usual way, nested model comparisons were conducted using the DIFFTEST procedure. When full scalar invariance was rejected, partial scalar invariance models were evaluated by freeing theoretically and empirically supported non‐invariant thresholds identified via modification indices. Improvement in model fit was confirmed using DIFFTEST.

Separate logistic regression models were constructed to estimate the association between the sensorimotor functioning construct and each individual sensory and motor component with MCI. These models were used to evaluate the absolute standardized effect sizes with MCI. Receiver operating curves were also constructed to evaluate discriminatory ability (area under the curve) of the sensorimotor functioning construct and each individual sensory and motor component for MCI status.

After establishing acceptable fit of the measurement model in ARIC, the unidimensional sensorimotor functioning construct was modeled as an exogenous variable (predictor), with MCI as the endogenous outcome. The full model was adjusted for age, race, sex, education, and BMI. The association between the latent sensorimotor functioning factor and MCI was estimated using probit regression, and the resulting coefficients were converted to odds ratios (ORs) for the binary outcome by multiplying each coefficient by a scaling factor of 1.7 and then exponentiating it to approximate ORs derived from logistic regression. 60 A sensitivity analysis was performed to further adjust for self‐reported cardiovascular disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, cancer, diabetes, and depressive symptoms as potential confounders.

A significance level of α = 0.05 was used for all analyses. Descriptive statistics were calculated using R version 4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), while SEM was carried out using Mplus version 8.4 (Muthén & Muthén).

Table S2 in supporting information shows the factor loadings for each CFA model using a full 7‐indicator model and then subsequent 6‐indicator models from leave‐one‐out models. Factor loadings for full 7‐indicator model ranged from 0.19 for olfaction to 0.77 for UGS. Between the best‐fitting models that removed either CSP or grip strength, the 6‐indicator model that removed CSP appeared to have more evenly distributed factor loadings across the rest of the indicators and was selected for SEM modeling to test the association between sensorimotor function and MCI status.

Configural invariance supported cross‐cohort comparability of the sensorimotor functioning construct, with excellent model fit (χ 2[18] = 34.60, RMSEA = 0.034, CFI = 0.974, TLI = 0.957, SRMR = 0.043). This indicates a common factor structure across both studies. Full scalar invariance was not supported (Table S3 in supporting information; Δχ 2[4] = 22.85, p = 0.0001). Further examination of modification indices identified threshold non‐invariance for FTS. A partial scalar invariance model freeing this threshold in BLSA cohort fits significantly better than the full scalar model (Δχ 2[1] = 9.15, p = 0.0025). Thus, partial scalar invariance was established, supporting meaningful latent‐level comparisons across cohorts while acknowledging localized measurement non‐invariance.

Higher latent factor sensorimotor scores were consistently associated with lower odds of the outcome in unadjusted models for both studies, with larger absolute standardized effect sizes and lower Akaike information criterion values than any individual component (Table S4 in supporting information). While several components showed statistically significant associations, the strength or model fit of the latent factor remained the greatest, supporting its value as a parsimonious summary of shared construct‐related variance.

The latent factor sensorimotor score demonstrated superior discrimination compared to all individual components in both cohorts (Table S5 in supporting information). Across both datasets, aggregation of component measures into a latent factor consistently improved discrimination relative to single‐indicator models.

Mean sensorimotor score and standard deviation from the 6‐indicator model that removed CSP generated from the SEM model was −3.23 (1.75) and −1.87 (2.54) for ARIC and BLSA, respectively. In the ARIC sample, for 70‐years‐olds, the average (SD) of sensorimotor scores is −2.86 (1.70), and for 80‐year‐olds, the average (SD) of sensorimotor scores is −3.98 (1.59). In the BLSA sample, for 70‐years‐olds, the average (SD) of sensorimotor scores is −1.65 (2.29), and for 80‐year‐olds, the average (SD) of sensorimotor scores is −3.41 (2.94). The prevalence of MCI tended to be lower across tertiles of higher sensorimotor functioning (Table S6 in supporting information).

DISCUSSION

Comprehensive evaluation of sensorimotor function may prove a useful non‐threatening potential early marker of present or emerging MCI. The results were consistent between two cohorts of older adults who together represent a wide range of low‐ to well‐functioning older adults without high morbidity burden. Importantly, our study suggests the key role that sensorimotor function may play in cognitive impairment. Future research to understand whether changes in sensorimotor function underlie the etiology of MCI is warranted.

Our findings suggest a robust relationship between sensorimotor function and MCI, supporting current evidence that underscores the importance of multisensory integration in cognitive health. 19 In 2024, our group published evidence showing that components of sensorimotor function, including multisensory impairment and fine motor movement, are linked with early cognitive impairment—a state preceding mild cognitive impairment. 39 Also, Schubert et al. showed that including mid‐life sensory and motor functioning improved risk prediction of 10‐year cognitive decline or impairment. 23 Together, this suggests a potential casual pathway, whereby declines in sensorimotor function might precede if not contribute to cognitive deficits. 39 As both findings are from cross‐sectional study designs, longitudinal studies will be required to reveal the temporality of this relationship.

Traditionally, sensorimotor assessments such as threshold detection of passive motion, joint position reproduction, and active movement extent discrimination primarily focused on proprioceptive function. 61 , 62 However, these tests often overlook broader sensory functions, including hearing and olfaction. Our approach integrates commonly performed sensory and motor tests, encompassing hearing, vision, olfaction, standing balance, UGS, and hand grip strength. Balance is linked with postural control and cognitive resource engagement in complex environments. 63 Grip strength potentially captures neuromuscular coordination and functional reaching of the upper body. 64 Usual walking speed reflects mobility and cognitive processing during the planning and execution of spatial navigation. 65 Hearing is involved with enhancing spatial awareness by informing navigation through auditory cues. 66 Vision plays a role in spatial orientation and environmental awareness, likely influencing both locomotion and cognitive load during movement. 67 , 68 Last, olfactory sensations are linked to cognitive processes like impulsivity and inhibitory control that may influence sensorimotor function and behavior. 69 Collectively, these components highlight the non‐threatening as well as informative nature of these sensorimotor assessments that may be important for screening for earlier identification of cognitive impairment and possibly intervention/corrective action.

We selected and evaluated a 6‐indicator model of sensorimotor functioning that included auditory (hearing), visual (vision), olfactory (olfaction), postural stability (standing balance), locomotion (UGS), and upper body strength (grip strength). The 6‐indicator model, which replaced grip strength with CSP, exhibited arguably equivalent model fit. A notable distinction between the two models was that the model with CSP demonstrated a lower factor loading for hearing and a higher loading for UGS. One possible explanation for these differences is that the relationship between hearing and lower extremity performance, which includes gait speed, may be moderated by cognitive processes 70 that control timing and coordination of movement. 50 Another reason is that grip strength may reflect discrete upper extremity functionality, representing aspects of fine motor skills and cognitive engagement that do not overlap with the relationship between hearing and mobility. 71 While both models exhibit slight variations in their relationships among the indicators of sensorimotor functioning, their comparable overall fit suggests that either model may be acceptable for preventive efforts or clinical use.

Although full scalar invariance was not supported, partial scalar invariance was achieved by freeing the threshold of one indicator, supporting meaningful latent‐level comparisons across cohorts. The observed non‐invariance was primarily attributable to structural differences in task protocols and resulting ceiling effects. Specifically, the shorter administration of FTS held in 10 seconds in BLSA, combined with its younger and healthier profile, rendered the task too easy, leading to near‐universal success, collapsed discrimination, and a shifted effective threshold. In contrast, the longer and more challenging FTS held for 30 seconds protocol in BLSA preserved response variability and functioned more stably across cohorts, thereby anchoring the latent scale more effectively. Additional contributors to differential item functioning may include differences in age distributions, cohort health status, and selection processes. Together, these findings highlight the importance of aligning measurement difficulty with cohort characteristics and demonstrate the value of partial invariance approaches for maintaining construct comparability when localized, protocol‐driven measurement differences are present.

Our study has limitations. First, the temporality of the relationship between sensorimotor function and MCI cannot be determined due to the cross‐sectional design. Second, several traditionally used sensory and motor measures such as proprioception, temperature, and muscle tone were not included in our evaluation. 72 Third, while we conducted analyses in ARIC and BLSA cohorts to increase external validity of the sensorimotor construct, the generalizability of the results may not extend to minoritized or historically underrepresented populations. Fourth, there are different confounding structures for many of the exposures that were combined into the sensorimotor construct. Fifth, there is a possibility for residual confounding. Sixth, mean sensorimotor score differences between ARIC and BLSA due to cohort variability (e.g., differing study methods to evaluate both sensorimotor function and MCI) should be acknowledged. Seventh, dichotomizing indicators of sensorimotor function as impaired or unimpaired potentially discards important information (e.g., dose–response). Eighth, the low MCI prevalence in both cohorts may attenuate the magnitude of association between sensorimotor function and MCI toward the null and is likely greater in populations with higher MCI prevalence. The strengths of this study include the use of large, well‐characterized cohorts and the inclusion of multiple objectively measured sensory and motor measures.

In conclusion, our study provides evidence that higher sensorimotor function measured with commonly used assessments is associated with a lower risk of MCI. The integration of sensory and motor abilities may play a role in cognitive impairment during the early stage of MCI, highlighting the importance of maintaining sensory and motor health as part of a broader and preventive strategy against cognitive decline. Longitudinal studies are warranted to replicate our findings and to assess temporality of the relationship of sensorimotor function with MCI, above and beyond its individual sensory and motor components.
