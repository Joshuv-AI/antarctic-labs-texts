Potential Drug–Drug Interactions in Patients Undergoing Hemodialysis: A Retrospective Observational Study
Authors: Buthainah B. Al-Ammash, Fawaz A. Alharbi, Omar D. Al-hejaili, Mansour A. Mahmoud, Mazin A. A. Abdelaziz, Alaa I. Alrais, Shahad A. Albakri, Ibrahim F. Alharbi, Sara A. Felemban, Abdullah H. Almatrafi, Salman A. Alabdali, Yaser M. Alahmadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:To evaluate the prevalence, characteristics, and determinants of potential drug–drug interactions (pDDIs) among patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) undergoing hemodialysis (HD) at a tertiary care hospital in Al-Madinah Al-Monawara, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia.Methods:Data for this retrospective observational study were extracted from the electronic medical records of adult patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with ESRD and receiving hemodialysis (HD) between October 2022 and March 2023. The pDDIs were identified and classified using Lexicomp® (Wolters Kluwer, Alphen aan den Rijn, Netherlands). Descriptive statistics summarized patient characteristics and pDDIs, and univariate logistic regression was implemented to investigate the determinants associated with the occurrence of pDDI.Results:Among 287 patients, 264 (92%) experienced at least 1 pDDI. In total, 4,157 pDDIs were identified, most of which were Category C (75.4%), followed by Categories B and D. Category X interactions were uncommon (n = 78), with clopidogrel–omeprazole and clopidogrel–esomeprazole being the most frequent. Patients received a mean of 14 ± 5.1 medications. Logistic regression showed that the number of prescribed medications was significantly associated with pDDI occurrence (odds ratio = 2.03; p = 0.001).Conclusion:The pDDIs are highly prevalent among patients with ESRD undergoing HD, with polypharmacy identified as the main determinant. These findings highlight the importance of structured medication review, multidisciplinary collaboration, and clinical pharmacist involvement to reduce medication-related risks and optimize patient outcomes.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) impacts roughly 10% of individuals globally, underscoring its role as a major public health concern [1]. Chronic kidney disease is associated with high morbidity and mortality rates and is often accompanied by cardiovascular disease [2], making it a major cause of death worldwide [23]. The global incidence of CKD increased by 156% from 1990 to 2021, totaling more than 20 million cases annually [4]. Overall, the incidence and prevalence of CKD are higher in low-income countries than in developed countries [56]. Forecasts for the year 2050 indicate a significant surge in mortality associated with CKD, positioning it as the fifth most common cause of death globally [7].

Chronic kidney disease is associated with several risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, prolonged use of analgesic medications, and lifestyle factors such as alcohol consumption, smoking, and obesity, all of which are linked to progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [89]. End-stage renal disease (Stage 5 CKD) is characterized by severe loss of renal function, with a glomerular filtration rate of < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2 [10]. By 2030, the number of patients requiring dialysis is expected to exceed 5.5 million worldwide [11]. In ESRD management, treatment options include hemodialysis (HD), peritoneal dialysis, and kidney transplantation [12]. According to the Saudi Center for Organ Transplantation registry data, 19,715 patients with ESRD were undergoing HD in Saudi Arabia (SA) in 2020 [13].

Patients undergoing HD require multiple treatment regimens to manage the complex array of comorbidities associated with ESRD [14]. Furthermore, the expanding geriatric population in SA is expected to increase the prevalence and incidence of dialysis, further complicating medication prescribing in this population [15]. Consequently, patients with dialysis-dependent CKD face a substantial disease burden and are frequently exposed to marked polypharmacy, typically receiving approximately 10 medications on average, which considerably complicates safe and appropriate prescribing [16]. Moreover, ESRD severity is influenced by the combined effects of the disease and the medications used to manage it [17]. Therefore, patients undergoing HD are highly vulnerable to medication-related problems (MRPs).

An MRP is any event or circumstance that interferes with or has the potential to interfere with, the achievement of an optimal treatment outcome [18]. The MRPs contribute substantially to rising morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs, placing a considerable burden on healthcare systems [19].

The presence of a second drug can significantly alter the clinical effects of a primary medication; this clinical event is known as a drug-drug interaction (DDI), resulting in changes in pharmacological or clinical responses. These interactions may occur at various stages of pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic processes, including absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion [20]. Cytochrome P450 enzymes, a membrane-bound superfamily highly expressed in hepatocytes and also present in extrahepatic tissues such as the brain and kidneys, mediate approximately 80% of oxidative phase I metabolism of xenobiotic and endogenous substrates. Their activity can be markedly altered by genetic polymorphisms and by drug-induced inhibition or induction, leading to significant interindividual variability in drug clearance and response [21].

Understanding these mechanisms is essential for predicting potential DDIs (pDDIs). Pharmacists play a vital role in detecting and preventing pDDIs, thereby contributing to patient safety and improved therapeutic outcomes. Before medications are dispensed, pharmacists apply their expertise in pharmacotherapy, drug mechanisms, and patient-specific factors to identify potential interactions and assess associated risks using clinical guidelines and drug interaction databases such as Lexicomp [22]. Pharmacists also collaborate with other healthcare professionals to identify and mitigate risks associated with polypharmacy, which is common among patients undergoing dialysis [20]. Recent studies have demonstrated that pharmacist-led interventions significantly improve clinical outcomes and quality of life among patients with CKD by reducing MRPs [23]. In addition, pharmacists support medication adherence and disease management through patient education and counseling, thereby reducing the likelihood of adverse drug reactions. This role is particularly important in patients with chronic conditions and complex medication regimens, underscoring the critical contribution of pharmacists to healthcare delivery. However, pDDIs remain insufficiently characterized in SA, potentially compromising therapeutic safety, treatment effectiveness, and quality of life among patients with CKD.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the characteristics and determinants of pDDIs among patients with ESRD undergoing HD at a tertiary care hospital in Al-Madinah Al-Monawara, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. The findings provide insight into the burden of pDDIs in renal practice and highlight the importance of regular prescription review to identify and prevent MRPs.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective observational study, conducted at a single institution, involved patients with ESRD having HD at a tertiary care hospital in Al-Madinah Al-Monawara, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. Electronic medical records were reviewed to identify and categorize pDDIs. The study was conducted over a 6-month period from October 2022 to March 2023. Adult patients aged ≥ 18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of ESRD who were receiving regular HD were included. Patients with incomplete medical records were excluded.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of General Health Affairs in Al-Madinah Al-Monawara, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (IRB No. 22-053). As this study involved a retrospective analysis, formal informed consent was not required for the participants. This study was conducted according to the Helsinki Declaration and reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist to ensure the rigor of our observational analysis. All patient data were handled in accordance with institutional policies to ensure privacy and anonymity throughout the study.

Sample size

The following formula was utilized to estimate the required sample size for this study:

N = Z2 × P (1–P)/d2, where N is the sample size, Z is the Z statistic (Z = 1.96 for a level 95% confidence interval), P is the expected prevalence (p = 0.25) and d is the margin of error (d = 0.05). Accordingly, N = 1.962 × 0.25 (1–0.25)/0.052 = 287, yielding a minimum required sample size of 287 patients.

Data collection

A standardized data collection form was used to assess pDDIs. Extracted variables included patient demographics (medical record number, age, gender, and comorbidities), comorbidities (diabetes, anemia, mineral bone disorder, dyslipidemia, congestive heart failure, ischemic heart disease, atrial fibrillation, deep vein thrombosis, venous thromboembolism, irritable bowel syndrome, gout, psychiatric disorders, epilepsy, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, tuberculosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, multiple sclerosis, organ transplantation), and prescribed medications.

The pDDIs were identified and categorized using Lexicomp® drug interaction software (Wolters Kluwer, Alphen aan den Rijn, Netherlands), which classifies interactions into Categories A, B, C, D, and X according to their clinical significance. For each patient, the total number of pDDIs, interaction severity, number of prescribed medications, and number of hospital readmissions during the study period were recorded.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were executed using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous data are expressed as means ± standard deviations, while categorical variables are summarized using frequencies and percentages. To evaluate differences in categorical variables across groups, the Chi-square test was employed, or Fisher's exact test was used when the expected frequency was insufficient. Univariate logistic regression was adopted to isolate predictors of pDDI occurrence, adopting a p - value of less than 0.05 (2-tailed) as the criterion for statistical significance.

Results

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population showed a nearly equal distribution of males (49.1%) and females (50.9%). Overall, 264 patients (92%) experienced at least 1 pDDI. Hospital readmissions occurred in 27.2% of patients, with 20.2%, 6.3%, and 0.7% experiencing 1-3 readmissions. In contrast, 72.8% had no hospital readmissions. The mean number of prescribed medications was 14 (± 5.1) per patient (Table 1). The majority of participants had 4 comorbidities (26.8%), followed by 3 comorbidities (22%) and 5 comorbidities (21.3%) (Fig. 1). Anemia was the most prevalent comorbidity, affecting 95.1% of patients, followed by hypertension (91.3%), metabolic bone disease (90.2%), and diabetes mellitus (48.1%).

A total of 4,157 pDDIs were identified in 264 (92%) patients. Category C was the most common interaction category (75.4%), followed by Category B (Fig. 2). Table 2 presents the 20 most common pDDIs and their risk ratings, including details of the identified Category X interactions. Most interactions were categorized as Category C, indicating a moderate level of clinical concern. Among Category C pDDIs, the most frequent combinations were amlodipine + iron sucrose (n = 183), iron sucrose + nifedipine (n = 127), amlodipine + furosemide (n = 80), and carvedilol + iron sucrose (n = 80). Two of the top 20 pDDIs were classified as Category D: diclofenac gel + meloxicam and acetylsalicylic acid + diclofenac gel. Only 1 interaction was classified as Category B (amlodipine + atorvastatin). Among Category X pDDIs, the most frequently identified interactions were clopidogrel + omeprazole (n = 17), clopidogrel + esomeprazole (n = 13), alfacalcidol + cholecalciferol (n = 5), calcium polystyrene sulfonate + meloxicam (n = 5), and carvedilol + salbutamol (n = 5). The least frequent Category X interactions included cetirizine + potassium chloride (n = 1), chlorpheniramine + potassium chloride (n = 1), dextromethorphan + linezolid (n = 1), diphenhydramine + tiotropium (n = 1), and lorazepam + metronidazole (n = 1).

Table 3 shows significant differences between the 2 age groups (16–55 years and ≥56 years) with respect to pDDI occurrence, number of comorbidities, and number of prescribed medications. Older individuals (≥56 years) had a higher prevalence of pDDIs than younger individuals (48.9% versus (vs.) 43.9%, p = 0.045) and were more likely to receive more than 5 medications (86.8% vs. 5.2%, p < 0.001). Conversely, younger individuals (16–55) were more likely to have multiple comorbidities, with 69% having 6 or more comorbidities compared with 23% of older individuals (p = 0.003). These findings suggest greater comorbidity burden among younger patients, whereas medication burden was higher among older patients. Logistic regression analysis showed that gender, hospitalization status, and number of comorbidities were not significantly associated with pDDI occurrence. However, the number of prescribed medications was significantly associated with pDDI occurrence (odds ratio (OR) = 2.029; 95% CI: 1.476–2.789; p = 0.001), indicating that an increase in the number of medications was associated with a higher risk of pDDIs (Table 4).

Discussion

Patients with ESRD undergoing HD represent one of the most pharmacologically vulnerable populations owing to their complex comorbidities, altered pharmacokinetics, and exposure to multiple concurrent medications [24]. Moreover, the rapidly increasing global prevalence of CKD has coincided with a rise in polypharmacy, resulting in an increased risk of DDIs and MRPs [725].

In this retrospective study of 287 patients with ESRD receiving maintenance HD, 4,157 pDDIs were identified. Notably, 92% of patients experienced at least one pDDI, highlighting the clinical relevance of this issue [26]. The mean number of prescribed medications was 14 ± 5.1 per patient, reflecting substantial polypharmacy and aligning with previous reports in CKD populations worldwide. For example, Liu et al [16] reported an average of 10 medications per patient undergoing HD, whereas Sales et al [27] observed similar polypharmacy patterns in the United States. Such extensive medication use is likely attributable to the coexistence of multiple chronic conditions, the need for symptom management, and frequent consultations with multiple healthcare providers.

Regarding demographics, male and female participants were represented in nearly equal proportions (49.1% vs. 50.9%), suggesting balanced access to dialysis care within the study population. Logistic regression analysis demonstrated that gender was not a significant predictor of pDDIs (OR = 0.64, p = 0.448), consistent with the findings of Khaiser et al [28] who reported no gender-related differences in interaction frequencies. Although no statistically significant association was observed between the number of comorbidities and pDDIs (p = 0.058), the mean comorbidity burden (4.4 ± 1.58) reflected the clinical complexity characteristic of patients with ESRD. This observation supports the multifactorial nature of medication-related risk in dialysis populations, where polypharmacy remains the primary determinant of pDDIs. Similarly, Shahzadi et al [29] in a retrospective observational study of patients with CKD, reported a pDDI prevalence of 69.7% and did not identify comorbidity burden as a strong independent predictor of pDDIs. The pattern of comorbidities observed in our study—anemia (95.1%), hypertension (91.3%), metabolic bone disease (90.2%), and diabetes (48.1%)—is consistent with regional and international reports [30]. Hypertension and diabetes are major etiological factors for CKD, whereas anemia and mineral bone disorders are well-recognized complications of advanced kidney disease and long-term dialysis. The coexistence of these conditions often necessitates the concurrent use of antihypertensive agents, iron preparations, erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, phosphate binders, and vitamin D analogs, all of which may contribute to pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic interactions.

Among the 4,157 identified pDDIs, Category C interactions accounted for 75.4%, followed by categories B and D. Category X interactions, representing contraindicated drug combinations, were relatively uncommon (n = 78) but clinically important. The most common Category C interactions included amlodipine + iron sucrose, iron sucrose + nifedipine, and amlodipine + furosemide. These combinations generally require monitoring rather than therapeutic modification. In contrast, Category X interactions such as clopidogrel + omeprazole and clopidogrel + esomeprazole have well-established clinical significance. These proton pump inhibitors inhibit CYP2C19, thereby impairing the conversion of clopidogrel to its active metabolite and reducing its antiplatelet efficacy [31]. These findings are consistent with previous pharmacogenomic and clinical evidence supporting the avoidance of such combinations in patients with cardiovascular disease, including those with ESRD [32].

Logistic regression analysis identified the number of prescribed medications as the only significant factor associated with pDDI occurrence (OR = 2.03, p = 0.001). This finding underscores polypharmacy as the primary driver of interaction risk and supports previous reports by Liu et al [25] and Shouqair et al [33] which demonstrated a substantial increase in pDDI risk with an increasing number of concurrently prescribed medications. In contrast, neither hospitalization frequency nor comorbidity burden was significantly associated with pDDIs. This may reflect the relatively standardized monitoring and medication adjustment practices inherent to HD care. Age-stratified analysis showed that patients aged ≥ 56 years had a higher frequency of pDDIs (48.9% vs. 43.9%, p = 0.045) and were more likely to receive more than 5 medications (p < 0.001), reflecting the well-established association between aging and polypharmacy. Conversely, younger patients (16–55 years) had a higher comorbidity burden (p = 0.003). These findings highlight the importance of individualized medication review strategies tailored to patient age and clinical complexity.

A 2025 meta-analysis of elderly African patients encompassing 15 studies reported an overall pDDI prevalence of 52.53%, with rates reaching as high as 90.1% in Ethiopian cardiovascular cohorts [34]. The higher prevalence observed in our study may be attributable to differences in prescribing patterns, healthcare access, patient characteristics, and the use of a comprehensive interaction database such as Lexicomp (Wolters Kluwer, Alphen aan den Rijn, Netherlands). The predominance of cardiovascular and hematologic drug classes among interacting pairs is consistent with the findings of Hughes et al [35] who identified anticoagulants, antihypertensive, and iron preparations as common sources of clinically relevant interactions in older patients with CKD.

From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the critical role of pharmacists in detecting, preventing, and managing pDDIs in HD settings. Through systematic medication reconciliation and the use of interaction-screening tools, clinical pharmacists can identify high-risk drug combinations before dispensing and collaborate with physicians to optimize therapy [36]. Pharmacist-led interventions have been shown to reduce MRPs and hospital readmissions among patients with ESRD, thereby improving clinical outcomes and healthcare cost-effectiveness. The high prevalence of Category C interactions further underscores the need for continuous monitoring rather than immediate discontinuation of therapy. Patient education regarding potential adverse effects, regular assessment of therapeutic response, and appropriate laboratory monitoring may help mitigate the clinical impact of these interactions. Furthermore, the development of institution-specific prescribing guidelines, regular prescriber training, and the integration of DDI alerts into electronic prescribing systems may further reduce medication-related risks.

Despite its contributions, this study is subject to certain limitations. First, it was conducted at a single HD center, which may restrict the applicability of the findings to alternative contexts and populations. Second, the retrospective design relied on routinely documented electronic medical records, which may vary in completeness and accuracy. Third, clinical and biochemical outcome data were unavailable, preventing assessment of the actual occurrence and severity of the identified pDDIs. Fourth, the 6-month study period may not have been sufficient to capture long-term prescribing trends or changes in medication use. Future multicenter prospective studies incorporating clinical outcomes are required to validate these findings and further clarify the clinical significance of high-risk pDDIs among patients with ESRD undergoing HD.

In conclusion, this study provides valuable insights into the burden, pattern, and determinants of pDDIs among patients with ESRD undergoing HD in SA and identifies polypharmacy as the primary predictor of pDDIs. Although most interactions were classified as moderate in severity, their cumulative effects may adversely affect treatment outcomes and patient safety. These findings emphasize the importance of pharmacist-led interventions to mitigate medication-related risks. Regular medication reviews, multidisciplinary collaboration, and active clinical pharmacist involvement are essential for optimizing pharmacotherapy in patients with ESRD. The implementation of structured pharmacovigilance programs within dialysis centers is recommended to minimize MRPs and improve the overall quality of care.
