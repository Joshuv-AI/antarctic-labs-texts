Asymmetries from a charged memory-burdened PBH
Basabendu Barman, Kousik Loho, and Óscar Zapata

Abstract
We explore a purely gravitational origin of observed baryon asymmetry and dark matter (DM) abundance from asymmetric Hawking radiation of light primordial black holes (PBH) in presence of a non-zero chemical potential, originating from the space-time curvature. Considering the PBHs are described by a Reissner-Nordström metric, and are produced in a radiation dominated Universe, we show, it is possible to simultaneously explain the matter-antimatter asymmetry along with right DM abundance satisfying bounds from big bang nucleosynthesis, cosmic microwave background and gravitational wave energy density due to PBH density fluctuation. We also obtain the parameter space beyond the semiclassical approximation, taking into account the quantum effects on charged PBH dynamics due to memory burden.

2
Dynamics of a charged BH

We consider the Reissner-Nordström (RN) metric, which is a solution to Einstein’s field equations that describes the spacetime around a spherically symmetric non-rotating body with mass
M
and an electric charge
Q
. Other than spherical symmetry we also have the assumption that the space is empty from matter (there is only an electromagnetic field).
The RN metric can be written as

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-\frac{\Delta}{r^{2}}\,dt^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{\Delta}\,dr^{2}+r^{2}\,d\Omega^{2}\,,

(3)

where

\displaystyle\Delta=r^{2}-2\,GM_{\text{BH}}\,r+G\,Q^{2}=(r-r_{+})\,(r-r_{-})\,,

(4)

with

\displaystyle r_{\pm}=M_{\text{BH}}G\pm\sqrt{M_{\text{BH}}^{2}\,G^{2}-G\,Q^{2}}\,.

(5)

We denote
M_{P}=1/\sqrt{8\pi\,G}
as the reduced Planck mass. Clearly, when
Q\to 0
, the metric should approach the Schwarzschild metric. For
M_{\text{BH}}<|Q|/\sqrt{G}
, no horizon exists and the singularity at
r=0
is naked. On the other extreme, for
M_{\text{BH}}>|Q|/\sqrt{G}
,
\Delta=0
or
r_{\pm}
, and the metric becomes singular. However, this is just a coordinate singularity. For
M_{\text{BH}}=|Q|/\sqrt{G}
, we get an extremal BH, where the inner and outer horizons merge into one. The temperature of the charged BH is given by

\displaystyle T_{\text{BH}}=4M_{P}^{2}\,\frac{\sqrt{M_{\text{BH}}^{2}-Q^{2}/G}}{\left(M_{\text{BH}}+\sqrt{M_{\text{BH}}^{2}-Q^{2}/G}\right)^{2}}\,,

(6)

with corresponding entropy,

\displaystyle S_{\rm BH}\equiv\frac{4\pi\,r_{+}^{2}}{4G}=\frac{1}{8}\,\left[\left(\frac{M_{\text{BH}}}{M_{P}}\right)+\sqrt{\left(\frac{M_{\text{BH}}}{M_{P}}\right)^{2}-8\,\pi\,Q^{2}}\right]^{2}\,.

(7)

Note that, for
Q\to 0
, we obtain the Hawking temperature for a Schwarzschild BH:
T_{\text{BH}}=M_{P}^{2}/M_{\text{BH}}
and
S_{\rm BH}=(1/2)\,\left(M_{\text{BH}}/M_{P}\right)^{2}
.

Since the BH has an electric charge, the ensuing electric potential plays the role of a chemical potential
\mu
at the horizon surface, in such a way the BH thermally evaporates into charged particles with charge
q_{i}
with a non zero chemical potential
\mu_{i}
.
Now, the energy of loss per unit area per unit time due to Hawking radiation is given by

\displaystyle\frac{dE}{dx^{2}\,dt}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i}\frac{g_{i}}{4}\int\,\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\frac{p}{e^{\left(p+\mu_{i}\right)/T_{\text{BH}}}\pm 1}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=f}\frac{g_{i}}{4}\,T_{\text{BH}}^{4}\,f\left(\frac{\mu_{i}}{T_{\text{BH}}}\right)-\sum_{i=b}\frac{g_{i}}{4}\,T_{\text{BH}}^{4}\,b\left(\frac{\mu_{i}}{T_{\text{BH}}}\right)\,,

(8)

where
g_{i}
is the degree of freedom and
\mu_{i}
is the chemical potential associated with evaporated species
i
. The functions,
f(x)=\left(-3/\pi^{2}\right)\,\text{Li}_{4}\left[-e^{-x}\right]
and
b(x)=\left(3/\pi^{2}\right)\,\text{Li}_{4}\left[e^{x}\right]
, with
\text{Li}_{a}[z]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}z^{k}/k^{a}
being a polylogarithmic function. The total energy lost per unit time is obtained by multiplying with the area of the event horizon
4\pi\,r_{+}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\frac{dE}{dt}\simeq-\frac{G\sqrt{M_{\text{BH}}^{2}-Q^{2}/G}}{2}\,T_{\text{BH}}^{3}\,\left(\sum_{i=f}g_{i}\,f(\mu_{i})+\sum_{i=b}g_{i}\,b(\mu_{i})\right)\,,

(9)

where
\mu_{i}\ll T_{\text{BH}}
. This can be further simplified to,

\displaystyle\frac{dE}{dt}\simeq-\frac{M_{P}^{4}}{16\pi M_{\text{BH}}^{2}}\,\left(\sum_{i=f}g_{i}\,f(\mu_{i})+\sum_{i=b}g_{i}\,b(\mu_{i})\right)=-\epsilon\,\frac{M_{P}^{4}}{M_{\text{BH}}^{2}}\,,

(10)

assuming
M_{\text{BH}}\gg Q/\sqrt{G}
and considering the BH to be a perfect blackbody with
\epsilon=27/4\times g_{\star,H}(T_{\text{BH}})\,\pi/(480)
, where the factor 27/4 accounts for the graybody factor
[
101
,
102
,
33
,
39
]
. Here,

g_{\star,H}(T_{\text{BH}})\equiv\sum_{i}\omega_{i}\,g_{i,H}\,;g_{i,H}=\begin{cases}1.82&\text{for }s=0\,,\\
1.0&\text{for }s=1/2\,,\\
0.41&\text{for }s=1\,,\\
0.05&\text{for }s=2\,,\\
\end{cases}

(11)

with
\omega_{i}=2\,s_{i}+1
for massive particles of spin
s_{i}
,
\omega_{i}=2
for massless species with
s_{i}>0
and
\omega_{i}=1
for
s_{i}=0
. At temperatures
T_{\text{BH}}\gg T_{\text{EW}}\simeq 160
GeV, BH evaporation emits the full SM particle spectrum according to their
g_{\star,H}
weights, while at temperatures below the MeV scale, only photons and neutrinos are emitted. For
T_{\text{BH}}\gg 100
GeV, the particle content of the SM corresponds to
g_{\star,H}(T_{\text{BH}})\simeq 106.75
. Assuming
g_{\star,H}(T_{\text{BH}})
remians approximately constant over the BH lifetime, we can integrate Eq. (
10
) to obtain the BH mass evolution as

\displaystyle M_{\text{BH}}(t)\simeq M_{\text{in}}\,\left[1-3\,\epsilon\,\frac{M_{P}^{4}\,t_{\rm in}}{M_{\text{in}}^{3}}\,\left(\frac{t}{t_{\rm in}}-1\right)\right]^{1/3}\,,

(12)

where
M_{\text{BH}}(t=t_{\rm in})=M_{\text{in}}
is the BH mass at formation. This provides the BH lifetime as,

\displaystyle\tau=t_{\rm in}+\frac{M_{\text{in}}^{3}}{3\,\epsilon\,M_{P}^{4}}\,.

(13)

If
q_{i}
is the electric charge of a species
i
emitted during BH evaporation, the rate of electric charge loss due to BH evaporation is given by,

\displaystyle\frac{dQ}{dt}

\displaystyle=4\pi\,r_{+}^{2}\,\sum\frac{g_{i}\,q_{i}}{4}\,\left(n_{i}-\bar{n}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=4\pi\,r_{+}^{2}\,\sum\frac{g_{i}\,q_{i}}{4}\,\int\,\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\left(\frac{1}{\exp\left[\frac{p+\mu_{i}}{T_{\text{BH}}}\right]+1}-\frac{1}{\exp\left[\frac{p-\mu_{i}}{T_{\text{BH}}}\right]+1}\frac{}{}\right)

\displaystyle\simeq-4\pi\,r_{+}^{2}\,\sum\frac{g_{i}\,q_{i}}{4}\,\left(\frac{\mu_{i}\,T_{\text{BH}}^{2}}{6}+\frac{\mu_{i}^{3}}{6\pi^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left[\frac{\mu_{i}}{T_{\text{BH}}}\right]^{2}\right)\,,

(14)

which leads to

\displaystyle\frac{dQ}{dt}\simeq-Q\,\sum_{i}\frac{g_{i}\,q_{i}^{2}}{24}\,T_{\rm BH}+\mathcal{O}[Q^{3}]\,,

(15)

where
\mu_{i}=q_{i}\,Q/r_{+}
is the chemical potential that originates from the BH charge and we have considered
\mu_{i}/T\ll 1
. The above equation shows

\displaystyle Q(t)\simeq Q_{\rm in}\exp\left[-\sum\frac{g_{i}\,q_{i}^{2}}{24\,\pi}\,\frac{M_{P}^{2}\,t}{M_{\text{in}}}\right]\,,

(16)

where we have considered
M_{P}^{4}\,t/M_{\text{in}}^{3}\ll 1
and used Eq. (
12
). Considering contribution solely from the SM fermions,
\sum g_{i}\,q_{i}^{2}=36\times(2/3)^{2}+36\times(-1/3)^{2}+12\times(-1)^{2}=32
. Here
Q_{\rm in}=Q(t_{\rm in})
is the initial BH charge. Thus, the electric charge of the BH is lost exponentially with time.

2.1
Baryon asymmetry from a charged BH

Following “no-hair theorem”, a BH is fully characterized by just three observable classical parameters: mass, electric charge, and angular momentum. It implies that once a BH forms, any other information about the matter that collapsed to form it is lost (or “invisible” to an external observer). As a result, a BH does not retain any information about global quantum numbers, e.g., baryon number. Thus, the non-existence of the BH’s global charge (due to the no-hair theorem) implies that losing particles or radiation with that charge does not change the BH’s fundamental parameters. Therefore, if continuous global symmetries such as
B-L
existed, when a charged particle becomes trapped inside a black hole, it would be impossible to detect this charge from outside the event horizon. As a result, the charge would seem to be ‘erased,’ contradicting the principle of charge conservation
[
103
]
.

In presence of an explicit chemical potential at the BH horizon with radius
r_{\rm BH}=2\,G\,M_{\text{BH}}
, we can follow the exact same approach as before that leads to a global
B-L
charge evolution given by,

\displaystyle\frac{dQ_{B-L}}{dt}\simeq 4\,\pi\,r_{\rm BH}^{2}\times\frac{T_{\text{BH}}^{2}}{24}\sum g_{i}\,\mu_{i}\,q_{i}^{bl}=\sum\frac{g_{i}\,\mu_{i}\,q_{i}^{bl}}{96\pi}\,,

(17)

where we have dropped
\mu_{i}^{3}
relative to
\mu_{i}\,T^{2}
, assuming
\mu_{i}\ll T
, and also
M_{\text{BH}}\gg Q/\sqrt{G}
. Also, note that the above equation certainly does not follow charge conservation. Here
q_{i}^{bl}
is the
B-L
charge of the emitted particles, while the sum is over particles only. Further, in Eq. (
17
) we have used the Fermi–Dirac distribution exclusively, since in the SM only fermions carry baryon number. If one assumed that bosons could carry baryon number then one should also use the Bose–Einstein distribution.

Because the expansion of the Universe, the Ricci scalar
\mathcal{R}
induces a chemical potential given by

\mu_{i}=\lambda\,q_{i}^{bl}\,\dot{\mathcal{R}}/(8\pi\,M_{P}^{2})
. Then, the
Q_{B-L}
loss rate becomes

\displaystyle\frac{dQ_{B-L}}{dt}=\lambda\sum\frac{g_{i}\,\left(q_{i}^{bl}\right)^{2}}{96\pi}\,\frac{\dot{\mathcal{R}}}{8\pi\,M_{P}^{2}}\,,

(18)

where the sum is over only particles. Now, The time derivative of the Ricci scalar in a FLRW background
[
97
]
,

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{R}}=-9\,(1+w)\,(1-3w)\,H^{3}\,,

(19)

where
w
denotes the background equation of state. To the lowest order, no net asymmetry is generated during the de Sitter
(w=-1)
and during the radiation dominated
(w=1/3)
phase, however this is not true for matter dominated (MD) epoch, for which
w=0
. At the one loop level, including quantum correction one finds
[
104
,
97
,
95

\displaystyle 1-3w=\frac{5}{6\pi^{2}}\,\left(\frac{g_{3}^{2}}{4\pi}\right)^{2}\,\frac{\left(N_{c}+5N_{f}/4\right)\,\left(11N_{c}/3-2N_{f}/3\right)}{2+(7/2)\,\left(N_{c}\,N_{f}/(N_{c}^{2}-1)\right)}\,,

(20)

where
N_{c}

is the

\alpha_{3}\equiv g_{3}^{2}/(4\pi)\approx 0.118

[
105

is the

, and
1-3w\simeq 8\times 10^{-3}

has a non-zero value. Further, for SM,
\sum g_{i}\,(q_{i}^{bl})^{2}=36\times(1/3)^{2}+9\times(-1)^{2}=13

\displaystyle\frac{dQ_{B-L}}{dt}=-\frac{245\,\lambda}{5056\,\pi^{4}}\,\frac{\alpha_{3}^{2}}{M_{P}^{2}}\times\sum g_{i}\,\left(q_{i}^{bl}\right)^{2}\begin{cases}\left(\frac{1}{2t}\right)^{3}&\text{for RD}\,,\\[10.0pt]
\frac{237\,\pi^{2}}{980\,\alpha_{3}^{2}}\,\left(\frac{2}{3t}\right)^{3}&\text{for MD}\,,\end{cases}

(21)

where in the second case, we have
w=0
. One can then integrate the above expressions till the BH lifetime [cf.Eq. (

\displaystyle Q_{B-L}(\tau)\simeq-\frac{\lambda}{M_{P}^{2}}\,\sum g_{i}\,\left(q_{i}^{bl}\right)^{2}\times\begin{cases}\frac{245\,\alpha_{3}^{2}}{80896\,\pi^{4}}\,\frac{1}{t_{\rm in}^{2}}&\text{for RD}\,,\\[10.0pt]
\frac{1}{576\,\pi^{2}}\,\frac{1}{t_{\rm in}^{2}}&\text{for MD}\,,\end{cases}

(22)

at the end of BH evaporation, where
\tau\approx t_{\rm ev}\gg t_{\rm in}
is assumed. Since,
Q_{B-L}
is equivalent to the
B-L
number, we can write the final asymmetry as

\displaystyle Y_{B}^{0}=\frac{n_{B}}{s}\Big|_{0}=Q_{B-L}\,a_{\rm sph}\,\frac{n_{\rm BH}}{s}\Big|_{t_{\rm ev}\approx\tau}=Q_{B-L}\,a_{\rm sph}\,Y_{\rm BH}(t_{\rm ev})\,,

(23)

where
a_{\rm sph}=28/79
is the spahelron conversion factor and
Y_{B}^{0}\simeq 8.7\times 10^{-11}
is the observed baryon asymmetry today
[

\displaystyle Y_{\rm BH}(T_{\text{ev}})\Big|_{\beta<\beta_{c}}\simeq\frac{3\,\sqrt{3}\beta}{2}\,\mathcal{F}(g_{\star},\,g_{\star s})\,\left(\frac{10\,\gamma^{2}}{g_{\star}(T_{\text{in}})}\right)^{1/4}\,\left(\frac{M_{P}}{M_{\text{in}}}\right)^{3/2}\,,

(24)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(g_{\star},\,g_{\star s})=\left(\frac{g_{\star}(T_{\text{ev}})}{g_{\star}(T_{\text{in}})}\right)^{3/4}\,\left(\frac{g_{\star}(T_{\text{in}})}{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{ev}})}\right)\,.

(25)

where

\displaystyle\beta_{c}=\left(\frac{g_{\star}(T_{\text{ev}})}{g_{\star}(T_{\text{in}})}\right)^{1/4}\,\frac{T_{\text{ev}}}{T_{\text{in}}}=\sqrt{\frac{t_{\rm in}}{\tau}}\,,

(26)

\displaystyle M_{\text{in}}=\gamma\,\frac{4\pi}{3}\,\rho_{R}\,\left(\frac{1}{H(t_{\rm in})}\right)^{3}\implies t_{\rm in}=\frac{M_{\text{in}}}{8\pi\gamma\,M_{P}^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle T_{\text{in}}=\left(12\,\gamma\,\sqrt{\frac{10}{g_{\star}(T_{\text{in}})}}\,M_{P}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\,\sqrt{\frac{M_{P}}{M_{\text{in}}}}\,.

\displaystyle Y_{B}^{0}\simeq 3\sqrt{3}\,\beta\,\left|Q_{B-L}\right|\,a_{\rm sph}\,\left(\frac{5\gamma^{2}}{8}\right)^{1/4}\,\left[\left(\frac{g_{\star}(T_{\text{in}})}{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{ev}})}\right)\,\left(\frac{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{ev}})}{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{in}})}\right)^{4}\right]^{1/4}\,\left(\frac{M_{P}}{M_{\text{in}}}\right)^{2}\,,

where we have neglected the time-dependence of

\displaystyle Y_{\rm BH}(T_{\text{ev}})=\frac{n_{\rm BH}(T_{\text{ev}})}{s(T_{\text{ev}})}=\frac{30}{\pi^{2}\,g_{\star s}(T_{\text{ev}})}\,\frac{M_{P}^{2}}{\tau^{2}\,T_{\text{ev}}^{3}\,M_{\text{in}}}\,,

\displaystyle T_{\text{ev}}=\left(\frac{45}{(2\pi^{2})\,g_{\star}(T_{\text{ev}})}\right)^{1/4}\,\sqrt{\frac{M_{P}}{\tau}}\,,

\displaystyle Y_{\rm BH}(\overline{T}_{\rm ev})\Big|_{\beta>\beta_{c}}=\left(\frac{405}{32}\right)^{1/4}\,\sqrt{\frac{3\epsilon}{\pi}}\,\left(\frac{g_{\star}(\overline{T}_{\rm ev})^{3}}{g_{\star s}(\overline{T}_{\rm ev})^{4}}\right)^{1/4}\,\left(\frac{M_{P}}{M_{\text{in}}}\right)^{5/2}\,.

\displaystyle\overline{T}_{\rm ev}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\,T_{\text{ev}}\,.

\displaystyle Y_{B}^{0}\simeq\sqrt{3}\left|Q_{B-L}\right|\,a_{\rm sph}\times\begin{cases}\frac{3\sqrt{3}\,\beta}{2}\,\left(\frac{10\,\gamma^{2}}{g_{\star}(T_{\text{in}})}\right)^{1/4}\,\left[\left(\frac{g_{\star}(T_{\text{ev}})}{g_{\star}(T_{\text{in}})}\right)^{3/4}\,\frac{g_{\star}(T_{\text{in}})}{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{ev}})}\right]\,\left(\frac{M_{P}}{M_{\text{in}}}\right)^{3/2}&\beta<\beta_{c}\,,\\[10.0pt]

Asymmetric dark matter from a charged BH

We now explore the possibility that the DM abundance is entirely due to the asymmetric component of a DM species, generated gravitationally because of a non-zero chemical potential generated in the dark sector. To achieve that, we write a pure gravitational interaction similar to the action in Eq. (
1
) as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\rm DM}=\int d^{4}x\,\sqrt{-g}\,\lambda_{\rm DM}\,\frac{\partial_{\mu}\,\mathcal{R}}{M_{P}^{2}}\,j^{\mu}_{\rm DM}\,,

(38)

with
\mu_{\rm DM}=\lambda_{\rm DM}\,q_{\rm DM}\,\mathcal{\dot{R}}/(8\pi\,M_{P}^{2})

q_{\rm DM}
is the charge (equivalent to
q^{bl}
in the visible sector) carried by each DM particle and a summation over the particles is implied. Here

is a current in the DM sector that generates an asymmetry within the dark sector only and
\lambda_{\rm DM}
is a free parameter that we shall constraint by demanding right abundance for the DM. Proceeding same as before, we can write down the time-evolution of net DM charge asymmetry as,

\displaystyle\frac{dQ_{\rm DM}}{dt}=\frac{3\,\Lambda_{\rm DM}\,g_{\rm DM}}{256\,\pi^{2}\,M_{P}^{2}}\,\,(1+w)\,(3w-1)\,H^{3}\,,

(39)

which is similar to Eq. (
17
), with
\Lambda_{\rm DM}=\lambda_{\rm DM}\,q_{\rm DM}^{2}
. Here
g_{\rm DM}
is the degrees of freedom of the corresponding DM candidate that depends on the DM spin. We integrate the above equation from
t_{\rm in}
till
t_{\rm ev}
to obtain the total dark charge generated,

\displaystyle Q_{\rm DM}(\tau)\simeq-\frac{\Lambda_{\rm DM}\,g_{\rm DM}}{M_{P}^{2}\,\pi^{2}}\,\frac{1}{t_{\rm in}^{2}}\begin{cases}\frac{135}{161792\,\pi^{4}}&\text{for RD}\\[10.0pt]
\frac{1}{576}&\text{for MD}\,,\end{cases}

(40)

where

is assumed. Again, since this is equivalent to

, i.e., difference between DM and anti-DM, we can determine the final DM yield as,

\displaystyle Y_{\rm DM}^{0}=\left|Q_{\rm DM}\right|\,\frac{n_{\rm BH}}{s}\Big|_{t_{\rm ev}\approx\tau}

\displaystyle\simeq\left|Q_{\rm DM}\right|\times\begin{cases}\frac{3\sqrt{3}\,\beta}{2}\,\left(\frac{10\,\gamma^{2}}{g_{\star}(T_{\text{in}})}\right)^{1/4}\,\left[\left(\frac{g_{\star}(T_{\text{ev}})}{g_{\star}(T_{\text{in}})}\right)^{3/4}\,\frac{g_{\star}(T_{\text{in}})}{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{ev}})}\right]\,\left(\frac{M_{P}}{M_{\text{in}}}\right)^{3/2}&\beta<\beta_{c}\,,\\[10.0pt]
\left(\frac{405}{32}\right)^{1/4}\,\sqrt{\frac{3\epsilon}{\pi}}\,\left(\frac{g_{\star}(\overline{T}_{\rm ev})^{3}}{g_{\star s}(\overline{T}_{\rm ev})^{4}}\right)^{1/4}\,\left(\frac{M_{P}}{M_{\text{in}}}\right)^{5/2}&\beta>\beta_{c}\,,\end{cases}\,.

(41)

To fit the whole observed DM relic density, it is required that

Y^{0}_{\rm DM}\,m_{\text{DM}}=\Omega_{\rm DM}h^{2}\,\frac{1}{s_{0}}\,\frac{\rho_{c}}{h^{2}}\simeq 4.3\times 10^{-10}{\rm GeV}\,,

(42)

where

9.4\times 10^{32}\times\left(\frac{M_{\text{in}}}{100\,\text{g}}\right)^{9/2}&\beta>\beta_{c}\,,\end{cases}

(43)

considering the number of relativistic degrees of freedom to be fixed at 106. From Eqs. (

4
A charged BH with memory burden

So far we have discussed the BH dynamics under the semiclassical approximation. Here we introduce the effect of memory burden that results in a longer PBH lifetime. In order to go beyond the semiclassical picture and invoke the effect of memory burden, we first rewrite Eq. (
12
) as

\displaystyle M_{\text{BH}}(t)=M_{\text{in}}[1-\Gamma_{\rm BH}^{0}(t-t_{\rm in})]^{1/3}\,,

(46)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm BH}^{0}=3\epsilon\,M_{P}^{4}/M_{\text{in}}^{3}\,,

(47)

is the decay width associated with the PBH evaporation. We assume the semiclassical regime remains valid till

\displaystyle M_{\text{BH}}=q\,M_{\text{in}}\,.

(48)

In Refs.
[
77
,
75
]
, it has been
proposed that the quantum
effects start to become important when
M_{\text{BH}}=0.5\,M_{\text{in}}
or
q=0.5
. We consider
t_{q}
to be the time scale at which the semiclassical phase ends, beyond which the self-similar regime no longer remains valid. Then, from Eq. (
46
) we obtain

\displaystyle t_{q}=\frac{1-q^{3}}{\Gamma_{\rm BH}^{0}}\,.

(49)

Once the mass of the PBH reaches
q\,M_{\text{in}}
, the quantum memory effect starts dominating. From this stage onward the BH mass evolution gets modified as

\displaystyle\frac{dM_{\text{BH}}}{dt}=-\frac{\epsilon}{\left[\mathcal{S}(M_{\text{BH}})\right]^{k}}\frac{M_{P}^{4}}{M_{\text{BH}}^{2}}\,,

(50)

where
S(M_{\text{BH}})
is given by Eq. (
7
). Once again, for
M_{\text{BH}}\gg Q/G
, we obtain

\displaystyle M_{\text{BH}}=q\,M_{\text{in}}\left[1-\Gamma_{\rm BH}^{q}\,(t-t_{q})\right]^{\frac{1}{3+2k}}\,,

(51)

with

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm BH}^{q}={2^{k}\,(3+2k)\,\epsilon}\,M_{P}\left(\frac{M_{P}}{qM_{\text{in}}}\right)^{3+2k}\,,

(52)

as the modified decay rate and
t_{\rm ev}^{q}=1/\Gamma^{q}_{\rm BH}
as the modified BH lifetime. Clearly, for a given
q
and
M_{\text{in}}
, a larger
k
results in a longer BH lifetime. We can now find the modified evolution of BH electromagnetic charge due to the onset of memory burden effect from Eq. (
15
) as,

\displaystyle\frac{dQ}{dt}\simeq-Q\,\sum\frac{g_{i}\,q_{i}^{2}}{24\pi}\,\frac{M_{P}^{2}}{q\,M_{\text{in}}}\implies Q(t)\simeq Q_{\rm in}\exp\left[-\sum\frac{g_{i}\,q_{i}^{2}}{24\pi}\,\frac{M_{P}^{2}\,t}{q\,M_{\text{in}}}\right]\,,

(53)

assuming
M_{\text{BH}}\gg Q/\sqrt{G}
and
t\gg t_{\rm in}
. Clearly, decay of the BH charge shall be delayed for
q>0
, however the charge still decays away exponentially as we have seen before [cf. Eq. (
16
)]. Once again, same as Eq. (
26
), during the memory-burden epoch there exist a critical
\beta
-value
[
25
,
72
]

\displaystyle\beta_{c}^{q}=\frac{1}{q}\,\sqrt{\frac{t_{\rm in}}{t_{\rm ev}^{q}}}\,,

(54)

above which the PBH energy density dominates over the radiation energy density, and a PBH dominated epoch starts until the evaporation completes. Because of the memory burden effect, the BH yield is modified for
\beta>\beta_{c}^{q}
, since now the BH evaporation time follows from Eq. (
52
), with corresponding evaporation temperature

\displaystyle T_{\text{ev}}^{q}\Big|_{\beta>\beta_{c}^{q}}=M_{P}\,\left(\frac{40}{\pi^{2}\,g_{\star}(T_{\text{ev}}^{q})}\right)^{1/4}\,\left[2^{k}\,(3+2k)\,\epsilon\left(\frac{M_{P}}{q\,M_{\text{in}}}\right)^{3+2k}\right]^{1/2}\,,

(55)

that results in a BH yield

\displaystyle Y_{\rm BH}(\overline{T}_{\rm ev}^{q})=\left(\frac{405}{32}\right)^{1/4}\,\sqrt{\frac{3\epsilon}{\pi}}\,q\,\left(\frac{g_{\star}(\overline{T}_{\rm ev}^{q})^{3}}{g_{\star s}(\overline{T}_{\rm ev}^{q})^{4}}\right)^{1/4}\,\left(\frac{M_{P}}{q\,M_{\text{in}}}\right)^{\frac{2k+5}{2}}\,\sqrt{2^{k}\,\left(1+\frac{2k}{3}\right)}\,.

(56)

In deriving Eq. (
55
) we have used the fact that in a PBH-dominated universe,
H(t_{\rm ev}^{q})=2/\left(3\,t_{\rm ev}^{q}\right)
. In the opposite limit, i.e., for
\beta<\beta_{c}^{q}
, the evaporation temperature is given by

\displaystyle T_{\text{ev}}^{q}\Big|_{\beta<\beta_{c}^{q}}=M_{P}\,\left(\frac{45}{\pi^{2}\,g_{\star}(T_{\text{ev}}^{q})}\right)^{1/4}\,\left[2^{k}\,(3+2k)\,\epsilon\left(\frac{M_{P}}{q\,M_{\text{in}}}\right)^{3+2k}\right]^{1/2}\,,

(57)

while the yield simply follows Eq. (
24
). For Eq. (
57
), we have utilized
H(t_{\rm ev}^{q})=1/(2\,t_{\rm ev}^{q})
, as the PBH evaporates during radiation domination. Note that, for
k=0,\,q=1
, as expected, Eq. (
56
) reproduces Eq. (
32
).

Now, any modification to Hawking evaporation would also significantly modify the GW associated with its density fluctuation. Following Refs.
[
71
,
73
]
, we find an upper bound on initial PBH mass fraction as,

\displaystyle\beta<1.2\times 10^{-4}\,q^{-3/4}\,\mathcal{K}(k)\,\exp\left[\frac{3}{8}\,\left(8-28k-\frac{7}{3+2k}\right)\right]\,\left(q\,\frac{M_{\text{in}}}{1\,\text{g}}\right)^{-\frac{(k+1)\,(14k+17)}{16k+24}}\,,

(58)

where
\mathcal{K}(k)=\left(1+\frac{2k}{3}\right)^{\frac{1}{4k+6}+\frac{7}{16}}(2k+2)^{-\frac{3}{16k+24}}
. For
k=0,\,q=1

37
) from Eq. (
58

The WDM constraint also gets modified. Following Eq. (
45
) and using
T_{\text{BH}}^{q}=M_{P}^{2}/(q\,M_{\text{in}})
, we obtain,

\displaystyle m_{\text{DM}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim 6.8\times 10^{-6}\,\text{GeV}\left(\frac{q\,M_{\text{in}}}{M_{P}}\right)^{\frac{2k+1}{2}}\,\frac{\delta}{1.3}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{k}\,\epsilon\,(2\,k+3)}}\,\left[g_{\star}(T_{\text{ev}}^{q})^{\frac{3}{4}}\,\frac{g_{\star s}(T_{\rm eq})}{g_{\star s}(T_{\rm ev}^{q})}\right]^{\frac{1}{3}}\,,

(59)

for
\beta>\beta_{c}^{q}
. For a conservative estimation, we chose
\delta\simeq 1.3
following Refs.
[
22

25

FRISBHEE

6
Conclusions

The dynamical generation of the matter-antimatter asymmetry and the identification of a viable particle candidate for dark matter (DM) remain enduring challenges in modern physics. In this work, we explore a purely gravitational origin for both phenomena. Given that gravity is an inescapable interaction, this approach provides a fundamental and compelling pathway. Our baryogenesis mechanism hinges on a quantum-gravity-inspired coupling between spacetime and the
B-L
current. This coupling introduces a chemical potential that discriminates between baryons and antibaryons, thereby driving the observed baryon asymmetry. In presence of such a chemical potential, the Hawking evaporation from a primordial black hole (PBH) becomes asymmetric.

We consider PBHs formed in a radiation-dominated epoch that evaporate before the onset of Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN), implying they are relatively light. Our findings indicate that the observed baryon asymmetry can be generated for such light PBHs with a coupling strength of
\gtrsim\mathcal{O}(10^{20})
[cf. Fig.
3
upper panel]. Similarly, we propose that an analogous coupling between spacetime and a current in the dark sector could account for the observed DM abundance via asymmetric DM generation [cf. Fig.
3
lower panel]. Beyond the semiclassical Hawking radiation framework, we incorporate the memory-burden effect to explore the parameter space that simultaneously satisfies the observed baryon asymmetry and asymmetric DM abundance [cf. Fig.
4
]. Our analysis outlines the viable parameter space consistent with constraints from CMB on the inflationary scale, BBN limits on PBH lifetimes, and the production of primordial gravitational waves arising from PBH density fluctuations [cf. Fig.
5
]. In conclusion, this study presents a purely gravitational framework for the generation of asymmetries in both the visible and dark sectors, offering a unified perspective.
