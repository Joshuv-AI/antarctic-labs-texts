Bedside three-dimensional acoustic angiography and perfusion monitoring in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage
Authors: Clément Gakuba, Louise Denis, Georges Chabouh, Maxime Gauberti, Jean-Denis Moyer, Tom Gavet, Jennifer Bourgès, Joséphine Malczuk, Anais Briant, Remy Morello, Nathalie Laquay, Sylvain Bodard, Arthur Chavignon, Vincent Hingot, Denis Vivien, Olivier Couture
Published: 2026

Delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) remains a leading cause of secondary brain injury after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH), typically occurring between days 4 and 14 post intensive care unit (ICU) admission [1, 2]. While transcranial Doppler (TCD) is widely used at the bedside, it provides only an indirect surrogate of tissue perfusion [3], and advanced imaging computed tomography angiography and perfusion (CTA/CTP) is limited by transport of critically ill patients, radiation/contrast exposure, and low repeatability in neurocritical care [4]. Moreover, DCI reflects a multifactorial process that extends beyond proximal vasospasm to include distal hypoperfusion and microcirculatory dysfunction, features that may not be captured by large vessel velocity-based monitoring alone. A bedside method capable of repeatedly assessing tissue-level cerebral perfusion could therefore improve DCI detection and longitudinal monitoring, particularly in patients with limited clinical examination.

We conducted a prospective single-center interventional feasibility study enrolling 11 adults (54% men, median age 42 [28–72] years) with aSAH. All patients received standard-of-care management, including daily TCD and CTA/CTP when clinically indicated. Patients underwent bedside bilateral three-dimensional contrast-enhanced ultrasound (3D CEUS) examinations at three predefined timepoints: early (≤ 72 h), vasospasm/DCI-risk window (days 5–10), and late (days 11–15). Each acquisition consisted of continuous high–volume-rate volumetric imaging during an intravenous bolus of 2.4 mL SonoVue® followed by saline flush. From each 3D CEUS acquisition, two reconstructions were derived: (1) 3D microvascular imaging (MVI) to visualize major arteries and distal territories (Fig. 1A favorable & Fig. 1C unfavorable case), and (2) 3D ultrasound perfusion imaging (UPI) using time–intensity evolution (TIE) curves and time-to-peak (TTP) maps to characterize parenchymal perfusion dynamics (Fig. 1B-D i, ii, iii). To support clinical interpretability, TIE curves were time-aligned to contrast arrival and assessed for symmetry, wash-in delay, and atypical morphologies (e.g., blunted rise or double-peak patterns). Phase-2 (days 5–10) perfusion profiles were independently rated (favorable/unfavorable/inconclusive) by three board-certified neuro-intensivists and compared with 6-month Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended (GOSE, favorable outcome defined as GOSE > 4).

Overall, 58/64 (91%) bedside acquisitions demonstrated adequate contrast enhancement. Among the six acquisitions without observable enhancement, three came from the same patient, all on the left side, where a consistently poor acoustic window was present. One occurred in a patient with postoperative bleeding on the side of interest. Two were from a patient with elevated intracranial pressure. In enhanced acquisitions, the 3D MVI reconstruction consistently visualized major intracranial arteries, supporting the feasibility of volumetric transcranial acoustic angiography at the bedside and offering anatomical context for perfusion interpretation. Importantly, examinations were performed without patient transport, enabling repeat assessments during the period of highest DCI risk. No adverse events related to CEUS were observed.

Perfusion dynamics differed according to clinical course. Patients with favorable neurological evolution exhibited stable, symmetric perfusion patterns across hemispheres and timepoints (Fig. 1A-Bi-iii). In contrast, unfavorable trajectories showed heterogeneous, delayed, or absent enhancement, sometimes progressing toward global loss of enhancement (Fig. 1C-Di-iii). The relationship between phase-2 (days 5–10) bedside ultrasound perfusion dynamics and 6-month neurological outcomes is presented in the supplementary eFigure 1. Phase-2 TIE curves illustrate inter-patient variability in contrast wash-in timing, peak amplitude, and wash-out morphology, consistent with distinct perfusion trajectories during the vasospasm/DCI-risk window. We compared how 3D UPI, TCD, and CT perfusion findings obtained during the same time window each correlated with 6-month outcome (GOSE > 4). Of 11 enrolled patients, 10 had evaluable phase-2 3D UPI profiles (one was inconclusive due to probe motion during acquisition). 3D UPI was concordant with outcome in 8/10 (80%) cases: all patients with a favorable perfusion profile had a favorable outcome (3/3), while 5/7 (71%) with an unfavorable profile had an unfavorable outcome, the two exceptions included one patient with vasospasm who ultimately recovered. In comparison, outcome agreement was 7/11 (63%) for TCD-based vasospasm suspicion and 4/7 (57%) for CT perfusion, acknowledging that CT perfusion was not systematically obtained but performed selectively based on clinical evolution.

In an exploratory subset of four patients who underwent both 3D UPI and CT perfusion within 24 h of each other, all four had abnormal 3D UPI profiles, subsequently developed DCI-related cerebral infarction, and had unfavorable 6-month outcome. In contrast, automated CT perfusion hypoperfusion thresholds were positive in only two of the four cases.

Although limited by sample size, this observation supports the hypothesis that volumetric UPI may identify clinically relevant hypoperfusion that is not consistently captured by threshold-based CT perfusion algorithms, which are largely optimized for other ischemic syndromes.

Limitations include small sample size, follow-up variability and multimodal imaging correlation (CTA/DSA) across patients. Nonetheless, bedside 3D CEUS appears feasible, safe, and capable of providing repeatable volumetric, tissue-level perfusion assessment during the critical DCI-risk period after aSAH. Larger multicenter studies with standardized imaging comparators are needed to validate diagnostic and prognostic performance and to determine whether 3D UPI–guided management improves outcomes. These biomarkers could provide quantitative insights comparable to CT while retaining the advantages of ultrasound.
