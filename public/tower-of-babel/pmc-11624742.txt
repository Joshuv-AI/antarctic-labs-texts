Is flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption associated with problematic alcohol consumption patterns? A cross-sectional study with the Japan COVID-19 and Society Internet Survey
Authors: Mami Wakabayashi, Aya Kinjo, Yoshifumi Sugiyama, Midori Takada, Hiroyasu Iso, Takahiro Tabuchi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study investigates the relationship between flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption in restaurants and bar and problematic alcohol consumption patterns identified by the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) during the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted using data from the Japan Society and New Tobacco Internet Survey in February 2022. A total of 19 585 current drinkers (55% of drinkers were men, and the mean age was 48.3 years) were categorised based on AUDIT scores; non-problem drinking (an AUDIT Score of 0–7), problem drinking (an AUDIT Score of 8 or over), hazardous alcohol use (an AUDIT Score of 8–14) and probable alcohol use disorders (an AUDIT Score of 15 or over). A score of 2 or higher in the third question of the AUDIT is identified as binge drinking. The explanatory variable was the presence of using flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption in the previous 12 months during the COVID-19 pandemic (February 2021 to February 2022). The associations of using the flat rate pricing with the problem or binge drinking, and with hazardous alcohol use or probable alcohol use disorders were analysed.ResultsPeople who used flat rate pricing during the COVID-19 pandemic were likely to be problem drinking and binge drinking compared with non-users of flat rate pricing; the respective adjusted ORs were 4.64 (95% CI: 4.24 to 5.07) and 3.65 (95% CI: 3.33 to 4.00) through multivariable binary logistic regression. The users of the flat rate pricing were associated with hazardous alcohol use and probable alcohol use disorder; the adjusted relative risk ratios were 3.40 (95% CI: 3.06 to 3.77) and 8.58 (95% CI: 7.51 to 9.80) through multinomial logistic regression.ConclusionsOverall, using flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption was associated with both binge drinking and problem drinking, including hazardous alcohol use and probable alcohol use disorders.Keywords: COVID-19, Health policy, Behavior, MENTAL HEALTH, Substance misuse

Introduction

Alcohol use is a major global concern associated with disease and often leading to death.1,3 The quantity of alcohol consumption and drinking patterns are associated with alcohol-related harm.4 To reduce the frequency and quantity of alcohol use, the WHO adopted a global strategy in the 63rd World Health Assembly in 2010 by banning or restricting flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption as a pricing policy.5 Flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption, also known as ‘all you can drink’, is common for restaurants and bars in many prefectures of Japan and is usually applied to group dinners or parties, not to individual person.6 This system was widely used for its convenience in allowing fixed budgets for customers to drink any kind of beverage, especially unlimited alcoholic beverages, usually within 2 hours. However, the risk of flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption was not recognised in Japan.

All-you-can-drink pricing system is a known risk factor for heavy episodic drinking.7 Heavy episodic drinking, also known as binge drinking, is described as a pattern of alcohol misuse that involves at least 60 g or more of pure alcohol consumption (approximately six standard alcoholic drinks) on at least one occasion within 30 days.8 Binge drinking is a public health issue because it is associated with increased susceptibility to disease and injury across the world.9

According to a systematic review of promotional price practices,10 including flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption and alcohol-related outcomes, 11 of 12 studies in the seven countries conducted from 1978 to 2018 reported a consistent positive correlation between promotional practices and increased alcohol consumption at the situation.7 However, the relationship between flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption and problematic alcohol consumption patterns identified by the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) has not been examined. AUDIT is the screening for excessive drinking conditions and alcohol use disorders based on past drinking behaviours, not identifying the one-time increases/decrease amount of drinking such as in previous studies.

This study was conducted under COVID-19 pandemic (February 2021 to February 2022). During each COVID-19 outbreak, the Japanese government implemented a restriction of group dining of more than four people and requested restaurants to shorten their business hours until 20:00 and refrain from offering alcoholic beverages after 19:00.11 12 This is because COVID-19 clusters often occurred in situations involving dining out and drinking alcohol.13 Under the COVID-19 regulations, there are fewer opportunities to enforce the flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption, especially seasonal group dining with colleagues such as a year-end party, although many non-drinkers used the flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption for seasonal group dining before the pandemic.

This study examines the relationship between the flat rate pricing and the risk for problem drinking identified by the AUDIT to investigate the risk of using flat rate pricing, not only for binge drinking but also problematic alcohol drinking patterns.

Material and methods

Data source and study population

Internet survey

A cross-sectional study was conducted by using data from the Japan Society and New Tobacco Internet Survey (JASTIS)—a large internet-based cohort study that focuses on tobacco issue since 2015 and has expanded to various health behaviours since 2021. Details of the JASTIS have been previously published.14 The information of JASTIS survey is also available on website.15 The survey for this study was conducted from 1 to 28 February 2022, examining how the COVID-19 pandemic affected individuals’ health behaviours in Japan. This web-based, self-reported questionnaire survey was administrated by a large internet research agency, Rakuten Insight, in Japan, which pooled approximately 2.2 million panellists.16 The same person is prohibited from registering as the panellist with multiple Rakuten IDs on the system. The survey requests were sent by the research agency to the panellists, who were each selected by sex, age and prefecture. The panellists who consented to participate accessed the designated website and responded to the survey. The participants were given the option to not respond to any part of the survey or discontinue it altogether at any point. The survey was closed when the target number of respondents for each sex, age and prefecture was met. The survey included 28 776 of 39 998 participants, who could be considered our cohort study participants (participation rate was 71.9%). Last, 4224 participants were included as new cohort members, resulting in a total of 33 000 participants in JASTIS 2022. All questionnaires were designed such that the respondents had to answer each question before they could proceed to the next question, thereby ensuring that the issue of missing data was not generated.

Managing data quality and generating the study population

To ensure data quality, respondents with discrepancies or artificial/unnatural responses were excluded from the study.14 In this regard, three items were used to detect discrepancies: (1) ‘please choose the second from the bottom’; (2) choosing ‘yes’ in all the questions for using substances, including illegal drugs; and (3) choosing ‘yes’ in all the questions for having eleven chronic diseases. Moreover, 147 respondents who had inconsistent answers to alcohol-related questions were excluded. This study focused only on current drinkers, excluding life abstainers or past drinkers who abstained from drinking. Therefore, 9700 respondents identifying as non-drinkers (life abstainers and past drinkers) were excluded. Non-drinkers were identified in the first question of the AUDIT; respondents answered ‘never’ for the question ‘how often do you have a drink containing alcohol?’. In addition, participation in this study was limited only to individuals who were 20 years of age and over, which is the legal drinking age in Japan and restaurants, or bars are prohibited from offering alcoholic beverages to people under 20 years. Then, the total number of participants who identified as current drinkers was 19 585. (The selection procedure is shown in figure 1. Measures

Explanatory variables

The participants were asked, ‘how often did you drink with flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption in the past 12 months (March 2021–February 2022)?’ For the main analysis, the frequency of the behaviours was categorised as either ‘never’ or ‘once or more’. In the sensitivity analysis, the frequency of participating in the flat rate pricing was categorised as ‘never’, ‘less than once per month’ and ‘once per month or more’.

Outcome variables

Current drinkers were identified by the AUDIT, which is developed by WHO and is a most effective screening tool in identifying subjects with alcohol use disorders and those involved in hazardous alcohol use.17 The AUDIT is a 10-item screening measure that assesses alcohol use during the previous 12 months.18 19 Although the AUDIT does not intend to offer a clinical diagnosis, it can indicate the presence and severity of alcohol problems or alcohol use disorders.20 Additionally, the AUDIT translated into Japanese was validated to identify hazardous alcohol use and alcohol use disorders as a screening tool.21 Each item in the AUDIT has a score of 0–4 points, except items 9 and 10, which inquire about alcohol-related injury or violence and has scores of 0, 2 or 4 points. The total scores range from 0 to 40 points. We excluded score of 0 as non-drinkers. The participants were categorised into three groups based on the total score: non-problem drinking (score: 1–7), hazardous alcohol use (8–14) and probable alcohol use disorders (scores ≥15).21 Participants who were categorised as the hazardous alcohol use group and probable alcohol use disorders group were regarded as problem drinking. Moreover, binge drinking was detected through the third question of the AUDIT (AUDIT-3), which asked, “how often do you have six or more drinks on one occasion?’ People who had six or more drinks on one occasion once a month or more were considered binge drinking (score of 2 or more in the AUDIT-3).22 Detailed information on each item in AUDIT is shown in online supplemental table 1.

Demographics and potential health factors related to alcohol use

The demographic questions inquired about the following: age, sex, educational level, marital status, equivalent annual household income, current living arrangements and job. Educational level was categorised as low (graduated from high school or lower), middle (graduated from vocational or junior college) and high (graduated from university or higher). Marital status was categorised as married, single and divorced/widowed. Equivalent annual household income is the household income divided by the square root of the number of household members. This factor is categorised as under 2 million yen, 2–4 million yen, 4–6 million yen, 6–10 million yen, 10 million yen or more and do not know/do not want to answer. The current living arrangements reported about whether the participant lived with someone or alone. The job was categorised as executive, manager, regular employee, self-employed, non-regular employee, no main job (a student or retiree), only housework and unemployed. Finally, potential factors that are related to alcohol use included a current smoker.

Potential factors related to the COVID-19 policy

To identify the participants who dined out in the previous month, the following question was asked: ‘how often did you refrain from dining out in the previous month (January–February 2022)?’ The frequency of the behaviours was categorised into two aspects: no dining out and dining out once or more. The survey was conducted in February 2022 (the sixth COVID-19 pandemic), when Japan had a significant number of people infected by COVID-19. As a countermeasure for COVID-19 pandemic, the government of Japan requested restaurants to shorten their business hours and refrain from offering alcoholic beverages from 9 January to 21 March 2022, at all prefectures in Japan regulated by the COVID-19 policy.23 The regulations for the prevention of COVID-19 pandemic were enforced for almost 7 months in 2021 and approximately two and a half months during the beginning of 2022 (as of July 2022) in Japan.23 (The summary of timeline of the regulation and the internet survey is shown in online supplemental figure 1.)

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata MP, V.15 (Stata Corp). The means and SD were presented for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables (table 1). First, we determined variations in the means and proportions of demographics and potential health factors according to the AUDIT scores for each category (table 1). We examined the frequency of using flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption by drinking categories (table 2). Second, multivariable binary logistic regression was performed to examine the relationship between using (or absence) flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption within the past 12 months and problem or binge drinking (table 3). Additionally, multinomial logistic regression was performed to analyse the association between using (or absence) the flat rate pricing and hazardous drinkers or probable alcohol use disorders (table 4). All statistical tests conducted were two sided, and a p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results

Participants’ characteristics

Of 19 585 drinkers, 55% (n=10 851) were men, the mean age was 48.3 (±17.4) years old and the age range was 20–79 years old. Based on participants’ characteristics and AUDIT scores, the demographic categories with the highest proportion of responses were higher education (54%), married (60%), living with someone (77%), regular employee (33%) and income level of 2–4 million (36%) (table 1). This study also included current smokers (20%) and those who dined out from January to February (under COVID-19 regulations during the six waves) (60%).

Participants’ characteristics according to the AUDIT scores

The number of people with problem drinking (AUDIT Score of 8 or over) was 4327 (22% of the total drinkers). Additionally, their mean age was 44.3 years (±16.3). The higher proportion of problem drinking compared with non-problem drinking in demographic categories included the following: age level of 40–59 years (39%), men (74%), a low/high education level (29%), marriage (62%), living alone (25%), executive/manager (20%), current smokers (34%) and people who dined out in January–February (66%).

The association between using flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption behaviours during the COVID-19 and AUDIT scores

Table 2 showed the frequency of using the flat rate pricing according to the AUDIT Score. There was a greater proportion of system users in problem drinking compared with system users in non-problem drinking (47% and 16%, respectively). Moreover, the proportion of individuals who used the system in the probable alcohol use disorders group was four times higher than that in the non-problem drinker group (64% and 16%, respectively).

Table 3 presents the aOR for each of the behaviours related to flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption according to the AUDIT Score. Using the flat rate pricing is strongly associated with problem or binge drinking compared with not using such systems (problem drinking (aOR: 4.64, 95% CI: 4.24 to 5.07) and binge drinking (aOR: 3.65, 95% CI: 3.33 to 4.00)). As shown in table 4, participants who used the flat rate pricing had a strong association with hazardous alcohol use and probable alcohol use disorders—aRRR: 3.40, 95% CI: 3.06 to 3.77 and aRRR: 8.58, 95% CI: 7.51 to 9.80, respectively. Furthermore, frequent use of the flat rate pricing (one per month or more) was strongly associated with probable alcohol use disorders compared with no use (aRRR: 40.7, 95% CI: 33.7 to 49.2).

Figure 2 presents the aOR for users of the flat rate pricing, stratified by related factors shown in table 1. Of all subgroups, a significant impact on using the system was observed. The association between using the flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption and problem drinking was similar across different demographics, except for the groups of younger age group (20–39 years) and middle education status. That group had higher aOR for users of the flat rate pricing and problem drinking comparing overall aOR in model 2.

Discussion

In this study, the relationship between flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption and current drinkers categorised by the AUDIT during the COVID-19 pandemic was examined. The results suggest that using flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption associated with the risk of individuals being problem drinkers by four times or more and that of being binge drinkers by three times or more, despite shortening the length of business hours or regulating not to offering alcoholic beverage after 19:00 in restaurants in Japan during the COVID-19 pandemic. The group that used the flat rate pricing had eight times or more probability of alcohol use disorders in people; similarly, those who used the system once per month or more were more likely to have probable alcohol use disorders. Thus, the flat rate pricing was associated with a great risk for problem drinking, especially in the younger age group.

As mentioned previously, WHO advised the government to ban or restrict flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption to prevent alcohol abuse.5 Japan widely accepted permissible drinking culture,24 and few measures against alcohol production or use were implemented.25 A study in Japan showed that college students who used flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption had a higher volume of alcohol intake compared with non-use status of the same person.26 The culture and social environment could reflect that flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption are widely accepted by groups dining out in Japan, even though non-drinkers have to pay the fee of the system because the system is applied to their group. The implementation of regulation for flat rate pricing as a national pricing policy for alcohol would be beneficial for both drinkers and non-drinkers to change the social norm and reduce alcohol-related harm in Japan.

Pricing policy is a most effective factor for controlling alcohol consumption and reducing alcohol harm.27 According to a meta-analysis of 112 studies, alcohol prices and taxes were inversely associated with drinking and their impact was more significant compared with other prevention policies.28 Although the pricing policies mentioned a unit price of an alcoholic beverage, flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption in restaurants make alcoholic beverages cheaper for consumers and facilitate drinking. In contrast, regulating the system may help reduce problem drinking as long-time effects. The previous study showed the correlation between promotional practice for drinking and increased alcohol consumption at the situation.7 26 The regulation of a flat rate pricing would contribute to reducing binge drinking in drinking parties as immediate results. Our result showed the association between the flat rate pricing and problematic alcohol consumption patterns. The regulating of flat rate pricing would prevent problematic alcohol consumption patterns including binge drinking and reduce the alcohol-related harm, especially when problematic alcohol drinkers used the flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption.

Alcohol consumption declined during the first few months of the COVID-19 pandemic, owing to the decreased availability of alcohol in Europe.29 30 However, the general drinking patterns during the COVID-19 pandemic reinforced the already existing drinking patterns in the UK.31 Moreover, panel data in the USA showed that AUDIT from June to September 2020 significantly increased compared with those in previous months.32 The Japanese government requested restaurants to shorten their business hours as a countermeasure to prevent COVID-19 transmission. These policies and countermeasures may result in a short-term reduction in the availability of alcohol in restaurants under these regulations. An internet survey in Japan showed that approximately 79% of people claimed that the policies reduced the opportunities for drinking alcohol outside during the COVID-19 pandemic and that 48% of people believed that they should not invite others to go drinking during the pandemic.33 In addition, the early closure of restaurants and bars as COVID-19 preventive measurements decreased the utilisation rate of restaurants and bars among young persons, although no discernible impacts were observed in the utilisation of restaurants and bars inactive subpopulations such as older adults in Japan.11 Overall, the general population was affected with reduced opportunities to go out to drink during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, whichever individuals dined out during the sixth COVID-19 pandemic, using the flat rate pricing at any point in time was associated with a higher risk of problem drinking. The restrictions under the sixth COVID-19 pandemic were weakened compared with the first emergency declaration, which made people hesitant in dining out from April to May 2020 (the first wave of COVID-19). The proportion of current drinkers who dined out was 60% in February 2022, compared with 25% who dined out from April to May 2020.34 There may be a change in an individual’s attitude towards the regulations, which may increase their potential risk of hazardous alcohol use and alcohol use disorder.

Strengths and limitations

The main strengths of this study are that it involves a large sample size and has a nationwide sampling design. Another unique point of this study is the surveys were conducted during the larger national outbreak of COVID-19 in Japan, when conducted the regulation for sales promotion. However, this study also has several limitations. First, this study attempted to investigate the relationship between flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption and drinkers categorised by the AUDIT. Because the frequency of using flat rate pricing, which was measured in the past 12 months, and problem drinking were identified at the survey, the occurrence of reverse causality between them remains: users of flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption may not likely be problem drinking; however, problem drinkers are likely to use the system. A cohort study is required to clarify the timing and frequency of exposure and the change of drinking behaviours.

Second, the participants’ demographic and drinking information was self-reported. The current study’s online survey design restricted the results from being generalised to Japanese. However, participant in our study had higher educational level than those in census 2020 among 20–79 years old; the proportion of university or higher education were 54% vs 37%.35

Third, our data showed higher proportion alcohol use disorder based on the AUDIT comparing national survey in 2018. Our data including non-drinkers show that 5% of people (8% for men, 3% for women) had AUDIT Score of 15 or over, which is greater than the findings from the national survey (2.9% of those, 5.2% for men and 2.9% for women, with AUDIT scores of 15 or over) in 2018.36 Alcohol use disorder may have increased during the COVID-19 pandemic,34 and our result may reflect the impact of the pandemic, although we cannot compare the AUDIT Score between our cohort members before the pandemic because our cohort study did not measure AUDIT before the pandemic.

Conclusion

This study examined the association between flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption under the imposed regulations during the COVID-19 pandemic and problematic alcohol consumption patterns (problem drinking and binge drinking). Despite the COVID-19 regulations on dining out, people who dined out at restaurants/bars using flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption under COVID-19 pandemic were more susceptible to potential alcohol use disorders or hazardous alcohol use. The further study is needed to identify whether this regulation for the sales promotion during the COVID-19 pandemic influenced accessibility and adaptability of flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption in Japan. Yet, the results also indicate that regulated flat rate pricing for unlimited alcohol consumption may have a positive impact on reducing harmful alcohol use.
