Community-based sentinel surveillance for Mpox-like disease among men who have sex with men and transgender individuals in India: a cross-sectional study

Introduction

Mpox is a zoonotic viral infection caused by the Mpox virus (Mpoxv), historically endemic to Central and West Africa [1]. However, during 2022-2023, it emerged as a global public health concern, with sustained human-to-human transmission in non-endemic regions, predominantly among men who have sex with men (MSM) [[2], [3], [4]].

The Mpoxv, belonging to the Orthopoxvirus genus and closely related to the smallpox virus, is mainly spread through close physical contact, respiratory droplets, and contaminated surfaces [5,6]. Although transmission from animals can occur, the dominant route in recent outbreaks has been human-to-human spread [6]. The infection typically causes a short-lived but noticeable illness, vesiculopustular rash, fever, painful lymph nodes, headaches, muscle aches, and fatigue that resolves in 2-4 weeks [7]. Notably, most cases in the 2022-2023 outbreak featured genital and perianal lesions [8], often associated with sexual contact, a pattern that differs from Mpox’s more generalized presentations seen in earlier African outbreaks [9].

As of March 2024, over 95,000 cases and 185 deaths have been reported worldwide. Although case counts have declined since the 2022 peak, continued transmission and periodic resurgences have been documented. Another upsurge in the Democratic Republic of Congo and neighboring countries led the WHO to reclassify Mpox as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) in August 2024 [10].

India reported its first Mpox case on July 14, 2022 in a man returning from the Middle East [11]. Since then, 66 confirmed cases have been documented, most with an international travel link [12]. Genomic studies have identified multiple subclusters of Clade IIb-A.2 in the country, typically discovered through entry screening and hospital surveillance [13], raising suspicion of ongoing, undetected community transmission, especially in high-contact groups.

With routine smallpox vaccination halted in the 1980s after global eradication, most Indians aged under 45 years lack cross-protective immunity, which raises population vulnerability, especially in settings where close contact and sexual network connectivity are common [[14], [15], [16]]. MSM and transgender (TG) populations have been disproportionately affected in recent outbreaks and often face barriers to health care access, leading to underrepresentation in conventional surveillance systems [[17], [18], [19]]. Traditional health monitoring easily overlooks early signals of Mpox in these groups, leading to delays in outbreak detection and intervention [20].

Sentinel surveillance in high-risk settings, particularly, through community-based organizations (CBOs), offers a pragmatic approach for early detection of emerging infections by enabling syndromic screening and targeted testing [21]. By embedding these systems within CBOs that already serve MSM and TG populations, Mpox case detection can become more sensitive and timelier [22]. In these settings, syndromic screening for Mpox-like dermatological lesions (MPLDs), supported by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) confirmation, creates early opportunities for isolation, contact tracing, and public health action [23]. Adding Mpox checks to existing sexual health services also supports sustainability and cost-effectiveness while breaking down access barriers.

Given the lack of community sentinel data for Mpox in India, this study was initiated collaboratively with CBOs operating clinics in a range of urban and semi-urban contexts. This study aimed to assess MPLD through community-based sentinel surveillance and describe the demographic and behavioral characteristics of affected individuals. Further, the study demonstrates how community-embedded surveillance not only improves case finding in otherwise underserved groups but also provides the actionable data needed to inform India’s ongoing Mpox preparedness and response.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional sentinel surveillance study was implemented from October 2023 to February 2024 across seven CBOs and their affiliated health clinics or routine outreach camps operating in six geographic zones of India. The geographic zones were defined to ensure representation across North, South, East, West, Central, and Northeast regions of India, capturing diverse demographic and epidemiologic contexts. Study locations were purposively selected to capture diverse demographic and geographic contexts, encompassing metropolitan cities, tier-2 urban centers, and semi-urban settings with established networks of MSM and TG populations. Participating sites included Amritsar and Ludhiana in Punjab, Delhi National Capital Region, Pune in Maharashtra, Hyderabad in Telangana, Thiruvananthapuram in Kerala, Kolkata in West Bengal, and CBO-affiliated clinics in Mizoram and Nagaland representing the eastern and northeastern regions.

Each participating CBO facilitated service delivery through a fixed community clinic or through periodic health camps providing comprehensive sexual health care, including HIV counseling and testing, diagnosis and management of sexually transmitted infections, and general health consultations. All individuals attending the participating clinics during the study period were consecutively screened for MPLD. On average, each clinic served approximately 300 outpatients per month, predominantly MSM and TG clients who routinely accessed services through established peer-led outreach and community engagement mechanisms.

Participant eligibility and case definition

All individuals attending the seven participating community clinics during the study period were eligible for initial screening. Each attendee was systematically evaluated for Mpox-like dermatological lesions (MPLDs) using a standardized case definition adapted from the WHO and US CDC guidelines [24]. Individuals meeting the MPLD criteria were subsequently invited to undergo detailed clinical evaluation and biological sample collection after the provision of written informed consent.

Individuals were screened for MPLD lesions based on the following criteria:

•Primary criterion: Presence of acute papulo-vesiculo-pustular or crusted skin lesions or lesions suggestive of a sexually transmitted infection.

Primary criterion: Presence of acute papulo-vesiculo-pustular or crusted skin lesions or lesions suggestive of a sexually transmitted infection.

•Secondary features: One or more of the following: swollen lymph nodes (lymphadenopathy); current or recently resolved fever preceding or accompanying the rash; headache, generalized body ache, or marked fatigue; history of close physical or sexual contact with a confirmed, probable, or suspected Mpox case within the preceding 21 days; or history of travel to a country with active Mpox transmission within the past 21 days.

Secondary features: One or more of the following: swollen lymph nodes (lymphadenopathy); current or recently resolved fever preceding or accompanying the rash; headache, generalized body ache, or marked fatigue; history of close physical or sexual contact with a confirmed, probable, or suspected Mpox case within the preceding 21 days; or history of travel to a country with active Mpox transmission within the past 21 days.

Individuals meeting the primary criterion along with at least one secondary feature were classified as suspected MPLD cases. As part of the preparatory phase, community awareness and sensitization activities were undertaken in collaboration with the participating CBOs to encourage early health-seeking behavior among at-risk populations and promote participation in clinic-based screening during the study period.

Data collection procedures

A structured, paper-based questionnaire was developed based on the WHO global clinical data platform for Mpox case report forms [25]. The questionnaire was translated into local languages (Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, Punjabi, and Marathi) to ensure comprehensibility across different study sites. All translations were back-translated and validated by bilingual research staff.

The questionnaire was pilot-tested in community settings to ensure clarity, cultural appropriateness, and comprehension of behavioral variables among MSM and TG participants. The structured questionnaire captured comprehensive information across multiple domains, including sociodemographic characteristics, clinical presentation, behavioral and exposure factors, travel and contact history, animal exposure, and medical history, as detailed in Table 1.

Biological sample collection

Participants meeting the MPLD case definition provided multiple biological specimens, such as lesion samples (lesion base scrapings, lesion roof, lesion fluid, lesion crust), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) blood (5 ml), serum (3 ml), oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal swabs, and urine for laboratory confirmation; the samples were stored at −80°C after collection (Table 2).

All samples were labeled with unique identifiers, logged in a sample tracking database, and transported under cold chain conditions to the nearest designated Virus Research and Diagnostic Laboratory (VRDL) within 6 hours of collection. Samples were subsequently transferred to the ICMR-National Institute of Virology, Pune for confirmatory testing [26]. Standard operating procedures for sample collection, packing, and transport were developed and followed at each step.

Laboratory methods

Screening of Mpox virus DNA by real-time PCR assay

All clinical specimens underwent real-time (RT) PCR testing targeting the Mpoxv using validated primers and probes [26]. Diagnostic approach included RT-PCR for Orthopoxvirus (non-variola and Mpox) using E9L gene, followed by additional testing for Mpoxv-specific PCR, including the B6R gene which indicated presence of Mpox viral DNA. The assay specifically amplified regions of the Mpoxv genome, with cycle threshold (Ct) values below 40 considered positive. Samples with Ct values between 35-40 underwent repeat testing for confirmation [26].

Screening for anti-Mpox virus immunoglobulin M and immunoglobulin G antibodies

Serum samples were tested for anti-Mpoxv immunoglobulin (Ig) G and IgM antibodies using indigenously developed enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs), as previously described [27]. Anti-Mpoxv IgG detection was performed using an indirect ELISA with inactivated Mpoxv antigen-coated microtiter plates (2 µg per well). Serum samples diluted 1:100 were screened, and bound IgG was detected using horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-human IgG. A cut-off optical density of 0.2 with a positive-to-negative ratio >1.5 defined seropositivity [27].

Quality assurance and training

Before study initiation, all participating sites trained on MPLD case identification, clinical assessment techniques, sample collection procedures, and biosafety protocols. Regular supervision visits were conducted to ensure adherence to standard operating procedures and data quality standards. Laboratory personnel at all VRDLs received specialized training from ICMR-NIV Pune on Mpox diagnostic techniques, sample handling, and result interpretation. External quality assurance was maintained through participation in proficiency testing programs.

Data management and statistical analysis

All data were double-entered into a secure, web-based data management system by trained data entry operators. Regular data quality checks were performed to identify missing values, logical inconsistencies, and outliers. Discrepancies were resolved through verification against source documents.

Given the descriptive nature of this sentinel surveillance study, statistical analysis was primarily descriptive. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were described using means, medians, and ranges, as appropriate. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with the data on ICMR server.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of ICMR-National Institute of Translational Virology and AIDS Research (NARI/EC/Approval/2023/681, dated February 23, 2023) and by the ethics committees at all participating sites. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in their local languages. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without affecting their access to routine care. All laboratory results were communicated through post-test counseling by trained staff, and those with confirmed or suspected Mpox were referred for appropriate clinical management and public health follow-up according to national guidelines [28].

Results

Study population and MPLD case detection

During the 6-month study period, a total of 1796 individuals attended the seven participating community clinics and underwent initial screening for MPLD. Of these, six (0.33%) individuals met the case definition criteria and were enrolled for detailed assessment and biological sample collection.

Sociodemographic characteristics of MPLD cases

All six individuals who met the MPLD case definition identified as men. The age distribution showed four (66.7%) participants in the younger adult category of 18-30 years, whereas two (33.3%) were aged between 31 and 40 years. The median age was 26 years (range: 22-35 years).

All participants had completed primary education, with three (50%) having attended secondary school and one (16.7%) having completed higher secondary education. None had pursued tertiary education. In terms of marital status, all six participants reported being unmarried, which is consistent with the predominantly young, urban MSM demographic served by these clinics.

Two (33.3%) participants worked in private sector jobs, two (33.3%) were engaged in daily wage labor, one (16.7%) was self-employed, and one (16.7%) was unemployed at the time of the study. All participants resided in urban areas, with five (83.3%) living with family or friends and one was (16.7%) living alone.

None of the participants reported a history of smallpox vaccination, and physical examination revealed no vaccination scars on any participant. This finding is consistent with the discontinuation of routine smallpox vaccination in India in the early 1980s and supports the vulnerability of this population to Orthopoxvirus infections [29]. The observed behavioral risk patterns were closely aligned with the demographic profile of the participants. Younger individuals (aged 18-30 years) more frequently reported multiple sexual partners and inconsistent condom use. All participants identified as MSM, and the clustering of high-risk sexual behaviors within this group reflects known transmission dynamics observed in similar populations worldwide.

Behavioral and exposure history

All six participants reported being sexually active within the 21 days preceding their clinic visit and symptom onset. When asked about the number of sexual partners, four (66.7%) participants reported having multiple sexual partners in the month before sample collection, whereas two (33.3%) reported having one partner during this period.

Regarding the types of sexual contact, all participants reported engaging in anal intercourse, with five (83.3%) reporting insertive and receptive roles. Four (66.7%) participants also reported oral sexual contact. Consistent condom use was reported by only two (33.3%) participants, whereas the remainder reported inconsistent or no barrier protection use.

None of the participants reported any history of international travel within the preceding 21 days or, indeed, within the past year. This finding is particularly significant because it suggests that any Mpox infection in these individuals would represent indigenous transmission rather than travel-related importation.

Similarly, none of the participants reported any contact with animals, including handling domestic animals, contact with wild animals, or consumption of undercooked meat products, within the 21 days preceding symptom onset. This absence of zoonotic exposure supports the hypothesis that human-to-human transmission is the predominant mode of spread in the current outbreak context.

Two (33.3%) participants reported having had contact with individuals who had experienced similar symptoms (skin lesions accompanied by fever or body aches) within the preceding month, although neither of these contacts had been formally diagnosed with Mpox or undergone confirmatory testing. The absence of travel history combined with these behavioral patterns suggests that any potential transmission, if present, would likely occur within local sexual networks.

Symptomatic presentation and clinical examination

The clinical presentation of MPLD cases showed considerable consistency across participants. All six (100%) individuals presented with papular skin lesions as their primary complaint. The lesions were predominantly located in the genital and perianal areas in five (83.3%) cases. with one participant also having lesions on the inner thighs. One (16.7%) participant had lesions exclusively on the hands and forearms.

The morphologic characteristics of the lesions varied depending on the stage of disease progression at presentation. Three (50%) participants presented with vesicular lesions, two (33.3%) had pustular lesions, and one (16.7%) had crusted lesions consistent with the healing phase. The number of lesions ranged from three to 15 per participant, with a median of seven lesions.

Systemic symptoms were universally present and provided important diagnostic clues. All six (100%) participants complained of headache and generalized muscle pain (myalgia). The most commonly reported systemic symptoms were headache and myalgia (100%), followed by fever (66.7%) and lymphadenopathy (66.7%). Other common symptoms included ocular pain and chest pain, each reported by three (50%) participants. Joint pain (arthralgia), sore throat, vomiting, and diarrhea were each reported by two (33.3%) participants.

Fever was reported by four (66.7%) participants, although only two had documented fever at the time of presentation. The reported fever was generally low-grade (37.5-38.5°C) and had either resolved spontaneously or responded to over-the-counter antipyretics by the time of clinic presentation.

Lymphadenopathy, a characteristic feature of Mpox infection, was clinically detectable in four (66.7%) participants. Enlarged lymph nodes were predominantly in the inguinal region in three cases and cervical region in one case. The lymph nodes were described as tender, mobile, and ranging from 1 to 3 cm in diameter.

Medical history and comorbidities

One (16.7%) participant was known to be HIV-positive and receiving antiretroviral therapy, with an undetectable viral load. This individual had been diagnosed with HIV 3 years before the study and had maintained good adherence to treatment.

One (16.7%) participant had been diagnosed with hypertension and receiving regular antihypertensive medication. The remaining four participants reported no significant past medical history or chronic medical conditions.

Regarding lifestyle factors, two (33.3%) participants reported current tobacco smoking, with both smoking approximately five to 10 cigarettes per day. One (16.7%) participant reported regular alcohol consumption, although not to the extent of alcohol use disorder.

None of the participants reported use of immunosuppressive medications, recent vaccination with any vaccine, or recent hospitalization. This information was important for interpreting serological results and assessing the likelihood of vaccine-induced cross-reactive antibodies.

Laboratory findings

All biological specimens collected from the six MPLD cases underwent RT-PCR testing for Mpoxv. All specimens from all six participants tested negative for Mpoxv DNA. Anti-Mpoxv IgG antibody testing revealed that one (16.7%) participant had detectable IgG antibodies, suggesting previous exposure to Mpox. The presence of IgG antibodies in the absence of detectable viral nucleic acid suggests a past, possibly subclinical or self-limiting, infection that had resolved spontaneously.

All six participants tested negative for anti-Mpoxv IgM antibodies, indicating no evidence of acute infection at the time of sample collection. This finding aligns with the negative PCR results and suggests that the clinical symptoms observed were either attributable to other causes or that any preceding IgM response had already waned.

Case management and follow-up

All participants received symptomatic treatment and counseling on their test results. The participant who was IgG-positive received additional guidance on previous Mpox exposure and prevention strategies. All participants received prevention guidance, including consistent condom use, recognition of Mpox symptoms, and the importance of early health care seeking if symptoms develop in themselves or their partners.

Discussion

This sentinel surveillance study represents the first systematic, community-based investigation of MPLD among high-risk populations in India. The findings provide important insights into the current epidemiologic landscape of Mpox in key populations and highlight the potential and limitations of syndromic surveillance approaches.

Case detection and prevalence

The identification of six MPLD cases among 1796 clinic attendees represents a detection rate of 0.33%, which is considerably lower than might be expected given the global burden of Mpox in similar populations [[30], [31], [32]]. The screened population primarily comprised MSM and a smaller proportion of TG individuals accessing community-based sexual health services (MSM 64.9% vs TG 24.8%). However, this low detection rate should be interpreted within the broader context of India’s relatively limited Mpox burden, with only 66 confirmed cases reported nationally by the time of this study.

The absence of confirmed Mpox cases by RT-PCR testing, despite clinically compatible presentations, raises important questions about the sensitivity of syndromic surveillance approaches and the differential diagnosis of MPLD in resource-limited settings [33]. The clinical features observed in our participants, particularly the presence of vesiculopustular lesions in genital and perianal areas accompanied by systemic symptoms, could be attributable to various infectious conditions, including herpes simplex virus infection, varicella-zoster virus reactivation, bacterial skin infections, [34,35].

Demographic and behavioral patterns

The demographic profile of MPLD cases aligns closely with global patterns observed during the 2022-2023 Mpox outbreak [36]. The predominance of young, urban MSM with multiple sexual partners mirrors findings from outbreak investigations in Europe, North America, and other regions where sexual networks facilitated rapid transmission [37].

The universal absence of smallpox vaccination history among participants confirms the vulnerability of younger populations to Orthopoxvirus infections. This finding is particularly relevant for public health planning because it suggests that most individuals aged under 45 years in India lack cross-protective immunity against Mpox [16].

The high proportion of participants reporting multiple sexual partners and inconsistent condom use highlights the potential for rapid transmission within sexual networks if Mpox were to establish sustained transmission in these communities. However, the absence of confirmed cases in our study suggests that such transmission had not occurred significantly by the time of data collection.

The interaction between demographic and behavioral characteristics observed in this study highlights a convergence of risk factors, particularly, among young, urban MSM engaging in multiple partnerships and inconsistent protective practices. Such clustering of behavioral risk within specific demographic groups has been widely reported in global Mpox outbreaks and underscores the importance of targeted surveillance strategies.

Clinical presentation and diagnostic challenges

The clinical presentations observed in our study participants closely resembled those reported in confirmed Mpox cases worldwide, with genital and perianal lesions, systemic symptoms, and lymphadenopathy being prominent features [38]. However, the negative RT-PCR results underscore the challenges of clinical diagnosis and the importance of laboratory confirmation [39].

Several factors may explain the discordance between clinical presentation and laboratory results. First, the timing of sample collection relative to symptom onset may have been suboptimal because viral shedding is known to be highest in the early stages of illness. Second, the quality of lesion samples may have been compromised by previous treatment with topical medications or by sampling from lesions in the later stages of healing. Third, the observed clinical syndrome may have been caused by other pathogens that can produce similar presentations [38,39].

The detection of IgG antibodies in one participant without a corresponding viral nucleic acid or IgM response suggests previous exposure to Mpoxv or a related Orthopoxvirus. IgG antibodies typically become detectable after the acute phase and may persist for months, indicating past exposure rather than active infection. This finding raises the possibility of no confirmed evidence of active transmission of the Mpoxv in the community, with individuals experiencing mild or subclinical infections that resolve without medical attention [27]. This finding should be interpreted cautiously and does not indicate active transmission within the community. However, the possibility of cross-reactivity with other Orthopoxviruses and limitations inherent to serological assays cannot be excluded. Although Orthopoxviruses (including vaccinia, cowpox, and buffalopox) share significant antigenic similarities capable of inducing cross-reactive antibodies, the absence of smallpox vaccination in younger cohorts simplifies the interpretation of serological results. In such populations, a positive IgG result is more likely to represent a specific response to a recent or previous Mpox infection rather than cross-reactivity stemming from a smallpox vaccine. The observed clinical presentations may also be attributable to other conditions, such as herpes simplex virus infection, varicella-zoster virus, or bacterial skin infections, which were not systematically evaluated in this study.

Transmission patterns and contact tracing

The absence of international travel history among all participants and the lack of animal exposure suggest that any Mpox transmission in this population would represent indigenous person-to-person spread rather than importation or zoonotic transmission. This finding has important implications for public health surveillance and control strategies.

The contact tracing efforts, although limited by the small number of cases and moderate participation rates, did not identify any additional suspected cases. The findings should, therefore, be interpreted cautiously because either transmission was not occurring efficiently within these networks or contacts were experiencing subclinical infections that did not meet our case definition criteria.

Surveillance system performance

The integration of Mpox surveillance into existing CBO-based sexual health services proved feasible and acceptable to providers and clients. The trust and rapport that CBOs maintain with their clients facilitated participation and reduced stigma-related barriers that might otherwise prevent case detection.

However, the study also highlighted several challenges in implementing syndromic surveillance for Mpox. The reliance on clinical case definitions, although practical for resource-limited settings, may lack sufficient specificity to distinguish Mpox from other conditions causing similar presentations. The need for laboratory confirmation becomes critical but may be constrained by limited diagnostic capacity and the stability of specimens during transport.

Limitations and considerations

One limitation of the study was the selection of participants from high-risk populations accessing CBO services because the study population was one of the important limitations of the study. This study population may not be representative of the broader MSM and TG population in India. Second, the reliance on self-reported behavioral data may introduce recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly given the sensitive nature of the information collected.

Third, the absence of systematic testing for other potential causes of MPLD limits our ability to determine the actual etiology of the observed clinical presentations. Fourth, the 6-month study duration may not capture seasonal variations or evolving transmission dynamics.

Public health implications

The findings of this study have several important implications for Mpox surveillance and preparedness in India. The successful implementation of CBO-based surveillance demonstrates the feasibility of leveraging existing community health infrastructure for emerging disease surveillance. This approach could be scaled up to provide broader geographic coverage and earlier detection of outbreaks.

The predominance of negative RT-PCR results despite clinically compatible presentations highlights the need for early periodic and timely surveillance to capture the cases during early phases of community transmission. The development of rapid, point-of-care diagnostic tests that could be deployed in community settings, which can be helpful in early detection and diagnosis of Mpoxv. [33]. Investment in training health care providers in MPLD recognition and differential diagnosis would also enhance surveillance sensitivity.

The detection of evidence for previous Mpox exposure in the absence of recognized illness suggests that enhanced community awareness and education programs are needed to promote early health care seeking and prevent onward transmission. Such programs should address stigma and discrimination that may prevent key populations from accessing care.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of community-based sentinel surveillance for MPLD among high-risk populations in India. Although no laboratory-confirmed Mpox cases were identified, the study provides valuable insights into clinical presentation and behavioral risk patterns in this population. These findings support the role of community-integrated surveillance systems in strengthening early detection and preparedness for emerging infections.

The findings highlight the need for sustained vigilance and surveillance for Mpox in key populations, coupled with strengthening laboratory diagnostic capacity, and building trust with community engagement to promote timely health-seeking behavior. As the global Mpox epidemiology evolves, with new clades, changing transmission dynamics, and expanding geographic reach, India must maintain readiness to rapidly detect and respond to emergent cases.

Future surveillance efforts should consider expanding case definitions to capture milder or atypical clinical presentations, integrating testing for differential diagnoses, and establishing more robust contact tracing and follow-up mechanisms. The demonstrated success of CBO-led surveillance in this study provides a scalable model for integrating community participation into national frameworks for emerging infectious disease surveillance in India.
