To the Editor:

We read the recent article by Tan et al. [1] entitled “Concavity of the maximal expiratory flow–volume curve, and incidence of COPD and respiratory symptoms: a population-based cohort study”, published in ERJ Open Research. This is an intriguing study demonstrating that the concavity indices, central concavity and peripheral concavity, are useful markers for assessing the future risk of respiratory symptoms.

The concavity in the descending limb of the maximal expiratory flow–volume (MEFV) curve is commonly observed in patients with obstructive lung disease. The mechanism of concaved shape is recognised as the loss of lung elastic recoil, suggesting a component of lung emphysema [2, 3].

We previously reported the relationship between the obstructive index, a simple indicator of the shape of the MEFV curve, and the extent of emphysema in 133 patients with chronic obstructive lung disease [4]. The obstructive index is defined as the forced vital capacity (FVC) divided by the volume between the points of half peak expiratory flow on the MEFV curve (figure 1a). Therefore, we have a great interest in confirming the relationship between the obstructive index and central concavity or peripheral concavity, because these are based on relatively similar concepts.

In this post hoc analysis, we reanalysed our study population with obstructive lung disease (COPD, n=65; bronchial asthma, n=53; and asthma–COPD overlap, n=15).

Emphysematous lesions were evaluated as the percentage of low-attenuation volume with a threshold of −960 Hounsfield units to the total lung volume (LAV%) on chest computed tomography (CT) [5].

The mean±sd age was 67.1±12.8 years, which differed from the population of Tan et al. [1], composed of middle-aged people. The mean±sd forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/FVC, FEV1 % predicted, central concavity and peripheral concavity were 67.1±15.0%, 66.4±24.4% predicted, 50.8±33.5% and 68.1±25.1%, respectively. The obstructive index was significantly correlated with central concavity (r=0.67, p<0.0001) and peripheral concavity (r=0.49, <0.0001) with certain variation. Figure 1b–d shows that the obstructive index varies even at the same level of central and peripheral concavity. While the significant correlations of LAV% were found with obstructive index (r=0.59, p<0.0001), central concavity (r=0.47, p<0.0001) and peripheral concavity (r=0.29, p=0.0007), the multivariable regression analysis adjusted by age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, the type of the CT scanner, FEV1 % predicted and FEV1/FVC below the lower limit of normal, revealed that the obstructive index, but not the central and peripheral concavity, was associated with LAV%.

The observed different associations with LAV% may depend on which part of the MEFV curve the index reflects. We can speculate that the obstructive index is influenced by the entire concavity of the MEFV curve, including forced expiratory flow at 50% of the FVC and forced expiratory flow at 75% of the FVC, which are the focus for calculating central and peripheral concavity. Furthermore, the present results may be due to the difference in the time point at which disease progression occurs. Our study population includes patients with established diagnosis, but the study by Tan et al. [1] showed a “future risk” of developing respiratory diseases.

In conclusion, the obstructive index is related to central concavity and peripheral concavity, which helps detect the extent of emphysema in chest CT. The obstruction index is likely to be widely used in the evaluation of obstructive lung disease, as it can be easily calculated from the MEFV curve.
