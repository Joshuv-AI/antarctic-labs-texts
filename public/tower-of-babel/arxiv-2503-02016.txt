Mind the (Belief) Gap: Group Identity in the World of LLMs
Angana Borah Marwa Houalla Rada Mihalcea

Abstract
Social biases and belief-driven behaviors can significantly impact Large Language Models’ (LLMs’) decisions on several tasks. As LLMs are increasingly used in multi-agent systems for societal simulations, their ability to model fundamental group psychological characteristics remains critical yet under-explored. In this study, we present a multi-agent framework that simulates belief congruence , a classical group psychology theory that plays a crucial role in shaping societal interactions and preferences. Our findings reveal that LLMs exhibit amplified belief congruence compared to humans, across diverse contexts. We further investigate the implications of this behavior on two downstream tasks: (1) misinformation dissemination and (2) LLM learning, finding that belief congruence in LLMs increases misinformation dissemination and impedes learning. To mitigate these negative impacts, we propose strategies inspired by: (1) contact hypothesis, (2) accuracy nudges, and (3) global citizenship framework. Our results show that the best strategies reduce misinformation dissemination by up to 37 % 37\% and enhance learning by 11 % 11\% . Bridging social psychology and AI, our work provides insights to navigate real-world interactions using LLMs while addressing belief-driven biases.

Multi-Agent Framework for Belief Congruence

2.1
Psychological Foundations

The Belief Congruence theory
Rokeach 1960
suggests that people value beliefs and prioritize individuals based on belief alignment. It argues that beliefs are the most important factor in determining social identities and provides a nuanced perspective on social prejudice and the influence of beliefs on social evaluations.

For our simulation, we focus on the framework by
Rokeach and Mezei 1966
, that uses a factorial design of
Race x Belief
presenting four stimulus confederates: (1) a Black person who agrees, (2) a Black person who disagrees, (3) a White person who agrees, and (4) a White person who disagrees with the participant. Two main experiments are conducted: (1) on campus and (2) in the field.

In the campus study, White participants discuss topics such as the
invasion of Cuba, campus fraternities, dormitory visitation, Bible reading in schools, or the grading system
with four confederates. They then
choose two confederates for coffee
, either in a public or a private place. Participants are also categorized as high- or low-prejudiced based on survey responses
Adorno et al. 1950
.

In the field study, both White and Black participants applying to psychiatric hospitals discuss handling psychiatric patient scenarios with four confederates. Topics include
missing meals, delusions, inappropriate behavior, seating changes, or juvenile offenders
. Participants then
choose two coworkers they would prefer to work with
in the future. Results show that shared beliefs often outweigh racial group membership in predicting social preference; for instance, White participants often preferred Black individuals with the same beliefs over White individuals with opposing ones.

The Impact of Belief Congruence on Downstream Tasks

Studies in psychology and social science show that belief congruence increases misinformation persuasiveness aligned with partisan identities
Cosgrove et al. 2024
. The “Belief Echoes Phenomenon”
Thorson 2016
reveals that attitudinal effects related to misinformation persist even after correction. Moreover, belief congruence hinders learning by reducing curiosity for opposing views
Ertl et al. 2022
and increasing mental load in students
Wesenberg et al. 2022
.

Misinformation Dissemination
: (heterogeneous group shown) involves political personas interacting and determining the veracity of a news item.

Therefore, we explore two downstream tasks to understand the effects of belief congruence in LLMs:
(1) Misinformation Dissemination:
Investigating how similar beliefs may disseminate misinformation and
(2) Learning:
Analyzing how belief congruence affects learning outcomes in LLMs. For both downstream tasks, we leverage political personas as individuals aligned with a particular political alignment often share deeply rooted and cohesive belief systems
Balliet et al. 2018
;
Brandt et al. 2019
;
Elder and O’brian 2022
. Additionally, ablation studies on the original belief congruence study (See Section
2.3
) demonstrate that political beliefs can serve as an effective proxy for examining belief-related behaviors in LLM agents.

3.1
Misinformation Dissemination

Previous research has demonstrated that individuals are more susceptible to misinformation from politically aligned sources, as they are more persuaded by and accepting of advice from like-minded individuals
Traberg et al. 2024
. Political polarization and echo chambers
3
3

3

Echo chambers are environments where individuals are exposed only to information that reinforces their existing beliefs, limiting diverse perspectives.
have been extensively studied in various contexts, such as Covid-19 pandemic
Jiang et al. 2021
, 2016 US elections
Center 2016
, etc. Building on these insights, we design our first task to investigate the dynamics of echo chamber formation and the spread of misinformation. We focus on the Democrat and Republican belief systems in the US, examining interactions within homogeneous groups (comprising exclusively Democrat or Republican agents). This approach allows us to explore if belief congruence influences the spread of misinformation.

Framework.

Personas.
Each interaction consists of four LLM agents initialized as political personas—either Democrat or Republican. We use a straightforward prompt:
‘‘Assume you are a <political alignment> person.’’
, where
political alignment = Democrat/Republican
. While we acknowledge that political alignment exists on a spectrum and individuals within a party may hold diverse beliefs
4
4

4

https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2021/11/09/beyond-red-vs-blue-the-political-typology-2/
, we do not incorporate fine-grained political distinctions in our experiments. This is because LLMs possess a generalized understanding of politically aligned personas (refer to Appendix
B.3
for details). By enabling interactions between these simplified personas with their generalized beliefs, we can study the spread of misinformation among LLMs with similar belief systems.

Interaction.
Each agent is presented with a news item—either true or false, and they engage in discussions to assess its veracity. The framework inspired from
Borah and Mihalcea 2024
, consists of four rounds: (1) Initial evaluations are provided by agents (if they think the news is true or false), (2) agents convince other agents that their own belief is correct, (3) agents are then prompted to be open to other agents’ perspectives and (4) agents share their final (revised) evaluations. This allows us to investigate the effects of group composition on the understanding and reinforcement of misinformation. We provide an example of the framework in Fig
4
and prompt design in Appendix
B.4
.

Datasets.
We use two datasets related to US Politics: LIAR benchmark
Wang 2017
and Fake News Elections
Raza et al. 2024
. The LIAR dataset contains 12,836 claims from May-Nov, 2017 and the Fake News Elections dataset contains 38,333 claims from Apr-Oct, 2023. For Fake News, we only use the 9513 human-verified claims with labels. Further details are in Appendix
B.1
.

3.2
Learning in LLMs

Inspired by
Marks et al. 2019
, the task examines how ideological alignment, or political homophily, influences learning and decision-making in LLMs. We analyze if LLM agents favor information from politically similar sources, even in non-political tasks, and to what extent this can hinder learning.

Framework.
We adopt a two-stage framework (
Learning
and
Choice
) similar to
Marks et al. 2019
( Fig
6
).
In the
Learning stage
, a participant agent interacts with four sources through two types of interleaved trials: (1) an abstract “Merlin” pattern recognition task (where “Merlin” is a randomly assigned sentence pattern with no inherent meaning) (Examples provided in Appendix
C
), and (2) observing sources’ political views.
Sources play a crucial role in the Choice stage and may influence how participant agents choose to learn. Sources are designed with specific patterns: Similar-Accurate (80% political agreement
5
5

5

political agreement with the participant.
, 80% Merlin accuracy), Similar-Inaccurate (80% political agreement, 50% Merlin accuracy), Dissimilar-Accurate (20% political agreement, 80% Merlin accuracy), and Dissimilar-Inaccurate (20% political agreement, 50% Merlin accuracy).
In the
Choice stage
, The participant agent begins by answering a Merlin task question and rating its confidence in the response. It then selects between two random sources to review their answers, revises its own answer if desired, and updates its confidence rating accordingly. This setup tests whether participants prioritize a source’s accuracy or political alignment (from the choice made between two sources), even in a non-political task such as pattern recognition.

Our experiments involve 50 LLM agents with varying political preferences, progressing through 20 learning and choice trials each, making decisions at each Choice step.

Dataset.
For the Merlin task, we generate random sentences using
gpt-4o
and randomly assign either “true” or “false” labels to indicate the presence or absence of a Merlin pattern respectively. For the political agreement task, we utilize statements related to political ideologies from
Marks et al. 2019
. Examples are provided in Appendix
C
.

Evaluation.
We employ two evaluation strategies:

1. Source choice
: In the Choice stage, if an agent consistently chooses similar political sources over those with higher Merlin accuracy, it shows that political belief affects learning. We focus on cases where the two available sources are either Similar-Inaccurate (SI) or Dissimilar-Accurate (DA).
Choosing a SI source suggests that political beliefs influence decisions, even when a more accurate source is available
.

2. Confidence ratings
: In the Choice stage, agents share confidence ratings for their answers before and after viewing a source’s answer. Higher confidence ratings after choosing politically aligned sources indicate that political beliefs influence the agent’s trust in the Merlin pattern recognition task.

Table

In terms of
source choices

Comparison to the original study.

Marks et al. 2019
show that participants chose to seek advice from politically like-minded sources for the shape categorization task, even when these sources were less competent. Participants falsely concluded that those sharing their political views were better at pattern recognition tasks and they were more influenced by politically similar sources’ opinions. These findings align with our results, which show that LLM agents tend to choose those with similar political viewpoints and have higher confidence in these sources’ answers. Therefore, belief congruence in LLMs influences their learning processes.

4.1
Contact Hypothesis Theory

Using contact hypothesis theory
Allport 1954
, we hypothesize that inter-group contact among LLM agents with varying beliefs can mitigate the negative effects of belief congruence, particularly in misinformation dissemination. Existing studies also indicate that while homogeneous groups are more likely to reinforce misinformation, whereas heterogeneous groups, characterized by diverse perspectives, can mitigate its spread
Röchert et al. 2021
. Thus, heterogeneous groups consisting of both Democrat and Republican agents are introduced. Note that we do not use the contact hypothesis in LLM learning as it consists of a single LLM agents learning about others without interaction, which is essential for the hypothesis.

4.3
Global Political Citizenship

The Global Citizenship
Dower 2003
framework expands human identity and responsibility beyond geographical boundaries. It emphasizes global awareness, compassion, cultural diversity, and a commitment to social justice and sustainability, with frameworks spanning multiple disciplines
Davies 2006
. We draw on the empirical framework by
Reysen and Katzarska-Miller 2013
, which explores the effects of globalization on identity and psychological functioning. The framework links antecedents (normative environment and global awareness) to outcomes (prosocial values like empathy, diversity, social justice, sustainability, and responsibility) through global citizenship. Building on their survey, we tailor the questions to introduce the concept of
Global Political Citizenship
, framing the questions within the context of US political dynamics. For example, the question,
‘I am able to empathize with people from other countries’
, is rephrased as
‘I am able to empathize with people from other political affiliations’
to suit the political setting. This concept characterizes individuals who are active, responsible, and inclusive across diverse political perspectives.

We hypothesize that an LLM simulating a global political citizen will reduce misinformation dissemination, and preserve its learning capabilities. The modified survey questions with scores depicting a global political citizen are added to the system prompts during LLM agent initialization. A complete list of survey questions is provided in Appendix
F.1
.

Related Work

Belief Congruence Theory.
The Belief Congruence theory
Rokeach 1960
states that people value individuals based on belief alignments. Further studies
Rokeach and Mezei 1966
showed that shared beliefs were a stronger predictor of liking than group membership. While the theory has been influential, it has also received several critiques (like many other psychological theories) regarding its universal applicability, especially in the context of larger social distances
Triandis and Triandis 1962
and environments where prejudice is institutionalized
Goldstein and Davis 1972
. Despite the criticism, it’s core premise that shared beliefs can transcend group boundaries continues to be an important direction of research.

Multi-Agent LLM Interactions.
Breakneck advances in LLMs have driven the development of multi-agent LLM interactions that leverage collective intelligence and specialized skills
(
Guo et al. 2024
)
, enabling agents to collaborate and make decisions in ways that mirror human cooperation. These systems have facilitated progress in diverse domains, including software development
(
Hong et al. 2023
)
, societal simulation
(
Park et al. 2023
)
, and polarization studies
(
Ohagi 2024
)
. For instance,
Park et al. 2023
perform societal simulations by incorporating observation, planning, and reflection to foster emergent behaviors. and
Zhou et al. 2024
enables LLMs to collaborate, compete, and serve as evaluators on complex social goals. However, underlying group behaviors in multi-agent LLM interactions remain under-explored. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that uses LLM agents to simulate belief congruence and examine its implications for downstream tasks.

6
Lessons Learned and Actionable Steps

Our findings reveal that LLMs exhibit belief congruence as humans, which in turn leads to further impacts across real-world societal tasks. We propose mitigation methods inspired from social psychology, and reduce the negative impacts of belief congruence to some extent. Through our experiments
and analyses, we learned several valuable insights and offer actionable recommendations:

LLMs exhibit amplified belief congruence.
Our LLM simulation experiments reveal that belief congruence is amplified in LLMs, becoming a dominant factor in LLM decision-making, driving fundamental group identity behaviors. Future work can investigate additional group psychological theories, such as Social Identity Theory
Tajfel 1970
and Realistic Conflict Theory
Sherif 1961
, to further explore LLM behavior in diverse group scenarios.

Belief congruence in LLMs can influence societally impactful tasks.
Belief congruence affects LLM performance in the context of misinformation, and learning capabilities. This mirrors the real-life impact of belief congruence in humans. Future work could investigate the role of belief diversity in improving LLM resilience and examine how group psychological behaviors can influence other societal applications.

Best mitigation strategies differ for downstream tasks
In our study, we explore mitigation techniques inspired by previous social psychological theories. Global Political Citizenship and Accuracy Nudges are the most effective mitigation strategies to address misinformation dissemination (increase in correctness rates by
37\%
) and LLM learning (increase in choosing dis-similar accurate sources by
11\%
) respectively. Future research can investigate additional mitigation strategies, including both generic strategies applicable across multiple tasks and specific ones tailored to individual tasks.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the presence of belief congruence in LLMs. We found that, similar to humans, LLMs exhibit belief congruence, often at levels higher than among humans. Further, as previously observed in human studies, we found that belief congruence can lead to negative effects in LLMs, such as misinformation spread and impact on learning abilities. To address these shortcomings, we introduced three mitigation techniques inspired from psychological research, and showed that they lead to improved performance in LLMs. To encourage future research in this direction, we open-source the code of our framework.

Limitations and Ethical Considerations.

Simplified Representations of Human Behavior.

Our approach to simulate belief congruence is based on one study by
Rokeach 1960
. Although seminal in its field, further simulation experiments and analyses in varied contexts would be helpful. Additionally, the simulation in LLMs might oversimplify the complex and dynamic nature of human belief systems. Human belief congruence is influenced by several factors such as sociocultural, emotional, and contextual factors, which LLMs cannot fully replicate. Hence, we encourage future work to dive deeper into the conversation logs of multi-agent LLMs which to better understand the complexities.

Generalizability across tasks.

We explore two downstream tasks in the context of misinformation and LLM learning to assess the impact of belief congruence in LLMs. However, these findings may not generalize to tasks or applications outside the specific downstream tasks analyzed. Belief congruence may also be helpful in certain tasks related to mental health and other fields. For instance, in psychotherapy, aligning with a patient’s core beliefs can build trust and rapport, leading to better therapeutic outcomes.

Cross-Cultural Implications.

The majority of studies and surveys referenced in our research were conducted within the U.S. context. As a result, our findings may unintentionally generalize across diverse cultural groups, potentially overlooking variations in belief congruence shaped by cultural differences. To address this limitation, future research should investigate how belief congruence and other psychological characteristics are influenced by cultural contexts, providing a more nuanced understanding of these dynamics across different populations.

Biases and Transparency.

Exploring belief congruence simulations in LLMs risks amplifying existing biases in their outputs, as observed by the rise in misinformation dissemination and its negative impact on learning. Additionally, concerns regarding transparency and accountability persist, as the decision-making processes of LLMs in real-world applications often lack sufficient explainability. While mitigation strategies derived from this study address issues caused by belief congruence, they could inadvertently reduce model effectiveness in other domains. These ethical considerations remain critical to ensuring that the study contributes positively and responsibly to both AI research and society.

Prompt Design for Misinformation Dissemination Task

ROUND 1

Assume you are <name> and your political affiliation is <Democrat/Republican>. Given claim: <c>. Based on your background as a <Democrat/Republican> person, determine if this is true information or misinformation. Respond in this format: <true information or misinformation>

ROUND 2

Assume you are <name>. Given claim: <c>. Based on your <memory>, convince others about your own perspective. You have to decide if this is true or misinformation. You have to choose one and respond in this format: <true information or misinformation> <brief reason>.

ROUND 3

Assume you are <name>. Given claim: <c>. Based on your <memory>, be also open to other perspective so that you can come to a consensus. You have to decide if this is true or misinformation. You have to choose one and respond in this format: <true information or misinformation> <brief reason>.

ROUND 4

Assume you are <name> and your political affiliation is <Democrat/Republican>. Given claim: <c>. Based on your background as a <Democrat/Republican> person and your <memory>, determine if this is true information or misinformation. Respond in this format: <true information or misinformation>

Figure 17:
Misinformation Dissemination Task: consists of 4 rounds - (1) initial identification of true/false information without access to others’ responses. (2) After first round, agents now have access to everyone’s responses, and they convince others about their own perspective, (3) Third round consists of keeping an open mindset to others’ responses, and (4) final identification of true/false information again without access to others’ responses. This task helps us understand how information is detected before and after interaction with other agents of similar or dis-similar affiliation.
