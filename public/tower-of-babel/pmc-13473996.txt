Nanofibrous PVA–PVP seed coating membranes for electrochemical monitoring of nutrient release and germination response
Authors: Sourav Dey, Soumen Basu, Elena Povernov, Nir Ohad, Moushumi Ghosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deciphering ion transport and nutrient release kinetics from polymeric delivery matrices under biologically active environments remains challenging because diffusion, polymer–electrolyte interactions, and biological uptake occur simultaneously. In the present study, a time-resolved electrochemical framework was developed to interrogate nutrient ion release from an electrospun polyvinyl alcohol–polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVA–PVP) nanofibrous seed-coating (Vigna mungo) membrane using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and cyclic voltammetry (CV). The nutrient-loaded nanofibrous layer was conformally deposited onto Vigna mungo seeds interfaced with a gold microelectrode platform, enabling in situ monitoring of electrochemical dynamics during seed imbibition, germination, and early seedling establishment. Structural and compositional characterization using X-ray diffraction, field-emission scanning electron microscopy, energy-dispersive spectroscopy, and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy verified homogeneous incorporation of essential macronutrient salts within a highly interconnected nanofibrous polymer network. These electrochemical responses correlated with improved germination efficiency, greater seedling vigor, enhanced biomass accumulation, and qualitative and comparative indicator control nutrient release.

Introduction

Efficient management of nutrient delivery during early plant development is an important challenge in sustainable agriculture. Conventional fertilizer application often results in uncontrolled nutrient loss through leaching, volatilization, and diffusion into surrounding soil environments.1 Such inefficiencies reduce nutrient utilization efficiency and contribute to environmental contamination of soil and groundwater.2 Seed coating technologies provide an attractive alternative by localizing nutrient sources directly at the seed interface, enabling improved germination and early seedling establishment while minimizing nutrient wastage.3

Polymer based seed coating systems have been widely explored for controlled delivery of agrochemicals.4 Among them, electrospun nanofibrous membranes offer significant advantages because of their high surface area, interconnected porosity, and tunable physicochemical properties.5 These structural characteristics facilitate rapid hydration, efficient ion transport pathways, and diffusion mediated release of encapsulated nutrients. Blends of polyvinyl alcohol and polyvinyl pyrrolidone are particularly suitable for such systems due to their hydrophilicity, biocompatibility, and ability to form hydrogen bonded polymer networks that promote ionic mobility and controlled transport of nutrient ions within hydrated matrices.6 Despite these advantages, the mechanistic understanding of nutrient ion transport from nanofibrous seed coatings during biologically active germination conditions remains largely unexplored. A critical limitation in current seed coating research is the absence of analytical approaches capable of monitoring nutrient release dynamics in real time. Conventional evaluation methods rely primarily on post germination growth measurements, which provide indirect information about nutrient delivery but do not resolve the underlying physicochemical transport processes.7 In particular, the evolution of ionic concentration gradients, diffusion kinetics, and interfacial charge transfer phenomena occurring at the seed coating interface during germination are poorly understood. Addressing this limitation requires analytical techniques capable of probing ion transport processes under dynamic biological environments.8

Electrochemical techniques offer a powerful platform for investigating such transport mechanisms. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy enables quantitative analysis of ionic conductivity, diffusion resistance, and interfacial charge transfer processes in hydrated polymer systems.9 Cyclic voltammetry provides complementary information regarding electroactive species, redox behaviour, and temporal variations in ionic concentration near electrode interfaces.10 When integrated with microelectrode platforms, these techniques allow in situ monitoring of ion transport phenomena with high sensitivity. However, electrochemical approaches have rarely been applied to study nutrient release from polymeric seed coatings during germination.11

In this work, a nutrient loaded electrospun polyvinyl alcohol polyvinyl pyrrolidone nanofibrous membrane is developed as a seed coating platform for controlled nutrient delivery to Vigna mungo. The central hypothesis is that the porous nanofibrous architecture regulates nutrient release through consistent with a diffusion-influenced transport character, based on impedance response shape, which can be quantitatively detected through electrochemical signatures generated at a microelectrode interface during seed germination.12 To test this hypothesis, the coated seed is integrated with a gold microelectrode chip that enables continuous electrochemical monitoring during germination and early seedling growth. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy and cyclic voltammetry are employed to capture the evolution of ionic transport, diffusion kinetics, and interfacial charge transfer associated with nutrient release and uptake.13,14 The resulting electrochemical responses are correlated with germination performance and seedling development to establish a mechanistic relationship between ion transport behaviour and biological growth outcomes. Designing precision nutrient management systems in agriculture. This study establishes a novel electrochemical framework for time-resolved monitoring of nutrient delivery in biologically active systems and highlights the potential of nanostructured polymer coatings for precision agriculture applications.

Materials and methodology

Materials and chemicals

Polyvinyl alcohol [PVA, (–C2H4O)n)], polyvinyl pyrrolidone [(C6H9NO)n], dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) [CH3SO·CH3], deionized (DI) water, ammonium dihydrogen orthophosphate [NH4H2PO4], potassium nitrate [KNO3], calcium nitrate tetrahydrate [(Ca(NO3)2·4H2O], magnesium sulfate heptahydrate [MgSO4·7H2O].

Formulation for seed coating membrane preparation

To prepare formulation for mung seed coating, PVA and PVP were taken in the weight percentages of 50% and 50% (i.e., 1 : 1 weight ratio), and their solutions were prepared using dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solvent. Afterward, a magnetic stirrer was used to combine the PVA and PVP solutions. Then, 1 mL, 2 mL, 4 mL, and 6 mL of aqueous solutions containing NH4H2PO4, MgSO4·7H2O, Ca(NO3)2·4H2O, and KNO3, respectively, at a concentration of 1.0 M were added to the PVA–PVP solution. S1 was the designation given to the mixture of PVA–PVP and nutritional substances.

Seed and microelectrode chip coating through electrospinning technique

A high-potential power supply (ESPIN-NANO, PECO-CHENNAI, India) was employed as the electric field source, generating potential differences up to 40 kV. Nutrient components loaded solution was employed as a formulation, supplied via four plastic syringes, each has a diameter of 8.1 mm and a volume of 2.5 mL, and attached to a capillary needle, which showed an 8.3 µL min−1 fluid flow rate. The anode and cathode were linked to the source (emitter of fluid jet) and the metallic drum-collector. The distance between the emitter and the collector was kept at 10 cm, and 24.3 kV was applied as the potential difference. The drum collector's speed was set at 296 rpm (rotated on the XZ-plane), and syringes were placed horizontally to the ground surface, translated along the Y axis by ± 30 mm on the XY-plane continually. Seeds and as well as, the microelectrode chips were attached to the drum-collector surface for seed coating, chip coating, and seed-on-chip coating. For seed-on-chip coating, primarily a single layer of electrospun material was coated on both the seeds and microelectrode chip through 4 hours of electrospinning; then, a single coated seed was placed onto the single-coated chip surface and again continued the electrospinning process to get the secondary coating; thus, it conformed the seed-on-chip coating through bilayers coating.

Characterizations of the synthesized nutrient-loaded PVA–PVP electrospun nanofiber membranes

The crystallite behaviour of the nutrients encapsulated electrospun membrane was determined through X-ray diffractometer (RIGAKU SmartLab SE) with Cu-Kα radiation (wavelength λ = 0.154 nm). Surface morphology of the synthesized membrane was studied using FE-SEM (Carl Zeiss Sigma 500 FEG-SEM) and elemental analysis of the synthesized nanoparticles is performed by EDS (Bruker). EDS was not used to directly determine the concentration of nutrients released solution. Instead, elemental mapping and structural analysis were conducted on the nanofibrous membrane before and after the nutrient-release experiment to examine changes in the relative elemental composition resulting from the release of the incorporated nutrient salts. Functional groups in the synthesized membrane were examined via FTIR spectrometer (PerkinElmer, Frontier) in between the range of 4000–400 cm−1. The electrochemical workstation (BioLogic, VSP-300) was used to electrochemical detection of encapsulated nutrient release and delivery from the fibrous membrane during seed germination and early growth stages of Vigna mungo.

The coated and uncoated seeds were kept in borosilicate glass beakers (250 mL volume), each containing 10 mL of DI water. The seeds were dipped into the DI water. The experimental setup for uncoated seeds served as the control, and the germination and growth of different coated seeds were investigated; each set included four seeds. Result and discussions

Structural and morphological characterization of nutrient-loaded electrospun nanofibrous membranes

The structural properties of the electrospun nanofibrous membrane composed of PVA–PVP and encapsulated nutrient salts were investigated using XRD, FTIR, FE-SEM, EDS, different concentrations of PVP/PVP FE-SEM study, and porosity test analyses to confirm the successful incorporation of ionic nutrients and to understand the physicochemical features governing ion transport.

The XRD pattern of the synthesized membrane reveals diffraction peaks corresponding to both polymeric and inorganic components (Fig. 2a). The prominent peak observed at 38.48° corresponds to the Miller index (111) for Al (JCPDS card number 00-001-1176) crystallographic plane of the aluminium substrate. Also, the peak at 29.43° corresponds to Al2O3 in the substrate (JCPDS card number 00-001-1305), which is reduced in intensity for S1. Additional diffraction features at approximately 11.66°, 19.04°, and 19.40° indicate the presence of semi-crystalline domains associated with interactions between nutrient salts and the polymer matrix.20,21 These peaks suggest partial ordering within the polymer blend induced by ionic coordination between nutrient ions and the polymer chains. The peak around 23.40° further confirms the presence of incorporated nutrient compounds. Such structural interactions are known to influence ionic mobility within polymer matrices by modifying free volume and segmental chain dynamics, thereby facilitating ion diffusion through the nanofibrous network.22

The FE-SEM micrographs (Fig. 2c) reveal a highly interconnected nanofibrous network with uniform morphology. The membrane consists of a combination of thick fibers and ultra-thin, spider-web-like structures with a diameter of 266.50 nm.31 Such hierarchical structures are typical of electrospinning processes involving ionic additives and arise due to electrostatic stretching of charged polymer jets. The resulting porous architecture provides a large surface area and interconnected pathways that facilitate water penetration and ion diffusion.32

EDS elemental analysis (Fig. 2d) confirms the presence of C, O, N, K, Mg, Ca, S, and P elements corresponding to the polymer backbone and the encapsulated nutrient salts. The elemental distribution verifies the homogeneous incorporation of nutrient ions throughout the nanofibrous matrix. The combination of high porosity, nanoscale fiber diameters, and ionic interactions within the polymer network is expected to enhance electrolyte infiltration and promote consistent with a diffusion-influenced transport character, based on impedance response shape of nutrient ions during germination (Fig. 3).

Evaluation of nutrient-loaded nanofibrous seed coatings

Germination performance of coated seeds

The influence of the nutrient-loaded nanofibrous coating on seed germination was evaluated by comparing coated seeds with uncoated control seeds. Germination was monitored by counting seeds exhibiting visible radicle emergence. The nanofiber-coated seeds demonstrated significantly improved germination kinetics compared with the control group. Each experimental set had 32 seeds (N = 32). A germination percentage of 100% was achieved within 24 hours for coated seeds, whereas the control seeds required approximately 120 hours to reach the same level of germination. The accelerated germination suggests that the electrospun coating provides a favourable microenvironment that promotes rapid hydration and nutrient availability at the seed surface. The highly porous nanofibrous architecture facilitates rapid water absorption and efficient gaseous exchange, while the encapsulated nutrients provide a localized nutrient reservoir. Together, these factors contribute to enhanced metabolic activation during the early stages of germination (Fig. 4a).

Seedling Growth and vigor enhancement

Following germination, the influence of the nanofibrous coating on early plant development was evaluated by measuring seedling length (SL), root length (RL), and seedling vigor index (Svi) over time (Fig. 5). The results indicate that seeds coated with the nutrient-loaded nanofibrous membrane exhibit (Fig. 4b) significantly improved seedling growth compared with uncoated controls. The coated seeds display greater shoot elongation (Fig. 5a) and higher seedling vigor (Fig. 5b) indices throughout the growth period. Enhanced root development (Fig. 5c) is also observed during the early growth stages, indicating efficient nutrient delivery from the coating matrix to the developing seedling. The improved growth performance can be attributed to the controlled release of nutrient ions from the nanofibrous membrane. The sustained availability of essential ions such as potassium, phosphate, calcium, and magnesium supports cellular metabolism, enzymatic activity, and osmotic regulation during seedling development.

Influence of nanofibrous coating on biomass accumulation

Fresh and dry weight measurements were performed to quantify biomass accumulation and evaluate nutrient uptake efficiency (Fig. 6). Plants grown from coated seeds (S1) exhibit significantly higher fresh and dry weights compared with control (S0) plants. The increased biomass indicates enhanced nutrient assimilation and improved water uptake capacity. The higher water content observed in coated seed treatments suggests that the nanofibrous membrane improves water retention around the seedling root zone, thereby facilitating efficient nutrient transport through the plant vascular system. These findings confirm that the electrospun coating acts as an effective nutrient delivery system capable of enhancing plant growth during early developmental stages.

Electrochemical monitoring of nutrient release during seed germination

Electrochemical detection of ion release from the nanofibrous membrane

The electrochemical behavior associated with nutrient release from the nanofibrous membrane was investigated using cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) integrated with a gold microelectrode sensing platform. Upon immersion of the coated seed in deionized water, dissolution of nutrient salts within the nanofibrous matrix leads to the generation of mobile ionic species in the surrounding electrolyte. These ions diffuse toward the electrode interface and participate in electrochemical processes that generate measurable impedance and voltammetric signals. The observed electrochemical responses therefore provide direct information about ionic transport processes, diffusion kinetics, and interfacial charge transfer events associated with nutrient release and uptake during seed germination. Electrochemical dynamics during the pre-germination stage

Nyquist impedance spectra (Fig. 8) recorded during the variation of ionic conductivity of the coated membrane onto the microelectrode chip surface due to leaching nutrient components to the aqueous media and nutrient components uptake by the seed throughout seed germination and seedling growth; thus, exhibiting the variation in bulk resistance (Rb). Also, the variation in bulk resistance of nanofibrous coated membrane with time during germination and seedling growth is shown in Fig. 8f. After calibration, the detection process is based on consecutive steps such as, in the presence of water (after dipping the coated membrane-embedded microelectrode chip in water) the nutrient components are released from the membrane and transported towards the electrode surface, then ionization of nutrient components that cause ionic conduction at the electrode surface, next, discharge of that ionized products away from the electrode surface and their transportation35,36 into seed or seedling during germination and growth phases. It is seen that from the initial to 1 hour a rapid increase in Rb indicates rapid transportation of nutrient ions in seed just after releasing it from the membrane towards the electrodes, which after 3 hours suddenly decreases, and also the same trend is followed by up to 5 hours, which hints a repetition of slow release of nutrient ions from the membrane and their accumulation to the electrode surface. At this early stage, before germination, the dried seeds swell as they take up water and nutrients, resulting in a sudden increase in Rb after 1 hour. Afterward, the seed swelling process slows, leading to less nutrient transfer with water to the seed and more ions accumulating on the electrode surface due to the slow release of nutrients from the membrane, resulting in a decrease in Rb for up to 5 hours. After seed germination at 18 hours, Rb slightly increases and reaches a value almost identical to that at 1 hour, indicating a relatively higher nutrient uptake rate by the germinated seed than the rate of nutrient release from the membrane. Rb decreases again after 21 hours, even exhibiting a lower value relative to that seen after three hours of observation, indicated by charge transfer across the membrane due to the release of nutrients. Next, Rb gradually increases until 29 hours due to nutrient ion transportation to the up-growing seedling. After 45 hours, Rb decreases slightly compared to the previous data of 29 hours, subjected to nutrient release from the membrane, which after 48 hours increases and reveals the transportation of nutrients from the membrane to the growing plant, and helps seedling growth promotion. This trend continues until 50 hours, with the same Rb as at 48 hours, indicating steady-state equilibrium between nutrient release and uptake during these periods. Once again, a fall in Rb at 53 hours relative to that at 48 and 50 hours can be attributed to charge transfer due to the movement of nutrient ions across the membrane, indicating nutrient release by the membrane. Then, Rb exhibits a continuous, slow increase from 66 hours to 82 hours, without any sudden decrease, suggesting higher nutrient transport in the seedling than nutrient release from the membrane. After 96 hours, a remarkable rapid increase in Rb is observed and continues through 108 hours, indicating faster nutrient transport to the seedling and promoting faster seedling growth. No periodic fluctuation in Rb is seen in this stage rather than a rapid increment. It predicts that nutrient uptake dominates over nutrient ion release from the membrane. However, after 120 hours, no change in Rb is observed, with the same value as at 108 hours. This signifies no more charge transfer across the membrane, that is, no nutrient ion remains left to transport from the membrane, which can translocate to the seedling through roots. Thus, throughout this experimental period, discrete transfer of nutrient ions across the membrane, rather than continuous ionic transport, indicates a slow, qualitative and comparative indicator control release of nutrients during germination and growth of Vigna mungo, which regulates seedling health.

The cyclic voltammogram (CV) (as shown in Fig. 9) carries the signatures of the above phenomena related to the controlled release of nutrients from the electrospun nanofibrous membrane and their delivery during seed germination and seedling growth. After calibration, CV measurements were taken. At initial, an oxidative (anodic) peak (in the potential window of +0.10 V < E < +0.20 V) and two reductive (cathodic) peaks (in the potential widows of −0.05 V < E < 0.00 V and +0.05 V < E < +0.15 V) are seen due to the abundance of nutrient elements on the nanofiber membrane embedded microelectrode chip surface and their successive oxidation and reduction, respectively. These peaks are raised by the transportation of electroactive nutrient components from the nutrient-encapsulated nanofiber membrane to the electrode via diffusion. This is also supported by the lowest Rb value obtained from the preceding Nyquist plot in Fig. 8. After 1 hour the nutrient components are transported from the electrode surface into the water and thereafter to the seed, resulting in the disappearance of oxidation and reduction peaks. After 3 hours, the anodic current is increased at the positive end without any oxidative peak, indicating a slow surface oxidation and a fast electrode reaction,37 also a broad reduction wave in the potential window of −0.05 V < E < 0.15 V is raised, suggesting transportation of a few electroactive nutrient components from the membrane to the electrode via diffusion. The same phenomenon is noticed even after 5 hours with further enhancement in anodic current at the positive end without any oxidative peak, but the presence of a prominent cathodic peak (during reduction) in the potential window of −0.05 V < E < 0.15 V. This period (3–5 hours) indicates a less nutrient transfer to the seed and more ions accumulation on the electrode surface due to the slow release of nutrients from the membrane. After seed germination, at 18 hours, a slight decrease in anodic current along with a reduction in the cathodic peak amplitude relative to the previous case (5 hours) indicates lower ionic transportation from the membrane to the electrode and relatively higher ionic transportation into the aqueous medium that causes a slight enhancement of nutrient uptake by the germinated seed. Again, the cyclic voltammogram changes its pattern after 21 hours, which is quite similar to that obtained at 5 hours, exhibits increasing anodic current at the positive end without any oxidative peak, and a broad cathodic peak is noticed in the potential window of +0.00 V < E<+0.15 V with slight shifting in position, indicates ionic nutrients transportation from the membrane to the electrode through diffusion and their lower translocation to the seed through water. This phenomenon is also reflected in reducing Rb of the coated nanofibrous membrane. Again after 24 hours and up to 29 hours, the anodic current at the positive end gradually decreases; also, a successive reduction in the cathodic peak amplitude is seen. This phenomenon can be denoted by a decreasing nutrient transportation rate from the membrane to the electrode and enhancement of their movement to the up-growing seedling through the water. After 45 hours, a prominent cathodic peak is seen in the potential window of +0.05 V < E<+0.05 V with an increment in peak amplitude as compared to that at 29 hours, the anodic current at the positive end also slightly enhanced, signifying further slow release of nutrient ions from the membrane and their accumulation to the electrode surface, which after 48 hours slightly decrease again, denoting an ionic transportation to the growing seedling and lowering the ionic movement from the membrane to the electrode. After 53 hours, the anodic current at the positive end is continually in decreasing order, and also a prominent cathodic peak is found but with a lower amplitude. It hints about the transportation of a few nutrient ions across the membrane to the electrode alongside their transfer to the growing seedling through water. However, after 66 hours, the cathodic peak amplitude diminishes significantly with an almost unchanged anodic current at the positive end, which provides an indication of slowing down the nutrient release process from the membrane, and a continuous transfer of ionic nutrients to the seedling through the water. Even the similar tendency is seen after 69 hours. Next, no more cathodic or anodic peaks are found, the anodic current at the positive end successively increases, but the enclosed area of cyclic voltammograms gradually reduces until 77 hours. The reducing CV curve area denotes the decrease in the specific charge of the electrode,34 indicating the lowering accumulation of ionic species (nutrients) after releasing from the membrane to the electrode surface, and their rapid transportation to the water during nutrient uptake by plants. Once again, at 79 hours, the anodic current at the positive end and cathodic current at the negative end decrease slightly with slightly enhancement of CV curve area. This phenomenon can be defined by a slower nutrient transportation rate from the membrane to the electrode and an accumulation of ionic nutrients on the electrode surface, causing a slight decline of ionic transportation of nutrients to the up-growing seedling through the water. After 82 hours, the CV curve area decreases with further increasing the anodic current at the positive end point, signifies the ionic transportation from the membrane to the electrode, but lowering the accumulation of ionic species to the electrode surface due to rapid translocation to the seedling via water. A similar pattern of the cyclic voltammogram is noticed after 96 hours and also at 98 hours. The pattern does not change significantly in the time intervals of 82 hours to 98 hours, indicating the same trend as at 82 hours. Then, at 99 hours, the anodic current at the positive end and the cathodic current at the negative end are decreased remarkably, specifying a process of slowing down the nutrient release from the membrane to the electrode surface. Again, both the anodic and cathodic currents at the positive and negative ends increase suddenly at 100 hours, causing further release of nutrients from the membrane, which decreases again at 104 hours and exhibits the same phenomenon as seen at 99 hours. After that, at 108 hours and after 120 hours, further decreases in both the anodic and cathodic currents reflecting the deficiency of ionic species on the electrode surface. The most likely CV patterns after 108 hours and 120 hours indicate no more nutrients that remain left in the membrane for further release.

Thus, the concentration of the electroactive nutrient species at the electrodes varies over time indicating a slow and qualitative and comparative indicator control release of nutrients from the nutrient-loaded electrospun nanofibrous seed coating membrane.

A limitation of the present electrochemical analysis is that bulk resistance (Rb), rather than the individually resolved charge-transfer resistance (Rct) and solution resistance (Rs), was used as the primary impedance metric, since the acquired spectra were not fitted to a full Randles equivalent circuit. Similarly, the diffusion coefficient of the dominant electroactive species was not calculated, as this requires either frequency-resolved Warburg analysis or a scan-rate-dependent CV series (via the Randles–Ševčić equation), neither of which was performed in the present study. Future work incorporating equivalent-circuit fitting of the impedance spectra and scan-rate-dependent voltammetry would allow Rct and D to be resolved directly, providing a more mechanistically resolved picture of ion transport at the seed–coating interface than the aggregate Rb metric used here.

Correlations between electrochemical signals and nutrient concentration

Bulk resistance (Rb) values obtained from the Nyquist fits reported in Fig. 8 were used to derive a dimensionless proxy for the relative concentration of mobile, electroactive nutrient ions in the medium surrounding the microelectrode. For a dilute electrolyte, ionic conductivity is approximately proportional to ion concentration; the reciprocal of bulk resistance, G(t) = 1/Rb(t), was therefore taken as a conductance-based proxy for ionic concentration at time t. A relative ion-depletion index was defined asD(t) = 1 − [G(t)/G(tref)]where tref denotes the reference time point marking the transition from the oscillatory pre-germination phase to the monotonic germination/growth phase (t = 29 h; Section 3.3.2). D(t) was subsequently offset so that its minimum value over the growth-phase window equalled zero, removing a small negative excursion introduced by a transient conductance increase at t = 45 h.

The time-dependent depletion index was fitted to a first-order kinetic model,D(t) = Dmax × [1 − exp(−k(t − t0))]Using nonlinear least-squares regression (Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm; SciPy v1.x, Python 3), where Dmax is the fitted asymptotic depletion index, k is the apparent first-order rate constant (h−1), and t0 is a fitted onset/lag parameter (h). Goodness of fit was assessed by the coefficient of determination, R2 = 1 − (SSres/SStot). Data from the pre-germination window (0–29 h), during which Rb exhibited non-monotonic oscillation attributable to alternating swelling-driven imbibition and discrete nutrient-ion release (Section 3.3.2), were excluded from the kinetic fit and are presented separately for reference.

Electrochemical depletion kinetics

Digitized Rb values (Fig. 8) exhibited two kinetically distinct regimes. Between 0 and 29 h, Rb fluctuated irregularly between 754.9 Ω and 2631.8 Ω, consistent with the alternating imbibition–release cycles described qualitatively in Section 3.3.2. From 29 h onward, Rb increased in an essentially monotonic manner, from 2631.8 Ω at 29 h to a maximum of 23361.8 Ω, a value reached by 108 h and sustained unchanged through 120 h an approximately 8.9-fold increase in bulk resistance over the growth-phase window.

The corresponding nutrient depletion (Fig. 12a) index D(t), derived from the reciprocal transformation of these Rb values, increased from 0 at 29 h to 0.98 by 108 h (Fig. 12b). Nonlinear regression against the first-order model returned Dmax = 4.20, k = 3.68 × 10−3 h−1, t_0 = 36.0 h, with R2 = 0.927 (n = 19 time points, 29–120 h). The fitted onset parameter, t0 ≈ 36 h, coincides closely with the period identified independently (Section 3.3.5) as the point at which ion uptake by the germinating seed begins to dominate over ion release from the membrane, providing an internally consistent cross-check between the kinetic model and the qualitative impedance/voltammetry interpretation already given for this stage.

Derivation of an electrochemical (a) nutrient-depletion index and (b) kinetic modeling.

The transformation of Rb into a conductance-based depletion-kinetics index (Fig. 12b) converts the previously descriptive impedance narrative into a quantity that can be fitted, compared, and reported with an explicit goodness-of-fit statistic. The R2 = 0.927 obtained here indicates that a single first-order process captures the great majority of the variance in the growth-phase electrochemical signal, supporting the interpretation that nutrient-ion depletion from the electrolyte proceeds as an apparent first-order process once active seedling uptake begins, rather than as a continuously fluctuating or stochastic phenomenon.

Two points constrain how far this result can be pushed mechanistically. First, Dmax = 4.20 is a fitted mathematical asymptote, not an independently measured maximal depletion capacity; because the observed depletion index reaches only 0.98 by 120 h well below Dmax the fit implies that the system had not yet approached its true asymptotic endpoint within the observation window. This sits in some tension with the plateau in raw Rb between 108 and 120 h (identical values at both time points), which is more readily read as the system reaching a measurement or ionic-exhaustion limit. This is a known identifiability limitation of three-parameter exponential models fitted to data that only partially approach saturation: Dmax and k trade off against one another, and a longer observation window (or a constrained fit with Dmax fixed at the empirically observed maximum) would resolve the ambiguity the constrained alternative is reported in the accompanying workbook for comparison and yields a lower but still informative R2 (0.82).

Second, D(t) is explicitly a relative, unitless proxy for ionic strength near the electrode, derived from Rb under the dilute-solution approximation that conductivity scales linearly with concentration. It has not been calibrated against an independently measured nutrient concentration (e.g., via ICP-OES, ion-selective electrode, or ion chromatography of the surrounding medium), and the manuscript should not represent it as equivalent to an absolute release percentage. Framed this way, the kinetic parameters (k, t0, R2) quantify the electrochemical signal's own kinetics which is what the manuscript's title and framing require while stopping short of the stronger claim that these kinetics are numerically identical to the underlying chemical release kinetics, a distinction the conclusion should preserve.

Within these bounds, the analysis nonetheless strengthens the manuscript's central claim: it demonstrates that the electrochemical response to nutrient release and uptake is not merely directionally consistent with germination and growth timing (Fig. 4–6) but can be described by an explicit kinetic model with a quantified rate constant and fit quality, directly answering the reviewer's request for a quantitative, rather than purely descriptive, treatment of the impedance data.

Electrochemical behaviour during seed germination

During the germination phase, the electrochemical signals exhibit dynamic changes corresponding to nutrient uptake by the emerging seedling. Nyquist plots (Fig. 8b) recorded between 18 and 29 hours show alternating increases and decreases in bulk resistance. The increase in resistance observed at approximately 18 hours indicates enhanced ion uptake by the germinating seed, which reduces the concentration of mobile ions in the surrounding electrolyte. Subsequent decreases in resistance reflect continued release of nutrients from the membrane, which replenishes the ionic concentration in solution. Cyclic voltammetry measurements (Fig. 9b) show a gradual reduction37 in peak currents over time. This decrease indicates a progressive depletion of electroactive ions near the electrode due to transport toward the growing seedling. The diminishing electrochemical signals therefore provide direct evidence of nutrient uptake by the developing plant.

Electrochemical response during early seedling development

The electrochemical response during seedling development (45–120 hours) reveals a gradual stabilization of ionic transport processes. Impedance measurements show minor fluctuations in bulk resistance (Fig. 8f) during the early growth stage, indicating intermittent ionic flux from the membrane. As the seedling continues to develop, resistance gradually increases due to the progressive depletion of mobile ions within the membrane matrix. By approximately 120 hours, the impedance reaches a stable maximum value, suggesting that most of the electrochemically active nutrient ions have been released and consumed by the growing seedling. Consistent with the impedance results, cyclic voltammetry signals exhibit a gradual decrease in both anodic and cathodic peak currents. Eventually, the voltammetric peaks nearly disappear, confirming the exhaustion of electroactive nutrient species within the system.

Mechanistic correlation between electrochemical response and nutrient uptake dynamics

The electrochemical measurements provide direct insight into the mechanism of nutrient ion transport from the nanofibrous membrane to the germinating seed. Variations in impedance and voltammetric responses reflect the dynamic balance between ion diffusion from the polymer matrix, accumulation in the surrounding electrolyte, and subsequent uptake by the developing plant system.

During the initial hydration stage, rapid water infiltration into the porous nanofibrous network promotes dissolution of encapsulated nutrient salts and initiates diffusion-driven ion release. This process generates an increase in ionic conductivity in the surrounding medium, which is reflected in the electrochemical signals obtained from impedance and cyclic voltammetry measurements. As germination progresses, the developing seed begins to actively absorb nutrient ions. The electrochemical data indicate a gradual decrease in the concentration of mobile ionic species near the electrode surface, suggesting that ion transport from the membrane becomes coupled with biological uptake processes. The observed fluctuations in bulk resistance during this stage represent the dynamic equilibrium between nutrient release from the coating and ion absorption by the germinating seed. During the later stages of seedling development, the electrochemical signals progressively diminish, indicating depletion of electroactive ionic species within the membrane and surrounding electrolyte. The stabilization of impedance values and reduction of voltammetric peak currents confirm that nutrient delivery from the nanofibrous coating has largely been completed.

The electrochemical observations correlate well with the biological outcomes of improved germination, enhanced seedling growth, and increased biomass accumulation (Fig. 4–6). This correlation demonstrates that electrochemical monitoring can effectively capture the consistent with a diffusion-influenced transport character, based on impedance response shape and transport of nutrient ions during germination, providing a qualitative and comparative indicator approach for evaluating the performance of polymeric seed-coating delivery systems. Identifying a rigorous sense requires quantitative release-kinetic modeling (e.g., Warburg coefficient extraction, ICP-based nutrient quantification fitted to Higuchi or Korsmeyer–Peppas models), which has been identified as an important direction for future work.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates an electrochemical platform for time-resolved monitoring of consistent with a diffusion-influenced transport character, based on impedance response shape of nutrient ions from an electrospun PVA–PVP nanofibrous seed-coating membrane during germination of Vigna mungo. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy and cyclic voltammetry reveal dynamic variations in ionic transport, interfacial charge transfer, and ion depletion associated with sequential stages of nutrient release and plant uptake. The electrochemical responses correlate directly with enhanced germination and seedling growth, confirming controlled nutrient delivery from the nanofibrous matrix. These findings establish electrochemical sensing as a qualitative and comparative indicator approach for investigating ion transport mechanisms in polymer-based seed coatings and provide a foundation for designing intelligent agro-membrane systems for precision nutrient management.
