DNMT1 promotes the malignant progression of colorectal cancer by silencing HOXD11 via promoter hypermethylation
Authors: Jing Wang, Ran Xiang, Chunhua He, Fuye Li, Meng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is a prevalent malignancy worldwide, and epigenetic modifications, particularly methylation of key genes, play a crucial role in its initiation and progression. DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) is aberrantly overexpressed in CRC; however, its specific mechanisms of action and downstream targets remain unclear. This study aims to elucidate how DNMT1 epigenetically regulates HOXD11 expression and subsequently influences CRC progression.MethodsThe expression and methylation status of HOXD11 in 30 pairs of CRC tissues and adjacent normal tissues were detected by RT-qPCR, Western Blot, and methylation-specific PCR (MSP). HOXD11 overexpression assays were performed in CRC cell lines, and cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis were evaluated using CCK-8, colony formation, wound healing, Transwell assays, and flow cytometry. To identify the key enzyme regulating HOXD11, we conducted specific shRNA knockdown screening of DNA methyltransferase family members (DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B). The regulatory effect of DNMT1 on HOXD11 was then verified using the demethylating agent 5-AzaC. Finally, a nude mouse subcutaneous xenograft model was established to validate the therapeutic potential of targeting the DNMT1/HOXD11 axis in vivo.ResultsHOXD11 was significantly downregulated in CRC tissues, accompanied by promoter hypermethylation. Overexpression of HOXD11 markedly inhibited CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while inducing apoptosis. Mechanistic studies revealed that among the three DNMTs screened, only DNMT1 knockdown significantly upregulated HOXD11 expression, confirming DNMT1 as the specific methyltransferase mediating HOXD11 promoter hypermethylation and transcriptional silencing. Treatment with 5-AzaC restored HOXD11 expression and suppressed malignant phenotypes, whereas simultaneous knockdown of HOXD11 reversed these tumor-suppressive effects. In vivo experiments further confirmed that targeted inhibition of DNMT1 significantly curbed tumor growth by upregulating HOXD11.ConclusionThis study reveals that DNMT1-mediated promoter hypermethylation of HOXD11 is a critical mechanism driving the malignant progression of CRC. The DNMT1/HOXD11 axis may serve as a potential therapeutic target of CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05248-x.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a major public health concern worldwide [1, 2]. According to the GLOBOCAN 2022 report, CRC ranks third in incidence and second in mortality among all cancers, claiming over 900,000 lives in that year alone and posing a severe threat to human health [3, 4]. Despite significant advances in comprehensive therapeutic strategies, including surgery, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, the overall prognosis for patients remains dismal, largely because a substantial proportion of cases are diagnosed at advanced stages accompanied by local invasion and distant metastasis [5–7]. Consequently, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying CRC initiation and progression, and discovering more effective therapeutic targets are imperative for improving patient survival and clinical outcomes.

The initiation and progression of CRC are a complex, multi-step process driven not only by classical genetic mutations but also by epigenetic dysregulation [8]. DNA methylation, as the most thoroughly studied epigenetic modification, refers to the process in which methyl groups (CH3) are added to DNA molecules under the catalysis of DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) [9]. Aberrant promoter hypermethylation can effectively regulate the transcription of downstream genes, thereby propelling disease initiation and progression [10, 11]. In the context of tumorigenesis, specific DNA methylation patterns ensure the stable inheritance of silenced tumor suppressor genes, thus perpetuating the malignant phenotype [12]. DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) serves as the key maintenance methyltransferase, primarily responsible for preserving established methylation patterns during DNA replication [13]. Accumulating evidence indicates that DNMT1 is aberrantly overexpressed in various malignancies, including CRC, and its overexpression is closely associated with tumor aggressiveness, poor prognosis, and chemotherapy resistance [14–16]. However, despite existing studies on DNMT1 in CRC, its specific mechanism by which it regulates methylation within CRC remains to be fully explored.

HOXD11, a member of the homeobox (HOX) gene family, has been confirmed to be aberrantly overexpressed in various tumors, such as glioma [17], ovarian cancer [18], and squamous cell carcinoma [19]. Studies have shown that HOXD11 can not only activate downstream pathways to promote cell invasion and metastasis, but its expression levels are also closely correlated with advanced clinical stages and poor prognosis [20, 21]. Furthermore, the expression of HOXD11 is regulated by DNA methylation, although the outcomes vary depending on the cancer type and methylation site: it is silenced by promoter methylation in ovarian cancer [22], whereas it is activated by 3’ UTR methylation in oral cancer [23]. Despite existing research on HOXD11 in other malignancies, whether it is regulated by DNA methylation in the context of CRC and subsequently influences tumor progression remains unknown.

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying DNMT1 and HOXD11 in CRC progression, we first elucidated the expression pattern and methylation status of HOXD11 in CRC by analyzing clinical tissue samples. Subsequently, we evaluated its effects on malignant behaviors such as CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion through functional assays. We further explored the upstream epigenetic mechanisms regulating HOXD11 expression and identified DNMT1 as the key DNA methyltransferase involved. Finally, we assessed the therapeutic potential of targeting the DNMT1/HOXD11 axis using both in vitro and in vivo animal models. Our study establishes DNMT1-HOXD11 as a novel and critical regulatory axis in CRC, providing a theoretical framework for the prevention and treatment of CRC.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples and ethics statement

This study collected tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues from 30 patients with CRC who underwent surgical resection at the Hospital of The Affiliated Tumour Hospital Xinjiang Medical university. All samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after excision and subsequently stored at -80 °C until further use. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Tumour Hospital Xinjiang Medical university (Approval No. S-2024091), and written informed consent was obtained from all participating patients.

Cell lines and cell culture

The human normal colonic epithelial cell line NCM-460 and CRC cell lines HCT116, SW480, SW620, and RKO were all purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Plasmid construction, shRNA, cell transfection, and drug treatment

The pCMV vectors for overexpressing HOXD11 and DNMT1, as well as short hairpin RNA (shRNA) plasmids for knocking down HOXD11, DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B, along with their negative controls (sh-NC), were designed and synthesized by RiboBio. Cell transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen) strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection, and the efficiency of overexpression or knockdown was verified by RT-qPCR and Western Blot for subsequent experiments. For drug treatment, cells were treated with the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor 5-Azacytidine (5-AzaC; A1287, Sigma-Aldrich) at a concentration of 5 µM for 48 h [24].

Cell treatment and grouping

For HOXD11 functional validation experiments:

OE-NC Group: Cells were transfected with the pCMV empty vector (pCMV-NC).

OE-HOXD11 Group: Cells were transfected with the pCMV-HOXD11 overexpression vector.

For methylation regulation and DNMTs screening experiments:

Demethylation treatment:

Control Group: Cells were treated with DMSO as a control.

5-AzaC Group: Cells were treated with the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor 5-AzaC.

DNMTs knockdown screening:

Cells were divided into four groups: sh-NC, sh-DNMT1, sh-DNMT3A, and sh-DNMT3B, transfected with their respective shRNA plasmids.

DNMT1 functional validation:

OE-NC Group: Transfected with pCMV-NC empty vector.

OE-DNMT1 Group: Transfected with pCMV-DNMT1 overexpression vector.

For targeting the DNMT1-HOXD11 axis experiments:

sh-NC Group: Cells were transfected with negative control shRNA (sh-NC) and treated with DMSO vehicle control.

5-AzaC Group: Cells were transfected with sh-NC and treated with 5-AzaC.

sh-HOXD11 Group: Cells were transfected with sh-HOXD11 and treated with DMSO vehicle control.

5-AzaC + sh-HOXD11 Group: Cells were transfected with sh-HOXD11 and treated with 5-AzaC.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA from cultured cells and periodontal tissues was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). Before extraction, tissue samples were mechanically homogenized in Trizol using a homogenizer. For all samples, cDNA was subsequently synthesized using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems). Gene expression was quantified by qRT-PCR using TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with gene specific primers (Table S1). The relative expression levels were determined using the comparative Ct (2-ΔΔCt) method, with GAPDH as the endogenous control [25].

Western blot

Cells or tissue samples were lysed on ice using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) containing a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 1 h, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody against HOXD11 (18734-1-AP, Proteintech). Following washing, the membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Protein bands were visualized using an Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL) kit, with GAPDH (AF3051, Beyotime) serving as the loading control [26].

Methylation-specific PCR (MSP)

Genomic DNA was extracted from tissues or cells using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen). Subsequently, 500 ng of DNA was subjected to bisulfite treatment using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (Zymo Research). The treated DNA served as a template for PCR amplification using specific primers targeting methylated (M) and unmethylated (U) regions of the HOXD11 promoter. PCR products were electrophoresed on a 2% agarose gel and visualized using a gel imaging system. The band intensity was measured by densitometry using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The methylation level was calculated as the density of the methylated band divided by the sum of the methylated and unmethylated bands. Specific primer sequences are detailed in Table S2 and Table S3.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

The binding of DNMT1 to the HOXD11 promoter region in SW480 cells was detected by the ChIP technique. The experiment was conducted in accordance with the standard operating procedure of the ChIP Assay Kit (#P2078) of Beyotime. First, cells were collected and lysed, and then chromatin was broken into fragments of 400–800 bp through ultrasonic treatment. The samples were divided into two groups. Rabbit DNMT1 antibody (1:500, #24206-1-AP, Proteintech) or rabbit IgG antibody (1:1000, #3000-0-AP, Proteintech) were added to the respective groups and incubated overnight on a shaker at 4°C. The next day, 60µl of A + G Agarose/Salmon Sperm DNA was added to precipitate the protein-DNA complex bound to DNMT1. Finally, specific primers were used: upstream primer 5’-GATCCGCCCCCAGCG-3’ and downstream primer 5’-TCTATATCAACGCTCGCCAAC-3’. The HOXD11 promoter region was amplified by real-time quantitative PCR, producing a 71 bp product to evaluate the enrichment of DNMT1 in this region.

CCK-8

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; CA1210, Solarbio) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates. At 0, 24, 48, and 72 h of culture, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to calculate cell viability.

Colony formation assay

Cells were digested into a single-cell suspension and seeded into 6-well plates. Fresh medium was replaced every 3 days, and cells were cultured for 2 weeks. After discarding the medium, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. Colonies were counted under a microscope after washing and drying.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured until 90%-100% confluence. A linear scratch was created in the center of the cell monolayer using a pipette tip. Detached cells were washed away with PBS, and the medium was replaced with serum-free medium. Images were captured at 0 and 48 h using an inverted microscope at the same position. Wound healing rates were calculated using ImageJ software.

Transwell invasion assay

To assess cell invasion, Transwell inserts were coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to form a gel. Cells resuspended in serum-free medium were added to the upper compartment, while the lower compartment contained complete medium with 10% FBS. Forty-eight hours later, non-migratory cells were removed using cotton swabs. The invaded cells on the lower surface were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and captured using microscopy.

Flow cytometry

To assess the rate of cell apoptosis, we performed flow cytometry analysis using an Annexin V-FITC/Propidium Iodide (PI) Apoptosis Detection Kit (BL110A, Amresco). Following treatment, cells were harvested, washed, and stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI for 15 min at room temperature in the dark according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were immediately analyzed on a flow cytometer. The total apoptosis rate was defined as the combined percentage of the early apoptotic (Annexin V+/PI−) and late apoptotic/necrotic (Annexin V+/PI+) cell fractions.

Tumor xenograft experiments in nude mice

All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Medical university (Approval No. IACUC-20240522-22). Twenty-four female BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks old, 18–20 g) were purchased from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center, China and housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions (24 ± 1 °C, 50%±5% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. After one week of acclimatization, 5 × 106 treated CRC cells were resuspended in 100 µL of PBS: Matrigel (1:1) mixture and subcutaneously injected into the right axilla. When tumors reached an average diameter of ~ 0.5 cm, mice were randomized into four groups (n = 6/group):

Control Group: Injected with sh-NC cells; treated with saline (i.p., twice weekly).

5-AzaC Group: Injected with sh-NC cells; treated with 5-AzaC (4 mg/kg, i.p., twice weekly).

sh-HOXD11 Group: Injected with sh-HOXD11 cells; treated with saline (i.p., twice weekly).

Combination Group: Injected with sh-HOXD11 cells; treated with 5-AzaC (4 mg/kg, i.p., twice weekly).

Tumor volume was measured every 3 days. Treatment continued until day 27 post-inoculation, at which point mice were sacrificed. Tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed. In accordance with the approved protocol, the maximal permitted tumor volume was 2,000 mm³. Throughout the study, all tumor-bearing animals were monitored closely, and none exceeded this limit (the maximum observed volume was 1,440 mm³).Portions of tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

H&E staining

Tissues fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 5 μm. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed using a commercial kit (ServiceBio, G1005) according to standard procedures. Stained slides were then dehydrated, cleared with xylene, and visualized by microscopy.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tumor tissue Sect. (5 μm) underwent deparaffinization, rehydration, and citrate buffer-mediated antigen retrieval. After quenching endogenous peroxidases with 3% H2O2 and blocking with goat serum, slides were probed overnight at 4 °C with Ki67(27309-1-AP, Proteintech) or Caspase-3 (HY-P72874, MCE) antibodies. Detection was achieved using an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and DAB substrate. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin prior to microscopic analysis.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) derived from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.4 software. For comparisons between two groups, the unpaired Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons of paired clinical samples (CRC tissues vs. adjacent normal tissues), the paired Student’s t-test was employed. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons was utilized. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001).

Results

HOXD11 was significantly downregulated in CRC tissues and accompanied by promoter hypermethylation

To investigate the expression and promoter methylation status of HOXD11 in CRC, we analyzed 30 pairs of CRC tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues. RT-qPCR results revealed that HOXD11 mRNA levels were significantly downregulated in CRC tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, Western Blot analysis confirmed that HOXD11 protein levels were significantly lower in tumor tissues than in normal tissues (Fig. 1B). Further MSP indicated that the HOXD11 gene promoter was predominantly hypermethylated in CRC tissues, whereas it remained largely unmethylated in adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1C). Collectively, these data indicated that HOXD11 expression is downregulated in CRC, which may be associated with hypermethylation of its promoter region.

The influence of HOXD11 on the functions of CRC cells

To investigate the functional role of HOXD11 in CRC cells, we first examined its expression levels in various CRC cell lines. Compared to the normal colonic epithelial cell line NCM-460, HOXD11 was significantly downregulated in multiple CRC cell lines (SW480, HCT-116, SW620, RKO) (Fig. 2A). SW480 and HCT-116 cells were selected for the subsequent experiments, and the overexpression efficiency of HOXD11 was verified by RT-qPCR and Western Blot (Fig. 2B, C).

Further functional assays revealed that HOXD11 overexpression significantly inhibited CRC cell viability and proliferation (Fig. 2D, E). Additionally, it reduced cell migration and invasion capabilities while promoting apoptosis (Fig. 2F–H). In summary, HOXD11, as a tumor suppressor factor, impaired the functions of CRC cells (Fig. 3).

DNMT1 specifically mediated HOXD11 promoter hypermethylation and inhibited its expression

To explore whether the changes in HOXD11 mRNA levels are regulated by DNA methylation of its promoter region, we first treated CRC cells (SW480 and HCT-116) with the DNMT inhibitor 5-AzaC. The results showed that 5-AzaC treatment significantly reduced the methylation level of the HOXD11 promoter, accompanied by an upregulation of HOXD11 expression (Fig. 4A–C).

Knockdown of HOXD11 reversed the inhibitory effects of 5-AzaC on the malignant phenotypes of CRC cells

To investigate whether DNMT1 affects CRC cell function by regulating HOXD11, we treated CRC cells with 5-Azacytidine (5-AzaC) and performed HOXD11 knockdown. The results showed that HOXD11 knockdown significantly reduced HOXD11 expression levels, while 5-AzaC treatment significantly increased HOXD11 levels. Importantly, combined treatment with 5-AzaC and HOXD11 knockdown partially reversed the effect of 5-AzaC on HOXD11 expression levels (Fig. 6A, B).

Further functional assays showed that 5-AzaC treatment significantly inhibited CRC cell viability and proliferation, reduced cell migration and invasion, and increased apoptosis. However, subsequent knockdown of HOXD11 in 5-AzaC-treated cells partially reversed the effects of 5-AzaC on cell function (Fig. 6C–G). These results indicated that HOXD11 knockdown can reverse the inhibitory effects of 5-AzaC on the malignant phenotypes of CRC cells (Fig. 7).

Targeting the DNMT1/HOXD11 axis inhibited CRC xenograft growth

To investigate the impact of targeting the DNMT1-HOXD11 axis on the pathological process of CRC in mice, we established a nude mouse subcutaneous xenograft model. The results showed that compared with the control group, 5-AzaC treatment inhibited tumor growth, whereas HOXD11 knockdown alone significantly promoted tumor growth. Importantly, the combination of 5-AzaC and HOXD11 knockdown reversed the tumor-suppressive effect of 5-AzaC (Fig. 8A–D).

H&E staining results indicated that 5-AzaC treatment led to cell necrosis and tissue disorganization (nuclear shrinkage and fragmentation) in tumor tissues, while the combination treatment reversed the effects of 5-AzaC on tumor tissue morphology (Fig. 8E). Further immunohistochemical analysis showed that 5-AzaC treatment significantly decreased Ki67 expression and increased Caspase-3 expression levels, whereas HOXD11 knockdown reversed the effects of 5-AzaC on Ki67 and Caspase-3 expression (Fig. 8F). Additionally, HOXD11 knockdown reversed the upregulation of HOXD11 protein levels induced by 5-AzaC (Fig. 8G). These results indicated that targeting the DNMT1-HOXD11 axis can ameliorate the pathological progression of CRC in mice.

Discussion

CRC is a malignancy with high global incidence and mortality rates [27, 28]. Epigenetic dysregulation, particularly aberrant DNA methylation, is an important factor driving the malignant progression of CRC and is closely associated with disease severity and prognosis [29, 30]. Although DNA methylation is closely linked to CRC, the specific molecular mechanisms remain to be elucidated. Our study demonstrated that in CRC, DNMT1 specifically mediated the hypermethylation of the promoter region of the tumor suppressor gene HOXD11, thereby inhibiting its transcription. The loss of HOXD11 expression further enhanced CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities, ultimately exacerbating the malignant progression of CRC. This study provides a new perspective for understanding the epigenetic regulatory mechanisms of CRC and offers potential molecular targets and a theoretical basis for the development of clinical prevention and treatment strategies.

Through the analysis of clinical samples, this study reveals that HOXD11 is significantly downregulated in CRC tissues, accompanied by aberrant hypermethylation in its promoter region. The HOX gene family not only plays a crucial role in embryonic development and cell differentiation, dysregulation of which is also closely associated with the initiation and progression of various malignant tumors [31]. HOXD11, a member of the HOX gene family, has been reported in current literature to act as an oncogene in multiple malignancies. For instance, in prostate cancer [32], laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma [33], and hemangioblastoma [34], high expression of HOXD11 has been confirmed to drive tumor cell invasion and metastasis by activating downstream signaling pathways such as the FN1/MMP2/MMP9 axis [19]. Although progress has been made in understanding HOXD11 in various solid tumors, relevant research in CRC remains lacking. Our study indicates that in CRC, HOXD11 functions as a key tumor suppressor gene whose expression is regulated by DNA methylation; HOXD11 significantly curbs the malignant phenotypes of cancer cells, inhibiting CRC proliferation, migration, and invasion, and inducing apoptosis.

To elucidate the upstream mechanism underlying HOXD11 downregulation in CRC, we focused on DNA methylation. DNA methylation, a key epigenetic modification catalyzed by DNMTs, involves the addition of methyl groups to CpG dinucleotides [35]. In CRC, global hypomethylation coexists with site-specific promoter hypermethylation, which typically represses gene transcription [36, 37]. This aberrant hypermethylation is a critical driver of CRC progression, influencing cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, and metastasis [38, 39]. Our study reveals that the HOXD11 promoter is hypermethylated in CRC tissues, identifying this epigenetic alteration as a cause of its reduced expression.

Further investigation revealed that DNMT1 exerts its oncogenic role in CRC by orchestrating the epigenetic silencing of HOXD11. As the primary maintenance DNA methyltransferase, DNMT1 is crucial for epigenetic reprogramming, faithfully propagating methylation patterns during DNA replication to maintain long-term gene silencing [40]. Consistent with its critical role, a recent study demonstrated that targeting DNMT1 for degradation can induce cellular senescence in CRC via the CDK2/p53 axis [41]. While DNMT1 primarily maintains existing methylation patterns to repress targets like HOXD11, DNMT3A and DNMT3B are typically associated with de novo methylation. Our findings reinforce that in CRC, the specific overexpression of DNMT1, rather than just global DNMT activity, is a fundamental driver of the malignant phenotype through the silencing of homeobox genes. Aberrant overexpression of DNMT1 is a hallmark of CRC progression, driving malignancy through diverse mechanisms. For instance, DNMT1-mediated transcriptional repression of DYRK2 has been shown to promote colorectal tumorigenesis, while its hypermethylation of NR3C1 facilitates angiogenesis during disease progression [42, 43]. Furthermore, miR-887-3p inhibits CRC progression by downregulating DNMT1 and subsequently modulating p53 expression [44]. Previous studies have shown that DNMT1 is essential for tumor survival, as its depletion blocks tumorigenesis even in the presence of potent oncogenic drivers [45]. In this study, we identified HOXD11 as a key downstream target of DNMT1 in CRC. Mechanistically, DNMT1 directly binds to the HOXD11 promoter, inducing hypermethylation and transcriptional repression. Functionally, DNMT1-mediated HOXD11 suppression significantly enhances CRC cell proliferation and invasion. Importantly, in nude mouse xenograft models, both 5-AzaC treatment and specific restoration of HOXD11 expression significantly curbed tumor growth. This study establishes the DNMT1/HOXD11 axis as a critical driver of CRC progression, offering a novel therapeutic strategy.

Limitation

Despite these findings, our study has several limitations. First, the sample size of clinical tissues (n = 30) is relatively small, and we were unable to perform extensive bioinformatics analysis on public datasets to validate the prognostic value. Second, the in vivo experiments relied on pharmacological inhibition and shRNA rescue rather than specific genetic knockdown of DNMT1. Future studies utilizing ChIP-seq and transgenic mouse models are warranted to further validate the direct regulatory mechanism.

Conclusion

In conclusion, through clinical sample analysis, in vitro and in vivo functional validation, and mechanistic exploration, this study demonstrates that DNMT1-mediated promoter hypermethylation of HOXD11 is a critical epigenetic mechanism driving the malignant progression of CRC. Specifically, DNMT1 suppresses HOXD11 expression via promoter hypermethylation, thereby enhancing CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while inhibiting apoptosis. Targeting the DNMT1/HOXD11 axis effectively curbs tumor growth. Our findings establish HOXD11 as a potential tumor suppressor gene and prognostic biomarker, providing a solid theoretical basis for developing epigenetic therapeutic strategies for CRC.
