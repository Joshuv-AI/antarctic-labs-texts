Abstract
. Content safety teams need metrics that reflect what users actually experienced, not only what was reported. We study prevalence : the fraction of user views (impressions) that went to content violating a given policy on a given day. Accurate prevalence measurement is challenging because violations are often rare and human labeling is costly, making frequent platform-representative studies slow. We present a design-based measurement system that (i) draws daily probability samples from the impression stream using ML-assisted weights to concentrate label budget on high-exposure and high-risk content while preserving unbiasedness, (ii) labels sampled items with a multimodal LLM governed by policy prompts and gold-set validation, and (iii) produces design-consistent prevalence estimates with confidence intervals and dashboard drill-downs. A key design goal is one global sample with many pivots . A single sample supports prevalence by surface, viewer geography, content age, and other segments. We detail the statistical estimators, variance and CI construction, label-quality monitoring, and an engineering workflow that makes the system configurable across policies. Because absolute prevalence levels for sensitive policies are confidential, we report production evidence primarily in relative terms and include a fully specified synthetic simulation appendix illustrating sampling efficiency.



1.
Introduction

User-generated content platforms need fast, repeatable feedback loops to detect emerging harms and evaluate mitigations.
Operationally, metric refresh must be frequent enough to surface new patterns, support rapid root-cause analysis, and measure whether interventions and product changes move safety outcomes in the intended direction.

However, a reports-only view is incomplete since many harms are under-reported, some users do not report content they seek, and rare categories yield too few reports for statistically powered monitoring.

Prevalence
complements reporting by estimating the share of user experiences exposed to policy-violating content, enabling guardrails, trend monitoring, goal-setting, and intervention evaluation
(
3
)
.
Because prevalence is computed from probability samples, it also supports drill-downs that help teams localize changes (e.g., by surface, geography, or content age) without re-running bespoke studies.

Historically, prevalence studies relied on human review and were conducted infrequently due to significant cost and latency. Recent multimodal LLMs fundamentally shift this operating point: by bulk-labeling samples with an LLM using subject-matter-expert (SME) reviewed prompts and ongoing gold-set validation, we reduce end-to-end labeling latency by
\sim
15
\times
(relative to a human-only review workflow) and operational cost by
>10\times
at comparable decision quality. This efficiency makes daily, platform-representative measurement feasible at scale
(
3
)
.

This paper describes an approach that enables daily measurement by combining (i) ML-assisted probability sampling from impression logs and (ii) LLM-assisted labeling with ongoing decision-quality monitoring.
While production enforcement models provide auxiliary risk scores, they are not used as labels and do not gate inclusion; design-based reweighting removes sampling “lensing” so estimates remain comparable even as enforcement thresholds evolve.

In production at Pinterest, we run this pipeline daily (for over a year) across multiple policy areas, supporting daily monitoring, OKR-style goal tracking, and automated alerting. Each run publishes prevalence estimates with 95% CIs and drill-down dashboards, and pages an on-call owner when weekly shifts exceed policy-specific minimum detectable effect (MDE) thresholds (§
11
).
Because absolute prevalence levels for sensitive policies are confidential, we report production evidence primarily in relative terms (e.g., sampling-efficiency lift and detectable relative changes) and provide a fully specified synthetic simulation appendix (§
A
) to enable methodological verification. Across policies, prevalence correlates weakly with confirmed user reports, indicating it provides complementary visibility beyond reporting.

Contributions.

This work makes three contributions:
(1) a design-consistent estimator for daily prevalence of violative views with
single-sample drill-downs
across many segments;
(2) practical uncertainty quantification (CIs, effective sample size, and optional correction for label error);

Related Work

Probability proportional-to-size (PPS) sampling and the Hansen–Hurwitz estimator provide unbiased (or design-consistent) estimation under with-replacement designs
(
5
)
.
For without-replacement designs, Horvitz–Thompson style estimators and their ratio variants are standard tools

Weighted reservoir sampling provides an efficient streaming implementation of PPSWOR using per-item random keys

.

Recently,

Meta (Facebook) released an open-source implementation (

) that combines auxiliary model scores with probability sampling for estimation
(

.
In the content-safety setting, prevalence-style exposure metrics complement reports by measuring what was actually shown to users and enabling intervention evaluation
(

On the labeling side, there is growing evidence that LLMs can serve as high-quality annotators for certain tasks, often matching or exceeding crowd-worker performance when prompts are well specified

.

3.
Problem and Metric

Let
d
denote a day. The population is the set of content units (e.g., Pins) that received at least one impression on day
d
.
For each content unit
j
we observe its total impressions
C_{jd}\geq 0
from impression logs.
A policy-specific label
Y_{jd}\in\{0,1\}
indicates whether
j
violates the policy (1) or not (0) under the policy definition in effect on day
d
.

We measure exposure-weighted prevalence:

(1)

\theta_{d}\;=\;\frac{\sum_{j}C_{jd}Y_{jd}}{\sum_{j}C_{jd}}.

This definition emphasizes user experience: a single item can be posted once but receive many impressions, or none.

3.1.
Segments and drill-downs

For drill-downs, we define segments
g\in\mathcal{G}
over impressions (e.g., surface=Homefeed/Search, viewer country, content age bucket).
Let
C_{jdg}
be the impressions to content
j
on day
d
attributable to segment
g
, with
\sum_{g}C_{jdg}=C_{jd}
.
Segment prevalence is

(2)

\theta_{dg}\;=\;\frac{\sum_{j}C_{jdg}Y_{jd}}{\sum_{j}C_{jdg}}.

A primary design goal is that a
single global sample
supports all
g
.
We achieve this by (i) sampling content units once per day using global weights, and (ii) storing
\{C_{jdg}\}_{g\in\mathcal{G}}
for sampled items so the estimator can be evaluated for any pivot without re-sampling.

4.
ML-Assisted Probability Sampling

Violations may be rare, so naive random sampling can yield too few positive examples for stable daily estimates.
We therefore use auxiliary signals to allocate label budget more efficiently while preserving unbiasedness.

4.1.
Sampling weights

Each content unit
j
receives an auxiliary risk score
s_{jd}>0
from production safety models.
Missing scores are imputed (e.g., to the day’s median) to keep fresh content in-frame
(
3
)
.
We define a nonnegative weight

(3)

w_{jd}=C_{jd}^{\nu}\,\bigl(s_{jd}^{\gamma}+\epsilon\bigr),\qquad\nu,\gamma\geq 0,\;\epsilon>0.

The tunable exponents control efficiency trade-offs:

\gamma=0
yields impression-weighted sampling;
\gamma=\nu=0
yields (approximately) uniform sampling.
Intuitively,
\gamma>0
shifts probability mass toward model-high-risk items (increasing the sample positive rate),
but can reduce effective sample size when score distributions are spiky; choosing
\nu<1
softens exposure weighting to control weight variance.

We construct a daily sampling distribution over content units:

(4)

p_{jd}=\frac{w_{jd}}{\sum_{k}w_{kd}}.

Crucially,
s_{jd}
is used only to prioritize review and does not become a label; the estimator reweights by
1/p_{jd}
so prevalence reflects impressions, not model thresholds.

4.2.
Efficient implementation via weighted reservoir sampling

Operationally, we need to draw a fixed-size daily sample from a very large stream.
At platform scale (billions of impressions/day and hundreds of millions of users), the population can contain tens of millions of content units; streaming sampling keeps memory
O(m)
and avoids materializing the full population.
We implement weighted reservoir sampling using the Efraimidis–Spirakis key trick
(
2
)
:
for each unit, draw
U_{jd}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1]
and compute

(5)

\kappa_{jd}=-\frac{\log(U_{jd})}{w_{jd}}.

Keeping the
m
smallest keys yields a fixed-size PPSWOR sample (equivalently, the
m
earliest events in an exponential race).
The threshold
\tau_{d}
(the
m
-th smallest key) provides a useful inclusion-probability approximation via
\pi_{jd}\approx 1-\exp(-w_{jd}\tau_{d})
; for small sampling fractions,
\pi_{jd}\approx w_{jd}/\sum_{k}w_{kd}
.
We persist
\tau_{d}
so these approximations (and conservative variance estimators) are recoverable.
For platforms that prefer with-replacement PPS sampling (PPSWR), we also support multinomial draws from (
4
).
Both options admit the estimator; we focus on PPSWR for exposition and note the PPSWOR alternative in §
5
.

5.
Design-Consistent Estimation and Single-Sample Drill-downs

Let the sample on day
d
contain
m
draws
\{J_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
.
For draw
i
, we observe
(C_{J_{i}d},\,Y_{J_{i}d})
and the draw probability
p_{J_{i}d}
.
Define

(6)

x_{id}=C_{J_{i}d},\qquad z_{id}=C_{J_{i}d}Y_{J_{i}d}.

5.1.
Hansen–Hurwitz ratio estimator (PPS with replacement)

For PPSWR, define the HH mean estimators

(7)

\hat{Z}_{d}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{z_{id}}{p_{J_{i}d}},\qquad\hat{X}_{d}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{x_{id}}{p_{J_{i}d}}.

The Hansen–Hurwitz (HH) ratio estimator
(
5
)
is

(8)

\hat{\theta}_{d}=\frac{\hat{Z}_{d}}{\hat{X}_{d}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{z_{id}}{p_{J_{i}d}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{x_{id}}{p_{J_{i}d}}}.

In practice, score drift can show up as wider confidence intervals rather than bias, because the reweighting corrects for the sampling design.

5.2.
Without-replacement alternative

When using weighted reservoir sampling (PPSWOR), we can use a Horvitz–Thompson/Hájek style ratio estimator with inclusion probabilities
\pi_{jd}
.
In practice we approximate
\pi_{jd}
using the threshold
\tau_{d}
from (
5
) (Poissonized approximation) or use variance estimators that do not require exact
\pi_{jd}
; we store
\tau_{d}
alongside the sample.
Empirically, for large populations and moderate
m
, PPSWR and PPSWOR yield similar point estimates, with PPSWOR typically slightly lower variance.

5.3.
Drill-downs from a single sample

For a segment
g
, define per-draw quantities

(9)

x_{idg}=C_{J_{i}dg},\qquad z_{idg}=C_{J_{i}dg}Y_{J_{i}d}.

Two common cases arise:

•

Known segment denominators.
If
D_{dg}=\sum_{j}C_{jdg}
is computed exactly from logs, we estimate only the numerator:

(10)

\widehat{N}_{dg}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{z_{idg}}{p_{J_{i}d}},\qquad\hat{\theta}_{dg}=\widehat{N}_{dg}/D_{dg}.

•

Sample-based denominators.
If
D_{dg}
is not directly available (e.g., ad-hoc pivots), we use the HH ratio form:

(11)

\hat{\theta}_{dg}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{z_{idg}}{p_{J_{i}d}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{x_{idg}}{p_{J_{i}d}}}.

The critical engineering step is persisting
\{C_{jdg}\}
for sampled items (and optionally a compact segment key) so dashboards can compute (
10
) or (
11
) on demand.

6.
Uncertainty Quantification

We report 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to convey precision.
For PPSWR, we use a Taylor linearization for the ratio estimator.
Let

(12)

\hat{X}_{d}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{x_{id}}{p_{J_{i}d}},\qquad\hat{Z}_{d}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{z_{id}}{p_{J_{i}d}}.

Define residuals

(13)

r_{id}=\frac{1}{p_{J_{i}d}}\bigl(z_{id}-\hat{\theta}_{d}x_{id}\bigr).

An approximate variance estimator is

(14)

\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{\theta}_{d})\approx\frac{1}{\hat{X}_{d}^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(r_{id}-\bar{r}_{d}\bigr)^{2},\qquad\bar{r}_{d}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{id}.

We report

(15)

\hat{\theta}_{d}\pm 1.96\,\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{\theta}_{d})}.

The same construction applies to each segment by replacing
(x_{id},z_{id})
with
(x_{idg},z_{idg})
.

6.1.
Effective sample size diagnostics

Highly variable weights can reduce precision.
To communicate this effect to metric owners, dashboards report an effective sample size (ESS) proxy, e.g.

(16)

\mathrm{ESS}_{d}=\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{id}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{id}^{2}},\qquad a_{id}=\frac{x_{id}}{p_{J_{i}d}},

along with the sample positive rate and CI width.
This is the standard Kish effective sample size proxy for weighted estimators.
These operational diagnostics can help tune
(\nu,\gamma)
and label budgets.

6.2.
Optional correction for label error

LLM labels may have nonzero false positive and false negative rates.
If we estimate the labeler sensitivity
r
and false positive rate
f
on a contemporaneous validation set, we can correct the prevalence via Rogan–Gladen
(
8
)
:

(17)

\theta_{d}=\frac{\theta_{d}^{(L)}-f}{r-f},

where
\theta_{d}^{(L)}
is the measured prevalence using the LLM labeler.
We propagate uncertainty by combining sampling variance ((
14
)) with validation uncertainty on
(r,f)
(e.g., delta method or bootstrap).
In production, we prefer to keep the core metric simple and instead monitor
(r,f)
over time to ensure measurement conviction.

7.
LLM Labeling and Decision Quality Monitoring

Given the sampled content IDs, we label each unit with a multimodal LLM using (i) the content (image/video keyframes, title, text metadata), (ii) an SME-reviewed policy description, and (iii) an optimized prompt that returns a structured label and brief rationale.
In our deployment discussed here, we used GPT-4-class multimodal models and have recently switched to current-generation models. Prompts include the policy text plus content identifiers and metadata (e.g., title/description and keyframes), and return a structured JSON-like output (label, reason, confidence).

7.1.
Quality gating and monitoring

Before launching a policy metric, the LLM+prompt configuration must meet a minimum decision-quality bar on a gold set labeled by policy SMEs (ground truth).
After launch, we continuously monitor decision quality using two mechanisms:
(1) a random subsample of daily items routed to human validation, and
(2) periodic re-evaluation on refreshed gold sets to detect drift (policy drift, content shift, or model updates).
This governance mirrors best practices for aligning automated evaluators with human judgment
(
4
;
11
;
6
)
.

In internal evaluations on SME-validated gold sets, the LLM-based labeler achieved accuracy within
\sim
3.2% (relative difference) and an F1 score within
\sim
0.5% (relative difference) of human agent performance. These results demonstrate that the automated pipeline performs on par with human review while providing the scalability required for daily measurement
(
9
)
.

8.1.
Config-driven measurement

A key engineering principle is that prevalence measurement is
configurable
.
Each metric definition specifies:
(1) the target policy taxonomy (including sub-policies),
(2) data sources for impressions and auxiliary scores,
(3) sampling parameters
(m,\nu,\gamma,\epsilon)
,
(4) the LLM prompt and model identifiers,
(5) required gold sets and quality thresholds,
and (6) output schemas and dashboards.
This abstraction allows teams to stand up new policy measurements quickly.

8.2.
Lineage, auditability, and cost controls

For each daily run we persist:

(i)
sampled IDs with weights and segment breakdowns,

(ii)
LLM outputs with prompt/policy/model versions,

(iii)
estimator diagnostics (CI width, ESS, positive rate), and

(iv)
token usage and per-run cost.
Compared with a human-only workflow that can take weeks to reach statistical significance, LLM-assisted labeling can produce high-quality labels within hours and at orders-of-magnitude lower cost
(
3
)
.
Operationally, we tune daily label budgets
m
to balance precision (CI width) against run-time and token cost: larger
m
tightens CIs but increases spend and can challenge a daily refresh SLA.
Prompt and policy updates are gated by SME review and gold-set thresholds, adding governance overhead but preventing silent metric shifts.
As part of measurement governance, when updating the auxiliary-score model used for sampling we run a consistency check using both the new and previous score versions (and, optionally,
\gamma=0
) and confirm that point estimates agree within statistical uncertainty; differences should mainly appear in CI width through weight variability.
Because the LLM labeler is independent of enforcement models (which are used only as auxiliary scores), the resulting measurement is less coupled to the production thresholds.

This section provides compact quantitative evidence for three key claims: (i) ML-assisted probability sampling improves sampling efficiency for rare policy areas at a fixed label budget, (ii) design-based reweighting preserves design-consistent point estimates under auxiliary-score drift (with drift primarily affecting variance via weight variability), and (iii) a single global probability sample supports multiple segment pivots with usable uncertainty.
We report results from a simulation POC plus anonymized production statistics in relative terms.

10.
Extensions: Experimentation and User Reach

Beyond monitoring, prevalence supports decision-making for interventions.
Two extensions are particularly useful.

Experimentation.

To measure treatment effects on rare policy areas with acceptable variance, we can combine (i) probability sampling for unbiased estimation and (ii) calibrated score-to-risk mappings that translate model scores into estimated violation probabilities, enabling surrogate prevalence metrics for large-scale A/B testing
(
10
)
.
A practical approach is a two-step pipeline. First, estimate a calibration map from an unbiased labeled sample. Second, apply the map to larger impression logs to form lower-variance experiment metrics.

User reach.

Prevalence measures exposure volume as a fraction of impressions. A complementary metric is
user reach
, defined as the fraction of daily active users (DAUs) who saw at least one violative impression. Estimating user reach requires user-level aggregation (counting each user once if they saw any policy violating content) and can be approached via a two-stage design (sample users, then sample impressions within users) or via model-assisted approximations.
We leave a full treatment to future work.

11.
Case Study: Sensitivity, Minimum Detectable Effects, and Alerting

In production, two questions come up repeatedly: (i)
How sensitive is the metric?
and (ii)
How large of a change can we reliably detect?

Because prevalence is computed from a probability sample, sensitivity is a tunable knob: increase daily label volume (or improve weight efficiency) to tighten CIs.

11.1.
Sensitivity as CI half-width

We define daily
sensitivity
as the half-width of the 95% CI,

(18)

h_{d}\;=\;1.96\,\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{\theta}_{d})}.

When violations are rare and weights are not too variable, a useful back-of-the-envelope approximation relates sensitivity to an effective sample size (ESS):

(19)

h_{d}\;\approx\;1.96\,\sqrt{\frac{\theta_{d}(1-\theta_{d})}{\mathrm{ESS}_{d}}}\;\approx\;1.96\,\sqrt{\frac{\theta_{d}}{\mathrm{ESS}_{d}}}\quad(\theta_{d}\ll 1).

This makes explicit that sensitivity scales roughly as
1/\sqrt{\mathrm{ESS}_{d}}
; doubling label volume improves CI width by about
\sqrt{2}
.
For ML-assisted designs,
\mathrm{ESS}_{d}
can be materially smaller than the raw sample size
m
when inverse-probability weights are heavy-tailed.

We next translate daily sensitivity into weekly minimum detectable effects (MDEs) used for operational alerting.

11.3.
From daily sensitivity to weekly MDE

For operational alerting we smooth the daily series with a 7-day moving average,

(20)

\bar{\theta}_{d}\;=\;\frac{1}{7}\sum_{t=d-6}^{d}\hat{\theta}_{t}.

If daily estimation noise is approximately independent with variance
\sigma^{2}
, then

\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\theta}_{d})\approx\sigma^{2}/7
and the standard error shrinks by
\sqrt{7}
.
To detect a step change by comparing two (approximately independent) 7-day windows at two-sided level
\alpha
and power
1-\beta
, a standard normal approximation yields

(21)

\mathrm{MDE}_{\mathrm{abs}}\;\approx\;\bigl(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta}\bigr)\,\sqrt{\frac{2}{7}}\,\sigma.

Expressed as a relative change from a baseline
\theta_{0}
and using
h_{d}\approx z_{1-\alpha/2}\sigma
,

(22)

\mathrm{MDE}_{\mathrm{rel}}\;\approx\;\frac{\mathrm{MDE}_{\mathrm{abs}}}{\theta_{0}}\;\approx\;\underbrace{\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta}}{z_{1-\alpha/2}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{7}}}_{\approx\,0.76\ \text{for }\alpha=0.05,\ 1-\beta=0.8}\,\frac{h_{d}}{\theta_{0}}.

Equation (
22
) highlights that relative detectability scales with the ratio
h_{d}/\theta_{0}
and benefits from temporal aggregation (e.g., weekly rollups).

Correlated fluctuations.

Real systems exhibit autocorrelation due to content mix shifts, policy changes, and labeler drift.
If
\hat{\theta}_{d}
has lag-
\ell
correlation
\rho_{\ell}
, then

\mathrm{Var}(\bar{\theta}_{d})
is inflated by

1+2\sum_{\ell=1}^{6}(1-\ell/7)\rho_{\ell}
.
We therefore treat (
22
) as a best-case planning rule and validate MDEs empirically from historical residuals.

11.4.
What dominates short-term fluctuations

In many policy areas, day-to-day variability is driven less by sampling error and more by (i)
decision quality
(prompt/policy interpretation, ambiguous edge cases) and (ii)
topic mix shifts
(changes in the distribution of content types and contexts).
We mitigate these effects through (a) decision-quality gates and recurring gold-set evaluations (§
7
), (b) drift diagnostics (auxiliary score distributions, topic/surface composition), and (c) smoothing for alerting ((
20
)).
When decision quality is the limiting factor, increasing
m
alone does not improve metric conviction; instead, prompt iteration and targeted human validation are required.

In addition, because the estimator is exposure-weighted, a small number of high-weight sampled units can disproportionately affect the daily estimate, particularly when the underlying rate is very low. We retain the sampled units and metadata outputs in production tables (§
8
), enabling rapid drill-downs and targeted human validation to distinguish genuine distributional changes from measurement effects.

12.
Operational Impact

AI-assisted prevalence measurement transforms platform safety monitoring from infrequent audits to a high-frequency, statistically grounded feedback loop.
In practice, this enables (i) proactive detection of emerging risks with faster root-cause analysis via drill-downs, (ii) quicker evaluation of launches and mitigations using trend and sensitivity signals, and (iii) scalable governance through versioned prompts, gold sets, and lineage that preserve auditability while reserving human review for targeted validation.

13.
Discussion and Limitations

Rare policy areas.
Very low base rates lead to wide daily CIs. We adapt by tuning
(\nu,\gamma)
, increasing
m
, stratifying, or pooling to weekly windows.

Prompt and policy drift.
Policy definitions evolve and content patterns shift. We version policies/prompts and periodically backfill labels for comparability.

Labeler drift.
LLM providers update models; we mitigate with pinned model versions when possible and ongoing validation.

Privacy and safety.
The workflow is designed to minimize human exposure to harmful content by using LLM labeling for the bulk and reserving human review for targeted validation.

14.
Conclusion

We presented a real-time prevalence measurement system for platform safety that combines ML-assisted probability sampling with LLM labeling.
The system yields design-consistent daily prevalence estimates with confidence intervals and supports many drill-downs from a single sample.
A configurable, lineage-rich implementation enables rapid expansion to new policies while maintaining governance through gold sets and continuous decision-quality monitoring.
