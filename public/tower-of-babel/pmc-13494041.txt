Isolation and evaluation of candidate plant-protecting bacteria from rice seedlings asymptomatic to Burkholderia glumae infection
Authors: Takumi Nagasawa, Masato Takahashi, Chiharu Akimoto-Tomiyama, Hideki Aoyagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBurkholderia glumae is a major causal agent of bacterial panicle blight (BPB) in rice, which is characterized by seedling and grain rot. Therefore, the aim of this study was to isolate potential biocontrol bacteria from rice and investigate their efficacies against B. glumae. Potential biocontrol bacteria were isolated from rice seedlings asymptomatic to B. glumae infection using the in planta evaluation screening (IPES) method. In vitro experiments were performed to examine the biocontrol activities of the bacterial isolates. Cultivation of rice seeds in soil (Bonsol No. 2) inoculated with these bacteria and B. glumae suppressed the pathogenicity of B. glumae. Isolates L5 and L6 showed 99.78% and 99.60% identity with Paenibacillus hunanensis, respectively, and isolate R2 was identified as Sphingomonas aquatilis. In vitro evaluation showed that R2 did not exhibit any biocontrol activity. Although the isolates showed inhibitory effects against B. glumae in rice seedlings grown in Bonsol No. 2, they did not show biocontrol activities in Naemidori soil. Notably, inoculation with L5, L6, and R2 bacterial mixture inhibited B. glumae pathogenicity in Naemidori. Conclusively, this study provides a strategy for the effective screening of biocontrol organisms for the sustainable prevention of B. glumae infection in rice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54587-w.Keywords: Burkholderia glumae, Rice, Biocontrol, Plant-protecting bacteria, in planta, Bacterial cocktailSubject terms: Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Burkholderia glumae is a major causal agent of bacterial panicle blight (BPB) in rice, which is characterized by seedling and grain rot1,2, depending on the region3. Notably, other causative agents of BPB have been reported, including B. gladioli, B. plantarii, Fusarium sporotrichioides, and Pseudomonas oryzihabitans4–7. However, their impact is not as severe as that of B. glumae in terms of pathogenicity and the scale of infection8,9. BPB has been reported to cause damage to rice production in various regions, including Asia, South America, and North America9. Although efforts have been made to develop resistant cultivars to control B. glumae infection2, there are currently no commercially available cultivars that are completely resistant to B. glumae10. Recent findings indicate the development of B. glumae strains that are resistant to oxolinic acid10, a previously effective compound against the pathogen11,12. Moreover, oxolinic acid is not commercially available in many countries, including the United States13.

Biocontrol, which involves the use of bacteria to control plant pathogens, has attracted considerable attention as a sustainable and ecofriendly alternative to chemical agents in agriculture14. Biocontrol agents have been shown to suppress B. glumae pathogenicity through various mechanisms, including antimicrobial activity3,15, plant growth promoting activity16, toxoflavin-degrading enzyme17–19, and quorum sensing (QS) signal degrading enzymes2. Importantly, biocontrol activity is often based on multiple mechanisms, some of which remain unknown20,21. Although previous studies have mainly focused on screening bacteria using in vitro evaluations that focus on known specific mechanisms20,22,23, in planta experiments are necessary to investigate, develop, and validate potential biocontrol bacteria20.

Considering that the bioassays and field assays used to evaluate the suppressive effects of biocontrol agents on bacterial pathogens require considerable effort and time, in vitro evaluations using agar plates are often used in the initial screening stage20,22,24. However, the biocontrol efficacy of bacteria is usually affected by environmental conditions such as temperature, soil pH, soil composition, and humidity25–27, limiting the translation of in vitro results to field scenarios. Moreover, bacteria that show biocontrol activity in vitro often do not show considerable effects in planta20. Previous studies have mainly focused on the use of a single bacterium for biocontrol24, with limited studies on biocontrol efficacy of multiple bacteria combinations24,25,28.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to identify potential biocontrol bacteria that can suppress B. glumae pathogenicity in rice. To identify potential biocontrol bacteria targeting B. glumae, a novel approach (in planta evaluation screening (IPES) method) was used to screen bacteria in rice seedlings that were asymptomatic to B. glumae infection. Additionally, in vitro and in planta experiments were performed to investigate the biocontrol efficacies of the isolated and cultured bacteria. Moreover, the relationship between in planta pathogenicity suppression and in vitro biocontrol activity was investigated.

Results

Bacterial isolation and simple identification using the in planta evaluation screening (IPES)

Rice seedlings were inoculated with a suspension of B. glumae (O.D.600 = 0.0004) and infected with B. glumae. Notably, one of the 15 rice seedlings did not show any symptoms (Fig. 1). Some infected rice seedlings (B. glumae-infected) showed longer root and leaf lengths than those inoculated with sterile distilled water (SDW; termed control) (Fig. 1). The rice seedlings that did not exhibit any symptoms (indicated with a red arrow in Fig. 1b) were selected based on the hypothesis that they inhabited bacteria with biocontrol activity against B. glumae. Microorganisms, excluding B. glumae, were isolated from the leaves and roots of selected rice seedlings on Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates. Importantly, ten culturable isolates were obtained from the leaves (L1, 3, 4, 5, and 6) and roots (R1, 2, 3, 4, and 6). 16S rRNA sequence analysis showed that the culturable isolates from the control mainly belonged to the genera Xanthomonas (7/20 strains) and Pantoea (5/20 strains) (Table S1). Among the culturable isolates from asymptomatic seedlings infected with B. glumae, the proportions of Paenibacillus (4/10 strains) and Sphingomonas (3/10 strains) were high (Table S2). Although one strain of Paenibacillus and three strains of Sphingomonas were also identified in the control, their proportions were lower than those observed in the asymptomatic seedlings (indicated by a red arrow in Fig. 1b; Tables S1 and S2). However, the sample size is extremely limited and may not be representative of the overall microbial community of the seedlings.

Inhibitory effects of single culturable isolates against B. glumae pathogenicity

To evaluate the inhibitory effects of the single culturable isolates against B. glumae pathogenicity, B. glumae and a culturable isolate were co-inoculated into rice seeds, which were grown in Bonsol No. 2 (soil from which the bacteria were isolated). L5 and R2 significantly suppressed the pathogenicity of B. glumae in leaves and roots, with L6 showing a similar trend (Fig. 2a). Analysis of the full-length sequence (approximately 1,500 bp) of the 16S rRNA showed that L5 and L6 showed 99.78 and 99.60% similarity with Paenibacillus hunanensis. Additionally, R2 showed 100% similarity to Sphingomonas aquatilis (Table S3).

In contrast, L5, L6, and R2 did not exhibit any inhibitory effect against B. glumae under different soil conditions (Naemidori, for cold regions; Fig. 2b). Additionally, isolates L1 and R1 did not suppress B. glumae in both the leaves and roots of the rice seedlings grown in Bonsol No.2 (Fig. 2a). On the other hand, L1 and R1 only showed a tendency to suppress B. glumae pathogenicity in the roots of seedlings grown in Naemidori (Fig. 2b). Collectively, these results suggest that the inoculation with single culturable isolates may not consistently suppress the pathogenicity of B. glumae, depending on the soil used for cultivation.

In vitro effects of the culturable isolates

In vitro experiments showed that the culturable isolates L1, L5, R1 and R6 exhibited siderophore production, phosphate solubilization, and indole-3-acetic acid [IAA] production abilities and antibacterial activity against B. glumae (Table 1; Figs. S2-S5).

Isolates L1, L5, and R1 suppressed B. glumae pathogenicity in Bonsol No. 2 and Naemidori. In contrast, isolate R6 did not suppress B. glumae pathogenicity in any of the soil types (Fig. 2). Among the culturable isolates that suppressed B. glumae pathogenicity in Bonsol No. 2 (Fig. 2a), L5 exhibited all in vitro activities and L6 exhibited phosphate solubilization and IAA production abilities, whereas R2 did not show any activity (Table 1). Based on these results, it can be speculated that the inhibitory effects of L5 and L6 against B. glumae may be related to their specific in vitro characteristics. On the other hand, L1 and R1, which suppressed B. glumae pathogenicity in the roots in Naemidori, showed all in vitro activities (Table 1). Overall, it is possible that the biocontrol activities of L1 and R1 against B. glumae may be due to changes in the soil environment.

B. glumae pathogenicity suppression using multiple culturable isolates (approach for the soil where B. glumae pathogenicity suppression by a single culturable isolate was low)

Mixtures of multiple culturable isolates were prepared using L5, L6, and R2 (which suppressed B. glumae pathogenicity in Bonsol No. 2; Fig. 2a) and L1 and R1 (which suppressed B. glumae pathogenicity in Naemidori; Fig. 2b and showed all activities in vitro evaluation; Table 1). When the suspensions of B. glumae (O.D.600 = 0.0004, 0.004) and mixtures of multiple culturable isolates (O.D.600 = 0.04) were co-inoculated into the rice seeds and the rice seedlings were grown using Naemidori, similar trends were observed between different inoculation concentrations of B. glumae (Fig. 3). Inoculation with L5, L6, and R2 mixture significantly suppressed B. glumae pathogenicity in the leaves and roots (Fig. 3), and similar results were observed in multiple repeated experiments (Fig. S1). In contrast, inoculation with each isolate (L5, L6, and R2) separately did not suppress B. glumae pathogenicity in Naemidori (Fig. 2b). Overall, these results indicate that L5, L6, and R2 coordinately suppressed B. glumae pathogenicity in rice seedlings. Although inoculation with L1, L5, L6, R1, and R2 mixture also suppressed B. glumae pathogenicity, its inhibitory effect was lesser than that of L5, L6, and R2 mixture (Fig. 3). Inoculation with L1 and R1 mixture suppressed B. glumae pathogenicity in the roots but not in the leaves (Fig. 3), consistent with the effects of each strain (Fig. 2b). Thus, it was shown that L1 and R1 do not coordinately suppress the pathogenicity of B. glumae. Notably, L5, L6, and R2 bacterial mixture showed a superior inhibitory effect against B. glumae to L5, L6, and R1 bacterial mixture (Fig. 3), suggesting that the presence of R2 resulted in a stronger inhibitory effect on pathogenicity.

Discussion

In vitro evaluation and screening of biocontrol bacteria are often accomplished using agar plates and liquid media due to their high throughput and simplicity20,22,23,29. However, most bacteria with in vitro biocontrol activities often do not exhibit similar activities in planta20. Based on the hypothesis that rice seedlings that do not show symptoms of B. glumae infection may contain bacteria with biocontrol activity against B. glumae, we isolated potential biocontrol bacteria from rice seedlings using the IPES method (indicated with a red arrow in Fig. 1b).

Culturable isolates obtained using the IPES method showed a higher proportion of Paenibacillus and Sphingomonas genera compared to the control (Table S1, S2). Additionally, the three strains that suppressed B. glumae pathogenicity in planta (Fig. 2a) were identified as Paenibacillus hunanensis (L5, L6) and Sphingomonas aquatilis (R2) (Table S3). Overall, these results suggest that the IPES method may be effective for the isolation of Paenibacillus and Sphingomonas, which suppress B. glumae pathogenicity. However, to clarify its effectiveness, further comparison with other established in planta assays and more quantitative microbial community analyses are necessary. Importantly, the genus Paenibacillus is known to possess siderophore production, phosphate solubilization, and IAA production abilities and antimicrobial activity30. The genus Paenibacillus isolated in this study (L1, L3, L5, L6) exhibited these characteristics in vitro (Table 1; Figs. S2-S5). Additionally, the genus Sphingomonas promotes plant germination and growth by producing plant hormones such as IAA, gibberellin, and salicylic acid, and there are few reports on its siderophore production and phosphate solubilization abilities31. The culturable isolates L4 and R3 obtained in this study belong to the genus Sphingomonas and exhibited siderophore production, phosphate solubilization, and IAA production abilities in vitro (Table 1; Figs. S2-S5). Therefore, considering that B. glumae is an opportunistic pathogen28,32, the onset of BPB may be associated with the presence (or ratio of the presence) of Paenibacillus and Sphingomonas in rice seeds and seedlings.

Our study showed that isolates belonging to the Paenibacillus genus (L1, L3, L5, and L6) and Sphingomonas genus (L4, R2, and R3) (Table S2) showed various biological activities in vitro (Table 1). Although R2 did not show any biocontrol activities in vitro, it suppressed B. glumae pathogenicity in planta (Fig. 2a), indicating that in vitro assay alone may not be sufficient for screening potential biocontrol bacteria such as R2. To clarify the mechanisms underlying R2-mediated inhibition of B. glumae, it is important to examine the production of toxoflavin-degrading enzymes17–19, QS signal-degrading enzymes2, and ISR21,33. Additionally, biocontrol activities often depend on multiple mechanisms, some of which remain unknown20,21. Thus, it is desirable to comprehensively evaluate potential mechanisms during experimental screening. The approach applied in this study, in which the causative pathogen of the target disease is infected into seeds and the microorganisms are isolated from asymptomatic plants, is considered one method for isolating useful microorganisms in planta.

L5, L6 and R2 suppressed B. glumae pathogenicity in rice seedlings grown in the sterilized soil used for bacterial isolation (Bonsol No. 2 [Fig. S6a; Table S4]), but did not suppress B. glumae pathogenicity in a different sterilized soil (Naemidori [Fig. S6b; Table S4]) (Fig. 2). Considering that the biocontrol activity of bacteria depends on the environmental conditions26,27, it may be concluded that the activities of L5, L6, and R2 decreased due to differences in soil environments (Fig. S6; Table S4). To clarify the impact of the soil environment on bacterial biocontrol activity, further analysis of soil physical and chemical properties is required, as well as field trials under actual cultivation conditions where competition with microorganisms occurs. In contrast, L1 and R1 did not suppress B. glumae pathogenicity in Bonsol No. 2; however, a tendency to suppress B. glumae was observed in the roots in Naemidori. Considering that L1 and R1 exhibited all biocontrol activities in vitro (Table 1), they were suggested to show these activities in planta and suppress the pathogenicity of B. glumae due to changing the soil from Bonsol No.2 to Naemidori.

Previous studies have been performed on the biocontrol efficacy of multiple bacterial mixtures in various environments to achieve consistently high biocontrol activities in various environments24,25,28. Combining Pseudomonas putida (RSB1, RSB10, and RSB15) and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (RSB2) synergistically suppressed B. glumae pathogenicity in rice28. Additionally, combining yeast (Pichia guilermondii) and bacteria (Bacillus mycoides) consistently suppressed Botrytis cinerea pathogenicity and prevented gray mold in strawberries under various conditions (temperature, relative humidity, and application timing of P. guilermondii and B. mycoides)25. In the present study, although L5, L6, and R2 did not suppress B. glumae pathogenicity in rice seedlings grown in Naemidori, inoculation with a mixture of the three species suppressed B. glumae pathogenicity (Fig. 3). Notably, L1, L5, L6, R1 and R2 mixture showed a lower inhibitory effect against B. glumaes than L5, L6 and R2 mixure (Fig. 3). Overall, these results suggest that R2 is necessary in the bacterial mixture to achieve a strong pathogen inhibition effect. However, R2 did not show in vitro activities, including siderophore production, phosphate solubilization, and IAA production abilities and antibacterial activity against B. glumae (Table 1), suggesting the involvement of other mechanisms in R2-mediated suppression of B. glumae pathogenicity. Consistent with the effects of the individual strains (Fig. 2b), L1 and R1 mixture suppressed B. glumae pathogenicity in the roots but not in the leaves (Fig. 3). Collectively, these results indicate that L1 and R1 mixture did not suppress B. glumae pathogenicity via a synergistic mechanism. Considering that L1 and R1 showed similar characteristics in vitro (Table 1), it can be speculated that combining bacteria with different characteristics in vitro is necessary to synergistically suppress B. glumae pathogenicity in planta. A previous study showed that S. maltophilia (RSB2) isolated from rice, which exhibited a higher biofilm formation ability than P. putida (RSB1, RSB10, RSB15) in vitro, enhanced the inhibitory effect of P. putida (RSB1, RSB10, RSB15) against B. glumae28. To evaluate the effects of L1, L5, L6, R1, and R2 on rice growth, the strains were separately inoculated into rice seeds. Importantly, there were no significant differences between the inoculated rice seedlings and those in the control group (Fig. S7), indicating that inoculation with L1, L5, L6, R1, and R2 did not affect rice growth.

Most of the studies on biocontrol have focused on using a single bacterium and there are few reports in which the combination of multiple strains has improved biocontrol effects24. In the present study, combining L5, L6 and R2 (Fig. 2b), which individually did not show a biocontrol effect in Naemidori, suppressed the pathogenicity of B. glumae in both leaves and roots (Fig. 3). Overall, these results indicate that the IPES method may be useful for isolating bacteria with inhibitory effects against B. glumae in planta. Nipponbare, rice seeds used in this study, is known to be susceptible to B. glumae at the seedling and panicle stages10,34; however, in this study, we evaluated the efficacy of the culturable isolates only at the seedling stage. Conducting field trials and investigating the biocontrol efficacy at the panicle initiation stage may contribute to a better understanding of the practical effectiveness of these culturable isolates. Future studies should focus on elucidating the mechanisms by which R2 suppresses B. glumae pathogenicity, it may be possible to clarify the unknown mechanism of B. glumae pathogenicity suppression. Additionally, if there are individuals that do not show symptoms in susceptible cultivar in other plants, the IPES method may be applied to isolate microorganisms with potential application in pathogen biocontrol. Notably, the optimal abundance ratio of useful microorganisms can be obtained by analyzing the microbiota of healthy plants that do not show any symptoms even after inoculation with a pathogenic microbe. Conclusively, the approach employed in this study (inoculation with bacterial mixture) is expected to provide a stronger pathogen inhibition and robust efficacy under various soil and growth conditions.

Materials and methods

Preparation of rice, pathogenic organism, and soil

Rice seeds (Nipponbare), which were harvested between 2020 and 2024 at the Tsukuba Plant Innovation Research Centre of the University of Tsukuba and stored at 4 °C, were used in this study. Briefly, rice seeds were soaked in antiformin (available chlorine 5%) (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co., Osaka, Japan) for 10 min and washed with 5 mL of SDW at least 10 times. Additionally, the pathogenic bacterium, B. glumae MAFF301682, was provided by the Genetic Resource Center of the National Agriculture and Food Research Organization (Ibaraki, Japan). The wet soil used were Bonsol No. 2 (Sumitomo Chemical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and Naemidori (Kantoh Nosan Co., Ltd., Tochigi, Japan), and were sterilized using an autoclave (121 °C, 15 min).

Bacterial inoculation to rice seeds and cultivation of rice seedlings

B. glumae and culturable isolates stored in 20% (v/v) glycerol stocks at ˗80 °C were used after thawing. Briefly, the strains were cultured in 500 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 100 mL of LB medium at 30 °C for 24 h at 150 rpm, followed by the addition of SDW to adjust the concentration. Thereafter, 20 rice seeds were soaked in 2 mL of the adjusted bacterial suspension and placed in a vacuum polydesiccator type 300 (As One Co., Osaka, Japan) after stirring for 10 min at normal pressure. A vacuum pump DW-60 (Sato Vac Inc., Tokyo, Japan) was used to maintain the vacuum conditions for 10 min, and the seeds were infected. Each clean disposable pot (75 × 75 × 65 mm) (Meiwa Co., Ltd., Aichi, Japan) containing 150 mL of soil was placed in a clean plastic container with a capacity of 2,200 mL (155 × 219 × 81 mm), followed by the introduction of 150 mL of SDW into each pot. Thereafter, rice seeds inoculated with B. glumae were sown at a rate of five seeds per pot, making a total of 15 seeds. The seeds were cultivated for 7 days under 14 h light period (temperature, 30 °C) and 10 h dark period (temperature, 26 °C) in the plant growth chamber MLR-350T (Sanyo Electric Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan).

Bacterial isolation using IPES method

Briefly, rice seeds were inoculated with B. glumae suspension (adjusted to O.D.600 = 0.0004). After 7 days of cultivation, leaf and root lengths of the seedlings were measured to evaluate the severity of B. glumae symptoms. Rice inoculated with SDW (B. glumae non-inoculation) and cultivated using the same method was used as the control group. A rice seedling that did not show symptoms of B. glumae (i.e., the seedling with both root and leaf lengths longer than the control average [leaf length: 8.8 cm; root length: 7.6 cm]) was selected, and the soil adhering to it was washed with SDW and removed. After separating the rice seedling into leaves and roots, the various portions were placed in 2.0 mL screw cap micro tubes (Sarstedt AG & Co. KG, Nümbrecht, Germany). Thereafter, 1 mL of SDW and two stainless steel beads (diameter 4.8 mm) per seedling were crushed using a Micro Smash MS-100 (Tomy Digital Biology Co., Ltd.) at 3,500 rpm for 45 s. The crushed liquid and 40% (v/v) glycerol solution were added to a 1.5-mL sterilized tube at 500 µL each, mixed using the G-560 vortex mixer (Scientific Industries, Inc), and stored at ˗80 °C until use. Leaf and root homogenates were diluted 104 times with SDW, spread on xylem-mimicking medium (XMM) agar plates35, and incubated at 30 °C for 4 days until colonies were visible. Morphologically different colonies were randomly selected, and 10 strains each (L1–L10 and R1–R10) were isolated from leaf and root homogenates. When the isolated strains were streaked on LB agar plates and cultured at 30 °C for 4 days, some strains produced a yellow pigment that was presumed to be B. glumae used for seed inoculation. Culturable isolates without B. glumae were purified by repeating the streak culture on LB agar plates two or more times (up to 14 times). Thereafter, the culturable isolates were inoculated using Disposable Inoculation Needle (Watson Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and cultured in 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask with 100 mL of LB medium at 30 °C for 24 h at 150 rpm (amplitude 70 mm). After cultivation, 1.5 mL of the culture medium and 500 µL of 40% (v/v) glycerol solution were added to a 1.5 mL sterile tube, mixed using G-560 vortex mixer (Scientific Industries, Inc.), and stored at ˗80 °C until use.

16S rRNA analysis of culturable isolates

DNA was extracted from culturable isolates using phenol: chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1; Nacalai Tesque Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan). DNA amplification was performed via PCR using the 8 F primer (5′- AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG − 3′) and the 1492R primer (5′- GTGCCAGCAGCCGCGG − 3′). PCR products were purified using a QIA Quick Purification Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). DNA sequencing reagent (Premix2) was provided by Fasmac Co., Ltd. (Kanagawa, Japan), and sequencing was performed using primers 8 F, 785 F, 805R, and 1492R. Successfully amplified base sequences (approximately 500 bp) were identified from the obtained sequences using Chromas ver. 2.6.4 (Technelysium Pty Ltd., South Brisbane, Australia). Thereafter, high-quality sequences were searched against known bacterial species in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST).

Evaluation of the effects of culturable isolates on B. glumae pathogenicity in planta

B. glumae and culturable isolates, which were restored and cultured from stored glycerol stocks, were adjusted to O.D.600 of 0.008 and 0.08, respectively, using SDW. Rice seeds inoculated with 1 mL each of B. glumae and the culturable isolate suspensions were cultivated for 7 days, after which the lengths of the leaves and roots of the rice seedlings were measured. To investigate the inhibitory effects of the culturable isolates against B. glumae, we compared the performance of rice seedlings inoculated with SDW and B. glumae suspensions (O.D.600 = 0.004).

Evaluation of the antibacterial activity of culturable isolates against B. glumae in vitro

B. glumae and culturable isolates were adjusted to O.D.600 = 0.4 using SDW. Thereafter, 10 µL of the isolate suspensions were added to the disks (antibiotic test filter paper, thickness: 0.7 mm, diameter: 6 mm) (Advantec Toyo Kasisha, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), which were set on LB agar plates, and incubated at 30 °C for 7 days. After inactivating the isolates using the chloroform vapor method29, 2 mL of B. glumae suspension was added to the plates, followed by incubation at 30 °C for 3 days. Culturable isolates forming halos around the colony were classified as active (+), whereas those without halos were classified as inactive (-).

Evaluation of the siderophore production ability of culturable isolates in vitro

Briefly, 10 µL of adjusted isolate suspension was added to the disks, which were set on chrome azurol S (CAS) agar plates36,37, and incubated at 30 °C for 7 days. Culturable isolates with halos around the colony were classified as active (+), whereas those without halos were classified as inactive (-).

Evaluation of the phosphate solubilization ability of culturable isolates in vitro

A 10 µL volume of adjusted isolate suspension was added to the disks, which were set on National botanical research institute’s phosphate growth medium (NBRIP) agar plates38, and incubated at 30 °C for 7 days. Culturable isolates exhibiting halo formation around the colony were classified as active (+), whereas those without halos were classified as inactive (-).

Evaluation of the IAA production ability of culturable isolates in vitro

IAA production was evaluated using a modified version of a previously reported method39. Specifically, 10 µL of culturable isolate suspension was added to three disks, set on l-tryptophan-added XMM agar plate, and cultured at 30 °C for 3 days. Thereafter, the disks were removed, and 20 µL of Salkowski reagent40 was added to each area where the discs were placed. After reaction at room temperature for 30 min, IAA production ability was visually assessed based on the color transformation of the Salkowski reagent to pink.

B. glumae pathogenicity suppression using multiple culturable isolates (approach for the soil where B. glumae pathogenicity suppression by a single culturable isolate was low)

B. glumae, each culturable isolate, and mixture of isolates were adjusted to O.D.600 of 0.0008, 0.008, and 0.08, respectively, using SDW. To prepare a mixture of the culturable isolates, 1 mL of each adjusted strain suspension was transferred to a-15 mL conical tube and mixed to ensure equal inoculation pressure under each condition. Rice seeds inoculated with 1 mL each of B. glumae and bacterial mixture (B. glumae: final O.D.600 = 0.0004, 0.004; culturable isolate mixture: final O.D.600 = 0.04) were cultivated for 7 days. Thereafter, the lengths of the leaves and roots of the rice seedlings were measured. Additionally, rice seeds inoculated with SDW and B. glumae suspensions (O.D.600 = 0.0004, 0.004) were cultivated using the same method to compare B. glumae pathogenicity between both groups.

Statistical analysis

Data obtained from the in planta experiments were expressed as averages, with error bars representing standard errors (n = 15), and were calculated using the STDEV.S and SQRT functions in Microsoft Excel for Office 365 MSO (Microsoft Co., WA, USA). Significant differences between rice seedlings inoculated with B. glumae and those inoculated with both B. glumae and culturable isolates were determined using Dunnett’s test in R software version 4.4.2. In vitro evaluations were performed using three plates with three discs per plate under the same conditions.
