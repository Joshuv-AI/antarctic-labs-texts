A Fully Si-Compatible Ni/Si3N4/Al2O3/p + Poly-Si RRAM Device for Analog Synapse and Its System-Level Assessment toward Processing-in-Memory Applications
Authors: Yeji Lee, Soomin Kim, Seongmin Kim, Wonbo Shim, Sungjun Kim, Seongjae Cho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We propose a fully Si-compatible resistive-switching random-access memory (RRAM) device consisting of a Ni/Si3N4/Al2O3/p + poly-Si structure for processing-in-memory (PIM) applications fabricated through a process integration with Si CMOS compatibility. By introduction of an ultrathin Al2O3 tunneling layer between the Si3N4 switching layer and p + poly-Si bottom electrode, the leakage current in the high-resistance state (HRS) is suppressed, resulting in stable bipolar switching characteristics with a high on/off current ratio of approximately 104, maintained for more than 107 switching operations, as evidenced through durability measurements. The retention characteristics were also maintained without degradation for a considerable time (>105 s). When potentiation (6.5 V, 3 μs) and depression (−3.7 V, 3 μs) pulses with the same voltage and time conditions were applied, the device exhibited stable conductance change characteristics with high linearity and symmetry, demonstrating its potential for use as an analog synaptic device through repeatable signal modulation. The average conductance changes were 1.72 and 1.60 μS for potentiation and depression, respectively, confirming balanced weighting characteristics. Furthermore, based on experimentally obtained device characteristics, we implemented a VGG-8 convolutional neural network using the NeuroSim 1.4 framework and achieved high inference accuracy (90.25%), along with low latency and high energy efficiency on the CIFAR-10 data set. Thus, the proposed device holds promise for next-generation nonvolatile memory and analog neural network computing, particularly for energy-efficient processing-in-memory architectures.

Introduction

With the recent emergence of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies, the demand for high-performance computational processing is rapidly increasing. − However, the traditional von Neumann architecture suffers from limitations in terms of the physical separation between the processor and memory, which requires continuous data transfer during the computation, resulting in a significant system bottleneck. − This bottleneck not only consumes more power and slows down the overall system speed. Processing-in-memory (PIM) technology, which can overcome this limitation, has gained attention as a promising solution. PIM integrates processing units directly within the memory, thereby reducing data movement and enabling both low-power operation and high-speed computation. − Resistive-switching random-access memory (RRAM) is emerging as a next-generation memory device owing to its simple metal–insulator–metal structure, excellent scaling characteristics, and nonvolatility. − Particularly, RRAM is capable of forming multiple resistive states, which has remarkable potential for application as neuromorphic devices that emulate neural synapses. − Herein, we propose a Ni/Si3N4/Al2O3/p + poly-Si device: a Si-based RRAM structure with excellent Si processing compatibility. By introducing the dual-layer structure of Si3N4 and Al2O3 as the switching and tunneling layers, respectively, the device effectively suppresses the current in the off state and contributes to improving the on/off ratio to more than 105. The device also demonstrates analog synaptic behaviors, including potentiation and depression, and shows excellent endurance and retention characteristics.

Experimental Details

The fabrication process was carried out entirely in a standard Si CMOS facility to ensure compatibility with foundry processes. The bottom electrode was formed by depositing poly-Si via low-pressure chemical vapor deposition (LPCVD) at 620 °C, followed by ion implantation using a medium-current implanter and activation annealing at 1000 °C. The tunneling Al2O3 layer was deposited by atomic layer deposition (ALD) at 350 °C, while the Si3N4 switching layer was deposited by plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD), which typically reaches ∼350 °C during the process. All these steps are standard in CMOS manufacturing and fall within the typical back-end-of-the-line (BEOL) thermal budget (≤450 °C). Nickel was chosen as the top electrode metal owing to its established use in self-aligned silicide processes and its BEOL-friendly nature. − A summary of the integrated process sequence and associated thermal conditions is provided in Table .

Results and Discussion

Figure a shows the results of DC I–V measurements performed on the fabricated Ni/Si3N4/Al2O3/p + poly-Si RRAM device. The measurements were performed by using a semiconductor parameter analyzer (Keithley 4200A-SCS, Keithley Instruments, Ohio, USA) and 4225-PMU modules in a probe station. Figure a also shows that the cycle-to-cycle variation of the resistive-switching characteristics was effectively reduced by carefully adjusting the sweep boundary conditions, such as the starting and ending voltages. Furthermore, the fabricated device operated stably without an external compliance current (I CC), owing to its intrinsic self-compliance behavior, which autonomously regulates the current and suppresses excessive filament growth. Figure b demonstrates consistent switching over 100 consecutive DC cycles without failure or distribution broadening. The statistical analysis further reveals that the coefficient of variation (σ/μ) of the set voltage is less than 0.60%, confirming that the optimized self-compliance mechanism effectively minimizes switching variability. These results indicate that stable and uniform switching with a high on/off ratio can be achieved under I CC-free operation, which is highly advantageous for low-power in-memory computing applications. Figure c shows a box plot of the extracted set and reset voltages after 100 switching cycles for each of the 20 devices. The switching voltages were stable across all devices with limited device-to-device (D-to-D) variation. Figure d shows the cumulative distribution of HRS and LRS resistances measured across the 20 devices. The calculated σ/μ values at a reading voltage of 0.2 V show that the LRS and HRS were 4.28% and 2.33%, respectively. The LRS had a relatively wide distribution owing to the characteristics related to the formation of the filament path as the filaments were formed at random locations in the device each time, resulting in differences in path and thickness. By contrast, the HRS variability was relatively narrow, as the off state was governed by a breakdown or disruption of the conductive filaments, which was more dependent on the uniformity of the insulating layers. Overall, these statistical analyses validate the excellent switching repeatability, and the quantification of both cycle-to-cycle (C-to-C) and D-to-D variability provides a foundation for device-level modeling and system-level design optimization in PIM and neuromorphic computing applications.

Figure a shows the endurance characteristics of the fabricated RRAM device, evaluated by applying alternating set (6 V, 500 μs) and reset (−4.5 V, 500 μs) pulses over 107 switching cycles. The HRS and LRS states remained clearly distinguishable throughout the entire cycling process, indicating stable and repeatable switching behavior. However, the observed on/off resistance ratio ranged between 102 and 103, which is relatively smaller than that observed under DC I–V measurements. This discrepancy is primarily attributed to hardware limitations, such as the noise floor and compliance resolution of the pulse-measurement system, which are less sensitive than DC analysis tools. Throughout the measurement time, both HRS and LRS exhibited distinct state retention characteristics, with the LRS maintaining a stable resistance value within a relatively narrow range of variation. Comparison of Device Structures and Switching Characteristics

As shown in Figure a, the device without the tunneling layer exhibited ohmic behavior in the HRS with a slope of approximately 1.25 in the low-voltage region, followed by an increase in the slope to approximately 8.92 in the high-voltage region. This rapid increase in slope suggests the possible involvement of a space-charge-limited conduction (SCLC) mechanism, where the traps are filled as the voltage rises and the current increases rapidly thereafter. − In other words, after the carriers were injected into the traps in the oxide film, the current increased nonlinearly as the traps were saturated. Moreover, for the device with the tunneling layer shown in Figure b, the slope started with a relatively low slope (1.58) in the HRS region and increased to slopes of 3.14 and 7.09 in the medium- and high-voltage regions. Particularly, the slope change in the high-voltage region can be interpreted as the involvement of the Fowler–Nordheim (FN) tunneling mechanism. − FN tunneling is the quantum tunneling of electrons through an insulator barrier as the field increases, which is typically manifested by a sharp increase in current seen in the curve in the high-voltage region. Therefore, the structure including the tunneling layer regulates the field distribution in the oxide film to enable FN tunneling, which contributes to the effective suppression of leakage current in the HRS. The two devices also showed differences in LRS. Devices without a tunneling layer flowed relatively high currents even in the LRS state, which can cause scattering of resistance values and poor stability. By contrast, devices with the tunneling layer exhibited a more stable conduction path with reduced current in the LRS region while still maintaining the ohmic behavior. These results imply that the tunneling layer provides a certain filtering effect on the formation of the current path inside the device, which, in turn, contributes to the improvement of the on/off ratio, switching voltage stability, and energy efficiency of the device. In conclusion, the comparison in Figure a,b clearly reveals that the presence or absence of the tunneling layer has a significant impact not only on the conduction mechanisms of the RRAM device but also on its overall electrical characteristics. Specifically, the tunneling layer plays a key role in improving the device performance by inducing and modulating the SCLC and FN tunneling mechanisms through control of traps in the amorphous oxide film and modulation of the field distribution.

System-Level Performance Evaluation

C-to-C and D-to-D uniformities were systematically evaluated to assess the device reliability. For the C-to-C analysis, potentiation and depression pulses were repeatedly applied for 20 consecutive cycles on a single device. As shown in Figure , the conductance update behavior remained highly stable and reproducible, with nearly unchanged conductance states across all cycles.

Potentiation and depression behaviors under 20 sets of consecutive identical pulses, where each set consisted of 50 potentiation and 50 depression pulses, making up a total of 2000 pulses.

To further investigate D-to-D uniformity, 10 devices were randomly selected across a wafer, and repeated measurements (10 cycles per device, yielding 100 data sets in total) were performed. The statistical results are summarized in Figure a–c. As shown in Figure a, the average conductance change was consistently maintained at ∼1.7 μS for potentiation and ∼1.4 μS for depression pulses. As shown in Figure b, the linearity error, expressed as the root-mean-square error (RMSE), remained very small in the range of the 10–5–10–4 order. As shown in Figure c, the symmetry values averaged ∼0.89. The slight deviation from the ideal value of 1.0 is likely attributed to the presence of the tunneling oxide. These results confirm reliable uniformities across both cycles and devices, providing quantitative evidence of device robustness.

Conclusions

In this work, a fully Si-compatible Ni/Si3N4/Al2O3/p + poly-Si-based RRAM device was successfully designed and fabricated. Its resistive switching behavior, electrical reliability, and neuromorphic computing applicability were systematically investigated. DC voltage-based measurements showed that the proposed device reliably exhibited clear bipolar resistive switching characteristics and a high on/off ratio (∼105), which was attributed to the effective suppression of the off-state leakage current by the Al2O3 tunneling layer. Pulse-based endurance and retention characterization measurements also confirmed 107 repeated switching and long-term data retention over 105 s, demonstrating long-term reliability as a memory device. Furthermore, excellent linearity, symmetry, and repeatability were obtained through conductance variation experiments under identical pulse conditions, and these characteristics led to practical application potential through CNN-based neuromorphic learning accuracy and system-level energy efficiency evaluations. Overall, the proposed RRAM device not only serves as a reliable nonvolatile memory element but also can be a promising candidate for energy-efficient PIM systems, utilizing analog synaptic properties.
