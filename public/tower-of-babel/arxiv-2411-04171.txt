Fractionally Quantized Electric Polarization and Discrete Shift of Crystalline Fractional Chern Insulators
Yuxuan Zhang and Maissam Barkeshli

Abstract
Fractional Chern insulators (FCI) with crystalline symmetry possess topological invariants that fundamentally have no analog in continuum fractional quantum Hall (FQH) states. Here we demonstrate through numerical calculations on model wave functions that FCIs possess a fractionally quantized electric polarization, P → o \vec{{P}}_{\text{o}} , where o is a high symmetry point. P → o \vec{{P}}_{\text{o}} takes fractional values as compared to the allowed values for integer Chern insulators because of the possibility that anyons carry fractional quantum numbers under lattice translation symmetries. P → o \vec{{P}}_{\text{o}} , together with the discrete shift S o {S}_{\text{o}} , determine fractionally quantized universal contributions to electric charge in regions containing lattice disclinations, dislocations, boundaries, and/or corners, and which are fractions of the minimal anyon charge. We demonstrate how these invariants can be extracted using Monte Carlo computations on model wave functions with lattice defects for 1/2-Laughlin and 1/3-Laughlin FCIs on the square and honeycomb lattice, respectively, obtained using the parton construction. These results comprise a class of fractionally quantized response properties of topologically ordered states that go beyond the known ones discovered over thirty years ago.

Universal charge response of defects

The invariants
\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}
and
{S}_{\text{o}}
determine universal contributions to the electric charge in subregions of the system. For a given system, pick a choice of unit cell and a spatial region
W
that is large compared to the correlation length
\xi
(for example as shown in Fig.
2
(a)). The boundary
\partial W
should lie along the boundary of the unit cells and far away (compared to
\xi
) from the boundary of the system and any lattice dislocations or disclinations. The total charge in
W
is defined as:

\displaystyle Q_{W}:=\sum_{i\in W}\text{wt}(i)Q_{i},

(1)

where
\sum_{i}
is over all lattice sites
i
in
W
.
\text{wt}(i)=1
for the interior points and
\text{wt}(i)=\theta_{i}/(2\pi)
if the site
i
is on the boundary of
W
, where
\theta_{i}\in[0,2\pi)
is the angle subtended by the interior of
W

[
21
]
.
Q_{i}
is the ground state expectation value of the charge on site
i
.
Let
q_{\star}
be the minimal quasiparticle charge. We find that
Q_{W}\mod q_{\star}
is universal, independent of perturbations, and can be decomposed into distinct contributions:

\displaystyle Q_{W}=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}{S}_{\text{o}}+\vec{L}_{\text{o}}\cdot\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}+\nu n_{W,\text{o}}+\sigma_{H}\delta\Phi_{W,\text{o}}\mod q_{\star}

(2)

Here
\Gamma
,
\vec{L}_{\text{o}}
, and
n_{W,\text{o}}
are certain geometrical quantities that depend on the subregion enclosed by
W
, which keep track of corner and disclination angles, vectorized edge lengths, and number of unit cells, respectively. See
[
25
]
for a detailed discussion, which we briefly review below.

Consider a loop
\gamma_{\text{o}}
in
W
obtained by a sequence of elementary lattice translations starting at
o
.
\gamma_{\text{o}}
is far from any lattice defects and lattice boundaries, and is smoothly deformable to the boundary
\partial W
without crossing any lattice defects or boundaries. The vectorized length
\vec{L}_{\text{o}}\equiv\sum_{j\in\gamma_{\text{o}}}\hat{L}_{j}
is the sum of unit translation vectors
\hat{L}_{j}\in\{\pm\hat{x},\pm\hat{y}\}
over
\gamma_{\text{o}}
.
j
indicate points on
\gamma_{\text{o}}
related by the translations, all
j
points correspond to the same MWP as
o
.
When
\gamma_{\text{o}}
only encloses a dislocation,
\vec{L}_{\text{o}}
is the Burgers vector
\vec{b}_{\text{o}}=\vec{L}_{\text{o}}
. The term
\vec{L}_{\text{o}}\cdot\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}
thus indicates the universal contribution from lattice dislocations, impure lattice disclinations, and lattice boundaries.

\Gamma
is defined as
\Gamma\equiv 2\pi-\sum_{j\in\gamma_{\text{o}}}K_{j}
, where
K_{j}
is the geodesic curvature of the loop
\gamma_{\text{o}}
at
j
. Specifically,
K_{j}=\pi-\theta_{j}
where
\theta_{j}
is the angle subtended by the inside of the loop at
j
. We define
\theta_{j}
to be in the range
0<\theta_{j}<2\pi
, which enables us to define
\Gamma
absolutely instead of just modulo
2\pi
. When
\gamma_{\text{o}}
only encloses a disclination, the disclination angle
\Omega_{\text{disc}}=\Gamma
; when
\gamma_{\text{o}}
only encloses a boundary with corner(s), the total corner angle
\Omega_{\text{cor}}
can be determined with
\Omega_{\text{cor}}=\Gamma-2\pi
. Therefore the term
\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}{S}_{\text{o}}
above indicates the universal contribution from lattice disclinations and corners.

In the presence of crystalline symmetry, each parton state is labeled by a set of many-body crystalline invariants. These invariants were recently classified systematically for the five orientation-preserving wallpaper groups in
[
23
,
24
]
. For this paper, we need only keep track of the electric polarization and discrete shift for each parton state,
\vec{{P}}_{\text{o},p}
,
{S}_{\text{o},p}
, in addition to the filling (charge per unit cell)
\nu_{p}
for each parton state, which must be the same as the filling
\nu
of the projective wave function:
\nu=\nu_{p}
. Each parton state has a flux per unit cell
\phi_{p}
.
The projected state effectively has a flux per unit cell
\phi=\sum_{p}\phi_{p}
, because the microscopic degrees of freedom are a composite of all
k
partons. That is, the particle annihilation operator is
c=\prod_{p=1}^{k}f_{i}
, where
f_{i}
are the individual parton annihilation operators.

The parton construction introduces a
U(1)^{k-1}
gauge symmetry, associated with the transformations
f_{i}\rightarrow e^{i\theta_{i}}f_{i}
which keep the microscopic physical operator
c
gauge invariant. One can then derive an effective field theory associated with the projected wave function by starting with the effective field theories of each individual parton state and coupling them to a
U(1)^{k-1}
gauge field. This allows us to determine
\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}
and
{S}_{\text{o}}
of the FCI in terms of
\vec{{P}}_{\text{o},p}
,
{S}_{\text{o},p}
for each parton state. We carry out this calculation in Appendix
C
, with the result:

\displaystyle{S}_{\text{o}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{p=1}^{m}{S}_{\text{o},p}\mod M_{\text{o}}/k,

\displaystyle\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{p=1}^{m}\vec{{P}}_{\text{o},p}\mod\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{Z}^{2}

(5)

Ref.
[
22
]
showed how
\vec{{P}}_{\text{o},p}
and
{S}_{\text{o},p}
transform linearly under a shift of origin
\text{o}\rightarrow\text{o}+\vec{v}
. Linearity of Eq.
5
then implies the same transformation rules apply to
\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}
and
{S}_{\text{o}}
for the projected states. These transformation rules are summarized in Table

in Appendix

Numerical Calculations

We now numerically verify the charge response Eq. (
2
) and demonstrate the universal fractionally quantized contributions from lattice disclinations, dislocations, corners, and boundaries. We use model wave functions obtained using the parton construction. Depending on the parton invariants,
{S}_{\text{o},p}
and
\vec{{P}}_{\text{o},p}

-Laughlin states with different values of quantized electric polarization
\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}
and discrete shift
{S}_{\text{o}}
to validate the general theory.
In this section, we will focus on

\text{o}=\alpha,\beta
where
M_{\text{o}}=4
. We also study
1/3
-Laughlin states on the honeycomb lattice with disclinations and dislocations for origins
\text{o}=\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
where
M_{\text{o}}=3
, and
\text{o}=\alpha
where
M_{\alpha}=6
.

For the parton states, we use the ground states of the Harper-Hofstadter model of free fermions with flux
\phi_{p}
per unit cell and filling
\nu_{p}
. See Appendix
D
for additional details. For the square lattice with
M=4
, we use the convention that
\beta
represents vertices and
\alpha
represents plaquette centers. For the honeycomb lattice with
M=6
, the only choice of unit cell places
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{2}
at the vertices,

To obtain the FCI wave functions on the lattices with defects, we first construct the free fermion parton states on the lattices with defects, using methods discussed in

Discussion

Our results demonstrate how microscopic charge measurements can be used to extract the invariants
\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}
and
{S}_{\text{o}}
. These invariants were originally predicted in
[
20
,
19
]
using the more abstract setting of topological quantum field theory and
G
-crossed modular category theory
[
53
]
; the agreement of these abstract methods with concrete microscopic computations is remarkable. Note that these invariants can also be extracted in FCIs using partial rotation operations
[
54

, giving a remarkable example of duality in quantum many-body systems. The fractional quantized electric polarization

and are thus invariants fundamentally tied to the crystalline setting.

Appendix B
Review of topological crystalline gauge theory

In this section, we review the topological crystalline gauge theory derived in
[
19
]
to describe crystalline invariants for Abelian topological orders. We consider Abelian topological orders, which can be described by a
U(1)^{D}
Chern-Simons theory and an integer symmetric
K
-matrix. For now we consider bosonic topological orders, for which the diagonal entries of
K
are even integers. We discuss the fermionic case afterwards.

We consider the global symmetry group

G=U(1)\times_{\phi}[\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{M}],

(21)

where
\mathbb{Z}_{M}
(for
M=2,3,4,6
) represents the point group rotational symmetry about a fixed point
o
. The
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
denotes translation symmetry in the
x
and
y
directions, generated by
\tilde{T}_{x}
and
\tilde{T}_{y}
. Here,
\phi
is the
U(1)
flux per unit cell. The notation
\times_{\phi}
indicates a central extension of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
by
U(1)
, corresponding to the magnetic translation algebra:
\tilde{T}_{y}^{-1}\tilde{T}_{x}^{-1}\tilde{T}_{y}\tilde{T}_{x}=e^{i\phi\hat{N}}
, where
\hat{N}
is the number operator.

As discussed in
[
19
,
20
]
, topological phases with crystalline symmetry can be characterized and classified by developing a topological field theory with a background gauge field for
G
. Specifically, we have a
G
gauge field
B=(A,\vec{R},\omega_{\text{o}})
, where
A
is a background
U(1)
gauge field,
\vec{R}
is a background
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
gauge field, and
\omega_{\text{o}}
is a background
\mathbb{Z}_{M}
gauge field for the
M
-fold rotations about
o
. In what follows, for ease of notation we will drop the subscript
o
in
\omega_{\text{o}}
, but keep it in the coupling constants.

To develop the topological field theory, we first triangulate the space-time manifold
\mathcal{M}^{3}
. Then we define gauge fields on 1-simplices
ij
of the triangulation. That is, we have
A_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\vec{R}_{ij}\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
\omega_{ij}\in\frac{2\pi}{M}\mathbb{Z}
. Technically
A
and
\omega
are lifts of
U(1)
and
\mathbb{Z}_{M}
to
\mathbb{R}
and
\frac{2\pi}{M}\mathbb{Z}
, respectively.

The topological terms can be derived in the following way. We start by considering an invertible theory with
U(1)^{D}\times G
symmetry. The topological terms are classified by group cohomology:
[
56
,
57
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{3}(U(1)^{D}\times G,\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})\simeq\mathcal{H}^{3}(U(1)^{D},\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})\oplus\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}^{D})\oplus\mathcal{H}^{3}(G,\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}).

(22)

Here we have used the Künneth decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{3}(U(1)^{D}\times G,\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{3}\mathcal{H}^{3-k}(G,\mathcal{H}^{k}(U(1)^{D},\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})),

(23)

together with the fact that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(U(1)^{D},\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}^{D}
and
\mathcal{H}^{2}(U(1)^{D},\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
is trivial. An element of
\mathcal{H}^{3}(U(1)^{D}\times G,\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
can thus be written as
([\alpha_{K}],[\alpha_{\text{frac}}],[\alpha_{\text{SPT}}])
, with
[\alpha_{K}]\in\mathcal{H}^{3}(U(1)^{D},\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
,
[\alpha_{\text{frac}}]\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}^{D})
, and
[\alpha_{\text{SPT}}]\in\mathcal{H}^{3}(G,\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})
.

Given flat
U(1)^{D}
gauge fields
a^{I}
,
I=1,\cdots,D
, and a flat
G
gauge field
(A,\vec{R},\omega)
, we can pull back these cohomology classes to the space-time manifold to get a topological action with Lagrangian density

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}K_{IJ}a^{I}\cup da^{J}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{frac}}+\mathcal{L}_{SPT},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{frac}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}a^{I}\cup(v_{I}dA+s_{\text{o},I}d\omega+\vec{t}_{\text{o},I}\cdot(1-U(2\pi/M)^{T})^{-1}d\vec{R}+m_{I}A_{XY}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{SPT}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{\sigma}_{H,SPT}}{4\pi}A\cup dA+\frac{{S}_{SPT,\text{o}}}{2\pi}A\cup d\omega+\frac{\vec{{P}}_{SPT,\text{o}}}{2\pi}\cdot A\cup d\vec{R}+\frac{\kappa_{SPT}}{2\pi}A\cup A_{XY}+\cdots

(24)

Here
d
is the coboundary operator,
\cup
denotes the cup product, and
A_{XY}
is a 2-cochain whose explicit formula in terms of
\vec{R}
and
\omega
is given in
[
19
]
. The
\cdots
refers to additional terms that do not affect the charge response and do not concern us in this paper; see
[
19
,
20
]
for details. Repeated indices are implicitly summed over. The
K
-matrix specifies the cohomology class
[\alpha_{K}]
. The SPT coefficients
{S}_{SPT,\text{o}}
,
\kappa_{SPT}
are integers,
\bar{\sigma}_{H,SPT}
is an even integer, and
(1-U(2\pi/M)^{T})\vec{{P}}_{SPT,\text{o}}
is an integer vector. Similarly,
\vec{v}
,
\vec{s}_{\text{o}}
,
\vec{m}
,
\vec{t}_{\text{o},x},\vec{t}_{\text{o},y}
are
D
-component integer vectors.

Note that since
\omega
describes a
\mathbb{Z}_{M}
gauge field,
M\omega
is trivial, so we have an equivalence

\displaystyle s_{\text{o},I}\simeq s_{\text{o},I}+M.

(25)

In particular, a term of the form
M\int_{\mathcal{M}^{3}}a^{I}\cup d\omega=M\int_{\mathcal{M}^{3}}\omega\cup da^{I}=2\pi\mathbb{Z}
is trivial on a closed space-time manifold
\mathcal{M}^{3}
. Analogously,
(1-U(2\pi/M)^{T})\vec{R}
is also trivial, which implies the equivalence

\displaystyle\vec{t}_{\text{o},I}\sim\vec{t}_{\text{o},I}+(1-U(2\pi/M)^{T})\vec{\Lambda},

(26)

where
\vec{\Lambda}
is an integer vector.

Next, we gauge the
U(1)^{D}
symmetry by promoting
a
to a dynamical
U(1)^{D}
gauge field. The symmetry fractionalization is specified by the terms in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{frac}}
, involving the charge vector
\vec{v}
, the spin vector
\vec{s}_{\text{o}}
, the discrete torsion vector
\vec{t}_{\text{o}}
, and the area vector
\vec{m}
, which are all integer vectors. Since
a
is dynamical, we can shift the gauge fields
a^{I}\rightarrow a^{I}+\Lambda_{I}A
, where
\Lambda_{I}
is an integer, without affecting the path integral. This induces the remaining equivalence relations mentioned in Appendix
A
, and has the effect of reducing the classification of topological terms in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{frac}}
to
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}^{D}/K\mathbb{Z}^{D})\simeq\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathcal{A})
, which is the known classification of symmetry fractionalization. Here
\mathcal{A}\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{D}/K\mathbb{Z}^{D}
is the Abelian group formed by fusion of the Abelian anyons.

Fermionic topological orders, where
K
can have diagonal entries that are odd, are treated similarly, with a few minor modifications. As described in Appendix
A
, the spin vector
\vec{s}_{\text{o}}
should have the property that
\vec{s}_{\text{o}}\cdot\vec{\Lambda}=1/2\mod 1
whenever
\vec{\Lambda}^{T}K\vec{\Lambda}
is odd. We also can assume without loss of generality that the SPT contributions correspond to stacking with fermionic SPTs which have zero chiral central charge, in which case all other couplings have the same quantization as in the bosonic case. We note that a more systematic derivation of the topological field theory for fermionic systems, which also applies to non-Abelian topological orders, can use the more sophisticated categorical formalism developed in
[
58
,
59
,
60
]
.

To understand the topological response properties, we formally integrate out the dynamical gauge field

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{resp}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{H}}{2}A\cup dA+\frac{{S}_{\text{o}}}{2\pi}A\cup d\omega+\frac{\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}}{2\pi}A\cup d\vec{R}

\displaystyle+\kappa A\cup A_{XY}+\cdots,

(27)

where

\displaystyle\sigma_{H}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}(\vec{v}^{T}K^{-1}\vec{v}+\bar{\sigma}_{H,SPT})

\displaystyle{S}_{\text{o}}

\displaystyle=\vec{v}^{T}K^{-1}\vec{s}_{\text{o}}+{S}_{SPT,\text{o}}

\displaystyle\vec{{P}}_{\text{o},i}

\displaystyle=[1-U(2\pi/M_{\text{o}})^{T}]^{-1}_{ij}\vec{v}^{T}K^{-1}\vec{t}_{\text{o},j}+\vec{{P}}_{SPT,\text{o}}

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=\vec{v}^{T}K^{-1}m+\kappa_{SPT}

(28)

The above implies a charge response

\displaystyle Q_{W}

\displaystyle=\int_{W}\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta A_{0}}=\sigma_{H}\frac{\Phi_{W}}{2\pi}+{S}_{\text{o}}\frac{\Omega_{\text{disc}}}{2\pi}+\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}\cdot\vec{b}+\kappa n_{W},

(29)

where
\Phi_{W}=\int_{W}dA
is the flux in
W
,
\Omega_{disc}=\int_{W}d\omega
is the disclination angle in
W
(note that
\omega
was a lift, so
\Omega_{disc}
is defined as a real number, not just modulo
2\pi
),
\vec{b}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{W}d\vec{R}
is the Burgers vector in region
W
,
n_{W}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{W}A_{XY}
is the number of unit cells in
W
, and
A_{0}
is the time-component of
A
. Defining
\delta\Phi_{W}:=\Phi_{W}-\phi n_{W}
and using the relationship between the filling
\nu
and
\kappa
:

\displaystyle\nu=\phi\sigma_{H}+\kappa,

(30)

Eq. (
29
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle Q_{W}=\sigma_{H}\frac{\delta\Phi_{W}}{2\pi}+{S}_{\text{o}}\frac{\Omega_{\text{disc}}}{2\pi}+\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}\cdot\vec{b}+\nu n_{W}.

(31)

So far, the topological action has been defined for flat gauge fields. This translates to the requirement that for any
region
W
,
\Phi_{W}/2\pi
,
\Omega_{\text{disc}}/2\pi
,
(1-U(2\pi/M))^{-1}\vec{b}
, and
n_{W}
are integer-valued
[
19
]
. Nevertheless, we can use the effective action to deduce the response to non-flat gauge field configurations, which corresponds to the presence of lattice disclinations and dislocations. This leads to the prediction:

\displaystyle Q_{W}=\sigma_{H}\frac{\delta\Phi_{W,\text{o}}}{2\pi}+{S}_{\text{o}}\frac{\Omega_{\text{disc}}}{2\pi}+\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}\cdot\vec{b}_{\text{o}}+\nu n_{W,\text{o}}\mod q_{\star}.

(32)

Here we have incorporated the fact that in the presence of lattice disclinations, the Burgers vector
\vec{b}_{\text{o}}
depends on
o
. We have also incorporated the fact that there may be fractional, irregular unit cells, and their counting empirically is
o
-dependent, leading to the
o
-dependence of
n_{W,\text{o}}
and
\delta\Phi_{W,\text{o}}
. Finally, we take
Q_{W}
modulo
q_{\star}
because of the equivalences on
{S}_{\text{o}}
and
\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}
. Physically the
\mod q_{\star}
arises because any local potential at the core of the defect can trap an anyon, changing the charge by a multiple of
q_{\star}
, and this is also beyond the topological field theory description.

As explained in
[
25
]
, we can treat corner angles and vectorized edge lengths on the same footing as disclination angles and Burgers vectors, so we can replace
\Omega_{\text{disc}}
and
\vec{b}_{\text{o}}
in the above equation with
\Gamma
and
\vec{L}_{\text{o}}
to get Eq. (
2
).

Appendix C
Parton effective field theory

Here we use the parton effective field theory to derive the invariants of the FCI model wave functions in terms of the invariants of the parton states.

Each parton state is defined by its own effective field theory. For the 1/2-Laughlin states, the full effective Lagrangian consists of the effective field theories for each parton state coupled to a dynamical
U(1)
gauge field
\alpha
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}a\cup da+\frac{1}{2\pi}(\alpha+A+{S}_{\text{o},1}\omega+\vec{{P}}_{\text{o},1}\cdot\vec{R})\cup da

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\pi}b\cup db+\frac{1}{2\pi}(-\alpha+{S}_{\text{o},2}\omega+\vec{{P}}_{\text{o},2}\cdot\vec{R})\cup db.

Here
a,b
are dynamical
\text{U}(1)
gauge fields describing the two parton species whose field strength describes the conserved current of each parton. Integrating out
\alpha
give us the constraint that
a=b
up to a gauge transformation.
The effective field theory for
1/k
-Laughlin states can be similarly derived with
k
parton flavors and
k-1
internal
\text{U}(1)
gauge field between them. Integrating out the internal gauge fields outputs the coupling:

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\kappa_{p},

\displaystyle\vec{t}_{\text{o}}

\displaystyle=[1-U(2\pi/M_{\text{o}})^{T}]\sum_{p}\vec{{P}}_{\text{o},p}

\displaystyle s_{\text{o}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}{S}_{\text{o},p},

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}_{H}

\displaystyle={S}_{SPT,\text{o}}=\vec{{P}}_{SPT,\text{o}}=\kappa_{SPT}=0.

(34)

Plugging into Eq. (
28
), we have

\displaystyle{S}_{\text{o}}

\displaystyle=s_{\text{o}}/k=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{p=1}^{k}{S}_{\text{o},p},

\displaystyle\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}

\displaystyle=[1-U(2\pi/M_{\text{o}})^{T}]^{-1}\vec{t}_{\text{o}}/k=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\vec{{P}}_{\text{o},p},

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=m/k=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{p=1}^{k}\kappa_{p}.

(35)

For invertible states,
{S}_{\text{o}}
and
\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}
transform under a shift of origin
\text{o}\rightarrow\text{o}+\vec{v}
following Table
3
. Note that every transformation rule is linear in
{S}_{\text{o}}
and
\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}
. For the projected wave functions, Eqs. (
34
)(
35
) shows that
{S}_{\text{o}}
,
\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}
is linear in the shift
{S}_{\text{o},p}
and polarization
\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}
of each parton state. Therefore the transformations of Table
3
are expected to hold for FCIs by linearity. Our numerical calculations confirm this.

Appendix D
Numerical Methods

D.1
Constructing FCI wave function from ground states of Hofstadter model

In this section, we review the construction of projected wave functions from the ground states of the free fermion Hofstadter model. The Hofstadter model is defined by the Hamiltonian

H=-t\sum_{<ij>}e^{-iA_{ij}}f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{j}+h.c.

(36)

where
i,j
are site indices, and
A_{ij}
represents the vector potential that threads
\phi
flux per unit cell.
The ground state is characterized by the topological invariants
\{c_{-},C,\kappa,\Theta_{\text{o}}^{\pm}\}

[
23
]
. It turns out that for the Hofstadter model, the many-body ground states with
C=\pm 1
can be uniquely labeled by
C,\kappa
(though this is generally not true for states with
|C|>1
); we label these
C=\pm 1
states as
\ket{\psi_{C,\kappa}}
. For these
C=\pm 1
states, once
C
and
\kappa
are specified, the corresponding Hofstadter lobes can be identified, and the quantities
{S}_{\text{o}}
and
\vec{{P}}_{\text{o}}
can be determined as described in
[
21
,
22
]
. We can then define different flavors of parton ground states labeled by
p
,
\{\ket{\psi_{C_p,\kappa_p}}\}
.
These parton ground states must be defined on the same lattice and have the same filling, but they need not share the same topological invariants.

The FCI wave function is then created by projecting these parton ground states. The 1/2-Laughlin states require two parton flavors with
C_{1}=C_{2}=1
, and their wave functions are constructed by projecting the partons to the same locations:

\displaystyle\Psi_{1/2}(\{r_{i}\})=\psi_{C_{1},\kappa_{1}}(\{r_{i}\})\psi_{C_{2},\kappa_{2}}(\{r_{i}\})

(37)

The 1/3-Laughlin states require three parton flavors with
C_{1}=C_{2}=C_{3}=1
, and their wave functions are written as

\displaystyle\Psi_{1/3}(\{r_{i}\})=\psi_{C_{1},\kappa_{1}}(\{r_{i}\})\psi_{C_{2},\kappa_{2}}(\{r_{i}\})\psi_{C_{3},\kappa_{3}}(\{r_{i}\})

(38)

The ground states
\psi_{C,\kappa}(\{r_{i}\})
with
N
particles are Slater determinant states:

\displaystyle\psi_{C,\kappa}(\{r_{i}\})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\text{Det}(\mathcal{M}),

(39)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{ij}=\chi_{i}(r_{j}),

(40)

and
\chi_{i}
are the single particle eigenstates.

D.2
Variational Monte Carlo

In this section, we outline the process of sampling the projected wave function
\ket{\Psi}
from parton construction using the Metropolis Monte Carlo method.

We aim to calculate the charge at each site
Q_{i}=\bra{\Psi}\hat{n}_{i}\ket{\Psi}
, where
\hat{n}_{i}
is the number operator. The expectation value
Q_{i}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\bra{\Psi}\hat{n}_{i}\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r_{1},r_{2},\dots r_{N}}\langle\Psi|\hat{n}_{i}|r_{1},r_{2},\dots r_{N}\rangle\langle r_{1},r_{2},\dots r_{N}|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r_{1},r_{2},\dots r_{N}}\frac{\bra{\Psi}\hat{n}_{i}\ket{r_1,r_2,\dots r_{N}}}{\bra{\Psi}\ket{r_1,r_2,\dots r_{N}}}P(r_{1},r_{2},\dots r_{N}),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{r_{1},r_{2},\dots r_{N}}\delta_{i}P(r_{1},r_{2},\dots r_{N})

(41)

where
P(r_{1},r_{2},\dots r_{N}):=|\bra{\Psi}\ket{r_1,r_2,\dots r_{N}}|^{2}
is the probability of the configuration
\ket{r_1,r_2,\dots r_{N}}
, and
\delta_{i}
is defined as

\displaystyle\delta_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }r_{i}\in\{r_{1},r_{2},\dots r_{N}\}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(42)

This expectation value
Q_{i}
is sampled using the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm
[
61
]
through the following steps:

1.

Start with a random configuration
\ket{r_1,r_2,\dots r_{N}}

2.

Propose
a new configuration
\ket{\tilde{r}_1,\tilde{r}_2,\dots\tilde{r}_{N}}
by moving one particle to a random unfilled position.
Update
to this new configuration if

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\bra{\Psi}\ket{\tilde{r}_1,\tilde{r}_2\dots\tilde{r}_{N}}}{\bra{\Psi}\ket{r_1,r_2,\dots r_{N}}}\right|^{2}>q,

where
q
is a random real number between 0 to 1 (
q
is chosen randomly at each step). Otherwise
Reject
the proposal and revert to the old configuration
\ket{r_1,r_2,\dots r_{N}}
if the above check is false.

3.

Repeat Step 2. The expectation value of
Q_{i}
in Eq. (
41
) is obtained as the average of
\delta_{i}
over the sample trajectory (with the first few iterations discarded as they do not converge to the target distribution).
