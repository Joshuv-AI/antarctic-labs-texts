Validation of the new IMS/IMWG consensus genomic staging (CGS) of high‐risk multiple myeloma (HRMM) in a contemporary cohort of 1209 patients
Authors: Efstathios Kastritis, Charalampos Filippatos, Maria Gavriatopoulou, Foteini Theodorakakou, Eirini Solia, Ioannis Ntanasis‐Stathopoulos, Panagiotis Malandrakis, Nikolaos Kanellias, Evangelos Eleutherakis‐Papaiakovou, Magdalini Migkou, Vasiliki Spiliopoulou, Ilias Katsadouros, Aikaterini Vlachou, Despina Fotiou, Konstantinos Giannakas, Erasmia Boutakoglou, Stavroula Giannouli, Panagiotis Tsirigotis, Asimina Papanikolaou, Evangelos Terpos, Meletios A Dimopoulos

ABSTRACT
AbstractDue to its high clinical and biological complexity, multiple myeloma (MM) is a malignancy of heterogeneous prognoses and outcomes. The International Myeloma Society (IMS)/International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) formulated a consensus genomic staging (CGS) of high‐risk MM (HRMM), aiming to refine and homogenize the classification of high‐risk disease. We evaluated the new HRMM criteria in a cohort of 1209 consecutive newly diagnosed MM patients with complete CGS data (except TP53 mutations), treated in a single center with contemporary regimens, including triplets and quadruplets, between 2010 and 2024. Based on CGS criteria, 25.2% of our cohort's patients were classified as HRMM. High‐risk status was associated with significantly inferior outcomes: median overall survival (OS) was 44 months for HRMM versus 93 months for standard‐risk (SR) patients (hazard ratio [HR] 1.81; P < 0.001), corresponding to 5‐year OS rates of 40% versus 61%. Similarly, median progression‐free survival was 21.3 months for HRMM versus 36 months for SR (HR 1.64; P < 0.001). The presence of ≥2 high‐risk features identified an ultra‐high‐risk group (4.4% of patients) with a threefold increased risk of death, compared to SR patients. CGS remained prognostic irrespective of renal dysfunction, stratifying patients with sCr > 1.2 mg/dL (HR 1.71; P < 0.001) and retained prognostic significance across different age groups, transplant eligibility, and treatment eras (pre‐ and post‐2020). The IMS/IMWG CGS effectively stratified patients in a large cohort treated at a tertiary academic center, identifying a substantially high‐risk population with poor outcomes despite modern therapies. This validation supports its deployment in clinical practice and research to enhance the precision of risk‐adapted management.

INTRODUCTION

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a biologically complex plasma cell neoplasm characterized by significant variability in patient outcomes despite advancements in therapeutic agents and treatment regimens. 1 , 2 Prognostication systems such as the International Staging System (ISS) and its subsequent revisions, the Revised International Staging System (R‐ISS) and its update (R2‐ISS), have been widely adopted for risk stratification and treatment planning in clinical practice; however, these models were largely derived and validated in cohorts treated with earlier‐generation therapies, which may limit their relevance in the era of novel combinations and immunotherapies.3, 4, 5 As our knowledge in the biology and genetics of MM expands, 6 , 7 , 8 new genetic lesions have emerged as significant prognostic factors; moreover, sensitive tools are now widely available (such as NGS) and are routinely used in many hematologic malignancies providing valuable prognosis and predictive information. 9 , 10

To address these limitations and expand applicability, the International Myeloma Society (IMS) and International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) recently developed a consensus genomic staging (CGS) system for high‐risk multiple myeloma (HRMM), based mainly on the presence of cytogenetic abnormalities and less on biochemical markers, to more precisely define patients at higher risk. High‐risk features, per the consensus, include the presence of particular chromosomal deletions and translocations—such as del(17p) or TP53 mutation, IgH translocations [t(4;14), t(14;16), t(14;20)] in combination with abnormalities in chromosome 1, combined abnormalities in chromosome 1 and also with consideration for β2‐microglobulin (sβ2m) in the context of preserved renal function. 11

Importantly, this new CGS model was established primarily from large clinical trial cohorts but remains to be validated in large, independent patient cohorts. 12 , 13 , 14 In this study, we validated the IMS/IMWG HRMM definition in a contemporary, single‐tertiary center cohort of 1209 newly diagnosed MM (NDMM) patients.

METHODS

A total of 1209 consecutive NDMM patients diagnosed and managed at the Department of Clinical Therapeutics, Athens, Greece, between 2010 and 2024 with complete clinical and FISH‐derived cytogenetic assessment were included. TP53 mutational status was available in very few patients, and chromosome 1p abnormalities were assessed only by FISH and not by NGS. Data collected at baseline included demographics, conventional laboratory values, cytogenetic/FISH analyses for high‐risk abnormalities, β2‐microglobulin, serum creatinine (for renal function), and staging at diagnosis per ISS, R‐ISS, and R2‐ISS. The study has been approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB#: 570/03/11/2025).

CGS staging

The evaluation of CGS (high risk [HR] vs. non‐high risk [SR]) was based on FISH data only. Based on the new IMS/IMWG definitions patients were classified as high‐risk based on the new IMS/IMWG criteria which require at least one of the following: (A) del(17p) (>20% clonal fraction), (B) presence of IgH translocations [t(4;14), t(14;16), t(14;20)] plus 1q+ and/or del1p32, (C) presence of monoallelic del1p32 plus 1q+ or biallelic del1p32, and (D) a serum β2‐microglobulin ≥ 5.5 mg/L with normal renal function (serum creatinine < 1.2 mg/dL). All FISH evaluations were performed in CD138+ selected cells. A cutoff of 10% of positive cells was used for IgH translocations and 20% for chromosome 1 abnormalities.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize population demographics and baseline clinical characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as medians accompanied by range, while categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Progression‐free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from treatment start to disease progression or death from any cause, and overall survival (OS) as the time from treatment start to death from any cause, respectively, or censored at last follow‐up. Survival functions were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed with the log‐rank test. The median follow‐up was estimated by the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to evaluate the association of the new CGS HRMM criteria with PFS and OS. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals.

Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided P‐value < 0.05 across this manuscript. All analyses were performed using R/R‐Studio Version 2025.05.0+496 (2025.05.0+496), and Sankey plots were created with the ggven package in R.

RESULTS

Cohort description

Between 2010 and 2024, 1558 patients with previously untreated MM started therapy in our department; 1209 (76%) patients had full data for cytogenetics and were included in the analysis. Among 349 patients (22.4%) that were excluded due to missing data for CGS, reasons were missing data for del17p in 161, for chromosome 1 abnormalities in 201, t(4;14) in 181, t(14;16) in 283, t(14;20) in 287, and baseline b2‐microglobulin in 10. The outcomes of patients with missing data were not different from those included in the analysis (Figure S1).

The median age of the analyzed cohort was 68 years, 61.3% were older than 65 years, and 15.1% were 80 years or older, while there was a slight predominance of male patients (56%).

ISS stage distribution at diagnosis was 31% for Stage I, 30% for Stage II, and 39% for Stage III. For Revised ISS (R‐ISS), the distribution for Stages I, II, and III was 23%, 59%, and 17.5%, respectively, while for the R2‐ISS, the distribution was 19% for Stage I, 26% for Stage II, 45% for Stage III, and 11% for Stage IV (Table 1).

Thirty‐five percent (n = 419) of patients had serum creatinine > 1.2 mg/dL at diagnosis; the median eGFR (calculated by the CKD‐EPI formula) was 75 mL/min/1.73 m2, and 18.5% of the patients in the cohort had an eGFR less than 30 mL/min/1.73 m2.

Treatment regimens reflected changing standards between 2010 and 2024, with 37% receiving combinations of proteasome inhibitors and immunomodulatory drugs, 20% received regimens with anti‐CD38 monoclonal antibodies, and 29% underwent high‐dose therapy with autologous stem cell transplantation (HDM‐ASCT); 86% received maintenance after ASCT and in total 74% of the patients in the whole cohort received maintenance or were on continuous therapy (Tables 1 and S1).

CGS

Using the new IMS/IMWG definition, 25.2% of the analyzed cohort was classified as HRMM. For patients with a sCr < 1.2 mg/dL, 29.5% met the HRMM criteria and were rated as such based on the presence of del17p in 9.0%, on IgH/Chr1abn in 8.0%, on Chr1abn alone in 4.0%, and on β2m ≥ 5.5 mg/L in 17.0% (Figure S2).

The number of HR features in those with sCr < 1.2 mg/dL was one in 188 (24.0%), two in 40 (5.0%), and three in 5 (0.6%) patients. For patients with a sCr > 1.2 mg/dL (for which the b2‐microglobulin criterion is not applicable), 17.0% met HRMM, based on the presence of del17p in 11.0%, of IgH/Chr1abn in 6.0%, and Chr1abn only in 1.5%; most often, patients had a single HR feature (Figure 1).

We further assessed the distribution of HR and SR patients by the novel HRMM criteria across established clinical staging systems. The application of the novel criteria revealed significant heterogeneity within the traditional ISS stages, identifying HR patients even within the low‐risk ISS‐1 group (13.4%). This discordance persisted to a lesser degree in the R‐ISS, where the highest risk category (R‐ISS‐3) comprised only 57.7% HR patients. The novel criteria showed the strongest concordance with the R2‐ISS stratification; notably, the R2‐ISS‐1 category was entirely free of CGS high‐risk patients (0.0% HR), while the R2‐ISS‐4 category demonstrated the highest enrichment for high‐risk disease (76.4%) (Table S2).

Outcomes

The median follow‐up of the whole cohort was 5.25 years, and the median calculated OS is 75 months (95% CI: 67–84).

The median OS for HR (vs. SR) patients was 44 versus 93 months (HR = 1.81, 95% CI: 1.51–2.20, P < 0.001) corresponding to a 5‐year survival rate or 61% versus 40%, respectively (Figure 3A). The median PFS for HRMM versus SRMM was 21.3 versus 36 months (HR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.40–1.90, P < 0.001) corresponding to a 5‐year PFS rate or 36% versus 18%, respectively (Figure 3B). Among patients with SR per CGS, 11% relapsed within 12 months from the start of therapy and 18.4% within 18 months. However, among patients treated with CD38‐based regimens, only 6% of SR per CGS progressed within 18 months.

Deletion 17p (del17p)

The presence of deletion 17p was associated with poor outcomes. Patients harboring the abnormality had a median OS of 35.7 months, compared to 78.8 months for those who did not (P < 0.001, Figure 4A), translating into a twofold increase in the risk of death (HR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.50–2.50). This adverse prognosis also carried over to PFS, as the median for patients with the abnormality was 17.9 months, compared to 33.3 months for those without (P < 0.001, Table 2 and Figure S3A). The characteristics of patients with isolated del 17 are shown in Table S3. In Figure S4, the impact of isolated del17p in comparison to other CGS‐defined high‐risk genetic abnormalities and their co‐occurrence is shown. In Figure S5, the outcomes of patients with isolated del17p before and after 2020 are shown, suggesting a trend (although non‐statistically significant) for improvement in PFS and OS in more recent years. Among the 96 patients with isolated del17p, 44 received also maintenance (mostly with lenalidomide); the respective PFS was 29 months versus 16 months for those that did not (P = 0.018), and OS was 51 versus 31 months (but did not reach statistical significance, P = 0.168).

IgH translocations and chromosome 1 abnormalities

IgH translocations coinciding with chromosome 1 abnormalities (1q+ or del1p) had an effect similar to that of del17p. More specifically, patients harboring them had a median OS of 36.7 months, compared to 78.7 months for those without (P < 0.001, Figure 4B). These patients demonstrated more than two times increased mortality (HR = 2.14, 95% CI: 1.60–2.80, P < 0.001). The median PFS was 18.3 months for patients with the abnormalities and 32.1 months for those without (P < 0.001, Table 2 and Figure S3B).

Isolated chromosome 1 abnormalities

Patients with isolated chromosome 1 abnormalities (1q+ or del1p) demonstrated a median OS of 29.9 months, compared to 75.4 months for those without (P < 0.001, Figure 4C). While these individuals had more than 2.5‐times the risk of death (HR = 2.60, 95% CI: 1.20–5.70, P = 0.015), no differences were noted for PFS (median, 27.9 vs. 31.2 months; P = 0.769, Figure S3C).

Serum β2‐microglobulin ≥ 5.5 mg/L

The impact of increased β2m was evaluated separately in patients with and without sCr < 1.2 mg/dL. Among those with low serum creatinine, high sβ2m was strongly associated with worse OS (HR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.80–2.90, P < 0.001); median OS was 44.4 versus 104.7 months for those with low sβ2m, and 5‐year OS rates were 43% versus 68% (P < 0.001, Figure 5A). Similar was the case for PFS, as the median PFS for patients with high sβ2m levels was less than half (20.1 months) than those with low levels (44.9 months) (Figure S3D).

Overall survival, stratified by the consensus genomic staging (CGS) high‐risk multiple myeloma (HRMM), among patients with (A) sCR < 1.2 mg/dL and (B) sCR ≥ 1.2 mg/dL. HR, high risk; SR, standard risk.

We also evaluated the prognostic impact of high sβ2m only in patients with low serum creatinine but without other high‐risk genetic features (n = 656): among them, 15% had elevated sβ2m and had significantly shorter PFS (median 24 vs. 47.6 months, P < 0.001) and OS (5‐year OS 71% vs. 52%, P < 0.001). Among such patients, there was a trend of improved outcomes (PFS and OS) after 2020, which, however, did not reach statistical significance (Figure S6).

Overall survival of consensus genomic staging (CGS) high‐risk patients, stratified by the number of high‐risk multiple myeloma (HRMM) features.

Among patients with elevated serum creatinine (≥1.2 mg/dL, n = 419), high β2m was still associated with worse OS (HR = 2.16; 95% CI: 1.50–3.10, P < 0.001); median OS was 34.4 versus 85.8 months, and 5‐year OS rates were 38% versus 66% (P < 0.001, Figure 5B). Moreover, patients with high sβ2m had particularly dismal PFS (15.6 months), compared to those with low levels (58.3 months) (Figure S3E). However, approximately 81% of the patients in this group had sβ2m ≥ 5.5 mg/L, thus it seems that among patients with serum creatinine ≥ 1.2 mg/dL, it is low β2m that identifies a subgroup with “favorable” prognosis.

The prognostic performance of CGS in patients with and without renal dysfunction

The median OS of patients with sCr < 1.2 mg/dL was 99 months versus 40.3 months for those with sCr > 1.2 mg/dL (HR = 1.98, P < 0.001), and the median PFS was 39 versus 19 months, respectively (HR = 0.58, 95% CI 0.50–0.67, P < 0.001) suggesting a significant association of renal dysfunction with adverse prognosis. However, CGS was prognostic irrespective of renal dysfunction: among those with low serum creatinine the 5‐year OS was 71% versus 45% (HR = 2.30; 95% CI: 1.80–2.90, P < 0.001) and among those with high serum creatinine (≥1.2 mg/dL) was 47% versus 25% (HR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.30–2.39, P < 0.001) for HRMM and SRMM, respectively (Figure 5).

The number of high‐risk features further stratifies HRMM patients

We then evaluated the prognostic impact of the number of different HRMM features (not including sb2m in patients with sCr > 1.2 mg/dL) present in each patient. Out of the total 305 HRMM patients, 53 harbored (17.3% of HRMM and 4.4% of the total cohort) more than 1 high‐risk feature. The median OS was 52 months for those with one high‐risk feature versus 25 months for those with >1 feature (P < 0.001). These patients demonstrated a 90% increase in the risk of death (HR = 1.90, 95% CI: 1.30–2.80, P = 0.001), compared to those with one high‐risk feature and a threefold increase (HR = 3.10, 95% CI: 2.20–4.20, P < 0.001) compared to those with SRMM (Figure 6). The respective median PFS according to the number of HRMM features was 23.8 months for one versus 15.8 months for >1 (P = 0.003) (Figure S7). Thus, 4.4% of patients carry two or more high‐risk CGS features and have an extremely poor prognosis, identifying an ultra‐high‐risk group. We further evaluated the impact of the number of HR cytogenetic abnormalities per CGS, but without b2‐microglobulin, which suggested similar PFS and OS for those with one (n = 117, 9.7% of patients) or two (n = 79, 6.6%) of them but shorter (not reaching statistical significance) for patients with 3 or more (n = 15, 1.2%) (Figure S8 and Table S4).

Application of the CGS in key patient subgroups

Age is a known major prognostic factor: median PFS was 52 months, and the OS was 11.5 years for patients <65 and 21.4 and 49 months, respectively, for those ≥65 years (P < 0.001). The distribution of HRMM in the two age groups (i.e., <65 vs. ≥65 years) was similar (25.2% and 23.8%). We analyzed the prognostic impact of CGS in three age groups (<65, 65, and ≥80 years), which with current standards may be treated with different strategies.

The 5‐year OS rate for HRMM versus SRMM for patients <65 years was 80% versus 49% (HR = 3.14, 95% CI: 2.30–4.40, P < 0.001) (Figure 7A), for patients 65–79 years was 53% versus 38% (HR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.20–1.90, P = 0.0018) (Figure 7B) and for those ≥80 years was 35% versus 27% (HR = 1.20, 95% CI: 0.80–1.80, P = 0.390) (Figure 7C). Similarly, PFS was significantly shorter for HRMM patients <65 years (P < 0.001) and 65–79 years (P < 0.001) versus those with SRMM, but for patients 80 years or older, there was no difference in the PFS between HR and SRMM (P = 0.481) (Figure S9A–C). The lack of significant difference in OS and PFS among patients ≥80 years may be related to the impact of frailty, increased toxicity, and more limited treatment options and use of salvage therapies in octogenarians. In an exploratory analysis, we evaluated the outcomes of patients 80 years or older treated with anti‐CD38 antibodies, which suggested an improvement in PFS (P = 0.031), which has not yet translated to a statistically significant improvement in OS (P = 0.15), despite numerical differences in OS (Figure S10).

As treatment paradigms and availability of new drugs in both first and subsequent lines of therapy evolve rapidly, we also evaluated the prognostic impact of HRMM in patients that started treatment before and after 2020: CGS retained its prognostic significance irrespective of the calendar period, use of transplant, and was informative in the era of anti‐CD38–based induction (Table S5).

Among patients who underwent ASCT, CGS HR was associated with worse OS than SR (HR = 2.40, 95% CI: 1.50–4.00, P = 0.004). Similarly, high‐risk patients who did not receive ASCT also had a poor prognosis (HR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.40–2.00, P < 0.001) (Figure 8A,B).

Overall survival for patients that (A) underwent autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT), (B) did not undergo ASCT, (C) were treated before 2020 and (D) after 2020, and (E) were treated with anti‐CD38 frontline regimens and (F) were not treated with anti‐CD38 frontline regimens. HR, high risk; SR, standard risk.

When stratified by period of treatment initiation, CGS remained prognostic both before 2020 (HR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.40–2.20, P < 0.001; 5‐year OS 56% for SR vs. 37% for HR) and after at or after 2020 (HR 1.95, 95% CI 1.30–2.90, P = 0.002; 3‐year OS 83% vs. 62% for SR vs. HR, respectively) (Figure 8C,D).

In patients receiving anti‐CD38 monoclonal antibodies at induction, 3‐year OS was high overall, but a trend toward inferior survival was observed in CGS HRMM (76%) than in SRMM (92%; HR 2.20, 95% CI 0.93–5.00, P = 0.067) (Figure 8E,F).

In a sensitivity analysis, anti‐CD38 exposure was independently associated with improved OS (HR = 0.34, P < 0.001), and more patients <65 years received anti‐CD38, suggesting that additional follow‐up is needed to show the difference in the prognosis between HRMM and SRMM in patients treated with the most modern standard regimens.

Similar results were also observed for PFS (Table S6). Among patients that received ASCT, CGS HRMM was associated with worse PFS (median 42.9 vs. 97.6 months, P = 0.001), and a similar adverse effect was observed in patients who did not receive ASCT (16 vs. 25.2 months, P < 0.001) (Figure S11); similarly in the two calendar periods there was a significant impact of the presence of HRMM in PFS (in the group before 2020, median PFS for HRMM vs. SRMM was 17.9 vs. 27.6 months, P < 0.001 and in the group after 2020 was 27.5 vs. 52.2 months, P < 0.001, respectively) (Figure S12). Notably, there has been a significant improvement in the PFS between the two calendar periods both for patients with HRMM (HR: 0.60; 95% CI 0.44–0.82, P = 0.001) and with SRMM (HR: 0.52, 95% CI 0.42–0.64, P < 0.001). Among patients treated with anti‐CD38‐based induction therapy, there was no statistically significant difference in the PFS between HR and SRMM (P = 0.181), but the 3‐year PFS was 72% versus 59%, and the outcome of the patients treated with these regimens is substantially better than that of the rest of the cohort, suggesting that further follow‐up is needed.

DISCUSSION

The treatment landscape in MM moves rapidly to more effective therapies, and risk stratification needs to adapt as well. 15 The advent of novel therapeutic agents has improved outcomes for patients features that once were commonly associated with poor outcomes 16 , 17 ; however, the clinical heterogeneity of the disease remains, enduring challenges for individualized care. 18 , 19 In addition, as more data become available, the need to compare outcomes of different therapies using harmonized tools is important. The new IMS/IMWG CGS criteria for HRMM provide a tool that harmonizes risk stratification and provides clinically relevant prognostic information. The current study validated the new CGS criteria to provide information on (A) the prevalence of HRMM in a contemporary cohort, (B) describe how CGS changes current risk stratification, which was based on ISS, R‐ISS, and R2‐ISS, and (C) evaluate the prognostic performance of the novel definition.

According to CGS, about 25% of the patients in our cohort were HR. This prevalence of HRMM at diagnosis is close to what one would expect from a risk stratification system that aims to identify the ~20% of patients with worse outcomes. In addition to that, the presence of two or more high‐risk features (even adjusting for serum creatine levels) identified an ultra‐high‐risk group (~4.4% of patients in our cohort), and this is of clinical relevance: such patients have extremely short PFS and poor OS, indicating patients in which conventional salvage options have limited efficacy. 20

Using only the ISS or R‐ISS/R2‐ISS criteria may fail to identify some high‐risk patients who harbor adverse genomic features that are missed by these models 19 , 21 such as those with biallelic 1p32 deletions or combinations of monoallelic 1p32 loss with 1q gain and, importantly, those with TP53 mutations. 22 , 23 When outcome analyses were stratified by the R‐ISS, R2‐ISS, and ISS, the bulk of patients (nearly 60% by R‐ISS; 40% by R2‐ISS) were relegated to intermediate‐risk categories, reflecting inherent limitations in precise risk stratification by these systems alone. The CGS model, in contrast, directly identifies a discrete high‐risk group with a distinct clinical trajectory and significantly inferior outcomes.

In order to establish a new risk stratification system with wide applicability, it is important to validate it in different cohorts and subgroups of patients. Such information is important to establish CGS as the new standard for risk stratification, allowing similar stratification in clinical trials, homogeneity when comparing therapies and strategies, and a common simple‐to‐use terminology for what high‐risk myeloma is today. To this end, our analysis validated the prognostic impact of CGS in patients that received different treatments at induction and as consolidation (ASCT), diagnosed in different eras (but being relevant to modern therapies used in the last 15 years), and in patients of different age groups. In the latter analysis, CGS effectively identified patients with HR and SRMM among patients <80 years. Two more studies have validated CGS in cohorts of patients treated with quadruplet and ASCT 13 or quadruplet with and without ASCT, 14 with similar results. Our study aligns with data from the IFM 12 with respect to the prognostic stability of CGS across key patient subgroups such as in patients who underwent ASCT (median PFS 47 vs. 62 months for HR and SRMM vs. median of 42.9 vs. 97.6 months, in our study). The importance of CGS risk classification in younger patients and those treated with ASCT aligns also with the data from Venkatesh et al. 13 (which included only patients treated with quads and ASCT) and the study by MacLachlan et al. 14 (in which patients received quads and half also ASCT). The lack of discriminatory ability of CGS in octogenarians in our study is notable and emphasizes the importance of other patient‐related factors, such as frailty, comorbidities, sensitivity to toxicity, and treatment adherence, which probably overshadows the biology of the disease.

High sβ2m > 5.5 mg/L (or ISS‐3) identifies HRMM in the CGS, but only in patients with creatinine < 1.2 mg/dL. Although this definition may seem controversial, we observed that if such a distinction was not made, then >80% of patients with sCr > 1.2 mg/dL would have been rated as HRMM. Renal dysfunction is a known risk factor, associated not only with disease biology and tumor burden but also with age and other comorbidities. 24 Importantly, however, CGS could identify HRMM both in the group of patients with and without renal dysfunction. The study also revealed the limitations of using biochemical surrogates (such as sβ2m cutoff values) in the context of impaired renal function. Thus, it is noteworthy that CGS specifies β2m ≥ 5.5 mg/L as high‐risk only in the context of preserved renal function, an advance over prior models which did not fully account for the confounding effect of renal impairment on β2m levels and better aligns biomarker interpretation with true clonal burden and tumor biology.

Our findings are consistent with recent validations of the CGS by the French group (Schavgoulidze et al.), 12 who utilized a targeted‐NGS panel in a cohort of 6528 patients, with the study by Venkatesh et al., 13 which evaluated CGS only in patients treated with quadruplets and ASCT, and the study by MacLachlan et al., 14 in which CGS was evaluated in patients treated with quadruplets with and without ASCT. However, these studies have significant differences from our current study, but all provide additional evidence in different populations of patients. Our study reported a slightly lower prevalence of HRMM (25.2% vs. 30.4% in the IFM when including the biochemical criterion of sβ2m, 31% in the study by MacLachlan et al., 14 and 29% in the study by Venkatesh et al. 13 ), probably due to the different methods of assessment (FISH vs. NGS). Thus, without NGS, about 5% of patients may be misclassified as SR.

Most HRMM features had a comparable adverse impact across cohorts supporting their prognostic value, and patients harboring more than one feature had particularly dismal prognosis across studies (median PFS in the French cohort for 2 and ≥3 features was 20 and 7 months, respectively, compared to 15.8 months in our cohort for patients with >1 feature). The accumulation of genetic features seems to drive prognosis; however, isolated del17 has a substantial prognostic effect, as shown in our analysis.

The IFM study emphasized the utility of NGS in detecting “double‐hit” TP53 events and biallelic 1p32 deletions: we align with this conclusion, but our data indicate that a standard FISH‐based approach is able to capture the majority of HRMM and also to identify an “ultra‐high‐risk” population (4.4% of patients) with dismal survival.

A notable divergence between the IFM study and ours as well as those from US centers 13 , 14 exists regarding the prognostic value of isolated high sβ2m (≥5.5 mg/L) in patients with preserved renal function (sCr < 1.2 mg/dL). While the French group observed that among patients with sβ2m as the only high‐risk feature did not experience significantly inferior PFS, our analysis, as well as the data from the US centers, demonstrated that isolated high sβ2m remained a predictor of poor outcomes. This difference may be explained by the significantly lower frequency of isolated sβ2m ≥ 5.5 mg/L (7.3% in the IFM vs. 15% in our cohort); also, there are significant differences between cohorts in terms of renal function, while sβ2m and sCr were missing in a significant proportion of patients in the IFM study. Another notable difference is that the IFM study relied solely on PFS due to data maturity (median follow‐up of 35 months); similarly, in the study by MacLachlan et al. 14 the median follow‐up was 2.2 years and in the study by Venkatesh et al. 13 was 3.4 years; our study provides critical validation of CGS for OS with a median follow‐up of 63 months (5.25 years), confirming that the risk stratification holds true for long‐term mortality.

Our analysis has limitations: first, the lack of data for TP53 mutation misses patients with double TP53 hit and those with TP53 mutations and without del17p. Second, we evaluated chromosome 1p abnormalities only with FISH and not NGS; thus, a small deletion may have been missed. Although these limitations prevent full adherence to the strict CGS definition, the strong prognostic stratification observed in our cohort underscores the robustness of the model. This finding suggests that CGS retains significant clinical utility even in centers where NGS is not yet routinely performed. Finally, our data come from a single‐tertiary center; thus, further external validation of CGS in other populations and healthcare settings is warranted.

In conclusion, the current large study in consecutive patients not only validates the utility of the new CGS but also enhances its generalizability in daily clinical practice. Our findings demonstrate that the IMS/IMWG consensus CGS effectively complements and extends prior MM staging systems by recognizing both a high‐ and an ultra‐high‐risk population, even in the era of novel therapeutics. Our data complement other studies and establish the use of CGS as a reference stratification tool for clinical trials and clinical practice.
