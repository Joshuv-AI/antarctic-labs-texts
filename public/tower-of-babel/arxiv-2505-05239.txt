Bounds on k k -hash distances and rates of linear codes
Stefano Della Fiore and Marco Dalai

Abstract
In this paper, we bound the rate of linear codes in 𝔽 q n {\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{n} with the property that any k ≤ q k\leq q codewords are all simultaneously distinct in at least d k d_{k} coordinates. For the case of particular interest q = k = 3 q=k=3 we recover, with a simpler proof, state of the art results in the case d 3 = 1 d_{3}=1 and new bounds for d 3 > 1 d_{3}>1 . We finally discuss some related open problems on the list-decoding zero-error capacity of discrete memoryless channels.

Introduction

For integers
n\geq 1
and
q\geq 2
, a
q
-ary code
C
of length
n
is a subset of
\{0,1,\ldots,q-1\}^{n}
. We denote its rate as

R=\frac{1}{n}\log_{q}|C|\,.

In general, in the whole paper, when the base of a logarithm is not specified it is assumed to be the cardinality
q
of the code alphabet being considered. Also, in what follows, for every positive integers

, we use the notation

for the falling factorial

and for every real

we write the positive part as
(\alpha)^{+}=\max(0,\alpha)
.

Given

fixed codewords of a

-ary code, we say that they are

-hashed if there is a coordinate where they are all pairwise distinct. A
q
-ary code is said to be a
(q,k)
-
hash code
if any
k
distinct codewords are
k
-hashed. The problem of finding upper and lower bounds for the maximum size of
(q,k)
-hash codes is a fundamental problem in theoretical computer science and information theory. It appears, as the name suggests, in the study of families of perfect hash functions and in the study of the zero-error capacity of some discrete channels with list decoding, see
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
for more details (see also
[

]
for a related problem).
We review some of the most important results in this direction, as it will be useful to give a context to the results presented in this paper.

An elementary double counting argument, as shown in
[
2
]
, proves that the cardinality of
(q,k)
-hash codes is bounded as

|C|\leq(k-1)\left(\frac{q}{k-1}\right)^{n}\text{ for every }q\geq k\geq 3\,.

(1)

In 1984 Fredman and Komlós
[
1
]
improved the bound in (

) for every

and sufficiently large

, showing that

|C|\leq\left((q-k+2)^{q^{\underline{k-1}}/{q^{k-1}}}\right)^{n+o(n)}\,.

(2)

They also provided, using standard probabilistic methods, an achievability result proving the existence of codes with cardinality lower bounded as

|C|\geq\left(\left(1-\frac{q^{\underline{k}}}{q^{k}}\right)^{-1/(k-1)}\right)^{n+o(n)}\,.

(3)

Körner and Marton
[
2
]
presented a generalization of the upper bounds given in inequalities (
1
) and (
2
) which takes the form

|C|\leq\min_{0\leq j\leq k-2}\left(\left(\frac{q-j}{k-j-1}\right)^{q^{\underline{j+1}}/{q^{j+1}}}\right)^{n+o(n)}\,.

(4)

Note in particular that (
2
) corresponds to only using

in the argument of the minimization in (

). For values of

sufficiently larger than

, these bounds become weak; in 1998 Blackburn and Wild
[
4
]
(see also
[
7
]
) showed that they can be improved by the following bound

|C|\leq(k-1)q^{\lceil\frac{n}{k-1}\rceil}\,.

(5)

Much effort has been spent during the years to refine the bounds given in (
1
), (
2
) and (

,
3

for the case of

(q,k)
-hash codes have been recently obtained in

|C|\geq(9/5)^{n/4+o(1)}\,.

(6)

In contrast, no exponential improvement has been made on the simple bound given in (

|C|\leq\frac{c}{n^{2/5}}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{n}

Additional results were obtained for the case of

\displaystyle|C|

\displaystyle|C|

In this paper we focus on linear codes which satisfy a generalization of the

In

\displaystyle|C|

\displaystyle\leq(k-1)q^{\left\lceil\frac{n-d_{k}+1}{k-1}\right\rceil}\,,

In

In this paper we provide upper bounds on the rate of linear codes in

IV
A Related Open Problem in Zero-Error Capacity

As mentioned in the introduction, the problem of perfect hashing can be alternatively described as a problem of zero-error capacity under list decoding for a certain class of discrete memoryless channels. Consider for example two discrete memoryless channels as in Figure
4
, where arrows represent transitions with positive probability. For both channels, any two inputs symbols are “confusable”, in the sense that they share at least one output. This means that any two input sequences of length
n
are also confusable, in the sense that there is an output sequence which is compatible with both. If the decoder is required to output one single estimated input sequence, with a positive probability there will be an error. In list-decoding with list-size
L

L
possible input sequences, and an error is declared if the true input sequence is not in the list. A zero-error code is one for which the decoder is always able to produce a list of
L
codewords which include the one sent by the encoder. This can be equivalently stated by saying that for any output sequence there are at most
L
compatible input sequences.
For the channel on the left of Figure
4
with list-size
L=2
, we see that zero-error codes are precisely the trifferent codes discussed in Section
II-B
. Indeed, a code is zero-error with
L=2
if no output sequence is compatible with more than two distinct codewords, which means that for any three codewords there is a coordinate where they all differ, making one of them incompatible with the observed output. A similar reasoning shows that also for the channel on the right, zero-error codes are possible with list-size
L=2
, and these are precisely the
(4,3)
-hash codes. More generally,
(q,k)
-hash codes are zero-error codes with decoding list-size

for symmetric channels with
q

In this section we want to bring the attention to the possible use of the method presented in Section
III
in the more general context of bounding zero-error capacities. In particular, we consider a specific case that in our opinion represents a significant example for more general studies in these kind of problems. While the problem seems to be similar to the one studied in the previous sections, and the method can in principle also be applied in a similar way, it does not give useful results. So, this section is in a certain sense a story of failure, but we believe the problem raised is of sufficient interest to be still discussed in relation to the present work.
