Gestational Gigantomastia: A Century of Evidence with a New Case
Authors: Sama K. Fofung, Janan H. Malik, Waluma M. Fofung, Benjamin Wabwire
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Gestational gigantomastia (GG) is an extreme, rapidly progressive breast enlargement that can culminate in ulceration, sepsis, cardiopulmonary compromise, and, when care is delayed, maternal death. Scattered case reports leave management uncertain. This study synthesizes more than a century of GG literature and contextualizes a new, complex case in context, clarifying epidemiology, pathogenesis, and treatment pathways.Methods:Systematic searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Ovid Global Health, Google Scholar, and French-language archives (1900–2025) identified 69 publications describing 76 GG episodes. Dual reviewers applied explicit criteria; extracted demographic, clinical, and outcome data; and assessed quality. These findings were integrated with a life-threatening GG case managed at 27 weeks’ gestation.Results:Median presentation was 23 weeks (range 6 wk gestation to 1 wk postpartum). Ulceration (53%), hemorrhage (22%), and infection (13%) were common; 4 maternal deaths (5.1%) occurred due to delayed intervention. One-third of assays showed elevated prolactin. First-line medical therapy (bromocriptine, cabergoline, or norethindrone) achieved partial arrest in approximately 35% of trials; definitive control required surgery in 57% (reduction mammaplasty 29%, mastectomy 28%). Maternal outcomes improved when surgery preceded ulceration or systemic decompensation. Our patient recovered fully after an emergency skin-sparing mastectomy, though preterm labor ensued.Conclusions:GG remains rare, yet potentially fatal. Early recognition, prompt endocrine modulation, and timely, staged surgery are critical. We provide the largest curated dataset to date, quantify the mortality risk, and propose a stepwise management algorithm to guide multidisciplinary teams confronting this formidable obstetric challenge.

Takeaways

Question: What are the presentation patterns, optimal management strategies, and outcomes of gestational gigantomastia, and can these data inform a treatment algorithm?

Findings: We conducted a narrative review (1900–2025) of 69 publications (76 cases), including a contemporary case treated by staged mastectomy with planned autologous reconstruction. Dual-reviewer selection produced the largest curated dataset to date, revealing high morbidity when treatment is delayed, a 24% recurrence rate after reduction versus less than 2% after mastectomy, and favorable maternal–fetal outcomes with early multidisciplinary care.

Meaning: Early recognition and a staged approach, culminating in definitive mastectomy when conservative therapy fails, optimize safety and long-term outcomes in gestational gigantomastia.

INTRODUCTION

Gestational gigantomastia (GG) is an exceptionally rare, debilitating, and potentially life-threatening enlargement of the breasts during pregnancy that can culminate in ulceration, infection, hemorrhage, malnutrition, and profound psychosocial distress. Because most evidence consists of isolated case reports scattered across decades and languages, clinical guidance remains fragmented.

This review synthesized all accessible primary reports of GG published from 1900 to 2025—69 articles describing 76 well-characterized episodes—identified through comprehensive searches, complemented by manual screening of French-language journals. A dual-reviewer process with explicit eligibility criteria ensured reproducibility; structured extraction produced the largest curated dataset to date. We integrate these findings with a contemporary, fully detailed case that illustrates rapid progression to critical ulceration, successful lifesaving mastectomy, and planned delayed autologous reconstruction.

Our analysis traces historical trends, delineates presentation patterns, clarifies endocrine and immunologic hypotheses, compares medical and surgical strategies, and distills maternal–fetal outcomes. On this foundation, we propose a practical, tiered management algorithm that prioritizes early multidisciplinary assessment, aggressive optimization, and timely definitive surgery when conservative therapy fails. By consolidating a century of scattered evidence and adding new clinical insight, this review provided the most comprehensive reference to date and highlighted research priorities needed to refine future care.

METHODS

We conducted a comprehensive narrative review aiming at capturing all primary reports of GG published since the early 20th century. As GG is exceptionally rare, narrowing the search window could exclude informative single-case reports. Our approach thus prioritized sensitivity over rigid systematic review protocols, while maintaining transparency and reproducibility.

A native English/French team member developed Boolean strings for “gestational OR gravid* AND (gigantomastia OR macromastia)” and recognized synonyms (“gravid breast hypertrophy,” “gestational breast enlargement”). Searches were run in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase (Ovid), Google Scholar, and Ovid Global Health from inception through March 31, 2025. Reference lists of eligible articles were hand-searched, and citation tracking in Scopus identified additional relevant publications. To broaden geographic coverage, we manually screened French-language journals; no other languages yielded additional cases.

We included original case reports, series, or cohorts in human pregnancy reporting breast enlargement consistent with the authors’ definition of GG, regardless of management or outcome. We excluded nongestational hypertrophy, abstracts without primary data, and narrative reviews. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts; disagreements were resolved by consensus. This process yielded 69 publications (67 English, 2 French), covering 76 well-described patient episodes. Our own case was included. The deidentified case is ethics-exempt per policy; written consent was obtained, and Case Report (CARE Guidelines: Consensus-based Clinical Case Reporting Guideline Development)/International Committee of Medical Journal Editors standards were followed.

We used a piloted spreadsheet to extract demographic, clinical, laboratory, treatment, and outcome data, mapped to a 77-item data dictionary based on prior reviews. When standard elements were missing in older papers, these were marked “not reported.” As most studies were single-case reports, formal risk-of-bias tools would have uniformly rated them as low certainty; instead, we assessed methodological completeness (diagnostic confirmation, follow-up, histology). A second reviewer cross-checked the data to minimize transcription errors.

Given heterogeneity and the predominance of narrative data, meta-analysis was not feasible. We therefore performed structured tabulation and descriptive analysis (frequencies, percentages), and developed cross-series tables summarizing presentation patterns, management pathways, and maternal–fetal outcomes. HISTORY AND EPIDEMIOLOGICAL PERSPECTIVES

The first documented case of GG was reported by Simpson, who described breasts enlarging to the pubis with ulcerated and infected skin.1 Sporadic reports from the 1950s to 1960s2,3 highlighted the heterogeneous clinical courses and examined supportive therapy, hormonal agents, and even therapeutic abortion, with mixed success.4–6 During the 1970s and 1980s, a stream of detailed case series refined hormonal and genetic hypotheses and prompted the development of more systematic treatment algorithms.7–15

GG remains rare, with an estimated prevalence of 1 in 28,000–100,000 pregnancies.16,17 Underrecognition is likely (Table 1), especially where diagnostic resources are scarce. Although most publications originate from North America, incidence may be higher in parts of Africa and Asia, where reporting infrastructure is limited.

Typical patients are aged 18–35 years, often in their first or second pregnancy; prior breast hypertrophy, multiparity, or a family history of breast disease increase the risk.18–21 Onset typically occurs in the second or third trimester but may appear earlier. A genetic predisposition is suspected, yet unproven.

ETIOLOGY AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

The leading theory is an exaggerated response of breast tissue to normal gestational hormones—estrogen, progesterone, prolactin, and human chorionic gonadotropin (Table 2).22–24 Most—but not all—patients exhibit elevated prolactin levels or increased receptor sensitivity; dopamine agonists can partially reverse growth, underscoring prolactin’s role.25–31 Cases of parathyroid hormone-related peptide-mediated GG have been reported.32,33 Exogenous progesterone has also been implicated.34 Immune-mediated mechanisms are proposed after associations with autoimmune disorders and raised inflammatory cytokines.35–38

No familial or monogenic pattern has been confirmed. Idiopathic cases suggest a multifactorial interplay of hormonal, genetic, and environmental influences.39

Histology shows lobular hyperplasia with stromal edema, necrosis, and florid angiogenesis.40–43 Pseudoangiomatous stromal hyperplasia is another histological feature associated with GG.44 Upregulated vascular endothelial growth factor, fibroblast growth factors, and possibly transforming growth factor beta drive vascular and stromal expansion.45

CLINICAL PRESENTATION AND DIAGNOSIS

Rapid, often bilateral breast enlargement—sometimes extending into axillary or supernumerary tissue—emerges in mid-pregnancy; pain, edema, tenderness, ulceration, bleeding, back pain, posture change, and marked psychosocial distress are typical. Unilateral cases also occur.46–48 (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the baseline patient and pregnancy characteristics with key clinical features, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E482.) Differential diagnoses include virginal hypertrophy, inflammatory breast disease, and malignancy, necessitating imaging (ultrasound ± magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) to exclude cancer.49

The work-up comprises history, physical examination, measurement of breast circumference at the point of widest girth, breast weight, and ultrasound as a first-line imaging modality, and selective mammography or MRI may be used. Hormonal assays (prolactin, estrogen, progesterone, human chorionic gonadotropin) aid in diagnosis but are not diagnostic.16 Laboratory screens for anemia/hypoalbuminemia guide optimization. Biopsy is reserved for suspicious masses or unexplained lymphadenopathy; histology confirms benign hyperplasia.

MATERNAL–FETAL IMPACT AND COMPLICATIONS

Maternal morbidity includes severe pain, back strain, ulceration, infection, hemorrhage, anemia, and hypoalbuminemia.50 Severe cases with deep infection and sepsis could need urgent antibiotics or surgery.51 Psychosocial sequelae—anxiety, depression, social withdrawal—are common.52–54

Physiological stress and nutritional deficits predispose individuals to preterm labor, fetal growth restriction, and occasional fetal demise.55,56 Fulminant sepsis can lead to devastating bilateral breast infarction.57 If unchecked, massive hemorrhage can be fatal; prompt surgery may be lifesaving.58

Common complications are skin ulceration, infection, spontaneous hemorrhage, and shock.3,16,39 Postpartum recurrence can occur if residual glandular tissue remains.2

MANAGEMENT AND OUTCOMES

Initial hormonal therapies (testosterone, estrogen, progesterone) rapidly proved ineffective and even deleterious.3,5–7,16 Other conservative measures—dopamine agonists, corticosteroids, analgesia, compression, slings, binders, nutritional and psychosocial support—offer symptom relief but seldom halt progressive disease.59–63 Marked postpartum regression is possible with abstention till parturition.64 (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays management strategies and maternal–fetal outcomes, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E483.)

Surgery is indicated for refractory pain, ulceration, infection, or hemorrhage. Reduction mammaplasty alleviates weight but carries a recurrence risk in future pregnancies.65 Total mastectomy eliminates recurrence and may be lifesaving in cases of advanced necrosis or bleeding.66 Timing during pregnancy demands a multidisciplinary balance of maternal benefit versus fetal risk; emergent situations override concerns related to gestational age.29,34 Therapeutic abortion is rarely chosen but can reverse the hormonal stimulus and breast enlargement.67 Early definitive surgery improves pain, posture, and mobility; delayed intervention increases wound complications and mortality.58,68

Comprehensive management must include mental health support; counseling or psychotherapy should accompany medical or surgical treatment. Instruments such as the BREAST-Q provide validated, patient-reported metrics of psychosocial and physical well-being and can help monitor outcomes in this population.69

CASE PRESENTATION

A 31-year-old G2P1 (previous uncomplicated term delivery) presented at 27 weeks’ gestation with a 3-week history of explosive bilateral breast enlargement. She reported constant pain, dyspnea from chest compression, inability to lie flat or ambulate, and spurting hemorrhage from a necrotic ulcer on the right breast (Fig. 1). During her prior pregnancy, she experienced only mild hypertrophy that resolved postpartum. There was no family history of breast enlargement or disease.

Preoperative frontal view showing severe bilateral breast hypertrophy.

The right breast weighed 10 kg (circumference 127 cm) and the left 8.8 kg (105 cm). Both were tense, edematous, and draped with friable skin; lower pole ulcers oozed serosanguinous fluid, and the patient stood hunched forward to counterbalance the mass (Fig. 2).

Preoperative lateral view demonstrating compromised posture due to excessive breast weight.

Laboratory findings revealed severe anemia (Hb 6.9 g/dL) and hypoalbuminemia (27 g/L); endocrine studies showed no prolactin or other hormonal excess. Staging ultrasound excluded malignancy but confirmed diffuse glandular hyperplasia without a discrete mass.

A multidisciplinary team (obstetrics, plastic surgery, anesthesia, neonatology, nutrition, psychotherapy) instituted broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics, albumin replacement, and 7 units of packed red cells. Daily fetal nonstress testing remained reassuring.

Despite maximal support, progressive hemorrhage, spreading cellulitis, and rising lactate signaled imminent septic and hemodynamic collapse. Elective delay was therefore abandoned in favor of urgent operative management at 30 + 4 weeks (Fig. 3).

Immediate preoperative inferior view of the right breast showing a hemorrhagic ulcer.

Initial skin-sparing reduction was aborted after encountering torrential venous oozing and matted axillary lymph-node clumps suggestive of phyllodes or malignancy (Fig. 4). The team converted to bilateral nipple-sparing mastectomy with free-nipple grafts, achieving faster control of the vascular pedicles and reducing blood loss. Continuous external cardiotocography showed no fetal distress throughout the 9-hour procedure (Fig. 5).

Intraoperative findings of fibrovascular proliferation and matted lymph nodes, prompting a switch to mastectomy.

Right mastectomy specimen weighing 10 kg.

Postoperatively, maternal hemodynamics stabilized, but she entered spontaneous labor at 31 weeks; the neonate died despite corticosteroids and intensive neonatal support. Final histopathology demonstrated lobular hyperplasia and fibrocystic change without atypia or malignancy.

Wound healing was uncomplicated apart from partial superficial nipple graft necrosis that resolved conservatively. At the 6-week review, the patient was pain-free, upright, and fully mobile. BREAST-Q (v2.0) scores improved from 49 to 90 (physical well-being) and from 18 to 78 (psychosocial). She expressed profound relief at the cessation of bleeding, foul odor, and functional incapacity (Figs. 6, 7). We agreed to revisit delayed reconstruction 6–12 months after surgery. This case underscored the need for rapid escalation from conservative care to definitive surgery when GG is complicated by infection or hemodynamic instability, even at the cost of extreme prematurity risk.

DISCUSSION

This case highlighted 3 pivotal themes: (1) GG may erupt as late as the third trimester yet still progress to critical ulceration and hemorrhage within weeks; (2) conversion from attempted reduction to nipple-sparing mastectomy provided rapid hemostatic control and definitive cure; and (3) pain, posture, and psychosocial scores rebounded dramatically 6 weeks postresection, underscoring the functional payoff of timely surgery. Key synthesis:

1. Survival is the rule: Across 77 mothers, only 4 maternal deaths (5.1%) were recorded—due to sepsis and presurgical exsanguinations from ulcerated gigantomastia.

2. Medical therapy helps some—but rarely cures: Dopamine agonist therapy stabilized 15.3% of patients but completely obviated surgery in only 3 (10.4%) of medically treated cases.

3. Timing matters:

- Antepartum surgery halts disease but carries a prematurity risk (mean gestational age 32 wk; 75% neonatal intensive care unit admission)

- Postpartum surgery is markedly safer for mother and infant, with superior cosmetic options

4. Reduction versus mastectomy trade-off:

- Reduction mammoplasty preserves lactation potential and body image but recurs in up to 1 in 5 subsequent pregnancies and occasionally even without pregnancy

- Mastectomy ends the disease more definitively; modern skin-sparing + delayed autologous reconstruction yields high satisfaction with a <2% major complication rate

5. Termination of pregnancy is rarely necessary: Termination resolved endocrinologic crises but was chosen in < 6% of cases.

COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS AND INTERPRETATION

Across more than a century of reports, the presentation is strikingly uniform: relentless, painful breast enlargement beginning early in gestation, with tense edematous skin and prominent superficial veins (Supplemental Digital Content 4, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E485). Systemic symptoms are uncommon unless secondary complications develop.

Our patient mirrors the classical demographic for GG—a multiparous woman in her early 30s with abrupt second-trimester onset—corroborating pooled epidemiological trends.39 Ulceration is the pivotal event that converts a disabling condition into a potentially fatal one. Once the dermis breaks down, infection and catastrophic venous bleeding dominate the clinical course; anemia and, rarely, hypovolemic shock or sepsis can follow. The sheer breast mass (≈19 kg) and concomitant ulceration, anemia, and hypoalbuminemia align with earlier “catastrophic” descriptions.1–4,6,12,56,57 As summarized in Supplemental Digital Content 1 (https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E482), ulceration and hemorrhage remain the most frequent and decisive complications. Endocrine panels were normal, aligning with Supplemental Digital Content 4 (https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E485), where only one-third of cases had elevated prolactin, supporting the receptor hypersensitivity hypothesis.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGICAL CONVERGENCE

Heterogeneous endocrine profiles—ranging from frank hyperprolactinemia to biochemical normality—support the hypothesis of end-organ hypersensitivity rather than simple hormone excess.25 Autoimmune comorbidity appears in 16.8% of documented cases, suggesting that immune priming may amplify receptor signaling, yet lymphocytic lobulocentrism is documented in only 3 histological reports.30,59 Systematic immunophenotyping and receptor polymorphism studies are needed to unite these strands.

TREATMENT PATHWAYS VERSUS MATERNAL–FETAL OUTCOMES

Surgical Choices

Postmastectomy Reconstruction

Urgent bilateral nipple-sparing mastectomy in our patient (30 + 4 wk) controlled hemorrhage and infection and rapidly relieved pain and postural strain (Table 7; Supplemental Digital Content 4, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E485). Definitive reshaping was deferred, reflecting consensus that aesthetic reconstruction is safest 6–12 months or more postpartum, once hormones settle and nutrition stabilizes.70 Most reports favor delayed autologous flaps (deep inferior epigastric perforator or latissimus dorsi) because they provide durable, hormone-insensitive volume, whereas immediate implants risk capsular contracture in edematous skin. Complete mastectomy all but eliminates recurrence, yet patients still need counseling on residual axillary tissue and future pregnancies. Overall, a 2-stage plan—lifesaving mastectomy followed by tailored autologous reconstruction—offers the best blend of maternal safety, stable aesthetics, and long-term disease control in GG. Tables 4 and 5 reinforce that mastectomy is associated with the lowest recurrence and lowest intraoperative risk in critical-stage GG.

Psychosocial, Breastfeeding, and Sexual Health Outcomes

Pain relief, restored mobility, and elimination of odor drive immediate gains in self-image, yet long-term data are sparse. Ours is the first to document a 41-point BREAST-Q psychosocial improvement by 6 weeks. Breastfeeding remains feasible after reduction but is precluded after total mastectomy; this trade-off should be discussed proactively with all patients, particularly in primigravids planning larger families.

CONCLUSIONS

GG, though rare, poses life-threatening maternal–fetal hazards when complicated by infection or hemorrhage. Our case and cumulative evidence underscore the primacy of early multidisciplinary assessment, aggressive nutritional and antimicrobial optimization, and timely surgery when conservative therapy fails. A unified diagnostic-management algorithm can streamline care, whereas large-scale registries and genetic studies address persistent gaps in epidemiology, pathogenesis, and therapeutics. Ongoing collaborative research is essential to refine medical therapy; clarify optimal surgical timing; and, ultimately, improve both maternal function and fetal survival.
