Abstract
Economic theory implies strong limitations on what types of consumption behavior are considered rational. Rationality implies that the Slutsky matrix, which captures the substitution effects of compensated price changes on demand for different goods, is symmetric and negative semi-definite. While negative semi-definiteness has been shown to be nonparametrically testable, a fully nonparametric test of symmetry has remained elusive due to the inherent multidimensionality of the problem. Recently, it has even been shown that the symmetry condition is not testable via the average Slutsky matrix, prompting conjectures about its non-testability. We settle this question by deriving nonparametric conditional quantile restrictions on observable data that permit construction of a fully nonparametric test for the symmetry condition. The theoretical contribution is a multivariate extension of identification results for partial effects in nonseparable models without monotonicity, which is of independent interest. The derived conditional restrictions induce challenges related to generated regressors and multiple hypothesis testing, which can be addressed using recent statistical methods. Our results provide researchers with the missing tool in many econometric models that rely on Slutsky matrices: from welfare analysis with individual heterogeneity to testing an empirical version of rationality in consumption behavior. 1
Introduction

Rationality forms the backbone of classic economic theory. In the setting of consumer demand, rationality posits that a smooth demand function is generated by utility maximization. One testable characterization of rationality is via the matrix of Slutsky substitution effects of compensated price changes on demand for goods, namely: a demand function is rational if and only if such a matrix is symmetric and negative semidefinite
[
Samuelson 1948
,
Houthakker 1950
,
Hurwicz and Uzawa 1971
]
. Because of their central importance in economic theory, these Slutsky conditions—symmetry and negative semidefiniteness—have spurred a vast literature concerned with their empirical testability.

1.1
Existing approaches to testing rationality

Empirical analyses of Slutsky conditions date as far back as
Barten 1967
who provided formal tests for both Slutsky conditions using a parametric demand model. This work initiated a wave of tests for Slutsky conditions and homogeneity (see
Laitinen 1978
,
Meisner 1979
,
Bera et al. 1981
,
Bewley 1983
,
Bera et al. 1981
,
Taylor and Shonkwiler 1985
and
Silver and Ali 1989
). Most of these tests, predominantly of the likelihood ratio or Wald type, hinge on parametric specifications of demand functions, thereby raising the issue of misspecification.

Alternative tests in this parametric setting use multiple observations of individual demands and leverage the implications of Slutsky conditions for revealed preference to examine or measure violations of the Slutsky conditions. Under the revealed preference framework, Slutsky symmetry is equivalent to Ville’s axiom of revealed preference (VARP), a differential form of the strong axiom of revealed preference (SARP) due to
Hurwicz and Richter 1979

while the negative semidefinitess of the Slutsky matrix is equivalent to the weak axiom of revealed preference (WARP)
[
Aguiar and Serrano 2025
]
.
Jerison and Jerison 1993
, for example, take this approach. One major limitation of these parametric approaches is that it is practically impossible to ascribe the rejection of the null hypothesis to either violation of the Slutsky condition or simply wrong choice of functional form. Even richer functional forms such as those considered by
Pollak and Wales 1992
and
Banks et al. 1997
fall short of addressing this shortcoming.

These specification concerns have motivated a pivot to nonparametric alternatives. While there is a plethora of tests for both Slutsky conditions in parametric settings, tests for these conditions in more general nonparametric settings remain scant. Earliest attempts at nonparametric specification of demand models and accompanying tests of rationality restrictions go way back as far as
Gallant and Souza 1991
as well as
Yue 1991
. They propose Fourier based or polynomial based demand models and use an element in a sequence of these semi-nonparametrically specified demand models to test Slutsky conditions based on standard test procedures such as Wald or likelihood ratio test statistics.
Lewbel 1995
provides a
\sqrt{n}
-consistent, moment-based, nonparametric test of Slutsky symmetry. As already pointed out by
Haag et al. 2009
, however, the moment-based test by
Lewbel 1995
involves strong assumptions and functional form restrictions.
Haag et al. 2009
and
Hoderlein 2011
provide nonparametric tests of Slutsky symmetry, but under additional restrictions. For instance,
Hoderlein 2011
identifies unverifiable restrictions on specific covariance matrices while
Haag et al. 2009
work with nonlinear cross-equation restrictions on the levels and derivatives of expenditure share equations.

Analogous to the revealed preference approach to testing rationality in parametric settings,
Epstein and Yatchew 1985
use a revealed preference argument and consider testing of Slutsky conditions in a nonparametric model with additively separable unobserved heterogeneity. More recently,
Hausman and Newey 2016
provided a necessary and sufficient condition for rationalizability of cross-sectional demand distributions in a setting with two goods.
Kitamura and Stoye 2018
develop and implement a nonparametric test for rationalizability of cross-sectional demand data by a population of rational consumers in a discrete choice setting. Their test is based on the axiom of revealed stochastic preference by
McFadden and Richter 1991
.
Bhattacharya 2025
characterizes rationalizability of demand in the setting of multinomial discrete choice models. One problem with revealed preference arguments is that testable restrictions implied by these arguments are often so weak that they are almost invariably satisfied in any data
[
Beatty and Crawford 2011
]
. Our test of Slutsky symmetry in this paper is not derived from revealed preference arguments.

In the nonparametric tests for Slutsky conditions in the preceding paragraph, unobserved heterogeneity usually enters linearly and additively in the regression model. As observed by
Lewbel 2001
and
Hoderlein 2011
, however, such nonparametric models imply restrictions on preferences that are not consistent with standard economic theory. We, therefore, work with a more general nonseparable model. In this sense, this work contributes to a strand of literature in nonparametric estimation and testing in nonseparable demand models (
Hoderlein 2011
,
Hoderlein and Mammen 2007
,
Chernozhukov et al. 2015
).

1.2
Contributions of this paper

Within this literature, our paper contributes to and builds on recent work using quantile methods to model and estimate demand with nonseparable unobserved heterogeneity. The key insight from this literature is that under some restrictions of the demand function in the unobservables and full independence of the unobservables and covariates, quantile estimation recovers demand at given points of the distribution of the unobserved heterogeneity.
Hoderlein and Mammen 2007
show, under a weaker conditional rather than full independence assumption and without monotonicity, that average marginal effects of covariates on demand over all individuals with the same observable data (covariates and response) are identified. More importantly, they show that the derivative of the quantile gives the best approximation in the
L^{2}
-sense to these average marginal effects (dubbed
“local average structural derivatives”
) such that we can identify the marginal effects by quantile derivatives.

While the result in
Hoderlein and Mammen 2007
has been instrumental in approaches to welfare analysis with unobserved heterogeneity
[
Hausman and Newey 2016
]
and notions of empirical versions of rationality
[
Dette et al. 2016
]
due to its weak assumptions and general applicability, it is inherently univariate. In fact, it has been used for characterizing Slutsky negative semi-definiteness
[
Hausman and Newey 2016
,
Dette et al. 2016
]
, because this condition is also inherently univariate. Characterizing the symmetry of a matrix is by definition a bivariate problem, however; making the existing identification results not applicable and leaving open the question of characterizing Slutsky symmetry via observable data.

Therefore, as the main theoretical contribution, we generalize the univariate
identification result in
Hoderlein and Mammen 2007
to a bivariate setting. We achieve
this by representing bivariate isoquants via their conditional and marginal restrictions and
prove that this provides a unique decomposition and hence identification. Interestingly, the bivariate case is significantly more complex and introduces several terms in the identification result that are not present in the univariate setting. This
generalization then allows us to derive the quantile restriction implied by symmetry of the
Slutsky matrix. The result can straightforwardly be extended to any dimension, but we only state the bivariate case since it is simple to state

This novel identification result allows us to augment several nonparametric models in the literature, in particular the nonparametric approach to welfare analysis with unobserved heterogeneity in
Hausman and Newey 2016
and an empirical approach to testing rationality of consumption behavior in
Dette et al. 2016
. In the former case, we provide a characterization for Slutsky symmetry in terms of observable quantile functions, complementing the existing characterization of Slutsky negative semi-definiteness. In the latter case, we provide a nonparametric testing procedure that allows to test the empirical definition of consumer rationality as defined in
Dette et al. 2016
.

As another contribution, we propose a practical testing procedure based on the derived characterization of Slutsky symmetry. Compared to the negative semidefiniteness condition, the symmetry condition is more complicated as it involves testing an equality restriction across multiple pairs of goods. This induces a multiple testing problem that distorts the size of the test. Furthermore, the quantile restriction implied by the symmetry of the Slutsky matrix also includes another quantile in the conditioning set. During estimation, this becomes a two step estimation problem and our inference needs to take into account a generated regressor problem this induces.

Finally, this article answers the open question about nonparametrically testing Slutsky symmetry. Recently,
Maes and Malhotra 2024a
, who focus on testability using simple moments of the data, conjectured that in the general setting considered in
Dette et al. 2016
and using cross-sectional data, the Slutsky symmetry condition is untestable. Our work shows that this conjecture is false.
Maes and Malhotra 2024a
resort to using the first and second moments of the demand distribution while we use the entire distribution. Another recent negative result by
Kono 2025
suggests that individual symmetry cannot be tested from the average symmetry matrix without data on the joint distribution of demand at two different income levels. Our approach avoids the identification issues raised by
Kono 2025
by deriving our nonparametric test from testable implications on the entire distribution of demand for each good via
conditional
quantiles instead of collapsing distributional information into an average Slutsky matrix.

2
A nonparametric Slutsky symmetry condition

This section contains the main identification result for the Slutsky symmetry condition, which leads to a testable nonparametric restriction. To state it, we start with the required assumptions on the data and an extension of the ideas in
Hoderlein and Mammen 2007
to conditional quantiles.

2.1
Setup and assumptions

The key idea is based on the connection between quantile functions of the data-generating process and properties of the Slutsky matrix, as shown in
Dette et al. 2016
for the case of negative semi-definiteness. We carry over the notation and model from
Dette et al. 2016
to offer a unified treatment:

\displaystyle Y=\psi(P,X,U)=\psi(P,X,\nu(Q,A))=\phi(P,X,Q,A)

\displaystyle X=\mu(P,Q,S,V)

\displaystyle A\perp\!\!\!\perp(P,X)\mid(Q,V).

Still, only considering bivariate joint quantiles does not provide identification of the relevant conditions, because the result in
Hoderlein and Mammen 2007

3
Slutsky Symmetry and Nonparametric Welfare Analysis

As mentioned in the introduction, Slutsky conditions form the bedrock of a recent literature on nonparametric welfare analysis. This section provides an outline of the connection between the Slutsky conditions and nonparametric welfare analysis, and suggests how the results in this paper advance this literature.

The key to empirical welfare analysis is the observation that measures of welfare such as equivalent variation (EV) and compensated variation (CV) are functionals of demand. In this section, we restrict our attention to EV in a setting with unobserved individual heterogeneity. As noted by
Hausman and Newey 2017
, EV can be used to compare welfare across different price changes while CV cannot. Furthermore, deadweight loss (DL) is more complicated for CV.

3.1
Slutsky Symmetry as a Foundational Assumption

EV, as a measure of welfare, captures the effect on expenditures of varying prices while holding utility fixed. Following
Hausman and Newey 2016
, we define EV as follows, adapting their notation to our setting:

S(A)=X-e(P^{0},U^{1},\eta)

where
X=e(P^{1},U^{1},\eta)
and
e(P^{0},U^{1},A)
are income and expenditure functions;
A
is unobserved heterogeneity, and;
P^{1}
and
P^{0}
are the new and initial prices respectively.

Let
\{P(t)\}_{t=0}^{t=1}
be a continuously differentiable price path. For a price change,
\Delta
, from
P^{0}
and
P^{1}
, and income fixed at
\bar{X}
,
Hausman and Newey 2016
show that
S(A)
is a solution to the following ordinary differential equation (ODE):

\frac{ds(t,A)}{dt}=-\phi(p(t),\bar{x}-s(t,A),A)^{T}\frac{dp(t)}{dt},\quad s(1,A)=0

S(A)
does not depend on a particular choice of price path,
\{P(t)\}_{t=0}^{t=1}
provided the Slutsky matrix,
\mathscr{S}=D_{p}\psi(p,x,u)+\partial_{x}\psi(p,x,u)\psi(p,x,u)
, is both negative-semidefinite and symmetric. This suggests the need to verify these Slutsky conditions for demands before performing a nonparametric welfare analysis to ensure that the EV, as a solution to the ODE above, is invariant to the choice of path of prices.

Generally, nonparametric welfare analysis, as surveyed in
Hausman and Newey 2017
and also recently in
Bhattacharya 2024
, takes as given that the population demand function arises from rational consumers, i.e., it satisfies the integrability conditions of
Hurwicz and Uzawa 1971
, including Slutsky symmetry, which ensures existence of an indirect utility function. In practice, these integrability conditions may not hold and need to be verified instead of being assumed or imposed by the researcher. To this end, our work offers a nonparametric test of Slutsky symmetry for demand functions admitting unrestricted unobserved heterogeneity, which has hitherto been elusive. This test along with the test for negative-semidefiniteness by
Dette et al. 2016
allow researchers to check whether Slutsky conditions hold up in data before they apply the tools for nonparametric welfare analysis with general unobserved heterogeneity.

3.2
Implications for nonparametric welfare analysis with multiple goods and individual unobserved heterogeneity

Hausman and Newey 2016
apply the identification result in
Hoderlein and Mammen 2007
and negative semidefiniteness to show that quantile demand is true demand. In our notation,
Hoderlein and Mammen 2007
identify local average structural derivatives as follows:

E\left[\partial_{w_{1}}\psi\mid W=w^{*},e_{i}^{\prime}Y=k_{\alpha,i}\left(w^{*}\right)\right]=\partial_{1,w_{1}}k_{\alpha,i}\left(w^{*}\right)

Hausman and Newey 2016
show that for demand of a single good in a two-good market where one of the two goods is a numeraire, quantile demands represent true demands. In particular, in our notation, they show that quantile demands satisfy the Slutsky negative semidefiniteness condition as follows:

\displaystyle\partial_{1,w_{1}}k_{\alpha,i}\left(w^{*}\right)+\partial_{x}k_{\alpha,i}\left(w^{*}\right)k_{\alpha,i}\left(w^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=E\left[\partial_{w_{1}}\psi+k_{\alpha,i}\left(w^{*}\right)\partial_{x}\psi\mid W=w^{*},e_{i}^{\prime}Y=k_{\alpha,i}\left(w^{*}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=E\left[\partial_{w_{1}}\psi+Y_{i}\partial_{x}\psi\mid W=w^{*},e_{i}^{\prime}Y=k_{\alpha,i}\left(w^{*}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=E\left[\partial_{w_{1}}\psi+\psi\partial_{x}\psi\mid W=w^{*},e_{i}^{\prime}Y=k_{\alpha,i}\left(w^{*}\right)\right]\leq 0

where the last inequality follows from the negative semidefiniteness of the Slutsky matrix for a smooth single-valued demand function,
\psi
(note that we use
\psi
for a single-valued demand function to distinguish it from the vector-valued demand function,
\phi
).

The identification result in
Hoderlein and Mammen 2007
applies to the univariate setting where only the demand for a single good changes, as mentioned. Indeed,
Hausman and Newey 2016
consider a two-good setting with a single-valued smooth demand function and illustrate how bounds on the average surplus can be constructed using knowledge of the magnitude of income effects. In their two-good case, the negative semidefinitess condition and the budget constraint are necessary and sufficient for a function to be a demand function. This is because in the two-good case Slutsky symmetry is satisfied by construction; WARP coincides with SARP (
Cherchye et al. 2018
), and therefore VARP. In the case of multiple good and vector-valued demand, both the negative semidefiniteness and the Slutsky symmetry condition should be satisfied for a valid vector-valued demand function, as WARP no longer coincides with VARP. The two-good nonparametric welfare analysis in
Hausman and Newey 2016
, therefore, does not scale up naturally to this economic environment insofar as
Hoderlein and Mammen 2007
’s identification argument, which yields only the negative semidefiniteness condition, does not apply to multivariate demands.

Circumventing this limitation, recent literature has abandoned the quantile-based approach or imposed restrictions on heterogeneity.
Maes and Malhotra 2024b
propose an approach to nonparametric welfare analysis based on compensating variation and using only moments of the distribution of the observed demand distribution while
imposing
Slutsky symmetry in their analysis of the many-good case. On the other hand,
Hoderlein and Vanhems 2018
estimate the distribution of welfare using quantiles but assume that demand is monotonic in scalar unobserved heterogeneity. However, this restriction is stringent as it implies rank invariance in the conditional distribution of demand when prices or income change.

Our extension of
Hoderlein and Mammen 2007
in Lemma
2.1
along with the symmetry condition in equation (
1
) restores the quantile-based approach to nonparametric welfare analysis without restricting unobserved heterogeneity, thereby allowing researchers to use the entire marginal and conditional distributions of demand of multiple goods rather than only
moments. By extending the identification argument of
Hoderlein and Mammen 2007
to a bivariate setting, we augment
Hausman and Newey 2016
by showing in Theorem
2.1
that with vector-valued demand functions under general heterogeneity, true demands are identified by conditional and marginal quantiles that satisfy the equality in (
1
) almost surely.

Notice that, unlike in
Hausman and Newey 2016
who show that in their setting
marginal
quantile demands coincide with true demands via the negative semidefiniteness condition, in our case we show that
conditional
quantile demands for each good coincide with true demands of the good, only to up to a correction factor
C_{ij}
in (
3
) induced by conditioning on the quantile of the other good. Under additional assumptions, this correction factor is second-order, and conditional quantile demands behave like Hicksian demands. Suppose that for a given change,
\Delta p
, the following restriction holds:
\max C_{ij}=o(\|\Delta p\|)
(same condition holds for
C_{ji}
). Then as
|\Delta p|\rightarrow 0
,
\max C_{ji}=o(1)
and the correction factor vanishes. We can rationalize this restriction by arguing that for sufficiently small price changes, demand is locally nearly linear, and hence the nonlinear heterogeneity implied by the
“cross-quantile”
effects due to conditioning on the quantile of the other good is second-order.

If
L=2
, then we are back to
Hoderlein and Mammen 2007
and the correction factor in (
1
) disappears without additional restrictions. Otherwise, our result suggests that if we want to generalize the nonparametric welfare analysis of
Hausman and Newey 2016
to a setting with multiple goods, where Slutsky symmetry has empirical content, then without any additional restrictions, we need to work with the more general symmetry condition in (
1
) and grapple with the analytical complications thereof in downstream nonparametric welfare analysis. We leave further methodological work exploiting the implications of this identification result in nonparametric welfare analysis to future research.

4
A Practical Test for Slutsky Symmetry

In this section, we provide an estimation approach for the Slutsky symmetry condition derived in Theorem
2.1
along with a test for the condition. This is only one approach. To estimate the objects in Theorem
2.1
, we leverage the theory of estimation of conditional quantiles based on series developed in
Belloni et al. 2019
and recent work by
Cattaneo et al. 2024a
on boundary adaptive estimation of conditional distributions and conditional densities based on local polynomial regressions. Since we have to perform simultaneous inference for all
i,j
pairs of goods, our test involves a multiple testing procedure to achieve nominal asymptotic coverage. For this procedure, we closely follow
Lehman and Romano 2022
.

4.1
Consistency

We restrict our attention to the left hand side of equation (
1
), as the ensuing arguments apply
mutatis mutandis
to the right hand side. Our goal in this section is to furnish consistent estimators of the components of this expression, namely:

\displaystyle\nabla_{p,1}k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right),\partial_{x,1}k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right),k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right),\nabla_{p}k_{\alpha,j}(w^{*}),

\displaystyle\partial_{x}k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right),\partial_{2}k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right),\text{ and }\quad\frac{\partial_{2}F_{Y_{i}\mid W=w^{*},Y_{j}=k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)}\left(k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)\right)}{f_{Y_{i}\mid W=w^{*},Y_{j}=k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)}\left(k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)\right)}.

We then combine these estimators to prove via continuous mapping theorem that the sample analogue of this expression is consistent.

We estimate each of the components by series methods. Following
Belloni et al. 2019
, we approximate the conditional quantiles using series approximating functions as follows:

k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)=Z(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right))^{\prime}\beta(\gamma),\quad\text{and}\quad k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)=Z(w^{*})^{\prime}\beta(\alpha),

where
Z(\cdot)
is a series of approximating functions of dimension
m=m_{n}
where
n
emphasizes that the dimension increases with the sample size; and
\beta(.)
is a quantile regression (QR) coefficient process.
Z(.)
encompasses all transformations of its arguments with good approximation properties such as power series, trigonometrics, splines, local polynomials or wavelets
[
Belloni et al. 2019
]
.

With respect to
k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)

=Z(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right))^{\prime}\beta(\gamma)
, the expression
\beta(\gamma)

=(\beta_{1}(\gamma),\beta_{2}(\gamma),...,(\beta_{m}(\gamma))
is a solution to the following QR-series approximation problem:

\min_{\beta\in\mathcal{R}^{m}}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{\gamma}(Y_{i}-Z(w^{*},k_{\alpha},j(w^{*})^{\prime}\beta-\rho_{\gamma}(Y_{i}-k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)],

where
\rho_{\gamma}
is an asymmetric loss function in quantile regression modelling known as the
“check function”
(see
Koenker 2005
).
R_{\gamma}(w^{*},k_{\alpha})=k_{\gamma}(w^{*},k_{\alpha}))-Z(w^{*},k_{\alpha})^{\prime}\beta(\gamma)
is the attendant QR-approximation error. We can construct an analogous population problem for
\beta(\alpha)
.

The following QR series estimator follows naturally:

\hat{k}_{\gamma}(w^{*},\hat{k}_{\alpha}(w^{*}))=Z(w,\hat{k}_{\alpha})^{\prime}\hat{\beta}(\gamma)

where
\hat{\beta}(\gamma)=\argmin_{\beta\in\mathcal{R}^{m}}\mathbb{E}_{n}[\rho_{\gamma}(Y_{i}-Z(w,k_{\alpha},j(w^{*}))^{\prime}\beta(\gamma)]
for
\gamma
ranging over
\Gamma=[0,1]
.
\mathbb{E}_{n}
is the empirical analogue of the expectation operator,
\mathbb{E}
and
Y_{i}=e_{i}Y
. This is a two-step estimator whereby
k_{\alpha}(w^{*})
is estimated in the first step for each
\alpha
and the estimates are used in the second step to estimate
\hat{k}_{\gamma}(w^{*},\hat{k}_{\alpha}(w^{*}))
. The QR-series estimator for
k_{\alpha}(w^{*})
follows analogously:

\hat{k}_{\alpha}(w)=Z(w)^{\prime}\hat{\beta}(\alpha)

for
\alpha
ranging over
A=[0,1]
.
The estimators of QR coefficient processes,
\hat{\beta}(.)=\{\beta(\gamma):\gamma\in\Gamma\}
and
\hat{\beta}(.)=\{\beta(\alpha):\alpha\in A\}
, can be computed using interior point methods even with multiple quantile indices
A
and
\Gamma
(see
Koenker and Portnoy 1996
and
Koenker 2005
).

4.1.1
Estimation of derivatives of conditional and marginal quantiles

Except for

\frac{\partial_{2}F_{Y_{i}\mid W=w^{*},Y_{j}=k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)}\left(k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)\right)}{f_{Y_{i}\mid W=w^{*},Y_{j}=k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)}\left(k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)\right)},

all other terms involve a derivative of a conditional quantile and a marginal quantile. For these derivatives, which are linear functionals, we exploit the linearity of series approximations
[
Belloni et al. 2019
]
, and write
k_{\gamma}(w,k_{\alpha}(w))
as follows:

\theta(w^{*},k_{\alpha})=k_{\gamma}(w^{*},k_{\alpha}(w^{*}))=\ell(w,k_{\alpha})^{\prime}\beta(\gamma)+r_{\gamma}(w^{*},k_{\alpha})

where
\ell(w,k_{\alpha})^{\prime}\beta(\gamma)=\partial_{k}Z(w,k_{\alpha})
and
r_{\gamma}(w,k_{\alpha})=\partial_{k}R_{\gamma}(w,k_{\alpha})
is a QR-series approximation error.
\partial_{k}
denotes a partial derivative with respect to element
k

k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)

k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)=\ell(w^{*})^{\prime}\beta(\alpha)+r_{\alpha}(w^{*})
.
It follows that for the respective derivatives in Theorem
2.1
, we have:

\displaystyle\nabla_{p,1}k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{p,1}Z\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)^{\prime}\beta(\gamma);

\displaystyle\partial_{x,1}k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x,1}Z\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)^{\prime}\beta(\gamma);

\displaystyle\nabla_{p}k_{\alpha,j}(w^{*})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{p}Z(w^{*})^{\prime}\beta(\alpha);

\displaystyle\partial_{x}k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}Z(w^{*})^{\prime}\beta(\gamma)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\partial_{2}k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\partial_{2}Z(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right))^{\prime}\beta(\gamma).

We require the following standard regularity assumptions.

Assumption 4.1
.

Let
Z_{j}(.)
be the
j^{th}
element of the vector,
Z(.)
. The following conditions for the sequence,
\{Z_{j}\}
hold: i) each
Z_{j}(.)
is continuously differentiable in all its arguments, up to a null set; ii)
Z_{j}(.)
’s are orthornomal; iii) the sequence
\{Z_{j}\}
forms a complete basis for the Hilbert space
L^{2}([0,1])

Assumption
4.1
is standard. Smooth transformations such as piecewise polynomials, wavelets and splines satisfy this continuity requirement as do several other transformations. Assumption
4.1
part (iii) implies, in particular, that as
n\rightarrow\infty
, there exists an
M_{n}
such that:

\displaystyle Z(w^{*})^{\prime}\beta(\alpha)

\displaystyle=k_{\alpha}(w^{*})+o_{p}(1)\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle Z\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)^{\prime}\beta(\gamma)

\displaystyle=k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)^{\prime}\beta(\gamma)+o_{p}(1),

where

\displaystyle Z(w^{*})

\displaystyle=(Z_{1}(w^{*}),Z_{2}(w^{*}),\dots Z_{M_{n}}(w^{*}))\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle Z\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(Z_{1}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right),Z_{2}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right),\dots Z_{M_{n}}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)

An important choice in series estimation is the number of terms in the vector
Z
. If the number of series terms is too small, this will result in a non-negligible bias. On the other hand, if the number of terms is too large, then the variance will be high due to estimation of too many parameters. An optimal number of series terms optimally trades off the bias and the variance. We are not aware of any procedure for selecting the optimal number of series terms in nonparametric conditional quantiles based on series. We, therefore, follow
Belloni et al. 2019
in using an ad hoc cross-validation procedure to determine the optimal number of series terms and choosing a number smaller than the optimal one for undersmoothing. Future theoretical work on conditional quantiles based on series could explore and assess other tuning parameter selection methods such as Mallows’ Criterion, Stein’s unbiased risk estimation or Lepski’s method
[
Chetverikov 2024
]
.

Proposition

Proposition 4.1
.

Under Assumption

\displaystyle\nabla_{p,1}\hat{k}_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},\hat{k}_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{p,1}Z(w^{*},\hat{k}_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right))^{\prime}\hat{\beta}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\partial_{x,1}\hat{k}_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},\hat{k}_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x,1}Z(w^{*},\hat{k}_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right))^{\prime}\hat{\beta}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\nabla_{p}\hat{k}_{\alpha,j}(w^{*})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{p}Z(w^{*})^{\prime}\hat{\beta}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\hat{k}_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}Z(w^{*})^{\prime}\hat{\beta}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\partial_{2}\hat{k}_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},\hat{k}_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)

4.1.2
Estimation of derivatives of the conditional distribution function

We now turn to the estimation of the ratio term. For the numerator, which takes the form
\partial_{2}F_{Y_{i}\mid W=w^{*},Y_{j}=k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)}\left(k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)\right)
, we need our estimator of the conditional distribution function,
F_{Y_{i}\mid W=w^{*},Y_{j}=k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)}\left(k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)\right)
to be differentiable for the differential operation to make sense. Standard estimators, such as the
“double kernel”
approach proposed by
Yao and Tong 2000
, the local linear approach of
Fan et al. 1996
and the modified Nadaraya Watson estimator of
Hall et al. 1999
, are generally discontinuous in
Y
because they involve indicator functions, and therefore do not satisfy this differentiability requirement. Other issues that plague estimation of the conditional distribution function include boundary and design biases, a multiplicity of hyperparameters to fine-tune, and lack of monotonicity or boundedness of the estimated CDF.

To get around these challenges, we use the boundary adaptive local polynomial regression proposed by
Cattaneo et al. 2024a
. Proposition
4.2
below shows that the ratio of the derivatives of the local polynomial regression based estimator of the conditional distribution function is consistent for the ratio term under some regularity assumptions we spell out in the appendix.

Proposition 4.2
.

Under Assumptions
A.2
-
A.4
, the ratio of derivatives of the smoothed estimated CDF,

\frac{\partial_{2}\hat{F}_{Y_{i}\mid W=w^{*},Y_{j}=\hat{k}_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)}\left(k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},\hat{k}_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)\right)}{\hat{f}_{Y_{i}\mid W=w^{*},Y_{j}=\hat{k}_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)}\left(\hat{k}_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},\hat{k}_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)\right)}

\frac{\partial_{2}F_{Y_{i}\mid W=w^{*},Y_{j}=k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)}\left(k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)\right)}{f_{Y_{i}\mid W=w^{*},Y_{j}=k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)}\left(k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)\right)}
.

4.2
The Testing Procedure

We first recall that our estimation proceeds in two steps. In the first step, we estimate
\hat{k}_{\alpha,j}(w^{*})
with
\alpha
ranging across the index set and plug the estimates into the estimators of the derivatives of
k_{\gamma,i}\left(w^{*},k_{\alpha,j}\left(w^{*}\right)\right)
discussed in the previous section. This first-step estimation potentially biases the asymptotic variance of the estimator, though the direction of the bias cannot be determined ex ante without further assumptions. While it is possible to invoke enough smoothness conditions for the nonparametric first-step estimation so that the sampling variation from that step does not have first-order influence on the asymptotic variance
[
Mammen et al. 2012
]
, we instead account for this step in the resampling procedure that we describe later in this section.

In this section, we provide a pairwise test statistic for good
i
and good
j
that corresponds to the individual hypothesis implied by the condition in Theorem
2.1
. We then construct a joint test statistic that corresponds to an intersection hypothesis for all the pairs of the
L-1
goods. Finally, we discuss how we obtain critical values and estimate unknown rates of convergence using sub-sampling.

4.2.1
Test Statistic for Individual Hypotheses

We provide a test statistic for any
(i,j)
-pair of goods. This thenforms the basis for a supremum-type test statistic that permits simultaneous inference over all
(i,j)
-pairs. Let
S_{i}
and
S_{j}
denote the left hand side and the right hand size in (
1
) respectively. This yields the following individual null hypothesis:

H^{0}_{ij}:S_{i}=S_{j},

with the alternative hypothesis:

H^{1}_{ij}:S_{i}\neq S_{j}.

We can also rewrite the null hypothesis as

H^{0}_{ij}:S_{ij}=0,

where
S_{ij}=S_{i}-S_{j}
.
Then assuming independence of the distribution of the
i
and
j
goods in the population, we obtain the following putative t-statistic:

T_{n,(i,j)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\tau_{n}\hat{S}_{ij},

(2)

where
\tau_{n}
is a sequence of numbers; and
\hat{S}_{ij}
is a consistent estimator of
S_{ij}
.

With a suitable choice of the normalizing sequence,
\tau_{n}
, the test statistic in (
2
) converges to a non-degenerate limit distribution. One complication is that we do not have its closed form expression. Furthermore, the rate of convergence
\tau_{n}
is also not known. We therefore approximate the limit distribution and the convergence rate via sub-sampling. Subsampling allows us to approximate the sampling distribution of our statistic by computing the values of the statistic on smaller subsets of the data . Subsampling only requires that the limit distribution of the statistic is non-degenerate.

We can view
\hat{S}_{ij}
as
\hat{S}_{ij}=\Phi(\hat{\theta})
where
\theta\mapsto\Phi(\theta))
is a smooth functional mapping the collection of
“true”
objects,
\theta=\left\{k_{\alpha,j},\nabla_{p}k_{\alpha,j},\partial_{x}k_{\alpha,j},k_{\gamma,i}(\cdot,\cdot),\ldots,f_{Y_{i}\mid W,Y_{j}},\ldots\right\}
to the difference,
S_{ij}
and
\hat{S}_{ij}
is obtained by replacing
\theta
by its sample counterpart,
\hat{\theta}
. Given that every element,
\hat{\theta}_{n,r}
of
\hat{\theta}_{n}
, converges in distribution at some rate,
r_{n}
, then, provided the map,
\Phi
, is Hadamard differentiable almost everywhere with a nonvanishing gradient,
\hat{S}_{ij}
admits a functional delta method and (under the null hypothesis) converges to a non-degenerate limit distribution at some rate,
\tau_{n}
. The subsampling procedure allows to estimate the unknown rate of convergence of the estimator,
\tau_{n}
together with the limit distribution of the statistic. We defer details of this subsampling procedure to Section 3.2.4. Note that for bootstrap consistency, we would need to additionally ensure that either the bootstrap is consistent for
\hat{\theta}
(and therefore the consistency is inherited by the map
\Phi
) or the limit distribution of
\hat{\theta}
is Gaussian at
\theta_{0}

[
Fang and Santos 2019
]
. Another candidate resampling procedure is the one proposed by
Kuchibhotla et al. 2023
under milder conditions than those for the bootstrap, but the procedure applies to estimators that satisfy some median unbiasedness condition that needs to be verified on a case-by-case basis for non-standard estimators like ours.

4.2.2
Test Statistic for an Intersection Hypothesis

We want to test the individual null hypothesis in Section
3.1
for all
(i,j)
-pairs. We have
L-1
goods for
L\geq 2
, which gives a total of
{L-1\choose 2}
individual hypotheses. If we reject the null for even a single individual null hypothesis, then the symmetry condition in Theorem
2.1
is violated. For a symmetric Slutsky matrix, we expect that none of the individual hypotheses will be rejected. As is well documented in the statistics literature (see
Lehman and Romano 2022
for a full treatment), performing each of the individual hypotheses in turn at a given nominal level leads to a multiple testing problem whereby the probability of a false rejection increases in the number of individual hypotheses and thus the true size of the test for each individual hypothesis does not coincide with the nominal size. We, therefore, use the generic multiple hypothesis testing procedure detailed in
Lehman and Romano 2022
.

Let
P
be the true probability distribution generating the data and
I(P)\in{1,\dots s}
be the indices of the set of true hypotheses. Note that we can write the null hypothesis in terms of probability distributions. From this perspective,
(i,j)\in I(P)
if
P\in\omega_{ij}
. Then define the
intersection hypothesis
:

H_{K}:p\in\bigcap_{(i,j)\in K}\omega_{ij}.

That is, all
H_{ij}
with
(i,j)\in K
are true for
K\subseteq\{1,\dots{L-1\choose 2}\}
.
The basic idea is that we want critical values to approximate the distribution of
\max_{(i,j)\in K}T_{n,(i,j)}
, which is the statistic corresponding to the null intersection hypothesis above.

Let
T_{n,r_{1}}\geq T_{n,r_{2}}\geq T_{n,r_{3}}\geq\dots T_{n,r_{s}}
be the observed ordered test statistics with corresponding hypotheses:
H_{(1)},H_{(2)},\dots H_{(S)}
, and let
\hat{c}_{n,k(1-\alpha)}

be the critical value for testing the intersection hypothesis,
H_{K}
, at nominal level,
\alpha
. We can use a generic stepdown multiple testing procedure, as outlined in Chapter 9 of
Lehman and Romano 2022
. Let
K_{1}=\{1,2,\dots S\}
. Here
K_{1}
is the index corresponding to all the individual hypotheses. If
T_{n,r_{1}}\leq\hat{c}_{n,k_{1}(1-\alpha)}
, then we accept all hypotheses and stop; otherwise, we reject
H_{1}
and proceed with comparison of
T_{n,r_{2}}
against the critical value,
\hat{c}_{n,k_{2}(1-\alpha)}
where
K_{2}=\{2,\dots S\}
. As before, we accept all the remaining null hypotheses if the test statistic does not exceed
\hat{c}_{n,k_{2}(1-\alpha)}
and stop; otherwise, we continue. Performing the procedure this way controls the family-wise error rate (FWER):

\operatorname{FWER}_{P}=P\left\{\text{ reject }H_{i,j}\text{ for any }(i,j)\in I(P)\right\}\leq\alpha.

4.2.3
From Multiple Testing to a Single Test

Only the first step of the generic stepdown procedure outlined above is salient; if we reject the null hypothesis corresponding to the intersection of all individual hypotheses, then the symmetry condition in Theorem
2.1
is violated so what happens after this step is irrelevant to our conclusion. So, continuing with the procedure after the rejection of
H_{K_{1}}
is not necessary. It turns out that we do not need to perform the test step by step in this manner. Instead, we reduce the multiple testing problem of controlling the
\operatorname{FWER}_{P}
to a single test of controlling type I error as in standard single test settings
[
Lehman and Romano 2022
, Theorem 9.1.3]
. The only requirement for invoking the theorem is monotonicity: for any
K\supset I(P)
,

\hat{c}_{n,K(1-\alpha)}\geq\hat{c}_{n,I(P)(1-\alpha)}.

This monotonicity condition holds in our setting since, whenever
K\supset I(P)
it holds that
\max\{T_{n,(i,j)}\mid(i,j)\in K\}\geq\max\{T_{n,(i,j)}\mid(i,j)\in I(P)\}
so it follows that
\hat{c}_{n,K(1-\alpha)}\geq\hat{c}_{n,I(P)(1-\alpha)}
.

The outstanding question is how to choose

4.2.4
Estimating the Unknown Rate and Critical Values via Subsampling

We now provide subsampling procedures for estimating the unknown rate of convergence,
\tau_{n}
and the critical value for the intersection hypothesis in the previous section. The procedure for estimating the unknown rate that is proposed in this paper draws upon
Bertail et al. 1999
while the subsampling construction of the critical value for our multiple testing procedure draws from
Romano and Wolf 2005
. As shown in
Bertail et al. 1999
, the two step procedure of using subsampling to estimate the unknown rate first and then using subsampling to construct confidence regions for parameters yields asymptotically valid inference.

To account for the generated regressor problem acknowledged in Section 3.1, we incorporate sample splitting in the subsampling procedure we propose in this section. Variants of sample splitting are now routinely used in machine learning to mitigate model selection and regularization biases from first step estimation (see, e.g
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
and
Chernozhukov et al. 2022
). In our case, the concern is that we would be using the same sample for estimating
\hat{k}_{\alpha,j}(w^{*})
and the two-step estimators into which
\hat{k}_{\alpha,j}(w^{*})
is plugged, which contaminates inference. To alleviate this concern, in the subsampling procedure, we split the sample in each iteration and separately estimate the first and second step estimators on the two sample splits. The price to pay for this is computational: performing both estimation steps on two separate sample splits in each iteration is computationally expensive. Since we also estimate rate of convergence from the data, the subsampling procedure along with the sample splitting strategy accounts for the bias in inference induced by the first step estimation of the marginal quantile. The sample-splitting also potentially induces other nice theoretical properties as shown in other tests in the statistics literature (.e.g.
Chakravarti et al. 2019

Wasserman et al. 2020
and
Kuchibhotla et al. 2023
).

The first task is to estimate the unknown rate of convergence,
\tau_{n}
. We use the subsampling approach proposed by
Bertail et al. 1999
. Besides some weak consistency requirements, we assume that the rate is polynomial in the sample size, namely:
\tau_{n}=n^{\alpha}
. We follow the procedure in
Bertail et al. 1999
, and we refer the reader to Appendix C for details of the procedure as applied in our setting.

The next task is to find the critical values for the test statistic corresponding to the intersection hypothesis in section 3.2.2. In section 3.2.3, we defined
L_{n,K(P)}
to be the distribution under
P
of
\max\{T_{n,(ij)}\mid(i,j)\in K\}
. We estimate the corresponding distribution function,
L_{n,K}(x,P)
, using the following empirical distribution constructed through subsampling:

\hat{L}_{n,K}(x,\mathbb{P}_{n})=\binom{n}{b}^{-1}\sum_{m}1\{\max\{T^{m}_{b,(ij)}\mid(i,j)\in K\}\leq x\},

where
b
is the size of each subsample of the data and

indexes each of the
n\choose b
datasets. The estimate of the critical value can then be backed out as follows:

\hat{c}_{n,K}(1-\alpha)=\inf\left\{x:\hat{L}_{n,K}(x,\mathbb{P}_{n})\geq 1-\alpha\right\}.

This critical value achieves strong
\operatorname{FWER}_{p}
control, at least asymptotically. We formalize this result in the following proposition:

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose the following conditions hold:

1.

,
\frac{b}{n}\rightarrow{0}
and
\frac{\tau_{b}}{\tau_{n}}\rightarrow{0}

2.

The subsampling approximation satisfies:
\rho(\hat{L}_{n,K}(x,\mathbb{P}_{n}),L_{n,K}(x))\xrightarrow{P}0
for any metric

metrizing weak convergence on

3.

The subsampling critical values satisfy:

\limsup\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}P\{\operatorname{max}(T_{n,(i,j)}:(i,j)\in I(P)>\hat{c}_{n,K}(1-\alpha)\}\leq\alpha.

Then

is a weak consistency condition on the empirical subsampling distribution, while Assumption 2 is a weak consistency condition on the estimated critical values. Assumption 2 is satisfied when the estimator or test statistic, suitably normalized, has a nondegenerate limit distribution. On the other hand, Assumption 3
requires some reasonable behavior of the estimated critical value: the estimated critical value that is used to test the intersection hypothesis is
asymptotically
level

Proposition

While this paper provides one test based on the symmetry condition in (
1
): the supremum-type test in this section, other tests are possible. For example, one may argue that our test is stringent because it focuses on a
“worst-case deviation”
: the test is designed to reject if the symmetry condition is violated even for just one pair of goods. Sympathetic to that concern, we would suggest an
L^{2}
distance type of test analogous to the one used by
Haag et al. 2009
in a different setting from ours. Instead of focusing on
“worst case deviation”
, the
L^{2}
test averages the squared deviation over the entire range of
w
. In that case, the null hypothesis is as follows:

H_{0}:\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=S_{j}\right)=1,\forall j\neq i

An alternative hypothesis would be
H_{1}:\mathbb{P}\left(S_{i}=S_{j},\forall j\neq i\right)<1
(
Haag et al. 2009
). Here
S_{i}
and
S_{j}
are the two sides of (
1
) while in the setting of
Haag et al. 2009
, these are cross-derivatives of expenditure share functions. The following equivalent formulation of the null hypothesis, a la
Haag et al. 2009
, could then be adapted to construct the test statistic:

\Gamma_{S}=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{j<i}\left(S_{i}-S_{j}\right)^{2}a(W)\right)=0,

where
a(W)
is some nonnegative and bounded weighing function. A test statistic would be constructed by obtaining a sample analogue of
\Gamma_{S}
and suitably rescaling it. Note that assumptions on the support of the weighting function are invoked to avoid boundary problems and to guarantee equivalence between the two null hypotheses (see
Haag et al. 2009
). The validity of the accompanying test therefore depends on the validity of these assumptions. Furthermore, while the
L^{2}
test might have more power when there is a moderate deviation from symmetry over the support of the test statistic, it may be insensitive to sharp local deviations since these are
“averaged out”
.

5
Conclusion

We provide a fully nonparametric test for the symmetry of the Slutsky matrix, complementing existing work that provides a test for the negative semidefiniteness of the matrix. This work, therefore, closes the gap on testing full rationality and provides a testable symmetry condition relevant for nonparametric welfare analysis in general nonparametric demand models with nonseparable unobserved heterogeneity. Incidentally, the results show that the symmetry condition is testable using
conditional
quantile restrictions. This complements recent non-testability results that show that symmetry is not testable using the average Slutsky matrix
[
Kono 2025
]
. The method developed in this paper can be implemented using existing R packages, such as the
lcpde
by
Cattaneo et al. 2024b
for the derivatives of the conditional distribution and the
quantreg.nonpar
package of
Lipsitz et al. 2016
for a partially linear specification of the conditional quantile regressions.

A.6
Estimating the rate of convergence in subsampling

We provide a description of the procedure of estimating the rate of convergence, following steps in
Bertail et al. 1999
. As in
Bertail et al. 1999
, we assume that
\tau_{n}=n^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
. It is possible to allow for a log-factor in this rate
[
Bertail et al. 1999
,
Romano and Wolf 2005
]
.

Let
b_{i,n},i=1,\ldots I
be subsamples (that is,
b_{i,n}<n
for each
i=1,\cdots I
) such that
b_{i,n}=n^{B_{i}}
for
1>B_{1}>B_{2}\ldots>B_{I}
. Let
K_{b_{n}}\left(x\mid\underline{X}_{n}\right)\equiv K_{b_{n}}\left(x\mid\underline{X}_{n},1\right)
be the subsampling distribution of the root,
T_{n,(i,j)}-\theta
, where
T_{n,(ij)}=\hat{S}_{ij}
,
\theta=S_{ij}
and
\underline{X}_{n}
are data (assumed to be identically and independently distributed). Notice that the normalizing sequence for this root is just constant, i.e.,
\tau_{n}=1
. For the distribution function
K_{b_{n}}\left(x\mid\underline{X}_{n}\right)
, let
K^{-1}_{b_{n}}\left(U\mid\underline{X}_{n}\right)
be the corresponding quantile transformation where
U\in(0,1)
. Given some partition of
U
,
U_{j},j=1,2\dots J
, the following proposition shows that
\alpha
can be feasibly estimated.

Bertail et al. 1999
show that under the above conditions:

\alpha_{I,J}\equiv-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{I}\left(y_{i,\cdot}-\bar{y}\right)\left(\log\left(b_{i,n}\right)-\overline{\log}\right)}{\sum_{i=1}^{I}\left(\log\left(b_{i,n}\right)-\overline{\log}\right)^{2}}

where
y_{i,\cdot}=J^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}y_{i,j}=J^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log\left(K_{b_{i,n}}^{-1}\left(U_{j}\mid\underline{X}_{n}\right)\right)
,
\bar{y}=(IJ)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{j=1}^{J}y_{i,j}
and
\overline{\log}=I^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\log\left(b_{i,n}\right)

Theorem 1 in
Bertail et al. 1999
guarantees that this estimator converges to
\alpha
at the rate of
1/\text{log}(n)
. The test statistics for the individual hypotheses can now be written as
T_{n,(ij)}=\hat{\tau}\hat{S_{ij}}
as we have done in the paper.
