Skeletal editing of ether-based electrolyte diluents by oxygen-distal fluorination for energy-dense Li metal battery
Authors: Xingmin Yu, Yong Li, Zhaofen Wang, Haichen Huang, Yaoyao Liu, Lequan Deng, Jun Zhan, Xianglin Yin, Haoying Qi, Yushuang Yang, Shuhua Wang, Yuanhua Sang, Daixin Ye, Jian-Jun Wang, Hong Liu, Hao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnhancing cycling reversibility in high-energy-density lithium metal batteries necessitates precise management of electrolyte-derived electrochemical reactions at electrodes and interphases, yet recently developed localized high-concentration electrolytes suffer from limited tunability of these reactions for the non-solvation-participating nature of oxygen-proximal fluorinated diluents. Here we address this issue by synthesizing an oxygen-distal fluorinated di-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropoxyethane diluent whose molecular skeleton is strategically edited to position fluorine atoms distal to oxygen centers that attenuate electron-withdrawing effects at Li+-coordination sites, enabling: enhanced diluent/anion participation and reduced volatile solvents in solvation shells; atypical H-F bonding between diluent and solvent toward enhanced oxidation resistance; and promotion of diluent and salt-derived highly stable inorganic-rich interphase formation. This electrolyte achieves 99.8% Li plating/stripping Coulombic efficiency, 450 stable cycles in 4.5 V high-voltage Li || LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 cells, and 5.9-Ah, 504.6 Wh kg–1 (based on mass of all components including packaging) pouch cells that exhibits 0.053% per-cycle capacity decay. This work introduces oxygen-distal fluorination as a potential molecular skeleton editing strategy for stable energy-dense lithium metal batteries.Subject terms: Batteries, Batteries

Introduction

The growing demand for portable electronics and electric vehicles necessitates rechargeable batteries with higher energy densities1. Lithium metal is the ultimate negative electrode material for such high-energy-density Li batteries, owing to its highest specific capacity (3860 mAh g–1) and the lowest electrode potential (–3.04 V vs. standard hydrogen electrode) among known negative electrode candidates2–4. When paired with advanced high-voltage positive electrodes such as LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 (NCM811), lithium metal batteries (LMBs) achieve a specific energy exceeding 500 Wh kg–1 doubling that of commercial Li-ion batteries and making them critical for overcoming range limitations in electric transportation5. However, practical implementations of LMBs remain constrained by low reversibility of Li negative electrode reaction (scientifically measured as Li plating/stripping Coulombic efficiency, CE) and resultant fast battery capacity decay, primarily caused by detrimental side reactions between electrolyte and Li metal negative electrode6. At the Li negative electrode, the most commonly used, strong Li+-coordination electrolyte solvent (e.g. dimethoxyethane, DME; Fig. 1a) decomposes to form an unstable, low-ionic-conductivity and mechanically fragile organic solid electrolyte interphase (SEI). This DME solvent-derived organic SEI accelerates Li-electrolyte corrosion reaction, promotes dendritic Li growth, and aggravates capacity fade while raising safety concerns7. Concurrently, at the positive electrode, oxidative decomposition of electrolyte components (particularly DME) forms an unstable positive electrode (cathode)-electrolyte interphase (CEI), resulting in rapid structural degradation and capacity loss in positive electrode8. Given the electrolyte’s fundamental blood role as the ion-transport medium in battery, these intrinsic limitations of conventional electrolyte formulations critically impede the development of viable high-performance LMBs.

To address these challenges, extensive electrolyte optimization strategies have been explored, such as fluorinated electrolytes favoring enhanced oxidation stability and LiF-rich electrolyte-electrode interphases9–11, high-concentration electrolytes to mitigate the side reactions between Li metal and free solvent12, fluorinated diluent-based localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs) for further improving electrolyte stability while reducing viscosity13, electrolyte additives for constructing high-stability interphase14–16, electrolyte salt17 and solid-state electrolyte18. Among these strategies, LHCEs employing non-fluorinated solvents (especially DME) and oxygen-proximal fluorinated diluents (Fig. 1b) represent a particularly promising approach for improving the cycling stability of high-voltage Li metal batteries, as they concentrate almost all the volatile DME solvent into the inner Li+ ion solvation shell for Li+ ion coordination/transportation with negligible amount of unstable free solvent molecules, and segregate non-solvating fluorinated diluent outside the solvation shell in the bulk electrolyte, thereby reducing free DME solvent-induced parasitic reactions and enhancing oxidation resistance of electrolyte (Fig. 1c)6,13. However, current LHCE systems exhibit critical limitations: Wang et al. very recently discovered that these solvation-absent oxygen-proximal fluorinated diluents (especially 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropylether, TTE) were not involved in the solvated anion and DME solvent-dominated interfacial decomposition reactions (forming SEI and CEI), affording negligible impact on LMB performance19. Recent in-situ magnetic resonance and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy imaging characterization illustrate that the coordinated Li+-solvent solvation shell and released free solvent-abundant environment are locally concentrated at the electrode surface during battery operation, resulting in unstable solvent-derived SEI and CEI (Fig. 1c) that significantly limit the influence of non-solvating electrolyte molecule on interphase optimization or oxidation resistance enhancement20,21. Many recent LHCEs researches also support these discoveries on the minor effects of the non-solvating, oxygen-proximal fluorinated diluent on tuning the electrolyte solvation structure, interphase formation reaction or LMB performance, such as a newly developed oxygen-proximal fluorinated diluent di-1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethoxyethane (D4FEE) displays negligible Li+ coordination capability that yields suboptimal ~97.8% Li plating/stripping Coulombic efficiency,22 while another newly synthesized oxygen-proximal fluorinated diluent 3,3,4,4,5,5-hexafluorotetrahydropyran (HFTHP) minimally influences solvation structures during 4.3 V cycling23. This fundamental limitation originates from the strong electron-withdrawing nature of oxygen-proximal fluorinated groups in existing fluorinated diluent designs (such as TTE, D4FEE and HFTHP), which deplete electron density at proximal oxygen atoms (Fig. 1b), severely impairing Li+ coordination capacity of the diluent and necessitating a large proportion of performance-limiting DME solvent in electrolyte for Li+ ion coordination/transportation. Consequently, the development of fluorinated diluents that simultaneously deliver enhanced oxidation stability and adequate Li+ solvation participation ability remains an essential yet underexplored frontier for high-performance LHCEs in practical LMBs.

To solve these problems, here we propose an oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte molecular design strategy that features selective fluorination in the editing of the molecular skeleton at the positions distal to the ether oxygen moieties (Fig. 1d), addressing the issues of insufficient solvation capacity inherent in conventional oxygen-proximal fluorinated diluents, excessive reliance on unstable non-fluorinated solvents (e.g., DME) for lithium salt dissolution and ionic transportation, and compromised electrode/interphase/electrolyte stability in state-of-the-art oxygen-proximal fluorinated diluent-based LHCE technology. This oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte diluent molecular skeleton editing design enables holistic performance enhancement of LMBs through three interconnected mechanisms as shown in Fig. 1e: (1) By fluorination at oxygen-distal sites, the attenuated fluorine-induced electron-withdrawing effects at oxygen coordination sites preserve moderate diluent-lithium coordination capability, significantly increasing the proportion of stable fluorinated diluents and anions within the solvation shells while reducing volatile solvent content; (2) within the solvation shell, an atypical hydrogen bond emerges between fluorinated diluents and non-fluorinated DME solvents to enhance bulk oxidation resistance, complementing increased concentration of highly oxidation-resisting fluorinated diluent species in both the solvation shell and bulk electrolyte; (3) promoted participation of fluorinated diluent and anion (in the solvation shell) into interfacial reactions orchestrates the formation of inorganic-rich SEI and CEI with improved electrochemical stability compared to conventional non-fluorinated solvent-derived organic-dominated interphase. Owing to these merits, oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolytes deliver enhanced electrochemical performance: 99.8% Li plating/stripping Coulombic efficiency with uniform dendrite-free Li deposition morphology, 450-cycle operation in negative: positive capacity ratio (RN/P) of 2.45 and 4.5 V Li || NCM811 full cells with preserved positive electrode integrity and interfacial stability, as well as pouch cell operation at 504.6 Wh kg–1 for a 5.9 Ah cell (exhibits a low 0.053% capacity decay per cycle) and 547.9 Wh kg–1 for a 7.6 Ah cell (based on mass of all components including packaging). This oxygen-distal fluorinated molecular design strategy provides a framework for concurrently achieving high-energy-density, high-voltage compatibility and enhanced cycling stability in LMB systems.

Result

Electrolyte property based on oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte molecule design

In comparison to the conventional electrolytes employing commercially available Li+-solvating solvents (e.g., DME) and oxygen-proximal fluorinated diluents (e.g., D4FEE), herein developed, commercially unavailable oxygen-distal fluorinated di-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropoxyethane (D4FPE) molecule was specifically synthesized by a three-step Williamson ether synthesis procedure (Supplementary Fig. 1), including condensation of 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropoxyethanol with 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl p-toluenesulfonate, followed by distillation and dehydration to achieve a high-purity product (Supplementary Figs. 2–6). Density functional theory (DFT) calculations in Fig. 2a reveal that this synthesized D4FPE exhibits intermediate electrostatic potential at oxygen sites (–36.7 kcal mol–1) versus strong-solvating DME (–48.5 kcal mol–1) and non-coordinating D4FEE (–25.8 kcal mol–1). Correspondingly, a moderate Li+ binding energy of –9.3 kcal mol–1 and coordination bond lengths of 2.07 Å were observed for the Li+-D4FPE complex, contrasting sharply with the stronger coordination in Li+-DME (–13.3 kcal mol–1; 1.76 Å) and weaker interaction in Li+-D4FEE (–5.7 kcal mol–1; 2.66 Å)24–30 as further confirmed by the verification calculations in Supplementary Fig. 7 which demonstrate a moderate Li+ binding energy of D4FPE (also verified by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) results in Supplementary Fig. 8 and optimized coordinates for DME, D4FEE and D4FPE are provided in Supplementary Data 1 and 2). These electronic and coordination characteristics endow D4FPE with appropriate solubility, as further evidenced by its substantially enhanced lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) solubility (0.46 mol L–1) compared to oxygen-proximal fluorinated D4FEE (< 0.1 mol L–1, Supplementary Fig. 9). These comparative analyses demonstrate that oxygen-distal fluorination enables optimal solvation functionality while preserving fluorochemical advantages over conventional oxygen-proximal fluorinated molecules, which is a critical dual achievement for developing advanced LMB electrolytes.

Building upon prior analytical and experimental optimization of salt concentration and solvent ratio (Supplementary Fig. 10), three kinds of reference electrolyte were formulated: a conventional 2.54 M LiFSI in DME system (named as “DME-100%“), a LHCE comprising 2.54 M LiFSI in mixed solvent/diluent of DME/D4FEE (mole ratio of solvent: diluent = 43: 57, by replacing DME solvent with D4FEE diluent until saturation of LiFSI in electrolyte, named as “D4FEE-57%“), and another LHCE comprising 2.54 M LiFSI in DME/D4FPE electrolyte (mole ratio = 43: 57, for fair comparison between D4FPE and D4FEE diluents, named as “D4FPE-57%“). Capitalizing on D4FPE’s enhanced Li salt solubility, we further reduced oxidation-vulnerable DME content to prepare a fourth electrolyte: 2.54 M LiFSI in DME/D4FPE electrolyte (mole ratio = 28: 72, named as “D4FPE-72%“, Supplementary Fig. 11), which reaches the maximum LiFSI solubility that cannot further increase the ratio of D4FPE in the electrolyte. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed, and visualized snapshots along with the coordination numbers of Li+-FSI– and Li+-DME in the simulated electrolytes were obtained (Supplementary Figs. 12, 13). The solvation structure of DME-100% electrolyte exhibited abundant solvent-separated ion pairs (SSIPs) with coordination numbers (CNs) of FSI⁻ = 0.97 and DME = 2.57 (Fig. 2b and structure files for DME-100%, D4FEE-57% and D4FPE-72% are provided in Supplementary Data 3), which favors the formation of solvent-derived organic SEI31. In contrast, an anion-enriched solvation structure was observed in both D4FEE-57% electrolyte (CNs of FSI– = 1.12 (+15.46% vs. DME-100%, same as following) and CNs of DME = 2.31 (–10.13%), respectively) and D4FPE-72% electrolyte (CNs of FSI– = 1.51 (+55.67%), and CNs of DME = 1.32 (–48.6%)). But the D4FEE-57% electrolyte shows negligible participation of D4FEE in the inner solvation shell, as illustrated by the CNs of D4FEE being 0.2 (Fig. 2c). In comparison, the CNs of D4FPE in the D4FPE-72% electrolyte was 0.79 (+287.25% vs. D4FEE diluent in D4FEE-57%, Fig. 2d). These simulation results demonstrate that D4FPE successfully replaces a portion of strong-solvating DME to coordinate with the Li+ ions in the solvation shell, thereby enhancing the anti-oxidation stability of bulk electrolyte, promoting the formation of an anion and fluorinated diluent-enriched solvation structure, and favoring the formation of inorganic SEI and CEI derived from salt and diluent. Spectroscopic analyses corroborated this structural evolution: Raman spectra results in Figs. 2e, f further confirm the SSIP-dominant solvation structure (79.89%) in DME-100% electrolyte versus the aggregate (AGG)-dominant solvation configuration in D4FEE-57% (71.58%), D4FPE-57% (82.99%) and D4FPE-72% (88.96%) electrolytes that favors battery performance31,32. 7Li NMR spectroscopy in Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 8 revealed systematic downfield shifts of the 7Li signal versus DME-100% electrolyte (D4FEE-57%: +0.0147 ppm; D4FPE-57%: +0.0177 ppm; D4FPE-72%: +0.021 ppm). This can be attributed to the distinct coordination behaviors of the two fluorinated ethers: although D4FPE exhibits a stronger solvation strength than D4FEE, D4FPE is capable of integrating into the Li⁺ primary solvation sheath, whereas D4FEE cannot. Consequently, the D4FPE-72% exhibits a slightly weaker solvent-Li⁺ interaction33,34 as supported by the Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy in Supplementary Fig. 14. At the same time, herein developed electrolytes exhibit slightly higher viscosity and lower ionic conductivity, primarily due to the increased ionic radius of the solvated Li⁺ species (Supplementary Figs. 15, 16 and Supplementary Table 1). Notably, D4FPE-72% exhibits a slightly higher Li⁺ transference number and a lower ion diffusion coefficient, which can be attributed to its weaker solvation strength and its AGG-dominated solvation structure (Supplementary Figs. 17, 18 and Supplementary Table 2). Even though herein developed electrolytes afford slightly lower ionic conductivity, slightly higher viscosity and similar Li⁺ transference numbers, the advantages of AGG-dominant solvation structure and other modified electrolyte/interphase properties in herein developed electrolytes should still improve battery performance to a large extent. Based on the weakly solvating fluorinated diluent and anion-coordinating, DME-rare and AGG-dominant electrolyte structure, the D4FPE-72% electrolyte-derived SEI yielded the highest exchange current density of 0.22 mA cm–2 and the lowest Li+ migration barrier of 88.6 kJ mol–1 that is much better than D4FPE-57% (0.19 mA cm–2 and 93.7 kJ mol–1), D4FEE-57% (0.16 mA cm–2 and 102 kJ mol–1) and DME-100% electrolyte (0.1 mA cm–2 and 144 kJ mol–1), suggesting a higher ionic conducting and more stable interphase for Li metal negative electrode operation (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig.19). These results prove D4FPE’s capacity to act as a functional co-solvating diluent that concurrently coordinates with Li+ ion and reduces DME content while enhancing anion participation in the solvation shell, thus enables affording a DME-rare, moderately weak-solvation electrolyte for improving stability of electrolyte and electrode-electrolyte interphase at the same time.

DFT calculation results in Supplementary Fig. 20 demonstrate an elevated highest occupied molecular orbital energy for D4FPE (–8.62 eV) that is comparable to the oxygen-proximal fluorinated D4FEE (–9.06 eV) and substantially higher than oxidation-vulnerable DME (–7.56 eV). Simultaneously, the electrochemical stability window of the D4FPE-based electrolyte has been significantly expanded, owing to the improved oxidative stability of D4FPE and the increased proportion of AGGs (88.96% for D4FPE-72%, 82.99% for D4FPE-57%, 71.58% for D4FEE-57% and 7.79% for DME-100%) and Li+-FSI– coordination (as depicted by recent literature11,35–44 and experimental results in Supplementary Figs. 21, 22). Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) results in Fig. 2i corroborate this advantage: at a current density threshold of 0.05 mA cm–2, D4FPE-72% electrolyte withstands oxidation until 8.20 V exceeding D4FPE-57% (7.70 V), D4FEE-57% (6.80 V) and DME-100% (4.90 V). Meanwhile, the Li || NCM811 cells using D4FPE-72% electrolyte exhibit minimal leakage current densities of 0.0026 mA cm–2 at 4.5 V and 0.01 mA cm–2 at 4.6 V, significantly outperforming comparative electrolytes (D4FPE-57%, 0.004 and 0.019 mA cm–2; D4FEE-57%, 0.0057 and 0.023 mA cm–2; DME-100%, 0.007 and 0.027 mA cm–2, top of Fig. 2i). These comparisons demonstrate significantly improved electrolyte oxidation stability from our oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte molecular design. Notably, D4FPE-57% exhibits unexpectedly improved oxidation resistance to D4FEE-57%, despite the lower anti-oxidation stability of its D4FPE diluent than D4FEE, suggesting an irregular anti-oxidation stability mechanism. Mechanistic investigations via 2D ¹H-¹⁹F Heteronuclear Overhauser Effect Spectroscopy identified a distinct intermolecular interaction: an obvious atypical H-F hydrogen bond between the terminal hydrogen atoms in DME and the fluorine atoms in D4FPE (Fig. 2j), while absence of this hydrogen bond in D4FEE-based electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 23). At the same time, 1H and 19F NMR spectra in Supplementary Fig. 24 illustrate a characteristic downfield shift (Δδᴴ = 0.16 ppm) in hydrogen-related peaks of DME and upfield shift in the fluorine-related peaks of D4FPE, supporting the existence of an atypical hydrogen bond between D4FPE and DME34,45. This atypical H-F hydrogen bond in D4FPE electrolyte is due to the co-participation of DME and D4FPE molecules in the nano-sized primary solvation shell to shorten intermolecular distances and enable stabilizing interactions, thereby mitigating the oxidation vulnerability of DME (as explained by DFT calculations in Supplementary Fig. 20). Based on these synergetic effects, our oxygen-distal fluorination molecule design can provide enhanced oxidation stability besides solvation structure and interphase optimizations, thus enables high-performance LMB electrolyte.

Improved battery electrochemical performance from oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte molecule design

The efficacy of our oxygen-distal fluorinated molecular design on Li negative electrode stabilization was first evaluated by electrochemical analyses in Li || Cu half cells. Through Aurbach’s CE measurement protocol46, the D4FPE-72% electrolyte exhibited an enhanced average Li plating/stripping CE of 99.8%, outperforming values for D4FPE-57% (99.4%), D4FEE-57% (99.3%) and conventional DME-100% electrolytes (99.1%) (Fig. 3a). Long-cycle tests in Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 25 show that the D4FPE-72% electrolyte enables sustained Li negative electrode operation over 900 cycles with an averaged CE of 99.65% during 800–900th cycle. Similarly, the D4FPE-57% electrolyte exhibited 800 stable cycles with a relatively high CE of averaging 99.48% over 550–650th cycles. In contrast, the D4FEE-57% electrolyte affords a shorter cycle life (severe CE fluctuation since 600th cycles) and lower CE (averaging 99.19% during 550–650th cycle), and the conventional DME-100% electrolyte illustrates severe CE degradation to 95.08% after 300 cycles. Corresponding voltage profiles in Fig. 3c confirmed these trends, with D4FPE-72% displaying the lowest stabilized overpotential (141.9 mV after 300 cycles) versus D4FPE-57% (157.8 mV), D4FEE-57% (174.4 mV) and DME-100% (445.9 mV). These comparisons demonstrate that the significantly enhanced Li plating/stripping CE performances from our oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte molecule design. This stabilization also extended to Li || Li symmetric cells, where D4FPE-72% enabled uninterrupted cycling for 2900 h that surpasses D4FPE-57% (2450 h), D4FEE-57% (2370 h), and DME-100% (2300 h) (Supplementary Fig. 26). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) on Li || Li symmetric cells further substantiated enhanced interfacial properties: D4FPE-72% exhibited both lower initial charge-transfer resistance (9.73 Ω) and high stability (to 6.79 Ω after 100 cycles) compared to D4FPE-57% (10.84 to 7.31 Ω) and D4FEE-57% (11.4 to 12.3 Ω, Supplementary Fig. 27 and Supplementary Table 3), indicating improved SEI integrity and accelerated ionic transport kinetics from D4FPE-72% electrolyte design. Collectively, these results demonstrate the significant advantages of oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte design for high-efficiency lithium metal negative electrodes.

Building upon enhanced Li negative electrode reversibility with oxidation resistance and interphase optimization of electrolyte, our oxygen-distal fluorination strategy significantly improves the performance of high-voltage, negative-electrode-limited Li || NCM811 full cell. In a 4 mAh cm–2 Li metal negative electrode || 1.63 mAh cm–2 NCM811 positive electrode full cell configuration with negative: positive electrode capacity ratio (RN/P) of 2.54 and charging cut-off voltage of 4.5 V, the Li || NCM811 full cells using D4FPE-72% electrolyte delivered an initial discharge capacity of 189.5 mAh g–1 (based on mass of NCM811, 1 C = 190 mA g–1, Fig. 3d). Before reaching 80% capacity retention (w.r.t. 3rd cycle), the Li || NCM811 full cells using D4FPE-72% electrolyte can afford 450 stable cycles with a high average CE of 99.80% and stable low-polarization voltage profiles (Fig. 3e), outperforming D4FPE-57% (320 cycles), D4FEE-57% electrolyte (235 cycles) and conventional DME-100% (80 cycles). Additionally, the performance of electrolytes with repeated long-term cycling performance, different LiFSI concentrations and application in LiTFSI salt-based electrolyte system further confirm the advantages of our electrolyte molecular-skeleton design (Supplementary Figs. 28–33). Rate capability analyses further demonstrated improved kinetics by D4FPE-72% electrolyte (94.4 mAh g–1 retained at 740 mA g–1) beyond D4FEE-57% (74.5 mAh g–1) and DME-100% electrolyte (58.1 mAh g–1, Supplementary Fig. 34), corroborated by reduced interfacial impedance (Supplementary Fig. 35 and Supplementary Table 4). Compared with some relevant state-of-the-art Li metal full cell results recently published, this D4FPE-72% electrolyte-based Li || NCM811 full cell achieves competitive cycling stability and a competitive RN/P ratio (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Table 5)11,47–54. Furthermore, we systematically evaluated the electrochemical performance across a wide temperature range. At –20 °C, the CEs of D4FPE-72%, D4FPE-57% and D4FEE-57% are observed to be 99.1%, 96.8% and 95.3%, respectively. Li || NCM811 full cell using D4FPE-72% electrolyte delivers a capacity of 159 mAh g–1 with stable cycling over 100 cycles, markedly outperforming D4FPE-57% (105.2 mAh g–1) and D4FEE-57% (66.2 mAh g–1), whereas DME-100% fails to operate under –20 °C. At 60 °C, D4FPE-72% exhibits a Li plating/stripping CE of 99.7%, whereas D4FPE-57%, D4FEE-57%, and DME-100% show values of 99.5%, 99.3% and 99.1%, respectively. D4FPE-72% maintains 74% of its initial capacity after 150 cycles (w.r.t. 3rd cycle), significantly surpassing D4FPE-57% (68.78%, 118cycles), D4FEE-57% (45.76%, 50 cycles), and the DME-100% electrolyte (61%, 10 cycles) (Supplementary Figs. 36, 37). Scaling to practical configurations, a 5.9-Ah pouch cell employing D4FPE-72% (electrolyte: capacity ratio (E/C ratio) =1.7 g Ah–1) achieved high specific energy (504.6 Wh kg–1 and 1136.8 Wh L–1, Fig. 3i, Supplementary Fig. 38 and Supplementary Table 6), approaching twice of conventional lithium-ion battery systems5. After 70 cycles, this pouch cell maintained 96.3% capacity retention (w.r.t. 1st cycle) with stable voltage profiles and an average CE of 99.7% (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 38), establishing competitive stability metrics among amp-hour-scale LMBs for both specific energy and capacity fade resistance per-cycle (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Table 7)9,55–61. Moreover, an additional pouch cell exhibiting higher capacity (7.6 Ah, Supplementary Fig. 39 and Supplementary Table 8) and reduced E/C ratio (1.1014) was fabricated, achieving a specific energy of 547.9 Wh kg–1 (the specific energy of the pouch cell in this work was calculated based on the total cell weight). This cell demonstrated stable cycling performance over 150 cycles, retaining 70.5% of its initial capacity (w.r.t. 1st cycle), while maintaining an average Coulombic efficiency (CE) of 99.7% (Fig. 3j–l). These advances substantiate the oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte paradigm for developing high-voltage, high-energy-density LMBs with extended cycle life.

Li negative electrode structure and interfacial chemistry variation from oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte molecule design

To investigate the mechanistic basis by which oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte molecular design stabilizes Li metal negative electrode, we systematically evaluated Li deposition morphologies via scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in Fig. 4a–c, Li plating at an areal capacity of 3 mAh cm–2 in DME-100%, D4FEE-57%, and D4FPE-57% electrolytes results in loosely deposited Li with average grain diameters of 5.1 ± 1.6 μm, 5.87 ± 1.4 μm, and 5.96 ± 1.5 μm, respectively, contrasting sharply with the compact, consolidated-grain Li deposition structure without any dendrites (average diameter 6.37 ± 1.6 μm) in D4FPE-72% (Fig. 4d). After 100 cycles, loosely packed Li deposits and partial dendritic features were still observed in DME-100%, D4FEE-57%, and D4FPE-57% electrolytes (Fig. 4e–g), in stark contrast to the densely packed, uniform and large grain Li negative electrode morphology in D4FPE-72% electrolyte (7.19 ± 2.2 μm, Fig. 4h). We attribute these structural advantages to a mechanically robust, ionically conductive inorganic SEI enabled by oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte molecular design, which derives from reduced solvents (precursor of organic SEI) in the solvation structure combined with anion and fluorinated diluent co-enriched AGG solvation structure. The resulting interfacial architecture promotes deposition uniformity, which reduces the Li negative electrode surface area to minimize active lithium exposure, suppresses parasitic decomposition reactions between Li and electrolyte, and mitigates the inactive Li accumulation in Li negative electrode, therefore improving the overall cycling reversibility of Li negative electrode.

To elucidate electrolyte-dependent SEI composition evolution, we conducted X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis on the SEI of Li negative electrodes cycled in different electrolytes. As shown in Fig. 4i and Supplementary Fig. 40, the SEI formed in the DME-100% electrolyte is predominantly composed of organic species such as C-C (284.6 eV in C 1s spectra) and C-O (531 eV in O 1s spectra, 286.5 eV in C 1s spectra), along with minor amounts of CFx (289.5 eV in C 1s spectra, 689 eV in F 1s spectra). Additionally, incomplete decomposition products of LiFSI, including RCO2Li/Li3N (55 eV in Li 1s spectra, 399 eV in N 1s spectra) were observed, along with very trace amounts of LiF (56 eV in Li 1s spectra, 685 eV in F 1s spectra) and Li₂S (54 eV in Li 1s spectra, 163 eV in S 2p spectrum). The quantified atomic ratio analysis also shows that the SEI layer formed in DME-100% electrolyte had a very high carbon content (64.2% at the outer SEI surface and 28.9% in the most inner layer), in addition to the relatively low lithium content (ranging from 12.4 to 40%) and low fluorine content (4.1 to 9%) (Fig. 4j). This suggests that the conventional DME-100% electrolyte decomposes into an organic component-dominant SEI, yielding intrinsically low surface energy, inferior ionic conductivity, elevated swelling tendency7 and compromised mechanical stability17,62 that are responsible for the low Li plating/stripping CE and short cycle life. Meanwhile, the SEI formed in the D4FEE-57% electrolyte exhibited marginal compositional shifts including very slightly reduced intensity of C-C and C-O organic components-related peaks in XPS spectra, a slightly increased intensity of LiF and Li2S components-related peaks (the fully decomposed products of LiFSI) (Fig. 4k), persistently high carbon content (57.5 to 27%) and marginally increased fluorine content (6.09 to 13.4%, Fig. 4l), reflecting an organic-dominant SEI with slightly more inorganic components that should improve Li negative electrode performance to a minor extent. The spatial distribution of components within the SEI layer revealed by time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) further indicates an SEI composition enriched in CHO2–, accompanied by reduced contents of LiF– and LiO2– (Supplementary Fig. 41). This should be attributed to the DME-abundant solvation structure that dominates SEI formation reaction during Li metal negative electrode operation20. In sharp contrast, D4FPE-72% electrolyte generated a fundamentally restructured SEI featuring: a significant reduction in organic components (C-C and C-O) and pronounced LiF enrichment, along with minor amounts of Li₂S and Li₂O (Fig. 4m), as further confirmed by the elemental composition analysis showing greatly decreased carbon content (31.58% in the outer layer of SEI to 7.79% in the inner layer of SEI), increased lithium content (24.4 to 37.7%), and higher fluorine content (16.1 to 36.6%, Fig. 4n). The TOF-SIMS measurements further corroborate this observation, showing an LiF–-dominated SEI composition with increased LiO2– and lower amounts of CHO2– (Supplementary Fig. 42). This should be attributed to the increased amounts of D4FPE and anions in the solvation shell from oxygen-distal fluorinated molecular design, which enables the formation of a LiF-dominant inorganic-dominant SEI layer conferring critically important properties of high surface energy, high ionic conduction, diminished swelling tendency and enhanced mechanical robustness for suppressing Li-electrolyte side reactions or dendritic Li growth, thus enabling highly reversible Li negative electrode cycling.

Positive electrode sturcture and interfacial chemistry variation from oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte molecule design

To evaluate the impact of oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte molecular design on positive electrode stability, we assessed structural evolution in high-voltage NCM811 positive electrodes after extended cycling. As shown in Fig. 5a, b, the NCM811 positive electrodes after 100 cycles in either conventional DME-100% or D4FEE-57% electrolyte illustrate severe particle fracturing on the surface. This positive electrode cracking is caused by the inadequate passivation stability of the DME-derived, highly swelling-prone organic CEI, which leads to persistent side reactions between positive electrode and electrolyte63, triggers irreversible surface reconstruction64, and increases transition metal dissolution65,66 that collectively drive rapid capacity fade. In contrast, the NCM811 positive electrode after 100 cycles in D4FPE-57% electrolyte illustrates substantially reduced microcrack density (Fig. 5c), while the positive electrode using D4FPE-72% electrolyte exhibits a crack-free surfaces without any appearance of structural degradation (Fig. 5d). This morphological preservation further corroborated by invariant (003)/(104) peak intensity ratios in X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 43), demonstrates the efficacy of our oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte molecular design in stabilizing positive electrode architecture. The enhancement is attributed to a synergistic trifecta: a) partial replacement of oxidation-labile DME solvents by robust fluorinated species in inner solvation shells, b) H-F bonding stabilization between D4FPE and DME that elevates molecular oxidation resistance and c) formation of mechanically resilient inorganic-rich CEIs derived from preferential anion/fluorinated-solvent decomposition. These interfacial modifications collectively mitigate degradation pathways, thereby enabling prolonged cyclability of high-voltage NCM811 full cells.

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images in Fig. 5e, f further reveal that the CEIs formed by DME-100% and D4FEE-57% electrolytes are comparatively thicker and morphologically heterogeneous (6.55–8.9 nm for DME-100%, and 4.65–7.6 nm for D4FEE-57%), which hinders lithium-ion transport kinetics and proves more parasitic positive-electrode-electrolyte reactions at the interphase. In contrast, the CEI formed by D4FPE-57% electrolyte has a lower thickness with minor unevenness (3.47 nm to 7.04 nm, Fig. 5g), while the CEI formed in the D4FPE-72% electrolyte exhibits a uniformly distributed thickness of 3.5 nm (Fig. 5h). This thin and uniform CEI proves effectively suppressed positive-electrode-electrolyte side reactions, and enhanced interfacial stability from our oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte molecular design, which is beneficial to the cycling reversibility of high-voltage positive electrode67. To further disclose the influence of our oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte design on the chemical composition of the CEI layer, XPS depth analysis was conducted on the NCM811 positive electrode after 100 cycles in different electrolytes. The results prove that the primary constituents of the CEI formed in the DME-100% electrolyte were organic materials derived from the DME solvent, as evidenced by the high-intensity organic composition-related peaks throughout the whole thickness of CEI, including C-C peaks, C-O peaks, C-F peaks and RO-Li (529.5 eV in O 1s spectra) (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 44). Meanwhile, very minor amounts of LiFSI salt-derived, positive-electrode-protecting LiF species-related peaks were detected. Quantitative elemental analysis also shows that the lithium and fluorine contents in the CEI were as low as 12.5–13.8%, and 22.1–17.2%, respectively, while the carbon content increased from 37.0 to 47.1% (Fig. 5j). These results demonstrate that DME-abundant solvation structure in conventional DME-100% electrolyte generates an organic component-dominant CEI with low lithium-ion mobility, excessive swelling propensity, mechanical vulnerability and non-uniformity properties, which are responsible for the degraded electrochemical performance of high-voltage NCM811 positive electrode68. At the same time, the introduction of oxygen-proximal fluorinated, non-solvating D4FEE diluent into regular LHCE electrolyte illustrates minor impact on the CEI structure, as illustrated by the constantly high-intensity C-C peak and low-intensity Li-F peak in the XPS depth profile (Fig. 5k), as well as the high carbon content (42.7–49.5%), low Li (11.9–12.8%) and fluorine content (27.2–20.5%) (Fig. 5l). This comparison proves that the conventional non-solvating, oxygen-proximal fluorinated diluent molecule design fails to modify the solvation-driven CEI composition and formation, because the DME-dominant and D4FEE/LiFSI-rare solvation structure prevent the formation of D4FEE or LiFSI-derived inorganic CEI20,21. In sharp contrast, the D4FPE-72% electrolyte-derived CEI illustrates a fundamentally reconstituted inorganic LiF-dominant composition, as proved by the significantly intensifying LiF peaks and attenuated C-C peaks in XPS depth profile (Fig. 5m). Elemental analysis further quantitatively verifies this inorganic LiF-dominant CEI composition by the increased lithium content (32.8–41.4%), fluorine content (31.8–37.8%) and reduced carbon content (22.3–15.6%, Fig. 5n). This transformation stems from the promoted participation of D4FPE diluent and FSI– anion in the inner solvation enabled by our oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte molecule design, yielding a D4FPE and FSI– co-derived inorganic-rich CEI architecture with high ionic conductivity, mechanical robustness and interfacial homogeneity, beneficial for the cycling reversibility of high-voltage NCM811 positive electrode.

Discussion

In summary, we report an oxygen-distal fluorination strategy for the electrolyte molecule skeleton editing design targeting high-performance lithium metal batteries, wherein selective fluorination of electrolyte diluents at molecular positions distal to ether oxygen moieties to mitigate fluorine-induced electron cloud depletion on oxygen atoms. Unlike conventional oxygen-proximal fluorinated diluents in localized high-concentration electrolytes where diluent demonstrates insufficient solvation engagement to alter lithium metal battery performance parameters, this oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte diluent molecular skeleton editing design synergistically combines moderate Li+-ion coordination capability, enhanced fluorinated diluent/anion enrichment within the inner Li+ solvation shell and atypical H-F hydrogen bonding between diluents and solvents. This trifold mechanism elevates electrolyte oxidation resistance, reduces volatile DME solvent content, and enables inorganic-rich solid electrolyte interphase /positive electrode (cathode)-electrolyte interphase formation derived from oxygen-distal fluorinated diluents and anions enriched in the inner solvation shell. From this oxygen-distal fluorinated electrolyte molecular skeleton editing design, a Li-plating/stripping Coulombic efficiency of 99.8% with dendrite-free uniform Li deposition is achieved in Li metal negative electrode, along with 450 stable cycles in negative-electrode-limited, 4.5 V high-voltage Li || NCM811 full cells with preserved positive electrode integrity and homogeneous cathode-electrolyte interphase. Practical scalability is also demonstrated via a 504.6 Wh kg–1 5.9 Ah Li-metal || NCM811 pouch cell exhibiting low capacity decay (0.053% per cycle) and 150 cycles in 547.9 Wh kg–1, 7.6 Ah pouch cell. These findings provide insights into molecular skeleton editing strategies for high-performance battery technologies.

Synthetic details of di-2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropoxyethane (D4FPE)

To prepare 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropoxyethanol, tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (200 mL) was placed in a 1 L round-bottom flask under argon. 2,2,3,3-Tetrafluoropropanol (277.3 g, 2.1 mol) was then added slowly with stirring, followed by sodium hydroxide (5.6 g, 0.14 mol). After stirring at room temperature for 2 h, ethylene carbonate (123.3 g, 1.4 mol), which had been preheated at 60 °C for 30 min, was added dropwise. The mixture was stirred for a further 1 h at room temperature and then heated to 150 °C for 48 h. After the reaction, the product was isolated by vacuum distillation and fractionation, affording 181.47 g of 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropoxyethanol as the final product. Yield: 181.47 g, yield ratio of 73.65%. 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3, 25 °C) δ/ppm): 6.10-5.81 (tt, 1H), 3.94-3.86 (tt, 2H), 3.69-3.77 (m, 4H), 2.66 (s, 1H).

For the preparation of 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl p-toluenesulfonate, p-toluenesulfonyl chloride (150.15 g, 0.7875 mol) was placed in a 1 L round-bottom flask under argon, and ultra-dry dichloromethane (300 mL) was added by syringe. The mixture was stirred until complete dissolution was achieved. In a separate 1 L flask under argon, 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropanol (99.045 g, 0.75 mol) and triethylamine (114.75 mL, 0.825 mol) were combined and cooled to 0 °C in an ice bath. The p-toluenesulfonyl chloride solution was then added dropwise to the stirred alcohol/triethylamine mixture at a rate of no more than 20 mL min–1. After the addition was complete, the reaction mixture was maintained at 0 °C for 20 min, then gradually heated to 60 °C and stirred overnight. Deionized water was subsequently added slowly to dissolve the precipitated solids. The organic layer was separated, dried over anhydrous magnesium sulfate for 12 h, and filtered. After removal of the solvent under reduced pressure, 151.875 g 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl p-toluenesulfonate was obtained. Yield: 151.875 g, 70.79%. 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3, 25 °C) δ/ppm: 7.82-7.80 (d, 2H), 7.40-7.338 (d, 2H), 6.00-5.71 (tt, 1H), 4.37-4.30 (tt, 2H), 2.47 (s, 3H).

Sodium hydride (20 g, 60% dispersion in mineral oil; 12.0 g, 0.5 mol) was placed in a 1 L round-bottom flask under argon. Ultra-dry THF (300 mL) was then added by gas-tight syringe at 0 °C in an ice bath, and the mixture was stirred to give a heterogeneous suspension. 2,2,3,3-Tetrafluoropropoxyethanol (70.42 g, 0.4 mol) was added dropwise by gas-tight syringe at 0 °C, and the reaction mixture was maintained at this temperature for 30 min. After gas evolution had ceased, 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropyl p-toluenesulfonate (137.38 g, 0.48 mol) was added slowly by gas-tight syringe at a rate below 5 mL min–1 while the temperature was kept at 0 °C. The mixture was then allowed to warm gradually to room temperature over 20 min and subsequently refluxed at 70 °C overnight. After cooling to room temperature, deionized water (400 mL) was added slowly, and THF was removed under reduced pressure. The aqueous phase was extracted with dichloromethane (400 mL), and the combined organic layers were washed with brine, dried over anhydrous magnesium sulfate, and concentrated. Purification by distillation afforded D4FPE as a colorless liquid (102.6 g). Yield: 102.6 g, 88.44%. 1H-NMR of D4FPE (400 MHz, CDCl3, 25 ˚C, δ/ppm): 3.74 (s, 4H), 3.91-3.84 (tt, 4H), 6.05-5.76 (tt, 2H). After a three-step Williamson ether synthesis, D4FPE was obtained in an overall yield of 46.1%.

Electrolytes and electrodes preparation

The materials used for electrolyte preparation, stir bar and magnetic stirrer are described in detail in the “Materials and Equipment” section of the Methods. All salts and solvents were used as received without further purification. The prepared electrolyte solutions were stored in standard 3 mL or 20 mL glass vials. Polypropylene pipette tips were used for transfer the electrolyte. The electrolytes were stored in an argon-filled glovebox (H2O and O2 < 0.01 ppm) at 25 °C and were stirred for 12 h prior to use. The DME-100%, 2MDME-100% and 3MDME-100% electrolyte were prepared by dissolving 0.28 g, 0.22 g and 0.33 g LiFSI in 590 μL of DME. Similarly, the D4FPE-57% electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 0.3 g LiFSI in 650 μL of a DME/D4FPE (43: 57) mixture. The D4FPE-72% electrolyte was prepared in the same manner by dissolving 0.28 g LiFSI in 590 μL of a DME/D4FPE (28: 72) mixture. The positive electrode for 7.6 Ah Li || NCM811 pouch cell was prepared by dispersing NCM811, PVDF, Super P, and VGCF in NMP at a weight ratio of 95.5: 2.2: 1.8: 0.5 and mixed using a planetary mixer at 2000 rpm for 2 h in dry air at 25 °C to obtain a homogeneous slurry. Each pouch cell comprised 13 layers of double-side positive electrode and 14 layers of double-side negative electrode, with separators placed between adjacent electrodes, and the stack was packaged with Al-plastic film. The N/P ratio was 1.59 (10 mAh cm–2 for the negative electrode and 6.265 mAh cm–2 for the positive electrode; the areal capacities were calculated based on the electrode area). The 5.9 Ah lithium metal pouch cells were purchased from Nanwu Technology (Guangzhou) Co., Ltd. The cells were assembled in a stacked configuration with 10 positive electrode sheets and 11 negative electrode sheets using a PE separator. The NCM811 positive electrode had a thickness of 134 μm and was coated on 12 μm aluminum foil current collectors, with an areal capacity of 4 mAh cm–2 and a specific capacity of 210 mAh g–1. The positive electrode size was 54 × 122 mm, with an N/P ratio of 1.5 and an active material content of 96%. The negative electrode consisted of lithium foil (60 μm thick) with a size of 56 × 124 mm, and aluminum and nickel tabs were used as the positive and negative electrode tabs, respectively. Polypropylene pipette tips were used to inject the electrolyte, after which the pouch cells were degassed and vacuum heat-sealed using a vacuum pre-sealing machine. After formation, the total mass of the 7.6 Ah pouch cell was 53.59 g (the masses of NCM811, Li foil, and electrolyte were 36.7 g, 3.56 g, and 8.37 g, respectively, while the masses of the Al foil, separator, and Al-plastic film were 1.956 g, 1.51 g, and 1.5 g, respectively). Meanwhile, the total mass of the 5.9 Ah pouch cell was 45.1 g (the masses of NCM811, Li foil, and electrolyte were 28.59 g, 2.6 g, and 10 g, respectively, while the masses of the Al foil, separator, and Al-plastic film were 1.41 g, 1.2 g, and 1.3 g, respectively). The 7.6 Ah pouch cell was cycled at 18.8 mA g–1 for charging and 37.6 mA g–1 for discharging, while the 5.9 Ah pouch cell was cycled at 9 mA g–1. During cycling, a stack pressure of 350 kPa was applied to the pouch cells. Long-term cycling tests were performed at 25 ± 1 °C in a constant-temperature environmental chamber. During testing, the pouch cells were cycled under a constant-current (CC) mode without applying any constant-voltage (CV) steps. The specific current was defined as the applied current divided by the mass of the active material (NCM811). The specific energy of the pouch cell was calculated based on the total mass of the pouch cell (53.59 g for the 7.6 Ah cell and 45.1 g for the 5.9 Ah cell).

Electrochemical performance test

The electrochemical measurements were carried out in a constant-temperature environmental chamber (YDC-300HT) with convection heating. The temperature was maintained at 25 ± 1 °C during testing. No local temperature measurements were performed for the Ah-scale multilayer pouch cells. Pouch cells were assembled in a dry room (dew point below −51.2 °C) at 21 °C, while coin cells were assembled in an argon-filled glovebox with H₂O and O₂ levels below 0.01 ppm. After activation, long-term cycling was carried out between 3.0 and 4.5 V at charge/discharge rates of 0.2 C/0.3 C, corresponding to 0.32 mA cm–2 for charging and 0.48 mA cm–2 for discharging. The current densities were calculated on the basis of an electrode area of 1.13 cm2. In addition, a constant-voltage charging step was applied at 4.5 V until the current decreased below 0.05 C or 20 min had elapsed. All electrochemical measurements were performed using a LAND battery testing system. Li || Al half cells were assembled following the same procedure as that used for Li || Cu half cells, except that an aluminum foil was used as the working electrode and a 100 μm pristine lithium metal foil served as the counter electrode. After resting at room temperature for 24 h, linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was conducted on a CHI760 electrochemical workstation at a scan rate of 0.5 mV s–1 over a voltage range of 2.0–9.0 V. The exchange current density (log i0) was obtained from cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements performed on the same CHI760 electrochemical workstation. CV scans were performed within a potential window of –0.2 to 0.2 V at a sweep rate of 0.5 mV s–1. The linear region of the Tafel plot, obtained from the CV data, was extrapolated to zero overpotential (η = 0) to extract log i0. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed using a CHI760 electrochemical workstation with a 5 mV AC perturbation over a frequency range of 0.1 to 105 Hz with 12 points per decade. The measurements were conducted near open-circuit potential after a quiet time of 2 s prior to data acquisition. EIS measurements were conducted on both full cells and Li || Li symmetric cells using an identical protocol. EIS data were analyzed using Zview software, in which the raw data were imported as.txt files, and the fitting was performed by constructing appropriate equivalent circuit elements. The fitted values of the equivalent circuit elements were then extracted after fitting, and the fitting quality was evaluated based on the fitting error. Characterizations

Raman spectra were acquired using an in-situ UV laser confocal Raman spectrometer (LabRAM HR Evolution) equipped with a 473 nm laser at ambient temperature, with each electrolyte sample loaded into a capillary tube inside an Ar-filled glovebox and flame-sealed using an alcohol lamp prior to measurement. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded on a Nicolet NEXUS 670 spectrometer. Electrolytes were sealed in glass vials inside an Ar-filled glovebox and transferred for testing. The electrolyte was then dropped onto a KBr film using polypropylene pipette tips for FT-IR analysis. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) measurements, including 1D 1H, 19F, and 7Li spectra as well as 2D 1H-19F Heteronuclear Overhauser Effect Spectroscopy, were conducted on a Bruker AVANCE-NEO 400 MHz liquid-state NMR spectrometer with deuterated chloroform (CDCl3) as the NMR internal standard solution, all sample preparation procedures were carried out inside an Ar-filled glovebox, and the prepared samples were sealed in the glovebox prior to testing. DOSY-NMR experiments were performed on a Bruker Ascend 600 MHz spectrometer at 298 K with DMSO-d6 inject into Aco-axialtube then inserted into the NMR tube. DOSY-NMR measurements were carried out on a Bruker Ascend 600 MHz spectrometer at 298 K. For sample preparation, the electrolyte was transferred into an NMR tube in an argon-filled glovebox, while DMSO-d6 was separately placed in a coaxial insert, which was then positioned inside the NMR tube before sealing. 7Li pulsed-field-gradient (PFG) NMR experiments were conducted to determine the Li+ diffusion coefficients using the standard ledbpgp2s pulse sequence. 19F PFG NMR measurements were performed to evaluate the diffusion coefficients of FSI– using the 19oneshort45cpd pulse sequence. The surface morphology of fresh and cycled electrodes was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS EVD-18). For SEM observation, the electrodes were mounted on the sample holder inside an Ar-filled glovebox and temporarily enclosed before transfer to minimize air exposure. Immediately prior to measurement, the samples were introduced into the SEM chamber with only brief ambient exposure (~5–10 s). All SEM measurements were conducted under vacuum. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; ESCALAB 250, Al Kα radiation) was employed to investigate the SEI and CEI compositions. Depth profiling was carried out by Ar-ion sputtering at 1 keV for 60 s per cycle to analyze the subsurface regions. Before XPS measurement, the cycled electrodes were rinsed with anhydrous DME inside an argon-filled glovebox to remove residual LiFSI and then transferred with a vacuum transfer vessel. The binding energies were referenced to the C 1s peak at 284.6 eV. Time-of-flight secondary-ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) was performed on TOF.SIMS5-100 instrument and a 30 keV Bi3+ beam was used for analysis over an area of 50 μm × 50 μm. The sputtering was conducted with a 1 kV Cs+ beam at 60 nA, covering an area of 200 μm × 200 μm. Before testing, the cycled electrodes were rinsed with anhydrous DME inside an argon-filled glovebox to remove residual LiFSI. The samples were then placed onto a sample holder inside the glovebox, followed by loading the holder into a dedicated vacuum transfer vessel. The vessel was subsequently transferred to the instrument and evacuated before measurement. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) was carried out on a ThermoFisher F200X G2 field-emission microscope. After cycling, 9.8 mg of active material was scraped from the NCM811 electrode surface inside an Ar-filled glovebox, dispersed in 3 mL of anhydrous ethanol, and ultrasonicated for 5 min. A 10 μL aliquot of the suspension was then dropped onto lacey formvar/carbon film-coated TEM grids and dried in air at 25 °C and 45–55% relative humidity for 30 min before TEM analysis. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on a Bruker D2 diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) operated at 30 kV and 10 mA. After cycling, the NCM811 electrodes were rinsed with 100 μL of anhydrous DME in an argon-filled glovebox, cut into smaller pieces, fixed onto the sample stage, and then transferred for measurement. The viscosity of the electrolytes was measured at 25 ˚C using a Lichen NDJ-8S viscometer in an argon-filled glovebox.

The verification calculations including Geometry optimizations, vibrational frequency analyses, and energy computations were carried out with Gaussian 1669 at the B3LYP(D3BJ)/ 6-31 + G(d,p) level and then the single-point energy was evaluated using the B3LYP(D3BJ) functional and the 6-311 + G(d,p) basis set.

The electrostatic potentials were calculated based on Density Functional Theory (DFT), a quantum mechanical computational method, using the Gaussian 16 software. Specifically, the B3LYP functional and the 6-31 G basis set were selected for the calculations. All molecular structure optimizations were performed at the B3LYP/6-31 G(d,p) level, followed by the computation of electrostatic potentials and other related parameters. After the calculations, the results were analyzed using Multiwfn, which was employed to output the final data.

Radial distribution functions were calculated through all-atom molecular dynamics simulations using Materials Studio (2020). The simulations were carried out based on molecular dynamics (MD) or quantum mechanical (QM) calculations. The electrolyte models containing LiFSI dissolved in DME, LiFSI dissolved in DME/D4FEE, and LiFSI dissolved in DME/D4FPE were constructed using the Amorphous Cell module, with a total of 150 molecules. Specifically, the molar ratios for each system were as follows: LiFSI dissolved in DME (1:3.94), LiFSI dissolved in DME/D4FEE (1:1.135:1.507), and LiFSI dissolved in DME/D4FPE (1:0.601:1.578). These systems were optimized using the Smart Minimized method, with periodic boundary conditions applied. The appropriate COMPASS II force field was selected to describe the system, and the force field’s default parameters were used to assign bond, angle, torsion, and non-bonded interaction parameters for all atoms. Partial charges for Li+, anions, and solvent molecules were assigned according to the default settings of the force field to ensure reasonable ion-solvent interactions.

Geometry optimization was performed using the Forcite module to minimize molecular energy, employing either the conjugate gradient or quasi-Newton algorithm for energy minimization. The convergence criteria were set to an energy gradient of less than 0.001–0.01 kcal/mol/Å to eliminate unreasonable initial contacts and short bond lengths. Atomic charges were automatically assigned by the force field. Subsequently, pre-equilibration simulations were carried out under the NVT ensemble, with temperature and pressure controlled using the Andersen and Berendsen methods, respectively, at 293.15 K and 101325 Pa. A cutoff radius of 16.5 Å was applied. After equilibration, the system was fixed and a production simulation was performed under the NVT ensemble, with a time step of 1.0 fs and a total simulation time of 1000 ps.

Finally, the Forcite/Analysis module was used to perform statistical analysis of the equilibrated configurations. Radial distribution functions (g(r)) were calculated by selecting Li+ and specific atoms in the solvent molecules (such as the oxygen atoms in DME/D4FPE/D4FEE) as central pairs.
