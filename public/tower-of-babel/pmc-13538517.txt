Toward continuous quality observability in medicine
Authors: Advait Patil, William T Curry, Brian V Nahed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHospitals often detect complications retrospectively via error-susceptible mechanisms including self-reporting, manual abstraction, or billing codes. We propose a paradigm of continuous clinical quality observability: a background, non-interruptive layer where AI agents continuously read the EHR narrative to surface emerging safety signals. Continuous observability may shorten the time from event to detection from weeks to hours or days and reduce manual case-finding, accelerating learning consistent with high-reliability care principles.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

From retrospective quality measurement to continuous observability

Hospitals still find out about complications the same way they did decades ago, through manual review of records weeks after the fact1,2. However, every day of that same care leaves a rich, time-stamped record in the EHR, including progress notes, nursing entries, orders, labs, imaging, discharge summaries. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) make it increasingly feasible to read that record automatically and flag early signs of trouble, though feasibility varies with event type, documentation quality, local data infrastructure, and validation requirements3. Natural language processing systems identified postoperative complications more than a decade ago4. The more important shift is from summarization to agency. Now, an AI agent can pursue a safety question across the entire record through gathering evidence, tracking changes over time, and leaving an auditable trail of what it found and why it raised a concern. That agentic assembly of dispersed evidence into a reviewable safety brief is the contribution this Perspective develops.

Observability as a conceptual framework for clinical safety

In software, observability explains system behavior from the signals it already emits (logs, metrics, traces) so one can answer why an alarm tripped. Monitoring watches known thresholds; observability lets you ask not just whether a threshold was crossed, but why. An LLM agent can move across notes, orders, labs, and imaging, updating its working hypothesis as new data arrive and maintaining a running account of what it found.

We propose applying this principle to hospital quality and safety through a dedicated agentic AI system. This is something different from ambient documentation tools or threshold-based alerts. The system would screen for likely complications, route candidate events to reviewers, and support real-time learning, serving three complementary purposes. First, it surfaces emerging complications and deterioration closer to real time, so that care teams can intervene during the episode of care. Second, it identifies time-sensitive process lapses, such as a guideline-directed medication that has not been ordered by the expected day of an admission, before the window for compliant care closes. Third, by detecting candidate events prospectively and assembling source-linked evidence, it reduces the latency and manual burden of retrospective abstraction for registries and governing bodies. Each purpose reflects the same underlying goal: to shorten the delay between an event and institutional learning for the benefit of both the current patient and future patients. To summarize, what we envision is a bounded, human-supervised agent that scans each patient’s evolving record in the background, identifies candidate complications, and generates a concise, evidence-linked brief for review without autonomous decision-making.

This approach differs from predictive AI models and from current clinical deployments of LLMs. Predictive models, including early warning scores and sepsis alerts, estimate the probability of a future event from structured data captured at a single point in time, and they typically interrupt bedside clinicians once a risk threshold is crossed. Rather than forecasting future risk, the observability agent detects complications already recorded in the clinical narrative and follows the supporting evidence longitudinally as documentation accrues. Existing clinical LLM tools, discussed further below, remain within individual encounters and workflows and do not pursue a safety question across the record over time.

A non-interruptive, human-supervised observability layer

Operationally, we believe this “non-interruptive layer” is best implemented as a parallel service that runs outside the bedside workflow. Such a design directly supports HRO practice, including continuous vigilance for failure, attention to weak signals before they compound, and expert review before any action is taken. Keeping a human in the loop helps bound agent autonomy5,6.

It ingests time-stamped EHR artifacts (notes, orders, labs, imaging reports, and communications) and performs scheduled or event-triggered “safety sweeps” for predefined failure modes. When a plausible concern is detected, the system generates a constrained-format signal card for a quality review queue. This could contain (1) the suspected issue, (2) a brief timeline, (3) supporting source-linked excerpts, (4) key missing data, and (5) uncertainty or alternative explanations. By default, the output is silent triage in a reviewer dashboard; escalation occurs only after human adjudication and through existing clinical safety pathways. In this sense, the system is quality surveillance infrastructure rather than clinical decision support. Any escalation while the patient remains admitted is a human decision executed through existing clinical governance, and it carries the accountability and liability involved with such action.

This design preserves continuous awareness while avoiding new bedside notifications and mitigating alarm fatigue7. We recognize that a queue without prioritization can overwhelm its reviewers. Signal cards should therefore be ranked by metrics such as severity, urgency, confidence, and expected utility of intervention. It is likely these will require tunable thresholds that limit queue growth when review capacity is constrained.

Commercial aviation offers a useful comparison. Routine flight data are reviewed continuously to spot deviations that, if repeated, can lead to harm. Safety teams triage signals and feed lessons back into training and procedures. A healthcare version requires timely access to everyday clinical data, a small, trained review team with clear responsibility to escalate concerns, pathways for acting on them, and a commitment to system improvement over time.

Quality programs have long measured observed-versus-expected event counts. This is useful for benchmarking institutions, but it is backward-looking by design. Prevention requires feedback arriving while there is still time to act. Claims-based measures routinely misclassify events, and clinical registries are updated too late to change what happens to the patient during their care1,2. Continuous observability directly addresses this gap by detecting signals while the admission is still ongoing.

Continuous observability also changes the work of quality abstraction. Abstractors currently identify candidate cases weeks after discharge by screening charts against registry definitions, then reconstruct each event from scattered documentation. With an observability layer in place, the abstractor instead begins from a queue of signal cards, each containing a timeline and source-linked excerpts, and confirms or refutes each candidate event against registry criteria. Effort shifts from unscreened chart review to verification of pre-assembled evidence. The hours of manual case-finding retired through this change can be reported as part of the work exchange (Table 1), and abstractor adjudications provide the supervision that refines the system over time.

Defining primary quality metrics

Given the emphasis on early detection, timeliness is a primary goal. Cardiology organized care around timeliness with door-to-balloon targets, catalyzing process redesign and improved outcomes at scale8,9. Quality surveillance deserves an analogous key performance indicator: time-to-signal, the duration from the first documented concern to the moment a responsible team is aware (Table 1). Aligned with HRO sensitivity to operations and akin to door-to-balloon in PCI, hospitals can track safety detection (time-to-signal) and response (signal-to-response). Advantages can be evaluated in a pilot deployment by comparing time-to-signal, adjudication yield (share of signals confirmed or action-triggering), and reviewer workload versus baseline workflows, while tracking interruptions in clinical workflows from the observability layer. Because signals differ in severity and urgency, time-to-signal and signal-to-response should be interpreted within priority tiers rather than treated as uniform across all signals.

Clinicians think in narratives, as do their notes. The earliest, most specific signs of a complication are usually already scattered in the record. An LLM can gather those fragments and produce a brief, reviewable summary of the concern3,4. So far, clinical LLM deployments have mostly stayed within individual workflows (e.g., ambient documentation, portal messaging). These applications are valuable, but they also generate EHR text bloat, potentially making the record harder to parse10. An observability agent turns this problem around: it reads the expanding narrative and surfaces plausible safety signals for human review. Running separate from the bedside workflow, it can screen each patient’s record and surface previously undetected issues as they become clinically relevant.

For example, a patient who undergoes surgery has a nursing note mentioning new tachycardia and a respiratory therapist documenting rising oxygen needs. An AI system assembles these fragments into a single signal card for “possible venous thromboembolism,” containing a timeline and source-linked excerpts (Table 1). The signal reaches the quality review queue within hours, rather than surfacing through month‑end abstraction or delayed morbidity and mortality processes. The interval from the first documented abnormality, the new-onset tachycardia, to review-team awareness is the time-to-signal. A reviewer adjudicates the signal, confirms the concern, and escalates care through existing safety pathways, with process adjustments made to avoid future incidents. The interval from that alert to the first documented clinical response is the signal-to-response. Confirmed signals of this kind count toward the adjudication yield, and refuted signals inform refinement of prompts and thresholds. Just as it would today, the event eventually enters a registry, but the health system can act on the problem and improve itself sooner (Fig. 1).

Seeing harm sooner enables earlier review and action.

In the status quo, the first documented concern appears in the record but is often recognized weeks later during abstraction, registry review, or incident processes, frequently outside an actionable window. In a continuous observability workflow, an AI agent performs periodic sweeps of the evolving EHR narrative, assembles evidence-linked signal cards (timeline and source excerpts), and routes them to a human review queue more rapidly. Confirmed signals are escalated through existing clinical and safety pathways, enabling faster feedback and system learning.

Incentives, workload, and culture bias human reporting, and “near misses” rarely surface in incident systems. A background LLM-based agent can aggregate weak, dispersed clues into candidate signals. These may include novel failure modes not captured by existing taxonomies or registries. While AI models are not free from bias, a supervised, human-adjudicated approach with equity monitoring can offer material improvement over fragmented, ad-hoc reporting5,6,11. Additionally, standardization of reporting via conserved signal card formats across institutions enables multi-center learning.

Architecture, evaluation, and learning health systems

Figure 2 illustrates a reference architecture for continuous observability, including evidence grounding, governance, and feedback loops from adjudication to system improvement. A pragmatic approach constitutes retrieval-augmented prompting of existing LLMs deployed within the health system boundary, with strict access controls and comprehensive logging. Concretely, the agent comprises one or more LLMs operating under fixed instructions that define the failure modes under surveillance; tools for retrieval over timestamped notes, orders, laboratory results, and reports; a control loop that alternates retrieval and reasoning while updating a working hypothesis as documentation accrues; context management that bounds the evidence considered at each step; and an output schema that requires every signal card to enumerate its supporting excerpts, key missing data, and stated uncertainty. A signal card is generated when the accumulated evidence for a predefined failure mode crosses a configurable threshold.

Reference architecture for continuous clinical observability.

EHR artifacts (notes, orders, labs, reports) stream into a secure observability service. An agentic workflow performs scheduled or event-triggered sweeps for failure modes, retrieves timestamped supporting context, and generates evidence-linked signal cards with explicit uncertainty. Signals enter a human review queue for adjudication and routing through existing clinical/safety pathways. Adjudication outcomes and drift/fairness monitoring feed iterative updates to prompts, thresholds, and retrieval sources, with comprehensive logging for auditability.

Our proposed architecture is LLM-agnostic, and two broad options for choosing LLM agents exist: (1) deploying frontier LLMs from industry providers (e.g., OpenAI, Anthropic) via a business associate agreement for HIPAA compliance, and (2) locally hosted, “on-prem” open source LLMs, each with their own pros and cons with respect to control, costs, and security12. Input data includes timestamped notes (nursing, physician, etc), orders, labs, and reports, unlabeled and provided to agents in an unsupervised fashion from an institutional data lake. The evidence for performance gains from finetuning commercial frontier LLMs remains poor, and the cost-benefit can be considered on a case-by-case basis13. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has shown significant performance gains over base models, though it is not without limitations and may be obviated by growing LLM context windows14,15.

Reviewer adjudications provide natural supervision for iterative prompt/rubric refinement and, where appropriate, local fine-tuning or lightweight adapters on de-identified text to improve calibration and specialty-specific terminology. Because documentation practices and complication profiles differ across organizations, this improvement must be local and continuous. Borrowing from continuous integration and MLOps practice, prompts, retrieval sources, thresholds, and routing rules should be versioned, evaluated against held-out adjudicated cases before deployment, and logged so that every change remains auditable; this discipline makes the system portable across health care organizations while permitting local customization.

False positives can be productive in systems designed to flag quality and safety issues: non-events often reveal near misses, documentation gaps, or processes needing repair5,6. However, each false positive consumes reviewer attention, and where review capacity is limited even informative false positives become harmful if they crowd out higher-priority cases or contribute to review fatigue. False positives should therefore be treated as a tradeoff requiring active monitoring and capacity management. Programs should track the share of flagged cases leading to any action (treatment change, documentation improvement, or pathway update) and manage the queue accordingly.

One design option is to screen signals before they reach a person. A second agent, kept separate from the generating agent to limit context leakage, could assess whether the assembled evidence supports the stated concern and whether the case warrants human attention. Such an arrangement may reduce review burden, but an additional LLM does not by itself improve reliability, and for safety-critical triage this filtering layer would require the same prospective validation as the agent it screens.

Maintaining equity is of critical importance with any AI system. Two guardrails anchor this: (1) detection parity: similar detection rates across languages, service lines, and patient groups; and (2) time-to-signal parity: similar speed from first documented concern to team awareness. Because algorithms can encode inequities, parity monitoring and periodic re-calibration should begin early and continue as documentation patterns drift11. Operationally, parity can be monitored using stratified control charts (e.g., by race/ethnicity, language, insurance, and service line), with discrepancies triggering targeted chart audits and prompt/threshold recalibration to address systematic under-detection or delayed awareness in any subgroup. When recalibration does not resolve drift or subgroup underperformance, remediation should escalate through defined stages. These can include tighter prompt constraints and rule adjustments first, followed by changes to retrieval sources, and ultimately replacement of the underlying model. For locally hosted open-source models, output guardrails offer intermediate mechanisms short of full replacement.

Safeguards, bias, and equity in continuous observability

LLMs hallucinate, miss uncommon presentations, and degrade as documentation habits shift over time16–18. For this reason, a clinical observability layer should be designed as a human-supervised triage system, not an autonomous decision-maker. Every proposed signal should cite its source, state its uncertainty, and flag what is unknown rather than filling gaps by inference. Performance tracking should be routine, with prompt or threshold adjustments when quality slips.

Event counting follows encounters, but complications do not. A patient’s postoperative course can span the OR, the ward, an outpatient visit, and a rehabilitation facility; a handoff in any of those transitions can obscure the problem. The observability layer must link documentation across the full episode. Because programs balance multiple objectives, they should report the work exchange transparently: extra cases found, the share confirmed on review, median time-to-signal, signal-to-response, and hours of manual case-finding retired. Early deployments will likely assign signal review to existing staff alongside their current duties, so the added review can delay existing quality activities even when the new queue itself performs well. Queue growth, reviewer hours required, and indicators of review fatigue should therefore be tracked against baseline and used to refine prioritization rules and escalation criteria. Crucially, the true intervention is the process changes the proposed AI system enables. Earlier signals can prompt earlier discussions with more reliable post-discharge follow-up. Programs should document these steps and link them to better time-to-signal. Over time, adverse-event and near-miss lists can guide targeted fixes, much like incident learning in other high-reliability settings19. Even when detection occurs outside the actionable window (e.g., after discharge), reducing latency in health system learning can reduce risk for future patients.

Conclusion

Clinicians are stretched thin, the EHR keeps growing, and complications are still found too late to change what happened. The foundation for a better approach exists. Recent advances in agentic AI enable systematic, iterative EHR-scale identification of emerging issues. Shortening the path from signal to action is a patient safety imperative, and clinical observability provides a practical way to close that gap20,21.
