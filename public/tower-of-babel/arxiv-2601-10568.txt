Hydrodynamic Limit with a Weierstrass-type result
Gabriel S. Nahum † {}^{\mathrel{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{${\dagger}$}}}

Abstract
. We show that any positive, continuous, and bounded function can be realised as the diffusion coefficient of an evolution equation associated with a gradient interacting particle system. The proof relies on the construction of an appropriate model and on the entropy method.

1.
Introduction

Understanding the macroscopic behaviour of interacting particle systems is a central theme in probability and statistical mechanics. This work studies the local density of particles in a discrete Markovian system, which, after a rescaling in space and time, converges to the solution of a particular differential equation. From a probabilistic viewpoint, this corresponds to a law of large numbers. This passage from the discrete to the continuum, or from the microscopic to the macroscopic, is known in the physics literature as the hydrodynamic limit. Within the landscape of interacting particle systems, gradient systems play an important role, as their local structure makes them mathematically very tractable. These are models in which the algebraic current can be expressed as the discrete gradient of a potential
[
21
, II, Subsection 2.4]
, further simplifying hydrodynamical analysis when compared to non-gradient models. For example, for a variety of models both equilibrium and non-equilibrium fluctuations can be obtained
[
1
,
10
,
11
,
9
]
as opposed to non-gradient models where this remains largely an open problem (see discussion in
[
6
]
and references therein). Moreover, in general the diffusion coefficient is described with Green Kubo formula
[
21
, II, Subsection 2.2]
, and particularising for gradient systems one can compute it explicitly with the knowledge of the invariant measures. This is convenient in devising models associated with a target diffusion. A physical interpretation has also been proposed for these systems, pointing to a dynamical term in the description of the general diffusivity that vanishes if
[
21
, II, Subsection 2.2]
and only if
[
19
]
the model is of gradient type, thus representing a purely static behaviour. It is therefore surprising that few general results within this class are available. The present work is a first attempt to address the fundamental question of how flexible such models can be: given a target diffusion equation, can one construct a microscopic dynamics whose empirical density evolves accordingly? It is worth mentioning that generality of functions has been investigated in the context of boundary conditions
[
16
]
, allowing for generic polynomial functions via prescribed boundary dynamics evolving at the scale of the current. An analysis of the dynamics in the bulk, as in the present work, becomes in some aspects more challenging due to the requirement of maintaining the gradient property. In this way, we shall restrict the studies to the one-dimensional discrete torus.

To provide some additional context regarding possible diffusivities, a classical example of linear diffusivity arises from the Symmetric Simple Exclusion Process
[
15
]
, arguably the simplest dynamics for which much is known. In this model, particles perform nearest-neighbour random walks with no directional bias in a discrete lattice and subject to an exclusion rule that prevents multiple occupancy of sites, yielding a Markov process with conserved density. Gradient models can encapsulate polynomial diffusivity, with the Porous Media Model (PMM)
[
8
]
acting as a toy model for diffusion coefficients of the form
D(\rho)=\rho^{m}
, for positive integer values of
m
. The dynamics is easily described: under the exclusion rule, a pair composed by a vacant and occupied sites exchange their occupation values with rate given by the number of boxes composed by
m
aligned particles around the pair. The derivation of non-polynomial diffusion from exclusion type dynamics was addressed in
[
12
]
, where it was performed an extension of the dynamics to non-integer values of the parameter
m
via a generalised binomial expansion, successful for values
m\in(-1,1)
. This allows a microscopic description of the transition from slow to fast diffusion, continuously parametrised, by a collection of gradient models. It is relevant to mention that the two-parts works
[
7
,
5
]
identify the diffusivity
D(\rho)=\rho^{m}
for
m\geq 1
for processes that are continuous in nature, associated with coupled oscillators and where other tools apply.

The work
[
4
]
follows on the direction of investigating non-polynomial diffusion in more generality and with long-range interactions. The works
[
12
,
4
]
evidence a deficiency in the porous media collection in generating particular diffusion, yielding possibly ”negative rates” by definition, that was then addressed in
[
18
]
. In it, the author introduces a collection of gradient dynamics modelling the Bernstein polynomial basis and generalising the PMM. Linear combinations in this basis, in the same spirit as
[
12
]
, were analysed in
[
18
]
, allowing for the introduction of the complete PMM family, continuously parametrised and yielding the diffusivity
D(\rho)=\rho^{m}
for any
m>1
, thus resolving the main open problem posed in
[
12
]
. The aforementioned works paved the way for the analysis carried out in the present text, culminating in a first Weierstrass-type result.

The model introduced here has critical differences from those studied in
[
18
]
, and requires the introduction of some novel arguments. The main strategy for proving the hydrodynamic limit, however, is analogous, as we follow the well established entropy method
[
13
]
. This choice of methodology is justified both by the possibility of relying on previous and convenient results, and by the fact that it prepares the ground for a future complete open-boundary analysis. With this in mind, in the next sections we present only the main model and novel results, directing the reader to appropriate references for specific steps of the proof. We highlight the need for novel arguments due to the non-uniform continuity of the microscopic potential associated with the gradient property (see Lemmas
3.1
and
4.2
and their applications in the proof of Theorem
2.8
). This is the main technical challenge with respect to
[
18
]
.

At a more technical level, and pointing to future work and open problems, the restriction to
positive
diffusivities is crucial, as it ensures that the model is irreducible.

1.1.
Open problems and future work

Our main model is irreducible, and the hopping rates depend on a “mesoscopic” part of the configuration: letting
N>>1
be the length of the system, the constraints are dependent of the information in a ball of radius
\ell_{N}+1=o(N)
, where
\ell_{N}\xrightarrow{}\infty
as
N\xrightarrow{}\infty
. If the function
\beta
were allowed to take the value zero, the resulting dynamics would belong to the class of
non-cooperative

[
20
]
models, for which ”mobile clusters” exist. Mobile clusters are blocks of configurations that can move together and facilitate the transport of mass thorough the system. These objects play a central phenomenological and technical role in non-irreducible systems, associated with diffusivities that can attain trivial value for specific densities
[
8
,
2
,
12
,
18
,
3
,
4
]
. In the present setting, however, allowing diffusivities that attain zero would give rise to mobile clusters whose length depends on the size of the system, making them too large to be analysed via standard arguments. This is in contrast with
[
18
]
, where their size is independent of the length of the system and therefore of completely local nature. The nature of these clusters brings the system technically closer to a
cooperative
model, that are we known to be non-gradient
[
20
]
. In this sense, it may act as a bridge between cooperative and non-cooperative models. This is left for future work.

We also identified a phase transition related with the range of interaction
2\ell_{N}+1
, when
\ell_{N}=O(N)
. The author is currently investigating the hydrodynamics of the resulting system in this regime, when in contact with reservoirs. One may also question the extension to higher dimensions. This can be performed straightforwardly by letting the process evolve as a one-dimensional model along each coordinate direction, yielding a higher-dimensional hydrodynamic equation. A challenging question concerns the derivation of fractional hydrodynamic equations, associated with long-range models. This would require a “long-jumps” extension of the nearest-neighbour dynamics in
[
17
]
, for which the arguments in
[
3
]
violate the gradient property.

Outline of the paper

\displaystyle\pi_{t}^{N}(\eta,G)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}G(u)\pi_{t}^{N}(\eta,\mathrm{d}u).

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle G,H\rangle_{1}}}=\langle\partial_{u}G,\partial_{u}H\rangle

The next definition corresponds to the notion of weak solution considered here.

Definition 2.6
.

For

a measurable function, we say that
\rho:[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}\mapsto[0,1]
is a weak solution of the equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho=\partial_{u}^{2}\Phi(\rho),&\text{in }(0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d},\\
\rho_{0}=\rho^{\text{ini}},&\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d}\end{cases}

(2.16)

if

(1)

\Phi(\rho)\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
;

(2)

for any

and

\rho
satisfies the formulation
\mathfrak{F}_{t}(\rho^{\rm ini},\rho,G)=0
, where

\mathfrak{F}_{t}(\rho^{\rm ini},\rho,G):=\langle\rho_{t},G_{t}\rangle-\langle\rho^{\rm ini},G_{0}\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}\bigg\{\langle\rho_{s},\partial_{s}G_{s}\rangle+\langle\Phi(\rho_{s}),\partial_{u}^{2}G_{s}\rangle\bigg\}\mathrm{d}s.

(2.17)

The uniqueness of solutions of the weak formulation in last definition is consequence of the regularity in (1). This is very straightforwardly shown via Oleinik’s method (see, for instance,
[

, Lemma B.3]
).

2.3.
Main Result

The hydrodynamic limit establishes a weak law of large numbers: starting from a
local equilibrium distribution
, the empirical measure converges weakly to an absolutely continuous measure, whose density is the unique solution of the hydrodynamic equation (
2.16
). Note that, indirectly, this establishes the existence of solutions of (
2.16
) in the weak sense of Definition
2.6
. We aim to present this statement rigorously.

Definition 2.7

(Local equilibrium distribution)
.

Let
\{\mu_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
be a sequence of probability measures on
\Omega_{N}
, and let
g:\mathbb{T}\to[0,1]
be a measurable function. If, for any continuous function
G:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
and every
\delta>0
, it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to+\infty}\mu_{N}\left(\eta\in\Omega_{N}:\big|\pi^{N}(\eta,G)-\langle g,G\rangle\big|>\delta\right)=0,

we say that the sequence
\{\mu_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
is a local equilibrium measure associated with the profile
g
.

A simple example of local equilibrium measures are the Bernoulli product measures with, for example, a continuous parameter-function. We now state the main theorem.

Theorem 2.8

(Hydrodynamic limit)
.

Let
\rho^{\rm ini}:\mathbb{T}\to[0,1]
be a measurable function and let
\{\mu_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
be a local equilibrium measure associated with it.
Then, for any
t\in[0,T]
and
\delta>0
, it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{N}}\left(\big|\pi_{t}^{N}(\eta,G)-\langle\rho_{t},G\rangle\big|>\delta\right)=0,

where
\rho
is the unique solution of the diffusion equation (
2.16
), in the sense of Definition
2.6
, with initial data
\rho^{\rm ini}
and

\displaystyle\Phi(\rho)=\int_{0}^{\rho}\beta(u)\mathrm{d}u.

(2.18)

3.
Proof of the Hydrodynamic Limit

In order to prove Theorem
2.8
, we apply the entropy method, first introduced in
[
14
]
. We start by presenting the general scheme of the proof, where we discuss the tightness of the measure and regularity of the density. Next, we proceed with the characterization of the limit measure of the process.

3.1.
General scheme

The link between our process and the weak formulation (
2.17
) is given through Dynkin’s martingale (see
[
15

),

\displaystyle\text{M}_{N}^{G}(t):=\pi_{t}^{N}(\eta,G_{t})-\pi_{0}^{N}(\eta,G_{0})

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\pi_{s}^{N}(\eta,\partial_{s}G_{s})+\mathfrak{L}_{N}[\pi_{s}^{N}](\eta,G_{s})\mathrm{d}s

(3.1)

for
G\in C^{2,1}(\mathbb{T}\times[0,T])
.

One can show that the sequence of probability measures
(\mathbb{Q}_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight with respect to the Skorokhod topology of
\mathcal{D}\left([0,T],\mathcal{M}_{+}\right)
by following
[

, Subsection 3.2]
, which invokes Aldous’ conditions. As evidenced in
[

, Proposition 3.1]
and
[
18
, Subsection 3.2]
, tightness for exclusion processes in the torus satisfying the gradient condition is consequence of the following:
(i)

|\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}|_{\infty}=o(N)
;
(ii)

|\mathbf{h}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}|_{\infty}
being uniformly bounded from above by a constant independent of
N
;
(iii)
and the estimate in Lemma

vanishing as
N\to\infty
. The former two are straightforwardly identified, therefore we refer the reader to the aforementioned references for the specific details to include them in the proof.

With this, we conclude that the sequence of empirical measures is tight, existing then weakly convergent subsequences. Since there is at most one particle per site, one can show that the limiting measure of a convergent subsequence of
(\mathbb{Q}_{N})_{N\geq 0}
, that we write as
\mathbb{Q}
, is concentrated on paths of absolutely continuous measures with respect to the Lebesgue measure. This means precisely that the sequence
(\pi_{\cdot}^{N}(\eta,\mathrm{d}u))_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly, with respect to

, to an absolutely continuous measure with a density that we write as

\pi_{\cdot}(\mathrm{d}u)=\rho_{\cdot}(u)\mathrm{d}u
.

Provided the aforementioned convergence by subsequences and absolutely continuity of the limit measure, one then argues that the density

satisfies the notion of weak solution in Definition
2.6
. This encompasses two results to be held

almost-surely:
(i)
satisfaction of the weak formulation

(ii)
regularity, as in (1) in Definition

A.2
and
A.3
), and the vanishing, as

2.13
), as in Lemma
4.1
, the regularity property (1) is consequence of

being uniformly bounded from above by a constant independent of
N
. For the precise computations, we refer the reader to
[
12
, Section 5]
, where this was shown for the ”Interpolating process” in the slow-diffusion regime; or
[
18

\mathbb{Q}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\norm{\varphi(s,\cdot)}_{\infty}}{4NA}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\frac{1}{\ell_{N}+1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{N}}\sum_{z=1}^{j}\tau^{-z+x}\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}^{j}\big|\nabla_{x+z+1,x+z}[\sqrt{f}]\big|^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N}

(4.8)

\displaystyle+\frac{A\norm{\varphi(s,\cdot)}_{\infty}}{2}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\frac{1}{\ell_{N}+1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{N}}\sum_{z=1}^{j}\tau^{-z+x}\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}^{j}\big(\theta_{x+z+1,x+z}+\mathbf{1}\big)[f]\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N},

(4.9)

for an arbitrary
A>0
. Let us focus on the term (
4.9
). Performing the change of variables
\eta\mapsto\eta^{x+z,x+z+1}
and recalling that
\alpha
is a constant profile, the integral in (
4.9
) is bounded from above by

\int_{\Omega_{N}}f\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\frac{1}{\ell_{N}+1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{N}}\sum_{z=1}^{j}\theta_{x+z+1,x+z}\tau^{-z+x}\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}^{j}\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N}\\
+\int_{\Omega_{N}}f\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\frac{1}{\ell_{N}+1}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{N}}\sum_{z=1}^{j}\tau^{-z+x}\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}^{j}\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N}\leq 2\ell_{N}|\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}|_{\infty},

(4.10)

from where we conclude that (
4.9
) is bounded from above by

\displaystyle\norm{\varphi(s,\cdot)}_{\infty}|\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}|_{\infty}\ell_{N}A.

(4.11)

We now focus on (
4.8
). Exchanging the summations,

\displaystyle\frac{\norm{\varphi(s,\cdot)}_{\infty}}{4NA}\sum_{z=1}^{\ell_{N}}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\frac{1}{\ell_{N}+1}\sum_{j=z}^{\ell_{N}}\tau^{-z+x}\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}^{j}\big|\nabla_{x+z+1,x+z}[\sqrt{f}]\big|^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N}

(4.12)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\norm{\varphi(s,\cdot)}_{\infty}}{4NA}\frac{\ell_{N}\mathfrak{D}_{N}(\sqrt{f})}{N^{2}},

(4.13)

and (
4.6
) can be bounded from above by

\displaystyle\frac{c_{\alpha}}{B}+M_{T,\varphi}|\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}|_{\infty}\ell_{N}A+\frac{1}{2N}\mathfrak{D}_{N}(\sqrt{f})\bigg(\frac{M_{T,\varphi}\ell_{N}}{2AN^{2}}-\frac{T}{B}\bigg),

(4.14)

with
M_{T,\varphi}=\int_{0}^{T}\norm{\varphi(s,\cdot)}_{\infty}\mathrm{d}s
. Solving

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{c_{\alpha}}{B}=M_{T,\varphi}|\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}|_{\infty}\ell_{N}A\\
\frac{M_{T,\varphi}\ell_{N}}{2AN^{2}}=\frac{T}{B}\end{cases}

(4.15)

for
A
and
B
yields the upper bound in the statement of this lemma.

∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Fixed
M\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, for each
0\leq m\leq M
and
u,v\in[0,1]
let

\displaystyle\mathfrak{V}_{m,M}(u,v)=\text{H}_{m,M}(u)-\text{H}_{m,M}(v)

(4.16)

with
\text{H}_{m,M}
as in (
3.20
). For any
t\in[0,T]
, any
G:[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
c_{T,G}:=\int_{0}^{T}\norm{G_s}_{\infty}\mathrm{d}s<\infty
and positive integer
L<\ell_{N}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\bigg[\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G_{s}(\tfrac{x}{N})\mathfrak{V}_{m,M}(\langle\tau^{x}\eta_{N^{2}s}\rangle_{\ell_{N}},\langle\tau^{x}\eta_{N^{2}s}\rangle_{L})\mathrm{d}s\bigg|\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c}{L}+c^{\prime}\frac{\ell_{N}}{\sqrt{\min\{\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}\}N^{2}T}},

(4.17)

with
c\equiv c_{T,G}c_{m,M}
,
c^{\prime}\equiv c_{m,M}^{\prime}c_{T,G}\sqrt{c_{\alpha}}
and where
c_{m,M},c_{m,M}^{\prime}>0
are constants dependent only on
m
and
M
.

Proof.

Proceeding as in the previous lemma, it is enough to control

\displaystyle\frac{c_{\alpha}}{B}+\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{f\text{ density}}\bigg\{\bigg|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G_{s}(\tfrac{x}{N})\int_{\Omega_{N}}\mathfrak{V}_{m,M}(\langle\tau^{x}\eta\rangle_{\ell_{N}},\langle\tau^{x}\eta\rangle_{L})f(\eta)\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N}(\eta)\bigg|-\frac{\mathfrak{D}_{N}(\sqrt{f})}{2NB}\bigg\}\mathrm{d}s,

(4.18)

where
B>0
will be fixed by the end of the proof. Noting that
\text{H}_{m,M}
is an increasing and non-negative polynomial of degree
M+1
, we can express

\displaystyle\mathfrak{V}_{m,M}(\langle\tau^{x}\eta\rangle_{\ell_{N}},\langle\tau^{x}\eta\rangle_{L})=\big(\langle\eta\rangle_{\ell_{N}}-\langle\eta\rangle_{L}\big)\text{V}_{m,M}(\langle\eta\rangle_{\ell_{N}},\langle\eta\rangle_{L})

(4.19)

for some
non-negative
form
\text{V}_{m,M}\in C^{M,M}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T})
. It is important to identify that from Taylor’s theorem

\displaystyle\big|\text{V}_{m,M}(\langle\eta\rangle_{\ell_{N}},\langle\eta\rangle_{L})-\text{V}_{m,M}(\langle\eta\rangle_{\ell_{N}},\langle\eta\rangle_{L}\pm\tfrac{1}{L+1})\big|\leq\frac{1}{L+1}\norm{\partial_2
\text{V}_{m,M}}_{\infty}.

(4.20)

Moreover, we also express

\displaystyle\langle\eta\rangle_{\ell_{N}}-\langle\eta\rangle_{L}=\frac{1}{(\ell_{N}+1)(L+1)}\sum_{w\in B_{\ell_{N}}}\sum_{z\in B_{L}}\big(\eta(w)-\eta(z)\big).

(4.21)

With the previous information, we express further

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Omega_{N}}\mathfrak{V}_{m,M}(\langle\tau^{x}\eta\rangle_{\ell_{N}},\langle\tau^{x}\eta\rangle_{L})f(\eta)\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N}(\eta)

(4.22)

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{(\ell_{N}+1)(L+1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in B_{\ell_{N}}\\
z\in B_{L}\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\big(\eta(w)-\eta(z)\big)\text{V}_{m,M}(\langle\eta\rangle_{\ell_{N}},\langle\eta\rangle_{L})\nabla_{w,z}[\tau^{-x}f](\eta)\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N}(\eta)

(4.23)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{(\ell_{N}+1)(L+1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in B_{\ell_{N}}\\
z\in B_{L}\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\big(\eta(w)-\eta(z)\big)\text{V}_{m,M}(\langle\eta\rangle_{\ell_{N}},\langle\eta\rangle_{L})\big(\mathbf{1}+\theta_{w,z}\big)[\tau^{-x}f](\eta)\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N}(\eta),

(4.24)

with
\mathbf{1}\equiv\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{N}}
the identity operator in
\Omega_{N}
. Let us focus on the second line in the previous display. From a change of variables and the fact that
\alpha
is a constant profile,

\int_{\Omega_{N}}\big(\eta(w)-\eta(z)\big)\text{V}_{m,M}(\langle\eta\rangle_{\ell_{N}},\langle\eta\rangle_{L})\theta_{w,z}f(\tau^{-x}\eta)\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N}(\eta)\\
=-\int_{\Omega_{N}}\big(\eta(w)-\eta(z)\big)\text{V}_{m,M}\big(\langle\eta\rangle_{\ell_{N}},\langle\eta\rangle_{L}\big)f(\tau^{-x}\eta)\mathbf{1}_{\{z,w\in B_{L}\}}\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N}(\eta)\\
-\int_{\Omega_{N}}\big(\eta(w)-\eta(z)\big)\text{V}_{m,M}\big(\langle\eta\rangle_{\ell_{N}},\langle\eta\rangle_{L}+\tfrac{\eta(w)-\eta(z)}{L+1}\big)f(\tau^{-x}\eta)\mathbf{1}_{\{w\notin B_{L}\ni z\}}\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N}(\eta).

(4.25)

This implies that, from the triangle inequality and then (
4.20
), the absolute value of the quantity in (
4.24
) can be bounded from above by

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{L+1}\norm{\partial_2
\text{V}_{m,M}}_{\infty}.

(4.26)

It is worth noting that it is implicit that
B_{L}\subset B_{\ell_{N}}
, which can be assumed since we will perform the limit
L\to\infty
after
N\to\infty
.

Now we focus on the term originating from (
4.23
). This is analysed with a standard path argument, exchanging the occupation values of the sites
w
and
z
. First bound from above

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G_{s}(\tfrac{x}{N})\frac{1}{\ell_{N}L}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in B_{\ell_{N}}\\
z\in B_{L}\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\big(\eta(w)-\eta(z)\big)\text{V}_{m,M}(\langle\eta\rangle_{\ell_{N}},\langle\eta\rangle_{L})\nabla_{w,z}[\tau^{-x}f](\eta)\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N}(\eta)\mathrm{d}s\Big|

(4.27)

\displaystyle\leq c_{T,G}\norm{\text{V}_{m,M}}_{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell_{N}L}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in B_{\ell_{N}}\\
z\in B_{L}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{N}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\big(\mathbf{e}_{x+w,x+z}+\mathbf{e}_{x+z,x+w}\big)\big|\nabla_{x+w,x+z}[f]\big|\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N}.

(4.28)

Next, proceeding with a classical path argument, exactly as from (
A.3
) to (
A.6
), we see that the right-hand side above is no larger than

\displaystyle 2c_{T,G}\norm{\text{V}_{m,M}}_{\infty}\Bigg\{\frac{\ell_{N}}{AN}\frac{\mathfrak{D}_{N}(\sqrt{f})}{N^{2}}+2^{2}\frac{\ell_{N}A}{\min\{\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}\}}\Bigg\}.

(4.29)

From the previous display and the analysis of (
4.24
), in order to conclude the proof it is enough to solve, for
A
and
B
, the system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}2c_{T,G}\norm{\text{V}_{m,M}}_{\infty}\frac{\ell_{N}}{ANN^{2}}=\frac{T}{2NB},\\
\frac{\textbf{c}_{\alpha}}{B}=c_{T,G}2^{3}\frac{\norm{\text{V}_{m,M}}_{\infty}}{\min\{\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}\}}\ell_{N}A.\end{cases}

(4.30)

∎

Lemma A.1
.

Fix
L\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,
w\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
and
y\notin B_{L}(w)
. For each
x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
, let
\varphi_{x}:[0,T]\times\mathcal{D}_{\Omega_{N}}[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
be independent of the occupation-values in
\{x+w+r,x+y\}_{r\in B_{L}}
, and such that
\text{c}_{t,\varphi}:=\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N},\eta\in\Omega_{N}}|\varphi_{x}(\cdot,s)|\mathrm{d}s<\infty
.

For every
t\in[0,T]
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\bigg[\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\varphi_{x}(s,\eta_{N^{2}s})\big(\eta_{N^{2}s}(x+w)-\eta_{N^{2}s}(x+z)\big)\mathrm{d}s\bigg|\bigg]\leq 4\sqrt{2\text{c}_{\alpha}}c_{T,\varphi}\frac{\mathrm{d}(w,z)}{\sqrt{\min\{\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}\}TN^{2}}}.

(A.1)

Proof.

From
[
18
, Proposition 4.2]
, it is enough to estimate

\displaystyle\frac{c_{\alpha}}{A}+\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{f}\bigg\{\int_{\Omega_{N}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}}\varphi_{x}(s,\eta)\big(\eta(x+w)-\eta(x+z)\big)f(\eta)\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N}(\eta)-\frac{\mathfrak{D}_{N}(\sqrt{f}|\nu_{\alpha}^{N})}{2NA}\bigg\}\mathrm{d}s

(A.2)

with the
\sup
taken over the set of densities with respect to
\nu_{\alpha}^{N}
. From (
4.2
), we can bound from above the integral over
\Omega_{N}
in (
A.2
) by

\displaystyle\frac{\text{c}_{s,\varphi}^{\prime}}{2N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\int_{\Omega_{N}}(\mathbf{e}_{x+w,x+z}+\mathbf{e}_{x+z,x+w})|\nabla_{x+w,x+z}[f]|\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N},

(A.3)

where
\text{c}_{s,\varphi}^{\prime}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}|\varphi_{x}(\cdot,s)|_{\infty}
.

We now proceed with a classical path argument. Let
S=2\mathrm{d}(w,z)-1
, denote by
\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{N}}
the identity operator in
\Omega_{N}
, and consider the sequence of operators defined through

\displaystyle\begin{cases}P_{n+1}=\theta_{x_{n},x_{n+1}}P_{n},&0\leq n\leq S-1,\\
P_{0}:=\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{N}},&\end{cases}

(A.4)

where the ordered sequence of bonds composed by nearest-neighbour sites
(\{x_{n},x_{n+1}\})_{n}
describes a path exchanging the occupation-value of the sites
w
and
z
. Concretely, let us suppose that the site
z
is at the right of
w
. The other case is analogous. Fixing for example,
x_{0}=x+w
, we have
x_{1}=x+w+1
,
x_{2}=x+w+2,\dots,x_{\text{d}(w,z)}=x+w+(z-w)
– in this way changing the occupation-value of the site
x+z
; next,
x_{\text{d}(w,z)+1}=x+z-1,\dots,x_{S-1}=x+w+1
,
x_{S}=x+w
– in this way switching the occupation-value of the sites
x+w
and
x+z
, while leaving the rest of the configuration invariant.

With this, we decompose

\displaystyle\nabla_{x+w,x+z}[f](\eta)=\sum_{n=0}^{S-1}\nabla_{x_{n},x_{n+1}}[f](P_{n}\eta),

(A.5)

apply the inequality
|a-b|\leq\frac{1}{2A}(\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b})^{2}+A(a+b)
, that holds for any
A>0
and
a,b>0
; and, writing
\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}^{k,k+1}\equiv\tau^{k}\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}
(resp.
\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}^{k,k-1}\equiv\tau^{k-1}\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}
) as the hopping rate in a generic node
\{k,k+1\}
(resp.
\{k-1,k\}
), the quantity in (
A.3
) can be bounded from above by

\frac{\text{c}_{s,\varphi}^{\prime}}{AN}\sum_{n=0}^{2\mathrm{d}(w,z)-1}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}^{x_{n},x_{x+1}}(P_{n}\eta)\big|\nabla_{x_{n},x_{n+1}}\sqrt{f}(P_{n}\eta)\big|^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha}^{N}+4\text{c}_{s,\varphi}^{\prime}\frac{\mathrm{d}(w,z)A}{\min\{\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}\}}\\
\leq 2\text{c}_{s,\varphi}^{\prime}\frac{\mathrm{d}(w,z)}{AN}\frac{\mathfrak{D}_{N}(\sqrt{f})}{N^{2}}+4\text{c}_{s,\varphi}^{\prime}\frac{\mathrm{d}(w,z)A}{\min\{\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}\}}.

(A.6)

In this way, (
A.2
) can be estimated from above by

\displaystyle\frac{c_{\alpha}}{B}+4\text{c}_{T,\varphi}\frac{\mathrm{d}(w,z)A}{\min\{\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}\}}+\frac{1}{N}\bigg(\text{c}_{T,\varphi}\frac{\mathrm{d}(x,z)}{AN^{2}}-\frac{T}{2B}\bigg)\mathfrak{D}_{N}(\sqrt{f}|\nu_{\alpha}^{N}).

(A.7)

Solving for
A
and
B
the system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{c_{\alpha}}{B}=4\text{c}_{T,\varphi}\frac{\mathrm{d}(w,z)A}{\min\{\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}\}},\\
\text{c}_{T,\varphi}\frac{\mathrm{d}(x,z)}{AN^{2}}=\frac{T}{2B}\end{cases}

(A.8)

concludes the proof.

∎

Corollary A.2

(One-block estimate)
.

Fix
L\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,
w\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
and
y\notin B_{L}(w)
. For each
x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
, let
\varphi_{x}:[0,T]\times\mathcal{D}_{\Omega_{N}}[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
be independent of the occupation-values in
\{x+w+r,x+y\}_{r\in B_{L}}
, and such that
\text{c}_{t,\varphi}:=\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N},\eta\in\Omega_{N}}|\varphi_{x}(\cdot,s)|\mathrm{d}s<\infty
.

For every
t\in[0,T]
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\bigg[\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\varphi_{x}(s,\eta_{N^{2}s})\big(\langle\tau^{x+w}\eta_{N^{2}s}\rangle_{L}-\eta_{N^{2}s}(x+y)\big)\mathrm{d}s\bigg|\bigg]

(A.9)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2\text{c}_{\alpha}}c_{T,\varphi}}{\sqrt{\min\{\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}\}TN^{2}}}\bigg(\mathrm{d}(w,y)+\frac{1}{L}\sum_{r\in B_{L}}\mathrm{d}(r,y)\bigg).

(A.10)

Proof.

Expressing

\displaystyle\langle\tau^{x+w}\eta\rangle_{L}-\eta(x+y)=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{r\in B_{L}}\big\{\eta(x+w+r)-\eta(x+y)\big\},

(A.11)

and replacing the right-hand side above inside the expectation in (
A.9
), then applying the triangle inequality and Lemma
A.1
on each term concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary A.3

(Two-block estimate)
.

Fix
\ell,L\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
such that
\ell>L
, and
y,w\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
such that
B_{\ell}(w)\cap B_{L}(y)=\emptyset
and
y<w
. For each
x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
, let
\varphi_{x}:[0,T]\times\mathcal{D}_{\Omega_{N}}[0,T]
be independent of the occupation-values in
B_{\ell}(x+w)\cup B_{L}(x+y)
and such that
c_{t,\varphi}:=\int_{0}^{t}|\varphi_{x}(s,\cdot)|_{\infty}\mathrm{d}s<\infty
.

For every
t\in[0,T]
it holds that

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\bigg[\bigg|\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\varphi_{x}(s,\eta_{N^{2}s})\big(\langle\tau^{x+w}\eta_{N^{2}s}\rangle_{\ell}-\langle\tau^{x+y}\eta_{N^{2}s}\rangle_{L}\big)\mathrm{d}s\bigg|\bigg]\\
\leq\frac{4\sqrt{2\text{c}_{\alpha}}c_{T,\varphi}}{\sqrt{\min\{\mathbf{r}_{\beta,\ell_{N}}\}TN^{2}}}\bigg(\frac{1}{L}+\mathrm{d}(w,y)+\ell+L\bigg).

(A.12)

Proof.

Let
\ell
be divisible by
L
. Otherwise, one can replace
\ell/L
by
\lfloor\ell/L\rfloor
for the rest of the proof, obtaining in the next display an additive error of the order of
1/L
. For any set
A\subset\mathbb{T}_{N}
, and
r\neq 0
, we shall write
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}rA}}=\{ra:\;a\in A\}
. Note that
\cup_{p\in LB_{\ell/L}}B_{L}(p)
forms a covering of
B_{\ell}
by non-intersecting sets, and clearly,
|LB_{\ell/L}|=\ell/L
. Decomposing then

\displaystyle\langle\tau^{x+w}\eta\rangle_{\ell}-\langle\tau^{x+y}\eta\rangle_{L}=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in LB_{\ell/L}\\
r\in B_{L}\end{subarray}}\big\{\eta(x+r+p+w)-\eta(x+r+y)\big\},

(A.13)

one proceeds analogously to the proof of previous lemma and then apply Lemma
A.3
. To conclude the proof, one bounds from above

\displaystyle\frac{L}{\ell}\sum_{p\in LB_{\ell/L}}\mathrm{d}(p,y)\leq\ell+L

(A.14)

∎
