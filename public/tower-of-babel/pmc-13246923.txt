Characteristics of Mycobacterial, COVID-19, and Talaromyces Marneffei Infections in Patients with IL12RB1 and IFNGR1 Mutations in Western China
Authors: Wenjing Zhang, Zhenzhen Zhang, Cong Liu, Xi Yang, Wenjing Tang, Shubin Xing, Ran Chen, Xuemei Tang, Zhiyong Zhang, Yuan Ding, Lina Zhou, Yanjun Jia, Yunfei An, Xiaodong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD) is a rare immunodeficiency characterized by increased vulnerability to weakly virulent mycobacteria. However, its clinical and molecular spectrum, as well as its susceptibility to infections beyond mycobacteria, remains incompletely understood.ObjectiveTo evaluate the clinical and genetic characteristics of MSMD patients, focusing on their susceptibility to mycobacteria, SARS-CoV-2 and endemic fungal infections.MethodsThirteen MSMD patients underwent genetic analyses, immunological assays, and clinical evaluations. COVID-19 outcomes were documented. Pathological findings and mNGS confirmed Talaromyces marneffei infections.ResultsFollowing BCG vaccination, 92.3% (12/13) developed regional or disseminated infections. Additional infections included severe tuberculosis and Mycobacterium avium. All patients experienced SARS-CoV-2 infections and present mild symptoms. One IL12RB1-deficient patient presented with isolated disseminated Talaromyces marneffei infection but otherwise healthy. Another AR IFNGR1-deficient patient with concurrent mycobacterial and fungal infections. Genetic analysis identified five novel mutations (c.155G > C, p.C52S, c. 617 A > C, p.Q206P, c.923 A > G; p.Y308C, c.1282_1301deIACCTTGTGGTCTACGGTCCTinsC; p.T428fs* in IL12RB1 and c.811 A > T, p.K271X in IFNGR1). Functional assays confirmed impaired IL12-IFNγ axis signaling. Patients with partial IFNGR1 defects showed complete clinical remission with higher doses IFN-γ therapy basing on STAT1 phosphorylation dynamics analysis under IFN-γ activation in vitro.ConclusionThe patient experienced significant diagnostic delays. Adverse reactions to BCG vaccination, NTM infection or severe mycobacteria tuberculosis infections in other wise healthy children strongly suggest MSMD. MSMD patients are susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 but do not exhibit severe COVID-19. Talaromyces marneffei could be an initial manifestation of MSMD in endemic regions. Early diagnosis and targeted therapy, including IFNγ and anti-tuberculosis treatments, are critical to improving outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10875-026-01991-6.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global health challenge, ranking as the second leading cause of death from infectious diseases after COVID-19. Individuals with primary immunodeficiencies, such as Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease (MSMD, OMIM 209950), are characterized by a selective predisposition to weakly virulent mycobacterial infections, such as Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) and environmental mycobacteria, although a minority of patients may also develop disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. MSMD is a rare genetic condition, inherited in a Mendelian pattern, that typically presents with normal immune responses to most pathogens but pronounced susceptibility to mycobacteria. Routine immunological screening in MSMD often appears normal, leading to the description of such infections as “idiopathic.” [1, 2] Since the first cases of disseminated BCG infection following vaccination were reported in 1951, the genetic basis of MSMD has been increasingly clarified. In 1996, Jouanguy et al. and Newport et al. independently identified the first causative gene, the IFN-γ receptor 1 (IFNGR1) gene [3–5]. To date, 22 genes and conferring 47 different conditions associated with impaired innate IFN-γ immune response have been identified, with IL12RB1 and IFNGR1 as the most commonly mutated [6]. Despite an estimated global incidence of 1 in 50,000 [7], MSMD is likely underdiagnosed due to its complex pathogenic mechanisms. For nearly half of affected patients, the precise genetic and immunologic underpinnings remain unknown [8]. In this study, we analyzed the clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, immunologic and genetic profiles, and treatment outcomes of 13 pediatric MSMD patients, including 7 with IL12RB1 mutations and 6 with IFNGR1 mutations, from 12 families referred to our hospital between June 2020 and June 2023. By exploring the clinical and experimental features of these patients, we aim to enhance understanding of the pathogenesis associated with IL12RB1 and IFNGR1 mutations and provide further clinical evidence supporting the use of interferon-γ in managing multidrug-resistant TB in this population.

Materials and Methods

Patients

Thirteen patients presenting clinical features of Mendelian Susceptibility to Mycobacterial Disease (MSMD) were enrolled. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians.

Detection of Gene Mutations

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kits (Qiagen, Germany). Mutations were identified through whole exome sequencing (WES) or targeted primary immunodeficiency (PID) panel sequencing, then confirmed by sanger sequencing.

IFNγR1 and IL12Rβ1 Protein Expression Detection by Flow Cytometry

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated and stimulated with phytohemagglutinin (PHA, 5 µg/mL; Pepro-Tech) at 37 °C for 72 h in a 5% CO₂ incubator to enhance protein expression. After stimulation, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies, including CD3-PerCPcy5.5, IL12RB1-APC or its isotype control, and IFNGR1-PE or its isotype control, for 30 min at 4 °C. Stained cells were analyzed on a FACSCanto™ II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) to quantify IFNγR1 and IL12Rβ1 expression on CD3⁺ T cells. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using FlowJo software.

Phospho-STAT1 Dynamics Analysis by Flow Cytometry and Western Blotting

PBMCs were stimulated with graded doses of IFN-γ (0, 1000, 10000, and 10,0000 IU/mL) for varying durations (0 and 15 min) or with IFN-α (20,000 IU/mL) for 15 min at 37 °C, as described in previous studies [9]. After stimulation, cells were stained with CD3-BV421, CD19-FITC, CD14-PE-Cy7, CD56-APC, and phospho-STAT1 (Tyr701)-PE or total STAT1-PE to assess phosphorylated and total STAT1 levels. Stained cells were analyzed on a FACSCanto™ II flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, USA) using FlowJo software (TreeStar, USA).

For Western blotting, PBMCs were stimulated with IFN-γ (20,000 IU/mL) for 0, 15, 30, 60, and 120 min at 37 °C, lysed, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were mixed with 5× loading buffer (1:4) and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with quick blocking buffer and incubated with primary antibodies against phospho-STAT1 (Tyr701) and total STAT1 (1:1000 dilution). β-actin served as the loading control, detected with an HRP-conjugated rabbit monoclonal antibody. Protein bands were visualized and quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Proportions of Th1, Th2, and Th17 Detection by Flow Cytometry

Whole blood samples were labeled with CD3-PerCP, CD4-PE-Cy7, CD45RA-FITC, CXCR5-BV421, CCR6-PE, and CXCR3-APC antibodies. After incubation at room temperature for 20 min in the dark, red blood cells were lysed, and cells were washed twice with PBS. The samples were analyzed on a FACSCanto™ II flow cytometer.

Neutrophil Respiratory Burst Detected by Flow Cytometry

Neutrophil respiratory burst was measured using dihydrorhodamine-123 (DHR; Sigma). Peripheral blood was divided into two 50 µL reactions: one incubated with phorbol myristate acetate (PMA; 2 ng/mL) and DHR (40 mmol) and the other with PBS and DHR, both at 37 °C for 20 min. Red blood cells were lysed, and remaining cells were resuspended in PBS for flow cytometric analysis.

Detection of IL12-IFNγ Axis Function by Flow Cytometry

PBMCs (2 × 10⁶ cells/mL) were stimulated with PMA (50 ng/mL) and ionomycin (500 ng/mL) for 4–6 h or with BCG (MOI = 20) alone or BCG + IL-12 (100 ng/mL) for 72 h. GolgiPlug (1 µg/mL) was added during the final 4–6 h. Cells were harvested, washed with PBS containing 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and stained with antibodies against CD3, CD4, CD8, CD19, and CD56, together with a fixable viability dye (eFluor™ 780, eBioscience). After fixation and permeabilization (Fixation/Permeabilization Kit, BD Biosciences), intracellular IFN-γ was detected using APC-conjugated anti–IFN-γ (BioLegend). Data were acquired on a flow cytometer and analyzed flow cytometry.

Construction of Plasmids to Mimic IFNGR1 Mutations in Patients

The full-length human IFNGR1 cDNA template (NM_000416) was subcloned into a 7.1–3×Flag-CMV expression plasmid as the wild-type (WT) IFNGR1 using a Seamless Cloning Kit (Biomed, China). Site-directed mutagenesis was used to generate IFNGR1 mutants, confirmed by Sanger sequencing. HEK 293 T cells were seeded in 24-well plates (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) and transfected with WT or mutant IFNGR1 plasmids using polyethylenimine (PEI). After 48 h, cells were stained with live/dead-APC-Cy7 and IFNGR1-PE and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) unless specified otherwise. Flow cytometry data were processed with FlowJo 10.3, and sequencing data with SnapGene. Statistical tests are described in figure legends. Significance was indicated by *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Genetic Findings in 13 MSMD Kindreds

Next-generation sequencing identified 11 mutations in the IL12RB1 gene among seven patients, all located within the extracellular domain. All of them had bi-allelic (autosomal recessive) variants, including five patients carried compound heterozygous mutations and two carried homozygous variants (Fig. 1A-B). Six patients carried mutations in IFNGR1: five with mono-allelic (autosomal dominant) mutations (c.811 A > T, p.K271X; c.817delA, p.Ile273Leufs*4; c.819_822del TAAT, p.N274Hfs*2), and only one with bi-allelic (autosomal recessive) mutations (c.655G > A, p.G219R). Previously reported mutations included c.637 C > T, p.R213W, c.632G > C, p.R211P, c.1791 + 2 T > G, c.872G > A, p.C291Y, and a deletion of exons 10–11 in IL12RB1 [10, 11], as well as c.819_822del4, Ile273Leufs*4, and c.655G > A, P.G219R in IFNGR1 [12]. Four mutations were novel: c.155G > C, p.C52S, c. 617 A > C, p.Q206P, c.923 A > G; p.Y308C, c.1282_1301deIACCTTGTGGTCTACGGTCCTinsC; p.T428fs* in IL12RB1, along with c.811 A > T, p.K271X in IFNGR1.

Clinical Manifestations of IFNGR1 and IL12RB1 Deficiency Patients

In P11, the infection disseminated to a lymph node in front of the left ear (Fig. 2D). Other Infections

In addition to mycobacterial infections, bacterial, viral infections were common in patients with IL12RB1 or IFNGR1 deficiencies (Fig. 3). Staphylococcus infections occurred in 3 patients, and 1 patient developed Salmonella sepsis. All 13 patients contracted COVID-19, but none experienced severe symptoms requiring hospitalization. Of these, 10 had fevers lasting no more than three days, 2 experienced low-grade fever with diarrhea, and 2 exhibited upper respiratory symptoms without fever. All cases resolved with symptomatic treatment, including ibuprofen. Parents reported that their children’s COVID-19 symptoms were milder and shorter in duration than their own, suggesting a relatively benign clinical course in MSMD patients. Additional infections included hand-foot-and-mouth disease in 3 patients and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection in 4 patients. Fungal infections were observed in 5 patients, including 3 cases of Candida infection and 2 cases of Talaromyces marneffei infection.Fig. 3Clinical Manifestations and Pathogens Identified in MSMD Patients. Symptoms

Symptoms varied across the cohort (Fig. 3). Five patients (5/13) experienced fevers lasting five or more days. Lymphadenopathy was observed in all patients (13/13), primarily due to infection. Other common symptoms included hepatomegaly (3/13) and splenomegaly (4/13). Most patients experienced one or two episodes of sinopulmonary infection, except for P13 (with AR IFNGR1), who had six episodes of pneumonia requiring hospitalization. Autoimmune manifestations were mild, with only one patient presenting photo allergy. Additionally, six patients (6/13) reported food allergies, eczema, or urticaria. No significant involvement of other systems, including cardiovascular, endocrine, nervous, or gastrointestinal systems, was noted.

Treatments

All patients underwent at least one surgical procedure to remove infected lesions (Table 1). Ten patients (10/13) required treatment with four or more anti-tuberculosis drugs. Adjunctive IFNγ therapy was administered to six patients, resulting in significant clinical improvement in most cases (a representative example, P11, is shown in the Supplementary Materials). However, one patient (P13, with autosomal recessive IFNGR1 deficiency) showed no satisfactory response to IFNγ treatment and eventually discontinued its use. Notably, P2, who harbors an IL12RB1 mutation, presented with an initial infection caused by the rare endemic fungus Talaromyces marneffei (Fig. 4). The patient was treated with intravenous liposomal amphotericin B for two weeks, followed by oral itraconazole for ten weeks, and subsequently achieved full recovery (details in Supplementary Materials). None of the patients underwent hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Molecular Findings

IFNGR1 or IL12RB1 Mutations Lead to Abnormal IFNγR1 and IL12Rβ1 Expression

To confirm the pathogenicity of identified mutations, IL12Rβ1 and IFNγR1 protein expression levels were analyzed in patients’ PBMCs. IL12Rβ1 expression was nearly absent on the T cell surface in patients P1, P4, P5, P6, and P7, consistent with IL12RB1 mutations (Fig. 5A). Conversely, IFNγR1 expression was elevated in patients P8, P10, P11, and P12 (Fig. 5B), while patient P13, with a homozygous IFNGR1 mutation, exhibited reduced IFNγR1 expression (Fig. 5C). To further validate these findings, plasmids encoding the identified IFNGR1 mutations were constructed and transiently transfected into 293 T cells. Impaired Function of the IL12-IFNγ Axis in Patients with IL12RB1 Mutations

Functional assays of the IL12-IFNγ axis revealed that T cells from patients with IL12RB1 mutations (P3, P5, P6 and P7) produced lower levels of IFNγ compared to controls after BCG stimulation (Fig. 5D). In controls, IFN-γ production was further enhanced by the addition of IL-12, whereas patient T cells (P1, P3, P5, P6, and P7) showed no significant difference between BCG alone and BCG plus IL-12 stimulation. Overall, IFN-γ production in patient T cells remained consistently lower than in controls, confirming impaired IL-12–IFN-γ axis function associated with IL12RB1 mutations.

Reduced Phosphorylation of STAT1 in IFNGR1 Deficient Patients

Under basal conditions, total STAT1 levels in patient P10 were lower than in controls. Upon IFNα stimulation, phosphorylated STAT1 (p-STAT1) levels were comparable between patients P10, P12, and controls (Fig. 6A). However, IFNγ stimulation revealed partially impaired p-STAT1 levels in patients P10, P11, and P12. Western blot analysis confirmed that p-STAT1 was significantly reduced after 15 min of IFNγ stimulation in patient P10 and was nearly degraded at 60 and 120 min, unlike controls, which maintained high p-STAT1 levels (Fig. 6B). To further investigate this partial impairment, we performed dose-gradient IFNγ stimulation on PBMCs from IFNGR1-deficient patients. Increased doses of IFNγ led to a gradual increase in p-STAT1 levels (Fig. 6C-D), indicating that the IFNγ response impairment caused by the autosomal dominate IFNGR1 mutation can be partially restored with higher IFNγ doses. In contrast, patient P13, with complete autosomal recessive IFNGR1 deficiency, did not show improved STAT1 phosphorylation with increased IFNγ doses in vitro (Fig. 6C-D).

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the clinical and genetic profiles of 13 patients with Mendelian Susceptibility to Mycobacterial Disease (MSMD) caused by IFNGR1 and IL12RB1 deficiencies. We identified novel mutations, clarified their effects on protein expression, and demonstrated their pathogenic consequences on the IL12-IFNγ axis. Our findings highlight the diverse clinical manifestations, ranging from localized to severe disseminated infections, COVID-19, endemic fungal infection and underscore the importance of adjunctive IFNγ therapy in certain cases.

Our results align with previous studies identifying IL12RB1 and IFNGR1 mutations as critical contributors to impaired IFNγ signaling and susceptibility to mycobacterial infections. The novel mutations identified—C52S, Q206P, Y308C, T428fs* in IL12RB1, and K271X in IFNGR1—expand the known genetic landscape of MSMD. In our West China cohort, 92.3% of patients initially presented with BCG-related lymphadenopathy, and five later developed tuberculosis. This finding is consistent with reports from other Chinese studies, which showed a high BCG infection rate of 93.3% (56/60) [10, 11, 13, 14]. A global review of MSMD patients revealed that 84.8% experienced adverse reactions to BCG vaccination, with lymphadenopathy as a common initial symptom in 46.8% [15]. Given the risks associated with BCG vaccination in this population [16], we also recommend delaying vaccination, especially for children with a family history of tuberculosis, pending immunological evaluation.

Defects in IFNAR1, IRF7, TBK1, and TLR3, as well as mutations associated with the Type I interferon pathway or the presence of autoantibodies against Type I interferons, have been linked to critical COVID-19 due to impaired antiviral immunity mediated by type I IFNs [17–21]. In contrast, patients with MSMD caused by IL12RB1 or IFNGR1 mutations may become infected with SARS-CoV-2 but rarely develop severe disease. This likely reflects the preservation of their type I IFN-α/β–dependent pathway, which provides effective antiviral protection, whereas the immunological defect is confined to the type II IFN-γ axis responsible for antimycobacterial defense. We also observed that the type I IFNα-dependent pathway remains functional in PBMCs from patients with autosomal dominant (AD) IFNGR1 deficiency.

Additionally, milder COVID-19 symptoms in children, as reported in other studies [22–24], may further explain the relatively benign clinical course observed in our cohort. To our knowledge, this is the first study to document COVID-19 outcomes in MSMD patients, providing reassurance that their condition does not predispose them to severe COVID-19.

We observed that patients with IFNGR1 mutations were at higher risk for osteomyelitis, while IL12RB1 deficiencies predominantly led to mycobacterial lymphadenitis. Studies indicate that IFNGR1 is highly expressed in bone marrow, potentially limiting anti-mycobacterial defenses in IFNGR1-deficient individuals [25]. Additionally, IFNγ plays a known role in inhibiting osteoclastogenesis, suggesting that reduced IFNγ signaling may increase osteoclast activity, contributing to osteomyelitis [26]. Recent findings also show enhanced osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption in IFNGR1-deficient cells, implicating impaired RANK signaling and excessive osteoclast activation in bone pathology [27].

The case of P2, presenting with Talaromyces marneffei infection as the initial manifestation, in addition to co-infection in P13 underscores the importance of thorough fungal diagnostics, particularly in endemic regions [28]. The distinct morphological features of T. marneffei, such as the “sausage” shape with central septation, serve as valuable diagnostic markers. Given the high mortality associated with delayed diagnosis [29], we advocate for fungal screening in MSMD patients, as T. marneffei infection may represent an initial manifestation of MSMD in regions like China and Southeast Asia.

The clinical course of P11 illustrates the life-threatening nature of Mycobacterium avium infection in IFNGR1-deficient individuals. Although extensive multidrug therapy was initially unsuccessful, adjunctive IFNγ therapy achieved significant improvement. The case highlights the potential of IFNγ to partially restore IFNγ pathway functionality in patients with partial IFNGR1 defects, aligning with previous studies that report clinical improvement in IFNGR1-deficient patients receiving IFNγ [10, 30–33]. Conversely, P13, with a complete autosomal recessive IFNGR1 deficiency, showed no response to IFNγ, underscoring the need for functional testing to guide treatment strategies. IFNa could enhance the phosphorylation of STAT1 both in partially and complete IFNGR1 deficient cells. We assume the reason for this was that IFNa activation overlaps to some extent with IFNγ, and another possible reason is that ISG15 produced by IFN-α/β activation can induce NK and T cells to produce interferon γ [34]. This suggests that IFNα may be considered in cases where IFNγ is unavailable, a strategy supported by prior reports on IFNα use in treating mycobacterial infections [35, 36].

This study is limited by its small sample size and variability in clinical management due to the rarity of MSMD. Due to the delayed availability of next-generation sequencing (NGS) results confirming P2’s IL12RB1 genotype, insufficient samples were available to complete protein expression analysis for this patient. Additionally, the lack of long-term follow-up for some patients limits insights into the sustained efficacy of IFNγ therapy. Future studies with larger cohorts and standardized protocols are essential to refine treatment strategies. Exploring novel therapeutic agents targeting other pathways may provide alternative options for patients unresponsive to IFNγ. Furthermore, developing early screening and diagnostic tools, particularly in endemic regions, could facilitate timely intervention and reduce the disease burden.
