TinZr: A compact wireless ESP32-C3 platform for multi-modal physiological sensor integration and data acquisition
Authors: Ludvik Alkhoury, Tony Moore, Petras Swissler, N Jeremy Hill, Sudhin A Shah, Moshe Kam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrocontrollers have become central to wearable technologies. As these systems continue to evolve, both form factor and functional integration have become critical design considerations. In this study, we present TinZr, a compact, wireless, and open-source ESP32-C3-based microcontroller that serves as a central hub for interfacing with external sensing modules, data logging, and wireless transmission. The TinZr integrates an inertial measurement unit (IMU), an onboard battery management circuit, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth Low Energy, a microSD card reader, and multiple GPIOs supporting digital, analog, I2C, SPI, and UART interfaces. The complete system measures only 25 mm × 25 mm, making it well-suited for wearable applications. All production files, including printed circuit board (PCB) design, firmware, and a 3D model of a wearable enclosure, are publicly available. Three primary use cases for TinZr are demonstrated: (a) multi-device communication and coordination, (b) wireless IMU data acquisition, and (c) simultaneous multi-modal physiological signal acquisition using external electrocardiography (ECG) and photoplethysmography (PPG) sensing modules. The TinZr supports both real-time data streaming via Wi-Fi/BLE and local logging to a microSD card. Local logging makes the TinZr suitable for extended research experiments and field studies where continuous wireless connectivity may not be available. An accompanying Arduino library, TinZrConnect, is provided to facilitate programming and integration with existing systems. Overall, the combination of compact design, wireless flexibility, and open-source accessibility makes TinZr a versatile solution for various sensing applications.Keywords: ESP32-C3 microcontroller, Wearable devices, Multimodal physiological signal acquisition, Multi-device coordination, Wireless communication, MicroSD data logging

Hardware in context

Wearable sensing platforms are gaining popularity for their ability to deliver non-invasive, continuous health insights to athletes, patients, and everyday users — while evolving to incorporate sophisticated on-device sensors and analytics. The global wearable sensors market was valued in 2024 at USD 1.4 billion and is projected to grow to USD 6.3 billion by 2033, at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 18.3% over 2025–2033 [1], [2]. This surge is largely driven by growing demand for telehealth, long-term monitoring, real-time health tracking, and military applications.

Physiological signal monitoring has become increasingly widespread, with wearable systems now employed by athletes, patients, soldiers, and everyday users to enable continuous health and performance tracking [3], [4], [5], [6]. Consequently, many everyday wearables (e.g., watches) now go beyond basic functionality by including onboard sensing hardware, such as accelerometers for activity and motion tracking [7]; photoplethysmography (PPG) sensors for heart rate [8] and oxygen saturation calculation [9]; electrocardiography (ECG) sensors for cardiac rhythm monitoring [10]; temperature sensors for body or skin thermal profiling [11]; and even biochemical sensors for measuring variables like glucose [12], for richer health and activity monitoring [4], [13].

Wearable systems for physiological monitoring increasingly depend on microcontroller units (MCU) and platforms that provide tight integration, compact form factors, and support for multi-modal sensing. The continued miniaturization and increasing computational capability of modern MCUs have made it possible to embed real-time sensing, processing, and wireless communication within small, battery-powered systems. Additionally, such MCUs offer sufficient local processing power, robust data logging, and versatile interfaces for auxiliary modules such as inertial, optical, and electrophysiological sensors.

Several open hardware and research platforms have emerged to address these challenges. For instance, CogWatch [14] is an open-source multi-sensor wrist platform that integrates heart rate, respiration, skin conductance, and temperature sensing, to study physiological and behavioral responses in daily activities. CogWatch uses the Adafruit QT Py ESP32-S2 MCU [15] for data acquisition and measures around 29 mm × 38 mm (just the PCB without any sensor enclosures). H-Watch [16] extends these capabilities into a wristwatch-like platform that incorporates multiple radios and energy harvesting, maintaining ultra-low power consumption for long-term wearable deployment. The main purpose of H-Watch is early COVID-19 detection. BioGAP-Ultra [17] is a modular edge-AI platform that supports synchronized acquisition of EEG, EMG, ECG, and PPG signals with on-device processing and flexible form factors. The BioGAP-Ultra firmware runs on the nRF5340 SoC using the Zephyr RTOS, and the accompanying Android software suite enables background data reception, real-time visualization, some parameter configuration, and data storage. Finally, the Multi-Sensor Wearable Health Device (MWHD) framework [18] uses the CC2640R2F Cortex®-M3 [19] MCU for real-time heart rate and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) monitoring from PPG signals, temperature, and motion, coupled with wireless data transmission and mobile application feedback. Together, these platforms illustrate the state-of-the-art in wearable microcontroller integration, where the convergence of low-power embedded processing, miniaturization, and open hardware design has enabled compact, reconfigurable, and affordable solutions for physiological signal monitoring.

Some of the above-mentioned platforms demand significant expertise to program or extend at the driver and firmware level. However, there are compact and flexible MCUs that can be easily programmed via the Arduino ecosystem — making them highly accessible for rapid prototyping, research deployments, and wearable device development. For example, we list several widely used controllers such as Seeed XIAO ESP32-C3 [20], DORHEA ESP32-C3 Mini [21], DFRobot Beetle ESP32-C6 [22], Adafruit GEMMA v2 [23], and PJRC Teensy 4.1 [24]. In general, most of these controllers are compact and thus suitable for wearable applications. The Seeed XIAO ESP32-C3 is very compact and offers Wi-Fi/BLE connectivity with onboard battery management, though it lacks a microSD card reader and a dedicated on-board QWIIC connector for plug-and-play I2C peripheral expansion. The DORHEA ESP32-C3 Mini does not include battery management, an on-board QWIIC connector, or an SD card interface. The DFRobot Beetle ESP32-C6 similarly lacks an SD card reader and an on-board QWIIC connector. The Adafruit GEMMA v2 is extremely small and lightweight but does not include a charging unit, SD card reader, or an on-board QWIIC connector. The PJRC Teensy 4.1 includes an SD card reader, making it well-suited for data logging, but it lacks Wi-Fi/BLE and is physically bulkier than the other options.

In this study, we introduce the TinZr, which builds on existing designs by providing a fully wireless controller in a compact 25 mm × 25 mm form factor, combining sensing, data logging, and communication in an open-source package. It uses the ESP32-C3 MCU and integrates an inertial measurement unit (IMU) comprising a 3-axis accelerometer and 3-axis gyroscope, battery management circuitry, Wi-Fi and BLE 5.0 connectivity, a microSD card reader, and GPIOs supporting digital, analog, I2C (it provides an on-board QWIIC connector), SPI, and UART. While TinZr can directly acquire analog signals through its onboard ADC, many physiological sensing applications require dedicated sensor modules or analog front-end circuitry to provide signal conditioning, amplification, filtering, and digitization. In such configurations, TinZr serves as the central acquisition, coordination, data logging, and communication hub for the connected sensing devices. A detailed specification comparison between the commercially available controllers and the TinZr is available in Table 1. Importantly, the TinZr can be programmed via the Arduino ecosystem, lowering the barrier to use. This functional density enables multimodal physiological signal acquisition, on-device processing, and flexible data handling (wireless streaming or local logging). All production files for the TinZr, including PCB designs and 3D enclosure models for wearables applications, are publicly available at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21892119. The onboard microSD reader supports long-duration, high-resolution logging, essential for field or extended experiments where continuous wireless transmission may not be feasible. Wireless connectivity via Wi-Fi and BLE remains available when real-time streaming is needed. We provide an accompanying Arduino library, TinZrConnect, to facilitate programming and integration of TinZr with existing systems.

ESP32-C3 development board

The central component of the TinZr is an ESP32-C3-based microcontroller module, labeled “ESP32-C3” in Fig. 1. The ESP32-C3 module integrates a single-core RISC-V processor with native 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi and Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) connectivity, providing a compact and power-efficient solution well suited for wearable and portable applications. The processor operates at clock frequencies of up to 160 MHz, offering sufficient computational performance for real-time control, coordination, and wireless communication tasks.

The ESP32-C3 module provides a sufficient number of general-purpose input/output (GPIO) pins to support the required digital and analog interfaces, including I2C, SPI, and UART communication buses, while maintaining low power consumption during both active operation and deep sleep modes. Although the ESP32-C3 module offers fewer GPIOs than larger development boards such as the Teensy 4.1, the pin allocation in TinZr was carefully designed to prioritize the interfaces and peripherals most relevant to physiological signals coordination, wearable sensing, and wireless data transfer. As a result, all core system functionalities are supported without reliance on external multiplexing or expansion hardware.

The ESP32-C3 module is positioned centrally on the TinZr PCBA, with its USB interface accessible at the board edge to facilitate firmware development, debugging, and power delivery (Fig. 1).

Overall, the ESP32-C3 module provides a compact, wireless, and power-efficient computational core that enables TinZr’s functionality while supporting scalable, open-hardware development.

Inertial measurement unit (IMU)

Motion sensing in the TinZr platform is provided by an STMicroelectronics LSM6DS3TR-C inertial measurement unit (IMU), labeled “LSM6DS3TR-C IMU” in Fig. 1. The LSM6DS3TR-C unit integrates a 3-axis accelerometer and a 3-axis gyroscope in a compact, low-power package, enabling continuous monitoring of linear acceleration and angular velocity.

The accelerometer supports selectable full-scale ranges up to ±16 g, while the gyroscope supports angular rate measurements up to ±2000 dps, allowing the device to capture both fine-grained motion and high-dynamic activities. The IMU supports output data rates up to several kilohertz and communicates with the ESP32-C3 module via the I2C bus, providing reliable and low-latency motion data acquisition.

The LSM6DS3TR-C unit was selected for its low power consumption, small footprint, and proven reliability in wearable and embedded applications. In the context of TinZr, IMU data can be used to support motion-aware sensing, artifact detection, and coordination tasks, particularly in mobile and wearable experimental settings.

Battery management and power regulation

Power management in the TinZr platform is handled by a Texas Instruments BQ24075RGTR battery management integrated circuit, labeled “BQ24075RGTR Battery Manager” in Fig. 1. The BQ24075RGTR is a single-cell lithium-ion/lithium-polymer (Li-ion/Li-Po) charger integrated circuit (IC) with power-path management, enabling simultaneous battery charging and system operation.

The device supports USB-compatible input sources and provides regulated power to the system while safely managing battery charging, including preconditioning, constant-current, and constant-voltage charging phases. Integrated safety features, such as thermal regulation, overvoltage protection, and charge termination, ensure reliable and safe operation in wearable and portable applications.

Power-path management allows TinZr to operate directly from an external power source when available, while automatically switching to battery power when disconnected, without interrupting system operation. This capability is particularly important for long-duration experiments and mobile use cases, where uninterrupted operation and safe battery handling are critical. The BQ24075RGTR was selected for its compact footprint, low external component count, and proven reliability in battery-powered embedded systems.

The charging behavior of the BQ24075RGTR unit in TinZr is configured using three external resistors connected to the “ISET”, “ILIM”, and “TMR” pins. The fast-charge current is set by a resistor on the “ISET” pin. With an 8.2 kΩ resistor, the resulting charge current is approximately 100 mA, which is well matched to the 150 mAh single-cell lithium-polymer (Li-Po) battery [28] used with the TinZr and corresponds to a charge rate of approximately 0.67C.

The maximum input current drawn from the external power source is limited via the “ILIM” pin to ensure compatibility with USB power supplies while supporting simultaneous system operation and battery charging. With a 3 kΩ resistor connected to “ILIM”, the input current is limited to approximately 520 mA, providing sufficient headroom for system operation while remaining within typical USB power constraints.

In addition, a resistor connected to the “TMR” pin sets the safety timeout for the charging process. With a 46.4 kΩ resistor, the precharge safety timer is approximately 37 min, and the fast-charge safety timeout is approximately 6.2 h, providing protection against abnormal charging conditions. These resistor values were selected specifically for the 150 mAh battery [28] used with the TinZr, and can be adjusted (by desoldering the onboard resistors and replacing them with desired ones) to accommodate larger-capacity batteries by increasing the allowable charge and input currents, while maintaining manufacturer-recommended charge rates and thermal limits.

The battery is soldered directly to the rear side of the PCB at the pads labeled (+) and (-), ensuring correct polarity and compact mechanical assembly.

On-board controllable RGB LED

TinZr includes an addressable RGB status LED (“NeoPixel”-class), labeled “RGB LED” in Fig. 1. This type of LED integrates red, green, and blue emitters together with a small control driver in a single package, allowing the LED color and brightness to be digitally controlled from a single microcontroller GPIO pin.

The RGB LED can be programmed to provide a visual indication of device state during development and deployment (e.g., BLE advertising/connection state, recording status, or error conditions). Since the LED can draw non-negligible current at high brightness, TinZr firmware limits brightness and disables the LED during low-power operation when appropriate. This feature preserves battery life while retaining a simple, user-facing diagnostic interface.

The RGB LED is a fully software-controllable indicator intended for general system feedback and debugging. TinZr also includes dedicated power and charging LEDs (bottom right corner of the top layer in Fig. 1) that operate independently of firmware, providing a visual indication of device power and battery charging status.

MicroSD card interface

Local data storage in the TinZr platform is provided by an on-board microSD card reader, labeled “microSD Reader” in Fig. 1. The microSD interface allows sensor data, timestamps, and system logs to be stored directly on removable flash media, enabling fully standalone operation without reliance on a continuous wireless connection.

The microSD card communicates with the ESP32-C3 module via the SPI bus, providing a simple and widely supported interface for embedded systems. This design choice ensures broad compatibility with standard microSD cards and commonly used embedded file systems, while maintaining predictable timing and resource usage. The SPI-based implementation also simplifies integration alongside other peripherals on the shared communication bus.

The inclusion of local storage is particularly important for long-duration recordings, field deployments, and wearable experiments where wireless bandwidth or connectivity may be limited or unavailable. By offloading data logging to the microSD card, TinZr supports reliable data capture without requiring continuous wireless transmission. We validated data logging using a 32 GB microSDHC card (Silicon Power Elite, FAT32-formatted).

QWIIC connector

The TinZr platform includes an on-board QWIIC-compatible expansion connector, labeled “QWIIC” in Fig. 1, providing a standardized interface for external peripheral integration. The QWIIC connector exposes the I2C bus along with power and ground, enabling rapid connection of compatible sensors and modules without soldering.

By leveraging the I2C interface, the QWIIC connector allows additional devices — such as environmental sensors, auxiliary motion sensors, or other low-power peripherals — to be easily integrated into the system. This design supports modular expansion while minimizing wiring complexity and reducing the risk of incorrect connections during prototyping or deployment.

The inclusion of a QWIIC connector enhances the flexibility of the TinZr platform by enabling rapid hardware extensions without modifying the core PCB design. This feature is particularly useful for experimental and research-oriented applications, where sensor configurations may evolve over time or vary between deployments.

Reset and user push buttons

TinZr includes two on-board push buttons: a dedicated reset button, labeled “Reset PB”, and a multifunction push button, labeled “Boot/Main PB” in Fig. 1. The reset button provides a hardware reset of the ESP32-C3 module, allowing the system to be restarted without cycling power during development or troubleshooting.

The main button serves a dual purpose. When used in conjunction with the reset button, it can be used to place the ESP32-C3 module into bootloader mode for firmware flashing (see ‘Program’ in Section 6.1). During normal operation, the main button can be repurposed by the firmware as a general user input, enabling application-specific functionality such as mode selection, event marking, or user interaction.

The main button is implemented as right-angle (90°) tactile switch. This orientation allows the button to be actuated from the side of the enclosure rather than the top surface of the PCB, simplifying mechanical integration and improving usability in compact enclosures. The right-angle design is particularly advantageous for wearable applications, where low profile, accessibility, and protection against accidental presses are important considerations.

Micro-USB connector

The TinZr platform includes a micro-USB connector, labeled “USB Connector” in Fig. 1. It provides both power input and a wired communication interface to the ESP32-C3 module. This connector is used for battery charging via the on-board power management circuitry as well as for firmware flashing, debugging, and development during device configuration.

When connected to an external power source, the micro-USB interface supplies power to the system and enables charging of the on-board Li-Po battery through the BQ24075 battery management IC. During development, the same interface allows firmware to be uploaded to the ESP32-C3 module and supports serial communication for debugging and status monitoring.

The micro-USB connector was selected for its compact form factor, mechanical robustness, and wide availability of cables, making it well suited for both early-stage development and enclosed wearable applications. Its placement at the edge of the PCB facilitates straightforward access when the device is integrated into a protective enclosure.

Build instructions

Step-by-step instructions are provided, on how to manufacture the TinZr.

PCB manufacturing

As previously mentioned, all the files needed in the PCB manufacturing process are publicly available. These files are Gerber, Pick-and-Place, and BOM, and they can be found in the Zenodo repository. We have used JLCPCB (https://jlcpcb.com/)1 to manufacture the boards since it provides a wide variety of base materials and colors and other PCB specifications. We designed and built the TinZr Rev 5 as a 4-layer PCB, using a standard quality PCB with flame retardant (FR-4) as the base material. We picked a PCB thickness of 1.6 mm with a hot air solder leveling (HASL) (with lead) surface finish. We also chose the copper weight on the top and bottom to be 1 oz/ft2. We used two ground planes (top and bottom).

Wearable enclosure 3D printing

We provide a 3D model (.stl files) for an enclosure designed to fit the TinZr Rev5’s PCBA as well as a 150 mAh Li-Po battery that power the TinZr.

The wearable enclosure was printed using a local Creality Ender-3 3D printer (Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM)), using PLA filaments. The quality of the print was acceptable for our purposes. In case the user requires a higher resolution, we suggest using a higher quality alternatives such as Multi-Jet Fusion (MJF) printing, which could result in a higher-quality product (but comes at a higher price).

Component soldering

For our design, we used the mounting and soldering services provided by JLCPCB to assemble and solder all components on the TinZr board. The only exception was the main push button, which was not available through JLCPCB at the time of ordering and was therefore soldered manually.

TinZr provides a battery-level monitoring feature. Battery voltage is measured via the bat-lvl pad, which must be soldered to the Pin 1 pad (see the bottom layer of Fig. 1); this pin is designated in the TinZr firmware as the default battery-sensing input. Programming

The TinZr Arduino library, TinZrConnect, can be downloaded directly from the TinZr GitHub repository https://github.com/ludvikalkhoury/TinZr.git. In addition to the Arduino library, TinZr provides a custom Arduino Board Manager package with full support for the TinZr Rev 5 hardware, enabling seamless installation and configuration within the Arduino IDE. The TinZrConnect library includes a collection of example sketches designed to test and demonstrate the full functionality of the TinZr Rev 5 board.

For instance, the TinZrCore sketch provides a basic demonstration of core hardware features, including the main push button, status LED, and battery level monitoring (assuming the battery-level pad has been soldered to Pin 1 pad as described in Section 5.4).

The TinZrComCenter sketch establishes a centralized communication framework in which multiple TinZr devices connect to a single host system acting as a communication hub. In this architecture, each TinZr operates as an independent node that can transmit data and receive control messages over Wi-Fi. The central hub manages all connected devices and can selectively broadcast commands to all TinZr units or address individual devices independently. This design enables coordinated multi-device operation and is well suited for distributed or swarm-style applications, such as coordinated multi-device sensing or collaborative robotics.

Another example, TinZrWearableSD, enables communication between one or more TinZr devices and a central PC over Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), allowing the PC to control recording start and stop events while enabling local logging of inertial measurement unit (IMU) data (accelerometer and gyroscope) as well as red and infrared photoplethysmographic (PPG) data when a MAX30102 sensor [29], [30] is connected via QWIIC.

Both TinZrComCenter and TinZrWearableSD are accompanied by graphical user interfaces (GUIs), along with the underlying Python source code, allowing users to operate the system without requiring extensive experience in firmware or software development. In later sections of this paper, we demonstrate the capabilities of the TinZr Rev 5 using TinZrComCenter and TinZrWearable as representative example use cases.

Operation instructions

User instructions

The TinZr Rev 5 operates similarly to other microcontrollers in the ESP32 family. Here we follow a simple three-step workflow: Power, Program, and Deploy.

(1)Power: The TinZr Rev 5 can be powered either by connecting it to a host computer via USB or by using a connected rechargeable battery for standalone or wearable operation. When powered through USB, a white status LED illuminates, indicating that the board is receiving power. When a battery is connected and charging, a green charging-status LED remains illuminated and turns off once charging is complete. When operating solely on battery power, no LEDs are illuminated. The power and charging status LEDs are located to the left of the USB connector at the same vertical level (see Fig. 1, top layer).

(2)Program: The TinZr Rev 5 can be programmed with any ESP32-compatible firmware, including user-developed sketches or example programs provided with the TinZrConnect library. Programming is performed using the Arduino IDE after selecting the appropriate board and serial port. In some cases, the board must be placed into bootloader mode manually. This is accomplished by pressing and holding the main button, briefly pressing and releasing the reset button while continuing to hold the main button, and then releasing the main button. Safety considerations

The TinZr Rev 5 is a low-voltage, battery- and USB-powered microcontroller platform intended for embedded sensing, wearable prototyping, and wireless and local data acquisition. The device does not make direct electrical contact with the wearer, nor does it generate or apply electrical stimulation or excitation signals. As such, when used as intended, the TinZr presents minimal inherent electrical risk.

When deployed in wearable or human-adjacent applications, the TinZr is typically powered solely by its onboard battery and communicates wirelessly via BLE or Wi-Fi. This battery-only, wireless mode of operation significantly reduces the risk of electrical shock associated with mains-powered systems.

If the TinZr is connected to external sensors or peripherals that may contact a human subject, it is the user’s responsibility to ensure that those components meet appropriate electrical safety and isolation standards. The platform does not provide medical-grade electrical isolation and should not be considered a standalone biomedical acquisition device. When used with human-connected sensors, the safety of the overall system depends on the design and validation of the complete measurement chain, including the external analog front-end circuitry, sensors, power supplies, grounding strategy, and any required electrical isolation. Users are responsible for ensuring that the complete system complies with applicable safety standards and institutional requirements for human-subject research. The TinZr Rev 5 has not been tested, certified, or approved for clinical or medical use, and should therefore be used exclusively for research, prototyping, or non-clinical applications unless additional safety certification is obtained.

As with most open-source hardware platforms, the TinZr is provided without warranty, and its use is undertaken at the user’s own risk. The authors disclaim all liability to the extent permitted by applicable law.

Validation and characterization

To validate the operation and characterize the performance of the TinZr Rev 5 platform, we conducted several complementary validation and characterization tests. These tests were designed to evaluate the system across key intended use scenarios, including (a) multi-device communication and coordination, (b) real-time sensor (IMU) data acquisition, (c) simultaneous multi-modal physiological signal acquisition, (d) power consumption and battery life, and (e) wireless communication performance.

Multi-device communication and coordination

Multi-device communication and coordination were validated using the TinZrComCenter framework. In this configuration, multiple TinZr devices simultaneously connect to a central host system (a personal computer) that functions as a communication hub. The firmware supports bi-directional communication over TCP/IP, enabling both TinZr-to-hub and hub-to-TinZr messaging, as well as broadcast commands addressed to all connected TinZr units. This test case demonstrates the TinZr platform’s ability to support coordinated, multi-node operation, enabling use cases such as distributed sensing, coordinated data acquisition, and swarm-style control architectures. Real-time sensor data acquisition

We also demonstrated end-to-end wirelessly-controlled acquisition of inertial data using the TinZrWearableSD configuration. Data were written to a 32 GB FAT32-formatted microSDHC card as comma-separated values (CSV) files in the /TinZrLogs directory. Each CSV file included a metadata header containing the device name, subject identifier, PC start timestamp, sampling interval, target sampling rate, sensor detection status, and column units. The recorded variables included elapsed time, red and infrared PPG measurements from the MAX30102 (when available), and three-axis accelerometer and gyroscope measurements from the onboard IMU. Specifically, the logged columns were t_ms, red_nA, ir_nA, ax_g, ay_g, az_g, gx_dps, gy_dps, and gz_dps. Multi-modal physiological data acquisition

To demonstrate TinZr’s capability to serve as a central hub interfacing with external physiological sensing modules for multi-modal physiological sensing, electrocardiography (ECG) and photoplethysmography (PPG) signals were measured simultaneously. The ECG signal was collected from a custom single-lead ECG front-end based on the MAX30001 [31], while red and infrared PPG signals were collected from the MAX30102 [29], [30] optical sensor. Both external sensing modules interfaced directly with the TinZr platform for data acquisition and wireless transmission. The MAX30102 and MAX30001 were selected as representative examples, and TinZr can also interface with other compatible external physiological sensing modules.

During the recording, TinZr acquired the ECG and dual-wavelength PPG signals concurrently and transmitted the data wirelessly (via BLE) for visualization and storage. Because the sensors operate at different native sampling rates, the data streams were packetized using a common transmission framework in which each packet included timing and sample-counter information. This allowed the received signals to be aligned during post-processing and enabled simultaneous visualization of ECG, red and infrared PPG waveforms.

Fig. 6 shows a representative 10 s recording of simultaneously acquired physiological signals. The blue ECG trace exhibits periodic cardiac cycles with distinguishable QRS complexes, while the red and infrared PPG signals shown as red and black traces, show waveforms corresponding to peripheral blood volume changes. This example showcases the TinZr’s ability as a multi-modal physiological data acquisition platform.

Power consumption and battery life

Power consumption was characterized under representative TinZr operating modes to estimate battery life during standby, wireless communication, and physiological signal acquisition. Current was measured manually using an ammeter connected in series between the TinZr board and a 150 mAh single-cell Li-Po battery. Estimated battery runtime was calculated by dividing the battery capacity (150 mAh) by the measured average current consumption (mA). We characterized the power consumption and estimated runtime for the following modes:

(1)Sleep Idle: In the sleep Idle condition, TinZr remained powered with Wi-Fi, BLE communication, sensor acquisition, and data streaming disabled, while the ESP32-C3 was placed in deep sleep without periodic wakeups. This condition represents the minimum standby current of the device when sensing and wireless communication are inactive.

Wireless communication characterization

Wireless communication performance was characterized to evaluate the practical reliability of TinZr BLE data transmission as an integrated wearable system. Although BLE communication is provided by the ESP32-C3 microcontroller, the practical wireless performance of TinZr also depends on the board layout, antenna placement, enclosure, firmware implementation, receiver hardware, and operating environment. Therefore, the goal of this experiment was not to revalidate the ESP32-C3 wireless specification, but rather to quantify the functional BLE performance of TinZr under representative real-life conditions.

Two BLE characterization tests were performed. The first test evaluated single-device high-rate transmission, typically used for physiological data acquisition from a wearable sensor. The second test evaluated simultaneous multi-device coordination, used in applications where multiple TinZr devices are used together, such as collaborative robotics.

For both tests, TinZr devices continuously transmitted BLE data packets to a laptop receiver running a custom Python application. Each transmitted BLE packet contained a device identifier, packet counter, timestamp, and representative dummy sample frames to emulate continuous data streaming. The receiver logged all received packets and used the packet counter sequence to identify missing data. Packet loss was calculated as the difference between the expected number of packets and the number of packets received, divided by the expected number of packets and expressed as a percentage. The received signal strength indicator (RSSI) was also recorded to provide a practical measure of the signal’s quality. All wireless tests were performed for 180 s, and the reported results represent the average values over the test duration. A detailed description of the two tests is as follows:

(1)In the single-device test, one TinZr device transmitted continuously at 100 Hz; a rate suitable for physiological data acquisition from wearable sensors. The device was placed at distances of <1 ft, 5 ft, 10 ft, 15 ft, and 20 ft from the laptop receiver. Measurements were performed under direct line-of-sight (LOS) conditions and with one-door separation, where the TinZr was positioned behind a wooden door relative to the receiver. This test evaluated whether TinZr could maintain reliable high-rate BLE transmission over practical indoor distances.

The device was placed at distances of <1 ft, 5 ft, 10 ft, 15 ft, and 20 ft from the laptop receiver. (2)In the multi-device test, five TinZr devices transmitted simultaneously to the same laptop receiver at 10 Hz per device using the same BLE packet format used in the first test. The lower per-device rate was selected to represent multi-TinZr coordination scenarios, such as collaborative or swarm robotics, where each TinZr transmits lower-rate state information such as position, motion, or status rather than continuous high-rate physiological waveforms. Multi-device tests were performed under similar distances and conditions.

Across the single-device test, TinZr maintained continuous BLE transmission with no observed packet loss for all distances (from <1 ft to 20 ft) under both direct LOS and one-door conditions. RSSI decreased with increasing distance and obstruction, as expected, but remained sufficient to maintain the BLE connection and data stream throughout the 180 s trials. In the multi-device test, the five TinZr devices were able to stream simultaneously to the laptop receiver, demonstrating practical multi-node operation under the tested conditions.
