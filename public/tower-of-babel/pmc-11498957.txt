Unnecessary diagnostic imaging requested by medical students during a first day of residency simulation: an explorative study
Authors: Julia Gärtner, Lisa Bußenius, Sarah Prediger, Sigrid Harendza
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysicians’ choice of appropriate tests in the diagnostic process is crucial for patient safety. The increased use of medical imaging has raised concerns about its potential overuse. How appropriately medical students order diagnostic tests is unknown. We explored their ordering behavior of diagnostic imaging during a simulated first day of residency.MethodsIn total, 492 undergraduate medical students participated in the simulation. After history taking with simulated patients, the students used an electronic system for requesting diagnostic tests. The analysis focused on 16 patient cases, each managed by at least 50 students. We calculated the total number of ordered images and unnecessary radiation exposure in millisievert per patient and performed one sample t-tests (one tailed) with an expected mean of zero on a Bonferroni-corrected alpha level of 0.003 for the independent variable of unnecessary radiation exposure.ResultsUnnecessary diagnostic imaging was ordered across all patient cases. Ultrasound, especially abdominal ultrasound, X-rays of the thorax, and abdominal CTs were notably overused in 90.9%, 80.0%, and 69.2% of all patient cases, respectively. Unnecessary requests of imaging related to radiation resulted in radiation over-exposure for nearly all patients, with 37.5% of all patients being exposed to a significant radiation overdose on average.ConclusionMedical students’ overuse of diagnostic imaging can be explained by patient-related factors like anxiety and medical factors like missing clinical information leading to cognitive biases in patient workup. This suggests the need for interventions to improve students’ clinical decision-making and reduce cognitive biases. Investigating student-specific factors being associated with overuse of diagnostic imaging would be of additional interest.Keywords: Clinical reasoning, Diagnostic tests, Medical imaging, Patient safety, Radiation, Ultrasound, Undergraduate medical education

Background

Choosing appropriate diagnostic tests plays a crucial role in physicians’ clinical reasoning processes. During patient history-taking and physical examination, hypotheses for differential diagnoses are made and weighted. Diagnostic tests are chosen to increase or decrease the likelihood of a certain diagnosis until enough evidence is collected to make a therapeutic decision [1, 2]. Since the turn of the century, medical imaging procedures like ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have been on the rise as diagnostic tests, generating a financial burden for health care systems [3–5]. The increasing number of imaging also raises the question whether all imaging requests are actually indispensable in the diagnostic reasoning process [6, 7]. As humans are ‘visual creatures’ [8], physicians frequently tend to favor diagnostic images over their diagnostic reasoning judgments [9]. For instance, a notable amount of unnecessary radiographic imaging was identified by retrospective analysis of electronic patient records [10, 11] which can additionally lead to patient harm [12–16].

To minimize patient harm, it is important to apply diagnostic imaging wisely and safely, especially procedures involving radiation [5, 17, 18]. For this aim, it is imperative for medical students to acquire the respective clinical reasoning skills of ordering tests in a hypothesis-driven way [19–21] and important to sensitize them to the appropriate use of diagnostic imaging [22]. There are indications that even advanced undergraduate medical students tend to overuse radiology tests [23]. It is unknown why students ordered unnecessary imaging in this study. One possible reason could be a potential lack of hypothesis building [24, 25]. Other reasons could include the influence of role models, i.e. senior physicians, whom they observed ordering radiological diagnostics ‘for legal reasons’ prompted by the escalating concerns regarding litigation [7], or a certain need for cognitive closure [26]. Therefore, it is crucial to understand medical students’ behavior regarding ordering images in the presence of uncertainty, as it shapes their future approach to patient care and influences patient safety.

To investigate medical students’ conduct of ordering diagnostic imaging is a difficult undertaking because they are not given the opportunity legally to order diagnostic tests themselves during their undergraduate studies. Therefore, we decided to investigate image ordering during a competence-based assessment simulating a first day of residency [27, 28], in which students can participate in a resident’s role with all responsibilities. In this study, we explore advanced undergraduate medical students’ conduct of ordering diagnostic imaging in the context of different patient cases.

Methods

Study design and participants

In total, 492 undergraduate medical students (62.6% female, 37.4% male; average age 26.5 ± 3.9 years) who had participated for the first time in a telemedicine-based simulated first day of residency simulation [29] between May 2020 and March 2023 were included in this study. This simulation training was designed using previously developed competence-based 360°-assessment formats [27, 28] and includes three main phases: (1) a consultation hour with four simulated patients, (2) a management phase where laboratory tests and diagnostic imaging can be ordered via an electronic patient file, and (3) a patient handover with subsequent case discussion. The patient cases were developed based on real cases from the local emergency department. The cases centered around a chief complaint and were conceptualized to be solved by analytical thinking rather than by pattern recognition alone [30]. Furthermore, all patients had characteristic personal states, e.g., acting in a worried or taciturn manner. The roles were portrayed by trained professional actors and actresses. A total of 36 different patient cases have been used in various combinations over the years. Diagnostic imaging requests from 16 patients who were at least seen by 50 students were included in the study. The patients’ characteristics are described in Table 1. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Chamber of Physicians, Hamburg (reference number: PV3649). Students’ participation in the simulation was voluntary on a first-come, first served basis after invitation by SH. All participants gave their written consent. Data were digitally collected by the study coordinator SP. Processing of diagnostic imaging requests was performed by JG and discussed with SH. All data were consolidated and analyzed by LB and anonymized for publication.

Patient cases

Electronic file system for diagnostic requests

During the management phase, participants were given the opportunity to request diagnostic tests, e.g. imaging procedures, if they felt this was necessary for the respective patient’s diagnostic workup. We implemented an electronic file system in LimeSurvey version 5.6.31 (LimeSurvey GmbH, Hamburg, Germany) comparable to widely used electronic medical record software such as Epic (Epic Systems Corporation, Verona, WI, USA) or Soarian (Siemens AG, Erlangen, Germany). The students could select the type of imaging they wished to order for the specific patient. The selection included non-radioactive imaging procedures like ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as well as imaging based on radiation like X-ray, computed tomography (CT), angiography, and scintigraphy. Additionally, students could indicate the focal body area they wished to investigate and specify their diagnostic question in an open text box.

Data collection and processing

Individual imaging requests for each patient and medical student were screened and every imaging request was counted in the respective category (Ultrasound, MRI, X-ray, CT, Angiography, Scintigraphy). When no specified body parts were mentioned, the specific imaging types were either derived from the diagnostic question or, when that was missing as well, from the patient’s symptom (e. g., a CT request for a patient with severe abdominal pain was counted as abdominal CT). Notes that implied a sequential order of image requests (e. g. tagged by ‘if necessary’ and ‘possibly’) were quantified as follows: If these requests contained an explicit ‘if, then’ logic or were clearly understandable in the context of the respective patient case (“if ultrasound is inconclusive, run abdominal CT”), it was assessed by SH, an internist, whether the respective imaging would or would not have occurred and counted accordingly.

To identify requested imaging as unnecessary, we determined the indicated imaging orders for each patient based on expert discussion of the real case in the emergency room attending round with SH and the eventual patient outcome based on the discharge letters, which were reassessed by SH. Indicated imaging was defined as indispensable for making a diagnosis. All imaging orders deviating from that definition for each patient were counted as unnecessary in their respective category. To analyze unnecessary radiation exposure per patient, we multiplied the X-rays, CT scans and angiographs by their average effective dose in millisievert (mSv) as catalogued by Mettler et al. [31]. Due to lack of reference data on mSv, we excluded unnecessary scintigraphy orders in this calculation. The ordered mSv doses were summed up for a new variable of total radiation for a specific patient caused by each participant. If an imaging order was classified as necessary (if ordered by the participant for a specific patient), we subtracted the respective corresponding dose from the total radiation variable to determine unnecessary exposure.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 29 (SPSS Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) on a general alpha level of 0.05. We calculated the total number of ordered images (including the proportion of necessary and unnecessary orders) and unnecessary radiation exposure in mSv per patient. We also performed one sample t-tests (one tailed) with an expected mean of zero on a Bonferroni-corrected alpha level of 0.003 for the independent variable of unnecessary radiation exposure. We calculated descriptive statistics of the outcome variables of total number of ordered images. In a next step, we analyzed ultrasound and its subcategories, representing the most common and readily available non-radiographic test available in emergency departments. For diagnostic imaging with radiation exposure, we included X-ray and CT and their subcategories, representing the most common examinations. We further included angiography as a rare imaging procedure with very high radiation exposure.

Results

In all patient cases, unnecessary diagnostic imaging was ordered by the students. The number of unnecessary orders ranged on average from 0.12 ± 0.33 (patient 15) to 1.70 ± 0.89 (patient 12). The lowest range of unnecessary orders (0–1) occurred in patient 15, the highest (0–7) in patient 6. Requests for unnecessary ultrasounds are shown in Table 2. Except for two patients, 10 and 15, unnecessary ultrasound was ordered for all other patients (87.5%). With respect to body regions, abdominal ultrasound was most frequently ordered unnecessarily in 10 of 11 patients (90.9%). For patient 2 almost all students (92%) ordered an unnecessary abdominal ultrasound. Requests for unnecessary imaging involving radiation, i.e. X-rays, CTs, and angiographies, are also shown in Table 2. Expect for patient 7 and 15, all other patients (87.5%) received unnecessary images in either of the three categories. Unnecessary X-rays of the thorax were requested in 8 out of 10 patients (80%). With regard to CTs, 9 out of 13 patients (69.2%) received an abdominal CT even though it was not needed in the diagnostic process. For patient 6, with unnecessary requests for CTs and X-rays of joints and spine, the results show a more diverse distribution of students’ orders.

Discussion

Overall, medical students requested unnecessary diagnostic imaging for all patients, but to different extents. The lowest average number of unnecessary images occurred in patient 15 (left-sided lower abdominal pain), presumably because her symptoms triggered very focused and hypothesis-based diagnostics based on an illness script [32] for diverticulitis. Patient 12 (right-sided chest pain), who behaved in a reproachful and complacent way, and patient 11 (left-sided chest pain), who was grumpy mistrusted doctors, received the two highest average numbers of unnecessary images. This could be due to the challenging personal states of both patients triggering fear of missing out on diagnostics which could have led to the better-safe-than-sorry bias or the extension bias (more is better than little) [33]. These biases particularly occur in handling medical technology [33] and can be a sign of defensive medicine [34].

With respect to types of imaging procedures, abdominal ultrasound was the procedure most frequently ordered unnecessarily in our study. In emergency medicine, abdominal ultrasound in particular has become a very accessible, quick and uncomplicated technique that can be performed immediately, which lowers the threshold for its use to “just have a look” [35]. Perhaps students’ overuse of abdominal ultrasound in our study is due to its readily availability as point-of-care ultrasound and its increased teaching in undergraduate medical education [36–38]. However, ultrasound being readily available should not make students forget the diagnostic rationale to order it only in case of a distinct question what to look for and why [39]. The unnecessary abdominal ultrasound requests for patient 9 (malaise and high blood sugar) and patient 16 (palpitations and muscle weakness) by more than half of the students, respectively, do not seem to be based on hypotheses as it remains unclear what exactly is to be examined. Furthermore, almost all students requested an ultrasound for patient 2 (very severe abdominal pain), apparently ignoring the documentation that the patient did not tolerate physical examination of his abdomen by palpation due to his pain. Hence, the unnecessary ordering of abdominal ultrasound in patient 2 could have been caused by students’ inattentional blindness [40] due to a seemingly overwhelming amount of information from the history and physical examination. On the other hand, it could also mean that the students treated the patient in the simulation not according to his condition, but – as soon as it came to ordering diagnostics – like a paper case [41, 42], where abdominal ultrasound in theory would have been a suitable next diagnostic step.

As far as radiology imaging was concerned, unnecessary CT scans of the abdomen as well as unnecessary chest X-rays were ordered most frequently, showing an overutilization of radiology imaging as observed in physicians [43, 44]. Most frequently, an unnecessary abdominal CT was ordered for patient 10 (painless jaundice). Presumably, this pattern for pancreatic carcinoma was so overwhelmingly present in the students’ minds that they were not able to include the disconfirming evidence for this diagnosis from the laboratory results in their analytical thinking. Thus, many seem to have succumbed to the bias of premature closure, one of the two heuristic errors observed most frequently in medical students [45]. Patient 6 (severe neck and shoulder pain) received the highest number of unnecessary chest X-rays, including unnecessary radiology imaging for joints and spine. This could either have been caused by the patient’s personal pragmatic and tough state, framing him as an athletic person who was presumed to have an orthopedic problem [46]. Furthermore, ordering a chest X-ray could also be a sign of confirmation bias after incorrect framing, of not having elicited enough information from the patient’s history to make an informed judgement (i.e. unpacking principle) due to the patient’s restrained personal state, or of anchoring bias, when a student stuck to the initial hypothesis of an orthopedic problem despite having elicited contradictory information form the patient’s history [33, 47, 48].

On average, all patients except for patient 16 received unnecessary radiation, which even reached significant levels in six patients. In three of these patients, patient 3 (unwanted weight loss), patient 4 (fatigue), and patient 10 (painless jaundice), a high percentage of the students who ordered unnecessary radiation imaging would even have exposed the patients to up to 10 mSv, in some cases even more. For comparison, according to the International Commission of Radiological Protection, exposure to cosmic radiation for the general public is about 1 mSv/year and the recommended effective dose limit for radiation workers or aircrew is 20 mSv/year [49]. The students’ fear of missing cancer, triggered by these patients, could have led to more cognitive effort for diagnostic outcome in students with higher need for cognitive closure in the form of using more diagnostic imaging to look for possible cancer [26]. In this context, ordering more imaging diagnostics without taking the high radiation exposure into account could be due to the better safe than sorry bias or to aversion to ambiguity [33]. Patient 3 mentioned her fear of her symptoms being possibly related to cancer during history taking, patient 4 suffered from unexplained fatigue even though he seemed to lead a healthy lifestyle, and patient 10 presented a symptom that strongly triggers an illness script for cancer as described above. It has been shown that unrecognized feelings such as fear of cancer led in medical students to unfavorable history taking behavior [50]. Hence, we hypothesize that such a ‘countertransference’ [50] could also have an unwelcome impact on diagnostic decision making which can even lead to patient harm.

Our study has several limitations. For our simulation, we established a pool of 36 different patient cases. However, for this analysis we were only able to include 16 cases that had been explored by a sufficient number of students. Furthermore, due to technical reasons, the imaging requests could not be made sequentially by the students, e.g. ordering a CT-scan after having received the results of an abdominal ultrasound. In the ordering request form for imaging, many students did not use the box to provide a question to the examiner of the imaging. Therefore, we were not able to include this aspect of explicit reasoning in our analysis. A strength of our study is the high number of students, at least 50 per case, that could be included in our investigation, providing a solid data basis. Another strength is using a simulation instead of paper cases to provide a realistic work situation for the students to order diagnostic imaging after having taken a patient’s history including a realistic digital patient record. Using simulated patients based on real patient cases provided the opportunity to equip the patients with a personal state as possible source of interference with patient workup. It also enabled us to match students’ patient workup with the original workup of experts in the emergency room situation.

Overall, the main reasons for imaging overuse in our simulation could be attributed to patient-related factors such as anxiety or expectations and medical factors such as missing clinical information or specific patients’ symptoms, which led to cognitive biases in patient workup. This entails the need for curricular interventions to reduce overuse of imaging in medical students by improving clinical decision-making and reducing cognitive biases such as confirmation bias or premature closure. The latter is one of the most commonly occurring cognitive biases in medical practice [51] and can lead to incorrect treatment if, for example, a case is closed based on imaging that seems to fit the suspected initial diagnosis. Incorporating case-based learning and collaborative discussions in clinical reasoning courses can support students to improve their diagnostics, case presentations and make them aware of cognitive biases [52]. Whether students learn to use imaging in a more focused way, discuss a broader spectrum of differential diagnoses, and will be less prone to cognitive biases could be assessed in simulations with controlled conditions such as the one used in this study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study revealed that a relevant number of students ordered unnecessary diagnostic imaging in a first day of residency simulation, which would have led to increased costs in the health system, undue use of staff, and in some cases even patient harm. Curricular interventions supporting clinical reasoning, case discussions and the use of focused diagnostic imaging are suggested to tackle this problem. As we found that the way clinical decisions were made by the students could be influenced by factors like case complexity and patients’ personal state, it would be of particular interest to investigate whether additional student-specific factors such as uncertainty or lack of knowledge are associated with inappropriate imaging requests. Understanding the interplay between patient case characteristics and possible individual student characteristics leading to unnecessary imaging requests could provide valuable insights for refining medical education strategies to optimize decision-making processes in clinical settings.
