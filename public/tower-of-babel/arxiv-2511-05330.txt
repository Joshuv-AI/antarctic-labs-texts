Learning Dynamics from Input-Output Data with Hamiltonian Gaussian Processes
Jan-Hendrik Ewering, Robin E. Herrmann, Niklas Wahlström, Thomas B. Schön, Thomas Seel

Abstract
Embedding non-restrictive prior knowledge, such as energy conservation laws, in learning-based approaches is a key motive to construct physically consistent models from limited data, relevant for, e. g., model-based control. Recent work incorporates Hamiltonian dynamics into gp ( gp ) regression to obtain uncertainty-quantifying models that adhere to the underlying physical principles. However, these works rely on velocity or momentum data, which is rarely available in practice. In this paper, we consider dynamics learning with non-conservative Hamiltonian gp , and address the more realistic problem setting of learning from input-output data. We provide a fully Bayesian scheme for estimating probability densities of unknown hidden states, of gp hyperparameters, as well as of structural hyperparameters, such as damping coefficients. Considering the computational complexity of gp , we take advantage of a reduced-rank gp approximation and leverage its properties for computationally efficient prediction and training. The proposed method is evaluated in a nonlinear simulation case study and compared to a state-of-the-art approach that relies on momentum measurements.

Introduction

Learning the dynamics of a system is a key approach for enabling high-performance model-based control methods and system insight, even when limited prior model knowledge is available.
Recent physics-informed machine learning approaches provide well-generalizing and data-efficient learning-based models by incorporating non-restrictive prior knowledge
(
12
)
.
Notable examples include embedding energy conservation laws into neural networks
(
8
)
or
gp

(

to learn models that yield physically interpretable predictions.
In this context, choosing a
gp

First, recent work has shown that kernel-based methods, e. g.,

(
34
;

.
Second,
gp

(

.

In particular, fusing Hamiltonian inductive biases into
gp

However, to the best of our knowledge, all existing work relies on measurements of the entire system state, meaning that, e. g., beyond position measurements, momentum or velocity data is required for training, which is a restrictive assumption in many practical settings.

In this article, we consider learning of non-conservative dynamics with Hamiltonian
gp
s. In contrast to previous research—which relies on measurements of momenta or velocities—we address the more realistic problem setting of learning from input-output data only.
We propose a novel Hamiltonian
gp
model, which is linear in its parameters, features simple closed-form gradient expressions, and exhibits computational complexity independent of the training data dimension, thereby improving its applicability in practical settings.
For learning the model, we present a fully Bayesian system identification scheme that estimates probability densities of unknown latent states, of
gp
hyperparameters, as well as of structural hyperparameters, such as damping coefficients.

Related Work

Integrating continuous-time dynamics into learning-based models has attracted significant attention over the last decade, with early work focusing on learning a vector-valued flow map that determines the evolution of a system’s state, e. g., using neural ordinary differential equations

Instead, a current approach is to compute this flow map from an approximation of the system’s Hamiltonian or Lagrangian function, which reflects the exchange of energy within a system.
In these approaches, a learning-based model, e. g., a neural network

Considering Hamiltonian-based

Although this represents a significant step toward practical problem settings, all existing work on Hamiltonian

Moreover, all previous research on Hamiltonian

Problem Formulation

We are concerned with learning physically consistent state-space models from input-output data.
To this end, we consider systems whose dynamics can be described by forced Hamiltonian mechanics.
Building on classical mechanics, this formulation provides a modeling paradigm that allows for describing energy storage and dissipation within a system, as well as energy exchange across systems, in a consistent and interpretable fashion.

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{e}\mid\boldsymbol{0},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{e})\,,

Reduced-Rank Hamiltonian Gaussian Processes

To construct a physically consistent, uncertainty-quantifying system model, the Hamiltonian can be represented using a zero-mean
gp
(
gp
), i. e.,
{H}(\boldsymbol{x})\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}))
, with covariance (kernel) function
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

(
22
)
, as proposed by
2
.
Importantly, not the Hamiltonian function itself, but its gradient is needed for prediction within the model structure (
1
).
Therefore, exploiting that
gp
are closed under linear operations, we model the gradient of the Hamiltonian as

\nabla_{\!\boldsymbol{x}}{H}(\boldsymbol{x})\sim\mathcal{GP}(\boldsymbol{0},\nabla_{\!\boldsymbol{x}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}))\,.

(2)

However, a common drawback of
gp
is that their computational complexity and memory requirements scale poorly with the number of training data points, which becomes even more severe in (
2
) because the partial derivative induces a multi-output
gp
.
Instead, for efficient learning in practical settings, it is desirable that a model features
(i)
a computational complexity independent of the training data dimension,
(ii)
a linear representation to facilitate closed-form learning, and
(iii)
access to computationally simple gradient expressions.

Luckily, the reduced-rank
gp
presented by
29
exhibits all of these properties, and we take advantage of this approach in the following.
Loosely speaking, the reduced-rank
gp

(
29
)
approximates the kernel with a finite-dimensional eigenfunction expansion that encodes the kernel’s spectral density
S
in the frequency domain.
The chosen covariance function is described by

k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\approx\sum_{k=1}^{M}S(\sqrt{\varrho_{k}})\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),

(3)

where
\phi_{k}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are eigenfunctions of the Laplace operator on the domain
\Omega=[-L_{1},L_{1}]\times\dots\times[-L_{n_{x}},L_{n_{x}}]
, and
\varrho_{k}
are the corresponding eigenvalues.
For this rectangular domain, the eigenfunctions have a closed form, that is

\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{n_{x}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{i}}}\sin\left(\frac{\pi j_{k,i}\left(x_{i}+L_{i}\right)}{2L_{i}}\right)\,,\qquad\varrho_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{x}}\left(\frac{\pi j_{k,i}}{2L_{i}}\right)^{2}\,,

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{x}=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{x}}]^{\top}
, and the indices
j_{k,i}
determine the frequency of the corresponding eigenfunction
(
24
)
.
Predictions of
{H}(\boldsymbol{x})\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}))
can be performed with the reduced-rank
gp
at computational complexity
\mathcal{O}(M)
using the simple basis function expansion

\widehat{H}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{M}a_{k}\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\,,

(5)

where the vector-valued function
\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})=[\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}),\ldots,\phi_{M}(\boldsymbol{x})]^{\top}
, and the weights
\boldsymbol{a}=[a_{1},\ldots,a_{M}]^{\top}
follow a distribution
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{V})
, with
\boldsymbol{V}=\mathrm{diag}(S(\sqrt{\varrho_{1}}),\ldots,S(\sqrt{\varrho_{M}}))
.
Please note that we use the superscript
\widehat{\square}
to describe the learned counterparts of the quantities in (
1

The reduced-rank
gp
converges to the exact
gp
in the limit
M,L_{1},\ldots,L_{n_{x}}\rightarrow\infty

(
29

Given this, we model the Hamiltonian gradient
\nabla_{\!\boldsymbol{x}}{H}
by taking the partial derivative of (
5

\displaystyle\nabla_{\!\boldsymbol{x}}\widehat{H}(\boldsymbol{x})=\nabla_{\!\boldsymbol{x}}\left(\boldsymbol{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)=\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{a}\,,

(6)

\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{x})

Please note, this model is still linear in its parameters
\boldsymbol{a}

To estimate the current Hamiltonian gradient (and using it for parameter learning) at a given sample time

\boldsymbol{h}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{h}_{t}=\left.\nabla_{\!\boldsymbol{x}}\widehat{H}(\boldsymbol{x})\right|_{\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{t}}+\boldsymbol{v}_{t}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{v}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\sigma^{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{I}})\,.

p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T},\boldsymbol{\vartheta})\propto p(\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{K}})\,,

(11)

using Bayes’ rule.
Considering individual time steps,
we can write the likelihood as

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})

\displaystyle=p(\boldsymbol{z}_{0})\cdot\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})\propto\prod_{t=0}^{T}p(\boldsymbol{h}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{\theta})\,,

(12)

where the proportionality is regarding the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
The factors
p(\boldsymbol{h}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{\theta})
are normal distributions, defined by (
7
), and the overall likelihood is, thus, a normal distribution

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=0}^{T}{b}_{t}\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}_{i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})-\Tr\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\boldsymbol{r}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\right)\right),

(13)

expressed in the canonical form of the restricted exponential family with the natural parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}=\boldsymbol{a}/{\sigma}^{2}
and
\alpha_{2}=-1/(2{\sigma}^{2})
, respectively, log-partition functions
\boldsymbol{P}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
, as well as (sufficient) statistics
\boldsymbol{s}_{i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})
and
\boldsymbol{r}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
.
Please note that
\Tr(\cdot)
is the trace operator, and that the base measures

contain the
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-independent state dynamics
p(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})

Conveniently, the chosen

prior with distribution parameters
\boldsymbol{\eta}=\{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{V},{\psi},\nu\}

={n}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\eta})-\Tr\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right)\right)\,,

, and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}^{+}

5.2
Hyperparameter Learning: Metropolis-within-Gibbs

For the hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}=\{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{K}},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}}\}
, similar closed-form results are not avialable.
Therefore, we employ Metropolis-within-Gibbs steps for learning, and, in each iteration
k
, generate a hyperparameter sample
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{*}
from a proposal distribution
\zeta(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{*}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}[k])
, e. g., a random walk.
The proposals are accepted with probability
\min\left(1,\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{*}|\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T})}{p(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}[k]|\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T})}\frac{\zeta(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}[k]|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{*})}{\zeta(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{*}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}[k])}\right)
, and rejected otherwise, i. e.,
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}[k+1]=\boldsymbol{\vartheta}[k]
. To evaluate
p(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{*}|\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T})\propto p(\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{*})p(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{*})
, we use the hyperparameter prior
p(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{*})
and compute the likelihood
p(\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}^{*})
for the kernel and structural hyperparameters individually.

Kernel Hyperparameters

For the likelihood of the
gp
kernel hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{K}}
, we have

p(\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{K}}^{*})=\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{K}}^{*})p(\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{K}}^{*})}{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{K}}^{*})}=\frac{n(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*})\prod_{t=0}^{T}b_{t}}{n(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{+*})}\,,

(17)

using Bayes’ rule, and we note that all terms are known in closed form.
The numerator and the denominator in (
17
) are proportional to each other.
In fact, both follow a
\mathcal{NIG}
distribution, and the
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-related components cancel.
Therefore, the likelihood for the kernel hyperparameters is a quotient of normalizing constants, and the first term of the acceptance probability can be computed as

\displaystyle\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{K}}^{*}|\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T})}{p(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{K}}[k]|\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T})}

\displaystyle=\frac{p(\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{K}}^{*})p(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{K}}^{*})}{p(\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{K}}[k])p(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{K}}[k])}=\frac{n(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*})p(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{K}}^{*})}{n(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{+*})}\frac{n(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{+}[k])}{n(\boldsymbol{\eta}[k])p(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{K}}[k])}\,,

(18)

where the base measure products
\prod_{t=0}^{T}b_{t}
in the numerator and the denominator cancel.

Structural Hyperparameters

For the structural hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}}
, the likelihood can be constructed by integrating out the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
from
p(\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})
and noting that the result is proportional regarding
\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}}
to the Gaussian density
p(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}\mid\boldsymbol{h}_{0:T-1},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})
, that is

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})

\displaystyle=\int\left[p(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\cdot\prod_{t=0}^{T}p(\boldsymbol{h}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{\theta})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right]\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}

(19)

\displaystyle\propto p(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\cdot\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})=p(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}\mid\boldsymbol{h}_{0:T-1},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})\,.

Thus, we compute the first quotient of the acceptance probability as

\displaystyle\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}}^{*}|\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T})}{p(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}}[k]|\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T})}

\displaystyle=\frac{p(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}\mid\boldsymbol{h}_{0:T-1},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}^{*}})p(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}}^{*})}{p(\boldsymbol{x}_{0:T}\mid\boldsymbol{h}_{0:T-1},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}}[k])p(\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}}[k])}\,.

(20)

where the normalizing constants cancel.

7
Conclusion

In this article, we present a method for learning a physically consistent dynamics model using non-conservative Hamiltonian
gp
.
In contrast to existing work, which relies on measurements of momenta or velocities, we consider a more realistic problem setting where learning is based solely on available input-output data.
The proposed reduced-rank Hamiltonian
gp
is linear in its parameters, and comes with closed-form gradient expressions.
While we exploit these properties for fully Bayesian learning of
(hyper-)
parameters, we emphasize that the model structure can also speed up training and prediction
significantly
in frequentist settings. Despite its advantages, we acknowledge that the computational complexity of the reduced-rank
gp
increases rapidly with the regression input dimension
(
24
)
, which future work should consider.

Taking a step back, we have two perspectives on our approach.
First, we contribute a physics-informed tool for dynamics learning from input-output data, extending
2
to a more practical setting.
On the other hand, the method can be interpreted as a system identification scheme, ensuring physical consistency in Bayesian inference and learning.
Using the proposed method, an uncertainty-quantifying and physically consistent nonlinear system model can be learned solely from input-output data, which has the potential to enable model-based control and insights in complex application systems without extensive prior knowledge.

A
Proposed Model in the Canonical Form of the Restricted Exponential Family

To derive the distributions employed in Section
5
, we express all densities in the canonical form of the restricted exponential family.
This is convenient for Bayesian inference in state-space models, as probabilistic dependence on the previous state can be incorporated
(
33
)
.

Formulation

The proposed model (
10
) can be written in the canonical form of the restricted exponential family as

p(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=Z\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}-\Tr\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}){\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}\right)\right)\,,

(21)

where the density is expressed in terms of the
natural parameters

\boldsymbol{\alpha}
that are obtained by transforming the original model parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\boldsymbol{a},\sigma^{2}\}
.
We have
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=[\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}^{\top},\alpha_{2}]^{\top}
with

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}=\frac{\boldsymbol{a}}{{\sigma}^{2}}\,,\qquad\alpha_{2}=-\frac{1}{2{\sigma}^{2}}\,,

(22)

and the parameter-dependent parts of the log-partition function being

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}{\alpha}_{2}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}^{\top}=\frac{1}{2{\sigma}^{2}}\boldsymbol{a}\boldsymbol{a}^{\top}\,,\qquad{P}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=-\frac{1}{2}\log|-2{\alpha}_{2}|=\frac{1}{2}\log{\sigma}^{2}\,,

(23)

where we denote
|\cdot|
as the determinant.
The remaining variables,
Z
,
\boldsymbol{s}_{i}
, and
\boldsymbol{r}_{i}
—depending on the employed prior and the data—are a normalization constant and statistics, respectively.

Likelihood

To express the likelihood

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T}|\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})

\displaystyle=p(\boldsymbol{z}_{0})\cdot\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})

(24)

\displaystyle=p(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})p(\boldsymbol{h}_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{\theta})\cdot\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})\,,

in the form (
21
), we assume, for simplicity, that
p(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
is known and consider each time step separately.
For individual transitions
p(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})
we have

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})

\displaystyle=p(\boldsymbol{h}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t},\boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})

(25)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{h}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\boldsymbol{a},{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{I})\,\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{f}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{u}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}}),\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{w})\,,

were the function
\boldsymbol{f}
and the noise covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{w}
describe the discrete-time state dynamics, and can be obtained from integrating (
10
).
Noting that only the first density is
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-dependent, the likelihood for one time step can be expressed as

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}})=\tilde{b}_{t}\cdot\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{h}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\boldsymbol{a},{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{I})

(26)

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{b}_{t}}{(2\pi)^{n_{x}/2}({\sigma^{2}})^{n_{x}/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2{\sigma}^{2}}(\boldsymbol{h}_{t}-\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\boldsymbol{a})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{h}_{t}-\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\boldsymbol{a})\right)

\displaystyle={b}_{t}\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}_{i}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})-\Tr\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\boldsymbol{r}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\right)\right)\,,

with the statistics

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{h}_{t}\,,\qquad

\displaystyle{s}_{2}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{h}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{h}_{t}\,,

(27)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{J}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right)\,,\qquad

\displaystyle r_{2}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})

\displaystyle=n_{x}\,,

and the base measures

{b}_{t}=\begin{cases}{(2\pi)^{-n_{x}/2}}\cdot p(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\,,&\text{if }t=0\,,\\
{(2\pi)^{-n_{x}/2}}\cdot\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{f}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{h}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{u}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{\vartheta}_{\mathrm{S}}),\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{w})\,,&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(28)

The overall likelihood for data
\boldsymbol{z}_{0:T}
is constructed by multiplying the one-step densities, which amounts to summing the corresponding trajectory statistics (
27
).

Prior and Posterior

To express the multivariate normal inverse Gamma (
\mathcal{NIG}
)
(
20
)
prior and posterior densities in the form (
21
), we first define the

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{a}\mid\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{V},{\sigma^{2}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{a}\mid\boldsymbol{m},{\sigma^{2}}\boldsymbol{V})

(29)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{M/2}({\sigma^{2}})^{M/2}|\boldsymbol{V}|^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2{\sigma}^{2}}(\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{m})^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{m})\right)\,,

\displaystyle={n}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\eta})-\Tr\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right)\right)\,,

where the statistics, dependent on the distribution parameters

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{V}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\tilde{{s}}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\eta})

\displaystyle=\psi+\boldsymbol{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}^{-1}\boldsymbol{m}\,,

(32)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{r}}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{V}^{-1}\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\eta})

\displaystyle=\nu+2+M\,,

and the normalizing factor

{n}(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\frac{({\psi}/2)^{\nu/2}}{(2\pi)^{M/2}|\boldsymbol{V}|^{1/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)}\,.

(33)

with the new distribution parameters

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}^{+}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{1}^{+}\right)^{-1}\,,

\displaystyle{\psi}^{+}

\displaystyle={s}_{2}^{+}-\boldsymbol{s}_{1}^{+\top}\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{1}^{+}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{s}_{1}^{+}\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\nu^{+}

\displaystyle=r_{2}^{+}\,.

Additional Simulation Results

Due to identifiability issues in the input-output setting, we impose an anti-symmetry constraint on the Hamiltonian
gp
model (
5
).
To this end, an advantage of the employed reduced-rank
gp
is that prior knowledge about the target function, such as symmetry, can be easily encoded in the basis function expansion
(
32
)
.
To impose the anti-symmetry constraint, we follow the lines of
3
and use only anti-symmetric basis functions in (
4
) by selecting the indices
j_{k,i}=2,4,\ldots
when constructing the reduced-rank
gp
.
This ensures that the approximated Hamiltonian satisfies
\widehat{H}(\boldsymbol{x})=-\widehat{H}(-\boldsymbol{x})
, which induces the symmetry
\nabla_{\!\boldsymbol{x}}\widehat{H}(\boldsymbol{x})=\nabla_{\!\boldsymbol{x}}\widehat{H}(-\boldsymbol{x})
for its gradient.
In Figure
5
, we compare the proposed method in three different settings, i. e., learning with

•

state measurements
\{\boldsymbol{y}^{(\mathrm{i/s})}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
, without anti-symmetry constraint (left column),

•

output measurements
\{y^{(\mathrm{i/o})}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
, with anti-symmetry constraint (middle column), and

•

output measurements
\{y^{(\mathrm{i/o})}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
, without anti-symmetry constraint (right column).

All approaches correctly learn the equilibrium positions along the
q
-dimension. In the input-output setting, i. e., without measurements of the momentum
p
available, learning along the
p
-dimension can result in ambiguous solutions, which we attribute to identifiability problems.
In the present nonlinear simulation example, the anti-symmetry constraint can facilitate learning from input-output data.
