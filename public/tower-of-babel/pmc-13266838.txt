Sex modifies the association between blood pressure variability and lung function decline among population over 45 years old: a pooled analysis of two national cohorts
Authors: Jing Fu, Longfei Xie, Jing Zhao, Rui Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe evidence linking blood pressure (BP) variability (BPV) to longitudinal lung function decline remains unclear. This cohort study aims to reveal the association between BPV and lung function decline among population over 45 years old.MethodsParticipants were from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Lung function was assessed by peak expiratory flow (PEF), and was standardized using Z-score transformation. Variation independent of mean (VIM) was mainly used to assess BPV. A linear mixed model was conducted. Subgroup analyses across age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and hypertension status were employed. A meta-analysis was performed to pool results, with heterogeneity reported.ResultsA total of 6,930 and 5,119 eligible participants from HRS and CHARLS were included, respectively. With increment of VIM, participants’ PEF decreased. Pooled analysis revealed that increased BPV was significantly associated with faster lung function decline [β for systolic BP: −0.224, 95% confidence interval (CI): −0.323 to −0.126; β for diastolic BP: −0.08, 95% CI: −0.011 to −0.005]. Compared with participants in the lowest tertile of VIM, those in the middle and highest tertiles showed greater declines. Subgroup analysis demonstrated that sex modified this association, with a more pronounced effect observed in males (pooled β for systolic BP: −0.347, 95% CI: −0.669 to −0.025; pooled β for diastolic BP: −0.012, 95% CI: −0.019 to −0.005).ConclusionsBPV is significantly associated with lung function decline in middle-aged and older adults, and this association is more pronounced in males. More early intervention for individuals with higher BPV is needed, particularly for males.Keywords: Blood pressure variability (BPV), lung function decline, pooled analysis, modification effect

Highlight box

Key findings

• This pooled analysis found that blood pressure variability (BPV) is significantly associated with lung function decline in middle-aged and older adults, and this association is more pronounced in males.

What is known and what is new?

• BPV may adversely affect lung function through shared pathways. However, evidence linking BPV to longitudinal lung function decline remains unclear.

• This study therefore investigates whether elevated BPV is an independent risk factor for accelerated peak expiratory flow decline, and identifies high-risk population, using pooled data from two national cohorts.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Our results provide further insights into the impact of BPV on adverse health outcomes and emphasize the importance of early intervention for individuals with higher BPV, particularly for males.

Introduction

Blood pressure (BP) variability (BPV) typically refers to the fluctuations in an individual’s BP over periods of weeks, months, or years (1). Compared to average BP, BPV can more accurately reflect the stability of the intrinsic regulatory function of the cardiovascular system, as its changes are closely associated with sympathetic nervous system activation and the impairment of sensory reflexes (2). Numerous studies have shown that elevated BPV is significantly associated with adverse health outcomes, including cardiovascular events such as coronary artery disease, stroke, and heart failure, as well as cardiovascular mortality, cognitive decline, and chronic kidney disease (3-8).

Peak expiratory flow (PEF) refers to the maximum flow rate achieved during forced expiration when the lungs are maximally inflated. It serves as an important indicator of pulmonary function and respiratory health, particularly in the elderly population (9,10). The measurement of PEF is simple, convenient, and cost-effective, which has led to its widespread application in clinical practice and epidemiological research (11). Although pulmonary function naturally declines with advancing age (12), an accelerated decline in this function is an early signal of various diseases and is closely associated with increased risks of frailty, mortality, and falls (11,13,14). Therefore, exploring the systemic risk factors that influence the accelerated decline in pulmonary function is of significant importance for improving health outcomes in the elderly population.

The cardiovascular and respiratory systems are physiologically interconnected, sharing a common circulatory foundation (15). Therefore, BPV may exert adverse effects on the respiratory system through shared pathophysiological pathways. However, there is currently a lack of evidence from large populations directly elucidating the association between BPV and the longitudinal decline in pulmonary function. This study aims to investigate, through multi-cohort pooled analysis, whether elevated BPV is an independent risk factor for accelerated lung function decline. The findings are expected to provide new epidemiological evidence for a deeper understanding of cardiopulmonary interaction mechanisms and may help identify high-risk populations in whom lung function could be preserved by improving BP stability. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0310/rc).

Methods

Study population

The study population of this research is derived from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The HRS cohort is a nationally representative longitudinal survey in the United States, which investigates more than 37,000 individuals aged 50 and above from 23,000 households. It aims to study changes in health status, labor force participation, and demographic shifts among older adults. The data encompass health indicators, economic status, biomarkers, and other aspects, holding significant importance for public health research. The HRS initiated in 1992, follow-ups are conducted biennially (16). The CHARLS cohort is one of the many sister studies of HRS and is also a nationally representative cohort that longitudinally tracks the social, economic, and health conditions of individuals aged 45 years and above and their spouses in China (17). The baseline survey of CHARLS was conducted in 2011, covering over 17,000 respondents, with follow-ups carried out every 2–3 years. The HRS and CHARLS surveys were approved by the ethics review committees of the University of Michigan (approval No. HUM00061128) and Peking University (approval No. IRB00001052-11015). All participants in both cohorts provided informed consent before being surveyed. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

This cohort study included participants from the HRS cohort across waves 2006–2018 and from the CHARLS cohort across waves 2011–2015, with the requirement that all individuals had at least three recorded BP measurements. The exclusion criteria for participants in both cohorts were as follows: (I) less than three times of follow-up measurements of BP and lung function; (II) baseline age younger 45 years; (III) diagnosed with chronic lung diseases such as asthma, chronic bronchitis, or emphysema at baseline; (IV) diagnosed with cardiovascular disease at baseline; (V) with missing on baseline covariates. Ultimately, there were 6,930 eligible participants from the HRS cohort and 5,119 from the CHARLS cohort being included in the formal analysis. Lung function and its decline

Participants’ lung function was assessed using PEF. PEF was measured using a peak flow meter in units of liters per minute (L/min). During the measurement, participants stood upright, took a deep breath, held their breath, and fully sealed the mouthpiece with their lips to prevent air leakage before exhaling as hard and fast as possible. This process was repeated three times, and the maximum value from the three measurements was used for the main analysis (10).

To account for interindividual physiological differences in baseline lung function and to ensure comparability across follow-up time points, PEF values were standardized using Z-score transformation (18). Specifically, the mean and standard deviation (SD) of PEF at the baseline wave (first measurement) of each cohort were treated as a reference, all follow-up PEF measurements were converted to Z-scores using the formula: Z-PEF = (raw PEF value − cohort-specific baseline mean)/cohort-specific baseline SD.

Definition and calculation of BPV

In the HRS and CHARLS cohorts, participants’ BP was measured by trained and certified research staff using the Omron HEM-780 Monitor and Omron HEM-7200 Monitor standard electronic sphygmomanometers, respectively (16,17). During measurement, participants were seated with their left arm supported and palm facing upward. The cuff was placed on the left upper arm, ensuring direct skin contact, positioned approximately half an inch (1–2 cm) above the elbow, with the air tube aligned toward the center of the inner arm. BP was measured three consecutive times on the same arm, with an interval of 45–60 seconds between each measurement to ensure data accuracy and reliability.

In the primary analysis, we used the variation independent of mean (VIM) to represent visit-visit BPV. VIM was calculated as 100 × SD/meanβ, where β is the regression coefficient based on the natural logarithm of SD divided by the natural logarithm of the mean (8). VIM is independent of the mean and can eliminate the confounding effect of mean BP level (17). In the sensitivity analyses, the coefficient of variation (CV) and average real variability (ARV) were used to assess BPV. CV was calculated by dividing SD by the mean BP. ARV was calculated as the average of the absolute differences between successive BP measurements across visits.

Confounding factors

This study collected covariates including participants’ demographics, lifestyle factors, and disease history. Marital status was categorized as married or unmarried (including single, divorced, widowed, etc.). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by the square of height in meters. Current cigarette consumption was defined as those who reported “currently smoking”, and current alcohol consumption were defined as those who had consumed alcohol in the three months prior to the baseline survey. Hypertension was defined as systolic BP ≥140 mmHg, or diastolic BP ≥90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication (19). Diabetes was defined as having been diagnosed with diabetes by a physician. In the HRS, overweight/obesity was defined as BMI greater than 25 kg/m2 (20), and high education level was defined as having education beyond high school (12 years or more). Race/ethnicity included White, Black, and other races. In the CHARLS, overweight/obesity was defined as BMI greater than 24 kg/m2 (21), and high education level was defined as having completed junior high school or above. Participants’ residence (urban/rural) was also included as a potential socioeconomic status variable for adjustment.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, and differences between groups were tested using analysis of variance. Categorical variables are expressed as frequency (percentage), and differences between groups were tested using the Chi-squared (χ2) test. Considering the longitudinal and repeated nature of participants’ BP measurements and the correlation among multiple measurements from the same participant, to effectively utilize all measurement information and control for within-individual correlation, we employed a linear mixed-effects model to analyze the longitudinal association between BPV and PEF, with β and 95% confidence interval (CI) reported. Model 1 was the crude model, without adjusting for covariates; Model 2 adjusted for participants’ age and sex; Model 3 further adjusted for race/residence, marital status, education level, cigarette consumption, alcohol consumption, BMI, hypertension, and diabetes; Model 4 additionally adjusted for participants’ baseline systolic and diastolic BP based on Model 3. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to participants’ baseline age (<60 versus ≥60 years), sex (male versus female), BMI (normal versus overweight/obese), and hypertension status (yes versus no). To explore potential modification effects, multiplicative interaction terms between the above covariates and BPV were included in Model 4, with the Wald χ2 test utilized. To further test the consistency of the results, we employed meta-analysis to pool the results across cohorts, with heterogeneity assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic (18). When I2 was greater than 50%, a random-effects model was used; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was applied. We conducted a series of sensitivity analyses to verify the robustness of the results. First, we use the average of PEF value across three measurements for analysis instead of the maximum value; second, alternative indicators of BPV were utilized for analysis, including ARV and CV; third, we excluded participants who did not exert maximal effort or stand upright during the PEF test; Additionally, we excluded participants with hypertension at baseline. In addition, given that PEF was strongly associated with anthropometric characteristics, particularly height, we further adjusted for it in the regression model. In all analyses, statistical significance was set at a two-sided P<0.05. All analyses were performed using R Studio software.

Association between BPV and lung function decline

Table 2 presents the results of the linear mixed models. In the crude model, increased systolic BPV was significantly associated with accelerated lung function decline in both HRS (β: −0.723, 95% CI: −0.839 to −0.607, P<0.001) and CHARLS (β: −0.467, 95% CI: −0.598 to −0.336, P<0.001). The pooled analysis showed that for each unit increase in systolic BPV, the Z-transformed PEF decreased by 0.60 (β: −0.597, 95% CI: −0.848 to −0.346, P<0.001), although heterogeneity was high (I2=87.84%). After adjusting for participants’ age and sex, the association remained significant (β: −0.225, 95% CI: −0.324 to −0.125, P<0.001). Further adjustment for baseline systolic and diastolic BP did not substantially change the results, and heterogeneity decreased (β: −0.224, 95% CI: −0.323 to −0.126, P<0.001, I2=45.55%). Compared with participants in the lowest tertile of VIM, those in the middle and highest tertiles showed significantly greater declines in Z-transformed PEF, with decreases of 0.058 (95% CI: −0.088 to −0.027, P<0.001) and 0.106 (95% CI: −0.137 to −0.075, P<0.001), respectively. Pooled analysis also revealed that increased diastolic BPV was significantly associated with greater lung function decline (β: −0.008, 95% CI: −0.011 to −0.005, P<0.001), with a more significant decline observed in participants with higher variability (β for Tertile 2: −0.032, 95% CI: −0.063 to −0.001, P=0.043; β for Tertile 3: −0.101, 95% CI: −0.135 to −0.068, P<0.001), and heterogeneity was low.

Subgroup analysis and interaction effects

The results of subgroup analyses and interaction effects are shown in Figure 2. For systolic BP, we found a significant modifying effect of sex on the association between BPV and lung function decline in HRS (Pinteraction<0.001), with a more pronounced decline in lung function observed in males (β: −0.506, 95% CI: −0.678 to −0.334, P<0.001). A similar trend was observed in CHARLS, though it was not significant (Pinteraction=0.38). For diastolic BP, sex also showed a (marginally) significant modifying effect on the association (Pinteraction<0.001 for HRS; Pinteraction=0.10 for CHARLS). Therefore, we further conducted a pooled analyses stratified by sex (Figure 3). The results showed that the effect of BPV on lung function decline was more significant in males, for both systolic BP (β: −0.347, 95% CI: −0.669 to −0.025, P=0.04) and diastolic BP (β: −0.012, 95% CI: −0.019 to −0.005, P<0.001).

Sensitivity analyses

When using the average PEF value instead of the maximum PEF for analysis, the results remained consistent with the main findings (Table S3). Additionally, using ARV and CV as alternative measures of BPV did not significantly alter the results (Tables S4,S5). Excluding participants who did not exert maximal effort or stand upright during the PEF test also yielded significant results (Table S6). Furthermore, after excluding participants with hypertension at baseline, the results remained consistent with the main findings (Table S7). Besides, the results did not change when we further adjusted for the heigh (Table S8).

Discussion

In this study, we used linear mixed-effects models to explore the longitudinal association between BPV and lung function decline in middle-aged and older adults from China and the United States. Our findings indicate that BPV (both systolic and diastolic) is significantly associated with an accelerated decline in lung function, and this effect is more pronounced in males.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the relationship between BPV and lung function decline. Research on BPV and lung health is currently very limited. A previous study explored the association between lung function and subsequent BPV in young individuals using indicators such as forced expiratory volume in the 1st second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) to reflect participants’ lung function. The results suggested that a lower peak FVC may independently increase the risk of long-term elevated BPV in mid-adulthood (22). A study from Japan examined the relationship between percent vital capacity (%VC) and daytime systolic BP, finding that reduced respiratory function was associated with increased dynamic BPV, especially in hypertensive patients without respiratory diseases (23). Another study conducted among African Americans assessed the association between reduced lung function and 24-hour BPV using day-night SD and ARV of BP to reflect short-term BPV; however, no significant results were found, regardless of adjustment for confounders (24). Different from previous studies, this research explicitly establishes long-term BPV as a potential risk factor for lung health decline. Through a pooled analysis, we systematically examined the longitudinal association between BPV and lung function decline in a large-scale population, which provided prospective epidemiological evidence for the cardiopulmonary interaction mechanism.

Although the pooled analysis revealed a significant result, some heterogeneity was observed between the two cohorts. This may be attributed to the differences in demographic and anthropometric characteristics (such as age, body size, and height), lifestyle factors and healthcare systems, which could influence both BPV and respiratory function. Additionally, measurement differences and study designs may further potentially cause the observed heterogeneity.

This study also found a significant sex difference in the association between BPV and lung function decline, with a more pronounced harmful effect observed in males, suggesting that sex is a key effect modifier. This sex difference may be attributed to multiple factors. On one hand, biological differences play an important role. The structure and function of the respiratory system itself exhibit gender differences (25). To be specific, from late pregnancy onward, female lung development is approximately 1.5 weeks ahead of males. After puberty, females exhibit a greater proportional growth of airways and lung parenchyma compared to males, resulting in better lung function indicators such as the ratio of FEV1 to FVC (25). In the period of older age, although lung and airway elastic recoil and alveolar air volume decrease with aging, these mechanical changes occur later and progress more slowly in females, which may contribute to poorer lung function in males (26). Additionally, hormones such as estrogen have been shown to be closely related to cardiovascular disease risk and inhibition of inflammatory responses (27,28). Therefore, higher endogenous estrogen levels in females may mitigate systemic damage caused by BP fluctuations. On the other hand, the higher smoking rates among males (29) and greater exposure to occupational hazards such as asbestos and dust (30) not only damage their lung function, but may also disrupt autonomic nervous balance, leading to increased BP fluctuations (31).

The potential biological mechanisms underlying the association between BPV and lung function decline remain unclear. Previous studies suggest possible involvement of autonomic dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and oxidative stress pathways. The autonomic nervous system (ANS) is a critical neural system for maintaining the stability of the cardiovascular and respiratory systems (32). The ANS maintains short- and BP stability through dynamic interactions between the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems, while autonomic dysfunction can lead to reduced regulatory capacity over cardiovascular parameters such as BP and heart rate (33). Imbalances between sympathetic and parasympathetic activity may also cause bronchoconstriction and increased mucus secretion, potentially leading to lung function decline (34). Another hypothesis suggests that imbalances in the ANS, characterized by increased sympathetic activation and reduced parasympathetic activity, may lead to dysregulated inflammatory reflexes and abnormal neurohumoral activation, promoting local and systemic pro-inflammatory activities and thereby advancing the progression of chronic lung diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (35). Apart from autonomic dysfunction, inflammation and oxidative stress may also play roles. Previous studies indicate that frequent BP fluctuations can directly damage vascular endothelial cells, creating a favorable environment for lipid deposition and inflammatory responses. Simultaneously, BP fluctuations can stimulate the immune system, promoting the infiltration of inflammatory cells and the release of inflammatory factors such as interleukins and tumor necrosis factor (36). This phenomenon has also been observed in epidemiological studies (37). These systemic inflammatory mediators can reach the lungs via the bloodstream, causing inflammation in the airways and lung interstitium, as well as inflammatory lung diseases, thereby accelerating lung function decline (36).

The main strength of this study lies in its use of multi-cohort pooled analysis to reveal, for the first time, a significant association between BPV and accelerated lung function decline in middle-aged and older adults, providing novel epidemiological evidence for a deeper understanding of cardiopulmonary interactions. This study also has several limitations. First, as an observational study, it cannot establish causality. Second, the calculation of BPV was based on a limited number of measurements (3–4 times per participant). Although this reflects the practical context of long-term follow-up to some extent, it may not fully capture the complex patterns of BP fluctuations and changes, potentially reducing the accuracy of the results. Third, this study used PEF as the primary measure of lung function. While it is a practical tool for screening airway obstruction and monitoring changes, suitable for large-scale epidemiological surveys, its measurement may be influenced by individual effort, and it is less sensitive to small airway dysfunction compared to indicators such as FEV1 and %VC. However, due to limitations inherent in the cohort design, these additional lung function parameters were not available, and PEF was therefore the only feasible measure. Consequently, its ability to fully capture overall lung function decline may be limited. Additionally, although our study adjusted for a range of confounders, the influence of potential factors such as occupation, air pollution, and genetic factors on participants remains unknown, and residual confounding cannot be overlooked. For the future studies, comprehensive assessment of lung function with multiple parameters, more frequent BP measurements, and careful consideration of additional confounding are recommended to further validate our findings.

Conclusions

In summary, this pooled analysis found that BPV is significantly associated with lung function decline in middle-aged and older adults, and this association is more pronounced in males. The results of this study provide further insights into the impact of BPV on adverse health outcomes and emphasize the importance of early intervention for individuals with higher BPV, particularly for males.
