A scoping review of outcomes and implementation challenges of mental health care interventions among conflict-affected populations in Africa
Authors: Olivier Sibomana, Tolulope Joseph Ogunniyi, Boluwatife Samuel Fatokun, Jildas Munyantore, Esther Edet Bassey, Gabriel Oke, Leonard Uzairue, Kehinde Precious Fadele, Melody Okereke, Egide Ndayambaje
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionConflict across Africa has led to widespread displacement, trauma, and mental health disorders, with limited access to care in already resource-constrained health systems. This scoping review is aimed at mapping existing evidence on mental health interventions for conflict-affected populations in Africa, examining their types, outcomes, and implementation challenges, to inform future policy, practice, and research.MethodsFollowing Arksey and O’Malley’s framework and PRISMA-ScR guidelines, a comprehensive search of PubMed, Google Scholar, WHO Global Health Library, and grey literature from UN agencies and NGOs was conducted. Publications from 2000 to 2025 in English were included if they examined mental health interventions among conflict-affected African populations. Data were extracted to identify intervention types, their outcomes, and implementation challenges.ResultsFrom 1368 records screened, 43 studies met our inclusion criteria. Most studies were conducted in Uganda (n = 14), the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) (n = 7), Nigeria (n = 6), and Sierra Leone (n = 5), and they targeted war-affected populations (n = 19), refugees (n = 17), and internally displaced persons (n = 12). Mental health interventions included counseling, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), psychosocial support, group therapy, community-based care, psychoeducation, and narrative exposure therapy. Reported outcomes demonstrated reductions in symptoms; improved daily functioning, psychological well-being, and community uptake; strengthened social support; better coping in participants; and gender-specific outcomes. Implementation challenges to those mental health interventions included human resource shortages, logistical barriers, funding constraints, insecurity, stigma, and cultural and social barriers, as well as governance and coordination gaps.ConclusionWhile diverse and contextually adapted mental health interventions in African conflict-affected settings have shown substantial benefits, optimum effectiveness can only be attained after issues like human resource capacity, funding, and coordination mechanisms are addressed. Improving workforce capacity, sustainable financing, coordination, and integration of culturally appropriate community-based care is critical to enhancing the reach and sustainability of mental health services in conflict-affected settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-026-00505-5.

Introduction

Wars and conflicts continue to be the concerning global threat in all life areas. In 2019, an estimated 132 million people in 142 countries were affected by war and violent conflict [1, 2]. Also, globally, 172 million people are affected by armed conflict, and at least 240 million children live in countries affected by conflict or fragility [3, 4]. The UN Refugee Agency reported that a record 117 million people were forcibly displaced worldwide in 2023 [5] and this number increased to 122 million people in 2024 [6]. During the first 6 months of 2024, more than 4.7 million people were forced to flee within their country due to armed conflicts, generalized violence, or human rights violations [7]. Low-income countries continued to host a disproportionately large share of the world’s displaced people, both in terms of their population size and the resources available to them [7].

In Africa, almost all countries have recently endured several significant events that resulted in wide-ranging societal impacts, causing the high statistics of significant trauma and loss through ethnic killings, forced servitude of child combatants, and sexual violence. A study conducted in Africa showed that many countries on this continent experience wars and conflicts [8]. A 2024 report reported that Africa currently has over 35 armed conflicts [9]. These disputes include groups fighting against governments and among themselves. Key countries involved are Burkina Faso, Mali, Mozambique, Nigeria, and Somalia [10]. In addition to armed conflict, human rights violations persist. Governments often suppress dissent and restrict civil liberties [11]. Currently, the World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) report that North Kivu in DRC has been plagued by conflict since the 1990s, with the first and second Congolese civil wars followed by decades of instability and violence. The World Food Program (WFP) estimates that the conflict has displaced more than 7.8 million [12].

With reference to the World Health Organization [13], 20% of those affected by conflict experience a mental disorder and another one-tenth will go on to develop behaviors that negatively impact their well-being [14]. The contribution to these statistics was highly observed from African countries and they are the ones affected the most [15]. Populations affected by wars and conflicts suffer from psychological distress, including acute stress reactions and bereavement, both children and adults [16–19]. In a meta-analysis conducted on mental health outcomes related to trauma in conflict- or displacement-affected populations, Steel and colleagues found an adjusted post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) prevalence of 15.4% from the most rigorous studies [20]. This calls for quick and successful interventions to help victims cope with the problems they face and if it is possible, to put an end to those society-ruining conflicts and disputes.

The burden of mental health problems in low- and middle-income countries is substantial, with the gap between those affected and those who receive any kind of treatment at large. In this set, where most of the African countries belong, up to 90% of patients do not receive appropriate health services [21]. Looking from the view of this gap, different mental health programs have been implemented to support those conflict-affected people, and most of the implemented interventions have been shown to be effective in randomized controlled trials [22, 23]. For instance, the World Health Organization’s Mental Health Gap Action Program (mhGAP) [24] provides treatment guidelines grounded in synthesized research evidence, recommending certain evidence-based interventions as primary approaches for addressing mental health issues.

Among others, community-based interventions have shown high effectiveness in reducing mental health problems in post-conflict settings. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis found that mental health interventions delivered by specialist healthcare workers significantly reduced PTSD symptoms (SMD = 0.99), while non-specialist interventions had smaller but significant effects (SMD = 0.25). Most frontline interventions were effective, especially for PTSD. However, it highlighted the need for more high-quality, adequately powered randomized trials—especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)—to assess the effectiveness and cost-efficiency of non-specialist interventions. Barriers such as resource limitations, training gaps, and contextual variability were also noted as challenges for implementation [25]. Another systematic review reported a wide range of psychosocial intervention initiatives; however, it noted a lack of consensus on which interventions are most effective and emphasized the need for culturally appropriate approaches that leverage local practices and resources [26].

Given the profound and widespread mental health consequences of conflict on the African continent, there is a need to understand available mental health interventions for conflict-affected people, their effectiveness, implementation challenges and how they can be solved. Meanwhile, there is currently no comprehensive synthesis that maps existing mental health interventions for conflict-affected populations in Africa, evaluates their outcomes, and identifies implementation barriers. The evidence in this field is heterogeneous, spanning diverse intervention types, populations, settings, and study designs, making a scoping review particularly suitable for mapping the breadth of existing literature, identifying key concepts, and highlighting gaps in evidence. This scoping review aims to close the gap by summarizing existing knowledge on available interventions, their outcomes, and implementation challenges. This synthesis will provide invaluable insights for policymakers, practitioners, and researchers striving to address the urgent mental health needs of conflict-affected populations.

Methodology

Methods

A scoping review of the literature examining mental health care for conflict-affected and post-conflict populations in Africa was conducted. This review aims to identify gaps in the existing literature, clarify key concepts, and guide future research priorities. With this aim in mind, a scoping review approach was adopted to fulfill the objective of mapping and summarizing mental health interventions, their outcomes, and implementation challenges across conflict-affected settings in Africa, following the framework developed by Arksey and O’Malley [27]. The review was conducted according to the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA), with specific adherence to the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [28].

Literature search

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed, Google Scholar, and the WHO Global Health Library, covering the period from January 2000 to April 2025. In addition, a manual grey literature search was performed, including reports from the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), the World Health Organization (WHO), non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and relevant policy documents, to capture unpublished or non-peer-reviewed evidence on mental health interventions for conflict-affected populations in Africa. Key terms included the following: “mental health care,” “mental health services,” “psychosocial support,” “trauma-informed care,” “conflict-affected populations,” “war-affected communities,” “post-conflict settings,” “humanitarian contexts,” “Africa,” “interventions,” “mental health interventions,” “effectiveness,” “outcomes,” “implementation challenges,” “access to care,” and “health systems”. Boolean operators were used to combine key terms strategically to ensure a comprehensive search (Appendix 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This scoping review applied the Population-Concept-Context (PCC) framework to guide study selection. The population of interest included conflict-affected groups in Africa, such as refugees, internally displaced persons, and war-affected communities. The concept focused on mental health interventions, encompassing their types, reported outcomes, and implementation challenges. The context comprised conflict, post-conflict, and humanitarian settings within African countries. Based on this framework, primary studies, program evaluations, and grey literature addressing mental health interventions among these populations were considered. Eligible study designs included observational studies (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional), experimental studies (randomized controlled trials and quasi-experimental studies), and government or organizational reports providing insights relevant to the review objectives. Only English-language publications from January 2000 to April 2025 were included to ensure comprehensibility. Studies were excluded if they focused on outcomes unrelated to mental health, were conducted outside Africa, or were published prior to 2000.

Literature screening

The gathered literature was imported into Zotero for data management and duplicates were removed before being uploaded into Covidence for both title and abstract screening, followed by full-text screening according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts was conducted independently by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. In cases where consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted to make a final decision after evaluating the reasons provided by the initial reviewers and assessing the study against the eligibility criteria.

Data extraction

An Excel spreadsheet was used to extract relevant data from the included studies to ensure systematic organization and analysis. Extracted information included the author’s name, year of publication, study design, types of interventions implemented, along with the target populations, including refugees, internally displaced persons (IDPs), and host communities. Key outcomes recorded were mental health indicators such as PTSD, depression, and anxiety, as well as resilience and coping mechanisms. Additionally, data on implementation strategies—such as delivery methods, sustainability, and scalability—were recorded, along with identified challenges including stigma, resource limitations, and logistical barriers (Appendix 2).

Data synthesis

Data synthesis was conducted using a combination of descriptive and thematic approaches [29]. A descriptive synthesis was first applied to summarize the types of interventions implemented, their reported outcomes, and the challenges encountered. Thematic analysis was then used to identify recurring themes across the studies, such as cultural barriers and resource limitations. To enhance clarity and facilitate interpretation, we used visual mapping techniques, including tables, charts, and diagrams. We also identified gaps in the existing literature and highlighted areas where further research and intervention are needed.

Reporting

Findings of this scoping review were reported with a focus on mental health interventions, their outcomes, implementation challenges, and recommendations for future efforts. Emphasis was placed on interventions within conflict-affected settings in Africa, outlining successful strategies while identifying persistent barriers such as stigma, resource constraints, and cultural challenges. The review offers guidance for future research and program development aimed at improving mental health outcomes among these vulnerable populations.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1385 records were identified through database and register searches, including PubMed (n = 329), Google Scholar (n = 970), WHO Global Health Library (n = 9), and other registers (n = 77). After removing 17 duplicates, 1368 records were screened based on titles and abstracts, of which 1193 records were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. The full texts of 175 reports were assessed for eligibility, all of which were successfully retrieved. Of these, 132 reports were excluded for reasons including wrong context, outcomes not related to mental health, irrelevant concepts, being protocals or paywall accessibility barriers. Ultimately, 43 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this scoping review (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

Of the 1368 studies identified from the study search, 43 studies were included in this scoping review (Appendix 2). Out of the 43 studies, the majority were conducted in Uganda (n = 14), followed by Democratic Republic of Congo (n = 7), Nigeria (n = 6;), and Sierra Leone (n = 5). Among the study population in the included studies, war-affected populations accounted for 19 studies, refugees accounted for 17 studies and IDPs accounted for 12 studies. Based on mental health focus, the studies majorly focused on psychological distress (n = 23), PTSD (n = 21), Depression (n = 17) and anxiety (n = 9). However, fewer studies focused on psychosis (n = 2), stigma (n = 2), developmental disorder (n = 1), mental distress (n = 1), mood disorder (n = 1) and substance abuse (n = 1).

Mental healthcare intervention reported in the included studies

A wide range of mental health interventions were implemented across studies included in this study, reflecting contextual adaptations and varied delivery models suited to conflict-affected settings. These interventions are thematically categorized in Table 1.

Counseling.

Counseling.

Counseling.

Counseling formed one of the core intervention types and was facilitated by trained mental health workers, social workers, or trauma counselors. In one study, group counseling was delivered over 6 months through a mix of clinic-based and community outreach follow-ups to effect [30]. Also, mental health outreach activities by organizations like Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) included individual counseling coupled with medication and child-focused therapy, aimed at supporting survivors in displacement camps [31]. Other studies have featured psychosocial counseling delivery in clinics for children, women, individuals, groups, and families [32, 33].

2.Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).

2.Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).

CBT was frequently deployed, often adapted to the local context and delivered after training sessions for facilitators. The Youth Readiness Intervention (YRI), for example, was repeatedly implemented through group sessions by trained facilitators [34–36]. The same was true for the Early Adolescents Skills for Emotions (EASE) intervention [37, 38]. Self-Help Plus (SH+), a low-intensity CBT-based model, was uniquely delivered via group audio sessions to participants and in one study, alongside brief substance use interventions [39, 40]. Other programs, like CETA-Youth, extended CBT to children and caregivers [41].

3.Psychosocial support.

3.Psychosocial support.

Psychosocial support.

Psychosocial support interventions showed a wide variety in scope, delivery, and target population. Some were delivered through trained counselors or facilitators leveraging group and individual discussions [42–45]. Others were embedded in peacebuilding or community healing processes [46–48]. Also, several programs included practical components like mentorship or economic support alongside psychosocial care [49–51]. Delivery timelines also varied, with some being as brief as a few weeks, while others ran for over a year [52–54]. Notably, when it is important to access hard-to-reach areas, the use of mobile teams and community outreach is favorably paired with psychosocial support [31, 44].

4.Group therapy.

4.Group therapy.

Group therapy.

Group therapy was another commonly used intervention type, especially where shared experiences among participants could be leveraged for healing. Several interventions used structured group sessions to deliver CBT-based content or peacebuilding narratives [35, 55, 56]. Also, some programs focused on storytelling and guided therapy within group settings, often integrating discussions and emotional regulation techniques [36]. Group therapy was also effectively delivered using a Theory of Change model and followed up with individual interviews [57].

5.Community-based care.

5.Community-based care.

Community-based care.

Community-based care approaches placed strong emphasis on local leadership and cultural relevance, with intervention implementation involving not just trained local staff/volunteers but also community leaders. The Rabbits for Resilience (RFR) program similarly targeted youth in rural villages using trained local staff and embedded the care within existing social structures [58]. Other studies that saw interventions being intertwined with community systems include the computer and information technology (CIT) training intervention and the community case detection tool (CCDT+) amongst others [59–61].

6.Psychoeducation.

6.Psychoeducation.

Psychoeducation.

Psychoeducation was used as a standalone low-resource strategy in a study that delivered training on theories of mind for nearly a year by NGO staff, emphasizing long-term behavior and attitude change [62]. Conversely, the Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP-HIG) represents an example where psychoeducation was usually layered into a broader program involving group training and focus group discussions to teach psychological first aid and trauma response techniques [63]. In other programs, psychoeducation was used to prepare communities for more intensive therapy or counseling [58, 60].

7.Narrative exposure therapy (NET).

7.Narrative exposure therapy (NET).

Narrative exposure therapy (NET).

NET was less widely used compared to other interventions but showed up in a few targeted programs. One of the use cases includes a randomized clinical trial where participants were monitored for 13 months through individual interviews [65]. Other NET interventions, like KIDNET, focused specifically on children in refugee settlements [65]. A study involving IDP camps used an 8-session home-based model delivered over 3 weeks, showing that NET could also be delivered by lay counselors with proper training and supervision [66].

Mental healthcare outcomes

The reviewed interventions demonstrated a range of mental health outcomes, from symptom reduction to broader psychosocial and functional improvements, amongst others. These outcomes are thematically categorized in Table 2.

Symptom reduction.

Symptom reduction.

Symptom reduction.

Several interventions showed symptom reduction outcomes in depression, anxiety, and PTSD. One study recorded a dramatic drop in depression and anxiety rates, going from about 90% each to less than 16% post-intervention sustained for over 6 months [67]. Narrative Exposure Therapy also effectively reduced PTSD symptoms in a study where only 29% of treated refugees met PTSD criteria post-intervention [64]. Group therapy also led to a reduction in depression symptoms of up to 9.79 points as compared to the control [52]. CBT-based interventions such as Youth Readiness Interventions, Employment Promotion Programs, and Self Help Plus delivered strong reductions in depressive symptoms, PTSD, and self-reported problems [36, 40, 41]. Symptom relief was also reported in large-scale programs, with 95% of patients in a multi-country intervention experiencing stress reduction and full PTSD symptom normalization post-intervention [44]. Other studies reinforced these findings across different populations and modes of intervention delivery [47, 48, 55, 65, 66].

2.Functional improvement.

2.Functional improvement.

Functional improvement.

Functionaloutcomes, usually involving improved daily function and school participation were recorded significantly across multiple studies. In one study, 96% of participants noted improved day-to-day functioning post-intervention, particularly with longer treatment exposure [44]. Functional improvements were also seen in school outcomes, where CBT-based programs improved attendance, enrollment, and performance, especially among vulnerable youth [56]. Other CBT-based interventions, such as the Youth Readiness Intervention and Self Help Plus, also demonstrated reduced functional impairment in participants [34, 40]. It is also important to note that even in cases where symptom reduction was moderate or varied, functionality still improved [30, 55].

3.Enhanced psychological well-being.

3.Enhanced psychological well-being.

Enhanced psychological well-being.

Several studies also reported gains in subjective well-being, emotional flexibility, and psychological resilience. A CBT-based program had participants reporting higher prosocial behavior and better emotion regulation post-intervention [34]. Caregivers and partners involved in another study also reported feeling better themselves, citing the improved behavior of participants as a contributing factor [68]. The Self Help Plus program also reduced the risk of developing disorders by 84%, while improving subjective well-being and psychological flexibility [45]. Other programs showed medium to large effect sizes on the overall well-being of participants post-intervention [40, 41, 54].

4.Strengthened social support.

4.Strengthened social support.

Strengthened social support.

Several interventions placed focus on or resulted in improved social support and reduced isolation. The Youth Readiness Intervention once again reported increased perceived social support post-intervention [34]. Caregiver-focused programs like Journey of Life also showed improvements in both parental warmth and reduced violence against children [69]. A psychosocial intervention targeting adolescents (EASE) reported improvements in parenting practices and caregiver distress [38]. Some capacity-building programs also led to improved staff ability for community-based support delivery [63].

5.Gender-specific outcomes.

5.Gender-specific outcomes.

Gender-specific outcomes.

Some studies looked into how gender may influence mental health outcomes, both in terms of baseline vulnerability and intervention outcomes. In one analysis, over 75% of males presented with PTSD compared to 54% of females, yet females with PTSD were more in number than men, indicating higher representation in the study population [37]. Another study that employed group therapy showed larger reductions in depression symptoms among girls than boys with a 12.61-point gap [52]. Similarly, girls participating in Child Friendly Spaces (CFS) experienced stronger improvements in psychosocial well-being and developmental assets, while benefits for boys were only evident in high-quality program settings [54].

6.Increased community uptake.

6.Increased community uptake.

Increased community uptake.

There was also evidence of broader reach, increased service use and better identification of mental health needs among participants [31, 60]. Another intervention that enhanced mental health issues detection methods saw a 2.05-fold increase in mental health service utilization [61]. Also, one WHO-linked MHPSS effort saw use of psychosocial support rise from 6600 to over 20,000 users [51].

7.Better coping.

7.Better coping.

Better coping.

Several programs reported improved coping strategies in participants post-intervention [49, 58]. Specific programs like the Islamic Trauma Healing initiative highlighted improved coping as a part of the broader impact of distress relief after intervention [39]. Peer-based programs that suggested improved coping strategies linked it to better emotional regulation and more normalized reactions to trauma [32]. In older populations, better coping leaned on task-focused systems [70].

Outcomes of Mental Health Interventions for Conflict-Affected Populations in Africa

Mental healthcare implementation challenges among conflict-affected populations in Africa

Human resource limitations.

Human resource limitations.

Human resource limitations.

Human resources limitation has emerged as a significant issue facing mental healthcare implementation among conflict-affected individuals. Several studies highlighted the lack of mental health professionals. A study highlighted that one of the challenges faced in the implementation of mental healthcare centered around the lack of local mental health professionals to facilitate monitoring, and the high turnover of facility- and/or community-based staff [63]. Also, owing to a lack of mental health professionals, many clinical diagnoses which should inform mental healthcare are lacking [52]. The reports by Médecins Sans Frontières [31] revealed an extremely limited professional mental health staff, with one psychiatrist per million in some African regions. Also, a study highlighted that even with available mental health professionals, there is limited training to build their capacity in rolling out mental healthcare to conflicted affected individuals [53, 65].

2.Logistical and operational barriers.

2.Logistical and operational barriers.

Logistical and operational barriers.

Several barriers relating to logistics and operation also complicate the effectiveness of mental health intervention among conflict-affected individuals. Many participants were lost to follow up on the outcomes of intervention in some studies. Some of the participants either relocated during the study timeline [64], or were difficult to locate during research and follow-up [35], which affected the outcomes of the intervention rolled out. Also, some issues relating to data collection and management were also reported. There was an exclusion of up to 24% of the data collected owing to errors and alsoissues associated with the access to some necessary data that should impact mental healthcare [47, 61].

3.Financial and resource constraints.

3.Financial and resource constraints.

Financial and resource constraints.

The effectiveness and implementation of mental healthcare centered around the availability of funds and necessary resources needed for its drive. Many mental health interventions rolled out were greatly affected owing to lack of funding. For instance, the effectiveness of youth readiness intervention and school attendance rolled out to address mental health issues among conflicted affected individuals was impacted by the inability to provide transportation for participants who are from far distances, which makes the intervention ineffective for the majority [34]. A study also reported a severe underfunding for the support of intervention rolled out, which is less than 30% of the $180 M protection plan funded [51]. The unavailability of some essential resources such as food and shelter with harsh camp conditions reduced the perceived need for mental health intervention among conflict-affected individuals [32].

4.Security and political context.

4.Security and political context.

Security and political context.

The security concerns among conflict-affected individuals are a great concern to the effectiveness and implementation of mental healthcare services. Interventions were not made possible and effective in some regions owing to security concerns in some villages [71, 69] and political constraints [46]. Also, the open-mindedness and the attitude towards mental health intervention were also stated to be impacted by fear of re-traumatization and mistrust in Truth and Reconciliation Commissions (TRCs) [46]. Also, renewed violence can also impact the implementation and effectiveness of mental health intervention through the disruption of transitional justice and peace-building processes, which are among the strategies in resolving conflict and mental health challenges [48].

5.Cultural and social barriers.

5.Cultural and social barriers.

Cultural and social barriers.

Cultural and social barriers were reported to be a significant influential factor in how individuals perceived mental health and mental health care. One of the major findings is the severe stigma faced by individuals with mental health, which is due to how mental health is perceived by individuals limiting seeking care among people affected by conflict [31]. Also, many of the interventions faced challenges with illiteracy and language barriers, that show that the inability of the facilitator to communicate the needs and the approach of the mental health intervention in a local dialect reduced the chance of accessing the intervention [37, 38, 62, 68]. Moreover, gender-associated concerns were also reported initial engagement of men and also the expression of emotion by men being a barrier to the access and implementation of mental health intervention, calling for a need for tailoring specific gender content and delivery of intervention [40]. Additionally, owing to socio-cultural and religious beliefs, these individuals frequently bring their own cultural prejudices with them influencing how they understand, apply, or even oppose program procedures. Their views on traditional healing, gender roles, and religious values may have an impact on service uptake and engagement dynamics in the communities they serve, limiting the impact of mental health care.

6.Governance and coordination challenges.

6.Governance and coordination challenges.

Governance and coordination challenges.

The implementation of mental healthcare in conflict-affected areas can be influenced by the governance and coordination of the programs by key stakeholders. Poor coordination has been a major contributor to the implementation of mental healthcare. Van den Broek et al. [61] reported that the implementation of mental healthcare initiatives is severely hampered by coordination gaps between gatekeepers and service providers, which also affect how the community views and anticipates the results of such programs [61]. Also, the inability to sustain the mental health care for a long time by the stakeholders and variability in its implementation owing to the lack of funds and resources greatly influenced the effectiveness among conflict-affected individuals. Furthermore, as Luana Giardinelli [33] points out, the MHPSS Working Group lacks a designated coordinator, which has led to disjointed planning and poor interagency cooperation. As noted by Sam Mednick and Ruth Alonga [51], the efficient provision and oversight of mental health treatments in humanitarian contexts is further jeopardized by this absence of central leadership and inadequate accountability systems.

Discussion

This review synthesized the existing literature on mental health interventions for conflict-affected populations in Africa, their outcomes and implementation challenges. The findings indicate that many interventions primarily targeted trauma-related symptoms, including depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and anxiety, and commonly employed approaches such as individual and group counseling, cognitive–behavioral therapy (CBT), community-based interventions, psychoeducation, and narrative exposure therapy. Most interventions were brief, culturally and locally adapted, and often delivered in group formats. This review highlights the importance of leveraging non-specialist cadres to deliver mental health interventions, which has been found to be effective with evidence from a systematic review and meta-analysis examining the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of mental health interventions delivered by frontline healthcare workers in emergency settings [25].

The reviewed studies generally report positive outcomes: reductions in PTSD and depression scores (e.g. narrative exposure therapy greatly lowered PTSD diagnoses by 71% [64]; group interventions reduced depressive symptoms of up to 9.79 points as compared to control [52], and improved coping and social functioning. These findings align with broader evidence that structured psychological interventions can ameliorate conflict-related distress [25]. For instance, a 2020 systematic review by Purgato et al. [72] found that psychological therapies, particularly CBT and narrative interventions, significantly improved PTSD and depression symptoms in conflict-affected populations, with standardized mean differences (SMDs) around 0.5 to 1.0. Besides, Pedersen et al. [26] observed that while a wide array of psychosocial interventions existed in humanitarian settings, success hinged on the extent to which interventions aligned with local beliefs, resources, and sociocultural dynamics.

Implications for practice and policy

Our findings underscore the need to integrate mental health care into humanitarian and primary-care systems. Community-based models—including task-shifting to lay and religious leaders—show promise for expanding reach in Africa, and should be formally supported through national policy and financing mechanisms. For instance, adopting WHO’s mhGAP guidelines to train both primary health workers and traditional healers improved depression recognition without specialist oversight [73], highlighting the feasibility of task-sharing in resource-constrained settings. Similarly, group psychotherapy and psychosocial support are well-suited for delivery in camps or community centers. To be effective, these programs should be culturally tailored (e.g. using local idioms of distress, leveraging faith-based approaches) and combined with practical supports (education, livelihood training). Policymakers and NGOs should thus invest in sustainable, community-anchored delivery (e.g. mobile teams, telepsychiatry) and in monitoring to ensure fidelity. Such investments have been advocated globally with WHO recommending integrating mental health into emergency preparedness and using multi-tiered MHPSS approaches ranging from community self-help to clinical care [73]. In line with this, our results suggest that national programs should prioritize task-sharing, stigma reduction campaigns, and resource mobilization (as seen in recent national plans in Uganda, Kenya, etc.) to translate evidence-based interventions into routine care [74, 75].

Implementation challenges

Throughout our scoping review, implementation barriers were prominent. Resource constraints and workforce shortages were recurrent themes: for example, a recent African review noted “a severe shortage of mental health professionals, inadequate budget allocation, limited access to services in rural areas, and resource limitations” across many countries [74]. In practice, this meant training programs often struggled with low attendance, limited supervision, and staff attrition. One mhGAP-based training in Kenya reported low PHC staff interest and retention, due to factors like lack of administrative support, insufficient incentives, and geopolitical instability [75]. Stigma and cultural differences also hampered access: many survivors preferred traditional healers or did not recognize mental illness, mirroring findings that stigma and reliance on non-medical care are major barriers in Africa [73, 75]. Geographic and security challenges(e.g., displacement camps, active conflict zones) further constrained delivery. Collectively, these findings suggest that although many interventions demonstrate effectiveness in controlled settings, scaling them in low-resource, conflict-affected contexts requires addressing funding gaps, workforce capacity, and local acceptability [75, 76].

Mental health interventions in conflict-affected African settings must be designed with careful attention to unique contextual challenges. Culturally, mental distress is often expressed through somatic or spiritual idioms, and help-seeking commonly occurs through family networks, faith leaders, or traditional healers rather than formal health systems, requiring interventions to align with local explanatory models of illness. Resource constraints—including severe shortages of mental health specialists, fragile health infrastructure, disrupted supply chains, and limited digital connectivity—further necessitate low-cost, task-shared, and non-specialist delivery models. Politically, protracted conflict, displacement, and governance instability can undermine service continuity, restrict access to affected populations, and limit national ownership of mental health programming. In some settings, mental health remains deprioritized within humanitarian and national agendas, while stigma and insecurity may deter both providers and service users. Addressing these cultural, structural, and political realities is essential to ensure that mental health and psychosocial interventions are acceptable, feasible, and sustainable in conflict-affected African contexts.

Gaps and future research

Few interventions addressed severe mental illnesses, such as psychosis or bipolar disorder, or substance use, despite their documented prevalence in conflict-affected settings. Similarly, only a minority of programs specifically targeted adolescents, grief and bereavement, or suicidality [73]. A recent review of community-based mental health programs in Africa likewise identified limited engagement with adolescents or traditional healers, as well as minimal focus on suicidal behavior. These gaps highlight the need for future research to expand the focus toward underserved populations and priority mental health issues. Methodologically, most studies were small or uncontrolled trials, emphasizing the need for larger, rigorously designed studies with long-term follow-up to establish effectiveness, scalability, and cost-efficiency in real-world conditions, as also recommended by recent meta-analyses [77].

In addition, there is a pressing need to evaluate strategies for overcoming implementation barriers, such as assessing telemedicine approaches in hard-to-reach areas or integrating mental health services into broader humanitarian and relief programs. Economic evaluations would further support investment decisions, given the substantial treatment gap in Africa, where WHO estimates suggest that only around 10% of individuals in need receive care [25]. Finally, cross-disciplinary research should build on local strengths and survivor resilience, complementing trauma-focused approaches with culturally grounded, community-driven coping strategies to ensure interventions are both effective and contextually appropriate.

Limitations

This scoping review has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, as is typical of scoping reviews, we scoped broadly to map existing interventions rather than assess quality; therefore, findings synthesize study claims more than proving accuracy. This limited our ability to assess the methodological rigor or comparative strength of evidence. Besides, the included studies varied widely in design, outcome measures, and intervention types, creating significant heterogeneity that precluded meta-analysis or direct comparisons of effectiveness. Also, the data reported often lacked standardized measures, particularly for mental health outcomes such as PTSD, depression, and anxiety, making it difficult to synthesize results beyond broad trends. Some studies also did not report the intervention effectiveness in a numerical manner like percentages or using other numerical scales, which made it difficult to appraise its effectiveness. Additionally, most studies focused on short-term symptom reduction, with limited exploration of long-term outcomes or sustainability of interventions.

Other limitations relate to the scope of our search strategy and the geographic distribution of available studies. We included only literature published in English, potentially excluding relevant studies in other languages common across Africa. Furthermore, our search was restricted to major databases and selected grey literature sources, which may have overlooked unpublished programs or regional reports not indexed online. Lastly, while we aimed for breadth, the exclusion of non-intervention studies and lack of stakeholder consultation limited our ability to capture broader contextual or cultural dimensions of mental health care across diverse African settings.

Conclusion

This scoping review demonstrates that a diverse range of mental health interventions—including counseling, cognitive behavioral therapy, psychosocial support, community-based care, and narrative exposure therapy—have been implemented for conflict-affected populations in Africa, with evidence of meaningful improvements in symptoms, psychological well-being, coping, and social support. However, widespread implementation and long-term sustainability remain limited by persistent challenges, including shortages of trained personnel, inadequate funding, logistical and security barriers, stigma, and weak coordination within health systems. Strengthening mental health systems in conflict and displacement settings is therefore critical, including investments in workforce capacity, culturally sensitive service delivery, and governance and financing mechanisms. The scalable, contextually adapted, and well-coordinated mental health interventions can be effective, but their success depends on addressing systemic barriers to ensure equitable and sustainable care for Africa’s conflict-affected populations.
