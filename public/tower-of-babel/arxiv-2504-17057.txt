The autotopism group of a family of commutative semifields
Lukas Kölsch, Alexandra Levinshteyn, Milan Tenn

Abstract
We completely determine the autotopism group of the (as of now) largest family of commutative semifields found by Göloğlu and Kölsch. Since this family of semifields generally does not have large nuclei, this process is considerably harder than for families considered in preceding work. Our results show that all autotopisms are semilinear over the degree 2 subfield and that the autotopism group is always solvable. Using known connections, our results also completely determine the automorphism groups of the associated rank-metric codes and the collineation groups of the associated translation planes.



Keywords:
Semifields, projective planes, autotopism group, collineation group.

Mathematics Subject Classification:
12K10, 17A35.

Introduction

A finite
semifield

\mathbb{S}=(S,+,\circ)
is a finite set
S
equipped with two operations
(+,\circ)

satisfying the following axioms.

(S1)

(S,+)
is a group.

(S2)

For all
x,y,z\in S
,

•

x\circ(y+z)=x\circ y+x\circ z
,

•

(x+y)\circ z=x\circ z+y\circ z
.

(S3)

For all
x,y\in S
,
x\circ y=0
implies
x=0
or
y=0
.

(S4)

There exists
\epsilon\in S
such that
x\circ\epsilon=x=\epsilon\circ x
.

An algebraic object satisfying the first three of the above axioms is called
a
pre-semifield
. While infinite semifields exist, we are in this paper only concerned with the finite case and thus all (pre)-semifields are always assumed to be finite. If
{\mathbb{P}}=(P,+,\circ)
is a pre-semifield, then
(P,+)
is
an elementary abelian
p
-group
[
7
, p. 185]
, and
(P,+)
can be viewed as
an
n
-dimensional
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-vector space
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
. If
\circ
is commutative, we call
\mathbb{S}
a commutative semifield.
Note that if
\circ
is associative then
\mathbb{S}
is the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
by
Wedderburn’s theorem which states that a finite division ring is a field.
A pre-semifield
{\mathbb{P}}=(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n},+,\circ)

can be converted to a semifield
\mathbb{S}=(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n},+,\ast)
using
Kaplansky’s trick

by defining the new multiplication as

(x\circ e)\ast(e\circ y)=x\circ y,

for any nonzero element
e\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
, making
(e\circ e)
the multiplicative
identity of
\mathbb{S}
. Pre-semifields have received a lot of attention from geometers since they can be used to construct non-desarguesian planes, more precisely, translation planes whose duals are also translation planes, see e.g.
[
2
,
5
]
for a comprehensive overview of the connections of semifields to finite geometry.

A pre-semifield is an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-algebra, thus the multiplication
is bilinear. Therefore we have
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-linear left and right multiplications
L_{x},R_{y}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
, with

L_{x}(y):=x\circ y=:R_{y}(x).

The mapping
L_{x}
(resp.
R_{y}
) is a bijection whenever
x\neq 0
(resp.
y\neq 0
)
by (S3). By fixing a basis of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
, we can view
L_{x}
and
R_{y}
as matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, i.e., as elements in
M^{n\times n}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
. So

\mathcal{C}=\{R_{y}(x)\colon y\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}\}

is an additively closed set where all non-zero matrices are invertible. Such a set is called a (semifield)
spread set
. It is also an optimal linear rank-metric code (in the sense that it satisfies the Singleton bound with equality) for specific parameters, see
[
13
]
.

One of the most important properties of (pre)-semifields is their
autotopism group
.

The autotopism group of a semifield is isomorphic to the automorphism group of the corresponding spread set, which we define now:

Definition 1.1
.

The automorphism group
\Aut(\mathcal{C})
of a spread set
\mathcal{C}\leq M^{n\times n}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
is

\Aut(\mathcal{C})=\left\{\right(X,Y)\in\GL(n,p)\times\GL(n,p)\colon X\mathcal{C}Y=\mathcal{C}\}.

The autotopism group of a semifield can for instance be used to show that semifields are inequivalent, see e.g.
[
4
]
. In the geometric setting, determining the autotopism group of a (pre)-semifield is equivalent to determining the collineation group of the corresponding translation plane, see
[
2
]
, and in the coding theory setting, it is equivalent to the automorphism group of the rank-metric code.

Certain subgroups of the automorphism group play an important role (see e.g.
[
12
]
).

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{S})
be the semifields spread set belonging to a pre-semifield
\mathbb{S}=(\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n},+,\circ)
. Define the sets

\mathcal{N}_{m}(\mathbb{S})^{*}=\left\{Y\in\GL(n,p)\colon\mathcal{C}Y=\mathcal{C}\right\},\mathcal{N}_{r}(\mathbb{S})^{*}=\left\{X\in\GL(n,p)\colon X\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}\right\}.

Then

\mathcal{N}_{m}(\mathbb{S}):=\mathcal{N}_{m}(\mathbb{S})^{*}\cup\{0_{n}\}
and
\mathcal{N}_{r}(\mathbb{S}):=\mathcal{N}_{r}(\mathbb{S})^{*}\cup\{0_{n}\}

are called the middle and right nucleus of
\mathbb{S}
, respectively. Here,
0_{n}\in M^{n\times n}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
is the zero matrix.

Clearly,
\mathcal{N}_{m}(\mathbb{S})^{*}
and
\mathcal{N}_{r}(\mathbb{S})^{*}
are normal subgroups of the automorphism group. The nuclei are always finite fields. It is follows immediate from the definition of the nuclei (see
[
10
, Theorem 3.4.]
) that

\mathcal{N}_{r}(\mathbb{S})^{*}\times\mathcal{N}_{m}(\mathbb{S})^{*}\leq\Aut(\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{S}))\leq N(\mathcal{N}_{r}(\mathbb{S})^{*})\times N(\mathcal{N}_{m}(\mathbb{S})^{*}),

where
N(S)
denotes the normalizer of the set
S
in
\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{p})
. This indicates the following rule of thumb:
The bigger the nuclei of
\mathbb{S}
, the easier it is to determine autotopism group of semifield.
Indeed, if one of the nuclei is very large, computing the autotopism group is comparatively easy, see e.g.
[
6
]
, where the autotopism group of the Dickson semifields of size
p^{2m}
is determined, using the fact that one of the nuclei has size
p^{m}
. Generally, it is however very difficult to determine the autotopism group of a semifield, and for most known families of semifields with small nuclei, the autotopism group is either not known, or determining the autotopism group takes considerable effort, like in the case of the cyclic semifields
[
3
]
.

Our contribution.
In this paper, we will determine the autotopism group of the following family of commutative semifields, found in
[

]

(

.

Define a binary operation

Consequently, the semifield spread set

Then

The nuclei were determined in
[
4
, Theorem 7.2.]
:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\mathbb{S}=(\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}\times\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}},+,\circ)
with the multiplication as in Eq. (
1.1
).
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{r}(\mathbb{S})

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\mapsto(ax,ay)\colon a\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{\gcd(k+m/2,m)}}\},

In particular, it is possible to choose

The main result of this paper is:

Theorem 1.5

(Main result)
.

Let
\mathbb{S}
be a pre-semifield with multiplication as defined in Theorem

In fact, we determine all automorphisms explicitly — the precise description can be found for the first case in Theorem

We want to note that even though the nuclei are possibly small, the autotopism group of these semifields is always large. This in itself is also unusual among the known semifields, see e.g.

The rest of the paper is devoted to prove Theorem

4
The diagonal case

We now consider the case of automorphisms
(X,Y)\in\Aut(\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{S}_{q,A,B}))
where
X
is in diagonal form. The goal in this section is to prove that for these automorphisms, the subfunctions are necessarily monomials, reducing it to the monomial case already considered in Section
3
. We start with some lemmas.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{M}
be defined by
f(x)=xu^{q}+x^{q}u
for some
u\in\mathbb{M}^{\times}
. Then
f
is invertible.

Proof.

Since
f
is linear over
\mathbb{E}
, it is enough to check that its kernel is trivial. Now
f(x)=xu^{q}+x^{q}u=0
simplifies to
x^{q-1}=-u^{q-1}
for nonzero
x
, and the result follows since
-1
is not a
(q-1)
-st power by Lemma
2.3
(i).
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f,g\colon\mathbb{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{M}
such that for each
w\in\mathbb{M}
, there is an
u_{w}\in\mathbb{M}
such that

f\circ(x^{q}u_{w}+xu_{w}^{q})=(x^{q}w+xw^{q})\circ g.

Then
w\mapsto u_{w}
is a bijection or
g=0
.

Proof.

Suppose that
u=u_{w_{0}}=u_{w_{1}}
. Then,

f\circ(x^{q}u+xu^{q})=(x^{q}w_{0}+xw_{0}^{q})\circ g=(x^{q}w_{1}+xw_{1}^{q})\circ g

So

(x^{q}(w_{0}-w_{1})+x{(w_{0}-w_{1})}^{q})\circ g=0

Which means that either
w_{0}=w_{1}
or
g=0
by Lemma
4.1
. So, either
g=0
or
w\mapsto u_{w}
is a bijection.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
f,g\colon\mathbb{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{M}
such that
g\neq 0
and

f\circ(x^{q}u_{w}+xu_{w}^{q})=(x^{q}w+xw^{q})\circ g

(4.1)

for all
w\in\mathbb{M}
and some bijection
w\mapsto u_{w}
of
\mathbb{M}
. Then
f
and
g
are semilinear over
\mathbb{E}
with the same associated field automorphism.

Proof.

We can write any function on
\mathbb{M}
in the following way:
Define
f_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq e-1
such that all
f_{i}
are
\mathbb{E}
-linear and

f=\sum_{i=0}^{e-1}x^{p^{i}}\circ f_{i}.

Similarly, let
g_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq e-1
such that all
g_{i}
are
\mathbb{E}
-linear and

g=\sum_{i=0}^{e-1}x^{p^{i}}\circ g_{i}.

Then, Eq. (
4.1
) can be rewritten as

x^{p^{i}}\circ f_{i}\circ(x^{q}u_{w}+xu_{w}^{q})=(x^{q}w+xw^{q})\circ x^{p^{i}}\circ g_{i}.

for each
i
and for all
w\in\mathbb{M}
.

For
w\in\mathbb{E}
,

(x^{q}w+xw^{q})\circ x^{p^{i}}\circ g_{i}=(x^{q}+x)\circ x^{p^{i}}\circ g_{i}\circ w^{p^{-i}}x.

So,

(x^{q}w+xw^{q})\circ x^{p^{i}}\circ g_{i}=x^{p^{i}}\circ f_{i}\circ(x^{q}u_{1}+xu_{1}^{q})\circ w^{p^{-i}}x.

Then, as
w^{p^{-i}}\in\mathbb{E}
,

(x^{q}w+xw^{q})\circ x^{p^{i}}\circ g_{i}=x^{p^{i}}\circ f_{i}\circ(x^{q}(u_{1}w^{p^{-i}})+x{(u_{1}w^{p^{-i}})}^{q}).

So

x^{p^{i}}\circ f_{i}\circ(x^{q}(u_{1}w^{p^{-i}})+x{(u_{1}w^{p^{-i}})}^{q})=x^{p^{i}}\circ f_{i}\circ(x^{q}u_{w}+xu_{w}^{q})

x^{p^{i}}\circ f_{i}\circ(x^{q}(u_{1}w^{p^{-i}}-u_{w})+x{(u_{1}w^{p^{-i}}-u_{w})}^{q})=0.

Note that either

or

due to invertibility. Next, suppose that for some

, we have
f_{i}\neq 0
and
f_{j}\neq 0
. Then,
u_{w}=w^{p^{-i}}u_{1}=w^{p^{-j}}u_{1}
for all

. So,
w^{p^{i}-p^{j}}=1
for all

, meaning that
i\equiv j\pmod{e}
. However, we also know that

, so

This means that there exists at most one

. Also, for all
i
, if
f_{i}=0
then

. So clearly there exists some
i
such that

and

, so

are semilinear over
\mathbb{E}
with the same associated automorphism.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{M}
be defined by
f(x)=x^{r}v+Axv^{r}
for some
v\in\mathbb{M}^{\times}
. Then
f
is bijective.

Proof.

f
is bijective if and only if its kernel is trivial. We have
f(x)=x^{r}v+Axv^{r}=0
for nonzero

if and only if
A=-(x/v)^{r-1}
. But
A
is a non-square in
\mathbb{M}
, and both
-1
and
(x/v)^{r-1}
are squares in
\mathbb{M}
, leading to a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{M}
be semilinear over
\mathbb{D}
. Suppose for all
u\in\mathbb{M}^{\times}
we have that
f\circ(x^{r}u+Axu^{r})
is
\mathbb{E}
-linear. Then,
f=0
.

Proof.

Let
f=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}f_{i}\circ x^{p^{i}}
for
\mathbb{D}
-linear

. Then, for all
i\neq 0
,

f_{i}\circ x^{p^{i}}\circ(x^{r}u+Axu^{r})=0

since this part of
f\circ(x^{r}u+Axu^{r})
has no powers in common with any
\mathbb{E}
-linear function. So,

for all

by Lemma

, so

is
\mathbb{D}
-linear. Let
f=g+h\circ x^{r}
for
\mathbb{E}
-linear

(recall that
\mathbb{E}
is a degree

\mathbb{D}
). Then,

g\circ x^{r}u+h\circ A^{r}x^{r}u^{r^{2}}=0

for all

by the same logic as above. Then, for

, we can simplify this to

g+h\circ A^{r}u^{r^{2}-1}x=0.

If

then

.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
f,g\colon\mathbb{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{M}
such that
f
is semilinear over
\mathbb{D}
and
g
is semilinear over
\mathbb{E}
and and let
w\mapsto v_{w}
be a bijection on
\mathbb{M}
such that

f\circ(x^{r}v_{w}+c_{1}xv_{w}^{r})=(x^{r}w+c_{2}xw^{r})\circ g

holds for all
w\in\mathbb{M}
, where
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{M}^{\times}
. Then,
f
is a monomial.

Proof.

Let us first assume that
f
and
g
are linear over
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{E}
, respectively. We will deal with the general case afterwards.

We have for
w=1

f\circ(x^{r}v_{1}+c_{1}xv_{1}^{r})=(x^{r}+c_{2}x)\circ g.

Rewriting the condition in the statement of the lemma, we get

(x^{r}w+c_{2}xw^{r})\circ g=(x^{r}w+c_{2}xw+c_{2}x(w^{r}-w))\circ g

Combining these, we obtain

f\circ(x^{r}v_{w}+c_{1}xv_{w}^{r})=wx\circ f\circ(x^{r}v_{1}+c_{1}xv_{1}^{r})+c_{2}x(w^{r}-w)\circ g,

(4.2)

which means that

f\circ(x^{r}v_{w}+c_{1}xv_{w}^{r})-wx\circ f\circ(x^{r}v_{1}+c_{1}xv_{1}^{r})

(4.3)

is
\mathbb{E}
-linear. Recall that
f
is
\mathbb{D}
-linear, so write
f=\sum_{i=0}^{m/d-1}f_{i}x^{r^{i}}
. Similarly, write
f=\sum_{i=0}^{m/d-1}g_{i}x^{r^{i}}
, where
g_{i}=0
for odd

, since
g
is
\mathbb{E}
-linear and
[\mathbb{E}\colon\mathbb{D}]=2
. Then function in Eq. (
4.3

\mathbb{E}
-linear if for all odd

f_{i-1}v_{w}^{r^{i-1}}+f_{i}c_{1}^{r^{i}}v_{w}^{r^{i+1}}=w(f_{i-1}v_{1}^{r^{i-1}}+f_{i}c_{1}^{r^{i}}v_{1}^{r^{i+1}}).

We know that
w\mapsto v_{w}
is a bijection, so let
v\mapsto w_{v}
be the inverse. Then, for all
v\in\mathbb{M}
,

f_{i-1}v^{r^{i-1}}+f_{i}c_{1}^{r^{i}}v^{r^{i+1}}=w_{v}(f_{i-1}v_{1}^{r^{i-1}}+f_{i}c_{1}^{r^{i}}v_{1}^{r^{i+1}}).

(4.4)

Suppose that

f_{i-1}v_{1}^{r^{i-1}}+f_{i}c_{1}^{r^{i}}v_{1}^{r^{i+1}}=0.

Then,

f_{i-1}+f_{i}c_{1}^{r^{i}}v^{r^{i-1}(r^{2}-1)}=0

for all
v\in\mathbb{M}^{\times}
so since

can be chosen arbitrarily, we must have
f_{i}=f_{i-1}=0
(note that

by the conditions on

). Now, we know that there is some odd

where either

or
f_{i}\neq 0
since

. Call this

. Then, by Eq. (

is clearly a polynomial function of

and
v^{r^{i_{0}-1}}
.

Now, suppose that

w_{v}=C_{1}v^{r^{i_{0}+1}}+D_{1}v^{r^{i_{0}-1}}=C_{2}v^{r^{i_{1}+1}}+D_{2}v^{r^{i_{1}-1}}.

By comparing degrees, we see that either

or two powers are the same and the others have coefficients of

. This means that for

, we have necessarily

. Rewriting Eq. (

leads to

for all

.
Since

, we have

f_{i_{0}}(v_{w}^{r^{i_{0}}}-wv_{1}^{r^{i_{0}}})=c_{2}g_{i_{0}+1}(w^{r}-w).

So either
f_{i_{0}}=g_{i_{0}+1}=0
or
{(\frac{v_{w}}{v_{1}})}^{r^{i_{0}}}=w^{r}
for all

.
Similarly, since

and either
f_{i_{0}-1}=g_{i_{0}-1}=0
or
{(\frac{v_{w}}{v_{1}})}^{r^{i_{0}}}=w^{r}
. Since one of

and

are non-zero, we necessarily have that
{(\frac{v_{w}}{v_{1}})}^{r^{i_{0}}}=w^{r}
. This means that

has only powers of

and so only
f_{i_{0}-1}\neq 0
. Thus,

is a monomial and
\mathbb{E}
-linear, since

is even.

Now consider the case where
f
and
g
are only semilinear over
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{E}
, respectively. Then we may write

and

, where

are linear over
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathbb{E}
, respectively. Then

f\circ(x^{r}v_{w}+c_{1}xv_{w}^{r})=(x^{r}w+c_{2}xw^{r})\circ g

holds if and only if

x^{i-j}\circ f^{\prime}\circ(x^{r}v_{w}+c_{1}xv_{w}^{r})=(x^{r}w^{-i}+c_{2}^{-i}xw^{r-i})\circ g^{\prime}.

Since the right hand is
\mathbb{D}
-linear, this means

has to be
\mathbb{D}
-linear as well, so, by our previous result,
x^{i-j}\circ f^{\prime}
is a monomial.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
f,g\colon\mathbb{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{M}
. Further, let
w\mapsto v_{w}
be a bijection on
\mathbb{M}
such that

f\circ(x^{r}v_{w}+Axv_{w}^{r})=(x^{r}w+Axw^{r})\circ g

is a monomial if and only if

is a monomial and both monomials are of the same degree.

Proof.

If

, we have
f\circ(x^{r}v_{w}+Axv_{w}^{r})=(x^{r}v_{w}^{i}+A^{i}xv_{w}^{r+i})\circ g
, so the condition becomes

(x^{r}v_{w}^{i}+A^{i}xv_{w}^{r+i})\circ f=(x^{r}w+Axw^{r})\circ g,

implying that

is a monomial as well. The other direction works in a similar way.
∎

Using the same idea, we get the following lemma as well.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
f,g\colon\mathbb{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{M}
and
let further

be a bijection on

such that

f\circ(x^{q}u_{w}+xu_{w}^{q})=(x^{q}w+xw^{q})\circ g

Then,

is a monomial if and only if
g
is a monomial. In that case, both monomials are of the same degree.

We are now ready to state the main result of this section that states that any automorphism
(X,Y)\in\Aut(\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{S}_{q,A,B}))

Theorem 4.9
.

Suppose that
(X,Y)\in\Aut(\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{S}_{q,A,B}))
where

is in diagonal form. Then,
f_{1},f_{4},g_{1},g_{2},g_{3},g_{4}
are all monomials.

Proof.

If
(X,Y)\in\Aut(\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{S}_{q,A,B}))
then there exists for all

some

such that

f_{1}\circ R^{(1)}_{u,v}=R^{(1)}_{w,t}\circ(g_{1}(x)+g_{2}(y),g_{3}(x)+g_{4}(y))

and

f_{4}\circ R^{(2)}_{u,v}=R^{(2)}_{w,t}\circ(g_{1}(x)+g_{2}(y),g_{3}(x)+g_{4}(y)).

So, we see that for all
u,v
there exist some
w,t
such that the following are all true:

\displaystyle f_{1}\circ(x^{q}u+xu^{q})=(x^{q}w+xw^{q})\circ g_{1}+B(x^{q}t+xt^{q})\circ g_{3}

(4.5)

\displaystyle f_{1}\circ B(y^{q}v+yv^{q})=(y^{q}w+yw^{q})\circ g_{2}+B(y^{q}t+yt^{q})\circ g_{4}

(4.6)

Suppose that
w=0
. Then, for all
t
there exists some
u
such that

f_{1}\circ(x^{q}u+xu^{q})=B(x^{q}t+xt^{q})\circ g_{3}

and so by Lemma
4.3

f_{1}
and
g_{3}
are semilinear over
\mathbb{E}
with the same associated field automorphism, or
g_{3}=0
. By the same logic, setting
w=0
or
t=0
and looking at Eq. (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), we find that
f_{1},g_{1},g_{2},g_{3},g_{4}
are all semilinear over
\mathbb{E}
with the same associated field automorphism, or are the zero mapping.

Likewise, by Lemma
4.2
either
t\mapsto u_{t}
for
w=0
is a bijection or
g_{3}=0
. Similarly, considering Eq. (
4.6
), we get that
t\mapsto u_{t}
for
w=0
is a bijection or
g_{4}=0
. Since
g_{3}
and
g_{4}
cannot both be
0
, we have necessarily that
t\mapsto u_{t}
for
w=0
is a bijection.

Next, we see that for all
u,v
there exist some
w,t
such that:

\displaystyle f_{4}\circ(x^{r}v+Axv^{r})=(x^{r}t+Axt^{r})\circ g_{1}+(Ax^{r}w+xw^{r})\circ g_{3}

(4.7)

\displaystyle f_{4}\circ(Ay^{r}u+yu^{r})=(y^{r}t+Ayt^{r})\circ g_{2}+(Ay^{r}w+yw^{r})\circ g_{4}

(4.8)

We proceed similarly to before. Let
w=0
. By Eqs. (
4.6
) and (
4.7
), we have that for all
t
there exists some
v
such that

f_{1}\circ B(y^{q}v+yv^{q})=B(y^{q}t+yt^{q})\circ g_{4}\text{ and }f_{4}\circ(x^{r}v+Axv^{r})=(x^{r}t+Axt^{r})\circ g_{1}.

Then, by Lemma
4.2
either
g_{4}=0
or
t\mapsto v
is a bijection. If the former is true then (since
f_{1}\neq 0
) we have
v=0
for all
(t,w)
where
w=0
. In this case, we see that
(x^{r}t+Axt^{r})\circ g_{1}=0
for all
t
so
g_{1}=0
by Lemma
4.4
.

If
g_{4}\neq 0
, which is equivalent to
g_{1}\neq 0
, then by Lemma
4.6
we see that
f_{4}
is a monomial and by Lemma
4.7
,
g_{1}
is a monomial as well.

We do likewise for all nonzero
g_{i}
by picking different combinations from Eqs. (
4.5
) to (
4.8
), and find that all nonzero
g_{i}
and
f_{1}
are monomials as well by invoking Lemmas
4.7
and
4.8
.

If
g_{1}=g_{4}=0
then
g_{2},g_{3}
are necessarily non-zero and we can again follow the same arguments as before by picking the correct combinations from Eqs. (
4.5
) to (
4.8
) to conclude that
f_{1},f_{4},g_{2},g_{3}
are monomials.
∎

5
The general case

In this section, we deal with the general case, i.e., we place no further restrictions on
(X,Y)\in\Aut(\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{S}_{q,A,B}))
except that
X
is semilinear over
\mathbb{D}
and
Y
is semilinear over
\mathbb{E}
. Our goal is to show that
X
is necessarily in diagonal form, reducing it to the case investigated in the previous section.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose
(X,Y)\in\Aut(\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{S}_{q,A,B}))
is an automorphism. Then
f_{2}=0
.

Proof.

Consider the monomials in
x
of Eq. (
2.1
) for
u=0
. Then, for all
v\in\mathbb{M}
,

f_{2}\circ(x^{r}v+Axv^{r})=(x^{q}w+xw^{q})\circ g_{1}+B(x^{q}t+xt^{q})\circ g_{3}

holds for some
w,t\in\mathbb{M}
. We know that
g_{1}
and
g_{3}
are both semilinear over
\mathbb{E}
with the same associated field automorphism since
Y
is semilinear over
\mathbb{E}
. So we can write
g_{1}=x^{p^{i}}\circ h_{1}
and
g_{3}=x^{p^{i}}\circ h_{3}
for
\mathbb{E}
-linear mappings
h_{1}
and
h_{3}
, and some
i
where
0\leq i\leq e-1
.

So, for all
v\in\mathbb{M}
there exist some
w,t\in\mathbb{M}
such that

x^{p^{-i}}\circ f_{2}\circ(x^{r}v+Axv^{r})=(x^{q}w+xw^{q})\circ h_{1}+B(x^{q}t+xt^{q})\circ h_{3}

And by Lemma
4.5
,
x^{p^{-i}}\circ f_{2}=0
and so
f_{2}=0
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose that for all
u\in\mathbb{M}
there exist some
w,t\in\mathbb{M}
such that

(e+f\circ x^{r})\circ(x^{q}u+xu^{q})=(x^{r}t+Axt^{r})\circ g+(Ax^{r}w+xw^{r})\circ h

holds for
\mathbb{E}
-linear functions
e,f,g,h
. Then,
h=0
.

Proof.

We write all
\mathbb{E}
-linear functions as linearized polynomials, e.g.
e=\sum e_{i}x^{q^{i}}
and the same for
f,g,h
which then have coefficients
f_{i},g_{i},h_{i}
. Comparing the monomials,
we see that for all
i
,

e_{i-1}u^{q^{i-1}}+e_{i}u^{q^{i+1}}=At^{r}g_{i}+w^{r}h_{i}

(5.1)

and

f_{i-1}u^{rq^{i-1}}+f_{i}u^{rq^{i+1}}=tg_{i}^{r}+Awh_{i}^{r}

must hold. Taking the latter equation to the
r
-th power yields

f_{i-1}^{r}u^{q^{i+1}}+f_{i}^{r}u^{q^{i+3}}=t^{r}g_{i}^{q^{2}}+A^{r}w^{r}h_{i}^{q^{2}}.

(5.2)

We now compare the powers of the monomials in Eqs. (
5.1
) and (
5.2
). We have to distinguish (for each
i
) the following different cases that can occur:

Case 1:

g_{i}=0
and
h_{i}=0
: Then clearly
e_{i-1}=e_{i}=f_{i-1}=f_{i}=0
is necessary.

Case 2:

g_{i}=0
and
h_{i}\neq 0
: Then,
w^{r}=Cu^{q^{i+1}}
for some constant
C
and
e_{i-1}=f_{i}=0
.

Case 3:

g_{i}\neq 0
and
h_{i}=0
: Similarly,
t^{r}=Cu^{q^{i+1}}
for some constant
C
and
e_{i-1}=f_{i}=0
.

Case 4:

g_{i}\neq 0
and
h_{i}\neq 0
: We first check if Eqs. (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) are linearly dependent. If they are,then

\frac{g_{i}^{q^{2}-1}}{A}=A^{r}h_{i}^{q^{2}-1},

or equivalently

{\left(\frac{g_{i}}{h_{i}}\right)}^{r^{2}-1}=A^{r+1}.

So
A
must be a square, which is impossible, so we know that Eqs. (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) are linearly independent. Then, clearly,
t^{r}
and
w^{r}
can both be written as trinomials
C_{1}u^{q^{i-1}}+C_{2}u^{q^{i+1}}+C_{3}u^{q^{i+3}}
. Here, we consider
t,w
as linearized polynomials in
u
.

We now show that
e_{i}=f_{i}=0
for all indices
i
, implying
e=f=0
.

If Case 1 holds for some index
i_{0}
then
e_{i_{0}}=f_{i_{0}}=0
is immediate.

Assume now Case 2 holds for some index
i_{0}
, so
w^{r}=Cu^{q^{i_{0}+1}}
for some constant
C
and
f_{i_{0}}=0
. Then we have Case 1 or Case 3 for
i_{0}+1
(note for instance that Case 4 is impossible since
Cu^{q^{i_{0}+1}}
is not of the form described in Case 4). In both cases
e_{i_{0}+1-1}=e{i_{0}}=0
, so we have
e_{i_{0}}=f_{i_{0}}=0
as claimed.

If Case 3 holds for some index
i_{0}
, an identical argument as in Case 2 yields
e_{i_{0}}=f_{i_{0}}=0
.

If Case 4 occurs for some index
i_{0}
, this immediately leads to Case 1 for index
i_{0}+1
, which also leads to
e_{i_{0}}=f_{i_{0}}=0
. We conclude that
e_{i}=f_{i}=0
for all indices
i
.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

Suppose
(X,Y)\in\Aut(\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{S}_{q,A,B}))
is an automorphism. Then
f_{3}=0
.

Proof.

Consider the monomials in
x
of Eq. (
2.2
) for
v=0
. Then, for all
u\in\mathbb{M}
,

f_{3}\circ(x^{q}u+xu^{q})=(x^{r}t+Axt^{r})\circ g_{1}+(Ax^{r}w+xw^{r})\circ g_{3}

(5.3)

holds for some
w,t\in\mathbb{M}
. Note that
g_{1},g_{3}
are semilinear over
\mathbb{E}
with same associated field automorphism, say
x\mapsto x^{p^{i}}
and
f_{3}
is semilinear over
\mathbb{D}
. So we can rewrite Eq. (
5.3
) as

f_{3}\circ x^{p^{-i}}\circ(x^{q}u+xu^{q})=(x^{r}t+Axt^{r})\circ g_{1}^{\prime}+(Ax^{r}w+xw^{r})\circ g_{3}^{\prime}

(5.4)

where
g_{1}^{\prime}
and
g_{3}^{\prime}
are the
\mathbb{E}
-linear mappings associated to the semilinear mappings
g_{1},g_{3}
. Note that the right hand side of Eq. (
5.4
) is
\mathbb{D}
-linear, so
f_{3}\circ x^{p^{-i}}
is necessarily
\mathbb{D}
-linear as well. Since
\mathbb{E}
is a degree 2 extension of
\mathbb{D}
, we can write
f_{3}\circ x^{p^{-i}}=e+f\circ x^{r}
where
e,f
are
\mathbb{E}
-linear. By Lemma
5.2
, we get

, proving our claim.
∎

Propositions
5.1
and
5.3
together thus show that

is in diagonal form which was the goal of this section.

6
Putting everything together: The proof of Theorem
1.5

We are now ready to prove the main theorem of this work, Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
(X,Y)\in\Aut(\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{S}_{q,A,B}))
.
By Propositions
5.1
and
5.3
,
X
is in diagonal form. Then by Theorem
4.9
, all subfunctions are monomials. By Proposition
3.1
,
Y
is then in diagonal or anti-diagonal form. These two cases are handled in Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
. Note that if
A^{2}
is not an
(r-1)
-st power, then
A^{p^{i}\pm 1}
also cannot be an
(r-1)
-st power, except
A^{p^{0}-1}
. So in this case, all autotopisms are from Theorem
3.2
with
i=0
. If
A^{2}
is an
(r-1)
-st power, then
A^{p^{i}+1}
is an
(r-1)-st
power if and only if
A^{p^{i}-1}
is, so we can for each admissible
i
exactly
2(p^{m}-1)(p^{e}-1)
autotopisms, with
(p^{m}-1)(p^{e}-1)
each from Theorem
3.2
and
3.3
.
∎

7
Conclusion

We have determined the autotopism group of the family of semifields found in
[
4
]
, which is equivalent to the collineation group of the corresponding projective plane as well as the automorphism group of the corresponding rank-metric code/spread set. This, in particular, makes it much easier to decide if a given semifield is isotopic to these semifields since isotopic semifields have conjugate autotopism groups (see e.g.
[
4
, Lemma 5.1.]
). For instance, if we find that some semifield
\mathbb{S}^{\prime}
has a subgroup that is not isomorphic to a subgroup of the group described in Theorem
1.5
, we know that
\mathbb{S}^{\prime}
is not isotopic to the semifields of the Göloğlu-Kölsch family investigated here.

As a simple example, the autotopism group of the twisted fields is known
[
1
]
, and a simple inspection shows that the respective autotopism groups are non-isomorphic, and as such the generalized twisted fields and the semifields we investigated in this paper are never isotopic. Many similar results of this type can be found.

We also want to highlight that a long standing conjecture is that autotopism groups of proper finite semifields are always solvable (see
[
5
, Chapter VIII., Section 6]
). This is equivalent to the collineation group of semifield planes being solvable (note that this conjecture is false in general for tranlation planes, with for instance the Hall planes giving a counterexample
[
5
]
). There is not much evidence in favor of this conjecture, outside of some explicit examples of semifields with solvable autotopism group, some structural results listed in
[
5
]
, and the result that
A_{5}
cannot appear as a subgroup of the autotopism group of a semifield
[
9
]
. Theorem
1.5
gives a new large family of semifields with known autotopism group, and as such gives more empirical evidence in favor of the conjecture. Indeed, our case is particularly important and interesting because the autotopism group is much larger than the subgroup generated by the nuclei, which was not the case in the previously known examples
[
3
,
6
]
. The nuclei, of course, are always solvable subgroups, so it seems natural to expect possible counterexamples to the conjecture coming from semifields with small nuclei.

For future work, it would be beneficial to explicitly determine the autotopism groups of more semifields to make the isotopism question easier to handle. In particular, some recent large and unifying constructions of semifields have recently been found
[
8
,
11
]
and determining their automorphism groups would be very desirable.
