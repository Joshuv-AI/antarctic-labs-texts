Acid-enhanced drinkable hydrogel co-delivering riboflavin-loaded nanozymes for orchestrated inflammation-immune modulation and redox homeostasis in colitis therapy
Authors: Wenting Pan, Ying Song, Yuchen Wang, Sakandar Abbas, YiChao Jing, Haibin Huang, Yuanzhe Li, Wang Ma, Jianguo Feng, Huan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional inflammatory bowel disease therapies are limited by inefficient colon-targeted delivery, systemic side effects, and a failure to rectify the underlying oxidative stress and immune dysregulation. Riboflavin (RAF) possesses ideal antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties for colitis therapy but suffers from rapid proximal absorption. While acid-degradable metal-organic framework (MOF) nanozymes can co-deliver RAF and catalytically eliminate reactive oxygen species (ROS), they lack colon-retentive capacity. To overcome these barriers, we engineered an acid-enhanced, drinkable hydrogel for the coordinated delivery of riboflavin-loaded Pt-MOF nanozymes (RAF@Pt-ZIF@CS, RPZCS). This food-grade sodium alginate system undergoes a drinkable sol-to-mucoadhesive gel transition triggered specifically by the acidic colonic microenvironment (pH 5.0–6.0). The subsequent acid-etching of the Pt-MOF framework enables spatiotemporally coupled burst release of RAF and Pt ions. In a DSS-induced colitis model, RPZCS demonstrated superior lesion retention and exerted comprehensive therapeutic effects. It robustly scavenged ROS via the SCAD-DJ-1–KEAP1–Nrf2 pathway, suppressed pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage polarization, rebalanced the Treg/Th17 immune axis and pro-/anti-inflammatory cytokines, enhanced mucosal/epithelial barrier integrity and restore gut microbiota. Furthermore, RPZCS favorably remodeled the gut microbiota structure. A 14-day repeated-dose toxicity study confirmed its “drinkable-grade” safety. Collectively, this acid-responsive hydrogel platform achieves orchestrated inflammation-immune modulation and redox homeostasis, offering a safe, effective, and highly compliant oral strategy for targeted colitis therapy.Graphical Abstract RPZCS, an acid‑enhanced drinkable hydrogel, undergoes colonic microenvironment‑triggered sol‑to‑mucoadhesive gel transition for targeted delivery of riboflavin and Pt ions, scavenges reactive oxygen species via the SCAD‑DJ‑1–KEAP1–Nrf2 pathway, rectifies immune imbalance, restores intestinal barrier integrity and gut microbiota, thereby alleviating DSS‑induced colitis. Introduction

Despite their widespread use, conventional therapeutics for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), such as 5-aminosalicylic acid, corticosteroids, and biologics, suffer from significant drawbacks that limit their efficacy and safety [1–3]. A primary issue is inefficient topical delivery: after oral ingestion, more than 80% of the drug is typically absorbed or degraded before reaching the colon, resulting in sub-therapeutic concentrations at the disease site [4–6]. Furthermore, systemic drug exposure is associated with serious adverse events, including myelosuppression and opportunistic infections, warranting black box warnings for some agents [7, 8]. Perhaps most fundamentally, these therapies are predominantly suppressive; they block inflammatory signals without addressing or reversing the self-perpetuating cycle of oxidative stress and gut microbiota imbalance that drives chronic inflammation [9, 10]. This failure to resolve the core pathophysiology contributes to high relapse rates, with 40% to 50% of patients experiencing disease flare-ups within one year of stopping treatment [11, 12]. Hence, the development of an oral, colon-targeted, and conditionally responsive delivery platform that selectively releases therapeutics in the inflamed colon remains an urgent unmet need in IBD management.

Riboflavin (RAF) is an essential vitamin that plays a pivotal role in maintaining cellular antioxidant defenses and immunomodulatory balance [13]. As an indispensable cofactor for various redox enzymes, it plays a direct role in maintaining intracellular antioxidant homeostasis [14, 15]. Furthermore, its active metabolite, flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), promotes the differentiation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and suppresses overactive Th17 immune responses, thereby helping to reshape intestinal immune homeostasis [16]. However, the high-water solubility of RAF leads to its complete absorption in the jejunum within 1–2 h following oral administration, resulting in insufficient luminal concentrations in the colon to exert local antioxidant and immunomodulatory effects. Although high-dose systemic administration (> 50 mg kg− 1) is not severely toxic, it causes bothersome side effects such as bright yellow urine and photosensitive dermatitis [15, 17]. Thus, achieving accurate delivery of RAF to the inflamed colonic mucosa, which is characterized by an acidic environment (pH 5.0–6.0), and facilitating its pulsed release at the lesion site presents a significant challenge.

Recently, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) employing Pt4+/Ru4+ nodes or 2-methylimidazole linkers have been shown to possess SOD- and CAT-like cascade activity, continuously eliminating reactive oxygen species (ROS) at inflammatory sites with high H2O2 [18, 19]. Unlike inorganic nanozymes, the metal–ligand coordination bonds of MOFs undergo “acid-etching degradation” at pH 5.0–6.0 [20], enabling: (1) framework collapse with synchronized release of metal ions and ligands; (2) burst release of encapsulated small-molecule drugs (e.g., RAF) as the pores disintegrate, generating an “acid-triggered, spatio-temporally coupled” delivery behavior [21]. Nevertheless, MOF nanozymes (50–200 nm) are easily destroyed by gastric acid and rapidly absorbed in the small intestine, with a residence time < 30 min, making it difficult to form a sustained protective film on the inflamed colonic surface [22]. Hence, a “drinkable, acid-responsive, in-situ gelling, and locally-retentive” carrier is urgently needed to safely ferry the RAF@ZIF complex to the colonic lesions.

Here we constructed a food-grade sodium-alginate (SA) reversible cross-linking system: under physiological conditions it remains a low-viscosity sol that can be swallowed with drinking water; at gastric pH 1.5-2.0, Ca2+ is rapidly released and forms stable Ca2+-SA chelation; upon entry into the colon, the local inflammatory acidic micro-environment (pH 5.0–6.0) triggers competitive H⁺/Ca2+ ligand exchange, causing instantaneous gelation and formation of a 3D reticular protective film on the mucosal surface. The gel network encapsulates “riboflavin-loaded Pt-MOF nanozyme” (RAF@Pt-ZIF, RPZ). When the local pH further drops to ~ 5.0, the Pt-MOF metal-ligand bonds break, synchronously releasing RAF and Ca2+, achieving an “acid-threshold-dual-amplification” ROS scavenging and immunomodulatory effect.

This study aims to develop an acid-responsive, drinkable hydrogel that delivers riboflavin-loaded MOF nanozymes. This hydrogel elucidates the in vivo sol-gel transition kinetics and the synergistic release profile of RAF/MOF triggered by acid (Scheme 1). Furthermore, it verifies lesion retention, eliminates ROS via the SCAD-DJ-1-KEAP-1-Nrf2 pathway, suppresses M1 macrophage polarization, rebalances inflammatory cytokine levels and the Treg/Th17 cell, enhances the integrity of the intestinal mucus barrier and epithelial barrier, and remodels the gut microbiota, demonstrating a comprehensive regulatory effect on the intestinal microenvironment in a DSS-induced murine colitis model. Additionally, it assesses “drinkable-grade” safety through a 14-day repeated-dose toxicity study in mice. The RAF@Pt-ZIF@CS (RPZCS) demonstrated therapeutic potential for colitis as a drinkable, gel-like formulation that reduces ROS and modulates immune responses. This offers a safe, cost-effective, and highly compliant solution for patients with IBD.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of RPZ

The core functional component (RPZ) was synthesized in two steps. First, Pt-ZIF nanocomposites were prepared via a one-pot co-precipitation method. An aqueous solution containing Zn(NO3)2, PtCl4, and CTAB was rapidly mixed with a 2-methylimidazole solution, followed by reduction with NaBH4. The product was collected, washed, and dried to obtain Pt-ZIF powder. Subsequently, RAF was loaded onto Pt-ZIF using a solvent-assisted grinding method with anhydrous methanol, yielding the final RPZ composite after washing and vacuum drying.

The morphology and composition of the synthesized materials were systematically characterized. TEM images (Fig. 1A-B) revealed that pristine Pt existed as uniform nanoparticles, which were successfully encapsulated within a zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIF) to form well-defined tetrahedral Pt-ZIF crystals. The SEM image of RPZ (Fig. 1C) confirmed the preservation of this crystalline morphology post-modification, with a slightly roughened surface indicative of RAF association. EDS elemental mapping (Fig. 1D) demonstrated the homogeneous distribution of C, N, O, Zn, and Pt throughout the RPZ composite, confirming the successful integration of components.

The crystal structure and chemical interactions were further investigated. XRD patterns (Fig. 1E) confirmed that RPZ retained the characteristic crystal structure of ZIF-8 without phase impurities, indicating the RAF loading process did not destroy the host framework’s integrity. FT-IR spectroscopy (Fig. 1F) provided insight into the interfacial interactions. The spectrum of RPZ retained key ZIF skeleton vibrations while showing new peaks characteristic of RAF molecules. A noticeable redshift and broadening of the O-H stretching band suggested the formation of hydrogen bonds between RAF’s functional groups (N–H, C = O) and the ZIF surface. These observations, alongside attenuated ZIF peak intensities in the composite, support that RAF is loaded primarily through non-covalent interactions like hydrogen bonding and potential π-π stacking.

XPS analysis revealed the surface chemical states. The survey spectrum (Fig. 1G) confirmed the presence of Pt, Zn, N, O, and C. The high-resolution Pt 4f spectrum (Fig. 1H) could be fitted with doublets corresponding to metallic Pt0 and oxidized Pt4+ species, with the dominant Pt0 state being crucial for the subsequent nanozyme activities.

Multienzyme-mimicking activities of Pt-ZIF and RPZ

The enzymatic antioxidant activities of the nanozymes, central to their therapeutic function, were evaluated. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 2A, both Pt-ZIF and RPZ were designed to mimic SOD and CAT. The SOD-like activity, measured via the cytochrome C reduction assay, showed that Pt-ZIF effectively catalyzed the dismutation of superoxide anion (O2•−) (Fig. 2B). The activity of RPZ remained comparable, indicating that RAF loading did not compromise the intrinsic SOD-mimicking sites. Conversely, the CAT-like activity assay (Fig. 2C) revealed a different trend: pristine Pt-ZIF demonstrated strong H2O2 decomposition activity, which was moderately reduced in RPZ. This decrease may be attributed to the partial occupation of surface-active sites or the altered local environment by RAF molecules. The broad-spectrum radical scavenging capacity was assessed using ABTS and DPPH assays. As shown in Fig. 2D and E, both materials exhibited free radical scavenging ability. Notably, RPZ showed enhanced scavenging rates compared to Pt-ZIF alone. This improvement is directly attributed to the intrinsic antioxidant activity of the loaded riboflavin, which acts synergistically with the nanozyme, endowing the composite with a more robust antioxidant defense system.

Preparation and characterization of the RPZCS

The RPZ nanozyme was incorporated into an alginate-based hydrogel to form an injectable therapeutic platform RPZCS. The overall synthesis scheme is depicted in Fig. 3A. The gelation mechanism, driven by ionic crosslinking between Ca2+ pre-loaded on RPZ and the guluronate blocks of sodium alginate, resulted in the gradual formation of a homogeneous network over 12–24 h, as illustrated in Fig. 3B. The physicochemical properties of the formed hydrogel were characterized. Rheological analysis (Fig. 3C) revealed pronounced shear-thinning behavior, where the hydrogel’s viscosity decreased significantly with increasing shear rate. This property is essential for injectability, allowing easy extrusion through a syringe followed by rapid structural recovery. SEM imaging of the freeze-dried hydrogel (Fig. 3D) revealed an interconnected, porous three-dimensional microstructure. The corresponding elemental mapping confirmed the uniform distribution of the RPZ nanozyme (via C, N, O, Zn, Pt signals) throughout the calcium alginate matrix, indicating successful and homogeneous integration. The release profile of Pt ions from the hydrogel was evaluated to assess its potential for sustained delivery. As shown in Fig. 3E, the RPZCS hydrogel exhibited a slow and sustained release of Pt4+ over time. This controlled release kinetics is advantageous for maintaining long-term therapeutic activity at the target site while minimizing risks associated with burst release. Notably, a similar sustained release behavior was also observed in PBS containing 10% fetal bovine serum, further confirming the stability and controlled release performance of the system under physiological conditions (Fig. S1).

To further elucidate the environmental responsiveness of the system, its behavior under simulated gastric conditions was investigated. Upon exposure to simulated gastric fluid, a time-dependent gelation process was observed, indicating in situ reconstruction of the hydrogel network (Fig. S2A). This behavior is attributed to proton-triggered dissociation of Ca2+ species pre-associated with RPZ, resulting in the release of free Ca2+ ions, which subsequently re-crosslink alginate chains via ionic coordination. Such dynamic Ca2+-mediated re-crosslinking accounts for the dissociation-reformation behavior of the calcium-alginate network under acidic conditions.

In addition, the in vitro release profiles of Pt were evaluated in both SGF and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF). The results showed a relatively limited Pt release in SGF, which can be attributed to the rapid formation of a crosslinked hydrogel network under acidic conditions (Fig. S2B-C). In contrast, a significantly enhanced and time-dependent Pt release was observed in SIF, indicating sustained and gradual drug release under intestinal conditions.

Following gastric gelation, the hydrogel is expected to undergo gradual network relaxation and partial degradation in the intestinal environment, enabling sustained release of the encapsulated nanozyme during gastrointestinal transit. This pH-responsive gelation-degradation process collectively contributes to enhanced retention and improved bioavailability.

In summary, we successfully synthesized and characterized the RPZ nanozyme, which exhibits multi-enzyme mimicking and enhanced antioxidant activities. This functional nanozyme was further integrated into an alginate-based hydrogel, creating an injectable platform (RPZCS) with desirable rheological properties, a porous structure, and sustained release capability. This composite system holds promise for applications requiring localized and prolonged oxidative stress modulation.

RPZCS exhibits superior intestinal retention and efficacy in alleviating UC compared with RPZ

We further quantified Pt levels in intestinal tissues at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post-administration by ICP-MS, demonstrating prolonged gastrointestinal retention of RPZCS accompanied by sustained in vivo release behavior (Fig. S3). Similarly, in vivo fluorescence imaging results confirmed that RPZCS exhibited longer retention in the body compared to PRZ (Fig. 4A-B). We also assessed the therapeutic effectiveness of RPZ and RPZCS in DSS-induced colitis model. Mice in the DSS group showed a marked loss of body weight and significant colon length reduction when compared to the control group. Treatment with both RPZ and RPZCS alleviated weight loss and restored colon length in DSS-treated mice; however, the improvement achieved by RPZCS was more pronounced than that of RPZ (Fig. 4C-E). Splenomegaly is one of the hallmarks of ulcerative colitis. The spleen-to-body weight ratio showed that DSS-induced colitis resulted in significant splenic enlargement [23]. Notably, RPZ failed to mitigate splenomegaly in colitic mice, whereas RPZCS effectively reduced spleen enlargement (Fig. 4F). Histopathological analysis revealed that the DSS group had many of the classic pathological features of colitis, including large ulcerative lesions, extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, destruction of villi and crypts, and severe disruption of the intestinal epithelial architecture. Conversely, RPZCS treatment considerably decreased inflammatory infiltration, alleviated mucosal edema, decreased ulcer area, improved epithelial injury, and restored villus morphology and goblet cell numbers. Although RPZ intervention produced partial improvement, its protective effect was notably weaker than that of RPZCS (Fig. 4G-H). Collectively, these findings indicate that RPZCS exhibits a longer intestinal residence time and provides superior therapeutic efficacy compared with RPZ. Both materials ameliorated symptoms of DSS-induced colitis, but RPZCS demonstrated significantly enhanced anti-inflammatory activity, likely due to its prolonged retention in the intestinal tract.

RPZCS improved intestinal mucus barrier homeostasis in DSS-Induced UC

The mucus layer and mucus-secreting goblet cells play essential roles in maintaining the integrity and function of the intestinal mucosal barrier. In ulcerative colitis, both the mucus layer and goblet cell function are severely compromised, leading to impaired barrier integrity that further exacerbates intestinal inflammation and tissue damage [24, 25].

To assess whether RPZCS protects the mucus barrier in DSS-induced colitis, we first evaluated goblet cell function. Alcian blue–PAS staining demonstrated that DSS treatment markedly reduced goblet cell numbers and significantly diminished mucin secretion (Fig. 5A-B). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed a substantial decrease in Muc2, the major mucin secreted by goblet cells, following DSS exposure (Fig. 5C-D).

Agr2, a goblet cell–specific molecular chaperone involved in regulating the unfolded protein response, was also significantly downregulated in DSS-treated mice (Fig. 5E-F). Consistent with impaired mucin secretion, the mucus layer on the colonic villus surface was markedly thinned or even absent in the DSS group (Fig. 5G-H). Collectively, these findings indicate that DSS disrupts the intestinal mucus barrier by damaging goblet cell function. In contrast, RPZCS treatment markedly improved goblet cell function in colitic mice. The RPZCS/DSS group exhibited restored goblet cell numbers, enhanced secretion of Muc2, and a marked recovery of mucus-layer thickness compared with the DSS group (Fig. 5A-H). We further examined the transcriptional levels of key genes related to goblet cell maturation and differentiation, including Muc2, Spdef and Klf4. Compared with the DSS group, RPZCS significantly upregulated all three genes in colon (Fig. 5I-K). Together, these results demonstrate that RPZCS effectively regulates goblet cell function and restores intestinal mucus-barrier homeostasis in DSS-induced ulcerative colitis.

RPZCS alleviates epithelial barrier damage in DSS-induced UC

Thinning or loss of the mucus layer exposes intestinal epithelial cells to harmful luminal substances, leading to direct epithelial injury. Aberrant apoptosis of epithelial cells is a key pathological feature of ulcerative colitis and plays a critical role in its onset and progression. Excessive apoptosis not only disrupts barrier integrity and contributes to ulcer formation but also intensifies immune responses and promotes microbial dysbiosis, thereby exacerbating disease severity [26, 27].

TUNEL staining revealed a marked increase in TUNEL-positive cells in the colons of DSS-treated mice, indicating substantial epithelial apoptosis. In contrast, RPZCS treatment significantly reduced the number of TUNEL-positive epithelial cells (Fig. 6A-B). Cleaved-caspase-3, a central executioner of apoptosis that directly drives programmed cell death, is widely recognized as a key regulator in various pathological conditions. Immunohistochemistry showed that cleaved-caspase-3 expression was significantly elevated in the DSS group compared with the NC group, whereas RPZCS intervention markedly suppressed cleaved caspase-3 expression (Fig. 6C-D). During the process of apoptosis, pro-apoptotic Bax and anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 serve as two key regulatory proteins that indirectly control the activation of caspase-3. We analyzed the Bcl-2/Bax ratio in mice from each group using Western blot. The results showed that, compared with healthy control mice, the Bcl-2/Bax ratio was decreased in the DSS group, while RPZCS treatment significantly increased the Bcl-2/Bax ratio (Fig. 3.4 E–F). These data indicate that RPZCS may inhibit caspase-3 activation by modulating the balance of Bcl-2 and Bax expression, representing one of the important pathways through which it alleviates intestinal epithelial cell apoptosis (Fig. 6E-F). Ki67 immunohistochemistry results demonstrated that RPZCS significantly increased Ki67 levels in colon tissues of colitis mice, indicating that RPZCS promoted epithelial cell proliferation in the inflamed colon, thereby contributing to the repair of intestinal epithelial damage (Fig. 6G-H).

Tight junction proteins are essential structural components that maintain epithelial barrier integrity [23, 28]. To assess whether RPZCS preserves tight junction organization, we examined the distribution and expression of Claudin-1, Occludin, and ZO-1 by immunofluorescence. DSS treatment disrupted the localization of these tight junction proteins and reduced their expression levels, indicating compromised epithelial barrier structure. Notably, RPZCS markedly restored the normal distribution and expression of Claudin-1, Occludin, and ZO-1 in colitic mice (Fig. 6I-N). Together, these results demonstrate that RPZCS effectively protects against epithelial barrier dysfunction and alleviates epithelial injury in DSS-induced ulcerative colitis.

RPZCS ameliorates immune dysregulation in mice with UC

The disruption of the intestinal mucosal and epithelial barrier acts as a direct trigger and central driver for immune barrier dysfunction, which induces persistent immune dysregulation, further disrupts the gut barrier, and forms a vicious cycle [29, 30]. We firstly measured the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ) and the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in colon tissues. Compared with the control group, DSS-treated mice exhibited increased levels of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, along with decreased IL-10. After the RPZCS intervention, the levels of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ in the colon were significantly reduced, while IL-10 was elevated (Fig. 7A). Increased intestinal mucosal permeability can lead to excessive translocation of LPS into the systemic circulation (Fig. 8B). This not only aggravates local intestinal inflammation but also triggers systemic inflammatory responses. We therefore examined serum cytokine levels. Results showed that DSS treatment elevated serum IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, while reducing IL-10. Following RPZCS administration, systemic inflammation was alleviated, with trends consistent with those observed in the colon (Fig. 7C).

In the DSS-induced colitis model, elevated MPO levels served as a marker for neutrophil migration and infiltration into the colon, indicating excessive activation of innate immunity (particularly neutrophils) [31]. Treatment with RPZCS markedly suppressed colonic MPO levels, suggesting that RPZCS inhibits the recruitment and infiltration of neutrophils (Fig. 7D). Classically activated M1 macrophages highly express iNOS upon stimulation, thereby amplifying the inflammatory response. We detected increased iNOS expression in the colon of colitis mice, and RPZCS treatment notably reduced iNOS levels, indicating that RPZCS suppresses the infiltration or activation of M1 macrophages (Fig. 7E-F). Consistently, immunofluorescence results showed a significant increase in CD68⁺CD86⁺ M1 macrophages in colitis mice. However, after treatment with RPZCS, the number of CD68⁺CD86⁺ M1 macrophages in colonic tissue was significantly reduced compared to that in untreated colitis mice (Fig. 7G-H). In the UC model, damaged intestinal epithelial cells and activated M1 macrophages release substantial amounts of IL-6 and IL-1β. These cytokines collectively create a local microenvironment that drives the differentiation of naïve T cells into pro-inflammatory RORγt⁺ Th17 cells while suppressing the function of anti-inflammatory Foxp3⁺ Treg cells, leading to severe disruption of immune homeostasis. Our results also demonstrated an increase in RORγt⁺ Th17 cells and a decrease in Foxp3⁺ Treg cells in the colon of DSS-treated mice, a phenomenon that was reversed by RPZCS (Fig. 7I-L). In summary, these findings indicate that RPZCS corrects the imbalance in the colonic immune barrier in mice with colitis and alleviates inflammation, which may represent a core mechanism underlying its therapeutic effects.

RPZCS attenuates oxidative stress in mice with UC

Oxidative stress in the colon is closely associated with ulcerative colitis. It not only plays a key role in the pathogenesis of UC but also serves as an important indicator for evaluating disease activity and prognosis. Excessive accumulation of ROS leads to oxidative stress imbalance, resulting in cellular damage, inflammatory responses, and tissue dysfunction, thereby worsening UC symptoms [32, 33]. Therefore, we measured oxidative stress–related parameters to assess whether RPZCS could alleviate oxidative stress in the colons of UC mice (Fig. 8A). The results showed that, compared with the normal group, ROS levels were significantly elevated in the DSS group, whereas RPZCS treatment markedly reduced ROS accumulation in colitic mice.

Antioxidant enzymes play a crucial role in maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis during oxidative stress. These enzymes protect cells from excessive oxidative damage and regulate ROS levels, participating in multiple pathological processes. We assessed the activities of SOD and GPX4 in the colon tissues. DSS-treated mice showed significantly reduced SOD and GPX4 activities compared with healthy controls. In contrast, RPZCS treatment significantly restored SOD and GPX4 activities in the colons of UC mice (Fig. 8B-C). Taken together, these findings indicate that RPZCS helps maintain redox balance and effectively reduces oxidative stress in the colon tissues of mice with ulcerative colitis.

Riboflavin can be converted into FAD in vivo, which subsequently promotes the activation of SCAD expression. SCAD plays a critical role in oxidative stress regulation, and its dysfunction leads to elevated ROS production [34]. Compared with healthy controls, SCAD expression in the colons of DSS-treated mice was markedly decreased, whereas RPZCS treatment significantly restored SCAD expression (Fig. 8D-E).

DJ-1 is an important cytoprotective protein involved in antioxidative defense and maintenance of cellular homeostasis. Upon ROS stimulation, DJ-1 enhances cellular resistance to oxidative stress by regulating Keap-1 and Nrf2. Our results showed that DJ-1 expression was reduced in the DSS group compared with healthy mice, while RPZCS administration significantly increased DJ-1 expression in the colonic tissues of UC mice (Fig. 8F-G).

Immunofluorescence analysis further revealed that RPZCS treatment decreased KEAP-1 levels and markedly increased Nrf2 expression in the colons of DSS-induced mice (Fig. 8H-J). Collectively, these findings indicate that RPZCS activates SCAD and subsequently stimulates the DJ-1–KEAP-1–Nrf2 signaling cascade, thereby alleviating ulcerative colitis in mice.

RPZCS modulates the gut microbiota

Gut microbiota homeostasis is a crucial component of the intestinal microenvironment and plays a significant role in the onset and progression of intestinal inflammation [35]. Therefore, we further examined the effect of RPZCS on the gut microbiota of mice with colitis. Microbial diversity is an important indicator of intestinal health. Alpha diversity reflects both species richness and community complexity. The α-diversity (represented by PD_whole_tree index) of the gut microbiota was significantly increased in the RPZCS–treated colitis mice (Fig. 9A), suggesting that RPZCS enhances phylogenetic diversity in DSS-induced colitis. β-Diversity reflects intergroup differences in microbial community composition. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) revealed a clear separation between the DSS and RPZCS–treated groups, indicating distinct microbial community structures, demonstrating that RPZCS significantly alters the gut microbial composition of colitic mice (Fig. 9B).

To further characterize these changes, we examined microbiota profiles at both the phylum and genus levels. At the phylum level, microbial communities in both groups were dominated by Bacteroidota, Firmicutes, Campilobacterota, and Proteobacteria, although relative abundances differed between groups. RPZCS increased the abundance of Bacteroidota while reducing Campilobacterota and Proteobacteria in DSS-treated mice. Firmicutes levels did not differ significantly between groups (Fig. 9C-G).

At the genus level, RPZCS markedly elevated the abundance of anti-inflammatory taxa, including unclassified_f_Muribaculaceae, Alistipes, and Parabacteroides. Although Bacteroides showed an increasing trend, the difference was not statistically significant. Whereas pro-inflammatory genera such as Escherichia–Shigella and Helicobacter were significantly reduced. Notably, Escherichia–Shigella and Helicobacter, two major endotoxin-producing genera within Proteobacteria, are key contributors to elevated luminal and systemic LPS levels in inflammatory bowel disease (Fig. 9H-N).

LEfSe analysis further identified distinct microbial biomarkers between groups. Harmful taxa—including Helicobacter, Helicobacteraceae, Campylobacteria, Enterobacterales, Escherichia–Shigella, and Gammaproteobacteria, were enriched in DSS mice. In contrast, RPZCS treatment enriched beneficial bacteria such as Bacteroidales, unclassified_f_Muribaculaceae, Rikenellaceae, and Alistipes (Fig. 9O). These findings indicate that RPZCS effectively restores microbial homeostasis by increasing beneficial taxa and reducing pathogenic bacteria, thereby mitigating DSS-induced inflammation and tissue injury.

RPZCS exhibits a favorable biosafety profile

A comprehensive biosafety evaluation of RPZCS was conducted. After two weeks of continuous oral administration, no significant differences in body weight were observed between the RPZCS-treated and control mice (Fig. 10A). Biochemical analysis further confirmed that the administration of RPZCS did not induce any alterations in hepatic or renal function markers (Fig. 10B–E). Histopathological examination of major organs via H&E staining revealed no discernible lesions or abnormalities in the RPZCS-treated group compared to the controls (Fig. 10F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that oral administration of RPZCS is well-tolerated and exhibits no apparent systemic toxicity.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully developed an acid-responsive, drinkable hydrogel (RPZCS) for the colonic co-delivery of riboflavin and Pt-MOF nanozymes to address the limitations of conventional IBD therapies. The food-grade sodium alginate-based system exhibited a specific sol-to-gel transition in the acidic colonic microenvironment, followed by acid-triggered degradation of the MOF framework, enabling spatiotemporally controlled release of RAF and Pt ions. In a DSS-induced colitis model, RPZCS demonstrated effective lesion retention and multi-faceted therapeutic effects, including robust ROS scavenging via the SCAD-DJ-1-KEAP1-Nrf2 pathway, suppression of M1 macrophage polarization, restoration of the Treg/Th17 immune balance, and reinforcement of mucosal and epithelial barrier integrity. Additionally, RPZCS favorably modulated the gut microbiota composition. A 14-day repeated-dose toxicity study confirmed its favorable safety profile. Collectively, these findings indicate that RPZCS represents a safe, effective, and patient-friendly oral therapeutic strategy for targeted colitis treatment through coordinated regulation of oxidative stress, immune homeostasis, and intestinal barrier function.

Methods

Preparation of Pt-ZIF

Pt-ZIF was synthesized via a one-pot co-precipitation method. Firstly, 362 mg of Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, 54.7 mg of PtCl3, and 10 mg of CTAB were dissolved in 18 mL of ultrapure water. The mixture was sonicated for 5 min to ensure complete dissolution and was denoted as solution (A) Separately, 5.65 g of 2-methylimidazole was dissolved in 82 mL of ultrapure water to obtain solution (B) At room temperature, solution A was rapidly poured into solution B under vigorous stirring. The resulting mixture was allowed to react with continuous stirring for 1 h. Subsequently, 2 mL of a freshly prepared 1 mol/L NaBH4 aqueous solution was added dropwise into the reaction mixture, which was then stirred for another 30 min. After the reaction, the obtained suspension was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min to collect the product. The precipitate was washed three times with 50% (v/v) ethanol aqueous solution to remove unreacted precursors and by-products. Finally, the washed product was dried overnight in a vacuum oven at 60 °C, yielding a grayish-black powder of Pt-ZIF material, which was stored in a desiccator for further use.

Preparation of RPZ

Drug loading was performed using a solvent-assisted grinding method. Precisely 50 mg of riboflavin and 200 mg of the as-prepared Pt-ZIF material were weighed and placed together in a clean agate mortar. While grinding continuously, a small amount of anhydrous methanol was added dropwise to form a homogeneous slurry. Grinding was continued for 30 min to ensure thorough dispersion and in-situ loading of the drug into the pores and onto the surface of Pt-ZIF. After grinding, the slurry was washed several times with a minimal amount of methanol, followed by centrifugation to remove any free drug physically adsorbed on the material’s surface. The washed solid was then dried under vacuum in the dark at 40 ℃ for 6 h, obtaining the riboflavin-loaded Pt-ZIF composite material RPZ.

Preparation of RPZCS

RPZ (2 mg) was incubated with 1 mL of CaCl2 solution (1 M) under stirring at room temperature for 12 h to facilitate Ca2+ loading. The mixture was then centrifuged (8000 rpm, 10 min), and the resulting pellet was washed repeatedly with deionized water to eliminate any non-adsorbed calcium ions. The washed precipitate was subsequently dispersed in 3 mL of sodium alginate solution (3% w/v) and mixed homogeneously. Upon standing, the slow release of Ca2+ ions from the material triggered ionic cross-linking with the alginate chains, leading to the gradual gelation and formation of the RPZCS hydrogel.

Multienzyme-mimicking activities assay of Pt-ZIF and RPZ

The CAT-like and SOD-like activities, as well as the ABTS⁺• and DPPH• radical scavenging capacities of Pt-ZIF and RPZ, were evaluated. Detailed experimental procedures are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Evaluation of therapeutic efficacy in DSS-induced colitis model

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhengzhou University (ZZU-LAC20231222 [15]). Female C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions and randomly divided into four groups: negative control (NC), DSS-treated (DSS), RPZ/DSS (RPZ/DSS), and RPZCS/DSS (RPZCS/DSS). Colitis was induced by 3% (w/v) DSS in drinking water for 6 days. The treatment groups received the corresponding formulations (100 mg/kg) orally once daily. Body weight was recorded daily, and at sacrifice, colon length and spleen weight were measured. Colonic tissues and fecal samples were collected to evaluate histopathology, intestinal barrier integrity, mucus layer preservation, immune cell infiltration, inflammatory cytokine levels, oxidative stress markers, apoptosis, and gut microbiota composition. Comprehensive experimental procedures for all animal studies are detailed in the Supplementary material.
