Zinc flux in skin: Identity and function of metallothionein 1–positive cells in skin homeostasis
Authors: Sofia Brito, Jeong-Yun Kim, Sung Kweon Cho, Gwangbin Lee, Ji-Hwan Park, Jiyoon Kim, Bum-Ho Bin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZinc is essential for skin homeostasis, yet the identity and function of zinc-rich epidermal cells remain poorly defined. MT1 (metallothionein-1), a zinc-responsive protein, is commonly used as an intracellular zinc marker, but whether MT1+ cells represent epidermal stem cells or specialized populations is unclear. In this study, we generated an Mt1-P2A-EGFP-2A-CreERT knock-in mouse model enabling lineage tracing of MT1+ cells. Reporter activation confirmed zinc-dependent expression and revealed that MT1+ cells are broadly distributed across the interfollicular epidermis, lower junctional zone, and hair follicles from embryonic stages through adulthood. Single-cell RNA sequencing identified MT1+ cells as a distinct cluster with minimal overlap with established epidermal stem cell markers, indicating that MT1 does not define a canonical stem cell population but instead marks a heterogeneous subset of epidermal cells. Lineage tracing during homeostasis showed contributions to multiple epidermal lineages, including sebaceous glands and isthmus regions. However, during wound healing, MT1+ cells exhibited limited migration or participation in regeneration, whereas MT1− cells primarily drove epidermal repair and follicle reformation. These findings suggest that MT1 marks zinc-associated epidermal cells involved in maintenance rather than regeneration and reveals heterogeneity within zinc-responsive epidermal populations, highlighting distinct roles for MT1+ and MT1− cells in skin physiology and repair.Keywords: Hair follicle, Metallothionein, MT1, Skin, Wound

Introduction

As a major zinc reservoir, the skin relies on zinc for homeostasis and regeneration, yet it remains particularly vulnerable to deficiency, which is linked to various skin diseases (Al-Khafaji et al, 2022; Bin et al, 2017; Brito et al, 2020). Zinc, the third most abundant essential mineral after iron and calcium, supports numerous enzymes, transcription factors, and structural proteins, with >10% of human proteins being zinc bound. Given that zinc tends to accumulate in undifferentiated progenitors, zinc-rich cells are often presumed to represent skin stem cells (Bin et al, 2018; Chen et al, 2024; Sahibdad et al, 2023). Despite its importance in the skin, the precise dynamics and stemness of zinc-rich cells remain poorly understood.

MT1 (metallothionein 1) is a zinc-dependent molecule that functions as a zinc storage molecule for cellular homeostasis and is widely used as a marker for intracellular zinc levels (Bin et al, 2017; Heuchel et al, 1994). Elevated intracellular zinc enhances its binding to metal regulatory transcription factor 1, translocation to the nucleus, and binding to metal-responsive elements in DNA, triggering transcription, most notably of MT1. Although previous studies have linked MT1 expression to zinc-rich cells localized primarily in hair follicles (Karasawa et al, 1991), the broader distribution and specific functions of MT1+ cells in the skin remain unclear. Understanding the roles of these cells could provide critical insights into how zinc homeostasis influences skin regeneration and disease.

Results and Discussion

MT1 identifies a distinct epidermal cell population enriched in hair follicles and basal compartments

We first generated a knock-in mouse model, Mt1-P2A-EGFP-2A-CreERT (Figure 1a and b), which was crossed with a R26R reporter mouse (Figure 1c). This allowed lineage tracing of MT1+ cells at specific time points through tamoxifen (TAM)-induced Cre activation and subsequent X-galactosidase staining. Immunofluorescence staining of mouse skin sections demonstrated clear colocalization between endogenous MT1 and EGFP reporter signals, confirming faithful expression of the knock-in allele in vivo (Figure 1d). Primary keratinocytes isolated from Mt1-EGFP mice and treated with zinc sulphate showed increased EGFP intensity in a zinc dose-dependent manner (Figure 1e).

Three days after TAM treatment, LacZ staining and EGFP fluorescence colocalized in hair follicles (Figure 2a), confirming the proper function of our Cre system. Although most epidermal cells remained unlabeled, LacZ-positive cells were detected in the placode region as early as embryonic day 12.5 (Figure 2b). In adult mice, these cells were distributed throughout the epidermis, predominantly in the interfollicular epidermis and lower junctional zone (Figure 2b and c). To assess long-term cell fate under conditions of relative epithelial quiescence, lineage tracing experiments were therefore initiated during telogen at postnatal day 55. LacZ-labeled cells examined at postnatal day 58 were mainly located in the interfollicular epidermis, lower junctional zone, and hair germ (Figure 2c [i]). These cells subsequently proliferated and contributed to multiple epidermal lineages, including the sebaceous gland and isthmus, as confirmed by histological analysis and quantitative assessment (Figure 2c and d). High-magnification images (Figure 2c [ii]) revealed that LacZ+ cells resided primarily in the basal and lower suprabasal layers of the interfollicular epidermis and junctional zone. However, only a few hair follicles were entirely composed of LacZ-labeled cells (Figure 2e). Mt1 reporter activity was further increased during anagen, and analysis across the hair cycle demonstrated a higher abundance of Mt1+ cells during anagen than during telogen (Figure 2f and g).

To further characterize MT1+ cells, we analyzed published single-cell RNA-sequencing datasets of mouse epidermis and compared Mt1 expression with established epidermal stem cell markers (Clevers, 2015; Joost et al, 2016). Clustering analysis using the Seurat package identified 12 transcriptionally distinct cell populations (Figure 3a [i]). Mt1 expression was primarily enriched in cluster 3, indicating a nonuniform distribution across epidermal cell states (Figure 3a [ii]). Moreover, feature plots revealed the distribution of Mt1, Trp63, Itga6, K14, and K15 across epidermal cell populations (Figure 3b [i–v]). Mt1-expressing cells showed minimal overlap with classical epidermal stem cell markers, including Lrig1, Prdm1, Lgr5, Lgr6, Gli1, and Cd34 (Figure 3c [i–vi]). Likewise, overlap with broadly expressed basal/stem-associated markers such as Trp63 (Figure 3b [ii]) and Itga6 (Figure 3b [iii]) was limited. In contrast, Mt1 expression partially overlapped with K14 (Figure 3b [iv]) and K15 (Figure 3a [v]) populations, consistent with their localization in basal epidermal compartments and hair follicles (Figures 2a and c and 3d). Differential expression analysis comparing Mt1-high and Mt1-negative cells identified multiple significantly altered genes (Figure 3e). Pathway enrichment analysis further revealed associations with epithelial differentiation, ion homeostasis, inflammatory response, and oxidative stress–related processes (Figure 3f). Together, these findings suggest that MT1 does not specifically mark canonical epidermal stem cell populations but rather identifies a distinct and heterogeneous subset of basal-associated epidermal cells with unique spatial localization and potentially specialized functional properties.

MT1− cells predominantly mediate epidermal wound regeneration

Given zinc’s importance in skin homeostasis (Miller et al, 1965), we examined the contribution of MT1+ cells to wound healing. Lineage tracing was initiated at the second telogen (postnatal day 55), followed by a 1-cm2 dorsal skin incision, and MT1+ cells were tracked over a period of 4 months. Despite their broad expression in the basal layer and hair follicles, MT1+ cells showed minimal contribution to wound regeneration. Two days after wounding, epidermal regeneration had begun (Figure 4a), but LacZ-labeled cells were scarce in the scab and newly formed epidermis, remaining predominantly in pre-existing hair follicles and the residual epidermis (Figure 4b). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the early regenerated epidermis was composed predominantly of MT1− cells (Figure 4c). Seven days after woudning, MT1+ cells at the wound margin maintained a scattered distribution similar to their steady-state pattern (Figure 4c and d) and did not migrate toward the wound area. This lack of migration suggests that MT1+ cells do not actively participate in the regeneration process. Notably, epidermal regeneration occurred irregularly, creating hyperproliferative regions (Figure 4d, red dotted area), which were largely populated by MT1− cells. The epidermis eventually closed, but the regenerated dermis exhibited low-density collagen fibers, and no new hair follicles were observed (Figure 4d, lower panel). This indicates that MT1− cells rather than MT1+ cells are primarily responsible for epidermal proliferation and wound closure. To determine whether MT1− cells remained predominant in the later stages of wound healing, we examined the wound site 49 days after wounding, when it was no longer macroscopically visible. Cross-sectional analysis revealed distinguishable wound margins and a distinct triangular region lacking hair follicles, filled with loosely packed collagen fibers (Wallace et al., 2017) (Figure 4e). Although MT1+ cells were detected in hair follicles and interfollicular epidermis outside the wound, they were absent in the regenerated epidermis. One hundred and thirty-three days after wounding, hair follicles had reappeared in the wound area, containing Mt1+ cells (Figure 4f). Given the TAM labeling prior to injury, the presence of LacZ-positive cells confirmed that these newly formed follicles originated from MT1− cells that had initially repopulated the wound site. Because CreERT and EGFP are driven by the endogenous Mt1 promoter, initial reporter activation is zinc responsive. However, LacZ expression in lineage-tracing experiments reflects historical Mt1 promoter activity at the time of TAM induction and does not report ongoing zinc levels.

Our findings reveal that MT1 marks a diverse and heterogeneous population of epidermal cells rather than serving as a specific marker for stem cells. Although MT1+ cells are primarily involved in hair follicle maintenance, their limited contribution to wound healing suggests a more specialized role in skin physiology. Instead, the regenerative process is predominantly driven by MT1− cells, highlighting distinct functional roles within the epidermal microenvironment. This distinction between MT1+ and MT1− cells highlights the complexity of zinc homeostasis in skin regeneration. Future studies exploring the specific functions of MT1+ cells and their interactions with MT1− cells could improve therapeutic approaches targeting zinc-related pathways in skin.

Materials and Methods

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use and conducted following the Guidelines for Ethical Conduct in the Care and Use of Nonhuman Animals in Research. Mt1iCre/iCre mice, generated on a C57BL/6N background, were generated using Biocytogen (number EGE-CYX-038) by inserting the P2A-EGFP-2A-iCreERT2 cassette into exon3 of Mt1 (Figure 1a). Two P2A sequences allow the expression of nature form of Mt1 and EGFP proteins under the same Mt1 promoter. The knock-in allele was confirmed by genotyping with 2 different primers (Figure 1a and b). These mice expressed TAM-inducible Cre recombinase. Mt1iCre/iCre mice were mated with R26Rfl/fl (The Jackson Laboratory, number 003474) reporter mice to generate Mt1iCre/WTR26Rfl/+ (Mt1-iCre) mice to characterize the role of Mt1+ cells and their lineages (Figure 1c). All offspring produced in this mouse model appeared healthy and fertile with no signs of discomfort. PCR confirmed genotyping of Mt1 and R26R, and the primers used are listed in Figure 1. To activate Cre-recombinase, Mt1-iCre mice were injected intraperitoneally with 200 μl of TAM (10 mg/ml in sunflower oil) (Snippert et al, 2010). Mouse skin was collected 3 days later to chase Mt1+ cells showing-galactosidase activity by TAM.

Flow cytometry

Primary keratinocytes were isolated and cultured from neonatal Mt1-EGFP mice as described previously (Bin et al, 2017). Cells were treated with increasing concentrations of zinc sulphate (0, 1, 5, or 10 mM) for 24 hours. Cytometry analysis was performed as previously described (Brito et al, 2025; Gerena-López et al, 2004). After treatment, cells were washed with PBS and harvested for flow cytometry analysis. EGFP fluorescence intensity was measured using a flow cytometer under identical acquisition settings for all conditions. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software, and EGFP intensity distributions were compared across zinc concentrations. Representative histograms from 1 of 3 independent experiments are shown.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Subtyping was performed as previously described (Joost et al, 2016). Briefly, cells were classified into three subtypes on the basis of their normalized log-scale expression values of Mt1. Cells with expression values >3 (Mt1 > 3) were defined as Mt1+ cells, those with values equal to 0 (Mt1 = 0) were defined as Mt1− cells, and cells with expression values > 0 and ≤ 3 (0 < Mt1 ≤ 3) were not used. For downstream analysis, we focused on comparing the Mt1+ (Mt1 > 3) with Mt1− (Mt1 = 0) groups.

Wound healing evaluation

Three days after treatment with TAM, Mt1-iCre mice were anesthetized with avertin solution (350 mg/kg) containing 80 mg 2,2,2-tribromoethanol (number T48402, Sigma-Aldrich), 50 μl t-amyl alcohol (number PHR1667, Sigma-Aldrich), and 4 ml saline (Cleancle, JW Pharmaceutical, Seoul, Republic of Korea) on the basis of a previously reported method (Huang et al, 2021). After shaving the dorsal areas of the mice, excisional wound injury models were prepared according to the protocol described by Brubaker et al (2015). After wounding, mice were killed on days 2, 7, 49, and 133 after wounding, and the dorsal skin was harvested.

β-Galactosidase staining

The dissected skin samples were fixed with a fixative solution (2% paraformaldehyde and 0.2% glutaraldehyde in PBS) for 30 minutes at room temperature and washed 2 times for 20 minutes with a rinse buffer (2 mM magnesium chloride, 0.1% NP-40 in PBS). The tissues were immersed in an X-galactosidase solution (1 mg/ml X-galactosidase, 5 mM potassium ferricyanide, 5 mM potassium ferrocyanide in rinse buffer) and stained for 24 hours at 37 °C.

Histological analysis

Samples were prepared as slides by paraffinization, sectioning, and counterstaining (eosin or hematoxylin and eosin staining) at the Korea Non-Clinical Technology Solution Center (Gyeonggi-do, South Korea). Macroscopic and microscopic images of all slides were acquired for histological analysis using an Olympus BX53 microscope equipped with a DP74 microscope digital camera.

Statistical analysis

Two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used to analyze differences between 2 groups. p-values <.05 were considered statistically significant.
