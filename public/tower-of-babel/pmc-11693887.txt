Impact of mandatory environmental information disclosure on the capital cost: Evidence from listed companies in China
Authors: Lulu Tian, Yanyuan Shen, Meng Du
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Mandatory environmental information disclosure is characterized by formal environmental regulation and that the content of the disclosure promotes public participation and oversight in the environmental field. This paper studies the impact of mandatory environmental information disclosure (EID) enforcement in China on corporate capital cost using a difference-in-difference model. The results indicate that mandatory EID increases debt capital cost, but has no significant impact on equity capital cost, which differs from mainstream research that suggest EID can reduce capital cost. Mechanism tests reveal that mandatory EID raises debt capital cost by deteriorating the financial performance of enterprises. Further research finds that the mandatory EID also reduce enterprise's total debt amount and restructure debt structure by increasing short-term liability and decreasing long-term liability. In addition, the increasing effect of mandatory EID on the debt capital cost is more pronounced among non-SOE corporations, small-scale corporations and recession corporations. This study provides new empirical evidence for the impact effectiveness of mandatory EID and suggests different research views in the related field.

Introduction

As global environmental issues become increasingly severe, enterprises face growing external environmental pressures from various stakeholders, including shareholders, creditors, governments, consumers, and social groups. In addition to bearing the costs associated with their environmental impact, enterprises are also required to disclose their environmental practices to the public. Consequently, corporate environmental information disclosure (EID) has become increasingly important. EID refers to the public disclosure of information related to the natural environment, resource utilization, and environmental protection [1], which can effectively reduce information asymmetry between enterprises and stakeholders, helping businesses establish a positive public image and promote sustainable development [2,3].

In global capital markets, EID is viewed as part of responsible investment, with investors increasingly prioritizing companies' environmental performance [[4], [5], [6]]. Voluntary environmental information disclosure (EID) is typically undertaken by environmentally responsible companies, with the aim of demonstrating their environmental efforts, enhancing their image in capital markets, and attracting more investors and customers [7]. However, companies with poor environmental performance often refrain from voluntarily disclosing such information, making it difficult for the public to monitor their environmental impact [8,9]. As a result, voluntary EID plays a limited role in driving green transformation and sustainable development. To address this limitation, an increasing number of countries and regions have introduced mandatory EID policies, requiring companies to disclose their environmental performance [10]. Mandatory EID not only serves as a formal environmental regulatory tool but also fosters public participation and oversight, functioning as a form of informal regulation.

In China, the rapid growth of the economy has exacerbated environmental issues. To tackle these challenges, the Chinese government has intensified its regulation of corporate environmental information disclosure in recent years. The introduction of the mandatory EID policy in 2010 marked a shift from voluntary to mandatory disclosure in China. This policy specifically requires companies in heavily polluting industries to regularly disclose their environmental information, subjecting them to public and market scrutiny. This policy provides a unique context for studying the impact of mandatory EID on corporate capital costs.

Existing literature shows that EID can reduce capital costs by mitigating information asymmetry [11,12]. By disclosing environmental performance, investors and creditors can better assess corporate risk and future cash flows, which lowers the required rate of return and alleviates financing pressure [[13], [14], [15]]. Despite the established connection between EID and capital costs, most studies focus on either equity [12] or debt markets [5,6], overlooking the potentially different effects of EID on both types of capital costs. Moreover, much of the existing research emphasizes the impact of voluntary EID, especially in developed markets, with little attention given to the role of mandatory EID in emerging markets such as China, where debt financing dominates. In China, companies rely more on debt markets than equity markets, making the impact of debt capital costs more pronounced. This paper, therefore, aims to examine the effect of the 2010 mandatory EID policy on both debt and equity capital costs in Chinese enterprises.

To achieve this goal, the study uses the quasi-natural experiment of the 2010 mandatory EID policy in China and applies the difference-in-differences (DID) method to evaluate its impact on capital costs. The results indicate that mandatory EID increases debt capital costs but has no significant impact on equity capital costs, which differs from previous studies suggesting that EID reduces capital costs. A mechanism test reveals that mandatory EID raises debt capital costs by worsening financial performance. Furthermore, the study shows that mandatory EID encourages companies to reduce their overall debt and restructure their debt by increasing short-term liabilities and reducing long-term liabilities. The impact of mandatory EID on debt capital costs is more pronounced among non-SOE companies, small-scale companies, and firms in recession. This paper provides new empirical evidence on the implementation of mandatory EID and offers fresh perspectives for related research.

The paper contributes to the literature in several ways. First, it focuses on the impact of mandatory EID on capital costs in emerging markets. Although previous research has distinguished between mandatory and voluntary EID, most studies focus on developed markets. There is limited research on the effect of EID in emerging markets, particularly in China. Thus, examining the relationship between EID and capital costs in an emerging market like China is essential [5]. Existing literature has not fully explored the differences between mandatory and voluntary EID in the Chinese context [[16], [17], [18]]. This paper clarifies these differences and uses the implementation of China's mandatory EID policy as a quasi-natural experiment, combined with an analysis of financial costs, to reveal the unique relationship between mandatory EID and capital costs.

Secondly, this paper utilizes data from listed companies to analyze the impact of mandatory Environmental Information Disclosure (EID) on capital costs from multiple dimensions, including financial performance, heterogeneity, and debt structure. Existing studies on environmental disclosure in China largely focus on specific industries [12] or particular types of firms, such as heavily polluting enterprises [19], firms facing high water resource risks [20] or energy enterprises [21]. In contrast, this paper analyzes the entire sample of listed companies and explores the mediating role of financial performance in the relationship between EID and capital costs. Additionally, it investigates the heterogeneous effects of mandatory EID on various types of companies, distinguishing between state-owned enterprises (SOEs) and non-SOEs, while also examining the role of firm size and lifecycle in determining capital costs. Through this subgroup analysis, the paper demonstrates that mandatory EID significantly increases debt capital costs for small firms, non-SOEs, and firms in recession.

Third, this paper explores the effects of mandatory EID on both equity and debt capital costs in China. Most studies focus exclusively on either debt [5,6] or equity capital costs [12], with few addressing both. This paper reveals that mandatory EID has a more significant impact on debt capital costs, particularly for non-SOEs, small firms, and firms in recession, while its effect on equity capital costs is not significant. The paper explains the non-significance of equity capital costs using shareholder dividends as an indicator. This finding contrasts with the general conclusion in existing literature that EID lowers capital costs. The paper suggests that the difference arises from previous research's failure to differentiate between mandatory and voluntary EID and insufficient attention to the unique characteristics of China's capital market. While enterprises in developed markets often rely on equity financing, leading most research to focus on equity capital costs, Chinese companies are more dependent on debt financing [22], making it necessary to investigate the relationship between EID and debt capital costs. This detailed work contributes significantly to the research in this field.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides a literature review and research hypotheses. Section 3 explains the data, variables, and model used in the study. Section 4 presents the empirical results. Section 5 explores the channel through which EID affects debt capital costs. Section 6 extends the analysis to changes in corporate debt and enterprise heterogeneity. Finally, Section 7 presents the conclusions and policy implications.

Literature review and research hypothesis

Literature review

EID has significantly influence on corporation's environmental performance. Some hold positive view that EID could reduce pollution emissions [23], improve the environment quality [24], play an energy-saving effect [16] and spur corporate innovation [25]. Authors in Ref. [26] investigate the causal impact of EID on environmental efficiency at the city level and find that environmental efficiency improves relative to the sample average. Treating the release of the Pollutant Information Transparency Index (PITI) in China as a quasi-natural experiment, authors in Ref. [27] find EID could promote enterprises to reduce emissions. However, others have argued that EID yields limited or no influence on pollution abatement [28]. There is a complex effect of EID on environmental performance.

In addition to environmental performance, EID could also affect other aspects of enterprises. Authors in Ref. [29] find the financial performance of corporation increases with the level of disclosure. In other words, EID positively (directly) influences corporation's financial performance [30,31]. However, some confirm the significant negative impact of the amount of EID on financial performance [32]. Similarly [33], points government EID will increase green total factor productivity. In addition, EID could also promote green technology [34]. Specifically, it improves the overall green innovation performance of the extractive corporations, but this improvement is mainly driven by the utility model patents rather than green invention patents with higher innovation levels [35]. Therefore, most businesses use EID as a strategic tool because it could enhance social reputation [36], improving price efficiency [37], attracting more investment [38] and reducing firm-specific risks [39].

Some literatures investigate the factors that affect the quality of disclosure. Since EID is value-relevant and that corporations may disclose such information in a strategic fashion to improve image, authors in Ref. [40] demonstrate organizational image and reputation have a significant impact on both the act and the content of EID. Size and the nature of business activities also could determine the disclosure level [41]. In addition, profitability, gender diversity and board independence are the important and critical factors that influence EID External environmental factors also have influence on the disclosure level, such as government regulation policies [25], legal requirements and pressures of society and economic factors [42], news media coverage of their environmental exposure [43].

Even though there is a lot of literature that studies the effect of corporation's EID behavior and its influencing factors from various aspects, the literature specifically focusing on mandatory EID is relatively sparse, and there is no literature points out the differences between them. Given that it is not of the same nature as voluntary disclosure, it is necessary to study the impact of mandatory EID on enterprises. The influence of non-financial information disclosure on the capital cost will determine what kind of effective environmental protection behaviors companies adopt, and also provide experience for the government's next step of environmental regulation. In the next part of this paper, we will discuss the effect in detail.

Research hypothesis

However, previous studies are primarily based on voluntary disclosure. When enterprises are forced to disclose environmental information, the results may be more complex. On the one hand, mandatory EID will directly send signal to investors that the enterprises are under special supervision, and their developmental prospects are unclear. These risk signals will make investors demand higher rates of return, which will ultimately increase the corporation's capital cost finally. On the other hand, mandatory EID makes enterprises face greater environmental pressure and spend more on the environment, which could increase their financial expenditure and deteriorate their financial performance. Poor financial performance will indirectly increase the capital cost. Therefore, mandatory EID is different from voluntary EID and may bring opposite results. We use the mandatory environmental disclosure policy implemented in China as a natural experiment to study its impact on the debt capital cost and the equity capital cost. Due to the different political background and economic development level in China compared with other mainstream developed countries, and the immaturity of the capital market, different analytical conclusions will be drawn.

First, we analyze mandatory EID's influence on equity capital cost in China. The lack of transparency and imperfect supervision mechanism of EID in China's capital market can lead to difficulty in obtaining reliable and accurate environmental performance information of corporations, which in turn affects the relevance of EID as a factor in investment decisions. In addition, China's capital market is dominated by small and medium-sized individual investors, with institutional investors accounting for a relatively small proportion. Although the structure of the stock market has been undergoing positive changes in recent years, with the proportion of shares held by institutional investors and transactions steadily increasing, individual investors still accounted for about 60 % of transactions in 2022. Most small- and medium-sized individual investors lack expertise in identifying corporation's environmental risks. Therefore, a culture of value investing has not really taken shape in China's capital market. Individual investors do not really care about corporate environmental information reporting or environmental behavior, with their primary concern being the short-term return on their stocks. They may not pay much attention to EID, and the impact of mandatory EID on equity capital cost may not be significant. Therefore, the effect of mandatory EID on equity capital cost is not significant in China. Hypothesis 1is proposed.Hypothesis 1Mandatory EID has no significant influence on equity capital cost.

Mandatory EID has no significant influence on equity capital cost.

Second, we analyze mandatory EID's influence on debt capital cost. In China, most enterprises have a single financing option and rely mainly on the traditional financing channel of bank loans. As professional institutional investors, banks have more professional ability to collect and analyze the environmental protection behaviors of enterprises than small and medium-sized investors. In addition, the Chinese government requires banks to develop green finance and give more credit to clean enterprises, so banks' loans to enterprises with bad environmental conditions are tightening year by year. Hence, banks have a relatively strict audit on enterprises when lending. The implementation of mandatory EID will send a bad signal about the corporate and further encourage banks, as creditors, to demand higher financing costs for enterprises. Therefore, the impact of mandatory EID on the debt capital cost will be significant. Hypothesis 2 is proposed.Hypothesis 2Mandatory EID increases debt capital cost.

Mandatory EID increases debt capital cost.

Mechanism analysis

There is a strong correlation between EID and financial performance. On the one hand, scholars have confirmed that high-quality EID can improve the reputation and image of enterprises, which can further enhance their financial performance [[13], [14], [15]]. On the other hand, some studies have found that the costs of improving the quality of EID may reduce productive investment and competitiveness, thus leading to a decline in financial performance [44]. In either case, EID can impact the financial performance of enterprises.

Additionally, there is a strong correlation between financial performance and capital cost. Financial performance reflects a company's profitability and solvency, and investors use this information to predict future cash flow and evaluate credit risk [11]. Poor financial performance can increase potential risks, which would lead to higher return rate demands from investors. Therefore, corporate financial performance is an important factor through which mandatory EID can affect the debt capital cost. This paper proposes the following hypothesis 3.Hypothesis 3Mandatory EID increases the debt capital cost by negatively impacting financial performance.

Mandatory EID increases the debt capital cost by negatively impacting financial performance.

Research design

Data

Our research is based on Chinese companies listed on A-share market from 2006 to 2020. The data comes from two sources. First, the mandatory EID policy is from the Guidelines on Environmental Information Disclosure for Listed Companies (short for Guidelines) published by the State Environmental Protection Bureau (SEPB, now the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of People's Republic of China) in 2010. Only companies in 16 heavy polluting industries are forced to disclose. Second, companies' basic characteristics and financial variables data come from the China Stock Market and Accounting Research (CSMAR) database. To make data more suitable for our research, some were excluded according to the following criteria: (1) Considering industry characteristics, enterprises from financial industry and insurance industry are deleted; (2) The samples with serious data missing; (3) A few listed companies that voluntarily disclosed environmental information. The continuous variables were winsorized at the 1 % and 99 % levels to exclude the influence of extreme values. The final available data consists of 14,833 firm-year observations from Chinese listed companies.

Variables

Explained variable

The explained variable being examined in the paper is capital cost, which is divided into equity capital cost (ECC) and debt capital cost (DCC). There are two basic methods used to measure equity capital cost, which are the internal rate of return method and the risk compensation method. The internal rate of return method is based on the discount of future earnings and uses forecast data to calculate, such as the Gordon model, GLS model, OJ model, and PEG model. The risk compensation method typically uses historical rate of return to measure the expected future rate of return, including the capital asset pricing model (CAPM) and the Fama-French three-factor model. Due to the relatively higher systemic risk in the Chinese stock market compared to developed countries, Authors in Ref. [45] suggested that the ex-ante costs of equity capital are better than the ex-post costs of equity capital and the PEG method can properly capture the influence of each risk factor. Moreover, since the PEG method does not require predicting dividend payments and only needs the predicted value of two periods of EPS, it is a precise and convenient method. Hence, we use it to calculate equity capital cost.(1)ECC=100∗(epst+2−epst+1)/ptWhere ECC represents equity capital cost, epst+2 is the average forecast of net earnings per share of the enterprise i in period t+2, epst+1 is the average forecast of net earnings per share of enterprise i in period t+1, and pt is the stock price at the end of period t.

The debt capital cost is typically measured using debt ratings and the average yield to maturity of debt. However, due to the absence of an authoritative debt rating agency in China and the difficulty in obtaining the average yield to maturity, we adopt the method proposed by Liu (2019) and use the ratio of interest expense to the average total debt of the corporate to measure the debt capital cost.(2)DCC=(interest+commission+other_FE)/TlWhere DCC is the indicator of debt capital cost, interest is the enterprise's interest expenditures, commission is the commission expenses, other_FE is other financial expenses of the enterprise, and Tl is total debt of the enterprise.

Core explanatory variable

The variable manEID serves as the key explanatory factor, representing the implementation of the mandatory EID policy introduced in China in 2010. It is a dummy variable with a value of 1 if the policy was implemented in 16 heavily polluting industries after the year 2009; otherwise, it is 0.

Control variables

According to Refs. [25,33,46], control variables that could influence capital cost are also including. These variables are: (1) firm size (size), measured as total assets; (2) firm age (age); (3) leverage ratio (lev); (4) book value per share (bvps); (5) earnings per share (eps); (6) return on assets (roa); and (7) total debt ratio (tdr). We add 1 to each of the above variables and then take the natural logarithm. Econometric model

Given that mandatory EID policy was implemented in 16 heavily polluting industries in China starting from 2010, we employ a difference-in-differences (DID) methodology to evaluate the long-term dynamic effects of this policy on capital cost, the model is presented below:(3)Costijt=α+β∗manEIDijt+φXijt+δi+σt+εijtWhere Costijt represents equity capital cost and debt equity capital cost. i represents the company, j represents the industry, t represents time, α is the intercept. manEIDijt is a dummy variable represent mandatory EID, and β is the influence effect of mandatory EID on capital cost. Xijt is a vector of the control variables. In addition, the firm fixed effect (δi) and the year fixed effect (σt) are controlled respectively. εijt is an error term.

Influence on debt capital cost

We observe that the impact of mandatory EID on debt capital cost (DCC) is more significant and stable than the impact on equity capital cost (ECC). Specifically, in column (4) of Table 2, the coefficient of manEID is 0.002 and significantly positive, indicating that mandatory EID increases the DCC. This result remains consistent and significant in columns (5) and (6), where we add more control variables and different fixed effects. Robustness tests

Since the impact on equity capital cost is not significant, we aim to test the robustness of our benchmark regression results regarding the impact of mandatory EID on debt capital cost in this section. We use three main different methods for robustness testing. Firstly, we check the parallel trend assumption, which is a prerequisite that must be met for the DID model to be used. Secondly, we use Propensity Score Matching method to match the samples before implementing the DID model to reduce the selection bias problem. Lastly, we provide two counterfactual evidences by conducting placebo test to support our results.

PSM-DID method

To improve sample matching, it is necessary to create a comparison control group using the Propensity Score Matching method, which is based on a model that predicts the likelihood of participating in the treatment. Specifically, we use the explanatory variables from model (1) as predictors and estimate the probability of implementing mandatory EID using a logit model. The resulting probability scores reflect the industry tendencies. We then match treatment and control groups with similar scores based on their industry. After obtaining the matched samples, we perform the baseline DID regression model (1) again with different fixed effects. The results are shown in columns (2) and (3) of Table 5. We observe that the coefficients of the manEID are significantly positive.

We substituted manEID_1 for DID in model (1) and conducted regressions with different fixed effects. The results in column (4) and (5) of Table 5 show that the coefficients of manEID_1 are not significant, indicating that the impact of mandatory EID is not due to chance.

Mechanism test

Previous analysis pointed investors pay more attention to corporate profitability and financial indicators when making credit decisions. They demand higher returns for companies with poorer performance or lower corporate value. If limited funds are invested in environmental protection activities under mandatory EID, then the increased EID implementation costs will occupy the funds needed for the core business of corporate and increase the risk of withdrawing credit funds. Through analyzing the corporate's performance, especially solvency of enterprises, creditors discover increased potential risks and thus demand higher debt costs. Next, we verify whether mandatory EID increases the debt capital cost by deteriorating corporate financial performance.

We select the cash ratio (CR) and liquidity ratio (LIQR) as short-term financial performance indicators and the interest coverage ratio (ICR) as a long-term financial performance indicator. These can directly reflect the financial performance of enterprises and their solvency ability related to capital cost. We take them as dependent variables in model (1) and regress them respectively. Columns (1)–(3) in Table 7 show that the coefficients of DID are −0.075, −0.003, and −0.087, which means that mandatory EID has a significant negative impact on short- and long-term corporate financial performance. Therefore, mandatory EID deteriorates corporate financial performance, which will lead to the increase of debt capital cost. Corporate's ownership

In different kinds of ownership enterprises of China, the state-owned enterprise (SOE) is a kind of “quasi-government organization”. It has important political relationship with government. Relative to the non-state-owned enterprise (non-SOE), the SOE enjoys more various preferential policies and has incomparable advantages in obtaining government resources, which is one of the typical characteristics of micro enterprises in China.

Here the question is what's the specific difference between enterprises with different ownership type about the debt capital cost brought by mandatory environmental information disclosure? To answer the question, we divide corporations into two subsamples——SOEs and non-SOEs. When the state-owned shares account for 50 % or more in a company, we define it as SOE; otherwise, it is non-SOE. Column (1)–(2) in Table 8 show the results. We could see EID significantly affects non-SOEs and has no significant influence on SOEs. It means mandatory EID only increase the debt capital cost on SOEs. The possible main reason is still related with the nature of disclosure. Mandatory EID policy is dominated by the government, the enterprises closely associated with the government may have opportunity to get lighter regulation or even exempt from regulation. Corporate's size

Considering that corporations of different sizes do not have the same ability to access capital, this paper divides the sample into large, medium, and small subsamples based on size and conducts regressions. Columns (3)–(5) of Table 8 show that corporations with small size bear a higher debt capital cost. One possible explanation is that smaller corporations are less resilient to risk, and creditors are more willing to lend capital to corporations that are large-scale enterprises to secure their own funds. Especially under mandatory EID, they have to pay more costs to obtain financing, so the debt capital cost faced by small corporations under mandatory EID will be higher. However, large-scale corporations are not plagued by this similar problem, and their debt capital cost are not significantly affected by mandatory EID.

Corporate's life cycle

Considering that corporations at different life cycle stages do not face the same cash flows, financing risks, and market competitiveness，this can also have a differential impact on the debt capital cost. Drawing on the cash flow portfolio approach developed by Dickinson (2011), the life cycle stage of the firm is measured from the perspective of the positive and negative combination of net cash flows in the three phases of operation, investment, and financing of the firm, and the sample corporations are categorized into start-up, growth, maturity, and recession. Due to the small number of start-up corporations in listed companies, this paper deletes them and only regresses separately for growth, maturity, and recession stages of corporations. The results are shown in columns (6)–(8) of Table 8. We can see that when a firm is in recession, it faces the highest debt capital cost. The probable reason for this has to do with the fact that during a recession the firm is in a poor state in all indicators and creditors will only borrow if they get a higher rate of return, thus resulting in a higher debt capital cost for the firm. In addition, maturity corporations face a higher debt capital cost compared to growth stage corporations. The possible reason is that maturity companies should have higher environmental awareness and good environmental performance compared to the imperfections and difficulties in environmental protection of growth-stage companies, but mandatory EID for them indicates that maturity companies have failed to act on environmental protection, which destroys the inherent impression of creditors on them, and the irresponsible image makes investors have less confidence in them and demand a higher rate of return on capital, thus the maturity companies face a higher debt capital cost.

Conclusion and policy implications

This study refines the distinction between mandatory and voluntary environmental information disclosure (EID) and examines the impact of mandatory EID enforcement in China on corporate debt and equity capital costs using a difference-in-difference model. The analysis explores corporate financial performance, heterogeneity, and debt structure, yielding more nuanced and comprehensive conclusions compared to previous studies. The results indicate that mandatory EID increases debt capital costs but has no significant impact on equity capital costs, diverging from mainstream research suggesting that EID typically reduces capital costs [5,49]. This discrepancy is largely due to the insufficient focus on the differences between mandatory and voluntary EID and the unique characteristics of China's capital market. Mechanism tests reveal that mandatory EID raises debt capital costs by deteriorating corporate financial performance. Since mandatory EID primarily targets heavily polluting enterprises, policy enforcement inevitably results in negative financial impacts, leading to increased capital costs either directly or indirectly. This study highlights the crucial role of financial performance as a mediating mechanism linking EID and debt capital costs.

Furthermore, the study finds that mandatory EID reduces the total debt amount of corporations and restructures debt by increasing short-term liabilities while decreasing long-term liabilities. This finding provides deeper insights into the effects of mandatory EID on corporate debt financing. Additionally, the analysis of heterogeneity in terms of ownership, life cycle, and firm size reveals that the impact of mandatory EID on debt capital costs is more pronounced among non-SOE corporations, small-scale firms, and recession-phase companies. This study provides new empirical evidence on the effectiveness of mandatory EID and suggests fresh perspectives for further research in the field.

Based on these findings, policymakers should adopt differentiated EID policies, providing tailored financial support to non-SOEs, smaller firms, and those in recession, who are more vulnerable to the financial impacts of mandatory EID. Promoting synergies between environmental regulations and financial markets, such as through green bonds and favorable lending terms, can alleviate financial burdens while ensuring compliance. Continuous monitoring and adjustments to disclosure policies will also be necessary to prevent adverse economic impacts on distressed firms. Future research should explore the global impact of EID across different market maturity levels, examine the long-term effects on corporate reputation and innovation, and investigate how sustainable investing trends might alter the relationship between EID and equity financing. The role of financial institutions in facilitating EID compliance through innovative products should also be explored. These insights will contribute to more integrated environmental and financial policies and sustainable economic development.
