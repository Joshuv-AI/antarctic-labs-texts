The application and progress of AI-based image analysis in tumor organoid research
Authors: Yicheng Xu, Shuai Fan, Yuxin Chen, Wanting Xu, Haodong Lu, Yutong Zhou, Yuetong Liu, Qing Du, Wenyu Wang, Tonghui Yu, Lei Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe development of preclinical models that recapitulate the physiological and pathological features of human tumors remains a central challenge in cancer research. Advances in cell biology have enabled the generation of three-dimensional tumor organoids, which closely mirror patient-specific therapeutic responses and facilitate the study of disease mechanisms. However, the trend of these models necessitates a shift from traditional, invasive analytical methods toward non-invasive, high-throughput imaging approaches. Here, we review the current state of tumor organoid culture and the emerging application of artificial intelligence (AI) in their evaluation. We discuss how AI-driven technologies are revolutionizing the analysis of fluorescence imaging, viability assessments, and dynamic cell tracking, thereby overcoming the limitations of manual interpretation. Finally, we provide a perspective on how integrating deep learning with organoid technology will enhance the precision and efficiency of drug discovery and personalized oncology.

Introduction

Organoids are three-dimensional (3D) models constructed using tissue-specific adult stem cells (ASCs), induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), or embryonic pluripotent stem cells (PSCs). Unlike traditional two-dimensional (2D) cultures, these models recapitulate the complex spatial structure, functions, and biological microenvironmental responses of in vivo organs. While the concept of self-organization without the need for external assistance back to H. V. Wilson’s 1907 discovery of sponge cell reassembly, current organoid research began in 2009 when Hans Clevers and colleagues successfully generated functional intestinal organoids from Lgr5+ ASCs.1,2 Organoid technology has then expanded rapidly, providing ideal platforms for studying cell differentiation, organ development, and tissue formation.

A primary focus of current research is the development of patient-derived tumor organoids (PDOs) for precision oncology. Traditional 2D models and animal models often fail to capture the genetic heterogeneity of human cancers or the complexity of the tumor microenvironment (TME). The TME is a dynamic ecosystem consisting of tumor cells, vascular structures, stromal fibroblasts, and a network of immune cells (e.g., macrophages, natural killer [NK] cells) that collectively drive disease progression.3 Tumor organoids address these limitations by accurately simulating these multicellular interactions and preserving the epigenetic landscape of the original tumor. For instance, DNA methylation profiling has shown that organoids maintain cancer-specific epigenetic signatures distinct from normal tissues, offering a high-fidelity model for mechanism studies.4 Recognizing this potential, major consortiums such as the Human Cancer Models Initiative (HCMI) aim to generate thousands of next-generation cancer models to accelerate clinical drug development. The rapid development of novel technologies has significantly accelerated the construction, functionalization, and application of organoids. For instance, 3D bioprinting enables precise control over spatial cell arrangement, bioink composition, and printing parameters, facilitating the fabrication of highly biomimetic organoid structures and high-throughput production, thereby greatly enhancing their physiological relevance and reproducibility. Functional bioink design guided by materiobiology, including the incorporation of bioactive inorganic materials, neurotrophic factors, or extracellular vesicles, enhances vascularization, innervation, and the microenvironment-mimicking capabilities of organoids, offering new strategies for complex tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

Despite the biological fidelity of organoids, their structural complexity poses a challenge for evaluation. Traditional endpoint assays are often destructive, labor-intensive, and fail to capture longitudinal dynamics. Consequently, there has been a pivotal shift toward high-throughput, non-invasive imaging techniques that preserve physiological integrity while tracking development and drug response.5,6 However, the sheer volume and complexity of data generated by high-content imaging require advanced analytical tools. Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a transformative solution in this space, enabling precise, automated interpretation of organoid phenotypes. Currently, there is a lack of comprehensive reviews bridging the gap between biological modeling and computational analysis. Therefore, this paper provides a detailed overview of the principles of AI in image processing and its innovative applications in the establishment and evaluation of tumor organoids.

Generation, evaluation, and applications of tumor organoids

The generation of tumor organoids marks a significant advancement in cancer research, enabling the in vitro recapitulation of tumor heterogeneity and the complex TME. These 3D models bridge the gap between traditional 2D cultures and in vivo physiology. Their accurate evaluation is paramount, as it determines their utility in downstream applications. However, the very complexity that makes them valuable also poses a challenge for conventional analytical methods, which are often destructive, labor-intensive, and fail to capture longitudinal dynamics. Generation of tumor organoids

Currently, tumor organoid research is primarily conducted through two approaches: one involves culturing PDOs using tumor tissue from patients, while the other utilizes genetically engineered iPSCs to generate organoids. PDOs, cultured directly from patient tumor tissue, are highly valued for preserving the original tumor’s genetic and cellular heterogeneity. The initial tissue processing step is critical; mechanical dissociation is increasingly favored over enzymatic digestion as it better preserves crucial intercellular connections and retains functional tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) within the tissue fragments. This preservation of native immune cells is essential for subsequently modeling the complex interactions within the TME. To address the limitations of manual mechanical dissection, standardized microdissection tools have been developed to enable precise and reproducible tissue fragmentation while maintaining the spatial relationship between tumor cells and immune cells.8,9 Following dissociation, cells are cultured using methods that support 3D growth, such as embedding in a basement membrane matrix or using an air-liquid interface (ALI).10,11,12 The choice of method and the supplementation of culture media with specific growth factors (e.g., FGF, EGF, Noggin) are tailored to the tissue of origin to mimic the physiological stem cell niche.13,14

Recognizing that a mono-culture of tumor cells fails to capture the complexity of the in vivo TME, significant efforts have focused on developing co-culture systems. Co-culture systems have been developed that integrate tumor organoids with other cellular components of the TME, such as immune cells (T cells, macrophages, NK cells) or cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), enabling unprecedented insights into tumor-immune interactions, stromal support mechanisms, and therapeutic resistance.15,16,17,18,19,20 For instance, co-cultures with CAFs have demonstrated that these fibroblasts can promote organoid growth and confer protection against drug-induced damage through direct contact and paracrine signaling, highlighting the critical role of the microenvironment.21

Evaluation of tumor organoids

Organoids, being 3D models, are far more complex than traditional 2D cell cultures. The evaluation of organoid growth involves monitoring key parameters such as organoid quantity, size, dynamic changes, marker expression, and morphological development. Accurate and efficient evaluation and parameter analysis are crucial for ensuring that organoid structures replicate in vivo conditions as closely as possible. The number of organoid growths is an important criterion in research, used to explore the characteristics and growth conditions of organoids. A higher number of generated organoids indicates a greater organoid-forming capability of the starting cells and also reflects the suitability of conditions such as the culture medium and growth factors.22,23 A key characteristic of organoids is the formation of 3D structures, making structural assessment a critical indicator of organoid formation quality, which is essential for exploring cell morphology, cell-to-cell interactions, and accurately replicating organ structures and the TME.24 Maintaining reproducibility across different batches and passages is also crucial, particularly for applications such as drug screening where consistent results are paramount.25

Organoids contain important biomarkers that can be utilized in omics studies to characterize their features and genetic alterations during tumor passage. Numerous studies also employ histological sections to observe organoid characteristics and use immunohistochemistry to detect key tumor biomarkers.26,27,28,29 For more complex co-culture systems, immunofluorescence can be used to analyze the distribution of key immune cells, fibroblasts, and other components within the TME of organoids, thereby exploring the interactions within the critical microenvironment. Additionally, immunostaining is commonly used to observe blood vessels and endothelial cell networks, aiding in the study of vascular structures and angiogenesis.30,31,32 However, these methods are destructive, preventing the longitudinal study of a single organoid’s development or response to treatment. While bright-field microscopy allows for non-invasive, time-lapse monitoring of organoid size and morphology, the manual analysis of these images is subjective, time-consuming, and struggles to capture subtle but important phenotypic changes. This analytical bottleneck creates a pressing need for automated and objective methods, a gap that AI-powered image analysis is uniquely positioned to fill.

Applications of tumor organoids

Tumor organoids have emerged as versatile platforms with transformative applications in cancer research and personalized medicine. They are powerful tools for studying tumor biology and genetics. By combining organoid technology with genome editing tools such as CRISPR-Cas9, researchers can introduce specific mutations into healthy or tumor organoids to study their role in tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis.33 Longitudinal studies of PDOs in culture have also revealed patterns of clonal evolution that mirror those seen in actual patients, providing a window into how tumors adapt and evolve over time.34 The development of complex co-culture systems has enabled their use in immuno-oncology research. These models allow researchers to dissect the mechanisms of immune cell infiltration, tumor cell killing, and immune evasion within a controlled in vitro setting.35,36,37 They are also being used to screen for and validate the efficacy of immunotherapies.

Most prominently, tumor organoids are revolutionizing drug development and personalized medicine. Their ability to recapitulate patient-specific drug responses makes them ideal for high-throughput drug screening. Organoid biobanks from various cancer types are being used to test libraries of compounds, identifying novel effective drugs and uncovering mechanisms of action, as exemplified by the discovery of a KRAS mutation inhibitor, perhexiline maleate, using pancreatic cancer organoids.38 PDOs can be used to investigate drug resistance mechanisms, helping to identify pathways that drive treatment failure.39,40 Most importantly, PDOs are demonstrating strong potential for personalized precision medicine.41 Drug sensitivity tests performed on a patient’s own tumor organoids can predict clinical response, with studies showing high concordance between organoid response in vitro and patient outcomes in both breast and gastric cancers.42,43 This approach holds the promise of guiding treatment decisions, identifying effective therapies and avoiding ineffective ones, all within a clinically relevant time frame.

The successful translation of these promising applications into routine clinical and industrial practice, however, depends critically on overcoming the analytical bottlenecks highlighted above. High-throughput drug screening and personalized medicine require the capacity to evaluate organoid responses rapidly, accurately, and consistently across thousands of experimental conditions—a task for which traditional manual or semi-automated methods are ill-suited. This is precisely where AI integration becomes transformative: As will be explored in the application of AI in the field of tumor organoid image analysis, deep learning (DL) models enable automated quantification of drug effects by segmenting organoids, tracking their growth or regression over time, and even predicting viability directly from bright-field images. By facilitating unbiased, high-content analysis at scale, AI is not merely an adjunct to organoid technology but a critical enabler of its full potential in both fundamental research and clinical decision-making.

Advancements in the application of AI in image processing

AI has made groundbreaking progress in recent years, driving profound changes across numerous industries. The core of AI lies in simulating human intelligent behavior through computers, which involves leveraging massive volumes of data to extract patterns and applying them effectively. Machine Learning (ML) constitutes a central approach to realizing AI, as it can automatically optimize its performance by analyzing data without the need for manually written complex rules. DL, a branch of ML, represents an advanced, specialized evolution of ML. The DL model is inspired by the biological nervous system, introducing multi-layered neurons as nodes for data and connecting the neurons between adjacent layers through a series of operations to form a complex mathematical model. Specifically, the operations include weighting (where the weight is a parameter that changes during the training process) and activation (where the activation function introduces non-linearity, enhancing the model’s generalization ability). In each iteration, the data undergoes forward propagation, the loss function is computed, and then the weights in the network are adjusted using the gradient descent method through the backpropagation algorithm to train the model. Additionally, a fully connected network is defined by its structure: Every neuron in one layer is linked to every neuron in the adjacent layer. However, when this full connection design is applied to image data, it results in an astronomically large volume of weight parameters—an amount that quickly becomes computationally overwhelming. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which are more suitable for image processing, use convolution operations for weighting, where the pixel values in the convolution kernel represent the weights (usually a 3 × 3 convolution kernel, corresponding to 9 weight values). This significantly reduces the number of weight values and alleviates the training pressure on the model. In recent years, some studies have further mimicked the working methods of the human brain. For example, neuromorphic computing uses hardware to simulate the transmission of pulse signals between neurons, enhancing the ability to capture detailed information. Non-backpropagation training methods learn feature extraction through two forward propagations of positive and negative examples, reducing reliance on labeled data.44

With the implementation of various high-throughput experimental methods, the number of images and experts has increasingly become mismatched. An expert needs 3-4 s to interpret each image to keep up with the clinical workload, making the introduction of automated systems a key means to alleviate researchers’ work pressure.45 While humans can easily perform visual classification, this task is highly challenging for machines. Image classification requires machines to extract complex features from images and correctly interpret the information behind these features; thus, AI models have significant advantages.

In the field of organoids, the application of these advanced technologies shows great potential. AI can quickly and accurately analyze complex information contained in organoid images, such as brightness, texture, and lines, providing researchers with effective visualization and analysis support. The rapid development of AI significantly improves the efficiency of organoid research, greatly enhancing the feasibility and accuracy of organoid image analysis, and laying a technological foundation for more in-depth research. ML algorithms for image processing

Typically, the most commonly used traditional ML algorithms are random forest (RF) and support vector machine (SVM). However, these algorithms do not support direct processing of image data. This is primarily due to the significant computational overhead required to process the high-dimensional raw pixel data, which necessitates calculating weight matrices or kernel function distances across an extensive set of features. On the other hand, these models can only focus on pixel-level information and cannot extract macro-level semantic information.

One common approach to address this is through hand-crafted feature extraction. Intensity features such as pixel grayscale values and contrast; edge features extracted by the Sobel operator and Canny operator; texture features extracted by gray level co-occurrence matrix, and so forth. This method has weak data modeling and generalization capability, which is mainly attributed to the lack of diverse training data and the limitation of manually designed features, along with the presence of numerous redundant features in the extracted data. For example, Gaussian mixture model (GMM) and wavelet transform can extract low-level features such as textures and edges.46 Additionally, the student’s t-mixture model (TMM) can be used as an alternative to GMM.47 Principal component analysis (PCA), independent component analysis (ICA), and the such as can be used for dimensionality reduction, which reduces computational load and mitigates overfitting to a certain extent. Based on this, classification can be performed using RF or SVM. But if an image segmentation task is to be performed, it is necessary to carry out a classification for each pixel. The segmentation task includes post-processing steps, such as morphological erosion, dilation, and opening and closing operations. The main advantage of these ML segmentation algorithms lies in their ability to extract features using unsupervised methods, with low requirements for labeled data. Additionally, they have lower computational demands and higher interpretability. Despite these benefits, they struggle to extract advanced semantic features in complex settings, leading to limited accuracy. This explains why applications of RF and SVM in organoid image analysis have predominantly addressed classification rather than segmentation tasks.48,49

Image classification

Image classification is one of the most fundamental image analysis techniques. The model assigns a category label to each input image. Since each image has a unique label, image classification tasks are only suitable for images containing a single detection object. In addition, image classification models can only perform simple analyses and cannot be applied in more in-depth scenarios.

CNN

CNNs form the foundation of image classification. As a type of multilayer neural network (with one or more hidden layers), at least one of the layers uses convolution operations. CNNs consist of an input layer, convolutional layers (which extract local features from images through convolution operations), pooling layers (which reduce data dimensionality), fully connected layers, and output layers.

ResNet and DenseNet

By increasing the number of convolutional layers in a CNN model, the model is able to focus on more information. However, as the model depth increases, traditional CNN models encounter issues such as vanishing/exploding gradients and degradation problems. The introduction of ResNet mitigated these issues, providing a method for training models with a large number of layers.50 In ResNet, each residual unit (which consists of several convolutional layers) incorporates a residual structure, directly passing the initial input to the output, thus establishing skip connections between layers. As a result, in this model, the output is the sum of the input (x) and the result of the input (x) passed through several layers of convolution, F(x). The learning task is no longer a simple mapping but instead focuses on the difference between input and output, F(x). This not only simplifies the learning task but also retains gradient information in each residual unit, supporting the construction and training of much deeper networks (up to 152 layers).

On the basis of ResNet, Huang et al. proposed DenseNet. DenseNet utilizes dense connections, linking each layer to the outputs of all previous layers, enabling more comprehensive feature reuse and further enhancing the flow of gradient information. Notably, DenseNet processes these outputs from the shallow layers by concatenation, facilitating better feature reuse.51 While this increases the storage space required for feature maps, the reuse of features reduces the number of parameters in the network, ensuring excellent model performance.

Semantic segmentation

In many cases, a single image often contains a multitude of scattered biological tissues—each with distinct characteristics. This makes it impossible to use image classification algorithms, which are designed to assign one unified label to an entire image, to accurately categorize the content. Instead, image segmentation algorithms, which excel at partitioning an image into meaningful, tissue-specific regions, become the necessary choice. One of the more basic and commonly used approaches is semantic segmentation. Semantic segmentation assigns a category label to each pixel to identify tissue or lesion pixels.52,53 It should be noted that semantic segmentation is not a simple independent classification of pixels but takes into account the information of neighboring pixels to ensure the continuity of the segmentation. This places higher demands on the model’s ability to extract contextual information, which in turn requires deeper convolutional layers and the retention of low-level information. Additionally, since the output of the model should be a segmentation map with resolution comparable to the original image, an up sample decoder is often needed to restore the image’s size. Some models (such as the ResNet introduced earlier) can serve as both encoders and decoders to participate in segmentation tasks.54,55 However, models specifically designed for segmentation typically include additional features to achieve better segmentation results. Below, we will introduce some common semantic segmentation models and their unique architectures.

FCN

Fully convolutional networks (FCNs) were first introduced in 2014.54 In traditional CNNs, convolutional layers are followed by fully connected layers that map feature maps into fixed-length vectors, suitable for image-level classification. FCN, on the other hand, is a FCN that lacks fully connected layers, enabling pixel-level classification of images and addressing the problem of semantic segmentation. It can accept input images of any size and samples the feature maps produced by the last convolutional layer to restore them to the size of the input image. In this way, FCN can perform more accurate pixel-wise classification.

However, FCN still has its drawbacks. During multiple downsampling processes, spatial information can be lost, particularly affecting segmentation accuracy when dealing with small lesions with complex boundaries. Additionally, the use of convolutional operations and pooling layers reduces image resolution, and although upsampling can partially restore details, it cannot fully reconstruct the original image.56 FCN’s ability to capture global contextual information is limited, which may pose limitations in medical imaging tasks that require broader contextual information.

U-net

U-Net is currently regarded as a classic model in medical image segmentation. Its U-shaped architecture consists of an encoder and a decoder, which use convolution and upsampling operations to extract and restore image features. The uniqueness of U-Net lies in its introduction of skip connections, which combine high-resolution features from the encoder with low-resolution features from the decoder. These skip connections address the limitations of FCN in capturing details and contextual information.55 Skip connections help preserve low-level features by directly passing the feature maps from each layer of the encoder to the corresponding layer of the decoder. This allows the decoder to not only utilize the abstract semantic information from the higher layers of the encoder but also integrate low-level high-resolution features (such as edges and textures), preventing the loss of these details during downsampling. In this way, the decoder can gradually restore image details during the upsampling process, improving segmentation accuracy. Therefore, U-Net is capable of capturing both global semantic information and local detail information, enabling high-precision image segmentation. This makes it particularly suitable for medical image segmentation tasks, where its generalization ability can be enhanced by data augmentation (a supplement to diverse training data) even with limited labeled data. The optimized skip-connection architecture also collaborates with high-quality annotation and minimal domain shift to improve generalization in organoid image segmentation.

Although U-Net was originally created for segmentation tasks, its ability to generate precise pixel-level information has enabled its application in other tasks as well. For example, we can simply remove the softmax function from the model’s output layer and use loss functions such as mean squared error (MSE) or mean absolute error to allow U-Net to perform pixel-wise regression tasks.57 Alternatively, U-Net can be combined with a generative adversarial network (GAN), as seen in the pix2pix model, where U-Net is used as the generator.58 This demonstrates the flexibility and versatility of the U-Net architecture.

Several improved versions based on U-Net have been proposed to enhance its performance in medical image segmentation. UNet++ strengthens feature fusion by using nested and dense skip connections, further improving segmentation accuracy59; Attention U-Net introduces an attention mechanism, enabling the model to focus better on important target regions; TransUNet combines the advantages of Transformer and U-Net, improving the model’s performance in multimodal medical imaging; the U-Net 3D model replaces convolution and pooling operations with 3D counterparts, enabling better segmentation of 3D images.60,61,62

Other models

DeepLab is a deep CNN model introduced by Google, notable for its incorporation of atrous convolution and conditional random fields (CRF), which significantly enhance segmentation accuracy.63 The DeepLab series has evolved through multiple versions, such as DeepLabv3 and DeepLabv3+, which improve performance by enhancing multi-scale feature fusion, refining the atrous spatial pyramid pooling (ASPP) module, and incorporating encoder-decoder structures.64

HRNet was proposed in 2019 and adopts a unique layer-by-layer fusion strategy to maintain low-level information.65 Similar to U-Net, HRNet retains multi-resolution feature maps through each convolution stage and propagates them to subsequent steps. Its key distinction lies in the information interaction between feature maps of different resolutions at fusion layers in every stage, integrating both global and local details.

CNNs that balance resolution, depth, and width often suffer from lower efficiency because they need to expand performance across all three dimensions simultaneously. EfficientNet addresses this issue by using compound scaling.66 Similarly, RegNet also adopts a “one type of network” design, which adjusts the depth and width of the network through a series of hyperparameters.67 This adjustment is more thorough and allows the model to automatically optimize its network structure, avoiding overly complex, manually designed networks while achieving stronger performance.

FPN focuses on the fusion of features from different scales.68 More importantly, FPN provides a prediction after upsampling at each layer. Compared to U-Net, which performs prediction only at the final layer, FPN can better handle tasks related to detecting and segmenting objects at different scales.

Instance segmentation

Instance segmentation is a more refined segmentation method compared to semantic segmentation. While semantic segmentation assigns a class label to each pixel, it does not distinguish between different individuals within the same label. Instance segmentation can distinguish between different detection targets without the need for image cropping, allowing it to handle images with multiple targets and perform further analysis on each target separately.

Mask-RCNN

Proposed in 2017, Mask-RCNN extends RCNN-based frameworks for instance segmentation by adding an FCN-based mask branch, which generates fixed-size masks for each region proposal while retaining category classification and bounding box regression capabilities.69 It replaces traditional region of interest (ROI) pooling with ROI align and remains widely used for tasks involving small or overlapping instances due to its integration of precise region proposal generation and spatial localization from RCNN architectures.

YOLO

YOLO, first proposed in 2015 for object detection, generates bounding boxes with confidence scores and class probability predictions for target objects via a single neural network—unifying detection into a regression task for fast inference and end-to-end optimization, with subsequent iterations from v1 to v11 optimizing its network structure and integrating advanced techniques like data augmentation.70,71 YOLOv5 was adapted for segmentation by adding a lightweight FCN-based branch that generates “prototypes” (small feature maps of common shapes and contours) and corresponding mask coefficients, using these as base features and weights to compute segmentation masks, and its inherent instance-aware design enables instance-level segmentation well-suited for identifying individual organoids in imaging analysis.72

Other models that can use mask branches

It is worth noting that the mask branch in Mask-RCNN is not particularly complex, and similar mask branches can be added to other models. Instance segmentation based on masks requires high precision and, ideally, should be capable of comprehensively extracting various information from the image. Therefore, some semantic segmentation models are able to combine mask branches to achieve instance segmentation.73 For example, U-Net, FCN, and FPN have been applied to accomplish instance segmentation tasks.74,75,76

Nonetheless, Mask-RCNN remains one of the most widely used models in instance segmentation. This is because instances in an image can be very small and may overlap, requiring precise boundary segmentation for accurate mask generation. The combined features of RCNN’s initial segmentation (region proposal generation) and post-processing (bounding box regression) ensure that the masks have highly accurate spatial locations as well.

StarDist and cCellpose

StarDist is an innovative model that combines traditional CNNs (such as the U-Net architecture) with a “star-shaped” image segmentation approach.77 It is designed to solve the instance segmentation task, particularly for complex, overlapping, or deformed objects. Unlike mask-based methods, StarDist uses star-shaped graphs (specifically distance maps and branch maps) to distinguish between different instances and perform more accurate segmentation. In this way, StarDist not only extracts the contour features of each instance but also effectively handles overlapping or tightly connected objects, making instance segmentation more precise and robust. This method demonstrates higher efficiency than traditional mask-based segmentation approaches, especially when dealing with complex shapes, deformations, or object overlaps.

Similarly, the Cellpose model is also an instance segmentation model designed specifically for cell image segmentation. It is also built on the U-Net architecture.78 Cellpose achieves instance segmentation via a gradient vector field-based mechanism. Its network predicts a 2D vector field that guides each pixel toward the centroid of its corresponding cell, along with a cell probability map indicating cellular regions. During inference, seed points from the probability map flow along these vectors until converging to unique fixed points. Pixels sharing the same fixed point are grouped as a single instance, generating instance masks without relying on explicit detection or dedicated mask branches—making it effective for cells with variable morphologies.

Panoptic segmentation

Panoptic segmentation is a more comprehensive segmentation result that combines semantic segmentation and instance segmentation. Semantic segmentation cannot distinguish between individual instances, while instance segmentation may over-focus on the differences between instances and ignore the commonalities within the same semantic category. Therefore, panoptic segmentation allows for more comprehensive information extraction, balancing both the overall structure and fine details.

One approach to panoptic segmentation is to use two networks to separately perform semantic segmentation and instance segmentation, and then merge the results at the final stage. In this approach, the backbone network performs precise semantic segmentation, while an instance segmentation branch, typically composed of Mask-RCNN, is added. For example, the Panoptic FPN proposed by Kirillov et al. uses a ResNet-FPN encoder-decoder structure as the backbone, while Mask-RCNN is used for instance segmentation.79 Similarly, the EfficientPS proposed by Mohan et al. uses an EfficientNet-bidirectional FPN as the backbone, and Mask-RCNN is also used for instance segmentation.80

Another approach is to use a single network to simultaneously perform both instance segmentation and semantic segmentation. K-Net is a typical model that has been applied to biomedical image segmentation. The basic principle of K-Net is to directly use two sets of convolution kernels to generate semantic masks and instance masks, and by combining these two masks, panoptic segmentation is achieved.81

In this regard, GANs are another promising model. GANs generate new data instances similar to the training data through adversarial training of a generator and a discriminator.58 Further, conditional GANs (CGANs) introduce conditional inputs on top of traditional GANs, allowing the generated images to be influenced by specific conditions.58 These conditional inputs can include labels, features, or other factors affecting image generation.82 A common type of CGAN model is pix2pix, which is based on U-Net architecture. When CGANs are used for image segmentation, whether the task is semantic segmentation or instance segmentation, the generator can end-to-end generate the corresponding masks, while the discriminator evaluates the quality of the generated results during training.83 Information such as bounding boxes or binary masks can be used as conditional inputs to enhance the segmentation performance of the network.

It is important to note that GAN models often have certain hardware requirements. This is because the model includes two networks: the generator and the discriminator, which require alternating iterative optimization during training. For example, classic CGAN training typically requires a GPU with ≥16 GB of VRAM, and pix2pix training usually requires a GPU with ≥10 GB of VRAM. In response to this, some researchers have proposed optimization solutions that, to some extent, reduce the computational cost.84,85,86,87

Combined with transformer

In recent years, the Transformer model has developed rapidly. Although it was initially used for natural language processing tasks, practice has proven that it also has extraordinary capabilities for image data. The core of Transformer lies in the self-attention mechanism. First, it is necessary to map the data to be processed into a sequence matrix X of a certain length, and then generate three matrices: Q (Query), K (Key), and V (Value) based on X and the projection matrix W to be learned. For each sequence, the corresponding matrix Q is dotted with all matrices K. After SoftMax normalization, scaling, and other processing, the weight of matrix V is obtained, and the output is obtained by multiplication. In general, the model also adopts a multi-head attention mechanism to enhance information capture capabilities. That is, it uses multiple split weight matrices to calculate matrices Q, K, and V respectively, and finally splices them.88 For the ViT model that processes image data, the original image is usually divided into non-overlapping patches of a certain size. Then, the pixels of each patch are flattened and linearly projected into a sequence matrix. It can be found from the above process that each sequence in the Transformer model will perform dot product calculations with all other sequences to calculate weights, which makes up for the problem of limited receptive fields of convolution operations in CNN. In 2021, some researchers combined ViT with U-Net and proposed the TransUNet model.60 The most important feature of TransUNet is that it replaces the convolution layers in the last few layers of the classic U-Net encoder with 12 Transformer layers. After extracting features through both CNN and ViT methods, the model reshapes the sequence data output by ViT into a high-dimensional feature map, and obtains the segmentation result after an upsampling step similar to the classic U-Net. This provides us with a paradigm for combining Transformer with CNN. In addition, the Swin Transformer reduces computation by dividing the image into small windows and calculating self-attention via a sliding window mechanism, while maintaining inter-window information transfer through the SW-MSA module.89 Deng et al. also reported two models combining Transformer and CNN. Common imaging modalities and optical architectures employed in tumor organoid research

To explore the application of AI in image analysis, it is essential to first clarify the common imaging modalities and their respective characteristics within the field of organoid research. This preliminary work provides a theoretical basis for selecting appropriate imaging approaches tailored to distinct image analysis objectives.

Imaging modalities

Imaging modality refers to the core classification of imaging techniques based on their fundamental principles and signal sources. It determines the type of contrast and signal properties of the acquired images, such as light absorption, fluorescence emission, phase shift, or interference. Common examples in tumor organoid research include transmitted light imaging, fluorescence imaging, phase contrast imaging, and label-free nonlinear optical imaging. A single imaging modality can be implemented through different optical architectures to meet specific experimental needs.

Transmitted light imaging: Transmitted light imaging uses visible light (either natural or artificial light sources) to pass through transparent or semi-transparent samples. After the light is absorbed and scattered by the sample, it is focused and magnified through the objective and eyepiece, forming an enlarged image of the sample on the image plane. The core principle relies on the difference in light absorption by the sample (regions with more absorption appear dark, while areas with less absorption appear bright). It is the most basic optical imaging technique, with a simple structure, easy operation, and low cost. Bright-field imaging is the most common mode of transmitted light microscopy. The light source is vertically illuminated from below the sample (transmitted illumination), and after passing through the sample, the light enters the objective lens. Areas of the sample that absorb more light (such as stained cell nuclei) appear dark against the bright background, while areas that absorb less light appear bright, creating a contrast in the image. It is easy to operate and is the most basic imaging tool in biological laboratories. However, it has extremely poor contrast for unstained live cells or transparent samples, making them nearly impossible to observe.

Fluorescence imaging: Fluorescence imaging utilizes the fluorescent labeling technique—target structures in the sample (such as proteins or organelles) are labeled with fluorescent dyes or proteins (such as GFP). These are then illuminated with excitation light of a specific wavelength (e.g., blue light). After absorbing energy, the labels emit fluorescence at a longer wavelength (e.g., green light). A filter is used to separate the excitation light from the emitted fluorescence, and only the fluorescence signal is imaged. This method has high specificity and can precisely localize target structures, but it relies on fluorescent labeling.

Phase contrast imaging: Transparent samples absorb very little light, but their thickness or refractive index differences cause phase shifts in the light. Phase contrast imaging uses a special phase plate to convert the invisible phase differences into visible amplitude differences (contrast), allowing transparent structures to display brightness and darkness differences. This method allows the observation of live cells without staining or damaging the sample. The contrast comes from differences in refractive index or thickness, making it ideal for the dynamic observation of cell movement.

Differential interference contrast (DIC) imaging: DIC is based on polarized light interference—incident light is split into two beams of perpendicular polarized light, which pass through adjacent areas of the sample (creating a path difference due to refractive index variations). After converging through the objective lens, the two beams interfere again, forming a contrast image with a 3D appearance. DIC uses polarized light interference to create a high-contrast image with a 3D appearance, revealing fine details like cell boundaries and nucleoli. It is ideal for observing transparent samples but not suitable for very thick samples.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging: OCT uses low-coherence light interference—splitting the light source into two beams (reference light and sample light). The sample light illuminates biological tissue and is reflected by structures at different depths, interfering with the reference light at the detector. By analyzing the intensity and delay of the interference signals, the cross-sectional structure of the tissue is reconstructed. OCT has moderate resolution and deep imaging depth. It is non-invasive, making it suitable for live tissue.

Optical architectures

Optical architecture describes the design and configuration of the light path in a microscope, including the arrangement of light sources, lenses, detectors, and other optical components. It governs key imaging performance metrics such as resolution, penetration depth, imaging speed, and phototoxicity. Representative optical architectures for organoid imaging include widefield, confocal, two-photon, and light-sheet microscopy. The selection of optical architecture depends on the imaging modality, sample characteristics, and research objectives.

Confocal microscopy: Confocal microscopy uses a point light source (such as a laser) to illuminate the sample, and the objective lens focuses on a point at a certain depth within the sample. A pinhole is placed in the imaging light path (conjugated with the point light source), allowing only the reflected light/fluorescence from the focal point to pass through the pinhole, while filtering out stray light from outside the focal plane. Through point-by-point scanning and depth adjustment, a 3D, clear image is finally reconstructed. The resolution is high, allowing for layered scanning of thick samples. With strong 3D imaging capabilities, it is suitable for observing the internal structures of thick samples.

Light-sheet microscopy: Light-sheet microscopy uses a cylindrical lens to focus a laser beam into a thin sheet of light and illuminates a specific focal plane of the sample from the side. A camera collects the fluorescence signal from the plane in a direction perpendicular to the light sheet. By moving the sample or light sheet along the z axis, it performs a layer-by-layer scan and assembles a 3D image. Only the focal plane is illuminated, and non-focal planes are not exposed to light. The imaging speed is fast (entire layers are imaged simultaneously, without the need for point-by-point scanning), making it suitable for thick samples.

Two-photon microscopy: Two-photon microscopy uses long-wavelength lasers to illuminate a fluorescently labeled sample. Fluorescent molecules are simultaneously excited by absorbing two photons only at the laser focus point, emitting fluorescence. In non-focal regions, where the laser intensity is insufficient, no fluorescence is produced. This method uses long-wavelength lasers that scatter less and have deeper penetration, with high resolution, making it suitable for imaging deep tissue. Fluorescence is generated only at the focal point, significantly reducing photobleaching and phototoxicity, making it ideal for long-term observation of live samples. In addition to imaging fluorescently labeled tissues, two-photon microscopy can also achieve label-free high-resolution imaging through second harmonic generation (SHG), third harmonic generation (THG), and other nonlinear optical phenomena. SHG is a label-free nonlinear optical phenomenon in two-photon microscopy that generates signals from non-centrosymmetric biomolecules in tumor organoids, enabling high-resolution visualization of stromal structures without phototoxicity. THG arises from refractive index inhomogeneities in organoids, providing complementary structural contrast to SHG for 3D morphological analysis.

Based on the features discussed above, imaging methods can be highly relevant to certain image analysis tasks. For image reconstruction, wide-field fluorescence imaging is fast but has low resolution, making it suitable for super-resolution reconstruction tasks. Light-sheet and confocal microscopy offer high axial resolution, ideal for 3D reconstruction. For image segmentation, bright-field imaging is low-cost and suitable for coarse organoid segmentation. Fluorescence microscopy can enhance cell boundary signals, or OCT can increase resolution without compromising speed, improving task completion. Fluorescently labeling cell nuclei or using light-sheet or two-photon microscopy with high axial resolution can reveal finer nuclear structures, helping identify nuclear positions in organoids and supporting nuclear segmentation tasks. Transmitted light imaging can provide structural contour information, creating fluorescence signals in unmarked images. Cell viability is directly related to the morphology of organoids (e.g., shrinkage, damage, boundary integrity). Bright-field imaging can monitor morphological changes over time without labeling, making it suitable for dynamic vitality assessment. Bright-field imaging can examine tumor organoid invasion into the matrix in well plates, and DIC can enhance contrast and reveal more details. Some imaging methods are low-toxic and ideal for long-term tracking of organoids, including bright-field, phase contrast, and OCT, two-photon, or confocal microscopy with higher resolution or 3D imaging capabilities.

Following the imaging process, the resulting image typically needs to undergo preprocessing to meet the quality standards required for subsequent workflows. Contrast enhancement is usually the first step in this preprocessing stage. The most commonly used contrast enhancement algorithms include linear contrast stretching, gamma correction, and histogram equalization. Wavelet transform can be applied to enhance signals at certain frequencies through weighted processing. Another step is denoising. Common denoising algorithms include Gaussian filtering, median filtering, and bilateral filtering. These algorithms use convolution operations in the spatial domain for denoising.95 Algorithms like wavelet transform and non-local means (NLMs) denoising take advantage of the differences in characteristics between image and noise in the frequency domain.96,97 Regular preprocessing steps also include artifact removal. Artifacts mainly appear in images acquired by technologies capable of long-term imaging. Different types of artifacts generated by different imaging methods can be removed using different algorithms. For example, Fourier transform can be used to remove strip artifacts caused by light-sheet microscopy or DIC microscopy, and NLM filtering can be used to remove speckle noise caused by OCT imaging.98,99

For the imaging of 3D tissues, images are typically captured at different vertical depths to acquire a set of z stack images. If the tissue undergoes displacement during the imaging process, z stack registration is required to ensure the accuracy of 3D reconstruction results. The earliest image registration algorithms were template-based, which entails identifying the optimal match of a sub-region of the source image within the target image and quantifying the matching degree using similarity metrics such as MSE and cross-correlation.100 An alternative approach is feature-based image matching, as exemplified by algorithms such as SIFT and SURF. These features can either be manually extracted or derived from the feature maps of DL models.101 In recent years, several sophisticated DL-based frameworks for image alignment have also been proposed, including SuperPoint, SuperGlue and LoFTR.102,103,104 By virtue of mechanisms such as self-attention and cross-attention, these frameworks are capable of constructing global rather than local feature point descriptors.

Applications in reconstruction of tumor organoid images

To obtain detailed 3D information, confocal microscopy can be used to acquire several cross-sectional images and stack them vertically (known as z stack technique).105 However, this method may become overly redundant when dealing with large organoid structures, requiring the acquisition of hundreds of images. Considering these factors, projecting 3D z stack images onto a single 2D image is a compromise that balances convenience, usability, and information integrity. Several software tools now exist to achieve this projection. ImageJ is a widely used image analysis software among biologists. The 2007 version of ImageJ already supported standard z-projection.106 Michael Abramoff’s VolumeJ can generate surface-rendered images depicting 3D structures using simulated ray tracing. In 2012, Schindelin et al. introduced Fiji software, which builds upon ImageJ’s infrastructure.107 Fiji also provides the Stitching plugin for z stack projection, using phase correlation for distortion-free image stitching to generate projection images.

However, in practical research, tumor organoids do not always exhibit regularity, primarily due to factors such as the influence of drug effects and the invasive properties of tumor tissues. Therefore, traditional algorithms struggle to project complex images, while AI technologies with their flexibility have shown advantages in solving such problems. Forsgren et al. used fast axial scanning techniques to acquire low-resolution images and were able to generate high-resolution projection images using a CNN model.108 This is known as a super-resolution reconstruction task. Researchers first established a dataset correlating low-resolution fast axial scan images with z stack projection images (using organoid models of A549 lung cancer, MCF-7 breast cancer, MDA-MB-231 breast cancer, and SK-OV-3 ovarian cancer), then built an encoder/decoder architecture based on U-Net and trained the model. Comparison between predicted and actual projection images demonstrated that the improved U-Net with One-Shot Aggregation (OSA) and CGAN submodel (OSA-CGAN) effectively completed the prediction task.

Apart from methods generating 2D projections, direct reconstruction of 3D models from z stack images allows for detailed depiction of organoid 3D structures. Beghin et al. designed an automated multiscale 3D imaging platform (referred to as the Jewell platform), integrating organoid cultivation (Jewell chip) with 3D single-objective lens imaging.94 This system enables high-throughput collection of z stack images, reconstruction of organoid 3D structures. Researchers stained cell nuclei with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and obtained 471 3D images (including 5274 manually identified cell nuclei) as a training set to train a StarDist model. The model’s 3D modeling predictions were validated on 10 organoid formations including liver cells, neuroectoderm, intestine, and HCT116 colon cancer cell lines. Song et al. developed VONet, which uses pre-generated simulated organoid 3D models to obtain z stack image datasets through slicing for training the FCN network.109 The virtual organoids (VOs) include normal colon tissues and cancerous tissues of various shapes and sizes, effectively simulating the morphology of real organoids. The model is ultimately able to generate VO 3D models from real organoid z stack images. It is worth mentioning that the VO dataset in this study underwent three iterations, resulting in the generated VO incorporating features such as the morphological diversity of stained cell nuclei, the spot patterns of stained cell nuclei, and the spatial distribution of cell nuclei. These features were consistent with the frequency and quality observed in the RO stained images. The researchers trained VONet using VO version 3, and when evaluating its performance with the RO dataset, the intersection over union (IoU) score was 0.82. Therefore, although this study used virtual datasets, it still maintains a high degree of reliability. However, there is currently a lack of standardized metrics for the quantitative evaluation of the quality of synthesized confocal fluorescence microscope images, and the practical application of such technologies remains limited.

When using DL techniques for reconstruction tasks, a common approach is to utilize U-Net to generate restored images. This method has been widely applied across various image restoration tasks, with most studies employing U-Net or its variants as the generator.110,111,112 The GAN architecture is recommended for further optimization of generated image quality. Specifically, training a generator using only the loss function that quantifies the discrepancy between synthetic and real images is prone to yielding smooth yet blurry outputs. By contrast, the GAN architecture exhibits distinct advantages. It allows the incorporation of fluorescence intensity into training objectives, which not only mitigates the non-linearity of the generator to a certain extent but also facilitates the quantitative restoration of intensity levels. For 3D reconstruction of organoid images, z stack images are essential for accurate restoration of 3D structures. An important direction for optimizing such models is to explore reconstruction using the minimum number of z-stacks.

Applications in segmentation of tumor organoid images

Segmentation techniques for organoid images are capable of outlining their contours, serving to filter out irrelevant noise signals or isolate individual organoids from composite images containing multiple ones. Traditional segmentation methods can be implemented using ImageJ as mentioned earlier.106 Chen et al. provide SpheroidSizer, a tool utilizing active contour algorithms to automatically monitor the boundaries of spheroidal organoids in uneven or noisy backgrounds.113 Users can also manually segment images that are not well identified through automated methods using manual initialization and drawing tools. The system was validated on BON-1 pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor spheroids. Philippo also offers AnaSP, which preprocesses images for segmentation by first converting them based on grayscale levels to binary lightness.114 Subsequently, the image undergoes intensity histogram analysis to roughly segment dark objects using thresholding. Segmentation is completed using algorithms for boundary closure and hole filling. The model was validated on A549 non-small cell lung cancer spheroids. However, traditional segmentation algorithms have significant limitations. Organoids exhibit diverse shapes and sizes, and the same organoid can undergo significant changes under different pathological conditions. Therefore, traditional algorithms based on prior knowledge (such as grayscale thresholds, preset shape templates, and so forth) have poor adaptability. The robustness of traditional algorithms is also insufficient, making them vulnerable to interference from noise, artifacts, or changes in grayscale range and resolution caused by varying imaging conditions. Although some preprocessing methods (such as histogram equalization, Gaussian filtering, and so forth) can improve image quality to a certain extent, the results are still limited and rely on manual tuning of parameters.

Nowadays, many AI-based spheroid segmentation techniques address generalizability issues. Sadanandan et al. combine FCN and GAN to develop a generative model.115 They selected image datasets comprising various cell types under different experimental conditions and culture settings to ensure diversity in cell morphology, thereby enhancing model generalization. Wang et al. built the RDAU-Net, which enhances the segmentation capability of U-Net using res-double dynamic convolution (Dy Conv).116 Using bright-field microscopy images of bladder cancer organoids as the dataset, RDAU-Net outperformed many other U-Net variant models. Zhang et al. adjusted the model’s attention through the residual attention gate.117 The model is built upon U2-Net and Attention U-Net. Streller et al. validated the performance of U-Net and HRNet on the more challenging bright-field images of tumor spheroids.118 In their study, they used a dataset of human head and neck squamous cell carcinoma spheroids that had been treated with radiotherapy, resulting in many dead cell debris obscuring the shrunken spheroids. Nevertheless, both models demonstrated excellent performance. Lacalle et al. provide the open-source tool SpheroidJ.119 Researchers cultivated U87-MG, U251-MG GBM, and HCT-116 colorectal cancer cell lines to form 3D spheroids and acquired bright-field and fluorescent images to construct datasets. They initially created non-ML segmentation algorithms to alleviate the burden of manually applying labels. Similar to other traditional algorithms, their approach is based on contour generation, dilation, erosion, and other steps. Specialized algorithms were designed for threshold, edge, threshold and edge, and fluorescence parameters to address segmentation tasks for different types of spheroids, demonstrating the poor generalization capability of traditional algorithms. Based on labeled datasets, researchers trained DL models of five architectures (U-Net, DeepLabV3+, Mask R-CNN, HRNet-Seg, U2-Net), proving their higher performance compared to traditional segmentation algorithms (such as K-means clustering and Canny edge detection). However, the segmentation results obtained by models that use only fluorescence images as the training set do not have high accuracy, as the contours in fluorescence images do not perfectly align with the actual contours of the spherical bodies. A multi-channel algorithm that considers both bright-field and fluorescence images simultaneously can perform the segmentation task most effectively. Tumor organoids can be co-cultured with immune cells to study phenomena such as immune cell infiltration. Leite et al. validated the performance of TransUNet on the bright-field image dataset for tumor spheroid segmentation tasks.120 The results show that on smaller datasets, due to fewer epochs, TransUNet cannot outperform other fully CNN-based U-Net variant models. Deng et al. also reported two models combining Transformer and CNN. The two models developed by the research team mentioned earlier, which respectively focus on long-distance pixel relationships and multi-scale fusion, have both achieved good optimization in the segmentation performance of organoid images.90,93 Kulkarni et al. created an image dataset of co-cultured immune cells with PDAC organoids and used two models (Cellpose and StarDist) for image segmentation.121 Compared to StarDist, Cellpose has shown a slight advantage, especially in images with higher organoid density, where StarDist tends to over-segment. The OrganoIDNetData public dataset inherently contains immune cells, and researchers conducted in silico ablation of these immune cells (replacing ablated pixels with the average grayscale value of the surrounding 64 pixels) as part of the dataset complexity analysis. This operation was aimed at comparing the performance of segmentation algorithms on datasets with and without immune cells, thereby verifying that the presence of immune cells increases dataset complexity and poses greater challenges to segmentation tasks. Another study utilized the aforementioned platform to investigate organoid responses to chemotherapy using their own PDAC dataset.122 OCT provides images with higher spatial resolution. Zhang et al. designed an EGO-Net based on spectral-domain OCT images to enhance boundary recognition of colorectal cancer organoids.123 Bao et al. also used EGO-Net for colon cancer organoid segmentation.124 However, prior to this, a small RPN network composed of VGG-Unet was added. The final 3D segmentation result is obtained by averaging the results from two orthogonal directions. Branciforti et al. trained the encoder-decoder network K-Net on OCT images of breast cancer organoids for organoid segmentation and post-processing.125 Matthews et al. developed OrganoID, based on the U-Net model, for pixel-wise organ identification in datasets of PDAC, adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC), and other organoid images, enhancing segmentation clarity using the Canny edge detector.5 The segmentation results of these models provide a basis for organoid dynamics analysis, which will be discussed in subsequent sections. Qin et al. introduced TransOrga-plus, which integrate biological knowledge and multi-modal (visual and frequency-domain) cues for automated, personalized analysis.126 Its architecture includes a multi-model feature extraction module and a multi-branch decoder module, which generate initial segmentation masks. These masks are then manually corrected by biological experts via iterative feedback until the required precision is achieved, with a dedicated biological knowledge-driven branch incorporating user-provided prior knowledge to guide tailored results.

In addition to whole organoid segmentation, technologies now enable finer segmentation of cells within organoids. Because of cell overlaps, artifacts stemming from insufficient resolution, and the fact that cells exhibit more complex shapes and textures than organoids, AI technologies have emerged as essential tools for cell segmentation. Sohaib et al. utilized the Swin Transformer to achieve nucleus segmentation in breast cancer organoid confocal images.127 The researchers successfully realized strong segmentation performance using a simple architecture with as few parameters as possible. Mukashyaka et al. developed Cellos for precise segmentation of triple-negative breast cancer organoids.128 Cellos initially applies intensity histogram analysis similar to AnaSP for organoid segmentation. Researchers then developed a model using StarDist-3D for nucleus segmentation. Both steps demonstrated sufficient accuracy. Wen et al. developed 3DeeCellTracker, using the 3D U-Net to segment cells in 3D images.129 The model predicts object categories in central voxels based on adjacent voxel labels (cell or non-cell). The model requires minimal training data and performs well on datasets of diverse 3D biological tissues such as Caenorhabditis elegans brain neurons, zebrafish larval hearts, and tumor spheroids. Tim Van De Looverbosch and colleagues employed light-sheet fluorescence microscopy for nuclear segmentation of GBM organoids.130 The basic segmentation was accomplished using a 3D U-Net. To optimize the segmentation results, the researchers introduced a “centroid map” for precise localization of cell nuclei. The process first involves filtering with a sphere equivalent to the average size of the cell nuclei to ensure that each nucleus has only one candidate position. Then, candidate voxels below a certain threshold are discarded to avoid noise interference. Finally, the centroid positions are screened according to two strategies: C-MAP and PAC-MAP. From the results, PAC-MAP performed better because it incorporates the prior knowledge that two centroid positions must not be too close to each other. Riendeau et al. successfully achieved accurate nuclear segmentation on a fluorescent image dataset with low signal-to-noise ratio using Cellpose.118,131 This study highlights the significant advantages of DL models over traditional segmentation algorithms in scenarios with poor imaging quality. Winkelmaier et al. used U-Net for nuclear segmentation of breast cancer organoids. Notably, the researchers introduced the concept of potential fields to perceive the boundaries of adjacent cell nuclei.132 The introduction of the potential field function into the loss function successfully enabled the model to learn such boundary features, enhancing the effect of nuclear segmentation.133 Ong et al. developed 3DCellScope and applied the StarDist-3D model to the DNA fluorescent images of PDAC organoids to achieve nuclei segmentation.134 This workflow also includes cell segmentation implemented by the watershed algorithm combined with actin fluorescent images, as well as organoid contour segmentation achieved by thresholding and morphological filtering. Based on the digital organoids constructed from these segmentation results, the researchers defined three specific topological structures formed by cells (spherical, rod-shaped, and discoid) and realized the automatic labeling of the positions of these structures. This helps in identifying specific sensitive regions in the complex TME. The Jewell platform also focuses on cell segmentation.94 By staining nuclei, researchers can roughly determine cell positions based on nuclear positions. In fact, the 3D organoid models on this platform are not just rough outlines but are directly composed of individual cells. The platform’s 3D models provide positional information of each cell within the organoid from multiple perspectives. These systems similarly provide tracking and localization capabilities for organoids from a cell segmentation perspective.

A wide variety of models are employed for organoid image segmentation, and based on certain conventional models, additional modules can be integrated to tailor them to specific tasks. For example, dynamic convolution can be applied to high-resolution datasets to expand the receptive field, and transformers can be used as encoders to capture long-range dependencies. Attention mechanisms can be introduced to enhance the recognition of certain features, and so forth. Among them, most studies on Transformer-based models have not used sufficiently large datasets due to the difficulty in organoid cultivation. Nevertheless, these models still demonstrate performance comparable to that of CNN-based models. Organoid segmentation tasks are often applied in many studies for drug evaluation, where drug efficacy is assessed through parameters such as total organoid area and aggregated morphological indicators. These parameters are intuitive but do not fully encompass all the morphological characteristics of organoids. DL methods may also be used to extract deep features to directly predict organoid cell viability for drug efficacy evaluation. For nuclear segmentation, the model is capable of recognizing individual cells and can further classify the cells. In this regard, Cellos distinguishes two cell subclones using two different fluorescence markers. Although experiments have shown that the fluorescence markers do not affect cell viability, this process still requires an additional fluorescence staining step. The Jewell platform, on the other hand, uses the YOLO model for cell classification at different division stages. By directly combining YOLO’s coarse segmentation results with the precise segmentation results from StarDist, accurate classification of individual cells can be achieved. Methods that combine DL models performing different tasks, like this, can efficiently accomplish more complex tasks.

Applications in fluorescent image generation for tumor organoids

Fluorescence imaging of organoids using dyes, performed before bright-field imaging, allows visualization of different subcellular structures depending on the dye used. In the widely used field of immunofluorescent labeling, fluorescent molecular probes have been employed to mark various biomolecules.

Typically, staining methods designed for 2D layouts are also applicable to 3D organoids, but the cell occlusion in organoids increases the difficulty of obtaining clear images. A common approach is tissue clearing of organoids. Lloyd-Lewis et al. proposed tissue clearing methods for immunofluorescent staining of breast cancer organoids.135 This study involved four commonly used clearing methods: solvent-based clearing (3DISCO), deep brain imaging (SeeDB), clear unobstructed brain imaging cocktails and computational analysis (CUBIC), and passive clarity technique (PACT). Analyzing imaging results, researchers found that SeeDB and CUBIC yielded the best imaging quality. Dekkers et al. proposed a novel clearing agent for immunofluorescent labeling or gene fluorescence detection effective in organoids such as colon and breast cancer with varying sizes, shapes, and cell contents.24 Compared to commercially available Focus Clear, researchers’ homemade fructose-glycerol clearing agent offers advantages such as long-term storage of stained tissues, reversibility, and no loss of fluorescence brightness.

However, the traditional methods noted above come with notable drawbacks: they demand additional reagents or specialized equipment, involve a cumbersome number of operational steps, suffer from low imaging throughput, and can induce both cytotoxicity and phototoxicity. Several studies have applied AI image translation techniques to generate fluorescence images from other types of images. In this regard, Hagen et al. provided a fluorescence microscope dataset for DL models in 2021.136 Christiansen et al. proposed an algorithm called in silico labeling (ISL), which is inspired by U-Net.137 This model can generate fluorescence-labeled subcellular structures such as nuclei, cell membranes, and damaged cells from z stack bright-field images of PSCs and various cancer cell lines. Similarly, Lee et al. developed DeepHCS(++), which generates fluorescence-stained images of apoptosis, nuclei, and cytoplasm from organoids of GBM using two U-Net-style networks (Transformation Network TN and Refinement Network RN), with TN and RN integrated via CGAN-derived associative mechanisms.138 Huang et al. also used CGAN to accomplish fluorescence image translation tasks.139 The researchers used colorectal cancer organoid fluorescence images as the dataset and trained a CGAN with Pix2PixHD as the generator. This model is capable of generating apoptosis fluorescence images. Ounkomol et al. used a U-Net architecture to predict various fluorescence images from transmitted light images obtained from transmission and electron microscopes in fibrosarcoma organoids.140 Subcellular structures involved in fluorescence include DNA (chromatin/body), cell membranes, nuclear membranes, and mitochondria. The model can even predict fluorescence images of subnuclear structures during mitosis processes. The study also attempted to predict immunofluorescence images from electron microscope images, achieving automatic spatial alignment.

For fluorescence image generation tasks, the model needs to be optimized in order to accurately generate the positions and intensities of fluorescence markers. The most common approach is to use a CGAN architecture, as it is specifically designed for image generation. The other two papers employed different methods, including Deep Non-local Attention and a multi-scale fixation point generation mechanism. However, such tasks have inherent limitations. The model must learn the connection between different imaging patterns, so the prediction performance depends on the existence of these correlations. If a fluorescence marker is weakly associated with unmarked features such as organoid morphology or texture, the model will have difficulty finding the connection between the images.

Applications in cell viability assay of tumor organoids

In the field of tumor organoids, cell viability assay is a crucial indicator for screening anti-tumor drugs. The qualitative assessment of drug efficacy can be visually inferred from changes in organoid size observed in images. However, precise evaluation of drug efficacy requires quantitative cellular viability measurements, necessitating an accurate mapping of image information to cellular viability.

Traditional methods for cellular viability assays are often invasive. Many methods involve dyes and other substances that induce cell toxicity, limiting them to endpoint analysis rather than continuous monitoring. In 2D cell culture, common methods for assessing cellular viability include direct methods (such as incucyte cytotoxicity assays, flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry) and indirect methods (luminescence assays, fluorescence assays, colorimetric assays).141 A large number of parameters of organoids can be obtained through biological sensors, such as electrochemical methods, to estimate cell viability. In 2017, Zhang YS and colleagues developed a multi-sensor integrated organ-on-a-chip platform, which enables real-time monitoring of biophysical and biochemical parameters through the integration of numerous sensors, paving a potential way to enhance the performance of current organ-on-a-chip models in drug screening.86 Additionally, viability of organoids can be assessed using live/dead cell staining. Fluorescent dyes are commonly used to distinguish between live and dead cells, thereby avoiding interference from irrelevant cells in images. In 2015, Oksana Sirenko and others developed a series of highly efficient staining and image analysis workflow.142 Cell viability assay based on fluorescent dyes is implemented using MetaXpress® 6 software. Due to variations in the supportive matrix (commonly using biological tissue materials such as collagen in organoid technology, or synthetic materials like hyaluronic acid, polyacrylamide, polyethylene glycol dissolved in hydrogel to simulate the natural extracellular matrix), the effectiveness of viability assay parameters (primarily indirect methods) validated in 2D cell culture needs verification in the context of 3D organoid technology.143 Dr. Anthony J. Dominijanni and colleagues conducted experiments using organoids composed of human colorectal cancer cells in three types of hydrogels (Coli, HA:Coli, HyStem). They employed luminescence assays (CellTiter-Glo (CT2D) and CellTiter-Glo 3D (CT3D)), fluorescence assays (PrestoBlue™ (PB)), and colorimetric assays (CellTiter 96 AQ® MTS single solution cell proliferation assay).141

AI technology can provide quantitative cell viability detection based on image analysis, offering significant advantages in continuous vitality monitoring due to its non-invasiveness and flexibility. For organoid-level evaluation, CNN architectures (originally for classification or segmentation) can be adapted to output viability values through regression. With a single organoid in an image, a classification-like model (often decoder-free) suffices to predict one viability value. For multiple organoids, instance segmentation models first distinguish individuals, then generate a specific value for each. Alternatively, leveraging live/dead fluorescent staining, cell-level segmentation models can identify and count live vs. dead cells within organoids. Viability is then calculated as the ratio of live to total cells, with this method also applicable to multi-organoid images. It is worthy to note that while traditional viability assays also rely on fluorescent imaging, AI-generated fluorescence methods mentioned earlier could be applied to estimate cell viability by generating live/dead fluorescence images.137 However, sometimes, we do not need to know the exact location of live/dead dye fluorescence within an organoid, but only the overall cell viability value of the entire organoid. Therefore, there are other models specifically designed to perform such tasks.

Some studies use expert judgments on different locations of organoids as the standard. For example, Huang et al. based their research on a colon cancer organoid dataset and applied six models that are particularly suitable for edge detection (such as Faster-RCNN, and variant of EfficientNet) to assess cell viability at the edges of organoids.139 The evaluation was carried out by comparing the IoU between the model segmentation results and the expert-labeled results. Trettner et al. designed SAVVY, which utilizes a fine-tuned Mask-RCNN model trained on PDAC organoid datasets.144 Cell viability ground truth is based on expert judgment. SAVVY can assess cell viability by identifying features such as transparency and overall morphology of organoid spheres, exhibiting capabilities on par with domain experts.

The overall cell viability of the organoid can also be characterized by the ratio of live cells and dead cells. Zhang et al. designed a multi-layer CNN to predict cell viability from bright-field images of tumor organoids.145 To quantitatively characterize the viability of tumor cells under drug influence, researchers defined a drug inhibition score variable on SUM159 breast cancer organoids: Drug Inhibition Score = Dead staining fluorescence intensity/(Dead staining fluorescence intensity + Live staining fluorescence intensity). Following live/dead cell staining experiments to construct experimental datasets, AI models trained on this data provide a numerical output representing the drug inhibition score. Chiang et al. defined a survival score variable: Survival Score = Net live staining fluorescence intensity/(Net live staining fluorescence intensity + Net dead staining fluorescence intensity), where net fluorescence intensity refers to total live/dead fluorescence intensity minus background fluorescence.146 This definition method eliminates background color interference, thereby achieving greater accuracy. This model is also based on multi-layer CNN and breast cancer organoids, achieving results comparable to expert judgment.

More studies utilize industry gold standard CellTiter-Glo ATP (CTG 3D) measurements as labels to potentially enhance accuracy. Powell et al. developed deepOrganoid, using an 18-layer ResNet to map bright-field and z stack projection images of colorectal cancer organoids to CTG readings.147 Giczewska et al. treated GBM organoids with different drug combinations, cultured them continuously for 18 days, and collected image and CTG data on the 18th day, upon which they built a DL prediction model using DenseNet.148 Deben et al. developed OrBITS software to analyze bright-field images, similarly trained on CTG readings in lung and pancreatic cancer organoids.149 Models structured with FCN can establish mappings from factors such as total image mask area to CTG fluorescence intensity. Ferreira et al. developed OrganoIDNet, leveraging StarDist to correlate factors such as average pixel intensity, size, and shape (eccentricity) of PDAC organoids with CTG readings.122 Particularly unique to this research is its consideration of immune cell impact on tumor cells, incorporating additional variables based on co-culturing with immune cells. Bian et al. used a fully connected CNN model to predict the ATP-based organoid viability based on bright-field images.150 The researchers introduced an attention mechanism that assigns weights according to the divided “instances.” Manually selected instances are generally image patches of a fixed size; for example, the original image is divided into 16 patches, with each patch being an instance. Alternatively, the model can be allowed to learn and generate instances through a module composed of several downsampled convolutional layers. The researchers also had the model perform multi-task learning with combined optimization of regression and classification to adapt to smaller datasets.

Models applied for cell viability assessment typically perform regression tasks, which require modifications to the model’s output layer to provide continuous numerical values as output. Some studies use industry experts’ judgments as ground truth to train the models. Expert judgments derive their value from leveraging domain expertise to interpret complex biological features and contextualize nuanced biological meanings—particularly critical in emerging fields like organoid research where mature automated metrics are lacking. However, its reliability is constrained by inherent limitations, including inter- and intra-annotator variability, subjectivity stemming from prior knowledge, and scalability challenges with large datasets. To address these, strategies such as multi-annotator consensus, standardized guidelines, semi-supervised workflows combining expert labels with model-assisted annotation, and integration of quantitative metrics have been adopted to enhance consistency and efficiency.151,152 Looking forward, advancements in dynamic consensus learning and cross-modal integration may further strengthen the robustness of expert evaluation as a foundational ref.153,154 Some researchers apply non-fully convolutional ResNet and DenseNet as models, meaning that a single image can only provide one prediction value. When multiple organoids are present in an image, summarizing them with a single cell viability value can lead to oversimplification. Therefore, it is recommended to train instance segmentation models to perform such tasks.

Applications in dynamics analysis of tumor organoids

In the field of organoids, dynamics of 3D cultures pose a significant departure from traditional 2D studies due to their unique 3D structures. This is particularly crucial in the realm of tumor organoids given the invasive nature of tumor cells. Image analysis, owing to its intuitiveness, stands out as a key method for studying dynamics.

Tumor organoids cultured in porous plates can exhibit invasiveness through their invasion into the surrounding stroma. The tumor organoid contour features can be extracted through imaging and post-processing algorithms to quantitatively characterize invasiveness. Initial analyses of tumor spheroid invasiveness relied on manual methods. Vinci et al. introduced a basement membrane matrix incorporated into porous plates to study tumor spheroid invasion into the matrix.155 Images acquired using a microscope were manually measured for invasive pseudopodia-related morphological parameters (such as area, diameter, perimeter, and so forth). To achieve automation, Hou et al. developed TASI.156 Researchers collected 3D bright-field images of non-small cell lung cancer spheroids infiltrated in a gelatinous matrix within 14 h, followed by establishing fitted models for various morphological parameters (including area, invasion radius, core radius, complexity, and so forth), and subsequently modeling spheroid dynamics over time using these morphological parameters.

CNNs are capable of achieving precise automatic semantic segmentation of images, delineating the edges of tumor organoids and facilitating more accurate quantitative analysis of invasiveness. Ngo et al. utilized images of MDA-MB-231 breast cancer organoids under DIC microscopy for study.157 They built a model using EfficientNet as an encoder and U-Net++ as a decoder. This model accurately delineates core area and invasive area of spheroid organoids. To detect individual cells separated from the tumor spheroids, the model needs to improve its ability to acquire multi-scale information. An architecture using RegNetY320 as the encoder and FPN as the decoder was employed. FPN completes the task of extracting small-scale information, while RegNet provides a streamlined and efficient network architecture. The model results in higher accuracy compared to the EfficientNet combined with the U-Net++ model in this task. Chen et al. developed SMART, based on the U-Net architecture, for the precise segmentation of breast cancer, colon cancer, and lung cancer organoids.158 To capture the subtle differences at the edges of organoids caused by tumor invasion, the researchers applied pyramid pooling to the encoder of U-Net. Based on the segmentation, the researchers proposed two indices: the Excess Perimeter Index (EPI) and the Multi-Scale Entropy Index (MSEI) as evaluation metrics for organoid invasiveness.

Over the course of long-term organoid cultivation, organoids might migrate across the focal plane. Additionally, variations in size and shape are liable to arise across distinct tissue types, within the same tissue type, and even among organoids from one and the same culture sample. Continuous real-time imaging of organoids is impractical due to the large data volumes from 3D z stack imaging mentioned earlier. Consequently, commonly used research methods involve periodic image sampling followed by subsequent tracking and analysis of organoid behavior. The following studies demonstrate the approach of whole-organoid tracking using organoid morphological features and positional coordinates, which relies on the model’s precise extraction of various information across the entire microscope field of view. Bao et al. employed the iterative closest point (ICP) algorithm for simplified point clouds to perform image alignment after segmentation using EGO-Net. They then matched organoids with similar centroid positions in adjacent images.124 The ICP algorithm treats organoids as a “point cloud” composed of a series of points; after certain simplification, it iteratively finds the closest points between the source point cloud and the target point cloud as the closest point pairs. All these closest point pairs are used to calculate the optimal transformation matrix, thereby aligning the two images.159 Matthews et al.'s OrganoID utilized a U-Net model for precise segmentation of organoids, followed by developing a tracking module.5 This module defines a cost as the reciprocal of shared pixels between organoids in two images, and then seeks the lowest cost by constructing a cost matrix, thereby aligning positions and shapes of organoids. Such algorithms rely on organoid imaging and segmentation accurate to the level of individual pixels. Branciforti et al.'s research employed segmentation results from K-Net to establish a dual-branch tracking algorithm, utilizing key information such as centroid positions, volumes, and shapes for organoid tracking.125 The algorithm first selects an image from a specific time point as a reference, and then advances forward and backward from this reference (i.e., a dual-branch approach) to locate the corresponding organoids in other images. The search is based on a score calculated from the organoid’s feature attributes (such as position, volume, and so forth) and similarity measures (such as shape descriptors, IoU, and so forth).

There are also studies that use DL techniques for cell-level tracking. One approach is to abstract cells as a set of points, then use a feedforward network (FFN) to learn the motion patterns of the point set, combined with a CNN architecture to achieve tracking. Wen et al.'s 3DeeCellTracker allows more detailed segmentation of cell nuclei.129 Researchers integrated a local unit strategy (using local area information for localization) and spatial distribution strategy (using overall migration patterns for prediction) with FFN. Since images cannot be directly used as the input data of FFN since the model does not include convolutional layers, this study first used 3D U-Net for image segmentation to obtain cell position information. Then, applying FFN to study cell migration patterns is feasible. A distinctive feature of this study is that the FFN-based model achieves instance segmentation tracking. The reason for this is that the FFN learns the distance between a specific cell and its neighboring cells, and since the overall migration pattern of the cells is known, it can also represent specific cell instances through this parameter. The model achieved a 97% correct cell tracking rate in a tumor spheroid dataset, including cells moving across layers, characterized by signal intensity, and describing macroscopic invasion features based on microscopic cell movements. Even in datasets with severe noise, the model exhibits robustness. Liu et al. developed SiQ-3D, which also uses FFN to analyze position vectors on the segmentation results of 3D U-Net for single-cell tracking of gastric cancer organoids.160 Building on this, the researchers further optimized the results using the Particle Representation-Graph Laplacian Smoothing (PR-GLS) algorithm. The study also highlighted the limitations of AI models for single-cell tracking. For cells that can move over large distances, such as NK cells, the researchers did not use AI algorithms but instead chose a Bayesian-based method for analysis. This was due to the difficulty of AI models in finding correlations caused by the long-distance migration of NK cells; in addition, the strong deformability of NK cells and the challenge in summarizing their contour features made instance segmentation models difficult to train effectively. However, since the migration ability of tumor organoids is more limited, AI still holds promising potential for applications in this area.

Models employed for studying stromal invasion typically perform precise segmentation tasks, then quantify invasion capabilities based on the segmentation results by measuring area, radius, perimeter, complexity ratio, EPI, and MSEI. The basic U-Net architecture can be utilized for segmentation, but applying FPN can handle images containing multi-scale objects, enabling more refined segmentation. When it comes to tracking the overall organoid, researchers typically match organoids between different frames based on parameters such as morphological features and spatial location. Two other studies aim to track individual cells, both of which achieve this by combining the known migration patterns of specific cell types with FFN. These studies primarily used semantic segmentation models. In the future, using instance segmentation networks is expected to help avoid misidentifying different organoids as the same one due to artifacts, overlaps, or other reasons, thereby improving the Summary of deep learning models and requirements for tumor Organoid applications

Future outlook

The AI technology based on images, as introduced above, has already been effectively applied to some downstream analysis directions of tumor organoids. The results of these analyses can detect structural damage to tumor cells, assess cytotoxicity, and evaluate tumor invasiveness suppression abilities, which are sufficient to support clinical tumor drug screening. However, these analyses do not provide a deep understanding of the mechanisms through which drugs act, thus offering limited support for the development of tumor drugs. These analyses cannot perform non-invasive and precise tumor classification, which restricts their application in clinical diagnosis. The current analytical methods are mainly limited by two factors: the simulation of the tumor environment and the interpretability of AI models. Addressing these two shortcomings could enable the application of AI technology in radiomics research, with the potential to reveal deeper molecular biological information and have broader applications in drug development, clinical diagnosis, and other fields.

The tumor organoids constructed in most studies are still simple spheroids formed from a single cell line. This lack of interaction between the tumor and its surrounding environment in a real in vivo setting prevents the formation of the structures required for radiomics research. Organoid co-culture technology can simulate the TME and create more realistic tumor-surrounding tissue structures.16,161 This technology combines organoids from different cell types into a larger system, such as co-culturing tumor organoids with immune cells or fibroblasts. This can simulate features of the in vivo environment, such as angiogenesis, immune cell infiltration, and tumor blood flow dynamics, which are important foundations in tumor radiomics research. Microfluidic technology can precisely manipulate specific substances in the TME, such as generating concentration gradients of oxygen, nutrients, pH, and so forth, using multiple gradient generators. The rise of 3D printing has provided a novel strategy. On the one hand, it enables the fabrication of perfusable biomimetic blood vessels capable of seeding endothelial cells, with tumor spheroids printed adjacent to these vessels to establish a co-culture system.162 On the other hand, the modification of commonly used hydrogel materials allows for the controllable induction of distinct cellular phenotypes at different spatial locations. For instance, in one study, the removal of the RGD motif from elastin-like polypeptide materials at specific sites (a motif also present in various widely used hydrogels including gelatin and acellular matrix) impaired integrin-mediated cell adhesion, thereby inducing the formation of distinct compartments within tumor organoids.163 This model can recapitulate key features of tumors, such as hypoxic and necrotic regions, which also serve as critical research foundations for tumor radiomics. Specifically, it enables the enhancement of multiple characteristics of organoid images, thereby furnishing abundant and valuable variables for omics research. These variables can include manually extracted features commonly used in radiomics, such as first-order gradient mean, variance, randomness, kurtosis, and skewness; second-order features like gray-level co-occurrence matrix, gray-level run length matrix, gray-level size zone matrix, and neighborhood gray-level difference matrix; as well as higher-order features like Minkowski functionals, wavelets, and Laplacian transformations of Gaussian bandpass filters. More abstract features can be extracted through CNNs, which raises the need for model interpretability.

Although DL models outperform traditional ML models in many aspects, the lack of interpretability remains a challenge, making it difficult for researchers to gain deeper insights from them in the biomedical field. ML models typically provide specific feature parameters, which can help researchers assess the model’s validity, cross-reference with other studies, screen for potential overfitting results, and increase trust in the model. DL models are “black-box” models, and humans cannot fully understand the significance of the large combinations of parameters within them. As medical decisions are directly related to patient health and life safety, the interpretability of the model (rather than just prediction accuracy) becomes a core requirement. AI applied in the medical field must go through strict regulatory approval, and interpretability is a core compliance indicator. Regulatory bodies require that the decision-making basis of the model be “traceable and verifiable” to avoid the medical risks posed by black-box models. Current explanation methods for DL models include saliency maps, counterfactual methods, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (GRAD-CAM), and Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP). The essence of saliency maps is to calculate the gradient of the input image pixels with respect to the model output. Pixels with larger absolute gradient values are considered more important for the model’s decision.164 This method is simple to compute and requires no modification to the model but is easily affected by noise. The generated saliency maps usually have the same resolution as the original image, so more information focuses on low-level edges and textures, with insufficient semantic information. The idea behind the counterfactual method is to construct an alternative sample (counterfactual sample) that differs from the original input by the “smallest difference” in such a way that the model’s prediction for this sample is different from the original result.165 By comparing the original sample and the counterfactual sample, we can infer which input features’ changes lead to a reversal of the decision. These features are the key factors in the model’s decision-making process. This method is intuitive, but the results may not be unique, and the computational cost is high. GRAD-CAM uses the feature map of the last convolutional layer of the CNN (which contains high-level semantic information) and calculates the contribution weight of each feature map to the target class through gradients.166 The weighted fusion of feature maps results in a heatmap. This method focuses more on the semantic regions of the image but requires the resolution of the last convolutional layer’s feature map. SHAP is a game-theory-based explanation framework that quantifies the contribution of each feature to the prediction result using Shapley values.167 Shapley values come from game theory and are used to fairly distribute “cooperative gains.” In ML, the model prediction is considered the “total gain,” and the Shapley value of each input feature (such as a region in an image) represents its contribution to the prediction. The results obtained from this method are based on rigorous theory, making them more reliable, but it is sensitive to the way regions are partitioned. In general, these methods can visualize the features extracted by CNN models, further supporting radiomics research and advancing the application of organoid technology in clinical oncology diagnosis and treatment.

Even if the aforementioned technical issues are resolved, tumor organoid technology integrated with AI image analysis still faces challenges in clinical translation. First, there is a lack of image datasets for tumor organoids. Most studies cultivate tumor organoids and create their own image datasets independently. Only a few studies have made their datasets openly available.119,121,168,169 However, these public datasets have limited imaging modalities and only involve specific types of tumor organoids. The quality of ground truth masks, which are occasionally manually or semi-automatically generated, is often poor, potentially adversely affecting model training. Furthermore, datasets that simultaneously include multi-omics information and images are extremely scarce. Alcala et al. reported a multi-omics dataset of neuroendocrine tumor organoids, which includes tumor organoids derived from various organs, with primary and metastatic lesions each accounting for half, and covering 2 to 3 time points.170 In addition to RNA sequencing data, the image data includes hematoxylin and eosin staining, bright-field images, and immunohistochemical images of EGFR protein. However, overall, there is still a lack of complete multi-omics image datasets for various types of tumors. To enhance the generalization ability of models, the ideal model should be trained on datasets encompassing various imaging modalities, resolutions, and types of organoids. Current public datasets cannot meet this demand. The quality of images captured by different research teams varies, making it difficult to ensure the robustness of AI models during training. Therefore, there is a need to develop quantitative evaluation metrics for 3D microscopy image quality, such as the Fourier ring correlation-based quality estimation (FRC-QE) proposed by Preusser et al.171 The volume of image datasets is also an important indicator. While some models (e.g., Transformer-based ones) do not require extremely large amounts of data, large datasets are crucial for anti-overfitting, improving generalization ability, and making full use of model capacity. Unfortunately, annotating tumor organoid image data is not a simple task. If purely manual annotation by experts is adopted, problems such as heavy workload and interference from subjective factors will arise. These issues will be further amplified in scenarios requiring precise segmentation results. In the case of semi-automatic annotation—where traditional segmentation algorithms are first used for processing, followed by manual screening or modification—there may still be certain omissions, and annotators may overly trust the results of automated procedures. A study mentioned earlier proposed an interesting approach to generating datasets using VOs.109 This has significantly increased the degree of automation and reduced workload, but systematic evaluation criteria for the credibility of VOs are still lacking.

Overall, organoids have provided a reliable experimental platform for tumor disease research. When combined with microscopic imaging and AI technologies, organoids will be able to provide researchers with more information. Through breakthroughs in organoid culture technology and CNN interpretability technology, tumor organoids can be applied to more clinical diagnosis and treatment scenarios, and are expected to become a core force in tumor disease research.
