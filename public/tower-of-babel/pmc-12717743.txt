The relationship between social risk profile of middle-aged and older adults in the united States and circadian syndrome and all-cause mortality: evidence from NHANES 2007–2018
Authors: Haojun Huang, Defeng Dong, Yi Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCircadian Syndrome (CircS) is a prevalent condition with public health implications. The Social Risk Profile (SRP) may be associated with metabolic function, sleep quality, and mental health. Limited research has explored the relationship between SRP, CircS, and all-cause mortality. This study aims to examine these associations in middle-aged and older adults in the U.S. using NHANES data (2007–2018).MethodsThis cross-sectional study used NHANES data, with mortality events determined through the National Death Index (2019). Logistic and Cox regression analyses assessed associations between SRP, CircS, and all-cause mortality. Restricted cubic splines were used to explore nonlinear relationships. Subgroup analysis was conducted to examine differences across groups.ResultsThe study included 2,681 participants. Logistic regression showed a positive correlation between SRP and CircS prevalence, as well as all-cause mortality. Subgroup analysis revealed stronger correlations in females and alcohol consumers. Cox regression showed a significant link between SRP and all-cause mortality. No nonlinear relationship was observed.ConclusionsSRP is positively correlated with CircS and all-cause mortality, particularly among alcohol consumers and females. Higher SRP levels are associated with increased risk of all-cause mortality in middle-aged and older adults.Keywords: Middle-aged and older adults, Social risk profile, Circadian syndrome, All-cause mortality, NHANES

Introduction

Economic development and changes in lifestyle have led to an increase in the prevalence of long-term suboptimal health, particularly within the elderly population. This health condition significantly reduces the stability of the circadian rhythm and increases sensitivity to external environmental disruptions, thereby further exacerbating the risk of multimorbidity in the elderly [1]. Multimorbidity has become a global public health issue, leading to increased disability and mortality rates, reduced quality of life, and a heavier disease burden [2–5]. In recent years, as understanding of internal biological rhythms has advanced, circadian syndrome (CircS) has been proposed as a more comprehensive symptom cluster for metabolic syndrome (MetS) and its comorbidities [1]. CircS is diagnosed when the following four conditions coexist: dyslipidemia (high triglycerides or low high-density lipoprotein), hypertension, central obesity, diabetes, depression, and sleep disorders [6, 7]. The circadian rhythm system, which plays a crucial role in regulating human health and metabolism, is receiving increasing attention from researchers, who are uncovering the profound impacts of circadian rhythm disruption on human health [7–11]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) latest age classification, individuals aged 45 and older are considered middle-aged or elderly [12]. When middle-aged and elderly individuals remain in an unhealthy state for extended periods, their circadian rhythms are more likely to become fragile and more susceptible to disruption [6]. Therefore, in the context of global health aging, research on circadian rhythm disruption in middle-aged and elderly individuals is of particular importance [13].

According to Healthy People 2030 [14], social factors include economic stability, accessibility and quality of education, healthcare accessibility and quality, the community and built environment, and social and community context. Social risk characteristics may be closely associated with metabolic function, sleep quality, and mental health. A national study has shown that shift work increases the risk of circadian syndrome (CircS) among workers in the United States [15]. Additionally, social factors such as education level and income may also elevate the risk of CircS in middle-aged and older adults in the United States [16]. However, there is limited research on the association between social factors and CircS, and studying individual social factors in isolation may not be sufficient for effectively treating CircS patients. When clinicians fail to recognize the social context of their patients, they may set treatment goals that do not align with the patient’s needs [17], increasing barriers to treatment and assessment. Screening for social risk factors helps describe social challenges, identify individuals at high risk for CircS, and overcome obstacles arising from insufficient clinician preparedness in clinical management [18]. Furthermore, the definition and scope of the social risk profile (SRP) vary across studies. While work, education, and income are associated with CircS [15, 16], the significance of many social factors remains underexplored, and their role in the survival prognosis of middle-aged and older adults in the United States has not yet been clearly established. Therefore, investigating the association between SRP and CircS, as well as all-cause mortality, is not only rational but also contributes to promoting CircS screening and provides important theoretical and practical implications for determining CircS care contexts in clinical practice.

This study aims to utilize data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning 2007 to 2018 to examine the association between social risk profiles (SRP), circadian syndrome (CircS), and all-cause mortality in middle-aged and older adults in the United States. We hypothesized that a higher social risk profile would be significantly associated with a greater prevalence of Circadian Syndrome and all-cause mortality. Additionally, it seeks to provide new guidance for the prevention and treatment of CircS patients.

Methods

Study population

This study employs a cross-sectional design based on the NHANES dataset. The data in the NHANES database is managed by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [19]. The primary aim of NHANES is to collect information on the health and nutrition of the U.S. population, including demographic, socioeconomic, dietary, and health-related aspects [20]. The physical examination section includes physiological measurements and laboratory tests. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), and all participants provided informed consent. This study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki. From 2007 to 2018, a total of 59,842 individuals were recruited. Exclusion criteria included:

Participants with no CircS diagnosis or SRP survey data;

Participants with no CircS diagnosis or SRP survey data;

Participants with missing or abnormal CircS or SRP data;

Participants with missing or abnormal CircS or SRP data;

Participants aged under 45 years;

Participants aged under 45 years;

Participants with missing all-cause mortality data;

Participants with missing all-cause mortality data;

Participants who did not undergo fasting blood tests.

Participants who did not undergo fasting blood tests.

Ultimately, 2,681 participants were included in the study (Fig. 1). Over a median follow-up of 75 months (6.25 years), corresponding to 17465.33 total person-years, 427 incident cases of all-cause mortality were documented.

Assessment of SRP

Data was collected through standardized questionnaires and face-to-face interviews. Based on the five domains of social determinants of health defined by Healthy People 2030 [14], specific indicators were selected based on the following criteria: (1) relevance to the study’s outcome (Circadian Syndrome and all-cause mortality) based on prior literature [21, 22]; (2) high public health significance as leading social determinants of health; (3) availability of standardized, high-quality data within the NHANES dataset; and (4) the need to minimize overlap between indicators to ensure each captured a distinct aspect of social risk. For instance, within the ‘Economic Stability’ domain, the poverty-income ratio (PIR) was selected over other potential metrics (e.g., household income) due to its standardization for household size and its widespread use in health disparities research. The final eight indicators were chosen as they best met these criteria and provided comprehensive coverage across all five domains.

These were dichotomized according to traditional cut-off points and analysis: employment (employed, unemployed); Income level (measured by the family income-to-poverty ratio [PIR]: low PIR < 3, high PIR ≥ 3), the threshold for economic hardship was set at a PIR of < 3.0. This cut-off is consistent with methodologies used in major public health studies to define ‘low-income’ populations [18, 23]. While a PIR < 1.0 defines the official poverty level, a higher threshold of < 3.0 allows for the identification of individuals and families who, despite being above the poverty line, still face substantial financial constraints and insecurity that can negatively impact health access and behaviors [24]. This approach captures a gradient of economic risk that is more sensitive to health disparities than the binary poverty definition; food security (measured using the food security survey module questions [25]: secure if 0 affirmative responses, insecure if ≥ 1 affirmative responses); education (low if high school or below, high if college or above); healthcare access (regular location, no regular location); health insurance (private insurance, government or no insurance); housing instability (owned, rented, or other arrangements); and marital status (married or cohabiting, widowed, divorced, separated, or never married) [26].

Based on previous studies on social risk profiles (SRP) in U.S. populations [18], we defined employment, high PIR, food security, high education level, private insurance, own the house, and being married or cohabiting as positive SRP indicators. SRP scores were graded based on the number of negative SRP indicators, with a range from 0 to 8. A Social Risk Profile (SRP) score was created by summing points assigned for the presence of each social risk factor. Each of the eight risk factors was assigned 1 point if present, and 0 points if absent. The total SRP score could thus range from 0 to 8, with a higher score indicating a higher burden of social risk. Subsequently, SRP scores were categorized into four levels based on quartiles: low (0–2), lower-middle (3–4), upper-middle (5–6), and high (7–8).

Assessment of circs

Based on previous diagnostic criteria for circadian syndrome (CircS), a diagnosis of CircS can be made if four or more of the following seven criteria are met, according to the criteria proposed by Zimmet et al. [6, 7]: (1) Waist circumference ≥ 100 cm for men and ≥ 90 cm for women; (2) Triglyceride levels ≥ 150 mg/dL; (3) HDL-C levels < 40 mg/dL for men and < 50 mg/dL for women; (4) Systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 85 mmHg; (5) Fasting blood glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL; (6) Sleep duration ≤ 6 h; (7) Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [27], a reliable and valid instrument with scores ranging from 0 to 27. A cut-off score of ≥ 10 was used to indicate the presence of clinically relevant depressive symptoms, as recommended in the original publication.

Assessment of mortality

Vital status was determined through December 31, 2023, by linking participant data (including name, date of birth, sex, and Social Security Number) with the National Death Index (NDI) [28]. Probabilistic matching was performed using established algorithms [29]. All-cause mortality was defined as any match confirmed with the NDI.

Covariates

Our selection of covariates are consistent with the adjustment sets used in major cohort studies and clinical guidelines investigating similar associations. Specifically, we followed the approach advocated by initiatives like the STRengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement, which emphasizes adjusting for important confounding variables identified a priori based on subject matter knowledge [30]. Furthermore, a similar covariate set has been employed in seminal papers on public health, such as the work by Li et al. [18] and Xiao et al. [31]. The set includes only baseline measures to avoid adjusting for potential mediators or colliders (variables influenced by both the exposure and outcome), which could introduce bias. The demographic covariates in this study include age (continuous variable), sex (male or female), and race (Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, or Other) [32]. Smoking status was categorized as “Yes” or “No” based on whether the participant smoked [33]. Alcohol consumption was categorized as “Yes” or “No” based on whether the participant consumed alcohol ≥ 12 times in the past 12 months [34]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²) and was categorized according to World Health Organization standards: underweight or normal weight (< 25.0 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–30 kg/m2), and obesity (≥ 30 kg/m2) [8]. Physical activity was categorized as “Never,” “Moderate,” or “Vigorous” based on whether the participant engaged in moderate or vigorous physical activity [35]. Medical history (including gout, stroke, and cancer) was categorized as “Gout,” “Stroke,” or “Cancer” based on whether the participant had been diagnosed with these conditions by a physician [34].Urine creatinine concentration (urine creatinine) was measured using laboratory enzymatic methods [36].

Statistical analysis

The distributions of continuous variables were assessed for normality both visually using histograms and quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots, and statistically using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov(K-S)test. A p-value > 0.05 on the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to indicate adherence to a normal distribution. Homoscedasticity (equality of variances) was evaluated using Levene’s test for comparisons between two groups. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are reported as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as median (P25, P75). Categorical variables are presented as proportions. The social risk profile (SRP) is categorized into quartiles (Q1 to Q4). In univariate analysis, t-tests are used for normally distributed continuous variables, chi-square tests for categorical variables, one-way ANOVA for continuous variables with more than two groups following normal distribution and homogeneity of variances, and non-parametric tests for non-normally distributed or heteroscedastic continuous variables.

Using multivariate logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards regression models, the associations between the social risk profiles (SRP) of middle-aged and elderly people in the United States and all-cause mortality, as well as between circadian rhythm syndrome (CircS) and all-cause mortality were evaluated. These two models were adjusted for the following potential confounders: Crude: Unadjusted for covariates; Model 1: Adjusted for age, sex and race; Model 2: Adjusted for age, sex, race, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, cancer, gout, stroke, and urine creatinine. Selecting these two models enables a more comprehensive analysis of the correlations between SRP and all-cause mortality, as well as between CircS and all-cause mortality.

We also examined SRP’s associations with CircS prevalence, all-cause mortality, and the relationship between CircS and mortality using restricted cubic spline (RCS) dose-response curves, adjusted by Model 2. Pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted by stratifying the sample by Sex (Male, Female), Smoking status (Yes, No), Alcohol consumption (Yes, No), BMI (< 25.0 kg/m2, 25.0–30 kg/m2, or ≥ 30 kg/m2), Physical activity (Never, Moderate, or Vigorous). The statistical significance of each interaction was assessed using the likelihood ratio test (LRT), comparing the model with the interaction term to the nested model without it. The covariates adjusted in the subgroup analysis included age, race, cancer, gout, stroke and urine creatinine. A p-value for interaction < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We also show whether the association between exposure and outcome is significant within each subgroup. This approach can better and more comprehensively present the results of the subgroup analysis. We use the symbol “*” to indicate the size of the P value. “*” indicates P < 0.05, “**” indicates P < 0.01, and “***” indicates P < 0.001. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and RStudio (version 4.4.2), with a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Subsequently, participants under the age of 45 were excluded (N = 4,546), as well as those who did not provide fasting blood samples, had missing mortality data, or had missing or abnormal data for other variables (N = 3,574). Ultimately, 2,681 participants were included in the final analysis. The average age of participants was 62.08 ± 10.40 years, with the majority being non-Hispanic White (52.9%). Participants with a high SRP were generally older, predominantly other Hispanics, had a body mass index (BMI) of less than 25.0 kg/m², were smokers, drank alcohol, engaged in less physical activity, did not have cancer, had a history of stroke, and had CircS (P < 0.05). Participants with CircS were typically female, Mexican American, had a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m², did not drink alcohol, engaged in less physical activity, and had a history of gout or stroke (P < 0.05). A significant difference in SRP scores was observed between participants with and without CircS (P < 0.05). Participants with lower SRP scores, including factors such as unemployment, low PIR, food insecurity, education level below college, and lack of private insurance, had a higher risk of CircS (P < 0.05). Older adults in the U.S. with high SRP had a higher risk of all-cause mortality (P < 0.05). See Tables 1 and 2.

Association between SRP levels and circs and all-cause mortality

We classified the SRP scores into four categories based on quartiles: low (0–2), low-medium (3–4), medium-high (5–6), and high (7–8), and evaluated the associations between each group and circadian syndrome (CircS, Table 3) and all-cause mortality (Table 4). The prevalence of CircS showed a positive correlation with SRP in the crude model, with participants in the third quartile of SRP having the highest risk of CircS. Participants in the fourth quartile of SRP exhibited a lower risk of CircS than those in the third quartile (P < 0.05). This relationship remained statistically significant after adjusting for covariates, including age, sex, race, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, cancer, gout, stroke, and urinary creatinine (P < 0.05). Therefore, a non-linear relationship may exist between SRP and CircS.

Cox regression analysis

Over a median follow-up of 75 months (6.25 years), corresponding to 17465.33 total person-years, 427 incident cases of all-cause mortality were documented, resulting in an overall mortality rate of 15.9% (427/2681). In the NHANES 2007–2018 survey, participants who experienced all-cause mortality had significantly higher SRP scores compared to those who did not, with a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05). The cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality was 31.1% (133/427) among participants with circadian syndrome (CircS), whereas it was 28.0% (631/2254) among those without CircS, but this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.186). Using the Cox regression risk model, we adjusted for covariates including age, sex, race, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, cancer, gout, stroke, and urinary creatinine, and further analyzed the relationship between SRP, CircS, and all-cause mortality through survival curves and cumulative incidence curves.

Cox regression analysis revealed that, in the NHANES 2007–2018 survey, participants with higher SRP scores had a higher cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality compared to those with lower SRP scores [HR = 1.86, 95% CI (1.21–2.87), P < 0.05]. After adjusting for 11 covariates, including age, sex, race, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, cancer, gout, stroke, and urinary creatinine, the results remained statistically significant (P < 0.05), as shown in Table 6. However, after adjusting for these same 11 covariates, no statistically significant difference in all-cause mortality was observed between participants with circadian syndrome (CircS) and those without CircS (P = 0.455), as shown in Table 7.

Discussion

This study explored the association between SRP, CircS, and all-cause mortality among middle-aged and older adults in the United States using a large, nationally representative sample (N = 2,681). The results revealed significant associations between SRP and both CircS and all-cause mortality, with interactions observed between these associations and other participant characteristics. We found that middle-aged and elderly people with high social risk scores tend to have a higher prevalence of CircS [OR = 1.49, 95% CI (0.97–2.29), P = 0.001] and mortality [OR = 2.44, 95% CI (1.42–4.20), P < 0.001]. Cox regression analysis revealed that middle-aged and older adults with high SRP had a higher risk of mortality [OR = 2.14, 95% CI (1.35–3.39), P < 0.001]. However, the non-linear relationship between SRP and both CircS and all-cause mortality was not significant. Among individuals with the same SRP level, females and those who consumed alcohol had a higher risk of CircS, and alcohol consumption was associated with a higher mortality risk. No significant non-linear relationships were observed in the restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves.

CircS is associated with various social factors. The results of this study indicate that unemployment, low PIR, food insecurity, education levels below college, and lack of private health insurance are risk factors for CircS among middle-aged and older adults in the United States. Unemployed individuals typically have lower PIR, and low PIR is associated with higher rates of depression, while higher education levels and private health insurance are considered protective factors against depression symptoms [37]. Research has shown that food insecurity is linked to insufficient sleep, which may, in turn, impact mental health [38], potentially contributing to the development of CircS. Married middle-aged and older adults are more likely to have access to regular healthcare, which is associated with better health outcomes, including reduced cardiovascular disease risk and improved sleep quality [39]. Housing instability undermines essential elements for managing cardiovascular risk factors, such as diabetes, obesity, and hypertension, including continuous management, reliable care, and access to basic needs like kitchen and bathroom facilities [40]. However, the results of this study show no significant association between housing status, access to regular healthcare, marital status, or cohabitation with the risk of CircS. Our study sample, derived from the NHANES survey, likely underrepresents the most vulnerable segments of the population. As we observed, most participants had stable housing, access to health care, and were cohabiting. This profile suggests a potential selection bias, as individuals facing extreme poverty, homelessness, or severe illness may be systematically less likely to participate in a comprehensive health examination survey like NHANES. Consequently, the association between social risk and Circadian Syndrome reported in our study may be a conservative estimate, and the true effect in the overall population, particularly among the most disadvantaged, might be even stronger. Future studies specifically designed to reach these underserved populations are needed.

Chronic disease comorbidity is a global challenge, particularly in countries with rapidly aging populations [41]. This phenomenon negatively impacts the daily life, caregiving, leisure, and professional activities of older adults with multiple chronic conditions. Additionally, it accelerates functional decline and shortens life expectancy [42]. Previous research has shown a positive correlation between the prevalence and mortality rates of CircS among middle-aged and older adults in the United States [43]. However, the results of this study indicate that, after adjusting for all covariates, there was no significant association between CircS prevalence and all-cause mortality. Nonetheless, survival and mortality curves suggest that middle-aged and older adults with CircS seem to have a higher risk of mortality, although this difference was not statistically significant. This phenomenon may be related to the nature of CircS as a comorbidity of chronic diseases, which may not directly lead to death in the short term, potentially introducing bias in the results. Moreover, the course of CircS is not fixed but a dynamic process that can change with acquired therapeutic interventions, lifestyle adjustments, and the passage of time [44].

The results of this study indicate that middle-aged and older adults who do not consume alcohol are more likely to have CircS. The observed association between alcohol abstinence and higher CircS prevalence should be interpreted with caution. In this study, alcohol consumption was classified only as “yes” or “no,” without information on quantity, frequency, or drinking patterns. This binary categorization conflates lifelong abstainers with former drinkers, the latter of whom may have ceased alcohol use due to underlying health issues (reverse causation). Additionally, without stratification by drinking level, it is not possible to differentiate potential effects of moderate versus heavy alcohol consumption. Consequently, the finding may reflect residual confounding rather than a causal relationship. However, subgroup analysis results indicate that among U.S. middle-aged and older adults with the same SRP scores, alcohol consumers have a higher risk of developing CircS, which may be linked to the higher tendency of alcohol abuse among those with higher SRP. Therefore, future research should further explore the relationship between varying levels of alcohol consumption and the prevalence of CircS. The subgroup analysis in this study may have the issue of insufficient effect size, resulting in the lack of statistical significance for the interactions between certain subgroups. Therefore, the point estimates for these subgroups still have reference value.

Social factors are associated with all-cause mortality among middle-aged and older adults in the United States. The results of this study indicate a positive correlation between the SRP and all-cause mortality in this population. All-cause mortality among middle-aged and older adults is influenced by a variety of socio-economic and health factors. Studies have shown that older adults with lower household PIR have higher all-cause mortality rates, likely due to limited access to resources [45]. Individuals with lower education levels also face higher mortality risks, which may be attributed to inadequate health behaviors and limited access to medical resources [46]. Furthermore, marital status significantly affects health, with unmarried, divorced, or widowed individuals facing higher all-cause mortality rates compared to married individuals, possibly due to the absence of psychological support and social networks [47]. Employment, food security, access to regular healthcare, private insurance, and housing conditions in middle-aged and older adults indirectly affect health outcomes by influencing socio-economic status, health behaviors, and access to medical resources [48]. SRP may lead to unhealthy lifestyle behaviors, such as irregular diets, lack of exercise, and excessive alcohol consumption. These behaviors are associated with circadian rhythm disruption and increased risk of chronic diseases, and may also increase psychological stress, leading to anxiety and depression, which can further affect circadian rhythms [49]. SRP is significantly associated with all-cause mortality. Older adults with high SRP often reside in resource-poor areas, where access to timely medical care is limited. Additionally, these areas often experience higher levels of air pollution (e.g., PM2.5) and community violence, which directly increase the risk of mortality from cardiovascular and respiratory diseases [50]. Therefore, improving these socio-economic and health-related factors is crucial for reducing all-cause mortality in middle-aged and older adults.

Compared with previous studies, this study has advantages such as a large sample size and a wide range of the study population, and the correlation between SRP and CircS was discovered for the first time. However, the design type of this study is a cross-sectional study, and it is impossible to accurately infer causal associations. And we lack data on the usage of medications, such as those for lowering blood pressure and treating depression. Due to the inability to adjust for these potential confounding factors, this may result in residual confounding factors when we assess the association between SRP and CircS, SRP and all-cause mortality, CircS and all-cause mortality. Future research should include longer follow-up periods, take into account more confounding factors and closely monitor the survival status of middle-aged and older adults with CircS to better understand the long-term impact of CircS on the quality of life and mortality in this population. Finally, our analysis did not incorporate the complex survey weights of the NHANES. Therefore, although the reported internal associations are applicable to testing our causal hypotheses, these estimates cannot be generalized to represent the national prevalence or absolute risk. These limitations are all issues that future research urgently needs to address. We hope that the results of this study can lay the foundation for future related research.

Conclusions

This study indicates a positive correlation between social risk profile (SRP) and the prevalence of circadian syndrome (CircS), particularly among women and alcohol consumers. SRP is also positively correlated with all-cause mortality, especially within the alcohol-consuming group. Middle-aged and older adults in the United States with higher SRP have an increased risk of all-cause mortality. However, further high-quality prospective cohort studies are needed to explore the causal relationship between SRP and CircS.
