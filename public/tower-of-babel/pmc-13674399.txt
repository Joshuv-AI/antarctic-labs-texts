Correlation Properties of Heart Rate Variability for Exercise Intensity Monitoring and Prescription During Constant‐Load Cycling: A Comparison With Traditional Physiological Variables
Authors: Noemí Sempere‐Ruiz, Agustín Manresa‐Rocamora, Ana Sanz‐Rocher, Sabina Baladzhaeva, Laura Fuertes‐Kenneally, Manuel Moya‐Ramón, Olaf Hoos, José M. Sarabia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study investigated the applicability of the short‐term scaling exponent alpha 1 of detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA‐a1) as a marker of internal load during constant‐load cycling. The aim was to explore DFA‐a1 dynamics across the moderate, heavy, and severe intensity domains and assess its utility for both monitoring and prescribing exercise intensity. Sixteen untrained adults (3 women) performed an incremental cycling stage test and three constant‐load sessions (moderate, heavy, and severe). Gas exchange kinetics, blood lactate concentration, and heart rate (HR) were measured. Ventilatory (VTs), lactate, and HR variability (HRV) thresholds were determined and used to define training zones. A two‐factor repeated‐measures ANOVA and the Friedman test assessed intensity‐ and method‐related effects. Significant intensity × time interactions were observed for all variables (p < 0.001). DFA‐a1 remained stable without crossing the first HRV threshold throughout moderate exercise, while in the heavy and severe domains, it showed a drift over time, mirroring the behaviour of traditional markers. In the moderate session, DFA‐a1‐based prescription maintained > 94% session time and 87%–100% of participants within the desired zone, outperforming HR methods based on VTs (71% time, 50%–81% participants; p < 0.05). In the heavy and severe domains, corresponding values were > 23% and > 50%, whereas gas exchange or lactate prescriptions exceeded 44% and 65%, respectively (p < 0.01). DFA‐a1 appears to be a suitable tool for monitoring internal load, especially in moderate intensity exercise. It offers a non‐invasive, integrative measure sensitive to both intensity and duration, providing a valuable complementary perspective on systemic regulation.

Introduction

Exercise intensity and its distribution are often considered among the most crucial and extensively discussed variables in training. Exercise intensity is generally categorised into three domains: moderate, heavy, and severe, each characterised by distinct physiological responses in heart rate (HR), oxygen uptake (VO2), and blood lactate concentration ([La−]) (Jamnick et al. 2020). In the moderate domain, these variables reach a steady state above resting levels (Zuccarelli et al. 2018; Whipp 1994; Beneke et al. 2011). In the heavy domain, VO2 exhibits a ‘slow component’, defined as a progressive increase in VO2 over time despite constant workload, resulting in a delayed steady state (Whipp 1994; Xu and Rhodes 1999); in parallel, HR typically shows a progressive increase over time (Zuccarelli et al. 2018), while [La−] rises above baseline and subsequently stabilises (Beneke et al. 2011). In the severe domain, VO2 continues to rise without attaining a steady state due to the slow component (Whipp 1994), and HR and [La−] increase progressively until exhaustion (Zuccarelli et al. 2018; Beneke et al. 2011). These exercise intensity domains are commonly delineated using submaximal physiological thresholds derived from either gas exchange or [La−] responses. The first threshold, identified by the first ventilatory (VT1) or lactate (LT1) threshold, represents the transition between the moderate and heavy domains, while the second threshold, identified by the second ventilatory (VT2) or lactate (LT2) threshold, reflects the transition between the heavy and severe domains. When these transitions are identified through gas exchange analysis, the methods used are commonly referred to as the gas exchange threshold (GET) for VT1 and the respiratory compensation point (RCP) for VT2, both representing the same underlying physiological transitions (Jamnick et al. 2020).

Although physiological thresholds are widely used to prescribe exercise intensity and delineate exercise domains within a robust framework, their determination frequently requires invasive and time‐consuming laboratory procedures, which may limit their practicality in applied settings. In addition, traditional threshold‐based approaches typically assume fixed boundaries between intensity domains, whereas physiological responses may evolve over time during prolonged exercise, reflecting the concept of durability, defined as the progressive deterioration of physiological characteristics under sustained workloads (Maunder et al. 2021), which may represent a fourth dimension of endurance exercise performance (Jones 2024). Moreover, threshold determination may vary depending on protocols and data interpretation (Jamnick et al. 2020), which may consequently influence exercise intensity prescription.

Despite these considerations, thresholds remain essential for defining training zones in the three‐zone model: Zone 1 (below the first threshold), Zone 2 (between thresholds), and Zone 3 (above the second threshold) (Seiler and Kjerland 2006). These zones correspond to moderate, heavy, and severe intensity domains, respectively, and exercise monitoring is typically based on these divisions.

Among the internal load monitoring methods, HR is the most widely used for its non‐invasive, simple, and cost‐effective nature. However, HR progressively increases over time during prolonged exercise, even at low‐to‐moderate intensities (Zuccarelli et al. 2018), which may complicate maintaining a consistent internal load without adjusting the external workload (Maunder et al. 2021; Smyth et al. 2022).

Technological advances have improved the accessibility of heart rate variability (HRV) measurement, have proven to provide valuable insights into internal load in both laboratory and field settings and enable real‐time feedback on exercise intensity when appropriate signal quality and processing are ensured (Gronwald, Berk, et al. 2021; Rogers et al. 2022). Among HRV metrics, the short‐term scaling exponent alpha one of detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA‐a1) has emerged as a promising biomarker of global organismic demand across exercise intensities (Rogers, Mourot, Doucende, and Gronwald 2021), and recent studies have demonstrated the validity of mobile applications for estimating DFA‐a1 during exercise, showing good agreement with established software (Rogers, Murias, and Fleitas‐Paniagua 2025). This dimensionless index quantifies fractal‐like correlation properties within HR time series, offering a non‐invasive and continuous measure of the autonomic nervous system response to exercise. It captures systemic stress induced by exercise and the organism's ability to sustain physiological stability over prolonged efforts (Rogers et al. 2022). The physiological basis underlying these changes is likely related to the complex interaction dynamics of autonomic and other physiological influences acting on the intrinsic rhythm of the heart. During exercise, there is a progressive withdrawal of parasympathetic activity alongside an increase in sympathetic drive at the sinoatrial node, which alters heart rate regulation and reduces the fractal correlation and self‐similarity of RR interval dynamics (Rogers et al. 2022; Gronwald and Hoos 2020). As a result, DFA‐a1 provides a continuous view of the dynamics of autonomic interaction across exercise intensity domains, from rest to severe intensity. In addition to autonomic modulation, DFA‐a1 reflects the integrated ‘organismic demand’ of the system. Consequently, its behaviour may also be influenced by non‐neural factors such as locomotor–respiratory coupling and metabolic and hormonal responses, as well as external conditions including temperature, hydration status, or oxygen availability (Rogers et al. 2022). Therefore, DFA‐a1 can be interpreted as an integrative marker of systemic load and cardiovascular regulation during exercise.

During resting conditions or low‐intensity exercise, DFA‐a1 values are generally around 1.0 or higher, indicating correlated HR dynamics. As exercise intensity increases, particularly when transitioning from the moderate to the heavy domain, DFA‐a1 values decrease towards 0.75, reflecting a partial loss of these correlations. When exercise reaches the severe intensity domain, DFA‐a1 values drop to around 0.5 or lower, indicating random or anti‐correlated behaviour, corresponding to a breakdown in fractal organisation and a shift towards a more chaotic state of physiological regulation (Gronwald and Hoos 2020; Gronwald et al. 2020). This progressive decline with increasing exercise intensity offers a wide dynamic range for intensity assessment across domains. Consequently, DFA‐a1 has emerged as a promising tool for identifying exercise intensity transitions based on absolute DFA‐a1 values. From elite athletes to clinical populations, several studies have reported associations between VT1/LT1 and VT2/LT2 with DFA‐a1 values of 0.75 and 0.5, respectively (Kaufmann et al. 2023; Rogers, Giles, Draper, Hoos, and Gronwald 2021; Rogers, Giles, Draper, Mourot, and Gronwald 2021; Schaffarczyk et al. 2022; Rogers, Mourot, Doucende, and Gronwald 2021; Rogers et al. 2022; Mateo‐March et al. 2023; Sempere‐Ruiz et al. 2024, 2025; Rogers, Mourot, and Gronwald 2021). In addition, current literature acknowledges that the first DFA‐a1 threshold may present interindividual variability, and its agreement with traditional threshold markers remains an area of ongoing methodological discussion (Rogers, Fleitas‐Paniagua, and Murias 2024). Importantly, DFA‐a1 also demonstrates acceptable to good between‐day reliability during repeated running and cycling sessions (intraclass correlation coefficients ≥ 0.80; smallest worthwhile change ≈ 0.06), further supporting its suitability for day‐to‐day monitoring and training prescription (Sempere‐Ruiz et al. 2024; Van Hooren, Bongers, et al. 2023).

Beyond threshold identification, DFA‐a1 progressive decline in response to accumulated exercise stress makes it a valuable tool to monitor systemic fatigue, durability, and physiological deterioration, and to adjust training loads during prolonged exercise (Rogers, Mourot, Doucende, and Gronwald 2021; van Rassel et al. 2024; Nuuttila et al. 2024; Gronwald, Berk, et al. 2021; Van Hooren, Mennen, et al. 2023; Rogers, Fleitas‐Paniagua, et al. 2025). Additionally, DFA‐a1 has shown potential for exercise prescription within the moderate‐to‐heavy domains during constant‐load exercise (Gronwald et al. 2024).

However, there remains a lack of studies examining DFA‐a1's dynamics across moderate, heavy, and severe domains during different exercise modalities and durations. Understanding its evolution under constant‐load exercise in each domain could offer valuable insights into the temporal dynamics of autonomic regulation. Importantly, DFA‐a1 provides a holistic perspective on exercise regulation by capturing the complex, dynamic, and adaptive interplay of neuro‐autonomic control mechanisms in response to internal and external stressors (Hoos et al. 2025). Exploring its relationship with traditional intensity physiological markers may further improve our understanding of how physiological subsystems respond to exercise duration and intensity.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the behaviour of DFA‐a1 alongside traditional physiological variables during prolonged constant‐load cycling across the three intensity domains. This would assess whether DFA‐a1 could serve as a reliable tool for exercise intensity prescription and monitoring in these conditions. Moreover, should DFA‐a1 demonstrate adequate sensitivity to the progressive physiological changes occurring during constant‐load sessions, it may furnish practitioners with a non‐invasive, real‐time means of monitoring exercise intensity and duration interaction without the need for invasive lactate measures or gas‐exchange analysis. In addition, the temporal evolution of DFA‐a1 would provide not only a practical alternative, but also novel insights into systemic regulation and the behaviour of complex adaptive systems (CAS) during endurance exercise.

Based on the existing literature regarding DFA‐a1 behaviour across exercise intensity domains and its proposed interpretation within the framework of systemic organismic demand, we hypothesised that DFA‐a1 would mainly remain above 0.75 throughout the moderate constant‐load session, while progressively declining over time during the heavy and severe sessions. Specifically, we expected a decrease followed by relative stabilisation around the range of 0.75 to 0.5 in the heavy domain, and a continuous decline below 0.5 in the severe domain.

Design

Each participant completed four sessions on a cycle‐ergometer (Monark, Exercise AB, Vansbro, Sweden): an incremental cycling test to exhaustion and three constant‐load sessions performed in the moderate, heavy, or severe intensity domains, determined by ventilatory thresholds. The order of constant‐load sessions was randomised. Sessions were separated by 6–9 days, scheduled at the same time of day, and participants were instructed to avoid strenuous exercise for 24 h and stimulants for 4 h before each session. Gas exchange kinetics, [La−], and HR were measured in all sessions.

Incremental Cycling Test and Intensity Thresholds Determination

The incremental cycling stage test started after a 3‐min warm‐up at 50 W. Workload increased every 3 min until exhaustion, with individualised increments of 15/20/25 W based on estimated maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) (Neder et al. 1999) to ensure consistent test durations (Jamnick et al. 2018). Previous research suggests that ramp slope does not substantially influence HRVT determination, at least not when expressed relative to HR or VO2 (Fleitas‐Paniagua et al. 2023). Participants cycled at 60–90 rpm (Gronwald et al. 2018). Gas exchange, HR, and power output (PO) were continuously recorded, while [La−] was collected at each stage.

Gas exchange was measured breath‐by‐breath with a calibrated metabolic cart (MasterScreen CPX, Jaeger, Hoechberg, Germany). Ventilatory data were averaged every 10 s, and VO2max was determined as the highest 10‐s average. The peak power output associated with VO2max was defined as Wpeak. VT1 and VT2 were determined using a mixed method approach (Van Hooren, Mennen, et al. 2023) by two experienced researchers (N.S.‐R. and J.M.S.), with discrepancies resolved by consensus. HR and PO at ventilatory thresholds were calculated using temporal interpolation. Previous work has demonstrated that step and ramp tests yield closely comparable ventilatory and lactate threshold responses, supporting the validity of using step‐derived thresholds for exercise prescription (Michalik et al. 2021; Caen et al. 2021).

[La−] samples were collected from the earlobe before the test, during the last 30 s of each stage, and at the end, using a Lactate Scout analyser (SensLab GmbH, Leipzig, Germany) (Tanner et al. 2010). LT1 and LT2 were determined at 2 and 4 mmol·l−1, respectively (Jamnick et al. 2020; Seiler and Kjerland 2006), using ExPhysLab (Keir et al. 2022), supported by the Lactater R package (Mattioni Maturana 2023). HR and [La−] associated with these thresholds were recorded.

HR was continuously monitored using a 3‐lead electrocardiogram (MP35; Biopac Systems Ltd., California, USA) at 1000 Hz using CM5 placement. Biopac filter settings included a 0.05 Hz high‐pass filter and a 150 Hz low‐pass filter. RR intervals were analysed in Kubios HRV Scientific 4.1.1 (Biosignal Analysis and Medical Imaging Group, Department of Physics, University of Kuopio, Kuopio, Finland), with preprocessing settings set to default, including the “Smoothness priors' (Lambda = 500) method for RR detrending (Tarvainen et al. 2014). The automatic beat correction filter was applied, and none of the participants exceeded a 5% artefact level, so all were included in the analysis. DFA‐a1 was calculated with windows of 4–16 beats (Peng et al. 1995), using 2‐min moving windows with 5‐s grid intervals.

HRVT1 and HRVT2 were defined at DFA‐a1 values of 0.75 and 0.5, respectively (Rogers, Giles, Draper, Hoos, and Gronwald 2021). To determine HR at HRV thresholds, we adopted the method proposed by Rogers et al. (2022), whereby DFA‐a1 values were plotted against HR during the incremental test, and linear regression was used to estimate the HR corresponding to DFA‐a1 values of 0.75 and 0.5.

For descriptive purposes, DFA‐a1 values corresponding to VT1 and VT2 were estimated by identifying the DFA‐a1 value associated with the closest heart rate recorded at each ventilatory threshold during the incremental test.

Constant‐Load Sessions

Sessions lasted until volitional exhaustion or up to 60 min. Workload was set as 10% below the workload at VT1 (moderate), halfway between the workloads at VT1 and VT2 (heavy), and halfway between the workload at VT2 and Wpeak (severe). All sessions began with a 3‐min warm‐up at half of the power prescribed for the session and were performed at 60–90 rpm (Gronwald et al. 2018).

The same measuring instruments used during the incremental tests were employed. VO2 and HR were continuously recorded, while [La−] was sampled at the beginning, every 10 min, and at exhaustion, with an additional [La−] measurement after the first 5 min during severe intensity sessions due to expected shorter durations. As session durations varied, the total time of each session was recorded. To enable comparisons across sessions of different durations, a relative ‘isotime’ approach was employed, as it is the only approach that avoids data loss and it is well‐suited for examining durability‐related effects, as it contextualises physiological responses in relation to the sustained duration (Nicolò et al. 2019). Each session was divided into quartiles (Q1–Q4), with the end of the warm‐up defined as Q0. A 1‐min window preceding each isotime point was used to average VO2, HR, and to interpolate [La−] values using a second‐order polynomial fit based on session samples. The DFA‐a1 values calculated from the 2‐min windows with 5 s grid size were also averaged over the corresponding 1‐min period. Each quartile was then classified into an intensity domain according to VT‐, LT‐, and DFA‐a1‐based thresholds, using both absolute values and corresponding HR equivalents. For example, a given time point was classified as belonging to the heavy domain if its corresponding value fell between VT1/LT1/HRVT1 and VT2/LT2/HRVT2, or between DFA‐a1 values of 0.75 and 0.5.

Training Zones Distributions

Six methods were applied to quantify time spent in training zones: VO2‐ and HR‐based ventilatory thresholds, [La−]‐ and HR‐based lactate thresholds, and DFA‐a1‐ and HR‐based HRV thresholds. At each quartile, participants were categorised accordingly, enabling comparison of zone allocation across methods.

Statistical Analysis

Means and standard deviations (SD) were calculated. A two‐factor repeated‐measures ANOVA (Intensity x Time) compared physiological variables (DFA‐a1, %HR, and %VO2, representing percentages of maximal HR and VO2max, respectively, and [La−]) across time points (Q0–Q4). Two separate one‐factor ANOVAs were conducted: one for normalised data (Z‐scores) to compare variables at specific time points, and another to evaluate time spent in the target zone of each session (zone 1, 2, and 3 in the moderate, heavy, and severe sessions, respectively). Additionally, ω 2 was used as a measure of effect size. Bonferroni post hoc analyses were conducted when significance was found. Alongside p values, the mean difference with 95% confidence interval (CI) and the standardised effect size (Cohen's d) were reported and interpreted as small (d = 0.2), medium (d = 0.5), and large (d = 0.8) (Cohen 2013). The non‐parametric Friedman test assessed differences in zone distribution across threshold methods. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. All analyses were performed using JASP v0.19.3 (JASP Team, 2019; jasp‐stats.org).

Results

Data Inclusion

Of the 16 participants, one could not perform the severe intensity session, and data from another were excluded from %VO2 analysis in the moderate session due to corruption.

All participants completed the full 60 min at moderate intensity sessions, while average durations were shorter in the heavy (54.6 ± 9.0 min) and severe (13.0 ± 7.2 min) intensity sessions due to the higher physiological demands.

Discussion

This study evaluated DFA‐a1 as a biomarker of internal load across moderate, heavy, and severe intensity domains during constant‐load cycling. As in previous research, HR, VO2, and [La−] exhibited domain‐specific responses, providing a robust reference for comparison. A key finding was that, using normalised data, DFA‐a1 displayed temporal patterns comparable to those of traditional physiological markers across all intensity domains, reinforcing its potential for capturing internal load dynamics and adding a different theoretical and methodological perspective. This alignment was particularly evident in the moderate domain, where DFA‐a1 appeared more stable and consistent. Another objective was to explore its suitability for guiding training intensity during prolonged constant‐load exercise sessions, finding domain‐dependent variability in its effectiveness, with higher agreement in the moderate domain and growing divergence in the heavy and severe domains.

DFA‐a1 in Moderate Intensity Exercise

During the moderate intensity session, [La−] levels remained stable and comparably low, reflecting a steady state typically associated with low intensity exercise (Beneke et al. 2011). VO2 and HR followed expected patterns, with an initial increase, followed by a steady state (Zuccarelli et al. 2018; Xu and Rhodes 1999). However, a significant increase in HR was observed in Q3 compared to Q2, consistent with cardiovascular drift, which has been observed even at low intensities during prolonged constant‐load exercise (Zuccarelli et al. 2018; Matomäki et al. 2023). This phenomenon is primarily attributed to factors such as increased core temperature and dehydration, which reduce plasma volume and venous return, leading to a decline in stroke volume. In addition, increased sympathetic nervous system activation may contribute to HR elevation and reduced diastolic filling time, which has also been proposed as a potential mechanism contributing to the decline in stroke volume during prolonged exercise (Souissi et al. 2021). Despite this HR drift, DFA‐a1 values remained stable above 1.0 throughout the session. This finding is consistent with the relatively high DFA‐a1 values observed at VT1 in our participants (1.31 ± 0.24), indicating that exercise prescribed at 90% of VT1 would be expected to remain well above the commonly proposed fixed DFA‐a1 cut‐off of 0.75. Therefore, in the present cohort, the fixed value of 0.75 did not appear to coincide with the VT1/LT1 boundary, but rather was located at a higher relative intensity. Beyond this threshold‐location effect, the maintenance of DFA‐a1 values above 1.0 throughout the session may also be physiologically meaningful, as values in this range reflect well‐correlated HR dynamics typically observed during low‐intensity exercise. Thus, the absence of a progressive DFA‐a1 decline although cardiovascular drift is compatible with preserved autonomic regulation under well‐tolerated exercise conditions. This aligns with previous literature on low intensity exercise (Casties et al. 2006; Gronwald et al. 2019; Hautala et al. 2003) and suggests that, while HR may progressively rise over time, DFA‐a1 may provide complementary information on systemic load and regulatory stability during prolonged low intensity exercise.

Further, our results support those of Nuuttila et al. (2024), who conducted a 90‐min durability test at low intensity (90% of LT1) on a treadmill, a comparable intensity to the moderate intensity session in our study (90% of VT1) although some differences may arise due to the different methods used to determine exercise intensity. They reported significant increases in HR and VO2 over time, while DFA‐a1 and [La−] levels remained unchanged throughout the session. However, a notable difference between studies lies in the absolute DFA‐a1 values: while their participants showed values ranging from approximately 0.80 to 0.60, DFA‐a1 remained consistently above 1.0 in our sample. This discrepancy could be attributed to differences in exercise modality, as running and cycling induce comparable reductions in neuromuscular function, although the mechanisms underlying fatigue differ between modalities, with greater central involvement during running and greater peripheral impairment during cycling (Brownstein et al. 2022). Additionally, variations in participant characteristics, such as training status or endurance capacity, may have influenced the results. Specifically, Nuuttila et al. (2024) studied recreational runners engaging in regular endurance training (> 250 h/year), whereas our participants were classified as untrained according to established criteria (McKay et al. 2021). This distinction is also reflected in their aerobic capacity, as they reported higher VO2max values despite an older cohort, consistent with their training status. Sheoran et al. (2024) demonstrated the influence of cardiorespiratory fitness in the DFA‐a1‐derived HRVTs—overestimating VTs in untrained individuals and underestimating it in trained ones—reflecting distinct autonomic responses. Consequently, prescribing constant work at 90% of first threshold is likely to show higher DFA‐a1 values in untrained participants, and lower ones in trained individuals. This could partly explain the higher DFA‐a1 values observed in our study.

DFA‐a1 in Heavy Intensity Exercise

The behaviour of traditional physiological markers during the heavy intensity session followed expected patterns associated with sustained submaximal exertion: [La−] levels gradually increased before stabilising at a steady state, VO2 showed an initial rise attributable to the slow component before reaching a plateau, and HR progressively increased throughout the session, consistent with cardiovascular drift, without achieving a steady state (Zuccarelli et al. 2018; Beneke et al. 2011; Xu and Rhodes 1999). Notably, DFA‐a1 values did not enter the anticipated fixed cut‐off range of 0.75 to 0.5 until Q2. This suggests that, when using previously proposed absolute thresholds, DFA‐a1 responses during heavy‐intensity exercise may not immediately align with the prescribed workload, although individualised threshold approaches could yield a different interpretation and potentially indicate earlier alignment (Rogers et al. 2024). Moreover, considerable interindividual variability was observed in this intensity domain, with a relatively low proportion of participants falling within the expected zone, which further suggests limited agreement when applying fixed DFA‐a1 cut‐offs for exercise prescription and monitoring, potentially reflecting the higher DFA‐a1 values observed at VT1 in the present cohort (1.31 ± 0.24).

These findings are consistent with previous studies examining DFA‐a1 behaviour during prolonged heavy‐intensity exercise. For instance, van Rassel et al. (2024) observed DFA‐a1 values around 0.78 at 15 min and 0.67 at 30 min during constant‐load running at maximal lactate steady state (MLSS), a workload commonly associated with VT2. Their trends in HR, VO2 and [La−] were similar to ours. Comparable dynamics were described by Rogers, Fleitas‐Paniagua, et al. (2025) during constant‐load cycling at 95% of the maximal metabolic steady state (MMSS), a workload that is substantially lower than the VT2‐derived power (Iannetta et al. 2020). They observed a progressive HR drift accompanied by a steady decline in DFA‐a1, while VO2 and [La−] remained stable after an initial increase. Additionally, Gronwald et al. (2025) described relatively stable DFA‐a1 values between 0.54 and 0.51 across 5‐min intervals at marathon race pace, an intensity generally located near the upper limit of the heavy domain. Although their protocol included recovery periods between intervals, the constant‐load structure of each bout provides relevant insights into DFA‐a1 behaviour. This DFA‐a1 pattern mirrored the stability of [La−], VO2, and subjective markers, while HR increased significantly until the final interval, likely reflecting cardiovascular drift or the progressive accumulation of internal load over time, as recently discussed by Seiler (2024).

Considering both the findings from previous studies and the present results, DFA‐a1 appears to exhibit a progressive decline during prolonged exercise in the heavy domain, with a more pronounced reduction during the initial phases followed by a slower rate of decrease as values approach the range of approximately 0.75–0.5. This temporal pattern broadly parallels the behaviour of key metabolic and cardiovascular markers, although notable interindividual variability is observed.

Nevertheless, some methodological differences should be acknowledged when interpreting between‐study comparisons, including variations in DFA‐a1 preprocessing and calculation procedures, exercise duration, and intensity prescription methods (e.g., midpoint VT1–VT2, MLSS, or a percentage of estimated MMSS). These factors may partly explain minor discrepancies in absolute DFA‐a1 values across studies. Collectively, the available evidence suggests that DFA‐a1 is sensitive to the physiological demands of prolonged heavy‐intensity exercise, although responses may be influenced by methodological and exercise‐specific factors.

DFA‐a1 in Severe Intensity Exercise

During the severe intensity session, the average exercise duration (13 ± 7.2 min) highlighted the substantial physiological strain and limited sustainability of work in this domain. In this line, physiological responses of the traditional markers reflected the high metabolic and autonomic demands, aligning with previously reported behaviour in severe intensity efforts: [La−], VO2, and HR rose progressively throughout the session without reaching stability (Zuccarelli et al. 2018; Beneke et al. 2011; Xu and Rhodes 1999). Similarly to the response observed during the heavy intensity session, DFA‐a1 values, expected to drop below 0.5 in this domain, only reached those values from Q2 onwards, suggesting a delayed autonomic response.

These findings are comparable to those reported by van Rassel et al. (2024), considering the between‐study differences discussed above, during constant‐load running at 5% above MLSS, where DFA‐a1 values progressively declined below 0.5, coinciding with continuous increases in HR, VO2, and [La−]. To the best of our knowledge, the present study is among the few that have examined DFA‐a1 behaviour under constant‐load conditions in the severe intensity domain. Nonetheless, the observed response is consistent with expectations based on data from incremental exercise protocols to exhaustion, where DFA‐a1 progressively decreases, reflecting a loss of fractal correlation with increasing intensity (Gronwald et al. 2019).

DFA‐a1 as a Tool for Intensity Monitoring and Prescription

When normalising the data using Z‐scores, we observed a homogeneous relative evolution across all physiological markers. Despite differences in absolute values, HR, VO2, [La−], and DFA‐a1 exhibited comparable standardised trends over time. This suggests that prolonged exercise exerts a consistent influence on systemic responses, reinforcing the importance of considering accumulated exercise duration when interpreting intensity‐related physiological changes (Mazzolari and Hecksteden 2025). The parallel evolution of DFA‐a1 with traditional markers may suggest its potential for monitoring internal load during prolonged efforts. Beyond this, DFA‐a1 offers a complementary physiological perspective, capturing dynamic changes in autonomic regulation that reflect the interaction of multiple subsystems involved in maintaining homeostasis. As discussed by Hoos and Gronwald (2025), DFA‐a1 provides a window into the complex, emergent behaviour of a self‐organising system under load, going beyond metabolic stress alone to encompass broader regulatory processes. This insight distinguishes DFA‐a1 from the conventional markers, reinforcing its added value in endurance training contexts. Furthermore, a practical advantage of DFA‐a1 as a monitoring tool is that it may enable both real‐time and retrospective analysis of internal load using widely accessible, low‐cost devices, which could enhance its applicability in endurance training environments (Rogers, Murias, and Fleitas‐Paniagua 2025), although further validation is still required in this context.

Regarding the use of DFA‐a1 for monitoring exercise intensity, our findings indicate important domain‐specific considerations. In the moderate intensity session, there were no significant differences between the diverse methods used to quantify the time spent in each intensity zone. Notably, methods employing DFA‐a1 values or HR associated with DFA‐a1 thresholds showed the highest alignment with the prescribed intensity, allocating over 94% of session time within the target zone. Given that endurance athletes spend over 75% of their training time in the moderate domain (low intensity or recovery sessions) (Rosenblat et al. 2025), DFA‐a1 presents a reliable and valid tool for monitoring internal load and guiding exercise intensity in this context. As an index of global organismic demand, DFA‐a1 would allow early detection of unintended drifts in autonomic stress (Nuuttila et al. 2024), facilitating timely adjustments to maintain the intended training stimulus.

However, this agreement decreased substantially in the heavy and severe intensity domains. In the heavy session, designed to remain within Zone 2, methods based on DFA‐a1 or HR associated with its thresholds resulted in only 22.6% and 26.7% of time spent in the target zone. In contrast, higher percentages were observed when using VO2 or [La−] methods, especially with VO2 values expressed in ml·kg−1 min−1, where significant differences emerged compared to DFA‐a1 and lactate‐based methods. A similar pattern was observed in the severe domain, where DFA‐a1 methods resulted in lower time spent in the goal zone compared to VO2 in its original variable, although differences were not significant relative to other methods.

These findings are in line with those reported by Gronwald et al. (2024), who concluded that DFA‐a1 may not effectively delineate heavy and severe intensity domains due to high interindividual variability. Nevertheless, DFA‐a1 remains a valuable dimensionless index for monitoring internal load, particularly in the moderate domain, where its temporal stability and sensitivity to autonomic changes are advantageous. Importantly, while DFA‐a1 shows potential for tracking exercise‐induced fatigue and durability, its application for direct exercise prescription at higher intensity domains may lead to overestimation of required intensities, particularly in the early phases of a session.

Further insight can be gained from individual fluctuations across intensity domains. During the moderate intensity session, nearly all participants remained within the target zone when DFA‐a1‐based methods were employed. In contrast, using HR values associated with ventilatory thresholds, currently the most widely used method in practice, led to a progressive upward drift, such that half the participants were classified at a higher than intended zone by the end of the session due to HR drift. This phenomenon, previously described in relation to the HR drift and VO2 slow component, reflects the progressive uncoupling between HR and metabolic demand during prolonged exercise (Zuccarelli et al. 2018; Maunder et al. 2021; Smyth et al. 2022). Recent findings suggest that despite this upward HR drift, the actual metabolic load may remain stable or even fall short of the desired zone‐specific stimulus, potentially compromising training effectiveness (Teso et al. 2025). In this context, DFA‐a1 appears to offer a more stable monitoring tool, capturing internal load reliably without such upward shifts. However, this consistency diminished in the heavy and severe domains. The discrepancies mainly emerged in the early quartiles, likely reflecting a delayed response of DFA‐a1. Notably, from Q2 onwards in the severe domain, absolute DFA‐a1 values aligned with the target zone, with more than 80% of the session spent in the intended domain. This finding supports its potential utility for monitoring at high intensities when the initial delay is considered. In the heavy domain, although differences beyond Q2 were also absent, time spent in zone 2 was consistently lower with DFA‐a1 methods compared to VO2 method, which yielded the greatest time in‐zone. This suggests that while DFA‐a1 may remain informative, its application in this domain is less precise and would benefit from integration with complementary physiological markers of internal load. From a NPE perspective, a possible explanation for this behaviour is that autonomic regulation may transiently maintain more correlated HR dynamics during the initial phases of heavy‐intensity exercise before progressively losing this correlated behaviour as organismic demand accumulates over time, particularly in less‐trained individuals (Sheoran et al. 2024).

Experimental Considerations

Several methodological and contextual factors should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. Although non‐linear HRV indices such as DFA‐a1 are generally less susceptible to artefacts than linear measures (Gronwald et al. 2019), HRV data collection during dynamic exercise remains sensitive to movement and signal noise. Ensuring high‐quality signal acquisition is therefore essential, particularly when monitoring physiological status in real‐world settings.

The choice of cycling as the exercise modality may also influence the generalisability of our findings. Cycling predominantly affects muscle contractile function, whereas endurance modalities like running tend to impose greater demands on the nervous system function (Brownstein et al. 2022). These modality‐specific demands may influence durability and should be accounted for when comparing across studies.

Regarding the protocol, our 60‐min target duration reflects the characteristics of typical endurance training sessions more accurately than shorter protocols. This is particularly relevant because prolonged exercise duration itself may meaningfully alter DFA‐a1 behaviour, even when external intensity is relatively low, likely reflecting accumulated systemic fatigue and autonomic perturbation (Rogers, Mourot, Doucende, and Gronwald 2021). All participants completed the full duration in the moderate‐intensity session, while the heavy and severe sessions resulted in shorter and variable durations due to individual tolerance levels. This approach enhances the ecological validity of our observations and provides more representative insights into internal load dynamics during prolonged exercise.

In addition, the determination of LT1 and LT2 should be interpreted with caution, as multiple lactate‐threshold concepts exist and no universal consensus has been established regarding the optimal method. In the present study, fixed values of 2 and 4 mmol·l−1 were selected due to their widespread historical use and practical applicability in both research and applied training settings (Jamnick et al. 2020; Faude et al. 2009; Pallarés et al. 2016). However, alternative individualised or curve‐derived approaches may yield different threshold positions and consequently different intensity‐domain classifications.

Moreover, while efforts have been made in recent research to align DFA‐a1 thresholds with traditional intensity demarcations, these boundaries do not necessarily coincide, and their agreement may be influenced by interindividual variability and methodological approaches. From an NPE perspective, however, DFA‐a1 may provide complementary information regarding systemic regulation and organismic demand beyond its agreement with traditional metabolic threshold concepts (Hoos and Gronwald 2025). In our study, the normalised responses of DFA‐a1, HR, [La−], and VO2 behaved similarly across intensity domains; however, a more comprehensive understanding of physiological strain could be obtained by including other measures such as neuromuscular function or muscle oxygen saturation. These additional parameters could help elucidate the broader systemic responses to exercise, particularly concerning the interaction of intensity and duration.

Finally, although previous studies have shown good agreement between DFA‐a1‐based thresholds and traditional markers of exercise intensity, substantial interindividual variability has been observed in the heavy‐to‐severe intensity range when monitoring intensity during constant‐load sessions. From one perspective, this suggests that DFA‐a1 may have limitations for monitoring exercise intensity in these domains, especially in individuals with atypical autonomic responses. Alternatively, DFA‐a1 may reflect systemic load resulting from the interaction between exercise intensity and duration in a manner distinct from conventional metabolic markers. From the perspective of CAS, it may therefore provide a more integrative view of internal load (Hoos and Gronwald 2025). Future research should therefore explore the robustness of DFA‐a1 across different exercise modalities and populations, as well as its applicability in field‐based settings where non‐invasive, practical monitoring tools are particularly valuable.

Conclusion

DFA‐a1 has demonstrated several practical advantages as an internal load monitoring tool, such as its non‐invasive nature, continuous assessment capability, and sensitivity to autonomic regulation, as well as its complementary role as a qualitative marker of systemic regulation within a CAS. These characteristics support its use as a complementary, or in certain contexts, alternative metric to traditional markers, particularly in field‐based or resource‐limited settings where laboratory assessments are not feasible. Based on the present findings, DFA‐a1 appears especially suitable for monitoring physiological responses during moderate intensity exercise, offering a dimensionless and integrative measure of systemic stress. However, its interpretation should account for contextual factors such as exercise modality and individual characteristics, which can influence its responsiveness.

It is important to note that fixed DFA‐a1 thresholds may not reliably reflect equivalent physiological states across all intensity domains, a limitation that is also shared by other fixed internal‐load markers such as HR. Using fixed values to guide exercise intensity could result in higher‐than‐expected internal load during the early phases of heavy to severe intensity constant‐load sessions. Nevertheless, the continued decline of DFA‐a1 even at higher intensities highlights its sensitivity to the interaction between exercise intensity and accumulated duration‐related load.

Given this temporal evolution, DFA‐a1 may also serve as a promising biomarker of durability, offering insights into an individual's capacity to tolerate prolonged stress. Incorporating DFA‐a1 into training monitoring could therefore enhance individualisation and support more effective endurance programming.
