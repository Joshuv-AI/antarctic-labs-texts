Clinical and microbiological profile of invasive Salmonella disease in Mexico: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Edwin Jeffry Palma-Díaz, Juan Carlos Domínguez-Hermosillo, Javier Tondopó-Guerrero, Alberto Chaparro-Sánchez, José Antonio Mata-Marín, Eva Aurora Hernández-Sánchez, Pamela Soto-Santillán, Jesús Enrique Gaytán-Martínez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSalmonella gastrointestinal infections are common in low- and middle-income countries; however, there is limited data on the clinical presentation and microbiological characteristics of invasive Salmonella disease in Latin America.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted, including all patients with Salmonella spp. isolated from usually sterile sites at our center from May 2016 to June 2025. Data on demographics, comorbidities, immunological status, symptoms, treatment, and outcomes were collected from medical records. The isolated serogroups and their antimicrobial resistance patterns were analyzed using historical data from the Bacteriology Laboratory electronic database. Descriptive statistical methods were used for data analysis.ResultsA total of 77 cases of invasive Salmonella infection were identified. Most patients were male (76.6%), with a median age of 33 years. HIV infection was present in 80.5% of cases, and 88.7% of these individuals had advanced disease. Fever was the most frequent symptom (85.7%), and 10.4% required admission to the intensive care unit. Overall mortality was 13%, and all fatal cases occurred among people living with HIV (PWH). The most common treatment was third-generation cephalosporins (58.4%). Resistance to aminoglycosides was documented in all isolates; 52.3% were resistant to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and 6% to ceftriaxone. Ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility reached 64.8%. Multidrug resistance was observed in 12.6% of isolates.ConclusionsInvasive Salmonella disease primarily affected immunocompromised individuals, particularly PWH, with fever and bacteremia being the predominant clinical manifestations. Although multidrug resistance was relatively low, the high prevalence of ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility highlights the need to revise regional empirical treatment strategies.Keywords: Salmonella invasive disease, HIV/AIDS, Antimicrobial resistance, Microbiology

Background

Salmonella is a genus of Gram-negative bacteria responsible for a wide range of clinical syndromes [1]. Although the disease caused by these bacteria usually presents as enteric fever or gastroenteritis, in certain conditions Salmonella species can spread beyond the gastrointestinal tract to normally sterile sites resulting in Invasive Salmonella Disease (ISD) [2].

Up to 95% of ISD cases occur in immunocompromised individuals, including those with malnutrition, advanced age, hemoglobinopathies, or people living with HIV (PWH). In the latter group, recurrent Salmonella bacteremia is recognized as an AIDS-defining condition [3–5].

ISD usually manifests as a febrile illness without an identifiable source, with bacteremia being the most frequent clinical presentation, though focal infections may occur, even in the absence of bloodstream involvement [6, 7].

The clinical and microbiological characteristics of ISD have been primarily described in studies from sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia, regions with a high disease burden. These studies have identified Salmonella Typhimurium and Enteritidis as the serovars most frequently associated with invasive disease [4, 8].

Without appropriate antimicrobial therapy, ISD mortality can reach 59%. In recent years, resistance to first-line antibiotics among Salmonella isolates has increased, with multidrug-resistant strains accounting for 50–75% of cases in some reports [9, 10].

In Mexico, despite the circulation of diverse Salmonella serovars in the food chain and the known burden of salmonellosis, no data exist on the clinical and microbiological profile of ISD [11]. The Infectious Diseases Hospital at Centro Médico Nacional La Raza serves a heterogeneous patient population, including a high proportion of immunocompromised individuals. This study aims to characterize the clinical presentation, microbiological features, and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of ISD in this setting.

Methods

A cross-sectional study was conducted at the Infectious Diseases Hospital, Centro Médico Nacional La Raza, Mexico City, Mexico, from May 2016 to June 2025. The Infectious Diseases Hospital is a national referral center and the only hospital in Mexico exclusively dedicated to the management of infectious diseases. It provides care for individuals with a broad range of infections, many of whom have underlying immunodeficiencies, including PWH (who represent approximately 20% of hospitalized patients annually), as well as patients with cancer and post-transplant recipients, among others.

Study population and data collection

All adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) with confirmed Salmonella spp. isolated from normally sterile sites (e.g., blood, cerebrospinal fluid, pleural fluid, or bone marrow) during the study period were included. Exclusion criteria included patients with incomplete medical records (missing > 50% of key clinical data), non-sterile site isolates (e.g., stool or urine without evidence of systemic invasion), or isolates not confirmed as Salmonella spp. by laboratory methods.

Clinical and demographic data were extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized case report form. Variables collected included:

Sociodemographic characteristics: age, sex, and place of residence.

Salmonella isolates were identified using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) or the VITEK 2 automated system (bioMérieux, France). Serogrouping was performed in selected cases based on the availability of antisera at the time of Salmonella spp. identification, following the Kauffmann-White scheme. Because full serotyping was not performed, individual isolates within each serogroup could not be assigned to specific serovars. Consequently, our analyses include both Typhi and non-Typhi isolates, as they could not be reliably distinguished from one another. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method or VITEK 2, following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. The panel of antibiotics selected for susceptibility testing was determined by local laboratory protocols at the time of microbiological isolation. Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined as resistance to three or more antibiotic classes.

Statistical analysis

A formal sample size calculation was not performed due to the descriptive, cross-sectional nature of the study, which included all eligible patients within the specified period. However, based on historical data from the Bacteriology Laboratory, approximately 50–100 cases of ISD were anticipated, providing sufficient power to describe clinical and microbiological characteristics with precision.

Data were analyzed using descriptive statistical methods. Categorical variables (e.g., comorbidities, serogroups, resistance patterns) were reported as frequencies and percentages, with missing data excluded from percentage calculations. Quantitative variables (e.g., age, duration of symptoms, hospitalization days) were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) due to expected non-normal distribution, assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test.

Comparative analyses were conducted to assess differences in variables between patients stratified by HIV status. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables, and the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for quantitative variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Data management

Data were entered into a secure, password-protected database with access restricted to study investigators. Patient confidentiality was maintained by de-identifying data during extraction and analysis. No direct patient contact or additional interventions were required, as all data were retrospective. Quality control measures included double-entry verification for 10% of records and cross-checking microbiological data against original laboratory reports to ensure accuracy.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 77 patients with ISD were included over the nine-year study period (May 2016–June 2025). Patient selection workflow is summarized in Fig. 1. The median age was 33 years (IQR 28.5–43.5), and 59 patients (76.6%) were male. The most prevalent comorbidity was HIV infection (62 patients, 80.5%), followed by type 2 diabetes (5 patients, 6.5%). Fever was the most common symptom, reported in 66 patients (85.7%), and the median hospital stay was 15 days (IQR 11–26). Eight patients (10.4%) required intensive care unit (ICU) admission. Treatment and clinical outcomes

The most commonly prescribed antibiotics were third-generation cephalosporins (45 patients, 58.4%) and fluoroquinolones (20 patients, 26.0%), with a median treatment duration of 10 days (IQR 7–14). The recurrence rate was 7.8%, and secondary prophylaxis was administered to 5 patients (6.5%). The ISD-associated mortality rate was 13.0%, exclusively among PWH.

Of the 62 PWH, 55 (88.7%) had advanced HIV disease (CD4 + count < 200 cells/mm³). Sixteen (25.8%) were receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART), and 8 (14.2%) had achieved virologic suppression. Opportunistic infections were diagnosed in 27 (43.5%) PWH during the hospitalization for ISD, with disseminated histoplasmosis and Mycobacterium avium complex infection being the most common.

Microbiological characteristics

A total of 99 Salmonella isolates were identified from 77 patients, with 58 (58.6%) recovered from blood, 25 (25.3%) from bone marrow aspirates, 6 (6.1%) from urine, 3 (3.0%) from pleural fluid, 3 (3.0%) from bronchoalveolar lavage, and single isolates from pleural tissue, synovial fluid, a hepatic abscess, and a lymph node. Twenty patients (26.0%) had Salmonella isolated from two or more anatomical sites.

Serogrouping was performed on isolates from 12 patients, identifying 6 (7.8%) as serogroup D, 4 (5.2%) as serogroup B, and one each as serogroups A and C. As full serotyping was not performed, specific serovar designation for individual isolates within each serogroup could not be established. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted on all isolates. Resistance to aminoglycosides was universal (100%), while no resistance to carbapenems was observed. Resistance to ceftriaxone was 6.0%, and resistance to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole was 52.3%. Non-susceptibility to ciprofloxacin (MIC > 0.06 µg/mL) was observed in 64.8% of isolates. MDR was identified in 12.6% of isolates. Antimicrobial resistance patterns are detailed in Table 3.

Discussion

In Mexico, non-typhoidal Salmonella gastroenteritis and Salmonella food chain contamination have been extensively studied [11, 12]; however, this is the first study to systematically characterize the clinical and microbiological features of ISD in the country. The demographic profile of this cohort revealed a predominance of young adult males, with a median age of 33 years, consistent with previously reported distributions in ISD [4]. Fever represented the most frequent clinical manifestation, documented in 85.7% of cases, which corroborates international series [8]. Notably, gastrointestinal symptoms were absent in over half of the patients, a finding strongly associated with advanced immunosuppression [13]. This clinical pattern aligns with the high prevalence of HIV infection observed in the cohort (80.5%), reinforcing the hypothesis that absence of gastroenteritis may constitute a clinical marker of profound immune dysfunction in ISD.

Despite the invasive nature of the infection, procalcitonin levels remained low, with a median of 0.5 ng/mL. This contrasts with other Gram-negative invasive infections, where procalcitonin is considered a sensitive biomarker. Previous studies have shown that in ISD, procalcitonin may remain normal or only mildly elevated [14]. This attenuated response has been attributed to the intracellular lifestyle of Salmonella spp., which limits activation of innate immunity and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, thereby blunting systemic inflammatory responses and the elevation of acute-phase reactants [15]. Bloodstream infection was the most frequent presentation (58.6%), yet bone marrow involvement was also notable (25.3%), in line with evidence that Salmonella spp. may persist in this compartment despite antimicrobial therapy [16].

Other sterile site infections were also identified, including Salmonella septic arthritis in a patient with systemic lupus erythematosus on chronic corticosteroids, consistent with reports linking immunosuppression to this manifestation [17, 18]. In addition, three patients developed pleural involvement without prior surgical interventions, diverging from published cases that primarily associate Salmonella empyema with postsurgical states [19]. Serogrouping was performed in a minority of cases; however, serogroup D predominated, a finding consistent with global reports identifying S. Enteritidis as one of the principal serovars implicated in ISD [2]. Because complete serotyping was not conducted, we cannot exclude the possibility that some serogroup D isolates corresponded to other clinically relevant serovars, including S. Typhi.

Therapeutic approaches in this cohort reflected the absence of specific international guidelines for ISD, as most recommendations focus on diarrheal or bacteremic disease in HIV-infected populations [20]. Third-generation cephalosporins were most commonly used (58.4%), followed by fluoroquinolones (26%). Follow-up cultures were rarely performed (6.5%), consistent with robust evidence showing that routine control cultures provide no added clinical benefit in Gram-negative bacteremia [21].

Recurrence was uncommon, occurring in only 7.8% of cases, substantially lower than the rates of up to 43% reported in the pre-antiretroviral era [22]. Nevertheless, the retrospective, unicentric design may have underestimated recurrence due to possible loss of follow-up. The low rate of antiretroviral use (25.8%) in the PWH cohort indicates that many patients with ISD either presented with a late HIV diagnosis or had not been retained in care. These factors may have contributed to the profound immunosuppression observed. Secondary prophylaxis was administered in only 8.1% of HIV-positive patients, despite 88.7% presenting CD4 + counts < 200 cells/mm³. This low utilization likely reflects concerns regarding antimicrobial resistance, the perception of limited recurrence risk, and the prioritization of immune reconstitution through antiretroviral therapy over long-term suppressive regimens, despite NIH/IDSA guidance recommending such strategies in selected patients [20].

Overall hospital mortality was 24.7%, with ISD-attributable mortality of 13%. All fatal cases occurred among HIV-infected individuals, with mortality in this subgroup reaching 16.1%. These figures compare favorably to international reports, where overall ISD mortality is approximately 20% and may rise to 59% in HIV-positive populations [3, 23]. The lower mortality observed in our cohort is likely attributable to the study setting, a national referral center with standardized protocols, timely access to diagnostics, and specialized expertise in the management of opportunistic infections. The high prevalence of concurrent opportunistic diseases (43.5%) reflects the advanced stage of HIV infection in this population and underscores their heightened clinical vulnerability, in which the incidence of ISD is estimated to be 20–100 times higher than in the general population [11].

Antimicrobial resistance patterns revealed MDR Salmonella spp. in 12.6% of isolates, a prevalence substantially lower than that reported in sub-Saharan Africa (50–75%) but higher than rates documented in the United States (2%) [24, 25]. MDR strains have been associated with adverse clinical outcomes and higher risk of invasive disease [10]. No carbapenem resistance was observed. In contrast, ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility was strikingly high (64.8%), resembling rates in Nepal (66%) and significantly exceeding the 7% reported in the United States [10, 25]. Considering the World Health Organization’s designation of fluoroquinolone-resistant S. Typhi as a high-priority pathogen [26], fluoroquinolones can no longer be regarded as reliable empirical therapy in this setting. Resistance to third-generation cephalosporins was limited (6%), supporting their continued role as first-line agents. Universal resistance to aminoglycosides was confirmed, in line with CLSI and EUCAST recommendations that discourage their use due to poor intracellular penetration and frequent therapeutic failure [27–29].

Strengths of this study include the systematic characterization of clinical, microbiological, and therapeutic features of ISD in a Latin American setting where data remain scarce. The use of confirmatory microbiological methods and detailed clinical records strengthens the internal validity of the findings. Although this study was conducted in a center with a high proportion of PWH, we consider that the findings retain broader relevance for Mexico and the wider Latin American region. Several considerations support this. First, invasive Salmonella infections disproportionately affect immunocompromised populations, including PWH; therefore, delineating circulating serogroups and resistance patterns in this high-risk group provides important insights for clinical decision-making and public health surveillance. Second, the antimicrobial resistance profiles observed in our cohort are consistent with regional reports, suggesting that similar patterns may be present beyond our institution [30]. Third, Mexico and many Latin American countries share comparable epidemiological contexts, including overlapping risk factors, similar antimicrobial-use pressures, and common socioeconomic determinants of infection. These similarities support the broader applicability of our findings. However, retrospective design, single-center scope, and incomplete serotyping limit generalizability. Furthermore, the absence of molecular resistance testing precludes precise characterization of resistance mechanisms. Future research should focus on multicenter studies, including non-HIV-specialized settings, that integrate molecular epidemiology, explore host–pathogen interactions, and assess long-term outcomes, thereby generating evidence to inform regional guidelines for diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of ISD.

Conclusions

This study constitutes the first systematic characterization of ISD in Mexico, confirming that the disease primarily affects immunocompromised individuals, particularly those PWH. Fever emerged as the dominant clinical feature, while bacteremia was the most frequent presentation, although bone marrow and other sterile site involvement were also common. Antimicrobial resistance analysis demonstrated low prevalence of MDR isolates but alarmingly high ciprofloxacin non-susceptibility, underscoring the need to revise local empirical therapy strategies. Relatively low recurrence and mortality rates likely reflect the benefits of care in a referral setting. Multicenter prospective studies are warranted to delineate the clinical spectrum, resistance dynamics, and prognostic determinants of ISD in Mexico and Latin America.
