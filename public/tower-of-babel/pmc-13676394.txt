ALDH Inhibition‐Primed Photocatalysis Disrupts Cancer Stemness Plasticity
Authors: Yujin Kim, Shankun Yao, Ji Hyeon Kim, Rongrong Huang, Hyeonji Rha, Shumeng Li, Kippeum Lee, Yunjie Xu, Xiaogang Liu, Yuncong Chen, Jong Seung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLight‐driven photoredox catalysis can shift intracellular NADH/NAD+ balance by oxidizing NADH, but intracellular NADH pools are dynamically replenished through enzyme‐coupled redox metabolism. We show that ALDH‐mediated restoration of NADH/NAD+ balance can buffer photoredox‐induced NADH depletion, providing a design rationale for coupling enzyme inhibition with photoredox catalysis. To counteract this redox‐buffering process, we introduce an enzymatic–photocatalytic strategy, termed E–PC, that integrates ALDH inhibition with NIR‐driven photocatalytic NADH oxidation within a single molecular construct. A small‐molecule conjugate, DE‐Et, was constructed by covalently linking DEAB, which can inhibit ALDH, to a near‐infrared (NIR)–active Nile Blue Sulfide–derived photocatalyst (NBS) through a ROS‐cleavable thioacetal linker. Upon NIR irradiation, DE‐Et releases DEAB while catalytically oxidizing intracellular NADH through single‐electron transfer, thereby attenuating ALDH‐mediated redox restoration and sustaining NADH/NAD+ imbalance. This dual action compromises mitochondrial ATP production and activates caspase‐3/GSDME‐dependent pyroptosis. In stemness‐enriched tumor models, DE‐Et suppresses ALDH activity, clonogenic growth, spheroid integrity, and tumor growth in vivo. Local photoactivation further promotes systemic antitumor immune responses, consistent with immunogenic pyroptotic cell death. E–PC thus establishes a chemical strategy for light‐activated co‐delivery of photoredox catalysis and inhibition of a tumor‐overexpressed enzyme, converting an adaptive enzymatic buffer into a therapeutic vulnerability.Keywords: photoimmunotherapy, photoredox catalysis, pyroptosis, redox homeostasis

Introduction

Artificial photoredox catalysis (PC) provides a light‐controlled approach for perturbing biological redox processes, particularly through the single‐electron oxidation of intracellular NADH (Scheme 1A) [1, 2, 3]. In cancer cells, this reaction can disturb NADH/NAD+ homeostasis, impair mitochondrial electron transport, and impose bioenergetic stress. However, NADH is not a static substrate in living systems; it is continuously regenerated through enzyme‐coupled redox pathways [4]. Because of this dynamic turnover, the persistence of photocatalytically induced NADH/NAD+ imbalance may depend on the surrounding enzymatic redox network. This consideration is particularly relevant to stemness‐enriched tumors, where redox plasticity supports metabolic adaptation, therapeutic resistance, and tumor persistence.

Cancer stemness is now widely recognized not as a fixed property of a rare cancer stem cell (CSC) population but as a dynamic and reversible cellular state within tumors. This plastic phenotype enables cancer cells to reacquire stem‐like traits, adapt metabolically, and survive therapeutic stress, thereby promoting tumor progression, metastasis, and treatment resistance [5, 6, 7, 8]. The acquisition and maintenance of stemness are closely tied to metabolic adaptability. In particular, redox homeostasis, mitochondrial bioenergetics, and anabolic metabolism must be coordinately regulated to sustain plasticity. A recurring biochemical feature of stemness‐enriched tumor cells is elevated aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) activity.

Beyond its canonical detoxification function, ALDH contributes to intracellular redox regulation by converting NAD+ to NADH and modulating redox flux through glutathione‐linked pathways [4, 9, 10, 11, 12]. By maintaining NADH availability and buffering oxidative stress [13, 14, 15, 16], ALDH supports mitochondrial function and metabolic resilience, both of which are essential for sustaining stemness plasticity.

Mitochondria lie at the center of this redox network. Among ALDH isoforms, the mitochondrial enzyme ALDH2 plays a critical role in preserving tumor cell fitness. By oxidizing endogenous and lipid peroxidation–derived aldehydes, ALDH2 limits mitochondrial damage and stabilizes redox balance, contributing to resistance against anticancer therapies [17, 18]. Notably, ALDH2 has also been implicated in suppressing pyroptotic cell death by modulating mitochondrial ROS signaling, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and HDAC3 translocation [19, 20, 21]. These roles extend ALDH2 beyond a simple metabolic enzyme, positioning it as a mitochondrial redox gatekeeper that preserves the biochemical environment required to sustain cancer stemness.

Given this central role, pharmacological inhibition of ALDH2 has been explored as a tumor‐sensitizing strategy [22, 23, 24]. However, such inhibition strategies are often positioned as adjuvants rather than standalone therapeutics. This limitation reflects, in part, the redundancy and adaptability of tumor redox metabolism: inhibition of a single enzymatic node may not be sufficient to collapse NADH homeostasis or mitochondrial function (Scheme 1B) [25, 26]. Conversely, PC can directly consume intracellular NADH, but does not intrinsically suppress enzymatic pathways that restore NADH/NAD+ balance. These complementary limitations led us to reason that durable redox disruption would require both photocatalytic NADH oxidation and simultaneous inhibition of the enzymatic routes that restore NADH.

To address these challenges, we sought to directly couple ALDH inhibition with photocatalytic redox modulation. Here, we report an integrated enzymatic–photocatalytic (E–PC) strategy that mechanistically combines ALDH inhibition with light‐driven NADH oxidation. In this design, a near‐infrared (NIR)–responsive organic photocatalyst (NBS) is covalently linked to a prodrug form of ALDH inhibitor DEAB, generating a self‐activating photocatalyst–inhibitor conjugate. Upon light irradiation, this system suppresses ALDH activity while catalytically oxidizing intracellular NADH to NAD+, thereby blocking enzymatic redox re‐equilibration, sustaining NADH/NAD+ imbalance, and undermining ALDH‐associated stemness phenotypes. This coordinated dual intervention enforces sustained mitochondrial redox imbalance, disrupts the electron transport chain and ATP production, and ultimately induces pyroptotic cell death. Preferential mitochondrial accumulation is facilitated by the elevated mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) of tumor cells (Δ𝜓𝑚 ≈ −180 to −220 mV) [27], while light activation affords precise spatiotemporal control over both inhibitor release and catalytic activity. By integrating enzyme inhibition with programmable photoredox chemistry, this E–PC design converts ALDH‐supported redox adaptability into a therapeutic vulnerability and provides a strategy for disrupting stemness‐associated tumor persistence (Scheme 1C).

Results and Discussion

Design and construction of the enzymatic‐photocatalytic (E–PC) platform. To overcome ALDH‐driven redox compensation that sustains cancer stemness and undermines phototherapeutic efficacy, we designed a dual‐functional enzymatic–photocatalytic (E–PC) conjugate, termed DE‐Et. The construct integrates two complementary elements within a single molecular framework: (i) 4‐(diethylamino)benzaldehyde (DEAB), an ALDH inhibitor (IC50 = 0.16 ± 0.03 µM; kinact = 85,800 ± 4200 M−1 s−1) [28], and (ii) a Nile Blue Sulfide analogue (NBS) that acts as a NIR‐responsive type I photosensitizer and photoredox catalyst (Figure 1A). Amine‐functionalized NBS derivatives are known to preserve favorable photophysical properties while allowing chemical diversification [29, 30], making them suitable scaffolds for multifunctional photoredox systems. To couple enzymatic inhibition with light activation, DEAB was tethered to NBS via a thioacetal linker, chosen for its ROS‐cleavability. This design enables photocatalytic uncaging triggered by light irradiation. The synthetic routes to NH2NBS and DE‐Et are shown in Schemes S1 and S2, respectively. Intermediates C1–C4 were characterized by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figures S1–S4), whereas DE‐Et and NH2NBS were characterized by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy and mass spectrometry (Figures S5–S10). Mechanistically, light excitation activates NBS to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) via type I photochemistry, which in turn promotes oxidative cleavage of the thioacetal linker and releases free DEAB. In this way, photoredox catalysis and enzyme inhibition are temporally and spatially coupled within a single molecular event.

Photophysical and Photochemical Properties of DE‐Et. DE‐Et displays a strong absorption band centered at 660 nm (ε = 1.63 × 104 M−1 cm−1) with emission around 700 nm (Figure 1B,C), closely matching the optical profile of NH2NBS (λabs = 660 nm, λem = 700 nm) and enabling efficient excitation under NIR light. Electrochemical analysis by cyclic voltammetry, referenced to ferrocene, yielded an excited‐state reduction potential (E*red) of 2.586 V (vs. NHE) (Figures 1D and S11), indicating strong oxidative capability upon photoexcitation.

To clarify the identity of the photo‐generated reactive species, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was performed using DMPO as a spin trap. Upon 660 nm irradiation, DE‐Et produced characteristic DMPO–O2 · − adduct signals (Figure 1E), confirming superoxide generation. This assignment was supported by solution‐phase fluorescence assays: dihydrorhodamine 123 (DHR123) displayed a marked increase in emission upon irradiation (Figure 1F), consistent with O2 · − generation. In parallel, hydroxyphenyl fluorescein (HPF) exhibited enhanced fluorescence at 516 nm, confirming the formation of hydroxyl radicals under identical conditions (Figure 1G). In contrast, no significant degradation of ABDA was detected, whereas methylene blue, used as a type II photosensitizer control, efficiently produced singlet oxygen under the same conditions (Figure 1H). These results establish that DE‐Et predominantly operates through type I photochemistry, consistent with the ROS profile of the parent NBS scaffold (Figures 1E–H and S12).

The light‐triggered linker cleavage and DEAB release from DE‐Et were verified by HPLC analysis under both solution‐phase and intracellular conditions. Upon light irradiation (660 nm, 100 mW cm−2, 5 min), a new peak corresponding to free DEAB was detected in both systems, supporting light‐triggered inhibitor release (Figures 1I and S13–S15).

Photocatalytic Oxidation of NADH by DE‐Et. Given the central role of NADH/NAD+ homeostasis in mitochondrial metabolism, we next examined whether the NBS‐derived photosensitizing/photoredox unit of DE‐Et could oxidize NADH upon excitation. The conversion of NADH to NAD+ under photoirradiation was monitored by UV‐Vis spectroscopy, as evidenced by the time‐dependent attenuation of the characteristic NADH absorption band at 340 nm. Under identical conditions, DE‐Et and NH2NBS exhibited comparable NADH oxidation time courses (Figure 2A–C). We further investigated the theoretical basis of photoredox catalysis under light irradiation. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed on the active chromophore (NHRNBS) as well as its close analog (NH2NBS). Upon photoexcitation, NHRNBS efficiently populates a long‐lived triplet (T1) state via intersystem crossing. Geometry optimization of the triplet state and its corresponding one‐electron‐reduced radical enabled the estimation of the adiabatic excited‐state reduction potential. The calculated value of +1.15 V vs. NHE substantially exceeds the experimentally established one‐electron oxidation potential of NADH (+0.93 V), indicating that photoexcited NHRNBS has sufficient oxidative power to promote NADH oxidation (Figures 2D and S20) [31]. Similarly, NH2NBS displayed a comparable excited‐state reduction potential of +1.19 V vs. NHE. In the CYPMPO radical‐trapping experiment, the addition of CYPMPO delayed saturation of the cytochrome c reduction signal under 660 nm irradiation, further supporting the involvement of radical intermediates in DE‐Et‐mediated NADH photooxidation (Figures S21, S22). Together, the UV–Vis NADH oxidation kinetics, CYPMPO radical‐trapping results, and calculated excited‐state redox properties support DE‐Et‐mediated photoinduced NADH oxidation under 660 nm irradiation.

Overcoming ALDH‐driven Redox Compensation. Although photoredox‐catalyzed NADH oxidation can disrupt mitochondrial function and induce cell death, ALDH‐high tumors may counteract this perturbation by enzymatically regenerating NADH. In ALDH‐rich environments, NAD+ acts as a cofactor for aldehyde oxidation, enabling continuous regeneration of NADH and thereby restoring the redox balance [12]. We therefore evaluated whether the E–PC design could overcome this compensatory mechanism. In a cell‐free system containing equimolar NAD+ and NADH (90 µM each), minimal spontaneous oxidation was observed over 12 h (1.51% increase in NAD+; Figure 2E). ALDH2 (0.67 µg mL−1) shifted the normalized signal below baseline (‐1.29%); inclusion of DEAB partially attenuated this decrease to 0.96%, supporting the contribution of ALDH2 activity to the observed redox compensation under these conditions (Figures 2E and S23, S24). Consistent with ALDH2‐mediated NADH compensation, the photocatalyst‐induced NADH depletion rate was progressively attenuated as ALDH2 increased.

Following irradiation, subsequent incubation with ALDH2 and its substrate regenerated NADH in a concentration‐dependent manner, reaching 15.02% above the pre‐irradiation level (Figure S25).

Under ALDH‐mediated redox compensation, a clear functional distinction emerged between conventional photocatalysis and the integrated E–PC design. Normalized NAD+ accumulation was calculated by subtracting the initial NAD+ signal from the signal measured after irradiation and incubation. Upon addition of ALDH, NH2NBS showed a marked reduction in normalized NAD+ accumulation, decreasing from 4.30 ± 0.15 under ALDH(−) conditions to 2.46 ± 0.05 under ALDH(+) conditions. This corresponds to a 42.8% decrease relative to the ALDH(−) condition, indicating that enzymatic NADH regeneration counteracted photocatalytic NADH oxidation. In contrast, DE‐Et retained substantial NAD+ accumulation in the presence of ALDH, decreasing from 4.09 ± 0.34 to 3.24 ± 0.13, corresponding to a smaller decrease of 20.8% relative to the ALDH(−) condition (Figures 2F–H and S26–S28). Additional comparative experiments using representative ALDH isoforms, including ALDH1A1, ALDH2, and ALDH3A1, showed that the observed ALDH‐related effect was not restricted to ALDH2 under the tested conditions (Figure S29). These results are consistent with the proposed role of integrated ALDH inhibition in partially suppressing the enzymatic reversal of NADH oxidation. This resistance to enzymatic reversal highlights the functional consequence of covalently integrating ALDH inhibition with photoredox catalysis. By simultaneously blocking NADH regeneration and promoting catalytic NADH oxidation, the E–PC construct disrupts redox recycling at its source (Figure 2I). Together, these findings demonstrate that DE‐Et effectively neutralizes ALDH‐mediated metabolic compensation—a resistance‐like buffering mechanism that otherwise diminishes the impact of conventional photoredox systems.

Mitochondrial Photoredox Activation Induces Redox Collapse and Pyroptotic Cytotoxicity. Mitochondrial ALDH2 maintains a reduced intracellular state by catalyzing the oxidation of aldehydes to generate NADH, thereby sustaining a high NADH/NAD+ ratio and redox homeostasis [17, 18]. This activity not only supports mitochondrial bioenergetics but also reinforces antioxidant capacity, which in turn suppresses pyroptotic cell death [19, 20, 32, 33]. In parallel, ALDH2 contributes to immune evasion by upregulating the checkpoint molecule VISTA, thereby attenuating cytotoxic CD8+ T cell function and promoting an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [34]. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of ALDH2 alone is often insufficient, as tumor cells rapidly restore NADH levels via compensatory metabolic pathways, thereby limiting durable therapeutic effects. These observations point to the need for strategies that simultaneously disrupt ALDH2‐dependent redox homeostasis and prevent metabolic compensation.

To address this, we developed an enzymatic–photocatalytic (E–PC) strategy that integrates ALDH inhibition with light‐driven NADH oxidation (Figure 3A). Aggressive cancers such as triple‐negative breast cancer are increasingly recognized to depend on mitochondrial redox homeostasis and metabolic reprogramming [35], pointing to NADH‐centered pathways as potential vulnerabilities [36]. In this context, enzymes that sustain NADH production, including ALDH2, may contribute functionally to redox buffering. Consistent with this, Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of ALDH2 in the studied cells (Figure 3B). We next examined the intracellular localization of DE‐Et prior to probing its redox effects. Cells were co‐incubated with DE‐Et and organelle‐specific probes—MitoTracker Green (mitochondria), LysoTracker Green (lysosomes), and Hoechst 33342 (nuclei). Confocal imaging revealed substantial colocalization between DE‐Et fluorescence and mitochondrial signals (Pearson's correlation coefficient R = 0.722), supporting a mitochondria‐associated subcellular distribution of DE‐Et (Figures 3C and S30, S31). This distribution is consistent with the molecular design of DE‐Et and its intended proximity to NADH‐dependent bioenergetic processes.

We next evaluated photocytotoxicity using MTT assays under light irradiation (660 nm, 40 mW cm−2, 5 min). DE‐Et elicited potent light‐dependent cytotoxicity with an IC50 value of 21.45 nM, markedly lower than that of NH2NBS (IC50 = 69.05 nM) under identical conditions (Figures 3D and S33, S34). Importantly, dark toxicity remained minimal up to 5 µM (Figure S32). Calcein‐AM/PI staining and bright‐field microscopy images further confirmed the photocytotoxicity of DE‐Et (Figures S35, S36). The covalently integrated construct also outperformed the simple combination of NH2NBS and DEAB, underscoring functional synergy achieved through molecular coupling. Similar trends were observed across multiple cancer cell lines, including T47D and 4T1 cells (Figure S38), indicating the platform's general applicability. Consistent with reduced cell viability, photoactivation of DE‐Et led to a marked decrease in the intracellular NADH/NAD+ ratio, indicative of efficient catalytic NADH oxidation (Figure 3E), accompanied by a substantial drop in ATP levels. JC‐1 staining further revealed extensive mitochondrial membrane depolarization following irradiation (Figure 3F), consistent with mitochondrial dysfunction.

At the cellular level, DE‐Et photoactivation in MDA‐MB‐231 cells induced rapid swelling, membrane rupture, and balloon‐like protrusions (Figure 3G), hallmarks of lytic cell death. In contrast, NH2NBS‐treated cells displayed comparatively modest structural changes (Figure S41). Membrane integrity was rapidly compromised, as evidenced by increased SYTOX Green uptake (Figure 3H). Notably, both the rate and extent of membrane permeabilization exceeded those observed in control groups, indicating accelerated cytotoxic kinetics driven by the coordinated enzymatic–photocatalytic mechanism.

Biochemical analyses further clarified the mode of cell death. Photoirradiation of DE‐Et–treated cells resulted in robust lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release, consistent with rapid membrane disruption (Figures 3I and S41). This was accompanied by a marked increase in extracellular ATP release in the DE‐Et + light group, supporting the occurrence of DAMP release during DE‐Et‐mediated pyroptotic cell death (Figure S42). Immunoblotting revealed activation of pyroptotic signaling, as indicated by cleavage of caspase‐3 and generation of the N‐terminal fragment of gasdermin E (GSDME‐N) (Figure 3J). Notably, although pretreatment with the ROS scavenger N‐acetylcysteine (NAC) partially restored cell viability, it did not abrogate GSDME cleavage. In addition, light‐triggered DEAB release from DE‐Et was retained in the presence of NAC, indicating that DE‐Et photoactivation and the subsequent GSDME‐mediated pyroptotic process are retained under these conditions (Figures S43–S45). Flow cytometric analysis of Annexin V/PI staining provided additional insight into cell fate. While early apoptotic cells are typically Annexin V‐positive with intact membranes, pyroptotic cells rapidly lose membrane integrity and appear Annexin V/PI double‐positive. Following DE‐Et photoactivation, a rapid accumulation of double‐positive cells was observed (Figure S49), consistent with early membrane permeabilization rather than progression through a prolonged apoptotic phase.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that mitochondrial photoredox activation by DE‐Et induces sustained NADH depletion, mitochondrial dysfunction, and rapid loss of membrane integrity, culminating in robust pyroptotic cell death (Figure 3K). By concurrently blocking ALDH2‐mediated redox compensation and catalytically oxidizing NADH, the E–PC strategy effectively overcomes metabolic buffering mechanisms that otherwise limit phototherapeutic efficacy.

Suppression of Cancer Stemness by Coordinated Redox Disruption. Because cancer stemness is sustained by metabolic plasticity and redox resilience, we next evaluated whether activation of the E–PC system attenuates stemness‐associated phenotypes. Light‐activated DE‐Et significantly reduced the ALDH‐active cell population (Figure 4A), a functional indicator of stemness enrichment. In parallel, the proportion of CD44High/CD24Low cells, a clinically relevant stem‐like signature associated with aggressive behavior and poor prognosis, was substantially reduced (Figure 4B). Notably, these reductions were greater than those observed with NH2NBS alone, with NH2NBS + DEAB, or in non‐irradiated controls. The superior efficacy of DE‐Et highlights the importance of simultaneously suppressing ALDH‐driven NADH regeneration and enforcing photocatalytic NADH oxidation.

The impact of DE‐Et on long‐term self‐renewal capacity was further assessed using clonogenic assays. As colony formation reflects the capacity for sustained proliferative expansion, a defining functional attribute of cancer stemness, reduced clonogenic survival provides a quantitative indicator of impaired stemness‐associated potential. DE‐Et photoactivation resulted in a pronounced reduction in colony formation (Figure 4C), demonstrating impaired long‐term proliferative capacity. In three‐dimensional T47D spheroids, treatment led to rapid architectural collapse and loss of structural cohesion (Figure 4D), accompanied by decreased ATP levels (Figure 4E).

In addition, immunoblot analysis showed a marked decrease in ALDH2 protein levels following photoactivation of DE‐Et (Figure 4F), which was consistent with the concurrent attenuation of CSC‐associated markers and functional readouts. These results are consistent with sustained mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic insufficiency. Collectively, these findings indicate that DE‐Et suppresses cancer stemness by destabilizing the redox and metabolic networks that sustain metabolic adaptability (Figure 4G). By concurrently blocking ALDH‐driven NADH replenishment and catalytically oxidizing existing NADH, the E–PC strategy imposes a persistent mitochondrial redox imbalance, destabilizing stemness‐associated survival programs.

In Vivo Antitumor Activity and Immune Activation. Encouraged by the in vitro findings, we next examined the antitumor efficacy of DE‐Et in a 4T1 murine breast tumor model. Following intravenous injection, DE‐Et rapidly accumulated in tumors, with fluorescence peaking at ∼10 h and persisting up to 60 h (Figure 5A–C), indicating favorable intratumoral retention.

For therapeutic evaluation, tumor‐bearing mice were randomized into six groups (PBS, NH2NBS + light, Comb (NH2NBS + DEAB), Comb (NH2NBS + DEAB) + light, DE‐Et, and DE‐Et + light). Treatment commenced once tumor volumes reached ∼40 mm3 and consisted of tail vein injection followed by either dark conditions or light irradiation (660 nm, 150 mW cm−2, 5 min). Upon photoactivation, DE‐Et induced pronounced tumor suppression (Figure 5D–H), achieving a TGI of 80.5% over 14 days, substantially higher than that of NH2NBS + DEAB + light (30.2%) (Figure 5G). Body weights remained stable (Figure 5F), suggesting acceptable tolerability.

Consistent with these observations, H&E staining of tumor and major‐organ sections further supported the therapeutic outcome and biosafety, showing pronounced tumor damage in the DE‐Et + light group while revealing no obvious histopathological abnormalities in major organs across all groups (Figure S50).

Given the pyroptotic mechanism observed in vitro, we next evaluated whether this modality elicited immunogenic activation in vivo. Pyroptosis‐associated release of cellular contents is known to promote dendritic‐cell (DC) maturation [37, 38]; accordingly, immune profiling was performed in tumor‐draining lymph nodes and tumor tissues. In tumor‐draining lymph nodes, the proportion of CD11c+/MHC II+ DCs increased to 11.66% in the DE‐Et + light group (Figure 5I–L), along with elevated CD80/CD86 expression (Figure 5M–P), supporting effective antigen‐presenting activation.

Consistent with enhanced innate immune priming, adaptive immune responses were also markedly amplified. Tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and CD4+ helper T cells reached 14.57 and 25.58%, respectively, in the DE‐Et + light group, substantially higher than those observed in the PBS group (8.22 and 6.18%) or in the NH2NBS + DEAB + light group (10.75 and 9.38%). Together, these findings demonstrate that DE‐Et not only suppresses primary tumor growth through redox‐mediated cytotoxicity but also elicits a robust photo‐stimulated antitumor immune response. This immune activation is consistent with pyroptosis‐driven immunogenic cell death triggered by the coordinated enzymatic–photocatalytic mechanism.

Systemic Antitumor Immunity in a Bilateral Tumor Model. To evaluate systemic effects, a bilateral 4T1 subcutaneous tumor model was employed, in which the right‐flank tumor served as the primary lesion and the contralateral tumor as a distant, untreated site (Figure 6A). Remarkably, DE‐Et + light suppressed both primary and distant tumors, achieving TGIs of 81.8 and 71.39% at each site, while body weights remained stable. In contrast, control treatments produced minimal inhibition of distant lesions (Figure 6B–D and S51).

Immune profiling supports systemic immune activation driven by local treatment (Figures 6E–L and S52). In the DE‐Et + light treatment group, the proportion of CD11c+/MHC II+ and CD80+/CD86+ dendritic cells markedly increased in the lymph nodes, consistent with enhanced antigen presentation and priming. In line with this, both primary and distant tumors from mice treated with DE‐Et + light showed elevated infiltration of both CD4+ helper T cells and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells relative to PBS and Comb (NH2NBS + DEAB) + light controls. These findings support that localized E–PC activation promotes systemic immune activation, manifesting as an immune‐mediated abscopal effect consistent with immunogenic cell death.

Conclusion

Although photocatalytic NADH oxidation can acutely perturb cellular redox balance, intracellular NADH pools can be restored through enzyme‐coupled redox metabolism. Our results show that ALDH‐mediated restoration of NADH/NAD+ balance can buffer photoredox‐induced NADH depletion, thereby limiting sustained redox imbalance and providing a mechanistic rationale for enzyme‐coupled photoredox catalysis. Guided by this mechanistic insight, we constructed a light‐activated enzymatic–photocatalytic conjugate, DE‐Et, that integrates ALDH inhibition with NIR‐driven photoredox catalysis within a single molecular framework. By covalently linking DEAB to a Nile Blue Sulfide‐derived photocatalyst through a ROS‐cleavable linker, DE‐Et converts optical excitation into a coordinated redox intervention. Upon irradiation, DE‐Et attenuates ALDH‐mediated NADH restoration while catalytically oxidizing intracellular NADH, thereby sustaining disruption of the NADH/NAD+ balance. This dual action limits redox recovery and contributes to the suppression of ALDH‐associated stemness phenotypes. The resulting mitochondrial redox collapse impairs ATP generation, destabilizes bioenergetic homeostasis, and culminates in rapid membrane failure. This coordinated redox disruption by DE‐Et drives caspase‐3/GSDME‐dependent pyroptosis, thereby overcoming the intrinsic resilience of ALDH‐high, stemness‐enriched tumor populations. The resulting metabolic failure suppresses stemness‐associated phenotypes and translates into robust tumor inhibition in vivo. Importantly, localized photoactivation further elicits systemic antitumor immunity, consistent with pyroptosis‐mediated dendritic‐cell maturation and enhanced T‐cell responses. Together, this study establishes a conceptual framework integrating enzyme inhibition and photoredox catalysis within a single molecular architecture to attenuate compensatory metabolic pathways. By converting ALDH‐mediated redox adaptability into a therapeutic vulnerability, the enzymatic–photocatalytic strategy offers a mechanistically grounded approach for targeting stemness‐driven tumor persistence and enhancing the durability of photoredox‐based cancer treatment.
