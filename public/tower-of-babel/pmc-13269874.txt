Novel associations of VPS13C with phenotype and conversion of idiopathic REM sleep behavior disorder
Authors: Yanfei Ding, Xinyi Zhou, Aonan Zhao, Min Zhong, Yang Jiao, Ningdi Luo, Lingbing Wang, Ningyuan Wang, Liche Zhou, Yuanyuan Li, Jun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While VPS13C is a recessively inherited Parkinson’s disease (PD) gene, its potential dominant effects in idiopathic Rapid-eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder (iRBD) remain unexplored. The relation between its monogenic form and the onset of PD suggested that subtype specificity may need to be considered. We examined the presence of likely pathogenic VPS13C variants in 150 iRBD and 180 α-synucleinopathy patients (iRBD-first and movement disorder-first). VPS13C variants were significantly enriched in iRBD patients, and ten iRBD risk variants have been identified. iRBD risk VPS13C variant carriers demonstrated more severe RBD symptoms and greater autonomic dysfunction, correlating with REM sleep EEG and autonomic network activity abnormalities. Notably, enrichment was specific to the iRBD-first α-synucleinopathy subtype, and iRBD risk VPS13C variant carriers showed accelerated progression to overt α-synucleinopathy. These results suggest that VPS13C not only contributes to iRBD susceptibility but also serves as a marker for the iRBD-first α-synucleinopathy and faster disease conversion.

Introduction

α-Synucleinopathies, including Parkinson’s disease (PD), dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), and multiple system atrophy (MSA), are neurodegenerative disorders characterized by the abnormal aggregation of α-synuclein.1–4 Based on different patterns of pathological spread, α-synucleinopathies can be divided into two categories: body-first and brain-first. In the body-first subtype, pathology is proposed to ascend from the periphery to the brainstem in a bottom-up manner, which aligns with the typical clinical sequence where autonomic dysfunction and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder (RBD) (a brainstem-mediated phenomenon) often precede motor symptoms. In contrast, the brain-first subtype is characterized by initial α-synuclein aggregation in the rostral parts of the central nervous system. The disease then descends more asymmetrically in a top–down manner, resulting in early, asymmetric motor symptoms. Top–down RBD may emerge at a later stage.5,6 In our study, we focused on subtypes characterized as RBD-first and movement disorder-first. RBD is a sleep disorder characterized by loss of muscle atonia with dream-enactment during REM sleep.7 More than 80% of idiopathic RBD (iRBD) patients will convert to α-synucleinopathy within 10–15 years. Thus, it is important to explore the occurrence of iRBD and the mechanism of its conversion to α-synucleinopathies.8 The partial genetic overlap between iRBD and α-synucleinopathies may be attributed to the fact that the occurrence of iRBD is involved in part of the α-synuclein pathogenesis, specifically linked to the iRBD-first subtype.9 Consequently, investigating genetic mutational abnormalities and their relationship with α-synucleinopathies based on iRBD is essential.

Currently, the relevance of lysosomal dysfunction to the pathogenesis of iRBD and α-synucleinopathies has been increasingly emphasized.10,11 Various genes regulating lysosomal function, such as GBA and TMEM175, are associated with iRBD and α-synucleinopathy pathogenesis.12,13 Vacuolar protein sorting 13 homolog C (VPS13C), a member of the vacuolar protein sorting-associated 13 gene family, encodes lipid transfer proteins that localize to contact sites between the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and late endosomes/lysosomes.14 VPS13C modulates several lysosomal functions, including lipid transfer at ER-lysosome contacts,15 lysosomal degradation,16 mitophagy,17 and lysosomal stress responses.18

Although VPS13C is primarily considered an autosomal recessive gene for PD,19 growing pieces of evidence indicate its genetic complexity: VPS13C can either cause a monogenic form of early-onset Parkinsonism or confer genetic susceptibility to PD.20–22 The inconsistent results may reflect subtype-dependent associations between VPS13C and α-synucleinopathies. Critically, endogenous VPS13C expression is significantly reduced even in individuals carrying a monogenic heterozygous mutation, as detected in patient samples.23 This further emphasizes that VPS13C variants, even single-allele mutations, should not be overlooked. In addition, VPS13C compound heterozygous mutations are associated with other α-synucleinopathies, such as DLB, suggesting that VPS13C variants may contribute not only to PD but also to α-synucleinopathy pathogenesis.23 Taken together, we speculated that the VPS13C variants may be associated with iRBD, resulting in iRBD-first α-synucleinopathies.

In this study, we observed a significant enrichment of low-frequency and rare genetic variants in VPS13C within our iRBD cohort, across 16 risk genes associated with α-synucleinopathy (including: VPS13C, ATP13A2, FBXO7, GBA, GCH1, INPP5F, LRRK2, MAPT, PARK7, PINK1, PLA2G6, PRKN, SNCA, TMEM175, VPS35, SCARB2). Notably, previous studies have not observed a significant association between VPS13C and iRBD in European populations24, suggesting that differences in ethnic genetic backgrounds may need to be considered. Furthermore, we systematically evaluated VPS13C variants in our iRBD cohort to assess both their population enrichment and influence on clinical phenotypes. Comparative analyses further examined the enrichment of VPS13C variants across α-synucleinopathy subtypes and their effects on iRBD conversion. This study aims to clarify the role of VPS13C variants in iRBD occurrence and progression to defined α-synucleinopathy subtypes in the Chinese population.

Results

Correlation of VPS13C variants with iRBD risk

150 patients with polysomnography (PSG)-confirmed iRBD were recruited and completed whole-exome sequencing (WES). To investigate whether variants in α-synucleinopathy risk genes of interest might lead to iRBD, we assessed the overall burden of likely pathogenic variants across 16 α-synucleinopathy risk genes and compared this with the control cohort from the East Asian gnomAD database (including: VPS13C, ATP13A2, FBXO7, GBA, GCH1, INPP5F, LRRK2, MAPT, PARK7, PINK1, PLA2G6, PRKN, SNCA, TMEM175, VPS35, SCARB2). Compared with the control cohort from the gnomAD East Asian database, we observed a significantly higher overall burden of likely pathogenic VPS13C and MAPT variants in the iRBD cohort (VPS13C: OR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.43–2.36, P = 3×10⁻6; MAPT: OR = 2.19, 95% CI: 1.32–3.64, P = 0.005) (Table 1 and Table S1). However, only the association between VPS13C and iRBD passed the Bonferroni correction. To further clarify the association between VPS13C and iRBD in the Chinese population, we selected the control cohort from the WBBC database for repeated comparisons. Compared with the WBBC Chinese control cohort, the RJ-iRBD cohort also exhibited a significantly higher burden of likely pathogenic VPS13C variants (OR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.19–1.99, P = 0.001) (Table 1). Analysis of the WES data for the presence of likely pathogenic variants revealed that 63 (42%) affected individuals (27 females and 36 males) of 150 carried missense in VPS13C, in either a homozygous or a heterozygous state (Table S2). The most common variants in our entire cohort were c.10847 G > A/p. Gly3616Asp, c.3905 A > G/ p. Tyr1302Cys and c.4483 A > G/ p. Ile1495Val, with complete variant profiles detailed in Table S2. In total, qualifying likely pathogenic VPS13C variants were identified in 42% of the individuals in the present RJ-iRBD cohort.

For each variant, we compared the differences in allele frequencies between the RJ-iRBD and the East Asian gnomAD control cohort. Ten VPS13C variants were found to be either observed individually or significantly enriched in the RJ-iRBD cohort (Tables 2, S3 and S4). We further used two genetic models, dominant and recessive, to investigate the association between the targeted variant and the susceptibility to iRBD. In the dominant model, it was again verified that these ten VPS13C variants were associated with higher iRBD susceptibility, which were defined as the iRBD risk VPS13C variants (Tables 2 and S4). All these associations passed the FDR correction. Furthermore, we validated our findings using the Chinese control cohort from the WBBC database. We observed that the associations of other variants with the iRBD were consistently replicated, except c.6031 A > G/ p. Thr2011Ala (Table S5). All associations passed the FDR correction. Interestingly, the c.10847 G > A/p. Quantitative analysis of PSG recordings demonstrated distinct power alterations in the electroencephalogram (EEG) of the iRBD_VPS13C group compared to the iRBD_nonVPS13C group (Fig. 2b–d and Fig. S1). Specifically, frequency analysis of EEG signals revealed statistically significant between-group differences across multiple frequency bands and electrode locations: (1) reduced delta band power at the P3 electrode (Fig. 2b); (2) diminished theta activity at both P3 and Cz regions (Fig. 2c); and (3) attenuated alpha oscillations across frontal (F3) and parietal (P3) regions (Fig. 2d). These consistent findings of decreased power across multiple frequency bands and cortical regions collectively suggest a pattern of globally reduced EEG energy during REM sleep in carriers of iRBD risk VPS13C variants. Such widespread electrophysiological alterations may reflect diminished neural synchronization during sleep in this genetic subgroup of iRBD patients.

iRBD risk VPS13C variants are associated with autonomic dysfunction

We assessed autonomic dysfunction in patients using the SCOPA-AUT questionnaire. We defined autonomic dysfunction as a score >1 on any item of the SCOPA-AUT. Based on this definition, the majority of patients in both groups exhibited some degree of autonomic symptoms, and only 2% of patients in the iRBD_VPS13C group (1/43) and 2% in the iRBD_nonVPS13C group (2/107) were completely asymptomatic. Importantly, patients in the iRBD_VPS13C group had significantly higher total SCOPA-AUT scores compared to those in the iRBD_nonVPS13C group, indicating that while autonomic dysfunction was prevalent in both groups, its severity was greater in carriers of VPS13C variants (P = 0.001) (Table 3). We thus assessed the changes in resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC) of the central autonomic network (CAN) using core autonomic modulation regions as seeds. Group analyses revealed significant differences across the cerebral and cerebellar gray matter of CAN. For the LAMYG network, iRBD_VPS13C patients showed significantly reduced connectivity in the right Temporal_Mid and left Cuneus (Fig. 2e, f and Table S6). Regarding the RaINS network, decreased connectivity was detected within the right Cerebellum_4_5 in the iRBD_VPS13C group patients (Fig. 2g and Table S6). No connectivity differences were observed in the pMCC, LpINS, or vmPFC networks. Furthermore, significant correlations emerged between non-motor symptom scales and specific functional connections (Table S7). SCOPA-AUT (digestive) and UPDRS-I scores correlated positively with FC between LAMYG and right Temporal_Mid, while UPDRS-I and SS-16 scores were associated with LAMYG-left Cuneus connectivity (Table S7). These findings imply that iRBD patients with iRBD risk VPS13C variants have more pronounced autonomic dysfunction, which is associated with altered rsMR-FC of the CAN.

VPS13C is associated with iRBD-first α-synucleinopathy, rather than movement disorder-first subtype

iRBD is considered a precursor to α-synucleinopathy, and >80% of patients with iRBD will convert within 10–15 years26. Therefore, we hypothesized that the VPS13C mutation would preferentially lead to iRBD-first α-synucleinopathies (with iRBD as a prodromal symptom, referred to as iRBDpre) rather than movement disorder-first subtype (with movement disorders as the first symptom, referred to as noniRBDpre). Therefore, we additionally recruited 180 α-synucleinopathy patients (including PD and MSA patients), consisting of 90 iRBDpre and 90 noniRBDpre patients matched for age, gender, and education (Table S8). Using consistent variant classification criteria to analyze the likely pathogenic variants (Fig. 1). We found the overall burden of VPS13C variants was significantly higher in iRBDpre patients compared with controls in the gnomAD East Asian database (OR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.33–2.54, P = 0.0004, Fisher’s exact test), whereas no enrichment was found in the noniRBDpre patients (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.74–1.57, P = 0.70, Fisher’s exact test) (Table 4). In contrast, the overall burden of VPS13C variants was also higher in the iRBDpre group than the noniRBDpre group (OR = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.04–2.78, P = 0.047, Fisher’s exact test) (Table 4). The enrichment of VPS13C in iRBDpre patients (compared with the gnomAD East Asian control cohort) passed Bonferroni correction. The mutation of VPS13C promotes iRBD conversion

VPS13C variants contribute to iRBD pathogenesis and are related to iRBD-first α-synucleinopathies. Given concerns about conversion timing, we hypothesized that iRBD risk VPS13C variants might predict disease progression. We followed up with the patients in the RJ-iRBD cohort and retrospectively analyzed the time to conversion to α-synucleinopathy in the iRBDpre group patients. The onset time of RBD symptoms was recorded for each patient. We compared the conversion time between carriers of the iRBD risk VPS13C variant and non-carriers (from the RJ-iRBD and iRBDpre cohorts). The Kaplan–Meier curve was used to assess the predictive value of the iRBD risk VPS13C variants in the course of disease conversion. We found carriers of iRBD risk VPS13C variants had significantly shorter α-synucleinopathy-free survival (mean 4 years) versus non-carriers (mean 7 years) (HR = 1.78, P = 0.021, Fig. 3). Discussion

Our study linked low-frequency and rare genetic variants in the VPS13C gene to the pathogenesis of iRBD within the Chinese population, and systematically analyzed their impact on clinical and converted phenotypes. We found that likely pathogenic VPS13C variants were significantly enriched in our iRBD cohort and identified multiple variants associated with iRBD susceptibility. Moreover, the VPS13C variants identified in our iRBD cohort exhibit marked differences from those present in the European iRBD cohort, suggesting that the association between VPS13C and iRBD demonstrates significant racial heterogeneity. Patients with iRBD risk VPS13C variants had a more severe RBD phenotype and autonomic dysfunction, corresponding to distinct EEG during REM sleep and brain autonomic function network abnormalities, respectively. Furthermore, likely pathogenic VPS13C variants were enriched in the iRBDpre cohort but not the noniRBDpre cohort, and promoted faster conversion of iRBD to α-synucleinopathies, further revealing a specific role of VPS13C in the iRBD-first subtype.

Genetic studies indicate that only a partial overlap between iRBD and α-synucleinopathies, suggesting that iRBD individuals represent only a specific subpopulation within α-synucleinopathies. So far, all five loci identified in the most extensive iRBD genome-wide association study seem to revolve around the GBA1 pathway.13 The GBA1 gene encodes a critical lysosomal enzyme, highlighting the importance of lysosomal function in iRBD pathogenesis.27 Recent findings also found that LAMP3, another lysosome-associated gene, was potentially involved in iRBD and reinforces the role of lysosomal dysfunction in iRBD.9,28 Collectively, lysosomal function-related gene mutations may be important factors contributing to the development of iRBD.

VPS13C encodes an important lysosomal function-related protein.19,29 Previous studies demonstrated that the genetic deletion of VPS13C in HeLa cells or iPSC-derived human neurons results in altered lysosome homeostasis.16,18 Notably, in VPS13C KO HeLa cells, the cGAS-STING innate immunity pathway30 is activated, potentially reflecting impaired STING degradation in dysfunctional lysosomes and escape of mitochondrial DNA into the cytosol.16 These findings indicate a potential role of VPS13C in maintaining the integrity of the lysosomal membrane and possibly in membrane repair. In addition, VPS13C modulates lysosomal contacts with the ER and mitochondria.18 Loss of VPS13C in neurons altered ER-lysosome contact dynamics by significantly increasing ER-lysosome contact formation. VPS13C also regulates the mitochondrial–lysosomal contact site formation and duration, and VPS13C knockdown can lead to abnormal mitochondrial morphology and mitochondrial dysfunction.17 Overall, VPS13C is critical for lysosomal homeostasis and function and thus may be an important factor influencing the pathogenesis and clinical phenotype of iRBD.

In our study, we observed a significant enrichment of likely pathogenic VPS13C variants in our RJ-iRBD cohort, and individuals with iRBD risk VPS13C variants had a more pronounced RBD sleep phenotype as well as autonomic dysfunction. This aligns with findings that body-first α-synucleinopathies have more pronounced autonomic dysfunction.31

Furthermore, we observed that the association of VPS13C variants with α-synucleinopathies requires consideration of subtype differences. In other words, VPS13C variants contribute to the development of iRBD-first α-synucleinopathies. Given that impaired autophagy, as well as dysfunctional lysosomal degradation, are potential convergent mechanisms for various forms of α-synucleinopathies,32,33 VPS13C may act through lysosomal effects. In addition, the shorter conversion window in VPS13C-mutated iRBD patients may also reflect its profound lysosomal effects, though additional studies are needed.

Notably, biallelic (homozygous or compound heterozygous) mutations in VPS13C are recognized as a cause of rare, autosomal recessive, early-onset Parkinsonism. However, several large cohort studies have observed associations between heterozygous VPS13C variants and late-onset sporadic PD.20–22 While in our study, we observed an association between VPS13C variants and the occurrence of iRBD with an onset age of ~60 years. We hypothesized that heterozygous VPS13C variants, although insufficient to directly cause early-onset α-synucleinopathy, lead to protein dysfunction that disrupts lysosomal homeostasis and function, thereby increasing individual genetic susceptibility to α-synucleinopathy. In this model, VPS13C acts as a risk factor that may ultimately promote the development of late-onset iRBD through the cumulative effects of long-term cellular damage. More importantly, we found that patients with iRBD risk VPS13C variants exhibited a significantly accelerated progression to α-synucleinopathy, further suggesting that VPS13C variants not only increase disease risk but may also accelerate disease progression. In summary, the VPS13C mutation may represent a genetic risk factor that increases susceptibility to iRBD and promotes disease conversion, rather than directly influencing the age of onset. This requires validation through subsequent studies.

This study has several limitations. First, our study has a relatively small sample size. In the future, we plan to conduct replication studies involving larger sample sizes to validate these findings. Second, we lacked an independent healthy control group. Given the potential for genetic heterogeneity bias when using database population data as controls, we plan to incorporate healthy controls with identical genetic backgrounds for further analysis in subsequent studies. Third, there is a lack of objective quantitative assessment tools for autonomic dysfunction in our study (such as blood pressure, autonomic tests or MBIG). Future cohort development and follow-up should incorporate these objective measures to more accurately evaluate autonomic function. Fourth, we did not incorporate important factors that indicated an elevated risk of phenoconversion in our study, such as the presence of α-synuclein in the cerebrospinal fluid or skin by the Seed Amplification Assay and dopamine transporter imaging. Future long-term follow-up studies of iRBD patients should systematically evaluate these markers to further clarify the mechanisms by which VPS13C variants influence disease conversion.

In conclusion, our results suggest that VPS13C variants may be associated with the development of iRBD and lead to a more severe RBD sleep phenotype and autonomic dysfunction. This could be explained by abnormalities in EEG function during REM sleep, as well as abnormalities in the brain autonomic function network connectivity. iRBD is an early symptom of body-first α-synucleinopathies, and a similar enrichment of VPS13C variants was found in iRBD-first α-synucleinopathy patients, which further demonstrates the important association of the VPS13C variants with the development of iRBD and iRBD-first α-synucleinopathies. Notably, the VPS13C mutation was a risk marker for the conversion of iRBD to α-synucleinopathy, which may be related to the involvement of VPS13C in important lysosomal functions. For the correlation between VPS13C and iRBD as well as iRBD-first α-synucleinopathies, further in-depth studies are needed to more accurately understand the pathogenesis and conversional mechanisms of iRBD, potentially opening new avenues for disease prevention and treatment.

Methods

Patients and clinical assessments

Participants were recruited between 2016 and 2025 in the sleep and movement disorder clinic of Ruijin Hospital. 150 iRBD patients were ‌diagnosed by PSG according to the International Classification of Sleep Disorders—3rd ed. (ICSD-3) criteria,34 and underwent annual follow-up, with an average follow-up period of 5.14 years. 180 α-synucleinopathies patients were evaluated by at least two experienced neurologists and underwent a standard series of examinations,35,36 and each patient was carefully questioned about iRBD-related medical history, including time of initiation and presentation. Among these, 90 α-synucleinopathy patients were diagnosed with iRBD, where RBD symptoms preceded motor impairment, referred to as the iRBD-first subtype (65 PD patients and 25 MSA patients). 90 patients with α-synucleinopathy presented with movement disorders but without RBD, referred to as the movement disorder-first subtype (62 PD patients and 28 MSA patients). The RBD symptom of all iRBD-first subtype patients was confirmed by PSG. The movement disorder-first subtype patients reported no RBD symptoms and screened negative on the REM sleep behavior disorder screening questionnaire (RBDSQ) (score <5).37 Clinical rating scales were used to conduct motor and non-motor evaluations by a neurologist, including the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS), RBDSQ, Scales for Outcomes in Parkinson’s Disease—Autonomic Dysfunction (SCOPA-AUT), Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA), Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD), and Non-Motor Symptoms Questionnaire (NMSQ), Sniffin’ Sticks Screening 16-item Test (SS-16), Wexner Constipation Scoring Scale (Wexner), Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Ruijin Hospital (approval no. 2020-165). Each patient gave written informed consent for participation in the study, and we followed the guidelines according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Genetic analysis

The DNA of 150 iRBD patients and 180 α-synucleinopathy patients (including PD and MSA patients) was subjected to WES. Genomic DNA (240 ng) was fragmented and enriched for human exonic sequences using the SureSelect HS Human All Exon V8 (Agilent Technologies) using the manufacturer’s protocol and sequenced on the NOVAseq xplus platform to generate 150 base-paired-end reads. Fastq files were mapped to the reference human genome (hg19/GRCh37) using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner package (v2.2.1). Local realignment of the mapped reads around potential insertion/deletion (indel) sites was carried out with the Genome Analysis Tool Kit v4.6.1.0. Duplicate reads were marked using Picard v.1.8, and BAM files were sorted and indexed with SAM tools v1.19. Sequence data was generated for these samples, and a minimum of 90.23% and 80.92% of the targeted exome was covered to a depth of at least 20× and 30× coverage, respectively.

CADD was used to predict the pathogenicity of the variants.38 We screened likely pathogenic variants by a MAF < 5% in the general population and a CADD score >15.25 Data from individuals of East Asian ancestry from the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) were extracted to serve as a control cohort. These data were utilized to calculate the frequency of likely pathogenic genetic variants in the population and for the enrichment analysis. Furthermore, Chinese individuals from the Westlake BioBank for Chinese (WBBC) database were employed as a control cohort to conduct a confirmatory assessment of the enrichment of genetic variants within the iRBD cohort. The gnomAD is a large-scale database of human genetic variants, providing background frequencies of specific variants in the general population. The use of gnomAD as an external control is a validated approach in genetics, as demonstrated by recent large-scale comparative analyses.39 The WBBC is a population-specific genomic database for Chinese individuals, offering whole-genome sequencing and genotyping data as a reference for genetic studies in Chinese populations.40 Data were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test (two-tailed) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). CADD scores were obtained from https://cadd.gs.washington.edu.

Polysomnography

All patients completed overnight PSG monitoring. The PSG recordings included ten EEG channels (F3, F4, Fz, C3, C4, Cz, P3, P4, O1, O2), bilateral mastoid reference electrode (M1, M2), bilateral electrooculography (ROC, LOC), submental and bilateral anterior tibialis electromyography, electrocardiogram, and measurements of nasal and oral airflow, thoracic and abdominal respiration, oxygen saturation, and body position. The PSG recordings were scored by experienced technicians, including sleep stages and related events, and reports were generated using Profusion Sleep Software (Compumedics). Epochs containing gross body movements, significant electrode artifacts, eye blinks, or excessive muscle activity were manually flagged and excluded from subsequent analyses. EEG data were segmented into 30-second epochs for further analysis.

All EEG signals were preprocessed using the MATLAB toolbox EEGLab (Delorme and Makeig 2004). We extract EEG data of the REM stage to explore the frequency characteristics. The signals from ten EEG channels were re-referenced to the average reference and band-pass filtered between 0.3 and 35 Hz. The frequency bands of interest were selected as delta (1.0–4.0 Hz), theta (4.0–8.0 Hz), alpha (8.0–13 Hz), and beta (13.0–30.0 Hz), which cover the primary EEG activities. Between-group differences were presented by topographic maps.

Neuroimaging

Structural and functional images were acquired with 3T Siemens scanners with a 12-channel head coil at the Department of Radiology at Ruijin Hospital. A 3D magnetization prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo sequence was used to acquire T1-weighted images with the following settings: slices = 192, field of view = 250 mm2, thickness = 1.0 mm, flip angle = 9°, voxel size = 0.5 × 0.5 × 1 mm3, echo time = 2.44 ms, repetition time = 1900 ms, and inversion time = 900 ms. A gradient echo-planar imaging sequence was used for resting-state fMRI scans with slices = 36, field of view = 192 mm2, thickness = 3.0 mm, flip angle = 90°, voxel size = 3.0 × 3.0 × 3.0 mm3, echo time = 22 ms, repetition time = 2000 ms, and inversion time = 900 ms.

Imaging preprocessing and FC analysis were performed in the DPABI software (http://rfmri.org/DPARSF). The functional images were slice-timing-corrected, centered to the origin of coordinates and realigned. Furthermore, images were normalized into standard Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space and smoothed using a 6-mm full-width at half-maximum isotropic Gaussian kernel. The time series were filtered with a band-pass filter (0.01–0.1 Hz). After preprocessing, the head motion was examined with a standard of 3° of maximal rotation and 3 mm of maximal translation, and the normalized images were checked by visual inspection.

FC alterations were explored by using core modulatory regions of CAN as seeds. Seed regions of interest (ROIs) for autonomic function were defined as posterior midcingulate cortex (pMCC) (MNI coordinates, x = 4, y = 0, z = 48), left amygdala (LAMYG) (−22, −8, −16), right anterior insula (RaINS) (34, 20, 4), left posterior insula (LpINS) (−32, −18, 12), and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) (−4, 38, −16).41,42

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and SPM12 (Math Works Inc., Natick, USA). Differences in quantitative data were analyzed using two-sample t tests, and categorical variables using Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests. Dominant and recessive models were applied to assess the risk of each variant. The following definitions were used, assuming A represents the major allele and a represents the minor allele: dominant was defined as 1 (aa + Aa) versus 0 (AA) and recessive as 1 (aa) versus 0 (AA + Aa). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-sided). Multiple testing corrections were performed: (1) For the overall burden of likely pathogenic variants in the risk genes, the Bonferroni correction was applied. (2) For each VPS13C variant, associations were assessed separately under three genetic models (allelic, dominant, and recessive), with correction applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR method. Correlations were evaluated using either Pearson correlation or Spearman correlation after verifying normality. For functional imaging analysis, we performed two-sample t-tests on those z-value maps within groups to determine statistically significant positive correlations with the seeds in each group, respectively. The statistical significance was set at the combination of a voxel-level uncorrected threshold of P < 0.001 and a cluster-level family-wise error-corrected threshold of P < 0.05. The Kaplan–Meier curve was used to assess the disease conversion.
