The relationship between school belonging and college students’ mental health: a chain mediation model involving physical exercise and a growth mindset
Authors: Yong Jiang, Wei Jin, Beier Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis cross-sectional study examined the association between school belonging and symptom-based mental health among college students and tested whether physical exercise and growth mindset statistically mediated this association.MethodsA cross-sectional questionnaire survey was conducted among 600 college students. Mediation analysis was performed using the PROCESS macro (Model 6), and 95% confidence intervals were constructed using 5,000 bootstrap repetitions (bias-corrected percentile bootstrap).ResultsThe total effect size of school belonging on college students’ mental health was 0.589, with a confidence interval of [0.563, 0.616]; its direct effect size was 0.260, with a confidence interval of [0.210, 0.309]; its total indirect effect was 0.329, with a confidence interval of [0.267, 0.384]. The indirect effect of school belonging via physical exercise was 0.111, with a confidence interval of [0.075, 0.146]; the indirect effect via growth mindset is 0.160, with a confidence interval of [0.119, 0.202]; the chained mediation effect between physical exercise and growth mindset is 0.057, with a confidence interval of [0.034, 0.084]. It should be noted that the mediation effects in this study are statistical mediation effects based on cross-sectional data and do not imply temporal sequence or causal mediation.ConclusionsSchool belonging was positively associated with the mental health indicator among college students. Physical exercise and growth mindset showed statistically significant indirect associations, both independently and through a sequential pathway. These findings should be interpreted as associations based on cross-sectional data. They do not establish temporal ordering or causal mediation, and the outcome mainly reflects fewer psychological symptoms rather than positive mental health.

International organizations place great emphasis on the social determinants of health. Institutions such as the World Health Organization (WHO) regard them as the key to improving the overall health and quality of life of populations, and have consequently defined health not merely as the absence of disease or suffering, but as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being [1]. Dr. Brock Chisholm, the first Director-General of the World Health Organization, once said, “There is no true physical health without mental health.” Meanwhile, the “Special Action Plan for Comprehensively Strengthening and Improving Student Mental Health Work in the New Era (2023–2025),” jointly issued by the Ministry of Education and 16 other departments, emphasizes the need to place mental health work in a more prominent position [2]. Mental health refers to a state of sound adaptation at the cognitive, emotional, and behavioral levels, serving as the foundation for normal life and learning [3]. College students are in a critical transitional phase from adolescence to adulthood. Psychological distress and mental health issues that arise during this specific developmental stage can have far-reaching impacts on their growth trajectory if not addressed in a timely manner. Particularly among college students, multiple challenges—including difficulties adapting to new environments, intensified academic competition, complex interpersonal relationships, confusion regarding self-identity, and pressure regarding future employment—have led to a continuous increase in psychological stress. Consequently, mental health issues have become particularly prominent, urgently requiring joint attention and effective responses from society, schools, and families. In recent years, school belonging has garnered increasing attention in the fields of education, health, sociology, and psychology. Research indicates that school belonging serves as a significant protective factor for college students and plays a crucial role in enhancing their mental health [4].A strong sense of school belonging helps boost students’ motivation and engagement in their studies, improves attendance, and ultimately leads to better academic performance. However, research on the relationship between school belonging and college students’ mental health requires further exploration, and there are still many areas that need to be investigated.Therefore, building on existing research, this study further integrates behavioral and cognitive pathways to construct a chained mediation model examining the relationship between school belonging and college students’ mental health, and explore potential pathways linking the two.Unlike previous studies that focused solely on a single mediating variable, this study attempts to systematically explain the relationship between school belonging and mental health by examining both behavioral and cognitive pathways, with the aim of providing a more targeted theoretical basis for mental health interventions in higher education institutions.

Research hypothesis

The relationship between school belonging and mental health among college students

School belonging refers to a student’s sense of being a part of the school community, believing that they are welcomed and respected by others within the school, and that they are valued by others [5]. Mental health refers to a state of well-adjusted functioning at the cognitive, emotional, and behavioral levels, and serves as the foundation for normal life and learning [6]. Mental health is not only a guarantee of personal well-being but also a fundamental element in building a harmonious society. Research indicates that individuals with a high sense of school belonging experience more positive emotions, which contributes to their mental health [7]. The theory of stage-environment fit posits that, as students are at different developmental stages, they have varying developmental needs; therefore, schools should provide learning environments suited to these stages to facilitate smooth individual development [8]. If the environment provided by the school does not align with students’ developmental needs, it will have adverse effects on their motivation, interests, educational values, attitudes, and mental health [9]. Empirical research from 2014 indicates a significant positive correlation between poor school adjustment and the incidence of psychological disorders; that is, individuals with low levels of school belonging consistently exhibit more psychological problems and intense depressive symptoms [10]. Conversely, individuals with high school belonging benefit from personal well-being and mental health, and this supports the formation of adolescent self-identity and group development [11]. Furthermore, longitudinal studies have found that groups with high school belonging not only experience a significant reduction in subjective physical discomfort but also show a marked trend toward alleviation in psychological risk indicators such as hostility, fear, and paranoia [12]. This cross-cultural body of evidence suggests that school belonging in educational settings is not only a protective factor for mental health but also a core mechanism for building cross-cultural adaptability.

In light of this, this study proposes hypothesis H1: school belonging is positively correlated with mental health.

The mediating effect of physical exercise

Physical exercise refers to physical activity of a certain intensity, frequency, and duration undertaken with the aim of improving physical health [13]. School belonging refers to the degree of emotional attachment and identification that college students feel toward the school environment, teacher-student relationships, peer groups, and campus culture. According to self-determination theory, school belonging is one of the three core psychological needs of human beings. When college students have a strong sense of school belonging, it means they feel accepted, respected, and secure on campus. The fulfillment of this need enhances their sense of autonomy and intrinsic motivation, making them more willing to actively participate in physical activities rather than doing so out of external pressure [14]. The stronger the intrinsic motivation, the higher the frequency, consistency, and proactivity of physical exercise. Therefore, the higher the level of school belonging, the more active college students’ physical exercise behavior becomes [15]. Social identity theory posits that individuals tend to consolidate their identity through participation in group activities [16]. When college students view themselves as members of the university community, they are more willing to participate in activities that carry symbolic significance for the campus; university sports meets, college-level competitions, and class activities are the most typical examples of such collective campus behaviors. Consequently, the stronger the school belonging, the more willing students are to reinforce their identity through participation in physical education [17]. Research indicates that this positive emotional connection directly and positively promotes physical exercise participation across four dimensions: motivation, environment, social support, and identity [18]. Furthermore, according to the emotional effects theory of exercise, the emotional experiences generated during physical exercise have an ameliorative and therapeutic effect on depression, anxiety, and stress, and are significantly positively associated with mental health [19]. Specifically, physical exercise can improve mental health by alleviating anxiety and depression and enhancing emotional regulation abilities [20].

In light of this, this study proposes hypothesis H2: physical exercise mediates the relationship between school belonging and mental health.

The mediating effect of a growth mindset

Dweck first introduced the concept of a growth mindset, which refers to an individual’s belief in the malleability of abilities—the view that one’s intelligence and potential are not fixed but can be developed and refined through sustained effort, effective strategies, and feedback [21]. School belonging—defined as the sense of acceptance, respect, and support students feel within the school environment—exhibits a significant positive association with a growth mindset, that is, the belief that abilities can be developed through effort [22]. Research indicates that school belonging indirectly promotes the development of a growth mindset by providing a psychologically safe environment [23]. The U.S. Student Experience Network, based on nationwide data from over 500,000 K-12 students, found that students with higher school belonging scores also scored significantly higher on growth mindset scales. Further international empirical studies confirm that school belonging positively promotes the development of a growth mindset through shared predictive factors and psychological safety mechanisms [24]. Murray et al. analyzed a sample of 2,305 college students and found that enhancing school belonging indirectly reinforces students’ belief that abilities can be developed through effort—that is, a growth mindset [25]. The theory of school belonging posits that when students feel accepted, respected, and supported on campus, their sense of belonging uncertainty decreases, making them more willing to view failure as a controllable opportunity for growth, thereby reinforcing the belief in the malleability of abilities [26]. Furthermore, research indicates that, in terms of mental health, growth mindset interventions reduce participants’ depression, anxiety, and stress, thereby promoting individual mental well-being [27]. The significant improvement in mental health scores among students in the intervention group validates the multiple benefits of growth mindset interventions. This finding aligns with the results of a meta-analysis by Burnette et al. (2013), which indicated a positive relationship between growth mindset interventions and mental health [28].

In light of this, this study proposes hypothesis H3: a growth mindset mediates the relationship between school belonging and mental health.

The chain mediation effect of physical exercise and growth mindset.

Findings from brain science research indicate that the brain is plastic and not static; both physical exercise and learning can promote positive changes in the brain [29]. Yu Tao et al. argue that individuals shape positive cognitions through physical exercise, transform their attribution styles, and enhance their growth mindset by learning and reinforcing cognitive processes [30]. A growth mindset emphasizes viewing challenges as opportunities for development and believing that abilities can be continuously enhanced through effort, learning, and sustained practice. This core concept aligns closely with the psychological resilience, mastery-oriented goals, exercise self-efficacy, and persistence in physical exercise. Physical exercise itself is a process of continuously pushing physical limits and overcoming fatigue and frustration. By guiding individuals to focus on their own progress rather than mere outcomes, and by viewing difficulties and mistakes as necessary stages in skill development, a growth mindset can effectively enhance resilience to setbacks and intrinsic motivation during exercise [31]. The two concepts logically reinforce one another: a growth mindset provides a positive cognitive foundation for physical exercise, while physical exercise offers a practical setting for shaping and strengthening the growth mindset, jointly promoting the individual’s dual development in both physical growth and psychological qualities. He Binbin et al. argue that an individual’s cognitive abilities are malleable; physical exercise is the most important means of promoting physical health, and physical activity, in turn, fosters the development of cognitive abilities [32]. Therefore, a supportive environment for physical exercise is more conducive to the growth and development of a growth mindset [33]. In this study, physical exercise is prioritized over a growth mindset, primarily based on the theoretical logic of behavioral precedence and cognitive internalization. First, from the perspective of embodied cognition theory, an individual’s cognitive development relies on concrete bodily experiences [34]. Through continuous challenges and feedback, physical exercise allows individuals to experience the process of skill enhancement in practice, thereby providing an experiential foundation for the belief in the malleability of abilities. Second, according to self-efficacy theory, successful experiences in physical exercise can significantly enhance an individual’s sense of self-efficacy, and this increased self-efficacy further reinforces the belief in the developability of one’s abilities, thereby promoting the formation of a growth mindset [35]. Third, based on self-perception theory, individuals often infer their internal beliefs from their own behaviors; when individuals consistently engage in physical exercise, they gradually develop a cognitive pattern that recognizes progress through effort [36]. Therefore, from the perspective of the behavior–experience–cognition developmental pathway, physical exercise, as an exogenous behavioral variable, may exert its influence prior to the endogenous cognitive variable of a growth mindset, thereby theoretically supporting a chained pathway structure in which physical exercise precedes the growth mindset.

Based on this, the present study proposes hypothesis H4: physical exercise and the growth mindset together constitute a sequential mediating pathway.

In summary, few studies have simultaneously examined physical exercise and a growth mindset as potential mediators in the relationship between school belonging and college students’ mental health. This study therefore proposes a theory-driven statistical mediation model, shown in Fig. 1, to examine whether physical exercise and growth mindset are associated with the link between school belonging and college students’ mental health. The study tests three hypotheses: (1) school belonging is positively associated with the mental health indicator; (2) physical exercise and growth mindset each show statistical indirect associations; and (3) physical exercise and growth mindset form a sequential statistical mediation pathway.

Research subjects and methods

Participants and sampling

Using convenience sampling, we surveyed 637 college students from 10 universities in Liaoning, Shandong, and Jiangsu provinces in China. The survey utilized the School Belonging Scale, the Mental Health Scale, the Physical Exercise Level Scale, and the Growth Mindset Scale to explore the mediating effect of school belonging on mental health. It should be noted that the sample for this study was drawn exclusively from universities in these three provinces, resulting in limited geographical coverage and certain limitations in the sample’s representativeness, which may affect the external validity of the conclusions. Questionnaires exhibiting any of the following issues were deemed invalid and excluded: (1) inconsistent responses on reverse-scored items; (2) completion time too short (completed in less than 50 s); (3) repetitive or pattern-based responses. Specifically, 21 questionnaires were excluded due to inconsistent responses on reverse-scored items, 11 due to completion time being too short, and 6 due to repetitive or pattern-based responses. Ultimately, a total of 600 valid questionnaires were collected, yielding a response rate of 94.19%. Among these, 278 respondents were male students (46.33%) and 322 were female students (53.67%). Additionally, all 600 respondents, regardless of their exercise levels, participated in physical exercise and demonstrated an understanding of the concepts of school belonging, growth mindset, and mental health.

Participants were recruited from 10 universities in three Chinese provinces. Therefore, the sample had a nested structure, with students nested within universities. However, multilevel modeling was not conducted because the number of level-2 units was relatively small, the sample sizes across universities were uneven, and no university-level variables were collected. Accordingly, the present analysis focused on individual-level associations. The possible influence of institutional-level characteristics should be examined in future studies using larger and more balanced multilevel samples. At the same time, the sample was limited to specific regions in China, and cultural factors influencing social support and cognitive structures were not fully taken into account.

Research tools

School belonging scale

The school belonging scale (2000), revised by Liu Peibing et al., was used to assess college students’ sense of school belonging [37]. The scale consists of 34 items and comprises five dimensions: peer support, personal status recognition, school environment, school integration, and teacher behavior. A 5-point Likert scale was used (1 representing “strongly disagree” and 5 representing “strongly agree”). For example, for the item “My classmates understand me,” “strongly disagree” is scored as 1 and “strongly agree” as 5. Higher scores indicate a stronger sense of school belonging. This scale is widely used to measure college students’ sense of school belonging, and existing research has consistently demonstrated that it possesses good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.949 for internal consistency.

Psychological symptoms scale

This study adopted the Chinese version of the mental health scale adapted by Wang Zhengyu (2006) [38], which uses fewer psychological symptoms as an indicator of mental health status and is widely used in mental health assessments of college students and adults in China. The scale consists of 90 items covering 10 symptom factors: somatization, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, interpersonal relationships, hypersensitivity, depression, hostility, phobias, paranoia, psychoticism, and other factors. A 5-point Likert scale was used (1 representing “severe” and 5 representing “none”). For example, for the item “headache,” “severe” scored 1 point and “none” scored 5 points. Reverse scoring was employed, such that a higher score indicates a lower level of psychological symptoms; therefore, the outcome variable in this study should be interpreted as symptom-based mental health, with higher scores indicating fewer psychological symptoms, rather than as a direct measure of positive mental health or overall well-being. In this study, the scale was verified to possess good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.946 for internal consistency.

Physical exercise level scale

This scale was revised by Liang Deqing et al. (1994) [39]. It assesses the volume of physical exercise by measuring three dimensions—intensity, duration per session, and frequency per week—and demonstrates high reliability and validity. This study used a 5-point Likert scale to calculate physical exercise levels; a higher total score indicates a greater volume of physical exercise. The mean score was used as the analytical variable, and values calculated using the original formula were not employed. Since this is a formative indicator, its internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) is provided for reference only. In this study, the scale was verified to have good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.736 for internal consistency.

Growth mindset scale

The study utilized the growth mindset scale (2018) [40], developed by Dweck and revised by scholar Xiaoyu. The scale consists of 20 items, 10 of which are reverse-scored: items 1, 4, 7, 8, 11, 12, 14, 16, 17, and 20. A 5-point Likert scale was used (1 representing “strongly disagree” and 5 representing “strongly agree”). For example, for the item “My intelligence is fundamentally fixed and cannot be changed much,” “strongly disagree” is scored as 1 and “strongly agree” as 5. After converting the reverse-scored items to forward scoring, a higher item score indicates that the individual is more inclined to believe that abilities are malleable and can be developed. This scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity in previous studies; in this study, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.917 for internal consistency.

Data statistics and analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 29.0 and the Process plugin, including tests of internal consistency, Pearson correlation analysis, regression analysis, and bias-corrected Bootstrap (bias-corrected percentile bootstrap) tests for the school belonging scale, mental health scale, physical exercise scale, and growth mindset scale. This study employed the bias-corrected Bootstrap method to test for mediating effects, with 5,000 bootstrap repetitions and a 95% confidence interval. A mediating effect was considered statistically significant if the confidence interval did not include zero.

Research results

Common method bias test

To prevent common method bias from influencing the research results, necessary controls were implemented during data collection, such as requiring participants to respond anonymously and using reverse-scoring for certain items. The Harman one-way test was employed to assess common method bias. The results showed that there were nine factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the first factor explained 35.4% of the variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40% (Podsakoff et al., 2003). This indicates that there is no severe common method bias in the data. Common method bias was not the primary concern in this study. Although the Harman one-factor test was employed, this method has limitations. To avoid the influence of a single data source on dimensional correlations, this study adopted the common latent factor (CLF) technique from confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Using AMOS 29, the model fit of the one-factor model was compared with that of the hypothesized four-factor model based on four constructs. The results indicate that the fit indices of the four-factor model are significantly superior to those of the single-factor model, suggesting that the constructs in this study possess discriminant validity, which indirectly reduces the likelihood that the results are dominated by a single methodological factor. While the results suggest that there may be no dominant common method bias in the data, such conclusions should still be interpreted with caution in the context of the study design.

Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis of variables

An independent samples t-test was used to analyze differences in school belonging, mental health, physical exercise, and growth mindset between genders. The results showed that there were no significant differences between male and female college students in core variables such as school belonging, mental health, physical exercise, and growth mindset (all p > .05). Furthermore, calculations of Cohen’s d revealed that the effect sizes for all variables ranged between 0.02 and 0.08. These results indicate that the influence of gender on the variables in this study is relatively limited. Therefore, gender need not be treated as a key grouping variable in the subsequent chain mediation analysis; however, it may be included in the model as a control variable as needed to enhance the robustness of the model estimates. To some extent, these findings support the hypothesis of homogeneity in the sample across the gender dimension, which helps reduce the interference of potential confounding variables on the estimates of the chain mediation model (see Table 1).

A Pearson correlation analysis was conducted on the variables. The results in Table 3 show that the mean scores for all variables were generally in the upper-middle range, with relatively small standard deviations, indicating that the distribution of the sample scores was relatively concentrated. This result may be related to the fact that the sample was drawn from a population of college students, a group that typically exhibits relatively stable psychological and behavioral characteristics within an academic setting. Furthermore, the small standard deviation for the mental health variable may reflect a relatively consistent level of mental health across the sample. The correlation analysis results indicate that there are significant positive correlations between school belonging, physical exercise, growth mindset, and mental health (r = .799–0.880, p < .001). Although the correlation coefficients are generally high, given that the variables involved in this study all fall within the category of positive psychological resources and are theoretically closely linked, issues such as construct overlap and common method variance may exist. To avoid these issues, this study conducted confirmatory factor analysis and a common method bias test. The common method bias test yielded a value of 35.4%, which is below the critical threshold of 40%. Therefore, despite the high correlations, the different variables still reflect distinct psychological constructs. Consequently, the results of this study possess a certain degree of structural validity. The findings indicate that higher levels of school belonging, physical exercise, and growth mindset are associated with higher levels of mental health, thereby confirming Hypothesis H1.

Structural equation modeling analysis

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted using AMOS 29.0 software to compare one-factor, two-factor, three-factor, and four-factor models, ensuring the robustness of the results. To test the discriminant validity among the latent variables, multiple competing models were compared. The results showed that the four-factor model fit the data better than the other models, with χ²/df = 2.310, CFI = 0.937, TLI = 0.928, RMSEA = 0.047, and SRMR = 0.042 (see Table 4). In contrast, the single-factor model exhibited a lower fit (CFI = 0.711), while the two-factor and three-factor models showed some improvement but still fell short of the ideal level. These results indicate that the four constructs in this study possess good structural distinctiveness.

To further examine common method bias, an analysis was conducted using the Common Latent Factor (CLF) approach. A common method factor was introduced into the original four-factor model, with loadings assigned to all measurement items. The results showed that model fit improved slightly after adding the CLF (χ²/df = 2.050, CFI = 0.948, TLI = 0.936, RMSEA = 0.054, SRMR = 0.042), but the change was small compared to the original model (ΔCFI = 0.006 < 0.01). Furthermore, a comparison of the standardized factor loadings revealed that the changes in loadings for each measurement item after adding the CLF were all less than 0.05. Therefore, it can be concluded that common method bias had a minimal impact on the results of this study.

Based on the validation of the above model, its structure was found to be reasonably sound. This study then proceeded to calculate the composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE) using the standardized factor loadings output by AMOS; the AMOS results were organized, the square root of AVE was calculated, and a discriminant validity matrix was constructed. As shown in Table 5, the CR values for each latent variable range from 0.880 to 0.930, all exceeding the recommended standard of 0.7; the AVE values range from 0.580 to 0.650, all exceeding the threshold of 0.5, indicating that the measurement variables possess good internal consistency and convergent validity.

Prior to conducting the mediation analysis, we examined potential multicollinearity issues using SPSS to perform multiple regression analysis on the independent variables. The independent variables in this study include school belonging, physical exercise, and growth mindset. The variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance were calculated. Discussion

To explore the intrinsic relationship between school belonging and college students’ mental health, this study constructed and tested a chained mediation model of the relationship between school belonging and mental health by introducing two mediating variables: physical exercise and growth mindset. The findings reveal the intrinsic relationship between school belonging and college students’ mental health, while also providing empirical evidence for improving college students’ mental health.Significant positive correlations were found among the variables (r = .799–0.880, p < .001). Although the correlation coefficients were generally high, the variables examined in this study—school belonging, physical exercise, and growth mindset—can all be conceptualized as positive psychological resources. Given their theoretical proximity and conceptual interrelatedness, a relatively high level of correlation among these variables is, to some extent, reasonable. The high correlation coefficients among the variables in this study may reflect the strong interdependence among positive psychological resources. School belonging, physical exercise, and growth mindset are all theoretically important factors that promote individual adaptation and development; thus, their strong interrelationships are reasonable. It is worth noting that confirmatory factor analysis conducted in this study (see Table 4) revealed good discriminant validity among the variables, indicating that despite high correlations, different variables still reflect distinct psychological constructs. Therefore, the results of this study possess a certain degree of structural validity. However, future research should further validate the relationships among these variables through multi-method measurements or longitudinal designs.

The relationship between school belonging and college students’ mental health

The results of this study indicate that a significant positive correlation exists between school belonging and college students’ mental health (r = .877, p < .01). Furthermore, after controlling for mediating variables, the standardized regression coefficient for the effect of school belonging on mental health remained significant (β = 0.386, p < .001), consistent with previous research [8–11]. Recent research further confirms that there is a stable association between school belonging and mental health, and that school belonging has a significant direct positive association with college students’ mental health. School belonging refers to students’ sense of identity as members of the school community, during which they experience acceptance, respect, and a sense of self-worth from others [4].Previous research has found that individuals with a strong sense of school belonging typically experience more positive emotions, which is positively associated with mental health [7].Analysis of other research data indicates that school belonging is significantly positively correlated with students’ positive emotions and has a significant indirect effect on life satisfaction. School belonging often leads to better academic performance, improved school adjustment, and higher levels of mental health [12]. At the same time, individuals with a strong sense of school belonging are more likely to experience inner peace, maintain good mental health, and contribute to the development of adolescents’ self-identity and group cohesion [11]. A 16-wave longitudinal study further confirmed that school belonging during adolescence significantly predicts mental health in early adulthood (ages 19–28); higher levels of belonging are associated with lower levels of depression, anxiety, and stress later on, highlighting the important value of school belonging as a long-term protective factor [12]. The theory of stage-environment fit posits that individuals have distinct developmental needs at different stages of life, and schools should provide learning environments tailored to these needs to support smooth individual development [8]. Conversely, when the school environment fails to meet students’ developmental needs, it may have adverse effects on their motivation, interests, educational values, attitudes, and mental health [9].

The mediating effect of physical exercise on the relationship between school belonging and mental health

This study shows that physical exercise has a significant indirect effect on the relationship between school belonging and mental health (β = 0.111, 95% CI does not include 0), indicating that physical exercise plays a statistically significant mediating role in this relationship. This finding suggests that, in the sample of this study, individuals with a higher sense of school belonging tend to engage in higher levels of physical exercise, and physical exercise is further associated with higher levels of mental health, consistent with previous research findings [13–17]. College students with strong school belonging exhibit greater acceptance of and attachment to the overall campus environment and are more willing to visit campus sports spaces such as athletic fields, gymnasiums, and fitness centers [18]. They also exhibit higher levels of acceptance and satisfaction with school physical education courses, sports activities, and sports culture. They are more easily influenced by the campus sports atmosphere, leading to the formation of regular exercise habits. When college students possess a strong sense of school belonging, it implies that they feel accepted, respected, and safe on campus. The fulfillment of these needs helps enhance their sense of autonomy and intrinsic motivation, making them more inclined to actively participate in physical activities. The stronger the intrinsic motivation, the higher the frequency, consistency, and proactivity of physical exercise [14]. Therefore, school belonging is positively associated with an individual’s physical exercise behavior [15].Research indicates that the opportunities for physical activity provided by schools and the quality of the environment are directly linked to students’ sense of belonging and are associated with their willingness to continue participating in physical exercise [18]. The exercise-affect theory posits that physical exercise helps alleviate negative emotions, thereby positively promoting mental health.

The mediating effect of a growth mindset on school belonging and mental health

The results of this study indicate that a growth mindset also exerts a significant mediating effect between school belonging and mental health (β = 0.160, 95% CI does not include 0). This suggests that individuals with a higher sense of school belonging tend to exhibit higher levels of a growth mindset, and that a growth mindset is associated with better mental health. Individuals with this mindset typically demonstrate greater perseverance and a willingness to take on challenges [27]. Dweck notes that individuals with a growth mindset tend to adopt positive attitudes and coping strategies when facing adversity, viewing setbacks as temporary and as opportunities to further develop their abilities; their cognitive processes are more oriented toward seeking change and solving problems [21]. This implies that a growth mindset encourages individuals to employ positive coping strategies, such as problem-solving and seeking help, thereby reducing the occurrence of subsequent psychological issues and maintaining a higher level of mental health. Research indicates that when facing psychological distress, individuals with a high growth mindset may, due to their more positive views and interpretations of problems, mitigate the negative emotional impact of adversity, experience more positive emotions, and consequently find more effective pathways and methods to resolve psychological distress. This helps them avoid the accumulation of difficulties leading to further psychological issues, thereby maintaining a healthier psychological state. The theory of school belonging posits that when students feel accepted, respected, and supported on campus, their sense of belonging uncertainty decreases, and they are more likely to view failure as a controllable opportunity for growth. In other words, individuals with a growth mindset are more likely to adopt positive coping strategies when facing challenges, thereby reducing the occurrence of psychological problems [26].A study of 2,305 college students found that school belonging was significantly positively correlated with a growth mindset and academic performance. Specifically, for students with a stronger sense of school belonging, the positive association between a growth mindset and GPA was even stronger.Conversely, for students with a lower sense of belonging, this association is weaker [25]. This suggests that a sense of belonging provides a conducive environment for the development of a growth mindset; the positive effects of a growth mindset can only be fully realized when individuals feel accepted and supported.

The chain mediation effect of physical exercise and a growth mindset

This study reveals a significant chain mediation pathway between physical exercise and a growth mindset (β = 0.057, 95% CI does not include 0). These findings suggest that school belonging is not only associated with physical exercise and a growth mindset, respectively, but may also be linked to mental health through the sequential pathway of physical exercise to growth mindset. Neuroscience suggests that an individual’s cognitive abilities are malleable, and physical exercise can facilitate this developmental process. This is because physical exercise helps individuals actively respond to challenges, triggers positive behavioral changes, and drives shifts in thinking patterns. At the same time, through sustained participation in physical exercise, an individual’s growth mindset can be continuously reinforced [29]. Furthermore, research has found a positive correlation between an individual’s exercise behavior and growth mindset; that is, individuals with higher levels of physical exercise exhibit more pronounced growth mindset patterns. A synthesis of existing research on physical exercise and growth mindset reveals a positive association between the two [32], suggesting that an individual’s growth mindset may strengthen as the volume of physical exercise increases, and that the shift in thinking patterns is, to some extent, driven by behavioral changes. Yu Tao argues that physical exercise can also boost an individual’s self-confidence and alter their attribution style. When individuals are accustomed to attributing failure to external factors, they are often better able to alleviate psychological stress and improve anxiety levels [30]. In contrast, individuals with a growth mindset are more likely to believe they can learn and grow from failure and rarely attribute the cause to their own abilities. This suggests that students with a growth mindset experience relatively lower levels of anxiety. Therefore, physical exercise promotes the formation of a growth mindset by facilitating positive cognitive transformation, thereby contributing to improved mental health [32].

Conclusion

School belonging was positively associated with the mental health indicator among college students. Physical exercise and growth mindset showed statistically significant indirect associations, both independently and through a sequential pathway. These findings should be interpreted as associations based on cross-sectional data. They do not establish temporal ordering or causal mediation, and the outcome mainly reflects fewer psychological symptoms rather than positive mental health.

Limitations, future prospects, and implications for practice

This study examines the relationship between school belonging and college students’ mental health. The construction of a chained mediation model reveals the underlying link between school belonging and college students’ mental health, offering new perspectives and insights. It holds significant theoretical and practical implications for improving college students’ mental health. However, this study has several limitations:

First, this study selected undergraduate students from certain universities in Liaoning, Shandong, and Jiangsu provinces as its sample; it did not include students from universities in other regions, students majoring in other fields, or other groups. Therefore, future research should broaden its scope and select a more diverse sample to assess the applicability of the study’s findings.In addition, the data in this study exhibit a certain hierarchical structure, with students nested within different universities; however, this study did not employ multilevel modeling methods to account for differences at the institutional level. Future research could further analyze the impact of different institutional environments on the relationships between variables using multilevel models.Although this study focuses on the relationships among four variables—school belonging, mental health, physical exercise, and growth mindset—there may still be uncontrolled underlying variables. For example, cultural values and social support may have significant associations with the pathways to mental health, and further cross-cultural comparative studies could be conducted in the future.This study may not have adequately accounted for the potential influence of disciplinary differences in the sample. There are significant differences across disciplines in terms of academic pressure, educational models, employment expectations, and social environments, all of which may indirectly affect mental health. Future research could conduct a stratified analysis by disciplinary background or include these factors as covariates to enhance the generalizability of the findings.Second, regarding the order of the causal chain. Although a growth mindset is often viewed as a motivational precursor to behavioral change, recent theoretical developments suggest that this sequence is not the only possibility. This study employs a cross-sectional design, with all variables measured at the same point in time; therefore, it is not possible to make empirical inferences about the direction of causality. It is important to note that reverse causality may exist; for example, individuals with higher levels of mental health may be more likely to engage in physical exercise, feel a greater sense of belonging, and hold a growth mindset. Since cross-sectional data cannot rule out any of the alternative models mentioned above, the analysis should not be interpreted as confirmation of a specific causal chain but rather as a preliminary test of the consistency of theoretical assumptions. Future studies should further clarify the directionality of relationships among variables through cross-lagged panel designs, random-intercept cross-lagged panel models, or experimental manipulation.Third, because this study employs a cross-sectional design, although inferences regarding relationships between variables were made based on theoretical models, strict causal relationships cannot be established. In particular, regarding the relationship among physical exercise, growth mindset, and mental health, bidirectional effects or potential reverse causal mechanisms may exist between the variables. Therefore, the findings of this study reflect associations rather than causality. Future studies may consider conducting longitudinal or experimental follow-up studies to strengthen causal conclusions.Finally, regarding the limitations of the scale. This study employed the Self-Rating Scale for Symptoms (SCL-90) as an assessment tool for college students’ mental health. It should be noted that the SCL-90 is essentially a symptom inventory that primarily measures the degree of psychological distress in dimensions such as somatization, anxiety, and depression. Therefore, the level of mental health reflected in this study essentially represents a low-symptom state with fewer psychological symptoms and cannot be equated with the full picture of positive mental health. Future research is recommended to incorporate positive mental health scales, such as the Mental Health Continuum Scale (MHC-SF) or the Well-Being Scale, to achieve a more comprehensive and balanced assessment of mental health.

Implications for practice

First, to enhance students’ school belonging, universities should strengthen students’ emotional connections by optimizing the campus environment and interpersonal support systems. For example, by fostering supportive teacher-student relationships, promoting peer interaction, organizing group activities, and cultivating an inclusive campus culture, universities can enhance students’ sense of acceptance and identity, thereby increasing their level of school belonging.Second, at the behavioral intervention level, universities should actively promote the regular practice of physical exercise. By improving campus sports facilities, enriching the variety of physical education courses and extracurricular activities, and organizing diverse group sports events, universities can guide students toward developing consistent exercise habits, thereby improving their emotional well-being and alleviating psychological stress through physical activity.Finally, at the cognitive intervention level, universities can guide students toward developing a growth mindset through coursework or mental health education. For example, by conducting growth mindset training, resilience education, and cognitive restructuring exercises, students can learn to view difficulties and challenges with a more positive attitude, thereby enhancing their psychological resilience.
