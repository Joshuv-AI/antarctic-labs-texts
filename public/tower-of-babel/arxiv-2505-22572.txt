Fusion Steering: Prompt-Specific Activation Control
Waldemar Chang, Dr. Alhassan Yasin

Abstract
We present Fusion Steering , an activation steering methodology that improves factual accuracy in large language models (LLMs) for question-answering (QA) tasks. This approach introduces flexible steering configurations, including full-layer steering and segmented steering . Unlike traditional methods constrained to single-layer or fixed-layer operations, Fusion Steering employs dynamic injection of prompt-specific activation deltas across all transformer layers. These activation deltas are derived from reference completions that combine the ground-truth answer with a model-generated explanation to facilitate semantically enriched, example-specific steering. The injection weights are optimized per prompt using Optuna, targeting a joint objective that balances token overlap (factual alignment) and perplexity (fluency proxy). Evaluation employs a composite score integrating token overlap and LLM-graded quality, encompassing factual accuracy, coherence, and relevance. Empirical results on 260 SimpleQA prompts (selected from 500 where the baseline failed) showcase the efficacy of segmented steering . Using Gemma-2-2B-IT with 8-bit quantization, segmented steering achieves an accuracy of 25.4% (outputs scoring ≥ 0.6 \geq 0.6 ), outperforming the baseline at 3.5% and full-layer steering at 16.2%. Under the stricter SimpleQA rubric, segmented steering boosts fully correct responses from 0.0% to 13.1%. These findings highlight the strengths of segmented, dynamic intervention strategies and the promise of per-prompt, full-network activation control. Fusion Steering is also amenable to sparse representations, such as Neuronpedia or sparse crosscoders, suggesting a promising direction for interpretable and scalable activation-level control in LLMs.

2.2
Capturing reference activations

We capture reference activations from Gemma-2-2B-IT using an enriched prompt that combines the question, the ground-truth answer, and an explanation generated by the model after being fed the question and the ground truth:

Question:
[prompt]

Answer and Explanation:
[ground truth + explanation]

This entire input sequence is tokenized and fed to the model. We then extract neural activations across all transformer layers, but only from token positions corresponding to the ground-truth answer and explanation. These activations are averaged at each layer to produce a single robust reference activation vector per layer. Formally, let
h_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the activation vector for layer
l
, calculated as:

h_{l}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}a_{l,t}

(1)

where
a_{l,t}
denotes the activation at layer
l
for the token position
t
, and
\mathcal{T}
denotes the set of token positions aligned to the ground-truth answer and explanation within the prompt.

2.3
Multi-layer patching and steering

At inference time, when presented only with the question (excluding the ground truth answer and explanation), we inject the previously captured reference activations across transformer layers using an additive steering mechanism. This steering dynamically nudges the internal representations toward the mean representation seen during reference activation capture.

Formally, let
h_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the reference activation vector at layer
l
, and let
\mu_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the mean of the activations at layer
l
across all token positions during inference. We compute a global shift vector:

\Delta_{l}=\gamma(h_{l}-\mu_{l})

(2)

This shift is added uniformly to each token’s activation in the layer:

a_{l,t}^{\text{steered}}=a_{l,t}^{\text{original}}+\Delta_{l}

(3)

Finally, we blend the steered and original activations using an interpolation parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
, which we refer to as the
fusion weight
:

a_{l,t}^{\text{final}}=\alpha a_{l,t}^{\text{steered}}+(1-\alpha)a_{l,t}^{\text{original}}

(4)

Here,
\gamma
modulates the intensity of the directional shift, and
\alpha
controls the extent of blending—the fusion step in our Fusion Steering method—between steered and original activations.

We explore two distinct implementations of this mechanism. In
full-layer steering
, reference activations are injected uniformly across all transformer layers using a single set of
\alpha
and
\gamma
parameters. In contrast,
segmented steering
divides the model into early, middle, and late layer groups, each steered using separately optimized
\alpha
and
\gamma
values. This allows for finer-grained control over how different representational stages of the model are influenced. We aim to understand the trade-offs between globally uniform steering and more targeted, segment-aware modulation of internal activations.

2.4
Prompt-specific parameter optimization

To ensure optimal parameter selection tailored to each input question, we employ Optuna-based hyperparameter optimization
akiba2019optunanextgenerationhyperparameteroptimization
. For each prompt-steering configuration, we ran five Optuna trials and selected the configuration with the highest composite score. Parameters are tuned separately for each prompt using a composite objective that captures both factual accuracy and linguistic fluency:

\text{Optimization Metric}=\lambda\cdot\text{Token Overlap}-(1-\lambda)\cdot\text{Perplexity Normed}

(5)

Here,
\lambda\in[0,1]
governs the trade-off between factual alignment and fluency. The token overlap score captures n-gram-level agreement with the reference answer, while perplexity—measured via GPT-2
radford2019gpt2
—estimates generation fluency. Since lower perplexity indicates higher fluency, its normalized value is subtracted so that maximizing the objective favors both alignment and coherence.

We fix
\lambda=0.7
to moderately prioritize factual alignment over fluency. While we did not perform an exhaustive sweep of
\lambda
, this setting reflects a deliberate tradeoff: token overlap serves as a reliable optimization signal because SimpleQA ground-truths consist primarily of concise factual phrases. This setting was chosen heuristically as a stable midpoint between full token overlap weighting (
\lambda=1.0
) and equal balance (
\lambda=0.5
), based on pilot trials. More importantly, this design enables efficient per-prompt tuning, as both token overlap and perplexity can be computed rapidly. During optimization, the search space for both the fusion weight
\alpha
and steering strength
\gamma
is bounded to
[0.01,1.0]
, ensuring sufficient expressiveness while avoiding degenerate extremes, and was found to offer stable convergence during pilot experiments.

In the
full-layer steering
setup, the same
\alpha
and
\gamma
values are applied uniformly across all transformer layers for a given prompt. In contrast,
segmented steering
partitions the model into early, middle, and late layer groups—defined as layers 0–8, 9–17, and 18–25 respectively, using 0-based indexing—with each group steered using its own optimized parameters. This finer-grained design allows more precise control over different stages of the model’s internal representation. While we apply this partitioning to Gemma-2-2B-IT, our choice is informed by prior work on LLaMA-style architectures showing that adjacent layers often share functional roles and exhibit high intra-group activation similarity
sun2025transformerlayerspainters
. These findings suggest that transformer layers naturally cluster into coherent groups, supporting layer-wise modulation at group granularity even across different decoder-only architectures.

2.5
Deterministic inference via greedy decoding

To ensure consistency and eliminate variance due to sampling, all generations whether baseline, full-layer steering, or segmented steering—are produced using greedy decoding. At each timestep, the model deterministically selects the highest-probability token by setting
temperature = 0.0
; other sampling parameters (e.g.,
top-p
) are left at default values and have no effect under this setting.

This inference mode guarantees a single, reproducible output for each prompt-steering configuration pair, allowing for precise attribution of differences in factuality or fluency to the effects of steering rather than stochastic variation. It also simplifies hyperparameter optimization by ensuring stable and reproducible metrics, such as token overlap and perplexity, for evaluating candidate configurations.

While greedy decoding may reduce output diversity, our primary focus is on factual correctness and reliability, both of which benefit from deterministic evaluation. Future work may extend this setup to sample-based decoding to assess the robustness and adaptability of activation-level steering under uncertainty.

2.6
Evaluation metrics

2.6.1
Composite metric (token overlap and 5-point LLM evaluation)

To comprehensively quantify the effectiveness of our steering methods, we compute a combined evaluation score that averages token overlap with a rubric-based LLM grade:

\text{Evaluation Metric}=0.5\cdot\text{Token Overlap}+0.5\cdot\left(\frac{\text{LLM Grade}}{5}\right)

(6)

The LLM grade (scaled 1–5) is generated by Mistral Small 3.1
mistral2024small
, using a scoring rubric that evaluates the correctness, completeness, and relevance of generated responses (Appendix
B
). A numerical 1-5 rubric is used instead of letter grades to enable direct normalization and smooth integration into our continuous evaluation metric. Token overlap provides a granular, n-gram-level alignment check against concise ground-truth answers, serving as a lightweight but precise correctness proxy. By combining these complementary perspectives, our evaluation metric captures both surface-level lexical fidelity and deeper semantic accuracy, enabling more graded analysis of factual alignment even when predictions fall short of exact correctness.

2.6.2
SimpleQA rubric grading

To measure improvement relative to the original failure cases, we also apply a stricter SimpleQA rubric (Appendix
A
) using the initial instance of Mistral Small 3.1, which was used to identify the 260
INCORRECT
baseline completions. Unlike the 1–5 rubric, which enables finer-grained differentiation in output quality, including partial correctness and relevance, the SimpleQA rubric enforces a categorical distinction between fully correct, incorrect, and unattempted answers. While the composite metric captures incremental improvements in factual alignment and response quality, the SimpleQA rubric serves as a high-precision filter for absolute correctness. This combination allows us to assess steering impact both in terms of subtle semantic gains and strict correctness, offering complementary perspectives on factual performance.

Computational resources

All experiments, including per-prompt hyperparameter tuning, activation extraction, and steering inference, were conducted on a single NVIDIA T4 GPU, representative of commonly available, resource-constrained environments. To reduce memory consumption and accelerate computation, we employed 8-bit quantization
nagel2021whitepaperneuralnetwork
via the HuggingFace
bitsandbytes
integration, enabling over 2,600 generations across tuning and evaluation phases without exceeding hardware limitations. To further ensure computational efficiency, all hyperparameter optimization was performed using Optuna
akiba2019optunanextgenerationhyperparameteroptimization
, leveraging efficient search algorithms to minimize optimization overhead.

This setup highlights the lightweight and scalable nature of Fusion Steering: our method operates entirely at inference time, requires no model fine-tuning or retraining, and performs effectively on modest hardware. Despite limited resources, we observe substantial gains in factual accuracy, demonstrating that internal activation-level intervention can be both efficient and impactful. The approach is fully reproducible, with open-source code and minimal system requirements, making it well-suited for adoption in resource-limited research contexts and rapid prototyping workflows.

5
Limitations and future directions

While Fusion Steering demonstrates promising improvements in factual accuracy, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the method currently operates under greedy decoding (
temperature = 0.0
), which, while ensuring deterministic outputs for controlled evaluation, may exacerbate brittleness and fluency degradation in edge cases. Second, our steering signals are derived from reference activations that include both ground-truth answers and explanations; this conditioning may implicitly encourage overly long or unnatural completions when explanation tokens dominate the activation space.

However, in practical deployments, such enriched reference prompts are often unavailable or incomplete. The requirement of precise ground-truth answers and detailed explanations limits the scalability of Fusion Steering in low-resource or real-time environments where curated supervision is rare. This reliance also poses challenges for domains involving open-ended or multi-hop reasoning, where ground-truths may be ambiguous or unstructured.

Additionally, the method is evaluated only on a subset of 260 incorrect prompts from the SimpleQA benchmark, limiting generalizability to broader QA or open-domain generation settings. The optimization process is prompt-specific, requiring per-instance hyperparameter tuning, which poses computational challenges in high-throughput or streaming scenarios. We also do not explore alternate decoding strategies (e.g., beam search, sampling) or assess robustness to adversarial or noisy activations.

These limitations motivate future work on plug-and-play steering mechanisms that do not require full reference completions. One promising direction is to integrate Fusion Steering with emerging neuron-level semantic representations, such as those produced by sparse autoencoders (e.g., Neuronpedia)
paulo2024automaticallyinterpretingmillionsfeatures
or crosscoder-based alignment methods
crosscoders2024
, which identify high-level concepts within specific units. Rather than injecting raw activations derived from entire answer–explanation sequences, future systems could extract interpretable features directly from the input prompt, then activate neuron subsets that correspond to relevant semantic regions of activation space. If the geometry of concept space becomes reliably mapped, where discrete features are tied to identifiable neuron clusters, then prompts could be enriched by embedding structured encodings of these features alongside the input. This would shift steering from recall-based intervention to a feature-driven, concept-conditioned mechanism, enabling scalable and interpretable factual alignment even in open-domain or under-specified generation settings.

Recent work provides encouraging theoretical and empirical foundations for this direction. The “Geometry of Concepts” study
Li_2025
demonstrates that sparse autoencoders produce structured representations, where related concepts cluster spatially in activation space, forming modular and hierarchical geometric patterns such as parallelograms and trapezoids. Similarly, Anthropic’s Scaling Monosemanticity project
scaling_monosemanticity2024
shows that sparse autoencoders can extract interpretable features from large language models, with semantically similar concepts clustering in localized neuron groupings across modalities and languages. Sparse crosscoders
crosscoders2024
extend this approach by analyzing features distributed across layers, revealing shared structures and potentially simplifying circuit analysis through cross-layer superposition. These advances suggest that future variants of Fusion Steering could selectively activate semantically-relevant feature neighborhoods instead of full reference traces, enabling more interpretable and concept-conditioned factual intervention, with potential trade-offs between transparency and computational overhead.

6
Conclusion

We introduced Fusion Steering, a novel activation-level intervention technique designed to improve factual accuracy in large language models for open-ended question answering. Our method combines enriched reference activations, multi-layer interpolated activation patching and additive steering, and prompt- and layer-specific hyperparameter tuning to guide model behavior at inference time. Unlike prior approaches targeting tasks with clearly defined linear directions, Fusion Steering is tailored for discrete, knowledge-intensive QA, where steering must guide the model toward specific factual entities rather than general stylistic shifts. It enables semantically grounded guidance without modifying model weights or requiring fine-tuning. Empirical results on 260 failed SimpleQA prompts show that segmented steering outperforms both baseline and full-layer methods, achieving over 7-fold accuracy gains under the composite metric, and an absolute improvement from 0.0% to 13.1% under the SimpleQA rubric. These results suggest that prompt-specific, interpretable activation interventions offer a promising direction for enhancing factual alignment in language models. Future work may explore how Fusion Steering integrates with neuron-level interpretability frameworks to enable even more targeted, concept-conditioned interventions.

Appendix B
LLM-based quality scoring rubric

To evaluate the quality of model outputs after steering, we used a second rubric-driven prompt with the Mistral-small model. This rubric assigns a 1–5 score based on correctness, completeness, and relevance, and also records hallucination flags and short justifications. The full prompt used is shown below:

You are a strict but fair evaluator.
Evaluate the generated answer for correctness, relevance, and completeness.

Question: {question}
Generated Answer: {generated_text}
Ground Truth Answer: {ground_truth}

Give a score from 1 to 5 based on the following:
- 5 = Fully correct, complete, and relevant.
- 4 = Mostly correct, with minor inaccuracies or missing details.
- 3 = Partially correct, has significant omissions or minor errors.
- 2 = Mostly incorrect or irrelevant, with small fragments that match.
- 1 = Totally wrong, irrelevant, or hallucinated.

Also specify:
- Hallucination (Y/N): Did the answer include fabricated information not found in the
ground truth?
- Notes: Brief justification.
