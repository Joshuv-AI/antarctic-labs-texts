Multimodal Characterization of Cortical Neuron Response to Permanent Magnetic Field Induced Nanomagnetic Force Maps
Authors: Connor
L. Beck, Andrew M. Kirby, Samuel Roberts, Anja Kunze
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Nanomagnetic forces deliver precise mechanical cues to biological systems through the remote pulling of magnetic nanoparticles under a permanent magnetic field. Cortical neurons respond to nanomagnetic forces with cytosolic calcium influx and event rate shifts. However, the underlying consequences of nanomagnetic force modulation on cortical neurons remain to be elucidated. Here, we integrate electrophysiological and optical recording modalities with nanomagnetic forces to characterize the in vitro functional response to mechanical cues. Neurons exposed to chitosan functionalized magnetic nanoparticles for 24 h and then exposed to magnetic fields capable of generating forces of 2–160 pN present elevated cytosolic calcium in neurons and a time-dynamic electrophysiological spike rate and magnitude response. Extracellular recordings with microelectrode arrays revealed a 2–8 pN force-specific increase in electrophysiological spiking with a trend in reduced activity following 2 min of continuous force exposure. Nanomagnetic forces in the 16–160 pN range produced increased electrophysiological activity and remained excited for up to 4 h under continuous stimulation before silencing. Furthermore, the neuronal response to nanomagnetic forces at 16–160 pN can be electrophysiologically mediated without calcium influx by altering the magnetic nanoparticle-neuron interactions. These results demonstrate that low pN nanomagnetic forces mediate neuronal function and suggest that magnetic nanoparticle interactions and force magnitudes can be harnessed to provoke different responses in cortical neurons.

Introduction

Mechanotransduction enables cellular responses to environmental forces and pressures across biological substrates. Compounding evidence indicates that neurons in the central nervous system exhibit mechanosensitivity,1 permitting forces to mediate neuronal function.2−4 In the perspective of neuronal firing, membrane deformation can evoke action potentials5 or alter neuronal excitability6 through mechanosensitive (MS) ion channels7 or downstream signaling cascades.8 Mechanically induced deformations have indicated that the neuronal response to mechanical stimuli depends on the rate of deformation,6 the subcellular location,9 and the magnitude of force.9,10 However, the scope and biophysical mechanisms of neuronal responses to force remain unclear. Given the potential for noninvasive mechanical neuromodulation technologies such as focused ultrasound,11,12 developing interfaces for generating and characterizing forces in neurons is critical to elucidate the potential for mechanical neuromodulation.

Nanotransducers show significant potential as a remote mechanism for mechanical neuromodulation through energy up-conversion.13 Force-generating nanotransducers use primary energy sources imperceptible to biological organisms and convert this energy to exert a mechanical force on the surrounding environment.14 For example, photoacoustic nanotransducers upconvert near-infrared light to generate strong acoustic waves capable of provoking calcium transients, indicative of neuronal activity.15 Optical tweezers enable mechanical probing by low-power laser manipulation of nanoparticles, which can generate low pN forces capable of evoking neuronal activity responses.16,17 Alternatively, forces can be generated by leveraging magnetic field interactions to manipulate magnetic nanotransducers with pN precision.18−20 Torque-generating magnetic nanodiscs apply rotational mechanical force on neuronal membranes under low-frequency alternating magnetic fields to modulate activity.21−23 Vortex generated torque is driven by the magnetization vector of the magnetic nanodisc, which is determined by the intrinsic geometry and the orientation of the external field. Hence, alternating magnetic fields are essential for delivering continuous forces by periodically altering the magnetization vector’s direction.24 Apart from the rotational control, magnetic fields can produce force along the magnetic gradient. A magnetic nanoparticle (MNP) residing in a magnetic field gradient will experience a magnetic force acting on its magnetic moment, which produces a nanomagnetic force on the surrounding environment.25 To employ a constant nanomagnetic force, a permanent magnetic field allows for the manipulation of magnetic nanoparticles without the use of alternating magnetic fields, which is beneficial when investigating electrically and temperature sensitive biologics like neurons.26

The remote access of MNPs enabled mechanical modulation of both calcium dynamics and neuronal spiking across networks. Surface-functionalized MNPs can be up-taken by neurons through endocytic mechanisms to provide cytosolic access to generate intracellular mechanical cues. Under external magnetic field application, 0.1–1 nN nanomagnetic forces drive slow cytosolic calcium influx and calcium event rate modulation in dissociated cortical neurons.27 Evidence indicated that nanoparticle internalization could mediate the neuronal response to nanomagnetic forces, as internalized particles drove less calcium influx than external particles.27 Further studies have shown that neuron-internalized MNPs can direct various cell responses such as filopodia generation,28,29 cell polarization,30−33 or vesicle trafficking.34 Such interactions suggest cytosolic access for nanomagnetic force enables the triggering of functional responses. Below 100 pN forces, little work has observed functional shifts in response to MNP-mediated forces. De Vincentiis and colleagues showed a reduction in somatic calcium in developing neurons during MNP-guided stretch growth.35 In functionally developed networks, cultured midbrain neurons exhibited reduced transient calcium event rates under continuous nanomagnetic forces,36 suggesting the neuronal response may be suppressed under prolonged force exposure. Similar forces have been shown to mediate voltage gated channel expression,10 suggesting intracellular signaling cascades play a fundamental role in the neuronal response. Altogether, these findings indicate that magnetic nanotransducers can act as a useful method to investigate the biophysics of neuronal function. It remains to be observed how sub-100 pN MNP-mediated forces impact the electrophysiological properties of neuronal spiking.

In this context, previous nanomagnetic force studies have relied on fluorescent calcium imaging to characterize the functional impacts, which limits the understanding of precise spike timing.37 Neuronal depolarization during action potential generation drives voltage-gated activity expressed through calcium sensors binding to free cytosolic calcium38 or calcium-binding proteins like calmodulin39 as a transient influx of calcium through voltage-gated calcium channels. As a secondary messenger, calcium regulates various cellular mechanisms, from synaptic vesicle release40 to gene expression.41 Calcium relies on the coordinated action of ion channels and pumps for spatiotemporal control to encode such a diverse set of mechanisms. Under action potential generation, these secondary mechanisms reduce the efficacy of precise spike readouts.42 Further, mechanical probing can induce calcium influx through nonvoltage driven mechanisms, impeding transient spike readouts.7,9 Therefore, precise recording of neuronal action potentials under mechanical deformation requires electrophysiological recording and calcium imaging.

Here, we combine and characterize simultaneous fluorescent calcium imaging and microelectrode array recordings with a magnetic field platform to elucidate the neuronal response to nanomagnetic forces. We validate our setup and provide evidence of force specificity between 2–8 pN, highlighting different functional responses in cortical networks dependent on the MNP interactions.

Results and Discussion

A Platform for Simultaneous Calcium Imaging and Electrophysiological Activity during Nanomagnetic Forces

To expand neuronal recordings under nanomagnetic forces, we designed a multimodal recording platform to access electrophysiological features and calcium dynamics with an integrated external magnetic field applicator (Figure 1a–c). We opted for extracellular recording with a microelectrode array (MEA) to avoid mechanical perturbations under pipet interaction.43 Electrophysiology was incorporated with fluorescent imaging through optical access by an inverted or top-down setup (Figure S1). The top-down setup enabled wide-view imaging across the MEA surface, but could not hardware synchronize with the MEA interface, thus requiring the inverted microscope platform for simultaneous imaging. We implemented pulse-triggered CCD capture through 10 ms triggers output from the microelectrode interface board, time-matched to the software-driven electrical recording. Force generation across the 6.5 mm microelectrode surface was mapped through finite element modeling (Figure S2) and validated using magnetic microparticle (8.4 μm) image velocimetry yielding 30 to 60 pN magnetic microparticle force (Figures 1d and S3–S6). Electrical potentials recorded with MEA chips (200 μm diameter, 700 μm pitch, and a dedicated internal reference electrode) delivered local and network-wide perspectives on neuronal activity (Figure 1e,f).

To validate the system, we cultured primary rat cortex neurons on the large-scale MEAs. Passive simultaneous fluorescent imaging of cytosolic calcium (Fluo-4AM, 100× total magnification) and electrical recording of 14 DIV cortical neuron networks over 2 min permitted functional characterization (Figure 2a). A temperature controller (Multichannel Systems) maintained a physiologically relevant environment (37 °C) during recordings. Neuronal calcium transient activity was characterized through somatic signals processed with peak detection (3 standard deviations, Figure S7). Electrophysiological signals were bandpass filtered (300–4000 Hz) and a falling edge spike detection (4.5 standard deviations) extracted electrical spikes from the signals. The standard deviation threshold was calibrated by recording 14 DIV cortical cultures for 4 min at 14 DIV. We extracted spikes across increasing standard deviation thresholds to compute a signal-to-noise ratio (Figure S8). Voltage potentials of detected spikes presented a signal-to-noise ratio of 39 ± 15 au at 4.5 standard deviations, significantly above the noise limit (1-sided, two-sample t test p = 0.0054, H0: μspike voltage = μRMS voltage, Ha: μspike voltage > μRMS voltage). To clarify the spatial resolution of the electrode recordings relative to individual neuronal signals obtained through calcium imaging, we sorted calcium signals into a top or bottom region by the distance to the nearest electrode (Figure 2b). Transient calcium influx events binned in 1 s intervals highlighted network-wide, synchronous activity matched with high-frequency electrophysiological spiking at both electrodes (Figure 2c) matching other simultaneous calcium microelectrode observations.44 Synchronous calcium events observed in only one of the sorted regions coincided with spike rate increases at the closer electrode, while spiking was minimal at the opposing electrode, suggesting that neuronal activity could be spatially resolved over the MEA (Figure 2d). Over the spontaneous recording window, we observed an average calcium event rate of 0.12 ± 0.070 events cell–1 s–1 and an electrophysiological spike rate of 1.9 ± 1.5 spikes/s. To further characterize the distance relationship between calcium events and electrode spikes we analyzed the concurrency of calcium spikes relative to electrode spikes as a function of neural distance (Figure 2f). Concurrency was measured as the percentage of time windows (1 s) in which an electrical spike and a concurrent calcium event were detected within the same window. Neuronal cells located more than 600 μm away from the electrode center showed a significant decrease in concurrency (Figure 2g). Large scale synchronous calcium events were present across networks as shown in Figure 2d, which we suspect caused an increase in the concurrency rate. This could indicate that the radius of detection is smaller than the 600 μm distance measured here. It is likely that the electrophysiological spikes are detected from neurons within 140 μm,45,46 with the remaining concurrency values being driven by synchronous calcium events.

After validating our system, we aimed to characterize the MNP interactions. We selected chitosan surface functionalized MNPs (chiMNPs) following previous work.10,27,33,34,47 Dynamic light scattering revealed that chiMNPs sonicated (40 kHz, 30 min, 40 °C) in culture media exhibited a hydrodynamic radius of 269.2 nm with a polydispersity index of 0.050 (Figure S4). Following previous results,33,47 this suggests that chiMNPs consistently aggregate into small clusters. To validate the forces exerted by the particles we contrasted microparticle motion with nanoparticle motion using high-resolution microscopy (Figure S5). Experimental measurements indicated that the magnetic microparticles exerted approximately 12 times greater force than the chiMNPs, which we used as a scaling factor to characterize the range of forces exerted by chiMNPs across the microelectrode array. Across the magnetic assay, the microparticle quantification showed an estimated variance of 0.95 pN with no significant correlation of increased variance within 0–80 pN force ranges (Figure S6). To ensure MNP exposure did not impact electrophysiological recordings, we employed impedimetric measures on the microelectrode arrays. We found no significant change to impedance or phase under nanoparticle interactions at dosage concentrations (Figure S9). We dispersed the particles in media during sonication as this is known to further promote neuronal uptake.48,49 In agreement with previous observations,34,47 the larger hydrodynamic radii indicate nanoparticle clustering was prevalent. Fluorescent imaging of chiMNPs revealed nanoparticles were delivered to cells and presented across neurons following 24 h incubation (Figure S10).

Various methods of integrating calcium and electrophysiological recording have expanded our understanding of neuronal function in vitro(50) and in vivo.(51) Typically, calcium imaging is used to identify representative subpopulations of coactive neurons.52 However, functional neurostimulation paradigms have highlighted the interplay between nonvoltage driven calcium dynamics under stimulation,53 thus convoluting action potential information within a calcium signal and requiring voltage recordings for accurate readout. Recently, Kasuba et al. exposed such interplay by integrating fluorescent microscopy with a CMOS microelectrode system to highlight the differential calcium and electrophysiological responses to AFM deformation.6 Through the combined system, they identified functional responses in neurons indistinguishable through calcium imaging alone. The developed system in this work lacks the subcellular resolution electrophysiological readouts, instead prioritizing large-scale network activity.

Nanomagnetic Forces on Cortical Neurons Drive Calcium Influx and Modulate Neuronal Activity

To introduce nanomagnetic forces on neuronal networks, cortical neurons were cultured on MEAs (200 μm diameter, 700 μm pitch) to 13 DIV and exposed to chiMNPs for 24 h to enter the neuronal cytosol. Following chiMNP exposure (24 h) but before force modulation, neuronal electrophysiological activity exhibited an average activity rate of 2.28 ± 1.69 spikes/s over a 2 min recording. We then applied the magnetic field while recording calcium (Fluo-4AM, 1 Hz) and electrophysiology (25 kHz). The local region used for calcium imaging was selected to characterize 4 pN forces (Figure 3a). Somatic fluorescent calcium traces revealed elevated (>10% ΔF/F) calcium levels in ∼60% of cortical neurons (Figure 3b). Transient calcium event rates increased from an initial 0.028 events cell–1 s–1 to a peak event rate at 45 s of force exposure of 0.087 events cell–1 s–1 (Figure 3c). The percentage of neurons responding to mechanical stimuli is within the range of studies of mechanical stimulation via MNPs,27 AFM,9 or ultrasound.7,9

Time-synchronized MEA recordings enabled the measurement of neuronal action potentials through spike detection (Figure 3d,e). We quantified standard deviation thresholding for spike detection across recording parameters by contrasting spike count against media-only MEAs (Figure S11). Repeated recordings of neuronal activity during chiMNP exposure indicated a decrease in neuronal spike rate, but nanoparticle exposure showed no significant effect on the average spike magnitude (Figure S12). To examine the electrophysiological impacts of nanomagnetic forces, we extracted the spike waveforms from electrodes to elucidate the electrophysiological impacts under nanomagnetic force (Figure 3f). Before magnetic field stimulation, the detected potentials exhibited a negative potential of −12.9 ± 0.4 μV. Under nanomagnetic forces, the peak negative potential of detected spikes decreased to −7.8 ± 0.4 μV with significant positive deviations following the spike. In the first 30 s of modulation, the positive potential increased to +6.6 ± 0.5 μV from the baseline +1.5 ± 0.3 μV maximum. The positive peak during force was reduced to 4.3 ± 0.5 μV and slowly increased to a peak 7.6 ± 0.7 μV in the final 30 s. We performed secondary electrophysiology assays with MEAs without calcium imaging. The spike rate and magnitude reduction were consistent across samples (Figure S13). The positive peak is often regarded as the neuronal hyperpolarization phase, where potassium efflux drives a negative membrane potential. The reduction in spike magnitude paired with the later positive deviation suggests nanomagnetic forces could modulate the voltage mechanisms, such as potassium efflux.

The neuronal activity rates during modulation were characterized by observing the spike rate over the recording epoch. The local electrode increased spike rate over the first minute, from an initial 4.3 ± 2.7 spikes/s up to 7.1 ± 2.8 spikes/s time-matched with the peak calcium event rates between 45–60 s before rapidly returning to initial spike rates for the second minute (Figure 3g). These findings reveal that low piconewton-force nanomagnetic modulation induces a gradual cytosolic influx of somatic calcium and modulates network activity. The decrease in activity during the second minute could be explained by functional synaptic regulation through internally driven calcium-dependent receptor desensitization54,55 or by network interactions through glial or increased inhibitory currents to mitigate excitotoxicity.56−58 To elucidate the force-sensitivity, we recorded calcium and electrophysiology in cortical neurons exposed to 2 pN forces and found no significant response to nanomagnetic forces in calcium or electrophysiology (Figure S14). These results indicate a force-sensitive response in the neuronal networks. To ensure that the response is not due to mechanical damage from the chiMNP generated forces, we performed Live/Dead stains 3 h after force application and found no significant difference in viability between nanoparticle or force interactions with neuronal cells (Figure S15).

This data suggests that nanomagnetic forces drive neuronal cytosolic calcium influx and impact neuronal spiking through voltage-mediated mechanisms. It is known that brief (<1 h) nanomaterial exposure can induce stronger postsynaptic currents by tuning the ion channel activations.59−62 However, exposure over extended periods mediates the intrinsic excitability of cultured neurons to reduce activity.63,64 The reduced activity observed in cultures with 24 h chiMNP exposure (Figure S12) suggests that neuronal activity was impacted by nanoparticle exposure. During force, we observed a significant reduction in spikes detected above 4.5 standard deviations, which modulated the voltage waveform. The decrease in detected spiking potential could also be explained by an alteration in the cleft between the neuron and electrode. As the pressure is exerted on the neuron the interface distance and corresponding coupling could be altered by the mechanical force. However, this is unable to explain the increase in the positive potential following spikes, so we expect that ionic conductance drives key features in the spiking alteration. Calcium-dependent or independent mechanisms could drive this voltage modulation. Under significant calcium influx, calcium-dependent potassium channels (KCa) activate to permit the efflux of cytosolic potassium, playing a key role in voltage stabilization during the afterhyperpolarization phase of action potentials to regulate neuronal adaptation and excitability.65,66 Rat cortical neurons also express the mechanosensitive two-pore potassium (K2P) channels, such as TREK-1 and TRAAK.67 K2P channels are vital to action potential conduction68 and have been shown to impair neuronal excitability under stretch.69,70

Given that the hyperpolarization waveform response observed under nanomagnetic forces (Figure 2d) precedes the calcium influx response (Figure 2b), this suggests the initial waveform response is mediated by calcium-independent mechanisms such as K2P. As a concentration-driven flux, the outward potassium current would decrease over time if the current was greater than the local somatic potassium inputs. This efflux could explain the reduced afterhyperpolarization response observed at 30–90 s as cytosolic calcium rises through other mechanosensitive calcium channels, such as voltage-gated9,27 or transient receptor potential channels,7,9 further activating KCa channels to amplify the hyperpolarization response by enhancing outward potassium current.

A key challenge to address in future work is optimizing the technology for time-paired recordings to provide multimodal recordings before and after the addition of the magnetic field. The current setup requires removal of the microelectrode headstage from the microscope to add the magnetic field above the optics. On readdition of the headstage with the magnetic field, the short time (<30 s) required to understand the magnetic field interaction created challenges in relocating neuronal cells observed during the preliminary recordings. To circumvent this in future technologies, engineering permanent magnetic field tools capable of interacting in the small space between the microscope optics and the electrophysiological headstage could permit clearer results without the need for headstage removal. Further, the influx of cytosolic calcium offers a valuable perspective on the interface between mechanical forces, calcium, and neuronal spiking dynamics. While fluorescent dyes such as Fluo-4AM enabled observation of dynamics, the observations could be further enhanced through ratiometric imaging with calcium probes such as Fura-2. The interface developed in this work is readily adaptable for different microscopes with standard working distances and could be implemented to investigate the nanomagnetic force interactions with concentration-based calcium dynamics.

Spatiotemporal Activity Decomposition Exposes Force and Time-Dependent Spiking Responses of Neurons to Nanomagnetic Forces

Next, we aimed to investigate the relationship between force and neuronal activity by recording over large areas, providing localized recordings across different force values. Calcium imaging was limited to a 1 mm2 region, while the MEA could record from an area of up to 36 mm2. Therefore, we opted to record exclusively from the MEA. Across the MEA, forces provided 2 to 8 pN (Figure 4a) enabling distinct force magnitudes across the network. The chiMNPs directly act on the neuronal cells, exerting consistent forces on individual neurons dependent on the strength and gradient of the magnetic field. As the neuronal network is functionally connected, we reasoned that localized spike rate modulations could propagate information across the whole network if the mechanical deformation evoked spikes. Prior to forces, initial baseline measurements of neuronal activity presented randomized activity across the tested neural population (Figure 4b). Upon nanomagnetic force application, we observed a decrease in local electrode activity correlated to regions of higher forces (Figure 4c). We decomposed the activity rate change into 15 s windows to clarify the activity progression over 2 min of forces (Figure 4d). Initially, neuronal activity exhibited increased activity with no spatial correlation. However, over the recording period, a time-progressive decrease in spike rates corresponded to force magnitude rises, suggesting a time-dependent sensitization or adaptation of the neurons to the applied forces.

Using the force map, we sorted electrode spike rates over time by the exposed forces. Electrode recordings in regions subjected to higher forces (>6 pN) appeared to present a more pronounced decrease in neuronal spiking activity over the force exposure compared to those experiencing lower forces (<6 pN). To quantitatively measure the changes in neuronal activity, we first computed a percent difference in spiking during the first 15 s of force application relative to the baseline recording. Neuronal activity significantly increased under force (<6 pN) in comparison to no force controls, which exhibited force-sensitive increases in activity (Figure 4e). We then measured the percent difference between the beginning and end of the nanomagnetic force period (Figure 4f). The spike rate trended toward significance (1-way ANOVA, p = 0.0582), suggesting that the observed decrease in neuronal activity could be attributed to the force.

These findings demonstrate that the neuronal response to nanomagnetic forces with chiMNPs is force-sensitive in the low pN range. The functional response to continuous forces exhibits a time-varying activity response under continuous stimulation, exhibiting instantaneous electrophysiological spiking increases with a slow, force-specific decay in activity over 2 min. The specific activation observed in the first 15 s of magnetic field application suggests 2–8 pN nanomagnetic forces modulate neuronal spiking across the network rather than evoking spikes. Functionally connected networks drive stimulus information across the network, which was recently shown with mechanical stimuli.71 As the network exhibited spatially mediated, force-specific responses, the spiking response of local electrodes is independent of the network forces. The trend in activity reduction over the modulation period suggests a force-specific response occurs after a delay, further indicating the excitability of neurons is tuned through these forces. Given the rapid time frame, this furthers the expectation of mechanosensitive ion channel activation in response to nanomagnetic forces to modulate the membrane potential.

MNP Interactions Mediate the Functional Response to Nanomagnetic Forces

With the ability to map the relationship between nanomagnetic forces, calcium, and electrophysiology, we then characterized the functional differences of nanomagnetic force application under differing nanoparticle conditions. To contrast against the chiMNPs, we used starch functionalized MNPs (staMNPs)27,47 and increased nanomagnetic force strength to 16–160 pN by relocating the magnetic field to produce a broader range of forces across the MEA (Figure S2a,b).

Control neurons exposed to staMNPs without a magnetic field exhibited spontaneous transient calcium events and network-wide events at 0.090 ± 0.027 events cell–1 s–1 (Figure 5a1). We observed an increase in neuronal activity in the spike rate from an initial 0.9 spikes/s to 4.8 spikes/s by the end of the recording (2 min), matching previous observations of nanomaterial exposure induced neuronal excitation.36,59,60 Under nanomagnetic forces with the staMNPs, neurons did not exhibit cytosolic calcium influx; rather, synchronous calcium transients occurred with an event rate of 0.050 ± 0.020 events cell–1 s–1 (Figure 5a2). A spike rate of 20.1 spikes/s at the local electrode suggested significant neuronal excitation under staMNP-mediated nanomagnetic forces. In contrast, the 120 pN forces with chiMNPs exhibited strong calcium influx and a gradual depression in calcium events from 0.078 events cell–1 s–1 initially to 0.037 events cell–1 s–1 final and spike rates from 2.7 spikes/s initially to 0.3 spikes/s in the final min (Figure 5a3), matching our previous observations. Network-wide spike rates reciprocated the local observations. We observed no significant change in the spike rate of neurons exposed to chiMNPs over the recording window (Figure 5b1). The high activity (>5 spikes/s) observed under staMNPs with the magnetic field was represented by 42% of electrodes with a spike rate of 33.9 ± 9.7 spikes/s with strong burst firing detected across electrodes (Figure 5b2). Similarly, network-wide spiking activity under chiMNPs followed exhibited strong activation at 45 s with minimal activity otherwise (Figure 5b3).

These results suggest that the neuronal networks respond to the force based on the MNP interaction. It could be possible that MNP uptake plays a role in this response. These staMNPs were previously recognized to reside primarily externally,27,33,47 while we observed chiMNPs across the cultures. These interactions could provoke a differential circuit response dependent on cellular interactions or could be a force sensitive response dependent on the internalization of particles. Previous experiments have observed calcium influx under staMNP-driven nanomagnetic forces through N-type voltage-gated calcium channels,10,27 but was correlated to forces well above 100 pN. This staMNP force (120 pN) lacked sufficient force to induce calcium influx, but significantly modulated neuronal activity. These observations could indicate that staMNP mediated, forces near 100 pN mechanically sensitize neurons through membrane stretch, decreasing the activation potential of N-type calcium channels.72 Taken together, we conclude that the cortical network’s functional response depends on MNP interactions. Future work should investigate these relationships to elucidate the role of MNP-neuron interactions and the dependency of force magnitude in modulating the functional response of neurons.

To investigate if the MNP-force interactions induced a lasting suppressive effect, we incubated networks with the magnetic field to achieve continuous nanomagnetic stimulation for 6 h with 2 min electrophysiological recording epochs occurring every 2 h. Under staMNP (no force) exposure, networks exhibited a significant decrease in activity by the second hour of exposure, but no further depression in activity over the recording period (Kruskal–Wallis ANOVA, p < 0.0001). Cultures exposed to nanomagnetic forces exhibited elevated activity over the first 2 h, followed by a significant decrease in activity by 4 and 6 h of continuous exposure (Kruskal–Wallis ANOVA, p < 0.0001). We mapped electrode activity rate over the recording periods to check for force sensitivity and found no correlation between forces between the 16–160 pN forces (Figure S16). As we hypothesized that MNP forces could mechanically desensitize neurons, we stimulated networks with a negative first biphasic pulse (100 μV, 200 μs) at a neighboring electrode, recording calcium and electrical activity (Figure S17). The no-force, staMNP-exposed cells consistently responded to the stimulus with activity in both calcium events and network-wide electrophysiological spiking, suggesting no desensitization occurred from staMNP exposure. Neurons exposed to continuous staMNP-mediated forces exhibited significantly fewer spike responses to the stimuli, with half of the stimuli eliciting a response in calcium. In contrast, chiMNP-mediated force exposure mitigated the calcium event response, but a consistent electrophysiological response was observed. As the stimulation responses matched our expectations following the initial responses to forces, we conclude this furthers our hypothesis that the neuronal response to MNP forces is location dependent. First, externally generated forces induced hyperactivity in spike detection with strong calcium transients and no observable calcium rise. Continuous exposure led to consistent calcium transient’s responses but minimal spiking under voltage stimulation. This reduced spiking could be explained as a mechanical voltage desensitization, either directly through mechano-gated channel current reduction73 or indirectly by synaptically induced desensitization.74 We note that K2P channels are desensitized independent of cytoskeletal interactions.75 Therefore, it is feasible that the staMNP modulation induces potassium efflux to desensitize the neurons over extended periods. Second, the chiMNP-generated forces immediately induced somatic calcium influx with weak calcium transients with moderate spiking, which presented spiking activity but minimal calcium response under voltage excitation. This response suggests that chiMNP forces induce a calcium influx desensitization, reducing the calcium but not the voltage response. As calcium plays a vital role in cellular health and apoptosis,76,77 this calcium desensitization could be explained as a mechanism to mitigate the excessive calcium influx.

In summary, we conclude that the neuronal response to 16–160 pN MNP-mediated forces depends on the particle location. Specifically, short-term exposure of staMNPs paired with magnetic pulling to generate forces impacted neuronal excitability independent of the slow calcium influx. In contrast, the chiMNP-mediated forces modulated neuronal activity in conjunction with the slow calcium influx, suggesting the neurons initiated functional responses under force with different MNP interactions. However, continuous (>4 h) stimulation drove activity reductions independent from MNP interactions, indicating nanomagnetic forces can induce similar functional suppression at longer exposure time.

Neuronal Force Sensitivity to Nanomagnetic Forces

Given the cytosolic access of chiMNPs, we aimed to clarify the low-force sensitivity of neurons in response to this intracellular mechanical stimulus. We were particularly interested in the low-pN force range, which closely matches cytoskeletal force generation.78,79 Mechanical sensitivity is native to neurons, where the compartmentalized viscoelasticity of the cell membrane plays a fundamental role in action potential function.3,6 The neuronal cytoskeleton tunes these rheological properties through filamentous and microtubular components to mediate neuronal mechanical response.80 Microfilaments such as actin play a critical role in cytoskeletal membrane anchoring.81 They can generate forces from 1–10 pN, measured through optical traps,82 which are predicted to mediate lipid bilayer curvatures at forces above 2 pN.83 As a fundamental component of cytoskeletal anchoring, talin permits force-specific transmission to integrin in only a few pN.84−86 Basal calcium levels are crucial in these cytoskeletal dynamics to mediate filopodia formation and guidance.87,88 Further, talin has been shown to be mediated by force-dependent calcium influx.89 Altogether, these observations hint at a mechanism for the mechanical gating of neuronal activity90 but require specific force generation methods to expand our understanding. Therefore, we engineered our system to apply uniform forces locally with 2 or 8 pN forces (Figure 6a). For rapid nanomagnetic force testing, we seeded cortical neurons on Petri dishes and imaged fluorescent calcium (Fluo-4AM) across the force range (Figure S19). The cortical neurons exhibited a linear increase in the calcium influx magnitude under 2 to 8 pN with minimal influx observed at 2 pN suggesting the neuronal response is sensitive to nanomagnetic forces at low forces.

Consistent localized forces from magnetic fields require homogeneous gradient forces to be applied to the cortical network. Therefore, we used localized MEAs (10 μm diameter, 100 μm pitch) with engineered local magnetic fields to generate 2 or 8 pN uniform forces across local cortical networks (Figures 6a and S2c,d). Cortical neurons were dropwise seeded (106 cells) over the electrodes to contain the network over the smaller electrode region and cultured to DIV 13 (Figure 6b). To introduce nanomagnetic forces, we used amine-functionalized bionized nanoferrite starch MNPs (afMNPs) which have been shown to cell-uptake with enhanced biocompatibility.91,92 Electrophysiological recording epochs (4 min) were performed at time points prior to the addition of the magnetic nanoparticles (baseline), during 24 h exposure of afMNP exposure, and through 8 h of continuous force with 2 h intervals between each recording to characterize the long-term response to force.

Prior to afMNP exposure, baseline recordings revealed neuronal spike rates of 0.33 ± 0.11 spikes/s. During MNP uptake, neuronal spiking decreased to 0.17 ± 0.11 spikes/s at 4 h exposure, with 24 h exposure returning spiking (0.33 ± 0.20 spikes/s) to baseline rates. Given particle uptake is an energy-dependent process, the depression in activity could be regulated by neuronal uptake around the 4 h mark.

Over the stimulation epochs, control (no force) samples presented minimal variability over 8 h of recording (Figure 6c1–d1). Local networks exposed to nanomagnetic forces responded with activity rates increasing through a delayed response to 2 pN or an immediate response to 8 pN forces. Neurons exposed to the low, 2 pN showed a brief initial increase in activity to 0.49 ± 0.02 spikes/s with a decrease to 0.22 ± 0.09 spikes/s over the initial stimulation period, indicating a brief response to the slow forces (Figure 6c2). Under continuous 2 pN stimulation, network activity rates remained consistent at 0.27 ± 0.04 spikes/s until 4 h, where the activity rates increased to 0.51 ± 0.04 spikes/s and maintained the higher activity through 8 h (Figure 6d2). The stronger 8 pN presented a significant increase in activity to 2.48 ± 1.98 spikes/s, decreasing over the 4 min to a final activity of 0.4 ± 0.06 spikes/s (Figure 6c3). The increased activity remained consistent over 2 h before matching its baseline activity rates of 0.29 ± 0.13 spikes/s by 4 h. At 6 h exposure of the 8 pN force, neuronal activity reduced to 0.26 ± 0.06 spikes/s which remained low through 8 h recordings (Figure 6d3).

These results show that nanomagnetic forces at 8 pN immediately induce neuronal excitability, which maintains over 2 h before a slow reduction in activity. The 2 pN forces did not exhibit a clear immediate response to forces but increased neuronal excitability over hours. To investigate if a local-network response drove the activity shift, we tracked the network functionality by 100 ms spike binning to track the number of coactive electrodes as a measure for synchronous firing (Figure S15). While the 8 pN forces caused a slight increase in synchronous firing, we observed a significant reduction in synchronous firing under the 2 pN forces. This suggests that 2 pN immediately impacts local-network functionality, driving asynchrony, but not neuronal spiking activity. In higher force and stretch experiments, traumatic brain injury has shown similar asynchronous dysregulation of network activity93,94 and temporal transitions from hyperexcitation to inhibition through excitatory-inhibitory balance.95 While these forces exist far above the low pN forces generated here, we find it interesting that networks exposed to a broad range of forces behave similarly.

Our observations of under nanomagnetic forces correspond to various mechanical probing methodologies. While experimental constraints limit our ability to contrast nanomagnetic forces against transient stimulation methods such as AFM, optical tweezers, or ultrasound stimulation, all methods induce cytosolic increases in calcium.7,9,17 Some mechanical stimulation methods have shown calcium release from intracellular stores.7 For example, Kim et al. used optical tweezers to show that large membrane deformations can trigger endoplasmic reticulum calcium release through actomyosin contractility.96 Therefore, examining MNP interactions with such intracellular stores and whether direct mechanical interactions can drive calcium release to trigger signaling cascades could be interesting.

These results expand on the growing work of neuronal mechanotransduction by developing and characterizing a simultaneous fluorescent imaging and electrophysiological recording technology for characterizing network with nanomagnetic force. Future work should prioritize using such technologies to distinguish key differences in cellular responses based on MNP-localization and force magnitude. Our results indicate that mechanical nanotransducers impact neuronal activity in the low pN force regime. We suggest future work should characterize the biophysical mechanisms that drive the neuronal functional response to forces.

Conclusions

We have presented an integrated platform for elucidating the functional response of the neuronal network to nanomagnetic forces. With this approach, one can track the calcium and voltage dynamics in response to MNP-driven mechanical force modulation across cortical networks. Characterizing neuronal mechanotransduction and the pathways propagating this mechanical information is critical to elucidate neuronal functionality and dynamics. We use this platform to show that nanomagnetic forces can provoke a cytosolic influx of neuronal calcium paired with an increased electrophysiological spike rate. We found a force-specific electrophysiological spike response in cortical networks exposed to 2–8 pN, suggesting the low-pN forces do not evoke spikes but modulate the excitability of neurons. Stronger, 16–160 pN nanomagnetic forces modulated neuronal networks with consistent calcium influx during force application, which could be altered by changing MNP-neuron interactions through altered surface functionalization and exposure time. Finally, we identify a time-delayed electrophysiological spiking excitation to 2 pN continuous nanomagnetic forces on the cortical neurons. This work elaborates on the mechanical neuromodulation from a network-scale to provide insights into neuronal spiking under the extremely low pN force application of magnetic nanoparticles across an assay. The simplicity of the magnetic field application allows this technology to be integrated into many ready-to-use in vitro recording stations for imaging and electrophysiology, which provides a great platform for investigating force application in vitro. When paired with localized nanoparticle delivery,97 this technology could reach the single-cell resolution for low pN forces to explore the cellular response to forces. This work provides accessibility for neuroscientists to investigate the functional response to external mechanical force generation.

Methods

Multimodal Recording Setup with Integrated Nanomagnetic-Based Force Modulation

Two combined imaging systems have been designed to use nanomagnetic force stimulation on microelectrode arrays with a top-view and an inverted microscope system (Figure S1). The top-view system consists of a glass-lens digital fluorescent microscope with colored illumination (LED-based, e.g., Dino-Lite AM4115T-GRFBY, ex. Wavelengths: 480 and 575 nm). The optical sensor of the digital microscope 1280 × 1024 pixels resolution (1.3 megapixels, CMOS) and can capture up to 30 frames per second (fps). A 3D printed platform aligns the MEA2100 headstage (Multi Channel Systems) with the digital fluorescent microscope, the magnetic ring platform, and the microelectrode array probes (60ecoMEA, on-glass). The inverted imaging system combines the MEA2100 headstage with an inverted fluorescent microscope (AmScope IN480, 30W Kohler source, 40–600× total magnification, 6 megapixel CCD), where a 3D printed stage aligns the magnetic ring platform with the MEA probe (Multi Channel Systems). Automated temporal synchronization of signal recording of voltage time series (25 kHz) and fluorescence imaging (2 Hz) was accomplished between the digital fluorescent camera of the microscope, and the MEA2100-Lite interface board through programming synchronized digital triggers based on 10 ms rising edges.

Primary Cerebral Cortex Culture and Nanoparticle Incubation

Microelectrode arrays (Multi Channel Systems, 60ecoMEA and 60MEA100/10iR-ITO) were prepared 2–4 days prior to culture by exposure to 65% ethanol for 1 min, followed by 3× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and 3× double distilled water rinses. The MEA was then completely aspirated and then covered in 10 μg/mL poly d-lysine (PDL) for 24 h. Following, the PDL was rinsed off 3× with PBS and incubated with pretreatment media (90% Neurobasal Plus, 10% Horse Serum) until cell seeding.

Rat embryonic brains (E18, BrainBits) were dissected following a previously established protocol.33 Cortical tissues were dissociated in 10% (v/v) papain (Carica papaya, Roche) in Hibernate-E (BrainBits) at 37 °C for 15 min. The tissues were then washed with 10% (v/v) horse serum in neurobasal plus (Gibco), triturated gently, and strained (40 μm filter). Dissociated cortical neurons were centrifuged (12 min, 600 rpm, at RT) and then seeded dropwise over the electrodes at a 106 cells per microelectrode array. Cortical neurons were incubated (95% air, 5% CO2, 37 °C) in culture medium (97% (v/v) Neurobasal Plus Medium, B-27 Plus Supplement 2% (v/v), and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin, Gibco) and grown for 2 weeks. MNPs with surface functionalized with chitosan (nano-screenMAG/R-Chitosan, Chemicell), starch (nano-screenMAG/B-Starch, Chemicell) or amine (BNF-Starch-far redF, surface: NH2, Micromod) were sonicated (1 kHz, 35 °C) for 10 min in water prior to suspension. MNPs were suspended in culture media (10 μg/mL, 37 °C) and added at times provided in text (For nanomagnetic forces: DIV 13 for 24 h exposure with chiMNPs or DIV 14 for 30 min exposure with staMNPs). Following experimentation, the MEAs were cleaned with solvent (Tween 20) and autoclaved after every third use.

Paired Calcium Fluorometry and MEA Recording during Nanomagnetic Forces

For calcium fluorometry, neuronal cultures were loaded with 1:1 (v/v) Fluo-4 AM with probenecid (Thermo) at 37 °C for 1 h. Cells were gently washed with media (97% Neurobasal Plus, 2% B-27 Supplement, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, v/v) before imaging. Fluorescence was imaged using the Amscope microscope (450–510 nm excitation, 100–200× total magnification, 400 ms exposure, 1 Hz triggered by Multi Channel Systems digital hardware output). Prior to forces, microelectrode arrays were placed in the MEA headstage (37 °C) for 5 min to permit stabilization. Then cultures were electrophysiologically recorded for 2 min through the Multi Channel Experimenter software. For nanomagnetic forces, the microelectrode interface board was then turned off and the headstage was placed on the microscope with the magnetic device set in place. The local electrode was found optically, the interface board was turned back on, and the recording with the CMOS pulse synchronization was started through the Multichannel Experimenter Software. For all experiments, we ensured less than 30 s passed from adding the MEA headstage on the magnetic field to the recording start. Concurrency was computed at individual neurons by first binarizing the electrode spike train into 1 s windows, where a 1 was indicative of at least 1 electrical spike detected within the window and a 0 indicated no spikes detected. The time indices of the electrode spikes were stored in a vector E with dimension N by 1. Following, for each neuron, concurrency was computed as relative frequency of timeframes where the cell exhibited a calcium event during a window where the electrode spike was present1

Calcium Imaging Analysis

In post, videos were imported into ImageJ and individual neurons were segmented using polygon selections around the soma. The mean fluorescent intensity was measured over the recording period. ΔF/F signals were computed as the difference relative to the initial resting state in MATLAB 2023B. Transient calcium events were detected through local peaks prominence greater the 3 standard deviations of the signal. All influx transients are plotted in calcium raster plots and histograms are of the cumulative measures.2

MEA Analysis

MEA recordings were performed with Multichannel Systems Analyzer software. Electric potentials were recorded at 25 kHz and saved for analysis using in-house software in MATLAB. Signals were bandpass filtered (300–4000 Hz) to remove significant noise and a 4.5 standard deviation falling edge spike detection protocol was applied for spike detection. Spikes were presented as spike rates, measured as the total number of spikes divided by the time window observed (e.g., 30 spikes in a 15 s window = 2 spikes/s). Spike waveforms were clipped from the filtered signal, centered on the detected falling peak with ±50 ms. For spatial electrode mapping, electrode spike rates were classified through multichannel systems provided electrode identification numbers. Force specific spike rate responses were parsed according to the approximated nanomagnetic forces (d = 300 nm). Statistical Analysis

All data was tested for normality with Kolmogorov–Smirnov. Force specificity in activity and Neuronal spiking response were found to be normal, therefore 1-way ANOVA was used to test significance. The neuronal spiking response over time was found to be non-normal but equal variance, leading to Kruskal–Wallis for comparisons within sample. Data collection and analysis were not performed blind to the experimental conditions. Statistical significance was evaluated at p ≤ 0.05. Data was not outlier-filtered. Bar plots are reported with bar at mean and error bar as standard deviation if not specified. All data reported in text is reported as mean ± standard deviation.
