Decolonisation of a multi-drug-resistant Escherichia coli and a vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium after intervention with faecal microbiota transplantation
Authors: Maja Johanne Søndergaard Knudsen, Ingrid Maria Cecilia Rubin, Hengameh Chloe Mirsepasi-Lauridsen, Jeff Tomiak, Sofie Ingdam Halkjær, Andreas Munk Petersen, Morten Helms
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn this study, we report a case of decolonisation of a multi-drug-resistant extended spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Escherichia coli, and a vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium after intervention with capsulated faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT). Following eradication, our patient acquired an extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae, which was subsequently eliminated after a second round of FMT capsules. Additionally, we report the detection of six distinct carbapenemase-producing organisms in this patient, who was evacuated from the war in Ukraine.

Introduction

Multi-drug-resistant organisms (MROs) such as carbapenemase-producing organisms (CPOs), extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing (ESBL) Enterobacterales, and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium (VREfm) are of major concern to public health globally. Infections with MRO often require treatment with broad-spectrum antibiotics, and in some cases last-resort antibiotics are needed [1]. The Antimicrobial Resistance Collaboration estimated that 1.27 million deaths were ‘directly attributable’ to antimicrobial resistance in 2019 [2].

To prevent infections with MRO, different strategies have been attempted to actively eradicate MRO from the intestine of people who are intestinal carriers. While interventions with probiotics and antibiotics have not proven efficient in previous studies, the first cases of decolonisation of MRO using faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) were reported a decade ago [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Since then, the use of FMT for decolonisation of MRO has been investigated in several uncontrolled studies, most of which have shown positive results [7,8].

In this study, we report the decolonisation of multiple MRO in one patient after intervention with FMT capsules. The patient had previously suffered from an invasive CPO infection, which required treatment with last-resort antibiotics. Additionally, we report the finding of six different CPO in rectal, urine, and tissue samples from the same patient, who was severely injured and evacuated from the war in Ukraine.

Presentation of case

A male patient in his 20s sustained severe injuries during the war in Ukraine. After undergoing multiple surgeries in Ukraine, he was evacuated to Denmark for further treatment.

Prior to his admission to our local hospital, he had already undergone surgery performed at another Danish hospital. Upon admission to one of our hospitals, the patient presented with multiple thoracic wounds, including one large wound measuring 8 x 5 cm, as well as a defect in the scar from previous surgeries on his right shoulder. An X-ray of the right shoulder showed a comminuted fracture and multiple foreign objects. A computed tomography (CT) scan revealed an abscess in the right shoulder, which was subsequently drained using ultrasound. The pus grew with methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and Prevotella buccae. Treatment with intravenous vancomycin 1 g twice daily, and piperacillin/tazobactam 4 g/0.5 g four times daily was initiated. The patient underwent surgery on the right shoulder, and five peri-operative biopsies were taken. The biopsies grew with carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae (2/5) (KPC, Table I), carbapenemase-producing Pseudomonas aeruginosa (4/5) (IMP, Table I), methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (5/5), Enterococcus avium (5/5), Clostridium species (5/5), Veillonella species (4/5), and Enterococcus faecalis (1/5). The antibiotic treatment with piperacillin/tazobactam was discontinued. As a laboratory test had shown synergy between ceftazidime/avibactam and aztreonam, treatment with ceftazidime/avibactam 2 g three times daily, in combination with aztreonam 2 g three times daily was initiated. Furthermore, metronidazole 500 mg three times daily, and colistin 4.5 MIU twice daily (after a loading dose of 9 MI) were added to the antibiotic treatment. The treatment with vancomycin was continued.

The intravenous five-drug antibiotic regimen was continued for seven weeks due to the absence of any oral alternatives for treating the multi-drug-resistant bacteria. After the period of seven weeks, the patient was discharged from the hospital with a continued antibiotic treatment of oral metronidazole 500 mg three times a day, oral trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole 800 mg twice daily, and intravenous dalbavancin 500 mg once weekly (after a loading dose of 1 g) for seven more weeks. During the outpatient follow-up period, rectal swabs were taken to verify continuous carriage of CPO. On these swabs, a carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae (OXA-48), a carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae (MBL, Table I), a carbapenemase-producing Escherichia coli (MBL, Table I), and a VREfm (vanA, Table II) were cultured. The patient was invited to participate in the FEMRO study, which investigates the effect of FMT on eradicating MRO in intestinal carriers (clinicaltrials.gov identifier: NCT05742074). Although he expressed interest and consented to participate, he was excluded due to language requirements. As an alternative, he was offered off-label FMT as an attempt to eliminate the MRO before any potential future surgeries. The patient received information about the procedure through a translator. Before FMT was given, a rectal swab showed a VREfm (vanA, Table II), and a multi-drug-resistant ESBL E. coli (Table II). No CPO was found. At the time of testing, three and a half months had passed since the last detection of CPO.

Laboratory methods

We used conventional culture methods to detect ESBL and CPO in clinical samples and rectal swabs. The samples were grown on selective media, and species identification was performed using MALDI-TOF (Bruker, Massachusetts, USA). We used the clinical breakpoints from the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing to determine susceptibility. [9] VREfm was detected by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for the vanA and vanB genes, and the growth of E. faecium was confirmed by culture. ESBL was confirmed either by an inhibition zone between the amoxicillin/clavulanic acid and cefpodoxime, or with MAST® disks (Mast Diagnostics, Amiens, France). To confirm a finding of CPO and to detect a specific resistance mechanism, we performed NG-Test CARBA 5 (NG Biotech, Guipry, France), Coris OXA-23 K-SeT (CORIS Bioconcept, Gembloux, Belgium), and/or a ROSCO PKC/MBL (ROSCO Diagnostica, Albertslund, Denmark) tests.

The microbiome samples were analysed at the National Laboratory using the 16S/18S sequencing in accordance with methods previously described. [10] Briefly, the amplification PCR was performed with the PrimeSTAR® HS DNA Polymerase premix (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan), and we used the Quant-IT™ dsDNA High Sensitive Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) for DNA quantification and the preparation of an equimolar pooled amplicon library. After removal of large and unwanted DNA amplicons, sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq desktop sequencer (Illumina Inc., San Diego, California, USA). Finally, we used BION (http://box.com/bion) for the processing of raw reads and taxonomical assignment using the Ribosomal Database Project ver. 1.10.05 database. [11] Data analysis to investigate the changes in the microbiota profile between samples was performed in R (ver. 4.4.3). Alpha diversity of each sample was evaluated using the Shannon diversity index by rarefying all samples to 18,000 reads, allowing for comparison between samples with uneven sequencing depth. For community comparison, only amplicon sequence variants with greater than 1% relative abundance were included in the analyses and visualised using the package ggplot2.

Intervention and outcome

The FMT capsules were produced at the Department of Infectious Diseases at Copenhagen University Hospital Hvidovre. We used 50 g of faecal material from one healthy donor to make one portion of 30 capsules. [12] The donor faeces were tested as recommended by Danish and European guidelines [13,14]. The patient was instructed to fast for six hours before the intervention, avoiding food and dairy products, and to refrain from liquids for two hours prior. Thirty minutes before the intervention, he was given metoclopramide 10 mg. The intervention consisted of one portion of capsules (30 capsules made from 50 g faecal material). The capsules were administered frozen and consumed on the same day within 30 min. We used the same donor for both interventions.

The follow-up screening samples after two and 11 weeks post-FMT were negative with no findings of VREfm, CPO, or ESBL Enterobacterales. The patient was scheduled for a long-term follow-up visit, but it was delayed due to travel abroad. Upon his return to Denmark, the rectal swab grew with an ESBL K. pneumoniae (Table II). Since this bacterium revealed a new mechanism than the ones we had seen before, we assumed it was acquired during his travels.

We offered the patient an additional dose of FMT capsules from the same donor, which he accepted. Follow-up screening samples taken after four and 26 weeks were negative, with no evidence of the previously identified MRO, indicating another decolonisation. Six weeks post-FMT, the patient was hospitalised for a dental abscess, which was surgically drained and treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics including vancomycin, ceftazidime/avibactam, aztreonam, cefuroxime, metronidazole, colistin, and amoxicillin/clavulanic acid during a period of 19 days. The pus from the abscess was culture-negative for MRO. When comparing alpha diversity, donor samples consistently exhibited higher Shannon diversity than those of the patient. In the patient, alpha diversity initially declined in the weeks following the intervention. However, by 11 weeks postintervention, diversity had rebounded, suggesting that FMT induced an initial disruption in microbial diversity, followed by a gradual re-establishment of a new community structure (Table SI). This is in contrast to the results of a French intervention study on CPO carriers, where the diversity had increased at two weeks post-FMT in the participants who decolonised. However, the participants in this study are not comparable to our patient, as they had a median age of 72.5 years old and a median Charlson comorbidity score of 5. [15].

Discussion

In our patient, multiple MRO were eradicated following intervention with FMT. This is consistent with the results of previous case reports [3,4]. A key strength of our study is the decolonisation of a newly acquired MRO with the second portion of FMT capsules. The fact that we were able to decolonise MRO twice demonstrates the efficacy of the intervention in our patient. Our study is limited by the single-patient design, and we cannot discount natural decolonisation as the cause behind the negative follow-up samples. We would recommend investigating the efficacy of FMT on the eradication of MRO in larger, randomised, controlled clinical trials.

Our patient had suffered from a severe infection with CPO, and we found a total of six different CPO in clinical samples and rectal swabs with four different carbapenemases (KPC, OXA-48, IMP, and NDM/MBL, refer to Table I). His infection required long-term treatment with last-resort antibiotics and surgical intervention. [1] This case illustrates the challenges we face in the treatment of infections with MRO, and particularly with CPO, when only last-resort antibiotics are an option. Furthermore, we would expect the treatment with broad-spectrum antibiotics to have an important negative impact on the gut microbiome composition. [16] For research purposes, it would have been interesting to have performed microbiome analysis prior to, during, and after the five-drug antibiotic regimen for comparison with the pre- and post-FMT microbiome analysis.

The finding of co-carriage or co-infection with multiple CPO has also been described also in other patients evacuated from the war in Ukraine to Denmark, Germany (a US military hospital), and the Netherlands [[17], [18], [19]]. Salmanov et al. conducted a multi-centre study on nosocomial infections in Ukraine from 2019–2021. In this study, 53.6 % of K. pneumoniae isolates were resistant to carbapenems. [20] Another study investigated nosocomial infections after the full-scale Russian invasion in 2022. The authors included isolates from patients with war injuries, and from new-born babies with ventilator-associated pneumonia. They found 58 % of Gram-negative isolates to be meropenem-resistant. [21] The improvement of infection prevention and control measures, as well as the detection of antibiotic susceptibility in laboratories are points of attention for the Center for Public Health of Ukraine. Since 2022, they have co-operated with international and local partners to implement interventions, currently in three Ukrainian regions. [22].

When we screened our patient before the first intervention, we did not find any CPO on the rectal swab. The following screening samples were also negative for CPO, and we assume this is due to natural decolonisation of the CPO. Ideally, we would have given FMT when our patient was still an intestinal carrier of CPO and at risk of developing an infection with CPO. The optimal use of FMT as an intervention to eradicate MRO in intestinal carriers is between the confirmation of carriage, and before the event of an invasive infection.

In conclusion, we have reported the decolonisation of multiple MRO in a patient, who received two interventions with FMT. Immediately before the first intervention, the patient was an intestinal carrier of multi-drug-resistant ESBL and VREfm. Both were eradicated after one portion of FMT capsules. After travelling abroad, the patient acquired a different ESBL strain, which was also eradicated following a second round of FMT capsules. This decolonisation of MRO on two separate occasions highlights the effectiveness of FMT as a tool to manipulate the microbiome in intestinal carriers of MRO. The efficacy of FMT on the decolonisation of MRO should be investigated in larger, randomised controlled clinical trials.
