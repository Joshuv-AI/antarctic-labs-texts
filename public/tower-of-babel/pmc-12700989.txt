Bridging thrombolysis versus direct thrombectomy in acute ischemic stroke with large vessel occlusion: a network meta-analysis
Authors: Jing Huang, Pan Tao, Chi Meng, Le-Le Qin, Wei Chen, Wen-Ye Wu, Yu-Qin Ran, Chang-Qing Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEndovascular treatment (EVT) is the standard of care for acute ischemic stroke caused by large-vessel occlusion (AIS-LVO). The benefit of intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) before EVT remains uncertain, particularly for patients presenting directly to EVT-capable centers. Tenecteplase, with simpler administration and potential to improve early reperfusion, may offer an alternative to alteplase. This network meta-analysis evaluates the efficacy and safety of bridging therapy with different doses of alteplase or tenecteplase compared with direct EVT within 4.5 h of stroke onset.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted across the MEDLINE, Embase, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials databases from their inception through September 20, 2025, to identify eligible randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Studies investigating EVT alone or EVT preceded by IVT with either alteplase or tenecteplase were included. The network meta-analysis was performed within a Bayesian framework, utilizing a fixed-effects model for the primary analysis.ResultsNine RCTs comprising 3386 patients were included in the network meta-analysis. Regarding efficacy outcomes, 0.25 mg/kg tenecteplase + EVT demonstrated superior functional outcomes to EVT alone and 0.9 mg/kg alteplase + EVT, evidenced by significantly higher rates of functional independence (mRS 0–2) (OR 1.52, 95% CrI 1.14–2.04 vs. EVT alone; OR 1.42, 95% CrI 1.03–1.94 vs. alteplase + EVT) and freedom from disability (mRS 0–1) (OR 1.38, 95% CrI 1.02–1.89 vs. EVT alone; OR 1.43, 95% CrI 1.02–2.01 vs. alteplase + EVT). Based on the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA), 0.4 mg/kg tenecteplase + EVT ranked highest for functional independence (mRS 0–2) with a SUCRA value of 0.855, while 0.25 mg/kg tenecteplase + EVT ranked second (SUCRA = 0.811). For successful reperfusion, 0.9 mg/kg alteplase + EVT demonstrated superiority over EVT alone (OR 1.28, 95% CrI 1.01–1.63). In terms of safety outcomes, 0.4 mg/kg tenecteplase + EVT was associated with a significantly increased risk of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage compared with EVT alone (OR 5.29, 95% CrI 1.03–44.56), while 0.6 mg/kg alteplase + EVT showed a higher risk of any intracranial hemorrhage versus EVT alone (OR 2.02, 95% CrI 1.15–3.57). No statistically significant differences in death within 90 days were observed among any treatment strategies.ConclusionsIn comprehensive consideration of efficacy and safety, 0.25 mg/kg tenecteplase + EVT may represent a preferable treatment option for AIS-LVO patients compared to other reperfusion strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00415-025-13567-2.

Introduction

Large-vessel occlusion (LVO) accounts for approximately 30.1% of all acute ischemic strokes (AIS) and represents a subtype associated with markedly increased mortality and disability [1–3]. The management of acute ischemic stroke due to large-vessel occlusion (AIS-LVO) has been transformed by endovascular treatment (EVT), which is now the cornerstone of treatment for eligible patients [4–6]. Current guidelines recommend intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) before EVT as bridging therapy for eligible AIS-LVO patients (Class I recommendation) [7, 8]. However, the clinical utility of this approach, particularly for patients presenting directly to EVT-capable centers, remains contentious. More than half of EVT-eligible patients now present directly to comprehensive stroke centers, where the marginal benefits of IVT may be offset by procedural delays, a higher risk of intracranial hemorrhage, and limited efficacy against large clot burdens [4, 9–11].

Alteplase (ALT) is the most commonly used thrombolytic agent in traditional bridging therapy and the focus of previous studies [12–17, 19, 20], but its relatively complex infusion protocol limits its suitability for first-line transport emergency care and on-site management [21]. Tenecteplase (TNK), a third-generation fibrin-specific thrombolytic, has emerged as a promising alternative to ALT, offering several pharmacokinetic advantages such as a prolonged half-life, enhanced fibrin specificity, resistance to endogenous inhibitors, and convenient single-bolus administration [22]. Recent clinical evidence [23] indicates that TNK may achieve superior early reperfusion compared to ALT in bridging therapy; however, its incremental benefit over direct EVT has not yet been conclusively established [24]. Moreover, the optimal TNK dosing regimen (0.25 mg/kg vs. 0.4 mg/kg) for AIS-LVO patients undergoing bridging therapy remains undetermined, and significant evidence gaps persist in this area. These limitations underscore the need for rigorous comparative evaluations to guide clinical decision-making.

Although six landmark randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [12–17] have compared EVT alone versus IVT plus EVT, an Individual Patient Data Meta-Analysis (IRIS) [18] found no statistically significant difference in the primary functional outcome between these two strategies, challenging the universal applicability of bridging therapy in contemporary stroke workflows. However, the IRIS analysis was limited to a direct comparison between bridging therapies and EVT alone. Furthermore, the recently published BRIDGE-TNK trial [25] demonstrated that bridging therapy with TNK was superior to EVT alone in achieving functional independence. Given these conflicting findings and the availability of new evidence, it remains unclear whether bridging therapy with different thrombolytic agents and at different doses confers distinct efficacy and safety profiles compared with direct EVT alone. Therefore, we performed a network meta-analysis (NMA) to evaluate the efficacy and safety of bridging therapy using different doses of TNK or different doses of ALT, compared with direct EVT in patients with AIS-LVO within 4.5 h.

Methods

Protocol and registration

Our analysis was guided by principles of the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) Extension Statement [26]. The study protocol has been registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251163943).

Search strategy and data extraction

A comprehensive literature search (strategy in Supplementary Appendix S1) was conducted across MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials from inception until September 20, 2025. Additionally, we manually screened the reference lists of relevant meta-analyses, systematic reviews, and conference abstracts to identify ongoing, unpublished, or additional eligible studies. Following duplicate removal, two independent reviewers (J.H. and P.T.) conducted the title/abstract screening and data extraction. The extracted data encompassed study characteristics such as authorship, design, publication year, country, sample size, interventions, and outcomes. Any disagreements were resolved through consensus or, when necessary, by arbitration from a third reviewer (C.-Q.Z.). Efforts were made to acquire missing data by contacting the original investigators.

Selection criteria

The search strategy was based on the PICOS principle (P: population/patient, I: intervention, C: control/comparison, O: outcome, S: study design) [27]. For patients, the inclusion criteria comprised those diagnosed with AIS-LVO, with no restrictions regarding age, gender, or geographic region. The interventions encompassed direct EVT alone and bridging therapy employing different dosing strategies of TNK or ALT in combination with EVT. The outcomes included assessment of functional independence (mRS 0–2) at 90 days, freedom from disability (mRS 0–1) at 90 days, successful reperfusion (TICI 2b–3) after completion of EVT, all-cause mortality at follow-up, and the incidence of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) or any intracranial hemorrhage (aICH). Only RCTs were included as the eligible study design; studies not designed as RCTs were excluded.

Outcomes

The study assessed both efficacy and safety outcomes, with the primary efficacy endpoint being the proportion of participants who achieved functional independence, defined as a mRS score of 0–2 at 90 days. Secondary efficacy endpoints included the proportion of participants attaining freedom from disability, defined as an mRS score of 0–1 at 90 days, as well as the rate of successful reperfusion, defined as a Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (TICI) grade of 2b–3 following the end of EVT. Safety outcomes encompassed all-cause mortality during follow-up, the incidence of sICH, and the incidence of aICH.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was independently assessed by two reviewers (J.H. and L.-L.Q.) who used the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for randomized clinical trials (RoB 2) [28]. The certainty of the evidence was assessed using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis framework (CINeMA; accessible at https://cinema.ispm.unibe.ch/), which evaluates confidence in NMA estimates across six key domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence [29, 30]. Any discrepancies in the CINeMA assessments were resolved through consensus discussions among the reviewers.

In addition, to further evaluate the risk of bias specifically for the NMA and the overall systematic review, we performed a combined assessment using Risk of Bias in Systematic Reviews (ROBIS) [31] and the Risk of Bias in Network Meta-Analysis (RoB-NMA) [32] tool, as recommended for comprehensive NMA evaluation. ROBIS was used to evaluate bias in study eligibility, study identification and selection, and data collection and appraisal. RoB-NMA assessed potential biases related to network geometry, effect modifiers, and statistical synthesis. Judgments were independently made by the same reviewers, and discrepancies were resolved by discussion. Details of this combined assessment are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical analysis

The comparative effectiveness of the interventions was evaluated using a Bayesian NMA in line with recommended guidelines [33, 34]. This methodology synthesizes both direct and indirect evidence, facilitating the coherent comparison of multiple interventions within a single framework. All Bayesian computations were carried out in the R statistical environment (v4.2.3) utilizing the gemtc package [35]. The model was fitted using Markov chain Monte Carlo methods with a Gibbs sampler, involving four independent chains. Each chain underwent a burn-in phase of 20,000 iterations, followed by 50,000 production iterations. Effect estimates for binary outcomes are expressed as odds ratios (ORs) accompanied by 95% credible intervals (CrIs). The probabilities of intervention rankings were summarized using the Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking Curve (SUCRA). Heterogeneity was assessed using I2 statistics, and a fixed-effect model was employed when I2 was below 50%. The agreement between direct and indirect evidence was examined via the node-splitting approach, and funnel plots were generated in R to explore potential publication bias.

For the sensitivity analysis, we first reanalyzed the data after excluding the DIRECT-SAFE trial [13] due to its use of a mixed thrombolytic regimen in the bridging arm without separate outcome reporting for each agent. Additionally, we conducted a separate sensitivity analysis by excluding studies [23, 25] identified as primary sources of high heterogeneity or with poor network connectivity. For the subgroup analysis, we first categorized trials based on circulatory territory according to their enrolled populations: those [12, 14–17] enrolling only anterior circulation AIS-LVO, and those [13, 23, 25, 36] enrolling patients with non-restricted circulation. Additionally, a second subgroup analysis categorized trials based on the median onset-to-randomization (OTR) time of enrolled patients into those [15, 17, 25] with a median OTR ≥ 155 min and those [12–14, 16, 23, 36] with a median OTR < 155 min. The lack of individual-level data imposed constraints that were limited on the subgroup analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the included literature

Our systematic search identified 5330 potentially relevant records. Following a screening process, nine RCTs met the predefined inclusion criteria [12–17, 23, 25, 36]. These trials enrolled a total of 3386 patients with AIS-LVO, who were included in the present network meta-analysis (Fig. 1). The evaluated interventions comprised five treatment strategies: EVT alone, 0.6 mg/kg ALT + EVT, 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT, 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT, and 0.4 mg/kg TNK + EVT. Data on the prespecified outcomes were reported by most studies, with two exceptions [23, 36] that did not report aICH, and one RCT [14] that did not report a 90-day functional outcome (mRS 0–1). The baseline characteristics of the included participants are summarized in Table 1. Across the studies, the median age ranged from 69 to 76 years, and the proportion of female participants varied between approximately 37% and 51%. Quality assessment

To clearly represent the comparability of interventions within the NMA, a network plot was constructed (Fig. 2). Both fixed-effect and random-effects network meta-analysis models were fitted for all outcomes (Table S1). Model selection was guided by a comprehensive evaluation, including global heterogeneity (I2), deviance information criterion (DIC), model complexity (pD), and the level of agreement between observed and fitted deviance. Across outcomes, heterogeneity was consistently low (all I2 ≤ 18%), and the fixed-effect model demonstrated lower or comparable DIC values—indicating superior or similar model fit with fewer effective parameters. Moreover, random-effects models yielded effect estimates that were highly consistent with those from the fixed-effect analyses. Given the relatively sparse network geometry with limited closed loops, a random-effects structure could introduce unnecessary uncertainty and reduced precision. Therefore, the fixed-effect model was selected as the primary analytical approach to ensure more stable estimates. The potential scale reduction factors (PSRFs) were consistently close to 1, confirming that a sufficient number of iterations had been conducted to achieve satisfactory convergence. This was further supported by trace plots and density plots (Supplementary Fig. S1). According to the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 assessment, three studies were rated as having “some concerns” primarily due to deviations from intended interventions, while the remaining studies were rated at low risk of bias (Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3). Based on the CINeMA framework evaluation, the certainty of evidence for the primary efficacy outcome was rated as Moderate for the comparisons between 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT and both EVT alone and 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT. Certainty for all other comparisons was Low or Very Low. Key factors leading to downgrades included imprecision (limited sample sizes), heterogeneity (clinical and methodological differences across trials), and incoherence (discrepancies between direct and indirect effects for some outcomes). Notably, certain limitations were driven by the detailed assessment standards employed. For example, for the aICH outcome, due to the absence of closed loops in the evidence network, all comparisons were automatically downgraded for major incoherence concerns. In addition, we performed a combined risk of bias assessment for the systematic review and network meta-analysis using ROBIS and RoB-NMA, as recommended for evaluating NMA reliability (Supplementary Table S3). The ROBIS assessment indicated low concerns across all domains for study eligibility, identification and selection of studies, and data collection and study appraisal. RoB-NMA evaluation revealed some concerns regarding network geometry, effect modifiers, and statistical synthesis, with overall judgments of “some concerns” for bias in the results and “concerns” for bias in conclusions. This comprehensive assessment supports the robustness and credibility of the NMA findings while acknowledging areas that require cautious interpretation.

Overall, the evidence supporting the key outcomes of functional independence (mRS 0–2) and freedom from disability (mRS 0–1) remains reasonably credible, particularly regarding the assessment of 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT versus either EVT alone or 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT. Other outcome estimates, particularly those based on sparser evidence, should be interpreted with more caution. Detailed evaluations are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Efficacy outcomes

Functional independence (mRS 0–2) at 90 days

Compared with EVT alone, only 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT among the four bridging therapies demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in achieving functional independence (OR = 1.52, 95% CrI = 1.14–2.04), while no significant differences were observed for 0.4 mg/kg TNK + EVT, 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT, or 0.6 mg/kg ALT + EVT. Moreover, most direct comparisons among the bridging therapies revealed no statistically significant differences, with the notable exception that 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT was associated with significantly higher odds of functional independence than 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT (OR = 1.42, 95% CrI = 1.03–1.94) (Table 2). The SUCRAs indicate a ranking of the potential best treatments as 0.4 mg/kg TNK + EVT (0.855), 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT (0.811), 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT (0.396), EVT alone (0.211), and 0.6 mg/kg ALT + EVT (0.197) (Fig. 3A). Notably, although the SUCRA value (0.855) indicated a strong probability of beneficial effect for 0.4 mg/kg TNK + EVT, its comparison with EVT alone reached only borderline statistical significance (OR = 1.70, 95% CrI = 0.99–2.93). Freedom from disability (mRS 0–1) at 90 days

Compared with EVT alone, 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT demonstrated significantly increased efficacy in achieving freedom from disability at 90 days (OR = 1.38, 95% CrI = 1.02–1.89) (Table 2). No other bridging therapies demonstrated statistically significant differences compared to EVT alone. Among the bridging therapies, 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT was associated with significantly higher odds of freedom from disability compared to 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT (OR = 1.43, 95% CrI = 1.02–2.01). No other statistically significant differences were observed between the various bridging therapies. The SUCRAs indicate a ranking of the potential best treatments as 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT (0.787), 0.4 mg/kg TNK + EVT (0.732), 0.6 mg/kg ALT + EVT (0.532), EVT alone (0.263), and 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT (0.187) (Fig. 3B).

Successful reperfusion

Compared with EVT alone, 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT demonstrated significantly improved successful reperfusion rates (OR = 1.28, 95% CrI = 1.01–1.63) (Table 2). No other bridging therapies demonstrated statistically significant differences compared to EVT alone. Among the bridging therapies, no statistically significant differences were observed between the various bridging therapies. The SUCRAs indicate a ranking of the potential best treatments as 0.4 mg/kg TNK + EVT (0.818), 0.6 mg/kg ALT + EVT (0.644), 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT (0.592), 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT (0.249), and EVT alone (0.197) (Fig. 3C).

Safety outcomes

Death within 90 days

For mortality outcome at 90 days, no statistically significant differences were observed in comparisons between any bridging therapy and EVT alone, or across the different bridging therapies. The SUCRAs indicate a ranking of mortality risk as 0.4 mg/kg TNK + EVT (0.662), 0.6 mg/kg ALT + EVT (0.564), EVT alone (0.475), 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT (0.400), and 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT (0.398) (Fig. 3D).

Assessment of heterogeneity, inconsistency, and publication bias

The assessment of heterogeneity and inconsistency for each outcome is summarized in Supplementary Figs. S4 and S5. With the exception of notable heterogeneity observed in the comparisons between 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT and either EVT alone or 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT for the outcomes of successful reperfusion and death within 90 days, heterogeneity was generally low (I2 < 25%) across all other outcomes. No evidence of inconsistency was detected in any comparison.

The observed high I2 values for the comparisons involving 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT versus EVT alone or 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT, together with non-significant inconsistency on node-splitting analysis, suggest that the substantial heterogeneity likely stems from within the indirect evidence loops contributing to these contrasts (potentially due to variations in study design among the contributing trials) or from the fact that each of these two comparisons is informed by only a single direct study [23, 25]. However, these findings do not affect the main conclusions of our study.

Publication bias and small-study effects were assessed using comparison-adjusted funnel plots. Visual inspection revealed no significant asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. S6), suggesting no substantial publication bias. Sensitivity analysis

After excluding the DIRECT-SAFE trial [13], which employed a mixed thrombolytic regimen in the bridging therapy arm (0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT [83%] or 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT [17%]) without reporting outcomes separately for each agent, a sensitivity analysis was conducted for all six outcomes. Regarding the outcome of freedom from disability (mRS 0–1) at 90 days, the original result for 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT versus EVT alone was statistically significant (OR = 1.38, 95% CrI 1.02–1.89). After exclusion, the result became non-significant (OR = 1.35, 95% CrI 0.98–1.84), a change potentially attributable to reduced transitivity following the removal of this study. This shift did not alter the primary conclusions, and all other outcomes along with the SUCRA rankings remained consistent. Furthermore, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding the studies [23, 25] identified as the source of high heterogeneity in the outcomes of successful reperfusion and death within 90 days (along with one study [36] that was poorly connected to the evidence network). Following this exclusion, the SUCRA ranking for 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT surpassed that of 0.6 mg/kg ALT + EVT in successful reperfusion, a pattern that may reflect a dose–response relationship, although the direct comparison between the two regimens remained statistically non-significant. All other outcomes were unchanged.

Subgroup analysis

We conducted a subgroup analysis of five studies [12, 14–17] that exclusively enrolled patients with LVO confined to the anterior circulation, and four studies [13, 23, 25, 36] that included patients with LVO in the non-restricted circulation. Because few interventions were shared across subgroups, comparisons between subgroups were limited to describing differences relative to the overall results. In the anterior circulation subgroup, only three interventions (EVT alone, 0.6 mg/kg ALT + EVT, and 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT) were included. Compared with the overall analysis, no significant changes were observed for any outcomes, except for variations in SUCRA rankings. For successful reperfusion, 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT (SUCRA 0.72) now ranked highest, surpassing 0.6 mg/kg ALT + EVT (SUCRA 0.674). For 90-day mortality, EVT alone (SUCRA 0.622) ranked first, exceeding 0.6 mg/kg ALT + EVT (SUCRA 0.616). These changes are likely attributable to the exclusion of two highly heterogeneous studies [23, 25], rather than differences in LVO location, consistent with the findings of the sensitivity analysis. In the non-restricted circulation subgroup, four interventions (EVT alone, 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT, 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT, and 0.4 mg/kg TNK + EVT) were included. Notably, the comparison of 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT versus EVT alone for successful reperfusion lost statistical significance (OR = 0.78, 95% CrI = 0.42–1.41) and the effect direction reversed in contrast to the overall results. These findings suggest that 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT may achieve greater reperfusion in patients with anterior-circulation AIS-LVO. No significant changes were observed for other outcomes.

Additionally, a second subgroup analysis was performed, focusing on three studies [15, 17, 25] that exclusively enrolled patients with a median OTR ≥ 155 min, and six studies [12–14, 16, 23, 36] that included patients with a median OTR < 155 min. In the median OTR ≥ 155 min subgroup, only three interventions (EVT alone, 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT, and 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT) were included, whereas the median OTR < 155 min subgroup included EVT alone and all available bridging therapies. Overall, no statistically significant and effect direction-inconsistent changes from the main analysis were observed in either subgroup. For instance, in the median OTR ≥ 155 min subgroup, 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT was associated with a significantly higher risk of aICH compared to EVT alone (OR = 1.31, 95% CrI = 1.00–1.73). However, since the direction of this effect was consistent with the overall findings and no reversals were identified in the corresponding subgroup, we consider that this isolated finding may be attributable to the reduced sample size inherent in subgroup analyses. All the specific subgroup analysis results are presented in Supplementary Table S4.

Discussion

This Bayesian NMA of RCTs indicates that TNK bridging therapy, particularly at 0.25 mg/kg, may offer a favorable balance of functional benefit and safety compared with EVT alone or ALT bridging therapy, although differences between TNK and ALT regimens remain outcome-dependent and imprecisely estimated. While SUCRAs ranked 0.4 mg/kg TNK + EVT highest for functional independence (mRS 0–2) and reperfusion, this regimen also showed the greatest risks of sICH and mortality. In contrast, 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT achieved statistically superior functional outcomes (mRS 0–2; mRS 0–1) compared with EVT alone and 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT, while maintaining a more favorable safety profile. SUCRAs also suggested that EVT alone had the lowest sICH risk and that 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT had the lowest mortality risk.

In terms of functional outcomes, 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT demonstrated superior efficacy over EVT alone and 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT for both functional independence (mRS 0–2) and freedom from disability (mRS 0–1), consistent with findings from BRIDGE-TNK [25] and EXTEND-IA TNK Part 1 [23]. SUCRAs indicated that 0.4 mg/kg TNK + EVT ranked highest for functional independence (mRS 0–2) and 0.25 mg/kg for freedom from disability (mRS 0–1), with both TNK-based regimens outperforming ALT + EVT and EVT alone. This advantage may reflect the single-bolus administration of TNK, which shortens door-to-needle time compared with the 1-h ALT infusion [23], as well as its longer half-life and greater fibrin specificity [22, 37–39]. Regarding reperfusion, 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT showed a statistically significant benefit over EVT alone, and all bridging strategies ranked higher than EVT alone in SUCRA analysis, with 0.4 mg/kg TNK + EVT achieving the highest probability. This may be explained by the fact that IVT in bridging therapies reduces thrombus burden and lysizes microthrombi that EVT alone cannot address [40, 41]. In the circulatory-territory subgroup analysis, the reperfusion benefit of 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT over EVT alone was evident only in the anterior-circulation subgroup, whereas the non-restricted subgroup showed a nonsignificant trend favoring EVT alone. This suggests that 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT may confer greater reperfusion benefit in anterior circulation occlusions, possibly reflecting differences in collateral status or thrombus characteristics [42–44].

Importantly, the findings for the 0.4 mg/kg TNK regimen should be interpreted with caution. Although this dose ranked favorably for functional independence (mRS 0–2) and reperfusion, its higher hemorrhage risk has limited its use in current practice. International guidelines do not recommend 0.4 mg/kg TNK for IVT-eligible patients within 4.5 h and consider it only for carefully selected cases without major intracranial occlusion [45, 46], consistent with our safety results. SUCRAs for functional outcomes and reperfusion were not fully aligned across bridging strategies. For example, although 0.6 and 0.9 mg/kg ALT achieved higher angiographic reperfusion rates, this did not translate into better functional outcomes. This discrepancy may be explained by the fact that angiographic recanalization does not necessarily translate into effective microvascular reperfusion or improved tissue viability, reflecting the phenomenon of futile recanalization [47–49]. In contrast, 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT may support more effective microcirculatory perfusion and reduce reperfusion injury due to its pharmacological advantages [22, 37–39].

In terms of safety, 0.4 mg/kg TNK + EVT was associated with higher risks of sICH and mortality compared with other strategies, whereas 0.6 mg/kg ALT + EVT had the highest probability of aICH. No significant differences in all-cause mortality were observed across most strategies. SUCRAs indicated that 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT had the lowest mortality probability, followed closely by 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT (SUCRA difference: 0.002; Fig. 3D). These findings align with the EXTEND-IA TNK Part 1 trial [23] and prior studies [21, 50]. According to the NOR­TEST 2 Part A trial [51], 0.4 mg/kg TNK thrombolysis was associated with higher hemorrhage and mortality risks than 0.9 mg/kg ALT, leading to early termination of the study. The elevated risks with 0.4 mg/kg TNK may reflect its rapid administration, longer half-life, and higher likelihood of inducing blood–brain barrier disruption and vasogenic edema [37–39, 51]. For aICH (data were missing for the 0.4 mg/kg TNK + EVT), 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT showed the lowest risk, with a statistically significant reduction compared to 0.6 mg/kg ALT + EVT, which had the highest risk. The increased risk with 0.6 mg/kg ALT + EVT may partly reflect the older average age (76 years) of patients in the SKIP trial [12, 52, 53].

As with any NMA, several limitations should be considered. First, the main limitations were the insufficient RCTs and imbalances between the evidence. Most studies focused on 0.9 mg/kg ALT + EVT and 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT, with some comparisons (0.6 mg/kg ALT vs. EVT alone; 0.4 mg/kg TNK vs. 0.25 mg/kg TNK) including only a single RCT, limiting assessment of publication bias and consistency. Second, there were differences in trial design and actual treatment practices across study centers (Table 1), including superiority versus non-inferiority design, time from stroke onset to randomization, imaging assessment criteria, and specifics of endovascular procedure techniques and devices used. Furthermore, the definition of symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage varied across the included studies, with five studies [13, 16, 17, 25] applying the Heidelberg criteria and others using either the SIT-MOST or ECASS II criteria. Finally, the CINeMA assessment yielded low or very low certainty of evidence for most comparisons. This is primarily attributable to concerns in key domains such as Imprecision, Heterogeneity, and Incoherence, reflecting the intrinsic limitations of the current evidence network. These findings underscore the necessity for future large-scale, high-quality RCTs.

Conclusion

In summary, this NMA compared bridging therapy with different doses of TNK or ALT against direct EVT. Although 0.4 mg/kg TNK + EVT demonstrated superior functional outcomes and reperfusion rates in AIS-LVO patients, it was also associated with an increased risk of mortality and intracranial hemorrhage. Consequently, current international guidelines do not recommend its use. Considering its simpler administration and a better balance between efficacy and safety, 0.25 mg/kg TNK + EVT may represent the preferred treatment option for these patients. Further investigation through rigorously designed, head-to-head randomized controlled trials with standardized protocols is warranted to validate these findings.
