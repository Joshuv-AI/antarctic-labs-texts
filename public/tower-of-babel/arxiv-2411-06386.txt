A formula for eigenvalues of integral Cayley graphs over abelian groups
Priya, Monu Kadyan

Abstract




Abstract

Let
Z
be an abelian group,
x\in Z
, and
[x]=\{y:\langle x\rangle=\langle y\rangle\}
. A graph is called integral if all its eigenvalues are integers. It is known that a Cayley graph is integral if and only if its connection set can be express as union of the sets
[x]
. In this paper, we determine an algebraic formula for eigenvalues of the integral Cayley graph when the connection set is
[x]
. This formula involves an analogue of M
\ddot{\text{o}}
bius function.

Keywords.
Eigenvalue of Cayley graph, Character of abelian group

Mathematics Subject Classifications:
05C50, 20C15.

1
Introduction

Eigenvalues of Cayley graphs gained popularity due to their significance in algebraic graph theory and applications in expanders, chemical graph theory, and quantum computing. A large number of results on eigenvalues of Cayley graphs was made in the last few decades, see
[
3
]
for a survey. Many algebraic methods, including representation theory and association schemes can be used to study the eigenvalue of Cayley graphs. In this paper, we use an analogue of M
\ddot{\text{o}}
bius inversion formula to construct an algebraic formula for the eigenvalues of integral Cayley graphs.

Throughout this paper, we consider
Z
to be an abelian group of order
n
. For a positive integer
r
and
x\in Z
, we write
rx
to denote the
r
times sum of
x
to itself, additive inverse of
x
by
-x
. We use
\langle x\rangle
to denote the cyclic group generated by
x
.

Let
S
be a close under inverse subset of
Z
. The
Cayley graph

{\rm Cay}(Z,S)
is an undirected graph, where
V({\rm Cay}(Z,S))=Z
and

E({\rm Cay}(Z,S))=\{(a,b)\colon a,b\in Z,b-a\in S\}.

The
eigenvalues
of the graph are the eigenvalues of its
(0,1)
-adjacency matrix. A graph is called
integral
if all its eigenvalues are integers.

Consider the equivalence relation
\sim
on the abelian group
Z
such that
x\sim y
if and only if
y=mx
for some integer
m
such that
m
is co-prime to the order of
x
. For any
x\in Z
, we use
[x]
to denote the equivalence class of
x
with respect to the relation
\sim
. That is,
[x]=\{y:\langle x\rangle=\langle y\rangle\}
. Define
\mathcal{A}(x)
to be a collection of the representatives of the equivalence classes of
\sim
in the cyclic group
\langle x\rangle
. For example, if
Z=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and
x=1
, then
\mathcal{A}(1)=\{d:d\mbox{ divides }n\}
.

In 1982, Bridge and Meena gave a characterisation of a Cayley graph whose all eigenvalues are integers. This result is as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

(
[
2
]
)
Let
Z
be an abelian group and
S
be a close under inverse subset of
Z
. The Cayley graph
\text{Cay}(Z,S)
is integral if and only if
S=[x_{1}]\cup[x_{2}]\cup\ldots\cup[x_{r}]
for some
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r}\in S
.

Consider
Z=\mathbb{Z}_{n_{1}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{n_{2}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{n_{k}}
. For
\alpha\in Z
, define a character
\psi_{\alpha}:Z\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
such that

\psi_{\alpha}(y)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\omega_{n_{j}}^{\alpha_{j}y_{j}}\textnormal{ for all $\alpha=(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{k}),~y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\in Z$},

and
\omega_{n_{j}}=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi\mathbf{i}}{n_{j}}\right)
. Note that
\{\psi_{\alpha}:\alpha\in Z\}
is the complete set of non-equivalent irreducible characters of the abelian group
Z
. For any
\alpha,x\in Z
, define

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}(x):=\sum_{s\in[x]}\psi_{\alpha}(s).

Consider
Z=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and
x=1
. We have

C_{\alpha}(1)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq s\leq n\\
\gcd(s,n)=1\end{subarray}}\omega_{n}^{s\alpha}=R_{n}(\alpha),

where
R_{n}(\alpha)
is the Ramanujan sum. It was introduced by Ramanujan in 1918. For more information, see
[
4
]
. It is known that

R_{n}(\alpha)=\varphi(n)\frac{\dot{\mu}({\frac{n}{\delta_{\alpha}}})}{\varphi(\frac{n}{\delta_{\alpha}})},

where
\delta_{\alpha}=\gcd(n,\alpha)
,
\dot{\mu}
is classic M
\ddot{\text{o}}
bius function, and
\varphi
is Euler’s phi function. Hence, we have a formula for
C_{\alpha}(1)
. Therefore, it is natural to ask whether a similar formula can be obtained for
C_{\alpha}(x)
in which the group
Z
is non-cyclic. In this paper, we answer it in an affirmative form. In 1979, Babai used characters to determine the eigenvalues of a Cayley graph. This result is as follows.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
1
]
)
.

Let
Z
be an abelian group and
S
be a close under inverse subset of
Z
. The eigenvalue of Cayley graph
\operatorname{Cay}(Z,S)
is
\{\lambda_{\alpha}\colon\alpha\in Z\}
, where

\lambda_{\alpha}=\sum_{s\in S}\psi_{\alpha}(s)\textnormal{ for each }\alpha\in Z.

By Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
, it is clear that
C_{\alpha}(x)
is integer and also an eigenvalue of the integral Cayley graph
\operatorname{Cay}(Z,[x])
. Thus, eigenvalues of an integral Cayley graph
\operatorname{Cay}(Z,S)
can be express as

\displaystyle\lambda_{\alpha}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{s\in[x_{i}]}\psi_{\alpha}(s)

(1)

for some
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r}\in S
. By Equation (
1
), we get

\displaystyle\lambda_{\alpha}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}C_{\alpha}(x_{i})~\textnormal{ for each }\alpha\in Z.

Hence the sum
C_{\alpha}(x)
plays important role in eigenvalues of an integral Cayley graph.

The classic M
\ddot{\text{o}}
bius function is a multiplicative function with the domain of positive integers. It was introduced by August Ferdinand M
\ddot{\text{o}}
bius in 1832. This paper discusses a similar function, taking the domain as a pair of the elements of abelian groups. We call it an analogue of M
\ddot{\text{o}}
bius function. This analogue is the following. For
x,y\in Z
and
x\in\langle y\rangle
, define

\mu(x,y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\mbox{if }\langle x\rangle=\langle y\rangle\\
(-1)^{r}&\mbox{if }\langle x\rangle=\langle q_{1}q_{2}\ldots q_{r}y\rangle\\
0&\mbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

where
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{r}
are some distinct prime divisors of the order of
y
. Note that

\mu(x,y)=\dot{\mu}\bigg(\frac{\mbox{order of }y}{\mbox{order of }x}\bigg),

where
\dot{\mu}
is classic M
\ddot{\text{o}}
bius function. Similarly, define

\varphi(x,y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\mbox{if }\langle x\rangle=\langle y\rangle\\
(q_{1}-1)(q_{2}-1)\ldots(q_{r}-1)&\mbox{if }\langle x\rangle=\langle q_{1}q_{2}\ldots q_{r}y\rangle\\
1&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

where
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{r}
are some distinct prime divisors of the order of
y
. The main result of the paper is the following.

Theorem

(Theorem
3.4
). Let
Z
be an abelian group and
\alpha,x\in Z
. Then

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=\mu(\bar{\alpha},x)~\frac{|[x]|}{\varphi(\bar{\alpha},x)},

where
\bar{\alpha}
is a generator of the cyclic group
\{y:\psi_{\alpha}(y)=1\}\cap\langle x\rangle
.

In Section 2, we present an analogue of the M
\ddot{\text{o}}
bius inversion formula. We will use it to prove the main result in Section 3.

2
Preliminaries

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
Z
be an abelian group and
x,y,\alpha\in Z
. The following statements are true:

(i)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{A}(y)\\
\langle\alpha\rangle\subseteq\langle x\rangle\end{subarray}}\mu(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\mbox{if }\langle\alpha\rangle=\langle y\rangle\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(ii)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{A}(\alpha)\\
\langle x\rangle\subseteq\langle y\rangle\end{subarray}}\mu(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\mbox{if }\langle\alpha\rangle=\langle x\rangle\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

(i)

If
\langle\alpha\rangle=\langle y\rangle
then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{A}(y)\\
\langle\alpha\rangle\subseteq\langle x\rangle\end{subarray}}\mu(x,y)=\mu(y,y)=1.

Assume that
\langle\alpha\rangle\subsetneqq\langle y\rangle
. We have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{A}(y)\\
\langle\alpha\rangle\subseteq\langle x\rangle\end{subarray}}\mu(x,y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{A}(y)\\
\langle\alpha\rangle\subseteq\langle x\rangle\\
\mu(x,y)\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mu(x,y).

Let
\langle q_{1}y\rangle,\langle q_{2}y\rangle,\ldots,\langle q_{r}y\rangle
are the only maximal subgroups of
\langle y\rangle
which contains
\langle\alpha\rangle
, where
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{r}
are some prime divisors of the order of
y
. For any
0\leq j\leq r
,
{r\choose j}
many distinct
x
exist in
\mathcal{A}(y)
with
\mu(x,y)=(-1)^{j}
(
\langle x\rangle
can be express as
j
many maximal subgroups of
\langle y\rangle
). Therefore, we get

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{A}(y)\\
\langle\alpha\rangle\subseteq\langle x\rangle\\
\mu(x,y)\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mu(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}{r\choose j}(-1)^{j}=(1-1)^{r}=0.

(ii)

If
\langle\alpha\rangle=\langle x\rangle
then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{A}(\langle\alpha\rangle)\\
\langle x\rangle\subseteq\langle y\rangle\end{subarray}}\mu(x,y)=\mu(\alpha,\alpha)=1.

Assume that
\langle x\rangle\subsetneqq\langle\alpha\rangle
.
Then
\langle x\rangle=\langle q_{1}^{b_{1}}q_{2}^{b_{2}}\ldots q_{r}^{b_{r}}\alpha\rangle
, where
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{r}
are some distinct prime divisors such that
q_{i}^{b_{i}}
divides the order of
\alpha
and
b_{i}\geq 1
for each
i=1,2,\ldots,r
. Let
\alpha^{\prime}=q_{1}^{b_{1}-1}q_{2}^{b_{2}-1}\ldots q_{r}^{b_{r}-1}\alpha
. We get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{A}(\alpha)\\
\langle x\rangle\subseteq\langle y\rangle\end{subarray}}\mu(x,y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{A}(\alpha)\\
\langle x\rangle\subseteq\langle y\rangle\\
\mu(x,y)\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mu(x,y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{A}(\alpha^{\prime})\\
\langle x\rangle\subseteq\langle y\rangle\end{subarray}}\mu(x,y).

Here, the last equality holds from the fact that
\mu(x,y)\neq 0
if and only if
y\in\mathcal{A}(\alpha^{\prime})
.
Therefore,
\langle q_{1}\alpha^{\prime}\rangle,\langle q_{2}\alpha^{\prime}\rangle,\ldots,\langle q_{r}\alpha^{\prime}\rangle
are the only maximal subgroups of
\langle\alpha^{\prime}\rangle
which contains
\langle x\rangle
. Let
\mathcal{S}=\{\langle q_{1}\alpha^{\prime}\rangle,\langle q_{2}\alpha^{\prime}\rangle,\ldots,\langle q_{r}\alpha^{\prime}\rangle\}
. Note that if
y\in\mathcal{A}(\alpha^{\prime})
and
\langle x\rangle\subseteq\langle y\rangle
then
\langle y\rangle=\bigcap\limits_{M\in\mathcal{T}}M
for some
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
. It implies that
\mu(x,y)=(-1)^{r-|\mathcal{T}|}
. Therefore, for any
0\leq j\leq r
,
{r\choose j}
many distinct
y
exist in
\mathcal{A}(\alpha^{\prime})
satisfying
\mu(x,y)=(-1)^{r-j}
. We get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{A}(\alpha^{\prime})\\
\langle x\rangle\subseteq\langle y\rangle\end{subarray}}\mu(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}{r\choose j}(-1)^{r-j}=(-1+1)^{r}=0.

∎

The classic M
\ddot{\text{o}}
bius inversion formula is a relation between two arithmetic functions, both functions are defined from the other by taking sums over divisors. It was introduced by August Ferdinand M
\ddot{\text{o}}
bius in 1832. In the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, all of the devisor of
n
can be taken as representatives of the equivalence classes of
\sim
. In general, if
Z
be an abelian group and
x\in Z
, then
\mathcal{A}(x)=\{x^{d}:d\mbox{ divides the order of }x\}
. Keeping this feature in mind, we define an analogue of M
\ddot{\text{o}}
bius inversion formula that aligns two functions of the domain of abelian groups. The analogue is as follows.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
Z
be an abelian group and
f,g:Z\to\mathbb{C}
. Then

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{A}(x)}g(y)

(2)

if and only if

\displaystyle g(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{A}(x)}f(y)~\mu(y,x).

(3)

Proof.

Assume that Equation (
2
) holds. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{A}(x)}f(y)~\mu(y,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{A}(x)}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}(y)}g(\alpha)~\mu(y,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}(x)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{A}(x)\\
\langle\alpha\rangle\subseteq\langle y\rangle\end{subarray}}g(\alpha)~\mu(y,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}(x)}g(\alpha)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{A}(x)\\
\langle\alpha\rangle\subseteq\langle y\rangle\end{subarray}}\mu(y,x)

\displaystyle=g(x).

Here the first equality follows from Equation (
2
) and last equality follows from Part
(i)
of Lemma
2.1
. Conversely, assume that Equation (
3
) holds. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{A}(x)}g(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{A}(x)}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}(y)}f(\alpha)~\mu(\alpha,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}(x)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{A}(x)\\
\langle\alpha\rangle\subseteq\langle y\rangle\end{subarray}}f(\alpha)~\mu(\alpha,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}(x)}f(\alpha)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{A}(x)\\
\langle\alpha\rangle\subseteq\langle y\rangle\end{subarray}}\mu(\alpha,y)

\displaystyle=f(x).

(4)

Here the first equality follows from Equation (
3
) and last equality follows from Part
(ii)
of Lemma
2.1
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
Z
be an abelian group and
x\in Z
. Then

\displaystyle|[x]|=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{A}(x)}|\langle y\rangle|~\mu(y,x).

(5)

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle|\langle x\rangle|=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{A}(x)}|[y]|.

Now the result follows from Theorem
2.2
.
∎

3
Proof of Main Theorem

Let
S
be a subgroup of the abelian group. Define

S^{\perp}=\{x:\psi_{s}(x)=1\mbox{ for all }s\in S\}.

For example, if
Z=\mathbb{Z}_{5}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{5}\otimes\mathbb{Z}_{25}
then

\langle(1,0,5)\rangle^{\perp}=\langle(1,1,4)\rangle\cup\langle(1,2,4)\rangle\cup\langle(1,3,4)\rangle\cup\langle(1,4,4)\rangle\cup\langle(1,0,4)\rangle\cup\langle(0,1,0)\rangle.

For any
\alpha,x\in Z
, define

f_{\alpha}(x):=\sum\limits_{s\in\langle x\rangle}\psi_{\alpha}(s).

We have

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}|\langle x\rangle|&\mbox{if }\alpha\in\langle x\rangle^{\perp}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

We can also write

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}(x)=\sum\limits_{s\in\langle x\rangle}\psi_{\alpha}(s)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{A}(x)}\sum\limits_{s\in[y]}\psi_{\alpha}(s)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{A}(x)}C_{\alpha}(y).

By Theorem
2.2
, we get

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{A}(x)}f_{\alpha}(y)\mu(y,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{A}(x)\\
\alpha\in\langle y\rangle^{\perp}\end{subarray}}|\langle y\rangle|\mu(y,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{A}(x)\\
y\in\langle\alpha\rangle^{\perp}\end{subarray}}|\langle y\rangle|\mu(y,x).

(8)

Now onwards, we will use the following terminologies:

•

We consider
Z=Z_{n_{1}}\times Z_{n_{2}}\times\cdots\times Z_{n_{k}}
with
n=n_{1}\ldots n_{k}
, where
Z_{n_{i}}
is an abelian group of order
n_{i}=p_{i}^{a_{i}}
with
a_{i}\geq 1
for each
i=1,\ldots,k
. And also,
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}
are distinct primes.

•

We consider
Z_{n_{i}}=\mathbb{Z}_{n_{i,1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{n_{i,k_{i}}}
with
n_{i}=n_{i,1}\ldots n_{i,k_{i}}
, where
n_{i,j}=p_{i}^{a_{i,j}}
with
a_{i,j}\geq 1
for each
j=1,\ldots,k_{i}
. Note that
a_{i,1}+\cdots+a_{i,k_{i}}=a_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots,k
.

•

We write the elements
x\in Z
as elements of the cartesian product
Z_{n_{1}}\times Z_{n_{2}}\times\cdots\times Z_{n_{k}}
,
i.e.

x=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}),\mbox{ where }x_{i}\in Z_{n_{i}}\mbox{ for all }i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}.

And also, we write the elements
x_{i}\in Z_{n_{i}}
as elements of the cartesian product
\mathbb{Z}_{n_{i,1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{n_{i,k_{i}}}
,
i.e.

x_{i}=(x_{i,1},x_{i,2},\ldots,x_{i,k_{i}}),\mbox{ where }x_{i,j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{n_{i,j}}\mbox{ for all }j\in\{1,\ldots,k_{i}\}.

•

For any
x,y\in Z
and
x\in\langle y\rangle
, we say

\gcd(x_{i},n_{i})=\gcd(y_{i},n_{i})

if it satisfies

\displaystyle\gcd(x_{i,j},n_{i})=\gcd(y_{i,j},n_{i})\textnormal{ for all }j=1,2,\ldots,k_{i}.

(9)

•

If
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
does not satisfy Equation (
9
) then we say
\gcd(x_{i},n_{i})\neq\gcd(y_{i},n_{i})
.

•

For
x,y\in Z
and
x\in\langle y\rangle
, define
1_{x,y}^{\prime}
and
1_{x,y}^{\prime\prime}
such that
i
-th coordinate of
1_{x,y}^{\prime}
is given by

\displaystyle 1_{x,y}^{\prime}(i)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}(0,0,\ldots,0)&\mbox{if }\gcd(x_{i},n_{i})=\gcd(y_{i},n_{i})\\
(1,1,\ldots,1)&\mbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

and
i
-th coordinate of
1_{x,y}^{\prime\prime}
is given by

\displaystyle 1_{x,y}^{\prime\prime}(i)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}(1,1,\ldots,1)&\mbox{if }\gcd(x_{i},n_{i})=\gcd(y_{i},n_{i})\\
(0,0,\ldots,0)&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
x,y\in Z
,
x\in\langle y\rangle
,
\gcd(x_{i,j},n_{i})=p_{i}^{b_{i,j}}
, and
\gcd(y_{i,j},n_{i})=p_{i}^{c_{i,j}}
. Then
\mu(x,y)\neq 0
if and only if
b_{i,j}-c_{i,j}=0
or
1
for all
i
and
j
.

Proof.

It is clear that
b_{i,j}-c_{i,j}\geq 0
for all
i
and
j
. Assume that
\mu(x,y)\neq 0
. If
\langle x\rangle=\langle y\rangle
then
b_{i,j}-c_{i,j}=0
for all
i
and
j
. And so, we are done. Now, assume that
\langle x\rangle\subsetneqq\langle y\rangle
. Assumption implies that
\langle x\rangle=\langle q_{1}q_{2}\ldots q_{r}y\rangle
, where
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots q_{r}
are the distinct prime divisors of the order of
y
. Therefore
\gcd(z_{i,j},n_{i})=p_{i}^{b_{i,j}}
where
z=q_{1}q_{2}\ldots q_{r}y
. Using the fact that
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots q_{r}
are distinct primes, we get
b_{i,j}-c_{i,j}=0
or
1
for all
i
and
j
. Conversely, assume that
b_{i,j}-c_{i,j}=0
or
1
for all
i
and
j
. If
y_{i,j}=0
, then
b_{i,j}-c_{i,j}=0
. Without loss of generality, let
r
be the smallest positive integer such that
b_{i,j}-c_{i,j}=1
for all
i\leq r
and some
j
. Therefore,
b_{i,j}-c_{i,j}=0
for all
i>r
and
j
. And so,
\langle x\rangle=\cap_{i=1}^{r}\langle p_{i}y\rangle
. Hence
\mu(x,y)=(-1)^{r}
, which is non-zero.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
x,y\in Z
and
x\in\langle y\rangle
. If
\mu(x,y)=0
then
\mu(tx,y)=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mu(x,y)=0
and
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\gcd(x_{i,j},n_{i})=p_{i}^{b_{i,j}}
,
\gcd(y_{i,j},n_{i})=p_{i}^{c_{i,j}}
, and
\gcd(tx_{i,j},n_{i})=p_{i}^{d_{i,j}}
. By Lemma
3.1
, we get
b_{i,j}-c_{i,j}\geq 2
for some
i
and
j
. Note that
d_{i,j}\geq b_{i,j}
. Therefore,
d_{i,j}-c_{i,j}\geq 2
for some
i
and
j
. Again, Lemma
3.1
implies that
\mu(tx,y)=0
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
x,y\in Z
, and
x\in\langle y\rangle
. If
\mu(x,y)\neq 0
then
|\langle x1^{\prime}_{x,y}\rangle|~|[y1^{\prime\prime}_{x,y}]|=\frac{|[y]|}{\varphi(x,y)}
.

Proof.

Let
x^{\prime}=x1^{\prime}_{x,y}
and
y^{\prime\prime}=y1^{\prime\prime}_{x,y}
. We have the following two cases:

Case 1:
Assume that
\gcd(x_{i},n_{i})=\gcd(y_{i},n_{i})
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,k
. Therefore,
\langle x\rangle=\langle y\rangle
. Then
x^{\prime}=(0,0,\ldots,0)
and
y^{\prime\prime}=y
. The result follows from the fact that
\varphi(x,y)=1
.

Case 2:
Without loss of generality, assume that
\gcd(x_{i},n_{i})\neq\gcd(y_{i},n_{i})
for all
i\leq r
and
\gcd(x_{i},n_{i})=\gcd(y_{i},n_{i})
for all
i>r
. Using the fact that
\mu(x,y)\neq 0
, we get
\langle x\rangle=\langle p_{1}p_{2}\ldots p_{r}y\rangle
. Let
|\langle y\rangle|=\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{b_{i}}
where
b_{i}\geq 0
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,k
. Note that
b_{i}\geq 1
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,r
. We have
|\langle x\rangle|=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}^{b_{i}-1}\prod_{i=r+1}^{k}p_{i}^{b_{i}}
,
|\langle x^{\prime}\rangle|=\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}^{b_{i}-1}
, and
|\langle y^{\prime\prime}\rangle|=\prod_{i=r+1}^{k}p_{i}^{b_{i}}
. It implies that

|[y]|=\prod_{i=1~\&~b_{i}\neq 0}^{k}(p_{i}-1)p_{i}^{b_{i}-1}\mbox{ and }|[y^{\prime\prime}]|=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=r+1~\&~b_{i}\neq 0\end{subarray}}^{k}(p_{i}-1)p_{i}^{b_{i}-1}.

The result follows from the fact that
\varphi(x,y)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(p_{i}-1)
.
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
Z
be an abelian group and
\alpha,x\in Z
. Then

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=\mu(\bar{\alpha},x)~\frac{|[x]|}{\varphi(\bar{\alpha},x)},

where
\bar{\alpha}
is a generator of the cyclic group
\langle\alpha\rangle^{\perp}\cap\langle x\rangle
.

Proof.

Assume that
\langle\bar{\alpha}\rangle=\langle\alpha\rangle^{\perp}\cap\langle x\rangle
. The Equation (
8
) implies

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{A}(x)\\
y\in\langle\bar{\alpha}\rangle\end{subarray}}|\langle y\rangle|~\mu(y,x).

(14)

If
y\in\langle\bar{\alpha}\rangle
then
y=y_{1}\bar{\alpha}
for some
y_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
. Equation (
14
) implies

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{A}(x)\\
y=y_{1}\bar{\alpha}\end{subarray}}|\langle y\rangle|~\mu(y,x).

(15)

If
\mu(\bar{\alpha},x)=0
then by Lemma
3.2

\mu(t\bar{\alpha},x)=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Equation (
15
) implies that
C_{\alpha}(x)=0
. So, assume that
\mu(\bar{\alpha},x)\neq 0
. Define
\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}=\bar{\alpha}1_{\bar{\alpha},x}^{\prime}
,
\bar{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}=\bar{\alpha}1_{\bar{\alpha},x}^{\prime\prime}
,
x^{\prime}=x1_{\bar{\alpha},x}^{\prime}
, and
x^{\prime\prime}=x1_{\bar{\alpha},x}^{\prime\prime}
. Note that
\bar{\alpha}=\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}+\bar{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}
and
x=x^{\prime}+x^{\prime\prime}
. We get

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{A}(x^{\prime}+x^{\prime\prime})\\
y=y_{1}(\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}+\bar{\alpha}^{\prime\prime})\end{subarray}}|\langle y\rangle|~\mu(y,x^{\prime}+x^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{A}(x^{\prime})~\&~v\in\mathcal{A}(x^{\prime\prime})\\
u=y_{1}\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}~\&~v=y_{1}\bar{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}\end{subarray}}|\langle u+v\rangle|~\mu(u+v,x^{\prime}+x^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{A}(x^{\prime})~\&~v\in\mathcal{A}(x^{\prime\prime})\\
u=y_{1}\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}~\&~v=y_{1}\bar{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}\end{subarray}}|\langle u\rangle|~|\langle v\rangle|~\mu(u,x^{\prime})~\mu(v,x^{\prime\prime}).

(16)

Assume that
u\in\mathcal{A}(x^{\prime})
,
u=y_{1}\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}
, and
\mu(u,x^{\prime})\neq 0
. Let
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
. We have
\gcd(\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}_{i,j},n_{i})>\gcd(x^{\prime}_{i,j},n_{i})
for some
j
whenever
\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}_{i}\neq(0,0,\ldots,0)
. Lemma
3.1
implies that
\gcd(y_{1},n_{i})=1
whenever
\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}_{i}\neq(0,0,\ldots,0)
. It implies that
y_{1}
is co-prime to the order of
\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}
, and so
\langle u\rangle=\langle\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}\rangle
. We get
|\langle u\rangle|=|\langle\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}\rangle|
and
\mu(u,x^{\prime})=\mu(\bar{\alpha}^{\prime},x^{\prime})
. Using the fact that
\mu(\bar{\alpha}^{\prime},x^{\prime})=\mu(\bar{\alpha},x)
, Equation (
16
) implies that

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=\mu(\bar{\alpha},x)~|\langle\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}\rangle|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{A}(x^{\prime\prime})\\
v=y_{1}\bar{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}\end{subarray}}|\langle v\rangle|~\mu(v,x^{\prime\prime}).

(17)

We have
\langle\bar{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}\rangle=\langle x^{\prime\prime}\rangle
. It implies

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathcal{A}(x^{\prime\prime})\\
v=y_{1}\bar{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}\end{subarray}}|\langle v\rangle|~\mu(v,x^{\prime\prime})=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{A}(x^{\prime\prime})}|\langle v\rangle|~\mu(v,x^{\prime\prime})=|[x^{\prime\prime}]|.

(18)

Here the equality holds from Lemma
2.3
. Now, substitute the Equation (
18
) in Equation (
17
), we get

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=\mu(\bar{\alpha},x)~|\langle\bar{\alpha}^{\prime}\rangle|~|[x^{\prime\prime}]|.

The result follows from Lemma
3.3
.
∎
