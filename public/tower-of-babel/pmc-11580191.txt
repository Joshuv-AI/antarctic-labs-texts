Clinical presentation, investigation findings, and treatment outcomes of intraluminal small intestinal obstruction by bezoars and other materials in adult cows– a retrospective study of 110 cases
Authors: Ueli Braun, Christian Gerspach, Elena Bennien, Monika Hilbe, Karl Nuss
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmall intestinal obstruction (SIO) is a blockage of the intestinal lumen by blunt foreign bodies, neoplasms originating from the intestinal wall or thick chyme. This study analysed the medical records (only data) of 110 cattle with SIO and described the clinical findings, treatment and outcome. These findings were compared between surviving and non-surviving cattle, and among the affected regions such as the duodenum, jejunum and ileum.ResultsColic occurred in 42.7% (47/110) of the cattle. Rumen motility was absent in all cattle and intestinal motility was reduced or absent in 82.6% (90/109). Ballottement and/or percussion and simultaneous auscultation were positive on the right side in 63.3% (69/109). Little or no faeces in the rectum occurred in 93.6% (102/109) of the cattle. Dilated loops of small intestines could be palpated transrectally in 46.8% (51/109) and the actual obstruction (palpated as a firm mass in the small intestine) in 5.5% (6/109) of the cattle. The main laboratory changes were hypokalaemia (80.9%, 89/110), hypermagnesaemia (75.3% 58/77), hypocalcaemia (71.8%, 56/78), haemoconcentration (66.4%, 73/110), azotaemia (66.4%, 73/110) and positive base excess (63.3%, 62/98). Abnormal ultrasonographic findings included dilated loops of small intestines (94.3%, 83/88) and subjectively reduced or absent small intestinal motility (85.4%, 70/82). The actual obstruction could be visualised in 3.4% (3/89) of the cattle by ultrasonography. In the 14 non-surviving and the 96 surviving cattle the frequencies of abdominal dilatation (57.1%, 8/14 vs. 22.1%, 21/95) and the presence of blood, mucus and/or fibrin in the rectum (92.9%, 13/14 vs. 63.2%, 60/95) were significantly different. Abomasal reflux syndrome was significantly worse in the cattle with duodenal obstruction (26/110) than in those with jejunal (51/110) or ileal obstruction (33/110). A total of 107 cattle underwent right flank laparotomy, and the obstruction was resolved by massaging the affected area or it was removed via enterotomy. Of the 110 cattle, 14 (12.7%) were euthanized and 96 (87.3%) were discharged 3 to 10 days after surgery.ConclusionsTransrectal and/or ultrasonographic diagnosis is the exception, and in almost all cases laparotomy, was required. The prognosis is good provided that surgical treatment is carried out promptly.

Background

Ileus (Latinization of the Greek εiλεός ileós) is the interruption of the passage of intestinal contents (intestinal obstruction). Ileus can be either functional (paralytic, adynamic) or dynamic (mechanical) [1]; the latter is most common in cattle. In the current study, ileus is used to describe mechanical obstruction of the intestinal tract unless otherwise indicated. The goal of this study was to analyse and describe 110 cattle with small intestinal obstruction (SIO), to compare the findings of surviving and non-surviving cattle and to examine the effect of the location of the obstruction on clinical and laboratory findings. Ileus caused by SIO results from blockage of the intestinal lumen by blunt foreign bodies or from space-occupying masses of intraluminal and/or intraperitoneal origin [2]. Numerous case reports have described SIO attributable to phytobezoars [3–7], trichobezoars [8, 9], gravel [10], coagulated blood [5, 7, 11], non-specified intraluminal masses [12], a calf-feeding nipple [13] and a cage magnet [14]. Pieces of wood and parts of a placenta were mentioned as causes of intraluminal obstruction in a textbook [2]. Haemorrhagic bowel syndrome (HBS), which is characterised by focal necrohaemorrhagic jejunitis and the formation of blood coagula, is a special form of SIO [15]. Some authors consider small intestinal intussusception, strangulation and volvulus as well as functional obstruction, such as paralytic ileus, to be obstructions [15–18], although the intestinal lumen is not obstructed by a mass. Likewise, cases of ileus caused by compression of the intestines by abscesses [5], fat necrosis [16, 18], the gall bladder [19], adhesions [20], constriction by ligaments and fibrous bands [8, 21–23] and constriction due a diverticulum [24] were also published under the umbrella of intestinal obstruction. These cases were not actual luminal obstructions but rather constriction of the intestinal lumen by extraluminal lesions. The present study focussed on cases of true SIO by intraluminal masses. SIOs may occur in the duodenum, jejunum or ileum, and these three parts appear to be affected with similar frequencies.

The most affected regions of the intestinal loops included the duodenum, jejunum and ileum in 31 [4, 5, 8, 10, 12, 13, 16], 21 [5, 9, 16] and 27 [3, 5, 6, 16] of the cases published since 1988, respectively.

The most frequent clinical findings in 15 cattle with trichobezoars in the small intestine were reduced or absent faecal output, anorexia, a dilated abdomen and colic [16]. Twenty-three animals with obstruction or compression of the duodenum had mostly a moderately to severely abnormal general condition, severe impairment of gastrointestinal function and severely reduced or absent faecal output [5]. The general condition of 22 cows with obstruction of the ileum was mildly to moderately impaired, and 15 cows had signs of mild colic including treading and kicking at the belly [6]. The principal laboratory finding in cows with ileus of the duodenum was hypochloraemic metabolic alkalosis [5], whereas cows with obstipation of the ileum only had minor abnormalities such as haemoconcentration and mild leukocytosis [6].

Ultrasonography can be used to localise the ileus by determining the number of dilated loops of intestine [25]. Few dilated loops are typical of ileus of the duodenum or proximal jejunum. An increasing number of dilated loops of small intestine is consistent with a more distally located ileus. The obstruction itself can rarely be visualised ultrasonographically or palpated transrectally. In most cases, right flank laparotomy is required to confirm the diagnosis [2, 6, 15, 26–28]. The surgeon first attempts to break up the obstruction into smaller pieces by massaging it [6]; saline solution may be injected into the obstruction to make it softer [6]. When the obstruction cannot be broken up, it is removed via enterotomy [2, 6]. Of 22 cases of obstruction of the ileum, 19 could be resolved with massage and 2 required enterotomy [6]. Of 9 cattle that underwent enterotomy to remove trichobezoars, 2 died after surgery as did all 6 that received medical treatment only [16]. Massage was successful in relieving duodenal obstruction in only 2 of 13 cases, whereas enterotomy or resection was required to treat the others. A second operation with resection of the last rib was necessary in 2 of the latter to gain access to the obstruction [5]. Medical treatment of SIO is analogous to other forms of ileus and consists of intravenous fluids including electrolytes, antibiotics, analgesics and prokinetics [29].

The prognosis of SIO is considered good provided that surgical treatment is carried out promptly. Of 9 operated cattle with trichobezoars, 7 (77.8%) survived, whereas all 6 that only received medical treatment died or were euthanized [16]. In another study, 11 (84.5%) of 13 cows operated for trichobezoars survived [5]. Of 22 operated cows with obstruction of the ileum, all survived and were discharged [6].

Methods

The medical records of 110 cattle diagnosed with SIO between January 1, 1987 and December 31, 2019, at the Department of Farm Animals, University of Zurich, were analysed. The present work is based on a dissertation [30].

Definitions used

The term small intestinal obstruction (SIO) was used for cattle with mechanical ileus due to intraluminal obstruction of the small intestine by bezoars and other materials. A final diagnosis of SIO (for the retrospective analysis of the medical records) was made during laparotomy and/or postmortem examination. Included were animals that were at least 1 year of age with SIO of the duodenum, jejunum or ileum. Excluded were previous published cases with SIO and animals with obstruction of the small intestine by a blood clot due to HBS. An obstruction that could be palpated intraoperatively and moved without incising the intestine was referred to as an ill-defined chyme accumulation. To determine the effect of location of the obstruction on clinical and laboratory findings, the animals were grouped according to whether the obstruction occurred in the duodenum, jejunum or ileum. Abomasal reflux syndrome was diagnosed in cattle that had hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic, metabolic alkalosis (chloride ≤ 95 mmol/L, potassium < 4 mmol/L, bicarbonate > 30 mmol/L, base excess > 2 mmol/L) with or without an increase in rumen chloride concentration (rumen chloride > 30 mmol/L). A surviving animal was defined as a patient discharged from the clinic. A deceased animal was defined as one that died spontaneously or was euthanized during hospitalisation.

Animals, anamnestic data, and comorbidities

The following data were evaluated for each animal: Signalment (cow/heifer/bull, age, race), anamnestic data (pregnancy status, last calving date, duration of illness, history of anorexia, signs of pain and comorbidities).

General clinical data

The general clinical data comprised the assessment of the general condition and vital signs (rectal temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate) [31]. The general condition was evaluated by determining the demeanour, behaviour, posture including recumbency, appetite, signs of abdominal pain, appearance of the hair coat and muzzle, skin elasticity, position of the eyes in the sockets and skin surface temperature. General condition was classified as normal or mildly, moderately or severely abnormal. Cows with a normal general condition were bright and alert and had normal behaviour, posture and appetite. The general condition was considered mildly abnormal when a mild decrease in alertness and/or mild signs of colic (defined below) were present. A moderate decrease in alertness and sometimes occasional grunting, and/or bruxism and marked signs of colic were observed in cattle with a moderately abnormal general condition. Cattle with a severely abnormal general condition showed marked apathy and were sometimes recumbent and unable to rise.

Digestive tract

The rumen was assessed for the degree of fill and the number and intensity of contractions [31]. The presence of foreign body tests included the pole test, back grip and percussion of the abdominal wall over the region of the reticulum using a rubber hammer. Each test was carried out 4 times, and the reaction of the animal was observed each time. A test was considered positive when it elicited a short grunt a minimum of 3 out of 4 times. Ballottement and simultaneous auscultation (BSA) as well as percussion and simultaneous auscultation (PSA) of the abdomen on both sides were also carried out. BSA was considered positive when splashing sounds were heard with a stethoscope while the abdominal wall was manually ballotted to produce a swinging motion. A PSA was considered positive when a ringing sound or ping was heard on percussion of the abdominal wall with the handle of a percussion hammer. Rectal examination was done in all cows. Faeces were assessed for colour, consistency, amount, fibre particle length and any abnormal contents.

Non-specific pain evaluation

Each animal was observed for non-specific signs of pain, such as bruxism, spontaneous grunting, piloerection and muscle fasciculations.

Specific pain evaluation

The cattle were divided into the colic, indolence (dullness) and intoxication phases. The number and severity of signs of colic/abdominal pain were determined [31]. Signs of mild colic included mild restlessness, shifting of weight in the hind limbs, looking at the flank, lifting the tail, lifting of individual limbs and tail swishing. Signs of moderate colic were moderate restlessness, brief periods of recumbency, kicking with the hind limbs, arching of the back and marked tail swishing. Signs of severe colic consisted of marked restlessness, frequent lying down and rising, sweating, grunting and violent kicking at the abdomen. The indolence phase followed the colic phase and was characterised by apathy and a markedly abnormal general condition. The last phase was intoxication in which cattle had tachycardia, congested scleral blood vessels, pale mucous membranes, cool skin surface temperature, sunken eyes and a dry muzzle.

Urinalysis and laboratory analyses

The collection and examination of blood, urine and rumen fluid were done as described [32]. Urine samples were collected by free-flow catch or catheterisation in 99 cattle and analysed using a test strip (Combur9, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and a refractometer (Krüss Optronic, Hamburg, Germany) to measure specific gravity.

The following blood samples were collected from all cattle from the left jugular vein: 5 mL of EDTA blood for haematological analysis, 10 mL of whole blood for serum biochemistry and 2 mL of whole blood mixed with 0.2 mL heparin for venous blood gas analysis. Haematological analysis included the determination of haematocrit, total leukocyte count and the concentrations of total protein and fibrinogen. The samples were analysed using the Contraves analyser AL820 (Contraves, Oerlikon, Switzerland) or the CELL-DYN 3500 (Abbott Diagnostics Division, Baar, Switzerland). The concentrations of urea, bilirubin, calcium, magnesium, inorganic phosphate, chloride, potassium, and the activities of the enzymes aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and γ-glutamyltransferase (γ-GT) were determined at 37 °C using an automated analyser (Cobas Mira, Cobas Integra 700, Cobas Integra 800, Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) and the manufacturer’s reagents (Roche-Reagents) according to the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine (IFCC). The venous blood gas analysis was performed with the RapidLab 248 analyser (Siemens Schweiz AG, Zurich, Switzerland).

A sample of rumen fluid was collected using a Dirksen probe and assessed for colour, odour, consistency and pH (data not shown). In addition, the concentration of chloride was determined (MK-II-Chlorid-Analyser 9265, Sherwood, Cambridge, Great Britain).

Ultrasonographic examination of the abdomen

The abdomen of 89 cattle was scanned from the right side as described [25]. Briefly, the area from the tuber coxae to the eighth intercostal space and from the transverse processes of the vertebrae to the linea alba on the right side was examined using a 3.5 to 6.5 MHz convex transducer (GE Logiq 7, GE Healthcare, Switzerland). The appearance of loops of small intestine and their diameter, contents and motility were assessed. Characteristic signs of an ileus were dilated small intestines with a diameter ≥ 4.0 cm and no or very little intestinal motility. When in addition an intraluminal mass was seen, this indicated an ileus attributable to SIO with the exception of HBS in which the rectum contained haemorrhagic faeces or blood. In the latter case (HBS), the medical record was excluded.

Diagnosis

A tentative diagnosis of ileus based on the clinical examination was made when the lead signs were characteristic of mechanical ileus, which included signs of colic, reduced or absent faecal output and progressive deterioration in general demeanour [33]. When the above-mentioned signs were accompanied by transrectal palpation of a mass within the bowel and dilated intestines proximal to the mass, a diagnosis of SIO was made. A final diagnosis of SIO (for the retrospective analysis of the medical records) was made during laparotomy and/or postmortem examination.

Laparotomy, intraoperative complications, and re-laparotomy

If possible, right-flank laparotomy was carried out in a standing position. Cattle that had difficulty standing because of a severely abnormal demeanour or marked visceral pain were operated in a left lateral recumbent position. Proximal paravertebral anaesthesia was most commonly used [34–37]. A 25- to 30-cm vertical incision was then made in the mid-paralumbar fossa through all layers of the abdominal wall. After completion of the incision, the abdominal cavity was explored using a structured approach starting from the outside of the greater omentum and moving cranially. In cases with obstruction of the descending duodenum, the dilated duodenum and obstruction were easily recognised. After palpation of the descending duodenum, the sigmoid flexure, liver, gallbladder, pyloric region, abomasum, omasum, umbilical region and reticulum were palpated for abnormalities. Exploration continued around the caudal edge of the greater omentum into the supraomental recess. In some cattle with duodenal obstruction, the mass was situated in the ascending duodenum near the medial aspect of the greater omentum. It was necessary to massage the obstruction toward the caudal border of the greater omentum and into the descending duodenum so that it could be broken up manually or removed via enterotomy. Manual exploration of the abdominal cavity continued to the cranial mesenteric root where the proximal jejunum curves around the mesentery. This region was palpated for the presence of dilated intestine and obstructive masses. Identification of the cause of the obstruction of the middle two-thirds of the jejunum was achieved initially by resting one hand between the intestines and then slowly moving them around until the section of dilated intestine was found. Careful palpation of the intestine usually revealed the obstruction at the distal aspect of the dilated bowel. In all cases, the ileocaecal region was examined to complete the examination of the small intestines. The caecum, ileum and ascending colon were inspected, and the ileocaecal opening was palpated for signs of obstruction.

For verification of the problem and potential removal of the mass, the affected part of the jejunum or ileum was exteriorised, whenever possible, and placed on a Mayo table covered with sterile drapes for visual inspection. When the obstruction could be broken up manually, the smaller pieces were massaged into the distal aspect of the jejunum or the caecum. When the obstruction could not be broken up or appeared to be attached to the mucosa, an 18-gauge 1 ½” (1.25 × 38 mm Sterican, B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany) needle was inserted into the bowel lumen and 1 L of lactated Ringer`s solution infused via a sterile Heidelberger extension line (B. Braun Medical AG, Sempach, Switzerland) to soften the mass and facilitate its breakup. After the infusion, a non-perforating suture was placed in a cruciate pattern for occlusion of the centesis site using 2 − 0 USP monofilament absorbable suture material (BIOSYN, atraumatic needle, ½ circle, 75 cm in length; Covidien-Medtronic, Neustadt, Germany); the stitch length and distance was approximately 0.3 cm. When the obstruction could not be broken up manually, enterotomy was performed. Lidocaine was applied close to the mesenteric vessels to block the mesenteric nerves. The relevant bowel part was carefully pulled to the outside of the abdominal incision and the remaining intestines were relocated or protected with sterile cotton towels soaked in lactated Ringer`s solution. Bowel clamps were applied on either side of the obstruction. Enterotomy was performed on the antimesenteric aspect of the intestine. The incision varied in length depending on the size of the obstruction and was made distal to the obstruction whenever possible. A haemostat was used to grasp the obstructing mass and remove it in one or several pieces. The incision was closed using 2 − 0 USP monofilament absorbable suture material (BIOSYN, atraumatic needle, ½ circle, 75 cm; Covidien-Medtronic); incisions longer than 5 cm were closed with single interrupted sutures, while shorter incisions were closed using a simple continuous suture pattern. With both methods, the first suture layer was oversewn with a serosal-serosal continuous Cushing suture pattern to prevent leakage and adhesion formation. The enterotomy and adjacent sites were rinsed with povidone-iodine solution (5 mL in 1 L lactated Ringer`s solution), followed by 1 L lactated Ringer`s solution. The cotton towels were removed, and the intestines were then returned to the abdominal cavity.

The peritoneum, fascia and transverse abdominal muscle were closed in one layer, as were the internal and external oblique muscles, using a synthetic absorbable suture material (Polysorb™, USP 2, taper needle, Covidien-Medtronic) in a simple continuous suture pattern. A modified mattress suture pattern (Polysorb™, USP 2.0, cutting needle, Covidien-Medtronic) was used to close the subcutaneous tissues, and skin staples (Appose™, ULC 35 W clips, 6.9 mm x 3.8 mm, Covidien-Medtronic) were used to close the skin.

Intraoperative complications such as the animal becoming recumbent on the right side during surgery with exteriorisation and subsequent contamination of the intestines, severe haemorrhage and inability to exteriorise the affected intestines were recorded.

Re-laparotomy was performed in cases with recurrence of the obstruction.

Postoperative treatment

The cows that were successfully operated and subsequently discharged (n = 96) received fluid therapy (to treat dehydration, n = 96), antibiotics (to prevent postoperative peritonitis, n = 96), analgesics (to reduce pain, n = 96), prokinetic drugs (to stimulate intestinal motility, n = 67) and electrolyte replacement (to treat hypochloraemia, hypocalcaemia, hypokalaemia and hypophosphataemia, n = 54) as described in detail in the Results section and justified in the Discussion.

Euthanasia/slaughter/postmortem examination

Cattle were euthanized using pentobarbital (80 mg/kg body weight, administered intravenously, Esconarkon, Streuli Pharma, Uznach, Switzerland) or, in earlier years, cattle were slaughtered at the slaughter facility of the Veterinary Hospital during or after the initial examination when they were in severe clinical condition or when the owner did not consent to surgery, and the meat was used for zoo-animal feeding (as long as it was allowed). Cattle were euthanized intraoperatively when lesions associated with a very poor prognosis (e.g., ruptured intestines, fibrinous peritonitis, haemorrhagic infarction) were seen or complications (e.g., becoming recumbent on the right side during surgery with exteriorisation and subsequent contamination of the intestines) occurred, and postoperatively when the clinical condition deteriorated. All cattle that died or were euthanized underwent postmortem examination, whereas in slaughtered cattle, only the internal organs were inspected.

Short and long-term outcomes

The short-term outcome was defined as the proportion of cattle discharged after hospitalisation. The general condition, appetite, faecal output, rectal temperature and heart rate were monitored from the time of laparotomy to discharge of the cattle.

The long-term outcome of the discharged cows was determined 2 years after discharge via telephone interview. Owners were asked whether the cow was still in the herd, in which case the level of productivity was determined. For cows no longer in the herd, owners were asked the reasons for culling (recurrence of obstruction, economic reasons, other diseases).

Effect of location of the obstruction on clinical and laboratory findings

The effect of the location of the obstruction on the occurrence and frequency of clinical findings was determined for each location of SIO (duodenum, jejunum, ileum). The same was determined for the laboratory findings.

Comparison of surviving and non-surviving cattle

The length of illness, clinical findings and blood results were compared between surviving and non-surviving cattle to determine possible differences.

Statistics

The program SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp. 2017, USA) was used for analysis. Frequencies and frequency percentages were determined for all variables of the history, results of the clinical and ultrasonographical examinations and laboratory analyses, surgery, postoperative treatment, short- and long-term outcomes, comparison of surviving and non-surviving cattle and the effect of the obstruction on clinical and laboratory findings. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test the numerical data for normality. Normally distributed data were presented as means ± standard deviations and non-normally distributed data as medians. The 95% CIs were calculated for the means and medians. Differences between numerical data of surviving (from admission to hospital discharge) and non-surviving cattle were analysed using the Mann-Whitney-U-test, and for the analysis of the differences between the three localisations of obstruction (duodenum, jejunum, ileum), the Kruskal-Wallis test was used. Differences in nominal data were analysed using the chi-square test, and if a sample number was < 5, Fisher’s exact test was used. When the P-value was < 0.05, a post-hoc test (Bonferroni correction) was used to identify groups that differed significantly. The variables heart rate and rectal temperature over time (day 0 to day 7) were analysed using the general linear model choosing ANOVA with repeated measures and replacing polynomial contrasts with difference. A value of P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

110 cattle with SIO were clinically examined (Fig. 1). Three of the 110 cattle died or were euthanized immediately after the initial examination because of the severity of the clinical signs, a grave prognosis or the owners did not consent to surgery. A total of 107 cows underwent right flank laparotomy; of these, 94 were discharged after successful postoperative treatment, four died and nine were operated a second time because of recurrence of the obstruction. Seven of the latter were euthanized and two were discharged. Overall, 12.7% (14/110) of the cattle died and 87.3% (96/110) were discharged.

Animals, anamnestic data and comorbidities

Ninety-seven (88.2%) of the 110 cattle were cows, 9.1% (10/110) were heifers and 2.7% (3/110) were bulls. They ranged in age from 1.0 to 14.0 years (median, 95% CI, 4.5, 4.0–5.0 years). Breeds included Swiss Braunvieh (79.1%, 87/110), Holstein (12.7%, 14/110), Swiss Fleckvieh (7.3%, 8/110) and 1 cow (0.9%) of the Norman beef breed. Forty-eight (44.9%) of the 107 female cattle were pregnant, 42.1% (45/107) were open and in the remaining 13.1% (14/107), the pregnancy status had not been recorded. The gestational age ranged from 5 to 37 weeks (median, 95% CI, 22.0, 17–26 weeks). The time since last calving was known in 54 cows and ranged from 1 to 44 weeks (median, 95% CI, 9, 6–13 weeks). The duration from the onset of clinical signs to admission ranged from 6 to 120 h (median, 95% CI, 24, 24–24 h). Sixty-eight (66.7%) of 102 cattle had a history of anorexia, and the remaining 33.3% (34/102) had a history of reduced feed intake. Colic had been recorded before admission to our clinic in 45.5% (60/110) of all the cattle.

One or several comorbidities, which in all likelihood were not related to the SIO, were diagnosed in 20.0% (22/110) of the cattle. They included fatty liver syndrome (n = 7), endometritis (n = 1) and parasitism (gastrointestinal nematodes, n = 9; fascioliasis, n = 3, dicrocoeliosis, n = 2).

General clinical data

The general condition was mildly abnormal in 14.5% (16/110), moderately abnormal in 67.3% (74/110) and severely abnormal in 18.2% (20/110) of the cattle. One cow was recumbent on admission. Twenty-nine (26.6%) of 109 cattle had uni- or bilateral abdominal distension. The most common vital sign abnormalities in the initial examination were lower-than-normal rectal temperature (56.9%, 62/109, range = 36.3–39.6 °C, normal range = 38.5–39.0 °C), tachypnoea (44.0%, 48/109, range = 12–66 breaths/min., normal range = 15–25 breaths per min.) and tachycardia (28.2%, 31/110, range = 44–128 beats per min., normal range = 60–80 beats per min.) (Table 1).

Digestive tract abnormalities

The most common digestive tract abnormalities found on clinical examination were reduced or absent rumen motility (100%, 110/110, normal rate of rumen sounds = 2 or 3 strong contractions per 2 min.), little or no faeces in the rectum (93.6%, 102/109), reduced or absent intestinal motility (82.6%, 90/109), positive BSA and/or PSA on the right side of the abdomen (63.3%, 69/109) and dilated loops of small intestines detected during transrectal palpation (46.8%, 51/109) (Fig. 2; Table 1). Transrectal palpation revealed an intestinal obstruction in six (5.5%) of 109 cattle and taut bands of mesentery in 4.6% (5/109). At least one positive foreign body test (back grip, pole test or percussion of the reticular area) was recorded in 18.3% (19/104), and 10.0% (11/110) had mild to moderate rumen tympany. The faeces were dark to black in 4.6% (5/109) of the cattle, and faecal consistency varied from liquid to pulpy (normal) to thick pulpy and pasty. Abnormal faecal content was recorded in 43.1% (47/109) of the cattle and included mucus, blood, fibrin and combinations thereof.

Non-specific pain evaluation

19.1% (21/110) had non-specific signs of pain, which included muscle fasciculations (8.2%, 9/110), bruxism (6.4%, 7/110), spontaneous grunting (3.6%, 4/110) or piloerection (0.9%, 1/110).

Specific pain evaluation

Forty-seven (42.7%) cattle were in the colic phase (visceral signs of pain, abdominal pain), but the clinical signs were described in detail in only 39. These included lordosis (26.4%, 29/110), treading (17.3%, 19/110), kicking (6.4%, 7/110), restlessness (3.6%, 4/110) and sweating (5.5%, 6/110). Of all cattle, 23.6% (26/110) had one, 7.3% (8/110) had two, 2.7% (3/110) had three and 1.8% (2/110) had four signs of visceral pain. The pain was assessed as mild (32.7%, 36/110), moderate (7.3%, 8/110) or severe (2.7%, 3/110). Sixty-two (56.4%) of the cattle were in the indolence phase (dullness phase), and one cow (0.9%) was in the intoxication phase at the time of admission. In addition, 60.0% (66/110) of cows had a tense abdominal wall accompanied by an arched back in two of them.

Urinalysis and laboratory findings

The urine pH ranged from 5.0 to 9.0 (median, 95% CI, 8.0, 7.5-8.0) and was decreased (5.0-6.9) in 26.3% (26/99) and increased (8.1-9.0) in 39.4% (39/99) of the cattle. Specific gravity ranged from 1.003 to 1.060 (median, 95% CI, 1.026,1.024–1.030) and was decreased (< 1.020) in 25.3% (24/95) and increased (> 1.040) in 7.4% (7/95). Glucosuria (50-1000 mg/dL urine) occurred in 35.4% (35/99), haemoglobinuria/haematuria (5-250 erythrocytes/µL) in 32.3% (31/96), ketonuria (10 to > 150 mg/dL urine) in 9.2% (9/98) and proteinuria (100–500 mg protein/dL urine in 7.1% (7/99) of the cattle.

The major abnormalities were hypokalaemia (80.9%, 89/110), hypermagnesaemia (75.3%, 58/77), hypocalcaemia (71.8%, 56/78), haemoconcentration (66.4%, 73/110), azotaemia (66.4%, 73/110) and increased base excess (63.3%, 62/98) (Fig. 3; Table 2). Other findings included hypochloraemia (55.5%, 61/110), hypercapnia (55.1%, 54/98) and hyperproteinaemia (50.0%, 55/110). Less common findings were increased bicarbonate concentration (39.8%, 39/98), increased concentration of rumen chloride (39.6%, 36/91), leukocytosis (37.3%, 41/110), increased activity of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (34.9%, 38/109) and acidosis (31.6%, 31/98; based on blood pH). Other abnormalities occurred in < 30% of the cattle.

Surgical findings, intraoperative complications, and re-laparotomy

All but two of the cows (98.1%, 105/107) were operated while standing, one was operated in left lateral recumbency and in the remaining cow, surgery was started with the animal standing and finished in left lateral recumbency (because the cow went down during surgery). One cow with obstruction of the duodenum underwent resection of the last rib to facilitate access to the obstruction. The obstructing mass could be manually crushed and broken up in 55.1% (59/107) of the cattle and was removed through enterotomy in the remaining 44.9% (48/107). In one of the latter cases with obstruction of the ileum, an ileocaecal side-to-side anastomosis was done as described [6] to prevent recurrence of the impaction. The obstruction1 was in the duodenum in 23.6% (26/110), in the jejunum in 46.4% (51/110) and in the ileum in 30.0% (33/110) of the cattle. The obstructions included phytobezoars (61.8%, 68/110) (Figs. 4 and 5), ill-defined chyme accumulations (29.1%, 32/110), trichobezoars (5.5%, 6/110), gravel (2.7%, 3/110) and foreign bodies (cigarette lighter and pieces of wood; 0.9%, 1/110). Intraoperative complications occurred in 13.1% (14/107) of the cattle and included recumbency (10/107), severe haemorrhage (3/107) and inability to exteriorise the affected intestines (1/107).

Four cattle died within 14 days (median, 95% CI, 2, 1–8 days) after surgery, and nine cows had recurrence of the obstruction and were operated a second time. Of the latter, one underwent resection of the last rib, and in eight, the obstruction was broken up by massage. In the remaining cow, in which a bezoar had been removed from the duodenum via enterotomy, a bezoar in the spiral colon was removed through enterotomy 5 days later. This obstruction had caused massive caecal dilatation, which was diagnosed transrectally. Of the nine cattle that had a second laparotomy, seven were euthanized because of deterioration and two were discharged.

Postmortem findings

Four of the 14 examined cattle had obstruction of the jejunum (n = 2), ileum (n = 1) and spiral colon (n = 1) by a bezoar, three had paralytic ileus (after treatment of the obstruction; dilated and ingesta-filled intestines with no mechanical obstruction), three had abomasal ulceration, two had an unknown cause of death, and bowel rupture, suture dehiscence and fibrino-haemorrhagic intestinal contents with adhesions and necrosis in the region of the ileocaecal anastomosis were diagnosed in one case each. Two cows also had local peritonitis.

Postoperative treatment

Medical treatment included fluid therapy (96/96), antibiotics (96/96), analgesics (96/96), prokinetic drugs (67/96) and electrolyte replacement (54/96). All cattle received 10 L of a solution containing 50 g glucose and 9 g sodium chloride per L daily for 1 to 8 days (median, 95% CI, 3, 3–3 days) administered as a slow intravenous drip via an indwelling jugular vein catheter. Antibiotic treatment included penicillin G procaine (12,000 IU/kg) given intramuscularly (77/96), amoxicillin (7 mg/kg) given intramuscularly (11/96), danofloxacin (1.2 mg/kg) given intravenously (3/96) and penicillin G procaine followed by amoxicillin (5/96). The antibiotic treatment was administered in most cases for 3 to 4 days (median, 95% CI 4, 4–4 days). Flunixin meglumine (1 mg/kg), ketoprofen (3 mg/kg) or a combination of flunixin meglumine and metamizole (35 mg/kg) was administered intravenously, once daily, for 2 to 6 days (median, 95% CI, 3, 3–3 days) in all the cattle. Prokinetic drugs were used in 67 cattle for a duration of 1 to 8 days (median, 95% CI, 3, 3–3 days). Thirty-five cattle received neostigmine (40–45 mg) administered via continuous drip infusion and 32 received intramuscular metoclopramide (30 mg), usually 7 to 9 times at 8-hour intervals (metoclopramide was only used in the first few years). Fifty-three cows with hypocalcaemia received 500 mL of 40% calcium borogluconate intravenously for 1 to 4 days as previously described in hypocalcaemic cows with small intestinal intussusception [29] and other kinds of ileus. Hypokalaemia was treated in 54 cattle with daily oral doses of 60 to 100 g of potassium chloride [29] until normokalaemia occurred (K ≥ 4 mmol/L). Forty cattle were treated once, 13 two or three times and one animal 4 times. Three cows with hypophosphataemia were treated orally with 500 g sodium dihydrogen phosphate (mono-sodium phosphate, NaH2PO4, Hänseler AG, Herisau, Switzerland), diluted in 1 L of water and administered into the rumen via an orogastric tube.

Short and long-term outcomes

In 92 (95.8%) of the 96 cattle that were subsequently discharged, the general condition normalised within 1 to 9 days (median, 95% CI, 2, 2–3 days). With 2 exceptions, appetite and faecal output returned to normal within 1 to 8 and 1 to 9 days postoperatively, respectively (median, 95% CI, for both 3, 3–3 days). The median rectal temperature of the surviving 96 cattle increased from 38.3 °C on admission to 38.6 °C on day 3 (P > 0.05), after which time it ranged from 38.5 to 38.7 °C. The median heart rate remained at 65 to 78 bpm. Seventy-eight (81.3%) of the 96 cattle were discharged within 3 to 6 days and the remaining 18 within 6 to 10 days (median 5 days) after surgery.

The long-term outcome of the 96 discharged cows was determined 2 years after discharge via telephone interview. Thirty-nine cows (40.6%) had remained productive in their herds. Seven (7.3%) had been slaughtered because of suspected recurrence of an obstruction and 19 (19.8%) for economic reasons or other illnesses. The outcome was not known for the remaining 31 (32.3%) cows.

Effect of location of the obstruction on clinical and laboratory findings

The frequency of colic (with at least 1 sign) was 26.9% (7/26) when the duodenum was affected, 41.2% (21/51) when the jejunum was affected and 57.6% (19/33) with ileal obstruction (chi-square = 5.7, P = 0.06). Lordosis, dilated small intestines and no faeces in the rectum were significantly more common in cattle with obstruction of the ileum than those with obstruction of the duodenum (Fig. 6A), whereas duodenal obstruction was more often associated with reduced skin turgor, tense abdominal wall and dilatation of the rumen (detected transrectally) than obstruction of the jejunum or ileum (Fig. 6B).

Hypochloraemia, hypokalaemia, increased bicarbonate concentration, increased pCO2 partial pressure, increased base excess and alkalosis were significantly more pronounced in cattle with duodenal obstruction compared with cattle with obstruction of the jejunum and ileum (Table 4). The haematocrit and concentrations of total protein, urea and magnesium were also significantly higher in cattle with obstruction of the duodenum.

Comparison of 96 surviving and 14 non-surviving cattle

The median duration of illness before admission (both 24 h) and the median heart (72 vs. 78 bpm) and respiratory rates (24 vs. 26 breaths per min) did not differ significantly between the groups. The median rectal temperature was significantly lower in the surviving cattle compared with the non-surviving cattle (38.3 vs. 38.7 °C, P < 0.01, Mann-Whitney-U-test). Abdominal dilatation was more common in non-surviving than in surviving cattle (57.1%, 8/14, vs. 22.1%, 21/95, chi-square = 14.2, P < 0.01) as was the presence of blood, mucus and fibrin in the rectum (92.9%, 13/14, vs. 63.2%, 60/95, chi-square = 16.1, P < 0.01). The median activity of AST was higher in the surviving cattle (95 vs. 80 U/l, P < 0.05).

Discussion

Animals, anamnestic data and comorbidities

Cattle under a year were not included because many clinical (e.g., heart rate, rectal temperature), laboratory (haematocrit, blood pH) and ultrasonographic (e.g., diameter of the small intestines) variables differ from those of adult cattle. In addition, in most animals < 1 year, a transrectal examination is not possible. The majority of cattle were Swiss Braunvieh and 4 to 5 years of age. The age and breed distributions were the same as described for cows with small intestinal intussusception [29], mesenteric torsion [38], small intestinal strangulation [32] and small intestinal volvulus [31]. Swiss Braunvieh cattle are the most common breed in our area and thus in our patient load. Pregnancy and its duration did not affect the occurrence of SIO. Interestingly, the majority of cows became ill 6 to 13 weeks after parturition. This is markedly later than abomasal displacement, which tends to occur in the early postpartum period [39]. The most common concomitant disease was gastrointestinal parasitism, which was diagnosed via faecal examination in 9 cattle. None of those animals had clinical signs characteristic of gastrointestinal parasitism. Thus, no association between parasitism and SIO was found. However, severe intestinal wall damage may lead to impaired intestinal motility, favouring SIO.

Signs of pain

Only 19.1% of the cattle had non-specific signs of pain, which are often discrete and easily missed. This is particularly true for bruxism and spontaneous grunting, which can be missed in a noisy barn without attentive observation. These signs occur with other disorders and are not pathognomonic for SIO. However, they must prompt a thorough clinical examination because they are associated with severe disease. Only 42.7% of the cattle were in the colic phase, while 56.4% were in the indolence phase. The colic phase in cattle is very short and only lasts approximately 12 h [40]. This means that the absence of colic signs does not rule out ileus, and ileus with other typical signs, such as no or reduced faecal output, abdominal dilatation, positive BSA and/or PSA on the right side and transrectal palpation of dilated loops of intestine, cannot be ruled out.

Causes of obstruction

Most obstructions were caused by phytobezoars (61.8%, 68/110) and ill-defined masses composed of chyme (29.1%, 32/110), whereas trichobezoars (5.5%, 6/110), gravel (2.7%, 3/110) and other foreign bodies (0.9%, 1/110) were less common. The high incidence of obstruction by phytobezoars is in agreement with the findings of other studies [3–6]. Adhesions in the reticulum caused by foreign bodies are often responsible for phytobezoar formation [5, 8]. It is conceivable that such lesions impair the reticular sorting mechanism at the reticulo-omasal orifice leading to poorly digested feed entering the omasum and abomasum and subsequently lodging in the duodenum. The second most common cause of obstruction, observed in 29.1% of the cattle, was poorly defined masses. Almost all of these could be broken up by massage alone and were therefore not directly seen; however, palpation findings suggested that most of these masses were phytobezoars. In one study, phytobezoars were thought to have caused 19 of 22 cases of ileum obstipation because the obstruction could be broken up by massage [6]. A food bolus was suspected of causing duodenal obstruction in one of two cows described in another study [12]. SIO attributable to gravel, which occurred three times in the present study, was also described in one cow with obstruction of the descending duodenum and one other with obstruction of the pylorus [10]. Trichobezoars were found in only six cattle in the present study, most likely because they occur almost exclusively in calves [41] and rarely in cows [42]. Trichobezoars are most common in calves with lice, and are caused by excessive licking or when mineral-deficient calves lick other calves, especially those that are shedding [9].

Digestive tract abnormalities

Other than abnormal general condition, the most common abnormal clinical findings were decreased ruminal (100%, 110/110) and intestinal motility (82.6%, 90/109) and little or no faeces in the rectum (93.6%, 102/109). These findings were similar to those seen in other forms of mechanical ileus including intussusception [29], mesenteric torsion [38] and small intestinal strangulation [32], in which decreased or absent rumen motility occurred in 93.2 to 97.6%, decreased or absent intestinal motility in 93.7 to 100% and little or no faeces in the rectum in 93.6 to 100% of the cattle. Dilated loops of small intestines were palpated transrectally in only 46.8% (51/109) of the cattle, and palpation of the actual obstruction was the exception (4.6%, 5/109). By comparison, dilated loops of small intestines were palpated in 49.2% (29/59) of cows with mesenteric torsion [38] and in 48.9% (23/47) of cows with volvulus [31]. Those frequencies were higher than those in cows with intussusception (24.6%; 31/126) [29] but lower than in cows with strangulation (63.6%; 38/60) [32].

General clinical data and effect of location of the obstruction on clinical and laboratory findings

The clinical findings in cows with ileus depend not only on the type of ileus but also on its anatomic location [43]. Cows that underwent experimental ligation of the small intestines 10 cm caudal to the pylorus and in the middle and at the distal end of the small intestines [43] had more severe signs of shock and dehydration after proximal ligation caudal to the pylorus than after distal ligation. By contrast, signs of colic were more frequent and more pronounced in cows with caudal intestinal ligation [43]. Our results were similar; findings that increased in frequency with a more caudally-located obstruction included colic, transrectal palpation of dilated small intestinal loops and no faeces in the rectum. Colic was more common in cattle with obstruction of the ileum (57.6%, 19/33) than in cattle with obstruction of the duodenum (26.9%, 7/26) or jejunum (41.2%, 21/51, P = 0.06). A possible explanation for this is that obstruction at the aboral/distal end of the small intestines results in dilatation of a longer segment of bowel and therefore more pain because more autonomous nerve endings are stimulated in the stretched intestinal wall [43]. It seems logical that dilated loops of small intestines are more frequently palpated in cattle with a distally located obstruction because a greater length of dilated bowel is more likely to be detected. Likewise, when the blockage is in the proximal part of the small intestines, the distal part takes longer to evacuate, extending the amount of time when the rectum still contains faeces. In contrast to the occurrence of colic, skin turgor was more frequently reduced in cattle with duodenal obstruction (88.5%, 23/26) than in cattle with jejunal (56.9%, 29/51) or ileal obstruction (62.5%, 20/32) because abomasal reflux syndrome associated with the former favours the onset of dehydration. In cattle with duodenal obstruction, the rumen was more often dilated (53.8%, 14/26) on transrectal palpation than in cattle with jejunal (27.5%, 14/51) and ileal obstruction (12.5%, 4/32) because ruminal congestion of ingesta in the forestomachs occurs more rapidly.

The laboratory abnormalities observed in the present study agreed with those seen after experimental ligation of the duodenum, jejunum and ileum [44]; the more proximal the experimental ligation, the faster the onset of abomasal reflux and the more pronounced its severity, characterised by hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis [44]. In the present study, rumen chloride concentration, the results of venous blood gas analysis, haematocrit and urea concentration were significantly higher and the concentrations of potassium and chloride significantly lower in cattle with duodenal obstruction than in cows with ileal obstruction.

Urinalysis and laboratory findings

The laboratory findings are discussed in the sub-section General Clinical Dataandeffect of location of the obstruction on clinical and laboratory findings and in the sub-section Postoperative treatment of the Discussion.

Ultrasonographic findings

The ultrasonographic abnormalities in cattle with SIO were similar to those seen with other types of mechanical ileus such as intussusception [29], strangulation [32] and volvulus [31]. The main findings were dilated loops of small intestines (94.3%, 83/88) and subjectively reduced or absent small intestinal motility (85.4%, 70/82). Of note, the actual obstruction could be visualised as a focal, echoic intraluminal mass in 2.7% (3/89) of the cattle. By comparison, the actual intussusception could be visualised as a typical bowel-within-bowel pattern in 5.7% (7/123) of affected cows [29], but the actual cause of the ileus was not seen in any of the 37 cows with mesenteric torsion [38], 43 cows with strangulation [32] or 31 cows with small intestinal volvulus [31].

Surgical findings, intraoperative complications and re-laparotomy

In more than half of the cattle of this study (55.1%; 59/107), the obstructive mass could be manually broken up, and in the remaining cases, it was removed via enterotomy. As in cases of obstruction by trichobezoars [16], impaction of the ileum [6] and mesenteric torsion [38], intestinal resection was not required in any of the cattle. Only one of 59 cows (of a total of 60) with strangulation [32] and two of 21 cows (of a total of 47) with volvulus required intestinal resection [31]. In contrast, 74 of 76 cows (of a total of 126) that underwent laparotomy to treat intussusception required intestinal resection because of severe intestinal wall necrosis [29].

Cattle with SIO required a second operation (8.4%, 9/107) more often than cattle with strangulation (1.9%, 1/53) [32]), mesenteric torsion (0%, 0/21) [38]), volvulus (0%, 0/21) [31]) and ileal impaction (0%, 0/22) [6]). The necessity of a second operation in cows with intussusception however, was twice that of SIO (17.1% (13/76) [29]). Recurrence of obstruction after intussusception was usually because of a new intussusception, likely attributable to impaired intestinal motility [5]. Recurrence of intestinal obstruction due to a mass was most often due to another obstructive mass, which may have been associated with an impaired sorting mechanism at the reticulo-omasal orifice resulting in poorly digested feed in the omasum, abomasum and duodenum [5]. Recurrences after intestinal strangulation [32], volvulus [31] and mesenteric torsion [38] were caused by factors not directly related to the gastrointestinal tract, such as persistent urachus and mesenteric or omental tears; after surgical correction of these anatomical lesions, the risk of recurrence decreased markedly.

Intraoperative complications were uncommon and occurred in only 14 of 107 (13.1%) of the cattle. The most common complication was the animal suddenly and unexpectedly lying down. This is particularly concerning when the animal becomes recumbent on the laparotomy site, contaminating the intestines. Thus, performing the laparotomy in a recumbent position is recommended in cattle that have difficulty standing during the clinical examination or have a markedly abnormal demeanour.

Nine cattle had recurrence of the obstruction and required a second laparotomy. The entire small intestinal tract must be carefully palpated to ensure other obstructions are found and removed to reduce the risk of re-laparotomy. However, recurrence of an obstruction can still occur especially when part of the obstructive material was in the abomasum at the time of surgery.

Postoperative treatment

Postoperative treatment consisted of correcting fluid, electrolyte and acid-base imbalances [33]. In addition, the cattle were treated with antibiotics, analgesics and prokinetic drugs. Antibiotic therapy was to reduce the risk of peritonitis, and analgesics are part of a standard postoperative treatment protocol [17]. Prerenal azotaemia, characterised by haemoconcentration and increases in urea and creatinine concentrations and attributable to shock and abomasal reflux, occurred in 66.4% of the cattle. Optimal treatment entailed intravenous administration of a sodium chloride solution containing glucose. In principle, the administration of a 0.9% sodium chloride solution would have been adequate for treatment of volume deficits and hyplochloraemia (55.5% of patients) [17]. We usually supplement the fluids with glucose to treat hypoglycaemia, which often occurs, and to treat or prevent ketosis. Hypokalaemia occurred in 80.8% of the cattle, which was caused by anorexia and abomasal reflux syndrome. The preferred method of treating hypokalaemia is oral administration of potassium because the intravenous route is associated with cardiac arrest [45]. The dose we used (60 to 100 g per day) was relatively low; 60 to 150 g twice daily is recommended [45]. Hypermagnesaemia was another frequent finding seen in 75.3% of the cattle and was possibly caused by impaired glomerular filtration attributable to dehydration [46]. This explanation is supported by a significant correlation between the serum concentrations of magnesium and urea (data not shown). Hypocalcaemia was seen in 71.8% of the cattle. Hypocalcaemia may be attributable to anorexia, gastrointestinal stasis and ischemia-related decrease in gastrointestinal absorption, and possibly milk production [15, 47]. Increased production or release of lipopolysaccharides (LPS, endotoxins), for example in cows with subacute ruminal acidosis, puerperal metritis or acute mastitis, must be considered as a risk for the development of hypocalcaemia [48]. In dairy cows, infusion of LPS is associated with a decrease in total serum calcium concentration in a dose-dependent manner; the greater the LPS concentration, the more severe the decrease in serum calcium levels. It has been suggested that a reduction in serum calcium concentration may protect against endotoxaemia [48]. Therefore, calcium administration in septic patients with hypocalcaemia should be done carefully to avoid exacerbation of calcium overload in tissues [49]. The administration of calcium during sepsis increases organ failure and mortality [48]. In the present study, cows with SIO probably did not have endotoxaemia, particularly the acute cases. Intravenous administration of a calcium borogluconate solution is therefore indicated to treat hypocalcaemia in cattle with ileus due to obstruction when endotoxaemia can be ruled out.

The prokinetic drugs we administered have also been used in horses to stimulate gastrointestinal motility [1], although their use in animals with impaired small intestinal motility has not been studied. In particular, the use of parasympathomimetic drugs in cattle with ileus has been criticised [33]. The same dose of these drugs has been used successfully in cows with caecal dilatation [50]. Metoclopramide, which is no longer approved for use in cattle in Switzerland, was recommended for supportive treatment of internal intestinal displacement [2].

Short and long-term outcomes

The discharge rate of cattle with SIO was 87.3% (96/110), which was similar to the rate of cattle with strangulation (81.7%, 49/60) [32] but considerably higher than in cows admitted because of intussusception (44.4%, 56/126) [29], volvulus (38.3%, 18/47) [31] and mesenteric torsion (23.0%, 14/61) [38]. The difference in discharge rates reflects the level of severity of the various forms of mechanical ileus. Mesenteric torsion is the most severe form and has the lowest discharge rate. Discharge rates are important because they allow a prognosis once a diagnosis has been made. However, it is unfortunate that a definitive diagnosis is usually only possible during laparotomy when a treatment decision has already been made. Ideally, a diagnosis and reliable prognosis would be possible based on the findings of the initial examination. Two years after discharge, 40.6% of the cattle were still productive, which was considered positive. Owners should be made aware of the risk of recurrence based on our rate of 19.8% so that they can choose between culling and further treatment should this occur.

Comparison of 96 surviving and 14 non-surviving cattle

Non-surviving cattle had abdominal dilatation and blood, mucus and fibrin in the rectum significantly more often than cattle that survived, which was an indication of the marked severity of disease. Surprisingly, surviving cattle had a significantly lower rectal temperature (38.3 °C) than non-surviving cattle; we expected the opposite. Interestingly, all other clinical and laboratory results did not differ significantly between the two groups. In other words, the non-surviving cattle were not in a more severe state of shock than the surviving cattle.

Postmortem findings

Three of 4 cattle with bezoars in the jejunum or ileum were euthanized immediately after the clinical examination, and SIO was confirmed at postmortem examination. In the fourth cow, laparotomy revealed multiple food masses obstructing the duodenum and jejunum. The cow did not completely recover from surgery and was euthanized 7 days postoperatively. Postmortem examination showed new food masses obstructing the spiral colon. Three cattle had postoperative paralytic ileus, which is rare in cattle compared with horses [1] and may be caused by overexpansion of the intestinal wall after mechanical ileus [51]. Abomasal ulcers seen in 2 cows and 1 bull may have developed after laparotomy because these animals deteriorated postoperatively and had to be euthanized on days 3, 6 and 9 postoperatively.

Conclusions

Small intestinal obstruction in cattle has a good prognosis provided that surgical treatment is carried out promptly; of 110 cattle admitted, 96 (87.3%) were successfully treated and discharged. Abdominal dilatation and the presence of blood, mucus and fibrin in the rectum were more common in non-surviving cattle and were associated with a poor prognosis.
