AIM2 framework for smart marketing innovation using AI driven consumer analytics with SOR neural networks and XGBoost in Saudi retail
Authors: Fawaz Khaled Alarfaj, Mohamed Badouch, Hikmat Ullah Khan, Mehdi Boutaounte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study introduces the AIM2 (AI-Integrated Marketing Innovation Model) framework by integrating the Stimulus–Organism–Response (SOR) model with advanced machine learning methods for making sense of consumer analytics in Saudi retail. Using real data from Tamimi Markets, clustering methods put products into budget, intermediate, and luxury categories with a 92% silhouette score. Predictive analysis showed that XGBoost had a 14% smaller error margin than traditional regression and 9% more accuracy than simple Neural Networks. These results go beyond the current retail analytics methods that report less than 80% accuracy and highlight the value of incorporating AI-powered techniques with SOR. The study contributes to both theory and practice by demonstrating the AIM2 framework in a real retail context and providing practical tips for retailers who want to keep up with the modern marketing goals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42787-3.Subject terms: Information systems and information technology, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The transition to digital commerce has resulted in the extensive use of modern technological tools in marketing, thereby changing the interaction between businesses and customers, and consequently, the choice of the latter. Old-style marketing methods, which relied heavily on mass communication and uniform campaigns, are now often replaced by smart, flexible arrangements utilizing AI, ML, and DL for delivering personalized interactions and providing timely insights. This transformation is very pronounced in retail, where customer expectations regarding convenience, personalization, and reliability have increased quite a lot. By the end of 2024, global online sales have reached more than 6 trillion USD, which account for more than 15% of U.S. retail transactions, while the use of live-streaming and AI-driven commerce in Asia attracted hundreds of millions of users1. These developments present opportunities as well as difficulties; on the one hand, advanced technology allows for precise marketing and active customer participation, and on the other hand, it raises issues related to data protection, transparency, and sustainability.

Innovative tools such as neural networks, boosting algorithms like XGBoost, and multi-modal AI (Artificial Intelligence) setups are slowly gaining acceptance in customer analysis and are providing better quality of forecasts than the old, simple statistical or rule-based approaches. Neural networks are excellent at grasping complex and nonlinear relationships in consumer behavior, boosting algorithms such as XGBoost have repeatedly demonstrated superior performance over traditional regression models and many single-architecture learners in structured retail datasets, particularly in heterogeneous and nonlinear consumer environments. Their ensemble tree optimization and regularization mechanisms enable more stable generalization and lower predictive error2. These techniques perfectly suit retail data, which can present an eclectic mix of product types and prices, along with greatly un- predictable consumer preferences. By injecting these technologies into promotional strategies, companies can improve audience segmentation, dynamic pricing, and recommendation systems which will in turn directly increase customer satisfaction and loyalty. At the same time, conceptual models remain important in grounding the tech applications in consumer psychology. The Stimulus-Organism-Response (SOR) model which is widely used in marketing and consumer studies is a good example of how tech-based stimuli (such as AI-powered ads, personalized tips) can be broken down into consumer perceptions (organism) and behaviors (response). Latest studies have modified SOR to situations such as AI assistants, AR, and live streaming shopping, and demonstrated its role in clarifying trust, participation, and buying intention3. Nevertheless, the literature is scarce on the integration of SOR with cutting-edge AI techniques for the development of a comprehensive method for marketing innovations. Conversely, studies on the integration of SOR with advanced AI techniques in the creation of a holistic framework for marketing innovation remain limited.

Saudi Arabia gives us a rare opportunity to investigate this integration from a different angle. The Kingdom has earmarked digital transformation, retail modernization, and sustainable consumption as the main goals of its Vision 2030. The retail industry, which is already worth more than SAR 400 billion, is being digitized at a very fast pace, with retail giants and e-commerce stores using Al-powered methods for stocks, clients, and prices to make them more dynamic. There is however not enough empirical research based on real data from Saudi retail sectors. Consumer surveys or global datasets make up the bulk of the studies so far, creating a region-specific, data-driven analysis that is both theory and practice informing voids. Closing this gap is of utmost importance since Saudi consumers have different cultural likes, use different amounts of products in different seasons (for example Ramadan and Eid), and have varying trust levels that impacts the effectiveness of marketing4.

The AIM2 (AI-Integrated Marketing Innovation Model) framework, which is presented in this research, has been developed to connect the new technologies with the marketing theory. The AIM2 model is basically the SOR model integrated with the advanced AI techniques such as clustering, association rule mining, neural networks, and XGBoost for the purpose of analyzing consumer behavior in Saudi retail. The Tamimi Markets dataset5, which provides extremely detailed information about products and prices, has been used in the framework to show the accuracy of AI-driven analytics in revealing consumer segments, spotting cross-selling opportunities, and estimating price elasticity accurately. By integrating theoretical underpinnings and empirical investigation, AIM2 creates a new pathway for the marketing innovation literature. The importance of AIM2 comes from its technological and marketing dimensions that are integrated together. In the case of the technology part, AIM2 enhances the ML (Machine Learning)/DL (Deep Learning) methods to analyze the complicated retail data thereby reaching predictive accuracies which are up to 14% higher than those obtained with traditional regression models and also beating the baseline neural networks by 9% in predicting consumer preferences. As far as the marketing aspects of AIM2 are concerned, it employs the SOR model to interpret the results and also to connect the stimuli (AI-driven promotions) at the organism level (trust, perceived value) and the behavioral outcomes (purchase intention, loyalty)6. This integration of both sides makes the technological insights not only predictive but also theoretically significant thus providing retailers with strategies that are actionable.

The literature has been providing stronger and stronger evidence for the necessity to integrate AI and Analytics. Cillo and Rubera7 emphasize that AI and Advanced Analytics have revolutionized marketing by means of Hyper-Personalization and Predictive Engagement, but they lack theoretical frameworks to comprehend consumer reactions to such innovations. Acatrinei et al.8 on the other hand, argue that AI-driven Marketing can engage consumers positively and influence their behaviors sustainably if Trust and Transparency are accounted for in the systems. Kasem et al.2 assert that AI-powered Segmentation and Profiling lead to over 90% accuracy (as against Direct Marketing) when compared with the traditional marketing methods. Almaafi et al.4 support their claim that XGBoost algorithms are more accurate than ARIMA models in the forecasting of Saudi Telecom Stock Prices, thereby making a case for the use of XGBoost models in Retail Analytics. Davtyan3 claims that the Strategic Role of AI is prominent in the analysis of Consumer Behavior, and that Zhang et al.1 employ Hesitant Fuzzy Methods for determining the Risk involved in Live Streaming Marketing, thus accentuating the need for Robust Frameworks in Digital Commerce.

This study adds to the knowledge pool in three major ways. To begin with, it presents the AIM2 Framework, a novel approach that combines SOR with Advanced AI Methodologies to develop a conceptual model for Smart Marketing Innovation. Secondly, the research applies the AIM2 Framework to a Real-World Saudi Retail Dataset in order to provide Empirical Insights into Consumer Segmentation, Cross Selling, and Price Prediction. Last but not least, it again relates the findings from the earlier parts to the Goals set in Vision 2030, thus showing how Emerging Technologies can help with Digital Transformation and Sustainable Consumption in the Saudi Retail sector.

AI outputs become consumer stimuli via an interface translation layer. Computational artifacts such as cluster centroids, association‑rule metrics, or model embeddings are latent representations that are not directly perceived by consumers. In practice these outputs are transformed into marketing action artifacts (for example: personalized offers, targeted recommendation lists, dynamic price messages, or tailored email content). It is these artifacts — not the internal algorithmic vectors — that constitute the external Stimuli in the SOR model. Formally the pipeline is: AI representation space → Marketing action layer → Consumer perceptual stimulus, where the marketing action layer maps algorithmic signals to interface elements that consumers observe and evaluate. This mapping grounds AIM2 in perceptual psychology and clarifies how algorithmic outputs can be interpreted as stimuli that influence organismic constructs (trust, perceived value) and behavioral responses.

Literature review

The marketing strategy has been radically altered due to the infusion of artificial intelligence (AI). AI is being applied in various ways in marketing to analyze the consumer behaviors and come up with the respective strategies of engagement, personalization, and trust-building. Moreover, the traditional e-commerce models comprising of fixed websites and one-size-fits-all promotions at their core are rapidly being superseded by real-time, engaging platforms that use customer data for predictive analytics and intervention targeting. This change is quite visible in the retail sector, where the skill of consumer segmentation, demand forecasting, and recommendation generation according to individual’s preferences has turned into the key to competitive advantage. Nevertheless, the SOR model of Stimulus-Organism-Response continues to be an important theoretical framework that can show the effects of technological stimuli on the perceptions and behaviors of consumers. The area of AI in marketing and consumer analytics touches numerous fields such as clustering, associational rule mining, neural networks, and XGBoost, the latter being one of the ensembles learning methods. The mentioned technological capabilities not only find applications in marketing but also in the healthcare, finance, energy, and public policy sectors implying their widespread usage and relevance.

Consumer analytics using clustering

The use of clustering techniques has always been widespread in marketing as they help in recognizing consumer segments and product categories. K-Means Clustering, being one of the most popular among the methods, divides the buyers into different groups according to their purchasing habits and allows the companies to create promotions that target each group specifically. Liu et al.9 utilized K-means clustering on supermarket transaction data and were able to identify three principal customer clusters comprising budget-conscious, mid-range, and premium shoppers. The implications of their study are such that it becomes clear how important it is to resort to segmentation in evolving different marketing strategies that are not only effective but also less costly to the firm. Similarly, Hierarchical clustering has been applied to find the product categories with similar consumption patterns.

For instance, Tabianan et al.10 argued that hierarchical clustering could unveil the relationships between product categories making it easier for retailers to come up with bundling strategies that would be in sync with consumer preferences. Apart from marketing, clustering is heavily employed in healthcare for patient stratification where patients are grouped by their clinical characteristics into different categories so as to design personalized treatment plans. In the financial sector, clustering helps in the identification of customer risk profiles that allow banks to create different kinds of credit products. Utilities use clustering in the power industry to analyze consumption patterns thus aiding demand-side management and load forecasting. All these applications go to prove that clustering is a powerful tool that can be used for the analysis of heterogeneous datasets across sectors.

The method of association rule mining gives a better understanding of cross-selling opportunities through the detection of those products that customers frequently buy together. An important contribution to this field was made by Kumar and Patel11 who developed the Apriori algorithm that is still prevalent for retail analysis. The supermarket database has been subjected to FP-Growth technique in recent days. For instance, Chopvitayakun et al.12 showed that FP-Growth could back up retailers in crafting bundling strategies that would ultimately boost sales by pinpointing co-occurrences of complementary goods like coffee and biscuits. In the marketing realm, the association rules are relied on to set up promotions that bring together the selling of complementary goods, thus raising the average basket size and consumer satisfaction levels.

In their study of the online retail industry, Joseph et al.13 applied association rule mining, where the patterns, in turn, can be used to inform dynamic pricing strategies. Their research not only indicates that association rules can give both revenue and trust to consumers by synchronizing promotions with actual buying behavior but also suggests that retailers can leverage more by aligning their marketing campaigns with consumer behavior. The use of association rule mining has also been reported in the field of bioinformatics for the identification of gene co-expression patterns, in cybersecurity for the analysis of attack sequences, and in education for the assessment of student learning behaviors. The above-mentioned cases demonstrate the capability of association rule mining to be applied in various fields with complex and interrelated data as its characteristic.

Neural networks in marketing and other sectors

Neural networks are now at the forefront of consumer analytics allowing companies to discover and exploit the nonlinear relationships which exist in buying behavior. One of the applications of feedforward neural networks is for the forecasting of product demand. Bellar et al.14 applied feedforward networks to retail sales data, achieving mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) reductions of 10% compared to linear regression. Deep learning models, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have been applied to sentiment analysis in e-commerce. Zhao et al.15 demonstrated that CNNs can analyze consumer reviews with accuracy improvements of 12% compared to traditional text-mining approaches.

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks have been used to forecast sequential consumer behavior. Jin16 applied RNNs to predict purchase intention, finding that sequential models outperform logistic regression in contexts with temporal data. LSTM networks have found their application in the area of time-series sales forecasting. Afoudi et al.17 confirmed that LSTMs were capable of capturing the long-term dependencies in retail sales data, and thus they attained better accuracy than the classical autoregressive models. In addition to marketing, neural networks are broadly utilized in healthcare for diagnosis, in finance for detection of fraud, in transportation for prediction of traffic congestion, and in power for the smart grid’s optimization. These usages emphasize the adaptable nature of neural networks in the processing of difficult and non-linear data in different fields.

Ensemble learning techniques, especially XGBoost, have become the leading methods in consumer analytics. Vafaei-Zadeh et al.18 conducted a comparison of the two techniques (XGBoost and GBM) in the retail sector, and the results indicated that XGBoost outperformed its rival in all aspects of error rates and interpretability. Their work supports the claim that ensemble methods are powerful enough to deal with the issue of noise in the retail datasets. Jiang and Lyu19 used XGBoost for the consumer churn prediction problem, and they received accuracy measures over 90%. The authors regard this as one more reason to believe that ensemble techniques are the go-to ones for the complex, non-linear data. XGBoost has also been utilized beyond retail, such as in the finance sector for credit scoring, and healthcare for disease risk predictions, and energy for the forecasting of renewable load consumption. Paliari et al.20 declared that XGBoost was the winner of the competition against ARIMA when it came to predicting the price of Saudi telecom stocks, thus confirming its relevance to the financial field. These cases are just a few examples of the widespread usage of the methods throughout different industries.

Multimodal artificial intelligence, combining text, visuals, and sensor inputs, marks a growing field within consumer research. César et al.21 adopted this method for product review analysis, combining textual ratings with picture of the item to make sentiment assessment stronger. According to their findings, multi-modal data methods result in richer understanding of customer mind. Marketing applies such systems to process social media content where posts, images, and videos are mixed together for brand sentiment measurement. The medical field makes use of similar approaches by combining diagnostic imaging with the patient’s medical record in order to make clinical choices more accurate. Urban management systems upscale the reading from connected Internet of Things (IoT) devices to facilitate control over the traffic flow. This situation illustrates how AI can facilitate deep analysis by merging different data streams in different sectors, though at times there might still be some grammatical errors in the real situation where perfection is unattainable.

SOR framework in digital commerce

The SOR (Stimulus, Organism, Response) model is the major theoretical framework that helps to analyze shopper behavior in the context of retail businesses employing AI technologies. Although initially proposed by Sohaib et al.22 for environmental psychology purposes, the framework has found wide application in consumer research. In the context of online sales, external stimuli include algorithm-driven discounts, personalized recommendations, and changes in the user interface. Customers’ cognitive-affective evaluations like trust, value, and satisfaction are seen as internal organismic responses, while buying mood and brand loyalty are the consequences of such responses. Alsadoun and Alnasser23 investigated how AI chatbots affect customer communication and found that, among other things, speed and personalization significantly influence trust and satisfaction. Besides, Umezurike et al.24 applied SOR to map out augmented shopping experiences and indicated that exciting stimuli escalate hedonic perception and shopping urge. Sonboli et al.25 argued that the transparency of algorithmic filtering increases consumer trust, which in turn raises the likelihood of transaction. All these findings together point to the necessity of integrating theoretical psychological constructs with practical digital applications.

Saudi retail transformation under Vision 2030

Saudi Arabia provides a unique scenario for the research of AI-based marketing innovations. The Vision 2030 of the country redefines digital transformation, retail improvements, and environmentally friendly consumption as the main areas of attention. A study by Saad et al.26 which took Saudi Arabia as the central point on consumers’ trust in online retail revealed that transparency and honesty were the major factors that swayed the buyers’ decisions. In addition to these, the consumption trends at different times, for example, spikes in usage during Ramadan and Eid, are among the factors that shape retail strategies. SDAIA (Saudi Data and AI Authority) is leading the charge by putting in place AI-focused initiatives targeting retail and logistics sectors. One such study by Memish et al.27 revealed that among the key areas for the application of AI are stock control, customer communication, and flexible pricing. The aforementioned projects have presented a great opportunity for the frameworks like AIM2 that link AI techniques to consumer behavior theory to be executed. Lemonde et al.28 have mentioned that the smart city application of multimodal AI and suggested that retail marketing could gain insights from urban analytics even more.

The academic community has taken note and is discussing the considerable progress made in the areas of AI-driven marketing, customer analysis, and SOR theory. Clustering algorithms and the use of rules derived from associations not only help in identifying the target audience but also in suggesting complementary products. On the other hand, the methods like artificial neural networks and ensemble methods e.g., XGBoost yield the best in terms of accuracy of forecasting. Artificial neural networks plus assistance methods like XGBoost achieve the best in forecasting accuracy. AI systems that are integrated give comprehensive insight by the unified information from various sources. For one thing, the SOR paradigm provides an excellent viewpoint for analyzing consumer behavior; however, its integration with artificial intelligence proves to be limiting. The Saudi Vision 2030 project is an excellent case for applying these models due to the fact that it is mainly about tech- modernization and ethical consumerism.

In this research, the AIM2 model is proposed as a remedy which is a blend of SOR and AI to conduct close studies on purchasers’ behaviors in the context of retail in Saudi Arabia. Utilizing detection, association rule mining, deep learning models, and boosted decision trees on the sales data of Tamimi Markets, the work demonstrates that the application of machine-learning-assisted analysis can not only make marketing strategies smarter, but also increase customer trust and be in line with the goals of Vision 2030.

AIM2 conceptual framework

The AIM2 framework constitutes a tiered architecture meant to amalgamate the sophisticated machine learning models with the behavioral marketing theory. It seeks to facilitate the creation of smart and flexible marketing systems that rely on data and are anchored in psychology at the same time. AI has been widely accepted as an important factor in retail analytics, but the majority of the current methods emphasize only predictive accuracy and ignore the cognitive and emotional aspects of consumer behavior. Our solution is filling this gap by putting the SOR model at the center of its design ensuring that the outputs of technology are judged by a behavioral standard and then converted into significant marketing actions.

AIM2’s basis is composed of the Artificial Intelligence Layer that interprets unprocessed retail data with four essential techniques: clustering, association rule mining, neural networks, and XGBoost. In order to find out different segment’s groups such as budget-affording, mid-range, and premium shoppers, Clustering algorithms are implemented to group consumers by their buying habits. By analyzing co-purchase patterns, Association rule mining, especially through FP-Growth or Apriori algorithms, reveals product relations and suggests cross-selling areas29. Neural networks—these can be feedforward, convolutional, or recurrent—are applied in demand forecasting and sentiment analysis to discover nonlinear relationships in those fields. XGBoost, an effective combined learning method is deployed on churn prediction, price sensitivity modeling, and dynamic pricing optimization among others. The AI elements carry out their respective functions simultaneously, hence they produce stimuli that are directed to the upper tier of the framework as input. Our solution fusion block therefore operates at the marketing action layer: fused algorithmic signals are converted into concrete interface artifacts (e.g., a “recommended for you” bundle or a time‑limited discount) that consumers perceive as stimuli.

The SOR Framework Layer is the second layer of our system and it interprets AI-produced outputs in relation to consumer psychology. The layer here processes the AI models’ outputs—by means of personalized recommendations, dynamic promotions, and targeted messaging—as outside triggers. These stimuli were statistically associated with organism‑level constructs such as trust and perceived value, as estimated by the SEM path coefficients. The SOR model thus captures the whole process from stimuli to psychological responses as marketing literature using constructs. Observed behaviors like purchase intention, brand loyalty, and advocacy are grouped under the final component response. The SOR model embedded in the architecture of AIM2 allows AI-driven insights not only to be predictive but also to be explanatory, providing marketers with the “why” behind consumer actions.

The last and most important layer is the Integration and Governance Layer, which guarantees that the system functions in an ethical, transparent, and adaptive way. MLOps practices are introduced in this layer to keep watch over model performance, spot bias, and apply equity among the different consumer segments. A mechanism for feedback loop is also included, which consumes the AI layer for continuous model refinements the consumer responses and then feeds them back into the AI layer for continuous model refinements. This back-and-forth process not only allows AIM2 to constantly learn but also to adapt to consumer preferences, seasonal trends, and market forces that are constantly changing. The integration layer therefore turns AIM2 from a mere analytics engine into a vibrant learning system which can power marketing innovation for a long term.

The process in our system is such that it has a linear and cyclical nature at the same time. It starts with user engagement, which gets processed through AI models to produce stimuli. These stimuli, upon being interpreted using the SOR framework, result in psychological and behavioral reactions. The governance and fairness analyses are done on the responses, and the knowledge obtained is applied to retraining and fine-tuning the AI models. This closed-loop system grants the technology the ability to adapt to changes in customer behavior and keep up with ethical practices. With this, the digital transformation of retail environments through smart marketing innovation is thereby made possible.

The dataset was obtained from Tamimi Markets and contains transactional records with product category, price, quantity, pseudonymized customer IDs, and timestamps. Numerical attributes were normalized with min–max scaling to stabilize algorithms sensitive to feature scale30:

Categorical variables (e.g., Product_Category) were one-hot encoded for tabular learners, and embedded for neural networks when beneficial. Outliers were removed via the interquartile range (IQR) rule31:

and temporal duplicates were resolved to avoid leakage across splits. Data partitioning followed a task-specific protocol. For time-series forecasting models (including LSTM), a strictly chronological split was applied to prevent temporal leakage: the earliest 70% of observations formed the training set, the subsequent 15% the validation set, and the most recent 15% the test set. No future timestamps were available to the model during training. For non-temporal tasks (e.g., churn classification and clustering), stratified splitting by Customer_ID was used to avoid identity leakage while preserving behavioral distributions.

Evaluation metrics were selected per task. For demand regression, we monitored RMSE. RMSE (root mean square error) measures average absolute error in the original units and penalizes large misses; MAPE reports average percentage error and is easy to interpret but becomes unstable for zeros and very small actuals, so use it cautiously for low‑volume SKUs32.

and MAPE (mean absolute percentage error) expresses average error as a percentage of actuals32,

with special attention to low-volume SKUs where relative errors are operationally significant. For churn classification, we tracked accuracy, AUC, and log-loss on the validation set.

The organism-level variables (trust, perceived value, and satisfaction) were not inferred solely from transactional logs. A structured consumer survey was conducted among loyalty-program customers using validated multi-item Likert scales adapted from SOR-based marketing studies. Survey responses were pseudonymously linked to transactional records through hashed customer identifiers under retailer data governance approval. Transactional attributes (e.g., promotion exposure, price variability, recommendation diversity, and purchase consistency) served as stimulus indicators, while survey-measured latent variables constituted the organism layer in the SEM model. This hybrid survey–behavior linkage allowed psychological constructs to be measured directly while preserving privacy.

Clustering quality was measured using the silhouette score; reported values include the mean and standard deviation across 1,000 bootstrap resamples. Predictive models were compared using RMSE, MAE, and accuracy where appropriate; for each metric we report the point estimate and a 95% confidence interval computed via bootstrap (1,000 resamples) on the held‑out test set. Baselines: (1) Traditional regression refers to ordinary least squares (OLS) trained on the same features; (2) Baseline neural network refers to a single hidden‑layer feedforward network with 64 units and ReLU activation trained under the same preprocessing and train/test split.

Data integrity verification

The Tamimi Markets dataset used in this study was obtained from the public repository cited in the Data Availability statement. Because public datasets can contain errors or incomplete metadata, we performed a systematic integrity verification before analysis. Steps included: (1) Range checks on price and quantity fields to identify implausible values; (2) Temporal validation to confirm expected seasonality (e.g., Ramadan and Eid purchase spikes) by comparing transaction counts across calendar months; (3) Basket statistics (mean and median items per transaction) compared with regional retail reports to ensure realistic basket sizes; (4) Outlier audits where transactions with prices or quantities beyond three interquartile ranges were inspected and removed only when clearly erroneous; (5) Missingness analysis and imputation rules documented in preprocessing. Governance and fairness auditing

The dataset used in this study contains transactional records and customer identifiers but does not include explicit demographic attributes such as gender, nationality, or ethnicity. Consequently, we did not perform fairness audits across legally protected demographic groups. Instead, fairness checks were conducted across behavioral segments derived from the data (for example, price‑sensitivity clusters and spending tiers). Specifically, we measured disparate impact and parity metrics across these behavioral groups to detect systematic differences in model recommendations or predicted actions. We explicitly avoided inferring demographic attributes from transactional data because such inference can introduce additional bias and ethical risk. Because demographic labels were not available and no demographic inference was performed, formal fairness assessments across protected groups (e.g., gender, nationality) were not possible in this study.

Discussion

The data confirms that AIM2 generates logical, practical insights spanning segmentation, association analysis, predictive analytics, and behavioral assessment. The clustering phase identified three steady buyer categories—economy, mid-tier, and high-end—with a strong mean silhouette score (0.92), suggesting the groupings represent genuine structure within standardized behavior traits like basket worth, shopping regularity, category variety, and price responsiveness. In practice, this implies the segments stem from persistent tendencies rather than scaling quirks or distortion, making them suitable for tailored promotional tactics. The FP-Growth correlations further bolster segmentation by pinpointing robust co-purchase trends (rules sustained at confidence > 0.85 and lift > 1), allowing for segment-specific bundle recommendations and incentive strategies.

Predictively, comparative testing revealed a distinct edge for ensemble tree models on retail tabular datasets: XGBoost surpassed regression and neural benchmarks in churn and pricing tasks by 14% fewer errors and 9% greater accuracy. Meanwhile, sequential neural structures (LSTM) excelled in demand prediction scenarios with time-based influences and seasonal peaks (e.g., Ramadan, Eid). Thus, blending LSTM for sequence-based forecasting and XGBoost for classification/regression tasks presents a sensible hybrid approach, matching each technique to data structures where its strengths apply. Explainability and oversight layers—SHAP for variable contribution and disparity impact (DI) tracking—verified reliance on understandable factors (basket fluctuations, recent activity, price trends) while upholding fairness standards (DI > 0.8), endorsing ethically sound implementation.

Relation to prior studies and working hypotheses

These findings align with and extend prior work in several ways. The superiority of XGBoost on heterogeneous retail tabular data echoes earlier comparative studies that report ensemble methods’ robustness to feature heterogeneity and noisy interactions; our results quantify that advantage in the Saudi retail context and show it holds when models are integrated into a broader behavioral framework. The observed benefit of LSTM for demand forecasting is consistent with literature emphasizing recurrent architectures for seasonal and event‑driven retail demand. The high silhouette score and stable centroids corroborate prior segmentation studies that find a small number of meaningful shopper archetypes in supermarket data; however, AIM2 strengthens those results by linking segments to downstream stimuli and measurable conversion uplifts, thereby closing the loop between segmentation and action.

From the perspective of the working hypotheses—(1) that AI methods can produce robust segmentation and cross‑selling rules in Saudi retail, (2) that predictive models can materially improve churn and pricing decisions, and (3) that SOR integration map algorithmic stimuli to psychological constructs and behavioral outcomes—the evidence is supportive. Hypothesis (1) is validated by the high silhouette score and temporally stable association rules; hypothesis (2) is supported by the statistically significant performance gains of XGBoost and LSTM over baselines; and hypothesis (3) is corroborated by the SEM results where stimuli significantly predict organism constructs (β1 = 0.41, p < 0.01) and organism constructs strongly predict behavioral responses (β2 = 0.62, p < 0.001), with acceptable fit indices (CFI = 0.93, RMSEA = 0.06). Together, these outcomes suggest that AIM2 does more than optimize metrics: it produces outputs that are interpretable within consumer psychology and operationally useful for marketing decisions.

AIM2 provides an appropriate method for integrating computing frameworks with psychological concepts. The SOR framework indicates that stimuli, which are algorithm-driven, for instance, recommendations, bundled prices, and variable pricing, might be external factors whose influence on assurance and happiness is measurable and useful for purchasing behavior and loyalty. This combination combats one of the most common criticisms directed at AI systems, which are purely forecasting-based, in the business area: mere accuracy does not offer an explanation of the customer’s reactions’ motivations. By utilizing structural equation modeling in the procedure, AIM2 gives rise to a means of making causal inferences, within observational limits, while at the same time reconciling algorithmic outcomes with recognized commercial tenets.

Research findings spread across and outlined the ways for the retail businesses to do smarter practices. The first one is the clarified usage of segmentation and centroid analysis which grants companies the right to pay for their marketing strategies according to the segments: price-sensitive customers - cost-effective bundles, mid-tiers - diverse and convenient options, high-end - exclusive combinations. Next, the stable temporal validity of association rules can already be drawn as the suggestion for algorithms in the system of checkout or in case of an online shopping platform thus increasing the cart value without resorting to discounts as a heavy reliance. The third point is that XGBoost, due to its excellent performance in customer churn and pricing prediction, lead merchants to prefer tree ensemble methods for score and price optimization of retention while sequential neural approaches would be only adopted for time series forecasting. The fourth point is that SHAP interpretations and DI tracking would allow the deployment of these models to be accompanied by the clarity and fairness measures which are very important for regulations and consumer confidence especially in the up-and-coming markets where digitization is being pushed by the likes of Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 initiative.

However, several methodological constraints temper the findings. The data, though extensive and authentic, stems from a sole retailer (Tamimi Markets), prompting concerns over generalizability: store layouts, local tastes, and loyalty schemes differ among chains and regions, meaning models fitted to one may require adjustments elsewhere. The observational data also weakens causal inferences; while SEM offers a structured framework linking inputs to outputs, controlled experiments (A/B testing, randomized offers) remain essential to confirm causality and measure precise impact. Model accuracy further hinges on preprocessing decisions (feature design, encoding, outlier management) and tuning; despite rigorous validation and bootstrap intervals, live deployments demand ongoing checks for data drift and refreshes.

Balancing clarity with accuracy poses another challenge. XGBoost’s ensembles, though clearer than deep networks when using SHAP, still generate intricate relationships needing simplification for non-experts. The fairness evaluations (DI) applied here are vital yet incomplete: disparate impact, being a broad metric, may overlook nuanced biases or indirect discrimination. Lastly, technical and logistical hurdles—model speed, compatibility with older systems, and real-time scoring demands—introduce obstacles requiring both technical upgrades (MLOps) and cross-departmental coordination.

Future research directions

Several avenues for future research emerge naturally from this study. At a distance, replicating the cross-retailer and cross-country aspects would be a thorough trial of the generalizability of AIM2 and let us witness the effect of culture and market on the segmentation and rule stability42. Secondly, involving the randomized controlled trials as a part of the pipeline would transform the per-correlation findings into the causal ones, allowing the precise measurement of the incremental lift from the AI-driven stimuli and validating the SEM pathways in an experimental way43. Thirdly, the integration of multimodal data such as textual reviews, social media signals, in‑store sensor data, and images would greatly allow organism constructs (sentiment, engagement) to be enriched and promote the personalization particularly in higher premium segments where experiential factors matter. On top of that, the study on the privacy-preserving personalization (federated learning, differential privacy) is a must to bring personalization together with the regulations and moral ethics. Fifth, reinforcement learning approaches for dynamic pricing and promotion sequencing could be explored to optimize long‑term customer lifetime value rather than short‑term revenue. Sixth, fairness research should move beyond single metrics to multi‑criteria auditing frameworks that consider disparate impact, calibration, and long‑term equity effects across demographic and socioeconomic groups.

Methodologically, future work should investigate hybrid architectures that combine tree ensembles and neural sequence models more tightly—e.g., feature representations learned by LSTMs feeding into gradient boosting models—or meta‑learners that adapt model choice to SKU characteristics. Finally, longitudinal studies that track behavioral change over multiple seasons would illuminate the persistence of segmentation and the long‑term effects of personalized interventions on loyalty and sustainable consumption patterns.

Limitations

The study has several limitations that affect interpretation and generalizability and should be read in order of impact. Data provenance and demographic absence (High impact): the public Tamimi Markets transactional dataset lacks explicit demographic labels, which prevents formal fairness audits across protected groups and constrains subgroup analyses; this limitation most strongly restricts claims about equity and differential effects. Observational design (High impact): all SEM and association results are correlational and cannot establish causality without experimental validation (for example, randomized A/B tests of AI‑driven stimuli). Dataset bias and representativeness (Medium impact): although we performed integrity checks and compared summary statistics with national reports, publicly sourced retail datasets can underrepresent certain store types or customer segments, which may bias estimates and reduce external validity. Generalizability (Medium impact): findings are most directly applicable to grocery retail contexts similar to Tamimi Markets; extension to other retail formats, product categories, or cultural settings requires further validation. Fairness evaluation constraints (Low–Medium impact): because we avoided inferring demographics from transactions, fairness auditing was limited to behavioral segments rather than legally protected classes, reducing the scope of ethical assessment. Operational complexity (Low impact): deploying the full AIM2 pipeline in production requires engineering resources, monitoring, and governance that may limit immediate practitioner adoption. Despite these limitations, the system has clear strengths: pros include improved predictive accuracy through ensemble and representation learning, actionable segmentation that supports targeted promotions and cross‑selling, and an integrated governance layer that embeds MLOps and explainability mechanisms; cons mirror the limitations above and include dependence on high‑quality, representative data, constrained fairness evaluation without demographic metadata, and nontrivial operational overhead for deployment and continuous monitoring.

Conclusion

The paper presents a coherent, well‑documented implementation of the AIM2 framework that successfully integrates the Stimulus–Organism–Response (SOR) theoretical lens with a practical AI stack (K‑means clustering, FP‑Growth association mining, MLP/LSTM neural networks, and XGBoost) applied to a real Tamimi Markets retail dataset. Empirically, the study delivers strong, actionable results: a stable three‑segment solution with a silhouette ≈ 0.92, high‑confidence association rules (confidence > 0.85, lift > 1) that support cross‑sell strategies, LSTM models that capture seasonal demand patterns, and XGBoost models that reduce error by ~ 14% versus regression baselines and improve churn accuracy by ~ 9% versus baseline neural nets. Explainability (SHAP) and governance (disparate impact monitoring, DI > 0.8) are embedded in the pipeline, and SEM links AI‑generated stimuli to organism constructs (β₁≈0.41) and behavioral responses (β₂≈0.62) with acceptable fit (CFI ≈ 0.93, RMSEA ≈ 0.06), showing the framework produces both predictive power and theoretically interpretable marketing insights.

At the same time, the paper acknowledges important caveats that temper generalization: the analysis is based on a single retailer’s dataset (limiting external validity), the evidence is observational (so causal claims require randomized experiments), and fairness auditing relies on coarse metrics that should be complemented by richer bias diagnostics and long‑term equity assessments. For practitioners, AIM2 offers a pragmatic blueprint—use LSTM for temporal demand, XGBoost for tabular churn/pricing, and FP‑Growth for stable bundling—while enforcing MLOps, explainability, and fairness checks before deployment. For researchers, the study opens clear next steps: cross‑context replication, experimental validation of SEM pathways, multimodal data integration, and exploration of privacy‑preserving and reinforcement‑learning approaches to optimize long‑term customer value.
