Baseline Dynamics and Sleep Loss–Driven Changes of Electrocorticographic Aperiodic Properties Are Altered in Mice Lacking Neuroligin‐2
Authors: Tanya Leduc, Janine El Helou, Erika Bélanger‐Nelson, Clément Bourguignon, Jean‐Marc Lina, Valérie Mongrain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSpectral power decay (1/f β ) of cortical activity has long been considered of low relevance to the understanding of brain function (e.g., associated with background noise of neuronal activity). However, growing literature suggests that it can reflect optimized spatiotemporal organization of neuronal activity across the cortex serving both cognition and behavior. Thus, clarifying the mechanisms underlying the regulation of this network property could have numerous impacts for the neuroscience field as well as for patients suffering from various neurological disorders with cognitive deficits linked to alterations in brain power decay properties. Using a constitutive knockout mouse and a multifractal wavelet‐leaders formalism, we here investigated the involvement of the synaptic adhesion molecule Neuroligin‐2, which shapes synaptic inhibition and associates with neurodevelopmental diseases, in modulating spectral power decay properties of the electrocorticographic signal (i.e., the most prominent Hurst exponent “Hm” and its local “Dispersion”). Knockout mice showed increased Hm values across all wake and sleep states together with amplified daily dynamics during wake and paradoxical sleep, and increased Dispersion specifically for slow‐wave sleep in comparison with wild‐type littermates. Following a 6‐h sleep deprivation, Hm daily variations were more altered in knockout mice than controls for wake and paradoxical sleep, but less affected for slow‐wave sleep. Importantly, mutant mice display hypersynchronized epileptic‐like electrocorticographic events, which were characterized by further increases in Hm and Dispersion. These findings provide support for the involvement of Neuroligin‐2 in shaping 1/f β cortical activity properties and bring perspectives, notably, for neurodevelopmental conditions.

Introduction

Electrocorticographic (ECoG) or electroencephalographic (EEG) activity comprises two main types of electrical patterns: oscillatory activity (generally interrogated for discrete frequency bands such as delta 1–4 Hz or theta 6–9 Hz) and scale‐free phenomena (e.g., broadband shift and spectral power decay; Buzsáki and Draguhn 2004; He 2014). These are respectively referred to as periodic (i.e., rhythmic) and aperiodic (i.e., arrhythmic) properties of neuronal activity. Although specific oscillations and narrow‐band rhythmic activity have been studied for decades and repeatedly linked to multiple brain functions (Fries 2009; Schroeder and Lakatos 2009; Jensen et al. 2012; Klinzing et al. 2019), interest in aperiodicity metrics is more recent (He 2014; Dubé et al. 2026). In particular, the ECoG spectral power decay, generally defined as 1/f β (where f refers to the frequency and β to a scaling exponent), is thought to be of major relevance to cognition and behavior. Indeed, this aperiodic property has been found to vary across development and aging (Voytek et al. 2015; Dave et al. 2018; Stanyard et al. 2024), vigilance states (Ma et al. 2005; He et al. 2010; Zorick and Mandelkern 2013; Wen and Liu 2016; Lina et al. 2019; Lendner et al. 2020; Schneider et al. 2022), cognitive tasks (He et al. 2010; Voytek et al. 2015; Dave et al. 2018; Ouyang et al. 2020), and various neurodevelopmental/neurodegenerative disorders (Robertson et al. 2019). Moreover, oscillatory and scale‐free properties appear to give complementary information regarding brain state, with 1/f β power decay exponents sometimes holding more predictive value for cognitive scores and neurological pathologies than oscillatory activity metrics (Ouyang et al. 2020; Zorick et al. 2020; Maschke et al. 2023; Peterson et al. 2023). Accordingly, clarifying the molecular mechanisms shaping the ECoG spectral power decay is needed to link complex cortical electrical properties to cognition/behavior, and also to help guide earlier diagnosis and development of novel therapeutic avenues for numerous neurological conditions.

Among mechanisms that have been proposed to shape aperiodic spectral properties of the ECoG signal, there is support for contributions of elements that regulate the excitation/inhibition balance (Gao et al. 2017; Lombardi et al. 2017; Lina et al. 2019; Chini et al. 2022; Brake et al. 2024; Areal et al. 2025). For instance, our group showed that the adhesion protein Neuroligin‐1 (NLGN1), which controls the ratio of excitation to inhibition by shaping excitatory glutamatergic synapses (Chubykin et al. 2007; Budreck et al. 2013; Jedlicka et al. 2015), impacts wakefulness and slow‐wave sleep (SWS) aperiodic ECoG activity in mice (Lina et al. 2019; Areal et al. 2025). Neuroligin‐2 (NLGN2) is an adhesion protein located at gamma‐aminobutyric acid (GABA)‐ergic postsynaptic terminals and regulates the molecular organization of inhibitory synapses (Blundell et al. 2009; Poulopoulos et al. 2009; Jedlicka et al. 2011). This protein is also controlling the excitation/inhibition balance given that Nlgn2 knockout (KO) mice present impaired inhibitory post‐synaptic currents for a majority of studied brain regions (Chubykin et al. 2007; Poulopoulos et al. 2009; Jedlicka et al. 2011; Liang et al. 2015; Babaev et al. 2016, 2018; Cao et al. 2020), as well as altered neuronal excitability (Jedlicka et al. 2011). NLGN2 has been associated with autism spectrum disorders and schizophrenia (Sun et al. 2011; Deciphering Developmental Disorders Study 2015; Parente et al. 2017), and Nlgn2 KO mice display ECoG hypersynchronized epileptic‐like events during wake and paradoxical sleep (PS) (Seok et al. 2018; Cao et al. 2020; Leduc et al. 2024). Importantly, EEG 1/f β spectral properties were reported to be altered in autism spectrum disorders, schizophrenia, and epilepsy (Gadhoumi et al. 2015; Jia and Yu 2019; Peterson et al. 2023; Duma et al. 2024; Liao et al. 2024; Hasanaj et al. 2025; Kang et al. 2026; Zhang et al. 2025). Consequently, NLGN2 is in a key position to act as a determinant of aperiodic activity in the cerebral cortex.

Aperiodic ECoG properties vary across vigilance states (i.e., wake, SWS, and PS) as mentioned above, with spectral power decay generally being higher during SWS in comparison with wakefulness (Ma et al. 2005; He et al. 2010; Zorick and Mandelkern 2013; Wen and Liu 2016; Lina et al. 2019; Lendner et al. 2020; Schneider et al. 2022). However, whether aperiodic ECoG activity is modulated over the nychthemeron or in response to a homeostatic challenge such as sleep loss remains poorly studied, which prevents the understanding of its full physiological value(s). There is support for aperiodic activity to show a 24‐h rhythm, in particular when quantified for wakefulness and SWS (Lina et al. 2019; Areal et al. 2025), and to respond to extended wakefulness (Meisel et al. 2013; Areal et al. 2025). Given that mice lacking NLGN2 show a lower amplitude of 24‐h variations and of the response to sleep deprivation (SD) when considering specific oscillatory (periodic) activity (Seok et al. 2018; Leduc et al. 2024), and that Nlgn2 gene expression in the mouse cerebral cortex is reduced by SD (Leduc et al. 2024), it is predicted that it represents a structural synaptic component involved in the regulation of cortical aperiodic activity across the 24‐h day as well as in response to sleep loss.

To test the hypothesis that Nlgn2 KO mice have altered ECoG aperiodic properties, including 24‐h dynamics under undisturbed and sleep‐deprived conditions, we used a previously described multifractal wavelet‐leaders formalism (Jaffard et al. 2006; Ciuciu et al. 2008; Wendt et al. 2009; Lina et al. 2019). This mathematical approach estimates the most prominent Hurst exponent (Hm), which quantifies the spectral decay (β = 2Hm + 1) of a given ECoG segment, as well as its local instability/variability (Dispersion value), an informative metric that is not considered by other frequently used approaches (e.g., fitting oscillations and one‐over‐F algorithm, and detrended fluctuation analysis). We found the absence of NLGN2 to considerably impact aperiodic properties (Hm and Dispersion), in addition to the 24‐h dynamics and response to SD of these metrics. In addition, ECoG epileptic‐like events (i.e., hypersynchronized events) observed in Nlgn2 KO mice were found to be characterized by further alterations in aperiodicity, and the subcellular (synaptic) localization of NLGN2 protein was observed to be modified in the cerebral cortex after SD. Overall, the findings establish NLGN2 as a potential regulator of ECoG spectral power decay (aperiodic activity), and are of particular relevance in the context of neurodevelopmental disorders.

Methods

Animals

Adult male Nlgn2 wild‐type (WT), heterozygous (HET), and KO mice of mixed background (B6;129‐Nlgn2 tm1Bros /J) were implanted with ECoG/electromyographic (EMG) electrodes and underwent ECoG/EMG signal recording as detailed below. The same recordings were previously analyzed for wake and sleep architecture (including time spent in states), ECoG spectral power, and slow‐wave properties (Seok et al. 2018; Leduc et al. 2024), and were here (re)analyzed to extract multifractal scale‐free properties. The constitutive KO of Nlgn2 was originally generated by homologous recombination targeting the first coding exon of the mouse Nlgn2 gene (Varoqueaux et al. 2006). HET breeders were acquired from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, USA; strain #008139), and breeding was conducted on site to obtain animals of the three genotypes. The current multifractal analysis has included 11 WT mice (age and weight at implantation surgery: 10.1 ± 0.3 weeks, 27.4 ± 0.5 g), 12 HET mice (10.2 ± 0.3 weeks, 27.0 ± 0.9 g), and 12 KO mice (10.1 ± 0.3 weeks, 24.3 ± 0.8 g). This sample slightly differs from previous analyses (Seok et al. 2018; Leduc et al. 2024) because of the exclusion of animals for which signal quality was not suitable for aperiodicity analyses (e.g., predominant artifact for a large part of the recording and aberrant values). The three groups did not significantly differ for age (F 2,32 = 0.06, p = 0.9), but Nlgn2 KO mice weighed significantly less than both WT and HET mice (F 2,32 = 5.5, p < 0.01 vs. WT and p < 0.05 vs. HET), as previously reported (Seok et al. 2018; Leduc et al. 2024). Mice were single‐housed starting 2 weeks prior to surgeries and until sacrifice, were kept under a 12‐h light/12‐h dark cycle at a room temperature ranging from 23°C to 25°C, and had free access to both food and water throughout the experiment. A separate cohort of male C57BL/6J mice purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (strain #000664) was used to assess the effect of SD on NLGN2 protein expression. These mice were housed under the same conditions described above for at least 2 weeks before a 6‐h SD and sacrifice (SD protocol described below), and were aged between 11 and 14 weeks at the moment of sacrifice. All procedures have been approved by the Comité d'éthique de l'expérimentation animale of the Centre intégré universitaire de santé et services sociaux du Nord‐de‐l'Île‐de‐Montréal, and have been conducted following the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care.

Implantation of ECoG/EMG Electrodes

Surgeries for ECoG/EMG electrode implantations were conducted similarly to previously described (El Helou et al. 2013; Areal et al. 2020; Ballester Roig et al. 2023). Briefly, anesthetized mice (ketamine/xylazine 120/10 mg/kg injected intraperitoneally; isoflurane 0.5%–1% inhaled) were installed in a stereotaxic frame for the implantation of three gold‐plated screws (diameter of 1.1 mm): two serving as ECoG electrodes (motor cortex 1.5 mm anterior to bregma and 1.7 mm lateral right to midline; visual cortex 1.0 mm anterior to lambda and 1.7 mm lateral right to midline) and one serving as the reference (somatosensory cortex 0.7 mm posterior to bregma and 2.6 mm lateral right to midline). Three additional anchor screws were implanted above the opposite (left) hemisphere, and two gold wires serving as EMG electrodes were inserted in neck muscles. Electrodes were soldered to a plastic connector, which was secured to the skull using dental cement. The details of surgery material can be found elsewhere (Dufort‐Gervais et al. 2021). Mice were cabled and connected to the recording system (tethered) through a slip ring contact 5 to 6 days later, and a week of habituation to tethered condition took place before starting the recordings.

ECoG/EMG Recordings and Vigilance State Identification

Two weeks after surgical implantation, signals (i.e., motor cortex, visual cortex, and two EMG channels) were continuously recorded for 48 h starting at light onset (Zeitgeber time 0 [ZT0]): 24 h of baseline (BL: undisturbed conditions), 6 h of SD, and 18 h of recovery (REC). SD started at light onset (ZT0) and was performed by gentle disruption of the litter next to the mouse upon adoption of a sleeping behavior and, only if required, gently touching the tail of the animal. Paper tissues were given to help keep the animals awake when necessary, only during the third and sixth hours of the SD, and animals were transferred to a cage with clean litter at the beginning of the fourth hour. Amplified signals (Lamont amplifier) were sampled at 256 Hz using the Stellate Harmonie software (Natus, San Carlos, CA), and recordings were segmented offline into 4‐s epochs (i.e., 1024 samples) for visual identification of vigilance states (wake, SWS, PS) according to published criteria (Franken et al. 1999; Areal et al. 2025). The bipolar ECoG signal (i.e., motor minus visual) was used for this identification of vigilance states, as well as for the identification of hypersynchronized events and the multifractal analysis (see below). The identification of vigilance states was done blind to genotype mainly by two experienced scorers (i.e., identification order done by increasing mouse ID number and ID assigned consecutively for each litter containing all three genotypes prior to genotyping). However, the presence of ECoG hypersynchronized events in Nlgn2 KO mice that are almost not detectable in other genotypes (Seok et al. 2018; Leduc et al. 2024) prevented the blinding procedure from being fully effective because of the experience of scorers with “normal” (event‐free) ECoG signal.

Identification of ECoG Hypersynchronized Events and Signal Selection

Hypersynchronized events were previously manually identified on 24‐h ECoG traces of BL and SD + REC for KO mice and littermates (Seok et al. 2018; Leduc et al. 2024). Given that events are almost absent in HET and WT mice (Seok et al. 2018; Leduc et al. 2024), only KO mice were used in the following analyses. After the selection of recordings with adequate event signal quality, a total of 8 and 10 Nlgn2 KO mice were respectively included for the BL and SD + REC conditions for aperiodicity analyses of the current study. Hypersynchronized events were scored by two separate scorers for BL and SD + REC recordings with the same strict identification criteria: an amplitude at least twice that of the background signal and a minimum duration of 1 s, and events separated by less than 0.5 s were considered as a single event (same event detection as in Seok et al. 2018; Leduc et al. 2024). With these criteria, events were only observed during wake and PS (Seok et al. 2018; Leduc et al. 2024). Since mice can rapidly switch between vigilance states, only events surrounded by a single vigilance state (i.e., wake prior and after the event, or PS prior and after the event) were analyzed to prevent the inclusion of state transitions (Figure 1A). The ECoG signal surrounding hypersynchronized events was also analyzed. For this, a time window of 1.5 s prior to or following each event was selected in a manner preventing overlap between different vigilance states or between neighboring events (Figure 1A), which often followed each other closely in time (i.e., time windows overlapping other events were excluded).

(B) Schematized mathematical steps of the multifractal wavelet‐leaders formalism. The Wavelet transform evaluates the contribution of a wavelet (Daubechies wavelet of six vanishing moments) to a given selected ECoG signal segment through convolution of the two (i.e., sliding the wavelet over the signal and measuring the goodness of fit). The discrete approach evaluates a “discrete” number of wavelet scales 2 j for a selected range of j. It also calculates a “discrete” number of wavelet coefficients (nk) = 2 J − j , with J = log2(Fs * Ls), which depend on the sampling frequency (Fs) = 256 Hz, the signal length (Ls) > 1 s, and the interrogated scale (j). A greater number of nk will thus be obtained for smaller scales (higher frequencies). The final interrogated frequency range is [Fs/2j+1; Fs/2j]. Next, the wavelet leaders L j (k) are selected (defined as the maximum absolute values of wavelet coefficients within a local time‐scale neighborhood which best capture the amplitude of fluctuations across scales). In the schema, the interrogated j/k combination is marked by an X, the neighborhood by a gray shade, and the wavelet leader by a red dot. From this, a structure S q (j) function raising L j (k) to the q th power for a range of q values is built. This statistical manipulation allows to plot the obtained raised values as a function of j, and the slopes of this distribution are used to define a quadratic equation that can be solved to obtain the most prominent Hurst exponent Hm and the Dispersion (D) of Hurst exponents around Hm. (C) Examples of wake, slow‐wave sleep (SWS), and paradoxical sleep (PS) 24‐h distributions and corresponding interval distributions for the baseline (BL) and sleep deprivation with recovery (SD + REC) recordings for one wild‐type (WT) mouse. The time of each interval is centered on the midpoint of vigilance state occurrence and represented by a black circle. Intervals are adapted to each individual animal state distribution.

Multifractal Wavelet‐Leaders Formalism

A wavelet‐leaders formalism was used to conduct a multifractal analysis, extracting ECoG time‐resolved power decay properties as performed previously (Lina et al. 2019; Areal et al. 2025; Hector et al. 2026). This approach was adapted from a mathematical formalism applied to other types of signals (Jaffard et al. 2006; Ciuciu et al. 2008; Wendt et al. 2009), and considers the signal as governed by multiple and irregular decay exponents/power laws (also referred to as multifractality; Ma et al. 2005; Weiss et al. 2009). Multifractal analyses better interrogate the intricacies of aperiodic properties than standard monofractal estimations since they not only capture self‐similarity propensity (i.e., spectral power decay; β = 2Hm + 1) but also levels of multifractality (i.e., local variability of power laws; Dispersion [D]) (Ma et al. 2005; Weiss et al. 2009; La Rocca et al. 2018). Considering the ECoG/EEG signal as multifractal is further supported by the literature showing that self‐similarity (Hm) and multifractality (D) metrics behave differently in numerous physiological and pathological contexts (Weiss et al. 2009; La Rocca et al. 2018; Areal et al. 2025; Hector et al. 2026). The mathematical steps are schematized in Figure 1B and were conducted blind to genotype (batch processing).

Briefly, a discrete wavelet analysis using a Daubechies wavelet of six vanishing moments was applied. At various scales and locations in time, the wavelets act as local band‐pass filters interrogating frequencies between 0.5 and 64 Hz (upper boundary = sampling frequency divided by 4; wavelet scaling factor j from 2 to 8). From the resulting wavelet coefficients, the so‐called wavelet leaders are extracted (defined as the maximum absolute values of wavelet coefficients within a local time‐scale neighborhood that best capture the amplitude of fluctuations across scales). The statistics of the q th‐order moments of the wavelet leaders over a range of moment orders q = [−5,−1;1,5] are then computed. The slopes of the distribution of these q th‐order‐raised values as a function of scale can then be plotted as a function of q, approximated as a quadratic expansion 𝜁(𝑞) = 𝐻𝑚 𝑞 + 1/2 𝐷 𝑞2. When solved, this model gives the two main parameters assessed in the current study: the most prominent Hurst exponent (Hm) and the Dispersion (D) of Hurst exponents around Hm. Hurst exponents are a measure of long‐range dependency/temporal correlation, indicative of the steepness of the slope of ECoG power decay across frequencies (β = 2H + 1), with a greater value referring to a steeper slope (Flandrin 1992). A high Hm can thus be interpreted as more self‐similarity (less irregularity) of signal properties/organization across time scales (i.e., more long‐range dependency). The literature also sometimes refers to higher Hm and spectral decay values as representative of lower “spectral” complexity. The Dispersion (D < 0 for the concavity of 𝜁(𝑞)) informs on local instability of the power laws and multifractality (presence of multiple power decay exponents) of the signal. For any given short time period (epoch), a greater deviation from a Dispersion value of zero indicates higher local instability/diversity of the power laws governing long‐range dependencies of the signal (see also Lina et al. 2019; Areal et al. 2025; Hector et al. 2026). For all graphical representations of Dispersion, an inverted scale was used since “more negative” values are indicative of higher Dispersion.

Analyses of Aperiodicity Metrics

Hm and Dispersion values were computed for 4‐s artifact‐free epochs of each vigilance state for both BL and SD + REC recordings. Values were calculated for time intervals defined according to the distribution of wake and sleep states (same number of epochs contributing to each interval and more intervals when a given state occurs the most; Figure 1C), similar to previously conducted (Areal et al. 2025; Hector et al. 2026). For wake, this represented 6 intervals during the light period (ZT0 to ZT12) and 12 intervals during the dark period (ZT12 to ZT24) of the BL recording, then 9 intervals for the following light period (6 during the SD from ZT0 to ZT6, and 3 intervals from ZT6 to ZT12), and 12 intervals for the dark period of the SD + REC recording (ZT12 to ZT24). For SWS, values of Hm and Dispersion were calculated for 12 intervals during the light period (ZT0 to ZT12) and 6 intervals during the dark period (ZT12 to ZT24) of the BL recording, then 8 intervals for the following light period (all after the SD from ZT6 to ZT12) and 6 intervals for the dark period of the SD + REC recording (ZT12 to ZT24). Finally for PS, fewer equal intervals were used given the low overall time spent in this state: 8 intervals during the light period (ZT0 to ZT12) and 2 intervals during the dark period (ZT12 to ZT24) of the BL recording, then 8 intervals for the following light period (all after the SD from ZT6 to ZT12) and 4 intervals for the dark period of the SD + REC recording (ZT12 to ZT24).

To more finely investigate daily dynamics and control for absolute variations in Hm and Dispersion values, normalized values were also analyzed and defined as a percentage of the daily (24‐h) average of each animal for each vigilance state (done separately for BL and SD + REC). As Hm seemed to vary with a 24‐h rhythm reminiscent of a circadian pattern, a cosinor analysis with a fixed 24‐h frequency was fitted to the data of each genotype separately (by including all animals of each genotype). This allowed us to extract the half‐amplitude of daily variations and timing of the peak (phase) using the following equation, where y is normalized Hm, x is the time in hours, baseline is the average value of the fit, amplitude is the half distance between the fit maximum and minimum, and phase is the timing of the peak: y=baseline+amplitude·cos2πx−phase24

To explore changes in dynamics between BL and SD + REC conditions, nonlinear data fits were applied (for each genotype with the mean of all animals). This approach was selected since the time courses of the BL and SD + REC did not include the same number of time intervals, and because the intervals did not correspond to the exact same times of the day. Thus, nonlinear data curve fits allowed for a direct comparison of daily (24‐h) dynamics between conditions. Following the Ockham's razor (or Occam's razor) parsimony principle (Van Den Berg 2018; McFadden 2023), the simplest model that best fitted the data was selected for each dataset. A linear fit was first explored, followed by different polynomial fittings from 3rd order up to 5th order. The fit giving the highest R 2 score for both BL and SD + REC datasets was subsequently used to test whether a single curve (null hypothesis) fitted best the two datasets (BL vs. SD + REC) or if the data were better modeled by two different curves (alternative hypothesis), and this for each vigilance state. Fifth‐order polynomials were found to best represent the dynamics of wake aperiodic parameters, whereas a 4th‐order polynomial was found to better fit SWS and PS aperiodic parameter dynamics. Of note is that for this analysis to be conducted, a single curve model had to be chosen for all interrogated datasets, which resulted in the selection of suboptimal fits at some instances (i.e., selection of a polynomial order with intermediate fit for both BL and SD + REC datasets). This strategy thus informs whether a single or rather two different dynamics are found in the tested datasets (and not on the best nonlinear fit).

As Hm is indicative of the steepness of spectral power decay (Flandrin 1992), and since the 1/f β power decay suggests dependence of faster frequencies on the simultaneous activity occurring at slower timescales (Bak et al. 1987), the question of whether the massive changes in ECoG delta (1–4 Hz) power and dynamics observed in Nlgn2 KO animals (Seok et al. 2018; Leduc et al. 2024) were contributing to the potential differences in ECoG aperiodicity between genotypes was also explored. Genotype differences in 24‐h averages of Hm and Dispersion values were thus compared between analyses covering the 0.5–64 Hz and 4–64 Hz ranges for each vigilance state and recording day (BL and SD + REC). This was performed by changing the wavelet scaling factor j from [2,8] to [2,5] (Figure 1B). Daily dynamics of normalized values were also compared using equal intervals as described above.

Finally, Hm and Dispersion values were extracted from hypersynchronized events of various durations (minimum of one second), and from 1.5‐s segments of ECoG signal immediately preceding and following these events (see above). Aperiodicity metrics were then compared for wake and PS separately between normal signal (outside events), events, and pre‐/post‐event segments, while considering the light and dark periods of BL. All available events of all Nlgn2 KO animals included in this manuscript were then pooled to allow analyses of daily variations because considering animals separately resulted in many empty intervals. To normalize hypersynchronized event Hm and Dispersion metrics, individual event values were divided by the average of all pooled events (per metric and per vigilance state). Wake and PS relative aperiodicity metrics of BL events (calculated per time intervals) were compared with the dynamics of aperiodicity metrics of BL Nlgn2 KO normal signal using nonlinear fitting as described above. A 5th‐order polynomial was used for comparisons between hypersynchronized events and normal signal relative metric dynamics for both wake and PS. A similar analysis was performed for the SD + REC recordings. Finally, wake and PS hypersynchronized event relative Hm and Dispersion daily dynamics were compared between BL and SD + REC recordings to assess the impact of sleep loss again using nonlinear fittings. Fifth‐ and 4th‐order polynomials were respectively used for wake and PS.

Brain Sampling, Protein Extraction, and Western Blot

Sleep‐deprived and control animals were sacrificed around ZT6 (6 h after light onset after 6 h of SD or of undisturbed conditions) by unanesthetized cervical dislocation to prevent confounding effects of anesthesia. Upon sacrifice, brains were immediately sampled, and the cerebral cortex (bilateral bulk including mostly frontal, motor, somatosensory, visual and auditory cortices) and hippocampus (bilateral bulk including CA1, CA3 and the dentate gyrus of both the ventral and dorsal hippocampus; approximately between −2 and −3.4 mm relative to bregma) were dissected, then rapidly frozen on dry ice.

Total (Tot) and synaptoneurosomal (SN) protein extractions were performed as previously detailed (El Helou et al. 2013; Ballester Roig et al. 2023). Brain tissues were first washed with cold phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS; Sigma‐Aldrich, Saint Louis, USA) and homogenized in SN lysis buffer using a rotor‐stator homogenizer (Fisher Scientific, Ottawa, Canada). The SN lysis buffer was composed of 10 mM Hepes at pH 7.4, 1 mM EDTA, 2 mM EGTA, 0.5 mM DTT, and a phosphatase/protease inhibitor mixture 1:100 (Sigma‐Aldrich). A fraction of each homogenate was then sonicated (Q Sonica, Newton, USA) and centrifuged for 2 min at 16,200 g, and the supernatant was kept as the Tot protein fraction (which was further washed with a 1:1 protein‐to‐buffer ratio and centrifuged another 2 min at 16,200 g for purification). The remaining fraction of the initial homogenate was mixed with additional SN lysis buffer, centrifuged for 2 min at 2000 g, and the supernatant was filtered first using a 10‐μm pore nylon mesh filter (Small Parts Inc., Logansport, USA), then using a 5‐μm pore centrifugal filter (Ultrafree‐CL, Millipore, Billerica, USA), before another centrifugation of 15 min at 5000 g. The pellet was resuspended in boiling SN lysis buffer and purified using a 1:1.5 ratio of protein‐to‐buffer with a centrifugation of 2 min at 2000 g, which was considered the SN protein fraction. Protein concentrations of Tot and SN fractions were determined using the Lowry Assay and a NanoDrop ND‐2000 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA) or an EnSpire Multimode Plate Reader (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, USA).

For Western blot, 40 or 25 μg of protein were respectively loaded for Tot and SN protein on 7.5% precast SDS/PAGE gels (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Mississauga, Canada) and then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Bio‐Rad Laboratories). Each blot included a similar number of control (Ctrl) and SD samples. Blots were blocked in PBS (Sigma‐Aldrich) containing 0.05% Tween 20% and 5% (w/v) non‐fat dry milk for 30 min. Antibodies used in these assays were rabbit anti‐NLGN2 (1:2000; Synaptic Systems #129203), mouse anti‐Actin (1:10,000; Sigma‐Aldrich #A5441), HRP‐conjugated donkey anti‐mouse (1:2500; Santa Cruz Biotechnology #sc‐2318) and HRP‐conjugated mouse anti‐rabbit (1:10,000; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories #211‐035‐109). Anti‐NLGN2 and anti‐Actin antibodies were diluted in PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 and 5% (w/v) non‐fat dry milk. Membranes were revealed using either an Immun‐Star Chemiluminescent kit (Bio‐Rad #170‐5041), a Supersignal West Femto Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Scientific #34096) with films, or a Multigenius Bioimaging system (Syngene, Cambridge, UK). Signals were quantified with QuantityOne (Bio‐Rad), and relative NLGN2 amounts were normalized to Actin signals (NLGN2 optic density divided by Actin optic density, separately for Tot and SN). The SN/Tot ratio was calculated by dividing the normalized SN NLGN2 level by the normalized Tot NLGN2 level. Some representative blots (cortex) were done using the Snap i.d. 2.0 Protein Detection System (Millipore) and an Odyssey CLx Infrared Imaging System (Mandel). For these, blockage was performed using Odyssey Blocking Buffer (Mandel) diluted 1:1 in PBS. Above primary antibodies were diluted in blocking buffer/PBS containing 2% Tween 20, and Odyssey‐compatible fluorescent secondary antibodies were used for detection (Li‐cor; 1:20,000 LIC‐926‐32211; 1:15,000 LIC‐926‐68070).

Statistical Analyses

Time courses and 24‐h averages of ECoG aperiodicity metrics were interrogated using two‐ or three‐way repeated‐measure analyses of variance (ANOVA) with factors Genotype, Interval, and/or Frequency range (animals assigned to genotype only at this stage). Statistical significance was adjusted with a Huynh‐Feldt correction when needed. All significant interactions with the factor Genotype resulting from two‐ or three‐way ANOVA were decomposed using planned comparisons (largely focused on differences between KO mice and other genotypes for 10–21 equal intervals; no additional correction). Time courses were also analyzed using cosine and nonlinear fits as described above, with nonlinear fits compared using F tests. For Hm cosine fittings, the extracted phase and half‐amplitude were compared between genotypes using F tests (no variance within genotypes given the fit computed on data of all mice of each genotype; in case of significant genotype differences, difference between KO and littermates also tested with F tests). For BL analyses of daily dynamics of PS aperiodicity metrics, two Nlgn2 KO animals were excluded because of minimal time spent in PS, resulting in missing data for some intervals. Similarly, one WT and one KO mouse were excluded from SD + REC analyses of wake Hm and Dispersion dynamics due to very few artefact‐free epochs. For Nlgn2 KO mice, aperiodicity metrics were compared between “normal” ECoG signal, hypersynchronized events, and pre−/post‐event segments of BL, and between the light and dark periods using two‐way ANOVA (significant effects again decomposed using planned comparisons). From the eight Nlgn2 KO mice included in this last analysis, two were excluded for PS metrics because of too few PS events. The dynamics of hypersynchronized event aperiodicity metrics were compared using nonlinear fits and F tests as described above, and all events from all usable KO mice (8 in BL and 10 in SD + REC) were included for these analyses. For protein quantifications, comparisons between Ctrl and SD mice for the cerebral cortex and hippocampus were done using paired t‐tests to ensure taking into account variability between blots. The statistical significance threshold was set to 0.05 for all analyses, and results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean or, for cosine fit parameters, as mean ± 95% confidence interval (graphics produced using the software GraphPad Prism).

Results

NLGN2 Absence Alters Dynamics of ECoG Multifractal Metrics

The ECoG aperiodic activity metric Hm was first examined across the nychthemeron using representative time intervals for each vigilance state (Figure 2A). Nlgn2 KO mice showed markedly higher Hm values than WT and HET littermates across the 24‐h light/dark cycle for all vigilance states, with a magnitude of differences that was dependent on time specifically for wake and PS. Indeed, the difference between genotypes for wake and PS was greater around the light‐to‐dark transition (Genotype by Interval interactions wake p < 0.001 and PS p < 0.0001; see Table S1 for full statistical results), whereas the impact of the KO on SWS Hm values was constant across the 24 h (main Genotype effect p < 0.0001). To control for the overall increased Hm in Nlgn2 KO mice and adequately unveil differences in daily dynamics between genotypes, Hm values were expressed as a percentage of the 24‐h average for each vigilance state and animal (Figure 2B). A cosine curve fit was then applied to the data to extract the half‐amplitude and the timing of the peak (phase) of 24‐h variations (Figure 2C). For wake, mutant animals were found to display higher relative Hm than WT and HET littermates specifically during the second half of the light period (Genotype by Interval interaction p < 0.05; Table S1 and Figure 2B, left panel), which was associated with a higher amplitude of Hm 24‐h variations (cosine fit half‐amplitude Genotype difference p < 0.0001; Figure 2C, left panel). Moreover, mutant mice showed a later timing of the peak of wake Hm 24‐h variations in comparison with WT and HET mice (phase at ZT6.8 in comparison with around ZT5.3 for other genotypes; cosine fit Genotype difference p < 0.01; Table S1). Similar observations were made when considering 24‐h variations of relative Hm calculated for PS. The absence of NLGN2 resulted in lower PS relative Hm than WT and HET littermates at the beginning of the light period but higher relative Hm near the end of the light period and first half of the dark period (Genotype by Interval interaction p < 0.0001; Table S1 and Figure 2B, right panel), together with a higher amplitude of Hm 24‐h variations and a large (6‐h) phase delay in the timing of the PS Hm peak (cosine fit Genotype difference half‐amplitude p < 0.0001 and phase p < 0.05, without specific significant differences with genotype pairs; Table S1 and Figure 2C, right panels). In contrast, for SWS, the mutation did not significantly impact relative Hm values and dynamics, which was peaking after the light‐to‐dark transition (around ZT15) for all genotypes (Interval effect p < 0.0001; cosine fit parameters not significantly different between genotypes; middle panels of Figure 2B and Figure 2C).

Next, the Dispersion of Hurst exponents around Hm (the most prominent value) was plotted for the same representative time intervals across the nychthemeron for each vigilance state (Figure 2D). An inverted scale was used since “more negative” values are indicative of higher Dispersion. For wake, Nlgn2 KO mice showed less Dispersion than WT and HET littermates specifically during the second half of the light period (Genotype by Interval interaction p < 0.05; Table S1). However, for SWS, KO mice exhibited a higher Dispersion in comparison with littermates, with a greater magnitude of differences around the light/dark transition (Genotype by Interval interaction p < 0.05). For PS, mutant mice showed a lower Dispersion specifically after light onset but a higher Dispersion prior to light offset (Genotype by Interval interaction p < 0.05). Dispersion values were also expressed as a percentage of 24‐h averages to investigate differences in daily dynamics (Figure 2E). However, no cosine fit was applied since no intrinsic 24‐h dynamics could be found for WT and HET animals. For wake and PS, this analysis of relative dynamics confirmed the differences described above for 24‐h variations of absolute Dispersion (Genotype by Interval interactions wake p < 0.01 and PS p < 0.01). For SWS, differences in relative Dispersion values were specific to the end of the light period and most of the dark period, with Nlgn2 KO mice displaying higher relative Dispersion than WT or HET littermates (Genotype by Interval interaction p < 0.05). These results thus suggest a higher amplitude of 24‐h variations in Dispersion of Hurst exponents around Hm in mutant animals across all three vigilance states (given the lack of detectable 24‐h variations in WT and HET mice). Overall, the findings indicate that NLGN2 is shaping both average values and daily dynamics of two ECoG aperiodicity metrics related to 1/f β spectral power decay for all vigilance states.

NLGN2 Absence Impacts the Response of ECoG Power Laws to Sleep Loss

To understand how NLGN2 absence affects ECoG aperiodic properties in the context of enhanced sleep pressure, Hm was first plotted for representative time intervals for each vigilance state across the SD and REC 24‐h recording (Figure 3A). Similar to BL, Nlgn2 KO mice showed higher Hm values than WT and HET littermates across the 24 h for all vigilance states, and the magnitude of the differences was dependent on time (Genotype by Interval interactions for all states p < 0.0001; Table S1). In general, differences between KO mice and other genotypes were larger towards the end of the light period for the three states. The same strategy was again applied here to control for the higher overall Hm of Nlgn2 KO mice and adequately expose daily dynamics, namely expressing Hm values as a percentage of the 24‐h average for each vigilance state and animal (Figure 3B). For wake, mutant mice displayed lower relative Hm than WT and HET littermates during SD, but higher values near the end of the light period and for a few intervals of the dark period (Genotype by Interval interaction p < 0.0001; Table S1). For SWS. Nlgn2 KO animals showed lower relative Hm than littermates immediately after the end of SD, but higher values at the end of the light and dark periods (Genotype by Interval interaction p < 0.0001). For PS, mutant mice also exhibited a lower relative Hm than littermates immediately after the end of SD, but higher values near the end of the light period (Genotype by Interval interaction p < 0.0001). These results indicate that higher Hm values observed in mutant animals are preserved under sleep‐deprived conditions and that altered dynamics in response to sleep loss are particularly prominent in the absence of NLGN2.

Next, to specifically assess how elevated homeostatic sleep pressure impacts the 24‐h dynamics of Hm, a nonlinear fit of BL and SD + REC datasets was conducted for each genotype separately (Figure 3C). As described in Section 2, this analysis determined whether a single polynomial fit best describes the BL and SD + REC dynamics, or whether two distinct polynomial fits better represent the separate datasets. For wake, a single curve fit was found to describe the two Hm 24‐h dynamics in WT and HET animals (WT p = 0.5 and HET p = 0.2; Table S1), which indicates no effect of elevated sleep pressure on 24‐h variations. However, BL and SD + REC Hm daily dynamics were better described by two different curve fits in Nlgn2 KO mice (p < 0.0001), with wake Hm being lowered during SD and augmented during the second half of the dark period after SD. For SWS, an effect of elevated sleep pressure for all genotypes is supported by observations that two different fits best explained BL and SD + REC 24‐h dynamics (p < 0.0001 for KO, HET, and WT mice). Indeed, relative Hm was increased immediately after the end of SD. Finally, regarding PS, a single curve fit described Hm 24‐h dynamics of BL undisturbed conditions and SD + REC in WT animals, indicating no impact of elevated homeostatic sleep pressure (p = 0.2; Table S1). However, two different curve fits were found to best describe BL vs. SD + REC 24‐h Hm variations of PS in HET and KO mice (p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively), with HET mice expressing an increase immediately after SD, whereas KO mice showed a decrease lasting several hours after the end of SD. In brief, although the curve‐fit strategy used is influenced by the lack of datapoint during SD for SWS and PS, the results point to an effect of the Nlgn2 mutation on the change in Hm 24‐h variations in response to elevated sleep pressure, in particular for wake and PS.

The effect of NLGN2 absence on the Dispersion of H values around Hm in the context of elevated homeostatic sleep pressure was investigated similarly to what was defined above for Hm. Accordingly, Dispersion values were initially plotted for representative intervals for each vigilance state across the SD and REC 24‐h recording (Figure 4A). For wake, Dispersion values varied across the 24‐h day without significant differences between genotypes for SD and REC conditions (Interval effect p < 0.01; Genotype effect p = 0.7; interaction p = 0.2; Table S1). This pattern differs from the genotype differences found for BL. For SWS, Dispersion values were higher in Nlgn2 KO mice than WT and HET littermates throughout the 24 h, with a larger magnitude of difference after the end of SD and during the first half of the dark period (Genotype by Interval interaction p < 0.05). For PS, the 24‐h dynamics after SD were found to differ between genotypes (Genotype by Interval interaction p < 0.05), with mutant animals not significantly departing from WT and HET mice for any specific interval (but showing large changes in the first 6 h after the end of SD, whereas WT and HET showed relatively stable Dispersion). These observations thus indicate that the higher Dispersion (i.e., local variability of aperiodicity power laws) found during SWS in the absence of NLGN2 remains after an acute SD.

When Dispersion values were expressed as a percentage of 24‐h averages to better expose the nychthemeral dynamics (Figure 4B), genotype did not significantly affect Dispersion 24‐h variations under sleep‐deprived conditions for wake and PS (Genotype by Interval interactions wake p = 0.2 and PS p = 0.05; Table S1). For SWS, Nlgn2 mutants showed different 24‐h dynamics in comparison with WT and HET littermates (Genotype by Interval interaction p < 0.01), expressing lower Dispersion towards the end of the light period and higher Dispersion at specific time intervals of the dark period. This suggests that NLGN2 removal alters the response to SD of Dispersion 24‐h dynamics specifically when considering SWS. When directly comparing BL and SD + REC 24‐h dynamics using nonlinear curve fits (Figure 4C), wake Dispersion 24‐h dynamics were found to be better described by two different polynomial fits for WT animals, but by a single fit for KO and HET mice (WT p < 0.05, HET p = 0.5, KO p = 0.05; Table S1). WT mice indeed expressed lower relative Dispersion during SD in comparison with the same clock time of BL. For SWS, Dispersion 24‐h dynamics were more adequately described by two different curve fits for the three genotypes, pointing to an effect of elevated homeostatic sleep pressure (WT p < 0.01, HET p < 0.05, KO p < 0.05). Indeed, after SD, rhythmic variations appear compressed (e.g., higher frequency of 24‐h variations) and to present with greater relative values immediately after the end of SD and with lower relative values near the end of the dark period. For PS, relative Dispersion 24‐h dynamics were best described by a single polynomial fit for WT and KO mice, but by two different fits in HET mice (WT p = 0.5, HET p < 0.01, KO p = 0.1). These findings indicate an altered response to sleep loss of the local stability/instability of wake ECoG power laws in Nlgn2 KO mice. Globally, the results demonstrate that sleep loss influences daily dynamics of ECoG spectral power decay metrics evaluated with a multifractal formalism, and that this homeostatic response is importantly modulated by NLGN2.

NLGN2‐Dependent ECoG Aperiodic Alterations Are Not Driven by Delta Rhythm

We previously reported that Nlgn2 KO mice show a markedly higher level of delta (1–4 Hz) power across all vigilance states and considerably higher density and amplitude of slow waves (detected within 1–4 Hz) during SWS in comparison with littermates (Seok et al. 2018; Leduc et al. 2024). Given the likelihood that delta power influences the global ECoG spectral decay interrogated in the current study, Hm and Dispersion metrics were extracted for scales encompassing only 4–64 Hz (Figure 5). In general, when considering mean 24‐h (absolute) values for both Hm and Dispersion under BL and SD + REC conditions, all differences between genotypes persist (Figure 5A,B). Indeed, Hm remained convincingly higher in all states, together with SWS Dispersion, in KO mice in comparison with littermates (Hm Genotype by Frequency band interactions for all states and 24‐h recordings p < 0.01; Dispersion Genotype effect SWS p < 0.0001; Table S1). These genotype differences remained robust despite a global increase in both multifractal metrics when extracted with the more restricted frequency range (Figure 5 and Table S1).

The impact of the frequency band on the 24‐h dynamics of Hm and Dispersion metrics was also tested using relative values. For the dynamics of Hm, all genotype differences in daily dynamics were still significant with the 4–64 Hz band (all states for both BL and SD + REC conditions Genotype by Frequency band by Interval interactions p ≥ 0.2; Figure 5C and Table S1). The similarity between initial analyses (0.5–64 Hz) and the restricted band (4–64 Hz) is emphasized by the datapoints that are almost always superimposed in Figure 5C. For Wake, Nlgn2 mutant animals displayed higher relative Hm than littermates (independently of frequency band) near the end of light periods and for some intervals of the last dark period, but lower Hm during SD (Genotype by Interval interactions p < 0.05; Table S1). For SWS, KO mice showed lower relative Hm than littermates (independent of frequency) for the first hours following SD, and higher values at the end of the second light and dark periods (Genotype by Interval interaction BL p = 0.5 and SD + REC p < 0.0001). For PS, KO mice expressed, independent of frequency, a lower relative Hm compared with WT/HET mice at the beginning of the first light period and for the first intervals after SD, but a higher Hm at the end of light periods and for one interval of the first (BL) dark period (Genotype by Interval interactions p < 0.001).

Concerning the 24‐h dynamics of Dispersion relative values, some of the genotype differences were marginally affected by the interrogated frequency band for wake and PS only (Genotype by Frequency band by Interval interactions: wake BL and SD + REC p < 0.05 and PS BL p < 0.05; Figure 5D and Table S1). However, most intervals with significant genotype differences were the same for the two frequency bands. For wake, Nlgn2 mutant animals displayed higher relative Dispersion than littermates around the middle of the first dark period and during SD, but lower relative Dispersion near the end of the second light and dark periods. For PS, KO mice showed lower relative Dispersion than WT/HET littermates for both frequency bands at the beginning and near the middle of the first (BL) light period, and a lower value for the last interval of the light period. Finally for SWS, Nlgn2 mutant animals displayed, independently of the frequency band, lower relative Dispersion than littermates for a few intervals around the middle of the BL light period and towards the end of the light period following SD, but higher values for several intervals of dark periods (Genotype by Frequency band by Interval interaction p = 0.2; Genotype by Interval interactions p < 0.05; Table S1). Overall, these results confirm that Nlgn2 KO mice have altered ECoG aperiodicity properties as assessed by Hm and Dispersion metrics, and that these phenotypes are not entirely driven by the targeted frequency range despite marginal impacts of removing the delta range. Still, the high density of slow waves in the cortex of mutant animals could be driving an intrinsic organizational process that remains despite the attempt to interrogate the power spectra outside the delta range, and a contribution to aperiodicity metrics cannot be fully excluded (further discussed later).

Altered Multifractal Metrics During Abnormal ECoG Events

The wake and PS ECoG of Nlgn2 KO mice is characterized by the presence of abnormal hypersynchronized events (Seok et al. 2018; Cao et al. 2020; Leduc et al. 2024). To explore whether ECoG hypersynchronized events of KO animals exhibit different aperiodic properties, the multifractal formalism was applied to events identified during BL and to 1.5‐s flanking segments (pre/post‐event segments), and the obtained metrics were compared with state values extracted outside events (“normal” Nlgn2 KO mice wake and PS signal). Absolute Hm and Dispersion values were found to be significantly higher during hypersynchronized events in comparison with standard/normal Nlgn2 KO ECoG signal for both wake and PS (Signal type effects Hm p < 0.0001 and Dispersion p < 0.0001; Figure 6A,B and Table S1). Interestingly, while being similar to each other, the pre‐ and post‐event segments displayed significantly higher Hm and Dispersion values than standard/normal KO ECoG signal, but significantly lower values than hypersynchronized events for both wake and PS. Only for wake Hm, values were higher during the light than the dark period for normal signal, pre‐event segments, and events, but not for post‐event segments (Signal type by Light/Dark period interaction p < 0.0001). These findings uncover a specific multifractal signature of abnormal ECoG hypersynchronized events, namely increased long‐range dependency (i.e., Hm) and local instability of power laws (i.e., Dispersion), which both increase in the seconds leading to the event and show a progressive diminution afterwards.

Next, to investigate whether ECoG hypersynchronized event aperiodicity metrics also present a 24‐h dynamics and whether this dynamics differs from that of the signal outside events (“normal” KO signal), relative Hm and Dispersion values were plotted together with values of the normal wake and PS KO signals for BL and SD + REC conditions, and nonlinear curve fits were applied (Figure 6C,D). Overall, 24‐h dynamics of Hm and Dispersion within abnormal events were similar to the dynamics of the signal outside events for both wake and PS because a single polynomial fit most adequately described both 24‐h variations (Hm wake p ≥ 0.7 and PS p > 0.1; Dispersion wake p ≥ 0.4 and PS p ≥ 0.4; Table S1). However, the low R 2 fit values warrant caution in the interpretation of these results (especially given the apparent difference in BL 24‐h patterns for wake relative Dispersion). Indeed, the single curve fits for abnormal events and normal signal could be strongly influenced by the large number of individual events contributing to the fits because of the strategy to include all events of all KO mice as individual datapoints (given the absence of data for some time intervals for several animals). Globally, hypersynchronized event aperiodic properties appear to differ from “normal” Nlgn2 KO signals at the level of absolute values but not in 24‐h dynamics as assessed with the current analytical design.

It was finally assessed whether the 24‐h variations of ECoG hypersynchronized event aperiodicity metrics were modified by elevated homeostatic sleep pressure. For this, the relative Hm and Dispersion values computed across the 24 h were compared between BL and SD + REC using nonlinear curve fits (Figure 6E,F). Interestingly, for wake event Hm, two different polynomial curves described the 24‐h variations better during BL and SD + REC conditions, pointing to an effect of SD (p < 0.001). Indeed, relative Hm values were decreased during SD but increased during the following dark period. This effect of elevated sleep pressure is very similar to that observed for the “normal” wake dynamics in Nlgn2 KO mice (Figure 3C). However, PS hypersynchronized event relative Hm dynamics were best described by a single polynomial curve fit, pointing to no effect of elevated sleep pressure on 24‐h variations (p = 0.8; Table S1; please note the poor curve fit suggesting that the fit captures very little of the variance and thus that 24‐h changes are very weak). For relative Dispersion during events, the dynamics were more adequately described by two different curve fits for both wake and PS (p < 0.05; Table S1). Wake event relative Dispersion was lowered by SD and higher at the beginning of the REC dark period in comparison with BL. PS event relative Dispersion dynamics, characterized also by a poor curve fit (R 2 < 0.01), were not markedly changed by elevated homeostatic sleep pressure except for higher values at the beginning of the REC dark period in comparison with BL. Overall, sleep loss seems to affect the 24‐h dynamics of ECoG hypersynchronized event aperiodicity metrics, with a particular impact on wakefulness Hm and Dispersion.

Sleep Loss Impacts NLGN2 Level of the Cerebral Cortex

Given the impact of the absence of NLGN2 on the response of ECoG aperiodic properties to sleep loss, the effect of SD on NLGN2 protein level and localization at the synaptic compartment was measured for the cerebral cortex (Figures 7A,B and S1). Measurements were also conducted for the hippocampus (Figures 7C,D and S1), which is involved in shaping the ECoG signal, notably theta rhythms (Siapas et al. 2005; Sirota et al. 2008), and is particularly sensitive to the effect of SD (Vecsey et al. 2012; Vanrobaeys et al. 2023; Koukaroudi et al. 2024). The total (Tot) level of NLGN2 protein was found to be lower in the cerebral cortex after SD compared with control/undisturbed (Ctrl) conditions (p < 0.01; Table S1). However, the synaptoneurosomal (SN) level of NLGN2 protein and the SN/Tot ratio were higher after SD (p < 0.05), suggesting a relocalization of NLGN2 protein to the synaptic compartment in the cerebral cortex upon elevated sleep pressure. This effect was not observed for the hippocampus, for which SD did not significantly impact Tot or SN NLGN2 levels, nor the SN/Tot ratio (p ≥ 0.7).

Discussion

Using a multifractal wavelet‐leaders formalism to investigate time‐resolved spectral power decay indicative of the long‐range dependency of ECoG activity, we uncovered vigilance state‐specific daily dynamics and sleep loss‐induced variations in spectral decay in mice, in addition to identifying a molecular determinant involved in modulating this power‐to‐frequency relationship. More precisely, Hm, an index of the steepness of spectral decay, was found to display notable 24‐h variations when captured during wakefulness, with higher values during the resting (light) period. In addition, elevated homeostatic sleep pressure achieved by SD was found to impact this metric, particularly when measured during SWS, as well as its local variability (Dispersion) during wakefulness. Importantly, the absence of NLGN2 caused a major increase in Hm across all vigilance states, together with a diversity of effects on Dispersion of power law steepness that were dependent on both time‐of‐day and state. Furthermore, mice lacking NLGN2 displayed altered 24‐h dynamics and responses to sleep loss, which could be linked to an absence of NLGN2 subcellular relocalization as found in the current study for the cerebral cortex after SD. Finally, altered Hm and Dispersion values were revealed to be a notable feature of ECoG hypersynchronized epileptic‐like events found in Nlgn2 KO animals. Altogether, the findings advance the knowledge on ECoG 1/f β spectral power decay properties, suggest NLGN2 as a regulator, and show relevance to neurodevelopmental disorders displaying altered cortical spectral power laws and excitation/inhibition balance.

In agreement with previously published data (Lina et al. 2019; Areal et al. 2025; Hector et al. 2026), the lowest Hm values were found for wake, intermediate values for PS, and highest values for SWS in WT animals (baseline 24‐h averages: wake 0.56 ± 0.02, PS 0.60 ± 0.02, SWS 0.88 ± 0.02). This also aligns with reports of slope steepness in humans (Ma et al. 2005; Zorick and Mandelkern 2013; Schneider et al. 2022), highlighting the validity of the chosen approach. However, even if the vigilance state dependency of the 1/f β spectral power decay slope of ECoG/EEG activity has been repeatedly reported, very few investigations of daily rhythmicity have been conducted. The present work clearly supports an effect of time‐of‐day on Hm for all vigilance states, with the highest amplitude rhythm for wakefulness (24‐h variation amplitude of about 8% in WT and HET mice, Hm peak around ZT5 [middle of light period]). This is similar to previous findings obtained from different cohorts of mice (Lina et al. 2019; Areal et al. 2025; Hector et al. 2026). EEG spectral decay has been linked to the optimization of the spatiotemporal organization of simultaneously ongoing neuronal computations inside the cerebral cortex (Buzsáki and Draguhn 2004), in addition to an optimal state of sensitivity/rigidity to input stimuli (Chialvo 2010; Cocchi et al. 2017), sometimes referred to as “criticality”. Such organization or sensitivity is likely to vary across the nychthemeron according to ongoing cognitive processing, which could be reflected in daily Hm variations. There is indeed growing support for a circadian regulation of neuronal network responsiveness to stimuli at the level of excitation/inhibition balance and membrane conductance (Chellappa et al. 2016; Paul et al. 2020; Nowacka et al. 2025). Future work in constant lighting conditions (e.g., constant darkness) will be needed to uncover a potential contribution of endogenous circadian rhythms to ECoG‐derived Hm and Dispersion. Of importance is that the altered daily dynamics of ECoG aperiodicity in Nlgn2 KO animals can point to synaptic remodeling as a potential mechanism of time‐dependent network responsiveness optimization. Accordingly, the current work underscores the importance of considering multiple timepoints for investigations of ECoG/EEG aperiodicity metrics (e.g., to prevent missing abnormal phenotypes in clinical settings), which is further supported by observations of time‐specific alterations (sometimes in opposite directions) in the absence of NLGN2 in mice (a model of neurodevelopmental diseases).

In parallel, our findings can align with the scarce literature reporting an effect of extended wakefulness on the power‐to‐frequency relationship of cortical activity (Meisel et al. 2013; Areal et al. 2025; Horváth and Bódizs 2025; Magyar et al. 2026). Indeed, a 6‐h SD caused numerous changes in the daily dynamics of Hm and Dispersion values. For instance, SWS Hm was found to be considerably increased in WT animals immediately after the end of SD when compared with the corresponding time in BL, and to then be diminished for the remaining recovery period. Concerning Dispersion, wake values were particularly responsive to SD, showing a progressive reduction in the course of SD (compared with BL), and a quick return to levels comparable with BL in the first hours of recovery sleep. Interestingly, the seemingly homeostatic response of these two metrics to extended wakefulness implies that they might be used as novel biomarkers of sleep need. This is especially true for SWS Hm, which dynamics behave similarly to that of SWS delta activity, the most recognized biomarker of sleep pressure (Borbély 1982; Franken et al. 1991). In line with this, others have found SD to progressively disrupt some aperiodicity metrics related to optimal cortical computational regimes (i.e., power law exponents of neuronal avalanches), disruptions dissipating with recovery sleep and which appear to better predict subsequent wake/sleep occurrence than slow‐wave activity (SWA; 0.5–4 Hz) (Xu et al. 2024). A direct measure of EEG spectral decay has similarly been reported to better capture the effects of extended wakefulness than SWA (Magyar et al. 2026). Importantly, the persistence of SWS Hm dynamics upon removal of delta frequencies from the multifractal analysis in the current study further suggests that this metric might act as a delta power‐independent marker of homeostatic sleep need. Nonetheless, removing the delta frequencies from the analysis does not remove delta activity generated by the cortex itself, and it should be kept in mind that these oscillations could still be driving the organization of cortical activity across frequencies (i.e., spectral decay power laws), even if the decay is calculated outside this frequency window. Testing the effect of SD on aperiodicity metrics in additional models showing a reduced sleep loss‐induced rebound of delta activity (e.g., models of inhibited gliotransmission or Dravet Syndrome; Halassa et al. 2009; Kalume et al. 2015) could further help answer this question in the future.

As hypothesized, alterations in properties indicative of the ECoG 1/f β spectral power decay were observed in Nlgn2 KO mice. In fact, higher Hm values in mutant animals across all states point to steeper slopes of ECoG spectral decay. However, the direction of this change is not in line with what could be expected in a context of a reduced inhibition as found under NLGN2 downregulation (Chubykin et al. 2007; Poulopoulos et al. 2009; Jedlicka et al. 2011; Babaev et al. 2016, 2018; Cao et al. 2020), because in silico and in vivo studies would support an attenuated slope of spectral power decay in the case of lower inhibition (Gao et al. 2017; Lombardi et al. 2017; Chini et al. 2022). Interestingly, a recent modeling study proposed that the positive correlation between the steepness of spectral power decay and general inhibition (i.e., more power decay with higher inhibition) would only occur in conditions of high membrane conductance (i.e., a high passive flow of ions across the neuronal membrane) and would reverse to a negative correlation under conditions of low leakage conductance (Brake et al. 2024). The authors indeed anticipated that when the membrane ion permeability is low, membrane potential is pushed towards the reversal potential of GABA receptors, respectively reducing and amplifying the impact of GABAergic and glutamatergic inputs, which inverses the power law correlation with the excitation/inhibition ratio (Brake et al. 2024). Interestingly, the knockdown of Nlgn2 was previously reported to depolarize the GABA reversal potential, possibly linked to a parallel reduction in the expression of the potassium‐chloride co‐transporter 2 (KCC2; Sun et al. 2013). If this occurs in Nlgn2 KO animals, at least to a certain extent, it could represent a mechanism explaining a higher Hm. In support of this hypothesis, increased neuronal excitability was reported in the dentate gyrus of Nlgn2 KO mice (Jedlicka et al. 2011).

Another hypothesis could be that the absence of NLGN2 has affected specific inhibitory synapse subtypes (i.e., somatic vs. dendritic, parvalbumin vs. somatostatin) and/or restricted subcortical regions contributing to cortical activity, modifying overall network organization and causing a change in cortical 1/f β organization that is not solely dependent on the excitation/inhibition balance. This is supported by reports of specific impairment of perisomatic synapses made by fast‐spiking interneurons such as parvalbumin‐expressing neurons in numerous brain regions upon constitutive Nlgn2 KO in mice (Gibson et al. 2009; Poulopoulos et al. 2009; Jedlicka et al. 2011; Babaev et al. 2016), and the expression of NLGN2 in brain regions other than the cerebral cortex including the hippocampus, amygdala and thalamus (Varoqueaux et al. 2004; Varoqueaux et al. 2006; Poulopoulos et al. 2009; Babaev et al. 2016). Furthermore, a recent study observed a (unexpected) steeper spectral power decay upon brain‐wide inhibition of parvalbumin neurons using designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (Salvatore et al. 2024), which supports this second hypothesis. Overall, different mechanisms are likely contributing to the altered spectral decay power laws (and the direction of these alterations) in Nlgn2 KO animals. However, a limitation of this “neurodevelopmental disease” model (constitutive KO) is that it does not allow the identification of a direct role of NLGN2 in the regulation of ECoG aperiodic properties, given the potential for different changes or compensations occurring during development without NLGN2 to contribute to the observed phenotypes.

Nlgn2 KO mice also displayed major alterations in the 24‐h dynamics of ECoG spectral power decay. Among other phenotypes, wake Hm 24‐h variations were amplified (twice the amplitude of WT mice) and showed a delayed timing of the peak. Similar observations were made for PS. This could suggest that the presence at the synapse of NLGN2 (most likely at inhibitory synapses), and potentially daily variations in its synaptic level, contribute to a certain buffering of the 24‐h dynamics of ECoG aperiodicity. Of interest, these KO animals could also show larger 24‐h variations in time spent awake, given that they spend considerably more time awake during the dark period (Seok et al. 2018; Leduc et al. 2024). These previous findings could also be compatible with some level of buffering of 24‐h changes by NLGN2, and may even contribute to larger wake Hm variations. Additionally, NLGN2 absence decreased wake Hm specifically during SD, an effect not seen in WT and HET mice. Nlgn2 mutant mice also showed an altered 24‐h dynamics of PS Hm after SD that was not found in WT littermates. Therefore, NLGN2 may be required to ensure a robust and contained 24‐h rhythm of wake and PS Hm variations, and also to protect these dynamics from adverse effects of sleep loss. Since the current data suggest that SD drives an enrichment of NLGN2 at the synaptic compartment in the cerebral cortex, and because NLGN2 acts as a central molecular organizer of the GABAergic synapse (Blundell et al. 2009; Poulopoulos et al. 2009; Jedlicka et al. 2011; Ali et al. 2020), it could structurally prevent other synaptic proteins from being moved to the extrasynaptic compartment or degraded under high sleep pressure, and consequently alleviate effects on network activity/communication (and thus on ECoG 1/f β spectral decay dynamics). Hence, the altered daily and sleep loss‐induced aperiodicity dynamics observed in Nlgn2 KO mice may indicate a role for NLGN2 in maintaining an adequate range of synaptic activity via a fine control of the function and trafficking of other synaptic components.

Another notable effect of the Nlgn2 mutation on the ECoG spectral power decay dynamics concerns the dampened relative increase of SWS Hm immediately after the end of the SD. As addressed earlier, a potential contribution of delta activity to the Hm measurement cannot be fully excluded. Indeed, the previously reported dampened delta activity rebound following SD in Nlgn2 KO mutants (Leduc et al. 2024) could be contributing to the attenuated sleep loss‐induced increase in SWS Hm. Alternatively, the opposite relationship could also occur (i.e., attenuated Hm increase contributing to attenuated delta rebound). It is possible that the absence of NLGN2 causes impairments in the sensing/accumulation of sleep need, as previously proposed (Leduc et al. 2024). Altered sleep pressure sensing could notably originate from altered expression and/or localization of chloride co‐transporters in the cerebral cortex of KO mice (see above discussion related to lower KCC2 expression with NLGN2 downregulation; Sun et al. 2013). Indeed, research has shown that chloride co‐transporters are modulated by extended wakefulness and regulate markers of homeostatic sleep pressure, potentially acting as sensors of sleep pressure in the cortex (Alfonsa et al. 2023). Altogether, this work identifies NLGN2 as a likely regulator of ECoG aperiodicity dynamics measured under undisturbed and sleep‐deprived conditions, with potential roles in buffering synaptic responses and sensing sleep pressure.

Additional novel phenotypes driven by NLGN2 absence are a SWS‐specific increase in local Dispersion of Hurst exponents around Hm indicative of an elevated multifractality of the signal and instability of spectral decay power laws, and time‐dependent reductions for wake and PS. The literature has reported that bursting and irregular electrical events can contribute to increased multifractality (La Rocca et al. 2018; Dumeur et al. 2025). An increased SWS Dispersion could thus indicate a higher content of bursting events in the SWS ECoG signal of Nlgn2 KO mice. This can be supported by a higher frequency and density of individual slow waves in these mutants (Leduc et al. 2024), which can indicate more frequent bursting/up states. Interestingly, some authors have even proposed that multifractality (as indexed by Dispersion in the current study) can reflect criticality (Zhao et al. 2017; Dumeur et al. 2023; de Sousa et al. 2026). More precisely, higher multifractality has been suggested to reflect a state closer to criticality when considering neuronal networks and other mathematical models (Zhao et al. 2017; Dumeur et al. 2023; de Sousa et al. 2026). Criticality is defined as a state of equilibrium at phase transitions, and is a core concept related to the association between ECoG/EEG spectral decay power laws and optimal network input processing and signal transmission mentioned earlier (Chialvo 2010; Cocchi et al. 2017). As such, lower wake Dispersion (multifractality) at specific times across the 24‐h day may be interpreted as a cerebral cortex intermittently deviating from criticality in KO mice, which could be interesting to directly test in future research as well as to put in relation with behavioral and cognitive deficits reported in Nlgn2 KO animals (Blundell et al. 2009; Jedlicka et al. 2011; Babaev et al. 2016; Ali et al. 2020; Cao et al. 2020). Hence, the absence of NLGN2 impacts the level of multifractality in addition to the long‐range dependency (or self‐similarity) of the ECoG signal, and these data thus underscore the need to consider both metrics when investigating molecular mechanisms regulating aperiodic properties.

Uncovering here alterations in ECoG 1/f β properties in mice lacking NLGN2 is of particular relevance to neurodevelopmental disorders. Indeed, as mentioned above, patients with neurodevelopmental disorders display altered aperiodic properties of the EEG, which present potential to refine clinical practice. More precisely, investigations using different mathematical approaches point to a reduced EEG spectral power decay in young patients with autism spectrum disorders as compared with neurotypical children (Jia and Yu 2019; Kang et al. 2026), and similar observations were made during both childhood and adulthood with functional magnetic resonance imaging and magnetoencephalography, two other measures of brain (including cortical) activity (Lai et al. 2010; Manyukhina et al. 2022). Conversely, patients suffering from schizophrenia were reported to have a higher slope of cortical activity power decay (Peterson et al. 2023; Hasanaj et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025), and epileptic patients were found to show higher spectral slope exponent (steeper power decay) than healthy controls, with higher decay values found to be linked to ictal events and seizure onset zone (Gadhoumi et al. 2015; Duma et al. 2024; Latreille et al. 2024; Liao et al. 2024). Although NLGN2 associates with all three disorders notably given reports of mutations and/or altered expression levels in patients, and disease‐related phenotypes in animal models (Jedlicka et al. 2011; Sun et al. 2011; Deciphering Developmental Disorders Study 2015; Babaev et al. 2016; Seok et al. 2018; Parente et al. 2017; Cao et al. 2020; Leduc et al. 2024), the current findings of increased Hm across vigilance states and upon hypersynchronized events in KO mice might render this rodent model more relevant to schizophrenia and epilepsy than autism, at least at the level of ECoG aperiodicity investigations. Accordingly, the present work may allow to propose NLGN2 as a target for the improvement of neuronal activity coordination and related cognitive functions in the context of schizophrenia and epilepsy more specifically. Findings could even suggest that one copy of the WT Nlgn2 gene is enough to maintain ECoG aperiodic properties in the precise context of loss‐of‐function mutations, given observations that HET mice are mostly indistinguishable from WT animals. It is finally proposed that the aperiodicity metric Hm be considered as a potential tool to refine diagnosis and treatment evaluation in neurodevelopmental disorders in the future.

Conclusion

Taken together, the findings point to an involvement of the synaptic adhesion molecule NLGN2 in shaping cortical 1/f β spectral power decay and its daily dynamics, which might bring new insights for brain diseases like schizophrenia and epilepsy. Although the current investigation was focused only on males, it should be noted that preliminary observations in KO mice of both sexes and with a uniform genetic background support similar phenotypes of ECoG aperiodicity properties in females and males (Lemmetti et al. 2026). Importantly, NLGN2 exists in two different isoforms, with one mostly expressed at GABAergic synapses and another also found at glutamatergic synapses (Chih et al. 2006). Accordingly, future research efforts will consider the differential implications of the two NLGN2 isoforms in shaping the ECoG aperiodicity. Additional important next steps will also be to use conditional/inducible KO models (such as Liang et al. 2015) or other types of NLGN2 manipulation in otherwise normally developed animals to establish a direct role for this adhesion protein. Another important contribution of the current results relates to exposing 24‐h dynamics of spectral power decay that are specific to the wake/sleep state and somehow affected by elevated sleep need (all differentially modulated by NLGN2 absence). This underscores the importance of standardizing ECoG/EEG aperiodic properties investigations in clinical settings and research investigations (e.g., careful control of time‐of‐day and vigilance state for measurements). Lastly, we found NLGN2 absence to alter Hm local variability (Dispersion values, indexing the multifractal property of ECoG power decay) at many instances, a metric that is rarely considered in clinical research and to which granting more importance seems of particular relevance.
