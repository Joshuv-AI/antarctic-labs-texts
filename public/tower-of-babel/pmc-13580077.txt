Introduction and rationale

Intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) is a cerebrovascular disease with high mortality and disability rates. Current data show that ICH accounts for approximately 28.8% of all stroke cases but contributes nearly 50% of the total stroke-related disease burden.1,2 The current clinical management of ICH primarily involves blood pressure control, haemostasis and haematoma evacuation surgery in selected patients.3 However, recent large-scale clinical trials involving intensive blood pressure lowering and haematoma removal have failed to significantly improve long-term functional outcomes.3–6 Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel neuroprotective strategies to improve patient prognoses.

The pathological process following ICH can be divided into 2 phases: direct primary injury caused by the mechanical mass effect of the haematoma and subsequent secondary injury.7 As haematoma removal does not show satisfactory efficacy, secondary injury after ICH warrants further attention.5,6,8,9 Secondary injury begins within hours of ICH and progresses for days to weeks, contributing to tissue damage. It primarily manifests as iron-induced neurotoxicity and ferroptosis, neuroinflammatory cascades and blood–brain barrier disruption, worsening perihaematomal oedema, which can adversely affect functional outcomes.7,9 Therefore, identifying multi-target protective treatments to alleviate secondary injury may be an effective strategy worth exploring.

Minocycline is a second-generation tetracycline.10 Owing to its lipophilicity, it can effectively penetrate the blood–brain barrier.11 As a multi-target anti-neuroinflammatory drug, it provides significant functional outcome benefits in patients after acute ischaemic stroke, without safety concerns.12 In patients with ICH, minocycline can not only prevent neuroinflammation but also protect the blood–brain barrier.13–16 Moreover, as an iron chelator, it can attenuate iron-induced neurotoxicity and ferroptosis.17,18 Thus, we hypothesised that minocycline could be regarded as an adjunctive treatment to improve the prognosis of patients after ICH. Accordingly, we designed the Efficacy and Safety of Minocycline in the Treatment of Intracerebral Haemorrhage (ENHANCE) trial to explore the safety and efficacy of minocycline in patients with acute ICH.

Methods

Design

The ENHANCE trial is a multicentre, randomised, open-label, blinded-endpoint clinical trial performed in China to test the hypothesis that minocycline promotes neuroprotective function at 180 days in patients with ICH when applied within 24 h of symptom onset. The study design was registered (Chinese Clinical Trials Registry identifier: ChiCTR2500114607) and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Jilin University (25K489) and each sub-centre. Randomisation and allocation concealment

We will employ block randomisation with random block sizes of 4, 6 and 8, stratified by centre. The random allocations will be concealed using a password-protected web-based programme. Participants will be randomly assigned to the minocycline or control group at a 1:1 ratio. Each participant will be strictly assigned a unique treatment randomisation number based on the chronological order of enrolment, which determines whether the participant enters the minocycline or control group.

Intervention

The minocycline group will receive an initial dose of 200 mg minocycline hydrochloride capsules within 24 h of ICH onset, followed by 100 mg every 12 h for 7 days, for a total of 14 administrations. The control group will not receive any additional treatment beyond guideline-based treatment.

Outcomes

The primary efficacy endpoint is the proportion of patients with favourable functional outcomes (mRS score, 0–2) at 180 days. The secondary efficacy endpoints include the proportion of patients with mRS scores of 0–1 at 180 days, the proportion of patients with mRS scores of 0–2 and 0–1 at 90 days, the distribution of mRS scores at 90 and 180 days and the NIHSS score after 7 days of treatment. The safety endpoints are the rate of surgical intervention, the incidence of adverse events, serious adverse events, minocycline-related adverse events and mortality during the study period.

Each centre will have 1 or 2 professional clinical observers conducting face-to-face visits at baseline and 7 days after enrolment. Ninety- and 180-day follow-up visits will be conducted via phone, video call or in person by professionally qualified observers at our stroke centre. To ensure anonymity, the observers will be blinded to the patient’s allocation and treatment plan.

Data and safety monitoring

An independent Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) has been established to monitor the progress of the trial, review and analyse all safety data and perform interim efficacy analyses. The DSMB evaluates the potential risks and benefits of treatment based on emerging efficacy data. The DSMB consists of at least 2 independent clinical experts who are not involved in participant recruitment for this study and 1 biostatistician whose identity may be masked. The DSMB will continuously review serious adverse events. It will also review data on the frequency and severity of all adverse events as well as demographic data. Ad hoc meetings will be held during the study period to address safety concerns. Based on the data review, the DSMB can terminate the study unconditionally.

Sample size estimate

Based on the interim analysis results of our safety and efficacy of remote ischaemic conditioning for spontaneous ICH (SERIC-ICH) trial and a previous study focused receiving patients with conservative treatment after ICH,21,22 the proportion of patients with ICH achieving favourable functional outcomes (mRS ≤ 2) at 6 months with conventional treatment is approximately 64%. According to previous studies of minocycline and deferoxamine (another drug with iron-chelating property) in ICH,23,24 we estimate that minocycline will increase this proportion by 8%. An interim analysis is planned after 50% of the participants have completed follow-up, using the O’Brien–Fleming alpha spending function to determine the testing boundaries. A sample size of 561 participants per group can detect an 8% difference between groups with 80% power (1 − β) at a 2-sided significance level (α) of 0.05. Assuming an estimated participant dropout rate of 10% and to maximise study efficiency, we will recruit a total of 1248 patients (624 per group).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses will be conducted using SPSS (version 26.0 or higher) or R (version 4.1.3 or higher). All statistical tests will be 2-sided, and a P-value < .05 will be considered statistically significant (unless otherwise specified). The analysis population will include intention-to-treat, per-protocol and safety populations. Missing data will be imputed using simple or multiple imputation methods. Categorical variables will be summarised as frequencies and percentages, with intergroup comparisons using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. The distribution of continuous variables will be assessed using a 1-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed continuous variables will be expressed as mean value ± SD, and Student’s t-test will be used to compare independent samples. Non-normally distributed variables will be expressed as median (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

For the primary outcome, a modified Poisson regression will be used, with the intervention effect reported as the risk ratio and 95% CI. Binary secondary outcomes will be analysed similarly. For continuous secondary outcomes, linear regression will be used, with the intervention effect reported as β and its 95% CI. If the normality or variance homogeneity assumptions are violated for continuous secondary outcomes, the Win Ratio method will be applied, with the effects reported as win ratios and 95% CI.25,26 Ordinal logistic regression will be used for ordinal secondary outcomes (eg, mRS scores), with effects reported as the common odds ratio and 95% CI. If the proportional odds assumption is violated, the Win Ratio method will be applied. For safety outcomes, the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test will be used to compare differences between groups. Analyses of primary and secondary outcomes will be conducted in the intention-to-treat population, whereas safety outcomes will be analysed in the safety population. Secondary analyses of the outcomes will also be performed in the per-protocol population, which will include patients who meet all enrolment criteria and receive the assigned treatment.

Current status

This study was registered on 15 December 2025, and recruitment began on 17 December 2025. At the time of first submission of this article (4 April 2026), 179 patients had been recruited. Recruitment will continue until the intended sample size has been reached, which is expected to be by January 2028.

Discussion

The ENHANCE trial will illustrate the efficacy and safety of minocycline in the treatment of ICH.

The iron-chelating property of minocycline is the main pharmacological basis for its potential use in treating ICH.27 The haemoglobin and heme in erythrocytes are released after ICH, generating biliverdin, carbon monoxide and iron.28 Sufficient evidence suggests that iron overload is a key contributor to secondary injury after ICH.17 Previously, iron chelators such as deferoxamine have been shown to have a tendency to improve outcomes in patients with ICH.24 However, deferoxamine is difficult to transport across the blood–brain barrier, limiting its neuroprotective function. In contrast, minocycline can easily pass through the blood–brain barrier because of its lipophilicity. Additionally, minocycline can prevent matrix metalloproteinases from destroying the blood–brain barrier, attenuate apoptosis and inhibit microglial activation.13–16 Therefore, minocycline shows potential for treating ICH.

To date, 3 clinical trials have investigated the effect of minocycline on ICH in the acute phase. The first study enrolled 95 patients within 24 h of stroke, of which 11 (5 in the minocycline group and 6 in the control group) had ICH. This study concluded that minocycline was safe but not efficacious.23 The second study included 16 patients (8 in the minocycline group and 8 in the control group) enrolled within 24 h of ICH onset. Although no significant differences in inflammatory biomarkers, haematoma volume or perihaematomal oedema were observed, this study confirmed that the administration of minocycline was safe.29 A subsequent study on 20 patients (10 in the minocycline group and 10 in the control group) enrolled within 12 h of ICH onset found that serum matrix metalloproteinase-9 levels tended to be lower in the intravenous minocycline group.30 All 3 trials consistently verified that minocycline administration during the acute phase of ICH was safe. However, the sample sizes in these studies were too small to reliably determine the treatment effect of minocycline. Therefore, we designed the ENHANCE trial, using a multicentre design to achieve a large sample size, to clarify the efficacy of minocycline in treating ICH.

A previous study reported that iron accumulation begins within 24 h.31 To expand the patient population that can benefit from minocycline treatment and ensure therapeutic effect, we selected patients within 24 h after ICH onset in our trial. Additionally, because the accumulation of iron peaks at 7 days after ICH,31 the patients in our study will be treated with minocycline for 7 days.

Summary and conclusions

Minocycline is a promising multi-target neuroprotective agent. The ENHANCE trial will investigate whether minocycline can be used as an adjunctive drug to improve functional outcomes in patients with ICH and identify any safety issues.
