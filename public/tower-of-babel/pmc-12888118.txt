Maternal Endocrine and Metabolic Health and Outcomes (MEMO): a prospective cohort study of gestational diabetes mellitus and subclinical hypothyroidism in China
Authors: Yuan Zeng, Jie Yu, Yaolin Ren, Jing Ren, Haiyan Chen, Qian Zhang, Xinhua Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn recent years, more and more evidence has confirmed the “Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD)” hypothesis, which states that adverse environments in early life can increase the risk of long-term metabolic diseases such as obesity and diabetes. Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) during pregnancy are the most common endocrine diseases, which directly interfere with the intrauterine development environment of the fetus and thus are ideal research entry points for validating and extending the DOHaD theory.ObjectiveThis study aims to deeply explore the early impact of maternal endocrine disorders during pregnancy (with a focus on GDM and SCH) on the healthy development of the fetus, using key indicators such as birth weight as measures of early health outcomes. The research objectives include: 1) To Identify potential biomarkers that can be used for early prediction of GDM and SCH; 2) To observe the extent to which GDM and SCH affect the pregnancy outcome.MethodsThis will be a prospective cohort study involving 300 pregnant women. In addition to collecting general demographic information and clinical data of pregnant women, we also collect peripheral blood, urine, and fecal samples from pregnant women during the early, middle, and late stages of pregnancy. We also collect placental and umbilical cord blood samples during delivery. We conduct multi-omics analysis on the samples to comprehensively screen for differentially expressed molecules related to gestational endocrine diseases (GDM and SCH) and adverse outcomes. The main evaluation indicators are the birth weight of the offspring, including low birth weight, macrosomia, small for gestational age, and large for gestational age. The secondary outcome indicators include the following: 1) neonatal-related outcomes that include intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), intrauterine fetal death (IUFD), preterm birth, low Apgar score, admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU); and 2) maternal pregnancy outcomes that include gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, eclampsia, anemia, spontaneous abortion, type of delivery (vaginal, assisted vaginal delivery or cesarean section) and other complications.ConclusionsThis study aims to establish a prospective Chinese pregnant cohort to investigate the effects of GDM and SCH on pregnancy outcomes. On this basis, this study will utilize multi-time-point collected biological samples and combine multi-omics technologies to identify high-risk phenotypic characteristics related to GDM and SCH, with the aim of achieving early identification, and hopefully, intervention in the future, thereby reducing adverse pregnancy outcomes and the risk of long-term chronic diseases in the offspring.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT05952739. Introduction

The number of people suffering from diabetes in China has risen sharply, and the complications impose a huge economic burden [1]. Although lifestyle interventions during adulthood have achieved some results, the number of diabetes patients is still increasing continuously [2, 3]. Advancing the intervention window to the early stages of life can improve prognosis, thereby enhancing prevention and treatment outcomes and reducing medical costs [4]. This strategy aligns with the "developmental origin of health and disease (DOHaD)" hypothesis, which states that adverse early environments increase the risk of metabolic diseases in future generations [5, 6]. Our research group has also previously confirmed the DOHaD theory in the Chinese population, namely that lower birth weight is an independent risk factor for later diabetes or impaired glucose regulation [7].

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) are the two most prevalent endocrine and metabolic diseases during pregnancy [8]. The prevalence of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) in the Beijing area is approximately 19.7% [9]. In China, the average prevalence rate of GDM is 14.8%[10], while the prevalence rate of SCH ranges from 4.0% to 17.8%[11–14]. GDM and SCH during pregnancy may cause short-term and long-term health problems for the offspring (Table S1). For instance, GDM increases the risk of premature birth, small for gestational age (SGA), large for gestational age (LGA), macrosomia, cesarean section, and admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) [15, 16]. Moreover, mothers with GDM and their offspring have an increased risk of developing chronic diseases such as type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease in their later lives [17–24]. SCH during pregnancy can also increase the risk of premature birth [25–27], preeclampsia [28], SGA [29], low birth weight (LBW) [29], male LGA [30], neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) [26], and low child intelligence quotient (IQ) [31, 32]. In addition, the efficacy of thyroid hormone replacement therapy in pregnant women with SCH remains controversial [33–35], underscoring the need for improved early detection and risk stratification.

Early identification of high-risk phenotypes and biomarkers for GDM and SCH is essential for mitigating associated adverse outcomes(Table S2). In recent years, numerous studies have focused on identifying the risk factors and biomarkers for GDM, with the aim of enabling early screening for GDM and predicting pregnancy outcomes [36–39]. Metabolomics [40] and microbiomics [41] have great potential in predicting GDM. In addition, there are relatively few studies on the biomarkers of SCH during pregnancy [42, 43]. Multi-time-point and multi-omics approaches will further reveal the biomarkers that can predict metabolic disease and pregnancy outcomes, thereby facilitating early intervention.

The uniqueness of this study lies in its prospective and simultaneous incorporation of both GDM and SCH during pregnancy—the two most prevalent and interrelated endocrine disorders—within a single research framework, enabling independent comparison and interaction analysis (Table 1). In addition, longitudinal (multiple time points) and multi-dimensional (multiple omics) data enables the identification of markers for early detection of GDM and SCH warnings. This paper aims to detail the study protocol and methodologies employed.

Objective

This study aims to deeply explore the early impact of maternal endocrine disorders during pregnancy (with a focus on GDM and SCH) on the healthy development of the fetus, using key indicators such as birth weight as measures of early health outcomes. The research objectives include: (1) Identifying biomarkers that can be used for early prediction of GDM and SCH; (2) Quantitatively assessing the extent of the impact of GDM and SCH on pregnancy outcomes.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

This is a prospective cohort study (Fig. 1). The study was prospectively registered with Clinical Trials (NCT05952739). The MEMO observational cohort study will recruit 300 eligible women who will deliver at Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH). PUMCH is a tertiary Grade A hospital located in the urban area of Beijing, China. It facilitates approximately 3,000 deliveries each year. As one of the hospitals in Beijing specifically responsible for treating high-risk pregnant women, PUMCH also receives referrals from other medical institutions. Therefore, the study cohort encompasses a wide spectrum of pregnancies, from low-risk women receiving routine care to high-risk cases referred for specialized management.

Fig. 1Conceptual framework of MEMO study

Conceptual framework of MEMO study

This research was approved by the Ethics Committee of PUMCH. Before undergoing any study procedures, all eligible pregnant women will provide written informed consent (Table S3). Participants will then complete baseline assessments, including anthropometry and biometric sample collection, and attend regular prenatal check-ups.

The first participant was recruited in July 2023. The projected timeline for recruitment is that the last participant will be recruited in October 2025. It is anticipated that the last participant will have completed all study visits by October 2026.

Sampling technique

The total sample size was primarily determined by the need to ensure a sufficient number of participants with GDM for meaningful analysis. Given the prevalence of GDM in our region is 19.7%[9] and the prevalence of SCH is 13.6%[61], we calculated that a total sample size of 300 would yield approximately 60 GDM cases and 40 SCH cases. We then verified the statistical power. We subsequently verified that the statistical power for comparing these groups would be more than adequate. For the primary comparison between the GDM group (n ≈ 60) and the non-GDM group (n ≈ 241), the power to detect a significant difference in adverse outcome rates (51.7% vs. 25.8%)[62] exceeds 99% (α = 0.05, two-sided) [63], far above the conventional 80% threshold. Similarly, comparisons involving the SCH group will be well-powered.

Withdrawal criteria

Participants will be able to withdraw at any time by revoking consent or ceasing communication, and such cases will be recorded as lost to follow-up. All data collected prior to withdrawal will be retained and included in the analysis. Participants with spontaneous abortion, embryo arrest or malformation will no longer continue the study, but the information collected up to that point that will be included in the analysis.

Study variables

Clinical data, including data from the early, middle and late stages of pregnancy and during delivery, will be derived from standardized medical records (Fig. 2). Demographic characteristics will be collected such as age, ethnicity, date of birth, occupation, medical history such as history of diabetes, thyroid diseases, hypertension, rheumatism and immune system diseases, heart diseases, cancer, adverse pregnancy and childbirth, surgical history such as thyroidectomy, gastrointestinal surgery, cholecystectomy, current medical history such as diabetes, thyroid disorders, hypertension, rheumatism and immune system diseases, as well as pregnancy-related conditions such as preeclampsia, eclampsia, gestational hypertension, and intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, medication history such as the use of insulin, oral hypoglycemic drugs, levothyroxine, aspirin, labetalol, and other medications, family history such as diabetes, thyroid diseases, cancer, labor data such as time of delivery, gestational weeks of delivery, mode of delivery, neonatal sex, length, birth weight, as well as information about birth before 37 weeks of gestation, neonatal hypoglycemia, birth trauma, birth weight ≥ 4500 g, respiratory distress, stillbirth, shoulder dystonia, neonatal death, NRDS, neonatal admission to the NICU and its causes.

Timeline and data collection

Anthropometric indicators that include prepregnancy height (cm), prepregnancy weight (kg), prepregnancy body mass index (BMI), weight gain during pregnancy (kg), blood pressure (mmHg), and weight at each visit (details in the supplementary materials) will be collected.

Ultrasound examinations are conducted by a fixed number of professional senior doctors, who collect information such as gestational weeks, biparietal diameter, head circumference, abdominal circumference, femoral length, and estimated fetal weight (EFW).

The biochemical test data collected during pregnancy mainly include blood routine tests, urine routine tests, 75 g OGTT results, fasting blood glucose, uric acid, inorganic phosphorus, HbA1c, thyroid function, thyroid antibody, liver function (alanine aminotransferase, total protein, albumin, prealbumin, total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, gamma-glutamyl transferase, alkaline phosphatase, aspartate aminotransferase and lactate dehydrogenase), kidney function (creatinine and blood urea nitrogen), blood lipids (total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (Table S4).

Quality assurance and quality control procedures

To ensure the consistency and reliability of the study, stringent quality assurance (QA) and quality control (QC) protocols have been implemented across all stages of the research, including participant registration, medical history collection, data entry, anthropometric measurements, and sample collection.

Participant registration and data collection

Participant registration adheres to standardized procedures to maintain uniformity. Medical history is collected using a comprehensive and structured protocol to ensure accuracy in subsequent data analysis.

Instrument calibration and measurement accuracy

All anthropometric and laboratory instruments undergo regular calibration in compliance with the manufacturer’s specifications. Calibration is performed at least monthly, with increased frequency if usage demands, to guarantee measurement precision and reliability.

Sample collection, processing, storage, and transportation

All the biological samples will be collected using standardized operating procedures to ensure consistency and minimize preanalytical variability (Table S5). Trained personnel will adhere to aseptic techniques, and appropriate sample containers (prelabeled with unique anonymized codes) will be used to prevent cross-contamination. The collection time, temperature, and handling conditions will be meticulously documented.

Immediately after collection, samples will be processed following predefined protocols (e.g., centrifugation, aliquoting) to maintain stability. The processing time and conditions (e.g., temperature and duration) will be recorded. Processed samples will be stored at specified temperatures (e.g., −80 °C for long-term storage) in monitored freezers equipped with alarm systems for temperature deviations. Backup power supplies will be in place to prevent storage failure.

The samples will be transported under controlled conditions using validated packaging materials (dry ice for the frozen samples). Temperature loggers will accompany shipments to ensure compliance with the required conditions. Chain-of-custody documentation will be maintained throughout transit.

Database management and data integrity

The database will be designed following established best practices and will incorporate clearly defined variable names and standardized definitions to maintain data integrity. To minimize entry errors, a double-data-entry system will be employed, followed by rigorous verification. Access permissions will be strictly controlled and all modifications will be logged with timestamps to maintain a secure audit trail. Regular database backups will be performed by the data administrator to prevent data loss and ensure continuity.

Ethical considerations and participant confidentiality

To protect participant privacy, no personally identifiable information will be stored within the research datasets. Each participant will be assigned a unique anonymized identifier, and confidentiality will be maintained throughout the study. The participants will retain the right to withdraw consent at any time. However, any data and samples collected prior to withdrawal will remain in the analysis to preserve study validity.

Multi-omics data analysis

In addition to identifying biomarkers for early detection of GDM and SCH, we will compare the trajectories of biomarker changes between the disease and healthy control groups at different time points during pregnancy. This comparison will allow us to identify abnormal patterns deviating from the normal developmental path. Moreover, by integrating multiple omics data, we aim to reveal potential biological pathways and networks driving these abnormal changes. Our research group has summarized potential biomarkers related to GDM, including blood metabolomics [36], urine metabolomics and proteomics [37], gut microbiota [64], and non-coding RNAs [65]. Therefore, we will utilize various types of samples to conduct metabolomic, proteomic, gut microbiome, and non-coding RNA analyses in pregnant women with GDM and SCH.

Statistical methods

Descriptive analysis will be used to summarize maternal characteristics and pregnancy outcomes. Continuous variables will be presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (or interquartile range) depending on normality, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. For the primary outcome (birth weight), linear regression models will be used to assess the association with maternal metabolic conditions (GDM or SCH), adjusting for potential confounders such as maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, smoking status, alcohol use, and family history of diabetes. For binary secondary outcomes (such as LBW, SGA, LGA, preterm birth), multivariate logistic regression models will be employed to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations with GDM or SCH. Similarly, the model will be adjusted for the aforementioned potential confounding factors. If the data permits, we will conduct subgroup analyses to more precisely reveal the effects of different disease states. Pregnant women will be categorized into four groups: “No GDM and no SCH (reference group)”, “GDM alone”, “SCH alone”, and “GDM and SCH coexisting group”. The same multivariate regression models (linear or logistic) will be applied to these four subgroups to evaluate the independent and combined effects of “pure GDM”, “pure SCH”, and their coexistence. To analyze omics data (such as metabolomics, proteomics, gut microbiomics), we will employ feature selection methods (such as LASSO regression or random forest) to identify potential biomarkers associated with adverse outcomes. Additionally, multiple testing correction methods (such as Benjamini-Hochberg procedure) will be applied to control the false positive discovery rate. Multiple imputation will be used to handle missing data under the assumption of missing at random (MAR). Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to evaluate the impact of missing data. All analyses will be performed using R version 4.3.0 (or higher) and SPSS version 26 (or higher). A P value < 0.05 (two-tailed) or a False Discovery Rate (FDR) < 0.05 after multiple corrections will be considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Among pregnant women, the high prevalence of GDM and SCH warrants significant attention due to their associated short- and long-term health risks. The MEMO study aims to evaluate the effects of GDM and SCH on maternal and newborn health, and to explore early disease phenotypes. A key strength of this study is its prospective observational design, which involves the collection of clinical information and laboratory data at multiple stages throughout pregnancy. Additionally, the study incorporates a comprehensive assessment of risk factors linked to adverse pregnancy outcomes, including medical history, prior pregnancy history, laboratory results, as well as biological samples retained for omics analysis. In this study, clinical data will be collected by two well-trained obstetricians to minimize measurement errors. However, as with other cohort studies, a major limitation is the potential for loss to follow-up. To address this, we will leverage regular prenatal visits and maintain ongoing communication with participants, aiming to minimize attrition. Furthermore, the study is conducted at a tertiary Grade A hospital in Beijing, China, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings to populations in non-urban or less specialized healthcare settings.

The MEMO prospective cohort study focuses on pregnant women in an urban Beijing environment. It seeks to advance current knowledge regarding the influence of conventional risk factors and biomarkers on delivery outcomes. The findings are expected to contribute to evidence-based medicine, support health policy decision-making related to maternal and newborn health, improve counseling for future pregnancies, and inform clinical management strategies.
