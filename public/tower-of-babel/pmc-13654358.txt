Cost-Effectiveness of Polygenic Risk Score–Guided Statin Therapy for Cardiovascular Disease Prevention in Singapore
Authors: Irene Li Peng Ooi, Aloysius Wei-Yan Chia, Ying Jie Chee, Liuh Ling Goh, Khai Pang Leong, Sanchalika Acharyya, Rinkoo Dalan, Akshar Saxena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in Singapore. Under current guidelines, individuals at intermediate risk often do not receive preventive statin therapy despite potentially elevated genetic risk. Polygenic risk scores (PRSs) offer an opportunity to refine risk stratification, but their economic value in Asian health care settings remains uncertain.ObjectivesThe authors aimed to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of PRS-guided risk assessment for a cohort of 45-year-old Singapore residents with intermediate (7.5%-20%) 10-year ASCVD risk.MethodsWe developed a state-transition Markov model comparing 2 strategies: 1) standard risk assessment using the pooled cohort equation, with statin initiation based on traditional risk factors; and 2) PRS-guided reclassification, with statin therapy initiated for individuals in the top 20% of the PRS distribution. Model inputs were derived from the literature as well as local registry data. Outcomes were evaluated from the health care payer perspective over 2- to 40-year horizons. Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted.ResultsPRS-guided therapy was not cost-effective over 2- to 20-year horizons. From 30 years onward, it became cost-effective (Singapore dollars [SGD]) at a willingness-to-pay threshold of SGD 75,000/quality-adjusted life year (QALY), with an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of SGD 60,334/QALY at 30 years and SGD 23,667/QALY at 40 years. Probabilistic analysis showed a 53.5% probability of cost-effectiveness, with mean incremental cost and effectiveness of SGD 292 (95% uncertainty interval [UI]: –SGD 490 to SGD 1,052) and 0.010 QALYs (95% UI: –0.062 to 0.087), respectively.ConclusionsIncorporating PRS into ASCVD risk stratification for intermediate-risk individuals in Singapore may represent a cost-effective long-term prevention strategy as benefits of avoided ASCVD events accumulate.

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in Singapore, accounting for nearly 1 in 3 deaths in 2024.1 Singapore's multiethnic population, comprising Chinese, Malay, and Indian communities, presents a unique context for cardiovascular risk management, as differences in genetic predispositions, lifestyle factors, and health behaviors across ethnic groups may influence both disease risk and the effectiveness of preventive strategies. This multiethnic complexity adds particular relevance to efforts aimed at refining individual-level risk stratification beyond conventional clinical tools.

Traditional risk stratification tools, such as the pooled cohort equations (PCEs), rely on clinical and lifestyle factors, including age, blood pressure, cholesterol levels, smoking status, and diabetes. Global guidelines on lipid management further recommend incorporating ASCVD “risk-enhancing factors,” such as family history of premature ASCVD, a history of preeclampsia, and elevated lipoprotein(a) concentration, when considering statin initiation for intermediate-risk patients.2, 3, 4, 5 However, neither PCE nor these risk-enhancing factors systematically capture inherited genetic susceptibility to ASCVD.6 As a result, individuals with high genetic risk who do not manifest conventional clinical risk factors may remain unidentified and may not be offered preventive statin therapy, a gap that may be especially consequential in multiethnic populations where genetic risk profiles differ across groups.2, 3, 4,7

Polygenic risk scores (PRSs), which aggregate the effects of multiple common genetic variants associated with disease risk, have emerged as a promising tool to refine cardiovascular risk stratification.8, 9, 10, 11 Studies have shown that individuals in the top quintile of the PRS distribution have twice the odds of developing ASCVD compared with those in lower quintiles,12 and they derive greater relative risk reduction from statin therapy.13 Importantly, recent evidence from large Asian cohorts supports the predictive value of PRS in non-European populations,11 and Singapore's National Precision Medicine Programme, including the ongoing SG100K initiative to build a 100,000-person Asian population reference cohort, reflects growing recognition of the need for population-specific genomic tools in preventive health.14,15

Despite this promise, the economic value of integrating PRS into preventive cardiovascular strategies remains uncertain, particularly in Asian health care settings. Genomic testing entails upfront costs, but it may enable earlier intervention and reduced downstream disease burden. These trade-offs are especially relevant in Singapore, where PRS-based risk stratification models are yet to be validated at scale and where health care resource allocation decisions require locally grounded evidence.

This study evaluates the cost-effectiveness of integrating PRS into ASCVD risk stratification in Singapore. Using a health economic modeling approach, we compare standard PCE-based assessment with a PRS-guided strategy among intermediate-risk individuals, a group most likely to benefit from genetic risk reclassification, given that current guidelines already recommend incorporating risk-enhancing factors when deciding on statin initiation in this group. We quantify the potential impact on health outcomes and health care costs from the health care payer perspective, with the aim of informing policy discussions around precision prevention and resource allocation in Singapore's health system.

Methods

Overview

Approval for this study was obtained from Tan Tock Seng Hospital Institutional Review Board. We developed a state-transition Markov model to project the health care costs and health outcomes associated with integrating the cardiovascular polygenic risk score (CVD-PRS) into the risk assessment among individuals at intermediate 10-year ASCVD risk. The target cohort consisted of 45-year-old Singapore residents, and the analysis adopted a health care payer perspective. The model compared 2 strategies: 1) standard care using the PCE alone, and 2) PRS-guided reclassification, with statin therapy initiated for individuals in the top 20% of the PRS distribution.7 Outcomes were evaluated over multiple time horizons ranging from 2 to 40 years. Study population

The model simulated a hypothetical cohort of 45-year-old individuals in Singapore with an intermediate (7.5%-20%) 10-year risk of ASCVD based on the PCE.2 The cohort was assumed to be disease-free at baseline and representative of Singapore's resident population in terms of age and sex distribution.

Strategies

Two strategies were compared: 1) Standard care: Risk assessment based solely on the PCE. Among individuals with intermediate 10-year ASCVD risk, we assumed that 52% would receive statin therapy under standard care, based on the proportion meeting statin-eligibility criteria through conventional risk-enhancing factors.17 2) CVD-PRS–guided strategy: Individuals at intermediate risk per PCE were additionally stratified using the CVD-PRS. Individuals in the top 20% of the PRS distribution were reclassified as high risk and received statin therapy, with ongoing risk management in primary care. Individuals not classified as high risk continued to receive statins according to standard eligibility criteria based on conventional risk-enhancing factors.

Model structure

We developed a state-transition (Markov) model in TreeAge Pro 2025 to estimate the long-term cost-effectiveness of incorporating the CVD-PRS into ASCVD risk stratification. We referenced similar studies from other countries that used the same approach.18, 19, 20 The target cohort comprised adults aged 45 years with a 10-year intermediate risk of ASCVD, defined using the PCE. We used a simulation model because real-world patient-level data were not available to examine the health and economic consequences of implementing the PRS in Singapore.

At model entry, the cohort was assumed to be free of ASCVD. In each annual cycle, individuals could experience a first myocardial infarction (MI) or ischemic stroke, a recurrent ASCVD event, or death, with death defined as the absorbing state (Figures 1 and 2). Both disease-specific and age-adjusted background mortality were incorporated. Health-state utilities and costs were assigned to each state to project quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and costs across the model time horizons. Statins are recommended for the primary prevention of ASCVD events in high-risk individuals, and they have been shown to reduce ASCVD risk by 44% (HR: 0.56, 95% CI: 0.40-0.78; P < 0.001) among those at high genetic risk.24 As the efficacy of statins among moderate-risk individuals with high CVD-PRS has not been examined, we used the efficacy of pravastatin 40 mg daily from a study in high-risk, high CVD-PRS populations.24 Atorvastatin is one of the commonly prescribed statins in Singapore, and it has been shown to have comparable effectiveness in reducing low-density lipoprotein cholesterol.25,26 For modeling purposes, we applied separate statin treatment effects for high-PRS and non–high-PRS individuals, based on published evidence demonstrating differential treatment benefit across genetic-risk strata. In the base-case analysis, we assumed that 57.5% of the individuals identified as eligible for preventive therapy would remain adherent to statin therapy over the time horizon of the analysis.27 Lastly, we assumed that an individual could have up to 3 ASCVD events over the analytical time horizon.

Utility values

Utility weights were assigned to reflect health-related quality of life associated with each health state in the model, with 1.0 representing perfect health and 0.0 representing death. Utility weights for MI, stroke, post-MI, and poststroke were derived from the literature. A weighted average of the utility values for MI and stroke was used to represent overall utility during ASCVD events and the post-ASCVD period.28, 29, 30 For individuals in the disease-free state receiving statin therapy, we incorporated a utility decrement to reflect the disutility of daily medication use. Based on evidence from the literature, the mean utility of taking a daily pill ranges from 0.990 to 0.994, and we conservatively applied the lowest estimate (0.990) in the base case.31 All utilities were discounted by 3% each year, in line with recommended economic evaluation guideline and consistent with guidelines used by the Agency for Care Effectiveness for Singapore's health technology assessments.

Costs

Direct medical costs included PRS testing, statin therapy, routine outpatient consultations, and the acute and long-term management of ASCVD events. Cost estimates for ASCVD events were derived from published Singapore-specific studies and administrative data, with distinct methodologies applied for stroke and MI. These estimates included costs for hospitalization, procedures, medications, and postevent care. All costs were reported in 2024 Singapore dollars (SGD) and were discounted at an annual rate of 3%, in accordance with standard economic evaluation guidelines.

The unit cost of PRS testing was SGD 100, while the annual cost of statin therapy (atorvastatin) was SGD 146. Individuals receiving statins were assumed to attend a primary care physician consultation every 9 months, costing SGD 102 per visit based on polyclinic (public sector primary care) rates.

For stroke, total health care costs per patient included both the 90-day short-term health care cost after initial ischemic stroke and lifetime follow-up costs.32,33 The resulting estimates were SGD 22,842 in the first year and SGD 10,724 annually thereafter. Fatal stroke was assigned a 1-time cost of SGD 6,666.

For nonfatal MI, cost estimates were adapted from a previously published cost-effectiveness analysis of ticagrelor in patients with acute coronary syndromes, in which a 2-step approach was used to address the limited availability of comprehensive cost data in Singapore.34 First, acute hospitalization costs were obtained from the public hospital accounting system using diagnosis-related group codes corresponding to MI. These costs were averaged across hospitals and were weighted by the number of patients within each diagnosis-related group. Second, a South Korean report was used to estimate the relative proportions of hospital versus outpatient costs in the first and subsequent years following MI.35 These relative cost ratios were then applied to the Singapore acute hospitalization estimates to impute total health care costs, including both inpatient and outpatient components, for the corresponding time periods. These estimates thus included costs for drugs, revascularization procedures, hospitalization, physician visits, and echocardiography, radiography, and laboratory investigations. The resultant costs were SGD 33,641 for the first year after the MI event and SGD 949 annually for subsequent years. The cost for fatal MI was assumed to be the same as the cost of treating an episode of MI and was assigned a 1-time cost of SGD 9,883.36

Lastly, we calculated the weighted average costs for ASCVD events based on the incidence distribution of MI (56.3%) and stroke (43.7%) in Singapore.37,38 Age-specific mortality rates were derived from the Department of Statistics Singapore.39

Analysis

We conducted the cost-effectiveness analysis from the Singapore public health care payer perspective, comparing total costs and health outcomes for the PRS-guided strategy versus standard PCE-based assessment across multiple time horizons: 2 years, 5 years, 10 years, 20 years, 30 years, and 40 years. Outcomes included total and incremental costs, total and incremental QALYs, incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), and net monetary benefit (NMB).

The World Health Organization recommends using thresholds of 1 to 3 times the country's annual gross domestic product per capita for assessing cost-effectiveness.40 A strategy was considered cost-effective if the ICER was below 1 times the gross domestic product per capita threshold (SGD 120,000 for Singapore in 2024). Given that this may overstate affordability, a more conservative willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of SGD 75,000/QALY was applied, consistent with thresholds commonly used by the Agency for Care Effectiveness, Singapore's health technology assessment body.41 A review of the Agency for Care Effectiveness's drug and device appraisals found that most drugs with ICERs below SGD 75,000/QALY received positive recommendations from the agency.42

We assessed external consistency by comparing projected outcomes under the standard care as well as the magnitude and direction of the incremental costs and effectiveness with published data from other modeling and clinical studies.10,18

We conducted 1-way deterministic sensitivity analysis to examine how variation in individual model parameters influenced the incremental NMB. We varied a single parameter at a time while keeping all other parameters at their baseline values. The plausible ranges for each parameter were derived from published studies where available. Because the MI cost estimates were partly derived using a nonlocal cost ratio, we used a wider variance (±50%) to reflect uncertainty associated with the cross-country transfer of cost inputs. Results were summarized using a tornado diagram to show the relative influence of each parameter on the incremental NMB.

We also performed probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) to quantify the combined impact of uncertainty in model parameters on the incremental NMB. We assigned beta distributions to probability parameters, log-normal distributions to relative risk and hazard ratios, and gamma distributions to cost parameters, as shown in Table 3. We performed 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations and plotted the resulting incremental cost-effectiveness pairs on the cost-effectiveness plane. To address potential correlation among PSA inputs, we reviewed model parameters for structural dependence and shared sources of uncertainty.45 Because empirical covariance matrices were not available for the key relative risk and cost inputs, we did not impose a full multivariate correlation structure, as specifying arbitrary pairwise correlations could introduce unsupported assumptions. Instead, we implemented a correlated-uncertainty scenario using common multiplicative scaling factors for parameter groups expected to move together. Specifically, a log-normal multiplier (μ = 0, σ = 0.084) was applied jointly to statin treatment-effect parameters, corresponding to a median multiplier of 1.00 and approximately −15% to +18% variation around the median. Separate log-normal multipliers with the same parameterization were applied to MI and stroke cost families, so that acute, fatal, and follow-up costs within each disease category varied together within each PSA iteration. We then reran the PSA and compared mean incremental costs, incremental QALYs, 95% uncertainty intervals (95% UIs), and the cost-effectiveness acceptability results with those from the primary PSA.

Discussion

Principal findings

We developed a Markov model to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of integrating PRS with PCE for guiding statin initiation among adults at intermediate ASCVD risk in Singapore (Central Illustration). Our results suggest that PRS-guided therapy was not cost-effective in the short term (2-20 years), reflecting the upfront costs of PRS testing and preventive treatment. From the 30-year horizon onward, however, the PRS-guided strategy became cost-effective compared with standard care at a WTP threshold of SGD 75,000/QALY. One-way deterministic sensitivity analysis indicated that the effects of statin therapy across PRS strata, baseline ASCVD risks, and adherence to statin therapy exerted the greatest influence on the results. Higher baseline ASCVD risk increased the number of ASCVD events and deaths averted, thereby improving cost-effectiveness. In contrast, a higher relative risk under statin therapy, corresponding to a smaller treatment effect, reduced the number of ASCVD events prevented and therefore worsened cost-effectiveness.

Interpretation in context

These findings have important clinical and policy implications in Singapore, where statin initiation for individuals at intermediate ASCVD risk is currently guided by conventional risk-enhancing factors rather than genetic risk.46 However, a subset of these individuals remains at substantially elevated genetic risk and may benefit disproportionately from earlier intervention. Unlike conventional risk calculators, which rely on clinical risk factors that change over time, PRS is genetically determined and remains fixed over the life course.47 Incorporating PRS testing enables reclassification of a proportion of this intermediate-risk group into a higher-risk category in which statin therapy is clinically indicated, thereby improving the efficiency of prevention strategies.8,9

In the scenario analysis using the PREVENT equation, the PRS-guided strategy was associated with a lower ICER than in the corresponding PCE-based analysis. This difference was driven primarily by differences in how PREVENT and PCE classify individuals into cardiovascular risk categories. PREVENT generally estimates lower 10-year ASCVD risk than PCE, particularly among older age groups.48 As a result, the population classified as intermediate risk differed between the 2 approaches. These differences influence both the number of individuals receiving preventive treatment and the baseline risk of ASCVD events within the target population. In our model, the PREVENT-defined intermediate-risk population had a higher baseline annual probability of ASCVD events than the corresponding PCE-defined population (0.0164 vs 0.010), which translated into a larger absolute benefit from statin therapy. Consequently, more ASCVD events and ASCVD deaths were averted, leading to greater QALY gains and lower incremental costs than in the corresponding PCE-based analysis. This translated into a more favorable cost-effectiveness profile for the PRS strategy under PREVENT.

Although PRS-guided prevention entails higher upfront costs for genotyping, statin therapy, and additional primary-care consultations, these costs are offset over time as avoided MI and stroke events accumulate. PRS can be applied at a younger age, for instance patients in their 20s and 30s, compared with risk calculators like PCE or PREVENT, which are usually applied at age 40 for 10-year risk. As a result, PRS can alert the patient of their ASCVD risk much earlier. This underscores the importance of assessing the clinical utility of genomic screening programs over extended time horizons to capture their full preventive value.

We incorporated statin adherence in the base-case analysis to better reflect real-world treatment effectiveness, and we further examined its influence in deterministic sensitivity analysis. Adherence was one of the parameters with the greatest influence on cost-effectiveness, indicating that long-term persistence with preventive therapy is an important determinant of the value of PRS-guided statin initiation.

In clinical practice, nonadherence to statins is often driven by concerns about side-effects, particularly statin-associated muscle symptoms.49 Although observational studies and registries report the incidence of statin-associated muscle symptoms in 5% to 20% of users, randomized controlled trials report an incidence <5%.50 Most patients experiencing mild muscle symptoms can continue statin therapy at a reduced dose or switch to an alternative statin; in a small subset where statin intolerance is confirmed, other lipid-lowering strategies may be considered in consultation with a specialist. Severe adverse effects, such as myopathy with markedly elevated creatine kinase or rhabdomyolysis, are extremely rare (approximately 1 in 10,000 patients), as is clinically significant hepatotoxicity (0.001%). Statins may also slightly increase the risk of new-onset diabetes mellitus in susceptible individuals.2 Overall, the available evidence suggests that the preventive benefits of targeted statin use outweigh the potential harms in this intermediate-risk population, supporting the feasibility of PRS-guided statin initiation.

Our findings are consistent with international evidence suggesting that the cost-effectiveness of PRS-guided statin therapy emerges only over longer time horizons. For example, a Canadian study using UK Biobank data found that PRS-informed statin initiation in intermediate-risk groups offers good value compared with conventional risk tools.10 Similarly, a U.S. payer-perspective study found PRS-guided workplace cardiovascular prevention programs to be feasible and economically favourable.18 Across these studies, as in ours, the short-term costs of genotyping initially outweigh the modest reductions in events, but long-term modeling consistently favors targeted treatment based on genetic risk.

Differences in the magnitude of cost-effectiveness estimates between Singapore and Western countries likely reflect Singapore's relatively high hospitalization costs for ASCVD, more conservative statin prescribing practices, and differences in baseline cardiovascular risk.46 A unique challenge in Singapore is its multiethnic population. Because most PRS models were derived from European cohorts, their predictive calibration may be less precise among Asian subgroups, with implications for both equity and real-world effectiveness. This underscores the need for local validation datasets before widespread clinical implementation. To address this need for diverse datasets, a 100,000-person Asian population reference cohort using high-throughput sequencing (the SG100K initiative) is currently underway in Singapore.14

Policy implications

Our results provide an economic rationale for incorporating PRS into cardiovascular disease prevention strategies for individuals at intermediate risk in Singapore. At the time of this study, PRS has not been incorporated into standard clinical practice, though it is approaching clinical translation. Importantly, a moratorium is in place preventing insurance companies from using PRS results to raise premiums, providing protection for individuals who undergo testing. Its adoption would align with the National Precision Medicine Programme, which aims to develop population-specific genomic tools for preventive health.15 Successful implementation, however, would require several systemic adjustments.1)Testing infrastructure and integration: Ensure that PRS testing can be offered seamlessly at scale and made accessible to the general population, potentially supported by central laboratories or polyclinic integration.2)Clinician education: Equip primary care physicians to interpret and communicate PRS results alongside conventional chronic disease risk factors.3)Patient behavior: Evaluate the impact of PRS disclosure on preventive behavior among target populations.514)Equity considerations and reimbursement: Provide subsidies or financial support to ensure equitable access to genomic testing among multiethnic populations.5)Ethical safeguards: Maintain strong data-protection standards, governance structures, and transparent communication with patients about the meaning and limitations of PRS.

Study limitations

First, the transferability of model inputs to Singapore's multiethnic population represents an overarching source of uncertainty. The PCE was used to define the intermediate-risk target population, but we did not perform an independent recalibration or external validation against Singapore-specific outcome data. The hazard ratios for ASCVD risk elevation in high-PRS individuals (HR: 1.62) and for statin efficacy in this group (HR: 0.56) were derived predominantly from European-ancestry populations, and their magnitude may differ in Asian individuals. The long-term predictive validity of current PRS models in Asian populations also remains underexplored. Critically, these 3 uncertainties, in risk classification, risk elevation, and treatment effect, are not fully independent, and all converge on the same model output: the absolute ASCVD benefit of PRS-guided statin initiation. Our sensitivity analyses address each source separately; notably, substituting an Asian-derived risk HR from a large Chinese prospective cohort study (Lu et al;44 HR: 1.84) did not materially alter the main conclusions. Nevertheless, the compounded effect of all 3 uncertainties acting simultaneously may exceed what is captured within the probabilistic sensitivity analysis uncertainty intervals. Local validation of both the PCE and CVD-PRS in Singapore's population is currently being addressed through the SG100K initiative and remains an important prerequisite for clinical implementation.

Second, we relied on cost estimates from other countries where Singapore-specific data were unavailable. MI costs were partly derived by applying a South Korean cost ratio to Singapore hospitalization data, which may introduce cross-country transfer bias. We addressed this by applying a wider variance (±50%) to the MI cost parameter in the probabilistic sensitivity analysis, and sensitivity analyses did not materially alter the main conclusions.

Third, our cohort-based state-transition Markov model assumes memoryless transitions. To better reflect recurrent disease progression, we incorporated differential incidence risks for second and third ASCVD events. Nevertheless, a patient-level microsimulation or semi-Markov model would provide greater flexibility in modeling duration dependence and individual heterogeneity; such an approach was beyond the scope of this analysis given the lack of suitable longitudinal data for the target population.

Fourth, although statin adherence was incorporated in the base-case analysis and examined in sensitivity analyses, real-world adherence may be lower and may vary across demographic subgroups. Given the limited published data, we did not model dynamic statin adherence trajectories over time.

Fifth, our model assumed that the top 20% of the PRS distribution would benefit most from early statin initiation. We were unable to explore alternative PRS thresholds to determine how initiating statins for a smaller or larger proportion of patients might affect outcomes.

Sixth, our analysis did not explore subgroup heterogeneity by sex or ethnicity. Because ASCVD risk differs by sex, differences in sex composition between Singapore's population and the source populations for model parameters may affect overall outcomes.

Finally, broader societal costs such as productivity losses and caregiving were excluded, which may underestimate the total economic benefit of avoided ASCVD events.

Conclusions

This cost-effectiveness analysis suggests that incorporating a cardiovascular polygenic risk score into conventional ASCVD risk stratification in Singapore is unlikely to be cost-effective in the short to medium term but may become cost-effective over longer time horizons as the benefits of avoided ASCVD events accumulate. At 30 years, PRS-guided statin initiation was associated with an ICER of SGD 60,334/QALY, below the SGD 75,000/QALY threshold. These findings suggest that PRS-guided prevention may represent a cost-effective approach to refining statin initiation among intermediate-risk individuals, aligning with Singapore's long-term precision-health strategy. Further research is needed to validate the predictive performance of PRS in local populations and to address implementation, equity, and ethical considerations before broad policy adoption.
