Prognostic significance of contrast staining following mechanical thrombectomy in acute ischaemic stroke
Authors: Jing Yan, Xiang Xu, Haiyan Li, Zhonghua Yang, Ximing Nie, Na Wei, Dandan Yu, Hongyi Yan, Miao Wen, Ling Wang, Liping Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvances in endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) and extended treatment criteria have improved outcomes in acute ischaemic stroke (AIS). However, contrast staining (CS) on postoperative CT complicates clinical decision-making and outcome evaluation. We investigated the association between postoperative CS and 90-day clinical outcomes in AIS patients.MethodsIn this multicentre observational study, we enrolled AIS patients treated with EVT who underwent non-contrast CT (NCCT) within 2 hours postprocedure. Patients were stratified into two groups based on the presence or absence of CS to further explore the relationship between CS characteristics and clinical outcomes. The primary outcome was poor functional outcome, defined as a modified Rankin Scale score ≥3 at 90 days, evaluated with the logistic regression analysis adjusted for age, sex and other clinical features.ResultsAmong the 420 patients (mean age 63 years; 74.3% male), CS was observed in 250 (59.5%) following EVT. Logistic regression analysis showed that CS was strongly associated with poor functional outcomes. At 3 months, the proportion of patients with functional dependence was significantly higher in the CS group (76.8%) compared with the non-CS group (62.4%). In addition, the CS group exhibited a higher death rate compared with the non-CS group (p=0.028). Our study found that CS in the pons, as well as larger and denser staining volumes, was often indicative of poor prognosis.ConclusionIn around half of AIS patients with EVT, CS can be observed and independently associate with poor clinical outcomes, primarily related to the location and density of CS.Keywords: Thrombectomy, Stroke

Introduction

Endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) is an established and effective treatment for restoring blood perfusion in acute ischaemic stroke (AIS) caused by large vessel occlusion (LVO). Postprocedural non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) often reveals high-density areas （HDAs）in the brain, with reported incidence ranging from 30.7% to 87.5%.1,4 These HDAs are not always indicative of haemorrhagic transformation (HT). Follow-up NCCT performed 24 hours later frequently demonstrates significant resolution or normalisation of these hyperdensities, suggesting their heterogeneous nature, including contrast staining (CS), HT or a combination of both.3 5

CS is attributed to blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption, allowing extravasation of contrast agents, coupled with impaired clearance due to ischaemia-induced neuronal metabolic dysfunction.6 7 The presence of CS is believed to increase the risk of HT.4 8 When ischaemic injury is limited to endothelial cells, CS is primarily composed of contrast material alone. However, when the damage extends to the basement membrane, CS may be accompanied by varying degrees of ICH.9 Studies using dual-energy CT (DECT) have shown an association between CS and higher rates of HT; however, its prognostic implications remain unclear.10 11 Moreover, the mechanisms and evaluation criteria of DECT differ from those of NCCT, leaving the prognostic value of NCCT-detected CS following EVT insufficiently understood and underexplored. There is also a lack of detailed characterisation of CS features, such as volume, density and location, and their associations with clinical outcomes.

This study aims to evaluate the incidence of CS after EVT and its influence on clinical outcomes in AIS patients. Additionally, the predictive value of CS imaging characteristics for outcome at 3 months after EVT was further explored.

Methods

Study design and populations

Participants were drawn from the Registration Study for Critical Care of Acute Ischemic Stroke After Recanalization, an ongoing, prospective, multicentre, observational registry study.12 Conducted at 18 comprehensive stroke centres in China, this registry includes patients with LVO-AIS who underwent EVT within 24 hours of symptom onset.

From January 2019 to June 2023, patients aged ≥18 years were enrolled if they underwent NCCT imaging within 2 hours post-EVT to assess for CS.13 Follow-up NCCT was performed 24–72 hours after EVT to evaluate for HT.10 Clinical outcomes were assessed at 90 days. Patients with incomplete imaging or clinical data and those with intraoperative haemorrhage during EVT were excluded.

Data collection

Baseline characteristics, including demographic information, medical history, clinical variables, stroke features and thrombectomy details, were collected by trained research coordinators. Stroke severity was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS). Initial CT imaging was evaluated with the Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) for anterior circulation strokes and the Posterior Circulation ASPECTS (pc-ASPECTS) for posterior circulation strokes. Stroke aetiology was determined using the Trial of Org 10 172 in Acute Stroke Treatment criteria, and prestroke functional status was assessed with the modified Rankin Scale (mRS).10 14

Successful recanalisation of EVT was defined as modified treatment in cerebral infarction (mTICI) 2b–3, while non-recanalisation was defined as mTICI 0 to 2a at the end of the procedure.14

Imaging assessment

All patients underwent non-contrast cranial CT scans (120 kVp, 170–300 mA/slice, 5.0 mm slice thickness reconstruction; Phillips, Cambridge, MA, USA) within 2 hours after EVT to determine whether there were new high-density areas, and a follow-up cranial CT scan was performed 24–72 hours after EVT. CT images were independently reviewed by two experienced investigators blinded to clinical data, involving a third neuroradiologist on disagreement. CS was defined as newly appearing high-density areas on the immediate post-EVT CT scan that disappeared or significantly decreased on follow-up CT at 24 hours without mass effect.5 15 16

The location of CS was classified as brain parenchymal, subarachnoid, or both (figure 1) and further categorised by vascular territory as responsible vascular area, non-responsible vascular area or both. The spatial distribution of CS was evaluated using ASPECTS or pc-ASPECTS scores (range: 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating fewer staining sites) for anterior and posterior circulation strokes, respectively.1 Anterior circulation CS was subdivided into deep nuclei and cortical staining, while posterior circulation CS was categorised as staining in the proximal, middle or distal basilar artery (BA) segments: proximal (medulla plane), middle (pons plane) and distal (midbrain plane). Brain parenchymal CS volume and CT density was measured using ITK-SNAP software, V.4.2.0 (online supplemental figure1).17

Preoperative CT perfusion imaging was processed using RAPID automated software to generate quantitative estimates, which included ischaemic core (relative cerebral blood flow, rCBF<30% vol), critically hypoperfused tissue (tissue with time to maximum intensity, Tmax >6 s) and mismatch volume (defined as the difference between the volume of Tmax >6 s and the volume of rCBF <30%).18

The status of collateral circulation was graded by pretreatment digital subtraction angiography (DSA), which was classified as poor (grades 0–1) or good (grades 2–4)19 according to the ASITN/SIR Collateral Flow Grading System.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was functional dependence or death at 90 days, defined as a mRS score of 3–6. Secondary outcomes included parenchymal haemorrhage (PH) within 72 hours after EVT, symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage (sICH) and early neurological deterioration (END). According to the ECASS II criteria, PH is defined as the presence of hematoma occupying ≤30% of the infarct area with mild mass effect (PH1) or haematoma involving >30% of the infarct area with substantial mass effect (PH2).20 sICH was defined according to the ECASS II criteria as any haemorrhage accompanied by neurological deterioration, indicated by an increase of ≥4 points on the NIHSS compared with baseline within 72 hours post-EVT.20 END was defined as an increase of ≥4 points in the NIHSS score within 7 days postprocedure compared with baseline.21 Mortality at 90 days was also recorded.

All outcomes were assessed by trained research coordinators blinded to baseline data through in-person clinic visits or follow-up telephone interviews.

Statistical analysis

Fisher’s exact test or χ2 test was used for categorical variables, and Mann-Whitney U test or t test was used for continuous variables. Continuous variables were expressed as mean±SD or median (IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages). To investigate the association between CS and clinical functional outcome variables, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed. The confounding factors selected based on clinical significance or statistical significance in the univariate analysis were adjusted for in two multivariable regression models. All models were tested for multicollinearity by tolerance (TOL>0.1) and for goodness-of-fit via the Hosmer-Lemeshow GOF test (>0.05). Furthermore, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by key confounding factors to evaluate their potential impact on the primary outcomes. logistic regression was used to analyse the relationship between different imaging features of CS and main clinical outcomes. Statistical significance was defined by two-sided p value <0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

From a total of 726 adult AIS patients underwent EVT within 24 hours of symptom onset, 420 patients were included in the final analysis after excluding those with incomplete data or intraoperative haemorrhage (figure 2). The exclusion criteria were as follows: 237 patients lacked postoperative within 2-hour CT, 37 patients did not undergo CT within 24–72 hours after surgery, 2 patients had poor image quality, and 18 patients with intraoperative haemorrhage and 12 patients with incomplete follow-up data were also excluded. The baseline clinical characteristics of included and excluded patients were comparable (online supplemental Table 1). The mean age was 62.6 years (SD, 12.4), and 74.3% of the cohort were male. The median admission NIHSS score was 13. CS was observed in 250 patients (59.5%) post-EVT.

Although the incidence of PH HT was significantly higher in the CS group (p<0.0001), no significant difference was observed in the incidence of sICH between the CS and non-CS groups in multivariate regression models (p=0.072; table 2).

CS was significantly associated with an increased risk of END in both univariate (14.87% vs 7.14%; crude OR, 2.27; p=0.019) and multivariate regression models (p=0.007; table 2). Contrast staining characteristics and clinical outcomes

Among patients with CS, 58 (23.2%) achieved a good functional outcome. As shown in online supplemental table 3, CS patients with poor functional outcomes were significantly older, with a higher median baseline NIHSS score, and had higher proportions of hypertension and diabetes. Additionally, these patients had higher blood glucose levels, lower eGFR, larger infarct core volumes, more thrombectomy attempts and poor collateral status, all of which were associated with worse outcomes (online supplemental table 3).

By analysing the different CS locations, we found that CS in the middle segment of the basilar artery was associated with worse clinical outcomes (adjust OR=28.43, 95% CI 1.91 to 423.54, p=0.015, table 3). In addition, larger CS volume (adjusted OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.24, p=0.033) and lower CS-ASPECT score (adjusted OR=0.78, 95% CI 0.63 to 0.96, p=0.021) were identified as indicators of poor prognosis (table 3).

Discussion

In this study, contrast staining was observed in constituting 59.5% of patients following EVT. We found significant associations between CS and adverse clinical outcomes, including an mRS of 3–6 at 90 days, END within 7 days, PH within 72 hours and 90-day mortality. Notably, among patients with CS, larger staining volumes and higher CT density were associated with a worse prognosis.

Prior research has identified risk factors for CS following aneurysm coil embolization, including hypertension, larger aneurysm size, prolonged procedure duration and increased contrast medium use.13 Similarly, in our cohort, several variables were significantly associated with CS after EVT: a history of atrial fibrillation, lower blood pressure, ASPECTS, larger core infarct volumes, cardioembolic stroke, longer procedure times, a greater number of thrombectomy attempts and higher rates of recanalisation. Importantly, multivariable logistic regression analysis identified cardioembolic stroke as an independent risk factor for CS. This finding aligns with the pathophysiological mechanisms of cardiogenic embolism, which are characterised by frequent occurrences of emergent large-vessel occlusion, inadequate collateral circulation and severe BBB disruption.22 It is well known that increased permeability of the BBB is a major cause of CS.23 Our results align with findings from An et al, who demonstrated similar associations between cardiogenic embolism and CS using DECT imaging.10 These data suggest that the haemodynamic instability and vascular injury caused by cardiogenic embolism may exacerbate BBB dysfunction, thereby increasing the likelihood of CS and subsequent adverse outcomes. Therefore, we should be alert to the possibility of CS in patients with cardiogenic embolism. Furthermore, comprehensive preoperative evaluation and screening should be conducted for these patients before undergoing EVT.

The occurrence of CS suggests increased BBB permeability in the AIS patients.23 In AIS patients, BBB disruption can lead to a variety of serious complications, such as vasogenic brain oedema, HT and ischaemia-reperfusion injury.24 25 Currently, there is no consensus regarding the clinical implications of CS following EVT. While studies have evaluated the relationship between CS and NIHSS scores at discharge, no statistically significant correlation has been consistently reported.26 However, it is important to note that many of these studies have relied on small sample sizes, which may undermine their statistical power and reliability. Other investigations have highlighted that parenchymal hyperdensity on post-EVT CT scans is strongly associated with HT and worse clinical outcomes.4 In our study, patients with CS demonstrated worse functional outcomes at 90 days compared with those without CS after adjustment for confounding factors such as ASPECT score. Therefore, CS may be an independent marker of poor outcome in AIS patients. Meanwhile, through subgroup analysis, we found that the impact of CS on clinical outcome was consistent across different subgroups. Patients with CS had a worse clinical outcome. Moreover, CS was identified as an independent risk factor for early neurological deterioration. Our study yielded an intriguing finding: while CS demonstrated a significant association with PH HT, no such correlation was observed with sICH. However, we observed an increased proportion of sICH in CS patients. The limited sample size of sICH patients may compromise the statistical power and reliability of the results. These findings suggest that the presence of CS after EVT is associated with HT and END in patients. Therefore, CS may serve as a valuable predictive marker for poor clinical outcomes, warranting further investigation into its underlying mechanisms and potential role in poststroke management. On the other hand, since CS is an independent risk factor for HT, the postoperative anti-platelet treatment strategy may require adjustment for patients with CS.

In our study, several factors were found to influence the prognosis of patients with CS, including blood pressure, blood glucose, eGFR, initial NIHSS score, core infarct volume, collateral status and the number of thrombectomy attempts. Previous studies also support the above conclusions.1327,29 A previous study analysing 64 patients with CS after EVT found that prognosis was influenced by the volume and location of CS.30 Our findings corroborate these conclusions. Specifically, analysis based on CS location revealed that in posterior circulation stroke (PCS), poor prognosis was significantly associated with pontine-plane CS. Interestingly, both our study and prior research10 observed a lower incidence of CS in PCS compared with anterior circulation stroke. This disparity may be explained by several factors. First, the posterior circulation has a greater ischaemic tolerance, with BBB disruption occurring later in this region compared with the anterior circulation.31 Second, PCS patients generally exhibit better collateral circulation.32 Finally, the posterior circulation’s reduced sensitivity to vasoreactivity and autoregulation may limit contrast staining in the brain parenchyma.33 34 Consequently, the occurrence of CS in the posterior circulation likely reflects more severe BBB disruption and lesions, which are indicative of worse clinical outcomes. For patients exhibiting CS, more rigorous management of blood pressure and blood glucose levels may be necessary to improve clinical outcomes.

Recent studies have shown that CS in non-infarcted tissue following EVT may be associated with successful reperfusion. These patients exhibited significantly lower mRS scores at discharge compared with those without CS.15 This phenomenon may be explained by reversible and milder BBB disruption in non-infarcted CS, as opposed to the severe and often irreversible BBB damage associated with infarcted areas.15 However, our study did not identify a significant association between non-infarct CS and clinical outcomes. This discrepancy may stem from differences in study populations, highlighting the need for larger sample sizes and more heterogeneous cohorts in future research.

Moreover, our study highlighted that CS volume and density are closely associated with clinical prognosis. Patients with favourable outcomes (mRS 0–2 at 90 days) had significantly smaller CS volumes compared with those with poor outcomes (2.58 mL vs 4.47 mL). Additionally, the maximum CT density of CS was significantly lower in the good outcome group compared with the poor outcome group (65 HU vs 80 HU). These findings emphasise the prognostic value of CS volume and density as potential imaging biomarkers for predicting post-EVT outcomes. Further studies are warranted to validate these findings and explore their clinical utility in tailoring post-EVT management strategies.

There are several limitations to our study. First of all, as a retrospective study, potential confounding factors cannot be completely controlled. Second, the sample size is relatively small, and some analyses may be biased. But there was sufficient capacity to detect clinically significant differences in primary outcomes. Third, there was only one brain CT within 24 hours after surgery, and no dynamic changes of contrast staining were observed. Moreover, this study excluded a relatively large number of patients with incomplete imaging data. Fourthly, due to limitations in sample size, we were unable to classify the types of HT in patients. Future studies should aim to expand the sample size to further investigate the relationship between CS and different types of HT. Last but not least, current studies on the clinical value of CS have shown different results in different populations.4 26 Our findings need further validation in other populations.

Conclusion

Contrast staining (CS) emerges as an independent and potent predictor of adverse clinical outcomes in AIS patients post-EVT. Patients presenting with CS in the posterior circulation or those with CS characterised by larger volume and density are often associated with a poor prognosis. Therefore, it is imperative to conduct thorough evaluation and vigilant postoperative monitoring for these patients. Developing an individualised postoperative management strategy based on the specific situation of CS presence may represent a promising new avenue for improving patient outcomes.
