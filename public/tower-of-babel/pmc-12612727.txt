The suffering experiences of patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma in China: a qualitative study
Authors: Suting Song, Xinyi Liu, Ya Lu, Wanhong Xiong, Dan Wang, Renhui Wen, Yan Zeng, Zhaoli Zhang, Yu Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore the suffering experiences of patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) in China.Design, setting and participantsThis qualitative study employed a descriptive phenomenological approach. Data were collected through semistructured interviews and analysed using Colaizzi’s seven-step method. The study was conducted at a university-affiliated cancer centre in Southwest China. A total of 20 patients diagnosed with NPC were recruited through purposive sampling between January and April 2024.Results‘Disruption to reinvention’ emerged as a unifying thread that integrated three main themes (and nine subthemes): (1) beginning of suffering: plunged into a world of illness (denial and fear, significant concern and life in chaos); (2) struggle for adjustment: a rollercoaster experience (suffering trajectory, aggravating factors and alleviating factors) and (3) beyond suffering: living with illness (perceived benefits, new normal in life and personal growth).ConclusionsThe suffering experiences of patients with NPC represent a complex and dynamic process. People with NPC frequently experience multiple sufferings, disrupting their lives. Notably, most patients progressed from disruption to reinvention, reflecting the possibility that suffering can be transcended. The findings of this study can assist healthcare professionals in better identifying the suffering of patients and its influencing factors, allowing for the tailoring of interventions to alleviate their suffering.Keywords: Nursing Care, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, China, Neoplasms, Head & neck tumours, MENTAL HEALTH

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is an epithelial carcinoma arising from the nasopharyngeal mucosal lining.1 The geographical distribution of NPC is extremely unbalanced globally, with a high prevalence in East Asia and Southeast Asia, particularly in South China.1 In 2022, approximately 120 416 new cases of NPC were diagnosed, with China contributing 51 000 cases (42.3%) and ranking first globally.2 3 NPC remains a major public health problem in China, which faces a heavy disease burden. NPC is highly sensitive to ionising radiation, and comprehensive radiotherapy-based treatment is the main treatment method for NPC.1 The 5-year overall survival rate of patients with non-metastatic NPC is 82.7% in China.4

Despite relatively favourable survival rates, patients with NPC often experience significant suffering. Suffering is an all-encompassing, dynamic and individual phenomenon characterised by the sufferer’s experience of alienation, helplessness, hopelessness and meaninglessness, which are often difficult to articulate. It is multidimensional and usually involves an undesirable, negative quality.5 Patients with NPC suffer not only from physical symptoms caused by the disease but also from a wide range of radiotherapy-induced side effects, such as dysphagia, xerostomia, taste alterations and mucositis.6,8 In addition, they also experience psychological distress due to various factors, such as economic burdens and a lack of social support.9 Notably, NPC is relatively more common in young and middle-aged men,1 who are pivotal to family functioning. A cancer diagnosis leads to a loss of family roles and control over life, coupled with hopelessness, helplessness, worthlessness and meaninglessness, further exacerbating existential distress.10 Even for NPC survivors, although advances in treatment have prolonged survival, late toxicities and their consequences can persist over a long period, causing damage to core aspects of daily life, psychological distress and social interaction dilemmas.11 12 This highlights the complexity of patients’ suffering and its profound impacts on their physical and mental health, daily life, social interactions and sense of existence.13

Previous studies on the suffering experiences of patients with NPC have mostly focused on specific dimensions of their suffering, such as physical suffering and psychological distress,7 9 and thus fail to capture the multiple dimensions of suffering. Although Jiang et al14 explored patients’ psychosocial distress and positive changes, they did not clarify the trajectory of psychosocial adaptation. Additionally, when faced with suffering, patients may experience a complex range of interconnected emotions and responses, which can be broadly categorised into continued suffering or moving toward transcendence.5 Ning et al15 identified the psychological distress trajectories of patients with NPC using a latent class growth model and explored the factors influencing these trajectories. However, there remains a lack of qualitative exploration of suffering trajectories.

Although previous studies have preliminarily revealed the suffering of patients with NPC from different perspectives, this understanding remains limited. Therefore, this study aims to explore the suffering experiences of patients with NPC through a qualitative approach, to understand the trajectory of their suffering.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a descriptive qualitative phenomenological approach, with a focus on the participants’ experiences. Throughout the entire research process, from data collection to data analysis and the reporting of the findings, the study was guided by the methodological principles of emphasising openness, critically questioning pre-understanding and adopting a reflective attitude.16 The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist was used to ensure reporting quality.17

Setting and participants

The participants were recruited through purposive sampling between 25 January 2024 and 21 April 2024 from a university-affiliated cancer centre in Southwest China. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) aged ≥18 years, (b) pathologically diagnosed with NPC regardless of treatment stage and (c) interested in participating in research and sharing their experiences. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) concomitant tumours, (b) mental illness and (c) another serious disease (such as heart or respiratory failure). The participants were recruited from the Radiation Oncology Centre. Potential participants were referred to the researchers by physicians or nurses, and eligibility was determined by the research team.

Data collection

The semistructured interviews were conducted using an interview outline. Luo Y and her research team, who primarily research supportive care, initially drafted the interview outline through a literature review and group discussions. The interview outline was revised on the basis of pre-interviews with two participants. The outlines included open-ended questions designed to explore participants’ suffering, changes in their experiences over time and the consequences of their experiences (box 1). All of the researchers received specific training in conducting qualitative research. The first author, who is an experienced female nurse and a trained qualitative researcher, conducted all of the interviews in a separate, quiet lounge. The interviewer established good rapport with the participants by thoroughly explaining the research prior to the interviews. The interviews were audio-recorded with the participants’ consent. An open stance that involves being observant, attentive and sensitive to the expression of experiences was maintained throughout, and field notes were taken to document non-verbal expressions during the interviews.

When the sample reached data saturation, that is, when no additional themes emerged from the analysis of subsequent transcripts, we stopped recruiting.18 Data saturation was achieved after 18 interviews, but two additional interviews were conducted to ensure that no new themes emerged. A total of 22 potential participants were approached, and two declined due to oral pain caused by radiotherapy-induced oral mucositis. The final analysis included 20 participants with no repeated interviews. The interviews lasted between 29 and 99 min, with a mean of 40 min, and each participant was interviewed once.

Data analysis

The audiorecordings were transcribed verbatim into text within 24 hours of each interview, and the field notes were added to the transcripts by the interviewer. Data analysis began with the first interview and was conducted in conjunction with the data collection. Researchers should set aside their own experiences and assumptions, maintain a critical stance and reflect on their understanding of the data and the phenomenon. This bracketing of presuppositions can ensure analysis is grounded in participants’ subjective experiences, safeguarding the authenticity and integrity of the investigated phenomenon. The Colaizzi seven-step method19 was used for the data analysis: (1) becoming familiar with the text materials, (2) identifying and extracting significant statements, (3) formulating meanings for viewpoints that repeatedly appear while systematically bracketing one’s presuppositions related to the phenomenon, (4) summarising encoded viewpoints to cluster themes, (5) developing an exhaustive description of the phenomenon, (6) identifying similar viewpoints and extracting themes and (7) returning the data to the participants for validation and modification. The data were analysed in Chinese. The first and second authors conducted the data analysis process independently, while the corresponding author supervised the process. All authors participated in in-depth discussions until a consensus was reached. The translation work commenced alongside summarising the results and drafting the manuscript. Two researchers translated the themes and participant quotes into English. The translated draft was subsequently sent to nursing and English professors for review, and the researchers revised and finalised the data based on their feedback.

Ethical review

We used numbers (P1–P20) instead of names to maintain anonymity and confidentiality, and relevant identifying information was removed.

Trustworthiness

This study adhered to Lincoln and Guba’s principles for qualitative research: credibility, confirmability, dependability and transferability.20 Credibility was ensured through rigorous methods, namely, independent analysis and coding by two researchers, verification by a third researcher, final confirmation during a research team meeting and data checking with the participants. Confirmability was achieved by maintaining self-reflection in the process of collecting, analysing and interpreting the data. Dependability was upheld through two strategies: first, the researchers maintained an open-minded approach and avoided being influenced by pre-existing assumptions; second, an experienced qualitative research expert served as an external supervisor monitoring the conduct of the study. Transferability was demonstrated through the detailed descriptions of participants and research results so that other researchers could apply the findings to similar populations.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Themes and subthemes

An outline of the themes and subthemes is presented in figure 1. Three themes emerged from the interviews: (1) beginning of suffering: plunged into a world of illness; (2) struggle for adjustment: a rollercoaster experience and (3) beyond suffering: living with illness. The online supplemental material provides selected quotes for each of the themes and subthemes.

Outline of the themes and subthemes.

Theme 1. Beginning of suffering: plunged into a world of illness

Subtheme 1: denial and fear

When the participants were asked about their emotional responses to their cancer diagnosis, they predominantly reported denial (N=13; 65%) and fear (N=11; 55%). Denial was described as feelings of shock and doubt about a cancer diagnosis and led to repeated healthcare-seeking behaviour among some participants. Fear was described as a fear of treatment and death, with some participants crying (N=5; 25%).

Subtheme 2: significant concern

The participants indicated that they had to come to terms with their cancer diagnosis over time. Many participants expressed their concerns, which stemmed from various aspects, including worries about the treatment effect (N = 17; 85%), their children (N = 11; 55%), treatment expenses (N = 9; 45%), their parents (N = 7; 35%), their work (N = 6; 30%), fear of recurrence and their fertility (N = 4; 20%). One participant received fertility counselling.

Subtheme 3: life in chaos

The participants (N = 16; 80%) emphasised that the diagnosis and treatment of cancer profoundly affected the patients’ daily lives, leading to a complete disruption of their existential order. This situation not only presented challenges to the participants’ personal identity, family responsibilities and social roles but also had an impact on the life of the whole family.

Theme 2. Struggle for adjustment: a rollercoaster experience

Subtheme 1: suffering trajectory

When the participants were asked how their suffering changed over time, they emphasised that their experience process was dynamic and involved continuous adjustment. Some participants reported two emotional lows: one at the time of diagnosis (N=15; 75%) and another during periods of severe symptom distress (N=9; 45%). Conversely, some participants (N=8; 40%) experienced hope regarding the high 5-year survival rate. Some participants (N=10; 50%) described a sense of relief upon being discharged from the hospital.

Subtheme 2: exacerbating factors

When the participants were asked how their suffering was exacerbated, they reported several exacerbating factors. These included symptom burden, lack of information and the labelling of NPC as a happy cancer in China. Some participants (N=11; 55%) emphasised that oral symptoms in particular caused them significant distress. This distress was not confined to the physiological level but also manifested at the psychological and social levels, and it may have had a lasting impact on the patients’ lives. Two participants (N=2; 10%) reported long-term oral side effects. Some participants (N=7; 35%) reported feelings of helplessness and panic due to a lack of information regarding NPC and radiotherapy. Some participants (N=3; 15%) reported that when medical professionals and family members emphasised relatively high survival rates, the participants felt that the actual suffering and challenges they experienced were disregarded.

Subtheme 3: alleviating factors

The participants discussed the factors that contributed to alleviating suffering. The main factors reported were the individuals’ ability to adjust and the support from family and peers. The participants discussed that the ability to adjust helped them to maintain a positive mindset. Almost all the participants (N=19; 95%) shared how their family members supported them. This family support played a crucial role in psychological recovery. Some participants (N=8; 40%) discussed receiving informational and emotional support from their peers. One participant emphasised the support they received from friends.

Theme 3. Beyond suffering: living with illness

Subtheme 1: perceived benefits

The participants reported that their suffering experience was not entirely negative; it also had some positive aspects. The majority of the participants (N=17; 85%) recognised the importance of health and developed good health habits. Some participants (N=13; 65%) reported an improvement in their adaptability. Some participants (N=7; 35%) became aware of the importance of self-worth and self-care.

Subtheme 2: new normal in life

The participants (N=11; 55%) indicated that they had formed a ‘new normal’ in their lives by adjusting their lifestyles, daily activities and psychological states. They explained that their quality of life could not be completely restored to the premorbid state; however, they were able to adjust to the alterations in their daily lives resulting from the illness.

Subtheme 3: personal growth

The participants (N=15; 75%) reported experiencing personal growth. Some participants (N=10; 50%) emphasised positive transformations in their interpersonal relationships. They recounted (N=7; 35%) receiving unexpected levels of help, understanding and support from family, friends and colleagues, which made them more willing to help others.

Discussion

This study explored the experiences of patients with NPC in China. We found that these patients experienced multiple types of suffering. Despite their experiences aligning with the conceptualisation of suffering in cancer,5 this study identified several underexplored dimensions. By conducting a qualitative investigation into the trajectory of suffering and its influencing factors among NPC patients, we propose the implementation of personalised supportive care to enhance patients’ experiences. Such care includes the development of culturally sensitive assessment tools, radiotherapy information support and peer navigator programmes. These measures aim to provide more precise and effective support for patients with NPC.

Patients with NPC experience multiple types of suffering. Consistent with previous findings,14 21 our participants reported that oral side effects are the greatest challenge for patients and can continue to affect them long after radiotherapy, severely impacting their quality of life. However, oral side effects extend beyond physical suffering. The participants reported that the oral side effects reduced their social engagement, triggered negative emotions and ultimately caused psychological distress and impaired social functioning. This phenomenon has been reported in patients with head and neck cancer.22 Moreover, Chinese culture emphasises family-centred collectivism, which leads to patients’ suffering not being confined to the individual level but also manifesting as concerns for their family. As reported in studies on middle-aged and young cancer patients, 23 our participants described worries stemming from fulfilling familial roles, fertility plans and economic situations, which intertwined and significantly affected the patients’ normal lives. Notably, China’s unique collectivist culture shapes patients’ emotional inhibition behaviour. Some participants restrained their suffering and were reluctant to disclose their illness to their family members to maintain collective harmony. They prioritised their family members’ feelings over their own.24 This behaviour may reduce family conflicts in the short term, but it can prevent the effective release of psychological stress and consequently exacerbate psychological distress. This study highlights the complexity of the experience of NPC within the Chinese cultural context. Culturally sensitive assessment instruments must be developed to evaluate patients’ suffering.

This study revealed that patients with NPC experience two significant emotional lows. The most challenging periods occur at the time of diagnosis and during episodes of severe symptom distress, during which patients generally lack relevant knowledge about radiotherapy and disease management. Some patients expressed a sense of uncertainty and anxiety due to a lack of understanding of radiotherapy. Providing pretreatment education and continual support throughout treatment can alleviate psychological distress.25 However, some healthcare professionals overemphasise the ‘happy cancer’ label (a term derived from its relatively high 5-year survival rate) when communicating disease-related information about NPC. Although such optimistic information may temporarily alleviate patients’ distress, importantly, a relatively high survival rate cannot fully reflect the comprehensive impact of a cancer diagnosis.26 When participants experienced complications or recurrences, optimistic messages were largely perceived negatively by them. Some patients also reported that their relatively high survival rates led to their feelings being neglected by family members and healthcare professionals. These findings are consistent with those of studies on thyroid cancer,26 27 which have reported that relatively high survival rates may lead patients to experience complex emotional responses. Therefore, when communicating with patients, healthcare professionals should comprehensively explain the potential complications, recurrence risks and challenges that may be encountered during treatment. Additionally, a personalised treatment plan should be formulated that considers the patient’s physical condition, psychological status and personal preferences to improve the patient’s acceptance of treatment and its effectiveness.

The study revealed that the suffering trajectory of patients with NPC is not straightforward and is described as a ‘rollercoaster’, with repeated oscillations between ‘disruption’ and ‘reinvention’. This phenomenon is consistent with the Dual Process Model proposed by Stroebe and Schut, which notes that when people face traumatic events, they will oscillate between loss-oriented and restoration-oriented coping.28 In our study, the disruption phase refers to the state in which patients are plunged into a ‘world of illness’, characterised by persistent suffering manifested as denial, fear, significant concern and a sense of life in chaos—consistent with loss-oriented coping. The reinvention phase reflects the state in which patients adapt to living with illness, focusing on perceived benefits, a new normal in life and personal growth—aligning with restoration-oriented coping. Furthermore, we found that most participants perceived family support as a key factor influencing the oscillation between these two phases. A study revealed that among people with cancer and their caregivers, four in five identified their family and friends as key sources of emotional support.29 Therefore, social support can be strengthened based on the patients’ different phases. During the disruption phase, family members need guidance to master emotional communication skills and require assistance in providing daily care to alleviate patients’ suffering experiences. When patients enter the reinvention phase, they should be assisted in developing practical life plans to promote social functioning recovery, including acquiring new skills, building new social relationships and resuming daily routines.

The theme of ‘Beyond suffering: living with illness’ emerged as a consequence of suffering, revealing three inter-related dimensions: perceived benefits, a new normal in life and personal growth. These findings show that the time required for patients to transcend their suffering varies. This variability emphasises the need for targeted strategies to help patients move beyond suffering more swiftly. Consistent with previous findings,14 patients with NPC undergo positive changes in the process of suffering. The participants reported positive changes related to health behaviours and personal strength, and these changes are consistent with the outcomes of post-traumatic growth after cancer.30 In the current study, the participants emphasised that they recognised NPC as a part of life and actively constructed a new normal life despite illness, which is similar to findings in the literature.31 32 A significant finding is that patients’ interpersonal relationships improved following their suffering. Much of the literature has focused on gratitude for relationships.33 Our research revealed that some patients exhibit a notable shift from being recipients of help to becoming helpers, indicating that NPC survivors want to assist others. A systematic review demonstrated that patient navigation improves quality of life and patient satisfaction with care in the survivorship phase and reduces hospital readmission in the active treatment and survivorship care phases.34 On the basis of these findings, we can train NPC survivors to become peer navigators and to offer informational, emotional and practical support to new patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although we accounted for diversity in sex, age and educational level as much as possible when selecting the participants and aimed to achieve data saturation, the participants were recruited from one hospital in Chongqing. Owing to the significant geographical trend in the incidence of NPC, Chongqing is not a high-risk area in China. Therefore, to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the experiences of NPC in China, future studies should explore other cities with higher incidence rates (such as Guangzhou and Guangxi). Second, because some of the patients’ experiences in this study were collected through recall, we cannot exclude the influence of oversight and recall bias on the research results. To reduce this bias, future studies could adopt repeated interviews method to further explore the true experiences of patients at different treatment stages. Repeated interviews can not only help verify patients’ statements during the initial interview but also potentially reveal new understandings or changes in patients’ treatment experiences over time. Third, all study participants were from China, and some nuances may have been missed in the translations between Chinese and English. Finally, patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research, which may have reduced alignment between the findings and clinical needs. Future research should invite patient representatives and the public to participate from the initial stage of protocol design to enhance the study’s practical and translational value.

Conclusions

This qualitative research enhances healthcare providers’ comprehension of the suffering of patients with NPC. We found that the process of suffering is complex and dynamic. People diagnosed with NPC frequently experience multiple types of suffering, leading to disruption of their lives. Fortunately, most of these patients can overcome their suffering and achieve reinvention through perceived benefits, a new normal in life and personal growth. Therefore, nurses can proactively plan interventions on the basis of potential suffering trajectories, help patients receive support from their families and peers and help them effectively use their existing resources to facilitate the transition from disruption to reinvention. However, the evidence regarding interventions to alleviate the suffering of patients with NPC is inadequate, and issues such as culturally sensitive assessment tools, radiotherapy information support and peer navigator programmes still receive insufficient attention. Further research is needed to address these gaps to alleviate suffering. Furthermore, patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. In future research, we will make a point to invite patients and members of the public to participate from the initial stage of protocol design to further enhance the value of the research.
