Near-infrared circularly polarized electroluminescence from axially chiral diplatinum(II) phosphors
Authors: Jian-Cheng Chen, Yuan-Yuan Zhao, Zhong-Liang Gong, Zhong-Qiu Li, Qingda Chang, Jiang-Yang Shao, Chuang Zhang, Jiannian Yao, Yu-Wu Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNear-infrared (NIR) organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) that exhibit efficient circularly polarized electroluminescence (CPEL) are highly promising for applications in medical imaging/therapy, night-vision and wearable optoelectronics. However, their current performance is limited by two major challenges: poor device performance metrics, such as low external quantum efficiency (EQE) and maximum radiance (Rmax), and a low CPEL dissymmetry factor (gEL). These limitations are especially severe for devices operating beyond 750 nm, due to the lack of efficient chiral NIR emitters, the constraints of the energy gap law, and the inherent trade-off between EQE and gEL. Herein, we report highly efficient NIR circularly polarized phosphorescent OLEDs (CP-PHOLEDs) by utilizing a pair of diplatinum(II) phosphors with an axially chiral bridging ligand. The introduction of the axial chirality leads to dramatic CPEL enhancement and emission redshift compared to another pair of model complexes with a centrally chiral bridging ligand. The solution-processed devices exhibit NIR CPEL at 780 nm with maximum EQE (EQEmax) of 8.46% and gEL of +0.114/−0.111. Furthermore, by using a thermally activated delayed fluorescence-sensitized phosphorescence strategy, an improved EQEmax of 11.38%, a high Rmax of 13943 mW sr−1 m−2, and comparable gEL approaching 0.1 ( + 0.097/−0.083) at 760 nm have been realized. In addition, these complexes perform equally well on flexible substrates, and a NIR CP-PHOLED skin patch has been demonstrated. These results establish the most efficient NIR CP-OLEDs reported to date, achieving state-of-the-art overall device performance and chiroptical properties.Subject terms: Photochemistry, Inorganic chemistry

Introduction

Organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) operating in the near-infrared (NIR) region are applicable in numerous fields including encrypted displays1, night vision2, sensing3 and medicine4–6. NIR light can penetrate tissue deeply due to it minimal scattering and absorption effect relative to other wavelengths and is thus known as the biological window7. In particular, NIR OLEDs with intrinsic flexibility enable the creation of wearable systems for non-invasive diagnosis and portable devices for phototherapy8–10. Furthermore, NIR OLEDs that emit circularly polarized electroluminescence (CPEL) can leverage their unique chiroptical properties. This not only expands their application potential, for instance in the enhanced imaging of chiral biomolecules, but may also improve the signal-to-noise ratio and imaging contrast11,12.

To date, a large number of circularly polarized OLEDs (CP-OLEDs) have been reported13–20. However, NIR CP-OLED technology is still in its early stages of development due to a number of technical obstacles. The most critical among these is the shortage of chiral NIR emitters that simultaneously exhibit high emissivity and strong chiroptical properties, particularly at wavelengths beyond 750 nm21,22. A further serious obstacle is the inherent trade-off between external quantum efficiency (EQE) and the CPEL dissymmetry factor (gEL), dictated by the quantum mechanical limits on the electric (μ) and magnetic (m) transition dipole moments. This trade-off makes it fundamentally challenging to achieve high EQE and large gEL simultaneously in a single device13,23,24. In addition, the energy gap law intensifies the non-radiative losses of excitons in the NIR region, further lowering the device efficiency25,26. As a result, the few reported NIR CP-OLEDs show relatively poor performance with EQE typically below 3% and gEL not exceeding 10−227–32. Some recently reported devices using chiral perovskites achieve higher efficiency, yet their gEL still remain on the order of 10−2 beyond 730 nm (Fig. 1b)33. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, no flexible NIR CP-OLEDs have been documented so far.

Among known NIR emitters25,34,35, platinum(II) complexes are advantageous for their high phosphorescence quantum yields (Φ) and an intrinsic capability to form a metal-metal-to-ligand charge transfer (3MMLCT) emission mode through Pt-Pt interaction. This 3MMLCT state effectively lowers the emission energy and readily shifts the emission into the NIR region. Consequently, Pt(II) complexes have become a leading candidate for next-generation NIR emitters, particularly in the fabrication of NIR phosphorescent OLEDs (PHOLEDs)36–42. In addition, the planar molecular structures of Pt(II) complexes facilitate chiral self-assembly and signal amplification in supramolecular systems, leading to efficient circularly polarized luminescence (CPL)43–48.

Herein, we present a pair of axially chiral dinuclear platinum(II) phosphors, R/S-Pt2, for achieving the state-of-the-art NIR CPEL in both conventional and flexible CP-PHOLEDs (Fig. 1, Supplementary Tables 3 and 4 and Supplementary Fig. 1). The spin-coated films of these compounds show efficient NIR emissions at 780 nm with a sub-microsecond lifetime of 299 ns, as well as a high CPL dissymmetry factor (|glum|) of 0.083. The solution-processed PHOLEDs based on monocomponent R/S-Pt2 exhibit NIR CPEL at 780 nm (λEL) with an EQEmax of 8.46% and remarkable gEL of up to +0.114/−0.111. In comparison, another pair of U-shaped di-Pt(II) complexes, RR/SS-Pt1 bridged by a bis-isonitrile ligand with carbon-centered chirality, show much poorer CPEL performance with gEL of ±0.020 and relatively blue-shifted emission maximum at 690 nm (Fig. 1c)49. Thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF)-sensitized NIR CP-PHOLEDs based on R/S-Pt2 have also been fabricated50, yielding distinctly improved EQEmax of 11.38% and maximum radiance (Rmax) of 13,943 mW sr−1 m−2 at 760 nm. These enhanced device efficiencies are achieved while retaining large and comparable gEL factors approaching 0.1 (+0.097/−0.083). With increasing concentration of TADF sensitizer in the TADF-sensitized devices, a maximum EQE of 13.48% with Rmax of 22,907 mW sr−1 m−2 at 758 nm have been achieved. Furthermore, flexible NIR CP-PHOLEDs are fabricated using R/S-Pt2 on polyimide (PI) substrates, exhibiting device efficiency and CPEL performance comparable to those of conventional devices on glass. These flexible devices demonstrate great potential for wearable NIR optoelectronics, as evidenced by the successful fabrication of large-area (4 cm2 and 9 cm2) NIR CP-PHOLED skin patches.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization

Complexes RR/SS-Pt1 and R/S-Pt2 were synthesized from the reactions of a platinum chloride precursor with the corresponding chiral diisocyano ligands (R,R)/(S,S)−1,2-diisocyano-1,2-diphenylethane and (R)/(S)−2,2’-diisocyano-1,1’-binaphthalene, respectively. The synthetic routes and characterization data using 1H-NMR, 13C-NMR, elemental analysis and high-resolution mass spectral analysis are presented in Methods and Supplementary Information. In these molecules, each Pt atom is chelated with a tridentate N^C^N-type cyclometalating ligand, and is connected to the chiral bridging ligand via Pt-isocyano coordination to form a U-shaped configuration. Incorporating n-octyl chains into these molecules improves their solubility (~50 mg/mL for RR/SS-Pt1 and >150 mg/mL for R/S-Pt2, in CH3CN), thereby enabling the preparation of high-quality thin films through spin-coating. The solutions and thin films of RR/SS-Pt1 show bright red luminescence (Fig. 1d), while those of R/S-Pt2 display intense NIR emission, which is barely visible to the naked eye (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 2).

As shown in Fig. 2a, the spin-coated film of R-Pt2 displays intense visible absorption in the range of 400 and 650 nm, while that of RR-Pt1 absorbs primarily in the shorter wavelength range (400–500 nm). These absorptions are mainly attributed to the 1MMLCT transitions51–56. The photoluminescence (PL) spectra of these films exhibit unstructured emission peaked at 690 nm with Φ of 58% and lifetime (τ) of 839 ns for RR-Pt1 and at 780 nm with Φ of 35% and τ of 299 ns for R-Pt2, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 1). The shape and wavelength of the PL emissions of these films remain largely invariant with increasing film thickness (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5). The photophysical properties of RR-Pt1 and R-Pt2 in the solution state were also investigated (Supplementary Figs. 3, 6, 7, and Supplementary Table 1), which suggest that the different isocyanide ligands in RR-Pt1 and R-Pt2 have little effect on the absorption and emission properties of their dilute solutions. However, they influence significantly the aggregation behavior and thus modulate the absorption and emission spectra of their aggregated states and thin films. Due to the intense visible absorption and high NIR Φ, R/S-Pt2 films can be excited by common indoor light, producing bright emissions detectable with a NIR camera (Fig. 2b). Upon cooling from 320 K to 80 K, the PL peaks undergo a distinct red shift from 680 nm to 705 nm for RR-Pt1 and 780 nm to 825 nm for R-Pt2, respectively (Fig. 2c). This red-shift is caused by a contraction of the Pt-Pt distance at lower temperatures57.

The chiroptical properties of RR/SS-Pt1 and R/S-Pt2 films were investigated by circular dichroism (CD) and CPL spectral analyses. Intense mirror-symmetric CD signals are observed with the maximum absorption dissymmetry factor (gabs) of −0.426/+0.417 at 400 nm for RR/SS-Pt1 and −0.409/+0.381 at 510 nm for R/S-Pt2, respectively (Fig. 2d). These CD signals are related to their 1MMLCT absorptions. As the film thickness increases from 100 nm to 200 nm, their gabs values progressively increase until they reach saturation (Supplementary Fig. 8). A similar trend is observed for their glum values as a function of film thickness (Supplementary Fig. 9). It should be noted that an annealing process and the annealing temperature (>100 °C) are important in achieving the high glum values of these films (Supplementary Fig. 10). In addition, the CD and CPL spectra of Pt1 and Pt2 in dilute CH3CN were measured. At a concentration of 1 × 10−5 M, the maximum ∣gabs∣ values of R/S-Pt2 are around 0.001, which is much higher than those of RR/SS-Pt1 (Supplementary Fig. 11). In dilute solutions, all of these complexes are essentially CPL-inactive (Supplementary Fig. 12).

It should be noted that, though RR/SS-Pt1 and R/S-Pt2 films display comparable maximum |gabs| values, their CPL magnitudes differ significantly. RR/SS-Pt1 films show relatively weak CPL signals with |glum| of less than 0.01 (+0.009/−0.008) at 650 nm, while R/S-Pt2 films exhibit intense CPL signals with |glum| approaching 0.1 (−0.083/+0.081) at 780 nm. This implies that the use of the axially chiral bridging ligand, with respect to the centrally chiral analog, not only yields a significant red-shift in emission, but also a notable, nearly tenfold enhancement of the CPL signal. In addition, when these films are rotated or flipped, their CPL signals do not vary distinctly, suggesting that the interference from artifacts such as linear polarization and birefringence is insignificant (Supplementary Fig. 13)58. Furthermore, the spin-coated films of R/S-Pt2 on flexible PI substrates exhibit strong CPL signals similar to those on glass (Fig. 2f). This suggests that the chiral assembly and chiroptical properties of R/S-Pt2 are retained on the flexible PI substrate, a prerequisite for flexible CP-OLEDs.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) and atomic force microscope (AFM) analyses show that RR/SS-Pt1 and R/S-Pt2 films are characterized by smooth surfaces, with a root mean square roughness (Rq) of 0.57 nm for RR-Pt1 and 1.62 nm for R-Pt2, respectively (Fig. 2g, h and Supplementary Fig. 14). Grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) was further employed to probe their molecular packings on substrates. A dominant diffraction spot at Qz = −0.41 Å−1 is observed for R-Pt2 film, corresponding to an interplanar spacing of 15.324 Å (Fig. 2i). This spacing is consistent with the distance of 15.352 Å between adjacent Pt-Pt supramolecular chains as revealed in the following single crystal analysis (Supplementary Fig. 19). This suggests that R-Pt2 film has a distinct degree of crystallinity and its (100) plane aligns parallel with respect to the substrate. In addition, another weak diffraction of Qx = 1.85 Å−1 is observed, which is assigned to the (001) plane of the film and consistent with the average Pt-Pt distance of 3.39 Å. These GIWAXS patterns are suggestive of an edge-on packing mode of the Pt-Pt supramolecular chains of R-Pt2 on the substrate. In contrast, no obvious GIWAXS peaks are observed for RR-Pt1 film (Supplementary Fig. 14), suggestive of an amorphous molecular arrangement. This difference suggests that the ordered molecular packing of R-Pt2 film is critical for its large CPL amplification.

Single crystal X-ray diffraction analysis

Perhaps due to their good solubility, we failed to obtain the single crystals of RR/SS-Pt1 and R/S-Pt2. Alternatively, two model Pt(II) complexes bridged by the same chiral ligand yet without long alkyl chains, RR-Pt1-C0 and R-Pt2-C0, were synthesized and their single crystals were successfully obtained for X-ray diffraction analysis (See details in Methods and Supplementary Information). The rod-like single crystals of RR-Pt1-C0 with the centrally chiral bridging ligand show bright red emission with emission maximum wavelength (λemi,max) at 685 nm (Fig. 3a, c, and Supplementary Fig. 15)49. It possesses a triclinic crystallographic structure with a space group of P1. This di-Pt complex adopts a U-shaped molecular configuration with an intramolecular Pt-Pt distance of 3.684 Å and stacks into 1D supramolecular chains by strong intermolecular metal-metal interactions with a Pt-Pt distance of 3.227 Å (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Figs. 16, 17). Within each supramolecular chain, adjacent di-Pt complexes are stacked in a staggered orientation with a cross angle of around 128°.

The single crystals of R-Pt2-C0, with the axially chiral bridging ligand, appear as hexagonal prisms and show a dark purple color and NIR emission with λemi,max at 780 nm (Fig. 3b, d, and Supplementary Fig. 15). It crystallizes into a P3121 space group, belonging to the trigonal system with a threefold screw axis. R-Pt2-C0 possesses a similar U-shaped configuration to RR-Pt1-C0, but assembles into 1D helical supramolecular chains with a periodicity of six repeating molecules (Fig. 3f). In each helical turn, two molecules form a paired unit with a mutual 40° rotation. This paired unit then rotates by 120° relative to the next unit along the chain, resulting in a complete 360° rotation over the span of six molecules. Furthermore, R-Pt2-C0 exhibits both strong intramolecular (3.317 Å) and intermolecular (3.159 Å) Pt-Pt interactions (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Figs. 18, 19). These interactions are responsible for the observed decrease in the energy of the 3MMLCT transitions, leading to the red-shifted absorption and emission spectra of R/S-Pt2 films relative to RR/SS-Pt1 analogues. In addition, the axial chirality in the rigid binaphthyl units drives R-Pt2-C0 to adopt a distinct M-helical conformation (Fig. 3g), which is further strengthened by intramolecular Pt-Pt interactions59.

Therefore, the use of the twisted and rigid axially chiral bridging ligand drives R/S-Pt2 to adopt a tighter molecular packing with shorter intramolecular and intermolecular Pt-Pt distances than RR/SS-Pt1. As a result, R/S-Pt2 films exhibit a distinct emission spectral redshift with respect to RR/SS-Pt1 films. Meanwhile, the introduction of the axial chirality is thought to enhance chirality amplification and CPL performance through multiple effects. The formation of helical molecular configurations (Fig. 3e), the enhancement of the intrinsic molecular chirality (Supplementary Fig. 11), and the helical-prone packing modes (Fig. 3f) driven by the axially chiral bridging ligand, jointly lead to the significant amplification of the CPL signal and CPEL performance discussed below.

Solution-processed NIR CP-PHOLEDs and CPEL

Inspired by the high emissivity, superior chiroptical property and good solution processability, the solution-processed NIR CP-PHOLEDs based on pure R/S-Pt2 have been fabricated with a typical multilayer device structure of indium tin oxide (ITO)/poly(ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS, 40 nm)/poly(9-vinylcarbazole) (PVK, 40 nm)/R/S-Pt2 (50 nm)/2,4,6-tris(3-(diphenylphosphoryl)phenyl)−1,3,5-triazine (PO-T2T, 60 nm)/lithium fluoride (LiF, 1 nm)/Al (100 nm) (Fig. 4a). In this device structure, PEDOT:PSS, PVK, PO-T2T and LiF serve as the hole-injecting layer (HIL), hole-transporting layer (HTL), electron-transporting layer (ETL) and electron-injecting layer (EIL), respectively. The chemical structures of these materials are presented in Supplementary Fig. 20. The pure R/S-Pt2 films are used as the chiral emitting layer (C-EML).

As depicted in Fig. 4b, c, the optimized devices based on R-Pt2 on glass exhibit an EQEmax of 8.46% with a NIR λEL at 780 nm (about 50 nm thick of R-Pt2; see Supplementary Fig. 21; referred to as R-D1; D = device). The current density-voltage-radiance (J-V-R) characteristics as a function of voltage are illustrated in Fig. 4d. These devices feature a low turn-on voltage (Von) below 3.0 V (radiance of 5.7 mW sr−1 m−2 at 3.0 V) and Rmax of ~1000 mW sr−1 m−2 at 7 V. The EL spectra of these devices essentially remain stable throughout the operating voltage range (Supplementary Fig. 22a). These impressive device performances are suggestive of the well-matched energy levels among the different layers and the low barriers to carrier injection and transport.

The CPEL characteristics of the above NIR CP-PHOLEDs were subsequently investigated. As shown in Fig. 4e, strong CPEL signals in the NIR region were recorded from R/S-D1. The gEL values at the maximum EL peak of 780 nm are +0.114 and −0.111 for R-D1 and S-D1, respectively, which represent the best performance among all known NIR CP-OLEDs (Supplementary Table 3). Under high driving conditions (Supplementary Fig. 22b, c), the gEL of the device remains stable with only slight attenuation across its entire operating range. The gEL value at 9 V is +0.097, which retains 89.8% of the value at 4.5 V (+0.108). This demonstrates that although the absolute radiance of the monocomponent device is moderate, it maintains strong CPEL characteristics even at high output power.

CP-PHOLEDs based on RR/SS-Pt1 have also been fabricated using the above conditions. Corresponding devices exhibit maximum gEL of +0.021/−0.023 at 685 nm (Supplementary Fig. 23). This performance is much poorer with respect to those based on R/S-Pt2, suggestive of the beneficial role of the axial chirality of Pt2 in CPL and CPEL amplification. In addition to chiral ligands60, the substituent alkyl chains may play an important role in achieving well-balanced device performance, as they influence both device performance and gEL values61,62. Further optimization of these chains will be pursued in the future.

Inspired by the excellent CPEL performance of R/S-D1 and the retention of the high film quality and chiroptical properties of R/S-Pt2 on PI, flexible NIR CP-PHOLEDs have been explored with these complexes on PI substrate with an ITO etching layer (abbreviated as R/S-D2). Corresponding device efficiency and CPEL properties are displayed in Fig. 4b–d, f. Compared to R/S-D1 on glass, these flexible R/S-D2 exhibit similar EL at 780 nm with comparable device efficiency of Von below 3.0 V (radiance of 8.8 mW sr−1 m−2 at 3.0 V), EQEmax of 7.46%, Rmax of 537 mW sr−1 m−2 at 9 V, and bright CPEL with gEL of +0.103/−0.096.

To demonstrate their potential for wearable NIR circularly polarized optoelectronics, we fabricated large-area, flexible NIR CP-PHOLEDs with R/S-Pt2 on PI (R/S-D3: 4 cm2, Figs. 4g, h; R/S-D4: 9 cm2, Fig. 4i, j). The excellent spin-coating processability ensures the large-area fabrication onto flexible substrates. In particular, a prototype wearable NIR CP-PHOLED patch has been demonstrated with R-D4 with a polyurethane (PU) dressing on a human arm, proving the feasibility of such a system as a portable NIR CPEL driver.

Solution-processed TADF-sensitized NIR CP-PHOLEDs and CPEL

The above devices with R/S-Pt2, including both conventional and flexible devices, demonstrate excellent NIR CPEL properties and good device performance. One shortcoming of these monocomponent devices is that they exhibit severe efficiency roll-off, with only 12% of EQEmax being retained (from 8.48% to 0.98%) at 100 mW sr−1 m−2 (Fig. 4b). This drawback is likely caused by molecular skeleton’s inherent flexibility and the non-radiative loss of excitons following the energy gap law.

To mitigate the efficiency roll-off, we constructed solution-processed TADF-sensitized phosphorescence (TsP) NIR CP-PHOLEDs, using the TADF emitter 3,4,5,6-tetrakis(3,6-diphenylcarbazol-9-yl)−1,2-dicyanobenzene (4CzPN-Ph, Fig. 5b) as a sensitizer63. The spin-coated film of 4CzPN-Ph emits orange light at 595 nm, which matches well with the maximum absorption band of R/S-Pt2 (Supplementary Fig. 24). This spectral overlap facilitates efficient Förster resonance energy transfer (FET) from the TADF sensitizer to the Pt(II) emitter64,65. In addition, the phosphorescent Pt(II) emitter may harvest excitons via potential Dexter energy transfer (DET), thereby establishing an efficient sensitization strategy (Fig. 5c)66. In order to get further insight into the energy transfer process between the TADF sensitizer and the Pt complex, time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements were performed on the emission of 4CzPN-Ph at 595 nm in the binary 4CzPN-Ph:R-Pt2 films with 4CzPN-Ph as the donor and R-Pt2 as the acceptor (Supplementary Fig. 25). With increasing content of R-Pt2 from 10 wt.% to 60 wt.% (total mass concentration: 10 mg/mL), the PL of 4CzPN-Ph at 595 nm is gradually quenched (Supplementary Fig. 25a), which is accompanied by the appearance of the NIR emission of R-Pt2 from 600 to 900 nm. In addition, both the prompt and delayed lifetimes of the emission of 4CzPN-Ph in these binary films are significantly shortened with increasing content of R-Pt2 (Supplementary Fig. 25b, and Supplementary Table 5), indicating an efficient energy transfer process.

When the concentration of the sensitizer 4CzPN-Ph is increased from 3 mg/mL to 7 mg/mL, the EQEmax increases from 9.45% to 13.48%, and Rmax increases from 9024 to 22907 mW sr−1 m−2 (~20 times of enhancement compared with monocomponent devices), which also ranks among the forefront level in non-chiral NIR OLEDs (Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Fig. 1). These TsP devices exhibit NIR ELs at 758–765 nm, which are slightly blue-shifted (15–22 nm) with respect to those of non-sensitized analogues (Fig. 5d, f). No EL of 4CzPN-Ph (λEL = 600 nm, Supplementary Fig. 27) is observed in the TsP devices throughout the entire driving process (Supplementary Fig. 22d), indicative of the efficient sensitization effect. Furthermore, compared to the devices with monocomponent C-EML, the efficiency roll-off of TsP CP-PHOLEDs has been significantly mitigated. For instance, for the device prepared with a sensitizer concentration of 4 mg/mL, around 87% and 54% of EQEmax are retained at 100 and 1000 mW sr−1 m−2, respectively (EQEmax decreases from 11.38% to 9.82% and 6.11%, respectively; Fig. 5d).

In addition to the improved device performance, the above TsP CP-PHOLEDs display strong CPELs in the NIR region. When a sensitizer concentration of 3–4 mg/mL is used, the |gEL| value can still approach 0.1 (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 28). As the applied voltage increases, the CPEL signals in the TsP device remain largely unchanged during operation (Supplementary Fig. 22e, f), with a gEL value of +0.093 at 11 V, which is 92.1% of the value at 5.0 V (+0.101). In addition, angle-dependent CPEL measurements confirm that the emissions from all devices originate from intrinsic CPEL signals (Supplementary Fig. 29). The |gEL| values are highly dependent on the sensitizer concentration. With increasing concentration of the sensitizer, the |gEL| value decreases significantly. In particular, the |gEL| value drops dramatically to 0.005 when 10 mg/mL of the sensitizer is employed. This suggests that increasing concentrations of the TADF sensitizer severely disrupt the chiral assembly of the Pt(II) emitters. In short, the TsP CP-PHOLEDs prepared with 4 mg/mL of 4CzPN-Ph (abbreviated as R/S-D5) show a balanced optimal device and CPEL performance with EQEmax of 11.38%, Rmax of 13943 mW sr−1 m−2 and gEL of +0.097/−0.083 with R/S-Pt2 (Fig. 5d–g). These results demonstrate the feasibility of the TsP strategy in enhancing the overall device performance while preserving the excellent chiroptical properties.

Encouraged by the above results, flexible TsP NIR CP-PHOLEDs have been explored on PI using a similar device structure as R/S-D5. These flexible TsP devices are abbreviated as R/S-D6. The excellent device efficiency and CPEL properties of R/S-D5 on glass are essentially retained in flexible R/S-D6 on PI, exhibiting EL at 760 nm, Von of around 3.5 V (radiance of 2.8 mW sr−1 m−2 at 3.5 V), EQEmax of 10.28%, Rmax of 13003 mW sr−1 m−2, and gEL of +0.093/−0.080 (Fig. 5d–g). Critically, the flexible TsP devices possess good mechanical bending fatigue resistance, ensuring the stability of both device performance and CPEL properties (Fig. 5h, i). After bending 60 times, 85% (R-Pt2) and 87% (S-Pt2) of their original gEL values are retained, suggestive of the robustness of the chiral co-assembly of R/S-Pt2 with the TADF sensitizer.

To get further insight of the TADF sensitization effect and its impact on chiroptical signals, monocomponent R/S-Pt2 (30 mg/mL) films and films doped with 3, 5, 7, and 10 mg/mL of 4CzPN-Ph (R/S-film 0, TsP R/S-films 3, 5, 7, 10 for simplicity) were prepared and used for the chiroptical and structural characterizations. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 30, the CD spectra of the TsP R/S-films exhibit a similar trend to the CPEL signals of the TsP devices, with signals gradually weakening as the TADF doping concentration increases. Supplementary Fig. 31 shows the PL spectra of these TsP R-films doped with different amounts of TADF sensitizer. In these films with a relatively low content of dopant, no TADF luminescence is evident and only the emission of Pt2 is observed. With increasing doping concentration of 4CzPN-Ph, the PL intensity of these films increases distinctly accompanied by a slight blue-shift of the emission wavelength. This trend is consistent with that observed for the EL of the corresponding devices (Supplementary Fig. 26c). Furthermore, GIWAXS images of these doped films (Supplementary Fig. 32) show a gradually broadened and weakened orientation diffraction peak of R-Pt2 at Qz = −0.41 Å−1 and Qx = 1.85 Å−1 with increasing doping concentration, in contrast to a featureless, concentric circular diffraction peak observed for the TADF-sensitizer at Qz = −1.19 Å−1. This indicates that the addition of the TADF dopant gradually disrupts the chiral-assembled ordered structure.

We then analyzed the Abs, PL, CD, CPEL, as well as the device’s EQE and radiance as a function of the TADF-sensitizer doping concentration. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 34, two opposite trends are observed in the TsP system. Properties related to the luminescence performance (Abs, PL, EQE and radiance) all improved progressively with increasing TADF sensitizer doping concentration due to the sensitization effect (The decrease in EQE and radiance at 4CzPN-Ph 10 mg/mL may be attributed to pin-holes caused by the solubility limitation of 4CzPN-Ph in AcN/THF, as shown in the AFM images in Supplementary Fig. 33). Meanwhile, the increasing doping concentration also negatively affects the chiroptical properties (gabs and gEL) by gradually disrupting the chiral assembly structure. Despite this adverse effect, the excellent sensitization effect enables a good balance at low doping concentrations (3–5 mg/mL)—the range used in the TsP devices—leading to well-balanced comprehensive performance and partially alleviating the inherent trade-off between EQE and gEL.

In brief, through introducing an axially chiral bridging ligand, we present a pair of chiral di-Pt(II) complexes, R/S-Pt2, to achieve prominent NIR CPEL with very high radiance and remarkable gEL. These complexes show efficient NIR emission at 780 nm with a short lifetime of 299 ns. The introduction of the axial chirality significantly amplifies the chiroptical properties of the spin-coated films of R/S-Pt2, yielding NIR CPL with |glum| of around 0.08. This performance is around one order of magnitude higher than that of the model complexes RR/SS-Pt1 with a centrally chiral bridging ligand. The axial chirality-regulated helical stacking of R/S-Pt2 molecules is believed to play a critical role in the amplification of the chiroptical properties. The solution-processed NIR CP-PHOLEDs based on monocomponent R/S-Pt2 exhibit bright NIR CPEL with gEL of +0.114/−0.111 at the electroluminescence peak of 780 nm and EQEmax of 8.46%. Furthermore, by using the TADF-sensitized phosphorescence strategy, solution-processed NIR CP-PHOLEDs have been constructed to achieve improved EQEmax of 11.38% with maximum radiance of 13943 mW sr−1 m−2 at 760 nm, while maintaining the remarkable gEL of around 0.1. Complexes R/S-Pt2 represent a promising candidate for chiral optoelectronics, which is also reflected by their equally effective in large-area and flexible devices. In particular, the flexible NIR CP-PHOLEDs on PI substrates exhibit comparable device performance and CPEL properties to those on glass, demonstrating their feasibility as wearable NIR CPEL drivers. This work shows an efficacious design concept for chiral NIR emitters and a promising device strategy for achieving ultrahigh performance in CP-PHOLEDs with balanced efficiency, brightness, stability, flexibility, and dissymmetry factor, paving the way toward practical applications of NIR emitters and CP-OLEDs.

Methods

Human participant statement

The human participant shown in Fig. 4i, j was included solely for a conceptual demonstration of the flexibility and conformability of the flexible NIR CP-PHOLED patch, and no further investigations were conducted. The demonstration was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Chemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences, and was determined to constitute a minimal-risk and non-invasive activity. In accordance with the institute’s ethical exemption policies for minimal-risk research, a formal waiver of ethical approval was granted to the experiment (Institutional reference: Institute of Chemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences, minimal-risk exemption policy).

Written informed consent was obtained from the participant prior to participation and for the publication of the images shown in Fig. 4i, j. One volunteer participated in this study (male, 28 years old). The sex and age information was recorded, but were not considered and used in the study. No physiological information, biological samples, or human participant data for experimental analysis were collected or analyzed other than the images shown in Fig. 4i, j.

Synthesis and material characterization

R/S-Pt2 were obtained by a reaction of the n-octyl-substituted Pt(dpb)Cl (dpb = 1,3-di(pyridin-2-yl)benzene) (200 mg, 0.348 mmol) with R/S−2,2’-diisocyano-1,1’-binaphthalene R/S-L2 (51 mg, 0.166 mmol). A CH2Cl2 (20 mL) solution of Pt(dpb)Cl and a CH3CN (20 mL) solution of R/S-L267 were mixed in a two-neck flask charged with KPF6 (305 mg, 1.66 mmol) and stirred overnight at room temperature. After the solvent was removed by vacuum evaporation, the products were filtered and washed using H2O, CH3OH, CH2Cl2 and Et2O, continuously. Following by the re-precipitation in CH3CN/Et2O, the final products R/S-Pt2 were obtained in the form of blue-black powders (235 mg, 85% yield for R-Pt2; 220 mg, 80% yield for S-Pt2). The synthesis routes of RR/SS-Pt1, RR/SS-Pt1-C0 and R/S-Pt2-C0 are detailed in Supplementary Information.

The characterizations of synthesis, photophysical properties, chiroptical measurements, thin-film stacking and crystal structure analysis are described in the Supplementary Information.

Preparation of single crystals

Single crystals of RR/SS-Pt1-C0 were grown from a concentrated solution (5 × 10−3 M in CH3CN) by slow evaporation. Single crystals of R/S-Pt2-C0 were obtained from a mixed solvent of CH3CN/CH2Cl2 (2 × 10−3 M, CH3CN/CH2Cl2 9/1, V/V).

Fabrication of rigid and flexible CP-PHOLED devices

The glass substrates with patterned indium-tin-oxide etching layer (ITO, thickness = 135 nm, 15 Ω sq−1) were cleaned by ultrasonic in deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol for 15 min, respectively. The PI substrates with ITO etching layer (ITO, thickness = 135 nm, 15 Ω sq−1) were cleaned by ultrasonic in deionized water and isopropanol for 5 min respectively. Both the two substrates were then treated with ultraviolet ozone for 20 min. PEDOT:PSS AI 4083 was spin-coated onto the ITO at 4000 rpm for 30 s and dried in an oven at 150 °C for 30 min. After transferred into a glove box (O2 < 1.00 ppm, H2O < 0.01 ppm), PVK was dissolved in chlorobenzene (5 mg/mL) and spin coated above at 2000 rpm for 30 s and then annealed at 100 °C for 20 min. The C-EML layer of the monocomponent devices was spin-coated onto the PVK layer using a CH3CN solution of RR/SS-Pt1 and R/S-Pt2 at 2000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 65 °C for 30 min. As for the C-EML layer of the TsP devices, 30 mg/mL of R/S-Pt2 and x mg/mL (x = 3–10) of 4CzPN-Ph were blended and dissolved using a mixed solvent of CH3CN and THF (4/1, V/V) before stirred for 10 min at 50 °C, and then the blends were spin-coated onto the PVK layer at 2000 rpm for 30 s and annealed at 65 °C for 30 min. After that, 50 nm of PO-T2T (0.5 Å/s), 1 nm of LiF (0.1 Å/s) and 100 nm of Al (0.1 Å/s for 5 nm and 1 Å/s for 95 nm) were deposited in turn by thermal vacuum evaporation at a base pressure below 5 × 10−5 Pa. The EL area was 0.06 cm2.

Fabrication of large-area flexible CP-PHOLED devices (4 cm2 and 9 cm2)

Owing to the excellent solubility and solution processing capability, R/S-Pt2 allows for the fabrication of high-quality large-area films via spin coating. The device fabrication procedures are identical to the fabrication process of normal devices introduced above. The wearable CP-PHOLED patches were covered by a polyurethane (PU) dressing, and the electrodes were welded and led out through enameled copper wire.

Characterization of CP-PHOLED devices

The external quantum efficiency (EQE)-current density characteristic and current density-voltage-radiance (J-V-R) were measured by Photo Research PR 750 with Keithley 2400 source meter. The EQE is defined as the ratio between the number of emitted photons and injected electrons. The CPEL spectra of the devices were measured by JASCO CPL-200 spectrometer. The rigid devices were encapsulated with optical adhesive and glass cover-plate, the flexible devices were encapsulated by Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) transparent tape. All device tests were conducted in ambient air.
