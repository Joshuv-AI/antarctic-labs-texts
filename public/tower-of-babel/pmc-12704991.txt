Factors associated with delay in initiating postexposure prophylaxis for rabies prevention in a district of Madhya Pradesh, India: 2023
Authors: Rajesh Kumar Gupta, Anurag Dhoundiyal, Sushma Choudhary, Ramesh Chandra, Laljoo Shakya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:India reports more than 9 million animal bite cases and 5000 deaths due to rabies every year, which postexposure prophylactic vaccines can prevent. Delayed vaccination can be fatal. We assessed the causes of the delay in initiating postexposure prophylaxis (PEP) in the Shivpuri district of Madhya Pradesh, India.Methodolgy:We defined delay as >24 h between the bite and initiation of PEP. We interviewed all animal bite cases reported in the outpatient or emergency departments at the District Hospital of Shivpuri in Madhya Pradesh for PEP from January to September 2023 using a structured questionnaire on the sociodemographic profile, nature, site, animal of the bite, and treatment time of the cases. In-depth interview of the cases who delayed the treatment about the factors responsible was done. Chi-square was applied as a significance test for factors associated with delay in treatment, and P < 0.05 was taken as significance.Results:A total of 422 animal-bite cases were interviewed; males were 289 (68%), rural residents were 293 (69%), and those aged 10–19 years were 100 (24%). Dogs caused 394 (93%) of bites, affecting lower limbs 220 (52%). Treatment delay occurred in 157 (37%) of cases. Factors were closed facilities due to holidays or delay in reaching, 56 (36%); work-related, 51 (32%); treatment unawareness, 38 (24%); lack of accompaniment, 22 (14%); and financial, 21 (13%). Delays were significantly higher among rural residents, those living >10 km from facilities, and cases with income <₹10,000/month.Conclusions:We recommend awareness campaigns in rural areas regarding rabies and extending timings of healthcare facilities.

Introduction

Rabies is a fatal disease caused by an RNA virus of the Rhabdoviridae family and mainly spreads through the bite of an infected domestic or wild animal; however, in most cases, it is due to dog bites.[123] When not treated, the disease is invariably fatal in humans and animals; thus, prevention is the only strategy available.[4] There are more than 50,000 deaths worldwide annually due to rabies and nearly 0.9 million Disability Adjusted Life Years. The annual global economic burden of the disease is around $8.6 billion.[5] The disease is a neglected zoonotic disease and, while mostly eradicated from developed nations, is still a public health problem in developing countries, especially in Asian and African counties.[6]

India is one of the highest-burden countries for rabies, contributing to nearly one-third of all global cases annually. There are around 5000 rabies deaths annually in India; the estimate of animal bites in India is over 9 million annually, with nearly three-fourths of them by dogs.[7] There has been a decline in the total number of rabies deaths in the country; this could be due to under-reporting as the disease is not notifiable in India, but the incidence of animal bites is still high, which makes it a significant public health problem in India.[789]

Rabies is preventable with the initiation of postexposure prophylaxis (PEP) vaccination immediately after the bite, which is provided based on the severity of the bite or contact with the animal. Category I represents a minimal risk, involving activities such as touching or feeding animals and animal licks on intact skin; this requires only wound washing and antiseptic application; category II poses a moderate risk, including nibbling of uncovered skin or minor scratches and abrasions that do not bleed; it requires PEP in the form of antirabies vaccine. The highest risk, Category III, encompasses transdermal bites or scratches, contamination of mucous membranes or broken skin with saliva, and any direct contact with bats. It requires PEP with rabies immunoglobulin and vaccine.[10]

Rabies vaccination is provided free of cost at all the government health facilities in the country, but deaths due to rabies are still an issue in India. The majority of rabies deaths in India are children, and in many cases, it is due to delay in treatment. An average delay of 2 to 3 days can prove fatal.[81112] These delays can be due to many factors like patient education and awareness, residence and distance from a health facility, or availability of vaccines at the facilities.[13141516]

Madhya Pradesh reports more than 20,000 cases of dog bites annually.[17] It is one of the states with a high number of stray dogs per 1000 population.[18] In the past few years, it has reported deaths due to rabies in animal bite cases. The delays in the vaccination could be one of the reasons for these deaths. We conducted a study in the Shivpuri district of Madhya Pradesh to estimate the proportion of delayed initiation of PEP in animal bite cases and to identify the possible factors responsible for it.

Methodology

We defined treatment delay as delay of more than 24 h between the animal bite and the initiation of PEP. We conducted a cross-sectional study at the District Hospital of Shivpuri in Madhya Pradesh, a tertiary care hospital that receives many animal bite cases/day from the whole district. We followed the complete enumeration sampling, and all the new and old cases of animal bite reporting in the outpatient or emergency departments for PEP from January to September 2023 and provided consent were enrolled in the study. We interviewed the cases using a structured questionnaire on the sociodemographic, category, site, and time of the bite, biting animal, and treatment initiation time. We did in-depth interviews of bite cases, who had delayed PEP initiation to collect information on the factors that have contributed to the delay in treatment initiation.

Data were collected using Epi-collect and analyzed in Microsoft Excel and Epi-info 7.0 developed by Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in Atlanta, Georgia (US). The cases were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Means and medians represented continuous variables. For categorical variables, proportions and percentages were used. The Chi-square test was applied as a significance test for factors associated with delay in treatment, and P < 0.05 was taken as a significance level.

Ethical considerations

The ethical clearance was obtained from the Public Health Emergency Operational Research and Review Board of the National Centre of Disease Control (PHEORRB-File No. 4-6/2018-EISCell, GHSA/16/05/2023).

Verbal consent was obtained from the participants before the interview and from the caregiver in the case of animal bite in <18 years olds.

Results

We interviewed 422 animal-bite cases who attended the animal-bite clinic from January to September 2023. The median age of cases was 24 years (interquartile range: 11–49), 289 (68%) were males, and 293 (69%) were from rural areas [Table 1]. Of the total animal bite cases, 93% were dog bites, and 52% were on the lower limbs. 71% of cases had a Category II bite followed by Category III in 29%. 62% cases were residing more than 10 km away from the health facilities [Table 2].

On univariate analysis, we found that treatment delay was significantly different among those who lived more than 10 km away from the health facility as compared to those living within 10 km [Table 3]. The proportion of delayed treatment was higher in lower-income groups of < Rs. 10,000 monthly income and lived in rural areas. There was no significant difference in the delays in bites by different animals or categories of bites. There was no gender difference in the delay in treatment.

Discussion

In our study, most participants were from rural areas and in cat III bites were significantly more among the cases who sought delayed PEP. This could be due to differences in the social determinants in rural and urban areas, with more social and geographical barriers in the rural areas in accessing healthcare as compared to the metropolitan areas[19]; rural areas also have a shortage of the availability of transportation facility to visit healthcare facility causing a delay in the initiation of treatment. A few studies in Iran also reported similar findings.[2021] We also found a significant association between distance to a health facility and delay in treatment-seeking, with delays more common when the distance is ≥10 km compared to <10 km. Joseph et al.[14] reported similar findings in their study in Delhi, India. However, in a study in Uganda, there was no significant difference in the delay in seeking treatment in patients who lived within or more than 10 km from the health center.[22]

The delay becomes more important in Category III bites, which can have more catastrophic results in case of delays. Nearly two-thirds of the cases that had a delay in our study had Category II bite. However, we did not find any significant difference in the treatment delay between Category II and Category III bites, which is similar to the finding by Khazaei et al., but it is different from the findings by Wani et al.[123]

Ease of access to a healthcare facility is one of the important aspects of promptness in seeking treatment. There are clear differences in the health-seeking behavior of the urban and rural populations, which can also explain the delays in the rural population.[24] In their study, Salve et al. also found an association between traditional pretreatment practice and delay in seeking treatment for animal bites.[25] These kinds of practices are seen more often in rural areas and can explain the delay in treatment seeking.[262728]

The potential reasons for the delay were elaborated on from the history of the cases. Most of the cases mentioned work-related barriers as the cause of the delay in seeking treatment; these could be issues with the inability to get leave from the job; a study by Dwivedi et al. has mentioned leave-related issues as an important cause of the delay in initiation of treatment, with 15% of cases reporting it.[13] Similarly, Sowbarnika et al. also mentioned that treatment was not continued in cases due to work-related issues.[29] Cases also mentioned that closed health facilities due to holidays or facility timings were one of the major issues that caused delays in treatment, in the study by Joseph et al. This was seen as a cause in around 37% of cases; in a country like India, closure of health facilities is a reason for the delay in treatment of not only animal bites but also other health conditions.[14] The reason for not reaching the health facility on time could be the distance from the health center, the unavailability of a transportation facility, or the lack of proper roads for conveyance; these factors are more probable in rural areas, as seen in other studies.[1929]

A prominent reason that was narrated by the cases in the causation of delay was the unawareness about the PEP among the cases. In the study by Sowbarnika et al., a similar finding was seen, with nearly one-fifth of the cases telling unawareness of the PEP as the reason.[29] Joseph et al. also reported nearly 30% of cases with the cause of delay as unawareness of the program; Dwivedi et al. also reported this as one of the factors in the delay in treatment. Wani et al. also reported this as a cause of delay in around 20% of cases.[131423]

Our study has the limitation that we could only identify the reasons for delay in treatment initiation related with initial factors that lead to delay in reaching the district health facility. The reasons for not seeking treatment for animal bite like lack of financial resources to reach the facility, lack of understanding of the risk of disease, and unavailability of an accompanying person were other reasons for the delay in treatment. Cases also mentioned that since the animal was alive and vaccinated, they did not need to go for PEP.

The study was hospital-based, and hence, findings were from cases belonging to the hospital’s catchment areas. Cases who sought treatment from a private health facility and people who did not seek treatment were missed from the study; however, we took a large sample size to account for such cases.

Conclusion

The study gives insights into the possible causes of delays in seeking treatment by animal bite victims; there is a greater divide between the rural and urban populations, with the delay being more prevalent in the former due to a lack of accessible healthcare. With rapid urbanization, it is imperative to decentralize healthcare and make it more accessible in rural areas with more equitable distribution and a robust transportation infrastructure in the rural areas. There is also a need for awareness campaigns in rural areas regarding the disease and extended timing of the healthcare facilities for such cases to mitigate delays in treatment.
