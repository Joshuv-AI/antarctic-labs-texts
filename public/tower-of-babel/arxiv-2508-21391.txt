Bohr-Mottelson Hamiltonian with octic potential applied to the 106-116 Cd isotopes Preprint: APS/123-QED
P. Buganu 1 and R. Budaca 1,2

Abstract
The Bohr-Mottelson Hamiltonian, with an octic potential in the β \beta -deformation variable, is numerically solved for a γ \gamma -unstable symmetry of the nuclear system. The analytical structure of the model allows the description of multiple phenomena of great interest for the nuclear structure such as ground-state shape phase transitions and their critical points, dynamical shape phase transitions, shape coexistence with and without mixing, anomalous in-band E ​ 2 E2 transitions, large E ​ 2 E2 intra-band transitions and large monopole transition between the first excited 0 + 0^{+} state and the ground state, respectively. As a first application of the present model is selected the 106-116 Cd isotope chain known in literature to manifest shape phase transition, respectively shape coexistence and mixing.

Introduction

The Bohr-Mottelson model
Bohr 1952
;
Bohr and Mottelson 1953
is probably the most suitable to describe nuclear phenomena related to the shape such as quantum shape phase transitions (QSPTs) and critical points (CPs)
Fortunato 2005
;
Casten 2006
;
Cejnar et al. 2010
;
Buganu and Fortunato 2016
;

;

;
Garrett et al. 2022
;
Bontasos et al. 2023a
;
Bontasos et al. 2023b
because within this model the properties of the collective states emerge naturally from vibrations and rotations of the nuclear surface around an equilibrium shape. Thus, one has a direct correspondence between nuclear shape and structure of the corresponding states which can represent a great advantage in investigation and interpretation of the experimental data. On the other hand, the microscopic models provide fundamental explanations and consequently a deep understanding for the presence of these phenomena. Some examples are the Shell Model
Mayer and Jensen 1955
;
Poves 2016a
;
Poves 2016b
, the Interacting Boson Model
Iachello and Arima 1987
;
Nomura et al. 2016
;

;

, Partial Dynamical Symmetry

, Proxy-SU(3)
Martinou et al. 2021
;
Martinou et al. 2023
;
Bonatsos et al. 2023
, Covariant Density Functional Theory (CDFT)
Bonatsos et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2023
;
Nikšić et al. 2008
, Ab initio approaches
Zhou et al. 2025
;
Hu et al. 2024
, a five-Dimensional Collective Hamiltonian based on CDFT
Majola and et
al. 2019
;
Matsuyanagi et al. 2016
and many others

;
Mennana et al. 2022
;
Benjedi et al. 2022
;
Buganu et al. 2025a
.

Many solutions have been given for the Bohr-Mottelson Hamiltonian since its proposal
Fortunato 2005
;
Buganu and Fortunato 2016
, most of them being motivated by a better description of the critical points of the shape phase transitions, initially represented by the E(5) and X(5) symmetries
Iachello 2000
;
Iachello 2001
. A class of these solutions, namely those associated with the polynomial potentials in the deformation variable, has distinguished by the fact that it can describe well not only the critical point but also the entire shape phase transition. Such an example is the sextic potential which can present two minima, a spherical and a deformed one. Moreover, this potential is between the few potentials for which the Schödinger equation can be exactly solved. Actually, this is achieved for only a part of the states through the so-called quasi-exactly solvable method
Ushveridze 1994
;
Budaca et al. 2016
;
Lévai and Arias 2023
. In order to describe other phenomena than CPs and QSPTs, a more general solution is needed involving a numerical diagonalization procedure. Recently, such a method have been proposed using as a basis of diagonalization solutions of the infinite square well potential expressed in terms of the Bessel functions of the first kind
Budaca et al. 2018
. The method has been applied up to the present to describe shape coexistence and mixing, shape transition in band as a function of the total spin or even unusually small quadrupole electromagnetic electromagnetic transitions in the yrast band
Mennana et al. 2021
;
Mennana et al. 2022
;
Benjedi et al. 2022
;
Buganu et al. 2025a
;
Budaca et al. 2018
;
Budaca and Budaca 2018
;
Budaca et al. 2019a
;
Budaca et al. 2019b
;
Buganu et al. 2025b
. There is also a different approach, previously proposed and called the General Collective Model, which uses as a basis of diagonalization the solutions of the five-dimensional harmonic oscillator
Gneuss et al. 1969
;
Hess et al. 1980
. A comparison of the two methods is done in
Budaca et al. 2019a
by underlying the main differences between them.

Recent applications to the experimental data of the Bohr-Mottelson Hamiltonian with sextic potential suggest that higher order of the polynomial potentials are necessary for a better description of the experimental data and for investigating more complicated structure of other nuclei. Consequently, in the present study, the Bohr-Mottelson Hamiltonian with octic potential in the
\beta
variable is numerically diagonalized in a basis of the Bessel functions of the first kind for the special case of the
\gamma
-unstable symmetry of the nuclear system. This symmetry offers the possibility of an exact separation of variables.

The plan of the present work is the following. In Section II, the solution of the
\gamma
-unstable Bohr-Mottelson Hamiltonian with sextic potential in
\beta
variable is introduced in detail. Numerical results revealing possible scenarios relevant for nuclear level structure, as well as preliminary applications to the experimental data of the isotopes of Cadmium, are shown in Section III. Finally, the last Section IV is dedicated to the main conclusions of the present study, respectively to underline possible new perspectives of the model development.

II
Description of the model

The Bohr-Mottelson Hamiltonian model, which describes the nuclear collective states in terms of vibrations and rotations of the nuclear surface, has the following expression written in the intrinsic reference frame
Bohr 1952
;
Bohr and Mottelson 1953
:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2B}\left[\frac{1}{\beta^{4}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\beta^{4}\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}+\frac{1}{\beta^{2}\sin(3\gamma)}\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}\sin(3\gamma)\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}\right]

(1)

\displaystyle+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{8B\beta^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{\hat{Q}_{k}^{2}}{\sin^{2}\left(\gamma-\frac{2\pi}{3}k\right)}+V(\beta,\gamma),

where
\hbar
and
B
are the reduced Plank constant and the mass parameter,
\beta
and
\gamma
are the intrinsic polar coordinates describing the shape of the nucleus, while
\hat{Q}_{k}
are the angular momentum projections which depend on the three Euler angles
\theta_{1}
,
\theta_{2}
and
\theta_{3}
. Thus, one has a five-dimensional Hamiltonian containing kinetic terms in both
\beta
and
\gamma
coordinates (vibrations), a rotational term (rotations) and an energy potential. Most often, the energy potential is selected as a sum between a potential in
\beta
and other in
\gamma

Fortunato 2005
;
Buganu and Fortunato 2016
:

V(\beta,\gamma)=V_{1}(\beta)+\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}V_{2}(\gamma).

(2)

This form of the potential allows an exact separation of the
\beta
from the
\gamma
and the three Euler angles, the latter still remaining coupled due to the rotational term:

\left[-\frac{1}{\beta^{4}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\beta^{4}\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}+v_{1}(\beta)-\varepsilon+\frac{\omega}{\beta^{2}}\right]F(\beta)=0,

(3)

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{1}{\sin(3\gamma)}\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}\sin(3\gamma)\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}+\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{\hat{Q}_{k}^{2}}{4\sin^{2}\left(\gamma-\frac{2\pi}{3}k\right)}+v_{2}(\gamma)\right]

\displaystyle\times\psi(\gamma,\theta_{i})=\omega\psi(\gamma,\theta_{i}),\;i=1,2,3.

(4)

This separation of the variables has been achieved by choosing the wave function
\Psi(\beta,\gamma,\theta_{i})=F(\beta)\psi(\gamma,\theta_{i})
, respectively by adopting the notations for the separation constant
\omega
, the reduced total energy
\varepsilon=\frac{2B}{\hbar^{2}}E
and potentials
v_{1}(\beta)=\frac{2B}{\hbar^{2}}V_{1}(\beta)
and
v_{2}(\gamma)=\frac{2B}{\hbar^{2}}V_{2}(\gamma)
.
Further, the solvability of the equations (
3
) and (
4
) is related to the treatment of the rotational term, but also to the type of potentials in
\beta
and
\gamma
variables. These aspects will be further addressed accordingly.

\displaystyle v(\beta)=\bigg\{\begin{matrix}0,&\beta\leq\beta_{w},\\

Eq. (
3

6

\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{d\beta^{2}}-\frac{4}{\beta}\frac{d}{d\beta}-\varepsilon_{w}+\frac{\omega}{\beta^{2}}\right)f(\beta)=0,

(7)

where by
\varepsilon_{w}
is denoted the eigenvalue of the
\beta

and
\nu=\sqrt{\omega+\frac{9}{4}}

\left[\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}+\frac{1}{z}\frac{d}{dz}+\left(1-\frac{\nu^{2}}{z^{2}}\right)\right]J(z)=0.

(8)

From the boundary condition
J(\beta_{w})=0

\varepsilon_{w;n,\nu}=\frac{z_{n,\nu}^{2}}{\beta_{w}^{2}},\,f_{n,\nu}(\beta)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\beta_{w}}\frac{\beta^{-\frac{3}{2}}J_{n,\nu}\left(\frac{z_{n,\nu}}{\beta_{w}}\beta\right)}{J_{\nu+1}(z_{n,\nu})},

(9)

zero of the Bessel function

of index
\nu

;
Taşeli and Zafer 1997

F_{\xi,\nu}(\beta)=\sum_{n=1}^{n_{Max}}A_{n}^{\xi}f_{n,\nu}(\beta),

(10)

where

indexea the diagonalization solutions and it is related to the
\beta
vibration quantum number
(n_{\beta}=\xi-1)

are the eigenvector components. Further, by substituting the wave function (
10

3
) for the OP (
5

the general matrix element is

Budaca et al. 2019a

H_{mn}=\left(\frac{z_{n,\nu}}{\beta_{w}}\right)^{2}\delta_{mn}+\sum_{i=1}^{4}a_{i}\beta_{w}^{2\cdot i}I_{mn}^{(\nu,i)}.

Here, by

are denoted the matrix elements for the potential terms:

\displaystyle I_{mn}^{(\nu,i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{J_{\nu+1}(z_{m,\nu})J_{\nu+1}(z_{n,\nu})}\times

(12)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}J_{\nu}(z_{m,\nu}x)J_{\nu}(z_{n,\nu}x)x^{2i+1}dx,

II.2
Solution of the
\gamma
equation in the
\gamma
-unstable case

In this case, the non-axial
\gamma
-deformation is not stable, oscillating between prolate (
\gamma=0^{o}
) and oblate (
\gamma=60^{o}
) shapes with an extended flat peak for the probability density distribution of deformation in

\gamma

\omega=\tau(\tau+3),\;\tau=0,1,2,...

(13)

\tau

Bohr 1952
;
Bès 1959
;
Wilets and Jean 1956
. Having the expression for
\omega

\nu

10

\nu=\sqrt{\tau(\tau+3)+\frac{9}{4}}=\tau+\frac{3}{2},

(14)

E_{\xi,\tau,L}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2B}\left[\varepsilon_{\xi,\tau}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},\beta_{w})+bL(L+1)\right].

(15)

The last term,

\tau

Caprio and Iachello 2007

L

\tau

\hbar^{2}/2B
can be removed if the following energy normalization is involved:

R_{\xi,\tau,L}=\frac{E_{\xi,\tau,L}-E_{1,0,0}}{E_{1,1,2}-E_{1,0,0}}

(16)

The infinite barrier
\beta_{w}

\beta_{w}

a_{2}
,
a_{3}

a_{4}

E2
) electromagnetic transitions between the states of the ground,
\beta

\gamma

\displaystyle t\beta D_{\mu,2}^{(2)}(\theta_{i})\cos\gamma+

III
Numerical results

The octic potential in the
\beta
variable allows a description of multiple phenomena in nuclei related to their shape. It can simulate a single spherical minimum, an approximately spherical one, less or well deformed minima, simultaneous spherical and deformed minima, a flat shape and many other interesting situations. Thus, all these cases can cover phenomena such as ground-state shape phase transitions and their critical points, shape fluctuations in the critical point, shape coexistence with and without shape mixing, shape evolution in band with the increasing of the total angular momentum, unusually small inter-band quadrupole electromagnetic transitions or very large intra-band ones, large monopole transitions and many others. Some of these aspects will be discussed in the following subsection by selecting some hypothetical scenarios which for sure could correspond to real cases, while in the next subsection the isotope chaine
106-116
is selected as a first application to the experimental data.

IV
Conclusions

The Bohr-Mottelson Hamiltonian with an octic oscillator potential in the
\beta
variable and
\gamma
-unstable symmetry is numerically solved in a basis of functions which are solutions of the same Hamiltonian, but for an infinite square well potential. The basis functions are expressed in terms of the Bessel functions of the first kind. Numerical calculations evidence the possibility of the model to describe phase transition and its critical point in respect with the shape of the ground state, shape transition in band as a function of the total angular momentum (dynamical phase transitions), shape coexistence with and without mixing, respectively associated quantities as unusually small inter-band
(E2)
transitions, large intra-band
(E2)
transitions or large monopole transition
(E0)
between the first excited
0_{2}^{+}
state and the ground state
0_{1}^{+}
. These remarks are supported by previous applications of the sextic potential to many experimental data, the sextic potential being a particular case of the octic potential, but also by the present application of the model to the
106-116
Cd isotopes.
The octic potential is obviously more versatile than the sextic one. One particular feature pertains to the possibility to have a wider separating barrier between distinct deformation minima. This is essential for a more nuanced description of the mixing between coexisting deformation configurations. This aspect was more evident in the investigation of the isotopes of Cadmium, where a more accurate description of the experimental data could be obtained. These results indicate a presence of the shape coexistence with mixing in
112,114,116
Cd, specific shape fluctuations of the critical point in
108,110
Cd, respectively a pronounced
\gamma
-unstable deformation with shape fluctuations in the ground state for
106
Cd.

It is worth mentioning here that this numerical diagonalization method for the octic potential, which will be for sure extended to other nuclear deformations such as prolate and stable-triaxial ones, can also be adapted to physics problems in other fields where one needs to solve the Schrödinger equation for a polynomial potential. Thus, its applicability can extend in the field on one hand, but also beyond nuclear physics.
