Centring Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Voices: A Framework for Culturally Safe and Racism‐Free Healthcare
Authors: Elissa Elvidge, Roianne West, Karen Nicholls, Kiara Harvey, Yeena Thompson, Geraint B Rogers, Yin Paradies
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCulturally safe, racism‐free healthcare is essential in Australia, yet Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people continue to face inequitable access, experiences and outcomes. Although cultural safety is widely cited, implementation remains shallow and disconnected from patients' realities. We argue that meaningful cultural safety requires Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander governance, robust measurement and institutional accountability. Evidence shows that culturally unsafe care, including racism, creates psychological distress, leads to care avoidance and results in poorer outcomes, whereas Indigenous‐led models perform considerably better. We propose a framework to strengthen cultural safety through Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander community‐governed assessment, greater accountability, data sovereignty and mandated cultural safety and anti‐racism standards.Keywords: accreditation, cultural competency, health policy, healthcare disparities, racism

Introduction

Ensuring that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people can access culturally safe healthcare is a moral and legal obligation. Despite policy commitments, the gap in knowledge and practice remains profound [2]. We argue that meaningful progress will only occur when cultural safety is embedded into service accreditation and when its assessment centres on the lived experiences of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients.

Cultural safety is now a common term in health discourse; yet for many healthcare professionals, it remains abstract. Without lived experience of navigating the health system as an Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander person, the fundamental nature and importance of cultural safety is difficult to understand. For many Indigenous patients (in this article, the terms ‘Indigenous’ and ‘First Nations peoples’ refer to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in Australia as well as Indigenous peoples internationally), experiences of culturally unsafe care, which can lead to distress, traumatisation and poorer clinical outcomes, is commonplace [3]. In a recent study, over half of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander respondents reported experiencing racism in healthcare [4], a factor that contributes to the erosion of trust and increased care avoidance.

They didn't understand that I needed my mum there. I'm a first‐time mum, my baby was sick and I was scared. They asked my mum to leave. [5]

The way they spoke to me, they spoke down to me and my family, and it made me feel anxious about asking questions. If you're too scared to ask questions, it makes it even harder. [5]

The Australian Institute of Health and Welfare estimates that more than a third of the health outcome disparity between Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander and non‐Indigenous Australians is associated with factors, including culturally unsafe care, along with cultural and historical influences, such as racism and intergenerational trauma [6].

Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations (ACCHOs) and related Indigenous‐led models of care, as well as Aboriginal health practitioner and/or Aboriginal hospital liaison officer programs, show that cultural safety can be achieved and sustained when Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander governance and leadership guides service design and delivery. Such models build trust, improve communication, promote engagement and reduce health disparities [7, 8].

A defining feature of cultural safety is that it can only be determined by people receiving care. This subjectivity is deliberate and fundamental: only Indigenous patients can judge whether their care is culturally safe [9]. Yet mainstream institutions typically lack the means to engage with communities appropriately and understand these experiences. Instead, they rely on indirect measures, such as staff training completions, complaint counts or policy adoption, that signal alignment with reform agendas but fail to reflect patient realities [10, 11]. These superficial, key performance indicator‐driven evaluations are unable to provide meaningful insight and are problematic for people who continue to experience racism or culturally unsafe care within healthcare settings, despite a wider narrative of successful reform. Moreover, they enable the persistence of systemic issues of disenfranchisement, stigmatisation, care avoidance and trauma.

With national health reform negotiations and public consultations on the third edition of the National Safety and Quality Health Service (NSQHS) Standards underway [12], this is an opportune time to strengthen cultural safety through four key steps, as described below.

Rigorous, Culturally Grounded Methodology

Develop a rigorous and reproducible Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander‐governed approach to assessing patient experiences that integrates Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander methodologies with metrics‐based measures to ensure that quantitative indicators reflect community‐defined priorities [13]. This could involve, for example, the routine administration of Indigenous‐designed patient surveys following hospital attendance that include measures of cultural respect, racism, family inclusion, trust and perceived safety [14]. To ensure that performance indicators reflect patient and community priorities rather than institutional assumptions, they would be co‐developed with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities and align with Indigenous Data Sovereignty principles [9].

Mechanisms for Accountability

Ensure that insights from end users directly shape service reform at all levels, with clear lines of accountability. Hospital boards, accreditation bodies and health administrators should be required to demonstrate how patient feedback informs policy and service delivery, with formal responses mandated. For example, health services could be required to present cultural safety performance reports to hospital boards alongside clinical quality performance reports and to demonstrate how identified concerns have informed service improvements. This process would be subject to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander governance mechanisms, with formal authority to review organisational responses and monitor implementation of corrective actions. An example of such an approach is provided by the First Nations Health Authority in British Columbia, Canada, where First Nations communities are directly involved in planning, delivering and evaluating health services, strengthening accountability to Indigenous peoples rather than institutions alone [11, 15].

Framework for Monitoring and Benchmarking

Create tools to assess performance both within and across institutions over time. Health services could benchmark, for example, patient‐reported cultural safety scores, rates of discharge against medical advice, complaints relating to racism and measures of trust in healthcare services. Public reporting would enable identification of under‐performance while supporting dissemination of successful models across the health system [7].

Integration Into Standards and Funding Agreements

Embed cultural safety and anti‐racism into all funding agreements and NSQHS Standards. Accreditation and funding should be tied to demonstrated improvements in cultural safety, making culturally safe and racism‐free care non‐negotiable in service delivery. For example, accreditation processes could require evidence of improvements in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patient‐reported cultural safety measures, while funding agreements could include performance expectations related to reducing experiences of racism and increasing trust among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients. Such approaches have been adopted internationally through Indigenous health equity and accountability frameworks that link organisational performance to measurable outcomes [9, 15, 16].

Is Culturally Safe, Racism‐Free Healthcare Achievable?

The realisation of an equitable, racism‐free healthcare system in Australia is entirely tenable; however, it will require profound shifts in practice that transcend policy statements and performative awareness, as well as structural change that centres Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people's sovereignty as the foundation of clinical best practice and institutional accountability.

The implementation, assessment and reporting processes must themselves be culturally safe: avoiding re‐traumatisation, respecting Indigenous data sovereignty and ensuring institutions act even when uncomfortable truths (i.e., institutional racism and culturally unsafe care) are revealed. Positive models already exist, particularly within ACCHOs, demonstrating that culturally safe care is both attainable and sustainable when communities are empowered through self‐determination [17].

Although situated in Australia, these principles resonate internationally, aligning with Māori health reforms in Aotearoa New Zealand and First Nations governance models in Canada. For clinicians, administrators and policymakers, the message is clear: cultural safety cannot remain an aspiration. It must become a measurable condition of equity, justice and clinical excellence, determined by the experiences of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples themselves.
