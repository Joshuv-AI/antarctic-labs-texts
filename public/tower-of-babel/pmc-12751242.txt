Altered cortical structure and networks associated with psychosocial adversity and pain hyposensitivity in adolescents with non-suicidal self-injury
Authors: Guanmei Zhao, Jiaqi Zheng, Yuan Li, Chengqian Jin, Siyu Tong, Meizhi Zheng, Wei Jin, Yao Xu, Hong Chen, Tiansheng Zheng, Hui Xu, Ke Zhao, Aimin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is prevalent among adolescents. Adverse psychosocial factors (e.g., childhood trauma, peer bullying) and altered pain hyposensitivity are core risk factors for NSSI. These factors have been associated with structural changes in brain regions involved in pain processing, which may relate to differences in pain perception. However, the specific relationship between cortical structure and pain perception in NSSI adolescents remains unclear. This study aims to investigated cortical structure and structural covariance network alterations in NSSI adolescents, analyzing associations with pain sensitivity changes and adverse psychosocial factors.Method44 NSSI adolescents and 37 healthy controls underwent MRI scans and clinical assessments. Cortical thickness (CT) and surface area (CSA) values per brain region were obtained for both groups and used to generate a structural covariance matrix. Group comparisons of cortical structure and network properties were performed, followed by correlation analyses integrating psychosocial questionnaires and pain sensitivity metrics.ResultsThe NSSI group showed significantly reduced CT in the right cingulate isthmus cortex. This reduction negatively correlated with the severity of peer bullying and childhood trauma, as well as the bilateral pain tolerance. Regarding structural covariance networks, the NSSI group exhibited reduced global properties, alongside two key nodal alterations: a decrease in nodal betweenness centrality in the rostral left middle frontal gyrus (based on CT), which correlated with childhood trauma severity, and an increase in nodal degree centrality in the left lingual gyrus (based on CSA), which was associated with experiences of physical bullying.ConclusionsThis study revealed brain structure and structural network alterations in NSSI adolescents, linked to changed pain perception and adverse psychosocial factors. Our findings suggest associations between adverse psychosocial factors and altered pain perception in NSSI, which coincide with brain structural and network changes and may reflect heightened vulnerability to NSSI behavior.Trial registrationClinical trial number: not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07606-5.Keywords: Non-suicidal self-injury, Pain perception, Cortical structure, Structural covariance network, Adverse psychosocial factors, Graph theory

Background

Nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI) refers to deliberate bodily tissue damage without suicidal intent, encompassing behaviors like cutting, biting, and burning [1]. As a highly prevalent phenomenon across multiple psychiatric disorders (e.g., depression, borderline personality disorder), NSSI was listed in the Fifth Edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) as a condition requiring further study due to its association with increased mortality risk [2]. Adolescents represent a population with a high prevalence of NSSI [3]. Lifetime prevalence rates reach approximately 22% in community samples [4], and the rate of recurrent NSSI is about 50% in clinical samples [5]. Although not all adolescents with NSSI attempt suicide, frequent NSSI may act as a key risk factor for future suicidal ideation and attempts [6]. Given the high prevalence and significant risk associated with NSSI, elucidating the neural mechanisms underlying it and its related symptoms is crucial for advancing effective prevention and treatment strategies.

NSSI arises from the interplay of multiple subjective and objective factors [5]. Among these, adverse psychosocial factors constitute a significant underlying risk factor for NSSI. Factors such as adverse family environments and parenting styles, strained peer relationships, and lack of social support may elevate the risk of engaging in NSSI behavior [7]. Altered pain perception represents another critical factor contributing to NSSI. Repeated engagement in NSSI may lead to diminished pain perception [8]. This reduction in pain sensitivity may subsequently increase the frequency and severity of NSSI behaviors [9, 10]. Furthermore, high pain tolerance could enhance the acquired capability for suicide in individuals with NSSI, thereby further increasing their risk for suicidal behavior [11].

Neuroimaging studies have demonstrated that adverse psychosocial factors may contribute to brain structure. Individuals with a history of childhood trauma exhibit adaptive alterations in brain regions involved in emotion regulation and pain processing [12]. These regions overlap with areas showing gray matter volume reductions in individuals with NSSI, including the insula, prefrontal cortex, and cingulate cortex [13–15]. The insula, as a multimodal integration area for sensation, emotion, and cognition, may influence an individual’s subjective experience of pain when its function is abnormal [16]. The cingulate gyrus, on the other hand, is closely associated with the affective component of pain and participates in encoding the emotional information of pain [17]. Abnormal changes in brain regions may contribute to abnormalities in affective-motivational and cognitive-evaluative pain processing [18–20], potentiating the use of NSSI as a means to obtain emotional relief. This process may thereby reinforce NSSI behavior through positive reinforcement mechanisms [21].

Notably, existing research has predominantly focused on cortical volume, often overlooking the independent contributions of cortical thickness(CT) and surface area(CSA). CSA and CT exhibit distinct developmental trajectories and susceptibilities [22, 23]. CSA, which is largely determined by prenatal neurogenesis, develops rapidly during the fetal period and subsequently tends to stabilize, being profoundly influenced by early genetic and intrauterine environmental factors [23, 24]. In contrast, CT, which is linked to postnatal synaptic pruning, follows an inverted U-shaped developmental trajectory, peaking during adolescence, and is more sensitive to environmental influences, experiences, and disease states [23, 25]. This divergence suggests that examining these two metrics separately may help to more precisely elucidate whether brain alterations in NSSI are related to early neurodevelopmental vulnerabilities or later adaptive/maladaptive plasticity.

Meanwhile, the structural covariance network (SCN), a graph theory-based analytical approach, focuses on investigating covariance and coordinated patterns of gray matter morphology across the entire brain, rather than solely examining structural changes in isolated regions [26]. While no formally published studies have systematically investigated SCN characteristics in adolescents with NSSI, a preprint study has preliminarily revealed reductions in both global and local topological properties of SCN among NSSI individuals, which were associated with emotional processing [27]. However, this study has not yet explored the relationships between these SCN alterations and psychosocial factors or pain perception, leaving a gap in our understanding of their potential roles in NSSI pathophysiology.

In summary, this study will focus on the adolescents with NSSI, combining cortical structural analysis and structural covariance network analysis to investigate alterations in brain structure among adolescents with NSSI. Furthermore, it will analyze the correlations between these alterations and abnormal pain perception as well as adverse psychosocial factors.

Methods

Participants

This study included a total of 81 participants: 44 adolescents with non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) and 37 age-matched healthy controls (HC). The NSSI group was recruited from psychiatric outpatient clinics and inpatient units at Wenzhou Kangning Hospital and the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University between February 2023 and January 2024. To control for psychiatric comorbidity, the study specifically focused on NSSI cases with comorbid depression—the most common clinical presentation.

Inclusion criteria for the NSSI group comprised: age 12–18 years; right-handed; meets DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for NSSI-D (≥ 5 episodes in the past 12 months) and for depressive or bipolar disorder with current depressive episode; absence of suicidal intent during NSSI. Patients showing acute psychotic symptoms, lacking speech comprehension, or having other psychiatric illnesses (such as borderline personality disorder, substance dependence, and post-traumatic stress disorder, etc.) are excluded. HCs were recruited from local middle schools through online and offline advertisements. Eligibility criteria for HC comprised: age 12–18 years; right-handedness; no history of NSSI; no current or previous diagnosis of any psychiatric disorder; no history of psychiatric treatment. Exclusion criteria for all participants consisted of: physical conditions that would interfere with sensory testing, contraindications for MRI, poor cooperation, or withdrawal from the study.

Psychological measures

Before the MRI scanning session, NSSI adolescents and healthy controls underwent structured clinical assessments administered by specially trained clinicians. During this clinical session, sociodemographic and lifestyle data (e.g., age, sex, education level) as well as clinical diagnosis information, including a history of NSSI, were collected. The severity of NSSI behavior was assessed with the Chinese version of the Adolescent Nonsuicidal Self-injury Assessment Questionnaire [28]. The consideration time before NSSI was assessed using six graded categories (no consideration / several minutes / less than 60 min / more than 1 h but less than 24 h / more than 1 day but less than 1 week / more than 1 week). Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was used to assess the severity of depression in adolescents with depressive episodes [29]. Childhood exposure to abuse and neglect was measured through the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) [30]. The Multidimensional Peer Victimization Scale (MPVS) serves as an instrument to quantify four distinct dimensions of peer victimization [31].

Pain perception assessment

Pain perception was assessed using standardized Quantitative Sensory Testing (QST) [32]. Pressure pain was specifically measured using calibrated von Frey filaments (range: 0.008 g to 300 g). The filaments were applied perpendicularly to the marked skin sites on the index fingers of both hands until bending slightly into a “C” or “S” shape, held for 1–1.5 s. Participants reported their sensations. Three key metrics were obtained. Pressure Detection Threshold (PDT): The minimal pressure perceived as touch (method of limits). Pressure Pain Threshold (PPT): The minimal pressure perceived as pain (method of limits). Pain Rating: Using a 0–5 Numerical Rating Scale (NRS; 0 = no pain, 5 = worst pain imaginable) [33]. Ratings were obtained after applying the three thickest filaments (100 g, 180 g, 300 g) three times each; the average rating per finger was used. See Fig. 1 for details.

MRI data acquisition and processing

All neuroimaging studies were performed at Wenzhou Heping International Hospital via a Siemens 3T MR scanner (model Skyra, Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany). Structural MR images of the entire brain were acquired via sagittal 3D magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequences with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 6.6 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.08 ms, field of view (FOV) = 256 mm × 256 mm, flip angle (FA) = 9°, matrix size = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 1 mm, total slice number = 192 sagittal slices, and total scan duration = 257 s. Cortical reconstruction was performed via FreeSurfer v6.0.0 (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu). Regional CT and CSA values were extracted for 68 regions of interest (ROIs) by using the Desikan‒Killiany atlas [34].

Construction of individualized structural covariance networks

Despite efforts to match the two groups, the HC group had a significantly lower proportion of females and higher education level due to recruitment difficulties. At the same time, considering the individual differences in brain size, gender, age, educational level, and estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) were included as covariates in all subsequent neuroimaging analyses to control for their potential effects. Individualized SCNs were derived from Z-scored residuals of CT/CSA, calculated by regressing covariates and normalizing residuals against HC group means/standard deviations. Binary adjacency matrices were generated via thresholding at network densities of 10%–40%. The network topology properties were calculated using Brain Connectivity Toolkit(https://sites.google.com/site/bctnet/) in Matlab 2013b for each network density. For each network attribute index, the area under the curve (Area Under Curve, AUC) of the whole network density was calculated and used for quantitative analysis. These indicators include standardized feature path length, global efficiency, standardized clustering coefficient, local efficiency and small-world index, which are global metrics used to evaluate the overall integration and separation functions of the entire brain network. Abnormalities may indicate a weakened overall information processing efficiency [35], which may be related to the cognitive and emotional processing difficulties of individuals with NSSI. Additionally, we also evaluated node degree, node efficiency and node betweenness centrality, among others, as node indicators to identify potential abnormal hub regions [36], which helps us understand the abnormal functional impairments of individuals with NSSI.

Statistical analysis

Group differences in demographic and clinical variables were assessed using independent-sample t-tests and chi-square tests. After controlling for covariates such as age, sex, education level, and eTIV, cortical metrics were compared via multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) with false discovery rate (FDR) correction (q < 0.05). Nodal network metrics were analyzed using independent-sample t-tests with FDR correction. Partial correlation analyses explored relationships between cortical metrics, clinical features, environmental exposures and pain sensitivity index. Analyses were performed via IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 (version 25.0, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY), R Version 4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2023), and RStudio (Version “Ghost Orchid”; RStudio Team, 2022). The ENIGMA TOOLBOX (http://enigma-toolbox.readthedocs.io) was employed for visualizing changes at the regional level. The data preprocessing and analysis steps are summarized in Fig. 2.

Results

Participants and characteristics

In this study, 44 NSSI adolescents and 37 HC participants were recruited (Table 1). Although there were no significant differences in age (t = -0.669, p = 0.799) and eTIV (t = 0.806, p = 0.127) between the two groups, there were significant differences in gender (χ2 = 10.636, p = 0.001) and year s of education (t = -2.227, p = 0.033). The proportion of females was higher and the years of education were lower in the NSSI group. To address the potential influence of age on brain development, the NSSI group was divided into two subgroups based on age 15: 12–15 years and 16–18 years. No significant difference in age distribution was observed between these subgroups (χ2 = 0.657, p = 0.418). This might be related to the nature of the disease and its impact on academic performance. These variables were controlled for as covariates in subsequent analyses. Significant differences were also observed between the groups regarding pain perception, childhood trauma, peer bullying and their respective subscales, excluding right pressure detection threshold (t = -5.933 ~ 10.958, p < 0.05). To account for the potential influence of SSRI medication on pain sensitivity, we excluded 11 NSSI individuals taking such drugs. The group difference in pain sensitivity remained significant after exclusion (Table S1). The NSSI group experienced more adverse psychosocial factors and exhibited reduced sensitivity to identical pain stimuli. Notably, over 50% of NSSI adolescents exhibited either no premeditation or only seconds of deliberation prior to NSSI.

Group differences in CT and CSA between the NSSI and HC groups

MANOVA revealed group differences in cortical structural indicators between the NSSI group and the HC group after controlling for covariates such as age, sex, education level, and estimated total intracranial volume (Table 2; Fig. 3). The results revealed widespread structural brain differences between the groups. Specifically: Firstly, compared to the HC group, adolescents with NSSI exhibited significantly reduced CSA in the right pars orbitalis of the inferior frontal gyrus, left entorhinal cortex, and left precuneus, while demonstrating significantly increased CSA in the right posterior cingulate cortex. Moreover, adolescents with NSSI showed significantly reduced CT in the right isthmus cingulate cortex and significantly increased CT in the left postcentral gyrus. Notably, after applying FDR correction, only the right isthmus of cingulate gyrus (IsthCing) maintained a significant group difference (F = 12.663, p = 0.001).

Partial correlation analysis of cortical indicators with clinical characteristics in the NSSI group

After controlling for covariates such as age, sex, education level, and eTIV, the results of the partial correlation analysis indicated that there was no significant correlation between the cortical indicators with inter-group differences and the severity level of depression. This suggests that the cortical differences in these brain regions may be more closely related to other factors. To eliminate the confounding effects of depression, we included the severity of depression as a covariate in the partial correlation analysis. The results are as follows. Partial correlation analysis revealed that the CT of the right IsthCing showed a negative correlation with the NSSI score (r = -0.379, p = 0.017), the sexual abuse subscale score of CTQ (r = -0.331, p = 0.039), the verbal victimization (r = -0.343, p = 0.032) and social manipulation (r = -0.388, p = 0.015) subscale scores of MPVS, the total MPVS score (r = -0.406, p = 0.010), and the bilateral pain thresholds (R: r = -0.368, p = 0.021; L: r = -0.410, p = 0.010). Furthermore, it demonstrated a positive correlation with bilateral pain scores (R: r = 0.442, p = 0.005; L: r = 0.560, p < 0.001). Additionally, in the SCN constructed based on CT, the nodal betweenness centrality of the left rMFG was negatively correlated with the emotional neglect (r = -0.435, p = 0.006) and physical neglect (r = -0.359, p = 0.025) subscale scores of the CTQ. In the SCN constructed based on SCA, the degree centrality of the left lingual was negatively correlated with the physical victimization subscale score of the MPVS (r = -0.349, p = 0.030). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between NSSI, adverse environmental factors and pain perception in adolescents, using both cortical structure and SCNs. Our findings are partially consistent with our primary hypotheses. NSSI adolescents exhibit alterations in regional cortical indices and disruptions in the topology of structural covariance networks. Additionally, these changes have been linked to environmental factors and abnormal pain perception.

Cortical indicators comparison and clinical correlation

Our research found that adolescents with NSSI exhibited a thinner CT in the right IsthCing, and this was negatively correlated with the degree of verbal victimization and social manipulation among peers, and the severity of sexual abuse experienced during childhood trauma. The cingulate gyrus plays a key role in emotion regulation, stress response and social cognition [37]. As the core hub of the default mode network (DMN), it is particularly sensitive to various stressors that require self-reference processing [38]. Individuals who suffered sexual abuse in early life often face more painful self-denial [39]. In collectivist Asian cultures, verbal bullying and exclusion from peers may inflict stronger social pain due to heightened emphasis on group belonging [40]. Chronic stress may affect the proliferation of neurons and synaptic pruning through excessive activation of glucocorticoids, leading to structural or functional abnormalities [41, 42]. We speculate that the abnormal changes in CT observed in NSSI group may represent a maladaptive form of neuroplasticity rather than a pure delay or a classic degeneration. This altered developmental trajectory may ultimately impair neural circuit function involved in emotion regulation and pain processing. The correlation results of this study are consistent with the ‘stress - neural plasticity’ theory, suggesting that the changes in the cingulate cortex may be related to early adverse experiences, but it cannot be determined whether this abnormality is the ‘result of trauma’ or the ‘precondition of trauma susceptibility’.

Furthermore, our research also revealed that NSSI individuals with thinner IsthCing had more severe self-harm, higher pain thresholds and lower scores to the same stimulus. As a limbic hub connecting posterior cingulate and parahippocampal regions [43], the IsthCing is implicated in memory [17], pain processing [44] and affect encoding [45]. A thinning of its thickness may weaken the function of the pain perception pathway, thereby leading to a reduction in pain sensitivity. Poor pain perception may reduce an individual’s fear of injury, making NSSI behaviors more severe or frequent [9, 10]. However, long-term and frequent NSSI behaviors may also damage neurons by triggering inflammatory responses, leading to structural changes in the cortex [46]. At the same time, the NSSI behaviors themselves may cause the pain pathways to exhibit a “desensitization effect” through repeated physical injuries, resulting in a reduced sensitivity to pain [8]. We hypothesize the observed “cingulate-hyposensitivity-NSSI” pattern may be a characteristic neurobiological manifestation for NSSI individuals. In contrast, adolescents with active suicidal ideation may show distinct neural changes in value assessment, future planning, and decision-making circuits [47]. Notably, the transition from NSSI to suicide is a gradual process, with corresponding dynamically adjustable brain pathway alterations [13]. Thus, clinical assessments with direct suicidal intent inquiries remain the most important, reliable method to distinguish NSSI from suicide risk and identify staged changes.

In conclusion, based on our research findings, we propose a hypothetical model: Peer bullying and childhood trauma affect the neural development of the cingulate cortex through long-term stress. The thinning of the CT inhibits the processing of pain emotions, reduces pain sensitivity, and promotes the occurrence of NSSI. And the NSSI behavior, in turn, further promotes the changes in the cortex and pain sensitivity. This hypothesis is consistent with the classic theoretical framework of ‘Early adversity → Brain structure changes → Psychological pathological behavior‘ [48]. However, the current cross-sectional data cannot clarify the causal relationship, and longitudinal studies are essential to establish the chronological sequence.

Decreased global network properties in individuals with NSSI

The present study revealed that in the SCNs constructed based on CT, the NSSI group exhibited significant reductions in global network properties compared with the HC group, including small-worldness, global efficiency, and normalized clustering coefficient. Notably, NSSI individuals exhibited structural alterations primarily in CT rather than CSA within both regional morphology and structural covariance networks, suggesting that this pattern may more reflect the influence of environmental factors rather than genetic factors. During brain development, coordinated maturation of cortical regions forms the neurobiological basis for structural covariance between regions, which is jointly influenced by genetic and environmental factors [26]. Longitudinal studies indicate that CSA (38% heritability) demonstrates stronger genetic determination than CT (14% heritability) [24]. The CT follows an inverted-U shaped developmental trajectory [25], increasing throughout childhood, peaking during adolescence, and subsequently declining in late adolescence. This prolonged developmental window renders CT more vulnerable to both pathological states and environmental perturbations [23]. Therefore, the results of this study support the role of environmental factors in the brain structural changes of NSSI patients, providing a possible explanation for the ‘environment-gene’ interaction mechanism.

The small-world properties of human brain networks, representing an optimal balance between global integration and local segregation during information processing [49], are characterized by global efficiency (reflecting functional integration capacity) and normalized clustering coefficient (quantifying functional segregation). The observed abnormalities in global network properties among adolescents with NSSI suggest an imbalance between integration and segregation in information-processing networks, manifested by reduced capacity in both network integration and segregation. This impairment leads to compromised efficiency in global and local information flow and processing within the neural networks [25]. Neuroimaging evidence indicates that pain, as a multifaceted experience, engages integrated processing across multiple brain [50]. We posit that reduced global network efficiency in NSSI individuals may impair processing efficacy in both sensory-discriminative and affective-motivational dimensions of pain, thereby altering pain perception.

Nodal network properties between the groups and clinical correlation

In addition to assessing global brain network metrics, we delved into alterations in nodal centrality. The NSSI group exhibited decreased betweenness centrality in the left rMFG cortex thickness. More importantly, the nodal betweenness centrality of the left rMFG showed distinct clinical correlations, demonstrating significant negative associations with both emotional neglect and physical neglect. The rMFG, a critical region within the prefrontal cortex, plays pivotal roles in impulse inhibition, and emotion regulation [51]. Childhood experiences of emotional and physical neglect have been associated with reduced sensory input, which may damage prefrontal neurons, leading to abnormalities in their structure (such as thickness) and function (such as betweenness centrality) [52]. The diminished hub efficiency may contribute to poor emotional regulation and weak impulse control, making individuals more difficult to relieve their emotions through healthy means such as “talking and exercise” when facing stress. Instead, they tend to rely on extreme behaviors like self-harm that can temporarily relieve stress, eventually forming a vicious cycle of NSSI.

Interestingly, we also found that the node degree centrality of the left LG brain CSA in NSSI individuals increased, and it was negatively correlated with physical bullying among peers.The LG, as a key component of the occipital lobe, plays a pivotal role in affective perception and visual information processing [53], primarily involved in visual memory encoding [54]. Previous studies have demonstrated that NSSI individuals exhibit aberrant hyperactivity in the occipital cortex during emotion recognition and processing, which may contribute to enhanced transmission of negative emotions [55]. This association suggests that the “network connection breadth” of the left LG may be a potential protective factor against “physical victimization”. When LG can more widely connect visual, emotional, and social judgment brain regions, individuals can detect the signals of bullying earlier and reduce the probability of being bullied through avoidance and seeking help methods.

Llimitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences; longitudinal studies are needed to address this issue. Second, the sample was relatively small, recruited from a single region, and included a poorly matched healthy control group, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future multi-center studies with larger samples are warranted. Third, the absence of MDD participants without NSSI behavior restricts the ability to distinguish between neural correlates of depression and those specific to self-harm. Future research should include adolescents with MDD only. Fourth, laboratory-based pressure pain testing may not fully emulate real-world self-injury experiences; contextual triggering paradigms could better simulate the emotional states associated with NSSI. Fifth, reliance on self-reported data introduces potential recall and social desirability biases. Incorporating multi-informant reports and objective behavioral measures would enhance validity. Finally, some participants were taking medication that may influence pain perception, future studies should prospectively control for medication effects.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings demonstrate that adolescents with NSSI exhibit significant alterations in cortical structure and structural covariance network properties. These neural changes are closely linked to both aberrant pain sensitivity and adverse psychosocial factors, with psychosocial influences more pronounced in cortical thickness variations. This suggests a potential role for neurobiological adaptations to negative experiences in emotion regulation and pain processing among NSSI youth.
