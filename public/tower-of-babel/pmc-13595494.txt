Out of Sight, Out of Mind: Shedding Light on Two Blind Spots in Harm Reduction Discourse
Authors: Simon Clay, Wayne Hall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHarm reduction policies aim to reduce drug‐related harms. This has historically involved providing tailored health services to people who use drugs to do so more safely. Social justice is another defining element of harm reduction. This component aims to reduce drug‐related stigma and minimise the social harms experienced by people who use drugs by advocating for the decriminalisation of the possession of illicit drugs. However, the harms that occur before drugs reach a user's hands or a country's borders have received less attention in harm reduction. These harms include the environmental damage that arises from illicit drug production and the humanitarian violence many individuals experience when carrying drugs across borders to escape extreme poverty and/or gang violence. In this debate piece, we highlight the extent of these harms and argue that they need to be considered when developing more comprehensive international approaches to harm reduction.

Introduction

In the late 1980s, the harm reduction paradigm was developed as a new way of trying to manage the proliferation of HIV/AIDS that was occuring around the world. It was defined as a drug policy approach aimed at reducing the adverse effects of drug use without requiring individuals who used drugs to cease their use [1]. This definition has largely stayed the same over the last 40 years. Harm Reduction International defines harm reduction as: ‘Policies, programmes, and practices that aim to reduce the adverse health, social and economic consequences of drug use … without necessarily reducing drug consumption’ [2]. Organisations such as the World Health Organization, the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime and the European Union Drugs Agency have defined harm reduction more broadly, without explicitly mentioning the aim of making drug use less harmful. The World Health Organization regional office in Europe, for example, defines harm reduction as activities intended to minimise the negative physical and social impacts of drug use, especially HIV and other harms. The United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime defines harm reduction as minimising the adverse health and social consequences of drug use as part of a comprehensive health response that includes prevention and treatment [3]. The European Union Drugs Agency defines harm reduction as comprising ‘interventions, programmes, and policies that reduce health, social, and economic harms’ [4].

The harm reduction paradigm, as originally conceived, rested on the view that drug use would always occur, so it was important to create non‐judgmental drug support services that met people who used drugs ‘where they were at’. Current advocates of this harm reduction approach often support policies that remove criminal penalties for drug possession and use (i.e., decriminalisation) to reduce the stigmatisation of persons who use drugs and minimise their interactions with the criminal justice system [5]. They also support a continuum of treatment modalities and support services that range from safer use strategies (e.g., distribution of injecting equipment and establishment of supervised injecting centres) to the provision of opioid agonist therapy and abstinence‐oriented treatment. Observational and controlled studies generally show that opioid agonist therapy, needle and syringe programs, medically‐supervised injecting rooms and drug testing kits reduce emergency room presentations, improve quality of life and save lives [6, 7].

The harms that occur before drugs reach their end users have received very little attention in discussions of harm reduction. These include the environmental harms that arise from manufacturing illicit drugs and the humanitarian harms produced by the trafficking process. Analyses of the environmental impacts of illicit drug production have been confined to the fields of waste management [8, 9], environmental toxicology [10, 11] and green criminology (the study of environmental justice, how environmental crimes occur and what can be done to reduce them) [12, 13]. The humanitarian issues related to drug trafficking have remained primarily within the purview of legal studies, criminology and human rights scholars.

Environmental Harms

Illicit drug production has significant adverse environmental impacts. Coca, cannabis and opium poppy farms use vast amounts of water. For example, one cannabis plant consumes almost 23 L of water per day during the growing season [14]. Indoor cannabis cultivation has a considerable energy demand due to the high‐intensity lighting, air‐conditioning and ventilation systems [14]. The current ‘psychedelic renaissance’ has created a demand for wild‐harvested ayahuasca vine, peyote, iboga and Sonoran Desert toad, leading to sustainability concerns [15]. Large areas of old‐growth forests in Central and South America are regularly burned down using the ‘slash‐and‐burn’ method to create land on which to grow coca, cannabis and poppies [16, 17]. This habitat destruction is worsened by the use of potent fertilisers, herbicides, pesticides and rodenticides to protect the crops, which also causes significant secondary poisonings of predators that eat poisoned prey [16]. Deforestation is a particularly serious problem with the cocaine trade as it takes approximately 12 kg of dried coca leaves to make 1 kg of cocaine [18, 19]. While deforestation is a problem in many industries (e.g., palm oil, cocoa and timber), the optimal soil and climate conditions for coca plants are in mountainous, isolated areas, which means that deforestation occurs in areas that would have otherwise been preserved [20].

The production of synthetic drugs such as methamphetamine is equally harmful to the environment. The methamphetamine synthesis process creates 6–10 kg of waste for every kilogram of methamphetamine [21]. ‘Cooks’ often pour the highly corrosive and flammable waste, which also contains heavy metals, into drains or dump it into waterways or on land [21, 22]. Cleaning up dumpsites and abandoned drug labs is dangerous and requires additional toxic chemicals to neutralise and remove the original contaminants [8]. Methamphetamine and the pollution from its production are especially toxic to aquatic life, and wastewater treatment plants cannot completely remove these pollutants before the water is returned to natural waterways [23].

The destruction of plantations and drug seizures comes with its own environmental challenges. Manual eradication of coca and poppy plantations involves law enforcement digging up and incinerating crops, or using the slash‐and‐burn method to destroy farms [12]. These approaches are time‐ and labour‐intensive, leave law enforcement officers exposed to attack and burning farms creates the risk of forest fires. The Colombian government has carried out aerial fumigation of coca plantations with a range of herbicides since the late 1970s [24] because it is a low‐cost and efficient way to target large blocks of land in hard‐to‐reach areas. Glyphosate is the primary herbicide now used. Its safety profile is contested, but it is claimed to be safe when applied correctly in small doses [25, 26]. However, high‐concentration glyphosate is often used in aerial fumigation operations in Colombia because planes/helicopters need to fly at high altitudes to avoid ground attacks from farmers. A potent formulation is therefore required to counter the effects of high‐altitude wind dispersal and ensure that enough herbicide reaches the target crops [12]. This approach results in devastating collateral damage to the surrounding environment [27]. Aerial fumigation using glyphosate was banned in Colombia in 2015, but has recently been reinstated in an attempt to quell cartel violence [28].

The method used to destroy drug seizures has a significant environmental impact, too. Incineration, chemical decomposition using strong acids or bases and biological degradation using microorganisms are the three primary disposal methods. Incineration and chemical decomposition are both efficient methods but generate a significant amount of pollution. Biological degradation is much ‘greener’, but is a slower and substantially more expensive process than the other two methods [9]. Consequently, incineration tends to be the preferred method for governments. Australian law enforcement seized 41.4 t of illicit drugs during 2020–2021 [29], so the environmental impact of this disposal method is considerable.

Humanitarian Harms

Individuals who carry drugs across international borders often do so because of extreme poverty, and women and girls are disproportionately affected by the harms of trafficking [30]. Interactions with gang/cartel members at each border of the journey frequently involve coercion and intimidation [31, 32]. The journey itself is risky and highly stressful due to the legal consequences of being caught with a commercial quantity of drugs in transit countries, particularly those with severe anti‐drug laws and notorious prison systems (e.g., Thailand, Vietnam and Colombia). Incarceration is expensive and has profound, long‐lasting impacts for individuals and their families and friends. Individuals who have been to prison experience significant stigma post‐release, which limits their ability to find gainful employment, can cause social ostracisation and heighten health disparities [33, 34]. Imprisonment can place families in socio‐economic precarity if the incarcerated individual was the family's primary breadwinner, and family members often experience secondary stigma [35, 36]. People of colour in Western countries are disproportionately criminalised for drug‐related offences, perpetuating generational disadvantage and reifying White/colonial power structures [37]. Many countries have the death penalty for drug trafficking [38], and prisons in nations with severe drug laws are known for their squalid conditions.

Some harm reduction advocates have highlighted these humanitarian harms, but it has often been in the context of either reforming domestic drug policy in Western nations or as a general call for law reform internationally [39, 40]. These harms tend to be presented as health and/or social problems rather than humanitarian harms that disproportionately affect foreign nationals trafficking drugs in an attempt to escape penury [41, 42, 43]. Some policy scholars have also noted these humanitarian harms, but have analysed them from a socio‐legal perspective rather than a humanitarian one [37, 38, 44, 45].

It is important to contextualise the unique environmental and humanitarian harms of the illicit drug trade and highlight that they are not outliers or unique to illicit drugs; they emerge alongside the array of harms created through modern modes of production and consumption. The slavery associated with the fast fashion industry is common knowledge; the cobalt used in many smart devices is extracted from mines in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where child labour and severe worker exploitation take place, and the mining process causes significant environmental destruction [46]; and many of our groceries are shipped in from overseas, packaged in single‐use plastic that will take centuries to degrade. The implication here is that the environmental and humanitarian harms of the illicit drug market are partly driven by larger systemic forces (i.e., capitalism and neoliberalism) and not solely the product of criminalisation, and therefore require dynamic, multidimensional solutions.

Responding to These Harms

These environmental and humanitarian harms associated with the manufacture and trafficking of illicit drugs are rarely—if ever—included in analyses of the social and economic costs of drug use. Cost analyses are typically limited to the health costs (e.g., inpatient and outpatient care, pharmaceuticals and social work), crime costs (e.g., policing, incarceration and prosecution) and productivity costs (e.g., loss of productivity due to incarceration, drug crime victims' losses, loss of employment and loss of productivity from mortality and illness) [47, 48]. Some scholars have highlighted the importance of including the ‘intangible costs’ of drug use, such as pain and suffering, when assessing its ‘social burden’ [49]. However, these intangible costs are usually restricted to the pain and suffering of individuals who use drugs and the people who are adversely affected by that individual's drug use (e.g., family members). Less consideration is given to the pain and suffering that occurs during the production and trafficking of the drugs, or the intangible environmental costs, such as loss of biodiversity.

Some critics of current drug policies argue that one way of ameliorating the extensive environmental and humanitarian harms associated with illicit drug production is to legalise the production and use of heroin, methamphetamine and cocaine by adults [5, 45, 50, 51]. This could be done, as it has with cannabis, by legalising production and retail sale, or in the case of opioids, making these substances available on prescription to adults. In principle, adult legalisation would dissolve the illicit market, remove the associated environmental and humanitarian harms and enable the regulation of drug production and sale to protect public health and the environment [42]. However, it is highly unlikely that methamphetamine, heroin and cocaine will be legalised for adult use anytime soon, and these environmental and humanitarian crises require action now.

Local harm reduction measures could be implemented in some illicit drug markets to mitigate these environmental and humanitarian harms. Rhodes et al. have described how coca farmers in Colombia care for the land and themselves when tending to their crops and synthesising cocaine. For example, some farmers apply pesticides and fungicides in a targeted manner and dispose of waste chemicals in ways that protect the surrounding land [52]. Some aquatic plants appear to be effective at capturing methamphetamine from wastewater [53]—these could be planted in affected waterways. This would also have the added benefit of enhancing aquatic biodiversity. There is an established waste management infrastructure for different types of hazardous waste, such as medical waste, toxic substances and chemical waste containing heavy metals. Designated waste disposal services in discrete locations could be established for methamphetamine cooks to use. For this approach to work, law enforcement would need to apply the same harm reduction approach they use for intravenous drug use and the disposal of needles and syringes (i.e., not prosecute for personal use and facilitate/encourage the disposal of used injecting equipment). This may prove challenging. Government/law enforcement bodies may be averse to adopting this approach due to concerns that it may portray them as tacitly endorsing drug manufacturing and strengthening the domestic drug market. Manufacturers may be reluctant to use formal disposal service due to concerns about detection, particularly if they are a major producer. However, the alternative path forward is to maintain the status quo and perpetuate the environmental harms associated with drug manufacturing.

The environmental and humanitarian harms associated with drug use occur across national borders, and will therefore require concerted multilateral efforts between governments to address them. While decriminalisation and legalisation are unrealistic goals, particularly in an international context, lightening the legal implications for moving drugs across borders would make a significant difference. This might entail removing the death penalty for drug crimes and providing legal protections for people caught carrying drugs across borders. The drugs would still be confiscated, but the individual would not be prosecuted and could be encouraged to work with law enforcement to assist in the apprehension of violent gangs. We need to start considering what a global model of harm reduction that aims to reduce the environmental harms of illicit drug production and the humanitarian harms arising from drug trafficking might look like.
