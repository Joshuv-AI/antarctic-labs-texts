Splitting theorems for weighted Finsler spacetimes via the p p -d’Alembertian: beyond the Berwald case
Erasmo CAPONIO, Argam OHANYAN, Shin-ichi OHTA

Abstract
A timelike splitting theorem for Finsler spacetimes was previously established by the third author, in collaboration with Lu and Minguzzi, under relatively strong hypotheses, including the Berwald condition. This contrasts with the more general results known for positive definite Finsler manifolds. In this article, we employ a recently developed strategy for proving timelike splitting theorems using the elliptic p p -d’Alembertian. This approach, pioneered by Braun, Gigli, McCann, Sämann, and the second author, allows us to remove the restrictive assumptions of the earlier splitting theorem. For timelike geodesically complete Finsler spacetimes, we establish a diffeomorphic splitting. In the specific case of Berwald spacetimes, we show that the Busemann function generates a group of isometries via translations. Furthermore, for Berwald spacetimes, we extend these splitting theorems by replacing the assumption of timelike geodesic completeness with global hyperbolicity. Our results encompass and generalize the timelike splitting theorems for weighted Lorentzian manifolds previously obtained by Case, Woolgar and Wylie.



Keywords
: Finsler spacetime, weighted Ricci curvature,
p
-d’Alembertian, splitting theorem, Berwald spacetime

Introduction

Splitting theorems are important rigidity results which appear in various fields of geometry. They state that a complete geometric space of nonnegative curvature containing an infinitely long, distance-realizing curve (i.e., a line) must split off that curve as an isometric factor of the real line. The intuition is that nonnegative curvature promotes the formation of conjugate points and thus curves should stop realizing the distance in finite time, however, the existence of a line is an anomaly that is only possible in product geometries.

In both Riemannian and Lorentzian geometry, splitting theorems under the assumption of nonnegative Ricci curvature are major milestones of their respective fields.
In the Riemannian case, Cheeger–Gromoll’s celebrated splitting theorem
[
20
]
and its generalizations (e.g., the almost-splitting of Cheeger–Colding
[
18
]
or the generalization to
\mathsf{RCD}
-spaces by Gigli
[
30
]
) are important building blocks for the theory of geometric structures of (possibly singular) spaces (see Cheeger–Colding
[
19
]
, Mondino–Naber
[
53
]
).
In the context of general relativity, the nonnegativity of the Ricci curvature in timelike directions is usually called the timelike convergence condition (which, under the Einstein equations, is implied by the strong energy condition). The splitting of a timelike geodesically complete spacetime under the strong energy condition was conjectured by Yau
[
72
]
and established by Newman
[
55
]
, building on earlier work of Eschenburg
[
22
]
and on the globally hyperbolic case proved by Galloway
[
28
]
. Consequently, a dichotomy arises between the product splitting of a spacetime and the existence of incomplete timelike geodesics (see the standard texts of Hawking–Ellis
[
32
]
, Beem–Ehrlich–Easley
[
3
]
).
These results are preceded by a splitting theorem under the stronger assumption of a timelike sectional curvature bound due to Beem–Ehrlich–Markvorsen–Galloway
[
4
,
5
]
(this latter result has recently been generalized to the synthetic setting of Lorentzian length spaces by Kunzinger–Sämann
[
38
]
; see
[
7
]
).

Both the Riemannian and the Lorentzian splitting theorems under nonnegative Ricci curvature bounds were generalized to the Finsler setting (see Ohta
[
58
]
in the Riemann–Finsler case, Lu–Minguzzi–Ohta
[
44
]
in the Lorentz–Finsler case), adopting appropriate weighted formulations.
In contrast to the results for Finsler manifolds in
[
58
]
, the work on Finsler spacetimes in
[
44
]
required stronger hypotheses. More precisely, in addition to the usual set of assumptions (timelike geodesic completeness, nonnegative weighted Ricci curvature in timelike directions, existence of a timelike line), it is assumed that there exists a timelike geodesically complete Lorentzian metric
g
on
M
whose Levi-Civita connection agrees with the Chern connection (thus, the Lorentz–Finsler spacetime
(M,L)
has to be of
Berwald type
) and such that the given timelike line for the Lorentz–Finsler metric lifts as a timelike line in the universal cover of
(M,g)
.

The aim of this article is to improve the splitting theorem in
[
44
, Theorem 1.2]
by removing these technical assumptions. Precisely, although the isometric splitting is clearly out of reach (see Remark
1.2
), we can obtain a diffeomorphic, measure-preserving splitting and, under the additional Berwald condition, also obtain a one-parameter family of isometries as translations (a local Finslerian isometric splitting can be obtained under significantly strong symmetry assumptions on
L
, see Caponio–Stancarone
[
16
, Theorem 4.8]
, that must also lack twice-differentiability along a timelike direction; see also
[
13
, Remark 7.5]
).
In particular, while
[
44
]
treated only Newman’s timelike geodesic completeness version
[
55
]
, we also prove a counterpart of Galloway’s global hyperbolicity theorem
[
28
]
in the Berwald setting.

Such an improvement stems from a new strategy for Lorentzian manifolds developed by Braun–Gigli–McCann–Ohanyan–Sämann
[
10
]
, based on the
p
-d’Alembertian, a quasi-linear second order differential operator defined analogously to the
p
-Laplacian in the positive definite case, but with
p<1
. The
p
-d’Alembertian was introduced by Mondino–Suhr
[
54
]
and later investigated in the non-smooth setting by Beran–Braun–Calisti–Gigli–McCann–Ohanyan–Rott–Sämann
[
6
]
. In the latter work, its connection with a convex
p
-energy functional was used to reveal its elliptic nature in metric measure spacetimes. This ellipticity was then used in
[
10
]
to simplify proofs of the Lorentzian splitting theorems.
Unlike the standard (
2
-)d’Alembertian (the spacetime Laplacian), which is hyperbolic and requires identifying a suitable spacelike hypersurface for maximum principle arguments, the ellipticity of the
p
-d’Alembertian allows for a direct application of the maximum principle, yielding a simpler, alternative proof of the timelike splitting theorem in both timelike geodesically complete and globally hyperbolic spacetimes (see
[
10
]
for details). The same set of authors also extended this approach to weighted Lorentzian manifolds with non-smooth metrics and weights (see
[
11
]
).

We extend the elliptic
p
-d’Alembertian strategy to smooth Finsler spacetimes.
In this setting the operator is anisotropic, and even the case
p=2
yields a quasilinear d’Alembertian.
Accordingly, the point is not to “trade linearity for ellipticity”
[
47
]
, but rather to obtain an anisotropic elliptic operator within the Finsler spacetime framework.

A Finsler spacetime
(M,L)
is a time oriented Lorentz–Finsler manifold; see Definition
2.3
. Lorentz–Finsler structures and Finsler spacetimes have been considered both as a
geometric description of gravity beyond general relativity (see, e.g.,
[
37
,
63
,
8
]
) and in the classical limit of modified dispersion relations
encompassing Lorentz violation in quantum gravity and in the Standard Model Extension (see, e.g.,
[
31
,
68
,
35
,
36
,
64
,
21
]
). Remarkably, they provide a counter-example to the Schiff conjecture
[
40
]
but they are compatible with a slight modification of Ehlers–Pirani–Schild axioms for general relativity (see
[
39
,
8
]
).

We denote by
\tau
the time separation function on
M
(see (
2.4
)).
Then, given a timelike straight line
\eta\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow M
(i.e.,
\tau(\eta(s),\eta(t))=t-s
for all
s<t
), the associated Busemann function
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}\colon M\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(x):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\bigl\{t-\tau\bigl(x,\eta(t)\bigr)\bigr\}

plays a vital role to establish a splitting theorem along
\eta
.
Our first main theorem is the following.

Theorem 1.1

(Splitting theorem)
.

Let
(M,L)
be a connected Finsler spacetime equipped with a measure
\mathfrak{m}
on
M
.
Suppose the following:

(1)

(M,L)
is either timelike geodesically complete, or Berwald and globally hyperbolic;

(2)

there is a complete timelike straight line
\eta\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow M
;

(3)

(M,L,\mathfrak{m})
satisfies
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}\geq 0
in timelike directions for some
N\in(-\infty,0)\cup[n,\infty]
, where
n=\dim M
;

(4)

in the case of
N\in(-\infty,0)\cup\{\infty\}
,

(M,L)
satisfies the timelike
\epsilon
-completeness for some
\epsilon
in the range (
2.14
) associated with
N
.

Then, the Lorentzian manifold
(M,g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})})
isometrically splits
in the sense that there exists an
(n-1)
-dimensional Riemannian manifold
(\Sigma,h)

such that
(M,g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})})
is isometric to
(\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma,-\mathrm{d}t^{2}+h)
.
Moreover,
(\Sigma,h)
is equipped with a measure
\mathfrak{n}
for which

\mathfrak{m}
coincides with the product of the Lebesgue measure
\mathrm{d}t
on
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathfrak{n}
.

The Lorentzian structure
g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}
is the second order approximation of
L
in the direction
\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})
(see (
2.2
)).
One can also see that, for every
x\in\Sigma
, the curve
\zeta(t)
corresponding to
t\longmapsto(t,x)
is a timelike straight line bi-asymptotic to
\eta
and an integral curve of the gradient vector field
\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})
.
We remark that
\Sigma
is given as the level surface
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}^{-1}(0)
, and then
(\Sigma,L|_{T\Sigma})
is spacelike if
L
is reversible and
\dim M\geq 3
, though it is unclear whether
L|_{T\Sigma}
is positive definite (strongly convex); see Corollary
6.1
for details.

The weighted Ricci curvature
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}
is a modification of the usual Ricci curvature taking into account the behavior of the measure
\mathfrak{m}
, and timelike
\epsilon
-completeness can be regarded as a weighted version of timelike geodesic completeness (see Definitions
2.9
,
2.13
).
When
N\in[n,\infty)
, we can take
\epsilon=1
and timelike
1
-completeness always holds true.

The Berwald condition in (
1
) means that the covariant derivative is independent of a reference vector (see Definition
2.4
).
It allows us to show that, in the globally hyperbolic setting, nearby asymptotes to the straight line
\eta
are complete, and that the local splitting around
\eta
can be globalized, along the lines of Galloway’s argument given in
[
28
]
.
Since such difficulties do not arise in the timelike geodesically complete setting, we do not need to assume the Berwald condition there.
Let us remark that, in Lu–Minguzzi–Ohta
[
44
, Theorem 1.2]
, only timelike geodesically complete Berwald spacetimes which are Lorentzian-metrizable were considered (see also Remark
2.5
).

Remark 1.2
.

Theorem
1.1
implies the diffeomorphic and measure-preserving splitting of
(M,\mathfrak{m})
, however, the metric
L
does not have any splitting structure.
This is actually natural in the Lorentz–Finsler framework, for one can modify
L
in spacelike directions without changing it in timelike directions. Since the hypotheses are concerned only with (future-directed) timelike directions, such a deformation in spacelike directions does not influence them.
Hence, in the situation of Theorem
1.1
, we have no control of
L
in spacelike directions.

In the Berwald case, thanks to its more rigid structure, we have in fact a certain control of
L
also in spacelike directions, in the same spirit as Ohta
[
58
, Proposition 5.2]
and Lu–Minguzzi–Ohta
[
44
, Corollary 1.3]
.

Theorem 1.3

(Isometric translations in Berwald spacetimes)
.

Let
(M,L,\mathfrak{m})
be a Berwald spacetime satisfying the hypotheses in Theorem
1.1
.
Then, we have the following.

(i)

In the product structure
M=\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma
, the translations

\Phi_{t}(s,x):=(s+t,x),\qquad(s,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma,\,\ t\in\mathbb{R},

are isometric transformations of
(M,L)
and preserve the measure
\mathfrak{m}
.

(ii)

The geodesic equation of
M
splits into those of
\mathbb{R}
and
\Sigma
.
Precisely, a curve in
M
is geodesic if and only if its projections to
\mathbb{R}
and
\Sigma
are geodesic.

As is explained in Remark
1.2
, one cannot expect the isometry of translations in general Finsler spacetimes.
We also remark that, since we consider measured Finsler spacetimes and there may be no canonical measure (like the volume measure in the Lorentzian case) in the Lorentz–Finsler setting, Theorem
1.1
does not cover the unweighted case (assuming
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits\geq 0
in place of
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}\geq 0
).
One can define the
p
-d’Alembertian without using a measure (Remark
3.5
), however, what is missing is a linear divergence, which appears in a Bochner-type identity (Proposition
5.3
) used to obtain the smoothness of the Busemann function.

In the Lorentzian case, the weighted version studied by Case
[
17
, Theorem 1.2]
(
N\in[n,\infty]
) and Woolgar–Wylie
[
69
, Theorem 1.5]
(
N\in(-\infty,1)\cup[n,\infty]
) includes the unweighted one of Galloway
[
28
]
and Newman
[
55
]
by choosing the volume measure as
\mathfrak{m}
and letting
N=n
.
For a weighted Lorentzian manifold
(M,g,\mathfrak{m})
, since
g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}
coincides with
g
, Theorem
1.1
recovers the splitting theorems in
[
17
,
69
]
as the special case of
\epsilon=1
(resp.
\epsilon=0
) for
N\in[n,\infty)
(resp.
N\in(-\infty,0)\cup\{\infty\}
) (the case of
N\in[0,1)
was excluded for a technical reason; see Remark
5.7
).

The article is organized as follows.
After reviewing necessary notions and known results on weighted Finsler spacetimes in Section

-d’Alembertian in Section

.
Then we discuss the properties of Busemann functions in Section

Preliminaries for Finsler spacetimes

We briefly recall the necessary notions for Lorentz–Finsler manifolds along the lines of

]

(we remark that

We refer to

]

]

Throughout the article,
let
M
be a connected
C^{\infty}
-manifold without boundary of dimension
n\geq 2
.
Given a local coordinate system
(x^{i})_{i=1}^{n}
on an open set
U\subset M
,
we will use the fiber-wise linear coordinates

v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v^{i}(\partial/\partial x^{i})|_{x},

, on the coordinate patch
TU
in the tangent bundle

.

2.1
Finsler spacetimes

For our purposes, we adopt the following notion of Lorentz–Finsler structure:

Definition 2.1

(Lorentz–Finsler structures)
.

A
Lorentz–Finsler structure
on
M
is a function

L\colon TM\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

L\in C^{\infty}(TM\setminus\{0\})
;

(2)

L(cv)=c^{2}L(v)
for all
v\in TM
and
c>0
;

(3)

For any
v\in TM\setminus\{0\}
, the symmetric matrix

\big(g_{ij}(v)\big)_{i,j=1}^{n}:=\bigg(\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial v^{i}\partial v^{j}}(v)\bigg)_{i,j=1}^{n}

(2.1)

is non-degenerate with signature
(-,+,\ldots,+)
.

Then we call
(M,L)
a (
C^{\infty}
-)
Lorentz–Finsler manifold
.

For
v\in T_{x}M\setminus\{0\}
, we can define a Lorentzian metric
g_{v}
on
T_{x}M

by using (
2.1
) as

g_{v}\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\bigg|_{x},\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\bigg|_{x}\Bigg):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g_{ij}(v)a_{i}b_{j}.

(2.2)

Then we have
g_{v}(v,v)=2L(v)
by Euler’s homogeneous function theorem.

A tangent vector
v\in TM
is said to be
timelike
(resp.
null
)
if
L(v)<0
(resp.
L(v)=0
).
We say that
v
is
lightlike
if it is null and nonzero, and
causal
if it is timelike or lightlike.

Spacelike
vectors are those for which
L(v)>0
or
v=0
.
Denote by
\Omega^{\prime}_{x}\subset T_{x}M
the set of timelike vectors and put

\Omega^{\prime}:=\bigcup_{x\in M}\Omega^{\prime}_{x}
.
For causal vectors
v
, we define

F(v):=\sqrt{-2L(v)}=\sqrt{-g_{v}(v,v)}.

(2.3)

Note that
\Omega^{\prime}_{x}\neq\emptyset
, every connected component of
\Omega^{\prime}_{x}
is a convex cone (see
[
2
]
,
[
42
, Lemma 2.3]
),
and that the closures of different components intersect only at
0
(see
[
48
, Proposition 1]
).

Remark 2.2
.

On the one hand, the number of connected components of
\Omega^{\prime}_{x}
may be larger than
2
in this generality (see
[
2
]
,
[
42
, Example 2.4]
).
On the other hand, if
L
is reversible (i.e.,
L(v)=L(-v)
for any
v\in TM
) and
n\geq 3
, then
\Omega^{\prime}_{x}
has exactly two connected components (see
[
48
, Theorem 7]
).

Definition 2.3

(Finsler spacetimes)
.

If a Lorentz–Finsler manifold
(M,L)
admits a smooth timelike vector field
X
,
then
(M,L)
is said to be
time oriented
(by
X
).
A time oriented Lorentz–Finsler manifold will be called a
Finsler spacetime
.

In a Finsler spacetime time oriented by
X
,
a causal vector
v\in T_{x}M
is said to be
future-directed

if it lies in the same connected component of
\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}\!_{x}\setminus\{0\}
as
X(x)
.
We denote by
\Omega_{x}\subset\Omega^{\prime}_{x}
the set of future-directed timelike vectors,
and define

\Omega:=\bigcup_{x\in M}\Omega_{x},\qquad\overline{\Omega}:=\bigcup_{x\in M}\overline{\Omega}_{x}.

A
C^{1}
-curve in
(M,L)
is said to be
timelike
(resp.
causal
)
if its tangent vector is always timelike (resp. causal).
Henceforth, unless explicitly stated otherwise, all causal curves are assumed to be future-directed.

Given
x,y\in M
, we write
x\ll y
(resp.
x<y
)
if there is a timelike (resp. causal) curve from
x
to
y
,
and
x\leq y
means that
x=y
or
x<y
.
Then we define the
chronological past
and
future
of
x
by

I^{-}(x):=\{y\in M\,|\,y\ll x\},\qquad I^{+}(x):=\{y\in M\,|\,x\ll y\},

and the
causal past
and
future
of
x
by

J^{-}(x):=\{y\in M\,|\,y\leq x\},\qquad J^{+}(x):=\{y\in M\,|\,x\leq y\}.

We also define the
time separation
(also called the
Lorentz–Finsler distance
)
\tau(x,y)
for
x,y\in M
by

\tau(x,y):=\sup_{\eta}\mathbf{L}(\eta),\qquad\mathbf{L}(\eta):=\int_{0}^{1}F\big(\dot{\eta}(t)\big)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(2.4)

where
\eta\colon[0,1]\longrightarrow M
runs over all causal curves from
x
to
y
. We set
\tau(x,y):=0
if
x\not\leq y
.
A curve
\eta
attaining the above supremum and having a constant speed (i.e.,
F(\dot{\eta})
is constant), which is then a causal
geodesic
, is said to be
maximizing
.
In general,
\tau
is only lower semi-continuous and can be infinite (see, e.g.,
[
49
, Proposition 6.7]
).
In
globally hyperbolic
Finsler spacetimes (see Definition
6.4
),
\tau
is finite and continuous,
and any pair of points
x,y\in M
with
x<y
admits a maximizing geodesic from
x
to
y

(see
[
49
, Propositions 6.8, 6.9]
).

Next, we introduce the covariant derivative.
Define

\gamma^{i}_{jk}(v):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{n}g^{il}(v)\bigg\{\frac{\partial g_{lk}}{\partial x^{j}}(v)+\frac{\partial g_{jl}}{\partial x^{k}}(v)-\frac{\partial g_{jk}}{\partial x^{l}}(v)\bigg\}

for
i,j,k=1,\ldots,n
and
v\in TM\setminus\{0\}
,
where
(g^{ij}(v))
is the inverse matrix of
(g_{ij}(v))
;

G^{i}(v):=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\gamma^{i}_{jk}(v)v^{j}v^{k},\qquad N^{i}_{j}(v):=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial G^{i}}{\partial v^{j}}(v)

for
v\in TM\setminus\{0\}
,
G^{i}(0)=N^{i}_{j}(0):=0
by convention.
(The above
G^{i}(v)
is defined in the same way as
[
59
]
and
[
60
]
and corresponds to
2G^{\alpha}(v)
in
[
44
]
, in order to avoid confusion in the calculations in Subsection
5.2
.)
For later use, note that the homogeneous function theorem yields (cf.
[
59
, Exercise 4.7]
)

N^{i}_{j}(v)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma^{i}_{jk}(v)v^{k}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}g^{ik}(v)\frac{\partial g_{kl}}{\partial v^{j}}(v)G^{l}(v).

(2.5)

Moreover, we set

\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(v):=\gamma^{i}_{jk}(v)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l,m=1}^{n}g^{il}(v)\bigg(\frac{\partial g_{lk}}{\partial v^{m}}N^{m}_{j}+\frac{\partial g_{jl}}{\partial v^{m}}N^{m}_{k}-\frac{\partial g_{jk}}{\partial v^{m}}N^{m}_{l}\bigg)(v)

(2.6)

on
TM\setminus\{0\}
,
and the
covariant derivative
of a vector field
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X^{i}(\partial/\partial x^{i})
is defined as

D_{v}^{w}X:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Bigg\{v^{j}\frac{\partial X^{i}}{\partial x^{j}}(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(w)v^{j}X^{k}(x)\Bigg\}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\bigg|_{x}

for
v\in T_{x}M
with a
reference vector

w\in T_{x}M\setminus\{0\}
.
We remark that the functions
\Gamma^{i}_{jk}
in (
2.6
) are the coefficients of the
Chern
(–
Rund
)
connection
.

In the Lorentzian case,
g_{ij}
is constant in each tangent space (thus,
\Gamma^{i}_{jk}=\gamma^{i}_{jk}
) and the covariant derivative does not depend on the choice of a reference vector.
In the Lorentz–Finsler setting, the following class is worth considering.

Definition 2.4

(Berwald spacetimes)
.

A Finsler spacetime
(M,L)
is said to be of
Berwald type
(or called a
Berwald spacetime
) if
\Gamma^{i}_{jk}
is constant on the slit tangent space
T_{x}M\setminus\{0\}
for any
x
in the domain of every local coordinate system.

By definition, the covariant derivative on a Berwald spacetime is defined independently of the choice of a reference vector.
An important property of a Berwald spacetime is that, for any
C^{1}
-curve
\eta\colon[0,1]\longrightarrow M
whose velocity does not vanish, the parallel transport along
\eta
gives a linear isometry from
(T_{\eta(0)}M,L)
to
(T_{\eta(1)}M,L)
(see, e.g.,
[
59
, Proposition 6.5]
for the positive definite case).
In particular, all tangent spaces are mutually linearly isometric.
Examples of Berwald spacetimes include Lorentzian manifolds
and flat Lorentz–Finsler structures of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(every tangent space
T_{x}\mathbb{R}^{n}
is canonically isometric to
T_{0}\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\Gamma^{i}_{jk}=\gamma^{i}_{jk}=0
).
We refer to
[
25
,
27
,
26
,
15
,
24
,
33
]
for some mathematical and physical investigations on Berwald spacetimes,
and to
[
1
, Chapter 10]
,
[
59
, §§6.3, 10.2]
for the positive definite case.

Remark 2.5

(Metrizability)
.

In the positive definite case,
Szabó showed that a Finsler manifold of Berwald type
(M,F)
admits a Riemannian metric
h
whose Levi-Civita connection coincides with the Chern connection of
F
,
i.e., the Christoffel symbols of
h
coincide with
\Gamma^{i}_{jk}
of
F

(see
[
67
]
and
[
1
, Exercise 10.1.4]
).
This is called the (
Riemannian
)
metrizability theorem
.
It is not known whether the metrizability can be generalized to Berwald spacetimes.
In
[
24
]
, some counter-examples were constructed for Lorentz–Finsler structures defined only on a subset of
TM
.
Their discussion is not applicable to Lorentz–Finsler structures defined on all of
TM
as in Definition
2.1
.

The
geodesic equation
is written as
D^{\dot{\eta}}_{\dot{\eta}}\dot{\eta}\equiv 0
.
The
exponential map
is defined in the same way as the Riemannian case,
which is
C^{\infty}
on a neighborhood of the zero section only in Berwald spacetimes
(see, e.g.,
[
50
]
, as well as

[
1
, Exercise 5.3.5]
in the positive definite case).
For
C^{1}
-vector fields
X,Y
along a causal geodesic
\eta
, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigl[g_{\dot{\eta}}(X,Y)\bigr]=g_{\dot{\eta}}(D^{\dot{\eta}}_{\dot{\eta}}X,Y)+g_{\dot{\eta}}(X,D^{\dot{\eta}}_{\dot{\eta}}Y)

(2.7)

and, if
X
is nowhere vanishing,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigl[g_{X}(X,Y)\bigr]=g_{X}(D^{X}_{\dot{\eta}}X,Y)+g_{X}(X,D^{X}_{\dot{\eta}}Y)

(2.8)

(see, e.g.,
[
42
, (3.1), (3.2)]
).

A
C^{\infty}
-vector field
J
along a geodesic
\eta
is called a
Jacobi field

if it satisfies the
Jacobi equation

D^{\dot{\eta}}_{\dot{\eta}}D^{\dot{\eta}}_{\dot{\eta}}J+R_{\dot{\eta}}(J)=0
,
where

R_{v}(w):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}R^{i}_{j}(v)w^{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\bigg|_{x}

for
v,w\in T_{x}M
and

R^{i}_{j}(v):=\frac{\partial G^{i}}{\partial x^{j}}(v)-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigg\{\frac{\partial N^{i}_{j}}{\partial x^{k}}(v)v^{k}-\frac{\partial N^{i}_{j}}{\partial v^{k}}(v)G^{k}(v)+N^{i}_{k}(v)N^{k}_{j}(v)\bigg\}

is the
curvature tensor
.
A Jacobi field is also characterized as the variational vector field of a geodesic variation.

Note that
R_{v}(w)
is linear in
w
, thus
R_{v}\colon T_{x}M\longrightarrow T_{x}M
is an endomorphism
for each
v\in T_{x}M
.
For
v\in\overline{\Omega}_{x}
, we define the
Ricci curvature
of
v
as the trace of
R_{v}
:
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits(v):=\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}\nolimits(R_{v})
.
We remark that
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits(cv)=c^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits(v)
for
c>0
.

2.2
Legendre transforms and differential operators

In order to introduce the d’Alembertian, we consider the dual structure to
L
and the Legendre transform
(see
[
48
]
,
[
51
, §3.1]
,
[
43
, §4.4]
for further discussions, and
[
59
, §3.2]
for the positive definite case).
Define the
polar cone
to
\Omega_{x}\subset T_{x}M
by

\Omega^{*}_{x}:=\big\{\omega\in T_{x}^{*}M\mid\omega(v)<0\ \text{for all}\ v\in\overline{\Omega}_{x}\setminus\{0\}\big\}.

This is an open convex cone in
T_{x}^{*}M
.
For
\omega\in\overline{\Omega}{}^{*}_{x}
, we define

L^{*}(\omega):=-\frac{1}{2}\biggl(\sup_{v\in\Omega_{x}\cap F^{-1}(1)}\omega(v)\biggr)^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\inf_{v\in\Omega_{x}\cap F^{-1}(1)}\bigl(\omega(v)\bigr)^{2},

in other words, setting
F^{*}(\omega):=\sqrt{-2L^{*}(\omega)}
for
\omega\in\overline{\Omega}{}^{*}
,

F^{*}(\omega)=-\sup_{v\in\Omega_{x}\cap F^{-1}(1)}\omega(v).

Then we have the
reverse Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

\sqrt{4L^{*}(\omega)L(v)}=F^{*}(\omega)F(v)\leq-\omega(v)

(2.9)

for any
v\in\overline{\Omega}_{x}
and
\omega\in\overline{\Omega}{}^{*}_{x}
.

Definition 2.6

(Legendre transform)
.

Define the
Legendre transform

\mathscr{L}^{*}\colon\Omega^{*}_{x}\longrightarrow\Omega_{x}
as the map sending
\omega\in\Omega^{*}_{x}

to the unique element
v\in\Omega_{x}
satisfying
L(v)=L^{*}(\omega)=\omega(v)/2
.

We can continuously extend to
\mathscr{L}^{*}\colon\overline{\Omega}{}^{*}_{x}\longrightarrow\overline{\Omega}_{x}
(then
\mathscr{L}^{*}(0)=0
), and equality holds in (
2.9
) if and only if
v
and
\mathscr{L}^{*}(\omega)
are proportional (see
[
48
, Theorem 3]
).

A coordinate expression of the Legendre transform is given by

\mathscr{L}^{*}(\omega)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial L^{*}}{\partial\omega_{i}}(\omega)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial[(F^{*})^{2}]}{\partial\omega_{i}}(\omega)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}},

(2.10)

where
\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x^{i}
.
We set

g^{*}_{ij}(\omega):=\frac{\partial^{2}L^{*}}{\partial\omega_{i}\partial\omega_{j}}(\omega)

and note that, by the homogeneous function theorem,

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial g^{*}_{ij}}{\partial\omega_{k}}(\omega)\omega_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial g^{*}_{ik}}{\partial\omega_{j}}(\omega)\omega_{k}=0

(2.11)

for all
i,j=1,\ldots,n
.
We also remark that
\mathscr{L}^{*}\colon\Omega^{*}_{x}\longrightarrow\Omega_{x}
is a bijection and
(g^{*}_{ij}(\omega))
is the inverse matrix of
(g_{ij}(\mathscr{L}^{*}(\omega)))
(see
[
48
, Corollary 4]
).

A continuous function
f\colon M\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called a
time function

if
f(x)<f(y)
for all
x,y\in M
with
x<y
.
A
C^{1}
-function
f\colon M\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to be
temporal
if
-\mathrm{d}f(x)\in\Omega^{*}_{x}
for all
x\in M
.
Note that a temporal function is a time function.

For a temporal function
f\colon M\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
define the
gradient vector
of
-f
at
x\in M
by

\nabla(-f)(x):=\mathscr{L}^{*}\bigl(-\mathrm{d}f(x)\bigr)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g^{*}_{ij}\bigl(-\mathrm{d}f(x)\bigr)\frac{\partial(-f)}{\partial x^{j}}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\in\Omega_{x}.

(2.12)

Observe from (
2.2
) and (
2.10
) that

g_{\nabla(-f)}(\nabla(-f)(x),v)=-\mathrm{d}f(v)
holds for any
v\in T_{x}M
.
For a
C^{2}
-temporal function
f\colon M\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
x\in M
, its
Hessian

\nabla^{2}(-f)\colon T_{x}M\longrightarrow T_{x}M
is defined by

\nabla^{2}(-f)(v):=D^{\nabla(-f)}_{v}\bigl[\nabla(-f)\bigr].

This spacetime Hessian has the following symmetry (see, e.g.,
[
43
, Lemma 4.12]
):

g_{\nabla(-f)}\bigl(\nabla^{2}(-f)(v),w\bigr)=g_{\nabla(-f)}\bigl(v,\nabla^{2}(-f)(w)\bigr)

(2.13)

for all
v,w\in T_{x}M
.
Then we define the
d’Alembertian
(or the
spacetime Laplacian
)
as the trace of the Hessian:

\square(-f):=\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}\nolimits\bigl[\nabla^{2}(-f)\bigr].

We remark that the d’Alembertian
\square
is not elliptic but hyperbolic,
and is nonlinear in our Finsler setting (since the Legendre transform is nonlinear).

Remark 2.7
.

For
v\in T_{x}M
and the geodesic
\eta\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\longrightarrow M
with
\dot{\eta}(0)=v
,
the second order derivative
(-f\circ\eta)^{\prime\prime}(0)
does not coincide with
g_{\nabla(-f)}(\nabla^{2}(-f)(v),v)
in general.
They coincide in Berwald spacetimes thanks to the fiberwise constancy of the connection coefficients
\Gamma^{i}_{jk}

(see
[
59
, §12.1]
for the positive definite case).

2.3
Weighted Finsler spacetimes

There are two ways of putting a
weight
on a Finsler spacetime
(M,L)
.
The first one, reasonable from the point of view of geometric analysis, is to choose a positive
C^{\infty}
-measure
\mathfrak{m}
on
M
.
The other possibility is to employ a
C^{\infty}
-function
\Psi\colon M\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
They are mutually equivalent in the Lorentzian case via the relation
\mathfrak{m}=\mathrm{e}^{-\Psi}\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}\nolimits_{g}
, where
\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}\nolimits_{g}
is the volume measure induced from the Lorentzian metric
g
.
In the Lorentz–Finsler setting, however, we do not have a canonical measure like
\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}\nolimits_{g}
(see
[
56
,
59
]
for the positive definite case).
In this article, we consider the first way because we will make use of the linear divergence operator induced by a measure.

Remark 2.8
.

One way to unify the above two cases is to consider a general
0
-homogeneous function

\psi\colon\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{0\}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
as in
[
42
,
43
]
.
However, even a weighted Laplacian on a Riemannian manifold corresponding to such a general function
\psi
is yet to be developed (see the end of
[
43
, §3.3]
).

Define a function
\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\colon\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{0\}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
associated with a positive
C^{\infty}
-measure
\mathfrak{m}
on
M
(i.e., its density function is positive and
C^{\infty}
in each local chart) by

\mathfrak{m}(\mathrm{d}x)=\mathrm{e}^{-\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\eta}}\sqrt{-\det\bigl[\bigl(g_{ij}(\dot{\eta})\bigr)\bigr]}\,\mathrm{d}x^{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x^{n}

along causal geodesics
\eta
.
In other words, given a causal vector field
V
on an open set
U\subset M

such that every integral curve of
V
is a geodesic,
we have
\mathfrak{m}=\mathrm{e}^{-\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ V}\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}\nolimits_{g_{V}}
on
U
.

Definition 2.9

(Weighted Ricci curvature)
.

Given
v\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{0\}
,
let
\eta\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\longrightarrow M
be the causal geodesic with
\dot{\eta}(0)=v
.
Then we define the
weighted Ricci curvature
by

\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}(v):=\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits(v)+(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\eta})^{\prime\prime}(0)-\frac{(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\eta})^{\prime}(0)^{2}}{N-n}

for
N\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{n\}
.
We also define
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{\infty}(v):=\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits(v)+(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\eta})^{\prime\prime}(0)
,

\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{n}(v):=\lim_{N\downarrow n}\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}(v)
, and
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}(0):=0
.

We remark that, in the positive definite case,
(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\eta})^{\prime}(0)
is called the
\mathbf{S}
-curvature
at
v
associated with
\mathfrak{m}
(see
[
59
,
65
]
).

We will say that
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}\geq K
holds
in timelike directions
for some
K\in\mathbb{R}

if we have
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}(v)\geq KF^{2}(v)=-2KL(v)
for all
v\in\Omega
(recall (
2.3
) for
F
).
By definition, we have the monotonicity

\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{n}(v)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}(v)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{\infty}(v)\leq\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N^{\prime}}(v)

for
N\in(n,\infty)
and
N^{\prime}\in(-\infty,n)
.
The following notion was introduced in
[
42
]
.

Definition 2.10

(
\epsilon
-range)
.

Given
N\in(-\infty,1]\cup[n,\infty]
, we consider
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
in the following

\epsilon
-range
:

\epsilon=0\,\text{ for }N=1,\qquad|\epsilon|<\sqrt{\frac{N-1}{N-n}}\,\text{ for }N\neq 1,n,\qquad\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}\,\text{ for }N=n.

(2.14)

The associated constant
c=c(N,\epsilon)
is defined as

c(N,\epsilon):=\frac{1}{n}\bigg(1-\epsilon^{2}\frac{N-n}{N-1}\bigg)>0\,\text{ for }N\neq 1,\qquad c(1,0):=\frac{1}{n}.

(2.15)

Note that
\epsilon=1
is admissible only for
N\in[n,\infty)
, while
\epsilon=0
is always admissible.
By the
\epsilon
-range, we can unify results for constant and variable curvature bounds
into a single framework.
See
[
42
]
for singularity theorems and
[
43
]
for comparison theorems.

A d’Alembertian comparison theorem with
\epsilon
-range was established in
[
43
]
as follows.
For a temporal function
f\colon M\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
x\in M
, we define the
weighted d’Alembertian
associated with
\mathfrak{m}
by

\square_{\mathfrak{m}}(-f)(x):=\square(-f)(x)-(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\eta})^{\prime}(0),

(2.16)

where
\eta\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\longrightarrow M
is the timelike geodesic with
\dot{\eta}(0)=\nabla(-f)(x)
.
One can alternatively define
\square_{\mathfrak{m}}(-f)
as
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}(\nabla(-f))
; the
divergence

\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}
with respect to the measure
\mathfrak{m}
(independent of
L
) is given in coordinates by

\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}V:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg\{\frac{\partial V^{i}}{\partial x^{i}}+V^{i}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x^{i}}\bigg\}

(2.17)

for differentiable vector fields
V
, where
\mathfrak{m}=\mathrm{e}^{\Phi}\,\mathrm{d}x^{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x^{n}
.
For test functions
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(M)
of compact support, we have the integration by parts formula:

\int_{M}\phi\cdot\square_{\mathfrak{m}}(-f)\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}=-\int_{M}\mathrm{d}\phi\bigl(\nabla(-f)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}.

The equivalence of these two definitions of
\square_{\mathfrak{m}}(-f)
can be seen, for example, in the same way as the positive definite case (see
[
59
, Lemma 12.4]
).

The
d’Alembertian
(
Laplacian
)
comparison theorem
(
[
43
, Theorem 5.8]
,
[
44
, Theorem 3.1]
) for nonnegatively curved spacetimes asserts the following.

Theorem 2.11

(d’Alembertian comparison theorem)
.

Let
(M,L,\mathfrak{m})
be a weighted Finsler spacetime of dimension
n\geq 2
,

N\in(-\infty,1]\cup[n,\infty]
, and
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
belong to the
\epsilon
-range (
2.14
).
Suppose that
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}\geq 0
holds in timelike directions.
Then, for any
z\in M
, the time separation function
f(x):=\tau(z,x)
satisfies

\square_{\mathfrak{m}}(-f)\big(\eta(t)\big)\leq\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\eta}(t))}\bigg(c\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\eta}(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)^{-1}

(2.18)

for any maximizing timelike geodesic
\eta\colon[0,T)\longrightarrow M
of unit speed

(
i.e.,
F(\dot{\eta})\equiv 1)
emanating from
z

and any
t\in(0,T)
, where
c=c(N,\epsilon)
as in (
2.15
).

In fact, in
[
43
]
, a general
0
-homogeneous function was considered as a weight (recall Remark
2.8
).
(Recall also that
\dim M=n+1
in
[
43
,
44
]
.)
We refer to
[
17
]
and
[
69
]
for the weighted Lorentzian case.

We will also need the reverse version of Theorem
2.11
.
The
reverse Lorentz–Finsler structure
of
L
is defined as
\overleftarrow{L}(v):=L(-v)
,
and we put an arrow
\leftarrow
on a quantity associated with
\overleftarrow{L}
.
The Lorentz–Finsler manifold
(M,\overleftarrow{L})
is time oriented by
-X
,
so that
\overleftarrow{\Omega}=-\Omega
.
The corresponding weighted d’Alembertian satisfies
\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m}}f=-\square_{\mathfrak{m}}(-f)

for temporal functions
f

(since
\overleftarrow{\psi}_{\mathfrak{m}}(v)=\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(-v)
).
We also remark that
\overleftarrow{\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits}_{N}(v)=\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}(-v)
for
v\in\overleftarrow{\Omega}
, and hence
the timelike curvature bound
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}\geq 0
is equivalent to
\overleftarrow{\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits}_{N}\geq 0
.

Corollary 2.12

(Reverse version)
.

Let
(M,L,\mathfrak{m})
,
N
and
\epsilon
be as in Theorem
2.11
.
Then, for any
z\in M
, the function
\overline{f}(x):=\tau(x,z)
satisfies

\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m}}(-\overline{f})\big(\eta(-t)\big)=-\square_{\mathfrak{m}}\overline{f}\big(\eta(-t)\big)\leq\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\eta}(-t))}\bigg(c\int_{-t}^{0}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\eta}(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)^{-1}

(2.19)

for any maximizing timelike geodesic
\eta\colon(-T,0]\longrightarrow M
of unit speed with
\eta(0)=z

and any
t\in(0,T)
.

Note that the reverse curve
\bar{\eta}(t):=\eta(-t)
is a maximizing timelike geodesic of unit speed with respect to
\overleftarrow{L}
.
In the application of the d’Alembertian comparison theorem to splitting theorems,
we need to require that the right-hand side of (
2.18
) (and (
2.19
)) tends to
0
.
To be precise, we will assume the following completeness condition introduced in
[
42
]
, adapted to our usage in splitting theorems.

Definition 2.13

(
\epsilon
-completeness)
.

A weighted Finsler spacetime
(M,L,\mathfrak{m})
is said to be
future timelike
\epsilon
-complete
if, given any future complete maximizing timelike geodesic
\eta\colon[0,\infty)\longrightarrow M
, there is a neighborhood
U
of
\eta(0)
such that, for any sequence
t_{k}\to\infty
admitting maximizing geodesics
\zeta_{k}\colon[0,\tau(x,\eta(t_{k}))]\longrightarrow M
from
x\in U
to
\eta(t_{k})
of unit speed, we have

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\tau(x,\eta(t_{k}))}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\zeta}_{k}(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s=\infty.

The
past timelike
\epsilon
-completeness
will mean the future timelike
\epsilon
-completeness of
(M,\overleftarrow{L},\mathfrak{m})
.
We say that
(M,L,\mathfrak{m})
is
timelike
\epsilon
-complete
if it is both future and past timelike
\epsilon
-complete.

Note that
\tau(x,\eta(t_{k}))\to\infty
by the reverse triangle inequality, and thus the timelike
1
-completeness always holds.
Timelike
0
-completeness recovers the
\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}
-completeness introduced in
[
71
]

in the Riemannian case (see
[
69
]
for the Lorentzian case).
A typical situation is that
\epsilon<1
and
\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}
is bounded above (see
[
17
]
, where
N=\infty
).

In the timelike geodesically complete case (see Definition
4.1
), for our purpose, it is in fact sufficient to assume

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\eta}(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s=\infty

(2.20)

for every future complete timelike geodesic
\eta\colon[0,\infty)\longrightarrow M
.

3
The
p
-d’Alembert operator

This section is devoted to a key new ingredient, the
p
-d’Alembertian, which is a modification of the d’Alembertian (
2.16
) in a similar manner to the
p
-Laplacian in the positive definite case (for
p>1
), but with
p<1
.
We refer to
[
6
,
9
,
10
]
and
[
46
,
54
]
for the (possibly singular) Lorentzian setting.

We first introduce the
q
-Lagrangian

L_{q}
as in
[
46
]
and
[
12
]
with
q\in(0,1)
(see also
[
66
]
for the case
q=1
and
[
51
, §3.1]
for a more general study including the Finsler case).
Define
L_{q}\colon TM\longrightarrow(-\infty,0]\cup\{\infty\}
as

L_{q}(v):=\begin{cases}-\dfrac{1}{q}\bigl(-2L(v)\bigr)^{q/2}&\text{if }v\in\overline{\Omega},\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Observe that
L_{q}(v)=-F(v)^{q}/q
for
v\in\overline{\Omega}
.
We remark that
L
is not convex in the radial direction,
while for
L_{q}
the following holds (see, e.g.,
[
12
, Lemma 3.1]
):

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
q\in(0,1)
and
x\in M
, the Lagrangian
L_{q}
is convex on
T_{x}M
and strictly convex on
\Omega_{x}
.

The convex dual
H_{p}\colon T^{*}M\longrightarrow[0,\infty]
of
L_{q}
,
called the
p
-Hamiltonian
, is given by

H_{p}(\omega):=\sup_{v\in TM}\bigl(\omega(v)-L_{q}(v)\bigr),

where
q^{-1}+p^{-1}=1
and
\omega(v)
is the canonical pairing.
Note that
p<0
and we have

H_{p}(\omega)=\begin{cases}-\dfrac{1}{p}\bigl(-2L^{*}(\omega)\bigr)^{p/2}&\text{if }\omega\in\Omega^{*},\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(3.1)

Here and henceforth, we fix a choice of
q\in(0,1)
and corresponding dual
p<0
as above.

Remark 3.2

(Ranges of
q,p
)
.

One can equally well consider
q<0
and thus
p\in(0,1)
.
The convexity properties of the Lagrangian and the corresponding Hamiltonian remain unchanged (cf.
[
51
, Example 3.1]
); moreover, the ellipticity of the
p
-d’Alembertian is also preserved, see
[
6
,
10
]
for details. Note that if
q\in(0,1)
and thus
p<0
,
L_{q}(v)=0
for
v\in\partial\Omega
, while
H_{p}(\omega)=\infty
for
\omega\in\partial\Omega^{*}
. These behaviors at the boundary become interchanged if one instead considers
q<0
and thus
p\in(0,1)
. For the purposes of our proof of the splitting theorem, we restrict ourselves to a fixed choice of
q\in(0,1)
and
p<0
, and will utilize the ellipticity of the
p
-d’Alembertian for this
p
to obtain our desired results for the Busemann functions. There is no advantage in varying
q
and
p
throughout the proof.

Define the
p
-energy functional
for a temporal function
f
,
associated to the measure
\mathfrak{m}
, by

\mathcal{E}_{p}(-f):=\int_{M}H_{p}(-\mathrm{d}f)\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}=-\frac{1}{p}\int_{M}\bigl(-2L^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)\bigr)^{p/2}\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}=-\frac{1}{p}\int_{M}F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}.

Then,
\mathcal{E}_{p}
is convex in the cone consisting of temporal functions.
Thus, the corresponding
p
-d’Alembertian
(or the
spacetime
p
-Laplacian
) defined by

\begin{split}\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}(-f)&:=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}\bigl(F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)^{p-2}\cdot\nabla(-f)\bigr)\\
&=F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)^{p-2}\cdot\square_{\mathfrak{m}}(-f)+\mathrm{d}\bigl[F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)^{p-2}\bigr]\bigl(\nabla(-f)\bigr)\end{split}

(3.2)

is elliptic on smooth temporal functions.
In fact, for any smooth function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(M)
of compact support,
f+t\phi
is temporal provided that
|t|
is sufficiently small. Since the integrand is smooth in
t
and uniformly bounded on the compact support of
\phi
for small
|t|
, by Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem and (
2.10
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{p}(-f-t\phi)-\mathcal{E}_{p}(-f)}{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}\bigl(-2L^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)\bigr)^{(p-2)/2}\cdot 2\mathrm{d}\phi\bigl(\nabla(-f)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}

\displaystyle=-\int_{M}\mathrm{d}\phi\bigl(F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)^{p-2}\cdot\nabla(-f)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\phi\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}\bigl(F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)^{p-2}\cdot\nabla(-f)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}.

Hence,

\mathcal{E}_{p}(-f-t\phi)=\mathcal{E}_{p}(-f)-t\int_{M}(-\phi)\cdot\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}(-f)\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}+o(t),

which identifies
\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}
as the Euler–Lagrange operator associated with
\mathcal{E}_{p}
.

Remark 3.3
.

In the right-hand side of (
3.2
), we observe from (
2.7
) or (
2.8
) that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bigl[F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)^{p-2}\bigr]\bigl(\nabla(-f)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}\Bigl[\Bigl(-g_{\nabla(-f)}\bigl(\nabla(-f),\nabla(-f)\bigr)\Bigr)^{(p-2)/2}\Bigr]\bigl(\nabla(-f)\bigr)

\displaystyle=-(p-2)F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)^{p-4}g_{\nabla(-f)}\Bigl(D^{\nabla(-f)}_{\nabla(-f)}\bigl[\nabla(-f)\bigr],\nabla(-f)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=-(p-2)F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)^{p-4}g_{\nabla(-f)}\Bigl(\nabla^{2}(-f)\bigl(\nabla(-f)\bigr),\nabla(-f)\Bigr)

(a similar calculation in the positive definite case can be found in
[
60
, §3.1]
).
Hence,

\frac{\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}(-f)}{F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)^{p-2}}=\square_{\mathfrak{m}}(-f)-(p-2)g_{\nabla(-f)}\biggl(\nabla^{2}(-f)\biggl(\frac{\nabla(-f)}{F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)}\biggr),\frac{\nabla(-f)}{F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)}\biggr).

(3.3)

In a suitable local chart
(x^{i})_{i=1}^{n}
around
x\in M
such that
(\partial/\partial x^{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is orthonormal with respect to
g_{\nabla(-f)}
with
\partial/\partial x^{1}=\nabla(-f)/F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)
at
x
,
recalling the coordinate expression of
\nabla(-f)
in (
2.12
), we can write down the second order part of (
3.3
) at
x
as

-\frac{\partial^{2}(-f)}{(\partial x^{1})^{2}}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}(-f)}{(\partial x^{i})^{2}}-(p-2)\frac{\partial^{2}(-f)}{(\partial x^{1})^{2}}=(1-p)\frac{\partial^{2}(-f)}{(\partial x^{1})^{2}}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}(-f)}{(\partial x^{i})^{2}}.

Therefore,
\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}
is elliptic exactly when
p<1
.
We also remark that, in the positive definite case, the
p
-Laplacian
\Delta_{p}f:=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}(F^{*}(\mathrm{d}f)^{p-2}\cdot\nabla f)
is usually considered for
p\in(1,\infty)
.

For
f(x):=\tau(z,x)
and
\overline{f}(x):=\tau(x,z)
, if we replace
\square_{\mathfrak{m}}
with
\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}

in Theorem
2.11
as well as Corollary
2.12
,
since
F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)\equiv 1
(resp.
F^{*}(\mathrm{d}\overline{f})\equiv 1
) on
I^{+}(z)
(resp.
I^{-}(z)
) outside the future (resp. past) timelike cut locus, we immediately obtain the following.

Lemma 3.4

(
p
-d’Alembertian comparison estimates)
.

Let
(M,L,\mathfrak{m})
,
N
and
\epsilon
be as in Theorem
2.11
.
Then, for any
z\in M
, the function
f(x):=\tau(z,x)
satisfies

\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}(-f)\big(\eta(t)\big)\leq\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\eta}(t))}\bigg(c\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\eta}(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)^{-1}

(3.4)

for any maximizing timelike geodesic
\eta\colon[0,T)\longrightarrow M
of unit speed
with
\eta(0)=z
and any
t\in(0,T)
.
Moreover, the function
\overline{f}(x):=\tau(x,z)
satisfies

\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m},p}(-\overline{f})\big(\eta(-t)\big)=-\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}\overline{f}\big(\eta(-t)\big)\leq\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\eta}(-t))}\bigg(c\int_{-t}^{0}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\eta}(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\bigg)^{-1}

(3.5)

for any maximizing timelike geodesic
\eta\colon(-T,0]\longrightarrow M
of unit speed with
\eta(0)=z

and any
t\in(0,T)
.

Remark 3.5

(Unweighted setting)
.

For completeness, we also discuss the
p
-d’Alembertian in the unweighted situation.
Let us set

\nabla_{p}(-f):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial H_{p}}{\partial\omega_{i}}(-\mathrm{d}f)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}=F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)^{p-2}\cdot\nabla(-f).

Then, it is natural to define

\begin{split}\square_{p}(-f)&:=\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}\nolimits\Bigl[D^{\nabla(-f)}\bigl[\nabla_{p}(-f)\bigr]\Bigr]\\
&=F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)^{p-2}\cdot\square(-f)+\mathrm{d}\bigl[F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}f)^{p-2}\bigr]\bigl(\nabla(-f)\bigr).\end{split}

Note that
\nabla_{2}(-f)=\nabla(-f)
and
\square_{2}(-f)=\square(-f)
.

4
Busemann functions

In this section, we summarize some properties of the Busemann function associated with a timelike straight line established in
[
44
]
, under the assumption of timelike geodesic completeness, along the lines of
[
29
]
(see also
[
3
, Chapter 14]
).
The globally hyperbolic case will be discussed later in Subsection
6.3
.

4.1
Rays and generalized co-rays

Definition 4.1

(Timelike geodesic completeness)
.

A Finsler spacetime
(M,L)
is said to be
future timelike geodesically complete

if any timelike geodesic
\eta\colon[0,1]\longrightarrow M
can be extended to a geodesic

\tilde{\eta}\colon[0,\infty)\longrightarrow M
.
We say that
(M,L)
is
timelike geodesically complete

if both
(M,L)
and
(M,\overleftarrow{L})
are future timelike geodesically complete
(in other words, the above
\eta
is extended to a geodesic
\tilde{\eta}\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow M
).

A future inextendible causal geodesic
\eta\colon[0,T)\longrightarrow M
(with
0<T\leq\infty
) is called a
ray

if each of its segments is maximizing, i.e.,

\mathbf{L}(\eta|_{[a,b]})=\tau(\eta(a),\eta(b))
for all
0\leq a\leq b<T
.
When
\eta
is future complete (i.e.,
T=\infty
, which is always the case if
(M,L)
is future timelike geodesically complete), we define

I^{-}(\eta):=\bigcup_{t>0}I^{-}\big(\eta(t)\big),\qquad I(\eta):=I^{+}\big(\eta(0)\big)\cap I^{-}(\eta).

(4.1)

Then
I(\eta)
is an open set including
\eta((0,\infty))
, and
\tau(x,y)<\infty
for all
x,y\in I(\eta)
with
x\leq y
(this follows from the reverse triangle inequality as well as the fact that
\tau(\eta(a),\eta(b))<\infty
for all
0\leq a\leq b<\infty
).

In the rest of this section, we suppose that
(M,L)
is future timelike geodesically complete.
Let
\eta\colon[0,\infty)\longrightarrow M
be a timelike ray.
Rays asymptotic to a given ray, called co-rays, play an important role in splitting theorems.
In the non-globally hyperbolic case, due to the possible absence of maximizing curves, we need to consider also generalized co-rays (see
[
4
,
5
,
23
]
).

We recall that a sequence
\zeta_{k}\colon[0,a_{k}]\longrightarrow M
of causal curves is said to be
limit maximizing
if

\mathbf{L}(\zeta_{k})\geq\tau(\zeta_{k}(0),\zeta_{k}(a_{k}))-\varepsilon_{k}
for some positive sequence
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
(see, e.g.,
[
23
, p. 210]
).
Then, as a consequence of a limit curve argument, if a limit maximizing sequence
\zeta_{k}\colon[0,a_{k}]\longrightarrow M
of causal curves satisfies
\zeta_{k}(0)\to x
and
\tau(\zeta_{k}(0),\zeta_{k}(a_{k}))\to\infty
, then there exists a subsequence of
(\zeta_{k})
that, up to reparametrizations by arclength with respect to an auxiliary complete Riemannian metric on
M
, converges uniformly on compact subintervals of
[0,\infty)
to a ray
\zeta\colon[0,\infty)\longrightarrow M
emanating from
x
(see
[
44
, Lemma 4.4]
,
[
29
, Lemma 2.4]
).

Definition 4.2

(Generalized co-rays)
.

Let
x\in I(\eta)
.
If a limit maximizing sequence of causal curves
\zeta_{k}\colon[0,a_{k}]\longrightarrow M
satisfies

\zeta_{k}(0)\to x
and
\zeta_{k}(a_{k})=\eta(t_{k})
for some
t_{k}>0
with
t_{k}\to\infty
,
then its limit curve
\zeta\colon[0,\infty)\longrightarrow M
is called a
generalized co-ray
of
\eta
.
If each
\zeta_{k}
is maximizing, then we call
\zeta
a
co-ray
of
\eta
.
A co-ray
\zeta
with
\zeta_{k}(0)=x
for all
k
is called an
asymptote
of
\eta
.

The limit curve
\zeta
is indeed a ray as we mentioned above.
To avoid convergence to a lightlike ray, we introduce the following condition.

Definition 4.3

(Generalized timelike co-ray condition (GTCC))
.

Let
x\in I(\eta)
.
We say that the
generalized timelike co-ray condition

(
GTCC
for short) for
\eta
holds at
x

if any generalized co-ray of
\eta
emanating from
x
is timelike.

The GTCC implies the following fine properties (see
[
44
, Lemmas 4.8, 4.9]
,
[
29
, Lemmas 3.3, 3.4]
).

Lemma 4.4

(Existence of maximizing geodesics)
.

Suppose that the GTCC for
\eta
holds at some
x\in I(\eta)
.
Then, there exist a neighborhood
U
of
x
and
T>0
such that, for any
z\in U
and
t>T
, there exists a maximizing timelike geodesic from
z
to
\eta(t)
.
Moreover, every
z\in U
admits a timelike asymptote of
\eta
emanating from
z
.

The GTCC holds on a neighborhood of
\eta
as follows (see
[
44
, Proposition 4.10]
,
[
29
, Proposition 5.1, Corollary 5.2]
).

Proposition 4.5

(GTCC near rays)
.

Any generalized co-ray emanating from
\eta(a)
with
a>0

necessarily coincides with
\eta
.
In particular, the GTCC holds on an open set including
\eta((0,\infty))
.

4.2
Busemann functions for rays

We continue to assume future timelike geodesic completeness,
and let
\eta\colon[0,\infty)\longrightarrow M
be a timelike ray of unit speed (
F(\dot{\eta})\equiv 1
).
We introduce a central ingredient of the proof of the splitting theorem.

Definition 4.6

(Busemann functions)
.

Define the
Busemann function

\mathbf{b}_{\eta}\colon M\longrightarrow[-\infty,\infty]

associated with
\eta
by

\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(x):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}(x),\qquad\text{where}\,\ \mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}(x):=t-\tau\bigl(x,\eta(t)\bigr).

The limit above always exists in
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
.
Precisely, if
x\not\in I^{-}(\eta)
, then
\tau(x,\eta(t))=0
for all
t
and hence
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(x)=\infty
.
If
x\in I^{-}(\eta)
, then the reverse triangle inequality implies that, for large
s<t
,

\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}(x)\leq t-\big\{\tau\big(x,\eta(s)\big)+\tau\big(\eta(s),\eta(t)\big)\big\}=\mathbf{b}_{\eta,s}(x).

Hence,
\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}(x)
is non-increasing in
t
and converges to
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(x)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
.
One can also see
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
for
x\in I(\eta)
since

\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}(x)\geq t+\tau\bigl(\eta(0),x\bigr)-\tau\bigl(\eta(0),\eta(t)\bigr)=\tau\bigl(\eta(0),x\bigr).

It follows from the reverse triangle inequality that
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(y)\geq\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(x)+\tau(x,y)
for
x\leq y
.
Moreover, since
\tau
is lower semi-continuous,
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
is upper semi-continuous in
I^{-}(\eta)
.
We summarize some further fundamental properties of
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
along timelike asymptotes.

Lemma 4.7

(Properties of
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
along asymptotes)
.

Let
\zeta\colon[0,\infty)\longrightarrow M
be a timelike asymptote of
\eta
of unit speed
with
x:=\zeta(0)\in I(\eta)
.

(i)

For each
t>0
,

\rho(z):=\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(x)+\mathbf{b}_{\zeta,t}(z)=\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(x)+t-\tau\big(z,\zeta(t)\big)

is an
upper support function
for
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
at
x
in the sense that

\rho\geq\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
on
I^{+}(\eta(0))\cap I^{-}(\zeta(t))
and
\rho(x)=\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(x)
.

(ii)

We have
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(\zeta(t))=\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(x)+t
for all
t\geq 0
.
In particular,
I(\zeta)\subset I(\eta)
holds.

(iii)

If
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
is differentiable at
x
, then we have
\zeta(t)=\exp_{x}(t\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})(x))
, and
\zeta
is the unique timelike asymptote of
\eta
of unit speed emanating from
x
.
In particular,
F(\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})(x))=1
holds.

(iv)

For each
t>0
,
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
is differentiable at
\zeta(t)
with
\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})(\zeta(t))=\dot{\zeta}(t)
.

Proof.

We refer to
[
44
, Lemma 5.2]
(and
[
22
, §2.2]
,
[
29
, Lemma 2.5, Proposition 2.6]
) for (
i
) and (
ii
).
Then, (
iii
) is a direct consequence of (
ii
).
Indeed, if
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(v)=\mathrm{d}\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(w)=1
for distinct timelike vectors
v,w\in\Omega_{x}\cap F^{-1}(1)
, then we have
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{b}_{\eta}((v+w)/2)=1
and
F((v+w)/2)>1
.
However, this is a contradiction since
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(y)\geq\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(x)+\tau(x,y)
for
x\leq y
.
Finally, (
iv
) is seen as in
[
22
, §2.2]
, by noticing that
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(\zeta(T))-\tau(\cdot,\zeta(T))
and
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(x)+\tau(x,\cdot)
are upper and lower support functions for
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
at
\zeta(t)
for
T>t
, respectively.
∎

Under the GTCC,
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
is in fact differentiable almost everywhere near
\eta
(see
[
44
, Theorem 5.3]
,
[
29
, Theorem 3.7]
).

Theorem 4.8

(Regularity of
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
)
.

Assume that the GTCC for
\eta
holds at
x\in I(\eta)
.
Then,
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
is Lipschitz continuous, and hence differentiable almost everywhere, on a neighborhood of
x
.

We remark that the Lipschitz continuity is understood with respect to an auxiliary Riemannian metric.

Recall that a function
u
defined on an open set
U\subset M
is said to be
semiconvex
with respect to a (background) Riemannian metric
h
if there exists a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that for every
x\in U
,

\liminf_{w\to 0}\frac{u(\exp_{x}^{h}(w))+u(\exp_{x}^{h}(-w))-2u(x)}{h(w,w)}\geq c,

where
w\in T_{x}M
and
\exp^{h}
denotes the exponential map for
h
.
We say that
u
is
semiconcave
if
-u
is semiconvex.
With the help of the semiconvexity of the time separation function in
[
12
, Proposition 3.9]
(along the lines of
[
46
, Proposition 3.4]
), we have a uniform estimate on the semiconcavity of
\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}
(cf.
[
10
, Proposition 5]
) as follows.

Lemma 4.9

(Equi-semiconcavity of
\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}
)
.

Fix a smooth Riemannian metric
h
of
M
, and let
W\subset I(\eta)
be an open set including
\eta((0,\infty))
as in Proposition
4.5
.
Then, for each
x_{0}\in W
, there exist a neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
,
T>0
and a constant
C=C(x_{0},T)\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}(\exp^{h}_{x}(-rv))+\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}(\exp^{h}_{x}(rv))-2\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}(x)}{r^{2}h(v,v)}\leq C

for all
x\in U
,
v\in T_{x}M\setminus\{0\}
and
t\geq 2T
.

Proof.

Given
x_{0}\in W
, take a neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
and
T>0
as in Lemma
4.4
.
We fix
x\in U
and
t\geq 2T
, and give a modification of Lemma
4.7
(
i
) to obtain an upper support function of
\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}
.
Let
\zeta\colon[0,\tau(x,\eta(t))]\longrightarrow M
be a maximizing geodesic from
x
to
\eta(t)
.
Note that

\tau\bigl(x,\eta(t)\bigr)\geq\tau\bigl(x,\eta(T)\bigr)+\tau\bigl(\eta(T),\eta(t)\bigr)\geq t-T\geq T

by the reverse triangle inequality.
Then, we infer that, for
z
close to
x
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}(z)-\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}(x)

\displaystyle=\tau\bigl(x,\eta(t)\bigr)-\tau\bigl(z,\eta(t)\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\tau\bigl(x,\eta(t)\bigr)-\tau\bigl(z,\zeta(T)\bigr)-\tau\bigl(\zeta(T),\eta(t)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\tau\bigl(x,\zeta(T)\bigr)-\tau\bigl(z,\zeta(T)\bigr).

Thus,

\rho(z):=\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}(x)+T-\tau\bigl(z,\zeta(T)\bigr)

is an upper support function of
\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}
at
x
.

Hence, for
\gamma(r):=\exp^{h}_{x}(rv)
with
v\in T_{x}M
such that
h(v,v)=1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}(\gamma(-r))+\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}(\gamma(r))-2\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}(x)}{r^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\rho(\gamma(-r))+\rho(\gamma(r))-2\rho(x)}{r^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\tau(\gamma(-r),\zeta(T))+\tau(\gamma(r),\zeta(T))-2\tau(x,\zeta(T))}{r^{2}},

provided that
r>0
is sufficiently small.
The right-hand side is bounded from above as follows.
Let
P
be the vector field along the reparametrized geodesic
s\longmapsto\zeta(sT)
,
s\in[0,1]
, such that
P(0)=v
and
D^{\dot{\zeta}}_{\dot{\zeta}}P\equiv 0
.
We also consider

V(s):=(1-s)P(s),\qquad V^{\perp}(s):=V(s)+g_{\dot{\zeta}}\bigl(V(s),\dot{\zeta}(sT)\bigr)\dot{\zeta}(sT).

Observe that
g_{\dot{\zeta}}(V^{\perp}(s),\dot{\zeta}(sT))=0
and, by (
2.7
) or (
2.8
),

D^{\dot{\zeta}}_{\dot{\zeta}}V^{\perp}(s)=-P(s)+g_{\dot{\zeta}}\bigl(-P(s),\dot{\zeta}(sT)\bigr)\dot{\zeta}(sT)=:-P^{\perp}(s).

Then, it follows from the calculation in
[
12
, Proposition 3.9]
that

\displaystyle\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\tau(\gamma(-r),\zeta(T))+\tau(\gamma(r),\zeta(T))-2\tau(x,\zeta(T))}{r^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq-T\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl\{g_{\dot{\zeta}}(D^{\dot{\zeta}}_{\dot{\zeta}}V^{\perp},D^{\dot{\zeta}}_{\dot{\zeta}}V^{\perp})-g_{\dot{\zeta}}\bigl(R_{\dot{\zeta}}(V^{\perp}),V^{\perp}\bigr)\Bigr\}\,\mathrm{d}s+g_{\dot{\zeta}}\bigl(D^{\dot{\zeta}}_{\dot{\gamma}}\dot{\gamma}(0),\dot{\zeta}(0)\bigr)

\displaystyle=-T\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl\{g_{\dot{\zeta}}(P^{\perp},P^{\perp})-(1-s)^{2}g_{\dot{\zeta}}\bigl(R_{\dot{\zeta}}(P^{\perp}),P^{\perp}\bigr)\Bigr\}\,\mathrm{d}s+g_{\dot{\zeta}}\bigl(D^{\dot{\zeta}}_{\dot{\gamma}}\dot{\gamma}(0),\dot{\zeta}(0)\bigr).

Since the right-hand side depends only on smooth quantities on
\zeta([0,T])
, and all such initial vectors
\dot{\zeta}(0)
belong to a compact set in
\Omega\cap F^{-1}(1)
(see
[
29
, Lemma 3.4]
), it is thus bounded from below by some constant
-C(x_{0},T)
.
This yields the desired semiconcavity of
\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}
.
∎

4.3
Busemann functions for straight lines

In splitting theorems, we assume the existence of a timelike
straight line

\eta\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow M
, i.e.,
\tau(\eta(s),\eta(t))=t-s
holds for all
s<t
.
We will denote by
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
the Busemann function associated with the ray
\eta|_{[0,\infty)}
.
Moreover, the curve
\bar{\eta}(t):=\eta(-t)
,
t\in[0,\infty)
, is a timelike ray of unit speed
with respect to the reverse structure
\overleftarrow{L}(v)=L(-v)
(recall Subsection
2.3
).
Hence, we can introduce the corresponding Busemann function as

\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\eta}(x):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\big\{t-\tau\big(\eta(-t),x\big)\big\}

(
\tau
is with respect to
L
).
It follows from the reverse triangle inequality that

\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(x)+\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\eta}(x)\geq\lim_{t\to\infty}\big\{2t-\tau\big(\eta(-t),\eta(t)\big)\big\}=0,

(4.2)

and equality holds on
\eta
.

For straight lines we modify
I(\eta)
in (
4.1
) into

I(\eta):=\Bigg(\bigcup_{t>0}I^{+}\big(\eta(-t)\big)\Bigg)\cap\Bigg(\bigcup_{t>0}I^{-}\big(\eta(t)\big)\Bigg).

Note that
I(\eta)=\bigcup_{s<0}I(\eta|_{[s,\infty)})
,
therefore, the previous results considered for rays continue to hold on
I(\eta)
for timelike straight lines
\eta
.
We infer from Lemma
4.7
(
ii
) the following (see
[
44
, Lemma 5.5]
,
[
22
, Lemma 4.1]
).

Lemma 4.10
.

Assume that
x\in I(\eta)
satisfies
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(x)+\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\eta}(x)=0
,
and let
\zeta^{+}
and
\zeta^{-}
be timelike asymptotes of
\eta
and
\bar{\eta}
of unit speed
emanating from
x
, respectively.
Then, the concatenation
\zeta
of
\zeta^{+}
and
\zeta^{-}
is a timelike straight line.

Precisely,
\zeta^{-}
is an asymptote of
\bar{\eta}
of unit speed with respect to
\overleftarrow{L}
,
and the concatenation
\zeta
is given by
\zeta(t):=\zeta^{+}(t)
for
t\geq 0
and
\zeta(t):=\zeta^{-}(-t)
for
t<0
.
We say that such a straight line
\zeta
is
bi-asymptotic
to
\eta
.

5
p
-harmonicity and smoothness of Busemann functions

With the basic properties of the Busemann function
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
in the previous section in hand, we proceed to the analysis of
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
using the
p
-d’Alembertian
\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}
.
We begin with the wider range
N\in(-\infty,1]\cup[n,\infty]
, and restrict ourselves to
N\in(-\infty,0)\cup[n,\infty]
at the last step (see Remark
5.7
).

5.1
Maximum principle and
p
-harmonicity

We first show that, in a rather standard way,
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
is
superharmonic
with respect to
\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m},p}
(cf.
[
10
, Corollary 8]
).

Lemma 5.1

(
p
-superharmonicity)
.

Let
(M,L,\mathfrak{m})
be a timelike geodesically complete, weighted Finsler spacetime as in Theorem
1.1
, but with
N\in(-\infty,1]\cup[n,\infty]
.
Then, the Busemann functions
\mathbf{b}_{\eta},\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\eta}
satisfy
\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m},p}\mathbf{b}_{\eta}\leq 0
and
\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\eta}\leq 0
in the weak sense on a neighborhood of
\eta(\mathbb{R})
.

Precisely, we assume timelike
\epsilon
-completeness for
\epsilon
as in (
2.14
) also when
N\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to find a neighborhood of
\eta(0)
on which the claim holds.
Lemma
4.4
and Proposition
4.5
provide a neighborhood
U
of
\eta(0)
such that, for sufficiently large
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a maximizing timelike geodesic from each
x\in U
to
\eta(k)
.
Moreover, such a sequence of maximizing geodesics
\zeta_{k}\colon[0,\tau(x,\eta(k))]\longrightarrow M
converges uniformly (up to subsequences and with respect to any complete auxiliary Riemannian metric on
M
) to a timelike asymptote
\zeta
of
\eta
.
Arguing as in
[
29
, Lemma 3.4]
, up to passing to another subsequence, also the initial vectors
\dot{\zeta}_{k}(0)
converge to
\dot{\zeta}(0)
, by smooth dependence of geodesics on initial data.

Put
\tau_{k}(x):=-\tau(x,\eta(k))
, which is Lipschitz and hence differentiable almost everywhere on
U
by
[
12
, Theorem 3.11]
.
Moreover, since
\mathbf{b}_{\eta,t}\searrow\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
as
t\to\infty
and
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
is Lipschitz on
U
(Theorem
4.8
),
(\tau_{k})_{k}
is uniformly bounded on
U
.
Combining this with the equi-semiconcavity of
(\tau_{k})_{k}
from Lemma
4.9
, we infer that, after possibly shrinking
U
, the family
(\tau_{k})_{k}
is uniformly Lipschitz on
U
.
Moreover, by Lemma
4.7
(
iii
), we have

\nabla(-\tau_{k})(x)\to\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})(x)\qquad\text{as }k\to\infty

for almost every
x\in U
at which
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
and
\tau_{k}
are differentiable (see also Theorem
4.8
).
Therefore, for any nonnegative test function
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U)
, the dominated convergence theorem yields

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{U}\mathrm{d}\phi\bigl(\overleftarrow{\nabla}\tau_{k}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}=\int_{U}\mathrm{d}\phi\bigl(\overleftarrow{\nabla}\mathbf{b}_{\eta}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m},

where we also used
\overleftarrow{\nabla}\mathbf{b}_{\eta}=-\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})
.

Now, given
x\in U
, it follows from the reverse form of the
p
-d’Alembertian comparison estimate (
3.5
) that

\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m},p}\tau_{k}(x)\leq\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\zeta}_{k}(0))}\biggl(c\int_{0}^{r_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\zeta}_{k}(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\biggr)^{-1},

(5.1)

where
r_{k}:=\tau(x,\eta(k))
and
c=c(N,\epsilon)>0
as in (
2.15
).
We claim that

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m},p}\tau_{k}(x)\leq 0.

(5.2)

This is indeed immediate from (
5.1
) and the future timelike
\epsilon
-completeness (recall Definition
2.13
).
Here, for completeness, we also give a proof under (
2.20
).
If (
5.2
) does not hold true,
then we deduce from (
5.1
) that there is a subsequence of
(\tau_{k})_{k}
,
again denoted by
(\tau_{k})_{k}
for brevity, such that

\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\zeta}_{k}(0))}\biggl(c\int_{0}^{r_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\zeta}_{k}(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\biggr)^{-1}\geq C

for some constant
C>0
.
By passing to the limit as
k\to\infty
(up to a subsequence), we find an asymptote
\zeta\colon[0,\infty)\longrightarrow M
of
\eta
with
\zeta(0)=x
, which satisfies

\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\zeta}(0))}\biggl(c\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{2(\epsilon-1)}{n-1}\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\zeta}(s))}\,\mathrm{d}s\biggr)^{-1}\geq C.

This contradicts (
2.20
), thus we obtain (
5.2
).
We remark that, in the case of
N\in[n,\infty)
, we can take
\epsilon=1
and timelike
1
-completeness always holds.

Under a uniform bound
\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m},p}\tau_{k}\leq C
on
U
guaranteed by (
5.1
) and the smoothness of
\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}
, Fatou’s lemma and
\phi\geq 0
imply

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{U}\phi\cdot\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m},p}\tau_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}\leq\int_{U}\phi\cdot\limsup_{k\to\infty}\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m},p}\tau_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}\leq 0.

Therefore, recalling that
F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}\mathbf{b}_{\eta})=F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}\tau_{k})=1

\mathfrak{m}
-a.e. on
U
(cf. Lemma
4.7
(
iii
)), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{U}\phi\cdot\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m},p}\mathbf{b}_{\eta}\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\phi\cdot\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m}}\mathbf{b}_{\eta}\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}=-\int_{U}\mathrm{d}\phi\bigl(\overleftarrow{\nabla}\mathbf{b}_{\eta}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}

\displaystyle=-\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{U}\mathrm{d}\phi\bigl(\overleftarrow{\nabla}\tau_{k}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{U}\phi\cdot\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m}}\tau_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{U}\phi\cdot\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m},p}\tau_{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}\leq 0,

which completes the proof of
\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m},p}\mathbf{b}_{\eta}\leq 0
.
The other inequality
\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\eta}\leq 0
is shown in the same way by using (
3.4
) and past timelike
\epsilon
-completeness.
∎

The next proposition, along the lines of
[
10
, Proposition 9]
, is the key step where the ellipticity of the
p
-d’Alembertian enables us to apply the maximum principle.

Proposition 5.2

(
p
-harmonicity)
.

In the same situation as Lemma
5.1
, there exists a neighborhood
W
of
\eta(\mathbb{R})
on which we have
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}+\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\eta}=0
pointwise as well as
\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})=\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\eta}=0
in the weak sense.
In particular,
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
and
\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\eta}
are
C^{1,1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}
on
W
.

Proof.

Set
u:=\mathbf{b}_{\eta}+\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\eta}
and recall from (
4.2
) that
u\geq 0
.
Let us consider

h_{t}:=-\mathbf{b}_{\eta}+tu=(1-t)(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})+t\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\eta}.

Note that
h_{0}=-\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
,
h_{1}=\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\eta}
, and that
-h_{t}
is a time function.
Thus, we observe from Lemma
5.1
that
\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}h_{1}\leq 0
and
\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}h_{0}=-\overleftarrow{\square}_{\mathfrak{m},p}\mathbf{b}_{\eta}\geq 0
in the weak sense on some neighborhood
W
of
\eta(\mathbb{R})
.

Now, we fix a local coordinate system
(x^{i})_{i=1}^{n}
on a (small) open set
U
in
W
and take a nonnegative test function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(U)
of compact support.
Then, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\int_{U}\phi(\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}h_{1}-\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}h_{0})\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}

\displaystyle=-\int_{U}\mathrm{d}\phi\bigl(F^{*}(\mathrm{d}h_{1})^{p-2}\cdot\nabla h_{1}-F^{*}(\mathrm{d}h_{0})^{p-2}\cdot\nabla h_{0}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}

\displaystyle=-\int_{U}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Bigl[F^{*}(\mathrm{d}h_{t})^{p-2}\cdot\mathrm{d}\phi(\nabla h_{t})\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}.

It follows from (
2.10
), (
2.12
) and (
2.11
) that the right-hand side can be calculated as

\displaystyle=-\int_{U}\int_{0}^{1}\biggl\{(2-p)F^{*}(\mathrm{d}h_{t})^{p-4}\mathrm{d}u(\nabla h_{t})\cdot\mathrm{d}\phi(\nabla h_{t})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+F^{*}(\mathrm{d}h_{t})^{p-2}\cdot\mathrm{d}\phi\biggl(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g^{*}_{ij}(\mathrm{d}h_{t})\frac{\partial u}{\partial x^{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\biggr)\biggr\}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}

\displaystyle=-\int_{U}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x^{i}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x^{j}}F^{*}(\mathrm{d}h_{t})^{p-2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\biggl\{\frac{2-p}{F^{*}(\mathrm{d}h_{t})^{2}}\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}g^{*}_{ik}(\mathrm{d}h_{t})\frac{\partial h_{t}}{\partial x^{k}}g^{*}_{jl}(\mathrm{d}h_{t})\frac{\partial h_{t}}{\partial x^{l}}+g^{*}_{ij}(\mathrm{d}h_{t})\biggr\}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}.

Therefore, by writing
\mathfrak{m}=\sigma\,\mathrm{d}x^{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x^{n}
on
U
,
u
is a supersolution to a linear second order differential operator, namely

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\biggl[a_{ij}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x^{j}}\biggr]\leq 0

in the weak sense on
U
with

a_{ij}:=\sigma\int_{0}^{1}F^{*}(\mathrm{d}h_{t})^{p-2}\biggl\{\frac{2-p}{F^{*}(\mathrm{d}h_{t})^{2}}\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}g^{*}_{ik}(\mathrm{d}h_{t})\frac{\partial h_{t}}{\partial x^{k}}g^{*}_{jl}(\mathrm{d}h_{t})\frac{\partial h_{t}}{\partial x^{l}}+g^{*}_{ij}(\mathrm{d}h_{t})\biggr\}\,\mathrm{d}t.

In the Fermi(–Walker) coordinates along
\eta
such that
(\partial/\partial x^{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is
g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}
-orthonormal and
\partial/\partial x^{1}=\dot{\eta}
on
\eta
,
(a_{ij})
is the diagonal matrix
\sigma\cdot\mathrm{diag}(1-p,1,\ldots,1)
on
\eta
, which is positive definite.
Hence, taking a smaller neighborhood
W
of
\eta(\mathbb{R})
if necessary,
u
is a supersolution to an elliptic equation on
W
.
Combining this with
u\geq 0
and
u=0
on
\eta
, we can apply the maximum principle, yielding
u=0
on
W
.
Then, we deduce from
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}=-\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\eta}
and Lemmas
4.9
,
5.1
that
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
is both semiconcave and semiconvex (hence
C^{1,1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}
) and satisfies
\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})=\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\eta}=0
in the weak sense (see
[
10
, Proposition 9]
for more details).
∎

5.2
Bochner-type identity and smoothness

In order to obtain the smoothness of the Busemann function
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
, we generalize the Bochner identity to our setting.
Different from the strategy in
[
54
]
and
[
10
]
directly working on
H_{p}
in (
3.1
) (cf. related works
[
41
,
57
]
on Hamiltonian systems), we shall closely follow the lines of thought in the Finsler case
[
59
,
60
]
.
In fact, we can apply the same calculation regardless of the signature of the metric; the only difference is that the quantity (
5.5
) corresponding to the Hilbert–Schmidt norm is not necessarily nonnegative in the Lorentzian setting.
Due to the close analogy with the positive definite setting, we only give an outline here and refer to
[
60
, §3.1]
and
[
59
, §12.2]
for more details.

Let
U\subset M
be an open set and take
h\in C^{3}(U)
such that
-h
is temporal.
For
y\in U
and small
t>0
, define a map
\mathcal{T}_{t}
and a vector field
V_{t}
by

\mathcal{T}_{t}(y):=\exp_{y}\bigl(t\nabla h(y)\bigr),\qquad V_{t}\bigl(\mathcal{T}_{t}(y)\bigr)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigl[\mathcal{T}_{t}(y)\bigr].

Fix
x\in U
and put
\zeta(t):=\mathcal{T}_{t}(x)
.
Since
\zeta
is a timelike geodesic and
\dot{\zeta}(t)=V_{t}(\zeta(t))
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial V_{t}^{i}}{\partial t}\bigl(\zeta(t)\bigr)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\Big|_{\zeta(t)}+D^{V_{t}}_{V_{t}}V_{t}\bigl(\zeta(t)\bigr)=0.

(5.3)

Let
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be an orthonormal basis of
(T_{x}M,g_{\nabla h})

such that
e_{1}=\nabla h(x)/F(\nabla h(x))
,
and consider vector fields along
\zeta
given by

E_{i}(t):=(\mathrm{d}\mathcal{T}_{t})_{x}(e_{i})=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\Bigl[\mathcal{T}_{t}\bigl(\exp_{x}(se_{i})\bigr)\Bigr]_{s=0}.

By the latter expression,
E_{i}
is a Jacobi field with
E_{i}(0)=e_{i}
.
Since
(E_{i}(t))_{i=1}^{n}
is a basis of
T_{\zeta(t)}M
(for small
t>0
), we can introduce an
n\times n
matrix
B(t)=(b_{ij}(t))
by
D^{\dot{\zeta}}_{\dot{\zeta}}E_{i}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{ij}(t)E_{j}(t)
.
Then, we have the following
Riccati equation
:

\bigl[\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}\nolimits B\bigr]^{\prime}(t)+\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}\nolimits\bigl[B(t)^{2}\bigr]+\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits\bigl(\dot{\zeta}(t)\bigr)=0.

(5.4)

Observe that
B(t)
represents the covariant derivative of
V_{t}
in the sense that

D^{\dot{\zeta}(t)}_{E_{i}(t)}V_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{ij}(t)E_{j}(t).

In particular, since
V_{0}=\nabla h
, we have
b_{ij}(0)=g_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}_{e_{i}}h,e_{j})g_{\nabla h}(e_{j},e_{j})
and infer from the symmetry (
2.13
) that

\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}\nolimits\bigl[B(0)^{2}\bigr]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}_{e_{i}}h,e_{j})^{2}g_{\nabla h}(e_{i},e_{i})g_{\nabla h}(e_{j},e_{j})=:\mathop{\mathrm{HS}}\nolimits_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}h),

(5.5)

which corresponds to the square of the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of
\nabla^{2}h
with respect to
g_{\nabla h}
in the positive definite case, but is not necessarily nonnegative in the current setting.

Concerning the first term in (
5.4
), we find

\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}\nolimits\bigl[B(t)\bigr]=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}V_{t}\bigl(\zeta(t)\bigr)+(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\zeta})^{\prime}(t),

and then (
5.3
) (together with the linearity of
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}
from (
2.17
)) implies

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Bigl[\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}V_{t}\bigl(\zeta(t)\bigr)\Bigr]=\mathrm{d}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}V_{t})\bigl(\dot{\zeta}(t)\bigr)-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}(D^{V_{t}}_{V_{t}}V_{t})\bigl(\zeta(t)\bigr).

Now, at
t=0
, we observe
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}V_{0}=\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h
and, since

g_{\nabla h}\biggl(D^{\nabla h}_{\nabla h}\bigl[\nabla h\bigr],\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\biggr)=g_{\nabla h}\Bigl(D^{\nabla h}_{\partial/\partial x^{i}}\bigl[\nabla h\bigr],\nabla h\Bigr)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\Bigl[g_{\nabla h}(\nabla h,\nabla h)\Bigr],

it follows that

D^{\nabla h}_{\nabla h}\bigl[\nabla h\bigr]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g^{ij}(\nabla h)g_{\nabla h}\biggl(D^{\nabla h}_{\nabla h}\bigl[\nabla h\bigr],\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\biggr)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}=\nabla^{\nabla h}\biggl[-\frac{F(\nabla h)^{2}}{2}\biggr],

where we define

\nabla^{\nabla h}u:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g^{ij}(\nabla h)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x^{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}

(we do not impose any causality condition on
u
).
Plugging those into (
5.4
) at
t=0
, we arrive at the following.

Proposition 5.3

(Bochner-type identity)
.

Let
(M,L,\mathfrak{m})
be a weighted Finsler spacetime.
For
h\in C^{3}(U)
on an open set
U\subset M
such that
-h
is temporal, we have

\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}\biggl(\nabla^{\nabla h}\biggl[\frac{F(\nabla h)^{2}}{2}\biggr]\biggr)+\mathrm{d}(\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h)(\nabla h)+\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{\infty}(\nabla h)+\mathop{\mathrm{HS}}\nolimits_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}h)=0

(5.6)

pointwise on
U
.

We remark that, if we replace
F(\nabla h)^{2}
with
-g_{\nabla h}(\nabla h,\nabla h)
, then (
5.6
) is exactly the same form as the Bochner identity in the positive definite case in
[
59
,
60
]
.

Remark 5.4

(Comparison with Raychaudhuri equation)
.

The
Raychaudhuri equation
can be shown by a similar argument (see, e.g.,
[
42
]
),
however, in that we deal with an endomorphism on the
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace
N_{\zeta}(t)\subset T_{\zeta(t)}M
which is
g_{\dot{\zeta}}
-orthogonal to
\dot{\zeta}
.
In the above discussion, the corresponding endomorphism
v\longmapsto D^{\dot{\zeta}(t)}_{v}V_{t}
on
T_{\zeta(t)}M
does not necessarily satisfy this condition.
A typical example where one can apply the Raychaudhuri equation is the time separation function from a point, and then the d’Alembertian comparison theorem follows (see
[
43
]
).

Since (
5.6
) is concerned with
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{\infty}
, for a weaker bound
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}\geq 0
with
N\leq 0
, we need a variant along the lines of
[
71
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Lemma 5.5

(
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{0}\geq 0
case)
.

Let
(M,L,\mathfrak{m})
be a weighted Finsler spacetime with
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{0}\geq 0
in timelike directions.
Then, for
h\in C^{3}(U)
as in Proposition
5.3
, we have

w\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}\biggl(\nabla^{\nabla h}\biggl[\frac{F(\nabla h)^{2}}{2}\biggr]\biggr)+\mathrm{d}(w\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h)(\nabla h)+w\frac{(\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h)^{2}}{n}\leq\frac{w}{2F(\nabla h)^{2}}\sum_{\alpha=2}^{n}\Bigl(\mathrm{d}\bigl[F(\nabla h)^{2}\bigr](e_{\alpha})\Bigr)^{2}

(5.7)

at any
x\in U
, where
w(\zeta(t)):=\mathrm{e}^{2\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}(\dot{\zeta}(t))/n}
for
\zeta(t):=\exp_{x}(t\nabla h(x))
and
(e_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is an orthonormal basis of
(T_{x}M,g_{\nabla h})
such that
e_{1}=\nabla h(x)/F(\nabla h(x))
.
Moreover, in the case where
F(\nabla h)
is constant, equality holds if and only if
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{0}(\nabla h(x))=0
and
\nabla^{2}_{v}h=cv
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
and all
v\in T_{x}M
.

Proof.

We first observe

\mathrm{d}(w\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h)(\nabla h)=w\cdot\mathrm{d}(\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h)(\nabla h)+2w\frac{(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\zeta})^{\prime}(0)}{n}\cdot\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h.

It follows from (
5.5
), the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and (
2.8
) that, at
x
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{HS}}\nolimits_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}h)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}_{e_{i}}h,e_{i})^{2}-2\sum_{\alpha=2}^{n}g_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}_{e_{\alpha}}h,e_{1})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n}\Biggl(-g_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}_{e_{1}}h,e_{1})+\sum_{i=2}^{n}g_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}_{e_{i}}h,e_{i})\Biggr)^{2}-\frac{2}{F(\nabla h)^{2}}\sum_{\alpha=2}^{n}g_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}_{e_{\alpha}}h,\nabla h)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\square h)^{2}}{n}-\frac{1}{2F(\nabla h)^{2}}\sum_{\alpha=2}^{n}\Bigl(\mathrm{d}\bigl[F(\nabla h)^{2}\bigr](e_{\alpha})\Bigr)^{2}.

Moreover, we have

(\square h)^{2}=\bigl(\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h+(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\zeta})^{\prime}(0)\bigr)^{2}=(\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h)^{2}+2(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\zeta})^{\prime}(0)\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h+(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\zeta})^{\prime}(0)^{2}.

Comparing these with (
5.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}(w\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h)(\nabla h)+w\frac{(\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h)^{2}}{n}-\frac{w}{2F(\nabla h)^{2}}\sum_{\alpha=2}^{n}\Bigl(\mathrm{d}\bigl[F(\nabla h)^{2}\bigr](e_{\alpha})\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq w\cdot\mathrm{d}(\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h)(\nabla h)+w\mathop{\mathrm{HS}}\nolimits_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}h)-w\frac{(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\zeta})^{\prime}(0)^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle=-w\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}\biggl(\nabla^{\nabla h}\biggl[\frac{F(\nabla h)^{2}}{2}\biggr]\biggr)-w\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{\infty}(\nabla h)-w\frac{(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\zeta})^{\prime}(0)^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle=-w\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}\biggl(\nabla^{\nabla h}\biggl[\frac{F(\nabla h)^{2}}{2}\biggr]\biggr)-w\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{0}(\nabla h).

Since
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{0}\geq 0
by assumption, this completes the proof of (
5.7
).

When
F(\nabla h)
is constant, the right-hand side of (
5.7
) vanishes.
Then, by the above proof, equality holds if and only if
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{0}(\nabla h(x))=0
and
g_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}_{e_{i}}h,e_{j})=c\cdot g_{\nabla h}(e_{i},e_{j})
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
, which means
\nabla^{2}_{v}h=cv
.
∎

With (
5.6
) and (
5.7
) in hand, we can show the smoothness of
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
.
We remark that the
N\in[0,1]
case is excluded here.

Corollary 5.6

(Smoothness)
.

Let
(M,L,\mathfrak{m})
be a timelike geodesically complete, weighted Finsler spacetime as in Theorem
1.1
.
Then, the Busemann function
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
is
C^{\infty}
and satisfies

\nabla^{2}(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})\equiv 0

(5.8)

on a neighborhood
W
of
\eta(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

We put
h:=-\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
for brevity.
Recall from Lemma
4.7
and Proposition
5.2
that
h
is
C^{1,1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}
,
F(\nabla h)\equiv 1
on
W
, and
\square_{\mathfrak{m},p}h=\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h=0
almost everywhere on
W
.

First, suppose that
h
is
C^{3}
.
When
N\in[n,\infty]
, we deduce from (
5.6
) and the hypothesis
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}\geq 0
that

\mathop{\mathrm{HS}}\nolimits_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}h)=-\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{\infty}(\nabla h)\leq-\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}(\nabla h)\leq 0.

Thanks to
F(\nabla h)\equiv 1
, in (
5.5
), for
\alpha=2,\ldots,n
, we observe

g_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}_{e_{1}}h,e_{\alpha})=g_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}_{e_{\alpha}}h,e_{1})=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}\bigl[F(\nabla h)^{2}\bigr](e_{\alpha})=0.

This yields
g_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}_{e_{i}}h,e_{j})=0
for all
i,j
, and hence (
5.8
) holds and
h
is
C^{\infty}
.

If
N<0
, then
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{0}\geq\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}\geq 0
and we can apply Lemma
5.5
.
Since equality holds in (
5.7
) with
F(\nabla h)\equiv 1
, we have
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{0}(\nabla h)=0
and
\nabla^{2}_{v}h=cv
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
Now, combining
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{0}(\nabla h)=0
with the hypothesis
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}\geq 0
, we find that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{N}\bigl(\nabla h(x)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{0}\bigl(\nabla h(x)\bigr)-\biggl(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{N-n}\biggr)(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\zeta})^{\prime}(0)^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{N}{n(N-n)}(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\zeta})^{\prime}(0)^{2}

is nonnegative, where
\zeta
is the geodesic with
\dot{\zeta}(0)=\nabla h(x)
.
Since
N<0
, this implies
(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\zeta})^{\prime}(0)=0
, thereby

\square h=\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h+(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\zeta})^{\prime}(0)=0.

Therefore, we obtain
c=0
and the proof is completed.

For general
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}\in C^{1,1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}
, we approximate it with
C^{\infty}
-functions, and obtain (
5.8
) almost everywhere by the above argument.
This yields that (
5.8
) holds everywhere and
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
is
C^{\infty}
.
We refer to
[
10
, Corollary 14]
for further details of the approximation procedure, via the integrated form of (
5.6
):

\int_{M}\biggl\{\mathrm{d}\phi\biggl(\nabla^{\nabla h}\biggl[\frac{F(\nabla h)^{2}}{2}\biggr]\biggr)+\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits_{\mathfrak{m}}(\phi\nabla h)\biggr\}\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}=\int_{M}\phi\bigl\{\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{\infty}(\nabla h)+\mathop{\mathrm{HS}}\nolimits_{\nabla h}(\nabla^{2}h)\bigr\}\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{m}

for (nonnegative) test functions
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(M)
of compact support. To sketch the argument, one may approximate
h
with smooth
h_{\varepsilon}
which satisfies
F(\nabla h_{\varepsilon})\geq 1-\varepsilon
and
h_{\varepsilon}\to h
in
W^{2,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(W)
, as well as
\nabla^{2}h_{\varepsilon}\to\nabla^{2}h
(and hence
\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h_{\varepsilon}\to\square_{\mathfrak{m}}h
)
\mathfrak{m}
-a.e. Using approximate versions of the arguments given above for
h
, now for
h_{\varepsilon}
, in the integrated Bochner-type formula above, one may thereupon pass to the limit to ultimately obtain
\nabla^{2}h=0

\mathfrak{m}
-a.e., which, in coordinates, is easily seen to imply that
h
is smooth.
∎

Remark 5.7

(
N\in[0,1]
case)
.

Wylie’s
[
71
, Lemma 3.1]
is in fact more general and concerned with the situation that
\nabla^{2}h
has at most
k
non-zero eigenvalues under the assumption
\mathop{\mathrm{Ric}}\nolimits_{n-k}\geq 0
.
Applying this with
k=n-1
for a Busemann function, whose second order derivative vanishes along asymptotes, we could extend the splitting theorem to
N<1
(cf.
[
71
, Corollary 1.3]
,
[
69
, Theorem 1.5(i)]
), and we can even obtain a warped product splitting for
N=1
(
[
71
, Theorem 1.2]
,
[
69
, Theorem 1.5(ii)]
).
In the above proof, however,
C^{\infty}
-functions
h_{\varepsilon}
approximating
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
may have
n
non-zero eigenvalues; this is the reason why we restricted ourselves to
N<0
.

6
Splitting theorems

We are in a position to complete the proofs of the splitting theorems.
We first consider the timelike geodesically complete (TGC) case, and the globally hyperbolic (GH) case is deferred to Subsection
6.3
.

6.1
Diffeomorphic splitting

Proof of Theorem
1.1

(
TGC case
)
.

We deduce from Lemmas
4.7
,
4.10
together with Corollary
5.6
that, for every point
x\in W
, the unique straight line bi-asymptotic to
\eta
passing through
x
is given by the integral curve of
\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})
.
In other words, every integral curve of
\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})
is a straight line bi-asymptotic to
\eta
.
This enables us to apply standard arguments in splitting theorems in Lorentzian manifolds (one can alternatively apply Wu’s de Rham-type decomposition theorem
[
70
]
).
Precisely, since
\nabla^{2}(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})=0
on a neighborhood
W
of
\eta(\mathbb{R})
, also the
g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}
-Hessian of
-\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
vanishes by
[
42
, Proposition 3.2]
, thus we deduce that
\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})
is a parallel (and thus a complete timelike Killing) vector field for
g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}
.
Hence, taking into account that
\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})
is also the gradient of
-\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
with respect to
g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}
, we set

\Sigma^{\prime}:=\mathbf{b}_{\eta}^{-1}(0)\cap W
and

\Theta\colon\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma^{\prime}\longrightarrow M,\quad\quad\Theta(t,x):=\zeta_{x}(t),

(6.1)

where
\zeta_{x}
is the integral curve of
\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})
with
\zeta_{x}(0)=x
. The map
\Theta
is then an isometry from
(\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma^{\prime},-\mathrm{d}t^{2}+g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}|_{T\Sigma^{\prime}})
onto its image in
(M,g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})})
.
Moreover, we deduce from
(\psi_{\mathfrak{m}}\circ\dot{\zeta}_{x})^{\prime}=0
shown in the proof of Corollary
5.6
that the measure
\mathfrak{m}
is also decomposed into the product
\mathrm{d}t\times\mathfrak{n}
.
Finally, we can show the global splitting by the standard open-and-closed argument (see, e.g.,
[
44
, Theorem 7.3]
).
This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
∎

We remark that
\Sigma
is given as the level surface
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}^{-1}(0)
equipped with the Riemannian metric
g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}|_{T\Sigma}
.
As for
L|_{T\Sigma}
, one can say the following.

Corollary 6.1

(Causal character of
\Sigma
)
.

In the situation of Theorem
1.1
, the level surface
\Sigma=\mathbf{b}_{\eta}^{-1}(0)
is transversal to future-directed causal vectors in
TM
.
Moreover, if
L
is reversible and
\dim M\geq 3
, then
L(v)>0
for any
v\in T\Sigma\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

If
v\in T_{x}\Sigma\setminus\{0\}
is future-directed causal, then we deduce from the reverse Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (
2.9
) that

0=\mathrm{d}\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(v)\geq\sqrt{4L^{*}\bigl(-\mathrm{d}\mathbf{b}_{\eta}(x)\bigr)L(v)}\geq 0.

Recalling the equality condition in (
2.9
), we find that
L(\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})(x))=L(v)=0
, which contradicts
F(\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta}))=1
.

In the case where
L
is reversible and
\dim M\geq 3
, the set
\Omega^{\prime}_{x}\subset T_{x}M
of timelike vectors has exactly two connected components, corresponding to the future and past directions (recall Remark
2.2
). In this case, since
-v\in T_{x}\Sigma
is not future-directed causal as well, every
v\in T_{x}\Sigma\setminus\{0\}
satisfies
L(v)>0
.
∎

We remark that, even when
L>0
on
T_{x}\Sigma\setminus\{0\}
, we do not know if
L|_{T_{x}\Sigma}
is strongly convex (in the sense that the Hessian of
L|_{T_{x}\Sigma\setminus\{0\}}
is positive definite).

6.2
Isometric translations in Berwald spacetimes

Next, we prove Theorem
1.3
concerning Berwald spacetimes.
The proof is common to the TGC and GH cases (once the splitting as in Theorem
1.1
is established).
Since the covariant derivative is independent of a reference vector by the very definition of Berwald spacetimes (recall Definition
2.4
), we may omit the reference vector in the following.

Lemma 6.2

(Busemann function is affine)
.

Let
(M,L,\mathfrak{m})
be a weighted Berwald spacetime as in Theorem
1.3
.
Then, for any geodesic
\xi\colon[0,1]\longrightarrow M
, we have
(\mathbf{b}_{\eta}\circ\xi)^{\prime\prime}\equiv 0
.
In particular, for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}^{-1}(t)
is a totally geodesic hypersurface with respect to
L
.

Proof.

This is straightforward from (
2.8
) and (
5.8
):

\displaystyle(\mathbf{b}_{\eta}\circ\xi)^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\bigl[-g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}\bigl(\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})(\xi),\dot{\xi}\bigr)\bigl]^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}\bigl(D_{\dot{\xi}}\bigl(\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})\bigr),\dot{\xi}\bigr)-g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}\bigl(\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})(\xi),D_{\dot{\xi}}\dot{\xi}\bigr)=0.

Precisely, the first term vanishes in general thanks to (
5.8
), while the second term vanishes only in the Berwald case (since
D^{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}_{\dot{\xi}}\dot{\xi}\neq 0
in general).
∎

To show Theorem
1.3
, we can follow the lines of
[
58
, Proposition 5.2]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

(
i
)
Take
v\in T_{x}M\setminus\{0\}
and put
V(t):=\mathrm{d}\Phi_{t}(v)
.
It follows from Theorem
1.1
that
V
is a parallel vector field with respect to
g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}
along the straight line
\zeta(t):=\Phi_{t}(x)
.
Hence, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}L(V)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigl[g_{V}(V,V)\bigr]=g_{V}(D_{\dot{\zeta}}V,V)\equiv 0,

thereby
\Phi_{t}
is isometric.
More precisely, we observe from (
2.8
) that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigl[g_{V}(V,V)\bigr]=2g_{V}(D^{V}_{\dot{\zeta}}V,V),

and the Berwald condition and
[
42
, Proposition 3.2]
imply
D^{V}_{\dot{\zeta}}V=D^{\dot{\zeta}}_{\dot{\zeta}}V=D^{g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}}_{\dot{\zeta}}V\equiv 0
, where
D^{g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}}
denotes the covariant derivative with respect to
g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}
.

(
ii
)
We shall split the geodesic equation
D_{\dot{\xi}}\dot{\xi}=0
on
M
into those on
\mathbb{R}
and
\Sigma
.
Given a local coordinate system
(z^{\alpha})_{\alpha=1}^{n-1}
on an open set
U\subset\Sigma
, we consider a coordinate system
(x^{i})_{i=1}^{n}
on
\mathbb{R}\times U\subset M
given by
x^{1}=\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
and
x^{i}=z^{i-1}
for
i\geq 2
.
Then, we have

\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^{1}}\bigl(\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})\bigr)=0\quad\text{for}\ i,j=1,\ldots,n

by Theorem
1.1
, and

\frac{\partial g_{1j}}{\partial x^{i}}\bigl(\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})\bigr)=0\quad\text{for}\ i=1,\ldots,n

by
F^{*}(-\mathrm{d}\mathbf{b}_{\eta})=1
for
j=1
, and by
g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}(\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta}),T\Sigma)=0
for
j\geq 2
.
Therefore, we find
\gamma^{i}_{jk}(\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta}))=0
unless
i,j,k\geq 2
.

Moreover, by the expression (
2.5
):

N^{i}_{j}(v)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\gamma^{i}_{jk}(v)v^{k}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,l,a,b=1}^{n}g^{ia}(v)\frac{\partial g_{ab}}{\partial v^{j}}(v)\gamma^{b}_{kl}(v)v^{k}v^{l},

we deduce that
N^{i}_{j}(\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta}))=0
for all
i,j=1,\ldots,n
.
This implies that, by recalling the definition (
2.6
) of
\Gamma^{i}_{jk}
,
\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta}))=0
holds unless
i,j,k\geq 2
.
Therefore, the geodesic equation in
M
is written as

D_{\dot{\xi}}\dot{\xi}=\ddot{\xi}^{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{1}}\Big|_{\xi}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\biggl\{\ddot{\xi}^{i}+\sum_{j,k=2}^{n}\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(\xi)\dot{\xi}^{j}\dot{\xi}^{k}\biggr\}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\Big|_{\xi}=0.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 6.3

(When
L|_{T\Sigma\setminus\{0\}}
is positive)
.

Although
\Sigma=\mathbf{b}_{\eta}^{-1}(0)
may not be spacelike in
L
in general, we notice that, by the fact that parallel transports preserve
L
and the decomposition of
\Gamma^{i}_{jk}
in the proof of Theorem
1.3
(
ii
), if there exists some
x\in\mathbf{b}_{\eta}^{-1}(0)
such that
T_{x}\Sigma
is spacelike (i.e.,
L(v)>0
for all
v\in T_{x}\Sigma\setminus\{0\}
) and
g_{v}|_{T_{x}\Sigma}
is positive semi-definite for all
v\in T_{x}\Sigma\setminus\{0\}
, then
(\Sigma,L|_{T\Sigma})
is a (non-strongly convex) Finsler manifold of Berwald type.
In this case, one can also give a Lorentzian metrization of
(M,L)
(recall Remark
2.5
).
In fact, by
[
45
, Remark(A), p. 2157]
, which extends Szabó’s metrization result of Berwald spaces
[
67
]
to non-strongly convex (and also non-smooth) Berwald metrics,
we obtain a Riemannian metric
h
on
\Sigma
such that the Levi-Civita connection of
h
coincides with the Chern connection of
L|_{T\Sigma}
.
Then, the Levi-Civita connection of the Lorentzian metric
-\mathrm{d}t^{2}+h
coincides with the Chern connection of
L
.
We also remark that, by
[
48
, Proposition 2, Lemma 2]
, if
n\geq 3
and
L|_{T\Sigma\setminus\{0\}}>0
, then, for all
x\in M
,
\Omega^{\prime}_{x}
has either one or two connected components.

6.3
Globally hyperbolic case

We conclude with the proof of the globally hyperbolic case.

Definition 6.4

(Global hyperbolicity)
.

We say that a Finsler spacetime
(M,L)
is
globally hyperbolic
if it is
causal
(there is no closed future-directed causal curve) and all causal diamonds
J^{+}(x)\cap J^{-}(y)
,
x\leq y
, are compact.

We remark that, since the cone structure
\overline{\Omega}
is proper in the sense of
[
51
, Definition 2.3]
, according to
[
51
, Corollary 2.4]
, the above definition is equivalent to requiring the strong causality and the compactness of causal diamonds (compare also with
[
14
, Appendix B]
).

Proof of Theorem
1.1

(
GH case
)
.

Let
(M,L,\mathfrak{m})
be a globally hyperbolic weighted Berwald spacetime satisfying the hypotheses in Theorem
1.1
.
In this case,
\tau
is finite and continuous and, for any
x,y\in M
with
x<y
, there is a maximizing geodesic from
x
to
y
(see
[
49
, Propositions 6.8, 6.9]
).

In the globally hyperbolic case, an asymptote
\zeta\colon[0,b)\longrightarrow M
constructed as in Definition
4.2
may not be future complete, namely
\zeta
may be future inextendible with
b<\infty
.
Nonetheless, concerning the behavior of Busemann functions, Lemma
4.7
holds for
t\in(0,b)
, and it is shown along the lines of
[
22
, Lemma 3.3]
(see also
[
10
, Theorem 2]
) that
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}
is locally Lipschitz on a neighborhood of
\eta(\mathbb{R})
(as in Theorem
4.8
).

Furthermore, following the lines of
[
10
, §6]
, we can apply the calculations as in Section
5
.
(In this case, due to the possibility of
b<\infty
as above, we need the timelike
\epsilon
-completeness as in Definition
2.13
rather than (
2.20
).)
Eventually, on a neighborhood
W
of
\eta(\mathbb{R})
, we have
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}+\overline{\mathbf{b}}_{\eta}\equiv 0
,
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}\in C^{\infty}(W)
, and
\nabla^{2}(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})\equiv 0
.
Therefore, we obtain a local splitting of
W
as in Theorems
1.1
,
1.3
.

Now, the difficulty in the globally hyperbolic case is that
W
may not be represented simply as the product
\mathbb{R}\times(\mathbf{b}_{\eta}^{-1}(0)\cap W)
.
To show the completeness of asymptotes, we follow the argument in
[
28
,
10
]
; at this step we need the Berwald condition.
As in (
6.1
), we define
\Theta(t,x):=\zeta_{x}(t)
for
x\in\mathbf{b}_{\eta}^{-1}(0)\cap W
and
t\in(a_{x},b_{x})
, namely
\zeta_{x}\colon(a_{x},b_{x})\longrightarrow M
is the future and past inextendible timelike asymptote of
\eta
with
a_{x}<0<b_{x}
.
We shall show that
a_{x}=-\infty
(i.e.,
\zeta_{x}
is past complete).

Let
\Sigma^{\prime}:=\exp_{\eta(0)}(B)
for a small ball
B\subset T_{\eta(0)}M\cap(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{b}_{\eta})^{-1}(0)
.
Precisely,
B
is an open ball with respect to the restriction of
g_{\nabla(-\mathbf{b}_{\eta})}
to
(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{b}_{\eta})^{-1}(0)
, which is a Riemannian metric, and the exponential map is for
L
.
By the decomposition of geodesics as in Theorem
1.3
(
ii
),

\Sigma^{\prime}
is included in
\mathbf{b}_{\eta}^{-1}(0)
.
We may assume that, by taking
B
smaller if necessary,
\dot{\eta}(0)+v\in\Omega_{\eta(0)}
for any
v\in B
.
Fix
v\in B
, put
\sigma(s):=\exp_{\eta(0)}(sv)
for
s\in[0,1]
, and consider
\zeta_{\sigma(s)}(t)=\Theta(t,\sigma(s))
.
To show
a_{\sigma(s)}=-\infty
for all
s\in[0,1]
, we claim that, for any fixed
T>0
,

a_{\sigma(s)}<-T+s

(6.2)

holds.
To this end, denote by
I_{T}
the set of
R\in[0,1]
such that (
6.2
) holds for all
s\in[0,R]
.
Then, clearly
0\in I_{T}
since
a_{\eta(0)}=-\infty
, and
I_{T}
is open in
[0,1]
.

We prove that
R:=\sup I_{T}\in I_{T}
.
If not, then we have
a_{\sigma(s)}<-T+s<-T+R\leq a_{\sigma(R)}
for all
s\in[0,R)
.
Hence,
\zeta_{\sigma(s)}(t)
is well-defined for all
s\in[0,R)
and
t\in(a_{\sigma(R)},0]
.
Now, we consider a timelike geodesic

\xi(s):=\exp_{\eta(-T)}\Bigl(s\bigl(\dot{\eta}(-T)+v\bigr)\Bigr)=\Theta\bigl(-T+s,\sigma(s)\bigr),

where
v\in T_{\eta(-T)}M
is identified with
v\in B
above and
\dot{\eta}(-T)+v\in\Omega_{\eta(-T)}
by the isometry of the translation as in Theorem
1.3
(
i
), and equality is seen from Theorem
1.3
(
ii
).
By construction, for all
s\in[0,R)
and
t\in(a_{\sigma(R)},0]
, we find

\eta(-T)\ll\xi(s)=\Theta\bigl(-T+s,\sigma(s)\bigr)\ll\Theta\bigl(a_{\sigma(R)},\sigma(s)\bigr)\ll\Theta\bigl(t,\sigma(s)\bigr).

Letting
s\to R
, we deduce from the closedness of causal diamonds (by the global hyperbolicity) that
\Theta(t,\sigma(R))\in J^{+}(\eta(-T))\cap J^{-}(\sigma(R))
for all
t\in(a_{\sigma(R)},0]
.
This, however, contradicts the non-total imprisonment, which follows from the global hyperbolicity (see, e.g.,
[
51
, Definition 2.20]
).
Therefore, we obtain
R\in I_{T}
and
I_{T}=[0,1]
, which implies
a_{\sigma(s)}=-\infty
for all
s\in[0,1]
since
T>0
was arbitrary in (
6.2
).
This completes the proof of the past completeness of asymptotes, and the future completeness can be shown in the same way as the past completeness for the reverse structure
\overleftarrow{L}
.

This concludes the proof of the fact that the local splitting neighborhood
W
can indeed be represented as a cylindrical product of the form
\mathbb{R}\times(\mathbf{b}_{\eta}^{-1}(0)\cap W)
.
In the globalization of the local splitting, one needs to extend the splitting of
W
to its boundary, where one must ensure that also the boundary asymptote is complete in the globally hyperbolic case.
Using the Berwald condition, the argument is entirely analogous to the completeness of asymptotes in the local case given above, and will be omitted.
We refer to
[
